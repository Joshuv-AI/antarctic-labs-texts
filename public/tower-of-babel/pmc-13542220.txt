Correcting diffusion artifacts in urinary oxygen tension monitoring
Authors: Ali Ramezani, Natalie A. Silverton, Laurence M. Saint-Pierre, Guillaume L. Hoareau, Kai Kuck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urinary oxygen tension (PuO2) is a promising continuous biomarker of renal medullary oxygenation, but distal catheter measurements are distorted by transit delay and wall diffusion, especially during oliguria. We developed and validated a physics-based algorithm that reconstructs bladder PuO2 from the distal signal. We formulated complementary steady-state and transient diffusion-transport models that couple wall diffusion to a lumen mass balance and solve an inverse problem to estimate bladder PuO2. Performance was assessed on a bench-top catheter system with controlled flows and in eight swine undergoing hemorrhagic shock and resuscitation (68,788 paired samples). On the bench top, adjustment reduced mean bias relative to bladder PuO2 from − 10.9 to −0.64 mmHg, narrowed 95% limits of agreement from 39.1 to 15.8 mmHg, and improved correlation with ground truth (R2 0.53→0.94). In vivo, pooled bias decreased from + 20.9 ± 11.5 to −4.1 ± 13.5 mmHg and mean absolute error from 21.1 to 9.5 mmHg, with the largest gains at low urinary flow. Modeling transport delay and wall diffusion enables reconstruction of bladder PuO2 from distal catheter measurements, supporting reliable, real-time urinary hypoxia monitoring and broader application to other conduit-based analyte measurements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-53461-z.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a serious complication across medical and surgical settings, affecting up to 60% of intensive care unit patients and occurring in up to 42% of adult cardiac surgery patients1–4. Even mild AKI is associated with sharply increased mortality, intensive care unit length of stay, and healthcare costs5–7. Standard AKI diagnostic criteria, such as a rise in serum creatinine concentration as defined by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines, often take several hours or days to manifest7–9, hampering early intervention. Novel biomarkers (such as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin or cystatin C) can predict AKI sooner, but require intermittent manual sampling and lab processing9–11. There is a pressing need for a continuous, real-time, non-laboratory monitor of renal damage that can signal impending AKI, allowing clinicians to intervene before irreversible damage occurs.

One key pathophysiological driver of AKI is inadequate renal oxygenation, particularly in the vulnerable renal medulla12. The outer medulla has a high metabolic demand and relatively low blood flow, making it prone to hypoxic injury during systemic insults like sepsis, major surgery, cardiopulmonary bypass, or heart failure13. Directly monitoring renal medullary oxygenation in patients is invasive and impractical clinically. However, the urinary tract presents an appealing possibility: when urine is first formed in the nephron and enters the collecting ducts, its urinary oxygen tension (PuO2) equilibrates with that of the surrounding medullary tissue. Thus, the oxygen tension of recently produced urine has been proposed as a surrogate window into the renal medullary oxygen partial pressure14,15. By the time urine reaches the bladder, some oxygen exchange may have occurred across the ureter, potentially confounding the measurement. Nevertheless, studies have shown that changes in bladder urinary oxygen tension track with changes in renal medullary oxygenation and hemodynamics13,15.

Continuous urinary oxygen monitoring could, therefore, serve as a real-time indicator of renal medullary oxygen insufficiency, and thus of AKI risk. When urinary catheterization is already clinically indicated, adding a distal PuO2 sensor does not require additional instrumentation within the urinary tract and preserves the standard catheter workflow16. However, a significant challenge in translating urinary oxygen monitoring to routine clinical use lies in the dynamics of urine flow and transit time. If urine flow is very low or intermittent (oliguria), the urine may linger in the catheter for extended periods, during which the oxygen tension can drift due to ongoing diffusion17. In other words, the transit of urine from bladder to the sensor not only delays but also distorts the PuO2 readings, reducing their reliability as real-time indicators of kidney status.

In prior cardiac-surgery data, bladder-to-distal urine transit times commonly ranged from approximately 10 to 30 min during low-flow states, estimated from catheter dead volume and measured urine output (~ 10–30 mL/h)18. Here, low-flow refers to states in which measured urine output is low enough to prolong catheter transit time, rather than to a visual assessment of bag filling. Such prolonged transit causes the corresponding O2 measurements to reflect renal O2 that occurred much earlier. Clearly, accounting for urine transit time is crucial to ensure that the measured PuO2 accurately reflects current renal medullary oxygenation. To overcome this limitation, our recent work has focused on computational solutions to estimate and correct for urine transit delays19.

However, a second important factor is radial oxygen diffusion across the urinary catheter wall. Urinary catheters are made of oxygen-permeable polymers (e.g., silicone and latex); when urine flow is low, molecular oxygen from the surrounding tissues (intra-abdominal catheter segment) and room air (extracorporeal catheter segment) readily diffuses inward or outward, altering the PuO2. This diffusion bias is larger during periods of oliguria when clinicians most need a reliable renal oxygen signal, and it cannot be eliminated by simply discarding low-flow data. Unless wall-diffusion is adjusted, PuO2 will systematically and nonlinearly overestimate true medullary oxygenation.

Accordingly, the objective of the present study was to develop and validate a mathematical model that quantifies and compensates for catheter-wall oxygen diffusion, allowing distal PuO2 readings to reflect bladder PuO2 and, consequently, medullary oxygen tension across a broad spectrum of urine flows. Building on our previously validated transit-time algorithm, we developed and validated diffusion-compensation models, and we hypothesized that they would reduce diffusion-induced error in both bench-top (in vitro) and large animal (in vivo) experiments19.

Methods

Mathematical modeling

To adjust the distortion that PuO2 acquires while transiting from the bladder to the distal sensor, we developed two mathematical models based on the principles of mass transport. The first was an analytical steady-state model that provides a foundational, closed-form solution. The second was a more computationally intensive transient model that accounts for the dynamic nature of diffusion within the catheter wall.

Transient diffusion model

The primary limitation of the steady-state model is its assumption of instantaneous equilibrium within the catheter wall. This simplification posits that the concentration gradient within the polymer wall immediately reaches a stable, linear profile in response to a change in lumen concentration. In reality, this process requires finite time to transition to a new equilibrium when lumen or environmental oxygen concentrations change, rather than equilibrating instantaneously22. The steady-state model, therefore, does not capture the initial, higher-flux phase of diffusion that occurs while the wall transitions toward a new concentration equilibrium. This discrepancy becomes particularly significant during rapid changes in urine concentration or for short transit times, when the wall may never reach an actual steady state. Long transit times are clinically important because they increase signal delay and cumulative distortion, whereas short transit times are the regime in which the transient and steady-state wall models differ most, because the wall may not have time to approach steady state. The transient model below addresses this by explicitly solving for the time-dependent concentration profile within the wall.

Real-time implementation

For clinical applications, the adjustment algorithm must operate in real-time. The steady-state model, being a closed-form analytical expression, is inherently real-time capable, as its evaluation is computationally trivial. The transient model, however, requires solving an iterative inverse problem (Sect. 2.1.3e), which might be computationally intensive for direct real-time use in simple processors. In our calculations, a single forward simulation of both segments with the default settings (12 wall cells, 60 time steps per second, 2 Picard sweeps per time step, total residence of 2 s) costs ~ 1 million floating-point operations (flops). The Brent root finder, seeded with the steady-state results, typically needs ~ 3–8 such forward evaluations (~ 3–8 million flops); the worst-case cap is 35 evaluations (~ 30 million flops). To translate the accuracy of the transient model to a real-time environment, a surrogate model must be created. This involves running the high-fidelity transient model offline across a wide grid of plausible inputs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:({P}_{distal},\:{t}_{in},\:{t}_{out})$$\end{document} to generate a comprehensive dataset. This dataset can then be used to create a computationally inexpensive surrogate, such as a multi-dimensional lookup table with linear interpolation, that can be deployed on a clinical monitor to provide instantaneous, adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{bladder}$$\end{document} values.

Apparatus and circuit

The core of the apparatus consisted of a simulated in-body environment created from a polycarbonate tube, within which a smaller, sealed tube served as a “bladder” reservoir. A standard clinical-grade 20 Fr Bard catheter (C. R. Bard, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was inserted into this system such that its proximal half was contained within the larger tube (the “tissue” environment) and its inflatable tip was sealed inside the smaller “bladder” tube.

Two flasks containing water served as reservoirs for the simulated urine and tissue fluid. The PO2 and temperature of the water in each flask were controlled by bubbling a mixture of nitrogen and oxygen gas through it. The gas mixture for each flask was regulated by a dedicated mass-flow controller (Alicat, Tucson, AZ, USA).

A diaphragm pump was used to circulate water from the “tissue” reservoir through the outer tube, creating a controlled environment around the in-body segment of the catheter. The “bladder” reservoir was connected to the catheter inlet. A valve placed at the distal end of the catheter was used to regulate the outflow, thereby controlling the flow rate of the simulated urine.

Experimental protocol

To test the models across a wide range of clinically relevant conditions, the experiment was designed to dynamically vary both the initial PO2 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{bladder}$$\end{document}) and the flow rate over several hours while maintaining the “in-body” segment at a physiologically relevant temperature (≈ 37 °C). The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{bladder}$$\end{document} was cyclically ramped between hyperoxic (PO2 > 100 mmHg) and low-oxygen (PO2 < 20 mmHg) states to simulate physiological fluctuations, with the low-oxygen condition serving as an experimental challenge state rather than a clinical definition of hypoxia. These bounds were selected to span a broad range of oxygenation states relevant to urinary PuO2 monitoring and to test the model under both low- and high-oxygen conditions, rather than to define clinical thresholds. Concurrently, the flow rate was modulated using the distal valve to create periods of high flow (> 400 mL/h) interspersed with periods of simulated oliguria, during which the flow was reduced to near-zero levels. This protocol ensured that the models were rigorously tested against a diverse and challenging dataset, including both high and low flow rates as well as rapid changes in oxygen partial pressure.

In vivo validation in a porcine model

To test the performance of the diffusion-adjustment models in a dynamic physiological environment, a porcine model of hemorrhagic shock and resuscitation was used.

Animal model and ethical approval

All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Utah (protocol number 00002200; approved 07/26/2023). The study was conducted using castrated male or non-pregnant female American Yorkshire swine (Sus scrofa domesticus) weighing 50–60 kg. A total of nine animals were included in the study; one animal was excluded intraoperatively due to a fatal embolic event (air embolism) resulting in a final cohort of eight animals for analysis. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations, and the study is reported in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Anesthesia and surgical preparation

Animals (Sus scrofa, Premier BioSource, CA, USA) were fasted overnight with free access to water. Anesthesia was induced with an intramuscular injection of tiletamine/zolazepam (6–8 mg/kg). Following endotracheal intubation, animals were mechanically ventilated and maintained under anesthesia with 1.5-3.0% isoflurane mixed in oxygen. The inspired oxygen fraction was adjusted to maintain pulse oximetry readings between 95 and 98%, and the respiratory rate was varied to maintain an end-tidal CO2 level of 35–45 mmHg. Core body temperature was maintained at approximately 39 °C using warming blankets. Warmed isotonic crystalloids (Plasma-Lyte 148, Baxter Healthcare Corporation, Deerfield, IL, USA) were administered intravenously at 5 mL/kg/hour. A midline laparotomy was performed to remove the spleen to prevent autotransfusion during hemorrhage.

Experimental protocol: hemorrhage and resuscitation

Following a stabilization period after instrumentation, animals were subjected to a controlled hemorrhage to induce renal hypoxia. Blood was removed from a femoral arterial line in staged increments (5 mL/kg, followed by two 10 mL/kg stages) to achieve approximately 25 mL/kg, corresponding to ~ 40% of the total blood volume. This was followed by a resuscitation phase, during which shed blood and isotonic crystalloids were administered to restore arterial blood pressure. This protocol was designed to generate a rich dataset with wide, dynamic variations in urine flow, renal perfusion, and urinary oxygen partial pressure for subsequent, post-hoc testing of the adjustment models.

At the conclusion of the experimental protocol, animals were euthanized under deep anesthesia by intravenous administration of pentobarbital/phenytoin sodium solution (Euthasol; 390 mg/mL pentobarbital sodium, 50 mg/mL phenytoin sodium) at a dose of 0.22 mL/kg, in accordance with AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. Death was confirmed by the absence of cardiac activity and respiratory movement prior to tissue collection.

Data processing

The transient model was implemented as the primary compensation algorithm because it explicitly resolves the time-dependent diffusion of oxygen across the catheter wall. To ensure comparison of corresponding urine element, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{\mathrm{b}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{r}}$$\end{document} signal was shifted forward by the sample-specific transit time, so that each distal measurement was aligned with its corresponding upstream value and both signals were expressed on the distal-tip time axis19. At each measurement event, the goal of the solver was to recover the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{bladder}$$\end{document} such that the simulated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{distal}$$\end{document} matched the observed end-of-catheter measurement. This inversion was framed as a root-finding problem, where the error function was defined as the difference between the measured and simulated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{distal}$$\end{document} values.

To accelerate convergence, we adopted a hybrid initialization strategy. Specifically, the steady-state two-segment model was evaluated first to generate a physically informed estimate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{bladder}$$\end{document}. This value served as the initial seed for the transient solver. By seeding the solver in this way, the search interval for Brent’s method root-finding routine could be narrowed, reducing the number of expensive transient evaluations required. The solver maintained a convergence tolerance of ± 0.1 mmHg at the distal end. Throughout the analysis, ‘flow’ refers to the average flow derived from transit time, not instantaneous sensor reading.

The catheter wall diffusion was discretized in the radial domain (15 nodes across the wall thickness) and advanced in time using a Crank–Nicolson scheme to maintain numerical stability. Lumen–wall coupling was enforced with Picard iterations (typically two per time step), and environmental boundary conditions were applied at the outer radius. Each residence interval was subdivided into 60 substeps per second of transit time, yielding a stable temporal resolution. To account for the experimental protocol, wall states were reset between measurement events; for clinical deployment, we instead propagated the wall state continuously across time, which avoids any dependence on event timing. This implementation strategy balanced physical rigor with computational efficiency: the steady-state seed prevented wasted iterations, while the transient model captured short-term diffusion dynamics.

For the animal data post-processing, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{tissue}$$\end{document} was not measured directly and was instead prescribed as a fixed boundary condition of 40 mmHg to approximate the peri-catheter tissue oxygen environment29.

For subgroup analysis of low-flow conditions, we examined an approximate KDIGO-based oliguria subgroup defined as flow ≤ 27.5 mL/h, corresponding to 0.5 mL/kg/h for the average body weight of the study animals8.

Discussion

The results support our hypothesis that a physics‑informed, transient diffusion–compensation algorithm can reconstruct \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{bladder}$$\end{document} from a distal urinary catheter PuO2 measurement, substantially reducing error relative to unadjusted readings. Bench‑top validations first showed that the algorithm could recover upstream \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{bladder}$$\end{document} under various controlled flow and transit conditions, thereby reducing the systematic diffusion bias, especially during low-flow conditions. In vivo, across eight pigs subjected to hemorrhagic shock and resuscitation (68,788 paired samples), the correction shifted the pooled mean bias from + 20.9 ± 11.5 mmHg to -4.1 ± 13.5 mmHg and reduced mean absolute error by ≈ 55%. Identity‑contour visualizations and a mixed‑effects model showed that adjustment moved the distal measurements closer to the identity line, with steeper slopes and lower intercepts than the unadjusted data. Notably, the most significant gains occurred during low‑flow (oliguric) epochs, when unadjusted distal values were most distorted, confirming that the algorithm preserves fidelity under the very conditions in which renal hypoxia is most likely to occur. At the lowest flow rates, however, some residual over-adjustment remained, likely because prolonged transit times drive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}}$$\end{document} closer to environmental equilibrium, making the inverse estimate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{\mathrm{b}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{r}}$$\end{document} more sensitive to boundary-condition and model-approximation uncertainty. In practical terms, this adjustment allows the distal catheter signal to track the bladder’s oxygen dynamics in real time, especially at low to moderate flows, whereas at extremely low or near-zero flow, uncertainty increases as the distal signal progressively approaches environmental equilibrium and the inverse problem becomes less well-conditioned.

Additional subgroup analyses showed that the adjustment substantially reduced bias and absolute error in both hypoxic and approximate KDIGO-based low-flow conditions, although threshold-based classification performance remained modest, particularly at the lowest flow states.

One subject (#4) represented an extreme low-flow regime, characterized by prolonged near‑zero urine flow, with intermittent large flow spikes (potentially from intraoperative manipulation or a sensor placement/malfunction). In this setting, very long transit times and highly variable flow likely reduced the stability of both transit-time estimation and inverse reconstruction, resulting in less consistent agreement compared with the rest of the cohort. Excluding this subject, pooled bias (all points) was 22.1 ± 10.8 mmHg (unadjusted) vs. -3.2 ± 10.9 mmHg (adjusted), an 85.5% reduction in |bias|; mean absolute error likewise tightened (≈ 22.1→≈8.8 mmHg), further strengthening agreement with bladder measurements.

The distal signal conflates two transport phenomena: (i) transit delay, whereby older urine arrives at the distal tip, especially when flow is low, and (ii) radial oxygen exchange across the catheter wall. In the present study, this exchange is not measured directly but inferred from its effect on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}}$$\end{document} under controlled flow and environmental conditions. These effects flatten the bladder–distal slopes and introduce a flow-dependent upward bias. The simple steady‑state model assumes a wall perpetually at equilibrium and therefore cannot capture the finite time required for wall saturation or recovery during rapid flow changes. Our advanced solver, an unconditionally stable Crank–Nicolson scheme coupled to a lumen mass balance, propagates the wall state in time and aligns the bladder reference to the distal time base. Both unadjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}}$$\end{document} and adjusted estimates were evaluated on the same distal time axis; only the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{\mathrm{b}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{r}}$$\end{document} reference was shifted to account for transit delay, so the comparison between unadjusted and adjusted signals was not affected by the alignment step.

Clinical implications and other applications

Transit time‑aware correction enables distal urinary PO2 sensing to serve as a monitor of bladder (urinary) oxygenation, which prior clinical studies have linked to AKI risk15. In perioperative and critical‑care settings (e.g., major vascular surgery, trauma, sepsis, cardiopulmonary bypass), the adjusted signal supports earlier recognition of renal hypoxia, interpretable trends across large flow swings, and pragmatic alarm thresholds. Prior clinical work has used a PuO2 ≤ 15 mmHg as a working definition of urinary hypoxia associated with postoperative AKI31; by removing transport artifacts, our approach makes such thresholds more reliable at the acute point of care and better suited as continuous endpoints for reno‑protective trials or protocolized resuscitation.

These findings also have implications for next-generation urinary catheter design. Catheters intended for oxygen monitoring should minimize wall permeability and uncontrolled exchange with the environment, for example through lower-permeability materials, shorter extracorporeal segments, standardized wall thickness, or integrated distal sensor housings positioned close to the catheter outlet. Such design features could reduce diffusion artifacts at the hardware level and lessen the computational burden of correction.

The framework generalizes to any conduit‑based measurement in which tubing transit and polymer interfaces blur dynamics. Candidate applications include distal measurements of other urinary analytes (PCO2, pH, metabolites), respiratory sampling lines and capnography adapters where long tubing damps transients, pleural/abdominal/ventricular drains, peritoneal dialysis catheters, microdialysis outlets, and sensors embedded in extracorporeal circuits or microfluidic point‑of‑care devices. In each case, explicitly modeling transport can turn a convenient but biased distal signal into a more faithful representation of the upstream compartment.

Computational considerations for deployment

Solving the inverse problem row‑by‑row is more computationally intensive than applying a static calibration, but it remains tractable offline. For real‑time integration into bedside monitors, the same algorithm can be delivered with a surrogate: precomputed lookup tables, compact polynomial maps, or neural emulators trained on the forward model across the physiological range. These surrogates retain interpretability while offering real-time inference and straightforward uncertainty quantification.

Limitations and mitigations

We model the lumen as laminar and well-mixed, and oxygen transport through the catheter wall as radially symmetric diffusion with fixed material properties. These simplifications are appropriate for standard urinary catheters and the flow ranges studied; however, unusual geometries, temperature gradients, aging, or multilayer walls could break these assumptions and introduce errors. At very low or zero flow, long transit times pull the distal signal toward ambient equilibrium, making the initial bladder value difficult to identify. To prevent over-correction, we constrain the inversion by bracketing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{bladder}$$\end{document} in the Brent search and capping the Picard sweeps per time step. If the solver cannot meet the convergence tolerance or the measured transit time exceeds a predefined limit, the sample is flagged as invalid and yet displayed to the user. In clinical practice, distal monitoring is performed using our custom sensor positioned between the urinary catheter and the collection bag, and the bag should remain below bladder level, since bag height and accumulated urine volume may alter hydrostatic pressure, thereby affecting flow, transit time, and indirectly contributing to variation in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}}$$\end{document} measurements. Finally, although the porcine model captures large hemodynamic excursions, prospective human studies across catheter sizes and materials are needed to establish generalizability.

Future directions

Prospective validation in surgical and ICU cohorts with prespecified decision thresholds is a priority. Real‑time implementation should pair the adjustment with confidence bands, flow‑aware alerts, and online parameter adaptation (estimating wall‑exchange and transit coefficients using routine perturbations such as voids or flushes). It is also important to establish how PuO2 monitoring can move beyond AKI risk prediction to actively guiding therapy—helping clinicians initiate, titrate, and evaluate reno-protective interventions. Extending the solver to multiplexed analytes and other catheterized systems will demonstrate the broader value of transport-aware corrections.

Conclusion

Explicitly modeling transport delay and wall diffusion converts a biased distal measurement into a physiologically faithful signal. This approach preserves the familiar clinical catheter workflow, achieves accuracy comparable to bladder sensing, and provides a broadly applicable framework for improving a wide range of conduit‑based monitoring systems.
