Notes on Ordinary Conformal Differential Equations of order α \alpha ( 0 < α ≤ 1 0<\alpha\leq 1 )
Carlos E. Cadenas R

Abstract
These notes aim to provide a classical approach to solving some conformable differential equations based on prior knowledge of how to solve ordinary differential equations. That is, using the methods of separation of variables, homogeneous equations, linear, Bernoulli and exact. Representative examples are presented in all cases. Emphasis is placed on the new definitions and notations.

Conformable derivative and
\alpha-

We will proceed to define the conformal derivative, which is due to Khalil et al., see
[
8
]
.

Definition 2.1

Given
f:\left[0,\infty\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Then the “conformable fractional derivative” of
f
of order
\alpha
is defined by:

\frac{df(x)}{dx^{(\alpha)}}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{f(x+\epsilon x^{1-\alpha})-f(x)}{\epsilon}

(2.1)

for all
x>0,\alpha\in(0,1)
.

In the original definition they use
T_{\alpha}
as the operator which they call the fractional derivative of order
\alpha
, which is given by
(
2.1
). In these notes we will use
\frac{df(x)}{dx^{(\alpha)}},(f)^{(\alpha)}
or
f^{(\alpha)}(x)
indistinctly.

Below we mention some of the properties of the conformal derivative that will be used as the document progresses. The following theorem can be found in
[
8
]
.

Theorem 2.2

[
8
]

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
f,g
be
\alpha
-differentiable at a point
x>0
. Then

1.

(af+bg)^{(\alpha)}=a(f)^{(\alpha)}+b(g)^{(\alpha)}
, for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

2.

(x^{n})^{(\alpha)}=nx^{n-\alpha}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{R}
.

3.

(C)^{(\alpha)}=0
, for all constant functions
f(x)=C
.

4.

(fg)^{(\alpha)}=f(g)^{(\alpha)}+g(f)^{(\alpha)}

5.

\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{(\alpha)}=\frac{g(f)^{(\alpha)}-f(g)^{(\alpha)}}{g^{2}}
.

6.

If
f
is differentiable, then
(f)^{(\alpha)}(x)=x^{1-\alpha}\frac{df}{dx}(x)
.

On the other hand, the chain rule for the conformable derivative is given by:

Theorem 2.3

[
2
]

Let
f
and
g
be two function differentiable, such that
g
is differentiable at any
t
, and
f
is differentiable at any
g(t)
, and then the conformable derivative obeys the Chain rule, meaning

(f\circ g(x))^{(\alpha)}=x^{1-\alpha}g(x)^{1-\alpha}\frac{dg(x)}{dx}f^{(\alpha)}(t)\left|{}_{t=g(x)}\right.

To determine whether a differential equation is exact, it is necessary to use Clairaut’s theorem, also known as Schwarz’s theorem or Young’s theorem. Therefore, the definition of a conformable partial derivative and Clairaut’s theorem for partial derivatives of fractional orders
[
2

Definition 2.4

[

Let
f

m

x_{1},\cdots,x_{m}

0<\alpha\leq 1

\frac{\partial f(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i},\cdots,x_{m})}{\partial x_{i}^{(\alpha)}}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{f(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i}+\epsilon x_{i}^{1-\alpha},\cdots,x_{m})-f(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i},\cdots,x_{m})}{\epsilon}

Theorem 2.5

[
2

Assume that
f(x,y)
is function for which
\partial_{x}^{(\alpha)}\left[\partial_{y}^{(\beta)}f(x,y)\right]

\partial_{y}^{(\beta)}\left[\partial_{x}^{(\alpha)}f(x,y)\right]

\partial_{x}^{(\alpha)}\left[\partial_{y}^{(\beta)}f(x,y)\right]=\partial_{y}^{(\beta)}\left[\partial_{x}^{(\alpha)}f(x,y)\right]

(2.2)

The

-fractional integral of a function

I_{\alpha}^{a}(f)(t)=\int_{a}^{t}\frac{f(x)}{x^{1-\alpha}}dx^{(\alpha)}

4
(\alpha)
-separable differential equations

Let the fractional differential equation be

M(x,y)dx^{(\alpha)}+N(x,y)dy=0

(4.1)

It is said to be of
(\alpha)
-separable variables if by means of an integrating factor it can be transformed into the differential equation

x^{1-\alpha}F(x)dx^{(\alpha)}+G(y)dy=0

so that to solve it the differential equation must be integrated, obtaining:

\int x^{1-\alpha}F(x)dx^{(\alpha)}+\int G(y)dy=C

which due to the application of property (
3.1
) is equivalent to

\int F(x)dx+\int G(y)dy=C

Remark: To solve the conformable ordinary differential equation, it is not necessary to transform the conformable indefinite integral into a classical indefinite integral. However, both options have been given here.

Example 4.1

Solve the fractional differential equation:

\frac{dy}{dx^{(\alpha)}}=\frac{x^{1-\alpha}e^{x}}{y\left(1+e^{x}\right)}

To solve this conformable differential equation, multiply by
ydx^{(\alpha)}
and apply the definition (
3.2
), thus obtaining

\frac{x^{1-\alpha}e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}dx^{(\alpha)}-ydy=0

(4.2)

Then, (
3.1
) is used and we obtain

\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}dx-ydy=0

Which is solved in the book
[
10
]
and whose solution is
y^{2}-2\ln(1+e^{x})=C
. Note that in equation (
4.2
) indefinite integration can also be applied directly and the same solution would be obtained by (
3.3
). That is

\int\frac{x^{1-\alpha}e^{x}}{(1+e^{x})}dx^{(\alpha)}-\int ydy=0

obtaining in the same way
y^{2}-2\ln(1+e^{x})=C
as a solution.

Example 4.2

Solve the fractional differential equation: (example 4.2 of
[
8
]
):

\frac{dy}{dx^{(\alpha)}}+y=0

By separating the variables we obtain

\frac{dy}{y}+dx^{(\alpha)}=0

Which when integrated indefinitely has the following solution:

\ln|y|+\frac{x^{\alpha}}{\alpha}=C

Or explicitly

y=Ae^{-\frac{x^{\alpha}}{\alpha}}

Example 4.3

Solve the conformable differential equation

\frac{dy}{dx^{(\alpha)}}+x^{\beta}y=0,\quad\beta\in\mathbf{R}-\left\{-\alpha\right\}

By separating the variables we obtain

\frac{dy}{y}+x^{\beta}dx^{(\alpha)}=0

That by integrating indefinitely and clearing the variable
y
we have the solution:

y=Ae^{-\frac{x^{\alpha+\beta}}{\alpha+\beta}}

NOTE: If in this example
\beta=-\alpha
, the solution to the fractional differential equation is
xy=C
.

4.1
A particular case

Let the differential equation be

\frac{dy}{dx^{(\alpha)}}=f(ax^{\alpha}+by+c)

(4.3)

By means of a change of variables
z=ax^{\alpha}+by+c
it can be reduced to a differential equation in separate variables. Then the derivative of
z
of order
\alpha
is given by

\frac{dz}{dx^{(\alpha)}}=a{\alpha}+b\frac{dy}{dx^{(\alpha)}}

(4.4)

Substituting (
4.3
) into equation (
4.4
) gives

\frac{dz}{dx^{(\alpha)}}=a{\alpha}+bf(z)

separating variable

\frac{dz}{a{\alpha}+bf(z)}=dx^{(\alpha)}

that by integrating indefinitely

\int\frac{dz}{a{\alpha}+bf(z)}=\frac{x^{\alpha}}{\alpha}+C

After solving the integral of the left-hand side, we proceed to return the change and thus obtain the solution to the differential equation (
4.3
).

Example 4.4

Solve the equation

\frac{dy}{dx^{(\alpha)}}=\left(x^{\alpha}+y\right)^{2}

(4.5)

By using the change of variables
z=x^{\alpha}+y
and differentiating
\frac{dz}{dx^{(\alpha)}}=\alpha+\frac{dy}{dx^{(\alpha)}}
. By using the equation (
4.5
)

\frac{dz}{dx^{(\alpha)}}=\alpha+z^{2}

Separating variables

\frac{dz}{\alpha+z^{2}}=dx^{(\alpha)}

(4.6)

By applying the indefinite integral to both sides of the differential equation (
4.6
) and solving said integrals, we obtain

\sqrt{\alpha}\arctan\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)=\frac{x^{\alpha}}{\alpha}+C_{1}

After returning the change and solving for
y
we have:

y=\sqrt{\alpha}\tan\left(\frac{x^{\alpha}}{\alpha^{3/2}}+C\right)-x^{\alpha}

This example is an adaptation of a similar one presented in

5
Differential equations
(n,\alpha)
-homogeneous

The function
M
is defined to be homogeneous of order
n+1-\alpha
if
M(\lambda x,\lambda y)=\lambda^{n+1-\alpha}M(x,y)
. Similarly,
N
is homogeneous of order
n
if
N(\lambda x,\lambda y)=\lambda^{n}N(x,y)
. Now, the fractional differential equation given in (
4.1
) is said to be homogeneous of order
(n,\alpha)
if
M
and
N
are homogeneous of order
n+1-\alpha
and
n
, respectively. In this case, we can proceed using the change of variable
y=xu
, so using the derivative rule of a product, we have

\frac{dy}{dx^{(\alpha)}}=\frac{dx}{dx^{(\alpha)}}u+x\frac{du}{dx^{(\alpha)}}

or what is the same

\frac{dy}{dx^{(\alpha)}}=x^{1-\alpha}u+x\frac{du}{dx^{(\alpha)}}

or in the differential form

dy=x^{1-\alpha}udx^{(\alpha)}+xdu

Replacing the variable change mentioned in (
4.1
):

M(x,xu)dx^{(\alpha)}+N(x,xu)(x^{1-\alpha}udx^{(\alpha)}+xdu)=0

Assuming that the fractional differential equation given in (
4.1
) is homogeneous of order
(n,\alpha)
then the above equation becomes:

x^{n+1-\alpha}M(1,u)dx^{(\alpha)}+x^{n}N(1,u)(x^{1-\alpha}udx^{(\alpha)}+xdu)=0

After separating variables, considering
F(u)=M(1,u)
and
G(u)=N(1,u)
we have:

\frac{dx^{(\alpha)}}{x}+\frac{G(u)du}{F(u)+uG(u)}=0

By integrating this differential equation

\frac{x^{\alpha-1}}{\alpha-1}+\int\frac{G(u)du}{F(u)+uG(u)}=C,\quad(\alpha\neq 1)

If
\alpha=1
, then

\ln|x|+\int\frac{G(u)du}{F(u)+uG(u)}=C,\quad(\alpha\neq 1)

After solving the indefinite integral, the change must be returned.

Example 5.1

The differential equation
(2x+3y)dy=x^{1-\alpha}(x+y)dx^{(\alpha)}
is
(1,\alpha)
-homogeneous which is equivalent to the ordinary differential equation

\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{x+y}{2x+3y}

which appears in example 4.4 of
[
8
]
.

5.1
A particular case

Consider the fractional differential equation
(0,n)
-homogeneous

\frac{dy}{dx^{(\alpha)}}=x^{1-\alpha}\psi(y/x)

When using the change of variables
y=ux
we have that

\frac{dy}{dx^{(\alpha)}}=x^{1-\alpha}u+x\frac{du}{dx^{(\alpha)}}=x^{1-\alpha}\psi(u)

Separating variables

\frac{dx^{(\alpha)}}{x^{\alpha}}+\frac{du}{u-\psi(u)}=0

By integrating indefinitely we have

\ln|Cx|+\int\frac{du}{u-\psi(u)}=0

Example 5.2

Solve the differential equation

\frac{dy}{dx^{(\alpha)}}=x^{1-\alpha}\left(\frac{y}{x}+\frac{x}{y}\right)

In this case
\psi(u)=u+1/u
, so solving this fractional differential equation would give

\ln|Cx|+\frac{u^{2}}{2}=0

Convertible to

Let

Example 5.3

Solve the differential equation

\frac{dy}{dx^{(\alpha)}}=-(x-1)^{1-\alpha}\frac{2x-3y+4}{3x-2y+1}.

Using the changes of variables

This ODE is

whose solution is

(v+u)^{5}=C(v-u)

Let the fractional differential equation (

\frac{dy}{dx^{(\alpha)}}+P(x)y=Q(x)

By multiplying it by the integrating factor

e^{I^{(\alpha)}(P(x))}y=\int e^{I^{(\alpha)}(P(x))}Q(x)dx^{(\alpha)}+C

y

y=e^{-I^{(\alpha)}(P(x))}\left(I^{(\alpha)}\left(e^{I^{(\alpha)}(P(x))}Q(x)\right)+C\right)

8
(
\alpha
)-exact differential equations

If
z=f(x,y)=C
, the differential is given by

dz^{(\alpha)}=\partial_{x}^{(\alpha)}f(x,y)dx^{(\alpha)}+\partial_{y}f(x,y)dy=0

(8.1)

By Clairaut’s theorem, for
\beta=1
in equation (
2.2
), if

\partial_{x}^{(\alpha)}\left[\partial_{y}f(x,y)\right]=\partial_{y}\left[\partial_{x}^{(\alpha)}f(x,y)\right]

We say that the equation (
8.1
) is (
\alpha
)-exact.

Then, to solve the differential equation (
4.1
) expressed in the form

\partial_{x}^{(\alpha)}f(x,y)dx^{(\alpha)}+\partial_{y}f(x,y)dy=0

It must be verified that:

\partial_{y}M(x,y)=\partial_{x}^{(\alpha)}N(x,y)dy

which would be the condition for the differential equation (
4.1
) to be (
\alpha
)-exact. Therefore, this differential equation is (
\alpha
)-exact.
So, to solve the exact (
\alpha
) equation, proceed as follows:

Since
M(x,y)=\partial_{x}^{(\alpha)}f(x,y)
then

f(x,y)=\int M(x,y)dx^{(\alpha)}+g(y)

then, as it is satisfied that

N(x,y)=\partial_{y}f(x,y)

so

N(x,y)=\partial_{y}\left(\int M(x,y)dx^{(\alpha)}+g(y)\right)

Solving for
g(y)
gives the solution to the ordinary differential equation of order
\alpha
:
f(x,y)+C
; that is

f(x,y)=\int M(x,y)dx^{(\alpha)}+\int\left(N(x,y)-\partial_{y}\int M(x.y)dx^{(\alpha)}\right)dy=C

Example 8.1

x^{1-\alpha}e^{-y}dx^{(\alpha)}-\left(2y+xe^{-y}\right)dy=0

Identifying
M
and
N
we have that:
M(x,y)=x^{1-\alpha}e^{-y}
and
N(x,y)=-\left(2y+xe^{-y}\right)
, so their respective derivatives are

\partial_{y}M(x,y)=\partial_{x}^{(\alpha)}N(x,y)=-x^{1-\alpha}e^{-y}

So the differential equation in this example is (
\alpha
)-exact. Solving according to the above, we have:

\partial_{x}^{(\alpha)}f(x,y)=x^{1-\alpha}e^{-y}=M(x,y)

calculating
f
we have:

f(x,y)=\int{x^{1-\alpha}e^{-y}dx^{(\alpha)}}=xe^{-y}+g(y)

On the other hand, as

N(x,y)=-\left(2y+xe^{-y}\right)=\partial_{y}f(x,y)

then

-\left(2y+xe^{-y}\right)=-xe^{-y}+g^{\prime}(y)

then
g^{\prime}(y)=-2y
, so
g(y)=-y^{2}+C_{1}
. Thus the solution to the differential equation is

f(x,y)=xe^{-y}-y^{2}=C

9
Final Comments

In these notes, a somewhat novel approach to solving conformable ordinary differential equations was presented.
