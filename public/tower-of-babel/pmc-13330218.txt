Design and evaluation of a bifunctional fusion polypeptide for targeted delivery of tigecycline against drug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae
Authors: Meiling Zhou, Yuming Yao, Shuo Ma, Yaya Chen, GuliNazhaer Abudushalamu, Dawen Chen, Xiao Li, Yan Tao, Yue Hou, Xun Gao, Fengfeng Zhao, Chen Zhang, Hua Shao, Guoqiu Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe overuse of antibiotics and the continuous evolution of drug-resistant bacteria present substantial obstacles to the clinical treatment of Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) infections. This study reports the rational design of a bifunctional fusion polypeptide, S-thanatin (Ts)-functionalized elastin-like carrier (Ts-ELP), which integrates the antimicrobial peptide (AMP) Ts with a human-derived elastin‑like polypeptides (ELPs). This designer polypeptide serves as a smart carrier, capable of efficiently encapsulating the broad-spectrum antibiotic tigecycline (Tig) via reversible temperature-responsive phase transition hydrophobic interaction, resulting in a self-assembled, targeted nano-antibiotic, Tig@Ts-ELP (Tig@TsE). Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo evaluations demonstrate that Tig@TsE exhibits multiple advantageous features, including rapid and simple preparation, specific lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-targeting capability, excellent in vivo targeted delivery efficiency, and dual-responsive drug release triggered by the acidic pH and elevated matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) levels in the CRKP-infected microenvironment, coupled with favorable biocompatibility. Moreover, the Ts peptide and Tig exhibit marked synergistic antibacterial effects against CRKP. This innovative nano-antibiotic delivery system is designed to increase local drug concentration at the infection site, boost bactericidal activity, and reduce systemic toxicity, thereby presenting a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of drug-resistant bacterial pneumonia.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04497-w.Keywords: Elastin-like carrier, Nano-antibiotic, Targeted delivery, Dual-responsive, Synergistic antibacterial

Introduction

Bacterial infections remain one of the major threats to global health. In recent years, driven by antibiotic misuse and the continued evolution of resistance, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has become an urgent public health challenge [1–4]. According to reports from the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 7.0 ~ 7.7 million deaths each year worldwide are attributable to bacterial infections; among these, more than 60% are associated with drug-resistant pathogens, and about 16% are directly attributable to infections caused by bacteria that are not treatable with available antibiotic therapy [5]. Notably, carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacilli (CRGNB) pose an especially severe threat. Klebsiella pneumoniae (KPN), a common pathogen responsible for hospital-acquired infections, has seen the emergence and spread of carbapenem-resistant strains, i.e., carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP), which has markedly reduced the number of clinically effective treatment options. Owing to high virulence, strong biofilm-forming capacity, and multidrug- resistance (MDR), CRKP has become a leading cause of nosocomial infections. CRKP exhibits high-level resistance to most antibiotics, including carbapenems [6], and the mortality associated with CRKP infections continues to increase [7, 8], posing a serious threat particularly to older adults and immunocompromised individuals and creating substantial challenges for clinical management [9, 10].

Tigecycline (Tig), a broad-spectrum glycylcycline antibiotic, inhibits bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the 30S ribosomal subunit and blocking the entry of aminoacyl-tRNA [11]. It retains antibacterial activity against MDR-GNB [12] and is therefore considered an important therapeutic option for CRKP infections [13, 14]. However, its clinical utility is limited by low systemic exposure, suboptimal tissue penetration, susceptibility to bacterial efflux, and insufficient efficacy as monotherapy [15, 16]. In severe CRKP or other Gram-negative bacterial (GNB) infections, combination regimens (e.g., with polymyxins, carbapenems or aminoglycosides) and/or high-dose strategies are often required [17–19], which may increase the risk of adverse events and have even been associated with higher mortality [20–22]. Accordingly, developing novel delivery strategies capable of enhancing Tig accumulation at infection sites, improving antibacterial efficacy, and reducing systemic toxicity is of substantial clinical significance.

Nanomedicine-based drug delivery systems (DDS) offer a promising approach to improve antibiotic performance [23, 24]. Among these, peptide- and protein-based carriers have attracted considerable attention because of their favorable biocompatibility, biodegradability, and amenability to functional modification [25]. Elastin-like polypeptides (ELPs) are thermoresponsive biopolymers derived from the repetitive motifs of human tropoelastin, typically composed of the canonical pentapeptide sequence Val-Pro-Gly-X-Gly (VPGXG), where X represents a guest residue that can be varied to modulate physicochemical properties [26, 27]. Above their lower critical solution temperature (LCST), ELPs self-assemble into nanoparticles driven by hydrophobic interactions [28]. Moreover, ELPs are readily engineered via genetic approaches and have demonstrated potential for drug delivery applications [29, 30]. Nevertheless, challenges remain in targeted antibiotic delivery, particularly in developing “smart” systems capable of microenvironment-responsive release in bacterial infections.

In recent years, functionalizing nanocarrier surfaces with targeting ligands (e.g., peptides or antibodies) to enhance bacterial recognition has become a key strategy for improving nano-antibiotic efficacy [31, 32]. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), which commonly act through membrane-disruptive mechanisms and are less likely to induce resistance, have been widely used to confer both targeting and antibacterial functions to nanoplatforms [33, 34]. S-thanatin (Ts) is a naturally occurring short AMP [35, 36]. Its β-hairpin conformation, stabilized by a disulfide bond between Cys11 and Cys18, enables specific binding to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) on the surface of GNB [37, 38], thereby disrupting membrane integrity and interfering with energy metabolism, with potent activity against a range of drug-resistant bacteria [39]. Prior studies have shown that Ts-modified nanocarriers promote antibiotic accumulation within bacteria [40, 41]. For example, Ts-functionalized levofloxacin-loaded liposomes enable targeted delivery and exhibit excellent therapeutic efficacy in a murine sepsis model induced by MDR-KPN [42]. In addition, Ts-modified calcium phosphate nanoparticles further increase Tig accumulation in bacteria [43].

Based on this rationale, the present study designed a Ts-peptide-functionalized, pH and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) dual-responsive elastin-like carrier (Ts-ELP) (Fig. 1A). In the construct design, the TrxA tag encoded by the pET-32a( +) vector was used to facilitate proper folding and improve soluble expression of the fusion protein. A thrombin cleavage site (LVPRGS) was introduced to precisely expose the N-terminus of Ts after enzymatic cleavage, thereby generating an intact Ts-linker-ELP fusion protein and avoiding redundant methionine (Met, M). In addition, an MMP-9-cleavable motif (GPLGLPG) was incorporated at the N-terminus of the ELP to enable specific cleavage by MMP-9, which is overexpressed in inflammatory environments triggered by CRKP infection [44]. This design allows the release of free Ts peptides, thereby strengthening bacterial targeting and antibacterial activity.

The purified Ts-ELP carrier encapsulates Tig under physiological conditions via liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) and self-assembles into a nanocomposite, Tig@Ts-ELP (Tig@TsE) (Fig. 1B). The ELP sequence contains histidine-rich units ([VPG(VH4)G]14), which confer pH-responsive drug-release behavior. In the acidic microenvironment associated with CRKP infection, protonation increases surface charge and weakens hydrophobic interactions, thereby promoting drug release. This pH-triggered release is expected to act in concert with MMP-9-mediated site-specific liberation of Ts peptides in inflamed tissues, enabling synergistic antibacterial activity of Ts and Tig (Fig. 1C).

In vitro and in vivo evaluations demonstrate that Tig@TsE specifically targets CRKP, achieves dual responsiveness to pH and enzymatic cues within the infection microenvironment, and exerts synergistic antibacterial effects of Ts and Tig. In a pulmonary infection model, Tig@TsE increases drug accumulation in the lung, mitigates inflammatory responses, prolongs survival, and exhibits favorable biosafety. Collectively, this study provides a novel nanodelivery strategy that overcomes key therapeutic limitations of Tig in treating CRKP infections and provides new insights for the clinical management of such infections.

Construction of an expression plasmid for the elastin-like carrier Ts-ELP

The prokaryotic expression plasmid pET-32a( +)-Thrombin-Ts-Linker-ELP encoding Ts-ELP was synthesized via a full-gene synthesis method. The designed amino acid sequence included a thrombin cleavage site (LVPRGS) and the natural AMP Ts sequence (GSKKPVPIIYCNRRTGKCQRM), introduced downstream of the TrxA tag in the pET-32a( +) vector. A linker (GGGSG) was incorporated at the C-terminus of the Ts sequence, followed by the MMP-9 cleavage site (GPLGLPG), which was fused to the N-terminus of a pH-sensitive, human elastin-like polypeptide (ELP). A His-tag was added at the C-terminus of the ELP via a linker for protein purification. The complete amino acid and nucleotide sequences of the Ts-ELP fusion protein are provided in Table S1. Codon optimization for Escherichia coli expression was performed. The Thrombin-Ts-Linker-ELP gene fragment was synthesized and cloned into the pET-32a( +) vector (Ampicillin resistance) using the 5′ XbaI and 3′ XhoI restriction enzyme sites. Recombinant plasmid DNA (pET-32a( +)-Thrombin-Ts-Linker-ELP) and bacterial stab cultures containing the recombinant plasmid were then prepared. The complete plasmid map and agarose gel electrophoresis results following restriction enzyme digestion are shown in Fig. S1. To construct a non-targeting control carrier, the two cysteine residues (Cys, C) in the Ts sequence were mutated to alanine (Ala, A), creating the mutant (MTs-ELP). The corresponding mutant plasmid (pET-32a-Thrombin-MTs-Linker-ELP) and bacterial stab cultures were generated similarly. Sequence accuracy was confirmed by DNA sequencing, and the physicochemical properties of the fusion proteins were predicted using the online Expasy tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/).

Expression and purification of fusion proteins

The stab culture was streaked onto an LB agar plate containing ampicillin and incubated overnight at 37 °C. A single colony was selected and cultured in 5 mL of LB medium for amplification. Once the culture reached the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 nm ≈ 0.6 ~ 0.8), it was transferred to 1 L of LB medium and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. Upon reaching the logarithmic phase, isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to a final concentration of 0.5 mM to induce protein expression, and the culture was incubated overnight at 20 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. After harvesting the bacterial cells by centrifugation at 6,000 rpm for 20 min, they were lysed using an ultrasonic cell disruptor to release the proteins. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 min to remove bacterial debris, and the target protein (Thrombin-Ts-ELP) was isolated using a nickel affinity chromatography column (Ni Focurose FF, IDA, GE). The protein was then exchanged into a Tris-NaCl buffer solution (pH 8.0) using a desalting column. Thrombin (200 U/mL) was added to the protein solution at a protein-to-thrombin mass ratio of 1:0.065, and the mixture was incubated overnight at 25 °C on an orbital shaker at 100 rpm to remove the TrxA tag and the thrombin cleavage site, resulting in the intact Ts-ELP (targeted) or MTs-ELP (non-targeted) delivery carriers. Further purification was performed using a nickel affinity chromatography column and desalting columns to achieve high purity. The purified protein was exchanged into phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored at -80 °C for future use. Protein purity was assessed using SDS-PAGE, and band intensities were quantified by densitometry using ImageJ; purity (%) was calculated as the integrated density of the target protein band divided by the total integrated density of all bands in the same lane, multiplied by 100. Endotoxin levels in the purified protein preparations were measured using a LAL assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Circular dichroism (CD)

CD measurements were performed using a J-1500 spectropolarimeter (JASCO, Japan) in a 1.0 mm path-length cuvette. The Ts-ELP or MTs-ELP fusion proteins were dissolved in PBS at a concentration of 0.4 mg/mL. CD spectra were recorded from 190 to 280 nm at 4 °C, 25 °C (room temperature, RT), and 50 °C, with a scanning speed of 200 nm/min and a bandwidth of 0.5 nm. Multiple scans were averaged for each sample, and all spectra were baseline-corrected by subtracting the solvent spectrum obtained under identical conditions. For secondary-structure analysis, the CD data were processed using the online tool BESTSEL (https://bestsel.elte.hu/index.php), and the measured ellipticity (mdeg) was converted to mean residue molar ellipticity (MRE) according to [θ]MRW = θ/(10 × cr × l), where θ is the measured ellipticity (mdeg), cr is the molar concentration per residue (cr = c × N), c is the molar concentration of the protein, N is the number of residues, and l is the pathlength (cm).

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

For FTIR spectroscopy, dried samples of Tig, MTs-ELP, Ts-ELP, Tig@MTsE, and Tig@TsE were mixed with potassium bromide at a 1:50 mass ratio, ground, and pressed into pellets, respectively. FTIR spectra were obtained using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet Summit X spectrometer (USA) in the wavenumber range of 4000 ~ 400 cm−1, with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 scans per measurement. The raw spectra were processed by baseline correction using the software workflow, where a linear or polynomial baseline was fitted across the spectral range and subtracted to remove baseline drift and offsets.

Determination of phase transition temperature

Ts-ELP and MTs-ELP were diluted to various concentrations (0.8 mg/mL, 0.4 mg/mL, 0.2 mg/mL, and 0.1 mg/mL) using PBS (10 mM, pH 7.4). Aliquots of 100 µL were transferred into a 96-well plate. Absorbance at 350 nm, indicating solution turbidity, was measured over a temperature range of 20 °C to 50 °C using a SpectraMax M3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices), with measurements taken at 2 °C intervals. The temperature-absorbance curve was generated using GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 software (GraphPad Software, Inc.), and the phase transition temperature (Tt) of the fusion AMPs was determined by nonlinear fitting (curve fitting) using a sigmoidal dose-response model with variable slope. The Tt was defined as the temperature at which the fitted curve reached 50% of the maximum turbidity.

Preparation and characterization of self-assembled nano-antibiotic Tig@TsE

The Ts-ELP solution (0.5 mg/mL) was stirred uniformly at 100 rpm using a magnetic stirrer, to which an aqueous solution of Tig (4 mg/mL) was added dropwise to achieve a Ts-ELP: Tig mass ratio of 1:4. After thorough mixing, the solution was stirred for an additional 30 min. The mixture was then transferred to a water bath and incubated at 50 °C for 5 min to encapsulate Tig. Zinc chloride (ZnCl2) was subsequently added as a stabilizer to a final concentration of 50 μM to maintain the integrity of the formed nanostructures [45], and the mixture was stirred uniformly. The resulting solution was placed in an ultrafiltration centrifuge tube (3.5 kDa, Millipore, USA) and centrifuged at 4,000 g for 30 min. The product was washed with 10 volumes of PBS (10 mM, pH 7.4) and subjected to additional ultrafiltration. The self-assembled nano-antibiotic, Tig@TsE, was then collected. A control, non-targeted self-assembled nano-antibiotic, Tig@MTs-ELP (Tig@MTsE), was prepared following the same procedure. A standard curve was established by measuring absorbance at 245 nm using a SpectraMax M3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, TU-1080, Beijing, China), after performing a twofold serial dilution of a Tig solution from 800 μg/mL to 50 μg/mL. Based on this standard curve, the total Tig content in the Tig@TsE suspension and the unencapsulated Tig content in the ultrafiltration tube filtrate were determined. The hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of Tig, Ts peptide, the fusion carriers Ts-ELP and MTs-ELP, and the nano-antibiotics Tig@TsE and Tig@MTsE were measured using a Malvern particle size analyzer (Malvern, UK). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) size distributions were reported as intensity-weighted distributions. All measurements were performed at room temperature (RT). The morphology of each component was observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEOL JEM-1230, Japan).

pH sensitivity and cumulative drug release

After 30 min of incubation in buffers at pH 5.0, 6.5, and 7.4, the particle size, PDI, and zeta potential of the nano-antibiotic Tig@TsE were determined. The in vitro drug release profile of the nano-antibiotic delivery system was assessed using the dialysis bag method. Solutions (1 mL) containing Tig, Tig@TsE, and Tig@MTsE (Tig concentration: 2 mg/mL) were placed in dialysis bags (MWCO: 3.5 kDa) and dialyzed against 100 mL of buffers at various pH levels (pH 5.0, pH 6.5, and pH 7.4). Dialysis was carried out in a shaker at 37 °C with constant agitation (100 rpm). At predetermined time points (0, 2, 4, 8, 24 and 48 h), 0.3 mL of the external release medium was collected and replaced with an equal volume of the corresponding fresh buffer. The Tig concentration in the release medium was measured by HPLC-MS (Waters 2707, USA), using D9-Tig as the internal standard (IS). The collected samples were diluted tenfold with blank matrix, followed by the addition of 0.3 mL of methanol containing the IS (500 ng/mL). After vortexing for 3 min, the mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C and 2,500 × g for 12 min and the supernatant was subjected to HPLC-MS/MS analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters BEH-C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm). The column temperature was set at 40 °C and the autosampler was maintained at 4 ~ 10 °C. Mobile phase A was water with 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. A gradient elution was used at a flow rate of 0.30 mL/min: 0 ~ 0.5 min, 5% B; 0.5 ~ 2.5 min, 5 ~ 95% B; 2.5 ~ 3.0 min, 95% B; 3.0 ~ 3.1 min, 95 ~ 5% B; 3.1 ~ 4.0 min, 5% B for re-equilibration. The injection volume was 5 μL. Mass spectrometry was performed in positive electrospray ionization (ESI) mode. The capillary voltage was set to 3.0 kV, and collision energies were optimized at 64 eV for Tig and 54 eV for the IS. The cone voltages were set to 30 V for Tig and 32 V for IS. Quantification was carried out using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM), with the protonated precursor ~ product ion transitions of m/z 586.3 ~ 513.2 for Tig and m/z 595.3 ~ 522.2 for IS. Calibration curves were constructed using IS-normalized peak area ratios with linear regression and 1/x2 weighting over 10 ~ 2000 ng/mL, and the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) was 10 ng/mL. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

The in vitro release profiles were further fitted using the Higuchi model (Qt = kHt1/2) to evaluate diffusion-controlled drug release behavior (Qt: the cumulative amount of drug released at time t; kH: the Higuchi release constant; t: the release time; t1/2: the square root of time).

Storage stability

To evaluate the structural stability of the nano-antibiotics at various temperatures, Tig@TsE was incubated at temperatures ranging from 4 °C to 45 °C. The changes in particle size and PDI were monitored. For in vivo stability simulation, Tig@TsE was incubated in PBS containing 10% FBS for 24 h, with particle size and PDI measured daily. Under identical conditions, the particle size and PDI of the formulation without Zn2⁺ were also monitored in parallel as a control group.

Specific binding to LPS

Fluorescent probes (Cy5.5-Ts-ELP or Cy5.5-MTs-ELP) were prepared by labeling Ts-ELP or MTs-ELP with Cy5.5 fluorescent dye at a protein: dye molar ratio of 1:5. The probes (4 μg/mL) were co-incubated with CRKP or KPN at 37 °C for 2 or 6 h. After centrifugation and washing, the bacterial pellets were resuspended in physiological saline. A droplet of the suspension was placed on a glass slide, and the binding of the fluorescent probes to the bacteria was observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope. The average fluorescence intensity of the fusion protein delivery vehicles targeted to CRKP or KPN was analyzed semi-quantitatively using ImageJ software.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)

The fluorescent probes, Cy5.5-Ts-ELP or Cy5.5-MTs-ELP, were co-incubated with CRKP or KPN at 37 °C for 2 or 6 h. The mixtures were then centrifuged, washed, and resuspended in saline. Cy5.5 fluorescence intensity was detected using a flow cytometer (BD FACSAria™ III, USA).

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The MIC values of Tig and the fusion AMPs against KPN and CRKP were determined using the microbroth dilution method in cation-adjusted Mueller-Hinton broth (CAMHB), in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M07-A11 guidelines. Tig, Ts polypeptide, and the fusion proteins Ts-ELP and MTs-ELP were serially diluted by a factor of two to concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 256 μg/mL, then added to a 96-well plate at 100 μL per well. KPN or CRKP cultures in the logarithmic growth phase were centrifuged at 4,000 g for 5 min, and the bacterial concentration was adjusted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL. Then, 100 μL of the bacterial suspension was added to each well, resulting in a final inoculum of 5 × 105 CFU/mL per well. Plates were incubated at 35 ± 2 °C for 16 ~ 20 h, and MIC was defined as the lowest concentration with no visible growth. To assess MMP-9 responsiveness, Ts-ELP or MTs-ELP was incubated with MMP-9 at 0.25 μg/mL, a concentration selected to mimic pulmonary inflammatory conditions [46–48].

In vitro antibacterial activity

Bacterial suspensions of KPN or CRKP were adjusted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL, and 100 μL of each suspension was added to the corresponding wells in a 96-well plate. Tig and the antimicrobial fusion carriers were diluted to their respective MICs, with 100 μL of each dilution added to the wells. The plates were incubated at 37 °C, and the optical density (OD) at 600 nm was measured at various time points (0, 2, 4, 6, 10, 12, 20 and 24 h). Bacterial growth curves were plotted based on these measurements. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Combined susceptibility testing of Ts-ELP and Tig

The checkerboard assay was performed in 96-well microplates following the microbroth dilution format. Briefly, Ts polypeptide was serially twofold diluted along the columns, and Tig was serially twofold diluted along the rows, to generate a checkerboard matrix covering 0 × MIC, 1/8 × MIC, 1/4 × MIC, 1/2 × MIC, 1 × MIC, 2 × MIC, and 4 × MIC for each drug. Each well therefore contained a unique combination of drug A and drug B concentrations. A growth control (no drug) and a sterility control (medium only) were included on each plate, and the bacterial inoculum and incubation conditions were identical to those used for MIC determination. The Fractional Inhibitory Concentration (FIC) index was calculated to evaluate the outcome of the combined susceptibility testing using the formula: FIC = MICA′/MICA + MICB′/MICB. In this formula, MICA′ and MICB′ represent the MICs of drugs A and B when used in combination, while MICA and MICB represent the MICs of drugs A and B when used alone. All checkerboard assays were performed in triplicate wells and repeated in three independent experiments, and FICI values were calculated using the median MIC values from replicate experiments. According to CLSI guidelines, an FIC index of ≤ 0.5 indicates synergy, 0.5 < FIC ≤ 1 indicates partial synergy (additive effect), 1 < FIC ≤ 2 indicates indifference, and FIC > 2 indicates antagonism.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

SEM was used to examine the antibacterial effects of nano-antibiotics on KPN or CRKP by analyzing morphological changes. Bacterial suspensions of KPN or CRKP in the logarithmic growth phase were adjusted to 1 × 109 CFU/mL. These suspensions were co-incubated with Tig, Ts-ELP + MMP-9 (0.25 μg/mL), or MTs-ELP + MMP-9 (0.25 μg/mL) at their respective MICs for 4 h. After incubation, bacteria were collected by centrifugation at 4,000 g for 5 min in 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes. A pre-cooled (4 °C) 2.5% glutaraldehyde fixative was added slowly along the tube wall, and the bacterial cells were fixed for 2 h. Following fixation, the samples were washed three times with PBS (100 mM, pH 7.0) and subjected to gradient dehydration using ethanol solutions of various concentrations (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95% and 100%, with two changes in 100% ethanol). The samples were then vacuum-dried, mounted on SEM stubs, and sputter-coated with platinum prior to imaging. The processed samples were observed using a Hitachi SU-8010 SEM instrument (Japan), and representative micrographs were captured. PBS-treated CRKP or KPN samples served as the positive control.

Efflux pump inhibition assay [49]

Based on MIC testing, 1 μL of a 10 mg/mL solution of the efflux pump inhibitor dihydrochloride (PAβN) was added to each well in the Tig and Tig@TsE + MMP-9 treatment groups, resulting in a final concentration of 50 μg/mL. After an overnight incubation period, the MIC value for each group was determined. Intracellular Tig accumulation was determined by LC-MS/MS. CRKP cells were adjusted to the same density and incubated with free Tig or Tig@TsE, with or without PAβN. After treatment, bacterial cells were immediately harvested by centrifugation at 4 °C and washed three times with ice-cold PBS to remove extracellular and loosely membrane-associated Tig. The resulting bacterial pellets were resuspended in extraction solvent, disrupted by sonication on ice, and centrifuged to pellet insoluble debris. The supernatants were collected and subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis. Intracellular Tig levels were normalized to bacterial counts and expressed as ng per 108 CFU according to the formula (C × V/N) × 108, where C is the measured Tig concentration, V is the extraction volume, and N is the corresponding viable bacterial count.

N-phenyl-1-naphthylamine (NPN) uptake assay [50]

KPN or CRKP cells were cultured overnight and grown to the logarithmic phase. The cells were then harvested and washed two times with ice-cold HEPES buffer (pH 7.2 ~ 7.4, containing 10 mM glucose), and resuspended in the same buffer. The bacterial suspension was diluted to an OD₆₀₀ of approximately 0.5. Subsequently, 100 µL of the suspension was aliquoted into each well of a 96-well plate. The samples were incubated at 37 °C with the respective test agents at their MIC concentrations: Tig, Ts, Ts-ELP + MMP-9 (0.25 µg/mL), Tig@MTsE + MMP-9, or Tig@TsE + MMP-9. To initiate the assay, the NPN stock solution (1 mM) was rapidly added to each well to achieve a final concentration of 10 µM. Fluorescence kinetics were immediately monitored for 45 ~ 60 min to dynamically assess changes in bacterial membrane permeability, using excitation and emission wavelengths of 350 nm and 420 nm, respectively. The following control groups were included: a negative control (bacterial suspension with NPN only) to validate the assay system; a positive control (EDTA, bacterial suspension, and NPN); and a blank control (buffer with NPN) for background fluorescence subtraction. Fluorescence was recorded as raw RFU and background-corrected using the blank control according to the formula: ΔRFU = RFUsample–RFUblank, and the background-corrected RFU (ΔRFU) was used for plotting and analysis. All experimental and control groups were performed in triplicate with three parallel replicates per group.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‑PCR)

CRKP strains in the logarithmic growth phase were treated with each test agent at its respective MIC concentration: Tig, Ts, Ts‑ELP + MMP‑9 (0.25 μg/mL), Tig@MTsE + MMP‑9, or Tig@TsE + MMP‑9. Following overnight incubation at 37 °C, total bacterial RNA was extracted using a commercial RNA extraction kit and reverse‑transcribed into cDNA. RNA purity was assessed by spectrophotometric analysis. Gene expression levels were measured for key CRKP efflux pump components (acrAB-tolC, oqxAB, KpnGH, and kexD), efflux pump regulators (ramA, ramR, and marA), the porin gene ompA, and the tetracycline‑inactivating enzyme gene tetA. Detection of virulence genes and drug resistance genes

The expression levels of virulence genes (iucA, iroB, peg-344, rmpA, rmpA2) and drug resistance genes (blaOXA-48, blaNDM, blaKPC, blaVIM, blaIMP) [52, 53] were quantified using SYBR Green-based qPCR on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Primer sequences and the qPCR protocol are provided in Table S3. The 16S rRNA gene served as the internal reference for relative quantification of the target genes. PCR amplification products of blaKPC were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing. The obtained sequences were analyzed by BLAST against the NCBI GenBank database to confirm sequence identity.

Induction of drug resistance

The concentrations of KPN was adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard (1 × 108 CFU/mL) using MH broth. The bacterial suspension was then diluted 100-fold, and 100 μL (1 × 105 CFU) was added to each well of a 96-well plate. The nano-antibiotics Tig@TsE + MMP-9 or Tig@MTsE + MMP-9 (MMP-9 at 0.25 μg/mL) were adjusted to 1/2 × , 1 × , 2 × , 4 × , and 8 × MIC concentrations and incubated with KPN at 37 °C. After 24 h of incubation (first generation), the absorbance at 600 nm was measured to determine the MIC values. For resistance induction, bacteria were passaged by selecting the bacterial population from the sub-MIC well (1/2 × MIC). The culture from this sub-MIC well was mixed thoroughly to represent the whole population, diluted in fresh MH broth, and used to inoculate a new plate containing the same drug concentration gradient for the next 24 h incubation. This process was repeated for seven generations to determine the final MIC values. A control group treated with Tig alone was included for comparison.

Cellular internalization

HUVECs were seeded in confocal dishes and allowed to adhere overnight. The cells were then incubated with Cy5.5-labeled Tig@MTsE or Tig@TsE fusion protein carriers for 1, 6 or 12 h. Following incubation, the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI. After washing the cells three times with PBS, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Imaging was performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Cell viability inhibition

HUVECs were seeded in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The cells were then incubated with either MTs-ELP or Ts-ELP fusion carriers for 24 or 48 h. Subsequently, 20 μL of enhanced CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by a 2-h incubation. Absorbance at 488 nm was measured using a microplate reader. The inhibitory effect of the fusion proteins on HUVECs viability was calculated, with cells not treated with any protein serving as the positive control.

Establishment of acute pneumonia models infected with KPN, CRKP and S. aureus

KPN and CRKP were cultured to the logarithmic growth phase, and the bacterial suspensions were diluted to a concentration of 1 × 109 CFU/mL in MH broth. Healthy BALB/c mice (18 ± 2 g) were anesthetized with isoflurane and positioned vertically. The glottis was exposed, and a blunt needle was inserted into the trachea. In vivo biodistribution

Cy5.5-labeled nano-antibiotics (Tig@TsE-Cy5.5 or Tig@MTsE-Cy5.5) were prepared as described above. Five hours post-modeling in BALB/c mice, the mice were randomly divided into groups and administered free Cy5.5, Tig@TsE-Cy5.5, or Tig@MTsE-Cy5.5 via tail vein injection. Fluorescence images of Cy5.5 were acquired using a small animal in vivo imaging system at 4 h and 24 h post-injection. Following anesthesia with isoflurane, the mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation. Major organs were harvested, and ex vivo fluorescence images of these organs were captured. A saline-injected group served as the negative control.

Pharmacokinetics of Tig@TsE

A pharmacokinetic study was conducted using healthy male BALB/cJGpt mice (18 ± 2 g, n = 3). Prior to the experiment, the animals were fasted overnight with free access to water. Nine mice were randomly assigned to three groups: free Tig, Tig@MTsE, and Tig@TsE. Each group received an intravenous injection of Tig via the tail vein at a dose of 15 mg/kg. Blood samples (heparin anticoagulant) were collected at predetermined time points (0.08, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 4, 8, 16, 24 and 48 h). The samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to separate the plasma. The plasma was then mixed with methanol at a volume ratio of 1:4, followed by further centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and the Tig concentration was determined by HPLC. Pharmacokinetic parameters were subsequently calculated using the statistical analysis tool DAS 2.0 [15].

In vivo antibacterial experiment

Five hours after the model was established in BALB/c mice, the animals were randomly assigned to different groups using a computer-generated randomization scheme, with body-weight balance considered where appropriate, and were administered Tig, Ts, Ts-ELP, and the nano-antibiotics Tig@TsE or Tig@MTsE via tail vein injection, at equivalent concentrations of Tig or Ts-ELP. Treatments were given twice daily (every 12 h), once in the morning and once in the evening, for a total of 5 days. Body weight was measured daily, and survival time was recorded. Upon the death of the first mouse in any group, three mice were randomly selected from each group. The alveolar region was lavaged with pre-cooled PBS, and the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected. The BALF was plated on blood agar plates to determine the lung bacterial load. Blood was collected via retro-orbital bleeding (using heparin as an anticoagulant) for a complete blood count and to quantify the blood bacterial load. Plasma was collected from whole blood, and the BALF supernatant was collected after centrifugation. CRP levels in the supernatant were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The BALF cell pellet was resuspended in PBS and stained with FITC-conjugated CD11b monoclonal antibody (M1/70) and APC-conjugated Ly-6G monoclonal antibody (RB6-8C5). Neutrophil counts among the total cells in the BALF were measured by flow cytometry. FACS data were analyzed using FlowJo software (n = 3). Lung lobes were isolated, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and pathological examination to assess lung tissue injury. Neutrophil infiltration in the lung tissues was evaluated by immunohistochemistry (IHC) using an anti-myeloperoxidase (MPO) antibody. Investigators responsible for histopathological evaluation and data analysis were blinded to group allocation whenever feasible. The remaining mice continued to receive their assigned treatments, with daily monitoring of body weight and survival (n = 5).

In vivo safety assessment

Healthy mice were administered Tig, Ts, Ts-ELP, or nano-antibiotics Tig@TsE or Tig@MTsE (at equivalent concentrations of Tig or Ts-ELP) once daily for 7 consecutive days. After anesthesia with isoflurane, blood was collected via enucleation of the eyeball for the determination of liver function markers (alanine aminotransferase [ALT] and aspartate aminotransferase [AST]) and renal function markers (creatinine [Cre] and blood urea nitrogen [BUN]). The heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with H&E. Tissue damage was assessed by microscopic examination, and images were acquired and analyzed.

Statistical analysis

All values are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between the two groups were made using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Survival curves were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 software (GraphPad Software, Inc.). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The self-assembled nano-antibiotic Tig@TsE exhibits temperature-sensitive and pH-sensitive drug release properties

High-purity Ts peptide-functionalized ELP carriers (Ts-ELP, Fig. 2B) and non-targeting mutant Ts peptide-functionalized ELP carriers (MTs-ELP, Fig. 2C) were produced through a series of procedures, including gene recombination, prokaryotic expression, protease digestion, and chromatographic purification (Fig. 2A). Using this workflow, the purified yield was 101 ± 2.74 mg/L culture prior to thrombin cleavage and 56.74 ± 4.52 mg/L culture after cleavage. The resulting preparations exhibited high purity, as confirmed by SDS-PAGE densitometric analysis (ImageJ), with purities of 97.52 ± 0.26% for Ts-ELP and 98.25 ± 0.36% for MTs-ELP. The endotoxin level of the purified protein was below 0.1 EU/mg. The molecular weights of both constructs matched the theoretical values predicted by the Expasy online tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) (Table S4). The Ts polypeptide was synthesized via solid-phase peptide synthesis and purified by HPLC (Fig. S2A). Its molecular weight was determined as 2.43 kDa using ESI-MS (Fig. S2B). To assess the temperature sensitivity of the Ts-ELP carrier, the turbidity of Ts-ELP solutions at various concentrations was measured at different temperatures. Results indicated that as the temperature increased, the absorbance at 350 nm (A350 nm) of Ts-ELP solutions in the concentration range of 0.1 mg/mL to 0.8 mg/mL increased progressively (Fig. 2D), demonstrating that Ts-ELP maintains favorable temperature responsiveness even at low concentrations. When the concentration reached or exceeded 0.4 mg/mL, the Tt of Ts-ELP was ≥ 39 °C, above the physiological temperature of 37 °C. The phase transition curve of MTs-ELP was then fitted using the same nonlinear regression model (Fig. S3A), and comparison of the fitted Tt values revealed no significant difference between Ts-ELP and MTs-ELP, suggesting that Ts modification did not alter the thermoresponsive behavior of the ELP carrier (Fig. S3B). Solutions containing 0.4 mg/mL of Ts-ELP and MTs-ELP were clear and transparent at 4 °C. Upon gradual heating to 40 °C, aggregation occurred, and when the temperature was subsequently reduced back to 4 °C, the solutions returned to a clear, dissolved state. MTs-ELP protein solutions exhibited the same reversible phase transition behavior (Fig. S4A), confirming that Ts-ELP retains excellent temperature sensitivity and reversible phase transition characteristics following Ts modification.

CD spectroscopy showed minor negative and positive bands at 203 and 209 nm, respectively, and a prominent negative band at 226 nm. The intensity of these bands increased with rising temperature (Fig. 2E). The predominant secondary structure of the protein transitioned from an α-helix at low temperatures (t < Tt) to a β-sheet (including both antiparallel and parallel conformations) at higher temperatures (t > Tt) (Fig. 2F). This transition is a key step in protein aggregation or amyloid fibril formation. Additionally, the CD spectra of MTs-ELP and Ts-ELP were consistent at RT, indicating that the non-targeting mutation in Ts did not affect the secondary structure of Ts-ELP (Fig. S4B).

TEM observation of 0.5 mg/mL solutions of Ts-ELP, MTs-ELP, or their respective mixtures with Tig, after incubation at 50 °C, revealed the formation of uniformly shaped, spherical particles. The particle sizes of Ts-ELP and MTs-ELP were both approximately 20 nm to 50 nm (Fig. 2G (a) and (c)). However, after incubation with Tig, the particle sizes increased significantly to approximately 50 nm to 150 nm (Fig. 2G (b) and (d)). These results suggest that Ts-ELP and MTs-ELP self-assembled with Tig during incubation to form the nano-antibiotics Tig@TsE and Tig@MTsE, respectively, encapsulating Tig. The self-assembly of these nano-antibiotics was unaffected by the disulfide bond mutation in the Ts peptide. Successful Tig loading was confirmed by FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 2H). The drug encapsulation efficiency and loading capacity of Tig@TsE, as measured by a microplate reader, were determined to be 65.91 ± 5.14% and 16 ± 3.45%, respectively.

Incubation of Tig@TsE in environments with varying pH values led to a decrease in particle size as the pH decreased (Fig. 2I and Table S5), accompanied by a gradual increase in surface zeta potential (Fig. 2J). This trend aligned with the predicted values for Ts-ELP and MTs-ELP (Fig. S4C). The cumulative release of Tig under different pH conditions was investigated using the dialysis bag method to confirm whether the nano-delivery system exhibited pH-responsive drug release characteristics. The results showed that free Tig was rapidly released at pH 7.4, with 80% of the drug released within 4 h, while Tig@TsE released Tig more slowly, with only 20% cumulative release over 48 h. As the buffer pH decreased, the release rate of the Tig@TsE group increased significantly. At pH 6.5, 60% of Tig was released within 8 h, and the fastest release occurred at pH 5.0, with 80% of Tig released within 8 h (Fig. 2K). This is consistent with the observed decrease in particle size under acidic conditions, suggesting that Tig@TsE exhibits pH-responsive drug release properties. To further characterize the release kinetics, the release profiles were fitted using the Higuchi mode. The fitting showed good agreement at pH 7.4 (R2 = 0.9256) and moderate agreement at pH 6.5 (R2 = 0.8738), indicating that diffusion contributes substantially to Tig release from the nanocarrier under near-physiological conditions. In contrast, weaker fitting was observed for free Tig (R2 = 0.6276) and under strongly acidic conditions at pH 5.0 (R2 = 0.7677), suggesting that additional mechanisms, such as carrier destabilization or an initial burst release, may also contribute to the release process (Fig. S4D ~ G).

The protonation of histidine residues, which are basic amino acids abundant in ELP, is the key factor underlying the pH sensitivity of the nano-antibiotic Tig@TsE (Fig. S4H). Below the protonation equilibrium constant (pKa ≈ 6.0), such as at pH 5.0, the strongly nucleophilic imidazole group (-NH =) of histidine becomes protonated, resulting in a positively charged histidine residue (-NH+ =), which increases the surface potential of Ts-ELP. Upon depolymerization of Tig@TsE and the release of Tig, the measured particle size in solution (approximately 30 nm) is similar to that of the Ts-ELP monomer, as the Tig particle is too small to be reliably detected. Concurrently, the surface potential of Tig@TsE increases significantly (to 16.080 ± 3.117 mV), and this high positive charge facilitates binding to negatively charged cell membranes, enhancing cellular internalization. In contrast, at a pH greater than the pKa value (e.g., pH 6.5), the imidazole group (-NH =) becomes deprotonated, rendering histidine electrically neutral. This reduces electrostatic repulsion between ELP molecules and enhances hydrophobic interactions, promoting the encapsulation of the hydrophobic Tig molecule via hydrophobic association. Consequently, under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), the self-assembled nano-antibiotic maintains a relatively stable nanostructure, minimizing the leakage of the loaded Tig. In summary, modulation of environmental pH regulates the surface charge of Ts-ELP, influencing intermolecular interactions among Ts-ELP monomers and ultimately controlling the loading and release of Tig.

When Tig@TsE was incubated in PBS containing 10% FBS, the particle size remained generally stable, with only minor fluctuations in PDI during the observation period (Fig. 2L), indicating relatively good colloidal stability under serum-containing conditions. In contrast, the Zn2⁺-free formulation exhibited markedly reduced stability, with a sharp increase in particle size accompanied by a progressive rise in PDI after incubation in PBS containing 10% FBS (Fig. S4I), suggesting aggregation and structural destabilization in the absence of Zn2⁺. As the temperature increased from 4 °C to 45 °C, a sharp increase in the particle size of Tig@TsE was observed around 40 °C (Fig. 2M), consistent with the previously determined Tt. Together, these results indicate that Tig@TsE maintains relatively good structural stability under physiologically relevant conditions, and that Zn2⁺ contributes to preserving the integrity of the histidine-containing Ts-ELP nanoassemblies.

To assess whether Ts-ELP can specifically target bacterial cell membranes, fluorescent probes labeled with Cy5.5 (Cy5.5-MTs-ELP or Cy5.5-Ts-ELP) were incubated with either KPN or a clinically isolated CRKP strain for 2 or 6 h. Extending the incubation time to 6 h further increased the fluorescence intensity in all groups; however, the significant difference between the Cy5.5-Ts-ELP and Cy5.5-MTs-ELP groups remained (p < 0.001). A similar trend in fluorescence intensity was observed in the CRKP group.

The self-assembled nano-antibiotic Tig@TsE exhibits significantly enhanced in vitro antibacterial activity

The in vitro antibacterial activity of Tig@TsE against KPN and CRKP was assessed using the checkerboard method. The MIC values of Tig against KPN and CRKP were 0.5 μg/mL and 4 μg/mL, respectively, indicating that CRKP exhibits reduced sensitivity to Tig. For the Ts peptide, the MIC values were 4 μg/mL against KPN and 16 μg/mL against CRKP. Ts-ELP showed a decrease in MIC in the presence of MMP-9, suggesting its responsiveness to MMP-9 and a corresponding enhancement of antimicrobial activity. In the presence of MMP-9, Tig@TsE displayed the lowest MIC against KPN (0.25 μg/mL), which was lower than those of Tig@MTsE (0.5 μg/mL) and either Tig or Ts alone, indicating a synergistic interaction between Tig and Ts (Fig. 4A). Against CRKP, Tig@TsE also achieved the lowest MIC (2 μg/mL), outperforming Tig@MTsE (4 μg/mL) and monotherapy. These results demonstrate that the ELP-based targeted nanodelivery system potentiates the antibacterial effect of Tig against CRKP (Fig. 4B). Growth curve analysis over 24 h demonstrated that the combination of Tig@TsE with MMP-9 exhibited the strongest antibacterial activity against both KPN and CRKP. This was followed by Tig@MTsE supplemented with MMP-9, which showed modestly higher efficacy than Tig alone. Furthermore, Ts-ELP in the presence of MMP-9 resulted in superior antibacterial performance compared to Ts-ELP alone, further confirming the MMP-9-responsive behavior of the Ts-ELP system (Fig. 4C and D).

Integrating the checkerboard method with antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed that Ts and Tig exhibited a Fractional Inhibitory Concentration Index (FICI) of 0.5 against both KPN and CRKP, indicating a synergistic antibacterial effect (Table S6). SEM analysis further revealed that bacterial cells treated with Tig@TsE in the presence of MMP-9 displayed the most severe membrane damage (Fig. 4E). Collectively, these findings indicate that the precisely engineered Ts-ELP undergoes self-assembly to encapsulate Tig, forming the stable nano-antibiotic Tig@TsE. This nanomedicine system responds to MMP-9 by fully exposing the Ts peptide, thereby enhancing the antimicrobial efficacy of Tig.

The effect of Tig@TsE on membrane permeability was assessed using an NPN uptake assay. Compared to Tig alone, treatment with Ts or Ts-modified agents significantly increased CRKP membrane permeability within 10 min. Tig@TsE showed the strongest enhancement (p < 0.001), with no significant difference observed between the Tig@TsE and free Ts groups (p > 0.05). Both approaches achieved levels comparable to the EDTA positive control, followed by the Ts-ELP group. In contrast, the non-targeted Tig@MTsE group showed no improvement over Tig monotherapy (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4G). A similar trend was observed in the KPN group (Fig. 4F). Together, these results indicate that Tig@TsE rapidly disrupts the CRKP cell membrane, facilitating intracellular accumulation of Tig and promoting effective killing of resistant bacteria. Adding the efflux pump inhibitor PAβN (50 μg/mL) decreased the MIC of Tig against CRKP by half, reaching the same level as Tig@TsE (2 μg/mL). In contrast, the MIC of Tig@TsE remained unaffected by PAβN (Fig. 4H). In line with this, LC-MS/MS quantification showed that intracellular Tig levels were significantly increased by PAβN in the free Tig group, whereas no significant difference was observed between the Tig@TsE and Tig@TsE + PAβN groups (Fig. 4I). These findings suggest that efflux pumps contribute to Tig resistance and that this resistance can be reversed by PAβN. Notably, Tig@TsE appears to circumvent efflux-mediated resistance through Ts-induced membrane disruption, which enables rapid and substantial intracellular uptake of Tig.

qRT-PCR analysis was performed to evaluate the transcriptional responses of resistance-related genes in CRKP following different treatments. The amplification specificity of all primer pairs was verified by melting-curve analysis, which showed single peaks for each target gene (Fig. S5A ~ N). As shown in Fig. 4J, treatment with either Tig or Tig@TsE significantly up‑regulated the expression of the CRKP efflux pump genes KpnG, KpnH, kexD, ramA, ramR, marA, ompA and tetA compared with the control group, with a more pronounced effect observed in the Tig@TsE group. In contrast, Ts‑ELP vector alone did not induce such changes, indicating that the up‑regulation was primarily driven by Tig. Together with the NPN uptake data, these findings suggest that Tig@TsE enhances membrane permeability in CRKP, thereby promoting intracellular accumulation of Tig and subsequently triggering elevated expression of efflux pump genes.

When KPN was subjected to continuous sub‑MIC (1/2 × MIC) drug exposure over eight passages, the MIC of Tig monotherapy increased 16‑fold (from 0.5 μg/mL to 8 μg/mL), whereas the MIC of Tig@TsE rose only eightfold (from 0.5 μg/mL to 4 μg/mL) (Fig. 4K), indicating a lower propensity to induce resistance. To further investigate the underlying mechanism, we analyzed the ramR-ramA efflux regulatory axis in the induced strains. Sanger sequencing of the ramR locus showed that the Tig-induced strain exhibited more pronounced sequence alterations than the nano-treated groups (Fig. S5O). Consistently, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that ramA expression was highest in the Tig group (Fig. S5P). Because ramR is an upstream repressor of ramA, these findings suggest that free Tig imposed stronger selective pressure on the ramR-ramA regulatory pathway, leading to greater derepression of ramA and enhanced efflux-associated adaptive responses. By contrast, although efflux-related genes were also upregulated after Tig@TsE treatment, the lower degree of ramR sequence alteration and weaker ramA induction in the nano-treated groups were accompanied by a more limited increase in MIC. Together with the membrane-disruptive activity of Ts and the increased intracellular accumulation of Tig, these results suggest that Tig@TsE may reduce the tendency to establish stable high-level efflux-associated resistance during serial passage.

Ts-functionalized Tig@TsE exhibits targeted accumulation at acute pulmonary infection sites in vivo

An acute pneumonia mouse model was established by intratracheal instillation of KPN or CRKP bacteria into the lungs of healthy mice. At 5 h post-infection, Cy5.5-labeled nano-antibiotics were administered via tail vein injection to assess their targeted delivery efficiency in vivo (Fig. 5A). Small animal in vivo imaging results showed that the targeted nano-antibiotics Tig@TsE-Cy5.5 exhibited similar distribution patterns in both KPN and CRKP groups, with targeted accumulation in the lungs observed as early as 4 h post-injection. In contrast, no pulmonary accumulation was detected in mice treated with free Cy5.5 or Tig@MTsE-Cy5.5 (Fig. 5B and D). Ex vivo tissue imaging revealed that the fluorescence signal in the lungs of mice treated with Tig@TsE-Cy5.5 was significantly stronger than in those treated with free Cy5.5 or Tig@MTsE-Cy5.5. Furthermore, the fluorescence intensity at 24 h was notably higher than at 4 h (Fig. 5C and E). In the KPN model, the quantitative fluorescence signal of Tig@TsE-Cy5.5 in the lungs was 2.52-fold higher than Tig@MTsE-Cy5.5 at 4 h (Fig. 5F) and 4.13-fold higher at 24 h (Fig. 5G). Similarly, in the CRKP group, the pulmonary fluorescence signal of Tig@TsE-Cy5.5 was 2.58-fold (Fig. 5H) and 4.35-fold (Fig. 5I) greater than that of Tig@MTsE-Cy5.5 at 4 and 24 h, respectively. By contrast, in the S. aureus pneumonia model, no obvious lung-targeted accumulation was observed among the different treatment groups, and the pulmonary fluorescence signals did not show the marked enrichment pattern seen in the KPN and CRKP models (Fig. S6A ~ F). However, in all mouse infection models, post-administration distribution was observed in both the liver and kidneys, suggesting that the formulation may undergo metabolism and clearance through the hepatic and renal systems.

Furthermore, the clearance (CL) of Tig in the nano-antibiotic formulations was significantly reduced compared to free Tig. The CL values of Tig@MTsE and Tig@TsE were reduced by 3.96-fold and 4.28-fold, respectively (Table S7). These results suggest that nano-antibiotics enhance the circulation time of Tig in the body, thereby improving its bioavailability.

Acute pneumonia activates pulmonary endothelial and epithelial cells through various inflammatory mediators, cytokine effects, and endothelial cell injury, disrupting endothelial barrier integrity and ultimately increasing vascular permeability [54]. This enhanced permeability allows nanomedicines to accumulate at infection sites in the lungs via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. Based on previously observed serum stability results, it is hypothesized that the nano-antibiotic Tig@TsE developed in this study can be effectively distributed to lung infection sites through systemic circulation following tail vein injection and subsequently internalized by vascular endothelial cells for transvascular transport. HUVECs were incubated with either Cy5.5-labeled Tig@TsE or Tig@MTsE for specified durations. Both nano-antibiotics were rapidly and efficiently internalized by the cells, with intracellular Cy5.5 fluorescence intensity increasing over time (Fig. S7A). After incubating HUVECs with Ts-ELP or MTs-ELP for 24 h (Fig. S7B) or 48 h (Fig. S7C), cell viability remained above 90% across the tested drug concentration range (0 ~ 200 μg/mL), compared to the PBS-treated control group. This indicates that the engineered ELP carrier Ts-ELP has excellent biocompatibility. Furthermore, when 2% RBCs were incubated with the same drug concentrations for 4 h, the hemolysis fraction was significantly lower than that in the positive control group. Notably, encapsulating Tig within Ts-ELP effectively reduced its hemolytic activity (Fig. S7D). Collectively, these results suggest that the Tig TDDS, Tig@TsE, can be efficiently internalized by vascular endothelial cells and demonstrates favorable safety profiles for potential in vivo applications.

Tig@TsE exhibits enhanced therapeutic efficacy against CRKP infection and favorable biosafety in vivo

According to the predetermined experimental protocol, a murine pneumonia model infected with either KPN or CRKP was established, followed by treatment via tail vein injection (Fig. 6A). Following the administration of various therapeutic agents to mice infected with KPN or CRKP, survival time was notably extended in the Tig@TsE-treated group (Fig. 6B and C). The BALF and whole blood were collected 24 h after treatment to assess neutrophil and White blood cell (WBC) counts, as well as CRP concentrations, in order to evaluate inflammatory responses. Non-modeled normal mice served as the negative control group. The Tig@TsE-treated group exhibited the lowest neutrophil (Fig. 6D and E) and total WBC (Fig. 6F and G) counts, most closely resembling the levels observed in the non-modeled control group. Plasma was separated by centrifugation of whole blood, and CRP concentrations in both plasma and BALF were measured using ELISA. CRP, an acute-phase protein synthesized by the liver, is significantly elevated during bacterial infections, correlating with infection severity and serving as a key diagnostic marker. The results showed that the Tig@TsE treatment group had the lowest CRP levels in both plasma and BALF, with significant differences (p < 0.05) compared to the Tig and Tig@MTsE groups. CRP levels in the Tig@TsE group were only slightly higher than those in the non-modeled normal mice, with no significant difference between the two groups (Fig. 6H ~ K). Consistent with these findings, Tig@TsE treatment also significantly reduced blood IL-6 and TNF-α levels compared with the infected control group (Fig. 6L ~ O). These results suggest that the severity of infection in mice was substantially reduced following Tig@TsE treatment, showing a significantly superior therapeutic effect compared to the Tig or Tig@MTsE treatment groups.

Neutrophils were labeled with an anti-Ly-6G antibody conjugated to APC (APC-Ly-6G) and an anti-CD11b antibody conjugated to FITC (FITC-CD11b), and the proportion of neutrophils in the total BALF cells was analyzed by flow cytometry to evaluate pulmonary neutrophil infiltration post-infection. Statistical analysis indicated that the reduction in neutrophils was greater in the CRKP group treated with Tig@TsE (4.83-fold) compared to the KPN group (3.92-fold) (Fig. 6Q and R). These results suggest that Ts-modified Tig@TsE significantly downregulates inflammatory indicators, particularly against CRKP, highlighting its potent bactericidal activity against CRKP.

The extent of bloodstream infection and pulmonary bacterial load following drug treatment was assessed through plate colony counting. After treatment, the BALF from the KPN and CRKP groups was diluted 106- and 109-fold, respectively, and evenly spread on blood agar plates before incubation overnight at 37 °C. In both models, the Tig@TsE treatment group exhibited the lowest colony count in the BALF (Fig. 7A), with bacterial load significantly reduced compared to other treatment groups (Fig. 7B and C). A 20 μL aliquot of whole blood was aspirated, spread on blood agar plates, and cultured overnight. In the KPN model, only the saline group showed colony growth; no colonies were detected in the other groups. In the CRKP model, only the Tig@TsE treatment group yielded a small number of colonies, followed by the Tig@MTsE group, with colony counts significantly lower than those in the other treatment groups (Fig. S8). In both colony culture experiments, the CRKP saline group exhibited a markedly higher colony count than the KPN saline group, indicating that the infection severity caused by the clinically isolated CRKP strain was significantly greater than that caused by the KPN reference strain. Further analysis of the antimicrobial susceptibility profile of the CRKP isolate showed that it belonged to the extensively drug-resistant (XDR) category (Table S8). Subsequently, qPCR analysis was performed to characterize virulence and resistance determinants in the CRKP strain. The amplification specificity of all primer pairs was confirmed by melting-curve analysis, which showed single peaks for each target gene (Fig. S9A ~ J). As shown in Fig. S9K, qPCR analysis of virulence genes revealed that the CRKP strain carries the high-virulence genes iucA and rmpA/rmpA2, suggesting enhanced invasive potential. In addition, qPCR results for drug resistance genes showed that the CRKP strain carried the KPC-type carbapenemase resistance gene (Fig. S9L). Sanger sequencing of blaKPC further confirmed this finding (Fig. S10). These drug resistance and virulence factors collectively contribute to the more severe infections and clinical treatment challenges associated with such strains.

Histopathological analysis of lung tissue sections stained with H&E revealed substantial inflammatory cell infiltration, edema, congestion, and thickened alveolar walls in the saline-injected group (Fig. 7D). The Tig, Ts-ELP, and Tig@MTsE treatment groups exhibited varying degrees of congestion and alveolar wall thickening. These in vivo findings demonstrate that intravenous administration of Tig@TsE in mouse models of acute pneumonia induced by KPN or CRKP infection significantly reduces bacterial load in the lungs, decreases the risk of bloodstream infection, lowers systemic inflammatory cell levels, and reduces immune cell infiltration at infection sites. Consequently, Tig@TsE alleviates CRKP-mediated lethality in pulmonary infection and exhibits superior antibacterial efficacy compared to Tig or Tig@MTsE.

Comprehensive biocompatibility and toxicological evaluation of Tig@TsE nano-antibiotic

To further assess the in vivo safety profile of the drug, a daily tail vein injection of the drug (Tig dose: 15 mg/mL) was administered to healthy mice for seven consecutive days. The mice were then sacrificed, and blood and organ samples were collected (Fig. 8A). Histopathological analysis of organ tissues via H&E staining revealed significant fatty hydropic degeneration in the liver of the Tig-treated group, while no notable damage was observed in other tissues (Fig. 8B). Biochemical parameters measured in the collected plasma showed a slight elevation in ALT and AST levels in the Tig group. In contrast, renal function markers, including Cre and BUN, were consistent with those of the control group, indicating no significant renal impairment (Fig. 8C ~ F).

Discussion and conclusion

Taken together, we successfully developed and characterized a bioengineered pH- and enzyme-responsive nano-TDDS Tig@TsE, which is based on ELP and Ts polypeptide modification. The system exhibits LPS-targeting and synergistic antibacterial activity, and is intended for the targeted delivery of encapsulated Tig to enhance its therapeutic efficacy against acute pneumonia induced by CRKP infection.

Against the backdrop of the continuously increasing global burden of AMR, improving the therapeutic efficacy of existing antibiotics remains a critical strategy alongside the development of novel antibacterial agents. Projected data indicate that AMR could directly cause 1.91 million (1.56 ~ 2.26 million) deaths worldwide by 2050, contributing to a total of 8.22 million (6.85 ~ 9.65 million) deaths globally [55, 56]. Developing new antimicrobial agents targeting GNB could prevent up to 11.1 million deaths associated with bacterial drug resistance [57, 58]. For instance, the next-generation tetracycline derivative omadacycline, owing to its favorable distribution in lung tissue and its ability to overcome certain classical tetracycline resistance mechanisms, shows promising application prospects in severe pneumonia and co-infections involving MDR-GNB [59]. At the delivery-system level, conjugation of AMPs with nanoparticles to form polymeric nanohybrids [60], or the combination of cationic polymers based on poly (β-amino esters) with antibiotics to create nanoparticle-antibiotic complexes [61], has demonstrated potent antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects.

The Ts-ELP delivery system established in this research is based on biodegradable, human-derived ELPs and is therefore anticipated to possess favorable biocompatibility and biosafety. Previous studies have demonstrated that incorporation of histidine residues into ELP repeat sequences confers pronounced pH responsiveness on the resulting assemblies [45, 62, 63]. In the present study, Tig release was most rapid under pH 5.0 conditions, further supporting the underlying design rationale. It is therefore reasonable to infer that, within the acidic microenvironment associated with infection, protonation of histidine side chains may attenuate intermolecular interactions within the Ts-ELP carrier, thereby promoting nanostructure disassembly and localized release of Tig.

In addition, Zn2⁺ has been reported to regulate ELP self-assembly behavior and enhance nanoparticle structural stability through coordination with histidine residues [45, 62, 64]. In our study, the incorporation of Zn2⁺ was associated with greater stability of the hydrodynamic particle size, suggesting that Zn2⁺ contributes to the maintenance of structural integrity within the nanoassembly. Meanwhile, Tig@TsE exhibited an EE of 65.91 ± 5.14% and a DL of 16.00 ± 3.45%, both of which fall within the ranges commonly reported for comparable self-assembling polymeric or ELP-based delivery systems [65–67]. These findings indicate that the introduction of pH-responsive features and Zn2⁺-mediated stabilization did not compromise the drug-loading performance of the system.

In terms of therapeutic efficacy, Tig@TsE demonstrated favorable anti-infective potential both in vitro and in vivo. Although Tig@TsE treatment induced upregulation of efflux pump-related genes, the increase in MIC after long-term induction remained lower than that observed in the free Tig group. In conjunction with the NPN uptake results, this phenomenon may be attributable to Ts-mediated enhancement of bacterial membrane permeability, which facilitates intracellular penetration of Tig and thereby partially offsets the resistance effect associated with efflux pump activation. In contrast, free Tig may be more likely to exert sustained selective pressure on the ramR-ramA regulatory axis, thereby promoting adaptive evolution of efflux-related genes [68–70]. With respect to targeted delivery, the interaction between Ts and LPS in GNB provides a basis for preferential accumulation at sites of infection [71]. Our results showed that, compared with the other groups, Tig@TsE exhibited targeted accumulation in lung tissues infected with KPN or CRKP, whereas no such phenomenon was observed in the S. aureus infection model. These findings suggest that the in vivo enrichment of Tig@TsE was not solely attributable to inflammation-associated nonspecific retention, but was more likely dependent on the interaction between Ts and LPS. At the same time, the effective exposure of Tig@TsE appears to be concentrated at the local infection site rather than broadly distributed across regions occupied by commensal flora, which may be related to the relatively low overall burden and local density of commensal GNB in vivo, as well as the limited accessibility of LPS under physiological conditions. Nevertheless, the potential impact of Tig@TsE on the host commensal microbiota remains to be evaluated in future studies. Further in vivo antibacterial experiments showed that Tig@TsE not only reduced bacterial burdens in infected lungs and systemic circulation, but also alleviated pneumonia-associated inflammatory injury, improved survival in infected mice, and exhibited a favorable in vivo safety profile under the present experimental conditions.

Overall, Tig@TsE represents a multifunctional platform that integrates membrane-targeted recognition and interaction, nanocarrier-mediated delivery, and environment-responsive release, thereby offering a new strategy for the precise treatment of drug-resistant GNB infections. Although several limitations remain, including the lack of LC-MS/MS-based quantification of in vivo biodistribution, the absence of a histidine-deficient control carrier, and the lack of protein-level and more in-depth functional validation of the efflux-related mechanism, the present findings nevertheless indicate that this bioengineered nano-antibiotic platform holds considerable promise for improving the delivery efficiency of Tig and enhancing its therapeutic efficacy against CRKP- and KPN-associated acute pulmonary infections.
