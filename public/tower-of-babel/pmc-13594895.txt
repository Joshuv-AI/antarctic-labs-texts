High-resolution spatial transcriptomics of irradiated submandibular glands treated with fortified fibrin hydrogels
Authors: Kihoon Nam, Mark Maienschein-Cline, Harim Tavares dos Santos, Frank M Maslow, Rodrigo A Osorio, Stelios T Andreadis, Olga J Baker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPrevious studies showed that fortified fibrin hydrogel (Ep-FH), generated by conjugating laminin-1 peptides (A99 and YIGSR) and growth factors (FGF7 and FGF10) to fibrin hydrogels, improves epithelial organization and secretory function in irradiated mouse submandibular glands (SMG). To define the cell type-specific mechanisms underlying these effects, we generated high-resolution spatial transcriptomic profiles using the Xenium Prime Mouse Pan Tissue and Pathways Panel (5,000 genes). Treatment with Ep-FH partially reactivated secretory- and polarity-related genes, shifted stromal and immune transcriptional programs toward the non-irradiated state, and reduced profibrotic signaling. Integrative clustering further showed increased similarity of acinar, stromal, immune, and myoepithelial populations to non-irradiated controls, whereas ductal, endothelial, and glial cells showed limited responses. These findings indicate that Ep-FH partially restores transcriptional programs in selected cell populations, suggesting that further optimization may improve regeneration throughout the irradiated SMG.Keywords: ionizing radiation, fibrin hydrogel, salivary gland repair, epithelial regeneration, cellular reprogramming, single-cell spatial mapping, spatial gene expression analysis

Introduction

Radiation treatment (RT) for head and neck cancer significantly damages the salivary glands (SG), leading in turn to hyposalivation and consequent xerostomia.1,2,3,4 Specifically, RT-induced SG damage is dose-dependent, with higher doses causing more severe and often irreversible injury to the tissue.5,6,7,8 Ionizing radiation (IR) primarily affects acinar cells, which often undergo a rapid, dose-dependent reduction in cell numbers due to uncontrolled apoptosis.9,10,11 Likewise, ductal cells are depleted but to a lesser extent and with morphological integrity often preserved, though some ectasia and senescence occur.12 Additional depleted populations include stem/progenitor cells in the SG ducts (e.g., SOX2+ or c-Kit+),13 a loss that markedly diminishes the gland’s regenerative capacity. Furthermore, IR causes dysregulation of immune (e.g., macrophages and natural killer [NK] cells)10 and stromal (e.g., fibroblasts and mesenchymal)14 cells, both of which display dysregulated cytokine/chemokine production leading to inflammation and fibrosis, respectively.11,15 Moreover, IR causes destruction in blood vessels and nerves, thereby affecting irrigation and innervation of the SG.16,17,18 Together, these events indicate that IR significantly impairs multiple SG cell populations, each in a particular manner, and collectively leads to loss of secretory function (i.e., hyposalivation), which contributes to oral infections and significantly decreases the quality of life for head and neck irradiated patients.19,20,21,22,23,24

Current treatments for hyposalivation include the use of saliva substitutes, which only provide temporary relief25,26,27 and secretory agonists that lead to significant side effects,26,28,29,30,31,32 thus presenting an urgent need to devise novel strategies for the restoration of irradiated SG function that might lead to more permanent and clinically viable treatment options. To that end, our previous studies developed a novel scaffold by chemically conjugating laminin-1 peptides (A99 and YIGSR) and growth factors (FGF7 and FGF10) to fibrin hydrogels (FH).33,34 The resulting fortified FH (Ep-FH), when applied via a single intraglandular injection to irradiated mouse submandibular gland (SMG), was found to enhance epithelial tissue organization while promoting secretory function after 60 days of treatment.33 Together, these results suggest a remarkable potential for translational application; however, it is necessary to better understand how Ep-FH affects distinct cell populations within SMG before applying it in a clinical setting. Therefore, the goal of this study was to characterize how treatment with Ep-FH alters irradiated mouse SMG cell populations by generating high-resolution gene expression data for a targeted panel of cells using the Xenium Prime Mouse Pan Tissue and Pathways Panel (5,000 genes), which allows for visualization of RNA fluorescence images after one cycle of decoding35 and in so doing reveals the detailed structure of the SMG tissue with an unusually high degree of resolution.

Results

Cell populations in mouse SMG treated with and without Ep-FH were identified

Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) clustering of Xenium spatial transcriptomic data at a resolution of 0.5 identified 28 transcriptionally distinct cell populations within the mouse SMG (Figure S2A). Major clusters included acinar (proacinar, serous, and seromucous), ductal (Ascl3+, intercalated, and basal), endothelial, myoepithelial, stromal, immune (macrophages and NK cells), glial, and smooth muscle cells (Figures S2B and S3). Cluster identities were assigned based on canonical marker genes provided in the Xenium Mouse Pan Tissue and Pathways Panel36,37,38 (Figure S1). This clustering analysis underscores the cellular complexity and organization of the glandular microenvironment, establishing a transcriptional reference map for subsequent comparisons of IR- and IR + Ep-FH-treated groups. To allow interpretability of the results across main cell types, we defined and reclassified SG cells along broad categories, including acinar, ductal, endothelial, glial, macrophages, as well as myoepithelial, NK, and stromal cells (Figure 1A). This UMAP topology reflects transcriptional proximity among SMG lineages, including acinar and ductal populations (epithelial domain), while stromal and endothelial cells (mesenchymal domain) occupy intermediate positions linking the epithelial and mesenchymal domains. In contrast, macrophages, NK, and glial cells form isolated regions, indicating immune and neural transcriptional identities distinct from the earlier discussed groups. Collectively, this organizational pattern highlights the cellular heterogeneity of the SMG and establishes a baseline transcriptional framework for comparison of these conditions in non-IR healthy control to IR- and IR + Ep-FH-treated groups. Cluster identities were validated by the coordinated expression of canonical marker genes across SMG cell types (Figures 1B and S1). Particularly, acinar clusters expressed specific markers, including Aqp5, Bhlha15, and Slc12a2, while ductal cells also expressed specific markers for this cell type, including Cftr, Clcnkb, Scnn1g, Scrib, and Stxbp5l. Moreover, endothelial populations expressed Egfl7, Esam, Flt1, Sox18, and Tie1, while glial cells expressed Aspa, Cdh2, Foxd3, Nlgn3, and Nrn1. Additionally, macrophages were defined by expression of C3ar1, F13a1, Fcrls, Mrc1, and Ms4a7, while myoepithelial cells expressed Cnn1, Fmod, Lama3, Osr1, and Rgs6, whereas NK cells were identified by expression of Eomes, Gzma, Il2rb, Ncr1, and Xcl1. Finally, stromal clusters exhibited strong expressions of C2, Fzd1, Lama2, Ogn, and Rgmb. Collectively, these marker-based signatures confirm an accurate classification of the major epithelial, immune, vascular, and stromal compartments within the SMG-generated dataset.37 Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined within each tissue to allow direct comparison of the most biologically relevant anatomical structure across samples, and the cells were restricted to those ROIs. UMAP projection of integrated Xenium-derived cell transcriptomes in these ROIs across experimental groups showed integration of single-cell transcriptomic profiles of mouse SMG from the non-IR, IR-, and IR + Ep-FH-treated groups (Figure S5). Each panel represents one biological replicate visualized after normalization using Seurat (v.5). Specifically, SMG cells from the non-IR healthy control group clustered tightly within well-defined domains, reflecting stable epithelial and stromal transcriptional programs. Conversely, SMG cells from the IR-treated group displayed broader and more diffuse distributions, consistent with increased transcriptional heterogeneity and loss of epithelial identity following IR exposure. Notably, SMG from the IR + Ep-FH-treated group showed a partially restored organization, with transcriptomic distributions more closely resembling those of non-IR healthy control group than IR-treated group. Together, the alignment of patterns across replicates confirms data reproducibility (i.e., no batch-to-batch effects), thereby highlighting condition-specific shifts in the cellular transcriptional landscape associated with IR-induced SG damage and Ep-FH-mediated SG regeneration.

Alteration of cell distribution in mouse SMG treated with and without Ep-FH was observed

Using Xenium spatial maps, we were able to visualize the distribution of major cell populations within mouse SMG from non-IR healthy control, IR-, and IR + Ep-FH-treated groups. Distinct epithelial, stromal, vascular, and immune compartments are represented according to cluster identity. For instance, in SMG from the non-IR healthy control group, acinar cell clusters (pink), ductal cell clusters (orange), and myoepithelial cell clusters (blue) were evenly arranged throughout the gland, with minimal presence of stromal cell clusters (hot pink), macrophage cell clusters (aquamarine), NK cell clusters (purple), and endothelial cell clusters (olive). In contrast, SMG cells from the IR-treated group exhibited a marked reduction of epithelial cell clusters and an expansion of stromal, macrophage, and endothelial cell clusters, thereby indicating structural disruption and inflammatory remodeling.39 Notably, distribution of some cell clusters in SMG from the IR + Ep-FH-treated group (i.e., acinar, stromal, immune, and myoepithelial cell) showed partial spatial similarity to the non-IR healthy control group whereas other cell clusters (i.e., ductal, endothelial, and glial cells) did not show such degree of similarity. These spatial distributions suggest that RT disrupts epithelial SMG architecture and that treatment with Ep-FH partially restores the organization of some cell clusters from epithelial and stromal compartments such that it is globally comparable to the non-IR healthy control group (Figure 2A). In order to verify whether the visuals were accurate, we calculated the relative cell abundance among the IR-treated group vs. non-IR healthy control group and IR + Ep-FH-treated group vs. IR-treated group for each cell cluster type. To this end, we observed differential cell abundance across all experimental groups (i.e., non-IR, IR-, and IR + Ep-FH-treated) with a cell cluster landscape that was specific for each condition, indicating remodeling of the post-IR SMG microenvironment following Ep-FH treatment. Specifically, the IR-treated group exhibited a marked enrichment of macrophages and NK cells (log2FC to control ≈ +0.99 and +0.76, respectively), together with depletion of acinar cell cluster populations (−0.23) and minimal change in endothelial cell cluster abundance. In contrast, the IR + Ep-FH-treated group caused a reversion of these trends, thereby significantly reducing macrophage and NK cell cluster representation (log2FC to IR-treated ≈ −0.60 and −0.40) while increasing acinar cell cluster abundance (+0.30). Moreover, endothelial cell populations were visually expanded in damaged regions but their overall proportion changed minimally, thus indicating that the principal effects of RT and recovery through Ep-FH treatment occur within the epithelial and immune cell cluster compartments.40 These results are shown in Table 1 with expanded groups in Table S1 and demonstrate that Ep-FH treatment mitigates RT-induced immune cell expansion and shifts epithelial composition toward the pattern observed in the non-IR healthy control group. We also show the relative cell cluster abundance profiles across all experimental groups (Figure 2B). Specifically, the IR + Ep-FH-treated group displayed a coordinated trend toward recovery of acinar, stromal, immune, myoepithelial, and glial cellular composition with a global trend toward the non-IR healthy control group. In contrast, ductal and endothelial cells did not show such degree of similarity when compared to the non-IR healthy control group. Although the differential abundance of most cell types in the IR + Ep-FH-treated group versus the IR-treated group is not statistically significant at the individual cell level, we observed a clear anti-correlation of log fold-change globally with values relative to their changes in IR-treated group/non-IR healthy control group to IR + Ep-FH-treated group/IR-treated group (Spearman correlation −0.83; p = 0.01), thereby indicating a partial reversion of IR-associated changes following Ep-FH treatment. To further quantify this trend, we computed analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) dissimilarity statistics between the non-IR healthy control group and the IR-treated group as well as between the non-IR healthy control group and the IR + Ep-FH-treated group, both based on cell type abundance as well as mean gene expression within each cell type. For cell type abundance, as well as for acinar, macrophage, myoepithelial, NK, and stromal cells, we see a decrease in overall dissimilarity after Ep-FH treatment (Figure S6), thereby indicating an inverse relationship where the Ep-FH-treated group shifted toward a transcriptional state more similar to the non-IR healthy control group, in this manner shifting the global transcriptomics profile toward that observed in non-IR healthy controls. To further substantiate the observed differences between these treatment groups, we generated principal component analysis (PCA) plots based on the cell cluster abundance results. Upon doing so, we observed that the IR + Ep-FH-treated group cells clustered in a manner completely distinct from those of the IR-treated group but were comparable to those of the non-IR healthy control group. Though an exception was noted (i.e., one of the replicates within the IR + Ep-FH-treated group clustered in a manner similar to the IR-treated group, thereby presenting an apparently conflicting result), we do not believe that this challenges our overall findings. Instead, we consider this result to likely be an outlier produced by irregular experimental factors (i.e., the injection of the Ep-FH in this particular mouse is thought to not have reached the target tissue, as depicted in Figure S7).41 To address this apparent outlier and corroborate our current findings, future studies involving larger cohorts with still more robust statistical power are warranted.

Observed alterations of signaling pathways varied by cell population in mouse SMG treated with and without Ep-FH

Regarding acinar cells, we observed coordinated transcriptional changes in pathways related to extracellular matrix (ECM) signaling, lipid mediator balance, and epithelial repair following IR treatment (representative subset of 20 differentially expressed genes [DEGs] in Figure 3, all with false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05 and full list shown on Figure S8). Specifically, the IR-treated group displayed an increased expression of Ccn1 and Sphk1, consistent with activation of pro-fibrotic signaling and sphingosine-1-phosphate-associated inflammatory pathways.42,43 In contrast, samples from the IR + Ep-FH-treated group exhibited a reduction of Ccn1 and Sphk1 expression while partially restoring Adgrg1 and Stat6 expression levels toward those observed in non-IR healthy controls. Results in ductal cells from the IR-treated group showed a collective downregulation of genes related to epithelial polarity in mouse SMG (e.g., Kremen2 and Robo2). In contrast, the IR + Ep-FH-treated group displays upregulation of Kremen2 and Robo2. These findings suggest that Kremen2 is involved in cell polarity via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway,44 which regulates establishing cell fate, tissue patterning, and cell polarity in SG. Likewise, Robo2 is associated with epithelial polarity as signaling pathways defects in mice lead to a loss of polarity in renal tubule epithelial cells, resulting in cystic kidneys.45 Robo2 is also required for the proper localization and expression of key polarity tight junction proteins, including ZO-1, claudin-2, and Par3 all of which are essential for establishing apical-basal polarity and lumen development45 (representative subset of 20 DEGs in Figure 4, all with FDR < 0.05 and full list shown on Figure S9). Likewise, stromal cells from the IR-treated group exhibited a transcriptional profile consistent with chronic fibrotic remodeling characterized by an increased expression of Ccn2 (a key mediator of fibroblast activation and matrix deposition)46 and a reduced expression of Tnc (an ECM protein associated with injury-responsive remodeling).47 In contrast, the IR + Ep-FH-treated group showed downregulation of Ccn2 expression with further suppression of Tnc, indicating inhibition of fibrosis alongside stabilization of ECM remodeling (representative subset of 20 DEGs in Figure 5, all with FDR < 0.05 and full list shown on Figure S10). As for macrophages, the IR-treated group displayed upregulation of apoptosis-associated genes, such as Wfdc18 and Fcgr3, suggesting alteration of innate immune response.48,49 In contrast, the IR + Ep-FH-treated group showed partial reversal of these transcriptional changes, shifting expression toward levels observed in non-IR healthy controls. The Wfdc18 gene encodes WAP proteins, which are thought to regulate host defense against invading microorganisms as well as endogenous proteolytic enzyme activity.50 On the other hand, Fcgr3 encodes a receptor protein that binds to the Fc portion of immunoglobulins G (IgG), which is involved in immune responses, such as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (representative subset of 20 DEGs in Figure 6, all with FDR < 0.05 and full list shown on Figure S11). Among NK cells, the IR-treated group displayed upregulation of genes associated with cytotoxicity and inflammatory recruitment, including Ccl8, Cxcl12, and Atf5. In contrast, the IR + Ep-FH-treated group showed downregulation of these genes toward levels observed in non-IR healthy controls. Notably, these genes are involved in immune regulation and paracrine stress signaling. Specifically, Ccl8 encodes a small cytokine protein that is a potent chemoattractant for various immune cells, including monocytes, T cells, eosinophils, basophils, and NK cells, to sites of inflammation,51,52 while Cxcl12 encodes the stromal cell-derived factor 1 (SDF-1), a chemokine protein also known as pre-B cell growth factor (PBGF), which regulates cell migration, stem cell niche homeostasis, angiogenesis (new blood vessel formation), and immune modulation.53,54 The interaction of Cxcl12 with its receptor CXCR4 is vital for embryonic development and is a key co-receptor for HIV entry into cells.55 Moreover, Atf5 regulates cell differentiation, survival, and cellular stress responses.56,57 Finally, NK cells from the IR-treated group showed downregulation of inflammatory mediators, such as Tmigd1 (representative subset of 20 DEGs all with FDR < 0.05 in Figure 7). Specifically, Tmigd1 encodes a protein that is involved in cell adhesion and is primarily expressed in the immune system, where it regulates T cell development and function.58 Collectively, these findings indicate that Ep-FH treatment mitigates RT-induced immune regulation, fostering a controlled NK-cell phenotype that supports tissue repair rather than inflammation-driven damage. Within endothelial cells, the IR-treated group displays downregulation of genes that are critical for vascular structure and angiogenic signaling, including Col4a1. However, the IR + Ep-FH-treated group did not significantly affect this gene, indicating no effect of the scaffold on angiogenesis (representative subset of 20 DEGs in Figure 8, all with FDR < 0.05 and full list shown on Figure S12). Regarding specific functions of Col4a1, previous studies showed that it is essential for the integrity of basement membranes in blood vessels, while mutations in this gene can lead to disorders involving abnormal vascular formation (angiopathy) and brain small-vessel disease, highlighting its importance in normal vascular development and function.59 Furthermore, cells exhibited transcriptional changes in the IR-treated group with downregulated expression of most genes associated with neurotrophic support and neural structural integrity, including Fgf1, Igf2,60 as well as genes related to cytoskeletal regulation, including Map1b, Mapt, and Ank261; however, the IR + Ep-FH-treated group did not show significant restoration of these glial-associated genes, suggesting that future studies are needed for stabilization of neuro-supportive programs rather than full reactivation of neurogenic pathways. In short, changes in glial cells are large from IR but very small from the Ep-FH treatment (i.e., FGF7 and FGF10), thereby indicating that incorporation of additional pro-neurogenic growth factors might be necessary to reverse glial cells to levels comparable to non-IR healthy controls (representative subset of 20 DEGs in Figure 9, all with FDR < 0.05 and full list shown on Figure S13). Finally, among myoepithelial cells, the IR-treated group displayed reduced expression of genes involved in ECM sensing, cell-cell adhesion, and cytoskeletal organization, including Adgrg1, Wnt5a, and Tmigd1,62 whereas the expression levels of genes within the IR + Ep-FH-treated group showed a partial recovery consistent with non-IR healthy controls (representative subset of 20 DEGs in Figure 10, all with FDR < 0.05 and full list shown on Figure S14). Our results are biologically significant as they correlate with previous findings where Ep-FH treatment caused a significant recovery of irradiated SMG in terms of structure and function.33,34 Likewise, we observed only minimal effects on endothelial and glial cells, thereby indicating future treatment improvements to deliver additional growth factors to promote vascular and neural growth.

Discussion

Our previous studies demonstrated that treatment of irradiated mouse SMG with Ep-FH not only restores epithelial cell organization but also significantly improves SG secretory function.33,34 In this study, we extended these findings using the high-plex, imaging-based in situ spatial profiling platform Xenium to analyze intact tissue sections from the prior study. Specifically, we generated single-cell Xenium RNA datasets and mapped major mouse SMG cell populations to assess treatment-related shifts in cell-type-specific signaling in three main classes including non-IR healthy control group, IR-treated group and an IR + Ep-FH-treated group. Our results suggest a major global effect of Ep-FH in irradiated mouse SMG to partially reverse IR-induced transcriptional changes by increasing the epithelial cell population, thereby mitigating immune overstimulation and shifting cells toward repair-oriented phenotypes instead of inflammation with specific contributory effects noted by cell type. Specifically, our findings in acinar cells showed that IR disrupted ECM signaling, lipid-mediator balance, and epithelial repair programs, while treatment with Ep-FH partially restored ECM- and repair-associated gene expression patterns. In ductal cells, IR caused a downregulation of genes governing epithelial polarity, while Ep-FH partially reinstated polarity-related gene expression. These results in epithelial cells are consistent with prior studies demonstrating that critical components of Ep-FH (i.e., L1 peptides, as well as FGF7 and FGF10) promote the formation of organized SG tissue, including acinar and ductal structures, which structurally resemble healthy glandular tissue in terms of proper lumen formation and cell polarization.34 Moreover, incorporation of FGF7 and FGF10 into the Ep-FH scaffold leads to intact signaling in epithelial cells through the FGFR2b receptor while likewise regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and epithelial tissue formation.33 Furthermore, our findings in stromal cells showed that IR induced a chronic remodeling transcriptional profile by an upregulation of fibroblast activation markers and matrix-deposition genes, while treatment with Ep-FH attenuated such pro-remodeling gene signatures. These results corroborate previous studies showing that the laminin-1 peptides YIGSR and A99 significantly promote a well-controlled fibroblast makeup within epithelial tissues, primarily by enhancing cell adhesion and promoting healthy collagen biosynthesis.33,34 Moreover, IR treatment damaged both macrophages and NK cells, as evidenced by upregulation of apoptotic and inflammatory genes, respectively; however, the expression of these genes shifted toward levels observed in non-IR healthy controls in tissues treated with Ep-FH, an effect that could be explained by the presence of the growth factors FGF7 and FGF10, both of which have been shown to regulate innate immunity.63 Additionally, IR-mediated suppressed genes are critical for vascular structure maintenance and angiogenic signaling in endothelial cells,64 and genes related to neurotropic support and neural structural integrity are downregulated in glial cells.65 However, treatment with Ep-FH did not affect vascular-related gene expression in endothelial cells or glial gene expression, thus suggesting that activation of additional pathways should be incorporated into the Ep-FH scaffold to facilitate formation of functional endothelial and glial networks. Furthermore, IR was found to reduce expression of genes involved in ECM sensing, cell adhesion, and cytoskeletal organization in myoepithelial cells, with a reversal of this effect noted upon treatment with Ep-FH. These results are consistent with previous studies indicating that FGF766 and FGF1067 are crucial paracrine signaling molecules that promote the growth, proliferation, and differentiation of myoepithelial cells by binding to their receptor FGFR2b.68 Finally, FGF7 and FGF10 were found to stimulate myoepithelial cell proliferation, help maintain tissue structure, and facilitate development and regeneration, acting through MAPK and PI3K pathways to regulate cell division and survival in multiple organs, including the breast,69 SG,70 and lungs.71

Integrative clustering studies further showed that some cell populations within Ep-FH-treated irradiated mouse SMG (i.e., acinar, stromal, immune, and myoepithelial) showed a trend toward similarity to non-irradiated SMG healthy controls in terms of abundance and distribution, whereas other cell types (i.e., ductal, endothelial, and glial cells) did not show such degree of similarity, thereby suggesting that despite the partial restorative effects of Ep-FH, improvements nonetheless need to be made in regard to treatment duration and activation of additional pathways to reach all cell types. To that end, future studies will draw on earlier findings indicating that VEGF with FGF9 as well as NGF with neurturin can be used to promote angiogenesis72 and neurogenesis,73,74,75,76,77 respectively. In general terms, transcriptomic analysis demonstrated at a previously unseen level of visual detail that treatment with Ep-FH partially reactivated key secretory- and polarity-related genes, partially restored stromal transcriptional profiles while showing limited effects on endothelial transcriptional programs, and shifted macrophages toward a reparative rather than inflammatory state while reducing profibrotic signaling. In this regard, integrative clustering further added biological significance to our previous studies by demonstrating that Ep-FH treatment shifted irradiated SG toward a transcriptional state more similar to non-irradiated healthy controls, indicating a trend toward coordinated recovery of tissue structure and function. Taken as a whole, these findings demonstrate that treatment with Ep-FH partially promotes structural and transcriptional regeneration through spatial reprogramming of cellular identities, thereby supporting partial restoration of the multicellular organization necessary for normal SG function following RT. Finally, the pro-regenerative responses observed here could potentially be applied to SG damage associated with other conditions, such as Sjögren’s disease (SD),31,32 in such cases new pre-clinical studies using mouse model for SD are warranted.78

Limitations of the study

Due to the limited gene complexity compared to whole-transcriptome methods, Xenium targets hundreds to ∼5,000 genes (depending on the panel), not the full transcriptome. This restriction makes the current work hypothesis-driven rather than unbiased discovery-oriented. Moreover, a highly accurate cell boundary definition is difficult, especially in complex or dense tissues such as the mouse SMG. The default might lead to incomplete capture of mouse SMG cell boundaries. Current methods for Xenium analysis, like the one used in this study, are not yet fully optimized for complex datasets where scaffold-driven regenerative processes are expected (i.e., multiple conditions with only one element of the Ep-FH scaffold) across multiple time points. Thus, the results from the current study should be carefully interpreted and further validated using protein expression analysis.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal model

Female C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks old, ∼18 g) were anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane and locally irradiated with a single 15 Gy dose to the head and neck using a Multi-Rad 225 irradiator. A custom-designed lead/aluminum shield with a 1 cm window was used to expose only the SMG region while protecting the rest of the body. Three days after exposure to IR, mice received bilateral intraglandular 10 μL injections of Ep-FH in each SMG (IR + Ep-FH-treated group) using a 28-gauge insulin syringe. These mice were compared with mice that did not receive RT (non-IR healthy control group) and untreated irradiated mice (IR-treated group). Next, SMGs were harvested at day 60 post-IR, fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm for Xenium spatial transcriptomic analysis.33 In total studies presented here show results from the three main experimental conditions: non-IR healthy control group, IR-treated group and IR + Ep-FH-treated group with a total of three mice per condition. All procedures were approved by the University of Missouri Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and conducted in accordance with established ethical guidelines protocol number: 70869.

Study design

Previous studies have shown that IR, commonly used for head and neck cancer treatment, typically leads to irreversible SG damage and consequent chronic hyposalivation, thereby necessitating development of more effective treatments to restore lost SG function and improve patient quality of life.19,20,21,22,23,24 To this end, we developed a novel hydrogel (Ep-FH) by chemically conjugating laminin-1 peptides (A99 and YIGSR) and growth factors (FGF7 and FGF10), to FH and found that this scaffold promotes SMG regeneration and functionality by improving epithelial tissue organization, thereby establishing a healthy network of blood vessels and nerves while likewise reducing fibrosis in a head and neck irradiated mouse model.33,34 Building on this work, we generated high-resolution gene expression data for a targeted panel of cells using the Xenium35 with the aim of revealing the cell types and signaling pathways affected by Ep-FH treatment by detailing the structure of the affected tissue with a high degree of resolution.

Method details

Ep-FH hydrogel preparation

The laminin-1 peptides (A99 and YIGSR) and growth factors (FGF7 and FGF10) were chemically conjugated to fibrinogen using sulfo-LC-SPDP chemistry to generate the fortified fibrin hydrogel (Ep-FH).33 Human fibrinogen was conjugated with laminin-1-derived peptides A99 and YIGSR using sulfo-LC-SPDP chemistry and the purified peptide-fibrinogen conjugates were lyophilized and stored at −80°C until use. For injections, A99-and YIGSR-conjugated fibrinogen were reconstituted in tris-buffered saline and mixed with CaCl2 (2.5 mM), ε-aminocaproic acid (2 mg/mL), and engineered MBP-FGF7 and MBP-FGF10 fusion proteins (100 ng/mL each). The final mixture was prepared immediately before administration. As in the original formulation, hydrogel polymerization occurred in situ via endogenous thrombin and factor XIII without the addition of exogenous enzymes. No modifications to the previously established Ep-FH composition or injection parameters were introduced for this study.

Spatial transcriptomics and imaging workflow

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) SMG tissue sections were mounted on Xenium slides, deparaffinized, and permeabilized to expose intracellular mRNA according to the Xenium Prime Mouse Pan Tissue & Pathways workflow.79,80 Next, 5,000 gene-specific probes, together with probe-set controls and genomic DNA controls, were hybridized overnight at 50°C (10 nM). After stringent washes, probes were ligated at 37°C for 2 h, followed by rolling-circle amplification (RCA), during which RCA primers annealed and enzymatically amplified (1 h at 4°C and 2 h at 37°C). Together, this process generated multiple fluorescent barcodes per transcript, yielding a high signal-to-noise ratio and background fluorescence was chemically quenched to reduce autofluorescence from lipofuscin, collagen, and other FFPE-associated components. Subsequently, image acquisition and primary processing were performed using the Xenium Analyzer platform and fluorescently labeled probes were then sequentially hybridized, imaged, and stripped across 15 automated cycles, with z-stacks acquired at 0.75 μm intervals through the full tissue thickness. After acquisition, the Xenium software pipeline performed image reconstruction, including stitching and alignment of z-stacks from each field of view (FOV) and fluorescence channel to generate a complete morphology reference (morphology.ome.tif). Next, optical distortion was corrected using instrument calibration parameters, Z-stacks were subsequently subsampled to a 3 μm step size to optimize cell segmentation. Finally, overlapping FOVs were registered using feature-based alignment, thereby yielding high-resolution and spatially integrated 3D transcriptomic maps.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Cell quality control and normalization

Raw Xenium cell-by-gene matrices were imported into Seurat v5.1 and a sample-specific identifier was appended to each dataset before merging to preserve sample provenance.81 Subsequently, low-quality cells were removed by filtering out cells containing fewer than 100 total gene counts or 20 detected genes. Moreover, gene expression was normalized and variance-stabilized using SCTransform in Seurat with default parameters except for clip.range = c(-10, 10) to restrict extreme residual values.82 Note that the SCTransform method performs normalization by regressing out regularized negative binomial coefficients modeled from total cell counts, as well as selecting the top variable genes (3000 by default) and zero-centering their values.

Dimensionality reduction, clustering and cell-type annotation

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on SCTransform-normalized data and the first 30 principal components selected based on inspection of PCA loading heatmaps. Next, cells were clustered using a shared nearest-neighbor graph with the Louvain algorithm at a resolution of 0.5, and low-dimensional visualization was performed using UMAP. Subsequently, multiple clustering resolutions were evaluated to confirm stability and a resolution of 0.5 was selected based on biological interpretability and reproducibility. Additionally, cell-type annotation was conducted using scType83 with marker genes curated from the CellMarker database36 using published SG datasets37,84 alongside canonical gene markers of acinar, ductal, endothelial, stromal, immune, glial, and myoepithelial populations including Aqp5, Krt8/18/19, Sox9, Kit, Col1a1 and Vim (Table S1). Furthermore, cell type scores over all marker genes were obtained for individual cells and average scores calculated for each cell cluster with specific identity assigned based on the maximal average score. Likewise, marker expression per cluster, along with assigned cell types were visualized as dot plots (Figure S1). Next, a UMAP of clustering results for the entire tissue across all samples and their assigned cell types is shown in Figure S2. Moreover, clusters with similar cell types were further consolidated into unified categories; proacinar, seromucous acinar, serous acinar and end bud were grouped as acinar cells while Ascl3+ duct, basal duct, intercalated duct, and granular convoluted tubule (GCT) were grouped as ductal cells. Finally, the Gstt1+ cell cluster was not included in the selection as its density detection was very small (Figure S3).

Lobule-based ROI segmentation and spatial masking

Whole-SMG Xenium spatial boundaries contained large inter-lobular empty regions and complex polygon geometries. To increase biological specificity, regions of interest (ROI) were defined at the level of individual lobular units rather than across entire SMG. Using the morphology reference image in Xenium Explorer, each SMG section was manually segmented into multiple lobule-based ROIs (e.g., Selection_1, Selection_2, Selection_3). To this end, each ROI encompassed a contiguous glandular lobule while excluding surrounding non-glandular tissue, peripheral artifacts, and inter-lobular empty areas (Figure S4). Moreover, ROI masks were exported from Xenium Explorer and applied to cell coordinates using the raster R package85 where only cells residing within lobular ROIs were used for downstream analyses. Furthermore, key quality-control parameters including total cell count, mean transcripts per cell, median genes and average spot intensity for all groups are shown in Table S2. Finally, smooth muscle cells detection was negligible (N = 487, 0.029% in whole tissue, N = 11, 0.001% in ROI) and therefore removed from subsequent analyses.

Cell-type abundance analysis

Abundance of multiple cell types were quantified for each sample using only cells within lobular ROIs. Differential abundance between non-IR healthy control group, IR- and IR + Ep-FH-treated groups was performed using edgeR.86,87 Given that counts represent discrete biological cell numbers rather than sequencing depth, trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) normalization was not applied, pairwise comparisons were conducted using the exactTest function while multi-group comparisons used generalized linear models (GLMs). Finally, p-values were adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method, abundances expressed as percentages of all ROI-restricted cells while heatmaps of cell type relative abundance generated using ComplexHeatmap.88

Differential gene expression analysis

Differential gene expression was assessed using a pseudo-bulk framework. Specifically, for each cell type, gene counts were summed across all cells belonging to the same sample and lobular ROI. Moreover, pseudo-bulk count matrices were analyzed using edgeR with TMM normalization whereas pairwise differences were tested using exactTest and multi-group analyses used GLMs. Next, FDR-corrected p-values were reported and normalized expression values expressed as counts per million (CPM) + TMM. Finally, differential expression analyses were performed independently for each annotated cell type and heatmaps of differentially expressed genes (DEGs, FDR <0.05) generated using ComplexHeatmap.88

Pathway enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes (FDR <0.05) were analyzed using Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO BP) and Reactome annotations from MSigDB v2023.2.88,89 while pathways containing at least 10 genes were evaluated using Fisher’s Exact Test with the 5,006 genes present in the Xenium Mouse Probe Panel used as the background universe. Finally, p values were adjusted for multiple testing using the FDR correction where p < 0.05 was considered significantly enriched.

Sample dissimilarity statistics

Bray-Curtis distances were calculated between samples based on log-scaled normalized cell type abundance or gene expression matrices while ANOSIM R statistics were obtained using the Vegan package in R.90
