Model Predictive Control is almost Optimal for Heterogeneous Restless Multi-armed Bandits
Dheeraj Narasimha, Nicolas Gast

Abstract
. We consider a general infinite horizon Heterogeneous Restless multi-armed Bandit (RMAB). Heterogeneity is a fundamental problem for many real-world systems largely because it resists many concentration arguments. In this paper, we assume that each of the N N arms can have different model parameters. We show that, under a mild assumption of uniform ergodicity, a natural finite-horizon LP-update policy with randomized rounding, that was originally proposed for the homogeneous case, achieves an 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ N ​ 1 / N ) \mathcal{O}\left(\log N\sqrt{1/N}\right) optimality gap in infinite time average reward problems for fully heterogeneous RMABs. In doing so, we show results that provide strong theoretical guarantees on a well-known algorithm that works very well in practice. The LP-update policy is a model predictive approach that computes a decision at time t t by planing over a time-horizon { t ​ … ​ t + τ } \{t\dots t+\tau\} . Our simulation section demonstrates that our algorithm works extremely well even when τ \tau is very small and set to 5 5 , which makes it computationally efficient. Our theoretical results draw on techniques from the model predictive control literature by invoking the concept of dissipativity and generalize quite easily to the more general weakly coupled heterogeneous Markov Decision Process setting. In addition, we draw a parallel between our own policy and the LP-index policy by showing that the LP-index policy corresponds to τ = 1 \tau=1 . We describe where the latter’s shortcomings arise from and how under our mild assumption we are able to address these shortcomings. The proof of our main theorem answers an open problem posed by 4 , paving the way for several new questions on the LP-update policies.



1.
Introduction and Related Works

We consider the sequential decision-making problem known as the
Restless Multi-Armed Bandit (RMAB)
over an infinite discrete-time horizon. Our problem consists of
N
weakly coupled arms, each arm may potentially be statistically different. At any given time step, the decision-maker must select a subset of no more than
N\alpha
arms to pull which we call the
budget constraint
. Each arm inhabits its own state space and may evolve differently depending on the selected action. Once the subset is chosen, each arm yields a reward based on its state and action, and transitions independently to a new state according to a state-action dependent transition kernel. Hence, the arms are “weakly coupled”, interacting only through shared constraints and our selection policy. Both the transition kernels and reward functions of all arms are known beforehand. The objective is to design a policy that maps joint arm states to actions to maximize the long-run average reward.

Sequential decision-making problems with weakly coupled constraints arise in many domains, including communication systems
3
, queueing theory
18
, web crawling
20
, and scheduling
6
;
23
, among others. A comprehensive overview of RMABs and their applications is presented in
19
. The RMAB problem is PSPACE-hard, as shown in
21
, motivating the study of algorithms that are optimal as
N\to\infty
, that is, asymptotically optimal.

The RMAB problem was first introduced by Whittle in his seminal work
26
, where he conjectured that under a condition called
indexability
, a priority-based policy—
Whittle’s index policy
—would be asymptotically optimal. This conjecture was later verified in the homogeneous setting (i.e., statistically identical arms) under a
uniform global attractor condition
(UGAP) in
25
, who also demonstrated counterexamples when this condition fails. Subsequently, many lines of research in the area have sought to relax both the attractor and indexability conditions, particularly in the homogeneous setting.

A generalization of Whittle’s index known as the
LP-priority index policy
was introduced in
24
, consequently, removing the need for indexability. While this work does not strictly require homogeneity it cannot cover the case where the heterogeneity scales linearly with the number of arms. More recent work
7
;
8
shows that such priority policies can be
exponentially close to optimal
under three key conditions centered around the optimal fixed point: unichain and aperiodicity of the underlying Markov process, the UGAP condition for the dynamical system and non-degeneracy of the Linear Program for the fixed point solution.

On the other hand, the UGAP assumption was first eliminated in
12
, where the authors constructed an unconstrained virtual system driven to the fixed point. To obtain an asymptotically optimal policy, they introduced a
synchronization assumption
, ensuring the real system aligns with the virtual one. This method of
steering systems to optimal fixed points
has inspired subsequent works such as
14
;
13
;
28
. For instance,
14
proposed a
two-set policy
to actively synchronize non-aligned arms, recovering exponential optimality by leveraging the fixed-point structure as in
8
. In contrast,
28
designed an
align-and-steer
policy that guides the mean field control system to the fixed point under a
controllability
assumption.

More recently,
9
demonstrated that an LP-update policy—solving a finite-horizon linear program—achieves asymptotic optimality in the infinite horizon setting without explicitly trying to steer the dynamical system to operate at the fixed point. This result bypasses assumptions on policy structure or indexability, requiring only assumptions on the transition kernel under the
0
-action. Our algorithm and results can be seen as a complete generalization of these results in the far more complicated heterogeneous arm setting.

In contrast to these rich developments in the homogeneous case, results for
heterogeneous RMABs
are relatively scarce. For the infinite-horizon case,
11
constructed a fluid-policy framework which resembles
28
align and steer policy, however, its application for a heterogeneous problem where each arm lives in its own state space is unclear since such a system does not have a notion of empirical measure that can be aligned with the optimal fixed point policy. Close in spirit to our results is the recent work of
30
which proves an order
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
result using a Lyapunov projection method. The core idea of their work is to create an expanding set of arms whose actions align with the optimal fixed point actions (used previously in the homogeneous setting in
13
), aligning the system with the invariant measure. In terms of theoretical results, our work primarily differs from their work by the assumptions made for ensuring performance. While our assumptions require a form of ergodicity in the
-0-
action, their assumptions require a mixing time result for the
optimal single arm policy
. To the best of our knowledge these assumptions cannot directly be compared to each other. On the other hand in terms of performance in simulations, our algorithm usually performs better.

The LP-update policy has been proposed by several authors, we highlight here the works by
4
for the finite-horizon heterogeneous RMAB, who posed the infinite-horizon extension as an open problem. Other works that have built upon variations of this LP-update algorithm, include
15
;
29
;
10
;
31
;
7
and references therein.

Our main result demonstrates that the ideas introduced in
4
can be used to stitch together an infinite horizon policy under very mild assumptions for the heterogeneous multi-armed bandit problem that is almost asymptotically optimal policy.

Main Contributions

Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

(1)

We establish the first
O\left(\frac{\log N}{\sqrt{N}}\right)
bound on the difference between the optimal value of the average rewards in the Restless Multi-Armed Bandit (RMAB) setting and the average reward accumulated by the LP-update policy under an easily verifiable Ergodicity condition. In doing so, we resolve a key open problem posed by
4
. In addition, our LP-update strategy has been shown to have excellent empirical performance and is easy to implement.

(2)

Our proof technique is modular in nature, meaning that our ergodicity conditions or concentration techniques can be independently replaced or extended. Our proof can be broken into two main components, 1) a convergence result for the approximate dynamical system and 2) a concentration result which we call the ’Jensen gap result’ to the dynamical system. Each of these problems can be solved independently, making the techniques we use easy to generalize to a wide range of constrained Markov Decision processes.

(3)

Our technique for proving convergence of our dynamical system uses a novel dissipative framework, previously only used by
9
in the homogeneous setting. This is the key concept that allows us to show that a finite horizon linear program solution can be used to string together an infinite horizon policy.

(4)

An important example of our proof technique is its use in the weakly coupled heterogeneous Markov decision process setting, where we recover the same results without much modification to our algorithm.

(5)

We show that our policy can be seen as a time-varying generalization of the well-known Whittle’s index policy. Our LP-update policy compensates for the pit-falls surrounding Whittle’s relaxation when the UGAP assumption fails.

2.
System Model

We consider an infinite-horizon discrete-time heterogeneous Restless multi-armed bandit problem parameterized by the tuple
\langle\mathcal{S}^{(N)},\{\mathbf{P}^{(n)}_{0}\}_{n=1}^{n=N},\{\mathbf{P}^{(n)}_{1}\}_{n=1}^{n=N},\mathbf{r}^{(n)};\alpha\rangle
. A decision maker controls
N
heterogeneous arms and must choose a subset of at most
\alpha N
arms to pull at each time, where
0<\alpha\leq 1
is a known constant. If arm
n
in state
s
is pulled (the action is denoted by
1
), it follows a transition kernel
\mathbf{P}^{(n)}_{1}
obtaining a reward
r^{(n)}(s,1)
. On the other hand, if it is left alone (the corresponding action is denoted by
0
), the arm transitions according to
\mathbf{P}^{(n)}_{0}
and obtains a reward
r^{(n)}(s,0)
. The reward vector
\mathbf{r}^{(n)}
is the concatenation of all such rewards. We will assume that each arm
n
lives on a finite state space
\mathcal{S}{(n)}
and that all rewards lie between
0
and
1
for each arm. So long as all rewards are finite, this assumption can be made without loss of generality by shifting and scaling rewards appropriately. We denote by
\mathcal{S}^{(N)}
the joint state space i.e, the cartesian product of the individual spaces of each arm,
\Pi_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{S}{(n)}
. Given the state of the arms at time
t
,
[s_{1}\dots s_{N}]=:\mathbf{s}^{(N)}\in\mathcal{S}^{(N)}
and the decision maker’s action
[a_{1}\dots a_{N}]=:\mathbf{a}^{(N)}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
, a reward
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}r^{(n)}(s_{n},a_{n})
is earned and the system of arms will transition jointly to the next state
\mathbf{\hat{s}}^{(N)}\in\mathcal{S}^{(N)}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{S}^{(N)}(t+1)=\mathbf{\hat{s}}^{(N)}|\mathbf{S}^{(N)}(t)=\mathbf{s}^{(N)},\mathbf{A}^{(N)}(t)=\mathbf{a}^{(N)},\dots\mathbf{S}^{(N)}(0),\mathbf{A}^{(N)}(0))

(1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{S}^{(N)}(t+1)=\mathbf{\hat{s}}^{(N)}|\mathbf{S}^{(N)}(t)=\mathbf{s}^{(N)},\mathbf{A}^{(N)}(t)=\mathbf{a}^{(N)})=\Pi_{n=1}^{N}P^{n}_{\hat{s}_{n}|s_{n},a_{n}}.

Where
P^{n}_{\hat{s}_{n}|s_{n},a_{n}}
denotes the
(s_{n},\hat{s}_{n})
entry of the corresponding transition matrix
\mathbf{P}^{(n)}_{a_{n}}
indicating the probability of an arm in state
s_{n}
transitioning to state
\hat{s}_{n}
.
It is important to note that the arms are only coupled through the budget constraint
\alpha
. Thus, our Markovian bandit arms are weakly coupled.

Let
\pi
be a stationary policy mapping each state to a probability of choosing joint actions across the
N
arms, i.e,
\pi:\mathcal{S}^{(N)}\to\Delta(\{0,1\}^{N})
subject to the budget constraint,
\alpha
at each time i.e, if
\mathbf{a}^{(N)}:=\{a_{1},a_{2}\dots a_{N}\}
is the realization of the joint actions of all the arms then,
\sum_{n}^{N}a_{n}\leq\alpha N.

Let
\mathcal{P}^{(N)}
denote the space of all such policies. Given a policy
\pi\in\mathcal{P}^{(N)}
, and an initial set of arm states
\mathbf{S}^{(N)}(0)=\mathbf{s}
, we define the average gain of policy
\pi
as

(2)

\mathbf{V}_{\pi}^{N}(\mathbf{s})=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}r^{(n)}(A_{n}(t),S_{n}(t))\Bigg|\mathbf{S}^{(N)}(0)=\mathbf{s}\right].

Here
(S^{N}_{n}(t),A^{N}_{n}(t))
denotes the state-action pair of the
n^{\text{th}}
arm at time
t
and
r^{(n)}(A_{n}(t),S_{n}(t))
denotes the
S_{n}(t)^{\text{th}}
entry of the
\mathbf{r}^{(n)}(\cdot,A_{n}(t))
vector. From
22
, one can show that for any stationary policy
\pi
, this limit is well defined. The heterogeneous infinite-horizon average reward problem or the heterogeneous restless multi-armed bandit problem dubbed RMAB is to find the optimal policy
\pi\in\mathcal{P}^{(N)}
that maximizes this limit. Concretely, we formalize the problem as follows:

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\text{OPT}}^{N}

\displaystyle:=\max_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}^{(N)}}\mathbf{V}_{\pi}^{N}(\mathbf{s}).

The optimal policy exists and the limit is well defined. It is not difficult to show that under mild conditions, the value of
\mathbf{V}_{\text{OPT}}^{N}
is independent of the initial state. Hence, we refrain from adding any explicit dependence on the initial state in our notation. Furthermore,
21
showed that such problems are PSPACE hard.

The RMAB problem then boils down to finding algorithms whose error grows vanishingly small as the number of arms tend to infinity.

In this paper we will show that a simple Linear Program (LP) update based algorithm with updates that compute a
finite time horizon LP
is
almost asymptotically optimal
for this problem.

4.
Main Theorem

Our main result will show that the LP update policy is asymptotically optimal as the number of arms
N
goes to infinity, under an easily verifiable mixing assumption on the transition matrices. To express this condition, for a fixed integer and a sequence of action
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1}\dots a_{k})\in\{0,1\}^{k}

P^{(n)}_{j|i,\mathbf{a}}

(i,j)

\prod_{t=1}^{k}\mathbf{P}^{(n)}_{a_{t}}
. We then denote by
\rho_{k}
the following quantity:

(14)

Assumption 1

There exists a finite

.

Remark 2

We pause to state that while our assumption may look complicated at first, a sufficient condition for the assumption to be satisfied is if the transition kernel is ergodic in the state space under action

We now come to the main theorem. We show that the LP-update policy is asymptotically optimal as the number of arms tends to infinity under an easily verifiable assumption on the transition kernels for each arm.

1

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}^{\star}-\epsilon-\tau\left(\left(1+\frac{k}{\rho_{k}}\right)\frac{k}{\rho_{k}}+2\|\mu\|_{\infty}+1\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log N\tau^{2}}{N}}\right)

(16)

\displaystyle-\left(2\|\mu\|_{\infty}+1+\tau\right)\frac{\alpha N-\lfloor\alpha N\rfloor}{N}.

As we shall show, there are three error terms that determine the upper bound between the difference in performance of the LP-update policy and the optimal policy. The first term is bounded because of

and its implementation using

. Our LP-policy solves a relaxed problem where the constraints are satisfied in expectation
at each time step

8

5.
Technical Framework for the Main Theorem

Several works have viewed the problem of using the LP fluid policy to solve a finite horizon heterogeneous restless multi-armed bandit problem. One of the main difficulties associated with extending the finite horizon policy to its infinite horizon average reward counterpart is that the difference in the value of solutions can grow exponentially over time, see for example the value of the Lipschitz constant in
8
as a function of time. As a result, the optimal solution induced by the finite horizon problem may have nothing to do with the infinite horizon counterpart.
Dissipativity
is a key feature of our framework which provides us with an equivalent surrogate loss function. We introduce this framework in Section
5.2
. As a result, the finite horizon problem cannot be too far away from the infinite horizon limit in value and hence, one is free to use a model predictive control approach to solve the average reward problem.

A second key challenge is to show that under heterogeneity we are able still able to obtain a
concentration of measure
result which allows us to use a deterministic result to solve a stochastic problem. We address this key challenge using what we call the
Jensen’s gap lemma
, introduced in
5.3
. This happens to be our most technical result.

We begin our framework by discussing an important continuity result for the bias function.

5.3.
Jensen Gap lemma

Let
\mathbf{x}:=\{\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\mathbf{x}^{(2)}\dots\mathbf{x}^{(N)}\}
be an initial state defined on
\Pi_{n=1}^{N}\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}{(n)}})
. Next, let

be a state so that each component is
X^{(i)}
is sampled independently from the distribution
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
. In Appendix

9
, we show that
\mathbf{W}^{\text{FL}}\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right)
is concave in
\mathbf{x}
. It follows from Jensen’s lemma that
\left(\mathbf{W}^{\text{FL}}\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathbf{x}}\left[\mathbf{W}^{\text{FL}}\left(t,X\right)\right]\right)\geq 0
. We call this non-negative difference between a concave function and its expected value the
Jensen’s gap
. We will show a ‘converse’ result that indicates that Jensen’s gap is, in fact, quite small for our problem. This lemma will prove essential for our concentration bound in the presence of heterogeneity.

Lemma 8

(Jensen Gap lemma)
.

The Jensen gap between
\mathbf{W}^{\text{FL}}\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right)
and the expected value of

where the state of each arm

\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
for any fixed

is bounded by

(28)

\mathbf{W}^{\text{FL}}\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathbf{x}}\left[\mathbf{W}^{\text{FL}}\left(t,X\right)\right]\leq t\left(\left(1+\frac{k}{\rho_{k}}\right)\frac{k}{\rho_{k}}+2\|\mu\|_{\infty}+1\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log Nt^{2}}{N}}\right).

Our proof of this lemma proceeds by induction. There are two related technical challenges associated with the proof, 1) generate a feasible policy for arm states
X
drawn according to
\mathbf{x}
. 2) Ensure that the arms evolve independently for one more step and remain close to each other in value. Use 1) and 2) with the inductive hypothesis to complete the proof. It is important to note that if the feasible policy is chosen incorrectly, the inductive hypothesis could potentially lead to an exponential dependence on the horizon
t
. This lemma is our most technically challenging result.
Our proof can be found in Appendix
D

7.
Conclusion

Through this work we show the asymptotic optimality of the LP-index update policy for the notoriously difficult heterogeneous multi-armed bandit problem under an easily verifiable assumption. What is more, our framework is modular enough so that a different assumption that proves the continuity of our bias function can be slotted in yielding much the same result. Furthermore, we observe the close relationship between our own notion of index which depends on the time horizon and the Whittle’s index policy. Crucially, using the framework of dissipativity, we are able to show that a finite-horizon computation of the index subverts the need to relax constraints (Whittle’s relaxation) while giving us a near-optimal policy for the infinite-horizon average reward problem. Along the way there are several open questions that we may pose given some of the shortcomings of our analysis. The most direct question requires a resolution of our Conjecture
1
, which we strongly believe can come through
turnpike properties
. A very related question now follows: can one optimize
\tau
for the LP-update? Our simulations clearly indicate that for all intents and purposes one can fix a very small time horizon, say
\tau<5
in order to solve this problem. Such questions are highly relevant, not only in solving weakly coupled MDPs but also in the more complex learning setting. We conclude by stating that Model Predictive Control methods are an underexplored avenue towards practical solutions to constrained MDP problems as we have demonstrated through our resolution of the open problem stated by
4
.

in state
s_{j}
when,

\displaystyle r^{(i)}(s_{i},1)-\mathbf{\lambda}^{\star}({\mathbf{x}})+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{i},1}\mu_{s^{\prime}}^{(i)}-r^{(i)}(s_{i},0)-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{i},0}\mu_{s^{\prime}}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle r^{(j)}(s_{j},1)-\mathbf{\lambda}^{\star}({\mathbf{x}})+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{j},1}\mu_{s^{\prime}}^{(j)}-r^{(j)}(s_{j},0)-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{j},0}\mu_{s^{\prime}}^{(j)}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle r^{(i)}(s_{i},1)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{i},1}\mu_{s^{\prime}}^{(i)}-r^{(i)}(s_{i},0)-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{i},0}\mu_{s^{\prime}}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle r^{(j)}(s_{j},1)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{j},1}\mu_{s^{\prime}}^{(j)}-r^{(j)}(s_{j},0)-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{j},0}\mu_{s^{\prime}}^{(j)}

From Definition
6
,
\mu
is another collection of multipliers for the same constraint. It now follows that
\mu_{rel}-\mu=\bar{c}\BFone
for some constant
\bar{c}
i.e, the two lagrange multipliers differ by a constant.

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle r^{(i)}(s_{i},1)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{i},1}(\mu_{s^{\prime}}^{(i)}+\bar{c})-r^{(i)}(s_{i},0)-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{i},0}(\mu_{s^{\prime}}^{(i)}+\bar{c})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle r^{(j)}(s_{j},1)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{j},1}(\mu_{s^{\prime}}^{(j)}+\bar{c})-r^{(j)}(s_{j},0)-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{j},0}(\mu_{s^{\prime}}^{(j)}+\bar{c})

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle r^{(i)}(s_{i},1)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{i},1}\mu_{rel,s^{\prime}}^{(i)}-r^{(i)}(s_{i},0)-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{i},0}\mu_{rel,s^{\prime}}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle r^{(j)}(s_{j},1)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{j},1}\mu_{rel,s^{\prime}}^{(j)}-r^{(j)}(s_{j},0)-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{j},0}\mu_{rel,s^{\prime}}^{(j)}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle r^{(i)}(s_{i},1)-\mathbf{\lambda}_{rel}+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{i},1}\mu_{rel,s^{\prime}}^{(i)}-r^{(i)}(s_{i},0)-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{i},0}\mu_{rel,s^{\prime}}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle r^{(j)}(s_{j},1)-\mathbf{\lambda}_{rel}+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{j},1}\mu_{rel,s^{\prime}}^{(j)}-r^{(j)}(s_{j},0)-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}{(i)}}P^{(i)}_{s^{\prime}|s_{j},0}\mu_{rel,s^{\prime}}^{(j)}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\mathbf{i}_{i}^{\text{lp}}\geq\mathbf{i}_{j}^{\text{lp}}

Therefore, the LP-priority policy solves the LP-update policy when
\tau
is set to
1
, completing the proof.
