Early stage nonsmall cell lung cancer: Toward a risk‐adaptive paradigm in the era of biologic precision
Authors: Chaoqi Zhang, Xuanyu Gu, Pan Wang, Dongyu Li, Guochao Zhang, Ting Wang, Peng Wu, Wei Rao, Shan Muhammad, Binghua Tan, Yun Che, Mobai Zhu, Qilin Qiao, Fanbo Lai, Bohui Zhao, Kailun Fei, Yue Wu, Zhihui Zhang, Min Dai, Wanqing Chen, Yawei Zhang, Nan Bi, Jie Wang, Jie He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe clinical landscape of early stage nonsmall cell lung cancer is at transformative crossroads. Driven by the widespread adoption of low‐dose computed tomography screening, the frequent detection of ground‐glass opacities, and a rising incidence among never‐smokers, the diagnostic center of gravity has shifted toward earlier, potentially curable disease. This shift has been accompanied by equally important therapeutic advances, including parenchyma‐sparing surgical techniques, minimally invasive platforms enhanced by digital navigation, and the transformative integration of perioperative immunotherapy and targeted agents. Concurrently, noninvasive monitoring approaches, such as liquid biopsy, have emerged as powerful tools to guide precision management. Despite this progress, substantial barriers to achieving a universal cure persist. Clinicians continue to face uncertainty in the management of ground‐glass opacities, the anatomy‐based TNM staging system fails to capture the biologic heterogeneity of early tumors, and global disparities in access to innovation remain unresolved. To address these challenges, the authors propose a shift toward a risk‐adaptive management paradigm that harnesses artificial intelligence–driven analytics and multi‐omics profiling to tailor treatment intensity according to each patient's biologic risk. Such an approach would enable appropriate escalation for high‐risk individuals while permitting safe de‐escalation for those at low risk. This holistic, lifespan‐oriented strategy must be embraced to deliver equitable and durable cures for patients with early stage nonsmall cell lung cancer.Keywords: cancer screening, early stage, health equity, nonsmall cell lung cancer, precision medicine

INTRODUCTION

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer‐related mortality globally, claiming approximately 1.8 million lives annually. 1 Nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC) constitutes the majority of these patients, representing approximately 85%. 2 , 3 Whereas the historical narrative of NSCLC was defined by late‐stage diagnosis and poor outcomes, a profound stage migration is now underway. The widespread implementation of low‐dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening programs, 4 the increasing incidental detection of ground‐glass opacities (GGOs) on chest imaging, 5 and the rising incidence of lung cancer among never‐smokers have shifted the diagnostic center of gravity toward early stage disease. 6

This shift has challenged the conventional one‐size‐fits‐all surgical model. Although anatomic resection remains the standard for curative‐intent treatment, 7 , 8 early stage NSCLC management has evolved into a multidisciplinary framework rooted in biologic precision. High‐precision surgical techniques are increasingly integrated with perioperative immunotherapy and targeted agents.

Substantial barriers to a universal cure persist. These include clinical ambiguity in GGO management, the growing insufficiency of anatomy‐based TNM staging to capture molecular and spatial tumor heterogeneity, unresolved questions regarding optimal perioperative treatment sequencing, and socioeconomic disparities in access to precision care.

This review synthesizes the evolving landscape of early stage NSCLC. We examine shifting epidemiological trends and risk factors, then evaluate the pillars of precision care, from screening and conventional pathologic assessment to multimodal therapy. We subsequently propose a framework to overcome existing barriers to a biologic cure. By integrating longitudinal surveillance with an emphasis on health equity and lifespan survivorship, we aim to provide a roadmap for navigating the complexities of NSCLC management in the precision medicine era.

EPIDEMIOLOGY, RISK FACTORS, AND THE CHANGING LANDSCAPE

Epidemiology

Global and regional burden

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer‐related death globally, with an estimated 2.48 million new cases and 1.82 million deaths reported in 2022. 1 China represented more than 40% of all new lung cancer cases (1,060,584) and deaths (733,291) worldwide; thus lung cancer was the most commonly diagnosed type of cancer and the leading cause of cancer‐related mortality in the country. 9 , 10

Geographic variation reflects complex interactions among genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures, and differential access to screening and treatment resources. The global burden is projected to increase by 86% in new cases and 95% in deaths by 2050 caused by population growth and aging, particularly in developing and transitioning countries. 11 China provides a prominent example: an estimated 5,150,600 new cancer cases and 2,582,200 cancer deaths occurred in 2024, of which lung cancer accounted for the largest share—1,175,900 new cases (22.8% of all cancers) and 743,300 deaths (28.8% of all cancer deaths), ranking first for both incidence and mortality nationally. 12 In comparison, the United States is projected to report approximately 226,650 new cases and 124,730 deaths in 2025, representing 21% of all cancer deaths. 13 , 14 , 15 Geographic heterogeneity is evident at subnational levels: within China, incidence is highest along the eastern coast, 16 whereas, in the United States, state‐level variation correlates with economic development and smoking prevalence. 13

Sex disparities persist. Among men, lung cancer has the highest incidence (15.3%) and mortality (22.7%); whereas, among women, it ranks second (9.4% and 13.6%, respectively). 1 However, among US individuals younger than 65 years, incidence was higher in women than in men for the first time in 2021 (15.7 vs. 15.4 per 100,000, respectively). 14 , 17 Over the past decade, incidence has declined by 3% annually in men compared with 1.3% in women; since 2013–2014, mortality has declined by 3%–5% annually in men versus 2%–4% in women. 13 , 18 This narrowing gap likely reflects historical differences in smoking patterns and potential biologic differences in susceptibility to tobacco carcinogens.

The median age at diagnosis ranges from 65 to 71 years. 2 , 13 , 14 NSCLC prevalence is increasing among younger patients (from 77.5 to 87.9 per 100,000 from 2010 to 2016) while decreasing among older patients (from 825.1 to 812.4 per 100,000), 2 indicating a need for heightened clinical vigilance when younger patients present with respiratory symptoms.

Shifting paradigm in stage distribution

Stage distribution at diagnosis has noticeably shifted toward earlier detection over the past 2 decades, fundamentally altering the lung cancer treatment paradigm. 19 , 20 The International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) tumor, lymph node, metastasis (TNM) staging system, now in its ninth edition (effective January 2025; Figure 1), remains the cornerstone of NSCLC staging. 21 , 22 In the United States, localized‐stage diagnoses increased from 17% in 2004 to 28% in 2018, representing a 4.5% annual increase that aligned with the implementation of LDCT screening according to the 2013 US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommendations. 17 Contemporary data indicate that approximately 27%–28.1% of cases are currently diagnosed at the localized stage, whereas 43%–50% are diagnosed as distant‐stage at presentation. 23 This improvement occurred along with sharp declines in advanced disease incidence; overall lung cancer incidence decreased by 2.4% annually, whereas localized‐stage rates increased. 17

Geographic variation in early detection is substantial. State‐level analyses reveal localized‐stage diagnosis proportions ranging from <25% to >30% across the United States, demonstrating correlations with screening facility availability and adherence to screening recommendations. 23 Internationally, organized screening programs have yielded even more pronounced results. In South Korea, 41.2% of patients with nonsquamous NSCLC were diagnosed at stage I between 2014 and 2019. 24 China has also demonstrated robust improvements in early detection, with approximately 32.5% of lung cancers diagnosed as early stage in a multicenter study. 25 Data from implementation of the China protocol, which integrates artificial intelligence (AI)‐assisted diagnostics and enhanced screening algorithms, indicate substantial increases in early stage detection rates; some advanced centers have achieved stage I diagnosis rates exceeding 60%. 12 This pronounced stage migration reflects the systematic implementation of enhanced screening protocols tailored to high‐risk populations. The granularity of this stage migration is further illustrated by the increasing identification of ultra‐early stage disease. Proposed in China, this term refers to stage IA1 disease, with a reported diagnostic rate of 28% and a 5‐year survival rate of 97.5%. 26 This migration toward earlier stages is likely driven by multiple factors, including increased incidental detection of pulmonary nodules on computed tomography (CT) scans performed for unrelated indications, expanded implementation of lung cancer screening programs, heightened clinical awareness of early manifestations, and more sophisticated diagnostic algorithms for nodule characterization. 2

This paradigm shift creates specific clinical imperatives. Emphasis on survival disparities—in which patients who have stage I adenocarcinoma achieve a 3‐year survival rate of 95.1% compared with 42.4% for those who have stage IV disease—serves as a powerful instrument for patient education. 27

Histology and molecular landscape

Adenocarcinoma has emerged as the predominant histologic subtype worldwide, representing approximately 40%–50% of all lung cancers and demonstrating particularly high prevalence in Asian populations. 2 , 27 , 28 The relation between adenocarcinoma and smoking is complex and clinically relevant. Although adenocarcinoma comprises 50%–70% of lung cancers in never‐smokers, 6 , 29 , 30 , 31 smoking substantially increases risk: heavy smokers demonstrate excess risk ratios exceeding 200 compared with never‐smokers. 32 This histologic shift over recent decades—from squamous cell predominance to adenocarcinoma predominance—likely reflects changes in cigarette composition and smoking patterns that alter carcinogen deposition within the lung. 30 Squamous cell carcinomas, which historically comprised the majority of smoking‐related lung cancers, now represent a smaller proportion and are more strongly associated with intensive, long‐term smoking exposure.

The molecular landscape exhibits profound heterogeneity that varies according to histology, ethnicity, and smoking status. Adenocarcinomas demonstrate strong associations with actionable driver mutations: epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations occur in 10%–15% of Western patients and in 40–60% of Asian patients 33 ; anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) rearrangements occur in 3%–7%, ROS proto‐oncogene 1 (ROS1) rearrangements occur in 1%–2%, and Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) mutations—predominantly in smokers—occur in 25%–30%. 34 In contrast, squamous cell carcinomas harbor fewer targetable alterations.

Risk factors

Modifiable risk factors

Tobacco exposure

Tobacco smoking is the most formidable, modifiable risk factor, conferring a 10‐fold to 30‐fold increased risk of lung cancer worldwide. 35 Specifically, in Asia, the risk associated with smoking is slightly lower, ranging between approximately 2.4 and 6.5. 36 Risk is directly proportional to cumulative exposure, duration, and intensity. Despite declining global consumption, tobacco control remains inadequate in middle‐income countries, which are projected to bear the majority of the future global lung cancer burden. 37 Smoking cessation represents a critical therapeutic intervention across the cancer care continuum. Prediagnosis cessation demonstrates a clear dose‐response relationship among ever smokers, whereby each doubling of years since quitting is correlated with a 4% reduction in mortality risk. 38 Among patients already diagnosed with stage IA–IIIA NSCLC, postdiagnosis cessation confers even greater benefit, with a median overall survival (OS) advantage of 21.6 months relative to persistent smokers. 39 Evidence‐based cessation programs, combining pharmacotherapy with structured behavioral counseling, must be integrated at diagnosis and sustained throughout survivorship.

Secondhand smoke exposure increases lung cancer risk by approximately 24% in never‐smokers 40 ; it affects household and workplace environments in a dose‐dependent manner. Comprehensive smoke‐free policies in health care institutions and broader communities are essential to protect vulnerable populations, coupled with educational interventions that encourage family members to establish smoke‐free homes.

The landscape of inhalation‐related risk is further complicated by emerging nicotine‐delivery systems and alternative substances. Electronic cigarette (e‐cigarette) use is increasing among younger populations; however, it remains unknown whether e‐cigarette use is associated with a greater risk of lung cancer, and long‐term longitudinal safety data are still required. Furthermore, the independent lung cancer risk of cannabis smoking is inconclusive because of confounding, concurrent tobacco use, despite sharing combusted carcinogens. 41 Systematic assessment of all tobacco‐related and inhalational exposures is essential at each clinical encounter.

Air pollution

Long‐term exposure to fine particulate matter has emerged as a major lung cancer risk factor, particularly in low‐income and middle‐income countries. Between 1990 and 2019, particulate matter–associated lung cancer mortality increased by 25% worldwide, whereas mortality associated with household air pollution decreased by 62%. 37 Moreover, climate change threatens to exacerbate air‐quality deterioration and related health disparities. 42 Emerging evidence links particulate matter exposure to specific molecular and radiographic subtypes, including GGO presentations and EGFR‐mutated adenocarcinomas. 43 These findings suggest that screening eligibility criteria should extend beyond smoking history in regions with severe air pollution.

Occupational and household chemical exposures

Despite stringent regulatory frameworks, occupational carcinogens—including asbestos, radon, arsenic, chromium, nickel, silica, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons—remain persistent drivers of lung cancer. 37 , 44 , 45 These exposures disproportionately affect workers in construction, mining, manufacturing, and agriculture. Household exposures represent an underrecognized risk factor, especially among never‐smoking women in Asian populations. 46 Detailed occupational and household exposure histories should be obtained for all patients, including remote exposures that may have occurred decades earlier.

Lifestyle factors

Emerging evidence indicates that anthropometric measures, including body mass index, height, and height‐normalized weight, independently influence prognosis in early stage NSCLC. 47 Nutritional status and body composition thus may affect treatment tolerance and cancer outcomes, warranting assessment at diagnosis and the integration of nutrition counseling into survivorship care.

Nonmodifiable and partially modifiable risk factors

Genetic and familial susceptibility

Family history of lung cancer and inherited genetic susceptibility inform risk‐stratification and screening strategies. Polygenic risk assessment demonstrates clinical utility for personalized screening 48 ; detailed cancer histories in first‐degree and second‐degree relatives should guide earlier or more intensive screening. Germline and somatic molecular alterations also have substantial prognostic implications. For example, EGFR‐mutated tumors demonstrate significantly improved OS across all stages, whereas KRAS mutations are associated with worse survival in stage I disease. 49 These findings underscore the importance of molecular testing even in early stage disease; specific alterations inform both prognosis and treatment selection.

Preexisting conditions

Chronic respiratory diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease 50 and interstitial pulmonary fibrosis, 51 share mechanistic pathways with lung carcinogenesis. Patients with prior smoking‐related malignancies and hematologic cancers exhibit the highest standardized incidence ratios for subsequent NSCLC. 52 These observations highlight the importance of enhanced surveillance in high‐risk cohorts.

Prior radiation exposure

Previous chest radiation therapy for lymphoma, breast cancer, or other malignancies substantially increases lung cancer risk. Radiation‐induced lung cancer typically develops years to decades after exposure; risk is proportional to radiation dose and the irradiated tissue volume. 53 All patients with prior chest radiation should undergo lung cancer screening regardless of other risk factors, beginning 5–10 years after exposure. 54 Given the complex medical intersections of these nonmodifiable factors, optimal lung cancer screening and management mandate close coordination between oncology specialists and primary care teams.

Pathogenesis

The biologic underpinnings of early stage NSCLC must be clarified for clinicians to interpret imaging findings, predict disease behavior, and guide treatment decisions. The two principal pathologic subtypes, lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), arise from distinct cellular origins and molecular evolutionary pathways, requiring divergent clinical management strategies (Figure 2).

The morphologic progression initiates in the bronchial epithelium, advancing through squamous metaplasia, dysplasia, carcinoma in situ, and finally invasive lung squamous cell carcinoma. Molecular drivers, including alterations in TP53, KRAS, KEAP1, STK11, and CDKN2A, can be present early in morphologically normal epithelium. Tumor evolution is characterized by a steadily increasing mutational burden, pronounced PIK3CA amplification, and a significant downregulation of S100A2 expression. In the tumor microenvironment, the density of CD8‐positive tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes progressively declines, with a notable reduction associated with the acquisition of PIK3CA amplification and KEAP1 mutations. In addition, programmed death‐ligand 1 levels are generally higher in lung squamous cell carcinoma compared with lung adenocarcinoma. (B) Lung adenocarcinoma. The progression from normal alveolar epithelium to invasive lung adenocarcinoma follows a defined histologic sequence: atypical adenomatous hyperplasia, adenocarcinoma in situ, minimally invasive adenocarcinoma, and fully invasive disease. Radiographically, this often correlates with a transition from ground‐glass opacities to solid nodules on computed tomography. Early molecular events include the appearance of KRT8‐positive alveolar intermediate cells and pivotal driver mutations (e.g., KRAS, EGFR, TP53, RB1). As the tumor progresses, genomic instability and mutational burden increase, marked by emerging APOBEC signatures and complex pathway alterations (p53/Wnt/Myc, BRAF V600E, STK11, PI3K). Molecular and histologic evolution

LUSC pathogenesis is strongly linked to smoking exposure and follows a histologic progression: metaplasia → dysplasia → carcinoma in situ → invasive carcinoma (Figure 2A). 55 , 56 LUAD follows another well characterized trajectory from atypical adenomatous hyperplasia to invasive adenocarcinoma (Figure 2B), which typically manifests as GGOs on CT. 26 During progression, tumors acquire stepwise genomic alterations. 57 , 58 Although early precursor lesions have a minimal mutational burden, driver mutations such as EGFR, TP53, and RB1 occur early. 59 The later emergence of APOBEC mutational signatures is associated with a high risk of postresection recurrence, 59 , 60 particularly in younger women, 60 identifying patients who may require intensified postsurgical surveillance. 61 Histologic subtypes further dictate management. Micropapillary and solid patterns exhibit greater genomic instability and more frequent targetable BRAF V600E mutations, warranting routine molecular testing to inform adjuvant therapy. 61 These biologic features correlate with radiomic clustering, in which aggressive imaging phenotypes are linked to STK11 alterations and increased recurrence risk. 62 Furthermore, regional epidemiology is critical. 63 Asian populations, particularly female never‐smokers, harbor high rates of EGFR mutations (up to 59.4%), 64 reinforcing the need for comprehensive molecular testing regardless of clinical stage.

Immune microenvironment evolution

Concurrent with genomic changes, progressive immune suppression characterizes early NSCLC development. In LUSC, KEAP1 mutations and PIK3CA amplification correlate with significantly reduced CD8‐positive T‐cell infiltration. 56 , 65 In LUAD, early stage, pure GGOs often maintain an immune‐active microenvironment with robust neoantigen recognition. However, as lesions progress to solid nodules, the milieu becomes increasingly suppressive, 66 , 67 , 68 driven by exhausted T cells, 69 senescent macrophages, and recruited myeloid‐derived suppressor cells. 70 This biologic transition from immune‐active GGOs to immune‐suppressive, solid nodules provides a critical mechanistic rationale for the differential responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors observed in early stage disease.

Eligibility

Major guidelines have converged on expanded eligibility criteria to maximize screening benefit while maintaining efficiency. Major Western guidelines, including the 2021 USPSTF, 74 the 2025 National Comprehensive Cancer Network, 54 and, notably, the 2023 American Cancer Society guidelines, 3 have converged on expanding eligibility to maximize benefit, generally standardizing around a greater than 20 pack‐year threshold while progressively removing restrictions such as the years‐since‐quitting criterion.

Recognizing distinct regional epidemiology, Asian guidelines have structurally recalibrated these Western thresholds. Organizations such as the Asian Thoracic Oncology Research Group and the Chinese Thoracic Society recommend age‐adjusted (e.g., 50–75 years) and risk‐stratified criteria tailored to local data, emphasizing that unique environmental exposures and smoking patterns in Asian cohorts warrant population‐specific screening models. 75 , 76 , 77 Ultimately, regardless of the specific criteria used, regional screening programs should prioritize robust patient enrollment and longitudinal adherence over the pursuit of perfect theoretical risk stratification.

The mortality benefit of LDCT screening is unequivocally established. 4 , 78 , 79 , 80 , 81 , 82 For a comprehensive review of the foundational trials demonstrating this survival advantage, readers are referred to current clinical guidelines, particularly those of the American Cancer Society. 3 , 83 Currently, the principal frontier remains the effective translation of these screening protocols into real‐world clinical practice.

From identification to management

From standardized criteria to risk‐adapted screening

The paradigm of lung cancer screening is rapidly evolving from categorical eligibility toward the widespread implementation of risk‐adapted strategies. Although conventional criteria based on strict age and pack‐year thresholds (e.g., USPSTF 84 and American Cancer Society guidelines 3 , 83 ) established the foundation of early detection, the true lung cancer risk across diverse populations and nonsmoking cohorts remains unspecified. 85

Consequently, the field is shifting toward multivariate risk‐prediction models, which have consistently outperformed conventional criteria in identifying high‐risk individuals. Tools such as the PLCOm2012 model provide a more nuanced assessment by integrating additional clinical factors. 86 Contemporary evidence from China further underscores the utility of this precision approach: by adjusting the starting age according to individualized risk profiles, nonsmokers may reach equivalent risk thresholds 1–14 years later than the standard starting age of 50 years. 87 Implementing a defined risk threshold (e.g., a 6‐year risk ≥1.3%) optimizes resource use and cost‐effectiveness while maintaining robust population coverage. 88 , 89 For patients near traditional eligibility thresholds or with complex risk profiles, integrating validated risk calculators may inform shared decision‐making.

Nodule management

Annual screening serves as the standard interval in most trials; the NELSON trial demonstrated efficacy with increasing screening intervals (1, 3, and 5.5 years after baseline). 85 The BioMILD study showed that combining baseline LDCT with circulating microRNA signatures could stratify participants into distinct risk categories, enabling personalized screening intervals. 90 For individuals with negative baseline scans, future screening frequency should remain dynamic and reflect evolving modifiable risk factors.

Effective nodule management requires systematic, evidence‐based triage to minimize overdiagnosis while ensuring timely intervention. Lung‐RADS 2022 (Lung CT Screening Reporting and Data System) is the predominant classification system in the United States; it provides category‐specific recommendations based on nodule size and imaging characteristics. 91 In parallel, the British Thoracic Society emphasizes volumetric analysis to guide follow‐up decisions. 92 The Chinese expert consensus (2024) provides recommendations tailored to Chinese populations, including integration of AI‐assisted diagnostics within multidisciplinary human–machine teams. 77 , 93 Models such as PanCan offer additional probability assessment by incorporating nodule characteristics (size, morphology, location, number) with patient factors (age, sex, family history, emphysema). 94 Advances in radiomics and machine learning now enable further personalization of nodule management beyond conventional size‐based criteria.

GGOs require particular consideration. The management paradigm for GGOs is shifting from immediate surgical intervention toward a more conservative approach. Three landmark Chinese studies published in 2025 demonstrated that serial CT surveillance is safe and appropriate for selected low‐risk GGO subtypes. 5 , 95 , 96

Incidental nodule detection

Incidental pulmonary nodules identified on imaging represent a critical pathway for early diagnosis. The clinical significance of these nodules in populations outside formal screening age ranges is substantial. For example, the 2‐year cumulative lung cancer incidence reaches 0.79% among adults aged 35–50 years and 6.5% among those older than 80 years. 97 Cancers detected in these age‐ineligible groups for screening often present at more advanced stages, underscoring the need for vigilance regardless of formal eligibility status.

Lung nodule programs provide structured management pathways for incidentally detected nodules. Patients diagnosed through lung nodule programs have demonstrated outcomes comparable to those of LDCT screening programs: 60% are diagnosed at stage I–II, and 42% undergo curative‐intent surgery. 98 Radiology departments should establish referral pathways to pulmonology or thoracic surgery for nodules that require surveillance or intervention.

Integration of smoking cessation

Screening programs provide a critical opportunity for smoking cessation intervention. Smoking cessation after an early stage lung cancer diagnosis increases median OS in a dose‐response manner. 38 , 39 However, the interaction between screening and cessation is complex. Although trial participation generally promotes cessation, 99 a negative baseline scan paradoxically may produce a license to smoke effect that diminishes motivation to quit. 100 Consequently, high‐quality screening programs must systematically integrate evidence‐based cessation support as a mandatory component of the care continuum.

Toward health equity

Regional, sex, and socioeconomic considerations

Effective screening implementation relies on specialized professional teams and high‐performance equipment. Substantial disparities in lung cancer screening access, use, and outcomes persist across demographic groups. Efforts to address these inequities must remain a central priority in screening program implementation.

Globally, the translation of clinical evidence into public health practice is suboptimal, such that fewer than 5% of eligible individuals participate in formal screening programs. 101 In the United States, although 90.8% of screened individuals met USPSTF criteria, only 22.3% completed repeat screening within 11–15 months; this proportion increased to just 34.3% at 24 months. 102 Annual screening adherence typically declines from 80%–90% after the initial screen to 50%–70% by year 3 without active retention strategies. 103 Multifactorial barriers drive this attrition, including limited clinician awareness, radiation‐related anxiety, concerns regarding overdiagnosis, and socioeconomic constraints. 104 Successful initiatives often use multifaceted personal outreach strategies. For example, the Yorkshire Lung Screening Trial achieved a 50.8% response rate by leveraging general practitioner recommendations, issuing follow‐up reminders, and engaging local communities. 105

Sex‐specific biologic and epidemiologic differences warrant a more inclusive screening approach. Women develop lung cancer with lower smoking exposure relative to men and demonstrate higher incidence rates in younger age groups (30–49 years). 106 Accordingly, pack‐year thresholds primarily derived from male cohorts may systematically underestimate the risk in women. The 2021 USPSTF guideline modifications, which lowered the pack‐year requirement from 30 to 20 pack‐years, substantially increased the proportion of eligible women. 107

Ethnic disparities also present a major barrier to health equity. The 2021 USPSTF criteria reduced disparities in eligibility, with similar proportions of White and Black patients meeting criteria (65% vs. 63%, respectively). 108 Screening utilization remains lower in ethnic minority communities, and uninsured populations demonstrate extremely low screening rates. 84 In addition, the rural–urban divide—exacerbated by regional shortages in screening capacity—is correlated with higher mortality rates in rural populations. 106 , 109 International models, including the risk‐based program in Ontario (Canada) and the SUMMIT study in London (United Kingdom), demonstrate that equitable implementation is achievable through targeted outreach and ethnically diverse recruitment strategies. 110 , 111 As evidenced by the French DEP KP80 study, initial success must be sustained through robust, long‐term adherence frameworks. 112

Screening programs should track participation and outcomes across demographic subgroups while implementing targeted outreach for underserved populations. Culturally appropriate messaging and involvement of community health workers may improve program uptake.

Special populations

Never‐smokers

The role of LDCT screening in never‐smokers continues to be an area of active clinical debate because the risk–benefit ratio substantially differs from the value observed in heavy smokers. 113 The TALENT study enrolled never‐smokers or minimal smokers (<10 pack‐years) aged 55–75 years with additional risk factors, primarily a family history of lung cancer; it achieved a detection rate of 2.6%, with adenocarcinoma representing 99.7% of detected cases. 114 Among never‐smokers, a family history of lung cancer represents the strongest risk factor, followed by female sex and age older than 60 years. 115 , 116 Concerns regarding overdiagnosis in screening never‐smokers require careful consideration. After promotion of LDCT screening among predominantly nonsmoking women in Taiwan, China, early stage lung cancer incidence increased more than six‐fold, whereas late‐stage incidence remained stable, suggesting overdiagnosis. 117 East Asian studies reveal a significant increase in lung cancer overdiagnosis, evidenced by surging rates of overdiagnosed LUAD in China 118 and a dramatic rise in self‐selected LDCT screenings among low‐risk individuals in South Korea. 119 Consequently, universal screening of never‐smokers without identifiable, independent risk factors is not currently recommended. Lung cancer screening also faces challenges of high false‐positive rates, which have been mitigated by Lung‐RADS and show further promise for reduction through AI‐powered radiomics. 120

Cancer survivors and second primary lung cancer

Patients with a prior history of cancer face an elevated risk of second primary malignancies. An analysis of the National Lung Screening Trial cohort demonstrated that participants with a prior history of cancer had higher age‐adjusted cancer detection rates at baseline relative to those without such a history. 121 Through improvements in long‐term survival after primary lung cancer, second primary lung cancer has become a key clinical challenge, affecting up to one in six survivors within 5 years. 122 This risk underscores the importance of lifelong annual LDCT surveillance, which effectively integrates posttreatment follow‐up with ongoing lung cancer screening. 123 Any new pulmonary nodule that appears after a period of radiographic stability warrants comprehensive evaluation as a potential new primary malignancy rather than a presumed recurrence. Smoking‐cessation counseling and pharmacologic support should be incorporated into each follow‐up visit for patients who continue tobacco use.

Significant incidental findings

LDCT screening frequently detects clinically important abnormalities unrelated to lung cancer. In the National Lung Screening Trial, 33.8% of participants had at least one significant incidental finding; 89.1% of those participants required clinical follow‐up. 124 Common findings were emphysema, coronary artery calcification, and masses in other organs (kidney, liver, adrenal gland, breast). The detection of coronary artery calcification enables integrated cardiovascular risk assessment in conjunction with lung cancer screening. 125 Management protocols established by the American College of Radiology 126 and European societies 127 provide evidence‐based recommendations for interstitial lung abnormalities, emphysema, coronary calcification, and thyroid nodules. Standardized reporting and management frameworks minimize harm while capturing the benefits of early detection of other clinically significant diseases. 128

Cost‐effectiveness

For population‐level implementation, cost‐effectiveness remains a primary policy driver. The 2021 USPSTF criteria yielded a mean incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio of $72,564 per quality‐adjusted life‐year gained compared with the 2013 criteria, well below the commonly accepted threshold for high‐value care. 129 Reduction of the minimum smoking threshold from 30 to 20 pack‐years while maintaining annual screening was associated with greater cost savings, regardless of the specific screening modality used. 130 These findings suggest that broader eligibility paradoxically enhances cost‐effectiveness by facilitating stage shift; the high costs of treating advanced‐stage NSCLC are offset by higher cure rates and lower treatment burdens associated with early detection.

Beyond conventional categorical criteria, such as age and smoking history, individualized risk model–based strategies demonstrate superior cost‐effectiveness. These models generate more quality‐adjusted life‐years at lower overall cost by concentrating screening resources on individuals with the highest absolute risk. 89 , 131 Integration of biomarker augmentation—such as microRNA signature classifiers—has emerged as a promising cost‐saving strategy. 130

Although some meta‐analyses have questioned the absolute lifetime gain from CT screening, 132 these findings often reflect limited follow‐up and may underestimate the long‐term mortality reduction achieved through early detection. A more effective limitation comprises the geographic generalizability of existing models. Most cost‐effectiveness analyses have been developed in Western populations and may not fully translate to Asian settings, in which lung cancer epidemiology, health care costs, and screening infrastructure substantially differ.

Screening for the future

Blood‐based and other minimally invasive biomarkers have substantial potential to enhance lung cancer screening by improving risk stratification, reducing unnecessary imaging, and extending screening benefits to populations inadequately served by current smoking‐based criteria.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and epigenetic signatures represent leading approaches for early cancer detection. Methylation‐based assays have demonstrated promising performance in case–control studies. 133 , 134 , 135 The SUMMIT trial enrolled 25,000 high‐risk individuals in London (United Kingdom) with ctDNA‐based screening. 111 MicroRNA profiling has undergone extensive validation in Italian LDCT screening cohorts. The BioMILD study demonstrated that integration of baseline LDCT findings with circulating microRNA signatures can stratify participants into distinct risk categories, enabling personalized screening intervals. 90 Additional studies have confirmed the utility of microRNA panels for enhanced screening candidate selection within high‐risk populations. 136 Plasma proteomics represents an emerging and promising strategy for early lung cancer detection. 137 Proof‐of‐concept studies have also demonstrated the feasibility of circulating protein panels for early stage detection. 138

Novel biomarkers that target the respiratory system offer opportunities for noninvasive sampling. Breath analysis that detects volatile organic compounds has demonstrated encouraging results in pilot studies; it may enable completely noninvasive screening if validated in larger cohorts. 139 Sputum biomarkers—including DNA methylation, microRNA, and proteomic signatures—provide direct sampling of airway epithelium at risk for malignant transformation. 140 Orphan noncoding RNAs derived from serum samples and analyzed with generative AI models have demonstrated promising early performance and represent an innovative application of machine learning to biomarker discovery. 141

CONTEMPORARY MANAGEMENT

Diagnosis

Accurate diagnosis and precise staging are foundational to early stage NSCLC management. These processes directly inform surgical approach, systemic therapy selection, and prognostic counseling. The diagnostic process has evolved into a multimodal, multidisciplinary endeavor that integrates advanced imaging, tissue‐based pathology, and molecular profiling to achieve histologic confirmation and identification of therapeutic targets. Not all CT‐detected suspicious nodules are malignant; the differential diagnosis includes granulomas, hamartomas, intrapulmonary lymph nodes, and organizing pneumonia.

Imaging evaluation

Imaging serves as the cornerstone for detection, characterization, and initial staging of suspected lung malignancies, including LDCT in screening settings. 91 , 142 High‐resolution CT with thin‐slice reconstruction enables precise assessment of nodule margins, internal architecture, and relationships to adjacent structures, which are critical for distinguishing indolent GGO‐predominant lesions from aggressive solid tumors. 143

For comprehensive staging workup, 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (PET)‐CT provides essential metabolic information regarding the primary tumor and tumor‐draining lymph nodes. 144 PET‐CT demonstrates high sensitivity for the detection of occult mediastinal and distant metastases that may upstage disease and alter management. False‐positive results occur in inflammatory and infectious conditions, whereas false‐negative findings may arise in tumors with low metabolic activity. 145 When brain metastasis is suspected, or when patients present with neurologic symptoms, magnetic resonance imaging provides superior soft tissue contrast and should be incorporated into an evaluation for possible curative‐intent therapy. 146 , 147

AI‐powered, computer‐aided detection systems and deep‐learning algorithms achieve highly sensitive pulmonary nodule detection and characterization, 148 , 149 functioning as powerful complementary tools that enhance diagnostic efficiency.

Preoperative pathologic evaluation

Histopathologic confirmation is the gold standard for lung cancer diagnosis. Pathologic diagnosis and lymph node evaluation are recommended for all patients with suspected lung cancer when feasible. 146 Selection of the tissue‐acquisition method should be guided by tumor location, patient performance status, and the need to obtain sufficient tissue for molecular profiling. For peripheral, small tumors (≤3 cm) with radiologically negative lymph nodes (cN0), pretreatment pathologic diagnosis is optional, and surgical resection may proceed with intraoperative frozen‐section analysis when clinical suspicion is high. 150 Invasive mediastinal staging remains recommended for central tumors, tumors >3 cm, or patients with PET‐positive mediastinal lymph nodes. 146

For preoperative pathologic evaluation of peripheral nodules, CT‐guided percutaneous transthoracic needle biopsy offers high diagnostic yield with acceptable complication rates. For central tumors or those accompanied by mediastinal lymphadenopathy, endobronchial and/or endoscopic ultrasound‐guided fine‐needle aspiration has largely supplanted mediastinoscopy as the first‐line invasive staging modality. 151 , 152 , 153 Nevertheless, systematic endoscopic mediastinal staging has demonstrated superior diagnostic precision. 154

Histopathologic diagnosis

For early stage NSCLC, surgical specimens provide comprehensive insight into disease characteristics. Histologic evaluation follows the 2021 World Health Organization classification of thoracic tumors, which emphasizes a tiered diagnostic approach: morphology first, with support from immunohistochemistry, followed by molecular techniques. 155 The fundamental diagnostic task is to distinguish primary lung tumors from metastatic lesions; this is followed by differentiation of small cell lung cancer from NSCLC. Morphology alone enables this distinction in greater than 75% of patients. 156 Within NSCLC, morphologic subtyping includes LUAD, LUSC, and other histologic entities such as large cell lung carcinoma.

When morphology is inconclusive, a minimal immunohistochemistry panel is recommended to preserve tissue for molecular testing. Current best practice designates thyroid transcription factor‐1 as the key marker for adenocarcinoma and p40 (ΔNp63) as the optimal marker for squamous cell carcinoma. 157 , 158 Napsin A serves as a useful secondary marker for adenocarcinoma, whereas p63 may support squamous differentiation. 159

For resected invasive nonmucinous adenocarcinomas, the 2021 World Health Organization classification incorporates the IASLC grading system, which stratifies tumors into three prognostic grades based on the predominant pattern and the presence of high‐grade components (solid, micropapillary, or complex glandular patterns ≥20%). 160 This system demonstrates superior prognostic stratification efficacy. 161 , 162 In addition, tumor spread through air spaces—defined as micropapillary clusters, solid nests, or single tumor cells beyond the tumor edge—represents an independent predictor of poor prognosis in stage I NSCLC and has been recommended for inclusion as a histologic descriptor in the ninth edition TNM classification. 163 , 164

Molecular diagnosis

Comprehensive biomarker testing is essential to guide precision treatment and should be initiated as early as possible. Essential testing in early stage NSCLC includes EGFR mutation analysis (exon 19 deletions, exon 21 L858R) in stage IB–IIIA nonsquamous NSCLC, 165 , 166 ALK fusion testing in stage II–IIIA nonsquamous NSCLC, 167 and programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) expression assessment by immunohistochemistry in all patients with stage II–III disease. 168 , 169

Although guidelines support focused gene testing, the routine use of next‐generation sequencing panels in stage II–III nonsquamous NSCLC represents a pragmatic and forward‐looking strategy. 170 , 171 As actionable alterations extend beyond EGFR and ALK to include ROS1, RET, BRAF V600E, MET exon 14–skipping, and others, comprehensive genomic profiling at the time of diagnosis positions patients to access targeted therapies if clinically indicated.

Biomarkers for management

Liquid biopsy, particularly ctDNA analysis, has emerged as a valuable noninvasive adjunct. Fragmentomic analysis of cell‐free DNA can differentiate malignant from benign lung diseases, offering an alternative for patients unable to undergo invasive procedures. 172 , 173 , 174 ctDNA also demonstrates substantial prognostic and monitoring value. In the neoadjuvant setting, ctDNA clearance after therapy is correlated with pathologic complete responses (pCRs) and improved outcomes. 175 , 176 , 177 Detection of ctDNA after curative‐intent treatment has emerged as a strong prognostic marker, identifying patients with a high recurrence risk, often months before radiographic detection. 173 Detection sensitivity and assay time points remain unstandardized; management strategies for ctDNA‐positive but imaging‐negative patients are undefined. ctDNA‐guided treatment escalation or de‐escalation outside clinical trial settings is not currently recommended. 146 , 178 Prospective interventional trials are still needed to establish its predictive value in guiding treatment strategies, and the effect of driver gene mutation status and regional differences must be fully considered in trial design. 179

Therapy

Surgery

Surgical resection with curative intent constitutes the cornerstone of treatment for stage I–II NSCLC. Over the past decade, surgical management has evolved from a uniform, lobectomy‐centric paradigm toward an individualized strategy that incorporates tumor biology, anatomic location, nodal status, and patient comorbidities (Figure 3).

Anatomic lobectomy with systematic lymph node evaluation has represented the gold standard since 1995, when the Lung Cancer Study Group demonstrated its superiority over limited resection with respect to local recurrence. 180 For stage I tumors >2 cm, solid‐predominant lesions, and all stage II disease, lobectomy is the preferred approach to ensure complete removal of the primary tumor and intrapulmonary lymphatic pathways. 146 Stage II NSCLC (T2b tumor classification [T2b] and negative lymph nodes [N0] or T1–T2N1) presents a distinct therapeutic challenge requiring multimodal management. Although lobectomy remains the surgical standard, integration of neoadjuvant or perioperative chemoimmunotherapy has transformed this landscape. Trials such as CheckMate 816 have demonstrated robust pathologic response rates and improved survival outcomes. 176 , 181 Consequently, surgical intervention for stage II disease is increasingly coordinated within a multidisciplinary framework to optimize treatment sequencing.

The role of sublobar resection has been redefined by two pivotal phase 3 trials, although long‐term data suggest caution regarding patient selection:

Thus current evidence supports a risk‐stratified approach. Lobectomy remains the standard for medically fit patients with solid‐predominant tumors to maximize local control. Segmentectomy is preferred for patients with compromised pulmonary function or those with GGO‐predominant tumors. When segmentectomy is performed, technical precision is essential to mitigate recurrence risk. This principle has led to the concept of anatomic partial lobectomy, which emphasizes meticulous dissection of segmental bronchovascular structures and achievement of adequate resection margins (≥2 cm or a margin‐to‐tumor ratio ≥1.0) to replicate the oncologic radicality of lobectomy within a sublobar framework. 8

For small, peripheral, GGO‐dominant nodules (≤2 cm, CTR ≤0.25), the JCOG0804 trial confirmed that sublobar resection provides exceptional recurrence‐free survival (99.7%), establishing it as the standard of care for this indolent subset. 184

For central tumors involving the lobar or main bronchus, sleeve lobectomy offers an oncologically sound alternative to pneumonectomy. Contemporary meta‐analyses report 5‐year survival rates of 50%–65%, comparable to pneumonectomy but with significantly lower operative mortality and superior quality of life. 185 This procedure should be considered a first‐line option for central stage I–II tumors whenever anatomically feasible.

Minimally invasive surgery has replaced open thoracotomy as the standard approach for early stage NSCLC, with widespread adoption of video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) and robotic‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS). 186 , 187 The UK‐based VIOLET trial demonstrated that VATS lobectomy provides oncologic outcomes equivalent to those of open thoracotomy while substantially reducing postoperative pain and hospital length of stay. 188 Even in stage II disease with N1 involvement, VATS achieves nodal upstaging rates and long‐term survival comparable to the results of open surgery. 189 Although the use of RATS continues to increase, meta‐analyses indicate that outcomes—including complete (R0) resection rates and nodal upstaging—are comparable to those of VATS. 190 RATS may confer technical advantages in complex operative settings, including reconstructive procedures and resections performed after neoadjuvant therapy.

Accurate pathologic nodal staging is a fundamental aspect of adjuvant therapy decision‐making. Although the American College of Surgeons Oncology Group Z0030 trial suggested that systematic nodal sampling is equivalent to complete dissection for clinically N0 disease, 191 , 192 current guidelines emphasize rigorous evaluation. To achieve R0 resection, IASLC criteria mandate assessment of at least six lymph node stations, including three mediastinal stations. 193 For stage II disease, formal ipsilateral mediastinal systematic nodal dissection (SND) is recommended. Because occult N2 disease is identified in approximately 5%–15% of patients with clinically staged N0–N1 disease, 194 comprehensive nodal evaluation remains a critical guiding factor for adjuvant systemic therapy. In contrast, for indolent tumors with low nodal involvement risk—such as GGO‐predominant lesions—routine mediastinal SND may not be necessary based on emerging clinical evidence. 96 , 195

Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways have standardized perioperative management. By optimizing analgesia and promoting early mobilization, ERAS protocols substantially reduce pulmonary complications. 196 In addition, preoperative pulmonary rehabilitation and smoking cessation represent essential interventions to maximize functional reserve. 197

Future refinements in surgical precision rely on advanced technologies. Innovations include AI‐driven imaging for preoperative planning, 198 intraoperative indocyanine green fluorescence for delineation of intersegmental planes, 199 , 200 and modern localization techniques for small, nonpalpable nodules 143 —all of which further enhance the accuracy of sublobar resections.

Ablation therapy

Among patients with early stage NSCLC, thermal ablation—including microwave ablation, cryoablation, and radiofrequency ablation—has emerged as an important alternative for those who are medically inoperable or require lung‐preserving strategies. 201 Clinical efficacy has demonstrated comparability to stereotactic body radiotherapy in similar populations, particularly in stage I disease or tumors measuring <5 cm. 202 , 203 , 204 National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines recommend ablation primarily for lesions <3 cm in greatest dimension or selected stage IA tumors. 34 A large‐scale trial evaluating transbronchial radiofrequency ablation with saline microperfusion demonstrated superior efficacy in GGOs relative to solid nodules, 205 suggesting that transbronchial approaches are particularly suitable for early stage GGO lesions.

Ablation is distinguished by its capacity to preserve lung function, a critical consideration for patients with multiple primary lung cancers or multiple GGOs. Unlike VATS, which may result in meaningful functional loss, ablation represents a lung‐sparing strategy that minimizes physiologic and psychological burden. 206 In patients with stage IA disease, radiofrequency ablation has been well tolerated, without clinically significant declines in global health status or pulmonary function. 202

Along with local control, ablation may augment systemic antitumor immunity. Investigations of cryoablation indicate that it can reprogram the tumor microenvironment, increasing CD8‐positive T‐cell infiltration and proinflammatory cytokine production through activation of the STING (stimulator of interferon genes)–dependent type I interferon pathway. 207 This mechanism suggests a synergistic effect when cryoablation is combined with PD‐1 blockade immunotherapy; it may be possible to overcome resistance in nonablated, distant tumors. 208

Radiotherapy

Stereotactic ablative radiotherapy

Stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR), also known as stereotactic body radiotherapy, has been established as the standard of care for medically inoperable stage I NSCLC. By delivering high‐dose, conformal radiation in a limited number of fractions (typically three to five), SABR achieves local control rates exceeding 90%—outcomes historically unattainable with conventional radiotherapy. 209 , 210

For medically inoperable patients with peripheral T1–T2N0 tumors, SABR provides superior outcomes compared with conventional fractionation. The phase 3 CHISEL trial confirmed that SABR significantly reduces local failure rates while maintaining OS. 211 A biologically effective dose ≥100 grays (Gy) is critical because it shows consistent correlations with improved local control and survival outcomes. 212 , 213 Caution is warranted in patients with interstitial lung disease. Although SABR is feasible in this population, it confers a substantial risk of severe radiation pneumonitis (grade ≥3) and grade 5 toxicity (approximately 7.7%), thus requiring careful risk–benefit stratification and strict adherence to dose constraints. 214 , 215

Tumor location strongly influences treatment‐related toxicity. The Radiation Therapy Oncology Group trial RTOG 0813 established that, for central tumors (within 2 cm of the proximal bronchial tree), a regimen of 50–60 Gy delivered in five fractions is effective, achieving a 2‐year local control rate of approximately 88%, however, grade 5 toxicity was observed. 216 Ultracentral tumors—defined as lesions directly abutting the central airways or esophagus—carry greater risk of fatal hemorrhage or severe airway and esophageal toxicity. The LungTech trial reported high local control (81.5%) but a notable incidence of grade ≥3 late toxicity (19.4%), underscoring the need for risk‐adapted, hypofractionated radiotherapy (e.g., from eight to 15 fractions) rather than aggressive hypofractionation for these lesions. 217

For operable patients who decline surgery, SABR represents a reasonable alternative. The pooled STARS/ROSEL analysis suggested comparable OS between SABR and lobectomy, although interpretation was affected by small sample sizes and early trial closure. 218 Long‐term results from the revised single‐arm STARS trial demonstrated a 5‐year OS rate of 87%, 219 comparable to historical outcomes after VATS lobectomy. Pending definitive results from ongoing randomized trials (e.g., VALOR, POST‐ILCOR), surgical resection remains the preferred standard of care for operable candidates because of lower regional recurrence rates and accurate pathologic nodal staging. 220 , 221

The combination of SABR with immunotherapy represents a promising therapeutic frontier. Early phase studies suggest that SABR can serve as an immunomodulator that enhances the efficacy of checkpoint inhibitors. Two recent randomized phase 3 trials, the KEYNOTE‐867 and Southwest Oncology Group/NRG S1914 trials, failed to confirm this finding. 222 Further phase 3 validation is required before this strategy can be routinely adopted in clinical practice. 223 , 224

Postoperative radiotherapy

For completely resected, stage I–II NSCLC (N0–N1), postoperative radiotherapy (PORT) is not recommended. Historically, meta‐analyses indicated that PORT might be detrimental to survival in early stage disease. 225 Two recent modern phase 3 trials conducted in higher stage populations (primarily stage III) have definitively clarified this issue: Lung ART (Europe) 226 and PORT‐C (China). 227

In contrast, for patients with positive resection margins (R1/R2), PORT remains a standard recommendation to maximize local control. Retrospective analyses from the National Cancer Database indicate that PORT is associated with improved OS in the setting of incomplete resection. 228 When adjuvant chemotherapy is also indicated, sequential administration of chemotherapy followed by PORT is generally preferred over concurrent chemoradiation to minimize toxicity. 229

Adjuvant therapy

For patients who undergo upfront resection, the primary goal of adjuvant therapy is eradication of micrometastatic disease. Risk stratification based on stage and histology remains the initial step; however, molecular characterization has emerged as the principal determinant of therapeutic strategy.

Chemotherapy

Cisplatin‐based doublet chemotherapy constitutes the foundational adjuvant treatment for resected stage II and III NSCLC. Although the survival benefit is modest—approximately 5% at 5 years—it provides the essential platform upon which modern immunotherapies are constructed. 235 Chemotherapy is not routinely recommended for earlier disease; it should be reserved for selected high‐risk cases (e.g., tumors ≥4 cm or patients with visceral pleural invasion), reflecting the consensus that treatment‐related toxicity often outweighs marginal survival benefit in this population. 236

Targeted therapy

Comprehensive genomic profiling is mandatory for all patients with nonsquamous, early stage NSCLC. For patients with resected, EGFR‐mutant (exon 19 deletion/L858R), stage IB–IIIA NSCLC, adjuvant osimertinib is the established standard of care. The ADAURA trial fundamentally changed clinical practice by demonstrating that targeted therapy significantly prolongs OS compared with placebo, supporting a 3‐year treatment course. 237 This strategy is supported by regional phase 3 data, including the EVIDENCE trial, which confirmed the superiority of EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors over chemotherapy in Asian populations with stage II–IIIA disease. 238 For ALK‐rearranged tumors, the ALINA trial established adjuvant alectinib (2 years) as the preferred standard of care, replacing chemotherapy based on superior disease‐free survival and central nervous system protection. 167

Immunotherapy

Among patients without actionable driver mutations, adjuvant immune checkpoint inhibitors administered after chemotherapy have become the standard of care for stage II–IIIA disease. Clinical decision‐making is largely guided by PD‐L1 expression. The IMpower010 (atezolizumab) and KEYNOTE‐091 (pembrolizumab) trials established that adjuvant immunotherapy significantly reduces recurrence risk. Although survival benefit is observed across populations, the magnitude of effect is greatest in patients with PD‐L1–positive tumors (tumor cells ≥1%), particularly those with high expression (≥50%). 239 , 240

Neoadjuvant and perioperative therapy

One of the most compelling advances has been expansion of systemic therapy into the preoperative setting, representing a paradigm shift in early stage disease control.

Neoadjuvant‐only

For patients with resectable, stage IB–IIIA NSCLC, neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy has emerged as a preferred option over chemotherapy alone. The landmark CheckMate 816 trial established that the addition of nivolumab to chemotherapy significantly increases pCR rates and event‐free survival without delaying surgery. 176 The NeoADAURA trial demonstrated that neoadjuvant osimertinib, with or without chemotherapy, yields high major pathologic response rates (approximately 25%) relative to chemotherapy monotherapy, although long‐term survival data remain immature. 241

Perioperative

For patients with higher disease burden (e.g., node‐positive disease or large tumors), the perioperative model—typically comprising preoperative chemoimmunotherapy followed by surgery and adjuvant immunotherapy—has become an established standard of care. A series of phase three trials validated this strategy, demonstrating consistent improvements in event‐free survival and, in certain cases, OS. The KEYNOTE‐671 trial (pembrolizumab) demonstrated a statistically significant OS benefit compared with chemotherapy alone, establishing a high benchmark for this approach. 169 The observed benefit is reinforced by findings from the CheckMate 77T (nivolumab) and AEGEAN (durvalumab) trials. 242 , 243 Trials such as Neotorch (toripalimab) and RATIONALE‐315 (tislelizumab) have demonstrated robust efficacy in Asian populations and across diverse histologies, including squamous cell carcinoma, validating the perioperative strategy as a globally applicable standard. 244 , 245

Neoadjuvant versus perioperative?

Given that multiple regimens are approved for early stage NSCLC, selection between a neoadjuvant‐only approach and a perioperative approach now requires individualized, patient‐centered discussion, especially since no head‐to‐head trials have been performed. The perioperative model is frequently prioritized for patients with high‐risk baseline presentations (e.g., multi‐station N2 disease, T3/T4 tumors) or those with residual viable tumor post‐resection, 242 where maximizing systemic disease control is paramount. Conversely, for patients who successfully achieve a pCR after induction therapy, omitting the adjuvant phase offers a rational treatment deintensification option to spare them from extended toxicities. 246 Multidisciplinary team consensus remains essential to balance the oncologic benefit against the risks of extended treatment‐related toxicity.

Follow‐up

Structured posttreatment surveillance is essential for patients with early stage NSCLC who have undergone curative‐intent therapy. The objectives are threefold: (1) detection of recurrences amenable to salvage therapy, (2) identification of second primary lung cancers, and (3) monitoring and management of treatment‐related late toxicities. Surveillance strategies should be risk‐adapted according to the patient’s initial tumor stage, molecular profile, and treatments received.

Recurrence monitoring

LDCT serves as the cornerstone of posttherapy surveillance. Although the IFCT‐0302 trial did not demonstrate an overall survival benefit for CT‐based surveillance compared with chest radiography alone, CT detects significantly more early recurrences and second primary lung cancers. 247 , 248 PET‐CT is not recommended for routine surveillance in asymptomatic patients but should be reserved for the characterization of indeterminate CT findings. Brain magnetic resonance imaging is indicated only for patients with neurologic symptoms, rather than as a routine screening modality.

Recurrence risk is highest within the first 2–3 years after curative therapy; it persists up to 5 years posttreatment. 249 Follow‐up intensity should be tailored to disease stage and treatments administered:

Stage IA (R0 resection, no adjuvant therapy): LDCT every 6 months during years 1–3 posttreatment, followed by annual surveillance thereafter 239 , 240 ;

Stage IA (R0 resection, no adjuvant therapy): LDCT every 6 months during years 1–3 posttreatment, followed by annual surveillance thereafter 239 , 240 ;

Stage IB–II (R0 resection with adjuvant chemotherapy with or without immunotherapy): Clinical evaluation and LDCT every 3–6 months during years 1–2, every 6 months during years 3–4, and annually thereafter 239 , 240 ; and

Stage IB–II (R0 resection with adjuvant chemotherapy with or without immunotherapy): Clinical evaluation and LDCT every 3–6 months during years 1–2, every 6 months during years 3–4, and annually thereafter 239 , 240 ; and

Stage IB–II with adjuvant targeted therapy (EGFR/ALK‐positive): During active treatment, follow‐up every 6 months is generally sufficient, given the substantial reduction in recurrence risk associated with targeted therapy. 165 , 237 , 241 After treatment discontinuation, more intensive surveillance is warranted—every 3 months during years 1–2 postdiscontinuation, every 6 months during years 3–4, and annually thereafter—to facilitate early detection of late recurrences.

Toxicity management

Comprehensive survivorship care warrants proactive surveillance for treatment‐related late effects; it requires a multidisciplinary approach involving thoracic surgery, medical oncology, pulmonology, and primary care. Long‐term sequelae of local therapies include persistent pulmonary function decline and chronic postthoracotomy pain syndromes after surgery, 250 as well as pulmonary fibrosis and potential cardiac complications in patients who receive PORT or stereotactic body radiotherapy. 251 Systemic therapies carry distinct late‐toxicity profiles: chemotherapy is associated with chronic peripheral neuropathy and cumulative cisplatin‐related ototoxicity, 252 whereas targeted therapies require monitoring for class‐specific effects, such as EGFR inhibitor–related dermatologic toxicity 253 and ALK inhibitor–associated hepatotoxicity or creatine phosphokinase elevation. 254 Particular vigilance is essential for immunotherapy recipients because immune‐related adverse events—including endocrinopathies and pneumonitis—may manifest months to years after treatment discontinuation. 255 , 256

Quality of life

Managing perioperative rehabilitation

Surgical resection for early stage NSCLC produces predictable functional challenges that require proactive management. Pain burden substantially varies by surgical approach: pneumonectomy results in more severe and prolonged pain than limited resection, 257 , 258 whereas VATS is associated with significantly less moderate‐to‐severe pain than anterolateral thoracotomy. 259 These differences underscore the importance of surgical technique selection when multiple approaches are feasible. Comprehensive, multimodal analgesia—including antidepressants, anticonvulsants, nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, and judicious opioid use—can effectively address both acute postoperative pain and chronic cancer‐related pain. 260

Pulmonary function inevitably declines after lung resection and may be further compromised by adjuvant therapy. 261 Pulmonary function testing should guide treatment planning, surgical eligibility assessment, risk stratification, and postoperative monitoring. Preoperative pulmonary rehabilitation—particularly structured exercise programs—maximizes functional reserve before surgery, accelerates postoperative recovery, 197 reduces postoperative pulmonary complications, and shortens hospital length of stay.

For medically inoperable patients or those who decline surgical management of early stage disease, SABR provides quality‐of‐life outcomes comparable to those of surgical cohorts despite worse baseline health status, 262 supporting its role as a legitimate first‐line option rather than solely salvage therapy.

Systematic quality‐of‐life assessment

The European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Core 30 Questionnaire (QLQ‐C30) and its 13‐question lung cancer‐specific module (QLQ‐LC13) are widely used instruments for measuring health‐related quality of life. 258 , 263 As the treatment landscape shifts toward immunotherapy and targeted agents, these tools are receiving updates to capture the unique symptom profiles associated with modern systemic therapies. A critical challenge lies in translating research instruments into routine clinical practice. Although patient‐reported outcomes provide substantial research value, their systematic integration into clinical monitoring remains inconsistent. Ongoing initiatives that explore digital platforms and automated symptom tracking face implementation barriers, including clinician time constraints, challenges with electronic health record integration, and disparities in patient digital literacy.

Psychosocial support and mental health

Lung cancer has the highest prevalence of psychological disorders among all cancer types. 264 Patients with early stage disease encounter unique challenges, including fear of recurrence despite potentially curative treatment, adjustment to physical limitations, existential concerns regarding mortality, and—in some cases—shame or self‐blame related to smoking history. 265 Psycho–behavioral interventions—such as mindfulness‐based therapies and cognitive behavioral therapy—help patients develop coping strategies for treatment‐related anxiety, improve emotional regulation, and support smoking cessation. 266 , 267 Clinicians should maintain awareness of the psychological dimensions of care throughout treatment and facilitate timely referral for mental health support when distress arises.

Physical rehabilitation strategies

Structured exercise and respiratory training are indispensable for holistic recovery. Diaphragmatic breathing and pursed‐lip breathing techniques optimize ventilatory efficiency and alleviate dyspnea. 268 General exercise training is safe and effective in terms of improving functional outcomes. 269 Modern fast‐track protocols incorporate high‐intensity endurance and strength training even for patients with recent surgical treatment 270 , 271 ; optimal training parameters remain under investigation.

Traditional Chinese exercise modalities, such as tai chi and baduanjin, offer culturally relevant, low‐impact alternatives that integrate physical movement with breathing techniques and mindfulness, addressing both physical deconditioning and psychological distress. 272 Despite strong evidence supporting their efficacy, implementation has been limited by barriers, including lack of insurance coverage, geographic access constraints, and insufficient clinician awareness. Establishment of standardized referral pathways, with attention to culturally appropriate rehabilitation modalities, comprises an important opportunity for quality improvement.

NAVIGATING THE FUTURE

The management of early stage NSCLC is positioned at a critical inflection point. Transition from clinical remission to a true biologic cure requires movement beyond empirical models to address persistent structural and scientific barriers through a multidimensional life‐cycle framework.

GGO management: Refining the window for clinical intervention

The management of GGOs requires a shift from size‐based surveillance to biology‐driven intervention. The objective is identification of the precise interception window—the point when preinvasive lesions acquire invasive potential. The JCOG1211 study elucidates management strategies for GGOs by focusing on tumor size and the proportion of the ground‐glass component. 273 Integration of radiomics with molecular clocks may enable distinction between indolent nodules and lesions that require immediate resection, thus avoiding the morbidity associated with premature surgery and the risks inherent in delayed intervention for aggressive subsolid tumors.

Precision surgery: Digital integration and selective de‐escalation

Modern thoracic surgery must reconcile technical precision with oncologic radicality. Integration of sublobar resection with digital platforms—including augmented reality navigation, three‐dimensional printed templates, and real‐time fluorescence imaging—enhances anatomic accuracy and ensures adequate surgical margins while facilitating lung‐sparing procedures. Along with technical execution, surgical precision encompasses rational de‐escalation of lymph node interventions. For GGO‐dominant tumors (consolidation‐to‐tumor ratio ≤0.5), omission of mediastinal SND is reportedly noninferior to routine SND. 96 Future practice will likely incorporate molecularly enhanced staging to confirm nodal status and optimize regional disease control.

Multimodal risk stratification beyond conventional TNM staging

Although the TNM system provides an anatomic framework, it often fails to address tumor heterogeneity. A revised paradigm is required—one that transcends anatomic staging through AI‐enabled integration of multimodal data, including radiomics, histopathologic features, and multi‐omics biomarkers. 274 , 275 The development of a subtype‐driven classification system may more effectively address management gaps in nonadenocarcinoma subtypes and ensure that therapeutic intensity aligns with biologic risk rather than tumor size alone.

Perioperative optimization: Timing, sequencing, and molecular guidance

The perioperative revolution requires clarification of the optimal sequence and patient selection for neoadjuvant, adjuvant, and sandwich therapeutic strategies. Biomarker‐driven selection is essential to define the most effective systemic therapy window. The timing of surgery after neoadjuvant therapy must be optimized because immune‐mediated inflammatory responses may strongly influence surgical complexity. In addition, the optimal timing and duration of adjuvant therapy and criteria for treatment discontinuation remain unresolved. 276 The integration of dynamic liquid biopsy platforms as proxies for molecular response may enable personalized cessation criteria.

Biology‐driven longitudinal surveillance and multi‐omics monitoring

Existing calendar‐based follow‐up paradigms will gradually require replacement by biology‐driven, personalized surveillance systems constructed on longitudinal, whole‐system frameworks. Extension of the tracking model established by the Lung Cancer TRACERx study to early stage NSCLC will be crucial in efforts to identify organ‐specific metastatic biomarkers. 277 Continuous monitoring of dynamic multi‐omics signatures across the disease trajectory will permit more precise characterization of spatiotemporal tumor evolution. Such integration aims to enable clinical intervention during the molecular relapse phase, a critical interval in which biologic recurrence may be detected well before radiographic manifestation.

Vulnerable populations: Balancing efficacy with frailty

Amid the global expansion of the aging population, clinicians are increasingly confronted with a growing cohort of older patients (older than 70 years) and individuals with clinically significant multimorbidity. Such vulnerable patients exhibit elevated recurrence risk but show diminished physiologic reserve to tolerate aggressive treatment. Efforts to address this challenge require a management framework that balances surgical extent with the intensity of personalized systemic therapy. The substantial reduction in treatment‐related toxicity and falls observed in the late‐stage GAP70+ trial underscores a critical need for further investigations dedicated to older adults and vulnerable cohorts to validate and refine geriatric assessment–guided early stage interventions. 278

Digital integration in survivorship and lifespan‐based support

A cure is incomplete until physical and psychosocial restoration goals are achieved. Digital transformation of cancer survivorship care through AI‐driven rehabilitation platforms and digital therapeutics may bridge gaps inherent in conventional postoperative care. Validated tools that address the anxiety–misinformation cycle and social stigma are essential. Adoption of a lifespan‐oriented framework that integrates culturally relevant interventions with digital monitoring may facilitate progression toward holistic health and sustained survivor resilience.

Addressing global disparities in access to precision care

The benefits of early stage eradication must be equitably distributed. Disparities in access to advanced diagnostics, minimally invasive technologies, and novel therapeutics remain formidable barriers. Underpinning these disparities is a systemic misallocation of health care funding and infrastructure, which disproportionately leaves vulnerable populations behind. Progress requires pathway‐specific democratization, leveraging AI‐driven decision support and remote monitoring to extend expert‐level quality assurance to community settings and resource‐limited regions. Confirmation of health equity represents both a social imperative and a scientific necessity for reducing global lung cancer mortality.

CONCLUSION

The management of early stage NSCLC has evolved beyond historical reliance on anatomic resection into a sophisticated, biology‐driven discipline. Widespread adoption of LDCT screening, combined with integration of perioperative immunotherapy and targeted therapies, has fundamentally elevated the curative‐intent threshold. As clinicians confront the complexities of GGO management and the diagnostic limitations of the TNM staging system, achievement of a universal cure remains challenging. Efforts to address these gaps will require transitioning toward a risk‐adaptive management paradigm. To operationalize the proposed risk‐adaptive paradigm, we have synthesized clinical, anatomic, and molecular variables into concrete decision algorithms for early stage NSCLC. For local therapy, specific risk thresholds, including surgical operability, tumor size, and radiologic phenotype, dictate the selection of surgery and ablation therapy. Systemic management is strictly stratified by molecular biomarkers (e.g., EGFR/ALK status) to direct targeted adjuvant tyrosine kinase inhibitors, whereas final pathologic stage and nodal status dictate neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemoimmunotherapy pathways. Through AI‐driven analytics and longitudinal molecular surveillance, this framework enables precise calibration of treatment intensity—escalating therapy for high‐risk cohorts while permitting safe de‐escalation for patients with indolent disease or confirmed molecular remission. The integration of a lifespan perspective with a commitment to reducing global disparities in access to precision care will ensure that technological and pharmacologic advances are translated into equitable, durable biologic cures for all patients with early stage NSCLC.
