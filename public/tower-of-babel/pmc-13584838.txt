Human Milk and Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia in Preterm Infants: The Mediation of Inflammatory Cytokines
Authors: Yunbei Rao, Xuan Wang, Qingling Li, Lingling Yang, Xiaoyun Xiong, Dongshan Han, Chuanzhong Yang, Xueyu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study investigates the mediating role of inflammatory cytokines in the protective effect of human milk against bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) in preterm infants.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 73 infants born at < 28 weeks' gestation.Infants were classified into high‐dose human milk and high‐dose formula groups according to feeding patterns at 36 weeks postmenstrual age (PMA). Cross‐sectional group comparisons of inflammatory factors at postnatal day 7, day 28, and PMA 36 weeks were examined by Mann–Whitney U test, while longitudinal changes in plasma inflammatory cytokines were evaluated using generalized estimating equations (GEE), and mediation analysis was performed using PROCESS version 4 to assess the potential indirect effects of inflammatory cytokines on the association between human milk exposure and BPD severity.ResultsInfants in the high‐dose human milk group had a significantly lower incidence of grade II/III BPD and lower plasma concentrations of CXCL10, IL‐1Ra, and IL‐13 at 36 weeks PMA than those in the high‐dose formula group. Longitudinal GEE analysis showed significant temporal changes in these cytokines but no significant group × time interactions. Mediation analysis demonstrated a significant joint indirect effect through IL‐1Ra and CXCL10 (effect = −0.245, 95% CI −0.487 to −0.046), accounting for 34.9% of the total association between human milk exposure and BPD severity.ConclusionHigh human milk feeding was associated with lower BPD severity and a less activated inflammatory profile at 36 weeks PMA. IL‐1Ra‐ and CXCL10‐related inflammatory pathways may partially account for this association, although the observational design precludes definitive causal inference.Keywords: cytokine, human milk, inflammation, lung, preterm infants

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is a chronic lung disease that primarily affects preterm infants and is characterized by underdeveloped alveoli and pulmonary vasculature [1]. In China, advancements in neonatal intensive care have improved survival rates among extremely preterm infants; however, the incidence of moderate to severe BPD (grade II/III) remains a significant clinical challenge [2]. Severe BPD increases the risk of acute complications such as life‐threatening respiratory failure and pulmonary hypertension, and it is also associated with long‐term consequences, including persistent lung dysfunction, neurodevelopmental delays, and a heightened susceptibility to respiratory infections [3, 4]. These complications place a considerable economic burden on families and healthcare systems.

Human milk is rich in essential nutrients and bioactive compounds that support infant growth and development, aid in immune system maturation, and strengthen immunological defenses [5]. A growing body of evidence indicates that feeding preterm infants human milk significantly reduces the risk of complications such as necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) and sepsis [6, 7]. Moreover, recent studies suggest that human milk may also offer protective benefits against BPD [8]. Observational data have demonstrated a dose‐dependent relationship, showing that greater human milk intake is associated with a reduced risk of BPD [9].

The pathogenesis of BPD is multifactorial, with dysregulated inflammation playing a central role. Factors such as hyperoxia, mechanical ventilation, and infection can trigger the excessive release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including IL‐6 and TNF‐α, and increase oxidative stress, which together impair pulmonary repair and hinder alveolar development [1, 10, 11, 12]. Although the mechanisms by which human milk protects against BPD are not yet fully understood, the regulation of inflammatory cytokines appears to be a critical component.

This study investigates the role of cytokine regulation as a key mechanism linking human milk exposure to reduced BPD risk. Using causal mediation analysis, we aim to quantify the extent to which changes in cytokine levels mediate the protective effects of human milk on BPD outcomes.

Method

Study Design

A prospective cohort study was conducted in a Level III Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) between February 2022 and May 2023. Infants born at a gestational age of less than 28 weeks or with a birth weight below 1000 grams were eligible for inclusion. Informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians for the use of leftover serum from routine clinical testing for cytokine analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Maternity and Child Healthcare Hospital [SFYLS (2024) 088]. Infants were eligible for enrollment if they met all of the following criteria: (1) born at the study hospital; (2) had a gestational age (GA) of less than 30 weeks, determined by the mother's last menstrual period and confirmed by first‐trimester ultrasound, or a birth weight under 1000 grams; (3) parental or guardian consent was obtained. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presence of major congenital malformations; (2) underwent gastrointestinal surgery before reaching a postmenstrual age (PMA) of 36 weeks; (3) discharged or transferred to another hospital before PMA 36 weeks; (4) total milk intake of less than 100 mL/kg/day at PMA 36 weeks; (5) plasma sample collection not completed at PMA 36 weeks.

The present study was conducted within the same prospective source cohort as our previously published study investigating longitudinal changes in IL‐1 family cytokines and their associations with BPD [13]. However, study‐specific eligibility criteria were applied according to the distinct objectives of the two analyses, resulting in partially overlapping final study populations. Of the 73 infants included in the present study, 59 (80.8%) had also been included in the previous report. The prior study focused on the longitudinal associations of plasma IL‐1 family cytokines with BPD, whereas the present study evaluates the association between human milk feeding and BPD and explores inflammatory biomarkers as a potential biological pathway underlying this association. Accordingly, some previously measured cytokine data, including IL‐1Ra measurements from overlapping participants, were incorporated into the present analysis and are explicitly acknowledged here.

Definitions

High‐dose formula feeding and high‐dose human milk feeding were both defined as a daily intake of a single milk type ≥ 100 mL/kg, thereby constituting the “dominant source of nutrition”. Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) was diagnosed following the 2018 NICHD workshop consensus criteria [1]. Infants born at < 32 weeks' gestation with persistent radiographic lung disease who required respiratory support for ≥ 3 consecutive days at 36 weeks PMA were classified as having BPD, with severity graded according to respiratory support modality and FiO2 requirement. The hemodynamically significant patent ductus arteriosus (hsPDA) was diagnosed by echocardiography: continuous left‐to‐right ductal flow plus duct diameter ≥ 1.5 mm or duct‐to‐left pulmonary artery ratio ≥ 0.5, accompanied by at least one of the following: LA/Ao ratio ≥ 1.5, mean transductal pressure gradient ≤ 8 mmHg, or holodiastolic reverse flow in descending aorta [14]. Early‐onset neonatal sepsis (EOS) presents within 72 h post‐birth, defined as pathogenic isolation from blood/cerebrospinal fluid with systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS); suspected cases with abnormal inflammatory markers but negative blood cultures are also included [15]. Small for gestational age (SGA) was defined as a birth weight below the 10th percentile for the infant's gestational age and sex, according to the Fenton 2013 growth curves [16]. Extrauterine growth restriction (EUGR) was assessed at 36 weeks PMA and defined as a weight below the 10th percentile on the Fenton growth charts (2013 version).

Human milk was sourced from the infant's own mother. In the absence of contraindications, enteral nutrition should be initiated within 24 h after birth at a volume of 10 mL/kg/day. Human milk is the preferred feeding; when unavailable, preterm formula (83 kcal/100 mL) may be used. The feeding volume may be advanced by 10–15 mL/kg/day according to tolerance, with a corresponding reduction in parenteral nutrition. Once total enteral intake reaches 100 mL/kg/day, the daily increment can be increased to 20–30 mL/kg/day. Intravenous nutrition should be discontinued when enteral feeding achieves 120–150 mL/kg/day, and the target total enteral feeding volume is set at 150–170 mL/kg/day. Human milk fortifier (Nestle PreNAN FM85) is introduced once feeding volumes reach 80 mL/kg/day.

Data Acquisition

Clinical data were retrieved from the hospital's electronic data management system. One researcher was responsible for collecting baseline information, including infant characteristics such as GA, BW, sex, mode of delivery (cesarean section or vaginal), prenatal steroid administration, multiple pregnancy, premature rupture of membranes, and pregnancy complications. Additional data collected included total weekly milk intake and types of enteral nutrition at PMA 36 weeks, weight at PMA 36 weeks, and clinical outcomes such as hsPDA, EOS, BPD, SGA, EUGR, duration of endotracheal ventilation, and duration of non‐invasive ventilation. A second researcher independently reviewed the data to ensure accuracy and consistency.

Inflammatory Cytokines Detection

By obtaining the small required volume from residual samples of routine clinical tests at PMA 36 weeks, no extra blood was drawn for this study. The blood samples were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min to separate plasma and stored at −80°C. All blood samples were stored at 4°C and processed within 6 h after collection. Plasma levels of interleukin‐1 receptor antagonist (IL‐1Ra), C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10) and interleukin‐13 (IL‐13) were quantitatively measured using the Bio‐Plex Pro Human Cytokine Panels (Bio‐Rad) performed by LabEx (Shanghai).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0. The Shapiro‐Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. Descriptive statistics were reported as numbers and percentages for categorical variables, and as means ± standard deviation or interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables, depending on the distribution. For group comparisons, the t‐test was applied to normally distributed continuous variables, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non‐normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. To evaluate longitudinal changes in plasma inflammatory markers, generalized estimating equation (GEE) models were fitted with repeated measurements within each infant. Time, feeding group, and the group × time interaction were included as model effects. The group × time interaction was used to determine whether temporal changes in inflammatory marker concentrations differed between feeding groups.

A bivariate logistic regression analysis incorporating both IL‑1 Ra and CXCL10 plasma levels was performed to assess their joint association with BPD. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate the discriminatory performance of these cytokines in differentiating between infants with and without BPD.

Mediation analysis was performed using the PROCESS macro version 4.1, Model 4 (Hayes, 2017), with 5000 bias‐corrected bootstrap samples to generate 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To account for potential confounding, the model was adjusted for gestational age and invasive ventilation ≥ 30 days. This analysis yielded total, direct, and indirect effects. Regression model results are reported as standardized beta coefficients (β), along with their 95% CIs, t‐values, p‐values, F‐statistics, and coefficient of determination (R2).

Violin plots were generated using the ggplot2 package in R (version 4.2.1) to compare plasma concentrations of IL‐1Ra, CXCL10, and IL‐13 between the two groups at 36 weeks PMA.

Result

Patients' Characteristics

A total of 73 preterm infants were enrolled in this study, all of whom had a gestational age of less than 28 weeks or a birth weight under 1000 grams. Of these, 32 infants received high‐dose formula feeding, while 41 were fed high‐dose human milk (Figure 1). Follow‐up covered the birth‐to‐discharge period. Among the baseline characteristics, only the incidence of preeclampsia showed a statistically significant difference between the groups (25.0% vs. 7.3%, p = 0.036). No other variables demonstrated statistically significant differences (p > 0.05, Table 1).

Plasma Cytokines Levels Associated With High‐Dose Human Milk Feeding and BPD

Plasma IL‐1Ra, CXCL10, and IL‐13 concentrations were assessed at postnatal day 7 (D7), postnatal day 28 (D28), and 36 weeks PMA. No significant between‐group differences were observed at D7 (all p > 0.05). At D28, IL‐13 was lower in the high‐dose human milk group [0.400 (0.300–0.550) vs. 0.500 (0.400–0.600) pg/mL, p = 0.047], whereas IL‐1Ra and CXCL10 did not differ significantly. At 36 weeks PMA, concentrations of all three cytokines were lower in the high‐dose human milk group [IL‐1Ra: 79.00 (59.28–148.65) vs. 135.50 (74.24–219.96) pg/mL, p = 0.032; CXCL10: 137.80 (94.54–194.80) vs. 182.00 (155.48–270.14) pg/mL, p = 0.004; IL‐13: 0.88 (0.67–1.25) vs. 1.10 (0.87–2.00) pg/mL, p = 0.035] (Table S1; Figure 2).

Longitudinal analysis using Gaussian GEE models of cytokine concentrations showed significant time effects for IL‐1Ra, CXCL10, and IL‐13 (all p < 0.001). A significant overall group effect was observed for IL‐13 (Wald χ2 = 6.242, p = 0.011), whereas the group effects for CXCL10 (p = 0.086) and IL‐1Ra (p = 0.401) were not significant. No significant group × time interactions were detected for any cytokine (all p > 0.05) (Table S2).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the ability of these cytokines to distinguish infants with grade II/III BPD. The area under the curve (AUC) for IL‑1 Ra, CXCL10, and IL‑13 was 0.621, 0.599, and 0.506, respectively (Table S2), indicating modest individual discriminatory capacity. To improve differentiation performance, a logistic regression model combining IL‑1Ra and CXCL10 levels was constructed. The ROC curve for this combined model yielded an AUC of 0.699 (p = 0.007), with a sensitivity of 0.617 and specificity of 0.739 (Table S3).

Mediation Analysis of High‐Dose Human Milk Feeding, IL‐1Ra Combined CXCL10, and BPD Severity

Employing mediation analysis with bootstrap validation (5,000 iterations), we investigated the mechanism by which high‐dose human milk feeding attenuates BPD severity through plasma IL‐1Ra and CXCL10 regulation. After adjustment for gestational age and prolonged endotracheal ventilation, the total effect of high‐dose human milk feeding on BPD stage remained significant (effect = −0.703, 95% CI −1.135 to −0.272). The indirect effect through IL‐1Ra and CXCL10 was also significant (effect = −0.245, 95% CI −0.487 to −0.046), accounting for 34.9% of the total effect. The remaining direct effect remained significant (effect = −0.458, 95% CI −0.906 to −0.010), indicating partial mediation [Table 3, Figure 3, Table S4 (linear regression model)]. Collectively, these results suggested that IL‐1Ra and CXCL10 may partly account for the association between high human milk feeding and reduced BPD severity.

Discussion

We provide novel insights into the protective mechanisms of high‐dose human milk feeding against BPD in preterm infants, emphasizing the critical role of inflammatory modulation. Our study showed that high‐dose human milk feeding in preterm infants reduced inflammatory cytokines levels and the incidence of grade II/III BPD at PMA 36 weeks. Plasma IL‐1Ra and CXCL10 might exert a partial mediating effect in attenuating the severity of BPD by high human milk.

In the present study, high human milk exposure was associated with lower plasma concentrations of IL‐1Ra, CXCL10, and IL‐13 at 36 weeks PMA, suggesting a less activated inflammatory profile at the time of BPD assessment. Previous studies have similarly reported lower circulating concentrations of several inflammatory cytokines among preterm infants receiving human milk, supporting the potential immunomodulatory effects of human milk. However, our longitudinal GEE analysis provided a more detailed picture. Although significant time effects were observed for all three cytokines, indicating substantial postnatal changes in inflammatory status, no significant group × time interactions were identified (Table S2). Nevertheless, mediation analysis suggested that IL‐1Ra‐ and CXCL10‐related inflammatory pathways may partially account for the association between higher human milk exposure and lower BPD severity, with approximately 34.9% of the adjusted total effect explained through the indirect pathway. Taken together, these findings suggest that human milk exposure may be linked to BPD through modulation of inflammatory status, although the absence of significant group × time interactions and the observational design preclude conclusions regarding a progressive or causal suppression of inflammation over time. Among these biomarkers, the interpretation of IL‐1Ra warrants particular consideration because, unlike CXCL10, IL‐1Ra functions primarily as an endogenous anti‐inflammatory antagonist rather than a conventional pro‐inflammatory mediator [17].

IL‐1Ra is an endogenous antagonist of the pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐1α and IL‐1β and serves as an important negative regulator of IL‐1‐mediated inflammation [18]. Therefore, lower IL‐1Ra concentrations should not necessarily be interpreted as reduced anti‐inflammatory capacity. In our previous longitudinal cohort, IL‐1α, IL‐1β, and IL‐1Ra concentrations did not differ between infants with and without BPD at D7; however, IL‐1Ra concentrations were higher in infants with BPD at D28 and remained higher at 36 weeks PMA [13]. These findings suggest that elevated IL‐1Ra in BPD may represent a compensatory response to persistent inflammatory stimulation rather than a direct pro‐inflammatory effect of IL‐1Ra itself. In this context, the lower IL‐1Ra concentrations observed among infants with greater human milk exposure may reflect a lower inflammatory burden and, consequently, a reduced requirement for compensatory IL‐1Ra upregulation. Elevated IL‐1Ra concentrations have also been reported in tracheal aspirates from preterm baboon models of BPD [19], providing additional evidence that enhanced IL‐1Ra responses may accompany sustained pulmonary inflammation, although differences in species and biological compartments should be considered. Taken together, these observations provide a plausible explanation for the lower circulating IL‐1Ra concentrations associated with greater human milk exposure, while this interpretation remains hypothetical and requires further mechanistic validation. CXCL10 may provide a complementary mechanistic link. Through binding to CXCR3, CXCL10 promotes the recruitment of inflammatory cells to injured tissues [20, 21], and experimental studies have implicated CXCL10 in neonatal hyperoxic lung injury [22]. Thus, the lower CXCL10 concentrations observed with greater human milk exposure are also consistent with a less activated inflammatory state. Together with the mediation analysis, these findings suggest that IL‐1Ra‐ and CXCL10‐related inflammatory pathways may partially account for the association between higher human milk exposure and lower BPD severity, although the underlying mechanisms remain to be established.

The biological plausibility of this pathway is supported by accumulating evidence that human milk contains multiple immunomodulatory components. Human milk‐derived exosomes have demonstrated anti‐inflammatory and immune‐regulatory effects in experimental models of inflammatory bowel disease, intestinal ischemia–reperfusion injury, and necrotizing enterocolitis [23, 24, 25, 26]. Other bioactive components of human milk, including human milk oligosaccharides, milk fat globule membrane components, short‐chain fatty acids, and lactoferrin, have also been implicated in the attenuation of pulmonary inflammatory responses in experimental studies [27, 28]. However, direct clinical evidence linking the immunomodulatory effects of human milk to BPD remains limited. Our findings therefore provide clinical evidence supporting a potential inflammatory pathway linking greater human milk exposure with lower BPD severity, while further prospective and mechanistic studies are needed to establish causality and clarify the specific biological mediators involved. This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small, single‐center cohort may limit the generalizability of our findings, which require validation in larger multicenter populations. Second, given the observational design, residual confounding and reverse causation cannot be excluded. In particular, the temporal proximity between cytokine measurement and BPD assessment at 36 weeks PMA limits causal interpretation of the mediation analysis. Third, although GEE models were used to account for repeated measurements, no significant group × time interactions were observed, and thus distinct longitudinal inflammatory trajectories between feeding groups could not be established. Finally, plasma cytokine concentrations may not fully reflect the local inflammatory environment of the developing lung. Further prospective and mechanistic studies are needed to clarify the biological pathways linking human milk exposure with BPD.

Conclusion

High human milk exposure was associated with lower BPD severity and a less activated inflammatory profile at 36 weeks PMA. IL‐1Ra‐ and CXCL10‐related inflammatory pathways may partially mediate this association, although the observational design precludes definitive causal inference.
