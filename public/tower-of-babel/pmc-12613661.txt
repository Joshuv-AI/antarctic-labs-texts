Feasibility and acceptability of a single-session online workshop targeting self-compassion skills for emerging adults with disordered eating
Authors: Arielle S Wolinsky, Julia M Hormes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesResearch supports a protective role of self-compassion in guarding against disordered eating, which is especially prevalent in emerging adults (ages 18–25 years old). This set of two studies explored the relevance of self-compassion to emerging adults, expectancies of the impact of having a self-compassion mindset on disordered eating, and the prospective and concurrent acceptability and feasibility of a single-session online workshop targeting self-compassion skills in emerging adults with disordered eating.MethodsStudy 1 examined emerging adults’ (n = 536) understanding of self-compassion and expected impact of having a self-compassion mindset on disordered eating, along with prospective workshop acceptability. Study 2 explored the concurrent feasibility and acceptability of the single-session workshop in a large and demographically diverse sample of emerging adults endorsing current disordered eating (n = 515).ResultsStudy 1 found that emerging adults understood the concept of self-compassion and appreciated its actual importance in their lives and perceived importance in the lives of others (i.e., peers, parents). Emerging adults with disordered eating expected self-compassion to positively impact their disordered eating symptoms. Study 2 found the online workshop to be feasible and acceptable to emerging adults with disordered eating, as indicated by adequate treatment recruitment and retention, good comprehension of workshop content, and positive expectancies that the self-compassion skills learned would have a positive impact on disordered eating.ConclusionA workshop building self-compassion skills is relevant to emerging adults and could be protective for those with disordered eating. Future work to examine the efficacy of the workshop appears warranted.Plain English summarySelf-compassion can serve a protective role in guarding against the development of disordered eating. The first study explored how relevant self-compassion is to emerging adults and how much they believe having a self-compassion mindset can impact disordered eating. The second study explored how preferable a one-time online workshop targeting self-compassion skills would be to emerging adults with disordered eating. Emerging adults understood what self-compassion is and appreciated its actual importance in their lives. Additionally, emerging adults expected that having a self-compassion mindset would positively impact their eating disorder symptoms. Further, the online workshop was feasible and acceptable to emerging adults with disordered eating. A workshop building self-compassion skills may be protective for emerging adults with disordered eating.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-025-01219-9.Keywords: Emerging adults, Disordered eating, Self-compassion, Single-session intervention, Digital health

Introduction

Disordered eating, the engagement in maladaptive eating behaviors and the experience of problematic weight- and shape-related cognitions, most often surfaces during the college years, with a reported 31% prevalence rate in this group [5]. This prevalence estimate is consistent with previous studies evaluating rates of disordered eating on college campuses; however, it is unclear whether these estimates generalize beyond college-attending emerging adults (i.e., 18 to 25 years old) to include adults of this age group regardless of occupation [4, 14, 22]. If not addressed, disordered eating can lead to the development of a clinically significant eating disorder [31]; Stice, Marti, & Rohde [41],; Volpe et al., [46], along with a range of detrimental physical and psychological consequences [32]; Pasold, McCracken, & Ward-Begnoche [33],; Wade, Wilksch, & Lee [47]). For example, severe dietary restraint often leads to low bone mineral density and eventual osteoporosis [5, 36]. Frequent compensatory behaviors, such as laxative use and self-induced vomiting, may negatively alter an individual’s fluid and electrolyte balance and increase risk of cardiac abnormalities [6, 9]. Psychological consequences such as decreased quality of life, depression, and anxiety also often co-occur with these maladaptive behaviors (Gregorowski, Seedat, & Jordaan [18],; Pasold, McCracken, & Ward-Begnoche [33],; Ward & Hay [48].

Given the significant adverse impact of disordered eating, it is important to identify protective factors that reduce vulnerability to maladaptive cognitions and behaviors around food, eating, and the body. There is robust empirical support for the protective function of three psychological factors in relation to disordered eating in adolescents and emerging adults, namely positive body image, body image flexibility, and self-compassion (Tylka, Russell, & Neal [45]. Self-compassion is, broadly speaking, “compassion turned inward” [30]. It is being actively aware of painful emotions during challenging situations while inviting kindness and understanding towards the self by believing that these challenges are a piece of the greater human experience [10, 28]. Self-compassion is considered an adaptive method for coping with one’s emotions by allowing individuals to experience distressing emotions with appreciation towards themselves [27].

Self-compassion and its impact on disordered eating is best understood through its three interconnected elements, namely self-kindness, common humanity, and mindfulness, and their interactions with each other [28]. Self-kindness is the act of showing patience, sensitivity, empathy, warmth, and forgiveness to the self [17, 27]. Preliminary research supports a link between body-related variables (e.g., appearance esteem, appearance comparison) and self-kindness [34]. For example, adolescents who show kindness towards themselves also have greater positive perceptions of themselves and thus, exhibit more body-related resilience regardless of social and individual factors (Braun, Park, & Gorin [8],; Neff & McGehee [30].

Common humanity involves accepting an individual’s connection with others, especially in weaknesses, shortcomings, and anguish [28]. Studies of links between common humanity and disordered eating, specifically body-related variables (e.g., positive body image), reveal mixed results; however, there is at least preliminary evidence to suggest that adolescent and emerging adult females and adolescent males with a stronger sense of common humanity experience more positive body image and body appreciation (Maher, Lane, & Mulgrew [23],; Rodgers et al., [34] Seekis, Bradley, & Duffy [37],; Wasylkiw, MacKinnon, & MacLellan [49]). Finally, mindfulness is the state of being aware, attending to, and accepting the present moment in a non-judgmental manner [7, 38]; Shapiro, Brown, & Biegel [39]. Higher levels of mindfulness have been shown to be associated with greater appearance esteem and lower appearance comparison in adolescent girls (Mendelson, Mendelson, & White [25],; Rodgers et al., [34] Thompson, Heinberg, & Tantleff-Dunn [43]).

To date, there is limited research exploring the utility of targeting self-compassion skills in interventions designed to prevent or treat disordered eating symptoms. A small set of studies of the impact of self-compassion interventions on disordered eating reveal overall small to moderate effects on disordered eating symptoms compared to active or waitlist controls [44]. Interestingly, the duration of interventions did not moderate treatment efficacy, suggesting that brief interventions are no less effective than more time-intensive approaches. An intervention based on the Mindful Self-Compassion (MSC) program developed by Neff and Germer [29] targeted key components of self-compassion (e.g., loving kindness, self-appreciation, common humanity) to ameliorate body image concerns (Seekis, Bradley, & Duffy [37]). Undergraduate women endorsing body image concerns were randomized to participate in a single, 50- minute, face-to-face workshop and two-week online discussion via Facebook or a waitlist control group [37]. Those who participated in the intervention endorsed lower drive for thinness, body dissatisfaction, and higher body appreciation and self-compassion, both at post- intervention and one-month follow-up and, except for body dissatisfaction, gains were maintained at three-month follow-up (Seekis, Bradley, & Duffy [37]. A significant limitation of this work is that the study only examined impact of the intervention on body image concerns and did not include other symptoms of disordered eating. The extent to which findings from these preliminary studies generalize to more demographically diverse samples groups (e.g., males, ethnic and racial minorities, sexual minorities, etc.) is also unclear.

Currently, fewer than one-quarter of individuals with disordered eating receive treatment [42], and the COVID-19 pandemic has further exacerbated barriers to treatment access. In an effort to address this crisis, digitally implemented interventions have received increasing attention from researchers. These digital interventions, which range from therapist-led sessions transmitted electronically through video conferencing or online chat (i.e., “e-therapy”) to self-paced, modular-based prevention or treatment programs delivered via smartphones, computers, or other digital modes (i.e., “e-health”), show promising effects in the prevention and treatment of adult mental health problems in general and eating disorders specifically (Linardon, Shatte, Messer, et al., [20]. Advantages of e-health interventions include easy scalability, cost effectiveness, and the potential for tailored delivery [2]. Preliminary research reveals generally positive patient attitudes towards e-therapy and e- health interventions for disordered eating (Linardon, Shatte, Messer, et al., [20] Linardon, Shatte, Tepper, & Fuller-Tyszkiewicz [21].

Present set of studies

While self-compassion and disordered eating are clearly related, there remains a lack of information regarding emerging adults’ understanding of self-compassion and this age group’s perceived benefits of self-compassion specifically on symptoms of disordered eating. Study 1 explored the relevance of self-compassion in the lives of emerging adults along with expectancies of having a self-compassion mindset on disordered eating. Building on preliminary evidence to suggest that interventions targeting self-compassion have a positive effect on aspects of disordered eating, Study 1 also explored the prospective acceptability of a proposed single-session, online workshop designed to impart skills grounded in self-compassion to address disordered eating behaviors and cognitions.

Findings from Study 1 informed Study 2, which was focused on the development and implementation of a 60-minute, self-paced, online workshop targeting self-compassion skills for emerging adults with disordered eating. Study 2 evaluated the concurrent acceptability and feasibility of this workshop in a large and demographically diverse sample of emerging adults endorsing disordered eating attitudes and behaviors. Indicators of feasibility and acceptability included recruitment and retention rates, participant self-report regarding perceived acceptability and utility of the workshop, and comprehension of workshop components.

Study 1

All study procedures were reviewed and approved by the local Institutional Review Board and performed in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [50]. Study-eligible individuals reviewed an informed consent form and indicated their consent to participate by advancing to the questionnaire on their computer screen.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from an undergraduate student research pool at a public university in the northeastern United States. Eligibility criteria included English language fluency and being between the ages of 18 and 25 (i.e. emerging adulthood). Students were able to view and sign up to participate in available research studies through an online research study computer program. Students interested in participating in the present study were instructed to review the informed consent which outlined eligibility criteria, the study rationale, benefits and risks, and researcher contact information.

Measures

All measures were administered via the secure server Qualtrics.

Demographics

Participants provided information regarding their biological sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, age (years and birthdate), race/ethnicity, and current school year.

Knowledge of self-compassion

To assess their knowledge of self-compassion, participants were shown five potential definitions of self-compassion and asked to select the most accurate definition of self- compassion.

Self-compassion

Self-compassion was assessed using the Self-Compassion Scale (SCS) [27], a 26-item measure of the three components of self-compassion: common humanity versus isolation, self-kindness versus self- judgment, and mindfulness versus over-identification. Participants were asked to indicate how often they act in the manner described in each item (e.g., “I try to see my failings as part of the human condition”) using a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). Items were averaged to obtain an overall score, with higher scores suggesting greater self-compassion (Cronbach’s α in the present sample = 0.80).

Perceived presence of self-compassion

Perceived presence of self-compassion was assessed with the following questions: “To what extent do you have a self-compassion mindset OVERALL?” [rated using a four-point Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (almost never) to 3 (almost always)] and “To what extent to you have a self-compassion mindset TODAY?” [0 (do not) to 3 (definitely)].

Importance of self-compassion to self and others

Investigator-generated items evaluated the importance of self-compassion in respondents’ own lives and its perceived importance in the lives of meaningful others as follows: “How important is having a self- compassion mindset to your daily living?” and “How important do you think self-compassion is to your parent(s)/caregiver(s)/ peers/ social media influencers?” These items were rated using a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (not important) to 5 (very important).

Disordered eating

Participants completed the Eating Disorder Examination - Questionnaire 6.0 (EDE-Q), a 28-item self-report measure of behavioral, cognitive, and emotional symptoms of disordered eating over the past 28 days (Cronbach’s α = 0.92) [15]. For the purposes of this study, to meet criteria for disordered eating, participants had to endorse at least one episode of disordered eating behavior (e.g., at least one episode of self-induced vomiting over the past 28 days) and/or demonstrate elevated weight or shape concerns defined as scoring one standard deviation above established population norms for men (weight- M = 1.29, SD = 1.27 and shape- M = 1.59, SD = 1.38) and women (weight- M = 1.79, SD = 1.51 and shape- M = 2.23, SD = 1.65) (Lavender, De Young, & Anderson [19],; Mond et al., [26].

Only participants who endorsed disordered eating defined in this manner completed the following measures. All other participants were redirected to the end of the survey after completing the disordered eating measure.

Self-compassion expectancies for disordered eating

Participants reported on expectancies about the impact of having a self-compassion mindset on each disordered eating symptom. Following each item assessing the severity of disordered eating symptoms, participants viewed the question stem “having a self-compassion mindset would (fill in with disordered eating symptom)…” and rate the severity on a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from − 2 (much more severe) to + 2 (much less severe). For example, one item stated, “Having a self-compassion mindset would (fill in with following rating: greatly decrease, slightly decrease, stay the same, slightly increase, greatly increase) the number of times that I make myself sick (vomit) as a means of controlling my shape or weight”. This approach is based on studies of expectancies of the impact of cannabis use on other symptoms [1, 13, 35, 40]. In the current sample, items quantifying expectancies demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.96).

Treatment outcome expectancies and credibility

Participants read a concise description of the proposed online intervention aimed at reducing disordered eating symptoms through improving self- compassion skills. The treatment description included the intervention aims, main components, and logistics (i.e., intervention format, time to completion, operating system) (see Supplemental Materials for complete language describing the intervention).

Credibility/expectancy questionnaire (CEQ)

The CEQ is a six-item questionnaire that assesses whether participants feel and think the treatment will be effective for them [11]. Four out of six CEQ items were included in the analyses reported here (three credibility questions and one expectancy question, all rated on a nine-point Likert-type scale, Cronbach’s α = 0.83). The two other CEQ items were excluded because they capture constructs deemed irrelevant to the present study because they quantify expected improvement in symptoms by the end of treatment whereas this study explored skill development in a single-session workshop.

Self-compassion treatment components prospective acceptability

After reading the intervention description and completing four items from the CEQ, participants were asked to rate the prospective acceptability and likeability of various aspects of the proposed intervention, including ideal time duration for the workshop, preference for workshop format (online versus in-person), preference for workshop lead (self-paced versus facilitator-lead), and preference for type of time to complete writing exercises (structured versus unstructured).

Attitudes towards e-therapy

The E-Therapy Attitudes Measure (ETAM) was used to assess participant attitudes towards online health-related interventions [3]. The 17 ETAM items are rated on a five-point scale ranging from 0 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree), generating a composite score (Cronbach’s α = 0.85) reflecting overall positive attitudes (composite scores above 2.5), neutral attitudes (1.5–2.5), or negative attitudes towards internet-based therapies (< 1.5).

Intentions to participate in online interventions

Participants were asked to indicate their intention to use a facilitator-guided or self-paced online intervention if they were currently experiencing (or would experience in the future) disordered eating. Participants responded using a five-point scale ranging from 1 (extremely unlikely) to 5 (extremely likely). Previous research categorizes responses 1 (extremely unlikely) to 3 (neither unlikely nor likely) as “no” and ratings of 4 (somewhat likely) or 5 (extremely likely) as “yes” (March et al., 2018).

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 27. We examined missing data, including the number of items missing and patterns of missing items, as an indication of measure feasibility. Less than 5% of cases had missing data. Univariate and multivariate outliers for all relevant study variables were identified and removed (n = 61) using standardized Z-scores and by calculating Mahalanobis distances, respectively. The 61 cases were removed due to containing at least one outlier on at least one EDE-Q item. Skewness and kurtosis for all relevant study variables was less than +/- 2, suggesting that no variable transformations were necessary.

Results

Participants

A total of 736 individuals signed up to participate in the study during the recruitment period (March–December 2022). About 90% of participants (n = 600, 89% of age-eligible individuals) completed all measures up to and including the EDE-Q, which was used to determine disordered eating status and eligibility to complete the remainder of the survey. Of the 449 participants who met the disordered eating (DE) eligibility criterion, 61 cases (13% of DE eligible participants) contained at least one outlier (defined as 3.29 standard deviations away from the mean) and were removed from the analyses, resulting in a final sample of 388 participants.

Participants were primarily cisgender female, White or Black/African American, in their freshman year of college, and about 19 years of age. There were no statistically significant differences in race/ethnicity, gender, and class year between DE participants (n = 388) and non-DE participants (n = 148). Non-DE participants endorsed significantly higher levels of self- compassion compared to DE participants [t(534)=-2.29, p =.02, d=-0.22; see Table 1 for all demographic information].

Prospective workshop acceptability

After reading a brief description of the proposed self-compassion workshop addressing disordered eating, participants who met the disordered eating criterion (n = 388) completed measures of prospective acceptability (e.g. treatment credibility, outcome expectancies, and attitudes). Using a Likert scale from 1 (not at all logical) to 9 (very logical), participants reported that, on average, the proposed workshop seemed somewhat logical (M = 6.0, SD = 1.6), and they thought the workshop would be somewhat useful in improving their self-compassion skills in order to reduce disordered eating thoughts and behaviors (M = 5.6, SD = 1.8, on a scale of 1[not at all useful] to 9[very useful]). Using a nine-point Likert scale from 1 (not at all confident) to 9 (very confident), participants also reported that, on average, they were also somewhat confident that they would recommend the workshop to a friend (M = 5.8, SD = 1.9). When asked about how they felt about the workshop, participants reported that, on average, they felt the workshop would somewhat improve their self-compassion skills to reduce disordered eating thoughts and behaviors (M = 5.1, SD = 1.8 on a scale of 1 [not at all] to 9 [very much]).

There was a significant multivariate main effect of gender on credibility and expectancy outcomes, F(4,262) = 2.99, Wilks’ 𝜆 =0.96, p =.02, partial 𝜂!=0.04. Women were significantly more likely than men to perceive the proposed workshop as logical [(M = 6.3, SD = 1.6 vs. M = 5.7, SD = 1.6), F (1, 265) = 9.45, p =.002, partial 𝜂! =0.03], and to agree that they would recommend the workshop to a friend [(M = 6.1, SD = 1.9 vs. M = 5.5, SD = 2.0), F (1, 265) = 6.84, p =.01, partial 𝜂! =0.03]. There was also a significant positive correlation between level of self-compassion and perceived utility of the workshop in improving self-compassion skills in order to reduce disordered eating thoughts and behaviors (r =.18, p =.001). There were no significant associations between disordered eating symptoms (as quantified via the EDE-Q) and any of the credibility and expectancy outcomes (r’s ranging from − 0.02 to 0.1; all p’s > 0.05).

Almost three-quarters of participants (n = 269, 75%) reported an average ideal workshop time frame to be between 30 and 90 min with open-ended responses suggesting an average preferred workshop time length of 45 min (SD = 17.7 min). In terms of workshop format (i.e., online versus in- person), 80 (28%) participants strongly preferred an online workshop, 49 (17%) participants somewhat preferred an online workshop, 68 (24%) participants did not have a format preference, 66 (23%) somewhat preferred an in-person workshop, and 26 (9%) strongly preferred an in- person workshop. Almost a third (n = 87, 31%) of participants strongly preferred a self-paced format, and about a quarter (n = 68, 24%) of participants somewhat preferred a self-paced format. About a quarter (n = 65, 23%) of participants did not have a workshop lead preference. Less than 17% (n = 47, 17%) of participants somewhat preferred a facilitator-guided workshop, and about 6% (n = 8, 6%) participants strongly preferred a facilitator-guided-workshop.

Approximately 10% of participants (n = 21, 9%) strongly preferred structured time to complete writing exercises as part of the workshop, and 53 (22.6%) participants somewhat preferred structured time to complete writing exercises. About 30% (n = 69, 29%) of participants did not have a preference for the writing exercises time format. Approximately a quarter (n = 54, 23%) of participants somewhat preferred unstructured time to complete writing exercises, and about 16% (n = 155, 16%) participants strongly preferred unstructured time to complete writing exercises.

When asked about their intentions to participate in an online self-compassion workshop if experiencing disordered eating, 24 (8%) participants indicated they would be extremely unlikely to participate, 74 (24%) participants indicated they would be somewhat unlikely, 81 (26%) participants were neither unlikely nor likely to participate, 114 (36%) participants were somewhat likely, and 22 (7%) participants were extremely likely to participate. In sum, about half of participants were positively inclined to participate in an online workshop if experiencing disordered eating.

Participants’ overall attitudes towards online health-related interventions were assessed using the ETAM. More than half of participants (n = 211, 68%) had neutral attitudes towards online interventions, a quarter of participants (n = 80, 26%) had overall positive attitudes towards online interventions, and less than 7% of participants (n = 21, 6%) had overall negative attitudes towards online interventions.

Study 2

All methods and materials were reviewed by the local Institutional Review Board. All study procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [50]. All participants provided informed consent prior to completion of questionnaires and study procedures.

Method

Participants

Participants were recruited from an undergraduate student research pool at a public university in the northeastern United States. Students were able to view and sign up to participate in available research studies through an online research study computer program. Students interested in participating in the present study completed a screener questionnaire to determine their study eligibility. A screening questionnaire verified eligibility criteria, which included being between the ages of 18 and 25 years old (i.e. emerging adulthood), English-speaking, and endorsement of disordered eating behaviors or elevated weight or shape concerns as described in more detail below. Study-eligible individuals were instructed to review the informed consent, which outlined the study rationale, benefits and risks, and researcher contact information.

Procedures

All study materials were hosted in Qualtrics, a secure online survey data collection platform. A pre-workshop survey asked participants to provide information on demographics, current level of self-compassion, current level of self-esteem, and knowledge of self-compassion. Once participants completed the pre-workshop survey, they were directed to participate in the approximately 60-minute, self-paced, online workshop. Time spent on each page was pre-defined, forcing all participants to remain on each page for a minimum amount of time before being allowed to proceed to the next page. After the workshop, participants completed a post-workshop survey containing measures of acceptability, helpfulness, and comprehension of workshop components as detailed below.

Workshop protocol

For a detailed description of the intervention content please see the Supplemental Materials. The brief, online, self-paced workshop introduced the overall concept of self-compassion as well as the following six strategies: (1) Mindfulness, (2) Common Humanity, (3) Self-Kindness, (4) Soothing Touch, (5) Self- Appreciation, and (6) Savoring Meditation. Participants were guided through psychoeducational material, self-reflection exercises, and a mindful eating meditation. Once participants completed the workshop, they were instructed to complete the post- workshop survey which contained measures of feasibility, workshop acceptability, workshop helpfulness, and comprehension of workshop components.

Measures

Eligibility screener measures

Age

Participants were asked to indicate their age in years as well as birthdate.

Disordered eating

Measure was identical to Study 1. The EDE-Q demonstrated excellent internal consistency in the current sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.92).

Pre-workshop measures

Demographics

Measure was identical to Study 1.

Knowledge of self-compassion

Measure was identical to Study 1.

Self-compassion

Measure was identical to Study 1. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency in the current sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.89).

Post-workshop measures

Workshop feasibility

Feasibility was assessed in multiple ways as follows: number of individuals screened for study eligibility, number of individuals meeting study eligibility, number of participants who consented to participate in workshop, number of participants who completed pre-workshop measures, number of participants who began the workshop, number of participants who completed the workshop, number of participants who discontinued the workshop at some point prior to completing the post-workshop measures, number of participants who discontinued the workshop after each workshop task, and number of participants who completed the post-workshop measures (i.e. study completers). Additionally, measurement appropriateness and burden, another measure of feasibility, was assessed by examining internal consistency, percentage of missing data, and measurement completion time.

Participants were also asked content-specific questions related to the material presented throughout the workshop. First, participants were asked to identify the three main components of self- compassion (mindfulness, common humanity, and self-kindness). Next, participants indicated the role that self-compassion has (protective) in the development of disordered eating. Lastly, participants indicated how savoring impacts happiness (increases happiness). To prevent individuals from going back into the workshop content to review the presented material before answering these comprehension questions, the workshop technical specification included an inability to reverse direction during the workshop.

Workshop acceptability

Participants were asked to complete an author-developed measure of concurrent workshop acceptability. The aim of this measure was to determine participants’ attitudes towards the online-delivered workshop. Five items assessed how easy participants found the online workshop platform to use, whether they would recommend the online workshop to others, whether they believed the workshop would have a long-term positive impact on their eating, and whether they believed the self-compassion skills they learned would reduce their disordered eating symptoms. The five items were rated on a five-point Likert scale with responses ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

Second, participants were asked to rate how helpful they found the online workshop overall as well as the helpfulness of individual workshop components. These items were rated on a four-point Likert scale with responses ranging from 0 (not at all helpful) to 4 (extremely helpful). Lastly, two qualitative questions asked participants to describe what they like least and most about the online workshop.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 27. Missing data analyses were conducted, such as number of items missing and patterns of missing items, to examine measure feasibility. Less than 5% of cases had missing data. Univariate and multivariate outliers for all relevant study variables were identified and removed (n = 54) using standardized Z-scores and calculating Mahalanobis distances, respectively. Skewness and kurtosis for all relevant study variables was less than +/- 2, suggesting that no variable transformations were necessary.

Workshop feasibility was assessed by examining dropout rates and comprehension of concepts covered by the workshop as indicated by proportion of participants who correctly responded to post-workshop comprehension questions. Concurrent acceptability of the workshop was quantified via ratings of various aspects of the workshop (e.g., workshop platform ease of use, recommendation of workshop to others, perceived workshop impact on disordered eating) and reported helpfulness of the information and skills conveyed.

Workshop feasibility

A total of 827 individuals signed up to participate in the workshop during the recruitment period (October– December 2022). Of the individuals who signed up, 515 (62% of total signed up) met the disordered eating and age eligibility criteria to complete the study. Of the 515 individuals who met study eligibility criteria, 54 cases (10% of study-eligible participants) contained at least one outlier (defined as 3.29 standard deviations greater from the mean) and were removed, resulting in 461 study eligible participants. These 54 outlier cases represented very severe eating disorder pathology. Given that the workshop was not designed to address clinically significant eating disorders, removing these cases made sense methodologically as well as conceptually.

Of the study eligible individuals, 420 individuals (91% of study eligible individuals) consented to participate in the study. Of the consenting individuals, 400 participants (95% of consenting individuals) completed all pre-workshop measures prior to beginning the workshop. Of the pre-workshop measure completers, 390 participants (93% of pre-workshop survey completers) began the workshop. Of the participants who began the workshop, 272 participants (70% of participants who began the workshop) completed the workshop. In contrast, 118 participants (30% of participants who began the workshop) discontinued the workshop at some point prior to completing the post-workshop measures. Of the 272 participants who completed the workshop, 242 study completers (89% of workshop completers) completed all post-workshop measures.

Comprehension

Of all study completers, 42 participants (21% of study completers) correctly answered all three comprehension questions, 141 participants (70% of study completers) correctly answered two out of three comprehension questions. 19 participants (9% of study completers) correctly answered one out of three comprehension questions. Thus, 202 participants (99% of study completers) correctly answered at least one comprehension question.

There was a statistically significant association between pre-workshop self-compassion level at baseline and the number of correct comprehension questions post-workshop [F(2,267) = 4.48, p =.01, 𝜂! = 0.03], such that participants who answered all three comprehension questions correctly had the lowest level of self-compassion at baseline (M = 2.6, SD = 0.5) compared to those participants who answered two out of three correct (M = 2.8, SD = 0.5, post-hoc p =.008) and one out of three correct (M = 2.1, SD = 0.4, post-hoc p =.008). There was no significant association between responses to comprehension questions and EDE-Q total or subscale scores.

Discussion

The present investigation employed a two-stage process to determine the feasibility and acceptability (prospective and concurrent) of a self-paced, online workshop targeting self- compassion skills for emerging adults with disordered eating. Study 1 explored the significance of self-compassion in emerging adults’ lives and expectations of having a self-compassion mindset on disordered eating as well as prospective acceptability of an online, self-paced proposed workshop targeting self-compassion skills. Findings from study 1 were used to develop and implement a modified version of the proposed workshop and evaluate its feasibility and concurrent acceptability.

Overall, findings from Study 1 suggest that self-compassion is an understood and relevant construct in emerging adults’ lives. Having a self-compassion mindset was expected to positively impact disordered eating symptoms. Participants endorsing elevated levels of disordered eating generally expected that having a self- compassion mindset would reduce disordered eating behaviors, weight concerns, shape concerns, eating concerns and dietary restraint. Furthermore, participants who expected that having a self-compassion mindset would have a positive impact on their weight or eating concerns actually experienced fewer weight or eating concerns. This finding indicates that individuals with more severe levels of disordered eating may be more suspicious of self-compassion’s impact on their behaviors and thus believe self-compassion may have a negative impact. Previous research by Geller and colleagues suggests individuals with eating disorders experienced increased fears about showing themselves compassion [16]. It is also important to note that these findings are correlational, and thus, it is unclear whether expectancies predict disordered eating severity or vice versa. Future longitudinal research that examines expectancies of having a self- compassion mindset on disordered eating, or mental health in general, and the development of disordered eating symptoms would be beneficial to determine a causal relationship between expectancy and actual symptom severity.

Notably, participants with disordered eating behaviors expected that having a self-compassion mindset would impact eating concerns, vomiting, and laxative use frequency more strongly compared to other symptoms (i.e., shape concerns, weight concerns, restraint, overeating, and driven exercise). This suggests that participants expect a self-compassion mindset to positively impact arguably more severe disordered eating symptoms more so than behaviors potentially perceived as more normative in today’s culture. Furthermore, emerging adults may incorrectly assume that self-compassion interventions are solely for individuals with more severe disordered eating. Thus, interventions targeting self-compassion may need to emphasize motivation to participate and include psychoeducation about normative discontent with body image and eating cognitions and behaviors as well as how detrimental even the more seemingly normative disordered eating behaviors are.

Based on reported levels of treatment credibility, outcome expectancies, treatment preferences, and intentions to participate, the proposed workshop appeared to be somewhat acceptable for study 1 DE participants. DE participants’ reported intentions and attitudes towards engaging in the proposed workshop revealed that a little more than half felt indifferent or less inclined to participate compared to fewer than 50% of participants being more inclined to participate. It is worth noting that these findings may be the direct result of our recruitment strategy which relied on a convenience sample identified via a university research participant pool. Nevertheless, future work should assess in more detail perceived barriers to workshop engagement, develop practical solutions to such barriers, and incorporate motivational enhancement strategies to increase workshop participation (e.g., identifying discrepancies between participants’ values and engagement in disordered eating behaviors could gently nudge disinterested emerging adults towards partaking in such intervention). A majority of DE participants reported preferring a workshop that was approximately 45 min long, online, self-paced, and had unstructured time for written exercises.

Findings from study 1 informed the development and implementation of a modified version of the proposed online workshop from study 1. Specifically, preferences regarding the ideal time frame and writing exercise time structure were incorporated into the workshop. The goal of study 2 was to examine feasibility and concurrent acceptability of the workshop. Measures of feasibility included recruitment and retention rates, workshop comprehension, and measurement appropriateness and burden. Dropout rate was much higher than what has been reported in studies of online interventions for eating disorders [12], but lower than dropout rates for online interventions for psychological disorders in general, which can reach as high as 83% of participants (Melville, Casey, & Kavanagh [24]). Dropout rates between workshop exercises remained relatively stable, meaning that no one task led to a substantial discontinuation in workshop participation.

Measures used in the pre- and post-workshop surveys contained minimal missing data and also had strong psychometric properties. Mean completion times were acceptable. Overall, measures seemed appropriate in capturing the constructs of interest and minimally burdensome to complete. Performance on the comprehension questions was somewhat variable, but overall suggested adequate understanding of workshop content. Interestingly, participants who answered all three post-workshop comprehension questions correctly had the lowest pre- workshop self-compassion level compared to participants who answered one and two out of three questions correctly. It is possible that those with the lowest self-compassion levels may be more invested and interested in learning about self-compassion. Future iterations of this workshop could specifically target disordered eating individuals with low levels of self-compassion. Further, work content modifications could include a comparison of having a self-compassionate versus self-critical mindset, a summary slide at the end that reviewed the workshop’s key points (e.g., three components of self-compassion), and comprehension checks throughout the workshop in addition to a post-workshop comprehension assessment. Doing so may result in greater retention of the key points of the workshop and increased skill practice after the completion of the workshop.

Acceptability was determined through perceptions of utility and helpfulness. Overall, most participants reported that the workshop platform was easy to use, they would recommend the workshop to a friend, and a belief that the workshop skills would have a positive, long-term impact on their eating. Additionally, a majority of participants reported that various components of the workshop to be very or extremely helpful. Together, it appears that various aspects of the workshop were both acceptable and helpful for emerging adults with disordered eating. Overall, findings suggest that an online, self-paced workshop targeting self-compassion skills could be a feasible and acceptable intervention for emerging adults with disordered eating and deserves future exploration to address changes in self-compassion skill development and disordered eating severity as a result of workshop participation.

There are significant strengths to this investigation, perhaps most notably the large sample sizes and demographic diversity of participants who represented a range of gender, racial, and ethnic identities, especially compared to prior work in this field. However, there are also some important limitations to this work that must be noted. While study 1 evaluated attitudes and intentions to participate in a prospective workshop targeting self- compassion, barriers to engaging in such intervention were not examined. Additionally, this study recruited a convenience sample of students participating in exchange for research credits and it is unclear whether emerging adults with disordered eating would be interested and motivated to participate in this workshop in a different context. The workshop format was determined in part by the technical constraints of Qualtrics, the online platform that housed the pre- and post-workshop survey as well as the actual workshop. Future iterations of the workshop would benefit from using a different online platform that allowed for embedding higher quality videos and more interactive and engaging exercises. Furthermore, motivational enhancement strategies (e.g. decisional balance exercise, values exploration) could be incorporated to increase participant’s motivation to engage in the workshop in order to get the most benefit from participation. Lastly, the brief nature of the workshop, while acceptable to the study’s participants, may not be sufficient to sustainably address longstanding disordered eating behaviors and cognitions. It would be beneficial to frame the workshop as an introduction to the concept of and skills to cultivate a self-compassion mindset while also emphasizing the potential need for participants to address their disordered eating concerns with a provider.

Conclusions

Improving self-compassion in emerging adults with disordered eating continues to be an important, yet understudied, treatment target in eating disorder prevention and intervention. Novel approaches to engaging emerging adults in treatment that effectively address factors impacting their disordered eating offer a promising opportunity to increase treatment accessibility. This set of studies demonstrates preliminary support for the prospective and concurrent acceptability as well as feasibility of a brief, self-paced, online workshop targeting self-compassion skills for emerging adults with disordered eating. Future research should assess initial efficacy of the intervention in increasing self-compassion as a protective factor against the development and maintenance of disordered eating behaviors in emerging adults.
