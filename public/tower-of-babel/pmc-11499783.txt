Impact of financial incentives introduced during the COVID-19 pandemic on nursing staff: a mixed-method protocol
Authors: Marianne Beaulieu, Dominique Viens, Mahée Gilbert-Ouimet, Sandra Rossignol, Marie-Pierre Gagnon, Natasha Turmel, Sandra Racine, Marleen Cameron, Nellie Roy, Liliane Bernier, Caroline Gravel, Stéphane Turcotte
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionThe COVID-19 pandemic has had a major impact on nursing staff, resulting in alarming turnover rates. As part of the Quebec (Canada) government’s response to the pandemic, nurses have been offered exceptional financial incentives. Considering the cost of these measures, the current article presents the research protocol of a study aiming to explore the impact of financial incentives on full-time equivalent, and retention rates among the nursing staff in two healthcare settings in Quebec.Methods and analysisA sequential mixed design (QUANT→QUAL) will be used. The quantitative phase will involve a quantitative descriptive analysis and the qualitative phase will consist of a qualitative descriptive study. Administrative data (working hours, employment status and retention rate) will be analysed over a 4.5-year follow-up (from 1 January 2019 to 30 June 2023) to explore the impact of the financial incentives. Focus groups will explore nurses’ views on financial incentives. The results will inform the development of future interventions to mitigate attrition problems among nurses and ultimately improve access to and the continuity of public health services.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by ethics committees of the participating healthcare settings (Comité d’éthique de la recherche sectorial en santé des populations et première ligne du CIUSSS de la Capitale-Nationale; Comité d’éthique de la recherche du CISSS de Chaudière-Appalaches). The results will be disseminated mainly in scientific publications and at academic conferences in addition to presentations tailored to various non-academic audiences.Keywords: Nurses, HEALTH SERVICES ADMINISTRATION & MANAGEMENT, COVID-19, Decision Making, Public Hospitals

Introduction

Background and rationale

While the phenomenon of nursing staff attrition had already been evident for a number of years, the COVID-19 pandemic increased the intention to quit among nursing staff all over the world.1,5 Indeed, the pandemic disproportionately affected the nursing workforce. COVID-19 caused deaths, infections and critical patient needs as well as mental health problems6 7 and increased demands on nursing staff at a time when resources were scarce.1 4

Worldwide ‘every time a nurse leaves a healthcare organisation there is an impact on the nurse, on the healthcare organisation and on the client population of the organisation. There will be organisational costs, and, where nurse retention is poor, there will be negative impacts on patient care and on the workload of nurses who remain.’1(p.46) Nursing turnover results in a loss of productivity due to the time taken to replace staff.8 When new staff are recruited, they tend to be less at ease with the procedures in place. In addition, since new staff generally have less than 5 years’ experience, the more experienced nurses end up being overloaded. Attrition also leads to a reduction in the quality of care (medication errors, patient falls).9 10 Replacement cycles can generate major recruitment and training expenses.10,12 Furthermore, the use of private healthcare agencies to complement the range of public care and services is adding to already high costs.10 12 13 Nursing staff attrition poses a significant threat to human resources planning. Not only does it increase the workload of the remaining staff, it also adds to fatigue, stress and burnout, all of which have a detrimental effect on mental and physical health.9 Ultimately, these consequences have a serious impact on access to care and services.9,11

Decision-makers are thus searching for solutions in terms of attracting and retaining nursing staff.14,16 Among these solutions, financial incentives in various forms are used. In some cases, the average salary has been increased overall to attract new graduates or to retain nurses.17 Many countries around the world offer financial incentives to attract nurses, especially in remote areas.18,22 However, the effectiveness of these initiatives to tackle the nursing shortage is inconsistent and even disputed.23 24 Their effects are often restricted to short-term results (attraction and retention)25 or are of limited scope in relation to the intended objectives.26 27

In the spring of 2020, in Canada, the Quebec Ministry of Health and Social Services started offering exceptional financial incentives to attract and retain staff. These incentives included bonuses, lump sums and increases in hourly wages. However, there is little information available to judge the effectiveness of these types of incentive. Given the expense of such measures, rigorous methods must be used to assess their impact on the nursing workforce. The pandemic provides an opportunity to rethink how the healthcare system is organised with a view to ensuring its sustainability by attracting and retaining nursing staff over the long term.

Objectives

The aim of this study will be to assess the impact of the financial incentives offered to nurses during the COVID-19 pandemic. More specifically, the study will examine the effect of these incentives on changes in full-time equivalent (FTE) and retention rates. Nurses’ views concerning the financial incentives will provide further insights into the analyses. Their potential sustainable solutions to turnover problems will also be explored.

Methods and analysis

Study design

A mixed-methods sequential design (QUANT→QUAL)28 will be used to analyse the impact of financial incentives offered to nurses as a ‘natural’ intervention. The descriptive study will include a quantitative phase followed by a qualitative phase.

The study will involve two healthcare settings (A and B) located in adjoining regions in the province of Quebec (Canada). Healthcare setting A has nearly 100 facilities, including 4 hospitals, and employs 12 000 workers. Healthcare setting B has more than 200 facilities, including 6 hospitals, and employs more than 18 000 workers.

The total population of the regions in which the two healthcare settings are located is more than 1.2 million people (region A: nearly 450 000; region B: over 750 000)29 and the average per capita income is between US$30 000 and US$35 000 (2020 data). The unemployment rate in 2023 is 2.0% for region A and 2.6% for region B.29 Nearly 35% of the population of region A lives in the largest urban area. In region B, 77% of the population lives in the largest urban area (2022 data).29 About 25% of the population in region A and 38% of the population in region B have a university certificate, diploma or degree as the highest level of education, compared with the Quebec average of 36% (2021 data).30

The study was designed with the collaboration of both human resources and nursing departments in participating healthcare settings. A union representative was also consulted when drafting the protocol. For example, the participating healthcare settings were responsible for identifying and defining each of the interventions and actively collaborated in defining the criteria for selecting the participants.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design of this protocol and will not be involved in its conduct, analysis, reporting and dissemination.

Quantitative phase (QUANT)

Data sources

Administrative data will be collected from the human resources departments of the two participating healthcare settings in Quebec. The study will cover the period from 1 January 2019 to 30 June 2023. This period was chosen to include approximately 115 time points in the analyses in order to better understand the ‘seasonality’ effects in the results. 1 January 2019 was selected as the start date to include a long enough period before the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic in Quebec, which was declared a public health emergency on 13 March 2020.31 The end date of 30 June 2023 was chosen to include a few months after most of the government’s financial incentives for nursing staff ended.

The study will include data on nursing staff working for healthcare settings A and B between 1 January 2019 and 30 June 2023. Nursing staff include nurses, nursing assistants, nursing advisors and specialised nurse practitioners. However, data from nursing staff working solely under ‘pandemic status’ (assigned exclusively to screening or vaccinating during the pandemic period) will be excluded from the study. Nurses moving from pandemic status to a regular staff position will be included in the study starting when this change in position takes effect.

Data collection

To create a comprehensive database enabling various levels of analysis, some data will be collected only once while others will be collected on a regular basis.

Data collected only once per nursing staff member will be as follows: gender, age, date of hire, date(s) of position change (if applicable), type of new position(s) (if applicable) and date of exit (if applicable).

The following data will be collected on a regular basis: job category, employment status (casual, regular full time, temporary full time, regular part time and temporary part time), working hours at regular rate, overtime, mandatory overtime, working hours at double rate, payment of incentive bonuses (if applicable). These data will be collected every 2 weeks, following the payroll schedule of healthcare settings A and B.

Outcome measures

Two main outcomes will be documented in the study to identify trends between 1 January 2019 and 30 June 2023.

The first outcome will be employment status (regular full time, regular part time, temporary full time, temporary part time and occasional) consistent with the fact that the interventions of interest operate at an individual level (incentives are paid to each full-time staff member). This outcome will be computed as the proportion of nursing staff working full time.

The second outcome will be the nurse retention rate measured with a stability approach.32 The rate will thus be calculated in percent using the number of employees at the start of each 6-month period who remain employed at the end of this period on the number of employees at the start of this period. A distinction will also be made between new employees and old employees in order to know if turnover mainly affects new employees.33

Contextual variables

The COVID-19 outbreaks and local initiatives by human resources departments will be considered as contextual variables.

Descriptive analyses

Descriptive and visual analyses will be used to examine administrative data between January 2019 and June 2023. For FTE, the data will be pooled into a point every 2-week period (ie, pay periods) while retention rate will be monitored on a 6-months basis. This will allow for graphical representation over time for FTE and financial incentives. Each financial incentive in dollars will also be pictured graphically to better understand the impact of financial incentives on outcomes. For retention, the rate will be calculated with different calendar time frames (every 6 months). The stability rate will be separated by new hire and retained employees to examine if turnover mainly affects new employees. Results will be summarised in a table for this indicator. A stratified description will also be drawn up according to work schedule (regular full time, regular part time, temporary full time, temporary part time, casual), gender and age and years of service groupings for each outcome. These analyses will provide a better understanding of the factors that may influence the main outcomes (FTE and retention rate).

The analyses will be performed by using SAS V.9.4. Findings from these analyses will be presented to nursing staff during the focus group meetings in the qualitative phase. The discussions will help interpret the quantitative results as the changes are likely to be attributable to a combination of complex multifaceted interventions.

The primary aim of the focus groups is to refine the quantitative results, in order to better understand how financial incentives impact FTE and retention rates, taking into account contextual variables (COVID-19 outbreaks and local retention actions). The focus group will give nursing staff an opportunity to be heard and to exercise a certain form of leadership by interpreting the results of the quantitative phase. They will also be involved in brainstorming about potential solutions to improve work conditions. In addition to collecting data at the individual level, focus groups will provide insight into group interactions and helping to identify potential areas of agreement and disagreement.34

Qualitative phase (QUAL): focus groups

Recruitment

Focus groups (6–8 participants per group) will be held until saturation is reached (approximately one group in each healthcare setting). Participants will be recruited via posters and memos. Selection criteria will be the same as those for the quantitative phase, that is, nursing staff working for healthcare settings A and B between 1 January 2019 and 30 June 2023. Nursing staff working solely under ‘pandemic status’ will be excluded again.

Data collection

The focus group will be conducted face to face by a naïve, independent facilitator (ie, not a nursing staff member or employee of the healthcare settings involved). Quantitative results (graphs and tables) will be presented to nursing staff to gather their views on factors impacting FTE and retention.

A group interview guide, based on Al-Haddad and Kotnour’s35 proposed taxonomy of successful organisational change, will be used. This taxonomy relates to change types, change agents and methods; the implementation of financial incentives will be considered as an organisational change. The potential role of contextual variables in the observed trends will be examined in more detail.

Qualitative analysis

Focus group discussions will be audio recorded with participants’ consent. The data will be analysed following the methods described by Braun and Clarke36 to identify, analyse, seek patterns and interpret themes. Recorded material will be transcribed verbatim and imported into NVivo. First, data were coded using the Al-Haddad and Kotnour’s35 taxonomy (deductive process). Then, the codes will be collated to identify emerging themes (inductive process). Two investigators will proceed to preliminary qualitative analyses that will then be reviewed during a meeting with the whole research team. Finally, each theme will be defined and named.

Expected implications

The COVID-19 pandemic, along with a pre-existing ‘shortage’ of nursing staff, has strained healthcare systems. The study will generate ideas on the impact of financial incentives, as well as on other measures that can be put in place to counter the staff ‘shortage’. Identifying effective solutions to improve attraction and retention may help in reducing the pressure on healthcare systems and ensuring the provision of quality care to the population.

Ethics and dissemination

The study has been approved and is being monitored by the Research Ethics Boards of the participating healthcare settings (Comité d’éthique de la recherche sectoriel en santé des populations et première ligne du CIUSSS de la Capitale-Nationale (MP-13-2022-2586); Comité d’éthique de la recherche du CISSS de Chaudière-Appalaches (MEO-13-2023-967)). Various dissemination strategies will be used to maximise the impact of the findings. For the scientific community, at least one article will be published in a peer-reviewed journal. The findings will also be presented at scientific conferences. Efforts will be made to disseminate the results outside academic circles, for example, through panel discussions and lay publications to inform decision-makers and managers in the public sector.
