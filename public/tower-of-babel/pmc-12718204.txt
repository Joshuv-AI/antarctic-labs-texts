A non-affinity-dependent high-resolution mass spectrometry method for detecting and typing monoclonal free light immunoglobulin chains
Authors: Per Bengtson, Magnus Förnvik Jonsson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Simple and robust non-affinity sample preparation.•Able to detect, isotype and semi-quantify monoclonal monomeric and dimeric light chains.•Identifies post-translational modifications.•Well-suited for automation.

Introduction

Automated nephelometric and turbidimetric quantification of kappa and lambda light immunoglobulin chains in serum has become increasingly important for diagnosing, monitoring, and prognosticating diseases associated with monoclonal immunoglobulins [1]. One shortcoming of these methods is that monoclonality is indicated by a surrogate marker, the ratio between the concentrations of free kappa and lambda chains [2]. This may result in false-negative findings when monoclonal light chains are present at low concentrations, particularly when the background of polyclonal light chains is elevated [3], or in false-positive findings, for example when an oligoclonal immunoglobulin response after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation produces an abnormal ratio [4].

Free light chains (FLCs) in serum may exist in different molecular forms. Generally, free kappa chains tend to appear as monomers, while free lambda chains predominantly occur as dimers [5]. However, this pattern may not hold true for every patient. The quaternary structure of an FLC can vary between different clones, and multimeric forms have also been described [6]. Interestingly, monomer/dimer patterns can help distinguish malignant from premalignant monoclonal components [7]. In addition, post-translational modifications appear to be relevant for risk stratification. A glycosylated light chain has been shown to increase the risk of progression from monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance to malignant plasma cell disease [8] and has been associated with amyloidosis and cold agglutinin disease [9,10].

Neither quaternary structure nor glycosylation is revealed by current immunoassays for measuring FLCs. However, high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) has demonstrated the ability to identify, isotype, and quantify monoclonal light chains, as well as to detect glycosylation and determine the monomeric or dimeric state.

HRMS enables direct identification of the monoclonal free light chain (mFLC) and provides the opportunity to characterize the molecular forms present in the sample.

In 2014, Barnidge et al. reported a method in which immunoglobulins enriched from serum, were reduced and analyzed in intact form on liquid chromatography (LC) HRMS, or more specifically LC electrospray ionization quadrupole time-of-flight (ESI-Q-TOF) mass spectrometry. In subsequent publications, the same group examined reduced urine samples as well as reduced and non-reduced serum samples [11,12].

From 2015 onward, the focus shifted primarily from LC-ESI-Q-TOF to matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI-TOF) analysis. Initially, the same albumin depletion method (Melon Gel) was used [13]. Subsequently, a more selective approach was developed, named MASS-FIX, in which selective nanobodies for IgG, IgA, total FLC, free kappa FLC, and free lambda FLC were employed for enrichment prior to analysis. This approach significantly reduced background noise and achieved higher selectivity and sensitivity [14,15]. Since then, MASS-FIX has become the mass spectrometry method of choice for the analysis of monoclonal immunoglobulins in clinical settings, both for intact monoclonal immunoglobulins [16,17] and for diagnosing and monitoring mFLCs [18,19].

Recently, Yeung et al. published an article describing HRMS analysis of mFLCs using a novel approach to FLC enrichment [20]. On-probe extraction X (OPEX) immunocapture was performed with microprobes loaded with anti-kappa or anti-lambda light chain antibodies. Thus, kappa and lambda selectivity was achieved during the enrichment step, partially resembling the approach used in MASS-FIX. The solution containing the immuno-enriched FLCs was then analyzed by LC-HRMS to determine possible clonality.

The main advantages of MALDI-TOF over LC-HRMS are higher throughput, lower complexity, and reduced equipment costs. On the preparative side of the analysis, immuno-based enrichment provides high selectivity and sensitivity with very low background. However, dependence on capture antibodies makes the method highly reliant on antibody quality and subject to common limitations of immuno-based techniques, such as batch-to-batch variability and potential cross-reactivity [20].

Here, we report the development of a non–affinity-dependent LC-HRMS method capable of identifying, isotyping, and semi-quantifying monoclonal monomeric and dimeric FLCs in serum, as well as detecting glycosylation. The method was developed for use in the clinical laboratory and includes a preparative step based on ultrafiltration. We evaluated its precision and accuracy, including a comparison with an immunochemistry-based method.

Materials and methods

Patient sample cohorts

The study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority. Consecutive patient samples from three randomly selected days were collected following routine serum FLC analysis performed at the Clinical Chemistry Laboratory in Region Skåne, Sweden. Blood was drawn into tubes with a gel separator and clot activator, allowed to clot for at least 30 min, and centrifuged within 4 h. Thereafter, S-FLC was measured using a particle-enhanced nephelometric immunoassay with N Latex FLC kappa and N Latex FLC lambda reagents, following the package insert instructions on a BNII instrument (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). After analysis, serum was decanted into polypropylene tubes and frozen at − 80 °C. During this process, FLC results were retrieved from the laboratory information management system and anonymized together with the serum samples. No clinical exclusion criteria were applied to the cohort. Samples were transferred frozen to the Clinical Chemistry Laboratory in Halmstad, Halland, Sweden, for mFLC analysis by LC-HRMS. Measurements were performed within two months of receipt.

Reagents and materials

Amicon Ultra-0.5 centrifugal MWCO filter devices with regenerated cellulose membranes (cutoff 3 or 100 kDa), ammonium bicarbonate, acetonitrile, dithiothreitol, methanol, leucine enkephalin, and formic acid were purchased from Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany. The custom recombinant lambda FLC protein pr0016 was obtained from Absolute Antibody Ltd (Redcar, United Kingdom). The amino acid sequence is provided in Supplemental Fig. 1.The LC column (Agilent Poroshell 300SB-C3, microbore 1.0 × 75 mm, 5 µm) was purchased from Agilent Technologies (Santa Clara, CA).

LC-HRMS

Sample preparation

Standards were prepared by diluting recombinant lambda FLC, pr0016, in mFLCs-negative serum to concentrations of 250, 62.5, 15.625 and 3.90625 mg/L. Samples with observed concentrations above 250 mg/L were reported as > 250 mg/L. Pr0016 is present in its dimer form with an observed average mass of 45590 Da.

Sample preparation was performed using ultrafiltration with MWCO membranes. Serum samples were thawed at room temperature for one hour. The thawed samples were mixed twice by inversion and vortexed at medium speed. Ammonium bicarbonate (50 mM, 475 µL) was pipetted into a 100 kDa MWCO spin column, followed by 25 µL of serum, standard, or control. The columns were closed, inverted twice, and centrifuged at 14000 x g for 10 min. The flow-through was then transferred to a 3 kDa MWCO spin column and centrifuged at 14000 × g for 30 min. The retained fraction was collected by inverting the MWCO columns and centrifuging at 1500 x g for 2 min (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the sample preparation workflow. Panel A is normal workflow and panel B is workflow with reduction included. The total time for ultracentrifugation is 42 min.

Schematic illustration of the sample preparation workflow. Panel A is normal workflow and panel B is workflow with reduction included. The total time for ultracentrifugation is 42 min.

For samples requiring reduction, 50 µL of 100 mM dithiothreitol in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate was added to the flow-through after 100 kDa filtration and incubated at 55 °C for 30 min on a rotational shaker at 600 rpm before 3 kDa filtration.

Analytical setup

Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters H-Class LC system (Waters Corporation, Milford, Massachusetts, USA) equipped with a C3 column. The LC setup was chemically similar to that reported by Barnidge in 2016 [12], with only minor modifications to the LC conditions to better accommodate the sample matrix and available equipment. The column was maintained at 80 °C. A 10 µL sample injection was used with a flow rate of 300 µL/min, and the total run time was 8 min.

The aqueous solvent (mobile phase A) consisted of water with 0.1 % formic acid, and the organic solvent (mobile phase B) was acetonitrile with 0.1 % formic acid. A gradient was applied to elute FLCs, starting at 99 % A/1% B, decreasing to 95 % A/5% B over 1 min, then to 75 % A/25 % B over 1.9 min, further to 5 % A/95 % B over 0.1 min, held for 4.6 min, and finally re-equilibrated at 99 % A/1% B for 0.4 min.

Mass spectra were acquired on a Waters Vion Q-TOF (Waters Corporation) operated in positive mode with an ESI ion source equipped with automated lock mass correction. Leucine enkephalin (100 pg/µL) was used as the lock mass. Source conditions were as follows: capillary voltage, 2000 V; sample cone voltage, 50 V; source offset voltage, 80 V; source temperature, 150 °C; desolvation temperature, 500 °C; cone gas flow, 100 L/h; and desolvation gas flow, 1000 L/h.

MS1 scans were acquired over an m/z range of 550–2000 with an acquisition time of 400 ms. MS2 scans were acquired over an m/z range of 400–1200 with an acquisition time of 600 ms. Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) triggering for MS2 was set at 1000 counts. Argon was used as the collision gas, and collision energies for MS2 scans were ramped from 25 to 65 V. The instrument was routinely calibrated using a ready-to-use calibration solution (Major Mix, Waters).

Data analysis

Waters UNIFI software (version 2.1.2.14) was used for instrument control, data visualization, MS2 fragment filtering, and MS1 deconvolution of intact masses. Deconvolution using the Maximum Entropy (MaxEnt1) algorithm was performed over a mass range of 13,000 to 70000 Da with a step size of 0.1 Da and an S/N threshold of 10.

Further data evaluation and mass matching were conducted using the open-source R Statistical Software (v4.4.1; R Core Team, 2024), as outlined in Fig. 2. Concentrations were determined from the centroid response of deconvoluted masses. Cysteinylated transthyretin was used as an internal control for sample preparation. The centroid peak response of deconvoluted transthyretin was evaluated in all samples. The response distribution was examined, and a threshold was established based on the sample with the lowest transthyretin response that still tested positive for mFLC. Samples with responses below this threshold were considered potentially affected by pre-analytical error and were re-prepared and re-analyzed.Fig. 2Schematic illustration of the analytical workflow.

Limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ)

In the HRMS method, a sample was considered positive if at least one unique mFLC intact mass was detected above LOD and the kappa or lambda identity of the mFLC was confirmed. The intact mass, obtained from deconvolution of MS1 data, had to fall within the range of 20–26.5 kDa and/or 40–53 kDa to meet the mFLC criteria. Kappa or lambda identity was confirmed by detecting at least two unique kappa- or lambda-specific DDA MS2 fragments originating from MS1 mass numbers within the charge envelope of the intact mFLC.

The lowest mFLC concentration at which a unique FLC DDA MS2 fragment was detected (LOD) was 1 mg/L. No quantification was performed below the lowest standard in the calibration curve (3.9 mg/L), corresponding to the LOQ. This follows the recommendation that, for reliable detection, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the analyte peak should be at least 3:1 for LOD and 10:1 for LOQ (Supplemental Fig. 4).

MS2 fragments unique to the constant regions of kappa or lambda were used to determine light chain isotype. A complete list of unique type-specific fragments is provided in Supplemental Table 1, and fragment coverage is illustrated in Supplemental Fig. 2. Fragment identities were confirmed using MS-Product (ProteinProspector, UCSF, CA).

The relationship between standard response and known concentration was fitted using a four-parameter logistic curve function [21].

Precision

As high and low controls, two mFLC samples, one containing a monomer and the other a dimer, respectively, were mixed, diluted with mFLC-negative serum, aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C. The two control sera (high and low) were prepared according to the standard protocol and analyzed on four separate occasions, resulting in 15 measurements for each control.

Dilution linearity

Four patient sera with relatively high mFLC concentrations were selected and serially diluted in mFLC-negative serum. Each original sample and its dilutions were prepared according to the standard protocol.

Carryover

Potential carryover was assessed by injecting an ultrafiltered mFLC-negative serum sample immediately after analyzing the sample with the highest recorded HRMS mFLC concentration.

Sample stability

Four samples at different concentrations were tested by comparing mFLC concentrations before and after a freeze–thaw cycle. The stability of prepared samples was evaluated by comparing mFLC concentrations from twenty samples before and after overnight storage at 10 °C.

Method comparison

The ability to identify mFLC was compared between the HRMS and the nephelometric FLC immunoassay (im-FLC) using a fourfold table.

HRMS: A sample was considered positive for an mFLC if the response was above the LOD and the type (kappa or lambda) could be determined.

im-FLC: A sample was considered positive for a kappa mFLC if both the concentration of free kappa and the kappa/lambda ratio were above the reference ranges (6.7–22.4 mg/L and 0.31–1.56, respectively) [20]. A sample was considered positive for a lambda mFLC if the concentration of free lambda was elevated and the kappa/lambda ratio was below the reference range (8.3–27.0 mg/L and 0.31–1.56, respectively) [22].

Results

The goal of serum protein preparation prior to mass spectrometric analysis is generally twofold: to deplete albumin and removal of non-volatile salts. Most existing depletion methods rely on either precipitation or affinity-based techniques. In this study, the use of two different MWCO spin filters provided a simple yet robust alternative for sample preparation.

In the first filtration step (100 kDa), high–molecular-weight components of diluted serum, including intact antibodies, were retained. Although albumin has a theoretical molecular weight of approximately 66 kDa and would, therefore, be expected to pass through the filter, most albumin molecules were found to be retained by the 100 kDa filter as well. The second filtration step (3 kDa MWCO) concentrated the FLC molecules. The resulting ultrafiltrates exhibited significantly lower concentrations of albumin and non-volatile salts, which led to markedly reduced background signals and noise.

To validate the preanalytical preparation, the HRMS signal strength from ten purified clones (including the calibrator Pr0016) diluted in FLC-negative serum and processed through the ultrafiltration steps was compared with the signal obtained when the same clones were diluted in ammonium bicarbonate buffer and injected directly into the mass spectrometer. Recovery exceeded 95 %.

All analyzed mFLC molecules—whether purified or in serum matrix—had retention times between 2.45 and 2.8 min. Residual albumin eluted distinctly later, at approximately 3.4 min. A representative chromatogram showing mFLC and albumin separation is provided in Supplemental Fig. 3.

All analyzed mFLCs exhibited the majority of their MS1 charge envelope signals within the m/z range of 1000–2000. The HRMS was programmed to alternate between acquiring intact MS1 ions and fragmenting MS2 ions derived from MS1 ions in the m/z 1000–2000 range that exceeded the DDA intensity threshold. In addition to exhibiting slightly different retention times, most FLC molecules also differed in their intact masses and corresponding charge envelopes. Carryover

Patient samples with HRMS mFLC concentrations below 1048 mg/L showed no significant carryover. When carryover did occur, it was below the LOD. However, injections of purified mFLCs at concentrations well above 1000 mg/L resulted in noticeable carryover. Such concentrations are expected to occur in only a small fraction of real-world samples and can be managed by monitoring intact masses in subsequent analyses.

Sample stability

A freeze–thaw cycle resulted in recovery rates exceeding 90 % for the measured mFLC concentrations. Furthermore, Deming regression analysis (95 % confidence interval) showed no significant systematic or proportional differences in HRMS results for prepared samples stored overnight at 10 °C.

Eleven samples were positive by HRMS (four kappa and seven lambda) but negative by im-FLC. All but one had mFLC concentrations above LOQ. Seven of these eleven samples showed high im-FLC concentrations for both kappa and lambda but a normal ratio, while the remaining four had normal or decreased concentrations according to im-FLC. Among these eleven, sample no. 43 contained only kappa dimers, and samples no. 57 and 62 contained only lambda monomers (Supplemental Table 2).

Five samples positive by im-FLC (all kappa) were negative by HRMS. Two of these showed elevated im-FLC concentrations for both kappa and lambda with a slightly increased ratio; two others had slightly elevated kappa concentrations and ratios. For one of these (no. 95), HRMS detected intact proteins with masses of 23,551 and 46863 Da. The fifth sample (no. 76) was clearly positive by im-FLC, but negative by HRMS.

Discussion

The method presented is a non–affinity-dependent HRMS approach incorporating an ultrafiltration-based preparative step.

Overall concordance between HRMS and the im-FLC method in identifying mFLCs was good (Table 3). However, eleven samples were positive by HRMS but negative by im-FLC. In four of these, the involved chain concentration was normal or decreased according to im-FLC, while in the remaining seven, FLC concentrations were increased but the ratio was normal. These findings suggest that HRMS is more sensitive than im-FLC for detecting mFLCs and highlight a limitation of the im-FLC method: a high background of polyclonal light chains can mask the presence of a monoclonal fraction.

In contrast, five samples were positive by im-FLC (all kappa) but negative by HRMS. In these cases, im-FLC values (kappa/lambda ratios) were only mildly abnormal. Because an increased light chain concentration and abnormal ratio are indirect indicators of monoclonality, it remains uncertain whether these samples actually contained mFLCs. In one of these five samples, peaks below the LOQ, potentially corresponding to cysteinylated monomers and noncysteinylated dimers, were observed, but DDA analysis failed to confirm FLC origin. The tendency of mFLCs to exist in monomeric and/or dimeric forms, combined with post-translational modifications, can disperse the signal across several distinct masses, thereby reducing detectability. Consequently, the concentration threshold for detecting an mFLC depends on clone- and patient-specific factors.

Because each clone is unique, precise quantification of intact mFLCs is inherently challenging. Ideally, an internal standard and calibrator identical in primary structure to the analyte would be used, but this was not feasible with the current method. Nonetheless, both spiking experiments with purified clones (including the calibrator) in FLC-negative serum and dilution-linearity testing of patient samples demonstrated acceptable recovery. This indicates that clones behave similarly to the calibrator during pre-analytical processing and that patient samples exhibit comparable dilution characteristics. Thus, for most patient clones, concentration monitoring using the HRMS method is expected to be reliable.

Sensitivity could be further improved by incorporating reduction and/or deglycosylation steps, although such modifications would increase sample preparation complexity and eliminate potentially valuable biological information. Alternatively, higher sensitivity could be achieved by upgrading to a newer mass spectrometer model. The HRMS instrument used in this study does not meet current-generation specifications for speed and sensitivity; preliminary data with a newer model suggest an approximately fourfold improvement in sensitivity.

The observed proportions of monomeric and dimeric forms of kappa and lambda mFLCs were consistent with the findings of Sölling [5]. In that gel filtration–based study, most kappa chains were monomers or non-covalently linked dimers, whereas lambda chains primarily formed stable, covalently linked dimers. Covalently linked kappa dimers were also detected, though in smaller numbers. With the present HRMS method, non-covalently linked dimers are unlikely to be observed because they probably dissociate into monomers during ionization, explaining the predominance of the monomeric form among kappa mFLCs.

Because both quaternary structure (monomeric versus dimeric state, as shown by SDS-PAGE under nonreducing conditions) and post-translational modifications (identified by mass spectrometry) have been associated with disease risk [7,8], the HRMS method described here may serve as a valuable tool for future studies investigating the clinical relevance of these molecular phenotypes.

Conclusions

The HRMS method developed can detect, type, and semi-quantify monoclonal monomeric and dimeric FLCs in serum, as well as identify glycosylation. The sample preparation procedure relies on robust, low-cost laboratory materials, with multiple supplier alternatives available to ensure continuity of supply. Preliminary studies indicate that the method is well-suited for automation and compatible with a 96-well format.

An endogenous internal standard provides an inherent quality control of the sample preparation, confirming that the correct serum fraction is being analyzed. The method may be particularly valuable for demonstrating the presence or absence of mFLCs at low concentrations and for monitoring individual clones – applications that could support diagnosis, risk stratification, and follow-up in a range of B-cell diseases.
