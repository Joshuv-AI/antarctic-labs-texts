Cutaneous effects of antihypertensive drugs: A comprehensive narrative review of side effects, management, and prevention
Authors: Luluh Z. Aljaloud, Mashael T. Alanazi, Kadi S. Alsubaie, Sali F. Alharbi, Sharifa M. Aljalfan, Sarah H. Alwadai, Walaa M. Aleran, Ruaa Alharithy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHypertension, a major global health burden affecting over one billion people worldwide, is primarily managed with pharmacological therapy. While effective for cardiovascular protection, antihypertensive drugs can cause under-recognized cutaneous adverse drug reactions (CADRs). This review summarizes CADRs associated with antihypertensive medications, including pathophysiology, clinical presentation, diagnosis, management, and prevention. A narrative review of PubMed (January 2001–August 2025) was conducted to identify studies on CADRs linked to antihypertensive therapy, and observational studies, case reports, case series, and clinical trials were included. CADRs arise through immunologic (Types I–IV hypersensitivity, most often Type IV) and non-immunologic mechanisms. Clinical presentations range from mild eruptions (maculopapular rash, photosensitivity, urticaria) to severe reactions such as Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN). Symptoms usually occur within weeks of initiation, though delayed reactions are possible. Diagnosis relies on detailed medication history, examination, and, in selected cases, histopathology, and resolution after drug withdrawal is a key diagnostic clue. Management involves discontinuing the offending agent, supportive care, and pharmacological interventions (e.g. corticosteroids, antihistamines). Preventive strategies include avoidance of reexposure, substitution with safer alternatives, and strict photoprotection. Antihypertensive medications can cause diverse CADRs with variable severity and clinical impact, and early recognition and prompt management are essential to minimize morbidity. Prospective studies with standardized definitions and pharmacogenomic approaches are needed to improve risk prediction and guide safer, individualized prescribing.

Introduction

Hypertension affects over one billion people worldwide and remains a leading cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.[12] Long-term therapy with thiazide diuretics, beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers (CCBs), angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) has reduced risk but also introduces cutaneous adverse drug reactions (CADRs).[3] CADRs are under-recognized in routine care, and their incidence is not well defined.[4] This review consolidates current evidence on mechanisms, clinical patterns, diagnostic work-up, and prevention/management to guide safer prescribing across primary care, cardiology, and dermatology. For practitioners, we synthesize class-specific red flags, substitution choices within/across classes, and when to refer, drawing on contemporary allergy practice parameters.[4] For patients, we emphasize identification and photoprotection to reduce complications and guide therapy decisions.[5]

Methods

A PubMed literature review (January 2001–August 2025) was undertaken to investigate CADRs associated with antihypertensive medications. Inclusion criteria were studies assessing cutaneous adverse effects in patients receiving antihypertensive drugs, of any design (observational studies, case reports, clinical trials, or reviews), that mentioned population, intervention, comparison, and outcome (PICO)-related keywords such as “antihypertensive agents,” “cutaneous adverse effects,” “skin reactions,” “rash,” “angioedema,” or “photosensitivity,” and were published in English. Exclusion criteria applied to studies unrelated to antihypertensives or cutaneous outcomes, reporting only systemic effects, or involving nonhuman subjects. The selection process followed preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, with final numbers of included and excluded studies detailed in the flow chart [Figure 1].[6]

Mechanisms of Cutaneous Reactions to Antihypertensive Drugs

CADRs to antihypertensive medications stem from immunologic and non-immunologic processes that vary among drug classes and patient factors.[7]

Immunologic Mechanisms

Immunologic CADRs represent hypersensitivity reactions commonly categorized using the Gell and Coombs framework.[4]

Type I (immediate) reactions are IgE-mediated with rapid onset and may present as urticaria, angioedema, or anaphylaxis; ACE inhibitors can also cause bradykinin-mediated angioedema via a nonallergic pathway.[4]

Type IV (delayed-type) reactions are T-cell mediated, often the predominant mechanism with antihypertensive-related eruptions, and encompass exanthematous, urticarial, vasculitic, blistering, pustular, and photoallergic patterns.[7]

Non-immunologic Mechanisms

Nonimmune CADRs arise from direct pharmacologic effects, drug–drug interactions, or host susceptibility, including predictable dose-dependent reactions such as vasodilation-related flushing or edema. Phototoxic photosensitivity occurs when a drug or metabolite absorbs ultraviolet radiation and causes direct cellular injury, producing exaggerated sunburn-like reactions. CADR expression reflects interactions among mechanisms, drug properties, comorbidities, concomitant therapies, and genetic variability, and understanding these pathways supports accurate diagnosis and management.[7]

Cutaneous Adverse Effects

CADRs associated with antihypertensive medications range from mild, self-limited eruptions to severe, potentially life-threatening conditions that can affect the quality of life and adherence; therefore, early recognition and appropriate management are essential.[7]

Mild Reactions

Maculopapular eruptions are common, nonspecific, and reported with multiple antihypertensive classes, including thiazide diuretics and CCBs. These eruptions are typically pruritic and resolve after withdrawal of the offending agent.[7] Urticaria presents as transient, intensely pruritic wheals; ACE inhibitors are a recognized cause.[8] Diltiazem can cause photodistributed hyperpigmentation consistent with a phototoxic pattern.[9] Prompt identification, patient education, and appropriate substitution help maintain adherence to essential antihypertensive therapy.

Moderate Reactions

Photosensitivity, an abnormal cutaneous response to ultraviolet radiation, is a recognized adverse effect across several antihypertensive classes.[10] Thiazides are well-documented culprits for photodistributed erythema and eczematous reactions, typically via photosensitizing effects.[7] Long-term thiazide exposure carries a photocarcinogenic signal, notably lip cancer with hydrochlorothiazide.[11] Photosensitivity has also been reported with ACE inhibitors, CCBs, and beta-blockers, but evidence is less consistent for these agents.[10] Angioedema can be life-threatening when the airway is involved; ACE inhibitors are the most common cause via bradykinin accumulation.[12] Angioedema associated with CCBs appears rare.[13] Reported risk factors include female sex, smoking, and a history of prior angioedema.[14] Triple therapy combining an ACE inhibitor, a CCB, and a dipeptidyl peptidase-IV (DPP-IV) inhibitor has been associated with increased risk.[15]

Severe Reactions

Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) are rare, severe mucocutaneous reactions; antihypertensives are infrequently implicated, with isolated reports involving CCBs.[16] Among antihypertensives, thiazides, especially hydrochlorothiazide, show the most consistent skin-cancer signal, including lip cancer with prolonged use.[11] For CCBs, earlier meta-analyses suggested a modest melanoma signal.[17] More recent large studies did not confirm this after adjustment for ultraviolet exposure and other confounders.[18] Accordingly, CCBs should not be avoided solely on cancer concerns; emphasis should be placed on photoprotection and routine skin surveillance in at-risk patients [Figure 2].[10]

Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis

Accurate CADR diagnosis is crucial for preventing complications and ensuring continuous hypertension management. Clinical presentation varies widely, from mild to severe.[3] Symptoms typically manifest within weeks, though delayed reactions are documented.[3]

History and Physical Examination

Meticulous patient history is the cornerstone for establishing causality, including medication review, prior drug allergies, symptom characteristics, exposure history (for photosensitivity), and comorbidities.[3] Thorough physical exam characterizes nature, extent, and severity, including full skin assessment (mucous membranes, hair, nails) and general systemic evaluation.[3] Observation of primary lesions and distribution helps narrow the differential diagnosis.[3] Mucous membrane involvement is critical for suspected SJS/TEN, and signs of systemic involvement indicate severe reaction.[12]

Diagnostic Investigations

Further investigations may be needed for ambiguous cases, and skin biopsy can provide histopathological confirmation.[3] Laboratory investigations (complete blood count (CBC), renal/liver function tests) are indicated when systemic involvement is suspected.[12] Drug provocation testing and standardized diagnostic approaches have been emphasized in recent clinical guidelines to improve the accuracy of drug hypersensitivity assessment.[19] Drug discontinuation and rechallenge (if safe) strongly support causality.[20] Causality assessment tools like the Naranjo Scale objectively assess drug causality.[12] Specific tests include skin patch tests (for delayed hypersensitivity) and cytokine-induced tests (lymphocyte activation), while oral rechallenge is a definitive but high-risk method.[20]

Management and Prevention

Effective CADR management is critical for patient safety, treatment adherence, and optimal hypertension control. Strategies vary by reaction type and severity.

Photosensitivity

Management begins with withdrawal of the offending drug, with symptomatic relief achieved using topical or systemic corticosteroids.[7] Preventive measures such as strict photoprotection through sun avoidance, protective clothing, and sunscreen use are essential and may permit the continuation of necessary photosensitizing thiazides when alternatives are lacking.[10]

Contact Dermatitis and Angioedema

For contact dermatitis, complete avoidance of the offending agent is the management cornerstone, with topical corticosteroids providing symptomatic relief for inflammatory lesions.[12]

Angioedema requires immediate recognition, particularly when linked to ACE inhibitors or CCBs. Permanent discontinuation of the causative drug is mandatory, and airway protection is the highest priority in cases of laryngeal involvement, with systemic corticosteroids and antihistamines commonly used for acute management.[12] In severe ACE inhibitor-induced cases, bradykinin pathway modulators such as icatibant or C1 esterase inhibitor concentrate may be considered.[21] To prevent recurrence, substitution with a different drug class is essential, and ARBs should generally be avoided following ACE inhibitor-induced angioedema. Other Cutaneous Adverse Drug Reactions

Management of additional CADRs involves immediate discontinuation of the causative drug and supportive care. Exfoliative rash requires supportive measures including intravenous fluids, electrolyte replacement, nutritional support, and systemic corticosteroids.[22] Psoriasiform eruptions typically resolve after drug withdrawal.[23] Pemphigus vulgaris necessitates withdrawal of the trigger (e.g., captopril) and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy.[24] Telangiectasia improves with discontinuation of the causative drug.[25] While less common than with ACE inhibitors, calcium channel blockers—particularly amlodipine—have been associated with angioedema, as supported by documented clinical cases.[26] Consequently, standardized emergency department management and disposition strategies have been described to effectively address these acute presentations.[27] Photodistributed hyperpigmentation should be managed by replacing diltiazem, enforcing strict photoprotection, and, when appropriate, applying topical agents such as tacrolimus 0.1% or hydroquinone 4% to reduce pigmentation.[9] A consolidated overview of these reactions and their initial management is given in Table 1.

Discussion

Earlier meta-analyses suggested a modest melanoma signal with CCBs (odds ratio ≈ 1.2).[17] More recent studies that adjusted for ultraviolet exposure and other confounders did not confirm this association.[18] By contrast, hydrochlorothiazide has been associated with photocarcinogenic risk, including lip cancer.[11] Beyond summarizing associations, this review emphasizes pragmatic management, including photoprotection strategies that can allow continuation when alternatives are limited.[10] It also details bradykinin pathway options for severe ACE inhibitor-induced angioedema and when to substitute within or across classes.[21]

Limitations and future directions

This narrative review lacked a formal risk-of-bias appraisal, so selection and publication bias are possible. Much of the evidence comes from case reports, small series, pharmacovigilance signals, and retrospective cohorts, limiting causal inference and incidence estimates. For cancer outcomes, residual confounding by ultraviolet exposure, skin phototype, and smoking further constrains interpretation; a comparison of older and newer analyses is provided in the Discussion section.[18] Future work should include prospective cohorts with standardized dermatologic endpoints and robust adjustment for ultraviolet exposure, skin type, and smoking. Rare but serious events warrant registries and linked datasets, and class substitution should be evaluated using contemporary angioedema guidance.[1221] Drug-allergy practice parameters can further standardize diagnostic and management pathways.[12]

Conclusion

CADRs to antihypertensive agents span a broad spectrum, from mild rashes to severe, life-threatening conditions such as SJS and TEN. All major classes, including CCBs, diuretics, ACE inhibitors, and beta-blockers, have been implicated, with risk shaped by drug properties, patient susceptibility, and underlying mechanisms. Given their widespread use, vigilance in recognition and timely management are essential to reduce morbidity and mortality. The evidence linking antihypertensives to skin cancer is uneven. Hydrochlorothiazide shows a consistent association with photocarcinogenesis, whereas findings for CCBs and other classes remain inconclusive and do not justify avoidance in practice. Clinicians should instead balance cardiovascular benefit against dermatologic risk, emphasizing photoprotection and regular surveillance in high-risk patients. Future research should prioritize prospective studies with standardized outcome measures and diverse populations, while advances in pharmacogenomics may enable more precise risk stratification. Ultimately, collaboration across specialties will be critical to optimize hypertension management while safeguarding cutaneous health.
