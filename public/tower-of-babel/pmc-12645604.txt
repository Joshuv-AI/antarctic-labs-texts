Antibiotic stewardship in suspected neutropenic fever (ASTERIC trial): a multicentre, type 1 hybrid effectiveness-implementation, stepped-wedge, randomised controlled trial study protocol
Authors: Timothy H Rainer, Rex Pui Kin Lam, Tat Chi Tsang, Abraham Ka-Chung Wai, Siu Chung Leung, Rock Yuk Yan Leung, Carlos King Ho Wong, Harinder Gill, Wendy W T Lam, Wendy Wing Lok Chan, Arthur Chi Kin Cheung, Man Ting Lau, Shun Fung Lee, Yu Fai Choi, Henry Fong Lun Lee, Ka Leung Mok, Ho Chuen Lam, Shi Yeow Lee, Manson Ho Ting Yeung, Ivan Fan-Ngai Hung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNeutropenic fever (NF) has a crude mortality rate of 3–18%. International guidelines recommend that all patients with NF receive ultrabroad-spectrum antibiotics (UBSAs) within 1 hour of emergency department (ED) registration. However, over 70% patients presenting to hospital with suspected NF (sNF) cannot access absolute neutrophil count (ANC) result within 1 hour, do not have NF and do not require UBSAs. In ED and hospitalised patients with sNF, we hypothesise that the ASTERIC protocol effectively and safely reduces the use of UBSAs compared with standard care alone.Methods and analysisThis pragmatic, parallel, multicentre, type 1, hybrid effectiveness-implementation, stepped-wedge, before-and-after, cluster randomised controlled trial aims to evaluate whether antibiotic prescribing can be safely reduced through implementing a multifaceted antibiotic stewardship intervention (ASTERIC) in adult patients with sNF presenting to EDs. The sNF was defined as a fever with a single oral temperature of ≥38.3°C (101°F) within 24 hours before ED registration or a temperature of ≥38.0°C (100.4°F) sustained over a 1-hour period, following last chemotherapy or targeted therapy within 6 weeks for any solid tumour, or in any period following therapies against leucaemia, lymphoma, myelodysplastic syndrome, aplastic anaemia, multiple myeloma or recipient of HSCT. The study will involve eight hospitals in Hong Kong with variable baseline practice. We will include 704 adult patients (352 patients in pre-implementation and post-implementation periods, respectively) with sNF (tympanic temperature ≥38.3°C) and 48 staff participants (6 staff participants in each hospital). Healthcare professionals will receive a multifaceted stewardship intervention consisting of risk assessment tools, fast-track ANCs, a decision tool for patient management and antibiotic use, supported by an educational package and staff interaction programmes (ASTERIC protocol). Patients’ blood ANC, and cancer therapy and chronic illness therapy scores will be measured. The RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance) and Proctor conceptual frameworks will be followed for evaluation of implementation. The main outcome measures are the mean total dose of UBSAs prescribed in 7 days and serious adverse events at 30 days. Data analysis will incorporate intention-to-treat, per-protocol and as-treated analyses for service outcomes (effectiveness, safety, quality of life assessments and cost-effectiveness) and mixed methods for implementation outcomes, informed by the Theoretical Domains Framework. We expect that the study results will inform health policy with improvement in hospital services in treating stable sNF, evidenced by improved safe antibiotic stewardship, early antibiotic de-escalation and reduced costs and length of stay.Ethics and disseminationThe institutional review boards of all study sites approved this study. This study will establish the ASTERIC protocol safely improves antibiotic stewardship and clinical management in adult patients with sNF. We will disseminate the findings through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and educational activities. All patients with sNF will be influenced by the new protocol which is agreed at hospital level. Randomisation is at hospital level, not patient level. Patient consent is sought for follow-up and data access, not for treatment. Staff consent is sought for interviewing.Trial registration numberNCT06794320.Keywords: INFECTIOUS DISEASES, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, ACCIDENT & EMERGENCY MEDICINE, Clinical Protocols, Clinical Trial, Implementation Science

Introduction

Neutropenic fever (NF), or febrile neutropenia, is characterised by a high body temperature and low absolute neutrophil count (ANC) after myelosuppressive cancer treatment.1 It occurs in patients with early-stage and metastatic solid tumours, non-leukaemic haematological cancers and acute leukaemia.1 2 The crude mortality rate varies from 3% to 18%.3 4 In the USA, each year, 7.83 per 1000 patients with cancer are hospitalised with NF.4 The mean direct hospitalisation costs of managing NF vary from €3950 (about HKD$34 000) to US$19 110 (about HKD$150 000) per patient.5 6

NF can have infectious and non-infectious causes.7 Although 30–50% cases appear clinically to be infections, only 20–30% have microbiologically documented infections.8 Bloodstream infections, bacterial translocations from the respiratory tract and perianal region, and central venous catheter-related infections are major sources. Fever may be the only manifestation of infection during neutropenia because the typical signs of inflammation are obscured. Neutropenia usually results from myelosuppressive cancer treatments, pre-engraftment phases of haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), bone marrow failure and/or defective neutrophil maturation.9

Local public emergency departments (EDs) have standard operating procedures and guidelines for managing NF (see figure 1). In line with international guidelines, the target time from ED registration to antibiotic administration (door-to-antibiotic time, DTA time) is 1-hour disregarding ANC.10 11 The clinical pathways for suspected NF (sNF) expedites medical consultation, septic workup and early prescription of ultrabroad-spectrum antibiotics (UBSAs) such as meropenem or piperacillin/tazobactam. After implementing clinical pathways in local EDs, DTA times have shortened.12

However, over 75–86% patients presenting to Queen Mary Hospital in Hong Kong with sNF have an ANC>1×109/L, do not have NF, do not require UBSAs and possibly do not require hospital admission.13 14 Widespread, inappropriate use of UBSAs may contribute to the emergence of antimicrobial resistance and multidrug resistance (MDR) strains.15 Thus, there is a need for prudent, pragmatic, personalised antibiotic stewardship and appropriate de-escalation.

As patients with cancer are frequently prescribed antibiotics, they are more vulnerable to MDR and are in special need of antibiotic stewardship.16 UBSAs are often started empirically in the ED for patients with sNF, assuming infection by drug-resistant bacteria. However, clinicians infrequently de-escalate once antibiotics have been started. Prolonged exposure to parenteral broad-spectrum antibiotic (BSA) imposes risks of nosocomial infection and injection site complications. There is also evidence that meeting the 1-hour target does not improve outcomes but that using appropriate antibiotic regimes does.17

There is reasonable evidence that patients with NF presenting to hospital benefit from early UBSA/BSA treatment, and that patients with a high Multinational Association for Supportive Care in Cancer (MASCC) Risk Index do not need to be admitted to hospital.10 The Infectious Diseases Society of America recommends oral empirical therapy for low-risk patients with NF with an MASCC score ≥21. For the Clinical Index of Stable Febrile Neutropenia, a score of 0, indicating low risk, is associated with a low risk of serious complications.18 However, many healthcare settings have adopted a 1 hour limit on starting BSA/UBSA treatment and admit all patients (high and low risk) to hospital. As ANC results are not usually available within 1 hour, more than 80% sNF may be treated unnecessarily with inappropriate antibiotics. Consequently, there is a need for implementation studies to identify barriers and facilitators of care, and to optimise early management.

Evidence-based practices are slow to be implemented into routine care.19 Implementation science supports translational medicine research to practice gaps.20,23 It identifies barriers and facilitators to effective implementation and designs evolving strategies to achieve desired service, implementation and client outcomes.

A multicentre, hybrid type 1 effectiveness-implementation trial is an appropriate study design to evaluate the real-world effectiveness of an antibiotic stewardship protocol, and the barriers to and facilitators of its implementation in the ED setting. Type 1 hybrid effectiveness-implementation trials test the effects of a clinical intervention on relevant outcomes while observing and gathering information on implementation.21 This multifaceted trial addresses three broad domains of implementation according to Proctor’s conceptual framework and taxonomy which incorporates the RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance) framework,22 23 namely Service Outcomes, Implementation Outcomes and Client Outcomes.

The ASTERIC trial is designed to address one primary question. In ED and hospitalised adult patients with cancer with sNF, does an antibiotic treatment protocol guided by early ANCs compared with standard of care (SoC) improve safe antibiotic stewardship in 7 days? We hypothesise that in the ED and hospitalised adult patients with sNF, the ASTERIC protocol effectively and safely reduces the use of UBSAs compared with SoC alone (see figure 2). We aim first to investigate the effectiveness service outcome of the ASTERIC protocol to reduce the use of UBSAs in adult patients attending the ED and admitted to hospital with a clinical diagnosis of sNF over 7 days; and second to investigate the safety of the ASTERIC protocol to reduce SAEs in adult patients attending the ED and admitted to hospital with a clinical diagnosis of sNF over 30 days.

Methods and analysis

Trial design and setting

Effectiveness phase

This study is a pragmatic multicentre, type 1 hybrid effectiveness-implementation, stepped-wedge, randomised controlled trial (figure 4).24 Eligible hospitals will be randomly allocated to staggered start dates using a central, computer-generated systems in a stepped-wedge design by our data analyst to commence using the ASTERIC protocol between 3 and 10 months after starting the trial (figure 5). This was a simple randomisation and the sequence was not concealed because hospitals needed to prepare in advance for the transition from phase 1 to phase 2. Importantly, the randomisation sequence determined the timing of each hospital’s transition, not the assignment of individual participants. Analyses will use generalised linear mixed models (random intercept for cluster; fixed effect for time), with prespecified site-level covariates to account for practice/case-mix differences.

The study will initially be conducted at eight hospitals in Hong Kong (Local Clinical Centres)—Gleneagles Hospital Hong Kong (GHK), North Lantau Hospital, Princess Margaret Hospital, Pamela Youde Nethersole Eastern Hospital, Queen Elizabeth Hospital, Queen Mary Hospital, Ruttonjee Hospital and United Christian Hospital. All are public or private hospitals. Patients will be recruited from the EDs or wards with subsequent follow-up in the medical wards, intensive care units and home after hospital discharge.

Trial Arm 1 (before implementation): existing hospital NF protocol+SoC

Standard care is defined as the daily practice of physicians in evaluating and treating patients with sNF. During the SoC period, physicians will assess the patients based on history, physical examination, laboratory tests, chest radiography or other imaging studies and will offer treatment based on clinical expertise and local or international guidelines. The Centre for Health Protection Interhospital Multi-disciplinary Programme on Antimicrobial ChemoTherapy guideline provides appropriate antibiotic prescription.25 Figure 1 provides generic information about the NF protocols for patients with NF. However, as ANC results are not usually available within 1 hour of triage,10 all patients with sNF tend to receive UBSAs.

Trial Arm 2 (after): ASTERIC protocol+SoC

The ASTERIC protocol seeks to introduce locally developed protocols around a multifaceted stewardship intervention consisting of four items: (1) risk assessment tools, (2) fast-track ANCs, (3) a decision tool for patient management and antibiotic use supported by (4) an educational package.

The evidence for using antibiotics in this trial is well-established. However, the evidence for the need to start UBSAs within an hour of ED arrival prior to an ANC result is absent. There is currently no evidence-based guidelines for the urgent initiation of antibiotics administered in the ED for adult patients with sNF, as there are concerns regarding safety. ASTERIC will also evaluate and address barriers to and facilitators of a fast-track ANC service coupled with prudent and timely antibiotic stewardship. Figure 2 shows the ASTERIC protocol.

Administration of protocols and other treatment

Hospital clinicians are responsible for administration of the allocated treatment. They are free to modify or stop study treatment if they feel that it is in the best interests of the patients without the need for the patient to withdraw from the study. The clinicians will decide on any further treatment. The use of medication will be subject to standard pharmacy reviews, which will guide modifications to both the study treatment and the use of concomitant medications (eg, in the case of potential drug interactions). Results of investigations may also guide modifications of treatment.

Baseline data

We will collect baseline and follow-up data using the REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) system, which is freely available through The University of Hong Kong (HKU) Clinical Trials Centre and IISupreme to collect data. Investigations will be requested in the local hospitals according to normal clinical practice. Platforms of commonly available point-of-care and early diagnostic tests should be used according to normal practice. SoC typically includes blood samples sent for complete blood count, urea and electrolytes, liver function tests, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, bacterial culture and sensitivity; sputum and/or saliva sent for microbiology and virology; and radiology (chest radiographs and/or CT scan).

Follow-up

All patients will receive a diary to record protocol adherence, symptoms and adverse events (AEs). Information will be collected up to 30 days after enrolment by telephone call, clinical attendance, data retrieval from electronic medical records in the Hospital Authority Clinical Management System (CMS) or Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System (CDARS), registries and medical staff. Compliance with medication will also be checked. All trial participants will be followed-up until death or discharge or at 30 days, whichever is sooner.

Implementation evaluation

The implementation evaluation will be guided by the 17 elements of the Proctor framework to evaluate the implementation of the ASTERIC protocol (online supplemental table 1).22 We will use a mixed methods design to collect qualitative and quantitative data concurrently at multiple levels and analyse them separately to assess different elements of implementation. No interim analysis is planned, but we do propose constant review and feedback to each department with monthly reports on protocol adherence. We will (1) prospectively monitor fidelity (eg, fast-track ANC uptake, initial UBSA prescribing, time to de-escalation), (2) provide remedial education if drift is detected and (3) report adherence metrics alongside outcomes. While no formal efficacy interim analysis is planned, the Trial Steering Committee’s oversight and regular fidelity audits will guide supportive actions without compromising the stepped-wedge structure. Three rounds of focus group interviews will be conducted: the first before the commencement of the effectiveness trial (T1), the second before phase 2 (T2) and at or after the end of phase 2 (T3). In each study site, a minimum initial stratified sample of six participants (one consultant/associate consultant, two junior doctors, one senior nurse of the rank advanced practice nurse or above and two junior nurses) will be invited. For the inclusion criteria of staff recruitment, three ED doctors and three nurses will be invited with no exclusion criteria for staff recruitment.

Outcomes and definitions

Co-primary outcome 1: 7-day mean UBSA dose

The mean antibiotic dose is defined as the average dose in milligrams of UBSA per patient from ED registration over 7 days. Examples of UBSAs include meropenem and piperacillin/tazobactam. Data on antibiotic, dose and duration of medication dispensing records will be collected from the CMS or CDARS. These data are centrally processed by the trial data management team. In Proctor’s Conceptual Framework and Taxonomy, this effectiveness criterion is a service outcome.

Antibiotic stewardship is not an all-or-nothing process. It requires clinical experience, available information and professional judgement on personalised escalation/de-escalation resources. Capturing all these aspects into a single outcome—a meaningful reduction of UBSA use—is a worthwhile measure of success.

Co-primary outcome 2: 30-day severe adverse events

The proportion of patients with an SAE (Binary Y/N) (Time Frame: up to 30 days). See online supplemental appendix 2 for the definition of an SAE. SAEs are recorded each day. This refers specifically to SAEs (not to AEs, suspected unexpected SAEs or suspected SAEs). In Proctor’s Conceptual Framework and Taxonomy, this safety criterion is a service outcome.

Our prior data showed that SAEs in patients with sNF varied from 11% to 21%. Although no SAEs were deemed to be due to current pathways or antibiotic treatment, we identified fewer SAEs in patients in whom UBSAs were quickly de-escalated when not needed. With such information, we will report on SAEs as a co-primary outcome.

Sample size calculation

For co-primary efficacy outcome I

For antibiotic stewardship

Our pilot feasibility study of 70 patients showed that the adoption of an antibiotic stewardship protocol reduced the mean UBSA (eg, meropenem or piperacillin/tazobactam) use in the first 7 days of hospitalisation from 21 (SD 25) to 12 (SD 18) g. There were 8 SAEs out of 35 (23%) cases in the control arm and 3 SAEs out of 35 (9%) cases in the intervention group.

Aiming for superiority in the primary outcome, with a stepped-wedge design, a total sample size of 360 patients (180 in each group) is required to have a 90% chance of detecting, as significant at the 5% level, a decrease in the mean dose of UBSAs (primary outcome measure) from 21 (SD 22) in the control group to 12 in the experimental group.29

For co-primary safety outcome II

Aiming for superiority in the co-primary outcome (proportion of patients without an SAE), with a stepped-wedge design, 648 patients (324 in each group) are required to have an 80% chance of detecting, as significant at the 5% level, an increase in the co-primary safety outcome measure from 77% in the control group to 91% in the experimental group.28 As there may be variation between hospitals, we will adopt a conservative sample requirement of 648 patients (324 patients in each group, or 81 patients per hospital in a 1-year period).

Data analysis

5% of all data will be checked for accuracy. All prevailing regulations by HKU and Hospital Authroity (HA) will be followed. An audit trail will be made to include the number of patients screened, approached, recruited and excluded (with reasons). Patient and clinical staff responses, and data completeness will be evaluated.

Primary analysis

The baseline characteristics of the two groups will be assessed for potential imbalances, which will be adjusted for when comparing outcomes. Intention-to-treat, per-protocol and as treated analyses will be performed by imputing all non-responses at follow-ups with baseline values, which yields more conservative estimates of effect sizes. The missing data for the primary outcome is expected to be <1% because most data is retrievable from electronic patient records with electronic time stamps. Missing values will not be imputed. We will report continuous variables as means and SDs, or medians and IQRs and categorical variables as proportions, as appropriate. Preliminary clinical impacts will be reported with estimates of the 95% CIs. In addition, we will describe the distribution of demographic characteristics, comorbidities and clinical severity of the patients recruited before and after the crossover date to identify factors that might require adjustment in the full-scale trial. We will use the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences for Windows V.27.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA) or R V.4.2.2 for statistical analysis with biostatistician support.

The first main effect is intervention versus control on mean dose of UBSA in 7 days. The second main effect is intervention versus control on SAE in 30 days. All secondary outcomes at different time points will be analysed using generalised mixed effect model for multiple intrasubject and intersubject observations. The main and interaction effects will be assessed. For the cost analysis, we will evaluate healthcare resource use, including length of hospital stay, clinical and ED visits, drug and time costs.

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative data related to the barriers to and facilitators of the implementation of the ASTERIC protocol in routine care services will be analysed using grounded theory methodology with thematic analysis of responses to open-ended questions. Responses across individuals will be sorted, coded and compared by a trained research nurse and research assistant to determine the core categories. Categories will be clustered to form a broader scope of categories. After systematic comparisons of the categories are made, properties and dimensions for the studied concepts will be developed from the interview data.30 31 We will maintain accountability and auditability (capacity to cross-check interpretation against data).30 31 Disputes will be resolved by repeated discussion and re-coding. Outstanding coding disputes will be examined by an expert in qualitative research.

Health economics

Costs will be calculated according to activities, which include the intervention, preparation and administration of all drugs, investigations, procedures and admission to hospital. We will obtain estimates for the time required to use each resource in each activity and the unit costs for each of these resources. Drugs will be costed separately according to dose, and cost estimates will be made for the actual treatment time for AEs. Care will be taken to avoid double counting. QALY will be quantified by the baseline and 30-day EuroQual-5D-L (EQ-5D-5L) Health Questionnaire utility scores based on the Hong Kong value set32 using the standard area under the curve approach. Incremental cost-effectiveness ratio will be measured in terms of cost per QALY gained, Δcost ASTERIC+SoC/ΔQALY NF+SoC, where incremental cost-effectiveness ratio below the willingness-to-pay threshold will be interpreted by the Phase 2 study group as cost-effective.

Ethics and dissemination

We have obtained research ethical approval from the institutional review boards of all study sites (HKU/HA HKW IRB Reference Number: UW 24–146; HA Central IRB Reference Number: CIRB-2024-540-4; HKU-GHK IRB Reference Number: GHK-2024–07). The study will be conducted in full compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All items from the WHO Trial Registration Data set have been included (online supplemental file 2).33

The data are centrally processed by the trial data management team. 5% of all data will be checked for accuracy. All prevailing regulations by HKU and HA will be followed. An audit trail will be made to include the number of patients screened, approached, recruited and excluded (with reasons).

We will disseminate the findings through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and educational activities.

Discussion

The ASTERIC trial will provide real-world data from multiple sources to improve antibiotic stewardship and management in patients with sNF presenting in ED and ward settings. Information gathered from the implementation evaluation will help us improve and tailor implementation strategies based on the barriers and facilitators identified across different study sites.

There are several inherent limitations of the stepped wedge study design. We attempt to avoid selection bias of the cluster by performing randomisation at a single time point after all study sites have consented to participate. However, we cannot conceal the allocation of the crossover date because of the need for implementation. The recruitment bias caused by recruiting staff foreknowledge of treatment assignment would be minimal because of the well-defined patient participant recruitment criteria. Blinding is also not possible because of the differences in clinical intervention in the standard care and ASTERIC periods. Our study findings can be generalised to EDs where ASTERICS is not routinely used.

Reporting and dissemination

We will report our findings according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials extension for the stepped wedge cluster randomised trial and Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials statements24 34; Reporting Implementation Studies Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies Statement35; Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research36; and Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022 statement.37 We will disseminate the results through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and educational activities with the public and professional bodies.
