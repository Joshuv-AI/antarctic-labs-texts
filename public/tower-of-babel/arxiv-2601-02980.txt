A Universal Upper Bound on the Pressure-to-Energy Density Ratio in Neutron Stars
Bao-Jun Cai ID, Bao-An Li ID, Yu-Gang Ma ID

Abstract
The equation-of-state (EOS) parameter � ≡ P / " \phi\equiv P/\varepsilon , defined as the ratio of pressure to energy density, encapsulates the fundamental response of matter under extreme compression. Its value at the center of the most massive neutron star (NS), X ≡ � c = P c / " c \mathrm{X}\equiv\phi_{\textrm{c}}=P_{\textrm{c}}/\varepsilon_{\textrm{c}} , sets a universal upper bound on the maximum denseness attainable by any form of visible matter anywhere in the Universe. Remarkably, owing to the intrinsically nonlinear structure of the EOS in General Relativity (GR), this bound is forced to lie far below the naive Special Relativity (SR) limit of unity. In this work, we refine the theoretical upper bound on X \mathrm{X} in a self-consistent manner by incorporating, in addition to the causality constraint from SR, the mass-sphere stability condition associated with the mass evolution pattern in the vicinity of the NS center. This condition is formulated within the intrinsic-and-perturbative analysis of the dimensionless Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff equations (IPAD-TOV) framework. The combined constraints yield an improved bound, X ® 0.385 \mathrm{X}\lesssim 0.385 , which is slightly above but fully consistent with the previously derived causal-only limit, X ® 0.374 \mathrm{X}\lesssim 0.374 . We further derive an improved scaling relation for NS compactness and verify its universality across a broad set of 284 realistic EOSs, including models with first-order phase transitions, exotic degrees of freedom, continuous crossover behavior, and deconfined quark cores. The resulting bound on X \mathrm{X} thus provides a new, EOS-independent window into the microphysics of cold superdense matter compressed by strong-field gravity in GR.

transitions from positive to negative.
The critical point
\overline{\mathrm{X}}
is determined by the condition:

\boxed{\text{onset of mass-sphere instability: }\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}A}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}}\right|_{\mathrm{X}=\overline{\mathrm{X}}}=0.}

(20)

For
\mathrm{X}<\overline{\mathrm{X}}
, the mass evolution is stable, while for
\mathrm{X}>\overline{\mathrm{X}}
, it is unstable, characterized by an accelerated growth of the enclosed mass.

These two criteria (a) and (b) can be summarized as follows: (i) mass should increase with
\mathrm{X}
for small
\mathrm{X}
:
{\mathrm{d}\delta\widehat{M}(\widehat{r}_{\textrm{f}})}/{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}>0\leftrightarrow{\mathrm{d}A}/{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}<0
; (ii) mass becomes eventually harder to increase with
\mathrm{X}
:
{\mathrm{d}^{2}\delta\widehat{M}(\widehat{r}_{\textrm{f}})}/{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}}<0\leftrightarrow{\mathrm{d}^{2}A}/{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}}>0
; and (iii) the mass-sphere instability occurs when
{\mathrm{d}^{2}\delta\widehat{M}(\widehat{r}_{\textrm{f}})}/{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}}>0\leftrightarrow{\mathrm{d}^{2}A}/{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}}<0
for
\mathrm{X}>\overline{\mathrm{X}}
, where
\widehat{r}_{\textrm{f}}
and
\varepsilon_{\textrm{c}}
are fixed and
\delta\widehat{M}(\widehat{r}_{\textrm{f}})
is given by Eq. (
19
).
See FIG.
3
for a sketch of the
A(\mathrm{X})
and
\mathrm{d}A/\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}
as functions of
\mathrm{X}
.
In the Newtonian limit, we have
s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}(\mathrm{X})\sim\mathrm{X}
,
A(\mathrm{X})\sim\mathrm{X}^{-1}
, and therefore
{\mathrm{d}^{2}A}/{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}}\sim\mathrm{X}^{-3}
, which is always positive; hence no such critical
\overline{\mathrm{X}}
exists in this case.

The Principle of Causality,
s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}\leq 1
, also defines a critical value for
\mathrm{X}
, denoted as
\mathrm{X}_{+}
:

\boxed{\text{causality boundary: }s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}\left(\mathrm{X}=\mathrm{X}_{+}\right)=1.}

(21)

These two upper limits,
\mathrm{X}_{+}
and
\overline{\mathrm{X}}
, are necessarily distinct; however, physically they should be close to each other, as both reflect the maximum compressibility allowed by the underlying physics of the NS cores.
We emphasize that even if
s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}(\overline{\mathrm{X}})>1
, as often occurs in certain non-relativistic model calculations, the NS M-R relation still behaves reasonably with no strange features.
This indicates that
\mathrm{X}_{+}
and
\overline{\mathrm{X}}
represent two independent criteria for setting the EOS-parameter upper bound.
More specifically, since the expression for
A(\mathrm{X})
for TOV NSs is

A(\mathrm{X})=\frac{B(\mathrm{X})}{s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}(\mathrm{X})}=\left.\frac{B(\mathrm{X})}{\mathrm{X}}\left(3-3\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln B}{\mathrm{d}\ln\mathrm{X}}\right)\right/\left(4-3\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln B}{\mathrm{d}\ln\mathrm{X}}\right),

(22)

see Eq. (
15
),
the condition
\mathrm{d}^{2}A(\mathrm{X})/\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}=0
reads

s_{\textrm{c}}^{4}(\mathrm{X})\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}}-2s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}(\mathrm{X})\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}\frac{\mathrm{d}s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}-s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}(\mathrm{X})B(\mathrm{X})\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}s_{\textrm{c}}^{4}}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}}+2B(\mathrm{X})\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}\right)^{2}=0,

(23)

which becomes (at the causality boundary
s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}(\mathrm{X})=1
) as

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}}-2\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}\frac{\mathrm{d}s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}-B(\mathrm{X})\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}s_{\textrm{c}}^{4}}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}}+2B(\mathrm{X})\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}\right)^{2}=0.

(24)

This condition is fundamentally different from the causality boundary constraint of Eq. (
21
).
In fact, Eq. (
21
) for
s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}(\mathrm{X})=1
and Eq. (
24
) for
\mathrm{d}^{2}A/\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}=0
(under
s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}(\mathrm{X})=1
) can be explicitly written as

\frac{3-4\mathrm{X}}{1-\mathrm{X}}\frac{B(\mathrm{X})}{3\mathrm{X}}-\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}=0,

(25)

as well as,

\displaystyle\left[-9\mathrm{X}^{4}B^{2}(\mathrm{X})\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}\right)+12\mathrm{X}^{3}B^{3}(\mathrm{X})\right]\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{3}}\right)

\displaystyle+18\mathrm{X}^{4}B^{2}(\mathrm{X})\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}}\right)^{2}+\left[27\mathrm{X}^{5}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}\right)^{3}-126\mathrm{X}^{4}B(\mathrm{X})\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}\right)^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\left.+144\mathrm{X}^{3}B^{2}(\mathrm{X})\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}\right)-48\mathrm{X}^{2}B^{3}(\mathrm{X})\right]\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle-36\mathrm{X}^{4}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}\right)^{4}+198\mathrm{X}^{3}B(\mathrm{X})\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}\right)^{3}-378\mathrm{X}^{2}B^{2}(\mathrm{X})\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+312\mathrm{X}B^{3}(\mathrm{X})\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}}\right)-96B^{4}(\mathrm{X})=0,

(26)

respectively.
Clearly, Eqs. (
26
) and (
25
) are independent conditions for evaluating the upper limit of
\mathrm{X}
: Eq. (
25
) depends only on the first-order derivative of
B(\mathrm{X})
, while Eq. (
26
) involves derivatives up to third-order, reflecting higher-order effects in the compressibility and stability of the dense matter.

As mentioned above, physically and consistently, the
\mathrm{X}_{+}
from
s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}=1
and the
\overline{\mathrm{X}}
from
\mathrm{d}^{2}A/\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}=0
should be close to each other:

\text{physical requirement: }\mathrm{X}_{+}\approx\overline{\mathrm{X}}.

(27)

Due to the perturbative scheme,
\mathrm{X}_{+}
and
\overline{\mathrm{X}}
may not necessarily be close to each other in our analysis.
Therefore, if they are not close, this consistency can be used to refine the upper limit for
\mathrm{X}
, which forms the main content of the following sections.
At even larger
\mathrm{X}>\mathrm{X}_{\textrm{unphys}}
,
A(\mathrm{X})
may become negative due to the singular behavior of
s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}
(
\to\pm\infty
), corresponding to an unphysical state, since
A(\mathrm{X})
should remain positive. FIG.
5
sketches these regions:

\mathrm{X}_{\textrm{unphys}}\gtrsim\mathrm{X}_{+}\approx\overline{\mathrm{X}},

(28)

and we call the region for
\mathrm{X}\gtrsim\mathrm{X}_{+}\approx\overline{\mathrm{X}}
the unstable region while that for
\mathrm{X}\gtrsim\mathrm{X}_{\textrm{unphys}}
the unphysical region.

IV.  Calculations of the Effective Correction to Upper Bound for
\mathrm{X}
and Verification from the NS Compactness Scaling

In this section, we analyze the effective correction to the upper bound for the central EOS-parameter
\mathrm{X}
by employing the consistency condition of (
27
).
We first consider the central SSS for TOV NSs without the
a_{2}
-term
CLZ23-a
, given by Eq. (
13
). In this case, the causality limit gives
\mathrm{X}\leq\mathrm{X}_{+}\approx 0.374
.
Applying the mass-sphere stability condition
\mathrm{d}^{2}A/\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}=0
yields
\overline{\mathrm{X}}\approx 0.377
.
The proximity between
\mathrm{X}_{+}
and
\overline{\mathrm{X}}
is encouraging: it indicates that the causality bound and the mass-sphere stability point impose mutually consistent and complementary constraints on the central EOS-parameter. This observation may also help explain the effectiveness of the IPAD-TOV approach in capturing the effective scalings of the mass, radius, and compactness of NSs
CL25-a
, even when the perturbative expansions are truncated at relatively low orders.

Next, we include the
a_{2}
-term in the NS mass by replacing the central energy density
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{\textrm{c}}=1
with the average energy density
\langle\widehat{\varepsilon}\rangle\approx 1+a_{2}\widehat{r}^{2}
, which modifies the central SSS to
CL25-b

s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}(\mathrm{X})=\mathrm{X}\left(1+\frac{1}{3}\frac{1+3\mathrm{X}^{2}+4\mathrm{X}}{1-3\mathrm{X}^{2}}\right)\left(1-\frac{3}{25}\mathrm{X}\right),

(29)

while the expression for
B(\mathrm{X})
remains unchanged. In this case, the numerical values become
\mathrm{X}_{+}\approx 0.381
and
\overline{\mathrm{X}}\approx 0.368
.
Again,
\mathrm{X}_{+}
and
\overline{\mathrm{X}}
are close to each other, indicating the internal consistency of our approach and reinforcing the physical significance of these upper-bound criteria.

For reference, the singularity of the
s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}(\mathrm{X})
occurs at
\mathrm{X}_{\textrm{unphys}}=1/\sqrt{3}\approx 0.577
, representing an unphysical state (as indicated in FIG.
3
) where the central SSS diverges, beyond which the model is no longer physically meaningful.

Going beyond the leading-order expansion of
\widehat{P}
in the dimensionless radial coordinate

\widehat{r}
becomes eventually complicated, as it requires analytically solving the TOV equations together with a specified dense matter EOS. Nevertheless, the NS pressure profile can be written in the effective
form

\widehat{P}(\widehat{r})=\mathrm{X}-B\widehat{r}^{2}+f(\widehat{r}),

(30)

where
f(\widehat{r})
collects all higher-order contributions in
\widehat{r}
starting at

\mathcal{O}(\widehat{r}^{4})
. Evaluating Eq. (
30
) at
\widehat{r}=\widehat{R}
then
determines the NS radius through the condition

\mathrm{X}-B\widehat{R}^{2}+f(\widehat{R})=0
.
The explicit form of
f(\widehat{r})
is uniquely fixed by the TOV equations together with the
dense matter EOS; in this sense, Eq. (
30
) remains exact.
However, although the exact expansion of
\widehat{P}(\widehat{r})
contains only higher-order
corrections
\sim\widehat{r}^{4},\widehat{r}^{6},\cdots
, the NS radius is determined by the pressure at a finite
\widehat{r}
, where the cumulative impact of these terms becomes important. To effectively model
the complexity of
f(\widehat{r})
without committing to a specific microscopic EOS, we therefore
introduce a dimensionless parameter
\sigma
and adopt the effective parametrization

f(\widehat{r})=-\sigma\mathrm{X}B\widehat{r}^{2},

(31)

where
\sigma
is to be determined self-consistently.
From this viewpoint, the quadratic correction should be interpreted as an effective renormalization
of the coefficient of the leading-
\widehat{r}^{2}
term, arising from the cumulative influence of microphysical interactions and nonlinear gravitational effects. In this sense, the parameter
\sigma
encodes how subleading contributions are projected onto the dominant curvature
term of
\widehat{P}(\widehat{r})
on macroscopic scales. A quadratic form therefore represents the
natural leading effective choice, since the
B
-term already multiplies
\widehat{r}^{2}
and
symmetry requires that only even powers of
\widehat{r}
appear in the expansion of

\widehat{P}

CL24-a
; correspondingly, the genuine subleading corrections originate from
terms such as
\widehat{r}^{4},\widehat{r}^{6},\cdots
.

This type of effective renormalization admits a direct analogue in classical hydrodynamics. In
particular, a similar situation arises for a solid sphere of radius
\ell
and density

\rho_{\textrm{sph}}
oscillating in an ideal fluid of density
\rho_{\textrm{fld}}
. The equation of motion
for the sphere is
Lan87-ex

\frac{4}{3}\pi\ell^{3}\left(\rho_{\textrm{sph}}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\textrm{fld}}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\vec{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\vec{k},

(32)

where
\vec{u}
is the velocity of the sphere and
\vec{k}
is the force acting on it.
The prefactor of
\mathrm{d}\vec{u}/\mathrm{d}t
can be interpreted as the renormalized (or
effective) mass of the sphere: it comprises the bare mass of the sphere,

(4/3)\pi\ell^{3}\rho_{\textrm{sph}}
, together with an added mass contribution

(1/2)(4/3)\pi\ell^{3}\rho_{\textrm{fld}}
arising from the coupling to the surrounding fluid.
Equivalently, this effect can be described as a renormalization of the sphere density,

\rho_{\textrm{sph}}\to\rho_{\textrm{sph}}^{\textrm{(R)}}=\rho_{\textrm{sph}}+2^{-1}\rho_{\textrm{fld}}
.
In terms of
\rho_{\textrm{sph}}^{\textrm{(R)}}
, the equation of motion takes the form

(4/3)\pi\ell^{3}\rho_{\textrm{sph}}^{\textrm{(R)}}\mathrm{d}\vec{u}/\mathrm{d}t=\vec{k}
,
which is formally identical to the unperturbed equation of motion for the sphere.
As a consequence of this mass renormalization, the velocity of the sphere is related to the
unperturbed flow velocity
\vec{v}
by
Lan87-ex

\vec{u}=\frac{3\rho_{\textrm{fld}}}{\rho_{\textrm{fld}}+2\rho_{\textrm{sph}}}\vec{v}=3\left(1-\frac{\rho_{\textrm{sph}}}{\rho_{\textrm{sph}}^{\textrm{(R)}}}\right)\vec{v}.

(33)

If the density of the sphere exceeds that of the fluid (
\rho_{\textrm{sph}}>\rho_{\textrm{fld}}
), the
sphere “lags behind” the flow (
\vec{u}<\vec{v}
). Conversely, if
\rho_{\textrm{sph}}<\rho_{\textrm{fld}}
,
the sphere “moves ahead” of the fluid (
\vec{u}>\vec{v}
).
One can has maximally
\vec{u}\lesssim 3\vec{v}
for
\rho_{\textrm{fld}}\gg\rho_{\textrm{sph}}
.

In the next section, we show that adopting alternative functional forms for
f(\widehat{r})
within this framework leads to very similar constraints on the upper bound of
\mathrm{X}
, demonstrating the robustness of our conclusions.
This is reasonable and understandable: even without the effective
f
-correction, the two bounds on
\mathrm{X}
are already close, indicating that the
f
-correction is a perturbation.
In addition, for small
\mathrm{X}
, the correction
\sigma\mathrm{X}\to 0
, i.e., it has no impact on low-mass NSs.
The effective
B
parameter then becomes

B(\mathrm{X})\to B_{\textrm{eff}}(\mathrm{X})=B(\mathrm{X})\left(1+\sigma\mathrm{X}\right)=\frac{1}{6}\left(1+3\mathrm{X}^{2}+4\mathrm{X}\right)\left(1+\sigma\mathrm{X}\right).

(34)

Consequently, the dimensionless radius reads

\widehat{R}=\sqrt{\frac{6\mathrm{X}}{1+3\mathrm{X}^{2}+4\mathrm{X}}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{1+\sigma\mathrm{X}}\right)^{1/2},

(35)

and the physical radius becomes

R=\frac{C_{R}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\textrm{c}}}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{X}}{1+3\mathrm{X}^{2}+4\mathrm{X}}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{1+\sigma\mathrm{X}}\right)^{1/2},

(36)

where

\widehat{\varepsilon}(\widehat{r})\approx 1+a_{2}^{\sigma}\widehat{r}^{2}+\cdots

\sigma=0

The NS mass can be expressed as

\displaystyle M_{\textrm{NS}}\approx

\displaystyle\frac{C_{M}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\textrm{c}}}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{X}}{1+3\mathrm{X}^{2}+4\mathrm{X}}\right)^{3/2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{1+\sigma\mathrm{X}}\right)^{3/2}\cdot\left(1-\frac{3}{5}\frac{\mathrm{X}}{s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}}\right),

(38)

Furthermore, the effective correction to

V.  Alternative Correction Forms

In the previous section, we adopt the effective correction in the form
f(\widehat{r})=-\sigma\mathrm{X}B\widehat{r}^{2}
in the expansion of
\widehat{P}
.
Physically, alternative forms of the correction are expected to produce similar effects. In this section, we provide a more detailed analysis of this issue.

First, we consider the following correction form:

f(\widehat{r})=-\varphi\left(B\widehat{r}^{2}\right)^{2}\sim\widehat{r}^{4},

(51)

where
\varphi
is an effective parameter;
then, since
B=6^{-1}(1+3\mathrm{X}^{2}+4\mathrm{X})<1
and
\widehat{R}\lesssim\mathcal{O}(1)
, the magnitude of
(B\widehat{r}^{2})^{2}

In this case, the dimensionless radius from the pressure equation
P(R)=\mathrm{X}-B\widehat{R}^{2}-\varphi B^{2}\widehat{R}^{4}=0

\widehat{R}^{2}=\frac{\sqrt{1+4\mathrm{X}\varphi}-1}{2B\varphi}\approx\frac{\mathrm{X}}{B}\left(1-\varphi\mathrm{X}\right).

(52)

The coefficient
a_{2}
remains unchanged,
a_{2}=b_{2}/s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}=-B/s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}
, since this correction enters at order
\widehat{r}^{4}
.
The NS mass then scales as

\displaystyle M_{\textrm{NS}}\sim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\textrm{c}}}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{1+4\mathrm{X}\varphi}-1}{2B\varphi}\right)^{3/2}\cdot\left(1-\frac{3}{5}\frac{B}{s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}}\frac{\sqrt{1+4\mathrm{X}\varphi}-1}{2B\varphi}\right),

(53)

39

\mathrm{X}

\mathrm{d}M_{\textrm{NS}}/\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{\textrm{c}}=0

s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}
over
\mathrm{X}
gives

s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}\approx\frac{4}{3}\mathrm{X}+\left(-\frac{2\kappa}{11}+\frac{5\varphi}{33}+\frac{88}{75}\right)\mathrm{X}^{2},

(54)

and comparison with Eq. (
40

\mathrm{X}

\displaystyle s_{\textrm{c}}^{2}\approx

VI.  Summary and Conclusions

The physical information encapsulated in the coefficient
A(\mathrm{X})\equiv-a_{2}(\mathrm{X})>0
with
\mathrm{X}=\phi_{\textrm{c}}=P_{\textrm{c}}/\varepsilon_{\textrm{c}}
, in the perturbative expansion of the reduced energy density
\widehat{\varepsilon}\approx 1-A(\mathrm{X})\widehat{r}^{2}+\cdots
in the IPAD-TOV approach is revealed through a Gedankenexperiment. Physically,
A(\mathrm{X})
decreases with
\mathrm{X}
at small
\mathrm{X}
with a positive second-order derivative
\mathrm{d}^{2}A/\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}
; the critical value
\overline{\mathrm{X}}
defined by
\mathrm{d}^{2}A/\mathrm{d}\mathrm{X}^{2}=0
signals the onset of mass-sphere instability: further increasing
\mathrm{X}
beyond
\overline{\mathrm{X}}
accelerates the rise of the mass-sphere near the center, which indicates an unstable state. The value
\overline{\mathrm{X}}\approx 0.377
is close to the causality-only upper bound
\mathrm{X}_{+}
for
\mathrm{X}
about 0.374, demonstrating the self-consistency and partially explaining the effectiveness of IPAD-TOV scalings for NS mass, radius, and compactness, even when truncated at low orders.

Building on this insight, we refine the upper bound on the central EOS-parameter
\mathrm{X}
by incorporating the mass-sphere stability condition near the NS center, which modifies the expression for the central SSS and consequently yields
\mathrm{X}\lesssim 0.385
. This bound is slightly higher but remains consistent with the causality-only estimate, and it improves the NS compactness scaling across 284 realistic microscopic EOSs. Consequently, the dimensionless trace anomaly at NS centers,
\Delta_{\textrm{c}}=1/3-\mathrm{X}
, is bounded from below by
\gtrsim-0.05
. In the present work, the effective correction to the central SSS is kept at the linear order
1+\Theta\mathrm{X}
with an optimal
\Theta\approx 0.927
. Future studies may aim to work out the higher-order form,
1+\Theta\mathrm{X}+\Theta_{2}\mathrm{X}^{2}+\cdots
, where terms such as
\Theta_{2}\mathrm{X}^{2}
are expected to be small since
\mathrm{X}\lesssim 0.38
-
0.39
, indicating that the upper bound on
\mathrm{X}
obtained in this work remains robust and practically useful.

The upper limit on
\mathrm{X}
defines a fundamental scale for dense matter in our Universe and provides a benchmark for the maximum compression achievable in NS cores. Determining this bound with precision offers a novel perspective on how superdense matter couples to spacetime curvature, illuminating the interplay between General Relativity, quantum many-body physics, and Quantum Chromodynamics under extreme conditions.
