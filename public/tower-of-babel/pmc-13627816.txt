Pathophysiological modulations by Trimad, a polyherbal formulation, in a rat model of high-fat diet-induced obesity
Authors: Asavari Anirudha Joshi, Megha Pradip Salunke, Suresh Purushottam Khadke, Arulmozhi Sathiyanarayanan, Supriya Sudhakar Bhalerao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity is the most prevalent metabolic disorder globally, characterized by oxidative stress and inflammation that play a key role in its pathogenesis. Current anti-obesity drugs have adverse effects and fail to address oxidative stress and inflammation. Therefore, there is a growing need for safe and effective therapies. Ayurveda recommends Trimad for obesity management. Trimad is a polyherbal formulation consisting of Cyperus rotundus tubers (Musta), Embelia ribes fruits (Vidang), and Plumbago zeylanica roots (Chitraka) in an equal 1:1:1 ratio.ObjectiveThis study evaluated the effect of Trimad on ameliorating pathophysiological changes in a rat model of high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obesity.Material and methodsMale wistar rats were divided into six groups (n = 6; IAEC approval no. BVDUMC/3293/2016/007/002). Group 1 served as the Normal control (normal chow diet), Group 2 as Disease control (HFD), Group 3 as Positive control (HFD + Atorvastatin, 1.2 mg/kg), and Groups 4–6 received HFD with Trimad extract at 50, 100, and 200 mg/kg, respectively for 48 days with treatment starting from day 24. Body weight, feed intake, biochemical parameters, oxidative stress markers, inflammatory cytokines, adipokines, adipocyte morphology, brain monoamines and hepatic lipid-metabolizing gene expression were evaluated.ResultsHFD caused lipid abnormalities, inflammation, oxidative stress, hepatic steatosis, and weight gain. Trimad significantly reduced weight gain, improved lipid metabolism by modulating hepatic lipid-metabolizing genes, and normalized biochemical parameters, oxidative stress, inflammatory markers, adipokines, and adipocyte hypertrophy. There was normalization of brain monoamines in Trimad treated groups.ConclusionTrimad effectively ameliorated HFD-induced obesity and demonstrated strong anti-obesity potential.

Introduction

Obesity results from an imbalance between energy intake and expenditure [1] and is marked by increased adipose tissue mass [2]. This expansion involves adipocyte hypertrophy and hyperplasia, accompanied by macrophage infiltration that releases pro-inflammatory cytokines and adipokines [3]. The imbalance between pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines contributes to obesity-related complications such as dyslipidemia, hepatic steatosis, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes [4]. With the global rise in obesity, the search for safe anti-obesity therapeutics has intensified. Pharmacological management of obesity has evolved from modestly effective agents like Orlistat to highly efficacious therapies like GLP-1 receptor agonists and metabolism targeting agents like Resmetirom [5]. However, majority of these drugs have been limited by adverse effects [6]. Ayurveda offers a wide range of anti-obesity drugs that can be evaluated for their efficacy. One such formulation is Trimad, traditionally recommended for obesity management [7]. It contains Cyperus rotundus (Musta), Embelia ribes (Vidang), and Plumbago zeylanica (Chitraka) in equal proportion. Each of this constituent is individually reported for weight-reducing potential [[8], [9], [10]], and the formulation has shown clinical efficacy in a randomized placebo-controlled trial [11]. Hence, the present study investigated the effects of Trimad in a high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obese rat model with focus on preliminary pathophysiological alteration viz. inflammation, oxidative stress and hunger-satiety [12].

Materials and methods

Materials

Trimad as a standardized aqueous extract was provided by Pharmanza Herbals Pvt. Ltd., Gujarat (Lot No. TMEP/RAD/005; Product Code: 00000EPTR1). The details of raw material authentication, aqueous extract preparation procedure, and phyto-constituent detection have been provided in Supplementary material- Fig. 1. Atorvastatin (Cipla Ltd.) was procured locally and used as the anti-hyperlipidemic Positive control. The normal chow diet (3% fat) was procured from Nutrivet Life Sciences, Pune. The high-fat diet (35% fat) used for obesity induction was obtained from VRK Nutritional Solutions, Pune. HFD was prepared using soybean oil, lard, and LR-grade cholesterol. Proximate analysis of normal chow diet and HFD is provided in Supplementary material- Table 1.

Animals and treatments

The work was conducted in accordance with the internationally accepted principles and guidelines for laboratory animal use and care. Institutional animal ethics approval (BVDUMC/3293/2016/007/002) was obtained before initiating the study. The study was carried out in 4-5 week old male Wistar rats weighing up to 125-150 g. The rats were purchased from National Toxicology Center, Pune and were housed in standard conditions (25 ± 1°C/45-55% relative humidity and 12 h light-dark cycle). Throughout the study, the animals had free access to feed and water.

Experimental design

The animals were randomly divided into 6 groups with n = 6 in each group (Table 1). Based on literature review for usage of herbal drugs in HFD induced animal models, doses for the treatment groups were selected [13]. The dose of Atorvastatin was extrapolated from human anti-hyperlipidaemic dose [14]. Group 1 (Normal control, NC) received only normal diet throughout the study period (i.e. 48 days) while groups 2 to 6 received HFD. Group 2 received only high fat diet throughout the study period (Disease control, DC). In addition to HFD, animals in groups 3 to 6 received treatments as per the assigned groups from day 24 to day 48 (last 24 days). Group 3 was Positive control (PC) and received Atorvastatin. To adhere to the 3Rs principles for animal welfare, the evaluation of Trimad was carried out by sharing control groups with a concurrent study on Triphala. Both polyherbal formulations utilized the same normal, disease, and positive control groups, with only the specific experimental groups added independently. The comparative data for the control arms and Triphala have been published previously [15].

Study parameters

Throughout the study duration, feed intake and body weight were recorded (once a week) using digital weighing balance.

Serum biochemical parameters

Blood was collected from all animals through retro-orbital puncture under anesthesia on day 0, 24, 48 after overnight fasting. Serum lipid profile (Total Cholesterol; TC, Triglycerides; TG, HDL-C) and glucose were determined using commercially available colorimetric kits from Coral Clinical Systems, India. Adipokines (Adiponectin and Leptin) and inflammatory markers (IL-6 and hs-CRP) were estimated by ELISA kits procured from My Bioresource, USA. Oxidative stress in serum was evaluated as Malondialdehyde (MDA; marker of lipid peroxidation) using TBARS method [16]. Oxygen Radical Absorbance Capacity (ORAC) was measured by fluorescence method described by Choudhari et al. [17].

Changes in key organs

On day 48, all the animals were sacrificed and key organs (liver, brain and adipose tissue) were harvested. The weights of the liver and the brain were recorded. Liver section was divided into two parts. The first part was stored in Trichloroacetic acid for determination of oxidative stress. Oxidative stress (lipid peroxidation) was measured in tissue homogenates using the Thiobarbituric Acid Reactive Substances (TBARS) method [15]. The second part of the liver tissue was stored in Liquid nitrogen and was used for molecular studies. Superoxide dismutase (anti-oxidant enzyme, SOD) was measured using a method described by Zhang et al. (2017) [18]. The details of the molecular studies have been provided in the section ‘Expression of genes involved in lipid metabolism in liver tissue’. Determination of adipocyte diameters was done for 10% formalin fixed adipose tissue by a reported method [15]. Briefly, representative images of adipose tissue sections (5–7 μm) were analyzed using ImageJ software. Measurements of adipocyte diameter were recorded at four different points across each cell and averaged. The average cell diameter value was used as an indicator of adipocyte hypertrophy. Additionally, brain monoamines (dopamine, serotonin and noradrenaline) were determined by HPLC method following a method earlier reported by Lakshmana et al. (1997) [19].

Expression of genes involved in lipid metabolism in liver tissue

Liquid Nitrogen frozen liver tissue was used for total RNA extraction using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Quality and quantity of the extracted RNA was determined using agarose gel electrophoresis (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA) and ND-1000 CV spectrophotometer (Nanodrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA) respectively. The genes selected were SREBP-1c (Sterol Regulatory Element Binding Protein-1c), PPAR-γ (Peroxisome Proliferator–Activated Receptor Gamma), ACC (Acetyl-CoA Carboxylase Alpha), ACSS2 (Acyl-CoA Synthetase) and FASN (Fatty Acid Synthase). cDNA was synthesized from extracted RNA (2 μg RNA from each sample) using Invitrogen's reverse transcriptase kit. Using TB Green Premix SYBR green expression assays (TAKARA, Japan), qRT-PCR was performed on Applied Biosystems' (USA) StepOne real-time PCR system. The following conditions were applied for the PCR protocol: an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of (1) denaturation at 95°C for 3 s, (2) annealing at 60°C for 30 s, and (3) extension at 72°C for 15 s. A final extension step was carried out at 72°C for 5 min. GAPDH (Glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase) was used as the housekeeping gene.

Statistical analysis

Data is presented as Mean ± SD. Treatment groups were compared with Normal control (NC) and Disease control (DC) group by applying Two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni posttests. GraphPad Version 5.03 was used for statistical analysis. Level of significance (p < 0.05) was considered significant.

Results

As mentioned in methodology, the anti-obesity effect of Trimad was evaluated with another polyherbal formulation (Triphala) to minimize usage of animals owing to 3Rs guidelines. Keeping 3 groups viz. Normal control, Disease control and Positive control same, three dose levels of both formulations were evaluated in the same experiment. The data of Triphala along with the control groups have been already published [15].

Effect of Trimad on expression of genes involved in lipid metabolism in liver tissue

Effect of Trimad on expression of genes involved in liver lipid metabolism was determined using qRT-PCR (Fig. 5). The genes studied were PPARλ, SREBP-1c, ACC, ACSS2 and FASN. The expression of PPARλ was found to be up-regulated by ∼1.5 times in DC group (p < 0.01) in comparison to the NC group (Fig. 5 (A)). Lower dose of Trimad (TM 50) along with PC had lower expression levels for this gene though the effects were significant only for TM 50 (p < 0.05). TM 50 down-regulated PPARλ expression as compared to DC. But highest Trimad dose (i.e. TM 200) had statistically significant higher level of PPARλ (p < 0.001 vs DC). SREBP-1c expression was up-regulated in DC group by ∼4.7 folds as compared to the NC group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5 (B)). TM 50 and TM 100 showed ∼2.0 and ∼1.8 fold down regulation of SREBP-1c as compared to the DC group (p < 0.001 and p < 0.05 respectively). Such down regulation was not detected in TM 200. Similar to SREBP-1c expression pattern, there was up-regulation of ACC gene expression by ∼1.7 fold in DC in comparison to NC (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5 (C)). In Trimad treated groups, TM 50 significantly (p < 0.001) down-regulated ACC gene expression by ∼1.6 times as compared to DC. But in TM 200 group, instead of down regulation, up-regulation in the expression of this gene was observed. The ACSS2 gene expression in the DC was up-regulated (p < 0.001) by ∼2.2 times in comparison to NC rats (Fig. 5 (D)). There was a statistically significant decrease in the expression of ACSS2 gene in all treated groups (p < 0.001) compared to the DC group. Discussion

In this study, in an HFD-induced obese rat model, Trimad demonstrated significant anti-obesity effects, evaluated alongside Triphala under 3Rs guidelines to minimize animal use. Trimad treatment led to reduced body weight and feed intake, improved serum lipid profiles, and lowered glucose levels compared to the Disease control group. It also attenuated leptin and CRP levels while enhancing adiponectin and antioxidant capacity (ORAC), and significantly reduced oxidative stress markers (MDA) in both serum and liver. Adipocyte hypertrophy was significantly reversed in a dose-dependent manner. Brain monoamine analysis showed normalization of serotonin, dopamine, and noradrenaline levels. Moreover, in liver, Trimad modulated expression of genes involved in lipid metabolism (significant down-regulating SREBP-1c, ACC, ACSS2, and FASN) especially at lower doses. These findings suggest that Trimad effectively ameliorates metabolic, inflammatory, oxidative, and molecular alterations associated with diet-induced obesity.

The effects of HFD on various metabolic pathways are well documented. HFD is reported to induce transient insulin resistance, glucose intolerance and hypertriglyceridemia with impact on total cholesterol [20]. We observed significantly elevated glucose and triglyceride levels along with significantly increased cholesterol levels in Disease control group which had received only HFD throughout the study period. Treatment with Trimad was found to reverse these perturbations. In liver tissue, compared to Disease control, MDA levels were lower and SOD levels were higher. These observations suggest that the beneficial effects by Trimad are probably due to improved fatty acid oxidation in liver [21]. Long term energy balance regulation is mediated by Leptin. It is mainly expressed in adipose tissue and lowers food intake, enhances energy expenditure with simultaneous induction of weight loss [[22], [23]] Inability to induce satiety even in the presence of high leptin levels and energy stores indicates reduction in the leptin sensitivity (leptin resistance). In our study, we observed increased levels of leptin along with increased feed intake in Disease control group indicating leptin resistance. All Trimad treated groups showed decrease in leptin levels along with decrease in feed intake as compared to Disease control group indicating improved leptin sensitivity. Adiponectin, another important adipokine, has both the metabolic and anti-inflammatory activity [24]. Higher adiponectin levels are associated with enhanced insulin sensitivity, fatty acid oxidation in liver & skeletal muscle and energy dissipation [[25], [26]]. In our study, after obesity induction by feeding HFD in Disease control group, adiponectin levels were lowered. This effect on adiponectin was reversed by Trimad treatment. We observed increase in adiponectin levels and corresponding decrease in serum triglyceride after treatment with Trimad. It can be concluded from the above observations that reduced leptin and enhanced adiponectin levels might have restored insulin sensitivity after Trimad treatment and ultimately have resulted in reduced lipid levels and weight.

IL-6 is involved in triglyceride release, lipoprotein lipase down-regulation, insulin sensitivity, and so on [27]. IL-6 receptors are abundantly expressed in hepatocytes (liver), they induce expression of C-reactive protein (acute phase protein, CRP) in liver. Additionally, there is positive co-relation between CRP levels and mass of adipose tissue [[28], [29]]. Elevated IL-6 further induces release of CRP [30]. CRP is not only a marker of inflammation, but it also participates in tissue damage. Trimad treatment showed marked decrease in IL-6 and prevented increase in CRP levels, which indicates that Trimad acts on inflammatory processes mediated through obesity and adipose tissue.

In obesity, elevated circulating glucose and lipid levels (excessive energy supply) can result in elevated metabolic pathway activities in adipose and non-adipose tissues which may result in elevated production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [31]. When these ROS attack polyunsaturated fatty acids, fatty acids undergo lipid peroxidation. HFD treatment for 48 days increased levels of lipid peroxidation that was measured as MDA in serum. A significant decline in the MDA levels was seen in Trimad administered groups. Further, while HFD treatment reduced the levels of anti-oxidants measured as ORAC, these levels were significantly increased at all dose levels of Trimad. Similarly, in liver tissue of Disease control group, significantly higher MDA levels were detected in comparison to Normal control. But the treatment groups (except Trimad 50 mg/kg) showed significantly lower levels of MDA as compared to Disease control group. At the same time, the levels of Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), an anti-oxidative enzyme was found significantly higher in Trimad treated groups. These findings confirm our earlier observation on anti-oxidant capacity of Trimad [32]. Considering the anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory and anti-hyperlipidaemic effects of Trimad, it may prove as a potential agent for preventing hepatic steatosis.

Brain monoamines play a vital role in obesity. Though some reports mention that the alterations in monoamines lead to obesity [33], there are reports stating that development of obesity occurs before any monoamine alterations [34]. The Disease control rats exhibited decrease in serotonin levels, which was improved by Trimad treatment. Serotonin (5-HT) is a monoaminergic neurotransmitter that modulates central and peripheral functions. It is known to have anorectic properties [35]. It controls feeding behavior, energy expenditure by restoring the caloric homeostasis within the body [36] and is connected to fat burning mechanism [37]. Opposite to serotonin, norepinephrine levels were found elevated in the Disease control group. Trimad treatment was found to decrease these levels. Norepinephrine contributes to stimulation of hunger and satiety. Additionally, it is also connected to thermogenesis [38]. Central nervous system controls energy balance through norepinephrine. It needs to be noted here that involvement of serotonin and norepinephrine in neural pathways has been targeted for anti-obesity drug development [39]. The levels of dopamine as compared to Disease control group were found significantly increased in all treatment groups. It is well reported that Dopamine is a major contributor in brain reward circuit [40]. It therefore contributes in food intake and eating behaviors. Engagement of dopamine in eating behavior has been proved by brain imaging studies in both animals and humans. Thus, targeting improvement in dopamine function is a new therapeutic option for the prevention and obesity management [41].

It is well known that in obesity, adipocytes store surplus energy as triacylglycerols that leads to expansion in their size (hypertrophy) and number (hyperplasia). Adipocyte hypertrophy results from a relative increase in lipid deposition versus reduced lipolysis. In our study, as compared to Normal control group, adipocyte diameters (adipocyte size) in Disease control group were significantly higher while in treatment groups adipocyte sizes were reduced.

In liver steatosis, lipogenic gene (PPARλ and SREBP1-c) expression is up-regulated [42] which leads to lipid accumulation in the form of droplets in liver. As a result of this, the liver becomes fatty [43]. We observed down regulated PPARλ expression with lower dose of Trimad implying decrease in fatty infiltration in liver. The histopathology of liver also confirmed the reduction in fatty changes in liver. Abundant expression level of SREBP-1c is observed in liver [44]. Over expression of SREBP-1c is associated with elevated triglyceride levels [45]. Various lipogenic genes (ACC, ACSS2 and FASN) are regulated by transcription factor SREBP-1c. All these genes are up-regulated in obesity [[46], [47]] In our study, elevated levels of SREBP-1c further leading to elevation of ACC, ACSS2 and FASN expression resulted in higher cholesterol and triglyceride levels in serum of Disease control group. As compared to the Disease control animals, in Trimad treated animals, these genes were down-regulated. Though, mechanistic investigations were limited to selected gene expression analyses, overall, these modulations in transcription factor and its targeted genes have resulted in the normalization of abnormal lipid profiles along with lowering of body weight in the Trimad treated animals.

It is important to note here that Trimad is a polyherbal formulation with complex phytochemical composition. These phytochemicals can act through distinct molecular pathways and can produce differential effects at different dose levels. Thus, for polyherbal formulations due to multicomponent nature, biological responses may not always be linear across the doses [[48], [49], [50]]. Investigations like receptor saturation studies would better explain the observed dose-dependent responses. Additionally, future in-depth studies assessing the modulation of hepatic steatosis and the molecular pathways associated with obesity and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) are warranted to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the observed effects of Trimad polyherbal formulation.

Conclusion

Obesity involves multiple pathways like hunger-satiety mechanism, insulin signaling, lipid metabolism, oxidative stress and chronic inflammation. To conclude, in our study, Trimad has shown promising results on almost each of these mechanisms though in varying degrees. Of the 3 doses studied, we found the maximum response with 50 mg/kg in terms of body weight, feed intake, leptin, lipid peroxidation, and liver weight. The response at higher doses viz. 100 and 200 mg/kg was found either diminished or blunted.
