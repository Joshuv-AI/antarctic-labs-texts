Sensation without perception: The white whale effect and perceptual blindness in autonomous vehicles
Authors: Philip Tseng, Chien-Chung Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autonomous vehicles (AVs) use sensors that exceed human capabilities in resolution and reaction speed, yet a catastrophic failure mode persists: the inability to detect massive, white obstacles (such as semi-trailers) against bright skies. Although engineering analyses attribute these accidents to sensor dynamic range or radar filtration errors, we argue they represent a fundamental failure of perceptual organization. We propose the term “white whale effect” to describe a form of machine visual blindness where high-luminance objects are misclassified as ambient background because of failures in lightness anchoring, figure-ground segregation, and global motion perception. We conclude that safer AV perception requires moving beyond pixel-level classification to architectures constrained by the principles of psychophysics.

Introduction

On a sunny May afternoon in 2016, a Tesla Model S operating on a driver-assist system in Florida drove directly into the side of a white semi-trailer crossing the highway. The vehicle did not brake, steer, or slow down, passing completely under the trailer in a fatal underride collision. In its final report, the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) delivered a chilling summary of the failure: “Neither Autopilot nor the driver noticed the white side of the tractor-trailer against a brightly lit sky, so the brake was not applied” (National Transportation Safety Board, 2017).

This was not an isolated anomaly. Three years later, in a nearly identical scenario, another vehicle struck a white trailer crossing its path, resulting in similar fatalities (National Transportation Safety Board, 2020). In Taiwan, a system ignored an overturned white truck spanning two lanes, accelerating directly into the massive obstacle as if it were empty space (Taiwan News, 2020). Recent analyses of NHTSA data suggest a recurring pattern in autonomous vehicle (AV) crashes: high-speed impacts into stationary or cross-traffic obstacles that were plainly visible to human observers (Siddiqui & Troughton, 2023).

A distinct and paradoxical visual signature unites these catastrophes. In each instance, the obstacle was not small, obscured, or camouflaged. On the contrary, the obstacles were massive, filling a significant portion of the visual field. They were featureless. And crucially, they were high-luminance objects (mostly white) set against high-luminance backgrounds. The machine successfully navigated complex, cluttered environments only to fail against the largest, clearest target on the road. This counterintuitive pattern suggests that the sheer visibility of the object, ironically its overwhelming brightness and size, is precisely what renders it invisible to the system.

Sensation is not perception

Current engineering post-mortems typically diagnose these events as failures of sensation, citing “camera dynamic range” limitations or “radar filtration” protocols. However, in biological systems, there is a fundamental distinction between the proximal stimulus (raw photon energy) and the perceptual object (the internal mental construction of a solid form).

Current AV architectures excel at sensation (feature registration) but fail at perception (active reconstruction). The system successfully captured the images of the truck, and even extracted local features, but failed to organize such data into a coherent representation of a solid object. The vehicle suffered from a specific form of functional blindness: it registered the scene (the sky, the road) but could not segregate the massive object within it.

We term this the “white whale effect.” Like Melville's leviathan, the object is rendered invisible not by its absence, but by its overwhelming, featureless homogeneity. Lacking the internal texture gradients required to define surface orientation or depth, the massive white obstacle is effectively normalized by the vision pipeline and perceived as a continuation of the ambient sky. We analyze this phenomenon as a cascade of three specific perceptual failures in visual and cognitive science.

Mechanism I: The anchoring failure

The primary failure is the inability to distinguish a white object from a bright sky. In biological vision, the perception of “white” is not absolute but relative. Gilchrist's anchoring theory posits that the visual system determines surface lightness by “anchoring” the scene to the brightest object in a specific depth plane or by computing local contrast ratios (Gilchrist et al., 1999). Humans successfully distinguish a white truck from a bright sky by using contextual cues, such as the darker road surface and cast shadows, to calibrate the scene's illuminant.

Current AV vision pipelines, however, often rely on global normalization techniques (e.g., histogram equalization) to manage high-dynamic-range scenes. In a “white whale” scenario, the white trailer is a massive collection of high-luminance pixels. For human observers, the trailer would be locally the brightest object and would be perceived as white. However, lacking a robust psychophysical model of color constancy, the algorithm attempts to normalize the “average” brightness of the entire frame and ignore local salience. In this case, the dark surface of the road dominates the average luminance during normalization, pulling the dynamic range much lower than the luminance of the sky and white truck and throwing them to the high extreme of the dynamic range. Consequently, the luminance response is compressed around the luminance regions for the white trailer and sky, effectively “whitewashing” the obstacle out of existence.

Mechanism II: The figure-ground failure

Once the anchoring failure has compressed the trailer's luminance values into the range of the sky, the system faces a second, compounding deficit: the inability to perform figure-ground segregation. To detect an object, a vision system must not only detect an edge but also assign border ownership by determining which side of the line is the “figure” and which is the “ground” (Wagemans et al., 2012).

Deep learning classifiers typically rely on sharp luminance gradients and internal texture cues to solve this problem. However, because Mechanism I has effectively equalized the brightness of the trailer and the sky, the contrast at the object's boundary approaches zero. Unlike a tree or a textured wall, the smooth white side of a trailer lacks the internal texture gradients that the visual system uses to infer surface orientation. Deprived of both strong edge contrast and internal texture, the vision pipeline groups the “whitewashed” trailer and the bright sky into a single, continuous region.

Mechanism III: Motion blindness

The final safety net is motion detection. Even if the classifier fails to recognize the object (“It's not a truck”), the perception stack should theoretically detect that something is moving across the path and brake. Why did the system fail to detect the optic flow?

This highlights a failure of global motion perception, specifically an extreme manifestation of the aperture problem in vision science. To perceive motion, a visual system must track the displacement of texture elements or edges over time. Although the small apertures of individual sensors often produce ambiguous signals, biological systems resolve this by integrating signals across multiple receptive fields. However, a massive, featureless white wall moving at the same speed creates a retinal slip that is indistinguishable from a stationary sky viewed from a moving vehicle (Wallach, 1935).

Because the sensor's field of view is filled by a stimulus with uniform luminance (Mechanism I) and no internal texture (Mechanism II), the system lacks both the optical flow and the stationary reference frame required to distinguish object motion from self-motion. Mathematically, the motion equations become undefined. The system calculates zero relative velocity for the obstacle, classifying it as a static overhead sign or bridge—objects tagged as “Safe to Drive Under” in highway driving policies. This failure might have been prevented by the presence of high-frequency texture cues (e.g., logos, dirt, or graffiti) to break the uniformity, which explains why confirmed “white whale” accidents exclusively involve vehicles with pristine, textureless surfaces.

Psychophysical constraints for engineering

The logic of this “perceptual cascade” dictates that downstream solutions are futile if the upstream sensory representation has been degraded. Therefore engineering interventions must prioritize the root of the perceptual chain. 1.Context-Anchored Normalization: The “white whale” effect is, first and foremost, a failure of lightness constancy. As noted in Mechanism I, global normalization algorithms often misinterpret dark pavement as an underexposed scene, boosting the gain until the sky and truck clip into indistinguishable saturation. To solve this, algorithms must instead adopt “Ground Anchoring” by establishing local frameworks and computing local contrast ratios (Gilchrist et al., 1999; Gilchrist, 2006). Rather than normalizing the global average, which inappropriately blends distinct illuminant zones, the system should calibrate its dynamic range strictly based on the adjacent road surface. By treating the road as a distinct depth plane with a known low albedo, the system establishes a local anchor. This ensures that the system's “highest luminance” rule is applied contextually, preventing the auto-exposure from over-correcting and thereby preserving the critical highlight detail necessary to distinguish the white obstacle from the bright background.2.Prioritizing “Good Continuation”: Even with proper anchoring, a white trailer against a white sky will present a low-contrast boundary. Current models often allow high-confidence semantic priors (“The road is clear”) to suppress low-confidence edge data. To prevent this, systems must weigh the Gestalt principle of good continuation (collinearity) more heavily than region similarity (Wagemans et al., 2012). A continuous horizontal edge that spans the horizon must be treated as a potential barrier (a “horizon wall") until depth sensors prove it is permeable.3.Active Sensing: The aperture problem is unsolvable if the sensor captures only a single frame of a featureless wall. However, biological systems solve this through spatiotemporal integration. If a “featureless” region is detected, the system should not default to “clear sky” (a false negative) but to an “uncertainty state.” This triggers a protocol of active sensing: explicitly suppressing acceleration to create a temporal window for integration. This is not merely theoretical; neurophysiological studies show that neurons in the macaque MT area initially miscalculate motion because of the aperture problem (at ∼70ms) but resolve the ambiguity through temporal integration by 140 ms (Pack & Born, 2001). Artificial systems must mimic this biological “wait-and-see” window.

Environmental interventions: Immediate low-tech solutions

Although the aforementioned algorithmic changes require fundamental shifts in AV architecture, the psychophysical mechanisms discussed here also suggest immediate, environmental interventions. Before vision pipelines are overhauled, the “white whale” effect could be mitigated by altering the visual properties of the trailers themselves to directly counteract the three mechanisms of failure. First, to address the anchoring failure (Mechanism I), regulatory bodies could mandate two-tone color schemes or matte, nonreflective finishes for large commercial trailers. A wide, low-albedo horizontal band painted across the midline of a white trailer would provide a permanent local contrast anchor, preventing the surface from matching the sky's luminance. Second, to aid figure-ground segregation (Mechanism II), mandating high-contrast, dark outlines along the physical edges of the trailers would artificially restore the border contrast lost to global normalization. Finally, to resolve motion blindness and the aperture problem (Mechanism III), applying large, high-frequency texture elements (such as checkerboard patterns, bold asymmetrical logos, or disruptive camouflage) to the side panels would break the uniform luminance, providing the internal texture required to generate robust optic flow. Future research using computer vision simulations to test the efficacy of these environmental modifications on crash reduction rates would be highly valuable.

Concluding remarks: The need for perceptual synthesis

The “white whale” effect is merely one symptom of a broader dissociation between sensing and seeing. Other recurring crash phenotypes suggest that current architectures suffer from systemic fragilities in perceptual inference. For instance, in what we call the “moth effect”, where vehicles inexplicably accelerate into stationary emergency vehicles displaying flashing lights, has been linked to at least 16 confirmed crashes (National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, 2021). Similarly, the system's susceptibility to “Phantom Objects” (braking for two-dimensional images of stop signs on billboards) demonstrates a brittle overreliance on view-dependent two-dimensional templates rather than three-dimensional structural coherence (Nassi et al., 2020). This limitation is strikingly reminiscent of the classic “view-dependence versus structural description” debate that dominated vision science in the 1990s (Biederman, 1987; Peissig & Tarr, 2007). It suggests that modern computer vision is unknowingly retracing the historical steps of cognitive psychology. In biological vision, organisms achieve object constancy not by memorizing static templates, but through active exploration—accumulating multiple views by moving around an object. Autonomous systems must similarly exploit their own ego-motion (e.g., using the 180° arc of perspective gained while overtaking a vehicle) to construct robust, viewpoint-invariant three-dimensional representations.

Many mysteries remain, and we do not yet know the precise algorithmic failures that cause a deep learning model to misperceive. However, the recurring pattern is undeniably perceptual. These are not failures of resolution (the cameras saw the truck); they are failures of interpretation. These outstanding mysteries highlight the urgent need for a new collaborative synthesis. Solving them requires engineers to look beyond training loss functions and engage with the rich, century-long literature of psychophysics. Conversely, the catastrophic failure of these artificial systems serves as a powerful computational counter-example for vision scientists. By observing the behavioral collapse of a system lacking lightness anchoring and figure-ground priors, we gain a renewed, empirical appreciation for why biological visual systems evolved these specific architectural constraints in the first place.
