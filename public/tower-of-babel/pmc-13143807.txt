FTO rs7195994 Is associated with TNF inhibitor response in lean rheumatoid arthritis patients: A BMI-stratified pharmacogenetic analysis
Authors: Yi-Ting Li, I-Chieh Chen, Hui-Wen Yang, Chung-Mao Kao, Yen-Ju Chen, Wen-Nan Huang, Yi-Ming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi) have transformed the management of rheumatoid arthritis (RA); however, up to 40% of patients fail to achieve an adequate clinical response. Current clinical predictors remain insufficient, highlighting the need for pharmacogenetic biomarkers to guide biologic therapy selection. In this study, we investigated genetic variants associated with TNFi treatment response, with a particular focus on body mass index (BMI)–dependent effects, using a large real-world cohort. A total of 519 patients with RA were identified from the Taiwan Precision Medicine Initiative (TPMI), an electronic health record–linked biobank. Eligible patients had received TNFi therapy for at least 6 months and had available genotyping data. Ninety-seven candidate single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) previously reported to be associated with TNFi response were identified through a systematic literature search. Five variants located in immune–metabolic genes (FTO, ZNF618, RANK, CD84, and LOC105375523) were further analyzed using univariable and multivariable logistic regression models. Subgroup analyses stratified by BMI were performed to explore potential effect modification. Three variants—FTO rs7195994, ZNF618 rs16911006, and LOC105375523 rs834811—were significantly associated with TNFi response in initial analyses. In multivariable models, only FTO rs7195994 remained an independent predictor of non-response (odds ratio [OR] 0.44, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.22–0.87; p = 0.019). Among patients with BMI < 27 kg/m², carriers of the rs7195994 risk allele had a 49% lower likelihood of achieving treatment response (OR 0.51, 95% CI 0.28–0.92; p = 0.0249), whereas no significant association was observed in patients with higher BMI. These findings identify FTO rs7195994 as a novel, BMI-modulated pharmacogenetic marker of TNFi non-response in RA. Incorporating BMI-stratified genetic profiling into clinical decision-making may facilitate early identification of patients unlikely to benefit from TNFi therapy, thereby supporting precision treatment strategies. Further validation in multiethnic populations and functional studies is warranted.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by persistent synovial inflammation and progressive structural joint damage [1]. The use of tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi) has substantially improved disease management, leading to reductions in disease activity, structural damage, and functional disability [1, 2]. Nevertheless, therapeutic response is heterogeneous, with 30–40% of patients failing to achieve sufficient clinical benefit despite standard treatment [3]. Given the substantial cost and potential adverse effects of TNFi, identifying predictive biomarkers to guide therapeutic decisions is a critical unmet need.

Clinical predictors of TNFi response—such as female sex, lower baseline disease activity, rheumatoid factor (RF) or anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPA positivity, and concurrent methotrexate use—have been consistently reported [4–6]. Genetic variants within TNF-related pathways and Fc gamma receptor (FcγR) loci have also been investigated [7–9], though replication across cohorts remains inconsistent and effect sizes modest. These limitations underscore the ongoing need for novel, biologically credible genetic markers that could account for interindividual variability in treatment efficacy.

The fat mass and obesity-associated (FTO) gene, originally associated with energy balance and adiposity, has recently been implicated in immune regulation and autoimmunity[10, 11]. Mechanistic studies suggest that FTO regulates immune responses through N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA demethylation and may modulate expression of cytokines and interferon-stimulated genes [12, 13]. In RA, FTO expression has been shown to promote synovial fibroblast proliferation, inhibit apoptosis, and exacerbate inflammation via the mitochondrial DNA–cGAS–STING signaling pathway, which activates type I interferon and pro-inflammatory responses [14, 15]. Collectively, these findings highlight FTO as a plausible immunometabolic contributor to RA pathogenesis and biologic therapy resistance.

Although a genome-wide association study (GWAS) by Massey et al. identified a signal near the FTO locus in relation to TNFi response, its direct functional relevance remains unclear[16]. However, emerging evidence from experimental and animal models supports the hypothesis that FTO activity may influence inflammatory thresholds relevant to TNFi efficacy, particularly in immune or adiposity-related contexts [12, 14, 15, 17].

In this investigation, we adopted a candidate gene framework to assess the potential association between immune-metabolic SNPs and clinical response to TNFi in rheumatoid arthritis.Candidate SNPs were selected based on a targeted literature review and PubMed-based search strategy, integrating recent GWAS, meta-analyses, and mechanistic studies in RA pharmacogenetics. Among these, we evaluated FTO rs7195994 due to its reported relevance to immunometabolic function. We further examined whether the association between FTO variation and treatment outcomes differs across BMI strata using stratified analyses.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study was undertaken at Taichung Veterans General Hospital, a major tertiary care center in central Taiwan. Patient-level data were obtained through the Taiwan Precision Medicine Initiative (TPMI), a nationwide genomic cohort coordinated by Academia Sinica [18] We identified adult patients diagnosed with RA who initiated TNFi therapy between January 1, 2005 and December 31, 2024. RA diagnosis was based on either the 1991 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) revised criteria or the 2010 ACR/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) classification criteria [19] confirmed by relevant ICD-9-CM (714.0, 714.30–714.33) or ICD-10-CM (M05.7–M05.9, M06.0, M06.2–M06.3, M06.8–M06.9, M08) codes recorded in at least one inpatient or two outpatient encounters. Eligible patients had received one of four TNFi agents (etanercept, adalimumab, golimumab, or certolizumab) for ≥6 months and had complete genotyping, baseline disease activity, and longitudinal treatment response data. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taichung Veterans General Hospital (CF21176A) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical data and laboratory assessments

Baseline characteristics—including demographics, disease duration, comorbidities, and medication use (e.g., csDMARDs, corticosteroids)—were extracted from structured electronic medical records. Laboratory parameters such as RF, ACPA, ESR, and CRP were assessed at baseline prior to TNFi initiation, using standardized immunoassay methods. Disease activity was evaluated via the DAS28 composite index, which integrates tender and swollen joint counts, ESR, and patient global assessment [19, 20].

Treatment response

Treatment response at 6 months was primarily defined using the EULAR response criteria, which incorporate both absolute disease activity (DAS28) and change from baseline to evaluate therapeutic efficacy in rheumatoid arthritis [21]. Patients achieving a good EULAR response (i.e. DAS28 < 3.2 with a significant improvement from baseline) were classified as responders, while all others were categorized as non-responders.

In instances where DAS28 scores were unavailable due to missing data, a pragmatic approach was adopted using additional objective markers of disease control. Specifically, patients were also classified as responders if they achieved either ESR < 20 mm/h or CRP < 0.3 mg/L at 6 months, reflecting low inflammatory activity [19]. This strategy ensured the inclusion of clinically relevant responders while minimizing data loss.

Based on this composite definition, 425 patients were classified as responders and 94 as non-responders. The completeness of DAS28 data at 6 months was assessed, and missing DAS28 data are described in the Results section.

Genotyping, quality controls and SNPs selection

This study was conducted using data from the Taiwan Precision Medicine Initiative (TPMI), a national biobank project integrating genomic and clinical data across multiple healthcare settings in Taiwan [22]. Between June 2019 and December 2024, a total of 63,934 participants were recruited into the TPMI at Taichung Veterans General Hospital (TCVGH). TPMI participants included adults (>18 years) who attended 28 internal medicine and surgical outpatient clinics. The cohort comprised individuals with a wide range of medical and surgical conditions rather than healthy volunteers. For the present analysis, we identified the subset of patients diagnosed with RA who initiated TNFi therapy and had available genotyping data[22]. All participants provided written informed consent to undergo genomic analysis and linkage with electronic health records.

Among these participants, 519 individuals were identified as having RA based on fulfillment of the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria, confirmed by board-certified rheumatologists through electronic chart review. These RA patients were selected from within the larger TPMI cohort and constituted the population for the present pharmacogenetic analysis [22].

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood using standardized isolation protocols of the TPMI using standardized isolation protocols [23]. DNA concentration was assessed via NanoDrop spectrophotometry, and genotyping was performed using the Axiom TWB 2.0 array (Affymetrix), which includes over 714,431 SNPs optimized for Han Chinese ancestry [23]. Quality control excluded SNPs with monomorphic alleles, call rates <95%, minor allele frequency <0.01, or Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium p < 1×10⁻⁵. After QC, 591,048 SNPs were retained and analyzed using PLINK v1.9 [24].

We analyzed data from 519 RA patients who initiated TNF inhibitor therapy. Ninety-seven candidate SNPs were selected via a structured PubMed search (2005–2024), incorporating MeSH terms for rheumatoid arthritis, TNF inhibitors, single nucleotide polymorphisms, and treatment response (see Supplementary Data 1). Although biosimilar agents were not explicitly included as search terms, broader class-level terms (e.g., “anti-TNF,” “TNF inhibitors”) were used. During the study period, biosimilar uptake in Taiwan remained limited due to reimbursement policies, and nearly all patients in our cohort received originator TNF inhibitors.

Initial SNP screening was performed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, and SNPs showing a nominal association with TNFi response (p < 0.05) were selected for subsequent multivariable and BMI-stratified analyses. Five SNPs of interest—ZNF618 rs16911006, RANK rs8086340, FTO rs7195994, LOC105375523 rs834811, and CD84 rs6427528—were prioritized for further analysis based on statistically significant associations with treatment response, identified through chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate (see Supplementary Table 1).

BMI stratification

Because Asian populations exhibit increased metabolic and cardiovascular risk at lower BMI levels compared with Western populations, we applied BMI thresholds specifically recommended for Asian cohorts. The World Health Organization (WHO) Expert Consultation identifies 24 kg/m² as the range at which metabolic risk begins to rise in Asian populations [25]. In Taiwan, epidemiologic studies similarly demonstrate that BMI ≥ 27 kg/m² is associated with substantially elevated obesity-related health risks [26]. Accordingly, we predefined two BMI categories for effect-modification analyses. These thresholds were determined to reflect population-appropriate adiposity patterns.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics, including means with standard deviations and proportions stratified by study group. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test under the assumption of normality, while categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-squared test. Fisher’s exact test was applied instead of the Chi-square test when the number of individuals in any comparison group was fewer than 30, to ensure statistical robustness. The specific test used for each SNP is listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Associations between individual SNPs and treatment response were evaluated using univariable and multivariable logistic regression models, adjusting for age, sex, disease duration, DAS28, ESR, CRP, RF/ACPA serostatus, biologic-naïve status, and comorbidities. BMI-stratified analyses were conducted to explore potential effect modification.

Multivariable analysis was limited to the five SNPs that demonstrated nominal significance in univariable screening and were considered biologically plausible candidates. All covariates were retained in the multivariable model because they were selected based on clinical relevance and established predictors of TNFi response.

Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Because this study was designed as a hypothesis-driven pharmacogenetic analysis using a predefined panel of biologically plausible candidate SNPs rather than a genome-wide discovery approach, multiple testing correction (e.g., Bonferroni or FDR) was not applied. Given the modest sample size, applying stringent correction procedures would considerably increase the risk of type II error and potentially obscure clinically meaningful genetic associations. This analytical strategy is consistent with prior exploratory pharmacogenetic studies.

Genetic associations with treatment response

Five candidate SNPs associated with treatment response were identified and are summarized in Table 2. In univariable analysis, rs16911006 (ZNF618), rs8086340 (RANK), and rs7195994 (FTO) were linked to a reduced likelihood of TNFi response, while rs834811 (LOC105375523) was associated with increased response. The rs6427528 (CD84) variant showed a trend toward benefit, but this did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.055). In univariable models adjusted for age and sex, rs7195994 (FTO) remained a significant predictor of reduced response (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.38–0.99; p = 0.045). Similarly, rs16911006 (ZNF618) and rs834811 (LOC105375523) were associated with lower and higher odds of response, respectively. To evaluate independent predictors, four multivariate logistic regression models were constructed (Table 3), each incorporating one SNP and adjusting for demographic factors, disease activity (DAS28, ESR, CRP), csDMARD use, and comorbidities. BMI-stratified analysis of rs7195994

In light of FTO’s known role in adiposity and metabolic regulation, we performed a BMI-stratified analysis to assess whether the association between rs7195994 and TNFi treatment response differed by body composition. Among patients with BMI < 27, carriers of rs7195994 exhibited significantly reduced odds of achieving response compared to non-carriers (OR 0.51, 95% CI 0.28–0.92; p = 0.0249). This pattern persisted in the BMI < 24 subgroup (OR 0.49, 95% CI 0.25–1.00; p = 0.0497). Discussion

This real-world cohort analysis evaluated the relationship between selected SNPs and clinical response to TNF inhibitors in patients with RA. Among five tested variants, three (FTO rs7195994, ZNF618 rs16911006, and LOC105375523 rs834811) were significantly associated with TNFi response after multivariable adjustment, with FTO rs7195994 demonstrating the strongest and most consistent effect. Among patients with lower BMI, carriers of the rs7195994 risk allele had significantly lower odds of achieving clinical remission, suggesting a metabolically defined subgroup with diminished TNFi efficacy. These findings support the potential value of incorporating genotype-informed stratification into personalized RA treatment strategies.

Prior evidence supports both metabolic and immunoregulatory functions of FTO, including roles in macrophage activation, adipocyte remodeling, and autoimmune inflammation [10, 13]. A UK GWAS by Massey et al. also identified rs7195994, but only in relation to improvement in swollen joint count (ΔSJC28) among infliximab-treated patients, whereas no variants predicted overall change in DAS28. Importantly, the UK association persisted after BMI adjustment, suggesting a functional effect not explained solely by adiposity. In contrast, our study identified an inverse association, ie. reduced remission in lean carriers, highlighting the possibility of context-dependent and phenotype-specific effects of FTO variants [16]

Several methodological and biological factors may account for these divergent observations. Critically, the two studies differ fundamentally in outcome definition. Massey et al. analyzed continuous changes in individual DAS28 components, and the rs7195994 association was confined to ΔSJC28, a domain reflecting local synovitis. In contrast, our stringent composite remission endpoint required simultaneous normalization of DAS28-ESR, ESR, and CRP, capturing systemic inflammatory control. Because DAS28 components reflect partially non-overlapping biological domains, genetic influences affecting joint swelling may not translate to multidimensional remission outcomes that incorporate acute-phase responses. Prior literature similarly demonstrates outcome-dependent variation in pharmacogenetic associations. These differences, along with variation in genetic background and TNFi distribution (mixed TNFi vs. infliximab-only), provide a mechanistically and methodologically coherent explanation for the contrasting effect directions across cohorts.

Mechanistically, FTO regulates N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA demethylation and activates the cGAS–STING–interferon axis, promoting synovial proliferation and cytokine production [27]. rs7195994 is in strong linkage disequilibrium with rs2540767, which interacts with the IRX3 promoter—a gene involved in immunometabolic regulation— supporting the plausibility of a regulatory mechanism at this locus. The stronger genetic effect in lean individuals may reflect a setting in which intrinsic immunogenetic pathways outweigh adipose-derived inflammatory effects. Future work integrating genotype, adipokine profiles, and synovial transcriptomics may clarify these mechanistic hypotheses.

Beyond FTO, we observed nominal associations for ZNF618 rs16911006, RANK rs8086340, and LOC105375523 rs834811. ZNF618 has been implicated in transcriptional regulation and cytokine signaling; a European cohort previously reported an association between rs16911006 and reduced TNFi response (OR 0.58, p = 0.042) [28]. However, this association was attenuated in our adjusted models, possibly due to differences in linkage disequilibrium patterns or confounding by BMI. RANK (also known as TNFRSF11A) mediates osteoclast activation and may influence TNFi receptor binding, but the previously reported association of rs8086340 with TNFi efficacy was not replicated in our study [29]. For LOC105375523, a non-coding RNA variant (rs834811) showed increased odds of response in our cohort, although it lacks external validation in existing literature. Functional annotation using GTEx or ENCODE may clarify the biological significance of these variants.

With respect to outcome definition, we employed a stringent composite endpoint for clinical remission, combining DAS28-ESR < 3.2 with low ESR and CRP thresholds. Compared to categorical indices like EULAR response criteria [30], composite remission measures may capture multidimensional inflammatory control beyond categorical response definitions, which is particularly advantageous in pharmacogenetic analyses with moderate sample sizes [31]. Nonetheless, future studies may benefit from reporting both categorical and continuous outcomes to enhance clinical relevance.

The proportion of patients achieving EULAR good response in our cohort appears somewhat higher than that reported in several large registry-based studies of TNFi-treated RA populations [32]. This may reflect the implementation of a treat-to-target strategy with structured follow-up [33], the near-universal use of concomitant methotrexate therapy known to enhance TNFi efficacy [34], and the inclusion of a substantial proportion of biologic-naïve patients, who typically exhibit more favorable treatment response [35]. These clinical characteristics likely contributed to the relatively favorable overall response rates observed in our cohort.

Concomitant background therapy may influence treatment response. In our cohort, methotrexate and glucocorticoids were used in more than 90% of patients as part of standard TNFi care. Methotrexate enhances TNFi efficacy and reduces immunogenicity [34], whereas glucocorticoids may transiently affect inflammatory markers [36]. However, neither was significantly associated with remission in univariate analyses, and adjustment for baseline covariates did not materially alter the genetic associations. Given the limited variability in exposure, concomitant therapy is unlikely to fully account for the observed genetic associations. Future study is needed to confirm our findings.

Our clinical findings confirmed established predictors of TNFi efficacy, including lower baseline inflammatory burden and biologic-naïve status [1]. Conversely, serologic markers such as RF and ACPA, and common comorbidities, were not independently associated with treatment outcomes—consistent with prior evidence that their predictive utility diminishes after adjustment for inflammatory burden [37].

Our results support the emerging model of gene–drug–disease interplay, whereby pharmacogenetic variants such as FTO rs7195994 may influence immune thresholds and modulate biologic response in a metabolism-dependent context [1]. These findings underscore the potential for the host immunometabolic environment to shape the clinical impact of genetic predictors of TNFi efficacy. While we did not perform formal gene-by-environment interaction testing, our BMI-stratified analyses revealed adverse effect direction in lean individuals, suggesting that immunometabolic factors such as adiposity may modify the functional relevance of pharmacogenetic variants in real-world settings [38].

This study has several strengths. It is based on a real-world cohort with comprehensive genotypic and phenotypic data, incorporates stratified analyses by BMI, and employs multivariate models to minimize confounding. First, this is a single-center study conducted in a relatively homogenous East Asian population, which may limit generalizability. Second, although SNPs were selected based on existing literature, functional validation and fine-mapping of causal variants were not performed. Third, BMI was used as a surrogate for metabolic status and may not fully capture adipose-related immunologic variation. Lastly, we lacked an independent validation cohort to confirm our findings.

In conclusion, we identify FTO rs7195994 as a BMI-stratified, clinically relevant genetic predictor of TNFi response in RA and provide exploratory evidence supporting the roles of ZNF618, RANK, and LOC105375523. These findings suggest that immunogenetics and metabolic profiling may be valuable tools for optimizing biologic treatment in RA. Further functional validation and replication in independent cohorts are warranted to substantiate these findings and clarify the biological mechanisms underlying variable TNFi response.
