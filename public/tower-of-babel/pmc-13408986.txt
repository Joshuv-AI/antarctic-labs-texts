Prevalence and predictors of immediate treatment need for anterior crossbite in primary dentition: a Baby ROMA study
Authors: Farah M. Babakurd, Khaled Omar, Mayssoon Dashash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of anterior crossbite among Syrian children in the primary dentition and to classify the severity of identified cases utilizing the Baby ROMA index. The research specifically evaluated the distribution of crossbite in relation to age, gender, and tooth type, as well as identifying predictors associated with the necessity for immediate orthodontic intervention. A cross-sectional study involving 2,700 children aged 3 to 5 years was conducted in public kindergartens in Damascus using multistage cluster sampling methods. Anterior crossbite was assessed clinically by a single calibrated examiner, with a reliability re-examination of 10% of cases yielding a Kappa value of 0.85 to 0.95. The affected cases were categorized into severity grades based on the Baby ROMA criteria. Analyses included descriptive statistics, Chi-square tests, McNemar tests, and multivariable binary logistic regression. The overall prevalence of anterior crossbite was determined to be 15.6%. In terms of severity classification, 61.4% (equating to 9.6% of the total sample) were classified as Grade 4N, indicating a requirement for immediate treatment, while 38.6% (6.0% of the total sample) were designated Grade 2N, suggesting monitoring only. The prevalence did not demonstrate any significant differences based on gender (p = 0.979); however, age significantly influenced the clinical presentation (p < 0.001). The maxillary lateral incisors were the most frequently involved teeth, and a significant difference was observed between sides (p < 0.001). Multivariable regression analysis identified the involvement of lateral incisors (OR = 21.1) and canines (OR = 10.85), along with left-side involvement (OR = 2.17), as the strongest predictors for classification into the immediate treatment group (Grade 4N); age was not found to be a significant predictor. The findings indicate a notable prevalence of anterior crossbite in the primary dentition, and the Baby ROMA index served as a practical framework for stratifying these cases by clinical urgency. These results underscore the importance of integrating dental and functional assessments to classify and prioritize high-urgency cases early, thereby preventing progression to more serious occlusal and skeletal complications.

Introduction

Child health and the safeguarding of children’s health rights represent fundamental priorities within the realm of global public health. In this context, the Syrian government has consistently endeavored to provide comprehensive, high-quality healthcare and to establish a robust epidemiological database to facilitate evidence-based health decision-making and policy formulation. These initiatives are designed to enhance prevention programs, improve early detection, and ensure timely treatment, ultimately reducing future societal health burdens1.

According to the World Health Organization, malocclusion constitutes a dentofacial anomaly characterized by abnormal occlusal or craniofacial relationships that can detrimentally impact function, aesthetics, and the quality of life for children2.

Malocclusion is recognized as a significant public health issue, ranking third in prevalence following dental caries and periodontal disease3.

Anterior crossbite is classified as a sagittal malocclusion wherein one or more maxillary anterior teeth are positioned lingual to the mandibular anterior teeth during centric or habitual occlusion. Beyond mere dental misalignment, anterior crossbite signifies a critical developmental concern, as it often entails functional shifts and neuromuscular adaptations capable of permanently influencing craniofacial growth and occlusal development4.

The prevalence of anterior crossbite is influenced by ethnicity and region, with a global mean prevalence of approximately 10% in the primary dentition. Reported rates are highest in Europe (7%) and Africa (5.6%). However, these statistics often represent a broad epidemiological spectrum, and data specifically concerning Middle Eastern populations, particularly Syria, are limited. This gap in the literature underscores the need for a more thorough understanding of the regional burden of this particular malocclusion during the early stages of dental development5.

The etiology of anterior crossbite is multifactorial, potentially encompassing premature anterior tooth contact, supernumerary teeth, trauma, prolonged retention of primary dentition, and genetic predispositions6.

The clinical justification for early intervention is underscored by the observation that this condition rarely self-corrects; rather, it tends to progress from a localized dental discrepancy to a more severe structural and skeletal Class III malocclusion6.

If left untreated, an anterior crossbite may lead to traumatic occlusion, accelerated dental wear, periodontal complications, and temporomandibular joint dysfunction, ultimately jeopardizing facial aesthetics and complicating future orthodontic treatment7.

A primary objective of pediatric dentistry is to address emerging occlusal issues during growth through simple, effective measures that enhance oral health and overall quality of life. Interceptive orthodontics entails early interventions aimed at correcting malocclusions while they are still developing8.

Evidence indicates that interceptive orthodontic treatment during the primary dentition can significantly diminish the necessity for comprehensive orthodontic care after the age of 129, with reported reductions in treatment needs ranging from 14 to 49% among treated children10.

To facilitate early decision-making, various indices have been established to assess the need for preventive and interceptive treatment in epidemiological studies11.

While the Occlusal Index (OI) was an earlier assessment tool12, the Baby ROMA (Risk of Malocclusion Assessment) index, introduced in 2014, offers a more contemporary and multidimensional approach, integrating dental, functional, and skeletal risk factors to assist in clinical prioritization13.

Consequently, the present study aims to estimate the prevalence and distribution of anterior crossbite in the primary dentition within an epidemiological framework and to identify predictors necessitating immediate orthodontic intervention through the utilization of the Baby ROMA index. This research will inform the prioritization of early orthodontic care and support evidence-based child health policy.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval for this research was secured from the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry at Damascus University, Syria (IRB No. UDDS 2614_28042025/SRC_2320). Furthermore, written consent was obtained from the Ministry of Education (No. 4/443, 02/07/2025). The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (2013)14. Before participation, written informed consent was procured from the parents or legal guardians of all participating children. The research was conducted from July 2025 to December 2025, and its reporting complies with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines15.

According to the Ministry of Education, a total of 9,948 children, comprising 5,048 boys and 4,900 girls, were enrolled in public kindergartens within Damascus City. The city has been divided into nine administrative regions, as classified by the Ministry of Health. For this study, four schools were selected from each region through a process of simple random sampling. All children aged between 3 and 5 years attending the chosen schools were invited to participate, which facilitated the implementation of a multistage cluster sampling methodology16.

Utilizing a 95% confidence level (Z) and a margin of error (D) of 2%, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 864 children. To account for the potential design effect associated with the multistage cluster sampling design—where children within the same institution may exhibit similar characteristics—a design effect was taken into consideration. This resulted in the initial sample size being multiplied by an approximate factor of 2, with a subsequent increase to further enhance the power for subgroup analyses. Ultimately, a total of 2,700 children were recruited for this study. This robust sample size ensures high precision and effectively compensates for the clustering effect observed at the school level18.

Participants were enrolled in the study following the provision of written informed consent from their parents. The inclusion criteria were restricted to children of Syrian descent aged between three and five years. Exclusion criteria encompassed uncooperative behavior, the presence of craniofacial anomalies, and ongoing orthodontic treatment.

All clinical assessments were conducted by a single, experienced, and calibrated examiner (FB) to minimize measurement bias. The examiner was assisted by two trained school health personnel, whose sole responsibility was to record data; they did not engage in any clinical assessments. To evaluate examiner reliability, ten percent of the participants were randomly re-examined after a brief interval of 20 to 30 min on the same day. This approach was necessitated by the large sample size and logistical constraints, which rendered re-examination on a different day impractical19. The Cohen’s Kappa values for categorical variables ranged from 0.85 to 0.95, indicating an excellent level of agreement20.

Examinations were performed in designated rooms at each school under standardized infection-control measures, which included the use of disposable gloves and face masks, as well as a head-mounted light to ensure adequate illumination. Each child was examined individually, with the presence of a teacher to facilitate cooperation during the procedure. Clinical measurements were carried out employing a stainless-steel Williams periodontal probe, characterized by a thin, non-traumatic tip and millimeter markings at 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, and 10 mm21. Furthermore, sterile cotton rolls and gauze were utilized during the examinations. Demographic and clinical data—including age, gender, tooth type involved, and affected side—were systematically recorded.

Anterior crossbite was clinically evaluated using the Baby ROMA (Risk of Malocclusion Assessment) index, a diagnostic tool specifically designed for the primary dentition that focuses on risk assessment. This index classifies malocclusions into scores that range from 1 to 5, indicating increasing levels of severity and treatment necessity. In the context of this study, anterior crossbite is operationally defined as the positioning of one or more maxillary anterior teeth in a lingual position relative to the mandibular anterior teeth during centric occlusion13.

To ensure a standardized assessment, the clinical standards of the American Board of Orthodontics (ABO) were employed alongside the Baby ROMA index. The objective measurement protocol involved assessing the negative overjet by measuring the distance from the labial surface of the maxillary teeth to the incisal edge of the mandibular teeth using a Williams probe22.

In instances where multiple teeth were affected, all relevant teeth were documented; however, the tooth with the most significant negative overjet was utilized for the classification of severity. This approach aligns with the epidemiological practices recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO, 2013)23.

In accordance with the Baby ROMA criteria about the 'N' suffix (Anterior Crossbite), cases were categorized as follows: Grade 2N (Monitoring), indicating a negative overjet of up to 2 mm without a functional lateral mandibular shift; and Grade 4N (Immediate Intervention), indicating a negative overjet exceeding 2 mm, or any degree of negative overjet accompanied by a functional lateral mandibular shift, defined as side-to-side mandibular movement upon closure due to dental interference. Other Baby ROMA grades (1, 3, and 5) pertain to different clinical conditions, such as sagittal or vertical discrepancies unrelated to anterior crossbite, and therefore were excluded from this study. This methodology ensures that the determination of treatment needs is predominantly based on the severity of sagittal discrepancies and functional alterations rather than the number of affected teeth, thereby providing a standardized and objective diagnostic framework13.

Data were input and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were employed to summarize the sample. The normality of age was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. The Chi-square test was utilized to examine associations between anterior crossbite and the variables of age and gender. The McNemar test was applied to compare involvement between paired symmetrical teeth. A multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with the requirement for immediate treatment, specifically comparing Baby ROMA grade 4 to grade 2. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05.

Results

During the primary dentition stage, a total of 2,700 children were examined, with a mean age of 4.19 years (± 0.69). Quantitative variables were analyzed using graphical methods, including histograms and Q–Q plots, supplemented by normality testing. A visual assessment, characterized by a near-linear pattern in the Q–Q plot and a skewness of − 0.27, indicated that the data could be treated as approximately normally distributed.

In terms of prevalence, anterior crossbite was identified in 422 children, yielding an overall prevalence of 15.6%. Following the identification of these cases, the Baby ROMA index was employed to classify clinical severity and prioritize treatment needs. Among the affected children, 61.4% (n = 259) required immediate treatment (Grade 4N), whereas 38.6% (n = 163) were deemed suitable for routine occlusion monitoring (Grade 2N) (Table 1). Discussion

Orthodontic treatment-need indices serve as crucial diagnostic instruments for evaluating malocclusion and directing appropriate management strategies. Nevertheless, many of these indices were designed primarily for the permanent dentition, which limits their applicability to the primary dentition24.

This limitation is particularly significant, as the primary dentition plays a critical role in shaping subsequent craniofacial development. The process of occlusion during early childhood is dynamic; therefore, any delay in diagnosis or intervention may lead to the emergence of more complex structural and functional issues in later stages of development25.

In response to these challenges, this study employed the Baby ROMA index, which is specifically designed for the primary dentition and incorporates dental, skeletal, and functional components into a unified classification system. The clinical utility and high reproducibility of this index were initially established by Grippaudo et al. (2014), who reported inter-examiner reliability K values ranging from 0.77 to 1.00. It is essential to distinguish between the epidemiological estimation of prevalence and the subsequent clinical classification of severity as articulated through this index13.

Regarding the prevalence of anterior crossbite, our findings, which indicate a rate of 15.6%, are positioned toward the upper end of the spectrum reported internationally. Comparisons with previous studies illustrate significant variability; reported prevalences among preschool populations vary from 1.9% to 8% in countries such as China26,27, Brazil28, and Portugal29.

Within the regional context of the Middle East, our findings present a notably higher prevalence than that reported in Lebanon (3%)30 and Iran (8%)31. This regional discrepancy likely reflects a lack of targeted epidemiological data in the Middle East, suggesting that the condition may have been underestimated in earlier regional studies, which were hindered by the absence of sensitive functional assessment tools such as the Baby ROMA index.

Upon identification of the condition, emphasis was placed on the classification of severity and the prioritization of treatment. In our study, 61.4% of the identified cases (representing 9.6% of the total sample) were categorized as Grade 4N, indicating a need for immediate treatment, while 38.6% (equivalent to 6.0% of the total sample) were classified as Grade 2N, necessitating observation and monitoring.

A targeted comparison with the foundational research conducted by Grippaudo et al. reveals a remarkable consistency in the prevalence of severe functional cases (Grade 4), which stands at 9.6% compared to 11% in their study. However, a divergence is evident in the prevalence of milder cases (Grade 2: 6.0% versus 24.5%)13.

This discrepancy underscores the effectiveness of the Baby ROMA index in identifying high-priority clinical needs across diverse populations, while the reduced frequency of mild cases in our study can be attributed to the school-based screening approach as opposed to the referral-based framework utilized in the Italian cohort13. This distribution highlights a significant clinical burden, with over half of the identified cases surpassing transient disturbances, thereby necessitating immediate intervention.

Gender was not significantly associated with anterior crossbite (χ2 = 0.001, p = 0.979), indicating a comparable prevalence among boys and girls during early childhood. This observation aligns with systematic reviews that report no significant gender differences in preschool malocclusion (RR ≈ 1.01; 95% CI: 0.96–1.06)32, considering that gender-related growth differences are minimal at this developmental stage. It is essential to differentiate between the occurrence of the condition (prevalence) and its clinical severity (treatment urgency). While gender does not influence the development of a crossbite, multivariable regression analysis shows that it does play a role in its clinical expression; specifically, males are less likely to require immediate treatment (Grade 4N) compared to females (p = 0.030). This finding suggests that gender serves as an independent predictor of severity rather than a determinant of prevalence, a distinction that becomes evident only after adjusting for confounding clinical factors.

Moreover, the study demonstrated a significant association with crossbite (χ2 = 51.875, p < 0.001), with the highest prevalence observed at age three (25.8%). It is important to note that in large sample sizes (N = 2,700), even minor differences can attain statistical significance without necessarily indicating a substantial clinical impact. The small effect size (Cramer’s V = 0.139) confirms that while age is statistically related to the occurrence of crossbite, it does not serve as a robust clinical determinant. These findings reflect the dynamic nature of early occlusion, whereby minor discrepancies at age three may either persist or evolve, while their clinical severity (Baby ROMA grade) remains largely consistent across the studied age groups.

A similar pattern was identified in a cross-sectional study conducted in Lucknow, which reported significantly higher rates of malocclusion at age three (80%, p < 0.05)33.

Further analysis of the distribution of Baby ROMA grades across age groups revealed a consistent trend. The proportions of Grade 4 (indicating immediate intervention) and Grade 2 (requiring monitoring) cases were similar among children aged three, four, and five years. This observation indicates that the higher prevalence of anterior crossbite observed in three-year-old children is not attributable to a higher proportion of milder cases, but instead reflects an overall increase in occurrence at that age.

With respect to tooth distribution, maxillary lateral incisors were identified as the most frequently affected, exhibiting a significant difference between sides (χ2 = 22.806, p < 0.001). In contrast, central incisors and canines displayed no such discrepancy. Although existing data on primary dentition are scarce, this pattern is consistent with observations in permanent dentition, where lateral incisors exhibit higher susceptibility to eruption disturbances, early crowding, and asymmetric occlusal interferences. This trend may render lateral incisors more vulnerable to localized positional challenges compared to central incisors or canines34,35.

A significant association was also established between the affected side and crossbite patterns (Cramer’s V = 0.646, Phi = 0.646, p < 0.001), indicating a predominantly unilateral involvement. This finding emphasizes the influence of early functional factors, such as occlusal shifts and oral habits, in the development of crossbite patterns within primary dentition36.

The multivariable binary logistic regression model, which predicted higher Baby ROMA scores (Grade 4 versus Grade 2), provided valuable insights by uncovering independent predictors that were not evident in the bivariate analysis. While gender initially appeared to show no crude association with prevalence, it emerged as a significant independent variable in the regression model (p = 0.030) after controlling for clinical covariates, including tooth type and side. This alteration in statistical significance suggests that the impact of gender on treatment urgency becomes discernible only when other confounding clinical factors are accounted for. Specifically, males exhibited a lower likelihood of necessitating immediate treatment in comparison to females, which may reflect variations in clinical presentation or referral practices. This finding underscores the importance of employing multivariable modeling to reveal hidden independent effects that simple frequency distributions may overlook.

Furthermore, in the same model, age was determined to be a non-significant predictor of immediate treatment needs, thereby reinforcing the clinical viewpoint that the severity and type of malocclusion—rather than chronological age—serve as the primary determinants in clinical decision-making.

Tooth type exhibited the strongest influence: the involvement of lateral incisors increased the odds of immediate intervention approximately 21-fold, while the involvement of canines increased the odds by approximately 10.8-fold. The significant odds ratio for lateral incisors reflects their substantial clinical impact, although the wide confidence interval (95% CI: 4.95–90.31) is attributed to the epidemiological distribution of cases; nonetheless, the lower bound affirms a robust and significant association.

The correlation between left-sided crossbites and higher odds of intervention, along with the lack of statistical significance for bilateral involvement, highlights the role of functional asymmetry and early occlusal shifts.

In summary, these findings underscore the importance of a comprehensive assessment utilizing the Baby ROMA index—integrating dental, functional, and demographic factors—to accurately identify cases necessitating timely orthodontic intervention and to mitigate the progression of structural and occlusal abnormalities.

Despite the scientific and clinical significance of these findings, several limitations merit acknowledgment. First, the cross-sectional design of this study precludes definitive causal inferences regarding the development of anterior crossbite. Additionally, there was a lack of comprehensive data on functional or behavioral determinants—such as oral habits, breathing patterns, and genetic factors—that may play a considerable role in the etiology of malocclusion. Furthermore, it is important to note that the study exclusively focused on public kindergartens, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to children in private schools who may differ in socioeconomic status and access to early dental care.

In terms of the assessment process, while intra-examiner reliability was found to be excellent, inter-examiner reliability could not be evaluated as the trained school health personnel were responsible solely for data recording and did not conduct clinical assessments. Moreover, due to the large sample size and logistical constraints, 10% of participants were randomly re-examined on the same day after a short interval (20–30 min). Although this procedure was necessary, given the scale of the study, it may have slightly inflated the agreement measures.

From a statistical and epidemiological perspective, two specific issues warrant caution. One limitation concerns the wide confidence interval (CI) observed for the odds ratio of lateral incisors. This phenomenon primarily arises from the epidemiological distribution of anterior crossbite, wherein cases are disproportionately concentrated in lateral incisors compared to central incisors. While this sparse data in the reference group affects the precision of the point estimate (OR = 21.1), the lower bound of the CI (4.95) and the strong effect size (Cramer’s V = 0.646) consistently corroborate the clinical relevance of the results. Finally, it should be noted that the statistical analysis did not formally account for the clustering effect at the school level during the estimation of standard errors. Although this could lead to more optimistic p-values, the risk was mitigated by utilizing a large sample size (N = 2,700), which substantially exceeded the minimum requirement, thereby enhancing the robustness and precision of our clinical estimates.

Nonetheless, the substantial sample size, the employment of a dentition-specific diagnostic index, and the rigorous statistical methodology all reinforce the validity of this study and underscore the necessity for longitudinal research on the progression of crossbite and the outcomes of early treatment.

Conclusion

Anterior crossbite is a common condition observed in primary dentition; however, its clinical management necessitates a clear differentiation between the identification of the condition and the assessment of its severity. This study employed the Baby ROMA index to facilitate the clinical categorization of affected cases, distinguishing those that require monitoring from those that necessitate immediate intervention by integrating dental, functional, and skeletal criteria. The findings indicate that, although prevalence serves as a significant epidemiological indicator, the priority for clinical intervention is primarily dictated by the severity of the malocclusion and its dental and lateral distribution, rather than by chronological age. These results underscore the importance of utilizing standardized criteria for classifying cases based on clinical urgency, thereby optimizing intervention timing and mitigating the progression of skeletal abnormalities.
