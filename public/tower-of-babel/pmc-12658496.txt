Trajectories and predictors of spherical equivalent among multiethnic school-aged children in Southwest China: a 2.5-year cohort study
Authors: Qiang Zhang, Litao Chang, Jie Xiao, Dafeng Huang, Xue-Ni Xie, Jinjiao Zhang, Zixue Ma, Xixi Li, Xiao Luo, Peiqian Li, Xinge Li, Ying Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThe development of myopia is subject to individual genetic predisposition and environmental risk exposures. This study aims to investigate the trajectories and predictors of spherical equivalent (SE) among multiethnic school-aged children in Southwest China.MethodsThe school-based cohort study was conducted from October 2020 to March 2023 in Yunnan province, Southwest China. Ocular examinations and questionnaire surveys were administered at each visit. A total of 679 students with complete records from all three visits were included in the final analyses. Group-based trajectory modelling was used to identify SE trajectories. Associations between the trajectory groups and baseline predictors were assessed using multinomial logistic regression.ResultsWe identified two distinct trajectory groups of SE in grades 2–3: slow progressive (84.4% of students) and rapid progressive (15.6%). Multivariate analysis indicated that children with myopic parents and those who often stayed in the classrooms during recess were more likely to belong to the rapid progressive group. By contrast, three trajectory groups of SE were identified in grade 7: slow progressive (52.3% of students), moderate initial and rapid progressive (33%) and low initial and rapid progressive (14.7%). In multivariate models, being a girl, having myopic parents, using mobile screens for more than 0.5 hours per day and often staying in the classrooms during recess were predictors of the progressive groups. Moreover, there were no significant differences in SE trajectories between Han and ethnic groups.ConclusionThere is heterogeneity in the developmental trajectories of SE, for both primary and secondary school students. Tailored intervention strategies based on the predictors of the SE trajectories should be under special consideration.Keywords: Myopia, China, Child

Introduction

Myopia has emerged as a major public health issue worldwide, particularly in East and Southeast Asia.1 2 In urban China alone, the economic burden associated with myopia is estimated to reach as high as US$26.3 billion annually.3 During childhood and adolescence, the visual system regulates refractive status within a normal range by compensating for changes in axial length and refractive components.4 Refractive error occurs when the regulatory mechanism is disrupted and is commonly quantified using spherical equivalent (SE), measured in diopters (D).5 6

Both genetic and environmental factors contribute to the onset and progression of myopia.7 8 Studies conducted in various countries have indicated that SE tends to progress more rapidly in children with myopic parents.9 10 Moreover, insufficient time spent outdoors and excessive screen use have been identified as key modifiable risk factors, particularly among school-aged children in China.11 12 Ethnic disparities have also emerged as an important consideration in myopia control and prevention.13 14 Due to variations in genetic backgrounds and eye usage habits, the progression rates of SE are inevitably inconsistent among students. For example, a 6-year longitudinal study of children in Singapore showed that about 18% of subjects exhibited progressive myopia, with a mean SE progression rate of less than −0.75 D per year over 3 years. And the incidence of high myopia in the progressive group was two times that of the non-progressive group.15 However, most current studies tend to focus on the mean changes of SE rather than the heterogeneity of SE changes within a population.16 This approach may limit the ability to identify children at high risk of myopia, thereby hindering the implementation of differentiated and targeted intervention measures.

In recent years, group-based trajectory modelling (GBTM) has been increasingly used to explore distinctive patterns of various health risks and outcomes in longitudinal data sets, such as screen use, childhood adversity and dementia.17,19 Unlike traditional static clustering methods, GBTM has the advantage of modelling the dynamic changes of indicators over time.20 21 This data-driven approach allows the most naturally occurring trajectories to emerge from the data rather than relying on predetermined trajectories, making it a suitable method for classifying the progression of SE. Currently, there is very limited research on SE trajectories based on GBTM. Last year, a study conducted in Hunan province, Central China, identified five distinct trajectories of SE among first-grade children without myopia during grades 1–4, which were associated with baseline characteristics such as parental myopia and the duration of outdoor activities.22 Currently, there is no research indicating the specific developmental trajectories of SE among middle school students who exhibit a higher prevalence of myopia and a more rapid decline in SE.23

Yunnan is a multiethnic province located in Southwest China, characterised by its unique geographical environment, cultural practices and lifestyles.24 As a border region, students in Yunnan province experienced increased home confinement and online learning between 2020 and 2023, which may have influenced the onset and progression of myopia.25 26 The purpose of this study was to investigate the SE trajectories and predictors among both primary and secondary school students during this period.

Methods

Study design, setting and participants

Data for this study were derived from the Yunnan Multiethnic Children Myopia Study, which is a school-based, prospective cohort study designed to track the myopia epidemic and associated factors. In August 2020, one primary school and one secondary school were selected from Dali and Lijiang, respectively, using a random clustering sampling method. To ensure that participants could fully understand the questionnaire and be followed up over the next years, only students in grades 2–3 and grade 7 were included. The sample size was determined through a careful power analysis that took into account the following factors: an annual SE progression rate of 0.35 D, a SD of 0.4 D, an allowable error of 5%, a type I error probability of 0.05, and a design effect of 1.5.23 27 Further assuming an attrition of 15%, the minimum sample size is 773.

All eligible students were invited to participate in the study, with the exception of those who declined, had eye diseases affecting their vision or had previously undergone refractive surgery. At the baseline survey conducted in October 2020, a total of 777 students from the four schools were enrolled in the study. The first and second follow-ups were conducted in October 2021 and March 2023, respectively. Over the course of 2.5 years, 98 students were lost to follow-up due to school transfers or absences from the survey as a result of illness, leading to a follow-up rate of 87.4%. There were no significant differences in the baseline characteristics between the follow-up group and those lost to follow-up (online supplemental figure S1 anf table S1).

Ocular examination

Ocular examinations were conducted by trained ophthalmologists in accordance with the national specifications at each visit.28 Cycloplegia was induced by instilling three drops of Mydrin-P, an eye drop containing 0.5% tropicamide and 0.5% phenylephrine (Santen, Japan) within a 10 min period. After a 30 min interval, the refractive status was measured using a desktop autorefractor (NIDEKAR-1, Japan). The SE was calculated as the sum of the sphere and half of the cylinder. Each measurement was performed three times, and the mean value was calculated for each eye.

Questionnaire survey

A self-administered questionnaire was completed by students and their guardians at baseline. This questionnaire was developed in accordance with the recommendations of the Chinese National Health Commission,29 collecting data on students’ basic information and myopia-related behaviours, including class recess, mobile screen use, reading and writing distances, dietary habits over the past year and parental myopic status. Class recess was categorised based on whether students typically stayed in the classroom or went outdoors. Mobile screen use was classified as either ≤0.5 hours or >0.5 hours per day. Reading and writing distances were evaluated by asking, ‘do you maintain a working distance of more than one Chi (33 cm)?’. Responses were then classified as either seldom or often. Dietary habits were assessed by asking, ‘do you pay attention to daily dietary intakes?’. Responses were similarly categorised as seldom or often. Parental myopia was defined as having at least one biological parent with myopia.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Statistical analysis

Altogether, 679 students with all three measurements of SE were included in the final analysis. Since SE in the right and left eyes were highly correlated (r=0.91–0.93, all p<0.05), we used data of the right eye for analysis (online supplemental table S2). The SE trajectories of primary and secondary school students were identified through the Stata traj package using the censored normal model, respectively. The optimal number of trajectory groups was determined based on the following criteria: (1) a lower Bayesian Information Criterion, (2) an average posterior probability greater than 0.8 and (3) the inclusion of at least 5% of the sample size (online supplemental table S3).

Continuous variables were presented as means±SD, while categorical variables were expressed as percentages. Group differences in the data were assessed using the t-test or one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with the Bonferroni correction for continuous variables, and the Pearson χ² test for categorical variables. Multinomial logistic regression was used to evaluate the associations between myopia-related factors and the trajectories of SE. Age, gender, race, parental myopia and the four environmental factors were included in the multivariate models. All analyses were conducted using Stata V.17.0 (StataCorp, USA), and a two-sided p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Table 1 outlines the demographic characteristics of the subjects at baseline. Among the 679 children included in the analysis, 292 were in grades 2–3, while 387 were in grade 7. Approximately half of the sample consisted of boys. The ethnic composition included 37.4% Han, 29.9% Naxi, 24.0% Bai and the remaining 8.7% comprised other ethnic groups. Additionally, 45.1% of the children had myopic parents.

SE trajectories between October 2020 and March 2023

Two distinct SE trajectory groups were identified in grades 2–3 (figure 1a), while three groups were observed in grade 7 (figure 1b). All groups experienced a decline in SE from October 2020 to March 2023. In grades 2–3, 84.4% of students were categorised into the slow progressive group, characterised by a high initial SE level and a low rate of progression. In contrast, 15.6% of students were classified as the rapid progressive group, which exhibited a low initial SE level accompanied by a high rate of progression. In grade 7, 52.3% of students were identified as the slow progressive group, also marked by a high initial SE and a low rate of progression. An additional 33% of students were classified as the moderate initial and rapid progressive group, characterised by a moderate initial SE level and a high rate of progression, while the remaining 14.7% were categorised as the low initial and rapid progressive group, characterised by a low initial SE and a high rate of progression.

Discussion

Using GBTM, we identified two distinct trajectories of SE among grade 2–3 students in Southwest China from October 2020 to March 2023. Specifically, 84.4% of the students experienced a slow decline in SE, while 15.6% exhibited a rapid progression. This result is consistent with prior research conducted by Li et al in Central China, suggesting that even among lower-grade students with either no or minimal myopia prevalence at baseline, a subset may have marked SE decline over subsequent years.22 These findings underscore the critical need for early intervention strategies aiming at delaying the onset and progression of myopia in school-aged children.30 31 Moreover, our study explored the trajectories of SE in grade 7 students for the first time. Compared with primary school students, the proportion of students with rapid SE progression is substantially higher among secondary school students (47.7% vs 15.6%). This disparity may be attributed to the increased academic stress and puberty onset among secondary school students.32 33 Regardless of school stage, students with a lower baseline SE are more likely to experience faster SE progression during the follow-up period. At present, myopia control and prevention for Chinese students are typically uniform, which may overlook high-risk subgroups.29 Thus, individualised or stratified interventions are urgently needed to address the heterogeneity of SE development among school-aged children.

Parental myopia has been well-documented as a major risk factor for childhood myopia.34,36 Our findings provide further insight into the association by demonstrating that children with myopic parents were approximately 2–4 times more likely to be classified into the progressive trajectory groups. Families share not only genes but also environmental and behavioural habits.37 For instance, a contemporaneous study of 1355 parent–child dyads demonstrated positive correlations between the levels of physical activity and screen time of parents and those of their children.38 Moreover, sex is another significant factor influencing the onset and progression of myopia. Large-scale cohort studies conducted in China show that girls tend to exhibit lower mean SE values than boys beginning at around age nine.23 39 In our study, although the proportion of girls in the progressive groups was higher, the difference was not statistically significant, potentially due to the limited sample size. Consistent with previous research on myopia prevalence among multiethnic groups in Yunnan province, our study found no significant differences in SE trajectories between Han and ethnic students (mainly Bai and Naxi).40 41 These findings imply that, as lifestyles and academic pressures became increasingly homogenised, ethnicity may no longer be a key determinant for myopia risk among school-aged children. Therefore, the influence of parental myopia status and sex on SE trajectories should be fully considered when designing myopia prevention and control strategies.

In addition to non-modifiable factors, our study also assessed key environmental risk factors on SE trajectories. We discovered that students who typically remained indoors during class recess were nearly two times as likely to belong to the progressive groups compared with those who regularly engaged in outdoor activities. This supports earlier studies suggesting that outdoor activity during recess may be more effective in preventing myopia than that after school.42,44 Substantial evidence from human and animal studies indicates that bright light stimulates the retina to release dopamine, which inhibits axial elongation.45,47 Notably, outdoor light intensity peaks between 10:00 and 14:00—hours that basically overlap with school sessions. Furthermore, increased mobile screen use is also a significant factor for rapid SE progression among grade 7 students. A recent review pointed out that although pandemic-related measures like online learning were temporary, the increased use of digital screen may foster long-term behavioural dependencies detrimental to eyesight.48 These findings highlight the necessity of implementing school-based outdoor recess policies and enforcing stricter regulations on mobile screen usage at home. Consistent with the literature, our study did not find significant associations between SE trajectories and either reading/writing distance or dietary habits.49 50 Nevertheless, nearly half of the participants reported seldom maintaining appropriate reading/writing posture or paying attention to dietary intake. Given the importance of these factors in children’s overall development, increased awareness and intervention from both schools and parents are warranted.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size and short follow-up period may limit our ability to fully capture the developmental trajectories of SE in school-aged children. Second, the predictors used in this study were derived from the baseline survey, which could not fully represent the myopia risks throughout the study period. Third, while our analysis incorporated major myopia-related factors identified in the existing literature, it did not account for some other variables, such as classroom and home lighting conditions. Last but not least, the participants were drawn exclusively from four schools in Dali and Lijiang, which necessitates caution in generalising the findings to other regions and populations. Despite these limitations, our study is among the first to describe SE trajectories and predictors among school-aged children in China. Future research with a larger sample size and extended follow-up period is essential to validate and expand on our findings.

Conclusion

This cohort study reveals significant heterogeneity in SE developmental trajectories among multiethnic primary and secondary school students in Southwest China. compared with primary school students, secondary school students exhibit a markedly higher proportion of rapid SE progression. Students with lower baseline SE are more prone to experiencing a rapid decline in SE during the follow-up period. Parental myopia, remaining in classrooms during recess and increased use of mobile screens are risk factors for rapid SE progression. These findings may contribute to a deeper understanding of the dynamic changes in SE in school-aged children and facilitate the development of targeted intervention measures.
