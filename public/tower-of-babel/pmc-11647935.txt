Role of radiation emergency medicine: historical view—a perspective on the past, present, and future
Authors: Makoto Akashi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe more science progresses, the more life and society change. Medicine also changes with the times and the culture. This is also true for radiation emergency medicine, which includes dose-assessment leading to diagnosis, treatment, medical follow-up and prognosis of persons who have developed acute injury or illness due to radioactive contamination or radiation exposure. Before the report of X-rays by Roentgen, there was evidence that X-rays had been emitted from the electrically excited Crookes tube and that skin injury had been caused by the X-rays. Thus, the history of radiation and its exposure started before Roentgen. During the early stage of radiation use, people were simply exposed to radiation but were unaware of any danger. Radioactive materials were found soon after Roentgen’s report, and contamination with these materials occurred. Together with the development of science and technology, sophisticated radiation devices were produced, and the use and application of radiation became much enhanced. New radionuclides were found one after another, leading to identification of different qualities of radiation. Development of nuclear physics allowed people to artificially produce radionuclides and to construct a nuclear reactor. After World War II, nuclear power plants were constructed, and related facilities such as nuclear fuel processing, reprocessing and spent fuel storage facilities were built. If radiation accidents or events occur at such facilities, radiation exposure with thermal or chemical burns could occur. Together with the expansion of globalism in the world and division in the society, there are now increasing concerns regarding the malicious usage of radiation by radiological dispersal devices (RDDs) including a dirty bomb. Upon detonation of RDDs, blast and thermal injuries with radiation exposure could be caused. In the present society, the natures of exposure to radiation and contamination with radioactive materials have become much more complicated. Not even mentioning the atomic bomb, the detonation of RDDs also necessitates scenarios of medical responses to complicated injuries and the involvement of numbers of people. This article looks back at the history of radiation and addresses the medical responses to radiation injuries that change with the times.Keywords: radiation emergency medicine, exposure, radiation combined injuries, medical responses, malicious use

INTRODUCTION

Nuclear or radiological events occur only rarely but regularly. After the dawn of radiation, its detrimental effects were soon observed. This was the beginning of radiation emergency medicine, one of the medical fields. At the same time, the detrimental effects of radiation led to the potential therapeutic value of radiation as well as the origin of its malicious use. At present, radiation is widely used in various fields, and radiation sources can be found in hospitals, factories and institutes, since radiation improves people's daily lives.

In 1939, nuclear fission was discovered and artificially-produced radioactive nuclides became available for medicine and its research by the end of the year [1–4] (Table 1). After World War II (WWII), the first reactor became operational in Idaho in 1951; electricity was first generated by a nuclear reactor [5]. In 1954, the first nuclear power plant (NPP) was constructed at the Obninskoye village of the Kaluga region near Moscow in the former Soviet Union, and it started to provide electricity to residences and businesses [6]. Since then, many NPPs and other facilities related to nuclear power have been constructed and sophisticated devices containing radiation sources have been produced. If accidents occur at these nuclear facilities or radiation sources are used maliciously, exposure to radiation and/or contamination with radioactive material may be complicated by thermal, chemical, physical or pressure injuries.

Discoveries of radiation and its application

It is well known that Roentgen reported X-rays to the Physical Institute of the University of Würzburg, Germany, on 8 November 1895 [7]. In the late 19th century, many scientists had been using a Crookes tube for their experiments; they did not notice that X-rays were emitted from the electrically excited tube until Roentgen reported X-rays, suggesting that people had been exposed to X-rays while not being aware of their existence [8, 9]. There is evidence that a Crookes tube emitted X-rays prior to the Roentgen’s report [10, 11]. In February 1890, Arthur Goodspeed and William Jennings were performing experiments using Crookes tubes with coins on photographic plates at the University of Pennsylvania. After the plates were developed, they noted the shadows of disks on the plates but could not explain these images. Upon hearing of Roentgen's work in 1896, they noticed that the photograph of coins was created by X-rays. At an early stage of X-rays, radiographs were taken at low voltage, and high-energy X-rays with a high dose rate could not be generated. Low-dose radiation does not result in signs or symptoms even if a whole body has been exposed. Therefore, signs or symptoms rarely developed unless the exposure was prolonged or repeated. Even if symptoms did appear, people did not realize that they were caused by X-rays.

The key finding of Roentgen’s discovery was that X-rays could penetrate most solid objects, but not bone or metal, and he produced the first human radiograph, that of his wife’s hand [12]. The discovery of X-rays was of great interest to Heinrich Hermann Robert Koch, who had found a species of pathogenic bacteria causing tuberculosis, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and many pictures of these patients by X-rays were published as early as April 1896, in America [13]. Information on X-rays spread into society surprisingly quickly, and the use of radiation and the production of radiographs increased in Europe and America [4]. Thus, people produced more X-ray devices for medicine and industry. Still, there was not sufficient knowledge of radiation effects for the prevention of exposure to over-doses of X-rays. Such knowledge, if any, was limited to certain scientists.

On 2 March 1896, 4 months after Roentgen’s discovery, Henri Becquerel reported at the Academy of Sciences that uranium salts emitted radiation without any stimulation from sunlight; he had found a natural radioactive material [14]. In December 1898, Pierre and Marie Curie discovered radium; they isolated radium from uranium ore [15]. These discoveries of radioactive materials were significant in terms of medicine for radiation exposure. Unlike X-rays from the electrically exited Cookes tube, radioactive materials spontaneously emit radiation and can also cause the other type of radiation exposure, contamination. On the other hand, discoveries of these radionuclides led to those of new rays. Since their discoveries were not noticed in comparison to Roentgen’s, most of the detrimental effects of radiation developed in people who were externally exposed to X-rays.

Detrimental effects of radiation on the human body

Detrimental effects caused by X-rays were also observed before the actual discovery of X-rays. Grubbé had observed pain, erythema, hyperemia and hyperesthesia on the back of his left hand almost a month after his experiments; he touched the exited Crookes tubes during most of his experiments before Roentgen’s report [16]. In January 1896, only 23 days after the report, Grubbé irradiated a woman with breast cancer [17]. This was the first case of the application of X-rays for a therapeutic purpose. In March 1896, just 5 months after the discovery by Roentgen, John Daniels reported epilation induced by X-rays [12, 18]. Dr Charles Dudley, Dean of the Medical School at Vanderbilt University, received X-rays on the head about one-half inch distance from a tube for 1 hour. He developed epilation on the exposed field 21 days later without any pain or skin injury. Also in 1896, Elihu Thompson showed that his left index finger exposed to an X-ray tube for half an hour a day for several days had developed erythema and swelling [19]. Clarence Dally, Thomas Edison’s assistant, was repeatedly exposed to X-rays and developed severe local burns. He died of a tumor as a result of external exposure to X-rays in 1904 [20]. Thus, within a decade from the report by Roentgen, acute and long-term detrimental effects of external exposure to X-rays were revealed.

Even Marie and Pierre Curie were unknowingly exposed to radiation and/or contaminated with radioactive materials during the isolation of 1 g of pure radium; they contaminated their food and clothes with radium and inhaled radon, the gaseous by-product of decaying uranium and radium [21]. Furthermore, in 1901, Pierre Curie intentionally exposed his forearm to radium for a several hours and showed that radiation emitted from radium caused burns [22]. However, a few decades passed before any concern regarding contamination was taken seriously within the scientific community, although there were many reports of skin burns and hair loss caused by radiation as early as the 1890s. In the early 1900s, workers, mainly young women who had been employed as dial painters for watches developed anemia, necrosis of the jaw and osteosarcomas; they were externally exposed to radiation and ingested radium during their work using radio-luminescent paint made by combining radium with luminous zinc sulfide containing copper [23, 24]. When radioactive materials enter the body via inhalation or ingestion, skin, or wounds or burns, they deposit on tissues and organs depending on their affinities to stable ones. Tissues and organs contaminated with these materials are continuously exposed to radiation in the body if not removed. This is recognized as internal exposure or contamination. When the skin or body surface is contaminated, which is called external contamination, the local radiation exposure occurs in a similar manner to external exposure. When the radium is ingested, ~30% of the radium is absorbed, and 95–98% is eliminated in feces and 2–5% in urine [25]. Thus, most of that absorbed is excreted within a few days after ingestion, and the radium remaining in the body acts like calcium and is almost entirely deposited in the skeleton, suggesting that these workers might have ingested radium repeatedly, resulting in significant deposition. By 1920, the number of dial painters had decreased substantially due to the end of World War I (WWI).

It is not surprising that many people in society were exposed to radiation regardless of intention, since they believed that radiation, like light that could not be seen or felt, was harmless [26]. Thus, the history of the detrimental effects of radiation exposure started almost in parallel with that of radiation. The concept of radiation protection was not developed until the Manhattan Project in the early 20th century [27].

Histories of malicious use of radiation and biological agents

Malicious use of radiation and biological agents such as bacteria, fungi or viruses is frequently compared. Neither exposure to radiation nor infection with biological agents induces immediate signs or symptoms. In contrast, radioactive materials never grow in the body although biological agents do. The history of the malicious use of biological agents is not clear, as reports on such events are difficult to obtain, and especially before modern microbiology [28]. However, there are reports that bioterrorism had already started 14 centuries before Christ, when the Hittites sent infected rams to their enemies [28, 29]. Furthermore, there are some descriptions of biological warfare as early as in Greek and Roman times, although they are difficult to confirm [30]. Since then, biological agents involved in terrorism or crimes have been reported [31]. Thus, war and intentional infection with biological agents have always been closely linked. After WWII, the malicious use of the biological agent Anthrax has been reported in Japan and USA [29, 32].

The history of the malicious use of radiation also has yet to be clarified. A Swedish chemist, Alfred Bernhard Nobel, invented the explosive dynamite, which is a combination of nitroglycerin and diatomaceous earth shaped into cylinders, in 1866 [33, 34]. In this form of dynamite, the explosive could be transported securely and detonated consistently. Almost three decades after the invention of dynamite, reports of X-rays and radioactive material appeared, and exposure to radiation was shown to cause detrimental effects on the body. It was no wonder that people could subscribe to the idea of the malicious use of radiation at that time. In 1903, Pierre and Marie Curie were awarded the Nobel Prize for physics for their study of many substances and minerals for signs of radioactivity, together with Becquerel [35, 36]. In the Nobel Lecture, Pierre Curie indicated the possible use of radiation in criminal hands by mentioning the dynamite produced by Nobel, suggesting that there was already concern about the malicious use of radiation especially in war almost 120 years ago, 10 years after the great discoveries [37]. Fortunately, to our knowledge, no malicious use of radiation was reported at the early time of its usage. In 2006, however, a man died in London, UK, and radiological investigation revealed that he had ingested a few GBq of 210Po, which may likely have been the first provable act of radiological terrorism [38]. The history of malicious use of radioactive materials for internal exposure still remains uncertain. The reason is probably because there are few scientific reports, and some of them were reported only in the mass media [39].

Events of exposure to radiation

Incidents of stolen, lost or orphaned radioactive devices have occurred regularly in recent times, although not at a high frequency. The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) hosts an online repository of the International Nuclear and Radiological Event Scale (INES) rating reports called Nuclear Events Web-based System [40]. This system is co-sponsored by the Nuclear Energy Agency of the Organization of Economic Cooperation Development and the World Association of Nuclear Operators. INES is a scale used for the rating of events that result in a release of radioactive material into the environment and in the radiation exposure of workers and the public. This site also provides recent information of accidents including over-exposure to radiation, missing or stolen sources, or release of radioactive materials into the environment reported from member states of IAEA [41, 42]. In the incident and trafficking database (ITDB), IAEA also showed that almost 11 (mean, 2–20) incidents related to trafficking or malicious use were confirmed each year between 1993 and 2019 [43]. In Texas, 113 sources were reported as stolen over the 45-year period between 1956 and 2000 [44].

Radiation sources as metal

Each radionuclide has its own half-life and emits different qualities of radiation, and its health effects on the body also vary depending on its activity. Radiation sources have different chemical forms such as metal, ceramic, salts, gas or in solution like other materials. An explosive radiological dispersal device or dirty bomb (RDD) is a mixture of explosives, such as dynamite, with radioactive powder or metal pellets; when the dynamite or other explosives are set off, the blast carries radioactive material into the surrounding area [45]. The RDD is considered one of the easiest forms of radiological terrorism [46]. Sources of 60Co and 192Ir emitting γ-rays are frequently used in various fields such as industry and medicine, and they are considered to be easily used for malicious purposes based on their availability from commercial sources, as well as for safeguards [47]. Cobalt is a relatively rare magnetic element with properties similar to iron and nickel and is stable at room temperature; most of the production of cobalt involves the metallic form used in the formation of cobalt superalloys [48]. Iridium is the most corrosion-resistant metal known, and is stable across a wide range or conditions such as with alkaline or acidic media, and temperature, which are directly related to its applications [49]. When the radiation source such as 60Co and 192Ir is contained in a dirty bomb, these radioactive materials in the form of metal are likely to generate shrapnel in the blast. People who stayed in the vicinity of such a bomb on explosion may develop blast or missile injuries, with scattered pieces of hard metal and deposits in the body after the explosion, depending on the initial physical state and the nature of the material [50]. There have been several reports on detonations of explosive devices, such as the Pan Am flight from Lockerbie, Scotland in 1988, the Madrid subway in 2004 or the Boston marathon in the USA in 2013 [51]. However, to our knowledge, no detonation events of dirty bombs causing radiation injuries have as yet been reported.

A stolen 192Ir source in Ichihara-city, Chiba in 2008

On 7 April 2008, it was found that one of the radiation sources of 192Ir was missing at a company doing non-destructive testing at Ichihara-city, Chiba prefecture, in Japan [52] (Fig. 1). Ichihara-city has an industrial area with petrochemical complexes, and its population is ~270 000. The source was shielded with a tungsten container and the activity of the source was 370 GBq (Fig. 2). The source emitted ~50 mSv/h of radiation at 1 m when unshielded. If shielded, the dose rate was 7 µSv/h. This company had been working on inspections of devices, equipment and materials supplied to various factories using radiation, ultrasound and others. The company had a license for the use of radiation such as 60Co and 192Ir since 1969. The company stored all sources chain-locked in storage in the irradiation room. On one day, however, one of the chain-locked sources was unlocked and missing (Fig. 3). The company informed the police department and the Ministry of Education, Science and Culture (MEXT), the regulatory authority for the radiation source at that time, about the missing source. The ministry asked the Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare, the National Institute of Radiological Sciences and the mass media to provide information about the source to the public. On 6 May 2008, a suspect was arrested, and the source was successfully retrieved from the bottom of the river in Yokohama-city, Kanagawa prefecture (Fig. 1). On 7–9 July 2008, 2 months later, the 34th Group of Eight Summit, where leaders of countries gather to discuss approaches to various issues, was to be held at Toyako, Hokkaido [53]. Fortunately, the source was not used for any purpose and no one in the public was exposed to the source. The dose to personnel who were involved in the recovery task was ~0.44 mSv.

DISCUSSION

If radiation events occur, the risks for radiation effects vary depending on the radionuclide used, its specific activity and chemical form, and how the source is involved. The events include accidents, disasters and malicious use of radiation as well as nuclear weapons. The histories of radiation and its use have shown that radiation injuries have changed with the times from simple external exposure to injuries combined with radiation and other injuries such as burns, traumas, infections and/or others. Radiation can be artificially generated and radioactive materials can be synthesized, and of course natural radioactive materials are also available. Furthermore, a large scale of events with a number of individuals being involved may also occur. Thus, the medicine for radiation injuries is becoming progressively more complicated.

Radiation combined injury is defined as radiation exposure coupled with other injury forms such as burns, blast, trauma and/or sepsis [54]. The combined injury has a synergistic effect where a less-than-lethal dose of radiation, when combined with a normally nonlethal injury, becomes a potentially fatal combination [55]. Treatment of the radiation combined injury is one of the key countermeasures to be urgently developed, since radiation sources are located anywhere in society and stolen or missing sources could be used for malicious purposes in the diversifying world under globalization. Upon detonation of a dirty bomb, individuals may experience burns and trauma as well as radiation injuries including internal contamination with radionuclides. In the detonations of atomic bombs in Hiroshima and Nagasaki, almost 70% of the victims were estimated to have combined injuries [56–59]. Since little medical care could have been available at that time, it is now difficult to estimate their treatment [60]. In the Chernobyl accident, consequences of combined injuries have been reported to be poor, although details have largely remained unknown [61, 62]. Thus, the knowledge and clinical experiences of combined injuries in humans are extremely poor. Prior studies using experimental animals showed that the combination of radiation with either trauma or burns markedly increases mortality compared with the same dose of radiation alone [61]. The LD50/30 in mice for radiation alone was 0.96 Gy but was reduced to 0.82 Gy when combined with an otherwise nonlethal 15% body surface area burn [63, 64]. Moreover, when mice are exposed to 7 Gy radiation, there is no lethality, and when infected with Bacillus anthracis by intra-tracheal administration alone, survival is 75%; if both insults are combined, survival is reduced to 4% [65]. These available studies suggest that survival from combined injuries is questionable at even relatively low doses of radiation, and these results are mostly in animals. Since medicine is based on not only science but also experiences, urgent extensive research and data collection are crucial.

If the number of casualties increases, the medical system may become overwhelmed. No matter how large or small, the use of nuclear weapons is the worst scenario. In Hiroshima and Nagasaki, since the medical infrastructure was destroyed, no sufficient medical equipment or human resources could be provided and the lack of medical equipment made treatment of patients with combined injuries more difficult at even relatively low doses. Treatment of these combined injuries requires well trained medical professionals from various fields, as well as a variety of equipment and supplies [46]. Moreover, a large proportion of victims with radiation combined injuries would require many beds at hospitals with the capability of special intensive care as well as many facilities for long-term rehabilitation. The medical system for such events including mobilizing human resources and supplies needs to be established.

Fortunately, the frequency of such events requiring treatment and/or involving many people is extremely low. As compared to these rarely occurring incidents, however, events where it is uncertain whether people are exposed to radiation occur at a relatively high frequency. In such events, concerns regarding health, such as long-term cancer risk, are very much increased. These events are usually reported in the mass media but not in scientific reports. Radiation is a unique insult because it cannot be detected by human senses, and it does not cause immediate signs or symptoms. Humans may not become aware of the occurrence of a radiological or nuclear event unless an elevated level of radiation is detected by a device. Clarification of the possibility of exposure scientifically and medically by dose assessment or reconstruction of the events is one of the important roles of radiation emergency medicine, since ‘radiation events’ induce psychological problems that lead to socioeconomical ones, but radiation itself does not. Therefore, collaboration of medical professionals with health physicists and experts of radiation protection and measurement is essential for the medical response system to radiation events [66].

In most countries, the response system to these radiation events is controlled by the ministries or departments of energy or radiation regulation/protection but not health/medicine. Expertise for responding to such events is required, but the number of experts in these fields is extremely limited. At present, radiation emergency medicine is isolated from other medical fields such as emergency medicine. For the treatment of victims from detonation of a dirty bomb, removal of scattered metal pieces emitting radiation from the body is needed. However, there is no report of any experience of removing scattered pieces of radiological hard metal deposited in the body. For the effective treatment of complicated injuries, establishment of a comprehensive medical system that responds to all hazards and also a training system of medical professionals as one body is crucial. In addition, considering that missing or stolen sources are real, people of the general public may pick up or touch these sources without knowing that they emit radiation. In fact, there have been many reports of such accidents [42]. It is obvious that a global security protection system for these sources is essential, and it is urgently required for the regulatory authorities to come together in order to respond to these events [47]. The medicine for these events must become a vital part of conventional regular medical care. If not, a comprehensive response would be difficult.

Injuries caused by radiation change with the times. Radiation is widely used in various fields and is essential for the present life. Devises containing radiation sources are becoming more sophisticated in both medical and industrial fields, and there are many nuclear facilities such as not only NPPs but also nuclear fuel processing, reprocessing and spent fuel storage facilities. We are currently in a period of adding a new chapter concerning the treatment of combined injuries and their related research, as well as a response to events where many individuals are involved in scenarios of the radiation emergency medical response plan.
