Efficient hyperparameter estimation in Bayesian inverse problems using sample average approximation
Julianne Chung, Scot M. Miller, Malena Sabaté Landman, Arvind K. Saibaba

Abstract
In Bayesian inverse problems, it is common to consider several hyperparameters that define the prior and the noise model that must be estimated from the data. In particular, we are interested in linear inverse problems with additive Gaussian noise and Gaussian priors defined using Matérn covariance models. In this case, we estimate the hyperparameters using the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate of the marginalized posterior distribution. However, this is a computationally intensive task since it involves computing log determinants. To address this challenge, we consider a stochastic average approximation (SAA) of the objective function and use the preconditioned Lanczos method to compute efficient approximations of the function and gradient evaluations. We propose a new preconditioner that can be updated cheaply for new values of the hyperparameters and an approach to compute approximations of the gradient evaluations, by reutilizing information from the function evaluations. We demonstrate the performance of our approach on static and dynamic seismic tomography problems.



Keywords:
preconditioning, Monte Carlo, inverse problems

1
Introduction

Inverse problems arise in many important applications, where the aim is to estimate some unknown inverse parameters from given observations or data. In particular, we consider the unknown parameters to be in the form of detailed spatial or spatial temporal maps of physical quantities, e.g., slowness of the medium in seismic tomography. The inverse problems that we tackle in this paper are ill-posed; which means that a solution to the problem may not exist, may not be unique, or may not depend continuously on the data. To address the ill-posedness, we adopt the Bayesian approach to find approximate solutions to the inverse problems.

In the Bayesian approach, we treat the unknown parameters as a random variable and impose a prior distribution on it. The forward operator, which maps the parameters to the observations, is assumed to be fixed; and the data is assumed to be corrupted by additive Gaussian noise. These ingredients define the likelihood. The Bayesian approach uses the Bayes rule to combine the prior and the likelihood to give the posterior distribution, which characterizes the uncertainty in the unknown parameters given measurement data. In this approach, there are several hyperparameters to be considered that govern the prior and the noise model, that are typically unknown in practice and must be estimated from the data. We follow the hierarchical Bayes approach and treat the hyperparameters as random variables as well, so we impose hyperpriors on them. Thus, now the posterior distribution depends jointly on the unknown parameters and the hyperparameters conditioned on the observed data.

The joint posterior distribution is non-Gaussian in general, and a full exploration of this distribution is computationally challenging for several reasons: the number of unknown parameters is large and evaluating the likelihood can be very expensive in practice. A typical approach to explore the posterior distribution, such as Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), can be infeasible in such situations.

In this paper, we consider the case where the forward problem is linear and the posterior distribution on the unknown parameters is Gaussian. The setting is similar to our recent work
[
13
]
, where we considered the marginal posterior distribution, obtained from the joint posterior distribution by marginalizing the unknown parameters, and compute the mode or maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate of the hyperparameters. Since this is still computationally expensive for large-scale problems, the approach in
[
13
]
was to use a low-rank approximation of the forward operator using iterative methods. However, the low-rank approximation is not always suitable. Therefore, we propose a different approach based on stochastic optimization.

Contributions

We propose a sample average approximation (SAA) type method for computing the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate of the marginalized posterior distribution. The novel contributions of this paper are as follows.

1.

The method to estimate the objective function combines a Monte Carlo estimator for the log-determinant of the matrix with a preconditioned Lanczos approach to apply the matrix logarithm. We analyze the impact of the number of Monte Carlo samples and Lanczos iterations on the accuracy of the log-determinant estimator.

2.

We use a novel preconditioner to accelerate the Lanczos iterations. The preconditioner is based on a parametric low-rank approximation of the prior covariance matrix, that is easy to update for new values of the hyperparameters. In particular, no access to the forward/adjoint solver is needed to update the preconditioner, and only a modest amount of precomputation is needed as a setup cost (independent of the optimization).

3.

We also use a new trace estimator to approximate the gradient that has two features: first, it works with a symmetric form of the argument inside the trace, and second, it is able to reuse Lanczos iterates from the objective function computations. Therefore, the gradient can be computed essentially for free (i.e., requiring no additional forward/adjoint applications).

We demonstrate the performance of our approach on model problems from static and dynamic seismic tomography.

Related work

The problem of optimizing for hyperparameters is closely related to parameter estimation in Gaussian processes via maximum likelihood (We may think of it as setting the forward operator as the identity matrix.). The literature on this topic is vast, but we mention a few key references that are relevant to our approach. In
[
4
]
, the authors propose a
matrix-free approach to estimate the hyperparameters. The authors also use a sample average approximation for either the optimization problem or its optimality conditions. In
[

]
, the authors propose a reformulation of the problem that avoids computing the inversion of the (prior) covariance matrix. Approaches based on hierarchical matrices are considered in

. Preconditioned Lanczos methods for estimating the log-determinant and its gradient are considered in

On the inverse problem side, there have been relatively few works for computing the hyperparameters by optimization. Several works (e.g.,
[

Hierarchical Bayesian Inverse Problems

In this section, we review background material on hierarchical Bayesian inverse problems. The inverse problem involves recovering the parameters
{\bf s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
from measurements
{\bf d}
, which have been corrupted by additive Gaussian measurement noise,
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and takes the form

{\bf d}={\bf A}{\bf s}+{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\qquad{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bf 0},{\bf R}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))

(1)

where
{\bf A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
represents the forward map and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}
, represents the (nonnegative) hyperparameters. In the hierarchical Bayes approach, we treat
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
as a random variable, which we endow with prior density
\pi_{\rm hyp}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
. We assume that the noise covariance matrix
{\bf R}:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}\,\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},
where
{\bf R}(\cdot)
is symmetric and positive definite (SPD), and has an inverse and square root that is computationally easy to obtain for any input (e.g., a diagonal matrix).

Prior distribution

We assume that the prior distribution for the parameters
{\bf s}
is also Gaussian of the form
\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),{\bf Q}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})),
where
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}\,\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
{\bf Q}:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}\,\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},
where
{\bf Q}(\cdot)
is assumed to be SPD. Given a set of points
\{{\bf x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
at which the unknowns
{\bf s}
are represented, the covariance matrix
{\bf Q}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is defined through means of a covariance kernel
\kappa(\cdot,\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\theta}}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
. More precisely, the entries of the matrix
{\bf Q}
take the form
[{\bf Q}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})]_{ij}=\kappa({\bf x}_{i},{\bf x}_{j};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
for
1\leq i,j\leq n.
The class of covariance kernels we focus on in this paper is the Matérn covariance class, for which we give details in Section
4
. The resulting covariance matrices are dense, so storing and computing with them is challenging. We assume throughout this paper that matrix vector products (matvecs) with
{\bf Q}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
can be performed efficiently; in particular, matvecs can be done in
\mathcal{O}(n)
or
\mathcal{O}(n\mathsf{log}n)
floating point operations (flops). Details are given in
[
2
]
. For the hyperpriors, following
[
5
]
, we take the prior distribution on
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
to be a Gamma prior with density

\pi_{\rm hyp}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\propto\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{K}\gamma\theta_{i}\right)\qquad\theta_{i}>0,\quad 1\leq i\leq K.

(2)

The parameter
\gamma
is set to be
10^{-4}
and chosen such that the probability density function is relatively flat over the parameter space.

Posterior distribution

Using Bayes’ theorem, the posterior density,
\pi({\bf s},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\bf d}),
is characterized by

\pi({\bf s},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\bf d})=\frac{\pi({\bf d}\,|\,{\bf s},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\pi({\bf s}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\pi_{\rm hyp}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\pi({\bf d})},

where
\pi(\cdot)
denotes an arbitrary probability density of its argument. Using the above assumptions, the posterior density can be expressed explicitly as

\pi({\bf s},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\bf d})\propto\frac{\pi_{\rm hyp}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf d}-{\bf A}{\bf s}\|^{2}_{{\bf R}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}-\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf s}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|^{2}_{{\bf Q}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\right)}{\det({\bf R}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))^{1/2}\det({\bf Q}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))^{1/2}},

(3)

where
\|{\bf x}\|^{2}_{{\bf K}}={\bf x}^{\top}{\bf K}{\bf x}
for any SPD matrix
{\bf K}.
The marginal posterior density is obtained as
\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}|{\bf d})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\pi({\bf s},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\bf d})d{\bf s}
and takes the form

\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\bf d})\propto\pi_{\rm hyp}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\det({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))^{-1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf A}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-{\bf d}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\right),

(4)

where
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
takes the form

{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})={\bf A}{\bf Q}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}){\bf A}^{\top}+{\bf R}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

(5)

Hyperparameter estimation

One approach is to draw samples (e.g., using MCMC) from (
4
), and using the samples to quantify the uncertainty in the hyperparameters. However, this may be prohibitive for large-scale problems because evaluating the density function (or its logarithm) requires evaluating the determinant of and multiple solves with the matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
that depends on
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, which can be expensive. To compound matters, hundreds or thousands of samples are required to get accurate statistics, which can involve several hundred thousand density function evaluations.

Instead, the approach we follow in this paper is based on the empirical Bayes approach. In this approach, we compute the MAP estimate, which corresponds to finding the point estimate that maximizes the marginal posterior distribution. Equivalently, this estimate minimizes the negative log of the marginal posterior. That is, the problem of hyperparameter estimation becomes solving an optimization problem:

\min_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}_{+}}\mathcal{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\equiv-\mathsf{log}\pi_{\rm hyp}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{logdet}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf A}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-{\bf d}\|_{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{-1}}^{2}.

(6)

Notice that for each set of parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, the objective function
\mathcal{F}
can be expensive to compute. In particular, the main computational cost corresponds to evaluating the log determinant of
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
.

For reference, we provide an analytical expression for the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{F}=\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right)_{1\leq i\leq K},

\displaystyle(\nabla\mathcal{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))_{i}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\pi_{\rm hyp}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\frac{\partial\pi_{\rm hyp}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\theta_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{trace}\left({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{-1}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38092pt-\frac{1}{2}\left[{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{-1}({\bf A}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-{\bf d})\right]^{\top}\left[\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\theta_{i}}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})({\bf A}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-{\bf d})-2{\bf A}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right].

(7)

In the following, we describe an approach based on the sample average approximation (SAA) method for solving the optimization problem (
6
).

3
Methods for estimating hyperparameters

In this section, we reinterpret the optimization problem (
6
) as a stochastic optimization problem and derive an SAA method to compute solutions efficiently.

3.1
SAA approach

We first interpret the objective function (
6
) as the expected value of a random variable, and then we use Monte Carlo methods to approximate the objective function.
To this end, since
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is an SPD matrix for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, we can write

\mathsf{logdet}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))=\mathsf{trace}(\mathsf{log}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))).

(8)

Next, for any random vector
{\bf w}
that is isotropic (i.e., it satisfies
\mathbb{E}[{\bf w}]={\bf 0}
and
\mathbb{E}[{\bf w}{\bf w}^{\top}]={\bf I}
), we can write
\mathsf{trace}({\bf M})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bf w}}[{\bf w}^{\top}{\bf M}{\bf w}]
. Therefore, now,

\mathsf{logdet}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))=\mathbb{E}_{\bf w}[{\bf w}^{\top}\mathsf{log}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})){\bf w}],
where
\mathsf{log}({\bf A})
denotes the matrix logarithm of an SPD matrix
{\bf A}
. The optimization problem (
6
) can be expressed as the stochastic optimization problem

\min_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}_{+}}-\mathsf{log}\pi_{\rm hyp}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bf w}[{\bf w}^{\top}\mathsf{log}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})){\bf w}]+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf A}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}-{\bf d}\|_{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{-1}}^{2}.

(9)

There are two main classes of methods
[
18
]
for solving stochastic optimization problems like (
9
). Stochastic approximation methods are iterative methods, where random samples (e.g., one or a batch) are used to update the solution at each iteration. SAA methods represent another family of stochastic optimization methods, which use Monte Carlo simulations. That is, assume we have a random sample of
n_{\rm mc}
independent realizations of
{\bf w}
:
{\bf w}_{1},\dots,{\bf w}_{n_{\rm mc}}
. In practice, for
{\bf w}
we either use Rademacher random vectors (entries independently drawn from
\{-1,+1\}
with equal probability) or standard Gaussian random vectors (entries independently drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
). Then, the Monte Carlo approach replaces the expected objective function in (
9
) with a sample average approximation of the form

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\rm mc}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\equiv-\mathsf{log}\pi_{\rm hyp}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\frac{1}{2n_{\rm mc}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\rm mc}}{\bf w}_{t}^{\top}\mathsf{log}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})){\bf w}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf A}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-{\bf d}\|_{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{-1}}^{2}.

(10)

The statistical properties of the SAA methods have been established in
[
18
, Chapter 5]
.

However, evaluating
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\rm mc}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
can be still very expensive since it requires computing
\mathsf{log}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))
, and the number of required samples
n_{\rm mc}
can be large. Moreover, in order to use efficient optimization methods, we need to either compute the gradient
\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\rm mc}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
or a suitable approximation of the original gradient
\widehat{\nabla\mathcal{F}}_{\rm mc}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
.
To mitigate the first issue, several approaches are available based on either a Chebyshev or Lanczos polynomial approximation to the matrix logarithm
[
14
,
20
]
. However, the degree of the required polynomial may still be too large to obtain accurate approximations. In this paper, to address both issues, we use a preconditioned Lanczos approach to approximate the Monte Carlo estimator for the function (
10
), and we follow an analogous Monte Carlo approach for the gradient of the original function, which will be summarized later.

For the remainder of this section, we drop the explicit dependence on
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
when possible to simplify the exposition.

3.2
Preconditioned log-determinant estimator

Consider the computation of the log determinant of an SPD matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
. Assume we have a preconditioner
{\bf G}
that is (a) easy to invert, (b) satisfies
{\bf G}^{\top}{\bf G}\approx{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{-1}
, and (c) its determinant can be readily computed, then we can write

\mathsf{logdet}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}})=\mathsf{logdet}({\bf G}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\bf G}^{\top})-2\mathsf{log}|\textsf{det}({\bf G})|.

In Section
3
3.3
, we show how to efficiently compute such a preconditioner.
The approach then is to apply the Monte Carlo estimator to the preconditioned matrix
{\bf G}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\bf G}^{\top}
rather than the matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
.
Therefore, the preconditioned Monte Carlo estimator to the objective function becomes

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\rm prec}=-\mathsf{log}\pi_{\rm hyp}+\frac{1}{2n_{\rm mc}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\rm mc}}{\bf w}_{t}^{\top}\mathsf{log}({\bf G}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\bf G}^{\top}){\bf w}_{t}-\mathsf{log}|\det({\bf G})|+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf A}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}-{\bf d}\|_{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{-1}}^{2}.

(11)

The resulting estimator is unbiased since

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n_{\rm mc}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\rm mc}}{\bf w}_{t}^{\top}\mathsf{log}({\bf G}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\bf G}^{\top}){\bf w}_{t}\right]-2\mathsf{log}|\det({\bf G})|=\mathsf{logdet}({\bf G}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\bf G}^{\top})-2\mathsf{log}|\det({\bf G})|=\mathsf{logdet}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}).

3.3
Choice of Preconditioner

To explain the computational benefits of our preconditioner, it is helpful to briefly reintroduce the dependence of
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
on
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. We assume that the prior covariance has a parametric low-rank approximation of the form

{\bf Q}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\approx{\bf U}{\bf M}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}){\bf U}^{\top},

(17)

where
{\bf U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
is a fixed set of basis vectors for representing the prior covariance matrix and
{\bf M}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is a symmetric and positive semidefinite matrix. We construct such a parametric low-rank approximation using the Chebyshev polynomial approximation
[
9
,
15
]
. The details of this derivation are given in Appendix
A
. As an alternative to the Chebyshev approximation, one could also use the approach in
[
19
]
based on Gauss-Legendre features. In both cases, we can construct an approximation to the matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
as

{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\approx\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\equiv({\bf A}{\bf U}){\bf M}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})({\bf A}{\bf U})^{\top}+{\bf R}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

The important point is that
{\bf A}{\bf U}
can be constructed in advance. More precisely, computing the preconditioner for new values of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
after initialization, does not require any matvecs with the forward operator (or its adjoint).

To explain how to construct the preconditioner
{\bf G}
, we once again drop the dependence on
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. We form the matrix
{\bf K}={\bf R}^{-1/2}{\bf A}{\bf U}{\bf M}^{1/2}
and compute its thin SVD
{\bf K}={\bf W}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\bf Z}^{\top}
. Then, using the Woodbury identity, we can factorize

\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}^{-1}={\bf R}^{-1/2}({\bf W}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{2}{\bf W}^{\top}+{\bf I})^{-1}{\bf R}^{-1/2}={\bf G}^{\top}{\bf G},

where
{\bf G}^{\top}={\bf R}^{-1/2}({\bf I}-{\bf W}{\bf D}{\bf W}^{\top})
with
{\bf D}={\bf I}+({\bf I}+{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{2})^{-1/2}
.
Furthermore, we can readily compute the log determinant of the preconditioner as

\displaystyle\mathsf{logdet}({\bf G})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{logdet}({\bf R}^{-1/2})+\mathsf{logdet}({\bf I}-{\bf D})

3.4
Gradient computations

To compute the derivative of
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\rm prec}
, we have to differentiate the matrix logarithm. One possible way of tackling this is to use the approach in
[
16
]
. However, we adopt a different approach in this paper that allows us to estimate the gradients
\{\widehat{\nabla\mathcal{F}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
with only a little additional computational cost. Note that in this approach, we are not computing exact derivatives of
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\rm prec}
or of
\mathcal{F}
, but we are constructing Monte Carlo approximations of the gradient of
\mathcal{F}
.

Consider the expressions for the derivative in (
7
). Recall that during the computation of the objective function, we already have computed the solution
{\bf z}
to
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\bf z}={\bf A}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}-{\bf d}
. Therefore, we can evaluate the expression (for
1\leq i\leq K
)

\left[{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{-1}({\bf A}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}-{\bf d})\right]^{\top}\left[\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{-1}({\bf A}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}-{\bf d})-2{\bf A}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right]={\bf z}^{\top}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}{\bf z}-2({\bf A}^{\top}{\bf z})^{\top}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{\partial\theta_{i}},

efficiently by taking advantage of the right-hand side in the above displayed equation. Therefore, we focus our attention on approximating the term
\mathsf{trace}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{-1}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}{\partial\theta_{i}})
in (
7
). Applying a naïve Monte Carlo estimate
\frac{1}{n_{\rm mc}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\rm mc}}{\bf w}_{t}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{-1}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}{\bf w}_{t}
requires us to solve linear systems of the form
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\bf z}_{t}={\bf w}_{t}
, which incurs an additional expense. Instead, we use the symmetric factorization

{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{-1}={\bf G}^{-\top}({\bf G}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\bf G}^{\top})^{-1/2}({\bf G}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\bf G}^{\top})^{-1/2}{\bf G}^{-1},

and the cyclic property of the trace estimator to use the Monte Carlo trace estimator

\displaystyle\mathsf{trace}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{-1}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}{\partial\theta_{i}})=

\displaystyle\mathsf{trace}\left(({\bf G}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\bf G}^{\top})^{-1/2}{\bf G}^{-1}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}{\bf G}^{-\top}({\bf G}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\bf G}^{\top})^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{\rm mc}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\rm mc}}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t}^{\top}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t},\qquad 1\leq i\leq K,

where
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t}\equiv{\bf G}^{-\top}({\bf G}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\bf G}^{\top})^{-1/2}{\bf w}_{t}
, for
1\leq t\leq n_{\rm mc}
. Recall that, to approximate the evaluation of the objective function, we had already computed an approximation to
{\bf w}_{t}^{\top}\mathsf{log}({\bf G}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\bf G}^{\top}){\bf w}_{t}
in (
14
) using Lanczos to construct
{\bf V}_{k}
and
{\bf T}_{k}
. If we consider reusing the same basis vectors, we can approximate
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t}
cheaply using the formula

{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t}\approx\|{\bf w}_{t}\|_{2}{\bf G}^{-\top}({\bf V}_{k}{\bf T}_{k}^{-1/2}{\bf e}_{1}).

(18)

Using this characterization, an approximation to the gradient can be computed by reusing information from the objective function. The details of the computation of the gradient are given in Algorithm

In this section, we investigate the performance of the Monte Carlo estimator for hyperparameter estimation. We consider two examples from seismic inversion, including a static problem and a dynamic problem.
To report computational time, we use MATLAB R20234b on a MacBook Pro with Apple M1 chip, with 16GB memory, and use 8 workers. For the optimization, we use the
fmincon
solver in MATLAB which uses the interior point method and nonnegativity constraints.

Note that the total number of Lanczos iterations per function (and gradient) evaluations is reported. This is subject to different stopping criteria.

10^{-7}
.
We solve linear systems with

5
Conclusions/Discussion

In this work, we develop an efficient and robust approach for hyperparameter estimation in hierarchical Bayesian inverse problems. By exploiting a Monte Carlo approach, combined with an efficient preconditioned Lanczos method, the proposed SAA approach enables objective function and gradient estimations of the negative log marginal posterior. We show how these approximations can be used within constrained optimization schemes for hyperparameter estimation. Future work includes investigating other types of trace estimators in this framework and combining alternative preconditioners, e.g., those that can exploit problem structure. Extensions to nonlinear forward models and non-Gaussian priors are also of interest.

Parametric kernel low-rank approximation for preconditioning

We explain the construction of the parametric kernel low-rank approximation technique, the details of which are given in
[
9
,
15
]
. For completeness, we offer a brief description but refer the reader to the above resources for a more complete description.

Our goal is to obtain an approximation of the form
{\bf Q}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\approx{\bf U}{\bf M}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}){\bf U}^{\top}
given a set of points
\{{\bf x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
and the kernel function
\kappa({\bf x},{\bf y};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
. Let the points
\{{\bf x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
be enclosed in a box
\mathcal{B}=[\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}]\times\dots\times[\alpha_{d},\beta_{d}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Similarly, let the parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\theta}=[\alpha_{1}^{p},\beta_{1}^{p}]\times\dots\times[\alpha_{K}^{p},\beta_{K}^{p}]\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}
be enclosed inside a box. For a nonnegative integer
j
, the
j
th Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind is
T_{j}(x)=\cos(j\arccos(x))
for
-1\leq x\leq 1
. To transform to and from the interval
[\alpha,\beta]
, we use the invertible transform
\varphi_{[\alpha,\beta]}(x)=\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}x+\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}
. We also define the interpolating polynomial

\phi_{[\alpha,\beta]}(x,y)=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{2}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}T_{j}(\varphi_{[\alpha,\beta]}^{-1}(x))T_{j}(\varphi_{[\alpha,\beta]}^{-1}(y)),

and the Chebyshev interpolation nodes (for
1\leq i\leq p
)

\eta_{i}^{(j)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\varphi_{[\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}]}(\zeta_{i})&1\leq j\leq d\\
\varphi_{[\alpha_{j-d},\beta_{j-d}]}(\zeta_{i})&d+1\leq j\leq d+K\\
\varphi_{[\alpha_{j-(d+d_{\theta})},\beta_{j-(d+d_{\theta})}]}(\zeta_{i})&d+d_{\theta}+1\leq j\leq 2d+K.\end{array}\right.

The roots of
T_{p}
define the Chebyshev nodes
\{\zeta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
.
The factor matrices
{\bf U}
can be expressed as
{\bf U}={\bf U}_{d}\ltimes\dots\ltimes{\bf U}_{1}
, where

[{\bf U}_{j}]_{it}=\phi_{[\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}]}(\eta_{t}^{(j)},[{\bf x}_{i}]_{j})\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,1\leq t\leq p,1\leq j\leq d,

and
\ltimes
denotes the face splitting product.

To define the matrix
{\bf M}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, we define the vector of interpolating polynomials

{\bf q}_{i}(\theta_{i})=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{[\alpha_{i}^{p},\beta_{i}^{p}]}(\eta_{1}^{(d+i)},\theta_{i})&\dots&\phi_{[\alpha_{i}^{p},\beta_{i}^{p}]}(\eta_{n}^{(d+i)},\theta_{i})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times p},\qquad 1\leq i\leq K.

We also define the tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}
, of size
p^{D}
where
D=2d+K
, and with entries

m_{i_{1},\dots,i_{D}}=\kappa({\bf x},{\bf y};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\qquad 1\leq i_{j}\leq p,1\leq j\leq D.

Furthermore,
{\bf x},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
{\bf y}
are defined via the relation
({\bf x},{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\bf y})=(\eta_{i_{1}}^{(1)},\dots,\eta_{i_{D}}^{(D)})
. Next, we define the parametric tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
via the tensor products

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}\times_{i=d+1}^{d+K}{\bf q}_{i-d}(\theta_{i-d}).

Finally, the matrix
{\bf M}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is obtained from a specific mode unfolding of the tensor as
{\bf M}_{F}^{(d)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
. Details are given in
[
15
]
.
