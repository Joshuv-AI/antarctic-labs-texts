Cardiac outreach services reduce mortality and readmissions
Authors: Zaidon AlFalahi, Dylan Rajaratnam, Srisa Boddupalli, Giuseppe Femia, Linda Gardiner, Krishna Kaddapu, Rohan Rajaratnam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure (HF) is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Despite significant improvements in the management of HF, the overall outcome remains poor. In addition to pharmacotherapy and device therapy, non-pharmacological interventions are needed to mitigate the effects of this illness. The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of the HF outreach programme on the rate of mortality, HF hospitalisations and guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) for HF in South-Western Sydney Local Health District (SWSLHD).MethodsIn this observational, registry-based study, adult patients diagnosed with HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) in SWSLHD were invited to participate in the HF outreach service between March 2011 and January 2016. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. The secondary outcomes were the rate of optimal GDMT and HF hospitalisations.ResultsThere were 818 patients included in the study; 470 (57.5%) patients were enrolled and 348 (42.5%) not enrolled into the programme. At the end of the follow-up period (median 978 days, IQR 720–1237), the primary outcome of mortality was observed less in the enrolled group (122 (26%) vs 133 (38.2%), p<0.001) independently of other variables. In addition, fewer enrolled patients had >3 hospital admissions for HF (16.2% vs 35.6%, p<0.001) and reduced median admission days (14.5 days (IQR 8–25) vs 22 days (IQR 12–37), p<0.001). Patients enrolled into the programme were more likely to be on optimal GDMT (76.6% vs 56.6%, p<0.001).ConclusionsEnrolment in the HF outreach programme was associated with a significant reduction in mortality and the frequency and length of hospital HF admissions. In addition, the rate of optimal GDMT was significantly higher in the enrolled group. With the high prevalence of HF, these programmes should be considered in the routine management of patients with HFrEF.Keywords: Cardiac Rehabilitation; Heart Failure, Systolic; Quality of Health Care

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a major cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality and has been described as a worldwide pandemic.1 The 1-year mortality for patients with HF ranges from 17% to 45%, with the majority dying within 5 years.2 In addition, HF is associated with reduced quality of life and a significant healthcare cost burden, making tailored interventions that reduce morbidity, mortality and hospitalisations vital to patients, families and healthcare systems.3 4 While the prevalence of HF is only set to increase, improvement in HF outcomes appears to have plateaued in the past two decades, and survival remains worse than that of prostate, bowel and breast cancers.5,8

Pharmacotherapy, in addition to device therapy in selected patients, has been the cornerstone of management for HF with reduced ejection fraction with improvement in mortality and hospitalisations.9 In addition, a number of non-pharmacological interventions have emerged, such as multimodal, multidisciplinary HF outreach programmes.10 Although trials, observational studies and systematic reviews have shown consistent safety, improvement in exercise capacity and overall quality of life, they have not shown a significant and consistent reduction in mortality.11,14 One possible explanation is the heterogeneity of these programmes and the short duration of both the intervention and the follow-up period. There appears to be a trend for mortality benefit with longer periods of follow-up,15 16 hence it is important to evaluate the role of HF programmes on long-term outcomes.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of the HF outreach programme within South-Western Sydney Local Health District (SWSLHD) on all-cause mortality, HF hospitalisations and guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT). Given the available evidence, we hypothesised that beyond the symptomatic and quality of life benefits of cardiac failure outreach programmes, mortality benefits were more likely to be evident with longer periods of follow-up.

Methods

The HF outreach service intervention

This HF outreach programme is a multidisciplinary, home-based service designed to provide comprehensive support for patients recently discharged from the hospital following a presentation of HF. Led by specialist HF nurses, this programme ensures continuity of care and focuses on optimising recovery and preventing readmissions. On discharge from any hospital in SWSLHD, adult patients treated for HF are routinely screened by a HF nurse. Patients with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), identified through echocardiography, are enrolled in the programme. Their clinical details are recorded in the HF registry, and they receive a written invitation to participate in home-based cardiac rehabilitation. For those who do not respond, a follow-up phone call ensures every effort is made to engage eligible patients in the programme.

The programme includes regular home visits where physical examinations, fluid status assessments and medication reviews are conducted. Tailored exercise programmes are introduced and performed under supervision to suit individual patient needs and promote physical recovery. A strong emphasis is placed on patient education, covering medication compliance, recognition and management of side effects, dietary guidance (including salt and fluid restriction) and psychosocial support to address the emotional challenges associated with chronic HF.

Additionally, patients with more complex needs can access physiotherapy, occupational therapy, social work and dietitian services through home visits. The outreach service fosters seamless coordination and collaboration between hospitals, the cardiology team, general practitioners and pharmacists with a focus on optimisation and uptitration of HF GDMT and early identification of fluid accumulation.

Study design and patient selection

This is an observational, multicentre, registry-based study. All adult patients identified by the SWSLHD HF outreach service with reduced LVEF <45% on echocardiography and admitted to hospital with an episode of symptomatic HF were invited to participate between March 2011 and January 2016 and are included in the analysis.

Baseline information collected included age, sex, height and weight, enrolment status, vital signs on initial review, basic biochemical profile (creatinine, electrolytes, haemoglobin levels), echocardiographic estimation of LVEF and pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP), smoking status, medication history and presence or absence of ischaemic cardiomyopathy and atrial fibrillation (AF). This information was populated prospectively into the HF registry and is obtained from the electronic health record as well as patient interviews. Mortality data were obtained from multiple sources including the electronic health records, follow-up data and the primary care centres where patients are enrolled.

Outcomes and definitions

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes were HF hospitalisations and uptake of optimal GDMT.

Hospital admissions were defined as any admission where management of HF exacerbation was included as a principal diagnosis in the electronic health record.

GDMT includes beta-blockers, ACE inhibitor (ACE-I) or angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB), and spironolactone. Optimal GDMT was defined as concurrent use of all three classes available during the study period: beta-blockers, ACE-I or ARB, and spironolactone.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS), GraphPad software and Microsoft Excel. Categorical variables were presented in numbers and percentage (%), and continuous variables were presented as mean±SD or median/IQR. Data were visualised using histograms, box and Q-Q plots, and normality was tested by Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. Fisher’s exact tests were performed to compare categorical variables between groups, Student’s t-tests for continuous variables and Mann-Whitney U tests to compare medians. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Logistic regression analysis was performed for statistically significant factors associated with mortality, and ORs were calculated with 95% CIs. Furthermore, a Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was constructed from the available HF diagnosis follow-up dates.

Results

Baseline characteristics

There were 818 patients included in the study (table 1); 470 (57.5%) patients were enrolled into the HF outreach programme. The mean age in the enrolled group was younger than the non-enrolled group (78.6±12 years vs 81.3±11 years, p<0.001). In the enrolled group, there were a lower number of women (40.6% vs 50.6%, p=0.055), lower LVEF (33±13% vs 36±14%, p=0.008) and lower median PASP (42±13 mm Hg vs 45±25 mm Hg, p=0.028). Furthermore, the enrolled group had a higher prevalence of ischaemic cardiomyopathy (69.6% vs 49.4%, p<0.001). Body mass index was comparable across the two groups, and there was no statistically significant difference in the prevalence of AF and smoking.

Primary outcomes

The median follow-up period was 978 days (IQR 720–1237). Over this period, enrolment in the clinic was associated with a significant reduction in mortality (26.0% vs 38.2%, p<0.001). Using logistic regression analysis, the strongest predictor of survival was enrolment status (OR 0.606, 95% CI 0.436 to 0.841, p=0.003), followed by statin therapy (OR 0.682, 95% CI 0.495 to 0.939, p=0.019), an effect driven mainly by mortality benefit in the subgroup with ischaemic cardiomyopathy (p<0.001). Conversely, the strongest predictor of mortality was non-enrolment (OR 1.65, 95% CI 1.189 to 2.29, p=0.003), followed by the presence of ischaemic cardiomyopathy (OR 1.533, 95% CI 1.089 to 2.156, p 0.014). In addition, the following factors made statistically significant contributions to mortality: older age (OR 1.033, 95% CI 1.017 to 1.049, p<0.001), higher creatinine levels (OR 1.004, 95% CI 1.003 to 1.005, p<0.001) and higher estimated PASP (OR 1.014, 95% CI 1.002 to 1.027, p=0.014).

Female sex (p<0.001), higher haemoglobin levels (p=0.039), ACE-I therapy (p=0.049) and smoking status (p=0.018) influenced mortality on group analysis but were no longer statistically significant when corrected in a logistic regression model.

Secondary outcomes

Enrolment was also associated with significantly lower rates of recurrent HF admissions (>3 admissions, 16.2% vs 35.6%, p<0.001) and reduced median admission days (14.5 days (IQR 8–25) vs 22 days (IQR 12–37), p<0.001).

Enrolment in the programme was strongly associated with GDMT compliance, including beta-blockers (78.9% vs 62.9%, p<0.001), ACE-I (50.9% vs 44%, p=0.056), ARB (40.2% vs 27.9%, p<0.001) and spironolactone (40.4% vs 28.4%, p<0.001). The rate of optimal GDMT was significantly more prevalent in the enrolled group (76.6% vs 56.6%, p<0.001); lack of all GDMT was observed far less frequently in the enrolled group (3.4% vs 14.4%, p<0.001). Frusemide intake was comparable across both groups (86.8% in the enrolled groups vs 83.6% in the non-enrolled group, p=0.229).

Survival analysis

A survival analysis plot (Kaplan-Meier curve) was constructed from the available diagnosis dates, death dates and follow-up data. There was an early and persistent divergence of the survival curves in favour of the enrolled group. Discussion

Despite the relatively high mortality rates associated with HF, non-pharmacological interventions have not clearly demonstrated significant improvements in survival. In randomised trials evaluating HF ‘outreach programs’ after hospital discharge, mortality effects vary by programme intensity and scope. A large, randomised US telemonitoring and telephone-coaching trial (BEAT-HF)17 showed no reduction in 180-day all-cause mortality. By contrast, a nurse-coordinated Interdisciplinary Network for Heart Failure disease management programme,18 combining face-to-face education, structured telephone follow-up, uptitration of HF medication and coordinated provider networking, led to a reduction in 180-day all-cause mortality, although its primary composite (death or rehospitalisation) was neutral due to more rehospitalisations in the intervention arm. Finally, a multicentre home-based telemanagement programme19 reduced readmissions but did not lower cardiovascular mortality over 1 year.

In this registry-based study, enrolment in our HF outreach programme was associated with reduced mortality, less frequent and shorter hospital admissions as well as increased adherence to GDMT. Notably, our study cohorts’ mean age group was higher than most of the other published literature, addressing a potential evidence gap in this group.20 Additionally, the enrolled cohort also had a significantly higher prevalence of ischaemic cardiomyopathy (69.6% vs 49.4%, p<0.001), a condition which typically portends a poorer prognosis than non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy.21 22 The mortality benefit of enrolment remained statistically significant after correcting for other potential confounders such as age, medications, gender and creatinine levels. Although previous studies have demonstrated similar reductions in hospitalisation frequency and duration, the significant mortality benefit observed in our study is in contrast to much of the published literature. There are multiple potential explanations for this observed effect. First, our study had data over a much longer follow-up period than much of the published literature, with a median follow-up duration of 978 days.

Second, our outreach service is not an isolated exercise programme. A crucial aspect of the programme is ‘getting the pills right’. This involves patient education on the importance of adherence, dose titration and addressing side effects and intolerances, as well as behavioural interventions, self-care, encouragement and support. Literature suggests that medication adherence interventions significantly reduce mortality and hospitalisation rates, a likely contributor to our findings.23 24 This conclusion becomes even more plausible when taking into consideration the non-enrolled group in our study, who were significantly undertreated with optimal medications compared with the enrolled group.

Third, in the local context, this study enrolled a diverse population from SWSLHD which is considered an area of socioeconomic disadvantage, putting this vulnerable population at risk of increased adverse health outcomes.25,27 Outreach services such as the one studied facilitate access to essential health services, including specialist nurse review and early referral pathways. While this could provide a potential health outcome benefit, there is a general lack of quality research in this area, and further research is needed.

Our study has limitations. While it provided a snapshot of real-world data in a socioeconomically disadvantaged population, it lacked randomisation, and the analysis was carried out retrospectively, hence the risk of bias. The non-enrolled cohort could have declined enrolment due to a multitude of factors, including complex medical conditions, psychological and behavioural factors, poor health literacy and adherence, all of which could have independently led to the worse outcomes observed. Furthermore, data inconsistencies from the prolonged collection period and reliance on multiple mortality data sources may have introduced inaccuracies; however, this would lead to under-reporting of mortality in the non-enrolled cohort particularly and would not have changed the conclusions of the study. Finally, data were collected in 2011–2016, before contemporary GDMT components, such as angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors and sodium-glucose linked transporter 2 inhibitors, were standard of care; however, the impact of a HF outreach programme may be even greater in current practice given the more complex titration and monitoring required to optimise GDMT.

Conclusion

Enrolment in the HF outreach programme was independently associated with a reduction in mortality, frequency and total length of admissions, as well as adherence with optimal GDMT. These results support the multifaceted benefits of a cardiac rehabilitation outreach service within this vulnerable population. The results emphasise the value of comprehensive, accessible care in addressing gaps in HF management, especially in a socioeconomically disadvantaged population, and encourage further research to validate these findings and explore the adoption of tailored innovative approaches in managing patients with HF.
