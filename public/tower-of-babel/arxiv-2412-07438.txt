Flows of vector fields and the Kalman Theorem
Fabio Bagagiolo 1 Cristina Giannotti ∗ 2 Andrea Spiro 2 Marta Zoppello 3

Abstract
. We give two proofs of the Kalman Theorem, alternative to the most common ones, which infer such a classical result of Control Theory using just very basic facts on flows of vector fields. These proofs are apt to be generalised in diverse directions – in fact one of them has been already generalised, yielding new criteria for local controllability of non-linear real analytic controlled systems.

1.
Introduction

Consider a controlled dynamical system, whose states are represented by the points
q=(q^{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
in the affine space
\mathscr{Q}:=\mathbb{R}^{n}

and with evolutions given by the solutions of a system of linear first order equations of the form

\dot{q}=Aq+Bu

(1.1)

for some non zero constant matrices
A=(A^{i}_{j})
and
B=(B^{i}_{a})
and in which the controls
u(t)
are measurable functions taking values in a bounded set
\mathscr{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
. For a fixed
\overline{q}\in\mathscr{Q}
, denote by
\mathcal{C}_{\overline{q}}
the set of all points
q\in\mathscr{Q}
that are
controllable to
\overline{q}
, i.e. for which there is a control
u(t)
which determines a solution
q(t)
to (
1.1
) that starts from
q
and reaches
\overline{q}
in a finite time. A local version of the famous Kalman Theorem consists of a criterion for determining whether the set
\mathcal{C}_{\overline{q}=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}
of controllable points to the origin contains an open set. It can be stated as follows.

Theorem
(Kalman).
If
\mathscr{K}
contains a neighbourhood of
0_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}
, the set
\mathcal{C}_{\overline{q}=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}
contains a neighbourhood of
0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
if and only if the space
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is spanned by the vectors

B_{1},\ldots,B_{m},\ \ A{\cdot}B_{1},\ldots,A{\cdot}B_{m},\ \ (A{\cdot}A){\cdot}B_{1},(A{\cdot}A){\cdot}B_{1}\ldots,\ (A{\cdot}A){\cdot}B_{m},\ldots\\
\ldots,\ \ \left(A{\cdot}\ldots{\cdot}A\right){\cdot}B_{1},\left(A{\cdot}\ldots{\cdot}A\right){\cdot}B_{2},\ldots,\left(A{\cdot}\ldots{\cdot}A\right){\cdot}B_{m},\ \ \ldots\ ,

(1.2)

has the maximal dimension
n
(here, we denote by
B_{a}
the columns of
B
).

The proof can be found in many textbooks (as for instance
[
1
,
3
]
), and it essentially uses results in
Linear Algebra and Convex Geometry.

The maximal dimension condition of the Kalman Theorem is very much reminiscent of a known corollary of the Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem on the local controllability of
systems of the form
\dot{q}=\sum_{a=0}^{m}X_{a}(q)u^{a}
. In fact,
the sufficiency parts of the Kalman Theorem and of the mentioned corollary of the Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem, can be derived from a very general criterion by Sussmann (
[
18
]
or
[
3
, Thm. 3.29 Ex.3.3]
; on this regard, see also foundational Sussmann’s and Stefan’s papers
[
16
,
17
,
13
,
14
]
).
Furthermore, a couple of results by Hermann
[
7
]
and Nagano
[
11
]
provide a common argument for proving the necessity parts of both theorems (on this regard, see also
[
1
,
3
]
).
Due to all this,
it is commonly understood that the Kalman Theorem is equivalent to the above quoted corollary of the Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem
. Since it is not hard to check that “the Kalman Theorem yields such a corollary of the Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem” (see e.g. §
4
below for a short proof or
[
4
,
15
]
for proofs holding in much more general settings), on the basis of the above common belief it is sensible to expect the existence of a straightforward way of deriving the Kalman Theorem from the Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem. In this paper we make explicit such a direct implication in two ways.
More precisely, our main achievements consist of two (at best of our knowledge, new) proofs of the Kalman Theorem, which also appear to be appropriate for generalisations to fully nonlinear control systems. Actually, the second of our proofs have been successfully exploited
in
[
6
]
and have led to new controllability criteria for nonlinear real analytic control system.

Our first proof is short and gives a simple way to pass from the (above described corollary of the) Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem to the Kalman Theorem. It shows that the first implies the second just on the basis of the following simple (but crucial) fact: if
\mathscr{K}
contains a neighbourhood of the origin – and thus, in particular, a convex symmetric neighbourhood of the
0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
– then also the controllable set
\mathcal{C}_{q=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}
of (
1.1
) contains a convex set (see (1) of Proposition
5.1
, below).
As we mentioned above we expect that this new proof admits generalisations for other kinds of control systems, provided that similar convexity properties for the controllable sets can be established. On this regard, we would like to mention that investigations, involving ideas very close to those of our first proof, can be found in
[
9
]
, where interesting extensions of the Kalman criterion are reached.

Our second proof is much longer than the first, it is based on a discussion on the higher dimensional space
\mathscr{M}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathscr{Q}\times\mathscr{K}
and it does not involve in a direct way the Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem – it just uses a few results on flows, which are properties that can be taken as ancestors of the Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem. However, these drawbacks are balanced by the fact that our second proof makes
no use of any information on convexity properties of the controllable sets. This makes this second proof
even more appropriate than the first for generalisations to non-linear control systems. Indeed, such generalisations have been established in
[
6
]
, where new local controllability criteria are given and diverse controllability problems, for which all previously known criteria are inconclusive, are solved.

The paper is structured as follows. After section §
2
, in which we give a few preliminaries and introduce some convenient notation, in §
3
we recall the statements of
the Chow-Rashevskiĭ and the Kalman Theorem, on which our discussion is based. In §
4
, for the sake of completeness, we exhibit a direct proof that, for the collections of
m+1
vector fields
X_{\alpha}
of the above described form, the property determined by the Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem can be inferred directly from the Kalman Theorem (as we mentioned above, other proofs that hold in a much more general setting are given in
[
4
,
15
]
). In §
5
and §
6
there are our main results, i.e. the mentioned new proofs for the Kalman Theorem, one based on the Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem and the other on general properties of flows.

Throughout this paper we adopt the Einstein convention on summations.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Vector fields, Lie brackets and flows

We recall that a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
vector field on
\mathscr{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an application in
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathscr{U},\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which maps each
x=(x^{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}\in\mathscr{U}
into a vector
X(x)=(X^{1}(x),\ldots,X^{n}(x))
applied to the point
x
. We also recall that each
\mathcal{C}^{k}
vector field
X=(X^{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
can be identified with the first order differential operator on real functions

X(f):=X^{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{i}}
.
Under this identification, the Lie bracket
[X,Y]
between two
\mathcal{C}^{k}
vector fields
X,Y
on
\mathscr{U}
,
k\geq 1
, can be defined as the
\mathcal{C}^{k-1}
vector field on
\mathscr{U}
identified with the first order differential operator

[X,Y](f):=X(Y(f))-Y(X(f))=\left(X^{j}\frac{\partial Y^{i}}{\partial x^{j}}-Y^{j}\frac{\partial X^{i}}{\partial x^{j}}\right)\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{i}}=\\
=\left(X(Y^{i})-Y(X^{i})\right)\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{i}}\ .

Given a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
vector field
X
on
\mathscr{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a corresponding (local)
flow
on a relatively compact open subset
\widetilde{\mathscr{U}}\subset\mathscr{U}
is a
map
\Phi^{X}:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times\widetilde{\mathscr{U}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is constructed as follows for a sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
. For any
x\in\widetilde{\mathscr{U}}
, let us denote by
\gamma^{(x)}(s)
,
s\in(-\varepsilon^{(x)}_{1},\varepsilon^{(x)}_{2})
, the unique maximal solution to the
differential problem

\dot{\gamma}^{(x)}(s)=X(\gamma^{(x)}(s))
,
\gamma^{(x)}(0)=x
. Given
0<\varepsilon\leq\min_{q\in\widetilde{\mathscr{U}}}\{\varepsilon^{(x)}_{1},\varepsilon^{(x)}_{2}\}
, the flow
\Phi^{X}
is the map defined by

\Phi^{X}:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times\widetilde{\mathscr{U}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}\ ,\qquad\Phi^{X}(s,x):=\gamma^{(x)}(s)\ .

By construction, for any fixed
s_{o}\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
the map

\Phi^{X}_{s_{o}}:=\Phi^{X}(s_{o},\cdot):\widetilde{\mathscr{U}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

is a diffeomorphism from
\widetilde{\mathscr{U}}
onto
\Phi^{X}_{s_{o}}(\widetilde{\mathscr{U}})
, with inverse
\Phi^{X}_{-s_{o}}=\Phi^{-X}_{s_{o}}
, and with

\frac{\partial\Phi^{X}(s,x)}{\partial s}\bigg|_{s=0}=X(x)\qquad\text{for any}\ x\in\widetilde{\mathscr{U}}\ .

Moreover,
\Phi^{X}_{s_{o}=0}=\Id_{\widetilde{\mathscr{U}}}
and, whenever
s,s^{\prime}
are such that
s+s^{\prime}\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
and
\Phi^{X}_{s}(x),\Phi^{X}_{s^{\prime}}(x)\in\widetilde{\mathscr{U}}
,

\Phi^{X}_{s+s^{\prime}}(x)=\Phi^{X}_{s}\big(\Phi^{X}_{s^{\prime}}(x)\big)=\Phi^{X}_{s^{\prime}}\big(\Phi^{X}_{s}(x)\big)\ .

2.2.
Pushed-forward vector fields and their flows

Let
X
be a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
vector field on an open set
\mathscr{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\varphi:\mathscr{U}\to\mathscr{V}=\varphi(\mathscr{U})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a diffeomorphism between
\mathscr{U}
and

\mathscr{V}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The
push-forward of
X
by the map
\varphi
is the vector field on
\mathscr{V}

\varphi_{*}(X)=\big(\varphi_{*}(X)^{1},\ldots\varphi_{*}(X)^{n}\big)

corresponding to the linear differential operator defined by

\varphi_{*}(X)(f)\big|_{q}:=X(f\circ\varphi)\big|_{\varphi^{-1}(q)}=X^{j}(\varphi^{-1}(q))\frac{\partial(f\circ\varphi)}{\partial q^{j}}\bigg|_{\varphi^{-1}(q)}=\\
=X^{j}(\varphi^{-1}(q))\frac{\partial\varphi^{i}}{\partial q^{j}}\bigg|_{\varphi^{-1}(q)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial q^{i}}\bigg|_{q}=\left(J(\varphi)^{i}_{j}X^{j}\right)\bigg|_{\varphi^{-1}(q)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial q^{i}}\bigg|_{q}\ ,

(2.1)

where
J(\varphi)
is the Jacobian matrix of
\varphi
.
In other words,
\varphi_{*}(X)
is the vector field on
\mathscr{V}
with components
\varphi_{*}(X)^{i}(q)=\bigg(J(\varphi)^{i}_{j}X^{j}\bigg)(\varphi^{-1}(q))
.

The following fact is well known (see e.g.
[
19
, Prop. 1.55]
):

For any two
\mathcal{C}^{k}
vector fields
X
,
Y
,
k\geq 1
, on
\mathscr{U}
and any
\mathcal{C}^{2}
diffeomorphism
\varphi:\mathscr{U}\to\mathscr{V}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
then
\varphi_{*}([X,Y])=\left[\varphi_{*}(X),\varphi_{*}(Y)\right]
. This and a direct calculation imply that the following relation between flows holds at all points where both sides are defined:

\Phi^{X}_{s}\circ\Phi^{Y}_{t}=\Phi_{t}^{(\Phi^{X}_{s})_{*}(Y)}\circ\Phi^{X}_{s}\ .

(2.2)

2.3.
Piecewise regular curves and their compositions

Given a manifold
\mathscr{N}
, by
parameterised regular curve
we mean a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
map
\gamma:[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathscr{N}
with nowhere vanishing velocity
\dot{\gamma}(t)
and which is an homeomorphism between
[a,b]
and the image
\gamma([a,b])
of the map. The image
\gamma([a,b])\subset\mathscr{N}
is called
(non-parameterised) regular curve
.
Two parameterised regular curves
\gamma(t)
,
\widetilde{\gamma}(s)
with the same image are called
consonant
if one is determined from the other via a change of parameter
t=t(s)
with

\frac{dt}{ds}>0
at all points. Consonance is clearly an equivalence relation and an
orientation
of a regular curve is a choice of one of the only two possible equivalence classes of its parameterisations.
An
oriented curve
is a regular curve with an orientation. In the following, we denote it by means of one of the consonant parameterisations
\gamma(t)
of the chosen equivalence class.

Given two oriented curves
\gamma_{1}(t)
,
\gamma_{2}(s)
,
t\in[a,b]
,
s\in[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
, with
\gamma_{1}(b)=\gamma_{2}(a^{\prime})
, their union is called
(oriented) composition
and we denote it by
\gamma_{1}\ast\gamma_{2}
. The oriented regular curves
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
that give the composition
\gamma_{1}\ast\gamma_{2}
are called
regular arcs
of the composition. In a similar way we define the
(oriented) composition
of a finite number of oriented regular curves, each of them sharing its final endpoint with the initial endpoint of the succeeding one. The subsets of
\mathscr{N}
, which are compositions of a finite number of oriented regular curves, are called
piecewise regular (oriented) curves
.

3.
The Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem and the Kalman Theorem

3.1.
Orbits of sets of vector fields and the Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem

Let
\mathscr{F}=\{X_{A}\}_{A\in\mathscr{J}}
be a family of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
vector fields on an open subset
\mathscr{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, indexed by the elements of a (possibly uncountable) set
\mathscr{J}
, and denote by
\widehat{\mathscr{F}}
the space of all finite linear combinations with constant coefficients of the vectors in
\mathscr{F}
, i.e.

\widehat{\mathscr{F}}=\langle\mathscr{F}\rangle:=\bigg\{Y=\lambda_{A_{1}}X_{A_{1}}+\ldots+\lambda_{A_{N}}X_{A_{N}}\ ,\ \lambda_{A_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}\ ,\ N\in\mathbb{N}\bigg\}\ .

The
orbit by
\mathscr{F}
of a point
x_{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

is the subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}}(x_{o})=\bigg\{\ x\in\mathscr{U}\ :\ x=\Phi^{Y_{1}}_{s_{1}}\circ\ldots\circ\Phi^{Y_{N}}_{s_{N}}(x_{o})\ ,\ \ N\in\mathbb{N}\ ,\ s_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\ ,\ Y_{i}\in\widehat{\mathscr{F}}\ \bigg\}\ .

(3.1)

The
Lie span of
\mathscr{F}
at the point
x_{o}
is the linear subspace of
T_{x_{o}}\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by

\Lie(\mathscr{F})_{x_{o}}:=\bigg\{\ v=[Y_{1},[Y_{2},\ldots[Y_{N-2},[Y_{N-1},Y_{N}]]\ldots]]\big|_{x_{o}}\ ,\ N\in\mathbb{N}\ ,\ Y_{j}\in\widehat{\mathscr{F}}\bigg\}\ .

This notation being fixed, we can state Sussmann’s refined and improved version of the Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem as follows (see also
[
13
,
14
]
for an even more general variant of Sussmann’s version and
[
5
,
6
]
for discussions and improvements in various directions).

Theorem 3.1

(Chow-Rashevskiĭ-Sussmann
[
2
,
12
,
16
,
17
]
)
.

For any family
\mathscr{F}
of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
vector fields on an open subset
\mathscr{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for any
x_{o}\in\mathscr{U}
, the corresponding set
\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}}(x_{o})
is a connected immersed submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of dimension greater than or equal to
\dim\Lie(\mathscr{F})_{x_{o}}
.
Moreover, if the vector fields are real analytic, then
\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}}(x_{o})
contains a neighbourhood of
x_{o}

if and only if
\dim\Lie(\mathscr{F})_{x_{o}}=n
.

3.2.
The Kalman Theorem

Consider the system (
1.1
), which, for brevity, in the following will be called
the linear system

(A,B)
. Given a measurable control
u_{o}:[0,T]\to\mathscr{K}
, we denote by
q^{(A,B|q_{o},u_{o})}:[0,T]\to\mathscr{Q}
the
unique absolutely continuous map which solves the equations with control
u_{o}
and initial condition
q_{o}
, that is the solution to

\begin{array}[]{ll}\dot{q}(t)=Aq(t)+Bu_{o}(t)\ \text{for a.e.}\ t\in[0,T]\ ,\\
q(0)=q_{o}\ .\end{array}

(3.2)

For any choice of points
\overline{q},q_{o}\in\mathscr{Q}
we may consider the following associated subsets of
\mathscr{Q}
:

–

The
set of the states that are controllable to
\overline{q}
, i.e. the set of
q\in\mathscr{Q}
from which the controlled system can start to reach the prescribed
final state

\overline{q}
in a finite time
T\geq 0
;

–

The
set of all states that are reachable from
q_{o}
, i.e. the set of
q\in\mathscr{Q}
that can be reached in finite time
T\geq 0
starting from the prescribed
initial state

q_{o}
.

We denote such two sets, respectively, by

\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)}_{\overline{q}}:=\bigg\{q_{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ :\ q^{(A,B|q_{o},u_{o})}(T)=\overline{q}\ ,\ \text{for some}\ T\geq 0\ \text{and}\ u_{o}:[0,T]\to\mathscr{K}\ \text{meas.}\bigg\}\ ,

(3.3)

\mathscr{O}^{(A,B)}_{q_{o}}:=\bigg\{\overline{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ :\ q^{(A,B|q_{o},u_{o})}(T)=\overline{q}\ ,\ \text{for some}\ T\geq 0\ \text{and}\ u_{o}:[0,T]\to\mathscr{K}\ \text{meas.}\bigg\}\ .

(3.4)

The subsets of these two sets, given by the points that are controllable to
\bar{q}
or reachable from
q_{o}
, respectively, by means of piecewise constant controls are denoted by
\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)\text{p.c.}}_{\overline{q}}
and
\mathscr{O}^{(A,B)\text{p.c.}}_{q_{o}}
, respectively.

Due to the linearity of the system (
1.1
) and the fact that the matrices
A
and
B
are constant,
the set of controllable states and the set of reachable states are related each other as follows. If we set
\mathbb{A}:=-A
,
\mathbb{B}=-B
, one can
directly see that for any
q\in\mathscr{Q}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathscr{O}^{(A,B)}_{q}=\mathcal{C}^{(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})}_{q}\ ,\qquad\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)}_{q}=\mathscr{O}^{(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})}_{q}\ .

(3.5)

Moreover, if the control set
\mathscr{K}
is symmetric (i.e. such that
\mathscr{K}=-\mathscr{K}
) we also have that

\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)}_{q}=\mathcal{C}^{(A,\mathbb{B})}_{q}=\mathscr{O}^{(\mathbb{A},B)}_{q}\ ,\qquad\mathscr{O}^{(A,B)}_{q}=\mathscr{O}^{(A,\mathbb{B})}_{q}=\mathcal{C}^{(\mathbb{A},B)}_{q}\ .

(3.6)

Similar relations hold for the sets
\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)\text{p.c.}}_{q}
,
\mathscr{O}^{(\mathbb{A},B)\text{p.c.}}_{q}
, etc.

We are now ready to state the version of the Kalman Theorem, which we are going to use in all forthcoming discussion and which is well known to be fully equivalent to the statement in the Introduction.
In a matter of clarity, in what follows the space
V
, generated by the vectors defined in (
1.2
), will be denoted by
V=\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}
.

Theorem 3.2

(Kalman Theorem)
.

If
\mathscr{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a neighbourhood of
0_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the set
\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)\text{\rm p.c.}}_{\overline{q}=0}
contains a neighbourhood of
0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if
\dim\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}=n
.

The equality
\dim\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}=n
is commonly called
Kalman condition
.

4.
The Kalman Theorem implies the corollary of the Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem for sets of
vector fields with either constant or linear components

Given a linear system
(A,B)
, let
\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}

be the finite set of real analytic vector fields
\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}:=\{X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
X_{\alpha}
defined at each point
q
by

\begin{split}&\hskip 113.81102ptX_{0}(q):=(-A^{1}_{j}q^{j},\ldots,-A^{n}_{j}q^{j})=-A\,q\qquad\text{and}\\
&X_{1}(q):=(B_{1}^{1},\ldots,B_{1}^{n})\ ,\ X_{2}(q):=(B_{2}^{1},\ldots,B_{2}^{n})\ ,\ \ldots,\ X_{m}(q):=(B_{m}^{1},\ldots,B_{m}^{n})\ .\end{split}

(4.1)

Note that, for
\alpha=1,\ldots,m
, the components of the vector field
X_{\alpha}
are precisely the entries of the
\alpha-
th column of the matrix
B
.
Finally, for any
\lambda=(\lambda^{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, let us set

Y^{(\lambda)}:=X_{0}+\lambda^{i}X_{i}
.

We want to show that
using the Kalman Theorem in a direct and elementary way, one can prove that the orbit
\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}(0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}})
contains a neighbourhood of the origin if and only if
\dim\Lie(\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)})_{q=0}=n
.

Indeed,
for any
\widehat{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the integral curve
t\mapsto\Phi^{Y^{(\lambda)}}_{t}(\widehat{q})
of the vector field
Y^{(\lambda)}
coincides with the solution
q^{(-A,B|\widehat{q},u_{o})}(t)
of the linear system
(-A,B)
determined by the constant control
u_{o}(t)\equiv\lambda
. Hence if we consider the linear system
(A,B)
with a control set
\mathscr{K}
which is not only open, but also symmetric, by (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) for any fixed point
\overline{q}
, the corresponding set
\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)\text{p.c.}}_{\overline{q}}\bigg(=\mathscr{O}^{(-A,B)\text{p.c.}}_{\overline{q}}\bigg)
coincides with the set of points of the form

q(T)=\Phi^{Y^{(\lambda_{N})}}_{s_{N}}\circ\ldots\circ\Phi^{Y^{(\lambda_{1})}}_{s_{1}}(\overline{q})\ ,\qquad T=s_{1}+s_{2}+\ldots+s_{N}>0\ ,

for some finite sequence of
m
-tuples
\lambda_{i}=(\lambda_{i}^{1},\ldots,\lambda^{m}_{i})
. Since each such a point is in
\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}(\overline{q})
, we conclude that

\mathcal{C}_{\overline{q}}^{(A,B)\text{\rm p.c.}}\subset\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}(\overline{q})
.
From this it follows immediately that
if the linear system
(A,B)
satisfies the Kalman condition, then, by the Kalman Theorem, not only
\mathcal{C}_{\overline{q}=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}^{(A,B)\text{\rm p.c.}}
contains a neighbourhood of
0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, but also the orbit
\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}(0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}})
contains a neighbourhood of the origin
as desired.

We claim that the converse is true as well. The proof of this is indeed elementary and needs neither the Kalman Theorem nor other results nor convexity assumptions, except some standard linear algebra.
For the sake of completeness, here are the details.

Assume that the linear system
(A,B)
does not satisfy the Kalman condition, i.e. that the vector space
\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}
has dimension
n^{\prime}<n
, and pick a set of
n^{\prime}
linearly independent vectors among the generators (
1.2
), say
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n^{\prime}}
. Then consider a set of
n-n^{\prime}
additional vectors, denoted by
C_{n^{\prime}+1}
, …,
C_{n}
not belonging to
\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}
, which complete the previous vectors to a basis for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Applying an appropriate linear change of coordinates, there is no loss of generality if we assume that
C_{1}=(1,0,\ldots,0)
,
C_{2}=(0,1,\ldots,0)
, …,
C_{n}=(0,0,\ldots,1)
and that

\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}=\langle C_{1},\ldots,C_{n^{\prime}}\rangle=\{q_{n^{\prime}+1}=\ldots=q_{n}=0\}.

Note that, being each vector
C_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n^{\prime}
, in
\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}
, the corresponding vector
A\cdot C_{i}
is also in
\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}
. This implies that the matrix
A
(in the considered new coordinates!) has the form

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}a_{1}^{1}&\ldots&a_{n^{\prime}}^{1}&a_{n^{\prime}+1}^{1}&\ldots&a_{n}^{1}\\
a_{1}^{2}&\ldots&a_{n^{\prime}}^{2}&a_{n^{\prime}+1}^{2}&\ldots&a_{n}^{2}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{1}^{n^{\prime}}&\ldots&a_{n^{\prime}}^{n^{\prime}}&a_{n^{\prime}+1}^{n^{\prime}}&\ldots&a_{n}^{n^{\prime}}\\
0&\ldots&0&a_{n^{\prime}+1}^{n^{\prime}+1}&\ldots&a_{n}^{n^{\prime}+1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ldots&0&a_{n^{\prime}+1}^{n}&\ldots&a_{n}^{n}\end{array}\right)\ .

(4.2)

In particular, for any
q\in\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}
, also the vector
A{\cdot}q
is in
\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}
. It follows that the value at any point of
\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}=\{q_{n^{\prime}+1}=\ldots=q_{n}=0\}
of a vector field in
\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}
is a vector which is tangent to
\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}
and that
any flow of a vector field in
\widehat{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}
maps the subspace
\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}
into itself. We conclude that
if the linear system
(A,B)
does not satisfies the Kalman condition, then the orbit
\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}(0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}})
is contained in
\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and cannot contain any neighbourhood of the origin
, as claimed.

In order to conclude the proof of the claim

“
\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}(0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}})
contains a neighbourhood of
0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
if and only if
\dim\Lie(\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)})_{q=0}=n
”

it remains to show that, for such a kind of set of vector fields, the Kalman condition is equivalent to the condition
\dim\Lie(\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)})_{q=0}=n
. For this, it suffices to notice that, due to the constancy of the vector fields
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
, in the set of all iterated Lie brackets
[X_{i_{1}},[X_{i_{2}},\ldots,[X_{i_{N-1}},X_{i_{N}}]\ldots]]
,
N\geq 2
, the only non trivial brackets are those having the form

\underset{\text{\rm$N-1$-times}}{\underbrace{[X_{0},[X_{0},\ldots,[X_{0},}}X_{j}]\ldots]]=\big(\big(\underset{\text{\rm$N-1$-times}}{\underbrace{A{\cdot}\ldots{\cdot}A}}\big)^{1}_{\ell}B_{j}^{\ell},\ldots,\big(\underset{\text{\rm$N-1$-times}}{\underbrace{A{\cdot}\ldots{\cdot}A}}\big)^{n}_{\ell}B^{\ell}_{j}\big)\ .

(4.3)

From this it follows immediately that
\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}=\Lie(\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)})\big|_{\overline{q}=0}
and this immediately gives the desired equality between the two dimensions.

5.
A proof of the Kalman Theorem,
based on the Chow-Rashewskiĭ Theorem

We give now our first new proof of the Kalman Theorem, which shows how that theorem can be obtained as a corollary of the Chow-Rashevkiĭ Theorem.

First of all, we stress the fact that the necessity part of Theorem
3.2
(or, equivalently, the fact that if the Kalman condition does not hold, then the set
\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)}_{\overline{q}=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}=\mathscr{O}^{(-A,B)}_{\overline{q}=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}
cannot contain any neighbourhood of the origin) is a consequence of the same linear algebra arguments considered at the end of §
4
. Indeed, if
\dim\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}=n^{\prime}<n
, by those arguments, there is no loss of generality if we assume that
\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}
is the space
\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}=\{q^{n^{\prime}+1}=\ldots=q^{N}=0\}
, that
A
has the form (
4.2
) and that all vectors
B_{\alpha}
,
1\leq\alpha\leq m
, are in the subspace
\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}
. If this is the case, one can immediately see that any solution of the linear system
(-A,B)
that starts from
0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is constrained to stay in
\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}
, so that
\mathscr{O}^{(-A,B)}_{\overline{q}=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}=\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)}_{\overline{q}=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\subset\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}
does not contain any neighbourhood of the origin.

Second, we point out that, in order to prove the sufficiency part of the Kalman Theorem, there is no loss of generality if we assume that
\mathscr{K}
is not only a neighbourhood of
0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, but is also
convex and symmetric. Indeed, if we use the notation
\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)\text{\rm p.c.}}_{\overline{q}=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)\text{\rm p.c.}}}_{\overline{q}=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}
for the sets of points that are controllable to
0_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}
by means of controls in
\mathscr{K}
and of controls in a subset
\widetilde{\mathscr{K}}
of
\mathscr{K}
, respectively, it is clear that
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)\text{\rm p.c.}}}_{\overline{q}=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\subset\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)\text{\rm p.c.}}_{\overline{q}=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}
. If in addition
\mathscr{K}
contains a neighbourhood of
0_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}
, then it certainly contains a subset
\widetilde{\mathscr{K}}
which is not only a neighbourhood of
0_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}
, but it also convex and symmetric (a ball, for example). Hence if we prove that the Kalman condition is sufficient for
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)\text{\rm p.c.}}}_{\overline{q}=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}
to contain a neighbourhood of the origin, we immediately get that the Kalman condition a fortiori is a sufficient condition for
\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)\text{\rm p.c.}}_{\overline{q}=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}
having that property.

Due this observation, the proof of the sufficiency part of the Kalman Theorem can be made under the stronger assumption that
\mathscr{K}
is open, convex, symmetric and contains the origin.
For such a proof we need the following

Proposition 5.1
.

If
\mathscr{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is open, convex, symmetric and contains the origin, then:

(1)

\mathcal{C}_{\overline{q}=0}^{(A,B)p.c.}
is symmetric and convex;

(2)

If
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the smallest (with respect to inclusion) linear subspace containing
\mathcal{C}_{\overline{q}=0}^{(A,B)p.c.}
,
then
\dim L\geq 1
and
\mathcal{C}_{\overline{q}=0}^{(A,B)p.c.}
contains a neighbourhood of
0
of the topology of
L
(considered as a topological subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
);

(3)

\dim L=\dim\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}(0)\geq\dim\Lie(\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)})_{\overline{q}=0}
.

Proof.

(1) is a well known property of linear system, which can be easily checked by just considering the classical integral representation formula for the solutions to (
1.1
) (see e.g.
[
10
, §II.2]
).

In order to check (2), note that, since
B\neq 0
, the set
\mathcal{C}_{\overline{q}=0}^{(A,B)p.c.}
contains at least a point different from
0
. This implies that
\dim L\geq 1
. Denoting
N=\dim L
, consider
N
points
q_{1},\ldots,q_{N}\in\mathcal{C}_{\overline{q}=0}^{(A,B)p.c.}
that are linearly independent (they exist because, otherwise,
L
would not be the smallest linear space containing
\mathcal{C}_{\overline{q}=0}^{(A,B)p.c.}
) and let
C
be the convex hull of the set
\{q_{1},-q_{1},q_{2},-q_{2},\ldots,q_{N},-q_{N}\}
. The interior of
C
in the topology of
L
is an open neighbourhood of the origin and it is contained in
\mathcal{C}_{\overline{q}=0}^{(A,B)p.c.}
because of (1).

We now prove (3). First of all we claim that each vector field
X_{\alpha}
, defined in (
4.1
), is tangent to
L
at all points of
\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)p.c.}_{\overline{q}=0}
.
In order to check this, for what concerns a vector field
X_{\alpha_{o}}
,
\alpha_{o}\neq 0
, suppose that the claim is not true, i.e. that
X_{\alpha_{o}}|_{q}\notin L
for some
q\in\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)p.c.}_{\overline{q}=0}
. Since
X_{\alpha_{o}}
has constant components, this implies that also
X_{\alpha_{o}}|_{\overline{q}=0}\notin L
. In this case, consider the differential problem
\dot{q}^{i}=-A^{i}_{j}q^{j}-\mu B_{\alpha_{o}}
,
q(0)=0
for some small constant value
\mu>0
and let
q(t)
be a solution to this problem,
hence a curve, which is tangent to the vector field
X_{0}(q)-\mu X_{\alpha_{o}}(q)
. Note that, in the linear system
(-A,B)
, this corresponds to the choice of the constant control

\mu e_{\alpha_{o}}
which, for small
\mu>0
, belongs to
\mathscr{K}
because it is a neighbourhood of the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, being
e_{\alpha_{o}}
the suitable element of its canonical basis.
Since
X_{\alpha_{o}}|_{\overline{q}=0}\notin L
and
X_{0}|_{\overline{q}=0}=0
, for small values of
t
the point
q(t)
is not in
L
. This is however impossible because all points of such a solution
q(t)
are in
\mathscr{O}^{(-A,-B)p.c.}_{\overline{q}=0}=\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)p.c.}_{\overline{q}=0}\subset L
. For what concerns the vector fields
X_{0}
(which has not constant components), the proof is different. As before, assume that the claim does not hold for
X_{0}
, i.e. that there is a
\overline{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)p.c.}_{\overline{q}=0}
such that
X_{0}|_{\overline{q}^{\prime}}
is not tangent to
L
. We may therefore consider

(i)

A solution
q(t)
of a differential problem
\dot{q}=-Aq-Bu(t)
,
q(0)=0
, that ends at
\overline{q}^{\prime}
and is determined by a piecewise constant control
u(t)
(it exists because
\overline{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)p.c.}_{\overline{q}=0}=\mathscr{O}^{(-A,-B)p.c.}_{\overline{q}=0}
);

(ii)

A solution
q(t)
to the differential problem
\dot{q}=-Aq
,
q(0)=\overline{q}^{\prime}
(such a solution is an integral curve of the vector field
X_{0}
and, since
X_{0}|_{\overline{q}^{\prime}}\notin L
, the points
q(t)
are not in
L
for small values of
t
; and again, it is an admissible curve for the controlled system
(-A,B)
because
0\in\mathscr{K}
).

Composing the curves in (i) and (ii), we get a curve which is entirely included in
\mathscr{O}^{(-A,-B)p.c.}_{\overline{q}=0}=\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)p.c.}_{\overline{q}=0}
, but also which is not entirely included in
L
contradicting the assumption
\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)p.c.}_{\overline{q}=0}\subset L
.

From the argument above, we see that all vector fields in the set
\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}
and, consequently, all vector fields in the spanned vector space
\widehat{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}
are tangent to
L
at the points of an open (in the topology of
L
) neighbourhood
\mathscr{U}\subset L
of
0
, which is entirely contained in
\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)p.c.}_{\overline{q}=0}
(it exists by (2)).Thus any integral curve
\sigma^{X}(s)
of a vector field
X
in
\widehat{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}
that starts from a point in
\mathscr{U}
stays in
\mathscr{U}
for small values of
s
.
Consequently, for any
q\in\mathscr{U}\subset\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)p.c.}_{\overline{q}=0}
, there exists an open neighbourhood of
q
of the intrinsic topology of
\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}(q)
which is included in
\mathscr{U}\subset L
. By Theorem
3.1
,
\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}(\overline{q}_{o}=0)
is an immersed submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of dimension greater than or equal to
\dim\Lie(\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}_{\overline{q}_{o}=0})
. Thus:

•

\dim\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}(q_{o}=0)\leq\dim L
because
\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}(q_{o}=0)
contains an open subset of its intrinsic topology included in
\mathscr{U}\subset L
;

•

\dim\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}(q_{o}=0)\geq\dim L
because
\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}}(q_{o}=0)
contains
\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)p.c.}_{\overline{q}=0}
which contains an open subset of
L
.

This implies that
\dim L=\dim\operatorname{Orb}^{\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}}(\overline{q}_{o}=0)\geq\dim\Lie(\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)})_{\overline{q}_{o}=0}
.
∎

We have now all ingredients to conclude the proof of the Kalman Theorem described at the beginning of this section. By
(
4.3
) and the remarks just before that equality, we know that

\dim\operatorname{Kalman}^{(A,B)}=n
if and only if
\dim\Lie(\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)})_{\overline{q}_{o}=0}=n
. Thus, by Proposition
5.1
(3), the linear subspace
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of claim (2) of that proposition is equal to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the set
\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)p.c.}_{\overline{q}=0}
is a symmetric, convex neighbourhood of
\overline{q}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular it is an open subset of
L=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Thus
if the Kalman condition holds, then
\mathcal{C}^{(A,B)p.c.}_{\overline{q}=0}
contains a neighbourhood of
0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
as we needed to prove.

Remark 5.2
.

We point out that a generic problem of controllability is a “forward-in-time problem” in the following sense: It consists of the query of points that can be considered as starting points for the controlled evolutions that reach a desired destination in a finite time
and moving just forward in time
. On the other hand, the points of an orbit of
\mathscr{F}^{(A,B)}
, as considered in the Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem, are points, which can be reached moving
both forward and backward in time
along the evolutions. This is the crucial difference between the two problems. In the above proof, the argument where the“moving-just-forward-in-time” constraint on the evolutions (i.e. the main difference between the controllability problems and the Chow-Rashevskiĭ type problems) is shown to be non effective for deriving the Kalman Theorem is when we prove that all the vector fields
X_{\alpha}
(and in particular, the vector field
X_{0}
) are tangent to
L
. Indeed this is equivalent to the fact that
C_{\overline{q}=0}^{(A,B)p.c.}
is a neighbourhood of
0
if and only if
C_{\overline{q}=0}^{(-A,B)p.c.}
is so and hence if and only if
C_{\overline{q}=0}^{(\pm A,B)p.c.}
is so as well. Note that
C_{\overline{q}=0}^{(\pm A,B)p.c.}
is precisely the set of the controllable points of the problem given by the controlled dynamics
\dot{q}=\sigma Aq+Bu
with controlling parameter
u=(u^{i})
together with the additional discrete parameter
\sigma\in\{-1,1\}
.

6.
Another proof of the Kalman Theorem,
based on general facts on flows of vector fields

We now give our second proof of the Kalman Theorem or, more precisely, only of the sufficiency part of that theorem. In fact we already pointed out that the necessity part is just a consequence of
a linear algebra argument, namely the same that proves the necessity part in the corollary of the Chow-Rashevskiĭ Theorem that we are considering in this paper.

This second proof is based on a translation of the control problem given by a linear system

into a corresponding equivalent problem on certain distinguished curves and flows of certain vector fields in the extended space-time

Theorem 6.5
.
