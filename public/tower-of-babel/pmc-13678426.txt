Socio-Demographic Inequalities in the Burden of Low Back Pain Across Development Strata: Sex-Specific, Age-Standardized Trends in Prevalence, Incidence and Disability from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023, 2003–2023
Authors: Mohammed M Alshehri, Mohammad Abu Shaphe, Rashid Ali Beg, Aafreen Aafreen, Shahul Hameed Pakkir Mohamed, G Shankar Ganesh, Abdur Raheem Khan, Monira I Aldhahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow back pain (LBP) is a leading contributor to non-fatal disability and rehabilitation needs worldwide. Monitoring trends across sociodemographic strata is important for understanding whether the disability burden is changing equitably across health systems.PurposeTo examine temporal trends and socio-demographic inequalities in the burden of LBP across Socio-demographic Index (SDI) groups from 2003 to 2023 using Global Burden of Disease Study 2023 estimates.MethodsGBD 2023 estimates for LBP prevalence, incidence and years lived with disability (YLDs) were analysed across High, High-middle, Middle, Low-middle and Low SDI groups. Age-standardized rates, all-age numbers, percentage changes and estimated annual percentage changes were calculated. Sex-specific, age-specific and inequality analyses were conducted. The change in all-age YLDs was also partitioned approximately into demographic (population growth and ageing) and epidemiological (age-standardized rate) components.ResultsAge-standardized prevalence, incidence, and YLD rates declined modestly across all SDI groups between 2003 and 2023. The greatest decline in age-standardized YLD rates occurred in High SDI countries (−6.2%), whereas Low-middle SDI countries showed the smallest reduction (−2.5%). Despite declining rates, all-age YLD numbers increased in every SDI group, with the largest increase in Low SDI group (+65.2%). In 2023, High SDI had the highest age-standardized YLD rate, followed by Low SDI. Female YLD rates exceeded male rates in all SDI groups, and age-specific YLD rates rose steadily with age. Demographic change alone would have increased YLDs by 28.4% in High SDI and by 51.6–73.9% in the other groups; falling age-standardized rates offset 28.1% of this demographic increase in High SDI but only 7.1–14.1% elsewhere.ConclusionThe age-standardized rates of LBP declined modestly, but the absolute disability burden increased substantially, particularly in lower-SDI settings. Persistent SDI, sex, and age inequalities indicate the need for scalable, gender-sensitive, and context-responsive rehabilitation services. Outside High SDI settings, improvements in age-standardized rates offset only a small share of demographically driven growth in disability.Keywords: low back pain, global burden of disease, socio-demographic Index, rehabilitation, years lived with disability, health inequalities

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) continues to be a major contributor to non-fatal health loss globally and consistently ranks as a leading cause of years lived with disability (YLDs).1,2 LBP is rarely fatal but has considerable consequences because of its effects on mobility, work participation, social functioning, independence, and quality of life.3,4 This condition also drives high demand for primary care, rehabilitation, workplace modification, and long-term self-management support.5,6 Thus, LBP is not only a clinical condition in rehabilitation systems, but also an indicator of musculoskeletal disability and the need for services at the population level.

Trend analysis is important because single-year burden estimates provide only a snapshot of population health. Age-standardized rates were examined for trends (improving, worsening, or stable) and whether these trends were similar across the development strata.7,8 This distinction is especially important in rehabilitation planning. A decline in age-standardized rates may reflect a slight improvement in the relative population risk, while an increase in all-age numbers may reflect increasing service volume due to population growth and aging. Health systems must consider both rates and numbers,9,10 particularly for LBP, in which the burden of disability accumulates over long periods and the demand for conservative, non-surgical, and rehabilitation-based care may increase even as age-standardized rates decrease.5,11

The GBD framework provides comparable estimates of prevalence, incidence, years lived with disability (YLDs) and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) across locations, age groups, sexes and time periods.7,8,12–14 Because mortality directly attributable to LBP is negligible, its DALYs consist almost entirely of YLDs, a measure of non-fatal health loss that is directly relevant to rehabilitation.1,7

The Socio-demographic Index (SDI), a composite of income per capita, educational attainment and fertility, allows burden to be compared across development strata.7,8 The association between LBP burden and SDI may not be linear. High-SDI settings may carry a substantial burden because of population ageing, sedentary lifestyles, obesity and higher detection or reporting,1,3,15 whereas low-SDI settings may do so because of physically demanding work, limited ergonomic protection, limited access to early rehabilitation and shortages in the rehabilitation workforce.6,16,17

This analysis is even more relevant in light of current global health priorities. The World Health Organization has identified rehabilitation as an essential health service and called for stronger integration of rehabilitation into health systems.16,18 The WHO guidelines for chronic primary LBP also highlight non-surgical management in primary and community care settings, including education, exercise, and self-management approaches.5 These recommendations are particularly relevant in settings where access to specialist care is limited, and scalable models of rehabilitation are required.

LBP should also be considered alongside other musculoskeletal conditions. The burden of osteoarthritis has been increasing in recent GBD analyses, partly reflecting ageing and obesity,19,20 so the demand on rehabilitation systems may grow even if age-standardized LBP rates decline.

Previous GBD studies have described the global, regional, and national burden of LBP and projected further increases in the affected populations.1 However, new analyses using SDI and the latest GBD 2023 estimates are needed to clarify whether age-standardized rates and absolute burden are changing similarly across development strata. Therefore, this study aimed to examine trends in LBP prevalence, incidence, and YLDs across five SDI groups from 2003 to 2023. It also explored sex inequalities, age-specific patterns and formal high-SDI versus low-SDI inequality metrics, and estimated how much of the change in absolute burden reflected demographic rather than epidemiological change. The aim was to produce rehabilitation-relevant evidence on the changing LBP burden across global development settings and identify implications for equitable, context-responsive rehabilitation planning.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This study was a secondary analysis of publicly available data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2023. The GBD framework produces standardized disease burden estimates for countries, territories, age groups, sexes, and time periods using a comprehensive modelling approach that combines multiple epidemiological data sources.21–24

Data were obtained from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) GBD Results Tool and corresponding downloadable datasets (accessed on June 01, 2026). The analysis presented here was confined to the cause “Low back pain” and provides estimates of prevalence, incidence, years lived with disability (YLDs), and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) by Socio-demographic Index (SDI) groups.

The period 2003–2023 was selected a priori to describe the two most recent decades of change, the horizon most relevant to current rehabilitation service and workforce planning. Longer-term trends from 1990 have been described in earlier GBD analyses, which reported that global age-standardized prevalence and YLD rates of LBP fell by 10.4% and 10.5%, respectively, between 1990 and 2020.1 The present analysis examines whether this modest decline has continued in the most recent GBD round and whether it has been shared equally across development strata.

Ethical Consideration

This study used publicly available, de-identified, aggregate secondary data from the GBD database. No individual participant data were accessed, and there was no contact with human participants. In accordance with the Indian Council of Medical Research National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants (2017), research using anonymised data available in the public domain involves less than minimal risk and is exempt from ethics committee review. Ethical approval and informed consent were therefore not required for this study.25

Socio-Demographic Index Groupings

The Socio-demographic Index (SDI) is a summary measure developed for the GBD framework that indicates the position of countries or other geographic areas on the spectrum of development. The SDI is based on the average income per person, educational attainment, and fertility rates. Specifically, it is a composite indicator of lag-distributed income per capita, average educational attainment among individuals older than 15 years, and total fertility rate among women younger than 25 years.22 The SDI ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating higher levels of socio-demographic development.

For this analysis, burden estimates were stratified according to the five Socio-demographic Index (SDI) categories defined in the GBD framework: High SDI, High-middle SDI, Middle SDI, Low-middle SDI, and Low SDI. These categories represent different levels of sociodemographic development and were used to assess variations in low back pain burden and inequalities across development strata. We investigated temporal patterns, disability burden, and inequalities in relation to different levels of income, education, and fertility across the SDI groups.22 These categories were also used to assess inequalities in LBP burden at different levels of sociodemographic development. SDI group estimates were obtained as aggregates produced by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation. Because the GBD Results tool does not report which countries contribute to each SDI group in each year, we were unable to examine whether the movement of individual countries between groups over the study period influenced group-level trends, or to conduct a country-level sensitivity analysis of such movement. As each group aggregates many countries, the effect is likely to be small, but it cannot be excluded.

Measures

Four epidemiological indicators were analyzed: incidence, prevalence, years lived with disability (YLDs), and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). Incidence is the number of new cases of LBP occurring over a defined period, whereas prevalence is the number of people living with LBP at a particular point in time.12,13 YLDs combine disease prevalence with disability weights reflecting the severity of health states to capture non-fatal health loss.9,14 DALYs are a summary measure of population health loss that combines years lived with disability and years of life lost due to premature mortality.9 For LBP, the DALYs are driven predominantly by YLDs because mortality directly attributable to LBP is negligible.1,7 GBD disability weights are derived from population surveys conducted in several countries and are applied uniformly to all locations, so they do not differ between SDI groups.14 Differences in YLD rates between SDI groups therefore reflect differences in prevalence and in the modelled severity distribution rather than differences in the valuation of health states.

The age-standardized rates (ASRs) per 100,000 population for both sexes combined from 2003 to 2023 were extracted for trend analysis. All-age estimates were used to estimate the absolute changes in the burden. Sex-specific age-standardized estimates were used to assess sex inequalities, and age-specific YLD rates were used to characterize the age distribution of the disability burden.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed to summarize the temporal trends in the low back pain burden across the SDI groups. Percentage change estimates were used to quantify changes in incidence, prevalence, years lived with disability (YLDs), and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) from 2003 to 2023. Both age-standardized rates (ASRs) and all-age counts were examined to distinguish changes in relative burden from those in absolute burden.

Temporal trends in age-standardized rates were assessed using the Estimated Annual Percentage Change (EAPC), a commonly used metric for evaluating long-term trends in burden-of-disease studies.26,27 EAPC was derived from log-linear regression models in which the natural logarithm of the age-standardized rate was regressed on calendar year. The regression coefficient was used to estimate the average annual percentage change in the burden over the study period. A positive EAPC value indicates an increasing trend, whereas a negative EAPC value indicates a decreasing trend in the rates. The statistical significance of temporal changes was assessed using the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

Age-standardized YLD rates were compared across SDI categories to assess sociodemographic inequalities.26 Absolute inequality was measured as the difference in age-standardized YLD rates between the high-SDI and low-SDI groups, whereas relative inequality was measured as the ratio of high-SDI to low-SDI rates.28,29 Absolute and relative inequality measures were compared between 2003 and 2023 to determine whether disparities in the burden widened or narrowed over the study period.

Sex-specific analyses were performed using age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and YLD estimates for males and females. Female-to-male ratios were calculated to assess sex-related inequalities in the distribution of the burden. Age-specific YLD rates were also used to characterise patterns of disability across the lifespan and identify age groups contributing most substantially to rehabilitation needs.

To estimate how much of the change in all-age YLDs reflected demographic rather than epidemiological change, the ratio of all-age YLD counts in 2023 to those in 2003 was divided by the corresponding ratio of age-standardized YLD rates. Because age-standardized rates remove the effects of population size and age structure, this quotient approximates the change in YLDs that would have occurred through population growth and ageing alone had age-standardized rates remained constant (the demographic component), while the change in the age-standardized rate approximates the epidemiological component. The share of the demographic increase offset by falling rates was calculated as (demographic factor − observed count ratio) / (demographic factor − 1). This is an approximate partition rather than a formal decomposition: it does not separate population growth from population ageing, and it depends on the GBD standard population used for age standardization.

Results

Trends in Age-Standardized Low Back Pain Burden Across SDI Groups

Between 2003 and 2023, the age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and YLD rates decreased across all SDI groups, however, the magnitude of the decrease varied considerably between the development strata (Table 1).

High SDI (−5.5%) showed the largest relative reduction in incidence, followed by Low SDI (−5.0%) and High-middle SDI (−4.6%). Smaller reductions were observed in the Middle SDI (−2.9%) and Low-middle SDI (−2.9%). EAPC estimates indicated decreasing incidence trends in all SDI groups, with High SDI showing the steepest annual decline (EAPC −0.22%; 95% CI −0.25 to −0.19) (Table 1).

The prevalence followed a similar pattern. The greatest declines in age-standardized prevalence rates were observed in those with a High SDI (−6.0%), while the smallest declines were observed in those with a low-middle SDI and Middle SDI (−2.6% each). The EAPC for prevalence ranged from −0.25% (High SDI) to −0.06% (Low-middle SDI), indicating a relatively stable prevalence burden in the latter group (Table 1).

Age-standardized YLD rates also decreased across all SDI groups. The largest reduction was observed in the High SDI group (−6.2%), followed by low SDI (−5.0%) and High-middle SDI groups (−4.8%). In comparison, the smallest decrease was seen in the low-middle SDI (−2.5%), and the EAPC confidence interval included zero, indicating little change in the disability burden over the study period (Table 1).

Temporal patterns of age-standardized YLD rates are shown in Figure 1. Decreasing trends were observed in all SDI groups, however, differences between the development strata persisted. The high SDI consistently had the highest age-standardized YLD rates, whereas the high-middle SDI generally had the lowest rates throughout the study period (Figure 1). Age-standardized prevalence followed a similar pattern (Figure 2). In all groups the decline in age-standardized prevalence and YLD rates was steepest after 2020; because the most recent years in each GBD round are informed by fewer primary data, this late change should be interpreted with caution.

Socio-Demographic Inequalities in the Burden of Disability

Despite the overall improvement in age-standardized rates, considerable socio-demographic inequalities persisted throughout the study period.

The age-standardized YLD rate was highest in the High SDI (926.3 per 100,000 population), followed by the Low SDI (809.2), Low-middle SDI (765.2), Middle SDI (743.2), and High-middle SDI groups (680.6) in 2023 (Table 1). These findings suggest a non-linear relationship between socio-demographic development and disability burden, with a high burden observed at both extremes of the development spectrum.

A comparison of inequality measures between 2003 and 2023 demonstrated a modest narrowing of disparities between High SDI and Low SDI groups (Table 4). The absolute difference in age-standardized YLD rates decreased from 135.2 per 100,000 population in 2003 to 117.1 per 100,000 population in 2023. The relative inequality ratio also decreased from 1.16 to 1.14. Although these findings indicate some narrowing of the gap, substantial disparities remain evident in 2023.

Changes in Absolute Burden

In contrast to the declining age-standardized rates, all-age burden increased markedly in all SDI groups during the study period (Table 2).

The largest increase in YLDs was observed in Low SDI (+65.2%), followed by the Low-middle SDI (+50.3%), Middle SDI (+48.4%), High-middle SDI (+44.3%), and High SDI (+20.4%). The prevalence and incidence estimates increased in a similar manner.

The prevalence of LBP increased by 64.7% in Low SDI and by 50.4% in Low-middle SDI, compared with 21.4% in High SDI. Similarly, the incidence increased by 64.2% in the Low SDI group and by 48.3% in the Low-middle SDI, compared with 20.7% in the High SDI (Table 2).

The approximate partition showed that demographic change alone would have increased YLDs by 28.4% in the High SDI group and by 51.6% to 73.9% in the other groups, with the largest demographic effect in the Low SDI group (Table 5). Falling age-standardized rates offset 28.1% of this demographic increase in the High SDI group, but only 14.1% in the High-middle SDI group, 11.8% in the Low SDI group, 7.6% in the Middle SDI group and 7.1% in the Low-middle SDI group. Outside High SDI settings, improvements in age-standardized rates therefore did little to moderate the growth in absolute burden.

These findings indicate that while the relative burden decreased slightly, the absolute number of people requiring care, rehabilitation, and support increased substantially, particularly in lower-SDI countries. The distinction between age-standardized rates and absolute burden is important for developing rehabilitation systems. Health services often use age-standardized measures to monitor population health trends; however, rehabilitation workforce needs are determined by the absolute number of people requiring intervention. The large increases in prevalence and YLD counts observed in Low SDI and Low-middle SDI settings indicate that the demand for rehabilitation is likely to continue increasing despite improvements in age-standardized metrics. This is particularly relevant in settings where the rehabilitation workforce density remains low and access to physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and multidisciplinary rehabilitation services is limited. Therefore, strengthening rehabilitation capacity, integrating rehabilitation into primary healthcare, and expanding community-based and digitally supported rehabilitation services may be necessary to meet future demands.

Sex Inequalities

Sex-based inequalities were observed across all SDI groups in 2023 (Table 3).

Female age-standardized incidence rates were higher than male rates in all SDI categories, and the female-to-male incidence ratios ranged from 1.42 (High SDI) to 1.83 (Low-middle SDI). Similar patterns were observed for prevalence and YLDs.

The female-to-male ratios for YLDs ranged from 1.43 in High SDI to 1.90 in Low-middle SDI (Table 3). The greatest sex disparity was observed in the Low-middle SDI, whereas the smallest disparity was observed in the High SDI. In 2023, female age-standardized YLD rates were higher than male rates across all SDI groups.

Age-Specific Disability Burden

Age-specific analyses revealed an increasing disability burden with advancing age in all SDI groups (Figure 3).

YLD rates were relatively low during childhood and young adulthood but increased steadily from middle age. Across all SDI strata, YLD rates rose steadily with age and were highest in the oldest, open-ended age group (70 years and older); because this group combines several age bands, it indicates a continued rise with age rather than a distinct peak. Although the shape of the age-disability curve was generally similar across the SDI groups, YLD rates were consistently higher in the High-SDI and Low-SDI settings than in the Middle-SDI and High-middle-SDI groups. From about 50 years of age, rates in the Low SDI group approached, and at 55–59 years matched, those in the High SDI group.

These findings suggest that population ageing is likely to remain a major driver of future rehabilitation demand and will contribute substantially to the increasing absolute burden observed across all developmental strata.

Discussion

This study examined trends in the burden of low back pain (LBP) across five SDI groups and sociodemographic inequalities in LBP burden from 2003 to 2023 using GBD 2023 estimates. Age-standardized incidence, prevalence and YLD rates declined modestly across all SDI groups, yet the absolute number of affected people rose substantially in every stratum, and the approximate partition indicates that, outside High SDI settings, falling rates offset little of the growth driven by population size and ageing. Considerable sociodemographic inequalities persisted throughout the study period, and the disability burden remained consistently higher among females and older adults. Although the relative burden of LBP may be improving, the demand for rehabilitation services is therefore likely to continue to increase worldwide.

Significance of Trend Analysis for Rehabilitation Planning

Trend analyses provide information beyond single-year burden estimates by indicating whether the population burden is increasing, decreasing, or stable, and whether these changes occur equitably across population groups.7,8,30 Such information is important for rehabilitation because workforce planning and service delivery depend on future demand rather than current burden alone.31 Age-standardized rates assess changes independent of demographic shifts, whereas all-age numbers reflect the absolute number of individuals requiring care.9,10 In the present study, age-standardized rates declined modestly, but all-age burden increased substantially, suggesting that demographic changes are likely to be a major driver of future rehabilitation demand.

What are the Reasons for the Decline in Age-Standardized Rates?

The observed reductions in age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and disability rates were generally consistent with recent GBD analyses showing modest declines in the LBP burden across several regions.1,15 Although causal inferences cannot be drawn from ecological data, several plausible explanations may account for these findings. These modest declines are consistent with the longer-term GBD 2021 analysis, which reported reductions of 10.4% in age-standardized prevalence and 10.5% in age-standardized YLD rates between 1990 and 2020,1 suggesting that the slow downward trend has continued into the most recent GBD round.

Musculoskeletal health has gained increasing prominence globally over the past two decades, with the wider dissemination of evidence-based recommendations emphasizing physical activity, exercise therapy, and self-management.5,11,32 Improvements in awareness of occupational risk factors, workplace health initiatives, and health literacy may also have contributed to modest reductions in age-standardized burden.33,34 However, the ecological nature of the present analysis precludes direct attribution of observed trends to specific interventions.

However, this decline was modest, suggesting that preventive and therapeutic strategies have not fundamentally altered the global burden of LBP, which remains highly prevalent despite increasing awareness and the wider availability of treatment options.34

Why Does This Burden Continue to Increase?

From a rehabilitation perspective, the most important finding was the substantial increase in all-age burden across all SDI groups. Population growth, particularly in lower-SDI settings, and global population aging are likely to be major contributors.35,36 Disability burden increased progressively with age, consistent with previous epidemiological studies.1,20 The approximate partition supports this interpretation: in the Low SDI group, population growth and ageing alone would have increased YLDs by almost three quarters, and falling rates offset only about one eighth of that increase (Table 5). Consequently, even modest improvements in age-standardized rates are unlikely to offset the increasing rehabilitation demand.

In older adults, LBP frequently coexists with osteoarthritis, frailty, sarcopenia, and multimorbidity, contributing to activity limitations and participation restrictions in daily life. Therefore, rehabilitation approaches should focus not only on symptom reduction but also on maintaining mobility, independence, and community participation.

Understanding the SDI Gradient: Why Do High-SDI and Low-SDI Settings Experience Greater Burden?

The findings suggest a possible “double burden of development”. In High-SDI settings, disability burden may be influenced by population aging, multimorbidity, obesity, sedentary lifestyles, and greater recognition of disability.36–38 Conversely, Low-SDI settings may experience a greater burden due to physically demanding occupations, delayed care-seeking, and limited access to rehabilitation services.16,17,33,39,40 Although the mechanisms differ, both pathways contribute to substantial disability burden and rehabilitation need.

Although the present analysis did not model risk factors by SDI, published GBD estimates help to ground these explanations. In the GBD 2021 analysis, 38.8% of global LBP YLDs were attributable to occupational ergonomic factors, smoking and high body-mass index.1 Exposure to occupational ergonomic factors is concentrated in settings where agriculture, manual labour and informal work predominate,33 whereas high body-mass index and population ageing are more pronounced in higher-SDI populations.21,36,38 At the same time, the need for rehabilitation is greatest, and access to it poorest, in lower-resource settings.6,40,41 These patterns are consistent with a burden driven by ageing- and weight-related exposures at the upper end of the development spectrum and by occupational load and limited access to care at the lower end. Formal testing of this interpretation would require SDI-stratified analysis of risk-attributable burden and of rehabilitation coverage, which we identify as a priority for future research.

The non-linear association observed across SDI strata indicates that socio-demographic development does not necessarily translate into lower disability burden. High-middle SDI settings demonstrated the lowest age-standardized YLD rates, suggesting that demographic structure, healthcare access, and socioeconomic development may interact in complex ways to affect YLD rates. Future studies are required to better understand these relationships.

These findings support the need for context-responsive rehabilitation strategies to be developed. High-SDI settings may benefit from interventions promoting healthy ageing and self-management, whereas Low-SDI settings may benefit from improved access to rehabilitation services, workforce expansion, and stronger integration of rehabilitation into primary healthcare systems.

Large Socio-Demographic Inequalities Remain

Although inequality measures showed modest narrowing between High-SDI and Low-SDI groups, substantial disparities remained in 2023. These findings suggest that reductions in burden have not been accompanied by comparable reductions in socio-demographic inequalities. Consistent with the WHO Rehabilitation 2030 initiative, future efforts should focus not only on reducing overall burden but also on improving equitable access to prevention, rehabilitation, and long-term support services.17,41

Sex Differences in the Burden of Low Back Pain

Consistent with previous epidemiological studies, females experienced higher incidence, prevalence, and disability burden than males across all SDI strata.42,43 Females experienced the greatest disadvantage in Low-middle SDI settings, where female YLD rates were almost twice those observed in males. The size of the sex gap varied with development: the female-to-male ratio of age-standardized YLD rates was 1.43 in the High SDI group but between 1.70 and 1.90 in the other four groups (Table 3).

Several explanations have been proposed for these differences. Biological factors, hormonal influences, differences in pain sensitivity, and sex-specific musculoskeletal adaptations may contribute to the higher prevalence of pain among women.44 However, social and environmental determinants are also likely to play important roles. Women are often exposed to greater unpaid caregiving responsibilities, domestic work, and barriers to healthcare.45 These factors may be particularly relevant in lower-resource settings where gender inequities remain substantial. These findings support the need for gender-sensitive rehabilitation approaches that address both biological and social determinants of disability.

Several features of lower-SDI settings may widen this gap. Women in these settings more often combine physically demanding agricultural and domestic work with unpaid caregiving, face greater financial, geographic and social barriers to health care and rehabilitation, and have less social protection when they are unable to work.40,45 Because the SDI itself incorporates fertility,22 higher fertility in lower-SDI settings may also contribute through the physical demands of pregnancy and caring for young children. The present data do not allow these explanations to be tested directly, and sex-disaggregated data on occupation, caregiving and access to rehabilitation would be needed to do so.

Comparison with Other Musculoskeletal Disorders

The present findings should be interpreted within the broader context of musculoskeletal health, and the contrasting trajectories of LBP and osteoarthritis illustrate why. Between 1990 and 2020, the global age-standardized YLD rate for LBP fell by 10.5%,1 whereas that for osteoarthritis rose by 9.5% and the number of people with osteoarthritis more than doubled.19 The divergence is plausibly explained by differences in determinants. Osteoarthritis is closely tied to ageing and high body-mass index, which contributed an estimated 20.4% of its burden, and it has no disease-modifying treatment,19,20,46 whereas LBP is more episodic and a larger share of its burden is linked to occupational exposures and smoking.1,33 For rehabilitation systems, the two conditions compound one another: they frequently coexist in older adults, both are managed with overlapping exercise-based and self-management approaches, and the rising burden of osteoarthritis will add to the demand already created by the growing absolute burden of LBP.

Taken together, these observations suggest that rehabilitation systems will face increasing challenges in managing multiple chronic musculoskeletal conditions simultaneously. Therefore, future rehabilitation planning should consider scalable interventions that address common risk factors such as physical inactivity, obesity, multimorbidity, and functional decline.

Implications for Rehabilitation

These findings highlight the need for rehabilitation systems to prepare for increasing demand despite modest improvements in age-standardized burden. Priorities include strengthening the rehabilitation workforce, integrating rehabilitation into primary healthcare, expanding community-based services, and increasing the use of digitally supported care models. Particular attention should be directed towards older adults, women, and populations in lower-resource settings.

Strengths and Limitations

This study had several strengths. It used the most recent GBD 2023 estimates and examined trends over a 20-year period across all SDI strata, using a standardized methodology. The study also considered both age-standardized rates and all-age counts, allowing distinction between changes in relative burden and changes in absolute burden. Furthermore, the inclusion of formal inequality measures, sex-specific analyses, and age-specific analyses provided a more comprehensive understanding of rehabilitation needs across different population groups.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. The analysis was based on modelled GBD estimates rather than primary epidemiological data, so the findings depend on the quality and availability of source data and on the assumptions underlying GBD modelling. Because each new GBD round revises modelled estimates for all historical years using updated data and methods, the reported 2003–2023 trend reflects GBD 2023’s current back-calculated estimates rather than what earlier GBD rounds reported for those years, and the steeper decline after 2020 should be interpreted cautiously because recent years are informed by fewer primary data. The ecological design precludes causal inference about the determinants of the observed trends, and risk-attributable burden was not modelled by SDI. SDI categories are broad and may conceal substantial heterogeneity between countries within the same group; the composition of each group could not be examined over time, and disability weights are applied uniformly across locations, so between-group differences in YLDs reflect prevalence and modelled severity rather than locally valued disability. The partition of change in absolute burden is approximate and does not separate population growth from ageing. Finally, although the study identified inequalities in burden, it could not directly evaluate inequalities in access to rehabilitation services, workforce distribution or quality of care.

Conclusion

Low back pain remains one of the leading causes of disability worldwide across all levels of socio-demographic development. Between 2003 and 2023, age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and disability rates declined modestly across all SDI groups. However, despite these improvements in relative burden, the absolute number of people affected increased substantially, particularly in lower-SDI settings.

The burden of LBP was not evenly distributed across development strata. Persistent socio-demographic inequalities were observed, with the greatest disability burden occurring in High-SDI and Low-SDI settings, likely through different underlying mechanisms. Females and older adults consistently experienced greater disability burden, identifying important target populations for prevention and rehabilitation interventions.

From a rehabilitation perspective, these findings highlight the need for equitable, scalable, and context-responsive rehabilitation services. Addressing the growing global burden of LBP will require strengthening rehabilitation workforce capacity, integrating rehabilitation into primary healthcare, expanding community-based and digitally supported service models, and addressing inequalities in access to care. Future rehabilitation policies should aim not only to reduce overall disability burden but also to narrow persistent disparities across socio-demographic groups.
