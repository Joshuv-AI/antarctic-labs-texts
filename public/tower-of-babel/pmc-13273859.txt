Large language models for generating longitudinal synthetic data in low-risk pregnancy care
Authors: Gonçalo Pinto, Vítor Crista, Regina Silva, Bruno Costa, Filipe Andrade Bernardi, Rute Almeida, Julio Souza, Alberto Freitas, Goreti Marreiros, Luís Conceição
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article describes an obstetric dataset covering the full continuum of care of 5000 synthetic low-risk pregnant women, from preconception to post-birth follow-up, generated using a large language model with zero-shot prompting. It includes trimester-specific clinical measurements, such as gestational weight, hemoglobin levels, glucose testing, anemia markers, and body mass index, as well as variables intrinsic to perinatal care, including preconception characteristics, antenatal consultations, and delivery outcomes.The dataset was generated through iterative prompt engineering. An initial prompt defined the longitudinal schema, essential variables, and domain-specific generation rules. The output was assessed by an expert panel in the field of General and Family Medicine and Data Science. Successive refinements incorporated expert feedback to improve plausibility, auditability, and overall consistency. After generation, the dataset underwent rule-based and plausibility-oriented validation procedures focused on internal statistical coherence and clinical assumptions’ assurance.In addition to mitigating data access constraints and privacy issues, the dataset enables the reconstruction of longitudinal obstetric care pathways for simulation studies. It can also be reused for methodological development, benchmarking, and educational machine learning experiments. However, it was not designed to directly support clinical decision-making and clinical model deployment.Keywords: Obstetrics, Synthetic patients, Artificial intelligence, Tabular data, Healthcare

Background

Access to high-quality data is paramount for advancing research and developing Artificial Intelligence (AI) models in healthcare. However, real-world data are often difficult to obtain due to institutional barriers, privacy regulations, and ethical concerns [1,2].

Synthetic data generation has emerged as a privacy-preserving alternative by enabling artificial datasets that preserve key domain and statistical properties while mitigating ethical risks [3,4]. In healthcare, synthetic data supports simulation and prediction studies, hypothesis testing, methodologies and algorithm exploration, epidemiology and public health research, software development and testing, and education [5].

Recently, large language models (LLMs) have offered data generation capabilities in zero or few-shot settings by encoding domain-specific knowledge through prompting [6]. In fact, ChatGPT, a distinguished LLM, has demonstrated utility in synthetic healthcare data generation [7].

In this article, we present a dataset of longitudinal pregnancy care trajectories for 5,000 synthetic low-risk women generated using a large language model. Pregnancy care trajectories represent a suitable use case due to well-defined clinical pathways and established guidelines for low-risk populations [[8], [9], [10]].

This dataset is provided in tabular format and contains 5,000 synthetic patient records representing longitudinal low-risk pregnancy care trajectories, thereby not containing any real patient information. Files can be freely downloaded from the Zenodo repository using the link provided in the specifications table. No request or authorization is required to access the data.

The dataset contains both base variables (e.g., laboratory measurements) and derived variables (e.g., diagnostic flags), which are computed according to explicit derivation rules detailed in Sheet 7 (Table 1). Binary variables are encoded using a 0/1 scheme, while categorical variables adhere to restricted value sets to ensure internal consistency. Date type variables are formatted according to the “yyyy-mm-dd" standard.

Temporal consistency is ensured through variables specified at clinically appropriate stages. Trimester-specific variables are consistent with their corresponding gestational periods, while pregnancy outcome variables are restricted to delivery and postpartum stages. Moreover, repeated measurements (e.g., maternal weight per trimester or breastfeeding status) collected throughout antenatal care enable reconstruction of individual patient trajectories over time. Additionally, the dataset incorporates realistic variability through controlled distributions, including structured missing values in non-critical variables (missing not at random), variations in clinical measurements, and differences in adherence-related variables. Furthermore, clinical and temporal constraints were applied to enhance the coherence of individual trajectories.

Overall distributions regarding pregnancy outcomes, including pregnancy complications such as gestational diabetes and hypertension, as well as demographics, clinical, and anthropometric variables, were constrained to be clinically plausible and compatible with low-risk populations (Fig. 2). In obstetrics, low-risk pregnancy is the one associated with no identified risk factors for the mother or in the fetus during antenatal care [13]. However, the model was induced to include records reporting gestational diabetes and hypertension, as these conditions may appear in low-risk populations at lower rates [14,15]. Examples of established and clinically plausible benchmark values for outcome prediction and simulation tasks supported by the dataset include: preterm birth rates ranging between 4 to 16% [16]; cesarean delivery rates aligned with the recommended 10-15% benchmark [17]; share of pregnant women aged 35 or older is approximately 20% [18]; prevalence rates of approximately 5% for gestational diabetes [19] and between 5% and 10% for gestational hypertension [20]; preeclampsia complicating approximately 2 to 8% of all pregnancies worldwide [21]; and macrosomia prevalence estimated at approximately 12% of normal pregnancies globally, although reported rates vary substantially across countries, ranging between 1.6% to 28% of all births [22].

Similarly, to improve statistical coherence, selected correlations and dependency patterns among variables were explicitly incorporated into the synthetic population [e.g., systolic (SBP) and diastolic (DBP) blood pressure measurements at 29 weeks are moderately correlated with gestational hypertension] (Fig. 3). Additionally, BMI (Body Mass Index) and OGTT (Oral Glucose Tolerance Test) were included as relevant clinical variables in the analysis.

Key correlations in the synthetic obstetric dataset.

Furthermore, established clinical associations between pregnancy outcomes and major maternal risk factors can be evaluated when using the dataset in regression analysis. Examples of epidemiological benchmark patterns that can be reproduced include: an approximately 2-fold increased risk of gestational diabetes amongst obese women [23] and amongst women aged 35 years or older compared with those younger than 25 years [24]; an approximately 1.6- to 1.8-fold increased risk of gestational hypertension and a 1.6- to 1.7-fold increased risk of preeclampsia for every 5-unit increase in BMI [25]; 2- to 3-fold higher odds of macrosomia amongst obese women [26]; and a 4- to 5-fold increased risk of preterm delivery amongst women who develop preeclampsia [27].

The dataset is fully synthetic and does not contain any real patient information. It is intended to serve educational purposes and support methodological research in synthetic data generation and obstetrics, machine learning models development, simulation and other exploratory analyses. FAIR principles were adopted to ensure that data are findable by means of standardized metadata, accessible via open resources, interoperable through shared formats (e.g., CSV) and clinical vocabularies, and reusable for both humans and machines [12].

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Synthetic data generation was performed using the ChatGPT web interface. During the generation process, the interface displayed the model identifier “GPT-5.3”, which is therefore recorded and reported in this study. Generation was conducted under default platform settings without API access, explicit hyperparameter specification, seed control, temperature adjustment, top-p configuration, or programmatic implementation. ChatGPT’s promising capabilities to produce and handle synthetic medical records at the patient level has been described elsewhere in the literature across distinct clinical domains [7,28,29].

The generation process was conducted using a structured prompt engineering approach, in which prompts were iteratively refined across four stages (Prompt 1–4) based on feedback from health and data science experts. Full original prompts are available in the Supplementary Material 1 (in Portuguese). At each iteration, experts evaluated data quality by checking for overall errors and providing guidance across key dimensions, namely:

Initial prompt design (Prompt 1)

The model was initially prompted with a list of essential variables, defined according to the National Low-Risk Pregnancy Surveillance Program [30] from the Portuguese National Health Service. Instructions required the LLM to act as a domain expert, and encode rules for low-risk pregnancies, such as visits and event patterns, plausible clinical parameters and outcomes ranges, and compliance with mandatory known relationships between variables. No real ground truth or patient data were used, thereby limiting the data generation process to a zero-shot setting.

Inclusion criteria constrained data generation to low-risk profiles. ChatGPT output was designed to include the main dataset in tabular format, either as CSV or Excel (XLSX) file including a self-explainable data dictionary, rules governing data generation, clinical assumptions considered, validation report and data limitations. Prompt refinement following first expert feedback (Prompt 2)

Following a first expert review for general errors and inconsistencies, the prompt was refined to incorporate expert feedback and include explicit derivation rules, stricter clinical reasoning, and improve pregnancy trajectory variability. Main prompt modifications can be summarized as follows:

(i)Explicit derivation of variables: ChatGPT was required to add binary flag variables to be systematically derived from underlying variables, e.g., EPDS flag indicating possible depression equals 1 when the underlying EPDS score is 12 or higher, Goodwin score categorization (0–2 low; 3–6 medium; ≥7 high), standard folic acid intake equal to 400 µg, or 5000 µg only if BMI ≥ 30.

(ii)Enhanced clinical reasoning: mutual exclusivity rules were introduced at this stage to improve clinical plausibility and coherence, e.g. indication of pre-existing diabetes excluding gestational diabetes. Detailed epidemiological associations were explicitly included by considering the relationship between the exposure to certain risks with specific conditions or events, namely: High BMI associated with higher risk of gestational diabetes and hypertension; Advanced maternal age associated with higher risk of gestational diabetes, hypertension, premature birth, cesarean delivery, and chromosomal abnormalities; Gestational diabetes associated with higher risk of labor induction or cesarean delivery; Preeclampsia associated with higher risk of premature birth and cesarean delivery; Intrauterine growth restriction associated with higher risk of low birth weight.

Preconception visit date is also constrained to have missing values, considering that the share of women receiving preconception care remains inadequate and about 40% of pregnancies do not receive the necessary services before pregnancy, according to the World Health Organization [31]. Variables with missing values thereby included preconception visit date (MR=72.5%), OGTT results at fasting and at 1 h and 2 h following glucose solution intake (MR=1.9%), cervical cytology status (MR=5.1%), pelvic floor status (MR=4%), folic acid dosage (MR= 8.1%), and AUDIT-C test results (MR=6.7%). Further variability was modelled by including an estimated 5 to 10% rate of non-adherence to potassium iodide supplementation, as well as minor administrative inconsistencies that do not compromise clinical coherence (e.g., cervical cytology status may occasionally be left blank for patients who have not undergone the test, despite the admission of a “Not performed” category).

Preconception visit date is also constrained to have missing values, considering that the share of women receiving preconception care remains inadequate and about 40% of pregnancies do not receive the necessary services before pregnancy, according to the World Health Organization [31]. Variables with missing values thereby included preconception visit date (MR=72.5%), OGTT results at fasting and at 1 h and 2 h following glucose solution intake (MR=1.9%), cervical cytology status (MR=5.1%), pelvic floor status (MR=4%), folic acid dosage (MR= 8.1%), and AUDIT-C test results (MR=6.7%). Further variability was modelled by including an estimated 5 to 10% rate of non-adherence to potassium iodide supplementation, as well as minor administrative inconsistencies that do not compromise clinical coherence (e.g., cervical cytology status may occasionally be left blank for patients who have not undergone the test, despite the admission of a “Not performed” category).

(iv)Variable-level constraints: several variable constraints were introduced, such as enforcement of integer-only scores whenever applicable (e.g., Goodwin score), properly structured laboratory measurements (e.g., 2-h Oral Glucose Tolerance Test taken at 0, 1, and 2-h, with automated derivation of gestational diabetes when any one value meets or exceeds the clinical threshold).

Variable-level constraints: several variable constraints were introduced, such as enforcement of integer-only scores whenever applicable (e.g., Goodwin score), properly structured laboratory measurements (e.g., 2-h Oral Glucose Tolerance Test taken at 0, 1, and 2-h, with automated derivation of gestational diabetes when any one value meets or exceeds the clinical threshold).

The resulting dataset following prompt refinement transitioned from general plausibility to a more rule-driven synthetic data generation with the inclusion of more domain-specific knowledge, as well as controlled noise, variability, and realism.

Prompt refinement focusing on clinical modelling (Prompt 3)

The third prompt was further refined to specify full clinical pathways and add quantitative constraints, focusing on formal clinical modelling aspects. Main additions included:

(i)Explicit longitudinal modelling: full pregnancy trajectory was established, namely by means of consultations (1 to 8 antenatal consultations), trimesters, delivery, puerperium, and follow-up variables.

(ii)Quantitative constraints: detailed modelling of realistic gestational weight gain by BMI category, postpartum weight loss ranges, and physiological variability across consultations.

Quantitative constraints: detailed modelling of realistic gestational weight gain by BMI category, postpartum weight loss ranges, and physiological variability across consultations.

(iii)Temporal logic enforcement: inclusion of strict rules ensuring that certain events only occur within valid gestational time frames, and that dynamic variables will evolve over time (e.g., maternal weight).

Temporal logic enforcement: inclusion of strict rules ensuring that certain events only occur within valid gestational time frames, and that dynamic variables will evolve over time (e.g., maternal weight).

(iv)Epidemiological dependency modelling: explicit encoding of epidemiological associations identified in the scientific literature was specified, namely the relationship between BMI with gestational diabetes and hypertension, preeclampsia with preterm birth, and Intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) with low birth weight.

This stage established a stricter synthetic data generation, adding stochastic variability and ensuring reproducible clinical reasoning.

Prompt refinement focusing on auditability and schema standardization (Prompt 4)

Following the last expert panel review, feedback pointed to the need for a final refinement focusing on data auditability, improved schema rigor, and validation transparency, aligning the dataset with essential data quality and reproducibility requirements. Key enhancements included:

(i)Auditability requirements: all derived variables must specify explicit base variables (for derived variables), documented derivation rules and clinical reasoning governing variable generation, and non-auditable variables must be explicitly flagged and justified.

(iii)Encoding constraints: binary variables were enforced as 0/1 only, with no text labels, whereas categorical variables required closed value sets.

(iv)Hard validation rules: quantitative constraints enforced weight gain ranges, postpartum weight loss, and glucose testing consistency.

Limitations

The dataset is restricted to low-risk profiles, and it should not be used for clinical inference, institutional benchmarking, or causal estimation, neither to directly support clinical decision-making and clinical model deployment. Due to its synthetic nature, rare complications and the full demographic and clinical diversity of real populations may be underrepresented. Moreover, data should not be assumed to reproduce real epidemiological distributions within specific institutions, geographic regions or national populations. Fetal growth curves, biometrics, and percentiles are simplified synthetic approximations designed for internal consistency and should not be interpreted as substitutes for established clinical standards. Preeclampsia modelling is simplified, excluding the full spectrum of laboratory criteria and target-organ involvement in favor of auditability over clinical detail. Social factors are not comprehensively represented, with key psychosocial variables such as social support and education omitted. The etiology underlying positive second-trimester Coombs test results is not explicitly modelled. Positive cases were kept as rare and internally consistent; however, variables that could reveal the underlying causes of positivity (e.g., autoimmune disease, inducing medications, prior blood transfusion) were not included, making individual etiological assessment impossible. The first-trimester combined risk assessment is based on a simplified rule using nuchal translucency, PAPP-A, β-hCG, and maternal age, and therefore does not replicate the full complexity of clinical trisomy screening algorithms. Finally, formal privacy-risk evaluation metrics were not the focus of this release, since the dataset was generated without direct use of real patient-level records.

Ethics Statement

This article describes a fully synthetic dataset generated without reference to real individuals. No real patient-level data were used for training, generation, evaluation, or validation. No human participants or identifiable data were involved. Therefore, ethics approval was not required.
