Embolism of coronary, cerebral, and limb arteries resulting from infective endocarditis of a prosthetic aortic valve: a clinical case report
Authors: Yunxian Chen, Yuhua Hu, Haiyang Liang, Jue Xia, Liangqiu Tang, Shebing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfective endocarditis (IE) involving prosthetic valves is a critical cardiac condition that can lead to complications such as structural heart damage, heart failure, and arterial embolism, with a high associated mortality rate. This report describes an uncommon instance of infective endocarditis of a prosthetic aortic valve leading to multiorgan embolism.Case presentationA 52-year-old male with a history of Bentall surgery and prosthetic aortic valve replacement presented with chest pain and dyspnea. Coronary angiography demonstrated blockage of the distal left anterior descending artery, which was addressed with balloon angioplasty. A transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) detected vegetation on the prosthetic valve, and metagenomic next-generation sequencing of blood confirmed infection with Cardiobacterium hominis. During treatment, he experienced acute ischaemia in the right lower limb necessitating thrombectomy, and MRI disclosed numerous tiny infarct foci in the brain. Following a period of six weeks of antibiotic treatment, the patient was discharged in a better condition. However, the valve dysfunction persisted and regular follow-up was required in order to determine whether to perform a further operation.ConclusionThis case underscores the risk of multi-organ embolic consequences in prosthetic valve infective endocarditis, highlighting the necessity for prompt identification, antimicrobial treatment, and surgical intervention where warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05287-2.

Introduction

Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE) is a severe complication following aortic valve replacement, whether surgical or transcatheter [1]. The incidence of infective endocarditis (IE) is higher after surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) than after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), with cumulative incidences at 60 months of 2.4% and 1.5%, respectively [2]. The most frequently isolated pathogens in PVE cases are staphylococci, streptococci, and enterococci [3]. Both TAVR and SAVR patients with IE exhibit high mortality rates, approximately 45% at one year [4]. PVE often results in severe complications, including perivalvular extension, heart failure, and embolic events [5]. Managing these infections is complex, often requiring a combination of surgical and medical interventions.

Cardiobacterium hominis(C. hominis), though rare, is a significant cause of infective endocarditis, particularly in individuals with pre-existing cardiac conditions [6]. As a member of the HACEK group, C. hominis is part of the normal oropharyngeal flora and is known for its slow growth and fastidious nature. Infections are often subacute, presenting with nonspecific symptoms that can delay diagnosis [7]. This case report details a patient with prosthetic aortic valve infective endocarditis complicated by coronary, cerebral and limb embolism, highlighting the need for prompt diagnosis and comprehensive management.

Case presentation

A 52-year-old male presented to the emergency department on February 28, 2025, with a one-day history of chest pain and exertional dyspnea, without fever or respiratory symptoms. The patient had a history of severe aortic stenosis and ascending aortic aneurysm. In March 2024, he underwent a Bentall procedure with replacement of a bioprosthetic aortic valve (23 A, Shanghai Xinxing Biotech, China). Postoperatively, he received warfarin for three months, with monthly echocardiograms confirming normal valve function. Two months prior to admission, he underwent dental cleaning without recommanded antibiotic prophylaxis.

Physical examination revealed a blood pressure of 128/87 mmHg, heart rate of 105 beats per minute, and a 3/6 systolic murmur at the aortic area. Electrocardiogram showed T-wave inversions in leads I, aVL, and V4–V6 (Fig. 1), with troponin T elevated at 52.0 pg/ml (Table 1). Transthoracic echocardiography showed no significant valve abnormalities. Diagnosed with non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndrome, he received aspirin 300 mg and clopidogrel 300 mg orally. Coronary angiography revealed distal left anterior descending artery occlusion, treated with balloon angioplasty (2.5 × 15 mm, 10 atm), restoring TIMI 3 flow without residual stenosis, suggesting thromboembolism (Fig. 2).

After angioplasty the patient continued to have persistent tachycardia and dyspnea, so we performed transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). TEE demonstrated thickening of the prosthetic aortic valve leaflets with multiple attached masses—the largest measuring 16 × 8 mm—and hemodynamic assessment showed a mean transvalvular velocity of 3.0 m/s, a mean gradient of 36 mmHg, an effective orifice area of 1.6 cm², and no paravalvular leak (Fig. 3). Blood cultures suggested gram-negative bacilli, and metagenomic next-generation sequencing identified Cardiobacterium hominis. Based on the modified Duke criteria, the patient fulfills the diagnosis of prosthetic valve infective endocarditis: (1) transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) demonstrated endocardial involvement with vegetations, and (2) microbiological confirmation of infection was obtained. Empirical antibiotics (vancomycin 1 g every 12 h, gentamicin 80,000 U every 8 h) were adjusted to ceftriaxone 2 g daily after three days based on sensitivity results.

On day five, sudden right lower limb pain led to CT angiography, confirming right popliteal and anterior tibial artery occlusion. Emergency thrombectomy retrieved a 6 × 6 mm vegetation and thrombi (Fig. 4a), resolving symptoms. Brain MRI revealed a 10 × 5 mm subacute parietal lobe infarction without neurological symptoms (Fig. 4b). Treatment continued with ceftriaxone and warfarin (INR 2–3).

Serial transesophageal echocardiograms at 1–2 week intervals showed gradual reduction in vegetation size, with complete resolution by the sixth week. However, the prosthetic aortic valve remained thickened, and a grade 2 systolic murmur persisted at the aortic area, with a transvalvular pressure gradient of 16 mmHg. The patient was discharged with instructions for regular echocardiographic follow-up to assess potential valve replacement if dysfunction worsens.

Discussion

Risk factors and pathogenesis

PVE is a serious complication following valve replacement, with an annual incidence of approximately 1% [8]. Common risk factors encompass advanced age, comorbidities like diabetes and chronic kidney disease, and the specific type of prosthetic valve employed [9]. The patient’s Bentall operation, a form of SAVR [10], coupled with a dental cleaning two months prior without recommended antibiotic prophylaxis, may have contributed to the development of the infection. Dental procedures are a well-established risk factor, particularly for HACEK organisms, which are part of the normal oropharyngeal flora [11]. Current guidelines recommend 2 g of oral amoxicillin one hour before dental procedures to prevent IE in patients with prosthetic valves [12]. The absence of this prophylaxis in this case may have contributed to the infection, underscoring the critical importance of patient education and clinical vigilance.

Microbiological characteristics

The causal agent, C. hominis, a gram-negative bacillus of the HACEK group, is an uncommon but significant pathogen in PVE [6]. While Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, and Enterococcus are predominant in PVE etiology, HACEK organisms account for 5–10% of infective endocarditis cases, typically associated with late-onset presentations. The patient’s subacute presentation aligns with the typical features of HACEK infective endocarditis, which can be obscured by non-specific symptoms [13]. Blood cultures supplemented by metagenomic sequencing were crucial for identifying C.hominis, a pathogen that is challenging to culture routinely. The application of advanced diagnostic tools such as metagenomic sequencing reflects recent microbiological advancements and has markedly enhanced pathogen identification in culture-negative IE cases [14].

Diagnostic considerations

On admission, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) failed to detect any vegetations, likely due to artifacts from the prosthetic valve material that hindered accurate assessment. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed thickened and redundant valve leaflets, thereby confirming the diagnosis and highlighting TEE’s superior sensitivity for prosthetic valve evaluation [15]. Additionally, cardiac CT is valuable for assessing periprosthetic complications—such as abscesses and pseudoaneurysms—and should be considered when transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is inconclusive or when there is clinical suspicion of perivalvular extension [16].

Therapeutic strategies

Antimicrobial therapy is fundamental in PVE management, with empirical antibiotics initiated pending pathogen identification. Upon confirming a C. hominis infection, treatment was transitioned to ceftriaxone, based on the typical susceptibility of HACEK organisms to third-generation cephalosporins [17]. T Guidelines recommend a 4–6 week antibiotic course for HACEK IE [18], and after completing this regimen, the patient’s infection was effectively controlled. The role of anticoagulation in PVE is debated due to the increased bleeding risk during active IE [19]. Anticoagulation with warfarin was administered due to elevated D-dimer levels and the presence of thrombus in the lower limb arteries. Subsequently, the vegetation on the prosthetic valve resolved. Surgical intervention is indicated in PVE cases complicated by heart failure, persistent infection, large vegetations (>10 mm), perivalvular extension, or recurrent emboli [20]. As the prosthetic valve function improved with antimicrobial therapy, surgery was not immediately necessary.

Prognosis and follow-up

Although the patient responded favorably to antimicrobial therapy, prosthetic valve thickening and a transvalvular pressure gradient of approximately 16 mmHg persisted. Ongoing monitoring of the prosthetic valve is therefore essential, with surgical intervention considered if further deterioration occurs. This highlights the need for long-term follow-up in PVE survivors to ensure timely detection of late valve dysfunction.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the significant embolic risk associated with prosthetic valve endocarditis, impacting the coronary, cerebral, and peripheral arteries. Prompt diagnosis, precise antimicrobial treatment, and timely surgical intervention are essential. It underscores the necessity of maintaining oral hygiene for patients having prosthetic valve replacement surgery and the avoidance of dental treatment or the administration of antimicrobial prophylaxis during dental procedures.
