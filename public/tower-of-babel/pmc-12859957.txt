Osmolarity trajectories and outcomes in patients with acute pancreatitis in the intensive care unit: group-based trajectory modeling
Authors: Bo Chen, Jingjing Cai, Simin Wu, Leping Fang, Nengwei Yuan, Jun Lyu, Zhigang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute pancreatitis (AP) is a common acute gastrointestinal disorder, with a subset of patients progressing to severe forms associated with high mortality. While osmotic pressure significantly influences its pathological processes, the relationship between trajectories of plasma osmolality changes and patient prognosis remains incompletely elucidated.MethodsUsing the MIMIC-IV database, we analyzed 1,138 ICU-confirmed acute pancreatitis cases. Group-based trajectory models were applied to identify distinct trajectories of plasma osmolality changes. Cox regression models and Kaplan-Meier analyses were used to examine associations between these trajectories and all-cause mortality at 28, 90, and 365 days, with adjustment for potential confounding factors.ResultsFive distinct subgroups were identified and validated based on plasma osmolality trajectories: the “Moderately Stable Group” (39.5%), “Low Stable Group” (22.8%), “High Sudden Rise Group” (9.5%), “High Sudden Drop Group” (6.3%), and “High Gradual Drop Group” (21.8%). After adjustment, both the “High Sudden Rise Group” and “High Sudden Drop Group” demonstrated significantly increased mortality risks at 28, 90, and 365 days, with the latter exhibiting the highest risk. After adjusting for confounding factors, the “High Sudden Rise Group” was significantly associated with 28-day mortality (HR = 2.15, 95% CI: 1.10–4.21), and this association was even stronger for the “High Sudden Drop Group” (HR = 3.62, 95% CI: 1.82–7.19). Additionally, 24-hour plasma osmolality displayed a U-shaped association with 28-day mortality. Subgroup analyses confirmed that the “High Sudden Drop Group” consistently showed an elevated mortality risk across all subgroups (P < 0.05).ConclusionThis study identified distinct phenotypic subgroups of acute pancreatitis by analyzing plasma osmolality trajectories. By incorporating dynamic changes in plasma osmolality, we applied a group-based trajectory model to stratify mortality risk among patients with acute pancreatitis. These findings may facilitate the early identification of high-risk individuals, optimize clinical fluid management strategies, and ultimately improve patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03330-y.Keywords: Group-based trajectory modeling, Acute pancreatitis, Plasma osmolality, All-cause mortality, MIMIC-IV

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a common gastrointestinal emergency with a global disease burden. Its increasing incidence has established AP as a leading cause of non-malignant gastrointestinal hospitalization [1, 2]. Approximately 20% of patients with AP progress to severe acute pancreatitis (SAP), characterized by persistent organ failure lasting more than 48 h or the development of pancreatic infection, with mortality rates ranging from 30% to 40% [3]. AP not only severely compromises patient survival and quality of life but also places a substantial strain on healthcare resources and systems [4–6].

Plasma osmolality balance plays a critical regulatory role in the pathophysiology of AP. Dysregulated osmolality directly exacerbates pancreatic injury through cellular edema induction, microcirculatory impairment, and inflammatory cascade activation, significantly impacting clinical prognosis [7, 8]. Specifically in hyperlipidemic acute pancreatitis (HLAP), dyslipidemia-induced local osmolality imbalance promotes unsaturated fatty acid deposition and lipolytic enzyme activation, thereby aggravating necrotizing inflammation and multi-organ damage—a key determinant of disease severity [9, 10]. Early fluid resuscitation, a cornerstone of AP management, modulates plasma osmolality levels via intravenous administration of hypertonic or balanced crystalloid solutions [11]. Abnormal plasma osmolality (hyperosmolarity/hypoosmolarity) correlates significantly with mortality in critical illness. In septic patients, plasma osmolality exhibits a U-shaped association with 28-day mortality, demonstrating the lowest risk within 285.80–296.29 mmol/L; deviations beyond this range substantially elevate mortality risk [12]. This phenomenon may stem from hyperosmolarity-induced cellular dehydration, microcirculatory dysfunction, and compromised organ perfusion [13].

This study hypothesizes that the trajectory of changes in plasma osmolality is associated with the inpatient mortality rate of patients with AP. Furthermore, this dynamic trend may serve as a predictive indicator for assessing the prognosis of AP. The research will utilize publicly available intensive care unit databases to conduct a retrospective analysis, systematically exploring the relationship between plasma osmolality trajectories and the prognosis of AP patients through group trajectory models. This research aims to provide theoretical support for clinical prognosis assessment and enhance fluid resuscitation strategies.

Materials and methods

Database and patients

This study is a retrospective analysis based on the publicly available Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, version 3.1) database. Constructed by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology’s Laboratory for Computational Physiology, this database contains clinical information from over 364,627 unique patients, including 94,458 ICU admission records. The data collection period spans from 2008 to 2022 and was sourced from the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) located in Boston, Massachusetts, USA. The database systematically collects multidimensional data on patients, including demographic characteristics, laboratory test results, medication regimens, vital signs, surgical records, disease diagnoses, drug management details, and follow-up survival outcomes. To access this database, the research team completed the human subjects protection training course led by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and successfully passed the certification assessment through the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI). The author, Bo Chen, has successfully passed the evaluation of this program (certification number: 11299321). Since the data has been de-identified and is subject to secondary analysis, informed consent may be waived following approval from the BIDMC Institutional Review Board (IRB).

The inclusion criteria for this study comprised patients diagnosed with pancreatitis during their hospitalization in the ICU, as confirmed by ICD-9/10 coding [14]. For patients who were admitted to the ICU multiple times, only data from their first admission were included in the analysis. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients who were discharged or died within 7 days after the diagnosis of pancreatitis, as continuous osmotic pressure data for 7 days post-diagnosis are required; (2) Patients lacking osmotic pressure testing data within 24 h following the diagnosis; (3) Patients who received treatment with globulin or mannitol; and (4) Patients aged under 18 years (Fig. 1).

Data collection

This study extracts clinical data from the MIMIC-IV database utilizing PostgreSQL and PgAdmin4 tools. The osmotic pressure data from the seven days preceding admission were defined as the exposure variable. These data were obtained from daily serum sodium, potassium, glucose, and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) measurements. When multiple laboratory results were available on the same day, their arithmetic mean was used. To ensure unit consistency for osmolality calculation, sodium and potassium were measured in mmol/L, while glucose and BUN were measured in mg/dL and converted into mmol/L using standard biochemical conversion factors (glucose ÷ 18; BUN ÷ 2.8). The formula for calculating plasma osmolality is as follows: Osmolality (mOsm/L) = 2 × [Sodium (mmol/L) + Potassium (mmol/L)] + Glucose (mg/dL) / 18 + BUN (mg/dL) / 2.8.

Except for sodium, potassium, blood urea nitrogen, and blood glucose—which were measured repeatedly within the first 7 days— all other laboratory parameters and vital signs were obtained within 24 h of ICU admission. For variables with multiple measurements during this period, the mean value was used for analysis. Medication history was extracted directly from the ICU hospitalization records.The dataset collected at ICU admission included several key categories. Demographic variables consisted of age, race, gender, weight, height, and marital status. Vital signs included heart rate (HR), mean arterial pressure (MAP), respiratory rate (RR), and body temperature. Clinical severity scores comprised the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Acute Physiology Score III (APS III), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), and Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS).Past medical history included acute kidney injury, acute myocardial infarction (AMI), hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), congestive heart failure (CHF), cerebral infarction, liver disease, and sepsis. Medication use captured vasoactive agents, diuretics, and glucocorticoids.Laboratory parameters included mean values of white blood cell count, red blood cell count, platelet count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, total calcium, ionized calcium, chloride, lactate, anion gap, creatinine, uric acid, total bilirubin, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, thrombin time (TT), prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), fibrinogen, international normalized ratio (INR), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). Interventions included continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) and mechanical ventilation.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome measure of this study is the all-cause mortality rate at 28 days and 90 days, while the secondary outcome measure is the all-cause mortality rate at 365 days.

Statistical analysis

This study utilized R software (version 4.3.3) for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics and intergroup comparisons were performed as follows: For continuous variables that follow a normal distribution, the mean ± standard deviation is reported, and t-tests are used for comparisons between two groups; one-way ANOVA is employed for comparisons among multiple groups. For continuous variables that do not follow a normal distribution, the median (interquartile range) [M(IQR)] is used for representation. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test is applied for comparisons between two groups, while the Kruskal-Wallis test is utilized for comparisons among multiple groups. Categorical variables are represented by counts (percentages) [n(%)] and comparisons between groups are conducted using the χ² test. When the theoretical frequency is less than 5, the Fisher exact test is employed instead. This study employs Group-Based Trajectory Modeling (GBTM) to identify plasma osmolality levels with similar developmental trajectories. The criteria for determining the number of trajectory groups are as follows: (1) The optimal number of trajectories is selected based on a combination of the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and the clinical interpretability of the results; (2) Higher-order polynomial fits (cubic) are preferred for all trajectory shapes, and if the fitting parameters are not statistically significant, lower-order parameters (linear or quadratic) are utilized instead; (3) The average posterior probability (AvePP) for each patient assigned to the corresponding osmolality trajectory is evaluated to ensure a minimum threshold of 0.70 is met. The survival differences among various trajectory groups were assessed using Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curves, and subgroup analyses were conducted to further investigate the survival outcomes of each trajectory group.In handling missing values, if the percentage of missing data for a confounding factor is less than 10%, imputation is performed using the mean or median based on the distribution characteristics. For data with a missing percentage between 10% and 20%, regression imputation is applied. If the missing percentage exceeds 20%, the variable is excluded from the analysis (see Supplementary Table 1). It is noteworthy that plasma osmolality, as a longitudinal exposure, contained intermittent missing values during the first 7 days. Under the assumption of missing at random, and given that GBTM accommodates irregular longitudinal measurements, no additional imputation was performed for these missing osmolality values to avoid introducing bias. To assess the discriminative ability of the trajectory model for short-term mortality, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were conducted. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated at 14 days and 28 days. In addition, no formal sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the impact of variability in osmolality measurement frequency across patients. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3), and a p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Group trajectory modeling

This study conducted a trajectory analysis involving 1,138 patients diagnosed with acute pancreatitis. The results indicate that as the number of trajectory groups increases from one to five, both the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values progressively decrease. When the number of trajectory groups is established at five, the average posterior probabilities (AvePP) for each group are 0.91, 0.95, 0.92, 0.93, and 0.95, all exceeding the empirical threshold of 0.70, which suggests that the model demonstrates a good fit. Following a comprehensive evaluation, the final determination for the number of trajectory groups is confirmed to be five. In addition, overall classification accuracy was assessed using model entropy. The entropy values for the one- to five-group models were 0.0000, 0.911, 0.880, 0.879, and 0.885, respectively. Notably, the five-group model exhibited the highest entropy among all multi-group solutions, indicating superior separation between trajectory classes and a clearer underlying grouping structure. Considering the progressively decreasing AIC/BIC values, the consistently high AvePP values, and the highest entropy observed across the multi-group models, the five-group solution was ultimately selected as the optimal trajectory classification model (see Table 1).

Baseline characteristics

This study initially included 1,457 patients with acute pancreatitis, and after applying the eligibility criteria, a total of 1,138 patients were ultimately confirmed (Fig. 1). Supplementary Table 2 summarizes the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics across the five identified osmolality trajectories (Traj1–Traj5), with sample sizes of 450, 260, 108, 248, and 72 patients, respectively.Regarding demographic characteristics, patients in Traj3 were notably younger than those in the other groups, with a median age of 51.0 years (IQR 39.0–62.0), whereas patients in Traj5 were the oldest, with a median age of 64.0 years (IQR 55.0–75.0). In terms of clinical complications, Traj5 exhibited the highest burden of comorbid and acute conditions, including markedly elevated rates of sepsis (86.1%) and chronic kidney disease (33.3%), both substantially higher than those observed in other trajectories.Traj3 and Traj5 represented the most severe clinical phenotypes. Traj5 had the highest Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score [9.0 (7.0–11.0)], followed by Traj3 [8.0 (5.0–10.5)]. A similar pattern was observed for the Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII), with values of [69.0 (50.0–83.0)] in Traj5 and [63.0 (48.0–79.0)] in Traj3.In terms of therapeutic interventions, Traj3 had the highest rate of mechanical ventilation (MV), while Traj5 had the highest rate of continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). Traj3 also showed the longest ICU and overall hospital length of stay. Although Traj5 had a shorter hospital stay than Traj3, its duration was still considerably longer than those of the remaining trajectories.Both Traj3 and Traj5 demonstrated substantially higher mortality rates compared with the other groups, with Traj5 exhibiting the worst outcomes—including 28-day, 90-day, and 365-day mortality rates of 36.1%, 47.2%, and 50.0%, respectively(see Supplementary Table 3).

Evaluation of model discrimination using time-dependent ROC curves

The time-dependent ROC analysis demonstrated that the trajectory model had moderate discriminative ability. The AUC values were 0.723 for 14-day and 0.688 for 28-day mortality, respectively. These findings are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Survival analysis affecting survival time

Kaplan-Meier survival analyses (Fig. 4a, b, c) revealed significant differences in mortality rates among distinct osmotic trajectory groups at 28 days, 90 days, and 365 days. Notably, the Traj5 cohort demonstrated the highest mortality rates across all three time points, followed by Traj3, while Traj1 consistently exhibited the lowest mortality rates. Intergroup comparisons showed no statistically significant differences in mortality between Traj2 and Traj1 at 90 days (P = 0.24) and 365 days (P = 0.22). By contrast, all other intergroup comparisons yielded statistically significant differences in mortality at 28 days, 90 days, and 365 days (all P < 0.05).

Kaplan-Meier survival curve by five trajectory patterns in acute pancreatitis cohorts

The severity of osmotic fluctuations and specific adverse patterns may directly impact patient prognosis. Temporal comparisons further indicated that although mortality rates across all trajectory groups declined at the 90-day and 365-day follow-ups relative to the 28-day assessment, a significant mortality risk persisted during these subsequent periods.

Subgroup analysis

The results of the subgroup analyses are summarized in Figs. 5 and 6. Traj5 was consistently associated with an elevated risk of mortality across all subgroups. In the sex-stratified analysis, with Traj1 as the reference group, Traj3 (HR = 2.33, 95% CI: 1.14–4.75, P = 0.02) and Traj5 (HR = 4.86, 95% CI: 2.35–10.04, P < 0.001) were associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality in the female subgroup, whereas Traj2 (HR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.36–1.62, P = 0.482) and Traj4 (HR = 1.45, 95% CI: 0.74–2.82, P = 0.275) did not demonstrate statistically significant associations. In contrast, statistically significant differences in mortality risk were observed among all five trajectory groups within the male subgroup.The age-stratified analysis (≤ 65 vs. >65 years) indicated that Traj5 was significantly associated with an increased mortality risk in both age categories. Among patients aged > 65 years, Traj3 (HR = 2.72, 95% CI: 1.41–5.28, P = 0.003) and Traj4 (HR = 5.45, 95% CI: 2.69–11.05, P < 0.001) were also associated with an elevated mortality risk. Further analysis showed that among patients receiving diuretics, only Traj4 was significantly associated with 28-day mortality (P < 0.05), whereas Traj3 did not demonstrate a significant association.

Discussion

This study applied a group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) approach and identified five distinct dynamic serum osmolarity trajectory patterns during hospitalization in ICU patients with acute pancreatitis. The results demonstrated that, compared with patients who maintained moderately stable osmolarity levels, trajectories characterized by initially elevated osmolarity followed by a rapid increase, rapid decline, or gradual decline were significantly associated with increased short-term and long-term mortality risks (specific effect sizes are provided in the Results section). Furthermore, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis revealed a U-shaped association between serum osmolarity and 28-day mortality: the mortality risk was lowest when osmolarity remained within 283–302 mmol/L, whereas substantial deviations from this range in either direction were associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes. These distinct dynamic patterns may reflect underlying differences in patients’ responses to fluid resuscitation, ongoing systemic inflammation, or variations in organ perfusion, suggesting that serum osmolarity trajectories could serve as a valuable dynamic indicator for risk stratification.

Current evidence highlights the pivotal role of osmotic homeostasis in maintaining both cellular function and systemic regulation [5]. Disruption of osmotic homeostasis is closely associated with exacerbated systemic inflammatory responses, electrolyte disturbances, and an increased risk of organ dysfunction [12, 15–17]. For instance, among patients who underwent cardiac arrest resuscitation, early hypernatremia was significantly associated with an increased risk of poor neurological outcomes (a OR 1.13 per 1 mmol/L increase; 95% CI 1.04–1.23; p = 0.004). The underlying mechanism may involve cerebral edema caused by osmotic shifts [18]. Similarly, overly rapid correction of osmolarity increases the risk of osmotic demyelination syndrome, whereas excessively slow correction is associated with increased in-hospital mortality, collectively highlighting the complexity of maintaining osmotic balance in clinical practice [19, 20].

At the mechanistic level, studies in sepsis models have demonstrated that a hyperosmolar environment can upregulate the expression of junctional proteins such as vascular endothelial cadherin, enhance intercellular tension, and promote cortical F-actin reorganization, thereby contributing to the stabilization of the vascular barrier and reduced endothelial leakage [21]. However, research by Whitcomb et al. further revealed that in acute pancreatitis, the pronounced systemic inflammatory response itself can impair vascular barrier function. Progression to multiorgan failure (MOF) is characterized by a shift toward dysregulated capillary leakage driven by endothelial dysfunction, leading to extensive protein and fluid extravasation. This, in turn, induces intravascular hypovolemia, hemoconcentration, and tissue edema, ultimately resulting in organ hypoperfusion and functional failure [22]. In this context, significant fluctuations in osmolality or sustained hyperosmolality often serve as important predictors of poor prognosis. Moreover, studies indicate that hyperosmolality may synergize with intracellular chloride levels to modulate WNK kinase activity, thereby exacerbating microcirculatory dysfunction and hypercoagulability and further promoting tissue hypoperfusion [23, 24].

As a key clinical approach to regulating osmotic pressure, fluid resuscitation strategies directly influence osmotic pressure trajectories and patient outcomes. Although aggressive resuscitation is widely recommended, studies have shown that, compared with a moderate resuscitation strategy, aggressive resuscitation (20 mL/kg Lactated Ringer’s solution) significantly increases the risk of fluid overload (20.5% vs. 6.3%; adjusted relative risk 2.85, 95% CI 1.36–5.94; p = 0.004), prolongs hospital stay, and does not improve disease severity [25]. Regarding fluid selection, evidence indicates that colloid solutions significantly reduce the incidence of intra-abdominal hypertension (IAH) compared with crystalloid solutions; moreover, the proportion of patients requiring mechanical ventilation is considerably lower (15.0% vs. 47.6%) [26]. In patients with severe pancreatitis, a conservative fluid resuscitation strategy that maintains stable osmotic pressure may more effectively preserve water–electrolyte balance, reduce the risks of electrolyte disturbances and acidosis, and mitigate complications associated with fluid overload (including pulmonary edema and exacerbated systemic inflammation), thereby reducing mortality [27–29]. In summary, this study suggests that the initial osmotic pressure level and its dynamic trajectories can serve as key indicators for risk assessment in patients with acute pancreatitis. Early identification of high-risk osmotic pressure trajectories and implementation of individualized, moderate fluid resuscitation strategies may help maintain internal homeostasis and ultimately provide new insights into improving clinical outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis based on a public database, residual confounding may persist despite adjustment for key covariates. In particular, indication bias is possible because patients with more severe illness or those receiving more intensive treatment tended to undergo more frequent measurements of osmolality. Such differences in measurement frequency may have influenced trajectory classification and, consequently, the observed associations. Second, the study spans a long time period (2008–2022), during which diagnostic criteria, management strategies, and fluid therapy practices for acute pancreatitis underwent substantial changes. These temporal shifts may have introduced heterogeneity in both exposure patterns and clinical outcomes. As a result, the exposure–outcome relationship may not have remained constant over time, potentially leading to either overestimation or underestimation of the association between osmolality trajectories and mortality. Third, the osmolality trajectory model did not undergo internal validation (e.g., bootstrapping or sample splitting) and was not externally validated in an independent cohort. Fourth, since trajectory modeling requires complete 7-day osmolality data, we excluded patients who were discharged or died within 7 days after diagnosis. Although this measure ensured the necessary longitudinal data integrity for constructing trajectories, it may have excluded patients with severe disease who deteriorated rapidly during the early phase, thereby limiting the applicability of the findings to the most critically ill population. Future studies could explore more flexible analytical strategies (such as shortening the observation window or adopting dynamic reference models) to improve the inclusion of early critically ill patients and enhance model applicability.

Conclusion

This study identified distinct phenotypic subgroups of acute pancreatitis by analyzing plasma osmolality trajectories. By incorporating dynamic changes in plasma osmolality, we applied a group-based trajectory model to stratify mortality risk among patients with acute pancreatitis. These findings may facilitate the early identification of high-risk individuals, optimize clinical fluid management strategies, and ultimately improve patient outcomes.
