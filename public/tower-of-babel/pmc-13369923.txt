Europe wide agricultural land use systems by harmonizing the Copernicus High Resolution Layers on grassland and croplands
Authors: Fernando Fahl, Marijn van der Velde, Martin Claverie, Momchil Yordanov, Laura Martinez Sanchez, Emanuele Lugato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper presents a Europe-wide agricultural land use systems map produced by the integration and harmonization of grasslands and croplands layers from the Copernicus Land Monitoring Service High Resolution Layers (HRL) in Europe. By utilizing high-resolution spatial data from the Copernicus Sentinel missions, the study applies a predefined set of logical rules to identify crop-grass sequences by combining the HRL products. The resulting maps provide detailed classifications of permanent grasslands, permanent crops, arable systems, and land use changes across 38 countries in the European Environment Agency network (EEA38) from 2017 to 2021. European agriculture is dominated by permanent grasslands (45.5%), followed by arable systems including temporary grassland (33.5%), permanent crops (3.8%), and land use change (1.6%). While the study is based on HRL products with substantial accuracy, some discrepancies (<3% of classified areas), such as disagreements in land use classifications across years and data gaps, present opportunities for further refinement and improvement. The derived harmonized agricultural land use systems data is a step forward for assessments in land and soil management, nature restoration, and climate mitigation targets.

Background & Summary

The European Union (EU) strives for Europe to become the first climate neutral continent by 2050. This ambition is at the core of the European Green Deal, and various laws, regulations, and strategies have been proposed or adopted to tackle climate change and environmental degradation. The land sector plays a crucial role in achieving these ambitions1. The Climate Law and the Nature Restoration Regulation are already in force, aiming to reach climate neutrality and restore degraded ecosystems, while the Forest and Soil Monitoring laws are under discussion.

Climate targets for land have been defined, with the land use, land use change and forestry (LULUCF) sector committed to reach the net carbon removals target of 310 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent by 2030, enhancing the capacity of natural sinks2,3. To support this objective, the Carbon Removal and Carbon Farming regulation4 was recently adopted to develop a voluntary carbon market in the EU. This regulation aims to increase soil and vegetation biogenic carbon pools and reduce emissions through certified “carbon farming” activities.

While EU and national level restoration and emission targets have been formulated, their implementation poses many challenges, due to the diverse policies and actors operating on the land. For instance, 38% of the land in the EU is farmed, and farmers receive subsidies under the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) to manage 80% of that land. Although the CAP for 2021–2027 has greener ambitions, the impact of agricultural policy on GHG emissions or ecosystem status is not directly quantified and the CAP monitoring framework lacks essential elements to assess performance5. At the same time, the granularity of the interventions is rarely captured by national GHG inventories. This creates a situation where the impact of the CAP is partly accounted for by the EU’s climate policy albeit without knowing the full picture.

A significant obstacle in defining unifying methodologies is related to the low interoperability of data belonging to different Member States (MS). For instance, the Land Parcel Identification System (LPIS) and Geospatial Application (GSA), the geospatial components of the Integrated Administration and Control Systems of MS, are made of geometrically accurate datasets with detailed thematic classifications used for CAP implementation and compliance evaluation. The LPIS and GSA of any country or region contain detailed parcel delineations with declarations of more than hundreds of different crops in their respective languages. This makes it cumbersome to use the data outside their specific operational domain. Furthermore, despite the obligation under the High Value Data act6, many are not publicly available, although efforts to semantically and hierarchically harmonize them exist7,8.

Land cover products, such as ESA CCI9 and Corine Land Cover, available at 300 and 100 m resolutions, respectively, lack the spatial detail to capture field level interventions10. Besides these and other land cover products, several applications have developed land-use and land-system products to better capture management and landscape characteristics. In Europe, such land system representations are typically found at a highest resolution of 1 km and are based on estimates on the intensity of the system11.

In recent decades, the Copernicus program has notably improved global land monitoring capacity, especially with the launch of Sentinel 1 and 2 missions. The availability of high-resolution spatial data with frequent revisiting time, in combination with machine and deep learning techniques12, is enabling a more biophysical and objective description of the land surface and land management. Creating consistent spatial datasets that describe land characteristics and comply with international harmonization procedures can be a significant step forward for monitoring and modelling processes to enhance the coherence of different EU-level policy perspectives.

Notably, advances in Copernicus Sentinel based 10-m resolution Earth Observation mapping of land cover and land use in Europe have resulted in the production of the Copernicus High-Resolution Layers (HRL Products)13–15 produced by the Copernicus Land Monitoring Service (CLMS)16,17. The HRL contains annual layers on crop type and grassland and associated layers that can be interpreted as proxies of agricultural management from 2017 to 2021.

This paper describes the map with a typology of agricultural land use systems developed through the integration and harmonization of the HRL products. The resulting spatial-temporal maps capture land use dynamics and vegetation patterns over multiple years, providing a comprehensive representation of changes and interactions among diverse vegetation types and land cover categories.

Our Europe-wide harmonized and comprehensive agricultural land use systems data may become an essential input for assessments, monitoring, or modelling activities related to land use/change, nature restoration and climate mitigation. Furthermore, using a common land information baseline can improve the coherence between agricultural, environmental, and climate policies.

HRL Products

The HRL products are the latest recent addition to the Copernicus Land Monitoring Service (CLMS) portfolio13–17. The HRL products are harmonized yearly maps focusing on thematic categories such as Forest (FTY), Grassland (GRA), Herbaceous (HER), and Cropland (CTY). These maps provide a catalogue of raster products at a 10-meter spatial resolution, detailing the annual status of these land cover types. Additionally, change layers are available at three-year intervals with a 20-meter resolution.

The primary dataset consists of HRL layers for various land cover types, provided as tiled GeoTIFF files with dimensions of 100 km per tile, and aligned with the EEA reference grid18. The time series covers the period from 2017 to 2021.

Methods

Pre-processing

In the pre-processing phase, the CTY product16 was stratified into two distinct yearly layers based on land cover types: arable crops (CTY-ARL) and permanent crops (CTY-PC). These newly derived layers were subsequently converted into a binary classification format, where a value of 0 denotes the absence of data and a value of 1 indicates the presence of cropland.

Similarly, the CPSCT product, which encompasses four primary classes, was transformed into a binary classification scheme. In contrast, the GRA and HER products17 were already available in this format, requiring no further conversion.

Aggregation of yearly HRL layers

Following the conversion of HRL products into a binary classification, a temporal classification was conducted on a year-by-year basis. A predefined set of logical rules was applied to aggregate these layers into seven distinct classes. This step resulted in the creation of a series of yearly reclassified maps spanning a five-year period.

Identification and Classification of Multiannual Crop-Grass Sequences

Crop-grass sequences identification was performed by integrating the five yearly classified layers (2017–2021) into a singular composite layer. This integration was achieved by concatenating class numbers into a unique numerical value, where each digit’s position corresponds to the respective year of classification. Technical Validation

Validation of HRL products

The validation of HRL products, specifically the grassland status layer (GRA) and crop types layer (CTY), is detailed in the cropland and grassland product user manuals13,15. According to these documents, the GRA layer was validated using 10000 samples randomly selected across the LUCAS20 grid. Reference data, including satellite images, cartographic information and GSA datasets (when available), was utilized for the response design. Results demonstrated an overall accuracy greater than 95% for both the EU-27 and EEA38 regions for the years 2018 and 2021, significantly exceeding the 85% accuracy target set for this assessment.

The validation of the CTY layer followed a similar approach, sampling random LUCAS points using multiple reference data sources. At the crop group level, the F1-score for both 2017 and 2021 at the EU-27 level was higher than 85% for each class, surpassing the stablished target of 80%. At the EEA38 level, the results were similar, except for the permanent crops class, which achieved accuracy between 83 and 84%.

At aggregation Level 1, some crop types (such as cereals, maize, rapeseed) exceeded the F1-score target of 80% for each class, while others did not reach this threshold. The rarity of some classes explains the limited number of samples, which may imply that lower F1-scores should be interpreted with caution for classes like flax, cotton, hemp, soybean, and rice. Unclassified classes had a null F1-score due to their nature, as these classes did not exist in the LUCAS or GSA reference datasets.

Validation of the agricultural land use systems layer

The validation of the agricultural land use systems layer19 was based on the EuroCrops v2 dataset21,22, which provides a multiannual collection of crop declarations from 16 EU Member States. Some Member States make Geo-Spatial Application (GSA) data available for specific NUTS regions (e.g. Italy, Germany, Belgium), while others provide public data for the entire country. The EuroCrops v2 dataset contains nearly 47 million agricultural parcels, offering detailed parcel-level information, including crop type, parcel geometry, and harmonized metadata according to the Hierarchical Crop and Agriculture Taxonomy (HCAT v4).

In addition to the validation analysis, it was possible to identify some related conflicts and inconsistencies among the different products (GRA, CTY, HER and CPSCT). These issues refer to the outcomes of the classification rules that detect discrepancies, anomalies, or missing information in the primary dataset. Three main types of inconsistencies were identified: overlapping classes, land use disagreements and data gaps.

Arable systems and temporary grassland overlapping

In the aggregation of yearly layers, pixels with overlapping classifications of cropland (CTY) and temporary grassland (HER) were identified in the HRL products. To reduce bias in favor of either classification, a pixel counting was performed using EuroCropsV2 as the reference layer (see Table S4). The results show that when the overlap occurred, most of the pixels corresponded to arable land in the EuroCropsV2, exceeding 85% in most analyzed tiles. As a result, priority was given to the arable land class in such conditions.

Land use disagreements

Disagreements in land use classification across years are derived from the combination, coexistence or sequence of land cover types that may not realistically occur in the field, as well as multiannual patterns that do not fit into any other classification criteria. These conflicts can appear in the yearly classification (Class 9 in Table 3) or in the pattern classification (Class 7, including their subclasses, in Table 5).

Most of these issues involve the presence of permanent grassland interspersed with other land covers (Class 71), except in cases identified as land use change (Classes 51 and 52). This situation leads to conflicts with the established definition of grassland (see Definitions section) which requires continuality in the grassland identification (at least 6 years). This class constitutes 2.1% of the total pixel count (see Fig. 1).

The mix of permanent crops and arable systems (Class 73) is also regarded as an inconsistent crop-grass sequence, accounting for 0.5% of the total pixel count. Furthermore, the inconsistencies identified in the yearly classification phase, such as pixels being classified as both permanent grassland and arable systems, are categorized under Classes 72 and 74, which collectively do not exceed 0.5% of the total pixel count.

The higher incidence of land use disagreements in the interannual classification occurs within the permanent crop domain (see Fig. 9). The unclassified permanent crops and fruits represent the crop types with the highest percentage of pixels classified as inconsistencies. Among the arable systems, other cereals and unclassified annual crops show higher percentages in nearly all years compared to the other classes. Data gaps can be divided into two categories: heading gaps, when the missing data occurs at the beginning of the crop-grass sequences, and middle and tailing gaps, when it happens at any other position. Usage Notes

The HRL products were utilized without any spatial post-processing to address noise, ensuring that the original attribution can be traced back to any location at pixel level. In this context, the crop-grass sequences layer reflects a sequence of interannual classifications, which can be used to retrieve the original pixel information from the GRA, HER, CTY and CPSCT products. This traceability is crucial for cross-referencing the results with the original datasets, allowing findings to be verified against the source data.

The typology of agricultural land use systems involves a degree of interpretation. For instance, defining the presence of a cover crop within a rotation as a recurrent management practice can be approached conservatively or liberally, by setting the number of cover crops over a five-year sequence. This is because confounding factors may interfere with an accurate detection of cover crops by remote sensing23,24, such as the regrowth of weeds that may result in overestimating the adoption of this farming practice, notably compared to reported statistics23. Further investigations are, them, worth to be carried out.

Moreover, the uncertainty associated with land cover detection via remote sensing suggests that some classifications should be approached cautiously, particularly those involving the transition from permanent grassland to cropland. Permanent grassland is defined by the CAP as land that has been used for growing grasses or other herbaceous forage for five years or more, without being part of the farm’s regular crop rotation25. Uncertainty in detecting ploughing may lead to a false interpretation of land use change, rather than indicating temporary grassland within the rotation.

A further limitation is that complex systems with sub-pixel heterogeneity (e.g., Montado/Dehesa systems, which combine sparse trees with herbaceous ground cover) can be difficult to classify accurately using the proposed classification scheme.

Despite these challenges, the improved accuracy and continuity of Copernicus derived Earth Observation products can assist in the development of agri-environmental policy impact assessment26. The agricultural land use system typology proposed here represents an attempt to provide additional information beyond land cover systems related to land use (e.g. cover crops, ley cropping systems). As a result, it may contribute to examining, assessing and modeling diverse climate, environmental and agricultural topics. Specifically, these layers can be directly used to better inform large-scale biogeochemical modelling, where inevitable assumptions on agricultural land use systems and management applications are currently necessary27.

Moreover, given the strong effect of agricultural land use systems in the soil organic carbon accrual, especially when involving ley system, grassland and land cover changes28, such detailed land cover representation is an a step forward to improve the LULUCF carbon accounting and setting carbon farming baselines.

Furthermore, the continuity of this Copernicus derived product can provide input to develop indicators to better track the impact of the CAP and benchmark performance of the EU agricultural sector. This should be done in combination with a thorough understanding of farmers’ decision making, for instance to tailor agri-environment schemes to improve ecosystem service supply from permanent grassland management29.

The agricultural land use systems layer19 can be directly employed to analyze and quantify the response of agricultural landscapes to extreme climate events like droughts, floods, or wildfires. By overlaying spatially explicit records of climate-driven disturbances onto yearly crop-grass sequences map19, it becomes possible to identify changes in management practices and land-use decisions triggered by these events. For instance, in the event of a flood, there might be a transition from arable land to arable land with cover crops, indicating soil recuperation. In case of land abandonment, it would be possible to identify a transition towards a herbaceous-cropping in the crop-grass sequence map.

Another potential use of the agricultural land use systems layer19 is to assess agricultural management practices by extracting, analyzing, and correlating spatially explicit information from various data sources. For example, total energy input of crop types30 can be utilized to assess agricultural intensity at a fine spatial scale. This approach can help identify patterns in agricultural practices, resource allocation, and productivity levels across different regions.
