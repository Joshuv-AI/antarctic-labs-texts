Myxoma of the round ligament mimicking femoral hernia: a diagnostic surprise in the operating room

Introduction

Groin masses in adult women are most frequently attributed to inguinal or femoral hernias; however, the round ligament of the uterus, which traverses the inguinal canal from the uterine horn to the labia majora, can itself be the origin of a variety of pathological conditions that clinically mimic these common diagnoses[1]. Round ligament pathology is overall rare, but the reported entities span both non-neoplastic lesions, such as mesothelial cysts and cystic dilation of the canal of Nuck[2,3], and neoplastic lesions, among which leiomyoma is by far the most frequently documented histological subtype[4–8].

Myxoma of the round ligament represents a considerably rarer neoplastic entity within this spectrum. It belongs to the broader family of myxoid soft-tissue tumors – mesenchymal neoplasms defined by an abundant extracellular myxoid matrix and ranging from benign lesions, such as classic myxoma, to malignant variants, including myxoid liposarcoma[9,10]. Unlike leiomyoma of the round ligament, which has been repeatedly reported, myxoma arising specifically from the round ligament has, to our knowledge, not been well characterized as a distinct clinical and histopathological entity[11].

We report a case of a benign myxoma of the round ligament presenting clinically as an uncomplicated left femoral hernia, an association that, to our knowledge, has not been previously described. We discuss the diagnostic challenge posed by this presentation, the intraoperative decision-making required when the operative findings diverged from the preoperative diagnosis, and the histopathological confirmation of the lesion; we also review the relevant literature to underline the importance of considering rare round ligament neoplasms in the differential diagnosis of groin masses in women.

This work has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria[12].

Case presentation

A 30-year-old Tunisian woman presented to the surgical outpatient clinic with progressive swelling in the left inguinal region. The mass had been present for approximately 1 year and had gradually increased in size. She reported no associated pain, discomfort, or systemic symptoms. There was no history of trauma, and the swelling did not vary with straining, coughing, or physical activity. Her medical, surgical, and family histories were unremarkable, with no connective tissue disorder, chronic cough, or straining; there was no family history of hernia, and she was not taking any regular medications. Her body mass index (BMI) was 23 kg/m2, and no other recognized hernia risk factors were identified. The patient was para 2 with regular menstrual cycles and no gynecologic complaints. She was referred for elective surgical management at our university hospital tertiary referral center; the interval between initial consultation and definitive surgery was approximately 2 months, during which the swelling remained clinically stable and unchanged in character.

On examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable, with normal vital signs. Local inspection and palpation of the left inguinal region revealed a soft-to-firm, non-tender, reducible mass measuring approximately 5 cm in diameter. The swelling was located below the Malgaigne line, medial to the femoral vessels, suggesting a femoral rather than inguinal origin. There was no cough impulse, and the overlying skin appeared normal, without erythema or discoloration. No inguinal or femoral lymphadenopathy was noted. Examination of the contralateral groin and abdomen was unremarkable. Based on these findings, a provisional diagnosis of an uncomplicated left femoral hernia was made.

The originally planned surgical technique was an open preperitoneal mesh repair using the Rives approach via an inguinal incision, in keeping with the standard practice and experience of the operating team. Routine preoperative laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, renal and liver function tests, and coagulation profile, were all within normal limits. In view of the straightforward clinical presentation and absence of concerning features, no preoperative imaging was performed. The patient was counseled regarding the presumed diagnosis of femoral hernia and the planned surgical repair, including the possibility that the procedure could be extended or modified according to intraoperative findings, and written informed consent was obtained.

Surgical exploration was performed under locoregional anesthesia using an inguinal approach, with no requirement for conversion to general anesthesia; the procedure was performed by the head of our surgical department. Contrary to the preoperative diagnosis, no femoral or inguinal hernia was identified. The femoral canal was intact, with no evidence of a peritoneal sac or herniated contents. Instead, a well-defined, encapsulated mass measuring approximately 5 cm was found arising from the round ligament. The lesion was soft, lobulated, and gelatinous on sectioning, with no infiltration of adjacent structures (Fig. 1). The inguinal canal and femoral vessels appeared normal. Given the unexpected intraoperative finding, the surgical plan was modified from a hernia repair to excision of the lesion.

The mass was meticulously dissected free from surrounding tissues while preserving adjacent neurovascular structures. The round ligament was traced proximally and distally, and the tumor was excised en bloc with the involved segment of the ligament. The excision was carried out through the same inguinal incision used for the initially planned hernia repair, without the need for a separate incision; no surgical drain was placed. Hemostasis was secured, the inguinal canal was reconstructed, and the wound was closed in layers. The total operative duration was approximately 45 minutes, with minimal, unquantified intraoperative blood loss. The specimen was submitted for histopathologic analysis.

Gross examination revealed a well-circumscribed, encapsulated, gelatinous mass measuring 5 × 4 × 4 cm, attached to a segment of the round ligament (Fig. 2). Microscopic analysis demonstrated abundant myxoid stroma containing scattered stellate and spindle-shaped cells with bland nuclear features. No mitotic figures, necrosis, or atypia were observed. A delicate capillary network was present throughout the lesion (Fig. 3). The final diagnosis was myxoma of the round ligament. Figure 2.Macroscopic examination of the excised specimen. The postoperative recovery was uneventful. The patient was discharged on the first postoperative day with instructions for wound care and analgesia. At follow-up visits 2 weeks and 3 months postoperatively, she remained asymptomatic, with complete wound healing and no evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

Anatomy and incidence of round ligament tumors. The round ligament of the uterus extends from the uterine horn through the inguinal canal to the labia majora and is composed of smooth muscle fibers, connective tissue, blood vessels, and nerves[1]. Tumors arising from this structure are rare overall. Leiomyoma is the most frequently reported neoplastic entity, with multiple case reports describing its presentation as an inguinal or femoral hernia mimic[4–8]; non-neoplastic round ligament pathologies, including mesothelial cysts, have similarly been described as hernia mimics[2,3]. Myxoma of the round ligament, as encountered in the present case, is a considerably rarer entity, and to our knowledge only isolated reports of myxoid tumors at this specific anatomical location exist in the surgical literature[11].

Clinical presentation and differential diagnosis. Clinically, round ligament tumors are frequently asymptomatic or present as a slowly enlarging, painless groin mass with non-specific physical characteristics that overlap substantially with the more common diagnoses of inguinal and femoral hernias[1,13]. Femoral hernia, in particular, is an important differential diagnosis: it accounts for a minority of groin hernias overall but occurs disproportionately in women, presenting as a reducible bulge below the inguinal ligament, and, like round ligament tumors, its diagnosis relies principally on physical examination rather than imaging[13–15]. In the present case, the clinical findings, reducibility, absence of a cough impulse, and a location below the Malgaigne line were entirely consistent with an uncomplicated femoral hernia, illustrating why round ligament tumors are so frequently misdiagnosed preoperatively.

Role of imaging: Although no preoperative imaging was obtained in the present case, given the absence of atypical clinical features and the straightforward presentation of a reducible groin bulge, imaging can play a useful role when the diagnosis of hernia is uncertain or when clinical findings are atypical[13,15]. Ultrasound is a widely accessible, low-cost, first-line modality for groin masses, while computed tomography and, particularly, magnetic resonance imaging provide more detailed soft-tissue characterization when available[9,10]. Myxoid tumors, including myxoma, typically appear hypoechoic on ultrasound and demonstrate high signal intensity on T2-weighted MRI sequences owing to their high water content, although these features are non-specific and shared with other myxoid neoplasms[9,10]. In retrospect, preoperative imaging, had it been obtained, might have raised suspicion for a solid or cystic mass distinct from a hernia sac and could have prompted a more tailored operative strategy; we acknowledge this as a limitation of the present case and now discuss the indications for imaging more explicitly in situations where clinical findings, even when reassuring, do not fully exclude an alternative diagnosis.

Histopathological differential diagnosis. Our diagnosis of myxoma was based on the identification of abundant myxoid stroma containing scattered bland stellate and spindle cells, a delicate capillary network, and the absence of mitotic activity, necrosis, or atypia. As noted during peer review, myxoid-appearing lesions in this anatomical region encompass a differential diagnosis that extends beyond primary myxoma to include aggressive angiomyxoma and myxoid degeneration of a leiomyoma, both of which are more frequently documented in the pelvic and vulvoperineal regions than primary myxoma[9,10]. Aggressive angiomyxoma typically demonstrates a more prominent, haphazardly distributed network of vessels of varying caliber, whereas myxoid leiomyoma retains at least focal fascicles of smooth-muscle differentiation; both entities can usually be further distinguished from primary myxoma by immunohistochemistry[9,10]. In the present case, the lesion was well-encapsulated, lacked the haphazard, thick-walled vascular pattern characteristic of aggressive angiomyxoma, and showed no smooth-muscle fascicular architecture on hematoxylin and eosin staining; however, immunohistochemistry was not performed, which we acknowledge as a limitation of the histopathological work-up. Our diagnosis of primary myxoma of the round ligament is therefore based on morphological grounds, and formal exclusion of myxoid leiomyoma or aggressive angiomyxoma would require immunohistochemical confirmation, which was not available in this case.

Comparison with previously reported round ligament tumors. Leiomyoma remains, by a wide margin, the most frequently documented neoplastic entity at this anatomical site, with multiple published cases describing its excision via an open inguinal approach and uniformly favorable outcomes without recurrence[4–8]. Non-neoplastic mimics, such as mesothelial cysts of the round ligament, have similarly been managed by simple excision with excellent outcomes[2,3]. Myxoma of the round ligament, by contrast, has not previously been the subject of a comparable case series; the present report, together with the sparse existing literature on myxoid tumors, suggests that, when completely excised, these lesions behave in a similarly benign fashion, with a low expected recurrence rate provided margins are clear[9–11].

Surgical approach and choice of technique. Current international guidelines recommend a laparo-endoscopic approach (TAPP or TEP) as the preferred technique for primary femoral hernia repair in women, given its superior diagnostic accuracy, with simultaneous visualization of the inguinal and femoral spaces, and its lower rate of missed or recurrent femoral hernias compared with open repair[15,16]. In the present case, an open repair using the Rives preperitoneal mesh technique via an inguinal approach was planned and performed, reflecting the operating team’s experience and familiarity with open groin hernia surgery, rather than a structured preoperative decision against a laparo-endoscopic approach; the operating surgeon, the head of our surgical department, did not have specific training in laparoscopic groin hernia repair. In keeping with current guidelines, we acknowledge that a laparo-endoscopic approach would likely have offered more complete visualization of the femoral and inguinal spaces and may have facilitated earlier recognition of the round ligament origin of the mass, and we now state this explicitly as a limitation of our surgical strategy. Beyond the specific issue of femoral hernia repair, a minimally invasive approach may also be considered for the excision of round ligament masses themselves in centers with the relevant expertise, offering improved visualization of the ligament’s course, although this must be weighed against the benefits of direct exploration when intraoperative findings are unexpected, as in the present case.

Perioperative principles. Beyond the specific surgical approach, this case underscores several broader perioperative principles. Intraoperative vigilance is essential, and the surgeon must be prepared to modify the operative plan when findings diverge from the preoperative diagnosis, as occurred here with the conversion from a planned hernia repair to tumor excision. Histopathologic evaluation of all excised masses, ideally supplemented by immunohistochemistry when the differential diagnosis includes locally aggressive or malignant myxoid entities, remains essential to confirm the diagnosis and guide postoperative surveillance. Preoperative counseling should also address the possibility that intraoperative findings may differ from clinical expectations, particularly in the absence of confirmatory imaging.

Strengths and limitations. The principal strength of this report lies in its detailed operative and histopathological documentation of an exceptionally rare entity, together with a structured review of the relevant literature on round ligament pathology. Its main limitations are the absence of preoperative imaging, the lack of immunohistochemical confirmation of the histopathological diagnosis, the use of an open, rather than guideline-recommended, laparo-endoscopic surgical approach, and a relatively short follow-up period of 3 months. Longer surveillance would be required to more definitively confirm the absence of recurrence, and we recommend that future reports of this rare entity include extended follow-up where possible.

Conclusion

In summary, this case of a round ligament myxoma mimicking a femoral hernia illustrates the diagnostic complexity of groin masses in women. Surgeons should be cognizant of rare mesenchymal tumors and include them in the differential diagnosis of groin swellings. When clinical features are atypical, adjunctive imaging and careful intraoperative assessment enhance diagnostic accuracy and optimize perioperative management. Preoperative imaging and, where diagnostic uncertainty persists intraoperatively, immunohistochemical analysis can further support accurate diagnosis and guide appropriate perioperative management.
