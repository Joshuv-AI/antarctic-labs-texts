Impact of Receiving Hospital on Out‐of‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest Outcome: Racial and Ethnic Disparities in Texas
Authors: Ryan Huebinger, Marina Del Rios, Benjamin S Abella, Bryan McNally, Carrie Bakunas, Richard Witkov, Micah Panczyk, Eric Boerwinkle, Bentley Bobrow
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFactors associated with out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) outcome disparities remain poorly understood. We evaluated the role of receiving hospital on OHCA outcome disparities.Methods and ResultsWe studied people with OHCA who survived to hospital admission from TX‐CARES (Texas Cardiac Arrest Registry to Enhance Survival), 2014 to 2021. Using census data, we stratified OHCAs into majority (>50%) strata: non‐Hispanic White race and ethnicity, non‐Hispanic Black race and ethnicity, and Hispanic or Latino ethnicity. We stratified hospitals into performance quartiles based on the primary outcome, survival with good neurologic outcome. We evaluated the association between race and ethnicity and care at higher‐performance hospitals. We compared 3 models evaluating the association between race and ethnicity and outcome: (1) ignoring hospital, (2) adjusting for hospital as a random intercept, and (3) adjusting for hospital performance quartile. We adjusted models for possible confounders. We included 10 434 OHCAs. Hospital performance quartile outcome rates ranged from 11.3% (fourth) to 37.1% (first). Compared with OHCAs in neighborhoods of majority White race, those in neighborhoods of majority Black race (odds ratio [OR], 0.1 [95% CI, 0.1–0.1]) and Hispanic or Latino ethnicity (OR, 0.2 [95% CI, 0.2–0.2]) were less likely to be cared for at higher‐performing hospitals. Compared with White neighborhoods (30.1%) and ignoring hospital, outcomes were worse in Black neighborhoods (15.4%; adjusted OR [aOR], 0.5 [95% CI, 0.4–0.5]) and Hispanic or Latino neighborhoods (19.2%; aOR, 0.6 [95% CI, 0.5–0.7]). Adjusting for hospital as a random intercept, outcomes improved for Black neighborhoods (aOR, 0.9 [95% CI, 0.7–1.05]) and Hispanic or Latino neighborhoods (aOR, 0.9 [95% CI, 0.8–0.99]). Adjusting for hospital performance quartile, outcomes improved for Black neighborhoods (aOR, 0.8 [95% CI, 0.7–1.01]) and Hispanic or Latino neighborhoods (aOR, 0.9 [95% CI, 0.8–0.996]).ConclusionsIn Black and Hispanic or Latino communities, OHCAs were less likely to be cared for at higher‐performing hospitals, and adjusting for receiving hospital improved OHCA outcome disparities.Keywords: cardiac arrest, hospital care, out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrestSubject Categories: Race and Ethnicity, Epidemiology

Individuals who experience out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) from minority and lower socioeconomic status communities often receive inferior care and sustain significantly worse outcomes after cardiac arrest. As Garcia et al recently found, these trends continue to be a significant issue in the United States, 1 despite a growing knowledge of disparities for more than a decade. 2

A major focus of cardiac arrest disparities has been bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), and many investigations have found that cardiac arrests occurring in Black and Latino neighborhoods experience lower rates of bystander CPR. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 However, research suggests that differences in bystander CPR do not fully explain outcome disparities. 8 This begs the question of what other components of cardiac arrest care could explain these observed outcome disparities.

Prior research links receiving hospital to outcomes after cardiac arrest. 9 , 10 We have additionally found that individuals who experience OHCA who are Black race, Hispanic or Latino ethnicity, and of lower socioeconomic status receive lower rates of hospital‐based, postarrest care modalities, primarily targeted temperature management (TTM) and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). 11 , 12 The best approaches to patient selection and application of both TTM 13 , 14 and PCI 15 , 16 are still actively being studied, but these therapies are associated with improved survival after OHCA. Although these therapies are certainly important aspects of hospital‐based care after cardiac arrest, there may also be other factors that contribute to outcomes, such as withdrawal of care policies and intensity of critical care strategies. In addition, the role of receiving hospital on outcome disparities after OHCA has not been previously studied.

We sought to evaluate the role of receiving hospital and receiving hospital performance on cardiac arrest outcome disparities using a prospectively collected OHCA registry in Texas, Texas Cardiac Arrest Registry to Enhance Survival (TX‐CARES). We hypothesize that patients from minority neighborhoods are cared for at lower‐performing hospital more frequently and that hospital performance impacts cardiac arrest outcome disparities.

METHODS

Circulation of data supporting this study is strictly limited by rules established by Cardiac Arrest Registry to Enhance Survival (CARES). Please contact the TX‐CARES state coordinator (micah.j.panczyk@uth.tmc.edu) with any questions.

Data Source

We performed a retrospective analysis of the TX‐CARES database. TX‐CARES is a state affiliate of CARES, the largest cardiac arrest registry in the United States. CARES includes data on nontraumatic, cardiac arrests for which resuscitation was attempted. Local emergency medical services (EMS) agencies and hospitals collect and submit cardiac arrest data to CARES for data validation and consolidation. CARES ensures uniformity of data submitted to the registry using standardized international Utstein definitions for reporting OHCA clinical variables and outcomes. 17 CARES is a collaborative effort of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and Emory University, Woodruff Health Sciences, and further details and methods for CARES have been previously described. 18 , 19 In conjunction with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, CARES combines cardiac arrest data with US census tract data for research. Census tracts are small county subdivisions or statistically equivalent entities that average 4000 (range, 1200–8000) inhabitants. Census tracts are defined by local government entities or the Census Bureau in situations where local entities declined to participate. 20 State CARES participants, like TX‐CARES, may request state data sets for research purposes. The TX‐CARES Data Sharing Committee as well as the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects and Institutional Review Board approved this research study. The study was given exempt status, and subject consent was not required.

Study Population

We included TX‐CARES OHCA cases from January 1, 2014 to December 31, 2021. In 2021, 54 EMS agencies and 178 hospitals submitted data to TX‐CARES. The data set included 2826 census tracts with a total population of 11 791 282 people, representing 39.9% of the Texas population. We included all nontraumatic, OHCA cases where resuscitation was attempted and the patient survived to hospital admission. We excluded patients aged <18 years and cases with missing data (N=95 [0.7%]).

Study Variables and Outcomes

We defined patient characteristics as age, patient race and ethnicity documented by EMS provider (non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, Hispanic or Latino, and all other races [American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, or Unknown]), and sex. We defined cardiac arrest characteristics as witness of the cardiac arrest, location of cardiac arrest, and initial shockable rhythm. We defined outcomes as survival to hospital discharge and survival with favorable neurologic outcome, cerebral performance category 1 (good: conscious, alert, able to work; might have mild neurologic or psychologic deficit) or 2 (moderate: conscious, sufficient cerebral function for independent activities of daily life; able to work in sheltered environment), as defined by CARES. 19

We defined neighborhood race in a similar manner to our prior research. 6 , 12 We stratified census tract race and ethnicity into 3 categories based on census tract proportion of race and ethnicity: majority White race (>50% non‐Hispanic or Latino White), majority Black race (>50% non‐Hispanic or Latino Black), and majority Hispanic or Latino ethnicity (>50% Hispanic or Latino). As a sensitivity analysis, we additionally created >80% cohorts for race and ethnicity, if the census tract contained >80% of a race and ethnicity. Using data from all patients surviving to admission with outcome data, we then created hospital performance quartiles based on the hospital rate for each outcome: (1) survival to hospital discharge and (2) survival with favorable neurologic outcome.

For our primary analysis, we defined our outcomes as survival to hospital discharge and survival with favorable neurologic status.

Statistical Analysis

We first described patient and cardiac arrest characteristics for each stratum. When describing bystander CPR rates, we used the CARES definition, excluding cases that are witnessed by EMS or occurred in a nursing home or medical facility. In addition, when calculating bystander automated external defibrillator application rates, CARES excludes cases witnessed by EMS personnel or that occurred in a home/residence, nursing home, or medical facility. We then described the distribution of OHCAs treated at each hospital performance quartile, stratified by race and ethnicity quartile. Next, we described outcome rates at each hospital quartile.

To evaluate the association between race and ethnicity and receiving hospital, we fit ordinal logistic regression models for each outcome to evaluate the association between race and ethnicity and receipt of care and improving hospital performance quartile (fourth to first) with majority White race as a reference and race and ethnicity handled as a categorical variable. To evaluate the association between hospital performance quartile and outcomes for the entire cohort and for each stratum, we used ordinal logistic regression to evaluate the association between receipt of care at improving performance quartile hospitals and outcomes. For survival, we used survival to hospital discharge performance quartiles as the independent variable; and for survival with favorable neurologic survival, we used favorable neurologic performance quartiles as the independent variable.

For our primary analysis, we created 3 models and compared their results. For the first model, we evaluated the association between community race and ethnicity and outcomes, adjusted for age, sex, witness of arrest, location of arrest, and initial shockable rhythm. For our second model, we used a mixed model logistic regression with the same independent, dependent, and covariables as model 1, but we additionally modeled receiving hospital as a random intercept. Our third model was the same as model 1, but we included hospital performance quartile as a covariable. We then compared the change in odds ratio (OR) between model 1 and models 2/3. We defined a significant difference as an OR in model 2 or 3 for which the 95% CI did not include the OR from model 1.

As a sensitivity analysis, we performed the above analysis but compared >80% Black race and >80% Hispanic or Latino ethnicity with >80% White race.

Statistical analyses were performed using standard commercial software (Stata, version 16.1; Statacorp, College Station, TX).

RESULTS

Of 54 206 cardiac arrests from 2014 to 2021 in TX‐CARES, 14 341 survived to admission. We excluded 392 patients with age <18 years, 1 patient with missing sex, 6 patients with missing age, 69 patients with missing census tract race and ethnicity, and 19 patients with missing outcome data. Of individuals who experienced cardiac arrest who were cared for in census tracts that met our majority census tract definition (>50% of a race and ethnicity), we included 10 434 cardiac arrests that survived to hospital admission to 144 hospitals. The survival to admission rate was similar between race and ethnicity strata: 26.0% for majority White race, 27.2% for majority Black race, and 26.7% for majority Hispanic or Latino ethnicity. For the included cohort, the median age was 62 (interquartile range, 52–72) years, 59.5% were men, White was the most common race (41.7%), home was the most common OHCA location (67.2%), and almost half of cases were witnessed by bystanders (49.7%). A total of 14.4% of cases had a shockable initial rhythm, 3452 of 7116 (48.5%) received bystander CPR, and 186 of 1493 (11.1%) received bystander automated external defibrillator.

Comparing neighborhood race and ethnicity strata using majority race and ethnicity as the strata definition, the age was similar among strata. Home/residence location of arrest was the most frequent for majority Black neighborhoods, and bystander‐witnessed arrest was more common for majority White neighborhoods. Bystander CPR and automated external defibrillator were more common for majority White neighborhoods (Table). Comparing the proportion of patients cared for at increasing hospital survival performance quartiles, those living in majority Black neighborhoods (OR, 0.3 [95% CI, 0.3–0.3]) and majority Hispanic or Latino neighborhoods (OR, 0.2 [95% CI, 0.2–0.3]) were less likely to be treated at higher‐performance quartile hospitals. Better hospital survival performance quartile was linked to increasing rates of survival to hospital discharge for the cohort as a whole (OR, 1.4 [95% CI, 1.3–1.4]) as well as each stratum: majority White race (OR, 1.3 [95% CI, 1.3–1.4]), majority Black race (OR, 1.3 [95% CI, 1.1–1.5]), and majority Hispanic or Latino ethnicity (OR, 1.3 [95% CI, 1.3–1.4]). For survival with favorable neurologic status, majority Black race (OR, 0.1 [95% CI, 0.1–0.1]) and majority Hispanic or Latino ethnicity (OR, 0.2 [95% CI, 0.2–0.2]) were much less likely to be cared for at higher‐performance hospitals. As with survival to discharge, there were associations between better‐performance quartile hospitals and survival with favorable neurologic status for the cohort as a whole (OR, 1.8 [95% CI, 1.5–2.1]) as well as each stratum: majority White race (OR, 1.6 [95% CI, 1.4–1.7]), majority Black race (OR, 1.8 [95% CI, 1.5–2.1]), and majority Hispanic or Latino ethnicity (OR, 1.6 [95% CI, 1.5–1.7]) (Figure 1 and Table S2). These findings were similar when using 80% race and ethnicity as our definition in the sensitivity analysis with 2 exceptions: for majority Black race, there were no associations between either increasing survival to discharge performance quartile or survival with favorable neurologic status quartile and better outcomes (Table S3).

Evaluating the association between census tract race and ethnicity and survival with favorable neurologic status, we found similar results. Although majority Black race (aOR, 0.5 [95% CI, 0.4–0.5]) and majority Hispanic or Latino ethnicity (aOR, 0.6 [95% CI, 0.5–0.7]) were associated with lower odds of survival with favorable neurologic status when ignoring receiving hospital, these associations were no longer present for majority Black race when adjusting for receiving hospital as a random intercept (aOR, 0.9 [95% CI, 0.7–1.1]) and hospital performance quartile (aOR, 0.8 [95% CI, 0.7–1.01]). Although majority Hispanic or Latino ethnicity still had a significantly lower odds of survival with favorable neurologic status after adjusting for receiving hospital, the odds of survival with favorable neurologic status significantly improved when adjusting for hospital as a random intercept (aOR, 0.9 [95% CI, 0.8–0.99]) and hospital performance quartile (aOR, 0.9 [95% CI, 0.8–0.996]; Figure 2).

Sensitivity Analysis

Using 80% of race and ethnicity to define strata, survival to hospital discharge for >80% Black race was not different from >80% White race in any of our models. There was higher odds of survival with favorable neurologic status when adjusting for hospital performance quartile (aOR, 0.8 [95% CI, 0.45–1.4]) when compared with the model ignoring receiving hospital (aOR, 0.4 [95% CI, 0.3–0.7]). For >80% Hispanic or Latino ethnicity, adjusting for receiving hospital quartile showed significantly higher survival to hospital discharge (aOR, 1.2 [95% CI, 0.95–1.6] versus aOR, 0.8 [95% CI, 0.7–1.0] when ignoring receiving hospital) and survival with favorable neurologic status (aOR, 1.0 [95% CI, 0.7–1.4] versus aOR, 0.6 [95% CI, 0.5–0.7] when ignoring receiving hospital; Table S4).

DISCUSSION

In the first analysis of the impact of receiving hospital on OHCA outcome disparities using data from the TX‐CARES, we found that receiving hospital may play a significant role in OHCA outcome disparities. Individuals who experience OHCA from minority neighborhoods are cared for at higher‐performing hospitals less often despite the link between hospital performance quartile and outcomes for all race and ethnicity strata. Most important, when adjusting for receiving hospital and hospital performance quartile, associations between neighborhood minority race and ethnicity and worse outcomes significantly diminish.

Historically, OHCA disparities research has focused on bystander response, primarily bystander CPR. This is logical given the strong link between bystander CPR and improved outcomes after OHCA. 21 , 22 Multiple publications have identified disparities in bystander CPR for OHCAs occurring in minority 1 , 2 , 3 , 5 , 6 , 7 and lower socioeconomic status 5 , 6 , 7 , 23 communities. However, as Chan et al found, adjusting for bystander CPR did not change the association between minority neighborhoods and outcome (aOR, 0.88 [95% CI, 0.82–0.95] without adjusting versus aOR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.84–0.98] after adjusting for bystander CPR). 8 Although bystander CPR is undeniably important, ‘the findings by Chan et al emphasize the need to identify other contributors to cardiac arrest disparities. In addition, we found that survival to admission rates were similar between strata, despite much worse hospital outcomes for majority Black race and Hispanic or Latino ethnicity. Although this differs from older data using EMS provider‐documented race, 24 the discordance between similar survival to admission rates and disparate hospital outcomes suggests that hospital care likely contributes to outcome disparities.

In our prior work, we found that receiving hospital plays a significant role in outcomes after OHCA, 9 corroborating findings from prior research. 10 In addition, we identified lower rates of postarrest interventions, TTM, and PCI for individuals who experience OHCA from minority 12 and lower socioeconomic status 11 neighborhoods in comparison to White and higher socioeconomic status neighborhoods. However, these associations diminished when adjusting for receiving hospital, suggesting that there are likely interhospital disparities in postarrest care. In addition, Morris et al identified lower odds of TTM at Hispanic or Latino serving hospitals (OR, 0.59 [95% CI, 0.47–0.74]). 25 Paired with the presented findings, interhospital disparities in care could be a significant contributor to OHCA outcome disparities.

There are many possible reasons that receiving hospital might play an important role in cardiac arrest outcomes. Postarrest care modalities, such as TTM 26 , 27 and PCI, 28 , 29 , 30 are important interventions for cardiac arrest that have been linked to improved outcomes. Although the recent TTM‐1 and TTM‐2 trials have called into question the ubiquitous benefit of TTM to 33 °C after OHCA, the trials do not completely refute the utility of TTM to prevent fever. The TTM‐1 trial compared TTM dosed to 33 °C verses normothermia dosed to 36 °C, and TTM‐2 compared TTM dosed to 33 °C versus a fever prevention strategy. However, in the fever prevention arm of TTM‐2, almost half of subjects still received device‐based TTM. As we found in our prior work, OHCAs from Black and Hispanic or Latino neighborhoods received lower rates of TTM, rates that were much lower than the fever prevention group arm of TTM‐2 (22.4% for Black and 26.9% for Hispanic or Latino individuals).

PCI is another possible contributor to outcome disparities, as we found low rates of PCI for OHCAs from Black (2.7%) and Hispanic or Latino (4.2%) communities compared with White (9.0%) communities. 12 These rates vary considerably from reported rates of culprit coronary artery lesions that required stent placement in a clinical trial of patients without ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (28.5%). 15 Last, early withdrawal of life‐sustaining therapy may be an important contributor to disparities as withdrawal of life‐sustaining therapy varies significantly and is linked to worse outcomes. 31 , 32 Signs of neurologic recovery can be absent before 72 hours, 33 , 34 so it is possible that early termination of care could occur more often at lower‐performing hospitals. Unfortunately, we were previously unable to develop models to evaluate early withdrawal of life‐sustaining therapy using data from CARES.

This is the first study we are aware of to find that hospital care contributes to minority cardiac arrest outcome disparities, but we did not evaluate what differences in hospital care account for the observed findings. Further research should evaluate different aspects of postarrest care and their influence on cardiac arrest outcome disparities. Our prior research suggests that disparities in postarrest care exist, but little is known about interhospital disparities in postarrest care. In addition, it is unknown if our findings exist at a national level. If these findings exist at a national level, then receiving hospital may be play a crucial role in OHCA outcome disparities and could represent an important target for interventions to improve cardiac arrest disparities.

Limitations

There are notable limitations to our study. We studied only OHCAs in Texas. However, TX‐CARES is the second largest state CARES, and Texas is 1 of the most diverse states in the United States. It is not known if our results are generalizable to other states or at a national level. The predominance of contributing agencies to TX‐CARES are in urban environments, so our findings may not be generalizable to rural OHCAs. Although evaluating different models that ignore and adjust for receiving hospital is an indirect model for evaluating the influence of receiving hospital on outcome disparities, there are alternative methods that may more directly evaluate disparities. One alternative would be to perform a difference‐in‐difference analysis, or alternatively an analysis could define hospitals based on race and ethnicity of the population served. Future studies could also consider causal analysis.

Minority race and ethnicity are both linked to cardiac arrest outcomes, but we did not adjust for or evaluate socioeconomic characteristics in this study. Socioeconomic status may act as a moderator between race and ethnicity and surviving hospital, outcomes, or both. Future research could evaluate the role of socioeconomic status on receiving hospital performance and outcomes. Sex may also play a role in race and ethnicity disparities, and although we did adjust for sex in our models, future studies could evaluate the interaction between race and ethnicity and sex. Preexisting health conditions and time to initial CPR might influence outcomes, but we were unable to adjust for these within the constraints of the data set. The receiving hospital is chosen by EMS, which may have preferences for which hospital they transport to, but we were unable to adjust for this. We were unable to adjust for the type of hospital (patient volume, urbanicity, and academic affiliation) caring for the patient, which may bias our results. Although we do have data on cardiac arrest and prehospital patient features, we have limited ability to evaluate patient or clinical care characteristics in the hospital. We only included patients who survived to hospitalization, so these results do not apply to patients who did not survive to hospitalization. However, survival to hospitalization was similar between our strata.

CONCLUSIONS

OHCAs occurring in all communities benefited from being cared for at higher‐performing hospitals, but in Black and Hispanic or Latino communities, OHCAs were much less likely to be cared for at higher‐performing hospitals compared with White communities. Adjusting for receiving hospital and receiving hospital performance quartile both significantly improved disparities in OHCA outcomes in Texas. Receiving hospitals may play a critical role in the association between minority race and ethnicity and worse cardiac arrest outcomes.
