Matrix and intermembrane space-specific mitochondrial stress response in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Shatakshi Shukla, Devin Kolmetzky, Shanikumar Goyani, Kunal Samantaray, Anupriya Sinha, Philip A. Kramer, Dhanendra Tomar, Pooja Jadiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) lacks effective therapies, partly due to an incomplete understanding of mitochondrial dysfunction, a key driver of neurodegeneration. Mitochondria activate the unfolded protein response (UPRmt) to maintain proteostasis, but the roles of matrix- and intermembrane space (IMS)-associated stress responses in AD remain unclear. Here, we used human microglial-like cells expressing mutant amyloid precursor protein together with compartment-targeted mitochondrial proteotoxic stressors to investigate matrix (UPRmt-MM) and IMS (UPRmt-IMS) stress responses. RNA-seq revealed activation of mitochondrial stress pathways and suppression of synaptic and lipid signaling in AD-like cells. UPRmt-MM promoted robust immune activation, severe oxidative phosphorylation defects, increased mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, and cell death. In contrast, UPRmt-IMS preferentially induced interferon signaling and suppressed antioxidant pathways. Notably, suppression of ATF5-dependent UPRmt signaling rescued mitochondrial dysfunction and reduced Aβ release. Together, these findings demonstrate that matrix- and IMS-targeted mitochondrial stress elicit distinct microglial responses and identify mitochondrial proteostasis as a potential therapeutic target in AD.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by cognitive decline and the accumulation of amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tangles. Despite extensive research, effective disease-modifying therapies remain lacking, largely due to an incomplete understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving disease progression1. Mitochondrial dysfunction has emerged as an early and central feature of AD, encompassing impairments in bioenergetics, redox balance, and protein homeostasis, and preceding classical pathological hallmarks2–8. However, the precise molecular pathways linking mitochondrial dysfunction to neurodegeneration remain incompletely defined. A key regulator of mitochondrial proteostasis is the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt)9,10, an adaptive signaling pathway that maintains protein quality control through the induction of chaperones, proteases, and stress-response programs11–14. In the context of AD, both Aβ15–18 and hyperphosphorylated tau19 have been shown to activate UPRmt, and elevated expression of UPRmt-associated genes have been observed in experimental models and patient samples15. However, whether UPRmt-associated signaling is protective or maladaptive in AD remains unresolved.

Importantly, most studies have treated mitochondrial proteostasis as a uniform process20, overlooking the functional heterogeneity of mitochondrial subcompartments. The mitochondrial matrix (MM) supports core metabolic pathways such as the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and relies on specialized chaperones and proteases for enzyme folding21,22. In contrast, the intermembrane space (IMS) hosts proteins involved in the electron transport chain (ETC) and apoptotic signaling, requiring distinct oxidoreductase systems adapted to its unique redox environment23,24. These compartments activate specialized stress responses25–27, yet their individual contributions to AD pathogenesis remain largely undefined. As a result, global UPRmt-associated signaling may lack specificity and potentially exacerbate dysfunction, underscoring the need for compartment-specific investigation of UPRmt signaling.

Here, we address this gap by investigating compartment-specific mitochondrial proteotoxic stress in microglia, distinguishing matrix-specific (UPRmt-MM) and IMS-specific (UPRmt-IMS) stress responses. Microglia are central mediators of neuroinflammation in AD and play critical roles in synaptic remodeling, immune regulation, and clearance of pathological aggregates, processes that are highly dependent on mitochondrial function and therefore sensitive to disruptions in mitochondrial proteostasis28,29. Although direct in vivo evidence linking microglial UPRmt to AD progression remains limited, studies using human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived models indicate that UPRmt-associated signaling drives metabolic rewiring, impairs phagocytosis, and promotes pro-inflammatory signaling30. Consistent with these observations, advanced microglia–brain assembloid and organoid models further reveal that mitochondrial stress responses in microglia can disrupt intercellular communication and propagate brain-wide senescence and neurodegenerative signatures. Together, these findings suggest that defects in mitochondrial proteostasis reshape microglial function and intercellular networks, contributing to neuroinflammatory and degenerative processes in AD. Given that mitochondrial dysfunction in microglia exacerbates neurodegeneration by promoting inflammatory responses and impairing the clearance of toxic proteins28,29, these observations provide strong justification for investigating compartment-specific mitochondrial stress responses and UPRmt -associated signaling in microglia.

To investigate these mechanisms, we used human microglial-like cells (HMC3) stably expressing amyloid precursor protein (APP) carrying the Swedish, Florida, and London mutations (APPswe/F/L)31, thereby modeling amyloidogenic stress. To investigate compartment-specific mitochondrial stress responses, we employed established misfolded protein models targeting either MM or IMS. UPRmt-MM was induced by expression of a deletion mutant of ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC-Δ)32, whereas UPRmt-IMS was modeled using a misfolding-prone double-mutant firefly luciferase33 (IMS-Fluc-DM-mut) targeted to the IMS. This approach enabled systematic analysis of transcriptional responses, mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, amyloid processing, and susceptibility to cell death. Using transcriptomic profiling and complementary functional analyses, we examined global and mitochondrial gene expression changes in AD-like microglial cells and under compartment-specific proteotoxic stress conditions. AD-like microglial cells displayed activation of mitochondrial stress pathways and ribosome biogenesis alongside suppression of synaptic and lipid signaling. Compartment-specific stress further amplified these effects in distinct ways. AD-UPRmt-MM stress induces robust immune activation, NF-κB signaling, and severe inhibition of OXPHOS, alongside increased mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) and activation of cell-death pathways. In contrast, AD-UPRmt-IMS primarily triggers type I interferon signaling and suppression of mitochondrial ribosome integrity and antioxidant defenses. These findings suggest that AD-associated mitochondrial stress primes microglia for vulnerability, whereas compartment-specific insults drive divergent stress responses. Under severe conditions, such stress appears to override adaptive UPRmt -associated signaling, indicating a shift toward maladaptive responses in AD. In addition, suppression of ATF5-dependent UPRmt signaling restored mitochondrial function and reduced Aβ release, supporting a maladaptive role for sustained UPRmt signaling under chronic APP-associated stress. Collectively, this study provides mechanistic insight into how mitochondrial proteostasis failure shapes microglial dysfunction and highlights compartment-specific mitochondrial stress pathways as potential therapeutic targets in AD.

Results

Compartment-specific mitochondrial proteotoxic stress drives distinct transcriptomic signatures in AD-like microglial cells

To validate compartment-specific mitochondrial proteotoxic stress, we introduced targeted stress-inducing constructs into human microglial-like HMC3 cells stably expressing amyloid precursor protein (APP) carrying the Swedish, Florida, and London mutations (APPswe/F/L), hereafter referred to as AD-like cells. Matrix-specific mitochondrial stress (UPRmt-MM) was induced using OTC-Δ, while IMS-specific mitochondrial stress (UPRmt-IMS) was modeled using IMS-Fluc-DM-mut fused to the HTRA2 targeting sequence. Both constructs are established tools for inducing compartment-specific mitochondrial proteotoxic stress32,33 and have been previously validated for mitochondrial targeting. Construct expression was confirmed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) and confocal imaging (Supplementary Fig. S1A–D). qPCR showed robust expression of OTC-Δ in AD-UPRmt-MM cells (HMC3-APPswe/F/L-OTC-Δ) and IMS-Fluc-DM-mut in AD-UPRmt-IMS cells (HMC3-APPswe/F/L-IMS-Fluc-DM-mut) compared with wild-type (WT) and AD-like (HMC3-APPswe/F/L) cells (Supplementary Fig. S1A, B). Confocal imaging showed mitochondrial localization of both constructs relative to ATP5A1 and served as supportive evidence of mitochondrial targeting (Supplementary Fig. S1C, D). To directly confirm compartment-specific localization, we performed mitochondrial subfractionation and a digitonin-dependent protease protection assay (Supplementary Fig. S1E). OTC-Δ remained protected from protease digestion until disruption of both mitochondrial membranes, consistent with matrix localization, whereas IMS-Fluc-DM-mut became accessible upon outer membrane permeabilization, consistent with IMS localization. Together, these data provide biochemical confirmation of accurate compartment-specific targeting of the UPRmt constructs.

Having established compartment-specific mitochondrial stress, we next examined global transcriptional changes using RNA-seq. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct transcriptomic profiles among WT, AD, AD-UPRmt-MM, and AD-UPRmt-IMS cells, with PC1 (47.19%) and PC2 (17.00%) explaining a substantial proportion of variance (Fig. 1A). AD-UPRmt-MM samples showed the greatest separation, indicating pronounced transcriptional remodeling. Differential expression analysis identified ~3382 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between WT vs AD, ~1198 between AD vs AD-UPRmt-MM, ~327 between AD vs AD-UPRmt-IMS (DESeq2, adjusted p < 0.05, |log2FC| ≥ 1). Heatmaps revealed distinct global and mitochondrial expression patterns (MitoCarta), with further amplification of transcriptional changes under compartment-specific stress (Supplementary Fig. S1F, G). MitoCarta analysis highlighted strong dysregulation of OXPHOS and proteostasis pathways, particularly under AD-UPRmt-MM stress (Supplementary Fig. S1G). Downregulation of mitochondrial-encoded OXPHOS genes (MT-CYB, MT-CO3, MT-CO2, MT-ATP6) suggested impaired Complex III–V activity under MM stress, whereas AD-UPRmt-IMS upregulated interferon-related genes (XAF1, IFI44L, OAS2, TRIM22, RSAD2, IFIH1), reflecting activation of innate immune pathways34,35. Volcano plots and global heatmaps further illustrated differential expression across conditions (Fig. 1B–E; Supplementary Fig. S1F). Relative to WT, AD-like cells upregulated genes associated with cytoskeletal organization and stress signaling (ALDH1A, RPS28, MAP1A, RPS28P7, TIE1, ID1, PML) and downregulated genes linked to metabolic regulation (EPB41L3, SH3BGRL, MMP1, GPC4, SKAP2, TTLL7, ZNF518A, PLPPR4, E3B41L) (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Fig. S1F). Compared with AD-like cells, AD-UPRmt-MM induced innate immune genes (RSAD2, TNFSF10, IFIT2, TNFAIP3, KLF4, FZD4, XAF1) while suppressing mitochondrial OXPHOS genes (MT-CYB, MT-CO3, MT-ATP6, MT-CO2, MT-ATP8, PLCE1, KIF20A) (Fig. 1C; Supplementary Fig. S1F). In contrast, AD-UPRmt-IMS strongly upregulated interferon-stimulated genes (XAF1, IFI44L, OAS2, TRIM22, RSAD2, PIK3AP1, IFIH1) and downregulated genes related to mitochondrial ribosome integrity (EIF3C, FP, STX1A, RPS19, IPO4, HES5, ENDOG, ZSCAN) (Fig. 1D; Supplementary Fig. S1F). Direct comparison of AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS revealed distinct stress signatures, with MM stress associated with metabolic dysfunction and survival pathways, and IMS stress associated with antiviral and inflammatory signaling (Fig. 1E; Supplementary Fig. S1F).

Intersection analysis further defined shared and unique transcriptional responses (Fig. 1F, G). WT versus AD-like cells showed the largest DEG set (2713 genes), while AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS contributed additional stress-specific subsets (284 and 313 genes, respectively) (Fig. 1F). A core set of 10,933 genes was shared across all conditions, while unique subsets (WT: 399; AD: 217; AD-UPRmt-MM: 274; AD-UPRmt-IMS: 80) highlighted compartment-specific transcriptional remodeling (Fig. 1G). These findings indicate that APP-driven and compartment-specific mitochondrial stress synergistically reprogram transcriptional networks in microglia.

To determine whether compartment-specific stress alters the integrated stress response (ISR), we examined key pathway proteins. Across both AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS conditions, we detected a clear divergence between upstream stress‑sensing pathways and downstream UPRmt-associated transcriptional components. Phosphorylation of eIF2α (p-eIF2α) was markedly elevated in both models, indicating strong activation of the ISR. In contrast, protein levels of the canonical UPRmt transcription factors ATF4, DELE1 and CHOP were significantly reduced with no change in ATF5 (Fig. 1H–J; Supplementary Fig. S1H–I). These findings indicate persistent ISR activation accompanied by reduced activity of the canonical ATF4–CHOP–ATF5 UPRmt-associated transcriptional axis. Concurrent upregulation of p-eIF2α indicates activation of cytosolic stress responses with no change in p-HSF levels (Fig. 1H; Supplementary Fig. S1J, K). The selective induction of p-eIF2α, together with suppression of canonical UPRmt -associated transcription factors, points to a shift away from mitochondrial proteostasis programs toward alternative chaperone-mediated stress responses and promote translation of stress-response genes. Collectively, these data demonstrate that matrix- and IMS-targeted mitochondrial stress elicit distinct stress-response programs in AD-like microglia, characterized by sustained ISR activation, altered UPRmt -associated signaling, and induction of HSF1. These findings complement the transcriptomic analyses and support the conclusion that compartment-specific mitochondrial stress drives differential cellular responses in AD-like microglia.

Immune activation and bioenergetic suppression under AD-like microglial cells and compartment-specific mitochondrial stress

To gain functional insights into DEGs, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses (Fig. 2A–D; Supplementary Fig. S2A–H). Comparison of WT and AD-like cells revealed enrichment of mitochondrial stress, proteostasis, and apoptotic pathways, including mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), regulation of proteolysis, and mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP) opening (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. S2A, B). Upregulated biological processes included ribosome biogenesis, mitochondrial membrane organization, and protein quality control, indicating enhanced mitochondrial translation and proteostasis. Additional enriched terms were related to cytoskeletal organization and ubiquitin-mediated protein turnover (Supplementary Fig. S2A). In contrast, downregulated pathways were primarily associated with synaptic function and lipid signaling, including synaptic vesicle exocytosis, calcium-regulated neurotransmitter release, and vesicle fusion (Supplementary Fig. S2B). These changes indicate a shift from neuronal communication toward stress adaptation in AD-like microglial cells. Suppression of sterol response and collagen-activated signaling suggests disruptions in lipid homeostasis and extracellular matrix organization36. Consistent with these findings, synaptic regulators (STX1B, SYT2, SNAP25) and lipid metabolism genes (ABCA1, CYP1B1) were downregulated. Compared to AD-like cells, AD-UPRmt-MM induced strong immune and inflammatory signaling, including cytokine-mediated pathways, antiviral responses, and interferon signaling (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Fig. S2C), with upregulation of RSAD2, TNFSF10, IFIH1, OAS2, and TRIM22. In contrast, mitochondrial energy metabolism and protein synthesis pathways were strongly downregulated, including OXPHOS, ATP biosynthesis, and cytoplasmic translation (Supplementary Fig. S2D). Downregulated genes included mitochondrial-encoded ETC components (MT-ND1, MT-ND4, MT-CO1, and MT-ATP6), indicating impaired mitochondrial respiration. Together, these findings indicate that AD-UPRmt-MM promotes immune activation coupled with severe bioenergetic suppression. Similarly, AD-UPRmt-IMS stress activated immune and antiviral programs, including TLR and chemokine signaling, with upregulation of CXCL10, CXCL11, RSAD2, IFI44L, and OAS2 (Fig. 2C; Supplementary Fig. S2E). Downregulated pathways overlapped with MM stress, including OXPHOS and mitochondrial membrane organization (Supplementary Fig. S2F). However, IMS stress more prominently engaged interferon-driven responses with comparatively less suppression of metabolic pathways, indicating distinct compartment-specific stress-response programs.

Direct comparison of AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS revealed distinct functional signatures (Fig. 2D; Supplementary Fig. S2G, H). AD-UPRmt-MM displayed stronger induction of cytokine signaling, chemotaxis, and antiviral defense, with key genes including TNFAIP3, CXCL10, CXCL11, RSAD2, and IFIH1. In contrast, metabolic and translational pathways were more strongly suppressed under MM stress, including OXPHOS, ATP biosynthesis, and cytoplasmic translation (Supplementary Fig. S2G). Correspondingly, mitochondrial-encoded ETC components (MT-ND1, MT-ND4, MT-CO1, and MT-ATP6) and ribosomal genes were downregulated, indicating impaired mitochondrial respiration and protein synthesis (Supplementary Fig. S2H). Together, these patterns indicate that MM stress drives more pronounced immune activation coupled with bioenergetic suppression than IMS stress, highlighting compartment-specific differences in mitochondrial stress–associated transcriptional reprogramming. This imbalance between immune activation and energy failure may contribute to neurodegenerative progression in AD.

Matrix- and IMS-targeted mitochondrial stress rewire mitochondrial gene networks in AD-like cells

To identify mitochondrial-related transcripts, we filtered the global RNA-seq dataset using the curated MitoCarta 3.0 gene set. Differentially expressed mitochondrial genes (FDR < 0.05, |log2FC| ≥ 1) were visualized by volcano plots (Fig. 3A–C; Supplementary Fig. S3A). AD-like cells exhibited significant alterations compared with WT, with upregulation of genes involved in mitochondrial ribosome assembly and OXPHOS (MRPS18A, SLC25A22, MRPS11, ENDOG, NDUFA3, TMEM11, ATP5ID, and MRPS34) and downregulation of genes linked to lipid transport and pyruvate metabolism (OSBPL1A, PDP1, PDK4, AK3, GLS, ABAT, MGARP, SLC25A40) (Fig. 3A). These changes suggest that AD-like cells enhance mitochondrial translation and respiratory complex assembly as an adaptive response, while reducing metabolic flexibility. Under AD-UPRmt-MM, transcripts related to iron-sulfur cluster transport and stress survival (SLC25A37, ACSM5, DHRS2, SQOR, BCL2A1, PDE2A, MCL1) were upregulated, whereas mitochondrial-encoded ETC components (MT-CYB, MT-CO3, MT-ATP6, MT-CO2, MT-ATP8, MT-ND1, MT-ND2, MT-ND3, and MT-ND4) were strongly downregulated (Fig. 3B), indicating severe OXPHOS impairment. This pattern suggests activation of survival pathways concurrent with disruption of energy production, consistent with matrix proteotoxic stress. In contrast, AD-UPRmt-IMS primarily suppressed genes involved in complex I assembly, ribosome stability, and antioxidant defense (NDUFB10, NDUFAF8, ENDOG, CHCHD10, MRPL41, NDUFA3, MRPL54, GPX1), without evidence of compensatory transcriptional upregulation (Fig. 3C), indicating increased susceptibility to oxidative stress. Direct comparison revealed limited transcriptional differences between AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS conditions, suggesting convergent suppression of mitochondrial function under severe stress (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Overall, these data show that both APP expression and compartment-specific stress remodel mitochondrial gene expression, but through distinct mechanisms. AD-like cells enhance mitochondrial translation machinery, whereas matrix-targeted mitochondrial stress (AD-UPRmt-MM) induces severe OXPHOS inhibition alongside survival signaling. In contrast, AD-UPRmt-IMS preferentially impairs ribosome integrity and antioxidant capacity. These findings highlight compartment-specific mitochondrial vulnerability that may contribute to AD-associated neurodegeneration.

We next assessed UPRmt-associated signaling pathways and mitochondrial stress responses, and heatmap analysis revealed distinct expression profiles of canonical UPRmt -associated genes (Fig. 3D). AD-like cells markedly induced UPRmt sensors (ATF5, ATF4, GDF15, HSF1) and MM proteostasis genes (TOMM40, LONP1, TRAP1, CLPP), indicating robust activation of MM-specific UPRmt-associated pathways (Fig. 3E; Supplementary Fig. S3B). These genes encode key components involved in protein import (TOMM40), proteolytic degradation (LONP1, CLPP), and chaperone-mediated folding (TRAP1), suggesting enhanced mitochondrial quality control under APP stress. However, both UPRmt-MM and UPRmt-IMS significantly reduced the expression of these genes, indicating suppression of APP-associated UPRmt -related transcriptional responses. IMS-related stress genes (HTRA2, CYCS, CYC1, COX17, COX19) showed moderate induction under AD-like conditions but were suppressed under both UPRmt-MM and UPRmt-IMS stress (Fig. 3F), indicating impaired IMS proteostasis. Together, these findings demonstrate that compartment-specific proteotoxic stress disrupts adaptive mitochondrial stress-response programs and UPRmt-associated signaling, thereby exacerbating mitochondrial dysfunction in AD-like microglia.

Compartment-specific mitochondrial stress disrupts mitochondrial bioenergetics and network architecture in AD-like cells

To evaluate mitochondrial function, we measured oxygen consumption rate (OCR) under different stress conditions (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Fig. S4A). Basal, ATP-linked, and maximal respiration were significantly reduced in AD-like cells compared with WT, with further reductions observed under AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS conditions. These results indicate that APP-driven stress compromises mitochondrial energy production, which is exacerbated by compartment-specific proteotoxic stress. RNA-seq analysis corroborated these findings, revealing downregulation of genes encoding respiratory chain complexes, including Complex I (MT-ND1–MT-ND6), Complex II (SDHA, SDHB, SDHD, SDHAF1/2), Complex III (MT-CYB, CYCS, UQCR subunits), and Complex IV (MT-CO1–MT-CO3, COX subunits) under AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS conditions (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) assessed by Tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM) staining was markedly decreased in AD-like cells compared with WT and further reduced under AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS conditions, indicating exacerbation of mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 4B, C). Loss of Δψm reflects impaired proton gradient maintenance required for ATP synthesis. We next analyzed mitochondrial network architecture using quantitative morphometric parameters (Fig. 4D–I). Mitochondrial morphology reflects cellular metabolic state and quality control, with changes in branching and connectivity indicating altered fusion–fission dynamics38–40. AD-like cells showed significantly reduced mitochondrial branching compared with WT, and AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS further decreased branch number and junctions, suggesting impaired connectivity and fragmentation (Fig. 4E, I). Total branch length decreased in AD-like cells but increased under UPRmt conditions, suggesting a compensatory shift toward fusion41 (Fig. 4F). There was no significant difference in average branch length per mitochondrion between WT and AD cells; however, it was significantly reduced under AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS conditions (Fig. 4G). A decrease in total branch length within the mitochondrial network reflects fragmentation, where mitochondria become shorter, more spherical, and less interconnected. Aspect ratio remained unchanged (Fig. 4H), whereas reduced branch junctions under AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS compared to AD-like cells (Fig. 4I) indicated a shift toward fission and decreased network connectivity. Collectively, these structural changes indicate that compartment-specific stress exacerbates AD-induced mitochondrial fragmentation.

Transcriptomic analysis further supported these observations (Fig. 4J, K; Supplementary Fig. S4C). Genes regulating mitochondrial biogenesis (TFAM, SIRT1, PRKAA1, MEF2D) were downregulated in AD-like cells, with further reductions under AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS conditions (Fig. 4J). Conversely, mitophagy and mitochondrial dynamics-related genes (BECN1, ULK1, PINK1, BNIP3, MFN2, FIS1) were upregulated, indicating activation of mitochondrial quality control pathways. However, this reciprocal pattern suggests dysregulated mitochondrial turnover rather than effective compensation (Fig. 4K; Supplementary Fig. S4C). To validate these findings at the protein level, we assessed representative mitochondrial respiratory complexes by Western blot. Representative subunits of Complex I (NDUFS1), Complex II (SDHA), Complex III (UQCRC1), Complex IV (CYTC), and Complex V (ATP5A1) were markedly reduced in both AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS conditions (Supplementary Fig. S4D, E). This reduction closely parallels transcriptomic changes, indicating concordance between mRNA and protein expression. These data demonstrate that compartment-specific mitochondrial stress is associated with a coordinated reduction in respiratory chain components, rather than selective remodeling. Overall, compartment-specific mitochondrial stress exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction in AD-like cells, resulting in impaired bioenergetics, mitochondrial network fragmentation, and dysregulated quality control. These combined defects highlight a critical vulnerability in mitochondrial homeostasis under AD-associated stress.

MM-specific mitochondrial stress intensifies mitochondrial ROS, cell death and amyloid-pathway dysregulation in AD-like cells

Compartment-specific mitochondrial stress in AD-like cells is associated with alterations in mitochondrial structure, membrane potential, and mitochondrial dynamics. We therefore next evaluated mitochondrial ROS production and cell death, two key processes implicated in AD pathology. Mitochondrial ROS, assessed by confocal microscopy using MitoBright Deep Red, was significantly increased in AD-like cells compared with WT. AD-UPRmt-MM further increased ROS levels, whereas AD-UPRmt-IMS showed no significant additional effect, indicating a stronger impact of MM-specific stress on mitochondrial redox balance (Fig. 5A, B). To assess whether these mitochondrial impairments increase vulnerability to cell death, we performed a SYTOX Green–based membrane rupture assay. AD-like cells exhibited increased cell death compared with WT, and AD-UPRmt-MM conditions further amplified this effect. In contrast, AD-UPRmt-IMS conditions did not significantly alter cell death relative to AD-like cells, indicating that MM-specific stress preferentially regulates cell death pathways in this context (Fig. 5C). Control experiments using wild-type OTC and IMS-Fluc constructs (ADMM-WT and ADIMS-WT) showed no significant effect on cell death, confirming the specificity of the OTC-Δ–mediated response (Supplementary Fig. S5A). RNA-seq analysis supported these findings. Genes involved in ROS detoxification, including mitochondrial antioxidant enzymes (PRDX3/5, GPX1/4, TXN1), were downregulated in AD-like cells, with further reductions under AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS conditions. Reduced PPARGC1A expression indicated impaired redox buffering (Fig. 5D). Cell-death pathway genes (CASP1, RIPK1, BCL2L1, CASP8, TRADD, MLKL, CASP9) were significantly upregulated under AD-UPRmt-MM, consistent with activation of multiple cell death programs (Fig. 5E). Similarly, inflammatory signaling genes (RELA, IL6, JAK2, NFKB1, IKBKB, IL1B, STAT3, MYD88, NLRP3, TNF) were markedly upregulated under AD-UPRmt-MM, indicating robust activation of NF-κB, JAK/STAT, and NLRP3 inflammasome pathways (Fig. 5F). To assess amyloidogenic processing, we measured extracellular Aβ1–42 levels using ELISA. Extracellular Aβ1–42 levels were increased in AD-like cells compared with WT and remained similarly elevated under both AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5B). RNA-seq data suggested increased expression of AD-related genes (APP, LRP1, PSEN2, APH1A, CLU, BACE2 and TREM2) in AD-like cells compared with WT. However, APP-processing genes displayed variable regulation under AD-UPRmt-MM and AD-UPRmt-IMS conditions, indicating context-dependent effects (Supplementary Fig. 5C). These findings suggest that mitochondrial stress does not uniformly regulate amyloid processing but instead differentially modulates specific pathway components. Despite consistent Aβ elevation, associated transcriptional changes were heterogeneous, which may contribute to variability in AD progression.

ATF5 knockdown rescues mitochondrial dysfunction, membrane rupture and Aβ levels in AD-like cells

Given that UPRmt -associated signaling and compartment-specific mitochondrial stress exacerbated mitochondrial dysfunction and cell‑death phenotypes in AD microglia, we next assessed whether targeted inhibition of this pathway could reverse these deficits. To selectively inhibit the UPRmt pathway, we targeted its master transcriptional regulator, ATF5, which is a well-established mediator of the mammalian mitochondrial unfolded protein response. ATF5 functions analogously to C. elegans ATFS‑1 and is required for transcriptional induction of canonical UPRmt genes, including HSP60, LONP1 and CLPP42,43. Wild‑type and AD-like microglial cells were transfected with human ATF5‑siRNA, and knockdown efficiency was confirmed by RT‑qPCR. ATF5 transcript levels were reduced by ~80–90%, demonstrating robust suppression of UPRmt signaling (Fig. 6A). Functional assays revealed that ATF5 knockdown significantly rescued key pathological features associated with AD-like stress. SYTOX-based membrane rupture assays showed a marked reduction in membrane rupture and cell death in ATF5 siRNA–transfected AD-like microglia, indicating improved cellular integrity (Fig. 6B). In parallel, extracellular Aβ1–42 levels, quantified by ELISA, were significantly decreased in ATF5‑silenced AD-like cells compared with controls, suggesting reduced extracellular Aβ accumulation (Fig. 6C). To determine whether inhibition of ATF5-dependent UPRmt signaling restored mitochondrial functions, we evaluated mitochondrial membrane potential and ROS production. TMRM fluorescence demonstrated a clear rescue of mitochondrial membrane potential in ATF5‑siRNA–transfected AD-like microglia relative to controls (Fig. 6D, E). In parallel, MitoSOX Red measurements showed a pronounced reduction in mitochondrial ROS levels following ATF5 knockdown (Fig. 6F, G). Collectively, these findings indicate that inhibition of ATF5-dependent UPRmt signaling mitigates mitochondrial dysfunction, reduces oxidative stress, and limits cell death and Aβ accumulation. Discussion

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a well-recognized hallmark of AD, driving impaired energy metabolism, oxidative stress, and neuronal degeneration44,45. The UPRmt has been proposed as a compensatory mechanism to maintain proteostasis through activation of chaperones, proteases, and stress-response regulators46. Consistent with this, elevated mitochondrial stress markers, including ATF4, ATF5, CHOP, LONP1, and CLPP, have been reported in patient samples as well as animal and cellular models, with some correlating with disease severity47, thereby supporting a conserved role for UPRmt activation in proteotoxic stress responses15. Our findings align with these observations, demonstrating that APPswe/F/L expression activates mitochondrial stress pathways and ribosome biogenesis while suppressing synaptic and lipid signaling, reflecting early AD-associated cellular remodeling37. Previous studies have also shown that Aβ15–18 and hyperphosphorylated tau19 activate UPRmt, with increased expression of UPRmt-related genes observed in AD models and patient brains15. Experimental models further indicate that modulation of UPRmt can influence Aβ pathology, underscoring its functional relevance in AD16,17. Together, these findings highlight a central role for UPRmt in AD while emphasizing that its functional outcome, whether protective or maladaptive, remains unresolved.

Here, we show that the cellular responses to matrix- and IMS-targeted mitochondrial stress are highly context-dependent and governed by mitochondrial compartment specificity. MM-targeted stress (UPRmt-MM) and IMS-targeted stress (UPRmt-IMS) produce distinct transcriptional and functional outcomes. MM-specific stress induces strong immune activation and profound suppression of OXPHOS, whereas IMS-specific stress primarily disrupts mitochondrial ribosome integrity and antioxidant defenses while engaging interferon signaling. These findings suggest that mitochondrial stress responses are compartment-specific rather than uniform, adding a new layer of complexity to UPRmt -associated signaling in AD. Consistent with this, transcriptomic and functional analyses show that compartment-specific stress reshapes mitochondrial and immune pathways in distinct ways. MM stress drives suppression of respiratory chain components and ATP synthesis alongside activation of inflammatory and interferon pathways, consistent with a metabolically compromised, pro-inflammatory state. In contrast, IMS stress induces translational attenuation and antiviral signaling with comparatively milder effects on core bioenergetics. These signatures are consistent with human AD datasets linking mitochondrial dysfunction and interferon-responsive microglia with disease progression48. Several stress-responsive genes identified in this study, including ATF4, ATF5, GDF15, and proteostasis regulators (LONP1, CLPP, YME1L1, HTRA2), highlight potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets49.

The pronounced downregulation of mitochondrial-encoded OXPHOS genes observed under MM stress indicates a marked impairment of respiratory chain function, likely leading to reduced ATP production and elevated mitochondrial ROS, consistent with early bioenergetic deficits reported in AD50,51. IMS stress, in contrast, preferentially activates interferon-responsive genes, reflecting engagement of innate immune pathways potentially triggered by mitochondrial stress signals34,35. Both stress conditions disrupt mitochondrial structure, consistent with AD-associated mitochondrial fragmentation, cristae disorganization, and reduced network connectivity52–54. Collectively, these findings indicate that mitochondrial dysfunction in AD reflects an integrated failure of bioenergetic, structural, and immune regulatory systems, with the mitochondrial matrix emerging as a particularly vulnerable compartment.

Importantly, our ATF5 loss-of-function experiments provide functional evidence that sustained ATF5-dependent UPRmt signaling contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction in AD-like microglia. ATF5 knockdown restored mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced mitochondrial ROS, and decreased cell death, indicating reversal of key pathological features. In addition, suppression of ATF5 reduced extracellular Aβ levels, establishing a functional link between mitochondrial stress signaling and amyloid processing. These findings demonstrate that persistent activation of the ATF5-dependent UPRmt signaling pathway can become maladaptive under chronic APP-associated stress conditions. In contrast to prior correlative studies, our data provide direct evidence that targeted inhibition of ATF5-dependent UPRmt signaling partially rescues mitochondrial and cellular dysfunction, supporting a causal role for dysregulated UPRmt-associated signaling in AD. Collectively, these results support a model in which amyloid pathology primes microglia for heightened stress sensitivity, and matrix- and IMS-targeted mitochondrial stress drive distinct maladaptive responses, with MM stress promoting bioenergetic collapse and inflammation, and IMS stress favoring antiviral and proteostasis-related signaling.

This study has several limitations. First, experiments were performed in immortalized microglial-like cells, which may not fully capture the complexity of in vivo microglial states or neuron–glia interactions. Second, the OTC-Δ and IMS-Fluc-DM-mut constructs used in this study are established tools for inducing mitochondrial proteotoxic stress and interrogating compartment-specific UPRmt signaling; however, they do not fully recapitulate endogenous UPRmt activation in vivo. Instead, they provide a controlled framework to investigate how AD-associated mitochondrial stress interacts with matrix- and IMS-specific proteostasis pathways. Additionally, while transcriptomic analyses provide critical insights, further validation at the protein and functional levels, particularly in patient-derived and in vivo systems, will be essential. Future studies should address these limitations by incorporating in vivo models, patient-derived cells, and longitudinal analyses to define therapeutic windows for modulating compartment-specific mitochondrial stress responses and UPRmt-associated signaling. While neuronal mitochondrial dysfunction is well established in AD, our focus on microglia reflects their central role in neuroinflammation, immune regulation, and clearance of pathological aggregates, processes tightly linked to mitochondrial function. Although direct in vivo evidence for microglial UPRmt remains limited, emerging data from human iPSC-based and organoid models support its relevance55. These findings provide a mechanistic framework linking microglial mitochondrial stress responses to AD progression.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that mitochondrial stress responses in AD are compartment-specific rather than uniform, with distinct stress responses emerging from the MM and IMS. APPswe/F/L expression activates mitochondrial stress pathways, and targeted proteotoxic stress amplifies these responses in a compartment-dependent manner, with MM stress driving immune activation and bioenergetic collapse, and IMS stress impairing translational and antioxidant defenses. Importantly, suppression of ATF5-dependent UPRmt signaling via ATF5 knockdown partially restores mitochondrial function and reduces key pathological features, supporting a causal role for dysregulated UPRmt-associated signaling. Together, these findings identify compartment-specific mitochondrial stress pathways as important regulators of microglial dysfunction and highlight mitochondrial proteostasis and stress-response pathways as promising therapeutic targets in AD.

Methods

Cell culture

Human microglial-like cells (HMC3, wild-type) and HMC3 cells stably expressing amyloid precursor protein with Swedish, Florida, and London mutations (HMC3-APPswe/F/L; AD-like model)31 were used to investigate compartment-specific mitochondrial stress responses and UPRmt signaling. HMC3 cells represent wild-type microglial-like cells, while HMC3-APPswe/F/L cells model amyloidogenic stress associated with familial AD. Cells were cultured in Minimal Essential Medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA), and 1 mM sodium pyruvate. APPswe/F/L cultures were maintained in medium containing puromycin (2 μg/mL) for selection; puromycin was removed before all functional assays to avoid interference with cellular responses. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Cells with passage numbers below 20 were used to ensure reproducibility.

Plasmids and transfection

To establish compartment-specific mitochondrial proteotoxic stress, mammalian expression constructs were used: OTC (wild-type control, Addgene #71877) and OTC-Δ (deletion mutant, Addgene #71878) for matrix UPRmt (UPRmt-MM), and IMS-Fluc-Flag (wild-type control, Vector Builder #VB221003-1243ust) and IMS-Fluc-DM-mut-Flag (double-mutant misfolding construct, Vector Builder #VB221003-1245bty) for IMS UPRmt (UPRmt-IMS). The OTC‑Δ construct encodes a deletion mutant of ornithine transcarbamylase that misfolds within the MM and has been previously validated as a tool to induce matrix-associated mitochondrial proteotoxic stress and UPRmt‑MM signaling32. The IMS‑Fluc‑DM‑mut construct encodes a misfolding‑prone double‑mutant firefly luciferase targeted to the IMS33 via an HTRA2-derived targeting sequence and has been validated as a reporter of IMS proteostasis and mitochondrial stress.

Transfections were performed using FuGENE 4 K (Promega #E5911) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 24 h, transfection medium was replaced with fresh growth medium to minimize cytotoxicity. Cells were either seeded for downstream assays or maintained in their original vessels. All assays were conducted 48 h post-transfection to ensure optimal transgene expression. Four experimental conditions were analyzed: HMC3-WT (WT), HMC3-APPswe/F/L (AD), HMC3-APPswe/F/L-OTC-Δ (AD-UPRmt‑MM), and HMC3-APPswe/F/L-IMS-Fluc-DM-mut (AD-UPRmt‑IMS).

To knockdown ATF5 levels, human ATF5 siRNA (Santa Cruz #sc-43580) was used and transfected using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen #13778-150) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Four experimental conditions were analyzed: WT-control-siRNA, WT-ATF5-siRNA, AD-control-siRNA and AD-ATF5-siRNA.

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy

Cells were seeded at a density of 1.0 × 106 cells per 35-mm glass-bottom dish (MatTek, #PG35GC-1.5-14-C) 24 h prior to transfection. Cells were transfected with OTC‑Δ or IMS-Fluc-DM-mut constructs as described above. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were fixed and immunostained with ATP5A1 (Proteintech, #66037-1-Ig; 1:300), OTC (Antibodies.com, #A39145; 1:150), and FLAG (Proteintech, #20543-1-AP; 1:800) antibodies. Secondary antibodies included goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A32723TR; 1:1000) for detection of ATP5A1 and goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A11037; 1:1000) for detection of OTC and FLAG. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #62248). Confocal imaging was performed on an Olympus FV4000 using a 60× objective. Image sharpening was applied uniformly across all groups using the Wiemer color deconvolution method. For quantitative analysis, individual mitochondrial filaments were traced by drawing a longitudinal line along each structure, and fluorescence line-intensity profiles were extracted and plotted. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated in ImageJ (NIH) and plotted.

RNA isolation and qRT‑PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen #74104) and reverse transcribed using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems). qRT-PCR was performed with PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) with RPS13 used as the internal reference gene. Relative expression was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method, normalized to HMC3‑WT controls. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

RNA-sequencing

RNA-seq was performed on four biological replicates per condition using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA protocol. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads, achieving an average depth of ~48 million reads per sample. RNA integrity was confirmed (RIN ≥ 8).

Quality control and preprocessing

Raw sequencing reads were assessed for quality using FastQC (v0.11.9), evaluating parameters such as base quality scores, GC content, and sequence duplication levels. Adapter sequences and low-quality reads were removed using Trimmomatic (v0.39), applying criteria to discard reads containing >10% undetermined bases (N) or those in which >50% of bases had a Phred quality score ≤5. After trimming, clean reads were retained for downstream analysis, and sequencing quality metrics were calculated, including Q20 and Q30 scores (>95% of bases exceeding a Phred score of 30), GC content (45–55%), and overall error rates. Alignment quality was confirmed with mapping rates exceeding 95%, ensuring high-quality data for downstream analysis.

Alignment and quantification

Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR (v2.7.10a) in two-pass mode to improve splice junction detection and mapping accuracy. HISAT2 (v2.2.1) was also used for splice-aware alignment to validate splice junctions. Gene-level counts were obtained using featureCounts (v2.0.1). Expression values for visualization were normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM), while raw counts were used for differential expression analysis, as recommended for DESeq2-based workflows.

Differential expression analysis

Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 (v1.38) in R (v4.3), applying the Benjamini–Hochberg method for false discovery rate (FDR) correction, with significance thresholds set at FDR < 0.05 and absolute log₂ fold change ≥1. Hierarchical clustering and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed on log₂ (FPKM + 1) values using Morpheus (Broad Institute).

Functional enrichment

Functional enrichment analysis was performed to identify biological processes, pathways, and disease associations represented among the DEGs. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was used to classify genes into biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components, while KEGG pathway analysis enabled pathway-level annotation. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was applied to evaluate enrichment of predefined gene sets. Additional pathway interpretation was performed using Reactome. Disease-related enrichment was assessed using the Human Disease Ontology and DisGeNET platforms, which provide structured annotations linking genes to human diseases.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in 1× RIPA buffer supplemented with SIGMAFAST™ protease inhibitor cocktail, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C for 30 min. Protein concentrations were measured using the Bio‑Rad assay at 660 nm. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS‑PAGE and transferred onto PVDF Immobilon‑FL membranes (EMD Millipore, #IPFL00010). Membranes were blocked in blocking buffer (Rockland, #MB‑070) for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following primary antibodies were used: ATF4 (Cell Signaling and Technology, #11815S; 1:1000), CHOP (Proteintech, 15204-1-AP; 1:1000), ATF5 (Proteintech, #67066-1-Ig; 1:1000), HSF1 (Cell Signaling and Technology, #51034-1-AP; 1:1000), DELE1 (Cell Signaling and Technology, #98271; 1:1000), p-eIF2α (Cell Signaling and Technology, #3398S; 1:1000), eIF2α (Invitrogen, #AHO-0802; 1:1000), p-HSF1 (Invitrogen, #PA5-101018; 1:1000), NDUFS1 (Proteintech, #12444-1-AP; 1:1000), SDHA (Abcam, #ab147151; 1:1000), UQCRC1 (Proteintech, #21705-1-AP; 1:1000), cytochrome c (CYCS) (Santa Cruz, #sc-13156 AF680; 1:1000), ACTIN (Santa Cruz, #sc-8432 AF680; 1:1000), GAPDH (Abcam, #AB18409S; 1:1000), TOM20 (Abcam, #ab56783; 1:1000), FLAG (Proteintech, #F1804; 1:1000), and ATP5A1 (Proteintech, #66037‑1‑Ig; 1:1000). After primary incubation, membranes were washed three times for 10 min each with TBS‑T (0.1% Tween‑20) and incubated with IR680 or IR800 secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Blots were imaged using a Bio‑Rad ChemiDoc system. Band intensities were quantified in ImageJ (NIH), background-subtracted, and normalized to GAPDH and ACTIN. Full-length blots are provided in Supplementary Fig. S6.

Mitochondria isolation and sub fractionation

HMC3-WT cells were grown in 150-mm dishes and transfected with OTC-Δ and IMS-Fluc-Flag constructs, followed by collection of cell pellets for mitochondrial isolation. The cell pellet was resuspended in isotonic mitochondrial isolation buffer (MIB: 200 mM mannitol, 70 mM sucrose, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM EGTA, protease inhibitors; pH 7.5) at fivefold the pellet volume and incubated on ice for 10 min. Cells were homogenized using a Dounce homogenizer (10 loose strokes followed by 15 tight strokes), and a 100 µL aliquot was reserved as whole‑cell lysate. Samples were first centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove debris; the resulting supernatant was then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to obtain crude mitochondria, while the supernatant was retained as the cytosolic fraction. The mitochondrial pellet was washed twice in isolation buffer and then subjected to digitonin‑dependent permeabilization by incubating aliquots (M1–M5) for 10 min at room temperature in defined mixtures of MIB, proteinase K, digitonin, or Triton X‑100. Reactions were terminated by adding 2 µL of 100 µM PMSF, followed by incubation on ice for 5 min before centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 8 min at 4 °C. Pellets were resuspended in MIB containing protease inhibitors and 6× SDS loading dye, heated at 95 °C for 10 min, and split into three equal aliquots for SDS–PAGE. Whole‑cell lysate, cytosolic, and mitochondrial fractions (M1–M5) were resolved by SDS–PAGE at 100 V for 110 min and transferred at 100 V for 60 min. Membranes were probed for ATP5A1, GAPDH, FLAG, OTC, and TOM20 to assess mitochondrial integrity and subcompartmental protein accessibility.

Quantification of extracellular Aβ peptides by ELISA

To determine whether compartment-specific UPRmt influences the Aβ pathway, extracellular Aβ peptides (Aβ1–42) were quantified from culture supernatants of all experimental groups. Supernatants were collected at the indicated time points and analyzed using Aβ-specific ELISA kits (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, #298-62401 for Aβ1–42) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 μL of standards and samples were added to pre-coated 96-well plates and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing to remove unbound material, plates were incubated with HRP-conjugated detection antibody for 1 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, TMB substrate solution was added and incubated for 30 min at room temperature to allow color development. The reaction was terminated by adding stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Concentrations of Aβ peptides were calculated from standard curves generated for each assay.

SYTOX green–based membrane integrity test

Plasma membrane integrity was assessed using SYTOX Green nucleic acid stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #S7020), which selectively penetrates cells with compromised membranes and binds to DNA, resulting in increased fluorescence in dead or damaged cells. Equal numbers of cells (2 × 104 per well) from each group were plated in a 96‑well format and subsequently incubated with SYTOX Green (1 µM) at 37 °C for 1 h. Fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader at excitation/emission maxima of 504/530 nm. To normalize for cell number and DNA content, CyQUANT reagent (Promega, #C35011) was added directly to the wells containing complete medium and incubated in the dark for 30 min. Fluorescence for CyQUANT was recorded at excitation/emission maxima of 508/527 nm. Data were expressed as SYTOX Green fluorescence normalized to DNA content.

Seahorse XF-based oxygen consumption rate measurement

Mitochondrial respiration was measured using an XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience) at 37 °C. Cells from all groups were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁴ cells per well in XF96 plates and cultured for 24 hours before the assay. Growth medium was replaced with Seahorse XF assay medium (pH 7.4) containing 25 mM glucose, 25 mM GlutaMAX, and 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and cells were equilibrated for 1 h in a non-CO₂ incubator. OCR was recorded under basal conditions and following sequential injections of oligomycin (2 μM; ATP synthase inhibitor), FCCP (2 μM; mitochondrial uncoupler), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM each; Complex I and III inhibitors). These drugs were used to dissect mitochondrial function: oligomycin for ATP-linked respiration, FCCP for maximal respiratory capacity, and rotenone/antimycin A for non-mitochondrial respiration. From these measurements, the following parameters were calculated: basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration and maximal respiratory capacity corrected by non-mitochondrial respiration. OCR values were normalized using CyQUANT reagent (Promega) by incubating in the dark for 30 min, and fluorescence was measured at 508/527 nm using a plate reader. Data were expressed as OCR normalized to DNA content (CyQUANT signal) using Seahorse Wave software.

Assessment of mitochondrial ROS production

Mitochondrial superoxide production was measured using MitoBright Deep Red (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, #MT16-12), a fluorogenic dye that selectively targets mitochondria and exhibits red fluorescence upon oxidation by superoxide. Equal numbers of cells (2 × 105 per well) from all experimental groups were seeded in 35 mm dishes. Following incubation with MitoBright Deep Red, live cell imaging was conducted at 60× magnification using an FV4000 confocal microscope. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #62249). MitoBright fluorescence intensity per cell was quantified using ImageJ software. A total of 50 images per group were acquired from n = 3 independent experiments, and fluorescence intensity was normalized to the number of Hoechst-stained nuclei and expressed as fold change relative to HMC3-WT controls. Data points represent fluorescence intensity normalized to Hoechst for each image across three independent experiments.

Mitochondrial membrane potential imaging assay

Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using TMRM (100 nM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #T668), a potentiometric dye that accumulates in active mitochondria. Cells from all experimental groups were seeded at 1 × 10⁵ per 35 mm optical-bottom dish and cultured under identical conditions. After incubation for 30 min at 37 °C, live-cell imaging was performed using an FV4000 confocal microscope at 60× magnification. Fluorescence intensity was quantified as mean TMRM signal per cell using ImageJ software, applying consistent thresholding and background subtraction across all samples. Approximately 300 cells from n = 3 independent experiments (50 images/group) were analyzed, and fluorescence intensity was normalized to Hoechst-stained nuclei and expressed as fold change relative to HMC3-WT controls. Data points represent fluorescence intensity normalized to Hoechst for each image across independent experiments.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM from at least n = 3 independent biological experiments. Statistical tests included unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test for two-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA for multi-group comparisons, and two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for multi-factor analyses. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, corresponding to a 95% confidence interval. The specific statistical methods used for each dataset are detailed within the corresponding figure legends. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.0.
