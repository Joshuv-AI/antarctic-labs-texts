B-site modulation tailors the size distribution of ex-solved nanoparticles for optimized active sites in perovskites
Authors: Jun Kyu Kim, Su Keun Kuk, Sungwoo Kang, Jong-Min Lee, Hyuk Jae Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supported nanoparticles fabricated by an ex-solution process from perovskite scaffolds serve promising capabilities for various thermal and electrochemical reactions. Although the size distribution of the nanoparticles plays a crucial role in catalysis, it remains difficult to control in ex-solution due to the lack of understanding of their underlying energetic factors. Here, we investigate the impact of B-site doping in two different types of perovskite hosts (A2+B4+O3 and A3+B3+O3) on the size distribution of ex-solved nanoparticles. By incorporating non-reducible ions at the B-site, we observe the variations in nanoparticles’ sizes and densities using model cleaved surfaces of ceramic pellets. Density functional theory calculations help correlate the observed experimental results with the computed energetic descriptors, providing deeper insights into the particle ex-solution and enabling the description of particle density via a first-order nucleation model. Furthermore, we utilize the tailored ex-solution materials as catalysts for N2O decomposition, and it exhibits a volcano-plot relationship between the catalytic activity and size distribution of ex-solution particles. It suggests that our findings can provide an alternative way to optimizing catalytic active sites for various environmental and energy applications.

Introduction

Metal nanoparticles (NPs) distributed onto the surface of functional oxides (i.e., supported catalysts) have garnered significant interest as key materials for a wide range of chemical and energy conversion applications1–6. Conventionally, such systems can be obtained by the external dispersion of metal salts to ceramic backbones (e.g., oxides, carbides, nitrides, etc.), followed by subsequent calcination and reduction processes. However, the supported catalysts yielded by the traditional deposition approach present weak interactions between NPs and the support frameworks. Thus, it usually faces the limited resistance to particle aggregation and carbon coking during material preparation and/or (electro)chemical reactions, causing catalytic performance degradation7,8.

To overcome these issues, an innovative process, ex-solution phenomenon has been proposed as an alternative method for fabricating the supported catalyst over the past few decades9–15. The ex-solution reaction can be described as follows: when the perovskite oxide system (ABO3) is exposed to high temperatures under a reductive atmosphere (e.g., low oxygen partial pressure), catalytically active metal NPs are formed at the surface of the host oxide through the selective partial reduction. This reaction is highly rapid, cost-effective, and capable of achieving excellent particle dispersion rather than general deposition methods. Furthermore, the NPs produced through the ex-solution process exhibit superior thermal and chemical stability due to their partial embedding geometry in the surface of the host skeleton16–20.

In general, it is widely considered that a smaller particle size and a higher NP dispersion on the oxide framework provide higher catalytic performance and more effective mass utilization. In this regard, extensive efforts have been made to date to tune the final characteristics of ex-solution particles, such as their size and number density21–29. For this purpose, scientific approaches have primarily focused on controlling the compositions of perovskite materials and/or modifying the conditions or methods of the ex-solution reaction. As representative examples of adjusting the compositions of oxides, Gao et al. investigated the number density of ex-solution particles in relation to La doping in the Ni-doped SrTiO3 system and revealed that La substitution at the Sr site decreases the surface density of ex-solved Ni particles30. Lee et al. altered the Goldschmidt tolerance factor of perovskite oxide by changing the A-site component in Ni-doped stannate materials (i.e., Ni-doped ASnO3, A=Ca, Sr, Ba) and found that a larger cation, which results in a higher Goldschmidt tolerance factor, produces large-sized ex-solved Ni particles with a low surface population compared to a smaller cation31. As other examples of controlling the ex-solution conditions or methods, Kim et al. observed that low oxygen partial pressure (pO2) leads to a large number of ex-solution particles with a smaller size under limited amounts of the ex-solution ingredient source in Sr0.98Ti0.95Co0.05O3-δ thin-film framework21. Moreover, unlike traditional reductive annealing, which governs external conditions, novel approaches for ex-solution have been recently reported. Myung et al. triggered Ni ex-solution through applied potential on a La0.43Ca0.37Ni0.06Ti0.94O3-γ electrode material, leading to a larger number of ex-solution particles compared to conventional gas-driven ex-solution14. Using the same perovskite material, Kyriakou and co-workers suggested a plasma-driven ex-solution process utilizing N2 RF plasma. The authors reported that the particles formed by plasma treatment exhibit a higher particle count than those from conventional H2 annealing at 650 °C32. In addition, thermal shock treatment and photothermal treatment approaches can also provide ultrafast ex-solution with a very high density of ex-solution particles33,34. More detailed information is well summarized in several articles, including review papers9,11,35.

Regardless of the types of approaches proposed in the aforementioned papers for tuning the size and density of ex-solved NPs, it is generally accepted, in principle, that the size and number density of ex-solution particles are determined by the combination of energetic driving force and kinetic nucleation/growth barrier during the partial reduction process of host oxide reservoirs. To utilize these backgrounds in more qualitative and quantitative descriptors, many researchers have defined and computed the segregation energy and reduction energy via density functional theory (DFT) calculations, because the ex-solution process involves the dopant surface segregation and reduction steps27–30,36–39. Typically, the segregation energy is defined as the energy difference between the ex-solution ingredient at the surface and the bulk sites of the host oxide, and the reduction energy is calculated as the energy change upon oxygen removal. It is noted that various experimental results are well described by these descriptors. Therefore, the descriptors offer valuable insights into understanding the ex-solution mechanism, furthermore, open new opportunities to describe the size and surface density of ex-solved NPs through high-throughput screening with a wide range of material compositions and annealing conditions.

Despite numerous studies, attempts to directly correlate experimental findings with computational results remain limited. To the best of our knowledge, the effects of A-site substitution in perovskite oxides on the ex-solution process have been well explored; however, those of B-site doping remain relatively limited, despite the BO6 octahedra serving as the skeleton of the perovskite structure. Moreover, while alkaline earth metals or lanthanide elements, which are mainly employed as potential A-site dopants, are thermodynamically non-reducible to the metal phase under common ex-solution conditions (e.g., 700–900 °C and pO2 ~ 10−20 atm), the reported candidates for B-site modulation in previous literature were typically selected as reducible elements such as Ni, Co, and Fe that can offer an additional degree of freedom for particle characteristics (i.e., NP composition). Thus, such reducible elements complicate the interpretation of B-site doping effect on the size and density of ex-solution particles40,41. In addition, since most papers restrict their scope of investigation to a single perovskite host material, the applicability of the computational descriptors (e.g., segregation and reduction energies) for determining the size and surface density of ex-solved NPs across various types of host perovskites has not been systematically explored.

In this study, to address the aforementioned issues, we investigated the influence of B-site substitution with non-reducible elements under typical ex-solution conditions on the size distribution of ex-solved Co particles, using two different perovskite parents: Co-doped SrTiO3 and Co-doped LaAlO3, which are representatives of A2+B4+O3 and A3+B3+O3 perovskites, respectively. We first prepared the model dense surfaces by cleaving a sintered pellet and subsequently estimated the size and density of ex-solved particles after reductive annealing (e.g., 900 °C under 5 % H2/Ar gases) as shown in Fig. 1a. Then, we calculated the segregation and reduction energy using DFT calculations and connected the results with experimental findings by employing a first-order nucleation model. Our observations and analyses figured out that (1) the type of host oxides predominantly determines the segregation energies of ex-solution ingredient (e.g., Co), providing an underlying explanation of differences in ex-solution particle distribution between the SrTiO3 and LaAlO3 hosts, (2) a B-site dopant which stabilizes perovskite materials under reducing atmosphere (i.e., leads a high value of reduction energy) produces larger particles with a lower population (Fig. 1b). Finally, to verify the potential impact of our suggested approach for tailoring the size distribution of ex-solution particles on chemical reactions, we tested the ex-solution materials as catalysts for N2O decomposition and found a volcano-plot relationship between catalytic activity and size distribution of ex-solution particles. Results

Fabrication and Characterization of B-Site Modulated Perovskite Oxides

To explore the influence of B-site substitution by the various hard-to-reduce elements under a common ex-solution condition on the two different types of host oxides (e.g., A2+B4+O3 and A3+B3+O3) on the size distribution of ex-solution particles, we selected 10 at% Co-doped SrTiO3 and LaAlO3 (referred to a SrTi0.9Co0.1O3-δ (STC) and a LaAl0.9Co0.1O3-δ (LAC)) as benchmark materials, respectively. By employing these starting materials, we have conceived the perovskite derivatives in which 10 at% of B-site host ions (i.e., Ti4+ in STC and Al3+ in LAC) are replaced after considering the several factors of the Hume-Rothery rules (e.g., size and valence of cations) and thermodynamics. Specifically, we designed four variants from a STC case: SrTi0.8Co0.1Zr0.1O3-δ (STCZ), SrTi0.8Co0.1Hf0.1O3-δ (STCH), SrTi0.8Co0.1Nb0.1O3-δ (STCN), and SrTi0.8Co0.1Ta0.1O3-δ (STCT); and three variants from a LAC case: LaAl0.8Co0.1Ga0.1O3-δ (LACG), LaAl0.8Co0.1Sc0.1O3-δ (LACS), and LaAl0.8Co0.1Ti0.1O3-δ (LACT) (9 compositions in total). As shown in Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1, the dopants used in this study exhibit similar charges and ionic sizes with the host ions, and show positive values of the Gibbs free energy of the reduction reactions (ΔGred) at 900 °C (the target reduction temperature of this study).

The crystal structures of the as-synthesized perovskite oxides are identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD) as shown in Fig. 2a. As shown in this figure, we confirmed that all the materials show major perovskite diffraction patterns with the space group of Pm-3m without significant second phases. Apart from the selected oxides, we additionally tried to yield other compositions, Zr-doped LAC and In-doped LAC, however, the samples contain a large amount of different crystals such as La2Zr2O7 and LaInO3 (Supplementary Fig. 2). The chemical properties of B-site doped materials were also investigated using X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) measurements (Supplementary Figs. 3, 4 and Fig. 2b, c). Figure 2b shows Co 2p XPS spectra after B-site substitution into each host framework. It is clearly shown that doping of the Zr4+ and Hf4+ (i.e., STCZ and STCH, respectively) which possess the same valence state with the host Ti4+ display the similar Co 2p3/2 binding energy (BE) value around 780.8 eV. However, the BE of Co 2p3/2 after the Nb5+ and Ta5+ (i.e., STCN and STCT, respectively) aliovalent substitution shifted to the lower binding energy value (BE ~ 780.3 eV), indicating the enrichment of electrons of Co. Similar trends were also observed in the case of the LaAlO3 host material. The substitution of isovalent elements such as Ga3+ and Sc3+ with the host Al3+ cation shows almost equivalent Co 2p3/2 BE value of 780.4 eV, but donor element, Ti4+, decreases the binding energy of Co species (i.e., 779.5 eV). The chemical states of the oxygen species were also checked as shown in Fig. 2c. All the O 1s XPS spectra of the materials could be deconvoluted by OI and OII which indicate the lattice oxygen and surface oxygen, respectively. It was found that the aliovalent donor substitution in both SrTiO3 and LaAlO3 systems decreases the ratios of surface-to-lattice oxygen (i.e., Area of OII/OI). Moreover, the capabilities of reduction of the B-site modulated oxides were also explored via hydrogen temperature-programmed-reduction (H2 TPR) analysis as shown in Supplementary Fig. 5. Quantitative Analysis of Ex-Solution Particles from B-Site Modulated Perovskite Oxides

Now, we produced the sintered pellets utilizing the as-synthesized B-site doped materials to quantitatively analyse the ex-solution characteristics (e.g., size and number density). Typically, a native surface generated by a high temperature annealing process offers a terracing structure rather than smooth surface, thus, we created the cleaved surface by fracturing the sintered pellets and choose it as a platform to obtain a reliable size distribution of ex-solution particles (Fig. 1a). Then, as-prepared pellet fragments were annealed under 5 % H2/Ar atmosphere at 900 °C for 10 h, and consequently, ex-solved NPs were yielded as shown in Fig. 2d. As a comparison, ex-solved particles were also observed in the powder samples (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). Furthermore, through scanning transmission electron microscopy combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (STEM-EDS) elemental mapping, we confirmed that the component of the ex-solved NP is Co (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9). After the reductive heat treatment, the crystal structures of the materials were examined, and we confirmed that the oxides preserved the main perovskite lattice (Supplementary Fig. 10). Subsequent to the yielding of the ex-solution particles, we first determined the particle density by counting the particles located on a specific oxide surface area (i.e., the number of NPs per oxide area). Then, we marked the particles and estimated the marked area to obtain the radius (or diameter) by assuming the particles were spheres. The representative of this approach is described in Supplementary Fig. 11. To secure the precision, approximately 50 – 100 NPs were statistically analysed. The histograms of particle size were presented in Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13. The average diameter and number density of Co ex-solution particles from all the perovskite derivatives were summarized in Fig. 2e. As shown in this figure, it can be found that the size and density exhibit an inversely proportional relationship. Moreover, the ex-solution particles show the size in a range of 7.9 – 45.6 nm (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7) and the number density in a range of 60 – 840 #/μmoxide2. The gray line in Fig. 2e indicates the volume of ex-solution particles with the fitted value of 2.62 ×105 nm3/μmoxide2, and it is shown that the amount of ex-solution is almost similar regardless of the types of host oxides and/or B-site dopants. One notable point is that doping with aliovalent cations (e.g., STCN, STCT, and LACT) results in sizes and densities that deviate significantly from those of the starting materials, STC and LAC, respectively. For example, STCN and STCT exhibit the smallest particle size and the highest surface density, while LACT shows the largest particle size and the lowest particle density. In addition to this, we also try to find a connecting point between the reducibility of B-site modulated materials (which is obtained by H2 TPR measurements) and ex-solution particles (Supplementary Fig. 14). It is revealed that a B-site dopant which is able to stabilize the perovskite materials under a reductive condition leads to larger-sized ex-solution particles in both STC and LAC systems (i.e., a positive relationship between H2 TPR peak position and ex-solution particle size).

To gain further chemical insight into the ex-solution trends, we analyzed how the two key descriptors, segregation and reduction energies, depend on the material compositions. Figure 3d presents the segregation energies for different host and dopant types. We found that the segregation energies of STC series are generally lower than those of LAC series, regardless of dopant types. Additionally, Fig. 3e, f show the particle density and diameter as functions of the reduction energy for STC and LAC, respectively. These figures prove that the reduction energy exhibits a linear relationship with both density and diameter. Specifically, a higher reduction energy value leads to larger and fewer ex-solution particles. To further quantify these trends, we evaluated the relative contributions of segregation and reduction energies to the density values using Eq. 5, confirming that the density values of STC series are mainly governed by segregation energy, whereas those of LAC series are mainly governed by the reduction energy (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 17). By integrating these outcomes, it can be deduced that the host material composition has a greater influence on segregation, while B-site modulations primarily affect reduction in the ex-solution process. This can be rationalized by considering that the segregation energy of the ex-solved element (i.e., Co in this study) is determined by the local chemical bonding environment of the host material, as the segregation process of ex-solution elements involves breaking numerous bonds between host oxide ions and the ex-solvable ions. In contrast, the reduction process is governed by the excess electron density of the system, which generally depends on the compositions of the B-site of a perovskite system.

Interestingly, we find that the density trends of ex-solution particles upon n-doping (e.g., aliovalent doping) exhibit opposite behaviors for STC and LAC systems. The particle densities of STCN and STCT are higher than that of STC, whereas the density of LACT is lower than that of LAC. Correspondingly, the reduction energies of STCN and STCT are lower than that of STC, while for LACT, the reduction energy is higher than that of LAC (Fig. 3e and f). According to conventional understanding, if the excess electron from n-doping localizes on surface oxygen, the reduction energy should decrease, as observed in STC. However, the unique behavior of LAC suggests that the excess electron occupies states other than surface states. To investigate this further, we plotted the density of states (DOS) for pristine and reduced STCN and LACT in Supplementary Fig. 18. The results show that, in LACT, the excess electron occupies bulk Co sites in addition to surface sites, whereas in STC, the electron primarily localizes at surface sites. Bader charge analysis (Supplementary Table 3) further supports this trend, revealing a significant increase in bulk Co electron occupancy in LAC upon Ti doping. This explains the opposite trend of reduction behavior in LAC upon n-doping.

Overall, these findings indicate that the number density and size of ex-solution NPs can be effectively tailored by (1) the types of host materials and (2) the compositions of B-site elements. Moreover, it is clear that, at the atomic level, these factors influence segregation and reduction energetics, which can be accurately described by theoretical calculations.

Demonstration as Catalytic Materials for N2O Decomposition for Greenhouse Gas Mitigation

Next, to explore the applicability of the methodology on tailoring the size and density of ex-solution particles in the fields of heterogeneous catalysis, we selected the direct nitrous oxide (N2O) decomposition as a model reaction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{2}O\to {N}_{2}+1/2{O}_{2}$$\end{document}N2O→N2+1/2O2). N2O gas is the third largest emitter of greenhouse gases, following CO2 and CH4, according to international regulations, and it causes approximately 310 times the greenhouse gas effect compared to CO2 gas (i.e., CO2-eq of N2O gas is 310)44–47. Despite its negative impact on global warming, the demand and use of N2O have increased, as it is often used as a processing gas in semiconductor and display manufacturing processes, including those of Samsung companies. In addition, research related to N2O decomposition is being conducted in academic circles as well. Hinokuma et al. reported that there is no NOx (NO and NO2) selectivity during N2O decomposition reaction using Ru/SnO2 catalyst, indicating that only N2 and O2 are produced48. Moreover, according to previous papers44, various types of metal oxides were employed as catalysts, such as simple binary oxides, spinel, and perovskite structures that contain Co, Fe, and Cu elements. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, it is very limited to apply the ex-solution technology for N2O decomposition reaction. Considering these factors, we try to demonstrate the feasibility of tailored ex-solution catalysts for N2O decomposition reaction.

The light-off curves of N2O conversion (decomposition) for various B-site modulated STC and LAC series after annealing at 900 °C and with the 5 % H2/Ar gases for 10 h are shown in Fig. 4a. For clarity, STC and LAC series are presented as square symbols with solid lines and circle symbols with dashed lines, respectively. As shown in this figure, it is found that the LAC series shows high N2O conversion activities compared to those of the STC series, indicating that LaAlO3 host materials are more active compared to SrTiO3 framework for N2O decomposition. We also checked the kinetic data of prepared samples (Fig. 4b). It is confirmed that the same oxide hosts exhibit similar activation energy values (i.e., STC series; 78 kJ/mol and LAC series; 108 kJ/mol), which suggests that the N2O decomposition mechanisms in each host framework are comparable, while the differences arise due to variations in the frequency factor (k0) in Arrhenius equation of reaction rate constant (k), which is proportional to the number of active sites. In addition, to provide a more comprehensive comparison of catalytic activity, we surveyed previously reported Co-containing perovskite catalysts for N2O decomposition (Supplementary Table 4). As shown in the Supplementary Table 4, although the reaction conditions vary slightly among studies, the T50 values are generally observed in the range of 450–700 °C in most cases. Therefore, the catalytic activity measured in this study is considered to be comparable to that of typical Co-containing perovskite catalysts for N₂O decomposition. Notably, while reported catalysts typically contain approximately 10–20 wt% Co, the Co content in our catalyst is only 2.71 wt%, which is roughly an order of magnitude lower. We also conducted durability tests on the two catalysts, LAC 900 R and LACG 900 R (i.e., LAC and LACG after 900 °C reduction with 5% H2/Ar gases, respectively), which exhibited high N2O decomposition efficiency. The LAC 900 R and LACG 900 R catalysts stably operated with N2O conversion values of 96.3 % and 88.8 %, respectively, for 100 h at 800 °C (Supplementary Fig. 19). The surface morphology of the samples before and after the long-term stability test was also examined using SEM. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 20, both catalysts were not degraded under long-term operation, demonstrating a robust and sustainable performance of ex-solution catalysts.

Many studies using Co species reported that Co acts as an active site in direct N2O decomposition, where redox reactions play a crucial role44–46. Taking this into account, we confirmed that ex-solved Co nanoparticles in LAC and STC series after the ex-solution process are the active sites of N2O catalytic conversion through control experiments. The LaAlO3 (LAO) and SrTiO3 (STO) exhibited significantly lower N2O conversion compared to that of the LAC 900 R and STC 900 R samples, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 21). These results proved that active sites of the catalysts are ex-solved Co nanoparticles rather than the host materials. Consequently, such an alternative approach opens up various possibilities for enhancing catalytic activity through the selection of host materials, B-site modulation, and changes in the size distribution of ex-solved NPs, which affect the number of active sites in catalytic reactions.

Discussion

We modulated the type of host frameworks (e.g., SrTiO3 and LaAlO3) and B-site composition of perovskites, and investigated the relationship between their physicochemical properties and the size distribution of NPs formed via the ex-solution process. Combining experimental observations with a computational approach (i.e., DFT calculations) provided a more comprehensive understanding of the material behavior, linking theoretical descriptions with experimental outcomes. In all experimental groups used in this study, the size and surface density of the ex-solved particles showed an inverse relationship. Notably, the particle density could be determined by the interplay of two atomic-level descriptors: segregation and reduction energies of materials. More specifically, the segregation energy is predominantly influenced by the type of host perovskites, while the reduction energy is determined by the composition of the B-site of perovskites. Within the same host material, less reducible perovskites tend to form fewer but larger ex-solved particles. Lastly, by incorporating such surface-tailored ex-solution materials, we measured the catalytic activity of the N2O decomposition reaction and identified the optimal number of catalytic active sites in the ex-solution systems. This work advances our capability to design tailored ex-solution catalysts for environmental and energy applications, optimizing material properties for specific catalytic functions.

Methods

Precursors and sample preparation

SrTi0.9Co0.1O3-δ (STC), SrTi0.8Co0.1Zr0.1O3-δ (STCZ), SrTi0.8Co0.1Hf0.1O3-δ (STCH), SrTi0.8Co0.1Nb0.1O3-δ (STCN), SrTi0.8Co0.1Ta0.1O3-δ (STCT), LaAl0.9Co0.1O3-δ (LAC), LaAl0.8Co0.1Ga0.1O3-δ (LACG), LaAl0.8Co0.1Sc0.1O3-δ (LACS) and LaAl0.8Co0.1Ti0.1O3-δ (LACT) (9 compositions in total) materials were synthesized by a conventional solid-state reaction method. Stoichiometric amounts of carbonate and oxide precursors, such as SrCO3 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9 %), TiO2 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.7 %), Co3O4 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.7 %), ZrO2 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99 %), HfO2 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.95 %), Nb2O5 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.99 %), Ta2O5 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.99 %), La2O3 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9 %), Al2O3 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99 %), Ga2O3 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.99 %), In2O3 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.99 %, not included in main experiment due to 2nd phase) and Sc2O3 (Alfa Aesar, 99.9 %) were mixed in ethanol or acetone media with zirconia balls and ball-milled overnight. The mixtures were vigorously stirred and dried on a hot plate; subsequently, the perovskite powders were obtained after annealing under ambient air. Detailed annealing conditions are follows: STC, STCZ, STCH, STCN, and STCT: 1200 °C for 8 h with a ramping rate of 4 °C/min, afterward, 1400 °C for 8 h with a ramping rate of 4 °C/min, LAC and LACG: 1300 °C for 24 h with a ramping rate of 5 °C/min, LACS and LACT: 1400 °C for 24 h with a ramping rate of 5 °C/min. To obtain cleaved surfaces for ex-solution analysis, the powders were shaped into a cylindrical form (diameter ~ 10 mm and height ~ 2 mm) by uniaxial press and each perovskite material was sintered under the same conditions as the final calcination condition. Each annealing condition was selected to yield optimal phase quality and morphology depending on the material composition. The as-sintered pellets were cleaved into four pieces and then subjected to a reduction heat treatment at 900 °C for 10 h in a 5 % H2/Ar atmosphere with a ramping rate of 5 °C/min to obtain Co ex-solution particles.

Physical and chemical characterization

The crystal structures of the synthesized perovskite materials were analysed using a X-ray diffraction (XRD, D8 Advance, Bruker) with Cu Kα radiation beam source. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Quantera II, ULVAC-PHI) measurements were performed to obtain the surface chemical states of the perovskites using Al Kα radiation source with 55 eV of pass energy and 0.1 eV step size. The microstructures of the cleaved surfaces after ex-solution reaction were observed by scanning electron microscopy (UHR SEM, SU-9000, Hitachi). By using SEM images, the size distributions of ex-solved nanoparticles were evaluated (the detailed method was described in supporting information). Reducibility of the perovskites were evaluated by H2 temperature programmed reduction (H2 TPR) measurement (AutoChem 2950 HP, micromeritics). The temperature was elevated from 50 °C to 800 °C with a ramping rate of 10 °C/min under 3% H2/Ar atmosphere.

DFT calculations

All DFT simulations were performed using the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP 5.4.4 version)50 with projector augmented-wave (PAW) pseudopotentials51. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional was employed52, and a Hubbard U correction53 was applied to the Co 3 d states, with U–J set to 3.3 eV. To model the surface slab, the (110) facet was considered. The surface areas of the ab planes for STO and LAO were set to 11.14 Å × 7.87 Å and 10.82 Å × 7.63 Å, respectively (see Fig. 3a for details). The cutoff energy and k-point grids were determined through convergence tests using criteria of 10 meV/atom for energy, 0.1 eV/Å for force, and 10 kbar for stress. A 1 × 2 × 1 k-point mesh was used to sample the Brillouin zone (BZ) for both materials. The plane-wave cutoff energy was set to 500 eV. The initial magnetic moments of Co atoms were set at 3.0–5.0, and were adjusted such that the resulting spin moments of Co atoms were approximately 2.6–2.7. The surface terminations of STO and LAO were determined following the method described in ref. 28 (Supplementary Fig. 22 for details). For STO, the most stable termination was identified as SrTiO2, consistent with the previous literature. In the case of LAO, the LaAlO1.5 termination was found to be the most stable. The dipole correction was applied to avoid spurious periodic interactions54. The structural relaxation was performed until the maximum force was reduced to 0.05 eV/Å.

Catalytic test for N2O decomposition

N2O decomposition was tested in situ with a Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, I4001-E, MIDAC Corporation) equipped with a gas cell in a fixed-bed Inconel reactor. In the catalytic bed, quartz wool was placed at the bottom to prevent catalyst displacement, and 100 mg of the catalytic material was packed. Prior to the measurement, the powders were heat-treated in synthetic air (100 sccm) at 180 °C for 1 hour to remove any surface adsorbates. For N2O decomposition test, 100 sccm in total reactant gas was injected into the reactor in a composition of 3000 ppm N2O with He balance gas corresponding to weight hourly space velocity (WHSV) of 60,000 mL gcat−1 h−1. The reaction temperature was elevated from 25 °C to 800 °C with a ramping rate of 4 °C/min.
