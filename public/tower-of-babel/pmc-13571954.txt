Sex differences and gender-related factors in the management and perception of LDL-cholesterol in ASCVD: Real-Life evidence from the EPHESUS registry in cardiology practice
Authors: Meral Kayıkcioglu, Özcan Başaran, Volkan Doğan, Oğuzhan Çelik, Kadir Uğur Mert, Gurbet Özge Yunus, Murat Biteker, The EPHESUS Study Collaborators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsAtherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally. While it impacts both sexes, women are frequently underdiagnosed and undertreated, particularly in lipid management. This study aimed to evaluate sex differences, and the gender-related factors potentially underlying them, in lipid control, the utilization of lipid-lowering therapy (LLT), and related perceptions within a large real-world cohort.MethodsWe analyzed 1482 patients with established ASCVD from the nationwide, multicenter EPHESUS registry, conducted in 40 cardiology outpatient clinics across Turkey. Differences between women and men in risk profiles, LLT use, low density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C) goal attainment, and perceptions from both patients and physicians were assessed. Women and men were compared before and after 1:1 propensity score matching for age and education; covariate balance was quantified with standardized mean differences (SMD).ResultsWomen showed a greater burden of cardiometabolic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and obesity, yet were less often prescribed statins. Total cholesterol, HDL-C, non-HDL-C and on-treatment LDL-C were higher in women than in men, whereas triglycerides were similar between the sexes; on-treatment LDL-C was significantly higher in women (115 mg/dL vs. 100 mg/dL, p < 0.001). Women also met LDL-C targets less frequently according to the 2016 ESC/EAS guidelines (14.9% vs. 19.6%, p = 0.017), while the difference for the 2019 target was not significant (6.3% vs. 8.3%, p = 0.186). After matching, 924 patients (462 women and 462 men) remained; age and education were well balanced (SMD < 0.05), whereas cardiometabolic covariates remained imbalanced by design. Differences in lipid levels were no longer apparent, and among patients receiving high-intensity statins women more frequently achieved the 2016 LDL-C target (26.0% vs. 13.6%, p = 0.017), whereas the corresponding difference for the 2019 target did not reach statistical significance (10.7% vs. 4.5%, p = 0.072). Media influence was the most common physician-reported reason for LLT cessation; side effects and physician-advised discontinuation were reported more often for women, although these differences were not statistically significant.ConclusionAge and education accounted for a substantial part of the observed differences between women and men in ASCVD care, whereas lower statin prescription in women persisted after matching. Studies addressing sex differences should incorporate gender-related demographic factors into their design and analysis.Keywords: Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, Familial hypercholesterolemia, Gender, LDL-Cholesterol, Lipid-lowering therapy, Secondary prevention, Sex

Introduction

Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD) continues to be the primary cause of mortality among both men and women worldwide [1]. Although its prevalence is comparable across sexes, it is well documented that women with ASCVD are frequently underdiagnosed and undertreated relative to men [2]. The under-representation of women in biological research and clinical trials has further compounded this disparity, resulting in substantial gaps in our understanding and management of ASCVD in female patients [[3], [4], [5]].

Recent data underscores that women with ASCVD are less likely to receive lipid-lowering therapies (LLT), such as statins [6]. Additionally, reports indicate lower adherence and less favorable outcomes in women than in men [7]. This discrepancy highlights a critical need for targeted research focusing on lipid management and cholesterol awareness in women with ASCVD. Both sex-based (biological) and gender-based (societal role) characteristics influence the burden of ASCVD in women [8]. While hormones and sex chromosomes contribute to biological effects, societal roles and perceptions of ASCVD risk factors also contribute to disparities.

The primary objective of our investigation was to assess lipid management and cholesterol awareness among women diagnosed with ASCVD, with a focus on differences between women and men and on the gender-related factors underlying them, utilizing data from the EPHESUS registry. Specifically, we evaluated attainment of low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C) targets in women with ASCVD in real-world settings. Furthermore, we examined the levels of awareness, perceptions, and possible misconceptions regarding hypercholesterolemia in this population. Additional matching for age and educational background was conducted to separate these gender-related demographic factors from the comparison between women and men. Moreover, we sought to determine whether women with familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) experience greater disparities compared to men with FH within the same registry.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study examines patients with established ASCVD (i.e., secondary prevention) to compare outcomes between women and men utilizing data from the EPHESUS registry. Throughout the manuscript, sex refers to comparisons between women and men according to the biological sex recorded at enrollment, whereas gender-related factors refers to social and cultural variables, including educational attainment, place of residence, health beliefs and interactions with healthcare providers. Women in this cohort were older and less educated than men; because age is a major biological determinant of ASCVD risk and education is a central gender-related factor in this population, propensity score matching was applied to these two variables. Matching was deliberately restricted to age and education, and the resulting analyses are therefore described as age- and education-matched rather than fully balanced.

The methodology of the EPHESUS study is comprehensively detailed in previous publications [9]. Briefly, EPHESUS (identifier no. NCT02608645, ClinicalTrials.gov) is a nationwide, multicenter, cross-sectional, observational registry conducted across 40 cardiology outpatient clinics in Turkey. The registry enrolled 1868 consecutive high- or very-high-risk patients, encompassing both primary and secondary prevention cases, between March 1, 2016, and January 1, 2018. Exclusion criteria included patients with low cardiovascular risk, individuals under 18 years of age, current pregnancy or postpartum status within six months, recent acute coronary syndrome within the past month, a history of liver or muscle disease, and renal failure indicated by creatinine levels exceeding 3 mg/dL. The study protocol received approval from the institutional review board (2016, 80558721/11), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Demographic and clinical data

We recorded demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, educational background, ASCVD-related medical history, traditional cardiovascular risk factors, current LLTs, and other relevant risk factors. All comorbidities were self-reported at enrollment. Usage and dosages of LLTs were recorded. Fasting venous blood samples were collected to ascertain lipid profiles, comprising total cholesterol, LDL-C, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and non-HDL cholesterol levels.

Surveys on hypercholesterolemia awareness

To evaluate awareness and perceptions of hypercholesterolemia, patients completed a 10-item questionnaire (Supp. Table 1), whereas their attending physicians responded to an 8-item questionnaire (Supp. Table 2). These instruments were developed by a panel of experienced cardiologists specializing in prevention and survey methodology (MK, OB, and MB).

Definitions and classifications

The prescription and adherence were assessed in accordance with the ESC/EAS Dyslipidaemia Guidelines [1,10]. Nonadherence was defined as a statin utilization rate below 80% or discontinuation of medication. The reasons for nonadherence were categorized as follows:

•Misunderstanding the physician's instructions (e.g., limited-time use, alternate-day dosing, or discontinuation after cholesterol normalization).

•Discontinued due to side effects.

•Discontinuing medication without providing any stated reason.

Hypercholesterolemia was defined as fasting total cholesterol > 240 mg/dL, LDL-C > 160 mg/dL, or the utilization of lipid-lowering therapy (LLT). Hypertriglyceridemia was defined as serum triglycerides >150 mg/dL or the current use of triglyceride-lowering therapy (fibrates); statin use alone did not classify a patient as having hypertriglyceridemia. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure > 140 mmHg, a diastolic blood pressure > 90 mmHg, or the use of antihypertensive medication. Smoking status was self-reported by patients, and obesity was defined as a body mass index >30 kg/m2. Coronary artery disease (CAD) was identified by a history of myocardial infarction, coronary revascularization, or angiographic evidence of more than 50% stenosis of the coronary artery luminal diameter. ASCVD was defined to include patients with CAD (including post-myocardial infarction), history of percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary bypass surgery, peripheral artery disease, or documented atherosclerotic cerebrovascular disease. High-intensity statin therapy was defined as atorvastatin administered at daily doses of 40–80 mg or rosuvastatin at 20–40 mg. For additional analysis of FH, a modified version of the Dutch Lipid Network Criteria (DLNC) was used for diagnosis. Patients with a score of 3 or higher were classified as having FH, covering possible, probable, and definite cases.

Laboratory analysis and LDL-C targets

Blood tests were conducted within six months before enrollment, and pre-treatment LDL-C levels were retrieved from patient records. Fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol, HDL-C, triglycerides, and creatinine levels were measured employing standardized biochemical methods. LDL-C levels were estimated using Friedewald's formula, and non-HDL-C was determined by subtracting HDL-C from total cholesterol. The Friedewald equation is unreliable when triglycerides are ≥400 mg/dL; 42 patients (2.8%) had triglyceride levels in this range, and a sensitivity analysis excluding them yielded materially unchanged results for lipid levels and LDL-C target attainment. Because laboratory tests could have been performed up to six months before enrollment, whereas medication use was recorded at the study visit, some temporal variability between lipid measurements and the documented treatment cannot be excluded.

Since the analysis focused on patients undergoing secondary prevention, we considered an LDL-C level below 55 mg/dL (1.4 mmol/L) the treatment goal, per the 2019 ESC/EAS Guidelines. Conversely, a target of less than 70 mg/dL (1.8 mmol/L) was applied based on the 2016 ESC/EAS Guidelines [1,10,11].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) or means ± standard deviations (SD). Normality was tested with the Shapiro-Wilk test. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and univariate analyses were performed using chi-square or Fisher's exact tests. A multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was performed to assess variables independently associated with LDL-C goal attainment. The dependent variable was attainment of the 2016 ESC/EAS target (LDL-C <70 mg/dL) in the whole cohort, because attainment of the 2019 target was too infrequent (n = 113) for a stable multivariable model. Seven pre-specified variables were entered simultaneously (forced entry, no stepwise selection): age, sex, education, current statin use, smoking, diabetes mellitus and hypertension. These were selected a priori as the demographic and clinical factors of primary interest for the research question rather than on the basis of univariate p values. The model was fitted using complete cases; LDL-C was missing in 3 patients, leaving 1479 patients in the model, and no imputation was performed.

To account for the marked differences in age and education between women and men, propensity score matching was conducted using a nearest-neighbor technique (1:1 ratio, without replacement). Covariate balance was assessed with standardized mean differences (SMD) rather than by p values alone, and an absolute SMD below 0.10 was considered to indicate adequate balance. SMDs before and after matching are reported in Table 1. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was regarded as indicative of statistical significance.

Results

General comparison of men and women with ASCVD

The current comparison of women and men included all consecutive secondary prevention patients with ASCVD (n = 1482) from the EPHESUS Registry. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study groups, as depicted in Table 1, revealed significant differences between women and men. Women were older, smoked less, and had a significantly higher prevalence of cardio-metabolic risk factors, although the frequency of a family history of CVD was similar between sexes. More women lived in rural areas (34.3% vs. 26.9%, p = 0.004) and had substantially lower educational attainment (43.5% illiterate or with no formal schooling vs. 6.6% in men, p < 0.001; 79.9% versus 57.1% for primary education or less, Table 1). Comorbidity profiles also differed; women had a lower prevalence of renal failure but a higher rate of atrial fibrillation. Additionally, ischemia with no obstructive coronary arteries (INOCA) was more prevalent in women (33.0% vs. 20.3%, p < 0.001). Laboratory findings indicated higher glucose and lower creatinine kinase levels in women. In terms of medication use, women were less likely to use aspirin but more likely to use oral anticoagulants, oral antidiabetics, insulin, and calcium channel blockers.

Lipid profile, LDL-C target attainment, and use of lipid-lowering therapy

As demonstrated in Table 2, total cholesterol, HDL-C, non-HDL-C and on-treatment LDL-C were higher in women than in men, whereas triglyceride levels were similar between the sexes (154 versus 149 mg/dL, p = 0.426). On-treatment LDL-C was significantly higher in women (115 mg/dL versus 100 mg/dL, p < 0.001). The higher HDL-C concentrations observed in women are not an adverse lipid finding and are consistent with the recognized sex difference in HDL-C. Lipid profiles and treatment patterns exhibited differences by sex. Although not statistically significant, a lower proportion of women were receiving statin therapy (64.1% versus 69.0%, p = 0.073) and high-intensity statins (33.8% versus 38.1%, p = 0.204), and very few women were administered non-statin therapies (for example, only one woman was on ezetimibe; none received proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors). Women were less likely to achieve the LDL-C target of the 2016 ESC/EAS guidelines (14.9% versus 19.6%, p = 0.017), whereas the difference for the 2019 target was not significant (6.3% versus 8.3%, p = 0.186). Among statin users, women also exhibited lower rates of goal attainment, although these differences were not statistically significant (21.9% versus 25.6%, p = 0.211, and 8.5% versus 10.3%, p = 0.385, for the 2016 and 2019 guidelines, respectively). LDL-C target achievement among patients on high-intensity statin therapy was similar between women and men for both the 2016 (26.0% versus 27.5%, p = 0.768) and 2019 (10.7% versus 11.0%, p = 0.932) guidelines.

Physician-reported LDL-C control and statin adherence (physicians survey)

Physician responses indicated that only 27.2% of women and 37.8% of men were perceived to have achieved their LDL-C targets (p < 0.001) (Table 3). Among those using statins, adherence, defined as consistent daily use, was reported at 75.3% among women and 78.2% among men (p = 0.153). The primary reasons for not reaching LDL-C objectives included the absence of statin therapy (42.6% in women versus 39.9% in men) and suboptimal adherence or dosing (49.4% in women versus 50.5% in men). Physicians noted that a significant proportion of women (74%) had discontinued statin therapy at least once in the past. The reasons for discontinuation were similar between men and women, with media influence and physician recommendation being the most frequently cited; notably, media influence was more prominent among men (27.1% versus 35.2%), whereas physician recommendation was more common among women (23.2% versus 17.2%) (Fig. 1).

Patient perceptions regarding cholesterol management

The patient perception questionnaire also indicated differences between women and men, most of which were related to gender-related demographic factors (Table 4). A higher proportion of women perceived themselves to have high cholesterol levels (51.4% versus 45.6%, p = 0.043), whereas fewer were aware of their actual cholesterol levels (26.1% versus 35.3%, p = 0.001). Attitudes toward discontinuing treatment after LDL-C normalization were similar between women and men. A greater percentage of men preferred lifestyle modifications over pharmacological therapy (39.8% versus 32.4%, p = 0.022). After matching, concerns that long-term cholesterol-lowering treatment could cause diabetes or cancer and dementia were more frequent among women, whereas concern about liver or kidney damage did not differ significantly between the sexes. Approximately 60% of individuals in both groups considered their diets to be healthy.

Multivariate analysis

A multivariate analysis was conducted to identify the determinants associated with achieving the LDL-C target. The dependent variable was attainment of the 2016 ESC/EAS target (LDL-C <70 mg/dL) and the model included 1479 patients with complete data. In univariate analysis, literacy compared with illiteracy (odds ratio [OR]: 1.57; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.07–2.31; p = 0.020), female sex (OR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.51–0.94; p = 0.017) and statin use (OR: 3.86; 95% CI: 2.65–5.61; p < 0.001) were associated with target attainment. When all seven pre-specified variables were entered simultaneously, only statin use (OR: 3.77; 95% CI: 2.59–5.49; p < 0.001) remained independently associated with achieving the LDL-C goal, whereas female sex was no longer significant (OR: 0.75; 95% CI: 0.54–1.07; p = 0.119) (Supp. Table 3).

Propensity score matching for age and education

A propensity score matching for age and education was conducted. Following propensity score matching, the study included 924 patients, comprising 462 women and 462 men. The median age was identical at 66 years (IQR: 59–72; p = 0.824) and the two matching variables were well balanced, with absolute SMDs falling from 0.36 to 0.01 for age and from 0.51 to 0.02 for education (Table 1). Because matching was intentionally limited to these two variables, the groups were not globally balanced: several cardiometabolic covariates remained different beyond the conventional threshold of 0.10, including smoking (SMD −0.54), body mass index (0.49), obesity (0.48), hypertension (0.21), diabetes (0.19), ischemia with no obstructive coronary arteries (0.24) and calcium channel blocker use (0.25). Smoking remained more prevalent among men, whereas women continued to demonstrate higher rates of hypertension, obesity, and diabetes. Matching for age and education did, however, remove the imbalance in rural residence between women and men (SMD 0.16 before versus 0.06 after matching). All analyses in the matched cohort should therefore be interpreted as age- and education-matched rather than fully adjusted comparisons.

After age and education were matched, most lipid profile differences were attenuated (Table 2). On-treatment LDL-C levels became statistically comparable (115 mg/dL vs. 111 mg/dL, p = 0.475). However, statin usage (64.1% vs. 70.3%, p = 0.042) and high-intensity statin usage (33.9% vs. 38.9%, p = 0.182) remained lower among women. LDL-C goal attainment according to the 2016 and 2019 guidelines was similar between the two sexes. Among patients receiving high-intensity statins, women more frequently achieved the 2016 LDL-C target (26.0% versus 13.6%, p = 0.017), whereas the corresponding difference for the 2019 target did not reach statistical significance (10.7% versus 4.5%, p = 0.072).

Age and education matching also influenced the physician-reported barriers to LDL-C control between women and men (Table 3). The reasons for not achieving LDL-C targets differed between the sexes, with non-adherence to statin therapy being the most frequently cited reason (49.4% among women versus 57.9% among men, p = 0.036), followed by not being on statin therapy (42.6% among women versus 33.3% among men, p = 0.017). Media influence remained the most commonly cited reason for statin discontinuation (27.1% among women versus 31.1% among men), although women more frequently reported side effects (9.0% versus 4.2%, respectively) (Fig. 2).

However, the responses to the patient questionnaire were not affected by age and education matching (Table 4). The disparity between women and men was eliminated regarding awareness of their own elevated cholesterol levels (51.4% vs. 57.4%, p = 0.086) and familiarity with their own actual cholesterol levels (26.1% vs. 29.4%, p = 0.271). Approximately one-third of both women and men believed that cholesterol treatment should be discontinued once cholesterol levels become normalized. No significant differences between women and men were observed before or after matching concerning beliefs about treatment cessation. Before matching, a greater proportion of men believed that exercise and diet are safer and more effective than pharmacotherapy for cholesterol management (39.8% vs. 32.4%, p = 0.022). Post-matching, this difference was also eliminated. Concerns regarding hepatotoxicity/nephrotoxicity persisted at similar rates. Conversely, worries about statin-induced diabetes or cancer became significantly more prevalent among women (10.8% vs. 6.1%, p = 0.031), as did concerns about dementia (10.4% vs. 4.3%, p = 0.002). Perceptions of dietary healthfulness remained consistent across groups.

Sex-based analysis of patients with FH

The study cohort consisted of 618 patients diagnosed with FH, of whom 214 were female (34.6%). Lipid profile analyses revealed that women with FH exhibited significantly higher total cholesterol, non-HDL cholesterol, and on-treatment LDL-C, as well as higher HDL-C, which is the expected sex difference rather than an adverse finding (all p-values <0.001). Conversely, pre-treatment LDL-C and triglyceride levels did not show marked differences between females and males with FH (Supp. Table 4). Male patients were more likely to receive statin therapy (82.9% versus 76.2%, p = 0.054), particularly high-intensity statins (44.4% versus 36.3%, p = 0.088). The achievement of LDL-C targets was suboptimal across both sexes: only 5.6% of women and 7.9% of men attained the 2016 ESC/EAS targets (p = 0.327), while 2.9% and 2.7%, respectively, achieved the more stringent 2019 targets (p = 1.00).

According to physician assessments (Supp. Table 5), a lower proportion of FH-women than of FH-men was perceived to have achieved their LDL-C targets (14.2% versus 20.6%, p = 0.049). The predominant reasons cited for failure to meet these targets were nonadherence or insufficient dosing (63.1% in women versus 70.3% in men), followed by a lack of statin therapy (26.7% versus 18.6%, p = 0.097) and inadequate lifestyle modifications. Among patients who discontinued statin therapy, media coverage was identified as the most commonly reported reason in both FH groups (33.8% in women versus 39.5% in men, p = 0.216). However, women were more likely to report adverse effects as the reason for discontinuation (13.0% versus 4.2%, p = 0.216).

Discussion

In this large, real-world cohort of patients diagnosed with ASCVD from the EPHESUS registry, we identified sex- and gender-related disparities in lipid management, attainment of LDL-C targets, perceived barriers to care among physicians, and patients' perceptions of cholesterol and LLT. Women exhibited a greater cardiometabolic risk burden, were less frequently prescribed statins, particularly high-intensity regimens, and were less successful in reaching LDL-C targets compared to men. A distinctive aspect of this study was its methodology: employing propensity score matching enabled us to compare women and men independently of confounding factors such as age and education. After matching, the lower proportion of women receiving statins persisted, whereas physician-reported regular daily statin use was similar between women and men. Nonetheless, following propensity score matching, differences previously observed in lipid profiles, specifically, LDL-C and non-HDL-C levels, and in treatment response by sex and gender were no longer apparent. Among patients treated with high-intensity statins, women more frequently achieved the 2016 LDL-C target than men, whereas the corresponding difference for the 2019 target did not reach statistical significance.

Sex-based disparities in lipid response and residual treatment gaps

Our analysis revealed that women with ASCVD were generally older and had a greater burden of comorbidities, including hypertension, obesity, and diabetes, compared to men. These findings are in line with previous reports of the EUROASPIRE V [12], VIRGO [13], and the TEKHARF studies [14], all of which demonstrated that women tend to present with poorer cardio-metabolic control and receive less intensive medical therapy than men. Similarly, data from the TURKMI registry [15] indicated that women experiencing myocardial infarction in Turkey were older, exhibited poorer metabolic control, and received less evidence-based medical therapy compared to men. Our study demonstrated that hypertension and diabetes were more prevalent among women after propensity score matching, underscoring the significant burden of cardiovascular risk factors beyond age and educational level. Additionally, in our cohort, women more frequently resided in rural areas and had lower educational attainment, both of which are recognized social determinants of disparities in cardiovascular health [16]. However, the differences in rural residence lost significance following matching.

Regarding lipid management, our study demonstrated that women had a significantly less favorable atherogenic lipid profile than men, characterized by elevated non-HDL-C and on-treatment LDL-C levels, as well as lower rates of LDL-C goal attainment; HDL-C was also higher in women, which is the expected sex difference and not an adverse finding. Furthermore, the use of both overall statins and high-intensity statins was less prevalent among women. These findings align with previous registries across various cardiovascular settings, including acute coronary syndromes and stroke, which have consistently underscored suboptimal lipid control and reduced statin utilization in women [17,18]. After propensity score matching, disparities in non-HDL-C and treatment LDL-C levels based on sex and gender were no longer evident, indicating a substantial influence of demographic factors. Although LDL-C target attainment remained low in both sexes, among patients on high-intensity statins women more frequently reached the 2016 target after matching, with a similar but non-significant trend for the 2019 target.

Physician perceptions, media influence, and gender-based inertia

Survey findings reported by physicians indicated that achievement of LDL-C targets was particularly inadequate among women, with nonadherence to statin therapy and suboptimal dosing identified as primary obstacles. These perceptions align with results from the DYSIS II study [19], where clinicians systematically underestimated LDL-C control rates and demonstrated therapeutic inertia, especially in managing female patients. The SANTORINI study also observed poor LDL-C goal attainment among patients with high cardiovascular risk, with two major contributing factors being underestimation of cardiovascular risk and underuse of LLT [20]. Similarly, findings from the AISONAOI study [21] revealed a widespread underestimation of lipid control, with a disproportionate impact on women. However, none of these studies were matched for age and education. Aging is one of the most well-known risk factors for the development of ASCVD, with the risk burden increasing as women advance in age. Notably, after matching for age and educational level, the difference between women and men in physicians’ perception of LDL-C goal attainment was no longer apparent in our cohort. However, post-matching, the differences between women and men became more distinct: men more frequently reported non-adherence or insufficient dosing of statins, whereas women more commonly discontinued statin therapy entirely. Nonetheless, randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have consistently shown that the efficacy of LLT, including statins, does not differ significantly between men and women, suggesting that these disparities are not biologically driven [22].

Propensity score matching did not diminish the perceived influence of media, which remained the most frequently cited reason for statin discontinuation in both sexes. This observation is consistent with recent Turkish surveys on adherence to cardiovascular medications [23]. It emphasizes the increasing impact of health-related misinformation, particularly through digital and social media, on undermining medical advice and contributing to poor adherence. Importantly, prior studies from Denmark have associated negative media coverage of statins with heightened cardiovascular mortality, demonstrating the extensive consequences of public perception [24].

Health literacy and digital health literacy are notably pertinent within this context. The data comparing health literacy between men and women in Turkey remains inconsistent [25] and is partially attributable to the confounding factors such as age, education, and socioeconomic status. But there is consensus that overall health literacy levels in the Turkish population are low [26]. Furthermore, the proportion of Turkish women exhibiting inadequate health literacy exceeds 45.9%, thereby intensifying difficulties in achieving effective lipid management [27] and further exacerbating their susceptibility to misinformation.

Another notable finding from our physician survey was that among patients who discontinued statin therapy, physician recommendation was more frequently cited by women than men (23.2% vs. 16.8%) (Table 3). Although this difference did not reach statistical significance after matching, it remained directionally consistent. One possible explanation is a gender-related influence on clinical decision-making, whereby treatment discontinuation may be considered more readily in female patients because of perceived intolerance or therapeutic pessimism. Such an interpretation is hypothesis-generating: clinical inertia and structural barriers were not measured in this registry and cannot be established from cross-sectional data. However, our study does not directly assess media influence, provider bias, or health literacy; findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

Gender differences in cholesterol awareness and risk perception

Patient-reported survey responses also revealed notable differences between women and men in cholesterol awareness and perceptions. While women were more likely than men to perceive themselves as having high cholesterol, they were significantly less likely to know their actual cholesterol levels. However, after matching on age and educational level, these differences in awareness and perceptions were no longer statistically significant, suggesting that disparities in health literacy are largely attributable to socio-demographic factors. Nonetheless, women continued to report significantly greater concern regarding the potential adverse effects of LLTs, particularly with respect to long-term risks such as hepatic or cognitive side effects. This heightened perception of harm may contribute to lower adherence and higher discontinuation rates observed among women, underscoring the importance of gender-sensitive patient education strategies to improve treatment acceptance and sustained engagement [28].

Are women truly different, or are age, education, and inertia the real drivers of disparities in cardiovascular care?

Although demographic characteristics contribute to observed disparities, our findings and those from the EUROASPIRE and VIRGO studies suggest they do not entirely account for sex differences [12,13]. In contrast, sex-related differences in lipid profiles, such as elevated on-treatment LDL-C and non-HDL-C levels in women, became non-apparent following adjustments. Among patients prescribed high-intensity statins, women more frequently achieved the 2016 LDL-C target than men, although the difference for the 2019 target was not statistically significant; these observations are compatible with meta-analyses indicating no sex disparity in statin efficacy [17].

Nevertheless, even after adjustments, women continued to be less likely to receive statins, particularly high-intensity regimens. This persistent under-treatment may be related to systemic and behavioral factors, including physician inertia, implicit bias, and heightened concerns regarding statin safety among female patients; these mechanisms were not assessed in the present study and are proposed as potential explanations rather than demonstrated causes. Our findings are consistent with international surveys, such as the PALM registry, which demonstrate that women more frequently cite fear of side effects as a reason for nonadherence [29].

Overall, these findings imply that although demographic variables such as age and education influence observed sex-based disparities, they do not entirely explain the persistent gaps in lipid management [30]. The ongoing underutilization of statins and high-intensity regimens among women, despite comparable responses to treatment, would be compatible with clinical inertia and with inequities in healthcare provision, although neither could be verified in this cross-sectional registry. To bridge these gaps, comprehensive interventions are required to address provider-related biases, enhance health literacy, and promote patient-centered approaches that recognize sex-specific concerns and adherence barriers [31].

Sex and gender specific disparities in FH

In our cohort, women with FH exhibited comparable baseline LDL-C levels, were less likely to be prescribed statins, particularly high-intensity regimens, and demonstrated higher on-treatment LDL-C values. These findings are consistent with both international [32] and national Turkish FH registries [33], as well as prior evidence from the CASCADE-FH Registry [34], all of which reliably document delayed diagnosis and reduced treatment intensity among women with FH despite an elevated cardiovascular risk [2,35].

Factors contributing to this elevated lipid burden may encompass delayed diagnosis, underutilization of maximal or combination LLT, and interruptions in treatment during pregnancy or breastfeeding [2,36]. Although such gaps are seldom documented in registries, they substantially elevate cumulative LDL-C exposure.

While media influence remained the most common physician-reported reason for statin discontinuation in both sexes, side effects, physician-advised discontinuation and long-term safety concerns were reported more often for women, echoing findings from both FH and non-FH populations [2,6,28,37]. These differences did not reach statistical significance and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than as established inequities in FH care. Bridging this gap requires gender-sensitive, patient-centered approaches to promote treatment continuity, improve health literacy, and ensure equitable access to intensive therapies, particularly PCSK9 inhibitors, for women.

Study strengths and limitations

The present study has several strengths. Firstly, it is based on a substantial multicenter cohort of patients with ASCVD enrolled from cardiology outpatient clinics across Turkey, thereby offering valuable insights into real-world lipid management practices in clinical settings. Moreover, to our knowledge this is the largest multicenter registry analysis, that is, the largest epidemiologic study, to date addressing sex differences and gender-related factors in hyperlipidemia management in Turkey. Secondly, the integration of physician-reported and patient-reported survey data provides a comprehensive and multidimensional perspective on barriers to treatment and perceptions of cholesterol. Thirdly, the application of propensity score matching for the two key demographic confounders, age and education, with balance documented by standardized mean differences, permits the residual differences to be examined separately from these two factors. Moreover, most available registry data compare women and men with ASCVD despite the significant age gap between sexes, which may lead to artificial differences. The separate comparison of women and men with FH adds clinical relevance to our findings and underscores critical gaps in secondary prevention strategies.

Limitations of the study should also be acknowledged. First, the EPHESUS registry is observational and cross-sectional; both the absence of randomization and the single time point of assessment preclude causal inference, and all reported associations should be read as descriptive. Second, the study was conducted entirely in a Turkish population recruited within one healthcare system, so the external validity of the findings for other populations, healthcare systems and reimbursement settings is limited. Data on medication adherence and patient perceptions were self-reported, introducing potential recall and social desirability bias. Although the study encompassed a broad range of demographic and clinical variables, certain potentially influential factors, such as detailed socioeconomic status and health literacy, were not available. Furthermore, since the study cohort was derived exclusively from cardiology outpatient clinics, it may not accurately represent community-based epidemiological patterns. Nevertheless, cardiologists are expected to be the physician group with the greatest awareness of ASCVD risk in women; therefore, the situation may be even more unfavorable in other specialties and primary care settings. Additionally, recruitment from hospital-based clinical settings poses a potential risk of Berkson-type bias, whereby patients attending clinics may systematically differ from the broader ASCVD population. Two examples illustrate how this could have influenced our results. First, women in this cohort had a high burden of hypertension, diabetes and obesity and were frequently taking several cardiovascular drugs; women with ASCVD but few comorbidities may be less likely to attend a cardiology clinic, so the excess cardiometabolic risk observed in women may be exaggerated relative to the community. Second, patients who had stopped statins because of side effects or media reports may be over-represented among clinic attenders, particularly among women, who consulted more frequently; the sex difference in physician-reported reasons for discontinuation may therefore reflect who reaches the clinic as much as who stops treatment. These selection mechanisms could distort the observed comparisons between women and men; however, the direction and magnitude of any resulting bias cannot be determined from the present data. However, this limitation is not unique to EPHESUS but is inherent to hospital-based registries in general.

The use of non-validated questionnaires may have affected the reliability and generalizability of our findings; however, the questionnaires were prepared by experts in ASCVD prevention, survey methodology, and lipidology. Another potential limitation is that matching was performed only for age and education, rather than for cardiometabolic risk factors. Nevertheless, in studies comparing women and men with ASCVD, women are typically older than men at the time of ASCVD development, which may contribute to a higher cardiometabolic risk factor burden. In the present study, we eliminated this age difference and still observed persistent clustering of cardiometabolic risk factors among women. Further matching for cardiometabolic risk factors was not feasible, as it would have substantially reduced the number of women available for analysis. In addition, conducting the study in the Turkish population highlighted educational differences between women and men; by matching for education, we minimized this imbalance and improved the generalizability of our findings to populations in which women and men have comparable educational backgrounds. This is particularly important because educational disparities may substantially influence disease perception, health awareness, treatment adherence, and other health-related behaviors. Moreover, age and education matching also removed the significant imbalance in rural versus urban residence observed between women and men before adjustment. Finally, because matching addressed only age and education, and because standardized mean differences showed that several cardiometabolic covariates remained imbalanced after matching, residual confounding due to unmeasured and unbalanced variables cannot be ruled out.

Conclusion

This study describes differences between women and men in lipid management among patients with ASCVD, even within cardiology practice. Differences in lipid profiles and in most patient perceptions were no longer apparent after matching for age and education, indicating that a substantial part of the apparent sex difference is attributable to these gender-related demographic factors. Gaps in statin utilization and treatment intensity nevertheless persisted in women. These disparities were more evident among individuals with FH, where suboptimal treatment and increased concerns about therapy further impeded cardiovascular risk reduction in women. Demographic, clinical, behavioral, and systemic factors, including physician inertia and health literacy, are plausible contributors to the residual gaps, although this design cannot demonstrate their causal role. Addressing these gaps will require treatment strategies that are sensitive to both sex and gender-related circumstances and that engage healthcare providers and patients in guideline-adherent lipid management.
