Multi-layer Cross-Attention is Provably Optimal for Multi-modal In-context Learning
Nicholas Barnfield, Subhabrata Sen, Pragya Sur

Abstract
Recent progress has rapidly advanced our understanding of the mechanisms underlying in-context learning in modern attention-based neural networks. However, existing results focus exclusively on unimodal data; in contrast, the theoretical underpinnings of in-context learning for multi-modal data remain poorly understood. We introduce a mathematically tractable framework for studying multi-modal learning and explore when transformer-like architectures can recover Bayes-optimal performance in-context. To model multi-modal problems, we assume the observed data arises from a latent factor model. Our first result comprises a negative take on expressibility: we prove that single-layer, linear self-attention fails to recover the Bayes-optimal predictor uniformly over the task distribution. To address this limitation, we introduce a novel, linearized cross-attention mechanism, which we study in the regime where both the number of cross-attention layers and the context length are large. We show that this cross-attention mechanism is provably Bayes optimal when optimized using gradient flow. Our results underscore the benefits of depth for in-context learning and establish the provable utility of cross-attention for multi-modal distributions.

Introduction

Large language models exhibit striking “in-context learning” (ICL) behavior
(
7
)
: given a sequence of input-output pairs for a new task, the model can infer the rule (the function from input to output) without any update to the model weights. This phenomenon has motivated a growing body of theoretical work that tries to understand when and how ICL succeeds in linearized attention-based models
(
49
;
28
;
51
)
.

Prior work on ICL focuses on unimodal data; in contrast, modern foundation models routinely process multi-modal data, such as text, images, video
(
24
)
, multi-omic data
(
12
)
, etc. This prompts the natural question:

Can attention-based neural networks learn prediction rules in-context when deployed on multi-modal datasets?

In this work, we formulate and study an ICL problem on multi-modal data. Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We introduce a statistical model for ICL on multi-modal data. The proposed data distribution is a latent factor model where the factors are dependent across the data modalities.

•

We show that a single-layer, linear self-attention (LSA) model fails to achieve Bayes-optimal performance in-context over this class of data distributions.

•

We propose a multi-layer model with cross-attention (CA) plus self-attention (SA) and skip connections to solve the ICL problem in this context. Linearizing the attention mechanism, we show that, upon a suitable restriction of the model parameters, gradient flow on the population loss of converges to the Bayes-optimal ICL predictor.

To highlight the technical challenge in our setting, note that prior work on ICL in a regression setting assumes that the covariates are sampled from a fixed distribution (usually Gaussian) independent of the learning task
(
49
;
51
;
28
)
. Indeed,
(
49
;
51
)
explicitly notes that the invariance of the covariate distribution across prompts is crucial in establishing the success of single-layer LSA. In sharp contrast, our multi-modal model exhibits covariate-shifts across prompts. In addition, the prediction rule in a prompt is naturally coupled with the covariate distribution in our setting.
In Theorem
4.1
, we formally establish that the natural covariate shift in our setup invalidates single-layer LSA (i.e., models based on single-layer LSA fail to be Bayes optimal). To overcome this challenge, we introduce a multi-layer model based on both linear cross-attention (LCA) and LSA, and show that it succeeds on the ICL task.

At an intuitive level, the success of attention-based neural networks is derived from their ability to learn “long-range” relations among the features. In multi-modal learning, it is critical to learn the dependence among features across the observed modalities. The CA mechanism has been suggested as a natural extension of the attention mechanism to this end
(
42
;
27
)
; in our work, we demonstrate the efficacy of deep neural networks which use both LCA and LSA layers for multi-modal learning.

Problem Setup

Notation.

Bold lower and upper case letters are used for vectors and matrices respectively. We use
\bm{0}_{d\times d}
and
\bm{0}_{d}
for the matrix and vector of all zero entries. We use
\bm{I}_{d}
to denote the identity matrix in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, omitting the subscript at times when the ambient dimension is evident. The standard basis vectors are written as
\bm{e}_{j}
for

to denote the vector of zeros except for a one in the
j

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is written
\mathcal{N}_{d}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
where
\bm{\mu}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
denotes the mean and
\bm{\Sigma}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
denotes the covariance. Lastly, for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we write

.

We study a multi-modal framework where each prompt (task) provides a context of input–output examples; the inputs contain features from two distinct modalities. To model a natural multi-modal setting, we assume the data is drawn from a latent variable model. Unlike prior work on ICL, we allow the distribution of covariates to differ across tasks.

Formally, we observe
N
prompts (tasks) with (training) context length
L
. For each prompt
j\in[N]
, we observe a sequence

(\bar{\bm{z}}_{i}^{(j)},\tilde{\bm{z}}_{i}^{(j)},y_{i}^{(j)})_{i=1}^{L}

where
y^{(j)}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
is a response and
\bar{\bm{z}}_{i}^{(j)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{1}},\tilde{\bm{z}}_{i}^{(j)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{2}}
are two modalities of covariates. For example,
\bar{\bm{z}}
could represent an image,
\tilde{\bm{z}}
some associated text annotation, and
y
represents a real-valued output. In each prompt, we additionally observe a tuple
(\bar{z}_{q}^{(j)},\tilde{z}_{q}^{(j)},y_{q}^{(j)})
. We input the prompt and the covariate pair
(\bar{\bm{z}}_{q}^{(j)},\tilde{\bm{z}}_{q}^{(j)})
into the model, and it outputs a prediction
\hat{y}_{q}^{(j)}
for
y_{q}^{(j)}
. While training, the model weights are tuned to minimize the error in predicting the
y_{q}
’s on the training prompts. To assess the ICL performance of the fitted model, we consider a fresh prompt, and study the prediction error of the fitted model on the
y_{q}
associated with the test prompt.

We now turn to a statistical model for the data. The covariates and response are generated via

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{z}}_{i}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=u_{i}^{(j)}\bm{v}^{(j)}+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{(j)}

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{z}}_{i}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=u_{i}^{(j)}\bm{r}^{(j)}+\bm{\eta}_{i}^{(j)}

\displaystyle y_{i}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\zeta^{(j)}u_{i}^{(j)}.

(3.1)

The latent variable
u_{i}^{(j)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is shared across both modalities and the response. The the prompt-specific regression coefficient, which relates
u_{i}^{(j)}
to
y_{i}^{(j)}
, is given by
\zeta^{(j)}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We will assume that across prompts,
\zeta^{(j)}
are sampled i.i.d. from a distribution with mean
0
and variance
1
. On the other hand,
\bm{v}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
\bm{r}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
are modality-specific vectors sampled i.i.d. across prompts. We defer precise assumptions about their distributions to later sections. Finally,
\bm{\xi}_{i}^{(j)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}_{d_{1}}(0,\bm{I})
and
\bm{\eta}_{i}^{(j)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}_{d_{2}}(0,\bm{I})
are i.i.d. noise variables. The setup in (
3
) formalizes mathematically a fundamental idea underlying multi-modal machine learning: each modality provides a different (noisy) view of a common latent feature
u
which drives the response
y
.

Focusing on a single task, omitting the prompt superscript, stacking the covariates and their modality vectors, we denote

\bm{x}_{i}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\bm{z}}_{i}\\
\tilde{\bm{z}}_{i}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\qquad\bm{m}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
\bm{r}\end{bmatrix}

(3.2)

and let
d=d_{1}+d_{2}
. Then, in each prompt (i.e. conditional on
\bm{m}
and
\zeta
), the covariates and response are jointly Gaussian

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{i}\\
y_{i}\end{bmatrix}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\cal N}_{d+1}\left(0,\bm{\Sigma}\right)\,\,\,\,\text{with}\,\,\,\,\bm{\Sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}+\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\top}&\zeta\bm{m}\\
\zeta\bm{m}^{\top}&\zeta^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

The joint Gaussianity of the covariates and response allows us to write

y_{i}=\langle\bm{w},\bm{x}_{i}\rangle+\varepsilon_{i}\quad{\rm where}\quad\bm{w}=\frac{\zeta}{1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2}}\bm{m}

(3.3)

\quad{\rm and}\quad\varepsilon_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\cal N}\left(0,\;\zeta^{2}/(1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2})\right).

Our goal is to learn the task-specific Bayes-optimal predictor
\langle w,x\rangle
.
The setup (
3.3
) is reminiscent of the regression setting considered in prior work
(
49
;
28
;
51
)
, yet presents several differences that make the learning problem significantly more difficult. Upon setting

\bm{\Lambda}=\bm{I}+\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\top}

(3.4)

as the covariance of the covariates
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}\cup\{\bm{x}_{q}\}
, we note that the distribution of covariates varies across prompts, since
\bm{m}
is sampled anew for each task. Furthermore, the task vector
w
and covariate distribution are coupled through
\bm{m}
. Similarly, the variance of the additive noise and the task vector are coupled through the task-specific parameter
\zeta
. These dependencies invalidate prior ICL theory in our setup.

As we will see below, this leads to the failure of single-layer linearized attention models previously shown to be successful for ICL when the covariate distribution does not change across prompts
(
49
;
51
)
.

4
ICL and Failure of LSA

In this section, we introduce ICL formally and show that a single-layer LSA model fails at the ICL task. To this end,
we represent a single prompt by its covariate matrix and target vector

\displaystyle\bm{X}

\displaystyle:=[\bm{x}_{1},\dots,x_{L},\bm{x}_{q}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(L+1)},

\displaystyle\bm{y}

\displaystyle:=[y_{1},\dots,y_{L},0]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{L+1},

where we append a dummy zero label at the query position.
We define the (non-learned) joint embedding matrix

\bm{E}_{\bm{X}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{X}\\
\bm{y}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{1}&\bm{x}_{2}&\cdots&\bm{x}_{L}&\bm{x}_{q}\\
y_{1}&y_{2}&\cdots&y_{L}&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(L+1)}

which has been employed in recent works on
(
1
;
49
;
9
;
20
)
.
A model

maps

to a scalar prediction
\hat{y}_{q}=f(\bm{E}_{\bm{X}})
with the goal of approximating
\hat{y}_{q}\approx y_{q}
.

So far, the context length

is a free variable. In the subsequent discussion, it will be helpful to assume that the context length
L=L_{\rm tr}
for prompts in the training data. We set

to denote the context length at test time. Our model architecture is independent of the context length, and thus this distinction is mostly for mathematical convenience. The parameter
L_{\rm te}

4.1
ICL Definition

Formal ICL Criterion.

Let
\hat{y}_{q}^{(L_{\mathrm{te}})}
denote the model prediction on a new prompt when the test context length
L=L_{\mathrm{te}}

We say a model

if

\hat{y}_{q}^{(L_{\mathrm{te}})}\longrightarrow y_{q}^{\rm Bayes}:={\mathbb{E}}[y_{q}|\bm{m},\zeta,\bm{x}_{q}]=\langle\bm{w},\bm{x}_{q}\rangle

almost surely as

where the almost sure statement is with respect to the randomness of the new prompt. In words, ICL in our framework is synonymous with the model recovering the
Bayes-optimal predictor

The criterion (

(

;

;
28
)
, the task vector

Baseline Model

As a starting point, we study a simple baseline model and show its inability to in-context learn in our setting.

Single-layer LSA.

We consider a single, LSA layer acting on

Weight Simplifications

Thus far, the learnable weights have remained unconstrained. We now restrict their parameter space to ensure analytical tractability. To this end, we consider two simplifications. For both, we freeze

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{t-1}^{K}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}_{t-1}^{Q}=\bm{I}_{d},\quad\bm{W}^{PV}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}_{d\times d}&\bm{0}_{d}\\

are learnable parameters, yielding a two-parameter model

\hat{y}_{q}=f(\bm{X},\bm{y};\alpha,\beta).

(5.7)

The first model is recovered from the second by setting
\beta=-\alpha
. In both cases, we have kept the original architecture while restricting the parameter space. Assuming a well-chosen training procedure, one can thus view the performance of the one- and two-parameter models as an upper-bound for the full model where all parameters are free. We show in the following that the two simplified models successfully achieve Bayes-optimal prediction in the ICL setting when trained by gradient flow.

6
Training Setup and Main Results

6.1
Training Setup

Herein, we consider the one- and two-parameter models
\hat{y}_{q}=f(\bm{X},y;\theta)
of Section
5.1
where
\theta
either denotes the parameters
\alpha
or
(\alpha,\beta)
. Starting with a set of
N
training prompts
\{(\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)},y_{i}^{(j)})_{i=1}^{L_{\rm tr}}\cup(\bm{x}_{q}^{(j)},y_{q}^{(j)})\}_{j=1}^{N}
, given real-valued, continuous targets, it is natural to consider the empirical squared loss on the target query

\ell_{N,L_{\mathrm{tr}}}(\theta):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(y^{(j)}_{q}-f(\bm{X}^{(j)},\bm{y}^{(j)};\theta)\right)^{2}.

As in prior work
(
49
;
1
)
, taking the number of training prompts
N\to\infty
, we operate on the population loss

\ell_{L_{\mathrm{tr}}}(\theta):=\lim_{N\to\infty}\ell_{N,L_{\mathrm{tr}}}(\theta)={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(y_{q}-f(\bm{X},\bm{y};\theta)\right)^{2}\right],

where the expectation is taken with respect to the joint distribution detailed in Section
3
. As a final reduction, we consider the asymptotic, training context-length regime and define the loss

\ell(\theta):=\lim_{L_{\rm tr}\to\infty}\ell_{L_{\mathrm{tr}}}(\theta).

(6.1)

This regime is qualitatively similar to prior work
(
49
)
whereby one establishes the ICL capacity of the model at large context length, however our presentation diverges by taking the
L_{\mathrm{tr}}\to\infty
limit before training on the loss function.

Having established the loss of interest, the training procedure for both variants of the CA model will simply be to run gradient flow on the limiting objective
\ell
. That is, the model parameters evolve according to the ordinary differential equation

\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!}{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!t}\theta_{t}=-\nabla\ell(\theta_{t}),

where
(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the trajectory of the parameters over the training horizon. Gradient flow can be seen as the limit of gradient descent in vanishing step size. Analyzing optimization in the gradient flow limit has enabled precise characterization of deep neural network training dynamics
(
37
;
11
)
; this approach has been leveraged in previous studies on ICL for transformers
(
49
;
51
)
.

6.2
Main Results

Recall the stacked modality-dependent vector
\bm{m}
from (
3.2
). We make the following assumption on the distribution of
\bm{m}
across prompts.

Assumption 6.1

(Support of
\|\bm{m}\|
)
.

Assume
\bm{m}
has a continuous distribution and

\underline{m}:={\rm ess}\inf\|\bm{m}\|^{2}<{\rm ess}\sup\|\bm{m}\|^{2}=:\overline{m}<\infty

so that
\|\bm{m}\|
is almost-surely bounded and non-degenerate.

The boundedness assumption on
\bm{m}
is mild. For example, it holds as soon as the covariates are normalized, for instance
\bm{x}_{i}\in[0,1]^{d}
. The density assumption is included for technical reasons; it simplifies the proofs of our main results. We do not believe this assumption is intrinsically necessary for the results described below.

We now present our main results. We show that a deep model of CA layers learns the Bayes-optimal prediction rule in-context for this multi-modal problem.

Theorem 6.2

(One-parameter model optimality)
.

Run gradient flow on the loss
\ell(\alpha)
for the one-parameter model.
Under the data generating process in Section
3
and Assumption
6.1
, we have:

1.

Gradient flow converges. Formally, letting
(\alpha_{t,T})_{t\geq 0}
denote the gradient descent trajectory for a CA stack of depth
T
, the limit

\alpha^{\ast}_{T}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\alpha_{t,T}
exists.

2.

As
T\to\infty
,

\alpha^{\ast}_{T}\to\frac{2}{2+\underline{m}+\overline{m}}=:\alpha^{\ast}.

3.

The resulting predictor is Bayes optimal as the depth diverges. Formally,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\lim_{L_{\rm te}\to\infty}\hat{y}_{q}=\langle\bm{w},\bm{x}_{q}\rangle

almost surely.

We prove this result in Appendix
D
. We sketch the proof ideas below.

Proof sketch.

The one-parameter loss assumes the compact form

\ell(\alpha)={\mathbb{E}}[\|\bm{m}\|^{2}(1-\alpha(\|\bm{m}\|^{2}+1))^{2T}].

The strict convexity and coercivity of this loss imply convergence of gradient flow using well-established theory
(
17
)
.
Subsequently, taking the
(\cdot)^{1/2T}
-th root of the loss, one shows epigraphical convergence
(
36
)
as
T\to\infty
to the limit

\phi(\alpha)=\max\{|1-\alpha(\underline{m}+1)|,|1-\alpha(\overline{m}+1)|\}.

This yields convergence of the sequence of minimizers
(\alpha^{\ast}_{T})_{T\geq 1}
to the unique minimizer of
\phi
which is merely
\alpha^{\ast}
. Bayes optimality follows by solving the recurrence
5.1
to find that

\lim_{L_{\rm te}\to\infty}\hat{y}_{q}=\langle\bm{w},\bm{x}_{q}\rangle+e(\alpha)

(6.2)

with error
e(\alpha)=O(\|\bm{I}-\alpha\bm{\Lambda}\|^{T})
where
\|\bm{I}-\alpha\bm{\Lambda}\|<1
at
\alpha=\alpha^{\ast}
and hence
e(\alpha^{\ast})\to 0
as
T\to\infty
.
∎

We now turn to the two-parameter setting in the following result which requires additional technical assumptions.

Theorem 6.3

(Two-parameter model optimality)
.

Under the data generating process in Section
3
and Assumption
6.1
, run gradient flow on the loss
\ell(\alpha,\beta)
for the two-parameter model with initialization

\beta_{0}\in(-2/(\overline{m}+1),0)\quad{\rm and}\quad\alpha_{0}=\alpha^{\ast}(\beta_{0})

(6.3)

where
\beta\mapsto\alpha^{\ast}(\beta)
is defined in
E.1
(Appendix
E
). Then, we have the following:

1.

Gradient flow converges. Formally, letting
(\alpha_{t,T},\beta_{t,T})_{t\geq 0}
denote the gradient descent trajectory for a CA stack of depth
T
, the limit

(\alpha^{\ast}_{T},\beta^{\ast}_{T}):=\lim_{t\to\infty}(\alpha_{t,T},\beta_{t,T})
exists.

2.

Suppose
\lim_{T\to\infty}\beta^{\ast}_{T}\notin\{-2/(\overline{m}+1),0\}
. Then, with
\alpha^{\ast}
as defined in Theorem
6.2
, as
T\to\infty
,

\ \alpha^{\ast}_{T}\to\alpha^{\ast}

and

\beta^{\ast}_{T}\to-\alpha^{\ast}.

3.

As in Theorem
6.2
, the resulting predictor is Bayes optimal as the depth grows. Formally,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\lim_{L_{\rm te}\to\infty}\hat{y}_{q}=\langle\bm{w},\bm{x}_{q}\rangle

almost surely.

As before, we include a brief sketch of the proof of Theorem
6.3
. This will also motivate the choice of the initialization in the theorem above.

Proof sketch.

The two-parameter loss has the explicit expression

\ell(\alpha,\beta)={\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bm{m}\|^{2}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}[(1+\beta(\|\bm{m}\|^{2}+1))^{T}-1]-1\right)^{2}\right].

(6.4)

This loss is non-convex and non-coercive; however, it is quadratic in
\alpha
for fixed
\beta
with profiled minimizer
\alpha^{\ast}(\beta)=\argmin_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}}\ell(\alpha,\beta)
. By using this structure, we first show that
(\alpha_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(at fixed depth
T
) is bounded.
Then, establishing that

\ell(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})<\inf_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}}\min\{\ell(\alpha,-2/(\overline{m}+1)),\ell(\alpha,0)\}

and given gradient flow is characteristically non-increasing on the loss
\ell(\alpha,\beta)
, it follows that
(\beta_{t})_{t\geq 0}\subset(-2/(\overline{m}+1),0)
—namely the entire gradient flow trajectory
(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
remains bounded. Starting with this a priori boundedness, we establish convergence of gradient flow at fixed
T
. To determine the limiting behavior as
T\to\infty
, one takes a log-transformation of
\beta\mapsto\ell(\alpha^{\ast}(\beta),\beta)
and finds that its derivative remains nonzero for sufficiently large
T
if
\lim_{T\to\infty}\beta^{\ast}_{T}\neq-\alpha^{\ast}
. This contradicts
(\alpha^{\ast}_{T},\beta^{\ast}_{T})_{T\geq 1}
being a sequence of stationary points. Convergence of
\alpha^{\ast}_{T}\to\alpha^{\ast}
as
T\to\infty
follows by examining
\lim_{T\to\infty}\alpha^{\ast}(\beta_{T})
as necessarily
\alpha^{\ast}_{T}=\alpha^{\ast}(\beta_{T})
. Finally, Bayes optimality is delivered via the same argument as in Theorem
6.2
.
∎

The proof of the above result is considerably more intricate than that of Theorem

and can be found in Appendix

6.3
shows that the limiting behavior of the two-parameter model mimics that of the one-parameter setting, whereby in both cases one achieves the Bayes-optimal predictor.

A careful reader may have noticed how the proof sketches above help inform the choice of initialization (

is quadratic in

6.3
The limiting parameter
\alpha^{\ast}

Thus far, we have only mentioned that the limiting parameter
\alpha^{\ast}=2/(2+\underline{m}+\overline{m})
suffices for Bayes optimality—which follows from (
6.2
) and (
6.5
)—but have not commented on its particular form. As remarked above, for the one-parameter model, one learns the ICL task at asymptotic depth if
\|\bm{I}-\alpha\bm{\Lambda}\|<1
with vanishing error
e(\alpha)
. Recalling
\bm{\Lambda}=\bm{I}+\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\top}
, along the spike direction, the absolute value of the largest attainable eigenvalue is

\phi(\alpha):=\max\{|1-\alpha(1+\underline{m})|,|1-\alpha(1+\overline{m})|\}

and we note
\alpha^{\ast}=\argmin_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}}\phi(\alpha)
. Interestingly, the limiting parameter
\alpha^{\ast}
coincides with the parametrization minimizing the worst-case error rate given by the spike contribution to
e(\alpha)
. Hence, Theorems
6.2
and
6.3
show that the CA models are in a sense minimax optimal in the large
T
and
L_{\rm te}
limits.

6.4
The role of depth

Consider the embedding
\bm{F}=\mathsf{LCA}(\bm{X};\theta)
produced by the
T
-layer CA stack, and let
\{\bm{f}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L_{\rm te}}
denote its (non-query) columns corresponding to the context tokens.
Under mild conditions (and ignoring lower-order terms that vanish as
L_{\mathrm{te}}\to\infty
), the final prediction produced by the LSA readout has the schematic form

\hat{y}_{q}\approx\bm{w}^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{L_{\rm te}}\sum_{i=1}^{L_{\rm te}}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{f}_{i}^{\top}\right)\bm{x}_{q}.

as shown in Appendix
C
(Lemma
C.1
). Thus, if the CA stack can enforce

\frac{1}{L_{\rm te}}\sum_{i=1}^{L_{\rm te}}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{f}_{i}^{\top}\longrightarrow\bm{I}_{d}\quad\text{as }T\to\infty,

(6.6)

then
\hat{y}_{q}\to\langle\bm{w},\bm{x}_{q}\rangle
as both
T\to\infty
and
L_{\mathrm{te}}\to\infty
, yielding Bayes-optimal ICL. An instance where (
6.6
) can occur is if

\bm{F}\approx\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}

(6.7)

—that is, the embedding
\bm{F}
whitens the covariates. Crucially, since the embedding
\bm{F}
is prompt-dependent, (
6.7
) is achievable even though
\bm{\Lambda}
is random: the model is not constrained to learning a single global inverse covariance, instead it can compute
\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}
implicitly from the prompt itself. Here lies the significance of the CA embedding as a whitening (denoising) mechanism for the covariates.

We note that in sharp contrast to the mechanism above, a single LSA layer can only learn an average covariance, and thus fails to attain Bayes-optimal performance in our setup.

8
Discussion

We studied a multi-modal task in which each prompt is generated by a latent factor model, inducing prompt-dependent covariate statistics which places the setting outside of standard unimodal ICL analyses. To elucidate this added complexity, we first demonstrated the failure of single-layer LSA models standard in the literature. Motivated by this limitation, we introduced a novel transformer-like architecture, incorporating both depth and CA, proving not only its expressibility for the ICL task, but also demonstrating its capacity to learn the Bayes-optimal predictor via gradient flow.

To our knowledge, our work is the first to tackle a multi-modal ICL task in a mathematically tractable setting and provably overcome covariate distribution shifts among prompts. Continuing on the theoretical side, a natural future direction will include exploring models where the covariance structure contains more than one spike—that is, generalizations of (
3.4
). Additionally, it will be of interest to study if the learnable weights can be varied over larger parameter classes, and whether a similar collapse as seen for the two-parameter to one-parameter case is observed for more complex settings.

Toward applications, the CA architecture proposed in (
5.1
) is novel and should be tested on real-world, multi-modal data without the linearization and with all the conventional sophistications of modern large-language models.

Lastly, while we analyzed the population loss for our CA architecture, it would be significant to extend this to sample-level results, as done earlier for unimodal problems
(
28
)
. In addition, extending to infinite token dimensions, in the spirit of
(
28
;
23
)
, would also constitute a crucial direction for future research.
These directions pose intriguing mathematical challenges, in particular, requiring potential new developments in random matrix theory, which we view as an exciting follow-up to the present paper.

A.2
Joint Gaussian Form and Bayes-predictor

Recall the data generating process (Section
3
): for
i\in[L]\cup\{q\}
,

\bm{x}_{i}=u_{i}\bm{m}+\bm{\mu}_{i},\qquad y_{i}=\zeta u_{i},

with
u_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
\bm{\mu}_{i}=[\bm{\xi}_{i},\bm{\eta}_{i}]\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
independent across tokens
i
and independent of
(\bm{m},\zeta)
.

Lemma A.1

(Joint covariance and Bayes coefficient)
.

Conditional on
(\bm{m},\zeta)
, the pair
(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
is jointly Gaussian with mean
0
and covariance

\bm{\Sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Lambda}&\zeta\bm{m}\\
\zeta\bm{m}^{\top}&\zeta^{2}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\bm{\Lambda}=\bm{I}+\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\top}.

Moreover,

{\mathbb{E}}[y_{i}\mid\bm{m},\zeta,\bm{x}_{i}]=\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i},\qquad\bm{w}=\frac{\zeta}{1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2}}\bm{m},

and we may write

y_{i}=\langle\bm{w},\bm{x}_{i}\rangle+\varepsilon_{i},\quad\varepsilon_{i}\,\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{x}_{i}\mid(\bm{m},\zeta),\quad\varepsilon_{i}\sim{\cal N}\left(0,\frac{\zeta^{2}}{1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2}}\right).

Proof.

We compute conditional covariances given
(m,\zeta)
:

\bm{\Sigma}_{x,x}:={\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\mid\bm{m},\zeta]={\mathbb{E}}[(u_{i}\bm{m}+\bm{\mu}_{i})(u_{i}\bm{m}+\bm{\mu}_{i})^{\top}]=\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\top}+\bm{I}=\bm{\Lambda},

\bm{\Sigma}_{x,y}:={\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}_{i}y_{i}\mid\bm{m},\zeta]={\mathbb{E}}[(u_{i}\bm{m}+\bm{\mu}_{i})\cdot\zeta u_{i}]=\zeta\,{\mathbb{E}}[u_{i}^{2}]\,\bm{m}=\zeta\bm{m},

\bm{\Sigma}_{y,y}:={\mathbb{E}}[y_{i}^{2}\mid\bm{m},\zeta]=\zeta^{2}{\mathbb{E}}[u_{i}^{2}]=\zeta^{2},

yielding the stated
\bm{\Sigma}
. For a jointly Gaussian vector, the conditional mean is

{\mathbb{E}}[y_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i},\bm{m},\zeta]=\bm{\Sigma}_{x,y}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{x,x}^{-1}\bm{x}_{i}=(\zeta\bm{m})^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\bm{x}_{i}

and so
\bm{w}=\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}(\zeta\bm{m})
. Using the Sherman–Morrison formula,

\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}=(\bm{I}+\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\top})^{-1}=\bm{I}-\frac{\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\top}}{1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2}}.

and hence,

w=\left(\bm{I}-\frac{\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\top}}{1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2}}\right)\zeta\bm{m}=\zeta\bm{m}-\zeta\frac{\|\bm{m}\|^{2}}{1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2}}m=\frac{\zeta}{1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2}}\bm{m}.

Finally, the conditional variance of
y_{i}
given
\bm{x}_{i}
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(y_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i},\bm{m},\zeta)

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{y,y}-\bm{\Sigma}_{x,y}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{x,x}\bm{\Sigma}_{x,y}

\displaystyle=\zeta^{2}-(\zeta\bm{m})^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}(\zeta\bm{m})

\displaystyle=\zeta^{2}\left(1-\|\bm{m}\|^{2}/(1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\zeta^{2}/(1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2}).

The residual
\varepsilon_{i}:=y_{i}-\langle\bm{w},\bm{x}_{i}\rangle
is independent of
\bm{x}_{i}
conditional on
(\bm{m},\zeta)
by Gaussianity.
∎

Let
\bm{X}=[\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{L},\bm{x}_{q}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(L+1)}

.

Standard application of the Law of Large Numbers.

[\bm{E}_{\bm{X}}^{\top}\bm{W}^{KQ}\bm{E}_{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{{L_{\rm te}}+1}]_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{i}\\
y_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}&\bm{b}^{\prime}\\
\bm{b}\top&c\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{q}\\
0\end{bmatrix}=\left(\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{x}_{q}+y_{i}\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{x}_{q}\right).

Moreover, the
({L_{\rm te}}+1)
-th entry of
\tfrac{1}{{L_{\rm te}}}\bm{E}_{\bm{X}}^{\top}\bm{W}^{KQ}\bm{E}_{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{{L_{\rm te}}+1}
is of order
O(\tfrac{1}{{L_{\rm te}}})
and thus negligible as
{L_{\rm te}}\to\infty
. Hence, we obtain

\hat{y}_{q}=\frac{1}{{L_{\rm te}}}\sum_{i=1}^{L_{\rm te}}\left(\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}+vy_{i}\right)\left(\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{A}+y_{i}\bm{b}^{\top}\right)\bm{x}_{q}+o(1).

(B.3)

Conditioned on
(\bm{m},\zeta)
, the context tokens
\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{L_{\rm te}}
are i.i.d., and
\bm{x}_{q}
is independent of
\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{L_{\rm te}}
with the same conditional law as
\bm{x}_{i}
. Applying Lemma
A.2
to each empirical moment that appears when expanding (
B.3
) yields, almost surely (for fixed
(\bm{m},\zeta,\bm{x}_{q})
),

\hat{y}_{q}\longrightarrow\langle\hat{\bm{w}},\bm{x}_{q}\rangle,

as
{L_{\rm te}}\to\infty
where the limiting regression vector
\hat{\bm{w}}=\hat{\bm{w}}(\bm{m},\zeta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is

\hat{\bm{w}}(\bm{m},\zeta)^{\top}=\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{A}+(\bm{m}\bm{u}\bm{b}^{\top}+v\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{A})\zeta+v\bm{b}^{\top}\zeta^{2}.

(B.4)

We now show that
{\mathbb{P}}(\hat{\bm{w}}(\bm{m},\zeta)=\bm{w}(\bm{m},\zeta))=0
. We begin by assuming, for contradiction, that indeed
\langle\hat{\bm{w}},\bm{x}_{q}\rangle=\langle\bm{w},\bm{x}_{q}\rangle
and then examine the measure of this event. Since
\bm{x}_{q}\mid(\bm{m},\zeta)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{\Lambda})
, we must have
\hat{\bm{w}}(\bm{m},\zeta)=\bm{w}(\bm{m},\zeta)
. Note that the Bayes vector
\bm{w}
is linear in
\zeta
, while, in contrast,
\hat{\bm{w}}(\bm{m},\zeta)
in (
B.4
) is a polynomial in
\zeta
of degree of degree two and so the
\zeta^{2}
-coefficient must vanish:
v\bm{b}=\bm{0}_{d}
. Similarly, since
\bm{w}
has no constant (
\zeta^{0}
) term, we must have
\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{u}=\bm{0}_{d}
. It then follows that

\hat{\bm{w}}(\bm{m},\zeta)=\bm{B}\bm{m}\zeta

(B.5)

for the fixed matrix
\bm{B}=\bm{u}\bm{b}^{\top}+v\bm{A}
. Recalling that

\bm{w}=\frac{\zeta}{1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2}}\bm{m},

by (
B.5
), it follows that
(1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2})^{-1}
is an eigenvalue of
B
. Since
\|\bm{m}\|
is atomless while the spectrum of
B
is finite, this cannot occur—explicitly,

\sup_{B\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{1}{1+\|m\|^{2}}\in\sigma(B)\right)=0

where
\sigma(B)
denotes the spectrum of
B
. Hence,

\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\bm{w}}(\bm{m},\zeta)=\bm{w}(\bm{m},\zeta)\right)=0

(B.6)

which establishes the desired result.

∎

Appendix C
CA Algebra and Preliminaries

This appendix collects algebraic identities for the CLA stack and the final LSA readout.
These identities are used in the proofs of Theorems
6.2
and
6.3
.

C.1
LSA Readout with Frozen Parameters

Recall the (fixed) readout parameters from Section
5.1
:

\bm{W}^{PV}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}_{d\times d}&\bm{0}_{d}\\
\bm{0}_{d}^{\top}&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad\bm{W}^{KQ}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{d}&\bm{0}_{d}\\
\bm{0}_{d}^{\top}&0\end{bmatrix}.

Lemma C.1

(Closed-form readout)
.

With the above
\bm{W}^{PV},\bm{W}^{KQ}
, the single-layer LSA readout satisfies

\widehat{y}_{q}=\mathrm{LSA}(\bm{E}_{\bm{F}})_{d+1,L+1}=\bm{w}^{\top}\left[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{f}_{i}^{\top}\right]\bm{x}_{q}+\left[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\varepsilon_{i}\bm{f}_{i}^{\top}\right]\bm{x}_{q}.

and

\left[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\varepsilon_{i}\bm{f}_{i}^{\top}\right]\bm{x}_{q}\to 0

almost surely as
L\to\infty
if
\sup_{i\geq 1}\|\bm{f}_{i}\|<\infty
.

Proof.

By definition,

\mathsf{LSA}(\bm{E}_{\bm{F}})=\bm{E}_{\bm{F}}+\bm{W}^{PV}\bm{E}_{\bm{F}}\cdot\frac{\bm{E}_{\bm{F}}^{\top}\bm{W}^{KQ}\bm{E}_{\bm{F}}}{L}.

Since
(\bm{E}_{\bm{F}})_{d+1,L+1}=0
, given the definitions of
\bm{W}^{PV}
and
\bm{W}^{KQ}
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{q}

\displaystyle=\bm{e}_{d+1}^{\top}\left(\bm{E}_{\bm{F}}+\bm{E}_{\bm{F}}\cdot\frac{\bm{E}_{\bm{F}}^{\top}\bm{E}_{\bm{F}}}{L}\right)\bm{e}_{L+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{e}_{d+1}^{\top}\frac{\bm{E}_{\bm{F}}\bm{E}_{\bm{F}}^{\top}}{L}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{q}\\
0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\bm{e}_{d+1}^{\top}\frac{1}{L}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{F}\bm{F}^{\top}&\bm{F}\bm{y}\\
\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{F}^{\top}&\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{y}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{q}\\
0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{F}^{\top}\bm{x}_{q}}{L}

\displaystyle=\bm{w}^{\top}\left[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{f}_{i}^{\top}\right]\bm{x}_{q}+\left[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\varepsilon_{i}\bm{f}_{i}^{\top}\right]\bm{x}_{q}.

Moreover, since
{\mathbb{E}}[\varepsilon_{i}]=0
and
\varepsilon_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{f}_{i}
as
\bm{F}
depends only on
\bm{X}
, by the Strong Law of Large Numbers, we have

\left[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\varepsilon_{i}\bm{f}_{i}^{\top}\right]\bm{x}_{q}\to 0

almost surely as
L\to\infty
.

∎

Recall now that
\bm{F}
and hence
\{\bm{f}_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
depend on the model depth
T
. From Lemma
C.1
, we see that if

\frac{1}{L}\bm{X}\bm{F}^{\top}\to\bm{I}_{d}

as
T\to\infty
, the asymptotic model predictor will be

\lim_{T\to\infty}\lim_{L\to\infty}\hat{y}_{q}=\langle\bm{w},\bm{x}_{q}\rangle,

hence yielding the Bayes-optimal prediction. This will be the key step is establishing Theorems
6.2
(3) and
6.3
(3).

C.2
Solving the CA Recurrence

In the one- and two-scalar simplification (Section
5.1
), we take (for all layers)

\bm{W}^{K}=\bm{W}^{Q}=\bm{I}_{d},\qquad\bm{W}^{S}=\alpha\bm{I}_{d},\qquad\bm{W}^{V}=\beta\bm{I}_{d},

where
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
are scalar parameters (with the one-parameter model corresponding to
\beta=-\alpha
). Layer-wise, the model then becomes, for
t=1,\dots,T
,

\bm{F}_{t}=\bm{F}_{t-1}+\alpha\bm{X}+\frac{\beta}{L}\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{F}_{t-1},

with
\bm{F}_{0}=\bm{0}_{d\times(L+1)}
and
\bm{F}=\bm{F}_{T}
. Define the sample covariance
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}:=\frac{1}{L}\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}
. The following result yields a closed-form expression for the final output of the CA stack and establishes the parameter window for which one obtains the Bayes-predictor.

Lemma C.2
.

The output of the final layer of the CA network is

\bm{F}=\bm{F}_{T}=\alpha\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\bm{M}^{k}\bm{X}\quad{\rm where}\quad\bm{M}=\bm{I}+\beta\hat{\bm{\Lambda}}.

(C.1)

and

\frac{1}{L}\bm{X}\bm{F}^{\top}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\left[(\bm{I}+\beta\hat{\bm{\Lambda}})^{T}-\bm{I}\right]

(C.2)

If
\alpha=-\beta\in(0,2/(1+\overline{m}))
, then as context length
L\to\infty
followed by the number of layers
T\to\infty
, we have

\frac{1}{L}\bm{X}\bm{F}^{\top}\to\bm{I}.

almost surely.

Proof.

We prove the first statement by induction. For the base case
T=1
, we have trivially
\bm{F}_{1}=\alpha\bm{X}
since
\bm{F}_{0}=\bm{0}_{d\times(L+1)}
. Now, suppose
\bm{F}_{T-1}=\alpha\sum_{k=0}^{T-2}\bm{M}^{k}\bm{X}
. Then,

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{T}

\displaystyle=\bm{F}_{T-1}+\alpha\bm{X}+\beta\hat{\bm{\Lambda}}\bm{F}_{T-1}

\displaystyle=\alpha\bm{X}+(\bm{I}+\beta\hat{\bm{\Lambda}})\bm{F}_{T-1}

\displaystyle=\alpha\bm{X}+\alpha\bm{M}\sum_{k=0}^{T-2}\bm{M}^{k}\bm{X}

\displaystyle=\alpha\bm{X}+\alpha\sum_{k=1}^{T-1}\bm{M}^{k}\bm{X}=\alpha\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\bm{M}^{k}\bm{X}

which completes the induction. As
\bm{M}
is symmetric and
\hat{\bm{\Lambda}}=\beta^{-1}(\bm{M}-\bm{I})
, a simple computation then yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\bm{X}\bm{F}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\alpha\hat{\bm{\Lambda}}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\bm{M}^{k}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}(\bm{M}^{k+1}-\bm{M}^{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}(\bm{M}^{T}-\bm{I})=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\left[(\bm{I}+\beta\hat{\bm{\Lambda}})^{T}-\bm{I}\right].

Now, if
\alpha=-\beta
, we have

\frac{1}{L}\bm{X}\bm{F}^{\top}=\bm{I}-(\bm{I}-\alpha\hat{\bm{\Lambda}})^{T}\to\bm{I}-(\bm{I}-\alpha\bm{\Lambda})^{T}

as
L\to\infty
by the Law of Large Numbers. As
\bm{\Lambda}=\bm{I}+\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\top}
, the eigenvalues of
\bm{I}-\alpha\bm{\Lambda}
are
1-\alpha
and
1-\alpha(1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2})
. Hence, for the choice of
\alpha\in(0,2/(1+\overline{m})
, it follows that the operator norm
\|\bm{I}-\alpha\bm{\Lambda}\|<1
almost surely and so
(\bm{I}-\alpha\bm{\Lambda})^{T}\to\bm{0}_{d\times d}
as
T\to\infty
almost surely.
∎

Remark C.3
.

From the proof of Lemma
C.2
, we see that at asymptotic context length the error away from the Bayes-optimal prediction decays geometrically in depth
T
so long as the model parameters satisfy
\alpha=-\beta\in(0,2/(1+\overline{m}))
. Hence, although the result concerns the behavior at infinite depth, in practice small to moderate depth networks should suffice for accurate in-context predictions. This intuition is supported by numerical experiments in Section
7
.

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
6.2

This appendix proves Theorem
6.2
for the one-parameter model, i.e. the simplified CA stack with
\beta=-\alpha
trained by gradient flow on the limiting population loss. Throughout, we assume Assumption
6.1
:
\|\bm{m}\|^{2}
is almost surely bounded and has non-degenerate continuous support. Define

Z:=1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2},\qquad\underline{Z}:=\mathrm{ess}\inf\,Z,\qquad\overline{Z}:=\mathrm{ess}\sup\,Z,

so
1\leq\underline{Z}<\overline{Z}<\infty
. We begin by determining a simplified form for the loss
\ell(\alpha)
and collecting some of its properties.

Lemma D.1

(One-parameter loss)
.

The loss
\ell(\alpha)
has the form

\ell(\alpha)={\mathbb{E}}[(Z-1)(1-\alpha Z)^{2T}]

where
Z=1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2}
. Moreover, the loss has the following properties:

1.

\ell(\alpha)
is strictly-convex and coercive in the sense that

\lim_{|\alpha|\to\infty}\ell(\alpha)=\infty;

2.

\ell(\alpha)
has a unique minimizer which lies between
(0,1)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
C.2
, the model output can be written as

\hat{y}_{q}=\bm{w}^{\top}(\bm{I}-\bm{M}^{T})\bm{x}_{q}+\frac{1}{L}\bm{\varepsilon}^{\top}\bm{F}^{\top}\bm{x}_{q}=\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{x}_{q}-\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{M}^{T}\bm{x}_{q}+\frac{1}{L}\bm{\varepsilon}^{\top}\bm{F}^{\top}\bm{x}_{q}.

where
\bm{M}=\bm{I}-\alpha\hat{\bm{\Lambda}}
and
\hat{\bm{\Lambda}}
is the sample covariance matrix with the true covariance being
\bm{\Lambda}=\bm{I}+\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\top}
.

We begin by conditioning on
\bm{\Lambda}
so that
\bm{x}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\cal N}(0,\bm{\Lambda})
and
\bm{w}\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{X},\bm{x}_{q}
. Then, set
\sigma^{2}:={\mathbb{E}}[\varepsilon_{i}^{2}]
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{w}:={\rm Cov}(\bm{w})
. Notice that

y_{q}-\hat{y}_{q}=\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{M}^{T}\bm{x}_{q}+\varepsilon_{q}-\frac{1}{L}\bm{\varepsilon}^{\top}\bm{F}^{\top}\bm{x}_{q}.

Let
\ell(\alpha|\bm{\Lambda})
denote the loss
\ell(\alpha)
where one conditions on
\bm{\Lambda}
. We have, suppressing terms that do not depend on
\alpha
,

\displaystyle\ell(\alpha|\bm{\Lambda})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[(\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{M}^{T}\bm{x}_{q})^{2}|\bm{\Lambda}]+\frac{1}{L^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}[(\bm{\varepsilon}^{\top}\bm{F}^{\top}\bm{x}_{q})^{2}|\bm{\Lambda}]+{\mathbb{E}}[\varepsilon_{q}^{2}|\bm{\Lambda}]

\displaystyle\propto{\mathbb{E}}[\tr(\bm{\Sigma}_{w}\bm{M}^{T}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{M}^{T})|\bm{\Lambda}]+\frac{1}{L^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}[\sigma^{2}\tr(\bm{\Lambda}\bm{F}\bm{F}^{\top})|\bm{\Lambda}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[\tr(\bm{\Sigma}_{w}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{M}^{2T})|\bm{\Lambda}]+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{L}{\mathbb{E}}[\sigma^{2}\tr(\bm{\Lambda}(\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\bm{M}^{k}(\bm{I}-\bm{M}^{T})))|\bm{\Lambda}]

where we neglect
{\mathbb{E}}[\varepsilon_{q}^{2}|\bm{\Lambda}]
as it does not depend on
\alpha
. In the first line above, the cross-terms do not appear by the independence of
\bm{\varepsilon},\varepsilon_{q}\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{w},\bm{X},\bm{x}_{q}
and given
{\mathbb{E}}[\varepsilon_{i}]=0
. As the training context length grows, we have

\ell(\alpha|\bm{\Lambda}):=\lim_{L\to\infty}\ell(\alpha|\bm{\Lambda})={\mathbb{E}}[\tr(\bm{\Sigma}_{w}\bm{\Lambda}(\bm{I}-\alpha\bm{\Lambda})^{2T})|\bm{\Lambda}]

as

\frac{\alpha^{2}}{L}{\mathbb{E}}[\sigma^{2}\tr(\bm{\Lambda}(\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\bm{M}^{k}(\bm{I}-\bm{M}^{T})))|\bm{\Lambda}]\to 0\quad{\rm as}\quad L\to\infty

since
\bm{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
and we are holding the embedding dimension
d
fixed.
Let us write the eigen-decomposition
\bm{\Lambda}=\bm{U}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{U}^{\top}
where
\bm{U}
is an orthogonal matrix and
\bm{\Gamma}=\diag(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{d})
with
\gamma_{i}\geq 0
the eigenvalues of
\bm{\Lambda}
. Writing
\bm{V}=\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{w}\bm{U}
which is positive semi-definite, we have

\displaystyle\ell(\alpha|\bm{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{ii}\gamma_{i}(1-\alpha\gamma_{i})^{2T}

where
v_{ii}\geq 0
for all
i\in[d]
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
v_{ii}
and
\gamma_{i}
are non-increasing in
i\in[d]
. Letting
\gamma_{\max}=\gamma_{1}
denote the largest eigenvalue of
\bm{\Lambda}
, we have that
\gamma_{\max}=1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2}=Z
and
\gamma_{i}=1
for
i\geq 2
. Since
{\mathbb{E}}[\zeta^{2}]=1
, we have

\bm{\Sigma}_{w}=\frac{\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\top}}{1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2}}=\bm{I}-\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}

and so
v_{ii}=1-1/\gamma_{i}
. Thus
v_{11}=\|\bm{m}\|^{2}/(\|\bm{m}\|^{2}+1)
and
v_{ii}=0
for
i\geq 2
. The conditional loss then simplifies to

\ell(\alpha|\bm{\Lambda})=\|\bm{m}\|^{2}(1-\alpha(1+\|\bm{m}\|^{2}))^{2T}=(Z-1)(1-\alpha Z)^{2T}.

and so the unconditional loss is given by

\ell(\alpha)={\mathbb{E}}[(Z-1)(1-\alpha Z)^{2T}]

as claimed.

1.

Since
(Z-1)(1-\alpha Z)^{2T}
is strongly convex in
\alpha
for
Z>0
and
{\mathbb{P}}(Z>0)>0
by assumption
6.1
, it follows that
\ell(\alpha)
is strongly convex. Similarly, coercivity of
(Z-1)(1-\alpha Z)^{2T}
carries over to
\ell(\alpha)
by simply taking the expectation.

2.

By (1),
\ell(\alpha)
has a unique minimizer. The first derivative of the loss is given by

\ell^{\prime}_{\infty}(\alpha)=-2T{\mathbb{E}}[\gamma_{\max}(Z-1)(1-\alpha Z)^{2T-1}].

Hence,

{\rm sign}(\ell^{\prime}_{\infty}(\alpha))=\begin{cases}-1,&{\rm if}\;\alpha\leq 0\\
1,&{\rm if}\;\alpha\geq 1\\
\end{cases}

(D.1)

since
(1-\alpha/Z)^{2T-1}\leq 0
when
\alpha\geq 1
because
Z\geq 1
. Thus, the unique root of
\ell^{\prime}_{\infty}(\alpha)
is contained in
(0,1)
.

∎

D.1
Convergence of Gradient Flow

Given the above established properties of
\ell(\alpha)
, by standard dynamical systems theory results, we can derive the global convergence of gradient flow on the one-parameter loss. We include a detailed proof to initiate the unfamiliar reader.

Lemma D.2

(One-parameter gradient flow convergence)
.

Suppose Assumption
6.1
holds. Then, gradient flow on
\ell(\alpha)
converges globally to the unique minimum of
\ell
whose existence is guaranteed by Lemma
D.1
.

Proof.

Let
(\alpha_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the gradient flow trajectory initialized at
\alpha_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and let
\alpha^{\ast}
denote the unique minimizer of
\ell(\alpha)
. By chain rule and the definition of gradient flow, we have

\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!}{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!t}\ell(\alpha_{t})=\nabla_{\alpha}\ell(\alpha_{t})\dot{\alpha}_{t}=-\|\nabla_{\alpha}\ell(\alpha_{t})\|^{2}\leq 0.

Hence, the trajectory of the loss function
\ell(\alpha_{t})
is decreasing and since
\ell(\alpha)\geq 0
, it follows that
\ell(\alpha_{t})
converges as
t\to\infty
. Moreover, by coercivity of
\ell
,
\alpha_{t}\in\{\alpha:L(\alpha)\leq\ell(\alpha_{0})\}
for all
t
which is compact. Thus, as
\ell\in C^{\infty}
,
\ell^{\prime}(\alpha_{t})
is uniformly continuous for all
t\geq 0
. Then, by Barbalat’s Lemma
[
40
]
,
\ell^{\prime}(\alpha_{t})\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. As
\alpha_{t}\in\{\alpha:L(\alpha)\leq L(\alpha_{0})\}
which is compact, moving to a convergent subsequence,
\alpha_{t_{k}}\to\bar{\alpha}
as
k\to\infty
. By continuity of
\ell^{\prime}
,
\ell^{\prime}(\alpha_{t_{k}})\to\ell^{\prime}(\bar{\alpha})=0
. Hence,
\bar{\alpha}=\alpha^{\ast}
which is the unique minimizer of
\ell
. Hence, as every subsequence of
(\alpha_{t})_{t\geq 1}
converges to
\alpha^{\ast}
, it follows that
\alpha_{t}\to\alpha^{\ast}
as
t\to\infty
.
∎

D.2
Asymptotic Depth Behavior

Having thus far established the convergence of gradient flow at finite depth
T
, we now complete the proof of Theorem
6.2
with the following result result which shows that as
T\to\infty
, we recover the optimal prediction of the model. Formally, let
\alpha^{\ast}_{T}
denote the limit of the gradient flow on the
T
-layer model which, from Lemma
D.2
, exists and coincides with the global minimum of
\ell(\alpha)
.

Theorem D.3
.

Suppose Assumption
6.1
holds. Let
\alpha^{\ast}_{T}
denote the global minimum of
\ell(\alpha)
for the
T
-layer CA model which exists uniquely by Lemma
D.1
. Then,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\alpha^{\ast}_{T}=:\alpha^{\ast}_{\infty}=\frac{2}{2+\underline{m}+\overline{m}}=\frac{2}{\underline{z}+\overline{z}}.

Hence, the model is Bayes optimal by Lemmas
C.1
and
C.2
.

Proof.

Consider for
\alpha\in[0,1]
, the function

\phi(\alpha):=\max_{z\in[\underline{z},\overline{z}]}|1-\alpha z|=\max\{|1-\alpha\underline{z}|,|1-\alpha\overline{z}|\}.

As the composition of a convex function with an affine function,
z\mapsto|1-\alpha z|
is convex. On any interval, the maximum of a convex function occurs at the interval’s endpoints and so

\phi(\alpha)=\max\{|1-\alpha\underline{z}|,|1-\alpha\overline{z}|\}.

Note that
\phi(\alpha)
is convex as the maximum of convex functions. The minimum of
\phi(\alpha)
occurs when
|1-\alpha\underline{z}|=|1-\alpha\overline{z}|
. Since
m<\overline{Z}
, the balancing occurs at opposite signs
1-\alpha\underline{z}=-(1-\alpha\overline{z})
which yields a unique minimizer

\alpha^{\ast}_{\infty}=\frac{2}{\underline{z}+\overline{z}}.

Now, let

f_{T}(\alpha):={\mathbb{E}}[(Z-1)(1-\alpha Z)^{2T}]^{1/{2T}}.

We use a shorthand
W=Z-1
and
X_{\alpha}=(1-\alpha Z)
so that

f_{T}(\alpha)={\mathbb{E}}[WX_{\alpha}^{2T}]^{1/{2T}}.

Since
X_{\alpha}\leq\phi(\alpha)
and
{\mathbb{P}}(W>0)>0
by the assumption that
\overline{z}>\underline{z}\geq 1
(so
{\mathbb{E}}[W]>0
), we have

\limsup_{T\to\infty}f_{T}(\alpha)\leq\limsup_{T\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[W]^{1/2T}\phi(\alpha)=\phi(\alpha).

(D.2)

Now, let
(\alpha_{T})_{T\geq 1}
be a converging sequence with
\alpha:=\lim_{T\to\infty}\alpha_{T}
. Notice that

\displaystyle\|X_{\alpha_{T}}-X_{\alpha}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\||1-\alpha_{T}Z|-|1-\alpha Z|\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\alpha_{T}-\alpha)Z\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(\alpha_{T}-\alpha)\overline{Z}\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}0.

Fixing
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the above implies
\|X_{\alpha_{T}}-X_{\alpha}\|\leq 1-\varepsilon
for
T
sufficiently large. The set
S_{\varepsilon}:=\{|X_{\alpha}|>\varepsilon\|X_{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\}
satisfies
{\mathbb{P}}(S_{\varepsilon})>0
by definition of
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. Moreover, since
W>0
a.s., there exists
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for
A_{n}:=\{W>1/n\}
, we have
{\mathbb{P}}(A_{n}\cap S_{\varepsilon})>0
. Putting things together, we have

\displaystyle f_{T}(\alpha_{T})

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{E}}[WX^{2T}_{\alpha_{T}}\mathbf{1}_{S_{\varepsilon}\cap A_{n}}]^{1/2T}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{1/2T}{\mathbb{E}}[(|X_{\alpha}|-\|X_{\alpha_{T}}-X_{\alpha}\|_{\infty})^{2T}\mathbf{1}_{S_{\varepsilon}\cap A_{n}}]^{1/2T}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{1/2T}{\mathbb{E}}[(\varepsilon\|X_{\alpha}\|_{\infty}-\varepsilon+1)^{2T}\mathbf{1}_{S_{\varepsilon}\cap A_{n}}]^{1/2T}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(S_{\varepsilon}\cap A_{n})}{n}\right)^{1/2T}(\varepsilon\|X_{\alpha}\|_{\infty}-\varepsilon+1)

E.2
Asymptotic Depth Behavior

Having established in the previous subsection that gradient flow converges to a
stationary point at any fixed depth

, we now study how these stationary
points behave as the depth
T\to\infty
. Our goal is to understand the
asymptotic depth behavior of the limits of gradient flow and to show
that, as in the one-parameter case, the limiting predictor is Bayes optimal. To make explicit the dependence on depth, let
\ell^{(T)}
denote the loss
\ell
where the CA model is at depth
T
.

In the two-parameter setting, the loss is not jointly convex, so we cannot identify the gradient-flow limit
(\alpha_{T}^{*},\beta_{T}^{*})
as a global minimizer of
\ell

(\alpha_{T}^{*},\beta_{T}^{*})\ \text{is a stationary point of }\ell^{(T)}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{T}^{*}=\alpha_{T}^{*}(\beta_{T}^{*}),

so in particular

F_{T}^{\prime}(\beta_{T}^{*})=0.

Thus, the large-depth behavior of the gradient-flow limits is governed by the
stationary points of the one-dimensional function
\beta\mapsto F_{T}(\beta)
. It will be convenient to work with a logarithmically rescaled version of

G_{T}(\beta):=\frac{1}{2T}\log\left(\frac{F_{T}(\beta)}{{\mathbb{E}}[W]}\right).

(E.5)

The function
G_{T}
is well-defined and smooth as
F_{T}>0
. Moreover,
G_{T}
is a strictly monotone transform of
F_{T}
so
F_{T}^{\prime}(\beta_{T}^{*})=0
if and only if
G_{T}^{\prime}(\beta_{T}^{*})=0
. Our task is therefore to understand the asymptotic behavior of
G_{T}
and

G_{T}^{\prime}
as
T\to\infty
, and to show that stationary points of
F_{T}
(and

) concentrate near a single value of
\beta
.

Lemma E.7

Suppose Assumption
6.1

Lemma E.8

(Asymptotics of the derivative)
.

Suppose Assumption

In particular, since

We begin by writing

Thus,

(E.13)

We analyze the two terms in (

From the proof of Lemma

o(1)
uniformly on
K

in
