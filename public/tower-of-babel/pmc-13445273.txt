Linking “Big” Geospatial and Health Data: Implications for Research in Environmental Epidemiology
Authors: Andrea R. Titus, Tarik Benmarhnia, Lorna E. Thorpe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND: Environmental epidemiology studies increasingly integrate “big” geospatial and health data sets to examine associations between environmental factors and health outcomes. Using such data setsand linking between thempresents a number of complexities with regard to study design and analytic approaches. These complexities are often magnified with the integration of additional contextual data representing other neighborhood characteristics including socioeconomic factors. Guidance regarding the design of environmental health studies that leverage “big” geospatial and health outcome data is limited and fragmented. OBJECTIVE: Drawing on methodological literature and case studies, this commentary outlines common challenges related to geospatial and health data linkages, posing a series of guiding questions and considerations for investigators conducting environmental health studies, particularly analyses with an etiological focus. DISCUSSION: Recommendations include: 1) using a target trial approach to guide causal analysis, 2) aligning measures with hypothesized causal mechanisms, 3) exploring opportunities to “groundtruth” and validate data, and 4) prioritizing interdisciplinary science. The goal of the commentary is to consolidate insights from multiple disciplinesincluding exposure science, epidemiology, and sociologyto provide a foundation for etiologic research focused on advancing environmental health for all populations.

Introduction

In recent decades, the proliferation of high-resolution, geospatial environmental data setsin combination with the increasing availability and accessibility of large-scale, real-world health data that includes geographic identifiers such as residential addresshas led to a growing interest in leveraging linkages between diverse “big” data sources to examine impact of physical and built environments on health and health disparities. Google Earth Engine and other publicly accessible platforms and data tools can offer highly spatially and temporally resolved information about air pollutants, green space, light, heat, and other environmental factors across broad geographic areas. For environmental epidemiologists, the spatial coverage of these data can help facilitate subgroup analyses in large samples and across diverse geographical contexts. , In addition, large-scale environmental data can be harnessed to track changing spatial patterns of climate-sensitive risk factors and understand how these factors impact health outcomes.

The purpose of this commentary is to unpack potential challenges associated with linking “big” geospatial data with “big” health data (particularly real-world health data, including claims and electronic health record (EHR) databases) in etiologic epidemiologic research that seeks to shed light on environmentally driven patterns of population health. In these types of studies, investigators must grapple with the nuances of multiple overlaid data sources, each with its own challenges related to validity and representation. We focus on common challenges associated with each data source, particularly in the context of data linkages. We then provide a series of guiding questions and considerations for leveraging these data sources for environmental epidemiology. While this commentary primarily focuses on etiological analyses conducted within the U.S., examples and implications are drawn from global research, highlighting the salience of many of these challenges across diverse geographical areas.

Data Sources

Geospatial Data

An immediate challenge facing investigators using many geospatial data sets is the need to achieve an adequate level of technical expertise in understanding how attributes of physical or built environments are represented, as well as their potential sources of bias and uncertainty. Geospatial data may be derived from multiple sources. Producing spatially and temporally resolved modeled air pollution exposure estimates, for example, may entail combining multiple types of data inputs, including information from ground monitors, satellites, and chemical transport modeling. , Efforts have been made in recent years to increase the accessibility of geospatial data products for environmental monitoring and analysis, including NASA’s Applied Remote Sensing Training (ARSET) program and state-developed environmental screening tools, such as CalEnviroScreen or Colorado EnviroScreen. − Yet, technical barriers may remain in understanding data inputs, processing data to align with available spatiotemporal health data, assigning exposure windows that are etiologically relevant, and characterizing potential sources of bias. The result is that opportunities for meaningful research may be missed or underutilized and research output is often insufficiently nuanced in articulating the limitations of component data sources.

In addition to these technical challenges, investigators may also be faced with challenging conceptual questions regarding the validity of the geospatial data for their research question as well as the most appropriate methods for assigning environmental measures to individuals or neighborhood boundaries, across time and space. Consider one commonly used, satellite-derived measure for greenspace or greenness, the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI). Prior studies have offered mixed evidence regarding the extent to which NDVI values align with individual perceptions of local greenness − and have noted that satellite-derived measures of exposure to greenness are not equivalent to nature access. Furthermore, there is a lack of scientific consensus regarding appropriate buffer sizes for NDVI-based exposures, and the most appropriate method for assigning individual-level exposures likely varies based on the research question and hypothesized causal pathway. For example, smaller buffer sizes may be favored when examining mental health outcomes, whereas larger buffers may be appropriate when examining physical activity outcomes. Complications associated with the generation and use of geospatial data may also include spatial dependencies between geographic unitsfor example, air pollution emitted from specific emissions sources can affect multiple spatial units. Such spatial dependence is accounted for in most state-of-the-art modeling methods. Geospatial data may also be impacted by the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP), in which variations in the size and shape of spatial units can yield different analytic results. Other common challenges accompanying the use of geospatial environmental data include issues related to missing data and measurement error, which may arise due to gaps in underlying data sources (e.g., spatiotemporal gaps in ground monitor measurements), missing covariates or mis-specified prediction models, or failure to account for mobility patterns (e.g., time spent outside of the home for work or leisure) when characterizing individual-level exposures. , In addition, while most geospatial data focus on the outdoor environment, individuals spend 90% of their time indoors, and indoor environments often remain poorly characterized. Combining multiple types of data from different sourcese.g., integrating gridded geospatial data with individual-level location information from wearable or Global Positioning System (GPS) devicesmay help refine individual-level exposure estimates, , though these efforts may be challenging to implement at scale and may magnify privacy concerns already inherent in linking sensitive health information with environmental exposures. In etiologic research, measurement issues may be further compounded in statistical modeling by high levels of correlation between environmental exposures or biased exposure-outcome effect estimates stemming from exposure measurement error.

Aligning temporality of environmental data with other data sources may present an additional challenge, due to uncertainty regarding duration and timing of relevant susceptible periods as well as potential limitations in the temporal scale and/or granularity of the data sources. Furthermore, in the context of dynamic exposures obtained from GPS measures, results estimated from different time-weighted spatial averaging methods may be sensitive to the structure of the environmental data, as well as movement patterns within the sample.

These challenges may be even more pronounced in etiologic research focused on examining drivers of health disparities given the multitude of assumptions and subjective decisions required by researchers to measure and interpret geospatial data in the context of these studies. For example, interpreting the NDVI can be highly dependent on context, as NDVI values do not distinguish between desirable and undesirable vegetation. A case study in Toledo, Ohio, found that vacant land parcels with strong overgrowth had relatively high NDVI values, highlighting how greenness should not always be considered an urban amenity, particularly in “shrinking cities.” Likewise, other studies have explored how relationships between green areas and health outcomes depend on land use, for example, highlighting how greenness surrounding agricultural areas may be associated with worse health outcomes, potentially due to proximity to pesticide use. In addition, the choice of modeling approach and the spatial resolution of geospatial data may impact observed patterns of environmental exposures. For example, several studies have documented substantial variation in PM2.5 exposure disparities, along with PM2.5-attributable mortality, depending on the spatial scale and air pollution estimation approach used for the analysis. − Finally, measurement error and gaps in spatiotemporal data availability may also obscure important variability in exposure within a population. For example, basing air pollution exposures on residential address, and failing to account for occupational exposures or mobility, may mischaracterize exposure differences across population subgroups. , In addition, a recent analysis suggested that 44% of U.S. urban areas exceeding the EPA’s current regulatory standard for annual average PM2.5 (9 ug/m3) would not be detected by the ground monitor network, and that misclassification of census tracts as in “attainment” (when they are not) may be more likely in tracts with higher proportions of some racial and ethnic groups and lower income households. Monitoring gaps are even more pronounced in low- and middle-income countries. While citizen science initiatives that expand the use of low-cost sensors can be leveraged to improve the spatial coverage and measurement of environmental exposures, including air pollution, these sensors may also be unequally distributed and may be more likely to be located in communities with higher income and education levels. ,

Health Data

While a great deal of prior evidence in environmental epidemiology has been generated from survey-based cohort studies or randomized control trials (RCTs), these data sources are often not representative and may exclude specific subgroups from the analysis. Results from studies using other types of health data can complement existing evidence and assist with evidence triangulation. Moreover, clinical or administrative data sets can offer large sample sizes, extensive geographic coverage, and, in some cases, rich clinical information. However, these data have their own limitations and complexities that interact with the aforementioned challenges associated with geospatial data. First, the potential for bias related to selection into the sample or missing clinical information is particularly acute when using data derived from patient populations. Claims and EHR-based samples are typically not representative of a target population (e.g., the entire US population), and efforts to address missing data by restricting to patients with more complete information may further exacerbate sample selection issues. In EHR or claims-based analyses, for example, participation may be impacted by health status, particularly if inclusion criteria for the study are based on prior patterns of health care utilization. The potential for selection bias based on differential participation is particularly relevant when studying long-term prevalent exposures, including environmental risk factors. While methods are frequently utilized to address the impact of missing data and sample selection (e.g., multiple imputation and inverse probability weighting), the success of these methods depends on data availability and on the assumptions of the underlying causal model. In some cases, data may be missing “not at random”, further limiting the arsenal of tools to address bias. In addition, because clinical and administrative data are typically not collected for research purposes, measures are not optimized for research validity. Determining if an individual has a particular chronic condition, for example, may entail employing complex algorithms using diagnosis codes, as well as laboratory and pharmacy data. Even then, sensitivity or specificity may be lacking, and algorithms may require cohort-specific validation.

Furthermore, in longitudinal studies using clinical or administrative health data, aligning an individual’s “time zero” for the follow-up period with environmental exposure data may be challenging. For example, survival analyses of time-varying environmental exposures that incorporate postbaseline information into exposure definitions (such as averaging individual exposure levels over follow-up time) can result in a form of immortal time bias, particularly when there are secular time trends in the exposure of interest. This potential bias was recently demonstrated in an analysis using data from the California Cancer Registry to examine associations between PM2.5 exposure and mortality following a lung cancer diagnosis. While a time-to-event analysis that averaged PM2.5 exposure over the follow-up period suggested that higher PM2.5 exposure was associated with a lower survival probability, this effect estimate was attenuated in discrete time models that more appropriately aligned the start of follow-up with exposure assignment. In prospective studies, investigators may also lack standard definitions for loss to follow-up, or may be unable to distinguish individuals who have left a given health care system or network from those who remain in the system/network but have not had a recent encounter.

As with geospatial data, challenges associated with the use of large-scale health data are particularly complex in studies focused on health disparities. For example, investigators may be unable to distinguish whether differences in care utilization across population subgroups represent differences in underlying health status or differences in healthcare access or use patterns. Address data may not be longitudinally captured, which complicates efforts to examine the trajectories of place-based drivers of health outcomes over time. In addition, studies often seek to examine individual- or neighborhood-level factors as effect measure modifiers, shedding light on the extent to which population subgroups may experience a differential vulnerability to environmental risks. However, defining the population subgroups of interest may not be straightforward. For example, race and ethnicity data are often poorly captured in claims or EHR data, with high levels of missingness and inadequate race and ethnicity classification practices, including coarse aggregation of racial and ethnic subgroups. , While algorithmic methods may be available to supplement collected race and ethnicity information, these algorithms may not be applicable to all racial and ethnic groups and may have variable performance, depending on the methods and data set. In addition, although health systems are increasingly incorporating information on social determinants of health into EHRs, uptake is still limited, and processes lack standardization. Such limitations may encourage researchers to explore additional sources of data to supplement collected information on factors relevant to the social and economic patterning of environmental risk factors and associated health outcomes (e.g., aspects of neighborhood socioeconomic status).

Contextual Data

As noted, disparity-focused studies that leverage big geospatial and health data frequently integrate other sources of data to address limitations in individual-level data collection (e.g., using area-level income as a proxy for missing individual-level income). Investigators may also incorporate local area, contextual variables in an attempt to address potential confounding by place-based characteristics or because they are interested in examining whether different facets of the neighborhood environment modify associations between environmental factors and health outcomes. One commonly used source of area-level data in the U.S. is the American Community Survey (ACS), which provides information on a diversity of topics (e.g., demographic, housing, and economic characteristics) summarized across geographic areas, ranging from the census block group to the national level. Common pitfalls associated with the use and integration of ACS or other Census data include failing to explicitly describe whether area-level markers are meant to capture aspects of the neighborhood environment or to serve as proxies for individual-level factors and failing to acknowledge pertinent data limitations. For example, the use of area-level measures as individual-level proxies may represent an ecologic fallacy. If these data are intended to capture neighborhood environments, it is important to acknowledge that administrative units (e.g., census block groups or tracts) may not represent meaningful “neighborhood” definitions. There are lags in ACS data availability, and in small geographic areas, data are aggregated across multiple years and may have large margins of error. Importantly, these data sources may undercount some population subgroups.

One type of study that has gained traction in recent years is to evaluate whether associations between an environmental risk factor and a health outcome are modified by an area-level index composed of multiple factors representing economic deprivation or social vulnerability, with data often derived from the ACS or other external data sources. − A range of indices have been developed to represent socioeconomic, material, environmental, and multidimensional neighborhood deprivation, or vulnerability to environmental hazards. The choice of one index over another for a given analysis is rarely justified, even though indices are constructed with different inputs, may represent different underlying constructs, and may exhibit a wide range of spatial concurrence with one another. − For example, a recent analysis of two commonly used area-level indicesthe area deprivation index (2019) and the social vulnerability index (2018)at the U.S. tract level found that correlation coefficients were only 0.51 when comparing continuous or categorical (deciles) versions of the two indices. In addition, indices constructed to capture vulnerability to specific environmental risks (e.g., heat vulnerability indices) may be highly variable, depending on both inputs and spatial scale.

In addition to examining effect modification by area deprivation indices, recent scholarship has also highlighted the need for environmental epidemiology studies that incorporate area-based measures of structural racism, which encompasses the “totality of ways in which societies foster racial discrimination through mutually reinforcing systems of housing, education, employment, earnings, benefits, credit, media, health care, and criminal justice.” These recommendations stem in part from the problematic treatment of race and ethnicity variables in many epidemiologic studiesincluding conflating race and ethnicity with racism and providing limited explanations regarding the operationalization and treatment of race and ethnicity variables in modeling approaches. ,− For example, a recent review of U.S.-based studies on air pollution and mortality found that many studies failed to justify the use of race and ethnicity variables as effect modifiers and, in interpreting the results, failed to acknowledge the social construction of race or to interpret differences in results across racial and ethnic groups as products of social stratification and structural racism. Rather than using race as a proxy for racism, investigators may seek to directly integrate measures of structural racism into their analysis. A body of literature has emerged that operationalizes multilevel and multidimensional area-level measures of structural racism, which may include scale- or index-based variables, indicators within a single domain, or policies. Mapping these data to local geographies, including zip codes, census tracts, or counties, allows for the linkage to health outcome data and for the exploration of the intersection between racism and environmental health inequities. , As with economic indices, the choice of measures to capture structural racism should be carefully considered, with an understanding of strengths and limitations of underlying data sources. ,

Incorporating additional area-level data with large-scale geospatial and health data can serve multiple purposes–complementing existing data collection and incorporating neighborhood-level contextual information. However, these data also contribute an additional layer of complexity to epidemiologic analyses, and their limitations have special implications for studies focused on health disparities.

Discussion

In Table , we present a series of questions and considerations that summarize the challenges associated with the three types of data discussed in this commentary: geospatial environmental data, real-world health data, and contextual area-level data (e.g., ACS data). We also provide suggested resources and tools for exploring the potential implications of each question in analyses of environmental risk factors. While giving detailed descriptions of best-practice sensitivity analyses, causal modeling approaches, and quantitative bias analyses is beyond the scope of this commentary, we encourage investigators to draw on a vast body of epidemiologic literaturemuch recently emergingcovering these topics. We offer four overarching considerations for analyses utilizing multiple large-scale data linkages: 1) use a target trial approach to guide causal analysis, 2) align measures with hypothesized causal mechanisms, 3) explore opportunities to “groundtruth” and validate data, and 4) prioritize interdisciplinary science.

Guiding Questions and Potential Resources for Environmental Epidemiology Studies That Leverage “Big” Data Linkages

Use a Target Trial Approach to Guide Causal Analysis

RCTs have several advantages over observational analyses with regard to satisfying identification assumptions (particularly exchangeability) for causal inference. The target trial framework represents an approach to causal analysis in which an observational study is designed to emulate a hypothetical RCT, when no RCT may be feasible. Mapping an observational analysis to a target RCT requires that the investigator think through the study design and analytic choices to limit bias and enhance the likelihood that identifiability conditions are met in the observational context. Investigators are compelled to carefully specify the research question and hypothetical “intervention” under study, refine the validity of measured variables, explicitly define the target population, and align “time-zero.” For example, prior work has shown how the use of a target trial framework may help researchers avoid biases stemming from the incorporation of postbaseline information into exposure assignment definitions in the context of time-varying environmental exposures. In addition, the use of causal diagramssuch as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs)within a target trial framework can further enhance understanding of potential confounding and selection bias pathways, pointing the researcher toward methods to address sample selection or missing data biases or toward high-priority sensitivity analyses or quantitative bias analyses to explore the potential impact of study design choices or biases on study results. DAGs have been used to illustrate potential confounding biases in research on air pollution, to demonstrate how selection bias due to differential participation may impact etiologic studies of prevalent environmental exposures, and to clarify the structure of informative presence bias EHR data. While the use of target trial frameworks may be especially relevant to the study of real-world health data , where issues related to selection bias, missing data, confounding, and time-related biases may be particularly complexto our knowledge, there are relatively few examples of studies examining environmental risks using real-world health data that employ the target trial approach. ,

Target trials can be especially pertinent to research focused on health disparities as they clarify the link between research and potential policy interventions and encourage the investigator to consider the potential for differential validity of key variables across subgroups of interest. Of note, emerging methods are adapting the target trial framework to measuring health disparities, including developing approaches to address nonrandom sample selection and to clarify the impact of these selection processes on resulting disparity measures.

Align Measures with Hypothesized Causal Mechanisms

Within a target trial framework, carefully specifying hypothesized causal pathways is often an essential prerequisite to aligning geospatial data with underlying constructs of interest as well as specifying appropriate spatial units and/or buffer sizes for the analysis. Prior work, for example, has outlined theoretical mechanisms through which different measures of greenness may impact different types of health outcomes, as well as the subsequent implications for analytic approaches. Selecting measures that closely align with hypothesized causal mechanisms can sharpen causal questions, guard against potential violations of the consistency assumption, and help minimize the potential for ambiguous effect estimates. Because analytic results may be sensitive to decisions about spatiotemporal scales, it may also be important to explore the potential impact of using different spatial and temporal units through sensitivity analyses. As with geospatial and health data, selecting and refining contextual variables also requires careful consideration, particularly in studies examining effect measure modification by neighborhood characteristics. For example, researchers interested in the potential modifying impact of neighborhood income may want to consider the role of both absolute income and relative income, defined in comparison to a geographic, demographic or other reference group, as both types of measures have been shown to be relevant for health outcome analyses. Likewise, as previously discussed, if structural racism is hypothesized as a potential modifying variable, investigators should consider directly incorporating measures of racism into their analysis rather than using individual race and ethnicity information as a proxy. Recent work by Hicken et al. and Buxton et al. provide useful guidance for considering how measures of structural racism may modify associations between air pollution and health outcomes. ,

Explore Opportunities to Groundtruth and Validate Data

Throughout this commentary, we have described how different data processing decisions have the potential to impact study results. In a telling example, Acolin and Kim describe an instance in which three different research groups developed three divergent maps of boundaries for Ho Chi Minh City from satellite imagery, largely due to inconsistent classifications of urban peripheral areas. Differences in classifications stemmed from differences in image resolution, “urban” definitions, and the extent to which data were “groundtruthed.” Groundtruthing may entail fieldwork or engaging in community partnerships to decode visual inputs and refine classification algorithms. In the example of Ho Chi Minh City, fieldwork helped investigators to better understand visual signatures of informal settlements in satellite imagery, and insights from this process became an essential part of calibrating classification algorithms. Groundtruthing may also entail triangulating information across different data sets. , To aid in interpreting NDVI, for example, prior research has combined information from satellite imagery with parcel-level data on housing vacancy and indicators of blight. This contextual information can shed light on the extent to which urban greenness may represent an amenity or disamenity. Likewise, wearable devices or GPS data may be used to refine individual-level estimates of environmental exposures and correct for measurement error. In health data, chart review can be used to validate clinical phenotypes. Since these methods may not be feasible to employ within the full study sample, investigators may choose to validate measures within a subset or external sample of individuals, and use that information to calibrate estimates within the study population. It is of particular importance in studies of environmental health disparities to consider whether measures appropriately represent underlying constructs across population subgroups and/or spatiotemporal contexts. With regard to clinical data, for example, recent work by Sun et al., describes a framework for evaluating clinical phenotypes using algorithmic fairness principles.

Prioritize Interdisciplinary Science

The many overlapping complexities inherent in linking geospatial and health data highlight the need for multidisciplinary team science. Consider an analysis that uses high-resolution modeled PM2.5 estimates linked with an EHR database to examine whether associations between air pollution and mortality differ by a census tract-level measure of socioeconomic status. This study would require a strong understanding of air pollution modeling methods and their sources of error. In addition, it would require an understanding of potential biases associated with the use of the EHR-based sample, including the potential for a highly selected patient population. This study would require careful selection and theory-based justification of a tract-level measure of socioeconomic status based on hypothesized mechanisms through which associations between PM2.5 and mortality may differ according to neighborhood context. An interdisciplinary teamincluding expertise in exposure science, causal epidemiology, and social theory, for examplecan be an asset to guide study design and interpretation of results in ways that improve validity and minimize sources of bias, while also prioritizing sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of study results to different variable and modeling specifications. Recent work has emphasized the need for interdisciplinary science to address compounding risks associated with climate-related events, as well as the need to continue to break down siloes between the geo- and health-science communities. Existing efforts to stimulate interdisciplinary engagement include NASA’s Health and Air Quality Applied Sciences Team, which aims to facilitate linkages between NASA data tools and public health stakeholders. ,

Importantly, team science is not limited to the integration of different academic disciplines. Multisectoral approaches and community partnerships are also needed to advance environmental health for all populations, grounded in participatory research principles. Community-academic collaborations are increasingly recognized as essential to advancing the coproduction of actionable knowledge to address environmental health risks. , However, community and stakeholder engagement in the context of “big data” geospatial and health analyses remains underutilized. For example, several recent studies have noted the relative scarcity of research that engages communities in the use and interpretation of satellite environmental data. , Stakeholder and community engagement can have multiple benefits, including supporting efforts to groundtruth and refine data constructs and to generate research questions and evidence that are more directly responsive to policy and stakeholder needs.

Conclusions

It is a fallacy that big dataincluding geospatial data on the built and physical environmentrepresent the objective source of truth. Instead, the processing and interpretation of these data reflect subjective decision making that can profoundly impact our understanding of the social patterning and consequences of environmental risk factors. Advances in computational power, data accessibility, and statistical analysis methods have fostered a growing interest in harnessing big data linkages to advance environmental health, but leveraging these resources is not without significant challenges. Without careful attention to assumptions, causal mechanisms, and diverse forms of expertise required, research studies have the potential to reinforce or obscure longstanding health disparities. , This commentary offers a series of guiding questions and considerations for investigators pursuing linkages between large-scale geospatial and health data, with the goal of advancing health for all populations.
