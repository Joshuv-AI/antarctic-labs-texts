Top to random and reverse: analysis of a new descent algebra shuffle
Darij Grinberg, Jonathan Parlett

Abstract




1
Introduction

Consider the symmetric group
S_{n}
, consisting of permutations of the
n
-element set
\left[n\right]:=\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
.
The permutations
w\in S_{n}
are often visualized as a shuffled deck of
n
cards that are labeled
1,2,\dots,n
:
Any permutation
w\in S_{n}
corresponds to the state in which the cards are ordered
w\left(1\right),w\left(2\right),\dots,w\left(n\right)
.
We may define a random state (i.e., a probability distribution on the set of states) to be a formal linear combination of permutations
\sum_{w\in S_{n}}a_{w}w\in\mathbb{R}[S_{n}]
where the
a_{w}\in\mathbb{R}
are non-negative and
\sum_{w\in S_{n}}a_{w}=1
.
Then
a_{w}
is the probability that the deck is in state
w
.
We may drop the
\sum_{w\in S_{n}}a_{w}=1
condition as we may simply “normalize” our sum (divide by
\sum_{w\in S_{n}}a_{w}
) to obtain a random state.
For example, the element
1+\operatorname{cyc}_{1,2,3}
(where
\operatorname{cyc}_{1,2,3}
is the
3
-cycle
1\mapsto 2\mapsto 3\mapsto 1
) corresponds to the random state where the probability of the deck being ordered
1,2,3
is
1/2
and ordered
2,3,1
is
1/2
.

A random shuffle can then be defined as a Markov chain on
S_{n}
, or, equivalently, a linear endomorphism of the
\mathbb{R}
-vector space
\mathbb{R}[S_{n}]
that is given by a stochastic matrix.
However,
\mathbb{R}[S_{n}]
is itself an
\mathbb{R}
-algebra – the group algebra of the group
S_{n}
– and thus each of its elements gives rise to two endomorphisms, one by left and one by right multiplication.
Hence, each element of
\mathbb{R}[S_{n}]
with nonnegative coefficients gives rise to two random shuffles.
For instance, left multiplication by
1+\operatorname{cyc}_{1,2}
swaps the cards labelled
1
and
2
with probability
1/2
and otherwise leaves the deck unchanged; whereas right multiplication by
1+\operatorname{cyc}_{1,2}
swaps the top two cards (whatever their labels are) with probability
1/2
and otherwise leaves the deck unchanged.
This rests on the fact that the multiplication in
\mathbb{R}[S_{n}]
is just extending by linearity the multiplication in the symmetric group
S_{n}
, which corresponds to composing permutations (performing one after the other).

This algebraic viewpoint on card shuffling first rose to prominence when Diaconis and Shahshahani bounded the mixing time of some shuffles by computing the eigenvalues of the respective operators
[
7
]
; this answers the (admittedly open-ended) question “how many times should we shuffle a deck of cards to obtain a well-shuffled deck”.
See
[
6
,
5
]
for surveys of work done along these lines.
Recent research efforts have focused on identifying shuffles whose eigenvalues admit a combinatorial description
[
12
,
7
,
Phatar91
,
8
]
.
We extend this line of research by studying a new shuffle derived from the well-known top-to-random shuffle.

Let
\mathbf{T}_{k}
be the element of the group algebra
\mathbb{R}[S_{n}]
given by

\mathbf{T}_{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in S_{n};\\
w^{-1}\left(k+1\right)<\cdots<w^{-1}\left(n\right)\end{subarray}}w\qquad\left(\text{for each integer }0\leq k\leq n\right).

The element
\mathbf{T}_{k}
is known as the
k
-top-to-random
shuffle and has been well studied in
[
Phatar91
]
.
In particular it is known that
\mathbf{T}_{1}
is diagonalizable with integer eigenvalues that admit a combinatorial description.
That is, the
minimal polynomial
of
\mathbf{T}_{1}
– meaning the lowest-degree monic polynomial that vanishes when applied to
\mathbf{T}_{1}
– is a product of linear factors, namely

\prod_{k\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,n-2,n\right\}}\left(x-k\right)\in\mathbb{R}[x]

(note the conspicuous absence of the
x-\left(n-1\right)
factor).
This entails that when acting on
\mathbb{R}[S_{n}]
either by left or right multiplication,
\mathbf{T}_{1}
is a diagonalizable endomorphism with eigenvalues
0,1,\ldots,n-2,n
; their multiplicities too can be computed.
This analysis can be extended to arbitrary base rings
\mathbf{k}
instead of
\mathbb{R}
(we are restricting ourselves to
\mathbb{R}
in this introduction for simplicity’s sake),
and to all the
\mathbf{T}_{k}
’s (in fact, each
\mathbf{T}_{k}
is a polynomial in
\mathbf{T}_{1}
, thus inheriting the diagonalizability of the latter).
See
[
10
]
(where
\mathbf{T}_{k}
is denoted
\mathbf{B}_{k}
) for details and further references.
Informally,
\mathbf{T}_{k}
– acting as a random shuffle by right multiplication – corresponds to taking the top
k
cards of the deck and moving them to random positions.

The
antipode
of
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
is the
\mathbf{k}
-linear map
S:\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]\to\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
that sends each permutation
w\in S_{n}
to its inverse
w^{-1}
.
This is a
\mathbf{k}
-algebra anti-isomorphism, and thus preserves the minimal polynomial.
Hence, to determine the minimal polynomial of some element it suffices to study its antipode.

The antipodes of the elements
\mathbf{T}_{k}
are instances of a wider class of shuffle-like elements, which form a basis of the
descent algebra
of
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
.
These are indexed by the
compositions
of
n
, that is, by the tuples of positive integers with sum
n
.
Given a composition
\alpha=\left(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\right)
of
n
, we set

\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}=\sum w,

where the sum ranges over all permutations
w\in S_{n}
that increase on the smallest
\alpha_{1}
elements of
\left[n\right]
, on the next-smallest
\alpha_{2}
elements of
\left[n\right]
, on the next-smallest
\alpha_{3}
elements of
\left[n\right]
, etc. (but can decrease between these blocks).
Note that
S\left(\mathbf{T}_{k}\right)=\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
for
\alpha=\left(1,1,\ldots,1,n-k\right)
(with
k
many
1
s).
These elements
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
are known as the “
\alpha
-shuffles” in the shuffling world, and are diagonalizable as well; their eigenvalues are known since Bidigare’s pioneering work
[
1
, §4.1.9]
.
Moreover, any linear combination of the
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
with nonnegative real coefficients is still diagonalizable and its eigenvalues are known, by a result of Brown
[
21
, Theorem 4.1]
.

In this work, we extend this analysis to a “mirror version” of the
\alpha
-shuffles and their linear combinations.
The “mirroring” refers to reversing the deck, which is modelled by an application of the
longest word

w_{0}
.
Formally,
w_{0}
is defined as the permutation in
S_{n}
with one-line notation
\left(n,n-1,\dots,1\right)
.
We shall study the shuffles
w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
and
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}w_{0}
, which are conjugate because
w_{0}^{-1}=w_{0}
.

We shall now overview our results in the simple-looking (but already highly nontrivial) case when
\alpha=\left(1,n-1\right)
, in which case
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}=S\left(\mathbf{T}_{1}\right)
.
The
1
-top-to-random shuffle
\mathbf{T}_{1}
is denoted by
\mathbf{A}
, and is known as the
top-to-random shuffle
or the
Tsetlin library
; it has appeared (among other places) in Lie algebra cohomology
[
24
, Appendix]
and machine learning
[
Reizen19
, Lemma 29]
.

Informally, the shuffle
w_{0}\mathbf{T}_{1}
corresponds to picking the top card, moving it to a random position, and reversing the order of all cards in the deck, while
\mathbf{T}_{1}w_{0}
corresponds to first reversing the deck, then moving the top card to a random position.
We thus call
w_{0}\mathbf{T}_{1}
and
\mathbf{T}_{1}w_{0}
the
reverse top-to-random
shuffles.

Our first main result for this case is saying that
w_{0}\mathbf{T}_{1}
is diagonalizable with integer eigenvalues. To be more precise, we have the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
9.5
further on)
.

Let
n>1
. The minimal polynomial of
w_{0}\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}w_{0}
over
\mathbb{R}
is

\prod_{k\in\left\{-n+2\right\}\cup\left[-n+4,n-3\right]\cup\left\{0\right\}\cup\left\{n\right\}}\left(x-k\right)\in\mathbb{R}\left[x\right]

(1)

(where
\left[a,b\right]:=\left\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:a\leq k\leq b\right\}
).
Thus, the elements
w_{0}\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}w_{0}
– acting either by left or by right multiplication on
\mathbb{R}[S_{n}]
– are diagonalizable linear endomorphisms whose eigenvalues are
0
, as well as
\pm k
for
k\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,n-4\right\}
, as well as
n-3
and
-n+2
and
n
.

Our path to proving Theorem
1.1
begins with a tour of Solomon’s descent algebra
D\left(S_{n}\right)
, which we define in Section
3
(including its
B-basis
elements
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
), and its connection to Bidigare’s face algebra
\mathbf{k}\mathcal{F}
, which we introduce in Section
4
.
In these two sections, we will prove various combinatorial and algebraic properties of these objects, including some classical results of Bidigare and Brown.
The underlying combinatorial objects are
compositions
(i.e., tuples of positive integers) and
set compositions
(also known as “ordered set partitions”, i.e., tuples of disjoint nonempty sets with a given union).
We will refer to set compositions as “
faces
”, as they are known to correspond to the faces of the (type-
A
) braid arrangement; however, we will not use any geometry in this paper.
We let
\mathcal{F}
be the set of all set compositions of
\left[n\right]
; this set is equipped with a monoid structure (Definition
4.4
).

In Section
4
, we will also introduce the
knapsack numbers

n_{\alpha}\left(F\right)
, which – roughly speaking – count the ways that the blocks of a given set composition
F
of
\left[n\right]
can be “packed” into bags of sizes
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
(the entries of the composition
\alpha
).
The set of all these knapsack numbers is denoted
n_{\alpha}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)
.
These numbers (for
\alpha
fixed and
F
ranging over all faces) turn out to be the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
, as (implicitly) shown by Brown
[
21
, Theorem 4.1]
.

One of our main results is a similar description of the eigenvalues of
w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
:
We will show (Theorem
8.1
) that these eigenvalues are the
signed knapsack numbers

\widetilde{n}_{\alpha}\left(F\right)=\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(F\right)}n_{\alpha}\left(F\right)
of all faces
F
, where
\ell\left(F\right)
denotes the number of blocks of
F
.
Again, the element
w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
is diagonalizable (over
\mathbb{Q}
), so that its minimal polynomial is therefore

\prod_{k\in\widetilde{n}_{\alpha}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)}\left(x-k\right).

This will be proved in Section
8
, after the necessary tools have been developed in Sections
5
,
6
and
7
.

In Section
9
, we will apply our results to the particular case
\alpha=\left(1,n-1\right)
, proving Theorem
1.1
(restated as Theorem
9.5
).
The perhaps strange-looking set
\left\{-n+2\right\}\cup\left[-n+4,n-3\right]\cup\left\{0\right\}\cup\left\{n\right\}
will be revealed there as the set
\widetilde{n}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)
of signed knapsack numbers.

In Section
10
, we will extend our analysis from a single element
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
to an arbitrary linear combination
\mathbf{B}_{\bm{\gamma}}:=\sum_{\alpha}\gamma_{\alpha}\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
(where
\alpha
ranges over the compositions of
n
) of such elements with nonnegative real coefficients
\gamma_{\alpha}\geq 0
.
Just like Brown determined the minimal polynomial of any such combination
\mathbf{B}_{\bm{\gamma}}
, we will compute it for any
w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\bm{\gamma}}
.
The result (Theorem
10.5
) is that any such
w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\bm{\gamma}}
is again diagonalizable, and its eigenvalues are the respective linear combinations
\sum_{\alpha}\gamma_{\alpha}\widetilde{n}_{\alpha}\left(F\right)
of the signed knapsack numbers.
The proof is rather similar to the case of a single
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
, and so we will just outline the differences.
Note that the nonnegativity of the coefficients
\gamma_{\alpha}
is important for the diagonalizability, but not for the computation of the eigenvalues.

With the eigenvalues computed, an obvious question to ask is about their algebraic multiplicities.
This question can be asked about any element of the descent algebra (nonnegative coefficients are irrelevant), and it comes in four versions: the element can act either by left or by right multiplication, and it can do so either on the descent algebra or on the whole
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
.
These four questions have three different answers (not four, because left and right action on
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
yield the same multiplicities).
The answer in the
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
case can be pieced together from the work of Bidigare
[
1
, Sections 3.8 and 4.1]
and Brown
[
3
, Appendix B]
(see
[
11
, Theorem 1.2]
,
[
18
, Proposition 4.2]
and
[
1
, Corollary 4.1.3]
for the specific results needed);
the descent algebra case appears to lead into the combinatorics of integer matrices (via Solomon’s Mackey formula,
[
20
, Corollary 2.4]
).
Thus, we will avoid this topic in the present paper, hoping to study it in the detail it deserves in future work.

As mentioned above, we used the base ring
\mathbb{R}
in this introduction for the sake of concreteness.
In the rest of the paper, we shall work over an arbitrary commutative ring
\mathbf{k}
much of the time; occasionally, we will restrict ourselves to fields of characteristic
0
(when discussing diagonalizability and minimal polynomials).
When
\mathbf{k}
is a field of positive characteristic, the polynomials that would normally be minimal still vanish at the respective elements, but are not necessarily minimal any more; sometimes diagonalizability is also lost.
(For example, for
n=4
and
\mathbf{k}=\mathbb{F}_{3}
, the element
w_{0}\mathbf{A}
has minimal polynomial
x\left(x-1\right)^{2}
.)
We do not venture any guesses on what the minimal polynomials over finite fields shall be.

4.1
Faces and their algebra

We shall now introduce another combinatorial notion – that of

Realizing their relevance to the descent algebra was Bidigare’s main contribution
[
1
]
.

5
5

5

Bidigare used them to reprove Solomon’s main results from

[

in a more general setting:
In fact, if their poset

This is implicit in Bidigare’s thesis
[

Definition 4.1

(Set compositions)
.

1.

(a)

(d)

(e)
For an integer

Auxiliary formula:

\left(F_{1},F_{2},\dots,F_{k}\right)^{\operatorname{red}}\cdot\left(G_{1},G_{2},\dots,G_{m}\right)^{\operatorname{red}}=\left(F_{1}\cap G_{1},\dots,F_{1}\cap G_{m},\dots,F_{k}\cap G_{m}\right)^{\operatorname{red}}.

and thus

\displaystyle E\left(FG\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{p}\right)}_{=\left(E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{p}\right)^{\operatorname{red}}}\cdot\left(F_{1}\cap G_{1},\ldots,F_{1}\cap G_{r},\ldots,F_{q}\cap G_{r}\right)^{\operatorname{red}}

\displaystyle=\left(E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{p}\right)^{\operatorname{red}}\cdot\left(F_{1}\cap G_{1},\ldots,F_{1}\cap G_{r},\ldots,F_{q}\cap G_{r}\right)^{\operatorname{red}}

\displaystyle=\left(E_{1}\cap\left(F_{1}\cap G_{1}\right),\ldots,E_{1}\cap\left(F_{1}\cap G_{r}\right),\ldots,E_{1}\cap\left(F_{q}\cap G_{r}\right),\ldots,E_{p}\cap\left(F_{q}\cap G_{r}\right)\right)^{\operatorname{red}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left(\text{by the auxiliary formula}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(E_{1}\cap F_{1}\cap G_{1},\ldots,E_{p}\cap F_{q}\cap G_{r}\right)^{\operatorname{red}},

where the triple intersections

on the
right hand side are listed in the order of lexicographically
increasing

, or, equivalently, of
lexicographically increasing
\left(i,j,k\right)
. A similar
computation yields the same expression for

, and thus associativity is proved.

It remains to show that

is a neutral element
for the multiplication on
\mathcal{F}
.
But this is clear, since any block

of any face

satisfies

∎

Definition 4.7

(Actions of

Consider the left action of the symmetric group

(Invariant subalgebra of
\mathbf{k}\mathcal{F}

.

\mathbf{k}
-subalgebra of
\mathbf{k}\mathcal{F}

Proof.

1.

2.

Thus,

\mathbf{k}
-subalgebra of
\mathbf{k}\mathcal{F}

(since this holds for every action by
\mathbf{k}
-algebra automorphisms).
∎

4.2
Bidigare’s anti-isomorphism

As we know, the family
\left(\mathbf{B}_{I}\right)_{I\subseteq\left[n-1\right]}
is a basis of the descent algebra
D\left(S_{n}\right)
(as
\mathbf{k}
-module).
Reindexing this family using compositions instead of subsets (see Definition
3.7

(c)
), we conclude that the family
\left(\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}\right)_{\alpha\models n}
is a basis of

This allows us to define the following crucial map (due to Bidigare
[
1
, Theorem 3.8.1]
):

Definition 4.11
.

Let
\rho:D\left(S_{n}\right)\to\left(\mathbf{k}\mathcal{F}\right)^{S_{n}}
be the
\mathbf{k}
-linear map defined by

\displaystyle\rho\left(\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}\right)=\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\alpha}\qquad\text{ for each }\alpha\models n

(7)

and extended linearly to all of

(We have used the fact that the

Theorem 4.12

(Bidigare)
.

The set

\mathbf{k}
-subalgebra of
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
.
The map

-algebra anti-isomorphism.

Proof.

This is Bidigare’s result
[
1

, and appears all across the literature.
We give a self-contained proof in the Appendix (Subsection
A.3
).
Here, however, let us outline how it can be obtained from more recent sources:
Saliola, in
[

, constructs a
\mathbf{k}
-algebra anti-morphism
\xi:\left(\mathbf{k}\mathcal{F}\right)^{S_{n}}\to\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]

\alpha\models n
(in his notations, this takes the form

being a set composition of type

The same is also proved in

Either way, we now know that the

-algebra anti-morphism

), this shows that the image of

Using this anti-isomorphism

\widetilde{w}_{0}:=\rho\left(w_{0}\right)

.

For any

, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}_{0}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\alpha}\widetilde{w}_{0}=\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\operatorname{rev}\alpha}

\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{w}_{0}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\alpha}\right)^{2}=\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\operatorname{rev}\alpha}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\alpha}.

Let
\alpha\models n
.
Then, Corollary

yields

.

Thanks to the anti-isomorphism

.

Let

The map
\rho

\displaystyle\rho\left(\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}w_{0}\right)

4.3
The containment relation

We return to the combinatorics of set compositions.

Definition 4.17
.

Let
F=\left(F_{1},F_{2},\dots,F_{k}\right)
and
G=\left(G_{1},G_{2},\dots,G_{m}\right)
be two faces in
\mathcal{F}
.
We say
F
is
contained
in
G
– and we write
F\twoheadrightarrow G
– if every block of
F
is a subset of a block of
G
. (More formally: if for each
i\in\left[k\right]
, there is a
j\in\left[m\right]
such that
F_{i}\subseteq G_{j}
.)

Example 4.18
.

If
G=\left(45,123\right)
(we omit the commas and the set braces, so for example
45
means
\left\{4,5\right\}
), then the faces
\left(5,4,123\right)
,
\left(123,4,5\right)
,
\left(45,2,13\right)
,
\left(5,1,2,4,3\right)
(and several others) are contained in
G
, while
\left(5,134,2\right)
is not (as are many others).

Clearly, the relation
\twoheadrightarrow
is transitive and reflexive.
It is not antisymmetric, because reordering the blocks of a face
F
yields a new face
G
that satisfies
F\twoheadrightarrow G\twoheadrightarrow F
.
More generally, the relation
F\twoheadrightarrow G
depends only on the
set
of blocks of
F
and the
set
of blocks of
G
, not on their respective orders.
Thus, the relation
\twoheadrightarrow
is better understood as a relation between set partitions (known as the
refinement order
of set partitions), not set compositions.
However, we will find it useful on set compositions.

If a face
F
is contained in a face
G
, then
G
can be obtained from
F
by merging and reordering its blocks. What blocks are merged and where they are placed can be encoded by a set composition of
\left[\ell\left(F\right)\right]
, which is obtained from
F
by renaming the blocks of
F
as
1,2,\ldots,\ell\left(F\right)
from left to right.
For example, for
F=\left(123,4,5\right)
and
G=\left(45,123\right)
, we can obtain
G
from
F
by merging the last two blocks and swapping the result with the first block; this is encoded by the set composition
\left(23,1\right)
of

6
Alternating sum identities for set compositions

To improve on the polynomial in Theorem
5.7
,
we need two combinatorial identities regarding set compositions:

Lemma 6.1
.

We have

\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}}\left(-1\right)^{\ell\left(G\right)}=\left(-1\right)^{n}.

Proof.

This is essentially
[
19
, Theorem 2.2.2]
(see the first paragraph in the proof of
[
19
, Theorem 2.2.2]
for an explanation).
However, let us give a new proof here, using the tools of this paper.

Consider the composition
\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)

of
n
(with
n
many
1
’s).
The definition of
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}

yields

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F};\\
=\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)\end{subarray}}F=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{F};\\
=\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)\end{subarray}}A.

(26)

One of the addends in this sum is the face

\left(\left\{1\right\},\left\{2\right\},\ldots,\left\{n\right\}\right)\in\mathcal{F}
.
Hence, the coefficient of this face in

\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}
is
1
.

Set

\lambda:=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(F\right)}\in\mathbb{Z}.

(27)

Let
A\in\mathcal{F}
be a face of type
\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)\models n
. (Note that there are
n!
such faces, each of them having the form

\left(\left\{\sigma\left(1\right)\right\},\left\{\sigma\left(2\right)\right\},\ldots,\left\{\sigma\left(n\right)\right\}\right)

for some permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
.)
Then, the blocks of
A
have size
1
, and therefore
cannot be subdivided any further by intersecting
them with the blocks of another face
F\in\mathcal{F}
. Hence,

AF=A\qquad\text{for each }F\in\mathcal{F}.

(28)

But Proposition
4.14
yields

\displaystyle A\widetilde{w}_{0}

\displaystyle=A\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(F\right)}F=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(F\right)}\underbrace{AF}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=A\\
\text{(by \eqref{pf.lem.altsum.faces-all.1})}\end{subarray}}=\underbrace{\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(F\right)}}_{=\lambda}A=\lambda A.

Forget that we fixed
A
. We thus have proved the equality

A\widetilde{w}_{0}=\lambda A
for each face

A\in\mathcal{F}
of type
\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)\models n
.
Summing this equality over all such faces

, we obtain

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{F};\\
=\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)\end{subarray}}A\widetilde{w}_{0}=\lambda\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{F};\\
=\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)\end{subarray}}A.

(29)

Using (
26
), we can rewrite
this as

\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}\widetilde{w}_{0}=\lambda\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}.

On the other hand,

\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)=\operatorname{gaps}\left(\left[n-1\right]\right)
, so that

\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}=\mathbf{B}_{\left[n-1\right]}=\sum_{w\in S_{n}}w

(by (
3
)), and therefore

w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}=w_{0}\sum_{w\in S_{n}}w=\sum_{w\in S_{n}}w_{0}w=\sum_{w\in S_{n}}w

(here, we have substituted
w
for
w_{0}w
in the
sum). Comparing these two equalities, we obtain

w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}=\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}
.
Applying the map
\rho
to this equality, we find

\rho\left(w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}\right)=\rho\left(\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}\right)=\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}\qquad\left(\text{by
the definition of }\rho\right),

so that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}

\displaystyle=\rho\left(w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\rho\left(\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}\right)}_{=\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}}\underbrace{\rho\left(w_{0}\right)}_{=\widetilde{w}_{0}}\qquad\left(\text{since }\rho\text{ is a }\mathbf{k}\text{-algebra anti-morphism}\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}\widetilde{w}_{0}=\lambda\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}.

Comparing coefficients in front of the face

\left(\left\{1\right\},\left\{2\right\},\ldots,\left\{n\right\}\right)\in\mathcal{F}

in this equality, we obtain
1=\lambda

(since the coefficient of this face in

\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\left(1,1,\ldots,1\right)}
is
1
).
Thus,
1=\lambda=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(F\right)}

(by the definition of
\lambda
). Dividing this
equality by
\left(-1\right)^{n}
,
we find
\left(-1\right)^{n}=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\left(-1\right)^{\ell\left(F\right)}=\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}}\left(-1\right)^{\ell\left(G\right)}
, qed.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{F}
be a face. Then,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\in\mathcal{F};\\
F\twoheadrightarrow G\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(G\right)}=\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(F\right)}.

Proof.

Write the set composition
F
as
F=\left(F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{m}\right)
,
so that
\ell\left(F\right)=m
.
Then,
Proposition
4.19

shows that there is a length-preserving bijection

\displaystyle f:\left\{G\in\mathcal{F}:F\twoheadrightarrow G\right\}

\displaystyle\to\left\{H:H\models\left[m\right]\right\},

\displaystyle\left(G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{k}\right)\text{ where }H_{i}=\left\{j\in\left[m\right]:F_{j}\subseteq G_{i}\right\}.

Using this bijection, we can reindex the sum

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\in\mathcal{F};\\
F\twoheadrightarrow G\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(G\right)}
as follows:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\in\mathcal{F};\\
F\twoheadrightarrow G\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(G\right)}=\sum_{H\models\left[m\right]}\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(H\right)}=\sum_{G\models\left[m\right]}\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(G\right)}=\left(-1\right)^{n}\sum_{G\models\left[m\right]}\left(-1\right)^{\ell\left(G\right)}.

However, applying Lemma
6.1
to
m

instead of
n
, we find

\sum_{G\models\left[m\right]}\left(-1\right)^{\ell\left(G\right)}=\left(-1\right)^{m}.

In light of this, the previous equality simplifies to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\in\mathcal{F};\\
F\twoheadrightarrow G\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(G\right)}=\left(-1\right)^{n}\left(-1\right)^{m}=\left(-1\right)^{n-m}=\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(F\right)}

(since
m=\ell\left(F\right)
),
as desired.
∎

Recall that

is a fixed composition of
n
.

)

,

(meaning that – at least when

First, we need some more notations.

Definition 7.1
.

(a)

The

\displaystyle\widetilde{n}_{\alpha}\left(F\right)=\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(F\right)}n_{\alpha}\left(F\right).

(b)

Note that each face

(by (

(b)
for the latter equality).

Our main result takes the following form
(recall that
\alpha
is a fixed composition of
n
):

.

The elements

and

(resp.
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}w_{0}-k

The proof of this will rely on a counterpart to Lemma
5.9
:

Lemma 7.3
.

Let

\mathbf{F}\left(\widetilde{w}_{0}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\alpha}-k\right)\in\mathbf{k}\mathcal{F}_{k_{i+1}}.

(a)

8
Identifying the minimal polynomial

Recall that if
\mathbf{k}
is a field, and if
a
is an element of a

\mathbf{k}
-algebra
A
, then the
minimal polynomial
of
a

(over
\mathbf{k}
) means the smallest-degree monic polynomial

p\in\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
satisfying
p\left(a\right)=0
.
This always exists when
A
is finite-dimensional over
\mathbf{k}
. In
particular, if
A
is a matrix ring
\mathbf{k}^{r\times r}
, then this is
just the minimal polynomial of a matrix.

In this section,
we shall assume that
\mathbf{k}
is a field of
characteristic
0
. Note that the minimal polynomial of an element

a
of a
\mathbf{k}
-algebra
A
does not change if we replace
A

by a larger algebra that contains
A
as a subalgebra. Thus, we shall denote
this minimal polynomial by
\mu\left(a\right)
without explicitly
specifying
A
.

If two elements of a
\mathbf{k}
-algebra are conjugate, then they have the
same minimal polynomials. Thus,

\mu\left(\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}w_{0}\right)=\mu\left(w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}\right),

since the two elements
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}w_{0}
and
w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
of
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
are conjugate
(indeed, conjugating
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}w_{0}
by
w_{0}
yields

w_{0}\left(\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}w_{0}\right)w_{0}^{-1}=w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
). This minimal polynomial

\mu\left(\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}w_{0}\right)=\mu\left(w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}\right)
will be the focus of our study
in this section.

Injective
\mathbf{k}
-algebra morphisms preserve minimal polynomials: That
is, if
f:A\to B
is an injective
\mathbf{k}
-algebra morphism, and
if
a\in A
has a minimal polynomial, then

f\left(a\right)
has the same minimal polynomial as
a

(that is,
\mu\left(f\left(a\right)\right)=\mu\left(a\right)
).
This is because each polynomial
p\in\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
satisfies

Assume that
\mathbf{k}

Lemma 8.3

(More blocks lemma)
.

Let

F\cdot\widetilde{w}_{0}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\alpha}=\widetilde{n}_{\alpha}\left(F\right)F+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\in\mathcal{F};\\
\ell\left(G\right)>\ell\left(F\right)\end{subarray}}c_{G}G\qquad\text{for some scalars $c_{G}\in\mathbf{k}$.}

Let

\mathbf{k}I

5.4

4.20

(d)

4.9

\displaystyle F\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=F\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\in\mathcal{F};\\

=\alpha\end{subarray}}FG

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\in\mathcal{F};\\

F\twoheadrightarrow G\end{subarray}}\ \ \underbrace{FG}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=F\\

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field.
Let
A
be a
\mathbf{k}
-algebra. Let
a\in A
be an element
that has a minimal polynomial.
Let

\displaystyle R_{a}:A

\displaystyle\to A,

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto xa

be the map known as “right multiplication by
a

This

is a
\mathbf{k}
-linear map, i.e., belongs to
the endomorphism ring

.
Then,

\mu\left(R_{a}\right)=\mu\left(a\right).

Proof.

Forget that we fixed
a
.
We thus have defined an endomorphism

for each

.
The map

that sends each

to this endomorphism

is easily seen to be

\mathbf{k}

(injective because

Thus, (

) yields

In other words,

Proof of Theorem
8.2
.

Let us denote this operator by
\Phi
. Define a polynomial

f\left(x\right):=\prod_{k\in\widetilde{n}_{\alpha}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)}\left(x-k\right)\in\mathbf{k}\left[x\right].

This polynomial factors into distinct monic linear factors
(distinct because
\mathbf{k}
has characteristic
0
).
Clearly, (
38
) says that

f\left(\widetilde{w}_{0}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\alpha}\right)=0
.
Thus, the minimal polynomial
\mu\left(\widetilde{w}_{0}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\alpha}\right)

must divide
f
(since the minimal polynomial

\mu\left(a\right)
of an element

of a
\mathbf{k}
-algebra always
divides any polynomial that annihilates
a
).

As we recall, the operator
\Phi
sends each element

\mathbf{F}\in\mathbf{k}\mathcal{F}
to
\mathbf{F}\widetilde{w}_{0}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\alpha}
.
In other words, this operator
\Phi
is
right multiplication by
\widetilde{w}_{0}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\alpha}
.
Hence, Lemma
8.4
(applied to

A=\mathbf{k}\mathcal{F}
and

)
shows that

\mu\left(\Phi\right)=\mu\left(\widetilde{w}_{0}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\alpha}\right)
.
Hence, the polynomial
\mu\left(\Phi\right)
divides
f

(since the polynomial
\mu\left(\widetilde{w}_{0}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\alpha}\right)

divides
f
).

Therefore, this polynomial
\mu\left(\Phi\right)

factors into distinct monic linear factors
(because the same is true of

).
Hence, the operator

\Phi
is diagonalizable (because
\mathbf{k}\mathcal{F}

\mathbf{k}
-vector space, and thus

The faces in

form a basis of the

-vector space
\mathbf{k}\mathcal{F}
.
Let us order this basis by increasing length of

Then, the operator
\Phi
is represented by a

gives an expansion of

But the eigenvalues of a lower-triangular matrix are

In our case, the diagonal entries of the matrix

are the numbers

. Hence, the eigenvalues of

8.2
.
∎

.

9
Application to top-to-random-and-reverse

In this section, we shall focus on the case when
\alpha
is the composition
\left(1,n-1\right)

(for
n>1
).
In this case,
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}
is the image of the
top-to-random shuffle
under the
antipode
map of the symmetric group algebra
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
.
We recall their definitions:

Definition 9.1
.

The
top-to-random shuffle
is the element

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{cyc}_{1,2,\ldots,i}\in\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right],

(43)

where
\operatorname{cyc}_{k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{m}}
denotes the
m
-cycle in
S_{n}
that sends
k_{1}\mapsto k_{2}\mapsto\cdots\mapsto k_{m}\mapsto k_{1}
.

Definition 9.2
.

The
antipode
of the symmetric group algebra
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
is the
\mathbf{k}
-linear map
S:\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]\to\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
that sends each basis vector (permutation)
w\in S_{n}
to
w^{-1}
.
It is well-known that
S
is a
\mathbf{k}
-algebra anti-isomorphism.

Now, our above claim about
\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}
can be stated as follows:

Proposition 9.3
.

We have

S\left(\mathbf{A}\right)=\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}.

Proof.

This is well-known, but we give a proof for completeness.
By definition of
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}_{\operatorname{gaps}^{-1}\left(1,n-1\right)}=\mathbf{B}_{\left\{1\right\}}\qquad\left(\text{since }\operatorname{gaps}^{-1}\left(1,n-1\right)=\left\{1\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in S_{n};\\
\operatorname{Des}w\subseteq\left\{1\right\}\end{subarray}}w.

(44)

But the permutations
w\in S_{n}
that satisfy
\operatorname{Des}w\subseteq\left\{1\right\}
are precisely those permutations that satisfy

w\left(2\right)<w\left(3\right)<\cdots<w\left(n\right)
; in other
words, they consist of an arbitrary element
i\in\left[n\right]
in their
first position, followed by the remaining elements of
\left[n\right]
in
increasing order. Such permutations can be written as cycles

\operatorname{cyc}_{i,i-1,\ldots,2,1}
. Hence, we can rewrite
(
44
) as

\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{cyc}_{i,i-1,\ldots,2,1}.

On the other hand, the definition of
\mathbf{A}
yields

S\left(\mathbf{A}\right)=S\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{cyc}_{1,2,\ldots,i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{cyc}_{1,2,\ldots,i}^{-1},

since the antipode
S
sends each permutation to its inverse. The right hand
sides of these two equalities are equal, since

\operatorname{cyc}_{i,i-1,\ldots,2,1}=\operatorname{cyc}_{1,2,\ldots,i}^{-1}
for each

i
. Therefore, the left hand sides are equal as well. In other words,

\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}=S\left(\mathbf{A}\right)
.
∎

Since the antipode
S
is a
\mathbf{k}
-algebra anti-morphism, we have

\displaystyle S\left(\mathbf{A}w_{0}\right)=S\left(w_{0}\right)S\left(\mathbf{A}\right)=w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}

(45)

(since
S\left(w_{0}\right)=w_{0}^{-1}=w_{0}
and
S\left(\mathbf{A}\right)=\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}
by Proposition
9.3
).
Thus, if
\mathbf{k}
is a field of characteristic
0
,
then the element
w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}=S\left(\mathbf{A}w_{0}\right)
has the same minimal polynomial as
\mathbf{A}w_{0}

(by (
39
), since
S:\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]\to\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
is an algebra anti-isomorphism).
From Theorem
8.1
, this minimal polynomial is

\mu\left(w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}\right)=\prod_{k\in\widetilde{n}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)}\left(x-k\right).

To make this concrete, we will determine the set

\widetilde{n}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)
indexing this product.

Proposition 9.4
.

Assume that
n>1
. Then, the set
\widetilde{n}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)
is the set

L\left(n\right):=\left\{-n+2\right\}\cup\left[-n+4,n-3\right]\cup\left\{0\right\}\cup\left\{n\right\}.

Once this proposition is proved, we will be able to conclude:

.

Assume that
\mathbf{k}
is a field of characteristic
0
,
and that
n>1
.
Then, the elements
w_{0}\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}w_{0}
of
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
have minimal polynomial

Proof.

The elements
w_{0}\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}w_{0}
are conjugate (via conjugation by
w_{0}
), and thus have the same minimal polynomial.
That is,
\mu\left(w_{0}\mathbf{A}\right)=\mu\left(\mathbf{A}w_{0}\right)
.
Furthermore,
S:\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]\to\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
is a
\mathbf{k}
-algebra anti-isomorphism.
Hence, (
39
) yields

\mu\left(S\left(\mathbf{A}w_{0}\right)\right)=\mu\left(\mathbf{A}w_{0}\right)
.
By (
45
), this rewrites as

\mu\left(w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}\right)=\mu\left(\mathbf{A}w_{0}\right)
.
But by Theorem
8.1
, the minimal polynomial of

\mu\left(w_{0}\mathbf{B}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}\right)=\prod_{k\in\widetilde{n}_{\left(1,n-1\right)}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)}\left(x-k\right)=\prod_{k\in L\left(n\right)}\left(x-k\right)\quad\left(\text{by Proposition \ref{prop.1n-1.specs}}\right).

Comparing these two equalities, we find

. Together with

∎

It remains to prove Proposition
9.4
. We begin with a definition.

Definition 9.6

(Singleton blocks)
.

For each face
F=\left(F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{k}\right)\in\mathcal{F}
,
we set

\displaystyle\operatorname{Sin}F

\displaystyle:=\left\{F_{i}:\left|F_{i}\right|=1\right\}=\left\{\text{all blocks of $F$ that have size $1$}\right\}\qquad\text{ and }

\displaystyle:=\left|\operatorname{Sin}F\right|.

Thus,

is the number of all size-

.

Example 9.7
.

The set composition
\left(4,25,1,7,36\right)
of
\left[7\right]
has singleton blocks
\left\{4\right\}
,
\left\{1\right\}
,
\left\{7\right\}
, so we have
\operatorname{Sin}\left(4,25,1,7,36\right)=\left\{\left\{4\right\},\left\{1\right\},\left\{7\right\}\right\}
and

.

Lemma 9.8
.

Consider the composition
\left(1,n-1\right)

For each

, we have

\displaystyle n_{\left(1,n-1\right)}\left(F\right)

\displaystyle=\sin F\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle=\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(F\right)}\sin F.

Let
\alpha=\left(1,n-1\right)
.
Fix
F\in\mathcal{F}
.

The faces

having type

Hence, in particular, all the faces

have this form.

Moreover, if a face

is written as

, so that each block of

in other words, each block of

Thus, it must satisfy
\left\{k\right\}\in\operatorname{Sin}F

Hence, we can define a map

\displaystyle N_{\alpha}\left(F\right)

\displaystyle\to\operatorname{Sin}F,

\displaystyle\left(\left\{k\right\},\left[n\right]\setminus\left\{k\right\}\right)

).
Hence, it is bijective.
Thus,
\left|N_{\alpha}\left(F\right)\right|=\left|\operatorname{Sin}F\right|=\sin F
, so that

\displaystyle n_{\alpha}\left(F\right)=\left|N_{\alpha}\left(F\right)\right|=\sin F.

This proves (

) (since

).
Moreover, the definition of

shows that

\displaystyle\widetilde{n}_{\alpha}\left(F\right)=\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(F\right)}n_{\alpha}\left(F\right)=\left(-1\right)^{n-\ell\left(F\right)}\sin F

(49)

(by (

)).
This proves (
47
), and thus completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
9.4
.

We assume that
n>3
; the other cases (

10
Positive linear combinations

Our above results can be generalized with fairly little effort, from single elements
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
to their linear combinations
\sum_{\alpha\models n}\gamma_{\alpha}\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}\in D\left(S_{n}\right)
.
However, since the proofs use inequalities between the knapsack numbers
n_{\alpha}\left(F\right)
(such as Proposition
4.26
), we cannot let the coefficients
\gamma_{\alpha}
be arbitrary.
It turns out that requiring them to be nonnegative reals is sufficient.
This idea goes back to Brown
[
4
, Theorem 5]
and was stated explicitly for the
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
by Schocker
[
21
, Theorem 4.1]
, but we shall now extend it to

and prove it anew.

Instead of fixing a composition
\alpha\models n
,

we now fix a family

of nonnegative real numbers
\gamma_{\alpha}\geq 0
indexed by the compositions
\alpha
of
n
.
We assume that the ring
\mathbf{k}
is ordered and contains these numbers
\gamma_{\alpha}
(for instance,
\mathbf{k}=\mathbb{R}
, or
\mathbf{k}=\mathbb{Q}
if these numbers are rational).

We define the element

\mathbf{B}_{\bm{\gamma}}:=\sum_{\alpha\models n}\gamma_{\alpha}\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}\in D\left(S_{n}\right).

For each face
F\in\mathcal{F}
, we define the
\bm{\gamma}
-weighted knapsack number of

and we define the
\bm{\gamma}

to be the number

\widetilde{n}_{\bm{\gamma}}\left(F\right):=\sum_{\alpha\models n}\gamma_{\alpha}\widetilde{n}_{\alpha}\left(F\right)\in\mathbf{k}.

We set

and
\widetilde{n}_{\bm{\gamma}}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)=\left\{\widetilde{n}_{\bm{\gamma}}\left(F\right)\mid F\in\mathcal{F}\right\}

Now, we claim the following generalizations of Theorem

and Theorem

Theorem 10.1
.

(50)

. In other words,
\left(H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{k}\right)\models\left[m\right]
.
This shows that
f
is well-defined.

2.

To see that
g
is well-defined, we let
H=\left(H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{k}\right)\in B
, that is,
\left(H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{k}\right)\models\left[m\right]
.
We define the set
G_{i}=\bigcup_{j\in H_{i}}F_{j}
for each
i\in\left[k\right]
.
We must show that
\left(G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{k}\right)\in A
, that is, we must show that
\left(G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{k}\right)\in\mathcal{F}
and
F\twoheadrightarrow\left(G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{k}\right)
.

Each of the sets
G_{i}
is nonempty, since it is a nonempty union of nonempty sets (as each
H_{i}
and each
F_{j}
are nonempty).
Furthermore, their definition shows that

\bigcup_{i\in\left[k\right]}G_{i}=\bigcup_{i\in\left[k\right]}\bigcup_{j\in H_{i}}F_{j}=\bigcup_{j\in\left[m\right]}F_{j}=\left[n\right]
,
where the second equality sign is a consequence of the fact that each
j\in\left[m\right]
belongs to some
H_{i}
.
Finally, the sets
G_{i}
are disjoint, since each element of
\left[n\right]
belongs to exactly one
F_{j}
, and since the corresponding
j
belongs to exactly one
H_{i}
.
These three facts together show that
\left(G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{k}\right)\models\left[n\right]
, that is,
\left(G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{k}\right)\in\mathcal{F}
.

It remains to show that
F\twoheadrightarrow\left(G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{k}\right)
.
This is again clear: Each block
F_{j}
of
F
is a subset of a block of
G
(namely, of
G_{i}
, where
i
is such that
j\in H_{i}
; this
i
exists because
H
is a set composition of
\left[m\right]
).

Altogether, we have thus shown that
\left(G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{k}\right)\in A
.
The map
g
is therefore well-defined.

Now we show that these maps
f
and
g
are mutually inverse:

1.

To show that
g\circ f=\operatorname{id}
, we must prove that
g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)=G
for each set composition
G=\left(G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{k}\right)\in A
. So let us fix such set composition
G
. Let
i\in\left[k\right]
.
Then, the
i
-th block of
f\left(G\right)
is
f\left(G\right)_{i}=\left\{j\in\left[m\right]:F_{j}\subseteq G_{i}\right\}
(by the definition of
f
).
Hence, the
i
-th block of
g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)
is in turn

\displaystyle g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)_{i}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{j\in f\left(G\right)_{i}}F_{j}=\bigcup_{j\in\left[m\right]\text{ satisfies }F_{j}\subseteq G_{i}}\underbrace{F_{j}}_{\subseteq G_{i}}\subseteq G_{i}.

Now, let us prove the reverse inclusion
G_{i}\subseteq g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)_{i}
.
Indeed, let
x\in G_{i}
.
Since
g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)
is a set composition of
\left[n\right]
, we have
\left[n\right]=\bigcup_{s\in\left[k\right]}g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)_{s}
.
Hence,
x\in G_{i}\subseteq\left[n\right]=\bigcup_{s\in\left[k\right]}g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)_{s}
.
In other words,
x\in g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)_{s}
for some
s\in\left[k\right]
.
Consider this
s
.
Just as we showed
g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)_{i}\subseteq G_{i}
, we see that
g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)_{s}\subseteq G_{s}
.
Hence,
x\in g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)_{s}\subseteq G_{s}
.
Combined with
x\in G_{i}
, this leads to
x\in G_{i}\cap G_{s}
, so that
G_{i}\cap G_{s}\neq\varnothing
.
But this is only possible if
i=s
, since otherwise the blocks
G_{i}
and
G_{s}
would be disjoint.
Hence, we have
i=s
. Thus, from
x\in g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)_{s}
, we obtain
x\in g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)_{i}
.
Since we have proved this for each
x\in G_{i}
, we thus conclude that
G_{i}\subseteq g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)_{i}
. Combined with
g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)_{i}\subseteq G_{i}
, this shows that
g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)_{i}=G_{i}
.

So we have shown that
g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)_{i}=G_{i}
for each
i\in\left[k\right]
.
In other words,
g\left(f\left(G\right)\right)=G
. Hence,
g\circ f=\operatorname{id}
is proved.

2.

Next, we show that
f\circ g=\operatorname{id}
.
Indeed, let
H=\left(H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{k}\right)\models\left[m\right]
. Then, for each
i\in\left[k\right]
, we have
(using the definitions of
f
and
g
as before)

\displaystyle f\left(g\left(H\right)\right)_{i}

\displaystyle=\left\{j\in\left[m\right]:F_{j}\subseteq g\left(H\right)_{i}\right\}=\left\{p\in\left[m\right]:F_{p}\subseteq g\left(H\right)_{i}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{p\in\left[m\right]:F_{p}\subseteq\bigcup_{j\in H_{i}}F_{j}\right\}=H_{i}

(since the blocks
F_{j}
of
F
are disjoint and nonempty, so that the only blocks
F_{p}
that are subsets of the union
\bigcup_{j\in H_{i}}F_{j}
are the very addends of this union).
That is,
f\left(g\left(H\right)\right)=H
.
This shows that
f\circ g=\operatorname{id}
.

Thus,
f
and
g
are mutually inverse, and hence are bijections.
These bijections
f
and
g
are obviously length-preserving.
In particular,
f
is a length-preserving bijection.
This proves Proposition
4.19
, since our
f
is precisely the
f
defined in the proposition.
∎

Proof of Proposition

.

Write the faces

and

in the forms

and

.
Thus, of course,
\ell\left(F\right)=k
and
\ell\left(G\right)=m
.

1.

(b)
Each block of

for some

and

).

(namely, of

)
and a subset of a block of
G
(namely, of

).
Thus,
FG\twoheadrightarrow F
and

, as desired.

2.

(c)
“If”: Assume that

is contained in

.
Then, each block

of

is a subset of some block

of

,
and therefore disjoint from all the other blocks of

(since the blocks of

),
whereas all other intersections

with

But the definition of

since the reduction operation removes all the empty intersections

and leaves only the nonempty intersections

around. But since the latter nonempty

This proves the “if” direction.

“Only if”: Assume that

. But part
(b)
shows
that

is contained in
G
.
Hence,

is contained in
G
(since

).
This proves the “only if” direction.

3.

(d)
The blocks of

are the nonempty intersections of the

(by the definition of
FG
).
Each block
F_{i}
of

gives rise to at least one

of

).
In other words,

has at least

In other words,

.

It remains to show that this inequality becomes an equality if and only if

is contained in
G
.

“If”: Assume that
F
is contained in
G
. Then, part
(c)

shows that

. Hence,

. Thus, equality

“Only if”: Now assume that

.
Then, there is a block of

– say,

– that is not a subset of

. Hence,

. But the blocks of

are the nonempty intersections of the

of

gives rise to at least one

but the block

of

. In other words,

.
Hence, equality does not hold in

.
The proof of part
(d)
is thus complete.

4.

(e)
This is similar to the proof of

in part

.
(This time, we have to argue that each block
G_{j}
of

gives rise to at least one nonempty intersection of the

F_{i}\cap G_{j}
.)
Alternatively, we can apply part
(d)
with the
roles of

and

interchanged, and then observe that

\ell\left(FG\right)=\ell\left(GF\right)
because the set compositions

and
GF
differ only in the order of their blocks.

5.

(a)
Assume that

is contained in

.
Then,

by part
(c)
.
But part
(e)

.
Since

, we can rewrite this as

.

A.3
Proof of Theorem
4.12

We shall now give a self-contained proof of Theorem
4.12
.
This is essentially just
Bidigare’s proof (
[
1
, Theorem 3.8.1]
,

[
20
, proof of Theorem 2.1]
,
[
14
, Theorem 1]
, etc.),
restated in our language for the sake of consistency.

We begin with a lemma that is so simple that its usefulness is hard to
believe. It is implicit in most expositions of Theorem
4.12
,
but in our view it is worth isolating, as it makes the rest of the proof more transparent.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
B
and
C
be two monoids (written
multiplicatively).
Let
f:B\to C
be any map.

Let
S
be a set on which both monoids
B
and
C
act from the left. Let

s\in S
be an element that generates a free
C
-orbit – i.e., that has the
property that

\text{if }c_{1},c_{2}\in C\text{ satisfy }c_{1}s=c_{2}s\text{, then }c_{1}=c_{2}\text{.}

(56)

Assume furthermore that

b\left(cs\right)=c\left(bs\right)\qquad\text{for all }b\in B\text{ and }c\in C.

(57)

Assume moreover that

bs=f\left(b\right)s\qquad\text{for each }b\in B.

(58)

Then,
f
is a monoid anti-morphism, i.e., it satisfies

f\left(ab\right)=f\left(b\right)f\left(a\right)\qquad\text{for all }a,b\in B

and
f\left(1_{B}\right)=1_{C}
.

Proof.

From (
58
), we have
1_{B}s=f\left(1_{B}\right)s
, so that
f\left(1_{B}\right)s=1_{B}s=s=1_{C}s
. Thus,
(
56
) (applied to
c_{1}=f\left(1_{B}\right)
and

c_{2}=1_{C}
) yields
f\left(1_{B}\right)=1_{C}
.

It thus remains to show that
f\left(ab\right)=f\left(b\right)f\left(a\right)
for all
a,b\in B
.

To prove this, we fix any
a,b\in B
. Then, (
58
)
(applied to
ab
instead of
b
) shows that
abs=f\left(ab\right)s
.
However, (
58
) also yields
bs=f\left(b\right)s

and
as=f\left(a\right)s
.

On the other hand, (
57
) (applied to
a
and
f\left(b\right)
instead of
b
and
c
) shows that
a\left(f\left(b\right)s\right)=f\left(b\right)\left(as\right)
. In view of
bs=f\left(b\right)s
, we can rewrite this as
a\left(bs\right)=f\left(b\right)\underbrace{\left(as\right)}_{=f\left(a\right)s}=f\left(b\right)f\left(a\right)s
. Comparing this with
a\left(bs\right)=abs=f\left(ab\right)s
, we find
f\left(ab\right)s=f\left(b\right)f\left(a\right)s
. Therefore, (
56
) (applied to

c_{1}=f\left(ab\right)
and
c_{2}=f\left(b\right)f\left(a\right)
) yields
f\left(ab\right)=f\left(b\right)f\left(a\right)
. This
completes our proof of Lemma
A.1
.
∎

.

In particular,

(59)

where the action of

A.4
The fixed points of a permutation module

Let us prove the following general fact (
[
17
, §3.3.1, “Invariants of Permutation Representations”]
) about permutation modules (which we have used in the proof of Proposition
4.10
):

Proposition A.2
.

Let
G
be a group, and let
X
be a finite left
G
-set.
Consider the invariant subspace
\left(\mathbf{k}X\right)^{G}:=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{k}X:w\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}\text{ for all }w\in G\right\}
of the permutation module
\mathbf{k}X
.

For each orbit
\mathcal{O}
of the
G
-action on
X
, let
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{O}}x
be the sum of all elements of this orbit in
\mathbf{k}X
. This sum is called an

.

Then, the invariant subspace
\left(\mathbf{k}X\right)^{G}
(as a
\mathbf{k}
-module) has a basis consisting of the orbit sums (i.e., of the sums
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{O}}x
for each orbit
\mathcal{O}
of the
G
-action on
X
).

Proof.

This fact is proved by combining three simple observations:

1.

Each orbit sum
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{O}}x
belongs to
\left(\mathbf{k}X\right)^{G}
, because the action of a
w\in G
on this sum merely permutes its addends:
w\sum_{x\in\mathcal{O}}x=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{O}}wx=\sum_{x\in w\mathcal{O}}x=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{O}}x
because
w\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}
.

2.

If an element
\mathbf{x}=\sum_{x\in X}\lambda_{x}x\in\mathbf{k}X
(with coefficients
\lambda_{x}\in\mathbf{k}
) belongs to
\left(\mathbf{k}X\right)^{G}
, then every two elements
x,y\in X
that lie in the same
G
-orbit have the same coefficient (i.e., satisfy
\lambda_{x}=\lambda_{y}
), because the action of an appropriately chosen
w\in G
puts
\lambda_{x}
in the position of
\lambda_{y}
.
Thus, given such an element
\mathbf{x}\in\left(\mathbf{k}X\right)^{G}
, we can rename its coefficients
\lambda_{x}
as
\lambda_{Gx}
, where
Gx
(as usual) denotes the
G
-orbit containing
x
.
Hence, we can rewrite
\mathbf{x}=\sum_{x\in X}\lambda_{x}x
as
\mathbf{x}=\sum_{x\in X}\lambda_{Gx}x=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\text{ is a $G$-orbit}}\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{O}}x\right)
, which shows that
\mathbf{x}
is a linear combination of orbit sums.
Hence, the orbit sums span
\left(\mathbf{k}X\right)^{G}
.

3.

The orbit sums
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{O}}x
are
\mathbf{k}
-linearly independent, since any
\mathbf{k}
-linear combination
\sum_{\mathcal{O}\text{ is a $G$-orbit}}\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{O}}x\right)
can be rewritten as
\sum_{x\in X}\lambda_{Gx}x
and thus cannot be
0
unless all the
\lambda_{Gx}
are
0
, that is, unless all the
\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}
are
0
.

Thus, the orbit sums form a basis of
\left(\mathbf{k}X\right)^{G}
.
