Relaxing Positional Alignment in Masked Diffusion Language Models
Mengyu Ye, Ryosuke Takahashi, Keito Kudo, Jun Suzuki

Abstract
Masked diffusion language models (MDLMs) have emerged as a promising alternative to dominant autoregressive approaches. Although they achieve competitive performance on several tasks, a substantial gap remains in open-ended text generation. We hypothesize that one cause of this gap is that strict positional prediction makes MDLM decoding highly sensitive to token misalignment, and we show through controlled interventions that a one-position shift can severely disrupt semantics. This observation suggests that enforcing strict positional supervision during training is misaligned with the irreversible denoising dynamics of MDLM decoding. Motivated by this mismatch, we adopt an alignment-flexible supervision strategy during fine-tuning. Specifically, we introduce a special token <slack> via the connectionist temporal classification objective. We apply this approach to the widely used MDLM and conduct experiments on five open-ended text generation benchmarks. Our method consistently outperforms the original model and improves robustness to positional shifts, indicating that relaxing strict positional supervision is an important factor in improving generation quality in MDLMs.

1
Introduction

Diffusion models have been applied to discrete data such as text
(
Sahoo et al. 2024
;
Arriola et al. 2025
;
Nie et al. 2025a
;
Zhu et al. 2025
;
Ye et al. 2025
)
.
Diffusion-based approaches support flexible token update orders, multi-token unmasking, bidirectional context modeling, and flexible conditioning.
These properties make them an appealing alternative to standard language modeling with autoregressive language models
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
;
Brown et al. 2020
)
.

Masked diffusion language models (MDLMs) are among the most widely used diffusion-based approaches for text generation.
Recent works such as the LLaDA family
(
Nie et al. 2025b
;
Zhu et al. 2025
)
have scaled MDLMs to 8B parameters, achieving performance comparable to similarly sized autoregressive models on several benchmarks.
However, these evaluations have focused on tasks assessed using exact token- or string-level matching, such as GPQA
(
Rein et al. 2024
)
, MMLU
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021b
;
Hendrycks et al. 2021a
)
, and MBPP
(
Austin et al. 2021
)
.
In contrast, benchmarks requiring open-ended generation and coherent responses, such as Arena-Hard
(
Li et al. 2025b
;
Li et al. 2024
)
and MTBench
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
, remain challenging for MDLMs.
This discrepancy suggests, despite recent progress, open-ended text generation remains difficult for current MDLMs.

We argue that an overlooked limitation of MDLMs in open-ended generation, particularly under standard diffusion decoding settings as used by the LLaDA family, is sensitivity to token misalignment caused by strict positional alignment between target tokens and output positions.
Text is often highly position-sensitive, and even a single misplaced token can severely disrupt semantic coherence.
This issue is further exacerbated in typical MDLM decoding because once a token is unmasked, it is committed and cannot be revised.
As a result, early local errors cannot be corrected and may persist across subsequent denoising steps, leading to global degradation in generation quality
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
.

We empirically demonstrate that repeated one-position shift interventions during MDLM generation, despite preserving local token content, cause a substantial drop in generation quality on open-ended benchmarks.
This suggests that a major source of error is not lexical choice, but rather that enforcing strict one-to-one positional supervision during training is misaligned with the irreversible nature of MDLM decoding.

Prior work has shown that positional misalignment can be harmful in settings where it is induced by task constraints, such as length-mismatched infilling (e.g., DDOT
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
, DreamOn
(
Wu et al. 2025b
)
).
In contrast, we show that positional misalignment can arise intrinsically during standard open-ended MDLM decoding, even when no explicit length or alignment mismatch is imposed.

To address this issue, we relax the strict requirement that each token must be generated at an exact position.
Specifically, we introduce an alignment-flexible sequence objective based on connectionist temporal classification (CTC), alongside the standard cross-entropy loss during supervised fine-tuning (SFT).
This objective allows the model to produce an auxiliary
<slack>
token that absorbs positional uncertainty in the alignment between the target sequence and the model’s output positions.
These tokens are removed after decoding and do not appear in the final output.
We apply this training objective to a widely used MDLM, LLaDA-8B-Instruct.
Across five open-ended text generation benchmarks, the resulting model consistently improves over both the original LLaDA-8B-Instruct and a matched baseline trained under identical settings.

Overall, this work makes three contributions.
(i) We identify position-level alignment sensitivity as a key limitation of MDLMs for open-ended text generation.
(ii) We show that adding an alignment-flexible auxiliary objective during SFT effectively relaxes positional constraints without altering the task interface.
(iii) We show that this approach improves performance across five open-ended generation benchmarks and enhances robustness to positional misalignment.

2
Masked Diffusion Language Models

MDLMs define a probability distribution over sequences through a forward noising process and a learned reverse denoising process.
Let
x_{0}=(x_{0}^{1},\dots,x_{0}^{L})
be a token sequence, whose length is
L
, and contain no masks.
The forward process independently masks each token with a probability given by a strictly decreasing function
\alpha_{t}\in[0,1]
in
t
.
Applying masks to the tokens with probability
1-\alpha_{t}
results in a partially masked sequence
x_{t}
.
When
t=1
, all tokens are masked; when
t=0
, the sequence remains unchanged.

2.1
Training

Pre-training.

The reverse process is parameterized by a mask predictor
P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{t})
, which takes the partially masked sequence
x_{t}
as input and predicts all masked tokens in parallel.
Let the indicator
\mathbf{1}_{[x_{t}^{i}=\textrm{M}]}
be 1 if the
i
-th token in
x_{t}
, namely
x_{t}^{i}
, is masked (represented by
x_{t}^{i}=\textrm{M}
), and 0 otherwise.
Then, training proceeds by sampling
t
from a uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}[0,1]
, i.e.,
t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]
, constructing
x_{t}
via the forward process, and minimizing a masked cross-entropy loss evaluated only at masked positions; thus, the pre-training objective is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{\tiny CE}}_{\theta,t}

\displaystyle\triangleq-\mathbb{E}_{t,x_{0},x_{t}}\Biggr[\gamma_{t}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}_{[x_{t}^{i}=\textrm{M}]}\log P_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{t})\Biggr],

(1)

where
\gamma_{t}=-\frac{\alpha^{\prime}_{t}}{1-\alpha_{t}}
, and
\alpha^{\prime}_{t}
represents the derivative of
\alpha_{t}
with respect to
t
.

1

1

Sahoo et al. 2024
.

Supervised fine-tuning.

SFT adapts a pre-trained MDLM to instruction-following using paired data
(q_{0},r_{0})
, where
q_{0}
denotes the prompt and
r_{0}
the corresponding response. The objective is to model the conditional distribution
P_{\theta}(r_{0}\mid q_{0})
.
Similar to pre-training, each token in
r_{0}
is independently masked with probability
\alpha_{t}
, as explained above, a monotonically decreasing function of
t
, to obtain
r_{t}
, while
q_{0}
is kept intact.
The mask predictor conditions on the concatenation of
q_{0}
and
r_{t}
to predict masked response tokens.
We then redefine the pre-training objective shown in Eq. (
1
) as the SFT objective as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{\tiny{CE}}}_{\theta,t}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{t,q_{0},r_{0},r_{t}}\Biggr[\gamma_{t}\sum_{i=1}^{L_{r}}\mathbf{1}_{[r_{t}^{i}=\textrm{M}]}\psi_{\theta}(r_{0}^{i},q_{0},r_{t})\Biggr],

(2)

where
L_{r}
denotes the response length, and

\psi_{\theta}(r_{0}^{i},q_{0},r_{t})=\log P_{\theta}(r_{0}^{i}\mid q_{0},r_{t})

.

Relation between pre-training and SFT.

Eq. (
2
) can also cover the pre-training objective if we view pre-training input token sequences as never including a prompt part
q_{0}
but only a response part; namely,
q_{0}
is always an empty sequence and
x_{t}=r_{t}
.

2.2
Inference

After training, text generation discretizes the reverse diffusion process to sample from the conditional distribution
P_{\theta}(r_{0}\mid q_{0})
.
Following standard MDLM decoding (e.g., LLaDA), we fix the response length
L_{r}
as a hyperparameter and initialize generation of
r_{t}
at
t=1
, yielding a fully masked response sequence, i.e.,

\displaystyle r_{1}\!=\!(\texttt{<MASK>},\ldots,\texttt{<MASK>})\!\in\!(\mathcal{V}\!\cup\!\{\texttt{<MASK>}\})^{L_{r}},

(3)

where
\mathcal{V}
denotes the model’s vocabulary.
At an intermediate timestep
t
(from
t=1
to
t=0
), all masked tokens are predicted simultaneously and independently. After prediction, a subset of the newly revealed tokens is re-masked at each step to ensure that the overall masking ratio follows the prescribed noise schedule. Following LLaDA, the low-confidence remasking
(
Chang et al. 2022
)
is adopted; tokens with lower prediction confidence are selected for re-masking, and tokens that remain unmasked are carried forward and not re-masked in subsequent steps, making this process irreversible.

3
Preliminary Experiments: Sensitivity to Positional Misalignment

MDLMs generate text by progressively revealing tokens at fixed sequence indices. Under standard decoding, once a token is unmasked, it remains fixed for the remainder of the generation.
Prior work has shown that this irreversible decoding dynamic allows errors introduced early in generation to persist and accumulate
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
.

This section studies positional misalignment as a source of accumulated error.
Unlike token identity errors, an index-level misalignment can affect the interpretation of all subsequent tokens. Our goal is to isolate the effect of accumulated local positional misalignment under irreversible decoding, while keeping token identities and local content unchanged.
We also observe shift-like local inconsistencies, such as short-range token duplication and character-level misalignment, in standard generations from LLaDA-8B-Instruct
2
2

A
provides further explanation of shift-like artifacts.
, further motivating the focus on positional misalignment.

To this end, we introduce a
controlled sequence shift intervention
.
We then evaluate the intervention’s negative effect as a proxy for the negative effects of true positional misalignment that occur during decoding.

3.1
Controlled Sequence Shift Intervention

This section describes the controlled sequence shift intervention we use to evaluate sensitivity to positional misalignment in MDLMs.
We first identify positions within text spans that are already unmasked, defined as token indices whose immediate left and right neighbors are also unmasked.
We then uniformly sample
K

shift boundary
locations, then locate the contiguous block of unmasked tokens immediately to their right and rotate this block by one position, moving a
<MASK>
token into the span and shifting the text tokens right by one index (see the example below):

Intervention Example

Before:
\ldots
A B C D <MASK>
\ldots

After:
\ldots
A <MASK> B C D
\ldots

This operation preserves sequence length and token identities, and does not overwrite or delete tokens.
Since all
shift boundaries
are determined from the same sequence snapshot and correspond to distinct boundaries, the shifted spans do not overlap.

Interventions are applied at fixed early decoding steps, every
0.05
diffusion time units for
t\geq 0.5
, yielding 10 intervention steps per generation.
At each scheduled step,
K

shift boundaries
are selected and shifted simultaneously.
Thus, a total of
10\times K
one-index shift operations are introduced over the decoding process.
While each individual operation introduces only a local one-index positional shift, repeating such interventions during early decoding allows these small misalignments to accumulate under irreversible generation.

4
Relaxing Positional Alignment

The standard training objective for MDLMs uses a masked cross-entropy loss, as defined in Eq. (
2
). This objective supervises each masked position independently and thus assumes a fixed one-to-one correspondence between output positions and target tokens. Consequently, training enforces a strict positional alignment.

To relax this constraint, we introduce a special
<slack>
token that can absorb local positional mismatch. We implement this using CTC, which allows the model to predict a longer latent sequence that may include
<slack>
tokens and repeated tokens. The CTC collapse operation maps this latent sequence to the target by removing
<slack>
tokens and merging adjacent duplicates; as a result, supervision is no longer applied at fixed output positions. Although CTC was originally developed for speech recognition, the same mechanism directly applies when the correspondence between prediction positions and target tokens is uncertain.

4.1
CTC-Based Objective

We start from the gold target response
r_{0}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}
, insert
<slack>
tokens to obtain a slack-augmented target

\tilde{r}_{0}\in(\mathcal{V}\cup\{\textsc{\textless slack\textgreater}\})^{\tilde{L}}

,
where
\tilde{L}
is the alignment length including
<slack>
tokens,
and apply masking as in standard MDLM training. Specifically, given a mask ratio sampled as
t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]
, we construct a corrupted response sequence
\tilde{r}_{t}
by replacing a subset of tokens in
\tilde{r}_{0}
with
<MASK>
, paired with a prompt
q_{0}
.
Let
a=(a^{1},\ldots,a^{\tilde{L}})
be a latent alignment sequence, where
a_{i}\in\mathcal{V}\cup\{\textsc{\textless slack\textgreater}\}
. Under the standard CTC conditional independence assumption, the alignment probability factorizes as:

\displaystyle P^{\text{\tiny CTC}}(a\mid q_{0},\tilde{r}_{t})=\prod_{i=1}^{\tilde{L}}P_{\theta}(a^{i}\mid q_{0},\tilde{r}_{t}).

(4)

The CTC likelihood of the clean target response
r_{0}
is obtained by marginalizing over all alignment sequences that collapse to
r_{0}
:

\displaystyle P^{\text{\tiny CTC}}(r_{0}\mid q_{0},\tilde{r}_{t})=\sum_{a\in\beta^{-1}(r_{0})}P^{\text{\tiny CTC}}(a\mid q_{0},\tilde{r}_{t}),

(5)

where
\beta^{-1}(r_{0})
returns all possible alignments compatible with
r_{0}
by collapses consecutive repetitions in
a
(i.e., replaces each maximal run of identical symbols with a single symbol), and then removes all
<slack>
symbols.
Then the corresponding CTC training objective is written as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{\tiny CTC}}_{\theta,t}=-\mathbb{E}_{t,\,q_{0},\,r_{0},\,\tilde{r}_{t}}\left[\log P^{\text{\tiny CTC}}(r_{0}\mid q_{0},\tilde{r}_{t})\right].

(6)

4.2
Combined Objective

We redefine the SFT objective to incorporate the CTC objective into SFT as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{\tiny SFT}}_{\theta,t}=\mathcal{L}^{\text{\tiny CE}}_{\theta,t}+\lambda\mathcal{L}^{\text{\tiny CTC}}_{\theta,t},

(7)

where
\lambda
controls the contribution of the CTC term.
The CTC term is computed against the original slack-free target
r_{0}
, as explained in Eq. (
6
), whereas the CE term is computed over the slack-augmented target
\tilde{r}_{0}
.
Moreover, both CTC and CE terms use the corrupted response sequence
\tilde{r}_{t}
, which is derived from
\tilde{r}_{0}
.
Therefore, when computing the CE term, we compute Eq. (
2
) by substituting
r_{t}
and
r_{0}
with
\tilde{r}_{t}
and
\tilde{r}_{0}
, respectively.

This encourages the model to emit
<slack>
tokens in
\tilde{L}
predictions when needed to accommodate positional uncertainty, while keeping the supervised output content equal to
r_{0}
after collapse.

4.3
Train–Inference Consistent Denoising

With the introduction of the CTC objective, adjacent identical tokens are treated as equivalent to a single token under the CTC collapse operation, together with
<slack>
tokens. This equivalence allows the model to generate the same token at multiple adjacent positions.

During training, intermediate noisy states are constructed by inserting
<slack>
tokens and applying random masking, and therefore contain no repeated tokens. At inference time, however, the CTC formulation permits repeated tokens in intermediate denoising states, creating a mismatch between training and inference.

To address this issue, we explicitly handle CTC-induced repetitions during decoding. Specifically, at the end of each denoising step, after token transfer and confidence-based remasking, we identify adjacent positions whose decoded tokens are identical, excluding masked positions and
<slack>
tokens. For each adjacent duplicate pair, the later position is converted into a
<slack>
token. This greedy and local operation preserves the fixed sequence length while ensuring that intermediate denoising states remain consistent with the alignment flexibility induced by the CTC training objective.

Experiments

To demonstrate the effectiveness of our method, we conduct experiments on five open-ended generation benchmarks

5.1
Data Preparation

For each training example, we augment the target response with a variable number of
<slack>
tokens to introduce alignment flexibility. The total amount of slack is controlled by a per-example slack ratio, which is sampled uniformly at random and capped at a fixed maximum.

Concretely, we sample a slack ratio
s\sim\mathcal{U}[0,s_{\max}]
, where
s_{\max}=0.5
in all experiments. Given a clean response of length
L
, this ratio determines the number of
<slack>
tokens as
\lfloor s\cdot L\rfloor
. These tokens are inserted at random positions within the response, increasing its effective length. The inserted
<slack>
tokens serve as alignment buffers that allow the model to absorb local positional uncertainty without committing to a specific token placement.

In addition to explicit insertion,
<slack>
tokens may also be obtained by repurposing padding positions. As noted in prior LLaDA work, during SFT, sequences within each mini-batch are padded to a uniform length by appending end-of-sequence tokens (
<EOS>
) to shorter responses, and these padding
<EOS>
tokens are supervised. While this teaches the model to control response length by explicitly generating
<EOS>
, it also increases the amount of supervision placed on
<EOS>
tokens.

To reduce this effect, we allow a portion of the padding budget to be converted into
<slack>
tokens. Specifically, after determining the total number of slack tokens for a response, if unused sequence capacity remains, we replace a subset of the padding
<EOS>
tokens with
<slack>
and randomly place them within the sequence, subject to the fixed length constraint. The number of such conversions is controlled by a separate ratio and capped at a fixed fraction of the response length. In practice, this reassigns part of the padding capacity to
<slack>
tokens while leaving the supervision on the final
<EOS>
unchanged.

Consistent gains over the matched baseline.

Across all evaluated benchmarks, the CTC-trained model outperforms the CE-only baseline, which is trained under identical settings but without the CTC objective. Since both models share the same architecture, initialization, data, and training schedule, this comparison isolates the effect of the training objective. Our results suggest that relaxing strict positional supervision in MDLM is effective in improving open-ended generation quality even under the basic choice of CTC.

Effect of standard MDLM SFT.

The CE-only baseline, trained with the standard MDLM SFT objective, underperforms LLaDA-8B-Instruct on four out of five benchmarks.
While differences in instruction-tuning data and training pipelines are likely contributors, this gap highlights the sensitivity of diffusion-based LMs to training objectives and alignment assumptions in the instruction-tuning regime.

Comparison with LLaDA-1.5.

The CTC-trained model exceeds LLaDA-1.5 on the Creative-Writing subset of Arena-Hard-v2.0 as well as on Creative-Writing-Bench v3, MTBench, and WildBench.
LLaDA-1.5 is trained with reinforcement learning on a
1.2\times
larger closed dataset, whereas our model is trained with SFT on an open dataset.
Despite these differences, the CTC-trained model achieves competitive or superior performance on several benchmarks.
Taken together, these results suggest that positional alignment is a factor affecting the effectiveness of standard training objectives for MDLMs, with particularly pronounced gains on open-ended and creative generation tasks.

6
Related Work

6.1
Positional Misalignment

A line of work addresses failures caused by fixed-length canvases and rigid position-wise structure in diffusion-based (and related) text generation, especially when the desired output length or span boundaries are uncertain
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
;
Wu et al. 2025b
;
Yang et al. 2025
;
Kim et al. 2025a
;
Havasi et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025a
)
.
These approaches improve robustness by enabling variable-length generation via mechanisms such as token position adjustment, end-of-sequence prediction, or explicit insertion and deletion operations.
DreamOn
(
Wu et al. 2025b
)
is most closely related in motivation, as it also introduces auxiliary tokens during generation.
However, DreamOn is designed for code infilling and uses special state tokens to dynamically expand or contract the masked canvas, thereby adapting the generation target to the task’s span-length requirements.

In contrast, our work studies token misalignment that arises under standard open-ended generation. Rather than modifying the target sequence or adjusting the effective canvas, we introduce a
<slack>
token to absorb alignment uncertainty during decoding.
This allows the model to remain within the MDLM generation setup while improving robustness in position-sensitive, open-ended generation tasks.

6.2
Early Commitment / Token-level Self-correction

Several recent works study the early commitment or absorbing state issue in MDLMs, where errors introduced early in decoding can persist and cascade through subsequent steps
(
Wang et al. 2025
;
Lee et al. 2025
;
Peng et al. 2025
)
.
Much of this literature focuses on enabling token identity revision, either by allowing explicit re-masking or refinement during inference
(
Wang et al. 2025
;
Peng et al. 2025
)
or by improving inference-time unmasking decisions and parallel decoding strategies
(
Lee et al. 2025
;
Horvitz et al. 2025
;
Jazbec et al. 2025
)
.
Another line of work introduces pre-training or fine-tuning objectives that support self-correction via token-quality estimation and targeted refinement
(
Huang et al. 2025
;
Kim et al. 2025b
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
.

Our work focuses on positional sensitivity: we treat early committed tokens as immutable and analyze how index-level misalignment propagates through subsequent generation, leading to systematic degradation.

6.3
Block Diffusion Sampling

Block diffusion
(
Arriola et al. 2025
)
refers to the generation strategies that update groups of token positions jointly during diffusion-based generation.
Several MDLMs support block diffusion purely as an inference-time decoding choice, without modifying the forward noising process or training objective
(
Nie et al. 2025b
;
Zhu et al. 2025
;
Ye et al. 2025
)
.
In these models, block diffusion groups token positions during the reverse process while retaining the one-to-one positional supervision used during training.

Our work operates under the same MDLM architecture and decoding setup and is orthogonal to, and can be combined with, block-based decoding.

6.4
Flexible Alignment Training Objective.

A range of alignment-flexible training objectives have been explored to relax strict position-wise supervision by marginalizing over latent alignments or edit paths
(
Ristad & Yianilos 1998
;
Oncina & Sebban 2006
;
Cuturi & Blondel 2017
;
Sarawagi & Cohen 2004
)
.
CTC is a representative instance of this class, originally introduced for sequence prediction problems with uncertain alignment, most notably in speech recognition
(
Graves et al. 2006
;
Graves 2012
;
Graves et al. 2013
;
Higuchi et al. 2020
)
. Variants and applications of CTC have since been studied beyond speech
(
Libovický & Helcl 2018
;
Saharia et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2023
;
Fujita et al. 2024
)
.

In this work, we adopt CTC as a simple and well-established representative of alignment-flexible objectives. Our focus is not on CTC itself, but on the broader principle of relaxing strict positional supervision during training to better match the irreversible decoding dynamics of MDLMs.

7
Limitations

We instantiate the standard CTC objective as a flexible alignment objective, which induces a collapse operation that removes both
<slack>
tokens and repeated adjacent tokens. This merging behavior can be undesirable for tasks that require exact surface forms, such as numeric strings or formatting symbols. As a result, it may introduce failure modes on format-sensitive evaluations; see Appendix
C
for detailed examples.
Since CTC represents a basic form of flexible alignment, a natural next step is to explore more advanced or task-specific alignment objectives within MDLMs, which may provide a more robust solution to positional misalignment while avoiding these surface-form issues.

Our experiments are limited to the SFT of a standard MDLM. The effect of relaxing positional alignment during pre-training remains unexplored. Integrating flexible alignment objectives directly into the pre-training stage is a natural direction for future work and may lead to MDLMs that natively support alignment flexibility.
Finally, we do not investigate architectural-level integration of flexible alignment mechanisms. Such integration could further improve robustness to token misalignment and may yield additional gains beyond objective-level modifications.

8
Conclusion

This work identifies strict position-level supervision as a factor in degraded open-ended generation in MDLMs.
Due to the irreversible nature of standard MDLM decoding, small token misalignments can persist and cascade, leading to substantial degradation in generation quality.
We demonstrate this effect using a controlled intervention experiment and show that repeated one-position shifts can affect open-ended generation performance. These results indicate that strict one-to-one positional alignment is misaligned with the decoding dynamics of MDLMs.

Based on this diagnosis, we introduce a minimal relaxation of positional supervision using CTC, a simple and well-studied alignment-flexible objective, to introduce a special
<slack>
token to absorb positional misalignment. When applied during SFT, this approach improves robustness to positional errors and consistently outperforms on five open-ended generation benchmarks on LLaDA-8B-Instruct.

Overall, our results show that relaxing positional alignment improves open-ended generation in MDLMs. These findings identify strict positional supervision as an important factor affecting failures and indicate that introducing alignment flexibility is an effective way to mitigate this issue, making it a promising direction for further study.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the understanding of MDLMs. We study a training-time objective modification and evaluate its effect on generation quality under controlled experimental settings.
We do not conduct a dedicated analysis of social biases, toxicity, or other downstream harms in model outputs. The models produced in this work are not intended for deployment or public use beyond reproducing the reported experiments.
As this work focuses on model diagnostics and training objectives rather than new capabilities or deployment scenarios, we do not identify specific societal or ethical impacts that require separate discussion beyond those commonly associated with research on language models.

Appendix B
Implementation Details

B.1
Overall Framework

We perform SFT using the training code of
Zhao et al. 2025
. The CE-only baseline is trained with the standard diffusion cross-entropy objective, reusing their implementation with only the dataset and preprocessing replaced.

For the CTC-trained model, we introduce an auxiliary CTC alignment loss on the response segment in addition to the diffusion cross-entropy objective. This requires a CTC-specific preprocessing pipeline. For each example, we separate the prompt prefix from the response span, construct a clean target sequence from the unmasked response tokens, and generate a
<slack>
-augmented response input by inserting
<slack>
tokens according to a random slack budget. End-of-turn and end-of-sequence tokens remain supervised, while padding positions are handled using
<slack>
tokens as described in Section
5.1
. The final training objective is a weighted sum of diffusion cross-entropy and CTC loss, with the CTC weight set to
0.1
. To prevent
<slack>
tokens from dominating token-level learning, the contribution of the
<slack>
token in the cross-entropy term is down-weighted by a factor of
0.01
.

Although we describe this process as inserting
<slack>
tokens, training is performed using fixed-length sequences. In practice, slack tokens occupy positions that would otherwise be padding, thereby increasing the effective sequence length of semantic content without changing the tensor length used for batching. The number of
<slack>
tokens is sampled from a bounded random budget on a per-example basis.

B.2
Training Hyperparameters

Both the baseline and CTC-trained models are trained under the same optimization settings. We use LoRA adapters with rank
r=128
, scaling factor
\alpha=32
, and dropout
0.05
, without bias adaptation. Adapters are applied to the transformer projection layers
\{\texttt{q\_proj},\texttt{k\_proj},\texttt{v\_proj}\}
, the token embedding layer (
wte
) and the output projection of the feed-forward block (
ff_out
).

Optimization uses AdamW
(
Loshchilov & Hutter 2019
)
with a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-4}
, weight decay
0.1
, and gradient clipping at
1.0
, under bfloat16 precision. Training is conducted for three epochs with a per-device batch size of 3, no gradient accumulation, and a maximum sequence length of 2048, resulting in 35,955 update steps. We use a linear learning rate schedule without warmup and fix the random seed to 42. Checkpoints are evaluated and saved every 10% of the training steps, and the checkpoint with the lowest evaluation loss is selected. We do not perform any hyperparameter search in this work, including for the CTC loss weight or the
<slack>
token contribution in the cross-entropy term.

C.1
Analysis of GSM8K Degradation.

On GSM8K, as shown in Figures
9
,
10
, and
11
, we observe a recurring error pattern where the model does not reproduce an exact number from the prompt at the very start of the chain-of-thought reasoning. This arises because the LLaDA tokenizer represents numeric values as sequences of digit-level tokens (e.g., “100”
\rightarrow
“1”, “0”, “0”). In particular, for prompt numbers ending in one or more zeros (e.g.,
500,800
), the CTC-trained model sometimes drops a trailing zero (e.g.,
500\rightarrow 50,800\rightarrow 80
) and then continues reasoning and computing based on the shortened value. In these cases, the subsequent calculations are internally consistent; the failure traces back to the initial number mismatch, rather than a breakdown of the arithmetic steps themselves.

At the same time, we also observe cases where the model handles trailing-zero numbers correctly and reaches the correct answer, as shown in Figures
12
,
13
, and
14
. Taken together, these examples suggest that the GSM8K drop is tied to occasional surface-form failures on trailing-zero numbers, not that the model is fundamentally unable to handle them. A natural next step, beyond the scope of this work, is to explore objective variants that reduce this failure mode, for example by modifying the standard CTC objective to merge and remove only
<slack>
tokens.

Overall, these results provide a basic check that CTC-based SFT does not substantially disrupt performance on these general benchmarks.
