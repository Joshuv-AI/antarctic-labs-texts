Efficacy of a multimodal conservative rehabilitation program for bladder control in individuals with incomplete spinal cord injury: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Youssef Elbalawy, Zizi M Ibrahim, Olfat Ibrahim Ali, Hany M Elgohary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of a multimodal intervention on urodynamic outcomes, urinary incontinence severity, and pelvic floor muscle strength in individuals with overactive bladder after incomplete spinal cord injury.MethodsA single-blind randomized controlled trial was conducted on 74 male participants diagnosed with overactive bladder and incomplete spinal cord injury. Participants were randomly assigned to an experimental group or a control group. Treatment was conducted for 8 weeks, three sessions per week. Outcomes were assessed at baseline, post-intervention, and the 8-week follow-up.ResultsThe experimental group showed significantly greater improvements in the measured outcomes compared with the control group (P < 0.001).ConclusionIncorporating a multimodal regimen demonstrated significant efficacy in enhancing bladder capacity, continence, and muscle function in individuals with overactive bladder due to incomplete spinal cord injury.Clinical trial registry (ID: NCT07008157). https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07008157?cond=Spinal%20Cord%20Injuries&intr=Multimodal%20Rehabilitation%20Program&viewType=Card&rank=1 Keywords: Overactive bladder, spinal cord injury, motor imagery training, pelvic floor muscle training, urodynamics

Introduction

Overactive bladder (OAB) is a common and challenging condition marked by symptoms such as urinary urgency, increased frequency, nocturia, and urge incontinence, all of which can greatly diminish quality of life (QOL) and functional independence. 1 Urinary incontinence frequently stems from impaired neural regulation of the lower urinary tract. 2

When spinal pathways responsible for coordinating bladder filling and emptying are disrupted, individuals may experience detrusor overactivity, detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia, and reduced bladder compliance. These impairments not only negatively affect daily functioning but also heighten the risk of urinary tract infections, kidney complications, and social withdrawal. 3

In individuals with spinal cord injury (SCI), neurogenic bladder dysfunction is a common complication, with neurogenic detrusor overactivity affecting up to 80% of cases, even among those with incomplete injuries where some sensory or motor function is preserved. 4 If not properly managed, this dysfunction can contribute to emotional distress, recurrent infections, renal deterioration, and a greater likelihood of institutional care. 5

Although pharmacological treatments such as antimuscarinics and β3-agonists can help alleviate symptoms, their long-term use is often limited by side effects such as dry mouth, constipation, and cognitive issues. 6 Consequently, growing attention has been directed toward conservative, noninvasive strategies that support better bladder control and improved QOL.

Pelvic floor muscle (PFM) training (PFMT) remains a first-line conservative intervention for urinary incontinence, helping strengthen the PFMs and improve urethral closure. 7 Furthermore, interferential current (IFC) stimulation has emerged as a promising noninvasive technique capable of influencing bladder function through transcutaneous neuromodulation of peripheral nerves involved in micturition regulation. 8

Motor imagery training (MIT), a cognitive strategy that involves mentally rehearsing PFM contractions without physical movement, has gained attention for its potential to activate cortical and subcortical pathways responsible for voluntary pelvic floor control, promoting neuroplasticity in neurological populations. 9 When combined with timed voiding (TV), a behavioral intervention aimed at re-establishing regular bladder emptying schedules, these techniques may offer a comprehensive and synergistic approach to managing OAB in individuals with incomplete SCI (ISCI). 10

OAB following ISCI is a multifactorial disorder characterized by detrusor overactivity, poor sensory processing, and poor coordination of the PFMs. Therefore, interventions that address only one component might not yield the best clinical results. 11

A multimodal rehabilitation intervention combining peripheral neuromodulation (interferential current), behavioral (TV), and motor rehabilitation (PFMT) can be synergistically beneficial by targeting a combination of underlying processes. Moreover, MIT has been suggested as an adjunct therapy that can boost cortical activity and promote motor relearning. Nevertheless, the cumulative impact of MIT as a component of a traditional multimodal rehabilitation program has not been sufficiently studied in patients with ISCIs. 12

While each of these interventions individually offers benefits in managing neurogenic bladder dysfunction, there is a lack of evidence supporting the combined use of IFC, PFMT, TV, and especially MIT in patients with ISCI. This study aimed to investigate the additional effect of MIT when combined with a conventional multimodal rehabilitation program (IFC, PFMT, and TV) on bladder function, urinary incontinence severity, and PFM strength in individuals with OAB following ISCI.

Materials and methods

Study design

The reporting of this study conforms to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statements. 13 This was a parallel-group single-blind randomized controlled trial (outcome assessors were blinded to group assignments). Randomization was conducted using a computer-generated sequence generated by online software to assign participants to two groups. Allocation concealment was carried out using sealed opaque envelopes, which were opened consecutively as participants were enrolled in the study.

Participants

In this study, a total of 92 participants diagnosed with ISCI were classified according to the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale (AIS), and only participants with AIS grades C and D were included. 14 Patients were referred to the outpatient clinic at the Faculty of Physical Therapy, Cairo University, from the National Institute of Urology and Nephrology in Mataria, as well as the Department of Urology and Nephrology at Kasr Alaini Hospital. Each participant was examined and diagnosed by a qualified medical professional, with confirmation from a urologist and neurologist. The diagnosis of OAB was confirmed using urodynamic testing based on International Continence Society (ICS) standards, characterized by the presence of neurogenic detrusor overactivity during the filling phase, with or without reduced maximum cystometric capacity and/or early first desire to void. 15 Patients were included in the study if they had the following criteria: (a) OAB symptoms confirmed with urodynamic testing; (b) ISCI classified as AIS C or D above the L1 spinal level; (c) medical stability; (d) ability to follow study protocols; and (e) signed informed consent. The patients were excluded if they had complete SCI (AIS A), urinary tract infections, pelvic malignancy, pelvic or bladder surgeries, or significant cognitive or psychiatric disorders. Seven individuals were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria, three chose not to participate, and four were withdrawn for reasons unrelated to the study's interventions or outcomes. Consequently, 78 male participants were screened and randomly assigned to 2 groups: the experimental group (EG), which received IFC, PFMT, MIT, and TV, and the control group (CG), which received IFC, PFMT, and TV. Two from the EG and two from the CG were excluded from the study due to transportation difficulties, leaving a total of 74 participants (37 in each group) who completed the trial (Figure 1). Both groups adhered to their respective treatment protocols three times a week for 8 weeks. Outcomes included maximum cystometric capacity (MCC), leak point pressure (LPP), and volume at first desire to void (VFDV), assessed via urodynamic testing at baseline, post-intervention, and after an 8-week follow-up. Other outcomes were PFM strength and urinary incontinence severity, measured by Myofeedback and the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire–Urinary Incontinence Short Form (ICIQ-UI SF), respectively. The study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Physical Therapy, Benha University, Egypt (PT.BU.EC.18), and was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov with ID NCT07008157, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to enrollment.

Sample size

The required sample size for this study was calculated based on the primary outcome, which was the MCC. The calculation was performed using G*Power software version 3.1.7 and was based on an α of 0.05, a power of 80%, and an effect size of 0.84. The estimated sample size was determined to be 36 participants per group. To account for a dropout rate of 5%, the sample size was increased to 38 participants per group.

Outcome measures

Urodynamic assessments

The DANTIC UD 5000/5500 Urodynamic Investigation System (Megamed, Germany) was used for the urodynamic assessment, which include MCC (the maximal bladder volume before strong urge or leakage), detrusor LPP (the pressure at which leakage occurs in the absence of a rise in abdominal pressure), and VFDV (the initial conscious urge to void). The multichannel cystometry protocol enabled precise measurement of the bladder filling phase. 16 These evaluations were carried out by experienced urologists. The patient was positioned in the supine position, followed by insertion of a sterile catheter through the urethra into the bladder for fluid instillation and pressure monitoring. During cystometric testing, the bladder was gradually filled with sterile saline at a steady rate, while intravesical and abdominal pressures were continuously recorded. 17

The ICIQ-UI SF

The ICIQ-UI SF is a valid and reliable tool used to assess the severity of urinary incontinence and its effect on QOL (Avery et al., 2004). 18 The questionnaire consists of three scored items and an unscored self-diagnostic item. It assesses the prevalence, frequency, and perceived cause of urinary incontinence and its impact on everyday life on a scale from 0 (not at all) to 10 (a great deal).

PFM strength assessment

The TG Myo Feedback 420v was used for the PFM strength assessment. A rectal probe electrode, lubricated with KY gel, was gently inserted through the rectum. It measures anal closure pressure and PFM strength. The internal probe not only served as a measurement tool but also enhanced proprioceptive feedback, improving muscle awareness. The Myofeedback display was explained to patients, where muscle relaxation was indicated by fewer illuminated bars and contraction by a greater number. Participants were asked to contract the PFM maximally around the probe, hold for a 10-count, and then relax, repeating this sequence three times. The average of these three readings was recorded and stored digitally on Excel sheet, providing an objective measure of PFM performance across the designated assessment periods. 19

All outcome measurements were carried out at three time points: before starting treatment, after 8 weeks of treatment, and after an additional 8 weeks of follow-up after the end of treatment.

Treatment procedures

IFC

IFC therapy was applied while the patient lay in a supine position with the knees slightly apart, following the protocol outlined by Patitapaban et al. (2018). Each treatment session involved four electrodes enclosed in lint cloth covers. Two electrodes were positioned bilaterally on the lower abdomen, immediately below the anterior superior iliac spines (ASIS), while the remaining two were placed on the inner surfaces of both thighs on the femoral triangles. To maintain hygienic standards, the cloth covers were replaced for each participant at every session. The intervention was administered three times weekly over an 8-week period. A frequency range of 0–10 Hz was used, and the current intensity was carefully adjusted based on each patient's comfort and tolerance. Each IFC session lasted 15 min, with the aim of modulating pelvic region activity to support improved bladder function. 20

TV

The approach involved instructing participants to follow a fixed, scheduled voiding routine, irrespective of the sensation to urinate (or typically every 3 h). Additionally, patients were advised to adjust their toileting posture by sitting and leaning forward at an angle of approximately 45°, allowing adequate time for complete bladder emptying. The technique also included practicing double voiding, where the individual would stand up and sit down again after the initial void to help ensure maximum bladder evacuation.10,21,22

MIT

Prior to initiating MIT, participants watched a 10-min instructional video in a quiet treatment space, illustrating proper PFM contractions through both visual and auditory cues. The therapist provided a detailed explanation, using a simple analogy of the bladder as a balloon filled with urine, connected by a tube (the urethra) to the outside, and controlled by the PFM. It was explained that contracting these muscles tightens the balloon, holding back urine, whereas weak or relaxed muscles may lead to leakage. Patients were then guided to mentally visualize contracting and holding these muscles until they reached a suitable time and place (the toilet), without physically performing the action. Following this explanation, patients were asked to sit comfortably with their eyes closed and spend 10 min visualizing the movement and control of their PFM while remaining physically relaxed. Throughout the session, the therapist used open-ended reminders to help maintain the patient's focus on the imagined muscle activity. This guided MIT was conducted only during the initial session. In subsequent sessions, patients continued with MIT independently, followed by PFM exercises and IFC therapy, following the protocol described by Jäger and Schulte-Frei (2017). This protocol was implemented three times per week over an 8-week period. 9

PFMT

Before beginning each treatment session, participants were instructed to empty their bladders to promote comfort and relaxation during the exercises. All patients were taught a structured PFMT routine to be performed daily in multiple positions, such as lying, sitting, and standing. The program consisted of contracting the PFM for 10 s, followed by a 10-s relaxation period, with this sequence repeated 15 times in each session. To gradually enhance the endurance of the slow-twitch muscle fibers, both contraction and relaxation times were increased by 1 s each week. In addition, to activate and strengthen the fast-twitch muscle fibers, patients were directed to perform 20 quick, repetitive contractions and relaxations of the levator ani muscles, simulating the act of stopping urine flow, followed by a 10-s rest. This rapid contraction sequence was repeated for two to four sets in each session. This protocol was implemented three times per week over an 8-week period. 23 Progression of PFMT was based on the participant's ability to perform correct isolated PFM contractions without compensatory activity. Exercise intensity was gradually increased by progressing from low to higher contraction duration and repetition, incorporating both sustained (endurance) and rapid contractions, and advancing from supine to more functionally demanding positions such as sitting and standing. Progression to the next level was permitted only when the participant demonstrated correct technique and full tolerance of the current level without fatigue or substitution patterns.

The intervention protocol was followed by supervised sessions, participant-managed logbooks, and weekly follow-up, which ensured adherence to the intervention protocol, including PFMT, TV, and MIT.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of the current study was carried out using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences Software (SPSS) Version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). Quantitative data of the demographic characteristics and measured outcomes were presented as means and standard deviations. To test the homogeneity of the measured outcomes, the Shapiro–Wilk test was used, along with the assessment of standard deviations, histograms, and outliers to indicate a normal distribution of the data. Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was conducted to distinguish between and within groups. Independent-sample t-tests were used to assess differences in demographic and clinical characteristics between groups at baseline. Additionally, effect sizes were calculated to measure the magnitude of the observed differences. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of IFC, PFMT, MIT, and TV on OAB symptoms in ISCI. The findings demonstrated that the intervention produced statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvements in key urodynamic and functional outcomes compared with the CG, with effects sustained at follow-up. At baseline, both the EG and CG were statistically comparable across all measured variables, affirming the effectiveness of the randomization process in ensuring group homogeneity prior to intervention.

Following the intervention, the EG exhibited significant improvements in MCC, VFDV, LPP, and PFM strength and reductions in ICIQ-UI SF scores relative to CG. Notably, these improvements were maintained at follow-up, underscoring the durability of the intervention's effects. The effect sizes ranged from moderate to large, particularly in LPP (Cohen's d = 0.91–0.96), ICIQ-UI SF scores (Cohen's d = −1.39 to −1.52), and muscle strength (Cohen's d = 1.48–1.52), highlighting the clinical relevance of the observed changes.

The mixed-model analysis of variance revealed significant main effects of time and group, as well as significant time × group interaction effects for all outcomes. These findings indicated not only that both groups experienced changes over time but also that the pattern and magnitude of change were significantly different between the groups, favoring the EG. The η2p values for interaction effects ranged from 0.07 to 0.25, with particularly large effects noted for ICIQ-UI SF scores (η2p = 0.22) and muscle strength (η2p = 0.25). According to established guidelines, values exceeding 0.14 represent large effect sizes, confirming the practical significance of the intervention.

The synergistic peripheral, behavioral, and central mechanisms can be attributed to the gains in bladder functionality and pelvic floor performance after the multimodal rehabilitation program. MCC might have increased due to the neuromodulatory action of interferential current stimulation, which has been demonstrated to control afferent bladder signaling and suppress detrusor overactivity, thus enhancing bladder storage capacity (Krhut et al., 2025; Dequirez et al., 2025).24,25 Likewise, delayed VFDV implicates enhanced sensory threshold modulation and maximized bladder compliance, probably by repeated bladder retraining with TV strategies, which are known to gradually augment functional bladder capacity and decrease urgency (Huang et al., 2026; Ziętek et al., 2026).26,27

PFMT is credited with the substantial improvement in LPP and the decrease in the severity of urinary incontinence due to improved urethral closure and reflex coordination of the PFMs. Enhanced development and better function of the PFMs increase urethral resistance during bladder filling and stressful states, which minimizes the occurrence of urine leakage (Erhan et al., 2025; Zheng, Y., & Stoffel, 2023).28,29

Moreover, PFM strength is a sign of enhanced neuromuscular activity and motor control through repetitive specific contractions. These effects could have been further increased by the use of MIT, which involves the activation of cortical and sub cortical motor networks engaged in pelvic floor control, facilitating motor relearning and enhancing voluntary muscle recruitment. It has been indicated that motor imagery is associated with similar neural substrates as real movement and has the potential to improve the success of rehabilitation by promoting neuroplasticity (Li et al., 2026; McLean et al., 2025).30,31 Hence, the combined effects of electrical neuromodulation, behavioral bladder retraining, and MIT could be considered the reason why the improvements in all outcome measures are sustained over time.

The improvements in MCC and VFDV observed in this study aligned with previous research emphasizing the importance of physiotherapeutic interventions in managing urologic symptoms associated with neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction in individuals affected by conditions such SCI. These approaches primarily focus on restoring voluntary control over bladder function through a combination of bladder training, PFM exercises, biofeedback, electrical stimulation, and techniques to suppress urgency. As a vital component of multidisciplinary rehabilitation, these therapies not only help improve bladder control but also significantly enhance patients’ self-esteem and overall QOL.32,33

Similarly, the significant reduction in ICIQ-UI SF scores indicated meaningful alleviation of urinary incontinence symptoms, consistent with prior studies demonstrating the efficacy of PFM rehabilitation and adjunct therapies in improving continence outcomes.34,35

PFM improvements observed in the EG further substantiated the potential of combined therapeutic approaches in enhancing PFM function. Increases in muscle strength (Cohen’s d = 1.48–1.52) suggested effective neuromuscular re-education and structural adaptation of the PFM, corroborating earlier findings in patients undergoing PFMT and adjunct interventions that PFMT enhanced muscle strength, endurance, coordination, and relaxation to support the urethra and reduce urine leakage and urgency. Since its first introduction by Kegel, PFMT has been widely recommended as a first-line, noninvasive treatment for urinary incontinence, especially stress urinary incontinence. 36

The sustained improvements at follow-up are particularly noteworthy, as they suggest long-term benefits of the intervention. This durability is crucial given the chronic nature of bladder dysfunction and the need for interventions that provide lasting symptom control and functional gains. Previous studies have highlighted the challenge of maintaining therapeutic benefits over time in continence management, making the present findings clinically significant. 37

A finding supported by neuroimaging studies demonstrated MIT-induced changes in brain activation patterns and structural adaptations (Sharma et al., 2006). As part of an ongoing pioneering study applying MIT techniques in individuals with urinary incontinence, the current article aims to explore the established effects of MIT in other patient populations, outline the similarities between imagined and actual movements, and discuss the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying motor imagery. Finally, it proposes strategies for integrating motor imagery into the management of urinary incontinence.38–40

Despite these promising results, the study had several limitations. First, while urodynamic measures and patient-reported outcomes provide a comprehensive assessment, incorporating QOL metrics and objective activity monitoring could offer additional insights into the broader impact of the intervention. Second, the relatively short follow-up period may not capture long-term relapse rates or adverse effects. Future studies should extend the follow-up duration to evaluate the persistence of benefits and the safety profile over time. Male subjects only were chosen to reduce the variation due to sex-specific anatomical and physiological variations that affect PFM activity and bladder dynamics, such as urethral length and prostate-related variables. However, this may limit the extent to which the results can be generalized, and future studies including female participants are required to determine whether the findings apply to both sexes. Further investigations are also needed to directly evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention in female groups, considering sex-specific anatomical and functional differences. Finally, the lack of participant blinding should be addressed in future studies.

The findings of this study have important clinical implications. The intervention demonstrated substantial improvements in both urodynamic parameters and patient-reported outcomes, suggesting its utility as a valuable adjunct in the management of bladder dysfunction and urinary incontinence. Given the observed improvements in PFMT, this intervention may also enhance overall pelvic health and continence mechanisms, contributing to a multidimensional therapeutic strategy. Clinicians should consider incorporating such interventions into conservative management protocols, particularly for patients with incomplete response to standard treatments.

Further research is recommended to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of the intervention's effects. Investigating optimal dosing, delivery modalities, and long-term maintenance strategies would also be beneficial in refining the intervention's clinical application.

Conclusions

This study confirmed the effectiveness of a combined physiotherapeutic approach including IFC, PFMT, MIT, and TV in improving OAB symptoms in individuals with ISCI. Significant enhancements were observed in urodynamic parameters, PFM strength, and incontinence severity, with sustained benefits at follow-up supporting its role as a noninvasive option for managing neurogenic bladder dysfunction.
