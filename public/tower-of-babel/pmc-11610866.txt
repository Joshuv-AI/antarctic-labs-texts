Psychological effects of Manipur violence among the internally displaced persons residing in relief camps across Imphal valley of Manipur – A cross-sectional study
Authors: Bishwalata Rajkumari, Dayananda Ingudam, Chitra Yengkokpam, Gunipriya Oinam, Rajshri Yumnam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Conflict and violence lead to the displacement of millions of people. A significant number of those who are exposed to potentially traumatic events, such as conflict and violence, may develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and other mental health or behavioral conditions. This study plans to estimate the prevalence and associated factors of PTSD and generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) among internally displaced persons residing in different relief camps during Manipur violence.Materials and Methods:Community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among internally displaced persons (IDPs) residing in relief camps located in Imphal East District of Manipur using a pretested, semistructured, validated questionnaire consisting of PTSD checklist for DSM 5 (PCL-5) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorders Assessment tool (GAD-7). The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee.Results:More than half (65.8%) of the respondents were found to have PTSD. One-fourth of the respondents (24.8%) were found to have moderate anxiety, and 15.2% have severe anxiety. Female gender, age between 20 and 59 years, marital status, and employment status after conflict were found to be significantly associated with PTSD. Marital status and witnessed destruction of property were found to be significantly associated with GAD. Around 3/4th of the respondents (235, 75.8%) reported witnessing destruction and burning of property, and 29 (9.4%) reported witnessing death of family members or friends. Around 58 (18.7%) were currently separated from family, and 25 (8.1%) had experienced some kind of trauma at the time of displacement.Conclusion:A majority of the participants have PTSD, whereas 15.2% have severe anxiety. Efforts and interventions should be made to improve the psychological well-being through psychological support and counseling from mental health professionals and provision of vocational training for income generation for the IDPs.

Introduction

It is well known around the world that conflict and violence lead to the displacement of millions of people. The American Psychiatric Association (APA) (2013) has stated that the development of mental disorders among survivors of traumatic events is due to a combination of interacting social, psychological, and biological factors.[1]

Currently, there are major conflict-induced humanitarian crises in numerous countries, including Afghanistan, Iraq, Nigeria, Somalia, South Sudan, Syria, and Yemen. UN estimates suggest that more than 68·6 million people worldwide have been forcibly displaced by violence and conflict, the highest number of people affected since World War two.[2] This increase in people affected by conflict coincides with growing interest in mental health, as exemplified by the recently approved 10-year extension of the Mental Health Action Plan by 194 WHO member states.[3] Interest is especially high in the mental health of people affected by humanitarian emergencies.[4] A significant number of those who are exposed to potentially traumatic events, such as conflict and violence, may develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and other mental health or behavioral conditions.[1]

Since May 3, the northeastern Indian state of Manipur has witnessed repeated interethnic clashes primarily between two local ethnic communities.[5] The conflict between these two communities has resulted in civilians experiencing widespread exposure to conflict and ongoing internal displacement, which continues to this day due to forceful evictions. At present, there are more than 50,000 individuals displaced due to the conflict situation in the state.[6] People endured deplorable living conditions which can impact on their mental health and well-being, affecting their livelihoods at the household level, resulting in concerns regarding access to food, medical, and basic services.[7] People experienced despair, anxiety, fear, confusion, death, displacement, sudden destitution, and breakup of their families. This devastating experience exposed survivors to severe traumatic events and propagated fear and distress among affected communities.[8] The Government of Manipur have responded by relocation of the internally displaced people, providing them basic housing as a temporary measure like setting up of various relief camps and temporary shelter homes till safety is ensured for return to their respective villages.[9]

The conflict has lasted for more than 6 months now, and a lack of research on the mental issues faced by this high number of internally displaced people (IDP) makes this study indispensable. Findings of the study may highlight or give insights to the policy makers on the mental health conditions among the residents of relief camps on making policy or schemes to improve their mental health condition for response, recovery, and rehabilitation.

Materials and Methods

This community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among the IDPs residing in two relief camps from October 2023 to December 2023 in Manipur. Manipur has a total of 16 districts constituted of 10 hilly districts and 6 valley districts. The study population included IDPs aged 14 years and above residing in relief camps located in the valley districts. IDPs who did not consent or give assent for the study were excluded. Also, IDPs who were mentally challenged (suffering from preexisting conditions like schizophrenia, Alzheimer, dementia) and those who were not available in relief camps in two consecutive visits were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling: The sample size was calculated using the prevalence of mental disorders as 22.1%.[10] Based on the formula Z2pq/e2 by taking 95% confidence interval and a 5% absolute error and adding 10% for nonresponders, the calculated sample size was found to be 298, rounded off to 300.

Imphal East was selected from among the 6 valley districts of Manipur by simple random sampling (SRS). There are 29 relief camps in Imphal East accommodating 4500 inmates in total. Out of 29 relief camps present in Imphal East district, (i) Ideal Girls College, Akampat (960 IDPs) and (ii) Sajiwa Jail Campus (660 IDPs) relief camps were selected purposively for being relief camps with the highest number of families displaced from different districts. Of the three blocks present in the selected relief camps, a block was selected by lottery. The calculated sample size was distributed in the ratio of 1.5:1 to the two relief camps, respectively. The family located nearest to the entry point of the selected block was selected first, and then the next family was selected consecutively till the required sample size was reached. Only one person more than or equal to 14 years was selected from each family; if more than two eligible were present in a family, participants were selected by lottery in the ratio of 1.5:1 from two selected relief camps until the allocated sample size was reached.

Study tool and operational definition: A pretested, semistructured, validated questionnaire was used consisting of five domains – (i) sociodemographic characteristics of the participants, (ii) displacement experience, (iii) initiative from Government, (iv) PCL-5 questionnaire for assessing the 20 DSM-5 symptoms of PTSD, and (v) GAD-7 questionnaire for identifying generalized anxiety disorder. PCL-5 PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 is a 20-item instrument that assesses the presence and severity of PTSD symptoms using a 5-point Likert scale, where 0 = not at all, 1 = a little bit, 2 = moderately, 3 = quite a bit, and 4 = extremely. The total score was computed by adding the 20 items so that possible scores range from 0 to 80. A cutoff of ≥ 33 is taken as presence of PTSD development.[11] GAD-7 is 7-item instrument used to measure or assess the severity of generalized anxiety disorder using a 3-point Likert scale. The total score was computed by adding the seven items so that the possible score ranges from 0 to 21. A score of 10 or higher is taken as presence of significant anxiety for the present study. The following cutoffs correlate with level of anxiety severity: Score 0–9: None or mild anxiety, Score 10–14: Moderate anxiety, Score 15–21: Severe anxiety.[12] Data were collected by face-to-face interview method.

Ethical considerations: Approval for conducting the study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee. Permissions were obtained from the organizing committee and caretakers of the relief camps before conducting study. Assent for ≤ 18 years with consent from their parents or close relatives were obtained. For more than 18 years, participants’ informed verbal consent was taken prior to the interview. Strict confidentiality of the information was maintained.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered in MS Excel and analyzed in IBM SPSS v20. Descriptive statistics were expressed in terms of mean (SD), percentages, etc., and analytical statistics like Chi-square test and Fisher exact test were used. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Out of the total of 310 respondents who participated in the study, 186 (60%) were from Ideal Girls College, Akampat Relief Camp, and 124 (40%) were from the relief camp at Sajiwa Jail Campus. The majority (197, 63.5%) of the respondents were displaced from the Hill districts, and the remaining (113,36.5%) were displaced from Valley districts of Manipur. The duration of displacement is 6 months. The mean (SD) age of the respondents was 39.25 ± 15.15 years, ranging from 14 to 85 years. The mean family income per month before the conflict and displacement in INR is Rs 33,260.16 ± 35,334.01, ranging from 1000 to 250,000. Out of the total 178 (57.4%) female respondents, 3 (1.7%) were currently pregnant [Table 1].

Discussion

This study was conducted among internally displaced persons residing in the relief camps in Imphal East district of Manipur to estimate the prevalence of PTSD and generalized anxiety disorder.

Findings from the current study revealed that the estimated prevalence of PTSD among internally displaced persons was 65.8%. Similar findings could be found from a previous study conducted by Musau JN et al.[1] in Maai Mahiu camp in Nakuru County, Kenya, where the prevalence of PTSD was found to be 61.2%, and a study conducted by Makango B et al.[13] in Ethiopia, where the study found the prevalence of PTSD as 67.5%. The similarity in the findings may be because of the similar traumatic experiences of the participants in these studies like witnessing destruction of property.

The present study’s findings are, however, higher than those of Bhat RM et al.,[14] who recorded a PTSD rate of 49.81% among young adults of Kashmir who had exposure to armed conflict. The difference in the instrument might be one reason for this variation as PTSD was assessed in the study by the post-traumatic growth inventory (PTGI). The findings by Madoro D et al.[15] also indicated a lower prevalence rate of 58.4% among internally displaced persons in South Ethiopia. Although it used PCL-5, the discrepancy may be due to different trauma types.

In our study, a higher number of females 127 (71.3%) had PTSD as compared to males, 75 (57.7%). A comparable finding can be obtained from a study conducted by Makango B et al.,[13] which reported higher (52.6%) prevalence of PTSD among female gender. This may be due to mental distress due to violent loss of partner and children and of becoming a single parent or window. This study is also in line with the study carried out by Madoro D et al.[15] in South Ethiopia, where the prevalence rate was higher among females (60.8%) as compared to males (39.2%). This study also revealed presence of PTSD among the two transgender respondents. This could be because of a lower number of participants from transgender population.

In this study, married internal displaced persons had more PTSD (72.5%). Separation from family members and worries about supporting the family may be some of the reasons. This study is comparable to a study conducted by Musau JN et al.[1] (70%) in Kenya. However, this study is in contrast with the study done by Makango B et al.[13] conducted in Debre, Amhara region, Ethiopia, where marital status had no statistically significant association with PTSD. In this study, the age of the respondents was 19–59 years and was found to be significantly associated with PTSD. This is in contrast to a study conducted by Bhat RM et al.,[14] whose prevalence of PTSD was higher among young adults from 19 to 24 years.

In our study, the estimated prevalence of those who have moderate and severe anxiety was found to be 24.8% and 15.2%, respectively. These findings are comparable to a previous study conducted by Ayazi T et al.[16] in South Sudan, where the prevalence of GAD was found to be 15.8%. This study is also in line with a study conducted by Housen T et al.[17] in Kashmir Valley, whose prevalence of anxiety was 26%. In the present study, the findings reveal witnessed destruction of property and currently married were significantly associated with GAD. The findings of this study are different from another study done in South Sudan by Ayazi T et al.,[16] where female gender and being severely socioeconomically disadvantage were significantly associated with having GAD and it has similar patterns with another study done at Kashmir by Housen T et al.,[17] where female gender and witnessed number of traumatic events are significantly associated with GAD. The reason for the discrepancy might be due to use of different tools for assessing GAD. Another reason might be because respondents with repeated displacement were more likely to be exposed to trauma and violence than participants who displaced only once; hence, repeated exposure to displacement-related trauma might increase the risk of developing GAD.[1819202122232425262728293031323334]

The study was limited to only one valley district, and the findings of the study may not be generalizable to the displacement experiences faced by IDPs in the Hilly districts. The responses are based on recall of the respondents, and all biases inherent to recall may arise. However, the study is the first of its kind in Manipur using a validated research instrument and may be generalizable to the displacement experiences of the IDPs residing in relief camps across the valley districts of the state.

Conclusion and Recommendations

A majority of the respondents were found to have PTSD, and more than a third of the respondents were found to have moderate to severe anxiety. Urgent actions and mediations are needed from all stakeholders to improve the psychological and functional well-being of internally displaced persons. Mental health professionals must be actively involved for provision of psychological support and counseling. Mental health camps and psychosocial support helplines maybe established. Establishment of vocational training along with income generation activities may be taken up with the involvement of local CSOs (Civil Society Organizations). The government need to take up concrete measures to end the conflict situation at the earliest by initiating dialogs between the different communities and take up resettlement initiatives to prevent further deterioration of the mental health conditions of the IDPs.
