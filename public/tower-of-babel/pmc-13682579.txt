Socioeconomic status and loss to follow-up after orthopedic trauma surgery
Authors: Yvonne Tan, Mina Tohidi, Imran Syed, Jeff Yach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Follow-up after surgery is important, to monitor patient recovery and to identify potential complications. Our primary objective was to determine the association between patient socioeconomic status and loss to follow-up after orthopedic trauma surgery.Methods:We performed a retrospective cohort study of patients aged 18 to 75 years who underwent orthopedic trauma surgery at a trauma centre in Ontario between January 2022 and December 2023. Patient socioeconomic status was determined by linking postal code with Material and Social Deprivation Index and presented as quintiles. We measured clinic follow-up attendance for 6 months after index surgery. We used logistic regression modelling to estimate the association between material deprivation quintiles and loss to follow-up, accounting for baseline patients and perioperative factors.Results:The study cohort included 1764 patients. Approximately 1 in 4 patients missed at least 1 follow-up appointment in the 6 months after surgery. Odds of missed follow-up with no return were 83% higher in patients in the most deprived quintile, compared with patients who were least deprived (odds ratio 1.83, 95% confidence interval 1.09 to 3.08). Younger age and higher American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status score were associated with increased odds of loss to follow-up. Geographic distance to hospital was not independently associated with loss to follow-up.Conclusion:Loss to follow-up is common after orthopedic trauma surgery, and the risk is higher in patients who are most socioeconomically disadvantaged. Innovative solutions should address the needs of specific patient groups to optimize patient access to appropriate, timely follow-up after orthopedic trauma surgery.

Early and effective clinical care often determines the trajectory of long-term functional outcomes after orthopedic injuries.1 Loss to follow-up — a concept describing the disengagement of patients and participants from clinical appointments — has been associated with poor outcomes across various patient groups. For individuals undergoing orthopedic trauma procedures, postoperative outpatient follow-up allows for the opportunity to identify complications such as infection, venous thrombosis, and fracture nonunions or malunions.2 Missed appointments hinder the ability to address elements of a patient’s postoperative recovery, including the adjustment of weight-bearing status or of a physiotherapy regimen.2 Loss to follow-up also influences the orthopedic trauma surgery literature and clinical research — it has the potential to introduce bias into a study, thereby jeopardizing the validity of results.3

Although various studies have investigated factors specific to patients lost to follow-up across different patient populations, a limited number of studies have been conducted within the orthopedic trauma population. One systematic review found that males, younger patients, current tobacco users, and patients who were uninsured or had government insurance plans were commonly lost to follow-up after orthopedic trauma.4 However, although a small number of studies in the review found that individuals with higher income and education levels were less likely to be lost to follow-up, it was concluded that additional research is needed to validate these findings. Another retrospective chart review found that patients who used tobacco, lived more than 100 miles (160 km) from the clinic site, did not have private insurance, had an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status Classification System score higher than 2, or had a fracture of the hip or pelvis were significantly less likely to follow up after orthopedic trauma surgery.5 Nevertheless, although the study used tobacco as a proxy for lower socioeconomic status, it did not make conclusions regarding the impact of socioeconomic status on loss to follow-up. A retrospective cohort study found that Area Deprivation Index had a weak, negative correlation with clinic attendance rates after distal radius fracture treatment.6 This study focused only on a small subset of orthopedic trauma patients, with a specific type of fracture. Broader perspective is required.

Recognizing factors linked to loss to follow-up can enable surgeons to gain insight into early detection and effective mitigation of potential postoperative complications.2 Amid increasing attention on rising health care costs, enhancing adherence to follow-up appointments may also contribute to the optimization of health care resource utilization.5

Methods

This was a retrospective cohort study using electronic medical chart review. In the study, we included all patients aged 18 to 75 years who underwent orthopedic trauma surgery at Kingston Health Sciences Centre between January 2022 and December 2023. We excluded non-Ontario residents.

Baseline characteristics

We collected the following baseline patient demographic variables: age, sex, distance between home residence and hospital in kilometres, ASA score, and history of hospital admission in the previous 12 months (excluding visits related to index surgery).

We collected the following perioperative details: time-based urgency of booking (A: case should be performed emergently; B: case should be performed within 8 hours; C: case should be performed within 48 hours; and other), wound class (1: clean wound; 2: clean-contaminated; 3: contaminated; 4: dirty or infected), and inpatient status at the time of surgery.

Socioeconomic status

We determined patient socioeconomic status by linking residence postal code with the Material and Social Deprivation Index (MSDI). Postal codes were linked by Statistics Canada to standard Census geographic areas, Dissemination Areas (DAs), which are small regions used for distributing statistical data.7 The MSDI, developed by the Institut national de santé publique du Québec, uses Statistics Canada Census data to calculate both material and social deprivation scores for each DA.8 The MSDI separates DAs into deprivation quintiles based on 6 indicators: average income, proportion of individuals without a high school diploma, proportion of employed individuals, proportion of individuals living alone, proportion of lone-parent families, and proportion of separated, divorced, or widowed individuals.8 We used the 2016 MSDI, the most recent version of the index at time of this study. The accuracy of matching residential postal codes to Census geography was studied in a report by Statistics Canada,7 and the method was used in studies looking at surgical wait times,9 as well as other local studies conducted.10 We reported material deprivation quintiles, as described in similar literature looking at the association between surgical wait times and socioeconomic status in Ontario hospitals.11

Outcome measures: loss to follow-up

Loss to follow-up was measured in 4 ways: no-show for first follow-up clinic appointment, no-show for subsequent appointment(s) within 6 months, no-show for an appointment within the first 6 months without any further follow-up, and no-show for at least 1 appointment within 6 months. Patients were considered “no-show” if they did not attend a scheduled appointment without cancelling or rescheduling.

Statistical analysis

We completed univariate analyses for all variables, with means, medians, and proportions described as indicated. We calculated the association between baseline covariates and loss to follow-up using χ2, Student t, and Wilcoxon rank sum tests, as indicated.

We used logistic regression modelling to estimate the association between socioeconomic status and loss to follow-up, while accounting for baseline patient and perioperative variables. For the logistic regression model, we excluded patients with ASA score IV or higher from assessment, to minimize the effect of death as explanation for missing follow-up. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS Studio. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

We obtained ethics approval for this study from the Health Sciences Research Ethics Board at Queen’s University.

Results

During the 2-year study period, 1756 orthopedic trauma surgeries were performed in Ontario patients between the ages of 18 and 75 years at Kingston Health Sciences Centre. Baseline demographic data are described in Table 1. Mean patient age was 45.8 (standard deviation 21.1) years. Most patients had an ASA score of II or greater, and almost half of patients were admitted to hospital before undergoing surgery. Only 7.1% of cases (n = 124) were considered emergencies that should be performed within 8 hours of booking.

Loss to follow-up

Approximately 10.4% of patients missed their first follow-up appointment (n = 182), and approximately 17.5% missed a subsequent appointment within the first 6 months after index surgery (n = 307). One in 4 patients missed at least 1 follow-up appointment in the first 6 months (24.9%, n = 438). Finally, more than 16.3% missed a follow-up appointment in the first 6 months and never returned to the clinic for follow-up (n = 287).

The association between patient baseline covariates and perioperative factors and loss to follow-up is shown in Table 2. Patients who missed at least 1 appointment were, on average, 3 years younger than those who did not miss any appointments (mean age 43.6 v. 46.6 years, p = 0.01). Male patients were, on average, slightly more likely to miss an appointment and never return (18.3% male v. 14.2% female, p = 0.02). Patients living farther from hospital were more likely to miss their initial follow-up appointment and to miss an appointment and never return, but there was no statistically significant association between distance to hospital and the probability of missing any appointment(s).

Patients who were admitted to hospital before surgery were more likely to miss their first follow-up appointment (odds ratio [OR] 2.56, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.51 to 4.09), but we found no statistically significant differences between inpatients and outpatients for any subsequent follow-up outcome.

Multivariable analysis

After controlling for patient and perioperative factors, including distance from primary residence to hospital, we found that patients who were most deprived (quintile 5) were more likely to miss subsequent appointment(s) (OR 1.77, 95% CI 1.09 to 2.90), miss an appointment and never return (OR 1.83, 95% CI 1.09 to 3.08), and miss at least 1 appointment within 6 months after index surgery (OR 1.67, 95% CI 1.06 to 2.62) than those in the least deprived quintile (Table 3). Younger age and higher ASA score were also associated with higher odds of loss to follow-up. Distance to hospital was not independently associated with loss to follow-up, except for higher odds of missing the first follow-up appointment for patients living more than 200 km away from hospital compared with those living less than 50 km away (OR 2.56, 95% CI 1.26 to 5.18) (Table 3).

We found an inverse relationship between urgency of surgery and loss to follow-up. Patients who underwent emergent or urgent surgery had lower odds of loss to follow-up in the 6 months after surgery than patients who underwent surgery that was deemed to be less urgent (OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.21 to 0.88). Similarly, patients undergoing emergent or urgent surgery were 3 times less likely to miss follow-up appointments with no return (OR 0.27, 95% CI 0.09 to 0.77).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study shows that patients in the lowest socioeconomic status group are at higher risk of being lost to follow-up after orthopedic trauma surgery. This relationship remains statistically significant even after controlling for distance from patient residence to hospital.

We measured and reported on loss to follow-up in a number of ways. Factors that contribute to missing a first follow-up appointment may differ from those that are associated with missing subsequent appointments or missing an appointment without rescheduling, and it is valuable to assess these relationships separately. For example, patients with more severe injuries and those requiring longer hospital admissions may miss their first follow-up appointment because they are still in hospital or in a rehabilitation facility. This would explain the statistically significant associations seen in this study, with higher odds of missing the first follow-up appointment in inpatients and patients with increased comorbidity status. Interestingly, these associations were not present when we looked at subsequent follow-up appointments. Therefore, missing a first follow-up appointment may have more to do with logistics of recovery after surgery than socioeconomic determinants. Conversely, increased socioeconomic deprivation may play a larger role in missing subsequent follow-up appointments and missing an appointment without any rescheduling or follow-up.

Additionally, patients who are more materially or socially deprived may have challenges attending multiple follow-up appointments. This may present itself as missing some appointments and then rescheduling, or missing appointments without further follow-up. This study highlights the importance of how the varying definitions of loss to follow-up can capture different elements of the complex events and decisions made by patients and providers after surgery.

We found a trend toward increased probability of loss to follow-up as material deprivation increased. However, in regression analysis, only the most deprived patients had a statistically significant increased odds of loss to follow-up. The impact of marginalization is not always linear, and this important concept has been demonstrated in other literature. 12 Therefore, when evaluating the effect of socioeconomic status, marginalization and deprivation, it is important to consider the patient experiences of those who are most marginalized compared with those in the least marginalized groups.

Limitations

First, our variables to measure socioeconomic data are aggregated by dissemination area. Some postal codes, especially those in rural regions, may serve more than 1 dissemination area, and the MSDI uses a weighted conversion file to randomly assign a dissemination area in proportion to the distribution of population within that postal code. Therefore, there is a possibility of misclassification at the patient level. Furthermore, we had some missing data. Most importantly, approximately 5% of patients were not assigned a material deprivation quintile. This may have affected patients living in rural settings more so than those living in urban centres, with the potential that data from the most rural patients are not captured in our regression analysis.

In addition, death is a competing risk when considering loss to follow-up. Unfortunately, we did not have universal access to death records for patients in this cohort. We attempted to mitigate the potential effect of death as a competing risk in 2 ways. First, we restricted cohort age to 75 years, to decrease the chance that death would be a high-risk event in the cohort. Second, the logistic regression analysis excluded patients who were assigned ASA IV or higher at the time of surgery, to minimize the chance that study results were skewed by patients who missed follow-up because they were deceased. Future studies should consider competing risks analysis while taking risk of death into account.

Postal codes provided by patients faced with housing instability may not accurately reflect their degree of marginalization, potentially introducing some exposure misclassification. Unfortunately, these patients likely face the most difficulty with timely follow-up. According to our population estimates, approximately 10 patients from this cohort were facing homelessness or housing instability. Although this number is small and should not affect our overall results, the challenges faced by this patient group do warrant further attention and dedicated research.

Potential barriers faced by patients of low socioeconomic status should be identified and support offered to implement solutions to these barriers. Targeted programs — such as transportation assistance programs, telemedicine services, and culturally competent care — can enhance access to follow-up care for patients from disadvantaged socioeconomic backgrounds. Interventions addressing loss to follow-up for patients from low socioeconomic backgrounds can reduce the risk of complications associated with disengagement from care, in turn leading to better functional outcomes, decreased duration of pain, and lower rates of postoperative complications and mortality.13

Results from this study can contribute to important health equity research, advocacy, and policy development aimed at addressing systemic barriers to health care access and delivery.

Conclusion

Loss to follow-up is common after orthopedic trauma surgery, with approximately 1 in 4 patients missing at least 1 follow-up in the 6 months after surgery. Risk of loss to follow-up is higher for patients who are in the most marginalized socioeconomic group. Even in a publicly funded health care system that strives for equal access for all patients, socioeconomic status remains an important determinant affecting loss to follow-up. Results of this study can be used to advocate for innovative solutions to address the needs of specific patient groups and to enable all patients to have access to appropriate, timely follow-up after orthopedic trauma surgery. By identifying and addressing the structural challenges that contribute to loss to follow-up, health care systems can work toward reducing disparities, optimizing resource allocation, and improving long-term outcomes for all orthopedic trauma patients.
