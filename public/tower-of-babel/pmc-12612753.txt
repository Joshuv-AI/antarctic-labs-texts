Can STAT video review replace operative experience?
Authors: Elliot S Bishop, Elizabeth R Benjamin
Published: 2025

This proof-of-concept study evaluates the effectiveness of a just-in-time video review tool on procedural competency in the performance of decompressive fasciotomies and vascular control of axillary and femoral vessels in cadaveric models. Systematic assessment using subjective and objective metrics was used by evaluators to score participants divided into matched cohorts that differed in their access (or lack thereof) to the STAT video review tool. The authors found that use of the video refresher tool improved technical success in performance of the fasciotomies but not in vascular control.1

A notable strength of the study is the authors’ impressive technical development of the STAT software to fill the crucial need for a portable technical resource for deployed surgeons whose stateside practice may include few or no trauma operations. The authors accurately point out key study weaknesses, including the failure to meet goal recruitment. They explain the tool’s success by associating video review with the positive effects of visualization. Although mental rehearsal has a place in surgical performance optimization, equating these benefits to the surgeon using a just-in-time technical review is somewhat discordant.2 3 Elimination of the subjective evaluation components would have further strengthened the scoring data.

Although it does not take a study to tell us that pre-emptive review of a “how to” procedural video will improve aspects of performance, this study leads the reader to consider the ideal technical resource for military surgeons. There is little downside for the deployed surgeon to have a video-based refresher tool as a resource. Rather than the described just-in-time utilization, one could envision that the optimal application may be as a tool for general preparedness. In this manner, surgeons could use STAT to frequently review basic trauma operations that may be needed at a moment’s notice. Such a resource’s strength would lie in its simplicity and limited but high-yield scope and potential applicability to the civilian setting in conditions where experienced trauma providers are not readily available. As evidenced by the lack of benefit seen in the more complex vascular exposures, this resource could be considered a stepping stone to more comprehensive and interactive telementoring technology, which allows real-time guidance and technical expertise for complicating anatomic or injury-related factors beyond the “textbook scenario”.4 Although limited by the need for connectivity, the increasing use of satellite-based telecommunications technology makes telementoring a feasible reality anywhere in the world.5

Further studies, experience, and time will guide which technical resources can best serve our deployed surgeons. The video refresher tool used by the authors in this study is a creative and encouraging step in this line of inquiry.
