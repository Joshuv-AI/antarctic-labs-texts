Prevalence and associated factors of sarcopenia risk and possible sarcopenia among older homeless adults in a home for destitute, Thailand: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Phatcharaphon Whaikid, Noppawan Piaseu, Sukanya Tantiprasoplap, Suphanna Krongthaeo, Wasana Srisuk, Theeraphorn Jitwiriyatham, Jirapa Tantipongsirikul, Chanokporn Subtema
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenia threatens the functional ability of older adults and may be underdiagnosed in homeless populations. Older homeless adults face heightened risks due to malnutrition and comorbidities. However, evidence on sarcopenia in this group remains limited, particularly in institutional settings in Thailand. Understanding the prevalence and associated factors of sarcopenia in this vulnerable population is essential to guide early detection, tailored interventions, and policy development.ObjectivesTo investigate the prevalence of sarcopenia risk and possible sarcopenia, and to identify associated risk factors among older homeless adults.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in 2023 among homeless adults aged 50 years or older residing in a home for the destitute. Participants were purposively recruited based on predetermined inclusion criteria. Sarcopenia risk and possible sarcopenia were determined according to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 guidelines. Calf circumference was measured to identify sarcopenia risk, while handgrip strength and physical performance assessed using the Five-Times Sit-to-Stand Test were used to determine possible sarcopenia. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and multinomial logistic regression.ResultsOf the 163 participants, 74.9% were classified as having sarcopenia risk, while 71.2% had possible sarcopenia. Multinomial logistic regression analysis revealed that a low body mass index (BMI) was significantly associated with sarcopenia risk (OR = 0.538, 95% CI [0.359, 0.807], p = 0.003). Possible sarcopenia was significantly associated with older age (OR = 1.173, 95% CI [1.061, 1.298], p = 0.002), lower BMI (OR = 0.564, 95% CI [0.448, 0.710], p < 0.001), and the presence of comorbidities (OR = 5.045, 95% CI [1.025, 24.847], p = 0.047).ConclusionSarcopenia risk and possible sarcopenia are highly prevalent among older homeless adults. BMI was a key protective factor, while age and comorbidities significantly increased the risk. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions focusing on nutrition and early prevention strategies to mitigate sarcopenia in this vulnerable population. Nurses play a critical role in early screening and implementing tailored interventions to support prevention and management efforts.

Background

Homeless people represent one of the most vulnerable groups, and they often experience poorer health with symptoms of mental and physical illness (Bensken et al., 2021). Homeless people experience an accelerated aging process as compared to the general population, and evidence indicates that homeless individuals aged 50 and above are considered older adults (Fazel et al., 2014). It is recognized that older adults experiencing homelessness often show signs of frailty and cognitive decline at significantly younger ages and have a higher prevalence of dementia (68.7 per 1,000) compared with their housed counterparts (51.0 per 1,000) (Booth et al., 2024; Suh et al., 2022). As a result, the lifestyle and health outcomes of older homeless adults lead to a greater predisposition to developing sarcopenia than the general population, with a high prevalence of the condition among this group. Consequently, they face an increased risk of developing other diseases associated with sarcopenia, which will undoubtedly have economic implications and contribute to future burdens on Thailand.

Sarcopenia is a disease characterized by decreased muscle mass, strength, and function, primarily associated with aging (Cruz-Jentoft et al., 2019). Older adults with sarcopenia face an increased risk of falls, physical disability (Cruz-Jentoft et al., 2019), and heightened mortality rates (Xu et al., 2022). The overall global prevalence of sarcopenia is 23.5% (Petermann Rocha et al., 2022), while Thailand reported a similar rate of 20.7% (Whaikid & Piaseu, 2024). Factors related to sarcopenia include the aging process, which is associated with physiological changes (Granic et al., 2023). While females have a higher prevalence of sarcopenia than males (Hwang & Park, 2022), some studies have contradicted this finding and found that males may be more affected due to age-related declines in testosterone, a key anabolic hormone regulating muscle mass (Altaf et al., 2024; Shigehara et al., 2022). In addition, decreases in other hormones, such as estrogen, growth hormone, and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), have been implicated in the development of sarcopenia (Buckinx & Aubertin-Leheudre, 2022; Liu, Chen, et al., 2024; Lu & Tian, 2023). Furthermore, low body mass index (BMI) in older adults shows that people with insufficient nutrition often experience nutritional deficits that lead to sarcopenia (Curtis et al., 2023). Older adults with co-morbid chronic disease involve complex metabolic problems, which result in a loss of muscle mass (Xia et al., 2024). This highlights the multifaceted nature of factors contributing to sarcopenia development in older adults.

Sarcopenia is emerging as a foremost concern. However, traditional assessments of sarcopenia have been constrained by the difficulty of evaluating muscle mass outside of hospital settings due to limitations in assessment equipment and expertise. Hence, in 2019, the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) published updated consensus guidelines that introduced the concept of “possible sarcopenia,” characterized by low grip strength and reduced physical performance. Older adults with possible sarcopenia should modify their dietary and exercise behaviors to prevent progression (Chen et al., 2020). It should be noted that AWGS provides the criteria, while prevalence estimates are reported by studies that apply them. The prevalence of possible sarcopenia varies from 23.7% to 48.64% (Chen et al., 2022; Lim & Kong, 2022; Yao et al., 2022), highlighting a significant risk among the older population. This underscores the critical importance of early awareness and screening for possible sarcopenia. Proactive management at this stage can serve as a vital prevention strategy, reducing the risk of progression to sarcopenia.

At present, there is no study on factors associated with sarcopenia in older Thai homeless adults. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate the prevalence and associated factors in older homeless adults residing in homes for the destitute. This study aims to examine the prevalence and associated factors of sarcopenia risk and possible sarcopenia among Thai homeless people in homes for the destitute. Our study is the first study focused on Thai homeless people, a vulnerable group. The results emphasize the imperative to provide care and develop effective strategies to maintain muscle mass and prevent sarcopenia, which not only improve health outcomes but may also reduce healthcare expenditures and long-term care costs in this population.

Methods

Study Design

This was a cross-sectional study. The participants were older homeless adults residing in homes for the destitute in Thailand who had lived in these facilities for at least six months. The study site, the Nonthaburi Home for Destitute, was purposively selected as it represents one of the largest government-supported institutions for homeless older adults. Within this site, eligible participants were identified with the assistance of staff, and purposive sampling was applied according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) age ⩾ 50 years at the time of evaluation, (2) ability to understand and communicate in Thai. Those with weakness in both arms, those who were bedridden, or those who could not perform the sit-to-stand were excluded from the study.

Our study calculated the required sample size based on the multinomial logistic regression analysis. The sample size was determined using a power analysis (power = 0.95, significance level = 0.05) in G*Power version 3.1. The effect size of 0.061 (Liu, Zhu, et al., 2023) was used with six predictors, resulting in an initial required sample size of 175. Twelve participants were excluded due to their inability to complete the Five Times Sit-to-Stand (5TSTS) Test; thus, the final sample size was 163.

Data Collection

The data collection process in this study was meticulously designed to ensure comprehensive and reliable information. Personal and health information questionnaires were developed based on an extensive review of relevant literature, ensuring that the tools aligned with current evidence and best practices in the field. These questionnaires were carefully structured to capture essential variables, including demographic details (age and gender), and health-related factors (comorbidities, BMI, and depression). According to AWGS 2019 criteria, the assessment for possible sarcopenia involved measuring calf circumference, muscle strength with a handgrip dynamometer, and physical performance with a 5TSTS test. All assessments were performed by trained research assistants using standardized protocols. Assessors were not involved in participants’ routine care and were unaware of the specific study hypotheses, thereby minimizing observer bias (Figure 1).

Participant recruitment in this study

The diagnostic criteria for possible sarcopenia established by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) are as follows: Begin with identifying older adults at sarcopenia risk by measuring calf circumference (<33 cm for females and <34 cm for males). Then, assess muscle strength using handgrip strength (<18 kg for females and <28 kg for males) or/and the 5TSTS Test, where a completion time of more than 12 seconds indicates reduced muscle performance.

Calf circumference. Calf circumference was measured using an anthropometric tape, and the cutoff values were defined as less than 33 cm for women and 34 cm for men, consistent with the case-finding criteria.

Handgrip strength (HGS). Muscle strength was evaluated using a digital handgrip dynamometer (Takei 5401, Takei, Tokyo, Japan) calibrated according to ISO guidelines. Each participant performed the test while standing with arms extended, instructed to exert maximal force. The handle setting was adjusted to match the individual’s hand size, and two measurements were taken; the mean was used for analysis. Handgrip strength values below 28 kg for men and 18 kg for women were interpreted as low muscle strength.

5-Times Sit-to-Stand (5TSTS) Test. Physical performance was assessed using the 5TSTS Test. Timing started from the initial sitting position and ended when the participant was fully seated after completing the fifth stand. A duration of 12 seconds or longer was considered indicative of low physical performance.

Data Analysis

The personal information was analyzed using descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations (SDs). We employed an ENTER multinomial logistic regression analysis to identify factors associated with sarcopenia risk and possible sarcopenia. Results were presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Age, BMI, fasting blood sugar (FBS), and depression were treated as continuous variables, whereas gender and comorbidities were treated as categorical variables. The data were presented collectively without disclosing participants' identities. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 21 for Windows.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted as part of the project “Development of a Health Care System and Surveillance at the Nonthaburi Home for the Destitute.” Ethical approval was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee at a university in Thailand (MURA2022/690 and MURA2025/573). We provide comprehensive research details to the participants, who could independently decide to participate and provide signed consent. Moreover, all participants provided written consent prior to study participation and were clearly informed that they could withdraw at any time without consequences. Their information was treated with strict confidentiality.

Results

The study initially enrolled 175 participants; however, 12 were excluded because they were unable to complete the 5TSTS Test. Finally, the data of 163 participants were analyzed. The possible sarcopenia indices —calf circumference, handgrip strength (HGS), and the 5TSTS Test—revealed the prevalence of sarcopenia among older homeless adults. Results showed that the prevalence of sarcopenia risk and possible sarcopenia was 74.9% and 71.2%, respectively.

Among the total participants, low calf circumference was observed in 74.9%, low handgrip strength in 87.7%, and poor performance on the 5TSTS in 74.9%. The BMI distribution indicated that 38.7% had a normal BMI, 29.9% had a BMI below 18.5 kg/m², and 11.0% had a BMI between 25.0 and 29.9 kg/m². The most prevalent chronic illnesses were psychiatric conditions (54.2%), followed by hypertension (17.4%) and dyslipidemia (14.6%). Comorbidity data showed that 55.2% had one comorbidity, while 14.1% had two or more. FBS levels were <100 mg/dL in 59.5% of participants, while 40.5% had FBS levels ≥100 mg/dL (Table 1).

Discussion

This study found that among older homeless adults aged 50 and above, the prevalence of sarcopenia risk and possible sarcopenia was 74.9% and 71.2%, respectively. Especially, the prevalence of possible sarcopenia in this study was considerably higher compared to that of non-older homeless adults, which ranged from 46% to 48.8% (Chen et al., 2022; Liu, Liu, et al., 2024). The difference in prevalence estimates may be attributed to variations in lifestyle factors. This population is at a higher risk of developing sarcopenia earlier than the general older population due to several factors, including accelerated aging. Homeless individuals often experience premature aging as a result of chronic stress, poor nutrition, and limited access to healthcare (Fazel et al., 2014).

Our study identifies BMI as a key protective factor against both sarcopenia risk and possible sarcopenia, while age is a major driver of sarcopenia progression. BMI consistently reduced the risk of both conditions. In contrast, age significantly increased the likelihood of possible sarcopenia but did not affect sarcopenia risk, suggesting its influence intensifies in later stages. Comorbidity was also significantly associated with possible sarcopenia, further emphasizing the vulnerability of individuals with complex health profiles. Meanwhile, FBS and depression showed no significant associations with either condition (p > 0.05), suggesting a limited role of metabolic and psychological factors in sarcopenia risk within this population. Sarcopenia Risk and possible sarcopenia represent progressive stages of muscle loss and functional decline. This study reveals a key finding: while BMI serves as a protective factor against both conditions, age significantly contributes only to possible sarcopenia. This suggests that age plays a pivotal role in later-stage muscle deterioration, whereas maintaining a higher BMI may help mitigate sarcopenia risk across both stages.

Age was identified as a significant predictor of possible sarcopenia but showed no significant association with sarcopenia risk. This suggests that aging may not be a major determinant in the early stages of sarcopenia but becomes a crucial factor as the condition progresses. Specifically, each additional year of age significantly increased the likelihood of possible sarcopenia (OR = 1.173, p = 0.002), reinforcing the well-established role of aging in muscle deterioration. Consistent with previous studies, advancing age is associated with a decline in mitochondrial function, resulting in impaired energy production and increased oxidative stress. These changes can subsequently damage muscle cells (Agostini et al., 2023), thereby contributing to the development of sarcopenia. This aligns with previous research indicating that muscle mass decreases by 1–2% per year after age 50, leading to a cumulative loss of 30-50% by age 80, while muscle strength declines by 10-15% per decade until age 70 and then accelerates to 25–40% per decade (Eckstrom, 2023); importantly, low muscle strength is the primary determinant of possible sarcopenia (Chen et al., 2023).

Our study indicated that a lower BMI was associated with an increased risk of sarcopenia and possible sarcopenia, aligning with prior evidence suggesting that BMI significantly influences muscle mass and physical strength, especially among sedentary older adults (Buchmann et al., 2022; Hashim et al., 2023). Lower BMI is often associated with increased muscle loss, driven by mechanisms such as low-grade inflammation and metabolic syndrome (Buchmann et al., 2022). Malnutrition and inadequate protein intake, both common among individuals with lower BMI, further contribute to muscle decline and sarcopenia (Shaw et al., 2017). The current dietary recommendation is to consume at least 0.8 g/kg/day of protein to maintain muscle health (Black & Matkin-Hussey, 2024). Previous research has confirmed that appropriate protein supplementation combined with resistance training can improve muscle mass and strength (Whaikid & Piaseu, 2024). However, some studies suggest that a higher BMI may also increase sarcopenia risk, possibly reflecting increased visceral fat accumulation, a precise indicator associated with muscle deterioration (Feng et al., 2022; Liu, Wong, et al., 2023). Thus, maintaining an appropriate BMI appears essential for reducing the risk of sarcopenia.

Older homeless adults, the focus of this study, typically face multiple life challenges that contribute to poor health outcomes. Factors such as unemployment and limited education often lead to adverse health behaviors and weight issues (Lotfata et al., 2022). Furthermore, income strongly influences health outcomes by shaping nutritional choices, dietary behaviors, and overall lifestyle (Anekwe et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2019; Sawchuk, 2019). Although participants in our research currently reside in a home for the destitute, their access to nutritious food remains limited and often beyond their control. This restricted access likely contributes to their notably low BMI (< 18.5), which was prevalent among approximately 38.6% of participants, placing them at heightened risk for sarcopenia.

Our study identified the comorbidities as significantly associated with possible sarcopenia, aligning with previous research linking multiple chronic conditions, such as chronic inflammation, malnutrition, and physical inactivity, to increased sarcopenia risk (Damluji et al., 2023; He et al., 2021). Chronic diseases, especially Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), directly influence muscle health through mechanisms involving insulin resistance, inflammation-induced cytokine release, oxidative stress (Chen et al., 2023; Marcotte-Chénard et al., 2023), and mitochondrial dysfunction, all of which impair muscle protein synthesis and energy metabolism (Zhang et al., 2024). Additionally, hypertension, which often co-occurs with insulin resistance, exacerbates muscle deterioration by enhancing angiotensin II and aldosterone activity, increasing sympathetic nervous system activity, and elevating oxidative stress (Li et al., 2019; Masi et al., 2019). Consistent with prior findings, our results suggest individuals with multiple comorbidities are at higher risk for developing possible sarcopenia, indicating the need for proactive screening and early interventions. Since possible sarcopenia represents a precursor stage of sarcopenia, early identification and management of comorbid conditions may prevent or delay the progression to full-blown sarcopenia. This highlights the importance of integrated, multidisciplinary care approaches tailored to older adults with complex health profiles (Xia et al., 2024).

Although gender was not significantly associated with either sarcopenia risk or possible sarcopenia in this study, its role in sarcopenia development is multifaceted. Biological and hormonal differences contribute to disparities in muscle mass and function between male and female (Buckinx & Aubertin-Leheudre, 2022; Lu & Tian, 2023). Males typically have greater muscle strength due to higher testosterone levels, which enhance muscle protein synthesis. Conversely, women often experience an accelerated decline in muscle strength, particularly following menopause, due to estrogen deficiency (Geraci et al., 2021). Females often experience a rapid decline in muscle strength after menopause due to estrogen deficiency. Despite having lower baseline muscle strength than males, older females tend to experience a greater decline, particularly between ages 70 and 74, with strength reductions of approximately 3.75 kg in females and 3.37 kg in males (Greco et al., 2019; Wisniowska Szurlej et al., 2021). These gender-specific hormonal and physiological differences may help explain the observed variations in sarcopenia risk and possible sarcopenia within our study population.

Our study found no significant association between fasting blood sugar (FBS) and either sarcopenia risk or possible sarcopenia. This finding may be attributed to the fact that the assessment of possible sarcopenia in our study was based on calf circumference (CC), HGS, and the 5TSTS, which primarily evaluate muscle strength and physical performance rather than metabolic dysfunction as defined by the AWGS 2019 guidelines (Chen et al., 2020). The relationship between FBS and 5TSTS performance remains poorly validated in the literature. While the 5TSTS test has shown a moderate correlation with muscle strength in individuals with severe mental illness (Lopez Moral et al., 2025), it primarily assesses lower limb strength rather than overall physical performance. Moreover, although chronic hyperglycemia can contribute to muscle degradation over time (Filgueiras et al., 2023), its immediate impact on physical function appears limited. Additionally, individuals with elevated FBS may maintain muscle function through effective glycemic control, which could obscure any observable association.

Our study found no significant association between depression and sarcopenia risk or possible sarcopenia. One possible explanation is the very low prevalence of depression in our participants (3.68%), which limited the statistical power to detect an association. While previous studies suggest that depression contributes to muscle loss (Jiang et al., 2025) through chronic inflammation, oxidative stress (Hassamal, 2023), and reduced physical activity (Codella & Chirico, 2023), its impact may be more apparent in populations with higher depression rates or more severe symptoms.

This study uniquely investigates possible sarcopenia in older homeless adults, an understudied population prone to premature aging and multiple comorbidities, providing critical insights for targeted public health interventions. Multinomial logistic regression improved risk factor identification and enhanced predictive precision for interventions. However, this study has limitations, including the absence of data on potentially influential variables such as physical activity and nutrition, a slightly smaller sample size, and a small effect size, all of which may reduce statistical power. In addition, the sarcopenia risk and possible sarcopenia groups were not entirely mutually exclusive, which may compromise category independence, and some estimates (e.g., gender and comorbidities) had wide confidence intervals due to sparse data. Single-site data limit generalizability, and excluding 12 participants may have underestimated sarcopenia prevalence. The cross-sectional design also restricts causal inference, highlighting the need for longitudinal research.

Despite these limitations, the findings have practical implications, highlighting the need for early screening and tailored interventions, particularly in nutrition and physical activity, to reduce the progression of sarcopenia among older homeless adults. These findings also underscore the importance of integrating community-based sarcopenia screening and management into holistic care models for vulnerable older adults worldwide. Future research should focus on longitudinal, interventional, and policy-driven studies to guide sustainable care models for this vulnerable group.

Conclusion

This study identifies age and BMI as key predictors of possible sarcopenia among older homeless adults, with lower BMI significantly increasing risk. These findings emphasize the crucial role of body composition in muscle preservation and the progression of sarcopenia. Given the heightened vulnerability of this population, targeted interventions focusing on nutritional support, weight maintenance, and routine screening are essential to prevent sarcopenia-related functional decline. Implementing these strategies within supportive housing could significantly improve overall health outcomes and quality of life for older homeless individuals.
