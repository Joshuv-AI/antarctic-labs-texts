Neutral chiral bidentate tellurium-triazoles for enantioselective non-covalent chalcogen-bonding catalysis
Authors: Lary Massold, James O’Brien, Kevin Fengler, Alica C. Keuper, Raquel Hidalgo-León, Cristina Trujillo, Olga García Mancheño
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chalcogen-bonding (ChB) has recently garnered significant interest in the field of non-covalent catalysis, providing new possibilities through σ-hole interactions. However, the advancement of the field in enantioselective catalysis relying purely on ChB has been impeded by the inherent directionality of the interaction and presence of several σ-holes on the chalcogen atom, being particularly challenging with neutral ChB-donors. Herein, we report the design and application of chiral ChB-donors able to accomplish the still challenging asymmetric chalcogen-bonding catalysis with neutral ChB systems, reaching enantioselectivities of up to 11:89 e.r. in the anion-binding benchmark Reissert-type reaction of (iso)quinolines. Optimization of the chiral central backbone in rather simple bistriazole-bistelluride-based structures to accomplish a bidentate binding to Lewis basic substrates is performed, leading to the identification of the (R,R)−1,3-diamino cyclohexane moiety as appropriate chiral spacer. While no binding is observed for other chiral central backbones, a moderate affinity to chloride anion as model substrate is recorded for the optimized neutral ChB-donor, for which key binding features are further revealed upon computational analysis on the corresponding catalyst-chloride complex.

Introduction

Chalcogen bonding (ChB)1–4 has emerged as a distinctive class of σ-hole interactions with growing relevance in non-covalent organocatalysis5–13. Unlike classical hydrogen bonding (HB)14–16, and along the lines of the more extensively explored σ-hole interactions in halogen bonding (XB)17,18, ChB offers a unique combination of strong directionality ( ~ 180°) and electronic tunability arising from the anisotropic electron density distribution at the chalcogen center. Although the Lewis acidic character of organochalcogen compounds has been recognized for several decades19,20, the deliberate use of chalcogen bonding as a catalytic activation mode has only gained momentum in recent years6–13. This delayed development reflects both the promise of ChB interactions and the intrinsic challenges associated with controlling their strength and selectivity, especially in asymmetric catalytic applications (Fig. 1A). The chalcogen bond originates from an electron-deficient region (σ-hole) located along the extension of a Ch–R bond, which interacts attractively with a Lewis-basic site21,22. This interaction can be modulated through the choice of the chalcogen atom and its substituents. However, in contrast to charged systems, neutral chalcogen-bond donors generally exhibit only modest Lewis acidity, resulting in weak substrate binding and limited activation. As a consequence, many catalytic applications employ cooperative activation modes, in which ChB interactions support stronger covalent23–26 or non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, halogen bonding, or π–π interactions27–30. In contrast, catalytic systems in which substrate activation is dominated by neutral, non-covalent ChB interactions remain rare. The first examples of ChB catalysis were reported by Matile and co-workers in 2017 using dithieno-thiophenes in hydrogen transfer reactions31,32. Right after, Huber and co-workers made a series of influential contributions, although yet relying on charged cationic systems bearing charged heterocyclic residues linked to selenium or tellurium atoms33–37. Despite this progress38–47, the design of efficient and predictable non-covalent ChB catalysts remains non-trivial, in part due to the presence of multiple accessible σ-holes at a single chalcogen center, which complicates controlled substrate recognition and activation (Fig. 1A, left).

These difficulties become even more pronounced in the context of enantioselective catalysis. While asymmetric induction via HB6–13 and XB17,18,48–53 is well established, enantioselective chalcogen-bond catalysis remains largely underdeveloped, and only a limited number of chiral ChB donors have been described to date (Fig. 1B)54–56. In 2019, Mamane and co-workers reported an axially chiral selenide catalyst (ChB*-Se) for the reduction of quinolines54, whereas Huber’s group developed centrally chiral bistriazolium-based tellurium catalysts (ChB-Te*) for a Povarov reaction55. Later on, our group introduced a class of chiral ChB catalysts with the stereochemical information embedded within a trans−1,2-diaminocyclohexane backbone for the oxidative functionalization of tetrahydroisoquinolines56. Notably, these systems did not provide significant enantioinduction. More recently, Kumar and Hodík showed that bidentate axial-chiral binaphthyl-selenophenes (Ch+B*), cationic at the chalcogen atoms, were efficient in inducing chirality in an aza-Henry reaction57. However, these strategies underscore fundamental limitations in the field: the persistent difficulty of translating chiral information into effective asymmetric control when relying on weak, non-covalent ChB interactions, while the design of neutral chalcogen-bond structures capable of effecting enantioselective catalysis persists as a challenge.

Encouraged by these barely addressed, unresolved issues in ChB catalysis—and particularly by the question of whether neutral σ-hole donors can be rendered catalytically competent in an enantioselective manner—we envisioned the development of neutral chiral ChB catalysts based on simple, bidentate bistriazole–telluride structures (Fig. 1C). We aimed to adjust the distance and relative orientation between the triazole–tellurium units to enable cooperative substrate binding within a confined chiral environment, thereby promoting efficient chirality transfer. To guide this process, a computationally assisted approach was employed to evaluate binding geometries and interaction strengths. Given the intrinsically weak affinity of neutral chalcogen-bond donors toward Lewis-basic substrates, an anion-binding activation strategy58 was deliberately chosen to reinforce catalyst–substrate association and formation of a key chiral contact ion-pair responsible for the chirality induction. Herein, we demonstrate that neutral chalcogen-bond interactions, despite their inherent weakness, can be harnessed to achieve enantioselective catalysis, which was shown in an enantioselective benchmark anion-binding catalytic Reissert-type reaction of (iso)quinolines (Fig. 1C)58–60.

Results and discussion

To validate our hypothesis, we chose the model Reissert-type reaction59,60 of quinolinium chloride salts, generated in situ from quinolines and 2,2,2-trichlorethyl chloroformate (TrocCl), with silylketene acetals as nucleophiles (Fig. 2)61–64. We started our investigation by analyzing the possible catalyst chiral backbones toward a bidentate binding modus to the chloride anion of the substrate and thus ensured the formation of the required chiral contact ion-pair complex for achieving enantioinduction. Thus, we decided to perform a rational computational-assisted design of a new small family of central chiral ChB-donors 1 for the development of enantioselective ChB-catalysis (Fig. 3A). In particular, we looked into different substitution patterns at a chiral cyclohexane backbone, bearing either a 1,2-substitution61–64 like in 1a and 1b (the latter with a methylene spacer) or a 1,3-substitution on the cyclohexyl-backbone (1c).Fig. 2Application in enantioselective catalysis.A Catalysts’ screening in the model reaction of 2a. Prior to DFT optimization, the conformational space of ChB‑donor structures 1a–c was systematically explored; subsequent geometry optimizations at the ωB97x‑D3/def2‑SVP level with SMD(Et₂O) solvation (see S.I. for computational details and conformational analyses)65 were then used to refine the accessible conformer ensemble of the isolated catalysts (Fig. 3A). For all three catalysts, the lowest‑energy conformers did not present an accessible bidentate binding arrangement. However, only 1c displayed an additional conformer compatible with bidentate chloride binding, whereas no such bidentate conformers were identified for 1a or 1b.

To assess whether this bidentate arrangement is realistically accessible, the rotational barrier between the dominant and bi-dentate conformers was evaluated. The results revealed that 1c displayed markedly greater flexibility than 1a and 1b, with a lower rotational barrier of 13 kcal/mol relative to the 20 kcal/mol required for 1a and 1b. Thus, enabling 1c to readily adopt the bidentate mode required for bidentate chloride binding more readily. The conformational space of the catalysts whilst engaging with the chloride was subsequently examined. Catalyst 1a exhibited multiple accessible binding modes, generating a non-selective ensemble. In contrast, catalyst 1b, adopted a far more restrictive conformational ensemble, yet its primary binding mode oriented the chloride towards the solvent, creating an unfavorable environment for enantioinduction. Lastly, catalyst 1c combined conformational restriction with chloride binding inside the catalytic pocket, providing a promising environment for enantioinduction. On this basis, catalyst 1c emerges as the most promising catalytic structure for further development in enantioselective ChB-catalysis.

To test the validity of the computational analysis, the bis-tellurium-triazoles 1a-c were synthesized from the corresponding proto-triazole derivatives by a deprotonation-tellurium incorporation strategy35 (Fig. 3B, see S.I. for full details). It is important to note that the use of an activated electrophile Te-reagent arylbromotelluride (RTeBr), prepared in situ from the commonly used diarylditelluride and bromine66,67, was needed in order to achieve the desired products in satisfactory yields (66-76%). Additionally, aiming at enhancing the ChB-donor character and performance, 1 d presenting an electron-deficient 3,5-(CF3)2C6H3 substituent at the tellurium atom was also prepared.

Once having the novel tellurium-triazoles 1a-d in hand, their catalytic ability in the anion-binding catalyzed enantioselective Reissert-type58–60 reaction of quinoline 2a with silyl ketene acetal 3a was investigated (Fig. 2A). Thus, after pre-activation of the quinoline with TrocCl in Et2O to form the N-acylquinolinium chloride salt, 2.5 mol% of the corresponding ChB-catalyst (1a-d) and the nucleophile were added to the reaction at −78 °C. As anticipated, neither the catalyst 1a nor 1b with a chiral 1,2-substituted cyclohexyl backbone was efficient, delivering high yields ( > 99%) but no enantioselectivity. Conversely, catalyst 1c with the predicted larger and bidentate binding-pocket due to the 1,3-substituted cyclohexyl structure led to the product 4a with a promising first enantioselectivity of 24:76 e.r. (in favor of (S)-4a)52, along with an excellent >99% yield. The introduction of electron-deficient aryl groups to the tellurium atoms in catalyst 1 d, which should provide it with higher Lewis acidity, also led to full conversion and a higher enantiomeric ratio of 17:83 e.r. Moreover, the parent H-bonding catalyst 1e led to diminished both yield and enantioselectivity (15%, 47:53 e.r.), suggesting the ChB interactions as the responsible for the observed enantioselectivity. These findings led to the conclusion that the cavities of catalysts 1c and 1 d are better suited for allocating the tellurium units and allowing an efficient interaction with the ionic substrate to achieve chirality. Lastly, catalysts bearing other σ-hole donor atoms than Te, such as iodo or other Ch like S or Se, were able to catalyze the reaction efficiently (up to >99%), but no enantioinduction could be achieved. Hence, the Te-based catalyst with the 1,3-substituted cyclohexyl backbone was found to be the most suitable candidate for enantioselective non-covalent ChB catalysis.

Intrigued by these findings, we decided to optimize the conditions for the model reaction with ChB catalyst 1c, which proved synthetically more accessible than 1 d within significantly higher overall yield (Fig. 3B, see S.I. for full screening). Different solvents were first evaluated (entries 1-7). While solvents such as toluene (entry 2) or CH2Cl2 (entry 3) lead to a good yield of 88%, an almost racemic product was obtained. The best results in terms of enantioselectivity were achieved with ethereal solvents, in particular the mixture n-propyl and ethyl ethers (1:2) (entry 7) which led to 56% yield and the enhanced enantioselectivity of 19:81 e.r. (for full solvent screening, see S.I. Table S2).

The influence of the amount of nucleophile 3a was next investigated. It was observed that an equimolecular amount of nucleophile (1 vs. 2 equiv.) was benign for the outcome of the reaction, providing an increased enantioselectivity of 13:87 e.r. (entry 9). This seems to be an important parameter, as an excess of nucleophile might lead to larger amounts of its hydrolysis by-products, which can poison the catalyst by undesired binding to the active σ-hole site(s). Thus, although accompanied by a slight penalty on the yield, 1 equiv. The nucleophile used was to obtain the highest enantioselectivity. At this stage, the reaction with catalyst 1 d was performed (entry 10). While a significantly higher reactivity was observed, 1 d led to no improvement in the enantioselectivity, providing the same initial 17:83 e.r. recorded in Et2O. Therefore, we continued our study with catalyst 1c.

Lastly, the catalyst loading of 1c was optimized. Reactions with 5.0, 3.5, and 1.0 mol% were carried out (entries 11-13). However, though higher yields (60-71%) were obtained with 3.5 and 5.0 mol%, lower enantioselectivities were recorded. This counterintuitive effect was observed with some of our previously reported triazole-based hydrogen58–60 and halogen-bonding61 catalysts, which have been attributed to an aggregation of the more efficient monomeric species by higher catalyst loadings. The use of just 1.0 mol% was still suitable, but with a slight detriment in both yield and enantioinduction (entry 13). Since the enantioselectivity of the reaction with 2.5 mol% gave the best enantioselectivity, this catalyst loading was chosen for as optimal.

In the next step, the scope and limitations of the ChB-catalytic reaction were explored (Fig. 2C). To this aim, various silyl ketene acetals 3 bearing linear (Me, Et, n-Pr, n-Bu) or branched bulky (t-Bu) alkyl chains were first tested for their suitability as nucleophiles. The best enantioselectivity could be achieved with the n-Bu nucleophile 3e with an enantiomeric ratio of 12:88 e.r. Since the yield of the reaction decreased to 34%, the “standard” i-Pr substituted silyl ketene acetal 3a was chosen for further investigations because it delivered a similar enantioselectivity of 13:87 e.r. and a higher yield of 54%. We continued the study by exploring the substrate scope. At first, a methyl group was rotated around the quinoline core. Substitution in C4-C7 position was well-tolerated, leading to moderate to good yields (4g-j, up to 71%) and similar enantioselectivities of up to 13:87 e.r. Changing the substituents to ethers, a benign effect on the enantioselectivity could be observed (4k-o). With the use of methoxy-groups in C5-, C6-, and C7-position, enantioselectivities of up to 11:89 e.r. and yields of up to 64% could be achieved. Substituting the C7-position with a butoxy- or benzyloxy lead to 53% and 67% yields and enantioselectivities up to 22:78 e.r. Interestingly, the incorporation of a halide moiety in the quinoline, which we previously used to provide extra interactions with the related iodo-triazole catalyst53, led to a diminished yield and no enantioselectivity (4p). This might indicate a poisoning of the ChB-donor by blocking one of the σ-holes of the catalyst, weakening the activation and changing to a less favored substrate-catalyst orientation. However, it was possible to have a bulky Bpin-moiety in the quinoline core (4q), although a lower yield of 29% and moderate enantioselectivity of 37:63 e.r. were achieved. Furthermore, other nucleophiles such as silylphosphites proved also compatible with the catalytic system, leading to the phosphorylated products 4s and 4t in good yields and similar levels of enantioselectivity (up to 70% and 14:86 e.r.).

Next, we expanded the scope to the related isoquinolines. At first, we tested simple isoquinoline in the dearomatization reaction, which provided the product 4 u in 55% yield and moderate enantioselectivity of 36:64 e.r. Since the best results with quinolines were observed with electron-donating groups, we decided to also test methoxy-substituted isoquinolines. While with the C6-OMe derivative (4w) no enantioselectivity was observed, the C5- (4v) and C7-OMe (4x) substituted derivatives led to improved enantioselectivities of up to 25:75 e.r., showing the possibility to extend the method to other heterocycles.

To bring some light into the key non-covalent interactions governing this catalytic process, the binding modus was analyzed both experimentally and computationally. First, the binding affinity to chloride as a model Lewis basic substrate was evaluated for both ChB-catalysts 1a and 1c upon 125Te and 1H NMR titrations using tetrabutylammonium chloride (TBACl) as a chloride source (see S.I. for details). The titrations were performed in THF-d8 (5 mM) instead of linear ethereal solvents at room temperature to ensure homogeneous solutions and reliable data sets. Although, higher anion-binding affinities to chloride can be expected at lower temperatures (i.e., −78 °C in the reaction vs. 20 °C for the binding studies) and in solvents such as Et2O or the Pr₂O/Et₂O mixture, as those are less coordinative and compete less for the σ-holes, these studies provide a qualitative trend that allows the comparison of the relative anion-binding strength/ability of the series of ChB donors. As expected, a dismissed or no binding was monitored for catalyst 1a with the chiral 1,2-cyclohexane-backbone, whereas 1c and 1 d, presenting the chiral 1,3-cyclohexane-backbone, showed significant binding constants. 1 d presenting electron-withdrawing 3,5-(CF3)2-phenyl groups at the Te-atoms provided the highest binding affinity (Ka~1600 M−1 by 125Te-NMR and Ka ~ 877 M−1 by 1H-NMR), whereas a moderate binding constant of ~50 M−1 (Ka = 61.7 M−1 by 125Te NMR, and Ka = 48.2 M−1 by 1H NMR) was determined for 1c. However, this seems sufficient to allow for effective substrate recognition and chirality transfer. A combination of quantum theory of atoms in molecules (QTAIM) and natural bond orbital (NBO) analyses confirmed that complex 1c-Cl- forms ChB interactions with the chloride anion through the Ph–Te catalytic arms (Fig. 4Aa). The binding occurs in a bidentate fashion, as evidenced by the presence of two simultaneous ChB interactions (Ph–Te···Cl⁻), which result in a substantial total charge transfer of 35.3 kcal mol⁻¹ from the chloride lone pairs into the Te σ* orbitals65. In addition to these dominant ChB interactions, three ancillary hydrogen bonds between the chloride ion and the hydrogen atoms of the catalyst were also identified, providing an additional total charge transfer of 17.0 kcal/mol. These secondary contacts act cooperatively to anchor the anion within the binding pocket, thereby reinforcing the stability and orientation of the complex. All involved areas of depleting electron density are further confirmed by the green isosurfaces present in the NCI analysis (Fig. 4Ab).

To investigate the apparent preference for ChB formation between the Ph-Te σ-hole and the chloride ion relative to the Triazole-Te σ-hole, the molecular electrostatic potential (ESP) of the lowest-energy non-bidentate isolated 1c structure was analyzed (Fig. 4Ba). This study shows similar ESP values for both the Ph-Te (35.8 kcal/mol and 47.0 kcal/mol) and Triazole-Te σ-holes (36.0 kcal/mol and 42.5 kcal/mol). Furthermore, the ESP of the lowest‑energy bidentate conformer was examined by removing the chloride ion from the optimized 1c–Cl⁻ complex (Fig. 4Bc). In this case, substantially increased ESP values of 49.1 and 45.1 kcal mol⁻¹ for the Ph–Te σ‑holes can be observed, most probably due to overlap associated with the bidentate binding mode.

The introduction of the 3,5-(CF3)2-phenyl groups in 1d significantly increases the ESP resulting from the Ar-Te σ-holes ( ~ 8 kcal/mol) (Fig. 4Bb). However, these groups also amplify the ESP for the triazole-based Tr-Te σ-holes by approximately 13 kcal/mol each. Consequently, the Ar–Te σ-hole becomes more positive, and the catalyst’s overall ability to selectively engage chloride is improved, although it might lead to less defined complexation and substrate fixation. This is further supported by analysis of the 1d–Cl⁻ complex, where removal of the chloride ion reveals a 63.3 kcal mol⁻¹ ESP at a Tr–Te σ-hole directed toward solvent, comparable to the 64.5 kcal mol⁻¹ ESP of the corresponding Ar–Te σ-hole pointing to the catalyst cavity (Fig. 4Bd). These results suggest that while –CF3 substitution on the aryl group directly bound to the Te atom enhances overall σ-hole strength, it also lowers differentiation and increases competing interactions at the triazole sites, potentially reducing the binding selectivity of catalyst 1d.

In conclusion, we have reported new neutral chiral bidentate ChB-donors for effective enantioselective ChB-catalysis. One of the keys to success was the use of a computational-aided design, which allowed the identification of an appropriate chiral central backbone, such as the (R,R)−1,3-diamine cyclohexane moiety, to link the active binding triazole-telluride units. Hence, the new structure class presents a bidentate coordination mode within the chiral environment of the catalyst’s binding pocket, leading to effective enantioinduction by Ch-bonding as the main activation mode, especially using neutral catalyst structures. Thus, enantioselectivities of up to 11:89 e.r. in the test benchmark Reissert-type reaction of (iso)quinolines were achieved. Key binding features were further revealed upon computational analysis of the corresponding catalyst-chloride and catalyst-chloride-substrate complexes. Thus, a bidentate ChB-interaction, along with three further HB-contacts between the catalyst and chloride anion, was disclosed, allowing for an efficient binding of the anion within the catalyst binding pocket. Moreover, this study revealed the beneficial substitution with electronically distinct rests at the Ch atom, as well as that the σ-holes of the Te-atoms in catalyst 1c involved in the ChB-interactions are the ones generated along the extension of the Ar-Te instead of the initially expected triazole-Te bonds to favor the cooperative binding. Taking this key information into account, further investigations and optimization for a more efficient fixation of the substrate and chirality transfer are currently being conducted in our group.

Methods

General procedure for the catalytic reaction

In a flame-dried Schlenk tube, the corresponding (iso)quinoline 2 (0.1 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) was dissolved in a mixture of nPr2O/Et2O (1:2) (2.0 mL). After cooling down to 0 °C, 2,2,2-trichlorethyl chloroformate (TrocCl) (13.8 μL, 0.1 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) was added. After stirring the mixture for 30 min at 0 °C, it was cooled down to −78 °C. Subsequently, ChB-catalyst 1c (2.6 mg, 2.5 mol%) was added, and the mixture was stirred for 10 min. After that, the corresponding nucleophile 3 (0.1 mmol,1.0 equiv.) was added, and the reaction mixture was stirred overnight. Subsequently, a small amount of SiO2 was added, and the solvents were removed under reduced pressure. The crude was purified by column chromatography to obtain the desired product 4.
