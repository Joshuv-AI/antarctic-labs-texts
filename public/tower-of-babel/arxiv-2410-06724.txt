Does small-scale turbulence matter for ice growth in mixed-phase clouds?
G. Sarnitsky, G. Sardina, G. Svensson, A. Pumir, F. Hoffmann, B. Mehlig

Abstract
Representing the glaciation of mixed-phase clouds in terms of the Wegener-Bergeron-Findeisen process is a challenge for many weather and climate models, which tend to overestimate this process because cloud dynamics and microphysics are not accurately represented. As turbulence is essential for the transport of water vapour from evaporating liquid droplets to ice crystals, we developed a statistical model using established closures to assess the role of small-scale turbulence. The model successfully captures results of direct numerical simulations, and we use it to assess the role of small-scale turbulence. We find that small-scale turbulence broadens the droplet-size distribution somewhat, but it does not significantly affect the glaciation time on submetre scales. However, our analysis indicates that turbulence on larger spatial scales is likely to affect ice growth. While the model must be amended to describe larger scales, the present work facilitates a path forward to understanding the role of turbulence in the Wegener-Bergeron-Findeisen process.



I
Introduction

In mixed-phase clouds, ice particles grow at the cost of evaporating water droplets via the so-called Wegener-Bergeron-Findeisen (WBF) process
[
1
]
. This occurs because the saturation vapour pressure differs for liquid water and ice. In the absence of any other processes, the WBF process turns mixed-phase clouds into ice clouds, which is commonly referred to as glaciation. However, mixed-phase clouds can be astonishingly stable (see e.g.
[
2
]
), evading a too simplistic interpretation of the WBF process. Thus, our understanding and ability to adequately represent the WBF process has important implications on the longevity and coverage of mixed-phase clouds and hence Earth’s radiation budget (e.g. Ref.
[
3
]
).

Korolev [4]
used a rising-parcel model to study growth of ice particles in mixed-phase clouds, under the assumption that ice particles, water droplets, water vapour and temperature are well mixed, showing that the cooling rates associated with a sufficiently strong updraft can prevent full glaciation. In a similar framework,
Ervens et al. [5]
highlighted the importance of the number of ice particle on the glaciation process, where smaller ice particle concentrations slow down glaciation.

A question that has received little attention in connection with the WBF process is the impact of small-scale turbulence.
Recently,
Chen et al. [6]
used direct numerical simulations (DNSs) to study the influence of turbulence on growth of ice particles in a mixed-phase cloud, determining which conditions favour ice growth in cloud-top generating cells (CTGC).
The authors found that a higher liquid-water content (LWC) and higher relative humidity (RH) favour ice growth by the WBF process.
Once the water droplets have evaporated, ice particles continue to grow consuming the remaining water vapour in the cloud.
The simulations show that small-scale turbulence has a weak effect on the change of the mean radii of water droplets and ice particles,
on the LWC and the ice-particle mass, and therefore on the glaciation time (defined as the time it takes to reach an ice-mass fraction of
0.9
).
On the other hand, the simulations show how small-scale turbulence increases the width of the particle-size distributions.

Wang et al. [7]
compiled parameter sets and specifications to compare models for mixed-phase processes in the Michigan Pi cloud chamber
[
8
]
, including small-scale turbulence. The corresponding microscopic equations for the core region of the Pi chamber are similar to those of
Chen et al. [6]
, except that a constant influx and removal of ice particles and droplets due to settling is specified.

Here, we analyse a statistical model for these processes, derived from the mapping-closure approximation
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
under the assumption that the Lagrangian supersaturation distributions (the distribution of supersaturation along droplet paths) are Gaussian, generalising statistical models for droplet-phase change in turbulence
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
to mixed-phase clouds. A strength of the model is that it is constructed from the microscopic governing equations. To assess the accuracy of the model, we compare its predictions to DNS results,
for the parameters of Ref.
[
6
]
, and for the Pi-chamber test case
[
7
]
. Another advantage of the model is that it is straightforward to disregard small-scale turbulence in the model, simply by ignoring the stochastic terms. In this way the model simplifies to a parcel model for mixed-phase clouds
[
17
,
4
,
18
]
. This allows us to study under which circumstances small-scale turbulence matters for glaciation, and when it does not.
Using the model we investigate the non-dimensional parameters of the problem, and discuss how our conclusions depend on the spatial scale of the turbulent fluctuations.

The remainder of this article is organised as follows. In Section
II
we describe the microscopic model for mixed-phase clouds,
the basis for our DNS, and those of Refs.
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
. The statistical model is introduced in Section
III
. Section
IV
summarises our results, for DNS, statistical model, and for its deterministic limit that disregards small-scale turbulence. In Section
V
we compare the results and discuss their implications for glaciation of mixed-phase clouds. We summarise our conclusions in Section
VI
. Three Appendices contain a summary of all parameters used in the calculations, and mathematical details regarding our statistical-model-analysis.

II
Microscopic model

II.1
Supersaturation over ice and water

The microscopic model of
Chen et al. [6]
describes how local temperature and the water-vapour mixing ratio are advected by the turbulent flow, and how droplets and ice particles grow and shrink in response to local fluctuations of these quantities. We start by showing how to simplify this microscopic dynamics by combining the water-vapour and temperature fields into supersaturation fields over ice and water. Thereby we extend the results of Refs.
[
19
,
14
,
20
]

that treat the case without ice. Water-vapour supersaturations
s_{\text{w}}
and
s_{\text{i}}
over liquid water and ice are defined via the partial pressure
p_{\text{v}}
of water vapour, and saturated vapour pressures
p_{\text{v},\text{w}}
and
p_{\text{v},\text{i}}
over liquid water and ice:

s_{\text{w}}=\frac{p_{\text{v}}}{p_{\text{v},\text{w}}}-1,\qquad s_{\text{i}}=\frac{p_{\text{v}}}{p_{\text{v},\text{i}}}-1.

(1)

For brevity, we refer to “liquid water” as “water” and use subscripts “
w
”, “
i
” and “
v
” respectively for liquid water, ice and vapor. Expressions for
p_{\text{w}}(T)
and
p_{\text{i}}(T)
as functions of temperature
T
are given by Eq. (
45
) in Appendix
A
. Supersaturations are often expressed in terms of the mixing ratio. The mixing ratio
q_{\text{v}}
of water vapour is defined as the ratio of the mass
m_{\text{v}}
of water vapour to the mass
m_{\text{a}}
of the dry air in a given volume,
q_{\text{v}}=m_{\text{v}}/m_{\text{a}}
; or in terms of densities
q_{\text{v}}=\rho_{\text{v}}/\rho_{\text{a}}
, where
\rho_{\text{a}}=p_{\text{a}}/({R_{\text{a}}T})
is the dry air density at partial air pressure
p_{\text{a}}
. Note that the full pressure
p
of the mixture is the sum of partial pressures of air and water vapour,
p=p_{\text{v}}+p_{\text{a}}
. But because
m_{\text{v}}\ll m_{\text{a}}
, we can take
\rho_{\text{a}}\approx\rho
(the density of the mixture) and
p_{\text{a}}\approx p
, so that
p_{\text{v}}=({R_{\text{v}}}/{R_{\text{a}}})pq_{\text{v}}
,
R_{\text{a}}
and
R_{\text{v}}
being the specific gas constants for dry air and water. Using this relation in Eqs. (
1
), one can compute the supersaturation over water and ice as

s_{\text{w}}=\frac{R_{\text{v}}}{R_{\text{a}}}\frac{p}{p_{\text{v},\text{w}}}q_{\text{v}}-1,\qquad s_{\text{i}}=\frac{R_{\text{v}}}{R_{\text{a}}}\frac{p}{p_{\text{v},\text{i}}}q_{\text{v}}-1.

(2)

In order to derive a consistent diffusion-convection-reaction equation for supersaturation, one approximates supersaturation as a linear function of
q_{\text{v}}
,
T
and
p
near their reference values
q_{\text{v},0}
,
T_{0}
and
p_{0}

[
14
]
. To this end we compute the differential of
s_{\text{w}}
from Eq. (
2
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s_{\text{w}}

\displaystyle=(1+s_{\text{w}})\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}q_{\text{v}}}{q_{\text{v}}}-\frac{L_{\text{w}}}{R_{\text{v}}T}\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{T}+\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{p}\biggr)=\frac{R_{\text{v}}}{R_{\text{a}}}\frac{p}{p_{\text{v},\text{w}}}\mathrm{d}q_{\text{v}}+(1+s_{\text{w}})\biggl(-\frac{L_{\text{w}}}{R_{\text{v}}T}\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{T}+\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{p}\biggr),

(3)

where
L_{\text{w}}
is the latent heat of water evaporation,
and
L_{\text{i}}
is the latent heat of ice sublimation.
We determine
L_{\text{w}}
and
L_{\text{i}}
in the following way to ensure consistency with the approximations of
p_{\text{v},\text{w}}
and
p_{\text{v},\text{i}}
,

L_{\text{w}}(T)=R_{\text{v}}T^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln p_{\text{v},\text{w}}}{\mathrm{d}T},\quad L_{\text{i}}(T)=R_{\text{v}}T^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln p_{\text{v},\text{i}}}{\mathrm{d}T}.

(4)

We can further simplify Eq. (
3
). Within the Oberbeck–Boussinesq approximation, the variations of
p
and
T
around
p_{0}
and
T_{0}
are small, allowing us to use constant coefficients in front of the differentials. Next, we deal with the factor
1+s_{\text{w}}
. We assume that the supersaturation variations
\Delta s_{\text{w}}
satisfy
\Delta s_{\text{w}}\ll 1+s_{\text{w},0}
, where

s_{\text{w},0}=s_{\text{w}}(q_{\text{v},0},T_{0},p_{0})=\frac{R_{\text{v}}}{R_{\text{a}}}\frac{p_{0}}{p_{\text{v},\text{w}}(T_{0})}q_{\text{v},0}-1.

(5)

We stress that this assumption is violated when the variations of
s_{\text{w}}
(equivalently of
q_{\text{v}}
) are not small, as
for example when completely dry air at
s_{\text{w}}=-1
saturates to
s_{\text{w}}=0
.

When the supersaturation fluctuations are small enough, we can integrate Eq. (
3
) using the simplifying assumptions of the previous paragraph
to obtain
s_{\text{w}}
as a linear function of
q_{\text{v}}
,
T
and
p
(and
s_{\text{i}}
is derived in a similar manner):

\displaystyle s_{\text{w}}

\displaystyle=s_{\text{w},0}+\frac{R_{\text{v}}}{R_{\text{a}}}\frac{p_{0}}{p_{\text{v},\text{w}}(T_{0})}(q_{\text{v}}-q_{\text{v},0})+(1+s_{\text{w},0})\biggl(-\frac{L_{\text{w}}(T_{0})}{R_{\text{v}}T_{0}}\frac{T-T_{0}}{T_{0}}+\frac{p-p_{0}}{p_{0}}\biggr),

(6a)

\displaystyle s_{\text{i}}

\displaystyle=s_{\text{i},0}+\frac{R_{\text{v}}}{R_{\text{a}}}\frac{p_{0}}{p_{\text{v},\text{i}}(T_{0})}(q_{\text{v}}-q_{\text{v},0})+(1+s_{\text{i},0})\biggl(-\frac{L_{\text{i}}(T_{0})}{R_{\text{v}}T_{0}}\frac{T-T_{0}}{T_{0}}+\frac{p-p_{0}}{p_{0}}\biggr).

(6b)

In order to derive the diffusion-convection-reaction equations for the fields
s_{\text{w}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
and
s_{\text{i}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
from Eqs. (
6
), we follow
[
19
]
and start from the corresponding equations for the fields
T(\boldsymbol{x},t)
and
q_{\text{v}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\varkappa_{T}\frac{\partial^{2}T}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{j}}+\frac{L_{\text{w}}(T_{0})}{c_{p}}C_{\text{w}}+\frac{L_{\text{i}}(T_{0})}{c_{p}}C_{\text{i}},

(7a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}q_{\text{v}}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\varkappa_{q_{\text{v}}}\frac{\partial^{2}q_{\text{v}}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{j}}-C_{\text{w}}-C_{\text{i}}.

(7b)

Here
C_{\text{w}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
and
C_{\text{i}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
are water and ice condensation and deposition rates, which are discussed in more detail in Section
II.3
,
c_{p}=\frac{7}{2}R_{\text{a}}
is the specific heat of air at constant pressure,
\varkappa_{T}
and
\varkappa_{q_{\text{v}}}
are the molecular diffusivities of
T
and
q_{\text{v}}
. Math-style Latin indices (as in
x_{j}
) denote vector/tensor components in Cartesian coordinates, and we use the Einstein summation convention for repeated indices. Next,
\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t=\partial/\partial t+u_{j}\partial/\partial x_{j}
are the components of the convective derivative, where
u_{j}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
is the turbulent velocity of air determined by the Navier–Stokes equations

\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{j}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_{j}}+\nu\frac{\partial^{2}u_{j}}{\partial x_{k}\partial x_{k}}+f_{j}^{(u)},\qquad\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{j}}=0,

(8)

Here
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity of air. The forcing
f_{j}^{(u)}
is required to maintain stationary turbulence, as in Refs.
[
6
,
7
]
.

Now we combine Eqs. (
6
) and (
7
). For this, we first calculate the Laplacian of
s_{\text{w}}
from Eq. (
6a
). Within the Oberbeck–Boussinesq approximation only the hydrostatic pressure affects the thermodynamic variables like
s_{\text{w}}
, and the Laplacian of hydrostatic pressure is negligible compared to the effects of Laplacians of
T
and
q_{\text{v}}
, which are dominated by small-scale turbulence. Thus, we can neglect the Laplacian
\partial^{2}p/{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{j}}
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}s_{\text{w}}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{R_{\text{v}}}{R_{\text{a}}}\frac{p_{0}}{p_{\text{v},\text{w}}(T_{0})}\frac{\partial^{2}q_{\text{v}}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{j}}-\frac{(1{+}s_{\text{w},0})L_{\text{w}}(T_{0})}{R_{\text{v}}T_{0}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}T}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{j}}.

(9)

The second step is to assume
\varkappa_{q_{\text{v}}}\approx\varkappa_{T}
, which is justified since
\varkappa_{q_{\text{v}}}=1.17\varkappa_{T}
(Eq. (
55
)). Defining
\varkappa=\sqrt{\varkappa_{q_{\text{v}}}\varkappa_{T}}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}s_{\text{w}}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\varkappa\frac{\partial^{2}s_{\text{w}}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{j}}-A_{2,\text{w},\text{w}}\,C_{\text{w}}-A_{2,\text{w},\text{i}}\,C_{\text{i}},

(10a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}s_{\text{i}}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\varkappa\frac{\partial^{2}s_{\text{i}}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{j}}-A_{2,\text{i},\text{w}}\,C_{\text{w}}-A_{2,\text{i},\text{i}}\,C_{\text{i}},

(10b)

where the equation for

\displaystyle A_{2,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{R_{\text{v}}}{R_{\text{a}}}\frac{p_{0}}{p_{\text{v},\phi_{1}}(T_{0})}+\frac{(1+s_{\phi_{1},0})L_{\phi_{1}}(T_{0})L_{\phi_{2}}(T_{0})}{c_{p}R_{\text{v}}T_{0}^{2}}.

(11)

In this expression, the variable
\phi
stands for a particular condensed phase, either
\phi=\text{w}
or
\phi=\text{i}
. If we disregard the ice phase, we obtain the supersaturation dynamics used in the statistical models for evaporation of water droplets at the cloud edge
[
14
,
11
]
. There are minor differences in the expressions of the
A
-parameters in those references, reflecting slightly different assumptions.

The model (
10
) can be further simplified if we express

as a
function of
s_{\text{w}}
, so that it is sufficient to solve a single partial differential equation for

and

used in our DNS and in the statistical model is

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}s_{\text{w}}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\varkappa\frac{\partial^{2}s_{\text{w}}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{j}}-A_{2,\text{w}}C_{\text{w}}-A_{2,\text{i}}C_{\text{i}}+f^{({s_{\text{w}}})},

(12a)

\displaystyle s_{\text{i}}

\displaystyle=A_{4}(s_{\text{w}}+1)-1.

(12b)

Here we introduced a forcing term

representing the forcing of small-scale fluctuations due to large spatial scales that are not resolved by the DNS
[
6
,
7
]
. The new
A_{2}
-parameters are given by
A_{2,\text{w}}=A_{2,\text{w},\text{w}}
and
A_{2,\text{i}}=A_{2,\text{w},\text{i}}
, in simplified notation. The parameter

is defined as:

A_{4}=\frac{p_{\text{v},\text{w}}(T_{0})}{p_{\text{v},\text{i}}(T_{0})}

(13)

Since
p_{\text{v},\text{i}}>p_{\text{v},\text{w}}
for

, we have

and

in the mixed-phase cloud. The WBF process corresponds to
s_{\text{i}}>0>s_{\text{w}}
, under this condition water droplets evaporate and ice particles grow. To assess how the single-supersaturation approximation works, we estimate the error
\delta s_{\text{i}}
between

calculated from (

) and (
12b
). For

between

231.15\text{\,}\mathrm{K}

\delta s_{\text{i}}/s_{\text{i}}\approx-$0.011\text{\,}\mathrm{K}^{-1}$\,(T-T_{0})/s_{\text{i}}.

We conclude that the single-supersaturation

The latter condition is satisfied at the initial and most interesting stage of glaciation, when air is saturated with respect to water,

II.2
Particle dynamics

Water droplets and ice particles are assumed to be so small that they follow the flow, their positions
\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{w}}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{i}}(t)
obey

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{\text{w},j}}{\mathrm{d}t}=u_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{w}},t),\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{\text{i},j}}{\mathrm{d}t}=u_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{i}},t).

(15)

In other words, effects of particle inertia
[
21
]
are neglected.
The particle radii
r_{\text{w}}(t)
and
r_{\text{i}}(t)
change according to:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}r_{\text{w}}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=2A_{3,\text{w}}\,a_{3}(r_{\text{w}}/r_{A_{3,\text{w}}})\,\bigl[s_{\text{w}}-s_{\text{w},\text{K}}(r_{\text{w}})\bigr],

(16a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}r_{\text{i}}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\biggl\{\begin{aligned} &2A_{3,\text{i}}\,a_{3}(r_{\text{i}}/r_{A_{3,\text{i}}})\,s_{\text{i}}&\text{if }r_{\text{i}}>0,\\
&0&\text{if }r_{\text{i}}=0,\end{aligned}

(16b)

with supersaturation taken at the particle position, e.g.
s_{\text{w}}(t)=s_{\text{w}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{w}},t)
. For ice particles,

{\rm d}r_{\text{i}}/{\rm d}t
is constrained to vanish at
r_{\text{i}}=0
to ensure that
r_{\text{i}}^{2}
remains non-negative, as it must.

Water droplets are not allowed to completely evaporate, due to
the Köhler correction term in Eq. (
16
), involving the radius-dependent function
s_{\text{w},\text{K}}
. This function is parameterized by the dry aerosol radius
r_{\text{dry}}
and the hygroscopicity coefficient
\kappa
:

\displaystyle s_{\text{w},\text{K}}(r_{\text{w}})

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{\text{w}}^{3}-r_{\text{dry}}^{3}}{r_{\text{w}}^{3}-r_{\text{dry}}^{3}(1-\kappa)}-1.

(17)

A more general expression for Köhler corrections contains an exponential term for
Kelvin curvature effects
[
22
]
. In Eq. (
17
) we approximated the exponential by unity.
This is a good approximation for our values of
r_{\text{dry}}
.
Unlike water droplets, ice particles are not allowed to reactivate: once an ice particle evaporates and
r_{\text{i}}
reaches zero, it stays evaporated with
r_{\text{i}}=0
(the ice is pure). This is in agreement with the specifications of both the CTGC and the Pi Chamber cases. Equations (
16
) contain corrections for the efficiency of accommodation of water vapour on the particle surface, introducing the particle-size dependent function
a_{3}(x)=x/(x+1)
with accommodation length
r_{A_{3,\phi}}

[
23
,
24
]
:

r_{A_{3,\phi}}=A_{3,\phi}\frac{\rho_{\phi}\sqrt{2\pi R_{\text{v}}T_{0}}}{\alpha_{q_{\text{v}},\phi}\,p_{\text{v},\phi}(T_{0})},

(18)

where
\phi
refers to the phase of condensed water, either
\phi=\text{w}
or
\phi=\text{i}
. Particles with radii
r_{\phi}\gg r_{A_{3,\phi}}
are not affected by these corrections. Here
\alpha_{q_{\text{v}},\text{w}}
and
\alpha_{q_{\text{v}},\text{i}}
are water-vapour accommodation coefficients over water and ice. The use of the particular forms of
A_{3,\phi}
,
a_{3}
and

for our cases is justified in Appendix
A
. Neglecting all radius-dependent corrections corresponds to

and
s_{\text{w},K}=0
. The
A_{3}
-parameters in Eqs. (
16
) and (
18
) are given by
[
25
]

A_{3,\phi}=\biggl[\frac{R_{\text{a}}}{R_{\text{v}}}\frac{\rho_{\phi}L_{\phi}^{2}(T_{0})}{\varkappa_{T}c_{p}T_{0}p_{0}}+\frac{\rho_{\phi}R_{\text{v}}T_{0}}{\varkappa_{q_{\text{v}}}p_{\text{v},\phi}(T_{0})}\biggr]^{-1}.

(19)

Finally, the specifications for the cloud-chamber test case allow for injection and removal of water droplets and ice particles
[
7
]
. Water droplets are injected in the form of dry aerosol at a constant rate
I_{\text{w}}\,[(\text{s}\,\text{m}^{3})^{-1}]
and removed at a rate defined by the settling velocity

. Ice particles are treated the same way, with

and

. Namely, each particle is removed with a probability

(droplet) or

(ice particle):

(20)

where

is is the total height of the Pi chamber. The settling velocities depend on the particle radii

and
r_{\text{i}}
:

\displaystyle u_{\infty,\text{w}}

\displaystyle=k_{\infty,\text{w}}r_{\text{w}}^{2},\qquad u_{\infty,\text{i}}=k_{\infty,\text{i}}r_{\text{i}}^{2}.

(21)

The values of the parameters

and

are specified by
[
7
]
. We note that particle shape impacts the sedimentation velocity. Here we assume spherical particles, but larger ice crystals (with radii > 30

tend to be non-spherical. This is not accounted for in the model (for the data discussed below, Figures
1
and

The overall number of particles changes as

\frac{\mathrm{d}N_{\text{w}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=VI_{\text{w}}+\frac{N_{\text{w}}}{H}\langle u_{\text{w},\infty}\rangle,\frac{\mathrm{d}N_{\text{i}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=VI_{\text{i}}+\frac{N_{\text{i}}}{H}\langle u_{\text{i},\infty}\rangle,

(22)

where

is the volume of the simulation domain (corresponding to the core of the Pi chamber).

II.3
Condensation and deposition rates

The condensation and deposition rates
C_{\text{w}}
,
C_{\text{i}}
for water and ice are defined through the rate of change of condensed water content:

\displaystyle C_{\text{w}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3}\pi\frac{\rho_{\text{w}}}{\rho_{0}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\text{w}}}G(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{w},\alpha})\frac{\mathrm{d}r_{\text{w},\alpha}^{3}}{\mathrm{d}t},

(23a)

\displaystyle C_{\text{i}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3}\pi\frac{\rho_{\text{i}}}{\rho_{0}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\text{i}}}G(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{i},\alpha})\frac{\mathrm{d}r_{\text{i},\alpha}^{3}}{\mathrm{d}t},

(23b)

where
N_{\text{w}}
is the number of water droplets,
N_{\text{i}}
is the number of ice particles, and

is the standard spatial kernel, normalized to unity
[
26
,
6
]
. The spatial range of

is the linear size of a DNS-grid cell. Using Eqs. (
16

and
C_{\text{i}}
as

\displaystyle C_{\text{w}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)

\displaystyle=4\pi\frac{\rho_{\text{w}}}{\rho_{0}}A_{3,\text{w}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\text{w}}}G(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{w},\alpha})r_{\text{w},\alpha}a_{3}(\tfrac{r_{\text{w},\alpha}}{r_{A_{3,\text{w}}}})\bigl[s_{\text{w},\alpha}-s_{\text{w},\text{K}}(r_{\text{w},\alpha})\bigr],

(24a)

\displaystyle C_{\text{i}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)

\displaystyle=4\pi\frac{\rho_{\text{i}}}{\rho_{0}}A_{3,\text{i}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\text{i}}}\!G(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{i},\alpha})r_{\text{i},\alpha}a_{3}(\tfrac{r_{\text{i},\alpha}}{r_{A_{3,\text{i}}}})s_{\text{i},\alpha}.

(24b)

Here
s_{\text{w},\alpha}
is the supersaturation field at the position of particle
\alpha
,
s_{\text{w},\alpha}(t)=s_{\text{w}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha},t)
. Note that the multiplication by
r_{\text{i},\alpha}
in Eq. (
24b
) correctly accounts for the
r_{\text{i}}=0
condition in Eq. (
16b
).

Without injection and sedimentation (
N_{\text{w}}
and
N_{\text{i}}
are constant) and for vanishing mean forcing
\langle f^{(s_{\text{w}})}\rangle
, Eqs. (
12a
) and (
23
) imply
that the quantities

\displaystyle s_{\text{w},\text{inv}}

\displaystyle=\langle s_{\text{w}}\rangle_{V}+\frac{4}{3}\pi\frac{\rho_{\text{w}}}{\rho_{0}}A_{2,\text{w}}n_{\text{w}}\langle r_{\text{w}}^{3}\rangle_{V}+\frac{4}{3}\pi\frac{\rho_{\text{i}}}{\rho_{0}}A_{2,\text{i}}n_{\text{i}}\langle r_{\text{i}}^{3}\rangle_{V},

(25a)

\displaystyle s_{\text{i},\text{inv}}

\displaystyle=A_{4}(s_{\text{w},\text{inv}}+1)-1.

(25b)

are invariant.
Here
n_{\text{w}}=N_{\text{w}}/V
and
n_{\text{i}}=N_{\text{i}}/V
are droplet and ice number densities, and
\langle s_{\text{w}}\rangle_{V}=\frac{1}{V}\int s_{\text{w}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}
is the spatially averaged supersaturation. Since Eq. (
12a
) for
s_{\text{w}}
is derived from the two Eqs. (
7
) for
q_{\text{v}}
and for
T
, the conservation law (
25
) combines both water and thermal energy conservation during condensation and deposition of water vapor
[
13
,
14
]
.
Physically,
s_{\text{w},\text{inv}}
and
s_{\text{i},\text{inv}}
correspond to supersaturation over water and ice if all the particles evaporate, so that all the water is contained in the form of water vapor. In case we ignore the Köhler corrections,
s_{\text{i},\text{inv}}
provides us with an insight into the final state of the cloud. Since water evaporates (
r_{\text{w}}=0
),
s_{\text{i},\text{inv}}>0
means that ice remains, while
s_{\text{i},\text{inv}}\leqslant 0
implies that ice evaporates too.

(CTGC) and

(Pi chamber) in Appendix

.

II.4
Direct numerical simulations

We performed DNSs using Eqs. (
8
) to (
22
) for mixed-phase processes in the core of the Pi chamber, for the parameters specified by
Wang et al. [7]
. The turbulent dissipation
rate per unit mass was
\varepsilon=66

{\rm cm}^{2}{\rm s}^{-3}
in a cubic domain
with side length
L=20
cm. With
\nu=1.278\times 10^{-5}
m
2
s
-1
, the Kolmogorov length
\eta=(\nu^{3}/\varepsilon)^{1/4}
is around
\eta=0.75
mm. To properly resolve the turbulent flow we used a numerical resolution with
256^{3}
collocation points to solve the Eulerian equations.

As specified by
Wang et al. [7]
, our DNSs used the forcing term in the Navier–Stokes equations (
8
) to maintain a statistically steady turbulent state with constant dissipation rate
\varepsilon
. In Fourier space, the forcing reads

\displaystyle\hat{\mbox{}\!\boldsymbol{f}}^{(u)}(\boldsymbol{k})=\varepsilon\mathcal{N}(t)\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{k},t)\quad\mbox{for $|\boldsymbol{k}|<3\pi/L$}.

(26)

Here
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{k}},t)
is the Fourier transform of the turbulent velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
,
\boldsymbol{k}
is the wave vector, and
{\mathcal{N}}(t)=\big(\sum_{|\boldsymbol{k}|<2\pi/L}{\boldsymbol{|\hat{u}}}({\boldsymbol{k}},t)|^{2}\big)^{-1}
is a normalisation factor.
The supersaturation equation (
12a
) is also forced. For the CTGC we use a Gaussian random forcing
[
27
]
,

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{(s_{\text{w}})}(\boldsymbol{k})=\beta{\rm d}W(\boldsymbol{k},t)\quad\mbox{for $|\boldsymbol{k}|<3\pi/L$},

(27)

where
{\rm d}W(t)
is white noise with unit variance independently chosen for different
\boldsymbol{k}
.
The numerical factor
\beta
is used to maintain the prescribed steady water supersaturation root mean square
\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}
before the aerosol injection. The forcing (
27
) differs from the one suggested by
Wang et al. [7]
where there is no explicit forcing term, instead the supersaturation Fourier coefficients of the forced wavenumbers are rescaled at each time step to mantain the prescribed
\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}
. We tested that the two forcing schemes yield the same condensation/evaporation statistics, provided that they achieve the same statistically steady-state value of
\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}
. For the Pi chamber,
Wang et al. [7]
specify an additional average forcing in Eq. (
12a
) that nudges the average supersaturation,

\langle f^{({s_{\text{w}}})}\rangle=-\bigl(\langle s_{\text{w}}\rangle-s_{\text{w},\text{force}}\bigr)/{\tau_{s_{\text{w},\text{force}}}},

(28)

where
s_{\text{w},{\rm force}}
is the mean supersaturation before aerosol injection, and
\tau_{s_{\text{w},\text{force}}}
is a forcing timescale. This forcing mimics the property of the Rayleigh–Benard convection inside the cloud chamber to achieve a statistically steady thermodynamic state
[
28
]
.

For the Pi chamber, we need to add and remove the particles during each time step
\Delta t
as specified by
Wang et al. [7]
, see Eqs. (
21
) and (
22
) in Section
II.2
. To this end, the numbers of added particles with radii
r_{\text{w},\text{initial}}
and
r_{\text{i},\text{initial}}
are

\displaystyle\Delta N_{\text{w}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{floor}(I_{\text{w}}V\Delta t)+\biggl\{\begin{aligned} &1,&&\mathrm{frac}(I_{\text{w}}V\Delta t)\geqslant\xi_{\text{w}},\\
&0,&&\text{else},\\
\end{aligned}

(29)

\displaystyle\Delta N_{\text{i}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{floor}(I_{\text{i}}V\Delta t)+\biggl\{\begin{aligned} &1,&&\mathrm{frac}(I_{\text{i}}V\Delta t)\geqslant\xi_{\text{i}},\\
&0,&&\text{else},\end{aligned}

(30)

where
I_{\text{w}}
and
I_{\text{i}}
are water and ice injection rates, and
\xi_{\text{w}}
and
\xi_{\text{i}}
are independent random variables uniformly distributed in [0,1]. The initial ice particle radius is
2\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{m}
, while the initial droplet size correspond to a dry aerosol particle with a diameter of
0.125\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{m}
.

For our DNSs, we used the same numerical solver as in Refs.
[
19
,
14
]
. The Navier-Stokes equations (
8
) were solved in Fourier space using fast Fourier transform. The nonlinear terms were calculated in configuration space using the de-aliasing 2/3 rule
[
19
]
. Time integration used a low-storage third-order Runge-Kutta method, where the terms are treated exactly by using integration factors,
while the non-linear terms followed an Adam-Bashforth scheme. The same Runge-Kutta scheme was used to integrate the equations of motion (
15
) for water droplets and ice particles, and their growth equations (
16
). A linear interpolation scheme was used to evaluate the air velocity and supersaturation at the particle positions, while linear extrapolation was employed to calculate the condensation rates
C_{\text{w}}
and
C_{\text{i}}
in Eqs. (
24
). The parameter values for the DNSs are summarised in Table

.

III
Statistical model

To understand glaciation dynamics and how it is affected by small-scale turbulence one could simulate the microscopic model described in Section
II
for a wide range of parameters. Here we take an alternative approach: we derive a statistical model that allows us to systematically study the parameter dependencies of the glaciation process, and provides immediate insight into possible effects of small-scale turbulence. We validate the model by showing that it yields quantitative agreement with
the DNS results of
[
6
]
. In essence, the model is a statistical model for the supersaturation, approximating Eqs. (
8
,
12
,
15
), while the Eqs. (
16
) for particle radii remain the same. The derivation of the model rests on two assumptions:

A1.

The supersaturation statistics along water-droplet, ice-particle, and Lagrangian fluid paths are the same.

A2.

The supersaturation statistics are Gaussian.

Our DNSs for the CTGC and for the core of the Pi chamber show that these assumptions hold. In Section
V
we discuss their range of validity.
To derive the model under the above assumptions, we start by decomposing the supersaturation along a particle trajectory into its mean and fluctuating parts

s_{\text{w}}=\langle s_{\text{w}}\rangle+s^{\prime}_{\text{w}}.

(31)

We use the usual notation
\langle\cdot\rangle
for ensemble averages of physical quantities and
\cdot^{\prime}
for their fluctuating parts.
The system is statistically homogeneous: the mean values may depend on
t
, but not on
\boldsymbol{x}
. Since water droplets and ice particles are Lagrangian tracers, and since the flow is incompressible, single-point Eulerian and Lagrangian statistics are the same. Therefore we can take the ensemble average of Eq. (
12a
) to obtain the evolution equation for the mean supersaturation
\langle s_{\text{w}}\rangle
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle s_{\text{w}}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}=-A_{2,\text{w}}\langle C_{\text{w}}\rangle-A_{2,\text{i}}\langle C_{\text{i}}\rangle+\langle f^{({s_{\text{w}}})}\rangle.

(32)

To close Eq. (
32
), we need expressions for the mean condensation rates

\langle C_{\text{w}}\rangle
and
\langle C_{\text{i}}\rangle
, which we derive in Appendix
B
:

\langle C_{\text{w}}\rangle=\frac{4}{3}\pi\frac{\rho_{\text{w}}}{\rho_{0}}n_{\text{w}}\biggl<\frac{\mathrm{d}r_{\text{w}}^{3}}{\mathrm{d}t}\biggr>,\qquad\langle C_{\text{i}}\rangle=\frac{4}{3}\pi\frac{\rho_{\text{i}}}{\rho_{0}}n_{\text{i}}\biggl<\frac{\mathrm{d}r_{\text{i}}^{3}}{\mathrm{d}t}\biggr>.

(33)

Using Eqs. (
16
), these expressions evaluate to

\displaystyle\langle C_{\text{w}}\rangle

\displaystyle=4\pi\frac{\rho_{\text{w}}}{\rho_{0}}A_{3,\text{w}}n_{\text{w}}\bigl<a_{3}\bigl(\tfrac{r_{\text{w}}}{r_{A_{3},\text{w}}}\bigr)r_{\text{w}}\bigl[(s_{\text{w}}-s_{\text{w},\text{K}}(r_{\text{w}})\bigr]\bigr>,

(34a)

\displaystyle\langle C_{\text{i}}\rangle

\displaystyle=4\pi\frac{\rho_{\text{i}}}{\rho_{0}}A_{3,\text{i}}n_{\text{i}}\bigl<a_{3}\bigl(\tfrac{r_{\text{i}}}{r_{A_{3},\text{i}}}\bigr)r_{\text{i}}s_{\text{i}}\bigr>.

(34b)

Note that averages involving particle radii also include averaging over particles, e.g.
\langle r_{\text{w}}s_{\text{w}}\rangle=\frac{1}{N_{\text{w}}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\text{w}}}\langle r_{\text{w},\alpha}s_{\text{w},\alpha}\rangle
. However for simplicity we do not introduce a special notation for such averages, except for Appendix
B
where we use
\langle{\cdot}\rangle_{\text{w}}
or
\langle{\cdot}\rangle_{\text{i}}
. For the Pi chamber, the average forcing
\langle f^{({s_{\text{w}}})}\rangle
in (
32
) is given by Eq. (
28
). For the CTGC
[
6
]
,
the average vanishes.

With a model for the mean supersaturation in place, we now introduce a model for its fluctuating part.
Fries et al. [11]
used the mapping closure of
Pope [9]
and
Chen et al. [10]
to accurately describe non-Gaussian dynamics of
s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}
during the evaporation of water droplets at the cloud edge. We start from the same model here. Since in our case the statistics of
s_{\text{w}}
is Gaussian, the mapping closure becomes a linear theory and reduces to an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process for
s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}

\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}_{\text{w}}=-\frac{1}{\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{({L})}}s^{\prime}_{\text{w}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}^{2}}{\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{({L})}}}\,\mathrm{d}W(t).

(35)

Here
\mathrm{d}W(t)
are white-noise increments, while the supersaturation variance
\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}^{2}=\langle s_{\text{w}}^{\prime 2}\rangle
and the correlation time
\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{({L})}
are the two parameters of the model. The model (
35
) is also known as the Langevin mixing model
[
9
, Eq. 5.52]
. Related models have been used to describe the effect of supersaturation fluctuations on the growth of water droplets in turbulent clouds
[
12
,
29
,
13
,
15
,
16
]
. Some of them contain additional condensation terms in the equation for
s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}
. To understand why such terms do not matter in our case, consider a more general statistical model for the supersaturation fluctuations

\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}_{\text{w}}=-A_{2,\text{w}}\langle C_{\text{w}}^{\prime}\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime},t\rangle-A_{2,\text{i}}\langle C_{\text{i}}^{\prime}\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime},t\rangle-\frac{s^{\prime}_{\text{w}}}{\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{({L})}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{D^{(2)}}\,\mathrm{d}W(t).

(36)

Here
\langle C^{\prime}_{\phi}\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime},t\rangle=\langle C_{\phi}\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime},t\rangle-\langle C_{\phi}\rangle
, where
\langle C_{\phi}\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}},t\rangle
are conditional condensation rates, and
D^{(2)}
is chosen such to conserve
\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}
[Eq. (
71
)].
Appendix
B
outlines how to derive Eq. (
36
)) using the method of
Sarnitsky and Heinz [30]
.

However, the fluctuating condensation-rate contributions are negligible if the timescale of turbulent mixing
\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{(L)}
is much smaller than the timescales
\tau_{s_{\text{w}},\text{w}}
and
\tau_{s_{\text{w}},\text{i}}
of supersaturation evolution due to the phase change of water droplets and ice particles,

\tau_{s_{\text{w}},\text{w}}=\frac{\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}^{2}}{A_{2,\text{w}}|\langle C_{\text{w}}^{\prime}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}\rangle|},\qquad\tau_{s_{\text{w}},\text{i}}=\frac{\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}^{2}}{A_{2,\text{i}}|\langle C_{\text{i}}^{\prime}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}\rangle|},

(37)

where
|{\cdot}|
denotes the absolute value. Their fractions with
\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{(L)}
define the supersaturation Damköhler numbers

\text{Da}_{s_{\text{w}},\text{w}}=\frac{\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{(L)}}{\tau_{s_{\text{w}},\text{w}}},\qquad\text{Da}_{s_{\text{w}},\text{w}}=\frac{\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{(L)}}{\tau_{s_{\text{w}},\text{w}}}.

(38)

We conclude: for small Damköhler numbers,
\text{Da}_{s_{\text{w}},\text{w}}\ll 1
and
\text{Da}_{s_{\text{w}},\text{i}}\ll 1
, one can use the model (
35
) instead of (
36
). Our model calculations confirm that the supersaturation Damköhler numbers are smaller than unity for the cases studied here.

To close the model (
35
), we need to provide the supersaturation variance
\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}^{2}
and the correlation time
\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{(L)}
. For the Pi chamber, the variance is given by
Wang et al. [7]
. For the CTGC,
Chen et al. [6]
specify the variances of temperature and the water-vapour mixing ratio, which allows us to compute
\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}^{2}
. The Lagrangian correlation time
\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{({L})}
is defined as

\displaystyle\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{({L})}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}^{-2}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}t\,\langle s^{\prime}(t)s^{\prime}(0)\rangle},

(39)

where
\langle s^{\prime}(t)s^{\prime}(0)\rangle
is the Lagrangian autocovariance, i.e. it is taken along Lagrangian trajectories. The numerical values of
\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{(L)}
and
\tau_{L}
, are given in Appendix
A
. We note that for the cases studied here, phase change does not affect
\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{({L})}
since the condensation terms are negligible in the dynamics of
s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}
for small supersaturation Damköhler numbers. Disregarding phase change,
\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{(L)}
is commonly related to the large eddy turbulent timescale
\tau_{L}=k/\varepsilon
:

\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{({L})}=\frac{C_{0,s_{\text{w}}}}{C_{s_{\text{w}}}}\tau_{L},

(40)

The Lagrangian Obukhov–Corrsin constant
C_{0,s_{\text{w}}}
comes from the Kolmogorov hypothesis extended to passive scalars and connects the correlation timescale
\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{(L)}
to the dissipation timescale
\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}^{2}/\varepsilon_{s_{\text{w}}}
,
\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{(L)}=C_{0,s_{\text{w}}}\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}^{2}/\varepsilon_{s_{\text{w}}}
, where
\varepsilon_{s_{\text{w}}}=2\varkappa\bigl<\frac{\partial s_{\text{w}}}{\partial x_{j}}\,\frac{\partial s_{\text{w}}}{\partial x_{j}}\bigr>
is the supersaturation dissipation rate. For the CTGC and Pi chamber, the values of
C_{0,s_{\text{w}}}
are
1.2
and
1.1
, inferred from the DNS described in Section
II.4
.
The quantity
C_{s_{\text{w}}}
is the so called mechanical-to-scalar timescale ratio, formally defined as
C_{s_{\text{w}}}=k\varepsilon_{s_{\text{w}}}/(\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}^{2}\varepsilon)
. The quantity
C_{s_{\text{w}}}
can be considered approximately constant only in specific types of flows, like the forced isotropic turbulence we deal with here
[
31
]
. Its numerical values calculated from the DNS for the CTGC and Pi Chamber cases are
1.8
and
2.2
respectivly. We stress again this discussion is valid only for
\text{Da}_{s_{\text{w}}}\ll 1
and
\text{Da}_{s_{\text{i}}}\ll 1
. In case of non-negligable supersaturation Damköhler numbers,
Fries et al. [11]
found that both
C_{0,s_{\text{w}}}
and
C_{s_{\text{w}}}
(denoted there as
2/C
and
2\phi_{*}
) cannot be considered constant.

To numerically integrate the statistical model, we use the Euler–Maruyama scheme with a time step of
0.05\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
for the CTGC, and
0.02\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
for the Pi chamber. To ensure that the numerical integration conserves the invariants (
25
), condensation rates are computed directly from Eqs. (
33
) and not Eqs. (
34
). For the CTGC, we use
N_{\text{w}}=N_{\text{i}}=10^{7}
to suppress the statistical noise for cases 1 and 2. The choice of
N_{\text{w}}
and
N_{\text{i}}
here has no other consequences, since the model depends only on
n_{\text{w}}
and
n_{\text{i}}
in Eq. (
33
), which are fixed in each run. For the Pi chamber, the number of particles is determined by the particle injection and removal process, which is implemented as described in Section
II.4
.

Below, we refer to the deterministic limit of the statistical model, or deterministic model. It is obtained by taking the limit
\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}\to 0
in the statistical model, which amounts to removing the white-noise term in Eq. (
35
) and setting
s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}=0
.

V
Discussion

Figures
1
and
2
show that the statistical-model predictions
agree very well with the DNS results. We now explain why this is the case here, and under which circumstances the model may fail. To this end we study the limits of validity of the statistical model, which we derived from the assumptions A1 and A2 listed in Section
III
.

The first assumption, A1, is that the supersaturation statistics along water-droplet, ice-particle, and Lagrangian fluid paths are the same. We expect this to hold when the Damköhler numbers
\text{Da}_{s_{\text{w}},\text{w}}
and
\text{Da}_{s_{\text{w}},\text{i}}
[Eq. (
38
)] are small, and when water droplets and ice particles are initially well-mixed. Under these conditions, turbulence transports water vapour to and from particles faster than phase change occurs. Hence the particles have no time to form a supersaturation field in their vicinity, which might differ from particle-free regions of the flow; all the particles experience the same supersaturation statistics.

The second assumption, A2, is that the supersaturation statistics are Gaussian. In a homogeneous system (no scalar gradients
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
), the steady-state distribution of a passive scalar in isotropic homogeneous turbulence is Gaussian
[
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
. Non-Gaussian tails that may be present in transient mixing
[
41
]
disappear as the steady state is approached.

In summary, the statistical model from Section
III
can be justified when
the Damköhler numbers
\text{Da}_{s_{\text{w}},\text{w}}
and
\text{Da}_{s_{\text{w}},\text{i}}
are small. The cases summarised in Section
IV
all have Damköhler numbers smaller than unity, explaining why the statistical model works so well. On the other hand, it is worth noting that the interaction between turbulence and phase change can be more intricate and harder to describe at larger Damköhler numbers. This is in line with the findings of
Fries et al. [11]
, and requires more refined mapping-closure approximations
[
10
,
9
]
.

Next, we quantify the role of turbulence on the dynamics of mean supersaturation and upon the mean particle radii. In the framework of the statistical model, the question is
how
\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}
– the measure of turbulence intensity – enters the dynamical equations for
\langle s_{\text{w}}\rangle
and
\langle r_{\text{w}}\rangle
. To this end we need to introduce two
more Damköhler numbers [besides the supersaturation Damköhler numbers (
38
)]:

\text{Da}_{r_{\text{w}}}=\frac{\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{(L)}}{\tau_{r_{\text{w}}}},\qquad\text{Da}_{r_{\text{i}}}=\frac{\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{(L)}}{\tau_{r_{\text{i}}}}.

(41)

They are associated
with the timescales
\tau_{r_{\text{w}}}
and
\tau_{r_{\text{i}}}
of the particle-size evolution:

\tau_{r_{\text{w}}}=\frac{\langle r_{\text{w}}\rangle^{2}}{\bigl|\tfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\langle r_{\text{w}}^{2}\rangle\bigr|},\qquad\tau_{r_{\text{w}}}=\frac{\langle r_{\text{i}}\rangle^{2}}{\bigl|\tfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\langle r_{\text{i}}^{2}\rangle\bigr|}

(42)

We note that these Damköhler numbers scale as
\sim 2A_{3}s_{\phi}\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{(L)}/\langle r_{\phi}\rangle^{2}
(
\phi=\text{w}
or
\phi=\text{i}
). Therefore they are larger
for smaller particles.

Figures
1
and
2
imply that small-scale turbulence has only a weak
effect on the evolution of the average supersaturation. To explain this, we look at the evolution equation (
32
) for
\langle s_{\text{w}}\rangle
. It is written in terms of the mean condensation rates
\langle C_{\text{w}}\rangle
and
\langle C_{\text{i}}\rangle
. For the CTGC (no particle injection or removal), we can estimate
\langle C_{\text{w}}\rangle
and
\langle C_{\text{i}}\rangle
as

\displaystyle\langle C_{\phi}\rangle

\displaystyle=4\pi\frac{\rho_{\phi}}{\rho_{0}}A_{3,\phi}n_{\phi}\bigl(\langle r_{\phi}\rangle\langle s_{\phi}\rangle+\langle r^{\prime}_{\phi}s^{\prime}_{\phi}\rangle\bigr)\approx 4\pi\frac{\rho_{\phi}}{\rho_{0}}A_{3,\phi}n_{\phi}\langle r_{\phi}\rangle\langle s_{\phi}\rangle\biggl(1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sigma_{s_{\phi}}^{2}}{\langle s_{\phi}\rangle|\langle s_{\phi}\rangle|}\text{Da}_{r_{\phi}}\biggr),

(43)

where
\phi=\text{w}
or
\phi=\text{i}
, as before.
These approximations are derived in Appendix
C
for a simplified particle-growth model, and assuming small particle Damköhler numbers, small supersaturation Damköhler numbers, and narrow particle-size distributions.
Equation (
43
) shows that the turbulence term
\langle r_{\phi}^{\prime}s_{\phi}^{\prime}\rangle
is proportional to the particle Damköhler numbers. It can therefore be neglected when
\text{Da}_{r_{\text{w}}}
and
\text{Da}_{r_{\text{i}}}
are small, when the particles are large enough. Neglecting
\langle r_{\phi}^{\prime}s_{\phi}^{\prime}\rangle
corresponds to the decoupling between the fluctuations of radii and supersaturation when
s_{\phi}^{\prime}
evolves much faster than
r_{\phi}^{\prime}
for large particles, so that
r^{\prime}_{\phi}
can respond only to the slow evolving
\langle s_{\phi}\rangle
, but not
s_{\phi}^{\prime}
. We note that Eq. (
43
) fails near
\langle s_{\phi}\rangle\approx 0
, when the turbulence term
\langle r_{\phi}^{\prime}s_{\phi}^{\prime}\rangle
becomes comparable to
\langle r_{\phi}\rangle\langle s_{\phi}\rangle
. In practice this affects most the stationary state where the system spends significant time at
s_{\text{i}}\approx 0
, i.e. after glaciation has happened.

Now consider the evolution of the average particle radii. In Appendix
C
we derive an approximate equation for
\langle r_{\phi}\rangle
under the same assumptions used to derive Eq. (
43
)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle r_{\phi}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}\approx A_{3,\phi}\frac{\langle s_{\phi}\rangle}{\langle r_{\phi}\rangle}\biggl(1-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sigma_{s_{\phi}}^{2}}{\langle s_{\phi}\rangle|\langle s_{\phi}\rangle|}\text{Da}_{r_{\phi}}\biggr).

(44)

This result shows that small-scale turbulent fluctuations are negligible for small particle Damköhler numbers. The physical reason why the mean radius dynamics is unaffected by turbulent fluctuations of supersaturation is the same decoupling between the dynamics of
r_{\phi}^{\prime}
and
s_{\phi}^{\prime}
discussed in the previous paragraph. Equation (
44
) comes with the same caveat as above, it fails near
s_{\phi}\approx 0
. We stress that the statistical model correctly describes the mean radius even when
s_{\phi}\approx 0
.

In conclusion, Figs.
1
and
2
, as well as Eqs. (
43
) and (
44
) show that turbulence has at best a weak effect upon the evolution of the mean particle size at small Damköhler numbers. This implies that small-scale turbulence neither affects the
WBF process nor the resulting glaciation time, defined as the time it takes for the ice-particle mass fraction IWC/(IWC+LWC+
q_{\text{v}}\rho_{0}
) to reach 90%
[
7
]
.

On the other hand, Figure
3
(
a
) shows that small-scale turbulence has an effect upon the fluctuations
\sigma_{r_{\phi}}^{2}=\langle r_{\phi}^{\prime 2}\rangle
of the particle radii, as
Chen et al. [6]
concluded in their DNS study. Our model can explain this as follows. Equations (
16
),
\mathrm{d}r_{\phi}/\mathrm{d}t\sim A_{3,\phi}s_{\phi}/r_{\phi}
imply that particles evaporate more rapidly the smaller they are. As a consequence, any small differences in radii of evaporating particles amplify in a deterministic fashion, explaining the rapid growth of the variance in Fig.
3
. However, when the initial variance vanishes as for the CTGC, turbulent supersaturation fluctuations are required to initially widen the particle-size distribution, triggering the amplification.
The widening of
\sigma_{r_{\phi}}
happens for water droplets, but not for ice particles. Since the ice particles grow, the variance
\langle r_{\text{i}}^{\prime 2}\rangle
shrinks instead. We note, finally, that the peaks of
\sigma_{r_{\text{w}}}/\langle r_{\text{w}}\rangle
in Fig.
3
coincide with the peaks of the droplet Damköhler number
\text{Da}_{r_{\text{w}}}
[Fig.
3
(
b
)], confirming that larger particle Damköhler numbers imply stronger coupling between
s_{\phi}^{\prime}
and
r_{\phi}^{\prime}
.

The above reasonings regarding the effect of small-scale turbulence on the average particle radii and their fluctuations have to be adjusted when discussing the core of the Pi chamber (Fig.
4
), because we did not account for the particle injection and removal. In this case the particle-size distribution of larger droplets (
r_{\text{w}}>$3.5\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{m}$
as in Fig.
4
) is dominated not by turbulence, but by injection of small particles and removal of larger ones, so small-scale turbulence has a weaker effect, compared with the CTGC. However, if we look at the PDF of
r_{\text{w}}
for all droplet sizes [Fig.
5
(
a
)], we see that turbulence has no influence only on cases with low ice injection rates. These cases correspond to larger particle sizes and smaller droplet Damköhler number
\text{Da}_{r_{\text{w}}}
. Once the ice injection rate increases and the cloud glaciates, only small or even unactivated droplets remain. Their
\text{Da}_{r_{\text{w}}}
is larger and they are sensitive to supersaturation fluctuations, which widen droplet size distribution compared to the case without turbulence. The distributions of ice particle size [Fig.
5
(
b
)] is unaffected by turbulence, because ice remains large and
\text{Da}_{r_{\text{i}}}
is small.

At larger Damköhler numbers, small-scale turbulence could have a larger effect, in particular for spatially inhomogeneous initial conditions
[
11
]
. Consider for example increasing the simulation-box size in the DNS to increase the size of the unresolved turbulent scales. This increases the Damköhler numbers (
41
), causing small-scale turbulence to be more important.
In
[
6
,
7
]
the linear size of the simulation domain was about
0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
, the Damköhler number Da
{}_{r_{\text{w}}}
does not exceed 1.5 [Fig.
3
(
b
)].
In order to get Damköhler numbers of order 15, where turbulence is expected to have a stronger effect
[
11
]
, the linear size should be at least about
20\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
(since
\tau_{L}\sim(L^{2}/\varepsilon)^{1/3}
). But as discussed above, the statistical model may fail when the Damköhler numbers become too large. In this case more refined approximations for the condensation rates are needed
[
11
]
.
It could also be of interest to formulate models aimed to describe the large-Da limit, as used in dense evaporating sprays
[
42
]
.

Here we considered high number densities of ice particles, typical for deeper clouds.
Polar stratus clouds tend to have lower ice-particle number densities, of the order
10^{-3}\,
cm
-3

[
2
]
. In this case it is hard to reach the steady state with DNS (although it is possible for the statistical model). Figure 7 of
Chen et al. [6]
shows results for the initial growth of ice particles at lower ice-particle number density
(the lowest value is
8\times 10^{-3}\,
cm
-3
). We performed our own DNSs for these cases, and find good agreement with the statistical-model results, and with the deterministic limit of the model (not shown).

Abade and Albuquerque [43]
found
that turbulence increases cloud glaciation times, and that ice particles and supercooled droplets experience different supersaturation fluctuations, in apparent contradiction with the DNS of
[
6
]
, and with our statistical-model results. This discrepancy is explained by the approximations for condensation and deposition rates used in
[
43
]
, where the influence of neighbouring particles (ice particles or water droplets) is not taken into account, hindering the WBF process.

Korolev and Milbrandt [44]
speculated that ice particles and
water droplets may be locally unmixed at small scales. In this
case one expects the supersaturation distributions to be
non-Gaussian
[
11
]
, causing the statistical model to fail, in the form used here. Instead, one must rely on improved approximations of the supersaturation dynamics, such as mapping-closure approximations
[
10
,
9
,
11
]
.
In this case we certainly expect the deterministic model to be inaccurate, in particular for the glaciation time. The question is by how much local unmixing delays glaciation, and how much turbulence contributes to the acceleration of it. This question also relates to a recent field campaign in which supercooled clouds were seeded with ice nuclei to initiate the WBF
[
45
]
. For these experiments, turbulence appears to be crucial to expose the newly nucleated ice crystals to water droplets
[
46
]
.

VI
Conclusions

We analysed the effect of small-scale turbulence on the glaciation process in mixed-phase clouds using a statistical model similar to those used to describe droplet evaporation
in warm clouds. We found that the model describes DNS results for the glaciation process very well, for the parameters
in
[
6
]
corresponding to a cloud-top generating cell and also for the parameters specified in
[
7
]
corresponding
to the core of the Michigan Pi cloud chamber.

The statistical-model analysis shows that small-scale turbulence has an overall small effect on ice growth. Small-scale turbulence affects the evaporation of water droplets in the CTGC in that it initialises the growth of the droplet-size variance. In the core of the Pi chamber, for the parameters specified by
Wang et al. [7]
, small-scale turbulence matters only close to the glaciation transition, where the water droplets are very small. When the corresponding time scale for droplet evaporation decreases
so that it is of the same order of magnitude as the Lagrangian mixing time, then small-scale turbulence may have a stronger effect.

Our calculations show more generally that the effect of turbulence on glaciation is expected to be larger at larger Damköhler numbers, i.e., on larger spatial scales
L
.
Since mixed-phase clouds exhibit a large range of vertical and horizontal length scales, covering shallow stratiform clouds of just hundred meters depth and a horizontal extent of several tens to hundreds of kilometers to deep convective clouds extending across the entire troposphere, the potential for small-scale turbulence to affect the glaciation process in these clouds needs to be acknowledged. This is especially true since most models used in the atmospheric sciences (from large-scale global circulation models to comparably high-resolution large-eddy simulation models) do not represent the effects of small-scale turbulence on cloud microphysical processes such as the WBF process on length scales smaller than at least few tens to a couple of hundred meters
(i.e., their grid spacing)
.

A statistical model, such as the one presented here, may not only enable us to assess the effects of small-scale turbulence on the WBF process on larger length scales in a follow-up study, but could also be the basis of a parameterisation to represent the effects of small-scale turbulence in larger-scale models, which still struggle to represent mixed-phase clouds, and especially the coupling of cloud microphysics and turbulence
[
47
]
.

\displaystyle=\biggl[\biggl(\frac{L_{\phi}(T_{0})}{R_{\text{v}}T_{0}}-1\biggr)\frac{R_{\text{a}}}{c_{p}}\frac{\rho_{\phi}L_{\phi}(T_{0})}{\varkappa_{T,\phi}\,p_{0}}+\frac{\rho_{\phi}R_{\text{v}}T_{0}}{\varkappa_{q_{\text{v}},\phi}\,p_{\text{v},\phi}(T_{0})}\biggr]^{-1}

(51)

\displaystyle=\biggl(\frac{R_{\text{a}}}{R_{\text{v}}}\frac{\rho_{\phi}L_{\phi}^{2}(T_{0})}{\varkappa_{T}c_{p}T_{0}p_{0}}+\frac{\rho_{\phi}R_{\text{v}}T_{0}}{\varkappa_{q_{\text{v}}}p_{\text{v},\phi}(T_{0})}+\frac{\rho_{\phi}\sqrt{2\pi R_{\text{v}}T_{0}}}{r_{\phi}\alpha_{q_{\text{v}},\phi}p_{\text{v},\phi}(T_{0})}\biggr)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\biggl(\frac{1}{A_{3,\phi}}+\frac{1}{A_{3,\phi}}\frac{r_{A_{3,\phi}}}{r_{\phi}}\biggr)^{-1}=A_{3}\,a_{3}(r/r_{A_{3,\phi}}).

The last row yields Eqs. (

) and (

).

Appendix B
Condensation rates in the statistical model

In this Appendix we derive the expressions for the conditional condensations rates
\langle C_{\text{w}}\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}},t\rangle
and
\langle C_{\text{i}}\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}},t\rangle
, the mean condensation rates
\langle C_{\text{w}}\rangle(t)
and
\langle C_{\text{i}}\rangle(t)
, and the statistical model (
36
). The notation
\langle{}\cdot{}\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}},t\rangle
is a shorthand for the usual notation for the conditional averages:

\langle{}\cdot{}\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}},t\rangle=\langle{}\cdot{}\mathbin{|}S_{\text{w}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=s_{\text{w}}\rangle.

(56)

This defines the ensemble average over all flow realisations for which the supersaturation field at point
\boldsymbol{x}
at time
t
equals
s_{\text{w}}
. We use upper- and lower-case letters to distinguish the random variable
S(\boldsymbol{x},t)
from its value
s_{\text{w}}
. All results here are valid for a statistically homogeneous system. For more details on the mathematical tools used here see Appendix H in Ref.
[
61
]
.

Conditional condensation rates.
To simplify the notation, we ignore here the subscript
w
in
s_{\text{w}}
and use just
s
for supersaturation, especially since the derivation is valid for
s_{\text{i}}
too. First we show that any conditional average for quantities of the form (
24
) can be computed as

\Bigl<\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\phi}}G(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})F\bigl(r_{\alpha},S(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha},t)\bigr)\Bigm|S(\boldsymbol{x},t)=s\Bigr\rangle=\frac{N_{\phi}}{V}\frac{f_{\phi}(s,t)}{f(s,t)}\,\langle F\mathbin{|}s,t\rangle_{\phi}.

(57)

Here
F
is any function of a particle radius and supersaturation at the position of this particle, and
V
is the volume of the simulation box. Next,
f(s,t)
is the PDF of the Eulerian supersaturation field, it can be written as

f(s,t)=\langle\delta(S(\boldsymbol{x},t)-s)\rangle,

(58)

where
\delta
is the Dirac delta function.
The PDF
f_{\phi}
of supersaturation at the particle positions (water droplets or ice particles,
\phi=\text{w}
or
\phi=\text{i}
) is:

f_{\phi}(s,t)=\frac{1}{N_{\phi}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\phi}}\langle\delta(S(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha},t)-s)\rangle.

(59)

Averages over the particle positions are denoted as
\langle{}\cdot{}\rangle_{\phi}
,
\phi=\text{w}
or
\phi=\text{i}
:

\langle F\mathbin{|}s,t\rangle_{\phi}=\frac{1}{N_{\phi}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\phi}}\langle F(r_{\alpha},S(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha},t))\mathbin{|}S(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha},t)=s\rangle.

(60)

To derive (
57
), we use that the conditional average of a field
H(\boldsymbol{x},t)
can be expressed as

\bigl<H\bigm|S(\boldsymbol{x},t)=s\bigr>=\frac{1}{f(s,t)}\bigl<H\,\delta(S(\boldsymbol{x},t)-s)\bigr>.

(61)

Second, we use that the system is statistically homogeneous. This allows us to take the spatial average of the l.h.s. of Eq. (
57
),
resulting in

\displaystyle\Bigl<

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\phi}}G(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})F\bigl(r_{\alpha},S(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha},t)\bigr)\Bigm|S(\boldsymbol{x},t)=s\Bigr>=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\phi}}\frac{1}{f(s,t)}\bigl<G(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})\,F\bigl(r_{\alpha},S(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha},t)\bigr)\,\delta(S(\boldsymbol{x},t)-s)\bigr>

(62)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\int_{V}\frac{1}{f(s,t)}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\phi}}\bigl<G(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})\,F\bigl(r_{\alpha},S(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha},t)\bigr)\,\delta(S(\boldsymbol{x},t)-s)\bigr>\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}.

Third, we require that the support of spatial kernels
G
is smaller than the length scale at which
s(\boldsymbol{x},t)
varies, so that we can treat
G
as a delta function. Under these conditions we obtain

\displaystyle\Bigl\langle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\phi}}G(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle F\bigl(r_{\alpha},S(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha},t\bigr)\Bigm|S(\boldsymbol{x},t)=s\Bigr\rangle=\frac{1}{V}\frac{1}{f(s,t)}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\phi}}\bigl<F\bigl(r_{\alpha},S(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha},t)\bigr)\,\delta(S(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha},t)-s)\bigr>

(63)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\frac{f_{\phi}(s,t)}{f(s,t)}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\phi}}\langle F\bigl(r_{\alpha},S(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha},t)\bigr)\mathbin{|}S(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha},t)=s\rangle=\frac{N_{\phi}}{V}\frac{f_{\phi}(s,t)}{f(s,t)}\langle F\mathbin{|}s,t\rangle_{\phi}.

Using this result and Eq. (
23
), we can rewrite the conditional condensation rates as

\displaystyle\langle C_{\text{w}}\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}},t\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3}\pi\frac{\rho_{\text{w}}}{\rho_{0}}\frac{N_{\text{w}}}{V}\frac{f_{\text{w}}(s_{\text{w}},t)}{f(s_{\text{w}},t)}\biggl<\frac{\mathrm{d}r_{\text{w}}^{3}}{\mathrm{d}t}\biggm|s_{\text{w}},t\biggr>_{\!\text{w}},

(64a)

\displaystyle\langle C_{\text{i}}\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}},t\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3}\pi\frac{\rho_{\text{i}}}{\rho_{0}}\frac{N_{\text{i}}}{V}\frac{f_{\text{i}}(s_{\text{w}},t)}{f(s_{\text{w}},t)}\biggl<\frac{\mathrm{d}r_{\text{i}}^{3}}{\mathrm{d}t}\biggm|s_{\text{w}},t\biggr>_{\!\text{i}}.

(64b)

These formulae are consistent with Eq. (5) in
[
11
]
, who considered a turbulent mixing problem with spatially inhomogeneous initial conditions and strong phase change, using mapping-closure approximations.

Mean condensation rates
.
Averaging expressions (
64
) for
\langle C_{\text{w}}\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}},t\rangle
and
\langle C_{\text{i}}\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}},t\rangle
over
s_{\text{w}}
, we obtain Eqs. (
33
) for the mean condensation rates.

Statistical model for
s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}
with condensation-rate fluctuations
. Now we derive the model (
36
) for the fluctuating supersaturation
s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}
that involves the condensation terms following the method of
[
30
]
. We start with the exact equation for
s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}
, which for a statistically homogeneous system follows from (
12a
) as

\frac{\mathrm{d}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\varkappa\frac{\partial^{2}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{j}}-A_{2,\text{w}}C_{\text{w}}^{\prime}-A_{2,\text{i}}C_{\text{i}}^{\prime}+f^{({s_{\text{w}}})^{\prime}}.

(65)

If
s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}
has a fast oscillating component we can model it as a stochastic differential equation

\mathrm{d}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}=D^{(1)}(s_{\text{w}}^{\prime},t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{D^{(2)}(s_{\text{w}}^{\prime},t)}\,\mathrm{d}W.

(66)

This equation describes the behaviour of
s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}
on timescales larger than
\tau_{\text{M},s_{\text{w}}}
, the Markov–Einstein timescale of
s_{\text{w}}
and is not applicable for modelling the real behavior of
s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}
on smaller timescales. The Markov–Einstein timescale is of the order of the Taylor timescale for
s_{\text{w}}
,
\tau_{\text{M},s_{\text{w}}}\sim\sqrt{\langle s_{\text{w}}^{\prime 2}\rangle/\langle(\mathrm{d}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}/\mathrm{d}t)^{2}\rangle}
and is of the order of the usual velocity Taylor timescale.
In Itô’s stochastic calculus that we use in Eq. (
66
), future values of white noise are independent on the current value of
s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}
(the non-anticipation property). Since from (
66
) we can express the white-noise term with

\sqrt{D^{(2)}(s_{\text{w}}^{\prime},t)}\,\mathrm{d}W=\mathrm{d}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}-D^{(1)}(s_{\text{w}}^{\prime},t)\,\mathrm{d}t

(67)

we requite it to obey the non-anticipation property:

\biggl<\frac{\mathrm{d}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t+\Delta t)-D^{(1)}(s_{\text{w}}(t+\Delta t),t+\Delta t)\biggm|s_{\text{w}},t\biggr>=0,\quad\text{for $\Delta t\geqslant\tau_{M,s_{\text{w}}}$}.

(68)

Since
D^{(1)}
evolves on timescales larger than
\tau_{M,s_{\text{w}}}
, we can approximate it from (
68
) as

\displaystyle D^{(1)}(s_{\text{w}},t)

\displaystyle=\biggl<\frac{\mathrm{d}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t+\tau_{M,s_{\text{w}}})\biggm|s_{\text{w}},t\biggr>=\varkappa\biggl<\frac{\partial^{2}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{j}}(t+\tau_{M,s_{\text{w}}})\biggm|s_{\text{w}},t\biggr>+\bigl<f^{({s_{\text{w}}})^{\prime}}(t+\tau_{M,s_{\text{w}}})\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}}\bigr>

(69)

\displaystyle-A_{2,\text{w}}\bigl<C_{\text{w}}^{\prime}(t+\tau_{M,s_{\text{w}}})|s_{\text{w}},t\bigr>-A_{2,\text{i}}\bigl<C_{\text{i}}^{\prime}(t+\tau_{M,s_{\text{w}}})\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}},t\bigr>.

Because the change of
s_{\text{w}}
due to evaporation/condensation is slow compared to
\tau_{\text{M},s_{\text{w}}}
in our setup, we can substitute
\langle C_{\text{w}}^{\prime}(t+\tau_{\text{M},s_{\text{w}}})\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}},t\rangle
with
\langle C_{\text{w}}^{\prime}(t)\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}},t\rangle
. For the diffusion term, we can use a usual Langevin mixing closure, while the forcing term will be accounted with
D^{(2)}
since its only role is to keep
\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}
constant. Thus we turn (
66
) into

\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}_{\text{w}}=-A_{2,\text{w}}\langle C_{\text{w}}^{\prime}\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime},t\rangle-A_{2,\text{i}}\langle C_{\text{i}}^{\prime}\mathbin{|}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime},t\rangle-\frac{1}{\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{({L})}}s^{\prime}_{\text{w}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{D^{(2)}(s^{\prime}_{\text{w}},t})\,\mathrm{d}W(t).

(70)

We could also relate
D^{(2)}(s_{\text{w}},t)
to the statistics of
\mathrm{d}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}/\mathrm{d}t
as further described by
[
30
]
. However, for simplicity we just take
D^{(2)}
to be independent of
s_{\text{w}}
, also ensuring that its value yields
\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}=\text{const}
:

D^{(2)}=\frac{2\sigma_{s_{\text{w}}}^{2}}{\tau_{s_{\text{w}}}^{({L})}}+2A_{2,\text{w}}\langle C_{\text{w}}^{\prime}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}\rangle+2A_{2,\text{i}}\langle C_{\text{i}}^{\prime}s_{\text{w}}^{\prime}\rangle.

(71)

Thus we arrive to Eq. (
36
).

turbulence on particle-size distributions

In this Appendix we summarise details regarding the effect of small-scale turbulence on the mean supersaturation and the mean particle size, needed for the discussion in Section
V
