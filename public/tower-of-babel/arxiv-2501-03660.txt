Involutive (simple) latin solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation and related (left) quasigroups
Marco BONATTO, Marco CASTELLI

Abstract
In this paper, we study involutive non-degenerate set-theoretic solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation with regular displacement group. In particular, we completely describe the blocks of imprimitivity and the congruences of the irretractable ones, that we show belonging to the class of the latin solutions. Among these solutions, we characterise the simple ones having nilpotent permutation group. A more precise description involving the First Weyl Algebra will be provided when the displacement group is abelian and normal in the total permutation group, and we enumerate and classify the simple ones having minimal size p p p^{p} , for an arbitrary prime number p p . Finally, we illustrate our results by some examples.

Introduction

The quantum Yang-Baxter equation first appeared in theoretical physics, in a paper by C.N. Yang
[
44
]
, and in statistical mechanics, in R.J. Baxter’s work
[
6
]
. On the other hand, it has been studied also been studied from a mathematical viewpoint. In 1992 Drinfel’d
[
25
]
suggested the study of
set-theoretical
solutions, i.e. solutions that act on a basis of the underlying vector space. Specifically, a
set-theoretic solution of the Yang-Baxter equation
on a non-empty set
X
is a pair
\left(X,r\right)
, where

r:X\times X\to X\times X
is a map such that the relation

\displaystyle\left(r\times\id_{X}\right)\left(\id_{X}\times r\right)\left(r\times\id_{X}\right)=\left(\id_{X}\times r\right)\left(r\times\id_{X}\right)\left(\id_{X}\times r\right)

is satisfied. Set theoretical solutions provide solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation by linearization.
Writing a solution
(X,r)
as
r\left(x,y\right)=\left(\lambda_{x}\left(y\right),\rho_{y}\left(x\right)\right)
, with

\lambda_{x},\rho_{x}
maps from
X
into itself, for every
x\in X
, we say that
(X,r)
is
non-degenerate
if
\lambda_{x},\rho_{x}\in\Sym_{X}
, for every
x\in X
, and
involutive
if
r^{2}=\id_{X\times X}
.

The seminal papers due by Gateva-Ivanova and Van Den Bergh
[
27
]
and Etingov, Schedler, and Soloviev
[
26
]
encouraged several authors to the study of the involutive non-degenerate set-theoretic solutions (which we simply call
involutive solutions
). Recall that a solution
(X,r)
is said to be
decomposable
if
X
can be expressed as a disjoint union of two non-empty subsets
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
such that
r(Y_{i}\times Y_{i})\subseteq Y_{i}\times Y_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,2\}
, and
(X,r)
is called
indecomposable
, otherwise. To attack the classification-problem of involutive solutions, as first attempt one can restrict the investigations to the class of indecomposable ones. This approach is motivated by the fact that indecomposable solutions are in some sense the fundamental blocks that allow to understand all the involutive ones (see
[
26
, Section
3
]
). Even if we focus on indecomposable involutive solutions, a full classification is still hard, and in the last few years several authors investigated the so-called simple indecomposable involutive solutions, where a solution
(X,r)
is said to be
simple
if every epimorphic image
(Y,s)
is isomorphic to
(X,r)
or is a singleton. A notion of simple involutive solution (not necessarily indecomposable) was at first introduced by Vendramin in
[
42
]
for involutive solutions not necessarily indecomposable. Even if his definition of simplicity is different, it coincides in the indecomposable case. Every simple involutive solution of size bigger than
2
is indecomposable by
[
19
]
. These solutions play a special role since they are the “fundamental blocks" to construct all the indecomposable involutive ones by dynamical extensions (see
[
11
, Proposition 2]
and
[
42
, Corollary 2.13]
).

Even if the construction of simple involutive solutions appeared quite hard, several interesting results were obtained in the last few years. If
p
is an odd prime number there exist a unique simple involutive solution of size
p
, see
[
26
, Theorem 2.13]
. For every natural number
n>1
, a simple involutive solution of cardinality
p^{n}
was recently constructed in
[
21
]
, and in the case
n=2
a full classification was given in
[
23
]
. Further examples of cardinality
n^{2}
and
mn^{2}
, where
n,m
are arbitrary natural number, were constructed in
[
19
,
17
]
. On the other hand, we know that an indecomposable involutive solutions of square-free cardinality can not be simple, as showed in
[
20
]
.

In order to provide a theoretic description of simple solutions, in
[
14
]
the second author characterised them in terms of skew left braces provided by their associated permutation groups. Recall that a skew left brace, introduced in
[
28
,
35
]
, is a triple
(B,+,\circ)
where
(B,+)
and
(B,\circ)
are groups and the equality

x\circ(y+z)=x\circ y-x+x\circ z

holds for all
x,y,z\in B
. The notion of simplicity was recently investigated in the general setting of bijective non-degenerate solutions (simply called
solution
) not necessarily involutive. In
[
13
]
, a criterion of simplicity was provided by blocks of imprimitivity of the associated permutation group. A remarkable generalization of
[
14
, Theorem
12
]
was recently provided in
[
22
]
, where the simple bijective solutions (not necessarily involutive) were completely characterised by the underlying permutation skew left braces, and several examples of non-involutive simple solutions were provided. In this way, the main Theorem of
[
22
, Section
1
]
represents a unifying tool to understand the notion of simplicity of several algebraic structures already present in literature.
By these last results, it is clear that for the simplicity of a solution the ideal
B^{(2)}
of the permutation left brace plays a fundamental role. However, we are still far away from a concrete description of the maps
r
related to simple solutions. Indeed, in
[
22
]
a complete description of simple solutions provided by skew left braces
B
with
B^{(2)}=0
or
B^{(3)}=0
was given, but the remaining case
B^{(2)}=B^{(3)}\neq 0
is still open. As noted in
[
22
, Section
1
]
, the last case includes the involutive simple solutions that are not of Lyuabashenko type. To partially fill this gap, one of the main goals of this paper is the study of finite involutive simple solutions in which
B^{(2)}
, that in this case coincides with the so-called
displacement group
, acts regularly on the underlying set. We will show that these solutions belong to the family of
latin
solutions, i.e. solutions in which the maps
\lambda_{x}
provide a quasigroup structure on the set
X
(see
[
8
]
).However, our investigation is not limited to simple solutions. Motivated by
[
16
, Theorem
3.1
]
, where involutive solutions with primitive permutation group were completely classified, we focus on blocks of imprimitivity of involutive solutions (not necessarily simple). In addition to the main result of
[
16
]
, which ensures the existence of a complete blocks system, we will show that a complete blocks system can be always obtained by the orbits of a minimal normal subgroup of the permutation group. To develop a theory useful to simple involutive solutions, in the second part we specialize to the irretractable ones. By tools that come from quasigroups theory, we decribe
all
the complete blocks system of irretractable involutive solutions with regular displacement group. Among these complete blocks systems, we distinguish the ones that give rise to congruences, providing an extension of a result involving the family of finite
affine
quasigroups (see
[
8
, Lemma 4.8]

[

]
, we develop a covering theory of left quasigroups that allows to recover the one given in
[
41
]
for indecomposable involutive solutions as a special case. This is the reason for which, throught the paper, we will use the language of cycle sets. Recall that a

is a left quasigroup
(X,\cdotp)
in which the equality
(x\cdotp y)\cdotp(x\cdotp z)=(y\cdotp x)\cdotp(y\cdotp z)
holds for all
x,y,z\in X
. Cycle sets are useful to detect solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation: indeed, non-degenerate cycle sets bijectively correspond to non-degenerate involutive solutions, and by
[
34
, Propositions 1-2]
the correspondence is given by

(X,\cdotp)\longrightarrow(X,r)\quad with\quad r(x,y):=(\sigma_{x}^{-1}(y),\sigma_{x}^{-1}(y)\cdot x).

(1)

As one can expect, indecomposable (resp. simple) involutive solutions correspond to indecomposable (resp. simple) cycle sets. In the core of the paper, we focus on simple involutive solutions
(X,r)
with nilpotent regular displacement group. We will show that, in this setting,
X
must have a prime-power cardinality
p^{n}
and the
p
-Sylow subgroup of the permutation group
\mathcal{G}(X)
is normal. In this case we can reduce the classification to solutions for which
\mathcal{G}(X)
is a
p
-group. A complete description of these solutions will be provided, while the ones having cyclic displacement group will be concretely classified. These results will be refined if, in addition, the displacement group is abelian and normal in the so-called
total permutation group
. In this last case, we exhibit a connection
with irreducible representations of the First Weyl Algebra over a field with a prime number of elements. We remark that the First Weyl Algebra has already appeared in the study of involutive solutions, see
[
8
,
39
]
. These solutions has always size
p^{k}
, for a prime number
p
and a natural number
k
divisible by
p
. Among these solutions, the ones having size

will be completely classified. Finally, we illustrate our main results with some examples, and we use some of them to answer in the affirmative to
[
19
, Question 7.6]
.

1
Left quasigroups

1.1
Basics

A
left quasigroup
is an algebraic structure given by a non-empty set
X
together with a binary operation
\cdot:X\times X\longrightarrow X
, such that the map

\sigma_{x}:y\mapsto x\cdot y

is bijective for every
x\in X
. Left quasigroups can also be understood as algebraic structure with two binary operations, namely
\{\cdot,\backslash\}
where
x\backslash y=\sigma_{x}^{-1}(y)
for every
x,y\in X
. In the finite case, the two description are equivalent (they might differ in the infinite case), so for the scope of this paper we are using the definition with just one binary operation.

The
associated permutation group
(also called
left multiplication group
) is defined as

\mathcal{G}(X)=\langle\sigma_{x}\mid x\in X\rangle\leq Sym(X).

If such a group is transitive on
X
we say that
X
is
indecomposable
(the same property is also known as
connectedness
).

The map

\delta_{x}:y\mapsto y\cdot x

does not need to be bijective. If
\delta_{x}
is bijective for every
x\in X
we say that
X
is
latin
. In this case we can define another binary operation as
x/y=\delta_{y}^{-1}(x)
for every
x,y\in X
. The set
X
endowed with the three binary operations
\{\cdot,\backslash,/\}
is called
quasigroup
. If the underlying set is finite the algebraic properties of the left quasigroup and the quasigroups are the same (they might differ in the infinite case). For latin left quasigroups we can define another subgroup of the symmetric group, namely the
total permutation group
as

\mathcal{TG}(X)=\langle\sigma_{x},\delta_{x}\mid x\in X\rangle.

An equivalence relation on
X
is said to be a
congruence
if for every
x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in X
we have that if
x\sim x^{\prime}
and
y\sim y^{\prime}
then
x\cdotp y\sim x^{\prime}\cdotp y^{\prime}
and
\sigma_{x}^{-1}(y)\sim\sigma_{x^{\prime}}^{-1}(y^{\prime})
. From now on, we indicate by
\sim_{1_{X}}
the congruence in which all the elements belong to the same equivalence class and by
\sim_{0_{X}}
the trivial congruence induced by singleton classes. Accordingly the operation
[x]\cdot[y]=[x\cdot y]
is well-defined and the quotient set
X/\sim
together with this operation is a left quasigroup.

Let
X,Y
be left quasigroups. A
homomorphism
from
X
to
Y
is a map
p:X\rightarrow Y
such that
p(x\cdotp y)=p(x)\cdotp p(y)
for all
x,y\in X
. If
p
is surjective, then it will be called
epimorphism
, while a bijective homomorphism is said to be
isomorphism
. An isomorphism from a left quasigroup
X
to itself will be called
automorphism
and the automorphism group will be indicated by
Aut(X,\cdotp)
.

Epimorphisms and congruence are essentially the same thing. Indeed if
\sim
is a congruence, the canonical map
X\longrightarrow X/\sim
is an epimorphism. On the other hand, the equivalence relation
\sim_{p}
induced by
p
given by
x\sim_{p}y\Longleftrightarrow p(x)=p(y)
for all
x,y\in X
, is a congruence of
X
and
Y\cong X/{\sim_{p}}
.

Lemma 1
.

[
2
, Lemma 1.8]

If
X,Y
are left quasigroups and
p:X\rightarrow Y
is a left quasigroups epimorphism, the assignment
\sigma_{x}\mapsto\sigma_{p(x)}
extends to a groups epimorphism
\bar{p}:\mathcal{G}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(Y)
.

We can define the binary relation

Indecomposable left quasigroups can always be described in terms of left cosets.

Proposition 3

Let
X
be a latin left quasigroup. Then,

Assume that

Given a left quasigroup
(X,\cdotp)
, we can define several operations that makes

into a different left quasigroup.

Definition 2
.

If
(X,\cdotp)
is a left quasigroup and

4.1
Right linear cycle sets

Let us introduce a family of left quasigroups constructed over groups.

Definition 5

A left quasigroup

\phi(\phi(y)+\psi(x))+\psi(\phi(y))=\phi(\phi(x)+\psi(y))+\psi(\phi(x))

The automorphism group of affine latin left quasigroups are completely described.

Lemma 28

Proposition 46

Let

6.2
Simple affine cycle sets of minimal size

By
Theorem

50
, several examples of simple cycle sets can be obtained by irreducible representations of the first Weyl Algebra
A_{1}(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})
. Even if these representations are not classified over arbitrary fields (to our knowledge), several examples can be recovered from the irreducible representations of the first Weyl Algebras on algebrically closed fields, that are completely classified. Indeed, if
K
is an algebrically closed field of characteristic
p>0
, all the finite dimensional irreducible representations of
A_{1}(K):=K[a,b]/(ab-ba-1)
are given by the
p\times p
matrices

M_{a}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&...&\mu\\
1&0&0&...&0\\
0&1&0&...&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&1&0\end{bmatrix}\qquad and\qquad M_{b}:=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&p-1&0&...&0\\
0&\lambda&p-2&...&0\\
0&0&\lambda&...&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&1\\
0&0&0&0&\lambda\end{bmatrix}

(10)

where
\mu,\lambda\in K
. The argument for the proof of the following Proposition was suggested in
[
1
]
.

Proposition 53
.

Let
p
be a prime number,
V:=(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{p}
and
\rho:A_{1}(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow End(V)
be an irreducible representation of
A_{1}(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})
of dimension
p
. Then, there exists a basis
\{e_{1},...,e_{p}\}
of
V
such that
\rho(a)=M_{a}
and
\rho(b)=M_{b}
for suitable elements
\mu,\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Let
M_{1}:=\rho(a)
,
M_{2}:=\rho(b)
with respect some basis
\mathcal{B}=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{p}\}
of
V
, and
K
be the algebraic closure of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. Since
dim(V)=p
,
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
provide a
p
-dimensional representation
\bar{\rho}
of
A_{1}(K)
, that must be irreducible. Moreover, we have that
\mathcal{B}
can be regarded as a basis of
K^{p}
, and we have
\bar{\rho}(a)=M_{1}
and
\bar{\rho}(b)=M_{2}
with respect
\mathcal{B}
.
By the previous remark, there exist a basis
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}:=\{e^{\prime}_{1},...,e^{\prime}_{p}\}
of
K^{p}
such that
\bar{\rho}(a)=M_{a}
and
\bar{\rho}(b)=M_{b}
for suitable elements
\mu,\lambda\in K
. Hence, there exist a matrix
C\in GL_{p}(K)
providing the change of basis from
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
to
\mathcal{B}
such that
M_{a}=C^{-1}M_{1}C
and
M_{b}=C^{-1}M_{2}C
. To show the statement, is sufficient proving that
C\in GL_{p}(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})
.

The characteristic polynomial does not change by conjugacy, therefore we have that the characteristic polynomial of
M_{2}
, which is equal to the one of
M_{b}
, is
(x-\lambda)^{p}=x^{p}-\lambda^{p}
, hence
\lambda^{p}\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
and this implies that
\lambda
is an element of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
.
Since the dimension of the
\lambda
-eigenspace of
M_{2}
as endomorphism of
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{p}
(which is equal to the dimension of the
\lambda
-eigenspace of
M_{b}
as endomorphism of
K^{p}
) is
1
, the vector
e^{\prime}_{1}
, up to multiply by a suitable scalar, is an eigenvector of
M_{2}
. Therefore, we can assume that
e^{\prime}_{1}=e_{1}
(up to consider matrices
M_{1}^{\prime}
and
M_{2}^{\prime}
similar to
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
in
GL_{p}(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})
).
Now, let
f_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}^{p}
having
0
in the
j
-th position, with
j\neq i
, and
1
in the
i
-th position. Therefore, the first column of
C
is
f_{1}
. Now, we have that
M_{a}^{j-1}f_{1}=C^{-1}M_{1}^{j-1}Cf_{1}=C^{-1}M_{1}^{j-1}f_{1}
and hence
Cf_{j}=M_{1}^{j-1}f_{1}
for every
j\in\{1,...,p\}
, and in a similar way
\mu Cf_{1}=M_{1}^{p}f_{1}
. From these equalities, we obtain
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
and
C\in GL_{p}(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})
.

∎

By
Theorem

50
and
Proposition

53
, we are able to construct concretely
all
the affine simple cycle sets of size
p^{p}
, for an arbitrary prime number
p
. The next goal is to distinguish the isomorphism classes.

Now, we define the algebra of the
\mu
-circulant matrices over a field
K
in analogy with the classical circulant matrices. Recall that a square matrix is
circulant
if it is constant on all broken diagonals, and denote by
Circ(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
the
n\times n
circulant matrix with first row equal to
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
. Let
\mu\in K
we define the
\mu
-circulant
matrix
\mu-Circ(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
to be the matrix obtained by
Circ(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
multiplying all the upper-triangular entries of
Circ(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
by the element
\mu
, as

\mu-Circ(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}&\mu c_{2}&\ldots&\ldots&\mu c_{n-1}&\mu c_{n}\\
c_{n}&c_{1}&\mu c_{2}&\ldots&\ldots&\mu c_{n-1}\\
c_{n-1}&c_{n}&c_{1}&\mu c_{2}&\ldots&\ldots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
c_{3}&\ldots&\ldots&c_{n}&c_{1}&\mu c_{2}\\
c_{2}&c_{3}&\ldots&c_{n-1}&c_{n}&c_{1}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(11)

The algebra of the circulant matrices coincides with the centralizer of the permutation matrix representing the
n
-cycle. We will show that the
\mu
-circulant matrices are the centralizer of the following matrix:

A_{\mu}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\ldots&0&\mu\\
1&0&\ldots&0&0\\
0&1&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&1&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(12)

Lemma 54
.

The set of the
n\times n

\mu
-circulant matrices is the subalgebra of the matrices which commutes with
A_{\mu}
(as defined in (
12
)).

Proof.

Let
C=(c_{i,j})
be a
n\times n
matrix. Then

(CA_{\mu})_{i,j}=\begin{cases}c_{i,j+1},\quad\text{if }j\neq n,\\
\mu c_{i,1},\quad\text{if }j=n,\end{cases},\quad(A_{\mu}C)_{i,j}=\begin{cases}c_{i-1,j},\quad\text{if }i\neq 1,\\
\mu c_{n,j},\quad\text{if }i=1.\end{cases}

Therefore
C
commutes with
A_{\mu}
if and only if

\begin{cases}c_{i,j+1}=c_{i-1,j},\,\text{ for every }i\neq 1,\,j\neq n,\\
\mu c_{i,1}=c_{i-1,n},\,\text{ for every }i\neq 1,\\
c_{1,j+1}=\mu c_{n,j},\,\text{ for every }j\neq n,\\
\mu c_{1,1}=\mu c_{n,n}.\end{cases}

Hence, the centralizer of
A_{\mu}
coincides with the set of the
\mu
-circulant matrices.
∎

Let
\Delta=(d_{i,j})
be the
n\times n
matrix defined by

d_{i,j}=\begin{cases}n-i,\,\text{ if }j=i+1\pmod{n},\\
0,\,\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

e.g. if
n=3
we have

\Delta=\begin{bmatrix}0&2&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}

Lemma 55
.

Let
C
be a
p\times p

\mu
-circulant matrix. Then
[C,\Delta]=0
if and only if
C=sI
for some
s\in K
.

Proof.

Let
C=\mu
-
Circ(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p})
. It is straightforward to compute that the first column of
\Delta\cdotp C
is
((p-1)c_{p},(p-2)c_{p-1},\ldots,c_{2},0)
and the first column
C\cdotp\Delta
is
(0,\ldots,0)
. Therefore if
[C,\Delta]=0
holds then
c_{2}=c_{3}=\ldots=c_{p}=0
and so
C=c_{1}I
.
∎

To identify the isomorphism classes of affine cycle sets of size
p^{p}
we are going to use the following criterion. The criterion is state for quasigroups and so it covers the case of finite latin cycle sets.

Theorem 56

(Drápal
[
24
, Thm. 3.2]
)
.

Let
Q=\mathrm{Aff}(G,\phi,\psi,c)
and
Q^{\prime}=\mathrm{Aff}(G,\phi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime},c^{\prime})
be affine quasigroups. Then
Q
is isomorphic to
Q^{\prime}
if and only if there are
\alpha\in\Aut{(G,+)}
and
u\in\mathrm{Im}(1-\phi-\psi)
such that
\phi^{\prime}=\alpha\phi\alpha^{-1}
,
\psi^{\prime}=\alpha\psi\alpha^{-1}
and
c^{\prime}=\alpha(c+u)
.

Now we are ready for the main result.

Theorem 57
.

Let
p
be a prime and
X
be an affine simple latin cycle set of size
p^{p}
. Then
X
is isomorphic to one of the following latin cycle sets:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{p},(\lambda I+\Delta)^{-1},\mathcal{A}_{\mu},(0,\ldots,0)),\quad\quad\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{p},(\lambda I+\Delta)^{-1},\mathcal{A}_{1},(1,0,\ldots,0)).

for
\mu,\lambda=1,\ldots,p-1
. In particular, there are
p(p-1)
affine simple latin cycle sets of size
p^{p}
.

Proof.

According to
Theorem

50
and
Proposition

53
, we have that
X
, which we indicate by
X(\lambda,\mu,c)
for its dependence on the three parameters, is given by
X(\lambda,\mu,c)=\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{p},(\lambda I+\Delta)^{-1},\mathcal{A}_{\mu},c)
. If
X(\lambda,\mu,c)
and
X(\lambda^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},c^{\prime})
are isomorphic then the matrices
A_{\mu}
and
A_{\mu^{\prime}}
(resp.
\mu I+\Delta
and
\mu^{\prime}I+\Delta
) are conjugate according to
Theorem

56
. Therefore such matrices have the same eigenvalues, i.e.
\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}
(resp.
\mu=\mu^{\prime}
). By this fact and
Theorem

56
,
X(\lambda,\mu,c)
and
X(\lambda^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},c^{\prime})
are isomorphic if and only if and only if
\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}
,
\mu=\mu^{\prime}
and there exists
\alpha\in GL_{p}(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})
and
u\in\mathrm{Im}(1-(\lambda I+\Delta)^{-1}-\mathcal{A}_{\mu})
such that
\mu I+\Delta=\alpha(\mu I+\Delta)\alpha^{-1}
,
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}=\alpha\mathcal{A}_{\mu}\alpha^{-1}
and
c^{\prime}=\alpha(c+u)
. By virtue of

then
\alpha=sI
with

. Therefore

c^{\prime}-sc=su\in\mathrm{Im}(1-(\lambda I+\Delta)^{-1}-\mathcal{A}_{\mu}),

i.e.

and

are generate the same subspace modulo
\mathrm{Im}(1-(\lambda I+\Delta)^{-1}-\mathcal{A}_{\mu})
. Thus, we need to understand the dimension of
\mathrm{Im}(1-(\lambda I+\Delta)^{-1}-\mathcal{A}_{\mu})
in order to account how many different isomorphism classes we have.
The dimension of
\mathrm{Im}(1-(\lambda I+\Delta)^{-1}-\mathcal{A}_{\mu})
equals the dimension of

\mathrm{Im}(\lambda I+\Delta)(1-(\lambda I+\Delta)^{-1}-\mathcal{A}_{\mu})=\mathrm{Im}((\lambda I+\Delta)(1-\mathcal{A}_{\mu})-1).

Now, we have that

can be written as

\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&p-1&0&0&0&\ldots&-\lambda\mu\\
-\lambda&\lambda+1&p-2&0&0&\ldots&0\\
0&-\lambda&\lambda+2&p-3&0&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&0&-\lambda&\lambda+p-3&2&0\\
0&\ldots&0&0&-\lambda&\lambda+p-2&1\\
0&&\ldots&0&0&-\lambda&\lambda+p-1\end{bmatrix}.

By a standard linear algebra argument, we have that the rank of the matrix above is
p
if
\mu\neq 1
and
p-1
if
\mu=1
. Thus, in the first case the rank of
\mathrm{Im}(1-(\lambda I+\Delta)^{-1}-A_{\mu})
is
p
and hence two arbitrary constant
c,c^{\prime}
provide isomorphic solutions. Now, let
\mu=1
and so the rank of
\mathrm{Im}((\lambda I+\Delta)(1-\mathcal{A}_{1})-1)
is
p-1
. By a standard linear algebra argument, we have that
\mathrm{Im}((\lambda I+\Delta)(1-\mathcal{A}_{1})-1)
is the subspace given by the vectors
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{p})
with
x_{1}+...+x_{p}=0
and therefore the vector
(1,0,\ldots,0)
does not belong to this space. Moreover, the vector
(1,0,\ldots,0)
does not belong to
\mathrm{Im}(1-(\lambda I+\Delta)^{-1}-\mathcal{A}_{\mu})
, because otherwise, being
(1,0,\ldots,0)
an eigenvector of
(\lambda I+\Delta)
and
\lambda\neq 0
, it would imply that
(1,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathrm{Im}((\lambda I+\Delta)(1-(\lambda I+\Delta)^{-1}-\mathcal{A}_{\mu}))
, a contradiction. By a standard calculation, we obtain that there are two isomorphism classes of simple cycle sets of the form
X(\lambda,1,c)
, one is given by
c:=(0,\ldots,0)
and the other one is given by
c:=(1,0,\ldots,0)
. Hence we obtain
(p-1)\cdotp(p-2)
simple affine cycle sets for
\mu\neq 1
and
2\cdotp(p-1)
for
\mu=1
, therefore the statement follows.
∎

7
Applications and examples

In this section, we collect examples of simple latin cycle sets obtained by our results and we discuss some applications to open questions present in literature.

We start by a non-existence result that extend
[
8
, Lemma 4.13]
. In particular, we show that we have no latin cycle sets with cyclic displacement group.

Theorem 58
.

Let
X
be a finite latin cycle set with cyclic displacement group. Then,
|X|=1
.

Proof.

Let
n:=|X|
and suppose that
\mathrm{Dis}(X)
is a cyclic group. At first, suppose that
n
has an odd prime divisor
p
and let
m
be the positive integer such that
n/m=p
. As in
Theorem

42
, the set
H:=p\mathrm{Dis}(X)
is an ideal of
\mathrm{Dis}(X)
of size
m
and is a characteristic subgroup of
(\mathrm{Dis}(X),+)
and
(\mathrm{Dis}(X),\circ)
, hence it is an ideal of
\mathcal{G}(X)
. Therefore,
H
induces a congruence
\sim
on
X
such that
|X/\sim|=p
, and this implies that there exist a latin cycle set of size
p
, but this contradicts
[
26
, Theorem 2.13]
. Now, we consider the case
n=2^{t}
for some natural number
t
. The case
t=1
is not possible by
[
26
, Theorem 2.13]
. The case
t=2
is excluded by an inspection of small cycle sets. The case
t>2
can be showed as in the first part by
[
4
, Proposition 5.4]
.
∎

By
[
23
]
every indecomposable and irretractable cycle set of size
p^{2}
is simple. A complete description of the latin ones follows by
[
23
, Theorem
5.1
]
, as we show in the following result.

Proposition 59
.

Let
X
be a latin cycle set of size
p^{2}
. Then,
X
is one of the two indecomposable and irretractable cycle sets of order
4
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

3
,
X
must be indecomposable and irretractable. If
p=2
, we know by
[
8
]
that the two indecomposable and irretractable cycle sets of order
4
are latin. If
p>2
, by
[
23
, Theorem
5.1
]

X
is given by
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
and
(a,x)\cdotp(b,y)=(\alpha b+\alpha x,\alpha y+\alpha\phi(b-a))
, where
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
\phi
is a map from
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
to
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
with
\phi(x)=\phi(-x)
and
\phi(\alpha x)=\alpha\phi(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
.
Let
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
with
a\neq b
and set
a^{\prime}:=2b-a
. Since
p>2
we have
a\neq a^{\prime}
, but
(a,x)\cdotp(b,y)=(a^{\prime},x)\cdotp(b,y)
, a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 60
.

Let
X
be a latin cycle set of size
p^{3}
. Then,
X
is simple.

Proof.

If
p=2
, one can show by computer calculation that there are no latin cycle sets of size
8
. If
p>2
and
X
is not simple, then we obtain a latin cycle set having size in
\{p,p^{2}\}
, but this contradicts
[
26
, Theorem 2.13]
and
Proposition

59
.
∎

In the following example, we apply
Theorem

44
to show the simplicity of a right linear cycle set.

Example 61
.

Using the transitive cycle bases of the left brace
SmallBraces(81,804)
of
[
43
]
, we obtain an irretractable cycle set
X
of size
27
having the displacement group, isomorphic to the extra-special
3
-group of size
27
, that acts transitively, and hence regularly, on
X
. By
Corollary

33
, this cycle set is right linear over
\mathrm{Dis}(X)
. By a long but standard calculation (helping us by GAP), one can write
X
as a cycle set on a group
(G,\circ)
isomorphic to
\mathrm{Dis}(X)
, where
G:=\{1,...,27\}
with
1
as neutral element, given by
x\cdotp y:=\phi(x)\circ\psi(y)
, where

\phi:=(1,12,27,13,18,10,11,20,17,9,19,21,22,16,26,5,15,2,4,7,23,3,25,6,14,24)

and

\psi:=(2,25,16)(3,14,13)(4,8,27)(5,23,21)(6,19,18)(7,26,22)(9,20,24)(10,11,17).

The unique
\psi
-invariant subgroups are
H_{1}:=\{1,12,15\}
and
H_{2}:=\{1,5,9,12,15,20,21,23,24\}
. On the other hand,
\phi(1)=12
and
\phi(15)=2
, hence
H_{1}
and
H_{2}

In
[
32
]
, the following question was posed.

Question 1
.

(Question
3.16
of
[
32
]
)
Let
X
be a cycle set. Is it true that if some
\sigma_{x}
contains a non-trivial cycle coprime with
|X|
, then
X
is decomposable?

For some classes of cycle sets, the answer to the previous question is positive (see
[
15
,
30
]
). In
[
29
]
, Kanrar and Rump found a family of counterexamples: they are non-simple cycle sets of
2-
power size and the smaller one has size
256
. Below, we show that the construction of smaller counterexamples that are also simple cycle sets is possible. At first, we need a lemma implicitly contained in
[
39
, Section 5]
.

Lemma 62
.

Let
X
be an affine cycle set given by
x\cdotp y:=\phi(x)+\psi(y)
for all
x,y\in X
. Then,
x\cdotp y=y
if and only if
\phi(x)=(I-\psi)y
. If
(I-\psi)
is invertible, this is equivalent to
(I-\psi)^{-1}\phi(x)=y
and in this case every
\sigma_{x}
has only one fixed point.

Example 63
.

Let
p
be a prime number, with
p>2
, and
X
be the simple affine cycle set of size
p^{p}
provided by
Theorem

57
with
\lambda=1
,
\mu\neq 1
, and
c=(0,\ldots,0).

Since the determinant of
I-B
is
1-\mu
, by
Lemma

62
every left multiplication of
X
has only one fixed point. Let
x
be an element of
X
. If every non-trivial cycle of
\sigma_{x}
is not coprime with
p
, and hence divisible by
p
, we obtain that number of the fixed points of
\sigma_{x}
is divisible by
p
, a contradiction. Therefore,
X
is a counterexample to
Question

1
.

In
[
19
]
, the following question was posed.

Question 2
.

(Question
7.6
of
[
19
]
)
Does there exist a finite simple cycle set
X
such that
X=Y\times Z
, the sets
X_{y}=\{(y,z)\mid z\in Z\}
, for
y\in Y
, are blocks of imprimitivity for the
action of
\mathcal{G}(X)
on
X
, and
|Z|
is not a divisor of
|Y|
?

Below, we provide a family of examples that answer positively to the previous question.

Example 64
.

Let
p
be a prime number, with
p>2
, and
X
be the simple affine cycle set of size
p^{p}
obtained by
Theorem

57
with
\lambda=\mu=1
and
c:=(0,\ldots,0)
. Since
\psi
has
-(x-1)^{p}
as characteristic polynomial, the Jordan canonical form of
\psi
exists in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
and is an upper triangular matrix. Let
\mathcal{B}:=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{p}\}
the basis of
X
that realizes the required form. Then, the subspace
H
generated by the set
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{p-1}\}
is
\psi
-invariant and has dimension
p-1
, moreover
X=H\oplus<e_{p}>
and it correspond bijectively to the set

given by

, where the correspondence is given by

for all

,

. By

34
,
X
has a complete blocks system
\{X_{y}\}_{y\in Y}
, where
X_{y}:=\{(y,z)\mid z\in Z\}
, for
y\in Y
, has size

, and with

.
