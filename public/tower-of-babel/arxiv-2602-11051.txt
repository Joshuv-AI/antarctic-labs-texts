How fast does the range of simple random walk grow?
Itai Benjamini, Justin Salez

Abstract
Consider a discrete-time simple random walk ( X t ) t ≥ 0 (X_{t})_{t\geq 0} on an infinite, connected, locally finite graph G G . Let R t := | { X 0 , … , X t } | R_{t}:=|\{X_{0},\dots,X_{t}\}| denote its range at time t t , and T n := inf { t ≥ 0 : R t = n } T_{n}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:R_{t}=n\} the n − n- th discovery time. We establish a general estimate on 𝔼 ⁡ [ T n ] \mathbb{E}[T_{n}] in terms of two coarse geometric parameters of G G , and deduce the universal bounds 𝔼 ⁡ [ T n ] ≤ 4 ​ n 3 ​ log ⁡ n \mathbb{E}[T_{n}]\leq 4n^{3}\log n and 𝔼 ⁡ [ R t ] ≳ ( t / log ⁡ t ) 1 / 3 \mathbb{E}[R_{t}]\gtrsim(t/\log t)^{1/3} . Moreover, we show that this is essentially sharp by constructing a multi-scale version of Feige’s Lollipop graph satisfying 𝔼 ⁡ [ T n ] ≳ n 3 \mathbb{E}[T_{n}]\gtrsim n^{3} for all dyadic integers n n . In light of this example, we ask whether the existence of trapping phases where the range grows sub-diffusively necessarily implies the existence of expanding phases where it grows super-diffusively. Finally, we provide a simple uniform transience condition under which the expected range grows linearly, and conjecture that all vertex-nonamenable graphs exhibit linear range.



1
Setup and main estimate

Consider an infinite, connected, locally finite graph
G=(V,E)
, and let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a simple random walk (SRW) on
G
, i.e. a discrete-time Markov chain on
V
with transition matrix

\displaystyle P(x,y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\deg(x)}{\bf 1}_{\{x,y\}\in E}.

The
range
of the walk at time
t\geq 0
is the number of distinct visited vertices:

\displaystyle R_{t}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle|\{X_{0},\dots,X_{t}\}|.

The rate at which
t\mapsto\mathbb{E}[R_{t}]
grows is a natural gauge for an array of geometric features of the underlying graph, including volume growth, return probabilities, isoperimetry, etc. The starting point of the present paper is the following natural question.

Question 1

(Universal growth rate)
.

What is the best universal guarantee that can be given on the growth of
t\mapsto\mathbb{E}[R_{t}]
, without any structural assumption on the underlying graph
G
?

We provide a general lower bound of order
(t/\log t)^{1/3}
and show that this is essentially sharp. Most of our analysis is in fact performed on the inverse process
(T_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, where

\displaystyle T_{n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\inf\{t\geq 0:R_{t}=n\},

denotes the
n-
th discovery time. Specifically, our main technical contribution is the following upper bound on
\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]
, which only features two coarse geometric parameters: the
maximal edge-density

f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and the
minimal volume growth

g\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, respectively defined as

\displaystyle f(n)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq V\\
|S|=n\end{subarray}}|E_{S}|;

\displaystyle g(n)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\min_{x\in V}|B(x,n)|.

Here,
E_{S}
denotes the set of edges with both endpoints in
S
,
B(x,n):=\{y\in V:\mathrm{dist}(x,y)\leq n\}
the ball of radius
n
around
x
, and
\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
the usual graph distance on
V
.

Theorem 1

(Main estimate)
.

For every
n\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4nf(n)\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{g(r)}.

The strength of this estimate lies in the fact that it only uses rudimentary volume information on the underlying graph (as opposed to, e.g., Proposition
2
below, which involves the more delicate return probabilities). This makes it rather loose in homogeneous situations such as
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where much more structure is available, but surprisingly sharp in less regular geometries, where no uniform control on return probabilities can be expected. In particular, it provides a near-optimal answer to Question
1
. Specifically, the worst-case bounds
f(n)\leq\binom{n}{2}
and
g(n)\geq n+1
, together with the estimate
1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n}\leq 2\log n
for all

n\geq 3
(plus
T_{1}=0
and
T_{2}=1
) yield the following universal estimate.

Corollary 1

(Universal estimate on discovery times)
.

For every
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4n^{3}\log n.

Proposition
1
below will show that this is optimal, save for the logarithmic term. Our upper bound on expected discovery times is easily converted into a lower bound on the expected range, allowing us to provide an almost optimal answer to Question
1
.

Corollary 2

(Universal range estimate)
.

There is a universal
c>0
such that for all
t\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R_{t}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c\left(\frac{t}{\log t}\right)^{1/3}.

Proof.

Fix
t\geq 2
and
n\geq 1
. Using the obvious identity
\{R_{t}<n\}=\{T_{n}\geq t+1\}
, Markov’s inequality and the above corollary, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{t}<n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4n^{3}\log n}{t+1}.

Now, one can make the right-hand side less than
1/2
by choosing

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\lfloor\left(\frac{t}{C\log t}\right)^{1/3}\right\rfloor,

with
C
a large absolute constant. We then have
\mathbb{E}[R_{t}]\geq n\mathbb{P}(R_{t}\geq n)\geq n/2
, as desired.
∎

Embarrassingly, we could not exhibit an example showing that the logarithmic correction in Corollary
1
is really necessary. Note that the latter comes from the worst-case estimate
g(r)\asymp r
in Theorem
1
, which is far too pessimistic on many natural examples of graphs. For instance, as soon as
g(r)=\Omega(r^{1+\delta})
for some
\delta>0
, we have
\sum_{r\geq 0}1/g(r)<\infty
, so that
\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]=O(n^{3})
and hence
\mathbb{E}[R_{t}]=\Omega(t^{1/3})
. Whether the same conclusion holds without any assumption on
G
remains an interesting open problem.

Proof of Theorem
1

We use the standard notation
\tau_{A}:=\min\{t\geq 0:X_{t}\in A\}
for the hitting time of a subset

A\subseteq V
, and
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\cdot]
for the expectation of the walk started from
X_{0}=x
.

2.1
Escape time from a finite set

Our first task consists in deriving a general estimate on the expected time needed to escape a given finite set. To this end, the following simple observation will be useful.

Lemma 1

(Time to hit a neighbor)
.

On a finite connected graph
H=(W,F)
, we have

\displaystyle\max_{\{x,y\}\in F}\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{y}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2|F|-1.

Proof.

Fix
\{x,y\}\in F
. The return time
\tau_{y}^{+}:=\min\{t\geq 1\colon X_{t}=y\}
classically satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tau_{y}^{+}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2|F|}{\deg(y)}.

On the other hand, the Markov property at time
1
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y}[\tau_{y}^{+}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{\deg(y)}\sum_{z\sim y}\mathbb{E}_{z}[\tau_{y}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{y}]}{\deg(y)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1+\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{y}]}{\deg(y)}.

Combining those two estimates readily yields the claimed bound.
∎

Lemma 2

(Escape time from a finite set)
.

For any finite set
S\subseteq V
and any
x\in S
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{S^{c}}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(2|E_{S}|+1)\,\mathrm{dist}(x,S^{c}).

Proof.

Let
(x_{0},\dots,x_{r})
be a shortest path from
x_{0}=x
to
S^{c}
, so that
r=\mathrm{dist}(x,S^{c})
and

x_{1},\dots,x_{r-1}\in S
,
x_{r}\notin S
. Let
G_{S}
be the subgraph induced by
S
, and let

H
be the graph obtained from
G_{S}
by adding the vertex
x_{r}
and the edge
\{x_{r-1},x_{r}\}
.
Now, we can couple SRW on
G
with SRW on
H
in the obvious way, so that they coincide until the former exits
H
. With obvious notation, this coupling implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{S^{c}}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}^{H}[\tau_{S^{c}}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{E}_{x_{i-1}}^{H}[\tau_{x_{i}}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(2|E_{S}|+1)r,

where the last step uses Lemma
1
and the fact that
H
has
|E_{S}|+1
edges.
∎

2.2
From escape times to discovery times

Fix
n\geq 1
and let
S_{n}:=\{X_{T_{1}},\dots,X_{T_{n}}\}
denote the set formed by the first
n
visited vertices.
Using the strong Markov property at time
T_{n}
, and Lemma
2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{n+1}-T_{n}\mid X_{0},\dots,X_{T_{n}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{T_{n}}}[\tau_{S_{n}^{c}}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(2|E_{S_{n}}|+1)\,\mathrm{dist}(X_{T_{n}},S_{n}^{c}).

Now, by definition of
f
, we have
|E_{S_{n}}|\leq f(n)
and
2f(n)+1\leq 2f(n+1)
, so that for
n\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[T_{k+1}-T_{k}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2f(n)\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathrm{dist}(X_{T_{k}},S_{k}^{c})\right].

Theorem
1
therefore follows from the deterministic bound below.

Lemma 3

(A deterministic packing bound)
.

For every
n\geq 1
, almost surely,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathrm{dist}(X_{T_{k}},S_{k}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2n\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}\frac{1}{g(r)}.

Proof.

Fix an integer
r\geq 0
and define

\displaystyle I(r)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{k\in\{1,\dots,n\}:\mathrm{dist}(X_{T_{k}},S_{k}^{c})>r\}.

If
k\in I(2r)
and
j>k
, then
\mathrm{dist}(X_{T_{k}},X_{T_{j}})>2r
, hence the balls

\{B(X_{T_{k}},r)\}_{k\in I(2r)}
are pairwise disjoint. Since those balls are contained in

S_{n}
and
|S_{n}|=n
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in I(2r)}|B(X_{T_{k}},r)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n.

In view of the definition of
g
, this implies

\displaystyle|I(2r)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{n}{g(r)}.

Now, for each
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(X_{T_{k}},S_{k}^{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{k\in I(r)\}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}\big(\mathbf{1}_{\{k\in I(2r)\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\{k\in I(2r+1)\}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{k\in I(2r)\}}.

Summing over
k
and using
|I(2r)|\leq n/g(r)
gives the claim.
∎

Combining Lemma
3
with the previous display concludes the proof of
Theorem
1
.

3
Extremal examples and a question on oscillations

To investigate the sharpness of Corollary
1
, we need to exhibit infinite connected graphs in which the discovery of new vertices is as slow as possible. The roles played by the parameters
f
and
g
in our main estimate provide an excellent indication as to what such trapping geometries should look like: in order for
n\mapsto\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]
to be large, we must simultaneously have
f
growing as quickly as possible (imposing the presence of high-connectivity regions) and
g
growing as slowly as possible (imposing the presence of narrow bottlenecks). An emblematic finite example is Feige’s
Lollipop graph
, obtained by merging a clique and a path as follows:

The lollipop graph

.

Graphs of this type are well known to exhibit worst-case hitting, cover and commute times
[

,
3

4
]

\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]\leq 4n^{3}\log n
in Corollary
1
is optimal, save for the logarithm.

Proposition 1

(Sharpness)
.

There is a universal
c>0
such that the following holds:

1.

For each
n\geq 2
, there is an infinite connected graph

G_{n}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle cn^{3}.

(1)

2.

There is an infinite connected graph
G
satisfying (
1
) for every dyadic
n\in\{2,4,8,\ldots\}
.

Proof.

For any integer
n\geq 2
, let
L_{n}
denote the Lollipop graph of order
n
obtained by attaching a path of order
\lceil n/2\rceil
to a clique of order
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, as illustrated on Figure
1
. Declare the
origin
of
L_{n}
to be any internal vertex in the clique, and its
end
to be the degree-one vertex at the opposite side of the path. Starting at the origin, the expected time needed to reach the end is well known (and easily seen) to be at least
cn^{3}
, for some absolute constant
c>0
. Now, let us turn
L_{n}
into an infinite graph
G_{n}
by simply extending the finite path into an infinite one. Clearly, a SRW started at the origin can not visit
n
distinct vertices without hitting the end of
L_{n}
, so (
1
) holds. Building upon this idea, we now construct a
single
infinite graph
G
satisfying (
1
) for infinitely many values of
n
simultaneously as follows: take infinitely many disjoint Lollipop graphs
L_{n_{1}},L_{n_{2}},\ldots
of respective sizes
n_{1},n_{2},\ldots
, and add an edge between the end of
L_{n_{i}}
and the origin of
L_{n_{i+1}}
for each
i\leq 1
. By the same argument as above, a SRW on
G
started at the origin of
L_{n_{1}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c\left(n_{1}^{3}+\cdots+n_{k}^{3}\right),

for all
k\geq 1
. In particular, choosing
n_{i}=2^{i}
produces a graph that satisfies (
1
) simultaneously at all dyadic integer scales
n
(with a slightly smaller, but still universal
c>0
).
∎

Interestingly, the growth of the function
t\mapsto R_{t}
on our multi-scale Lollipop graph
G
is far from homogeneous: it will typically experience a near-linear
burst
whenever the walk enters a fresh new clique, followed by a very long
plateau
during which the walk attempts to traverse the path leading to the next clique. Whether this oscillating picture is unavoidable is a natural question, which we could not answer. Here is a precise formulation.

Question 3

(Are oscillations unavoidable?)
.

Fix an infinite connected graph
G
, and define

\displaystyle\alpha\ :=\ \liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}[R_{t}]}{\log t},

and

\displaystyle\beta\ :=\ \limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}[R_{t}]}{\log t}.

If
\alpha<1/2
(sub-diffusive range along certain time-scales), must it then necessarily be the case that
\beta>1/2
(super-diffusive range along other time-scales)? If yes, is there a universal quantitative relation between
\alpha
and
\beta
? In particular, does
\alpha=1/3
imply
\beta=1
?

4
Faster range growth, and a conjecture

In this final section, we show that appropriate assumptions on the underlying graph can lead to much faster range growth than our worst-case
\Omega(t^{1/3})
scenario. Specifically, let

\displaystyle\ell_{x}(t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{t}\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{s}=x),

denote the expected local time at
x\in V
at time
t\geq 0
.

Proposition 2

(Range estimate using maximum local times)
.

For all
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R_{t}]\ \geq\ \frac{t+1}{\ell_{\star}(t)},

where

\displaystyle\ell_{\star}(t)\ :=\ \sup_{x\in V}\ell_{x}(t).

Proof.

Fix
t\geq 0
and
x\in V
. Using the Markov property and the definition of
\ell_{\star}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{s=0}^{t}{\bf 1}_{\{X_{s}=x\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau_{x}\leq t)\ell_{\star}(t).

Summing over all
x\in V
yields
t+1\leq\mathbb{E}[R_{t}]\ell_{\star}(t)
, which is the desired estimate.
∎

It is important to realize that, unlike our main estimate, Proposition
2
can
not
lead to a
universal
growth guarantee on the range. Indeed, for any given scale
t\geq 0
, one can construct an infinite connected graph
G_{t}
such that
\ell_{\star}(t)\geq t/2
, in which case Proposition
2
produces the useless estimate
\mathbb{E}[R_{t}]\geq 2
(consider a large enough star to which an infinite one-ended path is attached). However, Proposition
2
becomes very useful when restricted to particular graph ensembles where local times can be controlled. For example, the classical return-probability estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}=x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4\deg(x)}{\sqrt{t+1}},

(2)

see (
[
5
, Lemma 3.6]
and the remark after it) readily implies the diffusive range estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R_{t}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{t+1}}{8\Delta},

on any graph with degrees at most
\Delta
, whereas our main estimate would have led to an additional logarithmic correction here. Similarly, any graph which is
uniformly transient
, in the sense that its return probabilities are uniformly summable, will exhibit linear range:

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0,\qquad\mathbb{E}[R_{t}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle ct,

(3)

where
c=1/\ell_{\star}(\infty)
. This holds on
\mathbb{Z}^{d},d\geq 3
for example, but also on any bounded-degree
non-amenable
graph. More precisely, define the
outer vertex boundary
of
S\subset V
as

\displaystyle\partial_{V}S

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{x\in V\setminus S:\ \exists\,y\in S\text{ with }x\sim y\},

and recall that
G
is
vertex-nonamenable
if

\displaystyle\inf_{0<|S|<\infty}\frac{|\partial_{V}S|}{|S|}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 0.

(4)

On bounded-degree graphs, this isoperimetric condition is classically equivalent to the existence of a spectral gap for the random walk operator, which in turn implies uniform exponential decay of return probabilities, hence linear range. In the presence of unbounded degrees however, the relation between isoperimetry and uniform transience seems to be more delicate. Nevertheless, we conjecture that (
4
) still suffices to guarantee linear range.

Conjecture 1

(Vertex-nonamenability implies linear range)
.

If
G
is vertex-nonamenable, then (
3
) holds for some
c=c(G)>0
.
