Risk Prediction of Early‐Onset Hepatocellular Carcinoma: Derivation and Validation in a Nationwide Young Adult Cohort
Authors: Seogsong Jeong, Gi‐Ae Kim, Heejoon Jang, Dong Hyeon Lee, Sae Kyung Joo, Keon Wook Kang, Hwamin Lee, Won Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe global incidence of early‐onset hepatocellular carcinoma (eHCC) is increasing significantly; however, specific risk prediction tools for young adults remain scarce. This study aimed to develop and validate predictive models for eHCC using both conventional statistical and machine learning techniques.MethodsWe included 1,756,593 young adults aged 20–39 years who underwent health screenings in South Korea between 2013 and 2014 with follow‐up until January 31, 2022. Participants were randomly divided into training and validation sets (1:1 ratio). Prediction models were constructed using cause‐specific Cox proportional hazards regression, Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazards regression, generalized boosted model (GBM), and generalized boosted survival model (GBM‐survival).ResultsKey predictors of eHCC in the training cohort included viral hepatitis, history of non‐HCC cancer, liver cirrhosis, and gamma‐glutamyl transferase. In the validation cohort, GBM‐survival demonstrated best predictive performance for eHCC in young adults, with area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (auROCs) of 0.945 (1‐year) and 0.825 (5‐year). The most significant features in GBM‐survival were aspartate aminotransferase level, history of non‐HCC cancer, viral hepatitis, gamma‐glutamyl transferase, and serum creatinine. A global surrogate model for the GBM‐survival model enhanced interpretability (RMSE = 0.287; Pearson r = 0.887). Using the K‐group method, GBM‐survival achieved risk ratios of 2092.3 and 166.2 for 1‐year and 5‐year eHCC prediction, respectively.ConclusionsWe developed and validated robust risk prediction models for eHCC that integrated established risk factors with emerging metabolic indicators. This study provides actionable tools for risk stratification in young adults to address the growing burden of eHCC.Keywords: early‐onset, hepatocellular carcinoma, liver cancer, machine learning, risk prediction

Introduction

The global incidence of early‐onset cancer has risen significantly in recent years, with particularly notable increases in gastrointestinal malignancies [1, 2, 3, 4]. Although early‐onset cancers have lower incidence and mortality rates compared to older populations, their economic and social impacts are disproportionately high due to the substantial loss of productive life years [1]. Primary liver cancer ranks as the sixth most common malignancy worldwide and represents the third leading cause of cancer‐related mortality [5]. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), which accounts for over 80% of liver cancer cases, presents a significant clinical challenge, especially when occurring at a young age.

Several HCC risk prediction models have been developed and validated, primarily focusing on older populations and specific etiologies, such as hepatitis B or C virus (HBV/HCV), metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), and alcohol‐related liver disease (ALD) [6]. These models often include age as a broad variable without specifically addressing the unique characteristics and risk profiles of younger individuals. Established risk factors for HCC include chronic viral infections, MASLD, cirrhosis, obesity, alcohol consumption, and type 2 diabetes [6]; however, data on risk factors specific to early‐onset HCC (eHCC) remain notably limited [1].

Recent epidemiological shifts have witnessed a transition in HCC aetiology from predominantly viral causes to increasing contributions from non‐viral factors such as MASLD and ALD [5, 7, 8]. This transition is particularly relevant in younger populations, where non‐viral factors may play a more prominent role because of the relatively shorter exposure to viral infections compared to older individuals [4, 9]. Emerging evidence suggests that eHCC may exhibit distinct etiological mechanisms compared to late‐onset cases, including a lower prevalence of underlying cirrhosis [1, 10, 11], which further underscores the need for age‐specific risk assessment approaches.

In this nationwide study, we aimed to develop and validate risk prediction models for eHCC among individuals aged 20–39 years using comprehensive health screening data and medical history. These models were intended not for universal population screening, but rather for risk stratification among young adults undergoing health screening to identify those with possible undiagnosed HCC or elevated future risk who may benefit from targeted liver evaluation and follow‐up.

Methods

Study Population

We included young adults aged 20–39 years who underwent at least one health screening examination between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2014, as identified in the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) database (NHIS‐2023‐1‐588, NHIS‐2024‐10‐1‐025). In South Korea, participation in biannual health checkups is mandatory for individuals in this age group, whether insured through employment or self‐employment [12], ensuring broad population coverage and minimizing selection bias. Details regarding ethical approval and the NHIS database are provided in Supplementary methods (Appendix S1).

Outcomes

Hepatic steatosis and related conditions, such as MASLD, were assessed using the fatty liver index (≥ 30) [13]. The primary outcome was incident eHCC, defined as a diagnosis with ICD‐10 code C220, confirmed radiologically or pathologically, and accompanied by a cancer‐specific critical condition code (V193, V194). This case definition has demonstrated a high accuracy (positive predictive value > 95%) in previous studies. Participants were followed up until January 31, 2022, or until HCC occurrence, death, or loss to follow‐up, whichever came first. All participants were monitored for up to 8 years. eHCC was operationally defined as HCC diagnosed before 50 years of age, consistent with the commonly used definition of early‐onset cancer and previous cancer studies [14].

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean (SD) for normally distributed data or median (IQR) for non‐normally distributed data. Four prediction models were developed: cause‐specific Cox proportional hazards regression (CoxPH), Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazards regression (FG) with all‐cause mortality as a competing event, generalized boosted model (GBM), and generalized boosted survival model (GBM‐survival). All variables were tested for collinearity using the variance inflation factor, with values above five indicating significant multicollinearity.

Descriptions of key variables and their definitions are provided in Supplementary methods (Appendix S1), including Table S1. Details of the derivation, validation, and evaluation of the prediction models, statistical software, and modelling implementations are provided in Supplementary methods (Appendix S1).

Results

Study Population

A total of 1,758,884 young adults aged 20–39 years underwent health screening examinations in South Korea between 2013 and 2014. After excluding participants with a history of HCC (n = 334) and those with missing covariate information (n = 1957), the final study population of 1,756,593 participants was randomly divided into training (n = 878,296) and validation (n = 878,297) cohorts (Figure 1).

Flow diagram for the study population. This figure illustrates the selection process for the study population. The final cohort was randomly divided into training and validation sets (1:1 ratio) after excluding individuals with a history of hepatocellular carcinoma and those with missing covariate data.

The mean age of the study population was 33.8 years, and 64.5% were male. Chronic viral hepatitis was present in 15,152 (1.7%) and 14,955 (1.7%) participants in the training and validation cohorts, respectively. The prevalence of other comorbidities was also similar between the cohorts, confirming successful randomization. Derivation of Prediction Models

The variance inflation factors for all variables ranged from 1.00 to 3.56, indicating no significant multicollinearity among the predictors (Table S3). Through stepwise selection, we identified seven and 11 significant variables for the CoxPH and FG models, respectively (Figure S1, Table S4).

The final CoxPH model included chronic viral hepatitis (hazard ratio [HR] 20.43, 95% confidence interval [CI] 17.42–23.97), history of non‐HCC cancer (HR 11.03, 95% CI 8.94–13.62), liver cirrhosis (HR 5.81, 95% CI 4.43–7.60), male sex (HR 1.92, 95% CI 1.59–2.31), age (HR 1.06 per year, 95% CI 1.04–1.08), gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT; HR 1.01 per IU/L, 95% CI 1.00–1.01), and triglyceride (HR 1.00 per mg/dL, 95% CI 1.00–1.00) (Figure 2A). The FG model additionally included Wilson's disease (subdistribution hazard ratio [SHR] 7.64, 95% CI 1.69–34.58), waist circumference (SHR, 1.00/cm; 95% CI, 1.00–1.01), and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (Figure 2B).

For the ML models, optimal performance was achieved with 60 trees, a depth of 5, a shrinkage of 0.1, and 1000 minimum observations per terminal node. Shapley additive explanations analysis identified the Charlson comorbidity index, history of non‐HCC cancer, chronic viral hepatitis, GGT, and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) as the top features for GBM, with serum creatinine replacing the Charlson comorbidity index in GBM‐survival (Figure S2).

Validation and Comparison of Prediction Models

All models demonstrated strong discrimination ability in the validation cohort, with GBM‐survival achieving the highest predictive performance. For 1‐year eHCC prediction, the area under receiver operating characteristic curves (auROCs) were 0.942 (95% CI 0.911–0.973) for CoxPH, 0.943 (0.912–0.973) for FG, 0.932 (0.896–0.968) for GBM, and 0.945 (0.915–0.975) for GBM‐survival (Figure 3A). In sensitivity analyses using a 1‐year delayed‐entry landmark approach and a 6‐month washout approach, the predictive performance of the models for 1‐year eHCC prediction was slightly attenuated (Table S5). For the 5‐year eHCC prediction, the auROCs were 0.807 (0.784–0.829) for CoxPH, 0.808 (0.785–0.830) for FG, 0.822 (0.800–0.844) for GBM, and 0.825 (0.804–0.847) for GBM‐survival (Figure 3B). At the end of follow‐up, the auROC for GBM‐survival was 0.798 (0.780–0.816), significantly outperforming CoxPH (contrast estimate, 0.014; 95% CI, 0.002–0.026; p = 0.021; Table S6). Monte Carlo subsampling analysis based on 100 iterations of randomly drawing 10% subsamples from the validation cohort and recalculating the auROC showed similar results (Table S7). Additional sensitivity analyses, including extended look‐back period model, bootstrap optimism correction, and temporal split‐sample validation are shown in Table S8.

Correlation analysis identified chronic viral hepatitis, liver enzyme levels, history of non‐HCC cancer, steatotic liver disease, and waist circumference as key predictors (Figure S3). Interestingly, correlation patterns varied across prediction timeframes, with triglycerides showing a positive correlation with GBM‐survival scores in the overall validation cohort but a negative correlation in individuals with HCC onset within 1 year.

The net reclassification index demonstrated that GBM‐survival improved risk classification compared to CoxPH, reclassifying 1.9% of individuals with eHCC into higher risk categories and 2.4% of individuals without eHCC into lower‐risk categories (Figure 4), confirming the enhanced discriminative ability of the ML approach.

Risk Stratification Based on Prediction Scores

In the validation cohort, restricted cubic spline analysis showed a progressive increase in HRs with higher prediction scores across all models (Figure S4), confirming the dose–response relationship between the predicted risk and observed outcomes. When stratified by quartiles of prediction scores, individuals in the highest quartile demonstrated a 7.87‐ to 13.73‐fold higher risk of eHCC than those in the lowest quartile (Figure S5).

The incidence rates of eHCC ranged from 3.1–4.0 per 100,000 person‐years in the lowest quartile to 31.0–32.5 per 100,000 person‐years in the highest quartile across models, representing an approximately 10‐fold increase in absolute risk. Detailed information on the number of individuals, events, person‐years, and crude and adjusted HRs across quartiles are provided in Table S9.

Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that GBM and GBM‐survival models more effectively differentiated risk across quartiles than the CoxPH and FG models (Figure S6). This superior stratification was particularly evident for the highest risk quartile, where the ML models identified a subset with markedly worse eHCC‐free survival.

In the subgroup analyses, the association between higher model scores and increased eHCC risk remained consistent across age groups (20–29 vs. 30–39 years), sex, and even among participants without established risk factors (Tables S10 and S11). In addition, further adjustment for the total number of hospital visit days within 1 year before follow‐up as a proxy for healthcare utilization yielded consistent results (Table S12). This consistent performance across diverse subpopulations supports the broad applicability of the developed model.

Calibration assessment showed similar performance between the CoxPH and GBM‐survival models, with good agreement between the predicted and observed risks (Figure S7). This indicated that both the traditional and ML approaches provided reliable risk estimates, although GBM‐survival offered superior discrimination.

Development and Validation of a Global Surrogate Model

For better interpretability, we built a global surrogate model that approximates GBM‐survival predictions using generalized linear regression. This surrogate model achieved an excellent approximation with a root mean square error of 0.287 and Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.887 (Table S13).

When stratified by quartiles, the global surrogate score showed significant risk gradients for eHCC with consistent performance in both the overall cohort and subgroups without major risk factors in the validation cohort (Table S14). Using the K‐group method, the surrogate model identified a high‐risk group with markedly elevated event rates compared with the low‐risk group (Table S15).

While the high‐risk group defined by the K‐group method had a relatively low absolute event rate (2.6%) due to the rare nature of eHCC, its 5‐year event rate (11.8%) was the highest among all prediction models, demonstrating the surrogate model's ability to identify individuals at substantially elevated risk.

The global surrogate model showed excellent performance in cumulative gain and lift plots, outperforming the original CoxPH and GBM‐survival models in some metrics (Figure S8). The recall values for the higher half were 0.808, 0.528, and 0.826 for CoxPH, GBM‐survival, and global surrogate, respectively (Table S16). Importantly, the event rates in the highest decile (450 and 493 per 100,000 persons for CoxPH and the global surrogate models, respectively) were substantially higher than those in the overall validation cohort (92 per 100,000 persons), highlighting the effectiveness of the models in concentrating events among the highest‐risk individuals. The cut‐off scores (sensitivity/specificity) of the global surrogate model to achieve target numbers needed to screen of 200 (0.533/0.902), 100 (0.453/0.959), and 50 (0.356/0.984) were 1.444, 1.928, and 3.779, respectively (Table S17). In addition, decision curve analysis showed a consistently positive but quantitatively small net benefit of the model across the clinically relevant low‐threshold range, compared with treat‐none strategy (Figure S9).

Discussion

This nationwide cohort study represents a significant advancement in eHCC risk prediction by developing and validating robust models specifically tailored to young adults aged 20–39 years. By employing both traditional statistical and advanced ML approaches, we identified the key predictors of eHCC and demonstrated the superior performance of the GBM‐survival model. This model achieved excellent predictive accuracy, with auROCs of 0.945, 0.825, and 0.798 for 1‐year, 5‐year, and overall eHCC prediction, respectively. Risk stratification revealed that individuals in the highest quartile had a markedly elevated risk of eHCC (7.87–13.73 fold), highlighting the potential of artificial intelligence (AI)‐based approaches for targeted prevention in this population.

The increasing incidence of eHCC among young adults represents an emerging public health concern globally [4]. While HCC has traditionally been considered a disease of older populations, recent epidemiological data indicate a troubling rise in early‐onset cases, particularly in regions with changing risk factor profiles [4]. A recent analysis of HCC incidence and mortality in the USA revealed significant demographic variations, with increasing rates among younger populations that had previously shown stable or declining trends [4, 15]. This shift highlights the urgency of developing age‐specific risk assessment approaches.

Most existing HCC prediction models have focused predominantly on older populations, with few addressing the unique characteristics and risk factors of eHCC in young adults [16]. Our findings align with a recent comprehensive genomic analysis of early‐versus late‐onset HCC, which demonstrated distinct molecular signatures and potentially different carcinogenic pathways in younger patients [17]. To our knowledge, our study is the first to specifically develop and validate prediction models for eHCC in individuals under 40 years of age using both conventional statistical and ML methodologies.

Our results indicate that ML models, particularly GBM‐survival, may be better than traditional approaches in discrimination. This performance likely reflects the capability of ML algorithms to capture complex, nonlinear relationships and interactions between variables that conventional regression models might miss. Similar advantages of ML have been demonstrated in other liver disease contexts, including MASLD‐associated HCC prediction [18, 19]. The consistent improvement in predictive performance across the various subgroups in our study further supports the robustness of this approach.

While traditional viral risk factors, particularly chronic HBV infection, remained significant predictors of eHCC with the highest hazard ratios in our models, our findings also highlight the growing importance of non‐viral risk factors. This reflects the global epidemiological transition in HCC aetiology from predominantly viral causes to increasing contributions from metabolic and lifestyle‐related factors [5, 7, 8]. A recent global analysis demonstrated that while HBV accounted for more than half of the HCC cases in 1990, this proportion decreased to 42% by 2019, with corresponding increases in alcohol‐related and metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis‐related cases [20]. This shift is particularly relevant for younger populations in regions with successful HBV vaccination programs and improved HCV treatments [4, 5]. Furthermore, in the current study, the prominence of metabolic indicators (GGT, creatinine) reflects the global shift toward non‐viral HCC etiologies [21].

The inclusion of both established and novel predictors further enhanced the predictive power, consistent with recent literature [22]. The identification of serum creatinine and history of non‐HCC cancer as significant predictors in our GBM‐survival model is noteworthy and provides novel insights into eHCC risk assessment. Elevated serum creatinine levels may reflect underlying renal dysfunction or broader metabolic disturbances, increasingly recognized as contributors to liver disease progression [23]. Recent evidence suggests that advanced fibrosis in MASLD is independently associated with reduced renal function, potentially explaining the predictive value of creatinine in our model [24]. Similarly, a history of non‐HCC cancer may indicate shared mechanisms, such as chronic inflammation, genetic predisposition, or treatment‐related factors contributing to hepatocarcinogenesis. These findings underscore the importance of considering nontraditional risk factors for comprehensive eHCC prediction. Given the multifactorial nature of HCC development, integrating various risk factors, such as viral infections, metabolic syndrome, and genetic predisposition, into a predictive model is vital.

A key contribution of our study is the development of an interpretable global surrogate model that closely approximates the complex GBM‐survival predictions. Interpretability remains a significant challenge for implementing ML models in clinical settings, as most use ‘black box’ approaches that provide limited insight into the causal relationship between inputs and outputs. Our surrogate model addresses this limitation by translating the sophisticated ML algorithm into a simple, equation‐based tool that retains excellent predictive performance, while improving transparency and clinical applicability.

The risk stratification achieved by our models has important clinical implications. The approximately 10‐fold higher incidence of eHCC in the highest‐risk quartile compared to the lowest‐risk quartile suggests that a substantial subset of young adults could benefit from enhanced surveillance and preventive interventions. Early identification of high‐risk individuals could enable more targeted and cost‐effective strategies such as individualized imaging protocols or aggressive management of modifiable risk factors. This approach is particularly valuable given the generally low incidence of HCC in young adults, which makes universal surveillance impractical and resource intensive.

Our finding that the models maintained predictability even among participants without established major risk factors is particularly valuable for identifying individuals who might otherwise have been overlooked by conventional risk factor‐based screening approaches. Recent studies have shown that up to 38% of HCCs can develop without cirrhosis in patients with nonalcoholic steatohepatitis [25], highlighting the need for risk assessment strategies that extend beyond traditional paradigms. Our models incorporated multiple clinical and laboratory parameters that collectively capture risks beyond the individual established factors. The development of novel biomarkers for early HCC detection complements our risk prediction approach. Recent advances in circulating tumour DNA, microRNAs, and metabolomic markers have shown promise for enhancing early detection capabilities [26]. Integrating our risk prediction models with these emerging biomarkers could potentially create a two‐step approach: first identifying high‐risk individuals using readily available clinical data and then employing specialized biomarkers for more intensive surveillance in this enriched population. This approach can optimize resource utilization while maximizing early detection opportunities.

Our study has several strengths. First, we utilized a large, nationwide cohort with comprehensive health screening data, ensuring robust statistical power and generalizability within the Korean population. The inclusion of over 1.7 million young adults with up to 8 years of follow‐up represents one of the largest studies on eHCC risk prediction. Second, the integration of ML techniques with traditional statistical methods allows us to address the limitations of conventional approaches. Third, the development of an interpretable surrogate model enhances the clinical applicability of our findings, providing clinicians with a practical tool for risk assessment.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, our study population consisted of Korean individuals, potentially limiting the generalizability to other populations with different genetic backgrounds and risk factor profiles. Future studies should validate our models in diverse populations to confirm their broad applicability. Second, advanced biomarkers, such as liver stiffness measurements, genetic markers, and novel serum indicators, were unavailable in our dataset. Incorporating such markers could potentially further enhance the predictive accuracy. Third, as with any retrospective observational study, residual confounding factors cannot be entirely eliminated despite rigorous adjustments. Fourth, the relatively low incidence of eHCC in young adults presents challenges for model precision, necessitating a strategy that prioritizes sensitivity over the positive predictive value. The high 1‐year auROC should therefore be interpreted with caution, as discrimination may appear favourable in rare‐event settings despite limited absolute risk prediction. Although the overall cohort size was large, the effective sample size for model development was constrained by the relatively small number of incident events, particularly for the 1‐year prediction model. Accordingly, the apparent performance may overstate model stability. Future research directions include validation of the models in hospital‐based cohorts, and scores for the GBM‐survival and global surrogate models can be calculated using the Appendix S1 (gbm_surv_score.html).

In conclusion, we developed and validated robust prediction models specifically for young adults, with the GBM‐survival model demonstrating a superior performance. The global surrogate model provides an interpretable alternative that maintains excellent predictive accuracy while enhancing clinical applicability. Further research to refine these models and tailor them to the unique profiles of younger patients with HCC will be essential to maximize their clinical impact and improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.
