Response of licorice to different sources of nitrogen in soil and soilless culture systems
Authors: Hamid Reza Roosta, Ahmad Estaji, Nazim S. Gruda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nitrogen is an essential macronutrient for plant growth and development, taken up mainly as ammonium (NH₄⁺) and nitrate (NO₃⁻). To compare growth, physiological traits, and osmotic responses of licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra) to different N sources across culture systems, a completely randomized design with three replicates tested four cultivation methods, aeroponics, nutrient-film technique (NFT), substrate culture, and soil culture, and three nitrogen regimes: NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻, and an equimolar NH₄⁺ + NO₃⁻ mixture. Sole NH₄⁺ nutrition markedly increased proline, total soluble carbohydrates, sucrose, total phenolic compounds, and total flavonoids in both leaves and roots, and elevated activities of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, polyphenol oxidase, guaiacol peroxidase, glutamine synthetase, and overall free-radical-scavenging capacity. Plants fed with ammonium consistently exhibited the highest glycyrrhizic acid levels across all soilless culture systems. The soil cultivation system showed the lowest glycyrrhizic acid concentrations and no significant differences among nitrogen sources. However, NH₄⁺ decreased biomass, chlorophyll pigments, maximal quantum yield of PS II (Fv/Fm), performance index (PI), total soluble protein, starch, nitrate-reductase activity, and tissue concentrations of N, Ca²⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, and P. All parameters were significantly influenced by the culture system; aeroponics produced the highest growth and chlorophyll levels, followed by NFT, substrate culture, and soil culture. Licorice thus benefits most from a mixed NH₄⁺ + NO₃⁻ supply in advanced soilless systems, with aeroponics providing the remarkable overall performance. In conclusion, licorice cultivation benefits from the synergistic effects of combined nitrogen forms and advanced soilless culture systems. Future studies should aim to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed physiological responses to different nitrogen sources and further optimize nutrient ratios for sustainable, high-yield licorice production in aeroponic systems. Additionally, the long-term effects of these cultivation strategies on the quality and bioactivity of medicinal compounds in licorice should be investigated.

Introduction

Licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra L) is a well-known root medicine plant that belongs to the legume family. It has been traditionally used as an herbal medicine in Iran1. The main bioactive compound is glycyrrhizic acid, which has many critical pharmacological activities, like inhibiting the HIV-1 virus, anti-tumor effects, and anti-SARS-associated virus2. Saponins, naturally occurring plant glycosides, are another compound found in licorice root that is used commercially in the food processing industry3.

The production of medicinal herbs and root crops in controlled environments presents opportunities to enhance the quality, purity, consistency, bioactivity, and biomass production of the raw materials4,5. The hydroponic system is one of the controlled-environment conditions for producing high-quality medicinal plants, including high-quality root plants1. Managing plant nutrition is one of the most critical stages in controlling hydroponic systems, as it plays a key role in plant growth, physiological characteristics, and the biosynthesis of secondary metabolism6. Nitrogen is an essential element for plant growth, which is absorbed chiefly as NH4+ and NO3 −7. Plants usually are adapted to uptake different forms of nitrogen8. However, NH4+ seems to be more easily absorbed than NO3−, because it needs less energy to convert amino acids9, but most plant species use NO3− as a nitrogen source and show toxicity symptoms under NH4+ feeding conditions10. Applying both nitrogen sources together in a nutrient solution effectively increases the growth of higher plants compared to the sole application of NH4+ or NO3 −7. Additionally, NH4+ is a crucial intermediate ion in numerous physiological and biochemical processes. High concentrations of NH4+ in the plant tissue or sap lead to changes in photosynthetic pigments, osmotic adjustments, cation concentration, and secondary metabolites content11,12. However, the influence of nitrogen forms on the accumulation of secondary metabolites in medicinal plants, such as licorice, remains less well understood.

Photosynthesis is one of the most important processes that is affected by different NH4+/NO3− ratios in the leaf. Changes in this ratio affect photosynthetic enzyme activity, leaf chlorophylls, and carotenoid conten13. Although chlorosis is a prominent symptom of NH4+ toxicity, Dai et al. reported an enhancement in the photosynthetic capacity of leaves, photosynthetic pigments, and photosystem II (PSII) activity by increasing NH4+ in the nutrient solution14. Smolov and Semenova reported that an increase in amino acid concentrations in the cells of soybean callus was achieved by adding NH4+ to the medium culture15. Increasing the amount of amino acids causes an increase in chlorophyll content. On the other hand, a high level of NH4+ in the nutrient solution decreased amino acid synthesis and finally reduced the chlorophyll content16.

Osmotic adjustment is one of those mechanisms that many plant species use to tolerate stresses5. This process can occur by accumulating low-molecular-weight organic solute compounds, such as osmoregulators, which provide cellular turgor protection. Biosynthesis and accumulation of osmoregulators such as proline, soluble sugars, and sucrose seem to be positively correlated with cell membrane stability17. Synthesis and accumulation of osmotic adjustments in plants, which occur as a result of different NH4+/NO3− ratios, can cause changes in pH, ethylene synthesis, and polyamine content18. Additionally, some evidence has been reported that different sources of nitrogen fertilizer alter the amount of secondary metabolites, such as total phenolics, in both the root and shoot.

Results of Kováčik and Klejdus showed that the root and shoot phenolic compound content of Matricaria chamomilla plants increased by NH4+ application19. The first step in phenolic compounds synthesis depends on phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL, EC 4.3.1.5) activity that is regulated by NH4+ concentration in root and shoot tissue20. The importance of nitrogen fertilizer on phenolic compound accumulation in root and shoot can be explained by the role of NH4+ on PAL activity20. Therefore, the accumulation of osmoregulators and phenolic compounds acts as a signal to activate antioxidant enzymes21.

In addition to phenolics and flavonoids, terpenoids also contribute significantly to the pharmacological properties of licorice. Licorice roots are a significant source of glycyrrhizic acid, a bioactive compound with diverse pharmacological properties. The concentration of glycyrrhizic acid in licorice is influenced by both genetic and environmental factors, including nutrient management. Recent studies suggest that nitrogen form can modulate the accumulation of glycyrrhizic acid and related metabolites in medicinal plants22. Understanding the effects of ammonium and nitrate nutrition on glycyrrhizic acid content is essential for optimizing licorice cultivation and maximizing its therapeutic value.

Understanding antioxidant metabolism is essential because forming free radicals causes more oxidative damage and less antioxidant activity under various stress conditions19. The effects of nitrogen nutrition (NH4+) on reactive oxygen species (ROS) remain poorly understood. A change in NH4+ concentration in the nutrient solution leads to a change in the glutamate/glutamine ratio, as NH4+ is the main component of the glutamine molecule. NH4+ also acts as a stress signal to activate antioxidant enzymes21. Domínguez-Valdivia et al. indicated that NH4+ induced antioxidant enzyme activity in spinach and pea plants23. Different ratio of NH4+/NO3− has a direct effect on glutamine synthetase (GS) activity. GS activity is essential for nitrogen metabolism, by effectively catalyzing the condensation of glutamate and ammonia to glutamine24. Application of NH4+ usually causes more GS activity in the root, in comparison with NO3 −25.

Different ratio of NH4+ to NO3- in the nutrient solution also leads to changes in the concentration of other mineral elements26. High NH4+ in soil and nutrient solution can compete with K+ for entering through the K internal channel and non-selective channels27. However, adding NO3- to the nutrient solution reduces NH4+ toxicity by changing the pH of the root medium and decreasing the cation/anion ratio in the plant. Babourina et al. reported a reduction of cation content, such as K, Ca, and Mg, in canola roots as a reason for NH4+ application18.

Despite the known effects of nitrogen forms on growth and metabolism, there is limited comprehensive understanding of how different nitrogen sources interact with various cultivation systems to influence both primary growth characteristics and the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites in licorice. Given licorice’s economic and medicinal importance, optimizing nitrogen nutrition to maximize both biomass and bioactive compound accumulation is essential.

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the differential effects of nitrogen sources (NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻, and an equimolar mixture of both) across multiple cultivation systems (aeroponics, nutrient film technique, substrate culture, and soil culture) on licorice growth, physiological traits, and the production of key secondary metabolites.

We hypothesize that (i) different nitrogen sources would differentially affect both growth parameters and the accumulation of pharmacologically critical secondary metabolites in licorice, and combined NO₃⁻ + NH₄⁺ nutrition will result in superior growth performance and optimized secondary metabolite accumulation compared to sole application of either nitrogen form, due to the synergistic modulation of metabolic pathways; (ii) cultivation systems with enhanced root-zone aeration and nutrient delivery (e.g., aeroponics) will amplify these effects by improving nutrient uptake and physiological responses; and (iii) ammonium nutrition alone, while potentially reducing growth parameters, will enhance the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites and activation of associated antioxidant enzymes, reflecting a stress-induced metabolic adjustment beneficial for medicinal compound production.

Materials and methods

Plant material

This experiment was conducted at the experimental greenhouse of Vali-e-Asr University. A completely randomized design was applied with a factorial arrangement of two factors: the cultivation system (NFT, aeroponic, substrate, and soil) as factor one and the form of nitrogen (NH4+, NO3−, NH4++NO3−) as factor two at three replications. Seeds of licorice were provided by the Plant Bank, Biological Research Center (IBRC), Karaj, Iran. Seeds were soaked in H2SO4 (98%) for 10 min and then washed three times with distilled water. After these steps, seeds were planted in multicell trays containing coco peat and perlite (1:1 V: V ratio). Trays were transferred to greenhouse conditions with a relative humidity of 55 ± 3%, a temperature of 25/18 °C (day/night), and a 13/11 h photoperiod. 21-day-old seedlings were transferred to different soilless and soil culture systems (described in the next section).

Cultivation systems and growing conditions

Seedlings were grown under a controlled environment using the aeroponics, nutrient film technique (NFT), substrate, and soil (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Licorice plants cultivated in different cultivation systems. The aeroponic system consisted of three individual chambers (2 × 1 × 1 m) and four mist nozzles per chamber. The nutrient solution was sprayed every 15 min for 15 s on the plant roots. An electrical timer was used for adjusting the time of nutrient solution application. The nutrient solution was pumped from a tank (100 L) to the plant roots and returned to the nutrient solution tank. Seedlings (30 seedlings per chamber) were hung on holes (containing a sponge to support the seedling) of styrofoam sheets, which were placed in the chamber. The nutrient solution in the tank is mixed with a pump.

The NFT system was carried out in six polyethylene gullies (the size was 200 cm length, 20 cm width, and 12 cm height). Every two gullies are connected to a vertical gully. This vertical gully has a channel for transferring nutrient solution to the reservoir tank (50 L). The nutrient solution is pumped from that reservoir tank to the end of the polyethylene gully by a submerged pump. This solution returned to the reservoir by gravity-dependent flow. The gully inclination was 1%, and the flow rate was 2.4 L min− 1.

The substrate cultivation methods consisted of pots (35 cm upper diameter, 20 cm lower diameter, and 25 cm height) filled with cocopeat and perlite (1:1 V: V ratio). Three seedlings per plot were planted.

The same nutrient concentrations were applied in all cultivation systems. During this period, seedlings were irrigated by a modified Hoagland’s nutrient solution containing: 5 mM KNO3, 2.5 mM Ca(NO3)2, 2 mM MgSO4, 1 mM KH2PO4, 7 µM MnCl2, 0.7 µM ZnSO4, 0.8 µM CuSO4, 0.8 µM Na2MoO4, 25 µM Fe-EDDHA, and 2 µM H3BO3. The nutrient solution (pH 6.5 ± 0.1) was renewed in NFT and aeroponics every 3 days. In substrate cultivation system, the plants were fertigated with nutrient solution one time daily, and the substrate was rinsed with distilled water to prevent nutrient accumulation.

The nutrient treatment was applied 80 days after the plants were transferred to the cultivation systems, based on the type of treatments, and maintained for 120 days. At the end of the experiment, sampling was performed to measure the desired traits. To measure photosynthetic pigments, chlorophyll fluorescence, proline, total soluble carbohydrate (TSC), sucrose, starch, total phenolic compounds (TPC), total flavonoids (TF), total soluble proteins (TSP), enzyme activity, and mineral elements, three samples were selected per plant, and four plants were chosen per treatment.

Measurements and observations

Shoot and root dry weight and leaf area

At the end of the experiment, plants per treatment were collected and partitioned into root and shoot, and then oven-dried (48 h at 72 °C) to determine the root dry weight (RDW) and shoot dry weight (SDW). Leaf area (cm2 per plant) was measured by using a leaf area meter (CI-202, Avenue Camas, USA) before drying.

Photosynthesis pigments

The Chl a, b, and TChl, and carotenoid concentration expressed as mg g− 1 FW and measured 110 days after transplanting according to Lichtenthaler28. The middle part of the fully expanded leaf was collected and wrapped in aluminum foil to avoid degradation of pigments by light. One gram of fresh leaves was ground with 10 ml of 80% aqueous acetone in a mortar and pestle. After filtering, the absorbance of centrifuged extracts was measured at 470, 646, and 663 nm, using a spectrophotometer (model U-2000, Hitachi Instruments, Tokyo, Japan).

Chlorophyll fluorescence measurements

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were measured 110 days after planting using a portable photosynthetic efficiency analyzer (PEA, Hansatech Inc., UK). The measured parameters were the maximal quantum yield of photosystem II photochemistry (Fv/Fm) and performance index (PI). Fully expanded leaves were selected from each treatment and adapted to a dark period for 15 min using the dark adaptation leaf clips before taking measurements. After 15 min of dark adaptation, the sensor head was fitted onto the leaf clip for measurement. The Chl fluorescence transients were induced by red light at up to 3,500 µmol (photon) m– 2 s– 1 and recorded from 10 µs to 1 s, with a peak wavelength of 627 nm. The fluorescence transients were analyzed according to the equations of the JIP-test29.

Proline

To determine the free-proline concentration, 0.5 g of the youngest fully expanded leaf samples was homogenized with 5 mL of 95% ethanol. The insoluble parts of the extract were washed with 5 mL of 70% ethanol. Extracts were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was separated and stored at 4 °C for proline determination. An aliquot of this supernatant was taken, and a reactive Ninhydrin acid reagent (Ninhydrin, Phosphoric acid 6 M, and Glacial acetic acid at 99%) was added to each sample. In the next step, samples were placed in a bath at 100 °C. After 45 min, the samples were cooled, and the absorbance was determined at 520 nm using a spectrophotometer. Proline concentration was calculated with a standard curve and expressed as µg g− 1 FW30.

Total soluble carbohydrate

To determine TSC, 0.1 mL of ethanol extract was prepared for proline measurement, mixed with 3 mL of fresh Antron (200 mg Antron plus 100 mL of 72% sulfuric acid). In the next step, samples were heated in a boiling water bath (90 °C) for 10 min. Then, the resulting extracts were cooled, and light absorbance was measured at 625 nm by the above spectrophotometer. Standard curves prepared using pure glucose with different concentrations include 0, 250, 500, 750, 1000, 1250, 1500, 1750, 2000, 2250, and 2500 mg/L. The concentration of soluble sugars was expressed as mg g− 1 FW31.

Sucrose

To extract the sucrose, 0.5 g of fresh leaves was ground with 5 mL of 95% ethanol in a mortar and pestle. The result extract was poured into a Falcon tube, and the extraction process was repeated twice with 5 mL of 70% ethanol. These compounds were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min. 0.1 mL of 30% KOH was added to 0.2 mL of the extraction, and the resulting solution was heated at 100 °C for 10 min. 3 mL of Antron was mixed with 150 mg of Antron, 100 mL of diluted sulfuric acid, 76 mL of sulfuric acid, and 30 mL of distilled water, and added to the samples. Then, the samples were stored at 40 °C for 10–15 min, and the light absorbance was measured at a 620 nm wavelength after the samples had cooled. For preparing the standard curve, a set of sucrose solutions of various concentrations, including 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 µg g, was used31.

Total soluble protein

To determine TSP, 0.5 g samples were homogenized with 6.25 mL of Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.5) and centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 30 min. Then, reagent A, which contains Na2CO3 and 0.5 N NaOH, reagent B, which includes 1% copper sulfate, reagent C, which contains 2% sodium potassium tartrate, reagent D, which provides for reagents A, B, and C, and reagent E, which contains Folin-Ciocalteau, were prepared. In the next step, the samples were mixed with 1 mL of D reagent and 3 mL of E reagent. Samples were placed at a temperature of 15 °C, and absorbance was read at 625 nm using a spectrophotometer. Total phenolic compounds

The extraction was carried out according to Isfendiyaroglu and Zecker33. Fresh plant material was ground with 95% ethanol (5 ml) and kept in the dark for 48 h. Then, 1 mL of 95% ethanol was added to 1 mL of supernatant, and distilled water was added to bring the total volume to 5 mL. 0.5 mL of Folin Regent (50%) and 1 mL of calcium carbonate (5%) were added to the samples, which changed their color to black. In this step, samples were placed in a dark location for 1 h, and the concentration of phenolic compounds was measured at 725 nm using a spectrophotometer. Phenolic compound concentration was calculated based on a standard curve and expressed as µg g− 1 FW.

Mineral elements

Samples of dry leaves were ground and dry-ashed at 550 °C for 4 h. The ashes were dissolved in 5 mL of 2 N HCl, and then the volume was increased by adding 50 mL of distilled water. The concentration of K was measured using a flame photometer (Jenway, model PFP7, UK). Analyses of Ca were carried out with an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (Version 1/33 GBC Avanta, Australia), and phosphorus concentration was determined by using a spectrophotometer according to Olsen et al.34. Total nitrogen concentration was measured according to the Kjeldahl method and expressed as a percent of DW.

Determination of 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl free radical scavenging activity

The free radical scavenging activity was evaluated by 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) according to the method described by Hua et al. .35. One gram of dry root samples was shaken with 8 mL of 70% methanol for 30 min. The suspension was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 8 min. The supernatant was transferred to a 50 mL tube. The residual solid was extracted again. After the sample was extracted three times, the extract was condensed to 5 mL in an oven at 30 °C. Two milliliters of the extraction solution were added to 1 mL of a 0.2 mM methanolic solution of DPPH. After 30 min of incubation at room temperature in dark conditions, the absorbance of the mixture solution was measured at 517 nm using a spectrophotometer. Ascorbic acid was used as the reference compound. Total flavonoids

To determine total flavonoid concentration, 0.1 g of dry plant material (root and shoot) was homogenized in 10 mL of 80% methanol. The resulting extract was centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min, and 0.5 mL of supernatant was mixed with 1 mL of methanol, 4 mL of distilled water, and 0.3 mL of 5% NaNO2. After a 5-minute incubation at room temperature, 0.3 mL of a 10% AlCl3 solution was added. The mixture solutions were kept at room temperature for 6 min. Finally, 2 mL of 1 M NaOH solution was added, followed by 10 mL of distilled water to increase the total volume. After 10 min, absorbance was determined at 510 nm using a spectrophotometer. Flavonoid concentration was calculated using the standard curves, and the results were expressed µg rutin (Quercetin equivalent) g− 1 DW36.

Glycyrrhizic acid

For the extraction of licorice root powder, 70% methanol was used as the solvent. One gram of root powder was mixed with 8 mL of 70% methanol and shaken at 200 rpm for 30 min. The mixture was then centrifuged, and the supernatant was transferred to a volumetric flask. The remaining residue was re-extracted with another 8 mL of 70% methanol under the same conditions. This extraction step was repeated five times, and the combined filtrates yielded approximately 35 mL of extract37. To concentrate the extract to a final volume of 5 mL, the solution was placed in a ventilated oven at 50 °C. The concentrated samples were transferred to dark glass bottles and stored in a cool, light-protected environment until HPLC analysis. Prior to injection, each sample was diluted to 10 mL with pure methanol. Quantification of glycyrrhizic acid was performed using a Waters HPLC system (Model WAT510, USA) equipped with a UV–visible detector (UV 2500 Smartline). The mobile phase comprised 60% methanol as the organic component and an aqueous acetic acid solution (10:30, v/v), providing suitable polarity for a C18 column (Cosmosil 5C18-AR II, 5 μm, 25 cm × 4.6 mm I.D.; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan). For calibration, standard solutions of glycyrrhizic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were prepared in the mobile phase at various concentrations: 62.5, 125, 208.33, and 312.5 µg mL⁻¹ for glycyrrhizic acid. Calibration curves were constructed based on peak area versus concentration. Compound identification in the extracts was achieved by comparing the retention times of sample peaks with those of the standards. Before injection, both standard and sample solutions were filtered through 0.45 μm disposable membrane filters to obtain clear chromatograms suitable for analysis38.

Starch

The Sene et al. method was used to determine starch concentration39. Dry leaf and dry root tissues (0.2 g) were ground and added to 1 ml of deionized water. After 10 min, 4 mL of 5 M NaOH was mixed and shaken for 1 h at 25 °C. Then, 3.8 mL of 5 M HCl was added and made up to 10 mL with distilled water. In the next step, 1 mL of the mixture solution is diluted with 3 mL of deionized water. 120 µl of diluted solution mixed with 3.8 ml sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7) and 0.2 iodine reagent. After 10 min, absorption was recorded using a spectrophotometer at 365 nm. Starch concentration was calculated using the standard curves, and results were expressed as mg g− 1 DW.

Antioxidant enzymatic activity

Frozen samples (0.5) of leaves and roots were ground in a mortar with liquid nitrogen and then homogenized with the respective extraction buffer (pH 7.5) containing 50 mM KH2PO4, 1% PVP, and 1 mM EDTA. After centrifuging, the supernatant part was used for measuring the activity of guaiacol peroxidase (GPX) and polyphenol oxidase (PPO), which oxidize guaiacol and pyrogallol, respectively, at 470 and 420 nm wavelengths40,41.

The phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) activity was measured by calculating the production of trans-cinnamic acid from phenylalanine and by using the standard curve. Results expressed as nmol min− 1 mg− 1 protein42.

Nitrate reductase activity

Root and leaf samples (0.4 g) were ground in a 5 ml extraction buffer containing 100 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.5, 150 mM KNO3, and 1.5% propanol. Samples were placed in a bath with dark conditions at 30 °C for 1 h. After incubation, 1 ml of sulfanilic acid (diluted in 2 N HCl) and Aminoethyl 1 1-naphtylamin dihydrochloride were added. Then, samples were placed at room temperature, and absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a spectrophotometer. The NR activity was calculated with a standard curve and expressed as µgNO2.g− 1 FW.h− 143.

Glutamine synthetase activity

Fresh root and leaf samples (0.1 g) were homogenized by mortar and pestle at 0 °C with extraction buffer (100 mM Triethanolamine, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM MgSO4, 5 mM Glutamate, 10% (v/v) Glycerol, 0.1% Trition X100 and 2 mM DTT). Afterward, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min. Leaf and root GS activities were measured by an assay buffer previously described by Finnemann and Schjoerring44. A standard curve was made using ϒ-glutamyl hydroxamate.

Experimental design

All data were analyzed using SAS software (version 9.1), SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA. When analysis of variance (ANOVA) showed significant effects, the Duncan’s test was applied to compare means at P < 0.05.

Results

Plant growth

Root dry weight, shoot dry weight, and leaf area showed significant variance due to the effects of cultivation system, form of nitrogen, and their interaction effects (Table 3). Regardless of nitrogen form, plants grown in an aeroponic system showed maximum SDW, RDW, and leaf area compared to other hydroponic systems. In the case of nitrogen form, NH4+ significantly decreased plant growth parameters in comparison with NO3− and NO3−+NH4+, in all planting systems. RDW, SDW, and leaf area decreased by 51%, 53%, and 49%, respectively, under NH4+ conditions compared to NO3−+NH4+. The highest reductions of SDW, RDW, and leaf area were recorded in plants grown in aeroponic systems, by approximately 80%, 75%, and 56%, respectively, compared to NO3−+NH4+. In contrast, the lowest reductions of SDW, RDW, and leaf area were recorded in plants grown in substrate, by about 35%, 21%, and 23%, respectively. Sucrose and starch

Generally, sucrose accumulation in leaves was higher than in roots. The level of sucrose in leaves and roots of the licorice plant increased when NH4+ was applied as the source of nitrogen. The highest sucrose concentration of roots and leaves was observed in NH4+-treated plants in the aeroponic system. In contrast, the lowest sucrose concentration was recorded in the roots and leaves of the NO3−+NH4+ and soil medium (Table 5).

Culture systems and nitrogen source influenced the starch content of licorice leaf and root (Table 5). Starch concentration was significantly decreased by increasing the NH4+ and NO3− concentrations in the nutrient solution. However, the role of NH4+ in starch reduction was greater than that of NO3−. Generally, root starch content in NH4+-fed plants compared to NO3−+NH4+-fed plants decreased by about 33%, 38%, 35% and 27% in aeroponic, NFT, subestrate, and soil, respectively. Leaf starch content was decreased in NH4+-fed plants compared to NO3−+ NH4+-fed plants by about 29%, 36%, 26% and 33% in aeroponic, NFT, subestrate, and soil, respectively. (Table 5).

Proline

The concentration of proline in licorice leaves and roots was significantly influenced by the culture system, nitrogen form, and their interaction (Table 5). Plants grown in aeroponic systems exhibited the highest levels compared to other culture methods. The nitrogen source notably affected proline accumulation, with concentrations being markedly elevated under both NO3− and NH4+ treatments. Notably, substrate-grown plants treated with NH4+ showed significant increases in root and leaf proline content, by 103% and 159%, respectively, relative to the combined NO3− + NH4+ treatment. Conversely, the lowest increases were recorded in the NFT system, approximately 69% in roots and 86% in leaves.

Total soluble protein

According to the ANOVA results (Table 5), the TSP concentration in the roots of the licorice plant under different cultural systems was significantly affected by the nitrogen source. The amount of this compound in leaves was not affected by the form of nitrogen or culture systems. Regardless of nitrogen source, plants grown in an aeroponic system had maximum root TSP compared to other culture systems. The amount of this compound was significantly reduced in the roots of plants subjected to NH4+ as a nitrogen source. Plants grown in an aeroponic system had the most reduction of TSP by 38% and the minimum reduction was 18% in the roots of plants grown in a substrate system (Table 5). Generally, regardless of nitrogen form and culture system, the TSP of the root was higher than that of the leaf.

Nitrogen form and culture systems affected the total flavonoid concentration in the leaves and roots of licorice (Table 6). Glycyrrhizic acid

Table 6 indicates that glycyrrhizic acid concentration in licorice roots varied significantly with the nitrogen source and cultivation system. Plants fed with ammonium consistently exhibited the highest glycyrrhizic acid levels across all systems, with concentrations reaching approximately 2.7 mg·g⁻¹ RDM in aeroponic and substrate systems and around 2.3 mg·g⁻¹ RDM in the NFT system. The soil cultivation system exhibited the lowest glycyrrhizic acid concentrations (approximately 0.6 mg·g⁻¹ RDM) and showed no significant differences among nitrogen sources.

According to ANOVA results (Table 7), PAL activity in the leaf and root of licorice was significantly affected by nitrogen source, different cultural systems, and their interaction effects. In all four cultural systems, NH4+-fed plants showed higher PAL activity in root and leaf, compared to NO3− or NO3−+NH4+. The highest leaf PAL activity was recorded in plants cultivated in the NFT system under NH4+ application, at a rate of 30%, in comparison with NO3−. The highest increase in root PAL activity was observed in plants cultivated under the aeroponic system, at a rate of 42% under NH4+ treatment compared to NO3− treatment (Table 7).

Nitrate reductase activity

Results showed that NR activity in root and leaf strongly correlated with nitrogen sources and culture systems (Table 7). The results indicated that the roots and leaves of licorice that were grown in NFT systems had the maximum NR activity, compared to other cultural systems. In both organs, NR activity increased with increasing NO3− dose in the nutrient solution. Root and leaf of licorice plants grown in substrate showed the maximum increase of NR activity by about 270% and 300% respectively. Generally, NR activity in the root was higher than in the leaf (Table 7).

Glutamine synthetase activity

The cultural system, source of nitrogen, and their interaction had a significant effect on Glutamine synthetase activity in the leaves and roots of licorice (Table 7). Regardless of the nitrogen source, plant growth in aeroponic systems showed maximum GS activity compared to other culture systems. High NH4+ concentration significantly induced GS activity in comparison with NO3− and NO3−+NH4+ treatment. However, changes in GS activity under NH4+ treatment were different in the root and the leaf. Root GS activity under aeroponic and NH4+ treatment was higher than NFT, substrate culture, and soil culture by rates of 8.7%, 10%, and 24.3%, respectively. The maximum leaf GS activity was recorded in plants grown under aeroponic and NH4+ treatment. Generally, it can be said that the GS activity of the leaf was higher than the root (Table 7).

Discussion

Plant growth

Nutrient management in hydroponic systems is one of the most important factors for plant growth, physiological balance, and the production of secondary metabolite45. Therefore, determining the best nutrient solution significantly affects plant growth and yield46. The form of nitrogen supplied is a critical determinant of plant growth. Across diverse plant species, NO3− is generally associated with improved growth performance compared to NH4+, which can be toxic at high concentrations or when supplied as the sole nitrogen source47. Studies have consistently shown that exclusive NH4+ nutrition reduces RDW, SDW, and leaf area, while mixed NO3− + NH4+ treatments often yield the best growth outcomes48,49. For example, in Radermachera species, maximum biomass and leaf area were achieved with a 1:1 ratio of NH4+ to NO3−, while sole NH4+ or NO3− treatments resulted in reduced growth48. Our Data showed that plants treated with NO3−+NH4+ exhibited more RDW, SDW, and leaf area compared to plants subjected to other nitrogen sources. The positive effects of NH4+ could be explained by its regulatory role on the pH of nutrient solution and its role in changing mineral nutrient absorption50. On the other hand, high NH4+ concentration in nutrient solution leads to higher ethylene synthesis and lower amount of auxin in roots. All these changes reduce root growth and plant growth. Therefore, the balance between the amount of NO3− and NH4+, in nutrient solution, can increase root and stem growth as a result of decreasing the ethylene synthesis and increasing auxin synthesis51. Coskun et al. reported positive effects of nitrogen availability on CO2 assimilation and biomass production52.

Aeroponic systems, which provide high oxygen availability and efficient nutrient delivery, have been shown to maximize plant growth parameters such as SDW, RDW, and leaf area compared to other hydroponic or substrate-based systems. Enhanced aeration in aeroponics supports greater root development and nutrient uptake, leading to increased shoot biomass and leaf expansion53. Different plant growth in different hydroponic systems in response to nitrogen sources can be explained by the high cation exchange capacity (CEC) of growth media, i.e., soil and coco peat, which causes a reduction of the toxic effects of NH4+ in the rhizosphere. On the other hand, some bacteria in growth media, i.e., nitrosomonas and nitrobacteria, could cause the conversion of NH4+ to NO3−, which has no toxicity54.

The interaction between the cultivation system and nitrogen form is particularly notable. Aeroponic systems, while generally promoting superior growth, also exhibited the most pronounced reductions in SDW, RDW, and leaf area under NH4+ nutrition, suggesting that the high efficiency of nutrient delivery in aeroponics can exacerbate ammonium toxicity55,48. In contrast, substrate-based systems buffered the adverse effects of NH4+, resulting in smaller reductions in growth parameters. This highlights the importance of matching nitrogen form to the specific cultivation environment to optimize plant performance.

The results further support the consensus that a combination of NO3− and NH4+ is optimal for maximizing plant growth across various cultivation systems. This approach leverages the benefits of both nitrogen forms while minimizing the risks associated with ammonium toxicity or nitrate-only nutrition56. These findings underscore the importance of integrating both cultivation systems and nitrogen management strategies to optimize plant growth. Aeroponic systems offer superior growth potential, but careful selection of nitrogen form, favoring a balanced NO3− + NH4+ supply, is essential to avoid growth inhibition, particularly under high-efficiency systems.

Photosynthetic pigments and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters

Consistent with earlier findings in cucumber49 and strawberry57, sole NH₄⁺ supply decreased chlorophyll concentration, likely due to chlorophyll degradation. Similar results have been reported on Arabidopsis under NH4+ nutrition15. In green algae, ammonium as a nitrogen source led to lower chlorophyll content and reduced maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fm) compared to nitrate58. Previous studies indicate that the reduction of chlorophyll concentration in plants fed by NH4+ may be due to perturbation in the synthesis of amino acids, which has an important role in chlorophyll synthesis16. In Lonicera japonica, NH₄⁺ treatment reduced photosynthetic performance and photochemical efficiency, with evidence of photoinhibition and damage to the oxygen-evolving complex, resulting in decreased light absorption and electron transfer efficiency59. Reduction of chlorophyll and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters in plants treated by NH4+ may happen as a result of a change in the thylakoid membrane. These changes increase chlorophyll degradation and reduce transferred energy by antenna chlorophylls60. In addition, NH4+ nutrition causes some ultra-structural changes like grana degradation61. All these changes decrease the amount of chlorophyll and chlorophyll fluorescence13. However, it has been suggested that there was no direct relationship between chlorophyll concentration and NH4+ toxicity, even though chlorophyll loss may contribute to the survival of plants subjected to severe stress. This mechanism may occur as a reason for reducing the amount of photons absorbed by leaves, thereby increasing the photo-protective and antioxidant capacity of leaves per absorbed photon. Shenggl et al.21 suggested that photosynthetic pigment reduction may happen due to the decrease in root growth and reduction of Ca and Mg absorption, under NH4+-fed plats. According to our results, among hydroponic systems, aeroponic systems had the maximum Chl concentration, Fv/Fm, and PI. These results are in agreement with the results of Roosta and Hamidpour on tomatoes62. Different responses of photosynthetic pigments and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters to nitrogen sources and cultural systems could be explained by the level of absorbing none-toxic-ammonium.

Accumulation of active components

Osmoregulators, such as sucrose and TSC, increase in plants under stress conditions63. Accumulation of these substances is strongly correlated with tolerance against NH4+ toxicity64. Our results showed higher levels of TSC and sucrose in plants treated with NH4+, compared to NO3− or NO3−:NH4+-treated plants. Generally, the accumulation of TSC and sucrose in leaves was higher than in roots. This might be due to the high respiration rate of the root and energy expenditure for assimilating NH4+ into amino acids65. Elevated TSC and sucrose in root and leaves of NH4+-fed plants could be explained by the role of TSC and sucrose on osmotic homeostasis in the absence of NO3,− as the primary osmotic anion64. Less carbohydrate demands for growth in NH4+-fed plants could be another reason for this observation66,67. On the other hand, the lower starch concentration of roots and leaves of NH4+-fed plants showed a negative correlation with TSC concentration. This result is in agreement with the results of Bialczyk et al.64, who reported that the starch concentration in the leaves and roots of tomato plants decreased with increasing NH4+ concentration in the nutrient solution. This is consistent with findings that nitrogen addition often shifts carbon allocation from storage (starch) to soluble forms (sugars), supporting growth or stress adaptation68. The reduction in starch under NH₄⁺ may also reflect metabolic adjustments to ammonium stress, as plants prioritize the accumulation of soluble carbohydrates for osmoprotection or signaling. High levels of nitrate also decrease starch concentration by inhibiting AGPase activity, which plays a key role in the regulation of starch synthesis69. The reduction of starch under NH4+ or NO3− conditions can be attributed to reduced carbon storage and a lower photosynthesis rate. The photosynthesis rate and the activity of Calvin enzymes can increase due to the appropriate balance of NH4+: NO3−in the nutrient solution13. The accumulation of carbohydrates and converted carbohydrates in plant organs strongly depends on the root zone pH, which significantly affects NH4+ absorption70. According to our results, the increase in TSC and sucrose can be attributed to starch degradation. On the other hand, the amount of available NH4+ depends on root zone CEC and the activity of bacteria in the root zone.

The maximum TSC and sucrose concentrations in substrate-grown plants under NO₃⁻+NH₄⁺ treatment, and in aeroponic systems under NH₄⁺, align with evidence that nitrogen form and culture system modulate carbohydrate partitioning. The observed minimum TSC in NFT systems may be due to less efficient nutrient or oxygen delivery compared to substrate or aeroponic systems.

Proline, a key osmoprotectant and stress marker, increased markedly in both leaves and roots under NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻ treatments, especially in aeroponic systems. This mirrors results in other species where environmental stressors or high nitrogen availability, particularly as NH₄⁺, induce proline accumulation as part of the plant’s adaptive response71. The interaction between the culture system and nitrogen form modulates the magnitude of this response, with aeroponics often amplifying stress-related metabolite accumulation due to higher nutrient and oxygen availability.

Root TSP was highest in aeroponic systems and lowest under NH₄⁺ nutrition, while leaf TSP was less affected. Nitrogen source is known to influence protein synthesis, with NH₄⁺ sometimes reducing protein accumulation due to metabolic imbalances or toxicity72. The greater reduction of TSP in aeroponic roots may reflect heightened sensitivity to NH₄⁺ in high-efficiency systems. In the present study, high NH4+ concentration in the nutrient solution decreased the TSP of the leaf and root in the licorice plant. Other studies previously reported similar results on spinach and peas23, tomato73, and chamomile74. Previous reports have shown that high levels of NH4+ increased protein hydrolase, which leads to more measurable glutamate dehydrogenase activity75. On the other hand, the reduction in total soluble proteins may be a response to severe reductions in photosynthesis. Reduction in photosynthesis, under high levels of NH4+, caused a reduction of basic materials for protein synthesis. So decrease in photosynthesis eventually reduces protein synthesis. Synthesis of protein is also reduced by increasing free radicals, which affect methionine oxidation76. Results also indicate that different cultural systems resulted in varying amounts of proteins. According to our data, NH4+ availability changed the amount of TSP in all four culture systems.

NH₄⁺-fed plants accumulated more phenolic compounds and flavonoids in both roots and leaves, with the highest increases in soil and NFT systems. This is in line with meta-analyses showing that nitrogen addition, especially in excess, can suppress phenolic metabolism, but specific forms and concentrations may induce secondary metabolite accumulation as a stress response77. The relationship between nitrogen, carbon/nitrogen ratio, and phenolic biosynthesis is complex, with stress or suboptimal nutrition often triggering increased phenolic and flavonoid production77. Phenolic compounds i.e., flavonoids, phenylpropanoids, and lignin, are a large group of secondary metabolites, which are synthesized in different organs of plants under stress conditions78,79. Ammonium also regulates the activity of the PAL enzyme, which plays a crucial role in the initial and pivotal steps of the phenylpropanoid pathway. The phenylpropanoid pathway is the pathway of phenol biosynthesis80. Previous studies have reported that PAL activity is stimulated by increasing NH4+ and N deficiency. These studies also confirmed a positive correlation between PAL activity and soluble phenolic content in roots. Therefore, high PAL activity leads to increased synthesis of phenolic compounds81. phenolic compounds increase plant resistance under NH4+ stress conditions by reducing ROS formation20. Khaleghnezhad reported the high activity of PAL enzymes as a reason for increasing ABA concentration82. These results are in agreement with our findings on licorice plants grown under different systems. Our results showed that free radical scavenging activity increased at high levels of NH4+. Rice-Evans et al. reported that increasing free radical scavenging has a positive relationship with flavonoid and phenolic compound concentration83. The antioxidant activity of phenolic compounds is primarily attributed to their redox properties, which enable them to act as reducing agents, hydrogen donors, and singlet oxygen quenchers20. Khaleghnezhad suggested that increasing PAL activity increased plant resistance against hydrogen peroxide82.

The observed increase in glycyrrhizic acid concentration in licorice roots under ammonium feeding, particularly in aeroponic, NFT, and neutral substrate systems, suggests a positive influence of ammonium as a nitrogen source on this key secondary metabolite. Ammonium may enhance glycyrrhizic acid biosynthesis by affecting nitrogen metabolism pathways that are closely linked to the production of triterpenoid saponins such as glycyrrhizic acid22. This aligns with reports that nitrogen form can modulate secondary metabolite accumulation in plants through altered enzymatic activities and metabolite fluxes84.

The similar and lower glycyrrhizic acid levels in plants treated with nitrate and ammonium nitrate imply that nitrate as a nitrogen source, alone or combined with ammonium, does not stimulate glycyrrhizic acid synthesis to the same extent. Differences among cultivation systems likely reflect variations in the root microenvironment, nutrient availability, and oxygenation that affect nitrogen uptake and assimilation efficiency. The notably low and non-responsive glycyrrhizic acid concentrations in soil-grown plants may result from soil-related factors such as microbial activity, nutrient immobilization, or reduced root oxygenation, limiting the metabolic pathways involved in glycyrrhizic acid production85.

These findings highlight the importance of both nitrogen form and cultivation environment in optimizing licorice root quality, with ammonium nutrition in controlled systems such as aeroponics and neutral substrate culture providing superior conditions for enhancing glycyrrhizic acid content. Such insights can guide improved cultivation strategies for licorice, particularly for medicinal and industrial purposes, where maximizing the yield of active compounds is critical.

Physiological and biochemical indicators

The present findings demonstrate that both the form of nitrogen supplied and the plant cultural system significantly influence the antioxidant capacity, enzyme activities, and nutrient concentrations in licorice roots and leaves. Notably, the highest free radical scavenging activity was observed in plants grown in nutrient film technique (NFT) and aeroponic systems, particularly under ammonium (NH4+) nutrition, while soil-grown plants exhibited the lowest activity. The activity of antioxidant enzymes limited ROS production, induced by abiotic stresses20. In plants, fast removal of hydrogen peroxidase is critical for cell function; otherwise, hydrogen peroxidase can diffuse across the membrane to react with singlet oxygen, resulting in the production of destructive hydroxyl radicals production20. Peroxidase can decompose hydrogen peroxide to H2O and O2. It uses hydrogen peroxide as an electron donor to metabolize phenolic compounds21. In our study, plants grown under NH4+ showed more antioxidant enzyme activity in both organs, which is in agreement with the findings of Shengqi et al.21. They suggested that high concentrations of NH4+ can produce excess hydrogen peroxide, which causes more active decomposition of hydrogen peroxide by peroxidase. All these changes cause strong resistance against NH4+ stress. Similar results have been reported in pea and spinach23 and chamomile20.

One of the most remarkable responses to NH4+ nutrition is a considerable increase in GS activity, which increases ammonium tolerance86. Increasing the activity of GS under NH4+ may act as a defense mechanism against NH4+ toxicity, to produce amino acid44. In this study, a high level of NH4+ increased the GS activity in leaves and roots. Similar results were reported on oilseed rape44, spinach, pea23, and several other plant species25. It was also reported that the gene expression of the GS1 isoform significantly increased in terms of NH4 +24. The different activities of GS in root and leaf are tightly linked to the GS isoenzyme. In many plant species treated with high levels of NH4+, gene expression of chloroplastic GS and cytosolic GS in leaves is more than in roots87.

Nitrate reductase activity is a good indicator of nitrate uptake and nitrate usage in plants88. The level of NR activity is different between plant species. Nitrate assimilation center of many fast-growing plants takes place in their leaves, while the root is the central organ of NO3− assimilation in many woody plants. On the other hand, some perennial and annual plants exhibit NO3− assimilation in both root and shoot. NR activity depends on the form of available nitrogen and medium pH89. Claussen and Lenz reported that increasing levels of NO3− in the root zone raised NR activity in the root and shoot. NR activity enhancement under high concentrations of NO3− has been reported in eggplant89 and pine88, which confirms our results. However, the relationship between NRA and nitrate concentration remains uncertain; nevertheless, increasing NR activity depends on NR gene expression. Xu et al. reported that NR activity can increase nitrogen use efficiency90.

Leaf and root nitrogen concentration increased with the application of NH4+ along with NO3−. Increasing nitrogen concentration in NO3−+NH4+-fed plants previously reported in soybean91. This observation can be explained by high nitrogen absorption92. Ammonium transporters (AMTs) can prevent NH4+ toxicity at higher concentrations of NH4+. AMTs reduce NH4+ absorption by increasing glutamine accumulation93. Sensors located in the membrane can detect external NO3− concentrations, which regulate the gene expression of nitrate transporters (NRTs). A high level of NO3− significantly limits NRTs gene expression, while NRTs act as a defensive mechanism by decreasing NO3− absorption93. On the other hand, high levels of NO3− and NH4+ cause a reduction in root growth, a decrease in nitrogen uptake, and changes in internal hormone balance94. NH4+ treatment decreased the essential cation content, i.e., Ca2+, K+, and Mg2+. NO3− + NH4+ treatment reduced root zone pH and increased phosphorus absorption, resulting in enhanced root growth. In plants treated with NH4+, root growth, phosphorus, and absorption of other cations decreased significantly. These data are in agreement with previous studies that reported a reduction in cation concentration as a reason for high NH4+ treatment18. Competition between NH4+ and K+ for entry through the K+ internal channel or non-selective channels could explain these results27. On the other hand, NH4+ can alter nutrient element absorption by decreasing the pH of the nutrient solution95. However, adding NO3− to the nutrient solution causes less NH4+ toxicity due to changes in the root medium pH and a reduction in the cation/anion ratio in plant tissue49. Generally, different vegetative growth, physiological behavior, and minerals nutrient concentration in different hydroponic systems in response to nitrogen source may be due to the following reasons; (i) oxygen availability in the root zone in the aeroponic system, (ii) change of pH in the nutrient solution and substrate culture, (iii) cation exchange capacity of different substrate, which change absorption and level of NH4+.

Overall, the results highlight the crucial role of nitrogen form and cultural system in regulating antioxidant defenses, enzyme activities, and nutrient uptake in licorice. These findings are supported by recent research across diverse plant species, highlighting the potential for optimizing licorice cultivation through targeted nutrient and system management.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that both the form of nitrogen and the cultivation system critically influence licorice growth, physiological performance, and biochemical responses. Among nitrogen regimes, a combined supply of NO₃⁻ and NH₄⁺ consistently yielded the best overall plant growth, photosynthetic efficiency, nutrient uptake, and balanced secondary metabolite accumulation across all cultivation systems. Sole NH₄⁺ nutrition enhanced stress-related biochemical compounds and antioxidant enzyme activities but suppressed biomass production, chlorophyll content, photosynthetic parameters, total soluble protein, starch accumulation, and essential cation uptake, thereby limiting plant growth. Among cultivation methods, aeroponics presented the most favorable root-zone aeration and nutrient delivery environment, resulting in superior shoot and root biomass, leaf area, and chlorophyll content. However, the pronounced sensitivity of licorice to ammonium toxicity in aeroponics highlights the necessity of carefully balancing nitrogen forms in this high-efficiency system.

For practical application, this research recommends that licorice growers adopt a balanced NO₃⁻ + NH₄⁺ nitrogen regime, ideally in an aeroponic culture system, to maximize growth and physiological performance while maintaining the quality of bioactive compounds. In less intensive systems, such as substrate or soil culture, the combined nitrogen forms similarly improve performance. However, growers may experience less risk from sole NH₄⁺ nutrition due to substrate buffering effects.
