Hip joint image quality screening based on the Diffusion Mamba model
Authors: Pu Wang, Jialu Li, Zhuoran Zheng, Youshan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe scarcity of high-quality annotated datasets hinders the development of reliable AI for hip joint pathology diagnosis. To address this, we present Hip-35, a comprehensive 2D hip radiograph dataset for orthopedic abnormalities, with 35,000 curated synthetic images in 35 pathology categories and 76 expert-annotated clinical radiographic examinations. Our key innovation is a three-stage quality control framework ensuring anatomical fidelity and clinical relevance: (1) Chromatic artifact detection via RGB channel-difference thresholding with edge masking, significantly reducing contamination; (2) Multi-metric deblurring with Manhattan distance, cosine similarity, and SSIM-guided fusion; (3) Anatomical hashing deduplication with U-Net-derived ROI signatures and temporal checks. Leveraging Diffusion Mamba’s (DiM) efficiency, we generate and refine synthetic candidates through our quality pipeline, achieving 92% acceptance in radiologists’ blinded validation. The final dataset includes key clinical variations, such as femoral neck fractures, acetabular deformities, and post-surgical cases. By publicly releasing the dataset and our open-source quality toolkit, this work establishes a new benchmark for medical image augmentation, enabling reproducible AI diagnostic development for rare orthopedic conditions.Subject terms: Machine learning, Diagnostic markers

Background & Summary

Hip joint pathology diagnosis remains a critical challenge in orthopedic imaging, where accurate detection of fractures, dislocations, and degenerative changes directly impacts surgical planning and patient outcomes1–5. While deep learning methods have demonstrated potential in automatic fracture detection6–8, existing models suffer from insufficient generalization capabilities due to the scarcity of annotated medical imaging data. Current public datasets for hip joint analysis, such as the NIH DeepLesion dataset9, lack fine-grained pathological categorization and anatomical specificity, particularly for rare conditions like acetabular stress fractures. This data scarcity problem is exacerbated by the labor-intensive nature of expert annotations and privacy constraints in medical data sharing10.

Traditional data augmentation techniques, such as affine transformations11 and intensity variations12, fail to capture the complex morphological patterns of hip pathologies, such as the trabecular bone density gradients in occult fractures or the metallic artifact distributions in post-surgical implants. Recent advances in diffusion models13 are promising for medical image synthesis, yet their application to high-resolution radiographic image synthesis remains limited due to two fundamental challenges: 1) The quadratic computational complexity of transformer architectures14 becomes prohibitive for 1024 × 1024 resolution synthesis; 2) Existing methods struggle to preserve anatomical coherence in synthesized images15, particularly at bone-cartilage interfaces and fracture margins.

Building upon the recent success of Mamba-based architectures16 in sequential modeling, we adapt the Diffusion Mamba (DiM) framework17 for orthopedic imaging. DiM integrates state space models (SSMs)18 with diffusion models19,20, achieving linear computational complexity while maintaining global anatomical consistency. Our work extends DiM’s capabilities through key innovations tailored for medical synthetic hip radiograph generation:

Anatomical Constraint Injection: Structural priors are injected into the Diffusion Mamba framework through multi-directional scanning, learnable boundary-aware padding, and local feature enhancement, helping preserve joint morphology, cortical continuity, and pathology-relevant fine textures during radiographic image synthesis.

These innovations can efficiently generate high-resolution synthetic X-ray images and preserve the biomechanical relationship among the femoral head, acetabulum, and supporting structures, which is important for preserving pathology-relevant radiographic structure. To ensure clinical application, we further introduced a quality assurance framework combining color difference detection, multi-metric deblurring, and perceptual hash deduplication, and verified it through quantitative indicators and clinical expert reviews.

We provide a more detailed overview of previous work, and below report existing methods in two key areas: the application of deep learning in hip joint pathology analysis and advanced methods for synthetic data generation.

Deep learning in hip joint pathology analysis

Deep learning approaches have shown significant promise in automatic detection and classification of hip joint pathologies. Mutasa et al.21 proposed a CNN-based approach for hip fracture detection, achieving high accuracy on standard datasets. However, these models are often constrained by rare pathologies or subtle morphological changes due to limited training data. Public datasets like NIH DeepLesion9, while extensive, lack the fine-grained annotations required for nuanced hip joint analysis, such as differentiating early-stage osteoarthritis and normal variants22. To alleviate the data scarcity problem, recent studies23–25 have explored transfer learning for general medical imaging datasets, but domain-specific challenges remain, including unique anatomy26 and artifacts27, which limit generalization.

Synthetic data generation based on diffusion model

Traditional data augmentation methods fail to replicate the complex morphological patterns of hip pathologies. For instance, they cannot accurately simulate trabecular bone patterns in stress fractures or metallic artifacts in post-surgical implants. Generative models, particularly GANs28 and diffusion models, offer more sophisticated augmentation capabilities. GANs like CycleGAN29 have been used for image-to-image translation, but they often produce artifacts and lack anatomical fidelity, especially at tissue boundaries30.

Diffusion models13 have been applied to high-quality medical images generation. However, their application to high-resolution X-ray imaging is limited by computational constraints31,32. Transformer-based diffusion models14, while powerful, suffer from quadratic complexity, making them impractical for large-scale medical image synthesis. Furthermore, maintaining anatomical consistency, especially at key interfaces such as bone-cartilage junctions, remains a challenge for existing diffusion frameworks33. State Space Models (SSMs) provide a solution to these challenges. The Mamba architecture16 achieves linear computational complexity through a selective scanning mechanism while maintaining the global receptive domain. The combined architecture of the diffusion framework17 and the Mamba architecture decomposes 2D images into multi-directional 1D sequences by altering scanning modes (horizontal, vertical, diagonal), and extends these advantages to image generation. Our method builds on the latest advancements in hierarchical diffusion17 to limit multi-scale anatomical constraints. By combining SSMs with diffusion models, global anatomical structures and fine-grained pathological details are captured, addressing the limitations of existing methods in preserving tissue interfaces and fracture margins. This innovation associated with rare hip lesions can address the problem of limited real-world hip joint images and improve the generalizability of deep learning models.

Methods

This study introduces a hybrid real-synthetic 2D hip radiograph dataset designed to advance the development of AI-driven diagnostic models for hip joint pathologies. The dataset is constructed using a two-stage strategy: (1) curation and annotation of real hip radiographic examinations, and (2) large-scale synthetic data augmentation via the Diffusion Mamba (DiM) model–a novel diffusion framework built upon SSMs. The complete pipeline for generating and curating the Hip-35 dataset is depicted in Fig. 1.

Original Dataset

The source clinical dataset consisted of 76 de-identified hip radiographic examinations retrospectively collected from the Department of Orthopedics, Shuozhou People’s Hospital, in a single-center retrospective cohort, covering 35 distinct clinical pathology categories, including normal hip joints, dislocations, stress fractures, femoral neck fractures, acetabular deformities, and post-surgical conditions. Representative examples are shown in Fig. 2. The source radiographic examinations were acquired using digital radiography (DR) systems from Beijing Wandong and Philips. These source clinical images were used for expert annotation and subsequent synthetic image generation. All source images were reviewed and annotated under a double-blind protocol by three board-certified radiologists, each with over five years of experience in musculoskeletal imaging, to ensure label consistency and clinical relevance. The original 76 clinical radiographic examinations are not part of the public data release and are not publicly redistributed through this study. Synthetic Image Generation via DiM

In the synthetic data generation phase, we employed the cutting-edge Diffusion Mamba (DiM) architecture17. DiM overcomes the computational bottlenecks faced by traditional transformer-based models when processing high-resolution images. The computational complexity of traditional models scales quadratically, making the generation of high-resolution medical images expensive and time-consuming. In contrast, DiM, with its innovative SSM core, achieves linear computational complexity, showing an unparalleled advantage in efficiently generating high-fidelity, high-resolution images. We adapted the core DiM framework in this paper to better model the global anatomy and fine pathological details of hip radiographs.

Multi-Directional Scanning

To overcome the “causal bias” of a simple raster scan, which restricts the receptive field, we implement a multi-directional scanning mechanism. Let the 2D grid of patch features be denoted as P∈RH×W×D, where H and W are the number of patches vertically and horizontally, and D is the feature dimension. This mechanism defines a set of four distinct mapping functions {Sk}k=14. Each function Sk:RH×W×D→R(H⋅W)×D flattens the 2D grid P into a unique 1D sequence Xk. These sequences correspond to row-major, reverse row-major, column-major, and reverse column-major orderings. By altering these scanning patterns across different Mamba blocks, the model ensures that every patch can causally attend to all other patches, thus achieving a true global receptive field critical for maintaining long-range anatomical consistency.

Learnable Padding Tokens

When flattening a 2D patch grid into a 1D sequence, spatial continuity is broken at the boundary of each row or column. To address this, we introduce a learnable padding token, ppad∈RD, which is a trainable parameter vector. For a given scan direction, let a sub-sequence corresponding to one row or column be Xsub = (p1, p2, …, pL). This sequence is augmented by appending the padding token into Xsub′=(p1,…,pL,ppad). These augmented sub-sequences are then concatenated. This mechanism functions as an explicit “end-of-line” or “end-of-column” signal, allowing the model to learn and recognize the 2D boundaries of the image, thereby preventing it from incorrectly associating the end of a femoral head in one row with the beginning of a pelvic organ in the next, effectively preventing spatial distortions and anatomical misalignments.

Hierarchical Training and Generation Strategy

To maximize both training efficiency and final image quality, we designed and implemented a “Weak-to-Strong” hierarchical training and generation strategy.

Low-Resolution Pre-training

We first pre-trained the model on radiographic images that were downsampled to a 256 × 256 resolution. Training at a lower resolution is computationally inexpensive, allowing the model to quickly and efficiently learn the fundamental anatomical shapes, the general layout of the hip joint, and the coarse features of various pathologies, avoiding premature attention to high-frequency noise and minute details.

High-Resolution Fine-tuning

Once the model had mastered a solid structural foundation through pre-training, we moved it to fine-tuning on the native 512 × 512 resolution images. At this stage, the model could focus its full capacity on learning and refining high-resolution textures, complex pathological features (like occult fracture lines), and the natural transitions between different tissue types. This enables much faster convergence and a higher final image quality than training from scratch at high resolution.

Training-Free Upsampling

To generate even higher, 1024 × 1024 ultra-high-resolution images, we utilized an advanced, inference-time technique for upsampling that requires no additional training. This method allows us to generate images at resolutions the model has never been trained on, effectively overcoming the limitations of GPU memory and high-resolution training data. Technical Validation

Experimental Settings

The experimental framework is implemented on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with 32GB memory, utilizing PyTorch 2.0 for medical image processing. The original dataset comprises 76 hip radiographic examinations, anonymized and annotated by three board-certified radiologists across 35 pathology categories, including femoral neck fractures, acetabular dysplasia, and post-surgical revisions. Synthetic images are generated at 1024 × 1024 resolution using the proposed DiM variant, with training conducted in two phases: initial pretraining on downsampled 256 × 256 images for 50 epochs (batch size 32, learning rate 3e-4), followed by gradient-aware fine-tuning at native 512 × 512 resolution for 30 epochs (batch size 16, learning rate 1e-4). AdamW optimization with cosine annealing schedules the learning rate decay.

Clinical Validation

Because exhaustive expert review of all 35,000 synthetic images was not practical, we performed a stratified blinded validation on a representative subset sampled from the post-generation quality-controlled dataset, with 10 images drawn from each pathology category (350 images in total). Three board-certified musculoskeletal radiologists independently reviewed these images under a blinded protocol. The evaluation protocol assessed three key aspects: anatomical plausibility using a 5-point Likert scale (where 1 indicates anatomically impossible and 5 indicates perfect anatomy), pathological authenticity through binary classification (authentic or non-authentic representation), and diagnostic confidence rated on a 0–10 scale (from no confidence to absolute confidence).

For pathological authenticity, the final label for each image was determined by majority voting among the three reviewers rather than by requiring total agreement. In other words, an image was considered authentic if at least two of the three reviewers classified it as authentic. For anatomical plausibility and diagnostic confidence, we report the mean score across the three reviewers.

The validation results, summarized in Table 4, demonstrated high anatomical fidelity with a mean plausibility score of 4.2 (standard deviation of 0.7). Pathological authenticity was accepted in 92% of cases (95% confidence interval: 89–94%). Diagnostic confidence averaged 8.7 for fracture cases compared to 7.2 for degenerative conditions, suggesting that fracture-related synthetic images were perceived by expert reviewers as more diagnostically convincing than degenerative cases.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, Hip-35 is a 2D hip radiograph/X-ray dataset rather than a volumetric CT dataset, and therefore does not provide multi-slice or 3D anatomical information. Second, although the synthetic images were reviewed by board-certified radiologists, this evaluation was performed on a stratified subset of post-generation quality-controlled images and should not be interpreted as establishing equivalence to full clinical diagnosis in all orthopedic scenarios. In addition, because the source cohort was retrospectively collected and de-identified, complete scanner-level acquisition parameters were not consistently available for all examinations. Accordingly, the dataset should be regarded as a supplementary resource for 2D radiographic research, rather than as a substitute for real clinical cohorts or for multi-view/volumetric imaging in diagnostic applications.

Usage Notes

The Hip-35 dataset can be used by other researchers for various purposes, including: (1) Benchmarking synthetic hip radiograph generation and post-generation quality-control pipelines; (2) Developing algorithms for classifying rare orthopedic conditions; (3) Studying the impact of synthetic data augmentation on model robustness. Researchers should be aware that the dataset, while diverse, primarily consists of synthetic images derived from a specific patient cohort. The provided quality control toolkit can be used to apply similar filtering to other medical imaging datasets.
