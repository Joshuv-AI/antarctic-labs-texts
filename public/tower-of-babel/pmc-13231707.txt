Inhibition of protein kinase CK2 remodels the tumor immune microenvironment and sensitizes lung cancer to PD-L1 blockade
Authors: Sijia Zhang, Lihua Luo, Yangang Qu, Yan Zong, Leichong Chen, Qianwen Li, Zhenyu Li, Jian Wang, Xiaohua Jie, Yun Xia, Bin Qu, Junyu Li, Chunwei Xu, Bingwen Zou, Hongqing Zhuang, Rui Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProtein kinase CK2 is a pleiotropic oncogenic serine/threonine kinase implicated in multiple cancer-associated signaling pathways. However, its role in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and regulating immune checkpoint stability remains incompletely understood.MethodsWe integrated analyses of two independent lung cancer patient cohorts with mechanistic and therapeutic studies in lung cancer models. CK2α expression was evaluated in relation to immune infiltration, clinical outcome, and response to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). The therapeutic impact of combining PD-L1 mAb treatment with three structurally distinct CK2 inhibitors (TBB, CX4945, and SGC-CK2-1) was assessed in vivo. Underlying mechanisms were investigated using transcriptomic profiling, immunophenotyping, and biochemical analyses.ResultsElevated CK2α expression was clinically associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), reduced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, inferior survival, and diminished responsiveness to ICB. In preclinical models, pharmacological CK2 inhibition suppressed tumor growth and reprogrammed the TIME by enhancing T cell infiltration, activation, and proliferation, while alleviating CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion. Importantly, CK2 inhibition sensitized lung tumors to PD-L1 blockade, resulting in robust antitumor efficacy, including complete tumor regression and durable immune protection in a subset of treated mice. Mechanistically, CK2 inhibition reduced PD-L1 protein stability through a defined CK2–TRAF6–PD-L1 axis, in which TRAF6 acts as a critical E3 ubiquitin ligase mediating site-specific ubiquitination of PD-L1 at lysine 281, thereby promoting its proteasomal degradation.ConclusionsThese findings identify CK2 as a key regulator of tumor immune evasion and uncover a previously unrecognized post-translational checkpoint controlling PD-L1 stability via TRAF6-dependent ubiquitination. Targeting CK2 represents a rational strategy to sensitize immunologically “cold” tumors to ICBs and enhance immunotherapeutic efficacy in lung cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40164-026-00778-0.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint blockade (ICBs) has reshaped cancer therapy by unleashing cytotoxic T cell–mediated immunity and enabling durable tumor control across multiple malignancies [1–3]. Despite these advances, the clinical benefit of ICBs remains limited: sustained responses occur in fewer than 30% of patients with most solid tumors [4, 5]. This modest response rate highlights a central challenge in cancer immunotherapy—defining tumor-intrinsic programs that actively restrain antitumor immunity and developing rational strategies to overcome immune resistance [6–8].

A dominant determinant of ICB efficacy is the immune contexture of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [9, 10]. Tumors are broadly classified as immunologically “hot” or “cold” based on the abundance, spatial distribution, and functional state of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), particularly CD8⁺ T cells [11–13]. “Hot” tumors with pre-existing, functional T cell infiltration are more likely to respond to ICB [14]. In contrast, “cold” tumors—characterized by absent, excluded, or dysfunctional T cells—exhibit profound resistance to immune checkpoint therapy [14, 15]. Accordingly, elucidating tumor-intrinsic signaling pathways that suppress T cell infiltration and effector function has become a major objective for expanding the clinical impact of immunotherapy [16, 17].

Protein kinase CK2 is a constitutively active serine/threonine kinase that governs diverse cellular processes, including proliferation, survival, inflammation, and signal transduction [18, 19]. CK2 functions as a heterotetrameric holoenzyme comprising two catalytic subunits (CK2α and/or CK2α′) and two regulatory subunits (CK2β), with kinase activity residing in the catalytic subunits [20, 21]. By phosphorylating a broad spectrum of substrates—including STAT3, AKT, NF-κB, PTEN, and p53—CK2 integrates oncogenic signaling with inflammatory and stress-response pathways [22]. Consistent with these pleiotropic functions, CK2α is frequently overexpressed in human cancers, including lung cancer, and is widely used as a clinically relevant indicator of CK2 activity [23]. Our previous work showed that CK2α inhibition sensitizes lung cancer cells to radiotherapy by impairing DNA double-strand break repair and enforcing G2/M arrest [24–26]. However, whether CK2 directly orchestrates immune evasion and immune checkpoint stability within the TME has remained incompletely understood.

Recent studies have implicated CK2 in immune and inflammatory signaling and proposed indirect connections between CK2 activity and PD-L1 expression or immune checkpoint pathways [27–29]. Importantly, these reports primarily emphasized transcriptional regulation, cytokine-driven signaling, or correlative immune phenotypes, and did not establish a direct molecular mechanism by which CK2 controls immune checkpoint protein stability. In particular, whether CK2 regulates PD-L1 through post-translational mechanisms—and how such regulation shapes the TME and responsiveness to ICB—has not been systematically investigated.

Here, we identify a previously unrecognized post-translational checkpoint governed by a CK2–TRAF6–PD-L1 regulatory axis. We show that inhibition of CK2 catalytic activity upregulates the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRAF6, promoting site-specific ubiquitination of PD-L1 at lysine 281 and accelerating its proteasomal degradation. This mechanism is mechanistically distinct from previously described transcriptional or cytokine-mediated pathways that modulate PD-L1. Functionally, destabilization of PD-L1 through this axis drives robust remodeling of the TME, characterized by increased CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, activation, and sustained effector function, accompanied by delayed T cell exhaustion.

Notably, pharmacologic CK2 inhibition sensitizes lung tumors to PD-L1 mAb therapy in immunocompetent murine models, resulting in robust antitumor activity, including complete tumor regression and durable immunological memory in a subset of treated animals. By directly linking CK2 kinase activity to immune checkpoint stability via TRAF6-mediated ubiquitination of PD-L1, and by establishing the functional consequences for antitumor immunity and therapeutic response, our findings define a mechanistically distinct and therapeutically actionable mode of CK2-driven immune regulation. Together, these data provide a strong preclinical rationale for targeting CK2 to convert immunologically “cold” tumors and enhance the efficacy of ICBs in lung cancer.

Materials and methods

Patients and samples

This study included patients who underwent curative-intent surgical resection for lung cancer at Enshi Central Hospital between January 1, 2018, and June 30, 2024. Tumor tissue specimens from 104 patients were retrospectively collected and assembled into tissue microarrays for immunohistochemical (IHC) assessment of CK2α expression. To minimize potential confounding factors, patients were excluded if they had received neoadjuvant therapy, undergone sublobar resection, had a history of other malignancies, or lacked complete pathological TNM staging information (Fig. 1A). In addition, pretreatment tumor biopsy specimens were obtained from an independent cohort of 20 patients with lung cancer who received first-line ICBs at Wuhan Union Hospital. These samples were used to evaluate the association between CK2α expression and clinical response to ICB therapy. All clinicopathological data were collected retrospectively. Tumor staging was reassessed according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Cancer Staging Manual, 9th Edition [30]. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committees of Wuhan Union Hospital and Enshi Central Hospital (Approval No. 2021-0737-02).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF)

IHC was performed using a BOND-III Fully Automated IHC and ISH Staining System (Leica Microsystems, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocols [31]. Primary antibodies included CK2α (10992-1-AP, 1:500; Proteintech, USA), CD3 (85061 S, 1:100; Cell Signaling Technology, USA), CD4 (48274 S, 1:100; Cell Signaling Technology), CD8 (90257SF, 1:100; Cell Signaling Technology), CD68 (80119 S, 1:100; Cell Signaling Technology), PD-1 (86163 S, 1:100; Cell Signaling Technology), and PD-L1 (13684 S, 1:100; Cell Signaling Technology). Staining conditions were optimized using appropriate positive and negative controls to ensure specificity and reproducibility. CK2α expression in tumor cells was quantified using the H-score method (range: 0-300), which integrates staining intensity and the percentage of positively stained cells. Staining intensity was graded as 0 (negative), 1+ (weak), 2+ (moderate), or 3+ (strong). The H-score was calculated as: H-score = 1 × (% of 1 + cells) + 2 × (% of 2 + cells) + 3 × (% of 3 + cells) [32]. High and low CK2α expression groups were defined using an optimal cutoff value derived from survival analyses (Fig. S1). To minimize overfitting and ensure the robustness of the analysis, the optimal cutoff value for stratifying CK2α expression was determined and validated in an independent external cohort. Using this cutoff, patients were stratified according to CK2α expression levels, and the prognostic value of CK2α for overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) was systematically evaluated. Consistent survival stratification observed in the validation cohort demonstrated good reproducibility and clinical robustness of this cutoff. Detailed clinicopathological characteristics and survival analyses are provided in Table S1. Tumor-associated immune cell (TAIC) density was assessed in both the tumor core and invasive margin by staining 4 μm formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sections for CD3, CD4, CD8, CD68, PD-1, and PD-L1 [33]. Whole-slide images were acquired using an Aperio AT2 scanner (Leica Microsystems) and analyzed with ImageScope and Toolbox software. TAIC density was quantified as the mean number of positive cells across five randomly selected 1 mm² regions per case. Protein expression data for CK2α, immune markers, and corresponding DFS and OS information are summarized in Table S2. Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) staining was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor sections by the Kindstar Global Precision Medicine Institute (Wuhan, China), following standardized protocols provided by the service provider [34].

Bioinformatics analysis

RNA sequencing and clinical data of lung cancer patients were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), including 933 tumor samples and 99 normal controls. The prognostic significance of the biomarkers was assessed using the Kaplan-Meier plotter (http://kmplot.com/analysis/). Differential expression analysis was performed using the R package LIMMA and immune cell scores were stratified into high and low groups based on the median score. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted using the DAVID functional annotation tool.

Cell lines, reagents, and antibodies

Human lung cancer cell lines (A549, H292, H460, H522, H1299, and H1975) and the murine lung cancer cell line (LLC) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA) and maintained in the recommended culture media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, according to standard protocols. The pCasper cell line and the CD8⁺ T cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat. No. 130-096-495) were kindly provided by Univ. Prof. Dr. Bin Qu, Head of the Department of Biomedical Sciences, Institute for Health Research and Education (IGB), Osnabrück University, Germany. All cell lines were routinely authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and verified to be free of mycoplasma contamination; detailed results are provided in the corresponding cell line authentication report. Small-molecule reagents used in this study included SGC-CK2-1 (MedChemExpress, HY-139004), CX4945 (Selleck Chemicals, S0707), TBB (Selleck Chemicals, S5265), MG132 (Selleck Chemicals, S2619), and cycloheximide (Selleck Chemicals, S7418), and recombinant human IFN-γ was purchased from PeproTech (Cat. No. 300-02-100UG). Primary antibodies used for immunoblotting were anti-AKT (Cell Signaling Technology, 9272 S, 1:1000), anti-CK2α (Cell Signaling Technology, 2656 S, 1:1000), anti-GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, 5174 S, 1:1000), anti-Histone H3 (Cell Signaling Technology, 4499T, 1:2000), anti-PD-L1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 13684 S, 1:1000), anti-phospho-AKT1Ser129 (Cell Signaling Technology, 13461 S, 1:1000), anti-TRAF6 (Cell Signaling Technology, 67591 S, 1:1000), anti-Ubiquitin (P4D1) (Cell Signaling Technology, 3936 S, 1:1000), and anti-VINCULIN (Cell Signaling Technology, 13901 S, 1:1000).

SiRNA transfection

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting TRAF6 and CK2α, as well as a non-targeting control siRNA, were synthesized by Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd. (China). Cells were transfected with the indicated siRNAs according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocols, and knockdown efficiency was validated at the protein level prior to downstream analyses. The siRNA sequences used in this study were as follows:

Control SiRNA: 5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU-3’.

TRAF6 SiRNA#1: 5’-GGUGAAAUGUCCAAAUGAA-3’.

TRAF6 SiRNA#2: 5’-ACGAGCAAGUGAUAAUCAA-3’.

CK2α SiRNA#1: 5’-TCCATTGAAGCTGAAATGGTA-3’.

CK2α SiRNA#2: 5’-CAGGAGTACAATGTTCGTGTA-3’.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Takara Bio, Cat. No. 9109) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically prior to downstream applications. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from equal amounts of total RNA using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (Toyobo, Japan, Cat. No. FSQ-301). Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using gene-specific primers (Table S3), with each reaction conducted in technical triplicate. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the ΔCt method, in which the cycle threshold (Ct) value of each target gene was normalized to that of the internal control GAPDH. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility.

Western blotting

Cells were washed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, Cat. No. P0013B) for 15 min on ice. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation, and protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Beyotime, Cat. No. P0010S). Equal amounts of protein were resolved by 10% SDS–PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Cat. No. IPVH00010). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk prepared in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) (Beyotime, Cat. No. P0250) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Detailed information for all primary antibodies, including catalog numbers and working dilutions, is provided in Sect. 2.4. After washing, membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibodies, including anti-rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. No. 7074 S, 1:4000) or anti-mouse IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. No. 7076 S, 1:4000), as appropriate. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using SuperSignal™ West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific/Pierce, Cat. No. 34080) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. GAPDH, VINCULIN, or Histone H3 were used as loading controls as indicated; detailed antibody information for these internal references is provided in Sect. 2.4.

RNA sequencing and analysis

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed to profile transcriptomic alterations induced by CK2 inhibition. A549 cells were treated with vehicle or CX4945 for 24 h, washed once with ice-cold PBS, and harvested by centrifugation at 1,000 rpm for 5 min. Cell pellets were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until further processing. Total RNA was extracted simultaneously from all samples using the NucleoSpin RNA Plus kit (Macherey–Nagel) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop OneC Microvolume UV-vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 2.0 and 2.2 were considered of acceptable quality. RNA integrity was further evaluated by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and samples displaying distinct 28 S and 18 S rRNA bands were deemed intact. RNA sequencing was conducted by BGI Genomics (Shenzhen, China). Gene expression levels were quantified as Fragments Per Kilobase of exon per Million mapped fragments (FPKM) to normalize for gene length and sequencing depth, while raw read counts were used for differential expression analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between vehicle and CX4945 treated groups were identified using the DESeq2 R package, which models count data based on a negative binomial distribution. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and genes with an adjusted P value (padj) < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Functional annotation and pathway enrichment analyses of DEGs, including KEGG pathway analysis, were performed using the DAVID bioinformatics resource.

Generation of TRAF6 overexpression and ligase-dead mutant A549 cell lines

Stable A549 lung cancer cell lines with TRAF6 overexpression (TRAF6OE) or expression of an E3 ubiquitin ligase–dead TRAF6 mutant (TRAF6C70A) were generated with technical support from Creative Biogene (USA). Briefly, full-length human TRAF6 cDNA or a C70A point-mutated TRAF6 construct, which disrupts the RING domain and abolishes E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, was cloned into lentiviral expression vectors. High-titer lentiviral particles were produced and used to transduce A549 cells following standard lentiviral transduction protocols. Successfully transduced cells were selected using appropriate antibiotic pressure to establish stable cell populations, which were subsequently expanded and maintained under selective conditions. The expression of wild-type TRAF6 and the TRAF6C70A mutant was validated by immunoblotting, confirming robust overexpression and comparable protein expression levels prior to downstream functional, biochemical, and mechanistic analyses. These engineered cell lines were used in subsequent experiments to dissect the requirement of TRAF6 E3 ligase activity in CK2 inhibition-mediated PD-L1 ubiquitination and degradation.

Prediction of TRAF6-mediated PD-L1 ubiquitination sites and generation of PD-L1K281A mutant cell lines

Putative TRAF6-mediated ubiquitination sites on PD-L1 were predicted using the UbiBrowser web-based platform (http://ubibrowser.ncpsb.org/), which integrates experimentally validated ubiquitination datasets, protein–protein interaction networks, and sequence-based features to infer potential E3 ligase-substrate relationships. Candidate lysine residues on PD-L1 were ranked according to the UbiBrowser prediction scores and evolutionary conservation metrics. To assess the structural context of candidate ubiquitination sites, three-dimensional structural modeling of human PD-L1 was performed using AlphaFold2. The predicted PD-L1 structure was used to evaluate the spatial positioning and solvent accessibility of individual lysine residues within the protein. Based on these computational analyses, lysine 281 (K281) was selected for subsequent mutational studies. A PD-L1K281A point mutant was generated by substituting lysine 281 with alanine to prevent ubiquitin conjugation at this site. Wild-type PD-L1 and K281A-mutant PD-L1 cDNAs were cloned into lentiviral expression vectors. Stable A549 lung cancer cell lines expressing wild-type PD-L1 or PD-L1K281A were generated with technical assistance from Creative Biogene (USA) using standard lentiviral packaging, transduction, and antibiotic selection protocols. Following selection, stable cell populations were expanded for downstream experiments. Expression of wild-type and mutant PD-L1 proteins was verified by immunoblotting prior to functional and biochemical analyses. These cell lines were subsequently used for ubiquitination and protein stability assays.

Animal studies

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology. Male C57BL/6 mice (5–6 weeks old) were purchased from an accredited animal facility and maintained under specific pathogen–free conditions with free access to food and water. For tumor implantation, mice were subcutaneously injected in the right flank with 5 × 10⁵ LLC cells suspended in 100 µL of sterile PBS. Tumor growth was monitored every 2–3 days using digital calipers. Once tumors became palpable, mice were randomly assigned to the indicated treatment groups. All compounds were prepared and administered according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The following treatments were used: SGC-CK2-1 (MedChemExpress, HY-139004; 10 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, three times per week for 4 weeks), CX4945 (Selleck Chemicals, S0707; 10 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, three times per week for 4 weeks), and TBB (Selleck Chemicals, S5265; 10 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, three times per week for 4 weeks). ICB was performed using an anti-PD-L1 mAb (BioXCell, BE0101; 12.5 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, three times per week for 4 weeks). For CD8⁺ T cell depletion experiments, an anti-CD8α monoclonal antibody (BioXCell, BE0004-1; 100 µg per mouse, intraperitoneally, three times per week for 4 weeks) was administered as indicated. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = (L × W²) / 2, where L represents the longest tumor diameter and W represents the shortest diameter. Mice were humanely euthanized when tumor volume exceeded 1,500 mm³ or when signs of distress or morbidity were observed, in accordance with ethical guidelines. Tumor tissues were harvested immediately after euthanasia and processed for downstream analyses.

Flow cytometry–based phenotypic analysis

Subcutaneous tumors were harvested at the indicated endpoints and processed immediately. For each mouse, the tumor was equally partitioned into four pieces. Three pieces were processed in parallel for flow cytometric phenotyping using separate, purpose-built antibody panels to evaluate CD8⁺ T-cell (i) activation/effector function, (ii) exhaustion, and (iii) memory differentiation, respectively. This design reduced panel complexity, avoided fluorochrome overlap, and allowed panel-specific compensation to be optimized under stable conditions. The fourth piece was reserved as a backup for repeat measurements or additional downstream validation. Tumor tissues were mechanically dissociated and enzymatically digested into single-cell suspensions, followed by filtration through a 70 μm cell strainer. Cells were washed and stained with Zombie NIR™ Fixable Viability Dye (BioLegend, Cat. No. 423105) to exclude dead cells. Surface marker staining was then performed at 4 °C using fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against CD45 (Brilliant Violet 510, clone 30-F11, BioLegend, Cat. No. 103138), CD3 (FITC, clone 17A2, BioLegend, Cat. No. 100203), CD4 (PE/Cyanine7, clone GK1.5, BioLegend, Cat. No. 100422), CD8a (Alexa Fluor 647, clone 53 − 6.7, BioLegend, Cat. No. 100724), PD-1 (CD279) (Brilliant Violet 421, clone 29 F.1A12, BioLegend, Cat. No. 135217), LAG-3 (CD223) (PE, clone C9B7W, BioLegend, Cat. No. 125208), CD62L (PE, clone MEL-14, BioLegend, Cat. No. 104407), and CD44 (Brilliant Violet 421, clone IM7, BioLegend, Cat. No. 103039). Following surface staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 00-5523-00) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Intracellular staining was subsequently performed with antibodies against IFN-γ (PE, clone XMG1.2, BioLegend, Cat. No. 505807), TNF-α (Brilliant Violet 421, clone MP6-XT22, BioLegend, Cat. No. 506327), and Ki-67 (PE/Cyanine7, clone 16A8, BioLegend, Cat. No. 652417). Flow cytometric acquisition was conducted on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter), with compensation established using single-stained controls and fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls where appropriate. Data were analyzed in FlowJo. The full gating strategy is provided in Fig. S2. Antibody details (vendor, clone, fluorochrome, and catalog number) are listed in Table S4.

Live-cell imaging-based 3D cytotoxicity assay

To assess immune cell–mediated cytotoxicity in a three-dimensional (3D) context, a live-cell imaging–based collagen matrix killing assay was performed. Target tumor cells were resuspended in a pre-chilled, neutralized type I collagen solution (2 mg/mL; Advanced BioMatrix, Cat. No. 5005) and seeded onto Falcon 96-well Black/Clear Flat Bottom TC-treated Imaging Microplates (Corning, Cat. No. 353219) at a density of 2.5 × 10⁴ cells per well in a final volume of 40 µL. Plates were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ for 40 min to allow collagen polymerization and formation of a 3D matrix. After collagen solidification, effector cells were gently added to each well at the indicated effector-to-target (E: T) ratios. Real-time cytotoxic activity was monitored using an ImageXpress Micro Confocal High-Content Imaging System (Molecular Devices) equipped with a 20× objective. Time-lapse imaging was performed at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, with images acquired every 20 min over a total duration of 40 h. Acquired images were analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH) to quantify target cell viability and killing kinetics over time.

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis

Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) was employed to quantify drug concentrations in mouse plasma, tumor tissue, and lung tissue. At designated time points following drug administration, mice were euthanized, and blood samples were collected by cardiac puncture into EDTA-coated tubes. Plasma was obtained by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Tumor and lung tissues were excised, weighed, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C. For sample preparation, plasma samples were subjected to protein precipitation by the addition of ice-cold organic solvent containing an internal standard, followed by vortexing and centrifugation to remove precipitated proteins. The resulting supernatants were collected for LC–MS/MS analysis. Tissue samples were homogenized in appropriate extraction buffer using a tissue homogenizer, followed by protein precipitation with organic solvent containing the internal standard. After centrifugation, the supernatants were collected and used for analysis. Tissue drug concentrations were normalized to tissue weight and expressed as ng/g tissue. Chromatographic separation was performed using a reverse-phase C18 column on a high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled to a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid), delivered using a gradient elution at a constant flow rate. The mass spectrometer was operated in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode under optimized electrospray ionization conditions. Quantification was achieved using calibration curves generated from authentic standards spiked into blank plasma or tissue homogenates. Calibration curves were linear over the relevant concentration range, and quality control samples at low, medium, and high concentrations were included to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using dedicated mass spectrometry software. Drug concentrations in plasma were expressed as ng/mL, while tissue concentrations were expressed as ng/g tissue.

Statistical analysis

The optimal cut-off for CK2α expression was determined using Jamovi software (version 2.5.6; https://www.jamovi.org). DFS, OS, and progression-free survival (PFS) were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared between the groups using the log-rank test. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests were used. For analyses involving three or more groups, data distribution and variance assumptions were assessed prior to analysis. When assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were met, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons; otherwise, appropriate non-parametric tests were used. Unless otherwise specified, data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess associations between variables, and correlation coefficients (r) were calculated. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Statistical significance is indicated as P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), P < 0.001 (***); ns, not significant.

Results

High CK2α expression is associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, T-cell exhaustion, and poor clinical outcomes in non-small cell lung cancer

To define the clinical and immunological relevance of CK2α in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), we performed a retrospective analysis of 104 patients with stage Ia–IIIb disease who underwent curative surgical resection (Fig. 1A; Table 1). Multivariate cox regression analyses incorporating CK2α expression and established clinicopathological variables identified CK2α as an independent predictor of both DFS and OS (Table S5, S6). IHC analysis revealed that CK2α was predominantly localized to both the cytoplasm and nucleus of tumor cells, with substantial inter-patient heterogeneity in expression levels (Fig. 1B). Patients with low CK2α expression exhibited significantly prolonged DFS and OS compared with those with high CK2α expression (Fig. 1C), establishing CK2α as a robust adverse prognostic marker in NSCLC. Beyond its prognostic value, CK2α expression strongly correlated with the immune composition of the TME. Tumors with low CK2α expression displayed markedly increased infiltration of CD3⁺, CD4⁺, and CD8⁺ T cells, as well as macrophages (Fig. 1D). In contrast, tumors with high CK2α expression were characterized by reduced immune cell infiltration and enrichment of PD-1⁺ and PD-L1⁺ cells, consistent with an immunosuppressive and exhausted immune phenotype (Fig. 1E). Correlation analyses confirmed a negative association between CK2α expression and intratumoral densities of effector T cells and macrophages, while revealing positive correlations with PD-1⁺ and PD-L1⁺ immune populations (Fig. 1F). mIF analysis further substantiated these findings. Quantitative mIF profiling demonstrated significantly higher densities of CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in CK2α-low tumors, whereas CK2α-high tumors were enriched for FoxP3⁺ regulatory T cells (Tregs) and PD-L1⁺ cells, indicative of a profoundly immunosuppressive TME (Fig. 1G; Fig. S3; Table S7). To evaluate the predictive relevance of CK2α in the context of immunotherapy, we analyzed pretreatment tumor specimens from patients with stage IV lung cancer receiving first-line ICBs (Table 2). CK2α expression was significantly associated with therapeutic response, as assessed by radiographic evaluation. Patients with low CK2α expression achieved superior clinical responses compared with those with high CK2α expression (Fig. 1H). In contrast, patients with progressive disease (PD) exhibited markedly elevated CK2α levels relative to those achieving a partial response (PR) or stable disease (SD), as defined according to standard RECIST criteria (Fig. S4A). Consistently, low CK2α expression was associated with prolonged PFS (Fig. S4B). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were specifically performed in the ICB-treated cohort to evaluate the predictive value of CK2α for immunotherapy outcomes. CK2α demonstrated strong predictive performance, yielding areas under the curve (AUCs) of 0.945 for PFS and 0.978 for OS, respectively (Fig. S5). Collectively, these data demonstrate that elevated CK2α expression defines an immune-excluded, T cell-exhausted TME and is associated with poor clinical outcomes and diminished responsivenesto immunotherapy.

Low CK2α expression indicates a more activated tumor immune microenvironment

To independently validate our clinical observations, we interrogated publicly available transcriptomic datasets. Analysis of the TCGA lung cancer cohort demonstrated that CK2α mRNA expression was significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared with normal lung tissues (Fig. 2A). Consistent with our institutional cohort, increased CK2α expression was significantly associated with poorer clinical outcomes, including OS and DFS (Fig. 2B, C). CK2α expression was also significantly associated with multiple clinicopathological parameters, including age, sex, smoking status, and clinical stage (Fig. 2D, E; Tables S1, S2), indicating that CK2α expression captures broad biological heterogeneity within lung cancer. To delineate the immune landscape linked to CK2α expression, we performed immune cell deconvolution analyses. Tumors with low CK2α expression exhibited increased infiltration of multiple immune cell populations, including M1 macrophages, follicular helper T cells, resting CD4⁺ memory T cells, activated mast cells, monocytes, naïve B cells, γδ T cells, resting mast cells, resting NK cells, M0 macrophages, memory B cells, and Tregs (Fig. 2F). In contrast, elevated CK2α expression was inversely correlated with the infiltration of key antitumor immune populations, including CD4⁺ T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, and myeloid dendritic cells, while showing a positive association with PD-L1 expression (Fig. 2G), consistent with an immune-evasive tumor phenotype. To gain insight into the biological pathways associated with CK2α expression, pathway enrichment analyses were conducted. These analyses revealed significant associations between CK2α expression and multiple immune-related signaling pathways, including Toll-like receptor signaling, plasminogen activation, and organ-specific immune response pathways (Fig. 2H-J), implicating CK2α in the regulation of both innate and adaptive immune processes within the TME. Together, these integrative analyses demonstrate that low CK2α expression defines a transcriptionally and immunologically active TME, whereas elevated CK2α expression is associated with impaired immune infiltration and enhanced immune evasion. These findings further reinforce CK2α as a central regulator of tumor-immune interactions in lung cancer

TBB enhances CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity and mitigates T cell exhaustion

To functionally validate the immunological consequences of CK2 inhibition observed in human cohorts, we employed a LLC model in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice. Following subcutaneous implantation of LLC cells, mice were treated with the TBB by intraperitoneal injection according to the experimental design outlined in Fig. 3A. Pharmacokinetic analysis using LC–MS/MS demonstrated that systemically administered TBB was readily detectable in plasma, lung, and tumor tissues 24 h after a single injection (Fig. 3B), with quantitative enrichment observed within tumor tissues relative to plasma and lung (Fig. 3C), confirming effective tumor exposure. Consistent with adequate target engagement, TBB treatment significantly suppressed tumor progression. Gross examination of excised tumors revealed markedly reduced tumor size in TBB-treated mice compared with vehicle controls (Fig. 3D). Immunoblotting further confirmed elevated CK2α expression in tumor tissues relative to adjacent non-tumorous lung, supporting the biological relevance of CK2 targeting in this model (Fig. 3E). Longitudinal tumor measurements showed a pronounced delay in tumor growth in the TBB-treated group (Fig. 3F). Importantly, TBB administration was well tolerated, as evidenced by stable body weight throughout the treatment period (Fig. 3G). Consistent with the suppressed tumor growth kinetics, tumor weights at the experimental endpoint were significantly reduced in TBB-treated mice compared with vehicle controls (Fig. 3H). Accordingly, TBB-treated mice exhibited a significant survival advantage relative to vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 3I). Flow cytometric analysis of TILs revealed that TBB treatment significantly increased the proportion of CD8⁺ T cells, while exerting only modest effects on CD4⁺ T cell frequencies (Fig. 3J-L), indicating a preferential enhancement of cytotoxic T cell infiltration. Functional profiling of tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells further demonstrated that TBB treatment markedly increased the frequencies of IFN-γ and TNF-α producing CD8⁺ T cells, reflecting enhanced effector activity (Fig. 3M, O). In parallel, the proportion of Ki-67⁺ CD8⁺ T cells was significantly elevated, indicative of increased intratumoral proliferation (Fig. 3N). Given the central role of immune checkpoint signaling in T cell dysfunction, we next evaluated exhaustion markers on CD8⁺ T cells. Notably, TBB treatment significantly reduced the proportion of PD-1⁺ CD8⁺ T cells within tumors, consistent with alleviation of T cell exhaustion (Fig. 3P). These immune phenotypes were corroborated by tissue-based analyses. Immunofluorescence staining revealed markedly increased CD8⁺ T cell infiltration in tumors from TBB-treated mice compared with vehicle controls (Fig. 3Q), which was confirmed by quantitative analysis of CD8⁺ T cell density per field (Fig. 3R). Collectively, these results demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of CK2 by TBB not only suppresses tumor growth but also profoundly remodels the TIME by enhancing CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, proliferation, and effector function, while concurrently mitigating T cell exhaustion.

Pharmacologically distinct CK2 inhibitors sensitize tumors to PD-L1 blockade, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity and establishing durable immune memory

To determine whether CK2 inhibition augments the efficacy of ICB, LLC-bearing mice were treated with the TBB, anti-PD-L1 mAb, or their combination (Fig. 4A, B). Whereas TBB or PD-L1 mAb monotherapy produced only limited tumor growth delay, combined treatment resulted in robust tumor suppression and induced complete tumor regression in 4 of 8 mice (50%) (Fig. 4C–E). Consistently, mean tumor volumes at day 27 were markedly reduced in the combination group compared with either monotherapy, without evidence of systemic toxicity as reflected by stable body weight (Fig. 4F, G). The therapeutic benefit of combined TBB and PD-L1 blockade was strictly dependent on CD8⁺ T cells. Depletion of CD8⁺ T cells largely abrogated tumor control and survival advantages conferred by the combination therapy, establishing a CD8⁺ T cell-dependent mechanism of action (Fig. S6). Notably, mice achieving complete responses (CR) rejected subsequent tumor rechallenge, whereas naïve control mice rapidly developed progressive tumors, demonstrating the induction of durable immunological memory (Fig. 4H). Mechanistically, combination therapy significantly increased intratumoral CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and enhanced effector activity, as evidenced by elevated TNF-α production and increased Ki-67 expression (Fig. 4I–L). Phenotypic profiling further revealed a pronounced enrichment of T central memory (TCM; CD62LhiCD44hi) and T effector memory (TEM; CD62LloCD44hi) CD8⁺ T cell subsets—populations that are functionally associated with long-term persistence, rapid recall responses, and immediate effector capacity, respectively. This shift was accompanied by reduced PD-1 expression, indicative of alleviated T cell exhaustion and a transition toward a more durable and functionally competent antitumor T cell state (Fig. 4M–P). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that dual targeting of CK2 and PD-L1 sensitizes lung tumors to ICBs, thereby amplifying CD8⁺ T cell–mediated antitumor immunity and establishing long-lasting immune memory.

Building on our initial observations, we next examined whether the immunotherapeutic sensitization observed with TBB represents a broader, CK2 inhibition–dependent effect by testing two pharmacologically distinct CK2 inhibitors, CX4945 and SGC-CK2-1, in combination with PD-L1 blockade. CX4945 is a bioavailable and clinically advanced CK2 inhibitor with high selectivity and limited off-target activity, whereas SGC-CK2-1 is a potent, cell-active, and highly selective chemical probe for CK2 (CK2α/CK2α′), widely used to interrogate CK2-dependent signaling (Fig. 5A–C). In the LLC subcutaneous tumor model, combination treatment with either CX4945 or SGC-CK2-1 together with PD-L1 mAb resulted in a marked reduction in tumor volume and tumor weight compared with vehicle or monotherapy controls (Fig. 5D–F). Importantly, body weight remained stable across all treatment groups, indicating favorable tolerability of the combination regimens in vivo (Fig. 5G). To define the immunological basis of this enhanced antitumor efficacy, we analyzed tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes by flow cytometry. Combined CK2 inhibition and PD-L1 blockade significantly increased the infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells within the TME (Fig. 5H). Moreover, CD8⁺ T cells from the combination groups displayed increased proliferative activity, as indicated by elevated Ki-67 expression, and enhanced effector function, evidenced by increased production of IFN-γ and TNF-α (Fig. 5I, J). Notably, the proportion of PD-1⁺LAG-3⁺ tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells was significantly reduced following combination therapy, indicating a marked alleviation of T cell exhaustion (Fig. 5K). Consistent with these functional changes, phenotypic profiling revealed a pronounced expansion of central memory (TCM; CD62LhiCD44hi) and effector memory (TEM; CD62LloCD44hi) CD8⁺ T cell subsets in mice receiving combined CK2 inhibition and PD-L1 blockade, supporting the establishment of durable antitumor immune memory (Fig. 5L, M). These findings were further corroborated by IF analysis of tumor sections, which demonstrated increased CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and reduced PD-L1 expression within the TME following combination treatment (Fig. 5N–Q). In addition, reduced PD-1 protein levels within tumor tissues were observed after combined CX4945 and PD-L1 mAb treatment (Fig. S7). At the molecular level, immunoblotting confirmed downregulation of PD-L1 protein expression and suppression of CK2-dependent AKTSer129 phosphorylation in tumors treated with CX4945 or SGC-CK2-1 in combination with PD-L1 mAb (Fig. 5R, S), indicating effective target engagement and pathway inhibition. Finally, comprehensive safety assessment revealed no overt histopathological abnormalities in major organs, including heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, as assessed by hematoxylin and eosin staining (Fig. S8A; Fig. S9A). Consistently, serum biochemical analyses showed no significant alterations in hepatic, renal, cardiac, or metabolic parameters across treatment groups (Fig. S8B; Fig. S9B). Together, these data demonstrate that pharmacologically distinct CK2 inhibitors sensitize tumors to PD-L1 blockade, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity while maintaining a favorable safety profile in vivo

CK2 inhibition enhances CD8+ T cell-mediated tumor cell killing capacity

To directly assess whether CK2 inhibition enhances the cytotoxic function of CD8⁺ T cells, we isolated human CD8⁺ T cells from healthy donor peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) by negative selection and evaluated their tumor cell killing capacity in vitro. CD8⁺ T cells were co-cultured with target cells in the presence of the CK2 inhibitors CX4945 or SGC-CK2-1. To discriminate between apoptotic and necrotic target cell death, we employed a cell line stably expressing the pCasper apoptosis reporter, which consists of a GFP-RFP fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) pair linked by a caspase-3 cleavage motif (DEVD) [48]. Upon induction of apoptosis, caspase activation disrupts the FRET signal, resulting in a shift to GFP fluorescence, whereas necrotic cells with compromised membrane integrity lose both fluorescent signals (Fig. 6A). Real-time live-cell imaging revealed that exposure to CX4945 or SGC-CK2-1 significantly accelerated CD8⁺ T cell-mediated killing kinetics compared with control conditions (Fig. 6B–D; Supplementary Movie 1). Notably, CK2 inhibition enhanced the rate of tumor cell elimination without altering the mode of target cell death, as the relative proportions of apoptotic and necrotic events remained comparable across treatment groups. These findings indicate that CK2 inhibition potentiates the intrinsic cytotoxic efficiency of CD8⁺ T cells rather than skewing death pathway utilization.

CK2α protein expression positively correlates with PD-L1 protein levels and immune evasion in lung cancer

To define the relationship between CK2α and PD-L1 in lung cancer, we first examined CK2α expression across a panel of commonly used lung cancer cell lines, including H460, A549, H1299, LLC, and H1975. Compared with human bronchial epithelial (HBE) cells, CK2α protein levels were markedly elevated in all lung cancer cell lines examined (Fig. 7A, C), indicating aberrant upregulation of CK2α in malignant cells. To determine the functional relevance of CK2α, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown of CK2α in A549 (human) and LLC (murine) lung cancer cells, both of which exhibit high endogenous CK2α expression. Efficient depletion of CK2α was confirmed by immunoblotting (Fig. 7B, D). In parallel, pharmacological inhibition of CK2 using three structurally and mechanistically distinct inhibitors—TBB, CX4945, and SGC-CK2-1—was validated by monitoring phosphorylation of the canonical CK2 substrate AKT at Ser129. All three inhibitors robustly suppressed AKTSer129 phosphorylation without affecting total AKT levels, confirming effective CK2 inhibition (Fig. 7E). Given the central role of PD-L1 in tumor immune evasion, we next assessed whether CK2α modulates PD-L1 expression. Flow cytometric analysis further demonstrated that targeting CK2 significantly reduced cell surface PD-L1 expression across multiple human lung cancer cell lines, including A549, H1299, H522, and H292 (Fig. 7F, G). These findings were independently corroborated by IF staining, which revealed a marked decrease in PD-L1 mean fluorescence intensity following either CK2 inhibition or CK2α knockdown, in both human and mouse-derived lung cancer cells (Fig. 7H–J). Consistent with reduced membrane expression, total PD-L1 protein levels were also diminished upon CK2 inhibition or CK2α silencing, as detected by immunoblotting (Fig. 7K, L). Subcellular fractionation and IF analyses further indicated that PD-L1 was reduced in both cytoplasmic and nuclear compartments following CK2α suppression (Fig. 7M), suggesting a global effect on PD-L1 protein stability rather than selective redistribution. Notably, CK2 inhibition not only decreased basal PD-L1 expression but also significantly attenuated IFN-γ-induced PD-L1 upregulation, as demonstrated by both immunoblotting and flow cytometric analyses (Fig. 7N, O). Because several lung cancer cell lines used in this study harbor oncogenic KRAS mutations—most notably A549 (KRASG12S) and H460 (KRASQ61H)—we next explored the relationship between KRAS mutation status and CK2α expression. Immune deconvolution analyses revealed that KRAS-mutant lung cancers with high CK2α expression exhibited an immune-desert–like phenotype, characterized by diminished effector lymphocyte infiltration and relative enrichment of non-effector immune cell populations (Fig. S10). Consistent with this observation, analysis of TCGA datasets demonstrated significantly higher CK2α expression in KRAS-mutant tumors compared with KRAS wild-type counterparts (Fig. S11A). Across lung cancer cohorts, CK2α expression positively correlated with KRAS expression (Fig. S11B). Collectively, these data establish CK2α as a positive regulator of PD-L1 expression in lung cancer and link elevated CK2α levels to immune evasion, particularly in KRAS-mutant tumors.

CK2 inhibition destabilizes PD-L1 through a TRAF6-dependent ubiquitin–proteasome mechanism targeting PD-L1K281

To gain mechanistic insight into how CK2α regulates PD-L1 expression in lung cancer, we performed RNA-seq following pharmacologic CK2 inhibition with CX4945. Differential expression analysis identified 924 significantly altered genes (P < 0.05, |FC| > 2), including 641 upregulated and 283 downregulated transcripts in CX4945-treated cells compared with vehicle controls (Fig. 8A). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed prominent activation of the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and T cell receptor signaling pathways, highlighting protein turnover and immune regulation as major downstream consequences of CK2 inhibition (Fig. 8B). Consistently, KEGG pathway analysis identified 12 differentially expressed genes enriched in the PD-L1 expression and PD-1 immune checkpoint pathways (Fig. 8C). To validate the RNA-seq findings, A549 and H460 lung cancer cells were treated with CX4945 and subjected to RT–qPCR analysis. CX4945 treatment resulted in transcriptional downregulation of STAT1 and upregulation of FOS, JUN, TRAF6, MAPK12, and MTOR (Fig. 8D, E), confirming robust modulation of immune and stress responsive signaling programs. Notably, RT–qPCR further demonstrated a significant increase in PD-L1 mRNA levels following CK2 inhibition in both A549 and H460 cells (Fig. S12). This apparent divergence between PD-L1 transcript and protein abundance reflects a time-dependent, multi-layered regulatory mechanism: CK2 inhibition triggers rapid post-translational destabilization and degradation of PD-L1 protein, while a delayed compensatory transcriptional response emerges at later time points. Importantly, this transcriptional upregulation is insufficient to overcome the dominant CK2-TRAF6-mediated UPS degradation of PD-L1. Integrative cross-analysis of CX4945-upregulated genes with IFN-γ response and Reactome adaptive immune system gene sets identified five immune-associated genes—HERC6, SLAMF7, CD274 (PD-L1), FCGR1A, and ICAM1—which together define a CX4945-responsive immune signature (Fig. 8F; Table S8). Analysis using the GEPIA platform revealed a positive correlation between this CX4945 signature and markers of T cell exhaustion in lung cancer patient cohorts (Fig. 8G), suggesting activation of adaptive immune–related transcriptional programs under CK2 inhibition. In parallel, CX4945 treatment significantly increased the mRNA expression of multiple MHC class I genes, indicating enhanced tumor antigen presentation capacity (Fig. 8H). Finally, intersection analysis of genes enriched in ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis and PD-L1 expression pathways identified TRAF6 as a central node linking CK2 inhibition to PD-L1 regulation (Fig. 8I).

To elucidate the mechanism by which CK2 regulates PD-L1 at the post-translational level, we first examined whether CK2 inhibition affects PD-L1 protein stability via the UPS. Pharmacological inhibition of CK2 with CX4945 markedly reduced PD-L1 protein levels in lung cancer cells, an effect that was completely reversed by the proteasome inhibitor MG132, indicating that CK2-mediated regulation of PD-L1 is proteasome dependent (Fig. 9A). Consistently, siRNA-mediated depletion of CK2α similarly decreased PD-L1 protein abundance, which was restored upon proteasome inhibition (Fig. 9B). To directly assess PD-L1 protein turnover, cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays were performed. Compared with vehicle-treated controls, CX4945 treatment significantly shortened the half-life of PD-L1, demonstrating that CK2 inhibition accelerates PD-L1 protein degradation (Fig. 9C). In line with this observation, immunoprecipitation analyses revealed a pronounced increase in PD-L1 polyubiquitination following CK2 inhibition (Fig. 9D), supporting enhanced ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal targeting. RNA-seq analysis suggested that TRAF6 may function as a key E3 ubiquitin ligase linking CK2 inhibition to PD-L1 destabilization. Consistent with this prediction, co-immunoprecipitation assays confirmed a robust endogenous interaction between PD-L1 and TRAF6 in both A549 and H460 lung cancer cells (Fig. 9E, F). We next generated TRAF6-silenced A549 cells and verified efficient knockdown by western blotting (Fig. 9G). Notably, CX4945 treatment significantly upregulated TRAF6 protein expression (Fig. 9H), whereas TRAF6 depletion markedly attenuated CK2 inhibition-induced PD-L1 ubiquitination (Fig. 9I), establishing TRAF6 as a critical mediator of PD-L1 ubiquitination downstream of CK2 inhibition. To further define the functional requirement of TRAF6, we performed gain and loss of function studies. Ectopic expression of wild-type TRAF6 substantially reduced PD-L1 protein abundance and further potentiated CX4945-induced PD-L1 degradation (Fig. 9J, K). In contrast, expression of an E3 ligase-dead TRAF6 mutant (C70A) profoundly impaired PD-L1 ubiquitination and largely abolished CK2 inhibitor-induced PD-L1 degradation (Fig. 9L, M), demonstrating that the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of TRAF6 is indispensable for this process. To identify the molecular determinant of TRAF6-mediated PD-L1 ubiquitination, in silico prediction using UbiBrowser identified lysine 281 (K281) of PD-L1 as a high-confidence TRAF6-targeted ubiquitination site (Fig. 9N). Structural modeling and molecular docking analyses further revealed that K281 resides in a solvent-exposed region of PD-L1, providing structural accessibility for ubiquitin conjugation (Fig. 9O). Functional validation demonstrated that substitution of K281 with alanine (K281A) markedly reduced PD-L1 ubiquitination and rendered PD-L1 resistant to both TRAF6-mediated and CK2 inhibition–induced degradation (Fig. 9P, Q). Collectively, these data uncover a previously unrecognized CK2-TRAF6-PD-L1(K281) regulatory axis, whereby CK2 inhibition upregulates and promotes TRAF6, leading to site-specific ubiquitination of PD-L1 at K281 and subsequent proteasomal degradation

Discussion

ICBs have transformed lung cancer therapy; however, durable responses remain confined to a minority of patients, underscoring the persistence of tumor-intrinsic mechanisms that drive immune suppression and adaptive resistance [35–37]. Here, we identify protein kinase CK2 as a clinically relevant and therapeutically actionable regulator of immune suppression in lung cancer. Across independent patient cohorts, elevated CK2α expression associates with inferior clinical outcomes, reduced cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, and an immune contexture indicative of impaired antitumor immunity. Mechanistically, we define a post-translational immune checkpoint in which CK2 activity sustains PD-L1 protein stability through a TRAF6-dependent ubiquitination program, thereby maintaining PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitory signaling within the TME [38]. Functionally, CK2 inhibition remodels the TME toward an immune-engaged state and sensitizes tumors to PD-L1 blockade, resulting in enhanced tumor control and, in a subset of animals, durable immunological memory.

CK2 has long been recognized as a pleiotropic oncogenic kinase with roles in inflammation and immune signaling, and prior studies have suggested indirect links between CK2 activity and immune checkpoint regulation, primarily at the transcriptional or cytokine-driven level [27–29]. In contrast, our work establishes a mechanistically resolved E3 ligase–dependent pathway in which CK2 inhibition promotes TRAF6-mediated polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of PD-L1. This axis is supported by rigorous gain and loss of function analyses demonstrating that TRAF6 is both necessary and sufficient for CK2 inhibition–induced PD-L1 destabilization, requires intact E3 ligase activity, and targets PD-L1 at a functionally essential ubiquitination site, lysine 281. These findings define a previously unrecognized post-translational checkpoint controlling PD-L1 stability and directly link CK2 catalytic activity to immune checkpoint regulation.

Transcriptomic analyses revealed that CK2 inhibition increases PD-L1 mRNA while reducing PD-L1 protein abundance, an apparent paradox resolved by a time-dependent, layered regulatory model. CK2 inhibition rapidly accelerates PD-L1 protein turnover via TRAF6-dependent ubiquitination, whereas delayed transcriptional upregulation likely reflects compensatory inflammatory or interferon-driven feedback. This uncoupling of PD-L1 transcription from protein stability underscores the dominance of post-translational control in shaping checkpoint availability within the TME.

The enhanced efficacy of PD-L1 blockade under CK2 inhibition can be explained by the dynamic and compartmentalized nature of PD-L1 biology and by immune priming effects of CK2 targeting. PD-L1 expression is inducible during therapy as part of adaptive immune resistance and is not restricted to tumor cells, with host myeloid and stromal PD-L1 contributing to immune suppression [39–41]. Moreover, CK2 inhibition increases T-cell infiltration and effector function, rendering PD-1/PD-L1 signaling a dominant negative feedback pathway and thereby amplifying the functional impact of checkpoint blockade. Consistently, reduced PD-1 expression on tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells reflects relief from chronic antigen stimulation and restoration of effector or memory-like states, rather than direct repression of PD-1 expression.

Although CK2 inhibition destabilizes PD-L1, PD-1 blockade remains a plausible alternative combination partner. We selected PD-L1 blockade because PD-L1 operates as a multi-compartment checkpoint and participates in broader inhibitory circuitry, including interactions with CD80 [42, 43], but determining the optimal checkpoint partner for CK2 inhibition remains an important translational direction.

To ensure pharmacologic rigor, we employed three structurally distinct CK2 inhibitors—TBB [29], CX4945 [44], and SGC-CK2-1 [45]—spanning historical, clinical, and highly selective chemical probes. The convergence of mechanistic, immunologic, and therapeutic outcomes across these compounds minimizes the likelihood of off-target–driven artifacts. While our analyses focused on CK2α as the catalytic and clinically interpretable subunit, future studies should examine the contribution of CK2β and CK2α′ to immune regulation and holoenzyme function [46, 47].

Notably, several experimental models harbor activating KRAS mutations, and human data indicate that CK2α expression is elevated in KRAS-mutant lung cancers, which often exhibit high PD-L1 yet immune-cold phenotypes. These findings suggest that CK2 may act as a cooperating node within oncogene-driven immunosuppressive networks and that CK2 inhibition may be particularly effective as an immune-priming strategy in this context.

This study has limitations. Clinical correlative analyses are constrained by cohort size and residual confounding, and predictive claims in immunotherapy-treated subsets require validation in larger, prospective datasets. Subcutaneous models do not fully recapitulate human tumor heterogeneity or spatial immune architecture, and immune memory analyses remain underpowered. Moreover, immune exclusion is a strictly spatial phenomenon, and its definitive assignment requires spatially resolved immunophenotyping approaches. Defining upstream drivers of CK2α upregulation and direct CK2–TRAF6 interactions will be important goals for future work. Despite these limitations, our findings establish CK2 as a central regulator of tumor immune evasion and define the CK2–TRAF6–PD-L1 axis as a mechanistically distinct, post-translational checkpoint that can be therapeutically exploited to convert immune-cold tumors and enhance the efficacy of ICBs in lung cancer.

Conclusions

In summary, this study establishes CK2 as a central regulator of immune evasion in lung cancer and defines a mechanistically resolved post-translational checkpoint—the CK2–TRAF6–PD-L1(K281) axis—that governs PD-L1 stability and shapes antitumor immunity. By linking CK2 catalytic activity to TRAF6-dependent ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of PD-L1, demonstrating consequent remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment, and showing that CK2 inhibition sensitizes tumors to PD-L1 blockade, our findings provide a strong mechanistic and preclinical rationale for CK2-targeted combination immunotherapy strategies aimed at converting immunologically “cold” lung cancers into immunotherapy-responsive disease (Fig. 10).
