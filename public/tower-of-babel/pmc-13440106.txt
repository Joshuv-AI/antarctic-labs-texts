Severity of impostor phenomenon syndrome among Iranian nursing and allied health students
Authors: Mahla Kermani, Reyhane Sefidkar, Fatemeh Keshmiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Impostor Phenomenon Syndrome (IPS) is characterized by persistent self-doubt and fear of being exposed as intellectually fraudulent despite objective evidence of competence. Increasing evidence suggests that IP is common among health professions students, and demanding academic environments, together with early clinical exposure, have been associated with greater psychological distress. This study examined the severity of impostor phenomenon among undergraduate nursing and allied health students at Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences, Yazd, Iran.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in 2025 among 300 undergraduate students enrolled in Nursing, Midwifery, Operating Room Technology, Anesthesia Technology, and Medical Laboratory Sciences who had completed at least one clinical internship. These disciplines represent four-year undergraduate allied health degree programs. Participants were selected using stratified random sampling according to academic discipline. Impostor phenomenon was measured using the 20-item Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale (CIPS), which assesses three dimensions: Fear of being exposed as a fraud (8 items), Discounting achievements (9 items), and Attributing success to luck (3 items). The total CIPS score ranges from 20 to 100 and is categorized as follows: 40 ≤ Mild IPS, 41–60 Moderate IPS, 61–80 Severe IPS, 80 > Very severe IPS. Group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t tests, one-way ANOVA, or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Associations between participant characteristics and impostor severity were explored using an ordinal logistic regression model. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsBased on the conventional CIPS severity categories, 48.7% of students (n = 146) were classified as having severe IP symptoms, whereas 36.3% (109) experienced moderate symptoms. Only 7.0% of participants (n = 21) were classified as having mild symptoms, while 8.0% were categorized as having very severe symptoms (n = 24). The mean total CIPS score was 62.43 ± 14.06, placing the group means at the lower threshold of the severe symptom category. Among the CIPS dimensions, the highest mean score was observed for discounting achievements (28.26 ± 6.97), followed by fear of being exposed as a fraud (25.86 ± 5.69) and attributing success to luck (8.31 ± 2.81). Overall differences in impostor scores across disciplines were modest (p-value = 0.04), and analyses identified significant differences only for the attributing success to luck dimension (p-value = 0.01). Compared with Nursing students (reference category), Midwifery students (p = 0.004) and Anesthesia Technology students (p = 0.002) had significantly higher odds of belonging to higher impostor phenomenon severity categories. Operating Room Technology students showed a borderline association with higher severity (0.05), whereas no significant differences were observed for Medical Laboratory Sciences students compared with nursing (p > 0.05).ConclusionImpostor phenomenon was highly prevalent among undergraduate nursing and allied health students, with the overall mean score falling within the severe range. Compared with Nursing students, those in Midwifery and Anesthesia Technology had significantly higher odds of experiencing more severe impostor phenomenon. However, the regression model explained only a small proportion of the variability, indicating that demographic and academic characteristics alone are insufficient to explain impostor experiences. These findings provide a foundation for future multicenter longitudinal and intervention studies to identify modifiable educational factors associated with impostor phenomenon and to evaluate strategies for reducing impostor-related distress during clinical training.Trial Registration Number (TRN)None.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-05116-7.Keywords: Impostor phenomenon, Clance impostor phenomenon scale, Nursing students, Allied health students, Operating room technology, Anesthesia technology, Medical laboratory sciences, Medical education, Clinical training, Iran

Introduction

The Impostor Phenomenon (IP) refers to a persistent psychological experience in which capable individuals doubt their competence and fear being exposed as intellectually or professionally fraudulent despite objective evidence of success [1]. Rather than reflecting actual deficiencies in ability, IP is characterized by maladaptive cognitive appraisals, including chronic self-doubt, attribution of success to external factors such as luck, and discounting personal achievements [2]. These distorted attributional patterns have been associated with adverse psychological outcomes, including anxiety, depression, emotional exhaustion, reduced self-esteem, and professional burnout [3].

From a theoretical perspective, Social Cognitive Theory [4] provides a useful framework for understanding the development of IP. Individuals with low perceived self-efficacy tend to underestimate their competence despite objective accomplishments, resulting in persistent self-doubt and maladaptive attribution styles. Similarly, the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping [5, 6] suggests that psychological distress arises when individuals appraise environmental demands as exceeding their perceived coping resources. In clinical education, frequent evaluations, high academic expectations, responsibility for patient care, and continuous performance comparisons may therefore intensify impostor feelings among health professions students by reinforcing perceptions of inadequacy despite successful performance.

Consistent with these conceptual perspectives, accumulating evidence indicates that the Impostor Phenomenon is highly prevalent across higher education and health professions training. A systematic review by Bravata et al. (2020) [3] demonstrated that IP occurs across a wide range of educational and professional settings and is consistently associated with adverse psychological outcomes, including anxiety, depression, emotional exhaustion, burnout, reduced well-being, and impaired occupational functioning. More recently, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Salary et al. (2025) [7] estimated that approximately 62% (95% CI: 52.6–70.6) of healthcare professionals experience impostor syndrome, with low self-esteem, anxiety, depression, perceived stress, and burnout identified as its principal correlates. Likewise, Jansson et al. (2025) [8] reported that nearly two-thirds of university students experience frequent or intense impostor feelings, suggesting that IP represents a common psychological challenge throughout higher education rather than an isolated phenomenon confined to specific disciplines.

Among students in health professions education, impostor experiences may be particularly salient because clinical training requires learners to integrate theoretical knowledge with patient care while being continuously observed and evaluated by clinical instructors and healthcare professionals. This transition from classroom learning to authentic clinical practice occurs within complex learning environments characterized by high academic expectations, frequent performance assessment, and increasing professional responsibility. These circumstances have been hypothesized to foster maladaptive attributional patterns whereby successful performance is attributed to external factors such as luck or assistance, whereas routine mistakes are interpreted as evidence of personal inadequacy. Although these mechanisms have not been directly examined in most cross-sectional studies, they are consistent with theoretical models of IP and may contribute to lower academic self-efficacy, maladaptive perfectionism, social anxiety, academic burnout, reduced psychological safety, and difficulties in professional identity formation [3, 8].

Although research on IP has expanded substantially over the past decade, evidence from the Middle East—and particularly from Iran—remains comparatively limited. Nevertheless, emerging Iranian studies indicate that impostor experiences are also prevalent within local academic settings and are associated with important psychological and educational outcomes. Haghighi et al. (2022) [9] demonstrated that impostor phenomenon mediated the relationship between academic self-concept and academic self-efficacy among Iranian postgraduate students, highlighting its role in students’ academic adjustment. Doostani et al. (2025) [10] further showed that family dynamics, educational expectations, and perceived obligations toward supportive academic environments contribute to the development of impostor feelings among Iranian postgraduate students. In addition, Bagheri et al. (2024) [11] reported in a randomized controlled trial that Acceptance and Commitment Therapy significantly reduced impostor symptoms, perceived stress, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms among Iranian nursing students with perfectionistic tendencies, underscoring both the clinical relevance and modifiability of this construct within Iranian health professions education. Earlier Iranian evidence has likewise documented the presence of impostor feelings among medical students [12], further supporting the relevance of this phenomenon within the national educational context.

Despite these important contributions, contemporary evidence regarding impostor phenomenon among undergraduate nursing and allied health students in Iran remains scarce. Most available Iranian studies have focused on postgraduate students, medical students, single academic disciplines, or intervention-based research [10, 11], leaving limited evidence regarding undergraduate students undertaking clinical education across multiple health professions. This gap is particularly important because undergraduate nursing, midwifery, operating room technology, anesthesia technology, and medical laboratory sciences students are educated within hierarchical clinical learning environments where training is commonly delivered through physician-led teams and discipline-specific supervisory structures [13–15]. Although such contextual characteristics have been proposed to influence professional identity development, feedback experiences, and psychological safety, their relationship with the impostor phenomenon has not been adequately investigated and cannot be inferred from existing cross-sectional evidence. Moreover, these disciplines differ substantially in their clinical responsibilities, patient contact, supervisory models, and opportunities for autonomous participation in patient care, suggesting that students’ experiences of impostor phenomenon may not be uniform across programs. Understanding whether impostor experiences vary across undergraduate health professions is, therefore, essential for informing future hypothesis-driven educational research and developing contextually appropriate strategies to support students’ psychological well-being and professional development. Accordingly, the present study aimed to determine the prevalence and severity of impostor phenomenon among undergraduate nursing and allied health students at Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences, Yazd, Iran, and to compare impostor experiences across five undergraduate health disciplines.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted in 2025 at Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences, Yazd, Iran. The study is reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline.

Educational context

Undergraduate clinical education at Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences is primarily delivered through hospital-based clerkships under faculty supervision. Clinical training occurs within a hierarchical educational structure in which students receive discipline-specific instruction and clinical assessment, while opportunities for structured interprofessional learning are relatively limited, consistent with previous descriptions of clinical education in similar settings (14–19]. This educational environment was considered relevant because frequent performance evaluation, direct observation, and increasing clinical responsibility may influence students’ perceptions of competence.

Participants

The study population comprised undergraduate students enrolled in the Nursing, Midwifery, Operating Room Technology, Anesthesia Technology, and Medical Laboratory Sciences programs at Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences, Yazd, Iran. These are four-year undergraduate degree programs offered by the Schools of Nursing and Allied Health. According to the national undergraduate curriculum, clinical education begins in the second academic year.

Eligible participants were actively enrolled in one of the five academic programs during the study period, had completed at least one clinical internship rotation, and provided informed consent to participate. Students were excluded if they had not yet commenced clinical training, declined participation, or returned questionnaires with more than 10% missing responses.

The sampling frame was derived from official student enrollment lists obtained from the University’s Educational Affairs Office. A proportionate stratified random sampling strategy was employed, with each academic discipline considered a separate stratum. Within each stratum, eligible students were selected using computer-generated random numbers in proportion to the number of enrolled students, thereby ensuring representative sampling across all academic disciplines.

The required sample size was estimated using the mean (51.83) and standard deviation (12.77) reported in a previous study [20]. Assuming a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96) and a precision of 1.5 score units, the minimum required sample size was calculated using the formula n = (Z × SD / d)², yielding 279 participants. After allowing for an anticipated 5% non-response rate, the required sample size increased to 293 participants. To ensure adequate representation across all five academic disciplines and to compensate for potential exclusions due to incomplete responses, 330 questionnaires were distributed. Of these, 300 were returned with sufficient data for analysis, yielding a response rate of 90.90%.

Instrument

Impostor phenomenon was assessed using the Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale (CIPS) [2], a 20-item self-report questionnaire scored on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not at all true”) to 5 (“very true”). The CIPS evaluates three dimensions: Fear of being exposed as a fraud (8 items), Discounting achievements (9 items), and Attributing success to luck (3 items). Previous studies have demonstrated excellent internal consistency for the original instrument (Cronbach’s α ≈ 0.90) [21].

The Persian version of the Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale (CIPS), translated and psychometrically validated by Mehrabizadeh Honarmand (2005)[22] and Shahni Yeilagh (2007)[23], was used in this study. The Persian version has previously demonstrated satisfactory validity and reliability for use in Iranian populations. In the present study, internal consistency was excellent, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.92 for the total scale. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the three subscales were 0.84 for ‘fear of being exposed as a fraud’, 0.87 for ‘discounting achievements’, and 0.73 for ‘attributing success to luck’, indicating acceptable to good internal consistency across all dimensions.

The total CIPS score ranges from 20 to 100 and is categorized as follows: 40 ≤ Mild impostor symptoms, 41–60 Moderate impostor symptoms, 61–80 Severe impostor symptoms, 80 > Very severe impostor symptoms [1]. Although CIPS scores are frequently interpreted using the categories proposed by Clance (1985) (mild, moderate, severe, and very severe), impostor phenomenon is fundamentally a continuous psychological construct. Therefore, these categorical thresholds were used solely for descriptive purposes and should not be interpreted as diagnostic cut-off values.

Data collection

Data were collected using self-administered paper-based questionnaires. The principal investigator attended the participating faculties and affiliated teaching hospitals to explain the study objectives and answer participants’ questions. After providing written informed consent, participants completed the anonymous questionnaires independently in the researcher’s presence. Completion required approximately 10–15 min. No identifying information was recorded, and completed questionnaires were coded before data entry to ensure confidentiality.

A total of 330 questionnaires were distributed, of which 300 were completed and included in the final analysis. Ten questionnaires were excluded because participants either declined to complete the survey or returned questionnaires with substantial missing data.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), as appropriate, whereas categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages.

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, supplemented by visual inspection of Q–Q plots and evaluation of skewness and kurtosis. Variables meeting the assumptions of normality were compared using the independent-samples t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). In the present study, total CIPS scores and subscale scores compared across Clinical training levels were non-normally distributed; therefore, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for these analyses, whereas comparisons across gender and academic disciplines satisfied normality assumptions and were analyzed using parametric methods.

To identify factors associated with the severity of impostor phenomenon, an ordinal logistic regression model (proportional odds model with a logit link) was fitted using the conventional four-level CIPS severity categories as the dependent variable. Predictor variables included age, gender, academic discipline, and clinical training level (junior versus senior). The proportional odds assumption was evaluated using the Test of Parallel Lines. The goodness of fit of the model was assessed through deviance statistics, and multicollinearity was checked using variance inflation factors (VIFs). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences, Yazd, Iran (Approval No. IR.SSU.SRH.REC.1404.023). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. Participation was voluntary, confidentiality was maintained throughout the study, and participants could withdraw at any stage without academic consequences.

Discussion

The present study examined the severity of impostor phenomenon among undergraduate students enrolled in five allied health disciplines following their entry into clinical training. Overall, the findings indicate that impostor experiences were common across all disciplines, with average scores falling within the conventional range of severe IP symptoms according to the Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale. Because these severity categories are based on conventional score cut-offs rather than empirically validated clinical thresholds, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting elevated levels of impostor experiences rather than a clinical diagnosis. This distinction is important because IP is increasingly conceptualized as a continuous psychological construct rather than a categorical condition [3].

The predominance of the “discounting achievements” subscale suggests that many students tended to minimize or discount their accomplishments despite objective evidence of academic progression. This attributional pattern is consistent with the cognitive mechanisms proposed in Clance’s original conceptualization of impostor phenomenon, in which successful individuals systematically attribute achievements to external circumstances or regard them as insufficient indicators of competence [1, 2]. Rather than reflecting isolated concerns about performance, these findings indicate a broader tendency toward maladaptive self-evaluation that may accompany professional identity formation during clinical education.

These findings can be interpreted within Social Cognitive Theory, which emphasizes reciprocal interactions among personal beliefs, environmental experiences, and behavioral responses [4]. During the transition from classroom learning to clinical education, students encounter unfamiliar professional responsibilities, frequent performance evaluations, and increased exposure to expert supervisors. Such experiences may influence students’ self-efficacy beliefs and contribute to persistent self-doubt in some individuals, although the present cross-sectional design does not permit conclusions regarding temporal or causal relationships. Similarly, the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping [5, 6] provides a useful framework for understanding how clinical learning environments may be appraised as psychologically demanding when students perceive discrepancies between perceived competence and external expectations. Under this framework, impostor experiences may represent one cognitive response to challenging educational circumstances rather than an inevitable consequence of clinical training.

The overall pattern observed in the present study is broadly consistent with evidence indicating that impostor experiences are highly prevalent among health professions students. In a systematic review, Bravata et al. (2020)[3] reported prevalence estimates ranging from 9% to 82%, depending on the population studied and the assessment criteria employed. More recently, Jansson et al. (2025)[8] reported that approximately two-thirds of university students and Salari et al. (2025)[7] reported 62% health service providers experienced frequent or intense impostor feelings. Although direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because of differences in sampling strategies, educational systems, and measurement approaches, the relatively elevated scores observed in the present study appear compatible with the growing international recognition that impostor experiences represent an important challenge during professional education.

Previous Iranian investigations have primarily focused on medical students or general university populations and have reported substantial levels of impostor experiences associated with psychological distress and reduced academic self-confidence [12]. By examining multiple allied health disciplines simultaneously, the present study contributes additional evidence from an educational context that has received comparatively little empirical attention and responds to recent recommendations advocating broader investigation of the impostor phenomenon across different health professions [3].

Although statistically significant differences in overall impostor phenomenon scores were observed across academic disciplines, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The only subscale demonstrating significant between-discipline differences was Attributing Success to Luck, whereas the other dimensions did not differ significantly. Furthermore, the multivariable ordinal logistic regression model explained only a small proportion of the variance in impostor severity, indicating that academic discipline alone is unlikely to account for substantial differences in students’ impostor experiences. Accordingly, the observed associations should be regarded as exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Students in Anesthesia Technology exhibited higher scores on the ‘attributing success to luck’ subscale than Nursing students. One possible explanation is that differences in clinical learning experiences, perceived responsibility, or assessment practices across educational programs may influence attributional styles. However, because these contextual characteristics were not directly measured, the present findings do not permit conclusions regarding the mechanisms underlying these differences. Future studies incorporating measures of clinical supervision, psychological safety, educational climate, and perceived autonomy are required to clarify whether such contextual factors contribute to discipline-related variation in impostor experiences.

One of the principal findings was that academic discipline was the only measured variable associated with the severity of impostor phenomenon after adjustment for age, gender, and clinical training level. However, the overall explanatory capacity of the ordinal regression model was modest, indicating that the demographic and academic characteristics included in the model accounted for only a small proportion of the variability in impostor experiences. This finding suggests that factors not assessed in the present study—including perfectionism, psychological safety, supervisory relationships, educational climate, and coping strategies—are likely to contribute more substantially to impostor phenomenon than demographic characteristics alone.

Among the included disciplines, students in Anesthesia Technology and Midwifery demonstrated greater odds of reporting more severe impostor experiences than Nursing students. Although the present study was not designed to investigate mechanisms underlying these differences, several contextual characteristics of clinical education may warrant future investigation. Within the Iranian healthcare education system, students in anesthesiology, midwifery, and, to some extent, operating room technology commonly undertake clinical training within physician-led multidisciplinary teams characterized by relatively hierarchical supervisory structures. Compared with some other allied health disciplines, these educational settings may offer fewer opportunities for independent clinical decision-making and professional autonomy during undergraduate training structures [13–15]. Previous educational literature has suggested that limited autonomy, evaluation-intensive learning environments, and reduced psychological safety may contribute to self-doubt and fear of negative evaluation, which are central features of impostor phenomenon structures [3]. Nevertheless, because none of these environmental characteristics were directly measured in the present study, these interpretations should be regarded solely as hypotheses requiring empirical confirmation rather than explanations supported by the current findings.

The pattern observed among Operating Room Technology students was broadly consistent with this interpretation, although the association was only of borderline statistical significance. Consequently, the present findings are insufficient to conclude that hierarchical clinical environments are responsible for discipline-specific differences in impostor experiences. Rather, they suggest that characteristics of the clinical learning environment deserve further investigation using multicenter longitudinal studies incorporating direct measures of supervision quality, psychological safety, learner autonomy, and educational climate.

The findings for Midwifery students also require cautious interpretation because this discipline had the smallest sample size in the study. Consequently, the estimated association may be less precise than those observed for the larger discipline groups and should therefore be replicated in larger multicenter samples before firm conclusions are drawn.

Finally, neither age, gender, nor stage of clinical training was significantly associated with impostor phenomenon severity after adjustment for discipline. Importantly, the absence of statistically significant associations should not be interpreted as evidence that these characteristics do not influence impostor experiences. Given the unequal distribution of students across clinical training levels, the relatively smaller number of senior students, and the cross-sectional design, the present study may have had limited power to detect modest differences across stages of clinical training. Longitudinal studies following students throughout undergraduate education are therefore needed to determine whether impostor experiences change over time and to identify the psychological and educational factors that shape these trajectories.

The relatively limited contribution of demographic and academic variables observed in the present study is consistent with an emerging body of evidence suggesting that impostor phenomenon is influenced more strongly by psychological and contextual characteristics than by basic demographic factors. Bravata et al. (2020)[3] concluded that associations between age, gender, and academic performance are generally inconsistent across studies, whereas variables such as perfectionism, maladaptive attributional style, anxiety, and low self-efficacy demonstrate more robust relationships with impostor experiences. Likewise, Mak et al. (2019)[24] reported that self-critical perfectionism and reduced psychological resilience were substantially stronger correlates of impostor feelings than demographic characteristics among university students.

These observations are also compatible with contemporary conceptualizations of impostor phenomenon as a multidimensional construct arising from interactions among individual cognitive processes, social comparison, and educational context rather than from demographic characteristics alone [3, 25]. Accordingly, the limited explanatory power of the present regression model suggests that future research should move beyond demographic predictors and incorporate theoretically informed constructs, including perfectionism, self-efficacy, supervisory relationships, feedback quality, psychological safety, coping strategies, and organizational support. Such variables are more closely aligned with current theoretical models and may explain substantially greater variation in impostor experiences among health professions students.

The absence of statistically significant differences across stages of clinical training should likewise be interpreted cautiously. Because this cross-sectional study compared different cohorts rather than following the same students over time, the findings cannot determine whether impostor experiences change throughout professional training. Moreover, unequal sample sizes across clinical training levels and the non-normal distribution of scores may have reduced statistical power to detect smaller differences. Consequently, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence that clinical experience does not influence the impostor phenomenon. Instead, longitudinal studies are needed to examine how impostor experiences evolve during clinical education and professional identity formation.

Comparison with the literature also warrants careful interpretation. Studies conducted among health professions students in North America and Europe have generally reported mean Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale scores ranging from approximately 55 to 61 points. For example, Villwock et al. (2016)[26] reported a mean score of approximately 60 among U.S. medical students, whereas Levant et al. (2020)[27] observed average scores in the mid-50s among allied health trainees. Although the mean score observed in the present study appears somewhat higher, direct comparisons should be made cautiously because differences in educational systems, cultural expectations, admission processes, and assessment methods may influence reported scores. Cross-cultural comparative studies using standardized methodologies are therefore needed before firm conclusions regarding national or cultural differences can be drawn.

Practical implications for clinical education

The present findings have several implications for health professions education; however, these implications should be regarded as priorities for future intervention research rather than evidence-based recommendations, given the cross-sectional design of the study. First, the consistently elevated impostor scores observed across all disciplines suggest that routine educational monitoring of students’ psychological experiences during clinical training may be valuable. Integrating validated screening instruments within student support services could facilitate the early identification of individuals experiencing persistent self-doubt and maladaptive attributional patterns.

Second, the limited explanatory value of demographic and academic variables indicates that future interventions should focus primarily on potentially modifiable educational and psychological factors rather than on student characteristics alone. Variables such as feedback quality, supervisory relationships, psychological safety, self-efficacy, perfectionism, and coping strategies have demonstrated stronger associations with impostor phenomenon in previous studies [3, 25] and therefore represent promising targets for future educational interventions.

Third, although discipline-related differences were observed, the small effect size and limited explanatory power of the regression model suggest that these findings should not be interpreted as justification for discipline-specific educational policies. Instead, future multicenter intervention studies should first determine whether educational needs differ meaningfully across allied health disciplines before recommending tailored curricula or profession-specific support programs.

Finally, educational strategies such as structured formative feedback, faculty development in supportive supervision, reflective learning activities, and peer-support initiatives may represent potentially beneficial approaches for reducing impostor experiences. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of these strategies should be established through prospective intervention studies before their routine implementation within undergraduate health professions curricula.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding temporal sequence or causality. Consequently, the study cannot determine whether impostor experiences preceded clinical education, developed during clinical training, or were influenced by other educational or personal experiences. Likewise, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence that characteristics of the clinical learning environment caused impostor experiences. Longitudinal studies are required to clarify developmental trajectories and causal relationships.

Second, although proportional stratified random sampling was used and the response rate was high (90.90%), the study was conducted at a single university. Educational culture, assessment practices, and clinical supervision may differ across institutions, limiting the generalizability of the findings. In addition, information regarding students who declined participation was unavailable, preventing assessment of potential non-response bias.

Third, all variables were collected using self-administered questionnaires, introducing the possibility of common method bias. Although anonymous data collection likely reduced response distortion, social desirability bias cannot be excluded because participants may have underreported psychologically distressing experiences or responded according to perceived academic or professional expectations.

Fourth, despite proportional sampling, participant numbers differed across academic disciplines, particularly in the Midwifery program. Consequently, subgroup comparisons may have had limited statistical power, and the relatively higher mean scores observed in smaller groups should be interpreted cautiously until replicated in larger multicenter studies. Similarly, comparisons across stages of clinical training should be interpreted with caution because the unequal distribution of senior and junior students, together with the non-normal distribution of several variables, may have reduced the ability to detect potentially meaningful differences. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant differences across stages of clinical training should not be interpreted as evidence that clinical experience has no relationship with impostor phenomenon.

Fifth, although the Persian version of the Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale has demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties in Iranian populations and showed excellent internal consistency in the present study, its measurement properties have not been specifically established among undergraduate health students. Moreover, the commonly used CIPS severity categories (mild, moderate, severe, and very severe) are primarily interpretive classifications rather than empirically derived diagnostic thresholds. Because impostor phenomenon is inherently a continuous psychological construct, categorizing continuous scores into discrete severity levels may reduce measurement precision and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Sixth, the multivariable models explained only a small proportion of the variability in impostor scores, indicating that the demographic and academic characteristics measured in this study are unlikely to represent the principal determinants of impostor experiences. Important constructs such as perfectionism, psychological safety, quality of clinical supervision, educational climate, feedback experiences, resilience, coping strategies, and organizational support were not assessed and may account for substantially greater variation. Future research should therefore prioritize these theoretically relevant variables rather than relying primarily on demographic characteristics.

Finally, although differences in impostor scores were observed across academic disciplines, the study did not include qualitative data to explain the mechanisms underlying these differences. Consequently, interpretations regarding possible influences of educational context, supervisory practices, hierarchical clinical environments, or professional culture remain speculative and should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Future multicenter longitudinal mixed-methods studies are needed to better understand how clinical learning environments shape impostor experiences and to evaluate the effectiveness of targeted educational interventions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that elevated impostor phenomenon scores were common among undergraduate students enrolled in Nursing, Midwifery, Operating Room Technology, Anesthesia Technology, and Medical Laboratory Sciences after entering clinical education. Differences in overall impostor scores were observed across disciplines; however, statistically significant differences were primarily attributable to the ‘Attributing Success to Luck’ dimension, and the multivariable model explained only a small proportion of the variability in impostor severity. These findings suggest that demographic and academic characteristics alone are insufficient to explain individual differences in impostor experiences.

Overall, the findings support the need for future research examining psychological and educational determinants of impostor phenomenon, particularly perfectionism, self-efficacy, psychological safety, feedback quality, and supervisory relationships. Prospective multicenter studies and intervention trials are needed to determine whether modifications to educational environments can reduce impostor experiences and improve students’ psychological well-being and professional development during clinical training.
