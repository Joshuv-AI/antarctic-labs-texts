Knowledge, attitude, and practice of clinicians regarding antimicrobial stewardship: a cross-sectional study in a tertiary hospital of East China
Authors: Yonghui Ma, Yujie Xia, Xing Zhang, Xia Yue, Jixia Wang, Li Kong, Yusen Li, Ning Li, Juan Wang, Xiujuan Meng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance (AR) is an increasingly significant public health challenge worldwide owing to its negative impact on morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. Antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) is an important method for curbing AR. The knowledge-attitude-practice (KAP) model is a well-established theoretical framework applied to individual behavioral changes. There are some studies propose the challenges of AMS in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs). In December 2023, we conducted a cross-sectional survey among clinicians in a tertiary hospital of East China to assess their level of KAP toward AMS, using a validated electronic questionnaire. Of the 336 respondents, 329 were considered effective after screening, yielding a recovery rate of 97.9%. Clinicians scored higher in overall knowledge (43.6 ± 5.4) and attitude (44.3 ± 4.9), older and more experienced clinicians tended to have higher knowledge and attitude scores (P < 0.05). The content of antimicrobial surveillance, such as clinical use and bacterial drug resistance, had low scores. The overall practice score (41.8 ± 5.0) was low and the practice scores of clinicians in surgical departments and intensive care units were higher than those in other departments (P < 0.05). Clinicians resort to irrational antibiotic use because of concerns regarding adverse events. The correlation analysis of KAP with AMS revealed that knowledge, attitude, and practice were all related (P < 0.05). Our study identified the key populations and priorities for AMS education and training. Urgent measures are needed to improve clinicians’ practices in AMS and mitigate irrational antibiotic use. In order to strengthen the AMS, improving knowledge itself is not enough, both hospitals and national governments need to provide corresponding support and guarantees.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AR) is an increasingly significant public health challenge worldwide owing to its negative impact on morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs [1]. Consequently, AR is increasingly recognized as the next pandemic if proactive measures are not taken to address the escalating issue [2]. Widespread use and inappropriate prescription of antibiotics are major contributors to AR [3, 4]. Antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) is an important method for curbing AR. Some studies have proposed challenges of AMS in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs). For example, lack of governmental and political commitment, lack of knowledge in microbiology and antibacterial drugs, inadequate funding of healthcare, absence of health insurance, overcrowded healthcare systems, absence of electronic record systems, limited knowledge of infectious diseases, shortage of medicines and professional talents [5, 6].

A multi-center qualitative study conducted in Pakistan found that lack of knowledge about AMS and rational use of antibiotics are the main drivers of irrational antibiotic prescription and the development of AR [7]. One pivotal initiative in combating antimicrobial resistance globally was the development of the Global Action Plan by the World Health Organization in 2015 [8]. The primary objective of action should be to enhance awareness and comprehension of antimicrobial resistance among all individuals, particularly healthcare professionals in medical facilities, through education and training efforts. In 2016, China launched the National Action Plan to Curb Bacterial Resistance (2016–2020) to advocate for the judicious use of antibiotics [9]. In 2020, the National Health Commission issued a notice on Continuing to do a good job in the clinical application management of antibiotics. Furthermore, in 2022, the National Action Plan for the Containment of Bacterial Resistance (2022–2025) was introduced to promote the rational use of antibiotics.

Antimicrobial stewardship (AMS), which comprises a coherent set of actions, promotes the optimal use of antibiotics to ensure sustainable access to effective therapy. It stands as a cornerstone of any national policy and serves as the foundation of a hospital’s AMS program, aiming to optimize antimicrobial use and patient outcomes [10]. The knowledge-attitude-practice (KAP) model is a well-established theoretical framework applied to individual behavior change, including three continuous processes: knowledge acquisition, attitude or belief formation, and behavior change [11]. Much of the literature on antimicrobial KAP has focused on prescribing antibiotics [12] and antimicrobial resistance [13, 14]. Research on AMS has predominantly involved participants such as community pharmacists [15–17] (Pakistan, China, Australia), pharmacy staff [18] (China), nonhealthcare students [19] (Zambia), physicians in public county hospitals and township health institutions [20] (China), and nurses [21] (Norway).

China, one of the LMICs with the highest antibiotic consumption, has implemented AMS initiatives for several years. There is a noticeable absence of systematic research documenting the KAP of clinicians regarding AMS in China. Clinicians play a pivotal role in AMS, and fostering good KAP among them is crucial for enhancing future antimicrobial use and combating antimicrobial resistance.The objective of this study was to evaluate the AMS knowledge, attitude and practice of clinicians in a tertiary hospital in East China.This will also help the policymakers understand the barriers associated with the successful implementation of AMS.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting, and participants

We conducted a cross-sectional study in December 2023 among clinicians employed at a tertiary hospital in East China. This hospital is a third-grade class general hospital that integrates medical treatment, teaching, scientific research, preventive healthcare, and guidance at the grassroots level. Currently, the hospital comprises two areas with a total of 4100 open beds, encompassing 82 clinical departments and 15 intensive care units. It shoulders medical and healthcare responsibilities for more than 20 million individuals. Annually, the hospital accommodates four million outpatient visits and 200,000 inpatients, with an average length of stay of 6.4 days. It has 2093 registered physicians, including 567 internists, 474 surgeons, and 57 intensive care unit physicians.

Since the implementation of the long-term mechanism of AMS, the hospital has done a lot of work. The “Management System for Clinical Application of Antibiotics” has been formulated, and the AMS working group has been established, including medical department, nursing department, infection prevention and control department, pharmacy department, inspection department, information center and other clinical departments.Each department has its own responsibility.Simultaneously,a series of antimicrobial management training studies were conducted. In addition, the hospital is the provincial medical regional center of Shandong Province and has organized medical institutions at all levels in the region to hold the Multidisciplinary Scientific Management Seminar (AMS-MDT) every quarter since 2019.

The variables assessed in this study included clinicians’ KAP regarding AMS. This study included clinicians working in medicine, surgery, and their affiliated departments. Registered clinicians on duty at the time of the survey and voluntarily agreed to participate were included. Clinicians who were away from the study or on leave during the study, training, internship, or survey period were excluded from the study.

Questionnaire design and grading method

We conducted an extensive literature review using keywords related to AMS, knowledge, attitude, and practices, to generate a pool of questions [22, 23].With reference to relevant policy literature from the state and Shandong Province, including government websites and official websites such as the Health and Health Commission, we compiled an initial questionnaire based on literature retrieval and policy documents. To improve and modify the initial questionnaire, we organized experts familiar with the field of antimicrobial management, such as medical management, pharmaceutical management, hospital infection management, microbiology, infectious disease physicians, and other clinical physicians. Additionally, based on the results of the pretest involving randomly selected 20 clinicians, some words and expressions in the questionnaires were modified,and a questionnaire on knowledge and practice of AMS for clinicians was developed, covering assessment for KAP, with a total of 30 items consisting of two parts: part 1 included the characteristics of participants,and part 2 included the KAP entries (Fig. 1). A Likert five-level scoring system (1–5 points) was used. The pre-test indicated that the average time taken to complete the questionnaire was 5–10 min. Sample size determination

Physicians working in the examination sections were excluded as they do not routinely prescribe antibiotics of clinicians was 1407. The sample size was calculated using the online sample size calculator Raosoft (http://www.raosoft.com/samplesize.html).The sample size was for 1407 physicians was 282, with a 95% confidence interval, 50% response rate,and 5% margin of error; the minimum required number was determined to be 311, assuming a 10% non-response rate.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Hospital Ethics Committee (2024–01-C029).Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before they completed the questionnaire. Additionally, the participants were informed about the voluntary nature of their participation, the confidential handling of their data, and the purpose of the study. Sufficient time was provided for the participants to decide before consenting to participate.

Data collection process

The study was conducted between December 2023 and January 2024, with the assistance of the Antimicrobial Stewardship Multidisciplinary Team (AMS-MDT) conference of the Shandong Provincial Medical Regional Center and the regional contact group for antimicrobial management, a questionnaire survey was conducted using a random sampling method. The tool for collecting data was the questionnaire based in the WenJuanXing online survey service(English name ‘SurveyStar’,Changsha Ranxing Science and Technology, Shanghai,China) [24].

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (version 25). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize all parameters, including mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage. Additionally, variance analysis was used to compare the scores of the different characteristics.

The cut-off score for each question was 4.5, greater than 4.5 was considered good. A higher total score and scoring rate for each dimension indicates better performance of the research object in that particular dimension.

Discussion

The goal of this study was to comprehensively explain and analyze the KAP of clinicians in this medical institution on AMS, as well as the existing problems, in order to help summarize experience in time and to provide a basis for refining antimicrobial management and formulating applicable intervention strategies for drug resistance prevention and control. There is an unequal proportion of male and female clinicians in this study. The majority (82.4%) of clinicians were in the middle-age group (< 45 years), which is in connection with the findings from similar previous studies [24]. More than 80% of the clinicians surveyed held at least a master’s degree as their highest level of education, with over half of the respondents having at least 10 years of related working experience. A greater proportion of clinicians in our study came from internal medicine departments, which is followed by surgery departments.

In 2015, the Chinese government began to create a hierarchical medical system in public hospitals, and they successively issued a number of policy documents to strengthen the management of antimicrobial application in medical institutions. The implementation of AMS regulations had some positive impacts on the use of rational antimicrobials. The willingness and practice of doctors toward these regulations improved from 2012 to 2016 [25]. Through this study, we have discovered that some achievements have been made since the implementation of AMS, but gaps and obstacles were still observed.

Policy successes

Solid grasp of AMS theory. In this study, the overall knowledge score of clinicians in AMS was at a good level, and knowledge scores increased with age as well. Having good AMS theory was shown in clinicians with higher professional titles and longer working experience. Clinicians from intensive care units received higher scores, although there was no statistically significant difference in knowledge scores between departments. It has been found that, in a national survey among critical care physicians, 81% of them reported that AMS increases their knowledge of appropriate antimicrobial use in the intensive care unit setting [26]. Most of the high scores were related to specific requirements of national policies, including etiological examination prior to the use of antibiotics, application requirements of antibiotics for special use, and classification management of antibiotics.

Positive attitude toward AMS. Clinicians performed well in terms of AMS attitude scores, a finding that is consistent with a previous study conducted in Uganda on AMS attitudes [27]. The importance of AMS, in this study, was recognized internationally among the respondents, with older and more experienced clinicians showing better attitude scores. Clinicians generally agreed on their own responsibility in the procedures of antimicrobial management and the link between inappropriate use of antimicrobial drugs and resistance. This is because of sound policies and regulations, multidisciplinary cooperation models, and mandatory management measures.

Training of AMS-MDT. Since the implementation of the long-term mechanism of AMS, the management department has organized a lot of special activities on infection control, such as hosting several sessions of AMS-MDT. These trainings were conducted together with multiple departments, and sharing insights into infection diagnosis, infection prevention and control, and antibacterial drug reasonable use were some of the focus in these trainings. Moreover, we organized the knowledge contest on infectious disease prevention and control, particularly encouraging young people to actively participate.

Local gaps

AMS training for newly recruited clinicians or medical students should be enhanced. In this study, clinicians with low professional titles and short employment duration had lower AMS theoretical knowledge mastery scores. At present, AMS training has been incorporated into the training of residents, in addition to pre-service education. However, the learning content is relatively simple, in which the overall training related to the rational diagnosis and treatment of infectious diseases was lacking. Therefore, AMS education for newly recruited clinicians or medical students is one of our further long-term goals.

Local indicators should be more focused on the training of AMS theory. The low knowledge scores were concentrated on the relevant requirements of our hospital, such as clinical use of antibiotics in hospitals, drug-resistant bacterial infections, and overall goals for antimicrobial surveillance in hospitals. The two important pillars of AMS are surveillance of AR and antimicrobial consumption. In the future, access to antimicrobial monitoring data will be provided to clinicians, and any abnormal data will be promptly reported. This approach will enhance clinicians’ understanding of the indicators.

Clinicians should have a greater interest in the rational use of antibiotics. Research has shown that attitude is the most important factor influencing antibiotic prescribing decisions, a finding that is crucial when designing interventions to improve rational antibiotic prescribing [28]. The score of interest in the rational use of antibiotics in our study was low. The barriers of communication and cooperation between AMS-MDT and clinicians were eliminated, a sound and reasonable incentive mechanism was established, and policies were implemented flexibly in order to avoid mandatory management attacking the interests of clinicians.

The actual ability of AMS of clinicians is poor. Although clinicians are well-practiced in paying attention to adverse drug reactions and conducting etiological screening prior to the use of antibiotics [29], the overall practice score of clinicians in AMS was lower than that of knowledge and attitude. It is possible that the setting of the inverse question has an impact on the overall score of this part but, at the same time, can reflect the clinician’s neutral attitude toward negative events. Clinicians in the surgical and intensive care units had higher practice scores than physicians in internal medicine and other departments. Poor practice stems from the fear of adverse events, which results in the irrational use of antibiotics. It is important to improve the quality of doctor–patient communication as well as carry out public education activities.

Factors that contribute to the current gap may include a shortage of human resources [30], imperfect microbiology laboratories, and strained doctor–patient relationships. China has a large population base; clinicians or hospital managers only consider current interests [31], and they fear doctor–patient conflicts that are caused by adverse events, without considering the long-term effects caused by irrational use of drugs.

It is found in our study that clinicians have a high awareness of AMS, which is consistent with a nationwide questionnaire survey of clinic doctors in Japan [32]. Presently, the Diagnostic Intervention Package (DIP) payment method is implemented in China, and AMS is faced with double challenges. AMS has important value with beneficial clinical and economic impacts [33]. AMS can offset or reduce costs while improving some patient outcomes, and this can have positive impacts on the length of stay, resistance patterns, and infection incidence. More studies are needed to further enhance our understanding of AMS optimization to benefit from the positive attitude of clinicians toward this issue. KAP are related, and educational programs and feedback strategies, underpinned by robust organizational guidelines designed to promote AMS, have been shown to be effective in addressing prescription behavior among clinicians [34, 35]. While we are currently at a good cognitive stage, the formation of attitudes and change of behavior still require the joint efforts of both managers and executives. The actual barriers to clinician practice behavior in medical assistive systems should be the focus in future research, and the background and related factors need further investigation. Data-driven decision-making can be formulated through deeper research.

Conclusions

Clinicians demonstrated have a good overall cognition and positive attitude towards AMS, but they are not familiar with individual items. Although KAP are related, many clinicians exhibit fear of adverse events and use antibiotics irrationally, such as extending the course of antibiotics and empirically combining antibiotics. Our study identified the key populations and priorities for AMS education and training. Additionally, there is a pressing need to pay more attention to the practice of clinicians and to strengthen measures aimed at avoiding irrational antibiotic use. Strengthen the AMS, improving knowledge itself is not enough. The lack of motivation for physicians to change needs to be addressed through an asystematic approach.

Limitation of the survey

There are several limitations in this study: (1) : The survey was conducted at a tertiary hospital in eastern China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader Chinese clinician community. (2) As a cross-sectional study, it has inherent limitations. (3) This study uses a 5-level Likert scale, with predetermined answers, which may not fully capture the nuances of individual responses. Moreover, Likert scales only collect numerical data and may not provide a comprehensive representation of attitude and perceptions. (4) Despite adequate pre-investigation instructions, the background of the investigation could not be controlled, and some clinicians may not have a deep understanding of some problem articles. (5) The practice score section actually measures the intentions of the behavior, not the actual practices, because intentions do not always translate into intentions. Future studies should also be combined with more objective indicators to evaluate the use of antibiotics by clinicians, such as prescription comments, clinician’s correct rate of selection of antimicrobial varieties, rational rate of timing use, and intensity of use of antibiotics. Despite these limitations, this study has provided the latest insight of Clinicians Regarding AMS.
