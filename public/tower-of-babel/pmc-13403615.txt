A therapeutic relay strategy enabled by spatiotemporally programmable nanoplatforms for multilayered cardioprotection against MI/RI
Authors: Shengzhe Hou, Chen Cheng, Hongjin An, Fan Liu, Zeyan Huang, Yunfan Liu, Min Xu, Haitao Ran, Zhigang Wang, Zhiyi Zhou, Weixi Jiang, Jianli Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury (MI/RI) is driven by a cascade of pathological events, including oxidative stress and the activation of novel cell death pathways such as ferroptosis. The stage-specific shift in dominant cell death modalities coupled with the inherently low bioavailability of conventional therapeutics in the rhythmically contracting heart critically constrain the efficacy of single-target interventions. Here, we developed a spatiotemporally controllable “therapeutic relay” strategy based on a metal–phenolic network-hybridized liposomal system (MP@T NPs). During the early reperfusion phase, the tannic acid–cerium (TA–Ce) network shell exhibits reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging and anti-inflammatory activities, thereby effectively mitigating apoptosis and autophagy-associated cell death. Concurrently, its high affinity for collagen ensures the prolonged and targeted retention of the nanoparticles at the injury site. Upon the initiation of ferroptosis, ultrasound (US) irradiation subsequently induces the phase transition and vaporization of perfluoropentane (PFP), triggering the on-demand release of the arachidonate lipoxygenase (ALOX) inhibitor ML351 to precisely suppress ferroptotic cell death. This sequential action inhibits ferroptosis by downregulating ACSL4 and upregulating GPX4, thereby attenuating lipid peroxidation and restoring mitochondrial function in H9c2 cells. In the MI/RI rat model, MP@T NPs reduced ROS levels and iron deposition, suppressed inflammation, and restored the ejection fraction and fractional shortening. This novel, noninvasively regulated therapeutic platform enables temporally precise intervention in the key pathological cascades of MI/RI, offering a promising multitarget approach for enhancing myocardial salvage and functional recovery.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04587-9.Keywords: Myocardial ischemia/Reperfusion injury, Ferroptosis, Ultrasound, Metal-phenolic network, Therapeutic relay

Introduction

Myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury (MI/RI) refers to the paradoxical worsening of myocardial damage that occurs upon the restoration of blood flow following myocardial infarction [1–3]. This condition can lead to malignant arrhythmias, myocardial stunning, and microvascular dysfunction [4]. Epidemiologically, it affects a considerable proportion of patients undergoing reperfusion therapies, contributing to elevated morbidity and mortality rates [5]. The pathophysiological paradox arises from the dual role of reperfusion: while it restores oxygen and metabolic substrates to ischemic tissues, it concurrently triggers a cascade of secondary injury mechanisms, including oxidative stress, calcium overload, inflammatory responses, and the activation of multiple regulated cell death pathways [6–8]. Therefore, timely interventions targeting these pathological mechanisms are important for improving patients’ outcomes and facilitating the recovery of cardiac function [9, 10].

Although current clinical strategies for MI/RI, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory therapies, have displayed modest efficacy, the combination of diverse cell death modalities, including apoptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis, renders conventional single-target approaches ineffective [11, 12]. These therapies are unable to concurrently address multiple distinct pathological pathways, thus compromising the comprehensiveness and temporal precision of therapeutic interventions [13, 14]. Moreover, the dynamic contractile nature of the heart often leads to low bioavailability and inadequate retention of therapeutic agents [15]. More importantly, emerging evidence suggests that the predominant modes of cardiomyocyte death may vary across different temporal phases of MI/RI [16, 17]. For instance, studies have demonstrated that ferroptosis becomes the dominant form of cell death at 24 h after ischemia/reperfusion [18]. This process is driven by the synergistic effects of reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts and impaired antioxidant defenses, which disrupt the clearance of phospholipid hydroperoxides [7, 19, 20]. Concurrently, an increased intracellular labile iron pool and progressive accumulation of lipid peroxides collectively promote ferroptosis as the primary cell death pathway [21, 22]. Under these conditions, conventional antioxidant therapies exhibit limited efficacy in inhibiting ferroptosis-specific enzymes, such as acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) [23, 24]. To date, research on the spatiotemporal regulation of distinct cell death modalities at specific stages following reperfusion injury remains limited [25]. Therefore, the development of a targeted, multimodal, sequential therapeutic strategy is essential. These approaches should enable precise intervention in key pathological processes within defined time windows, thereby achieving synergistic therapeutic outcomes through the integration of temporal precision and spatial targeting [26, 27].

In this study, we developed a metal‒phenolic network (MPN) hybrid liposome system for the “therapeutic relay” of MI/RI. The system features a liposomal core coloaded with perfluoropentane (PFP) and the arachidonate lipoxygenase (ALOX) inhibitor ML351, which is enveloped by an outer shell composed of a tannic acid–cerium (TA–Ce) MPN. TA, a natural polyphenol, can chelate various metal ions to form stable metal‒phenolic networks [28, 29]. Notably, cerium ions exhibit reversible redox cycling between Ce³⁺ (reduced state) and Ce⁴⁺ (oxidized state), conferring enzyme-mimetic activity to the network [30, 31]. This MPN efficiently scavenges ROS in the MI/RI microenvironment [32]. During the early reperfusion phase, these dual antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions suppress oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, thereby attenuating apoptosis and autophagic cell death. Moreover, the high affinity of TA for collagen facilitates the formation of a stable biological interface, increasing targeted drug retention in the myocardium [33, 34]. PFP vaporization can be triggered by the heat and pressure generated upon ultrasound (US) stimulation. Subsequently, US applied at 24 h after modeling triggers the phase-transition vaporization of PFP [35, 36], enabling the spatiotemporally controlled release of the ferroptosis inhibitor ML351. This process effectively reduces lipid peroxide accumulation and blocks ferroptosis signaling, thereby sustaining the relay-based therapeutic intervention for MI/RI. Ultimately, it enables the precise modulation of the predominant cell death modalities across distinct pathological stages. The intervention effectively attenuates oxidative stress and pathological iron accumulation, maintains mitochondrial integrity and bioenergetic output, and ultimately improves cardiac outcomes. In the acute MI/RI model (within 24–72 h post-injury), the intervention effectively mitigated the initial wave of cellular injury and death, as evidenced by significant reductions in oxidative stress markers and pathological iron accumulation within the infarct zone. At the subcellular level, mitochondrial structural integrity and respiratory function were preserved, thereby sustaining basal ATP synthesis. Concurrently, an early anti-inflammatory response was observed, characterized by significantly decreased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines—including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6—in both infarcted and border-zone myocardium. In the chronic MI/RI model (up to 14 days post-injury), the therapeutic effect translated into robust structural and functional cardiac recovery. Key outcomes included a marked reduction in infarct size (by ~75% versus controls) and substantial restoration of systolic performance, as demonstrated by significantly improved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and fractional shortening (FS). Histopathological analysis revealed attenuated interstitial fibrosis, reduced collagen deposition, and preservation of myocardial cytoarchitecture. Consistently, cardiomyocyte apoptosis was profoundly suppressed, as confirmed by decreased TUNEL-positive nuclei and sustained expression of cardiac troponin T (cTnT)—a marker of cardiomyocyte viability and structural integrity. In summary, this study presents a temporally orchestrated, multitarget therapeutic strategy based on noninvasive energy-mediated precision regulation. By simultaneously targeting key injury pathways during MI/RI, such as oxidative stress and ferroptosis, it enables the multilayered and stage-specific repair of myocardial tissue (Scheme 1).

Synthesis of MP@T NPs

MP NPs were synthesized following the previously reported thin film hydration method and phacoemulsification method. First, 6 mg DPPC, 2 mg DSPE-PEG-2000, 2 mg cholesterol, and 1 mg ML351 were added to a round-bottom flask and dissolved in 4 mL chloroform. The round-bottom flask was connected to a rotary evaporator and evaporated for 1 h at 50 °C. After dissolving in water, 100 µL PFP was added, which was first emulsified with an ultrasonic sonicator at 40 W (5 s on, 5 s off) for 6 min. MP NPs were prepared by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C in a centrifuge, discarding the supernatant, and resuspending with 2 mL water solution three times. Then, TA solution (40 µL, 40 mg/mL) and (NH4)2Ce(NO3)6 solution (160 µL, 13.2 mg/mL) were added to the MP NPs suspension under emulsification. Then, adjusting the solution pH to about 8.0 with NaOH solution (0.1 M) to form MP@T NPs. The product was washed with water three times and the concentrated suspension was stored at 4 °C. The fluorescent dyes DiI or DiR were added during the rotary evaporation to prepared DiI/DiR-MP NPs and DiI/DiR-MP@T NPs.

Characterization of MP@T NPs

Transmission electron microscopy images of MP NPs and MP@T NPs were obtained using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI TECNAI G2 12, USA). To obtain standard concentration curve for evaluating the unloaded ML351 in the supernatant of MP NPs, the peak areas of ML351 solutions at different concentrations were measured by High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) system. The entrapment efficiency and drug loading were calculated as follows: entrapment efficiency = (Weight of total ML351 – Weight of unloaded ML351)/Weight of total ML351 × 100%; drug loading efficiency = (Weight of total ML351 - Weight of unloaded ML351)/(Weight of loaded ML351, DPPC, DSPE-PEG 2000 and cholesterol) × 100%. UV-vis absorbance spectra were obtained by SpectraMax iD3. The size distributions and zeta potentials were detected by a dynamic light scattering (DLS, Malvern, NanoZS, UK). X-ray photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) was recorded on a Thermo escalab 250Xi. Fourier transform infrared spectrophotometry of MP NPs and MP@T NPs were detected by FTIR (Nicolet 6700 FTIR spectrometer, USA). The cerium (Ce) content was detected by an Inductively Coupled Plasma (Thermo ICPOES7200, USA).

Drug release

Samples of MP@T NPs were placed in 40 mL of release medium (HEPES buffer of pH = 7.0 and acetic acid-ammonium acetate buffer of pH = 5.0) and were shaken gently (150 rpm/min). At certain time points (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 12, 24 and 48 h), 1 mL fresh release media was added after collecting 1 mL release medium at the same time. Then, the content of cerium ions in the supernatant was detected by ICP. Calculate the cumulative release rate.

O2 concentration monitoring

PBS, MP NPs, TA-Ce, MP@T NPs and different concentrations (0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2 mg/mL) of MP@T NPs were added to 30% H2O2 solution respectively (2.5 mL). The oxygen (O2) concentration was recorded with a dissolved oxygen meter for 30 min.

H2O2 scavenging property

Different concentrations (31.25, 62.5, 125, 250, 500 µg/mL) of MP@T NPs were added to 1.5 mM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) respectively and then reacted for 5 h. The H2O2 scavenging capacity was determined using a H2O2 content detection kit.

•OH scavenging property

Electron paramagnetic resonance (ESR) assays were used to evaluate the hydroxyl radical (•OH) scavenging capabilities of different concentrations (250, 500 µg/mL) of MP@T NPs. 100 µL FeSO4 (5 mg/ml), 10 µL 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO, Pure), 10 µL 30% H2O2 and 80 µL MP@T NPs. Water served as the control group. The •OH were also detected by TMB color reaction. Spectrophotometric measurements were taken at 652 nm subsequently (SpectraMax iD3, USA).

O2− scavenging property

Electron paramagnetic resonance (ESR) assays were used to evaluate the superoxide anion (O2−) scavenging capabilities of different concentrations (250, 500 µg/mL) of MP@T NPs. 20 µL xanthine (10 mM), 20 µL xanthine oxidase (1 U/ml), 10 µL DMPO (200 mM) and 50 µL MP@T NPs. PBS served as the control group. The O2− scavenging ability were also detected by Superoxide Assay Kit.

Total ROS scavenging property

The Total Antioxidant Capacity Assay Kit with ABTS method was used to evaluate the total antioxidation capability of different concentrations (31.25, 62.5, 125, 250, 500 µg/mL) of MP@T NPs, following the instructions.

Cell culture and animal model establishment

Rat myocardial cells (H9c2) purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China), were cultured in Dulbecco’s modification of Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% (w/v) penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO2 environment. In some experiments, discard the old medium and replace it with glucose free DMEM medium. A disposable, oxygen-absorbing and carbon dioxide-generating pouch (AnaeroPack, Mitsubishi Gas Chemical Company) was used to create an anaerobic environment for the oxygen-glucose deprivation, and the cells were incubated for 4 h. Then the medium was replaced with high-glucose DMEM containing nanoparticles, and the H9c2 cells were returned to a normoxic incubator for an additional 4-h incubation.

Every Sprague-Dawley rats (6 weeks) was raised in a humidity range of 18 to 20 °C. Water and food were freely available. All animal experimentations were approved by the institutional animal care commission and all procedures followed the ethical guidelines established by the Chongqing Medical University Use Committee for Animal Care. All laboratory animal carcasses were cleared according to with the animal ethical clearance principle (SYXK2018-0003).

MI/RI was induced by ligation of the left anterior descending coronary artery. Briefly, rats were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg). Following tracheal intubation, the animals were mechanically ventilated with room air (respiratory rate: 70–80 breaths/min, tidal volume: 6–8 mL). A thoracotomy was performed at the fourth intercostal space to expose the left ventricular wall. The LAD was then ligated with a 6 − 0 silk suture. After 30 min of ischemia, the ligature was released to allow reperfusion. Finally, the chest was closed in layers, and the tracheal tube was removed.

Cell activity tests

H9c2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates. After 24 h of adhesion, cells were either (1) treated with different concentrations of MP@T NPs for 24 h, or (2) exposed to US irradiation (1.0 MHz, 0–1 W/cm2, 50% duty cycle, 1 min) and then incubated for 24 h. Cell viability in both settings was subsequently determined by a CCK-8 assay, with absorbance readings obtained using a multi-mode microplate reader.

Cellular uptake performance

MP NPs and MP@T NPs were labeled with DiI. H9c2 cells seeded into a 12-well plate were incubated with DiI-MP NPs and DiI-MP@T NPs (50 µg/mL) for 0, 1, 2, 4, 6 and 8 h after cell attachment. The cells were washed three times with PBS. Inverted fluorescence microscopy was used to observe, and flow cytometry was used to evaluate the internalization of DiI-MP NPs and DiI-MP@T NPs into H9c2 cells.

H9c2 cells seeded into a 6-well plate were incubated with MP@T NPs (50 µg/mL) for 0, 1, 2, 4, 6 and 8 h after cell attachment. The cells were washed three times with PBS. Intracellular Ce levels in 1 × 106 H9c2 cells were quantified by ICP.

ROS measurements in vitro

H9c2 cells were seeded in 12-well plates. After 24 h of adhesion and subsequent induction of the oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD), cells were treated during reperfusion with the following agents: OGD/R; MP NPs; TA-Ce; MP@T NPs, with normoxic cells serving as the control group. Following treatments, cells were incubated with DCFH-DA (20 µM) for 30 min at 37 °C. ROS levels were assessed via inverted fluorescence microscopy, with mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) calculated from the images. Additionally, ROS levels were also analyzed by flow cytometry.

H9c2 cells were seeded in 12-well plates. After 24 h of adhesion and subsequent induction of the OGD, cells were treated during reperfusion with the following agents: OGD/R; MP NPs; TA-Ce; MP@T NPs, with normoxic cells serving as the control group. Following treatments, cells were incubated with RDPP (10 µM) for 4 h at 37 °C. O2 levels were assessed via inverted fluorescence microscopy, with MFI calculated from the images. Additionally, O2 levels were also analyzed by flow cytometry.

Results and discussion

Bioinformatics analysis of GPX4 expression

Ferroptosis is a pathological outcome driven by lipid peroxidation under specific regulatory pathways, notably iron dependence and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) inactivation [37]. As the key antioxidant enzyme responsible for scavenging phospholipid hydroperoxides, GPX4 utilizes reduced glutathione (GSH) as a co‑substrate to reduce lipid peroxides to their corresponding alcohols, thereby protecting cells from oxidative damage and serving as a critical barrier against ferroptosis. GPX4 expression was analyzed using data from the GEO database (GSE83472, 16 samples, including 8 controls and 8 disease samples). A box plot showed the expression levels of the GPX4, which revealed that GPX4 was significantly downregulated in diseased tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1 A). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) indicated significant enrichment of genes involved in lipid metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation pathways, such as fatty acid degradation, lipoic acid metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 1B). Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) further demonstrated that the variation in GPX4 expression was primarily associated with pathways including oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism, and inflammation such as IL-6 jak stat3 signaling, TGF-β signaling and TNF-α signaling via NF-κB (Figure S1). Figure 1 C displays the top 10 genes positively/negatively correlated with GPX4 expression. Several oxidation and inflammation related genes were identified. DYRK1B and GSTA4 showed a positive correlation with GPX4 expression, whereas CD68 and BCL10 exhibited a negative correlation (Fig. 1D, S2). Based on the key pathological pathways identified above, we designed and constructed a spatiotemporally controlled multifunctional nanoparticle system.

Synthesis, characterization and enzymatic activity of MP@T NPs

PFP@ML351/lip (termed MP NPs) was synthesized using the previously reported thin film hydration method and phacoemulsification method [38, 39]. Afterward, a TA solution and (NH4)2Ce(NO3)6 solution were added to the MP NP suspension and emulsified. The pH of the reaction mixture was adjusted to 8.0 with NaOH solution (0.1 M), resulting in the immediate formation of PFP@ML351/lip@TA-Ce (termed MP@T NPs) with a characteristic brown color. The TEM analysis revealed that both the MP NPs and MP@T NPs possessed a uniform, spherical morphology (Fig. 1E, F). Their average diameters were 147.27 ± 1.35 nm and 176.60 ± 4.57 nm, respectively (Fig. 1G), indicating a slight increase in size following TA–Ce coating. The zeta potentials were −22.8 mV and −32.2 mV (Fig. 1H), supporting the successful formation of the coating. The HPLC analysis revealed that the encapsulation efficiency and drug loading of the MP NPs were 99.51 ± 0.01% and 9.05%, respectively (Figure S3). The encapsulation efficiency of Ce in MP@T NPs, as measured using ICP, was 18.19%. UV‒Vis spectroscopy showed that both TA–Ce and MP@T NPs absorbed light across a broad spectral range from 400 to 800 nm (Fig. 1I). This finding indicates the successful loading of the TA–Ce coating onto the MP@T NPs, thereby confirming the successful fabrication of the metal‒phenolic network hybrid liposomes. XPS analysis confirmed the presence of Ce on the surface of the MP@T NPs (Fig. 1 J, K), with a calculated Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio of 1:4, indicating the predominance of the antioxidant Ce4+ state. FTIR spectroscopy confirmed the presence of ML351 (characteristic nitrile C ≡ N band at 2209.81 cm−1 for MP NPs and 2210.79 cm−1 for MP@T NPs) and lipid membrane components (C-H stretching vibrations at 2921.64 cm−1 and 2852.15 cm−1 for MP NPs and at 2924.85 cm−1 and 2852.63 cm−1 for MP@T NPs) (Fig. 1L). A distinct aromatic ring skeletal vibration was observed at 1615.70 cm−1 for TA–Ce and at 1643.72 cm−1 for MP@T NPs, suggesting a molecular interaction between the TA–Ce coating and the core MP NPs. Additionally, a peak at 757.44 cm−1 in the TA–Ce spectrum, which is characteristic of the C-H out-of-plane bending vibration in ortho-/para-disubstituted/polysubstituted benzene rings, was also present at 758.28 cm−1 in the MP@T NPs. These collective spectral findings confirm the successful construction of the MP@T NP structure. Given the acidic pH of the ischemia-reperfusion injury area, which originates from intense anaerobic metabolic behavior [40], we plotted the release profiles of Ce as a function of time under different conditions. At pH 5.0, the release rate of Ce continuously increased, indicating that TA-Ce exhibits pH-responsive degradation (Figure S4).

TA–Ce possesses enzymatic activity and is an efficient reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenger. Conducted by Michaelis-Menten equation, catalytic rates at different H2O2 concentrations were quantified (Figure S5, 6), showing a maximal reaction velocity (Vmax, a steady-state kinetic parameter) of 0.1097 mM/min and a half-Vmax substrate concentration (Km) of 84.04 mM for TA–Ce. The performance of MP@T NPs was evaluated based on four antioxidant assays: catalase‑like H₂O₂ decomposition, superoxide dismutase (SOD)‑like superoxide dismutation, hydroxyl radical scavenging capacity, and total antioxidant activity (Fig. 1M). Dissolved oxygen levels in H₂O₂ solutions containing MP NPs, TA–Ce, or MP@T NPs (1 mg/mL) were monitored using a dissolved oxygen meter (Figure S7). Compared with the MP NP group, the TA–Ce coating demonstrated a pronounced ability to decompose H₂O₂. Further evaluation of the dissolved oxygen levels in H₂O₂ solutions containing various concentration of MP@T NPs revealed that the efficacy of H₂O₂ decomposition increased in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1 N). At a concentration of 500 µg/mL, MP@T NPs achieved up to 80% decomposition of H₂O₂, and the SOD-like activity reached 42%, demonstrating substantial catalase-like (CAT) and SOD-like activity (Fig. 1O, P), which was attributed primarily to the presence of the TA–Ce coating. Hydroxyl radicals represent another major source of intracellular oxidative stress. The hydroxyl radical scavenging capacity of the MP@T NPs was evaluated using a TMB-based assay (Fig. 1Q). The absorbance at 652 nm decreased with increasing MP@T NP concentrations, indicating effective radical scavenging. At 500 µg/mL, MP@T NPs achieved a 69% scavenging rate, demonstrating the efficient elimination of the hydroxyl radical. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was employed to assess the superoxide anion and hydroxyl radical scavenging capabilities. The incubation of xanthine with xanthine oxidase generated superoxide anions, as confirmed by the characteristic EPR signal. The addition of MP@T NPs effectively attenuated this signal, indicating their potent SOD-like activity (Fig. 1R). The addition of Fe2+ and H₂O₂ generated hydroxyl radicals, as evidenced by the characteristic EPR spectral signal. The signal was slightly attenuated upon the addition of 250 µg/mL MP@T NPs and markedly reduced after treatment with 500 µg/mL (Fig. 1S), verifying the efficient hydroxyl radical scavenging ability of the MP@T NPs. The total antioxidant capacity was assessed using the ABTS radical scavenging assay. At 500 µg/mL, MP@T NPs achieved an ABTS radical scavenging rate of 83% (Fig. 1 T), indicating a strong free radical scavenging activity derived from the TA–Ce coating. The TA–Ce coating mimics CAT activity, converting H2O2 into water and oxygen, an effect attributed to the high Ce4+ content in MP@T NPs, which serves as the cornerstone of the antioxidant strategy. Consistent with our results, the TA–Ce coating successfully catalyzed the SOD and CAT cascade reactions, while also preventing the generation of highly cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals, thereby maintaining redox balance. These results demonstrate the ability of MP@T NPs to mitigate oxidative stress.

MP@T NPs protect H9c2 cells from intracellular oxidative damage

Given the notable antioxidant properties of the MP@T NPs, we next evaluated their protective effect on oxidative stress in H9c2 cells. The 24-h biocompatibility of a broad range of MP@T NP concentrations was evaluated in H9c2 cells and HUVECs (Fig. 2 A, B). The viability of both cell types was maintained above 80% after an incubation with MP@T NPs concentrations up to 100 µg/mL, indicating the favorable biosafety profile of the nanoparticles. A fluorescence analysis was performed to evaluate the uptake of DiI-labeled MP NPs and MP@T NPs by H9c2 cells (Fig. 2D). Cellular uptake increased with increasing incubation time in a time-dependent manner. At each time point, the fluorescence intensity in the MP@T NP group was stronger than that in the MP NP group. Flow cytometry analysis was performed to quantify cellular uptake (Fig. 2E). After 4 h of coincubation, H9c2 cells internalized 68.6% of the MP@T NPs. The ICP analysis of the intracellular Ce content (Fig. 2 C) showed a notable increase to 3.43 µg after 1 h, reaching 6.19 µg by 8 h. These results may be attributed to the high affinity of the TA-modified complex for elastin and collagen, conferring a degree of tissue specificity with preferential action on cardiac tissue over the vasculature or other tissues [41]. This property likely increases the spatiotemporal opportunity for MP@T NPs to enter cells.

MI/RI results in excessive ROS production within the myocardium, leading to cardiomyocyte injury and death. Based on the previously demonstrated antioxidant capacity of MP@T NPs, we next investigated their protective effects on oxidative stress in H9c2 cells. An oxygen‒glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) model was established in H9c2 cells to simulate radical-induced damage. ROS scavenging was assessed using the DCFH-DA probe, which emits green fluorescence in the presence of free radicals. The results showed that OGD/R markedly increased intracellular free radical levels, as indicated by intense green fluorescence (Fig. 2I, K). Both TA–Ce and MP@T NP treatments significantly attenuated this increase. MP NP treatment also reduced the free radical levels, potentially through the ML351-induced inhibition of ALOX synthase, leading to reduced lipid peroxidation and consequently lower free radical levels. MP@T NPs demonstrated excellent antioxidant and free radical scavenging activities, which were likely attributed to the predominance of the antioxidant Ce4+ within the nanoparticles, as indicated by the previous XPS analysis. The flow cytometry results confirmed these findings (Fig. 2 F, J). Intracellular O₂ generation was assessed using the red fluorescent probe tris(4,7-diphenyl-1,10-phenanthroline) ruthenium(II) dichloride (RDPP), whose fluorescence is quenched upon oxidation by O₂ (Fig. 2I, M). Compared with that in the OGD/R group, the red fluorescence in both the TA-Ce and MP@T NP treatment groups was significantly quenched. This observation was corroborated by the flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 2G, L), confirming that the TA–Ce coating retained potent intracellular CAT activity. In mammals, mitochondria serve as the primary source of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Given that TA has been reported to exhibit high affinity for mitochondrial outer membrane proteins [42], we employed the MitoSOX Red probe to examine the mitochondrial distribution of the superoxide anion under different experimental conditions. Fluorescence imaging revealed distinct red fluorescence in H9c2 cells following OGD/R (Fig. 2I, O). This signal was moderately reduced in the MP NP group and was barely detectable in the groups exposed to TA–Ce. These observations were corroborated by the results of the flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 2H, N). These data indicated that treatment with MP@T NPs effectively reduced excessive ROS in the OGD/R microenvironment, decreased oxidative stress, and strongly inhibited the initiation of ferroptosis. Specifically, the organelle-specific localization of TA to mitochondria enables it to counteract the ROS-triggered ferroptotic cascade, thereby preemptively attenuating the key drivers of ferroptosis.

Protective effect of MP@T NPs on OGD/R-injured cardiomyocytes

Research indicates that US irradiation, through its unique cavitation can triggers the phase-transition vaporization of PFP [43]. This noninvasive energy transfer method, combined with its millisecond-level response speed, enables the targeting of and intervention in the early critical stage of ferroptosis initiation after reperfusion. Building upon the cellular uptake of MP@T NPs and the subsequent reduction in oxidative stress induced by the TA–Ce coating, the spatiotemporal control offered by US further increases the intracellular delivery of ML351. Treatment with MP@T NPs reduced phospholipid hydroperoxide (PLOOH) generation by suppressing the expression of ALOX15 and ACSL4. Furthermore, MP@T NPs restored the expression of GPX4, which is essential for the reduction of PLOOH by GSH. This sequential therapeutic approach increased the attenuation of ferroptosis and reinforced the overall protective effect of MP@T NPs on cardiomyocytes [44] (Fig. 6 A). First, the effects of US on H9c2 cells were investigated. Following 24 h of exposure to US at various power levels, cell survival rates remained consistently high at greater than 90%, indicating that US had a high degree of biological safety (Fig. 3 C). US also demonstrated high biocompatibility in H9c2 cells subjected to OGD/R (Fig. 3D). Lipid peroxidation levels in H9c2 cells increase in response to MI/RI. Through the specific detection of total lipid peroxide levels using the BODIPY 581/591 C11 probe (Fig. 3E, F), the fluorescence results revealed pronounced green fluorescence following OGD/R treatment, indicating that the cells were undergoing intense lipid peroxidation reactions. The red-to-green ratios were elevated in the ML351 group, MP NP group, and MP NP + US group, indicating mild cellular oxidative stress. In contrast, oxidative stress was largely suppressed in the MP@T NP and MP@T NP + US groups, demonstrating that sequential treatment with TA–Ce and ML351 substantially reduced lipid peroxidation levels. Malondialdehyde (MDA) is a characteristic end product of lipid peroxidation, and its cellular level is directly proportional to the overall extent of peroxidative damage. Therefore, MDA is widely employed as a classical biomarker to assess lipid peroxidation. The results revealed that OGD/R-induced H9c2 cells produced significantly more MDA (3.78 nmol/mg) (Fig. 3G), confirming the induction of severe oxidative stress. ML351 treatment reduced MDA generation (1.72 nmol/mg), whereas the MP@T NP + US treatment achieved the most substantial reduction (0.80 nmol/mg). These findings are consistent with the results obtained using the BODIPY probe. Under the oxidative stress induced by OGD/R, the activity of glutathione peroxidase (GPx) was substantially decreased (28.6 mU/mg) (Fig. 3H). While the GPx activity did not markedly increase in the ML351 group, treatment with MP NPs + US, MP@T NPs, or MP@T NPs + US restored GPx activity (56.9, 63.9, and 67.2 mU/mg, respectively). This result suggests that the restoration of GPx activity may be primarily attributed to the targeting capability and enzymatic activity of the TA–Ce coating. Studies have shown that the inhibition or loss of GPX4 activity leads to uncontrolled accumulation of lipid peroxides from the abundant polyunsaturated fatty acid phospholipid substrates provided by ACSL4, ultimately triggering ferroptosis. Western blot analysis was performed to further examine the expression levels of ACSL4 and GPX4 and to investigate the mechanism underlying ferroptosis inhibition. Compared with the OGD/R group, the MP@T NP and MP@T NP + US groups exhibited decreased ACSL4 expression and increased GPX4 expression (Fig. 3I–K and S8). These findings suggest that the sequential treatment may confer increased resistance to ferroptosis, potentially by modulating these key regulatory proteins.

During ferroptosis, mitochondria undergo characteristic and diagnostic ultrastructural alterations. These changes typically include a marked reduction in the mitochondrial volume, along with a substantial decrease in the number of mitochondrial cristae, which are shortened or entirely lost [45]. TEM revealed that OGD/R induced mitochondrial contraction, increased the membrane density, and reduced or eliminated cristae (Fig. 3L). In contrast, the mitochondrial ultrastructure in the MP@T NP + US group was largely restored, approaching that of the control group. Mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization was assessed using a JC-1 dual-emission fluorescent probe (Fig. 3M, N). The ratio of red-to-green fluorescence intensity decreased following OGD/R, indicating a reduction in the mitochondrial membrane potential. In contrast, the incubation with MP@T NPs counteracted this decrease, indicating that these NPs can preserve mitochondrial integrity in cardiomyocytes following radical-induced injury and offer greater protection than MP NPs alone. ATP generation in OGD/R-treated cells (6.1 nmol/mg) was significantly lower than that in control cells (9.9 nmol/mg) (Fig. 3O). Treatment with MP@T NPs restored ATP production to 13.2 nmol/mg. Moreover, the controlled release enabled by US further enhanced the protective effect of MP@T NPs on the cellular ATP content. A transcriptomic analysis was performed on H9c2 cells treated with MP@T NPs + US and control cells (n = 5) to analyze the underlying mechanisms and gene expression profiles. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for dimensionality reduction (Fig. 3P), with the first two principal components, PC1 and PC2, accounting for 79.4% and 12.35% of the variance, respectively. The control and MP@T NP + US groups exhibited clear separation in the PCA plot, indicating distinct gene expression profiles between the two conditions. The volcano plot shows 527 genes that were significantly upregulated and 218 genes that were downregulated between the two groups, indicating that MP@T NPs and US substantially altered the transcriptional landscape of H9c2 cells (Fig. 3Q). The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these DEGs revealed significant associations with several key processes, including ferroptosis, necroptosis, and ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling (Fig. 3R). This enrichment supports the involvement of both ferroptosis-related and mitochondrial protective pathways in the mechanism of action of the MP@T NP + US treatment.

Targeting ability of MP@T NPs and the ultrasonic profile in MI/RI rats

We first examined the retention of DiR-labeled MP@T NPs in MI/RI model rats. MP@T NPs were administered intravenously following reperfusion (Fig. 4 A, B). Ex vivo fluorescence imaging revealed greater cardiac accumulation of MP@T NPs than of MP NPs, with the quantitative analysis indicating peak retention at 3 h postinjection (Fig. 4D–H). DiI-labeled MP@T NPs were injected, and heart sections collected 3 h later confirmed the same pattern of accumulation (Fig. 4 C). This targeting ability is primarily due to the affinity of TA for collagen. US imaging is a promising modality because of its noninvasive nature and high sensitivity. The perfluoropentane (PFP) encapsulated within MP@T NPs serves as an ideal phase-change material that can be triggered by various stimuli, such as US, temperature, or laser irradiation [46–48]. MP@T NPs can undergo cavitation upon exposure to US irradiation, indicating their significant potential as ultrasound contrast agents. US images of MP@T NPs exposed to varying levels of acoustic power (0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 W/cm2) for different durations (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 min) were obtained (Fig. 4I). The echo intensity of the MP@T NPs increased with exposure time starting at 0.2 W/cm2 and peaked at 0.8 W/cm2 (Fig. 4 J, K). However, the intensity decreased after exposure to 1.0 W/cm2, which may be attributed to the rupture of some MP@T NPs due to overexpansion beyond a critical size threshold. The corresponding echo intensities were quantified using dedicated analysis software, and the results were consistent with their in vitro performance. These findings collectively demonstrate that the PFP-encapsulated MP@T NPs exhibit favorable responsiveness to US irradiation. The in vivo ultrasound imaging ability of MP@T NPs and MP NPs was further validated using an MI/RI model in Sprague‒Dawley (SD) rats. Grayscale and contrast-enhanced US images were acquired before modeling. Images were captured 30 min after the intravenous injection of MP@T NPs or MP NPs at equivalent concentrations. After 3 min of US irradiation over the precordial region, images were acquired again for a comparative analysis (Fig. 4L). The images showed little to no contrast signal in the heart before modeling and at 30 min after injection. Following US irradiation, however, pronounced contrast signals were observed in the cardiac region for MP@T NPs. This enhancement may be attributed to the targeting ability of MP@T NPs toward cardiac tissue, which is likely mediated by their affinity for collagen. These findings provide evidence for the effective accumulation of MP@T NPs within the cardiac region and support their potential as ultrasound contrast agents.

MP@T NPs ameliorate acute myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury

Ferroptosis is the predominant type of cell death in cardiomyocytes 24 h after infarction/reperfusion. Based on the fluorescence findings obtained ex vivo, saline, MP NPs, or MP@T NPs were administered at 21 h after MI/RI. After a 3-h retention period, US was applied to the precordial region of the rats in the MP NPs + US and MP@T NPs + US groups at 24 h to increase cardiac nanoparticle uptake (Fig. 5 A). For the acute MI/RI model, cardiac tissues were harvested on day 3 for subsequent analysis. Prussian blue staining was performed to detect iron deposition in the MI/RI area (Fig. 5B, C). The results showed marked iron deposition in the MI/RI group, which was substantially reduced following intervention with MP@T NPs and US irradiation. Cardiac sections stained with the red fluorescent DHE probe showed intense red fluorescence in the myocardial ischemia‒reperfusion area of the MI/RI group, whereas the fluorescence of the MP@T NP + US group was markedly attenuated (Fig. 5D). These findings indicate that the ability of the MP@T NP + US treatment to scavenge significant amounts of ROS. Additionally, we examined the expression of inflammatory factors. MP@T NP + US treatment inhibited the expression of the proinflammatory cytokine TNF-α and upregulated the expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, thereby suppressing inflammation and promoting recovery from MI/RI (Fig. 5E, F). TEM revealed that compared with that in the sham group, the mitochondria in the MI/RI group exhibited marked contraction, an increased bilayer membrane density, and a reduction or loss of cristae. These observations indicate substantial mitochondrial damage following MI/RI. However, the intervention with MP@T NPs and US irradiation promoted the recovery of the mitochondrial volume and cristae density (Fig. 5B). These findings suggest that MP@T NPs improve cardiac function by restoring mitochondrial integrity in ischemic cardiomyocytes.

Six rats subjected to different treatments (randomly allocated to the MI/RI and MP@T NP groups; n = 3) were sacrificed to explore the changes in gene expression in rat cardiomyocytes. Total RNA was then extracted from the harvested cardiac tissues. The GO enrichment analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in the inflammatory response (Fig. 5H). A heatmap of the clustering analysis showed that in the MP@T NP group, proinflammatory genes were downregulated, while anti-inflammatory genes were upregulated, indicating a potential mechanism underlying the therapeutic effect (Fig. 5G). The serum levels of inflammatory cytokines were measured. MP@T NP + US treatment downregulated IL-6 and IL-1β while upregulating IL-10 (Fig. 5I–K), thereby suppressing inflammation and promoting the clearance of lipid peroxides. The serum levels of traditional markers of myocardial injury, creatine kinase (CK) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), were significantly elevated in the MI/RI group compared with those in the sham group. Sequential treatment with MP@T NPs and US markedly reduced their levels (Fig. 5L, M), indicating the alleviation of myocardial damage. Hematological and biochemical analyses of the treated rats revealed no statistically significant differences among the groups. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of major organs showed no obvious abnormalities (Figure S9, 10).

MP@T NPs ameliorate chronic myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury

The rat model of chronic MI/RI was treated with injections combined with US irradiation twice a week. Cardiac US monitoring was conducted on days 1, 7, and 14, with tissues harvested for analysis on day 14 (Fig. 6 A). M-mode echocardiography showed that cardiac contractile motion was attenuated or absent following ischemia‒reperfusion (Figure S11). Compared with the sham group, the MI/RI group exhibited a significantly reduced anterior wall motion amplitude, ejection fraction, and left ventricular fractional shortening, confirming the successful establishment of the MI/RI model in rats. Statistical analysis revealed that the ejection fraction (EF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (FS) were improved in both the MP NP group and the MP@T NP group (Fig. 6 C, D, and S12). Compared with these groups, the EF and FS were further improved after MP@T NP + US treatment. On day 14, statistically significant differences in both the left ventricular internal diameter at diastole (LVIDd) and systole (LVIDs) were observed in the MP@T NP + US group and the MI/RI group (Fig. 6E, F). No statistically significant differences in posterior wall motion amplitude were detected among the groups (Figure S13). These results demonstrate that MP@T NPs + US significantly improved cardiac function. H&E and Masson staining showed a well-organized, parallel alignment of cardiomyocytes along the cardiac contractile axis and minimal collagen deposition in the sham group (Fig. 6B). In contrast, the MI/RI group exhibited markedly widened interstitial spaces and a substantial increase in the deposition of blue collagen fibers. After MP@T NP + US treatment, the myocardial architecture approached normal, and collagen deposition was notably reduced (Fig. 6G). TUNEL staining revealed that MI/RI induced pronounced cardiomyocyte apoptosis in the infarcted myocardium. In the MI/RI group, MP@T NPs effectively protected ischemic cardiomyocytes from apoptotic cell death (Fig. 6H). Immunofluorescence staining for cardiac troponin T (cTnT), a marker of cardiomyocyte viability, injury, and structural integrity, was performed. The sham group exhibited a uniform, continuous striated pattern with a high fluorescence intensity. In contrast, the MI/RI group showed an attenuated fluorescence intensity and markedly decreased cTnT expression in the injured area. Following MP@T NP + US treatment, an increased fluorescence intensity and partial restoration of structural integrity were observed (Fig. 6B). These results corroborate the TUNEL staining results, indicating a positive role for MP@T NPs in maintaining cardiomyocyte structural integrity and attenuating cell death. As shown in Fig. 6I, J, viable myocardial tissue was stained red, whereas the infarcted area remained unstained (pale). The pale area was reduced in the MP NP, MP NP + US, and MP@T NP groups, with the smallest infarct size observed in the MP@T NP + US group. These results indicate that MP@T NP treatment and US irradiation reduced the myocardial infarct size.

Serum levels of inflammatory cytokines were also measured in the chronic MI/RI model rats. MP@T NP + US treatment downregulated the expression of the cytokine IL-1β and upregulated the expression of the cytokine IL-10, while no statistically significant change in IL-6 expression was observed (Fig. 6 K, L, and S14). Serum CK and LDH levels did not differ significantly across groups in the chronic MI/RI model rats. These observations may be attributed to partial recovery from myocardial injury in the MI/RI group (Figure S15). During the treatment period, no obvious abnormalities were detected by H&E staining of the major organs (Fig. 6M). Furthermore, body weight, hematological parameters, and serum biochemistry were monitored in all groups, and no statistically significant differences were observed (Figs. 6 N, O, and S16).

Healthy SD rats were administered the nanoparticles and monitored over time to assess the systemic toxicity of MP@T NPs. Hematological and biochemical analyses performed at various time points revealed no significant differences among the groups, both in short-term and long-term settings. The histopathological examination of major organs by H&E staining showed no significant inflammatory responses or morphological damage in any of the treated animals (Figure S17, 18).

Conclusion

In this study, a metal–phenolic network hybrid liposome system (MP@T NPs) was successfully developed for the sequential treatment of MI/RI. Its therapeutic action proceeds in two temporally orchestrated phases. The TA–Ce coating provides immediate antioxidant and anti‑inflammatory effects upon cardiac targeting, mitigating early oxidative stress and apoptosis. Then, US irradiation at 24 h triggers PFP vaporization and the on‑demand release of ML351. Ferroptosis is specifically inhibited by the downregulation of ACSL4 and the upregulation of GPX4, thereby attenuating lipid peroxidation. MP@T NPs combined with US irradiation exert synergistic cardioprotective effects, as evidenced by the reduced ROS levels and iron deposition, preserved mitochondrial structure and ATP production, a fourfold reduction in the infarct size, and a 25.88% improvement in EF. Furthermore, the system showed excellent biocompatibility and served as an effective ultrasound contrast agent, allowing the noninvasive monitoring of cardiac accumulation. Collectively, these findings establish a promising spatiotemporally controlled therapeutic relay strategy that addresses the multifactorial progression of MI/RI to achieve layered myocardial protection and enhanced functional recovery.
