Dynamic Non-Bayesian Persuasion
Masanori Kobayashi

Abstract
We study a multi-step persuasion problem involving a Bayesian sender and a non-Bayesian receiver. When the receiver deviates from Bayesian updating, the sender may benefit from—or be harmed by—gradually revealing information over time. We show that under divisible updating rules ( Cripps 2018 ) , delaying information provision does not affect the sender’s ex-ante value. Focusing on the α \alpha – β \beta rule of Grether 1980 , we derive the necessary and sufficient conditions under which the sender is strictly better or worse off under a two-step persuasion scheme.

Introduction

1.1
Overview and Motivation

Bayesian updating has played a central role in information economics.
Persuasion games, such as those analyzed in
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
and
Rayo and Segal 2010
, are prominent examples in this literature.
As emphasized by
Bergemann and Morris 2019
, studies on Bayesian persuasion offer insights into optimal information disclosure and provide robust predictions of behaviors that are insensitive to specific information structures.

However, data from laboratory experiments and real-world economic interactions, such as financial markets, suggest that individuals often deviate from Bayesian updating and systematically commit errors in probabilistic inference. These deviations include biases such as underreaction
(
Edwards 1968
;
Grether 1980
)
, overreaction
(
De Bondt and Thaler 1985
)
, base-rate neglect
(
Tversky and Kahneman 1974
;
Grether 1980
)
, among others.
1
1

1

See
Benjamin 2019
for a detailed and systematic review of empirical studies on non-Bayesian updating rules.

This raises the following questions: How does persuasion operate when the receiver updates beliefs in a non-Bayesian manner? What is the optimal way to disclose information to such receivers? What outcomes can be robustly predicted?

Our contribution addresses these questions by incorporating a novel feature: the sender may reveal information to the receiver gradually over time.
This dynamic aspect of information disclosure becomes particularly important when the receiver is non-Bayesian.

Alonso and Câmara 2016
and
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
address the above questions under the assumption that the sender can use only a single Blackwell experiment and its realization to persuade the receiver.
This one-shot interaction assumption entails no loss when both agents are Bayesian—in the sense that the sender’s ex-ante payoff set remains unchanged between one-shot and multi-step persuasion.

We extend the model of
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
to study a two-step persuasion framework in which a Bayesian sender interacts with a receiver who systematically distorts posterior beliefs.
In our framework, the sender first chooses an experiment.
After observing its realization, both sender and receiver update their beliefs according to their respective updating rules.
Given these updated (and heterogeneous) priors, the sender chooses a second experiment.
The receiver again updates her belief based on the second signal realization and then takes an action.
We assume that the sender and receiver ”agree to disagree” regarding their updating rules: their differing belief-updating processes are common knowledge.
As a result, it is commonly known—after the first signal—that sender and receiver hold heterogeneous beliefs.

We show that the second-stage persuasion problem with heterogenous priors reduces to a non-Bayesian persuasion problem of
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
with a common prior.
The sender’s ex-ante value under two-step persuasion is characterized via concavification, as in
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
.
Using this approach, we first show that when the receiver’s updating rule is divisible
(
Cripps 2018
)
, one-shot persuasion (as in
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
) is without loss—that is, the sender’s feasible ex-ante payoff set is identical in both one-shot and two-step settings.

Focusing on
Grether 1980
’s
\alpha
–
\beta
updating rule, we derive the conditions under which gradual disclosure benefits or harms the sender.
We find that when the receiver exhibits base-rate neglect (
\alpha\in(0,1)
), the sender strictly benefits from two-step persuasion.
Conversely, when the receiver overweights prior beliefs (
\alpha>1
), the sender is worse off.
Interestingly, the geometric distortion parameter
\beta
has no effect on this result.

The remainder of Section
1
reviews the related literature.
Section
2
provides a motivating numerical example by extending the judge–prosecutor setting from
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
to a non-Bayesian receiver with linear distortions and a sender engaging in two-step persuasion.
We show that this dynamic disclosure can influence the sender’s ex-ante payoff.
Section
3
formally introduces the two-step persuasion model and presents our main results. All the proofs are relegated to Appendix.

1.2
Related Literature

This paper relates to three strands of literature in economic theory: Bayesian persuasion, non-Bayesian belief updating, and dynamic information provision with receivers exhibiting psychological preferences.

The first strand has developed rapidly following the seminal contributions of
Rayo and Segal 2010
and
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
, and has since been extended in several directions, including models with multiple receivers in strategic environments
(
Bergemann and Morris 2019
)
, dynamic sender–receiver interactions
(
Hörner and Skrzypacz 2016
;
Che et al. 2023
)
, and behavioral biases
(
Alonso and Câmara 2016
;
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
)
.
By analyzing the intersection of dynamic persuasion and behavioral biases, the present paper investigates how the sender’s value evolves over time and how robust behavioral predictions can be derived.

The second strand concerns the theory of non-Bayesian updating, which has recently coalesced around a class of updating rules known as
systematic distortions
, where the updated belief is a deterministic function of the Bayesian posterior.

De Clippel and Zhang 2022
characterize systematic distortions using a homogeneity axiom, requiring that the updated belief depends only on the prior and the likelihood ratio of the signal.

Whitmeyer 2024
and
Bordoli 2024
investigate the properties of systematic distortions by examining their compatibility with Blackwell monotonicity—that is, whether more informative experiments (in the Blackwell sense) lead to more valuable outcomes under the updating rule.

Jakobsen 2023
introduce coarse Bayesian updating rules, a subclass of systematic distortions that capture various empirically observed belief patterns.

Cripps 2018
define a further subclass called
divisible
updating rules, under which sequential and one-shot belief updating yield the same result.
Every divisible updating rule is a systematic distortion.
In this paper, we show that divisibility plays a key role in determining whether multi-step persuasion yields additional value in non-Bayesian contexts.

The third strand examines how the timing of information affects receivers with psychological preferences.

Ely et al. 2015
introduce the psychological value of information—“suspense” and “surprise”—and characterize optimal information disclosure in this context.

Duraj and He 2024
incorporate a preference for news timing, showing that disaggregated (separate) news can have a larger utility impact than lump-sum news delivery.
The present study differs from this literature in that we do not model any non-instrumental value of information; yet, the timing of disclosure still matters due to the receiver’s non-Bayesian updating behavior.

Leading Example

To motivate our analysis, we revisit the canonical judge–prosecutor example from
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
.
Suppose there is a prosecutor (sender) who designs the information structure observed by a judge (receiver).
The defendant is guilty, denoted by
\theta=1
, with probability
p=0.3
, and innocent, denoted by
\theta=0
, with probability
1-p
.
The sender commits to a Blackwell experiment, the signal realization of which is observed by the receiver.
Upon observing the signal, the receiver updates her belief and chooses an action
a\in\{0,1\}
, where
a=0
represents acquittal and
a=1
represents conviction.

The sender always prefers conviction, so his utility is
v(a,\theta)=a
for all
\theta\in\{0,1\}
.
The receiver, on the other hand, prefers to match her action to the defendant’s true state. Her utility is
u(a,\theta)=\mathbbm{1}_{a=\theta}
.
Given her belief
q
that
\theta=1
, her optimal action is
\hat{a}(q)=\mathbbm{1}_{q\geq\frac{1}{2}}
.
2
2

2

We assume that if the receiver is indifferent between the two actions, she selects the sender-preferred action.

Accordingly, the sender’s indirect utility, given the receiver’s belief
q
, is
\hat{v}(q)=\mathbbm{1}_{q\geq\frac{1}{2}}
.

We assume the sender is Bayesian, while the receiver may not be.
Specifically, the receiver updates her beliefs according to a
linear distortion
with weight
\alpha>0
.
If the Bayesian posterior belief for
\theta=1
is
q_{B}\in[0,1]
, the receiver’s distorted posterior is

\alpha q_{B}+(1-\alpha)p

and her belief for
\theta=0
is

1-\left[\alpha q_{B}+(1-\alpha)p\right],

provided that the expression lies within
[0,1]
.
3
3

3

Cases where
\alpha q_{B}+(1-\alpha)p\notin[0,1]
are addressed in Example
2
in Section
3
, but these do not affect the numerical exercise here.

For completeness, we define the updated belief to be
1
if the expression exceeds
1
and
0
if it falls below
0
.
4
4

4

This is a reduced-form representation of biased belief updating. A formal definition based on distorted experiment evaluation is given in Section
3
.

When
\alpha=1
, the receiver is Bayesian; when
\alpha\in(0,1)
, she
underreacts
; and when
\alpha>1
, she
overreacts
.

Given the receiver’s distorted updating, the sender selects an experiment to maximize his ex-ante expected payoff.

De Clippel and Zhang 2022
show that this problem reduces to the canonical Bayesian persuasion framework with a modified indirect utility:

\check{v}(q):=\hat{v}(\alpha q+(1-\alpha)p)=\mathbbm{1}_{\alpha q+(1-\alpha)p\geq\frac{1}{2}}=\mathbbm{1}_{q\geq q_{\alpha}},\quad\text{where }q_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2\alpha}-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}p.

The sender’s expected utility is then determined by the concavification of this function, evaluated at the prior
p
.

1
illustrates the sender’s value (black dots) on the concavified modified utility.
The horizontal axis represents the sender’s Bayesian posterior belief. Solid lines correspond to modified indirect utilities for various
\alpha
, while dashed lines represent their concavifications.

When the receiver underreacts (
\alpha=0.5
), the sender faces greater difficulty in shifting her action, which is expressed as increased threshold
q_{\alpha}
compared to Bayesian case (
\alpha=1
).
To satisfy Bayes plausibility and the increased threshold belief
q_{\alpha}
, the sender must design an experiment yielding a random posterior taking
\tilde{q}=0
with greater probability.
Consequently, the sender’s ex-ante payoff decreases relative to the Bayesian case.
The opposite holds when the receiver overreacts (e.g.,
\alpha=1.5
).

For each
\alpha
, the optimal experiment induces a random posterior given by

\rho_{\alpha}^{*}=\left(1-\frac{p}{q_{\alpha}}\right)\delta_{\tilde{q}=0}+\frac{p}{q_{\alpha}}\delta_{\tilde{q}=q_{\alpha}}.

The blue arrows in Figure

\alpha=0.5
.

Now suppose the sender can perform two sequential experiments.
Consider the following procedure: the sender first runs an experiment that generates a mean-preserving contraction
\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}
of
\rho_{\alpha}^{*}
, defined as

\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}=\left(1-\frac{p}{q_{\alpha}}\right)\delta_{q=\frac{p}{2}}+\frac{p}{q_{\alpha}}\delta_{q=\frac{p+q_{\alpha}}{2}}.

The green arrows in Figure
2
illustrate this modified distribution.

When the first Bayesian posterior is
q_{B}^{1}:=\frac{p}{2}
, the receiver’s distorted belief becomes

p_{\alpha}^{1}:=\alpha q_{B}^{1}+(1-\alpha)p=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)p.

When
q_{B}^{2}:=\frac{p+q_{\alpha}}{2}
, the distorted belief is

p_{\alpha}^{2}:=\alpha q_{B}^{2}+(1-\alpha)p=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)p+\frac{\alpha q_{\alpha}}{2}.

Suppose that, after the first experiment, the sender conducts optimal non-Bayesian persuasion using each
q_{B}^{k}
as the sender’s prior and the corresponding distorted belief
p_{\alpha}^{k}
as the receiver’s prior.
As shown in Section
3
, this problem with heterogeneous priors can be transformed into a non-Bayesian persuasion problem with a common prior, and thus can be solved using the approach of
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
.
The sender’s interim payoff is determined by the concavification of the appropriately modified utility function for each case.
The ex-ante payoff is the convex combination of these two interim values, weighted by the probabilities of
q_{B}^{1}
and
q_{B}^{2}
determined by
\rho^{\prime}
.

) and

\alpha=1.5

Intuitively, under underreaction, each round of information provision reactivates the prior distortion term
(1-\alpha)p
,
which does not depend on the newly arrived information

In contrast, under overreaction, the sender can leverage the amplification of belief distortion across multiple rounds, thereby increasing his expected payoff.

3
Dynamic Non-Bayesian Persuasion

3.1
Environment

In this section, we introduce the framework for multi-step persuasion with a non-Bayesian receiver.
Let
\Theta
be a finite set of payoff-relevant states, and let
p\in\Delta(\Theta)
denote a full-support prior distribution over
\Theta
.
5
5

5

For any finite set
X
,
\Delta(X)
denotes the set of all probability distributions over
X
.

The sender chooses an experiment
\sigma=\langle S,(\sigma_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}\rangle
, where
S
is a finite set of signal realizations, and for each
\theta\in\Theta
,
\sigma_{\theta}\in\Delta(S)
.
Throughout the paper, we fix a sufficiently large signal space
S
that the sender can use.
Then, the set of feasible experiments is identified as the set of mappings
\Delta(S)^{\Theta}:=\mathcal{E}
.

The receiver chooses an action
a
from a finite action set
A
.
Both the sender and the receiver are expected utility maximizers and are endowed with von Neumann–Morgenstern utility functions
v(a,\theta)
and
u(a,\theta)
, respectively.
The receiver observes the experiment
\sigma
and the realized signal
s\in S
chosen by the sender, and then selects an action to maximize her expected utility. We call a tuple
(\Theta,p,A,u,v)
a
persuasion environment
. For simplicity, we assume that the sender’s payoff depends only on the receiver’s action throughout the paper, so
v(a,\theta)=v(a)
for all
\theta\in\Theta
.

Let
\hat{a}(q)
denote the sender-optimal selection from the receiver’s best-response set, that is,

\hat{a}(q)\in\arg\max_{a\in A}\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[u(a,\tilde{\theta})\right],

where
q
is the receiver’s belief about
\theta
.
6
6

6

Throughout the paper, variables with a tilde refer to random variables; those without refer to realizations.

We assume that the sender is Bayesian, but the receiver is not necessarily so. We define an updating rule as a function
\mu:\mathcal{E}\times\Delta(\Theta)\times S\to\Delta(\Theta)
,
where
\mu(\sigma,p)(\cdot\mid s)
denotes the posterior belief formed upon observing signal
s\in S
, given experiment
\sigma\in\mathcal{E}
and prior belief
p\in\Delta(\Theta)
under updating rule
\mu
.

The Bayesian updating rule, denoted by
\mu_{B}
, is defined as follows:

\mu_{B}(\sigma,p)(\theta\mid s):=\frac{\sigma_{\theta}(s)\,p(\theta)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\sigma_{\theta^{\prime}}(s)\,p(\theta^{\prime})}

for all
s\in S
with
\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}p(\theta)\sigma_{\theta}(s)>0
.

3.2
One-shot Persuasion

Let
v(a)
denote the sender’s payoff from action
a\in A
.
Given the receiver’s updating rule
\mu
, the sender’s problem is formulated as

V(p,\mu)=\sup_{\tau\in T(p,\mu)}\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[\hat{v}(q)],

where
\hat{v}(q):=v(\hat{a}(q))
is the sender’s indirect utility, and

T(p,\mu):=\left\{\tau\in\Delta(\Delta(\Theta)):\tau(q)=\sigma\left(\left\{s\in S:\mu(\sigma,p)(\cdot\mid s)=q\right\}\right)\text{ for some }\sigma\in\mathcal{E}\right\}

is the set of feasible distributions over posterior beliefs induced by some experiment
\sigma
under prior
p
and updating rule
\mu
.

De Clippel and Zhang 2022
characterize the solution to this problem for a restricted class of updating rules.
We say that an updating rule
\mu

systematically distorts updated beliefs
if, for any full-support prior
p\in\Delta(\Theta)
, there exists a
distortion function

D_{p}:\Delta(\Theta)\to\Delta(\Theta)
such that for any experiment
\sigma\in\mathcal{E}
and signal
s\in S
,

\mu(\sigma,p)(\cdot\mid s)=D_{p}\left(\mu_{B}(\sigma,p)(\cdot\mid s)\right).

Example 1

We provide five examples of updating rules that systematically distort updated beliefs:

1.

Linear distortion:

Let
\Theta=\{1,0\}
, and let
p\in\Delta(\Theta)
also denote
Pr(\theta=1)
(by slight abuse of notation). Define

q_{s}^{\alpha}(\sigma,p):=\frac{\alpha p\sigma_{\theta=1}(s)}{(1-p)\sigma_{\theta=0}(s)+\alpha p\sigma_{\theta=1}(s)}+(1-\alpha)p.

Then,

\mu_{\alpha}^{L}(\sigma,p)(\theta=1\mid s)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }q_{s}^{\alpha}(\sigma,p)>1\\
q_{s}^{\alpha}(\sigma,p)&\text{if }q_{s}^{\alpha}(\sigma,p)\in[0,1]\\
0&\text{if }q_{s}^{\alpha}(\sigma,p)<0\end{cases}

and
\mu_{\alpha}^{L}(\sigma,p)(\theta=0\mid s)=1-\mu_{\alpha}^{L}(\sigma,p)(\theta=1\mid s)
for some
\alpha>0
.
If
\alpha=1
, this reduces to Bayesian updating.
If
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we call this
linear underreaction
; if
\alpha>1
,
linear overreaction
.
The associated distortion function is:

D_{p}(q)(\theta=1)=\min\left[\max\left\{\alpha q(\theta=1)+(1-\alpha)p(\theta=1),0\right\},1\right],\quad D_{p}(q)(\theta=0)=1-D_{p}(q)(\theta=1).

2.

Geometric distortion:

\mu_{\alpha}^{G}(\sigma,p)(\theta\mid s)=\frac{p(\theta)\,\sigma_{\theta}(s)^{\alpha}}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}p(\theta^{\prime})\,\sigma_{\theta^{\prime}}(s)^{\alpha}},

for some
\alpha>0
.
If
\alpha=1
, this reduces to Bayesian updating.
If
\alpha\in(0,1)
, it represents
geometric underreaction
; if
\alpha>1
,
geometric overreaction
.
The associated distortion function is:

D_{p}(q)(\theta)=\frac{p(\theta)^{1-\alpha}q(\theta)^{\alpha}}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}p(\theta^{\prime})^{1-\alpha}q(\theta^{\prime})^{\alpha}}.

3.

Divisible updating
(
Cripps 2018
)

\mu_{F}(\sigma,p)(\theta|s)=(F^{-1})_{\theta}\left(\frac{F_{\theta}(p)\sigma_{\theta}(s)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}F_{\theta^{\prime}}(p)\sigma_{\theta^{\prime}}(s)}\right)

for some homeomorphism
F:\Delta(\Theta)\to\Delta(\Theta)
. Geometric distortion is a special case of divisible updating with
F_{\theta}(p)=p^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
. The associated distortion function is

D_{p}(q)(\theta)=(F)_{\theta}^{-1}\left(\frac{\frac{F_{\theta}(p)}{p(\theta)}q(\theta)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\frac{F_{\theta}(p)}{p(\theta^{\prime})}q(\theta^{\prime})}\right)

4.

Base-rate neglect / over-weighting:
For
\alpha>0
,

\mu_{\alpha}^{BR}(\sigma,p)(\theta\mid s)=\frac{p(\theta)^{\alpha}\sigma_{\theta}(s)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}p(\theta^{\prime})^{\alpha}\sigma_{\theta^{\prime}}(s)}.

If
\alpha=1
, this reduces to Bayesian updating.
If
\alpha\in(0,1)
, it represents
base-rate neglect
; if
\alpha>1
,
base-rate over-weighting
.
The associated distortion function is:

D_{p}(q)(\theta)=\frac{p(\theta)^{\alpha-1}q(\theta)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}p(\theta^{\prime})^{\alpha-1}q(\theta^{\prime})}.

5.

Grether’s
\alpha
–
\beta
model
(
Grether 1980
): For
\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
,

\mu_{\alpha,\beta}(\sigma,p)(\theta\mid s)=\frac{p(\theta)^{\alpha}\,\sigma_{\theta}(s)^{\beta}}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}p(\theta^{\prime})^{\alpha}\,\sigma_{\theta^{\prime}}(s)^{\beta}}.

This generalizes both geometric distortion and base-rate bias.
The associated distortion function is:

D_{p}(q)(\theta)=\frac{p(\theta)^{\alpha-\beta}q(\theta)^{\beta}}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}p(\theta^{\prime})^{\alpha-\beta}q(\theta^{\prime})^{\beta}}.

If the receiver’s updating rule
\mu
systematically distorts beliefs with distortion function
D
, the sender’s problem becomes

V(p,\mu)=\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}(p)}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\check{v}(q)]=\left[\operatorname{CAV}(\check{v})\right](p),

where
\mathcal{R}(p):=\left\{\rho\in\Delta(\Delta(\Theta)):\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\tilde{q}]=p\right\}
is the set of Bayes-plausible random posteriors,

\check{v}(q):=\hat{v}(D(q))
is the distorted indirect utility, and

\operatorname{CAV}(f)(p):=\sup\{z\mid(p,z)\in\operatorname{co}(f)\}

denotes the concavification of
f
at
p
, where
co(f)
is the convex hull of the graph of
f
.

In this way, the problem is transformed into a standard Bayesian persuasion problem à la
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
.

3.3
Two-step persuasion

3.3.1
Setup

Now, consider an alternative scenario in which the sender uses two experiments. The process unfolds as follows:

1.

The sender chooses the first experiment
\sigma\in\mathcal{E}
.

2.

The receiver observes the experiment
\sigma
.

3.

The sender and receiver observe a signal realization
s\in S
.

4.

The sender updates his belief from
p
to
\mu_{B}(\sigma,p)(\cdot\mid s)
, and the receiver updates her belief to
\mu(\sigma,p)(\cdot\mid s)
.

5.

The sender chooses the second experiment
\tau\in\mathcal{E}
.

6.

The receiver observes the experiment
\tau
and a signal realization
t\in S
.

7.

The receiver updates her belief from
\mu(\sigma,p)(\cdot\mid s)
to
\mu\left(\tau,\mu(\sigma,p)(\cdot\mid s)\right)(\cdot\mid t)
.

8.

The receiver chooses an action
a\in A
.

The sender makes two sequential decisions.
The second decision, which we refer to as the
interim problem
, is defined by the sender’s belief
\mu_{B}(\sigma,p)(\cdot\mid s)
, the receiver’s belief
\mu(\sigma,p)(\cdot\mid s)
, and the receiver’s updating rule
\mu
.

3.3.2
Solution

The interim problem is a non-Bayesian persuasion problem with heterogeneous priors.
When the receiver’s updating rule systematically distorts posterior beliefs, we can transform this problem into a non-Bayesian persuasion problem with a common prior and a transformed distortion function.
The following lemma—viewed as a generalization of Proposition 1 in
Alonso and Câmara 2016
—formalizes this transformation.

Lemma 1

Let
q:=\mu_{B}(\sigma,p)(\cdot\mid s)
and
r:=\mu_{B}(\tau,q)(\cdot\mid t)
.
Suppose the receiver’s updating rule
\mu
is associated with distortion function
D_{p}
.
Then, the receiver’s updated belief given prior
D_{p}(q)
, experiment
\tau
, and signal
t
is

\mu(\tau,D_{p}(q))(\cdot\mid t)=D_{q}^{II}(r):=D_{D_{p}(q)}\left(\frac{r\cdot\left(\frac{D_{p}(q)}{q}\right)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}r(\theta^{\prime})\cdot\left(\frac{D_{p}(q)(\theta^{\prime})}{q(\theta^{\prime})}\right)}\right).

The transformed distortion function
D_{q}^{II}
corresponds to the following two-step process:

1.

The receiver first reweights the Bayesian posterior
r
computed from the sender’s prior
q
, using her own distorted prior
D_{p}(q)
. This yields the adjusted belief

r^{\prime}:=\frac{r\cdot\left(\frac{D_{p}(q)}{q}\right)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}r(\theta^{\prime})\cdot\left(\frac{D_{p}(q)(\theta^{\prime})}{q(\theta^{\prime})}\right)}.

2.

The receiver then applies her original distortion function given prior
D_{p}(q)
,
D_{D_{p}(q)}
to
r^{\prime}
.

That is, when the sender’s Bayesian posterior in Step 4 is
q
, and the receiver’s distorted belief is
D_{p}(q)
, the interim problem becomes a one-shot non-Bayesian persuasion problem with common prior
q
and distortion function
D_{q}^{II}
.

Suppose that the first experiment
\sigma
induces a distribution of sender posteriors
\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{R}(p)
, and for each
q\in\operatorname{supp}(\rho_{1})
, the second experiment
\tau(q)
chosen by the sender given sender posterior
q
induces a posterior distribution
\rho_{2}(q)\in\mathcal{R}(q)
.
Then, the sender’s ex-ante utility under this two-step persuasion scheme is given by

\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{2}(\tilde{q})}\left[\check{v}^{\tilde{q}}(\tilde{r})\right],

where
\check{v}^{\tilde{q}}(\tilde{r}):=\hat{v}\left(D_{\tilde{q}}^{II}(\tilde{r})\right)
, following
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
.

When the sender optimally chooses Blackwell experiment given
q\in\operatorname{supp}(\rho_{1})
,
his ex-ante value becomes

V(\rho_{1},\mu):=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\operatorname{CAV}\left(\check{v}^{\tilde{q}}\right)\right](\tilde{q})

by
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
’s discussion.

Thus, the sender’s overall two-step persuasion problem reduces to

\mathcal{V}(p,\mu):=\sup_{\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{R}(p)}V(\rho_{1},\mu).

Again as in
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
, this can be rewritten using concavification:

\mathcal{V}(p,\mu)=\left[\operatorname{CAV}(v^{II})\right](p),

where

v^{II}(q):=\left[\operatorname{CAV}\left(\check{v}^{q}\right)\right](q).

Therefore, the sender strictly benefits from conducting optimal two-step persuasion if and only if

\left[\operatorname{CAV}(v^{II})\right](p)>\left[\operatorname{CAV}(\check{v})\right](p).

3.4
Sender’s Indifference Between One-Shot and Two-Step Persuasion

When is the result of
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
without loss? When does the sender gets better or worse off by conducting two-step persuasion? We provide a sufficient condition on receiver’s updating rules, which always leads to sender’s indifference between one-shot and two-step persuasions.

Let
\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu)
be the feasible set of sender-ex-ante payoff given prior
p
and receiver-updating rule
\mu
. That is,

\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu):=\{v\in\mathbb{R}:\text{for some }\rho\in\mathcal{R}(p),v=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\check{v}(\tilde{q})]\}

and
\mathcal{V}^{2}(p,\mu)
be the feasible set from two-step persuasion. That is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{2}(p,\mu):=\bigg\{v\in\mathbb{R}\,\bigg|

\displaystyle\text{ for some }\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{R}(p)\text{ and }\{\rho_{2}(q)\}_{q\in\operatorname{supp}(\rho_{1})}

\displaystyle\text{ with }\rho_{2}(q)\in\mathcal{R}(q)\text{ for all }q\in\operatorname{supp}(\rho_{1}),

\displaystyle\text{ it holds that }v=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{2}(\tilde{q})}\left[\check{v}^{\tilde{q}}(\tilde{r})\right]\right]\bigg\}

Proposition 1

If the receiver’s updating rule is divisible—that is,
\mu=\mu_{F}
for some homeomorphism
F:\Delta(\Theta)\to\Delta(\Theta)
—then for any persuasion environment
(\Theta,p,A,u,v)
, the feasible sets of the sender’s ex-ante payoff from one-shot and two-step persuasion coincide. That is,

\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu^{D}_{F})=\mathcal{V}^{2}(p,\mu^{D}_{F}).

The key for the proof is the fact that under divisible updating,
D_{q}^{II}=D_{p}
, which implies
\check{v}^{q}=\check{v}
. Conversely, if the receiver’s updating rule systematically distorts updated beliefs and is not divisible, then I conjecture there exists a persuasion environment
(\Theta,p,A,u,v)
such that the feasible sets under two-step persuasion and one-shot persuasion do not match.:

\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu^{D}_{F})\neq\mathcal{V}^{2}(p,\mu^{D}_{F}).

The proof of this statement is currently in progress.

Note that, as mentioned in Example
1
, geometric distortion is a special case of a divisible updating rule. Thus, under geometric distortion, the sender is indifferent between one-shot and two-step persuasion:

Corollary 1

If the receiver’s updating rule is a geometric distortion with
\alpha>0
, then the feasible set of the sender’s ex-ante payoff is the same under both one-shot and two-step persuasion:

\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu^{G}_{\alpha})=\mathcal{V}^{2}(p,\mu^{G}_{\alpha}).

Under geometric distortion, the bias introduced reflects the amount of information revealed in the first step. As a result, the extent to which the sender can exploit this bias remains unchanged, even if the sender splits a given amount of information into multiple parts and delivers them sequentially

3.5
Difference in Sender’s Ex-Ante Value

Let us turn to the case where the two-step persuasion makes a difference to a sender. From this section, we focus on a judge-prosecutor persuasion environment with any full-support prior
p
, which slightly generalizes the leading example in Section
2
. Abusing notation, elements in
\Delta(\Theta)
also denote the probability that
\tilde{\theta}=1
.

3.5.1
Optimal Two-Step Persuasion with Grether’s
\alpha-\beta
rule

For many receiver updating rules, the solution method described in Section
3.3.2
can be used to numerically derive the sender’s ex-ante value. However, since
D_{q}^{II}
can be a complex and nonlinear function, it is often difficult to obtain analytical solutions, especially for some updating rules such as linear distortions. In this section, we focus on Grether’s
\alpha
–
\beta
rule, which accommodates both base-rate neglect or over-weighting, as well as geometric distortions.

Proposition 2

Suppose
0<p<0.5
. Then, for any
\beta>0
,

1.

If
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the sender gets strictly better off by two-step persuasion:
\sup\mathcal{V}^{2}(p,\mu_{\alpha,\beta})>\sup\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu_{\alpha,\beta})
.

2.

If
\alpha>1
the sender gets strictly worse off by two-step persuasion:
\sup\mathcal{V}^{2}(p,\mu_{\alpha,\beta})<\sup\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu_{\alpha,\beta})
.

3.

If
\alpha=1
the sender is indifferent between one-shot and two-step persuasion:
\sup\mathcal{V}^{2}(p,\mu_{\alpha,\beta})=\sup\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu_{\alpha,\beta})
.

The final part immediately follows from Corollary
1
. In the proof, we evaluate the concavity and convexity of
v^{II}=\left[CAV\right](\check{v}^{q})(q)
and compare the value of
v^{II}
with its concavification at the prior,
\sup\mathcal{V}^{2}(p,\mu)
. I then compare the value of
v^{II}
with
\sup\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu)=\left[CAV\right](\check{v})(p)
. The conditions on
\alpha
in Proposition
2
are the necessary and sufficient conditions for the convexity and concavity of
v^{II}
, respectively.

The intuition behind Proposition
1
is similar to the one provided in Section
2
with linear distortion.
\alpha\in(0,1)
, which yields base-rate neglect, plays the same role as linear overreaction, and when
\alpha>1
, base-rate over-weighting plays the same role as linear underreaction. Notably, the conclusion of Proposition
2
does not depend on
\beta>0
. This is intuitive in light of Corollary
1
: geometric over- or underweighting of the signal likelihood does not affect the value of delaying information delivery.

A.1
Proof for Lemma
1

By the definition of Bayesian updating rule,

r(\theta)=\frac{q(\theta)\tau_{\theta}(t)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}q(\theta^{\prime})\tau_{\theta^{\prime}}(t)},

which is equivalently transformed into

D_{p}(q)(\theta)\tau_{\theta}(t)=\frac{D_{p}(q)(\theta)}{q(\theta)}r(\theta)\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}q(\theta^{\prime})\tau_{\theta^{\prime}}(t).

(1)

Since the receiver’s updating rule
\mu
systematically distorts updated belief with distortion function
D_{p^{\prime}}
given full support prior
p^{\prime}
,

\mu(\tau,D_{p}(q))(\theta|t)=D_{D_{p}(q)}(\mu_{B}(\tau,D_{p}(q))(\theta|t))=D_{D_{p}(q)}\left(\frac{D_{p}(q)(\theta)\tau_{\theta}(t)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}D_{p}(q)(\theta^{\prime})\tau_{\theta^{\prime}}(t)}\right).

Plugging (
1
) into this expression, we obtain

\mu(\tau,D_{p}(q))(\theta|t)=D_{D_{p}(q)}\left(\frac{r(\theta)\left(\frac{D_{p}(q)(\theta)}{q(\theta)}\right)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}r(\theta^{\prime})\cdot\left(\frac{D_{p}(q)(\theta^{\prime})}{q(\theta^{\prime})}\right)}\right).

A.2
Proof for Proposition
1

Fix any persuasion environment
(\Theta,p,A,u,v)
and suppose the receiver’s updating rule is divisible with homeomorphism
F:\Delta(\Theta)\to\Delta(\Theta)
. Let
q\in\Delta(\Theta)
be the sender’s posterior from the first experiment and its realization,
\tau
be the second experiment,
t
be its realization. Below we show
D_{q}^{II}=D_{p}
. We can use Lemma
1
, but because it leads to a messy display on the paper, we just follow discussion similar to the proof for
1
instead. Let
r
be the second sender posterior, that is,

r(\theta)=\frac{q(\theta)\tau_{\theta}(t)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}q(\theta)\tau_{\theta}(t)}.

Let
D_{p}
be the distortion function associated with divisible updating with homeomorphism
F
, described in Example
1
.
Then, the second-step posterior of the receiver with this divisible updating is

\displaystyle\mu(\tau,D_{p}(q))(\theta|t)

\displaystyle=F^{-1}_{\theta}\left(\left(\frac{F_{\theta}(D_{p}(q))\tau_{\theta}(t)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}F_{\theta^{\prime}}(D_{p}(q))\tau_{\theta^{\prime}}(t)}\right)_{\theta\in\Theta}\right)

\displaystyle=F^{-1}_{\theta}\left(\left(\frac{\frac{F_{\theta}(p)}{p(\theta)}q(\theta)\tau_{\theta}(t)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\frac{F_{\theta^{\prime}}(p)}{p(\theta)}q(\theta^{\prime})\tau_{\theta^{\prime}}(t)}\right)_{\theta\in\Theta}\right)

\displaystyle=F^{-1}\left(\left(\left[\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\frac{\frac{F_{\theta}^{\prime}(p)}{p(\theta^{\prime})}}{\frac{F_{\theta}(p)}{p(\theta)}}\frac{q(\theta^{\prime})}{q(\theta)}\frac{\tau_{\theta^{\prime}}(t)}{\tau_{\theta}(t)}\right]^{-1}\right)_{\theta\in\Theta}\right)

Plugging in
\frac{\tau_{\theta^{\prime}}(t)}{\tau_{\theta}(t)}=\frac{q(\theta)}{q(\theta^{\prime})}\frac{r(\theta^{\prime})}{r(\theta)}
derived from the definition of
r
,

\mu(\tau,D_{p}(q))(\theta|t)=F^{-1}\left(\left(\left[\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\frac{\frac{F_{\theta}^{\prime}(p)}{p(\theta^{\prime})}}{\frac{F_{\theta}(p)}{p(\theta)}}\frac{r(\theta^{\prime})}{r(\theta)}\right]^{-1}\right)_{\theta\in\Theta}\right)=D_{p}(r).

Namely,
D_{q}^{II}(r)=D_{p}(r)
. Thus,
\check{v}^{q}(r)=\check{v}(r)
for any
q
.

Now, pick any
v\in\mathcal{V}^{2}(p,\mu)
. Then, there exists
\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{R}(p)
and
\{\rho_{2}(q)\}_{q\in\operatorname{supp}(p)}
with
\rho_{2}(q)\in\mathcal{R}(q)
for all
q\in\operatorname{supp}(\rho_{1})
such that

v=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{2}(\tilde{q})}\check{v}^{\tilde{q}}(\tilde{r})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{2}(\tilde{q})}\check{v}(\tilde{r})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{*}}[\hat{v}(\tilde{r})],

where
\rho^{*}=\int_{\Delta(\Theta)}\rho_{2}(q)(\cdot)d\rho_{1}(q)
. Note
\rho^{*}\in\mathcal{R}(p)
by the law of iterative expectation. Therefore,
\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{*}}[\hat{v}(\tilde{r})]\in\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu)
, which establishes
\mathcal{V}^{2}(p,\mu)\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu)
. The opposite inclusion is trivial: Take any
v\in\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,m)
. Then, for some
\rho\in\mathcal{R}(p)
,
v=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\check{v}(\tilde{q})]
. Let
\rho_{1}=\rho
and
\rho_{2}(q)=\delta_{\tilde{q}=q}
, which corresponds to no information in the second step. Then,
\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{2}}\check{v}^{\tilde{q}}(\tilde{r})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{2}(\tilde{q})}\check{v}(\tilde{r})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\check{v}(\tilde{q})\right]=v
. This establishes
\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu)\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{2}(p,\mu)
.

A.3
Proof for Proposition
2

Suppose that receiver’s updating rule is Grether’s
\alpha-\beta
rule for some
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
. Then, by applying Lemma
1
or directly following argument in its proof or the proof for Proposition
1
,

D_{q}^{II}(r)(\theta)=\frac{p(\theta)^{\alpha(\alpha-\beta)}q(\theta)^{(\alpha-1)\beta}r(\theta)^{\beta}}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}p(\theta^{\prime})^{\alpha(\alpha-\beta)}q(\theta^{\prime})^{(\alpha-1)\beta}r(\theta^{\prime})^{\beta}}.

Specifically, since we have
\Theta=\{0,1\}
and each element in
\Delta(\Theta)
also denote probabilities for
\tilde{\theta}=1
,

D_{q}^{II}(r)=\frac{p^{\alpha(\alpha-\beta)}q^{(\alpha-1)\beta}r^{\beta}}{p^{\alpha(\alpha-\beta)}q^{(\alpha-1)\beta}r^{\beta}+(1-p)^{\alpha(\alpha-\beta)}(1-q)^{(\alpha-1)\beta}(1-r)^{\beta}}.

Hence, with judge-prosecutor setup,

\check{v}^{q}(r)=\mathbbm{1}\left\{D_{q}^{II}(r)\geq\frac{1}{2}\right\}=\mathbbm{1}\left\{r\geq r_{\alpha,\beta}(p,q)\right\},

where

r_{\alpha,\beta}(p,q)=\frac{(1-p)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}(\alpha-\beta)}(1-q)^{\alpha-1}}{(1-p)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}(\alpha-\beta)}(1-q)^{\alpha-1}+p^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}(\alpha-\beta)}q^{\alpha-1}}.

Thus,

\left[CAV\right](\check{v}^{q})(r)=\begin{cases}\frac{r}{r_{\alpha,\beta}(p,q)}&\text{if }r<r_{\alpha,\beta}(p,q)\\
1&\text{if }r\geq r_{\alpha,\beta}(p,q)\end{cases}

and hence

\begin{aligned} v^{II}(q)&:=\left[CAV\right](\check{v}^{q})(q)\\
&=\begin{cases}\frac{q}{r_{\alpha,\beta}(p,q)}&\text{if }q<r_{\alpha,\beta}(p,q)\\
1&\text{if }q\geq r_{\alpha,\beta}(p,q)\end{cases}\\
&=\begin{cases}q\left[1+\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}(\alpha-\beta)}\left(\frac{q}{1-q}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right]&\text{if }q<q_{\alpha,\beta}(p)\\
1&\text{if }q\geq q_{\alpha,\beta}(p)\end{cases}\end{aligned},

where
q_{\alpha,\beta}(p)=\frac{(1-p)^{\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\beta}}}{(1-p)^{\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\beta}}+p^{\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\beta}}}
. Let
f(q)=q\left[1+\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}(\alpha-\beta)}\left(\frac{q}{1-q}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right]
. Then, with
q\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(q)

\displaystyle=1-\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)^{\frac{\alpha(\alpha-\beta)}{\beta}}\left(\frac{q}{1-q}\right)^{\alpha}+\frac{\alpha\left(-1+\frac{1}{p}\right)^{\alpha-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta}}\left(-1+\frac{1}{q}\right)^{-\alpha}}{q}

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(q)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)^{\frac{\alpha(\alpha-\beta)}{\beta}}\left(\frac{q}{1-q}\right)^{\alpha}(\alpha-1)\alpha}{(1-q)q^{2}}

Note
f^{\prime\prime}(q)\leq 0
if
\alpha\geq 1
.
f^{\prime\prime}(q)>0
if
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Therefore, given
q<q_{\alpha,\beta}(p)
,
v^{II}
is concave if
\alpha\geq 1
, and it is strictly convex if
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Therefore,

\sup\mathcal{V}^{2}(p,\mu)=\left[CAV\right](v^{II})(p)\begin{cases}=v^{II}(p)&\text{if }\alpha\geq 1\\
>v^{II}(p)&\text{if }\alpha\in(0,1)\end{cases}

given
p\in(0,1)
.

Note that

v^{II}(p)=\left[CAV\right](v^{p})(p)=\begin{cases}p\left[1+\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)^{\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta}-1}\right]&\text{if }p<\frac{1}{2}\\
1&\text{if }p\geq\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

But

\sup\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu)=\left[CAV\right](\check{v})(p)=\begin{cases}p\left[1+\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1}\right]&\text{if }p<\frac{1}{2}\\
1&\text{if }p\geq\frac{1}{2}\end{cases}

Thus, if
p<\frac{1}{2}
,

v^{II}(p)-\sup\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu)=\begin{cases}>0&\text{if }\alpha\in(0,1)\\
=0&\text{if }\alpha=1\\
<0&\text{if }\alpha>1\\
\end{cases}

Therefore,

\sup\mathcal{V}^{2}(p,\mu)\begin{cases}>\sup\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu)&\text{if }\alpha\in(0,1)\\
=\sup\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu)&\text{if }\alpha=1\\
<\sup\mathcal{V}^{1}(p,\mu)&\text{if }\alpha>1,\end{cases}

which completes the proof.
