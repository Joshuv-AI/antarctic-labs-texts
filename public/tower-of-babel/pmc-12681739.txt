Efficiently enhancing ferroptosis-mediated immunosensitization in lung cancer by an inhalable immune activation nanoplatform
Authors: Zhimin Chen, Yanze Yin, Changjie Yang, Li Ye, Mei Yang, Han Du, Yongfei Fan, Ding-Kun Ji, Yayi He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Immune checkpoint blockade therapy holds promise for inducing lung cancer regression, but its effectiveness is often limited due to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (ITME). Ferroptosis can remodel the ITME and enhance immunotherapy, but traditional inducers exhibit weak immune modulation due to off-target toxicity and low drug concentrations at the tumor site, resulting in poor efficacy. To address these challenges, we explore an inhalable immune activation nanoplatform, named LMS, designed to enhance ferroptosis-mediated immunosensitization in lung cancer. LMS owns high ferroptosis inducer loading and multienzyme catalytic activity. By nebulization delivery, we demonstrate that LMS can fast and efficiently delivery Statins to lung cancer with minimal systemic toxicity. LMS effectively triggers an oxidative stress storm and lipid peroxidation burst to induce ferroptosis in tumor cells, while leveraging ferroptosis and dendritic cell maturation to synergistically enhance T cell immune responses, thereby reversing ITME. Our nanoplatform achieved efficient tumor suppression in an in-situ lung cancer model, demonstrating the great potential of the inhalable immune activation nanoplatform in ferroptosis-regulated cancer immunotherapy. This work paves a new path for safely and efficiently reversing the ITME in lung cancer, potentially accelerating the development of inhalable immune nanoagonists for clinical applications in cancer immunotherapy.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the deadliest malignancies worldwide, with a five-year survival rate of less than 19 % [1]. Traditional chemotherapy and radiotherapy are difficult to achieve precise treatment due to problems such as tumor heterogeneity, drug resistance, and systemic toxicity [2]. Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy has emerged as a promising strategy for lung cancer treatment [3,4]; however, the majority of patients exhibit poor responses or develop resistance, primarily due to the presence of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (ITME) [5,6]. The ITME promotes the accumulation of immunosuppressive cells, such as M2 macrophages, and impairs the infiltration and function of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) [7]. Therefore, effectively remodeling the immune microenvironment is essential for improving the efficacy of immunotherapy in lung cancer.

In recent years, ferroptosis, as a method of cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, has attracted much attention for its unique killing potential for tumors [[8], [9], [10]]. Emerging evidence indicates that ferroptosis not only induces tumor cell death [[11], [12], [13]] but also triggers immunogenic signaling through the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [14], enhancement of antigen presentation, and activation of immune effector cells. These DAMPs function analogously to pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) by promoting the maturation of dendritic cells (DCs), which, in turn, facilitate the activation of T cells, thereby amplifying the anti-tumor immune response [15]. Therefore, triggering immunogenic ferroptosis in tumor cells holds significant implications for lung cancer immunotherapy. However, the clinical translation of ferroptosis inducers in tumor treatment still faces a series of challenges, including strong systemic toxicity of drugs, poor targeting, and low bioavailability [16,17]. Statins, widely used as lipid-lowering agents in clinical practice, have recently been found to possess the potential to induce ferroptosis by inhibiting key regulators such as glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) [18,19]. Preliminary studies suggest that statins may exert immune-activating effects in cancer therapy, making them promising adjuvants for combination with immunotherapy [20,21]. However, statins are typically used at relatively low doses (e.g., 20–40 mg simvastatin per patient per day) for lipid-lowering therapy in clinical practice. To achieve potential anti-cancer effects, higher doses (e.g., 60 mg simvastatin per kg per day) are required [22]. Moreover, as the dosage increases, the risk of side effects associated with statins also increases. Therefore, there is an urgent need for an efficient delivery method to deliver hydrophobic ferroptosis inducers that can achieve high lung delivery, reduce systemic side effects, and possess strong immune activation ability.

Notably, Inhalation delivery represents a non-invasive and efficient strategy for lung cancer therapy, enabling direct deposition of nanodrugs into the deep lung via aerosolization [23]. This significantly increases local drug concentration (10–100 times higher than intravenous administration) while reducing toxic exposure to distant organs such as the heart and liver [24,25]. Nonetheless, the airway mucus barrier poses a major obstacle to inhaled nanomedicines, as its viscoelastic network of mucins can trap particles through hydrophobic, electrostatic, or size-based interactions, thereby limiting pulmonary deposition and therapeutic efficacy [24]. Molybdenum disulfide (MoS2), with its high surface area [26], tunable surface chemistry [27], and strong affinity for hydrophobic drugs [28], enables efficient loading of statins into nanoscale formulations (<1 μm, negatively charged). Based on this, the construction of a MoS2-based inhalable system capable of inducing immunogenic ferroptosis holds great promise for enhancing antitumor immune responses and improving the therapeutic efficacy against lung cancer. However, to the best of our knowledge, this has not yet been realized.

In this study, a novel inhalable immune activation nanoplatform (named LMS) was developed to enhance ferroptosis-mediated immunosensitization in lung cancer through nebulization delivery. Simvastatin was loaded on 2D MoS2 nanosheet by flexible self-assembly with a high drug loading. The resulting complex named MS was further encapsulated into phospholipid-based liposomes, resulting in the LMS with uniform size and spherical structure (Scheme 1). We demonstrated that LMS can efficiently deliver MS to cancer cells. The MoS2 component catalyzes hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) via a Fenton-like reaction, generating hydroxyl radicals (·OH) to boost intracellular ROS levels while releasing simvastatin. Additionally, simvastatin activates overexpressed polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in tumor cells, producing abundant lipid ROS that trigger lipid peroxidation and ultimately induce ferroptosis. Importantly, LMS-induced ferroptosis promotes the release of DAMPs. These DAMPs stimulate dendritic cell maturation, enhance T-cell activation, effectively reverse immunosuppression, and initiate a robust anti-tumor immune response.Scheme 1Schematic Illustration of remodeling Tumor Immune Microenvironment and Promoting Ferroptosis through LMS.Scheme 1

Experimental methods

Synthesis of 2D MoS2 nanosheets

Based on previous methods, stable molybdenum disulfide nanosheets were synthesized through a hydrothermal synthesis approach [29]. First, 120 mg of MoO3, 140 mg of thioacetamide, and 1.2 g of urea were dissolved in 100 mL of deionized water and magnetically stirred for 2 h. The resulting mixture was then transferred to a high-pressure autoclave and heated at 180 °C for 10 h in a high-temperature oven. Subsequently, the autoclave was removed from the oven and allowed to cool to room temperature to terminate the reaction. Finally, the product was washed several times with deionized water, followed by storage in deionized water before use.

Preparation and characterization of MS

First, 1 mg of methyl-DSPE-PEG was dissolved in 1 mL of PBS, followed by the addition of 1 mg of simvastatin. The mixture was magnetically stirred for 30 min. Next, 1 mg of MoS2 aqueous solution was added, and the mixture was stirred for 1 h under magnetic agitation. Finally, the unencapsulated drug was removed by centrifugation and filtration. The resulting complex was re-dispersed and stored at 4 °C for future use. The collected nanocomposite was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer at 230 nm to determine its absorbance, and the drug loading capacity was calculated based on the absorbance values.

The Drug Loading Efficiency (DLE) and Encapsulation Efficiency (EE) of simvastatin in the MS nanocomplexes were determined. After preparation, the nanocomplexes were separated from the aqueous medium by centrifugation to isolate the unencapsulated drug. The amount of free drug in the supernatant was quantified via UV–Vis spectrophotometry at 230 nm. The DLC and EE were then calculated using the following standard formulas:DLE(%)=(Weightofdruginnanoparticles/Totalweightofnanoparticles)x100EE(%)=[(Totaldrugadded–Freenon−entrappeddrug)/Totaldrugadded]x100

Synthesis and characterization of LMS

LMS was synthesized using the film dispersion-ultrasonication method. Briefly, 36 mg of soybean phospholipid and 9 mg of cholesterol were dissolved in 5 mL of dichloromethane, and the dichloromethane was completely evaporated using a rotary evaporator under vacuum. Then, 8 mL of PBS and a solution containing 2 mg of MS were added, and the system was sonicated in a water bath for 5 min. The resulting system was then processed using a sonicator in an ice bath at 300 W for 3 min, followed by filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane to obtain the LMS.

The size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of LMS were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS) on a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments Ltd.). The morphology of LMS was obtained using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The absorbance at 238 nm was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer.

Detection of ROS level

To evaluate ROS generation in LLC cells, cells were treated with PBS, liposome nanoparticle (Lnp), LM (containing 2.5 μg mL−1 molybdenum disulfide), LS (containing 2.5 μg mL−1 simvastatin), or LMS (containing 2.5 μg mL−1 simvastatin) for 6 h. DCFH-DA probe staining was performed, followed by CLSM analysis to quantify intracellular ROS levels.

Lipid peroxidation (LPO) assay

Lipid peroxidation induced by LMS was quantified using the BODIPY-C11 probe. LLC cells were incubated with PBS, Lnp, LM (containing 2.5 μg mL−1 molybdenum disulfide), LS (containing 2.5 μg mL−1 simvastatin), or LMS (containing 2.5 μg mL−1 simvastatin) for 24 h. Following treatment, cells were stained with BODIPY-C11 and analyzed by CLSM to visualize and quantify LPO levels.

Mitochondrial membrane. Potential and mitochondria distribution

To measure the mitochondrial membrane potential, LLC cells were treated with PBS, Lnp, LM, LS, or LMS for 12 h and stained with the JC-1 probe for analysis by CLSM. To assess mitochondrial distribution, after treatment with PBS, Lnp, LM, LS, or LMS for 12 h, LLC cells were washed with PBS, stained with the MitoTracker Deep Green FM probe for 30 min, and analyzed by CLSM.

Induction of DC cell maturation in vitro

Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were isolated from C57BL/6 mice. DC maturation was induced using a 12-well Transwell® system with 0.4-μm polycarbonate membranes (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA). Briefly, LLC cells were seeded in the upper chamber of the Transwell® system at 5 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 12 h. The cells were then treated with PBS, Lnp, LM (2.5 μg mL−1 molybdenum disulfide), LS (2.5 μg mL−1 simvastatin), or LMS (2.5 μg mL−1 simvastatin) for 24 h. After replacing the medium with fresh DMEM, BMDCs (1 × 106 cells per well) were added to the lower chamber and co-cultured for an additional 24 h. Harvested DCs were stained with rabbit anti-mouse antibodies: CD11c-FITC (1:100; BD Pharmingen), CD80-PE (1:100; BD Pharmingen), and CD86-PE-Cy7 (1:100; BD Pharmingen). Stained cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSCelesta™, BD Biosciences).

The DCs culture fluid was also collected and the concentrations of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-12p70 in the culture fluid were measured by the standard procedure of the ELISA kit.

Establishment of the tumor model

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines of Shanghai Jiao Tong University and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Renji Hospital of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Number: RJ2024-2298). The orthotopic lung tumor murine model was developed by injecting 1.0 × 106 LLC-luc cells in 25 μL of an injectable mixture (containing 12.5 μL PBS and 12.5 μL Matrigel) into the left lung of mice (BALB/c nude or C57BL/6). Tumor growth was monitored using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS Lumina Series III, PerkinElmer Inc., Waltham, MA, USA).

In vivo antitumor efficacy

After successfully establishing the orthotopic lung tumor model, bioluminescent imaging was performed on tumor-bearing mice to confirm tumor engraftment. Subsequently, mice with confirmed tumors were randomly divided into five treatment groups: (1) PBS (control group), (2) Lnp (blank liposome group), (3) LM (molybdenum disulfide liposome group), (4) LS (simvastatin liposome group), and (5) LMS group. The sample size for each group was 5 mice (n = 5). Administration was conducted daily via aerosol inhalation, with each session lasting 30 min, and the dose of simvastatin was equivalent to 40 mg kg−1. No animals were excluded from the study after randomization. The primary endpoints were tumor growth (monitored by bioluminescent imaging every other day) and overall survival. Body weight was measured every other day as an indicator of systemic toxicity. The efficacy study was terminated on day 14, at which point the mice were euthanized according to standard protocols. The heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were collected for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining. In addition, Ki-67 staining and GPX4 immunohistochemical staining were performed on lung tissues following standard procedures.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of LMS

First, we synthesized stable and flexible 2D metallic MoS2 nanosheets by the hydrothermal method with a high yield [30]. The morphology of the MoS2 nanosheets was then analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Under optimized conditions, nanosheets had an approximate size of 200 nm (Fig. 1a). Subsequently, MoS2 was stirred 1:1 with simvastatin dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing PEG. Promptly following this, the MoS2-loaded simvastatin (MS) was acquired through a combination of centrifugation and resuspension procedures. In the PBS dispersion, the MS has a characteristic absorption peak at 238 nm, which is similar to simvastatin (Fig. 1b). The results indicated that the drug was successfully encapsulated. When the weight ratio (MoS2: simvastatin) was 1:1, the drug loading efficiency of MoS2 to simvastatin was 47.95 %, and the encapsulation efficiency was 92.16 %. Moreover, under the TEM, a clear MS structure can be observed (Fig. 1c). We found that the particle size of MS becomes smaller than that of MoS2 after drug loading. This occurs because the coating of simvastatin increases the hydrophobicity of the 2D MoS2 surface, leading to the folding of the MoS2-simvastatin complex [31]. Next, the nanozymes-mimicking activities were measured. According to the previous research, 2D MoS2 nanosheets usually possess dual nanozyme activities similar to peroxidase (POD) and catalase (CAT) [32]. They can respectively catalyze tumor-endogenous H2O2 into •OH and O2 [33,34]. The catalytic ability of MS to generate •OH from H2O2 was evaluated by measuring the degradation of methylene blue (MB) using UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy. The results, shown in Fig. 1d and e, indicate that as the concentration of MS solution or the reaction time increased, the UV absorbance of MB gradually decreased, demonstrating an enhancement in degradation ability. This suggests that the production of •OH increased with both the MS solution concentration and the reaction time. Additionally, MS can also catalyze the production of O2 from H2O2. The oxygen generation in the system was monitored using an oxygen meter. The results, shown in Fig. 1f, demonstrate that as the reaction time increased, the concentration of O2 in the solution gradually increased. Moreover, the higher the concentration of H2O2 in the system, the greater the concentration of O2 produced by the MS.

To enable the simvastatin-loaded MoS2 nanosheets for lung cancer treatment via nebulized inhalation, liposomes were synthesized using a film ultrasonic method and further encapsulated with MS to form simvastatin-loaded MoS2 liposomes (LMS). The hydrodynamic diameter (DH) of the liposomes increased from 71.61 ± 1.128 nm to 93.71 ± 0.755 nm (Fig. 1g). After encapsulating LMS, the zeta potential of the liposomes was −9.5 ± 0.544 mV (Fig. 1h). The morphology of LMS was analyzed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM), revealing that the MoS2 nanosheets were successfully encapsulated by the liposomes (Fig. 1i). The elemental mapping showed the homogeneous distributions of Mo, S, C, and O in the LMS (Fig. 1j), illustrating the dispersive decorating of LMS. To further confirm LMS, UV–Vis spectroscopy was performed. As shown in Fig. 1k, MS exhibited a characteristic absorption peak around 238 nm; liposomes displayed a characteristic absorption peak around 210 nm; and LMS exhibited absorption between 210 and 238 nm, confirming that the liposomes successfully encapsulated the MS. Furthermore, we found that both MS (Fig. S1a) and LMS (Fig. S1b) exhibited a significant burst release in the presence of H2O2. This confirms that we have successfully constructed a hydrogen peroxide-responsive platform, wherein the MoS2 core acts as a highly efficient trigger for H2O2-mediated drug release.

Pulmonary delivery and distribution behavior of LMS

After obtaining the LMS, a comprehensive evaluation of the feasibility of LMS for lung delivery was first conducted to ensure accurate targeting of the lungs. Compared to other traditional drug delivery methods, such as oral administration, pulmonary delivery has been confirmed owing to better accumulation in pulmonary lesions and higher therapeutic efficacy [35,36]. The LMS suspension was atomized using an ultrasonic nebulizer attached to a self-designed delivery device (Fig. 2a). The distribution uniformity of the LMS atomized was investigated via a self-designed nebulization device. As shown in Fig. 2b and c, the fluorescence intensity across five independent channels showed no significant differences, indicating good atomization uniformity. After nebulizing the LMS suspension, changes in particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were negligible (Fig. 2d–f), demonstrating that the structural integrity of LMS was maintained during the nebulization process. The atomization characteristics of LMS were next evaluated by measuring nebulization time, output efficiency, and output rate. The nebulization time was approximately 0.30 ± 0.01 mL min−1, with an output efficiency of 88.09 ± 0.67 % and an output rate of 0.30 ± 0.01 g min−1, all of which are relatively high values [37], indicating a superb nebulization profile of LMS (Fig. 2g–i). In the process of drug delivery, one of the primary barriers to effective drug delivery to the lung is the presence of normal airway mucus, which covers the airway epithelium [39,40]. Drugs can become trapped within the airway mucus gel layer and are rapidly cleared from the lungs through mucociliary clearance or cough-driven expulsion, resulting in inadequate delivery and reduced therapeutic efficacy [41]. To investigate the extent of mucus penetration, we synthesized a cystic fibrosis artificial mucus (CF-AM) to evaluate whether LMS could traverse the airway mucus barrier. We found that rhodamine B-labeled LMS (Rhob-LMS) gradually penetrated the mucus layer over time (Fig. S3). Additionally, whether aerosolized drug inhalation induces airway hyperresponsiveness is a crucial aspect of aerosol drug safety. To assess this, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection and subjected to tracheal intubation. Tidal volume (TV), lung resistance (RL), and dynamic lung compliance (Cdyn) were measured before and after LMS aerosol administration. The results indicated no significant changes in TV, RL, or Cdyn before and after aerosolization, suggesting that LMS inhalation does not induce airway hyperresponsiveness (Fig. S4). Furthermore, the biodistribution of the nebulized liposomes (LLC-luc cells) was then tracked in a syngeneic lung tumor mouse model. We found that RhoB-LMS was predominantly distributed in the liver after intravenous injection, and only a small amount reached the lungs. In contrast, the lung accumulation of LMS following inhalation increased by approximately 3.4-fold higher than that of in intravenous injection group (Fig. 2j and k). These biodistribution results clearly demonstrate the significant organ-level targeting advantage of the inhalation route, which achieves a much higher accumulation of LMS in the lungs and tumor compared to intravenous injection. In summary, our LMS platform can be evenly dispersed in the trachea after nebulized inhalation and efficiently crosses the mucus barrier. According to previous research, after penetrating the mucus, these nanoparticles could deposit in the alveoli, enter the lung interstitium via transcytosis, and accumulate in the tumor through the EPR effect [42,43].

The anti-tumor effect of LMS in vitro

We next evaluated the anti-tumor effect of LMS in vitro. The intracellular uptake characteristics of LMS at different incubation times were investigated. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was used to observe the process of LMS uptake into LLC cells after co-incubation. As shown in Fig. 3a, as the incubation time extended from 0 h to 8 h, the fluorescence intensity of the LMS increased in a time-dependent manner, and the latter showed the highest fluorescence intensity after 8 h incubation. This process is likely mediated by endocytosis, a common pathway for the uptake of lipid-based nanoparticles by cancer cells, which often exhibit enhanced endocytic activity [44]. To evaluate its in vitro antitumor effect, the cytotoxicity of LMS was determined by the CCK-8 assay. The results indicated that LMS at 2.5 μg mL−1 can kill 50 % of tumor cells, and its cytotoxicity exhibits a clear dose-dependent relationship (Fig. 3b). In addition, we found that LMS can disrupt the integrity of the cell membrane. As shown in Fig. 3c, compared to the PBS group, LMS significantly disrupted the integrity of the LLC cell membrane, demonstrating its antitumor effect. Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) staining further showed that LMS significantly promoted apoptosis in LLC cells (42.97 % ± 6.14 in early apoptosis and 9.99 % ± 2.15 in late apoptosis, and the apoptosis rates were about 4.0 times, 4.3 times, 4.5 times and 2.0 times higher than those in the control group, Lnp group, LM group and LS group, respectively) (Fig. 3d). Next, we investigated the migratory ability of the cells using transwell (Fig. 3e) and scratch assays (Fig. 3f). The results demonstrated that LMS significantly inhibited the migration of LLC cells (Fig. 3e–h). LMS-induced ferroptosis in vitro

To further investigate the potential mechanisms underlying cell killing, transcriptomic analysis was performed on LLC cells exposed to LMS. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were employed to explore the differential effects of LMS nanoparticles on biological processes and pathways. As indicated by the GO annotation (Fig. 4a), LMS significantly affected several biological processes, including the cell cycle, autophagy, oxidative stress, iron ion homeostasis, iron ion transport, and iron ion transmembrane transport. Furthermore, LMS notably impacted various signaling pathways, including apoptosis, PI3K-Akt signaling, TNF signaling, cell cycle, autophagy, mTOR signaling, ferroptosis, non-small cell lung cancer, TGF-β signaling, Hippo signaling, Toll-like receptor signaling, fatty acid degradation, fatty acid metabolism, and AMPK signaling (Fig. 4b). GO and KEGG pathway analyses indicated that LMS promotes ferroptosis in tumor cells. To further explore the ability of LMS to induce ferroptosis in tumor cells in vitro, we continued our investigation using LLC cells. The intracellular levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxides (LPO) were assessed by staining with DCFH-DA and BODIPY581/591C11. As expected, LMS significantly increased the production of ROS (18.6-fold, 18.4-fold, 3.8-fold, and 2.6-fold, respectively) (Fig. 4c) and LPO (19.4-fold, 19.5-fold, 6.0-fold, and 3.8-fold, respectively) (Fig. 4d) compared to the PBS, Lnp, LM, and LS groups. Given that ferroptosis is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, we investigated the quantity and activity of mitochondria. After LMS treatment, a reduction in green fluorescence indicated a diminished mitochondrial distribution, suggesting a decrease in mitochondrial number and loss of mitochondrial activity (Fig. 4e). To further assess mitochondrial function, we used JC-1 to evaluate changes in mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) (Fig. 4f). In the control group, red fluorescence indicated higher MMP, whereas after LMS treatment, there was a significant increase in green fluorescence, indicating depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane potential. The strongest green signal was observed after LMS treatment, confirming mitochondrial dysfunction due to the reprogramming of mitochondria. Furthermore, the GPX4-GSH system is an essential defense mechanism against ferroptosis [45,46]. After LMS treatment, only about 25.39 % ± 12.97 of GPX4 was expressed in LLC cells (Fig. 4g and h), and about 51.6 % of intracellular GSH levels were reduced (Fig. 4i). In conclusion, these results demonstrate that LMS efficiently induces ferroptosis in tumor cells. This also explains the apoptosis seen in Fig. 3d. We propose that the initial ferroptotic insult, particularly the severe mitochondrial damage [47], triggers apoptosis as a downstream consequence, forming a synergistic cell-killing cascade.

Therefore, we further investigated whether the ferroptosis induced by LMS in tumor cells is associated with the release of DAMPs. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) results demonstrated that LMS significantly enhanced the release of HMGB1 (approximately 2.3-fold, 2.3-fold, 1.9-fold, and 1.3-fold higher than the control, Lnp group, LM group, and LS group, respectively) (Fig. 5a). Confocal microscopy images confirmed that LMS downregulated the expression of HMGB1 in LLC cells (Fig. 5b) and upregulated the expression of CRT on the cell membrane surface (Fig. 5c). To further explore the functional status of BMDCs, we quantified the secretion levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-12p70, using ELISA (Fig. 5g–j). Compared to the control group, BMDCs in the LMS group exhibited 5.9-fold upregulation in TNF-α secretion (Figs. 5g), 2.7-fold upregulation in IL-6 (Figs. 5h), 6.3-fold upregulation in IL-1β (Figs. 5i), and 2.4-fold upregulation in IL-12p70 secretion (Fig. 5j). In vivo antitumor effects of LMS

Inspired by the promising in vitro performance of LMS, we conducted an in-depth investigation into the in vivo anti-tumor effects of nebulized LMS using an LLC-luc cell-derived in situ lung tumor mouse model. Bioluminescence imaging was employed to monitor the growth of LLC-luc lung cancer cells. The tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into five groups, and PBS, Lnp, LM, LS, or LMS were nebulized daily (Fig. 6a). The strong signal in the control group confirmed the invasiveness and proliferative ability of LLC-luc cells (Fig. 6b and d). In the LMS group, the bioluminescence intensity of excised lungs on day 14 was the lowest (Fig. 6c), which was consistent with the in vivo results (Fig. 6b). Nebulized LM and LS showed limited inhibition of lung cancer growth (approximately 2.3-fold and 2.8-fold that of the PBS group, respectively), whereas nebulized LMS demonstrated significant anti-tumor effects (approximately 4.7-fold that of the control group). The LMS group exhibited the slowest tumor growth curve (Fig. 6e–j), indicating the highest tumor suppression efficacy. Unexpectedly, the body weight of tumor-bearing mice steadily increased throughout the treatment period, suggesting excellent biocompatibility and safety (Fig. 6k). Additionally, LMS significantly improved the survival rate of tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 6l). In summary, nebulized LMS exhibited remarkable anti-tumor capabilities, suggesting its potential as an effective cancer treatment.

Furthermore, pathological analysis of lung tissue slices was conducted. Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining of lung tumors in the control group revealed tightly packed tumor cells with high activity (Fig. S5a), while LMS significantly suppressed both tumor cell density and viability. Additionally, Ki-67 staining showed an increase in the number of dead cancer cells after LMS nebulization, with a clear inhibition of cell proliferation and metastasis (Fig.S5b up). GPX4, as a crucial regulator of ferroptosis, was also evaluated for its expression levels. Nebulized LMS induced the lowest expression of GPX4 (Fig. S5b down), confirming the occurrence of ferroptosis. In conclusion, LMS exhibited highly effective anti-tumor activity in vivo through the induction of ferroptosis, confirming that LMS is a promising candidate for lung cancer therapy.

A comprehensive evaluation of the tracheal tissue pathology and systemic safety of LMS was conducted (Fig. S6). H&E staining of the trachea confirmed the particular safety of the lipid nanoparticles (Fig. S6a). No significant pathological changes were observed in major organs upon H&E staining, and there were no notable differences in organ indices (Fig. S6b). Hepatotoxicity was assessed by measuring alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), nephrotoxicity was evaluated using uric acid (UA) and creatinine (CREA), and cardiotoxicity was determined by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB). The blood biochemistry results are normal (Fig. S7). These results suggest that LMS exhibits excellent biocompatibility and safety. While our study confirms superior short-term biocompatibility, the long-term fate of the MoS2 component is critical. Fortunately, MoS2 nanosheets are biodegradable, breaking down via enzymatic and oxidative pathways into low-toxicity, excretable molybdate and sulfate ions [53]. Molybdate, an essential trace element, can also be metabolically integrated. Our previous study also confirmed that 2D MoS2 can be biotransformed into various molybdenum-based nanostructures in the alveolar macrophages and the extracellular vesicles for up to 1 month, without causing inflammation [54].

Antitumor immunity induced by LMS treatment

Based on the excellent therapeutic effects of LMS on primary tumors, it is important to further analyze the anti-tumor immune activation induced by LMS treatment. Initially, bioluminescence imaging was used to monitor the growth of LLC-luc lung cancer cells. C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into five groups and were treated with daily inhalation of PBS, Lnp, LM, LS, or LMS (Fig. S8a). In the control group, strong bioluminescence signals confirmed the invasiveness and proliferative activity of LLC-luc cells (Fig. S8b). The LMS group showed the lowest bioluminescence intensity in ex vivo lungs on day 14 (Fig. S8c), which was consistent with the results observed in nude mice.

To investigate the immune response induced by LMS treatment, the activation of various immune cell types post-treatment was assessed. First, dendritic cell (DC) maturation in the tumor-draining lymph nodes was quantified using flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 7a and b, the DC maturation rate in the LMS group was 59.47 % ± 0.93 %, significantly higher than the 17.3 % ± 0.62 % observed in the control group, indicating that LMS treatment effectively triggered DC maturation. Furthermore, the activation of T cells by mature dendritic cells was analyzed, as these cells play a critical role in immune-mediated tumor cell killing, particularly through cytotoxic T cells (CD3+ and CD8+). Flow cytometry was used to detect T cell activation and infiltration in the tumor. Compared to the control group (12.03 % ± 0.81 % in the primary tumor), the LMS group showed a significant increase in the infiltration of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells within the primary tumor (29.87 % ± 0.15 %) (Fig. 7c and d). These results suggest that LMS treatment effectively activates the mouse immune system, enabling it to recognize and kill tumor cells. In the tumor microenvironment (TME), M1 macrophages play a critical positive regulatory role in tumor immunotherapy [55]. To further investigate the impact of LMS treatment on macrophage phenotypes in the TME, we analyzed the infiltration of M1 macrophages, characterized by F4/80+ and CD86+ markers, into the tumor tissue using flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 7e and f, the infiltration of M1 macrophages in the tumor tissue of the LMS group was significantly higher compared to the other groups. In the primary tumor, M1 macrophages accounted for 51.33 % ± 0.86 % of the total immune cells, which was 1.7 times higher than the control groups. These results indicate that LMS treatment promotes the activation of macrophages in the tumor microenvironment, driving them to adopt the anti-tumor M1 phenotype. Furthermore, the infiltration of M2 macrophages, characterized by F4/80+ and CD206+ markers, was assessed. As shown in Fig. 7g and h, the LMS group exhibited a significantly lower infiltration of M2 macrophages compared to the other groups. These findings suggest that LMS treatment can effectively induce M1 polarization of macrophages in the tumor microenvironment, which is beneficial for anti-tumor immunity. In summary, LMS treatment enhances the infiltration and polarization of M1 macrophages, supporting its potential to modulate the tumor microenvironment in favor of anti-tumor immunity.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a critical role in regulating immune responses through various cytokines [56]. Immunosuppressive cytokines such as TGF-β [57] and IL-10 [58] can inhibit the immune function of tumor-specific cytotoxic T cells by blocking the release of key proteins involved in the “cytotoxic program,” including perforin, granzymes, and cytotoxins. On the other hand, pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IFN-γ directly kill tumor cells and promote anti-tumor immune responses [59,60]. To further explore the changes in the tumor immune microenvironment following LMS treatment, we quantitatively analyzed the levels of major immune-regulating cytokines within the tumor tissue using ELISA. As shown in Fig. 7i–l, compared with the control group, the levels of immunosuppressive cytokines TGF-β and IL-10 were down-regulated by 1.8-fold and 1.8-fold, respectively, while the levels of anti-tumor cytokines TNF-α and IFN-γ were significantly up-regulated by 3.6-fold and 3.0-fold, respectively. These results provide strong evidence that LMS treatment effectively reverses the immunosuppressive microenvironment of the tumor and activates systemic anti-tumor immune responses. This finding highlights LMS as a promising therapeutic strategy that not only targets the tumor directly but also modulates the immune microenvironment to enhance the overall anti-tumor immune response.

Conclusion

In this study, we have successfully developed a novel inhalable immune activation nanoplatform to promote ferroptosis-mediated immunesensitization for lung cancer immunotherapy. The LMS nanoplatform owns high hydrophobic drug loading, uniform size and structure. Via nebulized inhalation, LMS can efficiently accumulate and deliver MS in lung cancer lesions with minimal systemic toxicity. Once internalized by tumor cells, the released MoS2 in MS complex can catalyze hydrogen peroxide to produce ·OH and release simvastatin. Furthermore, Simvastatin induces apoptosis and produces ROS, triggering lipid peroxidation, which in turn activates ferroptosis in tumor cells. We demonstrated that Ferroptotic tumor cells release DAMPs, such as CRT exposure and HMGB1 secretion, which act as immunogenic PAMPs, stimulating dendritic cell maturation, T cell activation, and tumor infiltration. Additionally, LMS promotes TAM polarization to the anti-tumor M1 phenotype, enhancing immune responses in synergy with cytotoxic T cells. This innovative strategy achieves efficient lung tumor suppression by promoting ferroptosis and activating autoimmunity, offering efficient inhalable nanomedicines for lung cancer immunotherapy. This work could accelerate the development of inhalable metal-based immune nanoagonists for clinical applications in cancer immunotherapy.

This work positions LMS as a potent immunosensitizing agent that transforms “cold” tumors into “hot” ones. When combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), LMS has the potential to enhance treatment efficacy. By priming the tumor for an immune attack while ICIs release the brakes on the immune system, this strategy could overcome ICI resistance and broaden the benefits of immunotherapy.
