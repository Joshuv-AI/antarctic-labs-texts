Stented vs. stentless in aortic stenosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Abdelrahman Saeed, Kyrillos Mahrous Gerges, Omar Saeed, Mirvat Alasnag, Richard Whitlock, Patrick O. Myers, Lognathen Balacumaraswami, Alzahraa Faris Alesawy, Ahmed Abraheem, Dina Elraggal, Dua Rajab Khalleefah, Abdulmoez Fauzi Elmesherghi, Ammar Fahaid, Nizar Aljefairi, Muhammed Elhadi, Mamas Mamas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSymptomatic aortic stenosis (AS) is often treated surgically with aortic valve replacement using stented or stentless prostheses. While stentless valves offer potential hemodynamic benefits, a robust synthesis of the evidence regarding clinical outcomes is needed. This systematic review and meta-analysis assessed the effectiveness and safety of both valve types.MethodsThis study followed the PRISMA guidelines and was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42025636952). We systematically searched PubMed, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Web of Science up to December 2024, with a search update in May 2025. We included both randomized and non-randomized studies. The outcomes were early and overall mortality rates, stroke, pacemaker implantation, endocarditis, postoperative atrial fibrillation, total reoperation, severe prosthesis-patient mismatch, hospital stay, cardiopulmonary bypass time, cross-clamp time, and hemodynamic parameters, including postoperative mean aortic valve gradient, left ventricular mass and left ventricular mass index, and left ventricular ejection fraction. A random-effects model with odds ratios (ORs) and mean differences (MDs) was employed.ResultsAfter screening 1,385 records, 20 studies were included, involving 3,940 participants with AS. Studies were first analyzed by design (randomized and non-randomized), and then pooled. There were no significant differences in all-cause mortality rates either early (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: [0.65 to 1.68], p = 0.861) or overall mortality at 1 year (OR = 0.86, 95% CI: [0.28 to 2.63], p = 0.797) and beyond 1 year (OR = 0.95, 95% CI: [0.62 to 1.46], p = 0.820). Postoperative complications showed comparable results between groups, except for pacemaker implantation, which was significantly increased in stentless valves (OR = 1.82, 95% CI: [1.14 to 2.92], p = 0.013). Hemodynamic outcomes showed no significant differences between groups.ConclusionOur study revealed that the rates of early and overall mortality, postoperative complications, and hemodynamic parameters were comparable between stented and stentless valve groups. However, patients receiving stentless valves exhibited a significantly higher incidence of pacemaker implantation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04292-6.

Introduction

Aortic stenosis is the most common valvular heart disease in the world, and its prevalence continues to increase as the world’s population ages. Current estimates suggest that approximately 12.4% of adults aged 75 years and older have aortic stenosis, with 3.4% experiencing severe aortic stenosis [1].

Aortic valve replacement (AVR) remains the cornerstone of treatment for severe AS, with surgery numbers rising in line with the disease burden. Current AHA guidelines recommend mechanical valves for patients under 60 years of age and biological prostheses for those over 70 [2]. There are two categories of bioprosthetic valves: stented and stentless. Both types of valves do not require long-term anticoagulation, thereby eliminating the risks of bleeding associated with it, which is beneficial for older, multimorbid patients [3]. However, each type has its own specific technical and blood flow challenges. Stented valves are frequently favored for their ease of implantation; however, their rigid supporting structure takes up space and may hinder flow, resulting in greater transvalvular gradients, incomplete left ventricular (LV) mass regression [4], and possibly increased mortality [5].

Stentless valves were developed to overcome these constraints by providing a larger effective orifice area (EOA), improved hemodynamics, and lower rates of prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM). PPM refers to the mismatch between a valve prosthesis’s hemodynamics and the patient’s required cardiac output when (EOAI < 0.85 cm²/m²), a condition linked with low output syndrome and higher postoperative mortality [4, 6]. However, such theoretical advantages are mitigated by practical difficulties; stentless valves frequently require more sophisticated surgical approaches, longer cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping times, and have been associated with longer ICU and hospital stays, as well as higher reoperation risks [7]. Moreover, the hemodynamic dominance of stentless valves is being questioned, particularly with the emergence of newer-generation stented valves that incorporate advanced design features aimed at improving flow and longevity [4].

Several meta-analyses have previously compared stented and stentless valves, but the findings have been conflicting. While certain stentless prostheses have shown favorable hemodynamic results, such as lower gradients and higher EOAI, these advantages have not consistently translated into better clinical outcomes, as mean transvalvular gradients and LV mass regression did not differ significantly between the two valve types [8]. New large-scale studies make earlier analyses less applicable to modern clinical practice and also provide new data to the current field.

Given these limitations, we conducted an updated systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the effectiveness and safety of stented vs. stentless aortic valves by synthesizing current data. The present research seeks to clarify the relative performance of each prosthesis type and help rationalize clinical decision-making in surgical aortic valve replacement.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) statement guidelines [9]. and were prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42025636952). The methodological quality of this review was rigorously assessed using the AMSTAR 2 critical appraisal tool [10]. Detailed PRISMA and AMSTAR 2 checklists are provided in Tables S1 and S2, respectively.

Search strategy

We conducted a thorough systematic search of PubMed, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Web of Science, targeting studies that met our inclusion criteria and spanning from inception to December 2024, with an update in May 2025. Our search focused on stentless and stented aortic valves and patients with AS. To ensure completeness, we also manually reviewed the reference lists of related studies for additional relevant publications. A detailed breakdown of the search strategy is presented in Table S3.

Study selection

We used Covidence software to screen studies that were potentially eligible for inclusion. After duplicate removal, two reviewers independently conducted screening, including title and abstract screening and full-text screening. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers or by consulting a senior reviewer.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following eligibility criteria: they were randomized controlled trials or non-randomized studies; involved adult patients with pure or predominant AS undergoing surgical AVR; and the intervention group received any form of stentless aortic valve, and the control group received any form of stented aortic valve. We excluded non-human studies, case reports, abstracts, posters, editorials, and reviews. Restriction was applied to studies in English only.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers extracted pre-specified data from the included studies using an online extraction form. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion between the reviewers or by consulting a senior reviewer. The extraction form was systematically organized into three primary domains: First, a summary section captured key study characteristics, including author, year of publication, study design, country, total sample size, study duration, eligibility criteria, and study conclusion. Second, data on the study population were collected, including age, sex , smoking status, associated comorbidities, body mass index (BMI), preoperative New York Heart Association (NYHA) Classification III/IV, preoperative EuroSCORE, annulus diameter, annulus diameter index, and prosthetic valve size. Third, information relevant to bias risk was recorded prior to formal assessment. Finally, all predefined outcomes were extracted as follows: primary outcomes including early mortality, overall mortality, stroke, pacemaker implantation and secondary outcomes including cardiopulmonary bypass time (min), cross-clamp time (min), severe prosthesis-patient mismatch, and various adverse outcomes, e.g., postoperative atrial fibrillation (AF), endocarditis, total reoperation, as well as hospital stay duration. Additionally, data on left ventricular mass (LVM) (g), left ventricular mass index (LVMI) (g/m²), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (%), and mean Aortic Valve (AV) gradient (mmHg) were extracted as secondary outcomes. Definitions and specific criteria for these outcomes are detailed in Table S4. Milano et al.‘s study was analyzed as two separate groups because it involved two distinct patient groups, each with different stentless and stented valve forms [11]. Additionally, because the Tsialtas et al. study included two types of stentless valves, we included only the Toronto stentless group as the intervention group [12].

Risk of bias assessment

All included studies underwent a rigorous quality assessment by two independent reviewers. For randomized clinical trials (RCTs), the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB-2) tool [13] was employed. This tool categorizes each RCT as having low, high, or some concerns regarding bias across five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported results.

Non-randomized and observational studies (NRSs) were assessed using the Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool [14]. This tool assesses overall bias as low, moderate, serious, or critical risk across seven domains: bias due to confounding, participant selection, intervention classification, deviations from intended intervention, missing data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results.

Data synthesis

Quantitative synthesis was performed in R (version 4.4.1) using the meta package with the metabin function for binary outcomes and the metacont function for continuous outcomes. Continuous data were presented as post-intervention means and standard deviations (SDs), with effect sizes reported as mean differences (MD) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For binary outcomes, the odds ratio (OR) with 95% CIs was used. A random-effects model was employed to generate generalizable results, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. We used REML as the τ² estimator, and heterogeneity among studies was determined using the Chi-square (Chi²) test and the I-squared (I²) statistic. Significant heterogeneity was defined as a p-value < 0.1 (Hp) in the Chi² test. The I² test was used to quantify heterogeneity, with interpretation following the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis. I² values of 0–25% indicate low heterogeneity, 25–75% moderate heterogeneity, and > 75% high heterogeneity [15]. A leave-one-out analysis was used as an influence diagnostic method to assess the contribution of each study to the overall pooled estimate. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis using standardized mean differences (SMD) was conducted. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were performed based on study design, publication date (before 2006 vs. 2006 and after), and annulus size. A small annulus was set at a diameter of ≤ 23 mm. Potential publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s test and a visual analysis of funnel plots [16]. Only outcomes with ten or more studies were evaluated for publication bias. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value < 0.05.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [17]. This approach assesses five key domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Based on these criteria, the overall certainty for each outcome was classified as very low, low, moderate, or high.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

We initially identified 1,385 studies. After duplicate removal of 715 studies, 670 studies were screened using titles and abstracts, of which 79 full texts were screened. Of these, 59 articles were excluded, yielding 20 studies included in the qualitative synthesis [11, 12, 18–35]. Two studies (Jasinski 2004 and Khoo 2012) were excluded from the meta-analysis due to insufficient quantitative data, and only 18 studies were suitable for the quantitative synthesis (Fig. 1).

The included studies comprised 7 RCTs [18, 22, 24, 26, 29, 31, 35] and 13 NRSs [11, 12, 19–21, 23, 25, 27, 28, 30, 32–34], collectively involving 3,940 participants (males = 1,865 [47.3%]), with mean ages ranging from 59.9 to 79.0 years and BMI between 25.7 and 29.4 kg/m2. Studies were obtained from diverse populations of different countries. The interventions investigated were diverse, primarily focusing on stentless versus stented bioprostheses across various valve types. The reported mean prosthetic valve sizes ranged from 22 to 27 mm. Risk of bias assessment

Of the seven RCTs, four demonstrated a low overall risk of bias across most domains, indicating robust methodology and transparent reporting [18, 29, 31, 35]. However, three studies raised concerns about potential bias [22, 24, 26], often related to specific domains, such as deviations from intended interventions or measurement of outcomes (Figure S1). All NRSs exhibited a moderate risk of bias, often related to confounding and participant selection (Figure S2).

Primary outcomes

All-cause mortality involved early (30-day) mortality and overall mortality. Early mortality had similar odds in the stentless group compared to the stented group (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: [0.65 to 1.68]), (p = 0.861) (Table 1; Fig. 2). Results were consistent (I2 = 0%, Hp = 0.52) and robust (Figure S3). Subgroup analyses stratified by study design yielded consistent results but were not statistically significant. Similarly, Subgroup analyses by annulus size and publication date revealed no significant differences (Figures S4, S5, and S6, respectively). Overall mortality was measured at one-year follow-up and at follow-up exceeding one year. No statistically significant differences in mortality rate were observed at one year (OR = 0.86, 95% CI: [0.28 to 2.63], p = 0.797), and more than one year (OR = 0.95, 95% CI: [0.62 to 1.46], p = 0.820) (Table 1; Fig. 3). Results were consistent for both follow-up periods (I2 = 37.70%, Hp = 0.20 and I2 = 0%, Hp = 0.87, respectively). Sensitivity analyses yielded robust results (Figures S7 and S8, respectively). Subgroup analysis, based on the study design, for follow-up periods of more than 1 year yielded the same conclusions (Figure S9). Similarly, no statistically significant difference was detected in the subgroup analysis stratified by publication year for overall mortality at 1 year and beyond (Figure S10 and S11, respectively).

Regarding stroke, no statistical significance was observed (OR = 0.65, 95% CI: [0.31 to 1.36], p = 0.250) (Table 1; Fig. 4). No heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 0%, Hp = 0.80). Sensitivity analysis showed robust results (Figure S12). As for pacemaker implantation, the stentless group showed a statistically significantly 82% higher likelihood of requiring a pacemaker (OR = 1.82, 95% CI: [1.14 to 2.92], p = 0.013) (Table 1; Fig. 5), with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, Hp = 0.55). Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included endocarditis, postoperative AF, total reoperation rate, severe PPM, hospital stay, hemodynamic outcomes, cardiopulmonary bypass time, and cross-clamp time. (Tables 1 and 2) Furthermore, (Figures S20–S124) provide details of these outcomes. We observed no statistically significant differences in the remaining postoperative complications or in the hospital stay. Following subgroup analysis according to the study design, the findings still showed no statistically significant difference. Subgroup analysis by annulus size for severe PPM and hospital stay showed similar effects. Regarding hospital stay outcomes, a sensitivity analysis using SMD similarly showed no significant difference.

Hemodynamic outcomes, including postoperative mean AVG at 12 months, LVM, LVMI, and LVEF at 6 and 12 months, showed comparable differences between groups (Table 2). For postoperative AVG at 12 months, analysis stratified by study design showed that RCTs had no significant difference, while non-randomized studies showed a statistically significant difference strongly favoring the stentless group (MD = -5.71, 95% CI = [-9.59 to -1.84], p = 0.004). Still, the LVM at 6 months and 12 months postoperatively remained statistically insignificant. LVMI showed a comparable difference at 6 and 12 months. Subgroup analysis by study design for LVMI at 12 months showed a statistically significant difference (MD = -11.97, 95% CI [-22.70 to -1.24], p = 0.029) for non-randomized studies, and an insignificant difference for RCTs. Heterogeneity was absent, except for the postoperative mean values of AVG and LVMI at 12 months. Sensitivity analyses were robust, except for LVM at 6 months. Subgroup analyses by study design revealed similar estimates for the aforementioned outcomes, except for AVG and LVMI at 12 months. Subgroup analyses based on annulus size showed similar non-significant differences. As for sensitivity analyses using SMD, it showed similar effects to the MD analysis.

The results for cardiopulmonary bypass time and cross clamp time both showed a statistically significant difference between the two groups (MD = 22.06, 95% CI: [14.09 to 30.03], P < 0.001, and MD = 20.89, 95% CI: [12.45 to 29.34], P < 0.001, respectively). With significant heterogeneity (I2 = 83.55%, Hp < 0.01 and I2 = 90.22%, Hp < 0.01, respectively). Sensitivity analysis showed robust results, and the SMD analysis had similar effects. Also, the results remained statistically significant across all subgroup analyses, including subgroups for study design, annulus size, and publication date.

3.5 Certainty of evidence.

The certainty of evidence was generally low for most outcomes, except postoperative LVMI at 12 months, which was very low, and overall mortality at 1 year, which was moderate (Table S7). Downgrading was attributed to issues in the domains of risk of bias, inconsistency, and imprecision. For risk of bias, the inclusion of both randomized and non-randomized studies, some of which raised concerns, warranted downgrading. Inconsistency was addressed by downgrading outcomes with unexplained heterogeneity. Imprecision was downgraded when 95% CIs were wide or the number of studies was limited. The majority of outcomes were downgraded due to concerns related to risk of bias and imprecision.

Publication bias

We conducted a funnel plot to detect publication bias in the cardiopulmonary bypass time outcome, and the results showed that there was no publication bias (p = 0.628) (Figure S125).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare various clinical and hemodynamic outcomes between stentless and stented AV bioprostheses in AS patients. Our comprehensive analysis revealed no significant differences in all-cause mortality, either early (within 30 days) or overall, between the two prosthetic types. This finding remained consistent across all subgroups and sensitivity analyses, aligning with the conclusions of earlier meta-analyses conducted by Cheng et al. [36], Kunadian et al., and Harky et al. [4, 37].

Regarding postoperative complications, our analysis showed no significant differences in the incidence of stroke, endocarditis, postoperative AF, and total reoperation between groups. Similarly, the length of hospital stay did not differ significantly. These results contrast with those of Harky et al. [37], who reported significantly longer hospital stays with stented valves. However, contemporary stented bioprostheses are now designed with simplified implantation techniques, which can potentially reduce operative time and, consequently, shorten hospital stays [38]. Collectively, our findings suggest comparable safety and recovery profiles for both prosthesis types, particularly given recent advancements in surgical techniques and prosthesis design. Modern stented bioprostheses now feature improved hemodynamic profiles and easier implantation techniques, which may reduce operative times and complications, thereby contributing to shorter hospital stays.

A notable finding was the significantly higher rate of permanent pacemaker implantation in the stentless group, highlighting some disadvantages of stentless valves, as permanent pacemakers were associated with worse overall outcomes. While subgroup analyses based on study design and annular size did not yield statistical differences, sensitivity analyses consistently confirmed the robustness of this observation. This raises concerns about potential interference with the cardiac conduction system during stentless valve implantation. This finding appears to contradict Stanger et al. [39], who reported a lower risk of pacemaker implantation with stentless valves than with stented valves. However, stentless valves typically require more extensive manipulation near the aortic annulus when implanted using the subcoronary approach, which relies on subannular suturing. This extensive manipulation can increase the risk of conduction system injury, particularly given that all studies in this analysis used subannular suturing in the stentless group and that more than half used the subcoronary approach rather than the full root technique. This distinction is relevant because, compared to subcoronary implantation, full root implantation is reported to have superior short-term hemodynamics and less structural valve degeneration [40], which could potentially explain the higher pacemaker implantation rates observed in our analysis, not to mention the use of older valve generations in earlier research, which may have had an impact on the rate of pacemaker implantation.

Regarding left ventricular remodeling, our meta-analysis found no significant difference in LVM and LVMI at 6 or 12 months postoperatively. We observed lower LVM in the main analysis at 6 months; however, LVM was higher in the stentless group at 12 months. This shift suggests that any early hemodynamic advantage of stentless valves may diminish over time, potentially due to differences in valve durability, patient adaptation, or other factors influencing long-term remodeling. Stented valves, with their more rigid structure, may provide consistent hemodynamic performance over time. This aligns with the findings of Payne et al. [8] for LVM at 6 months, in contrast to our findings at 12 months. For LVMI, we found no significant differences at 6 and 12 months, in agreement with Kunadian et al. at 12 months [4]. Interestingly, we observed a significant effect in non-randomized studies at 12 months, which may suggest a benefit of stentless valves in real-world settings; however, this finding may also be driven by selection bias or unmeasured confounders. Regarding postoperative LVEF, we observed no significant differences at both 6 and 12 months. This consistency was maintained even after sensitivity analysis, with no observed heterogeneity. This result agrees with Cheng et al. [36], who demonstrated no significant difference in LVEF at 6 and 12 months between elderly patients who received stentless valves and those who received stented valves.

Concerning the postoperative mean AVG at 12 months, while previous studies by Cheng et al. [36], Harky et al. [37], and Kunadian et al. [4] noted a significant reduction, our analysis showed no significant difference in this outcome at 12 months. This finding is consistent with Payne et al. [8], who also reported no differences between groups regarding the mean transvalvular gradient. However, we noted a significantly greater reduction when analyzing only non-randomized studies, highlighting that the inclusion of non-randomized designs likely inflates the effect size, which is expected to be slight in more controlled settings. Additionally, our analysis revealed no significant reduction in severe PPM, which aligns with the findings of Cheng et al. [36] and contrasts with those of Harky et al. [37]. These mixed results suggest that while stentless valves may offer superior hemodynamics, no clear superiority in terms of survival, function, or remodeling has been consistently observed.

The findings from this meta-analysis can suggest valuable clinical guidance on selecting the appropriate prosthesis type. While both stentless and stented valves demonstrate comparable safety and survival profiles, the hemodynamic advantages often associated with stentless valves appear to be inconsistent across studies. These findings underscore the importance of individualizing the choice between stented and stentless bioprostheses, taking into account patient anatomy, surgeon experience, and specific hemodynamic requirements. Stentless valves might be particularly well-suited for patients with small annuli, where maximizing the effective orifice area is paramount. Conversely, the implantation of stentless valves is inherently more demanding, often leading to longer cardiopulmonary bypass and cross-clamp times. This increased procedural complexity may limit their use, especially in patients presenting with high operative risk [4]. Furthermore, the higher rate of pacemaker implantation observed with stentless valves warrants careful consideration and should be integrated into surgical planning.

Also, it is crucial to emphasize that, while most findings were not statistically significant, this does not necessarily imply that all outcomes had small effect sizes. This may occur occasionally due to underpowered studies with limited sample sizes. However, this should be carefully examined, considering the majority of the outcomes in our analysis had low to moderate effect sizes and were not clinically meaningful.

Strengths of this review

Several notable strengths underpin this systematic review and meta-analysis, significantly contributing to the validity and reliability of its findings. We conducted a comprehensive, up-to-date literature search across four major databases, further bolstered by meticulous manual screening of references. Moreover, we performed extensive sensitivity and subgroup analyses, incorporating both standardized mean differences and mean differences, which provided a robust evaluation of the outcomes. Crucially, we also conducted a subgroup analysis stratified by annulus size, including small annuli, which provided invaluable insight into prosthesis performance in anatomically challenging patient populations.

Limitations

Despite our robust methodology, this review has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment when interpreting its findings. Including only English-language studies could introduce language bias by excluding relevant non-English studies relevant to our research. Also, including both non-randomized studies and RCTs may introduce bias; however, we conducted a thorough subgroup analysis to mitigate this concern. Additionally, some included studies had relatively short follow-up durations, making it challenging to fully assess the long-term effects of stentless valves; thus, longer follow-up periods are required in future research. Furthermore, specific outcomes, such as long-term effects and quality of life, were inconsistently reported across studies, limiting our ability to assess them comprehensively. In addition, we selected the most frequent and appropriate time points for outcomes, as applicable. However, for certain outcomes, this was not possible due to limited data availability.

Additionally, the lack of evidence regarding aortic root enlargement, which might influence prosthesis selection and postoperative hemodynamics, was another significant limitation. In addition, most included studies raised some concerns regarding risk of bias assessment, and the majority of the included outcomes had low certainty of evidence, which limits the interpretability of our findings.

Lastly, heterogeneity in follow-up durations and surgical techniques across the included studies may have influenced the results. We did not include any studies using aortic valve neocuspidisation (“Osaki procedure”), as this operation could be considered a form of hand-made stentless valve, which also seems very useful in small aortic annuli; however, there is still limited data available, and the variability of results due to custom-made and technical difficulties linked to neocuspidisation might introduce bias or heterogeneity if included.

More robust evidence is clearly needed. Future research should prioritize addressing current limitations by conducting high-quality randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up periods. Such studies should also incorporate patient-centered outcomes, such as quality of life and functional capacity, to definitively ascertain whether the hemodynamic benefits of stentless valves translate into meaningful long-term clinical advantages. Additionally, stratifying patients by relevant baseline characteristics could help minimize heterogeneity and potential bias, thereby yielding more reliable results in future investigations.

Conclusion

Our analysis indicates that stentless aortic bioprostheses do not differ significantly from stented valves in terms of safety and postoperative complications, except for the need for pacemaker implantation. These findings underscore the critical importance of selecting patient-specific prostheses. Further high-quality randomized trials are essential to deepen our understanding of the long-term effects of each bioprosthesis type and their ultimate impact on hemodynamics and patient quality of life in patients with AS.
