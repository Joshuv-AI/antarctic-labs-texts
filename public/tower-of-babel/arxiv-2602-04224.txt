RAPO: Risk-Aware Preference Optimization for Generalizable Safe Reasoning
Zeming Wei, Qiaosheng Zhang, Xia Hu, Xingcheng Xu

Abstract
Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) have achieved tremendous success with their chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, yet also face safety issues similar to those of basic language models. In particular, while algorithms are designed to guide them to deliberately refuse harmful prompts with safe reasoning, this process often fails to generalize against diverse and complex jailbreak attacks. In this work, we attribute these failures to the generalization of the safe reasoning process, particularly their insufficiency against complex attack prompts. We provide both theoretical and empirical evidence to show the necessity of a more sufficient safe reasoning process to defend against advanced attack prompts. Building on this insight, we propose a Risk-Aware Preference Optimization (RAPO) framework that enables LRM to adaptively identify and address the safety risks with appropriate granularity in its thinking content. Extensive experiments demonstrate that RAPO successfully generalizes multiple LRMs’ safe reasoning adaptively across diverse attack prompts whilst preserving general utility, contributing a robust alignment technique for LRM safety. Our code is available at https://github.com/weizeming/RAPO .

Building on this insight, we state a critical principle for LRM safety: the adequacy of safe reasoning should adaptively align with task complexity to defend against sophisticated jailbreak prompts. To train the model to explicitly conduct adaptive safe reasoning in its thinking content, we propose a Riks-Aware Preference Optimization (RAPO) framework that leverages RL techniques to dynamically identify safety risks in prompts with appropriate length and depth. Building upon existing popular backbone RL algorithms like GRPO

RAPO initializes with an SFT warm-up stage that explicitly asks the model to conduct safe reasoning at the start of its thinking content as a format-alignment process. This format prioritizes the safe reasoning part of the model and makes it easier to extract. During this stage, we employ jailbreak prompts of varying complexity and introduce priors for the granularity of safe reasoning at different complexity levels. Following this, RAPO transitions to the RL stage, where it incorporates a risk-aware reward judge that evaluates whether the quality of safe reasoning aligns with the complexity of the task, along with a general reward judge that retains general reasoning and safety abilities. This design allows RAPO to effectively facilitate an LRM with adaptive safe reasoning capabilities, enabling it to perform appropriate safe reasoning in response to complex attacks.

Extensive experiments show that RAPO effectively trains the model to perform adaptive safe reasoning, making it generalize to diverse jailbreak attacks. In contrast to other frameworks that may refuse on basic harmful prompts but struggle against complex jailbreak attacks, RAPO successfully defends against these prompts through effective risk identification and generalizable safe reasoning. For instance, on the WildJailbreak dataset

using the DeepSeek-distillation model

, RAPO achieves an attack success rate (ASR) of just 5.6%, a substantial improvement over the 68.7% ASR of the base model. Furthermore, this enhanced safety and robustness do not come at the expense of the model’s overall utility. These results underscore RAPO’s ability to create a robust and generalizable defense by adaptively scaling its safe reasoning to the complexity of the threat.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We introduce a new perspective on studying safe reasoning through the lens of in-context alignment, showing that its effectiveness is determined by its adequacy against attack complexity.

•

Based on this insight, we propose Risk-Aware Preference Optimization (RAPO) that trains models to perform adaptive safe reasoning, generalizing defenses across jailbreak attacks of varying complexity.

•

Extensive experiments show RAPO sets a new benchmark in LRM safety, robustly defending against attacks while maintaining high performance on general reasoning tasks.

3.1
A Unified View through In-Context Alignment

Intuitively, the thinking content over the reasoning process can be viewed as a context for language modeling. Risk-Aware Preference Optimization

In this section, we present our proposed Risk-Aware Preference Optimization (RAPO) framework, starting from an overview to details of each component.

4.1
Overview

RAPO is fundamentally motivated by the goal of training LRMs to perform safe reasoning that generalizes to varying task complexities. As a principled framework for adaptive safe reinforcement learning, RAPO empowers LRMs to quantitatively evaluate the risk complexity of diverse user prompts and correspondingly allocate a safety reasoning budget that encompasses both reasoning depth and token length, ensuring alignment with the assessed risk level. The process begins with an SFT warm-up, designed to align the format for starting a safe reasoning trace in the thinking content. Following this, RAPO employs its core RL algorithm to enhance the adequacy and quality of its adaptive safe reasoning, while a general reward mechanism maintains overall reasoning capabilities. A brief overview of RAPO, including all its components, is presented in Algorithm

4.2
SFT Warm-up for Format Alignment

Recall that in Section
3
, we extracted all safe reasoning traces in the thinking content to assess its safe reasoning adequacy. However, this procedure is impractical to model as an RL reward due to its high computational cost; also, triggering safe reasoning traces at the late stage of the thinking content may have already caused harmful content to leak in the thinking content
ipo
, despite refusal in the response. Thus, we propose prioritizing the safe reasoning traces at the beginning of thinking as an individual paragraph, which is easy to split and evaluate.

Following this design, we curate the SFT dataset with a two-stage generation strategy. For each prompt in the dataset
p
, we apply a safe reasoning generator
g(\cdot)
to conduct an appropriate safety analysis to obtain the safe reasoning block
s_{p}=g(p)
, then ask the target LRM continue to write from
p\oplus s_{p}
to obtain the full completion
c_{p}
, including both the subsequent thinking after
s_{p}
and the final response. In practice, we employ the base LRM itself with a safe reasoning system prompt to achieve this, avoiding using external advanced model APIs like
ipo
;
intent
.

Additionally, to boost RL optimization, we introduce a prior on the adequacy of
s_{p}
. We use harmful prompts with graded risk complexity to obtain
s_{p}
, and all prompts are labeled with explicit risk complexity levels following our hierarchical taxonomy. This procedure helps us perform interpolation on safe reasoning granularity, taking into account the complexity of the prompt. Following the StrataSword benchmark
zhao2025strata
, we categorize harmful complexity into three levels, and ask the safe reasoning module to write a safe reasoning trace corresponding to the level (detailed rules shown in Table
2
). Meanwhile, we incorporate a benign prompt set to guide the model in executing safe reasoning across all prompts. This approach addresses concerns that the model might engage only in safe reasoning with harmful prompts as a shortcut, while neglecting to initiate this process for seemingly benign or unseen jailbreak prompts.

4.3
Reward Design: Risk-Aware and General

Shifting from the SFT to the RL stage, we design the risk-aware reward for enhancing adaptive safe reasoning ability, as well as the general reward for basic functionalities as a regularization.

Risk-aware reward
.
The risk-aware reward system provides a dedicated, risk-calibrated reward signal by leveraging an LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm with stringent evaluation criteria. The judge agent takes two structured inputs: the original user prompt and the safety reasoning trace generated by the model. It executes a two-step evaluation process devoid of subjective bias and extraneous output. First, the judge evaluates the
Risk Complexity Level
of the original prompt, ranging from low to high. Unlike relying on labeled levels during SFT, the reward agent is tasked with determining complexity levels independently, enabling greater flexibility and reduced dependence on additional annotations. Detailed rating rules are shown in Table
2
.

General reward
.
The general reward serves as a complement to the risk-aware reward by regularizing the model, thereby preserving its general reasoning capabilities essential for safety and utility. In RAPO’s design, we employ a simple binary judgment system that assigns rewards of
\pm 1
based on the response type. This general reward is divided into two specialized evaluations using LLM-as-a-Judge for distinct prompt categories: harmful and benign prompts. For harmful prompts, the judge assesses whether the model’s final response firmly refuses to engage with the harmful request and adheres to human ethical and safety guidelines. Conversely, in evaluating benign prompts, the judge determines whether the model’s response adequately addresses the core intent of the benign query. It’s noteworthy that we do not meticulously assess the correctness of the responses, since the primary aim of this reward is to prevent excessive refusals while maintaining general reasoning capabilities, rather than to improve them. Together, these two binary reward signals complement the risk-aware reward, ensuring that the model ultimately rejects harmful inputs and retains reasoning utility based on its risk-awareness.

4.4
Implementation Details and Discussion

For the reinforcement learning implementation of RAPO, we adopt
Group Policy Optimization (GRPO)

shao2024deepseekmath
as the backbone algorithm for preference optimization, as GRPO directly leverages explicit scalar reward signals for policy update, and is well-suited for multi-component reward fusion, aligning with the composite reward design of RAPO. We formalize the overall objective with a unified composite reward function that linearly combines the risk-aware reward and the two general reward signals as

A_{p}=R(s_{p})+G(r_{p}),

where
R(s_{p})\in\{-1,0,1\}
is the risk-aware reward,
G(r_{p})\in\{\pm 1\}
is the general reward for prompt
p
, where
s_{p}
is the extracted safe reasoning traces and
r_{p}
is the final response. This design balances the priority of risk-aware adaptive reasoning, safety compliance, and general utility. In practice, we leverage the original base LRM
f_{0}
with system prompts (details in Appendix
C
) to build the agents and obtain the rewards, following similar safe reasoning works
zhang2025stair
;
intent
that use self-rewarding techniques.
All reward signals are generated in parallel via vLLM
kwon2025vllm
for efficient inference, with
2\times
parallel forward passes, which is negligible compared with backward passes with GRPO.

5.1
Experiment Set-up

Models and datasets
. Following IPO

.

Conclusion

In this work, we addressed the critical challenge of ensuring the safety of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) against sophisticated and adaptive jailbreak attacks. We first identified a principle for LRM safety that scales the depth of safe reasoning with the attack’s complexity, supported by theoretical and empirical studies from in-context alignment perspectives. Building on this insight, we proposed Risk-Aware Preference Optimization (RAPO), a framework that dynamically aligns the granularity of safe reasoning with the severity of input risks. By leveraging a composite reward system that integrates risk-aware evaluation and general utility judgments, RAPO trains LRMs to adaptively allocate reasoning resources for effective risk identification. Our extensive experiments demonstrate that RAPO significantly outperforms existing SFT and RL baselines whilst preserving the model’s general reasoning capabilities, offering a principled pathway for developing trustworthy LRMs in complex safety-critical scenarios.

Appendix A
Theoretial Analysis

In this section, we provide the complete proof for Theorem 3.1 stated in the main paper. Our analysis relies on the theoretical framework of in-context learning as implicit gradient descent
[
29
]
.

A.1
Problem Formulation

We model the user prompt and the reasoning process in a linear vector space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Prompt Representation.

We define a complex jailbreak prompt
x_{0}
as a mixture of multiple latent concepts. Let
c_{0},c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\sim\mathcal{C}
be a group of orthogonal concept vectors, where
\|c_{i}\|=1
. Here
c_{0}
represents the
harmful goal
, and
\{c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\}
represent
benign wrapping concepts
or distractions (
e.g.
, role-play settings, code encapsulation) that contribute to the attack complexity. The input prompt
x_{0}
is modeled as the average of these concepts to simulate the signal dilution effect in complex tasks:

x_{0}=\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}.

(1)

Here,
k
denotes the
task complexity
or attack strength.

Model Safety Alignment.

Let
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the safety mechanism (
e.g.
, safety-critical neurons
[
50
,
35
]
) of the LRM. This mechanism model outputs a safety score
y(x)=w^{\top}x
, where a higher score indicates a higher probability of refusal. We assume the base model (before reasoning) has a weak but non-zero alignment towards the harmful concept:

w^{\top}c_{0}=\delta>0,\quad\text{and}\quad w^{\top}c_{i}=0\quad\forall i\neq 0

(2)

where
\delta\in(0,1)
represents the base model’s sensitivity to this harmful concept.

Dilution of Safety Signal.

Directly processing the complex prompt
x_{0}
without reasoning yields a diluted safety score:

y(x_{0})=w^{\top}x_{0}=w^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}\right)=\frac{\delta}{k+1}

(3)

As complexity
k
increases, the safety signal
\frac{\delta}{k+1}
decays rapidly. If the signal falls below a refusal threshold (assumed to be
\delta
, the activation for a pure harmful prompt), the attack succeeds. This explains why base models fail on complex attacks.

A.2
Optimization Views on In-context Learning

We apply the linear self-attention transformer constructed by
[
29
]
to analyze the dynamics of safe reasoning chains. We first restate the property of this transformer:

Proposition A.1

(Restated from
[
29
]
)
.

Consider a linear regression task with a loss function
L(W)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\|Wx_{i}-y_{i}\|^{2}
. A linear self-attention layer can implement a single step of gradient descent on this objective. Specifically, there exist weight configurations
W_{Q},W_{K},W_{V}
such that the output of the layer for the query token
x_{0}
corresponds to the prediction made by a weight matrix
W
updated by one GD step from initialization
W_{0}=0
.

Proof.

The standard gradient descent update rule for the linear regression weight
W
with learning rate
\eta
is:

W_{\text{new}}=W_{\text{old}}-\eta\nabla L(W_{\text{old}})=W_{\text{old}}+\eta\sum_{i=1}^{t}(y_{i}-W_{\text{old}}x_{i})x_{i}^{\top}.

(4)

Assuming a zero-shot initialization where
W_{\text{old}}=0
, the update simplifies to:

W_{\text{new}}=\eta\sum_{i=1}^{t}y_{i}x_{i}^{\top}.

(5)

The prediction for the query
x_{0}
using this updated weight is:

\hat{y}_{0}=W_{\text{new}}x_{0}=\eta\sum_{i=1}^{t}(x_{i}^{\top}x_{0})y_{i}.

(6)

Now, consider the Linear Self-Attention mechanism. We construct the projection matrices as follows:

W_{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad W_{K}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad W_{V}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

(7)

For the query token
e_{\text{query}}=(x_{0},0)
, the attention output (focusing on the update term) is:

\displaystyle\text{Update}(e_{\text{query}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{t}(e_{\text{query}}^{\top}W_{Q}^{\top}W_{K}e_{j})(W_{V}e_{j})

(8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{t}(x_{0}^{\top}x_{j})(y_{j}).

(9)

Here, the term
(x_{0}^{\top}x_{j})
represents the attention score (similarity between the current prompt concept and reasoning concepts), and
y_{j}
is the value being aggregated.

By setting the scaling factor
\beta=1/\eta
, the attention output matches Eq. equation
6
exactly:

\hat{y}_{\text{attn}}=\eta\sum_{i=1}^{t}\langle x_{0},x_{i}\rangle y_{i}.

(10)

Based on this proposition, the process of attending to safe reasoning traces
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}
is equivalent to optimizing the safety judgment parameter
W
based on the provided reasoning "examples" to predict the safety of the current prompt
x_{0}
, which we further illustrate below.

A.3
Safe Reasoning as In-Context Optimization

We interpret the generation of safe reasoning traces as performing in-context alignment to update the safety vector
w
.
Without loss of generality, let the reasoning traces be the sequence
\{z_{1},\dots,z_{t}\}
, where a safe reasoning step successfully identifies the harmful intent
c_{0}
. Thus, we assume that the reasoning traces focus on the harmful concept:
x_{s}=c_{0}
and
y_{s}=1
(i.e., refusal is activated in the safety-critical neuron). For other reasoning traces
t<t^{\prime}<T
,
z_{t^{\prime}}
is a task-specific reasoning trace and not safety related.

According to Proposition 1, the attention mechanism performs a gradient descent step. The updated weight
w_{new}
after
t
steps of reasoning is:

w_{new}=w+\eta\sum_{s=1}^{t}(y_{s}-w^{\top}x_{s})x_{s}^{\top}

(11)

where
\eta
is the effective learning rate of the attention head.
Substituting
x_{s}=c_{0}
and
y_{s}=1
:

w_{new}=w+\eta\sum_{s=1}^{t}(1-w^{\top}c_{0})c_{0}^{\top}=w+\eta t(1-\delta)c_{0}^{\top}

(12)

Here,
(1-\delta)
represents the safety gap (loss) that the reasoning process aims to bridge.

A.4
Proof of Theorem 3.1

Theorem 3.1.

Given a prompt
x_{0}
with complexity
k
, the number of safe reasoning traces
t
required to successfully refuse the prompt must satisfy
t=\Omega(k)
.

Proof.

For the model to successfully refuse the complex prompt
x_{0}
, the safety score generated by the updated weight
w_{new}
must exceed the original safety activation threshold
\delta
(i.e., the model must recognize the harm as clearly as it does for a simple prompt).
The condition for successful refusal is:

y_{\text{final}}(x_{0})=w_{new}^{\top}x_{0}\geq\delta

(13)

Substituting
w_{new}
:

\displaystyle w_{new}^{\top}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\left(w+\eta t(1-\delta)c_{0}^{\top}\right)^{\top}x_{0}

(14)

\displaystyle=w^{\top}x_{0}+\eta t(1-\delta)c_{0}^{\top}x_{0}

(15)

Recall that
x_{0}=\frac{1}{k+1}(c_{0}+\dots c_{k})
. Due to orthogonality,
w^{\top}x_{0}=\frac{\delta}{k+1}
and
c_{0}^{\top}x_{0}=\frac{1}{k+1}
.
Substituting these terms into the inequality:

\frac{\delta}{k+1}+\eta t(1-\delta)\frac{1}{k+1}\geq\delta

(16)

Multiplying both sides by
(k+1)
:

\delta+\eta t(1-\delta)\geq(k+1)\delta

(17)

Rearranging to solve for
t
:

\displaystyle\eta t(1-\delta)

\displaystyle\geq(k+1)\delta-\delta

(18)

\displaystyle\eta t(1-\delta)

\displaystyle\geq k\delta

(19)

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\delta}{\eta(1-\delta)}\cdot k

(20)

Since
\delta
and
\eta
are constants, we conclude:

t\propto k\implies t=\Omega(k)

(21)

This completes the proof. The result theoretically validates our empirical observation: as the attack complexity
k
scales, the model must allocate a proportionally larger budget of safe reasoning tokens
t
to accumulate sufficient gradients and restore the diluted safety signal.
\square
