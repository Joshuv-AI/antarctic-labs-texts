Improving the efficiency and person-centredness of occupational therapy input into care-plans in a forensic mental health and rehabilitation service
Authors: Joshua Jesudunsin Ige Ige, Amanda Alston, Velmer Boreland, Megan Caudwell, Rhiannon Ashbourne, Bronwen Ford, Fortunate Katsere, Georgia Banham
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCare-planning is vital to the delivery of timely, person-centred, safe and effective care. Despite the understanding of the benefits of person-centred care-plan in both services, occupational therapists (OTs) within our forensic and rehabilitation services had difficulties in inputting into patient care-plan an efficient and person-centred manner.The OT team developed a Quality Improvement (QI) project to improve the efficiency of the OT process to create more time for care-planning and improve person-centredness of OT input into care-plans to 82% in the forensic services and rehabilitation services by December 2023.The results indicated an increase in the person-centredness of care-plans from 63.64% to 84.34% within the rehabilitation service and an increase from 65.15% to 95.45% in the forensic service. This project used feedback from patients to develop our input into care-plans. The project also increased the efficiency of the admission process and released time to be spent on care-planning and treatment.We improved the quality of our input to care-plans in a sustainable way by using the Royal Academy of Improvement sustainability measure and taking actions which included training our band 6 and 7 OTs to complete their monthly audit and reporting this as part of the head of nursing report for assurance in our monthly Clinical and Operational Assurance Team meetings. These actions ensured that our project fitted with the organisation’s strategic aims and values. We also used low carbon alternatives by holding most of our improvement meetings online and eliminating the use of paper.Lastly, we improved the generalisability of this project by using the Goal Directed Care Plan audit tool, which is a well-researched, evidence-based tool created by service-users, carers and members of staff. This was used to train members of staff and develop an example of a person-centred input into care-plans to improve their practice.Keywords: allied health occupations, clinical audit, continuity of patient care, patient-centred care, quality improvement

Problem

Care-planning is vital to the delivery of timely, person-centred, safe and effective care within the NHS.1 This is enshrined in law by the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014: Regulation 9.2 This law is regulated by the Care Quality Commission (CQC) and standardised in the form of a clear treatment plan and agreed goals which is created, presented to patients and people within their triangle of care and contains an agreed review date.3 Professionally, the Health Care Professions Council, the regulatory body for occupational therapists (OTs) recommends that OTs assess care-plans by using standard outcome measures coproduced and reviewed with service-users, where possible.4 Likewise, the Royal College of Psychiatry’s Accreditation for Inpatient Mental Health Services (AIMS) posited that professionals should support the creation of personalised care-plans by coproducing it with patients and carers and offering them a copy.5 It further recommended a timely review of such care-plans or according to clinical needs.

The mental health rehabilitation service provides evidenced-based person-centred assessments, evidence-based care and treatment to 23 service users. The forensic services provide a gender-specific low-secure service for males. The service provides multidisciplinary treatments and care for patients who have serious mental disorders and must be treated in a secure setting. It consists of two wards: an eight-bedded assessment ward and a 12-bedded rehabilitation ward. The Occupational Therapy (OTY) team within the forensic and rehabilitation services is supervised by one band 7 clinical lead OT, while each service has a band 6 OT and two band 5 OTs. The OT provision contributed to the delivery of person-centred treatment through assessments, care-planning and treatment interventions.

Despite the understanding of the benefits of person-centred care-plan in both services, both OT teams had difficulties in supporting patients to create person-centred care-plans. Process mapping exercises in both services indicated that both services were dedicating a sizeable amount of time to conduct initial assessments during the patient admission process. This resulted in the inability to dedicate time to produce person-centred care-plans. We also discovered that despite measuring some parameters of person-centredness, the recommended audit tool used by the Trust for care-planning (Campus Medical Proforma) was not valid for auditing the person-centredness of OT input into care-plans (online supplemental figure 1). This was because it did not measure attributes of person-centred care such as strength-based approaches and Specific, Measurability, Achievable and Reasonable and Timely (SMART) attributes of interventions which are vital qualities of therapeutic interventions in person-centred care-plans.

Although we set out to improve the person-centredness of care-planning, it was clear that we needed to improve the efficiency of the OT process to create more time for person-centred care-planning and find a valid audit tool to assess the quality of OT input into care-plans. We also wanted to ensure that the views of our service-users were integral to the improvement of the person-centredness of our care-plans as part of the principles of coproduction.4

The team developed a QI project with the following aims.

Improve the efficiency of the OT process to increase the time dedicated by OTs to create person-centred care-plans in the forensic and rehabilitation services.

Improve the efficiency of the OT process to increase the time dedicated by OTs to create person-centred care-plans in the forensic and rehabilitation services.

Improve person-centredness of OT input into care-plans from 63.64% to 82% in the rehabilitation services and 65.15% to 82% in the forensic services by December 2023 using the Goal Directed Care Plan (GDCP) audit tool.

Improve person-centredness of OT input into care-plans from 63.64% to 82% in the rehabilitation services and 65.15% to 82% in the forensic services by December 2023 using the Goal Directed Care Plan (GDCP) audit tool.

The target of 82% was determined by both teams after the baseline audit which was conducted with a valid audit tool (the GDCP audit tool) in May 2023. The audit consisted of 11 criteria for person-centred care-plans. Both teams determined that a score of 9 out of 11 (approximately 82%) for each of their care-plans over the project period would be achievable considering future acuity, sickness and vacancies in both services.

Background

Despite the benefits of mental healthcare, patients often report coercive experiences not related to the legal process of being detained but events they were exposed to during detention.6 These include constraints on freedom, mandatory treatment, reservations about the efficacy of many psychiatric interventions and inadequate patient welfare.7 Patients have also reported that positive experiences of mental healthcare centred around communication, the development of strong therapeutic relationships, predictable treatment and care-processes.68,10 Such experiences led to patients feeling safe, supported, treated with dignity and positive treatment outcomes.

Coproduced person-centred care-plans are potentially powerful tools in preventing coercive experiences and increasing positive experiences through effective communication, building therapeutic relationships, providing predictable treatment and improving clinical outcomes.811,14 Indeed, care-plans give patients more influence over their mental health conditions, makes them feel more valued and respected as individuals and increases their adherence to treatment plans.

Coproduction of person-centred care-plans is vital to its effectiveness because lived-experience perspectives can be particularly useful to investigate whether treatment actions have been effective in clear and useful terms.15 In best practice, when mental health patients are far from observers and actively engaged in their own care through care-planning, it results in a reduction in coercive care, improved quality of care and social integration.16,18 Similarly, involvement of carers can provide vital expertise and lived experience which can improve patient-centred care on a service development and individual level especially in the areas of care-planning.18 From the perspective of members of staff, a thorough care-plan can facilitate the flow of information to staff from different shifts, rotas or visits who can use the information to deliver similar standards of care and support.19 This ensures that patients receive a high quality of safe, effective, and responsive care in a service which is well-led.

Therefore, the bedrock of person-centred care-planning involves consulting patients and their carers regarding their goals and interventions, while focusing on their strengths resulting in a ‘doing with, not doing for’ approach.12 13 This approach enables staff to deliver interventions that are flexible and reflect the patients’ values and goals.

Barriers of patient-centred care-planning have been identified within research. From a service-user and carer perspective, these include inadequate service user/carer motivation; medically or diagnostically led care-plans; illness and dependency-focused dialogue; and previous negative service-user or carer experience.20 From an organisational perspective, system constraints that result in a rise in caseloads that limit staff therapeutic contact time, high amount of work for staff and a lack of review have been identified as barriers.21 22

This project was initiated to improve the quality of care-planning based on regulatory and professional requirements, while considering the facilitators and barriers based on patient and staff viewpoints.

Measurement

How the information was acquired

The OTs in the Forensic and Rehab services met in May 2022 to discuss the challenges to care-planning. The fishbone diagram was used to engage them to identify the challenges and solutions to improving the quality of care-planning (online supplemental figure 2). We used the findings from the fishbone diagram to create a driver diagram which formed our theory of change for the project (online supplemental figure 3) and identified the first change idea that we would test to initiate the project.

Design

The root cause analysis (RCA) session motivated the OTY team to embark on a QI project to create an efficient process to allow for more time to support patients with their care-planning, create person-centred care-planning with SMART interventions and use a valid audit tool to measure the quality of care-planning. The QI team consisted of two band 6 OTs, and four band 5 OTs clinically led by a band 7 OT and professionally led by the Divisional Lead OT (project lead). The teams used monthly meetings lasting 1 hour to discuss the implementation of tests of change and work in collaboration to find solutions to any challenges faced during the project. The teams held most meetings online except for the process mapping and RCA sessions which were held face to face. The OTY team also agreed to engage in surveys with some open questions mid-project and the end of the project. This was proposed to gather in-depth insights into the challenges and successes of the project.

Results

The person-centeredness of care-plans increased significantly in both rehabilitation and forensic services. In the rehabilitation service, it rose from 63.64% in May 2023 to an average of 84.34% from June to December 2023 (online supplemental figures 25 and 26). In the forensic service, it increased from 65.15% in May 2023 to 95.45% from July to December 2023 service (online supplemental figures 27 and 28), exceeding the 82% target for both services.

The second sustainability assessment showed a 50% improvement, with a total score of 90, reflecting better infrastructure for sustainability, monitoring system effectiveness and clinical leadership (online supplemental figures 29 and 30). OTs also reported enhanced efficiency in the OT process through focus groups and surveys, resulting in more time to collaborate with patients on care-plans (online supplemental figures 31–33).

We increased the sustainability of this project by empowering band 6 and 7 OTs to carry out audits to monitor the quality of care-plans and reporting the findings as part of the head of nursing report to the monthly Clinical Operational Assurance Team meetings (COATs) for assurance purposes. These measures ensured that the teams have maintained the quality of their care-plan 3 months after the end of the project (online supplemental figures 34–37).

Lessons and limitations

Engagement and collaborative approaches

We used collaborative approaches, including RCA, to encourage iterative feedback and address challenges in care-planning. RCA, aimed at understanding healthcare incidents, involved stakeholders and the project team to investigate issues affecting care-plan quality. The fishbone diagram was employed to pinpoint care-planning challenges, leveraging experiential knowledge to generate innovative solutions and enhance transparency and collaboration.29,34 Despite its benefits, RCA has limitations, such as difficulties in predicting cause-and-effect relationships and potential hindsight bias.35 To mitigate these, we compared RCA-identified challenges with research evidence, ensuring grounded, non-speculative findings.16,19 Additionally, the Key Drivers Diagram translated strategic interventions into specific, measurable actions.36 Surveys with closed and open-ended questions gathered feedback from OT teams throughout the project, providing opportunities for input.

Sustainability and implementation into practice

Sustainability was a key focus, aligned with previous research and the World Federation of Occupational Therapy principles.37,39 We minimised financial, social and environmental costs by reducing initial assessments without compromising quality, thereby increasing time for person-centred care-planning. An evidence-based prioritisation protocol ensured equitable OT resource allocation, while a clinical reasoning protocol used existing assessments to maintain continuity of care and reduce redundant patient interviews. To lower our carbon footprint, initial meetings were conducted face-to-face, followed by online sessions, reducing travel emissions. Empowering band 6 and 7 OTs to audit care-plans and report findings to the COATs monthly ensured sustained quality 3 months postproject (online supplemental figures 34–37).

Limitations

This study faced limitations, including the inability to objectively analyse the impact of process mapping and efficiency of the OT process on care-plan quality. Although these changes allowed more time for care-planning, the audit tool was implemented after the initial PDSA cycles. Additionally, the project’s focus on OT-specific input into multidisciplinay team care-plans meant that a comprehensive assessment of overall care-plan quality could not be ascertained. A multidisciplinary approach would have been more beneficial, improving care-plan brevity and overall quality through team collaboration. Despite this, verbal feedback from senior leaders indicated the project’s positive contribution to care-plan quality. Lastly, while using low-carbon alternatives, some team members preferred face-to-face meetings. Future projects should balance online and in-person meetings to enhance collaboration while maintaining low carbon emissions.

Conclusion

Person-centred care-planning is essential for reducing coercive interventions, enhancing therapeutic relationships, and improving treatment adherence in mental healthcare. This QI project successfully improved the efficiency and person-centredness of care-plans using an evidence-based audit tool and patient consultations. By addressing staff vacancies, sickness impacts, and reducing inefficiencies in the OT admission process, we increased time for person-centred care-planning. The next phase will focus on enhancing the quality of OT initial assessments, recognising their importance for developing effective care-plans. We will also apply the insights gained to improve person-centred OT input in care-plans across various teams, including adults, older adults, crisis and home treatment teams.
