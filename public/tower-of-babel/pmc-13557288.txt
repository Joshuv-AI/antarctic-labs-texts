Generational Divides and Guidance Needs in the Use of Social Media: Results from an Italian Dermatology Survey
Authors: Fortunato Cassalia, Andrea Danese, Carmine D’Acunto, Antonio Russo, Corrado Zengarini, Michelangelo La Placa, Christian Ciolfi, Filippo Scolaro, Ignazio Stanganelli, Francesco Cusano, Fabrizio Fantini, Salvatore Amato, Davide Melandri, Cesare Massone, Laura Ventura, Andrea Serracchia, Anna Belloni Fortina, ADOI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe growing influence of social media in healthcare raises ethical, regulatory, and professional concerns. Dermatology, positioned at the intersection of clinical, surgical, and aesthetic medicine, faces unique challenges in navigating this digital landscape.ObjectivesTo assess how dermatology professionals in Italy use social media; identify behavioural patterns and sociodemographic correlates; and explore perceived challenges, educational needs, and the potential role of institutional guidance.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted among Italian dermatologists, residents, students, and nurses (n = 201). The 25-item questionnaire explored demographics, platform use, content shared, and perceptions. Descriptive statistics, hierarchical clustering (Ward’s method), and Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) were used to assess usage patterns and their association with age, experience, and role.ResultsAmong respondents, 49.8% reported professional use of social media. Two clusters emerged: Cluster 1 (n = 52), mainly older, experienced professionals with low activity and limited platform use; and Cluster 2 (n = 48), younger, less experienced users more active on platforms like TikTok and Instagram. Significant associations were found with age (p < 0.001), experience (p < 0.001), and professional role (p = 0.0039). While most acknowledged social media’s value for education and prevention, concerns emerged about misinformation, legal risks, and inadequate training. Only 11.4% had received formal instruction. Over 75% supported official recommendations.ConclusionsA generational divide and lack of structured training result in fragmented digital engagement among Italian dermatologists. Institutional guidance could help promote ethical communication, reinforce professional credibility, and support public health through trustworthy digital content.Keywords: Dermatology, Social media, Survey, Digital, Divide, Misinformation, Ethics, Regulation

Introduction

The use of social media in the medical field has expanded significantly, prompting discussions about its regulation, ethical considerations, and overall impact on healthcare communication. This issue has already been raised by several other medical specialties, which have explored and structured guidelines for social media engagement[1–5]; however, among all of them, we believe dermatology—encompassing both reconstructive and aesthetic medicine—faces unique challenges. Skin disorders affect both medical and psychological aspects, as skin alterations are constantly visible to patients, often leading to overwhelming concerns and a search for quick and accessible solutions[6]. While promoting therapies and lifestyle may offer a win-win situation, these aspects may benefit some practitioners who can provide simplified explanations through videos and thus reach a broad audience, increasing their popularity and engagement. However, this also raises concerns regarding misinformation, unethical promotion, and the potential for commercial bias, always within the boundaries of national regulations[7,8].

Many practitioners suffer pressing concerns of the growing, uncontrolled influence of dermatologists and aesthetic practitioners who act as social media personalities[9–11], particularly in countries like the United States, where influencer doctors often recommend specific products or procedures because they are economically incentivised[12]. Such practices in the European contexts blur the boundary between patient education and commercial promotion, raising ethical concerns about potential conflicts of interest, particularly when financial or visibility incentives may outweigh patient-centred care[1,5,12–14].

The aesthetic medicine field is particularly exposed to the risks of unregulated social media use, with many non-specialists leveraging trends to attract patients by promoting minimally invasive procedures with exaggerated benefits and limited disclosure of risks[15]. Social media amplifies ethical concerns through selective case presentation, speculative practices lacking scientific basis, and the spread of misinformation[16–18]. This creates unrealistic public expectations, especially in aesthetic dermatology, where fast results are emphasized over long-term outcomes and complications[ 17,19,20]. Unqualified individuals may also gain undue credibility online, posing safety risks[21].

Despite these challenges, social media remains a valuable resource for health education, public engagement, and professional collaboration. Its integration into dermatological practice, however, requires a structured and ethically grounded approach. As medicine increasingly adopts technologies such as AI-assisted diagnostics, tele dermatology, and digital health platforms—all governed by clear regulatory standards—the use of social media should be held to similar expectations. Recent survey data from the general population in Italy suggest that dermatology-focused preventive content on social media is well received by the public, with a substantial proportion of users reporting that such content influenced their decision to undergo skin checks—leading in some cases to early diagnoses, including skin cancers[12,22].

This underscores the potential of social platforms to support risk reduction and early detection—provided that content is evidence-based, accessible, and ethically delivered. In response to these emerging challenges, national working groups within the dermatology community have begun to promote initiatives aimed at analysing professional engagement with social media and identifying strategies to improve digital communication for public benefit and professional standards[23,24].

The study explores patterns of platform use, the nature of shared content, and its influence on public awareness and health-related behaviours. It also assesses the reliability and appropriateness of dermatology-related content, highlighting concerns around misinformation, patient privacy, and ethical boundaries, while evaluating the perceived need for professional guidelines and regulatory oversight.

Material and Methods

Study Design

This was a cross-sectional observational study conducted through an online survey aimed at dermatology professionals in Italy. The primary objective was to assess the use of social media among dermatologists and dermatology-related professionals, evaluating its impact on public awareness, information accuracy, and the effectiveness of preventive health messages. The study’s target was a minimum of 200 completed questionnaires.

Survey Development and Distribution

The questionnaire included 25 structured questions, both single- and multiple-choice, across the following domains:

Section A: Demographic and professional characteristics

Section A: Demographic and professional characteristics

Section B: Social media use patterns and attitudes

Section B: Social media use patterns and attitudes

Section C: Perceived effectiveness, challenges, and training needs

Section C: Perceived effectiveness, challenges, and training needs

The survey was distributed electronically through the mailing list of a national dermatology society and during one of the main national dermatology congresses held in late 2024. Cookie tracking and IP recognition were used to minimise duplicate entries. The expected completion time was 10–15 minutes.

Participants

Eligible participants included dermatologists, dermatology residents, medical students specialising in dermatology, dermatology nurses, and other professionals involved in dermatological care. Inclusion criteria required that respondents be actively working in Italy. There were no restrictions regarding the type of institution (e.g., public hospitals, private clinics, universities, or private practices).

Data Collection and Analysis

All responses were collected in accordance with the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR 2016/679). Descriptive statistics were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v29.0) and Microsoft Excel (Office 2024) to analyse demographic variables, platform use, and perception items. Data visualizations were produced using Python (v3.11).

A Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) was conducted to explore relationships among categorical variables, such as platform choice, content type, and posting frequency. Key behavioural dimensions were identified and visualized through factor maps and scree plots.

Subsequently, an exploratory cluster analysis was carried out on respondents who reported using social media professionally. Hierarchical clustering with Ward’s method was applied based on platform use and content type. Cluster quality was evaluated using the silhouette coefficient, identifying two distinct user profiles. Associations between cluster membership and respondent characteristics were tested using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate.

Results

Respondent Characteristics

A total of 201 participants completed the survey. The majority were dermatologists (n = 130, 64.7%), followed by dermatology residents (n = 49, 24.4%), with the remaining participants comprising medical students (n = 6), dermatology nurses (n = 5), and other professionals involved in dermatological care (n = 11) [Figure 1]. Most respondents reported more than 15 years of experience in the field (n = 90, 44.8%), while 49 (24.4%) had 1–5 years of experience and 26 (12.9%) had less than one year. The predominant work setting was public hospitals (n = 124, 61.7%), followed by university or research institutes (n = 38, 18.9%) and private practices (n = 28, 13.9%).

The majority of participants were aged 25–34 years (n = 70), followed by respondents in the 55–64 and ≥65 age brackets (n = ~40 each). Younger professionals (<25 years) were underrepresented in the sample, while middle-aged groups (35–54 years) showed moderate representation. This age distribution reflects a broad range of professional seniority levels relevant to analysing digital behavior patterns.

Respondents were evenly distributed by gender (female: n = 93; male: n = 106), and the most represented age groups were 25–34 years (n = 70), 55–64 years (n = 37), and ≥65 years (n = 37) [Figure 2].

Most participants were board-certified dermatologists (n ≈ 130), followed by dermatology residents (n ≈ 50). Medical students and nurses specialising in dermatology represented a small fraction of the sample, along with a minority of respondents categorised as “Other.” This distribution reflects a sample of experienced dermatology professionals, providing insights into clinical perspectives on digital communication.

Professional Use of Social Media

Of 201 respondents, 100 (49.8%) reported using social media platforms for professional dermatological purposes. Among these, the most commonly used platforms were WhatsApp (n = 61), Instagram (n = 52), LinkedIn (n = 29), and Facebook (n = 27). Other platforms included YouTube (n = 21), professional forums (n = 26), TikTok (n = 8), and Twitter/X (n = 6). Most users posted infrequently, with 44 respondents indicating they published content rarely. 16 individuals reported daily posting.

Shared Content and Communication Practices

Among the 100 active professional users, the most frequently shared content included scientific articles and research updates (n = 51), information about skin diseases and treatments (n = 42), and preventive health messages (n = 28). 22 participants shared educational content targeted to patients, while 26 shared personal opinions, and only 12 explicitly reported efforts to correct misinformation. Notably, 28 respondents stated they did not share content but only consumed it passively.

Regarding evidence-based communication, 20 participants stated they always include references when posting medical content, while 19 often or sometimes did so. However, 22 respondents indicated they do not post medical information at all.

Perceptions of Impact, Challenges and Training Needs

Most respondents perceived their social media activity as effective in raising public awareness: 104 rated it as very practical or practical. Furthermore, 175 respondents agreed or strongly agreed that sharing accurate information on social media could influence preventive behaviors (e.g., sun protection, early screening).

92 participants frequently or occasionally reported feedback from patients or the public. Despite this, only 24 respondents said they always addressed misinformation encountered online, while 59 reported never doing so.

The main challenges reported in sharing appropriate content included fear of public misinterpretation (n = 92), difficulty simplifying complex information (n = 69), lack of training (n = 57), and legal concerns (n = 60). Only 23 respondents had received training on communicating medical information via social media, though 46 expressed interest in such education.

155 respondents (more than 75%) supported developing official guidelines to regulate professional social media use in dermatology. Similarly, 102 supported the creation of dedicated educational programs, while 69 were open to the idea. A summary of the results is provided in [Table 1].

Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA)

A Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) was performed to explore social media usage behaviours using categorical variables such as platform preference, content type, and posting frequency. The first two dimensions explained a cumulative variance of 24.6% (Dimension 1: 15.8%, Dimension 2: 8.8%). On the factorial map [Figure 3] [Figure 4], respondents were distributed primarily along Dimension 1 based on platform choice and engagement level. Platforms such as TikTok, Twitter/X, and YouTube were located furthest from the origin, indicating a distinct communication style associated with higher posting frequency and the use of interactive or visual formats.

In contrast, respondents who rarely posted or identified as passive observers clustered closer to the origin or in the opposite quadrant. Dimension 2 captured variation related to the type of content shared, with variables such as educational messages, prevention-focused posts, and correction of misinformation contributing most to this axis. Cluster Analysis

Among the 100 respondents who reported using social media for professional purposes, a hierarchical cluster analysis was performed using Ward’s method. The dendrogram and silhouette plot are presented in [Figure 5]. Based on silhouette width values, the optimal solution identified Cluster 1 (n = 52) and Cluster 2 (n = 48). The average silhouette width was 0.17, with internal cluster cohesion values of 0.10 for Cluster 1 and 0.25 for Cluster 2.

Statistical tests conducted to evaluate associations between cluster membership and sociodemographic characteristics revealed significant differences. As shown in the report, professional role (p = 0.0039), age (p < 0.001), and years of experience in dermatology (p < 0.001) were significantly associated with cluster assignment. Cluster 1 included a higher proportion of senior dermatologists, while Cluster 2 contained more respondents with fewer years of professional experience.

Discussion

Diversity of Engagement Among Dermatologists

The results reveal a wide variability in how dermatology professionals engage with social media. Only half reported using these platforms for professional purposes, and among them, behaviours ranged from passive observation to frequent posting of educational or scientific content. Many respondents reported low publishing frequency and limited platform diversification, favouring commonly used tools such as WhatsApp and Instagram.

This heterogeneity reflects the absence of a shared or standardised model for digital engagement in dermatology. Unlike specialities such as plastic surgery, where social media is often integrated into professional identity and patient communication strategies, dermatologists in Italy tend to follow individual and informal approaches. Prior studies have shown that plastic surgeons strategically use visual platforms like Instagram and TikTok to enhance visibility and connect with patients, often through curated procedural content[9,11,25–28]. In contrast, dermatology lacks unified communication guidelines or endorsed best practices to support an effective and coherent online presence[23,29].

These discrepancies highlight a broader systemic gap. While younger dermatologists are more active and visible online, the limited engagement of experienced professionals may have contributed to a public image of dermatology disproportionately focused on aesthetics. This imbalance may also have diminished the specialty’s influence on regulatory discussions and weakened its ability to safeguard the clinical standards of aesthetic practice [30–33].

Furthermore, this generational and institutional disconnect may partially explain Italy’s delayed participation in emerging areas such as artificial intelligence in dermatology[ 34,35]. Compared to other European countries and North America, Italian dermatologists have shown slower integration into multicenter AI initiatives[36], digital data sharing networks, and algorithm validation studies[37,38]—possibly reflecting a broader cultural and infrastructural resistance to digital innovation within the senior academic and clinical leadership[39,40].

Lack of Structured Training and Institutional Orientation

While most respondents recognized the potential of social media in dermatological education and public health promotion, only a minority reported having received specific training on communicating medical information appropriately through these platforms[41,42]. Even among those who regularly post content, essential practices such as referencing scientific sources or actively correcting misinformation were applied inconsistently. Notably, 31.3% of users stated they never intervene to address false or misleading information.

This lack of formal preparation appears to contribute to professional uncertainty and hesitation. Many respondents expressed concerns about potential misunderstandings, reputational damage, or legal consequences. Additional barriers frequently cited included time constraints, the challenge of simplifying complex clinical topics for a general audience, and the absence of structured training in digital communication[ 28]. Overall, these findings point to a significant disconnect between the growing importance of social media in medical communication and the limited institutional guidance currently available to dermatologists.

Despite these limitations, the survey revealed a clear interest in structured support. More than 75% of respondents expressed that official orientation would be beneficial, and over half were in favor of targeted training initiatives. These findings are consistent with international trends. The American Medical Association (AMA) has published social media guidelines to help physicians maintain professionalism in digital interactions. and the UK’s General Medical Council (GMC) has issued detailed advice for online conduct, including ethical boundaries and protection of patient confidentiality[ 43,44]. In the northern Europe the integration of Social media digital communication accounts is often managed at the institutional level, reducing individual liability and promoting consistent messaging[45]. Compared to these settings, Italy such as France and Germany lacks formal national or international guidelines dermatology-specific guidance and structured training in digital communication[46,46].

Scientific societies have yet to issue clear recommendations, resulting in heterogeneous communication practices and increasing the risk of misinformation[47]. The absence of shared standards also contributes to legal and ethical ambiguities, especially in cross-border contexts. Developing non-binding but authoritative guidelines—supported by education and endorsed by professional bodies—could promote consistent, accurate, and ethical use of social media in dermatological practice.

Implications for Professional Representation and Strategic Communication

The identification of distinct clusters of social media users within the dermatology community points to diverging approaches to public communication and self-representation. While one group—primarily composed of younger, less experienced professionals—appears more active and comfortable with frequent, diversified use of platforms, another remains passive or absent from digital engagement. This polarisation has implications for individual communication strategies and how the public collectively perceives dermatology.

Underrepresenting senior, academically established professionals in digital spaces may reduce the visibility of authoritative, evidence-based voices. In their absence, the online narrative around dermatology is often shaped by informal younger influencers or commercially driven actors, potentially leading to a distorted view of the speciality.

Finally, the lack of coordinated communication strategies can limit the profession’s ability to respond to misinformation, advocate for policy changes, and participate meaningfully in digital health promotion. Structured institutional involvement, such as coordinated campaigns led by scientific societies or health authorities, could help unify messaging and reinforce dermatologists’ role in public discourse.

The findings reveal a clear generational divide: younger professionals, particularly residents and early-career dermatologists, are more likely to adopt social media as a tool for communication and outreach, while senior figures remain largely absent. In a national context where aesthetic medicine is under-regulated compared to countries such as France or the United Kingdom, this imbalance may have contributed to a public image of dermatology skewed toward cosmetic procedures, leaving behind its clinical and scientific depth.

Respondents reported uncertainty in managing reputational risks, addressing misinformation, and communicating effectively with the public. The lack of formal training and shared guidelines contributes to heterogeneous communication styles and potential ethical risks. Nevertheless, there was broad support for structured support mechanisms, including professional recommendations and dedicated training resources.

In response to this need, one proposal emerging from the study is the development of a voluntary “quality seal” system—a certification endorsed by scientific societies to identify trustworthy, evidence-based content produced by certified dermatologists. Inspired by models like those promoted by the AMA or GMC, such a system could serve as a quality and credibility marker, helping the profession reclaim authority in digital spaces and rebuild public trust[43,44].

Finally the fragmented and informal use of social media by dermatologists contrasts with the more structured and widely accepted adoption of tele-education tools, especially since the COVID-19 pandemic [48,49]

While tele-education has become a recognized modality for continuing medical education (CME), with institutional endorsement and measurable outcomes, social media remains largely unregulated and individually driven. This divergence may limit the educational impact and credibility of digital content shared via social platforms. Younger dermatologists are more engaged with both media, but tele-education programs tend to attract broader participation across age groups due to their academic structure. Integrating social media with formal e-learning, using platforms to disseminate evidence-based content or engage learners interactively, improving content quality, and strengthen dermatology’s role in public health education.

Developing hybrid strategies that combine the outreach potential of social media with the quality assurance of accredited training may bridge generational divides and support dermatology’s digital evolution.

Strengths and Limitations

This is one of Italy’s first structured surveys investigating professional social media use among italian dermatologists. The study benefits from a robust sample (n = 201) and includes a range of professional roles, allowing for a broad perspective. The questionnaire was internally validated and covered multiple domains, from platform usage to ethical considerations.

However, some limitations must be noted: self-selection bias is possible, as those more active or opinionated about social media may have been more likely to participate. The online format may have excluded less digitally connected professionals. Additionally, the cross-sectional design captures behaviours at a single time and does not reflect evolving trends. While MCA and cluster analyses provided exploratory insights, further research is needed to validate these profiles and assess the accuracy of shared content through direct content analysis.

Conclusions

Our study reveals that the Italian dermatology community has a fragmented approach to social media, ranging from passive observers to prolific content creators, with only half of the professionals actively engaging on these platforms. A clear generational divide has emerged: younger clinicians are more comfortable with dynamic platforms, whereas senior experts largely remain absent from digital dialogue.

There is virtually no formal training in medical communication via social media, leaving practitioners uncertain about legal risks, evidentiary standards and how to correct misinformation. Nevertheless, most respondents endorse the need for official guidelines and targeted educational programmes to ensure consistent and ethical engagement.

A voluntary accreditation or some sort of ‘quality seal’ for medical content creators, endorsed by national and European dermatology bodies, could standardise best practices and reinforce professional integrity. This would help to distinguish evidence-based information from unregulated or promotional material, thereby rebuilding public trust.

Coordinated campaigns and cross-generational mentoring initiatives could bridge existing gaps and amplify preventive messages such as advice on photoprotection and tutorials on self-examination. By aligning individual efforts within a unified strategic framework, dermatologists can utilise social media’s full potential for risk reduction, early diagnosis, and patient empowerment.

We believe a transparent and ethically grounded culture of communication, backed by institutional support, will be essential to securing dermatology’s authoritative voice in the digital era.
