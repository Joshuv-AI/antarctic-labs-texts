PILAF: Optimal Human Preference Sampling for Reward Modeling
Yunzhen Feng

Abstract




Abstract

As large language models increasingly drive real-world applications, aligning them with human values becomes paramount. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged as a key technique, translating preference data into reward models when oracle human values remain inaccessible. In practice, RLHF mostly relies on approximate reward models, which may not consistently guide the policy toward maximizing the underlying human values. We propose Policy-Interpolated Learning for Aligned Feedback (PILAF), a novel response sampling strategy for preference labeling that explicitly aligns preference learning with maximizing the underlying oracle reward. PILAF is theoretically grounded, demonstrating optimality from both an optimization and a statistical perspective. The method is straightforward to implement and demonstrates strong performance in iterative and online RLHF settings where feedback curation is critical.

Introduction

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
(
Ouyang et al., 2022
)
has revolutionized large language models (LLMs) by incorporating human preferences, enabling significant progress in applications such as conversational AI
(
Achiam et al., 2023
)
, personalized tutoring
(
Limo et al., 2023
)
, and content curation
(
Yue et al., 2024
)
. At the core of RLHF is
reward modeling
, a critical process that translates human feedback—such as pairwise comparisons or rankings—into a measurable objective for model training. By formalizing human preferences, reward models then guide LLMs towards alignment through
policy optimization
.

While numerous studies have focused on improving language models (LMs) by optimizing fixed reward functions
(
Dong et al., 2023
;
Liu et al., 2024c
)
or leveraging pre-existing preference datasets
(
Ethayarajh et al., 2024
;
Azar et al., 2024
;
Xu et al., 2024
)
, comparatively less attention has been paid to the critical challenge of collecting
effective
data for human-labeling in RLHF, to maximize its utility.
This is an important problem, as the quality of preference data directly impacts the effectiveness of reward modeling and, consequently, the overall success of fine-tuning.
This challenge is further compounded by the high cost of expert preference labeling
(
Lightman et al., 2023
)
.

Preference data is usually generated by sampling response pairs
(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}_{i})
to a prompt
x_{i}
from a policy, and presenting them to human labelers for preference annotation. It is commonly assumed that the annotation follows the Bradley-Terry (BT) model, under an
oracle reward
. Next, we use maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) on these preference data to train a reward model, which then serves as a measurable objective to optimize the policy (i.e. LLM) while staying close to a reference policy. In
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
, this pipeline is simplified by optimizing the policy with implicit reward modeling.
However, all these pipelines give rise to a

misalignment of objectives:
RLHF (or DPO) should, in principle, train its policy to maximize the (inaccessible)
oracle objective
which combines the
oracle reward
from the BT model with reference regularization. In practice, RLHF relies on preference data through the MLE objective in reward modeling or through methods like DPO, which are
not
designed to guide policy optimization towards maximizing oracle rewards. Thus, reward optimization (either directly or implicitly via DPO) and (optimal) policy optimization are not inherently aligned, potentially leading to inefficiencies (Sec.
2
).

In this work, we study this misalignment by examining the sampling scheme that generates
response pairs
(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}_{i})
for preference labeling, which is especially important when additional preference data is collected mid-RLHF training to mitigate the off-policy distributional shift, as is empirically standard

(
Touvron et al., 2023
;
Bai et al., 2022
)
. We show that
uniform sampling from the current policy, as is common, leads to misaligned gradients of the two
objectives (reward model loss and true oracle objective).

To tackle this issue, we present
Theoretically Grounded Policy-Interpolated Learning for Aligned Feedback
(T-PILAF), a novel sampling method that aligns reward modeling with value optimization. Specfically, T-PILAF generates responses by interpolating the policy model and the reference model for a balanced exploration and exploitation.
We provide rigorous theoretical analysis to show that for preference data generated with T-PILAF, the gradient of the MLE loss with respect to the policy network’s parameters is aligned with the policy gradient of the oracle objective in a first-order sense. This alignment enables the policy to optimize directly for the oracle value, achieving both alignment and efficiency. Furthermore, we separately show from a statistical perspective that T-PILAF aligns optimization with the steepest directions of the oracle objective. It thus makes the sampled preference pairs more informative, reducing variance and improving training stability.

We then present PILAF, a simple modification of our theoretical sampling scheme T-PILAF, which naturally lends itself to practical implementation. For clarity of exposition, we present our method in the context of DPO; however, PILAF can be adapted to a wide class of preference optimization methods.

1.1
Related Work

Existing Sampling Schemes.
In academic papers, uniform vanilla sampling is the most commonly used approach, while methods such as best-of-N and worst-of-N have also been explored
(
Dong et al., 2024
)
.
Xie et al. (2024)
propose sampling one response from the current policy model and another from a reference model, modifying the loss function to encourage optimistic behavior. Similarly,
Zhang et al. (2024)
sample one response from the current model but rank it alongside two offline responses from the reference model.
Shi et al. (2024)
present a formula similar to ours based on intuition, introducing several hyperparameters and analyzing convergence speed with DPO in a tabular setting.
Liu et al. (2024d)
train an ensemble of reward models to approximate a posterior distribution over possible rewards and use Thompson sampling to generate responses with exploration. In contrast to these works, we theoretically establish the principles of response generation for preference labeling, making minimal assumptions and simplifications while demonstrating the optimality of our approach. Our approach eliminates the need for hyperparameter tuning.

Policy Gradient.
Our theoretical principle is closely related to the family of policy gradient methods
(
Williams, 1992
;
Sutton et al., 1999
)
in reinforcement learning, which optimize a policy
\uppi_{\theta}
by estimating and ascending the gradient of the expected return
\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)
.
Significant advancements have been made to improve the efficiency of these methods, including variance reduction techniques
(
Greensmith et al., 2004
)
, off-policy gradient estimation
(
Degris et al., 2012
)
, interpolating on-policy and off-policy updates
(
Gu et al., 2017
)
, deterministic policy gradients
(
Silver et al., 2014
)
, and three-way robust estimation approaches
(
Kallus & Uehara, 2020
)
.
Our study extends these principles to preference learning for LMs, aligning the MLE gradient with the oracle objective gradient by controlling the response sampling distribution, thereby improving learning efficiency.

A review of other RLHF literature, particularly on data selection for the preference dataset, is deferred to
Appendix

A
.

Problem Setup and Motivation

In this section, we introduce the setup for the problem studied in this work. In
Section

2.1
, we present the basic framework for aligning language models with human preferences. In
Section

2.2
, we provide an overview of the widely-used Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) method. Finally, in

2.3

2.1
Aligning LMs with Human Preferences

Language Model (LM).

At the core of RLHF is a language model that processes prompts
x\in\mathcal{X}
and generates responses
\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\in\mathcal{Y}
.
Each response is represented as a sequence of tokens
\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{T}).
The primary goal of RLHF is to guide the model to generate responses that align with human preferences. This translates to designing a policy
\uppi
(parameterized as a LM) that maps prompts to responses, maximizing a reward that reflects human preferences (with a KL regularization).

Preference Data.

The oracle reward for human values is inherently inaccessible. Instead, the alignment process approximates the reward using a dataset of human-labeled preferences,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\big\{(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\ell}_{i})\big\}_{i=1}^{n}\,,

where each sample contains: (i) a prompt
x_{i}
, independently drawn from a distribution
\uprho
, and (ii) a pair of responses
(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\ell}_{i})
, where
\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}_{i}
is preferred over
\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\ell}_{i}
in human labeling. The response pair
(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\ell}_{i})
is first generated from a joint distribution
\upmu(\cdot\mid x)

and then presented to human labelers for preference annotation. Human preferences are commonly modeled using the
Bradley–Terry (BT)
model, which assumes:

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\bigm|x\big)\;=\;\sigma\big(r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big)\,,

(1)

where
r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)
represents the (unknown) oracle reward of a response given a prompt, and
\sigma(z)=\{1+\exp(-z)\}^{-1}
is the sigmoid function, mapping differences in rewards to probabilities. We adopt the BT model throughout this paper.

Reward Modeling.

The preference data, encoding human judgment, is then used to train a reward model,
r_{\theta}
, which serves as a measurable objective for training the policy model.
r_{\theta}
is trained by solving a MLE objective:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\ \widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta):=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\sigma\Big(r_{\theta}\big(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}_{i}\big)-r_{\theta}\big(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\ell}_{i}\big)\Big).

(2)

This empirical loss approximates the expected negative log-likelihood

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)\;:=\;\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho,\,(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\sim\upmu(\cdot\mid x)}\Big[-\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w})-r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\ell})\big)\Big]\,.

(3)

Policy Optimization.

To align a language model
\phi
with human preferences, we optimize it to maximize the learned rewards
r_{\theta}
while staying close to a reference policy
\uppi_{{\rm ref}}
. The objective is

\max\nolimits_{{\phi}}\ \mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\big]-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\uppi_{\phi}\;\|\;\uppi_{{\rm ref}}).

(4)

It consists of two parts:

(i)

The
reward
term
\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho,\,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\sim\uppi(\cdot\mid x)}[r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)]
encourages the policy to generate high-quality responses.

(ii)

The
regularization
term
D_{\text{KL}}(\uppi\;\|\;\uppi_{{\rm ref}})
penalizes deviations from the reference policy
\uppi_{{\rm ref}}
and is defined as
\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho}\big[D_{\text{KL}}\big(\uppi(\cdot\mid x)\;\big\|\;\uppi_{{\rm ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\big]
.

Here,
\beta
is a regularization parameter that balances the trade-off between reward maximization and adherence to the reference policy.
We assume
\beta
is fixed and practitioner-specified.

2.2
Direct Preference Optimization

The above-described RLHF pipeline typically leverages the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm
(
Schulman et al., 2017
)
to perform policy optimization. This approach requires loading the policy network, reward model, reference model, and a value model onto the GPU during training, making it highly resource-intensive. To improve computational efficiency and practicality, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
has been proposed, enabling direct alignment without the need for a reward model or a value model.

A key insight of DPO is that any policy
\uppi_{\theta}
can be viewed as the optimal solution to problem (
4
) if the reward
r_{\theta}
is

\displaystyle r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\;:\,=\;\beta\cdot\log\bigg(\frac{\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)}{\uppi_{{\rm ref}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)}\bigg).

(5)

Thus, DPO can directly optimize the policy
\uppi_{\theta}
using
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)
in
Equation

2
, where
r_{\theta}
is replaced by
\uppi_{\theta}
as defined in
Equation

5
. This reformulation makes the objective dependent solely on
\theta
, with the reward being implicitly learned through the policy itself. As a result, the optimization process becomes significantly more efficient.

2.3
Motivation: Realigning Oracle Reward Maximization

To fully align with human values, RLHF should, in principle, train the policy to maximize the oracle reward,
r^{\star}
, as defined in the BT model. The corresponding oracle objective is then:

J(\uppi):\,=\;\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho,\,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\sim\uppi(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\big]\;-\;\beta\,D_{\text{KL}}(\uppi\;\|\;\uppi_{{\rm ref}})\,.

Since direct access to
r^{\star}
is unavailable, RLHF instead relies on preference data, either through MLE-based reward modeling or methods like DPO.
However, these processes are not inherently designed to train the policy to directly maximize the oracle objective,
J(\uppi)
.

In this work, we aim to design an optimal sampling distribution
\upmu
to realign DPO with the maximization of
J(\uppi)
. Such a sampling strategy will improve the quality of the preference dataset, maximize the utility of limited data, and enhance both performance and efficiency.

This focus is particularly crucial in scenarios where additional data is collected during mid-training—a key phase in the iterative fine-tuning of LMs

(
Touvron et al., 2023
;
Bai et al., 2022
;
Xiong et al., 2024
;
Guo et al., 2024
)
.
At this stage, a preliminary policy
\uppi_{\theta}
(distinct from
\uppi_{{\rm ref}}
) is already in place, but its performance may fall short of expectations. It is thus necessary to gather additional preference data, ideally on-policy data that target areas where the current policy shows room for improvement. An effective sampling design can significantly enhance the efficiency of leveraging human feedback in this process.

3
T-PILAF: Theoretical Sampling Scheme

We now present T-PILAF -
theoretically grounded policy interpolation for aligned feedback
- our sampling scheme for generating responses in data collection
2
2

2

The T in T-PILAF serves to distinguish the theoretical scheme from the derived, simplified, efficiently implementable PILAF.
. The scheme is shown (in
Section

4
) to be optimal from both optimization and statistical perspectives.

Consider we have an initial policy
\uppi_{\theta}
and aim to collect preference data to further refine its performance.
We propose two complementary variants of policy
\uppi_{\theta}
: one that encourages exploration in regions more preferred by
\uppi_{\theta}
, reflecting an optimistic perspective, and another that focuses on areas less favored by
\uppi_{\theta}
, reflecting a conservative adjustment.

Specifically, we define policies
\uppi_{\theta}^{+}
and
\uppi_{\theta}^{-}
around
\uppi_{\theta}
as

\displaystyle\uppi_{\theta}^{+}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{Z_{\theta}^{+}(x)}\;\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)\exp\big\{r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\big\}\,,

(6a)

\displaystyle\uppi_{\theta}^{-}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{Z_{\theta}^{-}(x)}\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)\exp\big\{-r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\big\},

(6b)

where the reward function
r_{\theta}
is defined in equation (
5
).
The partition function
Z_{\theta}^{+}(x)
(or
Z_{\theta}^{-}(x)
) is given by

Z_{\theta}^{+}(x):\,=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)\exp\{r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\}\,{d}\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,
.

For any prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
, our sampling procedure involves the following steps:

(i)

Draw a random variable
\xi
from
{\rm Bernoulli}(p_{0}(x))
, where

p_{0}(x):\,={Z_{\theta}^{+}(x)\,Z_{\theta}^{-}(x)}/\{1+Z_{\theta}^{+}(x)\,Z_{\theta}^{-}(x)\}.

(ii)

If
\xi=1
, independently draw responses
\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\in\mathcal{Y}
according to

\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\sim\uppi_{\theta}^{+}(\cdot\mid x)
and
\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\sim\uppi_{\theta}^{-}(\cdot\mid x)
.
If
\xi=0
, draw responses as
\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
.

In the next section, we will theoretically analyze T-PILAF. To account for the changes in sampling, we adopt a slightly modified loss function in the theoretical framework:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta):\,=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(x_{i})\cdot\log\sigma\Big(r_{\theta}\big(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}_{i}\big)-r_{\theta}\big(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\ell}_{i}\big)\Big).

The newly introduced weight function
w
is defined as

\displaystyle w(x)

\displaystyle\;:\,=\;\big\{1+Z_{\theta}^{+}(x)\,Z_{\theta}^{-}(x)\big\}/\,\overline{Z}_{\theta}\,,

(7)

where the normalization constant
\overline{Z}_{\theta}>0
is given by
\overline{Z}_{\theta}:\,=1+\int_{\mathcal{X}}Z_{\theta}^{+}(x)\,Z_{\theta}^{-}(x)\,\uprho(x)\,{d}x
. We also modify the population loss
\mathcal{L}
in
Equation

3
with the weight function.

4
Theoretical Analysis

This section provides the theoretical grounding and analysis of our proposed sampling scheme from two perspectives. In the
optimization
analysis (
Section

4.1
) we show that T-PILAF
aligns two objectives (gradient alignment property)
: maximizing the likelihood function (
Equation

3
) becomes equivalent to gradient ascent on the value function
J(\uppi_{\theta})
(
Section

2.3
). Consequently, policy updates on
\pi_{\theta}
move the parameters in the direction of steepest increase of
J
. T-PILAF thus provides the potential to accelerate training and improve generalization, compared to vanilla (uniform) sampling. In the
statistical
analysis (
Section

4.2
) we focus on statistical error and show that the asymptotic covariance of the estimated parameter
\widehat{\theta}
(inversely) aligns with the Hessian of the objective function
J
when sampling with T-PILAF. As a result, T-PILAF makes the sampled comparisons more informative, as they align with directions where
J
is most sensitive. The net outcome is reduced statistical variance of our method through tighter concentration of estimates in directions that matter most for performance.

4.1
Optimization Considerations

We begin by analyzing the DPO algorithm from an optimization perspective.

Theorem

4.1
below formally illustrates how T-PILAF ensures alignment between the MLE gradient,
\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta)
, and the oracle objective gradient,
\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi_{\theta})
.

Theorem 4.1

(Gradient structure in DPO training)
.

Using data collected from our proposed response sampling scheme T-PILAF, the gradient of
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
satisfies

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta)\;=\;-\,\frac{\beta}{\overline{Z}_{\theta}}\,\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi_{\theta})\,+\,T_{2}\,,

where the constant
\overline{Z}_{\theta}
is defined in equation (
7
), and the term
T_{2}
represents a second-order error.

The detailed proof of
Theorem

4.1
is deferred to
Section

B.1
.
It involves calculation of explicit forms of the gradients
\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta)
and
\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi_{\theta})
; the most notable technical contribution is showing how to leverage our sampling scheme to approximate the derivative
\sigma^{\prime}
of the sigmoid function. By using T-PILAF sampling, we can transform a difference term of the form
\sigma(\Delta r^{\star})-\sigma(\Delta r_{\theta})
in
\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta)

\Delta r^{\star}-\Delta r_{\theta}

\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi_{\theta})

Theorem

4.1
establishes the

property, demonstrating that minimizing the likelihood-based loss function
\mathcal{L}
closely aligns with maximizing the oracle objective function
J
, with only a minor second-order error. It highlights how the proposed sampling scheme enables the DPO framework to effectively guide the policy toward optimizing the expected reward.
Beyond DPO, in

, we show how the same principle can be applied to PPO-based RLHF algorithms to help improve the sampling.

.

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\star}
redefined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\star}:\,=\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho,(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\sim\overline{\upmu}(\cdot\mid x)}\Big[\,w(x)\cdot{\rm Var}\big(\mathds{1}\{\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}=\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}\}\bigm|x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\big)\cdot\boldsymbol{g}\,\boldsymbol{g}^{\top}\Big]\,,

(11)

where the expectation is taken over the distribution

\displaystyle\overline{\upmu}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x):\,=\frac{1}{2}\,\big\{\upmu(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)+\upmu(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\mid x)\big\}\,.

(12a)

The variance term is specified as

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\big(\mathds{1}\{\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}=\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}\}\mid x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\big)=\sigma\big(r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big)\,\sigma\big(r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})-r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})\big)

(12b)

and the gradient difference
\boldsymbol{g}
is defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}:\,=\nabla_{\theta}\,r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-\nabla_{\theta}\,r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\,.

(12c)

The general form of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\star}
offers valuable insights for designing a sampling scheme. To ensure
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\star}
is well-conditioned (less singular), we must balance two key factors when selecting responses
\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}
and
\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}
:

Large variance:
The variance in definition (
12b
) should be maximized. This occurs when
r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})\approx r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})
. Intuitively, preference feedback is most informative when annotators compare responses of similar quality.

Large gradient difference:
The gradient difference
\boldsymbol{g}
from definition (
12c
) should also be large. This requires responses with significantly different gradients. Only then can the comparison provide a clear and meaningful direction for model training.

5
PILAF Algorithm

We now demonstrate that the T-PILAF sampling scheme defined in
Equation

6a
and (
6b
) can be naturally extended into an efficient empirical algorithm (PILAF).

The first challenge in implementing these definitions lies in calculating the normalizing factors
Z_{\theta}^{+}(x)
and
Z_{\theta}^{-}(x)
, which can be computationally expensive for LLMs. To address this, we simplify the process by omitting these factors and replacing them with 1.
3
3

3

When the regularization coefficient
\beta
is sufficiently small, the term
\exp\{r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\}
in equation (
6a
) stays close to
1
and has only a minor effect. Consequently, the partition function
Z_{\theta}^{+}(x)
is approximately
1
. A similar reasoning applies to
Z_{\theta}^{-}(x)
.

Consequently, the sampling process becomes straightforward: with probability
1/2
, we sample using
\uppi_{\theta}
, and otherwise, we sample using
\uppi_{\theta}^{+}
and
\uppi_{\theta}^{-}
.

The second challenge lies in sampling a response
\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,
from
\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)\exp\big\{\pm r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\big\}
in an autoregressive way for next-token generation.
We argue that the policy
\uppi_{\theta}^{+}
(and
\uppi_{\theta}^{-}
) can be approximated in a token-wise manner:

\displaystyle\uppi_{\theta}^{+}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)\;\approx\;\uppi_{\theta}^{+}(y_{1}\mid x)\,\uppi_{\theta}^{+}(y_{2}\mid x,y_{1})\cdots\,\uppi_{\theta}^{+}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{1:t-1})\,\cdots\,\uppi_{\theta}^{+}(y_{T}\mid x,y_{1:T-1}),

where

\displaystyle\uppi_{\theta}^{+}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{1:t-1})\;=\;\frac{1}{Z(x,y_{1:t-1})}\,\uppi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{1:t-1})\bigg(\frac{\uppi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{1:t-1})}{\uppi_{{\rm ref}}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{1:t-1})}\bigg)^{\beta}

with
Z(x,y_{1:t-1})
being a partition function.
The substitution of
r_{\theta}
uses the correspondence between the reward model

r_{\theta}
and the policy
\uppi_{\theta}
in
Equation

5
, under the assumption that this correspondence holds for all truncations
y_{1:t-1}
. It gives us a direct per-token prediction rule:

\displaystyle\uppi_{\theta}^{+}(\cdot\mid x,y_{1:t-1})\;=\;{\sf softmax}\Big(\big\{(1+\beta)\,\boldsymbol{h}_{\theta}-\beta\,\boldsymbol{h}_{{\rm ref}}\big\}(x,y_{1:t-1})\Big).

Here
\boldsymbol{h}_{\theta}
and
\boldsymbol{h}_{{\rm ref}}
are the logits of the policies
\uppi_{\theta}
and
\uppi_{{\rm ref}}
, respectively.
\beta
is the regularization coefficient from the objective function
J(\uppi)
in
Section

2.3
. Responses are then generated using standard decoding techniques, such as greedy decoding or nucleus sampling. Similarly, the generation for
\uppi_{\theta}^{-}
follows

\displaystyle\uppi_{\theta}^{-}(\cdot\mid x,y_{1:t-1})\;=\;{\sf softmax}\Big(\big\{(1-\beta)\,\boldsymbol{h}_{\theta}+\beta\,\boldsymbol{h}_{{\rm ref}}\big\}(x,y_{1:t-1})\Big)\,.

For a detailed, step-by-step proof, see Proposition 1 in
Liu et al. (2024b)
.

We formalize our final algorithm in
Algorithm

. Vanilla DPO
(
Rafailov et al., 2023

Guo et al., 2024
)
employs a basic generation approach, sampling
\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}_{i}\sim\uppi_{\theta}
at Step 3. In contrast, instead of only sampling from
\uppi_{\theta}
, our sampling scheme interpolates and extrapolates the logits
\boldsymbol{h}_{\theta}
and
\boldsymbol{h}_{{\rm ref}}
with coefficient
\beta

\beta
here is the same parameter that controls the KL regularization in
Equation

4

Robustness Analysis

Having established the effectiveness of PILAF, we further evaluate its robustness by testing whether it improves optimization and statistical convergence under challenging conditions, as predicted from our statistical theory in
Section

4.2
. Specifically, we replace the initial model with one that has overfit on a fixed off-policy dataset. This setup allows us to examine how different methods handle optimization starting from a poor initial point.

In
Figure

4
, we compare the performance of PILAF and
Vanilla Sampling
when both are initialized from an overfitted policy. We observe that
Vanilla Sampling
rapidly increases its KL divergence from the reference model while its reward improvement diminishes over time. In contrast, PILAF undergoes an early training phase with fluctuating KL values but ultimately attains a policy with higher reward and substantially lower KL divergence. We hypothesize that PILAF’s interpolation-based exploration design enables it to escape the suboptimal region of the loss landscape in which
Vanilla
remains. These results underscore PILAF’s effectiveness in more robustly optimizing overfitted (or even adversarially initialized) policies.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced Policy-Interpolated Learning for Aligned Feedback (PILAF), a novel sampling method designed to enhance response sampling for preference labeling. Theoretical analysis highlights PILAF’s superiority from both optimization and statistical perspectives, demonstrating its ability to stabilize training, accelerate convergence, and reduce variance. The method is straightforward to implement and requires no additional hyperparameter tuning. We empirically validated its performance in both iterative DPO and online DPO settings, where it consistently outperformed existing approaches. To achieve the same level of performance, PILAF consistently requires lower annotation costs, which can be substantial when annotations require experts in knowledge-intensive domains.

In future work, we hope to extend PILAF to other paradigms, such as KTO
(
Ethayarajh et al., 2024
)
and IPO
(
Azar et al., 2024
)
. Due to resource constraints, our evaluations were conducted using 8B models and a reward model to simulate human feedback. Future studies involving larger-scale experiments and real human labeling would further generalize our findings.

Overall, this work takes an important step toward improving preference data curation in RLHF pipelines, laying the groundwork for more effective methods in alignment.

Appendix A
Additional Literature Review

RLHF
. RLHF has emerged as a cornerstone methodology for aligning large language models with human values and preferences
(
Achiam et al., 2023
)
. Early systems
(
Ouyang et al., 2022
)
turn human preference data into reward modeling to optimize model behavior accordingly. DPO has been proposed as a more efficient approach that directly trains LLMs on preference data.
As LLMs evolve during training, continuing training on pre-generated preference data becomes suboptimal due to the distribution shift. Empirically, RLHF is applied iteratively—generating on-policy data at successive stages to enhance alignment and performance
(
Touvron et al., 2023
;
Bai et al., 2022
)
. Similarly, researchers have introduced iterative DPO
(
Xiong et al., 2024
;
Xu et al., 2023
)
and online DPO
(
Guo et al., 2024
)
to fully leverage online preference labeling. Ultimately, the quality of preference data play a critical role in determining the effectiveness of the alignment.

Sampling in Frontier LLMs
. Technical reports of Frontier LLMs briefly mention sampling techniques. For instance, Claude
(
Bai et al., 2022
)
utilizes models from different training steps to generate responses, while Llama-2
(
Touvron et al., 2023
)
further use different temperatures for sampling. However, no further details are provided, leaving the development of a principled method an open challenge.

Data Selection.
There is a line of research aimed at improving sample efficiency for preference labeling by selecting question and response pairs.
Scheid et al. (2024)
conceptualize this as a regret minimization problem, leveraging methods from linear dueling bandits.
Das et al. (2024)
;
Mehta et al. (2023)
;
Muldrew et al. (2024)
;
Ji et al. (2024)
draw insights from active learning, using various uncertainty estimators to guide selection by prioritizing sample pairs with maximum uncertainty. These approaches focus directly on a dataset of questions and responses and are orthogonal to our work.

Other Changes in Response Sampling.
Several works also modify the sampling design directly
(
Liu et al., 2024c
;
Dong et al., 2023
)
, but with the goal of improving policy network optimization based on a reward model, rather than enhancing the reward modeling itself.
Liu et al. (2024c)
employ rejection sampling to approximate the response distribution induced by the reward model, thereby improving optimization. However, this approach requires access to the reward model and incurs higher computational and labeling costs. Similarly,
Dong et al. (2023)
use best-of-N sampling with the reward model to generate high-quality data for supervised fine-tuning (SFT). We consider these approaches orthogonal to our work.

Additionally,
Cen et al. (2024)
introduce a bonus term in the policy learning phase of online RLHF to promote exploration in response sampling, which aligns with the optimism principle.

With the tools provided by

\displaystyle w(x)\cdot\frac{\overline{\upmu}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}{\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\mid x)\,\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}

(17)

Substituting the density ratio from equation (

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta)\;=\;-\frac{1}{2\,\overline{Z}_{\theta}}\,\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho;\;\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\Bigg[\,

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma\big(r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big)-\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big)}{\sigma^{\prime}\big(r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big)}

\displaystyle\;=\;\Big\{\big(r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big)-\big(r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big)\Big\}\;+\;\mbox{second-order term}.

Reformulating equation (
18

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\phi)

\displaystyle=-\,\frac{1}{2\overline{Z}_{\theta}}\,\mathds{E}_{\,\begin{subarray}{l}\\

\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\end{subarray}}\Bigg[\,\Big\{\big(r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big)-\big(r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big)\Big\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\cdot\big\{\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big\}\Bigg]+T_{2}\,,

(19)

where

represents the second-order residual term related to the estimation error

.
By applying

, we observe that the primary term in equation (

, resulting in

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\phi)

\displaystyle=-\,\frac{\beta}{\overline{Z}_{\theta}}\,\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi_{\theta})+T_{2}\,.

Next, we proceed to control the second-order term

\displaystyle\|r^{\star}\|_{\infty},\|r_{\theta}\|_{\infty}\leq R\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\sup\nolimits_{(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\|\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\|_{2}\leq G,

(21)

Finally, combining equation (
21
) with equation (

\displaystyle\frac{\overline{\upmu}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}{\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\mid x)\,\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{2\,\{1+Z_{\theta}^{+}(x)\,Z_{\theta}^{-}(x)\}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big)\,\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})-r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})\big)}

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{2\,\{1+Z_{\theta}^{+}(x)\,Z_{\theta}^{-}(x)\}}\cdot\frac{1}{{\rm Var}_{r_{\theta}}\big(\mathds{1}\{\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}=\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}\}\bigm|x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\big)}\,.

We then apply condition (
27

\frac{\overline{\upmu}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}{\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\mid x)\,\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}\;\geq\;\frac{C{}^{-1}}{2\,\{1+Z_{\theta}^{+}(x)\,Z_{\theta}^{-}(x)\}}\cdot\frac{1}{{\rm Var}_{r^{\star}}\big(\mathds{1}\{\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}=\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}\}\bigm|x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\big)}\,.

(28)

Next, substituting this result (

from equation (

as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\star}

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho;\;\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigg[\,\frac{\overline{\upmu}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}{\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\mid x)\,\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}\cdot w(x)\cdot{\rm Var}\big(\mathds{1}\{\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}=\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}\}\bigm|x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\big)\cdot\boldsymbol{g}\,\boldsymbol{g}^{\top}\bigg]

\displaystyle\;\succeq\;\frac{1}{2\,C{}\,\overline{Z}_{\theta}}\,\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho;\;\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\,\boldsymbol{g}\,\boldsymbol{g}^{\top}\big]\,.

The conditional expectation of

simplifies as

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\,\boldsymbol{g}\boldsymbol{g}^{\top}\bigm|x\big]

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\Big[\big\{\nabla_{\theta}\,r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-\nabla_{\theta}\,r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big\}\big\{\nabla_{\theta}\,r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-\nabla_{\theta}\,r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big\}^{\top}\Bigm|x\Big]

Substituting this result into equation (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\star}\;\succeq\;\frac{1}{C{}\,\overline{Z}_{\phi}}\,\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho}\Big[{\rm Cov}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\sim\uppi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\nabla_{\theta}\,r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\bigm|x\big]\Big]\,,

which matches the simplified form in equation (

B.2.3
Proof of Theorem
4.3

Gradient
\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi^{\star})
and Hessian
\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,J(\uppi^{\star})
:

The equality
\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi^{\star})=0
follows directly from the gradient expression (
38
) for
\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi_{\theta})
, evaluated at
\theta=\theta^{\star}
with
r_{\theta}=r^{\star}
.

The proof of the Hessian result,
\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,J(\uppi^{\star})=-(1/\beta)\cdot\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\star}
, involves straightforward but technical differentiation of equation (
38
). For brevity, we defer this proof to
Section

C.3.1
.

Asymptotic Distribution of Value Gap
J(\uppi^{\star})-J(\widehat{\uppi})
:

To understand the behavior of the value gap
J(\uppi^{\star})-J(\widehat{\uppi})
, we start by applying a Taylor expansion of
J(\uppi_{\theta})
around
\theta^{\star}
. This gives

\displaystyle J(\uppi^{\star})-J(\widehat{\uppi})\;=\;\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi^{\star})^{\top}(\theta^{\star}-\widehat{\theta})-\frac{1}{2}(\theta^{\star}-\widehat{\theta})^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,J(\uppi^{\star})(\theta^{\star}-\widehat{\theta})+o\big(\|\theta^{\star}-\widehat{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\,.

By substituting
\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi^{\star})=\boldsymbol{0}
(a direct result of the optimality of
\uppi^{\star}
), the linear term vanishes. Introducing the shorthand
\boldsymbol{H}:\,=-\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,J(\uppi^{\star})=(1/\beta)\cdot\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\star}
, the expression simplifies to

\displaystyle J(\uppi^{\star})-J(\widehat{\uppi})\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\,(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star})^{\top}\boldsymbol{H}\,(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star})+o\big(\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\,.

(30)

When the sample size
n
is sufficiently large,
\widehat{\theta}
approaches
\theta^{\star}
, making the higher-order term negligible. Therefore, the value gap is dominated by the quadratic form.

From
Theorem

4.2
, we know the parameter estimate
\widehat{\theta}
satisfies

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\;\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Omega}).

Substituting this result into the quadratic approximation of the value gap, we find that the scaled value gap has the asymptotic distribution

\displaystyle n\cdot\{J(\uppi^{\star})-J(\widehat{\uppi})\}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\;\frac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{z}\;=\,:\boldsymbol{X}\qquad\mbox{where $\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})$}.

(31)

This approximation provides a clear intuition: the value gap is asymptotically driven by a weighted chi-squared-like term involving the covariance structure
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
and the Hessian-like matrix
\boldsymbol{H}
.

To rigorously establish this result, we will apply Slutsky’s theorem. The full proof is presented in
Section

C.3.2
.

Bounding the Chi-Square Distribution:

To bound the random variable
\boldsymbol{X}
, we first leverage the estimate of the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
provided by
Theorem

4.2
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}\;\preceq\;C{}\,\overline{Z}_{\theta}\,\|w\|_{\infty}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\star}^{-1},

where the constant
C
comes from condition (
27
).
It follows that the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}
appearing in equation (
31
) can be bounded as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}\;\preceq\;C\,\|w\|_{\infty}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\star}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\star}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\;=\;C\cdot\frac{\overline{Z}_{\theta}\,\|w\|_{\infty}}{\beta}\cdot\boldsymbol{I}\;=\;C\cdot\frac{1+\|Z_{\theta}^{+}Z_{\theta}^{-}\|_{\infty}}{\beta}\cdot\boldsymbol{I}\,.

Here the last equality uses the definition of the weight function
w
from equation (
7
). Substituting this bound into the quadratic form, we derive

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{z}\;\leq\;C\cdot\frac{1+\|Z_{\theta}^{+}Z_{\theta}^{-}\|_{\infty}}{2\beta}\cdot\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}\,,

where
\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})
.
Since
\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}
follows a chi-square distribution with
d
degrees of freedom,
\boldsymbol{X}
is stochastically dominated by a rescaled chi-square random variable

\displaystyle C\cdot\frac{1+\|Z_{\theta}^{+}Z_{\theta}^{-}\|_{\infty}}{2\beta}\cdot\chi^{2}_{d}.

Equivalently, we can express this dominance as

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\;\mathds{P}\bigg\{n\,\{J(\uppi^{\star})-J(\widehat{\uppi})\}>C\cdot\frac{1+\|Z_{\theta}^{+}Z_{\theta}^{-}\|_{\infty}}{2\beta}\cdot t\bigg\}\;\leq\;\mathds{P}\big\{\chi^{2}_{d}>t\big\}\qquad\mbox{for any $t>0$}.

(32)

This inequality, given in equation (
32
), corresponds to the first bound in equation (
10
).

The second inequality in equation (
10
) provides a precise tail bound for
\chi^{2}_{d}
. As its proof involves more technical details, we defer it to
Section

C.3.3
.

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{\beta}\,\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho}\bigg[\int_{\mathcal{Y}}r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\,\nabla_{\theta}\,\uppi_{\theta}({d}\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)\bigg]

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\,\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho,\,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigg[r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\Big\{\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)-\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\prime}\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\prime})\big]\Big\}\bigg]\,,

which verifies equality (

C.1.3
Proof of Lemma
B.3

In this section, we prove a full version of

B.3
as stated in
Lemma

C.3
below. Equation (
14a
) from
Lemma

B.3
follows directly as a straightforward corollary.

In
Lemma

C.3
, we consider a general class of distributions parameterized by
\theta
that models the binary preference
\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)
. The negative log-likelihood function is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho;\;(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\sim\upmu(\cdot\mid x)}\Big[w(x)\cdot\log\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\ell}\bigm|x)\Big]\,.

The Bradley-Terry (BT) model described in equation (
1
) and the corresponding loss function

) represent a special case of this general framework.

Lemma C.3

(Gradient of the loss function
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
, full version)
.

For a general distribution class
\{\mathds{P}_{\theta}\}
, the gradient of
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
with respect to
\theta
is given by

\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta)\;=\;-\,\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho;\;(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\sim\overline{\upmu}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigg[\,w(x)\cdot\Big\{\mathds{P}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\bigm|x\big)-\mathds{P}_{\theta}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\bigm|x\big)\Big\}\\
\cdot\frac{\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}{\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)\,\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\mid x)}\,\bigg]\,,

(45a)

where
\overline{\upmu}
is the average distribution defined in equation (
14b
).
Specifically, for the Bradley-Terry (BT) model where

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{\theta}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\bigm|x\big)\;=\;\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big)\;=\;\bigg\{1+\bigg(\frac{(\uppi_{\theta}/\uppi_{{\rm ref}})(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}{(\uppi_{\theta}/\uppi_{{\rm ref}})(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\mid x)}\bigg)^{\beta}\bigg\}^{-1}\,,

the gradient of
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
becomes

\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta)\;=\;-\,\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho;\;(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\sim\overline{\upmu}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigg[\,w(x)\cdot\Big\{\sigma\big(r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-r^{\star}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big)-\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big)\Big\}\\
\cdot\big\{\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\big\}\bigg]\,.

(45b)

For notational simplicity, we focus on the proof for the case where the weight function
w(x)=1
. The results for a general weight function
w(x)>0
can be derived in a similar manner.

Recall that the negative log-likelihood function
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathds{E}\Big[-\log\mathds{P}_{\theta}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\ell}\bigm|x\big)\Big]\,.

Based on the data generation mechanism, we can expand the expectation in
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho;\;(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\sim\upmu(\cdot\mid x)}\Big[\,\mathds{P}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\bigm|x\big)\cdot\big\{-\log\mathds{P}_{\theta}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\bigm|x\big)\big\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mathds{P}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\bigm|x\big)\cdot\big\{-\log\mathds{P}_{\theta}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\bigm|x\big)\big\}\Big]\,.

(46)

Notice that we can exchange the roles of
\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}
and
\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}
in the expectation above. This means that we can equivalently express the expectation using the pair
(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})\sim\upmu(\cdot\mid x)
.
This symmetry allows us to replace
\upmu
in equation (
46
) with the average distribution
\overline{\upmu}
as defined in equation (
14b
).

Next, we take the gradient of the loss function
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
with respect to the parameter
\theta
and obtain

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta)

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho,\,(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\sim\overline{\upmu}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigg[\,\frac{\mathds{P}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}{\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}\cdot\big\{-\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)\big\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{\mathds{P}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\mid x)}{\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\mid x)}\cdot\big\{-\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\mid x)\big\}\,\bigg]\,.

Note that
\mathds{P}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\bigm|x\big)=1-\mathds{P}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\bigm|x\big)
and
\mathds{P}_{\theta}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\bigm|x\big)=1-\mathds{P}_{\theta}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\bigm|x\big)
.
Using this, we can rewrite the gradient as

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta)

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho;\;(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\sim\overline{\upmu}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigg[\bigg\{\frac{1-\mathds{P}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}{1-\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}-\frac{\mathds{P}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}{\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}\bigg\}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathds{P}_{\theta}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\bigm|x\big)\bigg]\,.

We simplify the expression further to obtain

\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta)\;=\;\mathds{E}_{x\sim\uprho;\;(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a},\,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\sim\overline{\upmu}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigg[\Big\{\mathds{P}_{\theta}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\bigm|x\big)-\mathds{P}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\bigm|x\big)\Big\}\\
\cdot\frac{\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}{\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)\,\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\mid x)}\bigg]\,.

This establishes equation (
45a
) from
Lemma

B.3
.

As for the Bradley-Terry (BT) model, we use the equality

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(z)\;=\;\frac{1}{(1+\exp(-z))(1+\exp(z))}\;=\;\sigma(z)\,\sigma(-z)\qquad\mbox{for any $z\in\mathds{R}$}

to derive the following expression

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)}{\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\mid x)\,\mathds{P}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}\succ\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}\mid x)}\;=\;\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a})-\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b})\,.

(47)

By substituting this gradient expression from equation (
47
) into equation (
45a
), we directly obtain equation (
45b
), thereby completing the proof of
Lemma

B.3
.

C.2.1
Proof of Condition (
24
)

We begin by rewriting the left-hand side of equation (
24
) as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\;:\,=\;\sqrt{n}\,\big\{\nabla_{\theta}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\widehat{\theta})-\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\theta})\big\}-\sqrt{n}\,\big\{\nabla_{\theta}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\star})-\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\star})\big\}

\displaystyle\;=\;\sqrt{n}\,\big\{\nabla_{\theta}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\widehat{\theta})-\nabla_{\theta}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\star})\big\}-\sqrt{n}\,\big\{\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\theta})-\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\star})\big\}\,.

(48)

We then leverage the smoothness properties of the function
r_{\theta}
, which guarantee the following approximations:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\widehat{\theta})-\nabla_{\theta}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\star})

\displaystyle\;=\;\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\star})\,(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star})+o_{p}\big(\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}\big)\,,

(49a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\theta})-\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\star})

\displaystyle\;=\;\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\star})\,(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star})+o_{p}\big(\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}\big)\,.

(49b)

Assuming these equalities (
49a
) and (
49b
) hold, we substitute them into equation (
48
), leading to

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\;=\;\sqrt{n}\,\big\{\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\star})\,(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star})+o_{p}(\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star}\|_{2})\big\}-\sqrt{n}\,\big\{\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\star})\,(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star})+o_{p}(\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star}\|_{2})\big\}

\displaystyle\;=\;\sqrt{n}\,\big\{\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\star})-\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\star})\big\}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star})+o_{p}\big(1+\sqrt{n}\,\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}\big)\,.

(50)

Using the law of large numbers, we know that
\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\star})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\star})
, which implies

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,\big\{\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\star})-\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\star})\big\}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star})\;=\;o_{p}\big(\sqrt{n}\,\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}\big)\,.

Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\Delta\;=\;o_{p}\big(1+\sqrt{n}\,\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}\big)

as claimed in equation (
24
).

The only remaining task is to establish the validity of equalities (
49a
) and (
49b
).

Proof of Equalities (
49a
) and (
49b
):

We express the loss function
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)
in the form

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)\;:\,=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(x_{i})\cdot\ell_{\theta}\big(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\ell}_{i}\big)\,,

where the function
\ell_{\theta}
is defined as

\displaystyle\ell_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})\;=\;-\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1})-r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})\big)\,.

We then calculate the gradient
\nabla_{\theta}\,\ell_{\theta}
and
\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\ell_{\theta}
as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,\ell_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})

\displaystyle\;=\;\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})-r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1})\big)\cdot\big\{\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})-\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1})\big\}\qquad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\ell_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})

\displaystyle\;=\;\sigma^{\prime}\big(r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})-r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\cdot\big\{\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})-\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1})\big\}\big\{\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})-\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1})\big\}^{\top}

\displaystyle\quad+\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})-r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1})\big)\cdot\big\{\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})-\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1})\big\}\,.

When the reward function
r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)
, along with its gradient
\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)
and Hessian
\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)
, is uniformly bounded and Lipschitz continuous with respect to
\theta
for all
(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, it guarantees that the Hessian of the loss function,
\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\ell_{\theta}
, is also Lipschitz continuous. This holds with some constant
L>0
across all
(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, as demonstrated below:

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\ell_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})-\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\ell_{\theta^{\star}}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})\big\|_{2}\;\leq\;L\cdot\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}\,.

From this Lipschitz property, we deduce

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla_{\theta}\,\ell_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})-\nabla_{\theta}\,\ell_{\theta^{\star}}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})-\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\ell_{\theta^{\star}}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{1},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,_{2})\,(\theta-\theta^{\star})\big\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{L}{2}\cdot\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}

and further derive

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla_{\theta}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\star})-\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\star})\,(\theta-\theta^{\star})\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\frac{L\,\|w\|_{\infty}}{2}\cdot\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\star})-\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\star})\,(\theta-\theta^{\star})\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\frac{L\,\|w\|_{\infty}}{2}\cdot\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\,.

Finally, under the condition that
\widehat{\theta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\theta^{\star}
, these results simplify to the expressions given in equations (
49a
) and (
49b
), as previously claimed.

) in

C.2.3
Proof of Lemma
B.6
, Asymptotic Distribution of Graident
\nabla_{\theta}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\star})

In this section, we analyze the asymptotic distribution of the gradient
\nabla_{\theta}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)
at
\theta=\theta^{\star}
, where the loss function
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)
is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)\;=\;-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,w(x)\cdot\log\sigma\Big(r_{\theta}\big(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}_{i}\big)-r_{\theta}\big(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\ell}_{i}\big)\Big)\,.

Using the definition of the sigmoid function
\sigma
, we calculate that

\displaystyle(\log\sigma(z))^{\prime}=\sigma^{\prime}(z)/\sigma(z)=\sigma(z)\,\sigma(-z)/\sigma(z)=\sigma(-z)\qquad\mbox{for any real number $z\in\mathds{R}$}.

This allows us to reformulate
\nabla_{\theta}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)
as the average of
n
i.i.d. vectors
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\,.

(52)

Here each vector
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
is defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\;:\,=\;w(x)\cdot\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\ell}_{i})-r_{\theta}(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}_{i})\big)\cdot\big\{\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\ell}_{i})-\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}_{i})\big\}\,.

At
\theta=\theta^{\star}
, we denote
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
as
\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}
as
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{\star}
. Notably, vector
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{i}

\displaystyle\;=\;w(x)\cdot\big\{\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}_{i})-r_{\theta}(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}_{i})\big)-\mathds{1}\{\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}_{i}=\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}_{i}=\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\ell}_{i}\}\big\}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}_{i}\,,

(53)

where
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}
is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}_{i}:\,=\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}_{i})-\nabla_{\theta}\,r_{\theta}(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}_{i})\,.

From the structure of the BT model, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[\mathds{1}\{\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}_{i}=\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{w}_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}_{i}=\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{\ell}_{i}\}\bigm|x_{i}\big]\;=\;\sigma\big(r^{\star}(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{a}_{i})-r^{\star}(x_{i},\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,^{b}_{i})\big)\,,

which implies
\mathds{E}[\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}_{i}]=\boldsymbol{0}
.

To analyze the asymptotic distribution of
\nabla_{\theta}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\star})
, we apply the central limit theorem (CLT) to its empirical form given in equation (
52
).
By the CLT, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,\big(\nabla_{\theta}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\star})-\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\star})\big)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\;\mathcal{N}\big(\boldsymbol{0},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\big)\,,\qquad n\rightarrow\infty\,,

(54)

where the covariance matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathds{R}^{d\times d}
is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\;:\,=\;{\rm Cov}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}_{1})\;=\;\mathds{E}\big[\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}_{1}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}_{1})^{\top}\big]\,.

Here we have used the property
\mathds{E}[\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}_{i}]=\boldsymbol{0}
in the second equality.

We now compute the explicit form of the covariance matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
. Using the definition of
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
from expression (

Appendix E
Experimental Details

We implement our code based on the open-sourced OpenRLHF framework
Hu et al. (2024)
. We will open-source our code in the camera-ready version.

We use both the helpful and the harmless (HH) sets from HH-RLHF
(
Bai et al., 2022
)
without additional data selection. We adopt the chat template from the Skywork-Reward-8B model
(
Liu et al., 2024a
)
to align with the reward template. This reward model, fine-tuned from Llama-3.1-8B, is used to simulate human preference labeling and matches our network trained for alignment.

For SFT, we apply full-parameter tuning with Adam for one epoch, using a cosine learning rate schedule, a 3% warmup phase, a learning rate of
5\times 10^{-7}
, and a batch size of 256. These hyperparameters are adopted from
Hu et al. (2024)
.

For all the DPO training in both iterative and online settings, we use full-parameter tuning with Adam but with two epochs. The learning rate, warmup schedules, and batch size are all the same.

During generation, we limit the maximum number of new tokens to 896 and employ top
\_
p decoding with
p=0.95
for all experiments. For Online DPO, we use a sampling temperature of 1.0, following
Guo et al. (2024)
, while in Iterative DPO, we set the temperature to 0.7 to account for the off-policy nature of the data, following
Dong et al. (2024)
;
Shi et al. (2024)
.

Prompts are truncated to a maximum length of 512 tokens (truncated from the left if the length exceeds this limit) for SFT, DPO, and generation tasks. For SFT data, the maximum length is further restricted to 1024 tokens. When the combined length of the response and the (truncated) prompt exceeds 1024 tokens, the response is truncated from the right. These truncation practices align with the standard methodology described by
Rafailov et al. (2023)
. In contrast, for DPO, responses are not further truncated, as we are already limiting the maximum tokens generated during the generation process.

When reproducing the
Hybrid Sampling
baseline (Exploration Preference Optimization, XPO) from
Xie et al. (2024)
, we use
\alpha=5\times 10^{-6}
as suggested in the paper.

We do not include a comparison with
Shi et al. (2024)
and
Liu et al. (2024d)
in our experiments. While
Shi et al. (2024)
employs a sampling method similar to ours, their approach requires significantly more hyperparameters to tune, whereas our method involves no hyperparameter tuning. On the other hand,
Liu et al. (2024d)
relies on training an ensemble of 20 reward models to approximate the posterior. Their sampling method requires solving the argmax of these rewards, which is computationally intractable. As a workaround, they generate 20 samples and select the best one using best-of-N with
N=20
. This approach demands at least six times the computational resources compared to our method.

Appendix F
Extension to Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

In this section, we briefly explore how the core principles of our PILAF sampling approach can be extended to PPO-based RLHF methods.

Integrating Response Sampling in InstructGPT:

The PPO-based RLHF pipeline used in InstructGPT
(
Ouyang et al., 2022
)
consists of three key steps:

(i)

Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) that produces the reference model
\uppi_{{\rm ref}}
.

(ii)

Reward Modeling (RM) by solving the optimization problem (
2
), yielding an estimated reward function
r_{\theta}
.

(iii)

Reinforcement Learning Fine-Tuning, where the policy
\uppi_{\phi}
is optimized against the reward model
r_{\theta}
using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, following the optimization scheme (
4
).

The key distinction between the PPO and DPO approaches lies in how the reward model
r_{\theta}
is represented—explicitly in PPO and implicitly in DPO.
In response sampling for data collection, it is crucial to consider the iterative nature of the InstructGPT pipeline. During each iteration, additional human-labeled data is collected for reward modeling (step (ii)), and steps (ii) and (iii) are repeatedly applied to refine the model. Our proposed PILAF algorithm naturally integrates into this pipeline by improving the data collection process in step (ii), thereby enhancing reward model training and, in turn, policy optimization.

Extensions of T-PILAF and PILAF:

Extending our response sampling methods, PILAF and T-PILAF, to the PPO setup with an explicit
r_{\theta}
is both natural and straightforward.

•

Within the theoretical framework of T-PILAF, as introduced in
Section

3
, the only required modification is replacing
\uppi_{\theta}
with the language model
\uppi_{\phi}
and redefining the interpolated and extrapolated policies,
\uppi_{\phi}^{+}
and
\uppi_{\phi}^{-}
, following the same formulation as in equations (
6a
) and (
6b
).
Specifically, we define

\displaystyle\uppi_{\phi}^{+}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{Z^{+}(x)}\;\uppi_{\phi}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)\exp\big\{r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\big\}\,,

(62a)

\displaystyle\uppi_{\phi}^{-}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{Z^{-}(x)}\,\uppi_{\phi}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)\,\exp\big\{-r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\big\},

(62b)

where
r_{\theta}
is now explicitly produced by a reward network, rather than being implicitly derived from
\uppi_{\phi}
, as in equation (
5
).

•

To extend our empirical PILAF algorithm, as described in
Section

5
, we propose applying the same interpolation and extrapolation techniques directly to the logits of the language models
\uppi_{\phi}
and
\uppi_{{\rm ref}}
.
In particular, we take

\displaystyle\uppi_{\phi}^{+}(\cdot\mid x,y_{1:t-1})\;=\;{\sf softmax}\Big(\big\{(1+\beta)\,\boldsymbol{h}_{\phi}-\beta\,\boldsymbol{h}_{{\rm ref}}\big\}(x,y_{1:t-1})\Big),

\displaystyle\uppi_{\phi}^{-}(\cdot\mid x,y_{1:t-1})\;=\;{\sf softmax}\Big(\big\{(1-\beta)\,\boldsymbol{h}_{\phi}+\beta\,\boldsymbol{h}_{{\rm ref}}\big\}(x,y_{1:t-1})\Big),

where
\boldsymbol{h}_{\phi}
and
\boldsymbol{h}_{{\rm ref}}
represent the logits of the language models
\uppi_{\phi}
and
\uppi_{{\rm ref}}
, respectively.

Adaption of Theoretical Analysis:

Our theoretical analyses can be extended to the PPO framework, assuming that the optimization process (
4
) in step (iii) of InstructGPT is solved exactly. In this case, the policy satisfies
\uppi_{\phi}=\uppi_{r_{\theta}}
, where

\displaystyle\uppi_{r_{\theta}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)\;:\,=\;\frac{1}{Z_{\theta}(x)}\,\uppi_{{\rm ref}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,\mid x)\exp\Big\{\frac{1}{\beta}\,r_{\theta}(x,\vec{\boldsymbol{y}}\!\,)\Big\}\,.

Under this assumption, the output language model
\uppi_{\phi}
is implicitly a function of the parameter
\theta
.
Building on this, we can adapt our optimization and statistical analyses as follows:

•

Optimization Consideration:

Using the same argument as in
Theorem

4.1
, we can prove that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta)\;=\;-\,C^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi_{\phi})\,+\,T_{2}\,,

where
C^{\prime}>0
is a universal constant, and
T_{2}
represents a second-order approximation error.

In other words, if the policy optimization step is sufficiently accurate for the reward model
r_{\theta}
, then performing gradient descent on the MLE loss with respect to
\theta
is equivalent to applying gradient ascent on the oracle objective
J
, following the steepest direction in the parameter space of
\theta
.

•

Statistical Consideration:

Even with the new parameterization, the asymptotic distribution of
\widehat{\theta}
from
Theorem

4.2
remains unchanged. Moreover, the gradient and Hessian of
J
with respect to
\theta
retain the same form as in
Theorem

4.1
. As a result, the statistical analysis extends naturally to PPO, allowing us to conclude that PILAF also maintains structure-invariant statistical efficiency for PPO methods.
