Introduction

Self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) is the primary focus of Diabetes management as it helps the patient to check their plasma glucose levels on their own in the absence of experts and maintain strict glycaemic control [1]. They are more convenient, their affordability, portability and convenience of use gives them an edge over traditional laboratory based reference methods [2]. Glucometers are the tools used for SMBG which is important for surveillance of blood glucose levels [3] in patients with diabetes mellitus of all age groups. This helps in aversion of serious complications in times of emergencies resulting from asymptomatic hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia [4] and to take necessary steps to curb the effects of the same. Ever since the introduction of glucometers, their accuracy has always been the major topic of discussion and cause for clinical concern [5, 6]. Numerous studies have shown wide spread discrepancies in results. The patient and the caregiver especially in the ICU settings need to be particularly aware of the possibility of erroneous readings. A seemingly inaccurate monitor providing incongruous readings with respect to the reference method needs to be tested and replaced [4]. Laboratory based results should be used whenever there’s a doubt or margin for error. The strips in glucometers are essentially biosensors, coated with enzymes like Glucose Oxidase-Peroxidase acting as recognition agents. Reflectance or more commonly electrochemical detection is used [7].

Performance metrics of POCT tools available for glucose monitoring is evaluated in the backdrop of total error, which includes error from all the sources, not just analytical. Analytical error alludes to the bias between glucometer and reference method glucose readings [8].

CLSI-C30A quotes the ISO standard for acceptable performance of Blood Glucose Monitoring Systems [8]. International Organization for Standardization 15,197 specifies that, for minimum acceptable accuracy, when compared to the reference laboratory method at least 95% of the results should lie within ± 15 mg/dl of the corresponding value attained by the lab method at glucose concentrations < 75 mg/dl and within ± 20% at glucose concentrations ≥ 75 mg/dl [4]. The 95% limits are percentiles and not confidence limits. Thus, it can be deduced that the 95th percentile of the result i.e., the difference obtained between the measured value and the reference value should fall within the limits for the monitoring system to be clinically acceptable. ISO 15,197 [9] states that this requirement is “based on the medical requirements for glucose monitoring.”

The newer reviewed ISO 15197:2013 standard stipulates a tighter and more robust acceptable criteria for minimum satisfactory accuracy. According to it, the deviation allowed from the reference method for 95% of the Blood Glucose Monitoring System’s results is ± 15 mg/dL at blood glucose concentrations < 100 mg/dL or within ± 15% for BG concentrations ≥ 100 mg/dL for the system to be clinically acceptable. The second criteria states that in a Parke error grid analysis, 99% of the individual results should fall within zones A and B [10].

The SMBG U.S. FDA draft guidance quotes the ISO 15,197 standard for the error and user efficacy of the glucometer, but also suggests the inclusion of CLSI standards for assessment of linearity and interferences [8].

In 1987, the ADA recommended that at the time of measurement at glucose concentrations of 30–400 mg/dl the total error (user plus analytical) should be < 10%. In addition, the ADA stipulated that glucose measurement from a Blood Glucose Monitoring System should not deviate by more than 15% when juxtaposed to a laboratory reference method. The recommendation was modified in 1996, for the maximum analytical error to be < 5% [11]. Clarke and later Parke presented error grids as a way of specifying efficacy of glucometers for clinical purposes [12]. Although several Indian studies have assessed glucometer accuracy, most were limited to outpatient or diabetic cohorts. Data from tertiary-care inpatient populations remain sparse, necessitating this evaluation.

Materials & method

Our study (cross-sectional, method-comparison study) was organized in a Tertiary Care Hospital attached to Government Medical College (Rajiv Gandhi Medical College) in Thane district of Maharashtra, India. Subjects were selected from patients admitted to the medicine ward. The sole inclusion criterion was age. Participants aged between 18 and 60 years were considered eligible for inclusion in the study.

Participants were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria:

Age below 18 years or above 60 years.

Selection was random using random number generation software to avoid any bias and circumvent selection partiality. As per the Helsinki declaration, registration of the patients for the study was done only after procuring written informed consent. Venipuncture was performed for in vitro access for the purpose of collection of whole blood venous sample from the Median Cubital Vein randomly and then the plasma samples were analysed for glucose concentration by fully automated analyser in the central biochemistry laboratory. Simultaneously, Capillary whole blood samples were analysed by the glucometer. This helped to us to rule out bias of inherent difference. Blood glucose estimation was performed using the Accu-Chek Instant Blood Glucose Glucometer and the Erba XL-640 fully automated biochemistry analyser as the standard laboratory reference method. Both devices were calibrated prior to use. Glucose strips were used as per the instructions by the manufacturer. Sample size for this study was determined according to the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) guideline 15197:2013, which recommends a minimum of 100 fresh capillary blood samples for evaluating the analytical accuracy of glucose monitoring systems against a reference laboratory method. Accordingly, a total of 104 paired capillary and venous blood samples were collected by complete enumeration method and analysed. This sample size is considered adequate to provide a representative assessment of analytical performance across a wide glycaemic range and to evaluate the percentage of results meeting the ISO accuracy criteria (± 15 mg/dL or ± 15% of the reference value). In addition, method-comparison studies and Bland–Altman analyses have demonstrated that sample sizes of 100–120 pairs provide reliable estimates of bias and limits of agreement for glucose measurement comparisons. Therefore, the current sample size was deemed sufficient to meet the study objectives while maintaining statistical validity and compliance with accepted international standards.

Blood samples for both glucometer and standard laboratory analysis were collected simultaneously under similar pre-analytical conditions to eliminate variability due to timing or handling differences.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (RGMC/CSMH/IEC/A/299/10, dated 12/10/2022).

Bland-Altman method was used to plot the difference between two methods against the mean of the two methods for the quantification of agreement [13]. Lack of agreement was also evaluated through the BA plot. Parke’s error grid and Surveillance error grid were plotted for the evaluation of clinical efficacy of the glucometer [4]. The two methods were contrasted using the Passing-Bablok regression analysis and Deming Regression analysis. A scatter plot with a regression line was plotted to present the outcomes of the analysis. Constant and proportional errors were represented by the intercept and the slope respectively [14]. P value < 0.05 was considered for the results to be statistically significant. Software SPSS 20 and MS Excel 2019 were used to perform statistical analysis. Accuracy was assessed using International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 15197:2013 guidelines [15].

Unlike previous Indian studies focusing primarily on outpatient diabetics, this study evaluated simultaneous capillary–venous paired samples in a tertiary-care inpatient population using combined Bland–Altman, Passing–Bablok, Deming regression, and Surveillance Error Grid analyses—an approach not previously reported from this region.

The mean ± SD glucose values were 120.4 ± 55.2 mg/dL (laboratory) and 109.4 ± 56.5 mg/dL (glucometer). The mean difference between methods was 11.0 mg/dL (95% CI: 8.2 to 13.6 mg/dL; p < 0.001), indicating a statistically significant bias.

In Parke Error Grid Analysis (P-EGA), x-axis alludes to the value of blood glucose obtained by the standard reference method (GOD-POD) using the laboratory analyser and the result obtained by the glucometer is depicted by the y-axis [12]. In the figure given below (Fig. 1), P-EGA represents the outcomes of blood glucose assessment by glucometer versus that by the standard reference method (GOD-POD) in a disseminated manner. Exact agreement between the two i.e., glucometer and the standard reference method is demonstrated by the dotted line. The Parke error grid (like the Clarke error grid) depicts five risk levels. There are two Parke error grids (one for type 1 diabetes patients and one for type 2 diabetes patients on insulin therapy which is now defunct due to less rigorous acceptance criteria) with clear demarcated boundaries for the performance zones. Zone A in the error grid is the zone of clinically accurate measurements with no effect on clinical action. The next succeeding zones in the increasing order of severity are as follows, Zone B: altered clinical action with little or no effect on clinical outcome; C: altered clinical action, likely to affect clinical outcome; D: altered clinical action, could have significant medical risk [16]; E: altered clinical action, can lead to dangerous medical consequences. Figure 1 shows the grid with respect to our study. This method was chosen to directly assess the potential impact on clinical action and patient outcomes resulting from any measurement inaccuracies.

The Surveillance Error Grid (SEG) is a modern method for risk assessment of blood glucose levels measured by Glucometers with respect to the reference method. It is a 3-dimensional graph concocted on two dimensions, with the third dimension being colour. It assigns a unique risk score to each glucometer result with respect to the reference method. The SEG allows for greater precision in quantifying risks when compared to the traditional methods. In SEG, each point on the graph represents a data pair with X-axis representing the reference blood glucose level while the Y-axis represents the measured blood glucose level via glucometer. SEG presents gradual spectrum of risk within each risk zone that is demarcated by a range of risk scores. The spectrum of risk ranges from no risk whatsoever (the equality line between the values obtained by the glucometer and the reference method) to the greatest risk possible where there is maximum contrast in the values obtained by the two methods. It presents 9 risk zones ranging from extreme risk for hypoglycemia, to no-risk, to extreme risk for hyperglycemia. These risk scores range from − 4 to + 4, with zero corresponding to no risk [17]. With the SEG, a distinction among results because of two methods could be noted because of the continuity and the greater granularity of the analysis. Table 2 quantifies the dissemination of results in SEG with 47.1% of results showing no risk while 26% and 23.1% results show slightly lower and slightly higher risk respectively. Around 3.8% of results show moderately lower risk which are likely to influence inappropriate treatment decisions, emphasizing limited reliability in critical settings.

Bland-Altman (BA) plot is used to quantify agreement between two quantitative measurements and it does so by constructing limits of agreement. These statistical limits are calculated by using the mean and the standard deviation of the differences between the two measurements. The resulting graph is a scatter plot, in which the Y-axis shows the dissimilarity between the measurements obtained by the two methods (A-B) and the X-axis represents the mean of these measurements [(A + B)/2]. Very large differences in the high concentration range are averted by plotting a graph of relative difference. It is recommended that 95% of the data points should lie within ± 1.96 SD of the mean difference [19, 20]. Fig. 3 shows BA plot with increase in scatter with a relative increase in concentration which reflects an increase in random error with increasing concentration. Merely 3 results are outside the ± 1.96 SD with a whopping 101 (97.11%) within acceptable limits.

Passing and Bablok regression analysis (Fig. 5) is a method for nonparametric regression analysis suited for method comparison [21–23]. It is not sensitive to data outliers and distribution of errors. Results are presented with scatter diagram and regression line, and regression equation where intercept represents constant error and slope alludes to proportional error [14]. The result was y=-32.45872 + 1.100917x with (-54.0502,-18.9077) Confidence interval limits (95%) for the intercept and (0.97,1.32) CI limits for the slope. To exemplify the lack of constant and proportional systematic error with respect to method comparison, the 95% confidence intervals must encompass 0 for the intercept and 1 the for slope [21]. Our study reveals constant systemic error.

Deming Regression (Fig. 6) is a method for encompassing a straight line to two dimensional data where both the methods X and Y are assumed to be measured with errors, in which errors for the two variables are assumed to be independent of each other and normally distributed [24, 25]. This is in contrast to simple linear regression where only the Y variable is supposed to be occluded by errors. It is a special case of total least squares regression, which allows for innumerable predictors and comes with a more intricate error form [25]. The typical equation for the regression is y = ax ± b. The dashed line represents X = Y line of agreement. The black line corresponds to the simple linear regression line. The red line is the Deming Regression line with 95% confidence interval limits (shaded area). The procedure requires an error ratio which is either known or which can be estimated from the data with multiple measurements from each subject. When the error ratio is equal to 1, Deming Regression gives the same result as orthogonal regression.

Discussion

Glucometer provides a rather inexpensive way for monitoring blood glucose and they provide results within seconds and thus are used in hospitals, ICUs for diagnosis and to elicit immediate treatment [2].

We used the ISO standards as a benchmark for assessing the performance of glucometer. We think the ADA criteria are too stringent to base our study in the current scenario [1].

The newer reviewed ISO 15197:2013 standard stipulates a tighter and more robust acceptable criteria for minimum satisfactory accuracy. According to it, the deviation allowed from the reference method for 95% of the Blood Glucose Monitoring System’s results is ± 15 mg/dL at blood glucose concentrations < 100 mg/dL or within ± 15% for BG concentrations ≥ 100 mg/dL for the system to be clinically acceptable. The second criteria states that in a Parke error grid analysis, 99% of the individual results should fall within zones A and B [10].

In our study the glucometer used in our settings for SMBG didn’t meet the minimum acceptable requirements set by ISO 15197:2013 [10] as most of our results fall outside the recommended limits. However, it was observed that around 96.14% of the results find a place within the zone A and zone B of Parke’s error grid [10].

Similar trends were reported in Indian cohorts by Ullal et al. (2013) and Pokhrel et al. (2019), though our observed bias was slightly higher, possibly due to inpatient sampling variability.

Multiple factors have been identified in the literature that may contribute to discrepancies between glucometer and laboratory measurements including operator technique [26], pre analytical errors like presence of clots [5], extended exposure of strips to the extremes of temperature, moisture and humidity, medication effects [15] to physiological factors like haematocrit [27], triglycerides, uric acid levels etc. High doses of ascorbic acid (Vitamin C) can interfere when used on burn patients [28]. Certain sugars, like galactose, may be sensed as glucose by certain reagents, leading to inaccurately raised test results [15]. Delay in transportation is a potential factor causing divergent results between two methods. A constant decrease in Blood Glucose takes place at the rate of 5–7% per hour attributable to Glycolysis by RBCs. Fluoride, employed to inhibit glycolysis, initiates its action after 1–2 h, necessitating time to traverse the RBC membrane. Lack of or improper calibration might be one of the factors responsible for the erroneous results. The study was conducted at a single center with a modest sample size, which may limit generalizability. Lot-to-lot variability of glucometer strips was not tested. Interfering substances were not measured, which could influence accuracy. Additionally, although paired sampling minimized timing bias, inpatient conditions may differ from outpatient or home settings. However, we were unable to determine which specific factors contributed to the discrepancies observed in our study.

According to SEG, 47.1% of the results have no risk while 26% and 23.1% of results fall in slightly lower and slightly higher risk category respectively.

Passing and Bablok presented constant systemic error while probability of proportional systemic error can be ignored. The deviation of the regression line from the x = y ideal line, even in the presence of constant systematic error, was modest.

Though the inconsistencies between the two methods didn’t affect the clinical decision. However, this finding may not extend to ICU settings where tighter glycaemic control is required. Inaccurate readings in critically ill or anaemic patients could lead to inappropriate insulin dosing. Hence, laboratory confirmation remains essential for inpatients.

There is an ongoing controversy regarding the performance of glucometer in the care of critically ill patients [29]. Haematocrit of the participants not being considered is the major limitation of the study.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that a substantial gap exists in the analytical performance of the glucometer compared to the standard laboratory analyzer when evaluated against ISO 15197:2013 criteria. However, error grid analysis suggests that the majority of discrepancies observed had minimal impact on clinical decision-making in our study setting.

Glucometers are one of the finest Point-of-Care Testing (POCT) tools accessible for control and management of diabetes mellitus and glycaemic status of an individual. Accuracy and precision aid in efficacious selection of clinical treatment method hence regular calibration is desired so that concurrence with the standard laboratory method is sustained. Development in the technology is awaited so that the glucometers meet the ISO 15,197 and ADA standards.
