Actor–Critic Learning for Risk-Constrained Linear Quadratic Regulation
Weijian Li, Andreas A. Malikopoulos

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the infinite-horizon risk-constrained linear quadratic regulator problem (RC-LQR), which augments the classical LQR formulation with a statistical constraint on the variability of the system state to incorporate risk awareness, a key requirement in safety-critical control applications. We propose an actor–critic learning algorithm that jointly performs policy evaluation and policy improvement in a model-free and online manner. The RC-LQR problem is first reformulated as a max–min optimization problem, from which we develop a multi–time–scale stochastic approximation scheme. The critic employs temporal-difference learning to estimate the action–value function, the actor updates the policy parameters via a policy gradient step, and the dual variable is adapted through gradient ascent to enforce the risk constraint.



I
Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL), aiming to learn optimal policies directly from data in the absence of explicit model knowledge, has gained significant attention in recent years.
Its versatility has enabled progress across a wide range of sequential decision-making problems, including poker games
[
1
]
, robotic control
[
2
]
, and cyber–physical systems
[
3
]
.
Recent studies have investigated the connection between learning and control by introducing information–state representations that decouple estimation and control
[
4
,
5
]
, and by developing integrated learning–and–control frameworks for systems with unknown dynamics
[
6
]
.
Within the control community, there has been an increased interest in establishing a rigorous theoretical foundation for RL in linear systems—most notably through the
linear quadratic regulator
(LQR) problem, which seeks to regulate an unknown linear system to minimize a quadratic cost function
[
7
,
8
]
.

The LQR problem provides a mathematically tractable framework for studying the interplay between learning and control and serves as a canonical benchmark for analyzing the stability, optimality, and convergence of data-driven control algorithms.
However, the classical formulation is
risk-neutral
, as performance is evaluated solely through the expected cost, overlooking low-probability but high-impact disturbances that can cause unsafe behaviors in safety-critical systems
[
9
]
.
This shortcoming has motivated the development of
risk-aware
formulations that explicitly regulate the variability of the closed-loop behavior.
Early work introduced an exponential cost transformation to capture risk sensitivity
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, though such problems can become ill-posed under general noise distributions.
Conditional value-at-risk formulations
[
12
,
13
]
offer a more coherent treatment of tail events but often rely on tractable approximations.
The
risk-constrained LQR (RC-LQR)
framework
[
14
]
augments the standard LQR objective with a constraint on the predictive variance of the state penalty, yielding a closed-form optimal policy that accommodates arbitrary noise models.
Recent extensions established global convergence guarantees for policy optimization methods
[
15
,
16
]
.

Advances in reinforcement learning (RL) have been largely driven by
policy optimization
methods, such as policy gradient and actor–critic algorithms
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
, which directly parameterize and optimize control policies.
These approaches efficiently handle high-dimensional, continuous spaces and are simple to implement.
Theoretical analyses have also been developed for linear control systems
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
, while primal–dual policy gradient algorithms have been proposed for the RC-LQR problem
[
16
]
.
Among these methods, the
actor–critic
framework, which performs policy evaluation and improvement simultaneously, has received extensive attention
[
19
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
.
Compared with sample-based policy gradient schemes, actor–critic algorithms typically converge faster due to variance reduction in gradient estimation.
However, their extension to
constrained
RL problems remains relatively unexplored.

In this paper, we develop an actor–critic algorithm for the RC-LQR problem and characterize its theoretical properties.
The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

(1)
We study a
model-free
LQR problem subject to a risk constraint.
Unlike the classical LQR formulation
[
8
,
21
,
27
]
, the proposed setting minimizes a quadratic cost and also regulates the statistical variability of the system state.
While similar formulations appear in
[
14
,
15
,
28
]
, our focus is on the model-free regime.

(2)
We propose a
multi–time–scale actor–critic algorithm
under a
primal–dual
framework.
The critic employs temporal-difference learning, the actor performs policy-gradient updates, and the dual variable evolves via gradient ascent.
The proposed scheme generalizes two–time–scale actor–critic algorithms
[
19
,
25
,
26
]
to constrained RL and, unlike the sample-based policy gradient method of
[
16
]
, integrates policy evaluation and improvement in an online and model-free manner.

(3)
Using stochastic approximation theory, we establish
almost sure asymptotic convergence
of all iterates.
This result extends classical actor–critic convergence guarantees
[
19
,
25
,
26
]
by incorporating a dual update in the learning loop and strengthens prior results such as
[
16
]
, which established convergence only with high probability.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows.
In Section II, we review the necessary preliminaries that form the basis of our analysis.
In Section III, we present the formulation of the RC-LQR problem.
In Section IV, we develop the proposed actor–critic algorithm under a primal–dual framework, and in Section V, we establish its asymptotic convergence using stochastic approximation theory.
In Section VI, we provide simulation results that illustrate the performance of the algorithm, and in Section VII, we draw concluding remarks.

III
Problem Formulation

Consider the discrete-time linear time-invariant (LTI) stochastic system

x_{t+1}=Ax_{t}+Bu_{t}+w_{t},

(5)

where
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the state,
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
the control input, and
\{w_{t}\}
an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) noise sequence.
The system matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
are unknown but constant.
The infinite-horizon linear quadratic regulator (LQR) problem seeks a control policy
u:=\{u_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
that minimizes the long-run average cost

\mathcal{J}(u):=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\!\left(x_{t}^{\top}Qx_{t}+u_{t}^{\top}Ru_{t}\right)\!\right],

(6)

where
Q\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}
and
R\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{m}
are the state and input weighting matrices, respectively.

The LQR problem is
risk-neutral
, as it optimizes only the expected performance.
Consequently, the state may grow arbitrarily large by extreme noise realizations, particularly when
\{w_{t}\}
follows a heavy-tailed distribution.
To incorporate risk sensitivity, following
[
14
,
16
,
28
]
, we consider the
risk-constrained LQR (RC-LQR)
problem formulated as

\displaystyle\min_{u}

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\!\left(x_{t}^{\top}Qx_{t}+u_{t}^{\top}Ru_{t}\right)\!\right]

(7)

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=Ax_{t}+Bu_{t}+w_{t},

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\!\Big(x_{t}^{\top}Qx_{t}-\mathbb{E}[x_{t}^{\top}Qx_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\Big)^{\!2}\right]\leq\iota,

where
\iota>0
is a user-defined risk-tolerance parameter, and

\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma\{x_{s},u_{s}:s\leq t\}
denotes the
\sigma
-algebra generated by all the observations up to time
t
.

To ensure the well-posedness of (
7
), we impose the following standing assumptions.

Assumption 3

The pair
(A,B)
is stabilizable,
Q\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}
,
R\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{m}
, and the pair
(A,Q^{1/2})
is detectable.

Assumption 4

The noise sequence
\{w_{t}\}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}\|w_{t}\|^{4}<\infty
.

Assumption 5

The Slater condition holds; namely, there exists a feasible control policy that strictly satisfies the risk constraint in (
7
).

Remark 1

Unlike the standard LQR problem, (
7
) incorporates the predictive variance of the state penalty, thereby enhancing risk awareness.
Decreasing the risk-tolerance parameter
\iota
tightens the constraint, providing a systematic trade-off between average performance and risk sensitivity.

Let
\bar{w}:=\mathbb{E}[w_{t}]
and

W:=\mathbb{E}[(w_{t}-\bar{w})(w_{t}-\bar{w})^{\top}]

denote the mean and covariance of the noise, respectively, and assume
W\succ 0
.
Define the higher-order moments

\displaystyle M_{3}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[(w_{t}-\bar{w})(w_{t}-\bar{w})^{\top}Q(w_{t}-\bar{w})\right],

\displaystyle m_{4}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big((w_{t}-\bar{w})^{\top}Q(w_{t}-\bar{w})-\mathrm{tr}(WQ)\big)^{2}\right].

By
[
15
, Theorem 1]
, the optimal policy of (
7
) admits a time-invariant affine structure, i.e.,

u_{t}=-K^{*}x_{t}+b^{*},

where
K^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
b^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Hence, it is without loss of optimality to restrict attention to affine policies of the form
u_{t}=-Kx_{t}+b
.
Let
X:=[K,\,b]
denote the decision variable of (
7
), and
X^{*}:=[K^{*},\,b^{*}]
its optimal value.
Following the argument in
[
14
, Proposition 1]
, problem (
7
) can be equivalently reformulated as

\displaystyle\min_{X}

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(X)

(8)

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=Ax_{t}+Bu_{t}+w_{t},

\displaystyle u_{t}=-Kx_{t}+b,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{c}(X)\leq\bar{\iota},

where
\bar{\iota}:=\iota-m_{4}+4\,\mathrm{tr}[(WQ)^{2}]
, and

\mathcal{J}(X):=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\!\left(x_{t}^{\top}Qx_{t}+u_{t}^{\top}Ru_{t}\right)\!\right],

\mathcal{J}_{c}(X):=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\!\left(4\,x_{t}^{\top}QWQx_{t}+4\,x_{t}^{\top}QM_{3}\right)\!\right].

Remark 2

The RC-LQR problem was originally introduced in
[
14
,
15
]
and later extended to mean-field linear–quadratic systems in
[
28
]
.
Subsequent work
[
16
]
developed primal–dual policy gradient algorithms for this formulation.
In contrast, the present paper proposes an
actor–critic algorithm
that solves the RC-LQR problem in an online, model-free setting.
The method jointly performs policy evaluation and improvement within a variance-reduction framework, achieving faster convergence than standard policy gradient approaches.

IV
Actor-Critic Algorithm for RC-LQR

In this section, we analyze the properties of the state- and action-value functions and establish a policy gradient representation that links policy gradients to the action-value function. Building on these results, we develop a multi–time–scale actor–critic algorithm under a primal–dual framework.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x_{t}\\
u_{t}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\!\Upsilon_{K,\mu}\!\begin{bmatrix}x_{t}\\
u_{t}\end{bmatrix}+2\begin{bmatrix}p_{X,\mu}\\
q_{X,\mu}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\!\begin{bmatrix}x_{t}\\
u_{t}\end{bmatrix}-\mathcal{L}(X,\mu)

\displaystyle-\mu\bar{\iota}+z^{1}_{X,\mu}+g_{X,\mu}^{\top}\bar{w}+{\rm tr}(P_{K,\mu}\bar{w}\bar{w}^{\top})+{\rm tr}(P_{K,\mu}W),

where
\Upsilon_{K,\mu}
,
p_{X,\mu}
and
q_{X,\mu}
are defined in (
15
).
Hence, the second claim follows, which completes the proof.

\hfill\square

The following lemma characterizes the policy gradient of
\mathcal{L}(X,\mu)
with respect to
X
.

Lemma 5

For any
X\in\mathcal{S}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, it holds that

\nabla_{X}\mathcal{L}(X,\mu)=2H_{X,\mu}\Phi_{X},

(17)

where
H_{X,\mu}\!=\!\big[E_{K,\mu},\!G_{X,\mu}\big]
,
\Upsilon_{K,\mu}
and
q_{X,\mu}
are given in (
15
),

\displaystyle E_{K,\mu}=\Upsilon_{K,\mu}^{22}K-\Upsilon_{K,\mu}^{21},~G_{X,\mu}=\Upsilon_{K,\mu}^{22}b-q_{X,\mu},

(18)

and moreover,

\displaystyle\Phi_{X}

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\left\{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\begin{bmatrix}x_{t}\\
-1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{t}\\
-1\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\right\}

(19)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{K}+\bar{x}_{X}\bar{x}_{X}^{\top}&-\bar{x}_{X}\\
-\bar{x}_{X}&1\end{bmatrix}\succ 0.

Proof:

By arguments analogous to those in the proof of Lemma 2 in
[
16
]
, we obtain

\nabla_{X}\mathcal{L}(X,\mu)=2\big[E_{K,\mu},G_{X,\mu}\big]\Phi_{X},

where

\displaystyle E_{K,\mu}

\displaystyle=(R+B^{\top}P_{K,\mu}B)K-B^{\top}P_{K,\mu}A,

\displaystyle G_{X,\mu}

\displaystyle=(R+B^{\top}P_{K,\mu}B)b+B^{\top}P_{K,\mu}\bar{w}+\frac{1}{2}B^{\top}g_{X,\mu}.

From (
15
), we obtain (
18
), completing the proof.

\hfill\square

) differs from the deterministic counterparts considered in

]
by incorporating Gaussian exploration noise.
Nevertheless, the state-value function retains its quadratic structure, and the resulting policy gradients coincide with those in
[

]
.
Overall, the theoretical characterization parallels that of the standard LQR problem
[

]
and the mean-field linear–quadratic control setting
[

]
.

Remark 4

) and its analytical connection to the policy gradient for the RC-LQR problem has not been explicitly presented in prior work.

IV-B

Multi-Time-Scale Actor-Critic Algorithm

Next, we proceed to design an actor–critic algorithm for problem (
10
) within a primal–dual framework.
Specifically, the primal variable
X
is updated along the negative gradient direction of the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}(X,\mu)
, while the dual variable
\mu
is updated along its gradient ascent direction.
In the model-free setting, the exact policy gradients are unavailable.
To address this challenge, we estimate the action-value function
Q^{\mu}_{X}
in the
critic step
.
Then, using Lemma
5
, we compute the policy gradient of
\mathcal{L}(X,\mu)
with respect to
X
and update the primal variable in the
actor step
.
Finally, the dual variable
\mu
is updated based on the estimated constraint function
\mathcal{J}_{c}(X)
.

For a given policy
X\in\mathcal{S}
and a multiplier
\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we learn
Q_{X}^{\mu}
via a temporal-difference (TD) algorithm as follows.

For a pair
(x,u)
, we define the feature vector as

\displaystyle\psi(x,u)\!=\!\begin{bmatrix}\!\phi(x,u)\\
2x\\
2u\!\end{bmatrix}\!\!,~{\rm where}~\phi(x,u)\!=\!{\rm svec}\begin{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
u\end{pmatrix}\!\begin{pmatrix}x\\
u\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\!.

In addition, we denote

\displaystyle\Theta_{X}^{\mu}=\begin{bmatrix}{\rm svec}(\Upsilon_{K,\mu})\\
p_{X,\mu}\\
q_{X,\mu}\end{bmatrix}.

(20)

Recalling (
15
) gives

Q^{\mu}_{X}=\psi(x,u)^{\top}\Theta_{X}^{\mu}+\varsigma_{X,\mu}.

(21)

where
\varsigma_{X,\mu}
is a scalar independent of
x
and
u
.
Clearly, to estimate
\Upsilon_{K,\mu}
and
q_{X,\mu}
, it suffices to compute
\Theta_{X}^{\mu}
.

Note that the action-value function
Q^{\mu}_{X}
satisfies a Bellman equation as

\displaystyle Q^{\mu}_{X}(x_{t},u_{t})=

\displaystyle c_{\mu}(x_{t},u_{t})-\mathcal{L}(X,\mu)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\big[Q^{\mu}_{X}(x_{t+1},u_{t+1})\mid x_{t},u_{t}\big].

Substituting (
21
) into the above equation, we derive

\displaystyle\psi(x_{t},u_{t})^{\top}\Theta^{\mu}_{X}=

\displaystyle c_{\mu}(x_{t},u_{t})-\mathcal{L}(X,\mu)

(22)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\big[\psi(x_{t+1},u_{t+1})^{\top}\cdot\Theta^{\mu}_{X}\mid x_{t},u_{t}\big].

For notational convenience, we denote

c_{\mu,t}:=c_{\mu}(x_{t},u_{t})
,

\psi_{t}:=\psi(x_{t},u_{t})
,
and
\mathcal{L}_{t}:=\mathcal{L}(X_{t},\mu_{t})
.
Let
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{t}
be an estimate that tracks the long-term Lagrangian value
\mathcal{L}(X,\mu)
, and let
\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,t}
denote the estimate of the parameter vector
\Theta^{\mu}_{X}
at iteration
t
, where

\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,t}=\big[\big(\mathrm{svec}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{(K,\mu),t})\big)^{\!\top},~\hat{p}^{\top}_{(X,\mu),t},~\hat{q}^{\top}_{(X,\mu),t}\big]^{\!\top}.

(23)

Here,
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{(K,\mu),t}
,
\hat{p}_{(X,\mu),t}
, and
\hat{q}_{(X,\mu),t}
denote the estimates of
\Upsilon_{K,\mu}
,
p_{X,\mu}
, and
q_{X,\mu}
, respectively.

Then the TD algorithm is designed as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{t})\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot c_{\mu,t},

(24)

\displaystyle\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,t+1}

\displaystyle=\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot\delta_{t}\cdot\psi_{t},

(25)

where
\alpha_{t}>0
is the stepsize, and the TD error
\delta_{t}
is

\delta_{t}=c_{\mu,t}-\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{t}+(\psi_{t+1}-\psi_{t})^{\top}\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,t}.

(26)

The TD algorithm follows the general structure of those in
[
33
,
19
,
25
]
,
but is tailored to the RC-LQR problem, where both the feature vector and the action-value function are parameterized in problem-specific forms.

According to (
17
), computing the policy gradient requires an estimate of
\Phi_{X}
.
Analogous to the evolution of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{t}
, we estimate
\Phi_{X}
via

\hat{\Phi}_{X,t+1}=(1-\alpha_{t})\hat{\Phi}_{X,t}+\alpha_{t}\,z_{t}z_{t}^{\top},

(27)

where
\hat{\Phi}_{X}
tracks
\Phi_{X}
in (
19
), and
z_{t}=[x_{t}^{\top},-1]^{\top}
.

It is worth noting that our focus is on computing the exact policy gradient for the update of
X
, and hence (
27
) is used to estimate
\Phi_{X}
.
In contrast, if a natural policy gradient algorithm were adopted, as in
[
27
,
34
,
19
]
, such estimation would not be required.

Recalling (
9
) gives
\nabla_{\mu}\mathcal{L}(X,\mu)=\mathcal{J}_{c}(X)-\bar{\iota}
.
For the evolution of
\mu
, we estimate
\mathcal{J}_{c}(X)
by

\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{c}_{X,t+1}=(1-\alpha_{t})\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{c}_{X,t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot o_{t},

(28)

where
o_{t}=x_{t}^{\top}QWQx_{t}+4x_{t}^{\top}QM_{3}-\bar{\iota}
.

For the actor step, we first recover
\hat{\Upsilon}_{(K,\mu),t}
and
\hat{q}_{(X,\mu),t}
from
\vartheta_{X,t}^{\mu}
according to (
23
).
Then the estimates of
E_{X,\mu}
and
G_{X,\mu}
, denoted by
\hat{E}_{(K,\mu),t}
and
\hat{G}_{(K,\mu),t}
, are computed via

\displaystyle\hat{E}_{(K,\mu),t}

\displaystyle=\hat{\Upsilon}_{(K,\mu),t}^{22}K_{t}-\hat{\Upsilon}_{(K,\mu),t}^{21}

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{(X,\mu),t}

\displaystyle=\hat{\Upsilon}_{(K,\mu),t}^{22}b_{t}-\hat{q}_{(X,\mu),t}.

Define
\hat{H}_{(X,\mu),t}=[\hat{E}_{(K,\mu),t},\hat{G}_{(X,\mu),t}]
.
Based on these estimates, the policy gradient algorithm for
X
is designed as

\displaystyle X_{t+1}=\Gamma_{\mathcal{X}}\big[X_{t}-\beta_{t}\hat{H}_{(X,\mu),t}\hat{\Phi}_{X,t}\big],

(29)

where
\beta_{t}>0
is the stepsize, and
\Gamma_{\mathcal{X}}(\cdot)
denotes the Euclidean projection operator to ensure the boundedness of the sequence
\{X_{t}\}
. The set
\mathcal{X}
is compact and convex, satisfying

\mathcal{X}\subset\{X\mid\rho(A-BK)\leq 1-\epsilon\},

for some
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, and contains the optimal policy
X^{*}
.

In fact, projection operators are widely used to stabilize the stochastic approximation algorithms
[
30
]
. The aforementioned requirements are standard in the analysis of actor-critic algorithms
[
19
,
25
,
32
]
.
Notably, the projection is mainly introduced for theoretical analysis and may not be necessary in practical implementations.

Finally, the dual variable
\mu
is updated using a projected gradient ascent scheme:

\mu_{t+1}=\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\!\left[\mu_{t}+\gamma_{t}\big(\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{c}_{X,t}-\bar{\iota}\big)\right],

(30)

where
\gamma_{t}>0
is the stepsize, and
\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(\cdot)
denotes the Euclidean projection operator onto the nonnegative orthant, ensuring
\mu_{t}\geq 0
for all
t
.

We impose the following standard conditions on the stepsizes.

Assumption 6

Let
\{\alpha_{t}\}
,
\{\beta_{t}\}
, and
\{\gamma_{t}\}
be positive stepsize sequences satisfying

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{t}\alpha_{t}=\sum\nolimits_{t}\beta_{t}=\sum\nolimits_{t}\gamma_{t}=\infty,

(31)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{t}(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}+\gamma_{t}^{2})<\infty,

\displaystyle\lim\nolimits_{t\to\infty}\frac{\beta_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}=0,\quad\lim\nolimits_{t\to\infty}\frac{\gamma_{t}}{\beta_{t}}=0.

Remark 5

The first two conditions in Assumption
6
are standard in stochastic approximation theory
[
30
]
.
The final condition enforces a separation of time scales: the critic updates occur at the fastest rate, followed by the actor, while the dual ascent step evolves the slowest.
A similar assumption has been adopted in
[
35
,
36
]
.

We summarize the proposed multi–time–scale actor–critic algorithm in Algorithm
1
.
This framework extends the conventional two–time–scale actor–critic schemes
[
19
,
25
,
26
]
by incorporating an additional time scale associated with the dual variable.
Unlike the bilevel optimization structures considered in
[
27
,
32
]
, all decision variables are updated concurrently.
Furthermore, the method differs from the sample–based policy gradient algorithm in
[
16
]
in that neither the global cost nor the constraint function needs to be explicitly sampled.

Algorithm 1

Actor-Critic Algorithm for RC-LQR

Input:

The initial policy
X_{0}
, multiplier
\mu_{0}
, and state
x_{0}
.

The initial values of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
,
\hat{\Phi}_{X,0}
,
\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{c}_{X,0}
and
\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,0}
.

The stepsize sequences
\{\alpha_{t}\}
,
\{\beta_{t}\}
and
\{\gamma_{t}\}
.

Repeat:

Execute
u_{t}=-K_{t}x_{t}+b_{t}+\sigma\eta_{t}
.

Observer the state
x_{t+1}
and cost
c_{\mu,t}
.

Update
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{t+1}=(1-\alpha_{t})\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot c_{\mu,t}
.

Update
\hat{\Phi}_{X,t+1}=(1-\alpha_{t})\hat{\Phi}_{X,t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot z_{t}z_{t}^{\top}
.

Update
\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{c}_{X,t+1}=(1-\alpha_{t})\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{c}_{X,t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot o_{t}
.

Compute the TD error

\delta_{t}=c_{\mu,t}-\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{t}+(\psi_{t+1}-\psi_{t})^{\top}\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,t}
.

Critic step:

\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,t+1}=\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot\delta_{t}\cdot\psi_{t}
.

Actor step:

\displaystyle X_{t+1}=\Gamma_{\mathcal{X}}\big[X_{t}-\beta_{t}\hat{H}_{(X,\mu),t}\hat{\Phi}_{X,t}\big].

Dual ascent:

\mu_{t+1}=\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\big[\mu_{t}+\gamma_{t}(\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{c}_{X,t}-\bar{\iota})\big]
.

Output:
Policy
X
and the multiplier
\mu
.

V
THEORETICAL RESULTS

In this section, we analyze the convergence properties of Algorithm
1
using the ordinary differential equation (ODE) method developed in
[
30
,
29
]
.
The analysis is based on the multi–time–scale stochastic approximation framework of
[
37
]
.
In particular, under Assumption
6
, the results in
[
37
]
ensure the convergence of the critic update when the policy
X
and multiplier
\mu
are held fixed.
Subsequently, we establish the convergence of the policy update for a fixed
\mu
, and finally analyze the convergence behavior of the dual variable
\mu
.

For a fixed policy
X
and multiplier
\mu
, we first analyze the convergence of the critic step as follows.

Theorem 1

Let Assumptions
3
-
6
hold.
Consider the sequences
\{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{t}\}
,
\{\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,t}\}
,
\{\hat{\Phi}_{X,t}\}
and
\{\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{c}_{X,t}\}
generated by (
24
), (
25
), (
27
) and (
28
), respectively.
For any fixed policy
X\in\mathcal{X}
and multiplier
\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{t}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(X,\mu),~\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,t}\rightarrow\vartheta^{\mu,*}_{X},~

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}_{X,t}\rightarrow\Phi_{X},~{\rm and}~\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{c}_{X,t}\rightarrow\mathcal{J}_{c}(X)~{\rm a.s.}

where
\mathcal{L}(X,\mu)
,
\Phi_{X}
and
\mathcal{J}_{c}(X)
are defined in (
9
), (
19
) and (
8
),
\vartheta^{\mu,*}_{X}
is the unique solution to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x_{t},u_{t})}\big[\psi_{t}(\psi_{t}-\psi_{t+1})^{\top}\big]\cdot\vartheta^{\mu,*}_{X}

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{(x_{t},u_{t})}[\psi_{t}]\cdot\mathcal{L}(X,\mu)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(x_{t},u_{t})}[c_{\mu,t}\psi_{t}].

and moreover,
\mathbb{E}_{(x_{t},u_{t})}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation with respect to
x_{t}\sim\nu_{X}
and
u_{t}\sim\pi_{X}^{\mu}(\cdot\mid x_{t})
.

Proof:

The local update in (
24
) forms a stochastic approximation iteration, whose asymptotic behavior is captured by the ODE

\dot{\hat{\mathcal{L}}}=-\hat{\mathcal{L}}+\mathcal{L}(X,\mu).

Substituting
\delta_{t}
from (
26
) into (
25
), we derive

\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,t+1}=\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot\big[(c_{\mu,t}-\hat{\mathcal{L}})\psi_{t}+\psi_{t}(\psi_{t+1}-\psi_{t})^{\top}\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,t}\big].

Then the ODE characterizing the asymptotic behavior of this recursion is given by

\dot{\vartheta}^{\mu}_{X}=\Xi\cdot\vartheta^{\mu}_{X}-\bar{\psi}\hat{\mathcal{L}}+l,

where
\Xi=\mathbb{E}_{(x_{t},u_{t})}\big[\psi_{t}(\psi_{t+1}-\psi_{t})^{\top}\big]
,
\bar{\psi}=\mathbb{E}_{(x_{t},u_{t})}[\psi_{t}]
, and

l=\mathbb{E}_{(x_{t},u_{t})}[c_{\mu,t}\psi_{t}]
.
Denote by
\omega=[\vartheta^{\mu,\top}_{X},\hat{\mathcal{L}}]^{\top}
,
\hat{\Xi}=[\Xi,-\bar{\psi};~0,-1]
and
\hat{l}=[l^{\top},\mathcal{L}(X,\mu)]^{\top}
.
It follows that

\dot{\omega}=\hat{\Xi}\cdot\omega+\hat{l}.

(32)

Let
f(\omega)
be the right-hand-side of (
32
). Clearly,
f(\omega)
is Lipschitz continuous in
\omega
.
Define
f_{\infty}(\omega)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{f(r\omega)}{r}
.
It follows that

f_{\infty}(\omega)=\hat{\Xi}\cdot\omega
.
We next analyze the performance of a dynamics given by

\dot{\omega}=\hat{\Xi}\cdot\omega.

(33)

It follows directly that

\Xi+\Xi^{\top}=-\,\mathbb{E}_{(x_{t},u_{t})}\!\left[(\psi_{t+1}-\psi_{t})(\psi_{t+1}-\psi_{t})^{\top}\right]\preceq 0,

which implies that
\Xi
is negative semidefinite.

Next, we establish that
(\Xi+\Xi^{\top})\prec 0
.
Let
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{t}
denote the filtration

\mathcal{F}^{1}_{t}=\sigma(x_{0},w_{0},\ldots,w_{t-1},\eta_{0},\ldots,\eta_{t})
,
which forms an increasing
\sigma
-algebra with respect to time
t
.
Define
\Delta\psi_{t}=\psi_{t+1}-\psi_{t}
.
For any nonzero vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{\dim(\Delta\psi_{t})}
,
let
r_{y}=y^{\top}\Delta\psi_{t}
.
Then, it holds that

\displaystyle-\,y^{\top}(\Xi+\Xi^{\top})y

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(x_{t},u_{t})}[r_{y}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{(x_{t},u_{t})}\!\left[\mathbb{E}[r_{y}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}^{1}_{t}]\right].

Since
\mathbb{E}\left[(w_{t}-\bar{w})(w_{t}-\bar{w})^{\top}\right]\succ 0
, it follows from (
5
) that for any
y_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbb{E}\big[\|y_{x}^{\top}(x_{t+1}-x_{t})\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}^{1}_{t}\big]=0
only if
y_{x}=0
.
Using a similar argument, we have

\mathbb{E}[r_{y}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}^{1}_{t}]=0
if and only if
y=0
since
u_{t}=-Kx_{t}+b+\sigma\eta_{t}
, where
\eta_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{m})
and
\sigma>0
.
Consequently,
-\,y^{\top}(\Xi+\Xi^{\top})y>0
for all
y\neq 0
,
implying that
(\Xi+\Xi^{\top})\prec 0
.
Therefore,
\Xi
is a Hurwitz matrix.

By definition, the eigenvalues of
\hat{\Xi}
consist of those of
\Xi
together with an additional eigenvalue at
-1
.
Since all eigenvalues of
\Xi
have negative real parts,
\hat{\Xi}
is Hurwitz.
Hence, the ODE in (
33
) admits the origin as its unique globally asymptotically stable equilibrium.

Let
\mathcal{F}^{2}_{t}
be the
\sigma
-algebra given by

\mathcal{F}^{2}_{t}=\sigma(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{s},\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,s},s\leq t)
.
Define

M^{1}_{t+1}=c_{\mu,t}-\mathbb{E}[c_{\mu,t}\mid\mathcal{F}^{2}_{t}]
, and

M^{2}_{t+1}=\delta_{t}\cdot\psi_{t}-\mathbb{E}[\delta_{t}\cdot\psi_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}^{2}_{t}]
. It follows that
both
\{M^{1}_{t},\mathcal{F}^{2}_{t}:t\geq 0\}
and
\{M^{2}_{t},\mathcal{F}^{2}_{t}:t\geq 0\}
are martingale difference sequences.
For any
X\in\mathcal{X}
and
\mu\geq 0
, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|M_{t+1}^{1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}^{2}_{t}\big]\leq C_{1}\cdot\big(1+{\hat{\mathcal{L}}}_{t}^{2}+\|\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,t}\|^{2}\big),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|M_{t+1}^{2}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}^{2}_{t}\big]\leq C_{2}\cdot\big(1+{\hat{\mathcal{L}}}_{t}^{2}+\|\vartheta^{\mu}_{X,t}\|^{2}\big),

for some
C^{1},C^{2}<\infty
.

Recalling Lemma
2
yields
\sup_{t}\|\omega_{t}\|<\infty
a.s.
Then, by Lemma
1
, we have
\omega_{t}\!\to\!\omega^{*}
a.s.,
where
\omega^{*}=(\vartheta^{\mu,*}_{X},\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{*})
denotes the equilibrium of (
32
) satisfying

\Xi\,\vartheta^{\mu,*}_{X}-\bar{\psi}\,\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{*}+l=0,\qquad\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{*}=\mathcal{L}(X,\mu).

Moreover, the equilibrium
\vartheta^{\mu,*}_{X}
is unique since
\Xi
is Hurwitz.

By an analogous argument, we can show that

\hat{\Phi}_{X,t}\!\to\!\Phi_{X}
and

\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{c}_{X,t}\!\to\!\mathcal{J}_{c}(X)
a.s.
This completes the proof.

\hfill\square

Remark 6

The quantity
\vartheta^{\mu,*}_{X}
represents the exact solution of the Bellman equation (
22
) corresponding to the given policy
X
and multiplier
\mu
.
Theorem
1
therefore shows that the proposed TD algorithm accurately estimates the associated action–value function.

Next, we turn to the convergence analysis of the policy sequence
\{X_{t}\}
in Algorithm

, assuming a fixed multiplier

Theorem 2

Suppose Assumptions
3
–
6
hold.
Consider the sequence
\{X_{t}\}
generated by (

).
For any fixed
\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the iterates
\{X_{t}\}
converge a.s. to an equilibrium point of

\dot{X}=\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{X}}\big[-h_{\mu}(X)\big],

(34)

where
h_{\mu}(X)=H_{X,\mu}\Phi_{X}
, and moreover, both
H_{X,\mu}
and
\Phi_{X}
are defined in (

).

Proof:

Let
\mathcal{F}^{3}_{t}
be the
\sigma
-field generated by
\{X_{s},s\leq t\}
.
It is clear that the recursion (

) reads as

\displaystyle X_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\mathcal{X}}\big[X_{t}-\beta_{t}h_{\mu}(X)+\beta_{t}\xi^{1}_{t}\big],

(35)

where
\xi^{1}_{t}=h_{\mu}(X)-\hat{H}_{(X,\mu),t}\hat{\Phi}_{X,t}

and Remark

, gives
\lim_{t\to\infty}\xi_{t}^{1}=0

3
, the iteration (

For a fixed

, we have

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{1}

\displaystyle=\big\langle h_{\mu}(X),\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{X}}[-h_{\mu}(X)\big\rangle=-\big\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{X}}[-h_{\mu}(X)]\big\|^{2}\leq 0.

where the second equality follows from the Moreau decomposition.
As a consequence,
X_{t}
approaches the equilibrium of (

) a.s. by Lemma

, and this completes the proof.

\hfill\square

Remark 7

Since the optimal policy

lies in

, the equilibrium set of (

.
We note that Theorem

is consistent with the convergence results established in

\mu_{t+1}=\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\!\left[\mu_{t}+\gamma_{t}\big(\mathcal{J}_{c}(\tilde{X}_{\mu})-\bar{\iota}+\xi^{2}_{t}\big)\right],

VII
CONCLUSION

In this paper, we investigated an RC–LQR problem with unknown system dynamics.
Unlike the standard LQR formulation, we imposed a constraint on the one–step predictive variance of the state to enhance safety in practical applications.
To address this problem, we developed a primal–dual actor–critic algorithm, in which the critic employed a temporal–difference method to estimate the action–value function, the actor updated the policy via a policy gradient step, and the dual variable was adjusted through a gradient ascent update.
We designed multi–time–scale learning rates to ensure convergence of the learning process, and established asymptotic convergence using stochastic approximation theory.
Finally, we demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed approach through numerical simulations.
