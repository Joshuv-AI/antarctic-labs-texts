Languages of Words of Low Automatic Complexity Are Hard to Compute
Joey Chen, Bjørn Kjos-Hanssen, Ivan Koswara, Linus Richter, Frank Stephan

Abstract
. The automatic complexity of a finite word (string) is an analogue for finite automata of Sipser’s distinguishing complexity (1983) and was introduced by Shallit and Wang (2001). For a finite alphabet Σ \Sigma of at least two elements, we consider the non-deterministic automatic complexity given by exactly —yet not necessarily uniquely —accepting automata: a word x ∈ Σ ∗ x\in\Sigma^{*} has exact non-deterministic automatic complexity k ∈ ℕ k\in\mathbb{N} if there exists a non-deterministic automaton of k k states which accepts x x while rejecting every other word of the same length as x x , and no automaton of fewer states has this property. Importantly, and in contrast to the classical notion, the witnessing automaton may have multiple paths of computation accepting x x . We denote this measure of complexity by A N ​ e A_{Ne} , and study a class of languages of low A N ​ e A_{Ne} -complexity defined as L q = { x ∈ Σ ∗ : A N ​ e ​ ( x ) < q ​ | x | } L_{q}=\{\,x\in\Sigma^{*}\colon A_{Ne}(x)<q|x|\,\} , which is parameterised by rationals q ∈ ( 0 , 1 / 2 ) q\in(0,1/2) (generalising a class of sets first studied by Kjos-Hanssen). We show that for every q ∈ ( 0 , 1 / 2 ) q\in(0,1/2) , this class is neither context-free nor recognisable by certain Boolean circuits. In the process, we answer an open question of Kjos-Hanssen quantifying the complexity of L 1 / 3 L_{1/3} in terms of Boolean circuits, and also prove the Shannon effect for A N ​ e A_{Ne} .

1.1.
Technical Background

Fix a finite alphabet
\Sigma

Definition 1.1

Let

The pumping lemma shows that this definition is maximally restrictive on the number of words accepted by the witnessing automaton; trying to strengthen the definition by asking for outright uniqueness of the accepted word only leads to trivialities.

Definition 1.2

The

A_{D}(x)=\min\{\,k\in\mathbb{N}\colon\text{{there exists a DFA of $k$ states which exactly accepts $x$}}\,\}.

[

(1)

is not invariant under natural transformations on strings, such as reversals. For instance, Hyde and Kjos-Hanssen have verified computationally that

(2)

The DFA witnessing

To overcome these obstacles, Hyde introduced automatic complexity witnessed by the smallest

Definition 1.3

Let

Clearly, every DFA which exactly accepts

Let

of

A_{N}(x)=\min\{\,k\in\mathbb{N}\colon\text{{there exists an NFA of $k$ states which uniquely accepts $x$}}\,\}.

3.

Is Not Context-Free

Fix

We provide the required definitions. (For more details, see e.g.
[

]

•

•

•

•

We also insist that

symbols

. Elements of

are called

. Productions in

are pairs
(A,\gamma)
where

and

The

is derived from

in

The transitive and reflexive closure of

is denoted by

.

A language that is recognisable by a CFG is called a

, abbreviated “CFL”.

Definition 3.1
.

A CFG has

if:

(1)

each nonterminal is reachable from the starting symbol; and

(2)

a terminal string can be derived from each nonterminal.

Let

A

has a rich nonterminal but no useless nonterminals. A

is generated by a rich CFG.

Our motivation for rich CFGs follows from

2.6
, however, we note here that, in style, our richness characterisation is similar to classical results by Ginsburg

The equivalence in

We now give a characterisation of
\mathord{\bigoplus}\mathbf{SAC}^{0}
in terms of finite fields and their operations. This characterisation is akin to that of
\mathbf{SAC}^{0}
in
Lemma

4.2
in terms of propositional formulas.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
\phi_{n}\colon\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
be a linear isomorphism of vector spaces over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, and suppose
L\subset\Sigma^{*}
is
\mathord{\bigoplus}\mathbf{SAC}^{0}
-recognisable. Then there exists a family of polynomials
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
f_{n}\colon\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p}
for which

x\in L\cap\Sigma^{n}\iff(f_{n}\circ\phi_{n})(x)\neq 0

and for which there exists
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\deg(f_{n})\leq p^{n}-p^{n-\ell}
.

Proof.

As we work in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, we identify
\oplus
with addition modulo
p
, and write
x+y
for
x\oplus y
. Consider the family
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
given by
Definition

4.5
. Therefore, there exists
k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
for which

\psi_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\pi_{(i,j)}(x)\right)

where
\pi_{(\cdot,\cdot)}
is a projection function from
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
to
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Note that since the Boolean circuit has constant depth, the sequence
(m_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded. Consider the composition
f_{n}
defined by:

(1)

\displaystyle f_{n}(x)=(\psi_{n}\circ\phi_{n}^{-1})(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\left(\pi_{(i,j)}\circ\phi_{n}^{-1}\right)(x)\right)

Note that
f_{n}\circ\phi_{n}=\psi_{n}
and thus
x\in L\cap\Sigma^{n}
if and only if
\psi_{n}(x)=(f_{n}\circ\phi_{n})(x)\neq 0
; so,
f_{n}
is as needed. We now show that
f_{n}
is a polynomial. Since
\pi_{(\cdot,\cdot)}
and
\phi_{n}^{-1}
are linear, so is their composition, whose range is contained in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
Lemma

4.7
tells us now that
\pi_{(i,j)}\circ\phi_{n}^{-1}
may be expressed as

\left(\pi_{(i,j)}\circ\phi_{n}^{-1}\right)(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{n}a_{(i,j,\ell)}x^{p^{t}}.

Therefore, eq. (
1
) shows that
f_{n}
itself is a polynomial on
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
with range in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. To bound the degree of
f_{n}
, use distributivity in the field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
and
Lemma

4.7
to write

\displaystyle f_{n}(x)=\left(\psi_{n}\circ\phi_{n}^{-1}\right)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}a_{(i,j,t)}x^{p^{t}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{P}(\{1,\ldots,n\})}\left(a_{B}\prod_{j\in B}x^{p^{n-(n-j)}}\right)

where
\mathcal{P}(\cdot)
denotes the power set and
a_{B}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
for every
B\in\mathcal{P}(\{1,\ldots,n\})
. Recall from
Lemma

4.6

item

6
that
x^{p^{m+n}}=x^{p^{m}}
; thus there exists some
\ell\geq 1
for which

\deg(f_{n})\leq p^{n-1}+\ldots+p^{n-\ell}\leq(p-1)\left(p^{n-1}+\ldots+p^{n-\ell}\right)=p^{n}-p^{n-\ell}\qed

We now combine the field-theoretic tools above to prove the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
q\in(0,1/2)
and
|\Sigma|=p
for some prime
p
. Then
L_{q}\not\in\mathord{\bigoplus}\mathbf{SAC}^{0}
and
\Sigma^{*}\setminus L_{q}\not\in\mathord{\bigoplus}\mathbf{SAC}^{0}
.

Proof.

Suppose some circuit recognises
L_{q}
. By
Proposition

4.8
, there exists a family of polynomials
(f_{n})
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
for which
x\in L_{q}
if and only if
(f_{n}\circ\phi_{n})(x)\neq 0
and
\deg(f_{n})\leq p^{n}-p^{n-\ell}
. So, the number of roots of
f_{n}
—and, hence, the number of words not in
L_{q}
—is bounded above by
p^{n}-p^{n-\ell}
, so the cardinality of
L_{q}
is in
\Omega(p^{n})
, contradicting
Corollary

2.10
.

For
\Sigma^{*}\setminus L_{q}
, note that the circuit can be augmented by a constant number of layers to flip the output of
f_{n}\circ\phi_{n}
for any
n
(note that
\ran(f_{n}\circ\phi_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}
). If
a_{x}=(f_{n}\circ\phi_{n})(x)\neq 0
then use
Lemma

4.6

Item

6
to see that
a_{x}^{p}=a_{x}
; thus, we have
a_{x}^{p-1}=1
, and so the polynomial
\theta(x)=1-x^{p-1}
satisfies

\theta(x)=0\iff a_{x}\neq 0.

As
p
is fixed,
\theta
can be computed by a constant-depth circuit, which we may append to any
\mathord{\bigoplus}\mathbf{SAC}^{0}
-circuit recognising
L_{q}
to recognise
\Sigma^{*}\setminus L_{q}
. Since the former does not exist, neither does the latter.
∎

4.3.
Partial Generalisations to Non-prime-Cardinality Alphabets

We provide a partial generalisation to non-prime-alphabets. Although our theorem reaches the same conclusion as
Theorem

4.9
, the generalisation is partial as we redefine the definition of
\mathord{\bigoplus}\mathbf{SAC}^{0}
-recognisability to make our arguments amenable to non-prime cardinality settings.

Fix
q\in(0,1/2)
and an alphabet
\Sigma
with
|\Sigma|=r
, where
r
is not prime. Let
p>r
be the smallest prime greater than
r
. Let
\Sigma_{p}
be an alphabet of cardinality
p
which contains
\Sigma
. As before, identify
\Sigma_{p}
with
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. We now work over
\Sigma_{p}
.

Definition 4.10
.

A language
L\subset\Sigma_{r}^{*}
is
\mathord{\bigoplus}\mathbf{SAC}^{0}_{r}
-recognisable
if it is
\mathord{\bigoplus}\mathbf{SAC}^{0}
-recognisable over the field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
by a family of polynomials
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for which

f_{n}\colon\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p}

(as per
Proposition

4.8
) and for which the following conditions hold: for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(1)

f_{n}(x)=1
if
x\in\Sigma_{r}^{n}\cap L_{q}
;

(2)

f_{n}(x)=0
if
x\in\Sigma^{n}_{r}\setminus L_{q}
;

(3)

f_{n}(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\setminus\{1\}
otherwise.

We use this re-definition to code information about the language
\Sigma_{r}
as it is embedded in
\Sigma_{p}
. This renders
Definition

4.10
more restrictive than
Definition

4.4
, so the following theorem is slightly weaker than its counterpart
Theorem

4.9
; the proofs are similar.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
q\in(0,1/2)
and
|\Sigma|=r
for some
r\not\in\mathbb{P}
. Then
L_{q}\not\in\mathord{\bigoplus}\mathbf{SAC}^{0}_{r}
and
\Sigma^{*}\setminus L_{q}\not\in\mathord{\bigoplus}\mathbf{SAC}^{0}_{r}
.

5.
Open Questions

In this paper, we proved multiple results on the complexity of the measure
A_{Ne}
via the proxy family of sets
\{\,L_{q}\colon q\in(0,1/2)\,\}
. In particular, we showed that
L_{q}
is complicated from the viewpoint of pushdown automata (Theorems
3.12
and
3.13
and
Corollary

3.14
), and even certain Boolean circuits cannot recognise
L_{q}
, nor its complement (
Theorems

4.3
and
4.9
). We also proved the Shannon effect for
A_{Ne}{}
(
Theorem

2.13
). Pressing open questions pertain to refining these results on
L_{q}
—and, ultimately, to understanding the measure
A_{Ne}
even better.

In
Section

4.2
, and in
Theorem

4.9
in particular, we considered alphabets of prime cardinality and we gave a generalisation to non-prime-cardinality alphabets in
Section

4.3
. However, our proof uses a non-standard definition of
\mathord{\bigoplus}\mathbf{SAC}^{0}
. We wonder:

Question 5.1
.

Do the results from
Theorem

4.9
apply to arbitrary alphabets using the definition of
\mathord{\bigoplus}\mathbf{SAC}^{0}
given in
Definition

4.4
?

Finally, it is clear by definition that
A_{Ne}(x)\leq A_{N}(x)
for all
x\in\Sigma^{*}
, for any finite alphabet
\Sigma
. This allows the extension of certain results from
A_{Ne}
to
A_{N}
. For instance, we note here that
Theorems

3.11
,
3.13
and
4.3
also apply to
A_{N}
since
A_{Ne}(x)\leq A_{N}(x)
. However, whether the equality
A_{Ne}(x)=A_{N}(x)
holds in general remains the cardinal open question to fully understand the impact of exactness in
Definition

1.6
compared to
Definition

1.4
.

Question 5.2
.

Let
|\Sigma|=2
. Does there exist
x\in\Sigma^{*}
for which
A_{Ne}(x)<A_{N}(x)
?

Broadly, it is our hope that a better understanding of
A_{Ne}
, via proxies such as the
L_{q}
sets or otherwise, will lead to a better understanding of
A_{N}
, and (non-deterministic) automatic complexity in general. Similar to the comparison between regular languages being characterised by both NFAs and DFAs, a proof of the fact that
A_{N}=A_{Ne}
would simplify computing the non-deterministic automatic complexity while preserving its naturalness as a measure of complexity. Conversely,
A_{N}\neq A_{Ne}
would show that non-deterministic automatic complexity is dependent on the actual computation path, which would also be of philosophical interest.
