A direct protein bridge connects the ds and ds-ss junction telomeric DNA segments in C. elegans
Authors: Valerie M. Tesmer, Nils J. Lambacher, Io Yamamoto, Jayakrishnan Nandakumar, Hiroki Shibuya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The six-protein shelterin complex safeguards mammalian chromosome ends; however, its intricate protein-protein and protein-DNA interactions complicate biochemical dissection. The C. elegans DNA binding TEBP-1/2 (also known as DTN-1/2) and POT-1 proteins directly interact, offering a simplified system to dissect chromosome end protection. Here, we combined protein crystallography with in vivo functional analyses to elucidate the architecture and assembly of the C. elegans telomere-binding protein complex. We uncover a notable interface in which the canonical single-stranded DNA binding OB domain of POT-1 is repurposed to engage TEBP-1/2, rewiring telomeric protein connectivity. Disruption of the electrostatic interfaces between POT-1 and TEBP-1/2 disassembles the complex in vivo and causes telomere hyperelongation. POT-1 OB retains binding to the 5′-phosphorylated end of the telomeric ds-ssDNA junction, a role required for robust telomeric localization of POT-1 and suppression of telomere hyperelongation in vivo. Together, our findings support a model in which TEBP-1/2 recruit POT-1 to form a three-way TEBP-1/2–POT-1–DNA junction complex that ensures telomere length homeostasis.

INTRODUCTION

Eukaryotes protect the natural ends of their chromosomes from the DNA damage response machinery using specialized nucleoprotein structures called telomeres (1). Telomeric DNA consists of multiple repeats of a short sequence that is mostly double-stranded (ds), terminating in a short single-stranded (ss) G-rich overhang (Fig. 1A). A six-protein complex named shelterin binds mammalian telomeric DNA with high affinity and sequence specificity to protect natural chromosome ends from being recognized as dsDNA breaks by the DNA damage response machinery (Fig. 1A) (2). Shelterin proteins TRF1 and TRF2 protect the ds segment of telomeric DNA, whereas the POT1 protein binds the 3′ ss overhang and the 5′-phosphorylated dC (referred to as C hereon) nucleotide at the junction between the ds and ss segments (3–7). TIN2 serves as a structural scaffold that binds TRF1 and TRF2, connecting them to TPP1, the shelterin partner of POT1 (8–11). The sixth shelterin protein, Rap1, binds TRF2 to contribute to end protection (12, 13). Thus, a network of protein-protein interactions between dsDNA-binding and ssDNA-binding proteins establishes the assembly of mammalian shelterin, which remains a challenging target for in vitro reconstitution and structure determination.

Caenorhabditis elegans is a genetically amenable organism whose telomere biology not only shares similarities with mammals but also exhibits quirks whose impact is not fully understood (14). Its telomeric DNA is a few kilobases long, consisting of the repeat sequence GCTTAG, which differs only by one nucleotide from the human telomere repeat GGTTAG (Fig. 1, A and B) (15, 16). The 5′ end on the C-rich strand ends with a C in both organisms: TCC-5′ in C. elegans and ATC-5′ in humans (15, 17). Thus, the telomeric DNA is remarkably analogous in the two species, with C. elegans more similar to that of humans (15) than the yeast model organisms (18). One exception is the reported presence of a 5′-C-strand overhang at C. elegans telomeres (19). TEBP-1 and TEBP-2 (also known as DTN-1 and DTN-2, respectively) are the telomeric dsDNA-binding proteins in C. elegans (Fig. 1B) (20, 21). The domain structure of TEBP-1 and TEBP-2 is distinct among TRF1/TRF2 functional homologs characterized in various models, including budding and fission yeast. Whereas TRF1 and TRF2 homodimerize using a TRF homology (TRFH) domain and bind telomeric dsDNA using a C-terminal Myb domain, the TEBP proteins contain three Myb-containing domains (MCDs) and no discernible structural equivalent of the TRFH domain (Fig. 1C) (22, 23). We recently reported that the MCD1-MCD2 domains of TEBP-1 and TEBP-2 enable homodimerization and heterodimerization, and the MCD3 domain binds telomeric dsDNA in a sequence-specific manner (23). These studies highlighted how C. elegans and mammals use distinct structural means to achieve dimerization but show conserved mechanisms for DNA recognition at chromosome ends.

C. elegans contains four proteins with homology to human POT1: POT-1, POT-2, POT-3, and MRT-1 (19, 24–26). In contrast to human POT1, which uses two oligonucleotide/oligosaccharide-binding (OB) domains, OB1 and OB2, to bind DNA (6), each C. elegans homolog contains only one OB domain (24). The OB domain in C. elegans POT-1 is most closely related to the human POT1 OB1 and has been implicated in preferentially binding the C-rich 5′ overhang (19), whereas the OB domains in the other C. elegans homologs resemble OB2 and bind the ss G-rich telomeric DNA sequence (24). These observations suggest that the G-rich 3′ overhang-binding function of human POT1, which is shared between OB1 and OB2 domains, is split between the POT-1 homologs in C. elegans. Which, if any, of the C. elegans POT1 homologs protect the telomeric ds-ssDNA junction is not known. A novel interaction between TEBP-1/2 and POT-1 defines a direct contact between proteins that bind distinct parts of the telomeric DNA (20, 21). POT-1 binds the TEBP-1/2 proteins using its N-terminal TEBP-binding region (TBR), which contains an OB domain that extends into a region predicted to adopt a helical structure (Fig. 1C). The POT-1–binding region (PBR) of TEBP-1 and TEBP-2 is located at their C termini, downstream of their MCD modules. Such a direct interaction is consistent with the lack of a bridging protein like TIN2 at C. elegans telomeres, providing a potentially simpler architecture for chromosome end protection. Structurally characterizing the POT-1–TEBP-1/2 interface will improve our understanding of how coordinated interactions between different regions of the telomere in C. elegans contribute to chromosome end protection, and how these mechanisms are evolutionarily conserved or distinct from those in other eukaryotes.

The modular nature of the POT1 homologs, the apparent lack of a need for bridging and scaffolding proteins to connect ds and ssDNA regions, the biochemical amenability of its telomeric proteins, and the genetic tractability of C. elegans make this model organism ideally suited to dissect the structure and end protection functions of telomere-binding proteins. Here, we solved the high-resolution structure of the POT-1–TEBP-1 protein interface to reveal how the POT-1 OB domain has been adapted to bind its partner TEBP-1/2 through the same surface that binds ssDNA in other homologs, including human POT1. This structure provides the molecular basis for the direct bridging of distinct DNA binding activities within the telomere-binding protein complex. We show that binding to TEBP is essential for the stability of POT-1, its localization to telomeres, and maintenance of telomere length homeostasis in vivo. We found that POT-1 protects the 5′-phosphorylated C nucleotide at the telomeric ds-ss junction in C. elegans, using a structural mechanism indistinguishable from human POT1, highlighting the conservation of the underlying mechanism among diverse eukaryotes.

RESULTS

POT-1 and TEBP-1/2 form a complex critical for POT-1 stability

To understand how C. elegans telomeric dsDNA-binding proteins TEBP-1 and TEBP-2 bind POT-1, we designed protein constructs based on our previous yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) analysis of these proteins (21). Y2H mapped the PBR of TEBP-1 and TEBP-2 to a highly conserved region encompassing their C termini (Fig. 1C) (21). Similarly, the TBR of POT-1 was defined within the N terminus (21), a region that AlphaFold predicts to encompass an OB domain and a segment of a long α helix referred to as αC (Fig. 1C) (27).

His10-SUMO-TEBP-1PBR and His10-SUMO-TEBP-2PBR variants could be expressed and purified without the need for coexpression of POT-1TBR (Fig. 1D and fig. S1A). After Ni-NTA purification and removal of the His10-SUMO tag by Ulp1 protease, the TEBP-1/2PBR variants eluted as a single peak with a molecular weight consistent with the molar mass of a monomeric protein, as determined by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) coupled with multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS; Fig. 1D and fig. S1A). In contrast, the recombinant expression of POT-1TBR variants in Escherichia coli was dependent on coexpression of either TEBP-1PBR or TEBP-2PBR variants, consistent with binding of TEBP-1/2 stabilizing POT-1. SEC-MALS and SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis of the coexpressed and purified complexes demonstrated that they exist as a 1:1 heterodimer of the POT-1 and TEBP-1/2 components (Fig. 1E and fig. S1B). Unexpectedly, these complexes eluted later than TEBP-1PBR or TEBP-2PBR alone from the size exclusion column, suggesting that the heteroduplexes adapt a more compact structure (compare Fig. 1, E with D; fig. S1, B with A). In summary, purified C-terminal PBR domains of TEBP-1 and TEBP-2 form a 1:1 heterodimer with the N-terminal TBR domain of POT-1.

POT-1 depends on TEBP-1 and TEBP-2 to localize in vivo

To evaluate the importance of TEBP-1 and TEBP-2 in the in vivo localization of POT-1, we used a gfp::3×flag::pot-1 strain generated in our previous study, in which a GFP::3×FLAG dual tag was inserted at the endogenous pot-1 locus using CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing (21). Fluorescence microscopy of germline cells revealed bright green fluorescent protein (GFP) puncta within the nuclei, consistent with the localization of the tagged POT-1 protein at telomeres (Fig. 1F). To assess the contribution of each TEBP protein to POT-1 localization, we crossed the gfp::3×flag::pot-1 strain with tebp-1Δ and tebp-2Δ mutants. Significant reduction of GFP::3×FLAG::POT-1 foci intensity was observed in both tebp-1Δ and tebp-2Δ strains, reaching ∼78 and ∼30% of wild-type (WT) levels, respectively (Fig. 1, F and G), consistent with their direct interaction with POT-1.

We next asked whether simultaneous deletion of tebp-1 and tebp-2 would lead to a loss of GFP::3×FLAG::POT-1 localization. Because tebp-1Δ; tebp-2Δ double knockout animals cannot be maintained over successive generations in the laboratory due to their severe sterility accompanied by aberrant activation of the DNA damage response (21, 23), we used a genetic balancer strain, tebp-1Δ/tebp-1Δ; nT1[qIs51]/tebp-2Δ, in which the tebp-2Δ allele is maintained in a heterozygous state in the tebp-1 null background. By crossing this balancer line with the gfp::3×flag::pot-1 animal followed by selfing of the progeny, we obtained first-generation animals that were homozygous for both tebp-1Δ and tebp-2Δ and also carried gfp::3×flag::pot-1 as a homozygous allele. Notably, these double mutants displayed a loss of punctate GFP::3×FLAG::POT-1 signals in germline nuclei (Fig. 1, F and G). The loss of POT-1 signals is likely due not only to mislocalization but also to destabilization of the POT-1 protein in the absence of TEBP-1/2 binding (as described later). These findings suggest that TEBP-1 and TEBP-2 function redundantly to ensure the proper telomere localization of POT-1 in vivo and support the model that POT-1 requires interaction with TEBP proteins for its telomeric association and biochemical stability, as also observed in vitro.

Structural basis of the C. elegans POT-1–TEBP-1 interaction

To determine the structure of the POT-1TBR + TEBP-1PBR complex (Fig. 1E), we obtained high-quality crystals of the complex. Selenomethionine (SeMet)–substituted complex yielded the most strongly diffracting crystals, allowing us to solve a 1.48-Å resolution structure using a combination of molecular replacement [MR; using an AlphaFold structural prediction for POT-1 OB domain amino acids 1 to 128 as a search model (27)] and single-wavelength anomalous diffraction (SAD) (28) to obtain phase information (Materials and Methods and table S1).

As anticipated, POT-1TBR consists of an N-terminal OB domain connected to the N-terminal portion of the αC helix (amino acids 140 to 171) using a short, unresolved basic linker (131SRRKSGR137; Fig. 2, A and B). Although the amino acid sequence of the C. elegans POT-1 OB domain is only 22% identical to the first OB domain of human POT1 (Fig. 2B), their overall three-dimensional (3D) folds are highly similar, with a root mean square deviation of 2.1 Å (99 to 99 Cα atoms; fig. S2, A to C). TEBP-1PBR consists of five α helices (amino acids 729 to 837) that form two separate bundles (α1 + α2 and α3 + α4+ α5) (Fig. 2A). These bundles interdigitate with the POT-1 OB domain and αC helix to form an extensive (POT-1OB)–(TEBP-1α1 + α2)–(POT-1 αC)–(TEBP-1α3 + α4 + α5) heterodimeric interface that buries 4200 Å2 of surface area, with 1300-Å2 contact surface with the OB domain and 2900 Å2 with the αC helix of POT-1. The interaction surface comprises both electrostatic (Fig. 2, C and D) and hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 2, E and F). Key to the electrostatic contacts are five salt bridges between POT-1 and TEBP-1 (Fig. 2, C and D). Two salt bridges involve the POT-1 OB domain: POT-1 K39 with TEBP-1 D742 (or TEBP-2 D721) and a bipartite salt bridge of POT-1 R62 with TEBP-1 D746 and E750 (or TEBP-2 D725 and D729) (Fig. 2C). The other three salt bridges involve the POT-1 αC helix: POT-1 R152, E153, and K167 with TEBP-1 E812, R820, and E806 (or TEBP-2 E791, R799, and E785), respectively (Fig. 2D). The interdigitation of POT-1 and TEBP-1 is further corroborated by hydrophobic interactions involving several POT-1 and TEBP-1 residues (Fig. 2, E and F).

Sequence alignment of the human POT1 OB1 domain and the C. elegans POT-1 OB domain revealed poor conservation of the human POT1 ssDNA-binding and C. elegans POT-1 salt bridge-forming residues (Fig. 2B). Overlay of the two orthologous OB domains revealed a partial overlap of the ssDNA bound to human POT1 with the TEBP-1PBR region bound to POT-1 (fig. S2C). Equivalents of POT-1–binding residues, including those involved in salt bridge formation in TEBP-1, are conserved in TEBP-2, implying that both TEBP proteins use the same structural mechanism to bind POT-1 (Fig. 2G). These observations are consistent with the ssDNA-binding surface of the POT-1 OB domain being repurposed to bind its partner proteins, TEBP-1 and TEBP-2. The four human POT1 POT-hole residues (7) are fully conserved in C. elegans POT-1, suggesting that the latter may display 5′-phosphorylated telomeric ds-ssDNA junction binding activity (Fig. 2B and fig. S2, D and E; see later). Overall, the POT-1–TEBP-1 interface described here represents a structural module that has not previously been observed.

TEBP-1/2 PBRs redundantly stabilize and localize POT-1 in vivo

Stabilization of POT-1TBR by TEBP-1PBR was used as a proxy to validate their structural interface in vitro. To disrupt this interface, we introduced single and double mutations designed to perturb key interactions. Specifically, hydrophobic side chains were replaced with glutamic acid to disrupt hydrophobic packing, and charged residues involved in salt bridges were substituted with oppositely charged residues (e.g., D or E to R and R to E). Variants of His10-SUMO-TEBP-1PBR and SUMO-POT-1TBR were coexpressed in E. coli, combining WT protein on one side with an interface mutant on the other. Seven POT-1 mutants (I25E, F26E, L37E, R62E, M144E-L148E, R152E-E153R, and F156E-F160E) and two TEBP-1 mutants (D746R-E750R and E812R-R820E) were analyzed. Since TEBP-1PBR is stable on its own, we expected all His10-SUMO-TEBP-1PBR variants to be recovered by Ni-NTA purification, regardless of POT-1 coexpression. In contrast, POT-1TBR requires TEBP-1 binding for stabilization; thus, only POT-1 variants that retained the ability to bind TEBP-1 were expected to copurify. Fully consistent with our structural data and expectations, copurification of SUMO-POT-1 was evident only when both POT-1TBR and TEBP-1PBR were WT (Fig. 3A).

To investigate the physiological significance of the TEBP-1/2–POT-1 interaction and its molecular interface in vivo, we introduced charge-reversal mutations in the endogenous tebp-1 and tebp-2 loci (E812R-R820E and E791R-R799E, respectively), designed to disrupt predicted salt bridge interactions with POT-1 within their PBR domains using CRISPR-Cas9. These mutations did not alter the protein expression levels (Fig. 3, B and C) or subcellular localization at telomeres (Fig. 3, D and E), as assessed by immunoblotting and immunostaining using our anti–TEBP-1 and anti–TEBP-2 antibodies. These findings indicate that the stability and localization of TEBP-1/2 are independent of their interaction with POT-1, in agreement with the in vitro data.

Neither the single (tebp-1 or tebp-2) nor the double (tebp-1 and tebp-2) PBR mutants exhibited fertility defects, producing brood sizes comparable to those of WT animals (Fig. 3F). This contrasts sharply with the severe sterility observed in tebp-1Δ; tebp-2Δ double mutants (21) or in animals carrying double DNA binding mutations in MCD3 that abolish the telomeric localization of TEBP-1/2 (23), suggesting that the TEBP-1 and TEBP-2 PBR mutants retain essential functions to support germline immortality and suppress the DNA damage response machinery.

We next examined the impact of these mutations on POT-1 localization by introducing the gfp::3×flag::pot-1 allele into the tebp-1/2 PBR mutant backgrounds through genetic crosses. GFP::3×FLAG::POT-1 foci were moderately reduced in the tebp-1(E812R-R820E) mutant (∼83.2% of WT levels) and more substantially reduced in the tebp-2(E791R-R799E) mutant (∼39.8%), whereas they were abolished in the double mutant (Fig. 3G). Notably, a similar trend was observed in the corresponding tebp-1Δ, tebp-2Δ, and double knockout mutants (Fig. 1F), indicating that PBR disruption largely phenocopies loss of the respective proteins in terms of POT-1 localization.

To determine whether the loss of signal in the double mutant was due to POT-1 mislocalization or reduced protein expression, we performed immunoblotting on whole-worm lysates. A GFP::3×FLAG-tagged POT-1 band was readily detected in WT and single mutants using an anti-FLAG antibody but was markedly reduced in the double mutant (Fig. 3H), indicating that POT-1 protein is destabilized in the absence of both TEBP interactions. Together with prior results showing loss of POT-1 localization in tebp-1Δ; tebp-2Δ double mutants (Fig. 1F), these data demonstrate that targeted disruption of the PBR domains of TEBP-1 and TEBP-2 is sufficient to abrogate POT-1 stability in vivo. These findings provide in vivo validation of the electrostatic interaction interface characterized in vitro by crystallography (Fig. 2).

POT-1 is crucial for maintaining telomere length homeostasis, and its depletion leads to hyperelongation of telomeric DNA due to accelerated activity of the telomere extension enzyme telomerase (19, 25). To determine whether the PBR mutations phenocopy pot-1 deletion, we performed telomere restriction fragment (TRF) length analysis. Single mutants tebp-1(E812R-R820E) and tebp-2(E791R-R799E) exhibited telomere lengths comparable to WT (Fig. 3I). However, the double PBR mutant showed hyperelongation of telomeric DNA similar to the pot-1Δ strain (Fig. 3I). Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) in the nuclei of these C. elegans mutants using a (TTAGGC)3 telomeric probe recapitulated these defects (Fig. 3J). These data suggest that the tebp-1/2 PBR mutants represent separation-of-function mutations that specifically disrupt POT-1 function at telomeres and lead to telomere hyperelongation, phenocopying the pot-1 mutant while retaining TEBP-1/2 function in maintaining germline immortality.

POT-1 TBR stabilizes POT-1 and directs it to telomeres

To assess the in vivo function of the POT-1 TBR, we introduced charge-reversal mutations (R152E-E153R), designed to disrupt the salt bridge interactions with TEBP-1/2, by editing the endogenously tagged gfp::3×flag::pot-1 strain using CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing. Fluorescence imaging of this mutant strain revealed a pronounced loss of telomeric foci compared to WT gfp::3×flag::pot-1 animals (Fig. 4A), consistent with the notion that POT-1 localization to telomeres depends on its interaction with TEBP-1/2, further validating our crystallographic analysis and biochemical findings.

To determine whether this mutation abolishes physical binding to TEBP-1/2, we enriched the mutant POT-1 protein from C. elegans extract by immunoprecipitation and assessed the coimmunoprecipitation of TEBP-1/2. As expected, WT GFP::3×FLAG::POT-1 efficiently coprecipitated endogenous TEBP-1 and TEBP-2; however, this interaction was lost in the POT-1(R152E-E153R) mutant (Fig. 4B, top). The overall anti-FLAG signal for the mutant POT-1 protein was weaker in both input and immunoprecipitated fractions, raising the possibility that the loss of detectable coprecipitation of TEBP-1/2 may be attributed to reduced protein levels of POT-1 rather than a loss of binding to TEBP-1/2. To exclude this possibility, we repeated the immunoblotting by loading four times more immunoprecipitate from the mutant sample POT-1(R152E-E153R) to match the bait band intensity with that of the WT POT-1. Even under these normalized conditions, no detectable TEBP-1 or TEBP-2 was coimmunoprecipitated with mutant POT-1 (Fig. 4B, bottom), strongly suggesting that the mutant POT-1(R152E-E153R) is incapable of interacting with TEBP-1/2 in vivo. Together, these results validate the physiological importance of the TBR interface identified by our crystal structure and demonstrate that POT-1 binding to TEBP-1/2 through TBR is essential for its stability and telomeric localization in vivo.

Last, we performed TRF length analysis to phenotypically characterize the POT-1 TBR mutation in vivo. Consistent with a previous study (25), N-terminal tagging of POT-1 partially impaired its function, and thus gfp::3×flag::pot-1 animals exhibited slightly longer telomeres than WT (Fig. 4C). Notably, introduction of the TBR mutation resulted in more pronounced telomere hyperelongation, comparable to that observed in the pot-1Δ strain (Fig. 4C). These defects were further confirmed by FISH using a (TTAGGC)3 telomeric probe (Fig. 4D). Together, these results suggest that the TBR mutation largely phenocopies pot-1 deletion, consistent with the loss of its telomeric localization, and indicate that the essential function of POT-1 is mediated through its interaction with TEBP proteins at telomeres.

C. elegans POT-1 binds to the ds-ss telomeric junction

C. elegans POT-1 was previously described as preferentially recognizing multimers of the 5′ C-strand telomeric repeat over the 3′ G-strand overhangs typical of telomeres in other organisms, serving as an exception among all known POT1 homologs (19). Our attempts to further characterize the ssDNA-binding properties led to confusing results: POT-1 OB domain bound both the C-strand (CTAAGC)5 and G-strand (GGCTTA)5, as well as the scrambled C-strand (ACTGAC)5 but did not bind shorter G-strand repeats (fig. S3, A and B). To obtain further insights, we performed electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) experiments to compare the ssDNA-binding properties of three C. elegans POT1 paralogs (POT-1, POT-2, and POT-3) using short oligonucleotides containing only two telomeric G-rich or C-rich repeats (fig. S3, C and D). As expected, POT-2 and POT-3 bound specifically to two repeats of G-rich but not C-rich ssDNA (29). In contrast, neither the POT-1 OB domain nor POT-1TBR + TEBP-1PBR bound either substrate (fig. S3D). These observations suggest that the length or secondary structure (such as a DNA hairpin), rather than the sequence of the ssDNA, dictates binding to POT-1 OB.

We recently uncovered that human POT1 specifically binds to the ds-ss telomeric junction to ward off the cellular DNA damage response machinery (7). The phosphorylated 5′-C of the conserved terminal ATC-5′ at the human ds-ss junction is bound by four human POT1 residues (namely, Y9, R80, H82, and R83), creating a binding surface named the POT-hole (7). All these residues are conserved in C. elegans POT-1 (Fig. 2B), and they align structurally with their human counterparts (fig. S2, A to E). This conservation contrasts sharply with the lack of conservation in C. elegans residues that bind ssDNA in human POT1 (Fig. 2B), as well as with the steric clash between the ssDNA bound by human POT1 and the C. elegans POT-1 interface with TEBP-1 (fig. S2C). C. elegans POT-1 binding to a 5′-phosphorylated C nucleotide at the ds-ss junction would also be compatible with the natural TCC 5′ end of C. elegans chromosomes (15). We confirmed the presence of a 5′-phosphorylated TCC-5′ at the telomeric ds/ss junction in C. elegans by adapting the human C-strand single telomere length analysis (STELA) technique that relies on a 5′ phosphate for ligating to a 3′ hydroxyl-ending oligonucleotide with the correct permutation of the telomeric repeat (fig. S2, F and G) (17). In conclusion, both human and C. elegans chromosomes terminate in a phosphorylated 5′-C and have POT1 homologs containing a POT-hole.

On the basis of these observations, we hypothesized that C. elegans POT-1 binds the telomeric ds-ssDNA junction but not the ss overhang. We used four dsDNA substrates that contained an upstream 5 bp tether and 4 to 5 bp of ds telomeric sequence derived from the ds-ss junction. A top 5′ 32P-labeled G-strand was annealed to a complementary C-strand to create a blunt-ended substrate [BLUNT(−4 to 0)], an 8-nt 3′ overhang (3′-8nt), an 8-nt 5′ overhang (5′-8nt), or a single-nt 5′ overhang (5′-1nt) (Fig. 5A). POT-1TBR + TEBP-1PBR bound the blunt-end substrate [dissociation constant (Kd) 600 ± 110 nM] in a manner that was dependent on the phosphorylation of the 5′-C (Fig. 5, A and B), as reported for human POT1 (7). Similar binding profiles were observed for POT-1TBR + TEBP-2PBR (Fig. 5C) and the POT-1 OB domain alone (fig. S3E), confirming that the activity was derived from the OB domain of POT-1. The 3′-8nt substrate with a phosphorylated 5′-C bound POT-1TBR + TEBP-1PBR or POT-1 OB with similar affinity relative to the phosphorylated blunt-ended substrate, suggesting that the 3′ ssDNA overhang does not provide further binding affinity to POT-1 bound to a 5′-phosphorylated end (Fig. 5, A and D, and fig. S3F). In contrast, a ds-ssDNA with an 8-nt 5′ overhang bound much more weakly than its 5′-phosphorylated blunt-ended counterpart to POT-1TBR + TEBP-1PBR (Fig. 5E). Even a single nt 5′ overhang reduced binding to POT-1TBR + TEBP-1PBR relative to blunt-ended or 3′ overhang DNA, suggesting that POT-1 is not effective in protecting the ds-ss junction of the proposed 5′ overhangs at C. elegans chromosome ends (compare Fig. 5, F to B and D). However, the C. elegans POT-1 binding data are consistent with structural data for how human POT1 recognizes the 5′-phosphorylated C in the context of a G-C base pair at the telomeric ds-ss junction (7). Given the strict conservation of POT-hole residues between human POT1 and C. elegans POT-1, we reason that a ss 5′ terminus is unlikely to represent an optimal substrate for POT-1. Instead, we propose that C. elegans POT-1 binds the phosphorylated 5′-C at the telomeric ds-ss junction in vitro without the need to engage the adjacent ssDNA. In light of the recent finding that C. elegans telomerase RNA, like that of other species, contains a template complementary to the 3′ G-strand overhang (30), the previous claim that C. elegans telomeres uniquely maintain constitutive 5′ C-strand overhangs (19) may require reevaluation.

POT-1 R69 is critical for junction binding and telomere maintenance

POT-1 R69 is the structural equivalent of the human POT-hole linchpin residue R83, which binds the phosphorylated 5′-C at the ds-ss junction, and charge-reversal mutation of which (to E) elicits a robust telomeric DNA damage response in cultured cells (7). As C. elegans POT-1 binding also depends on the phosphorylated 5′-C at the ds-ss junction (Fig. 5 and fig. S3, D to F), we predicted that the POT-hole mutant R69E would lose specificity for the phosphorylated versus hydroxylated 5′ end. Consistent with our hypothesis, a purified complex of POT-1(R69E)TBR + TEBP-1PBR failed to bind strongly to the blunt-ended junction regardless of the presence of a 5′-terminal phosphate (Fig. 6A). These results demonstrate a conserved structural mechanism used by both human POT1 and C. elegans POT-1 to protect the 5′-phosphorylated end at the telomeric ds-ss junction.

To evaluate the significance of POT-1’s protection of the ds-ss junction in telomere maintenance, we introduced the R69E mutation using CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing in the context of the gfp::3×flag::pot-1 background. In contrast to the POT-1(R152E-E153R) mutant, which was poorly expressed due to weakened interaction with TEBP-1 and TEBP-2 (Fig. 4B), the POT-1(R69E) mutant was expressed at WT levels (Fig. 6B). GFP fluorescence was used to visualize the WT and mutant proteins in vivo. Notably, the GFP signal was reduced in both germline (76% of WT; Fig. 6, C and D) and somatic cells (57% of WT; Fig. 6, E and F), suggesting a moderate telomere recruitment defect for the R69E mutant despite normal cellular abundance (Fig. 6B).

In mammals, the POT-hole of POT1 is essential for protecting telomeric DNA ends from aberrant activation of the DNA damage response (7). To investigate whether the POT-hole in C. elegans POT-1 serves a similar function in suppressing DNA damage response activation, we examined the immunostaining signal of phosphorylated SQ/TQ motifs (phospho-SQ/TQ), which are widely recognized as markers of ATM/ATR-dependent DNA damage response signaling (31). In our previous studies, we showed that tebp-1Δ; tebp-2Δ double mutants, as well as animals carrying double MCD domain mutations that abolish telomeric localization of TEBP-1/2, exhibit increased phospho-SQ/TQ signals in germline nuclei—indicating activation of telomeric DNA damage response (23). On the basis of these findings, we hypothesized that POT-1 mutants might also show the similar elevation of phospho-SQ/TQ signals in germline nuclei. Unexpectedly, however, the POT-hole mutant (harboring the R69E substitution in the untagged endogenous pot-1 allele) did not show detectable accumulation of phospho-SQ/TQ signal in the germ line (fig. S4A). Notably, pot-1Δ animals likewise failed to show detectable phospho-SQ/TQ accumulation (fig. S4A), suggesting that POT-1 itself is not essential for suppressing DNA damage signaling in the C. elegans germ line under our experimental conditions.

To further assess whether DNA damage signaling is engaged in these mutants, we monitored the localization of the RAD9-RAD1-HUS1 (9-1-1) complex, a key component of the DNA repair pathway engaged at the ssDNA and dsDNA junction, using a previously established endogenously tagged HUS-1::mNeonGreen reporter strain (32). As expected, ultraviolet (UV) irradiation in WT animals led to robust formation of HUS-1::mNeonGreen foci in germline nuclei, confirming the sensitivity of the reporter for marking DNA repair foci. In contrast, no HUS-1::mNeonGreen foci were observed in either pot-1Δ or POT-hole mutant (R69E) animals (fig. S4B). These data strongly suggest that, unlike in mammals, C. elegans POT-1 is not essential for suppressing DNA damage signaling in the germ line. POT-1’s function in the DNA damage response suppression may be redundant with other POT proteins or is unmasked under certain physiologically relevant stress conditions not recapitulated in a laboratory setting, highlighting possible divergence in the telomere-protective mechanisms between mammals and nematodes.

To evaluate additional physiological consequences of the POT-1 R69E mutation, we performed TRF length analysis, comparing WT, pot-1Δ, and pot-1(R69E) mutants. The pot-1(R69E) mutant animals displayed telomeres that were moderately elongated relative to WT but significantly shorter than those observed in pot-1Δ animals (Fig. 6G). To determine whether the intermediate telomere length observed in the R69E mutant represents a terminally elongated steady state or a transitional stage of ongoing elongation, we tracked TRF profiles over 10 successive generations (from F29 to F39). Telomere length remained unchanged throughout this period, indicating that the results represent the steady-state phenotype and the R69E mutation induces a milder telomere elongation than that seen in pot-1Δ animals (Fig. 6G). This intermediate phenotype aligns with the observation that the telomeric localization of POT-1 R69E protein is detectable but significantly diminished compared to the WT protein, resulting in partial impairment of POT-1 function. Together, these results support the notion that C. elegans POT-1 requires its POT-hole region to stably localize to the telomeric ds-ss junction to contribute to telomere length homeostasis by suppressing telomere overextension (Fig. 6H).

DISCUSSION

Mammalian shelterin displays three DNA binding activities (binding to dsDNA, ssDNA, and the ds-ss junction) distributed over three proteins (namely, TRF1 and TRF2 to dsDNA, POT1 to ssDNA, and the junction) that are bridged together to form a six-membered complex that guards chromosome ends. Although the hierarchical recruitment and intersubunit interactions of mammalian shelterin components have been firmly established, studying the coordination of these activities at a molecular level remains a challenge. The simpler composition of the C. elegans telomere-binding protein complex provides a fresh opportunity to understand its assembly and function in vitro and also in the whole organismal context. Here, we reveal strict conservation of end protection strategies and unique adaptations in C. elegans that provide mechanistic insights into recruitment and assembly of the telomere-binding protein complex. The POT-1–TEBP-1/2 interaction characterized in this study reveals direct bridging of proteins that bind different segments of telomeric DNA (Fig. 7). Our biochemical and cellular localization results revealed that POT-1 stability is contingent upon binding to TEBP-1 or TEBP-2. This could ensure that POT-1 only localizes to telomeres, preventing its recruitment to other ds-ssDNA junctions in the cell produced during DNA replication or repair. These observations are reminiscent of studies in mammalian cells that reveal the importance of TPP1 in recruiting and stabilizing mammalian POT1 at telomeres. However, a major difference between C. elegans POT-1–TEBP-1/2 and analogous complexes in other systems, including mammals and fission yeast, is the absence of bridging proteins that act as a scaffold to assemble different DNA binding subunits at the telomeres (33). Mammalian TRF1 and TRF2 connect to POT1 using protein-protein interactions mediated by TIN2 and TPP1 (8, 34). Similarly, Schizosaccharomyces pombe Taz1 (telomeric dsDNA-binding protein) links to Pot1 with the help of Rap1, Poz1, and Tpz1 (35). These bridging proteins may provide structural flexibility for regulating end protection versus end replication. The absence of the equivalents of these additional shelterin partners in C. elegans suggests that these functions can be performed without additional bridging subunits (Fig. 7). Perhaps the ∼250 amino acid unstructured region between the DNA binding and PBRs of TEBP-1 and TEBP-2 (Fig. 1C) could provide the required flexibility between the DNA binding modules of the C. elegans telomere-binding protein complex (20, 21).

The POT-1TBR-TEBP-1PBR crystal structure represents a distinct protein-protein interface that has not been captured in other telomere-binding protein complexes from other organisms. Within the complex, the TEBP-1PBR folds into two helical bundles containing two and three helices, respectively. The large hydrodynamic radius of TEBP-1/2PBR inferred from its earlier elution from a SEC column than the POT-1PBR–TEBP-1/2PBR complex indicates that the TEBP-1/2PBR helices exist in an elongated apo conformation that compacts upon POT-1 binding. However, the similar stability and telomeric accumulation of TEBP-1PBR (or TEBP-2PBR) in the presence or absence of POT-1 suggest that the elongated conformation is well folded. This may be important since multiple copies of TEBP-1 and TEBP-2 are needed to coat the telomeric dsDNA, but only one molecule of POT-1 is sufficient to bind the ds-ss junction at a chromosome end. Because a majority of TEBP molecules may not be bound to POT-1, a well-folded apo conformation would help maintain the stability of the PBR.

The POT-1 portion of the POT-1–TEBP-1 interface represents a remarkable adaptation of the classical OB domain. Named as such because of its well-known ability to bind oligonucleotides (ssDNA) (36), the OB domain of human POT1 was recently shown to also bind the 5′-phosphorylated end of a telomeric ds-ssDNA junction using a surface called the POT-hole (7). Thus, the human POT1 OB1 domain has dedicated binding sites for both ssDNA (4, 37) and ds-ssDNA junctions. We show here that the C. elegans POT-1 OB domain binds the 5′-phosphorylated end of a blunt-ended duplex DNA or a ds-ssDNA junction with a 3′ overhang using a POT-hole that is structurally analogous to the human POT1 POT-hole. Notably, part of the surface used by human POT1 OB1 to bind ssDNA binds TEBP-1 in C. elegans POT-1. These observations qualify POT-1 OB as a unique OB domain that has mutually exclusive binding sites for a 5′-phosphorylated ds-ssDNA junction and a protein partner. Given that OB domains in other unrelated proteins, specifically DNA ligases, bind B-form duplex DNA (38–40), the OB domain qualifies as a robust structural scaffold that can bind various types of macromolecules, including ssDNA, dsDNA, ds-ssDNA junctions, G-quadruplexes, RNA, and proteins.

The cellular localization studies presented here revealed an unexpected dependence of POT-hole residue R69 on the accumulation of POT-1 at telomeres despite an intact interaction with TEBP-1 (or TEBP-2). The POT-1 R69E mutation reduced POT-1 signal at telomeres and promoted telomere elongation, consistent with reduced POT-1 protein at telomeres. We propose that the tethering of POT-1 to TEBP-1 (or TEBP-2) recruits it to telomeres, allowing this complex to latch on to the telomeric ds-ssDNA junction to form a robust three-way complex (Fig. 6H). Disruption of its interaction with TEBP destabilizes POT-1, resulting in its eviction from the telomeres (and the cell). By comparison, disruption of its POT-hole weakens the three-way complex with the ds-ssDNA junction and increases the propensity of POT-1 to dissociate from telomeres.

Our studies provide a detailed structural model for the recruitment of C. elegans telomere-binding proteins to the telomeric ds-ssDNA junction, highlighting evolutionarily conserved themes and nematode-specific adaptations for chromosome end protection. Future studies will investigate how the other telomere-binding proteins, comprising POT-2, POT-3, and MRT-1, are recruited to and protect the G-rich overhang at the 3′ end of the chromosome. In mammals, the bridging protein TPP1 plays a critical role by directly binding telomerase, facilitating its recruitment and promoting telomere elongation (41). Since C. elegans lacks any bridging proteins, including a TPP1 homolog, it will be of great interest to investigate the mechanisms underlying telomerase recruitment to C. elegans telomeres. Given that POT-1 and POT-2 are not required for telomerase-dependent telomere extension but rather act as negative regulators of telomerase (25), it is plausible that the telomerase-binding interface resides within the N-terminal sequences of TEBP-1/2 or involves other POT family proteins such as POT-3 or MRT-1. Defining the entire complex assembly pathway will help answer how the telomere-binding protein complex and its subcomplexes coordinate end protection with end replication by telomerase and the C-rich strand fill-in machinery.

Plasmid constructs and cloning

Constructs for protein expression in E. coli were derived from pMAL-c5X TEBP-1 and pMAL-c5X TEBP-2 FL (21), pMAL-c5X POT-1 FL, pET28bSmt3 [MTA with Cornell University (42)], and pETDuet-1 (Novagen). To purify TEBP-2PBR from E. coli, His10-SUMO–tagged TEBP-2PBR was expressed from pET28bSmt3-TEBP-2PBR, in which the region encoding TEBP-2 amino acids 697 to 818 was amplified from pMAL-c5X TEBP-2 and cloned into the BamHI/XhoI sites of pET28bSmt3. To purify the POT-1 OB domain from E. coli, His10-SUMO–tagged POT-1 OB was expressed from pET28bSmt3-POT-1OB, in which the region encoding POT-1 amino acids 1 to 135 was amplified from pMAL-c5X POT-1 FL and cloned into the BamHI/XhoI sites of pET28bSmt3.

Given that POT-1 was stabilized by TEBP-1 and TEBP-2, we generated a series of constructs for the coexpression of POT-1–TEBP-1/2 complexes in E. coli. For SEC-MALS analysis of the POT-11–187 + TEBP-2PBR complex, His10-SUMO–tagged POT-11–187 + SUMO-tagged TEBP-2PBR were coexpressed from pETDuet-His10Smt3-POT-11–187 + Smt3-TEBP-2PBR. For crystallization and EMSA analysis of the POT-1TBR/TEBP-1PBR complex, His10-SUMO–tagged POT-1TBR + SUMO-tagged TEBP-1PBR were coexpressed from pETDuet-His10Smt3-POT-1TBR + Smt3-TEBP-1PBR. In this construct, the region encoding Smt3-TEBP-1PBR was excised with NdeI and XhoI from pET28bSmt3-TEBP-1PBR and cloned into the corresponding sites of pETDuet. To validate the POT-1TBR/TEBP-1PBR complex interface, His10-SUMO–tagged TEBP-1PBR + SUMO-tagged POT-1TBR were coexpressed from pETDuet-His10Smt3-TEBP-1PBR + Smt3-POT-1TBR. Protein expression and purification

A similar protein expression and purification protocol was used for a variety of constructs. Plasmids were transformed into BL21(DE3) E. coli for overnight expression at 25°C (or 20°C for the POT-1 OB domain) in LB with 100 μM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside. Clarified cellular lysates were prepared by sonication in 25 mM tris (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 0.1 mM EDTA, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and cOmplete mini EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) followed by centrifugation. His-tagged proteins were bound to Ni-NTA agarose (QIAGEN), and the resin was extensively washed, typically with 25 mM tris (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol, with a final wash step that included 10 mM imidazole (pH 8.0) and 300 mM NaCl. For the POT-1 OB domain, the NaCl concentration was kept at 300 to 500 mM through the wash steps. Protein was subsequently processed on the basis of its size. For larger proteins, the protein was eluted from Ni-NTA with 300 mM imidazole (pH 8.0), and the SUMO-tag was cleaved with Ulp1 protease. Smaller proteins or complexes were cleaved directly off the beads with Ulp1 protease. Proteins were further purified by SEC on Superdex 75 (POT-1 OB, TEBP-1 PBR, TEBP-2PBR, and complexes of POT-1TBR + TEBPPBR) or Superdex 200 columns (GE Healthcare) in 20 to 25 mM tris (pH 8.0), 100 to 150 mM NaCl (or 500 mM NaCl for POT-1 OB), and 2 mM dithiothreitol (DTT). Protein quality was assessed by SDS-PAGE using Mini-PROTEAN TGX precast gels (Bio-Rad) that were run in SDS-containing buffer and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue.

Ni2+-NTA pull-down assay

The stabilization of POT-1 by TEBP-1 was exploited in a Ni-NTA pull-down assay to validate the interface between these proteins that was observed in the crystal structure. WT and mutants of His10-SUMO–tagged TEBP-1PBR + SUMO-tagged POT-1TBR were coexpressed from pETDuet-His10Smt3-TEBP-1PBR + Smt3-POT-1TBR (see the “Plasmid constructs and cloning” section). This expression strategy enabled the purification of TEBP-1PBR regardless of POT-1 expression. One liter of growth culture for each coexpression was processed as described above (see the “Protein expression and purification” section) using 0.5 ml of Ni-NTA resin. Proteins were eluted in 300 mM imidazole, and 20 μg of each sample was assessed by SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue.

SEC-MALS analysis

Stoichiometric analysis was established by SEC-MALS through a Superdex Increase 200 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) in line with a DAWN HELIOS II MALS detector and an Optilab T-rEX differential refractometer (Wyatt Technology), as described previously (43). The column was equilibrated with 25 mM tris (pH 8.0), 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT. Using a 0.5-ml load volume, the TEBP-1PBR–containing constructs TEBP-1695–837 (1 mg) and TEBP-2PBR (700 μg) were monomeric, and the POT-1TBR/TEBP-1PBR (1 mg) and POT-1(1–187)/TEBP-2PBR complexes (400 μg) displayed a 1:1 stoichiometry.

Protein crystallization

For crystallization, a complex of POT1TBR and TEBP-1PBR was purified from pETDuet-His10Smt3-POT-1TBR + Smt3-TEBP-1PBR as described above, cleaving untagged protein directly off of Ni-NTA agarose with Ulp1 protease for further purification on Superdex 75 in 20 mM tris (pH 8.0), 100 mM NaCl, and 2 mM DTT. Hanging drop crystallization was performed at 16°C. The native POT-1TBR and TEBP-1PBR complex (35 mg/ml) was mixed 1:1 with well solution 100 mM Hepes (pH 7.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 10% PEG 3350; SeMet-substituted complex (17 mg/ml) was mixed 1:1 with well solution 100 mM MES (pH 6.75), 100 mM NaCl, and 8% PEG 3350. Crystal structure of POT-1TBR-TEBP-1PBR complex

Diffraction data for the crystals of the SeMet-derived POT-1TBR-TEBP-1PBR complex were obtained at Argonne National Laboratory on the LS-CAT beamline 21 ID-D. Mosflm (44) was used for indexing and data integration, and the data were reduced with Aimless (45). MR was combined with SAD in the Phenix (46) suite as follows. Phaser (47) within the Phenix suite was used to find an MR solution using the AlphaFold prediction (27) of the POT-1 (amino acids 1 to 128) fragment as a search model. Next, AutoSol (48) was used to generate SAD phases from the Se in the crystal, which were combined with the phases from the MR solution of POT-1 (amino acids 1 to 128). AutoSol was able to build a preliminary model that was almost complete for both POT-1TBR and TEBP-1PBR, barring internal regions of POT-1 and the terminal residues on both proteins. AutoBuild (49) in Phenix was used to build the POT-1 (amino acids 70 to 78) region. Clear density was visible in the 2Fo-Fc maps, into which additional residues were built manually in Coot (50). Structural refinement was performed in Phenix to generate 2Fo-Fc maps. Iterative model building and refinement were performed, and ordered water molecules were placed at the final stages. The final structural model contained amino acids 1 to 171 of POT-1 and amino acids 728 to 837 of TEBP-1. The geometry of the final model was evaluated with MolProbity (51) (MolProbity score = 1.2; 97th percentile). See table S1 for data statistics.

C. elegans C-strand STELA

The technique we used to determine the 5′-terminal nucleotide for the worm genome was adapted from the C-strand STELA method for human telomeres (17). One microgram of genomic worm DNA was digested with 30 U of EcoRI-HF (NEB) in a 20-μl reaction for 2 to 4 hours at 37°C. Six worm telorettes were designed on the basis of the corresponding human sequences: (i) TGCTCCGTGCATCTGGCATCAAGCCTA; (ii) TGCTCCGTGCATCTGGCATCAGCCTAA; (iii) TGCTCCGTGCATCTGGCATCGCCTAAG; (iv) TGCTCCGTGCATCTGGCATCCCTAAGC; (v) TGCTCCGTGCATCTGGCATCCTAAGCC; (vi) TGCTCCGTGCATCTGGCATCTAAGCCT. In six separate tubes, 10 ng of digested DNA was incubated with 1nM of each telorette and T4 DNA ligase (NEB M0202S, 200 U) in a 10-μl reaction for 12 hours at 35°C. One nanogram of each ligated sample was polymerase chain reaction (PCR)–amplified in a 25-μl reaction containing 0.5 μl of 10 μM cTel17Bf primer (TGCGAAGGTACTGAACCACC), which binds in the single-copy cTel17B subtelomeric region, and 0.5 μl of 10 μM Teltail primer (corresponding to the underlined region of the C. elegans telorettes, as per the human protocol), 12.5 μl of Failsafe PCR buffer H, and 0.8 μl of Failsafe enzyme mix (2.5 U/μl, Biosearch Technologies). PCR conditions were 94°C 15 s, then 28 cycles of 95°C 15 s, 58°C 20 s, and 68°C 10 minutes, with a final extension of 68°C 10 min. Five microliters of 6× DNA loading dye was added to each sample, and 12 μl was resolved on a 0.7% agarose/1× TAE minigel run for an hour at 100 V. The gel was denatured, dried, and then neutralized before in-gel probing overnight with end-labeled cTel17Bf primer (GCCTAAGCAAATCCCCAGAAA).

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

For EMSAs, DNA oligonucleotides were 5′ 32P-end labeled with γ-32P-ATP and T4 DNA kinase. Binding reactions with 32P-end–labeled single-oligonucleotide targets (50 nM) and POT-1 OB domain were performed in a binding buffer of 50 mM tris (pH 8.0), 100 mM NaCl, and 6% glycerol. For duplex targets, only the top (telomeric G-rich) DNA strand was 5′ 32P-end labeled. A duplex was formed by annealing top and bottom strand oligonucleotides (final concentrations of 1.1 and 1 μM, respectively) by boiling and slow cooling to room temperature in a 20-μl reaction with TE (pH 8.0) and 50 mM NaCl. Protein DNA binding reactions (10 μl) were equilibrated on ice in a buffer containing 20 mM tris (pH 8.0), 100 mM NaCl, 7% glycerol, bovine serum albumin (10 μg/ml), and tRNA (1 μg/ml) with radiolabeled DNA duplex (typically 10 nM).

DNA substrates used in protein DNA binding reactions are described below. For POT-1 OB binding: (CTAAGC)5 is CTAAGCCTAAGCCTAAGCCTAAGCCTAAGC; (GGCTTA)5 is GGCTTAGGCTTAGGCTTAGGCTTAGGCTTA; (GGCTTA)4 is GGCTTAGGCTTAGGCTTAGGCTTA; and (ACTGAC)5 is ACTGACACTGACACTGACACTGACACTGAC. For BLUNT (−4 to 0) ds and 3′ 8-nt and 5′ 1-nt overhang substrates, the top strands GCGCGTTAGG, GCGCGTTAGGCTTAGGCT, and GCGCGTTAG, respectively, were 32P-labeled and annealed to one of two bottom strands (CCTAACGCGC or 5′ pCCTAACGCGC) for analysis at 10 nM in EMSA. Similarly, a 5′ 8-nt overhang was generated by annealing the 32P-labeled GCGCGTTAGG top strand to the 5′ AGCCTAAGCCTAACGCGC bottom strand.

Reactions were resolved on 6% polyacrylamide/0.5× TBE gels, typically run at 50 V for 50 min on ice. Gels were dried and analyzed on a phosphorimager (Amersham Typhoon IP, Cytiva), and the percent bound DNA was quantified by ImageQuant software. Binding curves were analyzed with a one site-specific binding model in GraphPad Prism.

Histological analysis by immunofluorescence, GFP fluorescence, and FISH

Thirty to 40 C. elegans hermaphrodites, 16 to 20 hours post-L4 larval stage (day 1 adults), were cut on a coverslip to extract germ lines in 20 μl of azide buffer [2.5 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 11.8 mM NaCl, 4.8 mM KCl, 0.2 mM MgCl2, 2 mM CaCl2, 0.1% Tween 20, and 15 mM NaN3]. The coverslip with germ lines was placed on a SuperFrost Plus slide (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by freezing on dry ice. The frozen coverslips were flicked off, and the slides were immediately placed in ice-cold methanol for 1 min [immunofluorescence (IF) GFP] or 20 min (FISH). For telomere FISH analysis, the slides were washed with 2× SSC containing 0.1% Tween 20. Slides were treated with ribonuclease A at 37°C for 30 min. After rinsing with 2× SSC four times, the slides were dehydrated with a series of ethanol (70, 85, and 100%). Slides were hybridized with Cy3-labeled (TTAGGC)3 probe (Panagene) in hybridization buffer [10 mM NaHPO4 (pH 7.2), 10 mM NaCl, 20 mM tris (pH 7.5), and 70% formamide] at 37°C overnight. After washing with 50% formamide/0.5× SSC twice and 1× SSC twice at 42°C, the slides were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole and mounted.

Southern blotting

For each worm strain, five × 10 cm, OP50 seeded, NGM plates filled with worms were transferred with M9 buffer to tubes, and genomic DNA was extracted via the Gentra Pure Tissue genomic DNA kit (QIAGEN). A total of 4 μg of C. elegans genomic DNA was digested with HinfI and RsaI (New England Biolabs). The digested DNA was subjected to phenol/chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation and separated on a 0.8% agarose gel at 8 V/cm for 4 hours. DNA was transferred to a nylon membrane (Roche) in denaturing solution (0.6 M NaCl and 0.4 M NaOH) overnight, washed with 2× SSC twice, and UV-crosslinked (Stratalinker UV 1800 Crosslinker, Stratagene). The membranes were hybridized with 32P-labeled telomeric probe in hybridization buffer [3.3× SSC, 0.1% SDS, and skim milk powder (1 mg/ml)] at 43°C overnight followed by washing with 2× SSC/0.1% SDS twice and 0.2× SSC/0.1% SDS once. The membranes were exposed to a phosphor imaging plate (FUJIFILM), and signals were detected with a Typhoon laser scanner (Amersham). For the probe, an oligonucleotide (TTAGGC)4 was labeled with γ-32P using T4 PNK (New England Biolabs).

Immunoprecipitation

Worms were harvested and suspended in IP buffer [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.0), 200 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol] supplemented with cOmplete Protease Inhibitor (Roche) and Phosphatase Inhibitor (Roche) and subjected to sonication [30% amplitude, 30 s ON and 50 s OFF for 15 min on a Q500 (Qsonica)], followed by centrifugation at 7830 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. The filtered supernatants were supplemented with Dynabeads protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific) conjugated with 30 μg of mouse anti-GFP antibody and incubated for 6 hours at 4°C. The beads were washed with high-salt wash buffer [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.0), 400 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol] supplemented with cOmplete Protease Inhibitor (Roche) and Phosphatase Inhibitor (Roche). The samples were eluted with 0.1 M glycine (pH 2.5) for further analysis with Western blotting.

Western blotting

Mixed-stage worms were washed from OP50 seeded NGM plates with M9 buffer, collected in 15-ml tubes, frozen, and resuspended in IP buffer [20 mM tris (pH 7.5), 200 mM KCl, 0.4 mM EDTA, 5 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 0.1% Triton X-100, 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol, cOomplete protease inhibitor (Roche), and Phosphostop (Roche)]. After sonication on ice, the cell extract was centrifuged at 7000g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected for the Western Blotting analysis. Imaging of Western blots was performed with an Image Quant LAS 500 (GE Healthcare).

Antibodies

The following commercial antibodies were used: rabbit anti–Phospho-ATM/ATR substrate motif (Cell Signaling Technology, 6966S, LOT:6) 1:500 for IF, mouse anti-FLAG (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804, clone: M2, LOT:1003310513) 1:1000 for Western blot, and mouse anti–β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich; A2228-100UL, clone: AC-74, LOT:65309400) 1:1000 for Western blot, and mouse anti-GFP (Roche; 11814460001, 42903200) for IP. In addition, in-house generated rabbit anti–TEBP-1 and anti–TEBP-2 antibodies (1:1000 for Western blot and 1:500 for IF) were used.

Microscopy

Images were generated with a Delta Vision Elite system, equipped with an Olympus IX71 stand [with 100× numerical aperture (NA) 1.40 and 60× NA 1.42 objectives], Photometrics CoolSnap HQ2 camera, and solid-state illumination; controlled with softWoRx 7.2.0 acquisition software (Cytiva). The acquired images were processed in softWoRx 7.2.0 (Cytiva) using 3D maximum intensity projection and subsequent processing via 2D deconvolution and finalized by Fiji or Photoshop (Adobe).

Brood size assay

Initially, strains were synchronized via bleaching and subsequent cultivation with E. coli (OP50) seeded NGM plates. At L4 larval stage, the worms were transferred to a new plate every day until reaching menopause. Viable progeny were counted, growing them for at least 24 hours after removing the parent.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantification of telomere foci was performed in softWoRx 7.2.0 (Cytiva), using the software’s inbuilt measuring tool in 3D projections, using a six-dot circle. Values gained from a circle—centered on a telomere signal—were subtracted by background values from the immediate neighbor area. All image quantifications involve normalization to the mean value of WT. Some representative images were deconvolved using softWoRx 7.2.0 (Cytiva) for visualization purposes. For FISH, nuclei of epidermal tail cells were analyzed. None of the C. elegans experiments were randomized, with predetermination of sample size, and the images or plates used were not blinded for analysis. Conclusions in the manuscript were based on results reproduced in at least two independent experiments with the exception of SEC-MALS runs (n = 1) and the EMSA experiments for POT-1 OB domain binding to G-strand oligonucleotides with repeat length less than 5 (n = 1). Sample sizes, statistical tests, and P values can be found in the text, figures, and figure legends. Unless otherwise indicated, all statistics were generated with the software Prism 9.0.0 (GraphPad).
