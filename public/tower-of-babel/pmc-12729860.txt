Beyond the screen: a social cognitive theory perspective on gender, online gaming, and social well-being in Jordan
Authors: Haitham Khatatbeh, Yahya Khatatbeh, Annamaria Pakai, Moawiah Khatatbeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global expansion of online gaming raises worries about its impact on adolescent well-being, particularly regarding Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) and its association with social health and bullying. This study addresses a research gap in the Middle East by exploring gender-specific trends in gaming habits, social perceptions, and the prevalence of IGD among adolescents in Jordan. Utilizing the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), the study aimed to identify predictors of IGD and exposure to social bullying.Methods403 adolescents (aged 10–17) were selected from the northern Jordan in this cross-sectional, survey-based study. Participants responded to the Arabic version of the IGD-20 scale, in addition to items about their gaming habits, social perceptions, and exposure to social bullying. Furthermore, was used to measure IGD. Linear regression models were employed to identify factors predicting IGD scores and exposure to social bullying.Results52.9% of the participants were at risk for or had IGD. Significant gender differences were found, with males spending more time gaming (p = 0.012) and preferring competitive games, while females favored “Fashion/dress” games. Key predictors of higher IGD scores were daily gaming hours, a perception of gaming as harmful, and poor social relationships. A higher IGD score also predicted increased exposure to social bullying. Crucially, gender did not significantly predict IGD or social bullying in the final models.ConclusionOur findings, guided by SCT, suggest that gaming habits are often gender-specific, however, the core risk factors for IGD are not. Developing IGD is more closely tied to excessive gaming, poor social relationships, and exposure to bullying. So, effective measures should promote healthy gaming habits and foster strong social support systems for all adolescents, rather than exclusively targeting a specific gender. Introduction

The worldwide expansion of online gaming highlights its impact on adolescents’ social wellbeing, including social health [1, 2], perceived harm to social relationships [3, 4], and exposure to social bullying [5, 6]. This may impact on their social dynamics and contribute to the development of the Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) [6]. IGD is described as a persistent and repetitive gaming activity influencing several domains of wellbeing, most importantly the social relationships [7].

Understanding the predictors and consequences of IGD is essential for developing effective policies [8]. Gender is a critical, but understudied, predictor in the relationship of IGD with social relationships and exposure to social bullying. Besides the negative association between IGD and adolescent’s social relationships [3], previous research suggests that IGD [3, 9] and social bullying [10] may significantly vary based on adolescent’s gender. These gender-specific relationships, modeled by social learning as emphasized by the social cognitive theory (SCT), could lead to varying IGD susceptibility and consequences [11]. For instance, the males characterized approximately 64% of the gaming time among the participants of a Spanish study [12]. Another Spanish study showed that male have significantly higher weekly gaming hours, enthusiasm to social interaction, and higher tendency to enjoy violent behavior [13]. Similarly, male adolescents were more susceptible to IGD and lower egotism than females in Taiwan [14]. Concerning the bullying, it was significantly higher in males than females [15]. However, an older study showed that female adolescents are more likely to commit bullying than their counterparts [16].

Despite the increasing global literature on IGD, a significant research gap still exists in the Middle East. Previous studies have usually assessed prevalence and simple gender differences. However, few studies in the Middle East employed a comprehensive theoretical framework to understand the mechanisms behind IGD. The novelty of this study lies in utilizing SCT to the Jordanian adolescent population. This framework would analyze the complex relationship of personal (e.g., gender), behavioral (e.g., gaming habits), and environmental (e.g., social bullying, relationships) factors. By doing so, we move beyond describing if gender differences exist to explain how various risk factors interact to shape outcomes, providing nuanced, evidence-based insights for culturally sensitive interventions. Utilizing the principles of SCT, this study aims to explore the gender-specific trends in adolescents internet gaming, including their gaming habits, preferences, and the prevalence of IGD. Specifically, this study aims to (1) recognize gender differences in daily gaming duration, preferred game types, perceived harm of online gaming to social relations, and exposure to social bullying; (2) assess the associations between gender and gaming habits, social perceptions, exposure to social bullying, and IGD scores; and (3) determine the factors that predict IGD scores and exposure to social bullying among adolescents.

Methodology

Study design, sampling, and data collection

In alignment with public education system in Jordan, this cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted in single-sex governmental schools in northern Jordan. This study targeted adolescents aged 10 to 17 years. To represent the general adolescent population in Jordan, no exclusions were done based on any mental health disorder. To minimizes the risk of stress as a confounder, data were collected between February 8th and April 7th, 2025, which did not overlap with major exam periods in Jordan.

A convenience sampling approach was used, selecting governmental schools belonging to the Ministry of Education (MoE) based on their availability and readiness to participate. Assuming 20% prevalence of IGD, a 5% margin of error, 95% confidence level, and 80% power, the required sample size was 403. To compensate for probable non-response rate, a total of 500 parental consent forms were distributed. Of these, 420 (84%) were returned, and 403 adolescents (96% of consented participants) completed the survey. The overall response rate was 80.6%.

The study design and data collection were guided by SCT to capture data related to personal, behavioral, and environmental factors that influence IGD.

In compliance with ethical research conduct and to facilitate data collection, a Google Form link was first sent to the parents or legal guardians of potential adolescent participants. This form explained study’s objectives, the voluntary nature of participation, guaranteed confidentiality, and the right to withdraw at any point without consequences. After obtaining parental consent via this form, another Google Form link containing the study questionnaire was forwarded to the adolescents through their parents or guardians. This research was reported in adherence to the STROBE checklist [17] (Appendix S1).

Before starting the data collection, teachers at the selected schools received online instruction about the data collection process and research ethical considerations. Researchers provided these teachers with links to both the parental consent and adolescent questionnaire for dissemination.

The survey instrument was purposely built to collect relevant study’s data in alignment with key constructs of the SCT. It included personal factors (such as age and gender), and behavioral factors (questions about online gaming habits, such as daily gaming hours, years spent playing, and preferred game types (e.g., fighting/wars, football/other sports, fashion/dress, racing/cars, building/designing, simulation, or other games like cards, chess, music, puzzle)). Furthermore, the survey included expectations (questions about perceived harm to social relations (yes/no), perceived social relationships (very weak, weak, average, good, very good)), environmental factors (exposure to social bullying (never, rarely, sometimes, often, always), and outcome (the IGD scale). The Arabic version of the IGD scale, comprising 20 items that assess gaming addiction, was used. The 20-item IGD scale (IGD-20) is a valid and reliable tool, with a Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient of 0.88 in the original study [18] and 0.915 in the Arabic version [19]. The IGD-20 scale conceptualizes disordered gaming behavioral addiction according to the following six components: salience, mood modification, tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, conflict, and relapse [20].

Pilot testing

To assess the survey’s content validity and the data collection methodology, a pilot test was conducted with the initial 30 participants, including younger adolescents (ages 10–11). Teachers were available during the pilot to clarify any questions. No major comprehension issues were reported, confirming the language was clear and understandable for adolescents and no modifications were needed. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the questionnaire during the pilot phase was 0.919, indicating moderate reliability. Responses collected during this pilot study were excluded from the final analysis. For the final sample, the questionnaire demonstrated acceptable reliability with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.928.

Ethical considerations

This survey adhered to the “Declaration of Helsinki” ethical guidelines. Furthermore, an ethical approval (Decision no. 1/2/2024/2025 dated 23/12/2024) was obtained from the Institutional Review Board at Jerash University for a broader correlational study investigating the multifaceted impact of online gaming on adolescents, of which the current study is a part.

The study’s purpose was fully explained to all participants. The voluntary nature of their participation, along with their unconditional right to withdraw at any time, were explicitly ensured for both students and their parents. So, a consent form was obtained from both the student’s parent/ legal guardians and the student themselves. To ensure participant’s confidentiality and privacy, the collected data were stored securely, with access strictly limited to the researchers.

Statistical analysis

Responses gathered via Google Forms were automatically compiled into a structured spreadsheet, which significantly streamlined data management and subsequent analysis. The percentage of missing data were minimal (< 2%) and handled using listwise deletion to ensure statistical rigor. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 24 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 24.0).

Descriptive statistics, including mean and standard deviation (for continuous variables) and frequencies and percentages (for categorical variables), were employed to characterize the sample. Given the non-normal distribution of the data, Spearman’s rank correlation was utilized to ascertain bivariate correlations among the study variables.

Utilizing the SCT, this analysis explored relationships between personal factors (age, gender), behavioral factors (gaming habits such as daily hours spent playing and the type of internet game usually played), expectations (perceived harm to social relations, and perceived social relationships), and environmental factors (exposure to social bullying). Furthermore, two linear regression models were conducted to identify factors predicting Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) scores and exposure to social bullying. See Fig. 1.

Results

Sample characteristics

As shown in Table 1, the study involved a total of 403 participants, with a mean age of 13.2 years (SD = 3.1). The sample comprised 187 females (46.4%) and 216 males (53.6%). On average, participants reported spending 3.1 h per day playing internet games (SD = 2.4), and the overall mean score for Internet Gaming Disorder was 52.9 (SD = 19.4). A substantial proportion of participants (49.9%) indicated long-term engagement, having played internet games for “≥ 3 years.” “Fighting/wars” games were the most frequently played type overall, accounting for 32.3% of responses, followed by “Football/other sports” at 22.3%. Regarding perceptions of harm, 25.3% of participants perceived internet games as harmful to social relations. The majority of participants generally rated their social relationships positively, with 26.6% as “Good” and 47.4% as “Very good.” Most participants (73.7%) reported never being exposed to social bullying. In terms of IGD prevalence, 30.8% of participants were categorized as “At risk of IGD,” while 22.1% met the criteria for “Having IGD,“. This means that a total of 52.9% of the adolescents in the study were either at risk for or having IGD.

Gender variations

Gender played a significant role in several key aspects of internet gaming habits and perceptions, as evidenced by statistically significant t-test results, representing a critical personal factor within SCT. A notable difference was observed in daily gaming hours (t = -2.512, p = 0.012), with males reporting a higher average daily gaming duration (Mean = 3.4 h, SD = 2.5) compared to females (Mean = 2.8 h, SD = 2.1). Furthermore, there was a highly significant difference in the type of internet game often played between genders (t = 7.157, p = 0.000). Males predominantly favored “Fighting/wars” (44.4%) and “Football/other sports” (33.8%), while females showed higher percentages in “Fashion/dress” (28.9%) followed by " Fighting/wars " (18.2%), clearly indicating distinct gaming preferences by gender. A significant gender difference was also found in the perception of harm to social relations (an SCT expectation, t = 2.854, p = 0.005), with a higher percentage of males (31.0%) perceiving internet games as harmful to social relations compared to females (18.7%). Lastly, a significant difference was observed in perceived social relationships between genders (an SCT expectation, t = − 3.24, p = 0.001), where males, on average, rated their social relationships higher (Mean = 4.27, SD = 0.96) than females (Mean = 3.95, SD = 1.07).

Correlations between child gender with other variables

As presented in Table 2, the correlation analysis showed several statistically significant relationships between gender and other study variables. Gender showed a significant negative correlation with the type of internet game often played (r = − 0.337, p < 0.01), a behavioral factor. Additionally, gender was significantly positively correlated with the perceived social relationships (r = 0.160, p < 0.01), suggesting that males tend to report more positive perceived social relationships. Conversely, Gender was significantly negatively correlated with the perceived harm to social relations (r = -0.141, p < 0.01), implying that females perceive more gaming’s harm to social interactions than males. Finally, gender showed a significant positive correlation with hours spent playing daily (r = 0.124, p < 0.05), a behavioral factor, indicating more daily gaming hours between among males than females. However, Gender did not show a statistically significant correlation with the environmental factor of exposure to social bullying, or total IGD score in this analysis.

Concerning other variables, the behavioral factor of hours spent playing daily showed a significant positive correlation with total IGD score (r = 0.343, p < 0.01). This indicates that increased daily gaming hours are strongly associated with higher IGD scores. Conversely, hours spent playing daily also exhibited a significant negative correlation with perceived social relationships (r = − 0.166, p < 0.01), suggesting that more time spent gaming daily is linked to poorer perceived social relationships. Similarly, a negative correlation was found between the number of years spent playing internet games and perceived harm to social relations (r = -0.112, p < 0.05). Also, a significant negative correlation was found between the perceived harm to social relations and perceived social relationships (r = − 0.199, p < 0.01). This finding indicates that individuals who perceive gaming as harmful to their social interactions tend to report less positive social relationships. Interestingly, perceived harm to social relations also showed a significant positive correlation with total IGD score (r = 0.276, p < 0.01). This finding indicates that a stronger perception of internet gaming’s harm is associated with higher IGD scores. The environmental factor exposure to social bullying was positively correlated with total IGD score (r = 0.241, p < 0.01), suggesting a link between experiencing social bullying and higher IGD scores. Furthermore, perceived social relationships were significantly negatively correlated with total IGD score (r = − 0.264, p < 0.01), indicating that poorer perceived social relationships are associated with higher IGD scores.

Factors predicting IGD score and exposure to social bullying

The regression results in Table 3 show the factors predicting IGD score and exposure to social bullying. The first model summary indicates that the regression model for IGD is statistically significant (F(8,394) = 16.889, p < 0.001) with an adjusted R2 of 0.24, suggesting that approximately 24% of the variance in IGD scores can be explained by the model. Key predictors of IGD include the behavioral factor of hours spent playing daily (β = 0.288, t = 6.154, p < 0.001), perceived harm to social relations (β = 0.262, t = 5.820, p < 0.01), and perceived social relationships (β = − 0.147, t = − 3.145, p < 0.01). Furthermore, both age and exposure to social bullying were also significant predictors. These results indicate that increased daily gaming hours and a higher perception of harm to social relations are associated with higher IGD scores, while better perceived social relationships are associated with lower IGD scores. For the environmental factor of exposure to social bullying, the regression model is also statistically significant (F (8,394) = 5.874, p < 0.001), with an adjusted R2 of 0.088. This means that around 8.8% of the variance in exposure to social bullying can be explained by the model. Significant predictors include IGD (β = 0.199, t = 3.662, p < 0.001) and perceived social relationships (β = − 0.143, t = − 2.778, p < 0.01). This indicates that higher IGD scores are associated with increased exposure to social bullying, whereas better perceived social relationships are associated with reduced exposure to social bullying. For both models, neither IGD score nor exposure to social bullying were predicted by adolescents’s gender.

Discussion

This study explores the connections between gender, online gaming habits, social well-being, and IGD in Jordanian adolescents. Our findings uncover a complex situation where gender might shape the gaming habits, but not susceptibility to its negative consequences.

Gender-specific behaviors and IGD prevalence in regional and global context

Our findings on gender-specific habits are congruent with global trends. Males in this study spent significantly more time gaming daily and preferred competitive games like “Fighting/wars” and “Football/other sports”, which aligns with previous studies from Spain [12, 13] and Taiwan [14]. This finding aligns with societal norms, in Middle Eastern contexts like Jordan, where gaming has conventionally been a more male-dominated hobby [21]. Conversely, the female preference for “Fashion/dress” games reflects global gender-based patterns in internet gaming, which often target females with aesthetics [22, 23].

Our findings showed that 52.9% of study participants have or at-risk for IGD. This rate is significantly higher than the 3.05% reported in a global systemic-review and meta-analyses [24] and the 29.3% found in a recent Saudi Arabian study [25]. Several possible reasons might explain this significant difference, including the use of IGD-20 scale that might be more comprehensive to capture a variety of symptoms. The rapid increase of internet access in Jordan (34) and the limited recreational alternatives, may strengthen online gaming among adolescents.

The puzzle of social perception: An SCT interpretation

Our findings showed an interesting contradiction. Although gaming more, males reported better perceived social relationships, yet a higher percentage perceived gaming as harmful to social relations. This contradiction can be interpreted through the SCT lens of reciprocal determinism. Many boys don’t perceive online gaming as an isolating activity but a platform for social interaction (behavioral factor). The collaborative nature of internet games facilitates team formation and communication, improving their perceived social connections (personal factor) [26]. Additionally, they might be aware of the societal chronicle that criticizes excessive gaming (environmental factor). While they appreciate the social benefits of online gaming, they recognize the poor traditional socialization [27]. Females play for less time and in less socially integrated games. So, they may not experience this compensatory social benefit to the same degree, making them ready to endorse the perceived harm. This underlines that the quality and social context of internet gaming are more critical to well-being than quantity.

Core risk factors and the mechanism of bullying: Moving beyond gender

Our findings showed that gender and gaming habits are correlated. This finding matches the findings of previous studies [12, 13, 21, 26, 27]. However, gender does not predict IGD or social bullying. According to the regression analysis, the number of daily hours spent playing (behavioral factor) emerged as the strongest predictor of IGD. This finding is consistent with several studies that ties excessive playing time to IGD [28–31]. Another interesting finding was the strong positive association between the perceived harm to social relations (expectation) and higher IGD scores. In line with a previous study [32], this finding proposes that adolescents who have problematic gaming habits may be aware of the negative consequences. This understanding could be a part of the loss of control that characterizes IGD, where individuals continue the behavior despite recognizing its harmful impacts [33]. Furthermore, the negative correlation between perceived social relationships (environmental factor) and IGD scores supports the social compensation hypothesis which suggests that adolescents with poorer traditional social connections (environment) may use online games (behavior) to compensate for this issue [34]. However, the increasing preoccupation can isolate them more and more and make them prone to bullying (environment). This, in turn, pushes them deeper into gaming as a coping mechanism. This creates a self-perpetuating cycle of distress, which characterize the SCT’s model of reciprocal determinism.

Limitations and recommendations

This study has several limitations including self-report data that may be biased from under- or over-reporting of gaming habits and perceptions. On the other hand, this study didn’t include private schools as they usually serve higher socioeconomic class than general population. Additionally, the cross-sectional design used cannot establish causality among IGD, and social bullying and relationships. Moreover, the study did not collect data on household internet quality, family size, parental education, or income due to questionnaire length concerns. Such data are crucial environmental factors within SCT that could systematically link to our outcomes. On the other hand, short exams or quizzes might be conducted during the data collection period. However, the researchers tried to avoid major exam periods. Lastly, counting on parental consent obtained through Google Forms may lead to a selection bias. Such bias could happen because of probable exclusion for families without internet access or internet literacy although and internet access in Jordan is estimated to be over 90% of households [35].

Future studies should utilize longitudinal designs to untie the causal relationships among social relationships and bullying, and IGD. Furthermore, qualitative studies would be useful to discover the subjective experiences behind gender-specific variations in social perceptions. Additionally, future studies are encouraged to include questions about household internet quality, family size, parental education, and income.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results demonstrate a pattern of reciprocal determinism, where SCT constructs (personal, behavioral, and environmental factors) interact to shape adolescent gaming habits and other outcomes. Our study confirms that adolescent internet gaming is a gender-specific activity in terms of gaming hours and preferred games. However, the pathways to harm are less about being male or female and more about a combination of lengthy gaming sessions, a perception of possible harms, and poor social relationships. For parents, teachers, and school nurses, this suggests that the effective methodology to mitigating the risks of IGD is to look beyond gender and focus on promoting healthy lifestyles and strong social support systems for all adolescents. Furthermore, interventions should prioritize promoting healthy gaming habits and time management, strengthening social support systems to foster positive offline relationships, and addressing bullying through school-based policies and awareness programs.
