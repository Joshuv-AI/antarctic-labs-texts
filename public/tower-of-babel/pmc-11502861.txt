U-shaped nonlinear relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy
Authors: Zhe Wu, Xiaoyue Song, Guan Wang, Bin Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background: Dietary copper intake is a promising predictor of peripheral neuropathy. There is no research exploring the potential link between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy. Methods: The information utilized in our research was collected from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) between 1999 and 2004. The relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy was analyzed using a multivariate logistic regression model and restricted cubic spline (RCS). Results: The RCS analysis results showed a U-shaped nonlinear relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy (P for nonlinearity < 0.001). The threshold effect analysis results indicated that when dietary copper intake was less than 0.889 mg/d, the risk of peripheral neuropathy decreased with increasing copper intake (OR: 0.388, 95% CI: 0.200-0.753). When dietary copper intake was ≥ 0.889 mg/d, the risk of peripheral neuropathy increased with increasing copper intake (OR: 1.129, 95% CI: 1.006–1.266). And the incidence rate of peripheral neuropathy in the first quantile (OR: 1.421, 95% CI: 1.143–1.766), the third quantile (OR: 1.358, 95% CI: 1.057–1.744), and the fourth quantile (OR: 1.676, 95% CI: 1.250–2.248) of dietary copper intake were significantly higher than that in the second quantile (where the inflection point was located). Conclusion: Our study suggests that both insufficient and excessive dietary copper intake may be associated with an increased incidence of peripheral neuropathy. However, further research is needed to provide definitive evidence and confirm these findings.

Introduction

Peripheral neuropathy (PN) is a common neurological disorder that primarily affects the elderly1. It primarily presents as sensory abnormalities in the lower extremities, and in severe instances, it can result in impaired motor function or even amputation. Peripheral neuropathy has a strong correlation with several factors, including diabetes, exposure to toxins such as heavy metals, organic solvents, and radiation, prolonged alcohol consumption, nutritional deficiencies, and kidney dysfunction2.

Copper is a crucial trace element that performs several essential physiological functions within the human body. It is involved in the formation of red blood cells, immune system function, and the development and operation of the nervous system3,4. The role of copper in the nervous system is particularly noteworthy, as it is involved in several functions such as cell signal transduction, myelin formation, and neurotransmitter synthesis5. Consequently, copper is a promising predictor of peripheral neuropathy. Diet is the primary source of copper for humans. However, excessive or insufficient copper levels in the body can have detrimental effects6. Studies have demonstrated that low dietary copper intake can increase the risk of diseases such as cognitive impairment7, while low dietary copper intake can increase the risk of conditions such as myocardial infarction, stroke and depression8–10. There is currently limited research on the relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy. We analyzed NHANES data from 1999 to 2004 to investigate the relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy in the general adult population.

Materials and methods

Data source

NHANES is a national survey project conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) in the United States11. Its purpose is to evaluate the health and nutritional status of Americans by collecting data from nationally representative samples of the US population through interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory testing. The information utilized in our research was collected from the NHANES between 1999 and 2004. The NCHS approved the NHANES 1999–2004 research protocol, and no additional institutional review committee authorization is required for this subsequent analysis. All participants provided written informed consent forms.

Inclusion criteria: Adults aged 40 years and older. Participants who underwent lower limb peripheral neuropathy assessment. Participants with complete data on dietary copper intake and relevant covariates.

Exclusion criteria: Participants younger than 40 years old. Participants lacking peripheral neuropathy-related data (Individuals with bilateral amputations. Participants weighing over 400 pounds. Participants with casts, ulcers, dressings, or other conditions that interfere with testing. Participants unable to understand test instructions. Participants who voluntarily did not participate in or refused testing.). Participants missing dietary copper intake information. Participants missing relevant covariate data.

Our study focused on adults aged 40 and above who underwent assessment of lower limb peripheral neuropathy. We excluded 23,224 subjects who lacked data related to peripheral neuropathy. Additionally, 176 subjects were excluded due to missing information on dietary copper intake, and 604 subjects were excluded due to a lack of relevant covariates. Ultimately, we analyzed data from 7122 subjects to investigate the relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy (Fig. 1).

PN

Health technicians evaluated foot sensation in participants by applying standard monofilament (5.07 gauge Semmes Weinstein nylon) to three areas on each foot (plantar-first metatarsal head, plantar-fifth metatarsal head, and plantar-hallux). The area was considered insensible if the respondent was unable to detect the filament’s position. PN was defined as having at least one insensible area on both feet12.

Dietary copper intake

Participants’ dietary copper intake was estimated using the 24-hour recall method, which involves collecting a short list of foods and beverages consumed over 24 h. The NHANES Dietary Interviewer Procedures Manual provides detailed instructions for this method.

Covariates

The covariates of this study include age, sex, race (white, black, Mexican American, or other races), smoking status (now, former, or never), drinking status, albumin, body mass index (BMI), and the presence of certain health conditions, including hyperlipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease (CKD). Hypertension is defined as an average systolic blood pressure of ≥ 140 millimeters of mercury and an average diastolic blood pressure of ≥ 90 millimeters of mercury, diagnosed by a doctor, or using antihypertensive drugs. Diabetes is defined as random blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L or fasting blood glucose ≥ 7 mmol/L or glycosylated hemoglobin ≥ 6.5% or two-hour OGTT blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or diagnosed by a doctor, or using hypoglycemic drugs. Hyperlipidemia is defined as total cholesterol ≥ 200 mg/dL or triglyceride ≥ 150 mg/dL or low-density lipoprotein ≥ 130 mg/dL or male high-density lipoprotein ≤ 40 mg/dL or female high-density lipoprotein ≤ 50 mg/dL, or the use of lipid-lowering drugs. CKD is defined as glomerular filtration rate < 6 ml/min/1.73m2 or 30 mg/g ≤ proteinuria ≤ 300 mg/g.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using R studio version 4.2.1. All statistical analyses were weighted based on the weight data of diet (wtdr4 year, wtdrd1). Continuous variables were tested using t-test, while categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages). The study categorized dietary copper intake into four quantiles, namely Q1 ≤ 0.754 mg/d, 0.754 mg/d < Q2 ≤ 1.061 mg/d, 1.061 mg/d < Q3 ≤ 1.480 mg/d, and Q4 > 1.480 mg/d. The relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy was analyzed using a multivariate logistic regression model. In addition, RCS was used to examine the dose-response relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy. The threshold for statistically significant differences is set at P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline information

Table 1 presents the baseline data of 7122 subjects who met the inclusion criteria. Among them, 1242 had peripheral neuropathy, and 5880 did not. The average age of all subjects was 53.99 ± 0.24. The average age of subjects with PN was significantly higher (63.71 ± 0.51) than that of subjects without PN (55.93 ± 0.24). The two groups also differed significantly in terms of sex, BMI, dietary copper intake, albumin level, smoking, drinking, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease. The prevalence of PN was found to be higher among elderly males with higher BMI, lower albumin levels, lower dietary copper intake, smokers, drinkers, and individuals with hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and chronic kidney disease. After grouping the subjects according to the quartile of dietary copper intake, the second quartile showed a lower prevalence of PN compared to the other groups (Table 2). This suggests that there may be a U-shaped relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy.

The correlation between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy

To explore the possible nonlinear relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy, we used a RCS plot. The RCS analysis results showed a U-shaped nonlinear relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy in both unadjusted and fully adjusted covariate models (Figs. 2 and 3) (P for nonlinearity < 0.001). After adjusting for all covariates, the inflection point of the curve relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy was found to be 0.889 mg/d.

The threshold effect analysis results indicated that when dietary copper intake was less than 0.889 mg/d, the risk of peripheral neuropathy decreased with increasing copper intake (OR: 0.388, 95% CI: 0.200-0.753). Discussion

Our study is the first to investigate the relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy in the general population of the United States. The analysis revealed a U-shaped nonlinear relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy with an inflection point at 0.889 mg/d. Below the inflection point, as dietary copper intake increased, the risk of peripheral neuropathy gradually decreased. However, when the dietary copper intake reached or exceeded 0.889 mg/d, the risk of peripheral neuropathy started to increase with further increases in copper intake. It indicates that both inadequate and excessive copper intake could potentially be associated with an increased risk of peripheral neuropathy. This discovery provides new insights into the relationship between dietary copper and neurological health, and has important public health implications for preventing peripheral neuropathy.

Previous studies have shown a strong link between abnormal copper homeostasis and various nervous system diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease6,13. However, there has been a limited number of studies exploring the connection between copper and peripheral neuropathy, and these studies have relatively small sample sizes and mostly use serum copper as the research content. Haynes et al. found that both copper deficiency and copper poisoning can lead to peripheral neuropathy, and patients with copper deficiency have a higher frequency of gait abnormalities14. Taylor et al. found through their research that patients with lower serum copper levels are more likely to experience axonal damage and are prone to length-dependent peripheral neuropathy15. In addition, multiple case reports have reported that copper deficiency may cause peripheral neuropathy16,17. To our knowledge, this is the first study investigating the relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy in the general population of the United States.

The U-shaped nonlinear relationship between peripheral neuropathy and dietary copper intake can be attributed to several factors. Firstly, copper is a cofactor for many enzymes, including enzymes involved in redox reactions such as cytochrome c oxidase and copper zinc superoxide dismutase18. These enzymes play important roles in nerve conduction, energy metabolism, and antioxidant defense19–22. Insufficient copper intake may lead to a decrease in the activity of these enzymes, which in turn can cause mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, all of which are potential mechanisms of neuropathy23,24. On the contrary, excessive copper intake may lead to excessive oxidative stress and neuronal damage, as the free ion form of copper can catalyze free radical generation, exacerbate lipid peroxidation and protein denaturation, thereby damaging nerve cells25–27. In addition, copper also plays an important role in neurotransmitter metabolism, including playing a critical role in the synthesis and release of neurotransmitters such as dopamine, norepinephrine, and glutamate28,29. The imbalance of copper levels may lead to impaired synthesis or abnormal release of neurotransmitters, thereby disrupting normal signal transduction between neurons. This may be another mechanism linking abnormal copper intake to neuropathy.

It is crucial to maintain a balanced copper level in the body. Copper can be obtained from various dietary sources, including shellfish, coarse grains, animal organs, nuts, and green leafy vegetables30. Our study suggests that consuming an appropriate amount of copper through diet can help prevent the occurrence of peripheral neuropathy. Specifically, an intake of around 0.889 milligrams of copper per day is optimal for preventing peripheral neuropathy. In fact, there is currently limited research on peripheral neuropathy. However, our study found that the incidence rate of peripheral neuropathy is very high. Advanced peripheral neuropathy often leads to serious complications such as nerve ulcers and even amputations31. Reducing the risk of peripheral neuropathy through diet is a highly cost-effective method. Meanwhile, this study further supplements the exploration of the relationship between dietary trace elements and peripheral neuropathy.

Nevertheless, it is essential to recognize the limitations of our research. Firstly, our study adopts a cross-sectional design. Therefore, we are unable to assess the specific causal relationship between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy. Secondly, the NHANES data from 1999 to 2004, which we used for analysis, lacked specific information on serum copper levels, preventing us from assessing the impact of serum copper on peripheral neuropathy. Thirdly, the assessment of dietary copper intake relied on the 24-hour recall method, which may not accurately reflect long-term copper intake levels in individuals, and the copper content in the same food may vary by region32.

Conclusion

Our research revealed a U-shaped nonlinear association between dietary copper intake and peripheral neuropathy, indicating that both insufficient and excessive copper consumption may be linked to an elevated risk of developing peripheral neuropathy. These findings offer valuable insights for the general population to maintain an optimal dietary copper intake. However, further longitudinal studies and clinical trials are required to establish a causal relationship and gain a deeper understanding of the implications of copper intake on peripheral neuropathy.
