Relationships between resilience, perceived social support, and mental health in military personnel: a cross-lagged analysis
Authors: Bin Liu, Lin Liu, Mingxuan Zou, Yinchuan Jin, Lei Song, Lei Ren, Mengze Li, Yuting Feng, Fengzhan Li, Qun Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the growing application of the Dual-factor model of mental health (DFM), there is a paucity of research on military personnel. Additionally, existing cross-sectional studies indicate that resilience and perceived social support are associated with military mental health, but the lack of cross-lagged studies precludes researchers from making causal interpretations. This study aimed to explore the longitudinal relationship between resilience, perceived social support, negative dimension of mental health (depressive symptom), and positive dimension (life satisfaction) among military personnel using cross-lagged analysis.MethodsA total of 215 military personnel were investigated longitudinally over a 12-week, two-phase period. The Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS), Patients’ Health Questionnaire Depression Scale-9 Item (PHQ-9), and Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) were employed to measure their resilience, perceived social support, depression symptoms, and life satisfaction, respectively. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the correlations between the aforementioned variables, and a paired t-test was employed to ascertain whether the variables changed over time. A cross-lagged panel analysis was employed to explore cross-lagged relationships between these variables in military personnel.ResultsAll participants in this study were male. At Time 1, the mean age of all participants was 22.98 years (SD = 2.99), and the mean scores for the CD-RISC, PSSS, PHQ-9, and SWLS were 71.61 (SD = 14.86), 68.60 (SD = 10.22), 1.74 (SD = 2.89), and 28.08 (SD = 5.51), respectively. Cross-lagged analysis showed that prior resilience significantly predicted later depression symptoms (β = -0.19, p = 0.028) and life satisfaction (β = 0.17, p = 0.038). Prior perceived social support significantly predicted later life satisfaction (β = 0.15, p = 0.040) but not depression symptoms (β = -0.04, p = 0.652). Prior depression symptoms significantly predicted later life satisfaction (β = -0.26, p < 0.001), and prior life satisfaction also predicted later depression symptoms (β = -0.23, p = 0.002).ConclusionsThe negative and positive dimensions of military mental health can interact with each other. Moreover, enhancing resilience and perceived social support may be a novel perspective for improving the mental health of military personnel.

Introduction

The dual-factor model theory of mental health

With the continuous progress of technology and the improvement of people’s quality of material life, mental health is receiving more and more attention. Prior to the 1960s, it was widely believed that the absence of psychosomatic illness was considered to be mental health, but with the continuous development of positive psychology, the traditional concept of using only psychopathological symptoms to measure mental health has been broken. In 2001, Greenspoon and Saklofske proposed the Dual-factor model theory of mental health (DFM) [1], which pointed out that individual mental health was the co-existence of the two states of eliminating the symptoms of negative mental illnesses and possessing a high degree of subjective wellbeing, in other words, mental health should contain two dimensions: negative dimension and positive dimension [2]. As an important part of the army’s fighting strength, the mental health of military personnel is an important prerequisite for accomplishing all kinds of military and non-military tasks, as well as an important guarantee for victory in future wars. Hence, it is essential to comprehend and assess the mental health of military personnel from the perspective of the DFM.

Negative and positive dimensions of DFM in military personnel

Negative indicators of mental health assessment mainly include various symptoms of mental illnesses, such as depression, anxiety, and neurasthenia [3]. In existing DFM studies, depression symptom is frequently regarded as a typical negative indicator of mental health [4, 5]. Depression symptom is an emotional problem characterized by significant and persistent slow thinking, slowness of speech, and a depressed state of mind, ranging from mild negative emotional experiences to severe mental disorders [6]. Due to occupational specificities, military personnel are often exposed to high-intensity training environments, high-closure management environments, and high-risk military environments, resulting in a higher incidence of depression symptoms in their service members. A study that included the Chinese Army, Navy, Air Force, and Armed Police found that the prevalence of depression symptoms among Chinese military personnel was 18.1%, which is significantly higher than the 6.8% in the general population [7, 8]. In addition, a survey showed that the incidence of depression symptoms in highland military personnel was as high as 37.26%, and their depression symptoms were aggravated with the prolongation of the stationing time [9]. For military personnel, depression symptoms are associated with a range of adverse outcomes, including physical health problems [10], comorbidity with other mental health conditions [11], and even suicide behavior [12].

Positive indicators of mental health assessment mainly include subjective well-being, life satisfaction, meaning of life, self-efficacy, self-esteem, and so on [13]. Life satisfaction is defined as the individual’s comprehensive evaluation of their present circumstances, as defined by their own standards, and belongs to the cognitive component of subjective well-being [14]. Since the 1970s, the field of life satisfaction has become a prominent area of research. A substantial body of research has demonstrated that life satisfaction most closely reflects the subjective well-being of the Chinese population and thus is regarded as a classic positive indicator of mental health [15]. Studies revealed that 73.5% of the U.S. Army and 68.7% of the Chinese Army were satisfied with their respective lives [16, 17]. Higher life satisfaction is beneficial to the generation of fighting forces, whereas lower life satisfaction can have a serious impact on the level of psychological well-being of military personnel, troop morale, and the efficiency of military operations [16].

Resilience and perceived social support

Resilience is the ability of an individual to return to a positive state after suffering a major shock or significant stress [18]. The psychological resilience process model proposed by Stewart [19] suggests that individuals generate a psychological protective mechanism when facing stress and that individuals dynamically enhance their adaptive capacity by reshaping their relationship with the surrounding environment. Individuals with higher levels of resilience are able to fully mobilize their internal resources when encountering negative events, which enables them to effectively cope with and adapt to challenging situations [20]. Hence, resilience is regarded as a crucial internal resource for individuals to combat mental problems [21]. Previous studies have found that the level of resilience is significantly negatively correlated with the level of depression symptoms [22, 23], and significantly positively correlated with life satisfaction [24, 25].

In addition, social support from family members, friends, colleagues, and peers can also assist individuals in alleviating psychological stress, enhancing social adaptation, and promoting mental health [26]. Social support can be classified into objective social support and perceived social support according to its nature. Objective social support can be defined as the tangible assistance that an individual receives from family, friends, or important others. Conversely, perceived social support can be understood as an individual’s subjective perception of the emotional support, respect, and understanding they receive from their external social environment [27]. Perceived social support has been found to be stronger than objective social support in predicting individuals’ psychology with behavior, buffering against stress, and maintaining mental health [28–30]. The buffering and direct effect hypotheses of social support posit that individuals’ perceived social support can mitigate the negative impacts of stress on mental health [31]. If individuals do not effectively perceive social support, they are unable to draw on external support to buffer the impacts of stressful events, which may increase the likelihood of suffering from mental health impairments. It has been found that the level of perceived social support is significantly negatively correlated with the level of depression symptoms [32, 33] and significantly positively correlated with life satisfaction [29, 34]. It can be seen that resilience and perceived social support can respectively mobilize an individual’s internal and external resources, and are therefore considered important protective factors for mental health [35, 36].

The current study

Regrettably, the existing research on the DFM mainly focuses on the general population [37], and lacks research on the military population, which is unclear about the relationship between the positive and negative dimensions of military mental health. Secondly, most previous studies have only explored the effects of resilience or perceived social support on the single dimension of military mental health (for example, the effects of resilience on depression symptoms in military personnel) [23], and there is a lack of comprehensive research on how resilience and perceived social support together affect the two dimensions of military mental health. Thirdly, most of the previous studies are cross-sectional [38, 39], lacking empirical evidence of dynamic tracking, which makes it difficult to provide evidence for the direction of the effects among those variables.

Based on the above problems, the current study constructed a conceptual model (see Fig. 1) to perform cross-lagged analysis, with the aim of exploring the longitudinal relationship between resilience, perceived social support, negative dimension of mental health (depressive symptom), and positive dimension of mental health (life satisfaction) among military personnel, thereby offering theoretical support for the enhancement of the mental health of military personnel.

Methods

Participants

The cluster convenient sampling method was employed. Two companies from a military unit in Xi’an, China were selected as participants in a 12-week, two-stage follow-up survey. The inclusion criteria for the study were: (a) active military personnel; (b) comprehension of the purpose and content of the study; and (c) consent to participate in the study. The first survey (Time1, T1) was administered in April 2023, with 230 questionnaires collected and 226 valid questionnaires retained after screening based on the usual criteria for invalid data (e.g., incorrectly answered polygraph questions, less than 75% response rate to total questions). The second survey (Time2, T2) was administered in July 2023, with 225 questionnaires collected and 222 valid questionnaires retained after screening using the same method as in the first survey. T1 and T2 data were matched by basic demographic information, and 215 data were used as the final sample for analysis. All subjects were male with a mean age of 22.98 years (SD = 2.99). The demographic characteristics are shown in Table 1. This study has been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of the Fourth Military Medical University (No.KY20202063-F-2). Informed consents were obtained from all the respondents for this study.

Measures

Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC)

The CD-RISC, developed by Connor and Davidson [40], is a classic self-assessment instrument for measuring resilience. This study employed the Chinese version of the scale, which was translated and revised by Yu [41]. The scale comprises three dimensions: tenacity, strength, and optimism, with a total of 25 items. Items were rated on a 5-point scale (0 = not at all, 4 = always). The final score of the scale was the sum of the scores of each item, and the higher the final score, the higher the level of resilience of the individual. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for this scale in the present study were 0.949 (T1) and 0.964 (T2).

Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS)

The PSSS was initially developed by Zimet [42] and subsequently revised by Huang [43]. The scale comprised 12 items, encompassing three dimensions: family support, friend support, and other support. Items were rated on a 7-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 4 = strongly agree). The final score of the scale was the sum of the scores for each item, with a higher final score indicating a higher level of perceived social support. The Cronbach’s α coefficients in this study were 0.943 (T1) and 0.967 (T2).

Patients’ Health Questionnaire Depression Scale-9 item (PHQ-9)

The PHQ-9 was developed based on the nine diagnostic criteria for depression disorder in the DSM-IV, which was widely used in clinical work and scientific research [44]. The scale consisted of 9 items and was rated on a 4-point scale (0 = never, 3 = almost every day). The final score of the scale was the sum of the scores of each item, and the higher the final score, the more severe the level of depression symptoms. The total score of the scale ranged from 0∼27, where 0∼4 was no depression symptom, 5∼9 was mild depression symptom, 10∼14 was moderate depression symptom, 15∼19 was moderately severe depression symptom, and 20∼27 was severe depression symptom [45]. The Cronbach’s α coefficients in this study were 0.897 (T1) and 0.908 (T2).

Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS)

The SWLS was initially developed by Diener [46] and subsequently revised by Xiong [47]. The scale consisted of 5 items and was rated on a 7-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). The final score was the sum of the scores for each item, with a higher final score indicating a higher level of life satisfaction. The total score ranged from 5∼35, where 5∼19 indicated dissatisfaction with life, 20 indicated neutrality, 21∼25 indicated slight satisfaction, 26∼30 indicated satisfaction, and 31∼35 indicated great satisfaction [48]. The Cronbach’s α coefficients in this study were 0.887 (T1) and 0.904 (T2).

Quality control

In this study, the researcher initially informed and explained the purpose of the study to the participants. Once informed consent had been obtained, the participants completed the questionnaires on the spot. Prior to completing the questionnaires, the participants were informed that the data collected would be utilized exclusively for scientific research purposes and that their personal information would be maintained in strict confidence. They were also advised that all items should be answered following their genuine sentiments. All questionnaires were preceded by standardized instructions. In the event of any confusion regarding the items, the researchers provided clarifications in accordance with the instructions outlined in the original scales.

Analytic strategy

The valid data were imported into SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk NY, USA) for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were performed on the data, data are presented as n (%) for categorical variables and mean (SD) for numerical variables. Relationships between variables were explored using Pearson correlation analysis. A paired t-test was used to explore whether resilience, perceived social support, depression symptoms, and life satisfaction changed over time in military personnel. A cross-lagged panel analysis using Mplus version 8.0 (Muthen & Muthen, Los Angeles, CA, USA) was used to explore cross-lagged relationships between resilience, perceived social support, depression symptoms, and life satisfaction in military personnel.

After comprehensively considering the simplicity and scientificity of the model, we employed resilience as the latent variable and the other three as the observed variables to construct the cross-lagged model. As shown in Fig. 2, a total of four models were constructed for comparison in this study, including a stability model (Model 1), two one-way models (Model 2, Model 3), and a two-way model (Model 4). In light of the findings of previous studies, age and education level were identified as two significant confounders affecting the mental health of military personnel. An umbrella review of adverse health in military and law enforcement personnel indicated that age was a significant factor in their level of health, including mental health [49]. Furthermore, a cross-sectional study revealed that the stigma of having mental health problems and the fear of the negative effects of seeking mental healthcare on a career were prevalent among military personnel, and these beliefs significantly increased with age [50]. This may result in an exacerbation of psychological problems with age among military personnel. A meta-analysis demonstrated that military personnel with a senior high school education had lower levels of mental health than those with a bachelor’s degree, indicating that educational level also exerted an influence on the mental health of military personnel [51]. Hence, we employed age and education level as covariates in all the cross-lagged models (Model 1–4), aiming to control for the influence of potential confounders on the results. The model fit was evaluated using the chi-square (χ2) test of model fit, comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standard root mean square (SRMR) [52]. According to previous studies [53], χ2/df < 5, CFI and TLI greater than 0.90, RMSEA and SRMR less than 0.08 indicated that the model fit is acceptable. For the above analysis, we set the significance level at 0.05.

Results

Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis

In the present survey, the detection rates of depression symptoms in military personnel were 13.96% (mild depression symptoms = 11.63%, moderate depression symptoms = 2.33%) and 16.28% (mild depression symptoms = 15.35%, moderate depression symptoms = 0.93%) at T1 and T2, respectively. The percentages of military personnel who were satisfied with their lives were 88.83% (slightly satisfied = 13.02%, satisfied = 44.65%, very satisfied = 31.16%) and 92.09% (slightly satisfied = 18.60%, satisfied = 33.49%, very satisfied = 40.00%) at T1 and T2, respectively.

Pearson correlation analysis showed significant longitudinal correlations between the same variables at two time points: resilience (r = 0.65), perceived social support (r = 0.61), depression symptoms (r = 0.56), and life satisfaction (r = 0.62, p < 0.001, respectively). The results of the simultaneous correlation analysis showed that resilience, perceived social support, depression symptoms, and life satisfaction were significantly correlated with each other at both T1 and T2. The above results indicated that those variables were suitable for cross-lagged analysis [54]. The detailed results are shown in Table 2.

Main results

The cross-lagged results showed that T1 resilience significantly predicted T2 resilience (β = 0.60, p < 0.001), T1 perceived social support significantly predicted T2 perceived social support (β = 0.36, p < 0.001), T1 depression symptom significantly predicted T2 depression symptom (β = 0.33, p < 0.001), and T1 life satisfaction significantly predicted T2 life satisfaction (β = 0.29, p < 0.001). These results indicated that all prior variables can significantly predict their subsequent variables.

Cross-lagged regression results showed that T1 resilience significantly predicted T2 perceived social support (β = 0.24, p = 0.005), T2 depression symptom (β = −0.20, p = 0.020), and T2 life satisfaction (β = 0.17, p = 0.033); T1 perceived social support significantly predicted T2 life satisfaction (β = 0.15, p = 0.038), but did not significantly predict T2 psychological resilience (β = 0.09, p = 0.270) or T2 depression symptoms (β = −0.03, p = 0.682); T1 depression symptom significantly predicted T2 perceived social support (β = −0.13, p = 0.041) and T2 life satisfaction (β = −0.26, p < 0.001), but did not significantly predict T2 resilience (β = −0.06, p = 0.300); T1 life satisfaction significantly predicted T2 depression symptom (β = −0.23, p = 0.002), but did not significantly predict T2 resilience (β = 0.01, p = 0.880) and T2 perceived social support (β = 0.05, p = 0.527). The specific results are shown in Fig. 3. These above results indicated that the resilience in military personnel significantly predicted both negative and positive dimensions of mental health, and that the perceived social support significantly predicted the positive dimension of mental health, and that the negative and positive dimensions of mental health could predict each other.

Discussion

The development of resilience, perceived social support, and two dimensions of mental health in military personnel

Through a 12-week longitudinal follow-up, the results showed that resilience and perceived social support of military personnel at two time points displayed different levels of significant correlation. Furthermore, we found that resilience and perceived social support demonstrated developmental characteristics, showing varying degrees of upward trends. According to positive psychology, individuals have the instinct to pursue self-improvement, and with the increasing life experience and rational cognition, their psychological qualities and mental abilities will continue to develop [55]. It is noteworthy that the resilience of military personnel increased significantly after 12 weeks, while perceived social support did not. This phenomenon may be related to the task allocation at different times and regular mental health education activities. On the one hand, the extant literature proposes that an individual’s level of resilience is not fixed, but rather dynamically changes when facing stressful events [56, 57]. The professional attributes of military personnel determine that they may undertake disparate tasks at different times, and these tasks with different pressures may have varying degrees of impact on their resilience. This assertion can be verified in previous literature. One study investigated changes in resilience over the course of a deployment cycle at four-time points among U.S. Army soldiers assigned to a combat brigade, including assessments conducted prior to deployment, during deployment, immediately following return to home station, and approximately 2–3 months thereafter [58]. The growth curve modeling indicated that self-reported resilience decreased across the deployment cycle, indicating that stressful and dangerous tasks had a significant impact on resilience. It can be reasonably inferred that military personnel may have taken on tasks of different intensity and danger between the two data collection periods, which may have resulted in significant discrepancies between the two measurements. On the other hand, the growing focus on the mental health of military personnel has led to the incorporation of mental health education as a regular activity in the military, which may also be a significant contributing factor to the improvement of their resilience [59].

In addition, the results indicated that the negative and positive dimensions of mental health also exhibited varying degrees of correlation and development at two time points. The negative dimension of mental health in military personnel demonstrated a downward trend, while the positive dimension exhibited an upward trend, indicating an overall upward trend of mental health among military personnel. The findings of this study align with those of previous research in this field. A cross-temporal meta-analysis of changes in the mental health status of Chinese military personnel showed that their mental health status has been improving over time [51]. Similarly, another cross-temporal meta-analysis of the mental health status of Chinese plateau military personnel also revealed an upward trend in their mental health status [60]. The occurrence of this phenomenon may be related to the dissemination of mental health education, the enhancement of the quality of life for military personnel, and the intensification of focus on the mental health of military personnel [61].

Cross-lagged analysis of resilience and perceived social support

The results of the cross-lagged analysis indicated that resilience was a unidirectional predictor of perceived social support in military personnel. Resilience, as a personality trait, may influence perceived social support by influencing how perceived supportive behaviors are received, eliciting different responses from others, and fostering individuals’ positive behaviors that align with their personalities [62, 63]. Individuals with high resilience tend to display optimism and self-confidence, which easily elicit supportive responses from others, leading to an enhanced perception of social support [64]. Moreover, individuals with high resilience tend to maintain positive relationships with others and are more likely to seek social resources for support when they face threatening or stressful events [65]. Additionally, it has been proposed that resilience, as an important internal psychological factor, may play a mediating role between the external environment and individual behavior. Consequently, resilience influences the manner in which individuals cope with negative events and adverse circumstances. Those with high resilience are more likely to adopt positive strategies and are more willing to seek assistance from family members, friends, or colleagues when faced with challenges [8]. Our findings align with those of prior studies conducted in other populations. In a survey of caregivers of older adults, Ong et al. [66] found that resilience had a positive predictive effect on perceived social support. In a similar vein, Chen et al. [67] explored the relationships between resilience, social support, spirituality, and quality of life in advanced cancer survivors, and the results also indicated that resilience was a positive predictor of perceived social support.

The present study found that the perceived social support in military personnel did not significantly predict resilience, which was not consistent with findings of previous studies conducted in other populations. A longitudinal study of social support, family psychological resilience, and psychological resilience in maintenance hemodialysis patients showed that perceived social support significantly and positively predicted subsequent psychological resilience [68]. In a similar vein, Costa et al. [69] found that perceived social support was a predictor of higher resilience in patients with colorectal cancer. Sippel et al. [70] put forth the proposition that positive social support systems can serve to bolster resilience, doing so by means of enhancing self-confidence or stimulating the parasympathetic nervous system and other neurobiological mechanisms. The discrepancy between the results of the present study and those of prior studies may be attributed to the following factors. On the one hand, this may be attributed to the fact that an individual’s level of resilience is not a fixed attribute, but rather exhibits dynamic fluctuations when confronted with stressful circumstances [56]. During these fluctuations, resilience enables the individual to regulate and adapt to the changes that occur as a result of these stressful circumstances [56, 57]. On the other hand, due to the specificity of the occupation, military personnel primarily derive social support from their comrades or superiors [71, 72]. This social support may be deficient in the crucial element of familial support, which could potentially impact its predictive capacity.

Cross-lagged analysis of negative and positive dimensions of mental health

In accordance with the DFM, this study employed depression symptoms as the negative dimension and life satisfaction as the positive dimension of military mental health. The results of cross-lagged analyses revealed a bidirectional predictive effect between the negative and positive dimensions of military mental health. Specifically, depression symptoms significantly predicted life satisfaction, and life satisfaction also significantly predicted depression symptoms. On the one hand, individuals experiencing more severe depressive symptoms tend to report stronger negative emotional experiences. These negative emotions can narrow an individual’s attention and cognition to responding to direct threats [73], thus impairing life satisfaction [74]. Depression symptoms can also lead to a variety of physical symptoms such as headaches and stomachaches, and this physical discomfort can further impair life satisfaction [75]. In addition, depression symptoms can impair sleep quality [76], which is an essential component of life satisfaction [77]. Thus, the depression symptoms significantly negatively predicted life satisfaction in military personnel. On the other hand, life satisfaction also significantly negatively predicted depression symptoms. The reason for this may be due to the fact that the higher the level of life satisfaction, the more positive emotions are experienced [78]. The broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions [79] proposes that positive emotions are evolved adaptations that function to build lasting resources. Abundant psychological resource is an important factor in preventing and improving depression symptoms [79]. Furthermore, life satisfaction has been found to influence psychological capital, including self-esteem [80] and self-efficacy [81]. Individuals with greater psychological capital are better equipped to handle and cope with stressful events, which in turn leads to a reduction in depressive symptoms [81].

These findings are in line with previous studies. A 15-year longitudinal study examined the temporal associations between depression symptoms, self-esteem, and life satisfaction. The results indicated that life satisfaction was a protective factor for future depression symptoms, while depression symptoms were a significant risk factor for lower life satisfaction [82]. Another 15-year follow-up study of life satisfaction and depression symptoms in healthy adults also demonstrated that life satisfaction and depression symptoms negatively predicted each other [83]. It can be seen that the negative and positive dimensions of mental health are inseparable and interactive. Therefore, when assessing the mental health of military personnel, it is essential to simultaneously consider both the negative and positive dimensions, rather than focusing on a single dimension, in order to gain a comprehensive understanding of their mental health status.

The relationship between resilience, perceived social support and mental health in military personnel

The results indicated that resilience significantly predicted both negative and positive dimensions of mental health in military personnel. Firstly, resilience can significantly negatively predict the negative dimension of mental health. As a protective factor, resilience can buffer the negative impact of stressful events, thereby reducing adverse reactions and ultimately decreasing the incidence of negative emotions such as depression [84]. Prior research has demonstrated that the buffering effect of psychological resilience persists even after exposure to severe stressors, and continues to play a pivotal role in maintaining mental health and preventing depression symptoms [85]. The capacity for resilience also influences an individual’s propensity to cope with adverse circumstances. Those with high levels of resilience tend to gravitate towards positive coping strategies, which not only facilitate the successful treatment of negative events but also serve to mitigate the severity of depressive symptoms [86]. Secondly, resilience significantly positively predicts the positive dimension of mental health in military personnel. In the context of negative events, resilience enables individuals to mobilize their strengths and resources to mitigate the adverse effects of negative events [20]. This ultimately enhances life satisfaction by decreasing the probability of negative consequences (Counteracting effect) and increasing the probability of positive outcomes (Compensating effect) [87]. In addition, resilience enables individuals to perceive events in a more favorable light, which in turn leads to the experience of more positive emotions in both life and work [88]. These positive emotions extend the individual’s thoughts and ultimately promote mental well-being and life satisfaction [79]. In addition, resilience can contribute to the positive dimension of mental health by influencing variables such as subjective well-being [89] and self-evaluation [90]. Our findings are consistent with previous studies. Hourani et al. [91] examined the longitudinal impact of resilience on mental health problems among Marines leaving military service and found that resilience positively predicted mental health. Similarly, Eisen et al. [92] conducted a survey of military personnel returning from Afghanistan and Iraq and found that resilience was a positive predictor of mental health in veterans.

The results indicated that perceived social support significantly predicted the positive dimension of mental health in military personnel. Firstly, the degree of social support perceived by individuals affects the level of fulfillment of their basic psychological needs (including autonomy needs, belongingness needs, and competence needs), which serve as the foundation for individual growth and improvement [93]. The level of fulfillment of basic needs contributes to the enhancement of life satisfaction and subjective well-being [94]. Furthermore, it has been found that perceived social support improved individuals’ ability to regulate their emotions, which helped them to maintain positive emotions and thus enhance life satisfaction [95]. It is noteworthy that perceived social support did not significantly predict depression, which was inconsistent with previous studies. A 7-year longitudinal study of the relationship between depressive symptoms and social support in Australian men indicated that social support and depression were both a cause and consequence of the other [96]. Another study investigated 272 veterans and found that perceived social significantly negatively predicted depression symptoms [97]. It seems reasonable to posit that this discrepancy may be attributable to the following factors. First, the subjects of this study were military personnel, and negative emotions such as depression are generally considered to be a sign of weakness and incompetence in this group. Consequently, military personnel are more likely to hide their depression and resist care from others, which may reduce the level of perceived social support and impair its effect on depression. Second, due to the particular nature of the occupation, the social support available to military personnel is largely derived from their colleagues or superiors. This is in contrast to the general population, where family support is a significant factor [71]. This may also influence the predictive capacity of social support in this context.

However, there are also the following limitations in this study. Firstly, due to the specificity of the occupation, the participants in this study are all male, and the group of female military personnel can be added in future studies to explore the differences between genders. Secondly, only two time points of data were collected in this study, so it was not possible to determine whether there was a longitudinal mediating effect among the variables, and in the future, data from three or more time points could be collected for the analysis of the longitudinal mediating effect. Lastly, both the negative and positive dimensions of mental health in military personnel contain various aspects, and the present study may not be able to comprehensively reflect the mental health of military personnel by using depression symptoms as the negative dimension and life satisfaction as the positive dimension. Therefore, future studies should add more observational indicators to the two dimensions of mental health and construct a more scientific structural equation modeling (SEM) of military mental health, in order to comprehensively and fully evaluate the mental health of military personnel.

Conclusions

Based on the DFM, the present study found that both positive and negative dimensions of mental health in military personnel interacted with each other. Therefore, in evaluating the mental health of military personnel, it is imperative to simultaneously consider both the negative and positive dimensions, rather than focusing on a single dimension, in order to obtain a comprehensive understanding of their overall mental health. Furthermore, resilience can significantly predict both the positive and negative dimensions of mental health in military personnel, and perceived social support can significantly predict its positive dimension. Hence, the cultivation and enhancement of resilience and perceived social support may be a novel perspective for improving the overall mental health of military personnel.
