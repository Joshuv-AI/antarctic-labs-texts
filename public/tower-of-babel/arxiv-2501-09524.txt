In the theory of complex interpolation one usually defines the interpolation
spaces in question using holomorphic functions taking continuous boundary

The main thesis advanced in this paper is that in many questions is much
more advantageous to consider the space

(of bounded
holomorphic functions) and further that it is in this connection often
easier to work with distributional boundary values rather than with

Our main result in this direction (sec. 4, theorem) says that one gets the
same interpolation space

no matter whether one uses

. (Here and in the sequel
\overline{X}=\left(X_{0},X_{1}\right)
denotes any fixed (compatible) pair of Banach spaces;

characterization of the second Calderon space
\overline{X}^{\theta}

Our new description of

\overline{X}_{\theta}
by substituting the functor

In fact our new proof of his result is almost trivial, modulo that fact that
”reflexive” entails ”RN”. We likewise give a new proof of the Calderon’s
duality theorem
[
5
]
and of Janson’s recent characterization
[
23

Apart from these applications to essentially known results of the Calderon
theory we can use our new insight to treat

versions of various problems traditionally considered for scalar

problem: Given a sequence of points

uniformly separating in the sense of Carleson

of elements of

, satisfying the appropriate boundedness condition, a function
f\in H^{\infty}\left(\overline{X}\right)
(definition in sec. 3) such
that

but a special case of a corresponding problem for the

operator (cf.

21

6.1.

7. A vector valued (or better Banach bundle valued) interpolation
problem.

Let
\left\{z_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq S^{i}
be any (fixed)
sequence
uniformly separating
in the sense of Carleson
[
6
]
. Then by Carleson’s theorem
[
6
]
for any (scalar) sequence
\left\{w_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq l^{\infty}
we can find a
(scalar) function
f\in H^{\infty}
such that
f\left(z_{n}\right)=w_{n}

\left(n=1,2....\right)
.

Now we turn to the corresponding vector valued problem: Given a (vector
valued) sequence
\left\{w_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
to find a function

f\in H^{\infty}\left(\overline{X}\right)
such that
f\left(z_{n}\right)=w_{n}

\left(n=1,2,...\right)
. Clearly a
necessary
condition for this to be possible is that
w_{n}\in\overline{X}_{z_{n}}

\left(n=1,2,...\right)
,
\sup_{n}\left\|w_{n}\right\|_{\overline{X}_{z_{n}}}<\infty
. (
\overline{X}_{z}

\left(z\in S^{i}\right)
denotes of course the space
\overline{X}_{Rez}
.
)\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

Claim.
This necessary condition is
sufficient
too.

To substantiate this claim we will make use of the functions constructed by
Per Beurling (see
[
7
]
): There exists a sequence of (scalar)
functions
\left\{F_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq H^{\infty}
such
that
F_{n}\left(z_{k}\right)=\delta_{nk}

\left(n,k=1,2,...\right)

and
\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|F_{n}\left(z\right)\right|\leq C<\infty

\left(z\in S^{i}\right)
. Then in the scalar case a particular
solution of our interpolation problem is provided by

(
1
)

f\left(z\right)=\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}_{n=1}^{\infty}w_{n}F_{n}\left(z\right).

In the vector valued case we must modify
\left(\text{\ref{eq-7-1}}\right)

somewhat. Indeed by definition (and by the theorem in sec. 4) we can find
functions
f_{n}\in H^{\infty}\left(\overline{X}\right)
such that
f_{n}\left(z_{n}\right)=w_{n}

\left(n=1,2,...\right)
and such that
C^{{}^{\prime}}\overset{def}{=}\sup_{n}\left\|f_{n}\right\|_{H^{\infty}\left(\overline{X}\right)}<\infty
. Then we put

(
1^{{}^{\prime}}
)

f\left(z\right)=\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}_{n=1}^{\infty}f_{n}\left(z\right)F_{n}\left(z\right).

Sub-claim.
The function
f
defined by
\left(\text{\ref{eq-7-1-prim}}\right)
is in
H^{\infty}\left(\overline{X}\right)
and
solves our interpolation problem, i.e.
f\left(z_{n}\right)=w_{n}

\left(n=1,2,...\right)
.

Proof
(of the sub-claim). It is clear that for any
z\in S^{i}

holds
\left\|f_{n}\left(z\right)\right\|_{\Sigma}\leq\left\|f_{n}\right\|_{H^{\infty}\left(\Sigma\right)}\leq\left\|f_{n}\right\|_{H^{\infty}\left(\overline{X}\right)}\leq C^{{}^{\prime}}

\left(n=1,2,...\right)
. Therefore the series
\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}_{n=1}^{\infty}f_{n}\left(z\right)F_{n}\left(z\right)
is normally convergent in
\Sigma
for
z\in S^{i}
and its sum
f\left(z\right)
is clearly a
holomorphic function with
\left\|f\left(z\right)\right\|_{\Sigma}\leq CC^{{}^{\prime}}
. In particular at least
f\in H^{\infty}\left(\Sigma\right)
and
f\left(z_{n}\right)=w_{n}

\left(n=1,2,...\right)
.

It remains as usual to investigate the boundary values. By the principle of
permanence (sec. 2) we have for the boundary functions on
\delta_{0}S
,
say,

\displaystyle f\left(i.\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}f_{n}\left(i.\right)F_{n}\left(i.\right),

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\left|F_{n}\left(i.\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C<\infty,\left\|f_{n}\left(i.\right)\right\|_{X_{0}}\leq C.

Therefore
f\left(i.\right)\in L^{\infty}\left(X_{0}\right)
. Similarly
we prove that
f\left(1+i.\right)\in L^{\infty}\left(X_{1}\right)
. So
that indeed
f\in H^{\infty}\left(\overline{X}\right)
.
\square

As is well-known (see
[
21
]
) the interpolation problem in the
previous sec. is a special case of a problem for the
\overline{\delta}
-operator which in the scalar case can be formulated as follows: Let

]

[
21
]

[

]
), to
fix the ideas

\left(\overline{\delta}=\delta/\delta\overline{z}\right)

by

formed with the sequence
\left\{z_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

We can

This problem always has a solution, in view of the Hahn-Banach theorem (see

[
21
]
), but it is of course not unique. Once an appropriate
substitute for the Per Beurling functions has been found we can however
write down a particular solution. To this end let us consider a kernel
K\left(z,\zeta\right)
assumed to be at least measurable in both
variables (measurable in
\zeta
with respect to
\mu)
such that

\left(i\right):K\left(z,\zeta\right)
is analytic in

for
\zeta

fixed
\mu
a.e.,

\left(ii\right):K\left(\zeta,\zeta\right)=1

\left(iii\right):\mathop{\textstyle\int}K\left(z,\zeta\right)P\left(z,\zeta\right)d\mu\left(\zeta\right)\leq C<\infty

\left(z\in\delta S\right)
.

Here
P\left(z,\zeta\right)

\left(z\in\delta S,\zeta\in S\right)

stands for the Poisson kernel. We define the kernel
H\left(z,\zeta\right)

\left(z\in S,\zeta\in S\right)
by stipulating that
1^{\circ}
it
should for
\zeta
fixed be analytic in

\left|H\left(z,\zeta\right)\right|=P\left(z,\zeta\right)

\left(z\in\delta S,\zeta\in S\right)
. (See
[
24
]
where a fairly
explicit construction of such a kernel

is given.
7
7

7

See note
\left\langle 6\right\rangle.
) Now the desired solution is
obtained as

(
1
)

u\left(z\right)=\int K\left(z,\zeta\right)H\left(z,\zeta\right)w\left(\zeta\right)d\mu\left(\zeta\right)\text{.}

Next let us consider the generalization to the vector valued case. It seems
natural to ask for a solution
u
of
\overline{\delta}u=w\mu
such that
u\left(i.\right)\in L^{\infty}\left(X_{0}\right)
,
u\left(1+i.\right)\in L^{\infty}\left(X_{1}\right)
. What do we then have to require from
w?
(A priori we assume only that
w
is
\Sigma
valued.) A minute’s
reflection gives that we should assume
w\left(\zeta\right)\in\overline{X}_{\zeta}

\mu-
a.e.,
\sup\left\|w\left(\zeta\right)\right\|_{\overline{X}_{\zeta}}<\infty
- to be exact, sup here means
\mu-
ess
sup - along with some measurability condition too. We postpone for a moment
the discussion of the exact form of the latter. Since

\zeta
(
\mu-a.e.)

We can also assume that
\left\|W\left(\cdot,\zeta\right)\right\|_{\overline{X}_{\zeta}}\leq C^{{}^{\prime}}<\infty

\left(\zeta\in S\right)
. Then we set (formally)

(
2
)

u\left(z\right)=\int_{S}K\left(z,\zeta\right)H\left(z,\zeta\right)W\left(z,\zeta\right)d\mu\left(\zeta\right)\text{ }\left(z\in S^{i}\right)\text{.}

Note that this formula
\left(\text{\ref{eq-8-2}}\right)
generalizes both

\left(\text{\ref{eq-8-1}}\right)
above and
\left(\text{\ref{eq-7-1-prim}}\right)

\left(\text{\ref{eq-8-2}}\right)

with a solution of

by the properties
\left(i\right)-\left(ii\right)

\overline{\delta}K\left(\cdot,\zeta\right)H\left(\cdot,\zeta\right)W\left(\cdot,\zeta\right)=w\left(\eta\right)\delta\left(\cdot-\zeta\right),

\displaystyle\left\|u\left(z\right)\right\|_{X_{k}}

\displaystyle\int\left|K\left(z,\zeta\right)\right|P\left(z,\zeta\right)\left\|W\left(z,\zeta\right)\right\|_{X_{k}}d\mu\left(\zeta\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle CC^{{}^{\prime}}\text{ }\left(k=0,1,z\in\delta S\right)

so that we get the correct boundary behavior.

To make all this rigorous we have to make precise the assumptions on
w
.
Let us denote by
\overline{\overline{X}}
the family of Banach spaces
\left(\overline{X}_{\zeta}\right)_{\zeta\in S^{i}}
. Let us also
introduce the norm

\left\|w\right\|=\mu-\text{ess sup}_{\zeta\in S^{i}}\left\|w\left(\zeta\right)\right\|_{\overline{X}_{\zeta}}\text{.}

Definition.
We say that
w
is in

is

can be approximated in the preceding
metric
\left\|w\right\|
with a sequence of functions

of

where each

is a

,

standing for its characteristic function, and the

With this definition it is an easy matter to prove rigorously the following

Proposition.
For any
w\in L^{\infty}\left(\mu,\overline{\overline{X}}\right)
there exists a function

such that
\overline{\delta}u=w\mu
and such that in the

\left(k=0,1\right)
.

Proof:
We assume first that
w

w\left(\zeta\right)=\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}_{v=1}^{N}\chi_{e_{v}}\left(\zeta\right)f_{v}\left(\zeta\right)
where the

are
\mu

f_{v}\in H^{\infty}\left(\overline{X}\right)
.Then we set

\displaystyle u\left(z\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}_{v=1}^{N}f_{v}\left(z\right)\int_{e_{v}}K\left(z,\zeta\right)H\left(z,\zeta\right)d\mu\left(\zeta\right)=

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}_{v=1}^{n}f_{v}\left(z\right)u_{v}\left(z\right)\text{.}

(This is formally
\left(\text{\ref{eq-8-2}}\right)
with
W\left(z,\zeta\right)=\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\chi_{e_{v}}\left(\zeta\right)f_{v}\left(z\right)!)
By
Jones’s results
[
24
]
we certainly have for each

. Therefore we get
\overline{\delta}u=\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}f_{v}\overline{\delta}u_{v}=\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}f_{v}\chi_{e_{v}}\mu=w\mu

(since
\overline{\delta}f_{v}=0!)

Moreover it is easy to see that we have for each

It is now obvious how to treat the general case too. If
\left\{w_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an approximating sequence for

sense of the above definition,
\left\|w-w_{n}\right\|\rightarrow 0

as

and if

is the
corresponding sequence of solutions of

tends to a solution

Remark.
A final comment is in order. The definition of
L^{\infty}\left(\mu,\overline{\overline{X}}\right)
ultra (as well as
the notation) might seem quite

, but it is not; in fact,

first have to change slightly our point of view. Instead of considering the

(until now denoted by
\overline{\overline{X}})
we consider their

set theoretic union
, using for this object the previous symbol
\overline{\overline{X}}
. Then
\overline{\overline{X}}
might be considered
a vector bundle over

(with no topology yet, however):

is a

a fortiori
a vector space. But more,

is in fact in a technical sense (see Fell
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
) a

. In particular
\overline{\overline{X}}
thus indeed carriers a natural topology by its own

[
18
]
,
[
19
]

the Banach bundle structure can quite generally (any vector bundle over a
(usually) locally compact space
\Omega)
be defined by first specifying a
suitable family of ”principal sections”. In our case
\left(\Omega=S^{i}\right)
there is a canonical choice of the principal sections. These

,

. For any Banach bundle
