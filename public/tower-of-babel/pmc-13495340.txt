Discordance Between Hip-Specific PROMs and Lumbar Symptoms After Total Hip Arthroplasty in Patients With Prior Lumbar Fusion
Authors: Robert E. Carrier, Daisy A. Ference, Gabriella N. Capote, Victor H. Hernandez, Michele R. D’Apuzzo, Colin A. McNamara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHip-specific patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are used to assess patient recovery after total hip arthroplasty (THA), but their efficacy in patients with prior lumbar spinal fusion (LSF) remains less clear. This study evaluated the discordance between improvements in hip-specific PROMs and persistent lumbar-related symptoms in patients with prior LSF who underwent THA.MethodsWe performed a single-center retrospective study of patients with prior LSF who underwent THA from January 2015 to October 2025. Multiple PROMs, including PROMIS-10, HOOS Jr., University of California Los Angeles Activity Score, and EQ-5D, were analyzed using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Lumbar-related symptoms were assessed by structured chart review and treated as binary variables.ResultsOverall, 54 total cases from 47 unique patients were identified, including 41 total cases in the primary cohort. Paired baseline and 3-month PROMs were available for 18 total cases. Significant improvements occurred in HOOS Jr. (+27.66, P < .001), PROMIS pain (−3.06, P < .01), PROMIS physical (+6.06, P < .01), and EQ-5D overall health (+17.0, P = .012). However, despite these improvements in multiple PROMs, lumbar-related symptoms still persisted at baseline, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years in 20.0%, 14.3%, 13.3%, 33.3%, and 25.0% of patients, respectively.ConclusionsTHA was associated with significant and meaningful early hip-specific functional improvements in patients with prior LSF. However, lumbar-related symptoms frequently persisted in many patients. This discordance suggests that hip-specific PROMs may incompletely capture postoperative recovery with hip-spine syndrome. However, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.Level of EvidenceLevel IV, Therapeutic study.

Introduction

Degenerative hip and lumbar spine disorders are among the most prevalent musculoskeletal pathologies encountered in orthopaedic practice [1]. The coexistence of these disorders, commonly referred to as hip-spine syndrome, presents a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge due to overlapping symptomatology that can obscure the primary source of pain and functional impairment [2,3]. The dynamic relationship between the hip and the spine, including interplay between spinopelvic alignment, lumbar lordosis, and pelvic tilt, plays a critical role in both native biomechanics and postoperative function. Alterations in this relationship, whether from progressive degenerative changes or surgical intervention such as lumbar spinal fusion (LSF) may influence outcomes following total hip arthroplasty (THA) [4].

Prior literature on hip-spine syndrome has emerged as a rapidly growing focus in the orthopaedic literature, with a strong emphasis on complication profiles and the sequencing of surgical interventions. Several studies have suggested that performing THA prior to LSF may reduce the risk of instability and revision [3,[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. However, clinical decision-making is often driven by symptom predominance, and many patients undergo lumbar fusion prior to arthroplasty [12,13]. While numerous studies have examined the relationship between surgical sequence and postoperative complication rates, less is known about how patients with prior LSF experience recovery following THA, particularly with respect to patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) [14,15].

PROMs provide important insight into patient-perceived benefit and satisfaction following surgery and are increasingly used to guide clinical decision-making and patient counseling. However, in patients with coexisting spinal disease, interpretation of hip-specific PROM scores is difficult, as persistent spinal symptoms can influence perceived recovery independent of hip function. In addition, hip-specific PROMs, such as Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement (HOOS Jr.), primarily assess hip-related pain and function and may demonstrate improvement even when overall functional recovery remains limited by spinal disease.

This study not only examines outcomes but it more specifically examines the interpretation of these outcomes in patients with hip-spine syndrome. We sought to evaluate improvements in hip-specific PROMs and to trend the evolution of lumbar symptom progression following THA in patients with a history of prior LSF. An emphasis was placed on identifying any discordance between hip-specific PROM improvement and persistent lumbar-related symptom burden in this patient population. We hypothesized that THA would result in a meaningful improvement in hip-related pain and function, but at the same time, that lumbar-related symptoms would persist, highlighting a limitation of relying on hip-specific PROMs in patients with hip-spine syndrome.

Material and methods

Study design and patient selection

This was a single-center retrospective observational study of patients who underwent THA in the setting of prior LSF between January 1, 2015, and October 27, 2025. Patients were identified using Current Procedural Terminology codes for instrumented LSF (22558, 22612, 22623, 22630, 22633) and primary THA (27130), followed by manual chart review to confirm procedure details. The primary analytic cohort consisted of patients who underwent THA after prior LSF. A secondary cohort of patients who underwent THA prior to subsequent LSF was also identified and reviewed descriptively to provide clinical context. However, due to the limited availability of PROMs in this group, quantitative comparative analysis was not performed.

Data collection

Demographic (age, sex, race, and ethnicity) and clinical variables (body mass index, American Society of Anesthesiologists classification, and smoking status) were collected at the time of THA. The number of lumbar fusion levels was obtained through operative reports and imaging documentation. When multiple lumbar procedures were performed, the fusion closest in time to the index THA was used for analysis. The interval between LSF and THA was calculated in years based on operative dates.

PROMs were collected through a standardized institutional platform (PatientIQ, Inc., Chicago, IL) and included the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Global-10 (PROMIS-10), HOOS Jr., University of California Los Angeles (UCLA) activity score, and EuroQol 5-dimension (EQ-5D). These assessments are routinely distributed at baseline, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years postoperatively following THA. Surgical timing for all analyses was relative to the date of the THA. For assessment of potential follow-up bias, paired 3-month PROM follow-up was defined as availability of baseline and 3-month HOOS Jr., PROMIS pain, PROMIS physical, and PROMIS mental scores.

Given incomplete PROM capture across follow-up intervals, analyses of PROM changes were restricted to patients with paired baseline and postoperative values at each time point. Patterns of missing data were not assumed to be random, follow-up rates were explicitly reported, and PROM findings were interpreted accordingly.

Structured symptom documentation review

Lumbar-related symptoms were identified through structured chart review and recorded as a binary variable (presence/absence). Symptoms included low back pain, stiffness, radicular symptoms, and functional limitations attributed to lumbar pathology. Symptom severity and frequency were not graded due to limitations of retrospective documentation.

Chart review was conducted by a single reviewer using a standardized abstraction approach. Interobserver reliability could not be assessed due to the single-observer nature of our extraction technique. Clinical documentation was also reviewed to qualitatively characterize postoperative trends in hip-related function and pain, supplementing PROM data on pain relief, mobility, and return to activities of daily living. This qualitative analysis was exploratory and intended to provide a clinical context for the quantitative PROM findings. Follow-up visits were reviewed if they occurred within ±1 month of the predefined postoperative intervals.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported for the primary cohort only (patients who underwent primary THA following prior LSF). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as counts with percentages. Follow-up rates were reported as the number of documented follow-up visits divided by the total number of patients in the primary cohort number.

Changes in PROMs were analyzed using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Given the limited sample size and incomplete data capture, statistical analyses were considered exploratory, with emphasis placed on the magnitude, direction, and variability of change rather than statistical significance alone.

Spearman rank correlations were calculated to assess the association between patient satisfaction, PROM changes, and the interval between procedures to PROM improvement. Exploratory multivariable linear regression models were constructed to evaluate for associations between age at THA, number of fusion levels, and the time interval between procedures with changes in select PROM domains (HOOS Jr., PROMIS pain, and PROMIS physical). To explore potential selection bias, baseline characteristics were also compared between cases that had vs cases that lacked paired baseline and 3-month PROM data using Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and Fisher exact tests for categorical variables. Given the limited number of observations, all models were interpreted cautiously and considered descriptive.

The proportion of patients with documented lumbar-related symptoms was reported at each follow-up time point. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. All analyses were performed using Python (SciPy v1.11). Institutional Review Board approval was obtained prior to study initiation.

PROM availability and follow-up

PROM data were not captured uniformly across follow-up time points. Within the primary cohort, HOOS Jr. scores were available in 20 of 41 cases (48.8%) at baseline and in 19 of 41 cases (46.3%) at 3 months, with progressively fewer cases at 6 months (8 of 41 cases, 19.5%), 1 year (7 of 41 cases, 17.1%), and 2 years (4 of 41 cases, 9.8%). From the 3-month paired PROM analysis which was performed (baseline and 3-month HOOS Jr., PROMIS pain, PROMIS physical, and PROMIS mental), 18 of 41 cases (43.9%) were available. Patients with paired 3-month PROM data were less likely to have a smoking history than those without paired follow-up data (P = .013), whereas all other collected baseline characteristics showed no significant differences (see Supplementary Table 1). However, given this attrition rate, collected PROM findings should be interpreted as exploratory and as susceptible to selection bias.

Patient-reported outcomes

Patients with paired baseline and 3-month follow-up data (n = 18) demonstrated significant improvements across several observed domains (Table 2). HOOS Jr. scores improved by a mean of +27.66 (P < .001), PROMIS Physical scores increased by +6.06 (P < .01), and PROMIS pain scores improved (decreased) by −3.06 (P < .01). EQ-5D overall health scores increased by +17.0 (P = .012). PROMIS mental scores demonstrated a mean increase of +0.94 (P = .522), and UCLA activity scores showed minimal change (+0.12, P = .783). Regarding these patients as a whole, 15 of 18 cases (83.3%) improved in HOOS Jr., 14 of 18 (77.8%) improved in PROMIS physical, and 15 of 18 (83.3%) improved in PROMIS pain, although the magnitude of change was variable (3-month HOOS Jr. change range, 0.0 to 69.6). At later follow-up time points (6 months, 1 year, and 2 years), cases with available follow-up data continued to demonstrate improvement in several PROM domains compared with baseline values; however, interpretation of these findings is limited due to very small sample sizes because of diminished follow-up. Lumbar fusion characteristics

Within the primary cohort, a subgroup analysis comparing patients with ≤2 fused lumbar levels vs >2 fused levels did not demonstrate meaningful differences in most PROM domains at the studied time points. PROMIS mental scores were similar between subgroups at baseline and 3 months (P > .05), though a statistically significant difference was observed at 1-year follow-up (P < .01). However, this finding is limited by small sample size.

Exploratory multivariable regression analyses evaluating associations between age, number of fused levels, and interval between procedures with PROM changes at 3 months did not identify significant predictors of improvement (Table 3). Model performance was limited (HOOS Jr.: R2 = 0.034; PROMIS pain: R2 = 0.178; PROMIS physical: R2 = 0.065). Correlation analyses also demonstrated no consistent relationship between the interval between procedures and PROM improvement at early time points (Table 4). A moderate negative correlation was observed between HOOS Jr. improvement and the 6-month surgical interval (ρ = −0.74, P = .037). Longitudinal lumbar symptom documentation

The proportion of patients reporting lumbar-associated symptoms is summarized in Table 5. In the primary cohort, lumbar-related symptoms were documented in 20.0% of patients at baseline, 14.3% at 3 months, 13.3% at 6 months, 33.3% at 1 year, and 25.0% at 2 years (Fig. 1). A transient reduction in lumbar symptoms was reported at 3 and 6 months, followed by increased symptom burdens at 1 and 2 years. It should be noted that later time point findings are constrained by small sample size (n = 9 at 1 year; n = 4 at 2 years). Our findings are additionally limited by the nonstandardized nature of retrospective symptom documentation. We defined "discordance" as any divergence between improvements in hip-specific PROM trajectories and the persistence of documented lumbar symptoms. Our analysis of discordance was performed at the cohort level, not at the patient level. Subjective documentation narrative synthesis

A review of clinical documentation demonstrated consistent improvement in hip-related symptoms across the primary cohort, including reduced hip pain, improved ambulation, and a return to activities of daily living. Several patients described increased walking distances and improved functional mobility during postoperative follow-up. However, over the full course of follow-up, a meaningful subset of patients still reported persistent lumbar-related complaints. The most reported lumbar-related complaints were low back pain, stiffness, radiculopathy, and functional impairment.

Secondary descriptive cohort (THA prior to lumbar fusion)

Thirteen cases of THA performed prior to subsequent LSF were identified and reviewed descriptively. PROM data were limited in this cohort and insufficient for quantitative analysis. The secondary cohort exhibited patterns similar to the primary cohort with improvement in hip-related symptoms following THA, and with ongoing reports of lumbar-related symptoms during follow-up (Table 6). Discussion

Hip-spine syndrome is a complex and frequently underappreciated clinical pathology that warrants increased attention in orthopaedic practice. Many patients with arthritis of the hip and the spine ultimately require both THA and LSF. In these instances, most guidelines recommend that THA be performed first to decrease the risk of dislocation and revision [[14], [15], [16]]. However, for many patients in whom lower back symptoms are the dominant complaint, LSF is often performed first. PROMs provide a structured measure of patient-perceived outcomes and are essential in patient counseling and in managing surgical expectations. Hip-specific PROMs are increasingly used to assess patient recovery after THA. However, it is less clear whether hip-specific PROMs can be accurately applied to patients with a history of previous LSF. This is the key question addressed in this study: whether hip-specific PROMs accurately reflect postoperative experience in patients with prior LSF following THA.

Our study evaluated patient-reported outcomes and longitudinal symptom patterns following THA in patients with a history of prior LSF. The primary findings were that improvement in hip-specific PROMs following THA does not necessarily correspond with resolution of lumbar-related symptoms in a substantial subset of patients with prior LSF. While most patients demonstrated significant improvements in hip-specific measures, a meaningful subset of our patients continued to report lumbar-related symptoms throughout all postoperative follow-up time points. Taken together, these findings highlight a divergence between hip-specific functional recovery and overall symptom burden in patients with hip-spine syndrome.

Patients who underwent THA after prior LSF demonstrated notable improvements in hip-specific PROMs, including HOOS Jr., PROMIS physical, PROMIS pain, and EQ-5D scores at early follow-up. The mean HOOS Jr. improvement was 27.7 points. This exceeds commonly cited HOOS Jr. minimal clinically important difference thresholds for THA [17] and supports the clinical relevance of the early hip-specific gains observed in our study. These findings reinforce that THA remains an effective intervention for addressing hip-related pathology, even in the presence of prior lumbar fusion.

However, hip-specific PROMs were less useful in identifying persistent lumbar pathology. This may be explained by multiple biomechanical factors. LSF alters normal spinopelvic motion, increasing stiffness in the lower back and resulting in increased mechanical stress across adjacent segments with persistent limitations in sagittal mobility [4,14]. Persistent or progressive lumbar disease—including foraminal stenosis or neural compression—may continue to contribute to functional limitation despite successful hip arthroplasty [18].

The fact that patients may report high scores on HOOS Jr. or PROMIS physical questionnaires while continuing to experience substantial lumbar-related limitations has important implications for both clinical care and research. From a clinical standpoint, reliance on hip-specific PROMs alone may lead to overestimation of recovery in patients with coexisting spine pathology. From a research perspective, studies evaluating outcomes in hip-spine populations may benefit from incorporating spine-specific measures, such as the Oswestry disability index, as well as objective assessments of spinopelvic alignment and mobility. Standard hip outcome measures are not designed to capture lumbar-specific symptomatology. As a result, hip-specific PROMs may not accurately reflect postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing THA with a history of prior LSF.

Exploratory analyses did not identify significant associations between patient characteristics, fusion levels, or the interval between procedures and PROM improvement. However, these analyses were markedly underpowered and should be interpreted in the lens of our limited sample size and incomplete PROM capture. Similarly, while a relationship between surgical interval and functional improvement was observed at select time points, the small number of observations precludes definitive conclusions. These analyses are intended to be descriptive and should be best utilized to guide future studies, rather than to guide current practice.

Limitations

Our findings should be interpreted in the setting of several limitations. Most importantly, different approaches were used to measure hip and spine outcomes. Hip outcomes were assessed using standardized PROMs, whereas lumbar-related symptoms were derived from retrospective clinical documentation and recorded as a binary variable. This directly limits the strength of any conclusions about discordance.

PROM follow-up was incomplete, with only 43.9% of patients having paired baseline and 3-month data. Patients with available follow-up differed in smoking status, suggesting potential selection bias. Follow-up was especially limited at later intervals, with lumbar-symptom documentation available for only 9 patients at 1 year and 4 patients at 2 years. Therefore, apparent fluctuations in lumbar symptom prevalence across follow-up should not be interpreted as stable temporal trends, and the 1- and 2-year estimates should be considered imprecise descriptive observations.

Of note, lumbar symptom burden was not assessed on a quantifiable scale, and data on spine-specific PROMs, spinopelvic parameters, surgical approach and construct, and/or indication for fusion were not collected. In addition, because lumbar-related symptoms were obtained from providers' standard postoperative clinic notes, the absence of documented symptoms cannot be confirmed as an accurate representation of symptom resolution. Provider-dependent inquiry and recording of symptoms may have resulted in under ascertainment of lumbar-related symptoms and incorrect classification of participant symptom status. Similarly, the post hoc exploratory subgroup analyses, correlation analyses, and regression analyses were severely underpowered due to their reliance on small numbers of matched pairs. These analyses are presented strictly to comment on observed trends and should not be misrepresented as determining true predictors of PROM improvement or postoperative resolution of lumbar symptoms. These limitations bias the study toward identifying patterns rather than establishing causal relationships. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Despite these methodological limitations, our results contribute clinically relevant insights pertaining to recovery following THA in the setting of prior LSF. Rather than addressing surgical sequencing or complication rates, this study highlights how outcome interpretation may be limited when only hip-specific instruments are considered. These findings should not be taken to mean that hip-specific PROMs are of little value. Rather, they suggest that hip-only instruments may incompletely characterize recovery when residual lumbar pathology remains in hip-spine patients.

Future studies should aim to prospectively evaluate patients with hip-spine syndrome using comprehensive outcome measures. Incorporation of validated spine-specific PROMs, objective assessment of spinopelvic alignment and mobility, and larger sample sizes will increase our ability to characterize recovery trajectories and optimize clinical decision-making in this complex patient population.

Conclusions

In patients with a history of LSF, THA was associated with meaningful improvement in hip-related pain and function. However, lumbar-related symptoms frequently persist, suggesting that hip-specific PROMs alone may incompletely capture postoperative recovery in patients with hip-spine syndrome. Incorporation of both hip- and spine-specific outcome measures may provide a more comprehensive assessment of recovery. Prospective studies are needed to further characterize this relationship.
