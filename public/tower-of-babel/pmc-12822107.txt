Association between De Ritis ratio and mortality in individuals with diabetes or prediabetes: evidence from NHANES (1999–2018)
Authors: Hui-Dong Long, Ruo-Yue Guo, Jing-Yu Zhou, Yi-Peng Du, Guang-Cheng Liu, Xiu-Di Pan, Zhao-Hua Zeng, Yun-En Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe De Ritis ratio (AST/ALT) has emerged as a potential biomarker for metabolic dysfunction, but its association with mortality in individuals with diabetes or prediabetes remains inadequately explored. This study investigated the relationship between the De Ritis ratio and mortality in this population.MethodsWe analyzed data from 23,873 participants with diabetes or prediabetes from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1999–2018. Participants were stratified into quartiles based on De Ritis ratio values. Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to assess mortality risk, adjusting for demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis examined potential non-linear relationships.ResultsDuring follow-up through December 2019, higher De Ritis ratios were associated with increased mortality risk. In fully adjusted models, the highest quartile showed significantly elevated risks for all-cause mortality (HR: 1.79, 95% CI 1.56–2.05) and CVD mortality (HR: 1.66, 95% CI 1.30–2.12) versus the lowest quartile. Each SD increase in De Ritis ratio corresponded to 26% and 28% increased risks for all-cause and CVD mortality, respectively. The association remained linear across examined values, with the De Ritis ratio showing superior predictive performance compared to individual liver enzymes.ConclusionsElevated De Ritis ratio is independently associated with increased all-cause and CVD mortality in individuals with diabetes or prediabetes, suggesting its potential utility as a prognostic biomarker for clinical risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03628-7.

Introduction

Diabetes has become a critical global health concern, with cases rising significantly in recent decades [1]. According to the International Diabetes Federation’s 10th Diabetes Atlas, 536.6 million adults (10.5% of the global population) had diabetes in 2021, projected to reach 783.2 million (12.2%) by 2045 [2]. Diabetes often develops undetected for years, frequently progressing from prediabetes, a condition where blood glucose concentrations exceed normal ranges yet remain below diagnostic thresholds for diabetes [3]. Prediabetes itself may cause early subclinical damage to cardiac tissue, blood vessels, and kidneys [4, 5]. Diabetes leads to multiple complications including coronary, peripheral vascular, and cerebrovascular diseases, and renal failure [6, 7]. Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are particularly significant, representing the leading cause of death in patients with diabetes [8].

The liver plays a crucial role in glucose regulation through insulin-responsive pathways and glycogen storage [9]. In diabetes, hepatic insulin resistance impairs glucose metabolism and promotes fat deposition (hepatic steatosis), leading to oxidative stress and chronic inflammation that further impair insulin signaling and β-cell function [10, 11]. Given this central role, the De Ritis ratio [aspartate transaminase (AST)/alanine transaminase (ALT)], a biochemical metric used in clinical medicine to assess liver function and disease severity [12], may serve as a valuable indicator for metabolic risk assessment. Research has demonstrated an inverse relationship between the De Ritis ratio and prediabetes risk [13]. Recent findings indicate that elevated De Ritis ratios correspond to reduced likelihood of diabetes progression among prediabetic individuals [14]. Further investigation revealed that decreased De Ritis ratios serve as diabetes-associated markers, with this relationship being mediated through the triglyceride-glucose index [15]. However, higher De Ritis ratios have been correlated with increased all-cause and CVD mortality rates in diabetic populations [16]. The stage of impaired glucose tolerance or early diabetes is marked by insulin resistance and steatohepatitis—metabolic disturbances reflected in a predominant ALT elevation rather than AST [17] and thus, a lower De Ritis ratio. As the disease advances, an increased De Ritis ratio signifies more severe liver injury and functional impairment, including cirrhosis, and is associated with a poorer prognosis [18, 19]. Additionally, patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus show accelerated fibrosis progression [20]. Therefore, the interplay between liver function and diabetes is bidirectional [21], and the De Ritis ratio may serve as a marker capturing this dynamic relationship.

Nevertheless, research investigating the connection between the De Ritis ratio and death-related outcomes among diabetic populations continues to be constrained, lacking confirmation from large cohort studies. Additionally, no dose–response association was determined in the previously mentioned research exploring the link between this biomarker and fatality rates in individuals with diabetes [16]. Furthermore, the correlation between this parameter and mortality among prediabetic patients has yet to be investigated. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) constitutes an extensive cross-sectional study framework established to evaluate health conditions and nutritional parameters among varied American population segments. Therefore, this investigation seeks to leverage this methodologically robust national database to examine the association between the De Ritis ratio and all-cause as well as CVD mortality risk among individuals diagnosed with prediabetes or diabetes.

Methods

Materials and methods: study population

The NHANES, conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), is a continuous nationwide evaluation consisting of biennial cross-sectional surveys that utilize advanced statistical and complex probability sampling techniques to assess nutritional habits and health parameters among non-institutionalized civilian populations in the United States [22]. The study protocol underwent review and received authorization from the Research Ethics Review Board at NCHS [23], with all study subjects providing written consent documentation prior to their participation. Therefore, this analysis was exempt from additional IRB review.

From an initial pool of 101,316 participants in NHANES 1999–2018, the selection process proceeded through multiple exclusion stages. The first screening excluded 74,336 participants without diabetes or prediabetes and 1335 participants with missing data of De Ritis ratio, resulting in 25,645 participants. Further refinement of the cohort involved removing 121 pregnant participants and 1651 participants with missing mortality data. Definition of De Ritis ratio

The De Ritis ratio was calculated by dividing the AST value by the ALT value. The NHANES Laboratory/Medical Technologists Procedures Manual provides detailed protocols for the collection and processing of specimens. The Collaborative Laboratory Services is responsible for receiving, analyzing, and managing the serum samples. Both AST and ALT analyses were conducted using the Beckman UniCel® DxC800 Synchron platform. This system employs a kinetic rate methodology to quantify ALT activity, while it utilizes an enzymatic rate technique for the measurement of AST.

Identification of diabetes and prediabetes

The identification of diabetes and prediabetes cases was based on laboratory measurements and self-reported responses to structured diagnostic questions. For diabetes classification, participants were required to meet at least one of the following conditions: laboratory values showing glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% or fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 126 mg/dL, or self-reported physician diagnosis of diabetes or current insulin use. For prediabetes classification, participants who did not meet diabetes criteria were assessed for either laboratory values within the prediabetes range (HbA1c between 5.7 and 6.4% or FPG between 100 and 125 mg/dL) or an affirmative response to the question about previous prediabetes diagnosis [24]. This comprehensive assessment ensured accurate categorization of participants for subsequent analyses.

Mortality information

We tracked mortality outcomes using the NHANES public-use linked mortality records extending through December 31, 2019. This dataset was generated by matching NCHS records with the National Death Index (NDI) using probabilistic algorithms. Mortality due to specific causes was classified using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), and further grouped based on the UCOD_113 classification system developed by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) for cross-year cause-of-death analysis. According to this system, deaths are categorized into ten major groups, such as heart diseases (codes 054–068) and malignant neoplasms (codes 019–043) [25, 26].

Covariables

The analysis incorporated several categories of control variables. Demographic factors included participant age, sex (male/female), and race/ethnicity (Mexican American, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, other Hispanic, and Other Race). Additional control variables encompassed lifestyle factors such as drinking status (current/former/never) and smoking status (current/former/never). Drinking status was defined as: never (< 12 drinks in lifetime), former (≥ 12 drinks in 1 year and did not drink last year, or did not drink last year but drank ≥ 12 drinks in lifetime), and current (except for never and former). Participants’ smoking status was categorized through questionnaire responses: those who reported currently using tobacco products were classified as current smokers, while individuals who had consumed over 100 cigarettes in their lifetime were designated as former smokers. The analysis also accounted for pre-existing health conditions, such as hypertension. Hypertensive status was established when participants met any of these conditions: previous physician-diagnosed hypertension, current antihypertensive medication use, or blood pressure readings showing systolic ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg [27]. Biochemical parameters evaluated included gamma–glutamyl transferase (GGT, U/L), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, U/L), triglycerides (TG, mg/dL), total cholesterol (TC, mg/dL), low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL–C, mg/dL), high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C, mg/dL), total bilirubin (μmol/L), albumin (g/L), creatinine (μmol/L), serum uric acid (UA, μmol/L), blood urea nitrogen (BUN mmol/L) and HbA1c (%). The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was determined using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaborative (CKD-EPI) equation [28].

Statistical analyses

For data analysis, we utilized the R statistical environment (version 4.4.1), employing the ‘survey’ package’s specialized functions to appropriately analyze complex survey information. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 (two-tailed) was established for all statistical tests. Our analytical approach adhered to CDC guidelines by incorporating appropriate sampling weights, stratification variables, and cluster designations to properly account for NHANES’ complex multi-stage sampling methodology. To address missing values in our covariates, we implemented multiple imputation with chained equations using the ‘mice’ package in R. The interpolated covariates and the proportion of missing values for each covariate are shown in Figure S1.

Participant characteristic differences across De Ritis ratio groupings were evaluated using survey-weighted statistical methods. Differences in continuous variables across De Ritis ratio categories were assessed using weighted Kruskal–Wallis tests, while distributions of categorical variables were compared using weighted Chi-square tests. Summary statistics included median values with corresponding interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous data, whereas categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages.

De Ritis ratio values were stratified into quartiles for analysis: Q1 (< 25th percentile), Q2 (25th–50th percentile), Q3 (50th–75th percentile), and Q4 (> 75th percentile). To investigate mortality risk associated with De Ritis ratio among individuals with prediabetes or diabetes, we employed three sequential Cox proportional hazards regression models, with Q1 serving as the reference group. Model 1 assessed unadjusted associations, while the second incorporated demographic adjustments (age, sex, and race). Model 3 built upon Model 2 by additionally adjusting for other covariates listed above (marital status, education, PIR, BMI, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, GGT, LDH, HDL-C, total bilirubin, BUN, UA and HbA1c). The statistical significance of trends across De Ritis categories was assessed by incorporating category medians as continuous variables. We validated the Cox regression assumptions through Schoenfeld residual analysis, which showed no meaningful violations across the models (Table S1).

We first applied Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis stratified by De Ritis ratio quartiles to visualize mortality patterns among individuals with prediabetes or diabetes, and subsequently explored potential non-linear associations using restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis. We constructed a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to compare the prognostic performance of the De Ritis ratio with individual liver enzymes (AST and ALT) using the area under the curve (AUC). To further evaluate the predictive capacity of the De Ritis ratio for mortality across different follow-up time points, we conducted a time-dependent ROC analysis. To verify result consistency, we conducted interaction tests to detect potential effect modifiers and performed stratified analyses across various covariate subgroups.

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted distinct sensitivity analyses: one excluding participants who died within the first 2 years of follow-up, and removing those with pre-existing liver disease at baseline. Furthermore, we eliminated cases with missing covariate data. To further clarify whether the prognostic significance of the De Ritis ratio reflects hepatic injury alone or partially captures systemic oxidative–antioxidative balance, we additionally constructed a series of antioxidant-focused sensitivity models. These major endogenous antioxidant biomarkers included total bilirubin, albumin, uric acid, and creatinine. Specifically, we developed four complementary modeling frameworks. Model A adjusted four endogenous antioxidant biomarkers on the basis of Model 2. Model B further adjusted albumin and creatinine on the basis of Model 3. Model C excluded four endogenous antioxidant biomarkers on the basis of Model B. Model D adjusted eGFR on the basis of Model B.

Results

Baseline characteristics of selected participants

Our study examined 23,873 individuals with prediabetes or diabetes. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics stratified by quartiles of the De Ritis ratio. The median age of participants was 55 years, and 52.62% were male. Individuals in the higher quartiles were generally older and more often female, and they exhibited a higher prevalence of hypertension, whereas differences in race/ethnicity, education, and marital status were present. BMI decreased progressively across quartiles, indicating a leaner phenotype among individuals with an elevated De Ritis ratio, who were more likely to be non-drinkers. Laboratory parameters demonstrated several consistent trends: lipid profiles were substantially less favorable in the lower quartiles, characterized by higher TG, LDL-C, and TC, along with lower HDL-C; liver-related markers showed significantly lower GGT and slightly higher LDH in the higher quartiles; renal function indicators suggested mildly reduced kidney function with decreasing eGFR across quartiles; and uric acid levels declined markedly from the lowest to the highest quartile. Association between De Ritis ratio and mortality

KM survival analysis revealed marked differences in mortality outcomes (all-cause and CVD) across the four De Ritis ratio quartiles. Participants in higher quartiles exhibited progressively diminished survival probabilities (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 2). Cox regression findings are detailed in Table 2. In the comprehensive adjustment model (model 3) with De Ritis ratio as a categorical variable, participants in Q3 and Q4 showed hazard ratios of 1.28 (1.12, 1.46) and 1.79 (1.56, 2.05), respectively, when compared to Q1 individuals. Analyzing De Ritis ratio as a continuous variable in model 3 indicated that each standard deviation (SD) increment corresponded to a 26% elevated risk for all-cause mortality (HR: 1.26, 95% CI 1.19–1.33). For CVD-specific mortality, comparable patterns emerged in the fully adjusted model, with the highest quartile maintaining significance (HR: 1.66, 95% CI 1.30–2.12) versus Q1, while each SD rise in De Ritis ratio was linked to a 28% heightened risk for CVD mortality (HR: 1.28, 95% CI 1.18–1.38). Subgroup analyses

Our analysis of the De Ritis ratio’s relationship with mortality outcomes incorporated extensive stratified assessments (Fig. 5). We evaluated this association across various demographic and clinical factors: sex (female, male), age (< 65 years, ≥ 65 years), smoking status (current, former, never), race (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, other races), drinking status (current, former, never), hypertension (yes or no), and disease condition (diabetes, prediabetes). Regarding all-cause mortality, interaction analyses revealed that only sex demonstrated significant effect modification (P for interaction < 0.05), while other variables showed no significant interactions. Additionally, when examining CVD mortality, age emerged as a significant effect modifier (P for interaction < 0.05). Sensitivity analyses

Our sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our primary results. The significant correlation between De Ritis ratio and mortality persisted even after we eliminated participants who died within the initial 2-year follow-up period, which effectively mitigated concerns about reverse causation (Table S2). Additionally, when we removed subjects with pre-existing hepatic conditions at study onset, our findings remained consistent (Table S3).

To further explore whether the association between the De Ritis ratio and mortality might be mediated or confounded by endogenous antioxidant status, we conducted additional analyses incorporating antioxidant-focused models. Compared with Model 2 (HR: 1.55, 95% CI 1.3–1.76), after adjusting for total bilirubin, uric acid, albumin and creatinine in Model A, the risk of a high quartile De Ritis ratio for all-cause mortality was reduced (HR: 1.47, 95% CI 1.29–1.68). The results related to cardiovascular mortality were consistent with all-cause mortality. After excluding endogenous antioxidant biomarkers from Model B, the association between the high quartile De Ritis ratio and prognostic risk remained robust in Model C (HR:1.77, 95% CI 1–− 2.03). After further adjusting for eGFR, the prognostic risk of Model D for all-cause mortality decreased compared to Model B, regardless of whether it was a high quartile or a continuous De Ritis ratio (Table S4).

The observed relationship retained its statistical power when analyses were restricted solely to participants for whom complete covariate information was available (Table S5).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between De Ritis ratio and all-cause and CVD mortality in individuals with diabetes or prediabetes in large-scale population health surveys, using NHANES data from 1999 to 2018. We observed a linear relationship, with both all-cause mortality and CVD mortality increasing as the De Ritis ratio rose. Stratified analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings across various subgroups, including age, sex, and comorbidities. Additionally, the De Ritis ratio demonstrated superior predictive ability compared to AST and ALT alone. These results suggest that the De Ritis ratio is a valuable, easily accessible biomarker for mortality risk stratification in diabetic and prediabetic populations. Based on these findings, we further explored the metabolic and organ damage mechanisms that the elevated De Ritis ratio might reflect in order to better understand its association with the risk of death.

We observed that individuals in the lowest quartile of the De Ritis ratio (Q1) were more often male and exhibited higher lipid profiles—including triglycerides, LDL, and total cholesterol—as well as a higher BMI. This finding aligns with previous studies showing that even mild liver dysfunction reduces the organ’s capacity to regulate lipid metabolism, leading to elevated circulating triglycerides and cholesterol. This is particularly evident under metabolic conditions where insulin resistance and oxidative stress further intensify lipid accumulation in the liver and blood [29]. The lower BMI observed in individuals with higher De Ritis ratios may reflect poor nutritional status and/or sarcopenia, both of which are strongly linked to increased mortality risk.

The liver is integral to maintaining glucose homeostasis through processes such as gluconeogenesis, glycogenolysis, and the regulation of insulin sensitivity [14]. In diabetes, particularly type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), the liver often exhibits altered function, which can exacerbate the disease’s complications [30]. Elevated liver enzymes, such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), are frequently observed in diabetic patients [31] and often indicate liver inflammation or injury, particularly in conditions such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [32]. Within this pathophysiologic framework, the De Ritis ratio offers additional insight: extreme values typically indicate advanced hepatic injury and may signal broader systemic involvement. Because AST can be released from injured hepatocytes as well as the heart and skeletal muscle, a higher ratio can reflect extrahepatic or multi-organ injury. For example, Wu et al. reported that, after adjustment for potential confounders, older adults with an AST/ALT ratio above 1.20 had a significantly higher risk of sarcopenia [33]. Yadav et al. further showed that this ratio can serve as an independent predictor of future metabolic syndrome, offering additional prognostic value for its onset and underscoring its utility as a marker of metabolic dysfunction [34]. The presence of such complications further worsens patient prognosis. As one study states, patients with sarcopenia have approximately twice the risk of death compared to the general population [35].

Chronic inflammation triggered by hepatic fat accumulation contributes to systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, promoting atherosclerosis and cardiovascular risk [36]. A study by Zhengri Lu et al. [37] found that elevated De Ritis ratios were independently associated with CVD mortality in consecutive adult cardiac arrest patients [37]. This might be due to the elevated inflammatory markers caused by liver injury (such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, tumor necrosis factor -α) and the presence of systemic inflammation, which are associated with poor vascular function and an increased risk of adverse events, especially in patients with complications such as coronary heart disease and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [38]. Moreover, hyperinsulinemia—common in early diabetes—stimulates vascular smooth muscle proliferation and lipid accumulation, thereby accelerating atherosclerosis [39]. Together with systemic inflammation, this synergistic effect further worsens prognosis. Another 10-year follow-up study from Japan shows that the De Ritis ratio can identify subjects with latent CVDs by reflecting the level of brain natriuretic peptide [40]. All the above evidence indicates that the De Ritis ratio is expected to become an effective and simple tool for predicting CVD mortality.

Furthermore, we found that there were sex and age differences between the De Ritis ratio and mortality among patients with diabetes or prediabetes. Females were more frequently represented in the higher quartiles of the De Ritis ratio, and the associated prognostic risk is also greater in females. On the one hand, compared with males, females with diabetes usually show a higher BMI [41–43], lipid levels [44] and insulin resistance status [45]. One possible explanation is that inflammatory levels in women are more influenced by hormonal status. For instance, after menopause, the decline in estrogen leads to a chronic low-grade inflammatory state, characterized by increased levels of cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-8, and TNF-α [46, 47]. These differences may partly explain why females with diabetes face a higher cardiovascular risk and are more susceptible to the adverse prognostic impact of an elevated De Ritis ratio [48].

However, no interaction between sex and De Ritis ratio on CVD mortality was observed in the stratification of subgroups, which might be due to the relatively small number of CVD mortality events studied. Furthermore, the association between De Ritis ratio and CVD mortality was not significant in people under 65 years old, while a high De Ritis ratio in people over 65 years old led to a greater risk of CVD mortality. This might be attributed to the higher proportion of cardiovascular deaths among elderly people with diabetes [49].

In the models centered on endogenous antioxidant biomarkers, Model A suggested that oxidative stress may partially influence the association between the De Ritis ratio and prognosis. Total bilirubin, albumin, uric acid, and creatinine are major non-enzymatic endogenous antioxidants in human circulation and have been linked in recent Mendelian randomization and large cohort studies to ischemic stroke, site-specific cancers, and overall mortality, as well as to lung and thyroid cancer risk through composite indices such as ALBI and PALBI [50–54]. These findings support the biological plausibility that variation in endogenous antioxidant status could confound or modify the relationship between the De Ritis ratio and adverse outcomes, and therefore we adjusted for these biomarkers in Model B. However, after removing endogenous antioxidant biomarkers from the fully adjusted Model B, the HR estimates in Model C changed only minimally and the confidence intervals largely overlapped between the two models, suggesting that, once other clinical and metabolic covariates are controlled for, the contribution of antioxidant indicators to the association between the De Ritis ratio and the risk of death is relatively limited. The results of Model D are consistent with previous studies. Diabetic patients usually have renal insufficiency [55], and this condition usually affects the prognosis of patients. Overall, the direction of the De Ritis ratio effect was consistent across the different sensitivity models, indicating that its prognostic significance was robust and not materially confounded by endogenous antioxidant biomarkers.

This study has several methodological strengths. Most notably, the use of NHANES data—with its multi-stage, stratified probability sampling design—enhances both the representativeness and external validity of our findings. The study design also benefits from a clear temporal sequence: exposure variables were recorded prior to outcome assessment and were directly obtained by trained personnel, mitigating the risk of recall bias. In addition, the statistical models were rigorously adjusted for a comprehensive set of confounding factors, increasing the robustness and reliability of our conclusions.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. A primary limitation is the reliance on single-time-point measurements of the De Ritis ratio, which restricts our ability to examine how longitudinal variations in liver enzyme levels may influence mortality risk among individuals with diabetes or prediabetes. Understanding how these biomarkers evolve over time is essential for elucidating their long-term health implications. In addition, although we adjusted for numerous known confounders, the potential influence of unmeasured variables—particularly environmental exposures—cannot be entirely excluded. These limitations should be considered when interpreting our results and underscore the need for future longitudinal studies with more comprehensive data collection.

Conclusion

Elevated De Ritis ratio is linearly associated with higher all-cause and CVD mortality in a population with diabetes or prediabetes, outperforming AST or ALT alone. Given its simplicity, accessibility and cost-effectiveness, the De Ritis ratio represents a practical biomarker for mortality risk stratification in diabetes management.
