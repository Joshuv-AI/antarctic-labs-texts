Control of myotube orientation using ultrasonication
Authors: Ryohei Hashiguchi, Hidetaka Ichikawa, Masahiro Kumeta, Daisuke Koyama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study investigated a technique for controlling the orientation of C2C12-derived myotube cells using ultrasonication for future clinical applications of cultured skeletal muscle tissues. An ultrasonicating cell culture dish, comprising a plastic-bottomed culture dish and a circular glass plate (diameter, 35 mm; thickness, 1.1 mm) attached to an annular piezoelectric ultrasonic transducer (inner diameter, 10 mm; outer diameter, 20 mm; thickness, 1 mm), was constructed. A concentric resonant vibrational mode at 89 kHz was generated on the bottom of the dish, and the orientations of myotube cells were quantitatively evaluated using two-dimensional Fourier transform analysis of phase contrast microscopy images captured over a 14 × 10 mm2 area at the center of the dish. Unsonicated myotube cells grew in random directions, but ultrasonication aligned them circumferentially in the culture dish. The timing of treatment was important, with ultrasonication for 48 h before differentiation having a greater impact on myotube orientation than ultrasonication after differentiation. A larger ultrasonic vibration, with an amplitude of over 20 Vpp, resulted in significantly smaller angles of deviation in the circumferential direction than the control. Ultrasonication enhanced the expression of differentiation-related genes and the formation of aligned myotubes, suggesting that it promotes differentiation of C2C12 cells into myotubes.

Introduction

Tissue regeneration is a primary objective in the fields of tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. It enables the restoration or replacement of tissue and organ functions that have been damaged by injury or disease. The orientations of cells and extracellular matrix are related to the function of living tissue12345. Mammalian muscle fibers are individual contractile units in which multinucleated myocytes form elongated bundles6,7. Skeletal muscle tissue is composed of skeletal muscle cells arranged in a unidirectional manner, resulting in a high cell density, and generating a significant contractile force8. Artificial muscles have been developed using biocompatible and functional materials that mimic the function and structure of skeletal muscle tissues91011 and have potential applications in regenerative medicine and bioactuators121314. Cell sheets that can be harvested on culture dishes have potential applications in regenerative medicine because they can be transplanted directly into the target tissue15,16. Although cell sheet techniques carry potential risks, such as lack of nutrition within the sheet and the need for surface treatment on the dish to remove the adhered sheet, myoblast cell sheets have been used to treat several cases of severe heart failure17. However, myoblasts are adherent and exhibit a random orientation under conventional culture conditions. For the future clinical application of cultured skeletal muscle tissues, a technique for controlling the orientation of myoblasts is needed18192021.

It is widely recognized that chemical, mechanical, electrical, and magnetic stimuli play a role in advancing tissue development and function222324, and skeletal muscle tissues are particularly sensitive to mechanical stimuli25,26. Although the fabrication of microstructures is technically challenging, microfabrication techniques can control the directivity of cell growth on culture dish surfaces3,4,27,28. Ultrasound represents a promising candidate for the manipulation of cells without contact. The use of high-frequency ultrasound enables the implementation of cell patterning techniques. The arrangement pattern can be controlled by switching the acoustic field29. Yin et al. described a novel cell manipulation technique in hydrogel fibers30. This technique allows for the precise control of cell patterning by selecting the acoustic resonant mode within the fiber. In a recent study, Wu et al. demonstrated that specific cells can be manipulated selectively using acoustic radiation force and nanosized gas vesicles31. Our group has examined alternative methods for controlling cell growth using ultrasonic vibration. The distribution of ultrasonic vibration applied to the bottom of a culture dish can control where cells adhere and their direction of growth because ultrasonic vibration with a large amplitude prevents cells from adhering to the dish. This indicates that ultrasonic vibration can direct cell growth in a specific direction. Our previous work used this technique to pattern adhesive HeLa cells32 and control the direction of neurite outgrowth of PC12 cells33. In this study, we investigated the effects of ultrasonication on the orientation of myoblasts under culture conditions.

Materials and methods

C2C12 cells, derived from mouse rhabdomyosarcoma, were used as adherent myoblasts and were purchased in an undifferentiated state from the Institute of Physical and Chemical Research, Japan. They were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 10% v/v fetal bovine serum (Lot No. 29110721FBS, Serana Europe GmbH, Brandenburg, Germany) and 1% v/v penicillin/streptomycin (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere and passaged every 2 d in accordance with the instructions provided by the cell manufacturer. For subculture, the cells were washed with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) after removing the medium from the culture dish. One milliliter of trypsin solution (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) was added to detach the cells from the bottom of the dish, and 5 mL of fresh culture medium was added to buffer the cytotoxicity of trypsin. The cell suspension was centrifuged, and the resulting cell pellet was resuspended and subcultured in fresh culture medium. To evaluate the effects of ultrasonication on cell orientation, the C2C12 cells were differentiated into myotubes. Differentiation was induced 48 h after cell seeding at a density of 4 × 105 cells/mL by replacing the culture medium with DMEM supplemented with 2% v/v horse serum (Lot No. 18LO63, Sigma-Aldrich, MO) and 1% v/v penicillin/streptomycin. Cell growth was observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (IX83, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Contiguous 1.40 × 1.04 mm2 phase contrast images with a spatial resolution of 1 μm/pixel were captured over a 14 × 10 mm2 area at the center of the dish. Figure 1 shows representative sequential images of C2C12 cells differentiating into elongated myotubes via cell fusion from day 1 to day 7.

Ultrasonication was applied to the C2C12 cells in culture. An annular piezoelectric lead zirconate titanate ultrasound transducer (inner diameter, 10 mm; outer diameter, 20 mm; thickness, 1 mm; C-213, Fuji Ceramics, Fujinomiya, Japan) polarized in the thickness direction was bonded to a circular glass plate (diameter, 35 mm; thickness, 1.1 mm) using epoxy. The glass plate was placed under a polystyrene-bottomed cell culture dish (diameter, 35 mm; AGC Techno Glass, Shizuoka, Japan) to generate ultrasonic vibrations on the culture surface (Fig. 2). Silicone oil was used as a coupling material between the culture dish and the glass plate to ensure that the ultrasonic vibration was efficiently transmitted to surface of the dish. This experimental configuration posed minimal risk of contamination because the ultrasound transducer did not contact the culture medium or cells. The apparatus was placed in a small chamber that allows for control of temperature, humidity, and CO2 concentration, and a function generator (WF1946A, NF, Yokohama, Japan) input a sinusoidal electrical signal to the piezoelectric transducer to generate ultrasonic vibration. The effects of the ultrasonication on cell orientation were investigated under two conditions: ultrasonication for 168 h after initiating differentiation (procedure 1) and ultrasonication for 48 h from cell seeding to the start of differentiation (procedure 2). In procedure 1, the cells were subjected to ultrasonication following the initiation of differentiation (48 h after cell seeding), and this process was continued for 168 h in the small chamber, with the vibrational amplitude of the ultrasound transducer maintained at a constant level. In procedure 2, the cells were subjected to ultrasonication for 48 h in the small chamber following cell seeding. Thereafter, the cells were cultured in the small chamber for 168 h in the absence of ultrasonication.

The orientation of myotube cells cultured on the dish was evaluated using two-dimensional fast Fourier transform (2DFFT) analysis of phase contrast images. This method decomposes spatial intensity data into frequency components for quantitative evaluation of the directionality of image features associated with myotube formation. Each image of the cells was binarized using an adaptive binarization method with a block size of 63 pixels. Representative phase contrast images of random and unidirectional cell orientations are shown in Fig. 3a and d, respectively, and the corresponding binarized images are shown in Fig. 3b and e. The central region of the image was then cropped to an area of 0.7 × 0.7 mm2 (512 × 512 pixels) to obtain the frequency power spectrum via 2DFFT (Fig. 3c, f). The average intensity of the power spectrum in the radial direction was then calculated from θ = 0° to 175° in 5° increments to evaluate its directional characteristics. Figure 3g shows the azimuthal distribution of the average amplitude of the power spectrum shown in Fig. 3f. The peak at θ = 75° indicates that the axis of cell elongation was perpendicular to this direction, which can be visually verified from the binarized image in Fig. 3e. The degree of cell alignment was determined by dividing the full width at half maximum of the above distribution by 36 (the number of 5° increments from θ = 0° to 175°). The vibrational amplitude distribution across the center of the dish was measured using a laser Doppler vibrometer (NLV-2500, PI Polytec, Waldbronn, Germany) over an area of 27 × 27 mm2.

Ultrasonication induces mechanical stress on cells, which affects gene expression. Real-time PCR analysis was used to examine the effects of ultrasonication on gene expression in C2C12 cells. RNA was extracted from the cells using a RNeasy kit (74104, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) after 48 h of sonication following cell seeding. Reverse transcription-coupled real-time quantitative PCR was performed using a LightCycler 96 system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The expression levels of Myod1, Myog, Mef2c, Erk5, Klf2, and Mck (also known as Ckm) genes related to muscle differentiation were measured relative to the expression level of the β-actin gene (Actb). Gene expression analyses were conducted three times for each experimental condition. The following probe sets were used to detect each target gene: Myod1 (forward) 5′-ggaagagtgcggctgtgt-3′, (reverse) 5′-gtggagatgcgctccactat-3′; Myog (forward) 5′-ggaagtctgtgtcggtggac-3′, (reverse) 5′-cttgagcctgcgcttctc-3′; Mef2c (forward) 5′-tgatcagcaggcaaagattg-3′, (reverse) 5′-ggatggtaactggcatctcaa-3′; Erk5 (forward) 5′-ctcacgcttacggactaggtg-3′, (reverse) 5′-gcattggtcaccacatcaaa-3′; Klf2 (forward) 5′-ctaaaggcgcatctgcgta-3′, (reverse) 5′-tagtggcgggtaagctcgt-3′; Mck (forward) 5′- cagcacagacagacactcagg-3′, (reverse) 5′-gaacttgttgtgggtgttgc-3′; Actb (forward) 5′-gccaaccgtgaaaagatgac-3′, (reverse) 5′-gaggcatacagggacagcac-3′.

Immunostaining of differentiated C2C12 cells were performed after 9 days after ultrasonication for 48 h. Cells were wash twice with phosphate buffered saline (PBS), and fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 15 min. After washing twice by PBS, blocking and permeabilization was performed by 0.5% triton X-100 and 5% normal goat serum in PBS for 15 min. Anti-skeletal muscle myosin heavy chain (MHC) antibody (MAB4470, R&D Systems, Minnneapolis, MN) was incubated at 2.5 µg/mL in 5% normal goat serum-containing PBS for 1 h. After washing twice with PBS, cells were incubated with 10 µg/mL fluoresceinisothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (55493, MP Biomedicals, Solon, OH) in 5% normal goat serum-containing PBS for 45 min. After washing four times with PBS, cells were mounted with vectashield (H-1000, Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA) containing 10 µg/mL Hoechst33342 (19172-51, Nacalai tesque, Kyoto, Japan). Cells were observed using confocal laser scanning fluorescence microscope (FV-3000, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Quantification and orientation analyses of fluorescent signals were performed using ImageJ software (National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD). Statistical analyses were performed using Origin software (OriginLab, Northampton, MA).

Results and discussion

To investigate the relationship between the vibrational amplitude distribution across the ultrasonicating cell culture dish and the orientation of myotube cells, axisymmetric resonant flexural vibration modes of the dish between 20 and 100 kHz were excited. We used the resonant mode at 89 kHz, taking into account the observation area of the microscope and the wavelength of the flexural vibration. Figure 4a shows the out-of-plane vibrational amplitude distribution across the surface of the dish when excited at 89 kHz, with the maximum amplitude at the center of the dish being surrounded by concentric circular nodes. The distribution was measured in the absence of culture medium because light reflected from the surface of the medium interfered with the Doppler measurement. Microscopy measurements were made within and around the most central node, which had a radius of approximately 4.5 mm (Fig. 4b). Figure 4c shows that the vibrational amplitude at the center of the dish increased commensurately with the input voltage and reached a maximum of 0.48 μm at an input voltage of 30 Vpp.

Procedure 1 was conducted three times at an input voltage of 5–20 Vpp, and procedure 2 was conducted five times at 10–30 Vpp. The detachment of cells from the bottom of the culture dish via ultrasonication was initially evaluated in procedure 1 with an input voltage of 5–20 Vpp. The number of cells detached from the bottom of the dish and suspended in the culture medium was quantified at 2, 5, and 7 days after initiating differentiation using a hemocytometer. In addition, the number of cells remaining attached to the bottom of the dish was quantified at day 7 to calculate the percentage of cell detachment. The results demonstrated that there was no significant difference in the percentage of cell detachment, approximately 15%, observed between the control and the samples in the cases at 5–20 Vpp. Figure 5a–d and e–h show the orientation distributions of C2C12 cells in the observation area for procedures 1 and 2, respectively. The direction and color of each line in the plots indicates the direction and degree of alignment of cells, respectively, calculated using 2DFFT analysis of phase contrast images. Procedure 2 resulted in a circumferential orientation of most myotube cells (Fig. 5f–h). In contrast, procedure 1 (Fig. 5b–d) and no ultrasonication (Fig. 5a and e) resulted in random cell orientations. Figure 5i and j show the deviation of the cell orientations in Fig. 5a–d and e–h from a circumferential direction. For the control condition (no ultrasonication), the average angle of deviation was approximately 45°, consistent with cell growth in random directions. In the case of procedure 1 (Fig. 5i), there was no significant difference between the control and ultrasonication conditions, whereas procedure 2 with input voltages of 20 Vpp and 30 Vpp resulted in significantly smaller angles of deviation than the control (Fig. 5j). These results indicate that myotube cell orientations were randomly distributed in the absence of ultrasonication, whereas circumferential cell orientations were induced in the presence of ultrasonication. The cell orientation distribution correlated with the vibrational amplitude distribution shown in Fig. 4a, indicating that cells grew in a direction that minimized variation in vibrational displacement across their long axes. Circumferential cell orientations were even observed at a radius > 4 mm, where vibrational displacement was nearly undetectable. Since cell–cell communication is transiently established in differentiating myoblasts prior to cell fusion34, ultrasonication-based control of cells at central high displacement area (a radius < 4 mm) may also result in alignment of cells in outer area (> 4 mm) by the cell–cell contacts. Additionally, Fig. 5i demonstrates that the orientation of myotube cells appears to be determined prior to differentiation because ultrasonication after induction of differentiation (procedure 1) had little effect on cell orientation.

To assess the subcellular impact of ultrasonication on myotube differentiation in C2C12 cells, gene expression profiles were analyzed. During differentiation of C2C12 cells into myotubes, a series of differentiation-related genes are known to be activated, including master regulators Myod, Myog, and Mef2c and late differentiation marker genes Erk5, Klf2, and Mck. Figure 6 shows the expression levels of these genes relative to the expression level of the reference β-actin gene (Actb) for unsonicated cells (control) and cells ultrasonicated at transducer input voltages of 20 and 30 Vpp in procedure 2. Ultrasonicated cells had significantly higher expression levels of six differentiation-related genes compared with the control cells, indicating that ultrasonication promotes myotube differentiation. Cells cultured for 9 days after ultrasonication were subjected to immunostaining against skeletal muscle myosin heavy chain (MHC) (Fig. 7a–f). Compared to the control, the ultrasonicated sample contained higher rate of MHC positive cells (Fig. 7g) and MHC signal intensity (Fig. 7h). Moreover, significantly higher alignment of myotube orientation (Fig. 8a) and the rate of cell fusion (Fig. 8b) were observed, indicating the effect of ultrasonication in controlling myoblast differentiation.

Chemical cocktails containing several compounds targeting histone modifications, metabolic pathways, growth signaling and so on, has been shown to strongly induce cardiomyocyte and skeletal muscle differentiation35,36. Compared to these chemically-induced methods, ultrasound-based stimulation seems to have a partial and supportive effect on the myotube formation considering the degree of mRNA and morphological responses revealed in this study. Since dose and timing of the compound treatment was critical for chemically-induced myotube formation37, screening of different ultrasound condition including different frequency, output intensity, timing and duration, may be useful to establish an optimized method for ultrasound-induced myotube formation in future.

Conclusions

A method for controlling the orientation of myotubes using ultrasonication was tested using a cell culture dish that undergoes ultrasonic vibrations via an external transducer. The cell orientations on the dish were quantitatively evaluated using 2DFFT image analysis. The dish underwent a concentric resonant vibrations that induced circumferential orientations of myotube cells, and a larger vibrational amplitude resulted higher circumferential alignment. These results suggest that cells grow in a direction that minimizes variation in vibrational displacement across their long axes. Ultrasonicated C2C12 cells showed higher level of differentiation-related gene expressions and higher rate of myotube formation than unsonicated cells, indicating that ultrasonication promotes the differentiation of C2C12 cells into myotubes.
