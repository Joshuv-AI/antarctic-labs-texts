Contemporary Survivorship of Highly Porous Hemispherical Shells in Aseptic Acetabular Revision Total Hip Arthroplasty
Authors: Colin C. Neitzke, Pravjit Bhatti, Shu-Han Wang, Alexis G. Gonzalez, Elizabeth B. Gausden, Brian P. Chalmers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundContemporary performance of revision hemispherical shells in acetabular revision total hip arthroplasty (rTHA) remains limited. This study evaluated survivorship free from revision for aseptic acetabular loosening, all-cause revision, and all-cause reoperation, as well as dislocation rate and patient-reported outcomes (PROMs) following aseptic rTHA with modern, highly-porous, revision acetabular components.MethodsWe identified 127 patients (mean age 66, mean body mass index 27, 57% female) who underwent aseptic rTHA with hemispherical shells from 2016 to 2022 with minimum 2-year potential follow-up. Paprosky defects type was I in 20%, II in 70%, and III in 10%. Cases using augments or cages were excluded. A posterolateral approach was used in 98% of patients, and 21% of patients underwent concomitant femoral revision. A mean of 3 (range, 0-5) screws were used. Liners included dual mobility (n = 94), elevated (n = 15), neutral (n = 12), constrained (n = 4), and offset (n = 2). Kaplan–Meier survivorship analysis assessed time-to-event outcomes.ResultsAt 2 years, survivorship free from revision for aseptic acetabular loosening was 97%, and free from all-cause revision was 94%. Revision indications included dislocation (n = 4), aseptic loosening (n = 2), periprosthetic joint infection (n = 1), and metallosis (n = 1). Dislocation occurred in 7 patients (6%). At 2 years, there was a median 21.4-point increase in the Hip Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement (P < .001).ConclusionsModern, highly-porous revision hemispherical shells with multiple screw fixation have excellent 2-year survivorship free from aseptic loosening (97%) but modest dislocation rate (6%). These results support the use of contemporary hemispherical shells but highlight the need for instability prevention.

Introduction

The incidence of acetabular revision total hip arthroplasty (rTHA) continues to rise in the United States [1]. Acetabular bone loss is one of the most significant challenges in acetabular revision, limiting the ability to achieve durable, long-term fixation and joint stability [2]. Patients with severe acetabular bone loss are at increased risk of complications including periprosthetic joint infection (PJI), dislocation, aseptic loosening, and mechanical failure leading to reoperation and revision [[3], [4], [5], [6]].

The Paprosky classification remains the most widely used classifications for acetabular bone loss which guides its management [7,8]. For Paprosky type I (mild bone loss with no hip center migration) and type IIA (moderate bone loss with mild [<2 cm superomedial] hip center migration), large hemispherical rTHA cups have become a commonly utilized reconstruction option. These hemispherical rTHA cups achieve initial fixation utilizing a rim pinch by “oversizing” the cup (ie, a 60-mm cup prepared with a 58-mm reamer), with their hemispherical shape resulting in equal hoop stresses around the cup [9,10]. Fixation is often augmented with multiple screws to enhance initial cup stability and achieve osseointegration [11,12].

Contemporary data on the performance of modern revision hemispherical shells remain limited or with an emphasis on only “jumbo” cups (≥66 mm for men or ≥62 mm for women) [2,[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. In this study, we sought to evaluate the survivorship free from revision for aseptic acetabular loosening, all-cause revision, and all-cause reoperation. Additionally, we assessed the rate of dislocation and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) following aseptic rTHA with modular, highly porous, hemispherical revision acetabular components.

Material and methods

Following institutional review board approval, utilizing a prospectively maintained revision arthroplasty registry from a single tertiary orthopaedic hospital, we identified all patients who underwent aseptic acetabular rTHA, with or without concomitant femoral rTHA, implanted with a highly porous acetabular hemispherical shell, from February 1, 2016, to December 31, 2022, to allow for a 2-year minimum potential follow-up period. Highly porous acetabular hemispherical shells were defined as acetabular shell components composed of tantalum or additively manufactured titanium alloy with a minimum porosity of 60%. Implant system, manufacturer, and porosity values can be found in Table 1. Patients were excluded if they underwent acetabular revision for a septic indication or received an acetabular augment and or cage (ie, cup-cage construct). Our electronic medical record was queried in addition to our revision arthroplasty registry to collect baseline demographic and surgical variables including: age, sex, body mass index, race, ethnicity, smoking status, Charlson Comorbidity Index, American Society of Anesthesiologists risk level, rTHA indication, surgical approach, acetabular shell implant, number of acetabular screws utilized, articulation design, operative time (minutes), and length of stay in days. Additionally, the registry and medical record was queried via manual review to determine all instances of all-cause reoperation, all-cause revision, revision for aseptic acetabular loosening, and dislocation. All-cause revision was defined as revision of nonmodular (cup or stem) and modular (head or liner) components. Data analyses

Descriptive statistics were reported as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables or count and proportion for categorical variables. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed for the endpoints of acetabular revision for aseptic loosening, all-cause revision, and all-cause reoperation. For PROMs, preoperative to postoperative comparisons were performed using a Hodges–Lehmann estimate derived from the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm method. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set using an alpha less than 0.05.

The median age was 68 years (IQR: 60 to 74), 57% of the cohort was female (n = 72), and the median body mass index was 26.3 (IQR: 23 to 30). Results

Survivorship

At 2 years, the survivorship free from revision for aseptic acetabular loosening was 97% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 93 to 100) (Fig. 2). Survivorship remained unchanged at 4 years as no further aseptic acetabular loosening event occurred after 22 months, resulting in a stable Kaplan–Meier survivorship estimate, despite a declining number at risk. The 2- and 4-year survivorship free from all-cause revision was 94% (95% CI: 89 to 100) and 80% (95% CI: 65 to 98), respectively (Fig. 3). Acetabular aseptic loosening

There were 2 hips that underwent revisions for aseptic loosening, both of which occurred in patients with an index indication of aseptic loosening with Paprosky bone loss of IIA and IIC. The patient with Paprosky IIC bone loss was a 76-year-old female with an index acetabular rTHA 9 years after primary cementless THA. The patient underwent acetabular rTHA utilizing a 56-mm Trident II Tritanium Multihole shell (Stryker, Portage, MI) with 2 supplementary screws. After 2 years, the patient again developed aseptic loosening of the acetabular component and underwent repeat acetabular revision with a 68-mm Trabecular Metal Revision Acetabular shell (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN) with four supplementary screws in addition to bone graft putty and a trabecular metal augment given a medial wall defect. After 2 months, the patient suffered pelvic discontinuity and implant loosening, ultimately receiving a posterior buttress augment and a custom flanged component. The patient with Paprosky IIA bone loss was a 21-year-old male who underwent primary THA 9 years prior for juvenile rheumatoid arthritis. For his index acetabular rTHA, he received a 54-mm G7 OsseoTi Multi Hole Acetabular shell (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN) with 3 supplementary screws and femoral head allograft. The patient suffered early acetabular loosening 2 months postrevision and underwent repeat acetabular revision with another 54 mm G7 OsseoTi Multi Hole Acetabular Shell with four supplementary screws.

Dislocations

A total of 7 hips (6%) experienced a postoperative dislocation (Table 4), all of which had an index rTHA performed via the posterolateral approach. Only one of these 7 patients had an index rTHA indication of instability. The articulation design present at the time of dislocation included 3 dual mobility constructs (all 28 mm heads), 2 elevated liners (32 and 36 mm heads), and 2 neutral liners (both 36 mm heads). Of the 7 hips that dislocated, four underwent subsequent revision. One patient had recurrent instability and underwent repeat acetabular revision with conversion from a neutral to DM construct. Another patient with recurrent instability, abductor weakness, and sciatic nerve compression underwent a head and liner exchange from a neutral liner to a DM construct in addition to a gluteus maximus transfer and sciatic nerve neuroplasty. A third patient underwent open reduction with head and liner exchange, retaining the acetabular shell component, after experiencing a posterior hip dislocation with a DM interprosthetic dislocation. The final patient with recurrent instability underwent head and liner exchange from an elevated liner to a constrained liner, with retention of a well-fixed, well-positioned acetabular shell and remained free of reoperation at 5 years postoperatively. Reoperations

There were 10 (8%) total reoperations, including the 2 hips revised for acetabular aseptic loosening and four hips revised for instability, discussed in the sections above. The remaining four reoperations/revisions included 1 patient who underwent 2 separate two-stage revisions for PJI and 1 patient who underwent a head and liner exchange and excision of tissue affected by adverse local tissue reaction secondary to trunnionosis/metallosis. Additionally, 2 patients who had concurrent femoral stem revision with trochanteric osteotomy at the time of index acetabular revision required reoperation for hardware failure related to the osteotomy fixation.

Patient-reported outcome measures

At 1 and 2 years, there were statistically significant improvements in PROMs evaluated (Table 5). Discussion

For aseptic acetabular rTHA, hemispherical cups with supplementary screw fixation (Fig. 5) have become an essential surgical strategy for arthroplasty surgeons reconstructing contained acetabular defects. Often in conjunction with acetabular autograft or allograft, modern, highly porous hemispherical shells provide durable fixation with a stable joint. However, there is a relative paucity of literature evaluating the more contemporary results of this procedure [28,29]. Here, we assessed the survivorship of contemporary, aseptic acetabular rTHA using highly porous hemispherical shells and found an extremely high survivorship free from revision for aseptic acetabular loosening, 97% at 2 years with no further aseptic acetabular loosening events after 22 months. In the present study, the 2-year survivorship free from revision for aseptic acetabular loosening was 97%. In total, there were 2 cases of aseptic acetabular loosening in 2 patients initially revised for aseptic loosening with Paprosky bone loss of IIA and IIC, respectively. This highlights the success of modern highly porous cups, with aseptic failures occurring in patients with prior aseptic loosening and compromised bone stock. Wilson et al. explored the survivorship of 3606 acetabular rTHA performed at their institution between 2000 and 2019, 20% of which were reimplantations during two-stage exchange for PJI [28]. When broken down by specific acetabular implants, they reported a 10-year component-specific cumulative incidence of re-revision for aseptic loosening ranging from 0.0 to 1.9%, with the highest incidence occurring in a titanium wire mesh-backed shell with all other highly porous components having aseptic loosening incidences of less than 1.0%. Compared to the 7.0% cumulative incidence in Wilson et al.’s [28] historical series of 258 Harris-Galante-1 (HG-1) components implanted with conventional polyethylene liners, this is a considerable improvement in aseptic loosening risk, owing to advances in both shell design as well as the adoption of highly cross-linked polyethylene (HXLPE) liners. All patients in the present study received HXLPE and there were no reoperations or revisions for polyethylene wear/osteolysis.

Dislocation was the largest contributor to reoperation in this cohort of patients, accounting for four of 10 all-cause reoperations, with 3 additional patients undergoing successful closed reduction without subsequent reoperation or revision. Of those who suffered a postoperative dislocation, only 1 had an index rTHA indication of instability. Instability is known to be a considerable cause of morbidity after acetabular rTHA, even in more contemporary series [30,31]. Stevenson et al. [31] retrospectively reviewed 200 isolated acetabular revisions performed between 2007 and 2017 and found an overall instability rate of 12% and a rate of 22.1% for those with an index rTHA indication of instability. Preventing dislocation is of the utmost importance following acetabular rTHA. While DM constructs, larger femoral head sizes (eg, 32 mm, 36 mm), and elevated liners are commonly used to mitigate instability and reduce dislocation risk, our findings suggest that in the rTHA setting, patient- and surgery-specific factors may have a greater influence on instability than implant design alone. Notably, the dislocations observed in our cohort were heterogenous in both etiology and management. In particular, cases involving abductor deficiency and neurologic impairment represent a substantially higher degree of complexity and inherent instability risk—exemplified by the patient in our cohort with recurrent instability, abductor weakness, and sciatic nerve compression who ultimately required revision with conversion to a DM construct, gluteus maximus transfer, and sciatic nerve neuroplasty. These findings highlight that in rTHA, surgical decision-making and implant selection should be individualized based on patient- and surgery-specific factors rather than relying on a single strategy alone. While we did not specifically evaluate the influence of computer-assisted navigation, we have found this to be a very useful tool to ensure appropriate cup placement in the revision setting, especially when revising a cup for instability. Besides those cups that are grossly loose, existing cups can be referenced intraoperatively, allowing for calculated changes in version and inclination [32]. Thankfully, given the concern for acetabular bone loss in this population, most patients will already have undergone a preoperative computed tomography (CT) scan and thus do not need to undergo additional imaging for CT-based navigation systems. Even when navigation is not being utilized, surgeons should use tools available to them to optimize patient-specific cup placement. At our institution, surgeons often utilize the hip–spine relationship to plan patient-specific cup placement, which can significantly reduce instability [33]. Overall, we suspect that our instability rate may be lower than other contemporary data secondary to steps frequently taken to mitigate dislocation including DM constructs (74% in the present series), large heads, and paying close attention to restoration of leg lengths and offset, in conjunction with the use of advanced technology.

Patients saw significant improvements in their PROMs. The median HOOS-JR score improved by 21.0 and 21.4 at 1 and 2 years, respectfully, and the LEAS improved by 2.0 and 2.0 at those time points. The VR-12 Physical Component Score also saw improvements at 1 and 2 years, with median improvement of 2.29 and 1.98. There was no significant change in VR-12 MCS, which is encouraging and likely indicates stable mental health-related quality of life throughout the perioperative and postoperative period in the setting of a revision total hip procedure. Overall, there was little change from 1 to 2 years for any PROM evaluated, keeping in line with recent literature suggestion that there is limited PROM improvement after 1 year [34,35].

While we did not stratify outcomes by bone loss severity, there is recent literature to suggest that preoperative bone loss severity does not influence postoperative PROMs. Driscoll et al. [36] evaluated 197 patients undergoing aseptic acetabular rTHA from January 2016 to February 2022, with bone loss severity determined intraoperatively including 47 Paprosky I hips, 113 Paprosky II hips, and 37 Paprosky III hips. They found that while severity of intraoperative bone loss was associated with lower preoperative HOOS-JR and LEAS scores, as well as increased HOOS-JR minimum clinically important difference achievement rates, that there were no significant difference in 1-year HOOS-JR or LEAS scores based on intraoperative bone loss severity [36]. As Driscoll et al. noted, worse bone loss and thus lower preoperative PROMs is likely a result of changes in implant fixation, hip center of rotation, and levels of pain, all decreasing overall hip function.

This study is not without potential limitations. Given its retrospective, single institution design, it is subject to the inherent limitation of retrospective analyses including selection bias and inability to control for all potential cofounders. Additionally, given this study was performed at a single, high-volume tertiary referral center, it may limit generalizability as it is well known that increased surgeon and hospital rTHA volume results in improved outcomes [[37], [38], [39]]. Given the absence of a comparative cohort, this study is limited in its ability to contextualize findings against alternative implant designs and fixation strategies. With regards to bone loss classification, all preoperative radiographs were reviewed by a fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeon. Preoperative radiographs do tend to underestimate the true level of acetabular bone loss and thus borderline cases of bone loss may have been misclassified [40,41]. While anteroposterior radiographs are likely sufficient for cases of minimal bone loss, Judet views should be obtained if the integrity of anterior and posterior columns are in question and CT if more advanced preoperative planning is required [42]. Nonetheless, preoperative planning is combined with intraoperative assessment to ensure appropriate reconstruction and fixation. With regards to implant design, there were multiple manufacturers and shell designs utilized at the discretion of the treating surgeons. We believe this enhances the external validity of this study rather than a single vendor experience. Finally, we only reported on surgically related outcomes that were captured within our electronic medical record and thus patients may have had additional nonoperative or medially related complications that were not captured in this study.

Conclusions

In this contemporary series of aseptic acetabular rTHA using highly porous hemispherical shells, we found high survivorship free from aseptic loosening. In conjunction with HXLPE and multiple screw fixation, these findings may provide surgeons with reassurance that hemispherical shells can provide durable fixation in the revision setting, acknowledging the increased risk of postoperative instability in this patient population and the need for preoperative planning to optimize acetabular component positioning.
