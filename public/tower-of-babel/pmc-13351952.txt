Endoscope-assisted prone lateral retropleural 360° reconstruction of thoracic pathological fracture in metastatic melanoma: a case report and technical note
Authors: Fidel Toomey, Mendel Castle-Kirszbaum, Frederick Mariajoseph, Jeremy Kam, Idrees Sher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvances in oncological therapies have increased survival rates, however, these may result in more patients developing secondary disease processes, including pathological spinal fractures requiring surgical decompression and stabilisation. Traditional open approaches carry significant morbidity, particularly for thoracic anterior column reconstruction. A minimally invasive single-position prone lateral retropleural (PRP) approach allows for optimal surgeon ergonomics, simultaneous anterior and posterior access, releases, deformity correction and fixation, while minimising pleural and tissue disruption, implant point loading and surgical time, and eliminating single-lung ventilation. Endoscope-assistance improves illumination and magnification, improving anatomical localisation and minimising complications. Real-time visualisation may shorten the learning curve.Case DescriptionA 67-year-old man with metastatic melanoma presented with progressive mechanical thoracic pain and myelopathy from a pathological T9 fracture. After initial posterior decompression and fusion, he re-presented 9 months later with hardware failure, fractured rods and subsequent progressive kyphosis. He underwent a mini-thoracotomy and endoscope-assisted minimally invasive PRP T9 corpectomy with posterior construct revision to achieve 360° reconstruction. The endoscope provided visualisation along pleural contours to enable safer vertebrectomy, ventral decompression, neurovascular protection, and cage placement. A titanium quad-rod construct with crosslink reinforced the posterior column. He remained well at 12-month follow-up.ConclusionsThis novel endoscope-assisted PRP case unifies two modern spine surgery techniques, offering a promising minimally invasive alternative for thoracic 360° reconstruction of complex fractures. By enabling simultaneous, single-position anterior and posterior column access and improving deformity correction, surgeon ergonomics and visualisation, it facilitates efficient surgery and could assist in navigating the steep learning curve associated with retropleural access while improving clinical outcomes. Future larger-scale observational studies can help provide insight into the generalisability of the technique and its clinical outcomes.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This case report suggests that an endoscope-assisted prone lateral retropleural (PRP) approach to thoracic 360° reconstruction of a pathological fracture in metastatic melanoma is feasible by enabling simultaneous, single-position, anterior and posterior column access. Endoscope assistance can improve visualisation and surgeon ergonomics in the retropleural corridor to enable vertebrectomy, ventral decompression, neurovascular protection, and cage placement.

What is known and what is new?

• Traditional open thoracic anterior column reconstruction via transthoracic or posterior approaches carries significant perioperative morbidity, often involving pleural entry and single lung ventilation.

• This report highlights that by utilising the endoscope, prone positioning and a retropleural approach, surgeons can optimise anterior and posterior column access and deformity correction. This approach minimised pleural and tissue disruption, eliminated single lung ventilation, promoted early mobility, reduced perioperative morbidity, and facilitated good functional outcome at 12 months.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Surgeons can consider adopting the endoscope-assisted PRP approach for complex thoracic reconstructions. It offers a feasible minimally invasive alternative for thoracic 360° reconstruction of a pathological fracture.

Introduction

Endoscopy has transformed spine surgery by providing illumination and high-resolution magnification to access deep corridors with minimal tissue disruption (1). In complex spinal pathologies such as oncological fractures, endoscopic assistance can offer distinct practical advantages by mitigating some of the technical limitations inherent in traditional approaches. When integrated with a single-position minimally invasive surgery (MIS) prone lateral retropleural (PRP) approach, endoscopy unites two modern techniques, helping facilitate safer decompression, precise implant placement and faster recovery while minimising perioperative morbidity (1-3).

Advances in oncological therapies have increased the number of frail patients with secondary disease processes such as vertebral body metastases and consequent pathological spine fractures requiring surgical decompression and stabilisation (4-6). Thoracic pathological fractures often necessitate 360° reconstruction to address anterior column failure, ventral cord compression, and deformity. Open transthoracic approaches are traditionally utilised, often requiring lateral positioning for anterior column access and consequently intraoperative repositioning for posterior access (7). Whilst feasible, they carry high morbidity due to prolonged anaesthesia, pleural entry, single-lung ventilation, chest tube placement, and pulmonary complications (7). With improved patient survival, there is a growing demand for MIS techniques that reduce perioperative morbidity, expedite recovery, enable timely adjuvant therapy and minimise delayed construct failure (5,6). Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) approaches emerged as one such MIS technique, providing a less invasive alternative to open approaches that maintain anterior column access (7-9). However, they still require pleural entry, single lung ventilation and often lateral positioning (7,8). Tubular retractor-assisted retropleural approaches avoid lung deflation; however are constrained by a fixed deep narrow corridor which can limit their utility in complex reconstructions (7,10). Posterior-only approaches such as transpedicular corpectomy and percutaneous fixation facilitate single-position 360° reconstruction and can improve perioperative recovery, however anterior column reconstruction is more challenging and requires spinal cord retraction (7,11,12).

The PRP approach to thoracic corpectomy and reconstruction is a novel MIS technique that can address these limitations by providing simultaneous anterior and posterior column access in a single prone position for 360° reconstruction, allowing posterior release and fixation without bone or implant strain and intraoperative repositioning (7,13,14). By avoiding pleural violation and single lung ventilation, it reduces blood loss, surgical time, and perioperative complications (7,14). Prone lateral positioning mitigates surgeon fatigue and musculoskeletal strain during MIS by enabling a neutral posture (15).

The retropleural corridor poses technical challenges and requires extensive dissection at awkward angles under suboptimal loupe visualisation (7). Endoscope-assistance can enhance illumination and magnification for clearer exposure whilst optimising surgeon ergonomics and posture and providing the team a clear view of the operative field (15). These benefits may enable safer decompression and implant placement. Here we present a novel endoscope-assisted PRP approach for 360° reconstruction of a thoracic pathological fracture. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2025-1-253/rc).

Case presentation

The patient is a 67-year-old male with metastatic melanoma and prior left lower lobectomy for a pulmonary metastasis in 2014. He had received adjuvant chemotherapy, combination targeted therapy, and stereotactic ablative radiotherapy to C4, T9, and T10 metastases by 2020. His medical history included osteoporosis (T-Score −2.6).

He presented in 2024 after a fall with 2 months of mechanical thoracic pain and myelopathy with investigations demonstrating an unstable pathological T9 fracture with 10 mm retropulsion and severe canal stenosis. He underwent an uncomplicated open posterior T6–T12 fusion and decompression (Figure 1).

Pre-operative pathology and subsequent post-operative construct. (A) Pre-operative computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging in sagittal and axial planes demonstrating a pathological unstable T9 vertebral body fracture causing severe canal stenosis. (B) Post-operative anteroposterior and lateral X-ray imaging demonstrating the T6–12 posterior construct.

He re-presented 9 months later after another fall with recurrent thoracic pain and progressive thoracic kyphosis with suspected right rod fracture (Figure 2). A T8 superior endplate fracture, right T10 screw displacement, and interbody graft pneumatisation were identified on computed tomography (CT) imaging, suggesting a mobile segment.

Structural failure of the index posterior-only construct. (A) Lateral thoracic spine X-ray imaging demonstrates progressive kyphosis with a subtle small lucency adjacent to T10 suggestive of a fractured rod, as highlighted by the red circle. (B) Fractured right rod that was removed intraoperatively.

Following discussions in our institutional tumour board meeting, the consensus was that posterior construct revision alone would be inadequate, thus anterior column reconstruction was required. A MIS PRP T9 corpectomy and posterior construct revision were planned to facilitate 360° reconstruction without the need for patient repositioning. The patient underwent general anaesthesia, prone positioning, and continuous intraoperative neuromonitoring using continuous motor evoked potentials (MEPs) and somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs). On re-exposure of the T6–T12 instrumentation, a fractured right rod with adjacent metallosis and right T10 pedicle screw loosening was identified. Bilateral T10 screws were revised with stereotactic large-calibre rescue screws. Persistent segment mobility confirmed anterior reconstruction was required. Pre-emptive T10 pedicle subtraction osteotomy was performed to facilitate later kyphosis correction.

While positioned prone, a small right lateral chest wall incision and mini-thoracotomy were performed with subperiosteal dissection and resection of a 6 cm segment of the right 9th rib, preserving the intercostal neurovascular bundle located inferiorly within the costal groove. Selection of the 9th rib was based upon the patient’s anatomy, supplemented by intraoperative neuronavigation which confirmed optimal cephalocaudal alignment to provide a direct orthogonal trajectory and sufficient exposure of the lateral aspect of the collapsed T9 vertebral body within the retropleural corridor. A right-sided approach was favoured for two reasons; first, given the patient’s prior left lower lobectomy we anticipated significant pleuropulmonary adhesions and reactive fibrosis which could obscure the anatomical retropleural plane and increase pleural violation risk; and second, pre-operative CT angiography suggested that the artery of Adamkiewicz originated from the left posterior aorta at the T10/11 level, thus a right sided approach was favoured to minimise iatrogenic injury (16,17). The retropleural dissection was performed by separating the parietal pleura from endothoracic fascia, then gently sweeping the parietal pleura anteriorly by manual blunt dissection under direct vision until the rib head and T9 vertebral body were reached. A table-mounted lateral retractor system with integrated illumination was placed to maintain the retropleural corridor and T9 corpectomy was performed using a high-speed burr drill and rongeurs to create a flat parallel footprint, removing bone and tumour fragments, and preparing the endplates (Figure 3). Segmental vessels were identified at the midpoint of the vertebral body, coagulated using bipolar diathermy and divided. Endoscopic and fluoroscopic assistance throughout the corpectomy helped improve illumination, optimise visualisation around the deep retropleural contours, and provide magnification to reveal intricate anatomical detail. A seated neutral posture with relaxed shoulders optimised surgeon ergonomics.

A corpectomy cage was infeasible due to severe T9 collapse. Instead, under continuous direct endoscopic visualisation and stepwise fluoroscopic guidance, a low-profile 18×40 mm 10° lordotic expandable titanium eXtreme Lateral Interbody Fusion cage was utilised, complemented by repurposed autograft from the acquired right 9th rib. The cage was inserted in its most compressed state (18 mm) and expanded in a stepwise manner. Initial expansion focused on engaging the T8 and T10 endplates to achieve secure purchase and subsequently expanded in 1–2 mm increments (final height 32 mm) to distract the anterior column and open the wedge kyphotic deformity. A titanium quad-rod posterior construct with cross-link and bone graft secured the T6–T12 screws against rotational and longitudinal forces, providing posterior support. The combination of T10 pedicle subtraction osteotomy, prone positioning leveraging gravity, and the anterior distraction from the expandable cage acting as a fulcrum, allowed posterior segmental compression and cantilevered manoeuvres via the rod construct to restore anterior and posterior column stability and a more physiological thoracic kyphosis. Pleura remained intact and instrumentation was removed under vision. No significant changes in MEPs or SSEPs were observed during corpectomy, cage expansion or kyphosis correction. Surgical time was 345 minutes with 150 mL estimated blood loss. No pneumothorax was evident on the immediate post-operative chest X-ray. Postoperative X-ray and CT showed satisfactory cage position and kyphosis correction which was preserved at 12-months (Figure 4). The patient was mobilised on day 1 and discharged home on day 5 without perioperative complications. His modified Rankin scale was 0 at 12 months.

Discussion

With advances in oncological therapies extending survival, spinal surgeons are confronted with secondary complications including pathological fractures and progressive deformity requiring robust reconstructions to minimise delayed construct failure. Traditional thoracic anterior column reconstruction carries considerable morbidity; open transthoracic approaches afford anterior access but risk pulmonary complications whilst posterior approaches limit ventral decompression and implant placement (7,18,19). MIS techniques are increasingly utilised to promote early mobility and minimise blood loss, operative time, and hospital stay without increasing pseudoarthrosis (14,19-21). The endoscope-assisted PRP approach extends MIS benefits while overcoming traditional open limitations (22).

Our case highlights that MIS PRP corpectomy with 360° reconstruction enables simultaneous anterior and posterior column access while reducing implant stress and avoiding repositioning or staged approaches. The MIS PRP approach limits pleural violation and avoids single-lung ventilation and chest drain requirement. Prone positioning facilitates kyphotic deformity correction and reduces soft tissue injury by gravity (13). Larger expandable cages can be placed without spinal cord manipulation and segmental vessels are more easily identifiable, improving deformity correction while minimising neurovascular injury risk (22,23). Compared with lateral, prone positioning provides anatomical familiarity and improves ergonomics and access (Table 1). Neutral neck posture and forward arm angles reduce surgeon fatigue and improve patient safety (Figures 5,6) (15). A radiolucent rotatable spinal table helps dynamically optimise patient positioning. Most retropleural approaches utilise lateral decubitus positioning thus further studies are needed to quantify the operative and clinical benefits of prone positioning (13,14,23).

A PRP approach is not without challenges, particularly the steep learning curve and technical limitations imposed by the narrow and deep retropleural corridor (22,23). When lateral or without endoscope-assistance, surgeons often adopt non-neutral posture and awkward arm positions, increasing fatigue and inefficiency (15). Technical difficulty in retropleural dissection and surgeon unfamiliarity with thoracic anatomy further complicate access. Adhesions secondary to pulmonary fibrosis or previous surgery may obscure the pleural-fascial plane, often necessitating contralateral access to minimise pleural violation risk (22,23).

The endoscope is a unifying MIS adjunct, providing direct visualisation, bright illumination, variable video magnification and angled optics to optimise exposure (24). Given the proximity of critical neurovascular and visceral structures, surgeons prefer direct visualisation rather than relying on fluoroscopy alone to delineate tissue planes and avoid iatrogenic injury. In the deep retropleural corridor, conventional loupes and retractor systems offer suboptimal illumination and fixed magnification (10,25). The endoscope overcomes this, allowing safer identification of neurovascular structures and improving surgical precision by revealing intricate anatomical detail (24). Combined with a scope holder and a “clear view” ergonomic setup, it enables two-handed operating (15,26,27). Within the retropleural corridor, the endoscope helps support neutral posture, minimises surgeon fatigue, assists with the steep learning curve of MIS particularly for surgeons trained in spinal endoscopy, which can then improve patient safety. It enables precise ventral decompression, implant placement and anterior column stabilisation while reducing incision size, tissue disruption and improving cosmesis (7,28,29).

The positioning, visualisation and access advantages of an endoscope-assisted PRP approach can help overcome the limitations of existing MIS alternatives for complex 360° reconstruction. Thoracoscopic approaches provide direct anterior column visualisation but require pleural entry, single-lung ventilation, and often lateral positioning, constraints risking patient morbidity particularly in frail oncological patients with pre-existing respiratory disease or lobectomy (7,19). Furthermore, lateral positioning precludes simultaneous posterior fixation without time-consuming repositioning. Tubular retractor-assisted retropleural approaches avoid pleural violation but impose a fixed narrow cylindrical corridor with constrained instrument angulation and suboptimal visualisation at depth which can limit more complex decompression (10). The endoscope circumvents these ‘line-of-sight’ limitations, providing a manoeuvrable field with magnification and angled optics. A posterior-only approach via transpedicular corpectomy with MIS percutaneous fixation facilitates circumferential decompression without approach-related pulmonary morbidity, however anterior column reconstruction, cage placement (particularly if large footprint), and deformity correction manoeuvres remain technically challenging and require spinal cord retraction (7,11,12). Posterior decompression and fixation alone without corpectomy provides limited anterior column support, evidenced by our patient’s index construct failure which necessitated subsequent 360° reconstruction.

Successful implementation of the endoscope-assisted PRP technique requires proficiency in navigating pulmonary and vascular anatomical challenges and possessing effective strategies to manage intraoperative complications. Pleural violation can occur during retropleural dissection or from prolonged retractor pressure, leading to pneumothorax. Primary repair should be attempted using a 4-0 absorbable polyglycolic suture (7,30). To promote lung re-expansion, a small calibre catheter (e.g. red rubber catheter) can be placed through the pleural opening surrounded by a purse string suture, submerging the free end in saline solution; a Valsalva is then performed until no more air is seen escaping, after which the catheter is removed and suture tied (2,30). Fibrin sealants or muscle graft can supplement primary closure. A chest drain is rarely indicated unless there is suboptimal lung re-expansion or significant pneumothorax on post-operative chest X-ray. Intercostal neuralgia can occur secondary to prolonged rib retraction or iatrogenic intercostal bundle injury during mini-thoracotomy. Though difficult to treat, it can be mitigated through judicious rib retraction, avoiding chest drains, and utilising early regional anaesthesia or gabapentinoids (31).

Haemorrhage from inadvertent segmental vessel laceration can lead to spinal cord ischaemia. Identification and bipolar cautery of segmental vessels at the mid-vertebral body prior to corpectomy can protect intersegmental anastomoses and radiculomedullary branches located adjacent to the neural foramen, facilitated by the visualisation advantages of the endoscope. Furthermore, pre-operative identification of the artery of Adamkiewicz is a prerequisite for safe operative planning given its highly variable origin, often arising on the left between T9–L1 (16,17). If necessary, alterations to the surgical approach can reduce iatrogenic injury risk and consequent anterior spinal artery syndrome (17).

Technical challenges regarding endoscopic visualisation, expertise, and cost can be barriers to successful implementation. The endoscopic field of view within the narrow retropleural corridor is highly susceptible to obscuration by blood products. Integrating a comprehensive range of haemostatic strategies can help minimise disruptions; incorporating warmed saline irrigation with adjuncts, optimisation of flow and pressure, and endoscopic rescue strategies (32). In instances of refractory haemorrhage, the approach offers the versatility to revert to direct visualisation for traditional haemostasis, reintroducing the endoscope once controlled. While the endoscope may reduce the learning curve for surgeons trained in spinal endoscopy, adequately managing such pitfalls and challenges particularly in complex cases requires proficiency in navigating both thoracic MIS prone lateral access and spinal endoscopy. Challenges relate to limited anatomical landmark visualisation and disorientation, with reductions in operative time and complications occurring after 30 cases (11). Surgeons should maintain a low threshold for conversion to open or posterior-only approaches should technical challenges arise intraoperatively that may compromise patient safety. Finally, this approach can have increased upfront costs and equipment requirements which may limit its widespread adoption within resource-constrained environments. However, the reduced hospital stay, post-operative complications and patient morbidity associated with MIS may render it more cost-effective long term (33).

Conclusions

This is the first reported case demonstrating the technical feasibility of an endoscope-assisted MIS PRP approach for 360° reconstruction of an unstable pathological thoracic fracture. It highlights how the endoscope can assist with mitigating the technical challenges, ergonomic strain, and visualisation limitations of MIS. While it supports its adoption in complex thoracic reconstructions, further prospective studies are required to evaluate clinical outcomes compared to established open and MIS alternatives.
