The behavioral profile of a detection dog is tuned for the dog’s role and their environment
Authors: Isain Zapata, Sofia Zapata, Alexander W Eyre, Jared A Schuetter, Cinthya M Otto, James A Serpell, Carlos E Alvarez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDetection roles such as search and rescue, wildlife management and detection of various substances are essential for emergency response, security and monitoring services. To increase their effectiveness, dogs performing these tasks are subject to intense training and selection. However, it is unclear how their behavior in general and for particular traits may be specifically tuned for their role. A total of 1,117 detection dogs assessed over 15 behavioral factors from the WDC-BARQ were included in this descriptive study. Effectiveness on scent detection and behavior performance rated by their handlers on a scale from 1 to 10 is also included. Detection roles such as contraband, medical, and pest detection show behavioral profiles that diverge from the average working dog, often with elevated scores in traits considered less desirable, like Dog Directed Fear and Touch Sensitivity. Breed-specific patterns reveal that Labrador Retrievers and German Shepherds generally align with average profiles, though Labradors show favorable traits for endangered species detection despite low handler ratings. Belgian Malinois display a mix of desirable and undesirable traits, particularly in dual training roles, while German Shorthaired Pointers excel in explosives and narcotics detection but are less suited for tracking tasks. These findings suggest that dog behavior is tuned by role and environment, underscoring the need for context-based interpretation where profile patterns may better predict success than individual traits. Introduction

For centuries, dogs have been bred for different roles and to aid in our daily lives1. Some lineages are bred to guard or herd livestock; others are bred to run down prey or track targets2. As of 2022, the U.S. government had over five thousand dogs working in jobs such as search and rescue (SAR), wildlife management, and the detection of various substances, including narcotics, bombs, and human remains3. The wide variety of jobs that dogs have been selected and bred for has also created a wide variety of dog temperaments, both intentionally and unintentionally4,5. Having a way to predict a dog’s temperament benefits both dog owners and the dog itself6, as it allows for the placing of dogs into roles that better suit them, whether it involves being part of a family with children or engaging in extensive search and rescue operations.

Scent detection dogs are particularly demanding of attention due to the high time and labor costs associated with raising and training them for safe and reliable performance. Trainers often seek specific dog breeds such as Labrador Retrievers or Belgian Malinois to raise the odds their dog will have the traits needed for detection work. However, there are diverse types of odor detection work and environments, such as for contraband, narcotics, explosives, or medical condition detection7,8. Many candidate dogs are not selected for the program or are eliminated from it due to unsuitable temperament9. This unsuitable temperament is often summarized as the capacity of the dog to adapt to working in specific environments that can pose inherent challenges. For example, terrain hurdles that can complicate access to areas of interest10, crowded areas with multiple simultaneous stimuli, situations where purposeful deceit to the dogs’ senses is attempted5, or high-pressure combat and law enforcement operations where direct threat and complex stimuli are present11. This is problematic for training agencies, as it is costly to the training program and results in fewer certified dogs, and possibly shortages in the field8.

While dogs were clearly selected for morphology and behavior, it is an open question to what extent different breeds are suited for diverse types of work2,12. Although our understanding of the average dog profile for most common breeds has grown in the past decade with the development of behavioral assessment tools and applications such as the C-BARQ13,14, our knowledge of the traits needed for a dog to succeed in a given role has not expanded to the same extent5,8,15 and these are often disconnected from performance benchmarks that focus on task performance efficiency. Within scent detection roles, the diversity of environments and tasks may benefit from certain behavioral traits would be more desirable in specific contexts. Consequently, breeds with a higher prevalence of those traits may hold a slight advantage in particular roles5. A stratified analysis of detection dog behavioral traits allows for the identification of those most important for the different types of work. This can assist in matching dogs to the specializations for which they are best suited, reducing the risks and costs associated with breeding programs for those applications.

We recently adapted the C-BARQ for use with working dogs (WDC-BARQ)16 where behaviors were assessed, and the factor constructs in this adapted version are defined in the context of working dog that may differ from the pet context. The WDC-BARQ questionnaire includes 15 factors where 10 of them are consistent with general behavior, and the remaining 5 factors are work-specific. Therefore, the objective of this study was to use the WDC-BARQ survey of privately owned dogs to describe detection dogs role profiles based on work type and performance environment. This study was developed in collaboration with many working dog communities, which are diverse and often guided by tradition and subjective experience. To foster engagement, we adopted a descriptive approach, avoiding any prescriptive narrative that might be perceived as a critique of existing training and selection practices. By identifying behavioral profiles prevalent to detection work, this study supports more effective selection, training, and deployment of working dogs across critical roles.

Results

The WDC-BARQ survey dataset included a total of 1,117 detection working dogs. This sample included 954 (85.41%) purebred dogs from 105 breeds. The highest numbers were for Labrador Retriever (n = 214, 19.16%), followed by German Shepherd (172 individuals, 15.40%), Belgian Malinois (n = 123, 11.01%) and the German Shorthair Pointer (n = 85, 7.61%). The proportions of female dogs are 43.51% and of neutered/spayed dogs 24.80%. Factor analysis of the WDC-BARQ revealed 10 factors in common with the original C-BARQ instrument developed for pets: Stranger Directed Fear, Dog Aggression, Excitability, Separation Related Behavior, Trainability, Dog Directed Fear, Touch Sensitivity, Stranger Directed Aggression, Energy, and Attachment Attention Seeking. The WDC-BARQ also captured four new factors addressing other domains of dog behavior such as Playfulness, Impulsivity, Distractibility and Basophobia (fear or falling) and an additional factor labeled Food Focus16.

All 15 WDC-BARQ factor constructs were used to create the profiles presented. Detection dog role profiles were generated by calculating the mean score difference of the dogs performing a specific detection role versus the full dataset mean, significance was determined using a two-sample t-test for independent means comparison assuming unequal variances (Welch’s test). Data to make these calculations are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. This difference highlights the relevance of some factor constructs and how significant they are for the specific role. Detection dog profiles by role are presented in Fig. 1. The most represented detection roles were human remains (n = 372), and live human air scent (n = 322). The least represented detection roles were contraband and accelerant detection (n = 15, 17, respectively). Average pattern profiles are unique to each role and exclusive to the WDC-BARQ context of working dogs. These average profiles highlight the relative importance of the measured factors. This is based on the linear interpretation of C-BARQ scores where higher scores are less desirable to humans with the exception of trainability (generally applicable to owners, trainers and handler). Even when there may be some selection bias from the beginning, dogs working in live human tracking, live human air scent and human remains detection do not show a profile different from the mean of all detection dogs. An example of a role with factors deviating from the average is contraband detection dogs, which have less separation related behavior (-0.401 ± 0.185, P = 0.0497) and more dog directed fear, food focus, and touch sensitivity although some not reaching significance (0.370 ± 0.238, P = 0.0019; 0.378 0.924, P = 0.1290; 0.434 ± 0.198, P < 0.0001 respectively) than the average detection dog. Contraband detection dog profiles are like those of narcotics and explosives detection dogs. Endangered species detection dogs have reduced stranger directed fear and touch sensitivity (-0.348 ± 0.162, P = 0.0301; -0.327 ± 0.106, P = 0.0405 respectively). Medical detection dog and pest detection dog profiles deviate the most from the average working dog profile. Most relevant factors for these roles fall on the side of less desirable when compared to the full cohort means. This suggests a potential gap where better suited dog breed could be introduced to such roles.

Overall WDC-BARQ ratings put in context these interpretations. For instance, endangered species roles had the highest number of above average ratings (-0.119 ± 0.139, P = 0.0012). In contrast, medical detection and pest detection dogs displayed the worst ratings (0.128 ± 0.159, P = 0.0014; 0.123 ± 0.129, P = 0.0010 respectively). Handler ratings on scent and behavior performance are often discrepant with WDC-BARQ overall ratings. There are instances where dogs poorly rated by their handlers seem to perform better according to their WDC-BARQ mean rating. For example, endangered and invasive species detection roles are often rated as having poor behavior despite falling on the desirable side of the WDC-BARQ mean. Conversely, pest detection dogs are generally rated positively despite being on the less desirable side of WDC-BARQ overall ratings.

Environments showed a similar pattern as roles. Detection dogs described as performing their job inside and outside of buildings, open areas and vehicles generally show few distinguishing traits. However, more unique contexts such as working in aircraft, cargo, humanborne, rail and watercraft display the most unique patterns although only one reaching significance (P = 0.4246, P = 0.0949, P = 0.1387, P = 0.0051, P = 0.0820, respectively). Across all environments with characteristic profiles, dogs with less excitability, separation related behaviors, and energy but more dog directed fear and food focus are more common. German Shepherds are more often employed in human remains and dual training roles. German Shepherds and Labrador Retrievers are strongly positive in narcotics detection roles but poorer in explosives detection roles, and that is accentuated in the former. However, German Shepherds have a smaller discrepancy between the WDC-BARQ overall rating and the handler rating in comparison to the Labrador Retriever. Environment-wise, the German Shepherd has mostly positive ratings for all environments, with the best being rail and aircraft.

In comparison to other breeds in the present survey, Belgian Malinois have a high proportion used for dual training and make up a high proportion of all dual training dogs. Belgian Malinois profiles are notable for having a greater mix of desirable and undesirable instrument ratings. The exception is Belgian Malinois working in the undefined “other” environments, where they have less desirable WDC-BARQ scores but are rated strongly positively by their handlers. In terms of environments, the Belgian Malinois contrasts with the German Shepherd by having some WDC-BARQ overall ratings falling on the undesirable side. This is generally concordant with the handler ratings.

German Shorthaired Pointers are predominantly used in an explosives detection role. They have desirable profiles for narcotics and live human air scent but less so for live human tracking detection. German Shorthaired Pointers are similar to German Shepherds in their performance across environments. Both are generally positive for overall WDC-BARQ and handler ratings.

Discussion

This study’s objective was to use the WDC-BARQ survey of privately owned dogs to describe detection dog’s role profiles based on work type and performance environment where we describe behavioral patterns linked to specific detection roles and environments. These findings offer initial criteria and set a behavioral profile reference to guide successful detection work, based on both WDC-BARQ data and handler ratings. Discrepancies between the two may reflect differences in task demands versus handler preferences. Rising demand for working dogs has intensified interest in selection and training. While traits like confidence, trainability, low fearfulness, and reduced body sensitivity are broadly desirable, others vary by job type17. Breed diversity is expanding, with sporting breeds such as Flat Coated Retrievers, Vizslas, and German Shorthaired/Wirehaired Pointers now recommended alongside Labrador Retrievers18. The use of sporting breeds for detection is generally attributed to adaptation of hunting predispositions for odor-based searching work. In addition, the notion that non-intimidating “floppy-eared” breeds are necessary for work among the public as opposed to intimidating “pointy-eared” breeds that are indicated for deterrence.

This descriptive study begins to define optimal behavioral profiles for olfactory detection across breeds and settings. Performance remains the key outcome, but the behavioral traits driving success are complex15 and long-term success is the main concern for trainers19. Often, moderate levels of traits (for example, energy) are ideal, and trait combinations matter. For example, arousal may enhance detection but also increase distractibility20. While C-BARQ scores in pets typically reflect problem severity, traits like trainability, energy, and excitability are more nuanced21.The WDC-BARQ and our comparative approach help clarify how behavior, environment, and performance interact. The profiles presented in this study can be indicators of the traits required for optimal performance and traits that are not highly relevant. Distractibility is a defined factor in the WDC-BARQ even when other factors may contribute to distractions based on context. For example, in our data we observed that food focus behaviors are indicative of below average performance for specific roles such contraband, explosives and narcotics detection since these activities will be performed in areas that are likely to have food around that can act as a distractor. Similarly, less excitable passenger screening dogs are preferred as they work among crowds with many distracting stimuli or varied types. Studies have also found that physical characteristics of the environments such as crowded passenger lines of baggage, outdoors and outside of cars can directly affect detections accuracy22,23 which may be influenced by distractors, in these spaces the presence of other strange dogs traveling with passengers may trigger Dog-directed and Stranger Directed fear. Also, the mere closeness to people makes more likely for unexpected and unintended physical contact with the detection dog triggering issues with Touch sensitivity and Playfulness to occur. Findings from this study provide important context for the optimization of specific behavioral requirement criteria that are particularly relevant for a specific kind of scent detection work.

Breed specific profiles

In this descriptive study we present profiles for the four most common breeds in the survey (Labrador Retriever, German Shepherd, Belgian Malinois and German Shorthaired Pointer). Each breed has unique characteristics that make it compatible with the preferred profile for different roles. Breeds are selected based on many aspects including tradition, expert recommendation, and research13,17,24. Labrador Retrievers, German Shepherds and Belgian Malinois have received the most attention in research of performance for explosives detection20,25,26. Labrador Retrievers have been rated highest in studies evaluating performance in detection of narcotics22 but are followed closely by Belgian Malinois and German Shepherds. A different study of drug detection performance also concluded that Labrador Retriever performed slightly better than German Shepherds23. The difference between German Shepherd and Belgian Malinois WDC-BARQ performance profiles is puzzling considering a study showed no scent accuracy differences27.

The engrained traditions about the effectiveness of certain breeds in detection work seem to rarely be questioned by the owners and institutions using them. This survey showed discrepancies between WDC-BARQ breed profiles and handler ratings, which suggests performance can be more subjective depending on the rater28. Dually trained dogs from these four breeds showed a high range of overall handler ratings but a small range and discordant ranking of overall instrument ratings. Handlers rated the German Shorthaired Pointer the best and the Belgian Malinois the worst. However, the overall instrument rating placed the Belgian Malinois first and the German Shorthaired Pointer second. Moreover, performance can be affected by the handler’s behavior29. It is also possible that breed stereotypes can give rise to unrealistic performance expectations of handlers23,30.

German Shorthair Pointers are highly represented in this detection dog survey. That reflects their dramatic and continuing rise in popularity as detection dogs and as pets (since 2019, among the top ten most popular breeds in the US according to AKC registrations). The core conventional wisdom behind their next-in-line introduction as detection dogs seems to be that retrievers and pointers are both hunting dogs. However, we are not aware of well powered studies comparing their detection performance. This work suggests how breeds compare across work types. Among the surprising findings, both overall instrument scores and handler ratings ranked German Shepherds the best for narcotics and worst for explosives among the top four breeds used in detection. Whereas many WDC-BARQ factors scored consistently in the two work roles, several seem to explain the difference by having large effects but with the direction flipped from positive to negative.

Considerations and limitations

Four main limitations can be identified in this descriptive study. First, the representation of roles and environments is sufficient for some categories but insufficient for others. Detection dog roles are subject to purpose and demand, therefore some of the detection activities are for the most part performed for specific government organizations and law enforcement agencies such as explosives, drug and narcotics detection among others5,17. Other activities are more often carried out by civilian service organizations or enthusiasts8. This segregation of roles is possible to have introduced some sampling bias and also limits the reliability of some of the estimates presented. Second, and very important for this study, is context. The context of the roles and environments in relation to behavior evaluated in this study can have a large amount of ambiguity15. The interpretation and relevance of each of the factor constructs will depend on the context of the role performed by the dog. For example, dogs working in Live Human Air Scent will be more likely to be working in outdoor environments while dogs working in contraband detection will be more likely working in confined interior spaces such as cargo and baggage areas. The combined interpretation of each role and environment will be dependent on such context. Third, breeds selection for dogs in a particular role is not random, agencies and organizations favor specific breeds with specific characteristics for each detection role. This can be based on tradition, expert trainer recommendation24 or research development17. This nonrandom selection can influence the profiles we present in this study since some roles will be more likely performed by specific breeds31,32. ; with some breed being unlikely to ever be performing any of these detection dog roles. These limitations open future research directions focused in developing the context of these roles and environments. This last argument leads into our fourth and last consideration on the inherent limitations of questionnaire-based methods without direct observation corroboration. Questionnaire will always be limited in capturing the full context and understanding of the status quo of owners/handlers and the tasks being performed. Our findings showed a discrepancy between handler overall ratings and WDC-BARQ overall averages suggesting areas for improvement of objective assessment.

Future directions

Our findings highlight the need to redefine “working dog skill” beyond simple performance metrics. Detection success depends on a complex interplay of traits such as cognition, temperament, olfaction, health, and environment. Access to standardized data from large institutions remains limited33–37, . More recently, several types of testing have been introduced to better understand working dog cognition and behavior17,20. Also necessary to move the field forward is the establishment of a set of behavioral terminology across work types17,38,39. Factor analysis of WDC-BARQ data revealed ten shared traits with the original pet-focused C-BARQ, plus five new ones (Playfulness, Impulsivity, Distractibility, Basophobia, and Food Focus) which may correlate with success and longevity in detection dogs. These instruments offer a foundation for a shared behavioral lexicon, even if interpretations vary. We’ve also proposed leveraging behavioral questionnaire data through latent class analysis13 and artificial intelligence40 to predict dog suitability across criteria. As observed with distractibility, although it is a factor, there is potential overlap with other factors warranting further assessment and refinement.

Integrating behavioral, genetic, and neurological methods will deepen understanding. Promising areas include genetic mapping41–44 ; brain imaging45–47. olfactory acuity variability48; and stress response biomarkers49. These approaches pave the way for more targeted and effective detection dog development50.

Conclusion

Detection dogs provide essential services for emergency response, safety and monitoring activities embedded in our everyday lives. An important aspect of their effectiveness is directly associated with behavioral characteristics that manifest in their performance. Due to the wide array of requirements and conditions in which these tasks must be performed, the preferred behavior of detection dogs can vary depending on the role and environment. Thus, behavior should be interpreted within the context of the task being performed. Our descriptive results point to areas which could yield information to optimize the training and selection of detection dogs. First, patterns of traits may be better predictors of successful detection dogs rather than individual traits or sets of traits. Second, both nature (genetics, incl. breed) and nurture (environment, incl. socialization and training) could be used for evaluating dogs. Lastly, the discrepancies between breed specific handler assessments and WDC-BARQ are not straightforward to interpret. The inclusion of performance metrics in future studies will resolve that and help define detection dog skills and behavioral dispositions for success. This work shows how comparative analysis can help isolate the effects of detection type, environment, and breed in such efforts.

Materials and methods

Approach and data

Data used in this study were sourced from the original WDC-BARQ development study that was verified and pruned of duplicated and incomplete responses. This dataset included the responses of detection dog owners and handlers who were recruited through working dog organizations and social media platforms. Owners were also recruited through direct invitation by the Penn Vet Working Dog Center during conferences and training events. Detailed description of recruitment efforts is presented in Hare et al. 202416. This research was pre-reviewed by the University of Pennsylvania Office of the Institutional Review Board and determined not to require IRB review. All owner/handler participants received informed consent and provided their data voluntarily. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

The database compiles data on the 53 WDC-BARQ questions that are used to define 15 factor constructs. These constructs are Stranger Directed Fear, Playfulness, Distractibility, Dog Aggression, Basophobia, Excitability, Separation Related Behavior, Trainability, Dog Directed Fear, Food Focus, Touch Sensitivity, Stranger Directed Aggression, Energy, Attachment Attention Seeking, and Impulsivity. The database also includes binary answers to specific detection activity roles, binary responses to environment types in which the detection work is performed, and dog demographics such as sex and breed. In addition, the questionnaire also collects scent and behavior handler ratings (1–10 scale, with 1 being the worst and 10 the best).

Statistical analysis

The main strategy in this study is to compare descriptively the mean profiles derived of the 15 WDC-BARQ factor constructs for each sub stratified group (detection role, environment and presented independently by the most represented breeds) and compare it to the full dataset overall mean. This approach allows us to visually detect profiles and factor constructs that are similar or different from the detection dog full dataset mean. This is also done with the handler scent and behavior ratings. Since the WDC-BARQ, just as the original pet C-BARQ14,21 codes answers as problematic behavior in a 0 to 4 scale (being 0, no display of the behavior, and 4, the most extreme display of the behavior). These factor-construct scores are arithmetic means or averages of the individual questions that define each of the factor constructs. These averages are calculated only within the context of the WDC-BARQ. Each of the 53 questions can only be included in one factor construct. Statistical significance was determined using a two-sample t-test for independent means comparison assuming unequal variances (Welch’s test). All the data to make these calculations (n, mean and standard deviation for each variable and overall mean variables) are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

WDC-BARQ factor construct, and handler ratings can be interpreted as being above or below the detection dog full database mean and therefore can be defined in terms of how preferable they are. For WDC-BARQ, scores are interpreted linearly, where scores that are above the detection dog full dataset mean are considered less preferred or less desirable (since higher WDC-BARQ scores indicate higher levels of problematic behavior). Handler scores are interpreted in the opposite way since higher scores are preferred. Overall WDC-BARQ and handler ratings presented in the figures are presented as averages of the individual items for each of the sub stratified group. All data is presented descriptively with sample sizes displayed in each figure. Profiles for which less than 5 dogs are included are not displayed. All calculations for means for the full database and for all sub stratified groups were performed in SAS v.9.4.
