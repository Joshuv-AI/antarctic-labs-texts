Mitochondrial dysfunction in sepsis-induced liver injury: from pathophysiology to preclinical therapeutic targets
Authors: Mingming Fei, Yao Xu, Peng Jin, Yinfeng Wang, Min Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sepsis, defined as a life-threatening organ dysfunction arising from a dysregulated host response to infection, often culminates in multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS). The liver, central to metabolism, detoxification, and immune regulation, is particularly vulnerable in this context. Sepsis-induced liver injury (SILI), a frequent and severe complication, markedly worsens clinical outcomes. A growing body of evidence implicates mitochondrial dysfunction as a central pathogenic hub in SILI. During sepsis, mitochondria exhibit profound derangements, including morphological abnormalities, collapse of the membrane potential, respiratory chain failure, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, altered dynamics, and defective mitophagy. Beyond intrinsic cellular injury, these dysfunctional organelles can amplify systemic inflammation by releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which in turn activate innate immune pathways mediated by Toll-like receptors (TLRs), NOD-like receptors (NLRs), and the cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) axis. This review provides an integrative overview of the current understanding of mitochondrial dysfunction in SILI. We critically evaluate preclinical advances in mitochondrial-targeted therapies and highlight emerging strategies that hold translational promise for mitigating hepatic injury and improving patient outcomes.

Introduction

Sepsis, a devastating syndrome triggered by infection, shock, or severe trauma, remains a leading cause of mortality in intensive care units (ICUs). It is now understood not as a localized inflammatory disorder but as a systemic condition defined by life-threatening organ dysfunction stemming from a dysregulated host response [1–3]. At its core, the pathophysiology of sepsis reflects a complex and dynamic interplay between invading pathogens and host defenses. Clinical outcome are contingent upon a precarious balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory responses. Disruption of this equilibrium frequently precipitates multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), a key determinant of prognosis and a major contributor to sepsis-related morbidity and mortality [3–5].

Within this complex pathophysiological landscape, the liver occupies a pivotal position as both a metabolic and an immune sentinel. It is indispensable for host defense and systemic homeostasis, mediating the clearance of pathogens and toxins. However, its extensive exposure to circulating inflammatory cytokines renders it highly susceptible to injury during sepsis [6, 7]. The clinical manifestations of sepsis-induced liver injury (SILI) are diverse, encompassing hypoxic hepatitis, cholestasis, impaired protein synthesis, and coagulopathy [8]. Although the liver possesses remarkable regenerative capacity, the onset of acute hepatic dysfunction during sepsis exacerbates the systemic illness and is strongly associated with adverse outcomes. Indeed, SILI has been identified as an independent predictor of mortality in septic patients [9]. Currently, specific and effective therapeutic strategies for SILI are lacking, and its underlying pathophysiology remains incompletely understood. Consequently, the development of effective preventive and therapeutic interventions for SILI represents a critical unmet need to mitigate hepatic injury and patient prognosis.

Recent scientific advances have substantially expanded our understanding of how sepsis orchestrates organ injury at molecular and cellular levels. Nevertheless, the pathogenesis of SILI remains exceedingly complex, involving a convergence of mechanisms that include the inflammatory cascade [10, 11], pyroptosis [12, 13], oxidative stress (OS) [14, 15], dysregulated mitophagy [16], and aberrant microRNA (miRNA) signaling [17]. These interconnected processes disrupt systemic homeostasis, ultimately leading to organ failure. Among these mechanisms, mitochondrial dysfunction has emerged as a central and pervasive contributor to the pathogenesis of SILI [18, 19]. A deeper mechanistic understanding of the role of mitochondria in SILI is therefore essential, as it may reveal novel therapeutic targets and pave the way for effective interventions.

Mitochondrial dysfunction

Beyond their canonical role as the “powerhouses” responsible for adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis, mitochondria function as central hubs for intracellular signaling [20, 21]. These organelles are indispensable for numerous essential cellular processes, including the urea cycle, iron metabolism, calcium homeostasis, thermoregulation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism, cell proliferation, and signal transduction [22–24]. Furthermore, mitochondria play a critical role in modulating innate immune responses, thereby shaping the progression of inflammation [25–28]. Accordingly, disruptions in core mitochondrial processes—such as biogenesis, fusion-fission dynamics, or mitophagy—can profoundly disturb systemic homeostasis and compromise organ function [29].

As a key metabolic organ, the liver is characterized by a high mitochondria density, which is indispensable for maintain hepatocyte homeostasis [30, 31]. Hepatic mitochondria are fundamental to glucose, lipid, and protein metabolism, as well as orchestrating diverse signaling pathways [32, 33]. They also function as sophisticated nutrient sensors that continuously adapt to fluctuating metabolic demands [34]. It is therefore not surprising that mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of numerous liver diseases, where it contributes directly to hepatocellular injury, apoptosis, and compensatory proliferation [35, 36]. In the context of sepsis, this dysfunction is aggravated by the excessive production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), which damage the mitochondrial respiratory chain (MRC) and precipitate widespread bioenergetic failure. Importantly, these impaired mitochondria not only lose their metabolic competence but also actively propagate inflammation by activating key signaling pathways mediated by Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and NOD-like receptors (NLRs) [25, 37]. A thorough investigation into the precise role of mitochondrial dysfunction in SILI is therefore essential for elucidating its pathogenesis and guiding the development of effective therapeutic strategies.

Pathophysiological mechanisms of mitochondrial dysfunction in sepsis-induced liver injury

Aberrant mitochondrial morphology

In SILI, structural derangements in mitochondria are a consistent pathological feature, closely linked to functional impairment. Electron microscopy of hepatic tissue from murine models of sepsis, such as that induced by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP), reveals marked mitochondrial swelling, compromised membrane integrity, and extensive fragmentation or loss of cristae [38]. Consistent with these observations, other studies have identified hepatic mitochondria are early and primary targets of endotoxemia-induced injury, emphasizing their pivotal role in SILI pathogenesis [39].

Interestingly, the mechanisms underlying injury to the inner and outer mitochondrial membranes appear to be distinct. Damage to the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) seems intrinsically associated with the mitochondrial permeability transition (MPT). Under pathological conditions marked by acidosis, calcium dysregulation, and iron overload, microbial metabolites may directly disrupt the selective permeability of the IMM, thereby promoting organ dysfunction [40]. For instance, aquaporin-8 (AQP8), a water channel protein localized to the IMM of hepatocytes, has been implicated in this process. In rat models of sepsis, reduced AQP8 expression correlates with mitochondrial swelling, whereas pharmacological agents like ethyl pyruvate appear to improve outcomes by attenuating this swelling via regulation of AQP8 expression [41].

In contrast, damage to the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) can occur independently of MPT. The translocator protein (TSPO), located on the OMM, exemplifies an alternative mechanism. TSPO has been shown to play a role in SILI, primarily by modulating liver macrophage polarization and attenuating inflammatory responses, rather than by directly altering mitochondrial membrane permeability [42].

Mitochondrial membrane potential collapse

A hallmark of SILI is the collapse of the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), an electrochemical gradient fundamental to ATP synthesis and a crucial indicator of mitochondrial integrity. This phenomenon directly correlates with diminished ATP levels and reduced enzymatic activity of mitochondrial complexes I and III, indicative of profound bioenergetic failure [43]. The dissipation of MMP in SILI appears to result from at least two interrelated processes: the pathological opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP) and the induction of ferroptosis.

A persistent decline in MMP is most commonly associated with the pathological opening of the MPTP, a high-conductance channel in the IMM. While transient MPTP opening is physiologically important for Ca²⁺ efflux and redox signaling, its sustained activation during sepsis enhances IMM permeability. This leads to a MMP dissipation, mitochondrial swelling, uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), excessive ROS production, and a profound decline in ATP synthesis [40]. Although its precise regulation remains incompletely defined, components such as F1F0-ATP synthase, adenine nucleotide translocase (ANT), and cyclophilin D (Cyp-D) are recognized as key mediators. Critically, changes in MMP, particularly the uncontrolled opening of the MPTP, invariably result in mitochondrial swelling, the release of matrix contents, and the complete collapse of the MMP. The deleterious effects of MPTP activation are not confined to the liver; its role in septic cardiomyopathy, for instance, underscores its systemic significance [44].

Ferroptosis has also emerged as a critical mechanism driving MMP collapse in SILI. Experimental evidence shows that SILI activates ferroptosis in murine liver tissue [45]. In vitro, erastin-induced ferroptosis was shown to reduce hepatocyte viability, increase lipid peroxidation, and compromise the MMP, further supporting this link [40].

Respiratory chain dysfunction

Dysfunction of the MRC—the central engine of cellular bioenergetics—represents one of the earliest and most critical insults during sepsis [46, 47]. This dysfunction precipitates a bioenergetic crisis through two major pathological consequences. First, diminished activity of the electron transport chain (ETC) complexes directly compromises OXPHOS, resulting in a precipitous decline in ATP generation. For example, studies in septic mouse models have consistently demonstrated a pronounced reduction in the activity of MRC complex IV (cytochrome C oxidase), which is accompanied by marked ultrastructural damage and culminates in a profound bioenergetic collapse within hepatocytes [48]. Second, a dysregulated MRC becomes a major source of ROS and other oxidative byproducts. This is exacerbated by the depletion of coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10)—a vital electron carrier—which disrupts redox homeostasis and predisposes the liver to hypoxic and oxidative injury. Preclinical investigations have shown that exogenous CoQ10 supplementation can normalize elevated hepatic malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and replenish intracellular glutathione (GSH), thereby conferring substantial hepatoprotection in septic models [49].

Reactive oxygen species overproduction

OS, arising from an imbalance between the production of ROS and the capacity of antioxidant defenses, is a fundamental pathogenic mechanism in liver injury. During sepsis, hepatic redox homeostasis is severely impaired, leading to biochemical disturbances that aggravate hepatic injury. These highly reactive species directly damage mitochondrial membranes, proteins, and DNA, thereby disrupting the ETC and perpetuating a cycle of mitochondrial dysfunction [50]. This is well illustrated in septic animal models, which show decreased serum superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity alongside elevated levels of the lipid peroxidation product MDA [38], reflecting extensive mitochondrial membrane damage.

Beyond their direct cytotoxicity, ROS serve as a pivotal molecular bridge linking mitochondrial OS to inflammatory signaling. A central pathway in this process is the activation of the NOD-, LRR-, and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome [51, 52]. Within the septic microenvironment, pathogen- or damage-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs or DAMPs) provide a “first signal” for inflammasome priming, while excessive mitochondrial ROS (mROS) can act as a crucial “second signal” for its full activation. This sequential activation amplifies the NLRP3 inflammasome response, thereby exacerbating SILI and promoting downstream pyroptosis [53, 54].

Together, these processes establish a self-perpetuating cycle where mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammation mutually reinforce one another. Given this dual role of ROS, modulating their generation has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. For instance, in vitro administration of growth arrest-specific gene 6 (Gas6) with antibiotics was reported to reduce ROS generation while enhancing mitochondrial bioenergetics and hepatocellular viability [55].

Dysregulated mitochondrial dynamics

Mitochondrial dynamics—the tightly regulated processes of fusion and fission—are essential for cellular homeostasis and adaptation to stress, yet they become profoundly dysregulated during sepsis. Mitochondrial fusion allows for the exchange of contents, preserving the mitochondrial network, whereas fission facilitates adaptation to energy demands and supports the selective removal of damaged mitochondria via mitophagy. These processes are orchestrated by key regulatory proteins: dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) governs fission, while mitofusin 1/2 (Mfn1/2) and optic atrophy 1 (Opa1) are indispensable for fusion. Under cellular stress, the interaction of DRP1 with fission 1 protein (Fis1) can initiate excessive mitochondrial fragmentation, leading to ROS overproduction and metabolic collapse [56].

Although direct evidence in SILI remains sparse, extensive research on other organ systems affected by sepsis suggests a conserved pathological mechanism, underscoring the need for liver-specific investigations. A seminal study demonstrated that the DRP1-Fis1 interaction mediates mitochondrial failure and blood-brain barrier (BBB) disrupting during sepsis, identifying DRP1 as a potential therapeutic target for sepsis-induced encephalopathy [57]. Similarly, in a septic mice, disruption of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) disruption exacerbated cardiomyocyte mitochondrial dysfunction, which was associated with upregulated DRP1 expression, suggesting that preserving balanced therapeutic approach for septic myocardial injury [58]. Furthermore, dysregulated mitochondrial dynamics are now recognized as core drivers of sepsis-induced acute kidney injury (SAKI), where DRP1 activation promotes mitochondrial disintegration [59–61].

Emerging liver-specific evidence has begun to elucidate the contribution of this pathway to SILI. A pivotal study provided compelling evidence that the pro-inflammatory hyperactivation of Kupffer cells (KCs) is an early and essential event in SILI pathogenesis. Under lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation, the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling pathway becomes activated within KCs. Importantly, DRP1 knockdown in KCs was shown to suppress STING activation and subsequent cytokine release, markedly alleviating liver injury [62]. This DRP1-mtDNA-STING axis appears to form a crucial molecular bridge linking mitochondrial dynamics to innate immune activation in the liver, offering novel mechanistic insights for SILI.

Impaired mitochondrial biogenesis

Given the extensive mitochondrial injury that accompanies sepsis, the cell’s capacity to replenish its mitochondrial pool through biogenesis is a decisive determinant of recovery. This intricate process, essential for renewing mitochondrial populations, is orchestrated by a transcriptional network involving master regulators such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1 alpha (PGC-1α), nuclear respiratory factor 1 (NRF1), and mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM). Aberrant mitochondrial biogenesis can compromise mitochondrial function and contribute to a wide spectrum of diseases [63, 64].

In sepsis, this regenerative program is modulated by both stimulatory and inhibitory signals, which often culminate in a net functional impairment. Consequently, interventions aimed at enhancing this process have been explored. For example, 2,3,5,4’-tetrahydroxystilbene-2-O-β-d-glucoside (TSG) was reported to promote mitochondrial biogenesis in septic mice by activating heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and upregulating PGC-1α, NRF1, and TFAM [65]. Similarly, calcitriol treatment was shown to restore GSH redox balance and enhance the expression of genes essential for mitochondrial biogenesis, thereby ameliorating SILI [66]. Conversely, this process can be suppressed by pathological mediators; for instance, miR-218-5p has been suggested to impair mitochondrial biogenesis by directly targeting PGC-1α, thereby exacerbating organ dysfunction [67].

Mitophagy defects

Mitophagy, a selective form of autophagy, serves as a critical intracellular quality-control mechanism for eliminating damaged or superfluous mitochondria [68]. By sequestering dysfunctional mitochondria within autophagosomes for lysosomal degradation, this process maintains mitochondrial quality and safeguards cellular function [69–71].

The mechanisms underlying this impairment are likely multifactorial. First, upstream recognition of damaged mitochondria may be compromised, as sepsis has been associated with the downregulation of key mitophagy receptors like FUN14 domain-containing protein 1 (FUNDC1), B-cell lymphoma 2-like 13 (BCL2L13), and prohibitin2 (PHB2) [72, 73]. Second, the downstream degradation machinery may be impaired. Intriguingly, studies have reported elevated expression of general autophagy markers in hepatic tissue and skeletal muscle from critically ill septic patients. This apparent paradox is thought to stem from impaired “autophagic flux”, indicating a bottleneck in the lysosomal degradation phase of autophagy [74–76]. Thus, even if specific mitophagy-related proteins like BCL2 interacting protein 3 (BNIP3) or Parkin are not consistently increased, a global reduction in autophagic flux could lead to the accumulation of dysfunctional organelles.

Given its central role, enhancing mitophagic activity has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. In a murine model of SILI, HO-1 was shown to regulate both Parkin- and BNIP3-mediated mitophagy conferring hepatic protection [77]. In another study, inhalation of 2% H₂ gas was reported to be hepatoprotective, potentially through modulation of FUNDC1-dependent mitophagy [78]. Collectively, these findings underscore that precise modulation of mitophagy may be pivotal for mitigating SILI.

In SILI, the mitochondrial pathologies detailed above appear to converge, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of cellular injury. This complex interplay encompasses morphological aberrations such as mitochondrial swelling and cristae structural disintegration; the collapse of membrane potential, triggered by MPTP opening and ferroptosis; ATP depletion and excessive ROS generation stemming from impaired OXPHOS; an imbalances in mitochondrial dynamics, biogenesis, and mitophagy. These interconnected dysregulations not only precipitate a severe hepatocellular bioenergetic crisis and widespread oxidative damage but also promote the release of DAMPs, which in turn amplifies the systemic inflammatory cascade (Fig. 1). Although this pathogenic framework is constructed largely from compelling preclinical evidence, its validation in the complex clinical setting of human sepsis represents a critical frontier for translational research. A thorough understanding of this pathological cycle provides a conceptual framework for the pathogenesis of SILI and the foundational rationale for the mitochondrial-targeted therapeutic strategies discussed subsequently.

Mitochondrial-targeted preclinical therapeutic strategies in sepsis-induced liver injury

The intricate pathophysiological network of mitochondria in SILI has prompted the exploration of diverse therapeutic strategies. It is crucial to acknowledge, however, that the vast majority of these approaches are confined to the preclinical stage, with evidence derived principally from animal or cellular models. Consequently, their definitive clinical translational value has yet to be established. Notably, none of the compounds discussed herein have received formal approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of SILI. Therefore, these experimental therapies warrant examination with a prudent yet forward-looking perspective. Targeting specific pathways with synthetic modulators

Synthetic small-molecule modulators offer the distinct advantage of well-defined targets and optimizable chemical structures, representing a conventional paradigm in precision drug development. Within the context of SILI, research on these compounds has predominantly focused on intervening in key nodes of mitochondrial quality control, energy metabolism, and inflammatory signaling. Among these areas, the regulation of mitochondrial dynamics and quality control has emerged as a particularly active area of investigation. For instance, in LPS-treated KCs, mtDNA released through DRP1-dependent mitochondrial fission has been shown to activate STING signaling. The DRP1 inhibitor Mdivi-1 was found to partially suppress this activation, thereby alleviating liver injury and attenuating the systemic inflammatory response, which may provide a new therapeutic target for SILI management [62, 79].

In addition to modulating mitochondrial morphology, directly counteracting OS remains a core strategy. The mitochondria-targeted antioxidant MitoQ, for example, can effectively reduce mROS levels [80]. It has also been reported that pan-apoptotic stimuli can induce mitochondrial reverse electron transfer (RET) and subsequent ROS production; this process can be inhibited by 1-Methoxy-5-methylphenazinium methyl sulfate (1-methoxy PMS) and dimethyl fumarate (DMF), which suppress RET-mediated mtDNA oxidation to ameliorate SILI [81].

Furthermore, novel targets are emerging within the regulatory networks of energy metabolism and inflammatory pathways intimately linked to mitochondrial function. S100A9, for example, appears to play a key role in promoting SILI, partly by activating AKT and inhibiting AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)-mediated mitochondrial bioenergetics. Administration of the S100A9 inhibitor paquinimod (Paq) to mice prevented SILI, suggesting S100A9 as a potential therapeutic target [18]. Similarly, the mitochondrial uncoupler BAM15 was reported to reduce the inflammatory response in M1-polarized macrophages, enhance M2-phenotype gene expression, and restore mitochondrial activity. In vivo, injection of BAM15 particles alleviated liver injury in LPS-induced septic mice, likely by reducing hepatic inflammation [82].

In summary, while the principal value of the synthetic modulator strategy lies in its high specificity, this very characteristic introduces potential challenges. Off-target effects and the long-term safety of inhibiting crucial physiological processes, such as mitochondrial fission, must be carefully evaluated in future clinical translation.

Therapeutic repurposing and bioenergetic support

In contrast to de novo drug development, “drug repurposing” or the utilization of endogenous biomolecules and metabolic precursors offers an expedited pathway for SILI intervention. A key advantage of this approach is the existing safety and pharmacokinetic data for many of these agents. Indeed, modulating endogenous hormones, growth factors, and inflammatory factors has been reported to exert mitochondrial protective effects. For instance, estrogen was shown to mitigate SILI in septic rats by reducing serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels, activating pyroptosis signaling pathways, decreasing mitochondrial superoxide production, and downregulating the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins [54].

Given that cholestasis is a significant contributor to SILI, intestine-derived fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19), a key regulator of bile acid homeostasis, has been investigated. Studies have indicated that FGF19 directly reverses LPS-induced intracellular ROS production and the decline in MMP. It may also alleviate SILI by activating the AMPK signaling pathway, thereby improving bile acid homeostasis and directly inhibiting ROS production [83–85]. In a different context, pentraxin-3 (PTX3), a secreted inflammatory mediator, has been implicated in liver homeostasis. PTX3 deficiency was found to mitigate LPS-induced SILI, paradoxically by increasing mROS and Fe2+ to modulate hepatocyte ferroptosis, and by recruiting more macrophages via the chemokine C-C motif ligand 20/chemokine C-C motif receptor 6 (CCL20/CCR6) axis to enhance inflammatory responses and clearance of noxious stimuli [86].

Other strategies focus on supplementing key metabolic substrates or precursors to provide bioenergetic support, an approach often considered closer to clinical application due to a favorable safety profile. Treatment with nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), an nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) precursor, enhanced mitochondrial function and significantly reduced pathological liver injury and inflammation. This finding suggests that interleukin (IL)-27 might exacerbate acute liver injury during sepsis by impairing mitochondrial function and macrophage phagocytic capacity [19]. Similarly, glutamine has been shown to exert a hepatoprotective effect in burn sepsis by regulating the assembly of the heat shock protein 60-heat shock protein 10 (HSP60-HSP10) complex, thereby restoring mitochondrial complex activity and maintaining cellular energy metabolism [87].

On the biologics frontier, extracellular vesicles (e.g., exosomes) are emerging as a novel class of “cell-free” therapeutics with unique potential. Liver-derived exosomes (L-Exo), for example, were demonstrated to significantly alleviate CLP-induced SILI by enhancing mitophagy via autophagy-related protein 2 homolog B (ATG2B) and inhibiting the release of mtDNA into the cytoplasm, which in turn suppresses macrophage STING signal activation [88].

Finally, leveraging insights into the common mechanisms of MODS, some endogenous pathways that regulate the systemic stress response have entered the investigative landscape. Deletion of the DNA-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit (DNA-PKcs) was found to protect multiple organs—including the heart, kidney, and liver—by improving mitochondrial metabolism and function, suggesting that strategies targeting DNA-PKcs could be valuable therapeutic options [89]. Moreover, mice deficient in BTB and CNC homology 1 (BACH1) exhibited better preservation of liver mitochondrial function after sepsis induction. This was associated with increased hepatic HO-1 expression, reduced liver injury and OS, and improved mitochondrial bioenergetics, ultimately leading to better organ function and survival [90].

Although the appeal of these strategies lies in their potential for rapid clinical translation, significant challenges persist. Endogenous molecules like hormones often have pleiotropic effects, leading to systemic side effects, while emerging therapies such as exosomes face substantial engineering hurdles, including production standardization, targeted delivery, and cost control.

Multi-target regulation by natural products

Traditional herbal medicines represent a rich source for the discovery of novel bioactive molecules. Unlike synthetic drugs that pursue high specificity for a single target, natural products are often characterized by synergistic, multi-target effects across multiple pathways. This feature may be particularly advantageous for intervening in complex pathological states such as SILI. Indeed, many active components derived from traditional Chinese herbs appear to function by modulating multiple mitochondria-related pathways, including mitophagy, OS, and inflammation. For example, Ginsenoside R3 (Rg3) has been reported to upregulate the long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) taurine upregulated gene 1 (TUG1), which in turn stimulates the sirtuin 1 (SIRT1)/AMPK pathway to enhance mitophagy, thereby improving mitochondrial dysfunction and SILI [91].

Several natural products have demonstrated direct effects on mitochondrial redox balance. Paeonol, for instance, can reduce mROS levels [92]. Similarly, Paeoniflorin (PF) and the mROS scavenger mito-TEMPO were shown to clear mROS and restore MMP via the SIRT1/forkhead box protein O1 (FOXO1)/SOD2 signaling pathway, consequently inhibiting mitochondrial damage and NLRP3 inflammasome activation [43]. Monotropein (MON) appears to alleviate SILI by reducing cytochrome P450 2E1 (CYP2E1) expression through the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2)/Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap-1) pathway, which mitigates OS and ROS accumulation while also limiting the mitochondrial apoptotic cascade [93].

Interestingly, antioxidant stress pathways such as NRF2 seem to be a common convergence point for the action of various natural products and compound formulas. For instance, Wen-Qing-Yin (WQY) was shown to protect against erastin-induced hepatocyte ferroptosis by activating Nrf2, thereby mitigating SILI [45].

While the therapeutic advantage of natural products lies in their potential for holistic regulation, their clinical translation is hampered by distinct challenges. These include the definitive identification of active ingredients, standardization of pharmacological effects, and the pervasive issue of low bioavailability. Future research must therefore more closely integrate traditional medicinal experience with modern pharmacological validation.

Conclusion and future directions

SILI represents a critical determinant of mortality in the critically ill, for which pathogenesis is intricate, with mitochondrial dysfunction occupying a central and multifaceted role. An expanding body of evidence demonstrates that during sepsis, hepatocyte mitochondria perpetuate liver damage through a confluence of mechanisms. These include bioenergetic failure leading to ATP depletion, OS stemming from excessive ROS generation, imbalanced mitochondrial dynamics favoring fission, and the activation of mitochondrial apoptosis pathways. Furthermore, by releasing DAMPs, mitochondria engage in detrimental mito-immune crosstalk, which activates intrahepatic innate immunity and thereby exacerbates the inflammatory cascade.

Given this pivotal role, targeting mitochondria has emerged as a compelling therapeutic direction for SILI. However, translating this concept into effective clinical practice necessitates overcoming substantial scientific challenges. An immediate priority is to advance strategies with high potential for near-term clinical translation. This encompasses the development of precision interventions, such as employing mitochondria-specific antioxidants (e.g., Mito-TEMPO, SkQ1) to neutralize excess ROS [94], applying mitochondrial dynamics modulators (e.g., the DRP1 inhibitor Mdivi-1, Mfn2 agonists) to restore network homeostasis, or using mitophagy enhancers (e.g., SS-31, urolithin A) to facilitate the clearance of damaged organelles [95, 96]. Parallel to these therapeutic efforts, the screening and validation of clinical biomarkers—such as circulating mtDNA and mitochondrial proteins—are crucial for enabling early diagnosis, prognostic stratification, and therapeutic monitoring of SILI. It is only through the synergy of such precision therapeutics and validated biomarkers that we can design and execute robust clinical trials aimed at mitochondrial protection, ultimately paving the way for personalized treatment paradigms that stratify patients based on their mitochondrial functional status.

Concurrently, deep and long-term basic research is imperative for pioneering future technological breakthroughs. This requires a more granular elucidation of the molecular mechanisms and spatiotemporal dynamics of mitochondrial dysfunction across different sepsis etiologies and disease stages. A particular focus should be placed on dissecting the heterogeneity of mitochondrial injury among different intrahepatic cell populations (e.g., hepatocytes, KCs, and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells) and delineating their complex interplay. In the pursuit of novel therapeutic targets, beyond small-molecule drug development, longer-term modalities such as gene therapy (e.g., mitochondria-targeted gene delivery) or epigenetic modulation (e.g., targeting mtDNA methylation or histone modifications) offer new avenues for reversing sepsis-induced mitochondrial dysfunction.

Furthermore, interdisciplinary research that integrates systems biology, metabolomics, and single-cell sequencing will be instrumental in constructing a comprehensive network map of mitochondrial dysregulation in SILI. Such approaches will also be vital for revealing its intricate connections with the gut-liver axis [97, 98] and the neuroendocrine system [99, 100]. These cutting-edge explorations should also extend to the long-term sequelae in sepsis survivors, such as liver fibrosis and metabolic syndrome, to investigate the persistent impact of mitochondrial damage and delineate potential repair mechanisms [101–103].

In conclusion, mitochondria are far more than a “core target” in SILI; they represent a critical nexus linking energy metabolism, OS, cell death, and immune-inflammatory responses. Future research demands a holistic, full-spectrum investigation—from fundamental molecular mechanisms and innovative therapeutic strategies to their ultimate clinical translation. By seamlessly integrating basic and clinical science, harnessing emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI)-driven drug screening and organoid modeling [104], and embracing transdisciplinary strategies, we can accelerate the development of precision intervention paradigms. This will ultimately offering novel and effective strategies for the diagnosis and treatment of SILI.
