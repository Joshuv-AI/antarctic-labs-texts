Habitat fidelity of adult grey seals over multiple years in a changing marine ecosystem
Authors: Benia V. R. Nowak, W. Don Bowen, Damian C. Lidgard, Cornelia E. den Heyer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndividual foraging site fidelity is a common mechanism for dealing with environmental variability across marine predator species. However, there has been little research on long-term individual repeatability to date, especially over time periods where significant environmental and ecological change has occurred. The grey seal is a long-lived pinniped species that forages on the Scotian Shelf and in the Gulf of St. Lawrence, two ecosystems which are characterized by spatio-temporal variability in oceanographic conditions and have also undergone dramatic changes to ecological structuring and functioning in recent decades.MethodsBetween 1995 and 2018, 22 adult grey seals (17 females, 5 males) were repeatedly instrumented with tracking devices in two or three different years. We fitted continuous-time correlated random walk state-space models to estimate grey seal locations and move persistence along tracks. We then calculated the kernel utilization distribution overlap for home ranges (99%) and core areas (50%) within and between individuals as well as for areas (95%) associated with apparent foraging behaviour (move persistence < 0.5).ResultsHere, we show that grey seals demonstrated long-term moderate to high levels of fidelity in home ranges between years. By contrast, there was little between-year overlap in core areas used. Nevertheless, areas associated with low move persistence, inferred as area-restricted search and hence where foraging maybe more likely, were highly consistent between years. Habitat overlap between individuals was low across years.ConclusionsHome range fidelity appears to persist over the life of grey seals, despite significant environmental and ecological change occurring within their habitat during our study period. These findings contribute to understanding the potential consequences of projected environmental change for this and other central place foraging species.

Background

Foraging decisions made by animals are associated with some level of risk, such as increased predation pressure, competition, or lower energy intake, all of which may have consequences for fitness [1]. As the decisions that enhance individual fitness increasingly characterize the average foraging behaviour within a population, shared foraging strategies may emerge [2]. Strategies that are favoured by natural selection depend on the spatial (i.e., habitat evenness vs. patchiness) and temporal (i.e., stable/predictable vs. stochastic/unpredictable) conditions of the habitat within which they evolve [3]. In a patchy environment, individuals must balance the energetic costs of remaining in areas with less suitable prey conditions against those of searching for new habitat with higher quality prey. Alternative food sources may be lower in availability or quality, and acquiring new information about their foraging environment also comes at an energetic cost without a guarantee of reward [4, 5]. This is particularly relevant in the marine environment, where the distribution of resources is three-dimensionally patchy and driven by complex oceanographic processes.

Individual foraging site fidelity (IFSF, when individuals consistently use only a small part of their population’s home range) is a widely documented foraging strategy used by marine predators [6, 7]. IFSF should be expected to occur in predictable environments as individuals return to previously profitable foraging sites (increasing foraging efficiency and potentially reproductive success), but this behaviour declines as resources are depleted (“win-stay, lose-switch”) [3, 8–11]. In unpredictable environments, IFSF may remain advantageous, but only over longer time scales (multiple years) when the ecological benefits (such as reduced cognitive costs of search or familiarity with resources) and relative profitability outweigh short-term variability, thereby enhancing lifetime reproductive success (“always stay”) [3, 12–15]. This foraging strategy is perhaps most beneficial for species that are long-lived. While long-lived species may experience more variation in environmental conditions over the course of their reproductive life, these species may be less sensitive to this variability as they have multiple opportunities to reproduce.

IFSF is especially common in colonial breeders and central place foragers where individuals depart from and eventually return to the same location, and must compete for resources within a limited area [16–18]. As such, individual repeatability paired with population segregation may additionally serve to alleviate competition among conspecifics [19] and can be reinforced by density dependence [20]. The persistence of IFSF over time can therefore represent a form of individual specialization, as either a cause or consequence of stable behavioural types within a population, with important ecological and demographic implications [21–26].

Methods of inferring foraging habitat fidelity have included stable isotope analysis (identification of isotopic niche and foraging habitat), the use of tracking devices (fine-scale habitat use), and most comprehensively, a combination of both [21, 25, 27–29]. While these approaches have demonstrated IFSF is widespread in marine predators, the limited availability of long-term datasets constrains our understanding of its proximate causes and potential responses of populations to ongoing environmental change [17, 30]. This limitation is largely owed to the difficulty of capturing the same individuals in subsequent years for repeated sampling or instrumentation, which is often impossible due to the natural histories of many species. As a result, a disproportionate number of long-term tracking studies have been conducted on colonial seabirds where individuals can be identified and instrumented together with identification tags in consecutive years [31]. In pinnipeds, individuals can often only be instrumented for part of the year as their fur, and thus the tag, are moulted annually. Consequently, most studies have examined within-year foraging site fidelity over consecutive foraging trips [32], often in income breeding pinniped species where females go on multiple foraging trips during an extended lactation period [10]. Alternative methods of attaching satellite tags can overcome this issue, such as flipper tags [9, 33] and more recently instruments clamped to the tusks of walruses [34], though they are less widely used (aside from ice seals) and again limited to consecutive years and by the lifespan of the tag. In few cases, uniquely identifiable individuals can be instrumented across multiple years, allowing for longer comparisons [35, 36].

Grey seals (Halichoerus grypus) are long-lived pinnipeds (30–40 y) that are capital breeding central place foragers. Sexual maturity occurs between four and seven years of age in females and at approximately six years and older in males. The proportion of females that give birth each year varies significantly with age, where younger inexperienced and older senescing individuals are more likely to skip breeding, more commonly under poorer foraging conditions [37, 38]. This species is a prey generalist that exhibits inter-annual and seasonal variability in diet at the population-level [39, 40]. However, stable isotope analyses indicate individual specialisation in diet, with greater consistency in habitat compared to trophic components [41, 42]. Previous telemetry research has also demonstrated fine-scale habitat use and foraging patterns that develop rapidly early in life and are shaped by oceanographic conditions [43, 44]. Together with evidence of fidelity to foraging areas between trips [32], these findings suggest at least some level of individual specialisation in habitat use also occurs, but that inter-annual variability in environmental conditions likely influences foraging behaviour in grey seals. Whether IFSF persists over longer temporal scales remains to be seen, but will have important consequences for our understanding of how this, and other, central placed foragers respond to changing environmental conditions [23, 30, 45].

The largest population of grey seals in the Northwest Atlantic breeds on Sable Island and forages primarily on the Scotian Shelf and in the Gulf of St. Lawrence in Eastern Canada. The Scotian Shelf has spatio-temporally variable oceanographic conditions [46], resulting in some of the highest inter-annual variability in the North Atlantic as well as frequent stochastic and anomalous events [47–49]. In addition to this variability, the Scotian Shelf has undergone dramatic ecological changes as a result of both top-down (e.g., overfishing; [50]) and bottom-up forcing (e.g., nutrient inputs and environmental conditions; [51–53]). These changes have been influential on primary productivity patterns and have resulted in considerable trophic restructuring including mortality and distributional shifts in groundfish populations [54]. Similar environmental changes have been observed in the Gulf of St. Lawrence (e.g., increased sea surface temperatures and reduced oxygen concentrations; [55, 56]) as well as spatial and temporal changes in demersal fish assemblages and species diversity associated with the collapse of groundfish stocks [57, 58]. This region is projected to experience continued warming along with substantial declines in primary production [59, 60] and concomitant declines in fish populations in coming years [61]. Thus, the individual plasticity and population level variability in foraging strategies of grey seals, which prey upon several of these groundfish species, is of considerable interest [62].

In this study, we used location data collected during repeated deployments of satellite-linked data transmitters (SLDTs) and archival tracking devices on adult grey seals to test for fidelity in home ranges, core areas, and foraging patches used over multiple years. Given that grey seals are a long-lived species, we expected individuals to revisit foraging patches to reduce search time and increase foraging success, and thus the probability of storing sufficient energy to support reproduction.

Methods

Data collection

Between 1995 and 2018, 22 adult grey seals (17 females, five males) were opportunistically re-instrumented two (18 individuals) or three (four individuals) times with SLDTs and archival tracking devices, totalling 48 instrument deployments (Table 1). This work was carried out during long-term research projects on Sable Island (43.03ºN, 59.91ºW), a crescent-shaped sandbar off the eastern coast of Nova Scotia, Canada. Groups of grey seals at this breeding colony were branded at weaning between 1969 and 2002, producing a pool of uniquely identifiable known-age adults from which these individuals were selected. Individuals were captured onshore and immobilized using an intramuscular injection of Telazol (female dose 0.90 mg kg− 1, male dose 0.45 mg kg− 1) [63]. Instruments were deployed in June, September, October (i.e., post-moult), and January (i.e., post-breeding season) (see Table 1 for seasonal data availability). Between 1995 and 2004 (26 deployments), SLDTs were programmed to transmit Argos location data (Wildlife Computers SDR, Telonics ST-18, and Sea Mammal Research Unit SRDL 7000), between 2009 and 2015 Fastloc GPS location data (Wildlife Computers Mk10-AF) were recorded and downloaded upon retrieval of archival tracking devices (18 deployments), and in 2018 both Argos and Fastloc locations (Wildlife Computers Mk10-AF) were transmitted via Service Argos (four deployments). Some instruments deployed between 1995 and 2004 were duty-cycled to transmit one day on and two days off [64, 65]. Instruments programmed to collect Fastloc data attempted locations after every 15 min when the animal was at the surface. Fastloc locations were suspended during haul out periods once a location had been recorded and the tag detected dry conditions for 45 s out of every one min for 20 min. Location attempts resumed when the seal returned to sea and the tag detected wet conditions for 45 s out of one min. Fastloc locations derived from < 5 satellites or with residual error values >30 were not included in the analysis [44, 66].

State-space modelling

Analyses were conducted using the statistical software R (Version 4.3.3; [67]). We fitted state-space models using the R package aniMotum (Version 1.2-04) [68, 69] to obtain the best estimate of true locations along tracks and approximately normalise the number of locations per day between instrument types. To improve state-space model convergence and model fits, location data (143,938 locations, 23,119 Argos and 120,819 Fastloc) were filtered prior to model fitting. We applied a coarse distance filter to remove location spikes using the great circle distance between consecutive WGS84 coordinates. Locations were removed if both the distance from the previous location and the distance to the subsequent location were greater than 25 km. The first location of each track was removed if the distance to the next location was greater than 25 km, and the last location in each track was removed if the distance from the previous location was greater than 25 km (141,683 locations retained). Tracks (i.e., locations separated by individual and by year) were split into track segments if time gaps between consecutive locations were greater than 72 h (206 track segments), in part to avoid predicting locations expected to have high standard error estimates. This threshold also allowed for locations to be estimated over gaps resulting from duty-cycling without resulting in looping artefacts that occurred when longer gaps were included. Track segments were processed with aniMotum (coordinates projected to Mercator) and filtered to ensure a minimum time difference of 10 s between consecutive locations and with a speed threshold of 2 m s− 1. Location spikes with angles smaller than 15 and 25 degrees were removed if they were located farther than 2500 m and 5000 m, respectively. Track segments with fewer than 24 locations after filtering were removed from the dataset (140,643 locations from 87 track segments over 47 deployments retained).

We fitted continuous-time correlated random walk state-space models using aniMotum, which permits the use of both Argos and Fastloc data. We used default error multiplication factors for longitude and latitude as no prior information was available for our study area. Fastloc locations are assumed to have error variances ten times smaller than Argos location class 3 variances for both longitude and latitude. Locations were predicted at a 4 h time step to approximately normalise the number of locations per day between instrument types, as earlier deployments using Argos transmissions had substantial data gaps, high location error (i.e., poor location quality), and many erroneous locations. A time step of 4 h was selected based on the frequency of both Fastloc (mean and standard deviation of 31.9 ± 100.2 min) and Argos (182.1 ± 451.7 min) locations. This represented the lowest time step at which nearly all state-space models and move persistence models (see Sect. 2.3) converged and diagnostics indicated models fitted reasonably well. A total of 32,148 locations were predicted.

Model validation was performed by visual inspection of both one-dimensional (time-series) and two-dimensional (geographic coordinate) plots of predicted standard error estimates to detect lack of fit. We conducted prediction residual diagnostics using One-Step-Ahead Residuals (OSAR) including time-series, Q-Q plots, and autocorrelation function (ACF) plots. We also assessed track uncertainty from 100 replicate tracks simulated from the joint precision matrix of each state-space model fit. The model for one track segment converged but fitted poorly and was removed from the data set (31,843 locations from 86 track segments over 47 deployments retained). Locations were then re-routed around land at a map scale of 1:110 m and with a buffer of 50 km.

Movement patterns

To compare the movement patterns of grey seals during foraging trips, we fitted a move persistence model to state-space modelled track segments also using aniMotum. This model estimates time-varying move persistence (γt) using autocorrelation in both speed and direction as a behavioral index that varies continuously between 0 (low move persistence, i.e., apparent foraging) and 1 (high move persistence, i.e., apparent directed travelling). Models for three track segments did not converge which were removed from the dataset (30,905 locations from 83 track segments over 47 deployments retained). Track segments were then recombined into tracks (i.e., locations separated by individual and by year) for subsequent comparison. To identify individual trips to sea, locations where the animal was within a 5 km radius of Sable Island were considered to be haul outs [43, 70]. We then sequentially assigned trip number to at-sea locations between haul outs. Locations where the animal was hauled out (that were not previously removed by track segmentation) were then removed to ensure comparison of at-sea home ranges (23,125 locations over 46 deployments from 22 individuals). All tracks had at least 7 days of tracking data. Individuals with fewer than five locations or only one deployment were removed from the dataset to enable home range comparison (22,799 locations over 45 deployments from 21 individuals). Move persistence estimates were normalised separately for each track to rescale estimates to the interval [0,1].

Habitat fidelity

To quantify individual home range and foraging site fidelity, we estimated utilization distributions (UDs) using the R package adehabitatHR (Version 0.4.21; [71, 72]). Locations were reprojected to UTM zone 20 N (EPSG: 2961) prior to calculation of UDs. The UD is the bivariate function giving the probability density that an animal is found at a point according to its geographic coordinates. We used the simple fixed-kernel method, which yields a nonparametric estimate of the UD, as our primary objective was to estimate the spatial distribution of habitat use. This approach is widely used and facilitates comparison across studies [65]. Although this method does not account for serial autocorrelation commonly present in tracking data (unlike Brownian bridge or autocorrelated kernel density estimators), we were unable to estimate continuous movement paths and some tracks contained large temporal gaps. We aimed to avoid introducing type I errors by not estimating home ranges across gaps when locations could not be reliably predicted by state-space models. Comparisons between tracks were likely already subject to type II errors due to irregular track lengths and variable deployment timings.

We estimated UDs of home ranges (99% kernel) and core areas (50% kernel) for individual deployments and calculated overlap indices between them using Bhattacharyya’s affinity (BA) [73] which ranges from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (complete overlap). We used the 99% kernel to represent home range so locations from all track segments (including those that were relatively short) were included. In order to ensure comparable results among deployments, we used the same fixed smoothing parameter for all UD calculations. A bandwidth of 12 km was selected based on the uncertainty associated with location estimates (standard deviations derived from standard errors produced by state-space model fits) and comparison of home ranges just before space use began to fracture [74–76]. We used a grid size of 2000 m and the pixels of the grid outside the kernel at a probability level equal to the percentage (99% or 50%) were set to zero. To compare overlap between home ranges and core areas, BA indices were rescaled to the [0,1] interval by dividing them by the highest possible value (0.99 and 0.50, respectively) [77, 78]. To determine whether overlap within individuals across years was significantly different from overlap between individuals we used two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (Mann-Whitney U test) as our data did not conform to normality.

To assess the influence on data gaps on our results, we also calculated UDs using location data from only complete trips (i.e., one track segment per trip and the last trip ends within 25 km of Sable Island) and assessed home range (99% kernel) overlap using the same methods above. Individuals with fewer than five locations or only one deployment were not included to enable home range comparison (16,800 locations over 40 deployments from 19 individuals). We then compared the overlap within individuals using all location data to overlap using only locations from complete trips with a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann-Whitney U test).

We quantified the foraging area fidelity of individual grey seals by estimating UDs for each deployment including only locations considered to be apparent foraging, conservatively defined as locations with move persistence < 0.5. To minimize the influence of locations where grey seals opportunistically foraged along tracks, we calculated overlap between the 95% kernels surrounding apparent foraging locations using the same methods as above. We then used a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann-Whitney U test) to determine whether overlap in foraging areas within individuals across years was significantly different from overlap in those used between individuals.

Results

In our study, opportunistic re-instrumentation of 22 adult grey seals (17 females, five males) in two (18 individuals) or three (four individuals) years resulted in 48 deployments over a period of 24 years between 1995 and 2018 (Table 1). The deployment date, seasonal data availability, number of locations recorded, and number of days tracked varied considerably across deployments. Individuals ranged between eight and 32 years of age at the time of deployment with a median age of 23 years (mean and standard deviation of 21.1 ± 7.23). Twenty-one individuals (16 females, five males) across 45 deployments were included in a total of 27 habitat overlap comparisons (Table 2). Re-instrumentation of grey seals included in these comparisons occurred between three and 18 years apart with a median difference of eight years (mean and standard deviation of 9.0 ± 4.55 years) (Table 2).

The median overlap in home ranges between years was 0.72 (0.64 ± 0.32) and ranged between 0.20 and 0.96 between deployment years (Table 2). Of the 27 comparisons, between-year overlap was less than 50% in nine cases (five of which were males), between 50 and 75% in seven cases (one of which was a male), and greater than 75% in eleven cases (one of which was a male). Thus, most grey seals exhibited moderate to high fidelity to home ranges between years (see Fig. 1). All three individuals (two females, one male) with three deployments exhibited relatively high overlap in their tracks (see Figs. 1a and 2, and 3c) and estimated home ranges (Table 2). Male grey seals tended to have lower overlap on average (0.42 ± 0.22) compared to females (0.72 ± 0.19), however the sample size of males was small. There was no correlation (r = 0.05) between number of years between deployments on individual grey seals and the level of overlap in their home ranges, suggesting the spatial behaviour of individuals was consistent over time. Home ranges including only complete trips without data gaps were still relatively high with a median of 0.66 (0.64 ± 0.24) and not statistically different than home ranges including all location data (W = 306, p-value = 0.94). The level of overlap in home ranges between individuals was significantly lower than overlap within individuals (W = 21918, p-value < 0.001), with a median of 0.28 (0.32 ± 0.06) and ranging from 0.01 to 0.95.

Discussion

Our results indicate that most grey seals exhibited moderate to high levels of fidelity in the spatial distribution of their home ranges in the two or three years sampled. Further, this fidelity appears to persist over the life of individuals, given that the interval between instrument deployments was nearly 20 years in some cases. Despite considerable ecological change during the study period [54, 79], home range and foraging site fidelity remained high in most individuals. Although IFSF has been studied in several marine predator species, including grey seals [32, 47–55], it has mostly been studied either within a single year (foraging trips) [7, 9, 10, 80] or over only a small number of years [81]. While these studies have provided valuable information on the causes and consequences of IFSF, our study provides insight into long-term behavioural consistency and individual specialisation.

Several individuals exhibited differences in home ranges used between years (i.e., low home range overlap). This may indicate some level of behavioural plasticity or variation in IFSF within the population, which has been observed in other central place foragers (e.g., great cormorants (Phalacrocorax carbo) [82] and California sea lions (Zalophus californianus) [83]). Although long-term IFSF should be expected to intensify over time (with age) [84, 85], such behavioural specialisation may diminish the adaptive capacity of species to climate change [30, 86]. A limitation of our study was that individuals were selected opportunistically and therefore our sample was not balanced for factors such as sex, age, and season or sampling durations. It is possible that for some individuals, these inter-annual differences in habitat use may be an artefact of inconsistent track durations (including data gaps) and timings. This could have resulted in incomplete home range estimates in some years and potentially artificially low overlap indices (see comparison to tracks without data gaps in Table 2).

We also observed fidelity to apparent foraging areas within years, consistent with previous findings for this population [32], and in some cases, inter-annual fidelity to more than one area (Fig. 5). This can provide important context for inferences made from short-term IFSF studies. The apparent foraging areas used by individuals were often consistent over time, yet the routes to and from them sometimes differed (Fig. 4). Individuals may explore nearby habitat during travel to known foraging patches to gain new information about their habitat without unduly increasing energetic costs of searching [35]. The smoothing parameter used to estimate UDs (and required by our tracks) may have masked minor differences in the routes used by individuals during travel (illustrated in Fig. 4). While we explored a range of smoothing parameters, overlap comparisons did not vary considerably, indicating different routes to and from foraging patches were generally close in proximity (Table 2).

While the effect of sex could not be tested, the few male tracks we do have showed inconsistent levels of spatial fidelity (Figs. 3a and b compared to Fig. 3c). Like in northern elephant seals, IFSF in grey seals occurred within broader patterns of sexual segregation [65, 87]. Male grey seals tend to have more diverse diet than females with a larger fraction of low energy density prey [40, 42, 88]. Consequently, they tend to have a broader geographic distribution [32], particularly along the outermost continental shelf (Fig. 3) and thus may be exposed to areas more immediately affected by inter-annual environmental phenomena [89]. Therefore, we might also expect males to show weaker site fidelity (and higher plasticity) than females, which tend to specialise on a few high energy prey species, but this remains to be investigated.

While individual foraging site fidelity is expected to occur in predictable environments over short time scales [1, 3], this foraging strategy can also act as an “insurance policy” to enhance lifetime reproductive success for species that inhabit variable environments and are also long-lived [12, 90]. It may be advantageous for grey seals to forgo searching for higher prey availability or quality under short-term unfavourable conditions, as this strategy is higher-risk and may consequently lower their lifetime reproductive success [12]. Our results indicate that despite the observed variability in prey populations and the ecosystem changes throughout the grey seal range [54, 60, 61], most individuals exhibited relatively little variability in their use of space, suggesting that sufficient profitability of foraging areas must remain at least somewhat predictable and generally profitable.

Given the time frame of repeated deployments, it is probable that some years may have corresponded to poorer foraging conditions. Our study coincided with anomalous warming events (2012 and 2015) which had drastic biological effects on Scotian Shelf groundfish species preyed upon by grey seals [39, 48, 88], yet there was no evidence of reduced fidelity for individuals tagged in these years (Fig. 1a and c). With only two or three repeated deployments on individuals, conclusions made on the extent of fidelity and the factors influencing repeatability are necessarily tentative.

Northern sand lance (Ammodytes dubius), a main prey species of female grey seals in this population [39], has rather specific bottom substrate grain size requirements for burrowing such that its spatial distribution is relatively stable [91]. This may partly account for the foraging site fidelity of many females in the face of environmental variability, which often manifests in temperature and salinity changes, as the limitation of sand lance habitat may serve as a relatively predictable resource. Repeated use of the same small fraction of the species foraging range, even with potential changes in prey quality, must ultimately imply that these areas adequately improve foraging efficiency across years to improve lifetime reproductive success [11].

The quality of an area in terms of prey and previous reproductive success may not be all that influences an animal’s repeated use of space and therefore the degree of foraging specialization observed here [3]. Risk of predation, spatial memory [15], personality [92], and intraspecific competition [22] may also influence site fidelity. Adult grey seals have few natural predators [16, 93] and although presence of white sharks (Carcharodon carcharias) and killer whales (Orcinus orca) could influence habitat use, data do not exist to test this hypothesis. The use of repeated foraging habitat in consecutive foraging trips or years necessitates remembering prey encounters over only part the landscape [94], such that IFSF may also serve to reduce energetic costs associated with memory (information storage) and cognition (recalling information). Previous research has demonstrated that individual variation in behaviour over time and/or contexts, referred to as personality, is influential on the maternal performance of grey seals, where higher boldness results in greater maternal output and larger pups [95, 96]. This boldness observed in grey seals also increases with age [97]. In black-legged kittiwakes (Rissa tridactyla), personality drives the degree of individual specialization, where bolder birds show higher habitat fidelity than shy conspecifics [92]. A positive relationship between the level of foraging site fidelity and breeding output has also been seen in African penguins (Spheniscus demersus) [98, 99]. It is therefore conceivable that individual variation in boldness may be associated with IFSF in grey seals, but is beyond the scope of this study.

Over the course of our study, the population inhabiting the Scotian Shelf and Gulf of St. Lawrence increased from approximately 80,000 to 300,000 individuals [100]. Previous satellite tracking research has revealed foraging hotspots over offshore banks used by numerous animals [43, 44, 64, 70, 93]. Therefore, it is expected that suitable foraging habitat will be occupied by many grey seals at any given time. Spatial segregation of age and sex classes suggests intraspecific competition in this population [65]. This segregation is also documented in southern elephant seals (Mirounga leonina) inhabiting the Southern Ocean [101, 102]. Thus the repeated use of home ranges and more importantly foraging patches as a form of individual specialisation within this generalist population may serve to further reduce intraspecific competition among grey seals [23].

The development of foraging strategies occurs rapidly in Sable Island grey seal pups over the first few months of life [43], with the foraging patterns of juveniles already resembling those of adults by six months of age [93]. Our study included repeated instrument deployments on individuals over a wide range of ages from eight (early adulthood) and 32 (late in life) years. Although a larger number of repeated deployments on individuals is clearly needed, reasonable fidelity over periods ranging from three to 18 years suggests that repeatability in habitat use may persist throughout the lives of grey seals despite variable ecological conditions. The movement patterns of grey seal pups and those of adults are associated with bathymetry, which largely structures the oceanographic and prey conditions of this region [43, 44]. Stable isotope analyses suggest that while grey seals may be generalists at the population level, there is individual specialization in their prey intake [41, 42, 103]. These results would be consistent with fidelity to individual foraging patches over time, if the quality of those patches was generally sufficient to meet energetic demands. We suggest that individual foraging behaviour and specialization of diet composition in grey seals may be greatly influenced by which prey species and habitats were successfully exploited as naïve pups. If so, the foraging patterns of grey seals are likely canalised early in life and not genetically encoded [14, 17, 85]. In Northern elephant seals, this confers a selective advantage as those who develop consistency in foraging behaviour earlier in life also have longer life spans [104]. As environmental conditions continue to become more unpredictable, the selective pressures driving individual foraging site fidelity in this population may also change [30].

Conclusions

In this study, we demonstrate that female grey seals exhibit moderate to high fidelity in their home ranges over multiple years. This fidelity appears to persist over the life of individuals, despite significant environmental and ecological change. Although there was little overlap in core areas between years, areas with low move persistence estimates and inferred as apparent foraging behaviour were generally consistent between years for individuals. However, the travel routes to and from these areas showed some variability between years. Together with previous research on the ontogeny of foraging behaviour in grey seals, our results suggest that the foraging patterns of grey seals are likely canalised early in life. Our findings provide a valuable contribution to a growing body of literature on behavioural consistency and demonstrate long-term repeatability and individual specialisation in habitat use in a large marine predator that inhabits a complex marine ecosystem.
