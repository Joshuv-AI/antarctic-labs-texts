Tensile strength of Co-Cr and Zr substructures on titanium abutments with different geometries
Authors: Zeynep Uzgur, Nihal Özcan, Saadet Atsü
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of different abutment geometries, abutment materials (Cobalt–Chromium and Zirconium), and cement types on the tensile strength of cement-retained implant-supported restorations in vitro.MethodsA total of 240 BioHorizons implant analogues (5.7 mm diameter) were used and initially divided into two groups based on the material: Cobalt–Chromium (Co–Cr) and Zirconium (Zr) (n = 120). Each material group was further divided into four subgroups according to abutment geometry (n = 30): Group C (no preparation), Group P (one wall), Group A (adjacent two walls), and Group O (opposite two walls). Subsequently, each geometry subgroup was divided into three subgroups (n = 10) according to the cement type: adhesive resin cement (ARC), and self-adhesive resin cement (SARC), resin-modified glass ionomer cement (RMGIC). Tests were carried out using a universal testing machine with a 0.5 mm/min crosshead speed. Two-way Anova statistical test was performed with post-hoc Tukey test, with 0.05 significance.ResultsIn the zirconia groups, Group O showed the highest retention (ARC: 695.27 ± 201.67 N; SARC: 507.01 ± 80.00 N; RMGIC: 389.42 ± 139.60 N), with significant pairwise differences between ARC–SARC, ARC–RMGIC, and SARC–RMGIC. In the metal groups, ARC achieved the highest values, particularly in Group P (928.27 ± 167.81 N) and Group C (822.15 ± 295.65 N). RMGIC yielded the lowest retention in most geometries, differing significantly from ARC in all geometries and from SARC in Groups P and C. Abutment geometry had minimal influence on ARC and SARC in metal substructures (p > 0.05 for most comparisons) but significantly affected RMGIC retention (Group O vs. P, C ).ConclusionsRetention of implant-supported restorations is strongly influenced by abutment geometry. Combining favorable geometry with high-performance cements in clinical practice is important.

Introduction

Implant treatments have become increasingly common in contemporary dental practice. Although various substructure materials are available for fixed prosthetic restorations, metal remains the most widely used option in the prosthetic treatment of osseointegrated implants [1]. However, with rising patient awareness and greater emphasis on aesthetics and biocompatibility among addressing, there is growing demand for metal-free alternatives in implant restorations [2]. The development of high-strength ceramic systems has consequently led to the widespread adoption of all-ceramic restorations in both anterior and posterior regions. These material developments have also sparked interest in how abutment and implant design features influence the performance and longevity of restorations.

As implant dentistry evolves, questions have emerged regarding optimal implant and abutment designs to ensure long-term clinical success. One key consideration is the nature of the adhesive connection between the restoration and the implant abutment [3]. This adhesive connection plays a central role in ensuring adequate retention and resistance of cement-retained prostheses.

The success of cement-retained prostheses largely depends on achieving adequate retention and resistance [4]. Numerous factors influence the retention of both natural and implant abutments, including the preparation angle, abutment diameter, height, and the type of cement used [5–7]. In implant abutments specifically, additional variables come into play, such as total surface area, platform width, the method of sealing the abutment screw access channel [8], the geometric features of the abutment (e.g., step configuration) [9, 10], and the number of remaining axial walls [11–13]. While there are similarities in retention behavior between natural teeth and implant abutments, there are also important distinctions making cement selection critical for the longevity and stability of implant-supported prostheses [14].

Notably, some studies have suggested that preparation design does not significantly impact retention when resin cements are used as luting agents [15], whereas others report that loss of retention or screw loosening remains among the most common complications in clinical follow-ups of implant restorations [16, 17].

Factors such as the type and composition of the cement, its viscosity, film thickness, the force applied during seating, and the abutment’s angle, diameter, height, surface area, and roughness all contribute to the retention of implant-supported prostheses [5–7, 18–20].

Cement selection is thus a critical determinant in achieving reliable retention [4]. When cementing metal-supported restorations to abutments, adhesive resin cements offer significantly higher retention compared to conventional cements [21]. Moreover, resin cements not only enhance the retention of Zirconia (Zr) restorations on natural teeth but also improve both the retention and fracture resistance of zirconia substructures in implant-supported restorations [13, 21, 22]. Despite numerous studies examining individual factors such as cement type or abutment design, few have comprehensively evaluated their combined influence across different abutment materials.

The aim of this study was to investigate and compare the effects of implant abutment preparation geometry and adhesive luting cements on the retention of cobalt-chromium (Co-Cr) and zirconia (Zr) frameworks in vitro. The null hypothesis was that variations in abutment geometry and cement type would not produce significant differences in tensile strength.

Materials and methods

A total of 240 wide neck implant analogues (5.7 mm diameter) and corresponding abutments measuring 5.5 mm in height (BioHorizons; Riverchase Center, Birmingham, AL, USA) were used in this study. Abutments were torqued to 30 N·cm using a standardized screwdriver and suffered standardized axial wall modifications to evaluate their effect on retention. Four preparation groups were defined based on axial wall modifications: Specimens were divided into four groups based on abutment wall geometry: Group A: Two adjacent axial walls prepared, Group P: One axial wall prepared, Group O: Two opposing walls prepared, Group C: No preparation (control group) (Fig. 1).

Each group (n = 30) was further subdivided into three subgroups (n = 10) according to the type of resin cement used: (1) Resin-modified glass ionomer cement (RMGIC) — RelyX™ Luting Cement (3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) (2) Adhesive composite resin cement (ARC) — Pa F 2.0 (Kuraray Noritake Dental Inc., Suite, NY, USA) (3) Self-adhesive resin cement (SARC) — Panavia SA Cement (Kuraray Noritake Dental Inc., Suite, NY, USA). All cements were applied in accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions.

Sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1 with 5% alpha error and 85% power, resulting in 120 total specimens across 12 groups (4 geometries × 3 cements).

Implant analogues were embedded in polymethyl methacrylate blocks (3 × 3 cm) (Meliodent, Heraeus Kulzer, Germany) with a 0.5 mm exposed collar. All modifications were performed using a precision milling machine equipped with a diamond bur (1.59–1.6 mm diameter, 7 mm length) (Hicare, Guangzhou Hicare Medical Equipment Co., China)under continuous water irrigation to prevent thermal damage. Screw access holes were sealed with cotton and composite resin (Filtek™ Z250). During specimen preparation, abutment finish lines in the prepared regions were standardized to a 90° shoulder, orthogonal to the base, using a parallelometer. Areas rebuilt with composite were prepared to a 10° total occlusal convergence (TOC) using the same device. Abutments were scanned (Dental Wings Inc.) and Zirconia (Zr) substructures were designed and milled accordingly. Wax patterns for metal substructures were designed with CAD software, incorporating 3.5 mm diameter gaps to facilitate force application during testing. A total of 120 Co-Cr substructures (Wirobond C, BEGO GmbH, Germany) were fabricated via conventional casting, then sandblasted with 50 μm aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) (Ivoclar Vivadent, Amherst, NY, USA).

Zr specimens (Whitepeaks Dental Systems GmbH & Co., Essen, Germany) were sintered for eight hours (Tegra MP 1500, Technical Dental, Istanbul, Turkey) and prepared with standardized cavities. Prior to cementation, all specimens underwent steam cleaning and sandblasting (50 μm Al₂O₃). Cementation was followed by a 24-hour storage period before mechanical testing. Tensile tests were conducted using a universal testing machine (Lloyd LRX; Fareham, UK), applying force at 0.5 mm/sec via a 3 mm steel wire. Failure loads were recorded in Newtons.

Data were analyzed using SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assessed and differences among groups were evaluated with two-way ANOVA and post-hoc Tukey tests, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrated significant interactions between material, abutment geometry, and cement type affecting the retention of implant-supported restorations. Abutment geometry influenced retention notably in zirconia groups but had less impact on metal groups, especially when adhesive and self-adhesive resin cements were used. Based on these findings, the hypothesis that abutment preparation geometry and cement type did not significantly affect retention is rejected.

There is no consensus in the literature regarding the ideal cement type for optimal retention. Some clinicians prefer hydrophilic cements such as zinc phosphate and glass ionomer [13], whereas others advocate for adhesive or self-adhesive resin cements [15, 21]. In a study investigating different cements on zirconia substructures [21], zinc phosphate and resin-modified glass ionomer cements exhibited lower retention than composite resin cements. However, other studies report no significant difference between adhesive and self-adhesive resin cements [23–25].

The low wettability and chemical inertness of zirconia present a challenge for bonding [22, 26–28]. In cases where moisture control is difficult, the performance of adhesive cements may be compromised. One study comparing five different cements found self-adhesive resin cements to perform better than adhesive cements on ceramic restorations [15]. In the present study, resin-modified glass ionomer cement consistently showed lower retention compared to both adhesive and self-adhesive resin cements for both substructures, corroborating earlier findings. The relatively weak performance of resin-modified glass ionomer cement may be attributed to the 24-hour storage period prior to testing, as its bonding strength is known to increase over time.

In the present study, no significant difference was found between adhesive and self-adhesive resin cements in the zirconia group (Group A), in agreement with Rödiger et al. [15]. However, in Groups P and C, resin-modified glass ionomer cement showed significantly lower tensile strength, also consistent with their findings. Conversely, Group O demonstrated the highest retention with adhesive resin cement, surpassing self-adhesive resin cement. Both studies identified abutment geometry and cement type as key determinants of retention. Yet, Rödiger et al. tested only zirconia copings with two abutment heights and reported higher values ​​for self adhesive resin cement, whereas the present study included zirconia and Co-Cr substructures with varied geometries, where adhesive resin cement achieved the highest retention. This discrepancy may stem from differences in cement composition, surface treatment, and abutment convergence angles. Furthermore, the performance of resin modified glass ionomer cement observed in this study was not examined by Rödiger et al., representing an additional contribution to the literature.

Preparation geometry is another key factor in retention. It is well established that increased convergence angles improve the internal fit of restorations [13]. Low-viscosity cements like zinc phosphate tend to produce better internal adaptation than resin-based or glass ionomer cements. In this study, Group O provided higher retention values compared to Groups A and P, with the difference being statistically significant in zirconia samples. This may be explained by the fact that having two opposing walls creates a more defined and parallel path of insertion, increases the frictional resistance during seating, and reduces cement escape paths, resulting in a thinner and more uniform cement layer that enhances micromechanical interlocking.

A recent investigation demonstrated that abutments with a 12° total occlusal convergence provided superior internal fit compared to 6° preparations, further highlighting the importance of convergence angle in cementation outcomes [13]. These findings align with the current study’s results in zirconia substructures, where increased convergence in Group O rather than Group C contributed to better retention, with the difference being statistically significant for ARC. This suggests that the improved seating and reduced cement thickness in higher convergence designs can directly enhance micromechanical retention.

Regarding material type, zirconia substructures exhibited lower retention values than Co-Cr, consistent with literature that reports poorer marginal fit and bonding potential for zirconia [29]. This reduced retention in zirconia is attributed to its lower surface energy and hydrophobic nature, which limit wetting and chemical bonding with most luting agents.

Comparing abutment geometries, those with two opposing walls showed higher retention than those with two adjacent walls, likely due to the difference in total occlusal convergence angles [12, 30]. Furthermore, cementation onto abutments with four intact axial walls may increase hydraulic pressure and lead to a thicker cement layer, which has been associated with reduced retention [23].

Other studies confirm the superior retention of resin-based cements over conventional cements such as zinc oxide-eugenol, glass ionomer, and zinc phosphate, especially in metal substructures [10, 31]. In this study, resin modified glass ionomer cement showed lower performance, while adhesive resin cement and self adhesive resin cement achieved comparable retention in both metal and zirconia specimens.

One study evaluating only metal substructures with zinc phosphate cement reported that abutments with two opposing walls yielded the highest retention, while intact four-wall abutments showed the lowest [12]. While this finding partially aligns with the present studies results, differences were observed: in the present study, Group P yielded the highest retention in metal substructures, whereas Group C did not show the lowest values.

Additionally, while Tan et al. [12] observed statistically significant differences among geometries, the present study found no statistically significant differences between metal groups among geometries. These differences may be explained by the cement type: mentioned study used only zinc phosphate, which depends purely on mechanical retention, making geometry more critical, whereas the present study used resin-based cements, which provide chemical adhesion and can compensate for less retentive geometries.

This in vitro study evaluated retention under controlled, unidirectional tensile loading, which does not fully replicate the complex, multi-directional forces and thermal variations present in the oral environment. Surface treatments, thermocycling, fatigue loading, and resistance form were not assessed, potentially limiting the clinical generalizability of the results. Future research should include in vivo studies incorporating aging protocols and functional loading to validate these findings. Additional investigations into the effects of surface treatments, cement space, abutment height, and taper could further optimize retention strategies for implant-supported restorations.

Conclusion

Enhancing abutment wall design can notably increase the retention of zirconia restorations compared to metal restorations. Self-adhesive resin cements, offering strong bonding and easy handling, are particularly effective for permanent zirconia and metal restorations.
