Thermodynamic, Transport, and Spectroscopic Characterization of Nicotinamide–Caffeine Aqueous Systems
Authors: Tanika Dutta, Malabika Talukdar, Siddhartha Panda, Prachi Choudhury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A holistic understanding of molecular interplay in multicomponent aqueous systems is crucial for developing the applications in pharmaceutical and biochemical domains. In the current research, the thermodynamic and transport properties and spectroscopic characteristics of nicotinamide (NA)–caffeine (CAF) aqueous systems have been investigated in a queue using specific volumetric, viscometric, and UV–visible spectroscopic approaches over a wide range of concentrations and temperatures (293.15 K–313.15 K). To elucidate the nature and strength of solute–solute, solute–cosolute, and solute–solvent interactions some of the parameters were evaluated such as apparent molar volumes, viscosity coefficients, activation parameters, and many more. The positive values of apparent molar volumes, together with transfer parameters, reveal the predominance of strong solute–solvent and solute–cosolute interactions mediaated through hydrogen bonding, dipole–dipole attractions, π–π stacking, and hydration cosphere overlap effects. Increasing CAF concentration enhances volumetric expansion and promotes molecular association, while elevated temperatures weaken the hydrogen-bonded water network and facilitate desolvation. Positive Jones–Dole coefficients and their temperature dependence indicate significant solvation accompanied by the structure-breaking behavior of NA in both binary and ternary systems. Thermodynamic activation parameters derived from Eyring’s treatment demonstrate that viscous flow proceeds through an activated process involving highly solvated transition states, with increasing free energy of activation reflecting enhanced molecular organization and restricted mobility in the mixed solvent environment. UV–visible spectroscopic observations further support the existence of intermolecular association between NA and CAF. Collectively, the results provide molecular level insights into hydration, solvation, and transport phenomena in biologically relevant aqueous systems, offering valuable information for the rational design of pharmaceutical, nutraceutical, and drug-delivery formulations containing NA and CAF.

Introduction

The escalating trend toward multifunctional therapeutic and nutraceutical formulations has intensified scientific interest in understanding how bioactive molecules interact when codissolved in complex aqueous environments. In this context, the combined presence of nicotinamide (NA) and caffeine (CAF), as a psychostimulant, has gained renewed relevance due to their frequent coexistence in energy supplements, cognitive enhancers, dermatological preparations, and metabolic health products.

NA is the amide derivative of vitamin B3 and a crucial water-soluble micronutrient involved in numerous biochemical and metabolic processes. It has numerous enhancing effects due to its character as a precursor of NAD+/NADP+ coenzymes. It regulates cellular redox balance, energy metabolisms, DNA repair pathways, and cell generation and improves neuronal and cognitive functions. Moreover, NA has attracted considerable attention in pharmaceutical, nutraceutical, and cosmetic applications because of its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties. In aqueous and mixed-solvent systems, its strong hydrophilic nature and ability to participate in extensive hydrogen-bonding interactions enhance the solubility, stability, and bioavailability of various bioactive compounds. These properties of NA improve metabolic efficiency and support for proper functioning of the skin, nervous, and gastrointestinal systems. On the other side, CAF is a naturally occurring methylxanthine alkaloid commonly found in various beverages (coffee, tea, cocoa etc.) and pharmaceutical formulations which is commonly consumed by human beings. It acts as a central nervous system stimulant by antagonizing adenosine receptors, thereby reducing fatigue and increasing alertness and cognitive performance. Other than its neurostimulatory effects, CAF modifies neurotransmitter release and intracellular signaling pathways influencing cardiovascular, metabolic, and gastrointestinal functions. To affect molecular association, solubility, and transport behaviors in aqueous medium, CAF participates in π–π interactions, hydrophilic interactions, and hydrogen bonding. ,

The simultaneous consumption of CAF and NA is mainly observed in energy drinks and dietary supplements, which may improve the energy utilization and cognitive performance. Furthermore, NA may help to reduce oxidative stress associated with extreme caffeine-induced metabolic stimulation. However, very high CAF intakes can cause adverse effects such as insomnia, nervousness, or increased heart rate, which are not prevented by NA supplementations. Moreover, owing to its mild diuretic action, CAF may increase urinary excretion, potentially leading to the loss of water-soluble vitamins like vitamin B and C, therefore influencing nutrient balance and metabolic homeostasis.

In contemporary food, chemical, and pharmaceutical industries, a detailed understanding of thermophysical and transport properties of multicomponent liquid systems is indispensable for optimizing formulation stability, processing efficiency, and product performance. These properties provide crucial perception into solute–solvent and solute–solute interactions that govern molecular association–dissociation balance, solubility behavior, molecular organization, and phase stability in solution medium. For discovering subtle changes in hydration, molecular packing, and interaction strength, volumetric, viscometric, acoustic, and conductometric measurements are widely recognized as sensitive and reliable probes. − All these approaches have been extensively hired to scrutinize physicochemical properties in aqueous environments and molecular-level interactions.

There are limited reports of the systematic findings on NA–water and a negligible number of papers available for NA–water–CAF systems, even though application of vitamin-based formulations in functional food, nutraceuticals, pharmaceuticals, and cosmetic products is in the air, revealing a critical gap in fundamental thermodynamic and physicochemical understandings. Some of the literature studies mentioned below will − ,, reveal only a small number of studies addressing how cosolutes modify the hydration behavior and interaction patterns of NA in aqueous medium. This lack of comprehensive understanding poses a challenge for the rational design of stable, synergistic, and bioavailable multicomponent formulations.

Remarkably, Dhondge et al. and Kundu et al. examined the volumetric and viscometric traits of NA–water mixtures over a range of temperatures and reported important visions into the thermodynamic behavior of the systems. , Meanwhile, Mohapatra et al., Roy et al., and Sinha et al. explored derived parameters of density and viscosity to study the intermolecular interactions in aqueous NA as well in mixed systems containing different cosolutes (citric acid, tetrabutylammonium bromide, KCl, and DAP) at different working temperatures. ,,

By trying to accomplish all the void in the research area, the existing work advances beyond conventional investigations by focusing on the physicochemical behavior of NA in both systems, i.e., pure water as well as in aqueous CAF. By integrating thermophysical perspectives and spectroscopy, this work bridges the gap between fundamental solution chemistry and emerging real-world demands for an evidence-based formulation design. In this work, UV–visible spectroscopic analysis has been performed to support the results of thermochemical parameters. The knowledge derived is expected to contribute implicitly to the development of next-generation nutraceutical and pharmaceutical systems, where controlled molecular interactions dictate performance, bioavailability, and consumer safety.

Experimental Procedure

Required Materials

In the present work, analytical reagent (AnalR)-grade chemicals were used. Prior to experimentation, powdered NA and CAF were kept in a desiccator containing anhydrous calcium chloride (CaCl2) for 48 h to eliminate moisture uptake. The present study focuses on evaluating the influence of temperature and the presence of CAF on the physicochemical behavior of NA solutions. The use of high-quality reagents sourced from reputable manufacturers ensured the accuracy, reliability, and reproducibility of the experimental data. Preparation of Solutions

Nicotinamide and caffeine were stored in a desiccator over anhydrous CaCl2 to ensure the complete absorption of moisture from the reagents. Completely dried reagents were weighed in a stoppered sample container, and utmost care was taken during solution preparation to minimize atmospheric moisture absorption.

CAF stock solutions of predetermined molalities (0.025, 0.050, and 0.075 mol kg–1) were prepared by dissolving precisely weighed amounts of CAF in 1 kg of distilled water (density at 293.15 K: 997.12 kg m–3). These stock solutions were subsequently used as solvents to prepare NA solutions with molality values ranging from 0.02 to 0.14 mol kg–1.

Double-distilled deionized water obtained from a Milli-Q Millipore water purification system (specific conductance <1 × 10–6 S cm–1) was employed for the preparation of all solutions under standard laboratory conditions (293.15 K and 101 kPa). Solute masses were determined using a Systronics digital balance with a precision of 0.1 mg. The prepared solutions were refrigerated for no longer than two days to prevent degradation and moisture absorption by storing it in airtight volumetric flasks. The molalities of both CAF and NA solutions were reproducible within an uncertainty of 1 × 10–4 mol kg–1.

Methods

Density Measurements

Density measurements of NA solutions, prepared both in pure water and in an aqueous CAF medium, were carried out using a vibrating-tube density analyzer (Anton PaarDMA 5000 M, Austria). The instrument operates on the oscillating U-tube principle, in which variations in the oscillation frequency directly reflect changes in the density of the sample. Calibration was performed using dry air and deionized distilled water under ambient conditions before each measurement. The calibration procedure generally involves cleaning and drying the U-tube thoroughly before the introduction of the calibration fluids. For improved reproducibility, the measuring tube was carefully rinsed with distilled water followed by anhydrous ethanol. The measured density values of water should be related to standard literature values to verify the accuracy of the instrument. ,,

Precise temperature regulation of the measuring cell was ensured through an integrated Peltier thermostat, maintaining temperature stability within ±0.01 K, while the experimental pressure was maintained at 101 ± 1 kPa. According to GUM guidelines, the density accuracy and repeatability were ±0.005 kg m–3 and 0.0001 kg m–3, respectively. Consequently, the combined uncertainty associated with density measurements was estimated to be 0.4 kg m–3.

The uncertainties for derived properties have been calculated by following the relation as per the GUM guidelines 1 U=1N−1∑i=1N(xi−x̅)2N where 1N−1∑i=1N(xi−x̅)2N is the standard deviation, U is the standard uncertainty, N is the total number of measurements, x̅ reflects the mean of the measurements, and x i signifies each measurement in the data set.

Viscosity Measurements

Viscosity measurements were performed under carefully controlled experimental conditions using a U-shaped capillary viscometer (Ostwald viscometer) equipped with a bulb of approximately (25 ± 0.01) mL capacity. Before measurements, the viscometer was thoroughly cleaned and calibrated with distilled water at five different temperatures to correct for temperature-dependent viscosity variations. The instrument must be cleaned with a suitable solvent and dried before each measurement to avoid contamination. The flow time of the water through the capillary readings are obtained and using standard viscosity data available in the literature, the viscometer constant is determined. The time required was assessed using a digital stopwatch with a precision of ±0.1 s for the liquid meniscus to pass between two fixed reference marks on the viscometer tube. Temperature control is particularly important because viscosity is highly temperature dependent, and therefore, measurements should be carried out using a thermostatically controlled water bath with proper equilibrium.

The dynamic viscosity of the solution (η1) was calculated relative to that of the pure solvent (η0) by incorporating the experimentally determined densities (ρ0 and ρ1) and flow times (t 0 and t 1), following the standard relation 2 η1η0=(ρ1t1)(ρ0t0)

All measurements were performed under constant thermal equilibrium to diminish mechanical disturbances and temperature fluctuations. Three repeated readings of each sample are noted to ensure reproducibility and the average values were reported with an uncertainty of ±0.002 mPa s. Viscometric coefficients (K) achieved after calibration at 298.15 K is K ≈ 10–6. Viscosity data offered valuable insights into solute–solute and solute–solvent interactions within the mixed aqueous systems.

The present assessment provides the first comprehensive and systematic molecular interaction analysis of NA–CAF aqueous systems over the temperature range from 293.15 to 313.15 K by integrating volumetric and viscometric tactics. Density and viscosity measurements were employed to derive key thermodynamic and transport parameters, including apparent molar volume at finite concentrations and infinite dilution (V φ, V φ 0), partial molar expansibility (E φ 0), Hepler’s constants ( (∂Eφ0/∂T)P ), and transfer parameters (Δtr V φ 0). Viscometric data were analyzed using the Jones–Dole equation to obtain viscosity coefficients A and B, where the A F reflects solute–solute interactions and the B J provide insights into solute–solvent interactions and confirms whether the solute behaves as a structure-maker or structure-breaker agent in a given solution. Additionally, ion-pair and triplet interaction coefficients (V AB/V ABB and B AB/B ABB) were determined to quantify higher order associative effects. Collectively, this multidimensional approach yields a detailed understanding of solute–solute and solute–solvent interactions, with direct relevance to biological, nutritional, pharmaceutical, and food formulation systems where CAF and vitamin components coexist.

UV–Visible Spectroscopy

UV–visible spectroscopic analysis was executed by using a PerkinElmer Lambda 365+ spectrophotometer. The technique is based on the absorption of ultraviolet and visible radiation by molecules in a solution. In this instrument, a xenon flash lamp acts as the light source, and the chosen wavelength passes through the sample contained in a quartz cuvette. The intensity of absorbed and transmitted radiation is measured using a high-sensitivity detector, and the absorbance is determined according to the Berr–Lambert law. The instrument ensures fast and reliable absorbance measurements across the UV–visible region, facilitating the investigation of electronic transitions and intermolecular interactions in solution. All spectra were recorded at room temperature over the wavelength range of 200–350 nm.

Apparent Molar Volume

The apparent molar volume of the solution was evaluated using experimentally measured density data and calculated according to eq . 3 Vφ=[(ρ0−ρ)(mρρ0)]+(Mρ) In this expression, ρ0 and ρ1 denote the densities of the pure solvent and the solution, respectively, m represents the molality of the solution and M corresponds to the molar mass of the solute. The uncertainty associated with the apparent molar volume (∂V φ) was estimated using eq . 4 ∂Vφ=(Mρ−Vφ)2(∂m/m)2+(Vφ+1/mρ0)2(∂ρ/ρ)2]1/2 which accounts for the contribution arising from uncertainties in molality, molar mass, and density parameters. Given that the uncertainty in molality is negligibly small for the present measurements, only the uncertainty in density (∂ρ) was considered in the error analysis. Consequently, at lower molality ranges, the uncertainty in apparent molar volume was approximated to be 0.2 × 10–6 m3 mol–1.

The introduction of NA into aqueous CAF solutions induces a reorganization of the solvent structure to accommodate the solute species, resulting in measurable changes in the volume occupied per mole of the solute. Depending on the strength and nature of NA–CAF–water interactions, the apparent molar volume of the system may either decrease due to enhanced solute–solvent attractions and structural compaction or increase when the formation of new molecular assemblies leads to volumetric expansion. Such variations in apparent molar volume are governed by factors including the chemical nature of NA and CAF, their relative concentrations, temperature, and the specific intermolecular interactions operating within the solution. In the present investigation, the apparent molar volume values (V φ) of NA in aqueous CAF media are reported in Table as functions of temperature and molality.

A perusal of Table shows that the values of V φ for NA are higher in aqueous CAF than in water. NA (C5H4N–CONH2) is produced by substituting the carboxylic group (−COOH) in niacin (C5H4N–COOH) with an amide (−CONH2) group. The NA molecule has an electrical dipole moment of 3.315 debye. It has many protonation sites (see Table ) connected to the moieties of pyridine (C5H5N) and amide (−CONH2). Since pyridine is a potent nucleophile, there is a significant electron density at this location on the NA molecule. When CAF is added as a cosolute to an aqueous NA solution, strong NA–CAF interactions (hydrogen-bonding, π–π stacking, and dipole–dipole) may establish, leading to mixed molecular association. These interactions disrupt the hydration structure of water, reduce electrostriction, and result in an expansion of the solution manifested as an increased apparent molar volume in aqueous CAF.

As discussed earlier, variations in apparent molar volume upon solute addition are strongly influenced by the nature of the solute and solvent, their concentrations, and the specific intermolecular interactions. The decrease in apparent molar volume V φ with increasing NA concentration, as evident from Table and Figure , can be attributed to the progressive enhancement of solute–solute interactions and overlap of hydration cospheres in solution. At low concentrations, NA molecules are extensively hydrated, and the surrounding water molecules remain relatively structured, resulting in comparatively larger V φ values due to strong solute–solvent interactions. However, as the concentration of NA increases, the average distance between solute molecules decreases, promoting a closer association among NA molecules through dipole–dipole interactions and possible stacking interactions between pyridine rings. Consequently, the apparent molar volume decreases with increasing NA concentration. The increase in V φ with increasing CAF concentration suggests the predominance of favorable heteromolecular interactions between NA and CAF in the aqueous medium. As the concentration of CAF increases, enhanced solute–solute interactions such as hydrogen bonding, dipole–dipole attraction, and possible π–π interactions between the aromatic rings of NA and CAF become significant. These interactions promote the formation of relatively expanded and structured molecular assemblies in solution. In addition, CAF, being comparatively hydrophobic with multiple electron-rich sites, can perturb the bulk water structure and facilitate the formation of loosely packed hydration regions around the associated NA–CAF complexes. This results in a net expansion of the solution volume, thereby producing higher V φ values with increasing CAF concentrations.

As the temperature increases, thermal agitation progressively weakens the ordered solute–solvent interactions and reduces the extent of structured hydration. The observed increase in V φ with temperature reflects the reduced compactness of the hydrated NA system and the gradual transition toward a less structured and more expanded solution environment.

Limiting Apparent Molar Volume

Although the Masson equation was originally developed for electrolyte solutions, its application to nonelectrolyte systems such as aqueous NA solutions can also be justified when significant solute–solute and solute–solvent interactions exist in the medium. In dilute solutions, the apparent molar volume V φ frequently exhibits a concentration dependence that follows a square-root term, reflecting the progressive overlap of hydration cospheres and intermolecular association effects. Accordingly, the variation of V φ with concentration can be represented by the Masson-type relation 5 Vφ=Vφ0+Svm1/2

Extrapolation of the above straight line produces intercept V φ 0 and slope S v . Limiting apparent molar volume (V φ 0) implies the value of apparent molar volume at infinite dilution and the volumetric pairwise interaction coefficient (S v ) implies the interactions between solute particles. The Masson equation is employed here as an empirical relationship to describe the concentration dependence of apparent molar volume and to extract meaningful interaction parameters from the experimental data.

NA and CAF possess distinct molecular features that enable them to exhibit both hydrophilic and hydrophobic characteristics in an aqueous medium. NA contains a polar amide group (−CONH2) along with an aromatic ring, whereas CAF comprises a fused aromatic ring with multiple polar sites capable of interacting with water molecules. This structural duality plays a vital role in governing their solvation behavior and intermolecular interactions in solution. The presence of hydrophilic functional groups facilitates participation in enzymatic and metabolic processes through effective hydrogen bonding with the surrounding water molecules.

As the concentration of NA increases in aqueous CAF solutions, enhanced interactions occur among the hydrophilic regions of the solute molecules. As a result, an increase in the limiting apparent molar volume (V φ 0) is observed in CAF containing systems. The positive values of V φ 0 increase with rising temperature, indicating enhanced ion–solvent interactions. The temperature derivative of eq at constant pressure defines the partial molar expansibility (E φ 0), a thermodynamic quantity that describes the extent to which the solution volume responds to temperature changes per mole of solute. This parameter provides valuable insights into solvation characteristics, solvent structure modifications, and the combined influence of solute size and hydrophobicity. Mathematically, it is expressed as 8 Eφ0=(∂Vφ0/∂T)p=b+2cT

Positive values of the limiting apparent molar expansibility (E φ 0), as presented in Table , strongly indicate an effective balance between ion–hydrophilic and hydrophilic–hydrophilic interactions within the system. Similar observations have been conveyed by Kumar et al. in thermodynamic and physicochemical investigations of zwitterionic amino acids such as l-serine and l-leucine in aqueous DAP solution. Positive expansibility (E φ 0) values suggested that solute–solvent interactions are sufficiently strong to reinforce the hydrogen-bonded network of water, thereby promoting an increase in solution volume.

In this context, hydrophilic functional groups of the solute form favorable hydrogen bonds with surrounding water molecules, whereas hydrophobic moieties introduce localized perturbations in the solvent structure. The combined contribution of these effects results in volumetric expansion. Hence, E φ 0 serves as an important thermodynamic indicator for assessing the long-range structure-making or structure-breaking behavior of solutes in aqueous and mixed solvent systems. , It is therefore particularly effective in distinguishing solutes that promote solvent structuring from those that induce structural relaxation and expansion. The positive yet gradually decreasing trend of E φ 0 values (from 0.025 to 0.075 mol kg–1) with increasing temperature can be attributed to the weakening of electrostatic constraints between solute and solvent molecules, which enhances molecular mobility and facilitates thermal expansion of the solution.

Hepler’s Parameter

An assessment of whether NA acts as a water structure-making or structure-deforming species can be achieved by evaluating the temperature dependence of the limiting apparent molar expansibility. Accordingly, the temperature derivative of E φ 0 at constant pressure was obtained using the thermodynamic relation given in eq 9 (∂Eφ0/∂T)P=(∂2Vφ0/∂T2)P=2c

This relationship yields a temperature-independent constant 2c, which mainly provides the quantitative measure of how the apparent molar expansibility of a solute varies with temperature, that is commonly known as Hepler’s constant, after the name of L. G. Hepler, and it is widely employed to interpret solute-induced modifications in solvent structures. The sign and magnitude of (∂Eφ0/∂T)P serves as reliable criteria for distinguishing between structure-making and structure-breaking behaviors of solute in a given solvent medium.

Specifically, positive values and small negative values of (∂Eφ0/∂T)P are associated with structure-making tendencies, whereas distinctly negative values correspond to the structure-breaking behavior. In aqueous systems, water molecules exist in a dynamic equilibrium between highly ordered three-dimensional hydrogen-bonded networks and a less structured monomeric form. When the presence of solute promotes or stabilizes the ordered hydrogen-bonded framework of water, the solute is categorized as a structure maker. Contrarily, solutes that disrupt this three-dimensional hydrogen-bonded network are identified as structure breakers.

From the data presented in Table , it is evident that for NA, the values of (∂Eφ0/∂T)P are insignificantly positive in 0.025 mol kg–1 aqueous CAF and negative in 0.05 and 0.075 mol kg–1 aqueous CAF and in pure water. This observation indicates that NA acts as a structure breaker in water and across the entire concentration range of aqueous CAF.

Falkenhagen and Jones–Dole Coefficients

An empirical relationship that describes the dependence of the relative velocity of a solution on its concentration is specifically denoted the Jones–Dole expression. It is mathematically expressed in eq , where the ratio of viscosity of the solution mixture to that of the pure solvent is designated as relative viscosity (ηr), c is the molar concentration of the solution, and A F and B J are empirical constants. A F or the A-coefficient, also acknowledged as the Falkenhagen coefficient, accounts for long-range ion–ion electrostatic interactions, while B J or the B-coefficient, also known as Jones–Dole coefficient, signifies ion–solvent interactions. 12 ηr=1+AFc+BJc

Rearrangement of the Jones–Dole equation yields a linear form 13 (ηr−1)√c=AF+BJc

This form of equation is specially required to understand because it permits the experimental determination of the A and B coefficients from the viscosity values. A plot obtained from (ηr−1)√c versus c (Figure and Table ) provides the values of intercept (A F ) and the slope (B J ). Thus, the rearrangement facilitates quantitative analysis of molecular interactions in solutions and confirms the validity of the equation in dilute systems.

The molal concentration (m/mol kg–1) of the prepared systems can be readily converted into molar concentration (c/mol dm–3) by incorporating the density (ρ) of the solution and molecular mass (M) of the solute into the following conversion relation 14 c=(ρ×m)/(1+mM)

Through the combined estimation of the viscosity coefficients A F and B J , a comprehensive awareness into solute–solvent structuring can be achieved. For electrolytes, A F is related to long-range electrostatic ion–ion interactions, and this interpretation is not directly transferable to neutral nicotinamide molecules. Therefore, the discussion is restricted mainly to B J .

The B J coefficient, largely governed by the molecular size, shape, and structural appearances of the solute, serves as a quantitative measure of the extent of solvation and the capability of the species to reorganize and modulate the surrounding solvent network. In total, these coefficients clarify the intricate interplay between the localized solvation phenomenon and long-range electrostatic interactions, thereby providing deeper insights into the viscosity behavior and microstructural organization of the solution. Table displays a positive and increasing trend of B J values for both binary and ternary systems of NA at all working temperatures confirming strong ion–solvent interactions. Positive B J values are also testified by Roy et al. while investigating molecular interactions of NA in aqueous citric acid monohydrate solutions.

A more consistent assessment of ion–solvent interactions can be obtained from the temperature derivatives of the Jones–Dole coefficient, expressed as (∂BJ/∂T)P . These thermodynamic parameters give knowledge regarding the structural organization of the solute, whether it is a breaker or a maker. Structure-making (kosmotropic) behavior is directed by the negative values of (∂BJ/∂T)P , alternatively positive values imply structure-breaking (chaotropic) inclinations. In the existing system, a positive dB/dT value suggests that the B-coefficient increases with the temperature, implying reduced water structuring and greater disruption of the hydrogen-bonded network, which is typical of structure-breaking solutes. This behavior also suggests that NA interferes with the hydrogen-bonded network of pure aqueous liquid, destroying the degree of intermolecular ordering among solvent molecules. Water–water interactions wane due to such kinds of disruption, thereby influencing the thermodynamic and transport properties of the NA–CAF aqueous systems. Therefore, it mirrors a tendency toward solvent structure disruption in the presence of CAF.

Thus, a system may exhibit positive B-coefficients due to appreciable solute hydration while simultaneously showing positive dB/dT values that classify the solute as a structure breaker. This apparent duality arises because B-coefficients primarily reflect the strength of local solute–solvent interactions, whereas dB/dT describes how these interactions influence the overall temperature-dependent structure of bulk water.

Transfer Parameters of Partial Molar Volume and Viscosity B-Coefficient

In the case of a highly diluted solution, solute molecules are widely separated, rendering negligible solute–solute interactions. The limiting apparent molar volume (V φ 0) is governed predominantly by solute–solvent interactions, under such conditions. Consequently, the transfer of limiting apparent molar volume (Δtr V φ 0) serves as an effective probe for assessing solute–cosolute interactions in mixture medium. The transfer parameter is defined as 15 ΔtrVφ(water→water+CAF)0=Vφ(NA+water+CAF)0−Vφ(NA+water)0

Similarly, the difference between the B-coefficient of a solute in a mixed solvent system and that in pure water is called the transfer parameters of the viscosity B-coefficient and is denoted by Δtr B J which is expressed in eq . 16 ΔtrBJ(water→water+CAF)=BJ(NA+water+CAF)−BJ(NA+water)

As discussed earlier, solute-co-solute interactions encompass a complex interplay of ionic, polar, and nonpolar domains, involving ion–ion, ion–hydrophobic, ion–hydrophilic, hydrophilic–hydrophilic, and hydrophobic–hydrophobic associations. Within the framework of Friedman and Krishnan, hydration cosphere overlaps the model, overlapping hydration shells of ions promote cooperative structuring of the solvent, thereby influencing the apparent molar volume. Specifically, the overlap of hydrophilic hydration cospheres generally exerts a positive volumetric contribution, while hydrophobic hydration cosphere overlaps yield a negative volumetric effect. Also, an increased structural ordering in mixed medium or ion–solvent interactions enhancement is indicated by positive values of Δtr B J , while the negative values of Δtr B J delivers evidence regarding weakening of interactions and structure-breaking effects. Thus, the transfer B-coefficient obliges as an imperative thermodynamic tool for assessing molecular interactions and structural amendments in multicomponent liquid systems.

Table shows that the values of Δtr V φ 0 are positive at all experimental temperatures and increases with the increase in concentration of CAF in the solution of NA–CAF. This phenomenon signifies an overall volume expansion of the system upon transfer. This behavior indicates that solute–solute interactions particularly NA–CAF associations through hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and hydrophobic interactions become increasingly dominant over solute–solvent interactions. This mutual presence of higher amounts of both solutes promotes desolvation and disruption of structured water around the molecules, leading to the release of hydration water into the bulk. Consequently, hydration is reduced and noncovalent molecular association is strengthened in the mixed solution.

In the same way, Δtr B J values are positive and systematically increase with the molality of the CAF and demonstrates an inverse dependence on temperature. Since the solute shows a structure-breaking nature in pure aqueous medium, the obtained positive values of Δtr B J mainly indicate strengthening of ion–solvent interactions in a NA–CAF mixed system. This behavior may be attributed to preferential hydration and enhanced intermolecular association in the presence of the cosolute. Therefore, the positive Δtr B J values mainly reflect the dominance of specific solvation effects and competitive interactions in the ternary medium rather than signifying the structure-making tendency.

Solvation Number

The average number of solvent molecules that are strongly bound to a solute particle (ion or molecule) in a system is commonly known as the solvation number (S n ). It replicates the formation of solvation shell around the solute and provides insights into the extent of solute–solvent interactions present in the system. In the case of a pure aqueous system, this parameter is often referred to as the hydration number, as water molecules surround and stabilize the solute particles. The parameter is assessed from the relationship between the viscosity coefficient (B J ) and the partial molar volume (V φ 0), mathematically expressed as , 19 Sn=BJ/Vφ0

Solvation number (S n ) determination is useful to understand the molecular interactions and structural effects occurring in solution. A solvation number higher than 2.5 indicates stronger solute–solvent interactions and a greater degree of structuring of solvent molecules around the solute. On the other hand, a lower value suggests weaker interactions. ,

S n values for the aqueous NA and NA in the aqueous CAF system are reported in Table . Table reflects that in pure aqueous NA, the S n value increases with the rising temperature, indicating the enhancement of solute–solvent interactions and greater accessibility of water molecules around the solute. With the increase in temperature, there is a presence of thermal motion of molecules which reduces the strong hydrogen bonding network between the water molecules and allows them to interact directly with the polar functional groups of NA (−OH). Whereas, in the case of NA in aqueous CAF solution, S n declines with the increase in concentrations of CAF and temperatures in all the systems, which is mainly due to the weakening of solute–solvent interactions. At higher CAF concentrations, enhanced solute–solute interactions between NA and CAF molecules cause reduction in the number of water molecules available in the primary solvation shell as the NA and CAF molecules come nearer to each other, which leads to sharing of the solvent molecules between the neighboring species. As a result, desolvation occurs, and hence, there is a decrease in the solvation number. Additionally, increasing temperature increases molecular motion and weakens hydrogen bonding between solute and water molecules, leading to partial disruption of the solvation shell.

Thermodynamics of Viscous Flow

The energy fluctuations associated with the movement of molecules in a liquid during flow are defined as the thermodynamics of viscous flow. As per the transition-state theory, viscous flow occurs when molecules overcome an energy barrier to move from one equilibrium position to another. This process is categorized by thermodynamic constraints such as the free energy of activation, enthalpy of activation, and entropy of activation. The free energy of activation represents the energy required for a molecule to move and create a temporary void in the liquid structure. The enthalpy of activation reflects the energy needed to break intermolecular interactions, while the entropy of activation indicates the degree of disorder during the flow process.

UV–Visible Spectroscopy

The recorded spectra in Figure correspond to the spectroscopic results of aqueous NA, aqueous CAF, and (NA + CAF + water) systems. In the case of aqueous CAF, an intense absorption band centered around 222 nm is attributed to the transition associated with the imidazole and pyrimidine ring systems, whereas the secondary absorption band near 301 nm corresponds to the lower energy transition involving the nitrogen atoms present within the heterocyclic framework. Similarly, aqueous NA exhibits characteristic absorption maxima around 236 and 283 nm, assigned π–π*and n–π* transitions, respectively, arising from the amide functional group.

UV–visible spectra of aqueous NA, aqueous CAF, and (NA + CAF + water) mixtures.

Upon incorporation of 0.075 mol kg–1 of CAF into changing concentrations of NA, noticeable spectral broadening and peak displacement were observed, indicating alterations in the electronic environment due to intermolecular interactions. For the system containing 0.14 mol kg–1 of NA and 0.075 mol kg–1 of CAF, the absorption maximum corresponding to the transition exhibited a bathochromic shift from 283 to 296 nm. Furthermore, with an increasing concentration of mixed solutions (NA + CAF + water), the lower-wavelength absorption region progressively shifted from 283 nm (0.02 m) to 248 nm (0.10 m), suggesting a pronounced red shift. The behavior specifies stabilization of the excited electronic state relative to the ground state, likely resulting from improved solute–solute and solute–solvent interactions at higher molecular concentrations.

The observed spectral broadening and bathochromic shifts suggest the existence of intermolecular association phenomena such as π–π stacking interactions and hydrogen bonding within the aqueous medium. However, UV–visible spectroscopy alone does not provide direct evidence for hydrogen-bond formation, interaction strength, or changes in the molecular structure. The FTIR study would be reinforcing the above concepts more accurately. FTIR studies are presented in a separate article communicated by the present authors. These interactions modify the local electronic surroundings of the chromophoric groups, thereby influencing the electronic transition energies and resulting in the observed spectral changes.

Conclusion

The present investigation offers a comprehensive and integrated understanding of the thermodynamic, transport, and spectroscopic behaviors of NA in water and aqueous CAF medium over the temperature range 293.15–313.15 K. The systematic analysis of density and viscosity data, supported by UV–visible spectroscopic observations, has provided valuable molecular-level visions into the complex interplay of solute–solute, solute–cosolute, and solute–solvent interactions governing the physicochemical properties of the given systems. The positive limiting molar volumes, transfer parameters, and Jones–Dole coefficient indicates strong NA–CAF interactions, which involves hydrogen bonding, dipole–dipole attractions, and π–π stacking. Increasing CAF concentration enhances molecular association, hydration-shell overlap, and volumetric expansion, while reducing the extent of solvation around NA. Hepler’s criterion and the positive temperature dependence of the B-coefficient suggest that NA behaves predominantly as a structure-breaking solute in investigated medium. Positive pair interaction coefficients and negative triplet interaction coefficients reveal the dominance of specific NA–CAF pairwise interactions over higher-order cooperative effects. Thermodynamic activation parameters further confirm the formation of organized transition states during viscous flow, whereas UV–visible spectroscopy supports the existence of intermolecular association in solution. Overall, the results deliver a coherent molecular level understanding of hydration, solvation, and transport phenomena in NA–CAF aqueous medium, offering valuable perceptions for the development of pharmaceutical and nutraceutical formulations.
