The effect of micro-movement on prevention of intraoperative acquired pressure injury in overweight patients undergoing posterior lumbar surgery: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jing Zhang, Ping Wang, Li En Qi, Shuo Feng, Feng Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn posterior lumbar surgery, overweight patients are more prone to intraoperative acquired pressure injury (IAPI) due to bleeding, longer operative time, and increased pressure. The current prevention strategies have limited effects.MethodsThis was a single-blind, prospective, randomized controlled trial conducted from April 2023 to September 2024. Eligible participants were overweight patients (BMI ≥ 24 kg/m²) scheduled for lumbar posterior surgery under general anesthesia. Patients were randomly assigned to two groups: the micro-movement group and the silicone foam dressing group. The micro-movement group had regular adjustments to the operating table to change the patient’s position and alleviate pressure. The silicone foam dressing group used standard foam dressings at pressure points.ResultsA total of 277 patients were included in the analysis (137 in the micromovement group and 140 in the silicone foam dressing group). Compared with the silicone foam dressing group (8.6%), the incidence of IAPI in the micromovement group was significantly reduced (2.9%) (P < 0.05). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of IAPI stage, duration, or location (P > 0.05).ConclusionThe micromovement protocol is a safe and effective method for preventing IAPI in overweight patients undergoing posterior lumbar surgery. This simple intervention could significantly improve patient outcomes and reduce healthcare costs associated with IAPI.

Introduction

Intraoperative Acquired Pressure Injuries (IAPI) refer to skin and subcutaneous tissue damage caused by continuous pressure, friction, or shear forces acting on specific parts of a patient’s body during surgery. These injuries manifest as erythema, bruising, blisters, or tissue necrosis [1], with incidence rates ranging from 0.3 to 57.4% [2]. Sherman et al. noted an IAPI occurrence rate of 17.6% in patients undergoing posterior spine surgery [3]. Lin et al. analyzed 209 prone position spine surgery patients and found a pressure injury incidence rate of 23% [4].

Perioperative risk factors for pressure injuries have been widely recognized, and these factors may vary depending on the type of surgery. Wu et al. conducted a retrospective analysis of 465 neurosurgical patients and found that obesity, prone or lateral position, use of a headrest, and surgery lasting more than 4 h were risk factors for IAPI. At the same time, high serum albumin and memory foam pads can reduce the risk of IAPI [5]. Luo et al. analyzed 3834 spinal surgery patients and found that age over 60 years, low body weight (BMI < 18.0 kg/m²), Braden score below 13, and prolonged preoperative and postoperative times were associated with an increased risk of IAPI [6]. Prone position is one of the complex positions in general anesthesia and is crucial for spinal, neurological, and urological surgeries. However, it may cause complications such as IAPI, visual problems, nerve damage, and pharyngeal edema. In particular, during spinal surgery, the patient’s chest, chin, and cheeks bear most of the load, making them prone to pressure injuries [7]. A study has shown that overweight patients undergoing spinal surgery have more blood loss and longer operation times, further increasing the risk of IAPI [8]. Therefore, preventing IAPI in patients undergoing prone position surgery has become a focus of perioperative care.

A study on patients undergoing spinal surgery indicates that soft silicone foam dressings are superior to polyurethane film dressings in preventing IAPIs [9]. A systematic review suggests that reactive gel surfaces may be more effective than foam surfaces in reducing the risk of IAPI, especially in the operating room [10]. Although dressings and positioning cushions can protect the skin from shear and friction forces, they cannot directly reduce pressure injuries between the skin and the supporting surface. To prevent pressure, repositioning has become a key intervention method, alleviating local skin pressure and reducing pressure exposure time through adjusting body position. Relevant guideline suggests that people who cannot adjust their position autonomously can relieve pressure through brief positional changes [11]. The micro-movement, referring to slight, controlled positional changes made at regular intervals without interfering with the surgical process. These changes involve tilting at a slight angle to alter pressure points, aiming to reduce surgery-related complications by changing the distribution of pressure and promoting blood circulation. Scholars propose micro-movement strategies, suggesting that patients in the supine position should perform 15° of fine positional adjustments every 2 h. When lying on a 30° lateral position, this method can effectively reduce skin pressure and the risk of IAPls [12]. However, the effectiveness of micro-movement protocols for prone patients is not yet clear. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of micro-movement strategies in posterior lumbar surgery, especially for overweight patients, to provide more scientific and practical preventive measures.

Research design and methods

We conducted a single-blind, prospective, randomized controlled trial conducted from April 2023 to September 2024 and has been registered in the China Clinical Trial Registry (Registration number: ChiCTR2400087696). The trial has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Central Hospital Biomedical Research (Approval number: XZXY-LJ-20240401-098). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the National Research Council and the Helsinki Declaration. A total of 298 overweight patients who underwent posterior lumbar surgery under general anesthesia were enrolled in this study.

Stratification process

Patients are stratified based on key covariates that may affect the outcome of the surgery, including age, gender, injury severity, history of diabetes, smoking status, and skin health condition. Age is divided into four levels: 18–29 years, 30–44 years, 45–59 years, and > 60 years; gender is divided into male and female; body mass index (BMI) is divided into two levels:24-27.9, ≥ 28. Within each stratum, randomization is performed to ensure that patients within each level have an equal chance of being assigned to either the silicone foam dressing group or the micro-movement group.

Randomization process

Random numbers are generated using excel to number patients from 1 to 298 after they sign the informed consent and enter them into an excel table. The random numbers are then reordered, a 1:1 randomization ratio was used, with the first 149 assigned to the micro-motion scheme group and the last 149 to the silicone foam dressing group.

Single-blind design

Due to the nature of the intervention (micromovement scheme vs. silicone foam dressing decompression scheme), double blinding is not feasible for the surgical team. Therefore, a single-blinded design is chosen, where the patient does not know which group they are in.

Intervention

Silicone foam dressing group

One day before surgery, according to the CORN postoperative pressure injury risk scale and perioperative hypothermia risk scale, take effective measures to prevent IAPI and perioperative hypothermia. The operation team collaborates to help the patient establish a comfortable prone position: Anesthesiologists protect the head to avoid tracheal intubation slippage, surgeons, circulating nurses work together to move the patient slightly to one side of the operating table, then place the patient prone on a one-piece prone pad. Place the head on a U-shaped silicone gel pad to prevent facial compression, ensure that the airway remains unobstructed during the operation, and avoid damaging the eyes, compressing the supraorbital nerve plexus. The arms are naturally curved, ensuring no excessive abduction resulting in brachial plexus damage. Both forearms rest on soft arm boards, abdomen suspended without increased intraabdominal pressure, hips placed on memory foam soft pads, knees placed on knee pads, feet lifted off the ground, ankles not compressed, shoulders and knees secured with protective straps, and hip bones adorned with silicone foam dressings.

Micro-movement group

On the basis of the control group, implement the “micro-movement scheme”. A bedside tilt angle roller measuring ruler is placed on the head side of the operating table, with the time immediately after placing the patient in the prone position as 0 h, and the bed is kept level. Establish a micro-movement registration book. One hour after the start of the operation, the circulating nurse communicates effectively with the anesthesiologist and surgeon, ensures that the surgeon stops the operation, checks the breathing tube, and tilts the operating table slowly to the left by remote control for 15°, tilting the head and face in the opposite direction. Maintain for 5 min, 2 h later, the same method tilts the operating table to the right for 15until the end of the operation (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Sample Size : The primary outcome measure of this study is the incidence of IAPI. Based on previous studies [12], the incidence of IAPI under silicone foam dressings is 10%, while it is expected to be reduced to 2% with the micro-movement scheme. Therefore, the effect size is 8%. Setting the significance level (α) at 0.05, statistical power (1 - β): as 0.80, into the formula for two independent sample rates, we preliminarily calculate that each group needs 134 participants. Taking into account a potential loss rate of 10%, the final sample size per group should be adjusted to 149.

Data Processing: Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0 statistical software. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. Descriptive statistics are as follows: For categorical data, chi-square test was used; when the total sample size (N) ≥ 40, but at least one cell value is between 1 and 5 (1 ≤ T < 5), apply the corrected formula. For continuous data, repeated measures ANOVA was used for comparison within groups, and two independent samples t test was used for pairwise comparisons between groups. P < 0.05, difference was statistically significant.

Discussion

This study explored the clinical efficacy of “micro-movement” scheme in preventing acquired pressure injuries in patients undergoing posterior lumbar surgery. The results showed that compared with the control group receiving silicone foam dressings, patients receiving micro-movement scheme had a significantly reduced incidence of acquired pressure injuries (P < 0.05). The incidence of acquired pressure injury may be slightly lower than that in Jin et al.‘s recent study [12]. The study shows that the incidence of IAPI is ranked as follows: chin (21.67%), nose (21.67%), knee (10.83%) and lip (3.61%).

Previous studies have shown that prone position is associated with an increased risk of pressure ulcers, with the face being the highest risk area, followed by knees and feet [13, 14]. Posterior lumbar spine surgery is a common spinal surgery. Patients usually need several hours or even longer on their stomachs. In the case of fixed body positions, especially on hard operating tables without adequate positioning protection measures, patients are more likely to develop pressure injuries due to local forces applied to vulnerable organ and soft tissue structures such as facial tissues, female breasts, lower ribs, iliac crests, genitals (especially in men), knees, tibiae, and dorsum of the foot. The occurrence of surgical stress injury can range from mild skin redness to severe tissue necrosis, and even affect muscles and bones. A Study has shown that ischemia-reperfusion is a key mechanism for the development of postoperative acquired pressure injuries [15]. Vertical pressure can lead to changes in cell morphology, prolonged vertical pressure greater than normal capillary pressure can block blood flow, leading to microcirculatory disorders and hypoxic damage, eventually leading to irreversible cellular damage Tissue ischemia followed by reperfusion after blood flow recovery, neutrophils and macrophages gather, release inflammatory factors and oxygen free radicals, leading to reperfusion injury, resulting in irreversible cellular damage [16]. Previous research also confirmed that patients who were prone for ≥ 3.3 h had a 3.2 times higher likelihood of developing postoperative acquired pressure injuries than those who were prone for < 3.3 h [17]. The most common stage of pressure ulcer according to the National Pressure Ulcer Advisory Group survey was II stage (64%), followed by I stage (28%) [18]. Pressure injuries can significantly impact a patient’s quality of life, potentially causing persistent chronic pain that limits their mobility and further reduces their autonomy in daily activities. These activities include personal hygiene, handling objects, walking, maintaining a sitting position, standing, sleeping, sexual activity, social interactions, and traveling, among others. Additionally, treating and caring for pressure injuries requires substantial medical resources, which may include long-term treatment costs, dressing costs, and potential surgery expenses, increasing the financial burden on patients and the operational costs for healthcare institutions [19].

Overweight patients face multiple specific risks during prone position surgery. There is a U-shaped relationship between BMI and pressure sores, meaning that patients with low or high BMI values are at the highest risk of developing two types of pressure sores [20]. Due to increased body weight, overweight patients experience greater pressure on the surface of their chest, abdomen, and hips. Overweight can reduce elastic fibers in the dermis, decreasing tolerance to pressure and shear loads, and thereby increasing the risk of pressure sores. When the posture is fixed, the blood circulation in the compressed areas of patients with high BMI values may be obstructed, leading to tissue hypoxia and damage [21]. In addition, Ni et al. investigated the effects of body weight on oxygenation and intra-abdominal pressure in 37 patients undergoing prone position lumbar spine surgery. The results showed that during prone position, the oxygenation index was significantly negatively correlated with BMI, and intra-abdominal pressure was significantly positively correlated with BMI [22].The prone position can compress the abdomen of overweight patients, potentially affecting normal respiratory function and increasing the risk of dyspnea during and after surgery [23]. Saiwai et al. presented a case of brachial plexus injury caused by muscular hypertrophy in a professional sumo wrestler during prone position surgery [24]. Du et al. retrospectively compared the safety of 466 patients undergoing cervical laminoplasty in the lateral and prone positions. The results showed that in the obese subgroup, the position time, surgical time, and blood loss were significantly shorter in the lateral group than in the prone group [25]. The risks faced by overweight patients during prone position surgery are complex, including pressure sores, obstructed blood circulation, tissue hypoxia, dyspnea, and possible nerve injuries [26]. Therefore, for these patients, medical staff should fully consider the patient’s BMI value when planning and performing surgery, and adopt appropriate preventive measures and surgical positions to reduce surgical risks and ensure patient safety.

Reducing and redistributing pressure on the skin of compressed areas during surgery is a core element of IAPI prevention strategies, with pressure relief and increased blood flow being two crucial aspects [27]. The current preventive measures for prone position IAPIT mainly focus on nursing care, increasing support surface, local skin protection and so on. Using customized silicone pads [28], dressings and polyurethane gel pads [10] etc. to reduce and relieve pressure, but its types are various, the use effect is still a big controversy [29]. A previous study reported the effectiveness of using soft silicone multi-layer foam dressing in preventing pressure injuries in patients undergoing spinal surgery in a Tokyo general hospital, where soft silicone multi-layer foam dressing was applied to the left area (iliac region and chest), while polyurethane film dressing was applied to the right area (iliac region and chest). The results showed that the incidence of acquired pressure injury during operation was significantly higher with polyurethane film dressing than with soft silicone multi-layer foam dressing. Therefore, the clinical effect of using soft silicone, multi-layer foam dressing to prevent pressure injury has been proved [9]. However, these methods can only softly cover the skin to reduce friction, but they cannot completely distribute pressure, especially in patients with significant weight. Its effect is limited. Some dressings have poor breathability and may lead to skin moisture, increasing the risk of infection, especially in prolonged operations. And some dressings may deform under long-term pressure, losing their original protective effect [30]. Preventive measures taken in clinics vary widely, and the results obtained from studies are also different, so it still needs multicenter, large sample, prospective research to verify.

Clinical practice emphasizes the prevention principles of acquiring pressure injury during operation, which is to reduce the pressure on bone protrusions and redistribute the pressure. Spinal posterior surgery mainly adopts prone position, especially for patients who need general anesthesia. This posture is chosen to optimize the surgical field while considering various aspects of anesthesia. This process requires coordinated operations between surgeons, anesthesiologists, and nurses to ensure that rough methods such as pushing, pulling, dragging, or throwing do not cause harm when positioning the patient. In addition, it is necessary to have a deep understanding of the physiological changes that may occur due to body position changes. During the operation, continuous dynamic monitoring of the patient’s condition should be carried out in time to adjust the parts of the body that are under pressure (such as the head), so as to reduce the risk of long-term compression. In this study, after the patient lies down on his back, he rolls over to a prone position before being placed in a prone position during surgery. The preoperative adjustment was made by adjusting the patient’s position with the consent of the surgical team. A micro-motion scheme was established in the standard operating day procedure, where the patient was rolled to a prone position lying flat, then positioned on the surgical bed. During the operation, communication with the surgical team was conducted in advance, and the tilt function of the surgical bed was used to adjust the patient’s position every two hours. The angle of inclination was set at 15 degrees, and decompression was performed for 5 min to ensure that the patient’s position was changed regularly. Micro-movement program through timely adjustment of the tilt angle of the operating table to change the continuous action of the patient’s weight on a single pressure point, effectively dispersing the pressure caused by prolonged fixation of the body position, thus reducing the risk of skin and deep tissue injury. Surgical procedures using tools may increase local friction and shear force, micro-movement program by changing positions, help distribute these forces concentrated, improve local blood circulation, promote oxygen and nutrient supply, optimize tissue oxygenation.

However, in the study, the exploration of the impact of micro-mobility solutions on pressure injuries has not yet incorporated objective indicators as evaluation bases. Serotonin is a key neurotransmitter that participates in regulating various physiological processes, including mood regulation, pain perception, sleep, and appetite, playing a crucial role in pain modulation and perception. Existing research indicates that fluctuations in serotonin levels can affect pain sensitivity and analgesic responses [31]. For future research directions, we will further explore the specific impact of micro-mobility on serotonin levels, with the aim of providing more comprehensive and robust evidence to support the prevention and treatment of pressure injuries.

Additionally, the work of repositioning is primarily undertaken by nursing staff, and the workload of operating room nurses is often quite heavy. The repositioning every two hours further increases their work intensity, and there is a need for a more in-depth investigation into nurse satisfaction. At the same time, nursing staff’s understanding of the pathological knowledge related to IAPI is still insufficient, and there is a lack of full recognition of the important role of repositioning in preventing IAPI. Given that IAPI are a significant risk factor in the medical process, it is particularly important to provide professional training courses for nursing staff to enhance their professional level, accurately grasp the dynamic changes in patient conditions, and thus more effectively prevent the occurrence of IAPI.

Conclusion

This study aims to explore the application effects of “micro-movement” scheme in preventing Intraoperative Acquired Pressure Injuries (IAPIs) among overweight patients undergoing posterior lumbar surgery. Through comparative analysis, we found that the incidence of related pressure injuries was reduced in patients who adopted the"micro-movement” scheme compared to those in the silicone foam group. Despite this, there are still some limitations in the design and implementation of this study. Firstly, the results are primarily based on data from overweight patients, and further in-depth research and validation are needed to assess the applicability of the “micro-movement” scheme in patients with normal weight or underweight, as well as its effectiveness in other types of surgeries. Secondly, the sample size of this study is relatively small, which may limit the broad applicability of the findings and the robustness of statistical inferences. Furthermore, this study has not yet incorporated objective indicators. Given these points, future research should consider increasing the sample size to enhance the universality and persuasiveness of the study conclusions.
