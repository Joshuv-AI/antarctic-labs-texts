‘In Texas, everybody wants antibiotics’: reducing inappropriate antibiotic expectations and use with a provider–patient communication tool in primary care
Authors: Ashley Collazo, Paige Wermuth, Johanan Luna Rodriguez, Kiara Olmeda, Azalia Mancera, Fabrizia Faustinella, Michael K Paasche-Orlow, Roger Zoorob, Barbara Wells Trautner, Larissa Grigoryan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/objectivePatients often expect antibiotics for self-limiting diseases, pressuring providers to prescribe antibiotics unnecessarily. These expectations also contribute to the unsafe practice of taking antibiotics without a prescription (non-prescription use), such as pills retained from prior prescriptions or antibiotics from non-medical sources. Previous work shows that non-prescription use is due to strong, widely held misconceptions regarding the curative power of antibiotics. To reduce unnecessary use of antibiotics, we developed and pilot-tested a patient-focused, bilingual (English and Spanish) educational tool with patient and provider stakeholder input. The tool, a trifold brochure, included information on safe antibiotic use, potential antibiotic harms and symptom management with over-the-counter medications.DesignUsing a qualitative design, we conducted a two-phase study to (1) develop a provider–patient communication tool and (2) pilot-test the tool in primary care clinics. Development of the tool involved patient advisory board meetings and healthcare professional (HCP) focus groups. Pilot-testing of the tool was done through semistructured interviews of randomly recruited patients from primary clinic waiting rooms and their providers.SettingPublicly funded safety net primary care clinics in Texas.ParticipantsPatients (n=18) and HCPs (nurses, medical assistants, pharmacists, nurse practitioners and physicians) (n=14) from participating clinics.ResultThemes were extracted from the qualitative data. Main themes from the development phase highlighted the need to create a simple tool to make it clear that antibiotics are not used to treat viral infections, pain or allergies and that using antibiotics without consulting a medical professional is not safe. During pilot-testing, providers noted the tool helped adjust patients’ antibiotic expectations. Providers felt that the tool gave them credibility in scenarios where antibiotics were not indicated. Patients felt that the tool provided alternatives to antibiotics for symptom relief. Patients and providers found the tool useful in supporting patient–provider communication around antibiotic use.ConclusionsA stakeholder-driven, patient-focused educational tool addressing inappropriate antibiotic use facilitated patient–provider communication around antibiotic usage and helped manage patients’ antibiotic expectations. Embedding this tool into a community-facing intervention may reduce use of antibiotics without a prescription.

Introduction

Each year in the USA, at least 2.8 million people are infected with antibiotic-resistant bacteria, and at least 35 000 people die as a complication of antibiotic-resistant infections.1 The incidence of antimicrobial-resistant (AMR) infections continues to rise globally.1 2 At the current rate, AMR could result in US$1 trillion of additional healthcare costs per year by 2050, and treating drug-resistant bacterial infections alone could cost up to US$412 billion annually.2 Global life expectancy is also expected to decrease by 1.8 years due to AMR by 2035.2 Despite national and global efforts to curb antibiotic usage, in 2023, there were 252 million antibiotics prescribed in the USA.3 Indeed, 30%–40% of the antibiotics prescribed in outpatient settings were inappropriate.4 5

Often, the responsibility of inappropriate outpatient antibiotic prescribing is placed on the prescriber; however, many studies have found that patient expectation and demand are large contributors to unnecessary antibiotic prescribing.6 7 Patients frequently expect antibiotics for viral illnesses8 due to widely held misconceptions among patients that antibiotics are effective against symptoms caused by viruses.810 These expectations and misconceptions can create confrontational clinic encounters between providers and patients, where patients leave dissatisfied with their care if an antibiotic is not prescribed.6 7 In value-based care contracts, patient satisfaction scores directly impact provider reimbursement for clinic encounters in primary care settings,11 potentially putting primary care providers in a lose-lose situation when trying to prescribe antibiotics appropriately.

Patient antibiotic expectation can also perpetuate the practice of using antibiotics without a prescription from a medical professional, that is, non-prescription use. Non-prescription users obtain antibiotics from local markets or ethnic stores, other countries, friends and relatives, or previously prescribed courses (ie, improperly retained ‘leftover’ antibiotics).12 13 Non-prescription antibiotic use is prevalent in the USA, with recent studies finding that 20%–45% of the population has used antibiotics without a prescription.1215 Studies exploring reasons for non-prescription use found gaps in knowledge about appropriate antibiotic use and strong misconceptions regarding the benefits of antibiotics contribute to this unsafe practice.1618 For example, people who believe that antibiotics are effective and valuable—‘like gold’—to relieve non-infectious symptoms such as pain and allergies are much more likely to use antibiotics without a prescription.16 Additionally, those who were unaware of the risks of antibiotic use were more likely to expect to receive antibiotics for diarrhoea and cold/influenza symptoms.8

A recent systematic review showed that patient education initiatives in primary care are effective tools to reduce antibiotic prescribing and patient expectation for antibiotics.6 Approaches to educate patients included displaying educational posters on antibiotics, providing written information on antibiotic use and viral symptom management, as well as open patient–provider discussion about appropriate use of antibiotics.6 7 However, to our knowledge, there is not an educational tool addressing the practice of non-prescription antibiotic use. To address this gap and to reduce unnecessary use of antibiotics in our primary care clinics, we developed a bilingual (English and Spanish) tool focused on educating patients about appropriate antibiotic use. This tool, a written brochure, was created to directly address antibiotic knowledge gaps and beliefs found in our previous work.81214 16 As an example, non-prescription users often believed over-the-counter (OTC) medications ‘did not work’.16 However, we do not know which specific OTC medications were thought to be ineffective and if these medications were appropriately used to treat specific symptoms. Thus, we ensured our tool provided OTC medication recommendations based on specific common symptoms. Our educational tool was iteratively developed with patient and provider stakeholder input and then pilot-tested in two primary care clinics for feasibility, acceptability and communication impact.

Methods

This study is the culmination of a 5-year project to reduce non-prescription antibiotic use among patients in safety net clinics in Houston, TX. Prior stages of this study included: (1) a quantitative survey with English-speaking and Spanish-speaking patients to study the relationship between patient, clinical encounter, and healthcare system factors and non-prescription use using the Kilbourne Framework of Health Disparities research19 and (2) qualitative interviews to explore the perspectives and experiences with non-prescription use among survey respondents who used antibiotics without a prescription. The quantitative and qualitative results from these prior phases informed this study and the subsequent creation of our communication tool.

Setting

Our study was conducted in the Harris Health System (HHS) safety-net clinics. HHS safety net clinics deliver care to residents of Harris County (Houston and surrounding areas) who are low income or uninsured. To receive care at HHS, patients qualify for the county financial assistance programme (household income at or below 150% of the federal poverty level) or pay for services based on a sliding scale payment structure. The two clinics participating in pilot-testing include a continuity care clinic and a walk-in (same-day) clinic. The continuity clinic provides care for a largely Black patient population, while the same-day clinic is largely used by a Spanish-speaking Hispanic population. We included a same-day clinic, as patients seen in same-day clinics may not have an assigned primary care provider, putting them at high risk for non-prescription use due to limited access to healthcare.

Study phases

We conducted our study in two stages: tool development and pilot-testing. The development phase engaged patients and healthcare professionals (HCPs) to design a communication tool (brochure) promoting safe antibiotic use motivated by results of our survey and qualitative interviews. Patient advisory board (PAB) discussions were used to identify educational priorities. With this input, two draft brochures were reviewed and refined through focus groups with HCPs. The pilot phase evaluated the brochure for feasibility, acceptability and communication impact through semistructured interviews with patients and providers in two primary care clinics. Focus groups and interviews followed guides (online supplemental file A and B) developed by research team members (LG, BWT, ML-A, MKP-O and PC) with expertise in antibiotic stewardship, qualitative research, health literacy and Hispanic cultural and linguistic competency. All focus groups and interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim, after obtaining written consent.

Phase 1: development

The development phase included a two-step participatory process for patient and HCP input (table 1). Recruitment of PAB members was completed with help from the HHS Patient Council at Large. The HHS Patient Council is comprised of motivated patients who are interested in improving patient care by regularly providing feedback on research studies conducted in the clinics of HHS. PAB members included adult residents of Harris County who could speak English or Spanish. HCPs were recruited through clinic director recommendation.

Phase 2: pilot-testing of the tool

In the second phase, we pilot-tested the tool in two primary care clinics from September 2023 to November 2023. Patients were randomly selected in clinic waiting areas, given the brochure before the visit and encouraged to discuss the brochure with their providers. Providers were recruited from a staff meeting explaining the piloting process and introducing the educational tool. Semistructured interviews were done with patients and providers (table 2). Data collection continued until saturation was reached, and no new concepts were reported.

Positionality

The research team brought varied disciplinary backgrounds and cultural perspectives to the project, including expertise in public health, medicine, health communication, behavioural science and qualitative methods. Interviewers were female and experienced in conducting interviews in clinical and community settings. The analysis was led by an expert in qualitative research who has a doctoral degree in behavioural science. (PW) KO, a bilingual, master’s-trained research coordinator, conducted patient interviews. None of the interviewers or coders had prior relationships with the participants. Data were analysed by three coders (PW, AM and KO). The coding team held regular reflexive discussions to consider how our disciplinary backgrounds and clinical/public health experiences might shape interpretation. These discussions allowed us to compare perspectives, challenge assumptions and ensure that emerging themes were grounded in the data rather than individual researcher biases. Preliminary themes and analytic decisions were then brought back to the broader research/author team in weekly meetings for further discussion and refinement.

Analysis

Transcripts from each phase were analysed using thematic analysis. Following Braun and Clarke’s framework, we generated initial themes by reviewing coded data extracts and identifying patterns across the dataset.20 Coding used predefined (deductive) codes based on prior research and the study’s aims, followed by inductive coding to capture emergent concepts. This hybrid approach balanced structured inquiry with responsiveness to participant perspectives.21 The team reviewed, adjudicated code disagreements and refined early codes through regular meetings, resulting in a codebook with clearly defined categories. Codes and themes were iteratively refined through team discussion to ensure they were coherent, analytically meaningful and grounded in the data (online supplemental file C). Analysis was conducted with Atlas.ti (V.25).22 Methodological rigour was enhanced through triangulation, including multiple data sources (PAB members, provider focus groups, and patient and provider interviews) and multiple coders engaged in the analysis. Although we did not use formal analytic memos, we documented coding decisions and theme refinements during team meetings. Quotations presented in our results are verbatim with minor grammatical corrections for clarity.

Patient and public involvement

Results

Participants

In both phases, participants included patients (n=18) and HCPs (n=14). For the pilot phase, 12 out of 19 patients approached in clinic waiting rooms agreed to participate. Phase 1: development

Three main themes informed the development of the educational tool: (1) what to say, (2) how to say it and (3) where to say it. Themes reflected the perspectives of both patients and HCPs on educational content, delivery strategies and the best design to support effective communication.

What to say: content priorities for safe antibiotic use

Participants identified educational priorities for tool content which included addressing antibiotic misconceptions, the use of leftover/shared antibiotics and the management of antibiotic expectations. Patients and HCPs identified persistent misconceptions about when antibiotics are necessary, especially for viral illnesses.

It was a myth that I had heard for many years in friends trying to help… and that was… “You’ve got a cold. I need to give you some of my antibiotics”, and later I found out that that’s not what antibiotics treat. (PAB member)

Participants also described misunderstandings about stopping antibiotics early and the common practice of retaining or sharing unused antibiotics from prior prescriptions.

I know I have a lot of leftover antibiotics, and it is tempting to take those leftover antibiotics. (PAB member)

Participants emphasised the need for the tool to provide clear information and messaging to discourage this behaviour.

If you are prescribed antibiotics, you need to know that you need to complete the course of antibiotics, you need to know what a reaction is, when you don't need to, when you can stop taking it, when to seek help… this [the brochure] can reinforce the education that they're supposed to get anyway. (HCP)

HCPs discussed how prior experiences shaped expectations around receiving antibiotics. HCPs were believed to contribute to patient expectations by using antibiotic prescriptions to save time during an appointment or avoid uncomfortable conversations about antibiotic use.

A lot of providers feel pressured to give antibiotics because patients expect it. It’s easier to give it than spend ten minutes explaining why they don’t need it. (HCP)

How to say it: designing messages for clarity and engagement

Participants emphasised that the tool’s effectiveness would depend on how messages were communicated. They stressed the need for clear, concise language and appreciated a simple layout that would encourage patients to read and retain important information.

The brochure has to be easy to understand. (PAB member)

It’s well done… written in a simple way that allows people to understand. It’s not too full or crowded, which is important because patients can get turned off by having too much to read. (HCP)

Where to say it: integrating education into clinical and everyday settings

Both patients and HCPs emphasised the importance of consistent, accessible distribution while acknowledging the realities of time constraints and competing demands in busy clinical settings. HCPs highlighted the difficulty of consistently distributing educational materials in busy clinic environments without systematic integration into workflows.

I think they could incorporate it into the visit. I don't think it would be a hindrance because there’s a benefit to it for everybody. (HCP)

Patients emphasised the value of having access to information not just during appointments, but before and after. One patient suggested:

It would be helpful to have a brochure in person, something you can keep in your car. Then if you go to the pharmacy, you could look at it and say, okay, I need to find this. (PAB member)

Phase 2: pilot

Following tool development, we conducted a pilot evaluation in two primary care clinics to explore how the brochure functioned in real-world settings. Thematic analysis of the interviews revealed three main themes: (1) integrating the tool into clinical workflows, (2) using the tool to support patient–provider communication and (3) reframing expectations about antibiotic use.

Integrating the tool into clinical workflows

Participants described the day-to-day use of the tool during clinical encounters. Providers emphasised that the tool could be easily incorporated into existing workflows and even save time during patient education.

This is not going to take more of your time; to the contrary, it may take less of your time if you have this brochure available, and you can hand it to the patient. (Same-Day Provider)

The tool was also used during the visit in response to a patient’s specific situation. One shared:

I was asking, “What have you done to treat your symptoms?” They mentioned, “Well, I’ve been taking this antibiotic that I got from out of town.” That’s when I started explaining, “Well, you know, there can be some issues with that.” And then they brought up, “Like resistance?” And I was like, “Oh! Okay, yeah.” (Same-Day Provider)

Using the tool to support patient–provider communication

The tool acted as a communication bridge between patients and providers supporting antibiotic education and encouraging patients to ask questions. Patients and providers reported that the tool prompted them to ask new questions during their visits, enhancing shared understanding.

I asked her about it [the brochure]. I just wanted to know more. She told me they can be dangerous—don’t take it off the street or anything. I learned a lot about how to take them, when to take them, and when not to. See, now I know. (Continuity clinic Patient)

Patients consistently discussed the tool’s value beyond their current medical appointment. They intended to keep the tool for future reference and share it with family, friends and others in their communities. The tool has the potential to extend education and influence health behaviours well beyond the original clinical encounter.

Yeah, it could be useful to me because we don’t know what’s going to happen in the future. Something could come up, and I might need antibiotics, or a family member could get sick, and yeah, you could use [the brochure] with them. If something happens, you can say, ‘Oh, I’ve got that brochure—let me show you what you can do with this or what an antibiotic is for.’ Like, what you’re trying to teach me? I could teach somebody else. (Same-Day clinic Patient)

Reframing expectations about antibiotic use

Providers used the tool to support conversations when antibiotics were not indicated. Providers described the brochure as a tangible tool to clarify when antibiotics are unnecessary and to serve as a go-between in conversations that were often disappointing or uncomfortable. Providers felt that the tool legitimised their message and helped to shift misconceptions into opportunities for education and shared understanding.

It would be very helpful because the need for or no need for antibiotics is very often a topic of discussion with our patients. They come to us thinking they need antibiotics, and when we say no, you don't need an antibiotic, it’s a big problem. That’s an immediate source of concern because they think they're not going to get the treatment they need. So, it’s concern, it’s disappointment. (Continuity Provider)

Patients and providers noted that expectations around antibiotic use were shaped by cultural norms and prior healthcare experiences. Countries or healthcare systems where antibiotics are more readily available or frequently prescribed influenced patients’ perceptions of antibiotic prescriptions. For some patients, learning that antibiotics are not always needed represented a significant shift in understanding.

I'm from Mexico, and over there they really do give you antibiotics for everything… Here, they just say it needs to go away on its own. It’s very different. (Same-Day Patient)

Providers recognised these patterns and explained how cultural habits contributed to persistent expectations for antibiotics, even when not medically indicated.

In Texas, everyone wants antibiotics. Sometimes they go to Mexico and get antibiotics… They love antibiotics even if it’s a common cold. (Continuity Provider)

Discussion

Through patient and provider stakeholder input, we identified key features of a communication tool on appropriate antibiotic use. Clear, simple messaging is essential, particularly to address misconceptions that antibiotics can treat colds, allergies and pain and that use of antibiotics without consulting a medical professional is safe. Stakeholders also believed that the tool was best used in the clinic encounter to optimise patient engagement. The tool was found to be useful in supporting patient–provider communication around antibiotic use and helpful when managing patient antibiotic expectations, especially when a provider did not believe an antibiotic was indicated.

Several studies have evaluated the effectiveness of patient education tools on antibiotic prescribing. A pre–post survey in hospital inpatients showed posters highlighting the futility of antibiotic treatment for colds, harms associated with antibiotic usage and awareness of AMR decreased patient expectation to receive antibiotics.23 In a mixed-methods study, primary care providers found a viral illness prescription pad (which directed patients to OTC remedies) to be a useful tool to facilitate patient education about antibiotic usage in upper respiratory tract infections.24 A quasi-experimental study found the rates of antibiotic prescribing significantly decreased after implementation of an intervention combining patient and provider education on antibiotic use in respiratory tract infections.25 Within the paediatric populations, a Cochrane review found that providing written information about antibiotics for acute upper respiratory tract infections significantly reduced the number of antibiotics used by patients.26

Our brochure is unique from previous educational tools in that it addresses the issues of non-prescription antibiotic use. With this tool, providers felt they could more successfully manage expectations of patients who are at high risk for non-prescription use. Patients with experience of non-prescription use found the tool provided helpful information and alternatives to antibiotics when they or their family members got sick. Most importantly, this tool facilitated dialogue between patients at risk for non-prescription use and providers in a busy primary care clinic.

Despite the perceived utility of the educational tool, addressing the unsafe practice of non-prescription use is a multifaceted problem that will take more than a single educational tool. Our previous work shows that non-prescription users often rely on antibiotics from non-clinical sources due to health system and access-related issues, such as cost of doctor’s visit, long appointment wait times, lack of transportation to clinic visits and language barriers with their providers.12 16 27 Imanpour and McMaughan also noted that patients from countries with OTC access to antibiotics often retain cultural and community norms around antibiotic use that may conflict with US prescribing guidelines.28 These long-standing perceptions and beliefs around antibiotic use require a multipronged approach to addressing the practice of non-prescription use - patient and community education, improving healthcare access and culturally sensitive public health campaigns, which have been successful in reducing antibiotic use in European countries.17 18 29 30

Our study is qualitative in nature and, therefore, focused on exploring perceptions of the tool’s usefulness rather than testing its measurable impact. This design allowed us to capture in-depth perspectives. We did not assess the efficacy of the tool in the current study with quantitative methods; the impact of this educational tool in busy clinical practices will be the focus of future study. Similarly, we did not test the impact of implementation over time, which limits our ability to examine the pragmatic effectiveness of the tool to reduce inappropriate antibiotic use and if such effects can be maintained. We plan to address these questions with studies that include clinics outside our healthcare system, where non-prescription antibiotic use may be even more prevalent and opportunities for open discussion may be even more limited.

Conclusions

We created a stakeholder-driven, patient-focused tool addressing inappropriate antibiotic use. This tool facilitated patient–provider communication around appropriate antibiotic usage and was useful for clinicians to manage patient antibiotic expectations. Embedding this tool into a community-facing intervention may reduce use of antibiotics without a prescription.
