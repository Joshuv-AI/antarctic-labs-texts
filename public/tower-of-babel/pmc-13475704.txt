Targeting host lipogenesis with a diarylamide inhibitor disrupts SARS-CoV-2 replication
Authors: Xintian Zhang, Tingfu Du, Yongjian Wang, Wenhai Yu, Tanxiu Chen, Ruixue Liu, Yunpeng Liu, Ruimin Zhu, Doudou Xu, Li Li, Bin Yin, Shuaiyao Lu, Zhuorong Li, Xiaozhong Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe vulnerability of direct-acting antivirals to resistance has motivated interest in host-directed strategies that target conserved cellular pathways essential for viral replication. Here, we report a diarylamide molecule, compound 10 (compd. 10), which inhibits SARS-CoV-2 more potently than the parent clofoctol. Mechanistic studies revealed that it is associated with suppression of host lipogenesis, evidenced by downregulation of fatty acid synthase (FASN) and stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1), and partial reversal of antiviral activity upon fatty acid supplementation. Compd. 10 was found to bind to and promote degradation of nuclear receptor coactivator 1 (NCOA1, also known as SRC-1), which suppressed transcription of these lipogenic enzymes. In vivo, intranasal compd. 10 reduced pulmonary viral loads and attenuated lung histopathology in SARS-CoV-2-infected hamsters, confirming its antiviral efficacy. These findings identify compd. 10 as a promising antiviral candidate that disrupts SARS-CoV-2 replication by targeting lipogenesis, providing a chemotype for host-directed antiviral development.

Introduction

Positive-sense RNA viruses, including coronaviruses, flaviviruses, and enteroviruses, remodel host intracellular membranes to establish replication organelles (ROs) that concentrate viral replication machinery and shield viral RNA from host immune recognition.1,2,3 A defining feature of coronavirus replication is the formation of specialized ROs derived from endoplasmic reticulum membranes.4,5 In the case of SARS-CoV-2, within these membrane-bound compartments, the replication-transcription complex (RTC), organized around RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), synthesizes complementary RNA strands from the positive-sense genome, producing double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) intermediates.6,7 Because dsRNA can be sensed by host pattern recognition receptors and trigger antiviral responses, coronaviruses primarily sequester dsRNA within virus-induced double-membrane vesicles (DMVs).8,9,10 These vesicles are the main components of viral ROs and provide a protected microenvironment for efficient genome replication and transcription.11,12,13

The biogenesis and stability of DMV critically depend on host lipid composition, and previous studies have demonstrated that SARS-CoV-2 actively remodels host lipid metabolic pathways to support viral replication organelle formation.11,13,14 Conversely, disruption of de novo fatty acid biosynthesis impairs viral replication. Pharmacological inhibition of fatty acid synthase (FASN) activity by orlistat has been reported to suppress SARS-CoV-2 replication,14,15,16 consistent with the idea that insufficient lipid supply compromises the morphogenesis and integrity of viral replication compartments.

Current direct-acting antivirals for COVID-19 primarily target viral enzymes essential for genome replication and processing, including the RdRp, inhibited by molnupiravir and azvudine, and the 3-chymotrypsin-like protease (3CLpro), targeted by nirmatrelvir/ritonavir (Paxlovid).17,18,19 However, their vulnerability to resistance has driven the search for alternative host-directed antiviral strategies.20,21 Host-directed antivirals offer broader applicability across viral families and a higher barrier to resistance, which makes them attractive candidates for pandemic preparedness against emerging viral threats.

One such host-directed candidate emerged from our optimization of clofoctol, an antibiotic with reported anti-SARS-CoV-2 activity via translation inhibition,22,23 which we previously found to modulate host transcription.24 To improve its selectivity and explore host factors involved in SARS-CoV-2 replication, we further modified the clofoctol scaffold. Here, we report an optimized clofoctol derivative, compound 10 (compd. 10), with potent antiviral activity in cell-based assays and in vivo models. As the mechanism of action, we show that compd. 10 engages nuclear receptor coactivator 1 (NCOA1), promotes its degradation, and is associated with decreased stability of its partner peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPARγ), leading to suppression of lipogenic genes including FASN and stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1).

Results

Identification of a clofoctol-derived diarylamide that suppresses SARS-CoV-2 replication

Building on the reported anti-SARS-CoV-2 activity of clofoctol, we synthesized a panel of clofoctol-derived diaryl molecules and compared their antiviral properties in Vero cells (Figures S1, S2, and Table S1). Of these, compd. 10 emerged as the most promising candidate and was selected for further evaluation (Figure 1A). In cell-based assays, compd. 10 potently inhibited replication of both the prototypic and BA.1 variants, with half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) values of 0.9 and 0.5 μM, respectively, and a 50% cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of 44.1 μM in Vero cells. This resulted in a markedly higher selectivity index (SI = CC50/EC50) than clofoctol under identical conditions (Figure 1B). This superior antiviral profile of compd. 10 was consistently observed across additional SARS-CoV-2 VOCs and was accompanied by reduced cytotoxicity in multiple cell lines compared with clofoctol (Table S2). Western blot analysis confirmed a dose-dependent decrease in nucleocapsid (N) protein expression with complete inhibition at 8 μM for compd. 10, whereas clofoctol at the same concentration failed to achieve this effect (Figures 1C and 1D). To further delineate the antiviral mechanism, a time-of-addition assay was performed (Figures 1E and 1F). The schematic of the experimental design is shown in Figure S3. To investigate whether the enhanced antiviral activity of compd. 10 stemmed from translation inhibition, a known mechanism of clofoctol, we first compared their translational inhibitory effects. As shown in Figure S4, clofoctol exhibited superior inhibition of viral translation, although compd. 10 showed greater overall antiviral activity. This discrepancy suggests that the enhanced antiviral activity of compd. 10 is not primarily mediated by translation inhibition, leading us to consider its effect on viral replication. Importantly, compd. 10 significantly reduced both genomic N (gN) and subgenomic E (sgE) RNA levels at 24 and 48 h post-infection (Figures 1G and 1H). Notably, at 8 μM, compd. 10 already markedly suppressed sgE expression as early as 24 hpi, further confirming that compd. 10 inhibits viral RNA replication. Consistent with this observation, compd. 10 also suppressed viral double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) accumulation (Figure 1I). Given the role of ROs in viral RNA synthesis, we employed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to assess the impact of compd. 10 on replication organelle morphology. Representative TEM images showed substantial morphological disruption of ROs following compd. 10 exposure. As shown in Figure 1J, aberrant ROs were observed upon compd. 10 treatment. These aberrant ROs were morphologically characterized as structures exhibiting partial double-membrane features, often associated with DMVs or convoluted membranes (CMs), but they failed to form properly coiled ROs. Quantitative analysis of DMV frequency distribution revealed that compd. 10 altered the number and distribution of DMVs within ROs (Figure 1K). Together, these results demonstrate that compd. 10 suppresses SARS-CoV-2 replication by inhibiting viral RNA replication and disrupting the architecture of ROs.

Compound 10 suppresses expression of FASN and SCD1

Given that compd. 10 markedly suppressed viral replication, we next investigated whether these antiviral effects were associated with perturbations in host pathways that support the formation and integrity of viral ROs. The membrane fluidity and stability of coronavirus-induced DMVs critically depend on host lipid composition, and SARS-CoV-2 actively rewires cellular lipid metabolism to facilitate efficient viral replication.14 Considering the essential role of host lipid metabolic remodeling in DMV formation, we examined whether compd. 10 modulates lipid metabolic pathways to exert its antiviral activity.

RNA sequencing and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis identified lipid metabolism as a top enriched pathway upon compd. 10 treatment (Figure S5). Consistently, protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis of 60 lipid metabolism-associated genes revealed FASN, a key enzyme governing de novo lipogenesis, as the central hub node (Figure 2A). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed pronounced inhibition of fatty acid metabolism by compd. 10 (Figure 2B). These transcriptomic findings were validated by quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR) in both Huh-7 and Caco-2 cells, which showed downregulation of fatty acid metabolism-related genes in both uninfected cells (Figure S6) and cells infected with prototypic SARS-CoV-2 (Figures 2C and 2D). Notably, FASN and SCD1 were selected for further mechanistic investigation due to their dual roles in regulating fatty acid biosynthesis and supporting viral replication organelle formation.14,15,16,25,26,27 Consistent with the transcriptional data, western blot analysis showed a dose-dependent reduction in FASN and SCD1 protein levels following compd. 10 treatment (Figures 2E and 2F). We next examined whether FASN or SCD1 depletion affects the morphology of viral ROs by TEM (Figure 3F). Knockdown of either gene induced clear morphological alterations, albeit with distinct phenotypic outcomes. FASN knockdown primarily reduced the number of DMVs, whereas SCD1 knockdown shifted the diameter distribution of DMVs toward larger sizes (Figure 3G). These observations suggest that saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids may play differential roles in DMV biogenesis: FASN-derived saturated fatty acids may be critical for DMV formation, while SCD1-derived monounsaturated fatty acids may be more important for maintaining proper membrane architecture.

To test whether the antiviral activity of compd. 10 is specifically mediated through free fatty acid depletion, we performed fatty acid supplementation assays under fatty acid-depleted conditions. Under these conditions, the antiviral effect of compd. 10 (8 μM) was markedly attenuated, reducing viral loads by only 0.3 log10 compared to the 2.5 log10 reduction observed in normal culture medium (Figures 3H and S10A). Intracellular assay results further confirm that fatty acid deprivation attenuates the antiviral potency of compd. 10 (Figure S10B). Exogenous supplementation with palmitic acid (PA) or oleic acid (OA) partially reversed the antiviral activity of compd. 10, eliminating the statistical difference between treatment groups. A similar trend was observed with arachidonic acid (ARA) supplementation. These findings support that compd. 10 inhibits viral replication primarily through interference with free fatty acid synthesis.

Collectively, these results indicate that inhibition of FASN and SCD1 represents a critical node underlying the antiviral activity of compd. 10. However, the observed transcriptional suppression of fatty acid synthesis genes points to a broader regulatory effect, possibly at the level of lipid metabolic regulatory transcription factors. We therefore speculate that compd. 10 may target such upstream regulators, thereby broadly suppressing the transcriptional program of saturated and unsaturated fatty acid synthesis.

Compound 10 targets NCOA1 and destabilizes PPARγ to suppress host lipid synthesis

To identify the molecular target through which compd. 10 inhibits host fatty acid synthesis, we performed drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) coupled with LC-MS/MS. Proteins differentially expressed upon compd. 10 treatment were subjected to PPI network analysis (Figure S11 and Table S3). Among candidates, NCOA1, a transcriptional coactivator that interacts via LXXLL motifs with nuclear receptors (e.g., PPARs, liver X receptors) to regulate metabolic genes, emerged prominently.28,29,30,31 Western blot analysis confirmed that compd. 10 significantly enhanced NCOA1 protease resistance in DARTS (Figure 4A). Consistently, cellular thermal shift assays (CETSAs) demonstrated that compd. 10 increased the thermal stability of endogenous NCOA1, which supported a direct interaction between the compound and the protein (Figure S12). To further characterize the interaction of compd. 10 with NCOA1, molecular docking analysis was conducted using the co-crystal structure of NCOA1 (PDB: 5NWX). Simulations predicted that compd. 10 interacts with residues D276, T277, and R281 of NCOA1 (Figure S13). To validate these predictions, we introduced alanine substitutions at D276, T277, and R281 into flag-tagged NCOA1 and expressed the mutants in HEK293T cells for CETSA. Having identified NCOA1 as a direct binding target of compd. 10, we next examined the functional consequences. Western blot analysis revealed that compd. 10 treatment resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in NCOA1 protein levels (Figures 4D and 4E). To determine if this reduction resulted from post-translational regulation, we performed cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays to monitor NCOA1 turnover. With CHX, NCOA1 decayed significantly faster in compd. 10-treated cells than in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) control cells (Figures 4F and 4G). In addition, a modest 10% reduction in cell viability was observed at 48 h in the compd. 10 group (Figure S14). Given the key role of NCOA1 in coordinating nuclear receptor-regulated lipid metabolic gene expression, we next examined whether its degradation affects a key interacting partner, PPARγ, the master regulator of nuclear receptor-mediated lipid metabolism. CHX chase analysis revealed that compd. 10 markedly reduced PPARγ protein stability (Figures 4H and 4I), which indicated that PPARγ impairment may underlie compd. 10-mediated disruption of downstream metabolic genes. To delineate the degradation pathway responsible for NCOA1 loss, cells were treated with compd. 10 plus the lysosomal inhibitors 3-methyladenine (3-MA) and bafilomycin A1 (BafA1), or the proteasome inhibitor MG132. Among these, autophagy-lysosome inhibition rescued NCOA1 levels, whereas proteasome inhibition had no effect (Figures 4J and 4K), which indicated compd. 10 promotes lysosome-dependent NCOA1 degradation. Cell viability assays confirmed no additional cytotoxicity beyond the ∼10% reduction caused by compd. 10 and other inhibitors (Figure S15). Collectively, compd. 10 directly targets NCOA1, induces its autophagic degradation and PPARγ destabilization, thereby linking compound binding to host lipid metabolism suppression. Compound 10 suppresses viral replication and alleviates lung pathology in a Syrian hamster infection model

Given that compd. 10 suppresses SARS-CoV-2 replication by perturbing host lipid metabolism that is essential for RO formation, we next evaluated its antiviral activity in vivo. As shown in Figure 5A, hamsters were intranasally inoculated with BA.1 (5 × 104 TCID50) and administered compd. 10 (12 mg/kg) or vehicle once daily from 0 to 3 days post-infection (dpi). No significant changes in body weight were observed during the whole treatment period (Figure 5B). At 4 dpi, lung tissues were harvested for viral RNA extraction and histopathology. RT-qPCR showed that compd. 10 reduced lung viral RNA by approximately 4.2 log10 versus vehicle (Figure 5C). As shown in Figures 5D and 5E, histopathological evaluation showed that compd. 10 treatment mitigated SARS-CoV-2-induced lung injury, including alveolar septal thickening (black arrows), intraalveolar inflammatory cell infiltration (green arrows), and peribronchial/perivascular inflammatory cell infiltration (yellow arrows). The pathological score confirmed that compd. 10 significantly reduced the total pathological score compared to vehicle. Discussion

Here, we investigated clofoctol-derived diaryl molecules and identified compd. 10 as a potent SARS-CoV-2 inhibitor in vitro and in vivo. Unlike compounds 1–9, which retained the clofoctol diarylmethane scaffold, compd. 10 features a modified diarylamide chemotype, highlighting the multi-substituted diaryl scaffold as a promising antiviral platform. Through structural optimization, compd. 10 achieves a selectivity index >10-fold higher than clofoctol and demonstrates efficacy in both cellular and animal models. As a clofoctol derivative, compd. 10 exhibits weaker translation inhibition than the parent compound, which accounts for its reduced cytotoxicity but does not explain its stronger antiviral activity. However, we did not observe selective inhibition by clofoctol in our lipofection system, contrasting with electroporation reports. This discrepancy reveals that the selectivity of clofoctol may be fragile and condition-dependent. Notably, Wang et al.32 reported eIF2α phosphorylation at 1 h post-stimulation by clofoctol. Based on this observation, we speculate that this discrepancy may be explained as follows. In electroporation, mRNA enters the cytosol directly and translation initiates in a relatively stress-free environment, where eIF2α phosphorylation levels fall within a window that suppresses the viral untranslated region (UTR), but not the control UTR. In lipofection, endosomal retention of mRNA may independently elevate the eIF2α phosphorylation baseline, pushing the total level beyond the tolerance threshold of the control UTR, thereby abolishing selectivity. Importantly, despite its weaker translation inhibition, compd. 10 displays stronger antiviral activity. This uncoupling strongly suggests that compd. 10 acts through a translation-independent mechanism.

Mechanistically, in addition to exerting a modest inhibition of viral translation, compd. 10 binds to NCOA1 and promotes its autophagic degradation, a process that suppresses the lipogenic genes FASN and SCD1. We further identify that FASN and SCD1 are functionally required for SARS-CoV-2 replication. Notably, as key enzymes in de novo fatty acid synthesis, FASN and SCD1 are thought to support viral replication by localizing ROs or by modulating membrane fluidity through fatty acid production to facilitate RO formation. In line with this, our data demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition or genetic silencing of FASN/SCD1 suppresses viral RNA replication, as evidenced by sgRNA reduction, and alters DMV morphology. Additionally, several studies have also indicated that loss of function of FASN and SCD1 can disrupt structural alterations of assembled viral particles and interfere with viral budding,27,33,34 suggesting a broader role in the viral life cycle. Given the fundamental role of lipids in membrane fluidity and biogenesis, compounds that suppress fatty acid synthesis may similarly affect later stages of the viral life cycle, such as assembly and release. Notably, in our study, the efficiency of compd. 10 treatment and FASN knockdown in reducing extracellular viral genomes was consistently greater than their effects on DMV number or dsRNA fluorescence intensity. This observation suggests that compd. 10, as a host-targeting antiviral that suppresses lipogenesis, may also impact viral assembly and release. Nevertheless, our data demonstrate potent inhibition of viral RNA replication, as evidenced by a significant reduction in sgRNA levels (particularly sgE with 8 μM of compd. 10 at 24 hpi). This strong blockade at the replication level likely represents the primary mechanism of action. The observed reduction in extracellular viral genomes and the morphological changes in ROs likely result from both impaired replication and, potentially, a superimposed block in late-stage events. From a therapeutic perspective, this multi-stage inhibition may be advantageous, as it could render the virus less likely to escape through single mutations.

While our data suggest that PPARγ serves as an intermediate node linking NCOA1 to downstream lipogenic regulation, whether degradation of NCOA1 or PPARγ directly affects replication organelle formation remains to be determined. Nevertheless, NCOA1 emerges as an underappreciated and potentially druggable host factor that warrants further investigation in the development of host-directed antiviral strategies. Importantly, moderate perturbation of lipid metabolism elicits a pronounced antiviral effect, supporting lipid metabolic programs as feasible antiviral targets. However, systemic inhibition of a central regulatory factor such as NCOA1 may carry risks of on- or off-target effects,35,36 highlighting the need to balance antiviral efficacy with potential liabilities. Lung-targeted strategies such as intranasal administration of compd. 10 can maximize local activity while minimizing systemic exposure and risks.

However, while the specific rescue by exogenous PA and OA (the direct products of FASN and SCD1) supports a fatty acid-dependent mechanism, we cannot formally exclude the possibility that non-specific effects in bovine serum albumin fraction V (BSA-V) may partially contribute to the attenuated antiviral activity of compd. 10. While the fatty acid supplementation assay supports that compd.10 inhibits viral replication at least in part by targeting FASN/SCD1-mediated fatty acid synthesis, the gap between the antiviral potency of compd.10 and the modest effect of FASN/SCD1 siRNA knockdown suggests that additional targets may also contribute to its antiviral activity, which is consistent with the multi-target feature of clofoctol.24,32,37,38,39 Targeting host lipid metabolism is a compelling broad-spectrum antiviral strategy, effective against diverse viruses including SARS-CoV-2, influenza, and hepatitis C virus (HCV).14,15,16,40,41,42,43 Compd. 10 exemplifies this approach by disrupting DMV biogenesis and offers a high barrier to resistance, as host factors are less prone to mutation than viral targets. It is therefore well suited for combination therapy with direct-acting antivirals (such as paxlovid or molnupiravir) that target later stages of the viral life cycle, potentially achieving synergistic effects. Moreover, its intranasal efficacy supports its potential as a rapidly deployable candidate against emerging respiratory viral threats. Future dose-escalation and regimen optimization studies in animal models are warranted to fully evaluate the therapeutic potential of compd. 10.

Limitations of the study

Although our data strongly support the NCOA1-lipogenesis axis as the central mechanism underlying the antiviral activity of compd. 10, the possibility of additional contributing targets cannot be completely excluded. The direct interaction between compd. 10 and NCOA1 has not been confirmed by biophysical methods such as surface plasmon resonance (SPR), bio-layer interferometry (BLI), or isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). Although sgRNA and dsRNA data demonstrate that FASN and SCD1 are closely associated with viral replication, and TEM revealed corresponding morphological changes in DMVs, the causal relationship between FASN/SCD1 knockdown and DMV alterations has not been validated in a replication-independent system (e.g., nsp3-nsp4 expression). The absence of direct viral titration limits the ability to quantify the extent to which compd. 10 reduces infectious virus production. Only female hamsters were used in this study due to animal availability, and sex-related differences were not examined.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cells lines and cell culture

The human-derived cell lines Huh-7, Caco-2, H1299, HEK293T, and CP-H209 were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China). The African green monkey kidney-derived Vero cells were obtained from Kunming Cell Bank (Kunming, China). For the human-derived cell lines, genomic DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen, GDP304-03). Short tandem repeat (STR) analysis was performed using a 5-dye direct-amplification STR kit (Microread, 10403003). The STR profiles were compared against the Cellosaurus database. All human cell lines were confirmed to match their respective reference profiles, and no cross-contamination was detected. For Vero cells, STR analysis of eight loci confirmed their monkey origin, with no evidence of cross-contamination with other cell lines. All cell lines passed routine mycoplasma testing. These cells were cultured in basal media supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, 10099141C) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15140-122). Vero, Huh-7, and HEK293T in DMEM (Gibco, C11995500BT); Caco-2 in MEM (Gibco, C11095500BT) with 1% NEAA (Gibco, 11140050); and H1299 in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, C11875500BT). CP-H209 was cultured in Human Alveolar Type II Epithelial Cell Complete Medium (Procell, CM-H209). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Syrian hamster infection model

Animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Medical Biology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (DWSP202109002).

Specific-pathogen-free (SPF) outbred female Syrian golden hamsters, aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 100–120 g, were used in this study. The hamsters were obtained from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Liaoning, China), were drug-naive, and had no prior experimental experience. All animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups (vehicle or compd. 10), and then group-housed at 3 per cage under standard laboratory conditions with unrestricted access to food and water. All hamster infection studies were conducted in certified ABSL-3 (Animal Biosafety Level 3) facilities following institutional biosafety and ethical guidelines. Compd. 10 was formulated in a vehicle consisting of 5% sterile DMSO, 10% Kolliphor HS-15 (Sigma-Aldrich, 42966), and 85% normal saline containing 9% (w/v) SBE-β-CD (MCE, HY-17031). At 0 dpi, hamsters were intranasally administered vehicle or compd. 10 (12 mg/kg), followed 1 h later by intranasal inoculation with the SARS-CoV-2 BA.1 strain (5 × 104 TCID50, in 25 μL DMEM). Animals received daily treatments for four consecutive days and were euthanized at 4 dpi. Lungs were collected at necropsy for viral load quantification and histopathological assessment.

Method details

Antiviral activities in vitro

The prototype SARS-CoV-2 strain (GD108) and the Beta (B.1.351) variant were obtained from Guangdong Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC); the Alpha (B.1.1.7) from China CDC; the Delta (B.1.617.2) from Chongqing CDC; and the Omicron BA.1 (B.1.1.529) from the Institute of Laboratory Animal Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. All virus stocks were propagated in Vero cells, and viral titers were determined by 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assays. All work with live SARS-CoV-2 was conducted in certified BSL-3/4 facilities.

To evaluate the antiviral activities of the candidate compounds against SARS-CoV-2, Vero cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well and incubated overnight. The culture medium was then replaced with serum-free medium containing gradient concentrations of the compounds (0–20 μM), followed by infection with SARS-CoV-2 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.05. After incubation for 1 h, the viral inoculum was replaced with fresh medium containing the same concentrations of the compounds, supplemented with 2% FBS. Supernatants were collected at 48 h post-infection (hpi) for quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis. Viral RNA was quantified using the standard curve method with the TaqMan Fast Viral 1-Step Master Mix (Thermo Fisher, 4444432). The viral inhibition rate was calculated using the formula:Viralinhibitionrate(%)=(SQpositive−SQtest)/(SQpositive−SQblank)where SQ represents the starting quantity inferred from the standard curve, SQ positive represents the infected positive group, SQ test refers to the treatment group, and SQ blank represents the mock group.

CCK-8 assay

Cells were seeded at the same density as before (2 × 104 cells/well) one day prior to the experiment. After 48 h of treatment with varying concentrations of the compounds (0–100 μM), the supernatant in each well was replaced with 100 μL fresh medium supplemented with 10 μL of the CCK-8 solution (Dojindo, CK04). The plates were then incubated at 37°C for 1 h, and the optical density (OD) value was measured at 450 nm. The cytotoxicity was calculated using the formula:Cytotoxicrate(%)=(ODNC−ODtest)/(ODNC−ODblank)

The EC50 and CC50 values were derived from the nonlinear fit of transform curves using GraphPad Prism.

Time-of-addition assay

To identify the specific stage of the SARS-CoV-2 life cycle targeted by compd. 10, a time-of-addition assay was performed. Briefly, cells were infected at an MOI of 0.05 and incubated with compd. 10 at three distinct phases: full-time (throughout the infection cycle), entry (during viral adsorption and entry), and post-entry (after viral entry). For the entry phase, compd. 10 was added to the viral inoculum for 1 h, followed by removal of the inoculum and further incubation in fresh medium for 48 h. For the post-entry phase, cells were initially exposed to viral inoculum for 1 h, after which the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing compd. 10 and incubated for an additional 48 h. Cell lysates were then collected and analyzed for SARS-CoV-2 N expression using Western blot.

RNA extraction and real-time qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher, 15596018). Extracellular RNA and tissue RNA were isolated using the Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit (Genfine, Y502-K30) and the Direct-zol RNA MiniPrep Kit (Zymo Research, R2052), respectively, following the manufacturers’ protocols. For intracellular RNA, the extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Eastep RT Master Mix (Promega, LS2052), and the synthesized cDNA was then analyzed by qPCR on a CFX384 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) using the Eastep qPCR Master Mix (Promega, LS2068). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with normalization to GAPDH as an internal control. Primers and probes used in this study are listed in Table S4 and the key resources table.

Western blot

Stimulated cells were lysed on ice in lysis buffer supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 4693132001, 04906837001). The prepared protein was then boiled with 5×SDS loading buffer at 95°C for 10 min. The denatured samples were separated by SDS-PAGE gels (EpiZyme, PG211, PG212), transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, 1620177), and immunoblotted with the indicated antibodies. Protein bands were quantified using ImageJ. The band intensity of the target protein was normalized to its corresponding loading control and then calculated as the fold change compared to the control sample. The primary antibodies used for Western blotting in this study were the anti-SARS-CoV-2 N protein (Sino Biological, 40143-MM08, mouse, dilution 1:2000), anti-FASN (Proteintech, 10624-2-AP, rabbit, dilution 1:1000), anti-SCD1 (Zenbio, R25675, rabbit, dilution 1:1000), anti-NCOA1/SRC-1 (Santa Cruz, sc-32789, mouse, dilution 1:400), anti-Flag (Sigma-Aldrich, F3165, mouse, dilution 1:2500), anti-PPARγ (Proteintech, 16643-1-AP, rabbit, dilution 1:1000), HRP-conjugated anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, HRP-60004, dilution 1:10000), and HRP-conjugated anti-Beta Actin (Proteintech, HRP-60008, dilution 1:5000). The secondary antibodies used were Goat anti-mouse HRP IgG (Proteintech, SA00001-1) and Goat anti-rabbit HRP IgG (Proteintech, SA00001-2), both at a dilution of 1:5000.

RNA interference

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) specifically targeting FASN and SCD1 (designated as FASN-si and SCD1-si, respectively), along with a negative control siRNA (NC-si), were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). For transfection, cells were seeded in 12-well plates or 10-cm dishes and grown to approximately 80% confluence. Cells were then transfected with siRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Reagent (Thermo Fisher, 13778) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Specifically, cells in 12-well plates were transfected with 10 pmol siRNA per well for Western blot or RNA analysis, while cells in 10-cm dishes were transfected with 100 pmol siRNA per dish for TEM analysis. At 24 h post-transfection, cells were infected with SARS-CoV-2 at a MOI of 0.05. After 48 h of infection, cells were harvested for further analysis.

TEM

TEM is a technique that allows direct visualization of morphological changes in viral ROs. To observe the changes in ROs following compd. 10 treatment, Huh-7 cells were treated with DMSO or compd. 10 and infected with SARS-CoV-2 at a MOI of 0.05 for 48 h. Similarly, to determine the impact of FASN and SCD1 on ROs, cells were transfected with FASN-si and SCD1-si for 24 h, followed by infection with SARS-CoV-2 (MOI = 0.05) for 48 h. Infected cells were pelleted (300 × g, 8 min, 4°C) and then pre-fixed overnight at 4°C in electron microscopy fixative (Servicebio, G1102). Subsequent processing and imaging were completed by KingMed Diagnostics (Guangzhou, China). Briefly, the cell pellets were rinsed with 0.1 M phosphate buffer, and post-fixed in 1% OsO4 for 2 h, dehydrated in acetone, and embedded in Epon. Ultrathin sections (50–70 nm) were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and 3% lead citrate, then examined using a JEM-1400 (Jeol, Japan) transmission electron microscope. DMV diameters were measured using ImageJ. For frequency distribution analysis, a Gaussian fit was then applied to the diameter data using GraphPad Prism.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence staining of cells was performed as previously described.46 Briefly, Huh-7 cells were seeded onto 14-mm glass coverslips in 24-well plates and incubated overnight. For all infection experiments, cells were infected with SARS-CoV-2 at an MOI of 0.05 for 48 h. For knockdown experiments, cells were transfected with control siRNA, FASN-si, or SCD1-si for 24 h prior to infection. For compound treatment experiments, cells were infected in the presence or absence of compd. 10. After infection, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight. Following fixation, cells were washed three times with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were blocked with PBS containing 3% normal goat serum (Solarbio, SL038) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used for immunofluorescence staining in this study were anti-dsRNA clone rJ2 (Sigma-Aldrich, MABE1134, mouse, dilution 1:100), anti-FASN (Proteintech, 10624-2-AP, rabbit, dilution 1:100), and anti-SCD1 (Zenbio, R25675, rabbit, dilution 1:100). After incubation with primary antibodies (overnight at 4 °C) and subsequent washing, cells were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. The secondary antibodies used were goat anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher, A11008) and goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 594 (Thermo Fisher, A11005), both at a dilution of 1:500. Images were obtained on a Panoramic MIDI digital slide scanner (3DHISTECH, Hungary) and TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica, Germany), using 10× and 63× objectives, respectively.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Lung tissues were paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with H&E. Briefly, the H&E staining was performed by Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) following their established standard protocols. All histological images were captured using a digital slide scanner equipped with a 20× objective lens, and the assessment of pulmonary pathological changes was based on previously published methods.47

Plasmid construction

To assess the effect of compd. 10 on viral translation, two reporter constructs were generated as described previously,22 including a SARS-CoV-2 UTRs reporter (pcDNA4-5′UTR-RLuc-3′UTR) and a control bicistronic vector (pcDNA4-FLuc-EMCV IRES-RLuc). Briefly, the 5′ and 3′UTRs of SARS-CoV-2 were amplified by PCR using Platinum SuperFi II DNA High-Fidelity Polymerase (Thermo Fisher, 12361010) with cDNA from SARS-CoV-2-infected Huh-7 cells as the template. The PCR products were resolved by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the target fragment was purified using a gel extraction kit (Tiangen, DP209). The pcDNA4.0 vector and the PCR-amplified UTR fragment were double-digested with HindIII (NEB, R3104S) and BamHI (NEB, R3136S) at 37°C for 2 h. The digested products were then ligated using T4 DNA Ligase (NEB, M0202M) at 16°C overnight to generate the SARS-CoV-2 UTRs reporter. In which, the viral 5′ UTR and 3′ UTR were placed upstream and downstream of the Renilla luciferase (RLuc) gene, respectively (Figure S4A). For the control bicistronic vector, the BamHI/EcoRI (NEB, R3101S)-linearized pcDNA4.0 vector was ligated with a fragment carrying the Firefly luciferase (FLuc) gene, the encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV) internal ribosomal entry site (IRES), and the RLuc gene, which was flanked by BamHI and EcoRI sites.

To investigate the potential target and binding sites of compd. 10, the coding sequence (CDS) of the NCOA1 gene was amplified by PCR using cDNA from Huh-7 cells. The wild-type DNA fragment was digested with restriction enzymes KpnI and XbaI (NEB, R3142S/R0145S) and then inserted into the pcDNA4.0 vector carrying a FLAG tag to construct the pcDNA4.0-NCOA1 wild-type recombinant vector. Mutant NCOA1 constructs containing alanine substitutions at residues D276, T277, and R281 were generated using a site-directed mutagenesis kit (Vazyme, C214). Specific primers containing restriction enzyme sites are listed in Table S5.

In vitro transcription

Plasmids pcDNA4-5′UTR-RLuc-3′UTR and pcDNA4-FLuc-EMCV IRES-RLuc were linearized by single digestion with AgeI (NEB, R3552S). Capped mRNAs were synthesized from 1 μg of linearized template using the mMESSAGE mMACHINE T7 Ultra Kit (Thermo Fisher, AM1344) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After transcription, TURBO DNase was added to remove the DNA template, and the capped mRNA was purified by lithium chloride precipitation. The purified mRNA concentration was measured, and the A260/A280 ratio of the samples used for subsequent experiments was between 1.8 and 2.0.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

Huh-7 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and grown to 70–90% confluence. For each well, 1 μg of capped mRNA was transfected into cells using Lipofectamine MessengerMAX (Thermo Fisher, LMRNA015) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing gradient concentrations of clofoctol and compd. 10 (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 μM), with 5 μg/mL puromycin as a positive control. Cells were incubated for an additional 8 h. After incubation, the medium was removed, and cells were washed with PBS before lysing in 200 μL of reporter lysis buffer per well on a shaker at room temperature for 15 min. Luciferase activities were measured using a dual luciferase reporter assay kit (Beyotime, RG029S). Briefly, the cell lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. Then, 50 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a white 96-well plate and assayed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Luciferase activities were evaluated by relative light units (RLU).

Fatty acid deprivation and supplementation

Fatty acid-free BSA-V (Solarbio, A8850) was dissolved in serum-free DMEM at 2% and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. This medium was supplemented with 8 μM compd. 10 alone or combined with 10 μM PA, OA, or ARA. After cell adhesion, the medium was replaced with the corresponding medium for 48 h.

CETSA

To assess ligand-induced stabilization of target proteins in cellular lysates, CETSA, a biophysical method based on thermal denaturation, was employed. For endogenous protein detection, Huh-7 cells were seeded in 10-cm dishes and grown to 80–90% confluence. The cells were then incubated with 10 mL of culture medium containing either DMSO (vehicle control) or 50 μM compd. 10 for 1 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For exogenous protein detection, HEK293T cells were seeded in 10-cm dishes and grown to 80% confluence. A total of 8 μg of plasmid DNA was transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, L3000015) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The culture medium was replaced with fresh medium 6 h post-transfection, and the cells were incubated for an additional 42 h. Then, the medium was removed, and the cells were collected in 1 mL of PBS supplemented with protease inhibitors. Total cellular proteins were extracted by three freeze–thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen and clarified by centrifugation (20,000 × g, 20 min, 4°C). Lysates were incubated with DMSO or compd. 10 (50 μM) for 1 h at room temperature, aliquoted, and heated at different temperatures for 3 min. Denatured proteins were removed by centrifugation, and supernatants were collected for Western blot.48

DARTS assay

The DARTS assay was performed as previously described.49 Briefly, approximately 5 × 107 cells were lysed in ice-cold M-PER buffer (Thermo Fisher, 78503) with protease inhibitors, clarified by centrifugation (18,000 × g, 10 min, 4°C), and aliquoted for treatment with DMSO or compd. 10. After 1 h incubation at room temperature, samples were subjected to limited proteolysis with pronase for 30 min. Reactions were terminated with 5× loading buffer and denatured at 95°C for 10 min.

CHX chase assay

To evaluate intracellular protein degradation, Huh-7 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight. The cells were then treated with DMSO or compd. 10 in the presence of CHX (100 μg/mL). Cells were harvested at the indicated time points (0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h) after treatment initiation and subjected to Western blot analysis.

Pharmacological inhibition of degradation pathways

To determine the specific degradation pathway involved, Huh-7 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight. The cells were then exposed to DMSO or 8 μM compd. 10 for 24 h in the continuous presence of CHX (100 μg/mL) and the respective inhibitor, including 3-MA (5 mM), BafA1 (0.5 μM), or MG132 (10 μM). After treatment, cell lysates were collected and analyzed by Western blot.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0). Data distribution was assessed to determine the use of parametric (Student’s t test or ANOVA) or non-parametric (Mann-Whitney U) tests. For multiple comparisons, ANOVA was followed by Dunnett’s test (one-way) or Šídák’s test (two-way) as appropriate. Results are expressed as mean ± SD. Experiments were repeated at least three times. Significance was defined as p < 0.05. In figures, ∗ indicates p < 0.05, ∗∗ indicates p < 0.01, ∗∗∗ indicates p < 0.001.
