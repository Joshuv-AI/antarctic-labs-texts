Exploration of preschool hearing screening at primary healthcare centers in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Ahmad A Alanazi, Rayan A Almutairi, Khalid S Alsuhaibani, Yazzan Alruwaily, Hellal A Alqudiey, Mohammed A Alabbas, Shuaa R Alanazi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe hearing status of children should be examined throughout early childhood, even if they have passed the newborn hearing loss because hearing loss can occur at any time and may affect their ability to learn. Preschool hearing screening (PHS) is vital to continue screening throughout early childhood. The current practice of PHS in the primary healthcare centers (PHCs) in Saudi Arabia is unknown. The purpose of this study was to investigate PHS in the PHCs. This cross-sectional descriptive study used an in-person-administered questionnaire to collect data. A total of 106 out of 120 participants (male = 61; female = 45) representing the PHCs in Riyadh were interviewed. Most of the participants were aged 31–40 years and held a bachelor's degree as the highest academic qualification with limited years of experience. PHS was mostly performed through subjective measures by asking the parents (71.7%) and the child (65.1%). The audiometric evaluation was conducted for preschool children in only half of the PHCs. General practitioners and nurses usually perform PHS. Substantial gaps in the practice of PHS were identified. The lack of training and appropriate instruments and the need for audiological services were the main challenges. Incorporating robust and objective protocols for PHS into the educational system is a valuable strategy for identifying hearing loss early and reducing its impact through the establishment of effective intervention plans.Keywords: Audiology, Hearing loss, Preschool children, Primary healthcare center, Screening, Saudi Arabia

Introduction

Primary healthcare centers (PHCs) are considered the first point of access to healthcare in Saudi Arabia (Al Asmri et al., 2020). These centers offer a variety of healthcare services to individuals, families, and the community, such as medical checkups and follow-ups for patients with chronic illnesses. Most of these healthcare services (60%) are provided by the Ministry of Health (Harlor et al., 2009) through 2120 PHCs nationwide, while the remaining services are offered by the commercial sector (23%) and other government health agencies (17%), such as Health Affairs of the Ministry of National Guard (Ministry of Health, 2022a; Ministry of Health, 2022b). Primary healthcare services have several obstacles, including illness trends, manpower, information systems, and accessibility (Al Asmri et al., 2020). Therefore, rapid changes according to the national transformation program of the nation, which is a part of Saudi Vision (2030), were taking to (a) increase accessibility to healthcare, (b) raise the standard and effectiveness of care, and (c) encourage the reduction of health risks (Al Saffer et al., 2021).

Hearing loss is one of the health conditions that affects people of all ages and requires effective healthcare services in Saudi Arabia (Alanazi, 2020). According to the General Authority for Statistics (2017), 1.4% of the Saudi population suffers from mild, moderate, or severe hearing impairments. Various prevalences of hearing loss ranging from 1.75% to 7.12% were reported among the Saudi population (Abolfotouh et al., 1995; Al-Rowaily et al., 2021). Both conductive hearing loss and sensorineural hearing loss are common among Saudi children (Al-Abdiljawad, 2007; Elmalik et al., 2020). “Failure to detect congenital or acquired hearing loss in children may result in lifelong deficits in speech and language acquisition, poor academic performance, personal-social maladjustments, and emotional difficulties.” (Korver et al., 2010, p. 1253). Therefore, hearing screening programs, such as preschool hearing screening (PHS) were established in many countries to identify hearing loss and reduce its impacts on language, cognitive, social, and emotional development when an intervention plan is established (Ching et al., 2013; Korver et al., 2010; Pimperton and Kennedy, 2012).

To screen hearing of children aged 3–6 years, pure-tone audiometry (PTA) test or condition play audiometry (CPA) test are widely recognized as the gold standard (American Academy of Audiology [AAA], 2011). During PTA, tones at different frequencies and intensities are played through headphones, and the child is asked to respond when they hear them by raising a hand or pressing a button. CPA is similar to PTA, but the child is asked to perform a simple task, such as placing a ball in a bucket when they hear the tone through headphones or speakers. Both PTA and CPA are used to determine the hearing threshold, which is the quietest sound the child can hear. It is the responsibility of the audiologist to develop, supervise, and implement PHS (AAA, 2011). Although the screening itself may be carried out by non-audiology personnel, an audiologist is usually ultimately in charge of providing the staff with the necessary training and supervision (AAA, 2011). PHS for children aged three years or older using either PTA or CPA should test each ear at 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz at 20 dB, and no response at one or more frequencies indicates a child failed the screening (AAA, 2011; American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, 1997). A hearing screening threshold of 25 dB HL was also used to reduce the impact of environmental noise that existed in the school settings on screening results because this noise typically masks pure-tone test stimuli. (Allen et al., 2004; Bamford et al., 2007; Mahomed-Asmail et al., 2016). Testing at 500 Hz has become less popular because it can be easily masked by ambient noise and has poorer reliability (Bell et al., 2016). Pure-tone hearing screening requires trained staff, calibrated equipment, and behavioral responses from the child. Combining both PTA screening with tympanometry screening at the same time can lower the number of referrals and false positives (Dodd-Murphy et al., 2014).

PHS using PTA was reported in many countries with differences in terms of screening protocols, such as screening thresholds, screening personnel, and screening location (Bussé et al., 2021). For example, pure-tone hearing screening is performed by speech-language pathologists (SLPs) at 25 dB HL across frequencies 500–4000 Hz for children aged 4–5 years in Austrian kindergartens, while pure-tone hearing screening is conducted by audiologists and nurses across frequencies 500–4000 Hz at 25–30 dB HL for children aged 4–5 years in English kindergartens and schools (Bussé et al., 2021). In Saudi Arabia, new students applying for the first grade of primary school are required to complete medical examinations at the PHCs assessing their general health, including hearing (Ministry of Health, 2024). However, there is a paucity of literature related to PHS in Saudi Arabia. To the best of our knowledge, no study has investigated PHS in the PHCs in Saudi Arabia. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the current practice of PHS in these centers, with the hope that the findings will inform the development of more effective PHS in Saudi Arabia.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional descriptive study evaluated PHS performed in the PHCs under the MoH administration because they provide most of the primary healthcare services in Saudi Arabia. Only the PHCs located in Riyadh, the capital city of Saudi Arabia, were included because these centers were accessible to the authors for data collection. This study was approved by King Abdullah International Medical Research Center Institutional Review Board under protocol #SP23R/094/05 and Riyadh Second Health Cluster Institutional Review Board under protocol #23–516E. Informed consent was taken from all participants before the data collection process. The procedures adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample size and sampling technique

Data were collected from the leaders of the PHCs, excluding the centers that were out of service or under reconstruction. One hundred and twenty PHCs are located around neighborhoods and are considered the first line of interaction between the patient and the healthcare system (Ministry of Health, 2017). The sample size was calculated to be 102 participants using Raosoft sample size calculator (Raosoft, Inc., United States) with a 5% margin of error, 99% confidence level, and 50% response distribution. However, the total population, purposive sampling technique were implemented to include all the leaders (n = 120) of the PHCs.

Instrumentation

Data were gathered from the participants through face-to-face interviews by using an in-person-administered questionnaire (Appendix), which was developed for data collection after a review of the literature. The questionnaire consisted of eleven questions distributed into two sections about the participants’ information and PHS. First, the participants were asked five questions about their gender, age, academic qualifications, experience, and whether they practice in newly designed PHCs. Second, six questions explored the following: who performed PHS, how it was conducted, the availability of appropriate hearing screening instruments and databases for children with hearing loss, how to deal with preschoolers with hearing loss, and the need for audiology clinics run by audiologists in the PHCs.

The questionnaire was developed in English language, and there was no need for forward and background translation to Arabic since English is spoken by the participants. The questionnaire's content validity was assessed by conducting a focus group of experts in the field who checked its difficulty, length, and format. Piloting the questionnaire on a sample of the target population was not performed due to the small population size. As a result, the questionnaire reliability using statistical methods, such as Cronbach's alpha or inter-rater reliability could not be calculated.

Results

The results are presented for each questionnaire category separately. Of the total population (n = 120), 106 participants representing their PHCs completed the questionnaire. The remaining 14 PHCs were excluded either because the leaders refused to participate or the PHCs were out of service or under reconstruction.

Demographic information

Table 1 summarizes the demographics of the participants. A total of 106 leaders (male = 61; female = 45) participated in the study. Approximately half of the participants were aged between 31 and 40 years, followed by 32 participants (30.2%) who were aged between 20 and 30 years. The highest academic/clinical qualification of the participants was a bachelor's degree (n = 38, 35.8%) followed by a medical board of specialization and fellowship (n = 35, 33%) and a master's degree (n = 17, 16%). The experience of participants was mainly between 1 and 10 years. Of the total participants, 43 (40.6%) had an experience of 1–5 years, while 33 (31.1%) had an experience of 6–10 years. Lastly, 67 participants (63.2%) reported working in newly designed PHCs.

Preschool hearing screening (PHS)

When the participants were asked how PHS was performed in their PHCs, they reported by asking the parents (71.7%), asking the child (65.1%), observing the child (50.9%), using PTA and tympanometry (49%), and using otoscopy (37.7%). Only one participant (0.9%) reported the lack of PHS (Fig. 1). Regarding the availability of appropriate hearing screening instruments in the PHCs, more than half of the participants (56.6%) stated the existence of these instruments in their workplace. The results showed that general practitioners (GPs, 69.8%) followed by nurses (23.6.%) and audiologists (11.3%) were the main healthcare professionals who performed PHS.

Preschool hearing screening methods in the primary healthcare centers of the Ministry of Health in Saudi Arabia. Note. The participants were allowed to select one or more options.

Most of the participants (72.6%) believed that the PHCs should have audiology clinics run by audiologists. The majority of participants (95.2%) referred preschool children to hospitals when they failed the screening, while 64 (59.4%) and 27 (25.5%) reported informing parents and schools, respectively. Rescheduling for another hearing screening was reported by only 13 (12.3%) participants (Fig. 2). Lastly, most participants (n = 83, 78.3%) reported the absence of databases for children with hearing loss, while the remaining participants (n = 23, 21.7%) stated a few databases, such as Sehhaty platform, Raqeem, and Electronic Medical Record (EMR) system.

Discussion

School-age is an important period in a child's life for acquiring language, social, and behavioral skills. Hearing loss may affect the student's school readiness and ability to learn in an auditory-based classroom. School health including PHS is one of the healthcare services under the scope of the PHCs in Saudi Arabia. PHS aims to detect hearing loss in children as early as possible, so referrals for further evaluation and treatment are made. This provides children with a better chance of receiving therapy and educational accommodations. For instance, it will be challenging without PHS to detect students with mild hearing loss who do not show any clear physical signs or indicators. Our study examined PHS at the PHCs by collecting data from 106 participants, who were mainly young healthcare professionals with undergraduate academic qualifications and limited years of experience. The lack of sufficient academic and clinical training could be seen as a challenge to the practice of PHS (Alanazi, 2017).

Our study revealed substantial gaps in the practice of PHS. Asking the parents and child and observing the child were the main methods used for screening children. This result is consistent with findings from other countries when subjective measures, such as a child's observation and responses, and a caregiver's reports are the main screening methods (Bussé et al., 2021; Khan et al., 2018; Petrocchi-Bartal and Khoza-Shangase, 2016). Questionnaires were developed and utilized as screening methods for children at school entry (Newton et al., 2001; Samelli et al., 2011). This highlights the need for more robust and objective screening protocols to ensure early and accurate detection of hearing loss among preschool children (Brodie et al., 2022). Our study revealed that only 49% of the PHCs used PTA and tympanometry for screening preschool children. PTA has been adopted as the standard procedure for screening the hearing of children three years of age and older because of its high sensitivity and specificity (Fortnum et al., 2016; Yong et al., 2020). The limited use of standardized, objective screening methods carries a significant risk of missing children with hearing loss (Lü et al., 2011). Otoscopy was used for hearing screening in less than half of the PHCs. Otoscopic examination is not a hearing test and should be used in conjunction with other physical examinations and objective hearing screening.

Our results showed that PHS was mostly performed by GPs followed by nurses at PHCs. Childhood hearing screening at ages 4–6 years was primarily performed by nurses in Europe, while audiologists, SLPs, GPs, Ear-Nose-Throat specialists, and pediatricians also screen children's hearing at different screening locations (Bussé et al., 2021). For example, PHS is conducted in kindergartens in Austria, Hungary, and England, in child health clinics in Finland, Germany, and Switzerland, and in schools in Ireland, Netherlands, and Sweden (Bussé et al., 2021). Some participants reported that the hearing screening was performed by audiologists. This occurs in the case of (a) referring children to other healthcare facilities because the PHCs do not have audiology clinics or audiologists, or (b) collaboration with visiting audiologists. Therefore, 72.6% of the participants believed that the PHCs should have audiology clinics run by audiologists. This indicates the need for establishing audiology clinics within these PHCs to address the existing gap in the current practice and ensure the utilization of appropriate early hearing detection and intervention (EHDI) in addition to other audiological services. This also aligns with recommendations from global health organizations and emphasizes the importance of specialized facilities for effective hearing screening and intervention (World Health Organization, 2024; Prieve et al., 2015).

As previously reported, PHS was commonly conducted by subjective measures (e.g., asking the parents and observing the child). This may occur because (a) more than half of the PHCs did not have audiometers and tympanometers regardless that most of the PHCs were newly designed centers, and (b) no training was provided to the PHCs staff. One of the challenges of hearing screening in low-to middle-income countries, is the limited resources, such as the availability of hearing screening tools (Shinn et al., 2021). In contrast, the health sector in Saudi Arabia has no limitations in terms of resources because of the huge financial support by the Saudi government to promote public health and disease prevention, facilitate access to healthcare services, provide the best quality of healthcare, and modernize facilities and equipment (Ministry of Health, 2003; Ministry of Health, 2016). Therefore, the PHCs are recommended to be equipped with appropriate hearing screening instruments placed in calm settings with limited distractions when conducting the screening.

The underutilization of available hearing screening instruments in some PHCs is attributed to the lack of training dedicated to healthcare providers. For example, Family physicians have inadequate knowledge about hearing assessments and the presence of EHDI in Saudi Arabia (Alqudah et al., 2021). Nurses are not trained on hearing loss and how it to be prevented, its impact on the development of speech and language, and the need for referral for diagnostic testing (Barbosa and Griz, 2014; Van der Linde and Kritzinger, 2013). This emphasizes the need for comprehensive training programs to improve the utilization of existing resources. One of the priorities that was suggested for developing Saudi PHCs is providing healthcare professionals with appropriate training in the current methods of health provisions (Al-Mazrou, 2002).

Due to the above-mentioned challenges to PHS in the PHCs, most of the participants reported referring children to hospitals when hearing loss is suspected. These referrals may be subjected to rejection based on the reason for being referred or the long waiting time to access the services, which may lead to loss of follow-up and loss of documentation and affect the delivery of appropriate hearing services (Alanazi, 2020). It was surprising that not all parents and schools were always informed about the child's hearing status by the PHCs personnel. The lack of awareness of EHDI leads to poor follow-up return rates (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024; Scheepers et al., 2014). The parents' awareness of their children's hearing was unsatisfactory in Saudi Arabia (Fageeh et al., 2023). Knowledge and attitude of school teachers regarding hearing loss were also insufficient (AlOtaibi et al., 2020). A few awareness programs about the importance of hearing screening and the negative consequences of hearing loss are conducted in Saudi Arabia (Alanazi, 2020). Parents and teachers should be educated about hearing loss and its effects and included as team members in the process of EHDI (AlOtaibi et al., 2020).

Most of the participants reported the absence of databases for children with hearing loss. The MoH has launched a few databases, as reported by some of the participants, such as the EMR system that could be beneficial for registering the PHS results, referrals, and follow-up. A similar unified database and e-system was developed by the MoH for registering newborn screening results and monitoring quality and performance through periodical reports (American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, 1997). The PHCs personnel are advised to get the optimal benefits of the existing databases when performing PHS or dealing with children who have hearing loss.

Study limitations and future research

This study has some limitations. PHS is expected to be similarly performed across all PHCs in Saudi Arabia; however, data were collected only from the PHCs located in Riyadh city. Although the data were highly representative of the target population, the limited sample size might have hindered capturing all variances in performing PHS. Further research with a larger sample size from all regions of Saudi Arabia is suggested. To determine any differences, comparisons between PHS at the PHCs that are under the administration of the MoH and PHS at the PHCs of other healthcare agencies (e.g., Ministry of National Guard Health Affairs) warrant further research. The questionnaire was designed to achieve the aim of the study and included limited questions exploring PHS. The design of detailed questionnaires for obtaining comprehensive information about PHS is advised.

Conclusion

Our study found that PHS is not always performed based on the recommended methods in the PHCs, which may result in some children with hearing loss going undetected and affecting their quality of life. To improve the current practice, all the PHCs are recommended to (a) implement clear, mandatory guidelines for PHS, (b) establish audiology clinics run by audiologists in the PHCs, and (c) utilize existing databases to track children with hearing loss and provide effective intervention plans.
