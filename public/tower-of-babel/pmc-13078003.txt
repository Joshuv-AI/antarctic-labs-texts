Weight-normalized norepinephrine dosing and apparent BMI-dependent vasopressin responsiveness
Authors: Pedro D Wendel-Garcia, Sebastian Morales, Paulo Melo, Greta Emilia Kiavialaitis, Peter Wohlrab, Ricardo Castro, Edda Tschernko, Glenn Hernández, Eduardo Kattan
Published: 2026

Melchers et al. recently reported that obese patients with septic shock exhibit an attenuated norepinephrine-sparing response to adjunct vasopressin (AVP), suggesting that obesity-related differences may reflect intrinsic physiological resistance rather than inadequate vasopressor dosing [1]. The analysis is rigorous and addresses an increasingly relevant clinical population. The observation of BMI-dependent heterogeneity in vasopressor trajectories is particularly thought-provoking. However, we believe that a series of alternative methodological explanations warrants consideration before attributing these findings solely to differential AVP responsiveness.

First, in the study, norepinephrine (NE) exposure and longitudinal trajectories were normalized to actual body weight (i.e. expressed in µg/kg/min). The authors reported that obese patients had significantly lower baseline NE doses compared with normal-weight patients. Critically, when normalizing vasopressor exposure to body weight, body mass inherently becomes part of the exposure metric itself. NE pharmacokinetics are commonly described using a linear one-compartment model with first-order elimination [2, 3]. However, in obesity, body weight increases disproportionately relative to circulating blood volume [4]. Consequently, expressing NE exposure in µg/kg/min systematically introduces a bias, as body weight does not directly correlate with the pharmacologically relevant central compartment. Furthermore, in clinical practice, NE’s administration is titrated to a pharmacodynamic endpoint—restoration of mean arterial pressure—rather than to a systemic concentration normalized to body mass. Under these premises, two identical patients receiving similar absolute infusion rates (µg/min), achieving comparable arterial pressures, and merely differing in their weight, will mathematically appear to have a different NE exposure once normalized to body weight.

This methodological artifact becomes particularly relevant in longitudinal analyses, whereby comparable absolute dose reductions translate into disproportionately small weight-normalized changes for obese patients compared to their non-obese counterparts. Consequently, scaling NE by body weight may artificially attenuate the apparent NE-sparing effect over time, potentially suggesting a reduced responsiveness to AVP even if absolute catecholamine reduction is similar across BMI groups.

Importantly, the reported three-way interaction between AVP dose, time, and BMI was derived using weight-normalized NE trajectories. Whether similar BMI-dependent patterns would persist if NE exposure were expressed in absolute units (µg/min) remains uncertain. Evaluating trajectories in absolute dosing units—or alternative anthropometric normalizations (using lean or ideal body weight)—may help distinguish between scaling effects and intrinsic pharmacodynamic differences. The distinction is clinically meaningful. If attenuation of NE reduction reflects true AVP resistance, this would support exploration of alternative adjunctive strategies in obese patients. However, if the apparent divergence arises from exposure normalization, a reinterpretation of dosing metrics, rather than receptor-level physiology, may be warranted.

Beyond implications for dose–response interpretation, the choice of weight-normalized NE exposure may also lead to improper clinical severity stratification. In septic shock, NE dose is frequently used as a surrogate marker of hemodynamic severity, both within composite scoring systems (e.g., cardiovascular components of organ dysfunction scores) [5] and as a trigger for escalation to adjunctive therapies such as AVP [6]. Due to the weight-normalized NE scaling artifact, obese patients may appear to require lower vasopressor support despite comparable absolute infusion rates and similar arterial pressures (Fig. 1). This could lead to an inaccurate estimation of shock severity and consequently to an inappropriate delay in treatment escalation. In settings where vasopressor thresholds guide clinical decision-making or eligibility criteria, scaling-related differences in exposure metrics may therefore have downstream effects on risk stratification and therapeutic decision making.

Second, an alternative explanation for the observed upward trajectory of NE in obese patients is a time-dependent pharmacokinetic effect rather than an intrinsic AVP resistance. Although AVP is a hydrophilic peptide, its volume of distribution may increase with body size, albeit non-linearly, as previously noted [4, 7]. In a one-compartment framework, this could prolong the time required to reach steady-state concentrations. While AVP is typically considered a rapidly equilibrating drug, with a half-life of 10–35 min [8], the time to achieve steady-state concentration (five half-lives) could potentially extend beyond the 2-h observation window used to define hemodynamic response in this study. Thus, an increased volume of distribution could contribute to delays in achieving effective plasma concentrations and may prevent observation of the drug’s complete hemodynamic effect in obese patients within a short time window.

Consistent with this interpretation, studies assessing vasopressor trajectories over longer time horizons have not demonstrated persistent obesity-related differences in hemodynamic effect [9, 10]. Moreover, inspection of the reported trajectories in the original study suggests that patients classified as non-responders exhibit a subsequent downward trend in NE requirements beyond the 2-h time window, very much comparable to that of responders [11]. Together, these findings raise the possibility that early classification may reflect delayed pharmacokinetic equilibration rather than true pharmacodynamic resistance.

These explanations are not mutually exclusive: weight-normalized exposure of NE may (1) significantly delay the initiation of AVP leading to a more severe disease state and (2) distort the magnitude of apparent NE reduction, (3) while a short observation window may bias its temporal interpretation. Together, they could produce an apparent BMI-dependent divergence in vasopressor response without the existence of an underlying intrinsic AVP resistance.

We commend the authors for highlighting BMI-dependent heterogeneity in vasopressor response and believe that careful consideration of exposure scaling will further strengthen the interpretation of these important findings.
