Trends in Osteoporotic Humerus Fractures and Refractures, and Mortality Rates: Analysis of NHIS Claims Data From 2006–2022
Authors: Tae-Young Kim, Kyoung Jin Kim, Jae-Hwi Nho, Hyun-Bin Kim, Jaiyong Kim, Jihye Kim, Hoyeon Jang, Jun-Il Yoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoporotic fractures have become a significant public health concern due to their high prevalence and associated morbidity and mortality. Humerus fractures, specifically, have gained attention as they are the third most common type of osteoporotic fracture. However, there is limited research on the trends and outcomes of humerus fractures, as well as the rates of refractures and mortality in this population.MethodsThis study aimed to analyze claims data from the National Health Insurance Service from 2006 to 2022 to investigate the trends in osteoporotic humerus fractures, refractures, and mortality rates in Korea. The study included all individuals aged 50 years and older who were diagnosed with a humerus fracture and had available data for one-year outcome ascertainment. Statistical analysis was performed to determine the incidence rates, trends over time, and associated mortality rates.ResultsAmong 197,086 humerus fracture cases, incidence increased sharply from 2006–2010 then plateaued through 2022. Female predominance was consistent at 2.5-fold throughout. One-year mortality improved in females, with standardized mortality decreasing from 9.6% to 5.5% during 2006–2021, but remained approximately 2-fold higher in males. Advanced age was the strongest predictor of mortality, with hazard ratios increasing exponentially from 2.19 at age 60 to 11.53 at age ≥ 85. Refracture rates increased 4-fold in females (17 per 1,000 to 67 per 1,000 from 2007–2021), with higher rates in younger age groups. Most strikingly, pronounced socioeconomic disparities persisted: Medical Aid beneficiaries showed the highest fracture incidence, approximately 2-fold higher than the highest workplace insurance group, with some years approaching 2.5-fold. A socioeconomic gradient was most evident among regional insurance beneficiaries and was more attenuated among workplace insurance beneficiaries.ConclusionThis comprehensive analysis demonstrates that advanced age is an important determinant of one-year mortality after humerus fracture, whereas lower socioeconomic status is associated primarily with higher fracture incidence. The persistently high refracture rate through 2021 underscores the need for improved secondary fracture prevention and fracture referral services.

INTRODUCTION

Osteoporotic fractures, characterized by low bone mineral density and deterioration of bone tissue, pose a significant public health concern due to their high prevalence and associated morbidity and mortality.1 Hip, spine, and wrist fractures have traditionally garnered the most attention in osteoporosis research, but humerus fractures have emerged as the third most common type of osteoporotic fracture.2 Despite their growing importance, limited research has focused on the trends and outcomes of humerus fractures, refractures, and mortality rates in this population. Osteoporotic fractures are a major health issue worldwide, especially in aging populations. It is estimated that one in three women and one in five men aged 50 years and older will experience an osteoporotic fracture in their lifetime.3 These fractures are associated with significant pain, reduced mobility, and impaired quality of life.4 Additionally, they place a substantial burden on healthcare systems and caregivers and are associated with increased mortality rates. Given the increasing longevity of populations globally, osteoporotic fractures are projected to become an even more significant public health concern in the future.5

Humerus fractures account for approximately 5–10% of all osteoporotic fractures, making them the third most common type after hip and vertebral fractures.6 The humerus is the bone of the upper arm and plays a crucial role in arm function.7 Fractures of the humerus can occur due to falls, trauma, or minimal force in individuals with weakened bones, such as those with osteoporosis. These fractures can result in significant pain, functional impairment, and reduced quality of life. Surgical intervention, such as open reduction and internal fixation or prosthetic replacement, is often necessary, further adding to the physical and financial burdens for individuals and healthcare systems.8 Despite the growing prevalence of humerus fractures, there is limited research on their trends, outcomes, refractures, and mortality rates. Previous studies have primarily focused on hip and vertebral fractures, given their higher prevalence and associated complications.9 This lack of research hampers efforts to effectively prevent, diagnose, and manage humerus fractures, ultimately impacting patient outcomes and quality of life. Therefore, there is an urgent need to investigate the burden, trends, and associated outcomes of osteoporotic humerus fractures.10

This study aimed to address the gap in knowledge regarding osteoporotic humerus fractures by analyzing claims data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) in Korea.11 By examining data from 2006 to 2022, the study sought to investigate the trends in humerus fractures, refractures, and mortality rates in this population. The findings of this study will provide valuable insights into the epidemiology and outcomes of humerus fractures, informing clinical practice and public health interventions aimed at reducing the burden of osteoporotic fractures.

Understanding the trends and outcomes of humerus fractures is crucial for healthcare providers, policymakers, and researchers to develop strategies and interventions that effectively prevent and manage these fractures.12 By identifying the risk factors and predictors of refractures and mortality, healthcare providers can better tailor treatment and rehabilitation plans for individuals with humerus fractures. Additionally, public health interventions can be designed to reduce the burden of osteoporotic fractures, promote early detection, and improve post-fracture care and management.

Through this study, we aim to contribute to a comprehensive understanding of humerus fractures, improving patient outcomes and ultimately reducing the societal and economic impact of osteoporotic fractures in Korea.

METHODS

Data source

This study utilized claims data obtained from the NHIS in Korea. The dataset included information on individuals aged 50 years and older who were diagnosed with a humerus fracture between the years 2006 and 2022. By focusing on this age group, the study aimed to capture the population most at risk for osteoporosis and related fractures. Additionally, to ensure data completeness and methodological rigor, NHIS claims data were analyzed across different time periods for each outcome indicator. The NHIS osteoporosis fracture fact sheet includes multiple fracture sites, including wrist, spine, hip, tibia, pelvis, and humerus fractures.13 However, the present study focused specifically on humerus fractures. Humerus fractures were identified using ICD-10 codes S42.2 and S42.3 combined with relevant treatment procedure codes.

Operational definition

Refractures were monitored at six-month intervals, with follow-up tracking extending to one year. Refractures were defined as new humerus fracture diagnostic codes (S42.2 or S42.3) appearing ≥ 6 months after the index (incident) fracture. This definition distinguished refractures from follow-up visits for the same fracture episode (typically occurring within 1–3 months post-injury) and from coding errors (isolated diagnostic codes without corresponding treatment procedures).

Humerus fractures were identified using a comprehensive operational definition that integrated both diagnostic and procedural codes. The index humerus fracture was defined by combining ICD-10th revision (ICD-10) codes S42.2 (fracture of upper end of humerus) and S42.3 (fracture of shaft of humerus) with related treatment procedures. A humerus fracture case was confirmed upon meeting one of the following criteria: 1) Hospitalization with humerus fracture as the primary diagnosis without surgical intervention; 2) Humerus fracture listed as a primary, secondary, or other diagnosis concurrently with surgical procedures, including open reduction of the humerus (N0602), open reduction of the humerus with plate fixation (N0612), closed reduction of the humerus with closed pinning (N0992), or closed reduction of the humerus (N0642); or 3) Diagnosis of humerus fracture accompanied by immobilization using shoulder spica cast (T6010), Velpeau cast (T6110), long arm splint (T6151), long arm cast (T6020), or Velpeau bandage (N0521: closed reduction and immobilization of humerus fracture with Velpeau bandage). To minimize misclassification from coding errors in administrative claims data, we required concurrent diagnostic and treatment procedural codes for case confirmation. Isolated diagnostic codes without corresponding treatment codes were excluded from the analysis.

Data analysis procedure

The claims data obtained from the NHIS were analyzed using various statistical methods. First, the researchers identified all individuals with a diagnosis of humerus fracture based on the ICD-10 codes. They then extracted demographic information, such as age and sex, as well as clinical details related to the fractures, including the presence of any surgical interventions.

Statistical analysis

Different time periods were employed for each analysis to ensure methodological rigor. Incidence rates were calculated from 2006 to 2022, representing the complete period with reliable data including Medical Aid beneficiaries. Mortality analysis was restricted to 2006–2021 to allow one-year follow-up for all patients, as those with fractures in 2021 required complete survival data through 2022. Refracture analysis covered 2007–2022 after applying a one-year wash-out period in 2006; however, 2022 estimates were considered descriptive because of possible incomplete claims capture and were not interpreted as part of the main 2007–2021 trend. The researchers employed advanced statistical analysis techniques to assess the trends in humerus fractures, refractures, and mortality rates over time. They used regression models to calculate the annual percent change in incidence rates and assess the statistical significance of any observed trends. Additionally, they conducted subgroup analyses to explore differences in outcomes based on factors such as age, sex, and presence of surgical interventions. Risk factors for one-year mortality after humerus fracture were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Seoul Bumin Hospital (IRB No. BMH202304009-HE002). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the use of de-identified secondary data. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The researchers adhered to guidelines and regulations pertaining to the use of secondary data for research purposes and ensured that no personally identifiable information was disclosed in the analysis or reporting of the results.

RESULTS

Incidence trend of humerus fractures

The analysis included a total of 197,086 humerus fracture cases during the 17-year study period from 2006 to 2022. Humerus fracture incidence demonstrated distinct temporal patterns characterized by two phases: a rapid increase from 2006 to 2010, followed by a plateau period through 2022 (Fig. 1). Marked gender disparities were evident throughout the study period, with females exhibiting consistently higher incidence rates than males. Female crude rates (CRs) increased from 0.76 per 1,000 in 2006 to 0.89 per 1,000 in 2010, then continued to rise gradually to approximately 1.0–1.1 per 1,000 through 2022. Male CRs rose from 0.31 per 1,000 in 2006 to 0.35–0.40 per 1,000 by 2010, representing approximately one-third of female rates throughout the study period. The standardized rates (SRs) closely paralleled the CRs, with female SR increasing from 0.76 per 1,000 in 2006 to 0.97 per 1,000 in 2022, and male SR rising from 0.36 per 1,000 to 0.39 per 1,000 over the same period. The persistent 2.5-fold female predominance throughout the entire study period highlights the substantial gender-based difference in humerus fracture risk. The early rapid growth phase (2006–2010) exhibited the steepest increase in incidence, after which rates stabilized with minor fluctuations.

Mortality trend of humerus fractures

One-year mortality rates following humerus fractures demonstrated a consistent declining trend from 2006 to 2021, with notable gender disparities throughout the study period (Fig. 2). Male mortality rates were consistently higher than female rates, maintaining an approximate 2-fold difference across all years. Male mortality showed substantial fluctuations in the early study period, with crude rates ranging from 10.8% to 13.0% and standardized rates ranging from 13.3% to 15.9% between 2006 and 2008. Following this volatile phase, male mortality rates exhibited a gradual declining trend, decreasing from approximately 13–14% in the early 2010s to 10.6% in 2018, before rising again to approximately 12.7% in 2021. The SR for males closely paralleled the CR, ranging from 11.6% to 15.8% and demonstrating similar temporal patterns throughout the observation period. Female mortality rates showed a more consistent downward trajectory throughout the entire study period. Standardized rates declined from 9.6% in 2006 to 5.5% in 2021, representing approximately a 43% reduction in mortality over the 16-year period. The most pronounced decline occurred between 2006 and 2016, with rates dropping from approximately 9–10% to 6%, followed by continued gradual reduction to approximately 5–5.5% in recent years (2018–2021).

Humerus fracture sub-analysis on the coronavirus period

During the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic (2020–2022), significant gender disparities were observed across multiple clinical outcomes in humerus fracture patients. Fracture rates demonstrated notable gender differences (Fig. 3A), with females consistently showing 2.5-fold higher incidence (0.98–1.06 per 1,000) compared to males (0.38 per 1,000), which remained stable throughout the study period. Female fracture rates exhibited a slight upward trend from 2020 to 2022, while male rates remained constant. Death rates were substantially elevated in males compared to females across all years (Fig. 3B). Male patients experienced death rates ranging from 9.8% to 11.3%, with peak mortality observed in 2021 (11.3%). In contrast, female death rates remained lower at 5.4–6.3%, representing approximately half the male mortality rate. Both genders showed peak mortality in 2021. Refracture rates revealed a dramatic temporal pattern in both genders (Fig. 3C). In 2020–2021, females maintained higher refracture rates (5.8–5.9%) compared to males (4.2–4.5%). However, by 2022, both groups showed an apparent decline in refracture rates; this 2022 estimate was considered descriptive and was not interpreted as a reversal of the preceding trend.

Socioeconomic disparities in humerus fracture incidence trend

Marked socioeconomic disparities in humerus fracture incidence were observed across insurance types and income quintiles throughout the 17-year study period from 2006 to 2022 (Fig. 4). Medical Aid beneficiaries consistently exhibited substantially higher fracture rates compared to all NHI groups throughout the entire observation period. Medical Aid beneficiaries demonstrated CRs ranging from approximately 1.3 per 1,000 in 2006 to 1.9 per 1,000 in 2018, with rates stabilizing around 1.6–1.7 per 1,000 in 2022 (Fig. 4A and B). SRs for Medical Aid showed similar patterns, ranging from 1.1 per 1,000 in 2006 to 1.8 per 1,000 in 2018, and remaining at approximately 1.6 per 1,000 in 2022 (Fig. 4C and D). These rates were approximately 2-fold higher than the highest workplace insurance group, with some years approaching 2.5-fold. Among regional insurance subscribers, a clear income gradient was evident, with the lowest quintile (Q1) showing approximately 2-fold higher fracture rates compared to the highest quintile (Q5) throughout the study period (Fig. 4A and C). Regional_Q1 demonstrated CRs ranging from 0.60 per 1,000 in 2006 to 0.95 per 1,000 in 2019, while Regional_Q5 maintained relatively stable rates between 0.43 and 0.69 per 1,000. The middle quintile (Q3) showed intermediate values (0.53–0.77 per 1,000), establishing a consistent dose-response relationship between income level and fracture risk across all years. Among workplace insurance subscribers, differences between quintiles were more attenuated and less consistent than among regional insurance beneficiaries (Fig. 4B and D). Workplace insurance groups showed more compressed distributions, with all quintiles (Q1, Q3, Q5) clustering between 0.42 and 0.73 per 1,000 for CRs, and between 0.53 and 0.70 per 1,000 for SRs. Nevertheless, a gradient persisted with lower-income quintiles demonstrating slightly higher fracture rates. Notably, all workplace insurance quintiles maintained substantially lower fracture rates than Medical Aid beneficiaries throughout the entire study period.

Humerus refractures result

Following a one-year wash-out period in 2006 to identify incident first fractures, refracture rates demonstrated a consistent upward trend from 2007 to 2021. The apparent decline in 2022 was considered descriptive and was not interpreted as a reversal of the long-term trend (Fig. 5A). Female patients exhibited higher refracture rates than males throughout most of the study period. Female CRs increased from approximately 18 per 1,000 in 2007 to 64 per 1,000 in 2021, while female SRs rose from approximately 17 per 1,000 in 2007 to 67 per 1,000 in 2021, representing a nearly 4-fold increase over the 15-year period. Male refracture rates showed similar temporal patterns but at lower magnitudes, with CR increasing from approximately 16 per 1,000 in 2007 to 45 per 1,000 in 2021, and SR rising from approximately 18 per 1,000 in 2007 to 44 per 1,000 in 2021. Both genders experienced a dramatic reduction in 2022, with rates dropping precipitously to approximately 6–10 per 1,000 for both crude and standardized measures. Age-stratified analysis across the entire study period (2007–2022) revealed distinct patterns in refracture risk across different age groups (Fig. 5B). Among males, the highest average refracture rate was observed in the 50-year age group (approximately 50 per 1,000), followed by the 55-year group (45 per 1,000), with progressive decline in older age groups. The 60-year group showed approximately 42 per 1,000, declining to 34 per 1,000 in the 65-year group, 24 per 1,000 in the 70-year group, 16 per 1,000 in the 75-year group, 10 per 1,000 in the 80-year group, and 9 per 1,000 in the 85-year group. Female patients demonstrated substantially higher refracture rates in younger age groups compared to males. The 55-year group showed the highest rate at approximately 76 per 1,000, followed by 74 per 1,000 in the 50-year group and 67 per 1,000 in the 60-year group. Female refracture rates decreased consistently with advancing age, declining to 49 per 1,000 in the 65-year group, 31 per 1,000 in the 70-year group, 20 per 1,000 in the 75-year group, 14 per 1,000 in the 80-year group, and 9 per 1,000 in the 85-year group. Notably, females exhibited substantially higher refracture rates than males across all age groups from 50 to 80 years, with the gender gap narrowing in the oldest age category (85 years) where rates converged at approximately 9 per 1,000.

DISCUSSION

This comprehensive, 17-year population-based study utilizing NHIS claims data provides important insights into the epidemiology, trends, and outcomes of osteoporotic humeral fractures. Analyzing 197,086 cases of humeral fractures from 2006 to 2022, the study revealed distinct temporal patterns, significant gender disparities, and profound socioeconomic inequalities in fracture incidence.

The observed incidence pattern in this study is characterized by a rapid increase from 2006 to 2010, followed by stabilization through 2022. This pattern aligns with the known epidemiology of osteoporotic fractures in aging populations, where the sharp increase in the initial phase appears attributable to heightened awareness and diagnosis of osteoporosis, expanded screening practices, and demographic shifts towards an older population in Korea. The subsequent plateau suggests that the system reached an equilibrium, or that preventive interventions and improved management strategies implemented during this period maintained a relatively stable incidence rate. The persistent 2.5-fold female predominance observed throughout the study period demonstrates a well-established biological predisposition to osteoporosis and related fractures, primarily in postmenopausal women. This gender-specific imbalance was consistently observed across all age groups from 50 to 75 years, underscoring a fundamental role for hormonal factors in the pathophysiology of osteoporotic fractures.

As demonstrated in Fig. 6, advanced age emerged as the most powerful predictor of 1-year mortality, with hazard ratios increasing exponentially from 2.19 at age 60 to 11.53 at age ≥ 85 compared to 50-year-old patients. This suggests that biological fragility rather than technical factors determines postoperative outcomes in humerus fractures. Socioeconomic factors and insurance type showed minimal impact on 1-year mortality risk following fracture, but demonstrated substantial influence on fracture incidence itself. indicating that access to surgery is not the primary barrier to survival in this population. These findings underscore the need for aggressive geriatric assessment and optimization strategies in elderly patients presenting with humerus fractures.

The consistent decline in one-year mortality rates following humerus fractures, particularly evident in females who experienced approximately a 43% reduction in standardized mortality from 2006 to 2021 (9.6% to 5.5%), represents an encouraging finding that likely reflects improvements in surgical techniques, perioperative care, infection prevention, and comprehensive post-fracture management.14 Previous studies have also examined the association between surgical treatment and mortality following humerus fractures.15 However, male mortality rates remained persistently approximately 2-fold higher than female rates throughout the entire study period. This male mortality disadvantage likely reflects the complex interplay of multiple interconnected biological and behavioral factors. Male patients with osteoporotic humerus fractures characteristically exhibit higher burdens of chronic comorbidities, including cardiovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and diabetes mellitus conditions that substantially elevate the risk of post-fracture mortality.

These findings are consistent with prior literature. Korean national-level studies have documented similar male excess mortality in osteoporotic fractures, and international meta-analyses consistently demonstrate that across multiple fracture types including hip and vertebral fractures, male mortality rates are persistently 1.5–3 fold higher than female rates.16 This consistent pattern across fracture sites and geographic regions suggests fundamental sex-based biological differences in fracture-related mortality independent of fracture location.17 The substantial fluctuations observed in male mortality during the early study period from 2006 to 2008 may reflect the implementation and refinement of evidence-based treatment protocols and quality improvement initiatives that subsequently stabilized outcomes. These findings emphasize the critical importance of targeted interventions to reduce post-fracture mortality in both genders, particularly in males.

Our most significant finding concerns the pronounced socioeconomic disparities in humerus fracture incidence, which revealed a compelling inverse relationship between income level and fracture risk. Medical Aid beneficiaries, representing the lowest socioeconomic stratum, demonstrated the highest fracture rates, approximately 2-fold higher than the highest workplace insurance group, with some years approaching 2.5-fold. This disparity persisted after age standardization, suggesting that age composition alone did not explain the observed socioeconomic differences. The income gradient was most evident among regional insurance beneficiaries and was more attenuated and less consistent among workplace insurance beneficiaries. These findings suggest that lower-income populations face multiple compounded challenges including limited access to preventive services, inadequate nutritional intake, reduced physical activity, higher prevalence of comorbidities, delayed diagnosis and treatment, and potentially different patterns of occupational hazards that increase fall risk.

The pronounced socioeconomic gradient observed in our study likely reflects multiple interconnected mechanisms that influence fracture risk independent of age or healthcare access disparities. Lifestyle factors associated with lower socioeconomic status including inadequate nutritional intake, reduced physical activity, and higher smoking prevalence contribute to impaired bone health and increased vulnerability to fractures.18 Additionally, lower-income populations may reside in more hazardous environments with greater fall risk, compounded by reduced capacity for environmental modification or home safety improvements. Critically, disparities in access to preventive care, including osteoporosis screening, early diagnosis, and pharmacologic treatment, result in delayed identification and management of osteoporosis before fracture occurs. These findings are consistent with established patterns in other osteoporotic fracture cohorts: socioeconomic gradients have been documented in hip fracture, vertebral fracture, and wrist fracture studies, demonstrating that income-related disparities in fracture burden are not unique to humerus fractures but rather represent a systemic challenge in osteoporosis prevention and management across all fracture types.

The substantial and persistent refracture rates throughout the study period, particularly the near 4-fold increase in female standardized refracture rates from 17 per 1,000 in 2007 to 67 per 1,000 in 2021 (following a one-year wash-out period in 2006), raise critical concerns about the adequacy of post-fracture prevention strategies and osteoporosis treatment in the Korean healthcare system.19 The higher refracture rates in younger age groups for both males and females suggest that younger individuals with humerus fractures may have more severe underlying osteoporosis or may face greater challenges in adhering to prolonged preventive medications and lifestyle modifications. The progressive decline in refracture risk with advancing age may reflect either survivor bias, improved bone quality in older patients who have survived without refractures, or differential treatment intensity across age groups.

A paradoxical finding emerged whereby in refracture. relatively younger patients aged 50–65 years demonstrated substantially higher refracture rates. This pattern contrary to expectation reflects multiple mechanisms. Younger patients with osteoporotic fractures may have more aggressive disease biology and elevated bone turnover, and may experience difficulty maintaining medication adherence due to extended life expectancy and prolonged treatment duration, and may also have difficulty adapting to lifestyle modifications. To reduce secondary fracture risk, healthcare systems need to implement prevention programs that include strengthened education regarding medication adherence, regular monitoring of bone turnover markers, and supervised rehabilitation that includes fall-risk assessment and environmental modification (Fig. 5B).

The apparent reduction in refracture rates in 2022 requires cautious interpretation. Given the possibility of incomplete claims capture or processing delays, the 2022 estimate was considered descriptive and should not be interpreted as a reversal of the established 2007–2021 trend.

However, certain limitations warrant acknowledgment. Administrative claims data inherently underestimate incidence when patients forgo healthcare, particularly among lower-income groups. The inability to assess osteoporosis severity, medication adherence, or specific behavioral risk factors limits mechanistic understanding of identified socioeconomic disparities. Residual confounding by unmeasured factors such as occupation, lifestyle, and comorbidity patterns is possible despite socioeconomic stratification. Regarding the 2022 observation specifically, administrative claims processing typically requires 90–120 days, and refractures occurring in late 2022 may not be captured until 2023 data retrieval. Single-year estimates with limited absolute event numbers are statistically unstable. Continued pandemic-related disruptions in data processing through 2022 may have further compromised completeness. Consequently, the 2022 refracture rate should not be interpreted as reversing the established 15-year upward trend without confirmation from subsequent years' data.

A 17-year study of 197,086 humeral fractures found that despite stabilization in incidence and an improvement in female mortality, Medical Aid beneficiaries had the highest fracture incidence, approximately 2-fold higher than the highest workplace insurance group, revealing significant socioeconomic disparities even after age standardization. The refracture rate quadrupled between 2007 and 2021, suggesting inadequate secondary prevention and the need for systematic fracture referral services. These findings highlight the urgent need for socioeconomically targeted prevention programs and improved osteoporosis management in the elderly population in Korea.
