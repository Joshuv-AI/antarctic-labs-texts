Development and evaluation of novel Brucella diagnostic antigen protein ST
Authors: Juan Shi, Tingting Tian, Xinxin Qi, Huidong Shi, Kaiyu Shang, Zhengwei Yin, Jianbing Ding, Ruixue Xu, Fuling Pu, Mingzhe Li, Junyu Kuang, Yuejie Zhu, Fengbo Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrucellosis continues to pose a significant zoonotic challenge, necessitating the development of improved diagnostic solutions. This study innovatively integrates bioinformatics predictions with prokaryotic expression techniques to create novel diagnostic antigens for the detection of Brucella melitensis.MethodsOur study systematically analyzed four outer membrane proteins (OMP10, OMP25, VirB8, and VirB10). B-cell epitope prediction was conducted using the IEDB and ABCpred algorithms. A chimeric antigen was designed by fusing these four proteins in the sequence OMP10-OMP25-VirB8-VirB10. The recombinant protein was expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) and purified using nickel-affinity chromatography. The diagnostic performance of the purified chimeric antigen was evaluated through indirect ELISA, utilizing 180 brucellosis sera, 20 healthy control sera, and 88 sera from other infections.ResultsIn our study, we successfully predicted and designed a novel recombinant Brucella diagnostic antigen protein, named OMP10-OMP25-VirB8-VirB10, referred to as the ST protein. We assessed its performance as a serological diagnostic biomarker for brucellosis using the indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. The results demonstrated that the ST protein exhibited a detection sensitivity of 98.33% and a specificity of 85.22%, with an AUC value of 0.9802.ConclusionThe ST protein, a fusion of four proteins derived from Brucella, demonstrates significant diagnostic potential in ELISA, offering a novel approach for the accurate diagnosis of brucellosis. This discovery provides an innovative perspective for enhancing early brucellosis screening in endemic regions.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12393-1.

Introduction

Brucella is a Gram-negative, facultative intracellular bacterium responsible for brucellosis, a significant zoonotic infectious disease globally [1]. Human infection typically occurs through direct contact with infected animal tissues or through the consumption of undercooked meat and unpasteurized dairy products [2]. Epidemiologically, brucellosis remains prevalent in the Mediterranean regions, the Middle East, Africa, and Asia, with Central Asia emerging as a new endemic area [3]. In China, the incidence has shown a gradual upward trend from the 1990s to the early 21st century, primarily concentrated in northern regions; however, southern areas are experiencing intensified outbreaks, which pose substantial public health challenges [4].

The disease presents with a variety of clinical manifestations, including headache, recurrent fever, myalgia, night sweats, weight loss, nausea, vomiting, and anorexia [5, 6]. In advanced stages, severe complications may arise, such as sacroiliitis, osteomyelitis, spondylitis, septic arthritis, and epidural abscess [7]. Current clinical management relies on antibiotics like rifampicin and doxycycline, though nonspecific symptoms often lead to misdiagnosis and treatment delays [8]. Therefore, accurate diagnosis is essential for timely intervention and effective transmission control.

Currently, the tube agglutination test (SAT), rose bengal plate agglutination test (RBPT), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) are widely employed serological methods for diagnosing brucellosis [9]. The SAT, known for its high specificity and accuracy [10],is the preferred method for early diagnosis. However, it is susceptible to false negatives due to the ‘prozone effect’—a phenomenon in which excessive specific antibodies in serum bind to antigen epitopes in a monovalent manner, thereby preventing the formation of cross-linked agglutination complexes [11].RBPT, with high sensitivity and ease of operation, is a good tool for preliminary screening but has a risk of false positives [12]. ELISA, recognized for its high sensitivity and specificity, is extensively utilized in large-scale screening and epidemiological studies, where the selection of the diagnostic antigen is crucial [13].

The design of ELISA diagnostic antigens for brucellosis currently focuses on the Brucella outer membrane protein (OMP) family [14], articularly the proteins related to the type IV secretion system (T4SS).In the serological diagnosis of brucellosis, OMP25, OMP10, VirB8, and VirB10, serving as antigens, hold significant diagnostic advantages that are backed by solid scientific evidence. When it comes to outer membrane proteins (OMPs), OMP25 is well - recognized for its high immunogenicity, surface exposure, specificity, and stability [15–17].OMP10, despite its smaller molecular weight, features diverse epitopes and strong immunogenicity, and it plays an active role in regulating the host immune response [17–19]. As for the type IV secretion system (T4SS) proteins, VirB8 and VirB10 are essential virulence factors in Brucella [20], and their antibody levels are strongly associated with active infection [21–23].The combined use of these antigens enables comprehensive monitoring of antibody dynamics across different stages of infection, thereby ignificantly enhancing diagnostic sensitivity and specificity.

This study innovatively integrates bioinformatics technology to conduct high-throughput predictive analysis of B-cell epitopes in Brucella outer membrane proteins (OMP10, OMP25) and key proteins of the type IV secretion system (VirB8, VirB10). Based on this analysis, a novel recombinant diagnostic antigen ST protein was designed and synthesized. After efficient expression via a prokaryotic expression system, the high-purity target protein was isolated using HIS affinity nickel column purification technology. The diagnostic performance of the recombinant antigen was rigorously and systematically validated and evaluated through ELISA technology.

In summary, as research on Brucella-specific antigenic proteins continues to advance, diagnostic methods for brucellosis are being continuously improved.The combined application of these antigens not only enhances diagnostic accuracy but also improves sensitivity, significantly boosting both the sensitivity and specificity of brucellosis diagnosis.

Methods

Analysis of physical and chemical properties of protein

In this study, we evaluated the potential of the candidate proteins Omp10, Omp25, VirB8, and VirB10 as diagnostic antigens through bioinformatic analysis. The amino acid sequence data for these proteins were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). We utilized ANTIGENpro (version 2010, https://scratch.proteomics.ics.uci.edu/), ProtParam (version 2023.5, http://web.expasy.org/protparam/), and SOLpro (version 2009, http://scratch.proteomics.ics.uci.edu/) to predict the antigenicity, hydrophilicity, and stability of these proteins. Furthermore, we employed the NCBI BlastP database (version 2.13.0+, https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi?PAGE=Proteins) to compare the homology of these candidate proteins with the protein database of Homo sapiens (taxid 9606), setting the comparison parameter to E > 0.05.

Signal peptide prediction

We employed the TMHMM server (version 2.0, https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/service.php?TMHMM-2.0) and the SignalP server (version 5.0, https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/service.php?SignalP-5.0) to conduct a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of the candidate proteins Omp10, Omp25, VirB8, and VirB10. TMHMM 2.0 was utilized to predict transmembrane regions within these proteins, while SignalP 5.0 was employed to identify signal peptides and their cleavage sites. Through these analyses, we aimed to elucidate the topology of the candidate proteins and assess whether they contain signal peptide sequences that are cleaved during maturation. In predicting protein structures and epitopes, we pay particular attention to excluding signal peptide sequences, as these sequences are typically not retained following protein maturation and do not contribute to the formation of the protein’s tertiary structure.

B cell epitope screening

To identify the most immunogenic B cell epitopes, both linear and conformational, we leveraged the IEDB online platform (version 2.25, http://tools.iedb.org/main/bcell/). Rigorous assessments of the selected B cell epitopes were performed to verify their hydrophilicity, stability, and antigenicity. The epitopes’ stability was evaluated using their thermal stability index, with a threshold of less than 40 for selection. Antigenicity was determined by the immunogenicity index, where epitopes with an index exceeding 0.8 were favored. Hydrophilicity was assessed through the surface hydrophilicity index of the epitopes.

Recombinant antigen construction

The predicted six linear B cell epitopes and five B cell conformational epitopes were linked together using the linker “KK” to designed a new Brucella recombinant diagnostic antigenic protein, named ST protein. The physical and chemical properties of this recombinant protein, including its molecular formula, isoelectric point, stability, hydrophilicity, and antigenicity, were analyzed using ProtParam (version 2023.5, http://web.ExPASy.org/protparam/) software. Additionally, VaxiJen (version 2.0, http://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html) online software was employed to further predict its antigenicity.

Solubility prediction

The solubility of recombinant proteins directly influences their expression efficiency in host cells. Proteins that remain insoluble may aggregate into inclusion bodies, which leads to reduced yields of active protein. In contrast, highly soluble proteins can be extracted and purified more readily from cell lysates [24]. To assess the solubility of recombinant proteins in E. coli expression systems, we utilized Protein-Sol (https://protein-sol.manchester.ac.uk) for predictive analysis. By comparing the amino acid sequence of the recombinant protein with the E. coli (Escherichia coli)proteome, we can predict the solubility of the recombinant protein and obtain a solubility ratio. This ratio indicates the difference in solubility between the recombinant protein and the corresponding E. coli protein.

Structure prediction

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the protein structural characteristics of the ST protein, we utilized two online analysis tools: SOPMA (version 2023.5, http://npsa-pbil.ibcp.fr/cgibin/npsa_automat.pl?page=/NPSA/npsa_sopma.html) and Robetta (version 2024.3, https://robetta.bakerlab.org/myqueue.php) to predict and analyze its structural properties. SOPMA is a sequence-based protein structure and function prediction tool that, by inputting the amino acid sequence of the ST protein, can predict the proportions of α-helix, β-sheet, and random coil in the protein, as well as their distribution. The predictions from Robetta provide a detailed three-dimensional structural model of the ST protein, facilitating further analysis of its biological function and potential antigenicity. Additionally, GalaxyRefine software (version 2023.2) was employed to refine the three-dimensional structure. Finally, we used Discover Studio (version 2020.2) and the ProSA web service (version 2023.4, https://prosa.services.came.sbg.ac.at/prosa.php/) to evaluate the quality of the three-dimensional structural model.

IgG molecular docking

We used H-dock molecular docking software(version 2.3.0, http://hdock.iasbs.ac.ir/) to verify the binding affinity of the ST protein with human immunoglobulin G (IgG). H-dock allows us to understand the precise manner in which the ST protein binds to IgG by modeling the interactions between these proteins. To provide a more intuitive demonstration of these molecular interactions, we utilized PyMOL(version 2.5.1, https://pymol.org/), an open-source molecular visualization system. ELISA is a widely utilized biochemical analysis technology that relies on the specific interaction between antigens and antibodies, as well as the color reaction catalyzed by enzyme-labeled antibodies or antigens to detect target substances.

Prokaryotic expression of recombinant proteins

To enhance the expression of the ST protein in host cells, codons were optimized using NGCodon to align with the host’s preference, adjust GC content, prevent mRNA secondary structures, and minimize rare tRNA use. The optimized gene was constructed into the pET-19b vector to form the recombinant plasmid pET-19b-ST protein by Xinjiang Youkang Biological Company (Urumqi, China). Plasmid transformation used the heat shock method. The dried plasmid powder was centrifuged, resuspended in distilled water to a concentration of 50 ng/µl, and mixed with BL21(DE3) PLysS competent cells (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China; Cat. C03012). After heat shock at 42 °C for 90 s, the cells were placed in ice water and then cultured in LB medium (Hopebio, Qingdao, China; Cat. L001) to recover. The experimental procedure was conducted as follows: Bacterial colonies were selected using LB solid medium containing ampicillin (100 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA; Cat. A9538) and chloromycin (34 µg/mL, Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat. C003) for dual antibiotic resistance screening. After 16–18 h of culture at 37 °C, monoclonal colonies were obtained through three-zone streaking method. Colonies with 1–2 mm diameter were then inoculated into 5 mL of liquid LB medium containing the same antibiotics. The integrity of plasmid inserts was verified by PCR to exclude plasmid loss.

When the bacterial culture reached an absorbance value of about 0.6, IPTG (Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside, Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat. ST001) was added to induce expression. Different temperatures (16 °C, 25 °C, 37 °C) and IPTG concentrations (0.5 M, 1 M) were tested to optimize expression conditions. Bacterial liquid samples were collected before and after induction for analysis.

Purification of recombinant proteins

The collected precipitate was dissolved in a cell disruption solution (5 mM Tris-HCl, Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat. T001, 150 mM NaCl, Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat. S001, PMSF) and ultrasonicated on ice. After sonication, the solution was centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 1 h to obtain a clear supernatant, which was then used for SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and Coomassie staining. The supernatant was filtered and loaded onto a nickel column. The column was washed with binding buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate buffer, 0.5 M sodium chloride, 20 mM imidazole), and elution was performed using elution buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate buffer, 0.5 M sodium chloride, 500 mM imidazole). Western blot experiments were conducted to verify the purification efficiency of the recombinant protein. Protein concentration was measured using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA; Cat. ND-2000) spectrophotometer. Finally, the purified ST protein was electrophoresed on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA; Cat. 161–0183), transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Burlington, USA; Cat. IPVH00010), washed three times with TBST buffer containing 0.05% Tween 20 (Amersham Biosciences, Little Chalfont, UK; Cat. GE2000) for 10 min each time, and subjected to Western blotting with rabbit anti-His tag polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, Rosemont, USA; Cat. 13844-1-AP) and HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, USA; Cat. sc-2004).

Blood samples collections

To address cross-reactions between Brucella and other Gram-negative bacilli in immunological testing, this study collected serum samples from brucellosis patients and others infected with pathogens like Escherichia coli, Haemophilus influenzae, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Proteus mirabilis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Streptococcus viridans and fever of unknown cause. By collecting these serum samples, we aimed to determine the specificity of the ELISA method developed in this study for detecting Brucella, and to differentiate it from other Gram-negative bacilli in immunological detection, thereby reducing the impact of cross-reactivity on diagnostic results. The samples originated from the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University, with the research protocol approved by the hospital’s Medical Ethics Committee (approval number K202409-17) and conducted in line with Helsinki Declaration principles.

Optimization of coating concentration and serum dilution for ST protein ELISA

In this study, checkerboard titration was employed to systematically optimize experimental conditions. The specific process involved using a 96-well polystyrene microplate to perform serial dilutions of purified ST protein from an initial concentration of 20 µg/mL down to 0.625 µg/mL via a 2-fold dilution. Positive reference serum was also serially diluted from 1:50 to 1:800. A preliminary experiment on matrix interference identified that the optimal blocking solution was PBS buffer (pH 7.4) supplemented with 5% skimmed milk (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA; Cat. 21258). Following three independent replicate experiments, the optimal ST protein coating concentration was determined to be 10 µg/mL (coating conditions: overnight at 4 °C). The working serum dilution was set at 1:100 (diluent: PBS with 0.05% Tween-20 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA; Cat. P1379)).

Specificity determination of the ST-ELISA method

The experimental procedure was as follows: ST protein was diluted to 10 µg/mL in coating solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat. B0001), and 100 µL was added to each well of a 96-well plate (Corning, New York, USA; Cat. 3595). The plate was incubated overnight at 4 °C, then washed three times with PBST (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA; Cat. 28360). Blocking was performed with 5% skimmed milk for 2 h at 37 °C. Sera were diluted to 1:100 and incubated for 1 h. HRP-labeled goat anti-human IgG (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA; Cat. A0499; 1:5000 dilution) was added and incubated again. After washing, TMB substrate (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; Cat. S0013) was added, incubated in the dark, and the reaction was stopped by adding 50 µL of 2 M H₂SO₄(Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA; Cat. S2784), followed by immediate measurement of absorbance at 450 nm (OD₄₅₀). The experiment was repeated three times independently, and the average and standard deviation were calculated. The cut-off value (threshold for positive determination) was determined as the mean OD450 of the negative control (unimmunized serum or blank buffer) plus three times the standard deviation (Mean + 3SD).

In addition, parallel comparison experiments were carried out using both a commercial Brucella antibody ELISA kit (Jianglai Biotechnology, Beijing, China; Cat. KB1527A) and the laboratory-developed ST recombinant protein detection system.

Statistical analysis

Version 6.05 of GraphPad Prism software was used to draw point plots and perform receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.

Results

The physicochemical properties of candidate proteins were analyzed

Bioinformatics analysis of the four candidate proteins—Omp10, Omp25, VirB8, and VirB10—revealed that these proteins exhibit favorable antigenicity, hydrophilicity, and stability, making them suitable as diagnostic antigens and promising candidates for protein design. Furthermore, a comparison of these proteins with the human protein database (Homo sapiens, taxid 9606) indicated no homology with human proteins (Table 1).

The analysis of the transmembrane helix and signal peptide characteristics of the candidate proteins

According to the results obtained from the TMHMM 2.0 and SignalP 5.0 servers, all 126 amino acids of Omp10 are predicted to be located outside the cell membrane, with residues 19–20 identified as potential signal peptides. Similarly, the 213 amino acids of Omp25 are also predicted to be positioned outside the cell membrane, with residues 23–24 likely functioning as signal peptides. In contrast, residues 1–43 of VirB8 are situated within the membrane, while residues 44–66 comprise the transmembrane region; residues 67–239 are located outside the membrane and are not expected to contain a signal peptide. For VirB10, residues 1–32 are located inside the membrane, residues 33–52 are part of the transmembrane region, and residues 53–380 are positioned outside the membrane, with no signal peptide anticipated (Fig. 1A and B). When predicting protein structure and epitopes, it is essential to exclude signal peptide sequences. Therefore, it is necessary to remove the corresponding signal peptide sequences from Omp10 and Omp25.

Prediction of B cell epitope

Using indirect ELISA principles and B cell epitope recognition mechanisms via B cell receptor (BCR) and antibodies, we screened B cell epitopes and developed a method to detect bacterial antibodies by binding anti-Brucella antigens. We first predicted B cell epitope distribution in four candidate diagnostic antigens (Fig. 2). To design the optimal diagnostic antigen, we evaluated the hydrophilicity, stability, and antigenicity of these epitopes. We selected six B cell linear epitopes (LBEs) and five B cell conformational epitopes (CBEs) with hydrophilicity below 0, stability below 40, and antigenicity above 0.8. These are detailed in the 3D structure column of Table 2, which includes the 3D structures of both six B cell linear epitopes(LBEs) and five B cell conformational epitopes(CBEs) (Fig. 3). These selected epitopes were then used to construct the ST protein.

Prediction, refinement and evaluation of tertiary structure

Predicting the tertiary structure of the ST antigen holds significant implications for its application as a diagnostic antigen. It not only aids in identifying critical epitopes and binding sites but also evaluates antigen stability and immunogenicity, thereby improving the sensitivity and specificity of diagnostic methods. By integrating bioinformatics tools with experimental validation, the development of diagnostic reagents and methods targeting the ST antigen can be achieved more efficiently. The RoseTTAFold software was utilized to perform predictive modeling of the tertiary structure of the ST protein (Fig. 7A), resulting in a model with high confidence. The hydrogen bonds of the ST protein were visualized using Discover Studio, which aligned well with the secondary structure (Fig. 7B). After refining the three-level structure of the ST protein, the final model demonstrated a GDT-HA parameter of 0.969, an RMSD of 0.344, a MolProbity score of 1.098, a collision score of 3.7, and a favorable area residual of 91.1% in the Ramachandran diagram (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, the ProSA-web Z score of -5.92 indicates that the overall quality of the model is good.

Docking of RDA with IgG molecules

Molecular docking is a cost-effective affinity determination alternative, useful for screening candidate antigens or antibodies [25]. While it may not fully mirror real - world affinity, the antigen regions it identifies aid in designing synthetic antigens and optimizing vaccines [26]. This study is grounded in the principle that ELISA utilizes the specific binding of known antigens and antibodies, along with an enzyme labeled on either the antibody or antigen, to catalyze a color reaction with a specific substrate for target detection. Molecular docking was performed using H-dock to evaluate the binding affinity of the designed ST protein to human IgG. Additionally, PyMOL v2.5.0 was used for visualization (Fig. 8). The docking score obtained from H-dock was − 238.71, accompanied by a Confidence Score of 0.8550 and a Ligand RMSD (Å) of 55.35. These results suggest that the ST protein demonstrates a favorable affinity for human IgG antibodies.

Results of Indirect Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay(iELISA)

This study systematically evaluated serological diagnostic biomarkers for brucellosis using the indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Table 4). The experiment involved 248 serum samples, including 180 from brucellosis patients, 88 from non-brucellosis patients, and 20 from healthy controls (Supplementary Material 1). As illustrated in Fig. 10A and C, dot plots were used to visualize the distribution of OD450 values across the different groups, providing a foundational dataset for the subsequent analysis of diagnostic efficacy. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis revealed distinct diagnostic characteristics between the ST protein(Fig. 10B) and the commercial kits (Fig. 10D). The ST protein demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.9802 (95% CI: 0.9680 to 0.9925), which, despite being insignificantly higher, still showed a minimal yet statistically significant advantage over the commercial kit’s AUC of 0.9888 (95% CI: 0.9812 to 0.9964). This slight superiority might be attributed to the ST protein’s enhanced ability to recognize low-concentration antibodies. Further evaluation of the diagnostic parameters showed that the ST protein exhibited a detection rate of 98.33%, surpassing the commercial kit’s 95.00% (Δ = 3.33%). In terms of specificity, the commercial kit demonstrated higher accuracy with 87.50% compared to the ST protein’s 85.22% (Δ = 2.28%). These results indicate that the ST protein, with its high sensitivity exceeding 98%, is especially well-suited for early screening in regions where brucellosis is endemic, enabling the detection of infections at stages with lower antibody concentrations, which could potentially facilitate earlier alerts and interventions. This approach will enhance the diagnostic accuracy of brucellosis and provide a more reliable detection method for clinical application.

Cross - Reactivity Evaluation in Brucellosis Serological Testing

In a study, 88 serum samples were collected from febrile patients who did not exhibit clinical manifestations of brucellosis. Cross - reactivity was assessed using iELISA with preset cutoff values. The results showed distinct patterns between two detection methods. When using ST protein as antigen, cross - reactivity occurred in 13 cases. Specifically, 5 cases were linked to Escherichia coli, 5 to Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and the remaining 3 cases involved 1 case of Staphylococcus aureus and 2 cases of unexplained fever. In contrast, cross - reactivity was observed in 11 cases with the commercial kit. These cases were spread across Klebsiella pneumoniae (2 cases), Haemophilus influenzae (1 case), E. coli (2 cases), S. aureus (2 cases), Streptococcus mutans (2 cases), and unexplained fever (2 cases). Discussion

Brucellosis, a zoonotic disease, poses a significant threat to animal husbandry in endemic regions and presents serious public health challenges.

Early diagnosis of infected animals and humans is critical for reducing the incidence of brucellosis [17]. Various techniques are available for diagnosing human brucellosis. Routine blood culture techniques are regarded as the gold standard due to their accuracy. Modern automated blood culture systems can effectively detect acute brucellosis cases within a standard 5 to 7-day culture cycle. However, blood culture technology has several drawbacks, including the risk of false-negative results, prolonged testing cycles, health risks to laboratory personnel, and substantial demands on laboratory equipment and environmental conditions [27]. In contrast, nucleic acid amplification technology demonstrates higher sensitivity and specificity, offers improved safety, and enables rapid disease diagnosis. It is crucial to note that even after a patient has fully recovered, a molecular test may still yield a positive result [28].

In resource-limited endemic areas, the implementation of existing diagnostic tools can pose significant challenges. In such contexts, the serological diagnostic method of iELISA holds particular significance. This study developed a protein-based detection method to identify Brucella antibodies, which was evaluated using iELISA technology. Given that brucellosis patients often present with gastrointestinal issues and fever, we selected patients with other bacterial infections, such as Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus, and Salmonella infections, along with those experiencing unexplained fever, as the control group.

The selection of diagnostic antigens is crucial. Numerous Brucella immunogenic proteins, which are valuable for serodiagnosis and subunit vaccine development, have been identified by researchers both domestically and internationally. Among these, outer membrane proteins such as OMP10 and OMP25 demonstrate significant immunogenicity and protective effects under laboratory conditions [19]. OMP25 and OMP10, characterized by high conservation and excellent detection properties, are particularly suitable as core diagnostic antigens [17–19, 29]. VirB proteins not only contribute to the virulence mechanisms of pathogens but also interact with host cells. These dual roles make them valuable diagnostic antigens, providing a direct reflection of Brucella infection status and indicating considerable potential for clinical applications [30]. VirB8, an early-secreted protein, can be recognized by the host immune system during the early stages of infection and has demonstrated diagnostic value. VirB10 likely affects antigenic epitope exposure indirectly by participating in T4SS complex formation, showing potential as a diagnostic antigen [23, 31].Compared to other candidate antigens, they show distinct comparative advantages. For instance, BP26 is prone to false - positive results [32, 33], L7/L12 exhibits cross - reactivity with Helicobacter pylori [34], PrpA has insufficient immunogenicity [35], and SOD is susceptible to nonspecific interference [36]. The diagnostic benefits of multi antigen - detection using OMP25, OMP10, VirB8, and VirB10 are remarkable.

Bioinformatics plays a pivotal role in ELISA diagnostic antigen design by employing various tools and methodologies to enhance antigen performance and diagnostic accuracy. Tools like Ellipro predict linear and conformational epitopes, hereby facilitating the selection of highly immunogenic regions that improve the binding capacity of recombinant proteins with antibodies [37, 38]. Meanwhile, structural prediction tools such as RoseTTAFold analyze antigen tertiary structures, ensuring proper conformational integrity and functionality during recombinant expression. Additionally, codon optimization tailored to the expression system’s preferences (e.g., E. coli or mammalian systems) boosts protein yield, while fusion tags (e.g., His, GST) are carefully evaluated to minimize interference with antigen immunogenicity.Virtual screening technologies have supplanted traditional trial-and-error experiments, while high-throughput analytical methods expedite the evaluation of candidate antigens. These advancements significantly reduce both the timelines and costs associated with research and experimental development (R&D). Furthermore, these strategies not only streamline the design of ELISA antigens but also enhance diagnostic sensitivity and specificity, thus providing robust technical support for the development of high-performance ELISA diagnostic reagents.

B cell epitopes can be classified as linear or conformational. Linear epitopes consist of continuous amino acid sequences within the antigen [39], whereas conformational epitopes are defined by the three-dimensional structure of the protein, where amino acids are spatially proximate but not necessarily sequential [40]. Bioinformatics tools play a crucial role in the identification of these epitopes, as they not only reduce the time and costs associated with experimental screening but also enhance the accuracy of epitope prediction [41].

The bioinformatics analysis of the structure of ST protein is crucial for understanding its function and stability. Common secondary structures include α-helices and β-sheets, while random coils are typically found in flexible regions [42]. SOPMA predictions give initial insights into ST protein’s secondary structure(45), while Robetta uses advanced algorithms to predict tertiary structures(46), aiding in understanding functions and designing drug targets. Molecular docking predicts optimal binding between molecules like proteins and ligands [43]. Using H-dock and PyMOL for docking and visualization shows ST protein can bind to human IgG antibodies, suggesting its potential as a diagnostic antigen [44].

This study used the indirect ELISA to evaluate the serological diagnostic biomarkers of brucellosis. The experiment included a total of 248 serum samples, comprising 180 samples from patients diagnosed with Brucella, 88 samples from patients without Brucella, and 20 samples from healthy controls. The results indicated that both the ST protein and the commercial kit demonstrated significant diagnostic efficacy for brucellosis. Specifically, the area under the curve (AUC) for the ST protein was 0.9802, which was slightly lower than the AUC of the commercial kit at 0.9888. Notably, the ST protein was particularly effective in detecting low-concentration antibodies. Furthermore, the ST protein exhibited a high positive rate of 98.33%, surpassing the commercial kit’s rate of 95.00%, which suggests a higher sensitivity for brucellosis detection. However, regarding specificity, the commercial kit achieved an accuracy of 87.50%, while the ST protein had an accuracy of 85.22%.

In detecting brucellosis, the ST protein showed high sensitivity but had significant cross - reactivity with Mycobacterium tuberculosis and E. coli due to shared antigenic epitopes. This might be due to residual host proteins in the purification process causing cross - reactivity with E. coli, and the conserved membrane proteins of Brucella overlapping with Mycobacterium tuberculosis antigens. Also, the commercial kit had limitations like a broad cross - reactivity spectrum and possible non - specific binding from polyclonal antibodies. Yet, as a conserved antigen, the ST protein’s high sensitivity makes it suitable for early - stage screening. For example, the ST - protein - based ELISA had over 98% sensitivity, a significant advantage over the traditional agglutination test. Additionally, the E. coli expression system for the ST protein facilitates low-cost mass production, making it ideal for grassroots implementation. This approach is expected to enhance the diagnostic accuracy of brucellosis and provide a more reliable detection method for clinical applications.

Conclusion

In summary, this study successfully predicted and designed a novel Brucella diagnostic antigen protein, referred to as ST protein. Following prokaryotic expression and purification, ELISA experiments confirmed that this protein exhibits high diagnostic sensitivity and specificity, thereby establishing a new foundation for the diagnosis of brucellosis. This research offers fresh insights and innovative strategies for the prevention and treatment of brucellosis.
