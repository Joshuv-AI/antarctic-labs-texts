The Candida auris Infection After the COVID‐19 Pandemic Seems to be an Urgent Public Health Emergency: A Call to Attention
Authors: Md Ashrafur Rahman, Evangelos Victoros, Yeasna Shanjana, Marie Roke Thomas, Md Rabiul Islam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsInconsequential diseases can sometimes become extremely dangerous through mutation. Antifungal resistance has increased by 24%, and resistance only due to Candida auris (C. auris) species have increased by 60%. Here, we aimed to assess the knowledge of antifungal treatment and preventative measures to mitigate the consequences of infections caused by C. auris.MethodsWe performed a comprehensive literature search and gathered information for this review from publicly available published articles. We used C. auris, C. auris infection, mycoses, and antifungal resistance as search terms in Google Scholar, PubMed, and Scopus. We extracted the relevant information from the available article after careful evaluation.ResultsThe genus C. auris was especially difficult to diagnose, as it was often mistaken for other types of yeast and led to incorrect treatment. The only effective method for diagnosing C. auris is through blood culture testing in laboratories and PCR tests. Because of its mutation, C. auris has grown increasingly resistant to all three classes of antifungals, with almost all strains resistant to fluconazole. These resistances were traced back to multiple‐point mutations in certain genes, such as ERG11 in the case of fluconazole and amphotericin B. In addition, C. auris is phylogenetically related to C. haemulonii, an emerging pathogen notably resistant to antifungals. So, it may be an evolutionary resistance that occurred earlier but has only begun to spread now. Echinoderm, a reliable treatment for C. auris infections showed ineffectively against FKS1 and ERG3 gene mutated Candida strains. Newly developed antifungal agents, like Ibrexafungerp, showed promising results against echinocandin‐resistant strains in clinical trials.ConclusionWithout vaccines and effective treatment, its capacity to mutate and spread has detrimental effects on humans. Therefore, extensive research on drug development, quick, reliable diagnosis, and effective strategies for disease prevention and treatment are required to mitigate the health impact of C. auris.Keywords: antifungal resistance, C. auris, Candida auris, Candida auris infection, Covid‐19 pandemic, infectious disease, mycoses

Background

During the Covid‐19 pandemic, a significant occurrence of Candida auris (C. auris) colonization or infections among patients in several geographical locations around the world has been reported [1, 2, 3]. Covid‐19 acts as a potential bed for increasing the risks of coinfections with microorganisms [4, 5, 6]. Data from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) showed that there has been a 15% increase in antimicrobial resistance among seven pathogens following the peak of the Covid‐19 pandemic in 2020 [7, 8]. Patients with severe Covid‐19 need intensive interventions, such as mechanical ventilation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, continuous renal replacement therapy, glucocorticoids, and intravenous immune‐globulin therapy [3]. These could cause long‐term hospital stays and high resistance to antifungal drugs, which elevates the risk of co‐infection by fungi [6, 9]. Antifungal‐resistant threats have also increased significantly, especially the C. auris strain which increased resistance by 60% during the year 2020 in comparison to a 2019 report [10]. A newly discovered isolate caused by C. auris was first discovered in 2009 and has been noted to be recently spreading in the United States. In recent years, there has been an increase of 314% in cases in the United States between 2020 and 2022 [11, 12]. In 2020, there were 757 clinical cases regarding C. auris, jumping to 2377 clinical cases in 2022 [11, 12]. Additionally, the CDC stated that C. auris infections have been reported from over 45–50 countries, enough to make it an international concern [4]. Due to its strong resistance to antifungal medications, infectious pathogenic properties, and vaccine unavailability, C. auris has quickly spread throughout the United States. Therefore, C. auris‐associated infections have become a life‐threatening concern that seeks immediate attention from scientists to raise awareness against this invasive fungal infection. Antifungal medications are effective for most of the yeast's strains, but certain medications, such as fluconazole, rarely have any effect against C. auris. Infections caused by C. auris may affect the blood, heart, and brain and can be fatal after only 30 days. Common symptoms include fever and chills and can only be diagnosed after laboratory testing. There is often difficulty identifying this certain Candida strain, as it can be confused with other types of yeasts and thus requires special laboratory testing, according to the CDC [13]. Several PCR methods have been developed for detecting C. auris from blood samples or swabs [14, 15]. The fatality rate, according to statistics from 2019, showed that 30%–60% of people with the C. auris infection have died [10]. Of those cases, most of the patients died from the infection already had medical complications beforehand. The reasons for C. auris survival in these environments are its ability to easily colonize the human body, and to survive for up to 28 days on a plastic surface [16]. This strong resilience is what differentiates C. auris positive cultures from other invasive fungal infections. Risk factors are like those of other fungal infections, which include diabetes, recent surgery, and antibiotic and antifungal use [17]. In addition, critical patients using invasive medical devices like breathing tubes, feeding tubes, catheters in a vein, or urinary catheters have a high risk of developing C. auris infections [17]. According to the CDC, its infections are more difficult to treat due to their low response to common antifungal drugs [18]. It was found that most C. auris infections are treatable with a class of antifungal drugs called echinocandins, although about 4% of strains have shown resistance making it difficult to treat [19]. The biggest concern about C. auris is that scientists do not know the exact mechanisms of resistance to antifungal medicines or the source of resistance development.

Methods

In this review, we performed a comprehensive search of Scopus‐indexed and Web of Science articles (published between 1998 and 2023) related to the C. auris outbreak, mutations, treatment, prevention, and guidelines strategies in the PubMed database and collected suitable information from appropriate ones. Manuscripts written only in English were selected for this review. Articles not indexed in Pubmed, Medline, Google Scholar, and authentic sites were removed from our selections.

Outbreak History of C. auris Infection

As previously mentioned, C. auris was first discovered in the ear canal of an elderly woman in a Japanese hospital in 2009 [2]. Since then, there has been a growing concern toward this specific strain of Candida species, which was first reported in the United States in 2016 [8]. This apprehension is justified, as there has been a low but concerning number of cases reported each year.

As previously mentioned, C. auris was first discovered in the ear canal of an elderly woman in a Japanese hospital in 2009 [4]. Since then, there has been a growing concern toward this specific strain of Candida species, which was first reported in the United States in 2016 [8]. This apprehension is justified, as there has been a low but concerning number of cases reported each year. Due to this significance, C. auris has been designated as reportable to the National Notifiable Diseases Surveillance System. This enables state and local public health departments to promptly submit newly identified cases, providing a greater availability of data for a more comprehensive analysis of the microbe [7, 20]. The number of cases surged from 479 in 2019 to 1471 in 2021, in the United States [7, 20]. The most rapid increase was noted from 2020 to 2021, with reported cases almost tripling to 4401 [20]. According to the CDC, from January 2022 to December 2022, there have been 2377 recorded clinical cases and 5754 screenings [20]. With data showing signs of multiplicative positive culture growth per year, there are increasing clinical concerns for the future.

Disease Burden Due to C. auris Infection

People with weakened immune systems, affected with multiple diseases, and frequently visiting hospitals and nursing homes are considered high‐risk groups for C. auris infections. Diagnosing C. auris is difficult without detailed blood culture testing in the laboratory.

People with weakened immune systems, affected with multiple diseases, and frequently visiting hospitals and nursing homes are considered high‐risk groups for C. auris infections. Diagnosing C. auris is difficult without detailed blood culture testing in the laboratory. According to the CDC, most individuals who died from C. auris had other medical complications, which may explain the high mortality rate of 30%–60% [11, 21, 22]. People with frequent hospital visits or those living in nursing homes were also considered a high‐risk group. This is expected because the predominant inhabitants of nursing homes are elderly with medical complications [23]. People with weakened immune systems with medical complications such as diabetes, blood cancers, or have tubes inserted into their body (feeding tubes, breathing tubes, catheters) were more likely to contract C. auris [17]. A systemic review, meta‐analysis, and randomized controlled studies including 48 published papers with 27,297 participants showed that biological agents acting on the immune system increased the risk of Candida infections [24]. The burden of this microbe is not its high contraction rate, but its ability to exacerbate the condition of individuals with complex medical cases. Symptoms may not be obvious due to patients already being sick in nursing homes or hospitals with other serious conditions, making diagnosis challenging. Additionally, C. auris symptoms can vary greatly, making it difficult to diagnose unless blood culture testing is done in a laboratory [13, 14, 15]. Some symptoms may consist of bloodstream or wound infections occurring in various parts of the body, further complicating the diagnosis such diverse symptoms [17]. In fact, there is no common set of symptoms unique to C. auris. Additionally, accidental wounds or cuts can lead to potentially fatal infections due to the high probability of wound/bloodstream infection.

Potential Reasons for C. auris Isolate Threat

C. auris has increased resistance to antifungal drugs by encoding enzymes and several point mutations. These help them to survive in harsher environments, evade the immune system, and develop resistance against several antifungal drugs, like fluconazole and amphotericin B. Mutation in FSK1 and ERG3 genes causes C. auris resistant to echinoderms class of drugs.

C. auris has become increasingly threatening due to its developing resistance to antifungal drugs. It was found that certain strains of C. auris had mutations in genes linked to drug resistance, some of which are involved in multiple drug resistance (Figure 1). About 90% of C. auris strains have acquired resistance to at least one antifungal drug, and about 4% have resistance to all three types [25]. These mutations did not occur without explanation. A recent study showed that within just 30 generations over 9 days, two different progenitor isolates of C. auris have acquired at least a twofold minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) value in fluconazole (Figure 1) [19]. One of these strains with a higher MIC value was found in Clade I, more specifically in gene ERG11. It was found to have a strong resistance against azole antifungal drugs, due to its specialty in encoding the enzyme lanosterol‐14‐alpha‐dimesylate, which solidifies cell integrity and structure [26, 27]. Similarly, some strains in Clade II had mutations in genes involved in regulating stress responses. More specifically, Clade II was found to have several point mutations that are associated with higher drug resistance to fluconazole and amphotericin B (Figure 1) [19]. This could potentially help those strains survive in harsher environments and continue to persist in healthcare settings. Additionally, some mutations allow the isolates to evade the immune system, contributing to their ability to cause invasive infections. Some clades, on the other hand, have not been researched enough to know where the mutations take place. Clade V is such a case, in which more evidence is needed to confirm TAC1B and ERG11 gene mutations [28]. Echinocandins have been relied on for treatment due to their strong resistance to azoles, but as a result, several strains of C. auris have been discovered to be resistant to the drugs in the echinocandin class. More specifically, there have been found to be mutations in the FKS1 and ERG3 genes [25]. Despite various speculations, conclusive evidence to identify the reasons for C. auris being less resistant to this class of antifungals is yet to be established. A recent theory speculates that C. auris may even be the product of climate change, resulting from the increase in temperature [29].

Evaluation of C. auris

Whole‐genome sequencing (WGS), multi‐locus sequence typing (MLST), proteomic analysis performed with matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (MALDI‐TOF MS), and amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP) can be utilized to identify the genetic relatedness of C. auris strains [30].

WGS

WGS has been widely used to identify and characterize outbreaks and transmission dynamics of C. auris [31, 32, 33]. Genetically divergent lineages have been globally identified and stratified geographically into four main clades: Clade I found in Southern Asia, Clade II detected in Eastern Asia, Clade III identified in Africa, and Clade IV found in South America [33]. Another clade (Clade V) was detected in an isolate from a patient in Iran [34]. WGS confirmed that this cluster showed a difference of > 200,000 single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from the other clades and deviated before the separation of the other clades [35].

Phenotypic and Molecular Identification

Since there are no phenotypic characteristics yet developed, it is difficult to distinguish C. auris from other Candida species. There are several reliable molecular techniques, such as MALDI‐TOF MS, amplification of DNA by real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), and sequencing of internal transcribed spacer (ITS) regions, that are used to identify C. auris [14, 15]. However, these advanced techniques need specialized equipment and training and are expensive, restraining their use, especially in countries where healthcare budgets are insufficient. Therefore, it is important to develop an easily accessible technique for reliable identification of C. auris infections. At present, various PCR techniques are utilized to achieve unambiguous and fast identification of C. auris [14, 15]. A useful, easy, low‐cost, and straightforward PCR approach based on the uniqueness of certain glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) protein‐encoding genes is used for accurate identification of C. auris infections in a clinical setting [36]. AFLP techniques have also been used for the typing of C. auris isolates. The role of AFLP analysis in the demarcation of the geographical clusters has been demonstrated [30].

Prevention and Policy Recommendations

C. auris can cause serious illness in high‐risk groups of people, like long‐term hospitalized patients, and individuals with multiple medical complications. Generally, it is recommended to avoid contact with an infected person and reduce the frequency of hospital visits. Healthy people who come in contact with an infected individual do not need the testing for C. auris, recommended by the CDC.

C. auris is a type of fungus that can cause serious illness in individuals who contract it. It is most commonly seen in hospital patients, with symptoms spanning from not being noticeable to bloodstream infections, wound infections, and/or ear infections [11, 37]. The accurate isolation of C. auris in culture is important for ensuring optimal patient care. The treatment should be started for patients affected only by invasive fungal infections, not patients who have a culture‐positive status, and identify patients to initiate proper infection prevention and control measures [38]. C. auris affects patients with multiple medical complications, such as immunocompromised, diabetic, or using device‐like tubes in their bodies. As such, it is difficult to diagnose because other invasive fungal infections have the same characteristics. Additionally, symptoms may not be noticeable when combined with other medical complications. It is not easily diagnosed, and the blood samples must be sent to a lab for special testing [39]. If a patient is found to have contracted C. auris, hospital personnel are to take special countermeasures as cited by the CDC (Figure 2) [37, 40]. Not only do hospitals aim to treat the patient who has contracted the microbe, but they also aim to eliminate it completely. Some of those countermeasures consist of moving the infected patient to a different room, wearing gowns and gloves during patient care, and having family members and healthcare personnel thoroughly clean their hands after each visit. Even after the infection is treated, it is recommended to stay cautious as the microbe may reside on the skin or other body sites for longer periods. However, it is considered very unlikely that family members who are healthy will contract the C. auris infection. In most instances, the CDC does not recommend getting screened for C. auris for those who are healthy, even if they come into contact with an infected individual, as it can be fended off by the immune system [37, 40].

Therapeutic and Vaccination Options

Three different drugs from the echinocandin class, anidulafungin, caspofungin, and micafungin, have been recommended by the CDC to prevent infections caused by C. auris. At present, there are no vaccines to produce antibodies against C. auris or any official human trials. Several new antifungal agents are going through Phase II or Phase III trials and have shown promising results against C. auris. Ibrexafungerp showed potent activity against C. auris strains, including echinocandin‐resistant isolates. Rezafungin also showed potential effects in vitro studies. Another drug, Tetrazole increases the survival chances and reduces the burden of C. auris‐infected mice.

A group of antifungals known as echinocandins has been researched to be the most effective, according to the CDC [37]. At present, there are three drugs belonging to the echinocandin class recommended to prevent the infection from spreading: anidulafungin, caspofungin, and micafungin. A previous study showed that mutations in the FKS1 and ERG3 genes of C. auris have a high resistance to echinocandins [19]. As of now, there are no available vaccines to produce antibodies against C. auris or any official human trials. However, current research is focusing on developing several new antifungal agents that are going through Phase II or Phase III trials and showed therapeutic effects against C. auris. Ibrexafungerp of the triterpenoid class of drugs is the most promising so far, as it possesses potent activity against C. auris strains, including echinocandin‐resistant isolates [27]. It works by leading to cellular deformation and pore formation, as well as inhibiting cell division [26]. Rezafungin, part of the echinocandin class, is also a potential relief and has shown in vitro activity against clinical C. auris isolates, but there is a lack of evidence to justify its efficacy against the isolates resistant to other echinocandins [41]. Additionally, a tetrazole by the name of VT‐1598 was studied in a neutropenic mouse model of C. auris isolate and was found to significantly increase survival chances and reduce the burden of fungus in the kidneys of the brain [40]. This was especially significant, as most of the patients who contracted C. auris isolates were already patients with medical complications. Guidelines for Empiric Antifungal Treatment

The CDC recommends echinocandins as the empiric therapy for C. auris infections [46]. However, recent studies have shown reduced susceptibility to echinocandins due to the development of resistance [33, 47]. According to the Infectious Diseases Society of America and CDC, the guidelines for the treatment of C. auris infections include first‐line and alternative treatment options [48, 49]. For adults 18 years or older, the recommended first‐line antifungal agents against C. auris are anidulafungin, caspofungin, or micafungin [48, 49]. If those do not work, an alternative treatment is liposomal amphotericin B. For children, 2 months of age or more, caspofungin or micafungin are recommended as first‐line agents. Similar to adults, the alternative treatment is liposomal amphotericin B [48, 49]. For patients 2 months of age or younger, amphotericin B deoxycholate is recommended as the first‐line agent. The alternative treatment options for neonates are liposomal amphotericin B, caspofungin, or micafungin [48, 49].

Conclusion

The emergence of C. auris has become a public health issue due to its resistance to all 3 antifungal drug classes. This resistance has been tracked back to gene mutations, in which a higher quantity and a larger variety of antifungals are needed to treat the fungal infections. A common mutation is found in the gene ERG11, which was found to have multiple mutations and higher resistance to fluconazole and amphotericin B. The fungal isolate is mostly found in those who already have several medical complications, such as those who are immunocompromised. Individuals considered healthy are considered extremely unlikely to receive the fungal isolate. Prevention measures to resist C. auris are standard medical procedures, such as washing your hands for at least 20 s and washing dirty surfaces often. Although no vaccine is currently available, ongoing studies are going through the second and third stages of clinical trials. These drugs consist of ibrexafungerp, rezafungin, and VT‐1598, which have shown effectiveness in strains with acquired resistance to echinocandins. Through continuing research and studies against this fungus, effective strategies are being developed to mitigate the impact of C. auris on public health as well as initiate treatment.
