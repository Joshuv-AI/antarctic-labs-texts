The efficacy of topological properties of functional brain networks in identifying major depressive disorder
Authors: Kejie Xu, Dan Long, Mengda Zhang, Yifan Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a common mental disorder characterized by cognitive impairment, and its pathophysiology remains to be explored. In this study, we aimed to explore the efficacy of brain network topological properties (TPs) in identifying MDD patients, revealing variational brain regions with efficient TPs. Functional connectivity (FC) networks were constructed from resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI). Small-worldness did not exhibit significant variations in MDD patients. Subsequently, two-sample t-tests were employed to screen FC and reconstruct the network. The discriminative ability of TPs between MDD patients and healthy controls was analyzed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC), ROC analysis showed the small-worldness of binary reconstructed FC network (p < 0.05) was reduced in MDD patients, with area under the curve (AUC) of local efficiency (Le) and clustering coefficient (Cp) as sample features having AUC of 0.6351 and 0.6347 respectively being optimal. The AUC of Le and Cp for retained brain regions by T-test (p < 0.05) were 0.6795 and 0.6956 respectively. Further, support vector machine (SVM) model assessed the effectiveness of TPs in identifying MDD patients, and it identified the Le and Cp in brain regions selected by the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), with average accuracy from leave-one-site-out cross-validation being 62.03% and 61.44%. Additionally, shapley additive explanations (SHAP) was employed to elucidate variations in TPs across brain regions, revealing that predominant variations among MDD patients occurred within the default mode network. These results reveal efficient TPs that can provide empirical evidence for utilizing nodal TPs as effective inputs for deep learning on graph structures, contributing to understanding the pathological mechanisms of MDD.Keywords: Major depressive disorder, rs-fMRI, Functional connectivity network, Topological properties, IdentificationSubject terms: Network topology, Neuroscience, Predictive markers, Depression

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a common mental disorder characterized by significant patterns of depressed mood, impaired cognitive abilities, and self-harming tendencies1. Neurological and psychiatric disorders can be viewed as a syndrome caused by a series of brain connectivity abnormalities. In recent years, resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) has been widely used to study the intrinsic changes in brain function in various neurological and psychiatric disorders2, Mental disorders are often associated with changes in large-scale brain systems, particularly changes in topological properties3,4. Graph theory-based methods have been extensively applied to quantify the abnormality of functional network topology structure in severe depression5,6 indicated that recurrent MDD patients exhibit reduced global efficiency and local efficiency. Additionally, a study found significant changes in nodal network indicators within the default mode network (DMN) and somatomotor network (SMN) among elderly individuals with severe depression, including decreased nodal betweenness and nodal efficiency7. However, despite extensive research on the functional network topology structure of MDD from large samples at multiple centers providing some understanding of the brain functional abnormalities present in MDD, there are still some limitations. Firstly, previous studies only conducted statistical difference analysis on quantitative indicators of functional network topology structure between MDD and healthy control groups without quantifying the discriminative ability of these attributes or identifying which indicators show greater differentiation between groups. Secondly, current research primarily focuses on characterizing graph topology structures encoding for MDD using advanced machine learning methods to enhance understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying MDD diagnosis8–12, they have framed diagnosing MDD as a graph classification problem and attempted to use graph convolutional neural networks with reasoning learning capabilities for graph topology structures represented by node inputs through graph convolution operators and pooling methods mapping into low-dimensional graph information encoding13. However, most studies input either functional connections or BOLD signals from brain regions as node information representations8–12, where a study evaluated five popular GNN architectures’ performance in an MDD identification task, showing that one-dimensional convolutional neural networks performed best on flattened functional connection data possibly due to redundant information leading to reduced recognition efficiency within GNN architectures10. Furthermore, a study utilized functional connectivity, BOLD signals, and functional metrics (including amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations14、fractional ALFF (fALFF)15 and regional homogeneity (ReHo)16) as input nodes for model learning, The findings indicated that, for the assessment of the efficiency of MDD, network functional metric information was found to be optimal, while functional connectivity and blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal information exhibited lower efficiency9. However, despite numerous studies based on graph theory, the topological properties of brain nodes as input information for identifying MDD efficiency have not been considered. Additionally, due to the relatively limited understanding of large-scale brain network topology configurations in MDD at present, it is necessary to explore the performance of topological properties in brain functional networks in large samples to further enhance our understanding of the pathological mechanisms underlying MDD. Furthermore, this exploration provides empirical evidence for using node topological properties as model inputs in future deep learning research on brain network structures.

This study aims to investigate the discriminative power of brain network topology between patients with MDD and healthy controls from a large-scale sample collected from multiple sites. It also seeks to explore effective quantitative indicators for distinguishing functional networks between MDD and healthy controls (HC), observe differences in these quantitative indicators within MDD brain functional networks, and assess their effectiveness in identifying patients with MDD. To achieve this goal, extensive experiments and analyses were conducted on 1160 participants from 10 data sites to further improve our understanding of the pathological mechanisms underlying MDD.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

The REST-meta-MDD project (http://rfmri.org/REST-meta-MDD) was the primary source of all collected data. Brain imaging data were gathered from 1300 patients with major depressive disorder and 1128 HC subjects, sourced from 25 research groups in 17 hospitals across China17. Subsequently, the data underwent further screening to meet the specific requirements of this study. For detailed methods, please refer to our previous papers18,19. The main processing steps included removing duplicate non-2.0 time point data and data with zero time series for participants. Gender and age matching tests were then conducted for each site’s data, and unmatched site data was excluded to ensure that all site data passed the age and gender matching tests. Next, we performed data preprocessing using DPARSF. The primary steps involved global signal regression, extraction of time series for each brain region based on anatomical automatic labeling (AAL) brain atlas, slice timing correction, realignment, spatial normalization, and nuisance regression. To ensure generalizability of results, we chose to define the nodes of the brain network using 116 regions of interest (ROIs) from the widely used AAL atlas20. For detailed information on data preprocessing procedures, please refer to our previous paper17. Finally, functional connectivity networks based on Pearson correlation were generated using BrainNetClass Toolkit21.

Network metrics

After the completion of functional connectivity network construction, significant functional connections were selected and the functional network was reconstructed through two-sample t-tests (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01) to explore whether differential functional connections affect the comparison results of topological brain network indicators between groups. Subsequently, Gretna software (version 2.0, www.nirc.org/projects/Gretna) was used to calculate several common global and nodal topological brain network indicators for two types of networks (original network, reconstructed network)22. Global indicators included global efficiency (Eglob), local efficiency (Eloc), shortest path length (Lp), clustering coefficient (Cp), and small-worldness (σ) based on 100 randomizations. Nodal-level indicators included nodal local efficiency, nodal clustering coefficient, nodal shortest path length, and nodal efficiency. In previous related studies, calculations were typically performed within a sparsity threshold range of 10–34%, with an increment interval of 1%, to ensure estimability of the threshold networks for the above-mentioned metrics while minimizing false edges23–25. In this study, all metrics were calculated within a two-stage sparsity threshold range: first calculating metric values at various thresholds within a wide range of sparsity thresholds (3-91%, with a step size of 2%), then selecting an effective sparsity threshold range through statistical analysis of metric differences at each threshold(p < 0.05) to ensure valid estimation within the threshold range. Subsequently, within this effective threshold range, the area under the curve (AUC) for each network metric was computed as a summary scalar for global topological features. This approach is independent from single-threshold selection and sensitive to topological changes in brain diseases23–26. Additionally, in order to further delineate differences in the functional brain network topology between the MDD and HC groups, we retained the results of global and nodal structural measurements under single thresholds.

Statistics analysis

In the assessment of demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with severe depression and healthy controls, the normality of data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Differences in age and education level were analyzed using non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data) or independent samples t-test (for normally distributed data). Gender comparison was conducted using χ2 test. To compare the statistical differences in brain network topological metrics between groups, non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test or independent samples t-test were initially used to examine the differences in global topological properties (including normalized clustering coefficient (γ), normalized shortest path length (λ), and σ values) between MDD and HC groups across a wide range of sparsity threshold levels (3-91%, with a 2% increment), in order to determine an effective threshold range. Subsequently, within the determined effective threshold range, differences in AUC values for global topological properties (including γ, λ, σ, Eg, Eloc) between the two groups were examined. Age, gender, and education level were included as covariates with Bonferroni correction.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis

The ROC curve27 is a graphical analysis tool that reflects the comprehensive indicators of sensitivity and specificity. It uses sensitivity or true positive rate (TPR) as the vertical axis and 1-specificity or false positive rate (TNR) as the horizontal axis. In this study, ROC curve area under the curve (AUC) was used to measure the discriminative ability of brain node topological properties between MDD and HC groups. A study emphasized the use of the area under the ROC curve for evaluating the diagnostic capability of a test. Despite its limitation to binary outcomes and disregard for continuity, it can still be utilized in decision theory by converting it into a simplified measure28. Similarly, brain functional networks’ topological properties are continuous variables categorized into MDD patients and HC. The AUC calculation of the topological properties of the reconstructed brain FC network post two-sample t-test is depicted under the sparsity threshold in Parts 3 to 5 of Fig. 1. The global attribute part (Part 4 of Fig. 1) divides the AUC value at all thresholds for each sample’s global topological property as a sample feature value. In the nodal part (Part 5 of Fig. 1), all average nodal topological property values across sparsity thresholds and functional network nodes serve as sample feature values. These feature values are categorized as 0 for MDD and 1 for HC to distinguish between the two populations. The true positive rate refers to correctly identifying non-diseased subjects among all non-diseased subjects, while the true negative rate refers to correctly identifying diseased subjects among all diseased subjects. Furthermore, there are two methods for selecting significantly different node topological property values and using their mean as sample feature: first, taking averages of node topological features at all sparsity thresholds; secondly, selecting nodes before averaging their values as sample features. Alternatively, conducting inter-group differential analysis on node topological property features at each sparsity threshold level before averaging selected nodes’ values; finally taking means across all thresholds to obtain overall sample features.

Construction of the brain FC network matrix (Part 1) involves reconstructing FC networks using two-sample t-tests (Part 2). Binarized brain networks are then reconstructed under a sparsity threshold (Part 3), followed by the calculation of global topological properties AUC under sparsity (Part 4) and nodal topological properties AUC under sparsity (Part 5). The LASSO method was employed to screen brain nodes, and the SVM model was utilized for prediction (Part 6).

Functional indicators calculations

ALFF is considered an effective method for detecting the spontaneous oscillation intensity of brain regions and reflecting the spontaneous activity of the brain in rs-fMRI BOLD signals29,30, Meanwhile, fALFF represents the relative contribution of specific low-frequency oscillations to the entire frequency range31, Regional homogeneity (ReHo), as a measure of regional synchronization in functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) time series, has been applied in MDD-related research32, In this study, three functional indicators were calculated: ALFF, fALFF, and Reho. Firstly, each voxel’s BOLD time series was transformed into the frequency domain using fast Fourier transform. Then, the square root of power spectrum was computed and averaged within a specified frequency range (0.01–0.08 Hz) for each voxel to obtain ALFF. fALFF represents a normalized measure of ALFF by dividing its value by the total amplitude within the entire frequency range, effectively suppressing physiological artifacts31. Reho measures local synchronous activity by calculating voxel-wise similarity in fluctuation during a given voxel’s time course under resting state conditions16, the calculation involves computing Kendall’s coefficient of concordance for brain voxels using a neighborhood size of 26 voxels.

Efficiency testing of topological properties

To further investigate the clinical application efficiency of topological features in identifying MDD, we utilized a support vector regression (SVM) model based on linear kernel33, which is a supervised learning model used for classification and regression analysis. In order to prevent test data leakage during the model training process, the reconstruction of sample functional networks and the selection of metric features of sample functional networks (including local efficiency of brain region nodes, functional indicators such as ALFF, and functional connectivity features) were all conducted within the training dataset. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)34 was employed as the feature selection method in this study. LASSO is a common linear regression regularization method suitable for feature selection35, After filtering out some brain nodes, the topological features of the training set’s brain nodes were input into the SVM model, as shown in part 6 of Fig. 1. To ensure robustness and reduce bias in the experiment, we adopted leave-one-site-out cross validation (LOSCV) strategy to validate the model. Analysis of the importance of brain functional regions

Understanding and determining the importance and impact of each feature value in predictive models is a challenging task36. SHAP analysis can be utilized to assess the significance of features37, Unlike black box models such as deep neural networks or SVM, this model encodes the relationship between input and output as a series of easily understandable if-then rules, providing a globally interpretable method based on optimal Shapley values to measure the contribution of each feature in the model38, and it demonstrates interpretable predictions for machine learning classifiers, addressing deficiencies caused by lack of feature directionality39. The feature selection method based on SHAP analysis determines the importance of brain functional regions carrying topological attribute features in distinguishing MDD from HC groups in a more interpretable manner. In experiments, functional region node features selected by LASSO were used as input features for an XGBoost model to determine the level of importance for distinguishing MDD from HC groups during LOSCV.

Classification result

In this study, the mean topological properties within the effective threshold of nodes were utilized as sample features. The range established through ROC analysis is [0.02, 0.16]. However, considering the prior experience between the MDD and HC groups in model prediction, the threshold range utilized for SVM model identification of MDD patients has been expanded to [0.02, 0.25, 0.01]. This increase in threshold range serves to mitigate the influence of prior experience while enhancing the input data for the identification model. The LASSO method was employed to select node information, with a penalty coefficient alpha ranging from 0.0001 to 0.01 at intervals of 0.0001, it is applied to screen 116 brain network regions. The selected regional features (NCp, Ne, NLe) were input into SVM model to identify MDD patients. For each alpha set value, a total of 10 cross-validations were conducted. The average number of screened features and the distribution of identification accuracy (only for alpha values = [0.0001, 0.0005, 0.001, 0.005, 0.01]) during the LOSCV process are presented as (a), (b), and (c) in Fig. 5. These results show that even with a reduction in the number of features screened, the model’s average accuracy in predicting MDD patients is almost unaffected. Possible explanations suggest that if the remaining features continue to provide strong explanatory power for model predictions, then significant impacts on predictive performance may not be observed. Throughout the process, the selected indices were retained and applied to the data of test sites. Similarly, the selection method was applicable to functional indicators (including Alff, fAlff, and Reho) features as well as functional connectivity as brain node features. The SVM model learned input features and identified test site data using grid search for optimal alpha under LOSCV with NCp, Ne, and NLe as input model node features resulting in identification accuracies of 61.44 ± 9.14% (mean value ± standard deviation %), 59.74 ± 9.51%, and 62.03 ± 8.12%. When using functional indicators (Alff, fAlff and Reho) as node features, identification accuracies were found to be 50.98 ± 3.11% ,51.43 ± 4.65% and 51.52 ± 3.77%. Using functional connectivity as a node feature (averaging the functional connectivity matrix horizontally or vertically on each functional region), resulted in an identification accuracy of 54.11 ± 5.16%. The accuracy distribution of identifying MDD patients, corresponding to the characteristic data of each brain region feature is shown in Fig. 5(d). Efficiency for MDD recognition was highest when using nodal topological properties as input features.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized a large multi-site data sample to investigate the topological changes in the intrinsic functional network of MDD, as well as the differences in topological metrics between MDD and HC. We also examined the efficiency of distinguishing MDD based on significantly different topological attributes. Our research revealed four main findings. Firstly, we did not observe significant differences in the original functional network of MDD compared to HC across multiple topological metrics, which is somewhat consistent with previous findings that reported variations in functional network topology only among recurrent and elderly patients rather than all MDD patients using the same dataset5–7. Furthermore, after removing potentially redundant or spurious ineffective functional connections at significance levels of p < 0.01 and p < 0.05, disruptions were observed in the brain network’s topological structure for MDD patients, reflected by changes in global and nodal network metrics including small-worldness, Eg, Ne, NLe, NLp and NCp. Secondly, there was significant discriminative power for NCp and NLe within the reconstructed functional networks (p < 0.05) between MDD and HC groups. Thirdly, NCp and NLe demonstrated superior performance in identifying MDD compared to other node-based measures such as Ne or NLp along with functional connectivity measures. Lastly, analysis using SHAP models identified important functional regions that differentiate between MDD and HC groups primarily concentrated within DMN with a few located within visual networks and cerebellar networks. Overall, our findings suggest that individuals with MDD exhibit abnormal configuration of their intrinsic function.

Firstly, in our study, we refined the binarization of the original network within a wide range of sparsity thresholds. Through statistical analysis of the topological metrics of MDD and HC, no significant differences were found between the two groups at all thresholds (p < 0.05). This lack of significance may be attributed to the presence of false edges in the functional network, which can impact the measurement of topological structure metrics. Subsequently, by removing potential influences on topological property expression using significance levels and reconstructing the functional network, significant differences in topological structure between groups were found within a low threshold range (p < 0.05), with an effective sparsity range concentrated from 0.03 to 0.14. Notably, this overlaps with the commonly used threshold range of 0.10 to 0.34 in previous studies23–25, To prevent empirical leakage in subsequent MDD efficiency studies, we expanded the sparsity threshold range to 0.02 to 0.25 and calculated global and nodal topological properties for networks within this sparsity range. Secondly, we conducted statistical analysis and group differentiation measurements on global and nodal topological properties. The results showed that in MDD patients’ brain functional networks, clustering coefficient, local efficiency, shortest path length, and small-worldness were significantly reduced while global efficiency was significantly increased compared to HC. Global efficiency is defined as inversely proportional to shortest path length40, while local efficiency is defined as directly proportional to clustering coefficient41, the above mentioned trends in the variations of functional network topology in MDD patients have been elucidated. The significant decrease in the clustering coefficient of MDD patients may indicate a disruption of neuronal connectivity between brain regions, and the loss of “small-world” properties may suggest that the optimal information processing and metabolic cost balance in the brains of MDD patients is not as efficient as that in HC42. Additionally, ROC analysis was employed to demonstrate differences in topological properties among inter-group brain nodes. The results indicated that local efficiency and clustering coefficients, following further screening (p < 0.05) of node means at sparsity aggregation, exhibited AUC values of 63.51% and 63.41%, respectively, as sample features with greater inter-group differences compared to other indicators. Furthermore, through sequential selection and aggregation of nodes at each threshold before aggregating node means across all thresholds to obtain sample feature values, AUC values were found to be 68.08% and 69.76%.

In order to further investigate the effectiveness of topological properties with high discriminability in identifying MDD, we primarily focus on node local efficiency, node clustering coefficient, and node efficiency as model input features. After conducting LOSCV with accuracy as the performance evaluation metric, the results indicate that the NLe and NCp are optimal for MDD identification. This finding is significant because previous research on recognizing mental illness has mainly concentrated on using functional connectivity of nodes as input information for graph convolutional neural networks8,10–12, the one-dimensional flattened functional connectivity vector information may prove to be more effective than a multi-dimensional approach10, This could be attributed to the redundancy of node functional connectivity information. Graph convolutional neural networks derive their representation of graph structure from aggregating node information within the graph43. A comparison was made between the direct impact of node information on model learning efficiency and performance in representing graph structure, specifically between node functional metrics and node functional connectivity. The results show that node topology attributes are more effective in identifying MDD applications than functional connectivity, BOLD signal, and functional metrics as input features8–12 of machine learning models in previous studies, providing empirical evidence for future deep learning of graph network structure to explore brain network topology in MDD patients.

Some autopsy studies have shown a decrease in the density of glial cells in emotion-related areas of the brain in depressed patients44. Glial cells provide metabolic and regulatory support for neurons, and astrocytes are responsible for increasing the number of mature and functional synapses45. The neural circuit pathway of the brain is not only affected by neurons, but also regulated by glial cells that have a significant impact on structural and functional connectivity46. In addition, there is a strong correlation between the microscopic neural circuit of the brain and the macro-functional network of resting states based on fMRI47. The connectivity of brain functional network directly affects the calculation of topological attributes of nodes in brain network regions. Therefore, SHAP model was used to analyze the significance of clustering coefficient and local efficiency of functional region for MDD recognition. The supplementary motor area (SMA) consistently emerged as one of the most crucial functional areas, with a negative correlation observed between left SMA metabolism and cerebral blood flow in MDD patients, while BOLD signals directly affect functional network connectivity17,19,48. Additionally, the SMA plays a critical role in action preparation, execution, and control, and is active during self-initiated movement. Increased inhibitory neural activity in the SMA may be associated with decreased motor activity48. In terms of negative emotional processes, differences were noted in the activation level of the middle temporal gyrus (TPOmid) between MDD patients and HC49. Furthermore, the orbital part of inferior frontal gyrus (ORBinf) was identified as a key region involved in emotion processing within the brain50,51, specifically, the human medial orbital prefrontal cortex plays a pivotal role in encoding reward value and pleasure. Changes have been observed in functional connectivity between ORBinf.R and adjacent lateral orbital prefrontal cortex52, leading to alterations in topological structure. These regions are part of default mode network (DMN), which plays a crucial role in the interpretation of the SHAP model for MDD identification and also includes PoCG.L, ORBinf.R, SFGmed.R, IFGoperc.L, and SFGmed.L. DMN is known to play a core role among MDD patients by participating in emotion processing, self-referential mental activities, and memory retrieval from previous experiences53,55. Additionally, the topological changes within certain nodes of cerebellum-subcortical network and visual network should not be overlooked as they may contribute to emotional and cognitive dysfunction as well as abnormal visual cortical processing56,58.

Our study has some limitations. Firstly, the current research is based on a large data sample from multiple sites, and certain functional connections were filtered and reconstructed at the significance level. The differences in topological properties of the reconstructed functional networks between MDD and HC groups need further validation with a wider applicability through more samples. In addition, we only used the commonly used SVM model to verify the efficiency of identifying MDD using different node information as inputs for graph neural network models. However, the classification accuracy observed in this study was relatively low, with most results falling below 70%. This can be attributed to two primary factors: Firstly, to ensure the robustness of the conclusions and the generalization of the classifier, we utilized a large dataset from 10 sites. Variations in machine models, scanning parameters, and equipment conditions can reduce the accuracy of multi-site data classification. Therefore, the low precision is also reasonable. Secondly, the cross-validation approach uses LOSCV, where we train the model by extracting data from other study groups and classifying the new data. Although this can ensure the generalization of the classifier, it reduces the accuracy of the results. Additionally, due to the difficulty in defining another important input (adjacency matrix) for graph convolutional neural networks, we did not train and validate node information as inputs to the graph neural network model. Future work can further develop more generalized identification models to improve MDD recognition accuracy and explore biomarkers for MDD patients on this basis.
