Hydra: Marker-Free RGB-D Hand-Eye Calibration
Martin Huber, Huanyu Tian, Christopher E. Mower, Lucas-Raphael Müller, Sébastien Ourselin, Christos Bergeles, Tom Vercauteren

Abstract
This work presents an RGB-D imaging-based approach to marker-free hand-eye calibration using a novel implementation of the iterative closest point (ICP)algorithm with a robust point-to-plane (PTP)objective formulated on a Lie algebra. Its applicability is demonstrated through comprehensive experiments using three well known serial manipulators and two RGB-D cameras. With only three randomly chosen robot configurations, our approach achieves approximately 90 % 90\% successful calibrations, demonstrating 2 − 3 × 2-3\times higher convergence rates to the global optimum compared to both marker-based and marker-free baselines. We also report 2 2 orders of magnitude faster convergence time ( 0.8 ± 0.4 ​ s 0.8\pm 0.4\,\text{s} ) for 9 9 robot configurations over other marker-free methods. Our method exhibits significantly improved accuracy ( 5 ​ mm 5\,\text{mm} in task space) over classical approaches ( 7 ​ mm 7\,\text{mm} in task space) whilst being marker-free. The benchmarking dataset and code are open sourced under Apache 2.0 License, and a ROS 2 integration with robot abstraction is provided to facilitate deployment 1 1 1 Software: https://github.com/roboreg , 2 2 2 Benchmark dataset: https://drive.google.com/file/d/1WAU9EgYlW-wVcVWL_4bldZYYWfXXX5eM/view?usp=sharing .



I
Introduction

Hand-eye calibration – determining a camera’s pose relative to a serial manipulator and thus relating joint space to a Cartesian reference space – is a core component of robotics and computer vision, enabling autonomous operation and structured interaction with the environment.
Task specification in Cartesian space minimizes redundancy
[
1
]
and remains agnostic to the robot’s kinematics
[
2
]
, enhancing efficiency and enabling more generalizable data-driven policy learning. Given the inherent data scarcity in robotics, effective hand-eye calibration remains essential for achieving autonomy.

Hydra takes advantage of multiple robot configurations. It aligns robot mesh vertices (purple) with observed point clouds (turquoise). The combined robot configurations resemble the mythical Hydra.

As detailed in Section
III-A
, classical approaches to hand-eye calibration rely on markers. However, markers
are subject to strict visibility constraints, and add complexity to the calibration process for users. Consequentially, a significant body of literature aims to alleviate these limitations by using
the robot
itself
as a marker surrogate.
In this work, we refer to these approaches as marker-free. Summarizing requirements, the ideal hand-eye calibration should be accurate in task space, marker-free, robust, applicable to any serial manipulator, and expedient.

Fig. 2
:
Schematic of the proposed Hydra method. Refers to Section
II
.

In
[
3
]
, it was demonstrated how supervised learning on synthetic data allows for keypoints extraction from the robot, enabling 2D-3D correspondence finding and calibration via
perspective-n-point (PnP). This idea was further improved towards self-supervised keypoint identification in CtRNet
[
4
]
. Both works rely on training dedicated models and applicability to any robot was not demonstrated. Recently, CtRNet-X was introduced in
[
5
]
to improve outlier robustness, but it directly inherits the limitations of CtRNet.
Alternative PnP-based approaches
[
6
]
rely on a known
tool center point (TCP), are thus incompatible with variable end-effectors, and remain constrained by visibility requirements.

Beyond keypoint exploitation, several point cloud registration based approaches exist for marker-free hand-eye calibration
[
7
,
8
]
.
These methods either rely on learning to distinguish the robot point cloud or require additional, albeit arbitrary, known objects within the scene.
Closest to our work, in that they identify the robot via foundation segmentation models in image space
[
9
]
,
are EasyHeC and EasyHeC++
[
10
,
11
]
.
However, both rely on
differentiable rendering (DR)and do not address the question of unknown initialization, leading to significant runtime overhead and a higher risk of convergence to local minima.

To enhance runtime efficiency and robustness, we propose directly registering robot meshes to SAM 2-segmented point clouds, rather than performing DRon mesh projections (see Fig.
1
and Fig.
2
).
To this end, this paper contributes:

•

A novel, sample-efficient and computationally efficient ICPalgorithm, incorporating a robust point-to-plane (PTP)objective formulated on a Lie algebra, with applicability to any serial manipulator.

•

Comprehensive multi-system evaluation on real hardware, accompanied by an openly available benchmark dataset.

•

Open-source implementation
1
, including ROS 2 integration for seamless deployment.

Mathematical Notation

Throughout the paper, scalars are denoted as lower case Greek / Latin symbols (e.g.
\sigma,s
), vectors as lower case bold Greek / Latin symbols (e.g.
\bm{\sigma},\mathbf{s}
), and matrices as capital case bold Greek / Latin symbols (e.g.
\bm{\Sigma},\mathbf{S}
). A vector in homogeneous coordinates is denoted with a tilde (e.g.
\tilde{\bm{\sigma}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\sigma}&1\end{bmatrix}^{T}

II-B

Hydra: Robust Point-to-Plane ICP on Lie Algebra

II-B
1
Objective

We seek to align a robot mesh through a rigid body transformation
\bm{\Theta}\in\text{SE}(3)

with an observed point cloud, thus effectively solving for eye-to-hand calibration (cf. Fig.
1
).
We frame the optimization as a PTPobjective.
Given point correspondences
\mathcal{C}=\{\left(\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\right)|i\in\mathcal{I}\}
(see Section
II-D
), we optimize for

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}||\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{T}\left(\bm{\Theta}\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}-\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{i}\right)||^{2}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{T}\bm{\Theta}\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}-\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{i}\right)^{2}

(1)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}
denote normals on the mesh vertices
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{i}
, and
\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
denote points in the observed point cloud. We aim to minimize (
1
) using an ICPapproach
[
12
]
.

II-B
2
Iterative Optimization on the SE(3) Lie Group

To guarantee
\bm{\Theta}
be in SE(3), we suggest to solve for it in a Lie group iterative optimization approach
[
13
,
14
]
. The objective in (
1
) becomes

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{T}\bm{\Theta}_{j}\expl\left(\delta\bm{\theta}\right)\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}-\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{i}\right)^{2}

(2)

where given the current estimate
\bm{\Theta}_{j}
, we now seek to find an increment
\delta\bm{\theta}\in\mathfrak{se}(3)
at each step
\bm{\Theta}_{j+1}=\bm{\Theta}_{j}\expl\left(\delta\bm{\theta}\right)
.

II-B
3
Linearization of the Lie Group Objective

To solve for the increment
\delta\bm{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\delta\bm{\omega}&\delta\bm{\tau}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
, with rotation
\delta\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and translation
\delta\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, in a least squares manner, we next linearize the objective in (
2
). The exponential map
[
15
]

\expl:\mathfrak{se}(3)\rightarrow\text{SE}(3)
has the closed form solution

\expl\left(\delta\bm{\theta}\right)=\exp\left(\delta\bm{\theta}_{\dagger}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\expl\left(\delta\bm{\omega}\right)&\mathbf{L}\left(\delta\bm{\omega}\right)\delta\bm{\tau}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\end{bmatrix}

(3)

where

\delta\bm{\theta}_{\dagger}=\begin{bmatrix}\delta\bm{\omega}_{\times}&\delta\bm{\tau}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\end{bmatrix}

(4)

is the matrix representation of
\delta\bm{\theta}
,

\expl\left(\delta\bm{\omega}\right)=\mathbf{I}_{3}+\frac{\sin(\delta\omega)}{\delta\omega}\delta\bm{\omega}_{\times}+\frac{1-\cos(\delta\omega)}{\delta\omega^{2}}\delta\bm{\omega}_{\times}^{2}

(5)

as per
the Rodrigues’ formula with
\delta\omega=||\delta\bm{\omega}||
, and

\mathbf{L}(\delta\bm{\omega})=\mathbf{I}_{3}+\frac{1-\cos(\delta\omega)}{\delta\omega^{2}}\delta\bm{\omega}_{\times}+\frac{\delta\omega-\sin(\delta\omega)}{\delta\omega^{3}}\delta\bm{\omega}_{\times}^{2}

(6)

Finally, linearizing (
5
) and (
6
) for small
\delta\omega
, turns (
3
) into

\expl\left(\delta\bm{\theta}\right)\approx\mathbf{I}_{4}+\delta\bm{\theta}_{\dagger}

(7)

II-B
4
Lie Algebra Linearization of the PTP Objective

The action of the exponential
\expl\left(\delta\bm{\theta}\right)\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
in (
2
) can now be linearized through (
7
) as follows

\begin{split}\expl&\left(\delta\bm{\theta}\right)\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\approx\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}+\begin{bmatrix}\delta\bm{\omega}_{\times}&\delta\bm{\tau}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\end{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\\
&=\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}+\begin{bmatrix}-\mathbf{o}_{i_{\times}}\delta\bm{\omega}+\delta\bm{\tau}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\\
&=\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}+\begin{bmatrix}-\mathbf{o}_{i_{\times}}&\mathbf{I}_{3}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\end{bmatrix}\delta\bm{\theta}:=\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}+\mathbf{D}\left(\mathbf{o}_{i}\right)\delta\bm{\theta}\end{split}

(8)

Plugging (
8
) into (
2
) finally yields

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{T}\bm{\Theta}_{j}\mathbf{D}\left(\mathbf{o}_{i}\right)\delta\bm{\theta}+\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{T}\bm{\Theta}_{j}\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}-\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{i}\right)^{2}

(9)

which represents a standard least squares problem

||\mathbf{A}\delta\bm{\theta}-\mathbf{B}||^{2}

(10)

where we define
\mathbf{a}_{i}:=\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{T}\bm{\Theta}\mathbf{D}\left(\mathbf{o}_{i}\right)
,
\mathbf{A}:=\left[\mathbf{a}_{0};...;\mathbf{a}_{N-1}\right]
,
b_{i}:=\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{T}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{i}-\bm{\Theta}_{j}\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\right)
, and
\mathbf{B}:=\left[b_{0};...;b_{N-1}\right]
.

II-B
5
Robust Formulation

To account for potential outliers in the observed point cloud
\tilde{\mathbf{o}_{i}}
, we treat (
10
) under an
iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS)approach
[
16
]
. This turns (
10
) into

||\mathbf{W}\left(\mathbf{A}\delta\bm{\theta}-\mathbf{B}\right)||^{2}

(11)

where the weights
\mathbf{W}=\diag\left(w_{\kappa}\left(b_{0}\right);...;w_{\kappa}\left(b_{N-1}\right)\right)
associated with the Huber loss are given through the current residuals
b_{i}

w_{\kappa}(b_{i})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\,\,|b_{i}|\leq\kappa\\
\kappa/|b_{i}|&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(12)

The typical choice
\kappa=1.345\sigma
provides robustness whilst retaining appropriate properties when the errors are Gaussian. We estimate the standard deviation of the noise
\sigma
using the current residuals via the
median absolute deviation (MAD)

\sigma\approx\frac{\median\left(|\mathbf{B}-\median\left(\mathbf{B}\right)|\right)}{0.6745}.

(13)

II-C

Point Clouds from Depth Maps and Segmentations

Taking advantage of multiple robot configurations in the objective function (
2
), we extract robot point clouds through instance segmentations applied to depth maps and fuse them in a combined point cloud
\{\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\}
(cf. Fig.
2
).
We initially segment the serial manipulator in RGB space through prompting SAM 2
[
9
]
, making the proposed approach generally applicable to any robot. Critically, we only keep the boundaries of segmentations via erosion. The eroded segmentations are then applied to the respective depth maps for masking only relevant areas (see Fig.
1
). This procedure significantly reduces the complexity of correspondence finding, which scales quadratically (see Section
II-D
), and favors the surface sliding mechanism of the PTPobjective (
1
).
Using the camera intrinsics
\mathbf{K}
, we then project depth maps into camera coordinates and obtain
\{\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\}
.

II-D

Algorithmic Implementation Remarks

The proposed Hydra algorithm draws its naming from registering robot meshes fused across multiple configurations
(cf. Fig.
1
).
We compute forward kinematics using PyTorch Kinematics
[
17
]
for each configuration under measured joint states
\mathbf{q}_{c}
, and transform individual link meshes accordingly. For an initial estimate
\bm{\Theta}_{0}
, we compute mesh and point cloud centroids for each configuration
\mathbf{q}_{c}
, then align them using the Kabsch-Umeyama algorithm
[
18
]
.
Hydra then runs an outer and an inner optimization loop. Given the current best estimate
\bm{\Theta}_{j}
, closest correspondences
\mathcal{C}
are sought for exhaustively between meshes
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{i}
and point clouds
\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
by evaluating the pairwise Euclidean distance, but on a per configuration
\mathbf{q}_{c}
basis.
The inner optimization loop then updates
\bm{\Theta}_{j+1}=\bm{\Theta}_{j}\expl\left(\delta\bm{\theta}\right)
using the linear least squares solution of (
11
).

III-A

Second, we investigated the classical marker-based eye-to-hand formulation

Fourth, we benchmarked against the marker-free EasyHeC DR-based method.

III-A
2
Quasi Task Space Metric

The robust localization of AprilTag centers
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{i}
in image space allows for providing precise ground truth. Given the calibrated robot base pose in the camera frame
\prescript{b}{c}{\bm{\Theta}}
and the AprilTag 3D position in the robot base frame
\prescript{}{b}{\mathbf{a}_{i}}
, we can re-project the model 3D AprilTag center into the image plane via
\prescript{}{c}{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i}}=\prescript{c}{b}{\bm{\Theta}}\prescript{}{b}{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}_{i}
, and
\prescript{}{c}{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i}}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{z_{a}}\mathbf{K}\prescript{}{c}{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i}}
.
We can then compute the
mean pairwise distance (MPD)in pixels
\MPD\left(\prescript{}{c}{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{i}},\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{i}\right)
. This metric, whilst useful to distinguish errors above and below the camera resolution, depends on the AprilTag scale, which varies with robot and camera configurations. Therefore, we additionally express
\prescript{}{c}{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{i}}
in coordinates rescaled according to the AprilTag boundaries, centered at
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{i}
(see Fig.
3
).
Simply computing the average length of
\prescript{}{c}{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{i}}
in this AprilTag-centric coordinate system and scaling it by the AprilTag size then allow the estimation of task space accuracy. Importantly, our image-based evaluation remains robust even under RGB–depth sensor calibration misalignments, which may occur in structured-light RGB-D cameras. We note however that this approach does not capture errors along the camera axis.

III-A
3
Repeatability and Robustness

For repeatability and robustness assessments in task space, we performed Monte Carlo cross validation. For each robot, camera, and camera pose, we randomly selected 5 sets of size
N\in\{3,6,9,12\}
out of the 15 robot configurations. We then performed the calibration on N samples and tested against the quasi task space metric above on the remaining
15-N
samples. We averaged over the sets and propagate errors accordingly.

V
Discussion and Conclusions

In conclusion, this work demonstrates for the first time that image-space segmentation using a foundation model, similar to EasyHeC, provides a viable abstraction for solving hand-eye calibration across different serial manipulators and cameras. In contrast to EasyHeC, we perform registration in Cartesian space using a novel objective for robust PTPICPon a Lie algebra. This approach is simpler than DRand relies solely on matrix inversion, as shown in (
11
), for the linearized objective in (
2
). Furthermore, the proposed approach achieves a convergence rate that is two orders of magnitude faster than EasyHeC, with a threefold increase in success rate (see Section
IV-B
and Fig.
5
). Additionally, it operates in a truly marker-free manner, eliminating the need for marker-based initialization. Since EasyHeC is only available for the xArm 7 DoF, and due to observed structural inefficiencies, no final comparison regarding accuracy can be drawn. However, EasyHeC++ reports an error of
3\,\text{mm}
, which is consistent with our reported accuracy (see Fig.
5
). Further, we observe significant improvements over Shah
[
21
]
and Tsai
[
19
]
(see Table
I
, Fig.
5
).

A limitation of the proposed Hydra method is its reliance on RGB-D cameras and the SAM 2 segmentation, which requires some, albeit limited, user interaction (see Fig.
2
and Section
II-D
).
Currently, no effort has been made to adapt the optimization for eye-in-hand setups, and all investigated setups are static, requiring recalibration after displacement. However, it was found that our method exhibits strong accuracy with only three robot configurations (see Fig.
4
).

Despite its limitations, Hydra represents a significant contribution to marker-free hand-eye calibration. Future research should focus on dynamic setups and further minimizing the residual error to match the accuracy of the PnPmethod. The open-source nature of the benchmarking dataset and Hydra facilitates advancements in this domain. The mature Python packaging and integration with ROS 2 enable seamless deployment across various robotic systems.
