Integrating DNA methylation and clinical features to predict prostate cancer prognosis
Authors: Yi Qian, Wei Zhu, Zhenxuan Fang, Zeyu Luo, Wenhua Xie, Yifang Cao, Wei Chen, Huiyu Fu, Jiayu Peng, Lijun Zhang, Siyu Lei, Jing Jin, Jieyi Li, Xiaokai Zhao, Ziying Gong, Daoyun Zhang, Yi He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the relationship between DNA methylation patterns and clinicopathological characteristics in prostate cancer. We performed targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) on methylation sites linked to prostate cancer and calculated the corresponding methylation rates. Clinical samples were divided into training and prediction sets. In the training set, we utilized unsupervised clustering and Support Vector Machine (SVM) modeling to distinguish between prostate cancer and non-cancer samples. For predictions, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) assessed sample similarity, while SVM facilitated classification. Bayesian methods integrated probabilities to predict cancer status and cluster assignments. To address uncertainties identified by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), we validated our results using Random Forest Support Vector Machine (RFSVM), which highlighted significant methylation sites for SVM training. Depth-correction methods were applied to mitigate variations in sequencing depth.In the training dataset, the leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) prediction accuracy of RFSVM was 0.85 (AUC: 0.91); for RFSVM-depth, it was 0.83 (AUC: 0.93). The LOOCV prediction accuracy for Bayesian SVM (BSVM) was 0.87 (AUC: 0.94), decreasing to 0.83 (AUC: 0.91) with depth correction. In the test dataset, our Bayesian prediction achieved an accuracy of 0.8793 (sensitivity: 0.8182, specificity: 0.9167), which improved to 0.9655 with depth correction (sensitivity: 1.0, specificity: 0.9444). RFSVM demonstrated an accuracy of 0.8621 (sensitivity: 0.8182, specificity: 0.8889), dropping to 0.7759 with depth correction. Among 58 samples, predictions showed 67% complete consistency, with 84% consistency in methylation rates and 78% in specific methylation sites. Figure 6 presents a comprehensive comparison of the performance of RFSVM, BSVM, XBSVM, and RFESVM on training and test datasets, including feature selection and prediction consistency. Confusion matrices, ROC curves, and Venn diagrams were used to detail the feature importance and prediction consistency of each method.These findings highlight the importance of analyzing individual methylation sites and broader methylation patterns in understanding the role of DNA methylation in prostate cancer, providing valuable insights into the effects of data preprocessing and feature selection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16249-y.

Background

Prostate cancer is an epithelial malignant tumor that occurs in the male prostate [1]. Currently, the standard screening methods for prostate cancer combine digital rectal examination (DRE) with quantitative detection of serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA). However, these methods have limitations. The accuracy of DRE largely depends on the clinician’s experience. Additionally, benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) and other benign conditions can also elevate PSA levels. Therefore, when results from PSA testing or DRE are positive, there is an urgent need for supplementary screening tools to screen for prostate cancer [2].

DNA methylation serves as a crucial mechanism driving tumorigenesis and tumor progression [3]. In various types of tumors, distinct DNA methylation patterns can be observed. Under the catalysis of DNA methyltransferase (DNMT), the specific base on the DNA sequence uses S-Adenosyl methionine (SAM) as the methyl donor, and obtains a chemical modification process of a methyl group through covalent binding [4]. The most common base that can be methylated is Cytosine (C), that is, 5-mc methylation [5].

DNA methylation has emerged as a promising avenue for developing biomarkers for prostate cancer, with several studies highlighting its potential. According to a review, the methylation status of specific genes can provide crucial insights into tumor invasiveness and prognosis, enhancing early diagnosis, particularly in high-risk populations [6]. Further emphasis is placed on the correlation between hypermethylation of certain genes and disease progression [7].

Additionally, research demonstrated that analyzing methylation patterns in serum DNA could serve as a non-invasive diagnostic tool, effectively distinguishing prostate cancer patients from healthy individuals [8]. Another study explored the epigenetic aspects of prostate cancer, noting that DNA methylation influences not only tumor development but also the tumor microenvironment, potentially revealing new therapeutic targets [9].

Furthermore, the significance of DNA methylation in the disease’s pathogenesis suggests that targeting specific methylation markers could provide novel treatment strategies. Ongoing clinical trials are investigating the viability of using DNA methylation as a monitoring indicator for treatment [10]. Overall, these findings suggest that DNA methylation holds significant promise for improving diagnosis and treatment in prostate cancer management.

From the perspective of biological mechanisms, genome methylation can reveal gene regulatory information in different life processes [11], such as cell differentiation and disease progression; From the perspective of information accessibility and convenience, body fluid samples, including free nucleic acids, contain specific DNA methylation signals from tumors [12], which can be used as the detection source of biomarker samples to identify prostate cancer. Technically, the development of next generation sequencing (NGS) platforms, such as Roche 454, Illumina and Applied Biosystems, has fundamentally changed the methylation analysis method in genomics [13]. NGS platform can realize DNA methylation analysis under single base resolution. Therefore, studying prostate cancer through methylation is undoubtedly an excellent choice [14].

Bisulfite treatment has always been the gold standard for DNA methylation research [15–19]. Due to the stability of its transformation efficiency, it ensures the accuracy of subsequent methylation detection results [20]. When conducting large sample size detection for specific target areas, WGBS becomes a relatively expensive technical solution. Relatively speaking, targeted sequencing technology can conduct accurate research on interested target areas [18, 21]. In addition to ensuring that the specific DNA methylation signal of tumors can be fully mined, it can greatly reduce the economic cost and data analysis complexity, and has important clinical application and promotion value [22].

Based on the targeted sequencing technology of bisulfate treatment DNA methylation [23], thousands of layers of NGS sequencing can be carried out for the target genome area [15]. In addition to obtaining whether the target methylation site is methylated, the sequencing depth of the methylation site and the corresponding methylation rate data with statistical significance can be obtained [24]. Due to sample quality, in actual experiments, there may be some depth anomalies in the targeted sequencing of the target area, resulting in statistical distortion of methylation rate, which has a certain interference effect on machine learning algorithms that use methylation rate for clinical diagnosis. To address this issue, we have implemented a compensation strategy and comprehensive diagnosis and treatment algorithm that lacks information. In view of the specific mode of methylation in different life processes and the assumption that the methylation rate of methylation characteristic sites in the population follows the normal distribution, in this work, four classification evaluators are creatively developed by combining the methylation mode and the normal distribution assumption in two dimensions and combining the depth data.

On the methylation mode aspect, the training data is subjected to dimensionality reduction clustering analysis using umap and kmean algorithms for the actual methylation rate and corrected methylation depth data. The samples gathered together have similar clinical tumor subtypes. The prediction set data is classified by KNN algorithm and previous clustering data to obtain prior probability, and the SVM algorithm is used to obtain likelyhood value. By using bayesian machine learning classification formulas to mine predictive samples for classification, it is possible to mine richer clinical information.

On the methylation site aspect, using the RandomForest algorithm to extract the sites that contribute significantly to the classification performance, as the input SVM classifiers features. It is a common approach to mine biomarkers in prostate cancer.

Library preparation and sequencing

The nucleic acid extraction and conversion reagents produced by Jiaxing Yunying Medical Inspection Co., Ltd. were applied to extract nucleic acids from the test samples and perform bisulfite conversion. The Methylation-specific primers [38–43] (see Supplementary Table S1 for sequences and genomic coordinates of the 60 methylation sites) were used to perform PCR amplification. The amplified products were purified with nucleic acid purification reagents to construct sequencing libraries, which were then sequenced using the MiniSeq™ Mid Output Reagent Cartridge (Illumina) on a MiniSeq sequencer (Illumina).

Methylation analysis pipeline

Illumina’s official software bcl2fastq (v2.200.422) is applied to convert the bcl file generated by NGS500 sequencing into the corresponding fastq file for the sample based on the sample batch, number, and corresponding index sequence information. Then Fastp (V0.20.1) software is used for data preprocessing, including data quality control. Sequence alignment software bwa (V0.7.17) is used to match the sequences in the quality controlled fastq file to a customized methylation reference sequence, and then using samtools (V1.2) software to sort the sequences according to genome coordinates, generate a bam file, and build a file index. igvtools (V2.3.98) to perform base depth analysis on each site in the bam file obtained from data comparison, in order to obtain the methylation status of each CpG and non CpG site.

TargetMethSeq Algorithm

In numerous experiments and analysis processes, we often face challenges related to sample quality and technical biases. Firstly, inadequate data measurement depth at some methylation sites can distort methylation rates, affecting our understanding of methylation in health and disease states. Secondly, targeted sequencing carries a probability of bias, leading to methylation rate data that may not accurately reflect actual values due to technical limitations or experimental conditions.

To address these issues, we developed the analysis process outlined in Fig. 1. This process involves a multi-branch classification for diagnosis and classification analysis, focusing on methylation rate and depth data. We explore two dimensions: sample methylation patterns and the distribution characteristics of methylation sites within the population. This approach yields four types of classification results under a two-dimensional analysis strategy, providing a more comprehensive understanding of how methylation data manifests in different physiological states.

This integrated analytical method effectively addresses technical biases and sample quality issues by excluding outliers and filling in missing data. During the model training phase, it employs methylation models to perform unsupervised clustering, thereby achieving a more detailed classification of clinical samples at the methylation level. Furthermore, when predicting samples, this model can accurately predict their potential clinical characteristics based on methylation information, providing a solid basis for the scientific classification of samples.

In the Fig. 1, the upper left module collects patient urine samples and performs laboratory building; the lower left module conducts NGS sequencing and methylation analysis on the samples to calculate targeted methylation levels. The right side performs statistical analysis of the clinical sample methylation data, starting with the removal of outliers, followed by calculating the mean and variance of methylation sites, fitting a normal distribution to fill in missing values, and classifying the filled data using RFSVM. In the methylation pattern analysis, UMAP is used to reduce the dimensionality of samples without missing values to two dimensions, followed by K-means clustering into three categories. For samples with missing values, the distance between their non-empty parts and the K-means centers is calculated, identifying the samples closest to the cluster centroids as representative samples (Ai). Mode estimation is used to fill in missing values, and the distances (Si) between non-empty methylation sites and Ai are computed, with normalization applied to fill in empty values.The non-missing and filled methylation rate data are merged, followed by another UMAP dimensionality reduction and K-Means clustering to analyze clinical information, obtaining classification and supplementary data. Finally, KNN and SVM algorithms are used to classify the prediction set, extracting prior probabilities and likelihood values, and employing Bayesian machine learning to further explore the classification of prediction samples and related clinical information.

Methylation site

The specific analysis process for the methylation site aspect is as follows:

Step 1 Remove outlier

Use the formula \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu\pm n*\sigma$$\end{document} , where μ represents the mean value, σ denotes the variance, and n is a multiplier of the variance. This formula allows for adjustments based on specific usage requirements. It is crucial to eliminate outliers when the methylation rate surpasses this predefined range to optimize the data’s utility.

Step 2: Fill in missing values

Compute the mean and variance of the methylation rate for each site in both the negative and positive sample sets.

Fit a normal distribution model to each set of data.

Use the fitted normal distribution models to impute missing values.

Step 3: Correction of methylation rate by methylation depth data

By multiplying normalized methylation depth data with the corresponding methylation rate at each site, methylation rate data is corrected.

Step 4: Feature screening and classification analysis

Use the RandomForest algorithm for filtering the filled data.

Conduct classification analysis on the filtered data using the SVM algorithm. Employ the same method as mentioned above for the classification diagnosis of the sequencing depth-corrected data obtained in step 3.

Methylation mode

The specific analysis process, considering the methylation mode aspect, is as follows:

Step 1 Remove outlier

Use the formula \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu\pm n*\sigma$$\end{document}, where μ represents the mean value, σ denotes the variance, and n is a multiplier of the variance. This formula allows for adjustments based on specific usage requirements. If the methylation rate exceeds this range, outliers should be eliminated.

Step 2: UMAP dimensionality reduction

Apply the UMAP algorithm to reduce the dimensionality of the data to 2D.

Step 3: K-Means clustering analysis

Use the K-Means algorithm to perform clustering analysis on the reduced data. Determine the number of subsets in the clusters and the cluster centroids by iteratively adjusting the appropriate clustering parameter K.

Step 4: Finding representative samples

Identify samples within each cluster that are closest to the cluster centroid. Designate these samples as representative samples (Ai), where i = 1, 2, …, K. Use modal approximation to estimate missing values. Calculate the distances (Si) between the non-empty methylation sites containing empty samples and Ai. Normalize the K Si and fill in the empty values of the samples by multiplying the values of Si with the corresponding methylation sites of Ai.

Step 5: Merge data and repeat analysis

Merge the methylation rate data that does not contain missing values with the data filled with missing values. Perform dimension reduction using the UMAP algorithm again. Use the K-Means algorithm to perform cluster analysis on the merged data. Conduct clinical information mining and analysis on almost clustered classifications. Obtain classification information and supplementary data.

Step 6: Correction of methylation rate by methylation depth data

By multiplying normalized methylation depth data with the corresponding methylation rate at each site, methylation rate data is corrected.

Step 7: Predictive sample classification

Classify the prediction set data using the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm and the previous clustering data to obtain the prior probability. Use the Support Vector Machine (SVM) algorithm to classify the dataset and obtain the likelihood value. Utilize Bayesian machine learning classification formulas to mine predictive sample classifications and abundant clinical information.

For the classification diagnosis of the sequencing depth-corrected data obtained in step 6, the same method as mentioned above is employed.

Model hyperparameters

This study utilizes a variety of model hyperparameters to analyze methylation patterns effectively. The variance multiplier is set to 3, which is crucial for eliminating abnormal methylation values. For the UMAP algorithm, several parameters are configured to enhance the analysis: n_neighbors is set to 3, allowing a local perspective; min_dist is set to 0.5, maintaining some separation while preserving global structure; the metric used is correlation, focusing on similarity patterns; and random_state is set to 16 for reproducibility.

Clustering parameters indicate that both positive and negative methylation patterns are analyzed with K values of 3. The KNN algorithm employs a configuration of n_neighbors set to 5, balancing local and global information.

The SVM model is specified with a linear kernel, allows probability estimates (set to True), and is initialized with a random_state of 1 for consistent results. In the RFSVM algorithm, the Random Forest parameters include n_estimators set to 10 for a smaller ensemble, max_depth set to None to allow full growth, min_samples_split set to 2 to require minimal samples for node splitting, and bootstrap set to True to enhance robustness.

Finally, feature selection for the SVM model is based on the top 20 features determined by the Random Forest’s importance values, ensuring that the most relevant characteristics are utilized in the analysis. Specific parameter details, as shown in Table 1.

Results

Data

This study analyzed a total of 236 samples, comprising 144 positive samples with data on 60 corresponding methylated sites and their respective methylation rates, as well as sequencing depth (refer to Supplementary Table S3). Additionally, it included 92 negative samples, also with data on the same 60 methylated sites and their methylation rates, along with sequencing depth information (see Supplementary Table S4).

To achieve a balanced representation of negative and positive samples within the dataset, we randomly selected and duplicated 52 negative samples, increasing the count of negative samples to 144. This approach ensured that the sample set maintained an equal distribution of positive and negative samples, which is critical for the robustness of the analysis.

To effectively demonstrate the clustering effect of methylation patterns, we established a relatively high sample size: 230 samples were designated for the training set, while 58 samples were allocated for the prediction set. Furthermore, the 52 randomly selected duplicate negative samples were proportionately distributed in both the training and prediction sets, ensuring consistency and enhancing the validity of our findings. This methodological rigor provides a solid foundation for analyzing the methylation patterns associated with prostate cancer and supports the reliability of our conclusions.

Impute missing values using normal distribution for the RFSVM algorithm

Among the population, the mean and variance of methylation rates for each methylation site in prostate cancer positive and negative samples were calculated based on the normal distribution dimension of methylation sites. The corresponding mean and variance were then used to fit normal distribution models for both positive and negative samples, and the fitted normal models were employed to fill in missing methylation site information. The positive and negative data before filling in the missing values were also included. supplementary Table S5 provides the mean, variance, and corresponding normal distribution test p-values of methylation rates for each methylation site in both positive and negative sample groups.

LOOCV performance of RFSVM and BSVM on training data set

In the RFSVM algorithm, we initially fill missing values using a normal distribution and subsequently perform feature extraction using random forest. The contribution of each methylation site to classification performance is detailed in Supplementary Table S7, with corresponding contributions of depth-corrected methylation rates shown in Supplementary Table S8.

To further analyze this, we selected the top 20 methylation sites based on their contribution values. These selected sites were recalculated using random forest to determine their contributions, and the results were visualized in bar charts. Part A of Fig. 2 presents the bar chart of selected methylation sites based on methylation rates, while Part B displays those selected based on depth-corrected methylation rates.

These selected methylation sites are used as features to diagnose cases of prostate cancer and non-cancer, input into an SVM classifier for classification.

Furthermore, in the Bayesian algorithm, we use methylation pattern clustering similarity to fill in missing values and train the BSVM algorithm with the filled training set data.

Discussion

In 2023, the American Urological Association (AUA) and the Society of Urologic Oncology (SUO) reaffirmed PSA testing as the cornerstone of prostate cancer screening. However, our study highlights the ongoing limitations of traditional diagnostic methods. Despite the continued recommendation of PSA ≥ 4.0 ng/mL [44] as the positivity threshold, its insufficient specificity (only 41% in Asian populations [45]) and the invasive nature of confirmatory biopsies underscore the urgent need for more precise diagnostic tools. The variability in PSA-guided biopsy positivity rates (25%-42.5% [46–49]) and the risk of overtreatment identified by the ERSPC trial [47, 50] further emphasize the necessity for technological innovation in this field.

By leveraging methylation patterns and utilizing clinical clustering phenomena as prior probabilities, our approach aims to integrate multiple clinical indicators for a comprehensive analysis of prostate cancer diagnosis. However, this method has certain limitations:

Methodological challenges

The complexity of prior probability clustering analysis may result in incomplete clarification of the effectiveness and specificity of clinical information. Additionally, unsupervised clustering algorithms such as k-means, due to their exploratory nature, might prioritize achieving optimal clustering performance over identifying clinically meaningful clusters.

Data and practical constraints

The limited clinical sample data collected, along with partial loss or incompleteness of clinical information, currently hinders the construction of a robust training sample set for guiding the classification of prostate-related disease subtypes. Moreover, potential regional and ethnic biases, constraints on machine learning model complexity due to small sample sizes, and assumptions underlying missing data imputation methods further limit the generalizability of our findings.

Future research directions

To address these limitations, we propose several strategies for future research:

Data expansion

Enhance collaboration with medical institutions to systematically collect more clinical samples and representative methylation maps of prostate-related diseases. This effort will help optimize our models and algorithms, ultimately improving prediction performance and better assisting clinicians in diagnosing related diseases.

Algorithmic advancements

Develop more sophisticated clustering algorithms that balance mathematical performance with clinical interpretability, thereby enhancing the precision and reliability of diagnostic tools.

Methylation atlas construction

Appeal to oncology researchers and relevant institutions to construct a comprehensive methylation atlas for different subtypes of cancer. This atlas can serve as prior probabilities for cancer identification and diagnosis, providing a more rational and accurate Bayesian inference model for diagnostics.

By integrating these approaches, we aim to establish a more robust and clinically relevant diagnostic framework for prostate cancer. Our ultimate goal is to provide a foundation for personalized and precise treatment strategies, thereby reducing the burden of invasive procedures and improving patient outcomes. Future work should focus on expanding sample sizes across diverse geographic and ethnic backgrounds to validate and refine these methodologies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the method based on targeted DNA methylation sequencing holds significant potential and effectiveness in prostate cancer screening. Through analysis of methylation patterns and their relationship with clinical pathological features, we successfully categorized clinical samples into cancerous and non-cancerous groups and validated prediction accuracy using multiple models. During application, the combination of Bayesian methods and random forests with deep calibration significantly enhanced the precision and specificity of cancer prediction. Furthermore, our findings indicate good consistency in methylation rates and sites, providing robust support for further optimizing early screening and diagnostic methods for prostate cancer.

In conclusion, the strategy based on targeted DNA methylation sequencing not only effectively distinguishes prostate cancer from non-cancerous samples but also offers new perspectives and methods for personalized therapy and disease management. Future research should expand sample sizes and clinical validations to establish its broad application and value in clinical practice.
