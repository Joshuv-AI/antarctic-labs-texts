A New Phototherapy Regimen Coupled with Oral Vitamin D Supplementation, for the Long-Term Control of Atopic Dermatitis: A French Multicenter, Randomized, Crossover, Pragmatic Trial
Authors: Catherine Droitcourt, Sébastien Barbarot, Valérie Visseiche, Annabel Maruani, Monica Dinulescu, Laure Darrieux, Laurent Misery, Emilie Brenaut, Angèle Soria, Annie Vermersch, Hervé Maillard, Julien Seneschal, Alain Taieb, Cécile Chabbert, Henri Adamski, Alain Dupuy, Emmanuel Oger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe global burden of atopic dermatitis (AD) will continue to rise in the coming two decades, underscoring the need for affordable and globally accessible therapies given restricted access to high-cost medications in many regions. The study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of a novel phototherapy-based regimen as an add-on therapy, together with vitamin D supplementation, on long-term disease control in AD.MethodsThe PRADA trial combined two independent randomizations within the same population: an open-label, crossover comparison of narrowband ultraviolet B (NB-UVB) phototherapy versus observation, and a double-blind, parallel-group comparison of vitamin D versus placebo, both superimposed on standard topical anti-inflammatory treatments (TAT). It was conducted between 2015 and 2023 across 16 French hospital centers. The trial included patients aged ≥ 15 years with moderate-to-severe (IGA > 2) AD and with fewer than 100 lifetime phototherapy sessions. The primary outcome was a hierarchical composite endpoint based on: (1) repeated measures of Patient-Oriented SCORing Atopic Dermatitis (PO-SCORAD) severity score over 1 year; and (2) the cumulative use of TAT from October to March.ResultsA total of 53 participants were allocated to the vitamin D (26 received NB-UVB during the first period), and 57 to the placebo (27 received NB-UVB during the first period). A total of 93 participants were available for the main analysis for the primary endpoint. Vitamin D was significantly associated with lower PO-SCORAD scores compared with placebo (mean [95% CI] difference −5.6 [−12.1 to −1.78]), however, this effect size remained below the prespecified minimum clinically important difference (nine points). NB-UVB showed no significant effect on PO-SCORAD scores (mean difference: 1.25 [−3.90 to 1.40]). Adverse event incidence was 0.33 per 100 patient-months, mainly nonserious photosensitivity reactions (n = 38).ConclusionsIn the PRADA trial (underpowered due to a recruitment short fall), NB-UVB phototherapy showed no clinically meaningful benefit on the long-term control of moderate-to-severe AD. The observed benefit of vitamin D supplementation on PO-SCORAD, although statistically significant, did not reach the threshold for clinical relevance.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT02537509.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13555-026-01914-8.Keywords: Randomized crossover pragmatic trial, Atopic dermatitis, Vitamin D, Phototherapy

Key Summary Points

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a common pruritic inflammatory skin disease (up 10% in adults) [1] that typically follows a chronic course of flares and remissions. A recent systematic analysis from the Global burden of Disease Study provides evidence of an increase in the total number of AD cases projected until 2050 [2].

On the severity spectrum, in-between patients (not eligible for systemic therapy) are treated with topical corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors (topical anti-inflammatory treatments [TAT]) and UV-light therapy [3, 4]. Phototherapy (narrowband UVB and UVA1) can be used as a second-line treatment for the short-term control of moderate AD with demonstrated efficacy, when administered in intensive schedules of two to seven sessions a week for 2 or 3 months [5]. However, the major therapeutic challenge in this population remains the long-term control (LTC) of disease activity, and current TATs do not fully meet this need [6–8].

In the context of innovative but costly treatments such as biologics and JAK inhibitors, “add-on” low-cost therapies appear promising for long-term control and could provide relief to many patients worldwide. UV-light therapy is a good candidate. However, only short-term control has been evaluated and only intensive schedules are used in current practice. Vitamin D supplementation is another good candidate; several studies have shown that lower serum vitamin D levels are associated with more severe AD, although randomized-controlled trials have produced conflicting results with short-term assessments [9–11].

We hypothesize that spaced-out phototherapy sessions during winter and vitamin D, as an add-on therapy, could improve the long-term control of disease activity in patients with AD.

Since both phototherapy and vitamin D supplementation are good candidates for long-term disease control in AD, we propose to study these two treatments jointly.

Methods

Design and Setting

PRADA (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02537509) was a French multicenter, national, randomized (1:1), superiority, crossover trial (two periods) lasting 2 years (including two successive winters), and exploring add-on phototherapy (one winter under spaced sessions of phototherapy, one winter under observation) in subjects receiving standard care (i.e., TAT).

On the same population, we simultaneously tested, through a second randomization, the long-term effect of oral supplementation of vitamin D versus placebo in a randomized, superiority, double-blind, parallel-group trial. The two randomizations (phototherapy sequence; vitamin D/placebo allocation) were combined within a single trial, allowing estimation of the main effect of each intervention on long-term disease control, as well as a formal test of their interaction; vitamin D was not tested specifically as an add-on within the phototherapy sequence.

This trial has been designed as a pragmatic trial [12]: recruitment in primary care population; standard vitamin D supplementation schema not adapted to serum levels; choice of NB-UVB as phototherapy, strictly “as usual” TATs use, and no lifestyle-changing recommendations.

Participants

Eligible participants were patients aged 15 years or more with moderate-to-severe AD (Investigator Global Assessment > 2), with more than 2 years of AD evolution, a seasonality in severity (improvement declared during summer), having treated with TAT for at least 12 weeks and had symptoms requiring an increase in therapy.

Patients were ineligible if they had AD known to be aggravated by UV exposure, any case of contraindication for vitamin D or artificial or solar exposure, more than 100 previous phototherapy sessions in lifetime.

Interventions

Patients received 100.000 UI of oral vitamin D (cholecalciferol, UVEDOSE®, marketed by NextPharma LIMAY), or placebo once every 3 months in combination with NB-UVB phototherapy for 6 months during winter (October to March).

Phototherapy regimen included a period of escalation of 3 weeks (three sessions a week for 3 weeks) and after that every 2 weeks for 6 months in all. The details of dosage according to the phototype are given in the study protocol article [13]. The exposure could be altered according to clinical tolerance.

No AD-specific treatment other than TAT (used strictly “as usual” per the pragmatic design), phototherapy, and vitamin D/placebo was permitted during the study period.

Randomization

Eligible patients were assigned, in a 1:1 ratio, to either vitamin D or placebo and within each arm to either UV–observation or observation–UV sequence. A permuted block randomization scheme was used through an automated web-based and with stratification according to clinical site, age (three age classes) and disease severity (two classes: moderate or severe). The list was balanced by blocks of random size in order to ensure unpredictability.

This study was conducted as open-label regarding phototherapy, since blinding cannot be implemented, but it was double-blind for vitamin D therapy.

Primary and Secondary Outcomes

The primary efficacy outcome was a hierarchical criterion based on: (1) repeated measures of Patient-Oriented Severity of Atopic Dermatitis Index (PO-SCORAD) severity score over 1 year; (2) cumulative consumption of topical anti-inflammatory treatments (number of therapeutic unit) from October to March (winter).

The secondary efficacy outcomes were repeated measures over 2 years for: investigator-based severity scores including Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI), Severity of Atopic Dermatitis Index (SCORAD), IGA; patient-reported severity and quality of life scores including patient-Oriented Eczema Measure (POEM) and Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI); the serum concentration of vitamin D levels (25-(OH)-vitamin D); the total immunoglobulin (Ig)E serum levels; the proportion of weeks of satisfactory AD control; the inter-visit cumulate consumption of TAT; and the synthetic patient-reported satisfaction at the end of each winter.

Sample Size

We used GLIMMPSE (general linear multivariate model power and sample size) [14], as for a two-arm trial. We compared the sample size calculations between (1) a two parallel arm trial and (2) a crossover trial, considering only the last measure as the outcome, to check that the sample size reduction was not too optimistic. Finally, anticipating that 10–15% of patients will not complete the entire study (two 1-year sequences), the planned number of subjects to be enrolled was set at 200. Details are provided in the study protocol and the statistical analysis plan [13]. No interim analysis was scheduled.

Statistical Methods

The summary statistics were: the area under the curve (AUC) and the mean amplitude of PO-SCORAD score ranges. Descriptive statistics [mean and standard deviation (SD)] of the PO-SCORAD for each visit will also be provided. Normal distribution is assumed in the modeling analysis for the primary outcomes (PO-SCORAD severity score over two 1-year periods; cumulate consumption of TAT during winter) and this was checked at this stage. The pattern of missing values for PO-SCORAD was described.

Analytic analyses were performed according to intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. The full analysis set (FAS) consisted of all randomized patients excepted those who lack any postrandomization data.

The first primary outcome was based on repeated measures (up to 12 PO-SCORAD measures for each 1-year period, collected every 4 weeks) and was analyzed using a mixed model with random subject effects. The second primary outcome was the cumulate consumption of TATs (number of tubes) during a 6-month period (October to March). It was analyzed using a mixed model with adjustment on age as a continuous variable, and disease severity (moderate versus severe).

We processed a time alignment using the month as a time scale, with the first NB-UVB session as an index date; this was an attempt to reduce deviation from the assumption measurements were evenly spaced. PO-SCORAD measurement before the index date had a negative integer and were considered baseline values. For the period without NB-UVB, the date of first NB-UVB plus or minus 365 days was considered as the index date. PO-SCORAD measurement in the last 3 months of the first period was used as baseline values for the second period. We fitted mixed models with autoregressive lag 1 variance–covariance structure. The mixed model compared means of a between-subjects factor (NB-UVB) adjusting for (averaging over) a within-subjects factor (month). A multiplicity adjustment strategy used a hierarchical closed test procedure, where the primary endpoints are ordered as follows: (E1) PO-SCORAD severity score over 1 year and (E2) cumulate consumption of TATs over 6-month period (October to March). We tested E1 and E2 sequentially at the same two-sided level of 0.05; we tested E1 first, if it proved significant, we tested E2.

For sensitivity analyses, we implemented two common strategies to formulate missing not at random (MNAR) assumption: control-based pattern imputation and delta-adjusted pattern imputation. We anticipated monotone missing patterns but also envision to handle arbitrary missing patterns, and then we used a MNAR statement together with fully conditional specification (FCS) which handle both monotone and nonmonotone missing patterns.

Additional information about the study methods are available in the study protocol article [13]. Data were analyzed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

Ethical Approval

The protocol was approved on 18 August 2015 by the “Comité de protection des Personnes” (authorization no. 6 – 887) and received authorization from ANSM on 27 July 2015 (authorization no. 150603A-42). The study was conducted in compliance with French law no. 2004-806 of 9 August 2004 and with Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki (Ethical Principles for Medical Research involving Human Subjects, Tokyo 2004). Participant consent was obtained for participation in this study.

Safety

All randomly assigned and treated patients were included in the safety analysis. A total of 80 patients experienced at least on adverse event, with a maximum of nine events per patient. A total of 202 adverse events were reported, of which 17 (8.42%) were serious. Among these, 9 were of high intensity, 3 events (2 carcinomas and one pregnancy) led to definitively stopping trial (per investigator or sponsor decision). Only one (a dispensation by error of an out-of-date placebo) was deemed related to the experimental therapy, as assessed by the clinical investigator. No withdrawal was related to a serious adverse event.

Among 40 adverse events classified as erythema or photosensitivity reaction, 38 [all nonserious, 7 (18.9%) of moderate intensity] occurred while on UV therapy sequence, and were causally related to phototherapy by clinical investigator. The median [min, first–third quartile, max] time to onset was 30 days [0, 7–99, 159]. These 38 events occurred in 30 patients over a cumulative time of UV exposure of 247,985 days; the incidence was 0.33 episode per 100 patient-months. The remaining two other events were classified as solar erythema or phototoxicity, as assessed by clinical investigator.

Discussion

In this pragmatic trial evaluating, within the same population, new phototherapy-based regimen and concomitant vitamin D supplementation, as an add-on therapies to TAT for the long-term control of AD in patients with moderate-to-severe disease in a primary care setting, the estimated effect size based on PO-SCORAD was null for NB-UVB phototherapy and −5.6 for vitamin D. Based on DLQI, the effect size was 1.7 for phototherapy and 1.1 for vitamin D. Considering the MCID for the PO-SCORAD score (nine points) [15] and for the DLQI (four points) [16], neither phototherapy nor vitamin D supplementation, whether alone or in combination, reached clinically meaningful benefit for the long-term control of AD, although the vitamin D effect on PO-SCORAD was statistically significant.

Recent advancements in understanding AD pathogenesis had led to an exponential expansion of targeted therapeutic, including biologics inhibiting the signaling of interleukin (IL)4 and/or IL13, IL31 and OX40 signaling, as well as JAK inhibitors. Current European guidelines recommend systemic treatment for patients with severe AD (based on SCORAD > 50), inadequate disease control, or significant impairment in daily life despite appropriate TAT. Patient care represents a substantial economic burden, particularly when modern targeted treatments are used. In the light of the expected increase of AD cases [2] and the high cost of targeted treatment not available in all countries of the world [17, 18], it is important to assess potentially effective and affordable low-cost therapeutic strategies for long-term disease control, benefiting both patients and public health. There is some evidence that phototherapy and vitamin D could represent such strategy for long-term AD control. Vitamin D may influence various steps in AD pathogenesis by suppressing Th1 lymphocytes production through the downregulation of interleukin 12, and by promoting the Th2 response via the upregulation of interleukins 4, 5 and 10 production [19]. However, placebo-controlled randomized trials testing vitamin D supplementation have produced conflicting results and have focused on short-term outcomes [20–22]. Phototherapy is included in both European and USA stepped-care plans [3, 4] for patients with moderate AD and has demonstrated efficacy on short-term disease control [23]. Furthermore, a recent Cochrane systematic review concluded that NB-UVB phototherapy is safe in patients with AD [24].

Because AD is a chronic inflammatory disease, maintaining long-term control of disease activity represents a major therapeutic challenge. Long-term control should be achieved by using “add-on” therapies, which are superimposed on standard care topical treatment. Systemic treatments are often used as LTC add-on treatments, but some raise unresolved safety issues [25, 26]. Pragmatic trials are the best suited design for evaluating add-on therapies in the context of LTC disease management [27]. Furthermore, the pragmatic approach allows for the inclusion of a representative AD population, including individuals with comorbidities, prior treatment exposure, and enhances the external validity of the study. In theory, this approach increases comfort for both clinicians and patients; however, it also introduces a risk of attrition bias, particularly in crossover designs, such as ours.

Strengths

The strengths of the present trial include (1) a pragmatic design reflecting real-life conditions, (2) long-term AD assessment based on serial patient-reported outcomes (PROs), and (3) evaluation of low-cost therapeutic strategy.

Limitations

This study also has several limitations. First, although we planned to recruit 200 patients, only 110 were ultimately enrolled. The main explanations for this lower-than-expected recruitment include the rapid expansion of the therapeutic pipeline, particularly following the success and FDA-approval of dupilumab, and the challenges of recruiting in a primary care population (despite several initiatives such as meetings with primary care dermatologists, and facilitating phototherapy in primary care settings). Promoting clinical research in primary care remains a key issue for improving the representativeness of trials in common chronic skin diseases, including AD [28, 29]. Second, compliance from patients with AD with repeated PRO completion was low and not constant, highlighting the need for future research to optimize PRO collection methods. The results remained unchanged despite the use of multiple imputation for missing data. Third, and most importantly, because only 110 of the planned 200 patients were recruited, the trial is likely underpowered; we therefore cannot determine, for phototherapy in particular, whether the absence of a significant effect reflects a true lack of efficacy or insufficient statistical power, and this finding should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of no effect.

Conclusions

In this pragmatic, randomized, superiority, cross-over trial, we were unable to demonstrate an effect of add-on phototherapy (spaced NB-UVB sessions during winter) and oral supplementation of vitamin D on patient-reported AD symptoms. It remains unclear whether this finding reflects a true absence of effect or a lack of power. Nevertheless, both interventions showed a good safety profile, supporting their potential use for selected patients requiring short-term relief under dermatologist supervision.
