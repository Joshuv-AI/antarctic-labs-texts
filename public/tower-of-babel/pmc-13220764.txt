Introduction

Primary cardiac tumours are rare, with metastases being 20–30 times more prevalent.1 The majority of primary cardiac tumours (75–90%) are benign, though even benign tumours can be problematic due to mechanical and electrical interference with normal heart function.1 Cardiac tumours are generally asymptomatic, being discovered fortuitously during imaging exams, often for unrelated causes.1 Of the malignant tumours, undifferentiated pleomorphic cardiac sarcomas are the most common. We present the case of a 72-year-old man presenting to the emergency department with non-specific symptoms in whom an undifferentiated pleomorphic cardiac sarcoma was discovered.

Case report

A 72-year-old man presented to the emergency department with complaints of fatigue and loss of appetite persisting for several weeks. These symptoms had progressively worsened, causing the patient to discontinue his usual exercise routine. Three weeks earlier, he had consulted his general practitioner and was diagnosed with NYHA class III following a gradual increase in fatigue and dyspnoea, which had occurred over the previous three months.

The patient had a long occupational history as a carpenter, during which he was regularly exposed to toxic solvents.

Four years earlier, he had experienced an acute coronary syndrome, which was managed with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) of the right coronary artery. At his last cardiology follow-up, over a year prior to the current presentation, no new symptoms or clinical concerns were reported.

On examination, the patient appeared dyspnoeic and had elevated blood pressure (161/92 mmHg). However, the remainder of his physical examination, including cardiac auscultation, was unremarkable.

An ECG was performed, revealing no abnormalities. Blood tests revealed elevated levels of CRP (46,6 mg/L), sodium (133 mmol/L), ALAT (64 U/L), GGT (96 U/L), LDH (276 U/L), ALP (132 U/L), and NT-proBNP (659 pg/mL).

Due to his persistent dyspnoea, a pulmonary CT angiography of the chest was performed to rule out pulmonary embolism. This revealed a tissue density intra-cavitary mass of 9 × 10 × 5 cm at the junction of the inferior vena cava and the right atrium, extending into the right ventricle, a 5 cm × 4 cm extracardiac mass abutting the right side of the pericardium, multiple sub-pleural masses of around 2 cm in size and minor bilateral pleural effusion (Figure 1). Furthermore, a large pericardial effusion was present, contributing to the patient’s dyspnoea.

Contrast-enhanced CT scan showing an intra-atrial mass (A, white asterisk), a metastasis attached to the pericardium (B, black asterisk) and a metastatic mass attached to pleura (C, arrow).

Due to his relatively unstable state, the patient was transferred to a university hospital for further investigation of the cardiac mass, with a cardiac ultrasound and pericardiocentesis performed. The ultrasound confirmed the presence of a mass within the right atrium, protruding into the right ventricle and causing significant tricuspid stenosis (mean gradient 8.5 mmHg) (Figure 2) with the pleural effusion being a possible sign of the suspected right heart failure.

B-mode ultrasound image showing the tumour (asterisk) protruding into the right ventricle (arrow).

After taking the patient’s overall presentation into account, the assumption was made that the sub-pleural masses were likely of the same origin as the cardiac mass. After careful consideration and multidisciplinary discussion, it was decided that biopsy of one of the sub-pleural masses would carry far less risk than attempting to biopsy the intra-cardiac mass and, if the assumption that they were indeed metastases of a primary cardiac tumour was correct, would yield a diagnosis for the cardiac tumour. Therefore, a transthoracic biopsy of a sub-pleural metastasis was performed, occurring two days after the CT angiography, with the sample being sent to pathology. Histopathological investigation, including genetic and immunohistochemical analysis of the samples, was performed. A next-generation sequencing panel of 400 genes and a panel of 145 possible gene fusions did not yield any specific mutations, fusions, or exon skipping. However, upon analysis of all of the diagnostic data, the diagnosis of an undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma was made.

A PET-CT was performed in order to stage the oncological disease, showing a hypermetabolic intracavitary mass arising from the right atrium, extending into the vena cava and right ventricle (Figure 3). Hypermetabolic foci consistent with metastases located in the sub-pleura, lung, pericardium, left diaphragm, as well as the supra and infra-diaphragmatic lymph nodes were present.

PET-CT scan showing a hypermetabolic intra-atrial mass (A, white asterisk), a hypermetabolic metastasis attached to the pericardium (B, black arrow) and a hypermetabolic metastatic mass attached to pleura (C, white arrow).

This was followed by a cardiac MRI, which revealed a large intracavitary mass with heterogeneous signal on both T1 and T2-weighted imaging (Figure 4). The pericardial metastasis adjacent to the left ventricle displayed an identical signal.

T1 weighted late gadolinium enhancement image showing heterogenous contrast uptake by the tumour (arrow) which lies in the right atrium and protrudes into the right ventricle.

Finally, two weeks after the initial diagnosis, a decision was made to operate, with a large tumour being resected to relieve the symptoms of heart failure (Figure 5).

Image of the surgical specimen, showing the large oval shaped tumour that was resected.

Given the patient’s age and the cardiac involvement, palliative treatment with pazopanib was initiated for the metastatic disease. However, due to digestive side effects, pazopanib was stopped after two weeks. Carboplatin was then initiated as a second-line treatment. However, as the disease progressed on a 3-month follow-up scan this treatment was stopped, and the patient was treated with gemcitabine and decetaxel.

The patient is currently receiving palliative treatment for the metastatic disease and is still alive at 1 year after the initial diagnosis.

Discussion

Primary cardiac tumours are extremely rare, with an incidence of only 0.001–0.28% on autopsy series, with up to a quarter being malignant.1–3 Of those malignant tumours 95% are sarcomas.2 When accounting for all cardiac tumours, undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas only represent 1.7% of these.3

Undifferentiated pleomorphic cardiac sarcomas are aggressive tumours that most commonly occur in the sixth decade of life, and may have a greater incidence in males and Caucasians, though this is debated.2,4,5 They can occur in any part of the heart, with the symptoms depending on the location, though they commonly occur in the left atrium.1,5,6 The symptoms are generally non-specific, such as dyspnoea, heart failure, palpitations and systemic symptoms.2,5,6 This makes diagnosing these patients based on symptoms alone very challenging, with the mass often being found incidentally during imaging.

Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas are often large at the time of diagnosis, frequently found to be over 5 cm at the time of diagnosis, with 10 cm tumours, as was the case of our patient, not being infrequent.3,7,8 Tumours of less than 4 cm in size have a better prognosis.8

There is, to our knowledge, no established causal relationship between being a carpenter and the development of undifferentiated pleomorphic cardiac sarcomas, possibly owing to the rarity of the tumour. However, we cannot exclude that this was a contributing factor due to the known higher incidence of a multitude of cancers in people practicing this profession.9

The diagnostic workup can include transoesophageal echocardiography, which can examine the size, location, mobility, and attachment of the tumour.6 CT, PET-CT, and MRI can aid the differential diagnosis, inform on the extent of the tumour, and can assist in surgical planning.5,6 In this context, the CT and MRI are essential for narrowing the differential diagnosis, whereas the PET-CT is essential to search for distant metastasis and staging, an important consideration as 80% of patients have disseminated disease at the time of diagnosis.5,6

Features of malignancy include rapid growth, local invasion, involvement of multiple cardiac chambers, pericardial effusion, and haemorrhage and necrosis.5

Surgical resection is the mainstay of treatment, with adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy, which are, however, used despite being unproven benefit.2,6

Despite treatment, the prognosis of undifferentiated sarcomas is particularly poor, with the longest known survival of a patient being 8 years.10 Debulking surgery had a role in these patients, being used for symptom relief.5

In cases like that of our patient, this can be a challenge as making a diagnosis when the patient already has metastatic disease means that curative tumorectomy is not feasible. Furthermore, if patients do not respond well to chemotherapy and continue to show disease progression, as was the case with our patient, the treatment may become palliative.

With regard to the radiological features, undifferentiated pleomorphic cardiac sarcomas present as broad-based masses with heterogeneous echogenicity on ultrasound.1 When imaged using CT, they are large, intracavitary, irregular, and hypoattenuating masses.1 On MRI imaging, the mass will typically be iso or hypertense on T1 and T2-weighted imaging, with variable enhancement on first-pass perfusion and late gadolinium imaging.1 PET-CT is reserved for staging.1 The most common differential is cardiac angiosarcomas, which may have similar imaging features due to haemorrhage and necrosis, though there are a number of other similar sarcomas which cannot be differentiated using non-invasive imaging alone.1 Other differential diagnoses include other sarcomas, lymphomas, and mesothelioma.11 Undifferentiated pleomorphic cardiac sarcomas are rare and highly aggressive. Patients generally present with non-specific symptoms, with most tumours being found fortuitously. Non-invasive imaging alone is unable to establish a firm diagnosis, requiring pathological analysis. Surgical treatment, often palliative in nature, aims to clarify the diagnosis while concurrently managing symptoms.
