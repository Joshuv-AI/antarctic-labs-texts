Structural plasticity of human leptin binding to its receptor LepR
Authors: Yufeng Xie, Xiaoxiong Li, Jianxun Qi, Guijun Shang, Defen Lu, George Fu Gao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Leptin receptor (LepR) signaling plays an essential role in balancing food intake and energy expenditure. The architecture of LepR signaling assembly is critical for its function. In this study, we determined the structures of three distinct conformations of human leptin–LepR using cryo-electron microscopy at resolutions of 3.88, 3.77, and 3.58 Å. Both 2:2 and 3:3 stoichiometric assemblies were observed, and the complexes exhibited asymmetric open conformations. Leptin undergoes substantial rearrangement of its flexible regions to accommodate binding to LepR. The assembled leptin–LepR complexes connect through a “hand-in-hand” geometry. The open, interlocked 3:3 trimeric assembly results from the engagement of a third leptin–LepR heterodimer with a 2:2 dimer. The asymmetric geometry of LepR is substantially distinct from that of other gp130 cytokine homologs, and that may be due to the twisted and rigid interface between the D3 and D4 domains. These results highlight the distinct engagement of leptin with LepR and provide important insights into the structural plasticity of LepR-signaling assemblies.

Introduction

Leptin plays pleiotropic roles in the regulation of both food intake and energy expenditure as well as in immunity, bone metabolism, reproductive function, and cancer [1]. Leptin-mediated signaling dysfunction is associated with obesity, delayed puberty, and infertility [[2], [3], [4]]. Leptin is an adipocyte-derived hormone that promotes satiety and energy sufficiency by binding to leptin receptors (LepRs) and is expressed primarily in the hypothalamus [5]. LepR is a potential therapeutic target, and multiple LepR agonists or antagonist modulators have been developed for treating leptin-related diseases [6,7]. Currently available agonist and antagonist drugs against LepRs include recombinant leptin mutants, leptin peptide antagonists, soluble receptor proteins, and LepR-targeting antibodies. Setmelanotide, which activates the LepR downstream pathway, is a drug for treating obesity caused by LepR deficiency that has been approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration [8]. However, the development of drugs targeting LepR has been hindered because the structure of the leptin–LepR complex remains unknown.

Leptin is a hormone of approximately 16 kDa encoded by the obesity gene and is primarily produced in adipose tissue [9,10]. Leptin levels are strongly correlated with body fat [11,12]. LepR is a member of the gp130 cytokine receptor family and is widely expressed in the brain [[9], [13]]. Secreted leptin can cross the blood–brain barrier and bind to LepR in the hypothalamus [14]. The binding of leptin to LepR activates the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription signaling pathway, resulting in the production of anorexigenic peptides, which increase energy expenditure and reduce food intake [15].

Alternatively spliced LepR has six isoforms, all of which share a common extracellular domain (ECD) [13]. The signaling assembly of leptin and LepR has long been debated. Multiple studies have shown that leptin binds to LepR in 1:1 stoichiometry [16,17]. However, a 2:4 stoichiometry between leptin and LepR has been proposed as a result of the three conserved epitopes present in leptin [18,19]. The ECD of human LepR is composed of six functional domains: an N-terminal domain (NTD), cytokine receptor homology domain 1 (CRH1), an immunoglobulin-like domain (IgD), and cytokine receptor homology domain 2 (CRH2), followed by two membrane-proximal fibronectin type III (FnIII) domains [19]. Mutagenesis analysis has revealed two distinct leptin-binding regions within the LepR [20,21]. The first binding region, termed “site 2,” is located within the CRH2 domain and is found to be responsible for high-affinity binding with leptin [21]. The second binding region, termed “site 3,” is located within the IgD and exhibits low binding affinity with leptin [20]. The mechanism by which LepR interacts with leptin on the cell surface through high-affinity site 2 and low-affinity site 3 to form a functional leptin–LepR assembly complex is controversial. A 2:2–stoichiometry binding mode has been proposed based on that of other gp130 cytokine family receptors [22]. However, other studies have revealed that the leptin–LepR signaling complex contains more than two receptors [19]. Human and mouse LepRs share about 75% amino acid identity in the ECD region. Whether human and mouse LepRs adopt similar assembly stoichiometry remains unknown.

Here, we determined the high-resolution structure of the human leptin–LepR signaling assembly complex using cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM). Structural analysis revealed that leptin did not form the expected symmetrical complex with LepR, similar to other gp130 family members. Both 2:2 and 3:3 asymmetric complex structures were observed in an open conformation. The flexible regions of leptin were substantially rearranged to engage with LepR. The 3:3 asymmetric complex adopts “hand-in-hand” stoichiometry and forms a pseudo-interlocked asymmetric leptin–LepR trimer. The asymmetric LepR assemblies are substantially distinct from that of gp130 cytokine family receptor homologs. These findings improve our understanding of the structural plasticity of the leptin–LepR signaling assembly at a molecular level and provide a foundation for the future design of drugs targeting LepR.

Results

Overall Architecture of the Leptin–LepR Complex

To obtain the human leptin–LepR complex, the ECD of human LepR and leptin were constructed and co-expressed in HEK-293F cells (Figure 1A). Stable leptin–LepR complex proteins were purified by size-exclusion chromatography (Figure S1). The flat peak in size-exclusion chromatography indicated inhomogeneous components, which were further verified by a single-particle cryo-EM study. To capture the structural assemblies of the leptin–LepR complex, cryo-EM samples were prepared. Cryo-EM analysis revealed the distinct oligomeric states of the leptin–LepR complexes, and further analyses suggested 2:2 and 3:3 asymmetric stoichiometries (Figure S2). The EM maps showed clear leptin–LepR interaction features (Figure S3). The percentages of the 2:2 and 3:3 asymmetric complexes were similar (Figure S4). Further analysis of the 3:3 stoichiometry revealed distinct open conformations (small or large gaps) (Figure S5). 2:2 and 3:3 Asymmetric Stoichiometry of the Leptin–LepR Complex

Analysis of the open 2:2 stoichiometric complexes using single-particle analysis yielded a three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of the leptin–LepR dimer complex of 3.58 Å (Figures 1B and S2, Table S1). The 2:2 stoichiometric complex contains two leptins and two LepRs. In the 2:2 dimer, IgD (residues 332–429), CRH2 domains (residues 430–637), and one leptin are well determined. Main chains of the second CRH1 subdomain (residues 234–331) and the first FnIII domain (residues 638–731) are traced, while the first CRH1 subdomain (residues 122–233) and the second FnIII domain (residues 732–839) were predicted using AlphaFold2 and were fitted into the EM density map. Because the NTD (residues 22–121) is flexible, the main chain predicted by AlphaFold2 is partially fitted into the relatively low density [34]. The LepR adopts a twisted architecture, in which, the CRH1 and IgD, two domains of CRH2, and two FnIII domains are bent-linked. Leptin binds to the groove of the two CRH2 subdomains of LepR at site 2. In addition, leptin binds to the IgD domain of an additional LepR at site 3, leading to the formation of an asymmetric leptin–LepR dimeric complex. Although the receptors share a similar overall architecture of the ECD domain and the ligands exhibit structural similarity with other gp130 cytokine family members, their twisted and asymmetric structures are substantially distinct from those of the symmetrical granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (GCSF)-GCSFR and interleukin (IL)-6–IL-6Rα–gp130 complexes [23], [24].

Analyses of the two distinct open 3:3 stoichiometric complexes using single particles yielded a 3D reconstruction of the leptin–LepR complex of 3.88 Å and 3.77 Å (Figures 1C, 1D, and S2, Table S1). These complexes contained three leptin and three LepR units, forming an open asymmetric leptin–LepR trimer. Two of the three leptins were bound to two LepRs simultaneously, and the third was bound to one LepR, forming a “hand-in-hand” architecture. The widths of the open gaps of the two distinct leptin–LepR trimer complexes were different (∼50.2 Å and ∼73.2 Å, respectively) (Figure 1E).

The 2:2 and 3:3 asymmetric stoichiometries of the leptin–LepR complexes described earlier are all open forms. The two open ends of the leptin–LepR complexes were exclusively gapped between site 3 in leptin and the IgD domain of LepR, which presumably formed a low binding affinity interface. Therefore, the low binding affinity interface of site 3 between leptin and LepR may result in varied 2:2 and 3:3 stoichiometries of LepR signaling assemblies mediated by leptin.

Detailed Interaction Between Leptin and LepR

Leptin interacted with LepR via two distinct binding interfaces (Table S2). The site 3 region of leptin binds to the IgD and forms a low-affinity leptin–LepR interface. The formation of the low-affinity leptin–LepR interface is mediated by complementary surfaces composed of residues from αC’, the N terminus of αD and AB loop of leptin, and residues from the β6 strand of the IgD and surrounding loops of LepR (Figure 2A and 2B). The residue Y140 in the N terminus of leptin αD is encompassed by LepR IgD loops and forms hydrogen bonds with N371 and R402. The binding is further stabilized by hydrogen bonds between S138 of leptin and the residue H420 of the LepR IgD. The neighboring residues, A137-G139, were buried in the groove formed by residues H420-Y422 of the IgD, with the formation of a hydrogen bond between the main chain of A137 and the side chain of R421. The site 2 region of leptin was inserted into the groove between the two CRH2 subdomains, D4 and D5, which constitute the high-affinity leptin–LepR interface. The high-affinity leptin–LepR interface mainly involves αA and αC of leptin, which engage with residues from loops of CRH2 (Figure 2C and 2D). The residues in the loops of the D4 domain of CRH2 spanned the hydrophobic groove formed by the two parallel helices, leptin αA and αC, forming a “Z”-like structure, which was further stabilized by an extensive network of hydrogen bonds. The I503 in the D4 domain of CRH2 form two hydrogen bonds with Q96 in the αC domain of leptin, flanked by four hydrogen bonds between LepR Y472–leptin D30, LepR L506–leptin N103, and LepR L442–leptin R41. The D5 domain of CRH2 interacts with leptin αA, performing a secondary role in the binding interface.

Conformational Rearrangement of Leptin for its Interaction with LepR

The previously reported human apo leptin structure enabled us to investigate the conformational changes in leptin upon LepR engagement (Figure 3) [10]. The superimposition of apo leptin with bound leptin from the leptin–LepR complex yielded a root mean square deviation of 0.78 Å. The main differences between the free and bound leptin were in the regions involved in the low-affinity binding interface. The major conformational changes mainly involved the AB and CD loops. The position of residue A137 from αC’ in leptin was substantially shifted with a maximum distance of 11 Å after binding to the receptor. The rotation in free leptin αC’ disrupted the intersection of A137-G139 between αC’ and αD, forming a loop instead. LepR Assembly is Distinct from that of gp130 Cytokine Receptor Homologs

We further compared the leptin–LepR complex structure with gp130 cytokine family homologs to elucidate the unique features of the LepR signaling assembly. IL-6 and leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) exhibit similar binding modes with their receptors, the IL-6Rα and LIF receptor (LIFR), and the shared gp130 co-receptor (Figure 4A and 4B) [23]. The binding of leptin to LepR is similar to that between IL-6 and gp130 and is also similar to that between LIF and LIFR. The binding orientations of leptin to the LepR IgD, which constitutes the site 3 low-affinity binding interface, and the LepR CRH2 domain, which constitutes the site 2 high-affinity binding interfaces, showed no substantial differences from those of the IL-6 and LIF homologs (Figure 4C and 4D). Further comparative analysis revealed that the orientation of the LepR IgD (D3) to CRH2 (D4D5) was substantially distinct from that of gp130, LIFR, other gp130 family cytokine receptors, IL-23R, and GCSFR (Figure 4E) [24], [25]. The distinct twisted orientation between the IgD and CRH2 domain also fitted well with the LepR structure predicted by AlphaFold2 [34]. Detailed analyses revealed that the interface between the D3 and D4 domains of LepR was involved in multiple hydrophobic interactions formed by residues L341 and I428 from the D3 domain and L457, L519, and L522 from the D4 domain, which shaped the rigid orientation between D3 and D4 of the LepR (Figure 4F, Table S3). The twisted and rigid orientation between D3 and D4 of the LepR prevents the formation of a symmetrical dimeric receptor conformation, as observed in the IL-6-mediated receptor assembly. Discussion

In the present study, we determined the cryo-EM structure of the human leptin–LepR complex. The 2:2 and 3:3 asymmetric stoichiometric complexes were found to have similar percentages of occurrence (Figure S4). Notably, the 3:3 stoichiometric complexes exhibited open conformations with gaps of different sizes. The unique structural assemblies of the leptin–LepR dimer and trimers are substantially different from those previously observed with gp130 cytokine family receptors and have greatly expanded our understanding of cytokine receptor signaling assembly at a molecular level.

How LepR engages with leptin to form a functional signaling assembly has long been debated. Recently, during the preparation of our manuscript, two studies determined the high-resolution structures of the mouse leptin–LepR complex with oligomerization tags and revealed 2:2 or 3:3 stoichiometric assembly models [26,27]. Saxton et al. reported a high-resolution 2:2 stoichiometric structure of the mouse leptin–LepR complex, whereas Tsirigotaki et al. determined a high-resolution 3:3 stoichiometric structure of the mouse leptin–LepR complex [26,27]. Though a low-resolution structure of human leptin–LepR complex was resolved (∼6 Å) by Tsirigotaki et al., the molecular details of human leptin–LepR complex remained undetermined [26]. The 3:3 stoichiometric mouse leptin–LepR complex was found to be in a closed conformation, whereas both closed and open 3:3 stoichiometric complexes were observed for the human leptin–LepR complex. Notably, in a study by Tsirigotaki et al., to stabilize the trimeric complex of LepR, an additional trimeric GCN4 tag was added to the C-terminal region of mouse or human LepR. The external forces induced by these additional artificial trimeric tags on the LepR assemblies could not be fully excluded. Therefore, the 2:2 and 3:3 stoichiometric complexes of human leptin–LepR without the added oligomeric tag investigated here may indicate the native signaling assembly of the LepR mediated by leptin.

Mouse and human LepRs share a sequence identity of approximately 75%. Our structural analyses revealed that a low-affinity binding interface may contribute to the formation of 2:2 or 3:3 stoichiometries. The low-affinity interface of the mouse leptin–LepR assembly involves more hydrogen bond interactions than that of human leptin–LepR one, as observed in the present study. The 2D classification of single particles revealed that no closed 3:3 stoichiometric structure was observed. All the 3:3 stoichiometric complexes were found to be in an open conformation with gaps of different sizes. Notably, the 2:2 and 3:3 stoichiometric complexes presented similar percentages in the 2D classification. Cross-linking of cytokine receptors has been suggested to be critical for signal transduction, and more extensively cross-linked receptors may produce stronger activation of signaling [28,29]. Therefore, we propose that both the 2:2 and 3:3 stoichiometric complexes are functionally competent signaling assemblies but may exert different signaling intensities.

The structures presented herein indicate a novel cytokine receptor assembly mediated by ligand engagement. Other gp130 cytokine receptor family homologs receive signals from four-helix bundle cytokines with CRH domains [22]. The IgD, FnIII domain, and a second CRH in the LepR contribute to diverse assembly mechanisms. Additionally, the shared signaling receptor regulates pleiotropic functions; for instance, two IL-12 family cytokines (IL-12 and IL-23) share IL-12Rβ1 and play distinct roles in immunity [25]. LepR belongs to the tall cytokine receptor family because of the FnIII domains that link their CRH to the membrane as the long “legs”, whereas leptin structurally belongs to the IL-6 cytokine family [19]. However, the LepR signaling assembly does not adopt a conventional heterotrimeric complex architecture that shares receptor subunits with gp130 in the IL-6 family. Meanwhile, leptin neither engages two identical chains nor forms symmetric homodimeric receptor complex. Our study provides novel insights into the mechanisms underlying LepR signaling. First, the leptin–LepR assemblies adopt a unique architecture that forms a “hand-in-hand” complex derived from the structural rearrangement of the binding regions of leptin, which is not observed in the structures of other cytokine homologs. Second, the 2:2 or 3:3 stoichiometries promoted the adjacency of the two membrane-proximal FnIII domains (Figure S5). The distinct hydrophobic interface between D3 and D4 of LepR may be a crucial factor in the twisted orientation of LepR to form asymmetric assemblies that are distinct from those of other gp130 cytokine receptor homologs. Our study indicates the formation of a “non-canonical” asymmetric signaling assembly, despite the high structural similarity between IL-6 and leptin.

In summary, we determined the mechanism of soluble human leptin-mediated LepR signaling. Both 2:2 and 3:3 asymmetric stoichiometric complexes were observed with similar frequencies of occurrence, determined by 2D classification using cryo-EM analysis. All LepR assemblies were found to be in an open conformation, and the 3:3 stoichiometric complexes were observed to be open with gaps of different sizes. The LepR assembly is substantially distinct from that of other gp130 cytokine family members. These findings will broaden our understanding of leptin-mediated LepR signaling at a molecular level and promote the design of drugs for leptin–LepR signaling–related diseases.

Materials and methods

Protein Expression and Purification

The human leptin coding sequence (GenBank: AAH69452.1) was codon-optimized, synthesized (GenScript, Nanjing, China), and cloned into the pEZTsp vector (pEZTsp-hleptin) without an affinity tag. The extracellular domain of LepR (GenBank: AAB09673.1, residues 21–839) fused with a C-terminal His tag was codon-optimized and cloned into the pEZTsp vector (pEZTsp-hLepR).

HEK293F suspension-cultured cells (Gibco, Cat#11625-019, Carlsbad, CA, USA) were cultured at 37 °C in SMM 293-TII Expression Medium (Sino Biological, Cat# M293TII, Beijing, China). pEZTsp-hLepR and pEZTsp-hleptin were co-expressed in HEK293F cells. The cells were transfected with a mixture of 0.25 mg pEZTsp-hLepR plasmid, 0.25 mg pEZTsp-hleptin plasmid, and 1.5 mg polyethyleneimine at a density of 2 × 106 cells/mL in 500 mL. A supplement (Sino Biological, Cat# M293-SUPI, Beijing, China) was added to the culture system at 24 h and 72 h after transfection.

Cell culture supernatants were collected after five days of infection. hLepR and hleptin complex proteins were purified using His-Trap HP columns (GE Healthcare, Uppsala, Sweden) and a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare, Uppsala, Sweden). Purified proteins were stored in a buffer containing 20 mM Tris–HCl and 150 mM NaCl (pH 8.0). The proteins were concentrated to ∼0.5 mg/mL for cryo-EM assay.

Cryo-EM Sample Preparation

The purified hleptin–hLepR complex proteins were loaded onto a Cu grid, which was blotted for 2.5 s at a humidity of 100% and 4 °C and then plunged into liquid ethane using Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The prepared cryogenic specimens were imaged using a Titan Krios transmission electron microscope equipped with a K3 direct electron detector and a GIF Quantum energy filter, using the EPU acquisition software. Videos were collected in counted super-resolution mode at a pixel size of 0.425 Å, with 32 fractions per movie and a total dose of 50 electrons per Å2, with defocus targets cycled from −1.0 to −2.0 μm.

Image Processing

For the dataset, 63,340 raw movies were motion-corrected using MotionCor2 (v1.2.4). The micrograph contrast transfer function (CTF) correction parameters were estimated using patch CTF estimation implemented in cryoSPARC (V.4.0.3) [30]. Using another previously collected dataset, we picked out particles from 12,528 micrographs, which were subjected to 2D classification. After two rounds of 2D classification, we selected particles with different orientations for Topaz training and generated the Topaz model [31]. The Topaz procedure was then applied to select particles from the entire dataset, and 2,296,523 particles were extracted and subjected to 2D classification. After three rounds of 2D classification, the best class averages containing 885,964 clean particles were selected to perform ab initio reconstruction and heterogeneous refinement, which separated 342,150 good particles. Particles from this group were used as the input for a second round of ab initio reconstruction, which produced three classes corresponding to the 3:3 stoichiometry with a small gap, the 3:3 stoichiometry with a large gap, and the 2:2 open stoichiometry of the leptin–LepRECD complexes. Non-uniform refinement and CTF refinement were carried out on three sets of particles with C1 symmetry imposed, yielding reconstructions with global resolutions of 3.88, 3.77, and 3.58 Å, respectively. The image-processing workflow is illustrated in Figure S2. The ‘gold standard’ Fourier shell correlation (FSC) criterion (FSC = 0.143) was used for calculating all resolution estimates. All maps were sharpened using DeepEMhancer [32].

Model Building and Refinemen

The structure of leptin (PDB: 1AX8) was rigidly docked into density maps using ChimeraX [33]. The model of LepRECD was initially predicted using AlphaFold2 [34] and then rigidly docked into the density map using Chimera with further manual adjustment. The initial coordinates were refined in real space using PHENIX with Ramachandran and secondary structural restraints [35]. The model was further corrected to improve local fit using COOT and was refined for several rounds, aided by a stereochemical quality assessment using MolProbity [36,37]. Representative densities and atomic models are shown in Figure S3. Structural figures were prepared using PyMOL (www.pymol.org) and ChimeraX [38].
