Improved Sampling Schedules for Discrete Diffusion Models
Alberto Foresti, Mustapha Bounoua, Giulio Franzese, Luca Ambrogioni, Pietro Michiardi

Abstract
Discrete diffusion models have emerged as a powerful paradigm for generative modeling on sequence data; however, the information-theoretic principles governing their reverse processes remain significantly less understood than those of their continuous counterparts. In this work, we bridge this gap by analyzing the reverse process dynamics through the lens of thermodynamic entropy production. We propose the entropy production rate as a rigorous proxy for quantifying information generation, deriving as a byproduct a bound on the Wasserstein distance between intermediate states and the data distribution. Leveraging these insights, we introduce two novel sampling schedules that are uniformly spaced with respect to their corresponding physics-inspired metrics: the Entropic Discrete Schedule (EDS), which is defined by maintaining a constant rate of information gain, and the Wasserstein Discrete Schedule (WDS), which is defined by taking equal steps in terms of the Wasserstein distance. We empirically demonstrate that our proposed schedules significantly outperform state-of-the-art strategies across diverse application domains, including synthetic data, music notation, vision and language modeling, consistently achieving superior performance at a lower computational budget.

Preliminaries

Consider a
Continuous Time Markov Chain (
ctmc
)

\overset{\rightarrow}{X}_{t}
, with
t\in[0,T]
, defined over a finite state space
\chi=\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
and specified by the infinitesimal generators
\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{t}:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, where the diagonal entries satisfy
\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{t}(a,a)=-\sum_{a\neq b}\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{t}(a,b)
, with
\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{t}(a,b)\geq 0,a\neq b
. As established by
1
, the time evolution of the probability distribution
\text{Pr}(\overset{\rightarrow}{X}_{t}=x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{t}(x)
satisfies the following
Ordinary Differential Equation (
ode
)
:

\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{t}=\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{s}\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{s}ds,

where the initial conditions of the process
\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{0}
determine the distribution
\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{t}
at any time
t
. A key property of
ctmc
s
is that their time-reversed counterpart
(
29
)
also follows a
ctmc
with reverse generators
\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}
. Defining the time-reversed process as
\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{T-t}
, the reverse process evolves according to
(
29
;
46
)
:
\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}=\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{T}+\int_{0}^{t}\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{s}\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{s}ds.

The reverse-time generator
\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}
is related to the forward generator

\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{t}
through the standard CTMC time-reversal formula. For
a\neq b
,
\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(b,a)=\frac{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(b)}{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(a)}\,\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{t}(a,b)
, whereas
\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(a,a)=-\sum_{b\neq a}\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(b,a)
.

Under appropriate technical conditions on the forward rate matrix
\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{t}

(
29
)
, the terminal distribution
\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{T}
converges to a known reference distribution
\pi
, independent of the initial data
\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{0}
. This property enables sampling from
\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{0}
by simulating a reverse-time
ctmc
via time-dependent generators
(
46
;
23
)
. In practice, the intractable density ratios
\tfrac{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{t}(b)}{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{t}(a)}
governing reverse transitions are approximated by a score network
s^{p}_{\theta}(a,t)_{b}
, optimized via the
Diffusion Weighted Denoising Score Entropy (
dwdse
)
loss
(
29
)
. This network encodes the structural information necessary for reconstruction. While sampling typically relies on discretization (e.g., Euler solvers), optimizing these dynamics requires a rigorous understanding of how information is provided by the score network. In the following section, we quantify the information stored in the network parameters
\theta
by applying entropic principles from stochastic thermodynamics. Specifically, we demonstrate how to measure this information storage and track its evolution as the reverse process converges to the final state.

3
Entropy Production in Discrete Diffusion

3.1
Entropy Production

The reverse generative trajectory can be analyzed through the lens of stochastic thermodynamics. In this framework, the irreversibility of the diffusion process is quantified by the
Entropy Production rate

\mathcal{H}^{\text{tot}}
. Following the standard formulation
(
42
;
13
;
32
)
, this is defined as the integral of the log ratio divergence between the backward path and the time-reversal of the forward path (See
Section

A.2
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\text{tot}}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{x,y}\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\ln\left(\frac{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)}{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{T-t}(y)\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{T-t}(x,y)}\right)dt.

(1)

In the context of continuous diffusion models, this quantity is termed
Neural Entropy

(
32
)
, representing the total information cost required to invert the corruption process. For reversible discrete processes, such as uniform diffusion, where the forward dynamics satisfy detailed balance, the total entropy production is finite. However, for non-reversible processes, such as absorbing diffusion, the reversal is singular: the transition rate from the absorbing state ‘
[MASK]
’ back to any data token is identically zero. This singularity causes the logarithmic probability ratio to diverge (
\mathcal{H}^{\text{tot}}\to\infty
), rendering the standard definition of total entropy ill-defined.

3.2
Decomposition of the total entropy production

To derive a finite quantity that works across different discrete processes, we adopt the adiabatic–non-adiabatic decomposition
of entropy production for non-equilibrium Markov processes

(
13
;
18
)
.
Within this framework, the total entropy production rate of a
ctmc
admits an exact decomposition into two contributions:
the
Adiabatic Entropy Production
and the

Non-Adiabatic Entropy Production
, which
follows from an explicit expansion of the logarithmic ratio
of forward and backward path probabilities.

For a
ctmc
with time-dependent generator
\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{t}
, the corresponding
entropy production rates read

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\text{ad}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\ln\frac{\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\pi_{t}(x)}{\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(y,x)\pi_{t}(y)},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\ln\frac{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\pi_{t}(y)}{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(y)\pi_{t}(x)}.

(3)

Here,
\pi_{t}
denotes the
instantaneous stationary distribution
of the
generator
\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{t}
, defined by the invariance condition

\pi_{t}\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{t}=0

(
13
)
.
By construction,
\mathcal{H}^{\text{ad}}(t)
quantifies the entropy production required to maintain the stationary non-equilibrium currents of the dynamics, while
\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}(t)
captures the relaxation of the system distribution
\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{t}

toward the reference distribution
\pi_{t}
. Integrating over time yields the total entropy production:

\mathcal{H}^{\text{tot}}=\int_{0}^{T}\!\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}(t)\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\!\mathcal{H}^{\text{ad}}(t)\,dt\;\equiv\;\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}+\mathcal{H}^{\text{ad}},

(4)

where we leverage the identity

\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(y)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(y,x)=\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{T-t}(x)\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{T-t}(x,y)
(see
Section

A.3
for more details).

In uniform diffusion, the stationary distribution
\pi_{t}\equiv\pi
is uniform, and detailed balance holds. Then, the adiabatic contribution vanishes
(
\mathcal{H}^{\text{ad}}=0
), and the total entropy production is entirely captured by the non-adiabatic term. thus in the case of uniform diffusion we have
\mathcal{H}^{\text{tot}}=\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}
.

In contrast, for masked (absorbing) diffusion, the stationary distribution concentrates on the absorbing state, and detailed balance is violated. In this setting, the adiabatic entropy production diverges due to irreversible probability currents into the absorbing state, reflecting the infinite housekeeping cost of maintaining the absorbing prior. Crucially, the non-adiabatic entropy production
\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}
remains finite, provided the marginal distributions
\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}
retain full support over non-absorbing states. In the context of diffusion-based generative models, we identify the integrated non-adiabatic entropy production
\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}
as the information theoretic cost associated with reshaping the probability mass of the model over time, independently of irreversible constraints imposed by the prior.

3.3
Wasserstein Speed Limit

The rate at which a generative model can transform an initial noise distribution

\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{T}
into the data distribution
\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{0}
is fundamentally constrained by
thermodynamic and geometric considerations. Let

\mathcal{W}(\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{0},\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{T})
denote the
L_{1}
-Wasserstein distance between
the two distributions. Following the work by
47
, this geometric transport distance is bounded by the total entropy production weighted by the mobility of the system (see
Section

A.4
):

\mathcal{W}(\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{0},\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{T})\leq\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{2\,\mathcal{M}(t)\,\mathcal{H}^{\text{tot}}(t)}\;dt,

(5)

where the
dynamical state mobility

(
47
)

\mathcal{M}(t)
captures the strength of the state transitions under the CTMC generator
\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{t}
. Conveniently,
47
show that :

\mathcal{M}(t)\leq\frac{\mathcal{A}(t)}{2}=\sum_{x,y}\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}(x,y)\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(y)

(6)

While the above bound holds for reversible dynamics with finite total entropy production, such as uniform diffusion, it diverges for masked absorbing processes due to irreversible housekeeping currents. However, probability transport remains finite and is driven primarily by the non-adiabatic entropy production
(
49
)
. In our experiments, we found that an extremely practical surrogate for this bound, using only non-adiabatic entropy production, provides the best empirical performance, as discussed in
Section

A.4
.

Intuitively, these inequalities impose a
speed limit
on generative dynamics:
for a fixed geometric displacement
\mathcal{W}(\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{0},\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{T})
, the minimal entropy production
\mathcal{H}^{\text{tot}}
determines the fastest possible trajectory through probability space. Equivalently, minimizing
\mathcal{H}^{\text{tot}}
corresponds to following the
optimal transport geodesic
between noise and data, achieving the most efficient generative transformation under the constraints of the
ctmc
dynamics.

3.4
Estimation of Neural Entropy

We consider the reverse process to be simulated using a neural network trained to approximate the probability ratio
s^{p}_{\theta}(x,y)
(see
Section

2
). We aim to estimate the non-adiabatic neural entropy
\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}_{\theta}
as a proxy for the information produced by the network during the generative process.

Information generation is quantified by the estimated non-adiabatic entropy production rate. Assuming the stationary distribution terms vanish (as in uniform or absorbing diffusion), the non-adiabatic entropy for the discrete diffusion parameterization in
(
29
)
can be computed as:

\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}_{\theta}(t)=-\sum_{x,y}\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\ln s^{p}_{\theta}(x,y).

(7)

The total non-adiabatic entropy generated over the full trajectory is obtained by integrating this rate over time. Replacing the summation with an expectation over the generated samples yields the
Total Non-Adiabatic Neural Entropy
:

\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}_{\theta}=-\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}}\left[\sum_{y}\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\ln s^{p}_{\theta}(x,y)\right]dt.

(8)

Once this non adiabatic entropy production is computed, the speed limit on the generative process, expressed as a bound on the Wasserstein distance, can be derived by
Equation

5
which we refer to as
\mathcal{W}_{\theta}(\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{0},\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t})
. These quantities provide a computable measure of the progress of the reverse process in reshaping probability mass, grounded in the thermodynamic framework of
(
18
;
13
)
. Detailed derivations are provided in
Section

A.5
.

4
Improved Sampling Schedules for Discrete Diffusion models

The efficacy of discrete diffusion sampling depends critically on the
time schedule
, which dictates the discretization grid for the reverse process. While efficient numerical integrators such as
\tau
-leaping
(
9
;
29
)
allow for practical inference by updating multiple tokens in parallel, they rely on the assumption that the transition rates remain approximately constant within each time step. The validity of this assumption is entirely controlled by the chosen schedule, which governs the rate of noise removal and, consequently, the magnitude of the distributional shift at each step.

Standard approaches to scheduling typically impose fixed, heuristic functional forms on the noise dynamics. Common baselines include Linear schedules, which are standard in masked modeling. However, these agnostic schedules advance time based on kinematic constraints rather than the information complexity of the generation. This misalignment often leads to inefficient allocation of the computational budget: the sampler may waste steps resolving trivial noise while moving too aggressively through critical phase transitions where semantic structure is established. Such aggressive discretization in high-entropy regions may yield approximation errors, leading to mode collapse
(
31
)
. We propose that the improved time schedule should be derived not from heuristic functions, but from the thermodynamic properties of the generative process itself.

As observed in
Figures

1
and
8
, the information content generated during the reverse process quantified by the non adiabatic entropy production rate
\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}_{\theta}(t)
evolves in a non-linear manner, subject to the prior diffusion framework and the data distribution. This non-linearity is mirrored in the transport dynamics: as the generation progresses toward the target state, it proceeds with a non-constant velocity, as evidenced by the Wasserstein distance in
Figure

1
.
Consequently, we propose a physically motivated scheduling approach, where the time discretization is governed by the intrinsic progress of the generative process. We introduce two sampling strategies: the
Entropic Discrete Schedule (
EDS
)
, which adapts to the information load, and the
Wasserstein Discrete Schedule (
WDS
)
, which adapts to the transport velocity in the Wasserstein metric.

4.1
Cumulative Progress

To quantify the progression of the diffusion process, we define the
Cumulative Progress
function
\mathcal{C}(t)
. This function measures the accumulation of a physical quantity (information or distance) from the initial state (
t=0
) up to the current noise level
t
:

\mathcal{C}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\rho(\tau)\operatorname{d}\!{\tau},

(9)

where
\rho(\tau)
represents the instantaneous rate of change of the chosen metric. Assuming
\rho(\tau)\geq 0
,
\mathcal{C}(t)
is non-decreasing and bounded by
\mathcal{C}(T)
, it allows to map the diffusion time
t\in[0,T]
to the progress domain
\mathcal{C}\in[0,\mathcal{C}_{\text{total}}]
, with
\mathcal{C}_{\text{total}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{C}(T)
.

The objective of our improved scheduling is to find a time discretization
\{t_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
such that the progress between consecutive steps is uniform. We define the Warping Function
\Phi:[0,T]\to[0,1]
as the normalized cumulative progress:

\Phi(t)=\mathcal{C}(t)/\mathcal{C}(T).

(10)

Physically,
\Phi(t)
represents the ratio of the total budget (information generated or distance traversed) that has been consumed by time
t
. The improved schedule is obtained by inverting this warping function. Assuming
\Phi
is strictly increasing, for
K
generation steps we define the discrete time points
t_{k}
as uniform samples in the progress domain:

t_{k}=\Phi^{-1}\left(k/K\right),\quad\text{for }k=0,\dots,K.

(11)

Next, we cast this framework for our proposed schedules.

4.2
Entropic Discrete Schedule (EDS)

The
EDS
is derived from thermodynamic principles. We identify the instantaneous rate of non-adiabatic entropy as the information load of the generative process:

\rho_{\text{EDS}}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}_{\theta}(t).

(12)

Here,
\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}_{\theta}(t)
can be obtained from
Equation

7
.
\rho_{\text{EDS}}(t)
represents the density of useful information retrieved by the model at time
t
. This schedule slows down time during phase transitions where information gain is high (
Figure

1
) and speeds up during low-information regimes, ensuring a constant information rate.

4.3
Wasserstein Discrete Schedule (WDS)

The
WDS
can be viewed from optimal transport geometry. We identify the distance to the data manifold as the progress metric. We define the rate
\rho_{\text{WDS}}(t)
as the instantaneous Wasserstein distance rate:

\rho_{\text{WDS}}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\operatorname{d}\!{t}}\mathcal{W}(\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{0},\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}),

(13)

where
\mathcal{W}(\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{0},\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t})
denotes the time-dependent Wasserstein bound up to time
t
, generalizing
Equation

5
.
Consequently, the cumulative function
\mathcal{C}_{\text{WDS}}(t)
is given by the distance itself:

\mathcal{C}_{\text{WDS}}(t)=\mathcal{W}(\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{0},\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}).

(14)

This schedule ensures that the generative process takes steps of equal geometric length in probability space, corresponding to a
constant transport speed
.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we presented an improved sampling framework that achieves a superior compute-budget performance trade-off. This was achieved by bridging non-equilibrium thermodynamics and optimal transport geometry within the context of discrete generative modeling. We demonstrated that by applying thermodynamic principles to compute the non-adiabatic entropy production, we can characterize how the reverse process evolves in terms of information rate. Furthermore, we derived a practical bound on the Wasserstein velocity to measure the geometric evolution of intermediate steps toward the final generation.

Motivated by the observation that information flow in the generative reverse process is highly non-linear and dependent on both the data and the diffusion kernel, we proposed two intrinsic scheduling strategies:
EDS
, based on non-adiabatic entropy production, and
WDS
, derived from a geometric transport bound. Our analysis established that standard uniform schedules are often inefficient, disproportionately allocating computational steps to regimes with minimal information gain. To address this, we derived improved schedules by uniformly sampling timesteps within the intrinsic space of non-adiabatic entropy or Wasserstein distance, rather than in linear time.

Empirically, our method requires no additional training and generalizes across diverse modalities. Both
EDS
and
WDS
demonstrated significant efficiency gains, achieving high-fidelity generation at a fraction of the function evaluations required by uniform baselines. Notably,
WDS
proved particularly robust, consistently achieving the lowest perplexity and surpassing existing error-based schedules.

Ultimately, our findings confirm that the “physical time” of a diffusion process rarely aligns with its “semantic progress.” By realigning the sampling trajectory to the model’s intrinsic dynamics, we offer a plug-and-play solution that significantly enhances the inference efficiency of discrete diffusion models.

A.2
Total entropy production

We derive the total entropy production
\mathcal{H}^{\text{tot}}
for the reverse process, mapping each step to the corresponding thermodynamic definition in
(
49
)
.

First, we define the thermodynamic force for a transition from state
x
to
y
. In our notation, the forward flux is
\mathcal{K}_{(x,y)}=\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)
. The thermodynamic force is the log-ratio of the forward and reverse fluxes:

F_{(x,y)}(t)=\ln\frac{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)}{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(y)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(y,x)}.

(15)

This formulation is equivalent to
Eq. (8)
in the original paper, where
F_{e}(x)=\ln(\mathcal{K}_{e}(x)/\mathcal{K}_{-e}(x))

(
49
)
.

The entropy production rate
\mathcal{H}^{\text{tot}}(t)
is defined as the sum of the product of currents and forces. In the continuous-time limit, this is equivalent to the sum of fluxes multiplied by the force:

\mathcal{H}^{\text{tot}}(t)=\sum_{x,y\in\chi}\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\ln\frac{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)}{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(y)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(y,x)}.

(16)

This corresponds to
Eq. (9)
in
(
49
)
.

We apply the standard CTMC time-reversal identity. The reverse generator
\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}
relates to the forward generator
\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}
via:

\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(y)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(y,x)=\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{T-t}(x)\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{T-t}(x,y).

(17)

Substituting this into the denominator of the log term in the EPR equation allows us to express the entropy in terms of the reconstruction trajectory.

The total entropy is obtained by integrating the entropy production rate
\mathcal{H}^{\text{tot}}(t)
over the time interval
[0,T]
. By applying the substitution from Step 3, we arrive at the final form:

\mathcal{H}^{\text{tot}}=\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{x,y}\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\ln\left(\frac{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)}{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{T-t}(y)\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{T-t}(x,y)}\right)dt.

(18)

A.3
Decomposition of the total entropy production

The decomposition follows from an explicit expansion of the logarithmic ratio
of forward and backward path probabilities
(
13
;
18
)
.

For the reverse
ctmc
with time-dependent generator
\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}
and instantaneous stationary distribution
\pi_{t}
(such that
\pi_{t}\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}=0
), the corresponding entropy production rates read:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\text{ad}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\ln\frac{\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\pi_{t}(x)}{\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(y,x)\pi_{t}(y)},

(19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\ln\frac{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\pi_{t}(y)}{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(y)\pi_{t}(x)}.

(20)

Summing these two components yields the instantaneous total entropy production rate
\mathcal{H}^{\text{tot}}
. The terms involving
\pi_{t}
cancel out:

\mathcal{H}^{\text{ad}}(t)+\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}(t)=\sum_{x,y}\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\ln\frac{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)}{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(y)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(y,x)}.

(21)

By applying the time-reversal where
\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(y)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(y,x)=\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{T-t}(x)\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{T-t}(x,y)
, we recover the exact form of the total entropy derived previously in
Section

3.1

\mathcal{H}^{\text{ad}}(t)+\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}(t)=\sum_{x,y}\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\ln\frac{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)}{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{T-t}(y)\overset{\rightarrow}{Q}_{T-t}(y,x)}.

(22)

A.4
Wasserstein bound

Here we provide additional details about the Wasserstein bound discussed in the main text. The dynamical state mobility is defined following
(
47
)
.

\mathcal{M}(t)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\;\sum_{x,y}\frac{\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(y)-\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}(y,x)\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)}{\log\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}(x,y)\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(y)-\log\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(y,x)\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)}.

(23)

Conveniently,
47
show that

\mathcal{M}(t)\leq\frac{\mathcal{A}(t)}{2}
,
where
\mathcal{A}(t)
is simply

\sum_{x,y}\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}(x,y)\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(y)
.

It follows that we can obtain another bound based on
\mathcal{A}(t)
:

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{0},\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{T})\leq\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\mathcal{A}(t)\,\mathcal{H}^{\text{tot}}(t)}\;dt.

(24)

While the bound in
Equation

24
is looser than the bound in
Equation

5
, the former turns out to be numerically more stable in practical applications.

For masked dynamics, total entropy production diverges due to irreversibility, rendering standard bounds uninformative. However, probability transport remains finite and is driven primarily by the
non-adiabatic
entropy production
(
49
)
. Specifically,
49
derive a thermodynamic uncertainty relation (Eq. H.10) bounding the Wasserstein distance:

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{0}),\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{T})\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{A}(t)\,\operatorname{d}\!{t}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}(t)\,\operatorname{d}\!{t}\right)}.

(25)

Motivated by this, we introduce the practical bound
\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\mathcal{A}(t)\,\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}_{\theta}(t)}\,\operatorname{d}\!{t}
. This metric refines
Equation

25
and serves as a stable proxy for
\mathcal{W}_{\theta}(p_{0},p_{T})
by utilizing only the finite non-adiabatic term. It naturally generalizes
Equation

24
; for reversible dynamics (e.g., uniform diffusion), the adiabatic term vanishes, and our metric recovers the standard total entropy production.

A.5
Derivation of the Non adiabatic Entropy Estimator

To estimate the non-adiabatic entropy production
\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}_{\theta}(t)
using a neural network, we express the probability ratio in terms of the learned score. The score network
s^{p}_{\theta}(x,y)
is typically trained to approximate the forward transition ratio:

s^{p}_{\theta}(x,y)\approx\frac{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{T-t}(y)}{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}_{T-t}(x)}=\frac{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(y)}{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)}.

(26)

Substituting the inverse relationship
\frac{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)}{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(y)}\approx s^{p}_{\theta}(x,y)^{-1}
into the expression for
\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}(t)
, we obtain the instantaneous estimator:

\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}_{\theta}(t)=\sum_{x,y}\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}(x)\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\left[-\ln s^{p}_{\theta}(x,y)+\ln\frac{\pi_{t}(y)}{\pi_{t}(x)}\right].

(27)

The total non-adiabatic entropy production,
\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}_{\theta}
, is obtained by integrating this rate over the generation interval
[0,T]
:

\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}_{\theta}=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}}\left[\sum_{y}\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\left(-\ln s^{p}_{\theta}(x,y)+\ln\frac{\pi_{t}(y)}{\pi_{t}(x)}\right)\right]dt.

(28)

Assuming the stationary distribution terms vanish (as in uniform or absorbing diffusion).

\mathcal{H}^{\text{na}}_{\theta}=-\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overset{\leftarrow}{p}_{t}}\left[\sum_{y}\overset{\leftarrow}{Q}_{t}(x,y)\ln s^{p}_{\theta}(x,y)\right]dt.

(29)
