Impact of Postoperative Infectious Complications on Long‐Term Survival After Curative Resection for Colorectal Cancer: A Multicenter Study in the Minimally Invasive Era
Authors: Takehito Yamamoto, Ryosuke Mizuno, Yoshiro Itatani, Tetsuya Shiota, Masahiro Yamada, Tadayoshi Yamaura, Mami Yoshitomi, Kazutaka Obama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimPrevious studies have reported that postoperative infectious complications (PICs) worsen long‐term outcomes after colorectal cancer surgery; however, most were conducted before widespread adoption of minimally invasive surgery. We evaluated the association between PICs and long‐term oncological outcomes after curative colorectal cancer resection.MethodsThis multicenter, retrospective cohort study enrolled patients who underwent curative resection for colorectal adenocarcinoma between 2017 and 2021 at 19 hospitals in Japan. PICs were defined as Clavien‐Dindo grade≥II infectious complications within 30 days after surgery. Multivariable Cox regression and propensity score matching (1:4) were used to evaluate associations with overall survival (OS) and relapse‐free survival (RFS).ResultsAmong 5377 patients, 90.6% underwent laparoscopic or robot‐assisted surgery, and PICs occurred in 500 (9.3%). In multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis, PICs were independently associated with worse OS (HR 1.32, 95% CI 1.01–1.72; p = 0.040) and RFS (HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.04–1.54; p = 0.020). After propensity score matching (500 vs. 1931 patients), PICs remained significantly associated with poorer OS and RFS. Patients with PICs had a higher rate of distant recurrence (5‐year rate, 19.1% vs. 15.2%; HR 1.33, 95% CI 1.02–1.72; p = 0.03), whereas locoregional recurrence did not differ significantly.ConclusionPICs are associated with impaired long‐term survival after curative colorectal cancer surgery, even in the modern minimally invasive era. Preventing postoperative infections is important to improve perioperative recovery and long‐term oncological outcomes.Keywords: colorectal cancer, minimally invasive surgery, postoperative complications, postoperative infection

Introduction

Colorectal cancer remains one of the most common malignancies worldwide and is a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality [1]. Surgical resection is the cornerstone of curative treatment for colorectal cancer (CRC). Despite advances in perioperative management, postoperative complications, particularly infections, continue to occur in a substantial proportion of patients undergoing colorectal surgery.

Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that postoperative infectious complications (PICs) adversely affect not only short‐term outcomes, but also long‐term oncological prognosis in patients with colorectal cancer [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Several retrospective studies and meta‐analyses have reported associations between PICs, especially anastomotic leakage and surgical site infection (SSI), and impaired overall survival (OS) and relapse‐free survival (RFS) [3, 11, 12]. These findings suggest that postoperative infections may extend beyond the immediate postoperative morbidity.

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the association between PICs and poor oncological outcomes. First, postoperative infections trigger systemic inflammatory responses, activate inflammatory cascades, and stimulate the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines. This inflammatory environment may promote the proliferation and spread of residual cancer cells [13, 14]. Second, infectious complications may prevent patients from starting adjuvant chemotherapy or delay its initiation, thereby reducing the potential benefits of postoperative systemic treatment.

Colorectal surgery is particularly prone to PICs compared with many other surgical fields, largely due to bacterial contamination of the intestinal lumen [15]. Therefore, infectious complications remain a critical issue in colorectal cancer management and require careful evaluation from both perioperative and oncological perspectives. However, the surgical practice has evolved substantially in recent years. The widespread adoption of minimally invasive approaches, including laparoscopic and robot‐assisted surgeries, has enabled colorectal resection through smaller incisions and reduced surgical trauma. These advances have contributed to reduced postoperative pain, faster recovery, and potentially reduced PIC rates. Consequently, the extent and nature of PICs' impact on long‐term prognosis may change in the modern era of minimally invasive surgery (MIS). Notably, many previous reports investigating the association between PICs and oncological outcomes were based on older cohorts, in which the proportion of patients with MIS was relatively low [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Therefore, contemporary evidence reflecting current surgical practices is required.

In this multicenter study, we evaluated the association between PICs and long‐term oncological outcomes after curative colorectal cancer surgery in the minimally invasive era.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patients

This multicenter, retrospective cohort study enrolled patients with colorectal cancer who underwent curative primary tumor resection at Kyoto University Hospital and 18 affiliated institutions in Japan and participated in the Kyoto Colorectal Surgery Study Group (KCRG) between 2017 and 2021. Patients with histologically confirmed colorectal adenocarcinoma were eligible for inclusion. Patients with distant metastases at the time of surgery were excluded.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kyoto University Graduate School and Faculty of Medicine (approval number R0286‐7) and the institutional review boards of all participating institutions. Infor The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and reported in compliance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study Outcomes and Data Collection

Cohort data were collected using the REDCap Electronic Data Capture System (Vanderbilt University). Baseline patient characteristics included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status (ASA‐PS), smoking history within one year, and the presence of diabetes mellitus with treatment, all of which were considered potential confounders associated with PICs. Operative and oncological variables included primary tumor location (colon or rectum), preoperative tumor markers, Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification, surgical approach, operative time, estimated blood loss, and histopathological findings.

PICs were defined as infectious complications, including superficial incisional surgical site infection (SSI), deep incisional SSI, organ/space SSI (including anastomotic leakage), urinary tract infection, pneumonia, and other remote site infections such as cholecystitis, catheter‐related blood stream infections, and enteritis (including Clostridioides difficile infections). PICs of Clavien‐Dindo grade II or higher were considered clinically relevant events [16].

The primary outcomes were OS, cancer‐specific survival (CSS) and RFS. OS was defined as the time from surgery to death from any cause. CSS was defined as the time from surgery to death attributable to colorectal cancer. RFS was defined as the time from surgery to cancer recurrence or death of any cause. As secondary outcomes, recurrence patterns were further classified as locoregional or distant. Locoregional recurrence includes anastomotic recurrence and peritoneal dissemination. Distant recurrence was defined as metastasis to other organs, such as the liver or lungs.

Statistical Analyses

We conducted a complete‐case analysis and excluded cases with missing data. Continuous variables are presented as median [interquartile range] or mean ± standard deviation (SD), as appropriate. Multivariate analyses of the association between PICs and survival outcomes were performed using Cox proportional hazards regression models.

In addition, to compare the prognoses of patients with and without PICs, propensity score matching was performed using the nearest‐neighbor method with a caliper width of 0.2 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score, at a ratio of 1:4. The propensity score model included clinically relevant baseline variables potentially associated with both PICs and long‐term prognosis, including age, sex, BMI, ASA‐PS, smoking history, diabetes mellitus, tumor location, surgical approach, pathological T stage, and pathological N stage. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an absolute SMD < 0.1 indicating adequate balance. Given the sufficient number of available control patients and satisfactory covariate balance achieved, 1:4 propensity score matching was chosen to optimize statistical efficiency while maintaining matching quality. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Differences between groups were evaluated using both the log‐rank test and the Wilcoxon (Breslow) test. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. Additional exploratory analyses included comparisons according to the severity of PICs based on the Clavien–Dindo classification and subgroup analyses according to surgical approach (minimally invasive surgery and open surgery).

All statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and JMP Pro (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

After propensity score matching (1:4), 500 patients with PICs were matched to 1931 patients without PICs. All baseline covariates were well balanced after matching, with SMDs < 0.1 (Table 3). Comparisons of OS, CSS and RFS between patients with and without PICs are shown in Figure 2. OS, CSS and RFS of patients with PICs were significantly worse than those without PICs (Figure 2A–C). Regarding recurrence patterns, distant recurrence rates were significantly higher in the PIC group compared to the non‐PIC group (5‐year cumulative recurrence rates: 19.1% vs. 15.2%, HR: 1.33, 95% CI: 1.02–1.72), whereas locoregional recurrence rates were not significantly different between two groups (5‐year cumulative recurrence rates: 9.3% vs. 5.7%, HR: 1.16, 95% CI: 0.71–1.90) (Figure 2D,E).

To further explore the potential influence of adjuvant chemotherapy, a subgroup analysis was performed in patients with pathological stage III colorectal cancer (Figure 4). Similar findings were observed for OS, CSS, and RFS. Patients without PICs who received adjuvant chemotherapy had the most favorable prognosis, whereas patients with PICs who did not receive adjuvant chemotherapy had the poorest prognosis. Importantly, among patients with PICs, receipt of adjuvant chemotherapy was associated with better OS, CSS and RFS than no adjuvant chemotherapy.

Subgroup analyses according to surgical approach are shown in Figures S1–, S3. Among patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery (MIS), PICs were associated with significantly worse survival outcomes and higher cumulative distant recurrence rate, consistent with the findings observed in the overall matched cohort (Figure S1). Furthermore, among patients who underwent laparoscopic surgery, the results were consistent with those of the overall cohort (Figure S2), whereas among patients who underwent robot‐assisted surgery, no significant differences were observed in long‐term outcomes (Figure S3).

When patients were stratified according to the severity of PICs using the Clavien–Dindo classification, both Grade II and Grade≥III PICs showed similarly unfavorable prognosis than no or minor PICs (Figure S4).

Discussion

In this large multicenter cohort study, we demonstrated that PICs were independently associated with worse OS and RFS after curative resection for colorectal cancer. Furthermore, we found that PICs were more strongly associated with distant recurrence. In addition, these findings remained consistent in subgroup analyses restricted to patients undergoing MIS and laparoscopic surgery.

Our additional analyses provided further insight into the relationship between individual postoperative infectious complications and recurrence patterns. Deep incisional SSI, organ/space SSI, and anastomotic leakage tended to show associations with locoregional recurrence than with distant recurrence, which is biologically plausible because local infectious complications may facilitate residual tumor cell implantation and local tumor progression. By contrast, although overall PICs were significantly associated with distant recurrence, no single infectious complication independently explained this association. This finding suggests that the increased risk of distant metastasis may not be attributable to a specific type of infection but rather to the cumulative systemic inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects associated with clinically relevant postoperative infections as a whole.

One plausible mechanism may involve the biological consequences of infection‐induced systemic inflammation [13, 14]. Postoperative infections trigger inflammatory cascades and the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, which may stimulate residual tumor cells and promote tumor growth and dissemination. In addition, dysregulation of cell‐mediated and humoral immunity in the setting of severe infection may impair antitumor immune surveillance. Indeed, numerous studies have demonstrated that elevated levels of preoperative inflammatory markers in colorectal cancer are associated with poorer oncological outcomes [17, 18].

Another explanation for the association between PICs and poor survival is a delay in adjuvant chemotherapy. In our study, the initiation of adjuvant chemotherapy was significantly delayed in patients with PICs, with a median delay of 10 days (median: 50 vs. 40 days, p < 0.001). Delayed initiation of adjuvant therapy has been reported to reduce oncological benefits in patients with colorectal cancer [19]. Furthermore, as shown in Figure 4, among patients who developed PICs, those who received adjuvant chemotherapy demonstrated better long‐term outcomes than those who did not receive adjuvant chemotherapy. These findings suggest that systemic chemotherapy is beneficial even after the occurrence of PICs and highlight the particular importance of appropriate adjuvant chemotherapy in this high‐risk population.

Although PICs were not independently associated with CSS in the overall cohort, they were significantly associated with distant recurrence after propensity score matching. This discrepancy may be explained by the relatively limited number of cancer‐specific deaths during follow‐up, reducing the statistical power to detect differences in CSS. Furthermore, advances in salvage treatments such as intensive chemotherapy and surgery for recurrent lesions may have mitigated the impact of increased recurrence on cancer‐specific mortality. The significant difference observed by the Wilcoxon test, but not by the log‐rank test in the matched cohort, also suggests that PICs may predominantly influence early cancer‐related events rather than long‐term cancer‐specific mortality.

When we examined the associations between individual PICs and recurrence patterns, pneumonia emerged as the infectious complication most strongly associated with worse OS and RFS. This finding may reflect not only the potential role of the systemic inflammatory response in promoting tumor metastasis and progression, but also the clinical consequences of severe systemic infection. Patients who develop postoperative pneumonia may experience deterioration in their general condition, resulting in reduced activities of daily living and impaired physical function, which may subsequently contribute to poorer long‐term survival. Therefore, perioperative measures to prevent pulmonary complications, such as respiratory rehabilitation and early mobilization, may be particularly important for improving long‐term outcomes.

Several studies have consistently demonstrated an association between PICs and impaired long‐term survival in colorectal cancer. In a large cohort of 12 075 patients, Artinyan et al. showed that infectious complications were independently associated with decreased survival [20]. Similarly, Tamagawa et al. and Matsuda et al. confirmed the adverse prognostic impact of infectious complications in multicenter Japanese cohort studies [4, 6]. However, these investigations were conducted in surgical contexts that differ substantially from current practice. In the study by Artinyan et al., laparoscopic surgery accounted for less than 1% of cases. In the cohort reported by Tamagawa et al., minimally invasive surgery comprised less than 30% of procedures [4]. Likewise, in the study by Matsuda et al., more than 80% of patients underwent open surgery, and the incidence of infectious complications (≥ Grade II) was 19.3%, considerably higher than in our cohort [6]. Thus, the existing evidence largely reflects outcomes from an era in which open surgery was predominant. In contrast, the present study represents a contemporary surgical landscape in which more than 90% of the patients underwent minimally invasive procedures, and the overall incidence of PICs (Clavien‐Dindo≥grade II) was 9.3%, which is slightly lower than that reported in earlier studies [4, 6]. Our findings indicate that the prognostic significance of postoperative infection is not confined to the open surgery era but persists in the minimally invasive era. Although Sugamata et al. previously analyzed a laparoscopic‐only cohort and reported similar findings, their study included 199 patients, limiting its statistical power and generalizability [10]. Therefore, robust multicenter evidence derived from minimally invasive cohorts is limited. Our study fills this gap by providing large‐scale data from the modern era, showing that postoperative infections negatively affect long‐term outcomes.

From a clinical perspective, our findings underscore the importance of rigorous perioperative infection prevention strategies in colorectal cancer surgery. Several evidence‐based measures, including enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols, appropriate perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis, and the combined use of mechanical bowel preparation and oral antibiotics, have been shown to reduce the incidence of SSIs [21, 22, 23]. Although these strategies have contributed to improved short‐term outcomes, it remains unclear whether reducing PICs through preventive interventions improves long‐term oncological outcomes. Given the persistent association between PICs and impaired survival, further prospective studies are warranted to determine whether the strict implementation of infection prevention bundles can ultimately influence cancer prognosis.

This study had several limitations. First, perioperative infection diagnostic criteria for infectious complications may vary among participating institutions. Only clinically relevant infectious complications (Clavien–Dindo grade II or higher) requiring therapeutic intervention were included in the present study and we believe that this definition reduced the influence of inter‐institutional differences in diagnostic practices; however, some degree of heterogeneity cannot be completely excluded. Second, rare types of postoperative infectious complications may not have been fully captured in the database because some events could not be accurately recorded in the retrospective registry. Third, although we presented the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy and its association with the PICs, this multicenter retrospective database did not include detailed information regarding treatment completion or dose intensity. Therefore, the influence of these treatment‐related factors on long‐term oncologic outcomes could not be fully evaluated. Finally, robot‐assisted surgery was introduced more recently at the participating institutions, the robotic cohort had a substantially shorter follow‐up period and relatively small sample size with limited statistical power. Therefore, the absence of a significant association does not necessarily indicate the absence of a true effect. Longer follow‐up and larger robotic cohorts will be required to clarify the long‐term oncologic impact of PICs.

Despite these limitations, the large sample size, multicenter design, and focus on a contemporary, minimally invasive cohort strengthen the validity and clinical relevance of our findings.
