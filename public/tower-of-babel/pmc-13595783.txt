Longitudinal comparison of peri-implant clinical and radiographic parameters between e-cigarette users and conventional cigarette smokers
Authors: Conghui Li, Yi Tan, Lu Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConventional cigarette smoking is associated with peri-implant disease, but longitudinal evidence for electronic cigarette (e-cigarette) exposure is limited. We compared 12-month peri-implant clinical and radiographic trajectories between e-cigarette users and conventional cigarette smokers and assessed unequal current exposure duration.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 328 patients with 452 implants (e-cigarette [EC] group, 152 patients/206 implants; conventional cigarette [CS] group, 176/246). Modified plaque index (mPLI), probing depth (PD), modified sulcus bleeding index (mSBI), bleeding on probing (BOP), mucosal recession (MR), keratinized mucosa width (KMW), and marginal bone level (MBL) were recorded at loading, 6 months, and 12 months while implants remained in situ. Patient-clustered generalized estimating equations (GEE) assessed group, time, and group-by-time effects. PD and change in MBL (ΔMBL) were prioritized. A post hoc sensitivity model additionally adjusted for current exposure duration; product-specific intensity variables were examined within groups.ResultsContinuous outcomes were available for 452, 448, and 440 implants at loading, 6 months, and 12 months, respectively; all 12 removals were entered into failure analyses. Current exposure duration was shorter among e-cigarette users (median, 3.0 vs. 12.0 years). At 12 months, mean PD was 3.18 versus 3.72 mm, and mean ΔMBL was 0.68 versus 1.01 mm. After duration adjustment, conventional cigarette exposure remained associated with greater PD (difference, 0.42 mm; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.25–0.59; interaction P = 0.003) and ΔMBL (0.26 mm; 95% CI, 0.15–0.37; interaction P = 0.015). The crude mucositis association attenuated after parsimonious adjustment (odds ratio [OR], 1.72; 95% CI, 1.02–2.90; P = 0.043; adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.64; 95% CI, 0.98–2.75; P = 0.060). Peri-implantitis and post-loading failure comparisons were not statistically significant.ConclusionsConventional cigarette exposure was associated with larger 12-month peri-implant changes than e-cigarette exposure. Unequal exposure duration, unmeasured previous smoking, self-reported exposure, device heterogeneity, informative implant removal, and absence of a never-smoker group limit causal and absolute-risk interpretation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09469-y.

Introduction

Peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis are major biological complications that affect the long-term stability of implant-supported prostheses. Existing evidence identifies smoking and a history of periodontitis as important risk indicators, suggesting that tobacco exposure should be incorporated into risk stratification and maintenance planning before and after implant therapy [1].

Use of electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) continues to expand, and the age distribution of users often differs from that of conventional cigarette smokers [2]. E-cigarette aerosol may contain nicotine, carbonyl compounds, metal particles, and reactive oxygen species, and the level of oral exposure is jointly influenced by device characteristics, liquid formulation, and use behavior [3]. Current periodontal evidence suggests that e-cigarette use is associated with changes in plaque, probing depth, inflammatory indicators, and treatment response, but exposure definitions and comparison groups vary substantially [4].

Clinical evidence on peri-implant tissues remains limited. Existing pooled data suggest that marginal bone level, plaque, and probing depth may be less favorable in e-cigarette users than in never-smokers [5]; comparisons across different nicotine-containing products show marked heterogeneity [6]. Recent evidence continues to indicate an association between e-cigarettes and poorer peri-implant parameters, but longitudinal post-loading trajectory data are insufficient [7]. Periodontal data for natural teeth generally suggest a risk gradient from never-smokers to e-cigarette users to conventional cigarette smokers, while residual confounding remains [8]. Differences in device type, nicotine salt formulation, concentration, power output, and inhalation pattern further limit dose conversion across products [9].

Conventional cigarette smoking has relatively robust evidence for its association with implant failure, whereas longitudinal implant outcome data for e-cigarettes remain insufficient [10]. This study compared longitudinal changes in peri-implant clinical and radiographic parameters between e-cigarette users and conventional cigarette smokers during the first 12 months after loading, and evaluated the influence of unequal exposure burden using a sensitivity analysis for current product-use duration and product-specific within-group intensity analyses. We hypothesized that parameter trajectories would differ between groups and that the differences might be partly related to imbalance in current exposure duration and use intensity.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This single-center retrospective study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations [11]. Cases of implant-supported restorations that began functional loading at Hainan General Hospital between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024, were included, and medical records were retrieved until February 28, 2026, to ensure complete 12-month outcome windows. Patients had to complete outcome monitoring 12 months after loading or have a documented implant failure within 12 months. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hainan General Hospital (ED-LC-2026–34–01), and the requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: age ≥ 18 years; implant surgery and initiation of functional loading completed at this hospital; completion of 12-month outcome monitoring after loading or documented implant failure within 12 months; in the e-cigarette (EC) group, continuous use of nicotine-containing e-cigarettes for ≥ 6 months before implant surgery, daily use from before surgery to T2 or failure, and no clearly documented use of conventional combustible tobacco; in the conventional cigarette (CS) group, continuous conventional cigarette smoking for ≥ 6 months before implant surgery, ≥ 10 cigarettes/day, continued conventional cigarette use from before surgery to T2 or failure, and no clearly documented e-cigarette use; preoperative full-mouth plaque index (PLI) ≤ 2; sufficient bone volume at the implant site to complete implant therapy; complete baseline data; and available planned follow-up clinical and radiographic records while implants remained in situ. The loading day was defined as baseline (T0), 6 ± 1 months after loading as T1, and 12 ± 2 months after loading as T2.

The exclusion criteria were uncontrolled diabetes (glycated hemoglobin A1c [HbA1c] > 8%), severe cardiovascular or immune-system disease, long-term use of medications affecting bone metabolism or soft-tissue healing, radiotherapy history in the implant region, pregnancy or lactation, major bone augmentation such as external sinus floor elevation or onlay bone grafting, previous treatment for peri-implantitis, smoking cessation or product switching during follow-up, clearly documented dual use, insufficient compliance, and absence of completed 12-month outcome monitoring except for documented definite failures.

Exposure classification and study flow

Tobacco exposure status was determined comprehensively from preoperative records, records on the loading day, and all available follow-up records until T2 or before failure. Patients were excluded if any available time point clearly documented dual use, smoking cessation, product switching, or substantial intensity change. During the study period, blood, saliva, or urinary cotinine, exhaled carbon monoxide, or tobacco-specific nitrosamines were not routinely measured, and exposure classification was based on medical records and patient reports. In the EC group, current duration of e-cigarette use, daily use frequency, device type, and nicotine concentration retrievable from the medical records were recorded. Conventional cigarette smoking history before initiation of e-cigarettes was not recorded with a unified questionnaire, start-stop dates, or pack-year units; therefore, no standardized variable suitable for quantitative analysis was available. We initially screened 417 patients with 576 implants and excluded 89 patients with 124 implants according to the criteria; 328 patients with 452 implants were finally included (Fig. 1).

Implant surgery and prosthetic protocol

All implant surgeries were performed by clinicians with more than 5 years of clinical implant experience. Preoperative clinical examination and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) were performed. Flapless surgery was used when bone volume and soft-tissue conditions permitted; flap surgery was used when direct visualization of bone defects or optimization of three-dimensional positioning was required. “Sufficient bone volume” meant that implant placement could be completed without major bone augmentation; localized guided bone regeneration (GBR) could be performed concurrently for limited dehiscence or insufficient bone-wall thickness. The same implant system was used in all cases. After implant placement, fixed restorations were delivered according to immediate, early, or delayed loading protocols and functional loading was initiated. Screw-retained or cement-retained restorations, loading protocol, insertion torque, localized GBR, and healing time were recorded. All patients received standardized oral hygiene instruction and recommendations for maintenance therapy.

Baseline data and periodontal risk

Preoperative oral data included full-mouth PLI, history of periodontal treatment, definite history and stage of periodontitis, full-mouth mean probing depth (PD), full-mouth bleeding on probing (BOP), presence of residual sites with PD ≥ 5 mm, and maintenance-treatment compliance. Two periodontists independently determined the history of periodontitis based on preoperative periodontal charts, radiographic bone loss, and reasons for tooth loss, and classified patients as having no definite history of periodontitis, Stage I-II, or Stage III-IV; discrepancies were resolved by discussion. In a random re-evaluation of 50 patients, inter-rater weighted κ was 0.86 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.78–0.94). Periodontitis staging followed the 2017 classification framework [12]. Regular maintenance was defined as documented professional maintenance at intervals not exceeding 6 months from T0 to T2 or before failure, including oral hygiene reinforcement, plaque-control assessment, and supragingival/subgingival mechanical debridement when required; implants remaining in situ at 12 months had to have at least two maintenance visits, and failure cases were classified according to attendance at planned visits before failure. Sex, age, body mass index (BMI), systemic diseases, medications, implant site, jaw, implant dimensions, prosthesis type, and loading protocol were also recorded.

Peri-implant clinical parameters

At T0, T1, and T2, the same periodontist examined six sites around each implant using a 1-mm graduated periodontal probe with a probing force of 0.20–0.25 N. Modified plaque index (mPLI), PD, modified sulcus bleeding index (mSBI), BOP, mucosal recession (MR), and keratinized mucosa width (KMW) were recorded. mPLI and mSBI were scored using modified 0–3 indices and averaged across six sites; PD was the mean of six sites; BOP was recorded as the number of sites bleeding within 30 s after probing and expressed as the proportion of the six sites. The inflammatory sign in follow-up case definitions was at least one BOP-positive site. MR was expressed as the distance from a fixed prosthetic reference point to the buccal mucosal margin. KMW was measured at the mid-buccal aspect from the mucosal margin to the mucogingival junction [13].

Radiographic parameters and repeatability

Standardized periapical radiographs were obtained at T0, T1, and T2 using a parallel technique and individualized bite-positioning devices. Examiners were blinded to exposure group, and magnification error was calibrated by the implant thread pitch or diameter. The vertical distance from the mesial and distal implant platform/abutment interface to the most coronal bone-implant contact was measured separately, and the mean was used as the marginal bone level (MBL). The change in marginal bone level was defined as ΔMBL = MBL(Tx) - MBL(T0), where Tx denotes T1 or T2. Standardized radiographs and continuous bone-level monitoring followed existing diagnostic and monitoring recommendations [14]. First-year bone-level change was reported as a continuous outcome and interpreted together with inflammation and PD changes [15]. Before formal measurements, the study examiner and an oral and maxillofacial radiologist with more than 10 years of experience completed training and calibration of reading criteria using 20 randomly selected implants not included in the repeatability analysis. After calibration, another 30 implants were randomly selected, and two examiners independently measured them under mutual blinding. The study examiner repeated measurements 14 days later while blinded to the initial results. The mean of mesial and distal values for each implant served as the unit of repeatability analysis. Inter-examiner and intra-examiner reliability were reported using a two-way mixed-effects, absolute-agreement, single-measure intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC); mean absolute difference, Bland–Altman bias, and 95% limits of agreement were also calculated [16].

Biological complications and implant failure

Prespecified biological outcomes were assessed at the implant level during follow-up. Inflammatory signs were defined as bleeding within 30 s after probing at at least one of six sites around each implant at either T1 or T2. The case definitions used standardized BOP records as the inflammatory indicator, whereas suppuration was not used as an analytic variable. Peri-implant mucositis was defined as an inflammatory sign at any follow-up time point with concurrent ΔMBL < 1.0 mm. The primary operational definition of peri-implantitis required an inflammatory sign, an increase in mean PD of ≥ 1.0 mm from T0, and ΔMBL ≥ 2.0 mm at the same follow-up time point. Implants with inflammatory signs and PD increase but ΔMBL of 1.0 to < 2.0 mm were recorded as an intermediate bone-loss phenotype; ΔMBL thresholds of ≥ 1.5 mm and ≥ 1.0 mm were used in sensitivity analyses. If the same implant met several prespecified outcomes at different follow-up times, the highest severity was recorded in the order of peri-implantitis, intermediate bone-loss phenotype, and peri-implant mucositis. Other findings not meeting the prespecified definitions were not counted in the corresponding case numbers but were retained in continuous outcome analyses. All binary outcomes were expressed as 12-month cumulative case proportions. Case definitions were based on existing diagnostic considerations for peri-implant mucositis [17] and the consensus framework for peri-implant diseases [18]. Implant failure was defined as removal after T0 because of mobility, persistent irreversible infection, uncontrollable progressive bone loss, or inability to maintain function; failure was recorded as an independent outcome and could overlap with biological outcomes met before removal.

Statistical analysis

All tests were two-sided, with α = 0.05. Patient-level normally distributed continuous variables were compared using independent-samples t tests, non-normally distributed variables using the Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables using χ² tests or Fisher exact tests. The observation unit in longitudinal analyses was the implant-time point. Generalized estimating equations (GEEs) were used with patient as the clustering level, an exchangeable working correlation structure, and robust sandwich standard errors to account for multiple implants within the same patient and repeated-measure correlations. mPLI, PD, mSBI, MR, KMW, MBL, and ΔMBL were modeled using normal working distributions and identity link functions. BOP was modeled as a binomial outcome based on the number of bleeding sites among six sites per implant using a logit link; between-group differences were expressed as percentage-point differences in model marginal probabilities. Time was treated as categorical, and models included group, time, and group-by-time interaction.

PD and ΔMBL were prioritized continuous outcomes, and other continuous and binary outcomes were secondary or exploratory. The main model adjusted for age, sex, BMI, preoperative PLI, history/stage of periodontitis, baseline full-mouth BOP, maintenance-treatment compliance, implant site, jaw, localized GBR, prosthesis type, loading protocol, and healing time. To evaluate imbalance in current exposure duration, a post hoc sensitivity analysis was performed beyond the main model by additionally including current exposure duration (years). Daily e-cigarette use frequency, nicotine concentration, conventional cigarettes per day, and pack-years were entered into separate exploratory models within the EC and CS groups; pack-years were not modeled simultaneously with smoking duration and cigarettes per day. Device-subgroup models were further adjusted for EC use duration and daily use frequency in addition to the clinical covariates in the main model. Because nicotine concentration had a high missing proportion, it was assessed only as an exploratory covariate in cases with available data and was not a prespecified adjustment variable in the main device-subgroup model. Peri-implant mucositis was analyzed using patient-clustered binomial GEE; the parsimonious model included group, age, history of periodontitis, baseline BOP, maintenance compliance, and current exposure duration. Crude odds ratios (ORs) and parsimoniously adjusted odds ratios (aORs) were reported. Because post-loading failure and peri-implantitis events were rare, only patient-clustered crude ORs and 95% CIs were reported. Outcomes were available for all 452 implants at T0; implant removal made subsequent continuous outcomes structurally unobservable for four implants at T1 and 12 cumulatively at T2. GEE used all observations available before removal and no imputation was performed. Apart from structural unobservability, there were no item-level missing data for the main outcomes or main-model covariates. In the EC group, nicotine concentration was missing in 34 patients (22.4%), and device power and flavor category were missing in 79 (52.0%) and 65 (42.8%) patients, respectively. All eligible cases during the study period were included, and no a priori sample-size calculation was performed. Secondary and exploratory analyses were not adjusted for multiplicity; findings were interpreted according to effect sizes and 95% CIs.

Results

Inclusion flow, baseline characteristics, and exposure characteristics

Initially, 417 patients with 576 implants were screened. Eighty-nine patients with 124 implants were excluded, including 31/43 with incomplete 12-month outcome monitoring and no definite failure, 18/27 with documented dual use, 13/18 with exposure mode or intensity changes during follow-up, 9/13 with major bone augmentation, 8/11 with incomplete main-outcome data, 6/8 with uncontrolled systemic disease or prohibited medications, and 4/4 for other reasons. Ultimately, 328 patients with 452 implants were included. Continuous outcomes were available for 452, 448, and 440 implants at T0, T1, and T2, respectively; unobservable values at T1 and T2 were all due to post-loading removal, and all 12 removed implants were included in the failure analysis (Fig. 1). Sex, age, BMI, preoperative PLI, systemic diseases, medications, history of periodontal treatment, history/stage of periodontitis, baseline full-mouth PD, baseline full-mouth BOP, residual sites with PD ≥ 5 mm, regular maintenance therapy, and number of included implants per patient were similar between groups (all P > 0.05) (Table 1). Current exposure duration was longer in the CS group than in the EC group (P < 0.001). Daily use frequency, device composition, and retrievable nicotine concentration in the EC group, as well as cigarettes per day and pack-years in the CS group, were described only within products (Table 2). Implant site, jaw, diameter, length, insertion torque, localized GBR, prosthesis type, loading protocol, and healing time were similar between groups (all P > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 1). There were no item-level missing data for T0 outcomes or main-model covariates. Structural unobservable values at T1 and T2 were more frequent in the CS group than in the EC group. Nicotine concentration, device power, and flavor category were missing to varying degrees in the EC group, and previous conventional cigarette smoking history was not collected in a standardized manner (Supplementary Table 2).

Peri-implant clinical parameters

At T0, mPLI, PD, mSBI, BOP, MR, and KMW were similar between groups. Over time, mPLI, PD, mSBI, BOP, and MR increased, whereas KMW decreased. Increases in mPLI, PD, mSBI, BOP, and MR were greater in the CS group than in the EC group, and the decline in KMW was greater in the CS group. The time effect and group-by-time interaction were statistically significant for all six indicators (all P < 0.05) (Table 3; Fig. 2).

Radiographic parameters and measurement repeatability

At T0, MBL was similar between groups. MBL and ΔMBL increased over time, with larger increases in the CS group than in the EC group; both outcomes showed statistically significant time effects and group-by-time interactions (all P < 0.05). The inter-examiner ICC was 0.92 (95% CI, 0.86–0.96), and the intra-examiner ICC was 0.95 (95% CI, 0.92–0.97). The mean absolute difference was 0.06 mm, and the Bland–Altman mean bias was 0.01 mm with 95% limits of agreement from − 0.15 to 0.17 mm (Table 4; Figs. 3 and 4).

Exposure duration and within-group dose analyses

After addition of current exposure duration, between-group estimates for mPLI, PD, mSBI, BOP, MR, KMW, MBL, and ΔMBL decreased. At T2, the CS group had higher mPLI, PD, mSBI, BOP, MR, MBL, and ΔMBL and lower KMW than the EC group; group-by-time interactions remained statistically significant for all outcomes (all P < 0.05) (Table 5). Within the EC group, current use duration was positively associated with T2 PD and ΔMBL (both P < 0.05); daily frequency was positively associated with T2 PD (P < 0.05) and borderline associated with ΔMBL (P = 0.050); nicotine concentration was not statistically associated with either outcome (both P > 0.05). In the CS group, smoking duration, cigarettes per day, and pack-years were each positively associated with T2 PD and ΔMBL (all P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 3).

E-cigarette device subgroups

In the EC group, closed disposable device users outnumbered open refillable device users. The closed-system subgroup had higher daily use frequency and retrievable nicotine concentration (both P < 0.05), whereas current use duration did not differ significantly between device types (P > 0.05). After adjustment for use duration and daily frequency, closed and open devices did not differ significantly at T2 in mPLI, PD, mSBI, BOP, MR, KMW, ΔMBL, or peri-implant mucositis (all P > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 4). Complete-case assessment of nicotine concentration was used only as an exploratory supplement and was not the basis for the main device-subgroup conclusion.

Post-loading failure and biological complications

At 12 months after loading, the point estimates of cumulative case proportions for implant failure, peri-implant mucositis, peri-implantitis, and the intermediate bone-loss phenotype were all higher in the CS group than in the EC group. The crude association for peri-implant mucositis reached statistical significance (crude OR, 1.72; P = 0.043), but the effect attenuated after parsimonious adjustment and the 95% CI included 1 (aOR, 1.64; P = 0.060). Patient-clustered crude comparisons for post-loading failure and peri-implantitis showed no statistically significant differences (both P > 0.05) (Table 6). When 1.5-mm and 1.0-mm radiographic operational thresholds were used, the cumulative number of peri-implantitis cases increased and the effect direction remained consistent; neither the intermediate bone-loss phenotype nor any threshold comparison showed statistical significance (all P > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 5).

Discussion

This study compared clinical and radiographic parameter trajectories between e-cigarette users and conventional cigarette smokers during the first 12 months after implant loading. The CS group showed larger increases in mPLI, PD, mSBI, BOP, MR, MBL, and ΔMBL and a greater decrease in KMW. After current exposure duration was added, between-group estimates for continuous outcomes decreased, but group-by-time interactions remained. The crude association for peri-implant mucositis attenuated after parsimonious adjustment, and effect estimates for peri-implantitis and post-loading failure were accompanied by wide 95% CIs.

The findings reflect observational associations between current main product type and peri-implant parameter trajectories. Existing evidence suggests that e-cigarette aerosol may affect osteoblast activity and function, providing biological plausibility for bone-level changes [19]. Effects of aerosol on the gingival mucosal barrier and inflammatory responses are also compatible with the direction of soft-tissue changes [20]. Immunologic, microcirculatory, and tissue-repair alterations related to conventional cigarette smoking are associated with poorer periodontal and implant-therapy outcomes [21], and the link between smoking and altered osteoimmune homeostasis further supports attention to marginal bone changes [22]. The present study did not measure these mediating variables, and mechanistic evidence was used only to explain biological plausibility.

Larger increases in mPLI, mSBI, and BOP in the CS group suggest between-group differences in longitudinal trajectories of plaque burden and inflammatory signs. Evidence that periodontal and peri-implant conditions improve after smoking cessation supports continuous assessment of tobacco exposure during maintenance [23]. The point estimate of post-loading failure was higher in the CS group, but the 95% CI was wide. Existing evidence for smoking and early implant failure is compatible with the direction of this study, whereas differences in event windows and endpoint definitions limit direct numerical comparisons [24].

At T2, the crude PD difference was 0.54 mm, and the exposure-duration-adjusted difference was 0.42 mm (95% CI, 0.25–0.59). There is no accepted minimal clinically important difference for peri-implant PD, and its clinical meaning should be judged in relation to increasing divergence over time, concomitant increases in BOP and mSBI, and individual PD changes. At T2, the crude ΔMBL difference was 0.33 mm, and the exposure-duration-adjusted difference was 0.26 mm (95% CI, 0.15–0.37). Bone-level change during the first year may include adaptive bone modeling and should be interpreted together with inflammation, PD increase, continuing bone loss, and measurement error. A history of periodontitis is associated with risks of implant failure and peri-implantitis [25]. Patients who have completed periodontal therapy still require stratification by previous disease severity and residual inflammation [26]. Regular supportive periodontal therapy is associated with higher implant survival, especially in patients with periodontitis-related tooth loss [27]. This study incorporated periodontitis stage, baseline BOP, and maintenance compliance into the model to more directly characterize underlying periodontal risk.

MBL changes during the first year after loading may include adaptive bone modeling, and peri-implant health can exist with stable differences in bone level [28]. The relationship between early marginal bone changes and subsequent risk should be judged in relation to continued progression, inflammation, and prosthetic factors [29]. The primary analysis used ΔMBL ≥ 2.0 mm to characterize more definite radiographic progression within 12 months and required concurrent BOP and PD increase at the same follow-up time point; sensitivity definitions of 1.5 mm and 1.0 mm were used to evaluate the influence of threshold selection. Cases with ΔMBL of 1.0 to < 2.0 mm accompanied by BOP and PD increase were reported separately as an intermediate bone-loss phenotype. The adjusted between-group difference in ΔMBL exceeded the mean absolute measurement difference, but individual values close to the operational threshold may still be affected by measurement variation.

MR increase and KMW reduction were more marked in the CS group. Peri-implant soft-tissue recession is associated with multiple factors, including local anatomy, tissue thickness, prosthetic contour, cleansability, and inflammation [30]. Therefore, soft-tissue trajectories should be assessed together with inflammation, prosthetic design, and patient maintenance behavior; a single exposure variable represents only one risk dimension.

E-cigarette exposure is substantially heterogeneous. Closed disposable devices and open systems differ in nicotine salt formulation, liquid concentration, power output, atomizer coil, temperature, and aerosol constituents. After adjustment for current use duration and daily frequency, CIs for device-subgroup effects remained wide, and data on power, liquid formulation, inhalation duration, and flavor were incomplete. E-cigarette aerosol extracts may affect responses of human gingival epithelial cells [31]. Existing oral evidence also suggests product heterogeneity among conventional cigarettes, heated tobacco products, and e-cigarettes, and differences in exposure definitions and comparison groups limit direct ranking [32]. The applicability of device-subgroup results should be limited to the products and use patterns included in this study.

Previous clinical data suggest differences in peri-implant clinical and inflammatory indicators among conventional cigarette smokers, e-cigarette users, and nonsmokers [33]. Cotinine in peri-implant sulcular fluid can distinguish nicotine exposure, although actual delivered dose remains affected by use behavior and device characteristics [34]. Longitudinal diagnosis requires integration of persistent BOP, PD changes, and bone-level progression rather than reliance on a single-time-point indicator [35]. Irregular maintenance and narrow keratinized mucosa are associated with peri-implantitis risk, supporting simultaneous assessment of maintenance frequency and soft-tissue conditions in follow-up plans [36].

The marked imbalance in current exposure duration between groups is an important source of confounding when interpreting between-group associations. Effect sizes decreased after duration adjustment, suggesting that imbalance in current exposure duration was related to part of the between-group differences; however, the exposure-duration distributions had limited overlap, and adjusted estimates may still rely on extrapolation near the edges of the observed range. Previous conventional cigarette history in the EC group was not systematically quantified, and previous dose, switching time, current behavior, and reporting accuracy could attenuate or amplify associations. Exposure status lacked biochemical verification, and unreported dual use or between-visit exposure changes could further cause misclassification with uncertain direction. The absence of a never-smoker control group limits conclusions to relative comparisons between EC and CS groups.

Other limitations include only 12 months of follow-up, small event numbers, single-center source, missing nicotine-concentration and device-parameter data in the EC group, and possible selection bias because complete outcome monitoring was an inclusion criterion. Post-loading removal rendered subsequent continuous outcomes structurally unobservable, so longitudinal parameters reflect conditional trajectories while implants remained in situ; informative removal may have underestimated overall unfavorable changes. Peri-implantitis and post-loading failure were not subjected to high-dimensional adjustment because events were sparse. Multiple secondary and exploratory analyses were not adjusted for multiplicity; their P values should be interpreted alongside effect sizes and 95% CIs. Future studies should include never-smoker controls, prospectively record lifetime tobacco trajectories, device parameters, and inhalation behavior, and incorporate biomarkers such as cotinine, exhaled carbon monoxide, or 4-(methylnitrosamino)-1-(3-pyridyl)-1-butanol (NNAL).

Conclusions

In this single-center retrospective study, conventional cigarette exposure was associated with larger 12-month peri-implant clinical and radiographic parameter changes while implants remained in situ. After adjustment for current exposure duration, associations for prioritized continuous outcomes attenuated but persisted. The crude association for peri-implant mucositis attenuated after parsimonious adjustment, and estimates for peri-implantitis and post-loading failure were imprecise. Imbalance in current exposure duration, lack of systematic recording of previous smoking history among e-cigarette users, absence of biochemical verification of exposure status, heterogeneity of e-cigarette devices, informative missingness after implant removal, and absence of a never-smoker control group limit causal inference and absolute-risk judgment.
