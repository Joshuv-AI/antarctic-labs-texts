1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{U}
be an algebra. The additive mapping
\phi:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
is said to be a
Lie centralizer
if
\phi([a,b])=[\phi(a),b]
for each
a,b\in\mathcal{U}
, where
[a,b]=ab-ba
is the Lie product of
a
and
b
in
\mathcal{U}
. A routine check shows that
\phi
is a Lie centralizer on
\mathcal{U}
if and only if
\phi([a,b])=[a,\phi(b)]
for all
a,b\in\mathcal{U}
. Lie centralizers are an important class of mappings related to the Lie structure of algebras. This is a classical notion in the theory of Lie and other non-associative algebras. We are only interested in the case, where associative algebras are endowed with the Lie product and create a Lie algebra. Lie centralizers have recently been studied a lot on algebras from different perspectives (For instance , see
[
1
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
11
,
12
,
14
,
17
,
18
,
20
,
21
]
and the references therein). One of these study paths has been the characterization of Lie centralizers at specific products. To be precise, this study path consists of characterizing the mapping
\phi:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
that fulfills the following condition:

a,b\in\mathcal{U},\,ab=w\Longrightarrow\phi([a,b])=[\phi(a),b]=[a,\phi(b)]\quad(\textbf{L}_{w}),

where
w\in\mathcal{U}
is fixed. The mapping
\phi
satisfying
(\textbf{L}_{w})
is called
Lie centralizable at
w
. Determined mappings with local properties have always been considered in mathematics. Among the local properties, we can mention mappings on rings or algebras that act on special products such as Lie centralizers. We refer to a number of studies conducted in this direction. The authors in
[
1
]
have studied the characterization of linear Lie centralizable mappings at zero on non-unital triangular algebras. In
[
15
]
, linear Lie centralizers at the zero products on some operator algebras are studied that
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
(the algebra of all bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{X}
) is a class of these operator algebras. In
[
6
]
, linear Lie centralizable mappings at zero on a 2-torsion free unital generalized matrix algebra under some mild conditions, are described. As a result of
[
6
]
(or
[
15
]
), it is proved that if
\mathcal{X}
is a Banach space over the complex field
\mathbb{C}
and
\phi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
is a linear mapping satisfying
(\textbf{L}_{0})
, then
\phi
is in the form
\phi(A)=\lambda A+\mu(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
\mu:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}I
is a linear map such that
\mu([A,B])=0
for all
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
with
AB=0
. In
[
5
]
, the problem of characterizing linear Lie centralizable mappings at a non-trivial idempotent element on triangular algebras is considered, and in
[
19
]
, additive Lie centralizable mappings at a non-trivial idempotent element on a 2-torsion free unital prime ring are determined. Also in
[
13
]
, additive Lie centralizable mappings at a non-trivial idempotent element on a unital Banach algebra have been studied. As a corollary of
[
13
]
, we have that if
\mathcal{X}
is a complex Banach space,
P\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
is a non-trivial idempotent (i.e.,
P^{2}=P,P\neq 0,I
) and the additive mapping
\phi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
satisfies
(\textbf{L}_{P})
, then
\phi(A)=\lambda A+\mu(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
\mu:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}I
is an additive mapping such that
\mu([A,B])=0
for all
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
with
AB=P
. In
[
23
]
, the authors studied the linear Lie centralizable mappings on generalized matrix algebras at a specific point
w
that includes non-trivial idempotents. To find more results in this regard, we refer to
[
10

,

,

and the references therein. In all the conducted studies, the Lie centralizable mappings have been characterized at the zero point or non-trivial idempotent element or the elements that are close to the non-trivial idempotents. Now, in continuation of these studies, in this article we want to determine the additive Lie centralizable mappings at points other than zero points and non-trivial idempotents on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
, and also obtain a generalization of the previous results on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
. More specifically, we will prove the following results.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
be the algebra of all bounded linear operators on a complex Banach space
\mathcal{X}
, and let
\phi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
be an additive mapping. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\phi
satisfies
(\textbf{L}_{W})
, where
W\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
and
\overline{W(\mathcal{X})}\neq\mathcal{X}
;

(ii)

\phi(A)=\lambda A+\mu(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
\mu:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}I
is an additive mapping such that
\mu([A,B])=0
for all
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
with
AB=W
.

The proof of this theorem is provided in Section 2. A consequence of this theorem is the case when
W
is zero or non-trivial idempotent (Corollaries
2.1
and
2.2
). This result is also presented in the third section, which shows that Theorem
1.1
is also a generalization of the previous results.

If we can prove Theorem
1.1
in the case where
\overline{W(\mathcal{X})}=\mathcal{X}
, then we actually obtain a characterization of the additive mappings on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
satisfying in
(\textbf{L}_{W})
for every point
W\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
. We were only able to prove this in the special case of Theorem
1.2
, and the general case remains an open problem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
be the algebra of all bounded linear operators on a complex Banach space
\mathcal{X}
, and let
\phi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
be an additive mapping. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\phi
satisfies
(\textbf{L}_{\xi I})
, where
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
is fixed and
I
is the identity operator;

(ii)

\phi(A)=\lambda A+\mu(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
\mu:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}I
is an additive mapping such that
\mu([A,B])=0
for all
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
with
AB=\xi I
.

The proof of this theorem is given in Section 3. To prove this theorem, we first prove Lemma
3.1
in which we characterize additive mappings on a unital Banach algebra
\mathcal{U}
satisfying
(\textbf{L}_{w})
for an invertible element
w\in\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{U})
, where
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{U})
is the center of
\mathcal{U}
. This characterization is in terms of additive commuting maps, which is an interesting result in itself.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section, we provide the proof of Theorem
1.1
and some of its results.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): If
dim\mathcal{X}=1
then
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})=\mathbb{C}
is commutative and it is enough to set
\mu:=\phi
and
\lambda:=0
. Therefore the result is valid in this case. Now we assume
dim\mathcal{X}\geq 2
. Since
\overline{W(\mathcal{X})}\neq\mathcal{X}
, there exists a nonzero element
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
such that

x_{0}\notin\overline{W(\mathcal{X})}
. Suppose
f_{0}\in X^{\ast}
such that,
f_{0}(x_{0})=1

and
f_{0}(\overline{W(\mathcal{X})})=0
. We set

P_{1}=x_{0}\otimes f_{0}
and
P_{2}=I-P_{1}.
Then,
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are nontrivial idempotents in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
such that
P_{1}W=0
and
P_{2}W=W
.

Consider the pierce decomposition of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
associated with the
P_{1}
as follows

\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})=\mathcal{U}_{11}+\mathcal{U}_{12}+\mathcal{U}_{21}+\mathcal{U}_{22}

in which
\mathcal{U}_{ij}=P_{i}\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})P_{j},(1\leqslant i,j\leqslant 2)
.
Then
\mathcal{U}_{11}
and
\mathcal{U}_{22}
are unital Banach algebras with identities
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, respectively and
\mathcal{U}_{12}
is a unital Banach
(\mathcal{U}_{11},\mathcal{U}_{22})
-bimodule and
\mathcal{U}_{21}
is a unital Banach
(\mathcal{U}_{22},\mathcal{U}_{11})
-bimodule. We denote each element
\mathcal{U}_{ij}
by
A_{ij}.
It is clear that
P_{1}=x_{0}\otimes f_{0}
is a one-dimensional idempotent. Also for each
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
we have

P_{1}AP_{1}=(x_{0}\otimes f_{0})A(x_{0}\otimes f_{0})=\lambda x_{0}\otimes f_{0}

for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.
Thus
\mathcal{U}_{11}=\mathbb{C}P_{1}
.

We complete the proof by proving the following steps.

Claim 1
.

For any
A_{11}\in\mathcal{U}_{11}
and
A_{22}\in\mathcal{U}_{22}
,

A_{22}\phi(A_{11})=\phi(A_{11})A_{22}

and

P_{1}\phi(A_{11})P_{2}=P_{2}\phi(A_{11})P_{1}=0.

Proof.

For any invertible element
A_{22}\in\mathcal{U}_{22}
and
0\neq\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, since

A_{22}(A_{22}^{-1}W)=W
and
A_{22}(A_{22}^{-1}W+\lambda P_{1})=W
(
A_{22}^{-1}
denotes the inverse of
A_{22}
in
\mathcal{U}_{22}
), we have

(2.1)

\phi([A_{22},A_{22}^{-1}W])=[A_{22},\phi(A_{22}^{-1}W)]

and

\phi([A_{22},A_{22}^{-1}W+\lambda P_{1}])=[A_{22},\phi(A_{22}^{-1}W+\lambda P_{1})].

Consequently,

(2.2)

\phi([A_{22},A_{22}^{-1}W])=[A_{22},\phi(A_{22}^{-1}W)+\phi(\lambda P_{1})].

Comparing (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), we see that
[A_{22},\phi(\lambda P_{1})]=0
for any invertible element
A_{22}\in\mathcal{U}_{22}
. By the fact that each element of a Banach algebra is a sum of two invertible elements we get
[A_{22},\phi(\lambda P_{1})]=0
for all
A_{22}\in\mathcal{U}_{22}
. Hence

P_{2}\phi(\lambda P_{1})=\phi(\lambda P_{1})P_{2}
. So

P_{2}\phi(\lambda P_{1})P_{1}=P_{1}\phi(\lambda P_{1})P_{2}=0.

Now, for any
A_{11}\in\mathcal{U}_{11}
, since
A_{11}=\lambda P_{1}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, we get desired results.
∎

Claim 2
.

For any
A_{11}\in\mathcal{U}_{11}
and
A_{22}\in\mathcal{U}_{22}
,

A_{11}\phi(A_{22})=\phi(A_{22})A_{11}

and

P_{1}\phi(A_{22})P_{2}=P_{2}\phi(A_{22})P_{1}=0.

Proof.

For any invertible element
A_{22}\in\mathcal{U}_{22}
, since

A_{22}(P_{1}+A_{22}^{-1}W)=W

and

A_{22}(A_{22}^{-1}W)=W
, we have

(2.3)

\phi(\left[A_{22},P_{1}+A_{22}^{-1}W\right])=\left[\phi(A_{22}),P_{1}+A_{22}^{-1}W\right]

and

(2.4)

\phi(\left[A_{22},A_{22}^{-1}W\right])=\left[\phi(A_{22}),A_{22}^{-1}W\right].

From (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), we see that

(2.5)

0=\phi(\left[A_{22},P_{1}\right])=\left[\phi(A_{22}),P_{1}\right].

For each invertible element
A_{22}
in
\mathcal{U}_{22}
, from (
2.5
), It is obtained that

P_{2}\phi(A_{22})P_{1}=P_{1}\phi(A_{22})P_{2}=0.

Now, for an arbitrary element
A_{22}
of
\mathcal{U}_{22}
, we consider the non-negative integer
n
as
n>\|A_{22}\|
. Since
nP_{2}-A_{22}
is invertible in the Banach algebra
\mathcal{U}_{22}
, it follows that

[\phi(A_{22}),P_{1}]=0

and

P_{2}\phi(A_{22})P_{1}=P_{1}\phi(A_{22})P_{2}=0,

for any
A_{22}\in\mathcal{U}_{22}
. Thus
[\phi(A_{22}),\lambda P_{1}]=0
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, and hence

A_{11}\phi(A_{22})=\phi(A_{22})A_{11}

for any
A_{11}\in\mathcal{U}_{11}
and
A_{22}\in\mathcal{U}_{22}
.
∎

Claim 3
.

For any
A_{12}\in\mathcal{U}_{12}
,

P_{1}\phi(A_{12})P_{1}=P_{2}\phi(A_{12})P_{1}=P_{2}\phi(A_{12})P_{2}=0.

Proof.

For any
A_{11}\in\mathcal{U}_{11}
and
A_{12}\in\mathcal{U}_{12}
, since

P_{2}(W+A_{11}+A_{12})=W,\quad and\quad P_{2}W=W

we have

(2.6)

\phi(\left[P_{2},W+A_{11}+A_{12}\right])=\left[\phi(P_{2}),W+A_{11}+A_{12}\right]

and

(2.7)

\phi(\left[P_{2},W\right]=\left[\phi(P_{2}),W\right].

Hence, we can infer from (
2.6
) and (
2.7
), that

(2.8)

-\phi(A_{12})=[\phi(P_{2}),A_{11}+A_{12}]

for all
A_{11}\in\mathcal{A}_{11}
and
A_{12}\in\mathcal{A}_{12}
. We substitute
A_{12}=0
into (
2.8
), thus
[\phi(P_{2}),A_{11}]=0
which implies

\phi(A_{12})=A_{12}\phi(P_{2})-\phi(P_{2})A_{12}.

By using Claim
2

(2.9)

\phi(A_{12})=A_{12}\phi(P_{2})P_{2}-P_{1}\phi(P_{2})A_{12}.

Multiplying (
2.9
) once from left and right to
P_{1}
, once from left and right to
P_{2}
, and once from left to
P_{2}
and from right to
P_{1}
, we conclude that

P_{1}\phi(A_{12})P_{1}=P_{2}\phi(A_{12})P_{1}=P_{2}\phi(A_{12})P_{2}=0.

∎

Claim 4
.

For any
A_{21}\in\mathcal{U}_{21}
,

P_{1}\phi(A_{21})P_{2}=0.

Proof.

For any
A_{21}\in\mathcal{U}_{21}
, since

(A_{21}+P_{2})(W+P_{1}-A_{21})=W
and
(A_{21}+P_{2})W=W
, we have

\phi([A_{21}+P_{2},W+P_{1}-A_{21}])=[\phi(A_{21}+P_{2}),W+P_{1}-A_{21}]

and

\phi([A_{21}+P_{2},W]=[\phi(A_{21}+P_{2}),W].

Now from previous equations and Claim
2
, it follows that

\begin{split}0&=\phi([A_{21}+P_{2},P_{1}-A_{21}])\\
&=\left[\phi(A_{21}+P_{2}),P_{1}-A_{21}\right]\\
&=\phi(A_{21})P_{1}-\phi(A_{21})A_{21}-\phi(P_{2})A_{21}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad-P_{1}\phi(A_{21})+A_{21}\phi(A_{21})+A_{21}\phi(P_{2})\\
&=\phi(A_{21})P_{1}-\phi(A_{21})A_{21}-P_{2}\phi(P_{2})A_{21}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad-P_{1}\phi(A_{21})+A_{21}\phi(A_{21})+A_{21}\phi(P_{2})P_{1}\end{split}

for each
A_{21}
in
\mathcal{A}_{21}
.
Now we multiply both sides of the above equation by
P_{2}
on the right and by
P_{1}
on the left. Therefore

P_{1}\phi(A_{21})P_{2}=0

for all
A_{21}
in
\mathcal{A}_{21}

∎

is additive, it follows that

is additive. From Claim

∎

Claim 7

A_{12}(\lambda_{A_{21}}P_{2}-P_{2}\phi(A_{21})P_{2})=0

for all
A_{12}\in\mathcal{U}_{12}
. Since
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
is a prime algebra, it follows that

P_{2}\phi(A_{21})P_{2}=\lambda_{A_{21}}P_{2}

for all
A_{21}\in\mathcal{U}_{21}
. Define the well-defined mapping
h_{3}:\mathcal{U}_{21}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}I
by
h_{3}(A_{21})=\lambda_{A_{21}}I
. Given the fact that
\phi
is additive, it follows that
h_{3}
is additive. From Claim
4
and the definition of
h_{3}
, we have

\begin{split}\phi(A_{21})-h_{3}(A_{21})&=P_{1}\phi(A_{21})P_{1}+P_{2}\phi(A_{21})P_{1}+P_{2}\phi(A_{21})P_{2}-\lambda_{A_{21}}I\\
&=\lambda_{A_{21}}P_{1}+P_{2}\phi(A_{21})P_{1}+\lambda_{A_{21}}P_{2}-\lambda_{A_{21}}I\\
&=P_{2}\phi(A_{21})P_{1}\in\mathcal{U}_{21}\end{split}

for all
A_{21}\in\mathcal{U}_{21}
.
∎

Now, for any
A=A_{11}+A_{12}+A_{21}+A_{22}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
, we define two additive mappings
\mu:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathbb{C}I
and
\psi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
by

\mu(A)=h_{1}(A_{11})+h_{3}(A_{21})+h_{2}(A_{22})~~~~\text{and}~~~~\psi(A)=\phi(A)-\mu(A).

By Claims
1
-
7
, it is clear that
\psi(\mathcal{U}_{ij})\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{ij}
for
1\leq i\neq j\leq 2
, and
\psi(A_{12})=\phi(A_{12})
for any
A_{12}\in\mathcal{U}_{12}
.

Claim 8
.

\psi
satisfies
\psi(A)=\lambda A
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

First, we show that
\psi(I)=\lambda I
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. For every
A_{21}\in\mathcal{U}_{21}
, since

(P_{2}-A_{21})(W+A_{21}+P_{1})=W

and

(P_{2}-A_{21})W=W,

it follows that

\begin{split}\phi(\left[P_{2}-A_{21},W+A_{21}+P_{1}\right])&=\left[\phi(P_{2}-A_{21}),W+A_{21}+P_{1}\right]\\
&=\left[P_{2}-A_{21},\phi(W+A_{21}+P_{1})\right]\end{split}

and

\phi([P_{2}-A_{21},W])=[\phi(P_{2}-A_{21}),W]=[P_{2}-A_{21},\phi(W)].

From these equations and the fact that
\psi(\mathcal{U}_{ij})\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{ij}
for
1\leq i\neq j\leq 2
, we have

\begin{split}0&=\phi(\left[P_{2}-A_{21},A_{21}+P_{1}\right])\\
&=\left[\phi(P_{2}-A_{21}),A_{21}+P_{1}\right]\\
&=\left[\psi(P_{2}-A_{21}),A_{21}+P_{1}\right]\\
&=\psi(P_{2})A_{21}-\psi(A_{21})\end{split}

and

\begin{split}0&=\phi(\left[P_{2}-A_{21},A_{21}+P_{1}\right])\\
&=\left[P_{2}-A_{21},\phi(A_{21}+P_{1})\right]\\
&=\left[P_{2}-A_{21}),\psi(A_{21}+P_{1})\right]\\
&=\psi(A_{21})-A_{21}\psi(P_{1})\end{split}

Thus

(2.12)

\psi(A_{21})=\psi(P_{2})A_{21}=A_{21}\psi(P_{1})

for every
A_{21}\in\mathcal{U}_{21}
. Since
\mathcal{U}_{11}=\mathbb{C}P_{1}
and
\psi(P_{1})\in\mathcal{U}_{11}
, there exists a
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\psi(P_{1})=\lambda P_{1}
. According to (
2.12
), we get

\psi(P_{2})A_{21}=\lambda A_{21},

and hence

(\psi(P_{2})-\lambda P_{2})A_{21}=0

for all
A_{21}\in\mathcal{U}_{21}
. Since
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
is a prime algebra, it follows that

\psi(P_{2})=\lambda P_{2}.

Consequentially,

\psi(I)=\psi(P_{1})+\psi(P_{2})=\lambda P_{1}+\lambda P_{2}=\lambda I.

From (
2.12
) and
\psi(P_{2})\in\mathcal{U}_{22}
, we see that

\psi(A_{21})=A_{21}\psi(P_{1})=A_{21}\psi(P_{1})+A_{21}\psi(P_{2})=A_{21}\psi(I)=\lambda A_{21}

for every
A_{21}\in\mathcal{U}_{21}

\psi(A_{12})=\phi(A_{12})
for any
A_{12}\in\mathcal{U}_{12}

2.9

\psi(A_{12})=A_{12}\phi(P_{2})P_{2}-P_{1}\phi(P_{2})A_{12}.

From the definition of
h_{2}
we have
P_{1}\phi(P_{2})P_{1}=h_{2}(P_{2})P_{1}
and therefore from the definitions of
\psi
and
\mu

\begin{split}\psi(A_{12})&=A_{12}\phi(P_{2})P_{2}-h_{2}(P_{2})A_{12}\\
&=A_{12}(\phi(P_{2})-\mu(P_{2}))P_{2}\\
&=A_{12}\psi(P_{2})\end{split}

for every
A_{12}\in\mathcal{U}_{12}
. In view of the fact that
\psi(P_{1})\in\mathcal{U}_{11}

for every
A_{12}\in\mathcal{U}_{12}

A_{22}\in\mathcal{U}_{22}
and

(A_{22}-A_{22}B_{21})(A_{22}^{-1}W+B_{21}+P_{1})=W

and

By applying
\phi
to these equations, we arrive at

\phi(\left[A_{22}-A_{22}B_{21},A_{22}^{-1}W+B_{21}+P_{1}\right])=\left[\phi(A_{22}-A_{22}B_{21}),A_{22}^{-1}W+B_{21}+P_{1}\right]

Proof of Theorem

To prove this theorem, we first need to introduce commuting maps. Let
\mathcal{U}
be an algebra. Recall that a map
\theta
of
\mathcal{U}
into itself is called a
commuting map
if
[\theta(a),a]=0
for all
a\in\mathcal{U}
. One can easily check that each Lie centralizer is a commuting map, but the converse is, in general, not true (see
[
15
, Example 2.3]
).Commuting maps have played a crucial role in the development of the theory of functional identities. We refer the reader to the survey paper
[
3
]
in which the author presented the development of the theory of commuting maps and their applications.

As we mentioned in the introduction, the following lemma is interesting in itself.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a complex Banach algebra with unity
1
, and
w
is an invertible element in
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{U})
. If
\phi:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
is an additive mapping satisfying
(\textbf{L}_{w})
, then
\phi
is a commuting map.

Proof.

For each invertible element
a\in\mathcal{U}
we have
aa^{-1}w=w
. So

[\phi(a),a^{-1}w]=0,

and hence
\phi(a)a^{-1}w-a^{-1}w\phi(a)=0
. In view of the fact that
w\in\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{U})
is invertible, we conclude that

(3.1)

\phi(a)a=a\phi(a).

We consider the non-negative integer
n>\|a\|
for the invertible element
a\in\mathcal{U}
. So
n1-a
is invertible and from (
3.1
) it follows that
\phi(n1-a)(n1-a)=(n1-a)\phi(n1-a)
, and hence
\phi(n1)a=a\phi(n1)
. Since
\phi
is additive we get
\phi(1)a=a\phi(1)
for all invertible element
a\in\mathcal{U}
. By the fact that each element of a Banach algebra is a sum of two invertible elements we conclude that
\phi(1)\in\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{U})
.

Now, for an arbitrary element
a
of
\mathcal{U}
, we consider the non-negative integer
n
as
n>\|a\|
. Since
n1-a
is invertible, it follows from (
3.1
) that
\phi(n1-a)(n1-a)=(n1-a)\phi(n1-a)
. Given that
\phi(1)\in\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{U})
, we see that
\phi(a)a=a\phi(a)
. Therefore,
\phi
is a commuting map.
∎

From the proof of the above lemma, it also follows that on Banach algebras additive commuting maps in invertible elements are commuting maps.
