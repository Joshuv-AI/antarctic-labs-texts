Design and optimisation of patient-specific porous femoral stems
Authors: Sarah Safavi, Hans A. Gray, Peter V. S. Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTotal hip replacement surgery is a well-established intervention that restores joint function and provides excellent outcomes for patients. In some cases, bone resorption caused by stress shielding leads to implant loosening. Stress shielding occurs because implants are much stiffer than bone and transmit a significant proportion of the load, leaving the surrounding bone to carry less load compared to an intact femur.MethodsTo address stress shielding, we aimed to design patient-specific additively manufactured porous femoral stems with reduced stiffness. Diamond lattice structure test specimens of varying porosities were manufactured and tested to measure elastic moduli and yield strengths. These properties were used in subsequent implant optimisation based on finite element analysis. Four implant templates were created based on the region of the implant that was assigned porous material. These templates were referred to as fully porous (FP), proximally porous with a solid distal end (PP), solid distal shell (DS), and fully solid shell (SS). In addition, the elastic modulus within the porous region was assigned either a linear or a radial distribution, resulting in eight possible implant designs.ResultsOptimisation yielded six distinct solutions, which were evaluated based on the reduction in stress shielding and micromotion at the bone implant interface. While all implant designs reduced stress shielding compared to a solid implant, only the PP and SS stems were predicted to pass the standard fatigue test for femoral stems (ISO 7206-4). Implant-bone micromotion was conducive for bone implant integration for all designs, with the potential exception of the fully porous stem. Of the implants that were predicted to pass the fatigue test, the linear proximal porous stem resulted in the largest reduction in stress shielding (9.5% for walking, 8.1% for stair climbing).ConclusionsBased on patient-specific computational models, the porous region of the stems influenced the reduction of stress shielding compared to fully solid stems. Considering implant fatigue failure and bone-implant micromotion, the PP and SS templates were found to be the most suitable design approaches to address femoral bone stress shielding after total hip replacement. Further investigation is required to fully comprehend the implications of porous femoral stems on long-term implant stability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06389-5.

Background

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a common musculoskeletal condition affecting over 500 million people globally [1] and the leading cause of joint replacement surgery [2]. Despite their success, implant longevity is limited, which can necessitate a more complex and costly revision surgery [3]. In Australia, 5.8% of cementless primary total hip replacements (THRs) are revised within 15 years after surgery [2]. A predominant reason for revision is component loosening which affects the femoral component in 20.8% of cases [2].

Non-cemented femoral stems are often made of titanium which has a significantly higher stiffness compared to the surrounding bone (Cancellous bone: 0.02–6 GPa, cortical bone: 3–30 GPa, solid titanium: 100–120 GPa) [4]. After implantation the joint load is partially transmitted through the implant reducing the loading in the bone significantly compared to the pre-implantation state, which is known as stress shielding [5, 6]. Reduced load exposure can lead to bone resorption, the clinically relevant manifestation of stress shielding, contributing to implant loosening [7].

In recent years, fully porous biomaterials have been explored to mitigate the effects of stress shielding. The advancement of additive manufacturing technologies such as electron beam melting (EBM) or selective laser melting (SLM) provide the opportunity to generate custom porous implants with reduced stiffness [8]. Porous structures can be based on repeating cubic cells, also known as unit cells [4]. Lattice structures that consist of interconnected beams (beam-based) can cover a wide range of mechanical properties and be designed to match those of bone tissue [4]. Diamond lattice structures have been studied for the application in medical devices due to their biomimetic properties and have the capacity to enable bone ingrowth and enhance biological fixation of the implant [8].

Porous joint replacement designs, that have been proposed in the literature, can be broadly grouped into three categories of porosity distribution: uniform, graded and optimised. Uniform distributions are characterised by a single unit cell design used throughout the entire porous implant region, while graded and optimised designs have changing porosity parameters [9]. The latter is based on an optimisation algorithm which allows for custom designs based on patient-specific bone material properties [9]. While some studies investigated the effects of different material property distribution strategies, in most cases only one design approach is considered in the analyses [10, 11]. Comparisons of porosity distributions and their effect on applying them in different regions of the implant are currently lacking. To quantify the achieved stress shielding reduction, some studies compare bone stress or strain, or strain energy density between intact and implanted bone [9]. Thus far, octahedral shear strain (OSS) has not been considered in the analysis of porous femoral stems, despite Piccinini et al. showing that OSS performed best as a stimulus for bone remodelling prediction in continuum finite element modelling (FEM) [12].

The aim of this study was to develop an optimisation approach for the design of a custom porous femoral stem using diamond lattice structures. This approach aimed to minimise bone resorption caused by stress shielding considering octahedral shear strain in the femur resulting from daily activities while also considering the fatigue strength of the femoral stem. Stress shielding reduction and micromotion were evaluated for varying porous implant regions and stiffness distributions to evaluate performance and to identify the most suitable design approach for patient-specific femoral stems with improved long-term stability.

Methods

The methods in this study were broadly summarised into three parts: determining material properties of lattice structures, optimising porous femoral stem designs, and evaluating the performance of the resulting optimised porous femoral stems.

Determining material properties of lattice structures

To determine the elastic modulus and yield strength of diamond lattice structures, we additively manufactured and tested 15 Titanium (Ti6Al4V) samples. The design of our samples was constrained by three parameters. Firstly, pore size was limited to a maximum of 800 µm because pore sizes larger than this may inhibit bone tissue growth into the implant [13–15]. Secondly, the porosities of the samples were set to be between 30 and 70% because this range has been approved by the FDA for porous coatings in hip replacements [16]. Finally, the minimum strut diameter was limited to 0.25 mm by the manufacturer. Samples with a unit cell size of 1.2 mm and porosities of 31%, 44%, 56%, and 68% met all requirements and were selected for testing (Fig. 1, Table 1). Each specimen comprised 1000 unit cells (10 unit cells × 10 unit cells × 10 unit cells) in size and was tested in compression in accordance with ISO 13314 using a universal material testing machine (INSTRON Universal Testing Machine 5569A, Norwood, MA, USA) [17].Fig. 1Additively manufactured samples with A 31% B 44% C 56% D 68% porosityTable 1Design parameters of additively manufactured and tested diamond lattice samplesPorosity (%)Strut diameter (mm)Pore size (mm)Number of samples440.480.495560.410.575680.340.645

As specified in the standard, one sample of each porosity (44%, 56% and 68%) was tested without a hysteresis loop to determine the required values for the hysteresis test protocol. The four remaining samples were tested using the hysteresis protocol and mean values were reported. Stress–strain curves calculated from the force–displacement data were plotted and elastic modulus and compressive offset strength (yield strength) were determined as per ISO 13314 [17]. Samples with a porosity of 31% could not be tested to failure due to limited load cell capacity with a maximum axial load of 10,000 N. As per ISO standard specifications, to determine the elastic modulus and compressive offset strength (yield strength) testing to failure was required. To estimate the material properties of the low porosity samples (31% porosity), the elastic modulus and yield strength were extrapolated from the mean values of the remaining three data points retrieved for porosities of 44%, 56% and 68%. The obtained material properties for diamond lattice structures with porosities between 31 and 68% were used in the optimisation to define the elastic modulus range within the porous implant region.

Porous femoral stem optimisation

Finite element models

Three finite element (FE) models were created to perform the optimisation. The first was an FE model of the intact femur to obtain the baseline octahedral shear strain (OSS) in the bone during activities of daily living before implantation. The second model represented a femur implanted with a femoral stem whose material properties were changed during the iterative optimisation process. The third FE model comprised only the femoral stem and simulated the ISO 7206–04 protocol for cyclic testing [18] within each iteration of the optimisation process (Fig. 2). The porous regions of the femoral stem were modelled as continuum material to reduce processing time for the iterative optimisation process. The previously experimentally determined mechanical properties of diamond lattice structures were applied. The implant was meshed using 2 mm tetrahedral elements (C3D4; Hypermesh 2021.2, Altair Engineering Inc., Troy, MI, USA).Fig. 2Finite element model for femoral stem fatigue testing as per ISO 7206-4

Optimisation process

Prior to optimisation, four implant templates were created based on the same implant geometry. The templates differed from each other only in the region of the implant that was assigned porous material in the form of diamond lattice structures (Fig. 3). The implant neck was kept solid in all four templates to maintain the highest possible neck strength. Based on the porous regions, these implants were referred to as fully porous (FP), proximally porous with a solid distal end (PP), a solid distal shell (DS) and finally a fully solid shell (SS) with a proximal opening for bone cell migration (Fig. 3). The DS design was fully porous in the proximal region of the implant and the inner part of the distal end. A shell of 1 mm thickness surrounded the distal porous section. In contrast, the SS design had a porous core and a solid shell throughout the entire length of the implant, except for an opening in the anterior and posterior proximal part. During each iteration of the optimiser, only the elements within the porous region of each template were assigned new material properties. The optimiser aimed to find the distribution of elastic moduli within the porous regions of each of the templates that resulted in minimal stress shielding while preventing failure.Fig. 3Four implant templates based on porous region: A FP B PP, C DS, D SS

From both a clinical and regulatory perspective, survival under fatigue testing (ISO 7206-4) needed to take precedence over minimising stress shielding. An implant that reduces stress shielding but fails early in service is not viable. To prioritise implant fatigue strength over stress shielding reduction, the penalty component of the cost function was set to be significantly higher than the other components of the cost function, even when only a single element was predicted to fail during the fatigue test. The penalty component was 100 for a single failed element, whereas the rest of the cost function was typically less than 1 or occasionally single-digit number. This hierarchical approach to setting up the cost function caused the optimiser to prioritise implant survival over stress shielding.

The cost function was optimised using an in-built MATLAB optimisation algorithm, namely ‘patternsearch’ (MATLAB, MathWorks, Inc., Natick (MA), USA). The lower and upper bound were set for the elastic modulus based on the experimentally determined mechanical properties of diamond lattice structures as described above. Performance evaluation

The performance of each of the optimised implants was evaluated using two measures. The first was based on the changes in stress shielding between a solid implant and each of the optimised porous implants. The second was based on micromotion at the implant-bone interface for each of the different implant designs.

The changes in stress shielding were quantified by comparing octahedral shear strains in bone elements between the intact femur, the femur with a solid implant, and the femurs with each of the optimised porous implants. Given the design constraints mentioned earlier, the minimum and maximum achievable elastic modulus for diamond lattice structures were 3110 MPa and 7970 MPa, respectively (Fig. 7A). This linear relationship was used to calculate the yield strength of each element within the porous region of the implant in the iterative optimisation process. Micromotion

Micromotion at the bone-implant interface decreased with increasing coefficient of friction assigned to the surface-interaction in the finite element model (Fig. 10). At the lowest COF of 0.1, the peak micromotion near the neck region of a fully porous implant exceeded the upper threshold (150 µm) for potential bone ingrowth. The predicted mean micromotion for all implant designs was optimal for bone ingrowth (< 50 µm) irrespective of the coefficient of friction assigned to the bone-implant interface. Discussion

This work investigated the potential of patient-specific porous femoral stems to reduce bone stress shielding caused by total hip replacements. In contrast to previous studies where one specific porous design or a specified porous implant region with varying distribution strategies was presented [9], the current study investigated four assigned porous implant regions and two stiffness distribution strategies. There is no consensus in the literature for quantifying stress shielding. While some studies have used stress-based criteria, others have used strain-based criteria [9]. The current study is unique in its use of an octahedral shear strain (OSS) based quantification of stress shielding. OSS was selected because it has been shown to be a suitable mechanical stimulus for predicting bone remodelling in finite element models that treat bone as a continuum [12]. Our results demonstrate that it is possible to design a porous femoral stem that is capable of reducing stress shielding, is likely to pass the ISO 7206-4 fatigue test and to promote bone ingrowth.

Femoral stems were optimised to reduce the difference in OSS between intact and implanted bone by altering the implant’s stiffness within a defined porous region. Two solutions with uniform stiffness distributions and four solutions with linear and radial stiffness distributions were obtained. The uniformly porous designs included the fully porous (FP) solution with the lowest porosity and the solid shell (SS) designs with the highest porosity. While the latter fulfilled the strength requirement based on the Soderberg failure criterion, the former was found to experience fatigue failure. Linear and radial stiffness distributions in the proximally porous (PP) and distal shell (DS) designs tended towards higher stiffness in the distal end (Fig. 8). Additionally, radial distributions resulted in greater variation of included elastic moduli (Table 4). By using four different defined porous implant regions and two stiffness distribution equations, six distinct solutions were obtained.

Differences in bone strain between the implanted and the intact femur (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta OSS$$\end{document}) for porous designs were compared to those of a conventional solid design. In general, a larger porous region led to a greater reduction in stress shielding. The FP design resulted in the greatest reduction in stress shielding (Table 5). Despite the internal structure of the SS design being fully porous, the reduction in stress shielding was less than 4% compared to the solid stem (Table 5). Despite the larger porous region in the DS design compared to the PP design, both exhibited similar performance in terms of stress shielding (Table 5, Fig. 9). The type of distribution, linear or radial, only resulted in minor differences in stress shielding. The SS and PP designs were the only ones that did not fail.

In addition to the reduction in stress shielding, introducing porosity in implants may improve osseointegration. Osseointegration requires the micromotions between bone and implant surfaces to be below the critical threshold for bone tissue formation. While small micromotions (< 50 µm) are favourable for bone on/ingrowth, excessive micromotions (> 150 µm) will inhibit osseointegration due to the formation of fibrous tissue [31]. The magnitude of maximum and mean micromotions varied depending on the applied coefficient of friction (COF), as well as the porous implant design. The calculated micromotions were highest for the FP design and lowest for the SS design. These differences were more prevalent for a lower COF. With increasing COF, the differences in predicted micromotion between the designs decreased. The radial distal shell (RDS) design was found to have higher peak micromotion compared to the linear distal shell (LDS), linear proximal porous (LPP) and radial proximal porous (RPP) designs. The higher peak micromotions in the RDS design may arise from the lower porosity in the areas of the implant that are in direct contact with the bone. The areas that experience higher micromotion corresponded to the Gruen zones 7 and 6 that have been found to be more prone to bone resorption [6, 32]. The proximal part of the bone appears to be at higher risk of excessive micromotion, but only in the fully porous design does micromotion exceed the critical threshold of 150 µm. We advise a cautious approach to interpreting these results, given that the COF at the bone-implant interface is not accurately known and is likely to vary across the interface and change over time [33].

In recent years, a variety of porous orthopaedic implant designs have been proposed to reduce stress shielding, including optimised porosity distributions [9]. Out of these studies, not all considered fatigue strength in their porous femoral stem designs. Among the studies that considered fatigue in their designs, implant porosity was optimised to achieve different objectives. In one study, researchers aimed to achieve minimal stiffness with an acceptable safety factor [34]. Others aimed to achieve maximum implant compliance [35], or maximum bone stress [36]. Other studies aimed for minimal bone loss based on strain energy density [32, 37]. Quantification of bone resorption is a common metric to evaluate implant performance [9]. One study calculated bone remodelling based on strain energy density in the bone and reported up to 75% reduction of bone loss based in comparison to a solid implant [32]. In contrast, this study showed a reduction of stress shielding of only 3 – 13% compared to the solid implant was achieved. However, bone loss may be challenging to reliably predict due to the lack of validation of bone remodelling algorithms for human bones [33]. Therefore, in the current study, a relative comparison between bone strains was preferred over the minimisation of total bone loss under the assumption that pre-implantation bone strains are the most favourable for maintaining bone density. Besides bone loss, some studies consider interface failure caused by micromotion at the implant-bone interface in their optimisation algorithm [32, 35, 37]. In contrast, in the current study, the performance of various designs was evaluated based on the achieved stress shielding reduction, as well as potential micromotions with varying COF. The decreasing micromotions observed with increasing COF may indicate reduced bone-implant micromotions under the assumption that bone tissue formation post-implantation gradually increases friction.

Despite the valuable insights offered by this study, there are certain limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. This investigation was conducted for a single implant type and geometry, likely impacting the resulting reduction in bone stress shielding. As shown in a previous study, the type of stem in combination with reduced implant stiffness, impacts the strain transfer between implant and bone [38]. In addition, the geometry and material properties of a single femur specimen were considered in this study. To address this, a future study aims to incorporate a larger sample size to investigate the potential reductions for a representative patient cohort. Additionally, there were limitations in the bone-implant FE approach to allow for manageable computation time for the iterative optimisation process. This includes the applied forces and boundary conditions which were based on previous studies [23, 24, 39], not considering patient-specific factors such as body weight. The elements used in the finite element model may lead to lower accuracy of stress and strain distribution. A 4-node linear tetrahedron element (C3D4) mesh with an edge length of 2 mm was applied for femur bone and implant. Other studies have used smaller edge lengths of 1 mm and 10-node tetrahedron elements (C3D10) [34, 40], while in another study, the authors suggested 2 mm edge length to represent cancellous bone, 4 mm to represent cortical bone and 0.8 mm to represent the femoral stem implant [41]. However, the analysis focused on comparative outcomes between intact and implanted bone rather than absolute values. Therefore, despite the models not capturing the full complexity of the bone-implant interactions, the assumptions made remained consistent between models and meaningful comparisons could be extracted.

Another limitation is how the porous implants were represented in the FE models. Due to the computational load when modelling porous structures, the porous structures were modelled as continuum materials in the current study. This approach fails to quantify stress concentrations, particularly in the struts of the lattice structures, leading to a potential underestimation of maximum stress. Despite the use of the Soderberg fatigue failure criterion in the optimisation, which is considered the most conservative compared to other criteria such as Goodman or Gerber, the representation of the porous material as continuum material may lead to an overestimation of the safety factor. In addition to this, the mechanical properties used in the optimisation were based on a limited number of samples and inter-/extrapolated values from the linear sample data. Further experimental studies are required to increase the available data on the mechanical properties of diamond lattice structures and to evaluate whether the continuum material model allows for accurate representation of the implant behaviour after additive manufacturing. Future work should also explore more accurate representations of lattice structures without extensively increasing computation time. Considering the desired osseointegration of porous bone interfacing implants, other relevant parameters of porous materials should be considered in future designs. For instance, tortuosity impacts the permeability and therefore bone cell migration into porous scaffold structures [42]. Given that adjusting porosity parameters, such as tortuosity, results in geometrical changes, the stiffness of the porous material will inevitably change. Therefore, future work may explore the incorporation of tortuosity into the presented design framework.

The optimisation approach described in the current study only considered the changes in OSS at a point in time immediately after implantation and therefore does not consider any bone remodelling that may take place subsequently. Time-dependent modelling could be used to understand the long-term changes in bone density and its effect on bone-implant interactions. Future studies could also consider the effects of using low stiffness material alloys, improved post-manufacturing treatment methods, and porous structures that exhibit more favourable strength-to-stiffness ratios, factors that may further reduce stress shielding, improve bone-implant integration, and extend implant longevity.

Conclusion

In this study, custom porous femoral stems were designed based on eight configurations, aiming to minimise stress shielding while considering implant fatigue strength. Six unique design solutions were reached. Finite element modelling predicted that all six designs would reduce stress shielding by 3–13% when compared to a solid implant, but that only three of the designs have the potential to pass the ISO 7206-4 fatigue test. These three designs comprised the two proximally porous designs and the solid shell design. All three of these designs also exhibited micromotion at the bone implant interface that was conducive to bone ingrowth. Therefore, considering stress shielding, implant fatigue strength and bone ingrowth, the proximal porous and solid shell designs were the most promising. However, further experimental studies are needed to evaluate whether 3D printed full-scale implants behave as predicted in the current study.
