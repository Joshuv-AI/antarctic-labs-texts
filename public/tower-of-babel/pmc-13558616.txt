Anti-obesity mechanisms of adzuki bean saponin: a multi-omics and experimental study
Authors: Jinhai Luo, Jincan Luo, Yingzi Wu, Baoning Liu, Nannan Wang, Bincheng Han, Zhi-Ling Yu, Baojun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adzuki bean saponin (ABS) has shown therapeutic potential in improving obesity, but its underlying mechanism and active saponin components are still unknown. This research estimated the anti-obesity effect of ABS in obesity using a mouse model. Advanced research methods such as spatial metabolomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, serum pharmacology, and network pharmacology were employed to study the molecular mechanisms of ABS and its bioacitve saponins. ABS achieved anti-obesity effects by improving HFD-induced body weight gain, abnormal lipid profiles and liver dysfunction liver lipid accumulation, and increased adipose tissue. Liver spatial metabolomics suggested a potential involvement of ABS in modulating lipid metabolism via alterations in the arachidonic acid pathway. The results of transcriptomics, proteomics, and immunohistochemistry indicated that ABS could achieve anti-obesity effects through activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Based on our previous study and serum pharmacology analysis revealed that soyasaponin Ba is a key saponin in ABS that achieves anti-obesity effects. Similarly, soyasaponin Ba can achieve anti-obesity effects by activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling. ABS and its active saponin Soyasaponin Ba can achieve anti-obesity effect by up-regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. This understanding of this mechanism provides valuable insights for developing drugs or health supplements based on adzuki beans to combat obesity.

Introduction

Obesity is characterized by excessive lipid accumulation and is a complex chronic disease with a close relationship with metabolic disorders like insulin resistance and low-grade inflammation, etc. For the past decades, the global prevalence of obesity has significantly ascended to a certain extent. Epidemiological surveillance data from the World Health Organization (WHO) implies that the global population with excessive body weight has surpassed 1.9 billion individuals, encompassing both the overweight and the obese ones1. Nowadays, obesity has become a major health problem worldwide for its negative effects on the quality of life, and the acceleration on fast increases about the incidence rate of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, stroke, cancer, depression, and other chronic and even life-long diseases2. Although weight loss surgery and medication intervention have significant effects, most obese individuals are without suitably physical conditions for surgery, nevertheless medication intervention also has obvious side effects3. Accordingly, it is urgent and necessary to find an alternative treatment that can improve obesity.

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway was first discovered in 1982 through the identification of the mouse Wnt1 gene4. The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway controls multiple developmental processes during early life stages and the homeostasis of mature tissues by regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and genetic stability5. The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is also associated with various human diseases, including cancer, osteoporosis, neurodegenerative diseases, diabetes, and obesity5,6. In regulating obesity, the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway can alleviate obesity by increasing the expression of β-catenin and inhibiting its downstream adipogenic factors, including CCAAT/Enhancer-Binding Proteins α (C/EBPα) and Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor γ (PPARγ)7. Adipogenic factors C/EBPα and PPARγ regulate the process of preadipocyte differentiation into adipocytes8,9. Specifically, PPARγ is a key nuclear transcription factor that regulates various cellular functions in adipocytes, including adipogenesis, fatty acid transport, and energy metabolism8. Furthermore, C/EBPα has been identified as a transcription factor regulating cell differentiation. It is expressed during the initiation phase of adipogenesis and, following the growth arrest phase, collaborates with PPAR-γ to activarte the expression of adipocyte-specific genes10.

Natural products have long been a source used by scientists to screen and develop related anti-obesity products or drugs due to their safety and stability in use11. In addition, natural products can achieve anti-obesity effects by up-regulation on the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and regulating its downstream transcription factors involved in adipogenesis, such as C/EBPα and PPARγ11. Adzuki bean (Vigna angularis) is a kind of food and medicine homology material, which has different health promoting effects including antioxidant effect, antiallergy effect, anti-diabetes effect, anti-obesity effect etc12. Regarding the anti-obesity potential of adzuki bean, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that adzuki bean water extract and protein hydrolysates can exert anti-obesity effects by modulating gut microbiota composition13,14. Additionally, Zhang et al. reported that adzuki bean flavonoids could alleviate high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obesity in a mouse model15. Building upon these findings, our group conducted preliminary investigations utilizing an HFD-induced obese mouse model to evaluate the anti-obesity effects of adzuki bean saponin (ABS) and flavonoids, demonstrating that ABS exhibited superior bioactivity16. The anti-obesity activity of ABS was subsequently preliminarily analyzed through molecular simulation approaches17. More specifically, our previous in silico and in vitro studies have preliminarily indicated that ABS and its bioactive saponin soyasaponin Ba could improve liver lipid accumulation and adipogenesis potentially by upregulating β-catenin and its downstream transcription factors18,19. However, the in vivo mechanisms by which ABS alleviate HFD-induced obesity remain unclear at the metabolome, transcriptome, and proteome levels.

Currently, multi-omics and network pharmacology are the most advanced and comprehensive research methods, usually combined with different cell or animal models to verify their findings20,21. In this study, we employed a strategically integrated methodology where each technique addresses distinct yet complementary aspects of mechanism elucidation: spatial metabolomics was utilized to visualize and quantify the tissue-specific distribution and local perturbations of metabolites (particularly arachidonic acid derivatives) within liver tissue, providing spatially resolved phenotypic evidence; transcriptomics and proteomics were applied to capture genome-wide gene expression profiles and corresponding protein level alterations, respectively, thereby identifying key regulatory nodes and pathway activations at the molecular level; and network pharmacology was utilized to study the molecular mechanism of active saponin from ABS in another perspective based on public databases. This multi-layered strategy enables convergence from metabolic phenotypes through transcriptional regulation to functional protein execution, thereby elucidating the systemic mechanism of ABS and its critical bioactive saponin, Soyasaponin Ba, in improving obesity. In summary, the purpose of this research is to study the role and molecular mechanism of ABS and its bioactive saponin, soyasaponin Ba, in improving obesity, providing a promising therapy for the treatment of human obesity.

Results

ABS alleviates HFD-induced obesity in obese mice

To evaluate the in vivo anti-obesity capability of ABS, we designed an animal experiment (Supplementary Fig. 2). This animal experiment comprised five experimental groups: a negative control group (NC), a model group (HFD), a positive drug group (HFD + 20 mg/kg Orlistat), a low-dose group (HFD + 50 mg/kg ABS), and a high-dose group (HFD + 200 mg/kg ABS). From the end of the acclimation period in the animal environment, the body weight change of the mice was recorded (Fig. 1A). After the acclimation period, the animals were grouped, and the body weight at starting point of the mice was recorded; there were no significant differences (Fig. 1B). The results indicated that after being fed with high-fat diet, the body weight of mice significantly increased with the increase in weeks, indicating in vivo modeling is successful. Lipid profiles were analyzed to study obesity-induced dyslipidemia (Fig. 1D–G). The results indicated that LDL-C, TC, and TG levels significantly increased after high-fat diet treatment, but this phenotype was also improved by ABS treatment. Moreover, the weights of epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) and liver were also recorded (Fig. 1H, I), and the results also indicated that ABS treatment can reduce lipid accumulation in the liver and adipogenesis in adipose tissue. Typically, liver accumulation in an obese state can lead to abnormalities in liver function. Therefore, two liver function indicators, ALT and AST, were tested, and the results indicated that ABS treatment improved liver dysfunction caused by liver lipid accumulation (Fig. 1J, K). To further investigate the pathological changes in tissues, we performed H&E staining on the liver, subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), EAT, and brown adipose tissue (BAT), which indicated that ABS treatment significantly alleviates liver lipid accumulation and adipocytegenesis (Fig. 1L). The improvement effect of ABS against liver lipid accumulation was further verified through Oil Red O staining (Fig. 1M).

Spatial metabolomics analysis of mice liver

Spatial metabolomics was performed to visualize metabolic perturbations and differences among groups of negative control group, model group, and ABS high-dose group. In negative ion mode, a total of 603 metabolites were detected. Differential metabolites were screened via apply the criteria of p < 0.05, VIP > 1, and FC > 2.0 or <0.5 (based on control vs. model groups), which yielded 157 significantly altered metabolites, as shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, VIP values were incorporating data from control, model, and ABS treatment groups, which identified 47 metabolites with VIP > 1 that exhibited consistent changes among three groups. All 47 metabolites were clearly distinguished among the three groups in principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 2B) and displayed in the clustering heatmap (Fig. 2C), all of which are related to oxylipin metabolism are marked with asterisks (★). For positive mode, in total, 1418 metabolites were detected, with 428 meeting the significance criteria (p < 0.05, VIP > 1, FC > 2.0 or <0.5) as shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 2E). Among them, 323 were significantly upregulated and 105 downregulated. After removing metabolites with VIP ≤1 (calculated across three groups) and unidentified species, only 55 differential metabolites were left and retained for next analysis (Fig. 2G, with oxylipin-related metabolites marked by asterisks). PCA confirmed that these 55 metabolites effectively distinguished among the control, model, and treatment groups (Fig. 2F). Enrichment analysis of them also highlighted ALA oxylipin metabolism as in the negative mode and arachidonic acid oxylipin metabolism as pathways potentially relevant to HFD (Fig. 2H).

Interacting analysis of the top 25 metabolic pathways in both modes revealed that the identified metabolic pathways are highly overlapping, and 19 pathways were identical, as shown in Fig. 2I. Arachidonic acid oxylipin metabolism was found to be important in the analysis of positive and negative modes data and warrants attention.

To further confirm the analysis results, PLS-DA was used to identify differential metabolites that were representative and effectively separated among the three experimental groups (Supplementary Fig. 3A, E). Hierarchical clustering analysis next confirmed the distinct clustering of control, model, and treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. 3B, F). Pathway analysis consistently stands for the arachidonic acid metabolism as a critical node for both HFD modeling and ABS treatment alleviation (Supplementary Fig. 3C, G). The network views of enrichment analysis also illustrated inter-relationships among the affected pathways (Supplementary Fig. 3D, H).

After removal of interfering ions, representative differential metabolites presented via ion imaging were presented according to the imaging quality, background noise levels, and inter-group differences. The screening criteria for DEGs were FDR < 0.05 and |logFC| > 1. Finally, a total of 2007 DEGs were found, including 1480 up-regulated genes and 527 down-regulated genes. The results suggest that the Wnt signaling pathway is the main pathway through which ABS regulates lipid metabolism (Fig. 4G). Therefore, our subsequent experimental verification mainly examines the major protein changes in the Wnt signaling pathway.

To further explore the association pattern of the Wnt signaling pathway between the liver and adipose tissue, we constructed a cross-omics correlation heatmap (Supplementary Fig. 5A) based on the hepatic transcriptomic and adipose tissue proteomic data. The results revealed that Wnt components between the two tissues exhibited distinct co-expression modules and antagonistic regulatory patterns. Specifically, the hepatic differentially expressed Wnt genes, including WNT2, LRP6, and LRP5, showed significant positive correlations with the core differentially expressed proteins NLK, APC, and GSK3B in adipose tissue, respectively (p < 0.05). Meanwhile, several molecules displayed obvious negative correlations: for instance, the hepatic genes MYC, LRP6, and LRP5 were negatively correlated with the adipose proteins CTNNB1, CREBBP, and BCL9, respectively. To identify the common factors driving cross-tissue variation between the hepatic transcriptome and adipose proteome, the highly significant correlation between hepatic Wnt5a and adipose β-catenin (R = 0.71, p = 0.032) in this study provides important biological insights (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Then, using MOFA2 to integrate the paired samples, this study successfully identified a key latent variable, termed the “Wnt-metabolic liver-adipose axis” factor (Supplementary Fig. 5C). Analysis demonstrated that the factor score was significantly higher in the Treat group than in the Control group, uncovering a highly synchronized pathophysiological shift between the two tissues under disease induction. GSEA further revealed its “signal activation and metabolic suppression” signature: canonical Wnt (NES = 2.68) and TGF-beta (NES = 1.94) signaling were significantly enhanced, whereas fatty acid metabolism (NES = −2.15) and the PPAR pathway (NES = −1.88) showed strong negative associations. Subsequently, a cross-omics synergistic network was constructed using the circlize framework (Supplementary Fig. 5D). The analysis showed significant positive correlation coupling (highlighted in gold) between hepatogenic Wnt ligands (e.g., WNT5A, WNT3A) and core effector factors, as well as metabolic regulatory proteins (e.g., CTNNB1, TCF7L2) in adipose tissue.

ABS achieves anti-obesity effect in obese mice through regulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

The analysis of the liver transcriptome and white adipose proteome in the previous section has revealed that the Wnt/β-catenin is crucial for ABS to improve obesity induced by HFD. We conducted immunohistochemical analysis on the liver, EAT, and SAT. Immunohistochemical results indicated that ABS treatment could significantly alleviate the inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway caused by HFD treatment through upregulating the protein levels of Wnt10b and β-catenin in EAT and SAT (Fig. 6A–D and Fig. 6G–J). In addition, ABS also upregulated the expression of β-catenin in the liver to improve lipid metabolism, which is consistent with our previous results from in vitro studies on fat accumulation in liver cells18. Furthermore, we also conducted further immunohistochemical analysis experiments on EAT and SAT to investigate the effects of ABS on the transcription factors (adipogenic factors) in the Wnt/β-catenin, including PPARγ and C/EBPα. Immunohistochemical results indicated that ABS can further inhibit its downstream transcription factors, PPARγ and C/EBPα, in EAT and SAT after activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway (Fig. 7A–H), which is a signaling pathway that controls adipogensis and is also a key target signaling pathway for phytochemicals in improving the obesity11. Soyasaponin Ba inhibits adipogenesis and oxidative stress

After identifying the bioactive saponin soyasaponin Ba in ABS, we further employed a classical preadipocyte differentiation model to investigate its anti-obesity bioactivity. We first investigated the cytotoxic effect of soyasaponin Ba on the cell viability loss of 3T3-L1 cells (Fig. 8A) and found that the maximum concentration used for subsequent studies was 64 µM, as soyasaponin Ba exhibited no significant inhibition on cell viability till the dosage reached 128 µM. To investigate the intracellular lipid content, we conducted Nile Red fluorescence staining to detect the intracellular lipid content and used EGCG (epigallocatechin-3-gallate) as a positive control (Fig. 8B). The dosage of EGCG was set at 16 µM based on our previous studies18,23, and this dosage was not cytotoxic to 3T3-L1 cells. The Nile Red fluorescence staining results indicated that soyasaponin Ba could significantly and dose-dependently inhibit preadipocyte differentiation and reduce intracellular lipid content (Fig. 8C). To further verify the biological activity of soyasaponin Ba in inhibiting preadipocyte differentiation and reducing intracellular fat content, we conducted Oil Red O staining, and the results also supported this finding (Fig. 8D). Typically, preadipocyte differentiation promotes mitochondrial biosynthesis and generates a large amount of reactive oxygen species (ROS), cause of intracellular oxidative stress24. To study the improvement effect of combating oxidative stress induced by preadipocyte differentiation by soyasaponin, we performed PK Mito and Carboxy-H2DCFDA fluorescence staining, and the results showed that treatment with soysaponin Ba significantly and dose-dependently reduced mitochondrial biosynthesis and ROS production, thereby improving oxidative stress (Fig. 8E, F). Discussion

This study extended our previous in vitro findings of ABS18, demonstrating that ABS and its active component, soyasaponin Ba, exert anti-obesity effects in vivo, evidenced by amelioration of HFD-induced body weight gain, hepatic lipid accumulation, and adipose tissue expansion, alongside improved dyslipidemia and liver dysfunction. This study also employed multi-omics approaches from multiple perspectives to discover the anti-obesity mechanism of ABS and its bioactive saponin, soyasaponin Ba, including spatial metabolomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics.

Spatial metabolomic analysis of mouse liver revealed that saponin treatment restored arachidonic acid metabolic pathways that were downregulated by high-fat diet feeding. More specifically, after being fed on a high-fat diet, prostaglandin D2, delta-12-prostaglandin J2, prostaglandin F3a, and prostaglandin I2 from the COX pathway were greatly downregulated, while they were significantly upregulated after mice were treated with ABS. 12-Leukotriene B4, 12-HETE, 5-HETE, 5-HEPE, 12-HPETE from LOX pathway and 5,6-DHET from CYP450 pathway also possessed similar change. Eicosapentaenoic acid was not significantly changed and was greatly deregulated after mice were treated with ABS. These results revealed that the COX pathway, the LOX pathway, and the CYP450 epioxidative metabolic pathway of arachidonic acid metabolism were downregulated when mice were fed on a high-fat diet, while upregulated after being treated with ABS, implying that saponin could improve obesity in mice via the arachidonic acid metabolic pathway. This finding aligns with the conclusions of Zhang et al. regarding obesity-resistant individuals. Their study found significantly reduced levels of various arachidonic acid metabolites (including products from the COX, LOX, and CYP450 pathways) in the serum of obesity-resistant mice and discovered arachidonic acid metabolism as a candidate pathway with a critical role distinguishing obesity-prone from obesity-resistant phenotypes25. Moreover, the upregulation of the arachidonic acid metabolic pathway can activate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway through the upregulation of the Ascl2 gene by arachidonic acid26,27.

To study the bioprocesses and signaling pathways involved in the regulation of lipid metabolism by ABS, we performed RNA sequencing in mice liver. The results suggest that the Wnt signaling pathway is highly enriched, indicating that it is likely to be the main regulatory pathway for ABS to inhibit fat accumulation. Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway can prevent adipogenesis and differentiation of preadipocytes by blocking the induction of C/EBPα and PPARγ signals, to achieve the effect of anti-obesity. Recently, many studies have suggested that saponins from different sources can regulate lipid metabolism and inhibit lipid accumulation through the Wnt signaling pathway. Ginsenoside Rb1 concentration-dependently reduces the formation of lipid droplets. And activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling can prevent browning11. A novel saponin, isolated from the tetraploid jiaogulan, might prevent adipogenesis via activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling in the progression of preadipocyte differentiation28. A natural substance called shikonin was extracted from Lithospermum erythrorhizon Sieb. Et Zucc prevents fat accumulation and adipogenesis via altering the Wnt/β-catenin pathway29. These research papers are similar to our study, further indicating that ABS is likely to inhibit lipid accumulation through the Wnt signaling pathway.

Excessive accumulation of white adipose tissue (WAT) has been well studied and regarded as a hallmark of obesity. We conducted proteomic analysis on the white adipose tissue of mice in the modeling and administration groups, respectively. Totally, 499 differentially expressed proteins were obtained. The identification of these differentially expressed proteins provides an important basis for our in-depth understanding of the regulatory mechanism of ABS on white adipose tissue, facilitated by a high-fat diet in obese mice. COG analysis showed that differentially expressed proteins were significantly enriched in functional categories such as lipid transport and metabolism. GO functional enrichment analysis also revealed multiple biological processes related to lipid metabolism, such as “cellular lipid metabolism process” and “lipid metabolism process”, which further support the importance of lipid metabolism in this study. Specifically, the differentially expressed proteins we identified include multiple enzymes and transporters related to lipid metabolism. The expression changes of Cd44, Akt2, and Gpx4 may participate in regulating the lipid metabolism balance of adipose tissue, thereby affecting the size and function of adipose tissue. Cd44 can participate in lipid accumulation by regulating mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)30; The decrease in ROS levels in mitochondria can reduce Akt2 protein phosphorylation facilitated by a high-fat diet in mice tissues, thereby reducing visceral fat precursor proliferation31; Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPx4) acts as both a structural protein and antioxidant enzyme, with an efficient prevention effect against lipid oxidation32. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that the Wnt signaling pathway is one of the pathways significantly enriched in differentially expressed proteins. Our research suggests that ABS may affect the physiological function of adipose tissue by modulating the Wnt signaling pathway. We also discovered multiple differentially expressed proteins associated with the Wnt signaling pathway. For example, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway was inactivated by LYN knockdown, including decreased levels of Wnt3a, β-catenin, snail family transcriptional repressor (Snail)1, and Snail233. Cd44 can act as a regulatory factor in the Wnt induced β-catenin signaling pathway, and overexpression of Cd44 subtypes increases Wnt/β-catenin signaling transduction34. There is a physical interaction between β-catenin and mTOR, and β-catenin, which is stable through Wnt signaling, plays a central role in lipid metabolism in steatotic liver. It upregulates fatty acid synthesis through Akt/mTOR signaling35.

Combining the transcriptomic analysis of liver and proteomic analysis of EAT by cross-omics correlation analysis (Supplementary Fig. S5), Wnt/β-catenin signaling is crucial for ABS combating against obesity progression. In the liver, β-catenin is a key protein with improvement capability on lipid metabolism and decreases liver lipid formation36. More specifically, β-catenin significantly affects multiple aspects of liver lipid metabolism, including fatty acid oxidation, ketogenesis, and lipogenesis37. EAT is a type of WAT whose primary function is to store lipids and release fatty acids when energy is needed. Wnt/β-catenin signaling can inhibit adipogenesis by upregulating β-catenin, thereby suppressing the expression of its downstream transcription factors PPARγ and C/EBPα, which are the primary regulators of adipogenesis38. In this in vivo study, the results also showed that treatment with ABS is capable of effectively alleviating the prevention of β-catenin expression in the liver by HFD. The above findings are consistent with our previous study on the improvement of liver lipid accumulation by ABS in vitro18. Moreover, Wnt/β-catenin signaling is an important pathway regulating adipogenesis in adipose tissue, and inhibiting this pathway can lead to the expression of downstream transcription factors PPARγ and C/EBPα39. PPARγ and C/EBPα, also known as adipogenic factors, their expression promotes preadipocyte differentiation and adipogenesis40. In our previous in vitro study, we found that ABS can promote the protein expression level of β-catenin and prevent the related downstream transcription factors PPARγ and C/EBPα in adipocytes to reduce adipogenesis18. In this in vivo study, we further found that ABS reduces adipose tissue in vivo by reversing the inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin signaling by HFD and preventing the related downstream transcription factors PPARγ and C/EBPα.

This study conducted serum pharmacology analysis to further explore the active saponins in ABS that achieve anti-obesity effects. The results showed that 3 out of 15 legium saponins in ABS can enter the serum to exert biological activity, including syasaponin Ba, azukisaponin III, and AZ IV. Their two legium saponin metabolites, syasapogenol A and syasapogenol B, were also found in the serum22. Based on our previous network pharmacology studies, soyasaponin Ba has the most anti-obesity potential among these three saponins18. Moreover, another study we previously conducted has explored the biological activity and potential molecular mechanism of soyasaponin Ba in improving liver lipid accumulation through in vitro and in vivo models combined with multi-omics analysis19. Therefore, in this study, soyasaponin Ba was identified as a key bioactive saponin in ABS and further investigated its anti-obesity effect and underlying molecular mechanisms in an adipocyte model. In the preadipocyte differentiation model, soyasaponin Ba can inhibit preadipocyte differentiation, intracellular lipid content, and oxidative stress caused by preadipocyte differentiation.

Through a series of bioinformatics analyses, we have identified four characteristic genes, including ITIH5, KIAA1522, COL12A1, and ARHGEF16, that may have potential implications for the diagnosis of obesity. Recent research discovered that the human body expresses (ITIH5) extensively, with adipose tissue showing the highest levels of this protein. Diet-induced weight loss reduces ITIH5 expression, which is elevated in obese individuals relative to lean individuals41. Liver is an important site for lipid metabolism. Research found that the protein expression amount of KIAA1522 was elevated in HCC cell lines and tissues. KIAA1522 also activates the expression of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway42. However, there is still a lack of research on the exact relationship between KIAA1522 and obesity, which may become a topic for future exploration by scientists. A study on identifying miRNAs and their target genes participating in the progression of obesity suggests that hsa-miR-425 and hsa-miR-126 have many target genes, and the interaction between hsa-miR-16/COL12A1 and hsa-miR-634/SLC4A4 significantly co-expresses, indicating that they may be involved in the pathogenesis of obesity as important player43. Oxidative stress is strongly correlated with lipid metabolism. It is reported that ARHGEF16 is closely relevant to oxidative stress44. But it is yet not well discovered about the exact relationship between this gene and obesity, which deserves our attention in the future.

Both bioinformatics, transcriptome, and network pharmacology analyses suggest that the Wnt signaling pathway is likely the main regulatory pathway for monomeric inhibition of lipid accumulation. Previous studies have shown that saponins from different plants, such as ginsenoside Rb145, and saponin from tetraploid jiaogulan Lithospermum erythrorhizon, can inhibit lipid accumulation by activating important proteins in the Wnt signaling pathway46. Therefore, our subsequent validation experiments of soyasaponin Ba were focused on the Wnt signaling pathway, and results indicated that soyasaponin Ba can inhibit preadipocyte differentiation and oxidative stress by activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling and preventing the related downstream transcription factors PPARγ and C/EBPα. However, these experiments demonstrate correlative associations rather than direct causality. Establishing a definitive causal link between soyasaponin Ba and Wnt/β-catenin activation requires further validation using siRNA-mediated knockdown or specific pathway inhibitors, which represents a limitation of the current study. Moreover, although the present study focused on the lipid-lowering effects of ABS mediated primarily through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, we acknowledge two important limitations regarding our mechanistic evaluation. First, the role of PPARα—a master transcription factor governing hepatic fatty acid β-oxidation—was not assessed, precluding a comprehensive understanding of whether PPARα-dependent mechanisms contribute to the observed reduction in hepatic steatosis. Second, dynamic assessments of glucose homeostasis, including fasting blood glucose, glucose tolerance tests, and insulin tolerance tests, were not incorporated, limiting our evaluation of systemic insulin sensitivity and the full metabolic impact of ABS. Future investigations addressing these metabolic parameters would provide a more complete characterization of ABS’s anti-obesity effects beyond its established lipid-modulating properties.

This study demonstrates that adzuki bean saponin (ABS) exerts comprehensive anti-obesity effects by ameliorating high-fat diet-induced obesity, including bodyweight increase, dyslipidemia, liver lipid accumulation, and adipose tissue expansion. Integrative multi-omics analyses reveal that these anti-obesity effects are mediated through two complementary mechanisms: (i) modulation of hepatic arachidonic acid metabolism to restore lipid homeostasis, and (ii) tissue-specific activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling to suppress adipogenic differentiation in adipose tissue and lipogenesis in the liver. Serum pharmacology further identified soyasaponin Ba as the critical bioactive constituent responsible for the anti-obesity effect of ABS. Mechanistically, soyasaponin Ba activates Wnt/β-catenin signaling and downregulates its downstream adipogenic factors C/EBPα and PPARγ to inhibit preadipocyte differentiation and attenuate oxidative stress. These findings establish a mechanistic framework wherein ABS, primarily through soyasaponin Ba, suppresses adipogenic differentiation and attenuates lipogenesis via activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Collectively, this work advances our understanding of adzuki bean-derived saponins as metabolic modulators, providing mechanistic insights for the rational development of nutraceutical or pharmaceutical interventions targeting obesity.

Methods

Chemicals and reagents

The materials and reagents used in this study included animal and cell studies. All the detailed and specific information, including catalog numbers and manufacturers of these materials and reagents used in this study are listed in supplementary Table S1. Additionally, the adzuki bean saponin used in the study was prepared and purified based on our previously established research methods18. More specifically, adzuki beans are crushed into powder, extracted with ethanol, defatted with anhydrous petroleum ether, extracted with water-saturated n-butanol, and finally purified with AB-8 macroporous resin and freeze-dried to obtain the adzuki bean saponin powder. Our previous study has identified 15 saponins, including azukisaponin I, azukisaponin II, azukisaponin III, azukisaponin IV, azukisaponin V, AZ I, AZ II, AZ III, AZ IV, soyasaponin I, dehydrosoyasaponin I, soyasaponin Ba, soyasaponin VI, soyasaponin Bd, and soyasaponin γg18.

Animal studies and experimental design

The detailed and specific procedure of animal experiments was strictly carried out according to the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Beijing Normal-Hong Kong Baptist University (Approval No. 2024-05, Date: 2024.03.08). Male C57/BL6 mice aged 4 weeks were purchased from Guangdong Kaishi Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangdong, China). A constant illumination time (12 h light/dark cycle), temperature (20 ± 2 °C), and humidity (40–60%) living environment were set up to keep the animals in compliance with the globally recognized regulations for the use of animals in the laboratory.

This study used 60 Kcal% HFD to induce a diet-induced obesity mice model47. After two weeks of adaptation to the environment, 6-week-old mice were randomly divided into 5 groups, and no intergroup differences were observed (n = 8 mice per group). In this animal experiment, a positive control was established to administer a treatment with known and predictable outcomes to the animals, whose purpose is to validate the experimental procedure, ensure the sensitivity of the model, and provide a comparative standard for the test samples48,49. The positive drug selected for this animal experiment is orlistat, a weight loss drug currently approved for use by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration50. In high-fat diet-induced obesity models, orlistat is widely used as a positive control. The dosage used of orlistat was set at 20 mg/kg based on the animal study (HFD-induced obesity in C57/BL6 mice) by Lee et al.51. These groups include a negative control group (NC), model group (HFD), a positive drug group (HFD + 20 mg/kg orlistat), a low-dose group (HFD + 50 mg/kg ABS), and a high-dose group (HFD + 200 mg/kg ABS). The mice were treated with orlistat and ABS by gavage for eight weeks. Moreover, the body weight of every mouse was determined every week.

Spatial metabolomics analysis of mice liver

Workflow of mass spectrometry imaging analysis of mice livers was described in supplementary Fig. 1, including sample collection and preparation, experimental design, data acquisition, data analysis, and visualization. The following was a detailed description of each key section.

Fresh mice livers were obtained and washed thrice with PBS to remove residual blood and contaminants and then blotted as dry as possible with filter paper to avoid ice crystals that would cause distortion of spatial structure. The 6-well culture dish was marked with sample grouping, spatial location, and tissue number in advance. After the mice livers were placed in the culture dish, its lid was quickly closed and sealed with a sealing film. The culture dish was immediately placed in liquid nitrogen for quick freezing for 1 min and then transferred to a −80 °C refrigerator for standby use. Before the experiment, the mice livers were taken out from the −80 °C ultra-low temperature freezer one day in advance and placed in a −20 °C refrigerator to thaw overnight. At the same time, the freezing slicer was turned on one day in advance and set to the desired slice temperature. The appropriate amount of embedding glue was added to the base and stood for 5 min for the glue to harden to fix the sample. The mouse livers were placed in a freezing microtome. The slice thickness was set to 12 μm. The obtained tissue slices were adhered to Fisher SuperfrostTM Plus positively charged anti-slip slides (epredia Enhancing precision cancer diagnostics, U.S.A.) and marked. Different groups of mice livers to be compared were placed on the same slide, with the gap between samples less than 2 mm. All slices should be level. The prepared tissue slices were uniquely marked, placed in a slide box covered with sealing film, and kept in a −20 °C refrigerator. The desiccator was pre-cooled in a −20 °C refrigerator for 30 min. The prepared slides were taken out from the −20 °C freezer, quickly put into the pre-cooled desiccator, and then brought to room temperature.

Data acquisition was conducted in both positive and negative ion modes using AFADESI-MSI platform (Viktor Technologies Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) with a Q-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Q Exactive, Thermo Fisher, United States) over an m/z range of 80–1000 at a mass resolution of 70,000. A flow rate of 5 µL/min Acetonitrile and water (8:2, v/v) was used as the spray solvent. The sprayer voltages were 5000 V in positive ion mode and −5000 V in negative ion mode. The capillary temperature and spray gas pressure were 350 °C and 0.65 MPa, respectively. The automated gain control (AGC) target was 3E6, while the maximum injection time was 200 ms. The MSI experiments were performed by continuously scanning the tissue surface in the x-direction at a constant speed of 100 µm/s, with a vertical step of 50 µm separating the adjacent lines in the y-direction.

The raw data of the mass spectrometer were read and extracted by MassImager (Version 1.0, Chemmind Technologies Co., Ltd). Peak merging and peak alignment (mass tolerance 5 ppm) were conducted using MarkerviewTM software (Version 1.2.1, SCIEX). MATLAB was used to replace zero values. P-values and fold change (FC) were calculated using excel, and variable importance in projection (VIP) value was obtained by SIMCA software. Metabolites with p < 0.05, VIP > 1, and FC > 2.0 or FC < 0.5 were considered significantly different and identified by MATLAB using local database of AFADESI. Massimager (Version 2.0, Chemmind Technologies Co., Ltd) was utilized to assist further identification using HMDB (Human Microbiome Database) (Available from: https://hmdb.ca/). Ion images of the key metabolites were reconstructed by MassImager software (Version 1.0, Chemmind Technologies Co., Ltd) by normalizing the response intensity at a certain mass-to-charge ratio. Specifically, the maximum response intensity at a certain mass-to-charge ratio was set to 100 (marked in red), the background response intensity was set to 0 (marked in black), and other response intensities were scaled down and represented by corresponding colors to reconstruct the ion images of these key metabolites.

RNA sequencing analysis of mice liver

Using Trizol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, U.S.A.), total cellular RNA was isolated from cell samples of the model group (MOL) and treatment group (TRE) (n = 4). Tsingke Biotechnology constructed the library and carried out the RNA sequencing. To put it briefly, oligo(dT)-linked magnetic beads were used to purify mRNA. To produce cDNA, random hexamer-primed reverse transcription and second-strand cDNA synthesis were used. DNA nanoballs (DNBs) were created by amplifying the final library and loading it into patterned nanoarrays. The BGISEQ-500 platform was used for sequencing, and SOAPnuke (version 1.5.2) was used for data filtering. StringTie (version 2.1.2) was used to calculate the gene expression levels52.

To visually display the distribution of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in terms of expression fold changes and statistical significance levels, this study used a standardized gene expression matrix and the limma package for differential expression analysis. The threshold was set to |log2FoldChange| >1 and false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 to screen for significantly differentially expressed genes. Subsequently, the difference analysis results were sorted out, and the log2 FC values were extracted along with the FDR values. Log10 (FDR) was calculated as the vertical axis indicator, and a volcano plot was plotted. During the visualization process, the ggplot2 package was used to label upregulated genes, downregulated genes, and non-significant genes in different colors18.

Performed GO enrichment analysis on differentially expressed genes using the clusterProfiler package, covering three categories: biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC). Set the threshold standard as p adjust (FDR-corrected p-value) <0.05 to screen for significantly enriched GO entries. Subsequently, the enrichment analysis results were sorted out, and the GeneRatio (enrichment ratio), p-adjust value, and related gene list of significant entries were extracted. Finally, the GO enrichment results were visualized using ggplot2 and enrichplot packages, and enrichment bubble plots (dot plot) were plotted to display the significantly enriched GO functional categories, corresponding enrichment levels, and statistical significance, intuitively presenting the key biological processes that differentially expressed genes may be involved in ref. 53.

For KEGG enrichment analysis, the enrichKEGG function in the clusterProfiler package was used to perform KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on differentially expressed genes. The parameter was set to organic “mmu” corresponding to the mouse database. The FDR correction method was Benjamini-Hochberg, and the enrichment significance threshold was set to FDR <0.05. Next, use the dotplot() and barplot() functions that come with the clusterProfiler package to draw a bubble plot of enriched pathways, showing significantly enriched KEGG pathways and their corresponding gene numbers and significance levels53.

This study conducted Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) based on gene expression matrices. Firstly, use the WGCNA package to cluster the standardized expression data, eliminate outlier samples, and ensure data quality. Subsequently, based on the gene expression correlation matrix, a suitable soft thresholding power is selected to construct an approximate scale-free network, typically selecting a threshold with R² > 0.85. Calculate the similarity between genes based on the Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM) and divide genes into different modules using the hierarchical clustering method. In the identified module, further calculate the correlation between the module and phenotype (experimental group/control group, lipid metabolism indicators), and screen modules highly correlated with lipid metabolism phenotype53. Then, using the genes from this module to conduct KEGG enrichment.

Proteomic analysis of mice epididymal adipose tissue

We collected white adipose tissue from mice fed with a high-fat diet without drug intervention (model group) and fed with a high-fat diet and orally treated with adzuki bean saponin (treatment group). The corresponding biological replicates for each group were 4 cases, and proteomic analysis was performed on a total of 8 samples. To better analyze the data, we carry out a series of data management on the raw data. The main steps include screening the unique peptide number of the protein and simulating missing value recombination in raw data. The numerical simulation method is filled with the minimum value half method. Next, we used Student’s t-test or chi square test with p-value <0.05 and FC ≤0.83 or FC ≥1.2 as criteria to screen for differentially expressed proteins54. Then, we constructed a network mapping of differentially expressed proteins by querying the STRING database’s Mus musculus (mouse) for protein-protein interactions55. Cluster of Orthologous Groups of proteins (COG) analysis is used for homologous classification of gene products. In this study, we conducted COG analysis on differentially expressed proteins56. Moreover, we mapped genes to various nodes in the Mus musculus (mouse) database in Gene Ontology and performed functional enrichment analysis utilizing the GO database (http://www.geneontology.org/)57. Finally, we used the KEGG website to analyze the metabolic pathways significantly enriched in differentially expressed proteins58.

In addition, cross-omics correlation heatmaps and regression scatter plots of core nodes were constructed based on 8 liver-adipose paired samples using the R packages ComplexHeatmap, psych, and ggplot2, to quantitatively verify the inter-tissue associations. The key factor of the “Wnt-metabolic liver-adipose axis” was identified via the MOFA2 R package, and its score distribution, as well as the signaling activation and metabolic suppression characteristics revealed by GSEA, were visualized in combination with the patchwork R package. Finally, a synergistic network was plotted using the circlize R package to intuitively present the significant interactions between hepatokine ligands and adipose effectors59,60.

Immunohistochemistry

Animal tissue slices were first dewaxed and dehydrated using xylene solution and anhydrous ethanol, and then cleaned with ddH2O till there was no residual solution. Subsequently, the slices were immersed in citrate antigen repair solution (pH = 6.0) and heated to repair the antigen, followed by washing with PBS (pH = 7.4). After washing, the slices were incubated in 3% hydrogen peroxide in the dark for 30 min and washed multiple times with PSB. Subsequent slices were placed in 3% BSA and blocked at room temperature for 30 min. After the blocking is completed, the slices were incubated with 1st antibodies and placed for at least 16 h at 4 °C, protected from light. Afterwards, on the next day, slices were washed with PBS (pH = 7.4) and incubated with the 2nd antibody for at least 50 min. Subsequently, DAB staining solution was used for staining, and hematoxylin staining was performed on the cell nucleus. Finally, xylene and anhydrous ethanol were used for dehydration and sealed for subsequent research. Semi-quantitative result of the related staining was achieved and evaluated via the multiplication of staining intensity, representing the signal of interest, and the percentage of positive staining cells, which is later used for the calculation of the IHC score61.

Serum pharmacological analysis

This experiment was conducted on an additional three mice treated in the same manner as the NC group. The oral gavage treatment was performed with adzuki bean saponins at the high-dose group dosage (0.2 mg/kg body weight), and this group was set as the experimental group. After 3 h of treatment with oral gavage adzuki bean saponins, mice were sacrificed, and serum was harvested for subsequent analysis.

Frozen serum specimens were all retrieved and stored at −80 °C inside the ultralow freezer. When needed, all samples were thawed and equilibrated to the RT (25 °C). Followingly, 100 μL serum was transferred precisely to a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube after 5-s initial agitation via vortex mixer, and afterward 200 μL of HPLC-grade acetonitrile was introduced. The supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5 mL centrifuge tube and dried with nitrogen gas. A portion of 50 μL reconstitution solution (methanol/H2O = 1:1) was introduced into the centrifuge tube and centrifuged under the same conditions as above after being vortexed. Followingly, the supernatant was collected and transferred to sample vials. A pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by combination of 5 μL from each sample.

All runs of analysis for each sample employed a 3 μL sample injection volume. The chromatographic separation and resolution were achieved via applying an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC platform equipped with a 6546 QTOF mass spectrometer. Metabolite separation was achieved via utilization of a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 analytical column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm particle size) equipped with a matching guard column (2.1 × 5 mm, 1.7 μm), maintained at a constant column temperature of 30 °C with mobile phase A: 100% aqueous containing 0.1% formic acid and mobile phase B: 100% acetonitrile. The linear gradient was set as below: 95% A (0.0–2.0 min), 80% A (2.0–20.0 min), 50% A (20.0–40.0 min), and 40% A (40.0–45.0 min). The total run time was 45 min and the post time was 3.0 min. The flow rate was set at 0.3 mL per 1 min. The mass spectrometer was equipped with Dual Agilent Jet-stream source operating in negative and positive ion mode with source parameters set as follow: Nebulizer gas, 35 psi; Sheath gas temperature, 350 °C ; Sheath gas flow, 11 L/min; Dry gas temperature, 320 °C ; Dry gas flow, 8 L/min; Capillary voltage, 3500 v for two ion modes and nozzle voltage, 1000 v for two ion modes. The QTOF autoMSMS acquisition parameters were set as below: MS Scan speed, 1.5 scan/s; MS scan range, 100–1700 m/z, MSMS Scan speed, 1.0 scan/s; MSMS scan range, 40–1700 m/z, and Collision Energy, 10, 20, and 40. The acquired data quality was monitored by a pooled QC sample and a blank. Peak extraction and compound identification were accomplished using MassHunter Qualitative 10.0 (Agilent).

Bioinformatics and RNA sequencing analysis of adipocytes

GSE162653, GSE88837, and GSE164760 are databases downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Gene expression retrieved configuration file data and related annotation files. As a training dataset, GSE162653 sample information is derived from white adipose tissue samples from 10 non-obese patients and 10 obese patients. GSE88837 and GSE164760 are used as validation datasets. Among them, the sample information of GSE88837 comes from 15 lean adolescents and 15 obese adolescents, while the sample information of GSE164760 comes from liver tissues of 6 healthy individuals and 74 NASH individuals. Detailed information was recorded in supplementary table 2.

WGCNA is employed to find co-expressed gene modules, significant genes in gene networks, and connections between genes and phenotypes. We identified modules that were closely associated with obesity using the GSE162653 data set. Additionally, we created a co-expression network for the 3867 genes using the WGCNA R program. The networks’ scale-free topology was then evaluated for a range of β shrinkage parameter values. Using the scale-free topology criterion, we decided on β = 12. Lastly, using the “dynamic tree cutting” approach, genes with comparable patterns were combined into a module with a minimum size of 60. Additionally, Pearson’s correlation test was utilized to analyze the relationship between module genes and sample traits.

The “Deseq2” package in the R language was used to identify the DEGs of GSE162653. The criteria for the differential gene were p < 0.05 and |logFC| >262.

We intersected the gene sets of WGCNA and DEGs for subsequent GO and KEGG enrichment analysis. By clustering analysis on the gene dimension, functional enrichment analysis looks for shared functions across genes, discovering biological pathways that are important to biological processes. The functions of proteins and genes are uniformly defined and described by the GO database, which categorizes functions into MF, CC, and BP. An online resource called KEGG comprehensively examines gene functions from the viewpoints of both genes and molecular networks. We performed GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (p-value <0.05) using the R language’s “ClusterProfiler” package63.

The SVM method and LASSO regression can be used to screen for obesity diagnostic indicators. The “glmnet” package, the response type set to binomial, and the alpha set to 1 were used to implement LASSO. Additionally, by halving the features every round in the case of a large number of features, the SVM-RFE can identify the optimal variables as a surveillant machine learning technique to support vectors. The R package’s SVM classifier (http://github.com/johncolby/SVM-RFE) was used to categorize the chosen biomarkers for obesity diagnosis. The parameters for the k-fold cross-validation were set to k = 10.

Using the R package “pROC”, we generated receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and computed the area under the curve (AUC) values to evaluate the diagnostic potential of obesity samples against controls in the GSE162653 dataset.

The “Deseq2” package in the R language was used to do a differential analysis on the samples in the TRE group and Mol group of the RNA sequencing data. The criteria for the differential gene were p < 0.05 and |logFC| > 0.585. The GO and KEGG enrichment method can refer to 3.462.

This study used gseapy (v4.1.0) for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). Firstly, the transcriptome data will be standardized and analyzed using the expression levels of all genes. Based on the KEGG database pathway information, a gmt gene set file is created. GSEA is run to calculate the enrichment score (ES) and normalized enrichment score (NES), and the final output includes a result table containing enrichment factors, p-values, and other parameters, as well as a visualized enrichment curve to analyze the biological functions of the differentially expressed gene set64.
