Diagnostic and management challenges of severe heart failure with reduced ejection fraction in a patient with extreme morbid obesity: a case report
Authors: Mafaz Mansoor, Peyton Matt, Monica Mohanty, Srujitha Ravuri, Matthew Varn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMorbid obesity is a growing global health crisis that significantly increases cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Patients with extreme obesity present unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, particularly in the management of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF).Case presentationWe report the case of a 29-year-old African American male with extreme morbid obesity (body mass index 84.62 kg/m2; weight 290 kg) who presented with chest pain, progressive dyspnea, orthopnea, and lower extremity edema. Laboratory evaluation revealed mildly elevated troponin levels and a brain natriuretic peptide of 176 pg/mL. Chest radiography demonstrated cardiomegaly with small bilateral pleural effusions. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed severe left ventricular dilation and reduced systolic function with an ejection fraction of 25–30%. Diagnostic evaluation for ischemic cardiomyopathy was severely limited due to institutional weight restrictions for cardiac catheterization, computed tomography, and nuclear stress testing. A wearable cardioverter-defibrillator was also not feasible due to chest circumference limitations. The patient was managed medically with intravenous diuretics and guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), including beta-blocker, angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor, and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor, and was discharged with close outpatient follow-up and aggressive weight reduction plan.ConclusionThis case underscores significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in the management of heart failure among patients with extreme obesity. With the rising prevalence of severe obesity, it highlights the urgent need for improved healthcare infrastructure and more inclusive, tailored clinical strategies.Keywords: Heart failure, Morbid obesity, Diagnostic limitations, Cardiomyopathy, Health equity, Infrastructure barriers

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) categorizes obesity according to body mass index (BMI), an indirect indicator of body fat (Table 1) [1].

Obesity significantly adds to the worldwide burden of the spectrum of cardiovascular diseases, including hypertension, coronary artery disease, heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, and arrhythmias [2]. The increased cardiovascular risk is attributed to multiple factors, including insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, systemic inflammation, and altered hemodynamics [2]. In addition, obesity complicates both diagnosis and management, often requiring modified imaging techniques, medication strategies, and multidisciplinary care approaches [3]. Although prior studies have described obesity-related cardiomyopathy and the technical challenges of imaging in obese patients, reports detailing cases of extreme obesity (BMI > 80 kg/m2) leading to complete inaccessibility of standard diagnostic and device-based therapies remain scarce. This case uniquely demonstrates the simultaneous failure of multiple diagnostic modalities and therapeutic options, highlighting a critical gap in current healthcare infrastructure. It underscores the urgent need for adapted strategies and specialized tools tailored to this population, particularly as projections estimate that the prevalence of severe obesity in the United States will increase by approximately 25% [4].

Case presentation

A 29-year-old African American morbidly obese male weighing 641 lb/290 kg (BMI 84.62) with a history of hypertension, asthma, and iron deficiency anemia presented in the ED with the chief complaints of chest pain and shortness of breath. The chest pain was described as intermittent, sharp, and throbbing on the left side, which had been ongoing for several weeks. He also reported increased dyspnea on exertion, orthopnea, and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea over the previous 2 months. In addition, he reported bilateral lower extremity edema and a recent episode of productive cough with white sputum approximately one week ago.

The day before arriving at the ED, the patient was evaluated at a walk-in clinic where pneumonia was suspected. He was prescribed antibiotics, but admitted to non-compliance. During transportation to the hospital, he was placed on 2L nasal cannula due to low oxygen saturation but was quickly weaned off. He was given two doses of sublingual nitroglycerin and 324 mg of aspirin, after which he reported improvement of his chest pain.

On admission, the physical exam was remarkable for left sided chest tenderness to palpation and mild rales heard throughout all lung fields. Initially, laboratory workup revealed serial troponin I levels of 42.3, 45.7, and 41.8 ng/L with BNP at 176 pg/mL. COVID and Influenza respiratory panels were negative. His EKG showed poor R-wave progression and left atrial enlargement. Chest X-ray revealed blunting of the costophrenic angles bilaterally, possibly representing bilaterally small pleural effusions as well as diffuse global enlargement of the cardiac silhouette. An echocardiogram was obtained and revealed a severely reduced left ventricular systolic function with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 25–30%. A severely dilated left ventricle and moderate concentric left ventricular hypertrophy. Further cardiac evaluation for newly diagnosed HFrEF was limited by the patient's weight, which exceeded the table weight limits for the cardiac catheterization, cardiac CT (though we didn’t have that at the time), and nuclear stress testing.

The differential diagnosis for this patient’s newly diagnosed heart failure with reduced ejection fraction includes ischemic cardiomyopathy, obesity-related cardiomyopathy, and hypertensive heart disease. Although ischemic evaluation is standard in new-onset HFrEF, this was not feasible due to weight limitations of available diagnostic modalities. The patient’s mildly elevated but stable troponin levels without dynamic rise or fall suggest a non-acute coronary syndrome etiology, likely reflecting myocardial strain or demand ischemia in the setting of decompensated heart failure [5]. Obesity-related cardiomyopathy, characterized by left ventricular dilation and hypertrophy, is a likely contributing factor in this case [3].

He started on Lasix 20 mg IV daily and guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), which included Coreg 6.25 mg PO twice daily, Entresto 24-26 mg PO twice daily, and Dapagliflozin 10 mg PO daily. Given the reduced ejection fraction and elevated risk for malignant ventricular arrhythmias, a wearable cardioverter-defibrillator (LifeVest) was considered. However, this option was not viable due to the device’s maximum chest circumference limitation of 56 inches [6].

The patient was scheduled for close outpatient cardiology follow-up within 1–2 weeks for optimization and titration of GDMT, along with reassessment of clinical status. Further evaluation for ischemic etiology was deferred due to current weight limitations of available diagnostic modalities, with plans to reconsider once weight reduction is achieved or access to bariatric-capable equipment is available. Given the high clinical suspicion for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), the patient was also referred for an outpatient sleep study. OSA is highly prevalent in patients with severe obesity and is an important contributor to cardiovascular morbidity, including hypertension, arrhythmias, and heart failure [7]. Intermittent hypoxia and increased sympathetic activity associated with OSA may exacerbate myocardial dysfunction and volume overload [7]. Although not formally diagnosed during hospitalization, OSA is strongly suspected in this patient and represents an important modifiable risk factor that warrants further outpatient evaluation and management. Given the patient’s extreme obesity and high cardiometabolic risk, tirzepatide was initiated at discharge for weight management, consistent with emerging evidence supporting its use in obesity and cardiometabolic disease, although its role in advanced HFrEF remains under investigation [8].

Discussion and conclusion

Diagnostic challenges

Our patient’s body habitus significantly restricted the use of conventional diagnostic modalities. Transthoracic echocardiography was limited by poor image quality, a common issue in patients weighing over 300 pounds [9]. This is because ultrasound waves are obstructed by the adipose tissue, leading to reduced image quality [9]. Moreover, the increased distance between the ultrasound transducer and the heart further compromises imaging [9]. Advanced diagnostic testing, such as coronary angiography, stress imaging, or CT/MRI scans, was not feasible due to the patient’s body weight and equipment limitations. Standard MRI tables generally support up to 350 lb (159 kg), while conventional CT scanners accommodate approximately 450 lb (205 kg) [10]. At our institution, cardiac catheterization procedures are performed using the Philips Azurion 7 interventional platform. According to manufacturer specifications, the standard catheterization table has a maximum patient weight limit of approximately 551 lb (250 kg) [11]. These restrictions posed a significant barrier to excluding acute coronary syndrome or infiltrative disease, which usually guides therapeutic decision-making. The inability to perform standard investigations demonstrates a crucial gap in the current healthcare infrastructure, as diagnostic equipment is often not designed to accommodate patients with extreme obesity (Fig. 1).

Therapeutic challenges

Morbidly obese patients also present unique challenges in pharmacologic management. Guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), including angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor, beta blockers, Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, remains the central component of HFrEF management [12]. However, severe obesity significantly alters both pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, complicating medication management in HFrEF [3]. Increased adipose tissue expands the volume of distribution for lipophilic drugs, whereas dosing of medications primarily distributed in lean tissues should be based on ideal body weight [13]. Hepatic metabolism may be variably affected, with increased hepatic blood flow in early obesity but potential reduction in the presence of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [14]. Enzymatic activity is also altered, including decreased CYP3A4 and increased CYP2E1 activity [15]. Diuretic dosages are particularly challenging due to the difficulty of accurately tracking weight and assessing fluid levels. Loop diuretics, essential for volume management, often require higher doses due to increased volume of distribution, which can heighten the risk of electrolyte imbalance [16]. Pharmacodynamically, obesity is associated with altered receptor sensitivity and increased inflammatory signaling, including elevated tumor necrosis factor-α contributing to insulin resistance and higher insulin requirements [17].

In this population, device-based therapies present additional difficulties. Although a wearable LifeVest was contemplated given the elevated arrhythmic risk, it was not feasible because the patient’s chest circumference exceeded the device’s maximum size of 56 inches [6]. Similarly, implanting ICDs or CRT devices carries increased procedural risks. Due to higher surgical risk and worse results, severe obesity frequently precludes eligibility for cutting-edge treatments like heart transplantation or LVAD implantation [18, 19].

The “obesity paradox,” wherein overweight and mildly obese patients with heart failure may have improved survival compared to normal-weight individuals, has been described in the literature. However, this paradox does not appear to extend to patients with extreme or class III obesity. In such cases, excessive adiposity contributes to adverse hemodynamic changes, systemic inflammation, and significant limitations in diagnostic and therapeutic interventions, as demonstrated in this patient [20]. As illustrated in this case, extreme obesity not only complicates pharmacologic management and volume assessment but also restricts access to advanced device-based and procedural therapies (Fig. 1).

Ethical considerations

Beyond the challenges of individual patient management, cases like this highlight the significant ethical and healthcare system implications. Patients with extreme obesity may experience limited access to standard diagnostic and therapeutic interventions due to constraints in medical equipment and healthcare infrastructure. From an ethical perspective, this raises questions about justice and equity in healthcare, emphasizing the obligation of institutions and providers to deliver high-quality care to every patient, irrespective of their body habitus (Fig. 1).

This case illustrates the multifaceted association between morbid obesity and advanced heart failure in a case where both diagnostic and therapeutic options were extremely limited. The fact that obesity rates are still climbing on a global scale means that health care systems will, in time, be forced to encounter these issues more frequently, depriving many more of the standard of care that exist for population with lower BMIs [4]. Therefore, it is of the utmost importance that the current infrastructure be modified and future infrastructure standards changed to adapt to the future populations so that medical care can be more inclusive and not neglect an important portion of the population. Additionally, it is imperative that medical oriented studies and trials also include this specific population group so that medication dosing efficacy and side effect profiles be optimized. Otherwise, we risk compromising the level of care that everyone deserves, regardless of their BMI.
