Maintaining teaching consistency across repeated sessions using structured pre-session planning in medical education
Authors: Amna Alotiby
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRepeated delivery of identical instructional content in medical education can result in variability in teaching quality and the phenomenon of content drift. Structured pre-session planning has been proposed to support the maintenance of teaching consistency and quality. This study aimed to evaluate the contribution of structured pre-session planning to maintaining teaching consistency and perceived instructional quality across repeated teaching sessions, using student perceptions and academic performance outcomes.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted at a medical college during the second term of the 2025 academic year. A single tutor delivered identical teaching sessions to four groups of second-year medical students (n = 147) over nine weeks, implementing structured pre-session planning to ensure consistency. Student perceptions were collected via an anonymous, validated 8-item post-module questionnaire assessing structure and organization, clarity and comprehension, and tutor preparedness and engagement. Academic performance was assessed using a standardized, blueprint-aligned examination administered uniformly across groups. Descriptive statistics, exact probability tests, Spearman correlation analysis, and one-way ANOVA were used for analysis.ResultsAcross groups, 85.7–91.4% of students agreed the content was well-organized and aligned with learning objectives (p = 0.098–0.769), and 82.4–94.3% reported clear explanations (p = 0.140–0.595). Tutor preparedness and engagement received high positive ratings (99.3% agreed or strongly agreed; p = 0.297). Strong positive correlations were observed between perceptions of structure, clarity, and engagement (Spearman’s rho = 0.585–0.874, p < 0.001), indicating interrelated aspects of consistent teaching. Academic performance was highly consistent across groups (mean scores 83.6–84.2%, p = 0.989; negligible effect size η² = 0.00068; overlapping 95% confidence intervals), supporting the reliability of student-perceived teaching consistency.ConclusionsStructured pre-session planning may help support the maintenance of consistent teaching quality across repeated sessions, as reflected in both student perceptions and academic performance outcomes. This approach appears to mitigate instructional variability and support equitable learning experiences. Incorporating structured pre-session planning into faculty development practices may represent a practical, low-cost strategy to enhance teaching consistency in medical education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08506-8.

Introduction

In contemporary medical education, instructors often deliver the same teaching session multiple times to different student groups over a short period. While repeated delivery can improve logistical efficiency and increase educational reach, it introduces significant risks, such as instructional variability, teaching fatigue, and content drift—the phenomenon in which minor changes in content, emphasis, or teaching style accumulate across sessions [1, 2]. Such inconsistencies may result in inequitable learning experiences, potentially affecting students’ grasp of key concepts and learning outcomes. Empirical studies suggest that such inconsistencies can compromise students’ understanding, engagement, and equitable learning outcomes [3, 4]. Despite its potential impact, research on the mechanisms, prevalence, and mitigation of these inconsistencies remains limited.

Maintaining teaching consistency—defined as the uniformity of instructional delivery in terms of quality, structure, and alignment with intended learning objectives across repeated sessions—is critical for equitable learning experiences [4]. In parallel, student-perceived teaching quality—which encompasses clarity, logical organization, tutor preparedness, responsiveness, and alignment with objectives—is widely used as an indicator of effective instruction [3, 5]. Achieving and maintaining consistency in these domains is challenging when instructors repeatedly deliver identical content under cognitive load, time pressures, and the potential for fatigue [2, 6].

While existing literature identifies these challenges, much of it is descriptive rather than analytical. For instance, studies referencing content drift and teaching variability often note the phenomena without critically examining their frequency, underlying mechanisms, or consequences for student learning [1, 2]. Moreover, the majority of work emphasizes individual cognitive factors (e.g., attention, memory) but does not systematically integrate instructional design principles or learning evaluation frameworks. This gap limits the ability to generalize findings and design interventions that ensure consistent instructional quality across repeated sessions.

Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) provides a useful lens for understanding variability in repeated sessions. According to CLT, instructors’ attentional resources are finite, and repeated teaching of identical material can deplete these resources, leading to omissions, uneven pacing, or reduced clarity [2, 6]. However, while CLT explains the cognitive mechanisms that may contribute to variability, it does not provide a comprehensive framework for evaluating teaching effectiveness or systematically designing interventions.

Integrating CLT with established pedagogical frameworks provides a broader perspective on maintaining teaching consistency. For example, Kirkpatrick’s Model of Training Evaluation evaluates instructional effectiveness across multiple levels, including learner reaction, knowledge acquisition, behaviour, and outcomes, offering a structured approach to assess teaching quality and its impact on learning [7, 8]. Likewise, Instructional Design Theory emphasizes systematic planning, alignment of learning objectives, structured sequencing, and continuous evaluation to promote consistency and effective learning [9]. Anchoring the study in these frameworks allows for both the identification of underlying mechanisms of variability and the development of evidence-based strategies to enhance teaching consistency and quality.

Structured pre-session planning, involving deliberate preparation, review of learning outcomes, organized content flow, annotated notes, and anticipation of student questions, has been proposed as a strategy to maintain teaching consistency [10, 11]. Structured planning has been shown to reduce cognitive burden, improve clarity, and enhance instructional coherence, particularly when sessions are repeated frequently. Romanova et al. (2024) reported that personal learning plans and structured instructional frameworks improved teaching delivery, student engagement, and alignment with learning objectives [12]. Similarly, van Diggele et al. (2020) demonstrated that small-group teaching effectiveness increased when sessions were preceded by deliberate pre-session planning. Despite these promising findings, few studies have quantified the impact of structured pre-session planning on maintaining perceived teaching consistency across repeated sessions [10]. Critical gaps remain, including the integration of cognitive, instructional, and evaluative frameworks and the triangulation of student perceptions with measurable outcomes. Addressing these gaps is essential for developing interventions that reliably enhance teaching consistency, clarity, and tutor preparedness. Given this context, the present study aims to evaluate the contribution of structured pre-session planning to maintaining consistent teaching quality across repeated sessions, based on student perceptions and academic performance outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a cross-sectional study conducted at a medical college during the second term of the 2025 academic year. Over nine weeks (May–July 2025), a single tutor delivered an identical series of lectures to four groups of second-year medical students (G1–G4) at different scheduled times.

The study was designed to evaluate the role of structured pre-session planning in maintaining teaching consistency and quality when the same tutor delivers repeated sessions across multiple student groups. Two complementary data sources were collected to assess maintenance of teaching consistency: (1) Student perceptions via an anonymous post-module survey; (2) Academic performance outcomes, used to corroborate student perceptions and provide indirect evidence of consistent teaching across groups. This design does not involve pre- and post-intervention comparisons, as the focus was on validating a method already implemented by the tutor to support teaching consistency.

Participants

A total of 147 s-year medical students were enrolled in the study, divided into four groups of 35–39 students each. All students who attended the sessions were invited to complete the post-module survey on teaching consistency and instructional quality. The survey response rate is reported: all 147 students completed the survey anonymously. Participation was voluntary, and all responses were collected anonymously. Ethical approval was obtained from the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Umm Al-Qura University (Approval Number: HAPO-02-K-012-2025-05-2693), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Intervention: structured Pre-Session planning

Before delivering the sessions, the tutor developed a structured plan to maintain teaching consistency, including: (1) notes ensuring all essential session content aligned with predefined learning outcomes, (2) annotated notes highlighting key points and examples, and (3) a fixed sequence and timing for content delivery across all sessions. This structured planning was implemented uniformly across all groups to reduce variability, prevent content omissions, and maintain instructor confidence during repeated sessions. The structured pre-session planning tool is included as Supplementary File S1.

Data collection

Student survey

At the conclusion of the nine-week module, after all sessions had been delivered to all groups, students completed a structured 8-item questionnaire. The survey was administered only once, at the end of the module, to capture their overall perceptions of instructional consistency, clarity, and tutor preparedness across all repeated sessions. Items of questionnaire were categorized into three domains: (a) Structure and Organization (e.g., logical flow, alignment with objectives, perceived consistency across groups); (b) Clarity and Comprehension (e.g., clarity of explanation, emphasis on main points, response to questions); and (c) Tutor Preparedness and Engagement (e.g., confidence, delivery style). Each item was rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 4 = Strongly Agree). The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study based on established teaching evaluation frameworks and prior published studies [3–5]. To ensure methodological rigor, the content validity of the questionnaire was reviewed and confirmed by faculty experts in medical education. The survey was pilot-tested with a small cohort of students not included in the study to verify the clarity, comprehensibility, and relevance of items.

Internal consistency for each domain was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding values of 0.82, 0.87, and 0.89 for Structure and Organization, Clarity and Comprehension, and Tutor Preparedness and Engagement, respectively, indicating good reliability. The full English-language version of the questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File S2.

Academic performance

Academic performance data were collected to triangulate student perceptions of teaching consistency. The assessment comprised multiple-choice questions (MCQs) and short-answer questions (SAQs) designed to comprehensively evaluate students’ knowledge and understanding of the session content. All assessment items were systematically blueprinted against the course learning objectives and aligned with the specific content of the repeated sessions to ensure content validity and relevance. Assessment items were developed by the tutor in consultation with faculty content experts. Each item underwent expert review for clarity, relevance, and alignment with learning outcomes.

The same assessment was administered under identical conditions to all student groups at the end of the module to ensure comparability across groups. The instrument’s content validity was confirmed through expert panel review, and item analysis was conducted to verify appropriateness, reliability, and coverage of learning objectives.

To strengthen the study’s validity, student survey responses were triangulated with these academic performance outcomes, providing objective confirmation of perceived teaching consistency across groups. These measures allowed triangulation between perceived teaching quality and objective learning outcomes across groups.

Survey data analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28 (IBM Corp., 2021). For each domain, Structure and Organization, Clarity and Comprehension, and Tutor Preparedness and Engagement, overall scores were computed by having the individual item composite mean scores within that domain. The resulting composite scores were categorized into three perception levels: scores less than 3 indicated low perception, scores ranging from 3 to less than 4 represented intermediate perception, and scores 4 reflected high perception. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize students’ responses across groups. Exact probability tests were applied to examine differences between groups. Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to assess the relationships among the three instructional domains within each group. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used for all statistical tests. Academic performance was analysed by calculating mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum scores for each group. One-way ANOVA tested for differences between groups, with significance at p < 0.05.

Results

Students’ perceptions of structure and organization

Table 1 summarizes students’ perceptions regarding the structure and organization of teaching sessions, reflecting maintenance of teaching consistency across the four groups (G1–G4). The majority of students agreed or strongly agreed that the content was delivered in a well-organized and logical manner, with overall positive responses ranging from 85.7% in G1 to 92.4% in G4. There were minimal negative responses, with only 12.2% of total participants disagreeing about the organization of content. The alignment of session content with stated learning objectives was highly rated across all groups, with over 95% of students agreeing or strongly agreeing that the content matched the learning outcomes. Similarly, the consistency of topics delivered across groups was strongly supported, with nearly all students indicating that the information they received was similar to what peers learned in other groups. Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences across groups in any of the structure and organization items (p-values ranged from 0.098 to 0.769). This lack of variability indicates that the structured pre-session planning effectively ensured consistent delivery of content across all repeated sessions, supporting the study’s aim of maintaining teaching consistency.

Students’ perceptions of clarity and comprehension

As shown in Table 2, students generally reported favourable perceptions of tutor clarity and comprehension. The majority agreed or strongly agreed that the tutor clearly emphasized the main points of the topic (94.6% overall) and effectively responded to questions with clarity (97.9% overall). The clarity and ease of understanding of explanations received slightly lower agreement, with 79.6% of students rating this positively. There were no statistically significant differences observed between groups in clarity-related items (p-values ranged from 0.140 to 0.595), indicating consistent clarity of instruction across repeated sessions.

Students’ Academic Performance.

To validate students’ survey-based perceptions, academic performance outcomes were compared across the four groups (Table 6). Students’ academic performance was highly consistent, with mean scores of 83.6 ± 9.17, 84.2 ± 10.30, 84.2 ± 9.46, and 84.0 ± 8.95 for Groups 1–4, respectively. One-way ANOVA indicated no significant differences between groups (p = 0.989), and the calculated effect size (η² = 0.00068) demonstrated negligible variance between groups. 95% confidence intervals for the mean scores also largely overlapped across groups (G1: 80.8–86.4, G2: 81.0–87.4, G3: 81.2–87.2, G4: 80.9–87.1), further confirming that students achieved comparable learning outcomes across sessions. These results support the survey findings, indicating that structured pre-session planning minimized variability in teaching delivery and ensured equitable learning experiences across all groups.

Discussion

This study evaluated the role of structured pre-session planning in maintaining teaching consistency across repeated sessions delivered by the same tutor to multiple medical student groups. The findings demonstrate consistently high student ratings across domains of structure and organization, clarity and comprehension, and tutor preparedness and engagement, with no statistically significant differences between groups. Importantly, student perceptions were triangulated with academic performance outcomes, strengthening credibility and helping to minimize potential confounding factors such as student familiarity with the tutor. These results indicate that structured pre-session planning supports consistent instructional delivery and equitable learning experiences.

The consistently positive perceptions of structure and organization indicate that structured pre-session planning effectively mitigated content drift and variability across repeated sessions. Nearly all students agreed that the session content aligned with learning objectives and was similar across groups, directly reflecting the maintenance of teaching consistency. These findings extend prior descriptive reports on teaching variability by providing empirical evidence that deliberate planning can mitigate inconsistencies commonly associated with repeated session delivery [1, 2]. From an instructional design perspective, the use of predefined learning outcomes, fixed sequencing, and annotated teaching notes aligns with principles of constructive alignment and systematic instructional planning [4, 9], reinforcing their value in medical education settings.

Clarity and comprehension were also rated highly, with no significant differences between groups. While explanations were rated slightly lower than emphasis of key points and responses to questions, overall perceptions remained positive and consistent. This pattern suggests that structured planning may support the tutor’s ability to maintain emphasis on core concepts and respond effectively to learners, even under the cognitive demands of repeated teaching. These findings are consistent with Cognitive Load Theory, which posits that reducing extraneous cognitive load through preparation and structure allows instructors to allocate attentional resources more effectively during instruction [6]. By externalizing cognitive demands into a pre-session plan, tutors may be better equipped to sustain clarity and coherence across multiple sessions.

Tutor preparedness and engagement received the highest ratings, with nearly all students perceiving the tutor as confident and well prepared. This finding is notable given concerns that repeated teaching can lead to fatigue, reduced enthusiasm, or diminished instructional quality [2]. The results suggest that structured pre-session planning may function not only as a cognitive support tool but also as a mechanism to maintain instructor confidence and engagement. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that deliberate preparation enhances teaching effectiveness and learner engagement, particularly in small-group and repeated teaching contexts [10–12].

The strong positive correlations observed among structure, clarity, and tutor engagement across all groups highlight the interdependent nature of these instructional domains. Higher organization was consistently associated with greater clarity and perceived preparedness, reinforcing the concept that effective teaching quality is multidimensional rather than compartmentalized. This interrelationship aligns with Kirkpatrick’s model, particularly at the reaction and learning levels, where learner perceptions of structure and clarity are closely tied to perceived effectiveness and learning outcomes [7, 8]. The triangulation of student perceptions with objective academic performance further strengthens the validity of these findings. The absence of significant differences in assessment scores across groups, together with a negligible effect size (η² = 0.00068) and overlapping 95% confidence intervals, supports the conclusion that consistent instructional delivery translated into equitable learning outcomes.

Collectively, these findings address key gaps in the literature by moving beyond descriptive accounts of teaching variability to empirically demonstrate how a specific instructional strategy—structured pre-session planning—can promote consistency across repeated sessions. Unlike studies that focus solely on overall teaching effectiveness, this work emphasizes session-to-session uniformity and integrates cognitive, instructional, and evaluative frameworks to provide a more comprehensive understanding of teaching consistency in medical education.

Limitations, Implications, and future directions

This study was conducted in a single institution with a single tutor, which may limit generalizability to other educational settings or instructors. The cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference, and student ratings may be influenced by familiarity with the tutor, personal biases, or contextual factors. However, triangulation with objective academic performance outcomes—demonstrating a negligible effect size and overlapping confidence intervals across groups—helps mitigate, though not eliminate, these potential sources of bias.

Despite these limitations, the study provides insight into practical strategies for maintaining teaching consistency across repeated sessions. Structured pre-session planning is feasible, low-cost, and supports stable instructional delivery, consistent student perceptions, and equitable academic outcomes. Future research should consider multi-instructor designs, pre–post or quasi-experimental evaluations, and incorporation of historical baseline data to rigorously assess the impact of structured planning on teaching consistency across broader educational contexts.

Conclusion

This study suggests that structured pre-session planning may help support the maintenance of consistent teaching delivery, as reflected in student perceptions and comparable academic performance across repeated sessions delivered by the same tutor. High and uniform ratings across instructional domains, along with consistent academic performance, are consistent with the possibility that deliberate preparation could contribute to instructional clarity, minimize content drift, and sustain tutor preparedness and engagement.

By drawing on concepts from Cognitive Load Theory, instructional design principles, and Kirkpatrick’s evaluation framework, this study provides a preliminary, theoretically informed perspective on teaching consistency in medical education. Incorporating structured pre-session planning into faculty development initiatives may offer a low-cost, practical approach to promote equitable and high-quality learning experiences in repeated teaching contexts. Further studies with controlled or pre–post designs are needed to confirm these findings.
