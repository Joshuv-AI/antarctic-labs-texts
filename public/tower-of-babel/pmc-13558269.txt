Safety and Efficacy of the Extravascular Implantable Cardioverter-Defibrillator (EV-ICD) in Japanese Patients
Authors: Mitsuru Wada, Nobuhiro Nishii, Kohei Ishibashi, Christopher Wiggenhorn, Takuto Yamamoto, Kengo Kusano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe extravascular implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (EV-ICD) has proven efficacy and a low complication rate in global populations, but data from Japanese patients is limited. We report the safety and efficacy of the EV-ICD system in a Japanese cohort through 12 months of follow-up.Methods and ResultsPatients from Japan with a Class I or IIa indication for a single-chamber ICD were enrolled and implanted with the EV-ICD system. Defibrillation success was determined following implant. System programming changes, device electrical performance, freedom from major system- or procedure-related complications through 12 months post-implant, and follow-up status at all visits were assessed. From February to September 2023, 14 patients (12 males, 2 females) were enrolled and successfully implanted across 2 sites (age: 53.6±14.7 years; left ventricular ejection fraction: 52.6±15.6%; secondary prevention: 42.9%). Defibrillation testing was successful in all patients (14/14, 100%). At 2 weeks and 12 months, freedom from major system- and/or procedure-related complications was 92.9% and 85.7%, respectively. One patient received 7 appropriate shocks that were successful in terminating the arrhythmia. There were no inappropriate shocks delivered, and a single inappropriate antitachycardia pacing therapy was delivered through follow-up.ConclusionsThe EV-ICD system was safely implanted and effective at terminating both induced and spontaneous ventricular arrhythmias in a small cohort of Japanese patients.

Central Figure

For >30 years, implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) have been an established therapy for preventing sudden cardiac death in patients at risk of life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias.1–3 Although conventional transvenous ICD (TV-ICD) systems can offer both defibrillation and pacing, the use of transvenous leads may be complicated by vascular or myocardial injury, venous thrombosis/occlusion, or systemic infection.4–6 Progressive adhesion of the lead to vascular or myocardial tissue can also increase the risk of lead extraction when lead revision becomes necessary or device-related infection occurs.4

To address these limitations, the subcutaneous ICD (S-ICD) was introduced, offering a fully extravascular approach.2,7,8 However, it does not deliver antitachycardia pacing (ATP), and the lead position outside the chest wall results in a larger device that requires more energy to deliver shocks, resulting in a decrease in battery longevity compared to traditional transvenous ICDs.8,9 Therefore, the extravascular ICD (EV-ICD) system was developed to combine the benefits of an extravascular approach with the functional capabilities of transvenous systems.10 The EV-ICD system deploys a lead within the retrosternal space in proximity to myocardial tissue, which facilitates the use of ATP therapy and defibrillation therapy with energies similar to those seen with TV-ICDs.10,11

The safety and efficacy of the EV-ICD system were assessed in the large, global Pivotal clinical trial, showing defibrillation efficacy and a low rate of major complications through 3 years.12,13 Subsequent real-world data from the post-market Enlighten study have further supported the safety and performance of the EV-ICD system, highlighting its potential for broader clinical adoption worldwide.14,15 However, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, Japanese sites were unable to participate in the global Pivotal trial,10 but given the novelty of the EV-ICD system and procedure, there was a desire to evaluate its acute characteristics and characterize its safety within the Japanese medical environment. In the present analysis, we evaluated the performance and acceptability of the EV-ICD system in Japanese patients over a 12-month follow-up period.

Methods

Study Design

The EV ICD Japan study was a prospective, multisite, single-arm, non-randomized, premarket clinical study designed to enroll approximately 15–25 subjects at ≤3 sites in Japan. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, the Ministerial Ordinance Concerning Good Clinical Practice for Medical Devices, and in accordance with national and local laws and the regulatory authorities and/or ethics committees of the participating sites. Eligible subjects included patients ≥18 years of age with a Class I or IIa indication for implantation of a single-chamber ICD according to current ACC/AHA/HRS or ESC guidelines.16–18 Main exclusions included those with bradycardia pacing, cardiac resynchronization therapy, existing pacemaker or other cardiac implantable electronic device, and previous sternotomy. All exclusion and inclusion criteria are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

EV-ICD System

The EV-ICD system and the basic steps of the implantation procedure have been outlined previously.11,19 The lead has an epsilon shape and is implanted via a minimally invasive approach within the retrosternal space. The ICD is implanted along the patient’s left midaxillary line, has a volume of 33 cm3, and delivers shocks of ≤40 J (Figure 1). The system offers 3 sensing vectors, which include a near-field vector between the 2 ring electrodes and 2 far-field sensing vectors from each ring electrode to the ICD device. Pacing vectors include coil-to-coil, ring-to-ring, and distal ring–proximal coil vectors. The device is capable of providing short-term pacing support, including ATP, pause prevention pacing, and post-shock pacing. All implantation procedures were conducted by cardiologists who had completed a dedicated, hands-on training program led by the device’s manufacturer and experienced implanters of the device. Primary Objectives

The primary efficacy objective of the study was defibrillation testing success at the time of implantation, and was defined as termination of a single induced sustained shockable ventricular arrhythmia (SSVA) with either a single 20-J shock or the termination of 2 consecutive SSVA episodes with 30-J shocks, in accordance with the global Pivotal trial.20 Subjects with successful SSVA termination with a single 20-J shock were also tested for SSVA termination with a single 15-J shock. The primary safety objective of the study was freedom from major system- and/or procedure-related major complications at 2 weeks post-implant. Major complications were defined as those that resulted in death, permanent loss of defibrillation function due to mechanical or electrical dysfunction of the device, hospitalization, prolongation of an existing hospitalization by ≥48 h or system revision, including repositioning, replacement or explantation. This analysis included an assessment of safety through all follow-up.

Ancillary Objectives

Ancillary objectives included the occurrence of appropriate and inappropriate therapies through all follow-up, characterization of any follow-up defibrillation testing, and electrical performance (pacing capture thresholds, pacing impedance, and sensing amplitudes) over time. All device-detected arrhythmic episodes were reviewed and classified by an independent episode review committee according to predefined criteria, ensuring consistent and reliable adjudication of arrhythmic events.

Quality of Life (QOL)

QOL was measured using the 12-Item Short Form Survey (SF-12) at baseline and 6-month follow-up for all patients.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used for patient demographics, medical history, and summaries of most endpoints. The rates of freedom from major system- and/or procedure-related complications were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method; because no patient was censored, these rates are the same as the simple proportions. The paired t-test was used to assess differences in SF-12 components between baseline and 6 months. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Basic Characteristics

A total of 14 patients were enrolled at 2 sites, with all patients proceeding to EV-ICD implantation. Of the 14 enrolled patients, 85.7% were male, the age range was 24–78 years with an average age of 53.6±14.7 years, the mean LVEF was 52.6±15.6%, and 6 patients (42.9%) underwent implantation for a secondary prevention indication for an ICD (Table 1). Underlying cardiac conditions included hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (n=4, 28.6%), primary/idiopathic electrical disease (n=4, 28.6%), and a history of atrial fibrillation (n=2, 14.3%). After implantation, patients were followed for an average of 16.0±2.4 months. Procedural Characteristics

Implantation was successful in 100% (14/14) of patients under general anesthesia. The mean time from initial incision to final lead placement was 58.9±25.2 min. Total procedure time from first incision to final suture, which included the protocol-required defibrillation testing, was 116.5±38.8 min with a mean fluoroscopy time of 14.8±23.1 min. Retunneling was required in 2 procedures (Table 2); in 1 patient, the lead was retunneled due to lead mispositioning, and in the other patient, the lead was retunneled due to lead dislodgement and inadequate sensing. The final mean R-wave amplitude in the ring 1 to ring 2 vector was 2.51±0.83 mV at implantation. The average length of stay from implantation to discharge was 7.9±2.6 days. Defibrillation Testing: Primary Efficacy Endpoint

Defibrillation testing was successful with an adequate (>10 J) safety margin in all 14 patients. A single SSVA episode was terminated with a 20-J shock in 9 (64.3%) patients, and 5 (35.7%) patients had 2 consecutive SSVA terminations with 30-J shocks for success (Figure 3). The 9 subjects with a successful single 20-J shock were also tested for SSVA termination with a single 15-J shock according to the defibrillation testing protocol, resulting in 6/9 (66.7%) successful terminations at 15 J. Additional defibrillation testing was performed in 2 patients at 1 and 65 days post-implantation due to major complications described next. Both tests were successful with a single 30-J shock. Safety: Primary Safety Endpoint

There was 1 major system- and/or procedure-related complication identified during the periprocedural period. The primary result was 92.9% freedom from system- and/or procedure-related major complications at 2 weeks post-implantation. Through the 12 months, a total of 2 major system- and/or procedure-related complications occurred, corresponding to a complication-free rate of 85.7% at 12 months post-implantation (Figure 4). Kaplan–Meier curve depicting the freedom from major system- and/or procedure-related complications through 12 months of follow-up. The number at risk is shown in blue.

The 2 major complications were as follows. In the first case, inadvertent lead placement into the pericardial sac was not recognized during the procedure. The patient reported post-procedural chest pain, and computed tomography performed on the same day revealed that the lead had traversed the pericardial space. The lead was replaced with a new lead, and defibrillation testing was reperformed, with no further major complications for this patient during follow-up. In the second case, inversion of the epsilon-shaped lead in the substernal space (lead rotation) from its preferred orientation was identified on follow-up chest X-ray at 36 days post-implantation. Additional defibrillation testing was later performed and was successful. As a result, no system modification was required, and the system was left in place.

Appropriate and Inappropriate Therapy

No inappropriate shocks were delivered to any patients through the final follow-up. One patient received 7 appropriate shocks 381 days post-implant for ventricular tachycardia (VT). All 7 shocks were successful, and the patient was hospitalized and VT eliminated by catheter ablation. In another patient, inappropriate ATP therapy was delivered for a sinus tachycardia episode. The patient was subsequently reprogrammed, and no further inappropriate therapies occurred through follow-up.

Excluding defibrillation testing, a total of 66 spontaneous device-recorded arrhythmic episodes were identified during follow-up. Of these, 10 were classified as non-sustained VT and 7 as sustained VT; no ventricular fibrillation episodes were observed. The sustained VT episodes corresponded to the VT events treated with shocks described above. The remaining episodes were adjudicated as supraventricular tachycardia (n=20) or noise (n=29).

Electrical Measurements

The R-wave amplitude, measured with the ring 1–ring 2 vector while the patient was seated, was 2.0±1.0 mV at discharge, 2.1±1.0 mV at 2 weeks, and 2.9±1.4 mV at 12 months (n=14) (Figure 5A). There were 2 episodes of undersensing reported during follow-up based on site assessments: 1 required sensitivity reprogramming; the other episode was observed during exercise and was not subsequently reported during routine follow-up. Pacing impedance from the ring 1–ring 2 vector increased over time with measurement of 312.7±96.2 Ω at discharge, 366.4±65.2 Ω at 2 weeks, and 479.1±126.3 Ω at 12 months (Figure 5B). At the ring 1–coil 2 vector, pacing capture threshold values were consistent from discharge to 2 weeks with values of 5.7±2.2 (n=10) and 5.8±1.7 V (n=4), respectively, with a mean of 6.3±2.1 V at 6 months (n=3) and no measurement at 12 months for this vector (Figure 5C). Pacing Programming

When the device is shipped, ATP is nominally OFF and is recommended to be turned ON at prehospital discharge. In this study, ATP was programmed ON at discharge in 12 (85.7%) patients, pause-prevention pacing ON in 4 (28.6%) patients, and post-shock pacing ON in 10 (71.4%) patients (Table 3). By 12 months 14 (100%), 11 (78.8%), and 13 (92.9%) patients had ATP, pause-prevention pacing, and post-shock pacing programmed ON, respectively. No patients reported a pacing therapy programmed OFF at any visit due to pacing sensation. Additionally, no patients requested ATP to be programmed OFF following testing. QOL

The SF-12 surveys of QOL showed no statistical difference in the mean values for the physical, mental, and role components from baseline to 6-month follow-up (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

Main Findings

This prospective, multisite study has provided the first dedicated evaluation of the EV-ICD system in a Japanese population, with follow-up through 12 months. Defibrillation testing was successful in all patients at implantation, and freedom from major system- and/or procedure-related complications was 92.8% at 2 weeks and 85.7% at 12 months. No inappropriate shocks occurred, and the only inappropriate ATP episode was successfully managed through device reprogramming.

No ATP therapies for VT were delivered during follow-up, likely reflecting the limited cohort size and low arrhythmic event rate. Thus, the performance of ATP could not be evaluated. The R-wave amplitudes observed in this cohort were approximately 2 mV, which is within the range reported in the global Pivotal trial. No clinical evidence suggesting undersensing or delayed therapy delivery for ventricular arrhythmia was observed during follow-up, despite the relatively low R-wave amplitude compared with conventional TV-ICD systems. The defibrillation performance observed in the present study was comparable to that reported in the global Pivotal trial, which demonstrated a 98.7% defibrillation success rate at implantation.

Clinical Context

Recent studies from Japanese cohorts have also provided important insights into ventricular arrhythmia management and clinical outcomes in real-world settings.21–23 In addition, studies incorporating both patient characteristics and device-related factors have further contributed to arrhythmic risk stratification and device performance.24,25

Contextualizing With Early Japanese S-ICD Experience

In Japan, the S-ICD has been available since 2016 and is often selected for younger patients to preserve venous access and minimize long-term lead-related complications.26 Early Japanese experience suggested favorable perioperative outcomes, in which no major perioperative complications were reported, although available data have largely been derived from small single-center studies, and systematic prospective evaluations remain limited.27,28

The present study is the first prospective evaluation of EV-ICD implantation in Japanese patients using predefined and adjudicated safety endpoints. In total, 2 major system- and/or procedure-related complications were observed. One was inadvertent lead placement into the pericardial sac, observed during the periprocedural period. The lead was subsequently replaced, and no long-term sequelae occurred. The other was lead rotation during follow-up, which did not require system revision.

These complications are specific to the substernal approach and differ from those typically encountered with S-ICD implantation. Compared with the subcutaneous approach, the substernal technique for the EV-ICD requires more detailed understanding of mediastinal anatomy and precise lead positioning, which may introduce additional procedural complexity, particularly during the early phase of clinical adoption. The occurrence of these events suggests that both lead placement and fixation are influenced by operator experience, highlighting the importance of the learning curve. In Japan, early clinical use of the EV-ICD system has been conducted under structured proctorship, which may enhance procedural safety during its introduction.

Feasibility in Japanese Patients

Although the EV-ICD requires a novel substernal implantation technique, all patients underwent successful implantation using the standard procedure under general anesthesia. The system demonstrated electrical performance similar to that in the Pivotal trial,12 and no patient required a programming change due to pacing sensation not being tolerated. These results support the anatomical and physiological feasibility of the EV-ICD in Japanese patients, despite their smaller body size.

Although the time from implantation to discharge was longer (7.9 days) than noted in the Pivotal trial, it aligns with what is reported for other ICD types in Japan.10,29 On average, in the USA healthcare system patients are discharged within 1 day of implantation, compared to 8 days for Japan.29

The freedom from major system- and/or procedure-related complications at 12 months was 85.7% in this study, which was lower than in the Pivotal trial (91.9%).12 However, this lower rate was likely affected by the small size of the Japanese cohort, and all complications were resolved without any sequelae. The observed complications, including pericardial lead placement and lead rotation, were effectively managed without long-term sequelae. These findings highlight the importance of operator training and careful procedural planning to ensure optimal outcomes with this novel substernal approach.

Study Limitations

There are several to note. First, the sample size was small (n=14), which limits the ability to detect rare complications or thoroughly evaluate therapy outcomes. The study did not reach the 15 patients stated in the study design due to low enrollment during the early phase. Based on the enrollment status as of July 2023 and following regulatory consultation, the enrollment period was closed on November 30, 2023, with a total of 14 patients enrolled. Second, the follow-up period was limited to 12 months. Longer follow-up is needed to assess late lead-related complications, device-related infections, and long-term electrical performance. Third, no patient experienced appropriate ATP for sustained VT during follow-up. As a result, the performance of ATP in this cohort remains unclear.

Future Directions

To address the limitations of the present study, further research in larger real-world populations is warranted. A new post-market registry has been established in Japan, “Enlighten Japan”, which will evaluate the EV-ICD system in real-world clinical practice. The findings from this ongoing registry are expected to provide complementary evidence regarding long-term safety, efficacy, and device performance in Japanese patients.

Importantly, the Aurora EV-ICD (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA), commercially available and used in the global Enlighten study, incorporates the Smart Sense algorithm, designed to reduce inappropriate shocks due to P-wave oversensing, a leading cause of inappropriate therapy in the Pivotal trial.12,14,15 Retrospective simulation data suggest that Smart Sense may reduce inappropriate shock rates by up to 29% without compromising sensitivity.12 Although this algorithm was not evaluated in the present EV ICD Japan Study, its availability in current commercial devices may further improve outcomes in future Japanese cohorts.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that the EV-ICD system can be safely implanted and used in Japanese patients. Although the sample size was small and arrhythmic events were limited, the findings support the feasibility of the EV-ICD system in a Japanese population, particularly for patients who may benefit from avoiding a transvenous approach.
