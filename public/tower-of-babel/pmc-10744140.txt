Meniscal Repair Compared to Meniscectomy for Meniscal Injury: 3-year Outcomes from a Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Ergali Nabiyev, Arnat Baizakov, Khadisha Kashikova, Ramazan Askerov, Zhenisbek Baubekov, Zhassulan Argynbayev, Kuanysh Baikubesov
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMeniscal injury is a common problem that can lead to knee pain and dysfunction. Meniscal repair and meniscectomy are two treatment approaches for meniscal injury, but the latter may increase the risk of osteoarthritis. We aimed to compare the 3-year outcomes of a new method of meniscal suturing with meniscectomy among patients with meniscal injury. Methods This retrospective cohort study compared meniscal repair (treatment group) and meniscectomy (control group) in patients with meniscal injury. We evaluated the outcomes of 134 patients. under treatment with these approaches based on the Lysholm scale, which measures knee function and symptoms. The study used the chi-square test and the Mann-Whitney U test to compare the proportion of patients with different outcomes and the Lysholm scale scores between the treatment and control groups. The study also conducted subgroup analyses based on gender and age using the Mann-Whitney U test. The level of significance was set at P < 0.05 for all statistical tests.Results The treatment group had a higher proportion of patients with excellent results, although the difference was not statistically significant (17.2% in the treatment group vs. 10.0% in the control group, P = 0.223). However, a comparative analysis of the proportion of patients with good results revealed statistically significant differences, with 67.2% of patients in the treatment group achieving good outcomes compared to 45.7% in the control group (χ2 = 6,256, df = 1 P = 0.012, HR 1,470 95%CI 1,081-1,999). The average score on the Lysholm scale was significantly higher in the treatment group (87.48, 95% CI 85.1-89.7, SD = 9.2) compared to the control group (81.73, 95% CI 78.4-84.9, SD = 13.7) (U = 1609, Z = -2.813, P = 0.005). Subgroup analyses based on gender and age also showed significant differences in the Lysholm scale scores.ConclusionThe study demonstrates that meniscal repair is more effective than meniscectomy in improving patient outcomes, with a higher proportion of patients achieving excellent and good results and higher scores on the Lysholm scale. These findings support the use of meniscal repair as a preferred treatment approach for patients with meniscal injuries.

↑What is “already known” in this topic:

Meniscal injury is associated with long-term adverse joint-related outcomes. Meniscectomy, a common intervention following meniscal injury may increase the risk of knee osteoarthritis.

→What this article adds:

The novel oblique-vertical meniscus suturing technique may produce superior outcomes to meniscectomy in terms of patients’ functionality.

Introduction

In clinical practice, meniscal injury often accompanies anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries. Among patients with fresh ACL injuries, up to 45% experience damage to the medial meniscus, and up to 65% experience damage to the lateral meniscus (1-3). This is particularly common among the able-bodied population, including individuals involved in sports and physical work (4-6). The frequency of knee joint meniscus injury is 40-60 cases per 100,000 people per year, with the majority of cases being among men (4, 7).

Currently, there is no standardized approach for treating fresh meniscal ruptures, and there are no clear recommendations for the meniscal suturing technique based on the type of injury (7). However, preserving the menisci during surgery has been shown to slow down the progression of osteoarthritis in the knee joint and lead to favorable long-term functional outcomes (7, 8). Multiple studies have confirmed that stitching the knee joint menisci after injury is associated with desirable outcomes (9). The most commonly used approach for meniscal repair is vertical stitching of the meniscus. However, there is no evidence that this method is associated with optimal outcomes (7).

We developed a new arthroscopic stitching method for meniscal repair that involves vertical-oblique stitching in contrast to the classical vertical-only technique. In another article, we have shown that this method is theoretically superior to the classical method by obtaining a larger surface area and more optimal grip on the knee joint meniscal radial and circular fibers, and as a result, a stronger hold of the rupture. In this study, we aimed to compare the postoperative outcomes of the patients who underwent the new technique of meniscal repair compared to those who underwent meniscectomy.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study is based on the analysis of the outcomes of surgical treatment of 152 patients with knee joint meniscus injuries (KJ) who were treated in the Department of Orthopedic Surgery of the GCP at the PCV of the State Clinical Hospital No. 7 in Almaty in the period from 2017 to 2021. Informed consent for surgical treatment was obtained from all patients. The university’s local ethics committee approved the study.

Participants consisted of 16- to 63-year-old patients with isolated damage to one of the menisci involving horizontal, paracapsular, and complete/incomplete meniscal ruptures. The injury had to be their first knee injury and they did not have a previous history of knee surgery. They did not have osteoarthritis or any inflammatory process in the knee joint. Patients who were older than 63 years, those with associated anterior cruciate ligament injury, and history of knee injury/surgery were excluded from the study(a total of 6 patients), and patients whose follow-up was shorter than one year (12 patients).

Thus, 134 patients were included and divided into treatment and control groups based on their management approach. The treatment group underwent meniscal repair using the author-developed meniscus suture technique under arthroscopic control (n = 64) and the control group underwent meniscus resection using an arthroscopic technique; n = 70). The surgery did not involve interference with other structures of the knee joint in either of the study groups.

Surgical techniques

The knee joint assessment was performed under epidural anesthesia using an arthroscope from the German company Karl Storz. Anteromedial and anterolateral arthroscopic portals and a transpatellar auxiliary portal were installed. The standard one was used - anterior arthroscopy. The outlet of the patella was created longitudinally along the center of the patellar tendon.

Then, the KJ was examined, the menisci, ligaments, articular surfaces, and inversions were examined and palpated with a probe, and the extent and severity of damage to the meniscus were assessed (Figure 1 a). If the tear was in the “red” or “red-white” zone, had a vertical longitudinal alignment, and was smaller up to 10 mm, and in the absence of secondary degenerative changes and grade 2 osteoarthritis, a meniscal repair would be planned.

Surgical intervention was performed under general anesthesia. The patients of the treatment group underwent meniscus stitching according to a new method developed by the authors, for which the patent of the Republic of Kazakhstan for utility model No. 35413 dated 10.12.2021 (“Method of arthroscopic stitching of the meniscus of the knee joint”) was obtained (3).

After visualization of the meniscus rupture, its edges were refreshed using a meniscal rasp or a shaver in case a suture repair was deemed possible (Figure 1 b, Figure 2). If stitching was not possible, meniscus resection was performed.

Next, the spinal needle was carried out from the inside-out through the thickness of the meniscus – at the lower edge, through the joint capsules, and removed through a small incision of the skin (Figure 3). Non-absorbable suture material was inserted through the spinal needle (Figure 3). The end of the suture material was seized with a clamp and removed from the joint cavity.

Holding the suture material with a clamp, the spinal needle was pulled back into the knee joint cavity and removed from the meniscus (Figure 4).

Then the spinal needle with suture material was delivered out at 3 mm above and 3 mm away from the initial puncture, also through the thickness of the meniscus – at the upper edge (Figure 5). The suture material was inserted obliquely vertically at an angle of 45 to the axial line of the meniscus to form a diagonally vertical seam (Figure 6). The needle was removed through a small incision on the skin. The suture material was extracted from the knee joint cavity with a clamp, and the ends of the threads were tied with extracapsular under arthroscopic visualization of the meniscus rupture zone until its edges completely closed (Figure 6)

Thus, the oblique-vertical seam of the meniscus was formed. After that, additional stitches were applied the same way to the meniscus, depending on the rupture length. Then, we ensured the tension of the suture material in the joint cavity with arthroscopic techniques. The distances between the sutures were 5 mm (depending on the type of meniscus rupture The latter was done so that the extracapsular arrangement of the nodes eliminates the irritating effect of traumatization of the articular cartilage of the femoral condyle during movement, as observed when the nodes are located inside the joint cavity.

The distance between the seams (d) should be equal to the length of the seam (l) (Figure 7).

All patients in the control group underwent partial resection of the meniscus or meniscectomy using arthroscopic techniques. In the postoperative period, KJ was not immobilized; patients were prescribed physical therapy of the operated limb.

Patient Assessment

All patients underwent a clinical assessment of the general condition and the local status state to diagnose the damage and determine indications and contraindications for surgical treatment. We used clinical, X-ray, and MRI measures for the assessments.

Before hospitalization, all patients underwent standard X-rays in straight (anteroposterior) and lateral projections. Radiographs were used to assess the state of the bone structure and the relationship of the articular surfaces, identify the detachment of ligaments with the bone fragment, and determine degenerative changes in the KJ. During the preoperative period, all the patients underwent an MRI examination to allow an objective assessment of the structures of the knee joint ligaments, menisci, and cartilage.

Surgery-related outcomes were then assessed for 3 years based on the Lysholm scale (10). This scale focuses on how knee problems affect daily activities in areas such as limping, pain, using crutches, locking sensation, giving way, swelling, climbing stairs, and squatting. Higher scores on this scale represent better functional ability. Table 1 presents the interpretation of the results on this scale (10). Other outcomes included the duration of surgery and the duration of hospital stay.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical data processing was carried out using the statistical program SPSS Inc version 20, Chicago, Illinois, USA, according to generally accepted standards. Comparison of qualitative variables of 2 subgroups was carried out using the Pearson Chi-squared criterion based on conjugation tables for four-field tables with the calculation of relative risk. Comparison of three subgroups with the analysis of the Kendall Tau criterion and subsequent a posteriori comparison. A comparison of quantitative variables given the abnormal distribution was carried out using the Mann-Whitney test. The critical significance level for comparing two subgroups was less than 0.05, and for three, less than 0.017.

Results

Patients’ Characteristics

Data from 134 patients was analyzed. A total of 18 patients were excluded due to missing data on follow-up outcomes and features in the exclusion criteria. The patients’ characteristics in the included cohort are presented in Table 2.

The treatment group included 64 (47.7%) patients with KJ meniscal ruptures. The control group consisted of 70 (52.3%) patients with long-standing ruptures of the KJ menisci. The patients of the treatment and control groups were comparable in terms of age, gender, nature, and causes of injuries which is the basis for a comparative analysis of the outcomes of surgical treatment. Patients’ characteristics in each of the study groups are presented in Table 3.

Patients’ Outcomes

The mean score on the Lysholm scale was significantly higher in the treatment group (87.48, 95% CI 85.1-89.7, SD = 9.2) compared to the control group (81.73, 95% CI 78.4-84.9, SD = 13.7) (U = 1609, Z = -2.813, P = 0.005). The treatment group had a significantly higher proportion of patients with good results (67.2% vs. 45.7% in the control group, χ2 = 6.25, df = 1, P = 0.012, HR 1.47, 95% CI 1.081-1.99). The majority of patients in the treatment group (84.38%) had good to excellent scores on the Lysholm scale compared to 55.71% in the control group. The comparative distribution of outcomes of the study groups is shown in Figure 8.

Among individual subgroups, men in the treatment group had a significantly higher score on the Lysholm scale compared to men in the control group (U = 919,500, Z = -2.527, P = 0.012), while there were no significant differences among women (P = 0.146).

Comparative analysis by age subgroups showed significant differences in the score on the Lysholm scale for patients under 20 years old (U = 10,500, Z = -2.062, P = 0.039) and patients aged 20 to 30 years (U = 365,500, Z = -2.759, P = 0.006).

The median length of surgery and duration of hospital stay were also significantly lower in the treatment group compared to the control group (Table 4).

Discussion

The present study aimed to compare the 3-year outcomes of meniscal repair (treatment group) and meniscectomy (control group) in patients with meniscal injuries. The main results showed that the treatment group had significantly higher scores on the Lysholm scale, a higher proportion of patients with good results, and a higher proportion of patients with excellent results compared to the control group. Additionally, the treatment group had shorter median lengths of surgery and duration of hospital stay.

The higher scores on the Lysholm scale and the higher proportion of patients with good and excellent results in the treatment group can be attributed to the use of a reliable new arthroscopic suture. This method of stitching the KJ menisci allowed for the avoidance of contractures and synovitis, leading to earlier restoration of the amplitude of movement of the operated joint and the patient's ability to work six weeks after arthroscopy. In contrast, the control group experienced a decrease in the number of excellent and good treatment outcomes due to delayed rehabilitation, the development of contractures, and synovitis in the KJ.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies that have reported better outcomes for meniscal repair compared to meniscectomy. A systematic review found that meniscal repair was associated with better long-term outcomes, including improved knee function and a lower risk of osteoarthritis, compared to meniscectomy (11). Similarly, a study showed that meniscal repair resulted in better knee function and a lower risk of subsequent knee surgery compared to meniscectomy (12).

Our subgroup analysis showed that men in the treatment group had significantly higher scores on the Lysholm scale compared to men in the control group, while there were no significant differences among women. This sex difference in outcomes may be due to differences in the biomechanics of the knee joint between men and women,

The analysis of individual subgroups in the current study revealed that men in the treatment group had a significantly higher score on the Lysholm scale compared to men in the control group, while there were no significant differences among women (1). This gender difference in outcomes has been reported in previous studies as well. A study found that men had better outcomes than women after meniscal repair, possibly due to differences in knee biomechanics and muscle strength as well as differences in the type and extent of meniscal injury between men and women. Also, the relatively smaller number of women with meniscal injuries may have been the reason for positive results in this subgroup (7).

Furthermore, the analysis by age subgroups revealed significant differences in the score on the Lysholm scale for patients under 20 years old and patients aged 20 to 30 years. This suggests that younger patients may benefit more from meniscal repair compared to meniscectomy, as they have a higher potential for tissue healing and regeneration. This is consistent with the findings of a study that showed that younger patients had better outcomes after meniscal repair (13). However, these findings can also be explained given the greater number of patients in these age groups which increases the statistical power for these groups.

Despite the strengths and consistency of the study's findings, several methodological limitations should be considered. First, the incompleteness of outcome data and unavailability of patients who were lost to follow-up may have introduced bias and confounding by indication. Additionally, the methods of patient assignment may have led to confounding by indication, as patients in the treatment group may have been selected based on factors that could influence their outcomes. These limitations should be taken into account when interpreting the study's results and considering their implications for clinical practice.

It is important to note that the method of meniscal repair used herein was novel and this study provides the initial evidence for its use. Further research is needed to assess the long-term comparative effects of the new vertical oblique suturing technique(introduced herein) with the classical vertical suturing for meniscal repair, ideally with a randomized controlled design. Also, outcomes like the rate of osteoarthritis require a longer follow-up which may be feasible in future studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, meniscal repair was associated with better outcomes, including higher scores on the Lysholm scale and a higher proportion of patients with good and excellent results, compared to meniscectomy. Thus, our study provides preliminary evidence for the benefits and safety of the novel suturing technique for meniscus repair. Also, our study supports the use of meniscal repair as a preferred treatment option for patients with meniscal injuries.
