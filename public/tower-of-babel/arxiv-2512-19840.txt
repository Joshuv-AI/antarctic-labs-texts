Quantum Mechanics on Lie Groups:
I. Noncommutative Fourier Transforms

Abstract




Abstract.
Starting from square-integrable wave functions on a Lie group, we build an invertible Fourier transform mapping them on wave functions on the dual of the Lie algebra. This is a group-theoretic version of the map from position space to momentum space, with generally noncommuting momenta owing to the group structure. As a result, the multiplication of momentum-dependent functions involves star products, which makes the construction of noncommutative Fourier series much more involved than that of their commutative cousin. We show that our formalism provides an isometry of Hilbert spaces, and use it to derive a noncommutative Poisson summation formula for any compact Lie group. This is a key preliminary for the computation of Wigner functions and path integrals for quantum systems on group manifolds.

Momentum space dual to a Lie group.

There are two obvious answers to what ‘momentum space’ means when ‘position space’ is a Lie group
G

[

46

It is thus tempting to call ‘momentum space’ the (discrete) set of such matrix elements and irreducible representations, in the same way that Fourier modes on a circle are labelled by an integer. The advantage of this approach is that Fourier series are explicitly given by the Peter-Weyl theorem; its drawback is that it appears naturally neither for Wigner functions nor for path integrals. Indeed, the latter call instead for the classical phase space
T^{*}G

[
13

14

There is nothing wrong with either choice; each has advantages and drawbacks that depend on context. For the case of Wigner functions, path integrals and their (semi)classical limits, the most appealing approach is the second one: to call ‘momentum space’ the dual space

2
Operator algebras on Lie groups

In this section, we construct the operator algebra of quantum mechanics on Lie groups through canonical quantization. We first review the Poisson bracket of functions on the phase space
T^{*}G
, then build an operator algebra by ‘putting hats’ on functions, and replacing Poisson brackets by commutators. The key subtlety resides in the fact that momentum operators fail to commute. This calls for an ordering prescription, which we choose to be symmetric.

2.1
Poisson brackets

Consider a mechanical system whose configuration space is a Lie group
G
, with elements
g
,
h
, etc. Call
\mathfrak{g}
the Lie algebra of
G
, viewed as the space of left-invariant vector fields on
G
; denote Lie algebra elements as
X
,
Y
, etc. Let
\exp:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow G
be the exponential map, understood as the time-1 flow of such vector fields starting from the identity. For later use, choose a basis
\{t_{i}|i=1,...,n\}
of
\mathfrak{g}
, so that each
t_{i}
gives rise to a Lie derivative operator
{\cal L}_{i}:={\cal L}_{t_{i}}
acting on functions on
G
. The Lie bracket of generators reads
[t_{i},t_{j}]=c_{ij}{{}^{k}}t_{k}
, with structure constants
c_{{i}j}{}^{k}
and implicit summation over repeated indices.

Phase space.

The corresponding phase space is the cotangent bundle
T^{*}G
, which turns out to be trivial: it is globally equivalent to the direct product
G\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, where
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is the dual vector space of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. (See
e.g.

[
52
, Appendix A]
for the proof of this equivalence.) We denote ‘momenta’ as
p,q\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
and write each of them as
p=p_{i}(t^{i})^{*}
in terms of the dual basis such that
\langle(t^{i})^{*},t_{j}\rangle=\delta^{i}_{j}
.
2
2

2

The pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is not a scalar product: it is just the pairing between a vector space and its dual, here
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. Scalar products of wave functions will instead be denoted
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
.
The phase space is endowed with a symplectic form whose inverse defines the Poisson bracket of functions on
G\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, namely

\displaystyle\{A,B\}=\mathcal{L}_{i}A\frac{\partial B}{\partial p_{i}}-\frac{\partial A}{\partial p_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{i}B-c_{ij}{}^{k}\frac{\partial A}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial B}{\partial p_{j}}p_{k}

(2.1)

for any two functions
A(g,p)
and
B(g,p)
. In particular, this applies to functions locally given by coordinates
g^{i}
on
G
and momentum components
p_{i}
on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, for which
3
3

3

Coordinate functions are typically not globally smooth on
G
. The brackets (
2.2
) involving

s are smooth.

\displaystyle\{g^{i},g^{j}\}=0,\qquad\{g^{j},p_{i}\}=\mathcal{L}_{i}g^{j},\qquad\{p_{i},p_{j}\}=-c_{ij}{}^{k}p_{k}.

(2.2)

We denote by
C^{\infty}(G\times\mathfrak{g}^{*})
the Poisson algebra of (smooth, complex-valued) functions on
G\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
endowed with the Poisson bracket (
2.1
). The key difference between this algebra and the more standard one of functions on
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
is that momenta fail to commute in (
2.2
). Put differently, momentum space is not a Lagrangian submanifold of phase space, so it admits no geometric quantization
[
53
]
. This is the main difficulty in dealing with quantum mechanics on Lie groups.

Principal branch coordinates on
G
.

Our aim is to quantize the Poisson algebra of functions on
G\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. In doing so, we will face the problem of defining ‘position operators’, which is complicated by the fact that
G
need not admit global coordinates. We therefore rely on the Lie algebra and the exponential map to define coordinates on the group. Namely, define the
principal branch
of the logarithm to be the open set in
\mathfrak{g}
that contains
0
, for which the exponential is both injective and dense in
G
, and that is ‘symmetric’ so that if
X
belongs to the principal branch, then
-X
does as well; see fig.
1
. Coordinates
X^{i}(g)
on
G
are then chosen such that
g=\exp(X^i(g)t_i)
in terms of the basis
\{t_{i}|i=1,...,n\}
introduced above. By construction, these coordinates are dense in
G
, so they uniquely label almost any
g\in G
. They are such that the identity
e\in G
sits at the origin
X^{i}(e)=0
, and such that the inverse of an element in
G
is the opposite vector in coordinates:
X^{i}(g^{-1})=-X^{i}(g)
. Furthermore, they satisfy

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}X^{j}=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial{Y^{i}}}\right|_{y=0}B\big(Y^{k}t_{k},X^{\ell}t_{\ell}\big)^{j},

(2.3)

where
B(Y,X)
denotes the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff expansion. The latter is defined so that, for any two Lie algebra elements
X,Y
, one has
e^{B(X,Y)}:=e^{X}e^{Y}
in the (closure of the) universal enveloping algebra of
\mathfrak{g}
, which explicitly yields the usual series
[
54
]

\displaystyle B(Y,X)=Y+X+\frac{1}{2}[Y,X]+\frac{1}{12}\left([Y,[Y,X]]-[X,[X,Y]]\right)+\cdots.

(2.4)

As a special case of eq. (
2.3
) note that
\mathcal{L}_{i}X^{j}(e)=\delta_{i}^{j}
at the identity, which will be useful below. We stress that (
2.4
) does not rely on any exponential map from the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
to the group
G
: the definition of
B(X,Y)
is purely algebraic and solely relies on commutation relations of
\mathfrak{g}
used in the universal enveloping algebra.

Time evolution.

We will not be concerned with quantum dynamics here
[
40
]
, but it is worth briefly recalling their classical version. As in any Hamiltonian system, the time evolution of a function on
G\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is given by its Poisson bracket with a Hamiltonian function
H(g,p)
. This leads to a noncommutative cousin of the standard Hamilton equations of classical mechanics on
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
: given a path
(g_{t},p_{t})
in phase space, it solves the equations of motion if

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g\,g^{-1}

\displaystyle=\partial_{p}H,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p+\mathrm{ad}^{*}_{\partial_{t}gg^{-1}}p

\displaystyle=-R_{g}^{*}\partial_{g}H.

(2.6)

Let us unpack each term. On the left-hand side of (
2.5
),
\partial_{t}g\,g^{-1}
is a Lie algebra element given by the (right) Maurer-Cartan form on
G
acting on the tangent vector
\partial_{t}g
at
g
. On the right-hand side of (
2.5
),
\partial_{p}H
is the exterior derivative of
H(g,p)
in momentum space,
i.e.
the differential of the function
H(g,\cdot)
on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. It is also a Lie algebra element, since
\partial_{p}H
is a linear form on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
,
i.e.
an element of
(\mathfrak{g}^{*})^{*}=\mathfrak{g}
. On the left-hand side of (
2.6
),
\mathrm{ad}^{*}
denotes the coadjoint representation of
\mathfrak{g}
, defined by
\langle\mathrm{ad}^{*}_{X}(p),Y\rangle:=-\langle p,[X,Y]\rangle
for any
X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}
and any
p\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. Finally, on the right-hand side of (
2.6
),
\partial_{g}H
is the exterior derivative of
H(g,p)
in position space,
i.e.
the differential of the function
H(\cdot,p)
on
G
. What appears in (
2.6
) is the pullback of this differential to the identity by right multiplication
R_{g}:G\to G:h\mapsto hg
.

In the special case where
H(g,p)=H(p)
only depends on momenta, we refer to eqs. (
2.5
)–(
2.6
) as
Lie-Poisson equations
. These reduce to Euler-Arnold equations in the even more restricted case where
H(p)
is a quadratic function, whereupon the solution
g_{t}
of (
2.5
) is a geodesic in
G
with respect to a left-invariant metric. As mentioned in section
1
, such dynamical systems are ubiquitous in physics; see
e.g.

[
13
,
14
,
3
]
for numerous examples. The goal of the present work is to set the stage for the quantization of Lie-Poisson systems, with a view towards quantum liquids
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
.

2.2
From Poisson brackets to operator algebras

We now define the abstract operator algebra meant to describe the canonical quantization of the Poisson algebra of functions on
G\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. Representations of the operator algebra on actual Hilbert spaces will be provided in section
3
.

Operator algebra and quantization prescription.

We wish to find operators mimicking the ‘position’ and ‘momentum’ operators of quantum mechanics. The latter are readily obtained by quantizing the momentum components
p_{i}
introduced above (
2.2
), which thus become operators
\hat{p}_{i}
. For position operators, we use the principal branch coordinates defined above eq. (
2.3
). Any function on
G
can thus be seen as a function on the principal branch, as
f(g)=f(X^{i}(g))
. We then promote the coordinate functions
X^{i}(g)
to operators
\hat{X}^{i}
and define abstract operators
\hat{f}=f(\hat{X}^{i})
. Owing to the Poisson brackets (
2.2
), this quantization must be such that the following commutators hold:
4
4

4

Here and below, we use units such that
\hbar=1
.

[\hat{X}^{i},\hat{X}^{j}]=0,\qquad[\hat{X}^{j},\hat{p}_{i}]=i\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{i}X^{j}},\qquad[\hat{p}_{i},\hat{p}_{j}]=-ic_{ij}{}^{k}\hat{p}_{k}.

(2.7)

The main complication compared with quantum mechanics on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
appears once again in the noncommutative momenta. We denote by
{\cal A}
the abstract algebra spanned by the
\hat{p}_{i}
s and the
\hat{X}^{j}
s with commutators (
2.7
). Let also
{\cal A}_{\text{pos}}
and
{\cal A}_{\text{mom}}
be the subalgebras of
\cal A
that respectively correspond to
C^{\infty}(G)
and
C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
. The subalgebra
{\cal A}_{\text{mom}}
, in particular, is (isomorphic to the closure of) the universal enveloping algebra of
\mathfrak{g}
owing to the last commutator in eqs. (
2.7
).

To be more precise, the procedure of ‘putting hats on functions’ means that we assume the existence of a linear quantization map

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}:C^{\infty}(G\times\mathfrak{g}^{*})\rightarrow{\cal A},A\mapsto\hat{A}:=\mathcal{Q}(A)

(2.8)

such that
\mathcal{Q}(\bar{A})=(\mathcal{Q}(A))^{\dagger}
, with
\hat{X}^{i}:={\cal Q}(X^{i})
and
\hat{p}_{i}:={\cal Q}(p_{i})
. This map prescribes an ordering for operators. For example, the classical function
p_{2}p_{1}=p_{1}p_{2}
can be quantized to
\hat{p}_{1}\hat{p}_{2}
,
\hat{p}_{2}\hat{p}_{1}
or
\tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{p}_{1}\hat{p}_{2}+\hat{p}_{2}\hat{p}_{1})
, each of which is valid, yet different from the others. What is unusual here, compared with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, is that the ordering of momenta matters. We specifically choose this ordering to be symmetric among the
p_{i}
s:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(p_{1}\dots p_{n}):=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\text{Sym}(n)}\hat{p}_{\sigma(1)}\dots\hat{p}_{\sigma(n)}.

(2.9)

(Another common choice of quantization for momentum operators is the Duflo map, deferred to appendix
A
.) Of course, the full quantization map (
2.8
) also relies on a choice of ordering for products of
X^{i}
s and
p_{i}
s, as in the standard Euclidean case. We assume that a choice of ordering has been made, but its details do not matter.

Exponential operators and branches of the logarithm.

The key virtue of the prescription (
2.9
) is to simplify computations that involve exponentials of momentum operators, which are crucial for Fourier transforms. Indeed, given any Lie algebra element
X\in\mathfrak{g}
, the quantization (
2.9
) maps the exponential function
p\mapsto e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}
on the exponential operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(e^{-i\langle\cdot,X\rangle})=e^{-i\langle\hat{p},X\rangle},

(2.10)

where the expansion of the exponential on the right-hand side contains all possible permutations of the
\hat{p}_{i}
s. Such operators should provide a (possibly projective) representation of
G
upon identifying
g=\exp(X)
. (Think
e.g.
of representations of SU(2), where exponential operators act as unitary rotations on any Hilbert space.) This is justified by the last commutator in eqs. (
2.7
), which implies that the product of two such operators reads

\displaystyle e^{-i\langle\hat{p},X\rangle}e^{-i\langle\hat{p},Y\rangle}=e^{-i\langle\hat{p},B(X,Y)\rangle},

(2.11)

where
B(X,Y)
is given by the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff expansion (
2.4
). We will denote by
\cal E
the group of exponential operators (
2.10
), seen as a subset of
{\cal A}_{\text{mom}}
.

The issue, though, is that the group
G
may have compact subgroups. As a consequence, the Lie algebra element
B(X,Y)
in (
2.4
) may be a nonzero logarithm of the identity:
\exp(B(X,Y))=e\in G
even though
B(X,Y)\neq 0
. In order for exponential operators (
2.10
) to yield a representation of
G
when acting on a Hilbert space, one needs to constrain the operator algebra that is meant to be quantized in the first place. Namely, consider the normal subgroup
\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{E}
consisting of operators that act as the identity when exponential operators represent the group:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}:=\Big\{e^{-i\langle\hat{p},X\rangle}\Big|X\in\mathfrak{g}\,\text{~such that }\exp(X)=e\Big\}\subset\cal E.

(2.12)

This is the ‘identity subset’ of exponential operators. It is designed so that the group
G
can be identified with the quotient
G\cong\mathcal{E}/\mathcal{I}
, in the same way that
\text{U(1)}\cong\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. Its elements are the logarithms of the identity. One may think that this is the same as labelling the different branches of the logarithm from
\mathfrak{g}
to
G
, but we will see that this is not so: the set (
2.12
) typically overcounts these branches significantly.

The identity subgroup (
2.12
) will appear extensively in this work, so let us describe it carefully. For any
g\in G
, let

\operatorname{Logs}(g):=\{X\in\mathfrak{g}|\exp(X)=g\}\subset\mathfrak{g}

(2.13)

be the set of its logarithms. Then one can write

\mathcal{I}\cong\operatorname{Logs}(e)=\{X\in\mathfrak{g}|\exp(X)=e\}\subset\mathfrak{g},

(2.14)

so characterizing
\cal I
requires a good grasp on elements of
\mathfrak{g}
that exponentiate to the identity. To this end, let
T\cong(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{r}
be a maximal torus of
G
, denote by
\mathfrak{t}\subset\mathfrak{g}
its Lie algebra, and let
\{a_{i}|i=1,...,r\}
be a basis of
\mathfrak{t}
such that
\exp(2\pi a_i)=e
but
\exp(\theta a_i)\neq e
for all
\theta\in(0,2\pi)
. The elements of
\mathfrak{t}\cong\mathbb{R}^{r}
that exponentiate to the identity are readily described in these terms:
X\in\mathfrak{t}
exponentiates to the identity if and only if
X=2\pi n^{i}a_{i}
for some integers
n^{1},...,n^{r}
.

5

\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}:x\mapsto e^{ix}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\bigcup_{g\in G}\bigcup_{n^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}\mathrm{Ad}_{g}(2\pi n^{i}a_{i})=\bigcup_{n^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}2\pi n^{i}\mathcal{O}_{a_{i}}\subset\mathfrak{g},

where
\mathcal{O}_{a_{i}}:=\{\text{Ad}_{g}(a_{i})|g\in G\}

\text{Ad}_{g}(X):=gXg^{-1}

By contrast, the set of logarithms (
2.13
) of a generic group element
g\in G
is discrete. This is because almost no element of
G
is fixed by conjugation, in contrast to the identity; the only exceptions are elements in the center of
G
. Again, it will be essential for later purposes to describe the set (
2.13
) explicitly, so let us do it here. Let
X\in\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra element that does not exponentiate to a central element in
G
, and let
T\cong(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{r}
be the maximal torus passing through
\exp(X)
. The Lie algebra of
T
then contains
X
, so let
\{a_{i}(X)|i=1,\dots,r\}
be a basis of
\mathfrak{t}
such that
X\propto a_{1}(X)
, with
\exp(2\pi a_i(X))=e
but
\exp(\theta a_i(X))\neq e
for all
\theta\in(0,2\pi)
. Since
X
is generic, all the logarithms of
\exp(X)
can be expressed as discrete translations along the Lie algebra of
T
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Logs}(\exp(X))=\{X+2\pi n^{i}a_{i}(X)\,|\,n^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

(2.16)

It follows that
|\operatorname{Logs}(X)|=|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}
for almost any
X\in\mathfrak{g}
, and that the branches of the logarithm are labeled by
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
. This will repeatedly be useful below.

Figure 2
:
Logarithms of the identity in the
\mathfrak{u}(1)
Lie algebra (left) and the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
Lie algebra (right). In the U(1) case, logarithms of the identity form a one-dimensional lattice of
2\pi
-separated points in the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{u}(1)\cong\mathbb{R}
. This is because the exponential is
\exp:x\mapsto e^{ix}
. In the SU(2) case, we take the exponential map to be
\exp:\vec{X}\mapsto e^{i\vec{X}\cdot\vec{\sigma}}
in terms of Pauli matrices (see section
5
). With this convention, the logarithms of the identity in
\mathfrak{su}(2)\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}
are concentric spheres with radii
2\pi n
,
n=0,1,2,...
, as predicted in general by eq. (
2.15
).

Quotienting the operator algebra.

In order to ensure that eq. (
2.11
) represents the group
G
, which is generally compact, one needs to identify operators in
\cal A
that differ from each other by the insertion of elements of the identity subset (
2.12
) of exponential operators. This can be achieved by requiring that the algebra be blind to elements of
\mathcal{I}
, in the sense of identifying
\hat{I}\hat{A}
and
\hat{A}\hat{I}
for any operator
\hat{A}\in\cal A
and any
\hat{I}\in\cal I
. In such a reduced algebra, any element in
\mathcal{I}
commutes with all operators, and becomes (proportional to) the identity.

Let us again make this more precise, for it will be essential in understanding the difference between the two pairs of Fourier-dual representations built in section
3
. Defining the two-sided ideal
{\cal A}_{\mathcal{I}}:=\big\langle\{\hat{I}\hat{A}\hat{I}^{-1}-\hat{A}\,\big|\hat{A}\in{\cal A},~\hat{I}\in\mathcal{I}\}\big\rangle
of
\cal A
, use it to quotient the operator algebra, thereby obtaining
{\cal A}/{\cal A}_{\cal I}
. The latter is isomorphic to the subalgebra of operators that are invariant under conjugation by
\mathcal{I}
. This is best seen by viewing the quotient
{\cal A}/{\cal A}_{\cal I}
as isomorphic to the image of the projector
{\cal P}_{\mathcal{I}}
that averages over conjugations by elements of
\mathcal{I}
:

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{\mathcal{I}}(\hat{A}):=\frac{1}{|{\cal I}|}\sum_{\hat{I}\in\mathcal{I}}\hat{I}\hat{A}\hat{I}^{-1}.

(2.17)

Written in this way, the projector is ill-defined because
|{\cal I}|:=\operatorname{Vol}(\mathcal{I})
is infinite. One can regularize the sum (
2.17
) by introducing an ‘infrared’ cutoff
\Lambda
, defining the set
\mathcal{I}_{\Lambda}:=\{e^{-i\langle\hat{p},X\rangle}|\,X\in\mathcal{I}\text{ such that}\|X\|\leq\Lambda\}
where the norm is taken with respect to a metric of
\mathfrak{g}
, and defining a projector
{\cal P}_{\mathcal{I}_{\Lambda}}(\hat{A}):=\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{\Lambda}|}\sum_{\hat{I}\in\mathcal{I}_{\Lambda}}\hat{I}\hat{A}\hat{I}^{-1}
. Then the projector (
2.17
) is the limit of
{\cal P}_{\mathcal{I}_{\Lambda}}
as
\|\Lambda\|\to\infty
. Similar regularizations will appear repeatedly below, but they are to be expected when dealing with operators and Hilbert spaces on quotient manifolds.

As a closing remark, note that the algebra
{\cal A}
defined in eqs. (
2.7
) contains no information on the global structure of the group
G
, since coordinate functions are only locally defined. We will soon see that taking the quotient
\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{I}}
effectively reinstates the global structure of the group in the operator algebra.

3
Position and momentum representations

The goal of this section is to represent the operator algebras
{\cal A}
and
{\cal A}/{\cal A}_{\mathcal{I}}
, respectively introduced around eqs. (
2.7
) and (
2.17
), on suitable Hilbert spaces. The plan is as follows. We begin by recalling the well-known position representation on
L^{2}(G)
, then turn to the momentum representation in a noncommutative version of
L^{2}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
. As we shall see, the latter cannot be isomorphic to the position representation, which is ‘smaller’ due to the fact that
G
may have compact subgroups. There are two ways out, which we explore in turn: one is to make the momentum representation smaller, which amounts to imposing
\mathcal{I}
-invariance of states in momentum space; this corresponds to having periodic functions in
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. The other way is to enlarge
L^{2}(G)
by considering instead the space
L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
, whose elements are not
\mathcal{I}
-invariant in general. Relating these representations of
{\cal A}
and
{\cal A}/{\cal A}_{\mathcal{I}}
will eventually lead to noncommutative Fourier transforms and series in section
4
.

3.1
Small position representation

We start by trying to represent the operator algebra
\cal A
in the most straightforward way, on the Hilbert space
L^{2}(G)
of square-integrable wave functions on
G

[
34
]
. This Hilbert space is actually obtained by geometric quantization of the cotangent bundle
T^{*}G
in real polarization
[
53
]
.

Hilbert space
L^{2}(G)
.

We denote wave functions on
G
by lowercase Greek letters
\phi
,
\psi
, etc. Their scalar product is

\displaystyle\langle\phi|\psi\rangle_{G}:=\int_{G}\differential g\;\overline{\phi(g)}\psi(g)=\!\!\!\!\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{principal}\\
\text{branch in $\mathfrak{g}$}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!J(X)\differential^{n}X\,\bar{\phi}(X)\psi(X),

(3.1)

where
\differential g
is the left Haar measure on
G
while
\differential^{n}X
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathfrak{g}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
in principal branch coordinates defined above (
2.3
). We write
J(X)
for the corresponding Jacobian of the Haar measure, namely
[
55
]
(see also
[
56
]
)

\displaystyle J(X)=\det\left(\frac{1-e^{-\mathrm{ad}_{X}}}{\mathrm{ad}_{X}}\right),

(3.2)

where
\mathrm{ad}_{X}:=[X,\cdot\,]
is the adjoint action by
X\in\mathfrak{g}
. This Jacobian will appear repeatedly below, any time an integral over
G
is expressed in principal branch coordinates. If
G
is unimodular (left and right Haar measures coincide), one has
\operatorname{Tr}(\mathrm{ad}_{X})=0
and eq. (
3.2
) can be recast as
J(X)=\frac{\sinh(\mathrm{ad}_X/2)}{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{ad}_{X}}
, in which case the Jacobian is an even function of
X
. We will exploit this in section
5
to derive non-Abelian Poisson summation formulas.

Regular representation of
G
.

The action of position and momentum operators on a wave function is now defined by the
position representation
that stems from the left regular representation of
G
:

\displaystyle(\hat{X}^{j}\psi)(g):=X^{j}(g)\psi(g),\qquad(\hat{p}_{j}\psi)(g):=-i(\mathcal{L}_{j}\psi)(g).

(3.3)

This enforces the commutation relations (
2.7
) with operators
\hat{X}^{i}
,
\hat{p}_{i}
that are Hermitian, as they should be. Strictly speaking, the action of position operators
\hat{X}^{j}
in (
3.3
) is ill-defined because the coordinates
X^{j}(g)
only make sense locally. However, the action of any ‘potential operator’
\hat{V}={\cal Q}(V(g))
is well defined and reads
(\hat{V}\psi)(g):=V(g)\psi(g)
, where
V(g)
is any smooth function on
G
.

As for momenta, having
\hat{p}_{i}
act as a derivation along
t_{i}
in (
3.3
) ensures that its exponential acts by the flow of the vector field
t_{i}
,
i.e.
by left multiplication of the argument of a wave function. This is indeed to say that the second equation in (
3.3
) is the infinitesimal counterpart of the left regular representation of
G
. As a result, exponential operators (
2.10
) yield a representation (
2.11
) of
G
via
(e^{-i\langle\hat{p},Y\rangle}\cdot\psi)(g)=(e^{-\mathcal{L}_{Y}}\psi)(g)=\psi(\exp(Y)g)
. It follows that the identity subset (
2.14
) acts trivially in this representation,
i.e.

(e^{-i\langle\hat{p},Y\rangle}\psi)(g)=\psi(\exp(Y)g)=\psi(eg)=\psi(g)

(3.4)

whenever
\exp(Y)=e
. Any wave function is thus trivially
\cal I
-invariant. In other words, it is really the quotient space
{\cal A}/{\cal A}_{\cal I}
that acts in the position representation (
3.3
). As we will see in subsection
3.4
, the projection (
2.17
) of position operators
P_{\mathcal{\mathcal{I}}}\cdot\mathcal{A}_{\text{pos}}\subset\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{I}}
leads to
bona fide
potential operators that act in a well-defined way, in contrast to
e.g.

\hat{X}^{j}\in\mathcal{A}
.

In most cases below, it will be clear that
\hat{A}\psi(g)
denotes the action of
\hat{A}\in{\cal A}/{\cal A}_{\cal I}
on the function
\psi\in L^{2}(G)
in the position representation. In places where confusion may occur, we will explicitly denote the representation map by
\rho_{\text{pos}}^{\mathcal{I}}:{\cal A}/{\cal A}_{\cal I}\rightarrow\operatorname{End}(L^{2}(G))
, meaning that
(\hat{A}\psi)(g):=(\rho_{\text{pos}}^{\mathcal{I}}(\hat{A})\psi)(g)
. The
\mathcal{I}
superscript is added here to stress that one is representing the projected operator algebra
\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{I}}
. By contrast, we will define in section
3.4
a ‘larger’ position representation
\rho_{\text{pos}}
, without superscript, where the full algebra
\mathcal{A}
acts on wave functions.

3.2
Large momentum representation and star product

Here we introduce an action of position and momentum operators on wave functions that live on the entire momentum space
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
,
i.e.
on the dual of the Lie algebra of
G
. In particular, this will involve a noncommutative action of momenta. It will quickly be apparent that the resulting Hilbert space is much larger than
L^{2}(G)
, so modifications will be needed to relate the two. These are addressed separately in sections
3.3
–
3.4
.

Momentum operators.

The momentum representation of the operator algebra
\cal A
should act on functions
\Phi(p)
,
\Psi(p)
, etc. that depend on momentum
p\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. However, naively defining
(\hat{p}_{i}\Phi)(p)=p_{i}\Phi(p)
fails to reproduce the last commutator in eqs. (
2.7
), so one needs to deform the multiplication law on the space of functions on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}

[
48
]
. This is achieved by a star product
\star
that replaces pointwise multiplication, such that

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{i}\Phi(p)=p_{i}\star\Phi(p)\qquad\text{and}\qquad p_{i}\star p_{j}-p_{j}\star p_{i}=-ic_{ij}{}^{k}p_{k}.

(3.5)

Not all ambiguities in the definition of the star product are fixed by the last commutators in eqs. (
2.7
), but those ambiguities that remain are fixed by the choice of quantization map (
2.8
). Indeed, let
\hat{A}=\mathcal{Q}(A)
be the quantization of a function
A(p)
; then the action of
\hat{A}
on a function
\Phi
in the momentum representation is given by
(\hat{A}\Phi)(p)=A(p)\star\Phi(p)
.

The star product of functions in momentum space is thus fixed by one’s choice of momentum quantization scheme. As in the Moyal case
[
29
]
, it reads

\displaystyle A\star B:=\mathcal{Q}_{\text{mom}}^{-1}(\hat{A}\hat{B}),\qquad\forall A,B\in C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{g}^{*}),

(3.6)

where
\mathcal{Q}_{\text{mom}}
is the quantization map (
2.8
) restricted to functions that only depend on momenta. For the symmetric ordering in (
2.9
), eq. (
3.6
) defines the so-called Gutt star product on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}

[
57
]
. One readily deduces from (
3.6
) that the star product is well-behaved under conjugation:

\displaystyle\overline{A\star B}=\bar{B}\star\bar{A}.

(3.7)

To avoid confusion we will sometimes stress the variable of the star product by a subscript, as in
e.g.

\Phi(p)\star\Psi(p)=\Phi(p)\star_{p}\Psi(p)
.

Using the fact that exponential functions are quantized to exponential operators (
2.10
) and the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff property (
2.11
) of momentum operators, star products of plane waves read

\displaystyle e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}\star e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}=e^{-i\langle p,B(X,Y)\rangle}.

(3.8)

This feature is essential because plane waves provide a basis of the space of functions of
p
, so knowing how the star product behaves on them suffices for many proofs.

Position operators.

Position operators need to commute and to be canonically conjugate to momentum operators, as in eqs. (
2.7
). As in standard quantum mechanics, one may define

\displaystyle(\hat{X}^{i}\Phi)(p):=i\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial p_{i}}(p).

(3.9)

Statement.
The
momentum representation
of
\cal A
defined in eqs. (
3.5
) and (
3.9
) reproduces the commutators (
2.7
).

Proof.
The vanishing commutator
[\hat{X}^{i},\hat{X}^{j}]=0
trivially holds by the definition (
3.9
), and the star product in (
3.5
) guarantees that the last commutator of eqs. (
2.7
) holds as well. Thus, it suffices to focus on the second commutator in eqs. (
2.7
), namely
[\hat{X}^{j},\hat{p}_{i}]=i\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{i}X^{j}}
. The latter is true, as an operator acting on all functions of
p
, if and only if it holds true on exponentials
e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}
, which form a basis of the space of functions of
p
. Let us therefore focus on computing the commutator
[\hat{X}^{i},\hat{p}_{j}]
acting on exponentials, namely

\displaystyle[\hat{X}^{j},\hat{p}_{i}]e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}

\displaystyle=i\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}\big(p_{i}\star e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}\big)-p_{i}\star Y^{j}e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial Z^{i}}\bigg|_{Z=0}e^{-i\langle p,Z\rangle}\star e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}-iY^{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial Z^{i}}\bigg|_{Z=0}e^{-i\langle p,Z\rangle}\star e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}

where we used
\hat{X}^{j}=Y^{j}
when acting through (
3.9
) on a plane wave
e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}
. By virtue of the star product (
3.8
), one now has

\displaystyle[\hat{X}^{j},\hat{p}_{i}]e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}

\displaystyle=i\frac{\partial}{\partial Z^{i}}\bigg|_{Z=0}\left(B(Z,Y)^{j}e^{-i\langle p,B(Z,Y)\rangle}\right)-iY^{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial Z^{i}}\bigg|_{Z=0}e^{-i\langle p,B(Z,Y)\rangle}

\displaystyle=i\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial Z^{i}}\bigg|_{Z=0}B(Z,Y)^{j}\right)e^{-i\langle p,B(Z,Y)\rangle}.

Finally, since the Lie derivative along
t_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}
is an infinitesimal translation along the
i^{\text{th}}
direction of the
Y
vector, eq. (
2.3
) yields
i\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{i}X^{j}}=i\frac{\partial}{\partial Z^{i}}\big|_{Z=0}B(Z,Y)^{j}
when acting on a plane wave
e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}
, from which it follows that
[\hat{X}^{j},\hat{p}_{i}]e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}=i\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{i}X^{j}}e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}
.
\blacksquare

Noncommutative scalar products.

Having defined the action (
3.5
)–(
3.9
) of position and momentum operators, the missing ingredient is the scalar product of wave functions. The carrier space of the momentum representation is the Hilbert space of
\star
-square-integrable functions on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, denoted
L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
, with scalar product

\displaystyle\langle\Phi|\Psi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}:=\int_{\mathfrak{g^{*}}}\frac{\differential^{n}p}{(2\pi)^{n}}\;(\bar{\Phi}\star\Psi)(p),

(3.10)

where
\differential^{n}p
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
and the star product is that introduced around (
3.6
). This bilinear form is sesquilinear thanks to (
3.7
). It also satisfies the key property of being invariant under exponential operators (
2.10
), which is to say that the set
\cal E
of such operators acts unitarily. Indeed, for any plane wave
e^{-i\langle\hat{p},X\rangle}\in\cal E
, one has

\begin{split}\big\langle e^{-i\langle\hat{p},X\rangle}\Phi\big|e^{-i\langle\hat{p},X\rangle}\Psi\big\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}&=\int_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}\frac{\differential^{n}p}{(2\pi)^{n}}\;\bar{\Phi}\star e^{i\langle p,X\rangle}\star e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}\star\Psi\\
&=\int_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}\frac{\differential^{n}p}{(2\pi)^{n}}\;\bar{\Phi}\star e^{-i\langle p,B(-X,X)\rangle}\star\Psi=\langle\Phi|\Psi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}\end{split}

(3.11)

owing to (
3.8
) and
B(X,-X)=0
by the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula (
2.4
).

A potential issue at this point is that the pairing (
3.10
) is not manifestly positive-definite. This will be remedied in section
4
thanks to the noncommutative Fourier transform: the latter will provide an isometry between
L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
and the space
L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
of square-integrable wave functions on the Lie algebra, endowed with a
bona fide
scalar product and closely related to
L^{2}(G)
.

Finally, a remark on notation. We mostly avoid explicitly writing the representation map but, when a specification is needed, we denote it as
\rho_{\text{mom}}:{\cal A}\rightarrow\operatorname{End}(L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*}))
, meaning that
(\hat{A}\Phi)(p):=(\rho_{\text{mom}}(\hat{A})\Phi)(p)
. This should be compared with the notation
\rho^{\mathcal{I}}_{\text{pos}}
introduced at the end of section
3.1
. In particular, note that there is no superscript in the momentum representation defined so far, for reasons that we now explain.

The problem.

The momentum representation, as built so far, is not isomorphic to the position representation (
3.3
). Indeed, in contrast to eq. (
3.4
), the action of the identity subset (
2.12
) on
L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
is nontrivial since, in general,

e^{-i\langle\hat{p},Y\rangle}\star\Phi(p)\neq\Phi(p)\text{ even when }\exp(Y)=e.

(3.12)

One might have hoped that star products save the day, but they do not, as the issue is one of topology rather than commutativity: even in the simplest case of the (commutative) U(1) group, with a pointwise (commutative) star product of functions on
\mathfrak{u}(1)^{*}=\mathbb{R}
, the identity subset
\mathcal{I}=\{e^{-2\pi inp}\,|\,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is such that
e^{-2\pi inp}\Phi(p)\neq\Phi(p)
in general. The problem, in other words, lies in the fact that the group
G
may have compact subgroups. The result is that the momentum representation on
L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
is ‘larger’ than the position representation on
L^{2}(G)
. To retrieve an isomorphism between them, one is left with two choices: either make the momentum space representation smaller, or make the position space representation larger. These are respectively treated in sections
3.3
and
3.4
.

3.3
Small momentum representation

What spoils the isomorphism between momentum and position representations is summarized in (
3.12
): the fact that states in
L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
are typically not
\mathcal{I}
-invariant, where
\cal I
is the identity subset (
2.12
) or (
2.14
). A straightforward solution is to project the large space
L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
on its subspace consisting of
\cal I
-invariant functions. Analogously to (
2.17
), this is achieved by the projection operator

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}:=\frac{1}{|{\cal I}|}\sum_{\hat{I}\in\mathcal{I}}\hat{I}=\frac{1}{|{\cal I}|}\sum_{Y\in\operatorname{Logs}(e)}e^{-i\langle\hat{p},Y\rangle},

(3.13)

whereupon the image
\imaginary(\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}})=\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\cdot L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
is the space of
\cal I
-invariant functions. The scalar product on that space is the restriction of (
3.10
) to
\cal I
-invariant functions,
i.e.
periodic functions.

Because
|{\cal I}|
is typically infinite, acting with
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}
on finite-normed wave functions in
L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
produces wave functions of vanishing norm. The same subtlety affected averaged operators (
2.17
), and a similar regularization is available for the operator (
3.13
). This is actually a minor problem, identical to the one relating Fourier
transforms
and Fourier
series
,
i.e.
functions on
\mathbb{R}
and functions on
S^{1}
(see section
5
). The way out is to allow oneself to renormalize states after projection with infinite factors, typically
\sqrt{|{\cal I}|}=\sqrt{\text{Vol}({\cal I})}
, in order to get a result that is finite in norm. This is analogous to infrared regularization in standard quantum mechanics: one works in finite volume with periodic boundary conditions, before taking the thermodynamic limit at the end of the day.

Note that the operator algebra which acts on the
\cal I
-invariant Hilbert space
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\cdot L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
is not quite
\cal A
, but its quotient
{\cal A}/{\cal A}_{\cal I}
introduced around (
2.17
). Equivalently, one may represent operators
\hat{A}\in{\cal A}
via the
\cal I
-invariant momentum representation
\rho_{\text{mom}}^{\mathcal{I}}
defined by

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{mom}}^{\mathcal{I}}(\hat{A}):=\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\rho_{\text{mom}}(\hat{A})\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}},

(3.14)

where
\rho_{\text{mom}}
is the ‘large’ momentum representation of section
3.2
and
P_{\mathcal{I}}
is the projector (
3.13
). We will use Fourier transforms in section
4
to show that the
\cal I
-invariant momentum representation (
3.14
) is unitarily equivalent to the position representation (
3.3
) on
L^{2}(G)
.

Comparison with
[
48
]
.

We stress that our current construction differs from that of
[
48
]
, as follows. First, note that the vector space
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\cdot L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
is isomorphic to the vector space of coinvariants under the action of
\mathcal{I}
:

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\cdot L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})\cong L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})\Big/\big\langle\{\hat{I}\Phi-\Phi\,|\,\hat{I}\in\mathcal{I},~\Phi\in L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})\}\big\rangle.

(3.15)

This is readily shown by representing a class of functions on the right-hand side by the unique
\mathcal{I}
-invariant function in that class. In ref.
[
48
]
, it is the quotient space on the right-hand side of (
3.15
) that is chosen as carrier space for the momentum representation.

The issue is that defining a scalar product on that quotient space cannot be done consistently without averaging over
\mathcal{I}
. Indeed, any two representatives of the class
[\Phi]
differ by the action of some
\hat{I}\in\mathcal{I}
; if one attempts to define the scalar product as in
[
48
]
, by
\langle[\Phi]|[\Psi]\rangle:=\int_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}{\differential^{n}p}\,\bar{\Phi}(p)\star\Psi(p)
, then a key consistency check is that the scalar product must not depend on the choice of representatives for
[\Phi]
and
[\Psi]
. The problem is that it
does
depend on that choice: picking another representative for
[\Phi]
, say
e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}\star\Phi(p)
with
\exp(Y)=e
, one generally has
\int\differential^{n}p\,\bar{\Phi}(p)\star\Psi(p)\neq\int\differential^{n}p\,\bar{\Phi}(p)\star e^{i\langle p,Y\rangle}\star\Psi(p)
. This confirms that the only way to properly define the scalar product of equivalence classes is to average the scalar product of representatives over whole classes. This, in turn, amounts to working in the subspace
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\cdot L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
of
\cal I
-invariant wave functions, as done here. A similar subtlety affects the definition of operators acting on wave functions in momentum space.

3.4
Large position representation

Let us now turn to the second way of relating position and momentum representations, by making the position representation of section
3.1
suitably ‘larger’. We begin with the simple example of the U(1) group to illustrate the procedure, then apply it to more general Lie groups.

A simple example: functions on
\mathbb{R}
.

We saw below (
3.12
) that there is no isometry between
L^{2}(\text{U(1)})
and
L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{u}(1)^{*})=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
because the action of ‘identity operators’ (
2.12
) is nontrivial. In the case
G=\text{U}(1)
, the set of identity operators is
\mathcal{I}=\{e^{-2i\pi n\hat{p}}\,|\;n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, which acts on
L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{u}(1)^{*})=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
according to

\displaystyle e^{-2\pi in\hat{p}}\Phi(p)=e^{-2\pi inp}\Phi(p).

(3.16)

This should be contrasted with the trivial action of identity operators on
L^{2}(U(1))
:

\displaystyle e^{-2\pi in\hat{p}}\phi(x)=\phi(x+2\pi n)=\phi(x).

(3.17)

How to reconcile these two statements? Had we not assumed that the function
\phi
is
2\pi
-periodic, eq. (
3.16
) would have been the Fourier transform of (
3.17
) upon identifying
\Phi
with the Fourier transform of
\phi
. In this respect, the space isomorphic to
L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{u}(1)^{*})
is in fact
L^{2}(\mathfrak{u}(1))=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and
L^{2}(U(1))
is its subset consisting of periodic functions. This simple fact can be generalized to any Lie group.

Before turning to the generic case, let us discuss how the isometry between
L^{2}(U(1))
and periodic functions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is to be understood. The main objection is that nonzero periodic functions on
\mathbb{R}
are never square-integrable, since they do not decay at infinity. This is the usual problem of plane waves in quantum mechanics, which technically belong to a ‘rigged’ Hilbert space rather than
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

[
58
]
. Formally, a solution is to keep track of divergent normalization factors such as
1/|\mathbb{Z}|
in order to get finite results. This is easily seen by picking, at random, a function
\phi_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
that decreases sufficiently fast at infinity. Applying the projector (
3.13
) to
\phi_{0}
yields

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\phi_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{Z}|}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\phi_{0}(x+2\pi n)=:\frac{1}{|\mathbb{Z}|}\phi(x),

(3.18)

where
\phi(x)
is now
2\pi
-periodic. As no function in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is
\mathcal{I}
-invariant (here meaning
2\pi
-periodic), the right-hand side of (
3.18
) vanishes. This is confirmed by computing the norm of
\phi
:

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{Z}|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\differential x\,|\phi(x)|^{2}=\frac{|\mathbb{Z}|}{|\mathbb{Z}|^{2}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential x\,|\phi(x)|^{2}=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{Z}|}\|\phi\|^{2}_{U(1)}.

(3.19)

In order to get a result that is finite in norm, one thus needs to multiply
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\psi_{0}
by a factor
\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}
. This is to say that, for periodic functions
\phi
,
\psi
, one has

\Big<\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}}\phi\Big|\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}}\psi\Big>_{\mathbb{R}}=\langle\phi|\psi\rangle_{\text{U(1)}}

(3.20)

where both sides are now understood to be finite, generally nonzero. In all such cases, one should view the factor
|\mathbb{Z}|
as specifying a prescription for finite-volume regularization.

General case: functions on
\mathfrak{g}
.

In order to adapt the discussion to any compact Lie group, the idea is to unwrap
G
onto its Lie algebra and define the space
L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
of square-integrable functions on
\mathfrak{g}
, as follows. First define the
Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff group
as being the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, endowed with a group law

X\cdot Y:=B(X,Y)

(3.21)

given by the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff expansion (
2.4
). The identity is just
0
and the inverse of an element
X
is given by
X^{-1}=-X
. Then consider the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
, with the scalar product on the right-hand side of (
3.1
) extended to the whole Lie algebra:

\displaystyle\langle\phi|\psi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}:=\int_{\mathfrak{g}}J(X)\differential^{n}X\;\bar{\phi}(X)\psi(X).

(3.22)

The action of position and momentum operators is defined by (
3.3
), as in
L^{2}(G)
. In particular, exponential operators act on that Hilbert space by (noncommutative) translations

\displaystyle e^{-i\langle\hat{p},Y\rangle}\cdot\phi(X)=\phi(B(Y,X)).

(3.23)

The action of ‘identity operators’ (
2.12
) is thus nontrivial, since in general
B(Y,X)\neq X
even when
\exp(Y)=e
. Also note that the action of position operators
\hat{X}^{j}
as multiplication by
X^{j}
is now well-defined, so that indeed the full operator algebra
\mathcal{A}
is represented on that space. Let us therefore denote this alternative position representation
\rho_{\text{pos}}
, this time with no
\mathcal{I}
superscript. We will see in section
4
that
L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
is isometric to
L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
through the noncommutative Fourier transform.

Similarly to the U(1) case, functions on
G
can be seen as functions on
\mathfrak{g}
that are periodic under the action of
\mathcal{I}
. This is to say that
C^{\infty}(G)\cong\{\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})\,|\,\phi(X)=\phi(B(Y,X))~~\forall X\in\mathfrak{g},~\forall Y\in{\cal I}\}
. One can again formally get an isometry between
L^{2}(G)
and periodic functions in
L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
, by defining
L^{2}(G)=\imaginary(\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}})
in terms of the projector (
3.13
). From the same reasoning as in the U(1) case (
3.20
), periodic functions in
L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
that are finite in norm are obtained by renormalizing states in the image of
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}
by a factor of
\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}^{r}|}
. As a result, for
\phi,\psi
that are
\cal I
-periodic in
C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})
, the analogue of eq. (
3.20
) reads

\begin{split}\bigg\langle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}^{r}|}}\phi\bigg|\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}^{r}|}}\psi\bigg\rangle_{\!\!\mathfrak{g}}&=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{Z}^{r}|}\int_{\mathfrak{g}}J(X)\differential^{n}X\;\bar{\phi}(X)\psi(X)\\
&=\!\!\!\!\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{principal}\\
\text{branch in $\mathfrak{g}$}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!J(X)\differential^{n}X\;\bar{\phi}(X)\psi(X)=\langle\phi|\psi\rangle_{G},\end{split}

(3.24)

where the principal branch of the logarithm was defined around eq. (
2.3
) and where we used that the branches of logarithm are labeled by
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
as explained below (
2.16
). The bottom line here is the scalar product (
3.1
) in
L^{2}(G)
, which was the expected result.

To conclude, we have now represented the operator algebras
{\cal A}
and
{\cal A}/{\cal A}_{\cal I}
on two pairs of Hilbert spaces. These were
L^{2}(G)\cong\hat{P}_{\cal I}\cdot L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})\subset L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
in position space, and
\hat{P}_{\cal I}\cdot L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})\subset L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
in momentum space. The same projector (
3.13
) appears in both cases, by construction. We will now define intertwiners linking these representations two by two, namely a noncommutative Fourier transform and its inverse for the ‘large’ representations, and Fourier coefficients and Fourier series for the ‘small’ representations.

4
Noncommutative Fourier transforms and series

The passage from position to momentum representations of
\cal A
is achieved by a unitary intertwining operator,
i.e.
an isometry that commutes with the action of operators. The isometry is just a Fourier transform in standard quantum mechanics. By analogy, following
[
48
]
, we now refer to it as the
noncommutative Fourier transform
to stress that momenta fail to commute as in eqs. (
2.7
)
[
30
,
34
,
35
]
.

Being an intertwiner is a stronger requirement than being a Hilbert space isomorphism. In our case, if
F
is the sought-for intertwiner between ‘large’ position and momentum representations, then the following two diagrams need to commute for any operator
\hat{A}
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho_{\text{pos}}(\hat{A})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho_{\text{mom}}(\hat{A})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}

and

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(G)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{P}_{\cal I}\cdot L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(G)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{P}_{\cal I}\cdot L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{\cal I}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho^{\cal I}_{\text{pos}}(\hat{A})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho^{\cal I}_{\text{mom}}(\hat{A})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{\cal I}}

(4.1)

Here
\rho_{\text{pos}}
and
\rho_{\text{mom}}
are the ‘large’ position and momentum representations of the operator algebra
\cal A
, respectively defined in sections
3.4
and
3.2
. Their analogues with a superscript
\cal I
are the ‘reduced’ or projected representations of sections
3.1
and
3.3
, respectively. The same commutative diagram thus holds in both projected and unprojected cases. We therefore first build the Fourier
transform

F
by working in the larger Hilbert spaces
L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
and
L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
, then we project down to the smaller
\mathcal{I}
-invariant subspaces
L^{2}(G)
and
\hat{P}_{\cal I}\cdot L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
to obtain Fourier
coefficients
and Fourier
series
. Most properties below will be stated for Fourier coefficients (with infinite
|\cal I|
), as their analogues for Fourier transforms are straightforward upon taking
\mathcal{I}=\{0\}
everywhere.

4.1
Noncommutative Fourier transform

Let us first show the existence of the intertwiner
F
mapping
L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
on
L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
, without reference to quotients by the identity subset (
2.14
) due to the possible presence of compact subgroups of
G
. To that end, write
F
as an integration kernel: for any wave function
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
,

\displaystyle F[\psi](p)=\int_{\mathfrak{g}}J(X)\differential^{n}X\;E(X,p)\psi(X)\in L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*}).

(4.2)

The integration kernel
E(X,p)
is unknown at this stage, and needs to be found. It must be such that
F\circ\rho_{\text{pos}}(\hat{A})=\rho_{\text{mom}}(\hat{A})\circ F
for any operator
\hat{A}\in\cal A
, as in the commutative diagram (
4.1
). This must hold, in particular, for position and momentum operators. Hence, for any
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
, one must have

\displaystyle\int J(X)\differential^{n}X\;i\frac{\partial E(X,p)}{\partial p_{j}}\psi(g)

\displaystyle=\int J(X)\differential^{n}X\;E(X,p)X^{j}\psi(X),

(4.3)

\displaystyle\int J(X)\differential^{n}X\;p_{j}\star E(X,p)\psi(X)

\displaystyle=\int J(X)\differential^{n}X\;E(X,p)\left(-i\mathcal{L}_{j}\psi\right)(X),

(4.4)

where we used the momentum representation (
3.5
)–(
3.9
) on the left-hand side, and the position representation (
3.3
) on the right-hand side. Now integrate by parts on the right-hand side of (
4.4
), keeping in mind that the Haar measure is left-invariant. Using the fact that (
4.3
)–(
4.4
) must hold for any
\psi
, one concludes that

i\frac{\partial E(X,p)}{\partial p_{j}}=X^{j}E(X,p),\qquad p_{j}\star E(X,p)=i\mathcal{L}_{j}E(X,p).

(4.5)

Here the first equation is readily integrated into

\displaystyle E(X,p)=f(X)e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle},

(4.6)

which fixes the momentum-dependence of the kernel in (
4.2
). To determine
f(X)
, integrate the second equation in (
4.5
) along left-invariant vector fields to get

\displaystyle E(B(Y,X),p)=e^{-Y^{i}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{i}}E(X,p)=e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}\star E(X,p).

(4.7)

Plugging this back into (
4.6
) yields
f(B(Y,X))=f(X)
for all
Y
, so
f(X)
is in fact constant. We will set this constant to 1 without loss of generality, so the kernel (
4.6
) of the noncommutative Fourier transform is a
plane wave

\displaystyle E(X,p)=e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}.

(4.8)

As a result, the
noncommutative Fourier transform
(
4.2
) coincides with the standard one on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, save for an additional Jacobian (
3.2
) due to the Haar measure:

\displaystyle F[\psi](p):=\int_{\mathfrak{g}}J(X)\differential^{n}X\;e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}\psi(X).

(4.9)

This will be our definition of
F
. We stress that the exponential appearing here is the usual one, as opposed to a noncommutative
\star
-exponential as in Wigner-Weyl calculus
[
29
]
. This simplification is due to the fact that exponential functions are mapped to exponential operators under the symmetric ordering (
2.10
) of the
p_{i}
s.

Properties of plane waves.

Let us list a few key properties of the plane waves (
4.8
). They are mostly self-evident, but our goal will be to reproduce them in the more complicated
\mathcal{I}
-invariant case. First, one has

E(X,0)=1,\qquad E(0,p)=1,\qquad E(-X,p)=\overline{E(X,p)}=E(X,-p),

(4.10)

which straightforwardly stem from (
4.8
). One more property involves star products, namely

\displaystyle E(X,p)\star_{p}E(Y,p+q)

\displaystyle=E(B(X,Y),p)E(Y,q),

(4.11)

where
B(X,Y)
is the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff expansion (
2.4
). This stems from the star product of plane waves implied by eq. (
2.11
) and the choice of ordering (
2.10
). It states that plane waves are well-behaved under both ‘multiplication’ in
\mathfrak{g}
and momentum addition. A special case of (
4.11
) that will repeatedly be useful is
E(X,p)\star E(Y,p)=E(B(X,Y),p)
.

One last property will be essential in section
4.3
. Namely, the momentum integral of eq. (
4.8
) yields

\int_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}\frac{\differential^{n}p}{(2\pi)^{n}}\;e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}=\delta^{n}(X)=\delta_{\mathfrak{g}}(X),

(4.12)

where
\delta^{n}
is the Dirac distribution for the Lebesgue measure on
\mathfrak{g}
, and
\delta_{\mathfrak{g}}
is the Dirac distribution for the left-invariant Haar measure on the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff group
\mathfrak{g}
. The second equality stems from the property
\int_{\mathfrak{g}}J(X)\differential^{n}X\psi(X)\delta^{d}(X)=J(0)\psi(0)=\psi(0)
, valid for any test function
\psi
thanks to the fact that
J(0)=1
owing to eq. (
3.2
). A closely related statement is that the integration kernel

\delta_{\star}(p,q):=\int_{\mathfrak{g}}J(X)\differential^{n}X\;E(X,p)E(X,-q)

(4.13)

is such that the following holds for any test function
\Phi
in momentum space:

\Phi(p)=\int_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}\frac{\differential^{n}q}{(2\pi)^{n}}\;\delta_{\star}(p,q)\star_{q}\Phi(q).

(4.14)

Indeed, it suffices to show that (
4.14
) holds for plane waves
\Phi(p)=e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}
, since they span
L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
; this is the case thanks to (
4.11
) and the property (
4.12
).

4.2
Noncommutative Fourier coefficients

We now present the
\cal I
-invariant version of section
4.1
. A consequence of
\cal I
-invariance is that the Fourier
transform
becomes a map
F_{\cal I}
that sends a function
\psi
on its Fourier
coefficients
in momentum space. These ‘coefficients’ turn out to be functions on specific submanifolds of momentum space
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
; they reduce to actual coefficients (labelled by discrete values of
p
) only when the group
G
is both compact and Abelian, as in the standard case of Fourier series with
G=\text{U}(1)
(
cfr
section
5
).

\cal I
-invariant plane waves.

To obtain noncommutative Fourier coefficients from the Fourier transform (
4.9
), one imposes
\mathcal{I}
-invariance of
\psi
so that
F[\psi]
lies in the
\cal I
-invariant subspace
\hat{P}_{\cal I}\cdot L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
, with
\hat{P}_{\cal I}
the projector (
3.13
). This motivates the definition of
\cal I
-invariant plane waves

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{I}}(X,p)

\displaystyle:=\rho_{\text{pos}}(\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}})E(X,p)=\rho_{\text{mom}}(\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}})E(X,p),

(4.15)

where
\rho_{\text{pos}}
and
\rho_{\text{mom}}
are the position and momentum representations respectively defined in (
3.3
) and (
3.5
)–(
3.9
). Since both representations of the projector may be used, we will omit the representation map below and simply let
\hat{P}_{\cal I}:=\rho(\hat{P}_{\cal I})
. Using the explicit series (
3.13
), one can also write

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{I}}(X,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|{\cal I}|}\sum_{Z\in\operatorname{Logs}(e)}e^{-i\langle p,B(Z,X)\rangle}=\frac{1}{\big|\operatorname{Logs}(\exp(X))\big|}\sum_{Y\in\operatorname{Logs}(\exp(X))}e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}

(4.16)

where the set
\operatorname{Logs}(g)
was defined above (
2.14
). The first rewriting is most convenient for abstract proofs, while the second is best for concrete computations.

One may be worried at this point by the appearance of infinite factors such as
|\mathcal{I}|
or
|\operatorname{Logs}(\exp(X))|
in eqs. (
4.16
). In fact, these factors are nothing out of the ordinary, as they already appear when relating standard Fourier transforms and Fourier series. The factor
|\operatorname{Logs}(\exp(X))|
, in particular, is much smaller than the
|\mathcal{I}|
factor for almost every
X
. Indeed, as discussed around (
2.16
), there are many more logarithms (
2.15
) of the identity than of a generic element of
G
. The factor
|\mathbb{Z}^{r}|
that arises from (
2.16
) is much more manageable than possibly divergent integrals along adjoint orbits, such as those occurring in
|\mathcal{I}|
owing to eqs. (
2.15
). Hence, for almost every
X
, the
\cal I
-invariant plane wave (
4.16
) simplifies to

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{I}}(X,p)=\frac{e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}}{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}}\sum_{n^{1},...,n^{r}\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-i\langle p,2\pi n^{i}a_{i}(X)\rangle}=e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}\sum_{k_{1},...,k_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\delta(\langle p,a_{i}(X)\rangle-k_{i})}{|\mathbb{Z}|},

(4.17)

where we used the standard Poisson summation formula (see again section
5
). It becomes clear in this way that the infinite
|\mathbb{Z}|
factor regularizes a
\delta(0)
. Thus, for almost every
X
, one has

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{I}}(X,p)=\begin{cases}e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}&\text{if }\langle p,a_{i}(X)\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all $i\in\{1,\dots r\}$},\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(4.18)

which is nothing but a noncommutative version of a discrete Fourier mode, compatible with the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.

Fourier coefficients; comparison with
[
48
]
.

Recalling the factor
1/\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}}
in the correspondence (
3.24
) between functions on
G
and periodic functions on
\mathfrak{g}
, define the
noncommutative Fourier coefficients

\begin{split}F_{\cal I}[\psi](p)&:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}}}\int_{\mathfrak{g}}J(X)\differential^{n}X\;E_{\mathcal{I}}(X,p)\psi(X)\\[3.00003pt]
&{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}{:}}=\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}}\!\!\!\!\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{principal}\\
\text{branch in $\mathfrak{g}$}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!J(X)\differential^{n}X\,E_{\mathcal{I}}(X,p)\psi(X)\end{split}

(4.19)

for any periodic wave functions
\psi\in\hat{P}_{\cal I}\cdot L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})=L^{2}(G)
, where the principal branch of the logarithm was defined above eq. (
2.3
). This is just the
\cal I
-invariant projection of the Fourier transform (
4.9
). Owing to eq. (
4.17
), it vanishes for almost any momentum
p\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, which is the sense in which it generalizes to any group
G
the standard Fourier coefficients that occur for
G=\text{U}(1)
. It can be made more explicit by identifying the integral over the principal branch with an integral over the group as in eq. (
3.1
), so that

\displaystyle F_{\cal I}[\psi](p)=\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}}\int_{G}\differential g\;E_{\mathcal{I}}(g,p)\psi(g).

(4.20)

Here we let
E_{\mathcal{I}}(g,p):=E_{\mathcal{I}}(\log(g),p)
since the sum over logarithms in (
4.16
) ensures that the value of
E_{\mathcal{I}}(\log(g),p)
does not depend on the choice of logarithm. As it turns out, the expression (
4.20
) of noncommutative Fourier coefficients will be the most convenient one below.

The definition (
4.19
) can be recast as

F_{\cal I}[\psi](p)=\sqrt{|{\mathbb{Z}}|^{r}}\,\hat{P}_{\cal I}\cdot\!\!\!\!\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{principal}\\
\text{branch in $\mathfrak{g}$}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!J(X)\differential^{n}X\,E(X,p)\psi(X),

(4.21)

where
E(X,p)
is the plane wave (
4.8
), without
\cal I
-invariance. This is similar to, but crucially different from, the definition used in
[
48
]
. Indeed, Fourier series there are defined by a version of eq. (
4.21
)
without
the projector
\hat{P}_{\cal I}
. The problem, then, is that scalar products and the action of operators are ill-defined as discussed below eq. (
3.15
). Including the projector
\hat{P}_{\cal I}
as in (
4.21
) circumvents the issue, with an outcome that is renormalized thanks to the
\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}}
factor, yielding a result that has finite norm.

Properties of
\cal I
-invariant plane waves.

Let us list some of the key features of the
\cal I
-invariant plane waves (
4.15
), following a sequence similar to that of section
4.1
. First, consider the
\cal I
-invariant version of eqs. (
4.10
), namely

E_{\mathcal{I}}(g^{-1},p)=\overline{E_{\mathcal{I}}(g,p)}=E_{\mathcal{I}}(g,-p),\qquad E_{\mathcal{I}}(g,0)=1,\qquad E_{\mathcal{I}}(e,p)=\mathcal{Q}^{-1}(\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}).

(4.22)

These readily follow from the definition (
4.15
). Eq. (
4.11
) similarly becomes

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{I}}(g,p)\star_{p}E_{\mathcal{I}}(h,p+q)

\displaystyle=E_{\mathcal{I}}(gh,p)E_{\mathcal{I}}(h,q),

(4.23)

showing that
\cal I
-invariant exponentials represent the group law under
\star
-multiplication, and behave well under momentum addition. A key corollary is
E_{\mathcal{I}}(g,p)\star E_{\mathcal{I}}(h,p)=E_{\mathcal{I}}(gh,p)
.

Proof of (
4.23
).
The definition (
4.16
) of
\mathcal{I}
-invariant plane waves yields

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{I}}(g,p)\star_{p}E_{\mathcal{I}}(h,p+q)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|^{2}}\sum_{Z\in\operatorname{Logs}(e)}\sum_{W\in\operatorname{Logs}(e)}e^{-i\langle p,B(Z,X)\rangle}\star_{p}e^{-i\langle p,B(Y,W)\rangle}e^{-i\langle q,B(Y,W)\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|^{2}}\sum_{Z\in\operatorname{Logs}(e)}e^{-i\langle p,Z\rangle}\star_{p}e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}\star_{p}e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}\star_{p}\sum_{W\in\operatorname{Logs}(e)}e^{-i\langle p,W\rangle}e^{-i\langle q,B(Y,W)\rangle}

where
g=\exp(X)
,
h=\exp(Y)
and we used eq. (
4.11
) to split into four a product of two plane waves. The product
e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}\star_{p}e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}
can be recast as
e^{-i\langle p,B(X,Y)\rangle}
owing to eq. (
3.8
). The latter can again be used to note that the sum over
Z
involves
e^{-i\langle p,B(Z,B(X,Y))\rangle}
, so

Properties of noncommutative Fourier series

Quantum mechanics on Lie groups.

The statements (
4.30
) and (
4.32
) show that noncommutative Fourier coefficients and series intertwine the position and momentum representations of quantum mechanics on a Lie group
G
, respectively defined on the Hilbert spaces
L^{2}(G)
and
\hat{P}_{\cal I}\cdot L^{2}_{\star}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
. In particular, one may now conclude that the pairing (

. Note that this is yet another difference between the present work and ref.
[
48

, where a different Fourier transform was shown to be injective but
not
surjective, so that no property such as (
4.32
) could be derived. (See in particular
[
48

At this point, the stage is set for quantum mechanics on any Lie group. One may think of the Hilbert spaces

and

as being one and the same, which is typically done when dealing with quantum mechanics on

. As is common practice, one may introduce a basis of position kets

and momentum kets

such that
\langle g|\psi\rangle=\psi(g)
and
\langle p|\psi\rangle=F_{\cal I}[\psi](p)
for any

4.20
). The change of basis is given by

5
Applications of noncommutative Fourier series

We now discuss two examples of Fourier series in detail, namely those appropriate to the groups U(1) and SU(2). Even though the former is Abelian, its compactness makes the construction nontrivial, illustrating the link between Fourier transforms and Fourier coefficients. The example of SU(2) furthermore exhibits all the subtleties of noncommutative Fourier series, leading to a new formula for the Poisson summation of functions in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We conclude with a discussion of noncommutative Poisson summation in more general compact Lie groups.

3.2
) is trivial, and eqs. (
4.10
)–(
4.11
) hold without any star product. As for the
\cal I
-invariant plane waves (
4.16
), they read

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{I}}(\theta,p)=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{Z}|}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-ip(\theta+2\pi n)}=e^{-ip\theta}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\delta(p-k)}{|\mathbb{Z}|},

(5.3)

which is a trivial special case of eq. (
4.17
). Evaluating the plane wave (
5.3
) at
p=n\in\mathbb{Z}
yields
E_{\mathcal{I}}(\theta,n)=e^{-in\theta}
which, considering the far right-hand side, justifies the prescription
\delta(0)=|\mathbb{Z}|
. In the end, one has

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{I}}(\theta,p)=\begin{cases}e^{-in\theta}&\text{if }p=n\in\mathbb{Z},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(5.4)

This is to say that
\cal I
-invariant plane waves (
4.15
) are best seen as Fourier modes.

To obtain Fourier coefficients following section
4
, one imposes
\mathcal{I}
-invariance to the argument of the Fourier transform. From (
4.20
) and the expression (
5.4
) of periodic plane waves, Fourier coefficients read

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{I}}(\psi)(p)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\delta(0)}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential X\;e^{-inX}\psi(X)&\text{if }p=n\in\mathbb{Z},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(5.5)

This was to be expected: Fourier coefficients are supported on integers in momentum space
\mathfrak{u}(1)^{*}\cong\mathbb{R}
. The Fourier coefficients (
5.5
) of a function
\psi\in L^{2}(\text{U}(1))
may thus be written as

\displaystyle F_{\cal I}[\psi](p)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sqrt{\delta(p-n)}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential X\;e^{-inX}\psi(X),

(5.6)

where the square root of the delta distribution enforces enforces square-integrability of
F_{\cal I}[\psi]
. Indeed, eq. (
4.26
) holds and may be recast as

\displaystyle\big\langle F_{\cal I}[\phi]\big|F_{\cal I}[\psi]\big\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\int\frac{\differential p}{2\pi}\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}\bar{\phi}_{n}\psi_{m}\sqrt{\delta(p-m)\delta(p-n)}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\bar{\phi}_{n}\psi_{n}

(5.7)

in terms of standard Fourier coefficients
\phi_{n}:=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential X\,e^{inX}\phi(X)
and similarly for
\psi
. This is to say that
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\cdot L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\cong\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\cong L^{2}(\text{U(1)})
, as had to be the case. Finally, the adjoint (
4.29
) of Fourier coefficients is given by

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{I}}^{\dagger}[\Phi](X)=\sqrt{\delta(0)}\int\frac{\differential p}{{2\pi}}e^{ipX}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\varphi_{n}\sqrt{\delta(p-n)}=\frac{1}{{2\pi}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\varphi_{n}e^{inX}.

(5.8)

This is the usual Fourier-series representation of a
2\pi
-periodic function
\psi(X)
.

Poisson summation.

The Poisson summation formula for U(1) is obtained by relating Fourier transforms and series through the projector (
5.1
), as follows. On the one hand, let
\psi(X)
be a function in the ‘large’ Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathfrak{u}(1))=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
of section
3.4
. Acting on
\psi
with the projector (
5.1
) turns
\psi
into a periodic function, namely
\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\psi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi(x+2\pi n)
. On the other hand, the commutative diagram (
4.1
) ensures that the Fourier transform and its inverse commute with the projector
\hat{P}_{\cal I}
. It follows that
\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\psi(x)=F^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{I}}[F[\psi]](x)
, where
F[\psi]
is the Fourier
transform
of
\psi
while
F_{\cal I}^{\dagger}
is the Fourier
series
(
4.29
). Since both computations of
\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\psi(x)
must give the same result, the definition (
4.29
) of
F_{\cal I}^{\dagger}
allows us to write

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi(x+2\pi n)=\int\frac{\differential p}{2\pi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta(p-k)e^{ipx}F[\psi](p)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{ikx}F[\psi](k).

(5.9)

where we used the expression (
5.3
) of
\mathcal{I}
-invariant plane waves.

Eq. (
5.9
) is the standard Poisson summation formula. The virtue of the derivation shown here is that it extends to any (compact) Lie group, as we now show.

5.2
The SU(2) case: non-Abelian Poisson summation

The group SU(2) illustrates how both compactness and noncommutativity affect the construction of Fourier coefficients and series. As we shall see, this leads to a new Poisson summation formula for functions in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\cong\mathfrak{su}(2)
.

Principal branch of SU(2).

The Lie algebra
\mathbb{R}^{3}\cong\mathfrak{su}(2)
of SU(2) is the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
of traceless, Hermitian matrices.
7
7

7

Technically, the Lie algebra consists of
anti
-Hermitian matrices. These are just obtained by multiplying any Hermitian matrix by
i
.
A convenient basis (orthonormal with respect to the suitably normalized Killing form) is provided by Pauli matrices

\sigma_{x}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{y}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{z}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(5.10)

Any element of
\mathfrak{su}(2)
can thus be written as a linear combination
X=X^{j}\sigma_{j}=\vec{X}\cdot\vec{\sigma}
, where
\vec{X}=(X^{x},X^{y},X^{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The exponential map from
\mathfrak{su}(2)
to SU(2) reads

\displaystyle\exp(X^i\sigma_i)=e^{i\vec{X}\cdot\vec{\sigma}}=\mathbb{I}\cos\|\vec{X}\|+i\,\vec{u}_{X}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\sin\|\vec{X}\|,

(5.11)

where
\vec{u}_{X}:=\vec{X}/\|\vec{X}\|
denotes the unit vector pointing in the direction of
\vec{X}
and
\|\vec{X}\|:=\sqrt{X^{i}X^{i}}
is the usual Euclidean norm.

The principal branch of the logarithm, on which the exponential (
5.11
) is injective with a dense image in SU(2), is the open ball of radius
\pi
centred at the origin in
\mathfrak{su}(2)\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}
. See fig.
1
for a cartoon, and recall the right panel of fig.
2
for the actual picture. The only missing element of SU(2) in the image of the principal branch is
-\mathbb{I}
, which can be obtained as the exponential of any point on the sphere of radius
\pi
in
\mathfrak{su}(2)
. As readily seen from (
5.11
), the set
\mathcal{I}
of elements that exponentiate to the identity are vectors of integer radius:

\displaystyle{\cal I}=\operatorname{Logs}(e)=\big\{\vec{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,\big|\,\|\vec{X}\|\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}\big\}.

(5.12)

This is the expected union (
2.15
) of adjoint orbits, which are spheres in this case; see again the right panel of fig.
2
. We stress that the logarithms of a generic Lie algebra element are far less numerous: provided
\|\vec{X}\|\notin 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Logs}\big(\exp(X)\big)=\{\vec{X}+2\pi n\vec{u}_{X}\,|\,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

(5.13)

This is the rank-one version of eq. (
2.16
). Therefore, the branches of the logarithm are labeled by
\mathbb{Z}
.

Hilbert space
L^{2}(\text{SU}(2))
.

As in section
3.1
, consider the space of wave functions on SU(2) that are square-integrable with respect to the Haar measure
\differential g
. In terms of the vector
\vec{X}
in eq. (
5.11
), the Jacobian (
3.2
) between the flat measure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the Haar measure reads
[
55
]
(see also
[
56
]
)

\displaystyle J(\vec{X})=\frac{\sin^{2}\|\vec{X}\|}{\|\vec{X}\|^{2}}.

(5.14)

The scalar product (
3.1
) can thus be recast as

\displaystyle\int_{G}\differential g\,\bar{\phi}(g)\psi(g)=\int\limits_{\|\vec{X}\|<\pi}\frac{\sin^{2}\|\vec{X}\|}{\|\vec{X}\|^{2}}\differential^{3}X\,\bar{\phi}(X)\psi(X)

(5.15)

in principal branch coordinates. Similarly, the Fourier transform (
4.9
) of a function
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathfrak{su}(2))=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
reads

\displaystyle F[\psi](p)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\sin^{2}\|\vec{X}\|}{\|\vec{X}\|^{2}}\differential^{3}X\,e^{-i\langle p,\vec{X}\rangle}\psi(X).

(5.16)

This will be crucial for Poisson summation, including the effects of the Jacobian (
5.14
).

Fourier modes.

Let us now turn to the
\cal I
-invariant plane waves (
4.16
). Since the set of logarithms is given by (
5.13
) for a generic Lie algebra element
X
, one can write

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{I}}(\vec{X},p)=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{Z}|}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-i\langle p,\vec{X}+2\pi n\vec{u}_{X}\rangle}=e^{-i\langle p,\vec{X}\rangle}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\delta(\langle p,\vec{u}_{X}\rangle-m)}{|\mathbb{Z}|}

(5.17)

for almost any
\vec{X}
. This is the rank-one version of eq. (
4.17
). Each such plane wave localizes on those surfaces where
\langle p,\vec{u}_{X}\rangle
is an integer, both when seen as a function of
p
at fixed
X
, and as a function of
X
at fixed
p
. See fig.
3
.

Let us unpack the condition
\langle p,\vec{u}_{X}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}
. At fixed
p
and varying
\vec{X}
, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality guarantees
|\langle p,\vec{u}_{X}\rangle|\leq\|p\|
. Hence there are at most
2\lfloor\|p\|\rfloor+1
non-vanishing terms on the right-hand side of (
5.17
), namely those for which
m\in\{-\lfloor\|p\|\rfloor,\dots,\lfloor\|p\|\rfloor\}
. Let us therefore give the suggestive name
j(p):=\lfloor\|p\|\rfloor
, thought of as the (integer) spin of a representation of SU(2) obtained by geometric quantization of a coadjoint orbit whose radius is fixed by
p
. In that case,
\vec{u}_{X}
is localized on circles of constant integer scalar product with
p
, and
\vec{X}
is localized on half-cones passing through these circles; see the left panel of fig.
3
. Conversely, at fixed
\vec{X}
,
p
can be split as
p=p_{\parallel}+p_{\perp}
, such that
\langle p,\vec{u}_{X}\rangle=p_{\parallel}
. Then
E_{\mathcal{I}}(\vec{X},p)
is localized on affine planes perpendicular to
\vec{u}_{X}
, passing through
m\vec{u}_{X}
. We will call these planes
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\vec{X})
; see the right panel of fig.
3
. Up to a multiplicative constant, these are planes perpendicular to the root lattice adapted to
\vec{X}
.

Figure 3
:
Localization of
{\cal I}
-invariant plane waves (
5.17
) in
\vec{X}
at fixed
p
(left), and in
p
at fixed
\vec{X}
(right). On the left panel, we chose
\|p\|=2.2
so that only five values of
m
are allowed in (
5.17
). On the right panel, the plane waves are localized on planes perpendicular to
\vec{u}_{X}
that are evenly spaced by integer multiples of
\vec{u}_{X}
.

Fourier coefficients for SU(2).

The definition (
4.20
) of Fourier coefficients, applied to the SU(2) case in (
5.16
), yields

\displaystyle F_{\cal I}[\psi](p)=\sqrt{\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\|\vec{X}\|<\pi}\differential^{3}X\,\frac{\sin^{2}\|\vec{X}\|}{\|\vec{X}\|^{2}}\,\psi(X)\!\!\!\sum_{m=-j(p)}^{j(p)}e^{-im\|\vec{X}\|}\frac{\delta(\langle p,\vec{u}_{X}\rangle-m)}{|\mathbb{Z}|}

(5.18)

for any wave function
\psi\in L^{2}(\text{SU(2)})
, where we used the
\cal I
-invariant plane wave (
5.17
). To simplify this expression, let us work in spherical coordinates
(r,\theta,\varphi)
adapted to
p
, such that the north pole
\theta=0
is aligned with
p
.
8
8

8

For SU(2), spherical coordinates naturally arise when using the Weyl integration formula that splits integrals in
\mathfrak{su}(2)
into toroidal (radial) directions and adjoint-orbit (angular) directions.
Then the noncommutative Fourier coefficients of
\psi
are

\begin{split}&F_{\cal I}[\psi](p)=\\
&\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}}\sum_{m=-j(p)}^{j(p)}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential r\,\sin^{2}(r)e^{-imr}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin(\theta)\differential\theta\,\delta\big(\|p\|\cos(\theta)-m\big)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\varphi\,\psi_{p}(r,\theta,\varphi),\end{split}

(5.19)

where the notation
\psi_{p}(r,\theta,\varphi)
stresses that the argument of
\psi
depends on
p
. Changing the integration variable from
\theta
to
u=\|p\|\cos(\theta)
makes it possible to integrate the delta function, leading to

\displaystyle F_{\cal I}[\psi](p)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}}\frac{1}{\|p\|}\sum_{m=-j(p)}^{j(p)}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential r\;\sin^{2}(r)e^{-imr}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\varphi\;\psi_{p}\big(r,\arccos(m/\|p\|),\varphi\big).

(5.20)

No further simplification is available, in general, for arbitrary functions
\psi
.

For class functions, which are constant on conjugacy classes, the integral (
5.20
) does simplify drastically. Such functions only depend on
r
, so the sum over
m
and the integral over
\varphi
can be carried out explicitly. The result is

\displaystyle F_{\cal I}[\psi](p)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}}\frac{4\pi}{\|p\|}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential r\;\sin(r)\cos(r/2)\sin\big((j(p)+1/2)r\big)\psi(r)\text{~~~($\psi$ a class function).}

(5.21)

A basis of class functions is provided by the characters of irreducible representations. Let us therefore compute the Fourier transform of the character of a highest-weight representation of SU(2)—say
\rho_{\lambda}
, with spin
\lambda
, in the Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{2\lambda+1}
. In principal branch coordinates, the character of the representation reads

\displaystyle\chi_{\lambda}(\vec{X}):=\operatorname{Tr}\Big[\rho_{\lambda}\big(\exp(X)\big)\Big]=\frac{\sin\big((2\lambda+1)\|\vec X\|\big)}{\sin\|\vec{X}\|}.

(5.22)

Plugging this expression into (
5.21
) yields the Fourier transform

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{I}}[\chi_{\lambda}](p)=\begin{cases}\frac{\pi^{2}}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}}\frac{1}{\|p\|}&\text{if }2\lambda\leq\|p\|<2\lambda+2,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(5.23)

Note that the Fourier coefficients of a character are thus localized around the coadjoint orbit entering Kirillov’s character formula
[
59
]
, namely the sphere with radius
\|p\|=2\lambda+1
.

For consistency, one may check that the Fourier series given by eq. (
5.23
) gives back the character (
5.22
). This can be done using the lone star lemma (
4.41
) with
r=1
, as follows:

\displaystyle F_{\cal I}^{\dagger}\big[F_{\mathcal{I}}[\chi_{\lambda}]\big](\vec{X})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}\frac{\|\vec{X}\|^{2}}{\sin^{2}(\|\vec{X}\|)}\int\frac{\differential^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,F_{\mathcal{I}}[\chi_{\lambda}](p)

(5.24)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|X\|^{2}}{4\sin^{2}(\|X\|)}\int_{2\lambda}^{2\lambda+2}\!\!p\,\differential p\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin\theta\,\differential\theta\,e^{ip\|X\|\cos\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{sin}((2\lambda+1)\|\vec{X}\|)}{\sin\|\vec{X}\|}=\chi_{\lambda}(\vec{X}),

(5.25)

where we chose to work in spherical coordinates adapted to
p
.

Poisson summation for SU(2).

Let
\psi
be a complex function on
\mathfrak{su}(2)\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}
that falls off sufficiently fast at infinity.

Statement.
For almost any
X\in\mathfrak{su}(2)
, one has the Poisson summation formula

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi(\vec{X}+2\pi n\vec{u}_{X})=\frac{-1}{\sin^{2}\|\vec{X}\|}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{im\|\vec{X}\|}\int_{\mathcal{P}_{m}(\vec{X})}\frac{\differential^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\partial_{\vec{u}_{X}}^{2}F[\psi](p).

(5.26)

Here
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\vec{X})
denotes the affine plane in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
perpendicular to
\vec{X}
and going through
m\vec{u}_{X}
(see Fig.
3
),
\partial_{\vec{u}_{X}}
denotes the partial derivative in the
\vec{u}_{X}
direction, and
F[\psi]
is the Fourier transform (
5.16
).

Proof of eq. (
5.26
).

As explained under (
4.17
), deriving the Poisson summation formula for a function
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathfrak{su}(2))
amounts to equating
\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\psi
and
F_{\mathcal{I}}^{\dagger}(F(\psi))(X)
. On the one hand, eq. (
3.23
) used in the projector (
3.13
) implies

\displaystyle\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\psi(\vec{X})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi(\vec{X}+2\pi n\vec{u}_{X})

(5.27)

for almost any
X\in\mathfrak{su}(2)
. On the other hand, let
F[\psi]:=\Psi
be the Fourier transform (
5.16
) of
\psi
. The inverse Fourier transform in its form (
4.41
) yields

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}F^{\dagger}(\Psi)(\vec{X})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\int\frac{\differential^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\frac{(\vec{X}+2\pi m\vec{u}_{X})^{2}}{\sin^{2}\|\vec{X}\|}e^{i\langle p,\vec{X}+2\pi m\vec{u}_{X}\rangle}\Psi(p),

where the factor
\sim\sin^{2}\|\vec{X}\|/\|\vec{X}\|^{2}
is the Jacobian (
5.14
) of the Haar measure. Now integrating by parts and rewriting
(\vec{X}+2\pi m\vec{u}_{X})^{2}e^{i\langle p,\vec{X}+2\pi m\vec{u}_{X}\rangle}
as a Laplacian
\Delta_{p}(-e^{i\langle p,\vec{X}+2\pi m\vec{u}_{X}\rangle})
, one obtains

\displaystyle\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}F^{\dagger}(\Psi)(\vec{X})=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}\sin^{2}\|\vec{X}\|}\int\frac{\differential^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\Delta_{p}\Psi(p)\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{i\langle p,\vec{X}+2\pi m\vec{u}_{X}\rangle}.

Finally using eq. (
5.17
) and the localization of plane waves discussed there, one can further simplify the result: if
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\vec{X})
denotes the affine plane perpendicular to
\vec{X}
through
m\vec{u}_{X}
, and if
\partial_{\vec{u}_{X}}
is the partial derivative in the
\vec{u}_{X}
direction, then

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}F^{\dagger}(\Psi)(\vec{X})=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|}\sin^{2}\|\vec{X}\|}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{im\|\vec{X}\|}\int_{\mathcal{P}_{m}(\vec{X})}\frac{\differential^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\partial_{\vec{u}_{X}}^{2}\Psi(p).

Equating this with (
5.27
) leads to the Poisson summation formula (
5.26
).

\blacksquare

5.3
Poisson summation for any group

We now sketch how the derivation of a Poisson summation formula extends to generic compact Lie groups. Eqs. (
5.9
) and (
5.26
) are thus special cases of a more general result on noncommutative Fourier series.

Note first that the SU(2) Poisson summation formula (
5.26
) involves two-dimensional integrals. Indeed, for SU(2), the localization of
\mathcal{I}
-invariant plane waves (
5.17
) occurs on planes rather than points, leading to integrals along those planes. For a more general Lie group
G
, Poisson summation similarly leads to
(n-r)
-dimensional integrals, where
n=\dim(G)
and
r
is the rank (the dimension of a maximal torus). The idea of the derivation is thus the same as in SU(2): start with a function
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
, then make it
\mathcal{I}
-invariant by acting with the projector (
3.13
). With the notation introduced around (
2.16
), and using the action (
3.23
) of exponential operators on functions, this yields the periodic function

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\psi(X)=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}\psi\big(X+2\pi n^{i}a_{i}(X)\big)

(5.28)

for almost any
X\in\mathfrak{g}
.

On the other hand, one can compute the Fourier transform
\Psi(p)=F(\psi)(p)
given by (
4.9
), and note that
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\psi=\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}F^{\dagger}(\Psi)=F^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{I}}(\Psi)/\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}}
. From the definition (
4.29
), the lone star lemma (
4.40
) and the expression (
4.17
) of noncommutative plane waves, this yields (for almost any
X
)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}}}F^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{I}}(\Psi)=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}\frac{1}{J(X+2\pi n^{i}a_{i}(X))}\int_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}\frac{\differential^{n}p}{(2\pi)^{n}}e^{i\langle p,X+2\pi n^{i}a_{i}(X)\rangle}\Psi(p).

(5.29)

Now a key point, motivated by eq. (
3.2
), is that
J(X)
can be expressed as a fraction
J(X)=N(X)/D(X)
, where the numerator
N(X)
is periodic under translations by
2\pi a_{i}(X)
and the denominator
D(X)
is polynomial in
X
. These are respectively
\sin^{2}\|X\|
and
\|X\|^{2}
for SU(2), owing to eq. (
5.14
). Assuming this holds, the numerator can be put in front of the sum in (
5.29
), while the denominator can be recast as a differential operator
D(-i\partial_{p})
acting on plane waves, leading to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{I}|}}F^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{I}}(\Psi)=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}N(X)}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}\int_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}\frac{\differential^{n}p}{(2\pi)^{n}}\left(D(-i\partial_{p})e^{i\langle p,X+2\pi n^{i}a_{i}(X)\rangle}\right)\Psi(p).

(5.30)

Integration by parts here makes all derivatives hit on
\Psi
, and the sum of plane waves can be localized thanks to (
4.17
), so that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}}}F^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{I}}(\Psi)=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}N(X)}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}\int_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}\frac{\differential^{n}p}{(2\pi)^{n}}e^{i\langle p,X\rangle}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{r}\delta(\langle p,a_{j}(X)\rangle-k_{i})\right)D(i\partial_{p})\Psi(p).

(5.31)

Now recall that the basis
\{a_{i}(X)\}
of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{t}_{X}
of the maximal torus
T_{X}
passing through
X
is chosen so that
X\propto a_{1}(X)
; denote the proportionality constant by
\kappa(X)
. Then the exponential can be pulled out of the integral (
5.31
), yielding

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}}}F^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{I}}(\Psi)=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}N(X)}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}e^{ik_{1}\kappa(X)}\int_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}\frac{\differential^{n}p}{(2\pi)^{n}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{r}\delta(\langle p,a_{j}(X)\rangle-k_{i})\right)D(i\partial_{p})\Psi(p).

(5.32)

The final step is to localize the integral of
\Psi
. To this end, let us explicitly split
\mathfrak{g}
as a direct sum of a Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}_{X}
and root spaces
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha,X}
, labelled by
\alpha
:
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{t}_{X}\oplus_{\alpha}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha,X}
. One can accordingly split
\mathfrak{g}^{*}=\mathfrak{t}_{X}^{*}\oplus_{\alpha}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha,X}^{*}
, so that
p=p_{\parallel}+p_{\perp}
, with
p_{\parallel}\in\mathfrak{t}_{X}^{*}
and
p_{\perp}\in\oplus_{\alpha}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha,X}^{*}
. The localization condition
\langle p,a_{i}(X)\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}
then translates into the fact that
p_{\parallel}
must belong to the root lattice of the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}_{X}
. By contrast,
p_{\perp}
is free, so only an integral in the direction of
p_{\perp}
remains. Thus, in the differential operator
D(i\partial_{p})
, all derivatives in the
\oplus_{\alpha}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha,X}
directions are total derivatives in the integral (
5.32
), to which they do not contribute for functions
\psi
that decay at infinity. Let us therefore denote by
O(i\partial_{p_{\parallel}})
the differential operator obtained from
D(i\partial_{p})
by discarding derivatives in the
\oplus_{\alpha}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha,X}
directions. All in all, eq. (
5.32
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}}}F^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{I}}(\Psi)=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}N(X)}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}e^{i\kappa(X)k_{1}}\int_{\oplus_{\alpha}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha,X}^{*}}\frac{\differential^{n-r}p_{\perp}}{(2\pi)^{n}}O(i\partial_{p_{\parallel}})\Psi(\mathbf{k},p_{\perp}),

(5.33)

from which we conclude the following:

Statement.
Given a complex function
\psi
on
\mathfrak{g}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
that falls off sufficiently fast at infinity, one has the Poisson summation formula

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}\psi(X+2\pi n^{i}a_{i}(X))=\frac{1}{N(X)}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}e^{i\kappa(X)k_{1}}\int_{\oplus_{\alpha}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha,X}^{*}}\frac{\differential^{n-r}p_{\perp}}{(2\pi)^{n}}O(i\partial_{p_{\parallel}})\Psi(\mathbf{k},p_{\perp})

(5.34)

for almost any
X\in\mathfrak{g}
. Here the numerator
N(X)
and the operator
O(i\partial_{p_{\parallel}})
stem from the Jacobian (
3.2
) as explained below eqs. (
5.29
) and (
5.32
).

Appendix A
Appendix: Duflo quantization

In section
2
, we chose to use the symmetric quantization map (
2.9
) to order the
p_{i}
s. This was motivated by the simple correspondence it induces between exponential functions and exponential operators (
2.10
). However, an other common choice of quantization map for functions in
C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
is the so-called Duflo map
[
60
,
61
]
. The latter does not map exponential functions to exponential operators, and leads to an alternative definition of noncommutative Fourier transforms and series.

The operator algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\text{mom}}
, obtained by quantizing functions of
p
, is (isomorphic to the closure of) the universal enveloping algebra
{\cal U}(\mathfrak{g})
. A subalgebra of
{\cal U}(\mathfrak{g})
is of particular interest: the
Casimir operators
, which commute with
\mathfrak{g}
. Relatedly, in the algebra of functions of
p
, a distinguished Poisson subalgebra consists of functions that are invariant under the coadjoint action,
i.e.

Casimir functions
such that
A(p)=A(\mathrm{Ad}_{g}^{*}p)
for all
g\in G
. The key interest of Duflo’s quantization map is that it provides an algebra isomorphism between Casimir operators and Casimir functions
[
60
,
61
]
. It is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(A(p))=\mathcal{Q}_{\text{mom}}\left(J^{1/2}(i\partial_{p})A(p)\right)

(A.1)

for any function
A
in momentum space. Here
\mathcal{Q}_{\text{mom}}
denotes the symmetric ordering (
2.9
) and, as in eq. (
4.40
),
f(\partial)
denotes the differential operator obtained by Taylor expanding
f(X)
and replacing each term by the corresponding differential operator. Note that
J(0)=1
, so that the operator (
A.1
) is a sum of operators that starts with the one obtained via symmetric quantization. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(e^{-i\langle\cdot,X\rangle})=J^{1/2}(X)e^{-i\langle\hat{p},X\rangle}

(A.2)

for the special case of plane waves (
2.10
).

One can therefore define the momentum representations as in sections
3.2
–
3.3
. This time, the star product is defined as

\displaystyle A\star B:=\mathcal{D}^{-1}(\hat{A}\hat{B}),

(A.3)

with an ordering that differs from that of eq. (
3.6
). Exponential operators now act as

\displaystyle e^{-i\langle\hat{p},X\rangle}\Phi(p)=\frac{e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}}{J^{1/2}(X)}\star\Phi(p)

(A.4)

owing to eq. (
A.2
). They still get composed thanks to Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula (
2.11
), so one has

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}}{J^{1/2}(X)}\star\frac{e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}}{J^{1/2}(Y)}=\frac{e^{-i\langle p,B(X,Y)\rangle}}{J^{1/2}(B(X,Y))}.

(A.5)

Thanks to (
A.4
), the projector (
3.13
) onto the
\mathcal{I}
-invariant subspace reads

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\mathcal{I}}\cdot\Phi(p)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{X\in\operatorname{Logs(e)}}\frac{e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}}{J^{1/2}(X)}\star\Phi(p).

(A.6)

The position representations of section
3
are unaffected. It is thus straightforward to adapt the derivation (
4.5
)–(
4.8
) to find that noncommutative plane waves now read

\displaystyle E(X,p)=\frac{e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}}{J^{1/2}(X)}.

(A.7)

Their
\mathcal{I}
-invariant counterparts (
4.15
) are

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{I}}(X,p)=\frac{1}{|\operatorname{Logs}(\exp(X))|}\sum_{Y\in\operatorname{Logs(\exp(X))}}\frac{e^{-i\langle p,Y\rangle}}{J^{1/2}(Y)}.

(A.8)

The definitions of Fourier transforms and series still read as in (
4.9
) and (
4.21
), and their adjoints are also defined as in (
4.29
), with plane waves now given by (
A.7
) and (
A.8
). Almost all the properties of section
4
hold without change, as they are nearly all based on the algebraic fact that star products of plane waves represent the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff group law (
A.5
). The only properties that gets modified are the lone star lemma (
4.40
) and the explicit expressions (
4.17
)–(
4.18
) of
\mathcal{I}
-invariant plane waves. Let us start by adapting the former.

Statement.
With the Duflo quantization map (
A.1
), the lone star lemma reads

\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}\frac{\differential^{n}p}{(2\pi)^{n}}\;\bar{\Phi}\star\Psi(p)=\int_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}\frac{\differential^{n}p}{(2\pi)^{n}}\;\bar{\Phi}(p)\Psi(p).

(A.9)

In particular, the inverse Fourier transform (
4.29
) can be recast as

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{I}}^{\dagger}[\Phi](g)=\sqrt{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}}\int_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}\frac{\differential^{n}p}{(2\pi)^{n}}e^{i\langle p,\log(g)\rangle}\Phi(p).

(A.10)

without any star product, nor any differential operator.

Proof.
Since the star product is bilinear, and since the Fourier transform is bijective, it suffices to show the property for noncommutative plane waves, which provide a basis of functions in momentum space. This yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}\frac{\differential^{n}p}{(2\pi)^{n}}\;\overline{E(X,p)}\star E(Y,p)=\int\frac{\differential^{n}p}{(2\pi)^{n}}\;E(B(-X,Y),p)=\delta_{\mathfrak{g}}(B(-X,Y)).

Now using the fact that the Haar delta function for the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff group is related to the Lebesgue delta function by a Jacobian, find

\delta_{\mathfrak{g}}(B(-X,Y))=\frac{\delta^{d}(X-Y)}{J^{1/2}(X)J^{1/2}(Y)}=\int_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}\frac{\differential^{n}p}{(2\pi)^{n}}\;\overline{E(X,p)}E(Y,p),

where we also used the fact that
J
is an even function of
X
.
\blacksquare

Finally, for the explicit expression of
\mathcal{I}
-invariant plane waves, let us adopt the same notations as around (
2.16
). Let us also make an assumption similar to the one below (
5.29
), namely that
J^{1/2}(X)
can be expressed as a ratio
J^{1/2}(X)=\frac{n(X)}{d(X)}
, with a function
n(X)
that is
2\pi a_{i}(X)
-periodic and
d(X)
a polynomial or a square root of a polynomial. These would be
\sin\|X\|
and
\|X\|
for SU(2). Then, from (
A.8
), one has

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{I}}(X,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}n(X)}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}d(X+2\pi n^{i}a_{i}(X))e^{-i\langle p,X+2\pi n^{i}a_{i}(X)\rangle}

(A.11)

\displaystyle=\frac{d(i\partial_{p})}{|\mathbb{Z}|^{r}n(X)}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}e^{-i\langle p,X+2\pi n^{i}a_{i}(X)\rangle}

(A.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{d(i\partial_{p})}{n(X)}\left(e^{-i\langle p,X\rangle}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\delta(\langle p,a_{i}(X)\rangle-k_{i})}{|\mathbb{Z}|}\right),

(A.13)

which holds for almost any
X\in\mathfrak{g}
. This mildly differs from the
\cal I
-invariant plane wave (
