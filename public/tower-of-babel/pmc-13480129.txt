Delayed Stimulus to V6 R-Wave Peak Time for Selective Left Bundle Branch Pacing: Characteristics and Underlying Mechanisms
Authors: Xinhui Peng, Longfu Jiang, Zaixing Zheng, Hao Wu, Haiming Feng, Jia bo Shen, Heng dong Li, Nan Zheng, Xiaoming Bao, Sujun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelective left bundle branch pacing (SLBBP) preserves left ventricular synchrony via low stable capture thresholds and shortened stimulus-to-V6 R-wave peak time (S–V6 RWPT), yet delayed S–V6 RWPT (DRWPT) occurs in some recipients. This study aimed to define DRWPT’s electrocardiographic and intracardiac electrogram features during SLBBP and investigate its mechanisms.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 368 SLBBP patients (September 2020 to November 2024, continuous monitoring), enrolled 11 with DRWPT as the study group, enrolled 33 with 1:3 matched shortened S–V6 RWPT (SRWPT) as controls, and analyzed electrocardiogram/intracardiac electrogram features with the isoelectric interval as the endpoint.ResultsDRWPT was identified in 3% (11 of 368) of the cohort. Baseline pacing parameters in the DRWPT group were as follows: mean QRS duration 128 ± 35.4 ms, unipolar initial myocardial threshold 1.6 ± 0.7 V, initial left bundle branch threshold 0.7 ± 0.1 V, sensed R-wave amplitude 8.0 ± 2.6 mV, and pacing impedance of 644.1 ± 157.8 Ω. Compared with controls, the DRWPT group had a significantly higher right bundle branch block incidence (63.6% vs 6.06%, P < 0.001), longer median QRS duration (138 ms [IQR 96-152] vs 96 ms [IQR 86-114], P = 0.03), longer isoelectric interval (45.0 ms [IQR 35.0-64.0] vs 31.0 ms [IQR 25.0-37.0], P = 0.005), and longer S–V6 RWPT (94 ms [IQR 81.0-122.0] vs 66 ms [IQR 63.0-72.0], P < 0.001). No significant between-group difference was found in those with stimulus-to-V6 R-wave peak time for left ventricular septal pacing (P = 0.203).ConclusionsDRWPT, linked to impaired left bundle branch conduction with prolonged isoelectric interval and S–V6 RWPT, is more prevalent in patients with native right bundle branch block. The isoelectric interval and current of injury (COI) are critical for guiding lead implantation during continuous monitoring-guided SLBBP.Keywords: left bundle branch pacing, delayed stimulus to V6 R-wave peak time, isoelectric interval, current of injury, continuous monitoring technology

Left bundle branch pacing (LBBP) has emerged in recent years as a novel physiological pacing technique with a low and stable threshold.1 Previous studies have validated its feasibility and effectiveness, and identified a variety of interesting electrophysiological phenomena during the transventricular-septal procedure.2, 3, 4 The electrocardiographic (ECG) and intracardiac electrogram (EGM) characteristics of selective LBBP (SLBBP) can be summarized as follows: (i) nonselective LBBP (NSLBBP) to SLBBP or NSLBBP to left ventricular septal pacing (LVSP) during threshold testing; (ii) a typical right bundle branch block pattern; (iii) an isoelectric interval in the EGM; (iv) sometimes with left bundle branch (LBB) potential (PoLBB); and (v) abrupt shortening of R-wave peak time (RWPT) or left ventricular activation time (LVAT) in V6 ≥ 10 ms at high output during deep septal positioning, with subsequent short and constant LVAT at low output with further advancement of the lead.5, 6, 7 Transition in QRS morphology is considered the gold standard to assess LBB capture. Wu et al.8 reported that using an isoelectric interval as an endpoint to guide SLBBP was feasible in a high proportion of captured LBB cases. However, we have discovered a novel electrophysiological phenomenon of delayed stimulus-to-V6 R-wave peak time (DRWPT) during SLBBP. Here, we report 11 cases to demonstrate the characteristics of the ECG and EGM, and to reveal possible mechanisms of DRWPT.

Methods

Study population

We retrospectively included 368 consecutive patients who successfully underwent SLBBP procedures using a continuous reccording method, from September 2020 to November 2024. Eleven of them with DRWPT and 33 with shortened stimulus-to-V6 R-wave peak time (SRWPT) were included in the study and control groups, respectively. The ethics committee approved the study protocol. Informed consent was obtained from all the patients.

Definition of DRWPT

DRWPT was defined as follows: (i) recording an isoelectric interval as the endpoint of SLBBP; and (ii) between 2 adjacent beats, no obvious electrophysiological phenomenon of abrupt shortening was observed in the SV6 RWPT; instead, prolongation was noted.

Lead implantation and mapping

A pacing lead (model 3830, 69 cm, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) was delivered into the right ventricle via a C315 sheath (Medtronic) with the assistance of John Jiang’s connecting cable, which helps monitor the ECG and EGM in real time during the lead screwing process in the right anterior oblique 30° fluoroscopic view. Compared with traditional connecting cables, the John Jiang’s connecting cable has a special rotating device that allows it to not only rotate freely but also maintain continuity9,10 (Fig. 1; Video 1, view video online). Twelve-lead ECG synchronized with EGM was continuously recorded with an EP-Workmate TM recording system (Abbott Laboratories, Chicago, IL). An optimal high-pass filter setting of 200 Hz was recommended for discrete local ventricular EGM detection.11 The isoelectric interval and the changes in the COI were utilized to guide LBB capture and prevent ventricular perforation.8,12,13

The John Jiang's connecting cable with a connected lead.

After all preparations were completed, the sheath was slowly withdrawn until the distal end of the catheter reached the superior margin of the tricuspid valve annulus, adjacent to the right ventricle. Right ventriculography in the right anterior oblique 30° view, which can show the tricuspid valve annulus, was performed to help ascertain the ideal location of LBBP, eliminating the need to locate the His bundle potential or rely on a typical QRS morphology in lead V1 (Fig. 2A). Continuous unipolar pacing at 2 V/0.5 ms was performed throughout the process with a sweep speed of 100 mm/s. The pacing mode was performed at > 10 beats per minute above the intrinsic sinus rate. As the lead was screwed in, V1 began to transform into a Qr morphology. The output was intermittently increased to 5 V/0.5 ms to monitor whether the S–V6 RWPT shortened abruptly compared with the pacing output (2 V/0.5 ms). The impedance was monitored during the whole process. When a significant decrease in impedance was detected at the initial pacing output (2 V/0.5 ms), the lead screwing speed was immediately reduced. The pacing output was then gradually decreased until the isoelectric interval could be observed, to ascertain that the LBB was directly captured. Meanwhile, COI in the unfiltered EGM was used to guide the speed of screwing of the lead. When the COI decreased significantly, lead screwing was slowed or halted.

Fluoroscopic image of Case 7. (A) Fluoroscopic image with contrast injection to show the tricuspid valve annulus in right anterior oblique 30°. (B) Placement of the electrode into the septum in the left anterior oblique 45°.

Consistent with prior studies,14 once the lead was properly positioned, sheath angiography in the left anterior oblique 45° view was performed to demonstrate the lead depth in the interventricular septum (Fig. 2B). After the pacing lead was fixed, the final pacing parameters test was performed before connecting the pacemaker.

Data collection

Baseline patient characteristics and pacing characteristics of ECG and EGM were documented, in addition to indications for pacing and echocardiographic data. Upon successful achievement of SLBBP, the unipolar pacing QRS duration, initial myocardial threshold, and initial LBB threshold were noted. The isoelectric intervals were also recorded and measured, in addition to the presence of PoLBB. The S–V6lvsp RWPT (stimulus-to-V6 R-wave peak time for left ventricular septal pacing) and S–V6 RWPT of 2 adjacent beats were measured between the 2 groups, respectively. Final thresholds, R-wave amplitude, and impedance were recorded, with lead implant depth included simultaneously. Acute procedure-related complications, such as pneumothorax, lead dislodgement, pericardial effusion, and subclavian vascular injury, were recorded.

Statistical analysis

The random sampling procedure was performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were expressed as percentages. For normally distributed data, the Student t test was used to compare differences between groups. When data were not normally distributed, the interquartile range (IQR) was used to describe dispersion. A χ2 test was performed to assess the association between RBBB and the incidence of DRWPT. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Macintosh (version 26.0, IBM, Armonk, NY).

The mean QRS duration, isoelectric interval, S–V6 RWPT, and S–V6lvsp RWPT of the DRWPT group were 128 ± 35.4 ms, 48.2 ± 19.7 ms, 102.2 ± 25.4 ms, and 88.2 ± 16.4 ms, respectively. The mean unipolar initial myocardial threshold was 1.6 ± 0.7 V, and the mean initial LBB threshold was 0.7 ± 0.1 V. The mean sensed amplitude of the R wave and the pacing impedance were 8 ± 2.6 mV and 644.1 ± 157.8 Ω. The mean implant depth was 15.7 ± 2.9 mm.

The entire study cohort had a mean age of 72.7 ± 11.1 years, and male participants accounted for 54.5% (24 of 44) of those in the study. When comparing the DRWPT group and the SRWPT group, no statistically significant differences were found in 2 key cardiac structural and functional parameters—left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVDD).

No statistically significant differences in threshold, impedance, or sensing were observed between the 2 groups. The average baseline QRS duration in the DRWPT group and the SRWPT group was 102.3 ± 21.1 ms and 128 ± 35.4 ms, respectively, with a significant difference between the 2 groups (median: 138 ms, interquartile range [IQR]: 96-152 ms vs median: 96 ms, IQR: 86-114 ms, P = 0.03). DRWPT resulted in a significantly longer isoelectric interval (45.0 ms, IQR: 35.0-64.0 ms vs 31.0 ms, IQR: 25.0-37.0 ms, P = 0.005) and S–V6 RWPT (94.0 ms, IQR: 81.0-122.0 ms vs 66.0 ms, IQR: 63.0-72.0 ms, P < 0.001) compared with SRWPT. Meanwhile, DRWPT was associated with a higher incidence of occurrence of RBBB than SRWPT (63.6% vs 6.0%, P < 0.001). No significant difference in S–V6lvsp RWPT was found between the 2 groups (88.2 ± 16.4 ms vs 82.6 ± 10.8 ms, P = 0.203). PoLBB was mapped in 5 patients (45.5%) in the DRWPT group, and in 24 patients (72.7%) in the SRWPT group. No complications were noted.

In the second case, under the same voltage stimulation, the prolonged isoelectric interval remained identical, whereas the S–V6 RWPT varied. Unipolar pacing with decreasing output showed a change in QRS morphology from LVSP to SLBBP. RBBB and left-axis deviation suggest that the pacing site is located in the diseased left posterior fascicle (Fig. 4, A and B; Fig. 8C). However, in the 11th case, not only does the S–V6 RWPT differ, but the isoelectric interval also showed discrepancies. RBBB and right-axis deviation indicate that the pacing site is located in the diseased left anterior fascicle (Fig. 4C; Fig. 8A). This suggests that such patients may have concurrent LBB conduction delay and intraventricular conduction delay. The concurrent LBB and intraventricular conduction delays may reflect heterogeneous myocardial fibrosis or impaired impulse propagation in the ventricular myocardium, which needs to be further evaluated by cardiac electrophysiology studies.

In the third case, when SLBBP was achieved, the PoLBB was intermittent, suggesting possible disorders in the LBB (Fig. 5C). Meanwhile, in the DRWPT group, the proportion of patients with PoLBB was lower than that in the SRWPT group, indicating that the probability of LBB disorders in the DRWPT group tends to be higher than that in the SRWPT group, provided that the sample size is larger. This result may also be a specific manifestation of LBB disorders.

In cases 2, 3, 4, and 6, the final LBB threshold was not measured because the myocardial threshold was lower than the LBB threshold. In case 10, during the sheath-withdrawal process, lead dislodgment occurred, resulting in failure to obtain the final myocardial threshold and LBB threshold; however, successful pacing was achieved in another LBB region.

Discussion

LBBP was developed to capture the LBB and achieve rapid and physiological left ventricular activation, thereby avoiding ventricular conduction delay with a low threshold and higher R-wave amplitudes, presenting with a typically narrower QRS duration with an RBBB pattern on ECG and EGM compared with intrinsic QRS.15 Almost all SLBBP show SRWPT. This study demonstrated an interesting electrophysiological phenomenon of DRWPT with a prolonged S–V6 RWPT. During the process, NSLBBP was not obtained by different pacing outputs, and a transition from NSLBBP to LVSP capture was not observed during the threshold testing. Besides, no abrupt shortening occurred between 2 adjacent paced beats in the EGM.

The ECG and EGM characteristics of DRWPT can be summarized as follows: (i) an RBBB pattern in intrinsic rhythm is usual; (ii) SLBBP occurs with a longer isoelectric interval; (iii) neither the transition from LVSP or NSLBBP to SLBBP, nor the transition process between LVSP and NSLBBP, can be identified during output reduction; and (iv) a prolonged S–V6 RWPT of ≥ 80 ms is usual (Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5, Figure 6, Figure 7). During continuous unipolar pacing with the lead rotated toward the left side of the intraventricular septum (IVS), the left ventricular myocardium activation transmits from the IVS to the left ventricular septum, and finally to the LBB before entering the purkinje fibres. However, when the LBB conduction slowly arrives, it does not cause significant abrupt shortening presented in the EGM, due to a delayed LBB conduction system. We propose that the LBB threshold must be lower than the myocardial threshold. At high voltage output, both the left ventricular myocardium and the LBB are activated simultaneously, whereas at low voltage output, the LBB is activated directly. Consequently, such a transition process cannot be distinguished. Meanwhile, prior to reaching SLBBP, the pattern may present as LVSP or a fused pattern of LVSP and NSLBBP. During the process of reducing output, the absence of direct myocardial capture and the delay in the LBB conduction system allow the recording of a prolonged isoelectric interval; consequently, this manifests as a DRWPT.

We hypothesize that one of the possible mechanisms for the occurrence of this electrophysiological phenomenon may be the presence of some kind of disorders in the LBB conduction system, which leads to conduction delay. Previous studies have confirmed that the electrophysiological and anatomic findings of the His bundle and its branches indicate longitudinal dissociation in the conduction system.16 Recent investigation has shown that focal block may simply be present at the level of the anatomic left-sided His fibres.17 The main trunk of the LBB extends 10 to 15 millimetres downward toward the apex.18 The trunk divides into 3 fascicles, followed by numerous subendocardial branches with interconnections. The size, branching pattern, number, and location of the fascicular branches vary, with significant individual differences in anatomic structure.19 Iida et al. have reported a patient with RBBB who underwent LBBP.20 During the process, a transition in QRS morphology from nonselective to selective pacing was observed. However, this change was accompanied by an extension of the LVAT from 96 ms to 116 ms. Meanwhile, the intracardiac EGM showed a pattern of left posterior fascicular (LPF). They considered this to be caused by conduction delay due to disorders in the left anterior fascicle (LAF). The possible mechanism they proposed was that the disorders in the left posterior fascicle led to conduction delay, thereby resulting in a prolonged LVAT.

In our patient cohort, the leads were precisely positioned on one of the LBB fascicles. When this specific fascicle is affected by a lesion, conduction delay occurs. In addition, the threshold of this fascicle is lower than both the myocardial threshold and the thresholds of other LBB fascicles, resulting in prolongation of the isoelectric interval; however, this difference does not truly reflect a significant difference in intraventricular conduction between the 2 groups. During LVSP in both patient groups, the S–V6LVSP RWPT was 88.2 ± 16.4 ms in the DRWPT group and 82.6 ± 10.8 ms in the SRWPT group, with no statistically significant difference observed (P = 0.203). At high-voltage output, the lead can simultaneously activate the left ventricular myocardium, the normally fast-conducting LBB fascicles, and the LBB fascicle with lesion-induced slow conduction; at this point, intraventricular conduction is consistent between the 2 groups. In contrast, at low-voltage output, only the lesioned LBB fascicle can be activated, resulting in left ventricular conduction delay. These findings fully confirm that conduction delay in the LBB is limited to a subset of LBB fascicles, excluding ventricular myocytes and the fast-conducting LBB fascicles.

Based on the QRS morphology of unipolar pacing, LBB disorders were suspected to originate from the LAF in 2 cases and from the LPF in another 2 cases among the 11 patients. Seven patients could not have their disorders determined as originating from either the LAF or the LPF. Burri et al. defined this type of pacing mode as left mid-septal fascicular pacing (LSFP).21 Left fascicular pacing (LFP) is defined by the capture of one of the LBB fascicles or its distal arborisation, including left anterior fascicle pacing (LAFP), left posterior fascicular pacing (LPFP), and LSFP. Jastrzebski et al. have demonstrated that LFP was the dominant form of LBBP, encountered in 69.5% of participants.22 Among the 7 patients, therefore, we hypothesize that the disorders may originate from a certain branch of the LBB fascicles, such as the left mid-septal fascicles (Fig. 8), and the threshold of this fascicle must be lower than both the myocardial threshold and the threshold of other unaffected fascicles.

Our research focuses on how to determine whether the LBB is captured while avoiding perforation without abrupt shortening of RWPT in V6 during the screwing process. First, COI in the unfiltered EGM was used to guide the speed of screwing of the lead and to avoid perforation (Video 2, case 1, view video online). Shen et al. demonstrated the clinical significance of changes in COI and QRS morphology for LBBP using a continuous pacing technique and filtered and unfiltered electrograms. When the amplitude of COI reaches peak, the lead must be rotated slowly, and when the QRS morphology changes from a QS to an rSR pattern dynamically with the decrease of COI amplitude, gradually decreasing the pacing output is important and necessary. The lead rotation should be stopped immediately until an isoelectric interval is directly observed in EGM with the initial pacing output (2 V/0.5 ms) or during a decrease in pacing output. If the COI amplitude decreases significantly or loss of capture occurs, which indicates possible perforation, the implantation should be stopped and the screw should be repositioned. Second, an isoelectric interval is used as the endpoint of SLBBP as previously reported. Third, a continuous recording device for real-time monitoring of ECG and EGM is essential during the entire transventricular-septal process.23

Study limitations

This study has some limitations. First, as a single-centre retrospective study, it included only a small sample, which may limit the ability to derive robust conclusions. Additionally, the incidence of this condition at our centre is relatively low. Second, the specific location of LBB conduction delay could not be determined. Third, the reason DRWPT is more likely to occur in patients with RBBB remains unclear. Fourth, long-term follow-up data for evaluating clinical outcomes are lacking.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to characterize the ECG and EGM features of DRWPT during SLBBP. DRWPT may be associated with disorders of LBB fascicles, resulting in conduction delay in a portion of the LBB. DRWPT is more prevalent in patients with electrocardiographically confirmed native RBBB. The COI and isoelectric interval are crucial for guiding the transventricular-septal lead implantation procedure for DRWPT through real-time continuous monitoring during SLBBP.
