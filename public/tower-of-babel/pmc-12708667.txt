Association of social support with sleep and subjective health outcomes in Ghanaian artisanal miners
Authors: Emmanuel Nyaaba, Kabila Abass, Razak M Gyasi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal social support (SS) has been linked to improved mental health, quality of life, cognition, daily activities, and social participation, but data on the association of SS with sleep quality (SQ) and self-rated health (SRH) outcomes is still scarce in lower middle-income settings. This study examined the associations of global social support with sleep and subjective health status, and tested whether these associations are mediated by depression. Data for this study were collected from 664 artisanal miners in Ghana. SS was measured using the Social Support Rating Scale-10. SRH was assessed using the 5-item from the Medical Outcomes Study Short Form-36, while SQ was measured with two items measured on a 5-point scale. Depression was measured using the PHQ-9 scale. To test these associations, multiple regression models and a mediation analysis were fitted (model 4). The mean ± SD age was 28.8 ± 8.2 years; males = 84.3%. After adjustment for socio-demographic and health related covariates, global SS was significantly and positively correlated with improved SRH (B = 0.45, 95%CI [0.43, 0.46]) and with improved SQ (B = 0.02, 95%CI [0.01, 0.03]). Parallel mediation analyses revealed that depression significantly explained the association of SS with both SRH (~ 1%) and SQ (~ 33%). This study has provided evidence that global SS plays a protective role in enhancing SRH and sleep outcomes among small-scale miners. Given the difficult and hazardous nature of their work, promoting general SS and addressing mental health challenges could contribute significantly to improving their overall well-being.Keywords: Social support, Sleep quality, Self-rated health, Depression, GhanaSubject terms: Psychology, Environmental social sciences, Health occupations

Introduction

Achieving the best quality of life possible remains a key public health objective, as highlighted by the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3, which aims to ensure health and well-being for all1. Addressing critical health determinants, including sleep quality (SQ) and self-rated health (SRH), is essential to achieve this goal. SQ, which encompasses sufficient duration, efficiency, and restorative function, is vital for cognitive performance, emotional regulation, and physiological recovery2. Similarly, as a subjective measure of one’s overall health, SRH is a well-established predictor of morbidity, functional decline, and mortality3–5. Empirical studies show that insufficient SQ and suboptimal SRH are correlated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease6, metabolic disorders7,8, mental health problems9,10, and mortality11. These health threats disproportionately affect the marginalized population, including those working in informal and hazardous occupations such as small-scale mining, which highlights the need for targeted research and intervention.

In high-risk occupational settings, such as small-scale mining, the interaction between sleep quality and SRH is notably intricate. The characteristics of small-scale mining subject workers to extreme physical exertion, extended working hours, occupational hazards, and chronic psychosocial stressors12. Indeed, Rugulies et al.13 noted in their global review that psychosocial work exposures, including job strain or low job control, are significant drivers of onset of depressive symptoms and poor mental health outcomes in working populations. These factors may contribute to impaired sleep quality and negative health perceptions among miners. Poor sleep among these workers may lead to increased fatigue, impaired cognitive function, and higher risks of occupational accidents14. At the same time, negative SRH assessments are often associated with chronic pain, stress-related disorders, and diminished productivity15. Crucially, the burden of poor SQ and low SRH among the vulnerable population in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is exacerbated by challenging working conditions, economic vulnerabilities16, and limited access to healthcare resources17. In this regard, identifying protective factors that enhance sleep quality and SRH is a critical first step in developing targeted interventions to improve overall well-being and long-term health outcomes in this high-risk population.

Existing literature has established social support as a key determinant of health, demonstrating its significant role in enhancing mental well-being, SQ, and SRH18. Social support, which includes emotional (comfort and reassurance), informational (guidance and advice), and instrumental (tangible aid) assistance19, mitigates stress, strengthens psychological resilience, and facilitates adaptive coping mechanisms20,21. Kim et al.22, in a conceptual review, identified physiological pathways through which social support enhances health, including reduced inflammation and improved cardiovascular function, both of which are critical for better sleep quality and overall well-being. Moreover, emerging empirical evidence supports the association between SS and psychological/mental well-being. For instance, in a prospective study using datasets from the national French working conditions survey (2013, 2016, and 2019) involving 15,971 employees, Niedhammer et al.23 found that lower psychosocial support at work was significantly associated with poorer self-rated health. Again, in a meta-analysis of 61 studies, Kent de Grey et al.24 found that strong social networks reduce the risk of sleep disturbances by 30%. Similarly, Mensah25 using a cross-sectional dataset from 35 countries in Europe and reported that greater social support significantly reduces the likelihood of developing sleep problems job stress and mental disorders over time. In a cross-sectional study involving 348 adolescents in Nepal, Poudel et al.26 observed that higher perceived social support was associated with better psychological well-being. SS has been hypothesized to improve well-being by alleviating mental distress21, particularly by reducing symptoms of depression27. Through enhanced emotional regulation and stress buffering, social support lowers depressive symptoms, which, in turn, contributes to improved sleep quality by reducing cognitive arousal, intrusive thoughts, and nighttime awakenings28. Furthermore, lower depression levels enhance SRH via fostering more positive health perceptions, increasing motivation for self-care, and reducing somatic complaints29,30.

Despite this growing evidence, data on the interaction of SS with SQ and SRH in SSA are still sparse. Most of the existing studies were conducted in high income countries and focused on samples of older adults22,27, which limits the generalization of the findings to populations in low resource settings and in particular to high-risk groups. What is important is that data on the role of depression in these relationships are poorly understood. Given the precarious working conditions faced by small-scale miners and the well-established link between SS and health outcomes, it is needful to review this relationship in the context of SSA. This knowledge can be of key importance in the development of targeted clinical and public health interventions to improve well-being, especially in LMICs context. The purpose of this study is to investigate the association of social support with sleep and subjective health status among Ghanaian small-scale miners, and the role of depression in these associations.

Theoretical framework and hypotheses development

Empirical evidence consistently shows that SS is a key factor influencing SQ and SRH. This study draws on two theoretical frameworks: the Stress-Buffering Model31 and the Psychological Mediation Model32, which help explain how social relationships affect health outcomes. The Stress-Buffering Model suggests that SS acts as a buffer against stress, supporting both psychological and physical health by reducing stress responses. Individuals with strong social networks tend to exhibit lower physiological and emotional stress levels, thereby increasing their resilience to stress-related health issues33,34. In the context of small-scale mining, which is marked by challenging working conditions and economic instability35, SS may help mitigate the negative health impacts of stress, thereby improving SQ and SRH.

Building on this, the Psychological Mediation Model32 suggests that SS affects health partly through its impact on mental health conditions such as depression. Depression is both a consequence and contributor to poor sleep and health outcomes, linked to increased rumination, arousal, and fatigue30. SS may help reduce depressive symptoms by enhancing emotional regulation and promoting a sense of belonging and support36. In turn, lower depression levels are associated with improved SQ and better self-perceptions of health. While it is acknowledged that depression and sleep problems often co-occur and that depressive symptoms may also shape individuals’ perception of support, the theoretical models adopted here emphasize a directional pathway where SS buffers against psychological distress, thereby improving health outcomes. These frameworks provide a basis for exploring direct and indirect pathways, while recognizing that complex and potentially reciprocal relationships may also exist. Accordingly, based on the above literature and theoretical perspectives, we hypothesize that:

H1:

Social support will be positively associated with sleep quality and self-rated health among small-scale miners, controlling for occupational and demographic factors.

H2:

Depression will partially mediate the relationship between social support and sleep quality, such that higher social support will be associated with lower depression, which in turn will relate to better sleep quality.

H3:

Depression will partially mediate the relationship between social support and self-rated health, such that lower depression will be associated with improved health status (see Fig. 1a and b, hypothetical model).

Methods

Design and population

This community-based cross-sectional survey was conducted in Obuasi, a town in the Ashanti Region of Ghana known for its rich gold deposits and long-standing history of both formal and informal mining activities37. It targeted communities that serve as key hubs for small-scale mining operations, particularly those located near active mining sites. These included Binsere, Nhyiaeso, Gausu, Memrewa, Ntonsua, Apitisu, and Sanso.

To estimate the required sample size, a prevalence rate of 50% (0.5) was assumed in accordance with standard practice for Cochran’s formula38, as this value maximizes variability and thus provides the most conservative sample size estimate when no prior data are available for eligible miners in the study area. The calculation used a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96) and a 5% margin of error (e = 0.05), resulting in a minimum required sample of 384 participants. To account for potential non-responses and outliers, the final sample size was increased to 664, ensuring adequate statistical power for multivariate and mediation analyses39,40.

Measures

Predictor

Social support (SS) was measured using Xiao’s41 10-item scale, assessing subjective (4 items), objective (3 items), and support utilization (3 items), rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Subjective SS measured perceived emotional support (e.g., “How much can you rely on others in tough times?”). Objective SS evaluated available tangible support (e.g., “Who have you turned to for financial support?”). Support utilization assessed help-seeking behavior (e.g., “How often do you seek support during challenges?”). Taken together, the scale reflects both emotional and instrumental aspects of global social support (thus, including assistance from one’s broader social network, family, friends, community members), rather than workplace-specific assistance (such as help from co-workers or supervisors during work processes) as commonly assessed in occupational health research42–44. Based on these 10 items, we computed the overall social support index (range: 10–50), where higher scores reflect greater overall perceived support (emotionally, tangibly, and in terms of support-seeking behavior), and lower scores indicate weaker or less accessible support. The scale demonstrated excellent reliability in this study (∝= 0.901).

Outcomes

Self-rated health (SRH) was measured using five questions from the Medical Outcomes Study Short Form-3645. The questions included: (1) “How would you rate your health today?” (2) “How does your health compare to two years ago?” (3) “I am as healthy as anybody I know,” (4) “My health is better now than two years ago,” and (5) “My health is excellent now.” Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale. A SRH index (range: 5–25) was calculated by summing all the variable items, with higher scores indicating poorer self-rated health, and lower scores reflecting better perceived health status. The internal consistency of the scale for this study was good (∝ = 0.83). Sleep problems were assessed using two items adapted from the World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule 2.0 (WHODAS 2.0)46. Although WHODAS 2.0 is primarily designed to assess functional limitations and disability related to health conditions, these two items specifically target key aspects of sleep problems relevant to overall sleep health, including difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep and feeling rested during the day. Such brief items have been effectively used in prior research as proxies for sleep disturbances, especially in low-resource settings where concise yet valid measures are required47,48. Given the context of a low-resource setting and the need for brief, valid measures, these WHODAS-derived items provide an appropriate and culturally relevant proxy for sleep quality. The two questions asked participants: (1) “Overall, in the last 30 days, how much of a problem did you have with sleeping (e.g., falling asleep, waking frequently during the night, or waking too early in the morning)?” and (2) “How much of a problem did you have due to not feeling rested and refreshed during the day (e.g., feeling tired, lacking energy)?” Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale from none (1) to extreme (5). A composite sleep problem score (range: 2–10) was calculated, with higher scores reflecting sleep problems/disturbance (i.e., more problems with sleep and feeling rested), and lower scores reflecting better sleep quality or fewer problems (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.78 in this study).

Mediator

Depression was measured using the nine-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), a validated tool for assessing depressive symptoms49. Participants indicated how often they were bothered by problems like feeling down or trouble sleeping over the past two weeks. Responses were recorded on a 4-point Likert scale: 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Nearly every day). The total PHQ-9 score ranged from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating moderate-to- severe depressive symptoms, and lower scores reflecting minimal or no symptoms. The PHQ-9 demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = 0.81) in this study.

Control variables

Sociodemographic data were collected, including age, sex, marital status, educational level, residential status, living arrangements, and work duration, following previous studies20,27,50. In addition to these variables, we controlled for key psychological and health-related factors known to influence both social support and health outcomes. Specifically, perceived stress and anxiety symptoms were included to account for potential confounding effects in the relationship between SS, depression, sleep quality, and self-rated health. Stress and anxiety are conceptually and empirically related to both perceived social support and health outcomes51,52, and adjusting for them allows us to isolate the unique associations attributable to SS. Perceived stress was assessed using the four-item Perceived Stress Scale53, where participants rated items on a 5-point Likert scale. A composite score was calculated (α = 0.78). Anxiety symptoms were measured with the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 scale54 with items rated on a 4-point scale, yielding scores from 0 to 21 (α = 0.78). Chronic conditions, including hypertension, diabetes, respiratory diseases, and arthritis, were assessed with a yes/no response. Alcohol intake was recorded as a binary variable (yes/no). These covariates were included to reduce bias from confounding in estimating the direct and indirect pathways between SS, depression, SRH, and SQ.

Data collection procedures and ethics

A multi-stage sampling strategy was implemented. First, communities were purposively selected based on proximity to mining activities and insights from expert consultations. Within these communities, houses were selected using a spatially guided systematic random sampling approach. Random starting points were identified in each community, followed by the selection of houses using a spatially guided systematic random sampling approach. For the purpose of this study, communities with fewer than 250 houses were classified as small, while those with 250 or more houses were considered large, based on estimates from local informants and prior community profiling. Accordingly, every third houses was selected in small communities, and every fifth in large ones. In households with more than one eligible respondent (e.g., individuals involved in small-scale mining for at least six months), simple random sampling (e.g., balloting) was used to select one participant. Additionally, miners were also recruited directly at active mining sites. At each site, eligible miners who were not reached at the household level were approached, and one individual was selected using a simple random sampling technique (balloting).

To ensure data quality and consistency, all interviewers underwent two weeks of intensive training on survey administration, research ethics, and field safety protocols. Data collection was conducted in both English and Twi, with survey instruments rigorously translated and back-translated to ensure linguistic accuracy. A dedicated field supervisor monitored daily operations and enforced adherence to the sampling protocol, using unique identifiers such as worksite location and demographic details to ensure participant uniqueness and maintain data integrity.

The study adhered to the ethical guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki55 and received ethics committee approval by the Committee on Human Research, Publications and Ethics (CHRPE) at the School of Medical Sciences, Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology, Kumasi, Ghana (Ref: CHRPE/AP/0103/25). Local authority permissions were obtained. Written informed consent was secured after explaining voluntary participation and withdrawal rights. Data were anonymized for confidentiality. Field safety protocols, working in pairs, maintaining base communication, and limiting visits to designated hours were strictly followed to ensure researcher safety.

Data analysis procedures

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sample characteristics. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies with percentages, while continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations. Pearson’s correlations assessed bivariate associations between SS, depression, SRH, and SQ. Multiple linear regression models explored the associations between SS and SRH/SQ. In Model 1, each outcome variable was regressed on SS to assess the unadjusted association. Model 2 adjusted for socio-demographic, behavioral, occupational, and health-related variables as covariates, but excluded perceived stress and anxiety to avoid potential overcontrol and underestimation of associations, allowing us to observe the associations between SS and the outcomes without the influence of these potential mediators. Model 3 then included all covariates, including perceived stress and anxiety, to assess the fully adjusted associations. The mediating role of depression was assessed using the PROCESS macro39 with bootstrapping and 5000 samples to estimate direct and indirect effects. Mediation was significant if the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the indirect effect did not include zero. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25, with significance set at p < .05. The models’ goodness of fit was assessed using R-squared values and F-statistics.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 664 artisanal miners were included in the final analysis. Table 1 present the details of the sample characteristics. The average age was 28.82 (SD = 8.19) years, with the majority (79.5%) falling within the 18–35 age group, and 84.3% were males. The majority of the sample were single (65.1%), had basic education (41.0%), and were migrants (51.8%). Moreover, half of the participants (50.6%) reported living alone, while 39.8% lived with family. On average, every participant had worked as a small-scale miner for 5.95 (SD = 5.32) years, and 60.5% reported consuming alcohol. Participants demonstrated a generally positive self-rated health status, with the majority (60.3%) reporting their health as excellent, very good, or good. The mean SS scores were 13.43 (SD = 4.54) for subjective SS, 10.02 (SD = 3.96) for objective SS, and 8.01 (SD = 2.67) for SS utilization.

Main regression models

The association of SS with SRH and SQ among artisanal miners was analyzed using multivariable regression models (Tables 3 and 4). In Model 1, higher SS score (greater perceived support) was significantly and positively associated with lower SRH scores (better self-rated health status) (B = 0.47, 95%CI [0.46, 0.48]). In Model 2, without controlling for perceived stress and anxiety, SS remained significantly associated with better SRH scores (B = 0.47, 95%CI [0.46, 0.48]). After adjusting for all covariates in Model 3, SS remained a significant predictor of good SRH (B = 0.45, 95%CI [0.43, 0.46]). This positive direction between SS and SRH implies that miners with more perceived general social support reported better self-rated health status. For SQ, Model 1 showed that higher SS score was significantly and positively associated with lower SQ scores, reflecting better sleep outcomes (B = 0.05, 95%CI [0.03, 0.06]), as higher SQ scores indicate sleep problems. In Model 2, without controlling for perceived stress and anxiety, the association remained positive and significant (B = 0.03, 95% CI [0.02, 0.04]). After adjusting for all covariates in Model 3, SS still demonstrated a significant, though attenuated, beneficial effect on sleep quality (B = 0.02, 95% CI [0.01, 0.03]).

Parallel mediation analysis

Our mediation analysis revealed that depression significantly explained the association between SS and both outcomes. Specifically, greater SS scores were associated with lower depression scores (B = – 0.04, 95%CI [– 0.06, – 0.02]), indicating that individuals with more perceived support experienced fewer depressive symptoms. This negative association suggests that social support may serve a protective role against psychological distress among artisanal miners. Lower depression scores, in turn, were positively associated with lower SRH (B = 0.12, 95%CI [0.08, 0.16]) and SQ (B = 0. 23, 95%CI [0.19, 0.28]) scores, which reflect better health and sleep outcomes. Depression significantly mediated the SS–SRH pathway (β = – 0.05, BootSE = 0.002, Boot 95% CI: -0.008, -0.001), accounting for 0.86% of the total effect, and the SS–SQ pathway (β = -0.009, BootSE = 0.003, Boot 95% CI: – 0.116, – 0.02), accounting for a more substantial 33.33% of the total effect (see Table 5) The bootstrap CI, based on 5000 samples, did not include zero, confirming the robustness of the mediation effects. These results indicate a significant indirect pathway through depression, where higher social support indirectly leads to better health and sleep outcomes by reducing depressive symptoms. Importantly, since the direct effects of SS on SRH (B = 0.126, SE = 0.006, 95% CI: 0.454, 0.477) and on SQ (B = 0.035, SE = 0.007, 95% CI: 0.0215, 0.0491) remained statistically significant after including depression in the model, this suggests partial mediation. Discussion

Summary of findings

We investigated the association between SS, SRH, and SQ among artisanal miners, as well as the mediating role of depression in these relationships. After adjusting for relevant confounding variables, higher levels of SS were found to be positively associated with better SRH and SQ. Further, the parallel mediation analysis revealed that depression significantly explained the relationships between SS and both SRH and SQ, accounting for ~ 1% and ~ 33% of the total effects, respectively. These findings provide empirical support for our study hypotheses that SS improves health outcomes, via reducing depression episodes.

Although research on the role of the global SS in the health outcomes of artisanal miners is limited, our findings are consistent with previous studies linking the SS to quality of life and well-being (18,21,56). Seo and Mattos57, in a review of 21 studies, found a positive association between SS and both subjective and objective sleep outcomes. Again, in a cross-sectional observational study involving 180 medical staff in China, Xiao et al.58 found SS enhanced sleep and self-efficacy among COVID-19 frontline worker. Similarly, Bekalu et al.59, using a nationally representative cross-sectional survey of 1,027 American adults, found that strong social ties were positively associated with better mental health and SRH. This study contributes to existing knowledge by highlighting SS’s positive effects on SQ in this high-risk occupational group. To our knowledge, this is one of the few studies to examine the direct and indirect association between SS with SRH and SQ among small-scale miners in SSA. Our results emphasize the need to integrate mental health interventions with SS initiatives to improve the well-being of this vulnerable group.

Although the exact pathway underlying the association of SS with SRH and SQ among artisanal miners remains unclear, our analysis identified depression as a potential factor mediating both the SRH and SQ links, accounting for a negligible proportion (~ 1%) of the SS–SRH association and a substantial proportion (~ 33%) of the SS–SQ association. Prior data exist that strong social ties act as a buffer against psychological distress via fostering a sense of belonging, emotional security, and resilience60, all of which may delay or offset the onset of depression symptoms. Thus, supportive social networks have been identified to provide psychological resources that help individuals manage work-related stressors, financial strain, and hazardous working conditions56, thereby reducing depression episodes. Reduced depression levels can promote sleep quality by restoring physiological equilibrium and lowering stress-related hyperarousal29. Research has shown that, individuals with lower depression levels exhibit healthier sleep behaviors, such as maintaining regular sleep schedules and reducing nighttime rumination, which contribute to overall sleep quality improvements28. Similarly, reduced depression levels may contribute to better subjective health ratings by enhancing psychological resilience, promoting positive health perceptions, and improving overall well-being30. Indeed, lower depressive symptoms are associated with greater motivation to engage in health-promoting behaviors including engaging in moderate physical activity, balanced nutrition, and regular medical checkups58, all of which contribute to better self-rated health62. Importantly, individuals with lower depression levels tend to experience greater optimism and self-efficacy in managing their health, leading to more favorable health assessments63.

Beyond the core mechanism estimated in our study, other pathways may explain the link between SS and SRH/SQ among small-scale miners. Instrumental SS helps reduce loneliness and social isolation, a major risk factor for mental health issues64,65. When individuals feel supported, they tend to engage in meaningful interactions, improving emotional regulation and lowering the risk of depression, thereby enhancing SQ and health ratings66. Again, strong social networks offer emotional, informational, and instrumental support that discourage risky behaviors like polypharmacy (thus, the concurrent use of multiple medications without medical supervision) and self-medication, which involves individuals using drugs (often over-the-counter or leftover prescriptions) to treat health conditions without consulting healthcare professionals67. Supportive individuals are more likely to adopt healthier coping strategies, reducing reliance on sedatives and stimulants (substances commonly misused in high-stress environments like mining)68. This reduction lowers the risk of adverse drug interactions, promotes physiological stability, and improves sleep quality69. Moreover, lower self-medication is associated with better long-term health outcomes, reinforcing more positive self-rated health perceptions70.

Moreover, SS is a significant predictor of lowering feelings of helplessness and hopelessness71. Thus, individuals with strong SS networks often experience a greater sense of control over their lives, as they receive reassurance, encouragement, and practical assistance when facing difficulties72. This sense of control reduces feelings of helplessness and hopelessness, which are well-established precursors to mental and psychological distress, including depression and anxiety symptoms73. Lower levels of helplessness contribute to greater emotional resilience, reducing depressive episodes and improving sleep quality74. Furthermore, individuals with higher perceived self-control are more likely to engage in proactive health behaviors, thereby fostering better self-rated health over time75. Research indicates that those with stronger social support networks exhibit lower levels of learned helplessness, which translates into improved health perceptions and better sleep outcomes76. Lastly, musculoskeletal pain may represent another critical mechanism linking SS to sleep and health outcomes in mining populations. The physically strenuous and injury-prone nature of small-scale mining exposes workers to chronic pain, which can disrupt sleep and deteriorate health perceptions77,78. Social support may mitigate pain burden through both psychological and practical avenues, by facilitating emotional coping, encouraging rest or medical care, and reducing perceived helplessness51,79. Importantly, the observed associations between the study variables may reflect an underlying pathway through which SS influences health outcomes via reductions in depression. However, the cross-sectional nature of the study imposes significant limitations. It restricts our ability to infer causality or determine the temporal sequencing of effects. For example, it is plausible that poor health or sleep problems may exacerbate depressive symptoms, which in turn might influence perceived SS80. Similarly, individuals with higher depression or mental distress may be more likely to report impaired sleep or lower health ratings. Thus, future prospective studies are warranted to rigorously test the directionality of these relationships and explore the potential mediating of these conjectures explained here in greater depth.

This study contributes significantly to global health research by addressing a key gap in understanding the psychosocial determinants of health in vulnerable groups of workers. Although SS has been extensively studied in general populations, few studies have examined its relationship with SRH and SQ in small-scale miners, a high-risk and poorly studied group who are exposed to significant mental and environmental stress. By placing depression at the center, the study provides critical insight into the psychological mechanisms that link social relationships to health. Importantly, our use of a multidimensional measure of SS, capturing subjective, objective and use dimensions, provides a more comprehensive assessment than the traditional one-dimensional approach and thus strengthens the validity of our findings. This is particularly valuable for the design of holistic community-based interventions in the field of disability in low-resource settings.

However, caution is warranted in interpreting the direction of associations due to the cross-sectional design, which limits causal inference. It remains unclear whether social support enhances sleep quality and self-rated health through reduced depression, or if better health facilitates stronger social ties, reciprocal links common in high-stress occupations. Although we adjusted for several covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as chronic pain, trauma history, work hours, or shift work cannot be ruled out. All core variables were self-reported, which may introduce recall or social desirability bias. Future research should use longitudinal and objective measures across more representative mining populations. Nonetheless, the use of validated tools and a theoretically informed framework supports the robustness and policy relevance of our findings.

Implications and recommendations

Our findings have implications for public health and policy intervention in LMICs. We suggest that interventions aimed at improving the well-being of small-scale miners should prioritize strengthening social support networks. Again, workplace policies should encourage peer support systems, facilitate mental health awareness programs, and provide access to psychological services to reduce depression-related health risks. Additionally, community-based initiatives that foster social cohesion and improve miners’ access to both emotional and practical support could have far-reaching benefits for their overall health and quality of life. From a policy perspective, integrating mental health support into occupational health and safety programs within the mining sector is essential. Programs such as subsidized health insurance, peer support interventions, and mobile mental health clinics tailored to miners’ needs could help bridge existing care gaps. Future research using longitudinal designs and experimental methods is warranted to establish causal pathways and explore additional mediating mechanisms linking social support to health outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Conclusion

In this study of small-scale miners, we found that social support is significantly associated with better self-rated health and improved sleep quality among small-scale miners, with depression acting as a key mediator. Strengthening social support systems may therefore play a pivotal role in safeguarding both the psychological and physical health of individuals engaged in this high-risk occupation.
