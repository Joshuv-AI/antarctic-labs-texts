Acquiescing to intuition in individuals prone to delusions: alterations in dual processes and cognitive control
Authors: Megan Liew, Emmett M Larsen, Jason Katz, Kayla R Donaldson, Megan R Serody, Aprajita Mohanty
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnomalous beliefs exist on a continuum ranging from conventional beliefs to delusions. Recent work proposes that delusions may be formed via acquiescence, in which intuitive beliefs arise and persist despite awareness that they conflict with rationality. Cognitive control theory can similarly explain how we fail to detect that delusional beliefs conflict with rationality, and/or marshal cognitive control (analytic) resources to override them. We measured intuitive processes using a decision-making task and analytic processes using Stroop and cognitive reflection (CRT) tasks. Stronger intuition and lower CRT/Stroop scores were associated with acquiescence. Importantly, those less prone to delusions were more likely to override their intuition as their analytic strength increased; however, for more delusion-prone participants, analytic strength predicted an increased likelihood of acquiescence. Findings highlight an interactive role of intuitive and analytic processes in anomalous beliefs, including delusions, such that cognitive resources may be rallied to rationalize intuitions.Subject terms: Psychology, Signs and symptoms

Introduction

Odd beliefs exist on a continuum of severity ranging from conventionally endorsed beliefs such as superstitions on one end of the spectrum and clinical delusions on the other end1. Delusions are strongly-held beliefs that are not amenable to change in the face of conflicting evidence; a quality they may share with other anomalous beliefs. Although they are a common symptom in psychotic disorders such as schizophrenia2, milder delusion-like beliefs are also endorsed by non-clinical populations3. Studies have attempted to elucidate the cognitive mechanisms of delusional beliefs, but our understanding regarding them remains limited4. The dual-process model is a promising but underexplored framework for identifying the cognitive disruptions underlying delusions5,6. This framework derives from the field of behavioral economics and divides cognition into intuitive (“Type 1”) and analytic (“Type 2”) processes7,8. An imbalance in these processes is hypothesized in delusions, such that intuitive processes tend to be overactive and analytic processes underactive5,6. However, empirical methods in support of this view have insufficiently dissociated disruptions specific to intuitive and analytic processes6,9–12.

Furthermore, the dual process model may be insufficient to explain why certain anomalous beliefs are maintained in the face of conflicting evidence13,14. Risen13 argued that classical dual process models do not adequately capture the phenomenon in which intuitively-generated responses are recognized by analytic processes as conflicting with rationality, and the intuitive response is expressed nonetheless. This phenomenon, termed acquiescence, is proposed to explain the persistence of beliefs such as superstitions in the general population13. To understand why acquiescence occurs and how it may relate to delusional beliefs, it is important to decouple detection of conflict (between intuitively-generated vs analytic responses) from overriding the conflict13. A similar idea is echoed by Pennycook et al.15 who describe distinct stages in which conflict (between the agent’s intuition and internal rationality) must first be detected, and subsequently resolved. This is consistent with the conflict hypothesis of cognitive control which proposes that detection of conflict between competing tendencies, one of them prepotent, acts as a signal that recruits control processes required to overcome the conflict16. In such models, the detection of conflict is attributed to different neurocomputational mechanisms than those involved in resolving the conflict17,18. Despite this synergy between augmented dual process and cognitive control approaches, the two have yet to be applied in tandem to the study of delusions.

Delusional beliefs may feasibly arise and persist due to failure to detect their conflict with rationality, or alternatively failure to marshal cognitive control (analytic) resources to override them even if conflict is detected. To our knowledge, no study has carefully distinguished these two stages of conflict processing and examined their relationship with delusional beliefs. Examining acquiescence in relation to delusions may clarify the stage of cognitive disruption in conflict processing that leads to the formation and maintenance of delusions. Analytic processes are thought to depend on basic capacities such as working memory and cognitive control19, which tend to be impaired in psychosis20 and positive schizotypy21, further underscoring the relevance of this framework for elucidating failures to override intuitions in delusion-prone individuals. Furthermore, it has been noted that analytic processes may be marshaled by decision makers during conflict resolution (following initial conflict detection) for either rationalizing or overriding the intuitive response, though the determinants of which occurs are unknown15. Given the growing literature identifying cognitive biases in delusions22, such as the bias against disconfirmatory evidence23 and prior bias24, delusional thinking may be associated with greater propensity for rationalization at this stage, though no study has tested such an effect.

The present work leveraged methods from the behavioral economics and cognitive control literatures to examine dynamics of intuitive and analytic processes during conflict processing in individuals prone to delusions. In particular, we administered a task taken from Walco and Risen14 in which participants were instilled with an initial intuition and then allowed to make a decision to override this intuition in order to greatly increase their chances of monetary gain, thus posing a conflict. By carefully confronting participants with the irrationality of sticking with their intuition, we ensured that conflict could be easily detected, with the effect that decisions could then be attributed fully to variation in the conflict resolution stage. Additionally, we leveraged an aspect of the Stroop task that allowed us to dissociate detection of conflict from its resolution. Instead of relying on conventional indicators of conflict stemming from incompatible stimulus dimensions on the Stroop task, our emphasis was on the conflict adaptation effect. This effect serves as a measure of conflict resolution by amplifying the relevant stimulus dimension and suppressing the irrelevant one25,26. This measure of conflict resolution is pertinent to situations of acquiescence, wherein the intuitive choice contradicts the rational one, yet the intuitive choice cannot be overcome. Lastly, we also measured cognitive reflection, or the ability to use deliberative thinking to correct or overcome an initial intuitive response. While Stroop conflict resolution and cognitive reflection measures may rely on slightly different cognitive mechanisms, both tap analytic aspects of the dual process framework.

We hypothesized that greater delusion-proneness would be associated with stronger intuition as well as reduced analytic capacities (measured as cognitive reflection and Stroop conflict resolution). Furthermore, we hypothesized an interactive relationship between delusion-proneness, intuition, and analytic processes associated with acquiescence behavior. Given the noted evidence regarding cognitive biases in delusions, we hypothesized high delusion-prone individuals would marshal analytic resources to acquiesce to an intuitive response while low delusion-prone individuals would use them to override it.

Methods

We used G*Power 3.127 to estimate a necessary sample size of n = 123 to detect a relationship of r = 0.25 between delusion-proneness and measures of intuitive and analytic processes based on prior work10 at 0.80 power. We overshot in anticipation of running correlations only in the subsample who passed attention and understanding/rationality checks. We recruited 201 participants (mean age = 20.3 (SD = 2.6), 69% female, 48% Asian/Pacific Islander, 29% White/Caucasian, 13% Latinx/Hispanic, 6% Black/African American, 3% Multiple/Other) who were enrolled in undergraduate psychology courses at Stony Brook University. Participants received course credit and a probability of winning $5 (see Supplemental Methods for the wording used in participant recruitment). Informed consent was obtained prior to study participation, study procedures were approved by Stony Brook University’s Institutional Review Board (IRB; FWA# 00000125), and research was performed in accordance with approved regulations/guidelines. Due to the onset of a global pandemic shortly after data collection began, 20 participants completed a physical version of the task while the remainder completed an online version. Additionally, in-person participants did not complete the Stroop or cognitive reflection tasks. All statistical tests with the in-person subsample excluded are presented in Supplemental Results (no changes were observed).

Envelope task

The Envelope Task (Fig. 1A) was recreated according to specifications in Walco and Risen14 and used as a measure of acquiescence.

Participants performed two tasks. (A) The Envelope Task. In the task, participants were (a) presented with three envelopes, one of which contained $5; (b) invited to shuffle the envelopes; (c) asked to choose an envelope to write/type their name on; (d) informed about intuitive and rational decision making strategies and asked to rate the strength of their intuition that the money was in their original envelope as well as their intuition that it was in one of the other two envelopes; (e) asked which envelope combination (one envelope or both of the other two envelopes together) was more likely to win based on rational analysis; (f) asked to make a final decision to keep or exchange their envelope. (B) The Stroop Task consisted of a series of trials, each beginning with (a) a fixation cross (1.5 s). Following the fixation, a color-word (e.g., the word “RED”) appearing in colored font (red, blue, or green). Participants were required to identify the ink color of the word. Hence, stimuli were designated as (b) “congruent” if the text meaning matched the font color (e.g. the word “RED” in red font) or “incongruent” if the two did not match (e.g., the word “RED” in blue font). Incongruent stimuli were either (c) preceded by congruent or (d) preceded by incongruent stimuli.

First, participants were presented with images of three envelopes and informed that one contained $5. They were told that if they guessed which envelope contained the money correctly they would be able to keep the money, which would be paid to them in the form of a gift card sent via email after the study. Second, they chose their envelope and typed/wrote their name on it; they were also permitted to shuffle the envelopes around on screen so that they were in any order. Third, participants were informed they were about to be offered the chance to exchange their envelope in return for both other envelopes. Fourth, they were provided with a description of rational and intuitive decision making and of the probability of winning for both options (their envelope would have a 1/3 chance, whereas the other two envelopes combined would have a 2/3 chance of winning). This was done to ensure activation of analytic processes and encourage understanding of economically rational behavior in the task. Fifth, participants were asked to respond to three questions. As the key measure of their intuitive strength experienced during this task, participants were asked to rate their gut feeling of whether they thought their envelope in fact contained the money (0–100). Participants rated their intuitive strength once for their chosen envelope and once for the two non-chosen envelopes as a whole. Next, to determine if they understood what the rational choice was, participants were asked to respond to the following question: “Based on rational analysis, with which envelope(s) are you most likely to win? Options: (A) your envelope, (B) the other two envelopes together.” Participants who answered this “rationality” question incorrectly, i.e., chose option A, were excluded from the final analysis sample (see “Statistical analyses” below). Sixth, participants made the choice of keeping their original envelope or taking the other two envelopes. Seventh, participants were offered the opportunity to provide commentary to an open-ended question about why they made their choice, and a summary of these responses is presented in Supplemental Methods. At the end of the task, it was revealed whether their choice was correct (whether they won the $5).

Stroop task

Participants completed a Stroop Task17,28, administered online through the Pavlovia software platform (PsychoJS version 2020.2), containing 3 blocks of 88 trials (Fig. 1B). The first and last four trials of each block were always congruent. The remaining 80 trials comprised 56 congruent and 24 incongruent words in randomized order.

Stroop data were excluded (n = 26) if participants (a) failed to respond on more than 20% trials, or (b) scored below 50% accuracy across all trials. The primary measure of interest from the Stroop task was the conflict adaptation effect. Participants tend to be slower on incongruent (vs congruent) words for which the prepotent word reading dimension conflicts with the task-relevant color naming dimension28,29. However, the detrimental effect of the incongruent dimension is reduced following incongruent versus congruent trials17,30. This “conflict adaptation” is attributed to the transient anticipatory increase in control processes resulting in improved conflict resolution in incongruent trials following incongruent trials (vs following congruent trials)16. A comparison of RT between trial types yields a measure of conflict resolution ability.

Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT)

The CRT31,32 is a 7-item test used to assess the extent to which one can activate their reflective and deliberative thinking to correct an initial intuitive and spontaneous response. One example question is: “A bat and a ball cost $1.10 in total. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?” It has been used in past studies as an explicit measure of the engagement of Type 2 analytical processes10,33. Number of items correctly answered out of 7 comprised the CRT metric.

Measure of delusion-proneness

Delusion-proneness was measured using the 21-item Peters Delusions Inventory (PDI; 34). The PDI is a widely used measure of delusion-proneness that has demonstrated good psychometric properties34, including reliability (ɑ = 0.82, test–retest r = 0.78), construct validity (relation to similar measures (rs = 0.37–0.65) and criterion validity (individuals prone to delusions have a stronger internal consistency compared to the control group (ɑ = 0.90)). A sample item from PDI is: “Do you ever feel as if you could read other people’s minds?” The number of PDI items responded to with “Yes” (ranging from 0 to 21) was the primary metric used for study analyses.

Procedure

The order of procedures was: Stroop task, questionnaires (demographics, PDI, and CRT), envelope task. We chose to administer the study components in this order because a participant’s motivation to complete study questionnaires may be lower after they have won money. Questionnaires were administered as part of a longer battery (presented in randomized order) that took approximately 30 min to complete. Other questionnaires (not relevant for this study) assessed traits and psychopathology symptoms aside from delusion proneness, including perceived stress, paranormal beliefs, thinking style, OCD, locus of control, and other domains of schizotypy.

Attention checks

In order to identify careless or hasty responders, we embedded two attention check questions into the questionnaire battery. The first question asked, “I have to breathe to stay alive. Select choice one if you are attending to this questionnaire.” The second asked, “Did you expend effort and attention sufficient to warrant using your responses for this research study? Type ‘X’ and move on.”35. Forty-four participants were excluded in the analysis sample who failed either attention check (i.e. if they indicated anything other than ‘1’ and ‘X’ respectively).

Statistical analyses

Analyses and visualizations were carried out in the R programming environment (R version: 4.2.2, R studio: 2023.03.0, stats package version: 4.22). We first evaluated what proportion of the sample correctly identified that it was rational to exchange their envelope (Supplemental Table S4 displays breakdown of participants’ responses to this question as well as other envelope task responses). We reasoned that if participants did not demonstrate understanding that “rationally speaking” it is more likely for the money to be in two envelopes than for it to be in one, then there is no cognitive conflict between rational and intuitive processes to be resolved. Thus, we limited subsequent analyses to the participants who correctly responded to this “rationality” question in order to specifically investigate the processes involved in resolving the conflict. In Supplemental Table S1, we describe differences between those who answered the rational question correctly/incorrectly. Next, we examined the relation of measures of intuitive strength and analytic processes (CRT and Stroop conflict adaptation scores) to acquiescence behavior (envelope choice) using logistic regression. To address our primary hypotheses, we then examined the relation of delusion-proneness (PDI score) to measures of intuitive and analytic processes using simple and multiple linear regressions. Lastly, we entered PDI into the logistic regression model with acquiescence as the outcome in order to examine its interaction with intuitive and analytic processes to influence envelope choice. Uncorrected p-values are reported for all tests; FDR-correction36 indicated that all p-values below 0.037 survived (resulting in one test becoming non-significant). In Supplemental Results, we repeat main statistical tests (a) without excluding those who answered the rational question incorrectly, and (b) using a measure of hallucination-proneness rather than delusion-proneness to test specificity.

Lastly, we carried out several additional checks in order to perform more stringent tests of the applicability of our results to clinical populations. To do this, we constructed three alternate PDI scales using studies that collected PDI data from both healthy and psychotic participants and reported the relative endorsement likelihood for each item37–39. These alternate PDI scales were created by summing specific PDI items that demonstrated the greatest ability to distinguish patients from controls across studies. We detail the specific criteria used for item selection in supplemental Table S2. Additionally, because distress is a symptom distinguishing clinical psychosis from its attenuated forms including in the PDI specifically (e.g.40–42, we also individually examined the PDI distress subscale. All analyses were repeated using these alternate PDI scales to examine robustness and clinical relevance.

Results

Of those who passed attention checks, 71% (N = 111) understood that it would be more rational to exchange their envelope (subsequent analyses were restricted to this group). Bivariate relationships of measures of intuitive and analytic processes with acquiescence behavior (Fig. 2) revealed that intuition strength despite being informed of rational choice was a significant predictor of increased acquiescence (p < 0.001, OR = 2.29, 95% CI [1.49, 3.82]). Additionally, decreased conflict adaptation on the Stroop task (i.e., lower recruitment of cognitive control) predicted increased acquiescence (p = 0.041, OR = 0.63, 95% CI [0.38, 0.97]), though this effect did not survive FDR correction. This effect did not reach significance for the CRT (p = 0.35, OR = 0.82).

Bivariate relationships between (A) Acquiescence behavior (choice whether or not to keep original envelope) and intuitive strength, CRT and Stroop conflict adaptation (z-scored for ease of viewing). (B–D) The same bivariate relationships with data superimposed.

Those with a higher PDI score reported stronger intuition about their original envelope containing the $5 (Fig. 3; β = 0.31, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.13, 0.49]). PDI also predicted weaker intuition about the money being in the other 2 envelopes; β = − 0.22, p = 0.021, 95% CI [− 0.40, − 0.03]). As hypothesized, PDI was associated negatively with analytic strength as measured by CRT (β = − 27, p = 0.009, 95% CI [− 0.46, − 0.07]); while PDI did not relate linearly to conflict adaptation on the Stroop task (p = 0.82), these two variables demonstrated a quadratic relationship (R2 = 0.10, p = 0.002). Lastly, all intuitive and analytic strength variables bore unique relationships with PDI when controlling for variance shared between them using a multiple linear regression with PDI as outcome (intuition strength: β = 0.34, p < 0.001; CRT: β = − 0.27, p = 0.004; squared Stroop conflict adaptation: β = − 1.60, p < 0.001; R2adjusted = 0.27, p < 0.001).

Relationships of PDI with measures of (A) intuitive and (B) analytic strength, with values z-scored for simultaneous presentation.

Finally, we observed that PDI was not a significant predictor of increased probability of acquiescence (p = 0.087, OR = 1.40). However, we found a significant 3-way interaction between PDI, intuitive, and analytic processes, which held for both measures of analytic processes (CRT: interaction = 1.38, p = 0.002, 95% CI [0.57, 2.38]; Stroop conflict adaptation: βinteraction = 1.04, p = 0.021, 95% CI [0.22, 2.04]). To visualize this effect (Fig. 4), we split the sample based on strong (at or above 50%) vs weak (below 50%) intuition for the original envelope as well as high vs low PDI (based on a median split; for an alternative method see Fig. S1). For low-PDI participants who experienced a strong intuition for their original envelope (dashed lines) increased analytic strength was associated with a decreased probability of acquiescence (ORStroop = 0.40; ORCRT = 0.43); for those with a weak intuition for their original envelope (solid lines), no effect of analytic strength was evident (ORStroop = 0.85; ORCRT = 1.07). Figure 4C, D depicts the same estimated curves for high-PDI participants. Those in this group who experienced a strong intuition for their original envelope (dashed lines) were more likely to acquiesce as their analytic strength increased (ORStroop = 1.40; ORCRT = 1.72). Conversely, for those high in PDI with weak intuition for their original envelope (solid lines), increased analytic strength was associated with decreased likelihood of acquiescence (ORStroop = 0.49; ORCRT = 0.06). Our modified PDI scores created to test clinical relevance of the findings comprised three new scales, consisting of 937–39, 837,39, and 9 items39 respectively. See supplemental Table S2 for details of item selection for these three subscales and supplemental Table S3 for descriptive characteristics of the alternate PDI scales. When repeating our primary analyses using these four scales (three modified PDI scales and the standard PDI distress scale), we observed similar results to those using the standard PDI score. All four variables demonstrated significant positive relationships with intuitive strength (ps < 0.05), negative relationships with CRT (ps < 0.05) and quadratic relationships with Stroop conflict adaptation (ps < 0.05). In addition, the 3-way interaction between PDI, intuitive strength, and CRT remained significant in all cases (ps < 0.01). In the interaction analysis using the Stroop, qualitative patterns remained similar with the new variables, but the interaction did not reach significance (ps = 0.11–0.17). These interaction relationships are depicted in supplemental Figures S3–S6.

Discussion

Dual process models regarding the interplay of intuitive and rational processes present a powerful framework for examining cognitive mechanisms of delusional thinking. However, previous models have not acknowledged the importance of an augmented framework that accommodates acquiescence, in which a decision-maker may recognize conflict between intuitive and rational responses but comply with the intuitive impulse nonetheless. Acquiescence may inform a key aspect of delusions because they are maintained even in the face of conflicting evidence. The cognitive control literature that separates conflict monitoring and resolution processes aligns well with the augmented dual process framework. Yet, the two have not been empirically examined in concert to shed light on mechanisms of delusional beliefs. The present study sought to measure dual process dynamics, acquiescence, and cognitive control to examine how these processes relate to delusion-proneness.

Past studies have assumed intuitive processes are at play when decision makers choose the intuitive over the rational choice. Here, we directly measured participants’ conviction regarding their intuitive choice, despite being informed of the rational choice, and showed that it was associated with increased acquiescence. Next, we measured aspects of rational/analytic processes including deliberative, reflective thinking and cognitive control. We found that decreased Stroop conflict resolution was associated with greater acquiescence to intuition. This relationship may indicate that even when conflict between intuitive and rational processes is clear, some participants are unable to marshal conflict resolution resources to inhibit the intuition resulting in acquiescence. Future research examining the role of lateral prefrontal cortices (PFC) associated with the resolution of conflict17,18,43 can further clarify how cognitive control contributes to acquiescence.

Next, we showed that stronger intuition and decreased cognitive reflectiveness or CRT scores were associated with increased delusion-proneness. While past studies have identified comparable relationships10,11, few have effectively dissociated intuitive and rational/analytic thinking to demonstrate independent relationships with delusion-proneness. In contrast to the linear relationship with CRT, Stroop conflict resolution bore a quadratic relationship with delusion-proneness. Interestingly, this relationship is reminiscent of the quadratic relationship between working memory load and lateral PFC activity in schizophrenia compared to the more linear relationship in controls44. Whether the present conflict resolution pattern reflects an inverted-U pattern of lateral PFC recruitment remains to be examined. Moreover, both our measures of analytic reasoning bore independent relationships to delusion-proneness. This result may map onto the distinction made by Stanovich45 between “algorithmic” and “reflective” processes, which he argued may form a bifurcation of Type 2 processes, suggesting that delusions involve alterations to both. Indeed, it is possible that our two measures of analytic processing may map roughly onto this distinction as well: while the CRT may be primarily a measure of conscious, deliberative, reflective capacity, Stroop adaptation likely relies on cognitive functions at lower levels of information processing that are involved in quick inhibition of automatic responses.

Importantly, this study allowed an examination of how dual process dynamics and delusion-proneness influence acquiescence behavior. High delusion-prone individuals with strong intuition were more likely to acquiesce to their intuition with increasing cognitive control. Conversely, low delusion-prone individuals were more likely to override such strong intuitions with increasing cognitive control, in line with the overall expected effects of the analytic system. The same pattern was observed for cognitive reflection. One interpretation of this effect is that individuals more prone to delusions tend to use rational resources to justify their intuitive beliefs. Pennycook et al.15 discussed the possibility that analytic engagement may be deployed for either post-hoc rationalization of an intuitively-generated response or for overriding it, and here we show that in cases of high delusion-proneness it is the former but the latter in low delusion-proneness. Interestingly, high delusion-prone individuals who did not experience a strong conflicting intuition displayed a greater likelihood of rational choice with increasing analytic strength. This again may point to analytic thinking being used to further strengthen their initial thought. Of note, a similar rationalization effect has been hypothesized to occur in those prone to conspiratorial beliefs (e.g.46,47)—a form of odd beliefs which are indeed related to delusions48.

Limitations

Online data collection during the pandemic has provided some advantages. It has increased external validity by making the study more accessible to those unable to travel to the lab either due to COVID-related safety concerns or accessibility needs. However, by maximizing external validity by primarily conducting our study online, we have reduced internal validity in that we have less experimental control. While we have attempted to compensate for the reduction in experimental control by adding attention checks, it is difficult to determine if participants are fully attending to the study. As such, future work should examine the relationship between acquiescence and delusion proneness in a controlled laboratory setting. Additionally, because acquiescence and intuition were assessed once, it is unclear how consistent the trait/behavior is.

An additional limitation of our study relates to sample size and the exclusion of some participants. By discarding participants who have either failed the attention checks or failed to understand what the rational choice was, this may have contributed to several non-significant findings due to the findings being underpowered. Another potential concern one may have is whether the relationships present in our main (“rational”) sample were also present in the irrational sample. In supplemental materials, we conducted several statistical tests to explicitly address the potential differences between subgroups; we observed that participants who incorrectly responded to the rationality question (i.e. whether it is rationally better to have one envelope or two) differed in several respects: they had higher intuitive strength for and greater likelihood of keeping their envelope, and their intuitive strength did not relate to delusion-proneness as in the main sample. We take this to reflect that the relationship between delusion-proneness and intuition is specific to participants who were able to correctly understood what was rational in the task.

A final limitation of this study is our sample homogeneity due to our usage of an undergraduate participant pool. While our sample lies within a critical age range in which earlier onset of clinically relevant delusions typically manifest50,51, many others develop delusional beliefs well into their late twenties and thirties. For this reason, we cannot extrapolate our findings to populations not matching our demographic characteristics. Additionally, there remains some controversy in the field of psychosis research as to the question of continuity of mechanisms between non-clinical populations and those with clinical psychosis49. Future work should attempt replication of current findings in patient samples as well.

Conclusions

In sum, our findings highlight interactions between intuitive/analytic and cognitive control processes as a potential mechanism contributing to delusion-like beliefs in nonclinical populations. While the vast majority of our findings were replicated when using stringent measures more likely to reflect processes relevant to clinical psychotic psychopathology, future work could attempt to reproduce these findings in a clinical sample to ascertain that the interplay of these processes are also involved in clinical delusions.

Notably, our conservative estimate indicated that dual-process measures accounted for an impressive 27% of delusion-proneness—none of which was attributable to measurement variance. Overall, these findings may provide insight into the large literature observing cognitive biases in delusions, such as diminished ability to incorporate contradictory evidence23,52 and computational evidence of increased reliance on prior beliefs when integrating new information24,53,54. Additionally, while we examined acquiescence and Type 2 analytic processes as it pertains to delusion-proneness, these findings may have implications for understanding the rigidity and cognitive biases that may appear in anxiety and depression and why belief in misinformation, fake news, and conspiracies persist.
