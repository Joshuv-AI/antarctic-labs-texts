Prenatal Remote Blood Pressure Monitoring and Detection of Chronic Hypertension
Authors: Loral Patchen, Madalyn C. Myers, Serenity G. Budd, Diyang Lyu, Stephen J. Fernandez, Tamika C. Auguste
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Universal prenatal remote blood pressure monitoring initiated at the first visit is associated with 68.0% higher odds of chronic hypertension diagnosis before 20 weeks of gestation.

Chronic hypertension in pregnancy is defined as hypertension diagnosed or present before 20 weeks of gestation.1 It is associated with multiple complications, including preeclampsia with severe features, acute renal failure, pulmonary edema, iatrogenic preterm birth, and cesarean delivery.2,3 Its prevalence has nearly doubled between 2008 and 2021 from 1.8% to 3.7%,3 yet diagnosis remains challenging during the critical first 20 weeks, when early detection enables prevention.

In 2017, the American College of Cardiology and the American Heart Association lowered the thresholds for diagnosing hypertension outside pregnancy, redefining stage 1 as 130–139/80–89 mm Hg (previously 140–159/90–109) and stage 2 as ≥140/90 mm Hg (previously ≥160/110).4 Current American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidance advises lifestyle modification as initial management for stage 1 hypertension with low 10-year cardiovascular risk, reserving medication for patients at higher risk or those not at target.5 A 2020 cohort study found that stage 1 hypertension increased preeclampsia risk, but only stage 2 hypertension raised severe maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality.6 The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) notes that it remains unclear how to manage pregnant patients with stage 1 blood pressures (BPs) in pregnancy and no prior diagnosis. Despite these changes, a 2024 review found the prevalence and treatment of chronic hypertension in pregnancy unchanged, with about 60% of patients with chronic hypertension treated with medication.3

A key diagnostic challenge is misclassification arising from the physiologic BP nadir of the first trimester. Systemic vascular resistance falls in early pregnancy, transiently normalizing readings even in patients with underlying chronic hypertension. Those who enter prenatal care without a prior diagnosis and present during this trough may be presumed normotensive; if BP later rises, they may be misdiagnosed with gestational hypertension or preeclampsia rather than chronic hypertension,7 denying them the distinct management pathway an accurate chronic hypertension diagnosis affords. Earlier, more frequent monitoring beginning at the first prenatal visit captures BP across multiple time points and is better positioned than office-based screening to surface a diagnosis before the 20-week window closes.

Timely diagnosis within the first 20 weeks opens a narrow but critical window for risk-reducing interventions not indicated in patients with normotension. Low-dose aspirin is recommended between 12 and 28 weeks to reduce preeclampsia risk, with greatest benefit before 16 weeks.8,9 Treating BP to below 140/90 mm Hg reduces preeclampsia with severe features, placental abruption, medically indicated preterm birth, and neonatal death without increasing small-for-gestational age birth.10 Chronic hypertension also indicates third-trimester growth ultrasound, antenatal testing,7 earlier delivery (37 0/7–39 6/7 weeks versus 39 0/7–41 6/7 weeks for patients with normotension),7 and counseling on long-term cardiovascular risk.

Remote BP monitoring during prenatal care may help diagnose previously unrecognized chronic hypertension. Out-of-office measurements predict cardiovascular outcomes better than office readings alone,4,11 and clinicians and advocacy groups have proposed that telecommunication and remote monitoring may improve access and reduce disparities in hypertensive disorders of pregnancy.12,13 Prior research established remote BP monitoring safety and acceptability14–16 but focused on managing known hypertensive disorders, not diagnosis.17,18 ACOG endorses home monitoring but emphasizes the postpartum period,18 leaving the prenatal diagnostic potential of remote BP monitoring largely unevaluated. Filling this gap is important given the rising prevalence of chronic hypertension and the potential for earlier diagnosis to improve outcomes.

This retrospective study evaluated a real-world implementation of a universal prenatal remote BP monitoring digital platform integrated into routine care from the first prenatal visit at an urban tertiary center within a distributed care network. We hypothesized that universal remote BP monitoring beginning at the first prenatal visit would be associated with higher rates of chronic hypertension diagnosis before 20 weeks compared with propensity-matched usual-care controls.

METHODS

This retrospective cohort study evaluated whether universal enrollment in prenatal remote BP monitoring was associated with higher rates of new chronic hypertension diagnosis before 20 weeks of gestation. Study participants delivered between September 14, 2021, and May 31, 2023, and initiated prenatal care before 20 weeks of gestation. Pregnant people with an established diagnosis of chronic hypertension before their first prenatal care visit were excluded. This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guideline for observational cohort studies (Appendix 1, available online at https://links.lww.com/AOG/F16).

Participants enrolled in the remote BP monitoring intervention received an internet-enabled BP cuff allowing values to flow directly into the electronic health record (EHR). At the first prenatal visit, a nurse fit the cuff, taught BP technique, oriented patients to the platform, and advised on monitoring frequency. At onboarding, patients were instructed to rest for 5 minutes before measuring, sit in a chair with both feet flat on the floor and back straight, position the arm at heart level, and remain still and quiet during the measurement (Appendix 2, https://links.lww.com/AOG/F16). Measurement frequency was set by the health care professional rather than a fixed schedule, and the time of measurement was not standardized. Blood pressure values entered by patients were visible to the clinical team. Patients did not need to manually enter BP values, but they had the option to do so if they used their own cuff instead. When BP was elevated, patients were prompted to answer a series of questions regarding symptoms and were given instructions on when and how to repeat the BP. Elevated readings were immediately triaged to a call center, which followed a protocol to alert the appropriate medical team—nurse triage lines during business hours and the health care professional directly on evenings and weekends—to determine a plan of care. The digital platform also hosted a resource bank with educational materials from ACOG, the March of Dimes, and the practice site. Weekly text alerts directed users to content aligned with gestational age, and the resource bank was accessible at any time.

We used observational EHR data to compare outcomes between the remote BP monitoring intervention and usual care groups within a single integrated distributed care health system. Participants in the intervention group received care at one location within this system offering the digital platform with remote BP monitoring, and the usual care group comprised participants who received care at alternative locations that did not offer remote BP monitoring. A specific order for the intervention identified participants in the intervention group. Participants in the usual care group received care at locations where the protocol was not active and no order existed. Given the retrospective data, an appropriate waiver of informed consent and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act authorization was requested and granted. The study received approval from the MedStar Health Research Institute IRB (study No. 6487).

The primary outcome variable was a new diagnosis of chronic hypertension among pregnant participants before 20 weeks of gestation. Chronic hypertension was ascertained from the clinical diagnosis recorded in the EHR (documented diagnosis and associated diagnostic codes) before 20 weeks of gestation rather than by applying a specific BP threshold to measured values. The independent variable was whether the patient was enrolled in the intervention (digital platform with remote BP monitoring) or received usual care. Age at delivery in years, race and ethnicity, insurance type, prior type 1 diabetes diagnosis, prior type 2 diabetes diagnosis, prior myocardial infarction diagnosis (MI), tobacco use during pregnancy, body mass index (BMI), and week of first prenatal care visit were covariates. Covariates were selected a priori on the basis of clinical relevance as established risk factors for chronic hypertension and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, together with gestational week of the first prenatal visit as a marker of care timing and ascertainment opportunity. For patients enrolled in the remote BP monitoring intervention, the number of BP readings recorded through the digital platform before 20 weeks was additionally captured as a measure of engagement. Age, BMI, parity, and week of first prenatal care visit were continuous variables, and race and ethnicity and insurance type were categorical variables. Race and ethnicity were collected by patient self-report at the time of registration within the EHR and were treated as socially defined categories. Race was examined as a marker of exposure to structural racism and social determinants of health that drive documented disparities in chronic hypertension, allowing assessment of whether remote BP monitoring supported equitable detection.1,11 Because data were drawn from multiple sites within a distributed care network, variability in how race and ethnicity categories were recorded and queried across sites was acknowledged as a potential limitation of these data. Race and ethnicity had four categories: White, Black or African American, Hispanic or Latino, and other or unknown. All categories were adopted directly from the EHR dataset. Insurance had four categories: commercial, Medicaid, Medicare, and self-pay or other. Type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, MI, and tobacco use were indicator variables.

Electronic health records and data fields were extracted from the Oracle Health EHR (formerly Cerner Millennium database) and MedStar Health's Analytics Platform, a MedStar Health–based electronic data warehouse. Indicators created for analysis were defined by the primary investigators and programmed by clinical informatics analysts using Python. Data accuracy was validated through a structured review process with discrepancies reviewed and adjudicated by the primary investigators.

Continuous variables were summarized with mean±SD or median and interquartile range, and categorical variables were summarized with frequency and percentage, stratified by remote BP monitoring intervention and usual care groups and overall. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) between groups before and after matching were reported. Two:one optimal pair propensity score matching without replacement was used to balance the covariate distributions between the usual care group and remote BP monitoring intervention group. The propensity score was estimated with a logistic regression generalized additive model with the covariates as predictors and smooth terms for all continuous variables. Missing categorical values were coded as “other or unknown”; patients with missing continuous covariates were excluded under a complete case design. Covariate balance was assessed with SMDs and variance ratios. Covariates were considered balanced if the absolute SMDs were <0.1 and the variance ratios were 0.5–2. A logistic regression model was fit with diagnosis of chronic hypertension within 20 weeks as the outcome and remote BP monitoring enrollment as the independent variable. Cluster-robust SEs were used to account for within-pair dependence. Odds ratios (ORs), 95% CIs, P values, and average predicted probabilities were reported. A significance level of 0.05 was used for all analyses, which were performed in R 4.5.1.

RESULTS

There were 7,931 patients who delivered between September 14, 2021, and May 31, 2023. Of those, 4,582 patients were excluded because they did not initiate prenatal care before 20 weeks of gestation, and 55 patients were excluded because they already had a diagnosis of chronic hypertension before their first prenatal care visit, leaving 3,294 patients in the cohort (Appendix 3, https://links.lww.com/AOG/F16). Table 1 displays prematching patient characteristics by group. There were 508 patients enrolled in the digital platform with remote BP monitoring and 2,786 in the usual care group. Before matching, the remote BP monitoring intervention group initiated prenatal care slightly earlier (median 9.9 weeks versus 10.9 weeks, respectively). The median gestational week of chronic hypertension diagnosis was similar between groups (12.9 weeks in both). Among remote BP monitoring–enrolled patients, the median number of BP readings recorded through the digital platform before 20 weeks was four (interquartile range 1–8). Median parity was one in both groups. Body mass index was the only continuous propensity-model covariate with missing values; the 31 patients (0.9%) missing BMI could not be assigned a propensity score and were excluded under a complete-case approach. The remaining covariates were complete. Parity is presented descriptively in Tables 1 and 2 but was not included as a matching covariate, so missing parity did not affect propensity score estimation or the outcome analysis.

After matching, the covariates were balanced. All covariates had absolute SMDs less than 0.05 and variance ratios less than 1.1. There were 1,014 patients receiving usual care matched with 507 patients receiving the remote BP monitoring intervention. Table 2 displays postmatching patient characteristics. After matching, groups were closely balanced across all covariates, including age, race and ethnicity, insurance type, comorbidities, BMI, tobacco use, and timing of first prenatal visit (median 9.9 weeks in both groups).

Patients in the remote BP monitoring intervention group had significantly higher odds of being diagnosed with chronic hypertension compared with those in the usual care group (OR 1.68, 95% CI, 1.10–2.57, P=.017). Among patients diagnosed with chronic hypertension, the median gestational week of diagnosis was 11.3 weeks (interquartile range 9.1–15.9) in the usual care group and 12.9 weeks (interquartile range 11.0–17.9) in the remote BP monitoring group. DISCUSSION

Patients enrolled in the digital platform with remote BP monitoring at the first prenatal visit had significantly more diagnoses of chronic hypertension in the first 20 weeks of pregnancy compared with propensity score–matched patients receiving usual care. Universal prenatal remote BP monitoring enrollment was associated with 68.0% higher odds of chronic hypertension diagnosis. This corresponds to an increase in the predicted probability of chronic hypertension diagnosis from 5.2% to 8.5%, which is an additional three to four diagnoses per 100 pregnant patients.

Early identification of previously undiagnosed chronic hypertension enables health care professionals to initiate best-practice management, including low-dose aspirin, antihypertensive therapy, third-trimester growth ultrasound and antenatal testing, earlier delivery, and postpartum cardiovascular follow-up; this management is not routinely indicated for patients with normotension without additional risk factors.7,9,19 By capturing BP across multiple time points, remote BP monitoring addresses a key limitation of office screening: The first-trimester decline in BP can mask underlying hypertension in patients who appear normotensive during routine visits, and home monitoring can identify these transiently normotensive patients. Conversely, it may refine diagnosis in the opposite direction by identifying white coat hypertension in patients who are normotensive outside the office, helping avoid overdiagnosis. Diagnosis before 20 weeks is critical; missing this window forecloses the distinct management pathway that chronic hypertension diagnosis affords.

Our findings suggest early remote BP monitoring may facilitate earlier risk stratification and management of pregnant people with chronic hypertension, whose care differs substantially from care given to normotensive patients during the perinatal period.1,3,4 Prior research shows mixed effects of remote BP monitoring on clinical outcomes, with substantial variability in program structure. The Blood Pressure Monitoring in High Risk Pregnancy to Improve the Detection and Monitoring of Hypertension (BUMP) 1 randomized clinical trial tested whether remote BP monitoring would expedite hypertensive disorders of pregnancy detection in individuals at risk but found no significant improvement despite higher patient satisfaction,20 and BUMP 2 found no difference in systolic BP between remote BP monitoring and usual care in patients with known hypertensive disorders.17 However, these trials examined remote BP monitoring as a monitoring tool in patients with known or suspected hypertension, not as a diagnostic tool for previously undiagnosed chronic hypertension at care initiation, a distinction in program structure, population, timing, and automated versus patient-initiated triage that may explain the divergent findings.

Another randomized clinical trial demonstrated tighter BP control with remote BP monitoring sustained 6 months postpartum using automated data transmission and immediate clinical response.21 Although remote BP monitoring criticisms include observer error, bias, improper cuff sizing, and patient burden,14,16 prior research shows high acceptability and feasibility.14–16 In our program, direct record integration, automated triage of elevated values, proper cuff fitting, and technique instruction were designed to mitigate these concerns and may have supported engagement.

The universal nature of enrollment warrants emphasis. All patients at the intervention site received a fitted cuff, hands-on instruction, and access to an immediately triaged monitoring system from their first prenatal visit. Unlike opt-in models, this design promotes broad reach and reduces the likelihood that benefits accrue only to patients who self-select into participation. How remote BP monitoring programs are structured and offered, not simply whether they are available, may meaningfully influence who benefits.

This study benefited from a large, diverse multisite sample and reflects successful integration of remote BP monitoring at an urban tertiary center, suggesting an intervention that can scale in real-world care. A key strength is propensity score matching, which balanced confounders at the design stage rather than through outcome-model adjustment alone, yielding covariate distributions approximating a randomized trial within the matched sample. Balance was excellent, with all absolute SMDs below 0.05 and variance ratios below 1.1, supporting the validity of the exposure estimate across age, race and ethnicity, insurance type, prior diabetes, prior MI, tobacco use, BMI, and timing of first prenatal visit.

Although these strengths are notable, we recognize limitations. As a retrospective design, unmeasured confounding and selection bias are possible, particularly because outcome ascertainment relied on documentation and diagnostic coding for chronic hypertension by health care professionals. The single health system sample may limit generalizability. In addition, technology adoption and digital health literacy vary across settings, health care professionals, and patients, with rural and urban settings presenting different implementation facilitators and barriers.13 Furthermore, although prenatal visits before 20 weeks were similar between matched groups, remote BP monitoring patients contributed additional home measurements and therefore had more opportunities for an elevated reading to be detected. This surveillance differential is intrinsic to the intervention because the added measurement opportunity is a hypothesized mechanism of improved detection.

Broader adoption of remote BP monitoring at the first prenatal visit may improve chronic hypertension detection and will benefit from the hands-on onboarding used here, specifically individual cuff sizing, technique education, and platform orientation, to build credibility and trust for patients and health care professionals.22 These steps align with evolving ACOG and U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommendations on remote BP monitoring in prenatal care.18,23

Whether earlier chronic hypertension diagnosis through prenatal remote BP monitoring improves downstream outcomes remains an important question for future study. Because chronic hypertension was ascertained from recorded diagnoses rather than measured values, this study could not compare detection under the traditional (140/90 mm Hg or higher) versus 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association stage 1 (130/80 mm Hg or higher) thresholds. Quantifying diagnostic yield under each definition is a valuable future direction. Because the analysis evaluated diagnosis within the first 20 weeks, obstetric and health care use outcomes (preeclampsia, antepartum hospitalization, medically indicated preterm birth, antihypertensive use, aspirin use) were not collected. Nonetheless, the meaningful improvement in chronic hypertension detection provides immediate clinical value by identifying patients who will benefit from evidence-based interventions.
