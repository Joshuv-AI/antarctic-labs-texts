Agri-solar water pumping design, energy, and environmental analysis: A comprehensive study in tropical humid climate
Authors: Rittick Maity, K Sudhakar, Amir Abdul Razak
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgrivoltaics is an innovative approach where solar energy, water, land and biodiversity are integrated into the same area to maximize resource utilization for sustainable development, also known as Agrivoltaism. The combination of solar water pumping and agri-solar has led to the development of a new generation of irrigation systems that are highly sustainable and efficient. Agri-solar water pumping can irrigate crops, feed livestock, clean solar modules, cool the PV system, generate energy, store water, and provide community drinking water. This paper addresses the basic design and capacity requirements of solar water pumping systems for irrigating a 0.5-ha Agrivoltaics system in Kuala Lumpur. The SISIFO tool has been used to simulate the Agri-solar water pumping performance for tropical humid climatic conditions. The various parameters like site details, climate data, type of PV modules, DC pump -motor and converters are used as input data to evaluate the energy yield parameters (output energy in DC and AC form), hydraulic parameters (volume of water pumped), loss parameters (capture and system losses) and efficiency parameters (performance of the system). Lettuce was chosen as it is fast growing with good yield per hectare. Based on the result, a detailed analysis of the agrivoltaic lettuce plant is performed. A detailed analysis of the solar resource assessment, system design, Key performance indicators, loss analysis and environmental analysis of the Agri-solar water pumping has been carried out. Considering their specific climatic conditions, this can significantly assist policymakers in selecting the optimal solar pumping station for agrivoltaic plants. The importance of considering various factors when choosing a solar pumping station for agrivoltaics is also highlighted.Keywords: Agriphotovolatics., Lettuce farming, SISIFO tool, Key performance indicators, Energy analysis, Loss analysis

Introduction

Water is an indispensable asset in agriculture, profoundly influencing crop productivity and quality. According to UN projections, by 2025, over 25 % of the world's population will reside in nations where access to water will consistently be an issue [1]. Conventional irrigation systems heavily lean on diesel generators or grid electricity in numerous areas. The water-food-energy nexus concept drives the solar PV pumping in agricultural land [2]. However, it allows the sustainable management of water resources to stabilize the groundwater table. Crops are a form of recharging the groundwater level, soil fertility and reclamation of barren land. However, with new technologies, sustainable and renewable energy sources, such as solar and wind power, are becoming more accessible and affordable. By transitioning to these eco-friendly alternatives, farmers can reduce their environmental impact while saving money in the long term.

Furthermore, the government offers solar PV irrigation subsidies with a 4-6-year payback time [3]. Solar water irrigation refers to irrigating agricultural fields using solar energy as the power source. It involves using solar panels or photovoltaic (PV) systems to capture sunlight and convert it into electrical energy. This energy is then utilized to operate water pumps or other irrigation equipment, facilitating water movement from a source, such as a well or a reservoir, to the fields where crops are cultivated. Several agricultural uses of solar energy are explored to advance solar-based technology, including solar crop dryers for food safety, photovoltaic hybrid (PV/T),solar water pumping systems, refrigeration, distillation and solar water desalination systems [[4], [5], [6]]These systems often incorporate intelligent technologies and deploying sensors for precision water and crop management. With 210 GW of its 269 GW solar potential located on the ground, Malaysia offers new opportunities for agrivoltaic technology [7].

Innovation and Motivation of the study

The necessity of this study lies in addressing key challenges within the agrivoltaic framework, particularly those related to the integration of water management with energy generation. Agrivoltaics, by its nature, combines energy production and agriculture, leading to competing demands for resources like water and electricity. However, the innovation of using a dedicated solar water pumping unit within an agrivoltaic system has not been adequately explored, particularly in terms of how it can resolve potential conflicts in resource allocation and enhance overall system efficiency. Agrivoltaic systems generate electricity using solar panels while allowing agricultural activities to continue underneath or around them. However, this dual-purpose approach can create competition for the electricity generated, especially when power is needed for essential farming activities such as irrigation. The use of a dedicated solar water pumping unit, which operates independently from the main agrivoltaic system, addresses this issue by ensuring that water supply for crops is always prioritized. Without such a unit, there is a risk of diverting electricity away from irrigation, which can negatively impact crop yield, especially in water-scarce regions. Different crops require varying amounts of water, and efficient water management is crucial for optimizing crop production in agrivoltaic systems. By integrating a solar water pumping unit tailored to the irrigation needs of specific crops, the system can support precision irrigation technologies.The decentralized nature of a separate solar water pumping unit is particularly important for farms located in remote areas. In such locations, access to a reliable energy supply can be inconsistent or non-existent. By having an independent solar water pumping system, farms can ensure a steady and reliable water supply for irrigation without depending on external energy sources. This autonomy is crucial for maintaining agricultural productivity, especially in off-grid areas where managing both energy and water resources can be challenging. As agrivoltaic systems are scaled up or as farms expand, the water demands will also increase. A flexible solar water pumping system allows for easy scalability, with additional units added as necessary. This ensures that water management can grow alongside energy production without either aspect outpacing the other. Ensuring this balance is critical for the long-term. Previous research on agrivoltaics has largely focused on energy generation and its impact on agricultural productivity, with limited attention to the specifics of water management. Few studies have explored the practical integration of solar water pumping units into these systems, especially in the context of decentralized energy production and precision irrigation. This study fills this gap by emphasizing the importance of dedicated water pumping systems and their role in optimizing both energy and agricultural outputs. By addressing this, the research contributes to a more holistic understanding of how agrivoltaic systems can be made more efficient, sustainable, and adaptable to diverse farming needs.

Research focus and key contributions

The paper primarily focuses on the general design of a solar water pumping system for an agrivoltaics farm growing lettuce in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia. The study thoroughly examines critical factors such as water requirements, motor efficiency, pump efficiency, system efficiency, electrical, hydraulic, and mechanical energy yield, and energy losses. While the paper presents a comprehensive analysis of water requirements, system efficiencies, energy yields, and losses for a 0.5-ha lettuce agrivoltaic farm in Kuala Lumpur, it primarily focuses on establishing a foundational framework for this emerging technology. While it does not explore the various comparative analyses of solar pump designs, it offers a robust starting point for future investigations into optimization and customization.

Uniqueness of the approach

This approach emphasizes the role of the solar water pumping unit within the broader agrivoltaic framework, enhancing both energy efficiency and agricultural productivity. Even though the agrivoltaic system generates electricity that could theoretically be used for water pumping, integrating a dedicated solar water pumping unit ensures that water supply for irrigation is prioritized. This prevents any competition for power between irrigation needs and other potential uses of electricity generated by the solar panels. A separate solar water pumping unit can operate independently from the main agrivoltaic system. This decentralization is crucial in situations where the energy demands for crop production and other farm activities might vary. Different crops have different water requirements. A solar water pumping unit tailored to the specific irrigation needs of the crops in an agrivoltaic system allows for more precise water management. The solar water pumping unit can be designed to work in conjunction with precision irrigation systems (like drip irrigation), which are highly effective in agrivoltaic setups. This ensures that the water pumped is used efficiently, directly contributing to the optimal growth of crops under the solar panels. As the farm expands or as the agrivoltaic system is scaled up, the water pumping unit can also be scaled or additional units can be added. This flexibility ensures that water management can grow alongside energy production without one aspect outpacing the other. This autonomy is crucial for farms in remote locations, where consistent energy access is a challenge. The unit ensures that water pumping remains operational without needing complex energy management systems.

Irradiation is one of the critical factors in solar energy systems, specifically solar arrays or photovoltaic (PV) panels. Irradiation refers to the amount of sunlight or solar energy that reaches a specific area. It is typically measured in kilowatt-hours per square meter (kWh/m2) or equivalent units. The amount of irradiation a solar array receives directly affects its energy production. High irradiation levels are desirable for solar arrays because they result in increased energy generation. Locations with ample sunlight, such as regions closer to the equator or areas with minimal shading from trees or buildings, tend to have higher irradiation levels. Assessing the average annual solar irradiation for a specific location helps determine the solar array's potential energy output and the feasibility of solar power generation. Tilt Angle and Orientation: The tilt angle of the solar array is also essential. The ideal tilt angle is typically equal to the location's latitude, but adjustments can be made to optimize energy production during different seasons. Tilting the panels at an appropriate angle helps maximize the capture of sunlight throughout the year, as the sun's height in the sky changes with the seasons. The solar panels are placed in the south with a tilt angle of 4° and an area of 0.5 ha. The orientation of a solar array refers to the angle at which the PV panels are tilted and their direction relative to the sun's path. Optimal exposure allows the panels to capture maximum sunlight throughout the day. The prevailing practice involves positioning the solar array towards the south (in the Northern Hemisphere) or north (in the Southern Hemisphere) to optimize energy production. This orientation provides the panels with the most direct sunlight exposure. However, slight variations in direction, such as east or west-facing arrays, may be suitable depending on specific circumstances, such as energy demand patterns or shading considerations.

Agri-solar water pumping simulation using SISIFO

The performance of the agrivoltaics system is done with the help of the simulation tool SISIFO [36] The software is in open access [36,37]. It integrates the bifaciality of solar PV systems and also helps simulate multiple parallel pumps for PV irrigation systems. Creating a free web framework was undertaken as part of the European Project focused on the Market Uptake of an Innovative Irrigation Solution Based On Low Water-Energy Consumption (MASLOWATEN). Fig. 7 provides the front end of the web framework for simulating photovoltaic system (SISIFO).

SISIFO Software interface (https://www.sisifo.info/en/default).

SISIFO is a freely available web framework designed for simulating Photovoltaic Systems. Users can use the SISIFO simulation tool to model PV grid-connected plants and PV irrigation systems by inputting relevant models and data. The tool presents results with a focus on ensuring quality and enhancing bankability. Key information now accessible for any site includes the 12 daily monthly average global horizontal irradiation, ambient temperature measurements, and crucial simulation inputs.

The web framework utilizes these average values to generate the year's artificial daily and hourly data series. The toolbox subsequently computes the irradiance on the PV generator's sloped surface. The simulation employs the Crystalline Solar PV module, a common choice for large solar PV irrigation systems, providing detailed results. Additionally, the modeling accounts for the impacts of shade, dirt, incidence-angle losses, and spectrum variations. The system components, including the PV generator, frequency converter, and transformer, are modeled based on parameters from standard information sources (datasheets, catalogs, specifications) or site experimental data. Loss parameters

Spectral Losses: This occurs in photovoltaic (PV) systems due to the mismatch between the solar radiation spectrum and the solar cells' spectral response [42]. Solar radiation consists of photons with different energies, but solar cells can only absorb photons with energies above their bandgap energy [43]. Photons with lower energies are not absorbed and are wasted. Photons with higher energies are absorbed, but only a fraction of their energy is converted into electricity [44]. The excess energy is dissipated as heat.

Inverter Input Power: When the irradiance is high, meaning it is more significant than 1000 W/m2, the inverter limits the input DC power, limiting the inverter power according to the inverter's rated capacity [45]. A high DC-to-AC ratio boosts generation at lower irradiation [45]. Thus, at high irradiance, the power produced by an enormous PV array is lost, known as inverter power clipping loss [45] or inverter input power loss.

Cell temperature: Cell temperature loss in solar PV refers to a power reduction caused by elevated temperatures in the solar cells. Increased temperatures reduce the bandgap of the semiconductor material, impacting both the open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current of the solar cell.

Low Irradiance: The current generated in the PV is affected by the irradiance levels on the per unit area. Generally, solar PV is rated at STC conditions i.e. 1000 w/m2, AMI 1.5 and 25° temperature.

DC Wiring Loss: This type of power loss occurs in photovoltaic (PV) systems due to the resistance of the wires that connect the solar panels and the inverter. The resistance of the wires depends on their material, length, and cross-sectional area.

Dust and Incidence Loss: Dust and incidence loss in solar PV are two types of power losses that affect the performance and efficiency of photovoltaic systems [46]. Incidence loss is the reduction of solar radiation absorbed by the solar cells due to the angle of incidence of the sunlight [40,47]. Incidence loss depends on the tilt angle and orientation of the modules, as well as the time of day and year [48]. Incidence loss can be minimized by using tracking systems that adjust the position of the modules to follow the sun's movement.

LV wiring or AC wiring loss: LV wiring loss is a type of power loss that occurs in photovoltaic (PV) systems due to the resistance of the wires that connect the low voltage (LV) side of the inverter to the load in the system.

Motor Loss: Motor loss in solar PV pumping refers to the energy losses that occur within the motor of the pump system due to inefficiencies such as heat generation, friction, and electrical resistance. Results and discussions

The results of the Agri -solar water pumping system performance were designed and analyzed by altering the various input parameters of this system, which were examined in detail for the proposed site.

Solar Resource assessment

In the design of a Agri -solar water pumping system, it is essential to conduct a thorough site analysis. This analysis should encompass studying the sun's path, assessing prevailing wind patterns, considering the surrounding terrain, and leveraging the natural environment to enhance photovoltaic (PV) module cooling. The solar site conditions like solar irradiation and other factors affect lettuce growth, annual energy generation and water pumping performance. Kuala Lumpur is an ideal location for small-scale solar water pumping installations due to its equatorial position. The monthly solar radiation in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, averages between 2.069 and 8.08 kWh/m2 with an average value of 4921.367 Wh/m2 (Table 5). The site experiences 11–12 h of bright sunshine daily. This radiation increases as the duration of the sun rises during certain months. Furthermore, the southwest monsoon season, which occurs from May to August, enhances global and extraterrestrial solar radiation on a horizontal surface. During this period, the sunshine duration increases to slightly over 12 h. It rises during the Southwest monsoon and decreases during the Northeast monsoon. Sunshine duration is notably higher in the Southwest monsoon period and diminishes during the Northeast monsoon. These values highlight the substantial solar potential for solar PV pumping in agrivoltaics site locations. The case study location experiences an annual average Peak Sun Hours (PSH) of 4.5 h daily. This particular value determines the water flow rate and the daily volume of water required for the irrigation system. Fig. 9 illustrates the sun-path diagram of the Kuala Lumpur location.

Agri- Solar pumping system design and sizing

The daily water demand and the storage tank sizing for irrigating the lettuce plantation are deduced from published information on lettuce cultivation and specific data from the case study plantation. This paper utilizes data on plant density, the plant's daily water needs, and the irrigation water flow rate to ascertain the daily water requirement for the lettuce plantation under consideration. The average monthly amount of water needed to irrigate Agri-solar lettuce farming in a land of 0.5 ha is theoretically estimated based on several assumptions. The farm requires 3256.52 cubic meters of water annually to cultivate lettuce on 0.5 ha of agricultural land in Kuala Lumpur. Similarly, the temperature variation recorded and used for simulation ranges from 15 °C to 40.5 °C, and the quantity of water required is 36 L/day for 1 acre of land. The water well was designed and constructed with a diameter of 15.24 cm and a depth of approximately 50 m. For water extraction from the well, a submersible pump (Grundfos-SQF5A-6) employing the centrifugal principle, equipped with a built-in microprocessor MPPT (maximum power point tracking) controller, was selected. The pump is designed to operate within the specifications of receiving DC voltage ranging from 30 V to 300 V. It has a power rating of 900 W, a head capacity of 30–50 m, and a flow rate within the range of 10–13 m3/h. Submersible pumps are considered noiseless, and no extra cooling is required. The technological feasibility of an Agri-solar water pumping system designed for implementation in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, is studied. However, determining the most suitable design proves challenging due to the influence of multiple parameters. Factors such as the type of photovoltaic module, the pumping system's location, pumping head, weather conditions, and system configurations all contribute to the complexity of selecting the optimum setup.

Loss analysis

The various losses that occur during the energy conversion from the solar water pumping simulation model for the study location are presented in the Sankey Loss diagram in Fig. 12. The provided Sankey diagram effectively visualizes the energy losses in a solar energy system, highlighting the efficiency challenges across different stages from reference yield to total energy yield. The significant losses identified include energy losses due to irradiation loss, temperature loss, module loss, mismatch loss of modules and strings, converter loss due to power threshold, and voltage losses. Losses owing to module temperature, irradiance level, Quality loss, losses related to efficiency, power threshold, over nominal converter voltage, and voltage threshold are all easily identifiable system flaws. Load Loss, Pump loss, Temperature-related loss and Motor loss are major loss contributors, while Irradiance below threshold and angle of incidence loss are moderate loss whereas spectrum loss, Inverter loss, wiring loss (DC and AC) and Real STC power loss contributes as minor loss.

Sankey/Loss diagram of Agri-solar water pumping system.

Unused energy available is very high in this location due to the type of cultivation, climate, energy harvesting etc. The most significant loss, accounting for 40 % of the total energy, occurs at the load stage. This indicates there is inefficiency related to energy consumption within the system. The pump stage also contributes to a considerable loss of 10.70 %. High loss could indicate inefficiencies in pump operation, possibly due to sizing and selection. Motor inefficiency is another notable contributor to energy loss. The 7.80 % loss suggests potential areas for improvement in motor design or operation. The 7.20 % temperature-related loss is due to high ambient temperature. Increased temperature reduce the efficiency if photovoltaic panels, thereby lowering the overall energy yield. Losses due to irradiance below the threshold occur when the sunlight is insufficient to generate the desired power output. This could be due to shading, cloud cover, or suboptimal solar angles. The angle of incidence is crucial for maximizing solar energy capture.

A 5.80 % loss indicates that the solar panels might not always be positioned optimally relative to the sun. Spectrum loss relates to the inability of solar panels to convert certain wavelengths of sunlight into electricity. This loss is relatively small compared to others but still notable. Wiring losses, though minimal, are a standard inefficiency in any electrical system. These losses occur due to resistance in the wires carrying current between system components. The inverter, responsible for converting DC to AC, has a small but significant loss of 1.70 %. Inverter efficiency typically ranges between 95 and 98 %, so this loss is within expected parameters. The 9.00 % loss due to real Standard Test Conditions (STC) power reflects the difference between the theoretical output under ideal conditions and the actual output in real-world conditions.

Loss mitigation

Implementing an energy management system (EMS) or demand-side management helps in optimizing energy use, which can reduce the percentage of load loss. Investing in high-efficiency pumps or optimizing pump operation schedules based on actual irrigation needs could mitigate this loss. Additionally, regular maintenance and monitoring could prevent unnecessary energy wastage. Mitigating temperature losses could involve using solar panels with better temperature coefficients or implementing cooling mechanisms like passive cooling systems or water cooling to maintain optimal panel temperatures. Optimizing panel placement and orientation, implementing tracking systems, or using bifacial panels that can capture reflected light could help in mitigating these losses. Installing tracking systems that adjust the panel orientation throughout the day could reduce incidence losses. Alternatively, optimizing the fixed tilt angles based on seasonal variations could also be beneficial. Utilizing panels with a broader spectral response range or advanced materials that capture more of the solar spectrum could help reduce this loss. Ensuring that the wiring is appropriately sized and of high quality, and minimizing the length of cable runs, could reduce these losses. Regular inspection and maintenance of wiring can also prevent unnecessary energy dissipation. Using high-efficiency inverters and ensuring they operate within their optimal load range can further minimize these losses.

The comparative efficiencies, carbon offset potential, and scalable design underscore the novel contributions of this work to the field of agrivoltaics, providing a practical roadmap for integrating solar energy into agricultural systems.
