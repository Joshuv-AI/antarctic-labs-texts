Risk of Cardiovascular Disease According to Standard Industrial Sectors: A Nationwide Longitudinal Study
Authors: Hee Joo Park, Youngsun Park, Juyeon Oh, Jian Lee, Juho Sim, Byungyoon Yun, Jin-Ha Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading global cause of morbidity and mortality, with occupational factors significantly contributing to risk. INTRODUCTION

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) continues to be a leading cause of mortality worldwide, with the number of CVD-related deaths steadily increasing.1 From 1990 to 2019, the global prevalence of CVD nearly doubled, rising from 271 million to 523 million cases,2 and by 2023, annual CVD-related deaths escalated to approximately 20.5 million, accounting for nearly one-third of all global deaths.3 In East Asian countries such as Taiwan, Singapore, Japan, and Republic of Korea (South Korea), CVD ranks as the second leading cause of death following cancer.4

CVD is influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including age, sex, genetics, chronic metabolic diseases, unhealthy lifestyle behaviors (such as smoking and physical inactivity), environmental exposures (such as air pollution and noise), and socioeconomic status.5,6,7,8 Occupational factors—such as physical demands, exposure to noise and hazardous chemicals, and prolonged working hours—also substantially contribute to CVD risk.9,10,11,12,13,14 For instance, U.S. studies have revealed lower CVD mortality rates among professionals compared with that among workers in the manufacturing or service industries,15 whereas research in France has highlighted a higher CVD risk among men in the construction and transportation sectors and among women in the health and social sectors.16 Conversely, studies in Japan and Korea have suggested that those with high-status occupations, such as professionals and managers, may face elevated CVD risks.17,18

Although the impact of specific occupational hazards—such as noise, chemical exposures, and long working hours—on CVD risk is well-documented, most studies have been focused on risk factors in isolation, often neglecting the complex interplay of multiple occupational and socioeconomic determinants within specific industries. CVD risk may be compared across industries to comprehensively capture the combined effects of occupational exposures, work environments, and socioeconomic factors unique to each sector. Unlike studies in which individual hazards are targeted, such an industry-based analysis reflects the real-world occupational context, enabling the identification of high-risk sectors and supporting tailored public health interventions. By leveraging standardized occupational classifications, this approach enhances comparability across populations and offers actionable insights for policymakers to mitigate the CVD burden in East Asian settings, where occupational health disparities remain underexplored. Given Korea’s distinctive industrial landscape and the high prevalence of metabolic diseases among working-age adults,1 we aimed to investigate CVD risk across standard industrial sectors using a nationwide dataset.

METHODS

Data source and study population

This study investigated middle-aged paid workers (aged 40–59 years) using data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) of Korea, which covers over 97% of the population and integrates biennial health screening records with insurance claims data.19,20,21 The NHIS database contains comprehensive information, such as demographic and socioeconomic data (including standard industry sectors), as well as medical information, such as outpatient visits, hospitalizations, diagnosis codes, prescribed medications, and structured lifestyle questionnaires. The study population comprised individuals aged 40–59 years who underwent national health examinations between 2009 and 2010, and incident CVD events were identified through follow-up insurance claims records. The index date was set to that of the baseline health examination for each participant.

The exclusion criteria for the study were as follows: 1) missing values for variables related to socioeconomic status, measurements, blood tests, or lifestyle factors; 2) a history of CVD; and 3) being categorized in one of the following major categories of the Korean Standard Industry Classification (KSIC), 10th edition: “Activities of Households as Employers; Undifferentiated Goods- and Services-producing Activities of Households for Own Use” or “Activities of Extraterritorial Organizations and Bodies” (categories T and U in Supplementary Table 1); 4) Diseases were assessed based on insurance claims data during the two-year look-back period from the index date.

Main outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of CVD. The diagnosis of CVD was defined as the first hospitalization with any of the following International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes: I20–I25, I50, and I60–I69.22 Secondary outcomes were all-cause mortality, incidence of ischemic heart disease (ICD-10 codes I20–I25),23 and incidence of Cerebrovascular diseases (ICD-10 codes I60–I69).24 Participants were followed until the development of the primary outcome or until December 2023, whichever occurred first.

Independent variables and covariates

The independent variable was the industrial sector, ranging from “Agriculture, Forestry and Fishing” to “Membership Organizations, Repair and Other Personal Services” (categories A–S of the KSIC, 10th edition; Supplementary Table 1). The covariates (obtained from a questionnaire) encompassed age; income level; residential areas (Seoul, Metropolitan, or others); company size; the presence of diabetes, hypertension, or obesity; and lifestyle variables (smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity).

Income level was estimated indirectly using monthly National Health Insurance premiums, which reflect participants’ income and assets. These premiums were categorized into quartiles, separated by sex. Company size was divided into four categories based on the number of employees: < 5, 5–49, 50–299, and ≥ 300.25,26 Chronic conditions, including diabetes, hypertension, and obesity, were identified using a combination of clinical measurements and medical claims data. Diabetes was identified through prescriptions for antidiabetic medications corresponding to ICD-10 codes E11–E14 or a fasting blood sugar concentration ≥ 126 mg/dL.27 Hypertension was determined by prescriptions for antihypertensive medications based on ICD-10 codes I10–I15 or blood pressure (BP) readings of systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg.28 Obesity was classified into four categories based on body mass index (BMI): normal (BMI < 23 kg/m2), overweight (BMI: 23–24.9 kg/m2), obese class I (BMI: 25–29.9 kg/m2), and obese class II (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2).29

Lifestyle factors, including smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity, were collected via structured self-reported questionnaires administered during the national health screening program. Smoking status was classified into three groups: non-smokers, ex-smokers, and current smokers. Alcohol consumption was categorized by weekly intake level: mild (< 210 g for males, < 140 g for females), moderate (< 420 g for males, < 350 g for females), and severe (≥ 420 g for males, ≥ 350 g for females).30 Each participant’s physical activity (metabolic equivalent of task [MET]min/week) was determined based on their reported levels of vigorous exercise (7 METs), moderate exercise (4 METs), and walking (2.9 METs).31 Physical activity levels were categorized into the following four groups: < 500, 500–999, 1,000–1,499, and ≥ 1,500 MET-min/week.32

Statistical analysis

All analyses were stratified by sex. Baseline characteristics of participants were analyzed using χ2 tests for categorical variables and ANOVA for continuous variables, respectively.

Standardized incidence ratios (SIRs) were calculated to compare the observed number of CVD cases in each industrial sector with the expected number, which was determined based on age- and sex-specific incidence rates from the total study population and served as the reference population. Both the primary and secondary outcomes were evaluated using SIRs. Age standardization was performed using the indirect standardization method, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the SIRs were calculated assuming a Poisson distribution. To evaluate the impact of risk factors, SIRs were further stratified according to obesity (BMI < 25 vs. BMI ≥ 25), smoking status (smoker and ex-smoker vs. non-smoker), and the presence of diabetes mellitus and hypertension. Each stratified SIR was calculated separately while maintaining the same age-standardization approach. Additionally, age-standardized mortality was assessed using the indirect standardization method. Multivariable Cox proportional-hazards models were used to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% CIs for both primary and secondary outcomes. To confirm the results, four different models were constructed: 1) model 1 was adjusted for age; 2) model 2 was adjusted for age, residential area, income level, and company size; 3) model 3 was adjusted for variables related to chronic disease factors (hypertension, diabetes, and obesity) in addition to those in model 2; 4) the final model was adjusted for lifestyle-related variables, along with those included in model 3. In all analyses, the reference group was defined as the Education sector (category P in Supplementary Table 1).

To evaluate the robustness of the primary outcome definition, a sensitivity analysis was conducted, defining CVD as the occurrence of three or more distinct outpatient or inpatient claims with relevant ICD-10 codes (I20–I25, I50, I60–I69). Additionally, a complementary analysis was performed, defining CVD as a hospital admission of ischemic events (ICD-10 code: I2123, I60–I64) accompanied by either a relevant cardiovascular procedure or a prescription for antiplatelet agents.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software version 8.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all results.

Ethics statement

This study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Severance Hospital (IRB No. 4-2022-0813). The need for informed consent was waived by the IRB owing to the retrospective nature of this study.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 3,745,753 participants who underwent national health screening between 2009 and 2010 were initially enrolled. We excluded 27,561 individuals with a prior history of CVD, 31,738 individuals with missing data on key variables (systolic BP: n = 247; fasting blood glucose: n = 230; BMI: n = 149; income level: n = 16,382; residential region: n = 1,515; smoking status: n = 2,259; physical activity: n = 5,420; alcohol consumption: n = 5,536), and 15,880 individuals employed in sectors T and U (Supplementary Fig. 1). After exclusions, 3,670,574 participants (2,421,513 males, 65.9%; 1,249,061 females, 34.1%) were included in the final analysis. The median age was 48 years for both males and females. High-income groups were predominantly employed in industry class K (“Financial and Insurance Activities”), whereas low-income groups were primarily employed in industry class F (“Construction”). Regarding health status, hypertension was most prevalent in industry class O (“Public Administration and Defense”), diabetes in industry class H (“Transportation and Storage”), and obesity in industry class P (“Education”) among males; similar trends were observed in females. In terms of company size and residential area, both males and females were mostly employed in large companies (≥ 300 employees) within metropolitan or non-metropolitan areas, although this differed among the sectors (Table 1, Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Stratified analyses for age-SIRs of primary outcomes

According to the subgroup analysis based on obesity, smoking, diabetes and hypertension (Table 3), males with a BMI ≥ 25 had the highest SIR in the “H. Transportation and Storage” sector (1.15; 95% CI, 1.12–1.17). Similarly, among females with a BMI ≥ 25, the “H. Transportation and Storage” sector also exhibited the highest SIR (1.34; 95% CI, 1.18–1.52). Among smokers, males exhibited the highest SIR in the same sector (1.20; 95% CI, 1.18–1.22), whereas no statistically significant SIRs were observed among female smokers. In the diabetes subgroup, the highest SIR among males was again observed in the “H. Transportation and Storage” sector (1.19; 95% CI, 1.15–1.22), and the same was true among females (1.39; 95% CI, 1.08–1.76). In the hypertension subgroup, the highest SIR among males was observed in the “A. Agriculture, Forestry and Fishing” sector (1.23; 95% CI, 1.07–1.40), whereas females with hypertension exhibited the highest SIR in the “H. Transportation and Storage” sector (1.38; 95% CI, 1.20–1.58).

DISCUSSION

In this study based on the standard industry classification, middle-aged participants who underwent a national health examination from 2009 to 2010 exhibited varying risks of CVD across industrial sectors over a mean follow-up period of almost 10 years. Participants in the sector “H. Transportation and Storage” consistently exhibited heightened vulnerability to CVD regardless of sex. Males also exhibited elevated risks in the “A. Agriculture, Forestry and Fishing” sector and females in the “D. Electricity, Gas, Steam and Air Conditioning Supply” and “I. Accommodation and Food Service Activities” sectors. Sensitivity analyses using alternative CVD definitions confirmed elevated risks in males for the “A. Agriculture, Forestry and Fishing”, “B. Mining and Quarrying,” “F. Construction,” and “H. Transportation and Storage” sectors, and in females for the “D. Electricity, Gas, Steam and Air Conditioning Supply” and “I. Accommodation and Food Service Activities” sectors. The consistent identification of these sectors across the main and sensitivity analyses suggests that they represent industries with genuinely elevated CVD risk.

Outdoor and field-based sectors, such as “A. Agriculture, Forestry and Fishing,” “B. Mining and Quarrying” and “F. Construction” were associated with higher CVD risks. However, observations in smaller sectors such as agriculture and mining should be interpreted cautiously owing to limited sample sizes, which might have resulted in an overestimation of attributable risks. The “F. Construction” sector, characterized by long working hours, shift work, and hazardous conditions,33,34 exhibited elevated CVD and mortality risks, consistent with the results of previous research.21

Workers in the “H. Transportation and Storage” sector had a particularly high CVD risk, likely due to a combination of prolonged sedentary behavior, disrupted circadian rhythms caused by shift work, and chronic occupational stress.33,35,36 Prolonged sitting impairs autonomic regulation and sleep quality, whereas long working hours impair cognitive performance, decision-making, and recovery, thereby amplifying the accident risk and cardiovascular strain.37,38 Chronic psychosocial stress further activates neuroendocrine pathways, promoting vascular inflammation and increasing the CVD risk.39

These occupational exposures also contribute to metabolic disorders. Our study revealed a higher prevalence of obesity and diabetes among male workers in transportation-related jobs such as truck driving, taxi driving, and public transportation, consistent with prior research results linking sedentary work, a Westernized diet because of the demands of their job (quick and frequent meals), and poor lifestyle habits to CVD.40,41,42,43,44 Additionally, smoking prevalence remains high in the “H. Transportation and Storage” and “F. Construction” sectors, further compounding the occupational CVD burden.45

Among female workers, the highest CVD risks were observed in service-oriented sectors, particularly the “D. Electricity, Gas, Steam and Air Conditioning Supply” and “I. Accommodation and Food Service Activities” sector. The elevated CVD risks observed in these sectors are not fully understood due to the limited information on occupations in the current dataset. While the “D. Electricity, Gas, Steam and Air Conditioning Supply” sector may include physically demanding, field-based roles such as meter readers or inspection personnel, potentially involving irregular working hours and exposure to adverse environmental conditions, the specific job roles within this sector remain unclear. Similarly, the “I. Accommodation and Food Service Activities” sector likely encompasses a range of roles, such as hotel managers, chefs, cooks, or baristas, which may involve emotionally demanding work and long or irregular hours. These occupational characteristics could contribute to increased emotional and physical stress, potentially leading to dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, sustained cortisol overproduction, and hippocampal atrophy, thereby elevating CVD risk.39 In contrast, office-based roles within these sectors may not exhibit comparable levels of CVD risk. Therefore, further research is needed to identify specific job roles that may contribute to elevated CVD risk.

These findings highlight the significant influence of occupational factors on CVD risk, emphasizing the necessity of industry-specific comparisons rather than solely within-occupation analyses. CVD risk was primarily examined within specific occupational groups in previous studies15,16; however, our study highlights substantial differences across sectors, particularly within the Korean industrial landscape in which job characteristics and work conditions vary widely.

The main strengths of this study are the comprehensive assessment of CVD and mortality risks across all industrial sectors in an Asian country, use of a nationally representative database that covers 97.2% of the Korean population, and application of multiple statistical methods for cross-verification of results. However, this study also has several limitations. First, owing to the nature of the database, we were able to identify participants’ industrial sectors but not their specific occupations. Second, employment status might have been misclassified for some individuals, as it was inferred from insurance type, which can be particularly inaccurate at both extremes of the income distribution. Third, the absence of cause-of-death data prevented us from distinguishing between CVD-related deaths and deaths from other causes, such as suicide or accidents. Fourth, this study specifically examined employees classified by industrial sector, excluding self-employed individuals. Consequently, sectors with a high proportion of self-employed workers, such as “A. Agriculture, Forestry and Fishing,” may not fully represent the actual disease burden. Fifth, lifestyle factors, including smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity levels, were derived from self-reported health screening questionnaires, which exhibited a relatively high proportion of missing data in the initial dataset. Although participants with incomplete data were excluded from the final analysis to ensure data integrity, this approach may have introduced selection bias, potentially limiting the generalizability of the study findings to broader populations. Finally, caution should be exercised when these results are generalized to other populations, as socioeconomic conditions and labor market structures differ across countries.

In summary, CVD risk varies substantially across industries, reflecting distinct occupational exposures and working conditions. Our results highlight the urgent need for targeted prevention strategies in high-risk groups, particularly male workers in the transportation sector and female workers in service-oriented roles.
