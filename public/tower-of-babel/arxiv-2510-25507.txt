Distributional Evaluation of Generative Models via Relative Density Ratio
Yuliang Xu, Yun Wei, Li Ma, [4mm] Department of Statistics & Data Science Institute, University of Chicago, Department of Mathematical Science, University of Texas at Dallas

Abstract
We propose a functional evaluation metric for generative models based on the relative density ratio (RDR) designed to characterize distributional differences between real and generated samples. We show that the RDR as a functional summary of the goodness-of-fit for the generative model, possesses several desirable theoretical properties. It preserves ϕ \phi -divergence between two distributions, enables sample-level evaluation that facilitates downstream investigations of feature-specific distributional differences, and has a bounded range that affords clear interpretability and numerical stability. Functional estimation of the RDR is achieved efficiently through convex optimization on the variational form of ϕ \phi -divergence. We provide theoretical convergence rate guarantees for general estimators based on M-estimator theory, as well as the convergence rates of neural network-based estimators when the true ratio is in the anisotropic Besov space. We demonstrate the power of the proposed RDR-based evaluation through numerical experiments on MNIST, CelebA64, and the American Gut project microbiome data. We show that the estimated RDR not only allows for an effective comparison of the overall performance of competing generative models, but it can also offer a convenient means of revealing the nature of the underlying goodness-of-fit. This enables one to assess support overlap, coverage, and fidelity while pinpointing regions of the sample space where generators concentrate and revealing the features that drive the most salient distributional differences.



Keywords:
Model assessment, nonparametric inference, deep learning, empirical processes, microbiome compositions.

1
Introduction

Generative AI has become an integral part of modern society, facilitating tasks in everyday life and advancing scientific discovery. With the remarkable development and deployment of generative models in domains ranging from text and image generation to scientific data synthesis, there is growing interest in determining which models perform better in different contexts.
We have witnessed great success in text and image generation using various model classes, including VAEs, GANs, diffusion models, and continuous flow matching models. However, despite the realism of generated samples to the human eye, studies have revealed distributional shifts between samples produced by advanced generators and those observed in real data. This phenomenon has been observed for many modern generators, such as GANs
(
Richardson and Weiss 2018
)
and diffusion models
(
Sehwag et al. 2022
)
, which are not easily detected by visual inspection. Such discrepancies are particularly concerning when generative AI is used for scientific applications, where the fidelity of the underlying data distribution is critical. Thus, a key step toward trustworthy generative modeling is to understand, at a distributional level, how and where the generator diverges from the true data generation mechanism.

Existing automated evaluation criteria for modern generative models have largely focused on benchmarking generators through
scalar
metrics. Popular metrics include the Fréchet Inception Distance
(
Heusel et al. 2017
, FID)
and the Inception Score
(
Salimans et al. 2016
, IS)
, both of which rely on strong parametric assumptions or low-dimensional feature mappings to assess model performance on large-scale, high-dimensional data.
Simon et al. 2019
;
Sajjadi et al. 2018
provides a precision-recall curve to describe the coverage and fidelity of generators based on low-dimensional features of the original data. While these metrics capture human-perceivable fidelity, they largely overlook underlying distributional discrepancies. As a result, even a generator that samples extensively from a narrow region of the true data support can achieve a high score, provided that the generated samples
look
realistic. More importantly, in scientific data generation, as we will demonstrate in a microbiome example, visually convincing generations may be biased and fail to represent the true data-generating mechanism in important ways that could bias the downstream scientific analysis. This issue is critical in many scientific application domains where the generated samples are used to augment observed data
(
Choi et al. 2023
)
and to perform downstream analyzes such as biomarker detection
(
Marouf et al. 2020
)
or cell clustering
(
Lall et al. 2022
)
. The goal of this work is to propose a
functional
metric, rather than a single scalar metric, capable of providing both distribution-level and sample-level assessments. Our approach is based on the relative density ratio, which is simple to implement and scalable to massive, high-dimensional datasets. We illustrate how this framework allows for the identification of features associated with the detected distributional discrepancies between observed and generated data.

The classic two-sample comparison methods in statistics have mostly adopted a hypothesis testing perspective. The predominant approach involves computing a test statistic, which is usually a scalar distance metric, and based on that, computing the p-value under its (asymptotic) null distribution. Examples of univariate samples include the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and the Anderson-Darling test
(
Razali et al. 2011
)
.
Modern nonparametric methods, such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) and Energy Distance
(
Gretton et al. 2012
;
Székely and Rizzo 2013
)
, use prespecified kernel embeddings or Euclidean distances to provide a distance metric between two distributions. These methods aim to address the question of how far two distributions are apart through a single scalar metric. As such, they cannot reveal exactly how and where they differ. One is often satisfied with a simulation based
p
-value for these metrics attained through resampling.

In general, we question the relevance of the hypothesis testing perspective on two-sample comparisons in the context of assessing modern generative models—
all models are wrong, and all generative models are definitely quite wrong
in applications that involve highly complex data generative mechanisms. There is no question that the null hypothesis, with sufficient data (often available in synthetic experiments),
will
be rejected. The more valuable question is how the generative model fails to capture the truth. As such, we believe the functional estimation perspective is more appropriate.

Which function should we estimate as a way to summarize two-sample discrepancies? A natural candidate is the
density ratio
, i.e., the ratio of the two sampling densities.
Indeed, density ratio estimation (DRE) has been studied, and well-known approaches include kernel-based methods such as KLIEP
(
Sugiyama et al. 2007
)
and uLSIF
(
Kanamori et al. 2009
)
. More recently,
Awaya et al. 2025
introduced an approach that uses additive tree ensembles along with a new loss function to learn the DR and achieves generalized Bayesian uncertainty quantification.
A fundamental assumption of the DRE approach is that the two distributions must have the same support; otherwise, the ratio is undefined in parts of the space. Such an assumption is easily violated in very high-dimensional, complex data generative settings. This issue can incur severe extrapolation errors, a form of overfitting, when highly flexible function approximators, such as neural-networks, are adopted for DRE.

We circumvent this difficulty in direct DRE by considering the following modification to the density ratio:

\displaystyle r(x)=\frac{p(x)}{\frac{1}{2}\left\{p(x)+q(x)\right\}}

(1)

as our choice of the functional metric, which
Yamada et al. 2013
first considered and referred to as the
relative density ratio
(RDR). In our setting, the denominator corresponds specifically to the 50–50 mixture of
p
and
q
. While
Yamada et al. 2013
is largely grounded in kernel-based methods for RDR estimation, our estimation approach is tailored to neural-network-based estimators using
\phi
-divergence objectives, following the general strategy introduced in
Nguyen et al. 2010
. We will show in Section
2
that the RDR has a bounded range, good interpretability, and numerical stability for neural network estimators.

Alongside the proposed relative density ratio framework, we contribute new theoretical insights and empirical results on both image generation and scientific domains, including the relative abundance microbiome data from the American Gut Project
(
McDonald et al. 2018b
)
. We first show that the ratio estimator can be viewed as an M-estimator, and we establish consistency and convergence rate results for general estimator classes that satisfy certain entropy bounds. Specifically, for the sparse neural network estimator, building on prior work by
Suzuki and Nitanda 2021
, we show that if the true ratio function lies in an anisotropic Besov space, a function space that allows differing degrees of smoothness across coordinates in high-dimensional settings, then the convergence rate of the neural network estimator is nearly optimal up to a logarithmic factor in
n
, and this rate is dimension-free.

In our numerical experiments, we compare a range of modern generative models, including variational autoencoders (VAEs), generative adversarial networks (deep convolutional GANs,
Radford et al. 2015
), diffusion models (DDIM,
Song et al. 2021b
), and continuous flow matching models (ICFM,
Lipman et al. 2022
), on image datasets such as MNIST and CelebA-64, as well as on the American Gut data. We found that even for the well-performing generators with high fidelity and good coverage, in a simple task of generating digits from MNIST data, the generator tends to have its own bias and concentrate on certain local regions in the high-dimensional support. A similar trend also appears in the higher-dimensional CelebA-64 data and the more complex, highly sparse microbiome data. Furthermore, in the experiments of CelebA-64 and the American Gut Project, we demonstrate how to use this ratio function to detect the features or attributes associated with such distributional discrepancies, making it more interpretable.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the properties of the relative density ratio and demonstrates its connection to existing evaluation criteria in a simple 1D setting. Section
3
provides the estimation methods and theoretical results. Section
4
provides the numerical experiments on MNIST, CelebA-64, and the American Gut Project data. We conclude in Section
5
with a discussion of potential future directions.

For a compact support

When designing an evaluation method, we aim for the following desiderata. Among them, (A) is a common focus of existing methods, but we argue is inadequate by itself, whereas (B) and (C) are additional objectives that are of high practical values which we also aim to achieve.

(A)

A global numeric score for comparing and ranking different generators;

(B)

An observation-level metric that provides insights into the fit of a generator;

(C)

Feature learning for summarizing distributional discrepancies, i.e., identifying features that contribute to the distributional difference.

The RDR, as defined in (
1

Proposition 1
.

The RDR

Connection to existing methods

Connection to DRE.

The main appeal of using
r(x)
rather than the density ratio
g(x)
is that, with a mixture of

and

P\ll\frac{P+Q}{2}

g(x)
, especially in high-dimensional problems. Even though we could assume in theory fully overlapping support for
p
and

; in practice, even for simple 1D Gaussians, the estimated density ratio
p/q
can be far less than the theoretical value when
p
has a nonzero measure but
q
is near-zero. The theoretical properties related to
r(x)
in Proposition
1
yield substantial improvements in numerical estimation compared to

.

and

We observe that for both

\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\theta}=\frac{1}{f_{2}}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial\theta}-\frac{f_{1}}{f_{2}^{2}}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\theta}.

It is more sensitive to the incremental changes in the denominator functions (

) than to the numerator changes in

Connection to binary classification and the “density ratio trick”.

As one of the most classical two-sample comparison methods, binary classifiers center around estimating
P(Z=1|X)
, where
Z\in\left\{0,1\right\}
is the group label, and
X
is the sample of interest. If we assume
X\sim P
when
Z=1
and
X\sim Q
when
Z=0
,

\displaystyle P(Z=1|X)

\displaystyle=\frac{P(X|Z=1)P(Z=1)}{P(X|Z=1)P(Z=1)+P(X|Z=0)P(Z=0)},

then the density ratio can be recovered from the estimated
P(Z=1|X)
through the “density ratio trick” because
g(X)=P(Z=1|X)/P(Z=0|X)\cdot P(Z=0)/P(Z=1)
and
r=2g/(g+1)
.
However, training a binary classifier under misclassification risk can be sensitive to the marginal sample probabilities
P(Z=i),i\in\left\{0,1\right\}
,
and
Awaya et al. 2025
demonstrate numerically that the density ratios estimated with binary classifiers can be severely biased when the two sample sizes are not equal and/or when the support of the two densities have regions of poor overlap.
Estimation of
r(x)
through the variational form of
\phi
-divergence is generally robust to unbalanced sample sizes and poor support overlap. This is highly relevant because both are common scenarios in applications involving generative models.

Connection to precision-recall evaluation.

(

)
. As illustrated in Figure

Q
can be generated by a part of
P
, and recall measures how much of
P
can be generated by a part of

. The left panel in Figure

(partial precision, full recall) indicates that the generator has high fidelity (all

-mass within
\text{supp}(P)
), but low coverage (

,

r(x)
, which gives a clear indication of the precision-recall/coverage-fidelity situation in each case; moreover,

pinpoints exactly which areas in the support suffer from low-fidelity (when

is near 0) and which areas suffer from low-coverage (when

is near 2). The bottom row of Figure

3
Theoretical Analysis

Based on the M-estimator theory
(
van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
)
, with the objective function being the variational form of the squared Hellinger distance (
3
), we first provide the general consistency and convergence rate results for estimators that satisfy a certain entropy bound of the function space in Section
3.1
. Then we explore the sparse neural network function space with 1-Lipschitz activation functions such as ReLU and provide a convergence rate result when the true ratio function is assumed to be in the anisotropic Besov space. In this section, we use
p_{0},q_{0}
to specifically denote the true density functions for distributions
P,Q
. Denote
g_{0}
as the true density ratio
g_{0}=p_{0}/q_{0}
, and
r_{0}
as the true relative density ratio
r_{0}=\frac{2p_{0}}{p_{0}+q_{0}}
.

3.1
General convergence theory

The following theorem establishes the almost sure consistency of the density ratio function as an M-estimator for the variational form of the squared Hellinger distance. The main proof techniques follow directly from
Nguyen et al. 2010
, with the adaptation to find a metric that can lower bound a functional difference induced by the Hellinger distance.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the general functional class representing the search space for the optimization problem in the variational form. As discussed in Section
2
, the variational form of the squared Hellinger divergence in Table
1
can be written as

\sup_{g>0}\left\{1-\frac{1}{2}\int g^{-\frac{1}{2}}\text{d}P-\frac{1}{2}\int g^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{d}Q\right\}.

(5)

The optimizer for this objective function is at
g_{0}=p_{0}/q_{0}
. Let
\mathcal{G}^{1/2}:=\left\{g^{1/2}:g\in\mathcal{G}\right\}
and
\mathcal{G}^{-1/2}:=\left\{g^{-1/2}:g\in\mathcal{G}\right\}
. Let
\mathcal{G}^{1/2}-g_{0}^{1/2}
be the shifted function space
\left\{g^{1/2}-g_{0}^{1/2}:g\in\mathcal{G}\right\}
, similarly
\mathcal{G}^{-1/2}-g_{0}^{-1/2}
the shifted space of
\mathcal{G}^{-1/2}
. To describe the size of the search space
\mathcal{G}
, we denote
N(\delta,\mathcal{G},L^{r}(P))
as the smallest covering number of the space
\mathcal{G}
with metric
L^{r}(P)
and radius
\delta
. We introduce the following assumptions for the general convergence results.

Assumption 1
.

Assume that
\frac{m}{n}\to\rho
as
m,n\to\infty
, where
\rho>0
is a finite constant.

Assumption 2
.

The functional class
\mathcal{G}
contains some
g
such that
g=g_{0}
almost surely.

Assumption 3
.

Define envelope functions
G_{0}(x):=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}|g^{1/2}(x)|
,
G_{1}(x):=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}|g^{-1/2}(x)|
. Assume
\int G_{0}\text{d}Q<\infty
,
\int G_{1}\text{d}P<\infty
. Denote
M_{0}:=\int G_{0}\text{d}Q<\infty
.

Assumption 4
.

Assume that for any
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\log N(\delta,\mathcal{G}^{1/2}-g_{0}^{1/2},L^{1}(Q_{m}))\overset{Q}{\to}0

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log N(\delta,\mathcal{G}^{-1/2}-g_{0}^{-1/2},L^{1}(P_{n}))\overset{P}{\to}0

Assumption
2
is used for the consistency proof. Assumption
3
is a standard assumption in empirical process theory about the envelop functions, and Assumption
4
is the entropy condition for the general estimator space
\mathcal{G}
. Similar assumptions as Assumptions
2
to
4
are made in
Nguyen et al. 2010
.

Recall the squared Hellinger distance is denoted as
H^{2}(P\|Q):=\frac{1}{2}\int(\sqrt{\text{d}P}-\sqrt{\text{d}Q})^{2}
. The corresponding sample-level objective value to (
5
) is denoted as

\hat{H}^{2}=1-\frac{1}{2}\int\hat{g}_{n}^{-1/2}\text{d}P_{n}-\frac{1}{2}\int\hat{g}_{n}^{1/2}\text{d}Q_{m}

(6)

Theorem 2
.

Under Assumption
1
to
4
,
|\hat{H}^{2}-H^{2}(P\|Q)|\overset{\scriptsize\mbox{a.s.}}{\to}0
,
\|\hat{g}_{n}^{1/2}-g_{0}^{1/2}\|_{L^{1}(Q)}\overset{\scriptsize\mbox{a.s.}}{\to}0
and
\|\hat{g}_{n}^{-1/2}-g_{0}^{-1/2}\|_{L^{1}(P)}\overset{\scriptsize\mbox{a.s.}}{\to}0
.

This proof of Theorem
2
follows the proof of Theorem 1 in
Nguyen et al. 2010
, whose theory is based on the Kullback-Leibler divergence, with adaptation to the squared Hellinger distance.

Note that Assumption
4
is a commonly used assumption when
\mathcal{G}^{1/2}-g_{0}^{1/2}
is replaced by some general functional class
\mathcal{F}
. However, this assumption characterizes the size of the functional class in a probabilistic statement through empirical measures
P_{n},Q_{m}
. In the following Lemma
2
, we show that a bounded bracketing number with the population metric
L^{1}(P)
implies that the entropy number with the empirical metric
L^{1}(P_{n})
is of the order
o_{P}(n)
.

A bracketing number of a functional class
\mathcal{F}
is defined as follows: for a metric
\|\cdot\|
, given any small
\delta>0
, a
\delta
-bracket is defined as a pair of functions such that
l\leq f\leq u
and
\|l-u\|\leq\delta
. The bracketing number
N_{[]}(\delta,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|)
is defined as the minimum number of
\delta
-brackets needed to cover
\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma 2
.

For a general functional class
\mathcal{F}
that satisfies for any
\epsilon>0
,
N_{[]}(\epsilon,\mathcal{F},L^{1}(P))<\infty
implies
\frac{1}{n}\log N(\epsilon,\mathcal{F},L^{1}(P_{n}))\overset{P}{\to}0
.

Lemma
2
implies that Assumption
4
can be replaced by
N_{[]}(\epsilon,\mathcal{G}^{1/2}-g_{0}^{1/2},L^{1}(P))<\infty
and
N_{[]}(\epsilon,\mathcal{G}^{-1/2}-g_{0}^{-1/2},L^{1}(P))<\infty
.

Based on the general consistency result in Theorem
2
, we can show the convergence rate of
g
with additional assumptions.

Define the functional squared Hellinger distance with respect to measure
Q
as

h^{2}_{Q}(g,g_{0}):=\int(\sqrt{g}-\sqrt{g_{0}})^{2}\text{d}Q.

(7)

In the proof of the convergence rate, we need to characterize the following two function spaces:

\mathcal{F}_{\delta}^{P}:=\left\{g^{-1/2}-g_{0}^{-1/2}:g\in\mathcal{G},h_{Q}(g,g_{0})<\delta\right\},\quad\mathcal{F}_{\delta}^{Q}:=\left\{g^{1/2}-g_{0}^{1/2}:g\in\mathcal{G},h_{Q}(g,g_{0})<\delta\right\}.

(8)

We follow the notations in
van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
and denote the bracketing entropy integral as follows:

\tilde{J}_{[]}(\delta,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|):=\int_{0}^{\delta}\sqrt{1+\log N_{[]}(\epsilon,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|)}\text{d}\epsilon.

The size of
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}^{P}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}^{Q}
will determine the convergence rate. We make the following assumptions to characterize the function spaces.

Assumption 5
.

Assume that
\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}|g|<M_{u}<\infty
,
\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}|g^{-1}|<M_{l}<\infty
for some constants
M_{u},M_{l}
.

Assumption
5
is stronger than Assumption
3
. A similar assumption is also required for the convergence rate in
Nguyen et al. 2010
.

Assumption 6
.

Assume that for any
\epsilon>0
,
\log N_{[]}(\epsilon,\mathcal{F}_{\eta}^{P},L^{2}(P))=O(\epsilon^{-\gamma_{p}})
, and
\log N_{[]}(\epsilon,\mathcal{F}_{\eta}^{Q},L^{2}(Q))=O(\epsilon^{-\gamma_{q}})
for some positive constants
\gamma_{p}<2,\gamma_{q}<2
, and some fixed small positive constant
\eta
.

Theorem 3
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
4
to
6
,
h_{Q}(\hat{g}_{n},g_{0})=O_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2+\gamma}})
where
\gamma=\min\left\{\gamma_{p}.\gamma_{q}\right\}
.

The above consistency and convergence rate results of the density ratio
g(x)
can be directly applied to the relative density ratio
r(x)
, since throughout the proof of Theorem
2
and Theorem
3
, we do not assume any independence between
P_{n}
and
Q_{m}
. For the result of
r(x)
, we can view it as a type of density ratio by simply changing
Q
to any general mixture
\widetilde{Q}:=\alpha P+(1-\alpha)Q,\alpha\in(0,1)
, similarly for
Q_{m}
. In our case,
\alpha=0.5
.

Corollary 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
4
to
6
, when replacing
Q
by
\widetilde{Q}
, we have that
h_{\widetilde{Q}}(\hat{r}_{n},r_{0})=O_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2+\gamma}})
where
\gamma=\min\left\{\gamma_{p}.\gamma_{q}\right\}
.

Remark.
Due to the boundedness of
r(x)
as shown in Proposition
1
, the upper boundedness assumptions in Assumptions
3
and
5
are no longer needed in Corollary
1
.

3.2
Sieved M-estimator for Neural Networks

The above section provides convergence rate results for general estimators, as long as the estimator’s function space satisfies the entropy conditions in Assumption
6
. In this section, we provide a convergence rate result in terms of sparse neural networks and assume that the true density
g_{0}
is in a very general functional class, specifically the anisotropic Besov space
(
Nikol’skii 2012
;
Triebel 2006
;
Suzuki and Nitanda 2021
)
. Our result is built on
Suzuki and Nitanda 2021
, which provides the approximation error of the sparse neural network for functions in the anisotropic Besov space. In this section, we assume that the support of the density ratio is on
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]^{d}
.

3.2.1
A Brief Review on the Anisotropic Besov Space

We first introduce the anisotropic Besov space, following the same notations as in
Suzuki and Nitanda 2021
.

Definition 1

(Anisotropic Besov space
(B^{\beta}_{s,t}(\mathcal{X}))
)
.

For any function
f\in L^{s}(\mathcal{X})
where
s\in(0,\infty]
, the
r
-th modulus of smoothness of
f
is defined as

\displaystyle w_{r,s}(f,t)

\displaystyle=\sup_{h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|h_{i}|<t_{i}}\|\Delta_{h}^{r}(f)\|_{L^{s}(\mathcal{X})},

(9)

where
\Delta_{h}^{r}(f)(x):=\Delta_{h}^{r-1}(f)(x+h)-\Delta_{h}^{r-1}(f)(x)
,
\Delta_{h}^{0}(f)(x):=f(x).

For
s,t\in(0,\infty]
, the smoothness vector
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{++}
,
r=\max_{i}\lfloor\beta_{i}\rfloor+1
, define the seminorm
|\cdot|_{B_{s,t}^{\beta}}
as

\displaystyle|f|_{B^{\beta}_{s,t}}:=\begin{cases}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\big[2^{k}w_{r,s}\!\left(f,(2^{-k/\beta_{1}},\ldots,2^{-k/\beta_{d}})\right)\big]^{t}\right)^{1/t},&(t<\infty),\\
\sup_{k\geq 0}\,2^{k}w_{r,s}\!\left(f,(2^{-k/\beta_{1}},\ldots,2^{-k/\beta_{d}})\right),&(t=\infty).\end{cases}

(10)

The anisotropic Besov space norm is defined as
\|f\|_{B^{\beta}_{s,t}}:=\|f\|_{L^{s}(\mathcal{X})}+|f|_{B^{\beta}_{s,t}}
. The anisotropic Besov space is defined as

B^{\beta}_{s,t}(\mathcal{X})=\left\{f\in L^{s}(\mathcal{X}):\|f\|_{B^{\beta}_{s,t}}<\infty\right\}

(11)

To understand this, the anisotropic Besov space
B^{\beta}_{s,t}(\mathcal{X})
is controlled by
\beta
, the smoothness vector that allows for different smoothness
\beta_{i}
in each direction;
s
, indicating that the smoothness is measured in
L^{s}(\mathcal{X})
; and
t
, used in the seminorm (
10
). The seminorm (
10
) measures the localized anisotropic smoothness of
f
in terms of the scale of oscillation. Unlike Sobolev spaces that measure smoothness through derivatives, the modulus of smoothness in (
9
) measures the finite difference given an incremental change
h
and does not require differentiability. The seminorm (
10
) dyadically partitions the incremental change
h
in different directions,
|h_{i}|<2^{-k/\beta_{i}}
, and
2^{k}
is a normalization factor. Summing over
k
aggregates the smoothness across different partition layers.
Triebel 2011
and
Suzuki and Nitanda 2021
provide more detailed results on the relation between Hölder space and anisotropic Besov space. For example (Proposition 1 in
Suzuki and Nitanda 2021
), when
\beta=(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\beta_{0}\notin\mathbb{N}
, then Hölder space
\mathcal{C}^{\beta_{0}}=B^{\beta}_{\infty,\infty}
.

The main advantage of assuming the true density ratio
g_{0}
lies in
B^{\beta}_{s,t}(\mathcal{X})
is that
B^{\beta}_{s,t}(\mathcal{X})
allows for different smoothness in each direction. This coincides with our belief in two-sample comparisons in high dimensions, especially when comparing generative models: the two-sample difference may only lie in a few directions.

3.2.2
Feedforward Neural Network Formulation

Feedforward neural networks, including common architectures such as MLP and CNN, are usually formulated as a sequence of compositions in the following way. Let
U
be the maximum number of nodes in each layer;
L
is the total number of layers. Let
f^{(1)}(x)=\sigma\left(W^{(1)}x+b^{(1)}\right)
be the first layer of the neural network for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},W^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{U\times d},b^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{U}
, where
\sigma
is the element-wise activation function. We assume
\sigma
is an element-wise 1-Lipschitz function and
\sigma(0)=0
; for example, the ReLU activation
\sigma(x)=\max\left\{0,x\right\}
satisfies the element-wise 1-Lipschitz condition (
|f(x)-f(y)|\leq|x-y|
). Let
f^{(l)}(\cdot)=\sigma\left(W^{(l)}(\cdot)+b^{(l)}\right)
be the
l
-layer,
W^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{U\times U},b^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{U}
. For the last layer
f^{(L)}(\cdot)=\sigma\left(W^{(L)}(\cdot)+b^{(L)}\right)
,
W^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times U},b^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}
.

We slightly abuse the notation and let
\|W\|_{\infty}:=\max_{i,j}|W_{i,j}|
, which is different from the matrix infinity norm. Similarly,
\|b\|_{\infty}:=\max_{j}|b_{j}|
. We define the following neural network function space characterized by the number of layers
L
, the maximum number of nodes in each layer
U
, the sparsity
S
, and the norm constraint
B
,

\Phi(L,U,S,B):=\left\{f^{(L)}\circ\dots\circ f^{(1)}(x):\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left(\|W^{(l)}\|_{0}+\|b^{(l)}\|_{0}\right)\leq S,\max_{l}\left\{\|W^{(l)}\|_{\infty},\|b^{(l)}\|_{\infty}\right\}\leq B\right\}

(12)

This is a commonly used method of neural network parameterization in the literature
(
Suzuki and Nitanda 2021
;
Kaji et al. 2023
)
.

3.2.3
Convergence Rate for Sieved Neural Network

Denote
\mathcal{G}_{n}
as the sieve space of neural networks. The sieved M-estimator allows the search space
\mathcal{G}_{n}
to grow with sample size
n
, and is eventually flexible enough to approximate the true function (see details in Section 3.4 of
van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
). We define the sieve space according to the neural network parameter scales in Proposition 2 in
Suzuki and Nitanda 2021
. Let
\mathcal{G}_{n}=\Phi(L_{N},U_{N},S_{N},B_{N})
, where the neural network parameters depend on
N
, and
N=N(n)
depends on the sample size
n
. The dependence of
(L_{N},U_{N},S_{N},B_{N})
on
N
is specified in Proposition 2 in
Suzuki and Nitanda 2021
and in our proof (
25
). The rate
N(n)
will be specified later in the proof.

Now we can assume that the true density ratio
g_{0}\in B^{\beta}_{s,t}(\mathcal{X})
. Denote
\tilde{\beta}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}1/\beta_{j}\right)^{-1}
as the harmonic mean of the smoothness vector
\beta
. Let
\mathcal{U}\left(B^{\beta}_{s,t}(\mathcal{X})\right)
be the unit ball of
B^{\beta}_{s,t}(\mathcal{X})
.

Theorem 4
.

Under Assumption
1
, assume the functions in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
and
g_{0}
satisfy the upper and lower bounds in Assumption
5
and
\|q_{0}^{2}/p_{0}\|_{\infty}<\infty
. If
g_{0}\in\mathcal{U}\left(B^{\beta}_{s,t}(\mathcal{X})\right)
where
\tilde{\beta}>(1/s-1/2)_{+}
, then the convergence rate for the sparse neural network density ratio estimator is

\displaystyle h_{Q}(\hat{g}_{n},g_{0})=O_{p}(n^{-\frac{\tilde{\beta}}{2\tilde{\beta}+1}}\left(\log n\right)^{\frac{3\tilde{\beta}}{2\tilde{\beta}+1}}).

(13)

Remark.
The boundedness Assumption
5
can be enforced for neural network estimators by setting a bounded image of the activation function in the last layer. The unit ball assumption of
g_{0}
is reasonable because we already assume the boundedness of
g_{0}
in Assumption
5
.

Previously, in the proof of Theorem
3
, consistency is required; the true function
g_{0}
must be in the same function space
\mathcal{G}
as the estimator; and the entropy condition is prespecified in Assumptions
4
and
6
. Here in Theorem
4
, consistency is implied but not required, and we allow
g_{0}
to be outside of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, although the distance between
\mathcal{G}_{n}
and
\mathcal{U}\left(B^{\beta}_{s,t}(\mathcal{X})\right)
goes to 0 as
N\to\infty
(see Proposition 2 of
Suzuki and Nitanda 2021
); the entropy condition of the sparse neural network space
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is shown in Lemma
4
rather than being prespecified. Similar to Corollary
1
, since the proof of Theorem
4
does not require the independence of
P_{n}
and
Q_{m}
, we can directly apply this result to the general relative density ratio
r(x)
by replacing
Q
with
\widetilde{Q}=\alpha P+(1-\alpha)Q
, and similarly for
\widetilde{Q}_{m}
.

Corollary 2
.

Under the same assumptions in Theorem
4
but with
Q,Q_{m}
replaced by
\widetilde{Q}
and
\widetilde{Q}_{m}
, we have
h_{\widetilde{Q}}(\hat{r}_{n},r_{0})=O_{p}(n^{-\frac{\tilde{\beta}}{2\tilde{\beta}+1}}\left(\log n\right)^{\frac{3\tilde{\beta}}{2\tilde{\beta}+1}})
.

Remark.
The convergence rate in (
13
) depends only on the smoothness parameter
\tilde{\beta}
and does not depend on the dimension
d
of the sample space. This is because we allow the true function
g_{0}
to have different smoothness in different directions, and the harmonic mean
\tilde{\beta}
is only driven by a few least smooth directions, rather than assuming a spatially homogeneous smoothness bound in all directions (Hölder space), where the dimension
d
will come into play, as in previous works
(
Nakada and Imaizumi 2020
)
.

4
Numerical Experiments

We use three numerical experiments to illustrate the rich information that can be extracted from the relative density ratio
r(x)
: the MNIST dataset
(
LeCun et al. 1998
)
, the CelebA-64 dataset
(
Liu et al. 2015
)
, and the microbiome data from the American Gut Project
(
McDonald et al. 2018b
)
. Different from
Simon et al. 2019
, where the evaluation is conducted on the extracted inception features rather than the raw data, we perform all of our numerical experiments on the raw data to preserve the authentic distributional discrepancies.

4.1
MNIST

The MNIST dataset
(
LeCun et al. 1998
)
contains
60{,}000
training and
10{,}000
testing grayscale images, and each image, of dimensions
28\times 28
, is preprocessed so that all the voxels are mapped to
[-1,1]
. We test on two pretrained generators with saved checkpoints provided in the GitHub repository
(
Sharma 2019
)
: VAE
(
Kingma and Welling 2014
)
and DCGAN
(
Radford et al. 2015
)
. To train the ratio function, we use batch updates and stochastic gradient descent to iterate through the training set; the architecture for the ratio function is the same as the discriminator in DCGAN, a CNN-based architecture, except for the output layer. To ensure the theoretical bound
[0,2]
for
r(x)
, we apply a Bounded Softplus output layer: map the Softplus (
\log(1+\exp(x))
) output of
[0,\infty)
to
[0,2]
through
\frac{2x}{x+1}
. The ratio function is estimated using the training data, and we evaluate the ratio function on the
10{,}000
real testing data and another
10{,}000
generated samples from each generator.

Figure
3
provides visualizations on
\{r(X_{p}),r(X_{q})\}
for VAE, and Figure
4
for DCGAN. Immediately from the scalar metric,
h^{2}(p,\frac{p+q}{2})
is 0.286 for VAE, and 0.162 for DCGAN, we know that DCGAN is closer to the real sample than VAE. Note that this distance
h^{2}(p,\frac{p+q}{2})
directly results from the negative training loss and does not require separate computation since the variational form is the dual problem of the
\phi
-divergence. The sample-level RDR allows us to dive much deeper than simply picking the best fit.

stratified by digits label in the test data. C: Top row: Real test images; Bottom row: Fake generated images; From left to right: images with
r(X)
values from the smallest, closest to 1, and the largest; the range of
r(X)
for the shown images is in each subplot title.

Panel A in Figures
3
and
4
shows the histogram of
\{r(X_{p}),r(X_{q})\}
. The majority of the VAE
r(X_{q})
mass concentrates near 0 and has almost no overlap with
r(X_{p})
, indicating low coverage and low fidelity; whereas for DCGAN,
the empirical support of
r(X_{q})
is quite similar to that of

,
indicating good coverage. But for DCGAN, there is still a noticeable portion of

centered around 0, even though this portion is still in or near the mass of certain

5
Discussion

In this work, we propose a distributional evaluation framework on generative models based on the relative density ratio. This evaluation method not only provides numerical comparisons but also reveals the distributional discrepancy between the generator and the true data-generating mechanism, with clear interpretations that enable downstream covariate analyses. The relative density ratio can be extended for
K
-sample comparison: given
P_{1},\dots,P_{K}
, the proposed RDR in (
1
) can be modified to
r_{k}(x):=\frac{p_{k}(x)}{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}(x)}
. Our theoretical results reveal that the (relative) density ratio mapping preserves
\phi
-divergence of the original distributions. For neural network estimators, when the true ratio function is in the anisotropic Besov space where the smoothness in different directions is allowed to vary, the convergence rate of the ratio function only depends on the harmonic mean of the true function’s smoothness and is free of the dimension, which provides a theoretical foundation that explains the good performance of neural-network-based ratio learner in high-dimensional spaces. We have conducted extensive numerical experiments on a wide range of modern popular generators in various frameworks, including VAE, GAN, diffusion model (DDIM), and continuous flow matching (ICFM). Through benchmark image datasets like MNIST and CelebA-64, and microbiome relative abundance data in the American Gut Project, we discover that even for generators that are able to provide realistic synthetic samples, on a distributional level, they all have various biases from the true data-generating mechanism. This phenomenon has been observed in the machine learning community, where synthetic data are used in retraining the generators to lower the cost of authentic data acquisition. However, due to the distributional shift of the synthetic data, these retrained generators tend to degrade to a local region, a phenomenon known as
model collapse
. We anticipate that by offering a principled approach to evaluating distributional discrepancies between synthetic and authentic data, this work will provide valuable guidance for correcting generative model behavior and facilitating model retraining and other applications involving synthetic data.

\displaystyle\overset{t=\frac{\epsilon}{2\sqrt{M_{l}}}}{=}2\sqrt{M_{l}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\delta}{2\sqrt{M_{l}}}}\sqrt{1+\log N_{[]}\left(t,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(Q)\right)}\text{d}t

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{M_{l}}\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(\frac{\delta}{2\sqrt{M_{l}}},\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(Q)\right),

hence

\mathbb{E}\|\mathbb{G}_{n}^{Q}\|_{\mathcal{F}_{\delta,n}^{Q}}\leq 2\sqrt{M_{l}}\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(\delta/(2\sqrt{M_{l}}),\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(Q)\right)\left(1+\frac{2\sqrt{M_{l}}\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(\delta/(2\sqrt{M_{l}}),\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(Q)\right)}{\delta^{2}\sqrt{n}}M\right).

(23)

For the upper bound of
\mathbb{E}\|\mathbb{G}_{n}^{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}_{\delta,n}^{P}}
, we can similarly apply Lemma 3.4.5 in
van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
. To verify that
P\left\{\left(g^{-1/2}-g_{n}^{-1/2}\right)^{2}\right\}\lesssim\delta^{2}
given that in
\mathcal{F}_{\delta,n}^{P}
,
h_{Q}(g_{n},g)<\delta
, we refer to the previous proof in (
21
) and replace
g
by
g_{n}
in (
21
). Similarly,
\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{g^{L}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{g^{U}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(P)}=\left\|\frac{g^{L}-g^{U}}{\sqrt{g^{U}g^{L}}\left(\sqrt{g^{U}}+\sqrt{g^{L}}\right)}\right\|_{L^{2}(P)}\leq\frac{1}{2M_{l}^{3/2}}\left\|g^{L}-g^{U}\right\|_{L^{2}(P)}\leq\frac{\delta}{2M_{l}^{3/2}}
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\mathbb{G}_{n}^{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}_{\delta,n}^{P}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2M_{l}^{3/2}}\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(2M_{l}^{3/2}\delta,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)\left(1+\frac{\frac{1}{2M_{l}^{3/2}}\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(2M_{l}^{3/2}\delta,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)}{\delta^{2}\sqrt{n}}M\right).

(24)

For simplicity, we unify the upper bounds in (
23
) and (
24
). For the upper bound in (
23
), note
\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(P)}\geq\frac{1}{M_{l}}\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q)}
,
N_{[]}\left(\epsilon,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(Q)\right)\leq N_{[]}\left(\epsilon/\sqrt{M_{l}},\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)
, and

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(\delta/(2\sqrt{M_{l}}),\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(Q)\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\delta/(2\sqrt{M_{l}})}\sqrt{1+N_{[]}\left(\epsilon,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(Q)\right)}\text{d}\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\delta/(2\sqrt{M_{l}})}\sqrt{1+N_{[]}\left(\epsilon/\sqrt{M_{l}},\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)}\text{d}\epsilon=\sqrt{M_{l}}\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(\frac{\delta}{2M_{l}},\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right).

Recall that in Assumption
5
,
\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}|g^{-1}|\leq M_{l}
, WLOG, let
2M_{l}>1
. Hence

\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(\delta/(2\sqrt{M_{l}}),\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(Q)\right)\lesssim\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(\delta,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right).

For the upper bound in (
24
), WLOG, let
2M_{l}^{3/2}>1
,
N_{[]}\left(2M_{l}^{3/2}\epsilon,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)\leq N_{[]}\left(\epsilon,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)
. Hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2M_{l}^{3/2}}\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(2M_{l}^{3/2}\delta,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2M_{l}^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{2M_{l}^{3/2}\delta}\sqrt{1+N_{[]}\left(\epsilon,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)}\text{d}\epsilon

\displaystyle\overset{t=\frac{\epsilon}{2M_{l}^{3/2}}}{=}\int_{0}^{\delta}\sqrt{1+N_{[]}\left(2M_{l}^{3/2}t,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)}\text{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(\delta,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right).

Now we have shown that both the RHS of (
23
) and (
24
) are upper bounded by

\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(\delta,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)\left(1+\frac{\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(\delta,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)}{\delta^{2}\sqrt{n}}M\right)

up to a constant that only depends on
M_{u},M_{l}
.
Let
\phi_{n}(\delta)=\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(\delta,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)\left(1+\frac{\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(\delta,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)}{\delta^{2}\sqrt{n}}M\right)
. To solve for
r_{n}
based on Conditions 2 and 3, we need to check that
\phi_{n}(\delta)/\delta^{\alpha}
is decreasing on
(\delta_{n},\infty)
for some
0<\alpha<2
.

Note that
\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(\delta,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)=\int_{0}^{\delta}\sqrt{1+\log N_{[]}\left(\epsilon,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)}\text{d}\epsilon
, and the integrand

f(\epsilon)=\sqrt{1+\log N_{[]}\left(\epsilon,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)}

is a decreasing function of
\epsilon
, the radius of the bracketing number. Let
h(\delta)=\frac{\int_{0}^{\delta}f(\epsilon)\text{d}\epsilon}{\delta^{\alpha}}
, and let
\alpha=1

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(\delta)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(\delta)\delta-\int_{0}^{\delta}f(\epsilon)\text{d}\epsilon}{\delta^{2}}=\frac{\int_{0}^{\delta}(f(\delta)-f(\epsilon))\text{d}\epsilon}{\delta^{2}}\leq 0.

Hence
h(\delta)
is a decreasing function
\delta
, and
\phi_{n}(\delta)/\delta=h(\delta)\left(1+\frac{h(\delta)}{\delta\sqrt{n}}M\right)
is also a decreasing function of
\delta
.

Now we’ve found the
\phi_{n}(\delta)
that satisfies Condition 2, and it depends on the neural network parameter bounds, which will be determined later when solving for
r_{n}
in Condition 3.
In Condition 3, the inequality
r_{n}^{2}\phi(1/r_{n})\lesssim\sqrt{n}
is equivalent to
r_{n}^{2}\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(1/r_{n},\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)\lesssim\sqrt{n}
(To see this, if we denote
x_{n}=r_{n}^{2}\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(1/r_{n},\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)
, then
r_{n}^{2}\phi(1/r_{n})=x_{n}(1+\frac{x_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}M)
, and note that
x_{n}+\frac{x_{n}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}M\leq 2\sqrt{n}\Leftrightarrow C_{M}x_{n}\leq\sqrt{n}
by solving the quadratic roots, where
C_{M}=\frac{\sqrt{1+8M}-1}{2M}
when
x_{n}>0
).

Note that
\tilde{J}_{[]}\left(\delta,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)=\int_{0}^{\delta}\sqrt{1+\log N_{[]}\left(\epsilon,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)}\text{d}\epsilon
. We can further simplify the result in Lemma
4
and let
A_{n}=S_{n}\log\left(B_{n}^{L_{n}}U_{n}^{L_{n}}L_{n}(2U_{n}L_{n}+d)e\|H\|_{L^{2}(P)}\right)-S_{n}\log\left(2S_{n}\right)+1
, then the above integrand can be upper bounded by

\sqrt{1+\log N_{[]}\left(\epsilon,\mathcal{G}_{n},L^{2}(P)\right)}\leq\sqrt{A_{n}-S_{n}\log\epsilon}.

We can further bound the integral
\int_{0}^{\delta}\sqrt{A_{n}-S_{n}\log\epsilon}\text{d}\epsilon
by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\int_{0}^{\delta}\sqrt{A_{n}-S_{n}\log\epsilon}\text{d}\epsilon\leq\sqrt{\delta}\sqrt{\int_{0}^{\delta}A_{n}-S_{n}\log\epsilon}\text{d}\epsilon=\delta\sqrt{A_{n}-S_{n}\log\delta+S_{n}}.

Condition 4 is true because both
\hat{g}_{n}
and
g_{n}
belong to
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, and
\hat{g}_{n}
is the maximizer of
\mathbb{M}_{n}(g)
over
\mathcal{G}_{n}
.

Note that
\delta_{n}\asymp h_{Q}(g_{n},g_{0})
, and
h_{Q}(g_{n},g_{0})
depends on the distance between the sieved neural network space where
g_{n}
belongs to and the anisotropic Besov space where
g_{0}
belongs to. To get
h_{Q}(g_{n},g_{0})
, we use Proposition 2 in
Suzuki and Nitanda 2021
, where the neural network parameters used in Lemma
4
are specified in the following way,

\displaystyle L_{N}

\displaystyle:=3+2\Big\lceil\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{3^{d\vee m}}{\epsilon c_{(d,m)}}\right)+5\Big\rceil\Big\lceil\log_{2}(d\vee m)\Big\rceil,\quad U_{N}:=NU_{0},

\displaystyle S_{N}

\displaystyle:=\big[(L_{N}-1)U_{0}^{2}+1\big]N,\quad B_{N}:=O\!\left(N^{d(1+\nu^{-1})(1/s-\tilde{\beta})_{+}}\right),

(25)

where
\epsilon=N^{-\tilde{\beta}}\log(N)^{-1}
,
m
is a positive integer such that
0<\bar{\beta}<\min(m,m-1+1/s)
,
\delta=(1/s-1/r)_{+}
(we use the
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
norm in (
26
) and let
r=2
),
\nu=(\tilde{\beta}-\delta)/(2\delta)
, and
U_{0}:=6dm(m+2)+2d
.
c_{(d.m)}
is a constant that only depends on
d
and
m
. The parameter
N
controls the total number of neural network parameters, and we will allow
N
to grow with the sample size
N=N(n)
as
n\to\infty
. Hence we abbreviate the notations
\left\{L_{N},U_{N},S_{N},B_{N}\right\}
to
\left\{L_{n},U_{n},S_{n},B_{n}\right\}
in the following discussion. Proposition 2 in
Suzuki and Nitanda 2021
states that, under (
25
),

\displaystyle\sup_{g_{0}\in\mathcal{U}\left(B^{\beta}_{s,t}(\mathcal{X})\right)}\inf_{g_{n}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}\|g_{n}-g_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}\lesssim N^{-\tilde{\beta}}.

(26)

Recall Assumption
5
,
\|g_{0}^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq M_{l}
. With the assumption
\|q_{0}^{2}/p_{0}\|_{\infty}<\infty
, we can derive the following inequality between distances:

\displaystyle h^{2}_{Q}(g_{n},g_{0})

\displaystyle=\int\left(g_{n}^{1/2}-g_{0}^{1/2}\right)^{2}\text{d}Q=\int\left(\frac{g_{n}-g_{0}}{\sqrt{g_{n}}+\sqrt{g_{0}}}\right)^{2}\text{d}Q\leq\int\left(g_{n}-g_{0}\right)^{2}\frac{q_{0}}{g_{0}}\text{d}\lambda(x)

\displaystyle\leq\|q_{0}^{2}/p_{0}\|_{\infty}\|g_{n}-g_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}\lesssim\|g_{n}-g_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}\overset{(*)}{\lesssim}N^{-2\tilde{\beta}}.

Note that
(*)
is based on (
26
) and
g_{n}
is taken to be the project of
g_{0}
in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
.

Hence
\delta_{n}\lesssim N^{-\tilde{\beta}}
. Now we need to solve for an upper bound for
r_{n}
such that

(i)

r_{n}\sqrt{A_{n}+S_{n}+S_{n}\log(r_{n})}\lesssim\sqrt{n}
;

(ii)

\frac{1}{r_{n}}\gtrsim N^{-\tilde{\beta}}
.

We first choose
N\asymp r_{n}^{1/\tilde{\beta}}
based on (ii). The inequality (i) depends on the neural network parameters. Recall (
25
), we can compute the order of neural network configuration parameters w.r.t.
N
first:
L_{n}=O(\log(N)),U_{n}=O(N),S_{n}=O(N\log(N)),B_{n}=O(N^{\eta})
, where
\eta
is a constant that depends on the smoothness
\tilde{\beta}
and the input dimension
d
. Here, we consider
d
as a constant that does not grow with
n
.

We can simplify the first inequality (i) in the order of
N
as follows

\displaystyle A_{n}+S_{n}

\displaystyle=S_{n}\log\left(B_{n}^{L_{n}}U_{n}^{L_{n}}L_{n}(2U_{n}L_{n}+d)e\|H\|_{L^{2}(P)}\right)-S_{n}\log\left(2S_{n}\right)+S_{n}+1

\displaystyle=S_{n}\left\{L_{n}\log(B_{n})+L_{n}\log(U_{n})+\log(2U_{n}L_{n}+d)+\log(L_{n})+O(1)\right\}-S_{n}\log(2S_{n})+S_{n}+1

\displaystyle=O(N\left(\log N\right)^{3}).

Because we set
N\asymp r_{n}^{1/\tilde{\beta}}
,
r_{n}\sqrt{A_{n}+S_{n}+S_{n}\log(r_{n})}\asymp r_{n}^{1+\frac{1}{2\tilde{\beta}}}\left(\log(r_{n})\right)^{3/2}
. Now we only need to solve for an upper bound of
r_{n}
from

r_{n}^{1+\frac{1}{2\tilde{\beta}}}\left(\log(r_{n})\right)^{3/2}\lesssim\sqrt{n}.

(27)

To solve for this inequality, we can guess that the upper bound of
r_{n}
takes the form
r_{n}=\frac{n^{c_{1}}}{(\log n)^{c_{2}}}
, because the RHS of (
27
) is of polynomial rate of
n
, whereas the LHS of (
27
) is a polynomial of
r_{n}
times a log rate of
r_{n}
. Then we solve for
c_{1},c_{2}
by first matching the polynomial rate,
c_{1}(1+\frac{1}{2\tilde{\beta}})=1/2
,
c_{1}=\frac{\tilde{\beta}}{2\tilde{\beta}+1}
. The log term in the LHS is
(\log(r_{n}))^{3/2}=O\left(\left(\frac{\tilde{\beta}}{2\tilde{\beta}+1}\log n-c_{2}\log\log n\right)^{3/2}\right)=O\left(\left(\log n\right)^{3/2}\right)
. Now the rate on the LHS is

\displaystyle r_{n}^{1+\frac{1}{2\tilde{\tilde{\beta}}}}\left(\log(r_{n})\right)^{3/2}

\displaystyle=O\left(n^{1/2}(\log n)^{-c_{2}(1+\frac{1}{2\tilde{\beta}})}(\log n)^{3/2}\right).

Hence by setting
c_{2}(1+\frac{1}{2\tilde{\beta}})=3/2
, we can solve for
c_{2}=\frac{3\tilde{\beta}}{2\tilde{\beta}+1}
. Hence the upper bound for
r_{n}
is
O(n^{\frac{\tilde{\beta}}{2\tilde{\beta}+1}}\left(\log n\right)^{-\frac{3\tilde{\beta}}{2\tilde{\beta}+1}})
.

Now the proof for the estimation error is complete,

h_{Q}(\hat{g}_{n},g_{n})=O_{p}(1/r_{n})=O_{p}(n^{-\frac{\tilde{\beta}}{2\tilde{\beta}+1}}\left(\log n\right)^{\frac{3\tilde{\beta}}{2\tilde{\beta}+1}}).

The approximation error
h_{Q}(g_{n},g_{0})\leq O(N^{-\tilde{\beta}})
follows the same rate by design
r_{n}=O(N^{\tilde{\beta}})
. Hence the convergence rate is
O_{p}(n^{-\frac{\tilde{\beta}}{2\tilde{\beta}+1}}\left(\log n\right)^{\frac{3\tilde{\beta}}{2\tilde{\beta}+1}})
.

∎
