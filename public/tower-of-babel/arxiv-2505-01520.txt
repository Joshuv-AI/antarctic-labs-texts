Content and Quality Analysis of Parent-Facing Applications for Feeding Children with Autism Spectrum Disorder
1 st Christopher Cofie Kuzagbe, 2 nd Fabrice Mukarage, 3 rd Skye Nandi Adams, 4 th N’guessan Yves-Roland Douha, 5 th Edith Talina Luhanga

Abstract
Approximately 1 in 100 children worldwide are diagnosed with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), and 46% to 89% experience significant feeding difficulties. Although mobile health (mHealth) applications offer potential support for caregivers, the quality and relevance of apps targeting autism-related feeding issues remain unclear. This systematic review evaluated mobile applications available on the Apple App Store and the Google Play Store between September and October 2024. The searches were carried out using 15 predefined terms (e.g., “child autism feeding,” “child autism food”). Applications were eligible if they were in English, free to download, updated within the past year, explicitly addressed feeding in children with autism, accessible in Africa, and had more than 100 downloads. Of the 326 apps identified, only two iOS applications met all inclusion criteria; no Android apps qualified. Behavior Change Wheel (BCW) analysis showed that the selected applications incorporated multiple intervention functions, such as education, training, enablement, incentivization, and modeling, though none addressed the full spectrum of behavioral strategies. Mobile App Rating Scale (MARS) indicated moderate to high usability, with features such as sensory-friendly food routines and structured caregiver tools. However, both apps lacked clinical validation and comprehensive customization. These findings highlight a critical gap in the availability of evidence-based high-quality mHealth tools for caregivers managing ASD-related feeding challenges and underscore the need for professionally developed and culturally sensitive digital solutions.



I
Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition characterized by challenges in social interaction, communication, behavior regulation, and sensory processing. The global prevalence of ASD has increased significantly in recent decades; the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reports a 178% increase in autism prevalence since 2000
[
1
]
, and the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that approximately 1 in 100 children is diagnosed with ASD
[
2
]
.

Among the various comorbidities observed in children with ASD, feeding difficulties are especially common and under-addressed. Studies report prevalence rates ranging from 46% to 89%
[
3
]
, with affected children exhibiting food selectivity, aversions to textures and flavors, rapid eating, and disruptive mealtime behaviors
[
4
,
5
]
. These challenges are linked to nutritional deficiencies, impaired growth, gastrointestinal disorders, and metabolic imbalances
[
6
,
7
]
. Children with ASD are estimated to be five times more likely to experience feeding problems than their neurotypical peers
[
8
]
. This situation places a significant burden on caregivers and parents, who often serve as the primary facilitators of feeding interventions and manage complex routines while navigating behavioral and communication challenges.

Parent-mediated interventions have become increasingly central in ASD care, with research highlighting their effectiveness in improving child outcomes and enhancing parent-child interactions
[
9
,
10
]
. The emergence of mobile health (mHealth) applications presents new opportunities for delivering such interventions, particularly in resource-constrained or remote settings. These mHealth applications (hereafter referred to as “applications” or “apps” for readability) offer potentially accessible and cost-effective support for parents of children with ASD through psychoeducation, skills training, and behavior management guidance
[
11
,
12
]
.

Several studies have reviewed mobile apps that address autism more broadly
[
13
]
, including those focused on early intervention
[
11
]
, communication support
[
14
]
, and parental empowerment
[
15
]
. However, research on mobile apps related to feeding for children with ASD remains limited. A few digital interventions have targeted the sensory or behavioral dimensions of mealtime challenges, but their quality and effectiveness are often inconsistent due to a lack of regulation and clinical validation
[
16
]
. Furthermore, digital interventions that fail to align with parents’ expectations, parenting styles, or cultural context may increase the stress experienced by parents of children with ASD
[
17
,
18
]
.

Given the high prevalence of feeding difficulties among children with ASD and the growing use of digital tools by caregivers, evaluating the quality of these resources is essential. The Mobile App Rating Scale (MARS)
[
19
,
16
]
has proven to be a reliable and multidimensional instrument to assess the quality of mHealth apps. In this study, we analyzed mobile apps available in the Apple App Store and Google Play Store that address feeding challenges in children with ASD. Using the MARS framework, we assessed the quality, functionality, engagement, and information content of mobile apps to identify evidence-based practices and highlight areas of unmet need.

This study pursued four main objectives: (1) to conduct the first systematic review of mobile applications specifically designed to address feeding challenges in children with ASD, thereby filling a critical gap in the literature; (2) to assess the quality of these applications using the MARS framework, with a focus on engagement, aesthetics, functionality, and informational content; (3) to identify the strengths and limitations of existing mobile solutions in supporting feeding interventions; and (4) to provide actionable recommendations for the development of future mHealth tools that more effectively assist caregivers in managing ASD-related feeding difficulties.

II
Methods

II-A

Search Strategy

We conducted a systematic search of the Apple App Store (iOS) and Google Play Store (Android) to identify mobile health applications related to feeding, food, meal planning, or nutrition for children with autism. The search was performed between September and October 2024. To ensure comprehensive coverage, we used the following search terms: “Child autism food,” “Kids autism food,” “Toddler autism food,” “Child autism nutrition,” “Kids autism nutrition,” “Toddler autism nutrition,” “Child autism meal prep planning,” “Kids autism meal prep planning,” “Toddler autism meal prep planning,” “Child autism feeding,” “Kids autism feeding,” “Toddler autism feeding,” “Child autism mealtime,” “Kids autism mealtime,” and “Toddler autism mealtime.” These terms were entered directly into the App Store and Google Play search interfaces. To reflect regional availability, the devices’ region settings were configured to Africa during the search. The searches were conducted using an iPhone 12 Pro Max (iOS) and a Google Pixel 3a XL (Android) to simulate a typical end-user experience on each platform.

II-B

Study Selection

Two reviewers (CCK and FM) jointly identified and removed duplicate entries from the search results. They then independently screened the information pages of mobile applications, focusing on app descriptions and user interfaces to assess eligibility. Applications were included if they met all of the following criteria: 1) the content was in English (both interface and core functionality); 2) the app was free to download (either fully free or offering in-app purchases); 3) the app had been updated within the past year (to exclude defunct or outdated apps); 4) the title or description explicitly addressed feeding in children with autism; 5) the app was available in the designated region (Africa); and 6) the app had more than 100 downloads (used as a proxy to identify apps attracting user attention).

Applications were excluded if they could not be installed on the test devices (iPhone 12 Pro Max for iOS and Google Pixel 3a XL for Android). The list of included and excluded applications was reviewed during weekly meetings with two additional reviewers (NYRD and ETL). Discrepancies were discussed and resolved by consensus.

II-C

Content Analysis

Applications that met the inclusion criteria were downloaded and independently analyzed by two reviewers (CCK and FM). CCK analyzed applications from the Apple App Store, while FM reviewed those from the Google Play Store. Upon completing their initial evaluations, the reviewers exchanged platforms to cross-validate the assessments of each other. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved through dialogue, and when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (ETL) was consulted. This process was implemented to improve the reliability and consistency of content analysis.

For each application, the reviewers systematically navigated all freely accessible app screens and documented both the informational content and interactive features. The extracted features and content elements were organized into a tabular matrix to facilitate the comparison between applications and support the inductive coding process. Inductive coding was then used to identify common themes across applications. In addition, two reviewers (FM and NYRD) examined application features using the Behavior Change Wheel (BCW) framework
[
20
]
. The BCW defines nine intervention functions: education, persuasion, incentivization, coercion, training, restriction, environmental restructuring, modeling, and enablement. These functions serve as core behavior change mechanisms in the development and evaluation of health interventions.

II-D

Quality Assessment

Application quality was assessed using the Mobile App Rating Scale (MARS)
[
19
]
, a validated tool for evaluating mobile health applications. MARS evaluates app quality across four dimensions: engagement (user interest and interaction), functionality (performance and usability), aesthetics (visual design), and information quality (accuracy, relevance, and credibility of content). Each dimension is rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Two reviewers (CCK and FM) independently rated each application after completing the official MARS training via the YouTube-based module to ensure consistent interpretation of the scale. Prior to the full evaluation, the reviewers established inter-rater reliability by co-rating all applications and comparing scores. Minor differences in ratings (e.g., 3.9 vs. 4.0) were considered acceptable, and the mean score across reviewers was used as the final rating. In cases of larger discrepancies (differences greater than 1 point), the reviewers discussed their evaluations and reached a consensus score.

III-D

Intervention Function Mapping Using the BCW Framework

Building on the Behavior Change Wheel (BCW) framework introduced in the Methods section, we examined how the two reviewed applications align with the BCW intervention functions. Table
II
summarizes the intervention functions implemented in each application.

EduKitchen – Toddlers Food Games
aligns with five functions: education, training, enablement, incentivization, and modeling. The application delivers education by introducing children to food categories and healthy eating through interactive lessons. Training is implemented via matching and sorting activities that reinforce foundational feeding behaviors. Enablement is supported by caregiver-controlled customization options, which allow adaptation of gameplay to the child’s learning level and sensory preferences. Incentivization is embedded in gamified elements such as animated rewards and praise. Modeling is reflected through in-app characters that demonstrate socially appropriate feeding behaviors, providing children with repeated visual examples.

Autism Food Coach 2
maps onto six functions: education, training, enablement, persuasion, incentivization, and environmental restructuring. The application provides education through guidance on the importance of slow eating and balanced meals. Training is facilitated via structured visual routines that guide caregivers in implementing consistent feeding strategies. Enablement is achieved through customizable meal plans and visual progress dashboards that support adaptive decision-making. Persuasion is present through motivational prompts and testimonials encouraging continued use. Incentivization is implemented through visual feedback and goal-setting mechanisms that reward progress. Environmental restructuring is operationalized through features that support the establishment of predictable, low-stress mealtime routines, such as visual schedules and routine templates, which alter the child’s feeding context to promote calm and structured eating environments.

IV
Discussion

This systematic review examined the Apple App Store and Google Play Store to identify and evaluate mobile applications addressing feeding challenges in children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Out of over 300 screened applications, only two (
EduKitchen – Toddlers Food Games
and
Autism Food Coach 2
) met the inclusion criteria. The scarcity of qualifying applications reveals a substantial gap in available mobile solutions specifically designed to address feeding difficulties among children with ASD.

The two selected applications reflected divergent design orientations.
EduKitchen – Toddlers Food Games
targets children directly, using interactive, gamified educational content to foster food familiarity and positive engagement during mealtimes. In contrast,
Autism Food Coach 2
is designed for caregivers, providing structured tools to support feeding routines through progress tracking and planning features. This distinction highlights the diversity of approaches within the limited number of available applications.

Mapping these applications to the BCW framework revealed that
EduKitchen – Toddlers Food Games
aligned with five intervention functions (education, training, enablement, incentivization, and modeling), whereas
Autism Food Coach 2
aligned with six: education, training, enablement, persuasion, incentivization, and environmental restructuring. Our findings reflect the distinct target audiences and use cases of each application and highlight the utility of evaluating the intervention logic of mobile health interventions using frameworks such as the BCW
[
20
]
. The quality assessment conducted using the MARS framework
[
19
]
revealed that both applications demonstrated moderate to high quality.
EduKitchen – Toddlers Food Games
received a marginally higher overall score (3.9) than
Autism Food Coach 2
(3.7), with particular strengths in functionality and visual appeal. In contrast,
Autism Food Coach 2
excelled in caregiver-directed features and structured routine support. However, neither application offered scientific validation or extensive customization options, which highlights the need for better alignment with evidence-based practices and end-user requirements.

Several limitations should be noted. The use of inclusion criteria such as a minimum of 100 downloads may have excluded valuable but lesser-known applications. Moreover, the geographic location of reviewers’ devices may have influenced app visibility on the platforms. The search terms employed, while extensive, may have omitted alternative phrasing used by developers. Additionally, the premium content behind paywalls could not be evaluated, which may have led to the omission of key features. Finally, although guided by established frameworks, the content analysis and quality assessment involved interpretive judgment.

Future work should expand the scope of search parameters and investigate how algorithms and regional configurations in the Apple App Store and Google Play Store influence application discoverability. Additionally, there is a need to develop culturally adaptive and clinically validated applications, particularly for resource-constrained settings. Co-design approaches that involve caregivers and clinicians, combined with longitudinal studies that assess real-world impact, will be essential to improve the utility and effectiveness of mobile health interventions in autism care.

V
Conclusion

This study reveals a critical shortage of mobile health applications specifically designed to address feeding challenges in children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Using the Mobile App Rating Scale (MARS) framework, we systematically evaluated the two eligible applications, identifying both functional strengths and notable limitations. Although each application incorporated behavior change principles and demonstrated moderate to high usability, none of them was grounded in clinically validated feeding interventions. These findings underscore the need for evidence-based user-centered development that better supports caregiver decision making and addresses the complex realities of feeding difficulties. Expanding access to effective, validated, and culturally sensitive mobile health solutions remains essential for advancing digital health equity, particularly for one of the most pressing challenges in autism care: feeding difficulties in low-resource settings.
