Analysis of multivariate event times under informative censoring using vine copula
Xinyuan Chen Yiwei Li Qian M. Zhou

Abstract
The study of times to nonterminal events of different types and their interrelation is a compelling area of interest. The primary challenge in analyzing such multivariate event times is the presence of informative censoring by the terminal event. While numerous statistical methods have been proposed for a single nonterminal event, i.e., semi-competing risks data, there remains a dearth of tools for analyzing times to multiple nonterminal events. This article introduces a novel analysis framework that leverages the vine copula to directly estimate the joint density of multivariate times to nonterminal and terminal events. Unlike the few existing methods based on multivariate or nested copulas, the developed approach excels in capturing the heterogeneous dependence between each pair of event times (nonterminal-terminal and between-nonterminal) in terms of strength and structure. We propose a likelihood-based estimation and inference procedure, which can be implemented efficiently in sequential stages. Through extensive simulation studies, we demonstrate the satisfactory finite-sample performance of our proposed stage-wise estimators and analytical variance estimators, as well as their advantages over existing methods. We apply the developed approach to data from a crowdfunding platform to investigate the relationship between various types of creator-backer interactions and a creator’s lifetime on the platform.



Keywords:
Graphical models, informative censoring, multivariate event times, stage-wise estimation, vine copula

1
Introduction

Multivariate Event Times under Informative Censoring (METIC) data arise when a terminal event precludes multiple nonterminal events, where censoring by the terminal event is informative for the nonterminal event times due to their possible dependence. For instance, in a business study on crowdfunding
(
Dai and Zhang, 2019
;
Su
et al., 2023
)
, the comments from campaign backers and the updates from creators are nonterminal events of interest, which are subject to censoring when creators leave the platform. Similarly, in a breast cancer survivorship study
(
Davis et al., 2014
)
, survivors may experience nonterminal events such as relapses, second cancers, or cardiovascular diseases, which are subject to informative censoring due to death or the end of the follow-up period. The primary interests in analyzing the METIC data are often to evaluate pairwise associations between event times (nonterminal-terminal and between-nonterminal) and to estimate covariate effects on these associations, as well as individual event times, via regressions.

Existing literature primarily focuses on data involving a single nonterminal event, commonly known as semi-competing risks
(
Fine
et al., 2001
;
Peng and
Fine, 2007
;
Hsieh
et al., 2008
;
Chen, 2012
;
Zhu
et al., 2021
;
Arachchige
et al., 2025
)
. Two recent studies, however, have explored the analysis of the METIC data.
Li
et al. (2020)
employed a parametric multivariate copula to specify the joint survival function for all event times (nonterminal and terminal) without covariates.
Li et al. (2023)
assessed the covariate effects on the association between two nonterminal event times using nested copulas. This approach modeled the joint survival function at two levels: the first level specified the joint survival function of all nonterminal event times with a multivariate copula, while the second level linked this joint survival function to the marginal survival function of the terminal event time through a bivariate copula.

Although the methods described in
Li
et al. (2020)
and
Li et al. (2023)
provide viable analysis pathways, they rely on relatively strong structural assumptions and do not offer convenient approaches to conducting regressions on individual nonterminal event times. Specifically, the nested copula structure in
Li et al. (2023)
assumes that each nonterminal event time has identical associations (in terms of strength and functional form) with the terminal, and that each pairwise between-nonterminal dependence is homogeneous. The assumption in
Li
et al. (2020)
was stronger, that all pairwise dependencies are assumed to be homogeneous. However, the events in the METIC data are of different natures, suggesting that the pairwise dependencies are likely heterogeneous in both strength and structure, which often renders the highly symmetric structures in these methods impractical. In addition, the multivariate copula in
Li
et al. (2020)
and the bivariate copula in
Li et al. (2023)
are restricted to the Archimedean copula family (e.g., Clayton, Gumbel, Frank); the nested copula parameters in
Li et al. (2023)
are required to satisfy sufficient nesting conditions
(
Hofert and
Pham, 2013
)
for the joint survival function to be well-defined. Lastly, although
Li et al. (2023)
presented straightforward regression modeling for the marginal survival function of the terminal event time, it did not provide the same for the nonterminals. Instead, they specified the marginal survival functions of first-event times (defined as the minimum of each nonterminal event and the terminal event times) using a Cox proportional hazards (PH) model, which did not directly convey the covariate effects on individual nonterminal event times.

In this article, we present a more flexible analysis framework, specifically designed for the METIC data. The approach leverages vine copulas
(
Joe, 2014
)
to decompose the complex multivariate dependence among all event times into a catalog of pairwise dependencies organized through a sequence of trees (connected acyclic graphs). Each pairwise dependence is represented by a bivariate copula, which can be from a different family. This allows us to capture the heterogeneity in the association between each pair of event times, whether they are nonterminal-terminal or between-nonterminal. Additionally, if two bivariate copulas belong to the same family, their parameters can differ without restrictions. In the vine graph, the first tree assumes a star shape, with the root node representing the terminal event time and connecting all nonterminal event times (see Figure
1
). This configuration enables direct regression modeling for each nonterminal event time (i.e., other nodes of the first tree). Additionally, it ensures consistent estimation of these marginal survival function parameters by accounting for the dependence between the terminal and each nonterminal event time (i.e., the edges of the first tree). Moreover, we allow users to specify the structure of subsequent trees, further highlighting the flexibility of the proposed method.

Simultaneous estimation of all model parameters is computationally challenging. Therefore, we develop a computationally efficient stage-wise pseudo maximum likelihood estimation (PMLE) procedure to estimate the parameters associated with each tree sequentially. We establish the asymptotic properties of the resulting estimators and provide analytical variance estimators, the validity of which is demonstrated through simulation studies. It is worth noting that, at the first stage of estimation, the parameters of each nonterminal marginal are estimated directly from a likelihood (together with the corresponding copula parameters). Consequently, the resulting estimator is inherently more efficient than the estimators in
Li
et al. (2020)
and
Li et al. (2023)
, confirmed by the simulation studies.

The vine copula-based approach

2.1
The METIC data

Let
\bm{T}_{[J-1]}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{J-1})^{\prime}
denote the
J-1
(
J\geq 3
) nonterminal event times (
[J]=\{1,\ldots,J\}
for
J\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
), and
T_{J}
the terminal event time. Let
A
denote the time to the independent administrative censoring, and we can write the observed terminal event time as
X_{J}=T_{J}\wedge A
with the censoring status indicator
\Delta_{J}=\mathbbm{1}(T_{J}\leq A)
. The nonterminal event time
T_{j}
for
j\in[J-1]
is right-censored by
X_{J}
, leading to the observed nonterminal event times,
X_{j}=T_{j}\wedge X_{J}
, and corresponding censoring status indicators
\Delta_{j}=\mathbbm{1}(T_{j}\leq X_{J})
for
j\in[J-1]
. Let
\bm{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}}
denote a
d_{Z}
-dimensional vector of observed covariates. For a sample of
n
independent subjects, the observed METIC data is
\mathcal{O}=\{\mathcal{O}_{i}\equiv(X_{1,i},\Delta_{1,i},\ldots,X_{J,i},\Delta_{J,i},\bm{Z}_{i}),i\in[n]\}
. We assume that there exists a maximum follow-up time
\overline{t}<\infty
and a constant
\underline{p}>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{j}>\overline{t}|\bm{Z})>\underline{p}
for all
j\in[J]
, and every element of
\bm{Z}
is bounded with probability one
(
Zeng, 2004
;
Zeng and Lin, 2006
;
Li et al., 2023
)
.

2.2
The vine copula-based modeling for METIC

By Sklar’s theorem
(
Sklar, 1959
)
, a unique
J
-variate survival copula conditional on covariates,
\mathbbm{C}_{[J]}:[0,1]^{J}\mapsto[0,1]
, exists such that the joint survival function of
\bm{T}_{[J]}
,

S_{[J]}(\bm{t}_{[J]}|\bm{Z})=\mathbbm{C}_{[J]}\{S_{1}(t_{1}|\bm{Z}),\ldots,S_{J-1}(t_{J-1}|\bm{Z}),S_{J}(t_{J}|\bm{Z})\}

(1)

where
\{S_{j}(t_{j}|\bm{Z}),j\in[J]\}
are respective marginals of
\{T_{j},j\in[J]\}
. Thus, the joint density of
\bm{T}_{[J]}
conditional on covariates, denoted by
f_{[J]}(\bm{t}_{[J]}|\bm{Z})
, can be expressed as

f_{[J]}(\bm{t}_{[J]}|\bm{Z})=\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{J}f_{j}(t_{j}|\bm{Z})\right\}\mathbbm{c}_{[J]}(\bm{u}_{[J],\bm{Z}}).

(2)

where
f_{j}(t_{j}|\bm{Z})=-\dot{S}_{j}(t_{j}|\bm{Z})
is the marginal density of
T_{j}
for
j\in[J]
,
u_{j,\bm{Z}}=S_{j}(t_{j}|\bm{Z})
, and
\mathbbm{c}_{[J]}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{J})=\partial^{J}\mathbbm{C}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{J})/\partial u_{1}\cdots\partial u_{J}
is the joint copula density function. Note that
\mathbbm{c}_{[J]}
can be regarded as the joint density function for
\bm{U}_{[J],\bm{Z}}=(U_{1,\bm{Z}},\ldots,U_{J,\bm{Z}})^{\prime}
with
U_{j,\bm{Z}}=S_{j}(T_{j}|\bm{Z})\in[0,1]
. The vine copula decomposes
\mathbbm{c}_{[J]}
into a product of bivariate copula densities, the structure of which can be illustrated using a graph of
J-1
ordered trees,
\mathscr{V}_{[J]}=(\mathscr{T}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{T}_{J-1})
, where the
k
-th tree
\mathscr{T}_{k}=(\mathscr{N}_{k},\mathscr{E}_{k})
consists a set of nodes
\mathscr{N}_{k}
and a set of edges
\mathscr{E}_{k}
,
k\in[J-1]
. Each edge is associated with a bivariate copula. The vine graph satisfies the following conditions
(
Bedford and
Cooke, 2002
)
: (i)
\mathscr{N}_{1}=[J]
; (ii)
\mathscr{N}_{k}=\mathscr{E}_{k-1}
,
k=2,\ldots,J-1
; and (iii) the proximity condition: for any edge
e_{k}\in\mathscr{E}_{k}
,
k=2,\ldots,J-1
, its nodes, i.e., two edges in
\mathscr{E}_{k-1}
, share a common node. We denote edge
e_{k}
from tree
\mathscr{T}_{k}
by
(j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}})
, where
\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}}
is the conditioning set of this edge
(
Czado and
Nagler, 2022
)
; for
\mathscr{T}_{1}
,
(j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{1}})=(j_{1},j_{2})
since
\mathfrak{D}_{e_{1}}\equiv\emptyset
.

To analyze the METIC data, the first tree is chosen to assume a star structure, with the root node set to be the terminal event time. Therefore, the edges of
\mathscr{T}_{1}
,
\mathscr{E}_{1}=\{(j,J),j\in[J-1]\}
, encompass all pairs of nonterminal-terminal dependencies. The bivariate copula associated with each edge is denoted by
\mathbbm{C}_{j,J}
, representing the joint distribution of
(T_{j},T_{J})
, which is the requisite for obtaining a consistent estimate for the marginal of
T_{j}

(
Fine
et al., 2001
)
. For subsequent trees, one may choose the structure according to the specific context, e.g., a
C
-vine or a
D
-vine. With such a vine graph, the joint copula density is given in Proposition
1
.

Remark 1
.

The identifiability issue in semi-competing risks data has been discussed in previous works
(
Fine
et al., 2001
;
Peng and
Fine, 2007
;
Chen, 2012
;
Li et al., 2023
)
. The bivariate copula
\mathbbm{C}_{j,J}
specifies the joint density of
(T_{j},T_{J})
on the region where
T_{j}\leq T_{J}
for
j\in[J-1]
, only on which the joint density is nonparametrically identifiable. Thus, in the METIC setting, we label
\mathfrak{R}_{[J]}=\{\bm{t}_{[J]}:t_{1},\ldots,t_{J-1}\in(0,t_{J}]\}
the identifiable region of
\bm{T}_{[J]}
, and the joint density of
\bm{T}_{[J]}
on
\mathfrak{R}_{[J]}
is specified by the joint density in (
3
).

Remark 2
.

The copula function
\mathbbm{C}_{[J]}
was originally introduced to connect the joint cumulative distribution function (CDF) of multivariate random variables with the marginal CDFs. The survival copula considered in this article shares similar properties to the original copula
(
Nelsen, 2007
)
, connecting the joint survival function with the marginals.

2.3
Model specification

To assess the direct covariate effects on the marginals, we specify
S_{j}(t_{j}|\bm{Z})
using a flexible semiparametric transformation model
(
Zeng and Lin, 2006
)
,

S_{j}(t_{j}|\bm{Z};\bm{\theta}_{j})=\exp\left[-G_{j}\left\{\int_{0}^{t_{j}}e^{\bm{\beta}_{j}^{\prime}\bm{L}}\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{j}(s_{j})\right\}\right],

(5)

where user-specified covariates
\bm{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}}
is an arbitrary bounded function of
\bm{Z}
. The marginal model parameters are
\bm{\theta}_{j}\equiv\{\bm{\beta}_{j},\Lambda_{j}(\cdot)\}
, where
\bm{\beta}_{j}
is a
d_{L}
-dimensional vector of regression coefficients, and
\Lambda_{j}(\cdot)
is an unspecified continuously differentiable baseline function assumed to be increasing. The function
G_{j}(\cdot)
is pre-specified, non-negative, strictly increasing, and continuously differentiable while satisfying
G_{j}(0)=0
,
\dot{G}_{j}(0)>0
, and
G_{j}(\infty)=\infty

(
Zeng and Lin, 2006
)
. Two examples of
G_{j}(\cdot)
among others are
G_{j}(x)=x
, resulting in a PH model, and
G_{j}(x)=\log(1+x)
, resulting in a proportional odds model.

Each bivariate copula in (
3
) is specified using a parametric copula family, e.g., Archimedean or elliptical; expressions of some popular families are provided in Section S1 of the Supplementary Materials. The copula parameter associated with
\mathbbm{c}_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}}}
, denoted by
\alpha_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}},\bm{Z}}
, might be dependent on
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{W}}
, an arbitrary bounded function of
\bm{Z}
that could be different from
\bm{L}
. It can be modeled by

\alpha_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}},\bm{Z}}=g_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}}}(\bm{\gamma}_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}}}^{\prime}\bm{W}),

(6)

where
\bm{\gamma}_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{W}}
is a
d_{W}
-dimensional event-pair-specific parameter vector, and
g_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}}}(\cdot)
is a pre-specified link function in conjunction with the selected copula family. Examples of
g_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}}}(\cdot)
include
g_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}}}(x)=e^{x}
for Clayton,
g_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}}}(x)=x
for Frank,
g_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}}}(x)=e^{x}+1
for Gumbel, and
g_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}}}(x)=(e^{2x}-1)/(e^{2x}+1)
for Gaussian. Because of the one-to-one correspondence between
\alpha_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}},\bm{Z}}
and
\bm{\gamma}_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}}}
, we hereafter write
\mathbbm{C}_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}}}(\cdot;\alpha_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}},\bm{Z}})
as
\mathbbm{C}_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}}}(\cdot;\bm{\gamma}_{j_{1},j_{2}|\mathfrak{D}_{e_{k}}})
.

2.4
The full likelihood

We give the full likelihood function under the vine copula-based approach, which depends on the censoring status indicator of each event time. For a non-empty subset
\mathfrak{J}\subseteq[J]
, define its observed index set
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{J},i}=\{j|j\in\mathfrak{J},\Delta_{j,i}=1\}
and censored index set
\mathfrak{c}_{\mathfrak{J},i}=\{j|j\in\mathfrak{J},\Delta_{j,i}=0\}
. Let
U_{j,\bm{Z},i}=S_{j}(X_{j,i}|\bm{Z}_{i};\bm{\theta}_{j})
,
j\in[J]
, often referred to as the pseudo observations, and define
\bm{U}_{\mathfrak{J},\bm{Z},i}^{\mathfrak{o}}=\{U_{j,\bm{Z},i},j\in\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{J},i}\}
and
\mathcal{U}_{\mathfrak{J},\bm{Z},i}^{\mathfrak{c}}=\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{c}_{\mathfrak{J},i}}[0,U_{j,\bm{Z},i}]
. Observed event times enter the full likelihood function via
\bm{U}_{[J],\bm{Z},i}^{\mathfrak{o}}
, while censored event times are integrated over their corresponding range
\mathcal{U}_{[J],\bm{Z},i}^{\mathfrak{c}}
. For ease of presentation, we rearrange the order of the arguments of
\mathbbm{c}_{[J]}
defined in (
3
) by their censoring statuses,
\mathbbm{c}_{[J]}(\bm{U}_{[J],\bm{Z},i}^{\mathfrak{o}},\bm{u}_{[J],\bm{Z},i}^{\mathfrak{c}})
, where
\bm{u}_{[J],\bm{Z},i}^{\mathfrak{c}}
is a set of
|\mathfrak{c}_{[J],i}|
variables associated with the integral. Let
\bm{\Omega}
denote the collection of all model parameters, and the log-likelihood function for subject
i
is

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{\Omega};\mathcal{O}_{i})=\sum_{j\in[J]}\Delta_{j,i}\log\left\{-f_{j}(X_{j,i}|\bm{Z}_{i};\bm{\theta}_{j})\right\}+h(\bm{\Omega};\bm{U}_{[J],\bm{Z},i},\bm{\Delta}_{[J],i}),

(7)

where

\displaystyle h(\bm{\Omega};\bm{U}_{[J],\bm{Z},i},\bm{\Delta}_{[J],i})=\log\int_{\mathcal{U}_{[J],\bm{Z},i}^{\mathfrak{c}}}\mathbbm{c}_{[J]}\left(\bm{U}_{[J],\bm{Z},i}^{\mathfrak{o}},\bm{u}_{[J],\bm{Z},i}^{\mathfrak{c}}\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{u}_{[J],\bm{Z},i}^{\mathfrak{c}}.

(8)

We also provided an alternative construction of the full likelihood function using a counting process representation in Section S2 of the Supplementary Materials.

2.5
Comparison with multivariate and nested copulas

Without considering covariates,
Li
et al. (2020)
specified the joint survival function of
\bm{T}_{[J]}
as
S_{[J]}(\bm{t}_{[J]})=\mathbbm{C}_{[J]}(\bm{u}_{[J]};\alpha_{[J]})
, a parametric
J
-variate Archimedean copula with parameter
\alpha_{[J]}
. This model implies that the joint survival function of any pair
(T_{j},T_{j^{\prime}})
is given by

S_{j,j^{\prime}}(t_{j},t_{j^{\prime}})=\mathbbm{C}(u_{j},u_{j^{\prime}};\alpha_{[J]})
, where
\mathbbm{C}
is a bivariate copula that belongs to the same copula family as
\mathbbm{C}_{[J]}
with the same copula parameter
\alpha_{[J]}
for all
(j,j^{\prime})
pairs.

Li et al. (2023)
relaxed this homogeneous pairwise dependence assumption using two-level nested copulas while incorporating covariates. Specifically, at the first level, the joint survival function of
\bm{T}_{[J-1]}
is specified using a
(J-1)
-variate copula, i.e.,
S_{[J-1]}(\bm{t}_{[J-1]}|\bm{Z})=\mathbbm{C}_{[J-1]}\{S_{1}(t_{1}|\bm{Z}),\ldots,S_{J}(t_{J}|\bm{Z});\alpha_{[J-1],\bm{Z}}\}
, and, at the second level,
S_{[J-1]}(\bm{t}_{[J-1]}|\bm{Z})
is linked with
S_{J}(t_{J}|\bm{Z})
via a bivariate copula, i.e.,
S_{[J]}(\bm{t}_{[J]}|\bm{Z})=\mathbbm{C}_{[J-1],J}\{S_{[J-1]}(\bm{t}_{[J-1]}|\bm{Z}),S_{J}(t_{J}|\bm{Z});\allowbreak\alpha_{[J-1],J,\bm{Z}}\}
. The first-level copula
\mathbbm{C}_{[J-1]}
can be from any family, whereas
\mathbbm{C}_{[J-1],J}
at the second level is required to be Archimedean. This implies that the nonterminal-terminal dependencies (between
T_{j}
and
T_{J}
,
j\in[J-1]
) can be different from the between-nonterminal ones (between
T_{j}
and
T_{j^{\prime}}
for
j\neq j^{\prime}
and
j,j^{\prime}\in[J-1]
). Still, the nested copula structure in
Li et al. (2023)
assumes (i) each nonterminal event time has the same association with the terminal, and each nonterminal-terminal copula is
\mathbbm{C}_{[J-1],J}(\cdot;\alpha_{[J-1],J,\bm{Z}})
, (ii) each pairwise dependence between two nonterminal event times is homogeneous with a bivariate copula that belongs to the same family as
\mathbbm{C}_{[J-1]}
with the same parameter
\alpha_{[J-1],\bm{Z}}
, and (iii) the between-nonterminal association is stronger than the nontermina-terminal one because of the sufficient nesting conditions
(
Hofert and
Pham, 2013
)
if both
\mathbbm{C}_{[J]}
and
\mathbbm{C}_{[J-1],J}
are Archimedean. The two copula parameters
\alpha_{[J-1],\bm{Z}}
and
\alpha_{[J-1],J,\bm{Z}}
depend on
\bm{Z}
via parametric or nonparametric regressions.

Li et al. (2023)
specified the terminal marginal
S_{J}(t|\bm{Z})
using a PH or accelerated failure time model. For each non-terminal event,
Li et al. (2023)
assumes that the marginal survival function
S_{j}^{*}(t|\bm{Z})
of a first-event time
T_{j}^{*}=T_{j}\wedge T_{J}
follows a PH model. The nonterminal marginal
S_{j}(t|\bm{Z})
is then obtained via transformation under the Archimedean copula
\mathbbm{C}_{[J-1],J}
, thus unable to directly estimate the covariate effects on
T_{j}
via a regression. Compared to
Li
et al. (2020)
and
Li et al. (2023)
, the proposed vine copula-based approach relaxes the symmetrical structural assumptions in these works by allowing each bivariate copula to be separately selected from any family with its unique copula parameter. The terminal and nonterminal marginals are directly specified using the model in (
5
), which, coupled with the stage-wise estimation procedure introduced in Section
3
, enables direct regression analyses on individual event times. In general, the vine copula-based method provides a flexible framework that is more suitable for the METIC data, while the multivariate and nested copula approaches are convenient for obtaining the analytical expression of the joint survival function.

3
Stage-wise estimation and inference

3.1
Stage-wise estimation

We estimate
\bm{\Omega}
via a stage-wise procedure. Let
\bm{\Omega}_{\mathscr{T}_{k}}
denote model parameters associated with tree
\mathscr{T}_{k}
for
k\in[J-1]
, where
\bm{\Omega}_{\mathscr{T}_{1}}=\{\bm{\theta}_{j},j\in[J]\}\cup\{\bm{\gamma}_{j,J},j\in[J-1]\}
, and
\bm{\Omega}_{\mathscr{T}_{k}}=\{\bm{\gamma}_{e_{k}},e_{k}\in\mathscr{E}_{k}\}
for
k=2,\ldots,J-1
. We define
\mathcal{O}_{j}=\{\mathcal{O}_{j,i}\equiv(X_{j,i},\Delta_{j,i},\bm{Z}_{i}),i\in[n]\}
as the observed data related to event
j\in[J]
, and the stage-wise estimation procedure is given as follows.

Stage 1: Estimate
\bm{\Omega}_{\mathscr{T}_{1}}
.

The estimation of
\bm{\Omega}_{\mathscr{T}_{1}}
consists of two steps. At the first step, we obtain
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{J}\equiv\{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{J},\widehat{\Lambda}_{J}(\cdot)\}
, the estimate of model parameters in
S_{J}(t_{J}|\bm{Z},\bm{\theta}_{J})
, with observed data
\mathcal{O}_{J}
alone, since
T_{J}
is only subject to independent censoring
(
Zeng and Lin, 2006
)
. Specifically, the baseline function
\Lambda_{J}(\cdot)
is regarded as a non-decreasing step function that jumps only at the observed terminal event times
(
Chen, 2012
)
. Let
0<t_{J,1}<t_{J,2}<\cdots<t_{J,\kappa_{J}}\leq\overline{t}
denote the ordered observed terminal event times, and let
\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{J,l}>0
denote the jump size of
\Lambda_{J}(\cdot)
at time
t_{J,l}
, for
l\in[\kappa_{J}]
. The terminal marginal parameters
\bm{\theta}_{J}
can be re-expressed as
(\bm{\beta}_{J}^{\prime},\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{J,1},\ldots,\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{J,\kappa_{J}})^{\prime}
. The estimate
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{J}
is obtained via maximizing the log-likelihood function
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{J}(\bm{\theta}_{J};\mathcal{O}_{J,i})
, where

\displaystyle\ell_{J}(\bm{\theta}_{J};\mathcal{O}_{J,i})

\displaystyle=-G_{J}\left\{\int_{0}^{\overline{t}}e^{\bm{\beta}_{J}^{\prime}\bm{L}_{i}}\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{J}(s)\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Delta_{J,i}\left[\log\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{J}(X_{J,i})+\log\dot{G}_{J}\left\{\int_{0}^{X_{J,i}}e^{\bm{\beta}_{J}^{\prime}\bm{L}_{i}}\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{J}(s)\right\}+\bm{\beta}_{J}^{\prime}\bm{L}_{i}\right].

At the second step, we
separately
estimate
\bm{\Omega}_{j,J}\equiv\{\bm{\theta}_{j},\bm{\gamma}_{j,J}\}
for each
j\in[J-1]
via the PMLE method in
Arachchige
et al. (2025)
proposed for the semi-competing risk data. Similar to the first step, let
0<t_{j,1}<t_{j,2}<\cdots<t_{j,\kappa_{j}}\leq\overline{t}
denote the ordered observed event times for the nonterminal event
j\in[J-1]
, and let
\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{j,l}>0
denote the jump size of
\Lambda_{j}(\cdot)
at time
t_{j,l}
, for
l\in[\kappa_{j}]
. The nonterminal marginal parameters
\bm{\theta}_{j}
can be re-expressed as
(\bm{\beta}_{j}^{\prime},\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{j,1},\ldots,\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{j,\kappa_{j}})^{\prime}
. For each
j\in[J-1]
, with observed data of subject
i
,
\mathcal{O}_{(j,J),i}=\mathcal{O}_{j,i}\cup\mathcal{O}_{J,i}
, the complete log-likelihood function is
\ell_{j,J}^{\mathrm{comp}}(\bm{\Omega}_{j,J},\bm{\theta}_{J};\mathcal{O}_{(j,J),i})=\ell_{j,J}(\bm{\Omega}_{j,J};U_{J,\bm{Z},i},\mathcal{O}_{(j,J),i})+\widetilde{\ell}_{J}(\bm{\theta}_{J};\mathcal{O}_{J,i})
, where
\widetilde{\ell}_{J}(\bm{\theta}_{J};\mathcal{O}_{J,i})=\Delta_{J,i}\left\{-G_{J}(\Lambda_{J,i})+\log\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{J}(X_{J,i})+\log\dot{G}_{J}(\Lambda_{J,i})+\bm{\beta}_{J}^{\prime}\bm{L}_{i}\right\}
,
a function of
\bm{\theta}_{J}
alone, and

\displaystyle\ell_{j,J}(\bm{\Omega}_{j,J};U_{J,\bm{Z},i},\mathcal{O}_{(j,J),i})

\displaystyle=\Delta_{j,i}\Delta_{J,i}\log\mathbbm{c}_{j,J}(U_{j,\bm{Z},i},U_{J,\bm{Z},i};\bm{\gamma}_{j,J})+\Delta_{j,i}(1-\Delta_{J,i})\log\dot{\mathbbm{C}}_{j,J}(U_{J,\bm{Z},i}|U_{j,\bm{Z},i};\bm{\gamma}_{j,J})

\displaystyle\quad+(1-\Delta_{j,i})\Delta_{J,i}\log\dot{\mathbbm{C}}_{j,J}(U_{j,\bm{Z},i}|U_{J,\bm{Z},i};\bm{\gamma}_{j,J})+(1-\Delta_{j,i})(1-\Delta_{J,i})\log\mathbbm{C}_{j,J}(U_{j,\bm{Z},i},U_{J,\bm{Z},i};\bm{\gamma}_{j,J})

\displaystyle\quad+\Delta_{j,i}\left[-G_{j}\left\{\int_{0}^{X_{j,i}}e^{\bm{\beta}_{j}^{\prime}\bm{L}_{i}}\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{j}(s)\right\}+\log\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{j}(X_{j,i})+\log\dot{G}_{j}\left\{\int_{0}^{X_{j,i}}e^{\bm{\beta}_{j}^{\prime}\bm{L}_{i}}\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{j}(s)\right\}+\bm{\beta}_{j}^{\prime}\bm{L}_{i}\right],

(9)

which depends on
\bm{\theta}_{J}
only through
U_{J,\bm{Z},i}
. The estimate
\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{j,J}=\{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{j},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j,J}\}
is obtained via maximizing the pseudo log-likelihood function
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\allowbreak\ell_{j,J}(\bm{\Omega}_{j,J};\widehat{U}_{J,\bm{Z},i},\mathcal{O}_{(j,J),i})
, where
\widehat{U}_{J,\bm{Z},i}=S_{J}(X_{J,i}|\bm{Z}_{i},\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{J})
. Since
\widetilde{\ell}_{J}
does not involve
\bm{\Omega}_{j,J}
, the estimate
\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{j,J}=\{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{j},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j,J}\}
, equivalently, maximizes
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\allowbreak\ell_{j,J}^{\mathrm{comp}}(\bm{\Omega}_{j,J},\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{J},\mathcal{O}_{(j,J),i})
, where
\bm{\theta}_{J}
is regarded as the nuisance parameter.

Stage
k
: Estimate
\bm{\Omega}_{\mathscr{T}_{k}}
,
k=2,\ldots,J-1
.

At stage
k
, we estimate each bivariate copula parameter
\bm{\gamma}_{e_{k}}
separately for edge
e_{k}\in\mathscr{E}_{k}
. Recall that
\mathfrak{n}(e_{k})
collects the index of the event times involved in this edge, and
\overline{\mathscr{E}}(e_{k})\equiv\{\overline{\mathscr{E}}_{r}(e_{k}),r\in[k-1]\}
consists of the edges of all previous trees that are the ancestor nodes of
e_{k}
. Let
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathfrak{n}(e_{k})}=\{\bm{\theta}_{j},j\in\mathfrak{n}(e_{k})\}
and
\widehat{\bm{U}}_{\mathfrak{n}(e_{k}),\bm{Z},i}=\{\widehat{U}_{j,\bm{Z},i},j\in\mathfrak{n}(e_{k})\}
, estimated pseudo-observations obtained from Stage 1, and
\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e_{k}}=\{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{e}:e\in\overline{\mathscr{E}}(e_{k})\}
, estimated copula parameters from previous trees. With observed data
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{n}(e_{k})}=\cup_{j\in\mathfrak{n}(e_{k})}\mathcal{O}_{j}=\{\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{n}(e_{k}),i},i\in[n]\}
, the pseudo-log-likelihood function for estimating
\bm{\gamma}_{e_{k}}
, is given by
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{e_{k}}(\bm{\gamma}_{e_{k}};\widehat{\bm{U}}_{\mathfrak{n}(e_{k}),\bm{Z},i},\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e_{k}},\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{n}(e_{k}),i})
,
with

\displaystyle\ell_{e_{k}}(\bm{\gamma}_{e_{k}};\widehat{\bm{U}}_{\mathfrak{n}(e_{k}),\bm{Z},i},\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e_{k}},\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{n}(e_{k}),i})=\log\int_{\widehat{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{n}(e_{k}),\bm{Z},i}^{\mathfrak{c}}}\mathbbm{c}_{\mathfrak{n}(e_{k})}\left(\widehat{\bm{U}}_{\mathfrak{n}(e_{k}),\bm{Z},i}^{\mathfrak{o}},\bm{u}_{\mathfrak{n}(e_{k}),\bm{Z},i}^{\mathfrak{c}}\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{u}_{\mathfrak{n}(e_{k}),\bm{Z},i}^{\mathfrak{c}},

where
\mathbbm{c}_{\mathfrak{n}(e_{k}),\bm{Z}}
is given in (
4
). The nuisance parameters in
\ell_{e_{k}}
are
\bm{\theta}_{\mathfrak{n}(e_{k})}=\{\bm{\theta}_{j}:j\in\mathfrak{n}(e_{k})\}
and
\bm{\Gamma}_{e_{k}}=\{\bm{\gamma}_{e}:e\in\overline{\mathscr{E}}(e_{k})\}
.

Remark 3
.

The stage-wise PMLE can be modified in conjunction with variations in model specification. For example, when some model component specifications are identical, we can estimate the shared parameters from a pooled pseudo-log-likelihood function. Specifically, in Simulation Study II of Section
4.2
with
J=3
and no covariates, we assume
\mathbbm{C}_{1,3}
and
\mathbbm{C}_{2,3}
are both Clayton with the same parameter
\alpha_{1,3}=\alpha_{2,3}=\alpha
, i.e., homogeneous nonterminal-terminal dependence (as the model specifications in
Li
et al. (2020)
and
Li et al. (2023)
). Under this assumption, at the first stage, the shared copula parameter
\alpha
along with marginal parameters
\{\bm{\beta}_{1},\Lambda_{1}(\cdot),\bm{\beta}_{2},\Lambda_{2}(\cdot)\}
are estimated by maximizing a pooled log-likelihood function
\sum_{i}\{\ell_{1,3}(\alpha,\bm{\beta}_{1},\Lambda_{1};\widehat{U}_{3,\bm{Z},i},\mathcal{O}_{(1,3),i})+\ell_{2,3}(\alpha,\bm{\beta}_{2},\Lambda_{2};\widehat{U}_{3,\bm{Z},i},\mathcal{O}_{(2,3),i})\}
.

3.2
Theoretical properties

We present the theoretical properties of the stage-wise estimators
\{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{j},j\in[J]\}
and
\{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{e_{k}},e_{k}\in\mathscr{E}_{k},k\in[J-1]\}
. Let
\{\bm{\theta}_{j}^{0}\equiv\{\bm{\beta}_{j}^{0},\Lambda_{j}^{0}(\cdot)\},j\in[J]\}
and
\{\bm{\gamma}_{e_{k}}^{0},e_{k}\in\mathscr{E}_{k},k\in[J-1]\}
respectively denote true values of
\{\bm{\theta}_{j},j\in[J]\}
and
\{\bm{\gamma}_{e_{k}},e_{k}\in\mathscr{E}_{k},k\in[J-1]\}
. We use
\|\cdot\|_{l^{\infty}[0,\overline{t}]}
to denote the supremum norm in
[0,\overline{t}]
. Let
BV[0,\overline{t}]
be the space of real-valued functions with bounded variations in
[0,\overline{t}]
, and
\|w(\cdot)\|_{BV[0,\overline{t}]}
the total variation of a real-valued function
w(t)
on
[0,\overline{t}]
. Also, define
\mathscr{L}=\{w(t):\|w(\cdot)\|_{BV[0,\overline{t}]}\leq 1\}
. Then,
\widehat{\Lambda}_{j}(\cdot)
, for
j\in[J]
, can be viewed as bounded linear functionals in
l^{\infty}(\mathscr{L})
. The consistency of the stage-wise estimators is given in Proposition
2
.

Proposition 2
.

Under the METIC setting and conditions (C1) - (C9) listed in Section S4.2 of the Supplementary Materials,

(a)

with probability one,
\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{J}-\bm{\beta}_{J}^{0}\|\rightarrow 0
and
\|\widehat{\Lambda}_{J}(\cdot)-\Lambda_{J}^{0}(\cdot)\|_{l^{\infty}[0,\overline{t}]}\rightarrow 0
;

(b)

for
j\in[J-1]
, with probability one,
\|\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j,J}-\bm{\gamma}_{j,J}^{0}\|\rightarrow 0
,
\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{j}-\bm{\beta}_{j}^{0}\|\rightarrow 0
, and
\|\widehat{\Lambda}_{j}(\cdot)-\Lambda_{j}^{0}(\cdot)\|_{l^{\infty}[0,\overline{t}]}\rightarrow 0
;

(c)

for
e_{k}\in\mathscr{E}_{k}
and
k=2,\ldots,J-1
, with probability one,
\|\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{e_{k}}-\bm{\gamma}_{e_{k}}^{0}\|\rightarrow 0
.

The proof of Proposition
2
and the required conditions are provided in Section S4.2 of the Supplementary Material. The consistency of the terminal marginal parameter estimators
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{J}
in Proposition
2
(a) was established in
Zeng and Lin (2006)
. The proof of Proposition
2
(b) largely follows the arguments of
Chen (2012)
, which investigated estimators of
(\bm{\theta}_{J},\bm{\Omega}_{j,J})
simultaneously maximizes the complete log-likelihood
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell^{\mathrm{comp}}_{j,J}(\bm{\Omega}_{j,J},\bm{\theta}_{J};\mathcal{O}_{(j,J),i})
. However, due to the nature of PMLE, the stage-wise estimators require different regularity conditions. The consistency of copula parameter estimators
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{e_{k}}
for
e_{k}\in\mathscr{E}_{k}
and
k=2,\ldots,J-1
in Proposition
2
(c) is established following techniques and procedures in
Chen
et al. (2010)
for the PMLE of the second-stage copula parameter in semiparametric multivariate survival functions. The consistency of the estimators in the later stages depends on that of those in the earlier stages.

We present the asymptotic normality of the stage-wise estimators in Proposition
3
; the proof and required conditions are provided in Section S4.3 of the Supplementary Material.

Proposition 3
.

Under the METIC setting and conditions (C1) - (C9) and (A1) - (A2) listed in Sections S4.2 and S4.3 of the Supplementary Materials,

(a)

n^{1/2}\{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{J}-\bm{\beta}_{J}^{0},\widehat{\Lambda}_{J}(\cdot)-\Lambda_{J}^{0}(\cdot)\}
converges weakly to a zero-mean Gaussian process in the metric space
\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}}\times l^{\infty}(\mathscr{L})
;

(b)

for
j\in[J-1]
,
n^{1/2}\{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{j}-\bm{\beta}_{j}^{0},\widehat{\Lambda}_{j}(\cdot)-\Lambda_{j}^{0}(\cdot),\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j,J}-\bm{\gamma}_{j,J}^{0}\}
converges weakly to a zero-mean Gaussian process in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}}\times l^{\infty}(\mathscr{L})\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{W}}
;

(c)

for
e_{k}\in\mathscr{E}_{k}
and
k=2,\ldots,J-1
,
n^{1/2}(\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{e_{k}}-\bm{\gamma}_{e_{k}}^{0})
converges weakly to a zero-mean
d_{W}
-variate normal distribution.

Consider the following linear functionals
n^{1/2}[\int_{0}^{\overline{t}}\bm{a}_{J}^{\prime}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{J}-\bm{\beta}_{J}^{0})+b_{J}(t)\{\mathrm{d}\widehat{\Lambda}_{J}(t)-\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{J}^{0}(t)\}]
and
n^{1/2}[\int_{0}^{\overline{t}}\bm{a}_{j}^{\prime}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{j}-\bm{\beta}_{j}^{0})+b_{j}(t)\{\mathrm{d}\widehat{\Lambda}_{j}(t)-\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{j}^{0}(t)\}+\bm{c}_{j}^{\prime}(\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j,J}-\bm{\gamma}_{j,J}^{0})]
, where
\bm{a}_{J}
,
\bm{a}_{j}
, and
\bm{c}_{j}
are real vectors, and
b_{J}(t),b_{j}(t)\in BV[0,\overline{t}]
. Also, let
\bm{b}_{J}
and
\bm{b}_{j}
respectively be vectors consisting of the values of
b_{J}(t)
and
b_{j}(t)
evaluated at the observed times for events
J
and
j\in[J-1]
. Proposition
3
implies that these linear functions are also asymptotically normal with mean zero and variance-covariance matrices
\bm{q}_{J}^{\prime}(\bm{\Sigma}_{J}^{0})^{-1}\bm{q}_{J}
and
\bm{q}_{j,J}^{\prime}\bm{\Sigma}_{(j,J),\bm{\Omega}}^{0}\bm{q}_{j,J}
with
\bm{q}_{J}=(\bm{a}_{J}^{\prime},\bm{b}_{J}^{\prime})^{\prime}
and
\bm{q}_{j,J}=(\bm{a}_{j}^{\prime},\bm{b}_{j}^{\prime},\bm{c}_{j}^{\prime})^{\prime}
. Here, the variance-covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{J}^{0}
for
\bm{\theta}_{J}
is equivalent to the inverse of the Fisher information, defined as
\bm{I}_{J}^{0}=-\mathbb{E}^{0}\{\ddot{\ell}_{J}(\bm{\theta}_{J}^{0};\mathcal{O}_{J,i})\}
with
\ddot{\ell}_{J}=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}_{J}}\ell_{J}
. However, the variance-covariance matrices
\bm{\Sigma}_{(j,J),\bm{\Omega}}^{0}
for
\bm{\Omega}_{j,J}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{e_{k},\bm{\gamma}}^{0}
for
\bm{\gamma}_{e_{k}}
are
not equal to the inverse of their corresponding Fisher information because of the nature of PMLE. The derivation of these variance-covariance matrices is provided in Section S4.3 of the Supplementary Material.

The variance-covariance matrices of the stage-wise estimators can be estimated by the corresponding sandwich variance estimators. For example, the estimated variance-covariance matrix of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{J}
is
\overline{\bm{I}}_{J,n}^{-1}\overline{\bm{V}}_{J,n}\overline{\bm{I}}_{J,n}^{-1}
, where
\overline{\bm{I}}_{J,n}=-n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ddot{\ell}_{J}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}};\mathcal{O}_{J,n})
, and
\overline{\bm{V}}_{J,n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dot{\ell}_{J}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{J};\mathcal{O}_{J,i})\dot{\ell}_{J}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{J};\mathcal{O}_{J,i})^{\prime}
with
\dot{\ell}_{J}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{J}}\ell_{J}
. The estimated variance-covariance matrices for other parameters have the same sandwich form:
\overline{\bm{I}}_{(j,J),\bm{\Omega},\bm{\Omega},n}^{-1}\overline{\bm{V}}_{(j,J),\bm{\Omega},n}\overline{\bm{I}}_{(j,J),\bm{\Omega},\bm{\Omega},n}^{-1}
for
\bm{\Omega}_{j,J}
,
j\in[J-1]
, and
\overline{\bm{I}}_{e_{k},\bm{\gamma},\bm{\gamma},n}^{-1}\overline{\bm{V}}_{e_{k},\bm{\gamma},n}\overline{\bm{I}}_{e_{k},\bm{\gamma},\bm{\gamma},n}^{-1}
for
\bm{\gamma}_{e_{k}}
,
e_{k}\in\mathscr{T}_{k}
with
k=2,\cdots,J-1
. Their expressions are given in Section S4.3 of the Supplementary Material.

4.1
Simulation study I

In the first simulation study, we assess the finite-sample performance of the proposed estimation and inference procedure. The administrative censoring time
A_{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(1,6)
, a uniform distribution over
(1,6)
. We include two covariates:
Z_{1,i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(1,2)
and
Z_{2,i}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(1/3)
, a Bernoulli distribution with parameter
1/3
. Given
\bm{Z}_{i}=(Z_{1,i},Z_{2,i})^{\prime}
, we generate event times
(T_{1,i},T_{2,i},T_{3,i})
following the steps below.

Step 1: Generate
T_{3,i}
.

Obtain
T_{3,i}=\exp[-\zeta_{3}-\bm{\beta}_{3}^{\prime}\bm{Z}_{i}+\log\{-\log(1-\epsilon_{3,i})\}]
, where
\epsilon_{3,i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
. Calculate
U_{3,\bm{Z},i}=\exp(-e^{\zeta_{3}}T_{3,i}e^{\bm{\beta}_{3}^{\prime}\bm{Z}_{i}})
. We set
\zeta_{3}=-0.2
and
\bm{\beta}_{3}=(\beta_{31},\beta_{32})^{\prime}=(2,2)^{\prime}
.

Step 2: Generate
(U_{1|3,\bm{Z},i},U_{2|3,\bm{Z},i})
.

Generate a bivariate variable, denoted by
(U_{1|3,\bm{Z},i},\allowbreak U_{2|3,\bm{Z},i})
, from a Frank copula with parameter
\alpha_{(1,2|3),\bm{Z},i}=\gamma_{(1,2|3),0}+\gamma_{(1,2|3),1}Z_{1,i}+\gamma_{(1,2|3),2}Z_{2,i}
, where
\bm{\gamma}_{1,2|3}=(\gamma_{(1,2|3),0},\gamma_{(1,2|3),1},\gamma_{(1,2|3),2})^{\prime}=(1.86,1,1)^{\prime}
.

Step 3: Generate
T_{j,i}
,
j=1,2
.

For each
j=1,2
, given
(\bm{Z}_{i},U_{3,\bm{Z},i},U_{j|3,\bm{Z},i})
, solve the equation
U_{j|3,\bm{Z},i}=\dot{\mathbbm{C}}_{j,3}(u|U_{3,\bm{Z},i};\alpha_{(j,3),\bm{Z},i})
with respect to
u
, and denote the solution by
U_{j,\bm{Z},i}
. Specifically,
\mathbbm{C}_{1,3}
is the Gumbel copula with
\alpha_{(1,3),\bm{Z},i}=\exp(\gamma_{(1,3),0}+\gamma_{(1,3),1}Z_{1,i}+\gamma_{(1,3),2}Z_{2,i})+1
, where
\bm{\gamma}_{1,3}=(\gamma_{(1,3),0},\gamma_{(1,3),0},\gamma_{(1,3),1})^{\prime}=(0.85,1,0.1)^{\prime}
;
\mathbbm{C}_{2,3}
is the Clayton copula with
\alpha_{(2,3),\bm{Z},i}=\exp(\gamma_{(2,3),0}+\gamma_{(2,3),1}Z_{1,i}+\gamma_{(2,3),2}Z_{2,i})
, where
\bm{\gamma}_{2,3}=(\gamma_{(2,3),0},\gamma_{(2,3),1},\gamma_{(2,3),2})^{\prime}=(0.29,0.1,1)^{\prime}
. Let
T_{j,i}=\exp[-\zeta_{j}-\bm{\beta}_{j}^{\prime}\bm{Z}_{i}+\log\{-\log(U_{j,\bm{Z},i})\}]
. We set
(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=(0.1,0.4)
and
\bm{\beta}_{j}=(\beta_{j1},\beta_{j2})^{\prime}=(2,2)^{\prime}
for
j=1,2
.

Under the above setting, the marginal survival function of each event time follows a Cox PH model:
S_{j}(t|\bm{Z}_{i})=\exp(-e^{\zeta_{j}}te^{\bm{\beta}_{j}^{\prime}\bm{Z}_{i}})
for
j=1,2,3
, with different censoring rates across events: 30%, 25%, and 10% for
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
, and
T_{3}
, respectively. To reflect the heterogeneity in pairwise dependencies, three bivariate copulas are from different families:
\mathbbm{C}_{1,3}
is Gumbel,
\mathbbm{C}_{2,3}
is Clayton, and
\mathbbm{C}_{1,2|3}
is Frank, and we incorporate between-individual heterogeneity by allowing the copula parameter to be dependent on covariates via (
6
). The strength of the pairwise association varies; by the usual one-to-one correspondence between the copula parameter and Kendall’s
\tau
,
\tau_{1,3,\bm{Z}}\in(0.55,0.85)
,
\tau_{2,3,\bm{Z}}\in(0.35,0.70)
, and
\tau_{1,2|3,\bm{Z}}\in(0.1,0.35)
.

We generate 500 data replications with sample sizes
n=500
,
1,000
, and
2,000
. For each replication, we obtain the estimates of the model parameters and their analytic variance estimates. Note that the estimation is obtained under the correct specification of all marginal survival functions and bivariate copulas. Table
2
reports the summary statistics over 500 estimates for parameters
\{\bm{\beta}_{1},\bm{\beta}_{2},\bm{\beta}_{3},\bm{\gamma}_{1,3},\bm{\gamma}_{2,3},\bm{\gamma}_{1,2|3}\}

\widehat{\theta}\pm 1.96\times\text{SE}_{\widehat{\theta}}

\widehat{\theta}

\bm{\Omega}

The relative empirical standard deviation (rESD) and the relative average standard error (rASE) remain close, with the empirical coverage proportion (ECP) of the 95% confidence intervals maintaining their nominal level, confirming the asymptotic normality and the validity of the analytic variance estimators.

4.2
Simulation Study II

In the second simulation study, we compare our method with the nested modeling approach in
Li et al. (2023)
under a setting where the joint distribution can be correctly specified by both the vine copula and the nested copula. Consider the nested Clayton model
S_{[3]}(\bm{t}_{[3]})=\mathbbm{C}_{[2],3}\{\mathbbm{C}_{[2]}(u_{1},u_{2};\theta_{[2]}),u_{3};\theta_{[2],3}\}
, where
u_{j}=S_{j}(t_{j})
,
j=1,2,3
, and both
\mathbbm{C}_{[2],3}
and
\mathbbm{C}_{[2]}
are bivariate Clayton copulas. In Section S5 of the Supplementary Materials, we show that if
\theta_{[2]}=\theta_{[2],3}
, the corresponding joint density function can be expressed in the form of (
3
), where
\mathbbm{C}_{1,3}
and
\mathbbm{C}_{2,3}
are both Clayton with
\alpha_{1,3}=\alpha_{2,3}=\theta_{[2],3}
, and
\mathbbm{C}_{1,2|3}
is also Clayton with
\alpha_{1,2|3}=\theta_{[2],3}/(1+\theta_{[2],3})
.

Here, we set
\theta_{[2],3}=4.67
, which leads to Kendall’s
\tau=0.7
.
Li et al. (2023)
did not consider regression modeling for the marginals of nonterminal event times, as their focus was on the association. Thus, covariates are not included in this study. To generate
(T_{1,i},T_{2,i},T_{3,i})^{\prime}
, we first simulate
(U_{1,i},U_{2,i},U_{3,i})^{\prime}
from the above nested Clayton copula. Then, we obtain
T_{j,i}=S_{j}^{-1}(U_{j,i})
for
j=1,2,3
, where
S_{1}
,
S_{2}
, and
S_{3}
are the survival function of three Weibull random variables with the same shape parameter of
2
but different scale parameters of
70
,
60
, and
85
respectively. The administrative censoring follows an exponential distribution with a mean parameter of
350
. The censoring rates for
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
, and
T_{3}
are about 30%, 25%, and 20%, respectively.

We generate
500
data replications with sample sizes
n=300
,
500
, and
1,000
. For the nested copula, we implement the estimation procedure in
Li et al. (2023)
to estimate
\theta_{[2]}
and
\theta_{[2],3}
. For the vine copula, we obtain two types of estimates for the parameters. The first one, referred to as the
regular estimate
, follows the same stage-wise procedure described in Section
3.1
, where, at the first stage,
\alpha_{1,3}
and
\alpha_{2,3}
are estimated using separate log-likelihood functions. The second is the pooled estimate, described in Remark
3
. It incorporates the assumption of homogeneous nonterminal-terminal dependence, i.e.,
\alpha_{1,3}=\alpha_{2,3}=\alpha
. The common copula parameter
\alpha
, along with nonterminal marginal parameters, is estimated in the first stage by maximizing a pooled log-likelihood function. Based on the estimates from each method, the second-stage parameter
\alpha_{1,2|3}
is then estimated.

We did not directly compare the estimates of
\theta_{[2]}
and
\alpha_{1,2|3}
, both being the between-nonterminal dependence parameters. It is because the parameter
\theta_{[2]}
under the nested model is associated with the unconditional joint distribution of
(T_{1},T_{2})
, but
\alpha_{1,2|3}
in the vine copula is associated with the conditional joint distribution of
(T_{1},T_{2})|T_{3}
. It is more appropriate to compare the estimates of the between-nonterminal Kendall’s
\tau
, denoted by
\tau_{1,2}
. For the nested model, using the one-to-one correspondence,
\tau_{1,2}=\theta_{[2]}/(\theta_{[2]}+2)
. For the vine copula,
\tau_{1,2}
is defined as
4\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{C}_{1,2}(u_{1},u_{2})\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{C}_{1,2}(u_{1},u_{2})-1
, where
\mathbbm{C}_{1,2}(u_{1},u_{2})=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{C}_{1,2|3}\{\dot{\mathbbm{C}}_{1,3}(u_{1}|u_{3};\alpha_{1,3}),\dot{\mathbbm{C}}_{2,3}(u_{2}|u_{3};\alpha_{2,3});\alpha_{1,2|3}\}\mathrm{d}u_{3}
, and consequently,
\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{C}_{1,2}(u_{1},u_{2})=\int_{0}^{1}\allowbreak\mathbbm{c}_{[3]}(\bm{u}_{[3]})\mathrm{d}u_{3}
. Thus, under the vine copula,
\tau_{1,2}
is a function of
\{\alpha_{1,3},\alpha_{2,3},\alpha_{1,2|3}\}
, for which a plug-in estimate can be obtained. The nonterminal-terminal Kendall’s
\tau
can be easily estimated by plug-in estimators; for the nested model,
\tau_{j,3}=\theta_{[2],3}/(\theta_{[2],3}+2)
, and for the vine copula,
\tau_{j,3}=\alpha_{j,3}/(\alpha_{j,3}+2)
, with
j=1,2
.

Besides the copula parameters, we also compared the nonterminal marginal estimates. In
Li et al. (2023)
,
S_{j}(t)
is estimated via the corresponding first event
T_{j}^{*}=T_{j}\wedge T_{J}
. Only under Archimedean copula,
S_{j}(t)
can be explicitly expressed as a function of
S_{j}^{*}(t)=\mathbb{P}(T_{j}^{*}>t)
,
S_{J}(t)
, and
\theta_{[2]}
, and therefore, a plug-in estimator can be obtained. This is the reason that the nonterminal-terminal copulas in
Li
et al. (2020)
;
Li et al. (2023)
are restricted to the Archimedean copula families. In comparison, for the vine copula, we estimated the nonterminal marginals (along with the corresponding nonterminal-terminal copula parameters) directly from a pseudo-log-likelihood in the first stage.

5
A real data example

We apply the proposed vine-copula-based modeling framework to analyze data from Indiegogo, one of the largest crowdfunding platforms
(
Zhang and
Liu, 2012
)
. In a crowdfunding campaign, the creator sets a fundraising goal and corresponding rewards or perks, and interacts with backers during the fundraising period
(
Su
et al., 2023
)
. These interactions include updates from the creator on the latest campaign progress
(
Herd
et al., 2022
)
and discussions between the creator and backers through comments and responses
(
Dai and Zhang, 2019
;
Cornelius and
Gokpinar, 2020
;
Fan
et al., 2020
)
. Thus, in this analysis, we study three essential types of creator-backer interactions that were extensively studied as nonterminal events: (i) the first update of the campaign
(
Herd
et al., 2022
)
, (ii) the first 10 comments from either backers or the creator
(
Fan
et al., 2020
)
, and (iii) the first response from a creator to a comment
(
Cornelius and
Gokpinar, 2020
)
. The terminal event is the creator’s departure from the platform.

The relationships among these nonterminal events, as well as their connection to the terminal event, are also widely explored. For example,
Camacho
et al. (2019)
found that when the creator updates more, the campaign also receives more comments, and
Jabr and
Rahman (2022)
proposed that excessive information may lead to information overload in crowdfunding campaigns. Therefore, understanding these dynamics can help creators better manage their relationships and interactions with backers, ultimately maximizing the amount raised. The existing literature suggests that creators’ experience in their first campaign has a significant impact on their future campaigns
(
Kim
et al., 2020
)
. Thus, we focus on creators with multiple sequential campaigns and set the end of their first campaign as the baseline. Let
T_{\mathrm{U}}
,
T_{10\mathrm{C}}
,
T_{\mathrm{R}}
, and
T_{\mathrm{D}}
denote the time from the baseline to the first update, to the first 10 comments, to the first response to comments, and departure, respectively. We also selected several covariates related to a creator’s first campaign that may influence the nonterminal and terminal events: (i) the length of the narrative/campaign description
(
Wei
et al., 2022
)
, (ii) the amount raised
(
Liu et al., 2025
)
, (iii) the number of updates
(
Herd
et al., 2022
)
, (iv) the mean perk price
(
Su
et al., 2023
)
,
1
1

1

An Indiegogo (crowdfunding) campaign usually offers multiple perks. Each perk is associated with a specific price.
(v) the number of comments
(
Gao
et al., 2025
)
, (vi) whether the creator responds to comments
(
Kozinets et al., 2010
)
, and (vii) whether the platform verifies the creator’s email address
(
Beck
et al., 2024
)
.

5.1
Data description

The data consists of
6,303
creators that (i) entered the platform between 2012 and 2014, (ii) had more than one campaign, and (iii) did not have missing values on the above covariates. The departure time is fully observed for all creators, and the censoring rates for
T_{\mathrm{U}}
,
T_{10\mathrm{C}}
, and
T_{\mathrm{R}}
are 50%, 81%, and 91%, respectively. Two binary covariates are coded: whether a creator’s email address is verified (47%) and whether a creator responded during the first campaign (1%). The other five covariates are continuous and heavily right-skewed; therefore, we used the log-transformed values in the analysis. Web Table S2 in Section S6 of the Supplementary Materials reports the summary statistics of the five log-transformed variables.

5.2
Bivariate nonterminal event times

We begin with the analysis of three pairs of bivariate nonterminal event times. We constructed a vine graph for each pair:
\mathscr{V}_{\mathrm{U},10\mathrm{C}}
for
(T_{\mathrm{U}},T_{10\mathrm{C}},T_{\mathrm{D}})
,
\mathscr{V}_{\mathrm{R},10\mathrm{C}}
for
(T_{\mathrm{R}},T_{10\mathrm{C}},T_{\mathrm{D}})
, and
\mathscr{V}_{\mathrm{U},\mathrm{R}}
for
(T_{\mathrm{U}},T_{\mathrm{R}},T_{\mathrm{D}})
, outlined in Web Figure S2 of Section S6 of the Supplementary Materials. For each bivariate copula, we consider four candidate families: Clayton, Gumbel, Frank, and Gaussian. The family with the maximum log-likelihood evaluated at the corresponding estimate is used in the analysis (reported in Web Table S3 in Section S6 of the Supplementary Materials). The selected families for
\mathbbm{C}_{\mathrm{U},\mathrm{D}}
,
\mathbbm{C}_{\mathrm{R},\mathrm{D}}
, and
\mathbbm{C}_{10\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}}
are all Gumbel;
\mathbbm{C}_{\mathrm{U},10\mathrm{C}|\mathrm{D}}
is Gumbel while
\mathbbm{C}_{\mathrm{R},10\mathrm{C}|\mathrm{D}}
and
\mathbbm{C}_{\mathrm{U},\mathrm{R}|\mathrm{D}}
are Clayton.

Tables
4
and
5
report the estimated covariate effects on marginals and bivariate associations, respectively. The standard errors of the estimates and two-sided
p
-values are also provided. Here, we highlight some observations that align with the existing literature, and reserve the rest for Section S6 of the Supplementary Materials. The email verification status has a significant negative effect on all four marginals.
Beck
et al. (2024)
suggests that identity verification (e.g., email verification for campaign creators) can help build trust among backers. Therefore, when creators use email verification to build backers’ trust, they may rely less on updates, responses, and comments to achieve the same goal. Also, since the creators have made extra efforts beyond a specific campaign to complete the email verification, they intend to manage the platform well to build a long-term relationship rather than leave (departure) early
(
Palmatier et al., 2009
)
. The email verification status, on the other hand, has a significantly negative effect for
\mathbbm{C}_{\mathrm{U},\mathrm{D}}
,
\mathbbm{C}_{\mathrm{R},\mathrm{D}}
, and
\mathbbm{C}_{10\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}}
. This could be explained by the dependence of a creator’s lifetime on the trust establishment with backers, which can be achieved through two major sources. The first is achieved through direct interactions, such as updates, responses, and comments
(
Acar
et al., 2021
)
, while the second is facilitated by identity verification, including email verification
(
Beck
et al., 2024
)
. When creators rely more on email verification to build backers’ trust, they will put less effort into direct interactions that foster trust. Consequently, these activities are less likely to be factors associated with creators’ departure.

Web Figure S3 in Section S6 of the Supplementary Materials plots the estimated survival curves of
T_{\mathrm{U}}
,
T_{\mathrm{R}}
,
T_{10\mathrm{C}}
, and
T_{\mathrm{D}}
, which are functions of the covariates. We select four “representative” groups of creators: all four groups have median values for five continuous covariates, but they vary by their email verification and response statuses during the first campaign. Based on the above results, we conducted additional investigations in Section S6 of the Supplementary Materials, which include the following. First, we modify an association metric, Kendall’s
\tau
, to summarize the association between each pair of nonterminal events during the creator’s stay on the platform and examine whether this association varies across creators with different lifetimes. Second, we develop various models to predict the risk of departure based on its association with nonterminal events, modeled by the vine copula. In addition, we evaluate and compare their prediction performance using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve.

5.3
Trivariate nonterminal event times

We move on to the analysis of all three nonterminal event times and are particularly interested in the association between
T_{\mathrm{U}}
and
T_{\mathrm{R}}
given
T_{10\mathrm{C}}
and
T_{\mathrm{D}}
. Creators interact with backers through campaign updates or responses to individual comments. However, when creators use both ways simultaneously, it may cause information overload and harm the campaigns
(
Jabr and
Rahman, 2022
)
. Thus, we build the vine copula graph shown in Web Figure S1 in Section S3 of the Supplementary Materials with
4=\mathrm{D}
,
3=10\mathrm{C}
,
2=\mathrm{R}
, and
1=\mathrm{U}
, which allows us to study
\mathbbm{C}_{\mathrm{U},\mathrm{R}|10\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}}
in the last tree. In Section
5.2
, we have estimated all the marginals and bivariate copulas of the first two trees:
\{\mathbbm{C}_{\mathrm{U},\mathrm{D}},\mathbbm{C}_{\mathrm{R},\mathrm{D}},\mathbbm{C}_{10\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}},\mathbbm{C}_{\mathrm{U},10\mathrm{C}|\mathrm{D}},\mathbbm{C}_{\mathrm{R},10\mathrm{C}|\mathrm{D}}\}
, reported in Tables
4
and
5
. Thus, here, we estimate the parameters associated with the bivariate copula
\mathbbm{C}_{\mathrm{U},\mathrm{R}|10\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}}
.

Web Table S4 in Section S6 of the Supplementary Materials reports the estimates of covariate effects under each of the four copula families: Clayton, Gumbel, Frank, and Gaussian. These estimates show that none of the covariate effects are significant under any copula family. Thus, we assume that
\alpha_{\mathrm{U},\mathrm{R}|10\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D},\bm{Z}}=\alpha_{\mathrm{U},\mathrm{R}|10\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}}
is a constant. Under this assumption, we select Frank as the optimal copula family for
\mathbbm{C}_{\mathrm{U},\mathrm{R}|10\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}}
because it has the largest log-likelihood among the four families; their log-likelihood values are reported in Web Table S3. Under Frank, the estimate of
\alpha_{\mathrm{U},\mathrm{R}|10\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}}
is
-0.303
with SE
0.2
and two-sided
p
-value of
0.13
for testing
H_{0}:\alpha_{\text{U},\text{R}|\text{10C},\text{D}}=0
. This weak evidence of negative association resonates with the findings in
Jabr and
Rahman (2022)
. A creator who frequently provides updates on the campaign’s status may be less inclined to respond to individual comments, as this can help avoid information redundancy and preserve the campaign’s clarity and conciseness. Note that the estimation of
\mathbbm{C}_{\mathrm{U},\mathrm{R}|10\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}}
in the last stage might suffer from a small “effective” sample size since, in this data, only 231 out of
6,303
creators have all the events observed.

6
Discussions

We develop a flexible modeling framework for the METIC data based on the vine copula, where the multivariate dependence among
\bm{T}_{[J]}
is decomposed into a catalog of pairwise dependencies mapped by the vine graph with the root node of the first tree set as
T_{J}
, time to the terminal event. This modeling framework addresses two main limitations of previous methods: (i) heterogeneous pairwise dependencies, as each pairwise dependence can be modeled by its own bivariate copula without restrictions on the copula family or parameter values, and (ii) regression modeling of all marginals, as marginals (nonterminal and terminal) are modeled by semi-parametric transformation models without detouring through the first event time survival functions, which thus provides estimates of the direct covariate effects. The proposed stage-wise estimation procedure reduces the computational cost required by the maximum likelihood approach, which simultaneously estimates all parameters, and is more efficient than the plug-in estimator of the first event time approach in estimating marginals and copula parameters for nonterminal-terminal dependencies. As complementary results, we provide a counting process representation of the METIC likelihood and modifications to Kendall’s
\tau
on the observable region in the Supplementary Materials, which may be useful to survival analysis researchers.

The current work has some limitations that can be improved in future research. First, though this study proposes a modeling framework to capture the unique structure and heterogeneity of dependencies in the METIC data, more robust and sophisticated specifications of model components can be adopted. Specifically, the bivariate copulas can be specified non-parametrically to avoid misspecifications resulting from using parametric copula families, and for covariates, one could consider semi-parametric specifications for the marginals and copula parameter, e.g., the bundled sieve approach for marginals developed in
Zhao
et al. (2017)
and the sieve-within-parametric approach for copulas used in
Zhang
et al. (2010)
and
Li et al. (2023)
. The stage-wise estimation procedure remains valid for these model specifications; however, the
n^{1/2}
-convergence and analytical variance estimators are likely unattainable
(
Li et al., 2023
)
. Second, the vine copula-based method requires numerical integrations to obtain the joint survival and log-likelihood functions because it is developed upon attaining a closed-form expression of the joint density function of
\bm{T}_{[J]}
. The numerical integration operation can be computationally intensive and unwieldy when
J
and
n
are large. Third, a model diagnostic tool for assessing the goodness-of-fit of bivariate parametric copulas can serve as a valuable complement to the proposed modeling framework. Fourth, in our data example, the terminal event time was fully observed, which significantly reduces the estimation difficulty. However, in many applications, the terminal event time is subject to administrative censoring, for which the computation induced by the numerical integration might be more intensive. It could be more cost-effective to adopt multiple imputation approaches
(
Taylor
et al., 2002
;
Hsu
et al., 2006
;
Hsu and
Taylor, 2009
)
on censored time to terminal event. Lastly, the copula parameters in the current model are allowed to be dependent on covariates to capture between-individual heterogeneity, which may also be expanded to be event-times-dependent, thereby relaxing the simplifying assumption
(
Nagler, 2025
)
.
