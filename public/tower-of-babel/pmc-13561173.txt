Effect of oral propranolol on clinical findings and complications in retinopathy of premature infants: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Sama Noorizade, Lida Ghorbani, Sohrab Iranpoor, Amin Najafi, Haleh Salati Momeni, Mehrdad Mirza Rahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:This study aimed to investigate the efficacy and safety of oral propranolol in reducing the progression of retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) and its associated complications.Materials and methods:This randomized controlled trial was conducted on preterm infants with ROP. Participants were randomly assigned to either a propranolol group receiving oral propranolol (0.5 mg/kg every 8 hours) or a control group receiving distilled water. The primary outcome was the duration of complete retinal vascularization. Secondary outcomes included ROP stage and severity, vital signs, and potential complications (hypoglycemia, bradycardia, and hypotension).Results:A total of 61 infants (31 in the propranolol group and 30 in the control group) were included in the final analysis. There was no significant difference in the distribution of ROP stage and severity between the groups (P = 0.40). However, the duration of complete retinal vascularization was significantly shorter in the propranolol group [76.9 standard deviation (SD) = 11.06) days] compared to the control group [89.6 (SD = 9.6) days] (P < 0.001). While hypotension was more frequent in the propranolol group (45.1%) compared to the control group (26.7%), this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.18).Conclusion:Oral propranolol significantly reduced the time required for retinal vascularization in infants with ROP. The treatment was well-tolerated, with no serious adverse effects such as hypoglycemia or bradycardia. Although hypotension was more common in the propranolol group, the difference was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that oral propranolol may be a safe and effective adjunct therapy for ROP.

Introduction

Premature or preterm infants are those born alive before the completion of 37 weeks of gestation. Preterm birth is categorized based on gestational age (GA) at birth into three groups: extremely preterm (<28 weeks), very preterm (28 to <32 weeks), and moderate to late preterm (32–37 weeks). Preterm birth may occur spontaneously due to risk factors associated with preterm labor, or it may be planned due to specific medical conditions or the need for emergency cesarean sections in cases of maternal-fetal diseases. Statistics indicate that approximately 12–15 million preterm infants are born worldwide each year[1–3].

The earlier an infant is born, the higher the risk of death or serious disability. A significant number of these infants do not survive, and many survivors will face learning disabilities, vision problems, and hearing impairments. Globally, prematurity is the leading cause of mortality in children under 5 years of age, and the rate of preterm birth is increasing in almost all countries with reliable data[4]. Preterm infants are at a higher risk of mortality and various health and developmental issues compared to term infants. These complications include acute respiratory, gastrointestinal, immunological, central nervous system, hearing, and vision problems, as well as long-term motor, cognitive, behavioral, socio-emotional, health, and developmental disorders[5].

Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) is one of the leading causes of preventable blindness in children. This condition arises from a multifactorial disorder in the development of the retina and its vascular network[6]. ROP is associated with multiple ocular complications, such as an increased risk of refractive errors, strabismus, and cortical visual impairment[7]. Despite advancements in neonatal care, the incidence of ROP and related visual disorders is increasing, and controlling them remains challenging. Therefore, preventing the progression of ROP through timely screening and treatment is the most critical aspect of managing ROP in preterm infants[8].

Laser photocoagulation has been established as the standard treatment for this condition; however, this treatment destroys parts of the retina and can lead to significant ocular complications[9]. Efforts to find a solution to this significant problem in preterm infants have led to the search for new and effective treatment methods with fewer side effects, and several treatments have been proposed[7]. These include intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), erythropoietin, propranolol, caffeine, antioxidants, insulin-like growth factor, and omega-3 unsaturated fatty acids, which are promising treatments for preventing ROP. However, to date, limited studies have been conducted on their therapeutic success rates and side effects, and more research is needed in this area[10].

One of the mentioned treatments is propranolol, a beta-blocker that has been shown in some studies to reduce the need for laser photocoagulation in infants with ROP[7]. Another study in this regard suggested that propranolol may indirectly improve visual outcomes and reduce the progression of ROP[11]. Propranolol is used in both oral and topical forms (0.1% eye drops) for the treatment and prevention of ROP progression, with more favorable results reported for the oral form. However, both forms are well-tolerated, and the study results are not entirely reliable due to the limited number of infants studied[12]. Previous studies have indicated that oral propranolol, when appropriately selected and administered to infants who are not high-risk, is safe and well-tolerated[13], and it has been confirmed that the safety profile of oral propranolol in healthy infants is good[14].

However, in some cases, adverse effects of propranolol have been reported, including serious side effects such as bradycardia, hypotension, inadequate weight gain, bronchospasm, hypoglycemia, apnea, and increased need for ventilator support[15]. Given the widespread prevalence of preterm births and the associated ROP, as well as the preventable nature of this condition and the consequences of invasive treatments such as surgery and laser photocoagulation, there is a need for extensive research on the effectiveness and side effects of pharmacological treatments. In this context, this study was designed and conducted to investigate the clinical findings and adverse effects of propranolol use in infants with ROP.

Methods

Study design

This is a randomized controlled trial conducted in 2022 to assess the effect of oral propranolol on clinical findings and complications in infants with ROP. The study adhered to the “Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials” (CONSORT) criteria[16] (Supplemental Digital Content File 1, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B297). This work has been reported in line with the Transparency in the Reporting of Artificial Intelligence (TITAN) guideline[17].

Participants

Newborns with ROP were considered the target population in this study. In this study, all newborns with ROP who were hospitalized at Alavi and Bu Ali hospitals in Ardabil, Iran, in the year 2022 were included using a random allocation approach. Participants were divided into two groups based on whether they received propranolol (Fig. 1). The inclusion criteria for this study are as follows: confirmation of the diagnosis of ROP by an ophthalmologist using conventional retinal examination; GA at birth of less than 32 weeks or birth weight of less than 1500 g; and ROP Stage 1 or higher. The exclusion criteria are as follows: presence of indications for initiating ROP treatment, congenital heart defects, ocular anomalies, renal anomalies, intracranial hemorrhage, hemodynamic instability, congenital infections, sepsis, cardiac arrhythmias, asthma, and other respiratory issues. At the outset of the research, two participants were excluded from the control group and three from the treatment group due to ethical concerns, specifically unwillingness to continue participation and hemodynamic instability. The sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome: time to completion of retinal vessels. The calculation assumed a two-sided α = 0.05, 80% power, an expected between-group difference (Δ) of 21 days, and an estimated standard deviation (SD) of 29 days, as reported for “time to completion of retinal vessels” in weeks postmenstrual (approximately 4.13 weeks or 29 days)[18]. A minimum of 30 infants per group was required. To account for potential post-randomization exclusions or incomplete follow-up, the target sample size was increased to 66 infants.

Blinding

Eligible infants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either a placebo or propranolol. An independent staff member, uninvolved in enrollment or outcome assessment, generated the randomization sequence using a computer-based method. To minimize bias, both the study drug and placebo were administered on the same schedule (every 8 hours), in identical volumes, and in indistinguishable containers labeled solely with the study code. The ophthalmologist responsible for retinal examinations and ROP staging, as well as personnel responsible for data entry and statistical analysis, were blinded to group allocation. Unblinding was permitted only when clinically necessary to ensure patient safety. The ophthalmologist who performed the retinal examinations and ROP staging did not know which group each patient was in. The retinal assessment forms and medical records given to the examiner did not show the treatment assignment.

Intervention

Infants in the intervention group diagnosed with ROP received oral propranolol at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg every 8 hours until retinal vascular maturation or hospital discharge. The control group was administered a placebo (distilled water) following the same schedule and dosage. Retinal examinations and disease staging were conducted by an ophthalmologist with expertise in ROP, in accordance with the national ROP protocol. In both groups, if disease progression occurred or standard treatment became necessary, the study medication was discontinued, and the infant was withdrawn from the study and managed according to standard clinical practice. The reason and timing of withdrawal were documented.

Screening and follow-up for ROP were conducted in accordance with the most recent national guidelines. Infants with a GA below 30 weeks or a birth weight under 1500 g underwent screening. Those with a GA above 30 weeks or a birth weight between 1500 and 2000 g were examined to determine if they were clinically unstable or deemed at risk by the treating physician. The initial ophthalmic examination occurred between 28 and 63 days of life, depending on GA at birth. For instance, infants born at 27 weeks of gestation or more were examined at 28 days, while those born at 22 weeks were examined at 63 days. The affected zone and stage were classified according to the definitions in Table 1. After the first examination, follow-up visits were scheduled regularly based on the stage and zone involved, as well as the ophthalmologist’s judgment. Infants were generally re-examined weekly. Moreover, retinal vascular maturity was defined as peripheral vessels reaching Zone 3 on indirect examination or wide-field imaging, meeting the International Classification of Retinopathy of Prematurity criteria for Zone 3. This criterion includes nasal vessels extending to the ora serrata and the absence of ROP within 2 hours of nasalization, with no active ROP or requirement for treatment, as confirmed and documented by the ophthalmologist[19]. The endpoint date was the first examination showing vascular maturation. If an infant was discharged before vascular completion was confirmed, post-discharge examinations continued using the same protocol until the outcome was recorded. Discharge did not interrupt follow-up for the primary outcome. An experienced ophthalmologist performed all ROP examinations and staging; therefore, inter-observer variability was not an issue in this study. To reduce observer error, zone/stage recording was performed in accordance with standard criteria and national protocols.

The primary vital signs and metabolic safety were monitored throughout the intervention. Heart rate and blood pressure were measured using standard neonatal equipment at set times: baseline, 1 hour after medication administration, and every 3 hours thereafter. Blood glucose was checked every 24 hours. Safety outcomes included hypoglycemia (blood glucose <40 mg/dL), bradycardia (heart rate <100 beats/min), and hypotension (sustained systolic blood pressure <60 mmHg), all recorded quantitatively. If any safety threshold was reached, the study medication was stopped, the infant was reassessed, and appropriate care was initiated in accordance with Neonatal Intensive Care Unit standards.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed and evaluated using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software version 17. Descriptive indices, such as mean (SD) and frequency (percentage), were used to display quantitative and qualitative parameters, respectively. Additionally, tables and graphs were used to present data more effectively, as needed. Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare the two study groups (those receiving propranolol and those not receiving propranolol) with respect to gender and ROP stage. Furthermore, all quantitative parameters were found to be normally distributed according to the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test; therefore, an independent t-test was also used to compare these parameters between the two study groups. A significance level of less than 0.05 was considered. It is notable that the primary outcome (time to retinal vascular maturation) was calculated as the number of days from treatment initiation to the first examination documenting vascular maturation and was compared between groups using an independent t-test.

Results

Participants

In this study, 66 infants with ROP were divided into two groups of 33: one receiving propranolol and the other not receiving propranolol. The gender distribution of the two groups of infants with ROP, with and without oral propranolol treatment, is presented. Overall, 61 infants were included in the two groups, with 31 in the propranolol group and 30 in the non-propranolol group. Among these, 49.2% were male. Specifically, 51.6% of the infants in the propranolol group and 46.7% in the non-propranolol group were male. According to the results of Fisher’s exact statistical test, there was no significant difference in gender distribution between the two groups (P = 0.80).

Frequency distribution of ROP stage and severity

As shown in Table 2, the frequency distribution of the stage and severity of ROP in the studied infants, categorized by study group, is presented. Among the 61 infants studied, the majority of ROP cases were in Stage 0/Zone 2 (41 out of 61 cases, 67.2%), with 61.3% in the propranolol group and 73.3% in the non-propranolol group. The frequency of ROP in Stage 0/Zone 3 and Stage 1/Zone 2 was 14.8% and 16.4%, respectively. In the propranolol group, these frequencies were 12.9% and 22.6%, compared to 16.7% and 10% in the non-propranolol group. According to the chi-square test, there was no significant difference in the distribution of ROP severity and zone between the two study groups (P = 0.40).

Duration of complete vascularization

As shown in Table 2, the distribution of the duration of complete vascularization in the studied infants, categorized by study group, is presented. On average, the duration of vascularization for all infants studied (61 cases) was 83.1 (SD = 12.1) days, with a range of 50–115 days. However, according to the independent t-test, the duration of vascularization was significantly shorter in the propranolol-receiving group [76.9 (SD = 11.06) days] compared to the non-propranolol group [89.6 (SD = 9.6) days) (P < 0.001].

Comparison of clinical parameters related to propranolol side effects

As shown in Table 2, the comparison of some clinical parameters related to the occurrence of propranolol side effects in infants, categorized by study group, is presented. The mean values of vital parameters, including respiratory rate per minute, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and blood glucose levels, were all lower in the propranolol-receiving group compared to the non-propranolol group. However, according to the independent t-test, none of these differences were statistically significant (all P > 0.05). The mean heart rate of infants in the propranolol-receiving group and the non-propranolol group also did not show a significant difference (P > 0.05).

Frequency of side effects: hypoglycemia, hypotension, and bradycardia

As shown in Table 2, the frequency of side effects related to hypoglycemia, hypotension, and bradycardia in infants treated with propranolol and the non-propranolol group is presented. Among all the studied infants, no cases of hypoglycemia (defined as blood glucose <40 mg/dL) were recorded. Additionally, the respiratory rate in none of the studied infants was recorded as being less than 40 breaths per minute. Regarding systolic blood pressure, the frequency of hypotension was higher in the propranolol group (45.1%) compared to the non-propranolol group (26.7%), but this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.18).

Discussion

Despite significant advancements in neonatal care, ROP remains one of the leading causes of blindness and visual impairment in children, both in developing and developed countries worldwide[20]. Although new therapeutic approaches have improved the prognosis for infants with ROP, unfavorable structural and functional outcomes remain prevalent. Recently, propranolol has been reported as an effective drug in reducing the progression of ROP[21]. However, few studies have examined the side effects and efficacy of oral propranolol, particularly at the national level. In the present study, we examined infants with ROP who were treated with propranolol at neonatal care centers in Ardabil, Iran, aiming to evaluate the side effects and clinical profile of the treatment.

In this study, 66 infants were divided into two groups of 33: one group received propranolol, and the other did not. Two infants in the propranolol group and three in the non-propranolol group were excluded due to disease progression and the need for further treatment. Baseline and demographic characteristics showed no significant differences between the two groups in terms of GA, sex, or birth weight. The study found that the duration of vascularization was significantly shorter in the propranolol group compared to the control group. Additionally, no cases of hypoglycemia were observed in the propranolol group, and although hypotension was more frequently recorded in the propranolol group, the difference was not statistically significant.

Mohammadi et al conducted a controlled clinical trial in Kermanshah, Iran, investigating the preventive effects of oral propranolol on ROP. Their findings were consistent with our study, showing that the duration of vascularization was significantly shorter in infants receiving oral propranolol compared to the control group[22]. Similarly, a clinical trial by Filippi et al evaluated the safety and efficacy of oral propranolol in preterm infants with early-stage ROP. They administered propranolol at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg every 6 hours and found that the incidence of Stage 3 ROP or Stage 3 plus disease was lower in the propranolol group. Additionally, the need for laser therapy and anti-VEGF treatment was approximately halved in the propranolol group. However, unlike our study, Filippi et al reported bradycardia as a side effect in 19.2% of infants treated with propranolol[23].

In a randomized controlled trial by Makhoul et al, 20 preterm infants with ROP (Stage 1 or 2), a GA of 24–28 weeks, and a birth weight below 1500 g were studied. The need for laser therapy and the progression of ROP severity were lower in the propranolol group, but the difference was not statistically significant, possibly due to the small sample size[24]. Conversely, a clinical trial by Ozturk et al involving 126 preterm infants found that oral propranolol did not reduce the risk of ROP in Stages 0 and 1, which contrasts with our findings. However, the study suggested that propranolol could be effective in treating Stage 2 ROP. The researchers attributed the differential efficacy of propranolol in various stages of ROP to the greater hypoxia in retinal tissue during Stage 2, making beta-blockers more effective for these tissues[25].

A recent meta-analysis by Stritzk et al analyzed six studies involving 461 infants and concluded that oral propranolol significantly and effectively prevents severe ROP in preterm infants. The meta-analysis found that progression to Stage 2 or higher, the incidence of plus disease, and the need for laser or anti-VEGF therapy were significantly reduced in infants receiving oral propranolol[26]. Similarly, Mirjalili et al reported a higher rate of ROP improvement in the propranolol group (81.34%) compared to the control group (66.77%), although the difference was not statistically significant[27]. Laboratory studies on mice have shown that propranolol selectively antagonizes VEGF in hypoxic retinal regions without affecting VEGF production in normoxic areas[28].

Propranolol is generally considered safe and well-tolerated in children. However, potential side effects, which are usually mild and transient, include bronchospasm, heart failure, prolonged hypoglycemia, bradycardia, and heart block[29]. These issues have also been reported in neonates[30] and may lead to more unstable clinical conditions in preterm infants. Preclinical studies in mice have shown promising results with limited side effects. For instance, Fillipi et al reported no significant adverse effects with a dose of 2 mg/kg/day of propranolol in mice[31]. Although oral propranolol is not Food and Drug Administration-approved for ROP, it has been widely used to treat infantile hemangiomas at doses of 0.5–1 mg/kg/day. Fu et al conducted a retrospective study on infants treated with oral propranolol for hemangiomas. They found that the observed side effects were mostly mild, with only three patients requiring a 7-day break for severe diarrhea before resuming treatment. The study concluded that propranolol is safe and well-tolerated in appropriately selected infants with hemangiomas, with no serious adverse effects reported[32].

Another study by Ji et al prospectively monitored vital signs and side effects of oral propranolol in infants. The results were consistent with our findings, showing a significant reduction in heart rate and systolic blood pressure after oral propranolol administration, but no clinical symptoms were observed[33]. In our study, systolic and diastolic blood pressures were lower in the propranolol group, although the differences were not statistically significant. Additionally, systolic blood pressure below 60 mmHg was recorded in 45.1% of infants in the propranolol group, compared to 26.7% in the non-propranolol group. However, no statistically significant difference in blood pressure–related side effects was observed between the two groups, and no acute clinical complications were reported. Similarly, Gwinup et al found that the mean heart rate and systolic blood pressure decreased significantly before and after propranolol administration, whereas the mean diastolic blood pressure did not decrease significantly. Despite these changes, all infants remained clinically asymptomatic[34]. Fu et al also reported an overall reduction in heart rate following propranolol administration, with no cases of arrhythmia or heart block[32].

Symptomatic hypoglycemia, although rare, is a potentially severe and dangerous side effect of oral propranolol. The exact mechanism by which propranolol affects blood glucose levels is not fully understood. However, propranolol can block catecholamine-induced lipolysis, glycogenolysis, and gluconeogenesis, which may lead to hypoglycemia in infants. Younger infants typically have lower glycogen reserves and rely more on caregivers for feeding. In the study by Fu et al, despite careful monitoring and regular feeding by parents, evidence of hypoglycemia was observed during follow-up. While no significant changes in blood glucose levels were noted during the first 3 days of propranolol administration, a significant trend toward hypoglycemia was observed over time, suggesting that the longer the duration of propranolol use, the higher the risk of hypoglycemia[32]. Therefore, educating parents about medication schedules and frequent feeding is crucial to prevent severe hypoglycemia during propranolol treatment.

Limitations

The study included a relatively small sample size of 61 infants, divided into two groups. While this sample size was sufficient to detect some differences, it may not be large enough to generalize the findings to a broader population. Larger studies are needed to confirm the results and ensure statistical power. This study was conducted at two centers in Ardabil, Iran. The findings may not be representative of other regions or populations with different demographic and clinical characteristics. Multi-center studies would provide more comprehensive and generalizable results. The study may have had a limited follow-up period, which could affect the ability to observe long-term outcomes and side effects of propranolol treatment. Longer follow-up periods are necessary to assess the sustained efficacy and safety of propranolol in the management of ROP.

Implications for healthcare workers

The study suggests that oral propranolol may be effective in reducing the progression of ROP, particularly by improving vascularization and potentially reducing the need for invasive treatments such as laser therapy or anti-VEGF injections. Healthcare workers should consider propranolol as a potential adjunct therapy in the management of ROP, especially in cases where traditional treatments are not feasible or effective. The management of ROP with propranolol requires a multidisciplinary approach involving neonatologists, ophthalmologists, and nurses. Regular communication and coordination among healthcare providers are essential to ensure optimal outcomes.

Implications for future research

Healthcare workers should stay informed about ongoing research and clinical trials related to propranolol and ROP. As more evidence becomes available, treatment protocols may need to be updated to reflect the latest findings. Healthcare workers in neonatal and pediatric care settings can contribute to the growing body of evidence by participating in or supporting clinical trials and studies on propranolol for ROP. This will help establish standardized guidelines for its use.

Conclusion

The results of the present study indicate that oral propranolol in infants with ROP significantly reduced the time required for vascularization, a parameter considered to accelerate recovery and reduce the need for additional treatments. Furthermore, oral propranolol at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg of body weight every 8 hours was well-tolerated in infants and was not associated with serious or dangerous side effects. Specifically, no cases of hypoglycemia or bradypnea were observed, and only asymptomatic cases of hypotension were recorded.
