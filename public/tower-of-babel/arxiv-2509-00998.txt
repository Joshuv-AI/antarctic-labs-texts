Chapter 1
Introduction

1.1.
Purpose and goals

This manuscript is about abelian varieties that are Jacobians of curves.
I started writing it for a lecture series at the Arizona Winter School in 2024 on abelian varieties.
A longer more descriptive title might be

The Torelli locus in the moduli space of abelian varieties, with
applications to Newton polygons of curves in positive characteristic.

To elaborate, this manuscript covers two topics:
the first is about the
geometry
of the Torelli locus;
the second is about the
arithmetic
invariants of
abelian varieties that occur for Jacobians of smooth curves in positive characteristic.

The main content is about (families of) abelian varieties,
with a focus on those with special properties, including
abelian varieties that are Jacobians of curves,
that have many automorphisms, or that have unusual Newton polygons in positive characteristic.
Throughout, there is a consistent interplay between the themes of arithmetic and geometry.
The dimension of an abelian variety places constraints on its arithmetic invariants; conversely,
arithmetic invariants of an abelian variety place constraints on its geometry.

Jacobians of curves can often be studied in a more explicit and concrete way than one can study a typical abelian variety.
On the other hand, there are techniques for studying (families of) abelian varieties that do not apply when studying (families of) Jacobians of curves. This leads to a valuable and rewarding exchange between these topics.
Furthermore, we can study moduli spaces of abelian varieties (or moduli spaces of curves).
These moduli spaces can be refined by adding additional structure. They
can also be stratified by a given invariant, where the points of a stratum represent abelian varieties (or curves)
having that invariant.

One of my goals is to provide a broad perspective within this document.
For simplicity, this manuscript restricts to working over an algebraically closed field,
but includes material both over
\mathbb{C}
(characteristic
0
) and
over
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
(characteristic
p
).
I include a diverse range of open questions, some of which are well-known goals
within the arithmetic geometry community, and some of which emerged from my own research program.
Another goal is to cover advanced material in a grounded way.
The purpose of the examples is to make the concepts and techniques more accessible.
More details about the technical background can be found in the provided references, which in turn connect to
many other important papers on these topics.
I hope that this manuscript is both broad and accessible enough to encourage many people
to work on these captivating open questions.

1.2.2.
Chapter
2
: Abelian varieties and Jacobians of curves

In this chapter, we provide a brief background about abelian varieties and Jacobians of curves.
We give examples of several types of abelian varieties and curves which play an important role in this manuscript.

Let
g
be a positive integer.
Suppose
C
is a (smooth, projective, irreducible) curve of genus
g
.
The Jacobian
\mathrm{Jac}(C)
of
C
is the moduli space of line bundles of degree
0
on
C
.
Its group of points is isomorphic to the quotient of the group of divisors of degree zero of
C
by the subgroup of principal divisors.
Many attributes of
C
can be characterized by its Jacobian.
An important theorem is that the Jacobian
\mathrm{Jac}(C)
is a principally polarized (p.p.) abelian variety of dimension
g
.

For
1\leq g\leq 3
, almost every principally polarized abelian variety is a Jacobian.
For example, a p.p. abelian variety of dimension
g=1
is an elliptic curve.
A p.p. abelian surface (resp. threefold) is the Jacobian of a smooth curve of genus
2
(resp. genus
3
)
unless it decomposes as a product, together with the product polarization.
For
g\geq 4
, the situation is very interesting because not every p.p. abelian variety is a Jacobian.

We end this chapter with several open variations of the Schottky problem
about whether certain types of abelian varieties are Jacobians of curves.

1.2.3.
Chapter
3
: The
p
-torsion invariants

Suppose
\mathrm{char}(k)=p
. In this chapter, we explain several generalizations of the distinction between
ordinary and supersingular elliptic curves.

An elliptic curve
E
over
k
is
ordinary
if it has a point of order
p
;
equivalently,
E
is ordinary if its Newton polygon has slopes of zero and one.
Otherwise,
E
is
supersingular
.
There are many results about ordinary and supersingular elliptic curves,
due to Hasse
[
Has35
]
, Deuring
[
Deu41
]
, and Igusa
[
Igu58
]
.
For a fixed prime
p
, almost all elliptic curves are ordinary and the number of
isomorphism classes of supersingular elliptic curves is approximately
p/12
.

Suppose
X
is a p.p. abelian variety
X
defined over
k
. To classify the action of Frobenius,
there are combinatorial invariants called the
p
-rank, the Newton polygon,
the Ekedahl–Oort type, and the
a
-number.
In this document, we call these the
p
-torsion invariants
.

The
p
-rank is the integer
f
such that the number of
p
-torsion points on
X
equals
p^{f}
.
The Newton polygon is determined by the characteristic polynomial of Frobenius
on the crystalline cohomology.
The Ekedahl–Oort type
classifies the structure of the
p
-torsion group scheme
X[p]
of
X
, along with the perfect pairing
X[p]\times X[p]\to\mu_{p}
.
The
a
-number is the number of generators of the local-local part of
X[p]
as a module under
F
and
V
.

For every positive integer
g
, the possibilities for the
p
-torsion invariants of a p.p abelian variety of dimension
g
are well understood.
We briefly include one question about the interaction between the Newton polygon and Ekedahl–Oort type,
and about purity of the stratification by Ekedahl–Oort type.

1.2.4.
Chapter
4
: Existence of curves with given
p
-torsion invariants

Suppose
\mathrm{char}(k)=p
.
For most integers
g
and primes
p
,
it is not known which
a
-numbers, Newton polygons, and Ekedahl–Oort types occur for
Jacobians of smooth curves of genus
g
over a field of characteristic
p
.

The
p
-torsion invariants of a curve are defined to be that of its Jacobian.
For a curve
C
defined over a finite field
\mathbb{F}
, its Newton polygon
keeps track of the number of points on
C
defined over finite extensions of
\mathbb{F}
.
The Ekedahl–Oort type of
C
determines, and is determined by,
the structure of the de Rham cohomology of
C

as a module under Frobenius
F
and Verschiebung
V
, plus the perfect pairing.
We explain some methods to compute the
p
-torsion invariants for Jacobians of curves.
These involve computations of the Frobenius action on various cohomology groups.

Some Newton polygons and Ekedahl–Oort types have been shown to occur for Jacobians and
a few Ekedahl–Oort types have been ruled out.
Historically, some of the first results in this area were, for every prime
p
:
(i) for every
g\geq 2
, there exists an ordinary smooth curve of genus
g

[
Mil72
]
,
and a non-ordinary smooth curve of genus
g
,
and (ii) there exists a supersingular curve of genus
2

[
Ser83
]
, or
[
IKO86
]
.

We also describe existence results for Newton polygons and Ekedahl–Oort types for curves of small genus
g=2,3,4
.
In particular, Kudo, Harashita, and Senda proved there exists a supersingular smooth curve of genus
4
for all primes
p

[
KHS20
]
.
We describe recent progress for
g=5
.
Much more is known for Artin–Schreier curves;
we cover some of the (cohomological and computational) results for Artin–Schreier curves.

We highlight Question
4.23
about whether there exists a supersingular smooth curve
for every genus
g
and characteristic
p
. There are also open questions about the number of
curves over finite fields with specified
p
-torsion invariants.

1.2.5.
Chapter
5
: Moduli spaces and the boundary

For
g\geq 1
, we give some background on the moduli space
\mathcal{A}_{g}
of p.p. abelian varieties of dimension
g
, and
the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g}
of smooth curves of genus
g
.

The Torelli morphism
\tau_{g}:\mathcal{M}_{g}\to\mathcal{A}_{g}
takes a curve
C
to its Jacobian
\mathrm{Jac}(C)
.
It is injective on
k
-points, meaning that a curve
C
over
k
is uniquely determined by
\mathrm{Jac}(C)
.
The
open Torelli locus

\mathcal{T}^{\circ}_{g}
is the image of
\tau_{g}
;
its points represent p.p. abelian varieties of dimension
g
that are Jacobians of smooth curves.

A primary goal is to characterize the open Torelli locus
\mathcal{T}^{\circ}_{g}
within
\mathcal{A}_{g}
.
For
g\geq 2
, the dimension of
\mathcal{M}_{g}
is
3g-3
, while the dimension of
\mathcal{A}_{g}
is
g(g+1)/2
.
When
g=1,2,3
, then
\mathcal{T}^{\circ}_{g}
is open and dense in
\mathcal{A}_{g}
,
reaffirming that almost every p.p. abelian variety of dimension
g\leq 3
is a Jacobian of a smooth curve.
For
g\geq 4
, the codimension of
\mathcal{T}^{\circ}_{g}
in
\mathcal{A}_{g}
is positive, and
this codimension increases with
g
.

Surprisingly, some facts about smooth curves can be proven using singular curves;
some facts about p.p. abelian varieties that are indecomposable can be proven using
p.p. abelian varieties that decompose.
For this reason, it is valuable to consider compactifications of moduli spaces, namely
the Deligne–Mumford compactification
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}
of
\mathcal{M}_{g}

and a toroidal compactification
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{g}
of
\mathcal{A}_{g}
.
The points of the boundary of
\mathcal{A}_{g}
represent semi-abelian varieties having positive toric rank.
The points of the boundary of
\mathcal{M}_{g}
represent stable singular curves.

A stable curve has
compact type
when its irreducible components are smooth and the dual graph
of the irreducible components is a tree.
The Jacobian of a singular curve of compact type is an abelian variety, although it does decompose
together with the product polarization.

The
closed Torelli locus

\mathcal{T}_{g}
is the closure of
\mathcal{T}^{\circ}_{g}
in
\mathcal{A}_{g}
;
its points represent p.p. abelian varieties of dimension
g
that are Jacobians of stable curves of compact type.
The moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g}^{ct}
represents stable curves of genus
g
of compact type.
The Torelli map extends to a map
\tau_{g}:\mathcal{M}^{ct}_{g}\to\mathcal{A}_{g}
, whose image is
\mathcal{T}_{g}
.
However,
\tau_{g}
is not injective on the boundary components of
\mathcal{M}_{g}^{ct}
.

This chapter ends with results and open questions about
complete subspaces of these moduli spaces.
For example, a result of Diaz
[
Dia84
]
, reproved by Looijenga
[
Loo95
]
in positive characteristic,
states that a complete subspace
W\subset\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}
having codimension
at most
g
must intersect the boundary.

1.2.6.
Chapter
6
: Intersection of the Torelli locus with
p
-torsion strata

Suppose
\mathrm{char}(k)=p
.
We provide some background on
the stratifications of
\mathcal{A}_{g}
by the
p
-torsion invariants.
These stratifications are well understood, in large part
because of work of Katz
[
Kat79
]
and the purity theorem of de Jong and Oort
[
dJO00
]
.
Many of the open questions in this manuscript for positive characteristic
can be rephrased as questions about the intersection of the
p
-torsion strata of
\mathcal{A}_{g}
with
the open Torelli locus of Jacobians of curves.

More precisely, given a
p
-torsion invariant
\xi
, let
\mathcal{A}_{g}[\xi]\subset\mathcal{A}_{g}
denote the stratum of p.p. abelian varieties that have
p
-torsion invariant
\xi
, with the reduced induced structure.
In most cases, it is not understood how
\mathcal{A}_{g}[\xi]
intersects
\mathcal{T}^{\circ}_{g}
,
or even whether they intersect at all. If the intersection is non-trivial, one can ask more refined questions about the geometry
of the intersection, starting with its dimension, as in Question
6.1
.

Consider the supersingular locus
\mathcal{A}_{g}[\sigma_{g}]
.
Using the geometry of
\mathcal{A}_{3}[\sigma_{3}]
,
Oort proved that there exists a supersingular curve of genus
3
for every prime
p

[
Oor91
]
.
For
g=4
, we describe a geometric proof that
\mathcal{A}_{4}[\sigma_{4}]\cap\mathcal{T}^{\circ}_{4}
is non-empty
[
Pri25
]
;
as an application, for every prime
p
,
this proves that there exists a supersingular curve of genus
4
.
This proof does not extend to curves of higher genus.

In this chapter, we also
review a theorem of Faber and Van der Geer about the dimensions of the
p
-rank strata in
\mathcal{M}_{g}

[
FvdG04
]
.
Furthermore, we discuss an inductive technique which sometimes yields new results about
the existence of curves with given Newton polygon or Ekedahl–Oort type
[
Pri19
]
.

We end the chapter with some open questions about the number of irreducible components of the
p
-rank strata in
\mathcal{M}_{g}

and mass formulas for non-ordinary curves.

1.2.7.
Chapter
7
: Curves and abelian varieties with cyclic actions

In this chapter, we discuss curves and abelian varieties that have an action by a non-trivial
cyclic group. Specifically, we consider cyclic degree
m
covers of the projective line, which are classified by
discrete invariants, namely the degree, the number of branch points, and the inertia type.
The Jacobians of these curves are p.p. abelian varieties with an action of
\mu_{m}
of a given signature, which is the information
of the dimensions for the eigenspaces of the
\mu_{m}
-action on the regular
1
-forms.

Families of covers of this type are represented by moduli spaces called Hurwitz spaces.
These Hurwitz spaces map to Deligne–Mostow Shimura varieties, whose points represent p.p. abelian varieties having
a
\mu_{m}
-action with this signature.
A family of covers is
special
when the image of the Hurwitz space is open and dense in (a component of) the Shimura variety.

The Legendre family of elliptic curves is one example of a special family, and
the moduli space of hyperelliptic curves of genus
2
is another.
The complete list
of families of cyclic covers of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
that have special moduli spaces was found by Moonen
[
Moo10
]
.
Curves in special families are often easier to analyze because of the additional techniques available on the
Shimura variety.

We end the chapter with open questions about the Coleman–Oort conjecture.

1.2.8.
Chapter
8
: Newton polygons and cyclic actions

Let
\mathrm{char}(k)=p
.
In this chapter, we discuss the Newton polygons of abelian varieties (and curves)
having a cyclic action as in Chapter
7
.

Historically, this material generalizes earlier results for Jacobians that have complex multiplication.
Many people computed Newton polygons of Jacobians of (quotients of) Fermat curves.
For these Jacobians, there are several methods to determine the Newton polygon, including computing the action of
Frobenius or using the Shimura–Taniyama formula.

Generalizing the complex multiplication situation, we discuss the Newton polygons of abelian varieties (and curves)
having a cyclic action as in Chapter
7
. There are restrictions on which Newton polygons occur in this
context which were studied by Kottwitz and others. The first of these arises from the action of Frobenius on the
eigenspaces for the
\mu_{m}
-action.

The Newton polygon stratification is easier to study on Hurwitz spaces which are special.
As an application, we show there exist supersingular curves of genus
5
,
6
, and
7
,
under certain (new) congruence conditions on the prime
p

[
LMPT19a
]
.

We end the chapter with some questions that are likely more tractable.
Specifically, these are questions about supersingular curves in special families
\mathcal{F}

of cyclic covers of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
,
including the field of definition of the supersingular curves when
\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{F})=1

and the geometry of the supersingular locus when
\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{F})=2
.

1.2.9.
Chapter
9
: Torsion points and monodromy

This chapter is about the
\ell
-adic monodromy groups of families of abelian varieties and curves,
for a prime
\ell\not=\mathrm{char}(k)
.
For a curve
C
of genus
g
, there is a connection
between the
\ell^{2g}
points on
\mathrm{Jac}(C)
that are
\ell
-torsion points
and the unramified
\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}
-covers of
C
.

Consider a family of abelian varieties
X\to W
of (relative) dimension
g
, with
W
irreducible.
Let
w\in W
be a chosen basepoint.
Then the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(W,w)
of the base acts on
the
\ell
-torsion of the abelian variety
X_{w}
, the fiber of
X\to W
over
w
. The
\ell
-adic monodromy group measures the image of
the associated representation of
\pi_{1}(W,w)
in the symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}_{\ell})
.

In this chapter, we highlight a result of Chai
[
Cha05
]
:
for a prime
\ell\not=p
, it states that the
\ell
-adic monodromy group is big if
W\subset\mathcal{A}_{g}

is an irreducible subspace stable under all
\ell
-adic Hecke correspondences such that
W
is not contained in the supersingular locus.
We discuss a related result about the
\ell
-adic monodromy groups
of the
p
-rank strata of
\mathcal{M}_{g}

[
AP08
]
.
This proves that the
p
-torsion and the
\ell
-torsion on Jacobians are independent of each other,
in a way that can be made precise and which has applications for the the generic behavior of curves with a given
p
-rank.

We end the chapter with some open questions about the
a
-number of a generic curve of
p
-rank
0
and about the
monodromy of the stratum of curves with
p
-rank
0
and
a
-number
a\geq 2
.

2.1.
Overview: Jacobians provide good examples of abelian varieties

There are some situations where abelian varieties can be constructed in a straight-forward way.
For example, in Section
2.2
, we give a brief description of elliptic curves, which
are abelian varieties of dimension
1
.
In Section
2.3
, we give a brief description of
complex abelian varieties through the lens of complex tori.

However, it can be complicated to study abelian varieties in many situations.
Jacobians of curves provide examples of abelian varieties which are often easier to analyze.
We provide some background on curves in Section
2.4
, with
numerous examples included in Section
2.4.4
.
Then we define the Jacobian of a (smooth, projective, irreducible) curve in Section
2.5
.
The main result is Theorem
2.34
, which states that
the Jacobian of a curve of genus
g
is a principally polarized abelian variety of dimension
g
.
This material is the foundation for the Torelli map.

As mentioned before, for
g\geq 4
, most abelian varieties of dimension
g
are not Jacobians of curves.
This leads to a fruitful exchange between the topics of abelian varieties and curves, where
techniques available for one topic are leveraged to study the other.

2.2.
Elliptic curves

We keep this section brief because there are excellent references about elliptic curves, including
textbooks by Husemöller
[
Hus04
]
and Silverman
[
Sil09
]
.

An elliptic curve
E
is a (smooth, projective, irreducible) curve of genus one, together with a chosen point
\mathcal{O}_{E}
.
It follows that
E
is an abelian group, with identity
\mathcal{O}_{E}
,
where the addition of points is defined algebraically.
Every elliptic curve can be embedded as a smooth cubic in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
.

Example 2.3
.

Suppose
p=\mathrm{char}(k)\not=2,3
.
After changing coordinates,
E
can be described as
the projective curve in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
given by the vanishing of the homogenous equation

Y^{2}Z=X^{3}+aXZ^{2}+bZ^{3}
, for some
a,b\in k
such that
4a^{3}+27b^{2}\not=0
,
and
\mathcal{O}_{E}
is the point
[X:Y:Z]=[0:1:0]
. Given a line in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, it intersects

in three points (counting with multiplicity)
and these three points sum to the identity in the group law of
E
.
More simply, we can describe
E
by the affine equation
y^{2}=f(x)
where

f(x)=x^{3}+ax+b
for some
a,b\in k
such that
f(x)
has distinct roots.

Over
\mathbb{C}
, an elliptic curve is a compact complex torus of dimension
1
.

Example 2.4
.

A compact complex torus of dimension
1
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
where
\Lambda
is a lattice.
After adjusting by the action of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
, we can suppose
\Lambda
is generated by
1
and
\tau
,
where
\tau
is in the upper half plane
\mathfrak{h}
.

The
j
-invariant of
E:y^{2}=x^{3}+ax+b
is
j=1728\cdot 4a^{3}/(4a^{3}+27b^{2})
.
The
j
-invariant
j(\tau)
of
E=\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
is a modular function of weight
0
for the special linear group

\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Two elliptic curves over
k
are isomorphic if and only if they have the same
j
-invariant.

Given two lattices
\Lambda_{i}
generated by
1
and
\tau_{i}
with
\tau_{i}\in\mathfrak{h}
for
i=1,2
,
the two complex tori
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{1}
and
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{2}
have

j(\tau_{1})=j(\tau_{2})
exactly when

\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
are in the same orbit of the action of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})

on
\mathfrak{h}
by fractional linear transformations.

The
j
-invariant can be viewed as the parameter for the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
of elliptic curves, or
the moduli space
\mathcal{A}_{1}
of p.p. abelian varieties of dimension
1
, both of which have dimension
1
.
The book by Katz and Mazur
[
KM85
]
provides more information about
moduli spaces of elliptic curves.

Remark 2.6
.

Elliptic curves are abelian varieties of dimension
1
.
Substantially more work is required to study abelian varieties of the next smallest dimension, namely dimension
2
.
From an algebraic perspective, one can consider Jacobians of curves of genus
2
.
These curves are hyperelliptic;
for
p\not=2
, each has an affine equation of the form
y^{2}=f(x)
, where
f(x)\in k[x]
is a separable polynomial
of degree
5
or
6
.
The isomorphism class of a genus two curve is determined by its three Igusa invariants.
However, it takes some additional work to construct the Jacobian.
Over
\mathbb{C}
, an abelian surface can be constructed as
\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Lambda
, where the lattice
\Lambda
has
additional constraints coming from the principal polarization.

2.3.
Complex abelian varieties

In this section, we provide the complex analytic viewpoint on abelian varieties.
We keep this section brief because there are excellent references about abelian varieties over
\mathbb{C}
, including
Swinnerton-Dyer
[
SD74
]
,
Birkenhake–Lange
[
BL04
]
,
Debarre
[
Deb05
]
, and Lange
[
Lan23
]
.

In this section, we work over
k=\mathbb{C}
.
We denote complex conjugation with an overline.
Let
g\geq 1
be an integer.

2.3.1.
Complex tori

Suppose
X=V/\Lambda
is a compact complex torus,
where
V
is a complex vector space of dimension
g
and
\Lambda
is a lattice in
V
.
Then
X
is a complex analytic space of dimension
g
.

Consider a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{2g}
for
\Lambda
and a basis

e_{1},\ldots,e_{g}
for
V
. Writing the former in terms of the latter gives a
g\times 2g
-matrix
\Pi
called the

period matrix
.

Proposition 2.7
.

[
BL04
, Proposition 1.1.2]

A
g\times 2g
-matrix
\Pi
is the period matrix of a
complex torus if and only if the
2g\times 2g
-matrix
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Pi\\
\overline{\Pi}\end{array}\right)
is invertible.

2.3.2.
Complex abelian varieties

A complex torus is an abelian variety if and only if it is an algebraic variety.
This is equivalent to the condition that it has an ample line bundle
\mathcal{L}
,
or equivalently that it can be embedded in projective space.
This condition can be described in several different ways.
First, here are the Riemann relations.

Theorem 2.8
.

[
BL04
, Theorem 4.2.1]

The complex torus
\mathbb{C}^{g}/\Pi\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
is an abelian variety if and only if
there exists a non-degenerate
2g\times 2g
alternating matrix
A
such that the following

Riemann relations
are true:

(i)
\Pi(A^{-1})^{T}\Pi=0
; and
(ii)
i\Pi(A^{-1})^{T}\overline{\Pi}>0
.

The second description involves Hermitian forms.
A
Hermitian form
on
V
is a map
H:V\times V\to\mathbb{C}
which is
\mathbb{C}
-linear in the first argument
and such that
H(v,w)=\overline{H(w,v)}
for all
v,w\in V
.
A Hermitian form is
positive semi-definite
if
H(v,v)\geq 0
for all
v\in V
;
it is
positive definite
if it is positive semi-definite and
H(v,v)=0
if and only if
v=0
;
it is
non-degenerate
if the condition
H(u,v)=0
for all
v\in V
implies
u=0
.

Example 2.9
.

Continuing with Example
2.4
, for a compact complex torus of dimension one,
the positive definite Hermitian form
H:\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is given by
H(v,w)=v\cdot\overline{w}/\mathrm{Im}(\tau)
.

Definition 2.10
.

A
Riemann form
on
X=V/\Lambda
is a positive definite non-degenerate Hermitian form
H
on
V

such that the restriction of the imaginary part

E:=\mathrm{Im}(H)
to
\Lambda
is integer valued.

In fact,
E
is the first Chern class of
\mathcal{L}
.

Theorem 2.11
.

A complex torus is isomorphic to an abelian variety
X
over
\mathbb{C}

if and only if it has a Riemann form.

We refer to
[
BL04
, Section 2.4, Theorem 2.5.5]
for a third description in terms of a principal polarization.

2.3.3.
The Siegel upper half-space

We follow
[
BL04
, Chapter 8]
.

Suppose
X=V/\Lambda
is a p.p. abelian variety of dimension
g
.
Let
H
be a Hermitian form defining its principal polarization.
Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{g},\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{g}
be a symplectic basis of
\Lambda
for
the alternating form
E:=\mathrm{Im}(H)
;
this means that
E(\lambda_{i},\mu_{j})=\delta_{i,j}
, the Kronecker Delta symbol.
With respect to this basis,
E
is given by the matrix

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&I_{g}\\
-I_{g}&0\end{array}\right)
.
The vectors
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{g}
form a
\mathbb{C}
-basis for
V
.
The period matrix is given by
\Pi=(Z,I_{g})
for some
g\times g
matrix
Z
.

Proposition 2.12
.

[
BL04
, Proposition 8.1.1]

(a)
{}^{T}Z=Z
and
\mathrm{Im}(Z)>0
; and
(b)
(\mathrm{Im}(Z))^{-1}
is the matrix of
H
with respect to the basis
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{g}
.

Note that the matrix
Z
defining
X
is determined by
g(g+1)/2
of its entries.

Definition 2.13
.

The
Siegel upper half-space

\mathfrak{h}_{g}

is the set of
g\times g
complex-valued matrices satisfying

{}^{T}Z=Z
and
\mathrm{Im}(Z)>0
.

We see that
\mathfrak{h}_{g}
has dimension
g(g+1)/2
because it is an open submanifold of
the vector space of symmetric
g\times g
matrices.
By
[
BL04
, Proposition 8.1.2]
,
\mathfrak{h}_{g}
is the moduli
space for principally polarized complex abelian varieties with symplectic basis.
This shows the following.

Theorem 2.14
.

[
BL04
, Theorem 8.2.6]

The moduli space
\mathcal{A}_{g,\mathbb{C}}
of principally polarized complex abelian varieties of dimension
g

is the quotient of
\mathfrak{h}_{g}
by the sympletic group
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
.
Thus it is irreducible and has dimension
g(g+1)/2
.

2.3.4.
Jacobian of a complex curve

Suppose
C
is a (smooth, projective, irreducible) curve over
\mathbb{C}
.
We define the Jacobian of
C
, following
[
ACGH85
, Section 1.3]
and
[
Mir95
, Chapter VIII]
.

Let
H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1})
denote the vector space of holomorphic
1
-forms on
C
.
A
linear functional
on
C
is an element of the dual space
H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1})^{*}
,
namely a linear transformation
H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1})\to\mathbb{C}
.
Loops
c
in
C
can be represented by homology classes.
The homology group
H_{1}(C,\mathbb{Z})
is a free abelian group of rank
2g
.
Every homology class
[c]
defines a linear functional

\int_{[c]}:H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1})\to\mathbb{C}
, which takes a regular
1
-form
\omega
to its integral over
c
.
The linear functionals that occur in this way are called
periods
.
The set
\Lambda
of periods is a subgroup of
H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1})^{*}
.

Definition 2.15
.

The
analytic Jacobian
of
C
is
\mathrm{Jac}(C)=H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1})^{*}/\Lambda
.

After choosing a basis for
H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1})^{*}
, there is an isomorphism
\mathrm{Jac}(C)\cong\mathbb{C}^{g}/\Lambda
,
which is a complex torus of dimension
g
.
One can show that the periods satisfy the Riemann relations.
The curve
C
determines a principal polarization on
\mathrm{Jac}(C)
.
Thus
\mathrm{Jac}(C)
is a principally polarized abelian variety.

The Abel–Jacobi map:
Choose a base point
p_{\circ}
on
C
. For each point
x\in C
,
choose a path
\gamma_{x}
from
p_{\circ}
to
x
.
This is possible because
C
is connected.
There is a map
C\to H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1})^{*}
, sending
x
to the linear functional
\int_{\gamma_{x}}

of integration along
\gamma_{x}
.
This map is not well-defined because different paths from
p_{\circ}
to
x
may not be homotopic.
Taking the quotient by the periods
\Lambda
yields a well-defined map,
still depending on the base point
p_{\circ}
, called the Abel–Jacobi map:
j:C\to\mathrm{Jac}(C)
.

Curves and the genus of curves

Definition 2.16

For a local description of a
1

at

a function on an affine subset
U
of

containing

such that

vanishes with order

at

\omega
has an expression of the

f(t)dt

The order of the zero (or pole) of

at

Example 2.18
.

Consider the
1

2.4.2.
Equations for curves

The easiest way to describe a curve of positive genus is with an affine equation.
Frequently, we consider an affine curve
C^{\prime}\subset{\mathbb{A}}^{2}

given by the vanishing of a polynomial equation
h(x,y)=0
.
It is no loss of generality to work with affine curves because of this fact:

Fact 2.20
.

The smooth completion of a smooth affine algebraic curve
C^{\prime}
is a complete
smooth algebraic curve
C
which contains
C^{\prime}
as an open subset.
Smooth completions exist and are unique over
k
.

Every (smooth, projective, irreducible) curve
C
can be embedded in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.
Sometimes
C
can be embedded in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
and sometimes not.
If not, it is often a hassle to find the blow-ups that resolve the singularities of a planar model of
C
.
In light of Fact
2.20
, we usually work with one affine equation for a curve.

Example 2.21
.

Let
C^{\prime}
be the curve with affine equation
y^{2}=x^{5}-2x
(here
p\not=2,5
).
The homogenization
y^{2}z^{3}=x^{5}-2xz^{4}
yields a curve in
\mathbb{P}^{2}

that has a singularity when
[x:y:z]=[0:1:0]
.
The smooth completion of
C^{\prime}
has another affine patch
C^{\prime\prime}
where this singularity is resolved.
To find it, we define
\bar{x}=1/x
and
\bar{y}=y\bar{x}^{3}
.
Then
C^{\prime\prime}
is given by the affine equation
\bar{y}^{2}=\bar{x}-2\bar{x}^{5}
.
The point
(\bar{x},\bar{y})=(0,0)
is colloquially called the point at infinity on
C^{\prime}
.

2.4.3.
Covers of curves

If
C,D
are curves, a
branched cover

\pi:C\to D
is a finite surjective morphism,
which is generically unramified. The branch locus is the finite set of points
B\subset D
over which
\pi
is ramified.
We frequently drop the word branched from the terminology.

The morphism
\pi
corresponds to an inclusion of the function field
k(D)
in the function field
k(C)
.
The
degree
of
\pi
is the dimension of
k(C)
as a vector space over
k(D)
.
The cover
\pi
is Galois if the finite extension
k(C)/k(D)
is Galois and is
cyclic
if it is Galois with cyclic Galois group.

For
\eta\in C
, let
R_{C,\eta}
be the complete local ring of
C
at
\eta
,
and let
S_{D,\pi(\eta)}
be the complete local ring of
D
at
\pi(\eta)
.
Let
t_{1}
(resp.
t_{2}
) be a uniformizing parameter of
R_{C,\eta}
at
\eta
(resp.
S_{D,\pi(\eta)}
at
\pi(\eta)
).
Let
e_{\eta}
denote the ramification index of
\pi
at
\eta
.
This is the positive integer such that
t_{2}=ut_{1}^{e_{\eta}}
for some
u\in R_{C,\eta}
.
If
\mathrm{char}(k)=p>0
, the cover is
tamely ramified
if
p\nmid e_{\eta}
for all
\eta\in C
,
and is
wildly ramified
otherwise.

The Riemann–Hurwitz formula provides a good way to compute the genus.

Theorem 2.22
.

(Riemann–Hurwitz formula)
Suppose
\pi:C\to D
is a tamely ramified cover of curves of degree
d
.
Then the genus
g_{C}
of
C
and the genus
g_{D}
of
D
are related by the formula:

2g_{C}-2=d(2g_{D}-2)+\sum_{\eta\in C}(e_{\eta}-1).

Finding the orders of poles of a
1
-form is a delicate process.
For a cover of curves, the following lemma is useful.

Lemma 2.23
.

Suppose
\pi:C\to D
is a tamely ramified cover of curves.
If
\omega
is a regular
1
-form on
D
, then the pullback
\pi^{*}\omega
is a regular
1
-form on
C
.
More specifically, if
\eta\in C
, then

\mathrm{ord}_{\eta}(\pi^{*}\omega)=(1+\mathrm{ord}_{\pi(\eta)}(\omega))e_{\eta}-1
.

Proof.

By definition of the ramification index,
t_{2}=ut_{1}^{e_{\eta}}
,
where
t_{1}
(resp.
t_{2}
) is a uniformizing parameter of
C
at
\eta
(resp.
D
at
\pi(\eta)
) as defined above.
Write
n=\mathrm{ord}_{\pi(\eta)}(\omega)
.
So
\omega=u^{\prime}t_{2}^{n}dt_{2}
, where
u^{\prime}
is a unit in the complete local ring
S_{D,\pi(\eta)}
.
So
\pi^{*}\omega=u^{\prime}(ut_{1}^{e_{\eta}})^{n}e_{\eta}ut_{1}^{e_{\eta}-1}
, whose order of vanishing at
\eta
is
ne_{\eta}+e_{\eta}-1
.
∎

2.4.4.
Examples of branched covers of curves

Definition 2.24
.

A
hyperelliptic curve
is a curve
C
that admits a degree two branched cover
\pi:C\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Fact 2.25
.

If
\mathrm{char}(k)\not=2
, a hyperelliptic curve has an affine equation
y^{2}=f(x)
for some separable polynomial
f(x)\in k[x]
.
The hyperelliptic involution
\iota
acts by
\iota((x,y))=(x,-y)
.
There is a unique hyperelliptic involution on a hyperelliptic curve
C
and it is contained in the center of the
automorphism group of
C
.

The next example can be checked using Theorem
2.22
and Lemma
2.23
.

Lemma 2.26
.

Suppose
\mathrm{char}(k)\not=2
.
Suppose
f(x)\in k[x]
is a separable polynomial of degree
2g+1
or
2g+2
.
The hyperelliptic curve
C
with affine equation
y^{2}=f(x)
has genus
g
.
A basis for
H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1})
is given by
\{dx/y,xdx/y,\ldots,x^{g-1}dx/y\}
.

The next example arises from Kummer theory.

Definition 2.27
.

A
superelliptic curve
is a curve
C
that admits a cyclic cover
\pi:C\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
(with degree
m\geq 2
).

Fact 2.28
.

If
\mathrm{char}(k)
does not divide the degree
m
of
\pi
, then
the superelliptic curve
C
has
an affine equation
y^{m}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(x-b_{i})^{a_{i}}
, for some integer
N\geq 2
,
some set of distinct values
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{N}\}
in
k
,
and some
inertia type
which is a tuple
\vec{a}
of integers with

(2.1)

\vec{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}),\ 1\leq a_{i}\leq m-1,\text{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\equiv 0\bmod m.

In this context,
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{N}\}
is the branch locus of
\pi:C\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
If we prefer
\infty
to be one of the branch points (say the last one), we remove the last term
(x-b_{N})^{a_{N}}

from the equation.

The
\mu_{m}
-action on
C
is given by
\phi((x,y))=(x,\zeta y)
for
\zeta\in\mu_{m}
.

Notation 2.29
.

To classify superelliptic curves
\pi:C\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
,
we record the discrete data
\gamma=(m,N,\vec{a})
, where

\pi
has degree
m
and
N
branch points, and
the inertia type
\vec{a}
is the tuple satisfying (
2.1
).

Lemma 2.30
.

With notation as in Fact
2.28
:
Above the point
x=b_{i}
, the curve
C
has
g_{i}=\mathrm{gcd}(m,a_{i})
points, each with
inertia group of order
m/g_{i}
.
By the Riemann–Hurwitz formula, the genus of
C
satisfies:

2g_{C}-2=m(-2)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}g_{i}(\frac{m}{g_{i}}-1).

If
g_{i}=1
for
1\leq i\leq N
(e.g., if
m
is prime), then

g_{C}=(N-2)(m-1)/2
.

The next example arises from Artin–Schreier theory.

Definition 2.31
.

Suppose
\mathrm{char}(k)=p>0
.
An
Artin–Schreier curve
is a curve
C
that admits a degree
p
cyclic cover
\pi:C\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Fact 2.32
.

An Artin–Schreier curve has an affine equation
y^{p}-y=h
for some rational function
h\in k(x)
;
the curve is connected if and only if
h\not=z^{p}-z
for any rational function
z\in k(x)
.

Without loss of generality, we can suppose that the order of each pole of

is relatively prime to
p
.
Then the branch locus of
\pi
is the set of poles of

and
\pi
is wildly ramified above each of these points.

The
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
-action on
C
is generated by the automorphism
\phi((x,y))=(x,y+1)
.

Example 2.33
.

With notation as in Fact
2.32
:
suppose
h\in k[x]
is a polynomial of degree
j
with
p\nmid j
.
Then the genus of

is
g=(p-1)(j-1)/2
.
This can be proven with a wild generalization of the Riemann–Hurwitz formula

]
.
A basis for

is given by

\{y^{r}x^{b}dx\mid 0\leq r\leq p-2,\ 0\leq b\leq j-2,\ rj+bp\leq pj-j-p-1\}.

2.5.
The Jacobian of an algebraic curve

References for this topic are
[

, Chapter VII]
and
[

, Chapter III]

Suppose
C
is a (smooth, projective, irreducible) curve of genus

Remark 2.35

Here is another perspective on the Jacobian.
Let

For

2.6.
Related topics: semi-abelian varieties and stable curves

For completeness, we include a definition of semi-abelian varieties, although they are not needed in this document.
An (algebraic) torus is a commutative group scheme which is isomorphic to finitely many copies of the multiplicative group scheme

\mathbb{G}_{m}
.
A
semi-abelian variety
is a commutative group variety which is an extension of an abelian variety by an algebraic torus
[
FC90
, Section I.2]
.

In later sections, we consider curves that are not smooth, but whose singularities are manageable.
A
stable curve
is a projective irreducible curve whose only singularities are ordinary double points,
and whose automorphism group is finite.

Example 2.37
.

The affine equation

defines a stable curve with an ordinary double point at
(x,y)=(0,0)
.

A stable curve
C
has

if each irreducible component of

is smooth and
the dual graph of

is a tree.

In Section
5.3
, we consider the Picard variety (or Jacobian) of a singular stable curve.
The Picard variety
\mathrm{Pic}^{0}(C)
is an abelian variety if and only if
C
has compact type.
If not, then
\mathrm{Pic}^{0}(C)
is a semi-abelian variety.

Fact 2.38
.

Torelli’s Theorem

is false for singular stable curves.

Example 2.39
.

Let
C
be a curve of genus
3
that has two components, an elliptic curve
C_{1}
and a genus

curve

,
which are identified (clutched together, see Section

) at the identity on

and a point

There is a one-parameter family of such curves, as the point

However,

,
and this p.p. abelian variety does not depend on the choice of

.

2.7.
Open questions on variations of the Schottky problem

The questions in this section aim to clarify the relationships between abelian varieties and Jacobians.

Every abelian variety over an algebraically closed field

[

]

see
[

Every abelian variety over a field can be embedded in a Jacobian by

Example 2.40
.

There are examples, usually attributed to Mumford, of p.p. abelian varieties that are not Jacobians.

2.7.1.
The Schottky problem

The Schottky problem
asks for a characterization of the p.p. abelian varieties that are Jacobians of curves.
Historically, this topic was introduced by Schottky and Jung.
The survey by Grushevsky
[
Gru12
]
gives a good introduction, together with many references.

The Schottky problem has been studied with many approaches including theta constants,
modular forms, singularities of the theta divisor, minimal cohomology classes, and Kummer varieties.
Theoretically, there are several methods to determine which p.p. abelian varieties are Jacobians.
There is the criterion of Matsusaka-Ran
[
Mat59
]
,
[
Ran81
]
,
[
Col84
]
.
A small selection of the papers on this topic includes Andreotti–Mayer
[
AM67
]
,
Gunning
[
Gun82
]
, Welters
[
Wel84
]
,
Shiota
[
Shi86
]
, and Krichever
[
Kri06
,
Kri10
]
.

From certain perspectives, the Schottky problem has been completely solved.
Yet these methods are fairly difficult to apply in practice for a given abelian variety.
There are still open problems on the characterization of the Torelli locus.
For example, for
g=4
, see two recent descriptions in
[
KW15
]

and
[
HPS
]
.

2.7.2.
Complete decomposability

For every integer
g\geq 2
, there exists a p.p. abelian variety of dimension
g

which decomposes completely as a product of
g
elliptic curves.
Ekedahl and Serre asked the following question. They provided examples for numerous values of
g
up to
1297
.

Question 2.41
.

[
ES93
]

Given
g\geq 2
, does there exist a smooth curve
C
of genus
g
such that the Jacobian
J_{C}

is isogenous to a product of
g
elliptic curves?

A paper by Paulhus and Rojas
[
PR17
]
shows that the question has an affirmative answer for many new
values of

Recently, thirteen new cases (including

[
PS
]
.
Currently, the smallest genus for which the answer is not known is

.

2.7.3.
Abelian varieties not isogenous to a Jacobian

For any
g\geq 4
, Chai and Oort
[
CO12
]
proved the existence of an abelian variety of dimension

defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
which not isogenous to any Jacobian conditional on the André–Oort conjecture;

[

]
proved this result unconditionally.
Recent work on this topic includes
[
MZ20
]
and
[

]

For abelian varieties of dimension

we expect there are still many results about isogenies with Jacobians waiting to be discovered.

Suppose

This section contains the definition of these invariants:

, the

We call these the

Good references for this section are
[

]

[

3.1.1.
Collapsing of

-torsion points modulo

Suppose

is an elliptic curve over

\mathrm{char}(k)=p

In this expository section, we show through some examples that the number of

is either

or

If
\ell\not=p
is prime, then there are

points of order dividing

on

One of these is the point at infinity
\mathcal{O}_{E}
.
The

\ell
-division polynomial of

Example 3.1
.

If
p\not=2
,
write
E:y^{2}=x^{3}+ax^{2}+bx+c
.
Let
\ell=3

A point

, equivalently

, so

Using this, we can show that

if and only if

is a root of the

-division polynomial:

d_{3}(x)=3x^{4}+4ax^{3}+6bx^{2}+12cx-b^{2}+4ac\in k[x].

If

, then

has 4 distinct roots in

-coordinates of points of order
3
on

.
For each

has 8 points of order 3.
The

-torsion

consists of these 8 points and

.

Now suppose that
\mathrm{char}(k)=p=3
.
Then

, and

has one (triple) root if

and has no roots if

.
So the number of
3
-torsion points is either

or

.

Example 3.2
.

Write
E:y^{2}=x^{3}+bx+c
.
When

, the

2bx^{10}-b^{2}cx^{5}+b^{6}-2b^{3}c^{2}-c^{4}

This has either

or zero roots, so the number of

-torsion points is either
5
or
1
.

When
\mathrm{char}(k)=7
, the

-division polynomial is

3cx^{21}+3b^{2}c^{2}x^{14}+(-b^{7}c-2b^{4}c^{3}+3bc^{5})x^{7}-b^{12}-b^{9}c^{2}+3b^{6}c^{4}-b^{3}c^{6}+2c^{8}.

or zero roots, so the number of

-torsion points is either

or

.

More generally, in characteristic

, the

-division polynomial of

has either
(p-1)/2
or zero roots.
It is not easy to show this explicitly for larger

-division polynomials have higher degree.
For this reason, to show that the

-torsion points on

collapse to either
p
points or

point in characteristic

we switch to looking at the kernel

of the multiplication by

map

on

and use the fact that
[p]
factors as
[p]=\mathrm{Ver}\circ\mathrm{Fr}

where the relative Frobenius morphism
\mathrm{Fr}:E\to E^{(p)}
is purely inseparable.

3.1.2.
Supersingular elliptic curves

Suppose that

.
Let

The zeta function of

The supersingular condition was studied by Hasse

Has35
]
, Deuring
[
Deu41
]

[

]

As seen in

In this section, we say

, see
[

, page 142]

is ordinary.

If

is supersingular; this is a special case of Lemma

In fact, this is an equation for the unique supersingular elliptic curve over

By
[
Sil09
, Example V.4.4]
, the elliptic curve
E:y^{2}=x^{3}+1
(

) is supersingular if and only if

p\equiv 2\bmod 3
and
p
is odd.
By
[
Sil09
, Example V.4.5]
, the elliptic curve
E:y^{2}=x^{3}+x
(

-invariant

)
is supersingular if and only if
p\equiv 3\bmod 4
.
When
p=3
, this is an equation for the unique

\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{3}
up to isomorphism.

Suppose

is odd and

is a cubic with distinct roots.
Then

if and only if the coefficient

of

As seen in

This shows that the number of isomorphism classes of supersingular elliptic curves

3.1.3.
Ordinary and supersingular elliptic curves

We revisit the case of elliptic curves and describe the
distinction between ordinary and supersingular elliptic curves from several points of view.

Let

be an elliptic curve and let

The

Then

\ell&\text{if }\ell=p,\text{ and }E\text{ is ordinary},\\

We refer to Section

Each of the following conditions is equivalent to

(see Example

Each of the following conditions is equivalent to

(A)’
The only

.

(B)’

is a line segment of slope

.

(C)’

is isomorphic to

group scheme of rank

,

Conditions (A)’ and (B)’ are equivalent

More information about condition (C)’ can be found in Example

.

The

The

The

-rank and

-torsion group scheme.

For each pair

3.2.4.
Supersingular abelian varieties

Definition 3.6
.

A principally polarized abelian variety
X
is

if

\lambda=1/2
is the only slope of
its

-divisible group

Letting

denote the

-divisible group of dimension

,

[

, Section 1.4]

When

(3.6)

.

A principally polarized abelian variety

of dimension

]
or
[

(2)

(3)

3.2.5.
The Newton polygon

The Newton polygon is an isogeny invariant of the
p
-divisible group of
X
(and the finest isogeny invariant).
If
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are isogenous, then they have the same Newton polygon.

Definition 3.8
.

Recall from (
3.5
) the decomposition of the
p
-divisible group

X[p^{\infty}]\sim\oplus_{\lambda=\frac{d}{c+d}}G_{c,d}^{m_{\lambda}}
.
The
Newton polygon

\xi(X)
is the multi-set of values of
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]
in (
3.5
), which are called the
slopes
.

The Newton polygon is determined by the multiplicities of its slopes.
For
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]
, let
n_{\lambda}
denote the multiplicity of
\lambda
in the multiset
\xi(X)
.
If
c,d\in\mathbb{N}
are relatively prime integers such that
\lambda=d/(c+d)
, then
n_{\lambda}=(c+d)m_{\lambda}
.
The Newton polygon is
symmetric
if
n_{\lambda}=n_{1-\lambda}
(or
m_{\lambda}=m_{1-\lambda}
)
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]
.

Lemma 3.9
.

The
p
-rank of
X
is the multiplicity
n_{1}
of the slope
1
in
\xi(X)
.

The Newton polygon is typically drawn as a lower convex polygon,
with slopes equal to the values of
\lambda
occurring with multiplicity
n_{\lambda}
.
The Newton polygon of a
g
-dimensional abelian variety
X
is symmetric and, when drawn as a polygon,
it has endpoints
(0,0)
and
(2g,g)
and integral break points.

There is a partial ordering on Newton polygons of the same height
2g
:
one Newton polygon is smaller (meaning less ordinary) than a second if the lower convex hull of the first is never below the second.
We write
\xi_{1}\leq\xi_{2}
if
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
share the same endpoints and
\xi_{1}

lies on or above
\xi_{2}
. This defines a partial ordering on Newton polygons for abelian varieties of dimension
g
.
In this partial ordering, the ordinary Newton polygon is maximal and the supersingular Newton polygon is minimal.

Notation 3.10
.

We use
\oplus
to denote the union of multi-sets. For any multi-set
\xi
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
we write
\xi^{n}
for the union of
n
copies of
\xi
.

Let
\mathrm{ord}
denote the Newton polygon
\{0,1\}
and
\mathrm{ss}
denote the Newton polygon
\{1/2,1/2\}
.
Let
\sigma_{g}:=\mathrm{ss}^{g}
denote the supersingular Newton polygon of height
2g
.

For
s,t\in\mathbb{N}
,
with
s\leq t/2
and
\mathrm{gcd}(s,t)=1
, let
(s/t,(t-s)/t)
denote the Newton polygon with slopes
s/t
and
(t-s)/t
, each with multiplicity
t
.

Thus the Newton polygon of an ordinary (resp. supersingular) abelian variety of dimension
g

is
\mathrm{ord}^{g}
(resp.
\sigma_{g}=\mathrm{ss}^{g}
).

Remark 3.11
.

Suppose
X
is defined over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.
Then there exists a finite subfield
\mathbb{F}_{0}
of characteristic
p

such that
X
is isomorphic to the base change to
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
of an abelian variety
X_{0}
over
\mathbb{F}_{0}

and Frobenius is an
\mathbb{F}_{0}
-endomorphism on
X_{0}
.

Let
W(\mathbb{F}_{0})
denote the Witt vector ring of
\mathbb{F}_{0}
.
Consider the action of Frobenius
\mathrm{Fr}
on the crystalline cohomology group
H^{1}_{\mathrm{cris}}(X_{0}/W(\mathbb{F}_{0}))
.
By Weil’s Theorem, there exists an integer
n

, such that
the composition of
n
Frobenius actions
\mathrm{Fr}^{n}
is a linear map on
H^{1}_{\mathrm{cris}}(X_{0}/W(\mathbb{F}_{0}))
.

In this situation, the
Newton polygon

\xi(X)
of

is the multi-set of rational numbers

such that

are the valuations at
p
of the eigenvalues of
\mathrm{Fr}^{n}
.
Note that
\xi(X)
is independent of the choice of
X_{0}
,
\mathbb{F}_{0}
, and

.

3.2.7.
The Ekedahl-Oort type

The
p
-torsion

\mathrm{BT}_{1}
-group scheme (of rank

This builds on work of Kraft
[

(unpublished, which did not include polarizations) and of Moonen

[

References on this material are
[

]
,
[

]
, and
[

]
.

The isomorphism type of a symmetric
\mathrm{BT}_{1}
group scheme

can be encapsulated into combinatorial data in numerous ways, including

See
[

]
and
[

]
for longer descriptions of these topics.

To define the Ekedahl–Oort type, as in
[

, Sections 5 & 9]

suppose

is a symmetric
\mathrm{BT}_{1}
group scheme over
k
with rank

.
Then there is a

of
{\mathbb{D}}_{*}(I)
as a

such that

The

Lemma 3.14
.

The
p
-rank is
\mathrm{max}\{i\mid\nu_{i}=i\}
and the

-number equals
g-\nu_{g}
.

There is a restriction

There are

since all sequences satisfying this restriction occur.
By
[

; (ii)

\mathrm{BT}_{1}

and (iii) pqp Dieudonné modules of dimension

[

Given

The

.

3.3.
Superspecial, supersingular, and
p
-rank
0
abelian varieties

3.3.1.
The difference between
p
-rank
0
and supersingular

Let
X
be a principally polarized abelian variety of dimension
g
over
k
.
Let
X[p]
be the kernel of the multiplication-by-
p
morphism of
X
.
The following conditions are all different for
g\geq 3
.

(A)
p
-rank
0
- The only
p
-torsion point of
X
is the identity:
\#X[p](k)=1
.

(B) supersingular
- The Newton polygon of
X
is a line segment of slope
1/2
.

(C) superspecial
- The group scheme
X[p]
is isomorphic to
(I_{1,1})^{g}
.

Proposition 3.17
.

For conditions
(A),(B),(C)
as defined above, there are implications:

(C)\Rightarrow(B)\Rightarrow(A),\text{ but }(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g\geq 3}}{{\not\Rightarrow}}(B)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g\geq 2}}{{\not\Rightarrow}}(C).

Proof.

(Sketch)

(1)

For the implication
(C)\Rightarrow(B)
:
if the
p
-torsion of a
p
-divisible group
G
satisfies (C), then

F^{2}G\subset[p]G
. By the basic slope estimate in
[
Kat79
, 1.4.3]
, the slopes of the Newton polygon are all
at least
1/2
; so the slopes all equal
1/2
, because the polarization forces the Newton polygon to be symmetric.
Thus
X
is supersingular.
Alternatively, the implication
(C)\Rightarrow(B)
follows from
[
Oor74
, Theorem 4.2]
.

(2)

For the non-implication
(B)\not\Rightarrow(C)
when
g\geq 2
:
an abelian variety can be isogenous
but not isomorphic to a product of supersingular elliptic curves;
for example, quotients of a superspecial abelian variety by a general
\alpha_{p}
-subgroup scheme have this property
when
g\geq 2
.

(3)

For the implication
(B)\Rightarrow(A)
: more generally,
the
p
-rank of a
p
-divisible group
is the multiplicity of the slope
1
in the Newton polygon, so if all the slopes equal
1/2
, then the
p
-rank is
0
.

(4)

For the non-implication
(A)\not\Rightarrow(B)
when
g\geq 3
:
there exists a principally polarized abelian variety over
k
whose
p
-divisible group is
G_{g-1,1}\oplus G_{1,g-1}
,
so its Newton polygon has slopes
1/g
and
(g-1)/g
.
This has
p
-rank
0
, but is not supersingular when
g\geq 3
. ∎

is a semi-abelian variety which is an extension of an abelian variety
X
by a torus of rank

Let

,
a_{X}
,
\xi_{X}
,

,
\nu_{X}
denote respectively the

p
-rank,

-number, Newton polygon,
p
-torsion group scheme, and Ekedahl–Oort type of

The definition of the

.
Note that
f_{\tilde{X}}=f_{X}+e
.

The Newton polygon of
\tilde{X}
is defined to be

and
e

In

4.1.
Overview: motivating questions about curves

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic
p>0
.
Suppose

is a (smooth, projective, irreducible) curve of genus
g
defined over
k
.
The
p
-torsion invariants of
C
are defined to be those of its Jacobian
\mathrm{Jac}(C)
.

This chapter contains some existence results for smooth curves
with certain Newton polygons or Ekedahl–Oort types.
More general results about the
p
-rank are contained in Section
6.3.2
.

Here are the motivating questions.

Question 4.1
.

If
p
is prime and
g\geq 2
: does there exist a smooth curve
C/\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
of genus
g

whose Jacobian (A) has
p
-rank
0
; (B) is supersingular; or (C) is superspecial?

For Question
4.1
, the answer to part (A) is yes for all
g
and
p
, see Theorem
6.18
;
part (B) is sometimes yes, as seen in this section, but is not known in most cases;
the answer to part (C) is not known in most cases, but is sometimes no when
p
is small relative to
g
,
see Theorem
4.18
. More generally, one can ask:

Question 4.2
.

If
p
is prime and
g\geq 2
: which
p
-ranks,
a
-numbers, Newton polygons, and Ekedahl-Oort types
occur for the Jacobians of smooth curves
C/\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
of genus
g
?

We survey some of the results on this topic.
In this chapter, we focus on results proven using cohomological calculations or decomposition of the Jacobian.

4.2.
Newton polygons of curves

The Newton polygon of a curve
C
is the Newton polygon of its Jacobian, as defined in Section
3.2.5
.
If
C
has genus
g
, then its Newton polygon
\xi(C)
consists of finitely many line segments, which break at points with integer coefficients,
starting at
(0,0)
and ending at
(2g,g)
.
It is symmetric; the multiplicities
n_{\lambda}
and
n_{1-\lambda}
of the slopes
\lambda
and
1-\lambda
in
\xi(C)
are equal.
As before, the
p
-rank equals the multiplicity
n_{1}
of the slope
1
in
\xi(C)
.
As before,
C
is supersingular if and only if the only slope of
\xi(C)
is
\lambda=1/2
.

4.2.1.
The Newton polygon of a curve over a finite field

In Remark
3.11
, we defined the Newton polygon of an abelian variety over a finite field.
When
C
is defined over a finite field, there is an alternative definition of the Newton polygon.

Let
C/\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a (smooth, projective, geometrically irreducible) curve of genus
g
defined over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of characteristic
p
.

Definition 4.3
.

For an integer
s\geq 1
, let
N_{s}=\#C(\mathbb{F}_{q^{s}})
be the number of points of
C
defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{s}}
.
The
zeta function
of
C/\mathbb{F}_{q}
is

Z(C/\mathbb{F}_{q},T)=\mathrm{exp}(\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{N_{s}T^{s}}{s}).

Here is the famous theorem of Weil.

Theorem 4.4
.

(Weil conjectures for curves
[
Wei48a
, §IV, page 22]
,
[
Wei48b
, §IX, page 69]
)
There is a polynomial
L(C/\mathbb{F}_{q},T)\in\mathbb{Z}[T]
of degree
2g
such that

Z(C/\mathbb{F}_{q},T)=\frac{L(C/\mathbb{F}_{q},T)}{(1-T)(1-qT)}.

Furthermore,

L(C/\mathbb{F}_{q},T)=\prod_{i=1}^{2g}(1-\alpha_{i}T),

where
the reciprocal roots
\alpha_{i}
of
L(C/\mathbb{F}_{q},T)
have the property that
|\alpha_{i}|=\sqrt{q}
.

The polynomial
L(C/\mathbb{F}_{q},T)
is the
L
-polynomial
of
C/\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Its roots all have archimedean absolute value
1/\sqrt{q}
in
\mathbb{C}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

The characteristic polynomial of the Frobenius endomorphism of
\mathrm{Jac}(C)
is

P(\mathrm{Jac}(C)/\mathbb{F}_{q},T)=T^{2g}L(C/\mathbb{F}_{q},T^{-1})
.

The Newton polygon keeps track of the
p
-adic valuations of the roots or, equivalently, of the coefficients
of
L(C/\mathbb{F}_{q},T)
.

Notation 4.6
.

Let
v_{i}
be the
p
-adic valuation of the coefficient of
T^{i}
in the
L
-polynomial
L(C/\mathbb{F}_{q},T)
.
Let
v_{i}/r
be its normalization for the extension
\mathbb{F}_{q}/\mathbb{F}_{p}
, where
q=p^{r}
.
The Newton polygon is the lower convex hull of the points
(i,v_{i}/r)
for
0\leq i\leq 2g
.

One can prove that the Newton polygons of
C/\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
\mathrm{Jac}(C)/\mathbb{F}_{q}
are the same.
So the curve
C/\mathbb{F}_{q}
is supersingular when the Newton polygon of
L(C/\mathbb{F}_{q},T)
is a line segment of slope
1/2
.
The
p
-rank of
C/\mathbb{F}_{q}
equals the number of roots of
L(C/\mathbb{F}_{q},T)
that are
p
-adic units.

There are several ways to characterize the supersingular property for curves, in addition to those already described in Lemma
3.7
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Consider a curve
C/\mathbb{F}_{q}
of genus
g
.
The following properties are equivalent:

(1)

the curve
C
is supersingular;

(2)

the normalized Weil numbers
\alpha_{i}/\sqrt{q}
are all roots of unity
[
Man63
, Theorem 4.1]
; and

(3)

the curve
C
is minimal (meaning that it satisfies the lower bound in the Hasse-Weil bound for the number of points) over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{s}}
for some
s\geq 1
.

Remark 4.8
.

Examples of supersingular curves were found by Weil
[
Wei48a
]
, Honda
[
Hon66
]
, Yui
[
Yui80
]
, Gross–Rohrlich
[
GR78
]
, and others.
Their technique was to calculate the normalized Weil numbers of Fermat curves using Jacobi sums.

Remark 4.9
.

Many people worked on finding fast algorithms to compute the zeta function of an arbitrary
curve over a finite field.
Schoof developed a deterministic polynomial time algorithm for counting points on elliptic curves
[
Sch85
]
.
This was generalized for hyperelliptic curves by Kedlaya
[
Ked01
,
Ked04
]
, Harvey
[
Har07b
]
,
and Harvey–Sutherland
[
HS14
,

]

For more information, we refer the reader to the (planned) lecture notes for the Arizona Winter School 2025.

4.3.
The Hasse–Witt and Cartier–Manin matrices

Consider the action of Vershiebung
\mathrm{Ver}
on the vector space
of regular differentials
H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1})
.
By
[
Car57
]
,
[
Man63
]
, this action is given by the
(so-called modified
1
1

1

The distinction between the modified and unmodified versions of the Cartier operator is a technical point that does not play a key role here.
) Cartier operator
\mathcal{C}
, which is the semi-linear map

\mathcal{C}:H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1})\to H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1})

satisfying these rules, for
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1})
and
f\in\mathcal{O}_{C}
:

(i)
\mathcal{C}(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})=\mathcal{C}(\omega_{1})+\mathcal{C}(\omega_{2})
;

(ii)
\mathcal{C}(f^{p}\omega_{1})=f\mathcal{C}(\omega_{1})
; and

(iii)
\mathcal{C}(f^{n-1}df)=\begin{cases}df&\mbox{ if }n=p,\\
0&\mbox{ if }1\leq n<p.\end{cases}

Lemma 4.10
.

The
p
-rank of
C
is the stable rank of the Cartier operator.
The
a
-number of
C
is the corank of the Cartier operator.

Fix a basis
\beta=\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{g}\}
for
H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1})
.
From Serre duality, this fixes a basis
\beta^{*}
for the dual space
H^{1}(C,\mathcal{O})
.
The Hasse–Witt matrix is the matrix for the action of Frobenius
\mathrm{Fr}
on
H^{1}(C,\mathcal{O})
with respect to
\beta^{*}
.
The Cartier–Manin matrix is the matrix for the action of Vershiebung
\mathrm{Ver}

on
H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1})
with respect to
\beta
.

For each
\omega_{j}
, let
m_{i,j}\in k
be such that

\mathcal{C}(\omega_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{g}m_{i,j}\omega_{i}
.
The
g\times g
-matrix
M=\left(m_{i,j}\right)
is the (modified) Cartier–Manin matrix
and it gives the action of the (modified) Cartier operator.

The Cartier–Manin matrix is the matrix for the (unmodified) Cartier operator, and it is given by

\tilde{M}:=M^{(p)}
, where each entry is raised to the
p
th power.
The
a
-number is the corank of
\tilde{M}
or, equivalently, of
M
.
The
p
-rank is the rank of the product of
g
twists of
\tilde{M}
(or
M
) but this needs to be computed
very carefully as described in Remark
4.13
.

Example 4.11

In
[

Specifically: he proved that

for which the Cartier–Manin matrix

Example 4.12

Let

Let

Remark 4.13
.

Warning:
if

Example 4.14

The Ekedahl–Oort type of a curve over

(4.1)

4.6.4.
More genera for supersingular Artin–Schreier curves

We take this opportunity to fix a mistake in
[
Pri19
, Corollary 2.6]
, where the genus was erroneously
computed as
g=\delta p(p-1)^{2}/2
because we did not take the projectivization of a vector space.

Corollary 4.22
.

[Karemaker/Pries]
Let
p
be prime.
Let
\delta\geq 1
be an integer such that
0
and
1
are the only coefficients in the base
p
expansion of
\delta
.
If
g=\delta p(p-1)/2
, then there exists a supersingular curve of genus
g
defined over a finite field of characteristic
p
.

When
p=2
, note that Corollary
4.22
yields every positive integer for the genus, like in Theorem
4.19
,
because the condition on
\delta
is vacuous and
g=\delta
.

Proof.

The condition on
\delta
implies that, for some integer
t\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t}p^{s_{i}}(1+p+\cdots p^{r_{i}}),

\displaystyle\text{ for some }r_{i},s_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}\text{ such that }s_{i}\geq s_{i-1}+r_{i-1}+2.

Let
u_{i}=(s_{i}+1)-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(r_{j}+1)
and note
u_{i+1}\geq u_{i}+1
.

Choose an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-linear subspace
L_{i}
of dimension
d_{i}:=r_{i}+1
in the vector subspace of

\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}[x]
of additive polynomials of degree
p^{u_{i}}
, with the requirement that

L_{i}\cap L_{j}=\{0\}
if
i\not=j
.
Let
{\mathbb{L}}=\oplus_{i=1}^{t}L_{i}
.

For
f\in{\mathbb{L}}-\{0\}
, let
C_{f}:y^{p}-y=xf
. By definition,
C_{f}
comes equipped with
a preferred map
C_{f}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
If
f\in{\mathbb{L}}-\{0\}
is such that it has a non-zero component in
L_{i}
,
but not in
L_{j}
for
j>i
, then
g_{C_{f}}=p^{u_{i}}(p-1)/2
.
By Theorem
4.21
,
\mathrm{Jac}(C_{f})
is supersingular.

Let
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{L}})
denote the projectivization of the
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-vector space
\mathbb{L}
.
Specifically, there is a diagonal embedding of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
in
\mathbb{L}
. If
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathbb{L}-\{0\}
, and
if
f_{1}=cf_{2}
for some
c\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
, then the curves
C_{f_{1}}
and
C_{f_{2}}
are isomorphic over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
,
and this isomorphism is compatible with the preferred maps to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
With some abuse of notation, we write
f\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{L})
to denote an equivalence class of

f\in{\mathbb{L}}-\{0\}
up to scaling by constants in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
and we write

C_{f}
for
f\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{L})
to denote the curve
C_{f}
for any representative of
f\in\mathbb{L}-\{0\}
in this equivalence class.

Let
Y
be the fiber product of
C_{f}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
for all
f\in\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{L}})
.
By
[
KR89
, Theorem B]
,
\mathrm{Jac}(Y)
is isogenous to
\oplus_{f\in\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{L}})}\mathrm{Jac}(C_{f})
.
So
\mathrm{Jac}(Y)
is supersingular.
The genus of
Y
is
g_{Y}=\sum_{f\in\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{L}})}g_{C_{f}}
.

There are
p^{d_{i}}-1
non-zero polynomials in
L_{i}
.
The number of
f\in{\mathbb{L}}
which have a non-zero contribution from
L_{i}
, but not from
L_{j}
for
j>i
, is

(p^{d_{i}}-1)\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}p^{d_{j}}
. The number of equivalence classes of these
f
in
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{L})
is the
quotient of this number by
p-1
. Thus we obtain:

\displaystyle g_{Y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{(p^{d_{i}}-1)}{p-1}(\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}p^{d_{j}})p^{u_{i}}(p-1)/2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{t}(p^{r_{i}}+\cdots+1)p^{\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(r_{j}+1)}p^{u_{i}-1}p(p-1)/2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{t}(p^{r_{i}}+\cdots+1)p^{s_{i}}p(p-1)/2=\delta p(p-1)/2.

∎

Components of the boundary

The boundary of

Definition 5.14
.

Let

Define

The generic geometric point of

represents a curve

and

,
having genera

and

By (

We also consider the boundary component

A point of

Define

Theorem 5.23

[

]

Suppose

Proof.

Construction: Take the elliptic curve

6.2.1.
Specialization and purity

This section includes two major facts about the behavior of Newton polygons in families.

The first is that the Newton polygon can only go up (become less ordinary) under specialization.
Specifically, building on Grothendieck’s specialization theorem, Katz proved the following:

Theorem 6.2
.

[
Kat79
, Corollary 2.3.2]

If
A
is an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebra,
the set of points in
\mathrm{Spec}(A)
at which the Newton polygon (of an
F
-crystal) goes up is Zariski-closed, and is locally on

\mathrm{Spec}(A)
the zero-set of a finitely generated ideal.

Theorem
6.2
provides a way to study Newton polygons in families. This was used by Koblitz in
[
Kob75
]
.

The second major fact is the purity theorem for Newton polygons proved by de Jong and Oort.
Here is the statement.

Theorem 6.3
.

Purity theorem
[
dJO00
, Theorem 4.1]

Let
(A,m_{A})
be a Noetherian local ring of characteristic

Let

be a
p

F
-crystal) over
\mathrm{Spec}(A)
.
Assume that the Newton polygon of
G
is constant over
\mathrm{Spec}(A)\backslash\{m_{A}\}
.
Then either
\mathrm{dim}(A)<1
or the Newton polygon of
G
is constant over
\mathrm{Spec}(A)
.

In practice, the purity theorem is used as follows.
Suppose

is an abelian scheme of relative dimension
g
defined over a reduced and irreducible scheme
W
.
Given a point

, let
X_{w}
denote the fiber of

over
w
.

Corollary 6.4
.

Suppose that
\xi
is the Newton polygon of

over the generic geometric point
\eta
of
W
.
Consider the sublocus

of points
w\in W
for which the Newton polygon of

is not
\xi

If

is not empty, then it has codimension
1
in
W
.

More generally, suppose
\xi,\xi^{\prime}
are symmetric Newton polygons of height
2g
with
\xi^{\prime}<\xi
in the partial ordering.
Let

be the distance between
\xi^{\prime}
and
\xi
in the

Then:

Corollary 6.5

With hypotheses as above: if

is a symmetric Newton polygon with

the sublocus of points

for which the Newton polygon of

6.3.1.
A geometric proof for supersingular genus

This result was inspired by a conversation with Oort, in which we discussed

\xi

This method applies when

As an illustration of this method, here is a new proof of
[

, Corollary 1.2]
.
Let
\mathcal{M}_{g}[\sigma_{g}]
(resp.
\mathcal{A}_{g}[\sigma_{g}]

\mathcal{M}_{g}
(resp.

Theorem 6.16
.

[
Pri25
]

For every prime
p
, there exists a smooth curve of genus
4
that is supersingular.
Thus
\mathcal{M}_{4}[\sigma_{4}]
is non-empty and its irreducible components have dimension at least
3
for every prime
p
.

Note that
[
KHS20
, Theorem 1.1]
(whose proof uses a different technique)
also gives information about the

namely there exists a supersingular smooth curve of genus
4
with
a
-number

for every prime

.

Proof of Theorem
6.16
.

Working over
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
, consider a chain of four supersingular elliptic curves, clutched together
at ordinary double points.
This yields a stable curve
C_{\circ}
of compact type that has genus
4
and is supersingular.
By (
5.2
), the Jacobian
J_{\circ}
of
C_{\circ}
is isomorphic to the product of four supersingular elliptic curves and
thus is supersingular.
As such,
J_{\circ}
is represented by a point in
{\mathcal{A}}_{4}[\sigma_{4}]\cap\mathcal{T}_{4}
, which shows that this intersection is non-empty.

The codimension of
\mathcal{A}_{4}[\sigma_{4}]
in
\mathcal{A}_{4}
is
10-4=6
.
The codimension of
\mathcal{T}_{4}\cap\mathcal{A}_{4}
in
\mathcal{A}_{4}
is
10-9=1
.
Since
\mathcal{A}_{4}
is a smooth stack, the codimension of an intersection of two substacks is at
most the sum of their codimensions
[
Vis89
, page 614]
. Thus

\mathrm{codim}(\mathcal{A}_{4}[\sigma_{4}]\cap\mathcal{T}_{4},\mathcal{A}_{4})\leq 7
.
To summarize, each of the irreducible components of the non-empty intersection
\mathcal{A}_{4}[\sigma_{4}]\cap\mathcal{T}_{4}

has dimension at least
3
.

Let
\Delta
denote the locus in
\mathcal{A}_{4}[\sigma_{4}]\cap\mathcal{T}_{4}

whose points represent the Jacobian of a curve
C_{s}
that is stable but not smooth.
By hypothesis, these points represent abelian varieties, so the curve
C_{s}
has compact type.
From Section
5.3
,
\mathrm{Jac}(C_{s})
is a p.p. abelian fourfold that decomposes,
with the product polarization.

Then
\mathrm{dim}(\Delta)\leq 2
.
This is because points in
\Delta
represent abelian fourfolds either of the form (i)
E\oplus X
where
E
is a supersingular
elliptic curve and
X
is a supersingular abelian threefold, or of the form (ii)
X\oplus X^{\prime}
where
X,X^{\prime}
are supersingular
abelian surfaces. In case (i),

\mathrm{dim}(\Delta)\leq\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{A}_{1}[\sigma_{1}]\times\mathcal{A}_{3}[\sigma_{3}])=2
.
In case (ii),
\mathrm{dim}(\Delta)\leq\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{A}_{2}[\sigma_{2}]\times\mathcal{A}_{2}[\sigma_{2}])=2
.
Since
2<3
, every generic geometric point of
\mathcal{A}_{4}[\sigma_{4}]\cap\mathcal{T}_{4}
represents
the Jacobian of a supersingular curve of genus
4
which is smooth.

Thus
{\mathcal{M}}_{4}[\sigma_{4}]
is non-empty for every
p
; equivalently,
there exists a smooth curve of genus
4
that is supersingular.
If
W
is an irreducible component of

{\mathcal{M}}_{4}[\sigma_{4}]
, then the image of
W
under
\tau_{g}
is open and dense
in an irreducible component of
\mathcal{A}_{4}[\sigma_{4}]\cap\mathcal{T}_{4}
.
Thus
\mathrm{dim}(W)\geq 3
by purity (Corollary
6.4
).
∎

Suppose

[

8.1.
Overview:

-torsion invariants for cyclic actions

Suppose

g

8.2.
History of complex multiplication examples

Historically, many interesting phenomena were discovered by studying abelian varieties with complex multiplication.

For example, if

[

,
Hon66

GR78
,
Yui80

Aok08
,
Á14

Such curves

The eigenvalues of Frobenius for

The Newton polygons of these curves (also for composite

[

]
or
[

]

Proposition 8.1
.

Let

Let

See Section

Example 8.2

8.3.
Kottwitz method for Newton polygons

Suppose
X
has an action by the group ring
\mathbb{Z}[\mu_{m}]

\mathfrak{f}
, with

In this section, we describe some restrictions that this places on the

-torsion invariants (

-rank,
a

Newton polygon, and Ekedahl–Oort type).

8.3.1.
Constraints arising from the Frobenius action on eigenspaces

The first step of understanding these constraints is to consider the orbits
o

[\times p]
on
\mathbb{Z}/m-\{0\}
.
Both the Dieudonné module and the
p
-torsion group scheme of

decompose into summands indexed by the orbits.

For example, if
m
is prime with

\mathfrak{p}
of

\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})
above
p
, and the length of the orbits equals the degree of the residue field of
\mathfrak{p}
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
The residue field of
\mathfrak{p}
acts on the summands.
Similar, but slightly more complicated, statements hold when

is not prime.

The constraints on the
p
-rank in this situation can be found in
[
Bou01
]
.
Specifically, the
p
-rank is bounded by the sum (over the orbits) of the length of the orbit multiplied by
the minimal dimension of an eigenspace
L
in that orbit.
In particular, if
m
is prime, then the
p
-rank is divisible by the order of
p
modulo
m
.
Constraints on the
a

[
Elk11
]
.

8.3.2.
The Kottwitz method

Furthermore, there are constraints on the Newton polygon in this situation.
These were analyzed by Kottwitz
[
Kot85
,
Kot97
]
, Rapoport and Richartz
[

]

Definition 8.3
.

For

\mathfrak{f}
, and

•

The Dieudonné module

decomposes into pieces

8.4.2.
Inductive results

In
[
LMPT22
]
, we developed an inductive method, analogous to that described in Section

for curves having an action by a cyclic group.
The idea is to study families of

-covers of

, letting the number of branch points (and the genus) grow.
(Earlier inductive results about the

can be found in
[

]
and
[
OPW22
]

Working with inductive systems of Hurwitz spaces

we analyze how the Torelli locus intersects the Newton polygon stratification in
some PEL-type unitary Shimura varieties
\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}
.
Fixing the degree

and the prime

with

,
the main idea is that we find infinitely many

and

the open Torelli locus
\mathcal{T}^{\circ}_{\gamma}
intersects the
\mu
-ordinary locus of
\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}
; and
for which
\mathcal{T}^{\circ}_{\gamma}
intersects the non-
\mu
-ordinary locus of
\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}
.

The full statements of the results are too long to include here
because they require some subtle conditions on the signatures.
Here is a sample application.

Theorem 8.12
.

[
LMPT22
, Theorem 1.2]

Let
\gamma=(m,N,a)
be a monodromy datum.
Let

be a prime such that

.

Theorem

an unlikely intersection of the Newton polygon stratification and the open Torelli locus in

From an arithmetic perspective,

For example, take any Newton polygon with interesting slopes from this section

Chapter 9
Torsion points and monodromy

9.1.
Overview: the monodromy representation on torsion points

This chapter is about monodromy groups, which measure the
action of fundamental groups of moduli spaces on torsion points on abelian varieties.
Recall that
k
is an algebraically closed field.
For a prime
\ell\not=\mathrm{char}(k)
,
the
\ell
-torsion of an abelian variety
X
of dimension
g
is a vector space of dimension
2g
over
\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}
.
Section
9.2
introduces the main theme:
given a family of abelian varieties
X\to W
over an irreducible base
W
and a geometric point
w\in W
, the
fundamental group
\pi_{1}(W,w)
acts linearly on the
\ell
-torsion points of the fiber
X_{w}
.
This produces the monodromy represention, whose image in
\mathrm{GL}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z})
is called the monodromy group.

We discuss an important result of Chai in Theorem
9.4
about the
\ell
-adic monodromy group being big for
an irreducible subspace
W
of
\mathcal{A}_{g}
which is stable under all
\ell
-adic Hecke correspondences
and whose generic point is not supersingular.
As an application, in Section
9.4
, we prove that
the
\ell
-adic monodromy of each irreducible component of the
p
-rank strata of
\mathcal{A}_{g}
and
\mathcal{M}_{g}
is big.
As a consequence, there are stark differences between the
p
-rank
0
stratum and the supersingular stratum
of the moduli spaces
\mathcal{A}_{g}
and
\mathcal{M}_{g}
when
g\geq 3
.

9.2.
Linear action of fundamental groups on torsion points

Let
\ell\not=\mathrm{char}(k)
be prime.
Let
X
be a p.p. abelian variety over
k
of dimension
g
. Let
X[\ell]
denote the
\ell
-torsion subgroup scheme of
X
.
There are
\ell^{2g}
points on
X
that are
\ell
-torsion points and
X[\ell](k)\cong(\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z})^{2g}
.

More generally, if
n\geq 1
, then
X[\ell^{n}](k)\cong(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{n}\mathbb{Z})^{2g}
. The
\ell
-adic Tate module is

T_{\ell}(X)=\varprojlim_{n}X[\ell^{n}](k)\cong\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}^{2g}
.

Suppose
X\to W
is a family of p.p. abelian varieties over an irreducible
k
-scheme
W
.
Let
w\in W
be a geometric point and consider the
fiber
X_{w}
of
X
above
w
. The
\ell
-torsion subgroup of
X_{w}

is a vector space of dimension
2g
over
\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}
;
we choose a basis for it.

The following result can be found in the appendix to
[
FK88
]
.

Theorem 9.1
.

With notation as above, for
n\geq 1
:

(1)

The fundamental group
\pi_{1}(W,w)
acts linearly on the
\ell^{n}
-torsion

X_{w}[\ell^{n}]\cong(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{n}\mathbb{Z})^{2g}
.

(2)

There is a homomorphism
\rho_{\ell^{n}}:\pi_{1}(W,w)\to\mathrm{GL}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{n}\mathbb{Z})
,
and a homomorphism
\tilde{\rho}_{\ell}:\pi_{1}(W,w)\to\mathrm{GL}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}_{\ell})
.

(3)

Because the principal polarization induces a symplectic form on
X_{w}[\ell]
and on the Tate module,
the image of
\rho_{\ell^{n}}
is contained in
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{n}\mathbb{Z})

and the image of
\tilde{\rho}_{\ell}
is contained in
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}_{\ell})
.

Definition 9.2
.

The
mod-
\ell
(resp.
\ell
-adic
)

monodromy group
of
X\to W
is the image of
\rho_{\ell}
(resp.
\tilde{\rho}_{\ell}
).
The family
X\to W
has
big
mod-
\ell
(resp.
\ell
-adic) monodromy
if the image of
\rho_{\ell}
(resp.
\tilde{\rho}_{\ell}
) is
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z})

(resp.
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}_{\ell})
).

If
g\geq 3
, then the conditions of having big
\ell
-adic monodromy and having big mod-
\ell
monodromy are
equivalent.

Now we consider monodromy groups associated with families of curves.
By
[
Mil86b
, Proposition 9.1]
,
if
C
is a curve of genus
g
, there is a connection between the
\ell
-torsion points on
\mathrm{Jac}(C)
and
unramified
\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}
-covers
C^{\prime}\to C
.

If
C\to W
is a family of curves, then we define its monodromy by considering the associated family of
p.p. abelian varieties
\mathrm{Jac}(C)\to W
.
Naively speaking, if a family of curves of genus
g
has big
\ell
-adic monodromy,
this implies that the generic curve in the family behaves in some ways like
a generic curve of genus
g
,
and its Jacobian behaves in some ways like a generic p.p. abelian variety of dimension
g
, at least in terms of
properties of its torsion points of
\ell
-power order.

9.3.
A big monodromy theorem

Remark 9.3
.

Historically, monodromy groups were studied from a topological perspective.
Results were proved in a more arithmetic setting by Katz–Sarnak
[
KS99
]
,
Chai
[
Cha05
]
, Hall
[
Hal08
]
, and Chai–Oort
[

]
.
We include only one result here from the extensive literature.

Theorem 9.4
.

[Chai]
[
Cha05
]

Suppose

is an irreducible subspace of
\mathcal{A}_{g}
which is stable under all
\ell
-adic Hecke correspondences.
If the generic point of

is not in the supersingular locus, then

has big
\ell
-adic monodromy.

9.4.
Related results on monodromy of the
p
-rank strata

Let
X
be a p.p. abelian variety of dimension
g
over
k
.
Suppose
0\leq f\leq g
.
Recall that
X
has
p
-rank
f
means that
\#X[p](k)=p^{f}
;
and that this is equivalent to the Newton polygon having exactly
f
slopes of
0
and
f
slopes of

.

Recall from
[
FvdG04
, Theorem 2.3]
(see Theorem

) that

-rank

locus of
\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{g}
has codimension

in
\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{g}
.
Likewise, the
p
-rank

has codimension
g-f

\mathcal{M}_{g}
.
This implies that there exists a smooth curve of
p
-rank

in

for all

and

.

In this context, Achter and I proved that conditions on the

\ell
-adic monodromy group.

Theorem 9.5
.

[Achter–Pries]
[
AP08
]

Let
g\geq 3
. Let
0\leq f\leq g
. Let
\ell\not=p
.
Let
W
be an irreducible component of the
p
-rank
f
stratum of
\mathcal{M}_{g}
.
Then
W
has big mod-
\ell
monodromy and big
\ell
-adic monodromy.

Sketch of proof of Theorem
9.5
.

For
g=3
, the image of
\mathcal{M}_{3}
is open and dense in
\mathcal{A}_{3}
.
Thus the
p
-rank
f
stratum is stable under all Hecke correspondences
and has a generic point which not supersingular.
So the result follows from Chai’s result (Theorem
9.4
).
For
g>3
, it is not possible to apply Theorem
9.4
because the Torelli locus is not stable under
Hecke correspondences. Instead, the strategy is to work inductively on
g
.

Suppose
g\geq 4
and
W
is an irreducible component of the
p
-rank
f
stratum of
\mathcal{M}_{g}
.
By
[
FvdG04
, Theorem 2.3]

\mathrm{codim}(W,\mathcal{M}_{g})=g-f
.
By Theorem
5.17
, this implies that the closure
\bar{W}
of
W
intersects a boundary component
\Delta_{i}

of
\partial\mathcal{M}_{g}
.
By
[
FvdG04
, Lemma 2.5]
,
when
f=0
, this intersection contains points representing chains of (supersingular) elliptic curves,
and so the closure of every irreducible component of the
p
-rank
0
locus intersects
\Delta_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq g/2
.
We strengthen
[
FvdG04
, Lemma 2.5]
to show that, for any
0\leq f\leq g
,
the closure
\bar{W}
contains points representing chains of elliptic curves, exactly
f
of which are ordinary;
also, we can choose the location of the ordinary elliptic curves in the chain.
This shows that
\bar{W}
contains the image of the clutching morphism

\kappa_{g_{1};1,g_{2};1}
on an irreducible component of

\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{1};1}^{f_{1}}\times\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{2};1}^{f_{2}}
(for any pair

such that
g_{1}+g_{2}=g
,
and pair

such that
f_{1}+f_{2}=f
and

).

The induction step is a little subtle for
g=4
and

. Putting aside those cases for a moment, suppose
g\geq 6

and the result is true for all
3\leq g^{\prime}<g
.
For
g\geq 6
, we use that
\bar{W}
intersects
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
to show that
the mod-
\ell
monodromy group of

contains subgroups isomorphic to
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g-2}(\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z})
and
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g-4}(\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z})
,
with non-trivial intersection, but with the latter not contained in the former.
The conclusion follows from group theoretic results about maximal subgroups of the symplectic group.

The
g=4

g=4
and
f=0
then
the intersection of
\bar{W}
with
\Delta_{2}
is contained in the supersingular locus whose mod-
\ell
monodromy group is not big.
Instead, we degenerate the family further into the boundary to show that the
mod-
\ell
monodromy group contains distinct subgroups of block form

and
1,1,2
with non-trivial intersection.
A similar idea works for
g=5
.
