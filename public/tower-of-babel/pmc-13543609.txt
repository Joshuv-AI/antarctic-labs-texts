Co-targeting EGFR and SUCLG2 disrupts a nuclear transcriptional program driving neuroendocrine differentiation and TKI resistance in castration-resistant prostate cancer
Authors: Wei-Yu Chen, Kuan-Hua Huang, Gagan Kajla, Yu-Ching Wen, Ming-Kun Liu, Yu Ching Fan, Phan Vu Thuy Dung, Hsiu-Lien Yeh, Wei-Hao Chen, Kuo-Ching Jiang, Han-Ru Li, Cheng-Sheng Chen, Jiaoti Huang, Himisha Beltran, Yen-Nien Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuroendocrine prostate cancer (NEPC) is a lethal, treatment-resistant subtype that arises through lineage transdifferentiation from castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) under selective pressure from androgen deprivation therapy. Although EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) represent a rational therapeutic strategy, their clinical efficacy remains limited, and the underlying resistance mechanisms are poorly defined. Through integrated molecular and functional analyses, we identified the mitochondrial succinyl-CoA ligase subunit SUCLG2 as a critical driver of neuroendocrine differentiation and EGFR-TKI resistance. EGF stimulation induced SUCLG2 nuclear translocation, where it formed a complex with phosphorylated EGFR and co-activated VEGFA transcription through co-occupancy of the VEGFA promoter to drive neuroendocrine gene programs. SUCLG2 overexpression conferred erlotinib resistance in vitro, whereas its depletion restored TKI sensitivity and impaired xenograft tumor growth, effects that were rescued by VEGFA reconstitution. Through in silico drug screening, we identified phenethyl isothiocyanate (PEITC) as a compound that reverses the SUCLG2-associated transcriptional signature and synergized with erlotinib to suppress patient-derived NEPC organoid growth and reduce xenograft tumor burden in vivo. In clinical specimens from CRPC patients and NEPC patient-derived xenografts, progressive nuclear co-accumulation of SUCLG2 and EGFR alongside elevated VEGFA expression correlated with disease advancement. Collectively, these findings define a non-canonical nuclear SUCLG2/EGFR/VEGFA signaling axis that mediates NEPC progression and therapeutic resistance, providing mechanistic rationale for combined SUCLG2 and EGFR inhibition as a strategy to overcome treatment resistance in this lethal malignancy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-02977-y.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is among the most frequently diagnosed malignancies in men worldwide and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. While androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) is the standard first-line treatment for advanced or metastatic prostate cancer, most patients ultimately progress to castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), a clinically formidable and currently incurable stage [2]. A subset of CRPC cases undergo further phenotypic transformation into neuroendocrine prostate cancer (NEPC), an aggressive variant characterized by marked lineage plasticity, rapid clinical progression, and limited responsiveness to current therapies [3].

Although the molecular mechanisms underlying the transition to CRPC and NEPC remain incompletely elucidated, accumulating evidence indicates that adaptive metabolic and signaling reprogramming are critical drivers of these processes [3–6]. Metabolic enzymes, historically regarded as regulators of cellular bioenergetics, are increasingly recognized for their roles in modulating oncogenic signaling pathways and shaping tumor cell phenotypes [7–9]. Beyond their canonical cytoplasmic and mitochondrial functions, a growing body of evidence demonstrates that metabolic enzymes can translocate to the nucleus and adopt noncanonical roles in transcriptional regulation and chromatin remodeling, a phenomenon termed metabolic enzyme moonlighting, which serves as a mechanistic bridge between cellular metabolic state and gene expression programs [10–12]. This metabolic adaptability appears particularly relevant in NEPC, where lineage reprogramming underpins the transition from an adenocarcinoma to a neuroendocrine phenotype [6, 13]. Among these enzymes, the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle component succinyl-CoA synthetase subunit β (SUCLG2) has recently been implicated in tumor progression across multiple malignancies [14–16]. While SUCLG2 is canonically localized to the mitochondrial matrix where it catalyzes the reversible conversion of succinyl-CoA to succinate, its potential nuclear functions in transcriptional regulation remain largely unexplored. The present study addresses this gap by characterizing a noncanonical nuclear role for SUCLG2 in the context of CRPC and NEPC. Our previous work demonstrated that ADT induces SUCLG2 expression in NEPC models, coinciding with increased EGFR signaling activity [17]. These findings suggest a potential mechanistic link between mitochondrial metabolism and pathways regulating lineage plasticity, though the specific contribution of SUCLG2 in this context warrants further investigation.

In this study, we investigate the role of SUCLG2 in modulating EGFR signaling to promote neuroendocrine differentiation (NED) and drive therapeutic resistance in advanced prostate cancer. We hypothesize that ADT-induced SUCLG2 expression facilitates its nuclear translocation and association with nuclear EGFR-linked transcriptional machinery, thereby augmenting the expression of oncogenic targets such as VEGFA. Given the limited clinical efficacy of EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) in CRPC and NEPC [18–21], it is important to recognize that this therapeutic failure reflects two distinct phenomena [22, 23]. Intrinsic resistance occurs in tumors with low or absent EGFR dependency that were never likely to respond to TKI therapy. Acquired resistance refers to initially EGFR-dependent tumors that develop escape mechanisms during treatment. The SUCLG2/EGFR axis investigated here is specifically relevant to EGFR-overexpressing CRPC tumors undergoing lineage plasticity toward the NEPC phenotype. In this molecularly defined subgroup, EGFR pathway activation functions as an active driver of neuroendocrine transdifferentiation rather than a passive bystander event, as EGF signaling has been shown to directly induce neuroendocrine marker expression in prostate cancer cells [24]. We further assess whether SUCLG2 represents a potential therapeutic vulnerability or a biomarker of resistance. Through these studies, we aim to delineate the mechanistic interplay between metabolic adaptation and oncogenic signaling and to inform the development of combinatorial strategies targeting the SUCLG2/EGFR/VEGFA axis in advanced prostate cancer.

Results

SUCLG2 integrates EGFR signaling with metabolic reprogramming in prostate cancer

In our previous research, we demonstrated that elevated levels of SUCLG2 may contribute to the progression of NED in prostate cancer following ADT [17]. To investigate the role of SUCLG2 in oncogenic signaling and metabolic reprogramming in prostate cancer, we performed RNA-sequencing (RNA-Seq) on C4-2 cells transduced with a SUCLG2 cDNA vector, which revealed substantial alterations in global gene expression profiles compared with empty vector controls. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) identified a significant correlation between SUCLG2 overexpression and an EGFR-responsive gene signature (Supplemental Fig. 1A). Consistently, analysis of TCGA prostate cancer dataset confirmed that elevated SUCLG2 expression is positively correlated with multiple EGFR signaling activation signatures in prostate cancer samples (Supplemental Fig. 1B). To assess SUCLG2 upregulation and EGFR signaling activation after ADT, we cultured LNCaP cells under prolonged androgen-deprived conditions using charcoal-stripped serum (CSS)-containing medium. We observed a progressive increase in SUCLG2 protein levels, accompanied by elevated EGFR and p-EGFR (at Tyr1068), leading to downstream ERK1/2 activation (Fig. 1A). Treatment of C4-2 cells, a cell line derived from a CRPC LNCaP xenograft, with increasing doses of enzalutamide/MDV3100 resulted in dose-dependent induction of SUCLG2 and enhanced EGFR signaling (Fig. 1B). These results indicate that both androgen deprivation and enzalutamide treatment induce SUCLG2 upregulation and activate the EGFR pathway, suggesting a metabolic adaptation in prostate cancer cells in response to AR inhibition. Exogenous EGF significantly increased SUCLG2 mRNA and protein levels, as well as p-EGFR, p-ERK1/2, and intracellular succinate accumulation (Fig. 1, C–E). Importantly, SUCLG2 knockdown significantly suppressed the EGF-induced increases in SUCLG2 mRNA and intracellular succinate (Fig. 1, F and G). To assess the relative contribution of SUCLG2 to this response, we normalized intracellular succinate levels to PBS-treated baseline within each group; SUCLG2 knockdown significantly attenuated but did not completely abolish EGF-induced succinate elevation (Supplemental Fig. 1C), indicating that compensatory anaplerotic pathways or reverse succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) flux contribute a SUCLG2-independent fraction. Collectively, these results support a model in which EGFR signaling promotes SUCLG2 expression and partially drives succinate accumulation, though additional metabolic inputs contribute to the full response. This finding suggests a potential interplay between SUCLG2 and EGFR signaling in the metabolic adaptation of prostate cancer, driven by both AR inhibition and EGF stimulation.

SUCLG2 drives resistance to EGFR-TKI therapy

EGFR expression drives prostate cancer progression by activating downstream PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways, thereby promoting androgen-independent growth and enhancing metastatic dissemination [18]. However, clinical trials with EGFR inhibitors have demonstrated limited efficacy in unselected prostate cancer cohorts, reflecting both intrinsic resistance in tumors with low baseline EGFR dependency and acquired resistance mechanisms that emerge under therapeutic pressure in initially EGFR-dependent tumors [22, 23]. To investigate the role of SUCLG2 in mediating this resistance, we evaluated the viability of several prostate cancer cell lines following treatment with the FDA-approved EGFR-TKI, erlotinib. While parental prostate cancer cell lines exhibited dose-dependent sensitivity, enzalutamide-resistant lines (LNCaP-MDVR and C4-2-MDVR) and small-cell prostate cancer (SCPC)-like LASCPC-01 cells maintained high viability even at increased erlotinib concentrations (Fig. 1H), consistent with the adaptive resistance observed in advanced disease. Overexpression of SUCLG2 in LNCaP and C4-2 cells significantly increased resistance to erlotinib, as shown by elevated viability compared to vector controls across various drug concentrations (Fig. 1I). Furthermore, SUCLG2-overexpressing C4-2 cells maintained their proliferative capacity despite a 5-day erlotinib treatment (Fig. 1J). Sphere formation assays, which indicate cancer stem-like characteristics [25], demonstrated that SUCLG2 overexpression enhanced both sphere size and number, regardless of erlotinib treatment (Fig. 1, K and L). At the signaling level, SUCLG2 overexpression maintained elevated levels of EGFR, p-EGFR, and downstream ERK1/2 activity even after erlotinib exposure (Fig. 1M). Conversely, SUCLG2 knockdown in enzalutamide-resistant LNCaP-MDVR and C4-2-MDVR cells partially restored sensitivity to erlotinib, resulting in reduced cell viability and decreased EGFR/ERK signaling activation (Fig. 1, N and O). These findings suggest that SUCLG2 promotes erlotinib resistance in prostate cancer cells by sustaining EGFR/ERK signaling and enhancing stem-like properties, highlighting SUCLG2 as a key mediator of EGFR-TKI resistance in prostate cancer.

SUCLG2 drives NED and therapeutic resistanceviaVEGFA in prostate cancer

Our previous studies established that SUCLG2 significantly contributes to NEPC progression and therapeutic resistance, primarily through modulation of EGFR signaling pathways [17]. To elucidate the role of SUCLG2 in resistance to EGFR-targeted therapies, we performed GSEA utilizing TCGA prostate cancer datasets. This analysis revealed significant enrichment of receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)-associated and EGFR-TKI resistance signatures in prostate tumors expressing high SUCLG2 levels (Supplemental Fig. 2A). Among these, the top three gene signatures, including "signaling by RTK," "EGFR-TKI resistance," and "protein tyrosine kinase activator activity," showed the highest enrichment (NES > 1.5, FDR < 0.1). Venn diagram analysis identified EGFR, VEGFA, EGF, and ERBB3 as overlapping genes among these leading signatures associated with SUCLG2 expression (Supplemental Fig. 2B). Examining the relationship to NED, we found that only EGFR and VEGFA (not EGF or ERBB3) significantly correlated with a NED-associated gene signature [26] in TCGA prostate cancer samples (Supplemental Fig. 2C). The positive association of EGFR and VEGFA upregulation with NEPC development highlights the need for further mechanistic investigation, particularly in the context of ADT, given that NEPC frequently arises as a consequence of ADT [27]. Indeed, published datasets confirmed that EGFR and VEGFA mRNA levels are increased in response to ADT (Supplemental Fig. 2D), a finding we validated in LNCaP cells cultured under androgen-deprived conditions (CSS medium), which showed increased EGFR and VEGFA mRNA expression (Supplemental Fig. 2E). Notably, ectopic SUCLG2 overexpression in C4-2 cells markedly elevated EGFR and VEGFA mRNA levels (Supplemental Fig. 2F), whereas SUCLG2 knockdown in enzalutamide-resistant LNCaP-MDVR cells significantly suppressed their expression (Supplemental Fig. 2G). Comparative analysis across multiple prostate cancer cell lines revealed that enzalutamide-resistant and SCPC-like cell lines specifically exhibited elevated SUCLG2 and VEGFA mRNA levels (Fig. 2A), indicating a positive correlation. EGF treatment substantially increased SUCLG2, VEGFA, and NED marker proteins (ENO2, SYP, SOX2) in C4-2 cells (Fig. 2B). SUCLG2 knockdown attenuated EGF-induced upregulation of VEGFA, NED markers (CHGA, ENO2, SYP, SOX2, NANOG), and proliferation markers (KI67, PCNA) at the mRNA level (Fig. 2C). Functional assays revealed that overexpression of VEGFA variants 1 and 2 significantly promoted cell proliferation and NED marker induction in C4-2 cells (Fig. 2, D and E). Notably, re-expression of VEGFA variant 2 in SUCLG2-deficient C4-2 cells partially rescued expression of NED and proliferation markers (Fig. 2, F and G). This recovery also partially restored cellular proliferation, sphere formation capability, and migratory and invasive behaviors as demonstrated by viability, sphere-forming, and transwell assays (Fig. 2, H–L). In contrast, VEGFA knockdown in LASCPC-01 cells led to substantial decreases in NED markers and proliferation indicators compared to controls (Supplemental Fig. 2H). Likewise, disrupting VEGFA in SUCLG2-overexpressing C4-2 cells partially reduced the SUCLG2-driven increase in neuroendocrine, stemness, and proliferation genes (Supplemental Fig. 2I), suggesting that VEGFA contributes to this pathway. In vivo xenograft experiments validated these findings, as tumors derived from SUCLG2-knockdown C4-2 cells showed significantly reduced volumes and weights, and overexpression of VEGFA variants 1 and 2 in the SUCLG2 knockdown background partially rescued tumor growth (Fig. 2, M–O). These results underscore the critical contribution of SUCLG2-mediated VEGFA signaling to tumor aggressiveness. Collectively, these findings establish VEGFA as a key downstream mediator linking SUCLG2 expression to NED, therapeutic resistance, and aggressive prostate cancer phenotypes, positioning SUCLG2 as a promising therapeutic target in advanced prostate cancer.

SUCLG2 interacts with nuclear EGFR to enhance VEGFA transcription in prostate cancer

Although SUCLG2 is predominantly recognized for its role in mitochondrial metabolism [28], its nuclear functions and relationship with nuclear EGFR are not well understood. To address this gap, immunofluorescence staining was performed on C4-2 cells subjected to either control or SUCLG2 knockdown following EGF stimulation. In control cells, EGF treatment resulted in a substantial increase in the nuclear localization and co-localization of SUCLG2 and p-EGFR (Fig. 3, A and B). In contrast, SUCLG2 knockdown markedly reduced nuclear SUCLG2 and disrupted its association with nuclear p-EGFR after EGF stimulation (Supplemental Fig. 3, A and B). These findings indicate that EGF-induced SUCLG2 nuclear translocation requires EGFR signaling activity. SUCLG2 knockdown reduced nuclear SUCLG2 but not nuclear EGFR, demonstrating that each protein enters the nucleus independently and associates within the nuclear compartment. This nuclear accumulation was further confirmed via cell fractionation and Western blot analysis, which revealed significantly increased nuclear levels of SUCLG2 and p-EGFR (Tyr1068) following EGF exposure (Fig. 3C). A similar pattern of nuclear SUCLG2 and p-EGFR enrichment was observed under androgen-deprived conditions (CSS medium) (Supplemental Fig. 3C), suggesting that both EGF stimulation and androgen deprivation promote SUCLG2 nuclear translocation and activate EGFR signaling. Co-immunoprecipitation (IP) assays were next performed using biochemically purified nuclear lysates to determine whether the SUCLG2 and p-EGFR interaction occurs within the nuclear compartment. Reciprocal IP with antibodies against SUCLG2 and p-EGFR Tyr1068 demonstrated a specific interaction in nuclear extracts, and this interaction was increased following either EGF stimulation or androgen deprivation (Fig. 3, D–G). Nuclear fraction purity was confirmed by enrichment of Lamin B1 and reduction of cytoplasmic β-tubulin in the nuclear fraction. These data support a model in which SUCLG2 and EGFR associate after nuclear entry and form a functional nuclear complex rather than requiring SUCLG2 for EGFR nuclear import. Given previous reports that nuclear EGFR participates in the transcriptional regulation of oncogenic genes [29, 30], and considering our data indicating that nuclear EGFR/SUCLG2 complexes may contribute to VEGFA upregulation, we next examined their potential genomic targets. Chromatin IP (ChIP)-seq analysis revealed enriched EGFR occupancy at AT-rich sequences (ATRS) within the VEGFA regulatory region (GRCh38: chr6), with binding signal markedly increased following EGF stimulation compared to vehicle control, coinciding with active H3K27Ac-marked chromatin as annotated by ENCODE (Supplemental Fig. 3D). Specifically, three consensus ATRS sites were located at −37,630, −20,160, and −16,591 bp upstream of the VEGFA transcription start site (Fig. 3H). ChIP-qPCR assays demonstrated significantly increased recruitment of nuclear EGFR/SUCLG2 complexes to ATRS1 and ATRS3 following EGF stimulation or androgen deprivation, as detected using antibodies against SUCLG2 and p-EGFR (Fig. 3, I and J). Moreover, ectopic SUCLG2 overexpression further enhanced binding to these ATRS sites (Fig. 3K), supporting its co-regulatory role in EGFR-dependent VEGFA transcriptional activation. To test whether SUCLG2 and nuclear EGFR simultaneously occupy the same VEGFA regulatory regions, we performed sequential ChIP re-ChIP assays in both antibody orders: anti-p-EGFR followed by anti-SUCLG2, and the reverse. Under EGF stimulation, significant co-occupancy of SUCLG2 and p-EGFR was detected at ATRS1 and ATRS3, but not ATRS2, within the VEGFA regulatory region, while IgG controls showed minimal enrichment (Fig. 3L). Notably, CSS recapitulated this pattern, with enriched co-occupancy at ATRS1 and ATRS3 compared to FBS-treated controls (Fig. 3M). Together, these results provide direct evidence that SUCLG2 and nuclear EGFR form a shared chromatin complex at select VEGFA regulatory elements under both EGF-stimulated and castration-like conditions. To confirm the functional relevance of these interactions, reporter plasmids containing either wild-type or mutated ATRS within the human VEGFA regulatory region were constructed (Fig. 3H). Reporter assays revealed increased transcriptional activity of the wild-type VEGFA regulatory sequence upon EGF or CSS stimulation (Fig. 3, N and O), as well as with SUCLG2 overexpression (Fig. 3P). In contrast, mutations in ATRS1 and ATRS3 significantly reduced reporter activity under all tested conditions (Fig. 3, N–P), highlighting their critical roles in nuclear EGFR/SUCLG2-mediated VEGFA transcription. To validate SUCLG2's role in EGF-driven VEGFA transcription, we transfected GFP reporter constructs carrying wild-type (VEGFA_WT) or ATRS point-mutant sequences (ATRS1M, ATRS2M, ATRS3M) into C4-2 shLuc or shSUCLG2 cells. EGF significantly induced VEGFA_WT reporter activity in shLuc cells, and this induction was markedly reduced by SUCLG2 knockdown, while PBS-treated basal activity remained unaffected (Fig. 3Q). Progressive ATRS site mutations progressively abolished EGF-inducible activity and eliminated the SUCLG2-dependent differential. These results demonstrate that nuclear SUCLG2 co-activates VEGFA transcription specifically through the ATRS-dependent nuclear EGFR axis. Pharmacological targeting of SUCLG2 suppresses prostate cancer growth and tumorigenesis

To identify compounds capable of reversing the SUCLG2-associated transcriptional program, we employed a computational drug-repurposing strategy using the Library of Integrated Network-Based Cellular Signatures (LINCS) L1000 dataset [33]. This approach identified phenethyl isothiocyanate (PEITC) and acyclovir as compounds whose transcriptional signatures in the LINCS L1000 dataset are significantly inversely correlated with the SUCLG2 overexpression gene signature, suggesting their potential to reverse SUCLG2-driven transcriptional programs (Fig. 4A). We note that PEITC is a pleiotropic phytochemical with multiple documented anti-cancer mechanisms [34–36]; its identification here reflects transcriptional signature reversal rather than direct biochemically validated SUCLG2 enzyme inhibition, and future binding studies will be required to establish mechanism-specific targeting. Subsequent in vitro validation demonstrated that PEITC markedly reduced cell viability in both AR-negative (PC3) and AR-positive (C4-2) prostate cancer cell lines in a dose-dependent manner over five days, whereas acyclovir exerted minimal antiproliferative effects (Fig. 4, B and C and Supplemental Fig. 4, A and B). Furthermore, PEITC significantly inhibited sphere-forming capacity in both cell lines, indicating a reduction in self-renewal and tumorigenic potential (Fig. 4, D and E and Supplemental Fig. 4C). To confirm SUCLG2 as a functional target of PEITC, C4-2 cells stably overexpressing SUCLG2 showed significantly reduced sensitivity to PEITC-induced suppression of cell viability compared to vector controls (Supplemental Fig. 4D), indicating that SUCLG2 expression level directly modulates PEITC responsiveness. We next evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of PEITC, alone and in combination with the EGFR-TKI erlotinib. Combination treatment led to significantly greater inhibition of cell proliferation compared to monotherapies, as evidenced by cell viability assays in both PC3 and C4-2 cells (Fig. 4, F and G). Consistently, the combination of PEITC and erlotinib reduced the self-renewal capacity of these cells in sphere formation assays (Fig. 4, H and I). Flow cytometric analysis revealed that combined treatment induced significant G0/G1 phase cell cycle arrest in both PC3 and C4-2 cells, indicating effective inhibition of cell cycle progression (Supplemental Fig. 4, E and F). Western blot analysis further showed that combination therapy significantly decreased protein levels of SUCLG2, VEGFA, EGFR, p-EGFR, ENO2, and SOX2 in both cell lines, consistent with disruption of the SUCLG2/EGFR/VEGFA signaling axis driving NED in prostate cancer (Fig. 4, J and K). Importantly, in enzalutamide-resistant LNCaP-MDVR cells, combined PEITC and erlotinib treatment significantly reduced cell viability and impaired sphere formation, outperforming either agent alone (Supplemental Fig. 4, G and H). These results reinforce the therapeutic potential of this combination for overcoming resistance in advanced prostate cancer models. Synergistic anti-tumor effects of PEITC and erlotinib in advanced prostate cancer

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of co-targeting SUCLG2 and EGFR in NEPC, we employed both a SCPC-like cell line (LASCPC-01) and a patient-derived NEPC organoid model (WCM154). In vitro assays demonstrated that the combination of PEITC and erlotinib significantly suppressed tumor cell proliferation and sphere-forming ability in both models (Fig. 5, A–D). Western blot analyses showed that combination treatment reduced the expression of SUCLG2, VEGFA, EGFR, p-EGFR, and neuroendocrine markers ENO2 and SOX2, indicating attenuation of the SUCLG2/EGFR/VEGFA signaling axis associated with NED (Fig. 5, E and F). Flow cytometric analysis further revealed an increase in G0/G1-phase arrest and a reduction in S-phase cell populations after treatment (Fig. 5, G and H). In vivo, the combination therapy resulted in significantly decreased tumor growth in subcutaneous LASCPC-01 xenografts relative to vehicle or single-agent groups (Fig. 5, I and J), as evidenced by substantially lower final tumor weights in the combination cohort (Fig. 5K). Nuclear SUCLG2/EGFR interactions promote VEGFA-mediated NED and therapy resistance in advanced prostate cancer

To characterize the distribution of nuclear SUCLG2 in prostate cancer, immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses were performed on clinical specimens. The results showed significantly increased nuclear SUCLG2 expression in high-grade adenocarcinoma and SCPC compared to normal prostate tissue and low-grade tumors (Fig. 6, A and B). Correlation analyses in prostate cancer datasets further established a strong association between SUCLG2 and EGFR expression levels (TCGA and GSE21036; Fig. 6C). To characterize cell type specific expression of SUCLG2, EGFR, and VEGFA across the prostate tumor microenvironment, we analyzed single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from the GSE181294 dataset, which comprises transcriptomic profiles from post castration CRPC patient specimens with annotated treatment histories, making it directly relevant to the ADT driven SUCLG2/EGFR axis investigated in this study. UMAP visualization identified 15 distinct cell populations comprising epithelial cells, stromal components including fibroblasts, pericytes, and endothelial cells, and diverse immune subsets including CD4 and CD8 T cells, CTL, NK cells, Treg, cycling T cells, B cells, plasma cells, monocytes, macrophages, mDC, pDC, and mast cells (Fig. 6D). Feature plot analysis revealed that SUCLG2, EGFR, and VEGFA exhibited overlapping expression patterns predominantly in epithelial cell populations, with co-enrichment also observed in endothelial cells and fibroblasts (Fig. 6E). This dataset is not enriched for confirmed NEPC cases, and the co-expression patterns identified here are therefore interpreted as correlative and hypothesis generating with respect to NEPC biology, rather than mechanistically definitive. To validate expression patterns identified in single-cell analysis, we interrogated two independent prostate cancer datasets representing distinct therapeutic contexts. Analysis of the GSE146402 dataset revealed differential expression of SUCLG2, EGFR, and VEGFA between cisplatin-treated and chemotherapy control samples in castration-resistant prostate cancer patients (Supplementary Fig. 5 A), while the GSE229805 dataset demonstrated similar treatment-responsive regulation in enzalutamide-treated versus DMSO control conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5B). The concordant upregulation of SUCLG2, EGFR, and VEGFA across independent cohorts treated with distinct modalities, specifically enzalutamide (AR pathway inhibition) and cisplatin (cytotoxic selection), supports the biological relevance of this axis in prostate cancer treatment resistance. The elevation observed in cisplatin-treated specimens is clinically significant, as platinum-based regimens represent standard-of-care chemotherapy for treatment-confirmed NEPC, and the enrichment for NEPC features in this cohort is consistent with published clinical data showing that platinum-based therapy predominantly benefits NEPC rather than conventional adenocarcinoma CRPC [27, 37]. To assess clinical relevance of nuclear SUCLG2/EGFR complexes after ADT, we performed IHC analysis of two metastatic CRPC patient samples. Both samples showed nuclear co-localization of SUCLG2 and p-EGFR with prominent cytoplasmic VEGFA expression (Fig. 6F). This pattern was recapitulated in a NEPC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model (WCM154), which displayed similar nuclear SUCLG2/p-EGFR co-localization and elevated VEGFA levels (Fig. 6G), confirming the presence of this molecular signature in advanced, therapy-resistant prostate cancer. We note that these IHC findings from two metastatic CRPC biopsies and one PDX model constitute proof-of-concept clinical observations; validation in a larger prospective cohort with quantitative IHC scoring, treatment history stratification, and correlation with established NEPC markers is required before these findings can support translational claims. In summary, these findings suggest that nuclear SUCLG2/EGFR interactions enhance VEGFA expression, potentially driving NED and promoting therapeutic resistance in advanced prostate cancer. Our study identifies the SUCLG2/EGFR/VEGFA signaling axis as a critical driver of lineage plasticity and therapy resistance (Fig. 6H). Nuclear co-localization of SUCLG2 and p-EGFR results in upregulation of VEGFA and the emergence of neuroendocrine phenotypes in clinical specimens. Pharmacological inhibition of SUCLG2 restores sensitivity to EGFR-TKI in resistant tumors and suppresses the growth of NEPC organoids and xenografts. These findings position SUCLG2 as a candidate predictive biomarker and therapeutic target, pending validation in prospective cohorts. Discussion

Our previous study suggests that SUCLG2 overexpression, a mitochondrial enzyme traditionally associated with the TCA cycle, may drive NED of prostate cancer cells and is linked to enhanced EGFR signaling activity [17]. In the present study, we identify a non-canonical function of SUCLG2 in regulating oncogenic signaling in prostate cancer, specifically in the context of ADT and acquired resistance to EGFR-TKIs. Our data show that SUCLG2 upregulation drives enhanced EGFR signaling, resistance to EGFR-TKIs and ADT, and aggressive tumor phenotypes via co-regulatory transcriptional activation of VEGFA through the nuclear EGFR/SUCLG2 complex. These effects are amplified by exogenous EGF stimulation or ADT, suggesting a feed-forward relationship in which EGFR signaling promotes SUCLG2 expression and nuclear activity, though additional SUCLG2-independent inputs contribute to the full metabolic and transcriptional response. Importantly, SUCLG2 is elevated and accumulates in the nucleus under ADT conditions, reinforcing its role in oncogenic transcriptional programs. Analysis of prostate cancer cell lines and patient-derived datasets further supports a strong association between SUCLG2 expression and activated EGFR signaling signatures. However, that clinical trials with EGFR TKIs have demonstrated limited efficacy in unselected prostate cancer cohorts, and this failure reflects two mechanistically distinct phenomena [22, 23]. Intrinsic resistance occurs in tumors that lack meaningful EGFR pathway dependency at baseline and were therefore never susceptible to TKI therapy. Acquired resistance refers to initially EGFR-dependent tumors that develop escape mechanisms under therapeutic pressure. The SUCLG2/EGFR nuclear signaling axis described in this study is specifically relevant to the subset of EGFR-overexpressing CRPC tumors undergoing lineage plasticity toward the NEPC phenotype, rather than a universal resistance mechanism across all prostate cancer subtypes. Future clinical efforts should therefore prioritize patient stratification by EGFR overexpression status and NED marker co-expression to identify populations most likely to benefit from SUCLG2/EGFR co-targeting strategies. Given that EGFR signaling is a known compensatory survival pathway activated by ADT [18], our findings suggest that SUCLG2 facilitates this adaptive mechanism, contributing to therapy resistance, lineage plasticity, and disease progression in advanced prostate cancer.

Our investigation further demonstrates that SUCLG2 confers resistance to EGFR-TKIs and ADT, representing a crucial mechanism underlying therapeutic failure. SUCLG2-overexpressing prostate cancer cells exhibit sustained viability and sphere-forming capacity even in the presence of erlotinib or ADT, suggesting that SUCLG2 enhances survival mechanisms that bypass these therapeutic pressures. SUCLG2 knockdown partially restores EGFR-TKI sensitivity, highlighting its role in sustaining EGFR signaling under therapeutic pressure. These findings are especially significant for ADT-induced NEPC, where increased EGFR/ERK signaling and metabolic reprogramming are known drivers of tumor adaptation [17, 40, 41]. The persistence of SUCLG2 expression under ADT conditions may provide a metabolic advantage that enables tumor cells to evade apoptosis and shift toward a more aggressive, neuroendocrine-like phenotype. Beyond its scaffolding function within the nuclear EGFR complex, SUCLG2's enzymatic role in the conversion of succinyl-CoA to succinate may further reinforce this metabolic advantage at the epigenetic level. Elevated intracellular succinate competitively inhibits α-ketoglutarate-dependent prolyl hydroxylases (PHDs), thereby stabilizing HIF-1α under normoxic conditions, a state of pseudohypoxia that directly activates NED-associated gene programs [42]. Concurrently, succinate inhibits α-ketoglutarate-dependent histone lysine demethylases (KDMs), promoting increased H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 marks at NED-associated loci and creating an epigenetic state permissive for neuroendocrine lineage specification [43]. We note that EGF-induced succinate accumulation is only partially attenuated by SUCLG2 knockdown, consistent with the possibility that compensatory anaplerotic pathways or reverse SDH flux contribute residual succinate elevation, and this SUCLG2-independent component warrants further investigation.

In addition to the role of SUCLG2 in metabolic reprogramming, our findings reveal that its overexpression is linked to the activation of EGFR signaling, which enhances the interaction between SUCLG2 and nuclear EGFR. The dual compartmentalization of metabolic enzymes between mitochondria and the nucleus has been increasingly recognized as a conserved mechanism by which cells integrate metabolic state with gene expression programs [10]. Although SUCLG2 does not contain a canonical nuclear localization signal (NLS), NLS-independent nuclear import is a well-documented paradigm. We propose that SUCLG2 may be co-imported with nuclear EGFR through the established endosome-to-nucleus trafficking pathway, in which internalized EGFR undergoes importin-β-dependent nuclear pore transit following Sec61 translocon-mediated retro-translocation from the endoplasmic reticulum [44, 45]. Alternatively, SUCLG2 may undergo nuclear import via importin-7, which recognizes non-canonical nuclear translocation signals beyond classical NLS motifs, as demonstrated for ERK2, which lacks a canonical NLS but translocates to the nucleus through a phosphorylated SPS motif that directly engages importin-7 [46]. Whether SUCLG2 harbors an analogous importin-7-binding motif remains to be determined. Recent work demonstrated that SUCLG2, as the G2 subunit of GTPSCS, can undergo nuclear translocation facilitated by the NLS residing in its partner subunit SUCLG1, establishing that nuclear import of SUCLG2 can occur through subunit-mediated co-import without requiring an intrinsic NLS [47]. Building on this precedent, our data demonstrate that SUCLG2 knockdown reduces nuclear SUCLG2 under EGF treatment without affecting nuclear EGFR abundance, indicating that EGFR nuclear import occurs independently of SUCLG2, whereas SUCLG2 nuclear enrichment is dependent on EGFR signaling. This observation supports a model in which both proteins arrive in the nucleus independently and subsequently form a functional complex, consistent with our co-IP data performed on purified nuclear fractions as confirmed by Lamin B1 and β-tubulin fractionation markers. This nuclear SUCLG2/EGFR complex acts as a transcriptional co-regulator of VEGFA expression, further underscoring the complexity of SUCLG2’s oncogenic functions. Because SUCLG2 lacks a recognized DNA-binding domain, its nuclear function is most likely mediated through protein–protein interactions with the EGFR/STAT3 transcriptional complex rather than through direct sequence-specific DNA binding, analogous to the established mechanism of nuclear EGFR itself, which is recruited to ATRS elements through association with STAT3 and other DNA-binding factors [31, 32]. Mechanistically, SUCLG2 associates with phosphorylated EGFR in the nucleus and co-occupies specific ATRS within the VEGFA regulatory region as part of the nuclear EGFR complex, as demonstrated by ChIP, Re-ChIP, and reporter assays. Mutation of ATRS1 and ATRS3 attenuates but does not abolish EGF-induced VEGFA reporter activity, indicating that SUCLG2/p-EGFR co-occupancy at these elements contributes to, but is not solely required for, transcriptional activation. Residual activity likely reflects input from alternate promoter elements recognized by STAT3, E2F1, or other nuclear EGFR-associated transcription factors. However, catalytically inactive and nuclear-excluded SUCLG2 mutants that directly separate metabolic from transcriptional contributions have not yet been generated and represent an important priority for future mechanistic investigation.

Analyses of CRPC patient tissues and NEPC PDX models consistently detect nuclear colocalization of SUCLG2 and p-EGFR, which aligns with high levels of cytoplasmic VEGFA expression. This pattern supports a model wherein nuclear SUCLG2/EGFR interactions stimulate VEGFA transcription, promoting aggressive tumor behavior in ADT-resistant and NEPC patients. Consistently, recent studies report that VEGFA pathway gene amplification is common in NEPC and CRPC, and that inhibition of VEGFA-NRP2 signaling suppresses NEPC progression and immune escape [48]. Beyond its cell-intrinsic effects, VEGFA produced through this nuclear transcriptional axis promotes tumor angiogenesis and creates an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by stimulating VEGFR2 on regulatory T cells and promoting M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages [49, 50]. Furthermore, extracellular succinate released from metabolically active SUCLG2-overexpressing tumor cells may act as a paracrine signaling molecule via the succinate receptor SUCNR1 on innate immune cells, potentially modulating dendritic cell function and macrophage polarization within the CRPC tumor microenvironment [51]. Taken together with the well-established role of VEGFA in angiogenesis and tumor progression [52, 53], our findings reveal nuclear SUCLG2/EGFR as a key transcriptional axis driving VEGFA-mediated prostate cancer progression, highlighting a critical regulatory mechanism with potential therapeutic implications.

PEITC is a pleiotropic phytochemical with multiple documented anti-cancer mechanisms in prostate cancer, including inhibition of IAP proteins (XIAP and Survivin), HDAC inhibition and CpG demethylation, suppression of invasion through the miR-194/BMP1/MMP axis, promotion of p53-independent apoptosis, modulation of Notch signaling, and enhancement of combination therapies with agents such as dibenzoylmethane or curcumin against androgen-independent tumor growth [34–36, 54–56]. To investigate the therapeutic potential of targeting SUCLG2/EGFR signaling in aggressive prostate cancer, we utilized enzalutamide-resistant and SCPC-like cell lines along with a PDO model of NEPC [57], and examined whether PEITC in combination with erlotinib could overcome therapy resistance. Our study shows that PEITC, combined with erlotinib, significantly reduces tumor burden and suppresses NED in SCPC-like xenografts. We identified PEITC through LINCS L1000 connectivity mapping as a compound whose transcriptional signature is significantly inversely correlated with the SUCLG2 overexpression gene signature, and we propose that PEITC reverses the SUCLG2-associated transcriptional program rather than functioning as a direct, biochemically validated SUCLG2 enzyme inhibitor. Future studies using binding validation approaches such as surface plasmon resonance (SPR) or cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) are warranted to establish mechanism-specific SUCLG2 targeting.

The upregulation of SUCLG2/EGFR/VEGFA observed in both enzalutamide-treated and cisplatin-treated CRPC specimens reflects convergent but mechanistically distinct routes to NEPC. Enzalutamide drives NED primarily through AR pathway suppression and EZH2-mediated epigenetic reprogramming [58], while cisplatin and carboplatin/etoposide, the standard-of-care regimen for treatment-confirmed NEPC, select for NEPC cells with intrinsic platinum tolerance [59]. The consistent elevation of the SUCLG2/EGFR/VEGFA axis under both treatment contexts suggests it represents a convergent downstream feature of the NEPC state irrespective of upstream trigger. These results highlight the role of SUCLG2/EGFR interactions in driving aggressive disease phenotypes and suggest that dual inhibition of SUCLG2 and EGFR with PEITC and erlotinib could be a promising strategy for the molecularly defined subgroup of EGFR-overexpressing CRPC tumors with evidence of neuroendocrine lineage plasticity, warranting prospective clinical evaluation in biomarker-selected cohorts.

In conclusion, this study identifies SUCLG2 as a novel regulator of EGFR signaling and a key driver of NED through co-regulatory transcriptional activation of VEGFA via the nuclear EGFR/SUCLG2 complex. In EGFR-overexpressing CRPC tumors with evidence of neuroendocrine lineage plasticity, co-targeting SUCLG2 and EGFR represents a mechanistically grounded therapeutic strategy warranting biomarker-driven clinical evaluation. Future studies should define the precise nuclear import route of SUCLG2 using NLS/MTS deletion mutants and import inhibitors such as importazole, generate catalytically inactive and nuclear-excluded SUCLG2 mutants to dissect metabolic from transcriptional contributions. Additionally, our clinical dataset is limited to two metastatic CRPC patient samples and one PDX model, and validation in larger cohorts with quantitative IHC scoring, treatment history stratification, and correlation with established NEPC markers including synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and NCAM, as well as clinical outcomes, is required to support broad translational application. Finally, our xenograft studies were conducted in immunodeficient hosts with limited group sizes, which precludes direct assessment of immune-tumor microenvironment interactions. Future studies using syngeneic or humanized immune-competent models with larger cohorts are needed to evaluate the full immunological consequences of the SUCLG2/VEGFA axis in NEPC.

Methods

Cell culture and reagents

Human prostatic adenocarcinoma cell lines, including LNCaP, PC3, 22Rv1, and C4-2, were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 11,875,093) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; EMD Millipore, US origin, Cat# ES-009-B) and 1% Pen-Strep (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 15,140,122). SCPC-like LASCPC-01 cells were also acquired from ATCC and cultured in HITES medium, consisting of RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10 nM hydrocortisone (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# H0888), 1 × insulin/transferrin/selenite (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 41,400,045), 200 nM β-estradiol (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# E8875), and 5% FBS. Androgen deprivation was induced by culturing cells in RPMI-1640 containing 5% CSS (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 12,676,029) for the specified duration or by treating cells with 10 μM enzalutamide (MDV3100; Selleckchem, Cat# S1250) under standard conditions for 48 h. Long-term enzalutamide resistance in LNCaP-MDVR and C4-2-MDVR cells was established by continuously culturing LNCaP or C4-2 cells in RPMI-1640 with 5% FBS and 20 μM MDV3100 for 12 months. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be mycoplasma-free within the past month. Erlotinib (Selleckchem, Cat# S7786) was dissolved in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# D2650) and applied at 1–10 μM for 48–72 h (cell viability assays) or 5 days (sphere formation assays), with DMSO vehicle controls included in all experiments. For EGF stimulation, cells were serum-starved for 16–24 h in RPMI-1640 containing 0.1% FBS, then treated with EGF (Selleckchem, Cat# A5094) at 100 ng/mL for 24–48 h (gene expression and nuclear fractionation assays). Phenethyl isothiocyanate (PEITC, Cat# 253,731) and acyclovir (Cat# A0220000) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and prepared as stock solutions in DMSO. All reagents were used at the indicated concentrations and durations.

Organoid culture

NEPC PDO (WCM154), established from a metastatic prostate cancer biopsy at Weill Cornell Medicine (New York, NY, USA), were obtained from the Beltran Lab at the Department of Medical Oncology, Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (Boston, MA, USA), and cultured in HM medium domes using an optimized protocol [57]. Before initiation, HM medium was prepared by thawing Advanced DMEM/F12 (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 12,634,028) supplemented with B27 (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 17,504,044), Glutamax (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 35,050,061), HEPES (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 15,630,080), Pen-Strep (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 15,140,122), conditioned media containing Noggin and R-Spondin derived from HEK293T cells (obtained from the Beltran Lab) expressing the respective transgenes, nicotinamide (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# N0636), N-acetyl-L-cysteine (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# A9165), recombinant human FGF10 (Shenandoah Biotechnology, Cat# 100–183), FGF-basic (PEPRO, Cat# 100-18B), SB202190 (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# S7067), prostaglandin E2 (Tocris Bioscience, Cat# 2296), recombinant mouse EGF (Thermo Fisher, Cat# PMG8045), Y-27632·2HCl (Selleckchem, Cat# S1049), A-83–01 (Tocris Bioscience, Cat# 2939), and recombinant human neuregulin (NRG) (ProSpec-Tany TechnoGene, Cat# 89,305–780) in a 37 °C water bath. Once thawed, all components were mixed under sterile conditions, filtered through a 0.22-µm membrane filter, and stored at 4 °C. For organoid passage, cells were dissociated by pipetting and incubated with 1 mL TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 2,604,013) at 37 °C with shaking (750 rpm) for 3–5 min. Digestion was stopped by adding 5 mL HM medium containing 10% FBS, followed by centrifugation at 1400 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was washed with 4 mL HM medium before a second centrifugation. Organoids were cultured in 2D by coating wells with 1% collagen I (Gibco, Cat# A1048301) prior to the addition of HM medium containing supplements. Media was refreshed every three days, and growth was monitored by phase-contrast microscopy. For drug treatment experiments, organoids were cultured for 7 days before inhibitor administration, and viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo 3D Cell Viability Assay (Promega, Cat# G9683).

Plasmid constructs and transfections

Gene overexpression and knockdown were achieved using a lentiviral transduction system. For overexpression, full-length SUCLG2 complementary DNA (cDNA) was cloned into the pLenti-CMV-Puro vector (Addgene, Cat# 17,452) and co-transfected with psPAX2 (Addgene, Cat# 12,260) and pCMV-VSV-G (Addgene, Cat# 8454) into Lenti-X 293 T cells (Clontech, Cat# 632,180) using X-tremeGENE™ HP DNA Transfection Reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 06366244001). Lenti-X 293 T cells were maintained in DMEM (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 11,965–092) supplemented with 5% FBS, Pen-Strep, non-essential amino acids (NEAAs; Thermo Fisher, Cat# 11,140–050), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 11,360–070), and Glutamax. After 48 h, virus-containing supernatants were collected, filtered through a 0.45-μm filter, and stored at −80 °C. Target cells were transduced with viral supernatants in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# H9268) and selected using 2 μg/mL puromycin. For SUCLG2 knockdown, short hairpin RNA (shRNA) constructs targeting SUCLG2 (Clone ID: TRCN0000235575) were cloned into the pLKO.1 vector and obtained from the RNAi Core Facility (Academia Sinica, Taipei, Taiwan). A non-targeting shRNA (Luc, Clone ID: TRCN0000072249) was used as a control. Lentivirus production and transduction were performed as described above, followed by puromycin selection. Promoter reporter constructs were generated by cloning the human VEGFA regulatory region containing putative nuclear EGFR-binding AT-rich sequences into the pGreenFire_blastocidin lentivector (Addgene, Cat# 174,103). Site-directed mutagenesis (Thermo Fisher, Cat# F541) was used to disrupt predicted binding motifs. Primer sequences used in qPCR are provided in Supplementary Table S1. All plasmids were sequence-verified, and experiments were performed in biological triplicates to ensure reproducibility.

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis was conducted to evaluate protein expression in cell lysates under standardized conditions for reproducibility. Cells were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific, Cat# 89,901) supplemented with protease (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 04693116001) and phosphatase (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 04906845001) inhibitors, and incubated on ice for 30 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove debris. Protein concentrations were quantified using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 23,225), ensuring 20 μg per lane to confirm equal loading. For standard Western blots, β-actin was used as a loading control. For subcellular fractionation and Co-IP experiments, Lamin B1 was included as the nuclear fraction marker and β-tubulin as the cytoplasmic fraction marker to confirm fractionation purity. Samples were separated on 10% or 12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, Cat# IPVH00010) using a semi-dry transfer system at 15 V for 45 min. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk or BSA in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies at appropriate dilutions, as detailed in Supplementary Table S2. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were used, and signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system (Bio-Rad, Cat# 1,705,060). Precision plus protein standards (Bio-Rad, Cat# 161–0374) were run in adjacent lanes to determine band molecular weights, with positions annotated on figures using dashed lines and kDa labels based on original run records. The original blots for all blots are provided in Supplementary File 3.

RNA extraction and qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 74,104) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# ND-2000). cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 4,368,813). qPCR was performed using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A25918) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 4,376,600). Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH, and all reactions were performed in triplicate. Fold changes in gene expression were calculated using the 2^ − ΔΔCt method. The qPCR cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Primer sequences used for qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability following drug treatment was measured using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega, Cat# G7571). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Drug treatments were administered at the indicated concentrations, and cell viability was assessed between 1 and 5 days post-treatment. At each time point, an equal volume of CellTiter-Glo reagent was added to each well, followed by a 10-min incubation at room temperature to allow luminescent signal stabilization. Luminescence was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek), and relative viability was calculated as a percentage of untreated controls. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Sphere formation assay

To assess sphere formation, genetically modified cells were seeded at a density of 1,000 cells per well in a 24-well plate. Cells were suspended in 100 μL of complete culture medium and mixed with an equal volume of Matrigel matrix (Corning, Cat# 356,234). The mixture was carefully added to the bottom edge of the well and incubated overnight to promote cell aggregation. The following day (day 0), 0.5 mL of fresh culture medium was added to each well, and the cells were maintained for one week. Spheres were visualized using phase-contrast microscopy (Olympus), and images were captured. The number of spheres formed per well was counted to evaluate self-renewal capacity. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Metabolite analysis

Intracellular succinate levels were quantified using the Succinate Colorimetric Assay Kit (BioVision, Cat# K649-100). C4-2 cells with SUCLG2 knockdown or those treated with EGF at the indicated concentrations for 48 h were washed with PBS and lysed. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove debris, and the supernatants were collected. Succinate levels were measured by mixing the supernatants with the assay reaction mix in a 96-well plate, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. Absorbance was recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek). Succinate concentrations were interpolated from a standard curve and normalized to total protein content measured using the Bradford assay. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

ChIP assay

ChIP assays were performed using the EZ-Magna ChIP A/G Kit (MilliporeSigma, Cat# 17–10,086) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, C4-2 cells were treated with 100 ng/mL EGF or CSS-containing medium for 24 h, or transfected with EV or SUCLG2 cDNA, and then crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature to preserve protein–DNA interactions. Crosslinking was quenched with 125 mM glycine, and the cells were lysed in ChIP lysis buffer. Chromatin was enzymatically sheared into 200–500 bp fragments using micrococcal nuclease digestion. Lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies targeting SUCLG2 (Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-390818), phosphorylated EGFR (p-EGFR, Tyr1068, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 3777), acetylated histone H3 (acetyl-H3, positive control, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 9671), or IgG (negative control, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 2729). Immune complexes were then precipitated using Protein G magnetic beads (MilliporeSigma, Cat# LSKMAGG). After multiple washes, the bound chromatin was eluted, reverse-crosslinked, and subjected to DNA purification. qPCR was performed using primers targeting the ATRS1, ATRS2, and ATRS3 sites within the VEGFA regulatory region. Relative DNA enrichment at each site bound by the respective proteins was expressed as a percentage of the total input DNA. The antibodies and qPCR primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Sequential ChIP re-ChIP

Sequential ChIP assays were performed to evaluate co-occupancy of EGFR and SUCLG2 at the VEGFA regulatory region. C4-2 cells were treated with 100 ng/mL EGF or CSS containing medium for 24 h and crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Crosslinking was quenched with 125 mM glycine and chromatin was prepared as described for the ChIP assay. The first IP was carried out overnight at 4 °C using anti-p-EGFR antibody and Protein G magnetic beads. After washing, immune complexes were eluted under non denaturing conditions using re-ChIP elution buffer containing 10 mM DTT for 30 min at 37 °C. The eluate was diluted 1 to 20 in ChIP dilution buffer and subjected to a second IP overnight with anti-SUCLG2 antibody or control IgG. After the second IP, samples were washed, reverse crosslinked, and purified DNA was analyzed by qPCR using primers spanning ATRS1, ATRS2, and ATRS3 of the VEGFA regulatory region. Enrichment was calculated relative to input chromatin and normalized to IgG controls.

Promoter reporter assay

To evaluate the transcriptional activity of the VEGFA regulatory regions, promoter reporter assays were performed in C4-2 cells treated with 100 ng/mL EGF or CSS-containing medium, or transfected with EV or SUCLG2 cDNA. Cells were co-transfected with either wild-type or mutant VEGFA promoter reporter constructs. In addition, C4-2 cells stably expressing shLuc or shSUCLG2 were co-transfected with VEGFA_WT, ATRS1M, ATRS2M, or ATRS3M reporter constructs and treated with PBS or 100 ng/mL EGF for 72 h, and GFP mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was measured by flow cytometry. Promoter activity was assessed 72 h post-transfection using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS, BD Biosciences). Relative GFP MFI values were quantified using FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences) and normalized to the control group. Experiments were conducted independently three times, each in triplicate. Data were presented as fold changes relative to the control group.

Immunofluorescence assay

Cells grown on glass coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 28,314) in PBS for 10 min. Cells were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Thermo Fisher, Cat# BP1600-100) for 1 h before incubation with primary antibodies against SUCLG2 (Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-390818), p-EGFR (Tyr1068, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 3777), at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, Alexa Fluor 488, Cat# A-11008; Alexa Fluor 594, Cat# A-11005) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# D1306), and coverslips were mounted with antifade mounting medium. Confocal microscopy (Zeiss, LSM 880) was used to capture images, and pixel intensities were quantified using ZEN imaging software (Zeiss). Correlation analyses of nuclear SUCLG2 and EGFR in cells were performed by correlation XY analyses using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, Inc.).

IP-western blot analysis

IP coupled with Western Blot was performed using the Pierce™ Classic Magnetic IP/Co-IP Kit (ThermoFisher, Cat# 88,804). C4-2 cells (2 × 10⁷) were treated with PBS, 100 ng/mL EGF, or CSS-containing medium for 48 h. Cells were harvested in ice cold PBS and resuspended in hypotonic buffer containing 10 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 10 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM DTT, and protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After incubation on ice for 10 min, cells were disrupted by gentle pipetting and centrifuged at 800 g for 5 min at 4 °C to separate the nuclear pellet. The nuclear pellet was washed once in hypotonic buffer and lysed in nuclear IP buffer containing 20 mM Tris HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 percent NP 40, 10% glycerol, and protease and phosphatase inhibitors for 30 min on ice with intermittent mixing. Nuclear lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Fractionation purity was confirmed by immunoblotting for Lamin B1 as a nuclear marker and β-tubulin as a cytoplasmic marker. Nuclear protein lysates (500 μg) were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 5 μg of either anti-SUCLG2 antibody (Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-390818), anti-phospho-EGFR antibody (Cell Signaling, Cat# 2236), or control IgG. Protein A/G magnetic beads were added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with gentle agitation. Beads were washed three times with IP lysis buffer, and immune complexes were eluted and subjected to Western blot analysis. Antibodies used for immunoblotting are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

IHC staining

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections from patient samples, including normal prostate tissue (n = 15), low-grade prostate cancer (Gleason score ≤ 7, n = 25), high-grade prostate cancer (GS ≥ 8, n = 18), and SCPC (n = 13), were obtained from the Duke University School of Medicine (Durham, NC, USA), and their use was approved by the Duke University School of Medicine Institutional Review Board (protocol ID: Pro00070193). Two metastatic CRPC samples were obtained from Wan-Fang Hospital, affiliated with Taipei Medical University. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Taipei Medical University Joint Institutional Review Board. The ethics approval number is N202303058. NEPC PDO xenograft sections (WCM154) were provided by the Beltran lab at the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (Boston, MA, USA). Tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating sections in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. Sections were then blocked with 5% normal goat serum for 1 h before overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies against SUCLG2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat# sc-390818), EGFR (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 4267), phosphorylated EGFR (p-EGFR_Tyr1068, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 3777), and VEGFA (Abcam, Cat# ab52917). Details of the primary antibodies and their targets are provided in Supplementary Table S5. Following PBS washes, slides were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signal detection was performed using DAB substrate (Vector Laboratories, Cat# SK-4100), and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Staining intensity was evaluated in a blinded manner by two independent pathologists based on staining intensity and the percentage of positive cells. Representative images were captured using a brightfield microscope (Leica, Cat# DM2500).

Xenograft studies

For the SUCLG2/VEGFA signaling functional study, male NOD SCID mice (initial n = 3 per group, 6–8 weeks old, National Laboratory Animal Center, Taipei, Taiwan) were subcutaneously injected with 2 × 10⁶ C4-2 cells per flank. Each mouse received injections of C4-2 cells with SUCLG2 knockdown or SUCLG2 knockdown rescued with either an empty vector or a VEGFA_v1 or VEGFA_v2 cDNA vector. For the drug treatment study, male athymic nude mice (6–8 weeks old, National Laboratory Animal Center, Taipei, Taiwan) were subcutaneously injected with 1 × 10⁶ LASCPC-01 cells per flank. Once tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomized into four double-blinded treatment groups (n = 4 per group) and administered either vehicle (DMSO), PEITC (10 mg/kg), erlotinib (10 mg/kg), or a combination of PEITC (5 mg/kg) and erlotinib (5 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection twice per week for four weeks. Tumor growth was monitored twice weekly using digital caliper measurements, and tumor volume was calculated according to the following formula [60]: V = 0.5236 × H × W × LV = 0.5236 \times H \times W \times LV = 0.5236 × H × W × L, where V, H, W, and L represent tumor volume, height, width, and length, respectively. At the study endpoint, tumors were harvested and processed for IHC to assess molecular markers of interest. All animal studies were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Taipei Medical University (Taipei, Taiwan), under approved protocol No. LAC2022-0362. The protocol explicitly outlines the maximum allowable tumor size and burden as permitted by the ethics committee. Throughout the study, these limits were strictly observed and not exceeded.

Drug repurposing screening

Drug reuse screening was performed using the LINCS L1000 dataset to identify candidate small molecules, with priority given to candidates that reverse SUCLG2-related transcriptional processes. This screening employed computational analysis of transcriptional profiles, comparing gene expression changes associated with SUCLG2 overexpression to a broad collection of drug-induced gene expression signatures available within the LINCS L1000 database. A gene signature reversal approach was applied, wherein compounds predicted to induce expression changes opposite to those elicited by SUCLG2 overexpression were prioritized as putative inhibitors. Similarity between SUCLG2-induced and drug-induced expression profiles was assessed through pattern-matching algorithms [33].

RNA-seq and GSEA

Total RNA was isolated from C4-2/EV and C4-2/SUCLG2 cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 74,104) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and RNA integrity was confirmed by an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) score greater than 8.0 prior to library preparation. Library preparation was performed using the standard Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA protocol, and paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform at a minimum depth of 30 million reads per sample. Raw reads were assessed for quality using FastQC (v0.10.1) and trimmed using Cutadapt (v1.9.1) with a Phred quality cutoff of 20, error rate of 0.1, and minimum read length of 75 bp to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases. Trimmed reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1), and gene-level read counts were quantified using HTSeq (v0.6.1). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.6.3), with genes considered differentially expressed at a false discovery rate (FDR) less than 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change greater than 1. GSEA was performed using the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB; https://www.gsea-msigdb.org) to identify biological pathways and gene signatures significantly enriched in relation to SUCLG2 expression levels, with a normalized enrichment score (NES) greater than 1.5 and FDR less than 0.1 considered statistically significant.

Clinical outcomes and dataset analyses

TCGA prostate cancer dataset contains 414 primary prostate cancer samples from patients treated with radical prostatectomy under National Cancer Institute, National Institutes of Health Review Board approval, which consisted of level 3 normalized microarray gene mRNA expression data (UNC_AgilentG4502A_07). Expression data (and resulting z-scores) were log2-normalized. For GSEA, GSEA software (Broad Institute) was downloaded from the Broad Institute [61], and gene signature responses to EGFR pathway (BIOCARTA, REACTOME, and KOBAYASHI [62]), RTK and EGFR-TKI resistance gene signatures (REACTOME, WP, and GOMF), and NEPC differentiation (Li [26] and Beltran [63]) were used to determine associations with mean expression levels of SUCLG2, EGFR, VEGFA, EGF, and ERBB3. Cutoff values used to identify “SUCLG2-high,” “EGFR-high,” “VEGFA-high,” “EGF-high,” or “ERBB3-high” patients were predetermined by half the number of patients in both GSEAs. The NES and FDR were calculated using the GSEA. Correlations among mRNA levels of SUCLG2, EGFR, VEGFA, EGF, and ERBB3 were validated using TCGA and GSE21036 prostate cancer datasets. The mRNA expression of EGFR, VEGFA, EGF, and ERBB3 in LNCaP cells following androgen withdrawal at specific time points was analyzed using the GDS3358 dataset.

ScRNA-seq data acquisition and processing

ScRNA-seq data from the GEO database (GSE181294) [64] comprising prostate cancer tissue from 20 post castration CRPC patients with annotated treatment histories were processed using the Seurat R package. This dataset was selected to characterize cell type specific co expression of SUCLG2, EGFR, and VEGFA across epithelial, stromal, and immune compartments at single cell resolution in the post androgen deprivation setting. Quality control filtered cells with fewer than 200 genes, fewer than 600 UMI counts, greater than 20 percent mitochondrial content, and doublets identified by Scrublet [65], followed by LogNormalize normalization with a scale factor of 10,000 and identification of 2,000 highly variable genes using variance stabilizing transformation. Principal component analysis and UMAP dimensionality reduction were performed, with unsupervised Louvain clustering identifying distinct cell populations. Cell types were annotated using canonical markers, including KRT8 and KRT18 for epithelial cells, COL1A1 and DCN for fibroblasts, PECAM1 and VWF for endothelial cells, CD3D and CD3E for T cells, CD79A and MS4A1 for B cells, LYZ and CD14 for myeloid cells, and GNLY and NKG7 for NK cells, identified via FindAllMarkers using the Wilcoxon test with adjusted p value less than 0.05 and log2FC greater than 0.25. Feature plots visualized SUCLG2, EGFR, and VEGFA expression across UMAP clusters with color gradients from purple (low) to yellow (high expression). Differential expression testing used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Bonferroni correction, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, Inc.). Two-group comparisons were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test. Comparisons among three or more groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons post-hoc test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test if expected frequencies were less than 5. All tests were two-sided. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with the following thresholds applied uniformly: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001. All data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise stated. The number of independent biological replicates (n) for each experiment is reported in the corresponding figure legends.
