Inverse eigenvalue problem for Laplacian matrices of a graph
Shaun Fallat, Himanshu Gupta, Jephian C.-H. Lin

Abstract
For a given graph G G , we aim to determine the possible realizable spectra for a generalized (or sometimes referred to as a weighted) Laplacian matrix associated with G G . This new specialized inverse eigenvalue problem is considered for certain families of graphs and graphs on a small number of vertices. Related considerations include studying the possible ordered multiplicity lists associated with stars and complete graphs and graphs with a few vertices. Finally, we present a novel investigation, both theoretically and numerically, the minimum variance over a family of generalized Laplacian matrices with a size-normalized weighting.



Keywords:

Inverse eigenvalue problems, generalized Laplacian matrices, multiplicity lists, variance, quadratic programming, gradient descent.

AMS subject classifications:

05C50, 15A18, 15B57, 65F18.

Introduction

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph on
|V|=n
vertices. Define
\mathcal{S}(G)
as the set of
n\times n
real symmetric matrices
A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{i,j}\end{bmatrix}
such that

a_{i,j}\begin{cases}\neq 0&\text{if }\{i,j\}\in E(G),\\
=0&\text{if }\{i,j\}\notin E(G),\ i\neq j,\\
\in\mathbb{R}&\text{if }i=j.\end{cases}

The inverse eigenvalue problem for a graph (IEP-
G
) asks for all the possible spectra among matrices in
\mathcal{S}(G)
. In particular, the IEP-
G
aims to explore algebraic and combinatorial connections between the graph and its associated spectral properties. Research on the IEP-
G
is extensive and has produced a myriad of important results; see, e.g., the monograph
[
17
]
and the references therein. One of the motivations of the IEP-
G
comes from vibration theory; see, e.g., Gladwell
[
12
]
. However, the inverse problems studied in vibration theory often focus only on generalized Laplacian matrices. For example, if a spring-mass system is represented by a graph
G
, its vibration behavior is governed by the differential equation
M\ddot{\mathbf{x}}=-L\mathbf{x}
, where
M
is a diagonal matrix given by the weights of the masses,
\mathbf{x}
is a vector composed of the displacements of the masses, and
L
is a matrix in
\mathcal{S}(G)
with nonpositive off-diagonal entries. Therefore, we begin a study along these lines by naturally restricting our attention to such weighted matrices. Let
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
be the set of
n\times n
real symmetric matrices
A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{i,j}\end{bmatrix}
such that

a_{i,j}\begin{cases}<0&\text{if }\{i,j\}\in E(G),\\
=0&\text{if }\{i,j\}\notin E(G),\ i\neq j,\\
-\sum_{k:k\sim i}a_{i,k}&\text{if }i=j.\end{cases}

Analogous to the inverse eigenvalue problem for graphs (IEP-
G
), we are interested in initiating a study on an inverse eigenvalue problem among matrices in the class
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
, and abbreviate this problem as
IEPL
(inverse eigenvalue problem for generalized Laplacian matrices associated with a graph
G
).
To this end, we say a collection of real numbers
0\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
, is
Laplacian realizable
if there exists
L\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
with
\operatorname{spec}(L)=\{0,\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
.

Evidently, any matrix
A
in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
is singular and positive semidefinite (just like the combinatorial Laplacian matrix). Moreover, if
\{0,\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
is the eigenvalues of some
A
in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
for some
G
on
n
vertices, then it follows that the nullity of
A
(or
\operatorname{null}(A)
) is exactly equal to the number of components of
G
, and hence
\lambda_{2}(A)>0
if and only if
G
is connected. Along these lines, this paper studies the possible spectrum of a matrix or
\operatorname{spec}(A)
for
A
in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
. Given a real symmetric matrix
A
, we write
\lambda_{k}(A)
for its
k
-th smallest eigenvalue and
\operatorname{mult}_{A}(\lambda)
for the multiplicity of
\lambda
as an eigenvalue of
A
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix,
\alpha\subseteq X
, and
\beta\subseteq Y
, where
|X|=m
indexes the rows of
A
and and
|Y|=n
indexes the columns of
A
. The submatrix of
A
induced on the rows in
\alpha
and columns in
\beta
is denoted by
A[\alpha,\beta]
. The submatrix of
A
obtained by removing the rows in
\alpha
and columns in
\beta
is denoted by
A(\alpha,\beta)
. When
\alpha=\beta
, we simply write
A[\alpha]
and
A(\beta)
. When
\alpha=\beta=\{i\}
, we write
A[i]
and
A(i)
to make the notation easier. Subvectors are defined in a similar way. If
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are both vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then we write
\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{y}
(i.e.,
entrywise partial order
) to mean that the
i
th coordinate of
\mathbf{x}
is less than or equal to the
i
th coordinate of
\mathbf{y}
.

For graph notations,
P_{n}
,
C_{n}
, and
K_{n}
stands for the path, the cycle, and the complete graph on
n
vertices, respectively. The complete bipartite graph with
p
and
q
vertices on each side is denoted by
K_{p,q}
. We also use
K_{4}-e
for the graph obtained from
K_{4}
by removing an edge, and
\mathsf{Paw}
for the graph obtained from
K_{3}
by appending a leaf.

The paper is organized as follows. We begin, in Section 2, with a treatment of the inverse eigenvalue problem for generalized Laplacian matrices associated with a graph. We consider stars and complete graphs in generality and investigate other Laplacian inverse eigenvalue problems for graphs on a small number of vertices. We then move to studies on ordered multiplicity lists for generalized Laplacian matrices in Section 3. In Section 4, we consider the minimum variance of a spectra of a certain normalized generalized Laplacian matrix and study connections to a specific quadratic programming problem.

2
Inverse Eigenvalue Problem for Generalized Laplacian Matrices

As mentioned above the IEP-
G
has been well studied, but generally remains unresolved for most graphs. Hence we specialize and focus our attention on characterizing the possible spectra of matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
for a given graph.
We begin with a simple observation for a clique (or edge) on two vertices.

Observation 2.1
.

Consider the graph
K_{2}
. Then every matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(K_{2})
has the form

L=\begin{bmatrix}a&-a\\
-a&a\end{bmatrix}

for some real number
a>0
. It is straightforward to check that this matrix has spectrum
\{0,2a\}
. Therefore, for any real numbers
\{0,\lambda_{2}\}
with
\lambda_{2}>0
, there is a matrix in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(K_{2})
with the spectrum
\{0,\lambda_{2}\}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices and
m
edges.
Let
N
be its
(0,1,-1)
vertex-edge oriented incidence matrix of some orientation of the edges of
G
. Then the combinatorial Laplacian matrix of
G
is
NN^{\top}
. Moreover, given an edge weight assignment
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})
with
W=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{w})
, then the corresponding generalized Laplacian matrix of
G
is
NWN^{\top}
, which has the same nonzero eigenvalues as the matrices
W^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{\top}NW^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
N^{\top}NW
. Note that
NN^{\top}
is independent of the choice of the orientation of edges, while
N^{\top}N
could be different but its spectrum remains the same.

Moving forward to graphs on 3 vertices, we consider the path on three vertices (
P_{3}
).

Proposition 2.3
.

A set of real numbers
\{0,\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\}
with
0<\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}
is Laplacian realizable for
P_{3}
if and only if
\lambda_{3}\geq 3\lambda_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
G=P_{3}
. Then a matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
has the form

\begin{bmatrix}a&-a&0\\
-a&a+b&-b\\
0&-b&b\end{bmatrix}

for some
a,b>0
. Suppose
A
has eigenvalues
\{0,\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\}
with
0<\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}
. By
Remark

2.2
, it follows that matrix product

\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\
-1&2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{bmatrix}.

has eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\}
. Thus, we observe that the trace and the determinant imply the following conditions

\displaystyle 2a+2b

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3}=s,

\displaystyle 3ab

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}=p,

where
s
and
p
can be computed once
\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{3}
are given. Hence the system of equations

\displaystyle a+b

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}s,

\displaystyle ab

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}p

is solvable with
a,b>0
if and only if

\frac{1}{2}s\geq 2\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}p}.

Treating
\lambda_{2}
as a constant to solve the inequality above for
\lambda_{3}
, we arrive at the desired inequality
\lambda_{3}\geq 3\lambda_{2}
.
∎

In contrast, we verify that there are not so many restrictions on the possible spectra of matrices in
\mathcal{S}(K_{n})
. The following lemma is a recasting of
[
11
, Lemma 2.2]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
A
be a
p\times p
matrix and
B
a
q\times q
matrix with both
A
and
B
being generalized Laplacian matrices, where
\operatorname{spec}(A)=\{0,\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{p}\}
and
\operatorname{spec}(B)=\{0,\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{q}\}
, respectively. Then, for
\rho>0
the matrix

M=\begin{bmatrix}A+\rho qI&-\rho J_{p,q}\\
-\rho J_{q,p}&B+\rho pI\end{bmatrix}

is a generalized Laplacian matrix with spectrum

\{0,\rho(p+q),\mu_{2}+\rho q,\ldots,\mu_{p}+\rho q,\tau_{2}+\rho p,\ldots,\tau_{q}+\rho p\}.

Proof.

It is straightforward to observe that
M\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
and that
M
is a generalized Laplacian matrix.
Let
\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{p}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
A

with
\mathbf{x}_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\mathbf{1}

corresponding to the eigenvalues
0,\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{p}
. Further, by direct computation

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{i}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}

is an eigenvector of
M
with respect to
\mu_{2}+\rho q,\ldots,\mu_{p}+\rho q
for
i=2,\ldots,p
. Similarly, given the orthonormal basis
\{\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{q}\}
of
B
with
\mathbf{y}_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\mathbf{1}

corresponding to the eigenvalues
0,\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{q}
, the vector

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{y}_{i}\end{bmatrix}

is an eigenvector of
M
with respect to eigenvalues
\tau_{2}+\rho p,\ldots,\tau_{q}+\rho p
for
i=2,\ldots,q
.

By the trace condition, we determine the final eigenvalue of
M
is
\rho(p+q)
. This completes the proof.
∎

We now consider the IEPL for the complete graph on
n
vertices. Note that in
[
7
]
, the authors proved that an arbitrary collection
\Lambda
can be Laplacian realizable by
some
graph, but we verify that
K_{n}
is a graph for which any collection
\Lambda
is Laplacian realizable.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
\Lambda=\{0,\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
be a multiset of real numbers with
\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
positive. Then
\Lambda
is Laplacian realizable for
K_{n}
. Moreover,
K_{n}
is the only connected graph that can realize all possible such collections
\Lambda
by a generalized Laplacian matrix.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda
be given as hypothesized. We determine a matrix
M\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(K_{n})
satisfying
\operatorname{spec}(M)=\Lambda
by induction on
n\geq 2
. Using
Observation

2.1
, we know
K_{2}
realizes all possible
\Lambda
with
|\Lambda|=2
. By induction, suppose
K_{n-1}
realizes all such possible
\Lambda
with
|\Lambda|=n-1
. For any
\Lambda
with
|\Lambda|=n
, we will construct an
n\times n
matrix
M\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(K_{n})
with
\operatorname{spec}(M)=\Lambda
.

Let
\Lambda=\{0,\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
be given. We may assume that
0<\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
. First we choose
\rho=\frac{\lambda_{2}}{n}
,
p=n-1
, and
q=1
so that
\rho(p+q)=\lambda_{2}
. Next, by the induction hypothesis, we choose a matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(K_{n-1})
with the nonzero eigenvalues
\lambda_{3}-\rho,\ldots,\lambda_{n}-\rho
. Note that these values are positive since
0<\rho=\frac{\lambda_{2}}{n}<\lambda_{2}
. By
Lemma

2.4
, the matrix

M=\begin{bmatrix}A+\rho I&-\rho\mathbf{1}\\
-\rho\mathbf{1}^{\top}&\rho(n-1)\end{bmatrix}

is a matrix in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(K_{n})
with spectrum
\Lambda
.

Conversely, if a connected graph
G
realizes all possible
\Lambda
, then it realizes the collection
\{0,\lambda,\ldots,\lambda\}
by some generalized Laplacian matrix
L
. Note that
L-\lambda I
is a rank-one matrix in
\mathcal{S}(G)
. Since
G
is connected,
G
must be the complete graph, as the only connected graph with minimum rank equal to one is the complete graph (see
[
9
, Observation 1.2]
).
∎

We now consider the IEPL for stars, namely for the graph
K_{1,n-1}
. Recall that the
k
-th elementary symmetric function
\sigma_{k}
on a set
S
is the sum of the product of all possible combinations of
k
numbers in
S
, where we vacuously define
\sigma_{0}=1
for any set.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\Lambda=\{0,\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
be a set of real numbers with
0<\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
. Then
\Lambda
is Laplacian realizable for
K_{1,n-1}
if and only if
(-1)^{k}f(-\lambda_{k})\leq 0
for
k=2,\ldots,n-1
, where

f(x)=\frac{\sigma_{0}}{1}x^{n-1}+\frac{\sigma_{1}}{2}x^{n-2}+\frac{\sigma_{2}}{3}x^{n-3}+\cdots+\frac{\sigma_{n-1}}{n}

with
\sigma_{k}
being the
k
-th elementary symmetric function of the positive numbers
\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
.

Proof.

Assume that the positive numbers
\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
are given and that
\sigma_{k}
is their
k
-th elementary symmetric function. Thus,

p(x)=\sigma_{0}x^{n-1}+\sigma_{1}x^{n-2}+\cdots+\sigma_{n-1}

has
n-1
negative roots
-\lambda_{2},\ldots,-\lambda_{n}
. Consequently,

q(x)=x^{n-1}p\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)=\sigma_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\sigma_{n-2}x^{n-2}+\cdots+\sigma_{0}

also has
n-1
negative roots
-\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}},\ldots,-\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}
.

On the other hand, suppose
M\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(K_{1,n-1})
is defined by the weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-1}
and has
\operatorname{spec}(M)=\{0,\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
. By
Remark

2.2
,
M=NWN^{\top}
and
W^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{\top}NW^{\frac{1}{2}}
have the same nonzero eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
. By direct computation,
N^{\top}N=J+I
, where
J
is the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
all-ones matrix, and any
k\times k
principal minor of
N^{\top}N
is
k+1
. Therefore, given
\alpha\subseteq[n-1]
with
|\alpha|=k
, the principal minor of
W^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{\top}NW^{\frac{1}{2}}
is
(k+1)\prod_{i\in\alpha}w_{i}
. Since the sum of all
k\times k
principal minors of
W^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{\top}NW^{\frac{1}{2}}
is
\sigma_{k}
by its spectrum, we know the
k
-th elementary symmetric function of
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-1}
is equal to
s_{k}=\frac{\sigma_{k}}{k+1}
for
k=0,\ldots,n-1
, which is the coefficient of
x^{n-1-k}
in
f(x)
. Therefore,
\Lambda
is Laplacian realizable if and only if
f(x)
has
n-1
negative roots, namely,
-w_{1},\ldots,-w_{n-1}
.

Now we can relate
f(x)
and
q(x)
via the following.
Define the function

Q(x)=\int_{0}^{x}q(t)\,dt=\frac{\sigma_{n-1}}{n}x^{n}+\frac{\sigma_{n-2}}{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+\frac{\sigma_{0}}{1}x.

Note that the constant term is
Q(0)=\int_{0}^{0}q(t)\,dt=0
. Also, observe that
Q(x)=f(\frac{1}{x})\cdot x^{n}
. Therefore,
f(x)
has
n-1
negative roots if and only if
Q(x)
has
n-1
negative roots and a zero root.
Since
Q^{\prime}(x)=q(x)
, the critical points of
Q(x)
occur at values
-\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}},\ldots,-\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}
. Since
Q(x)
has a positive leading coefficient,
Q(x)
has
n-1
negative roots and a zero root if and only if
Q(-\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}})<0
(which is guaranteed) and
Q(-\frac{1}{\lambda_{n-1}})\geq 0
,
Q(-\frac{1}{\lambda_{n-2}})\leq 0
, and so on. Equivalently,
(-1)^{n-k}Q(-\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}})\leq 0
for
k=2,\ldots,n-1
. As
Q(-\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}})
and
f(-\lambda_{k})(-1)^{n}
have the same sign, we know
(-1)^{k}f(-\lambda_{k})\leq 0
for
k=2,\ldots,n-1
if and only if
f(x)
has
n-1
negative roots if and only if
\Lambda
is Laplacian realizable for
K_{1,n-1}
.
∎

Theorem

2.6
.

Example 2.7
.

For the star on

vertices,
K_{1,2}
,

f(-\lambda_{2})=\frac{1}{6}\lambda_{2}(3\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}).

Thus,

.

\displaystyle f(-\lambda_{3})

.

The proof of

Although a complete characterization of the potential Laplacian spectra is presumed difficult, we can run a related simulation. For each graph

.

3
The Ordered Multiplicity List Problem for Generalized Laplacian Matrices

Let
G
be a connected graph on
n
vertices and suppose
L\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
with

\operatorname{spec}(L)=\{0^{(1)},\lambda_{2}^{(m_{2})},\lambda_{3}^{(m_{3})},\ldots,\lambda_{q}^{(m_{q})}\}
, where

\lambda_{i}^{(m_{i})}
means the eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
of
L
has multiplicity
m_{i}
(in this case we have
1+\sum m_{i}=n
). If further we assume that

0<\lambda_{2}<\lambda_{3}<\cdots<\lambda_{q}
, then the
ordered multiplicity list for
L
is defined to be
(m_{1}:=1,m_{2},\ldots,m_{q})
. A purpose of this section is to consider all possible ordered multiplicity lists over matrices in the set
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
.

We begin this study of ordered multiplicity lists by considering the case of
n
simple eigenvalues.

Theorem 3.1
.

The ordered multiplicity list
(1,\ldots,1)
is Laplacian realizable for every connected graph.

Proof.

Let
G
a connected graph on
n
vertices. Let
T
be a spanning tree of
G
with edges
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n-1}
. Let
G_{k}
be the graph with the vertex set
V(G)
and the edge set
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}
. Then the number of components of
G_{k}
is
n-k
.

Choose
L_{1}
to be the combinatorial Laplacian matrix of
G_{1}
. Hence
\operatorname{spec}(L_{1})=\{0^{(n-1)},2\}
. That is, the weight of
e_{1}
is
1
.

For
k=2,\ldots,n-1
, assign the weights of
e_{k}
using the following scheme.

1.

Let
\delta
be the minimum among all differences between any two distinct eigenvalues of
L_{k-1}
.

2.

Let
L_{k}
be the generalized Laplacian matrix of
G_{k}
with the existing weights of
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k-1}
and a weight
w_{k}
of
e_{k}
to be determined.

3.

Choose
w_{k}
small enough so that the perturbation from
L_{k-1}
to
L_{k}
does not move the eigenvalues further than
\frac{\delta}{3}
.

Note that
L_{k}
is obtained from
L_{k-1}
by adding the
2\times 2
matrix

w_{k}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\end{bmatrix}

to the
2\times 2
submatrix induced by the rows and columns corresponding to the endpoints of the edge
e_{k}
. Observe that the magnitude of the perturbation is continuous and controlled by

. Moreover, by the choice of

, no distinct eigenvalues of

will merge into a single eigenvalue in

. However, one of the zero eigenvalues of

becomes nonzero since

has fewer components than

has

has

distinct eigenvalues.

Finally, one may add small weights to edges in

in a way so that distinct eigenvalues remain distinct. Thus, we have constructed a matrix in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
with the ordered multiplicity list
(1,\ldots,1)
.
∎

It is well known that for any tree

and for any

must be simple (see
[

]
). Further for any connected graph

and any

we know that 0 is a simple eigenvalue. In the next result we verify that for a connected bipartite graph

Proposition 3.2
.

Let

be a connected bipartite graph. Then any matrix in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
has its largest eigenvalue simple.

Proof.

Let
A
be a matrix in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
. Since

is a connected bipartite graph, there is a unique way to partition

into

such that all edges are between

and
Y
. Let

be a diagonal matrix whose rows/columns are indexed by

. Define the
i,i
-entry of

as

if

and
-1
if

and

is an irreducible nonnegative matrix with

Theorem 3.4

4
Minimum Variance

As we have seen in previous sections, there is a lower bound on the ratio
\frac{\lambda_{2}(A)}{\lambda_{3}(A)}
for any matrices
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(P_{3})
, and, in general, the nonzero eigenvalues cannot be too concentrated unless
G
is a complete graph. In this section, we consider the minimum variance of the nonzero eigenvalues when we fix the sum of weights to be equal to the number of edges. Equivalently, we focus on
size-normalized generalized
Laplacian matrices defined as

\mathcal{S}^{s}_{L}(G)=\{A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G):\operatorname{tr}(A)=2|E(G)|\}.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
with the spectrum
\{0,\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
. Define

\mathbb{E}(A)=\frac{\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}}{n-1},\ \operatorname{Var}(A)=\frac{\sum_{i=2}^{n}(\lambda_{i}-\mathbb{E}(A))^{2}}{n-1},\text{ and }p_{2}(A)=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}.

Definition 4.2
.

The
minimum variance
of
G
is defined as

\operatorname{mv}(G)=\inf\{\operatorname{Var}(A):A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}^{s}(G)\}.

Remark 4.3
.

It is well-known (and by direct computation as well) that

\operatorname{Var}(A)=\frac{p_{2}(A)}{n-1}-\mathbb{E}(A)^{2}.

Moreover, when
G
is a graph on
m
edges and
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}^{s}(G)
, we have

\operatorname{Var}(A)=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(A^{2})}{n-1}-\left(\frac{2m}{n-1}\right)^{2}.

Since
\mathcal{S}_{L}^{s}(G)
is not a compact set, there might not be a matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}^{s}(G)
that attains
\operatorname{mv}(G)
. However, the infimum must occur in the closure of
\mathcal{S}_{L}^{s}(G)
, which allows some of the weights to be zero. This means the variances realized by matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
can be arbitrarily close to
\operatorname{mv}(G)
, which still provide significant worthwhile information for studying the IEPL.

On the other hand, let
\operatorname{var}_{\mathbf{1}}(G)
be the variance of the combinatorial Laplacian matrix of
G
. Then by definition

\operatorname{mv}(G)\leq\operatorname{var}_{\mathbf{1}}(G).

Here we provide alternative methods to calculate
\operatorname{Var}(A)
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, the
line graph
of
G
, denoted by
L(G)
, is graph whose vertices are
E
and two edges
e
and
e^{\prime}
are adjacent in
L(G)
if they share a common vertex.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
G
be a connected graph and
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
with the weight vector
\mathbf{w}
. Then
p_{2}(A)=\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{w}
, where
M_{2}=4I+B
and
B
is the adjacency matrix of the line graph of
G
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{w}=\begin{bmatrix}w_{i}\end{bmatrix}
,
W=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{w})
, and
W^{\frac{1}{2}}
the diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are given by
w_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, for
i=1,2,\ldots,m
. Let
N
be an vertex-edge incidence matrix of
G
and
M=\begin{bmatrix}m_{i,j}\end{bmatrix}=2I+B
. Then
A=NWN^{\top}
and
M=N^{\top}N
. Moreover,
A=NW^{\frac{1}{2}}W^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{\top}
and
W^{\frac{1}{2}}MW^{\frac{1}{2}}=W^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{\top}NW^{\frac{1}{1}}
have the same nonzero eigenvalues.

Therefore,

\displaystyle p_{2}(A)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}((W^{\frac{1}{2}}MW^{\frac{1}{2}})^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}(w_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}m_{i,j}w_{j}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{2}=\sum_{i,j}w_{i}m_{i,j}^{2}w_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{w}^{\top}(M\circ M)\mathbf{w},

where
\circ
signifies the entrywise product of conformally sized matrices. We also note that
M\circ M=4I+B=M_{2}
.
∎

Observe that
N^{\top}N
is a positive semidefinite matrix. (Or, equivalently, the adjacency matrix of a line graph has its minimum eigenvalue at least
-2
; see, e.g.,
[
1
]
.) Therefore,
M_{2}
is invertible with its minimum eigenvalue at least
2
.

By
Remark

4.3
and
Proposition

4.4
, for any given graph
G
with
m
edges, finding the value of
\operatorname{mv}(G)
is equivalent to finding the minimum of
\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{w}
subject to
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{w}=m
and
\mathbf{w}
being entrywise nonnegative.

By dropping the last condition, this minimization problem has a unique solution.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
G
be a graph with
m
edges and
M_{2}=4I+B
, where
B
is the adjacency matrix of the line graph of
G
. The minimization problem

\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{w}\\
\text{\rm subject to}&\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{w}=m\end{array}

attains its minimum at

\mathbf{w}=kM_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{1}\text{ with }k=\frac{m}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}M_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}

and the minimum value is

\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{w}=\frac{m^{2}}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}M_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}.

Therefore,

\operatorname{mv}(G)\geq\frac{m^{2}}{(n-1)\mathbf{1}^{\top}M_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}-\left(\frac{2m}{n-1}\right)^{2}.

while the equality holds if
M_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{1}
is entrywise nonnegative.

Proof.

By direct computation, the gradient over
\mathbf{w}
are

\nabla\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{w}=2\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\text{ and }\nabla\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{1}^{\top}.

Therefore, by the Lagrange multiplier theorem, the minimum happens when
k\mathbf{1}=M_{2}\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{w}=kM_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{1}
for some
k
. Since
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{w}=k\mathbf{1}^{\top}M_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{1}=m
, we have

k=\frac{m}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}M_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}.

The rest of the statements follow from direct computation.
∎

We now move on to consider the minimum variance associated with a graph.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices and
m
edges. Define the
approximated minimum variance
of

as

\operatorname{amv}(G)=\frac{\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{w}}{n-1}-\left(\frac{2m}{n-1}\right)^{2}=\frac{m^{2}}{(n-1)\mathbf{1}^{\top}M_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}-\left(\frac{2m}{n-1}\right)^{2},

where

\mathbf{w}=kM_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{1}\text{ with }k=\frac{m}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}M_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}.

We say
G
is

if
M^{-1}\mathbf{1}
is entrywise positive.

Thus, we know the
\operatorname{amv}(G)\leq\operatorname{mv}(G)\leq\operatorname{var}_{\mathbf{1}}(G)
for any connected graph

.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices and
m
edges whose line graph is

\operatorname{mv}(G)=\frac{m(4+r)}{n-1}-\left(\frac{2m}{n-1}\right)^{2}.

When the line graph of

Moreover,

by
Proposition

.
∎

Corollary 4.9

Let

Let

. Along with the condition

.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let

2.

3.

\frac{d_{k+1}}{d_{k}}\to 2+\sqrt{3}

By the Laplace expansion along the first row of

\displaystyle d_{k+2}

\displaystyle=4d_{k+1}-d_{k},

\displaystyle d_{0}

\displaystyle=1,\ d_{1}=4.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let

.

be its degree sequence. Then

\operatorname{var}_{\mathbf{1}}(G)=\frac{2m+\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}}{n-1}-\left(\frac{2m}{n-1}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

Let

, where

is the adjacency matrix of the line graph of

\mathbf{1}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{1}=4m+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\binom{d_{i}}{2}=4m+\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=2m+\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}.

Theorem 4.14
.

Over all trees
T
on
n
vertices,
\operatorname{var}_{\mathbf{1}}(T)
is uniquely maximized by the star with
\operatorname{var}_{\mathbf{1}}(T)=n-2
and is uniquely minimized by the path.

For general graphs
G
,
\operatorname{var}_{\mathbf{1}}(G)\leq\frac{m}{n-1}\left(n-\frac{2m}{n-1}\right)
.

Proof.

For trees
T
on
n
vertices,

\operatorname{var}_{\mathbf{1}}(T)=2+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}}{n-1}-2^{2}

by
Proposition

4.13
, so the quantity depends only on
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}
.

We claim that the sum of squares of degrees is maximized by the star and minimized by the path among all trees on
n
vertices. To see this, pick a leaf
i
of
T
with its unique neighbor
j
and pick some vertex
k
other than
i
and

. Then

is again a tree. If
d_{j}\leq d_{k}
, then the sum of squares of degrees of
T^{\prime}
is strictly larger than that of
T
. Repeating this operation with
i
an arbitrary leaf and
k
a vertex of maximum degree, the sum is increasing throughout the process, which terminates only when the tree becomes a star. On the other hand, if
d_{j}=d_{k}+1
, then the sum of squares of degrees of
T^{\prime}
equals that of
T
; and if
d_{j}\geq d_{k}+2
, then the sum of squares of degrees of
T^{\prime}
is strictly less than that of
T
. Repeating the operation with
i
an arbitrary leaf and
k
an endpoint of the longest path, the sum reaches its unique minimum when the tree is a path.

For general graph, there are several bounds for the sum of squares of degrees in
[
5
]
. In particular, de Caen
[

]
showed that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}\leq m\left(\frac{2m}{n-1}+n-2\right),

which gives the stated upper bound of
\operatorname{var}_{\mathbf{1}}(G)
.
∎

4.1
Algorithms for Finding the Minimum Variance

For this subsection, we provide algorithms to solve the minimum variance of a graph or, equivalently,
Problem

4.15
below.

Problem 4.15
.

Let
G
be a graph on
m
edges. Let
M_{2}=4I+B
, where
B
is the adjacency matrix of the line graph of
G
. Find the minimum of

f(\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{w}

in the region

R=\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{E(G)}:\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{w}=m\text{ and }\mathbf{w}\geq 0\}.

Recall that a function
g
is
concave
(upward) if

g((1-s)\mathbf{x}+s\mathbf{y})\geq(1-s)g(\mathbf{x})+sg(\mathbf{y})

for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
in the domain of the function and for any
s\in[0,1]
. The following proposition is well-known in the subject on quadratic programming.

Proposition 4.16
.

If
M_{2}
is a positive definite matrix, then the function
f(\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{w}
is concave. Moreover, there is a unique local minimum in any compact and convex region.

Proof.

We first show the concavity. Let
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
be two different points in the region. Define
g(s)=f((1-s)\mathbf{x}+s\mathbf{y})
with
s\in[0,1]
, which can be expanded as

g(s)=f(\mathbf{x}+s(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}))=\mathbf{x}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{x}+2\mathbf{x}^{\top}M_{2}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})s+(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})^{\top}M_{2}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})s^{2}.

Thus,
g(s)
is a quadratic function in
s
with a positive quadratic term since
M_{2}
is positive definite, which is a concave function.

A continuous function attains a global minimum at
\mathbf{x}
on a compact region. For any point
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{x}
in the domain, the segment
L
connecting
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
is also in the region by its convexity. Note that the function
f
restricted on
L
is a quadratic function with positive quadratic term. Since
\mathbf{x}
is a global minimum, the function
f
restricted on
L
must be an strictly increasing function from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
. Therefore,
\mathbf{y}
cannot be a local minimum, and
\mathbf{x}
is the unique local minimum.
∎

With
Proposition

4.16
applied to our case, it sheds light on two facts: First, if a point is verified as a local minimum by its derivatives, then it is also the global minimum. Second, numerical methods for finding a local minimum, such as gradient descent, are guaranteed to find the global maximum. These facts lead to algorithms for finding the minimum, where one is exact and the other is numerical.

Define the
support
of a vector as the set of indices on which the vector is nonzero. We first characterize the conditions for a vector
\mathbf{w}
with
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{w})=\alpha
being a local minimum.

Proposition 4.17
.

Let
m
,
M_{2}
,
f
, and
R
be as in
Problem

4.15
. Then
\mathbf{w}
is a local minimum in
R
with the
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{w})=\alpha
if and only if

1.

M_{2}[\alpha]^{-1}\mathbf{1}
is entrywise positive and

2.

M_{2}(\alpha]M_{2}[\alpha]^{-1}\mathbf{1}
is entrywise greater than or equal to
1
.

If these conditions hold, then
\mathbf{w}[\alpha]=kM_{2}[\alpha]^{-1}\mathbf{1}
with
k=\frac{m}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}M_{2}[\alpha]^{-1}\mathbf{1}}
and
\mathbf{w}(\alpha)=\mathbf{0}
is the only local minimum with support
\alpha
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

4.16
,
f
becomes a quadratic function with positive quadratic term on any segment. Therefore, a point is a local minimum if and only if the directional derivative at this point is positive or zero for any possible direction.

At
\mathbf{w}
, the gradient of
f
is

\nabla f(\mathbf{w})=2\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}.

That is, given any unit vector
\mathbf{d}
, the directional derivative of
f
along
\mathbf{d}
is

D_{\mathbf{d}}f(\mathbf{w})=2\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{d}.

Since we only care about the signs of
D_{\mathbf{d}}f(\mathbf{w})
, we know that
\mathbf{w}
is a local minimum if and only if
\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{d}\geq 0
for any possible direction
\mathbf{d}
.

Since
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{w})=\alpha
, the vectors
\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j}
for any (
i,j\in\alpha
) or (
i\notin\alpha
and
j\in\alpha
) are some possible directions for
\mathbf{w}
to move around
R
. In fact, all possible directions are the linear combinations of these vectors using nonnegative coefficients. Consequently,
\mathbf{w}
is a local minimum if and only if
\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{d}\geq 0
for these vectors
\mathbf{d}
.

By choosing
\mathbf{d}
as
\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j}
and
\mathbf{e}_{j}-\mathbf{e}_{i}
with
i,j\in\alpha
, we know the entries of
\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}
on
\alpha
are the same. By choosing
\mathbf{u}
as
\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j}
with
i\notin\alpha
and
j\in\alpha
, we know the entries of
\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}
outside
\alpha
are greater than or equal to those on
\alpha
. Since
\mathbf{w}
is zero outside
\alpha
, we have

\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}=\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}[\alpha]^{\top}M_{2}[\alpha]&\mathbf{w}[\alpha]^{\top}M_{2}[\alpha)\end{bmatrix}.

In summary, the entries of
\mathbf{w}[\alpha]^{\top}M_{2}[\alpha]=k\mathbf{1}
for some
k
, while each entry of
\mathbf{w}[\alpha]^{\top}M_{2}[\alpha)
is greater than or equal to
k
.

Since
M_{2}[\alpha]
is a principal submatrix of a positive definite matrix
M_{2}
,
M_{2}[\alpha]
is invertible. Thus,
\mathbf{w}[\alpha]^{\top}M_{2}[\alpha]=k\mathbf{1}
implies
\mathbf{w}[\alpha]=kM_{2}[\alpha]^{-1}\mathbf{1}
. Also,
\mathbf{w}(\alpha)=\mathbf{0}
as
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{w})=\alpha
. Thus, we may derive
k=\frac{m}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}M_{2}[\alpha]^{-1}\mathbf{1}}>0
from
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{w}=m
. With this formula of
\mathbf{w}
, it is a local minimum if and only if
M_{2}[\alpha]^{-1}\mathbf{1}
is entrywise positive, which ensures
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{w})=\alpha
, and
M_{2}(\alpha]\mathbf{w}[\alpha]
is entrywise larger than or equal to the constant value on
M_{2}[\alpha]\mathbf{w}[\alpha]
, which ensures
\mathbf{w}
is a local minimum. As
\mathbf{w}[\alpha]=kM_{2}[\alpha]^{-1}\mathbf{1}
, the second condition is equivalent to
M_{2}(\alpha]\cdot kM_{2}[\alpha]^{-1}\mathbf{1}
is entrywise greater than or equal to
k
, where
k
can be canceled on both sides.
∎

Based on
Proposition

4.17
, we may define
\mathbf{w}_{\alpha}
such that whose entries on
\alpha
match the vector
kM_{2}[\alpha]^{-1}\mathbf{1}
with
k=\frac{m}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}M_{2}[\alpha]^{-1}\mathbf{1}}
and otherwise zero. We say
\alpha
is
eligible
for
G
if
\alpha
satisfies the two conditions in
Proposition

4.17
.

Remark 4.18
.

Combining
Proposition

4.16
, there is a unique
\alpha\in[m]
that is eligible for
G
. Thus, if some eligible
\alpha\subseteq[m]
is found, then
\mathbf{w}_{\alpha}
must be the global minimum, and we do not have to check others. Meanwhile, by checking all possible
\alpha
, we found the minimum value of
f
, which provides an exact, yet exhaustive, algorithm for searching the minimum.

Unfortunately, this requires checking all
2^{m}-1
possible
\alpha
, as
\alpha\neq\emptyset
. Recall that for linear programming in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
r
restrictions, one also needs to check
\binom{r}{n}
exhaustively, so it might not be easy to improve it further.

Example 4.20
.

Let

be the double star obtained from

and

Observe that

M_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}J_{p}+3I_{p}&\mathbf{1}_{p}&O\\
\mathbf{1}_{p}^{\top}&4&\mathbf{1}_{q}^{\top}\\
O&\mathbf{1}_{q}&J_{q}+3I_{q}\end{bmatrix}.

Then by direct computation, we have

\mathbf{w}_{[m]}=M_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{1}=k_{1}\begin{bmatrix}(q+3)\mathbf{1}_{p}\\
\frac{9-pq}{3}\\
(p+3)\mathbf{1}_{q}\end{bmatrix}\text{ with }k_{1}=\frac{p+q+1}{\frac{5}{3}pq+3p+3q+3}.

This means,
G
is eligible if and only if

. On the other hand, let
\alpha=E(K_{1,p})\cup E(K_{1,q})
. By direct computation, we have

M_{2}[\alpha]^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{p+q}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{p+3}\mathbf{1}_{p}\\
\frac{1}{q+3}\mathbf{1}_{q},\end{bmatrix}

which is entrywise positive. Also,

M_{2}(\alpha]M_{2}[\alpha]^{-1}\mathbf{1}=\frac{p}{p+3}+\frac{q}{q+3}=\frac{2pq+3p+3q}{pq+3p+3q+9}

is entrywise greater than or equal to

whenever

. In this case,

\mathbf{w}_{\alpha}=k_{2}\begin{bmatrix}(q+3)\mathbf{1}_{p}\\

(p+3)\mathbf{1}_{q}\end{bmatrix}\text{ with }k_{2}=\frac{p+q+1}{2pq+3p+3q}.

Therefore, the minimum variance of

otherwise.

Next, we establish the gradient descent algorithm designed for solving
Problem

4.15
. Since
f
is quadratic along any direction, we may calculate precisely how far to go on each direction to decrease the function value.

Observation 4.21
.

Given a point
\mathbf{w}
and a direction
\mathbf{d}
, we may compute

f(\mathbf{w}+t\mathbf{d})=\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{w}+2\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{d}t+\mathbf{d}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{d}t^{2}.

The minimum of this function, with respect to
t
, happens at
t=\frac{-\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{d}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{d}}
. In the case when
\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j}
, we have
-\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{d}=(\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2})_{j}-(\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2})_{i}
and
\mathbf{d}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{d}
is
6
or
8
, depending on edge
i
and edge
j
are incident or not.

Algorithm 4.22
.

This algorithm solves
Problem

4.15
.

Input
: a graph
G

Output
: the
\mathbf{w}
that minimizes
\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{w}
with
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{w}=m
and
\mathbf{w}\geq 0
.

1.

Start with
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{E(G)}
.

2.

Compute
M_{2}\mathbf{w}
and let
i
be the index of its minimum entry.

3.

For each
j
, compute
t_{j}=\frac{(M_{2}\mathbf{w})_{j}-(M_{2}\mathbf{w})_{i}}{8}
.

4.

Choose
j
so that
\eta_{j}=\min\{t_{j},(\mathbf{w})_{j}\}
is maximized.

5.

Let
\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j}
and update
\mathbf{w}\leftarrow\mathbf{w}+\eta_{j}\cdot\mathbf{d}
.

6.

Repeat from Step 2 as needed.

Theorem 4.23
.

Algorithm

4.22
generates a sequence of
\mathbf{w}
that converges to the minimum of
f(\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{w}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{w}
subject to the given conditions.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{w}_{0}=\mathbf{1}
and
\mathbf{w}_{n}
be the
\mathbf{w}
vector in each iteration in
Algorithm

4.22
. By
Proposition

4.16
, there is a unique local minimum
\mathbf{w}_{\min}
in the region. We will show that
\mathbf{w}_{n}
converges to
\mathbf{w}_{\min}
.

First, we define a function

\eta(\mathbf{w})=\max_{j}\min\left\{\frac{(M_{2}\mathbf{w})_{j}-\min(M_{2}\mathbf{w})}{8},(\mathbf{w})_{j}\right\},

where
\min(M_{2}\mathbf{w})
is the minimum entry of
M_{2}\mathbf{w}
. By definition,
\eta(\mathbf{w})\geq 0
. Moreover,
\eta(\mathbf{w})=0
if and only if
\mathbf{w}
is a local minimum by
Proposition

4.17
, which means
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}_{\min}
. Also, we see that the algorithm updates
\mathbf{w}
by
\mathbf{w}_{n+1}=\mathbf{w}_{n}+\eta(\mathbf{w}_{n})\mathbf{d}
. According to the formula in
Observation

4.21
,
f(\mathbf{w}_{n})
is decreasing whenever
\eta(\mathbf{w}_{n})>0
; moreover,

|f(\mathbf{w}_{n+1})-f(\mathbf{w}_{n})|\geq 6\eta(\mathbf{w}_{n})^{2}

since
\mathbf{d}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{d}\geq 6
.

We claim that
\eta(\mathbf{w}_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Given any
\epsilon>0
, if there are infinitely many
\mathbf{w}_{n}
such that
\eta(\mathbf{w}_{n})\geq\epsilon
, then
f(\mathbf{w}_{n})
would drop by at least
6\epsilon^{2}
for infinitely many times, violating the fact that
f(\mathbf{w})\geq 0
. Therefore, there are only finitely many
\mathbf{w}_{n}
with
\eta(\mathbf{w}_{n})\geq\epsilon
, which is equivalent to
\eta(\mathbf{w}_{n})\to 0
.

Since
\mathbf{w}_{n}
is a sequence on the compact region, there is a convergent subsequence
\mathbf{w}_{n_{i}}
that converges to
\mathbf{w}_{\infty}
. By the previous discussion,
\eta(\mathbf{w}_{\infty})=0
, which means
\mathbf{w}_{\infty}=\mathbf{w}_{\min}
. Therefore,
f(\mathbf{w}_{n})
is decreasing and converges to the minimum.

Finally, we claim that not just
\mathbf{w}_{n_{i}}
, but also
\mathbf{w}_{n}
, converges to
\mathbf{w}_{\min}
. To see this, pick an arbitrary unit vector
\mathbf{d}
such that
\mathbf{w}_{\min}+t\mathbf{d}
is in the region for any small
t>0
. By
Observation

4.21
, we have

f(\mathbf{w}_{\min}+t\mathbf{d})\geq f(\mathbf{w}_{\min})+2t^{2}

since the minimum eigenvalue of
M_{2}
is at least
2
and
\mathbf{d}^{\top}M_{2}\mathbf{d}\geq 2
. This means
\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_{\min}\|\geq t
implies
f(\mathbf{w})-f(\mathbf{w}_{\min})\geq 2t^{2}
. Since
f(\mathbf{w}_{n})\to f(\mathbf{w}_{\min})
as
n\to\infty
, we also have
\mathbf{w}_{n}\to\mathbf{w}_{\min}
.
∎

5
Conclusions and Future Considerations

In this work, we have focused our attention on the possible spectra allowed for generalized Laplacian matrices associated with a given graph. For graphs on at most four vertices we derived results on potential spectra and allowed ordered multiplicity lists for generalized Laplacians. For the special case of complete graphs and stars we have general claims concerning the possible spectra of generalized Laplacians associated with these two families of graphs. In Section 4, we adjusted our focus and considered certain moments associated with the eigenvalues of normalized weightings of generalized Laplacian matrices of graphs. A key element in this work was the minimum variance of a graph corresponding to matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{L}^{s}(G)
. We then proceed to either compute or bound the minimum variance of specific families of graphs, including regular graphs and trees, and develop tools from quadratic programming to determine the minimum of a related quadratic form associated with a matrix (labeled
M_{2}
) of a graph in this context.

We close this section with a list of related potentially interesting directions concerning the spectra of generalized Laplacian matrices associated with a graph.

1.

Concerning the Laplacian spectra of small graphs, we ask is the boundary for
\mathsf{Paw},C_{4}
, and
K_{4}-e
in
Figure

1
linear?

2.

We have considered describing the allowed spectrum over all matrices
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
for given graphs or for specified families of graphs. A natural specialization along these lines is, for a given graph
G
, to consider computing the maximum possible multiplicity over the matrices
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
and to minimize the number of distinct eigenvalues over all matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
.

3.

Finally, strong matrix properties (such as the Strong Arnold Property or the
Strong Spectral Property, see
[
3
]
) have become very important for the inverse eigenvalue problem for graphs. Thus, it seems reasonable to study potential strong properties of matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
and their applications to matrix perturbations and possible spectral properties.
