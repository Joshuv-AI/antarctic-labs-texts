Kinematic differences in forehand serve-receiving techniques of the male tennis players at low and high-speed serves
Authors: Yuxin Zhang, Zhouye Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The purpose of this study was to compare the joint, racket, and ball kinematics between the different levels of male tennis players in tennis serve-return at slow and high serve speeds. Thirty male tennis players were divided into an advanced group (n = 15) and an intermediate group (n = 15) based on skill level. The advanced group and intermediate group matched shake hand-grip players performed serve-receive test at the different serve speeds. Kinematic data were collected on the trunk, upper limbs, racket and ball to compare the differences between the advanced and intermediate groups at different serve speeds. At both serve speeds, the AG has faster racket speed and ball speed, and is closer to the baseline placement (p<0.05). It is characterized by a faster trunk turn, greater speed with the shoulder joint forward and outward and greater angle of wrist flexion and extension during the stroke (p<0.05), and greater speed with the forearm inward and internal rotation during the follow-through swing, at the same time, there is a difference in the angle of the racket to the ground between the advanced group and an intermediate group(p<0.05). Elbow internal rotation and abduction angular velocity in the backswing phase, Timing of peak racket resultant velocity and Wrist extension in the stroke phase, and shoulder and elbow joint activities in the follow-through phase were significantly correlated with the ball speed of the return. With faster serve speeds, higher quality serve-receive can be achieved by shortening the duration of the backswing lifting the upper arm and rotating the forearm inward quickly, accelerating the speed of movement of wrist joints at the impact phase, adjusting the speed of shoulder adduction and elbow internal rotation to complete the follow-through swing. These findings help to improve elite players’ serve-receive skills at faster serve speed.

Introduction

Serve and serve-receive technique are the important technical aspect of tennis1. Tennis strokes produce a transfer of movement from the racket to the ball, and this transfer is an open linkage system from the lower limbs, trunk, upper limbs, and hands to the racket2. Any break in segment of the kinetic chain may reduce the power or energy of a tennis stroke and may lead to injury3, increasing racket speed minimizes the risk of injury for the players4.

The forehand serve-receive technique is similar to the forehand ball drop ground technique, but the forehand serve-receive technique responds to faster incoming ball speeds. The players enhance the racket velocity of forehand stroke by the angular velocity changes of trunk rotation, upper arm flexion/adduction, forearm external rotation, and wrist flexion/extension with the forehand stroke, and the kinematics of the racket al.so has an effect on the racket velocity5,6. The kinematics of the racket have a certain effect on the racket velocity. Some authors have suggested that trunk rotation is a key factor in the development of racket speed, regardless of skill leve7–9. It has been demonstrated that shoulder forward speed differentiates between elite (3.0 ± 0.4 m s-1) and high-performance players (2.5 ± 0.4 m s-1) forehands, and that trunk rotation also helps to pre-stretch the shoulder muscles of the internally rotating upper arm, thus indirectly increasing racket Speed. However, Fujisawa et al. found that the force generated by trunk axial rotation did not appear to be directly related to the ability of forehand to generate ball speed through isokinetic testing7. Long-axis rotation of the upper arm reaches peak velocity in the final milliseconds of the swing stroke and contributes approximately 35% of racket velocity in forehand strokes10. Horizontal flexion of the shoulder usually contributes approximately 25% of racket velocity. However, recent research evaluating players generating three velocities, but with the same placement, of returns through forehand strokes has shown that the degree of the shoulder internal rotation does not differenc8, and similarly, Landlinger et al. showed that the maximum angular velocity of the shoulder internal rotation was more consistent between elite and high-performance players when completing cross-court (~ 803–825°/s) and straight-line (~ 762–780°/s) strokes7. The slight contribution of shoulder internal rotation to racket speed may be due to the interaction of proximal trunk and shoulder torque11. These studies have questioned whether the momentum generated by the shoulder and trunk is a critical factor affecting ball speed. Rogowski et al. found that the wrist radial deviation increased the vertical speed of the racket more than shoulder abduction12,13. The wrist joint, as the most terminal transfer of momentum may be a key factor influencing racket speed and ball speed. In addition, racket motion is also a factor in the return quality. Elliott et al. suggest that elite players reduce the dimensions of their swing in response to a fast-incoming ball or a straight shot, which may have an effect on the swing speed14. Intermediate players (20°) will use a flatter swing trajectory than higher level players (30°) when stroke the ball to reduce the error magnitude in the vertical plane15. Elbow extension and internal rotation play a role in generating impact racquet speed14. These studies reflect that there are differing perceptions of the key factors that influence ball speed.

Forehand strokes are usually described as three phases or four moments16,17. Concluding that different stroke conditions (ball speed, phase, return direction, etc.) can have an effect on the activity of the player limb and racket7,8,14,17,18. Previous studies have compared the joint movement characteristics of players under different stroke directions (straight and diagonal), stroke styles (flat and rotational), and stance positions (open and closed) conditions19,20. Receiving is the beginning of the game, different serve speeds produce different impacts, how players should adjust the server-receiving technique is the key to improve their server-receiving quality, there is no study on the joints of forehand serve-receiving technique and the characteristics of racket kinematics changes under different serve speeds. The kinematic analysis can compare the differences in forehand serve-receiving techniques of the different level of the tennis player at different serve speeds, and can determine the key links that affect the racket speed.

Currently, more and more sports are beginning to try new methods to analyze the techniques and tactics in the game, and the characteristics of the movements under different conditions in the game, so that they can better select talents, develop training plans, and effectively improve the performance of the sport21–25. The fact that coaches tend to use subjective reports to evaluate forehand technique suggests that the objective assessment tool for evaluating the forehand serve-receive technique in tennis is a lack26. This cannot understand the kinematic differences between the joints and the racket of the player at different serve speeds27. Therefore, this study compares the kinematic differences in trunk, upper limb, and racket kinematics of forehand serve-receive technique at lower and higher serve speeds in the different levels of male tennis players to provide a method for players or coaches to improve the forehand serve-receive technique quality. We hypothesized that the AG would have a higher forehand serve-receive quality (ball speed and placement) than the IG, and hypothesized that there would be a difference in trunk rotation, the upper arm outward and forward movement, and the wrist palm flexion between the AG and IG in each the movement phase. At the same time, shortening the duration of each the movement phase is an effective strategy to at faster serve speeds.

Methods

Participants

We used Gpower to determine the 24 participants in the experiment28. Fifteen advanced group (AG: Chinese national level 1) and Fifteen intermediate group (IG: Chinese national level 2) male tennis players were selected to participate in the study. Height, weight, age, and training experience were compared between the two groups of players. There were no differences in all conditions except for training experience (p > 0.05, Table 1). All participants were right-handed and had no musculoskeletal injuries in the last six months. Before the start of the study, participants were informed about the design of the study, with a special emphasis on the possible risks and benefits, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to the enrolment. The study followed the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Shanghai Sport University (No.102772024RT008).

Procedure

Participants avoided any strenuous exercise for 24 h prior to the study. Players performed a 10-minute slow running session on the field wearing athletic clothing and tennis shoes. Jogging was followed by approximately 5 min of static stretching activities and 10 min of forehand stroke practice.

In this study, the speed and placement of the serve were restricted. An elite tennis player serves the ball at two speeds. Based on the statistics of first and second serve speeds of elite athletes, this study set the low-speed balls are kept between 130 and 140 km/h and high-speed balls are kept between 160 and 170 km/h. A study was conducted on the statistics of serve placement of high-level tennis players who issued ACEs in matches. It was found that the serve placement of high-level players was highly concentrated in T-type (near the centerline of the serve) or wide-type (near the singles sideline of the serve area), and the players were more likely to use forehand techniques to receive wide-type serves. Therefore, a wide-type placement field of 1 m² in the right outer corner of the right serve was selected as the test area for this study (Fig. 1). A 1 m bright-colored marking line is set parallel to the direction of motion of the serve, and a high-speed camera is placed perpendicular to the 1 m marking line to record the serve’s motion at 1000 Hz. In order to record the speed of the serve, determine whether the speed and placement of the serve (Fig. 1). Participants use a customary grip and a forehand serve-receive technique that requires participants to stroke the ball in a straight line to the same side of the court with maximum effort. A 1 m brightly colored marker line was set up parallel to the direction of movement of the return and a high-speed camera was set up perpendicular to the 1 m marker line to record the return’s movement at 1000 Hz in order to capture the speed and placement of the returned ball (Fig. 1). Data were considered valid if both of the following conditions were met: 1) the serve ball speed was within the low/high speed range and the placement was in bounds,2) the return direction was straight and the landing point was in bounds. Participants were tested with a low-speed serve followed by a high-speed serve, with three valid data collected at each serve speed and a 30-second rest period for each test. Different stances had an effect on the limb movement characteristics of the players on the receiving side of the serve. By observing the test video, it was found that all participants used a closed stance for the serve-received.

During the test, participants all used the same racket (grip 41/4 inches and mass 300 g; Pure Drive; Babolat Play, France, 2022) and used reflective strips to set six marker points on the racket head, the top of the racket head, the upper left side, the upper right side, the lower left side, the lower right side, and at the connection between the racket handle and the racket head (Fig. 2). Fifty-three marker points (15.0 mm in diameter) were placed on the whole body of the participant, and the participant who placed the marker points assumed a “T” pose to build a whole-body model (Fig. 2). Kinematic data were collected using a 20-camera motion capture system (Qingtong, China) at an acquisition frequency of 120 Hz (Fig. 3).

Data reduction

The shoulder center was defined as the middle point of the greater and lesser tuberosities of the humerus. The elbow center was defined as the middle point of the medial and lateral epicondyles of the humerus. The wrist center was defined as the middle point of the medial and lateral radial styloid and ulnar styloid. The shoulder was defined by the left shoulder, the right shoulder, and the seventh cervical vertebra. Local segment coordinates were defined as x in the anterior-posterior direction, y in the medial-lateral direction, and z in the vertical direction when the participant was standing in the anatomical position. Shoulder flexion/extension angle was defined by the angle between the line connecting the center point of the shoulder joint and the center point of the elbow joint and the line connecting the right and left shoulders. Forearm pronation angle was defined by the angle between the line connecting the ulnar and radial points and the horizontal line of the center of the elbow joint. Wrist flexion/extension angle was defined as the angle between the tip point of the middle finger and the line connecting the middle point of the wrist to the middle point of the elbow. Shoulder adduction/abduction angle was defined as the angle between the line connecting the middle of the elbow, the middle of the shoulder and the middle of the hip. Elbow adduction/abduction angle was defined as the angle between the line connecting the center of the wrist joint, the middle of the elbow joint and the middle of the shoulder joint. Trunk right/left rotation angle was defined as the angle between the line connecting the right and left shoulders moving around the vertical axis of the body. Joint angles and angular velocities were exported using CMMotion Analyze. The racket was defined by markers placed on the top of its head, top left side, top right side, bottom left side, bottom right side, and where the handle meets the head, and the CMMotion Analyze exported the 6 degrees of freedom data of the racket. The racket angles were between the racket and the ground when touching the ball (Fig. 4) and joint angles were considered to be the main contributors to racket line speed, and head speed was represented by the peak racket speed during the forward swing. The wrist joint adduction/abduction angle reflects the position of the racket in relation to the human body, and the racket face tilt angle reflects the angle between the racket and the ground (the roll of the racket). The direction vector d of the racket in the world coordinate system is calculated based on the rotation data of the racket obtained from the motion capture system, assuming that the normal vector of the ground is [0,1,0] T. In this study, the forehand serve-receiving can be divided into three phases: T1: Backswing phase: the phase was defined from when a player assumed the preparatory posture to the end of the backswing; T2: Impacting phase: the process of was defined from the end of the backswing to ball impact; T3: Follow-through phase: the phase was defined from impact until completion of the swing16–18. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 24 software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY). Data normality was verified by using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Independent t-tests were used to compare the kinematic differences between AG and IG in the three movement phases at slow and fast serve speeds. The statistical significance level was set at 0.05 with p-values between 0.05 and 0.1 indicating a trend toward significance.

Results

In the low-speed serve and high-speed serve conditions (Table 2), significant differences were observed for the ball speed and placement of return between the two groups of participants. We found that when the serve speed changed from low to high speed, the AG returned the ball speed faster and placement closer to the baseline (higher placement scores). In comparison, the IG returned the ball speed slower and placement closer to the serving line (lower placement scores).

Backswing

During the backswing phase, there was no significant difference in the duration of the backswing at low-speed serve between the AG and IG. The AG generally demonstrated greater shoulder abduction angle, smaller elbow abduction angle (Fig. 6), faster trunk right rotation angular velocity, faster shoulder extension angular velocity. The AG and IG tended to demonstrate a shorter time of end of racket backswing and faster shoulder extension angular velocity in AG at high-speed serve. Meanwhile, the racket external rotation angles are smaller at low-speed serves and larger at high-speed serves in AG. However, the racket external rotation angles have decreased as the speed of the serve increases in IG (Table 3). We found that the correlation between elbow internal rotation angular velocity and ball speed was higher for AG and IG. In contrast, the correlation between elbow abduction angular velocity and ball speed was higher for AG (Table 4).

Impact

During the stroke phase, the AG demonstrated greater shoulder flexion and abduction angle, greater wrist adduction angle and racket external rotation angles (Fig. 6), faster wrist extension angular velocity, and faster racket vertical, horizontal, and combined velocities at both serve speeds, with more significant difference at low-speed serve. With faster serve speeds, the AG demonstrated faster shoulder flexion and abduction angular velocity, faster wrist abduction angular velocity, while lower trunk left rotation angular velocity, smaller racket external rotation angle and lower racket speed, whereas the opposite changes were observed in the joints in IG, with no change in racket external rotation angle (Table 5). We found that the correlation between the timing of peak racket resultant velocity and ball speed was higher, and the wrist extension angle and ball speed was higher for AG (Table 4).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to compare the differences in joint, racket, and ball kinematics between the different level of tennis players in forehand serve-receive strokes when returning low-speed serve and high-speed serves, and analyze the factors influencing ball speeds. The funding supports the hypothesis that compared to IG, the AG’s forehand serve-received ball speeds are faster and the placement is deeper in the advanced group. The observed trunk, upper limb, racket and ball velocities in forehand serve-receiving when both speed serve were difference. It was found that trunk rotation angle and angular velocity may not be the direct cause of higher ball speeds. The advanced group was more adept at influencing the racket velocities by changing the wrist flexion and extension angle to create faster ball speeds. This study found that changes in the wrist and elbow joint angle have a significant effect on the ball speed of stoke. At the same time, elitist players have more efficient kinetic chain at high-speed serves.

The tennis forehand serve-receive technique requires the upper arm to be abducted and extended, while the forearm is pronation and the wrist is slightly flexed at the backswing phase29. These movements can shorten the rotation diameter, reduce the resistance for the body to turn, and further complete the backswing movement. Consistently, both groups of players have shorter backswing times at faster serves. The AG demonstrated rapid trunk rotation and higher elbow height, resulting greater angle between the trunk and arm, and a smaller forearm and upper arm angle. The correlation between the joint changes and ball speed for the AG suggests that the backswing phase can be accomplished through faster elbow rotation rather than forearm abduction while maintaining a greater racket face angle at the end of the backswing. Athletes can accomplish rapid backswing by having the upper arm at a greater angle to the trunk, the forearm at a smaller angle to the upper arm, and the forearm closer to the body while doing rapid internal rotation.

During the stroke phase, the players’ forehand strokes of the ground balls are characterized by exhibiting rapid trunk internal rotation, which drives the right arm forward and outward, the forearm internal rotation and adduction while the wrist speed peaks29. This study found that, compared with IG, the faster angular velocities of shoulder flexion, shoulder abduction, wrist extension, and racket extension in AGs with faster return speeds. Recent studies have demonstrated no difference in the shoulder internal rotation angle when using a forehand stroke to produce three return speeds8. However, our results found that the angular velocity of shoulder joint forward flexion and the angle of abduction were significantly greater for AG forehand serve-receiving maneuvers at faster serve speeds. Some authors have suggested that trunk rotation is a key factor in increasing racket velocity7–9. According to the correlation results (Table 4), the forces generated by the trunk rotation seem to lack a direct relationship with the ball velocity of forehand strokes, And the length of the stroke and the speed of wrist flexion have a high impact on the ball velocity. This result is similar to previous studies where the wrist plays an important role in modifying the trajectory of the ball and the racket13,15. According to Tables 3 and 5, it can be seen that the AG with faster ball speed has longer stroke duration and faster wrist flexion and extension angular velocity. This is because the receiving action is different from the groundstroke action, the preparation time of the receiving action is shorter, and the longer the stroke time, the fuller the stroke action at the same ball speed, which is more conducive to the improvement of the ball speed. From this result view, it seems reasonable that the AG would have a greater wrist flexion angle and higher racket vertical speed at both serve speeds. Due to the faster speed of the incoming ball, athletes have less time to prepare for the reception of the ball. Adequate stroke process is the key to realizing high quality strokes, and the athletes’ quick backswing is the pre-condition for realizing the adequate stroke process.

The follow-through phase is a continuation of the action after the impact phase. The results of the study show that that the players showed the forearm internal rotation and internal adduction during the follow-through phase. This is consistent with the study of Sealy et al., they study showed that the elbow flexion angular velocity increased with increasing incoming ball speed, which is consistent with the present study. During the follow-through phase, the athlete’s joint movement also affects the speed and depth of the return. The follow-through motion is a “brush” action to the upper left after touching the ball, to make the fast-hit ball rotate, form an arc, and make the ball fly at a high speed while landing in the boundary. According to Table 4, the correlation between the AG elbow joint internal rotation angle and the return speed is higher for slower serve speeds, while the correlation between the AG shoulder joint angular velocity and the ball speed is higher for faster serve speeds. This may be because there is enough time to complete the elbow joint movement at slow serve speeds, whereas the player needs to use the shoulder joint, which is closer to the body, to complete the follow-through swing when the ball speed becomes faster. Compare the IG, the forearms of the AG showed significantly faster internal rotation and inward retraction in the follow-through phase of the swing at high-speed serves. Therefore, in the face of different speeds of incoming balls, players can change the movement of the elbow or shoulder joint to complete the “brush” action, to improve the depth of the return and the probability of landing in the boundary.

At the same time, the wrist joint faster palmar flexion, that may be achieved by wrapping the ball with the racket surface so that the ball drops faster and lands in bounds while being closer to the baseline29. Thus, when facing a faster serve, the forearm rapid inward and internal rotation with the follow-through movement benefits the ball landing point closer to the baseline position.

Implications

Full preparation before the stroke is a pre-condition to maintain the stability of the receiving and serving technique. The receiving side uses a shorter lead radius as much as possible and completes the backswing action in a short period of time. Athletes can improve their backswing speed through quick turn of the body and small-amplitude elbow bend backswing training;The wrist can increase the speed of the racket through a greater motion range. Therefore, the wrist palm flexion before stroke and back-extension action during the stroke can effectively increase the racquet speed at forward strokes, thus increasing the ball speed.The process of serve receive action is very fast, and a quick and complete follow-through swing action after touching the ball can effectively increase the probability of the ball being in bounds.

Limitations

Firstly, this study only defined two serve speeds, which can be selected to test the serve speeds of high-level athletes; secondly, the effect of ball rotation on stroke effect was not considered in this study. Thirdly, this study selected a single landing point for the serve, and a variety of landing points can be added in the subsequent study to study the movement characteristics of athletes facing different landing points; fourthly, due to the large difference between male and female athletes of the same level of serve receiving technology, only male athletes were selected for the test of serve receiving technology in this study, and in the future, the characteristics of the female athletes’ serve receiving technology can be investigated to enrich the research on the serve receiving technology.
