Bundling of unhealthy food products in Johannesburg, South Africa: an exploratory study
Authors: Renay Weiner, Darshen Naidoo, Sameera Mahomedy, Susan Goldstein
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSouth Africa has high levels of obesity and overweight, which contribute to non-communicable diseases and are associated with poor diets high in ultra-processed foods. Bundling occurs when two or more products are packaged and sold together, often at a discount and is a marketing strategy for unhealthy foods. Given the paucity of data on bundling of unhealthy foods, this exploratory study sought to document how unhealthy foods are bundled together to be more attractive to consumers in Johannesburg, South Africa. Fifty food outlets were included and compared across two regions. In-store photos were taken of bundles and individual items, and data were captured on the costs of the bundle and of individual components. Descriptive analysis and the contents of the bundles based on the photographs and promotional material were conducted. Almost half of all outlets had more than five bundles and nearly a quarter had bundles targeting children. Most children’s meals were burgers, and all had fries as part of the bundle. The average savings per bundle when compared to the combined cost of individual items was R21,39 overall, or 18%. The study demonstrated that unhealthy food bundling is a common practice in food outlets in Johannesburg, often with cost advantages and promotional appeal. Policy options for promoting a favourable food environment include regulating portion sizes of bundles and offering healthy options as part of a bundle. Marketing food to children by bundling unhealthy food with toys is of particular concern and is prohibited in the recently gazetted Regulations Relating to the Labelling and Advertising of Foodstuffs.Keywords: bundling, non communicable diseases, South Africa, ultra processed foods, marketing to children

BACKGROUND

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are growing globally with more than 15 million people aged 30–69 years dying from NCDs each year and around 85% of these ‘premature’ deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries (WHO, 2021). In South Africa, deaths due to NCDs increased by 58.7% over a 20-year period between 1997 and 2018, and cardiovascular diseases accounted for half of all these deaths (Stats SA, 2023). Diet is a critical risk factor for NCDs among both men and women, leading to a global focus on diet-related NCDs (DR-NCDs) (Vos et al., 2020). Moreover, South Africa has one of the highest levels of obesity and overweight in the world, which is contributing to the high levels of NCDs, in particular cardiovascular diseases. Some 68% of women and 31% of men are either overweight or obese as indicated in the most recently published Demographic and Health Survey (Stats SA, 2016). Moreover, obesity rates have increased over two decades (1998–2017) from 24.7% to 35.2% in women of child-bearing age (Nglazi and Ataguba, 2022). With the current growth trends, South Africa is anticipated to have the tenth-highest level of childhood obesity in the world by 2030 (Lobstein and Brinsden, 2019; Frank et al., 2021).

NCDs and obesity are associated with poor diets that are high in unhealthy foods/beverages and poor food environments. Studies have shown that there is a correlation between high rates of fast-food outlets in poor neighbourhoods and the increasing rates of obesity (Block et al., 2004). The food environment in the Gauteng area of South Africa shows a low concentration of healthy food outlets in poorer areas compared to wealthier areas (Ndlovu et al., 2018). Unhealthy foods/beverages, which have been defined by the World Health Organisation (WHO) as products high in energy, added fat, added sugar or sodium (Sadeghirad et al., 2016), often include ultra-processed foods (UPFs). UPFs are defined as ‘formulations of ingredients, that result from a series of industrial processes many requiring sophisticated equipment and technology’ and are typically convenient, hyper-palatable and affordable (Monteiro et al., 2019; Adams et al., 2020). UPFs are typically energy-dense products, which are high in sugar, unhealthy fats and salt, such as carbonated drinks, confectionery, mass-produced packaged bread, biscuits, pastries, pizza, sausages, burgers and hot dogs (Adams et al., 2020). Amongst low-income consumers in South Africa, high levels of UPF intake combined with insufficient intake of protective dietary foods, namely fibre, legumes, fruit and vegetables, has been documented (Frank et al., 2021). This creates a heightened vulnerability to obesity and NCDs. There is substantial evidence emerging from prospective studies that higher UPF intake contributes to increases in obesity as well as negative health outcomes, such as cardio-metabolic morbidity and mortality in children, adolescents and adults (Adams et al., 2020; Pagliai et al., 2021; Delpino et al., 2022). This paper will refer to unhealthy foods as the broader umbrella term, which includes sugary beverages and UPFs.

Unhealthy foods are typically produced by transnational companies and are aggressively marketed using a combination of attractive product packaging, effective pricing, accessible placement and sophisticated promotion. In turn, food marketing has a strong influence on food preferences, purchasing and consumption, particularly in children and young people (Moodley et al., 2015; Boyland et al., 2016). In the WHO Guideline pertaining to policies to protect children from the harmful impact of food marketing, it was noted that research has pinpointed various tactics used in food marketing that are likely to resonate with children, including the use of promotional characters, toys, playful visuals, colourful packaging, brand imagery and fun themes (World Health Organization, 2023a,b).

One strategy for marketing unhealthy foods is ‘bundling’. Bundling has been defined and understood mainly in the context of competition law and occurs when two or more products are packaged and sold together, often at a discount (Disruptive Competition Project; Lane, 2019; Competition Commission Hong Kong, n.d.). (Bundling should be distinguished from ‘tying’ which occurs when the sale of one product (the tying product) is dependent (or tied) to the purchase of another (often unrelated) product (the tied product). In these cases, the tied product is not available for sale separately whereas with bundling, there are instances where the products can be sold separately.) In South Africa, while there is no legal definition of bundling, it has generally been described as selling products as a pair or making products available only as a bundle (Masuku, 2010). The South African Competition Tribunal has elaborated on the distinction between bundling and tying and has expanded on different types of bundling (Mobile Telephone Networks Holdings (Pty) Ltd v Verizon South Africa (Pty) Ltd).

The Competition Act 89 of 1998 (Competition Act) regulates instances of bundling and tying in Section 8. It prohibits a dominant firm from ‘selling goods or services on condition that the buyer purchases separate goods or services unrelated to the object of a contract’. This regulation relates more to anti-competitive behaviour than to public health (Republic of South Africa and Competition Act, 1998). The Consumer Protection Act 68 of 2002 (CPA) also regulates bundling in Section 13, which relates to consumers’ right to select suppliers (South African Government., 2008). Accordingly, bundling is only allowed if the supplier can demonstrate ‘that the convenience to the consumer in having the goods or services bundled outweighs the limitation of the consumer’s right to choice’ or if the supplier can demonstrate ‘that the bundling of those goods or services results in economic benefit for consumers’ (Republic of South Africa, Competition Act 1998).

Bundling can promote the purchase of products, including the consumption of unhealthy foods by offering lower prices for a bundle than for each individual item. It is generally recognized that lower prices increase sales, particularly amongst low-income consumers (Vignola et al., 2021). In the context of food insecurity in South Africa, from 2020 to 2021, an estimated 35% of households ran out of money for food (van der Berg et al., 2022). Therefore, cheaper unhealthy foods through bundling could increase their appeal to consumers from lower socio-economic areas, with increased consumption driving their risk of developing NCDs. Hence, as South Africa is an upper-middle income country with an increasing obesity and NCD burden of illness, its policies need to be innovative in creating a healthy food environment that contributes to the reduction of unhealthy foods and specifically UPF consumption.

While bundling of unhealthy foods may lead to an increase in their consumption, there is a paucity of data on the bundling phenomenon in the food industry. In the UK, an econometric study noted that in British supermarkets, sugar-sweetened beverages, confectionary and children’s cereal are promoted more often than fruit and vegetables. They concluded that multi-buy promotions encourage additional and greater volume purchases and that restricting price promotions could be effective in decreasing the consumption of food and drink, including for `multi-buys’ (Watt et al., 2023). In the USA, it was found that food bundles in grocery stores are preferred because they require less cognitive load to select, and in another study, consumers perceived increased value to bundled/combo meals not only due to the economic advantage, but also due to decreased ordering costs and promotional effects (Sharpe and Staelin, 2010; Caroll et al., 2022).

In South Africa, a recent study of both healthy and unhealthy bundles called ‘hampers’ or ‘combo deals’ in grocery stores across low-income areas in Cape Town found that hampers from formal stores typically consisted of five staple foods sold at discounted prices in bulk. The majority of these foods were processed foods, namely cake wheat flour, white sugar and par-boiled rice, while greater diversity was offered at spaza shops (local informal shops), including canned beans and pilchards. Most hampers were cheaper than the individual items, though this was not always the case (Coste et al., 2023). Despite the growing concern of obesogenic environments being a driver of NCDs, we could not find any previous study that investigates unhealthy or UPF bundles in South Africa across outlet types including fast-food outlets.

This exploratory study sought to address this gap by producing a snapshot of how unhealthy foods are bundled together to be more attractive to consumers in diverse parts of Johannesburg, South Africa. Specific objectives were (i) to document and compare the frequency of bundling of unhealthy foods in diverse outlets in two regions within Johannesburg, Gauteng province, (ii) to understand the relative appeal to consumers by looking at bundling as a form of promotion and (iii) to measure the price advantage in the purchase of unhealthy bundled goods in the study area.

METHODS

The study was conducted in Johannesburg, the most densely populated city in South Africa. Two regions were selected within the City of Johannesburg boundaries—Region B, which is a middle-class area in central Johannesburg and Region D, which consists of Soweto, a mostly lower income area with the highest unemployment level in the city (Republic of South Africa, Profile of Johannesburg, 2020). An exploratory convenience sample was used to select diverse food outlets into the study to ascertain if bundling was present in different contexts. Twenty-five outlets were selected from each region intending to document a range of five different outlet types based on settings where urban dwellers frequently obtain their food. Numbers selected varied across the outlet types based on their frequency and ease of access by the field team (Table 1).

Data were collected during December 2023 and were collected on-site by trained data collectors for all outlets except restaurants using e-hailing services where data were collected online. A standardized data capture sheet was used to collect information about the outlet and bundle. The focus of the data collection was on bundles of unhealthy foods, though bundles with a mix of unhealthy and healthy items were included. Bundles of only healthy items were excluded from the data collection. In-store photos were taken of bundling offers as well as photos of individual items where available. Leaflets of menus or special bundling offers were collected. For the online data collection, screenshots of menus were captured. In addition to the photographs, the following data were collected using a checklist: the number of bundles per outlet categorized as: few (1), moderate (2–5) and high (6+), the products included in the bundles, and whether the outlet offered bundles targeting children. In addition, data on the cost of the bundle and the cost of individual components of the bundle were captured.

A descriptive analysis was conducted to ascertain bundle distribution at outlets and proportions by area (region B or D) and outlet type, percentage of outlets that had bundles that targeted children, contents of bundles, cost ranges and cost averages of bundles as well as actual and percentage savings when bundles rather than individual items were purchased. Savings from bundles were compared between regions and by outlet type using ranges and means. The contents of the bundles were analysed based on photographs and promotional material. After reviewing the images, codes were created for the following domains: target audience, presence of a name/slogan of bundle, the bundle name and any specific connotation or association of the name, the presence of visual content as well as if the visual content was of the bundle and/or had a message that linked the bundle to an unrelated dimension such as fun, power and holidays.

Frequency of bundling in the two regions of Johannesburg

Almost half (48%) of all outlets had more than five bundles. Bundling occurred more frequently (6+ per outlet) in Region D compared to Region B—56% versus 40%. Fast-food outlets and e-hailing restaurants had more (6+) unhealthy bundles per outlet compared to the remaining outlet types. In contrast, the majority (77%) of convenience stores and 40% of grocery/general dealers had only one bundle (Table 3).

Contents of bundles

Data on 88 bundles across the 50 outlets indicated that the most common bundles were a main meal plus fries or a drink (43%) and the second most common bundle was a main meal plus fries and a drink (30%). Most main meals were hamburgers-beef or chicken (n = 24), chicken (n = 23) followed by pies (n = 14), with the remainder being a combination of pizzas, hot dogs, wraps/pita and fish. Chicken included those from groceries that may have been roasted rather than fried. Main meals that were potentially healthier were noted at three outlets and included wraps, pita and/or burritos. Some of the 13 bundles consisted of confectionary, sugary drinks and popcorn. Fifty nine per cent of bundles included sugary beverages (n = 52). The remaining drinks in bundles were sugar-free beverages (n = 8), coffee or water (n = 3).

Bundling targeting children

Nearly one-quarter, 24% (12/50), of the outlets had bundles targeting children. Of these, 36% were (9/25) in Region B, while fewer—12% (3/25) were in Region D. In Region B, outlets with bundles targeting children were fast-food outlets, cinemas and/or online e-hailing restaurants, whereas in region D e-hailing restaurants and cinemas had bundles that targeted children, but not fast-food outlets. Children’s meals from fast-food outlets or e-hailing restaurants consisted mostly of burgers (beef or chicken) and all had fries as part of the bundle. Bundles targeting children at cinemas, convenience stores and grocery stores consisted mostly of confectionary often with a sugary drink. Visual promotions with images of movie characters were common where bundles were linked to cinema tickets, for example ‘The Trolls Treat Combo’. Although the e-hailing restaurants did not have pictures to attract children, the names of the bundles indicated that children were targeted, e.g. ‘bambino meal’ or ‘junior chicken burger’, presumably because adults would mostly likely place the order. Five bundles included toys, and these were from a mix of outlet types—fast-food outlets, including e-hailing services and cinemas.

Bundling as a form of promotion

Nearly two-thirds (65%, n = 58) of bundles had a name or a slogan. Of these, 26% were related to savings, for example: ‘Daily lunchtime deli deals’ and ‘Pump up your savings’. Others denoted fun/happiness, ‘Make Happy Happen’ and ‘Soul Mates Classic Party’ and power/authority—‘Big boss burger meal’. Terms of affection such as ‘Chicky, Licky Munch’ or ‘Brekkie Crunch Box’ were also used in bundle names, while ‘Pizza Feast Faves’ and ‘Cram decker full house feast’ created a sense of abundance. Bundle names occurred in both regions.

Pricing was strongly reflected in the content of the bundle promotional material with some 58% containing both a picture of the bundle and the price, while a further 15% promoted the price only. Twenty-one per cent contained an image unrelated to the bundle’s contents, mostly conveying a message of fun and/or summer holidays.

Price advantage in the purchase of bundled goods

The bundle costs and savings per outlet type are presented in Table 4. Bundles sold through restaurants using e-hailing were the costliest, while those at grocery stores were the least costly. The average cost of bundles was higher in Region B compared to Region D (R110,46 vs R92,04, respectively); however, in Region B there was a much wider cost range (R25–R469.90) compared to Region D (R5.00–R209.90). The average savings per bundle (when compared to the combined cost of individual items) was R21,39 overall, representing a saving of 18%. Savings ranged from R0,00 to R115,00. The greatest savings were gained from grocery store bundles (24%) followed by restaurants using e-hailing services (20%).

DISCUSSION

This exploratory study is, to our knowledge, the first in South Africa to document bundling of foods across a range of food outlets. The study has demonstrated that bundling of unhealthy foods is prevalent across socio-economic areas and outlet types. A large number of bundles were documented in outlets situated in Region D (the poorer region), e-hailing restaurants and fast-food outlets. Almost one-quarter of the outlets had bundles that targeted children, and these were noted to be more frequent in Region B (the wealthier region). Moreover, the findings indicate that bundles are actively promoted through a combination of catchy names/slogans, attractive prices and images, while some 25% had an image unrelated to the bundle, usually denoting fun and/or summer holidays. Savings from purchasing a bundle did not vary according to socio-economic area.

A few studies from the USA and Europe have explored unhealthy food bundling and its impact on consumer choices. In the USA, Harris et al. found that promotions of bundling led to an increased intention to purchase bundles, particularly for those with low knowledge about calories (Harris and Thomas, 2017). A study that sought to better understand the purchasing behaviour of consumers who buy meals at fast-food outlets in order to inform policies aimed at curbing the intake of unhealthy foods found that there was an average increase of 110–130 calories per meal when bundles were purchased (Sharpe and Staelin, 2010). Additional factors, beyond pricing and promotions, that add to the appeal of bundling and greater consumption of unhealthy foods have been identified. For example, convenience to the consumer through ease of purchase and decreased search effort by limiting decision complexity are added advantages (Harris and Blair, 2006; Sharpe and Staelin, 2010). A more recent study of consumer preferences for food bundles similarly found that bundles were chosen since it required less information processing, even if there was no price saving (Carroll et al., 2022).

The finding that nearly 25% of all outlets had bundles targeting children is a concern given the emerging obesity epidemic in young South Africans and the impact of food marketing on children’s food preferences. There is strong evidence that unhealthy food advertising increases food intake in children. A systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that there was a significant association between unhealthy food advertising and food consumption for children, particularly young children, who are considered particularly vulnerable to marketing since they are unable to understand the advertising intent (Boyland et al., 2016). One aspect of the recent South African Draft Regulations Relating to the Labelling and Advertising of Foodstuffs (R.3337) pertains to the restriction of marketing of foods to children and specifically bans the use of items that may appeal to children when advertising such as cartoon characters, gifts and collectable items (Republic of South Africa., 2023).This restriction should be extended to the marketing of bundles to children which typically include cartoon characters and/or toys. Without these items, the appeal of the bundles will likely be diminished.

A key concern is that bundling influences consumption norms. Consumption norms help individuals determine how much they should consume, and they suggest a quantity of food that is acceptable (Wansink, 2004). The influence of unhealthy food bundling on consumption norms was observed in the current study. The presence of nearly 60% of sugary beverages in the bundles suggests that consuming a sugary drink with a meal is appropriate. This is supported by the study by Harris and Thomas (Harris and Thomas, 2017) who measured the role bundling plays on consumption norms and found that the message conveyed by bundle promotions is that all items should be purchased together and hence buying a bundled meal was perceived as an appropriate choice. They further showed that while consumption norms mediated the relationship between bundling and calories ordered, the effect was decreased where there was high caloric knowledge. Although they did not look at the impact of bundling on portion sizes, it is plausible that widespread bundling implies a norm about acceptable portion sizes. The size of packages and portion sizes influence consumption, i.e. what is an appropriate amount to eat. Bundling, which has set portion sizes for each item, suggests to consumers that these are appropriate and acceptable portion sizes (Wansink, 2004).

The provisions in the Competition Act and the CPA currently only regulate bundling and tying from a competition and consumer protection angle and not from a public health angle. From our study, most outlets offered bundles with individual items being available as well, although in some cases the size and/or volumes differed, resulting in mixed bundling (which is allowed under the CPA). It is only if there isn’t a minimal or no price benefit that bundling would not comply with the CPA. Although regulation of bundling of unhealthy foods and beverages does not exist, there is a need to investigate this, given the findings of this study. Room exists for such regulation to protect the interests of consumers and ‘ensure accessible, transparent and efficient redress for consumers who are subjected to abuse or exploitation in the marketplace’. The CPA also aims to improve access to quality information that will assist consumers in making informed decisions and protect consumers from hazards to their well-being and safety (in this case health).

Another avenue that points to the need for this type of regulation can be found in a new set of regulations related to the labelling and advertising of food, gazetted in 2023, which aims to enable consumers to make healthier food purchasing choices through large clear warning labels on the front of packaged foods that are high in added sugar, or fat or salt or a combination of the three (Regulations Relating to the Labelling and Advertising of Foodstuffs, R3337). We recommend that items with these warnings as a front-of-pack label should be excluded from bundles since these are confirmed to be unhealthy, and when packaged as part of a bundle, consumer choice may be overridden by the price advantage and powerful marketing.

Policy proposals from the aforementioned US studies may also have relevance to the South African context. For example, decreasing the portion size of the bundled items—particularly drinks and fries which would reduce overall calorie intake, is a plausible intervention. Making healthier bundles a more regular and routine option has also been recommended and could help shift consumer norms (Sharpe and Staelin, 2010; Harris and Thomas, 2017). Healthy food bundles, particularly fruits and vegetables, are already available in some supermarkets in South Africa and this could be expanded to include other unprocessed products, as well as offerings at other food outlets. By providing healthy bundling options that offer good price deals, promotions can steer consumers to buying healthy foods such as fruit, vegetables, pulses and nuts (EPHA 2023). Fast-food outlets, selling unhealthy foods often in large portions, need to routinely have a healthy food option that is affordable, palatable and promoted as an appropriate choice. Mandatory calorie labelling of bundles and increasing public caloric knowledge need to be explored further as potential policy options in South Africa. Importantly, as pointed out by Popkin et al. (Popkin et al., 2021), creating linked policies that are mutually reinforcing is likely to be more impactful than implementing policies in isolation.

The study had several limitations. Since a convenience sample was used, the findings may not be representative of all food outlets in the selected areas. There were only a few outlets that tied food to other services such as car washing and hence this type of bundling was not fully explored. While informal outlets were sought, none were identified in Region B, and in Region D, owners were suspicious of photos being taken of their prices and consequently informal outlets were excluded. At some outlets, individual items were either not available or there was a size difference between the bundled and individual items (e.g. 300 ml in bundle, 340 ml individual), making it difficult to compare the costs of the bundle versus that of the individual items combined. At some outlets, individual items from a bundle did not have a price until purchased since they had to be weighed, e.g. pies and full chickens. Finally, the study did not explore healthy bundles unrelated to unhealthy foods, an important area for future research.

CONCLUSION

This study has shown that bundling of unhealthy food is common practice in food outlets in Johannesburg, South Africa, and that this practice is likely to increase the availability, appeal and consumption of unhealthy foods, facilitated by strong marketing, cost savings and the promotion of consumer norms that support large portion sizes and consuming certain foods together. Given that bundling is now used by many companies to promote and encourage the consumption of unhealthy foods and beverages, and not just to gain a competitive advantage, the regulation thereof needs to be expanded to include public health regulation. Policy options for limiting unhealthy bundles and promoting a favourable food environment require urgent consideration to decrease the consumption of unhealthy foods and decrease the risk of obesity and NCDs. This could include regulating portion sizes for fries and drinks in bundles, offering healthy options as part of a bundle and/or promoting healthy food bundles at various outlets. Marketing food to children by bundling unhealthy food with toys, is of particular concern and this is prohibited in the recently gazetted Regulations Relating to the Labelling and Advertising of Foodstuffs. There is an urgent need to enact these regulations to protect children from unhealthy food intake. The regulations could be expanded to state that any products carrying a warning label may not be bundled. Future research focusing on consumer knowledge and attitudes toward both healthy and unhealthy food bundles in influencing food choices and consumption could contribute valuable information to shape relevant policies.
