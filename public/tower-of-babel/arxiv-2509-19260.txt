Reconstruction of a potential parameter in subdiffusion via a Kohn–Vogelius type functional: Theory and computation
Hamza Kahlaoui, Mourad Hrizi, Abdessamad Oulmelk, Xiangcheng Zheng, Mahmoud A. Zaky, Ahmed Hendy

Abstract
. This work considers the identification of spatial-dependent potential from boundary observations in subdiffusion using a stable and robust recovery method. Specifically, we develop an algorithm to minimize the Kohn-Vogelius cost function, which measures the difference between the solutions of two excitations. The inverse potential problem is recast into an optimization problem, where the objective is to minimize a Kohn-Vogelius-type functional within a set of admissible potentials. We establish the well-posedness of this optimization problem by proving the existence and uniqueness of a minimizer and demonstrating its stability under perturbations in the boundary data. Furthermore, we analyze the Fréchet differentiability of the Kohn-Vogelius functional and prove the Lipschitz continuity of its gradient. These theoretical results enable the development of a convergent conjugate gradient algorithm for numerical reconstruction. Numerical examples in one and two dimensions, including scenarios with noisy data, validate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed method.



1.
Introduction

The reconstruction of coefficients in normal or anomalous diffusion processes has a wide history in the literature of inverse problems from theoretical and numerical perspectives. The reconstruction of time dependent parameters in subdiffusion problems has been extensively investigated in literature
[
26
,
27
,
36
]
.
In particular, some studies have investigated the reconstruction of space-dependent potentials in subdiffusion inferred from lateral Cauchy data
[
34
,
37
,
45
,
69
]
. In this paper, we aim to address the inverse problem of reconstructing a space-dependent potential
q^{*}
that arises in subdiffusion, utilizing lateral Cauchy data. Our primary objective is to successfully reconstruct
q^{*}
using a stable numerical method. In most of recent studies, the numerical algorithms developed for identifying the potential
q^{*}
rely on a least-squares approach. However, this paper focuses on the mathematical analysis of an algorithm aimed at minimizing the so-called Kohn-Vogelius cost function with a Tikhonov regularization term that penalizes the potential to be reconstructed.

The Kohn–Vogelius cost functional quantifies the discrepancy between two auxiliary boundary value problems: one driven by boundary measurements and the other by boundary excitations. In the noise-free setting, this functional attains its minimum value of zero, and the corresponding minimizer coincides with the exact solution of the inverse problem. When the data are noisy or incompatible, the inverse problem itself may not admit a solution, yet the minimization problem remains well-posed and solvable. A key advantage of Kohn–Vogelius functionals lies in their robustness: they yield stable reconstructions even for ill-posed problems, owing to their definition over the entire domain, in contrast to classical least-squares functionals that are restricted to the boundary. This robustness has been confirmed in several studies, including those by Afraites et al.
[
4
,
5
]
and Chaabane et al.
[
17
]
. For completeness, we briefly recall the historical development of Kohn–Vogelius type functionals. The foundational idea dates back to Wexler, Fry, and Neuman
[
72
]
, who in 1985 proposed a method for detecting unknown impedance from boundary measurements. In 1987, Kohn and Vogelius
[
50
]
refined this approach by introducing a novel misfit gap-cost functional in an alternating-choice framework. Later, in 1990, Kohn and McKenney
[
49
]
implemented this formulation numerically for inverse conductivity problems. Since then, the method has been extended to a wide range of inverse problems
[
1
,

,
3
,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

, further establishing its versatility and effectiveness.

Recovering a potential in subdiffusion through the inverse problem has been extensively studied. In the one-dimensional setting, Rundell and Yamamoto
[
68
]
established the uniqueness of a non-negative potential
q^{*}\in C[0,1]
using Gelfand-Levitan theory. They further proposed a Newton-based iterative reconstruction procedure and provided an empirical analysis of the singular value spectrum of the linearized forward map, underscoring the problem’s severely ill-posed nature. This analysis was later extended to a broader class of solutions within an appropriate Sobolev space in time
[

. Beyond recovering a single parameter, Jing and Yamamoto
[

demonstrated the simultaneous identifiability of multiple parameters—including the fractional order, initial value, spatial potential, and Robin coefficients—from lateral Cauchy data at both endpoints in a one-dimensional subdiffusion model with homogeneous boundary conditions and no source term. A significant advancement was made by Jin and Zhou
[
34

, who achieved the simultaneous reconstruction of both the fractional order and the space-dependent potential
q^{*}\in\mathcal{Q}
using only endpoint data. Remarkably, their results hold even with only partial knowledge of the initial data or source, requiring only a mild constraint on the Neumann boundary condition for uniqueness. More recently, the focus has shifted to geometric inverse problems. In
[

, the authors developed a one-shot algorithm based on topological derivatives to identify the location and shape of a potential
q^{*}=\chi_{\omega^{*}}
supported on an unknown domain
\omega^{*}\subset\Omega
from partial boundary measurements. For a comprehensive overview of related work, including the recovery of time- or space-dependent potentials from other data types such as terminal observations, over-posed final time data, or average flux measurements, we refer the reader to
[

[

]
investigated the use of Artificial Neural Networks to solve the inverse potential problem for the standard diffusion equation, offering insights applicable to the fractional case. Finally, it is worth noting that for the classical parabolic case (
\alpha=1
), this inverse problem has been thoroughly studied by numerous authors employing a wide array of methods

In the present work, building on the Kohn–Vogelius formulation, we recast the inverse reconstruction problem as a regularized optimization problem. Specifically, the unknown potential
q^{*}
is identified as the minimizer of a Kohn–Vogelius type cost function augmented with a Tikhonov regularization term. Our main contribution is to establish existence and uniqueness results for the Kohn–Vogelius formulation with Tikhonov regularization in the subdiffusion setting, and to propose a convergent conjugate gradient method (CGM) specifically adapted to this structure. We establish theoretical results showing that this regularized minimization problem admits a unique solution. Moreover, we prove that if a sequence of minimizers corresponds to perturbed observation data converging to the exact (noise-free) data, then the associated sequence of potentials converges to the solution of the considered inverse problem. To compute the minimizer, we derive the Fréchet gradient of the Kohn–Vogelius functional explicitly and employ an iterative CGM. We rigorously prove the Lipschitz continuity of the Fréchet gradient, which underpins the convergence analysis of the CGM. A distinctive and challenging numerical aspect of our method is the selection of the optimal step size within the CG iteration. Unlike standard implementations that solve a linear equation, our formulation necessitates solving a non-trivial quadratic equation, a complication we address with a carefully designed and efficient numerical procedure.

For completeness, it is worth noting that in the context of electrical impedance tomography,
Kohn and McKenney
[
49
]
were the first to investigate numerically the performance of
the classical Kohn–Vogelius formulation (i.e., without a Tikhonov regularization term)
using a Newton-type algorithm. In its classical form, Newton’s method minimizes
a functional by iteratively approximating it with a quadratic, minimizing that quadratic,
and updating the approximation. This procedure is efficient as long as the Hessian of the
objective functional is positive definite. However, the Hessian of the Kohn–Vogelius functional
in their work is not always positive definite, which can lead to numerical instabilities
and unreliable descent directions. To address this, Kohn and McKenney introduced a

regularized Hessian
, obtained by modifying unstable terms and adding a corrective
factor of the form
(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}
(where
\varepsilon>0
), analogous to the Levenberg–Marquardt regularization.
This adjustment shifts the Hessian’s eigenvalues to the positive side, ensuring a well-conditioned
system and stable minimization. In contrast to the regularized Hessian approach of
[
49
]
, the present paper proves,
under a conditional assumption on the regularization parameter (see Theorem
18
and Remark
19
),
the uniqueness of the optimal solution for the considered Kohn–Vogelius-type cost functional. Importantly, this uniqueness and the resulting stability of the optimization problem critically rely on the presence of the Tikhonov regularization term; without it, these foundational properties cannot be guaranteed.

2.
Problem setting

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with boundary
\partial\Omega
of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, where
d\leq 3
denotes the spatial dimension. Let
T>0
and
\nu=(\nu_{1},\cdots,\nu_{d})
be the unit outward normal vector assigned to the boundary
\partial\Omega
. Moreover, we define
Q_{T}
as the space-time domain
\Omega\times(0,T)
and
\Sigma_{T}
as its lateral boundary
\partial\Omega\times(0,T)
. Consider the following initial boundary value problem of subdiffusion
[
32
,
42
,
52
,
53
]

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u-\Delta u+q^{*}\,u&=&0&\hbox{in}\ Q_{T},\\
\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}u&=&\varphi&\hbox{on}\ \Sigma_{T},\\
u(\cdot,0)&=&0&\hbox{in}\ \Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
\partial_{\nu}u=\nabla u\cdot\nu,
the given Neumann data
\varphi
is non-null, and
q^{*}
is a space-dependent potential. Moreover, the notation
\partial_{t}^{\alpha}
denotes the so-called Caputo fractional derivative of order
0<\alpha<1
in time and is defined by (see e.g.
[
35
]
)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u(t)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\alpha}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\tau}(\tau)\,\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\tau,\quad 0<t\leq T,

(5)

where
\Gamma
denotes the Euler’s Gamma function, which is defined on each complex number
z\in\mathbb{C}
with positive real part (i.e.
\mathfrak{R}\{z\}>0
), by

\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{z-1}e^{-s}\,\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s.

In the limiting case
\alpha=1
, the Caputo derivative (
5
) coincides with the classical first-order derivative
\partial_{t}u
. Consequently, the subdiffusion (
2
) reduces to the standard parabolic diffusion equation, which models conventional Brownian motion.

The subdiffusion (
2
) has garnered significant attention across the physical, engineering, and mathematical literature due to its powerful ability to model
anomalous diffusion
processes. These processes are characterized by a mean squared displacement of the form
\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle\sim t^{\alpha}
, which deviates from the linear growth
\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle\sim t
associated with standard Fickian diffusion.
From a microscopic perspective, the model (
2
) can be derived as the macroscopic continuum limit of a Continuous Time Random Walk
[
57
]
. In such a stochastic process, the waiting time between successive particle jumps is governed by a heavy-tailed probability distribution
\psi(t)\sim t^{-(1+\alpha)}
whose mean diverges. The probability density function of a particle being at location
x
at time
t>0
is precisely described by the solution
u(x,t)
to (
2
), generalizing the classical connection between Brownian motion and the standard diffusion equation
[
47
]
. The inclusion of the potential term
q^{*}(x)u
further allows for the modeling of reaction processes or interactions with an external field.

The system (
2
) defines a well-posed direct problem when the potential
q^{*}
and the Neumann boundary data
\varphi
are prescribed. In this work, we address its inverse problem: the reconstruction of the unknown space-dependent potential
q^{*}
from additional boundary measurements. To this end, we introduce the following set of admissible potentials:

\displaystyle\Phi_{ad}=\Big\{q\in H^{1}(\Omega),\hbox{ such that }0<c\leq q\leq c^{\prime}\Big\},

where
c
and
c^{\prime}
are given positive constants and
H^{1}(\Omega)
denotes the classical Sobolev space
[
11
]
. Hence, the inverse problem can be reformulated as follows:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\hbox{Given a boundary observation}\;\phi,\hbox{ reconstruct the potential}\;q^{*}\in\Phi_{ad}\;\hbox{such that}\\
\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\phi(x,t)=u(x,t),\;\;\;(x,t)\in\Sigma_{T}.\end{array}\right.

(6)

The boundary observational data
\phi
for which this inverse problem has a solution
q^{*}
is said to be compatible. The well-posedness of the inverse problem (
6
) has been studied in the literature: uniqueness was established by Kian et al.
[
45
]
(see also
[
68
]
), and a local stability result was later obtained in
[
39
]
.

To numerically solve this inverse potential problem, a reconstruction method is developed within the Kohn–Vogelius minimization framework. This strategy reformulates the ill-posed inverse problem as a well-posed optimization problem. The next section establishes the analytical foundation of the approach: the necessary function spaces are recalled, and a detailed well-posedness analysis of the direct problem (
2
) is presented, which provides the essential groundwork for the subsequent analysis of the inverse problem.

3.
Well-posedness of the forward problem

We briefly present a preliminary result on the well-posedness and regularity of the solution to the forward problem (
2
). We first introduce some notation on functional spaces.

3.1.
Notations and auxiliary results

For any
0<\alpha<1,
we define
H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}),
the Sobolev space of order
\alpha>0
over
\mathbb{R},
by (see, e.g.,
[
55
]
)

H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})=\left\{w(t);\;w\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R});\;\left(1+|\lambda|^{2}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{F}(w)(\lambda)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\right\},

endow with the norm:

\|w\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})}=\left\|\left(1+|\lambda|^{2}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{F}(w)(\lambda)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},

where
\mathcal{F}(w)
denotes the Fourier transform of
w
. In addition, we set

H^{\alpha}(0,T)=\left\{w\in L^{2}(0,T);\;\exists\,\tilde{w}\in H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})\text{ such that }\left.\tilde{w}\right|_{(0,T)}=w\right\},

where

\|w\|_{H^{\alpha}(0,T)}=\inf\left\{\|\tilde{w}\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})};\;\left.\tilde{w}\right|_{(0,T)}=w\right\}.

We also define the space

{}_{0}C^{\infty}(0,\,T)=\Big\{w;\;\;w\in C^{\infty}(0,\,T)\;\hbox{ with compact support in}\;(0,T]\Big\}.

The space
{}_{0}H^{\alpha}(0,\,T)
denotes the closure of
{}_{0}C^{\infty}(0,\,T)
with respect to norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{\alpha}(0,T)}.
For an abstract Sobolev space
Z
with norm
\|\cdot\|_{Z}
, let

\displaystyle\displaystyle H^{\alpha}(0,T;Z)

\displaystyle:=\Big\{w;\;\|w(.,t)\|_{Z}\in H^{\alpha}(0,T)\Big\},

\displaystyle{}_{0}H^{\alpha}(0,T;Z)

\displaystyle:=\Big\{w;\;\|w(.,t)\|_{Z}\in{}_{0}H^{\alpha}(0,T)\Big\},

endowed with the norm:

\|w\|_{H^{\alpha}(0,T;Z)}:=\|\|w(.,t)\|_{Z}\|_{H^{\alpha}(0,T)}.

We will also need to recall some definitions of fractional derivatives.

Definition 1
.

(see
[
35
]
).
Let
w\;:\mathbb{R}_{+}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}.
The left- and right-sided Riemann-Liouville derivatives of order
0<\alpha<1
of

{}_{0}D^{\alpha}_{t}w(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}w(s)\hbox{ds},\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

{}_{t}D^{\alpha}_{T}w(t)=\frac{-1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{t}^{T}(s-t)^{-\alpha}w(s)\hbox{ds},\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

respectively.

We have the following lemma:

Lemma 2
.

(see
[
54
, Lemma 2.6]
). Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Then for any
w\in{}_{0}H^{\alpha}(0,T)
and
v\in{}_{0}H^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(0,T)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\Big({}_{0}D^{\alpha}_{t}w(t)\Big)v(t)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\Big({}_{0}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{t}w(t)\Big)\Big({}_{t}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{T}v(t)\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

Lemma 3
.

(see
[
54
, Lemma 2.2]
).
For
0<\alpha<1
,
w\in{}_{0}H^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(0,T)
, then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\Big({}_{0}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{t}w\Big)\Big({}_{t}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{T}w\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\geq\cos\Big(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\Big)\|{}_{0}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{t}w\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}.

Now, for
0<\alpha<1,
we define the space

\displaystyle B^{\alpha}(Q_{T})

\displaystyle:={}_{0}H^{\alpha}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

equipped with the norm

\|w\|_{B^{\alpha}(Q_{T})}=\Big(\|w\|^{2}_{H^{\alpha}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Referring to
[
54
]
, we know that
B^{\alpha}(Q_{T})
is a Hilbert space. We also introduce the following space:

\displaystyle{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right):=L^{2}(0,T;H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega))\cap{}_{0}H^{\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\partial\Omega)\right).

(7)

We represent the dual space of
{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)
as
H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)
. Furthermore, we use the notation
\Big<\psi,w\Big>_{1/2,\alpha/4}
to describe the duality pairing between
H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)
and
{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)
, where
\psi
belongs to
H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)
and
w
belongs to
{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right).

Based on the findings from
[
55
, Theorem 4.2]
and
[
56
, Theorem 2.1]
, we can now derive the following result.

Lemma 4
.

The trace map
\gamma:\;u\longmapsto u|_{\Sigma_{T}}
is continuous and surjective from
B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T})
to
{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})
.

3.2.
Well-posedness of the forward problem

The well-posedness of the forward problem (
2
) with non-zero Neumann boundary conditions has been extensively investigated in the literature. Rundell and Yamamoto
[
69
]
established the existence and uniqueness of a weak solution to the one-dimensional problem (
2
) with
q^{*}\in C(0,1)
and Neumann data
\varphi\in H_{\alpha}(0,T)
, using a representation formula. Yamamoto
[
73
]
extended this result to the multi-dimensional case, proving the unique existence of a weak solution via the transposition method under the assumptions
q^{*}\in C(\overline{\Omega})
and
\varphi\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-\gamma}(\partial\Omega))
with
\gamma>0
. In
[
16
]
, Cen
et al.
further demonstrated the well-posedness of (
2
) when the Neumann data
\varphi
is separable, i.e.,
\varphi(x,t)=g(x)\mu(t)
with
g\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
and
\mu\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Guidetti
[
25
]
showed the existence of a strong solution
u
to (
2
), which is continuous in time, belongs to the class
C^{2}
in space, and admits a Caputo fractional derivative
\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u
that is Hölder continuous in both time and space.
For completeness, for a positive potential
q^{*}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, Kian and Yamamoto
[
47
]
studied the well-posedness of (
2
) for both weak and strong solutions. More precisely, they proved the existence of a weak solution with non-zero initial data, Neumann boundary data
\varphi\in L^{1}(0,T;H^{-\theta}(\partial\Omega))
(with
\theta\in[1/2,+\infty)
), and non-homogeneous source terms lying in negative-order Sobolev spaces. For strong solutions, they introduced an optimal compatibility condition and established the existence of solutions under this setting.
On the other hand, several authors have analyzed the well-posedness of (
2
) with homogeneous Neumann or Dirichlet boundary conditions (i.e.,
\varphi=0
) and non-zero source terms; see, for example,
[
70
,
74
]
.

Our main contribution is to establish the existence and uniqueness of a weak solution to the forward problem (
2
) in the case where the potential
q^{*}
is positive and belongs to
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and the Neumann boundary data
\varphi\in H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})
. Before proceeding, and following
[
54
]
, we recall the definition of weak solutions to (
2
).

Definition 5
.

(Weak solution of (
2
)). Let
q^{*}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
be a positive potential and
\varphi\in H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)
. A function
u\in B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T})
is said to
be a weak solution of the problem (
2
) if the following holds:

\mathcal{A}(u,\vartheta)=\mathcal{L}(\vartheta)\quad\forall\vartheta\in B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T}),

(8)

where the bilinear form
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\,\cdot)
and the linear form
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
are defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(w,\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big({}_{0}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{t}w\;{}_{t}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{T}\vartheta+\nabla w.\nabla\vartheta+q^{*}\,w\,\vartheta\Big)\,\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\quad\hbox{for all }\,w,\vartheta\in B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\Big<\varphi,\,\vartheta\Big>_{1/2,\alpha/4}\;\;\hbox{for all }\,\vartheta\in B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T}).

The ensuing result establishes the existence and uniqueness of a weak solution for problem (
2
) by employing the well-known Lax-Milgram lemma.

Theorem 6
.

(Well-posedness of (
2
)).
Let
q^{*}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
be a positive potential and
\varphi\in H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})
. Then the initial-boundary value problem (
2
) admits a unique weak solution

u\in B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T})

in the sense of Definition
5
. Moreover, there exists a constant
C_{N}=C(\alpha,\Omega)>0
such that the following stability estimate holds:

\|u\|_{B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T})}\leq C_{N}\,\|\varphi\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}.

(9)

Proof.

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we can show that

\displaystyle\Big|\mathcal{L}(\vartheta)\Big|\leq\|\varphi\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}\|\vartheta\|_{{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}.

From Lemma
4
, there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle\Big|\mathcal{L}(\vartheta)\Big|\leq C_{1}\|\varphi\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}\|\vartheta\|_{B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T})},

(10)

which implies the continuity of the linear form
\mathcal{L}.

We will now establish the coercivity of
\mathcal{A}.
According to Lemma
3
, we get

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}{}_{0}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{t}\vartheta\,{}_{t}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{T}\vartheta\,\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\geq\cos\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\Big)\|{}_{0}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{t}\vartheta\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}\qquad\forall\vartheta\in B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T}).

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\vartheta,\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}{}_{0}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{t}\vartheta\,{}_{t}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{T}\vartheta\,\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\vartheta\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{*}\Big|\vartheta\Big|^{2}\,\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq\cos\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\Big)\|{}_{0}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{t}\vartheta\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}+\|\nabla\vartheta\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{*}\Big|\vartheta\Big|^{2}\,\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

Exploiting the positivity of
q^{*}
in
\Omega
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\vartheta,\vartheta)

\displaystyle\geq\cos\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\Big)\|{}_{0}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{t}\vartheta\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}+\|\nabla\vartheta\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}.

Moreover, as shown in
[
54
, Lemma 2.4]
and
[
54
, Lemma 2.5]
, the semi-norm
\|{}_{0}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{t}\cdot\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}
is equivalent to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(0,T)}.
As a result, there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\vartheta,\vartheta)

\displaystyle\geq C_{2}\cos\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\Big)\|\vartheta\|_{H^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|\nabla\vartheta\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{2}}{2}\cos\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\Big)\|\vartheta\|_{H^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\frac{C_{2}}{2}\cos\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\Big)\|\vartheta\|_{H^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|\nabla\vartheta\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}.

Thanks to the compact embedding
H^{\alpha/2}(0,T)\subset L^{2}(0,T)
(as established in
[
51
]
) for all
0<\alpha<1
, we can conclude:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{A}(\vartheta,\vartheta)&\geq\frac{C_{2}}{2}\cos\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\Big)\|\vartheta\|_{H^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\frac{C_{2}}{2}\cos\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\Big)\|\vartheta\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}+\|\nabla\vartheta\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}\\
&\geq\frac{C_{2}}{2}\cos\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\Big)\|\vartheta\|_{H^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\min\Big\{1,\frac{C_{2}}{2}\cos\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\Big)\Big\}\|\vartheta\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}.\end{split}

Therefore, we can state

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\vartheta,\vartheta)

\displaystyle\geq C_{\alpha}\|\vartheta\|_{B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T})}^{2},

(11)

which implies the coercivity of
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot).
In the same way, we can verify that the bilinear form
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
is continuous. Then according to Lax-Milgram lemma (see
[
11
]
), there exists a unique weak solution
u\in B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T})
to (
2
). Furthermore, by (
10
) and (
11
), we deduce

\|u\|_{B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T})}\leq C_{N}\|\varphi\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})},

(12)

which completes the proof.
∎

In the remainder of this paper, we assume that the measured data
\phi
are compatible and belong to the space
{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)
as defined in (
7
). Moreover, the Neumann data
\varphi
is assumed to be a nontrivial element of
H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)
.

With these assumptions in place, we now turn to the central objective of this work: the development of a reliable and efficient numerical method for reconstructing the potential
q^{*}
. To this end, we reformulate the inverse potential problem (
6
) as an equivalent optimization problem. Specifically, we introduce an objective functional inspired by the Kohn–Vogelius formulation, whose minimization provides the desired reconstruction.

4.
Problem reformulation -A Kohn-Vogelius formulation

The Kohn-Vogelius formulation provides a powerful framework for recasting the inverse potential problem (
6
) as an optimization problem. This approach relies on the introduction of two auxiliary boundary value problems defined for any admissible potential
q\in\Phi_{\text{ad}}
.

-
The first auxiliary problem, termed the
Neumann problem
, incorporates the prescribed flux
\varphi
as a boundary condition:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u_{N}[q]-\Delta u_{N}[q]+q\,u_{N}[q]&=&0&\hbox{in}\ Q_{T},\\
\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}u_{N}[q]&=&\varphi&\hbox{on}\ \Sigma_{T},\\
u_{N}[q](\cdot,0)&=&0&\hbox{in}\ \Omega.\end{array}\right.

The existence and uniqueness of a weak solution to the Neumann problem (
4
) have been established in Theorem
5
.

-
The second auxiliary problem, referred to as the
Dirichlet problem
, employs the measured boundary data
\phi
as a Dirichlet condition:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u_{D}[q]-\Delta u_{D}[q]+q\,u_{D}[q]&=&0&\hbox{in}\ Q_{T},\\
u_{D}[q]&=&\phi&\hbox{on}\ \Sigma_{T},\\
u_{D}[q](\cdot,0)&=&0&\hbox{in}\ \Omega.\end{array}\right.

The well-posedness of the Dirichlet problem (
4
) was established by Kemppainen and Ruotsalainen
[
44
, Corollary 3.6]
. Specifically, for
\phi\in{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})
, there exists a unique solution
u_{D}[q]\in B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T})
satisfying the estimate

\|u_{D}[q]\|_{B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T})}\leq C_{D}\|\phi\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})},

(21)

where
C_{D}:=C(\alpha,T,\Omega)>0
is a constant independent of
q
. In
[
44
, Corollary 3.6]
, the boundary data
\phi
belongs to the anisotropic Sobolev space
\widetilde{H}^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})
. Since
\frac{\alpha}{4}<\frac{1}{2}
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, it follows from
[
55
]
that
{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})
coincides with
\widetilde{H}^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})
, which aligns with our framework.

A key observation underpinning the Kohn-Vogelius approach is that when
q
coincides with the true potential
q^{*}
, the solutions to the auxiliary problems must satisfy
u_{D}[q^{*}]=u_{N}[q^{*}]
throughout
Q_{T}
. This motivates the definition of the Kohn-Vogelius functional, which quantifies the discrepancy between these solutions:

\mathcal{K}(q)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q]-u_{D}[q]\Big)\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q\Big|u_{N}[q]-u_{D}[q]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

(22)

The equivalence between minimizing
\mathcal{K}(q)
over
\Phi_{\text{ad}}
and solving the inverse problem (
6
) follows from two implications:

-

If
q^{*}\in\Phi_{\text{ad}}
solves the over-determined problem (
2
)–(
6
), then
u_{N}[q^{*}]=u_{D}[q^{*}]
, and consequently
\mathcal{K}(q^{*})=0
.

-

Conversely, if
\mathcal{K}(q^{*})=0
for some
q^{*}\in\Phi_{\text{ad}}
, then
u=u_{N}[q^{*}]=u_{D}[q^{*}]
satisfies both the governing equation and boundary conditions of (
2
)–(
6
).

However, the minimization of the Kohn-Vogelius functional
\mathcal{K}(q)
constitutes an ill-posed inverse problem, exhibiting significant sensitivity to noise in the Dirichlet measurement data
\phi
. To mitigate this instability, we employ Tikhonov regularization, which leads to the following stabilized optimization problem: find the potential
q^{*}\in\Phi_{ad}
that minimizes

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Minimize}_{q\in\Phi_{ad}}\ \mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q):=\mathcal{K}(q)+\rho\|q\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(23)

where
\rho>0
is a regularization parameter. Next, we demonstrate the following properties regarding problem (
23
):

(i)

Existence and uniqueness: There exists a unique solution to (
23
).

(ii)

Stability: The solution of (
23
) exhibits stability with respect to the measured Dirichlet data.

5.
Existence and uniqueness of optimal solutions

This section establishes the existence and uniqueness of an optimal solution to problem (
23
). The analysis begins with a continuity result that is fundamental to the subsequent proofs.

5.1.
Continuity of the Dirichlet and Neumann solution operators

Here, we establish the continuity of the solution operator
q\longmapsto u_{D}[q]
on
\Phi_{\text{ad}}
. The continuity of the Neumann solution operator
q\longmapsto u_{N}[q]
follows by an analogous argument.

Lemma 7
.

Let
q\in\Phi_{ad}
and
q^{n}
be a sequence of functions in
\Phi_{ad}.
If
\{q^{n}\}_{n}
converges strongly to
q
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then

u_{D}[q^{n}]\longrightarrow u_{D}[q]\;\hbox{ strongly in }\;L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\;\hbox{ as }\;n\to\infty,

where
u_{D}[q]
is the solution of (
4
) and
u_{D}[q^{n}]
is the solution of (
4
) with
q^{n}
replacing
q
.

Proof.

Consider the difference
w_{n}=u_{D}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q]
, which is the solution to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}w_{n}-\Delta w_{n}+q^{n}w_{n}&=&-(q^{n}-q)u_{D}[q]&\hbox{in}\ Q_{T},\\
\displaystyle w_{n}&=&0&\hbox{on}\ \Sigma_{T},\\
\displaystyle w_{n}(\cdot,0)&=&0&\hbox{in}\ \Omega.\end{array}\right.

By employing the weak formulation of the problem (
5.1
) and choosing
w_{n}
as a test function, coupled with the application of Lemma
3
, we derive

\displaystyle\cos(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2})\|{}_{0}D^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{t}w_{n}\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla w_{n}\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{n}|w_{n}|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|(q^{n}-q)u_{D}[q]w_{n}\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

Considering the nonnegative nature of
\displaystyle\cos(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2})
(because
0<\alpha<1
) and the presence of
q^{n}>0
within
\Omega,
we obtain

\displaystyle\|\nabla w_{n}\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|(q^{n}-q)u_{D}[q]w_{n}\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

With the help of the Poincaré inequality, there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
n

such that

\displaystyle\|w_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|(q^{n}-q)u_{D}[q]w_{n}\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t,

(28)

Now, let’s proceed to estimate the expression
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|(q^{n}-q)u_{D}[q]w_{n}\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.
To this end, let
1<r,s<\infty
be such that
1/r+2/s=1.
Utilizing the Hölder inequality, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big|(q^{n}-q)u_{D}[q]\,w_{n}\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\leq\|q^{n}-q\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\|u_{D}[q]\|_{L^{s}(\Omega)}\|w_{n}\|_{L^{s}(\Omega)}.

Choosing
r=2
and
s=4
and from the Sobolev embedding theorem (see, for example,
[
11
]
), there exists a constant
C(\Omega,d)>0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|(q^{n}

\displaystyle-q)u_{D}[q]\,w_{n}\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C(\Omega,d)\|q^{n}-q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u_{D}[q]\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\|w_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}.

(29)

Based on the estimate provided in (
21
), we derive

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|(q^{n}-q)u_{D}[q]\,w_{n}\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C(\Omega,d)C_{D}\|q^{n}-q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}\|w_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}.

(30)

By incorporating (
30
) into (
28
), one can deduce that there exists a constant
C(\alpha,T,\Omega,d):=C(\Omega,d)C_{D}C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|w_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C(\alpha,T,\Omega,d)\|q^{n}-q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}.

Since
\{q^{n}\}_{n}
converges strongly to
q
in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
, we get

\displaystyle\|w_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\longrightarrow 0\;\hbox{ as }\;n\to\infty,

which eventually implies that

\displaystyle u_{D}[q^{n}]\longrightarrow u_{D}[q]\;\hbox{ strongly in }\;L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)).

∎

The continuity of the Neumann solution with respect to the potential
q\in\Phi_{ad}
follows from an analysis analogous to that used in the proof of Lemma
7
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
q\in\Phi_{ad}
and
q^{n}
be a sequence of functions in
\Phi_{ad}.
If
\{q^{n}\}_{n}
converges strongly to
q
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then

u_{N}[q^{n}]\longrightarrow u_{N}[q]\;\hbox{ strongly in }\;L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\;\hbox{ as }\;n\to\infty,

(31)

where
u_{N}[q]
is the solution of (
4
) and
u_{N}[q^{n}]
is the solution of (
4
) with
q^{n}
replacing
q
.

5.2.
Existence of optimal solutions

In this context, we aim to prove the existence of an optimal solution to the optimization problem (
23
).

Theorem 9

(Existence)
.

For every
\rho>0
there exists at least one solution of the optimization
problem (
23
).

Proof.

The Kohn-Vogelius functional
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
from (
23
) is non-negative, implying that
\displaystyle\inf\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
is a finite quantity in the class
\Phi_{ad}.
Consequently, we can establish the existence of a minimizing sequence
\{q^{n}\}\subset\Phi_{ad}
such that:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q^{n})=\inf_{q\in\Phi_{ad}}\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q).

From the definition of the admissible solution set
\Phi_{ad}
, the sequence
{q^{n}}
is bounded in
H^{1}(\Omega)
. Consequently, there exists a subsequence, denoted by
n
, for which
q^{n}
weakly converges to some
q^{0}
in
H^{1}(\Omega).
We show that
q^{0}
is in fact a minimum for
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}.

Being a proper closed convex subset of
H^{1}(\Omega)
,
\Phi_{ad}
is also a closed subset of
H^{1}(\Omega)
for the weak topology (see, for example,
[
11
]
), from which we derive that
q^{0}
belongs to the class
\Phi_{ad}

Firstly, utilizing the compactness property derived from the Rellich-Kondrachov theorem (see, for instance,
[
11
]
), we obtain the following convergence result:

q^{n}\longrightarrow q^{0}\;\hbox{strongly in}\;L^{2}(\Omega)\;\hbox{as}\;n\rightarrow\infty.

(32)

Subsequently, leveraging the convergence outcomes described in Lemmas
7
and
8
, we obtain

\displaystyle u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n}]\longrightarrow u_{N}[q^{0}]-u_{D}[q^{0}]\;\hbox{strongly in}\;L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\;\hbox{as}\;n\rightarrow\infty.

(33)

We shall now establish the convergence result:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n}\longrightarrow 0\;\;\hbox{as}\;n\rightarrow\infty,

where the term
\mathcal{I}_{n}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n}=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{n}\Big|u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n}]\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{0}\Big|u_{N}[q^{0}]-u_{D}[q^{0}]\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

To this end, we decompose the term
\mathcal{I}_{n}
in two terms as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{n,1}+\mathcal{I}_{n,2},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n,1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{n}\Big(\Big|u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n}]\Big|^{2}-\Big|u_{N}[q^{0}]-u_{D}[q^{0}]\Big|^{2}\Big)\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n,2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big(q^{n}-q^{0}\Big)\Big|u_{N}[q^{0}]-u_{D}[q^{0}]\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

Since
q^{n}\in\Phi_{ad}
, then
q^{n}\leq c^{\prime}
in
\Omega
for each
n
. So

\displaystyle\Big|\mathcal{I}_{n,1}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime}\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\Big|u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n}]\Big|^{2}-\Big|u_{N}[q^{0}]-u_{D}[q^{0}]\Big|^{2}\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\Big|.

Utilizing the convergence result presented in (
33
), we can conclude that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n,1}\longrightarrow 0\;\;\hbox{as}\;n\rightarrow\infty.

(34)

We now turn our attention to examining the convergence of
\mathcal{I}_{n,2}
. Employing the Hölder inequality, we can derive

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n,2}\leq\|q^{n}-q^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u_{N}[q^{0}]-u_{D}[q^{0}]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{4}(\Omega))}.

With the assistance of the Sobolev embedding theorem, we can infer:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n,2}\leq C(\Omega,d)\|q^{n}-q^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u_{N}[q^{0}]-u_{D}[q^{0}]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}.

By employing the triangle inequality in conjunction with the estimates (
9
) and (
21
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n,2}\leq C(\Omega,d)\|q^{n}-q^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big(C_{N}\|\varphi\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}+C_{D}\|\phi\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}\Big)^{2}.

Therefore, from (
32
), we can show that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n,2}\longrightarrow 0\;\;\hbox{as}\;n\rightarrow\infty.

(35)

As a result, by combining both (
34
) and (
35
), we arrive at the following convergence result:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{n}\Big|u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n}]\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{0}\Big|u_{N}[q^{0}]-u_{D}[q^{0}]\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

(36)

Consequently, by combining the convergence results (
32
), (
33
), and (
36
), along with the lower semi-continuity of the
L^{2}
-norm, we can establish the following inequality:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q^{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q^{0}]-u_{D}[q^{0}]\Big)\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{0}\Big|u_{N}[q^{0}]-u_{D}[q^{0}]\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\rho\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|q^{0}\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\inf\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n}]\Big)\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle+\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\inf\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{n}\Big|u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n}]\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\rho\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\inf\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|q^{n}\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\inf\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q^{n})=\inf_{q\in\Phi_{ad}}\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q).

This inequality implies that
q^{0}
serves as a minimizer for the optimization problem (
23
).
∎

5.3.
Uniqueness of optimal solutions

In this section, we address the uniqueness of the optimal solution to the minimization problem (
23
). To this end, we begin by analyzing the convexity of the Kohn–Vogelius functional
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
. This analysis requires first establishing the Lipschitz continuity of the forward operators and their differentiability with respect to the potential
q
.

5.3.1.
Lipschitz continuity analysis of the forward operators

Here, we will delve into the examination of the Lipschitz continuity property exhibited by the Dirichlet and Neumann solution operators.

Lemma 10
.

The mapping
q\longmapsto u_{D}[q]
is Lipschitz continuous from
\Phi_{ad}
to
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
, i.e., for any
q
,
q+\delta q\in\Phi_{ad}
and the corresponding
u_{D}[q],u_{D}[q+\delta q]\in B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T})
, there holds

\displaystyle\|u_{D}[q+\delta q]-u_{D}[q]\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(\Omega,d)C_{D}\|\phi\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}}{\min\{1,\frac{T^{-\alpha}}{2\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\}}\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(37)

where
C_{D}
denotes the constant specified in (
21
), while
C(\Omega,d)>0
represents another constant depending only on the domain
\Omega
and the space’s dimension
d
.

Proof.

Denote
\delta\vartheta_{D}=u_{D}[q+\delta q]-u_{D}[q],

then
\delta\vartheta_{D}
satisfies the problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\partial^{\alpha}_{t}\delta\vartheta_{D}-\Delta\delta\vartheta_{D}+(q+\delta q)\delta\vartheta_{D}&=&-\delta qu_{D}[q]&\hbox{in}\ Q_{T},\\
\displaystyle\delta\vartheta_{D}&=&0&\hbox{on}\ \Sigma_{T},\\
\displaystyle\delta\vartheta_{D}(\cdot,0)&=&0&\hbox{in}\ \Omega.\end{array}\right.

Since
q\in\Phi_{ad}
and
q+\delta q\in\Phi_{ad}
, it follows that
|\delta q|\leq c^{\prime}-c
. Moreover, according to (
21
),
u_{D}[q]\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, implying
\delta q\,u_{D}[q]\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. By applying
[
30
, Lemma 2.4]
, problem (
5.3.1
) admits a unique weak solution
\delta\vartheta_{D}
and
satisfying the regularity property:

\delta\vartheta_{D}\in{}_{0}H^{\alpha}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)).

(42)

Now, employing the weak formulation of the problem (
5.3.1
) and choosing
\delta\vartheta_{D}
as a test function, we derive

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\delta\vartheta_{D}\;\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\delta\vartheta_{D}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\delta\vartheta_{D}\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(q+\delta q)\Big|\delta\vartheta_{D}\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\Big|\\
&\quad\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\delta q\,u_{D}[q]\delta\vartheta_{D}\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(43)

According to
[
51
, Theorem 3.3]
, we know that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\partial_{t}^{\alpha}g\Big)\,g\,\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\geq\frac{T^{-\alpha}}{2\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;\,L^{2}(\Omega))}

(44)

for all
g\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\cap{}_{0}H^{\alpha}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

Using the regularity property (
42
) and the above coercivity inequality (
44
), we derive

\displaystyle\frac{T^{-\alpha}}{2\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\|\delta\vartheta_{D}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;\,L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\delta\vartheta_{D}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\delta\vartheta_{D}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

(45)

Applying the Hölder inequality, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\Omega}\delta qu_{D}[q]\,\delta\vartheta_{D}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u_{D}[q]\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\|\delta\vartheta_{D}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}.

On the other hand, from the Sobolev embedding theorem, there exists a constant
C(\Omega,d)>0
such that

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\delta qu_{D}[q]\,\delta\vartheta_{D}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\Big|

5.3.3.
The Fréchet derivative of
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}

In this section, we analyze the Fréchet differentiability of the Kohn-Vogelius type function
q\longmapsto\mathcal{K}(q)
on the class
\Phi_{ad}
. To this end, we decompose the Kohn-Vogelius functional
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
into four terms as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q)=\mathcal{K}_{NN}(q)+\mathcal{K}_{DD}(q)-2\mathcal{K}_{ND}(q)+\rho\|q\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where
\mathcal{K}_{NN}(q)
is the Neumann term given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{NN}(q)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla u_{N}[q]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q\Big|u_{N}[q]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t,

\mathcal{K}_{DD}(q)
is the Dirichlet term defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{DD}(q)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla u_{D}[q]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q\Big|u_{D}[q]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t,

and
\mathcal{K}_{ND}(q)
is the Neumann/Dirichlet term

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{ND}(q)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{N}[q].\nabla u_{D}[q]\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}qu_{N}[q]u_{D}[q]\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

Consequently, to compute the Fréchet differentiability of
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
, we analyze the Fréchet differentiability of each of its components with respect to
q
:
\mathcal{K}_{NN}
,
\mathcal{K}_{DD}
, and
\mathcal{K}_{ND}
. To begin, we examine the Fréchet differentiability of the function
q\mapsto\mathcal{K}_{NN}(q)
.

Lemma 14
.

The functional
\mathcal{K}_{NN}
is Fréchet differentiable. Moreover, the Fréchet derivative of the functional
\mathcal{K}_{NN}
at
q\in\Phi_{ad}
in the direction
\delta q
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{NN}^{\prime}(q)\delta q

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{N}[q]\cdot\nabla\mathcal{U}_{N}[q](\delta q)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q\,u_{N}[q]\;\mathcal{U}_{N}[q](\delta q)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\delta q\;|u_{N}[q]|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x.

Proof.

Let
\delta q\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be such that
q+\delta q\in\Phi_{ad}
. By direct calculation and utilizing Lemma
13
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{NN}(q+\delta q)-\mathcal{K}_{NN}(q)-\mathcal{K}_{NN}^{\prime}(q)\delta q=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\mathcal{R}_{i}+o\Big(\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big),

(69)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\delta q\,u_{N}[q]\;\mathcal{U}_{N}[q](\delta q)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{2}

\displaystyle=2\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{N}[q]\cdot\nabla\mathcal{U}_{N}[q](\delta q)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\Big)\times o\Big(\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{3}

\displaystyle=2\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(q+\delta q)u_{N}[q]\;\mathcal{U}_{N}[q](\delta q)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\Big)\times o\Big(\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{4}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\mathcal{U}_{N}[q](\delta q)\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(q+\delta q)\Big|\mathcal{U}_{N}[q](\delta q)\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

Next we give an estimate for each term

(

). Using a similar argument as in the derivation of (
46

9

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\delta q\,u_{N}[q]\;\mathcal{U}_{N}[q](\delta q)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\Big|\leq C(\alpha,\Omega,d)\|\varphi\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}^{2}\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(70)

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and using the inequalities (
9

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{N}[q]\cdot\nabla\mathcal{U}_{N}[q](\delta q)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\Big|\leq C(\alpha,\Omega,d)\|\varphi\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}^{2}\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(71)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{2}=o\Big(\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big).

(72)

Now, we estimate the term
\mathcal{R}_{3}

(i.e.,

for all

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q\,u_{N}[q]\;\mathcal{U}_{N}[q](\delta q)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\Big|&\leq c^{\prime}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|u_{N}[q]\,\mathcal{U}_{N}[q](\delta q)\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\\

On the other hand, we can observe that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{3}=o\Big(\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big).

(74)

Next, we estimate the term

From this and Lemma

\displaystyle\Big|\mathcal{R}_{4}\Big|\leq\max\{1,c^{\prime}\}\|\mathcal{U}_{N}[q](\delta q)\|_{B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T})}^{2}\leq C(\alpha,\Omega,d)^{2}\|\varphi\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}^{2}\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(75)

Inserting (

5.3.4.
Uniqueness

Here we establish the strict convexity of the Kohn-Vogelius functional
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
and demonstrate the uniqueness of its minimizer within the class
\Phi_{ad}
. This convexity result is presented in Theorem
18
. The proof relies on a classical property stating that the functional
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
is strictly convex if the condition
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q+\delta q)-\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q)-\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{\rho}(q)\delta q>0
holds (cf.
[

), where
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{\rho}(q)
denotes the Fréchet derivative of
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
.

Theorem 18

(Uniqueness)
.

For any
\rho>0
, there exists a positive constant
M
independent of
q\in\Phi_{ad}
such that for
M<\rho

\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q)
is strictly convex.

Let
\delta q\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q+\delta q)-\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q)-\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{\rho}(q)\delta q=K_{1}+K_{2}-2K_{3}+\rho\|\delta q\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where

\displaystyle{K}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}_{NN}(q+\delta q)-\mathcal{K}_{NN}(q)-\mathcal{K}_{NN}^{\prime}(q)\delta q,

\displaystyle{K}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}_{DD}(q+\delta q)-\mathcal{K}_{DD}(q)-\mathcal{K}_{DD}^{\prime}(q)\delta q,

\displaystyle{K}_{3}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}_{ND}(q+\delta q)-\mathcal{K}_{ND}(q)-\mathcal{K}_{ND}^{\prime}(q)\delta q.

Now, we proceed to analyze the lower bound of each term
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
, and
K_{3}

\displaystyle K_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}r_{i},

\displaystyle r_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big)\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(q+\delta q)\Big|u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle r_{2}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\delta qu_{N}[q]\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle r_{3}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{N}[q].\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]-\mathcal{U}_{N}[q](\delta q)\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}qu_{N}[q]\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]-\mathcal{U}_{N}[q](\delta q)\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

To control the term
K_{1}
by
\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
, we examine the components
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
, and
r_{3}

, hence

\displaystyle K_{1}\geq r_{2}+r_{3}.

(77)

Next, we examine
r_{2}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big|\delta qu_{N}[q]\,\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big)\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\leq\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u_{N}[q]\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\|u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}.

By integrating over time and applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality along with Sobolev embedding theorem, we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\delta qu_{N}[q]\,\Big(

\displaystyle u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big)\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C(\Omega,d)\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u_{N}[q]\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\|u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}.

Leveraging Lemma
11
and inequality (
9
), we get

\displaystyle\Big|r_{2}\Big|\leq\frac{2C(\Omega,d)^{2}C_{N}^{2}\|\varphi\|^{2}_{H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}}{\min\{1,\frac{T^{-\alpha}}{2\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\}}\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(78)

We now turn our attention to examining the last term
r_{3}
. By applying the same strategy developed to deduce (
48
), we can conclude that

\displaystyle\|u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]-\mathcal{U}_{N}[q](\delta q)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq\frac{C(\Omega,d)^{2}C_{N}\|\varphi\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}}{\min\{1,\frac{T^{-\alpha}}{2\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\}^{2}}\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Using the above inequality and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we can deduce that

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{N}[q].\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-

Remark 19
.

We notice that given

Next, we want to investigate the stable dependence of the minimizers of the Kohn-Vogelius functional with respect to the perturbation in observation data.

6.
Stability and convergence of the Kohn and Vogelius method

The inverse problem (
6
) may lack stability, but in this section, we establish the stability of the optimization problem (
23
). This stability arises because the Kohn and Vogelius cost function incorporates solutions computed from both the prescribed and measured data, extending beyond just the boundary. Consequently, it prohibits unstable behavior away from the prescribed part of the boundary, which could significantly impact the cost function. To address this, we introduce a sequence of “measurements”, denoted as
\phi^{n}
in
{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)
, with the property:

\displaystyle\|\phi^{n}-\phi\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)}\longrightarrow 0\;\;\hbox{as}\;n\rightarrow\infty.

(86)

Furthermore, for simplicity in further analysis, we define
u_{D}[q,\phi^{n}]
as the solution to the problem (
4
), where
q
serves as the potential, and
\phi^{n}
acts as the Dirichlet boundary data on
\Sigma_{T}.
Additionally, for each
n
, we denote by
q^{n}\in\Phi_{ad}
the solution to the perturbed optimization problem:

(\mathcal{O}\mathcal{P}^{n}_{\rho}):\;\;\;\displaystyle\operatorname*{Minimize}_{q\in\Phi_{ad}}\ \mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{n}(q):=\mathcal{N}(q,\phi^{n})+\rho\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Here, the function
\mathcal{N}
is defined as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rclll}\mathcal{N}:&\Phi_{ad}\times{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)&\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\\
&(q,f)&\longmapsto\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q]-u_{D}[q,f]\Big)\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q\Big|u_{N}[q]-u_{D}[q,f]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.\end{array}

Recall that
u_{D}[q,f]
represents the solution of the problem (
4
) with
q
as a potential and
f
as Dirichlet boundary data on
\Sigma_{T}
. In addition,
u_{N}[q]
denotes the usual solution of the problem (
4
) with
q
as a potential.

Two main challenges arise in this setting:

•

Existence of perturbed solutions
: When the Dirichlet data
\phi
is replaced by
\phi^{n}
, the pair
(q^{*},\phi^{n})
is no longer compatible, meaning that the inverse problem (
6
) may not have a solution. Consequently, the perturbed optimization problem
(\mathcal{O}\mathcal{P}_{\rho}^{n})
is no longer equivalent to the inverse problem. Nevertheless, we are interested in the solutions
q^{n}
of the optimization problem itself.

•

Convergence of perturbed solutions
: Assuming that
(\mathcal{O}\mathcal{P}_{\rho}^{n})
has solutions, we aim to establish whether
q^{n}
converges, in a suitable function space, to the solution of the inverse problem corresponding to the exact measurements
\phi
.

6.1.
Stability

Here, we justify the stability of the optimization problem
(\mathcal{O}\mathcal{P}_{\rho}^{n})
with respect to perturbations in the observation data
\phi
.

Theorem 20
.

Let
\rho>0
satisfy the uniqueness condition in Theorem
18
and let
\{\phi^{n}\}\subset{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})
be a sequence of perturbed measurements satisfying (
86
). Let
\{q^{n}\}\subset\Phi_{ad}
be a sequence of minimizers of problems
(\mathcal{O}\mathcal{P}_{\rho}^{n})
for
n=1,2,\cdots
. Then there exists a subsequence
\{q^{n_{\ell}}\}
of
\{q^{n}\}
, such that

q^{n_{\ell}}\longrightarrow q^{0}\;\hbox{strongly in}\;L^{2}(\Omega)\;\hbox{as}\;\ell\rightarrow\infty,

where
q^{0}\in\mathcal{Q}
is the unique minimizer of the optimization problem (
23
).

Proof.

The unique existence of each
q^{n}\in\Phi_{ad}
is ensured by Theorems
9
and
18
. Employing a similar technique as in the proof of the convergence result (
32
), we establish that the sequence
q^{n}
strongly converges to
q^{0}\in\Phi_{ad}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. In other words, we have

q^{n}\longrightarrow q^{0}\;\hbox{strongly in}\;L^{2}(\Omega)\;\hbox{as}\;n\rightarrow\infty.

(87)

Now it suffices to show that
q^{0}
is indeed the unique minimizer of (
23
). From (
87
) and Lemma
8
, one can deduce that

u_{N}[q^{n}]\longrightarrow u_{N}[q^{0}]\;\hbox{ strongly in }\;L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\;\hbox{ as }\;n\to\infty,

(88)

where
u_{N}[q^{0}]
is the solution to (
4
) with
q=q^{0}
, and
u_{N}[q^{n}]
is the solution to (
4
) with
q=q^{n}
.

Next, we prove that

u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\longrightarrow u_{D}[q^{0},\phi]\quad\text{stongly in}\quad L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\;\;\text{as}\;\;n\longrightarrow\infty.

(89)

To establish the above convergence result, we define
\vartheta_{n}=u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{0},\phi]
. It is straightforward to deduce that
\vartheta_{n}
is the solution to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\vartheta_{n}-\Delta\vartheta_{n}+q^{0}\vartheta_{n}&=&-(q^{n}-q^{0})u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]&\text{in}\;Q_{T},\\
\vartheta_{n}&=&\phi^{n}-\phi&\text{on}\;\Sigma_{T},\\
\vartheta_{n}(\cdot,0)&=&0&\text{in}\;\Omega.\end{array}\right.

We split
\vartheta_{n}
into
\vartheta_{n}^{1}+\vartheta_{n}^{2}
such that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\vartheta_{n}^{1}-\Delta\vartheta_{n}^{1}+q^{0}\vartheta_{n}^{1}&=&-(q^{n}-q^{0})u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]&\text{in}\;Q_{T},\\
\vartheta_{n}^{1}&=&0&\text{on}\;\Sigma_{T},\\
\vartheta_{n}^{1}(\cdot,0)&=&0&\text{in}\;\Omega.\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\vartheta_{n}^{2}-\Delta\vartheta_{n}^{2}+q^{0}\vartheta_{n}^{2}&=&0&\text{in}\;Q_{T},\\
\vartheta_{n}^{2}&=&\phi^{n}-\phi&\text{on}\;\Sigma_{T},\\
\vartheta_{n}^{2}(\cdot,0)&=&0&\text{in}\;\Omega.\end{array}\right.

By taking
\vartheta_{n}^{1}
as a test function in the weak formulation of (
6.1
), we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\vartheta_{n}^{1}\Big)\vartheta_{n}^{1}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\vartheta_{n}^{1}\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{0}\Big|\vartheta_{n}^{1}\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big(q^{n}-q^{0}\Big)\vartheta_{n}^{1}u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\,\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

Employing a similar technique as in the proof of inequality (
28
), we obtain

\|\vartheta_{n}^{1}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq C(\Omega)\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\Big(q^{n}-q^{0}\Big)\vartheta_{n}^{1}u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

(96)

Similar to (
5.1
), one can deduce

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\Big(q^{n}-q^{0}\Big)\vartheta_{n}^{1}u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\leq C(\Omega)\|q^{n}-q^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\vartheta_{n}^{1}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\|u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}.

(97)

From the inequality (
21
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C(\alpha,T,\Omega)\|\phi^{n}\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq C(\alpha,T,\Omega)\Big(\|\phi^{n}-\phi\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}+\|\phi\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}\Big).

Using the fact that
\phi^{n}\to\phi
in
{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})
as

, we obtain

\displaystyle\|u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C(\alpha,T,\Omega,\phi).

(98)

Inserting (
98
) into (
97
), we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\Big(q^{n}-q^{0}\Big)\vartheta_{n}^{1}u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\leq C(\alpha,T,\Omega,\phi)\|q^{n}-q^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\vartheta_{n}^{1}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}.

(99)

Inserting (

) into (

), we get

\|\vartheta_{n}^{1}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C(\alpha,T,\Omega,\phi)\|q^{n}-q^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(100)

Moreover, from the inequality (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\vartheta_{n}^{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C(\alpha,T,\Omega)\|\phi^{n}-\phi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\partial\Omega))}.

(101)

Combining (
100
) with (
101
), and using the convergence result (
87
) and the fact that
\phi^{n}\longrightarrow\phi
in
{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})
as
n\longrightarrow\infty
, we get

\|\vartheta_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\longrightarrow 0\;\;\text{as}\;\;n\longrightarrow\infty.

On the other hand, following the same strategy developed in the proof of (
36
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{n}\Big|u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{0}\Big|u_{N}[q^{0}]-u_{D}[q^{0},\phi]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

(102)

Consequently, by combining the convergence results (
87
), (
88
), (
89
), and (
102
) along with the lower semi-continuity of the

-norm, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q^{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q^{0}]-u_{D}[q^{0}]\Big)\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{0}\Big|u_{N}[q^{0}]-u_{D}[q^{0}]\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\;\;\quad+\rho\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|q^{0}\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\inf\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\Big)\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\;\;\quad+\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\inf\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{n}\Big|u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\rho\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\inf\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|q^{n}\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

Moreover, since
\{q^{n}\}\subset\Phi_{ad}
is a sequence of minimizers of problems
(\mathcal{O}\mathcal{P}_{\rho}^{n})
, and by using
u_{D}[q,\phi^{n}]\longrightarrow u_{D}[q,\phi]
in

as

, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q^{0})

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q]-u_{D}[q,\phi^{n}]\Big)\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q\Big|u_{N}[q]-u_{D}[q,\phi^{n}]\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q),\quad\;\;\;\hbox{for any}\;q\in\Phi_{ad},

which verifies that

is the minimizer of (

∎

6.2.
Convergence of the Kohn-Vogelius method

Here, we demonstrate that the solution of the minimization problem
(\mathcal{O}\mathcal{P}_{\rho}^{n})
converges to the solution of the inverse problem as
\rho\to 0.

Lemma 21
.

Let
\phi^{n}\in{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)
be a sequence of observation data such that
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\phi^{n}-\phi\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)}=0.
Let
q^{n}\in\Phi_{ad}
be a solution to the optimization problem
(\mathcal{O}\mathcal{P}_{\rho_{n}}^{n});
i.e.,

\inf_{q\in\Phi_{ad}}\mathcal{K}_{\rho_{n}}^{n}(q)=\mathcal{N}(q^{n},\phi^{n})+\rho_{n}\|q^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

where
\rho_{n}>0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\rho_{n}\longrightarrow 0
as
n\to\infty
. Consequently, it follows that:

\mathcal{N}(q^{n},\phi^{n})\longrightarrow 0\;\;\hbox{ as }\;n\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

Let
q^{*}
be a solution of the inverse problem (
6
). As
q^{n}
is a minimizer of the optimization problem
(\mathcal{O}\mathcal{P}^{n}_{\rho_{n}}),
we have by the minimization property

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathcal{N}(q^{n},\phi^{n})\leq\mathcal{N}(q^{*},\phi^{n})+\rho_{n}\|q^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(103)

Let us prove now that
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{N}(q^{n},\phi^{n})=0.
To obtain this result, let us define the following function

w_{n}:=u_{D}[q^{*},\phi^{n}]-u_{N}[q^{*}]=u_{D}[q^{*},\phi^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{*},\phi].

Here,
u_{D}[q^{*},\phi^{n}]
denotes the solution to (
4
) with
q=q^{*}
and
\phi=\phi^{n}
,
u_{N}[q^{*}]
represents the solution to (
4
) with
q=q^{*}
, and
u_{D}[q^{*},\phi]
signifies the solution to (
4
) with
q=q^{*}
. We can easily deduce that
w_{n}
is a solution to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}w_{n}-\Delta w_{n}+q^{*}w_{n}&=&0&\hbox{in}\ Q_{T},\\
\displaystyle w_{n}&=&\phi^{n}-\phi&\hbox{on}\ \Sigma_{T},\\
\displaystyle w_{n}(.,0)&=&0&\hbox{in}\ \Omega.\end{array}\right.

From (
21
), we can check that there exists a constant
C_{D}>0
(independent of
n
and
\phi
) such that

\|w_{n}\|_{B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T})}\leq C_{D}\|\phi^{n}-\phi\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}.

and since
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\phi^{n}-\phi\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}=0
, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\|w_{n}\|_{B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T})}=0.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\displaystyle\|w_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}=0.

(105)

On the other hand, we have

\begin{array}[]{rll}\mathcal{N}(q^{*},\phi^{n})&=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q^{*}]-u_{D}[q^{*},\phi^{n}]\Big)\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{*}\Big|u_{N}[q^{*}]-u_{D}[q^{*},\phi^{n}]\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\displaystyle\|\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q^{*}]-u_{D}[q^{*},\phi^{n}]\Big)\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}+c^{\prime}\displaystyle\|u_{N}[q^{*}]-u_{D}[q^{*},\phi^{n}]\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}\\
&\leq\max\{1,c^{\prime}\}\displaystyle\|u_{N}[q^{*}]-u_{D}[q^{*},\phi^{n}]\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}\\
&=\max\{1,c^{\prime}\}\displaystyle\|w_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}.\end{array}

Exploiting the convergence
\rho_{n}\longrightarrow 0
as
n\to\infty
and employing (
105
), we can infer that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{N}(q^{*},\phi^{n})+\rho_{n}\|q^{*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0

which, when combined with the inequality (
103
), yields:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(q^{n},\phi^{n})\longrightarrow 0\;\;\hbox{ as }\;n\rightarrow\infty.

Thus we complete the proof.
∎

We can now present the convergence result of our optimization problem.

Theorem 22
.

Assume a solution
q^{*}\in\Phi_{ad}
to the inverse potential problem (
6
) corresponding to the datum
\phi
exists.
Let
\{\phi^{n}\}\subset{}_{0}H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)
be a sequence such that

\displaystyle\|\phi^{n}-\phi\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)}\longrightarrow 0\;\;\hbox{ as }\;n\rightarrow\infty,

and
\{\rho_{n}\}
be a positive sequence such that
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{n}=0
. Let
\{q^{n}\}\subset\Phi_{ad}
be a sequence of minimizers of problems
(\mathcal{O}\mathcal{P}_{\rho_{n}}^{n})
. Then, there exists a subsequence of
\{q^{n}\}
, still denoted
by
\{q^{n}\}
, such that

\displaystyle q^{n}\longrightarrow q^{*}\;\hbox{ strongly in}\;L^{2}(\Omega)\;\hbox{as}\;n\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

Actually, repeating the same argument as that in (
32
), we can derive that there exist
\overline{q}\in\Phi_{ad}
and a subsequence of
q^{n}
, still denoted by
q^{n}
such that

\displaystyle q^{n}\longrightarrow\overline{q}\;\hbox{ strongly in}\;L^{2}(\Omega)\;\hbox{as}\;n\rightarrow\infty.

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(q^{n},\phi^{n})

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\Big)\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q^{n}\Big|u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\Big)\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+c\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq\min\{1,c\}\|u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}.

According to Lemma
21
, we have
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{N}(q^{n},\phi^{n})=0
, and then:

\displaystyle\|u_{N}[q^{n}]-u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}\longrightarrow 0\;\;\hbox{ as }\;n\rightarrow\infty.

(106)

Since
q^{n}\longrightarrow\overline{q}\;\hbox{ strongly in}\;L^{2}(\Omega)\;\hbox{as}\;n\rightarrow\infty,
we can derive from (
31
) the following:

u_{N}[q^{n}]\longrightarrow u_{N}[\overline{q}]\;\hbox{ strongly in }\;L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\;\hbox{ as }\;n\to\infty.

So, we use (
106
) to get

u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\longrightarrow u_{N}[\overline{q}]\;\hbox{ strongly in }\;L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\;\hbox{ as }\;n\to\infty.

Then, we use the trace theorem to deduce

u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\Big|_{\Sigma_{T}}\longrightarrow u_{N}[\overline{q}]\Big|_{\Sigma_{T}}\;\hbox{ strongly in }\;L^{2}(0,T;H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega))\;\hbox{ as }\;n\to\infty.

Moreover, since
u_{D}[q^{n},\phi^{n}]\Big|_{\Sigma_{T}}=\phi^{n}
and
\phi^{n}\longrightarrow\phi
in
H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}\left(\Sigma_{T}\right)
,
we get

u_{N}[\overline{q}]\Big|_{\Sigma_{T}}=\phi.

This implies that
\overline{q}
solves the inverse problem (
6
). Finally, by the uniqueness result (for the inverse problem (
6
)) established by Kian
et al.
in
[
45
]
, we conclude that
\overline{q}=q^{*}
.
∎

7.
Numerical algorithm

As outlined in the introduction, we solve the optimization problem (
23
) using the CGM to compute a minimizer of (
22
). A critical component of this algorithm is the efficient computation of the gradient of
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
. Although Theorem
17
provides an expression for the Fréchet derivative (see (
76
)), this form is not amenable to direct numerical computation. Consequently, we derive a computationally efficient expression for the gradient using the adjoint method, as detailed in the next section. The subsequent analysis establishes the Lipschitz continuity of the gradient of
\mathcal{K}
(Section
7.2
), a key property for proving convergence. In Section
7.3
, we present the iterative procedure for the CGM method. The convergence of this iterative scheme is then rigorously analyzed in Section
7.4
. Finally, we address the practical implementation by deriving an explicit formula for the step size selection within the iterative process and summarize the complete reconstruction algorithm.

7.1.
The gradient of the functional
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
with the adjoint method

Recall that the Kohn-Vogelius functional
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q)=\mathcal{K}_{NN}(q)+\mathcal{K}_{DD}(q)-2\mathcal{K}_{ND}(q)+\rho\|q\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{NN}(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla u_{N}[q]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q\Big|u_{N}[q]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{DD}(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla u_{D}[q]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q\Big|u_{D}[q]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{ND}(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{N}[q]\cdot\nabla u_{D}[q]\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}qu_{N}[q]u_{D}[q]\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

Let us now introduce the definitions of the Riemann-Liouville fractional left integral
I_{0}^{\alpha}w(t)
and right integral
I_{T}^{\alpha}w(t)
respectively as

I_{0}^{\alpha}w(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}w(s)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s,\quad I_{T}^{\alpha}w(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{t}^{T}(s-t)^{\alpha-1}w(s)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s

for
w\in L^{2}(0,\,T).
From this, the Caputo fractional derivative and the right Riemann-Liouville derivative
{}_{t}D^{\alpha}_{T}
can be rephrased as

\partial_{t}^{\alpha}w(t)=I_{0}^{1-\alpha}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t}w(t),\qquad{}_{t}D^{\alpha}_{T}w(t):=-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t}I^{1-\alpha}_{T}w(t).

(107)

The next lemma establishes the Fréchet derivative of the function
q\longmapsto\mathcal{K}_{NN}(q)
using an adjoint state.

Lemma 24
.

The function
\mathcal{K}_{NN}
is Fréchet differentiable and its gradient is

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{NN}^{\prime}(q)=\int_{0}^{T}\Big|u_{N}[q](x,t)\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}u_{N}[q](x,t)V_{N}[q](x,t)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t,

(108)

where
V_{N}[q]
is the solution of the following adjoint problem:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rclcl}{}_{t}D^{\alpha}_{T}V_{N}[q]-\Delta V_{N}[q]+q\,V_{N}[q]&=&-2J_{u_{N}}&\hbox{in}&Q_{T},\\
\partial_{\nu}V_{N}[q]&=&0&\hbox{on}&\Sigma_{T},\\
I^{1-\alpha}_{T}V_{N}[q]&=&0&\hbox{in}&\Omega\times\{T\},\end{array}\right.

where the linear mapping
\eta\longmapsto J_{u_{N}}(\eta)=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big(\nabla u_{N}[q]\cdot\nabla\eta+q\,u_{N}[q]\eta\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x
for all
\eta\in B^{\alpha}(Q_{T}).

Proof.

Let
\delta q\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be such that
q+\delta q\in\Phi_{ad}
.
A straightforward calculation, using the expression of
\mathcal{K}_{NN}
, gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{NN}(q+\delta q)-\mathcal{K}_{NN}(q)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\delta q\Big|u_{N}[q]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathcal{I}_{i},

(113)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big)\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q\Big|u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\delta q\Big|u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\delta qu_{N}[q]\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{N}[q]\cdot\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}qu_{N}[q]\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

First, let’s estimate the term
\mathcal{I}_{1}
. Due to Lemma
11
, we get

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big)\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t=o\Big(\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big).

(114)

Now, regarding the second part of the term
\mathcal{I}_{1}
. Since
q\in\Phi_{ad}
, hence

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q\Big|u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\leq c^{\prime}\|u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}.

From Lemma
11
, one can deduce

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q\Big|u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t=o\Big(\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big).

(115)

Combining (
114
) and (
115
), we get

\mathcal{I}_{1}=o\Big(\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big).

(116)

Let’s proceed to estimate the term
\mathcal{I}_{2}
. Once more, we can employ the Hölder inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big|\delta qu_{N}[q]\,\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big)\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\leq\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u_{N}[q]\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\|u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}.

Next, by integrating over time and applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in conjunction with the Sobolev embedding theorem, we can deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big|\delta qu_{N}[q]\,\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big)\Big|\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\leq C(\Omega,d)\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u_{N}[q]\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

Subsequently, leveraging Lemma
11
and Theorem

, we acquire

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\delta qu_{N}[q]\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\leq C(\Omega,d)C_{N}\|\varphi\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=o\Big(\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big).

(117)

Likewise, we can establish that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\delta q\Big|\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big)\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t=o\Big(\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big).

(118)

Gathering (
117
) and (
118
), we conclude

\mathcal{I}_{2}=o\Big(\|\delta q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big).

(119)

To complete the proof, let’s proceed with the estimation of the term
\mathcal{I}_{3}.
From (
4
), we deduce that
w_{N}=\Big(u_{N}[q+\delta q]-u_{N}[q]\Big)
is solution to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}w_{N}-\Delta w_{N}+q\,w_{N}&=&-\delta q\,u_{N}[q+\delta q]&\hbox{in}\ Q_{T},\\

Thanks to Lemma 2.2 in
[

]
, we can establish that problem (

) possesses a unique weak solution

. By utilizing the weak formulation of problem (

as a test function, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\partial_{t}^{\alpha}(w_{N})\;V_{N}[q]+\nabla w_{N}\cdot\nabla V_{N}[q]&+q\;w_{N}\;V_{N}[q]\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\\

On the other hand, from (

and noting that

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{T}w_{N}\Big({}_{t}D^{\alpha}_{T}V_{N}[q]\Big)\,\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x.

This implies that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\partial_{t}^{\alpha}w_{N}\Big)V_{N}[q]\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big({}_{t}D^{\alpha}_{T}V_{N}[q]\Big)w_{N}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

(125)

Inserting (
125
) into (
124

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big({}_{t}D^{\alpha}_{T}V_{N}[q]\Big)w_{N}+\nabla w_{N}\cdot\nabla V_{N}[q]&+q\,w_{N}\,V_{N}[q]\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\\

Now, by multiplying the first equation of (

\displaystyle\nabla w_{N}+q\,V_{N}[q]\,w_{N}\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

(127)

By subtracting (

) from (

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3}=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\delta q\,w_{N}\,V_{N}[q]\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\delta q\,u_{N}[q]V_{N}[q]\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t.

where

is the usual scalar product on

This means that the function

is Fréchet differentiable, and its gradient is given by (

\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{1})-\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{2})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{L}\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall q_{1},q_{2}\in\Phi_{\mathrm{ad}}.

Proof.

Let
q_{1},q_{2}\in\Phi_{\mathrm{ad}}

\delta\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{1})-\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{2})
. From (

\left\|\delta\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left\|\delta\mathcal{K}_{1}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|\delta\mathcal{K}_{2}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+2\rho\left\|q_{1}-q_{2}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(154)

where

\displaystyle\left\|\delta\mathcal{K}_{1}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{0}^{T}u_{N}[q_{1}](u_{N}[q_{1}]-u_{N}[q_{2}])\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|\int_{0}^{T}u_{N}[q_{2}](u_{N}[q_{1}]-u_{N}[q_{2}])\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+\left\|\int_{0}^{T}u_{D}[q_{1}](u_{D}[q_{1}]-u_{D}[q_{2}])\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|\int_{0}^{T}u_{D}[q_{2}](u_{D}[q_{1}]-u_{D}[q_{2}])\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+2\left\|\int_{0}^{T}u_{D}[q_{2}](u_{N}[q_{1}]-u_{N}[q_{2}])\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+2\left\|\int_{0}^{T}u_{N}[q_{1}](u_{D}[q_{1}]-u_{D}[q_{2}])\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\left\|\delta\mathcal{K}_{2}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{0}^{T}u_{D}[q_{2}](\xi_{N}[q_{1}]-\xi_{N}[q_{2}])\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|\int_{0}^{T}\xi_{N}[q_{1}](u_{D}[q_{1}]-u_{D}[q_{2}])\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+\left\|\int_{0}^{T}u_{N}[q_{2}](\xi_{D}[q_{1}]-\xi_{D}[q_{2}])\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|\int_{0}^{T}\xi_{D}[q_{1}](u_{N}[q_{1}]-u_{N}[q_{2}])\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

We begin by estimating the first term of
\delta\mathcal{K}_{1}
:

I_{1}=\left\|\int_{0}^{T}u_{N}[q_{1}](u_{N}[q_{1}]-u_{N}[q_{2}])\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

By Minkowski’s integral inequality
[

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{0}^{T}u_{N}[q_{1}](\cdot,t)(u_{N}[q_{1}]

\displaystyle-u_{N}[q_{2}])(\cdot,t)\;\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\Big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

Apply Hölder’s inequality:

\displaystyle\Big\|u_{N}[q_{1}](\cdot,t)(u_{N}[q_{1}]

\displaystyle-u_{N}[q_{2}])(\cdot,t)\Big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

By the Sobolev embedding theorem
[
11

, since

\Omega

. Then, we get

\displaystyle\Big\|u_{N}[q_{1}](\cdot,t)\Big\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\Big\|(u_{N}[q_{1}]

\displaystyle-u_{N}[q_{2}])(\cdot,t)\Big\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}

Combining these estimates:

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{0}^{T}u_{N}[q_{1}](\cdot,t)(u_{N}[q_{1}]

\displaystyle-u_{N}[q_{2}])(\cdot,t)\,\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\Big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\Big\|u_{N}[q_{1}](\cdot,t)\Big\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\Big\|(u_{N}[q_{1}]

\displaystyle-u_{N}[q_{2}])(\cdot,t)\Big\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\Big\|u_{N}[q_{1}]\Big\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\Big\|u_{N}[q_{1}]-u_{N}[q_{2}]\Big\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}.

This implies

I_{1}\leq C\left\|u_{N}[q_{1}]\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\left\|u_{N}[q_{1}]-u_{N}[q_{2}]\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}.

(155)

From Theorem
6
, there exists
C>0

\left\|u_{N}[q_{1}]\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C\left\|\varphi\right\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}.

(156)

By Lemma
11

\left\|u_{N}[q_{1}]-u_{N}[q_{2}]\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C\left\|q_{1}-q_{2}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(157)

By substituting equations (

) and (
157

I_{1}\leq C\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(158)

The remaining terms in
\delta\mathcal{K}_{1}

,

(159)

We now estimate the term
\left\|\delta\mathcal{K}_{2}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

Firstly, we proceed to estimate

Applying the change of variable

\displaystyle\eta\longmapsto\overline{J}(\eta(\cdot,t))=

\left\|\overline{\xi}_{N}[q_{1}]\right\|_{B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(Q_{T})}\leq C\left\|u_{N}[q_{1}]-u_{D}[q_{1}]\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))},

Using the bounds from (

\left\|\xi_{N}[q_{1}]\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C\left(\left\|\phi\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}+\left\|\varphi\right\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}\right).

\left\|\xi_{D}[q_{1}]\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C\left(\left\|\phi\right\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}+\left\|\varphi\right\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{4}}(\Sigma_{T})}\right).

satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\partial^{\alpha}_{t}\delta\overline{\xi}_{D}-\Delta\delta\overline{\xi}_{D}+q_{2}\;\delta\overline{\xi}_{D}&=&(q_{2}-q_{1})\overline{\xi}_{D}[q_{2}]+2\Big(J_{ND}[q_{1}]-J_{ND}[q_{2}]\Big)&\hbox{in}\ Q_{T},\\

where the linear mapping
\eta\longmapsto J_{ND}[q_{i}](\eta)=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q_{i}]-u_{D}[q_{i}]\Big)\cdot\nabla\eta\,\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}q_{i}\Big(u_{N}[q_{i}]-u_{D}[q_{i}]\Big)\eta\,\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x

By reiterating the same argument employed in the proof of Lemma

\|\delta\overline{\xi}_{D}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\|\xi_{D}[q_{1}]-\xi_{D}[q_{2}]\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(166)

Similarly,

\|\xi_{N}[q_{1}]-\xi_{N}[q_{2}]\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Now consider the first term in
\delta\mathcal{K}_{2}
:

M_{1}=\left\|\int_{0}^{T}u_{D}[q_{2}](\xi_{N}[q_{1}]-\xi_{N}[q_{2}])\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Using the boundedness of
u_{D}[q_{2}]
in

established in (

M_{1}\leq C\left\|q_{1}-q_{2}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

The remaining terms in
\delta\mathcal{K}_{2}

\xi_{N}
,
\xi_{D}
,
u_{N}
and
u_{D}
with respect to

and the boundedness of
u_{N}[q_{2}]
,
u_{D}[q_{2}]
,
\xi_{N}[q_{1}]
, and
\xi_{D}[q_{1}]
. Hence,

\left\|\delta\mathcal{K}_{2}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|q_{1}-q_{2}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(167)

Substituting (

) and (

\left\|\delta\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq(C+2\rho)\left\|q_{1}-q_{2}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

Next, to solve the optimization problem (

), we employ a CGM.

7.3.
Conjugate gradient method

In this section, we introduce the CGM as the numerical approach for solving the optimization problem under consideration. The following iterative process based on the CGM is used for the estimation of
q(x)
by
minimizing the Kohn-Vogelius functional
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}:

\displaystyle q_{n+1}(x)=q_{n}(x)-\beta_{n}P_{n}(x),\;\;n=0,\,1,\,2,\cdots

(168)

where:

-

the subscript
n
denotes the number of iterations;

-

the function
q_{0}(x)
is the initial guess for
q(x)
;

-

\beta_{n}
is the step search size in passing from iteration
n+1
;

-

the function
P_{n}(x)
is the direction of descent given by

P_{0}(x)=\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{0}(x)),\;\;P_{n}(x)=\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n}(x))+\gamma_{n}P_{n-1}(x),\;\;n=1,\,2,\cdots\,.

(169)

Various expressions are available for the
conjugate coefficient
\gamma_{n}
, such as in the Fletcher–Reeves method (see, for example,
[
21
,
66
]
):

\gamma_{0}=0,\;\;\gamma_{n}=\frac{\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n-1})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}},\;\;n=1,\,2,\cdots\,.

(170)

The step size
\beta_{n}
is determined by the exact line search method:

\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n+1})=\min_{\beta\geq 0}\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n}-\beta P_{n}).

(171)

This implies the first-order optimality condition for the function
\beta\longmapsto\psi(\beta):=\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n}-\beta P_{n})
at
\beta=\beta_{n}
. By the chain rule and the Fréchet differentiability of
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
(Theorem
27
), we have:

\psi^{\prime}(\beta_{n})=-\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n+1})P_{n}\;\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x=0.

Thus, the exact line search yields the orthogonality condition:

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n+1})P_{n}\;\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x=0.

(172)

7.4.
Convergence of the iterative process

In this subsection, inspired by the arguments in
[
20
]
, we discuss the convergence of the above iterative process (
168
)-(
170
).

Theorem 29
.

Let
\{q_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}\subset\Phi_{ad}
be generated by (
168
) with
\beta_{n}
satisfying (
171
). Then
\{\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n})\}_{n\geq 0}
is monotonically decreasing and convergent.

Proof.

From (
172
) and the definition of
P_{n}
in (
169
), we obtain for all
n\geq 0
:

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{\rho}(q_{n})P_{n}\;\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x=-\|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{\rho}(q_{n})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

By (
171
), for any
\beta\geq 0
:

\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n+1})\leq\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n}-\beta P_{n}).

Using the mean value theorem and Lipschitz continuity (Theorem
28
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n})-\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n}-\beta P_{n})

\displaystyle=-\beta\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n}-\theta\beta P_{n})P_{n}\;\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=-\beta\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})P_{n}\;\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x-\beta\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n}-\theta\beta P_{n})-\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\Big)P_{n}\;\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\geq\beta\|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{\rho}(q_{n})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\beta\|P_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\int_{0}^{1}\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n}-\theta\beta P_{n})-\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\geq\beta\|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{\rho}(q_{n})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}C_{L}\|P_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

The maximum decrease occurs at

\beta^{*}=\frac{\|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{\rho}(q_{n})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{C_{L}\|P_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}},

yielding

\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n})-\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n}-\beta^{*}P_{n})\geq\frac{\|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{\rho}(q_{n})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{2C_{L}\|P_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}.

Thus
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n+1})\leq\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n})
for all
n\geq 0
, and since
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
is bounded below, the sequence converges.
∎

Theorem 30
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
29
, the CGM (
168
)-(
170
) either terminates at a stationary point or converges in the following sense

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf\|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{\rho}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0.

Proof.

First, by exploiting the Lipschitz continuity of the gradient
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}(q)

and following the arguments in
[
20
,
66
]
,
we obtain that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{4}}{\|P_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}<\infty.

(173)

Next, we show by contradiction that

\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Suppose, on the contrary, that this is not the case. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}>0.

Hence, there exist a constant
\varepsilon>0
and an index
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that,
for all
n\geq N
,

\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\geq\varepsilon.

(174)

Taking the inner product with
P_{n}
and using the orthogonality
\langle\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n}),P_{n-1}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0
(which follows from (
172
) and the update rule), we obtain:

\|P_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\langle\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n}),P_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\gamma_{n}\langle P_{n-1},P_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2}\|P_{n-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

where the last equality uses the fact that
\langle P_{n-1},P_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\gamma_{n}\|P_{n-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
(which can be shown by induction). Substituting the definition of
\gamma_{n}
from (
170
) yields:

\|P_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{4}}{\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n-1})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{4}}\|P_{n-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(175)

Divide both sides of (
175
) by
\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|^{4}
:

\frac{\|P_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{4}}=\frac{1}{\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}+\frac{\|P_{n-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n-1})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{4}}.

Define
a_{n}=\frac{\|P_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{4}}
. Then the above becomes:

a_{n}=a_{n-1}+\frac{1}{\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}.

This is a telescoping recurrence. Applying it iteratively for
n\geq N
gives:

a_{n}=a_{N}+\sum_{k=N+1}^{n}\frac{1}{\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{k})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}.

(176)

Using the lower bound (
174
), we have for all
k\geq N+1
:

\frac{1}{\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{k})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

Substituting into (
176
) yields:

a_{n}\geq a_{N}+\frac{n-N}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

Recalling the definition of
a_{n}
, this implies:

\|P_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\geq\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{4}\left(a_{N}+\frac{n-N}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

(177)

Since the sequence
\{q_{n}\}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
(as it lies in
\Phi_{ad}
) and
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q)^{\prime}
is Lipschitz continuous, there exists a constant
M>0
such that for all
n
,

\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq M.

(178)

This follows because the image of a bounded set under a Lipschitz continuous map is bounded.

From (
177
) and (
178
), we have:

\|P_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\geq\varepsilon^{4}\left(a_{N}+\frac{n-N}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)=\varepsilon^{4}a_{N}+\varepsilon^{2}(n-N).

Therefore,

\frac{1}{\|P_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}a_{N}+\varepsilon^{2}(n-N)}.

Now, consider the terms in the series (
173
) for
n\geq N
:

\frac{\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{4}}{\|P_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{\|P_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{\varepsilon^{4}a_{N}+\varepsilon^{2}(n-N)}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}a_{N}+(n-N)}.

Summing these terms from
n=N
to
\infty
gives:

\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|^{4}}{\|P_{n}\|^{2}}\geq\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}a_{N}+(n-N)}.

The series on the right-hand side is a harmonic series, which diverges. This contradicts the convergence of the series in (
173
).
The contradiction implies that our initial assumption (
174
) is false. Therefore,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0,

which completes the proof.
∎

7.5.
Iterative algorithm

According to the above discussion, all the parameters of the iterative process (
168
) are explicitly defined, except for the search step size, denoted by
\beta_{n}
. The step size
\beta_{n}
can be determined through the following deduction.
Initially, setting
\delta q_{n}=P_{n}
, we can apply Lemma
13
to linearize the estimated Neumann solution
u_{N}[q_{n+1}]=u_{N}[q_{n}-\beta_{n}P_{n}]
using a Taylor series expression in the form:

\displaystyle u_{N}[q_{n}-\beta_{n}P_{n}]\approx u_{N}[q_{n}]-\beta_{n}\mathcal{U}_{N}[q_{n}](P_{n}).

(179)

Likewise, by utilizing Lemma
12
, we obtain a similar linearization for the Dirichlet solution
u_{D}[q_{n}-\beta_{n}P_{n}]
:

\displaystyle u_{D}[q_{n}-\beta_{n}P_{n}]\approx u_{D}[q_{n}]-\beta_{n}\mathcal{U}_{D}[q_{n}](P_{n}).

(180)

In these equations (
179
) and (
180
),
\mathcal{U}_{N}[q_{n}](P_{n})
and
\mathcal{U}_{D}[q_{n}](P_{n})
solve respectively problems (
13
) and (
12
) with
\delta q_{n}=P_{n}
and
q=q_{n}
. Therefore, with a straightforward calculation, we can derive:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n}-\beta_{n}P_{n})

\displaystyle\approx\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q_{n}]-u_{D}[q_{n}]-\beta_{n}\Big(\mathcal{U}_{N}[q_{n}](P_{n})-\mathcal{U}_{D}[q_{n}](P_{n})\Big)\Big)\Big|^{2}\ \displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q_{n}\Big|u_{N}[q_{n}]-u_{D}[q_{n}]-\beta_{n}\Big(\mathcal{U}_{N}[q_{n}](P_{n})-\mathcal{U}_{D}[q_{n}](P_{n})\Big)\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad-\beta_{n}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}P_{n}\Big|u_{N}[q_{n}]-u_{D}[q_{n}]-\beta_{n}\Big(\mathcal{U}_{N}[q_{n}](P_{n})-\mathcal{U}_{D}[q_{n}](P_{n})\Big)\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\rho\int_{\Omega}\Big|q_{n}-\beta_{n}P_{n}\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x.

As a result, the determination of the step size
\beta_{n}
involves minimizing the functional

\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n+1})=\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n}-\beta_{n}P_{n}).

To achieve this, we set up the following condition to find
\beta_{n}
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}_{\rho}}{\partial\beta_{n}}(q_{n}-\beta_{n}P_{n})=0,

which leads to the quadratic equation (with respect to the size
\beta_{n}
),

-3A_{n}\beta_{n}^{2}+2B_{n}\beta_{n}-C_{n}=0.

(181)

Here, the coefficients
A_{n},

B_{n},
and
C_{n}
are defined as follows

\displaystyle A_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}P_{n}\Big|\mathcal{U}_{N}[q_{n}](P_{n})-\mathcal{U}_{D}[q_{n}](P_{n})\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\nabla\Big(\mathcal{U}_{N}[q_{n}](P_{n})-\mathcal{U}_{D}[q_{n}](P_{n})\Big)\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}q_{n}\Big|\mathcal{U}_{N}[q_{n}](P_{n})-\mathcal{U}_{D}[q_{n}](P_{n})\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}P_{n}\Big(u_{N}[q_{n}]-u_{D}[q_{n}]\Big)\Big(\mathcal{U}_{N}[q_{n}](P_{n})-\mathcal{U}_{D}[q_{n}](P_{n})\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t-\rho\int_{\Omega}|P_{n}|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle C_{n}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\Big(u_{N}[q_{n}]-u_{D}[q_{n}]\Big)\cdot\nabla\big(\mathcal{U}_{N}[q_{n}](P_{n})-\mathcal{U}_{D}[q_{n}](P_{n})\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}P_{n}\Big(u_{N}[q_{n}]-u_{D}[q_{n}]\Big)\Big(\mathcal{U}_{N}[q_{n}](P_{n})-\mathcal{U}_{D}[q_{n}](P_{n})\Big)\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}P_{n}\Big|u_{N}[q_{n}]-u_{D}[q_{n}]\Big|^{2}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t-2\rho\int_{\Omega}P_{n}\;q_{n}\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x.

The discriminant
\Delta_{n}
of the quadratic equation (
181
) is given by

\Delta_{n}:=4(B_{n}^{2}-3A_{n}\,C_{n}).

Hence,

•

If
\Delta_{n}=0
, then

\beta_{n}=\frac{-B_{n}}{2A_{n}}.

•

If
\Delta_{n}>0
, there are two candidate step sizes:

\beta_{n}^{1}=\frac{-B_{n}-\sqrt{\Delta_{n}}}{2A_{n}}\;\;\;\hbox{and}\;\;\beta_{n}^{2}=\frac{-B_{n}+\sqrt{\Delta_{n}}}{2A_{n}}.

-

If
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n}-\beta_{n}^{1}P_{n})\leq\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n}-\beta_{n}^{2}P_{n}),
then

\beta_{n}=\beta_{n}^{1}.

-

Otherwise,

\beta_{n}=\beta_{n}^{2}.

•

If
\Delta_{n}<0
, the quadratic equation (
181
) has no real solution, and a direct minimizer cannot be obtained.
In this case, a standard approach is to employ a line-search technique, such as the Armijo condition
[
7
]
,
which ensures a sufficient decrease of the functional. More precisely, in the Armijo condition, the step size
\beta_{n}
is not determined from an explicit formula (as in the case
\Delta_{n}\geq 0
),
but is chosen iteratively so that the sufficient decrease condition

\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n}-\beta_{n}P_{n})\;\leq\;\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(q_{n})-\sigma\beta_{n}\,\langle\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\prime}(q_{n}),P_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

is satisfied, where
0<\sigma<1
is a small parameter (typically
\sigma\sim 10^{-4}
–
10^{-3}
).
This procedure guarantees both a decrease in the functional and stability of the iterative scheme.

8.1.2.
Effect of the regularization parameter

Finding an appropriate value for the regularization parameter
\rho
is a delicate yet crucial task, since it directly impacts the accuracy and stability of the reconstruction. If
\rho
is chosen too small, the solution may overfit the noisy data and become unstable, whereas excessively large values of
\rho
may oversmooth the solution, leading to a significant loss of important features. A standard practical approach to address this difficulty is to examine the reconstruction error for a range of
\rho
values and identify the one that yields the best balance between stability and accuracy. In the literature, various systematic strategies have been proposed for parameter selection. For instance, in
[
63
]
, a bi-level optimization framework was introduced, which provides an efficient and theoretically justified method for tuning the regularization parameter in regularized control problems. Meanwhile,
[
22
]
proposed the so-called Morozov discrepancy principle, where the parameter is chosen by enforcing a discrepancy measure between the data and the model prediction to match the noise level. In contrast, in our study, the parameter
\rho
was chosen empirically through a trial-and-error process guided by numerical experiments. Figure
4
illustrates this selection procedure for Example
2
, where the target potential is
q^{*}(x)=e^{-2x}\cos(2\pi x)
. Reconstructions obtained with different values of
\rho
are compared. The results clearly indicate that
\rho=10^{-4}
provides the most accurate approximation, offering a good compromise between suppressing noise and preserving the essential features of the exact solution.

9.
Further comments and conclusions

This paper has presented a comprehensive theoretical and numerical framework for solving the ill-posed inverse problem of reconstructing a space-dependent potential in subdiffusion from boundary measurements. The method is grounded in a Kohn-Vogelius optimization approach, regularized with a Tikhonov term, which reformulates the inverse problem by minimizing a cost functional that measures the energetic discrepancy between the solutions of Dirichlet and Neumann boundary value problems.

Our main contributions are threefold:

•

Theoretical analysis:
We established the well-posedness of the resulting optimization problem, proving the existence of a unique minimizer within a set of admissible potentials and demonstrating its stability with respect to perturbations in the measurement data. This provides a solid mathematical foundation for the approach.

•

Algorithm development:
We derived the Fréchet derivative of the Kohn-Vogelius cost functional and proved its Lipschitz continuity, enabling the design of an efficient conjugate gradient algorithm with a rigorous convergence guarantee. A distinctive numerical feature of our approach is the effective handling of the non-trivial quadratic equation for step size selection. It should be noted that the convergence analysis for the fully discrete problem, employing finite element approximations, remains a subject for future investigation.

•

Numerical validation:
The efficacy and robustness of the proposed algorithm were confirmed through extensive numerical experiments in both one- and two-dimensional cases. The method performs well in reconstructing both smooth and discontinuous potentials, even in the presence of substantial noise. These results demonstrate the stability of the approach and its ability to produce reliable reconstructions across diverse scenarios.

For future work, advanced optimization strategies could be employed, such as the topological derivative method
[
61
]
, which offers a powerful framework for precise reconstruction of subdifferential functions. Furthermore, integrating total variation regularization
[
18
]
would be highly beneficial for handling discontinuous or piecewise constant solutions, a common scenario in practical applications. Implementing these improvements would not only enhance reconstruction accuracy for non-smooth problems but would also greatly expand the scope and utility of the proposed framework.
Besides, future works could also focus on extensive numerical experiments in higher dimensions (e.g. three dimensions) and complex geometries (for example, when the support of the potential has complex geometries), a deeper analysis of stability under various noise models, and a comparison with the recently proposed Coupled Complex Boundary Method
[
19
]
, which transforms the inverse problem into a challenging boundary problem, where a complex Robin condition links Dirichlet and Neumann data.
