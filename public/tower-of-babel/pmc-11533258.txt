Development and validation of an early acute kidney injury risk prediction model for patients with sepsis in emergency departments
Authors: Chen Lin, Siming Lin, Meng Zheng, Kexin Cai, Jing Wang, Yuqing Luo, Zhihong Lin, Shaodan Feng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we aimed to develop and validate a nomogram to predicting the risk of sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) in patients admitted to emergency departments (EDs). We randomly divided a retrospective dataset of 391 patients with sepsis into a 294-person training cohort and a 97-person validation cohort, and developed three predictive models using multivariate logistic regression analysis and clinical insight. No difference was observed between the three models using the DeLong test and Model 3 was selected as the risk prediction model based on the principle of least inclusion indicators. The use of vasopressor drugs, patient age, platelet count, procalcitonin, and D-dimer levels were included. The training and validation cohorts had a consistency index of 0.832 and 0.866, respectively, indicating high accuracy and stability in predicting SA-AKI risk. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.832, showing excellent discrimination. The calibration curves for the training and validation cohorts showed excellent calibration. The decision curve and clinical impact curve analyses showed that the net clinical benefit of using the nomogram was greatest over a probability threshold of 0.05–0.90. In addition, the model showed moderate validity in predicting the 30-day survival and the incidence of major adverse renal events within 30 days. The nomogram developed for SA-AKI risk assessment in patients in EDs showed good discriminability and clinical utility. It can provide a theoretical basis for emergency physicians to prevent SA-AKI.

Introduction

Sepsis is a predominant diagnosis and a leading cause of mortality within emergency departments (EDs) and intensive care units (ICUs) [1,2]. Sepsis precipitates multiorgan dysfunction, markedly extends hospital stays, increases healthcare expenditures, and is correlated with adverse prognoses [3]. The kidneys are particularly vulnerable to sepsis because it can induce severe organ damage and profoundly affect the long-term prognosis of patients [4].

A recent cross-sectional survey [5] spanning 158 hospitals in Beijing found that the prevalence of sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) is approximately 48.1%. The rate of occurrence was significantly higher among patients in ICUs compared with those treated outside the ICU (59.2 vs. 31.6%). Regarding overall mortality, the rate of occurrence was as high as 55% among patients with SA-AKI. Notably, 72% of AKI cases occur in non-ICU settings [6]. Furthermore, a multicenter retrospective analysis [7] encompassing 1,243 patients with septic shock identified a 47.1% AKI prevalence rate, with 50.4% of these individuals receiving AKI treatment in the ED. This discovery underscores the criticality of prompt SA-AKI identification in such settings to facilitate timely interventions. Expedited treatment is pivotal to forestalling further renal function decline, enhance patient outcomes, and alleviate healthcare burdens.

However, in the current clinical scenario, the early identification of SA-AKI faces several serious challenges, including how to determine the exact time of sepsis and AKI onset accurately. Therefore, a large number of biomarkers have emerged for the diagnosing AKI [8] such as proenkephalin (PENK), urinary interleukin 18 (IL-18), urinary kidney injury molecule 1 (KIM-1), neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipofuscin (NGAL), and urinary tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase-2 (TIMP-2). The use of insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 7 (IGFBP7) is still controversial [9,10]. Previous studies [11,12] have shown that NephroCheck is a TIMP2/IGFBP7-based bedside test for early prediction of AKI, and its performance has been validated in multiple studies. Meanwhile, continuous monitoring is essential to predict severe AKI (Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes [KDIGO] stage 2 or stage 3) development. However, a meta-analysis found the opposite [13], with NGAL-containing biomarkers having the best predictive accuracy for AKI occurrence. Meanwhile, these novel markers cannot predict the likelihood of AKI in patients with sepsis in the ED, and the high cost and complex technical requirements limit their value in emergency medicine prediction. Therefore, these new markers cannot completely replace creatinine (Cr) in the early diagnosis of AKI. In addition, because of the urgency of treating sepsis and septic shock, healthcare teams often need to rapidly implement bundled treatment regimens, which creates challenges in the early prediction of kidney injury. For example, aggressive fluid resuscitation may mask a decrease in urine output, thereby delaying AKI diagnosis, while using diuretics may further complicate AKI diagnosis. These therapeutic interventions and the emergency setting further delay the timely recognition of SA-AKI.

Constructing nomograms in the ED with conventional indicators is essential to enhance sepsis management, mitigate the incidence of AKI in patients admitted to ED, and provide highly sensitive risk estimates. In this study, we aimed to develop and validate a risk prediction model using commonly used indicators in the ED to identify the risk of SA-AKI. This can provide a theoretical basis for emergency physicians to prevent SA-AKI.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This study was conducted at the First Hospital Affiliated to Fujian Medical University from 1 January 2019, to 31 December 2022. We extracted anonymized patient data from the institution’s electronic health record system. Our study included patients (age ≥ 18 years) with sepsis admitted to the ED and hospitalized in the ED for at least 24h. The diagnosis of sepsis and septic shock was based on the Third International Consensus Definition of Sepsis and Septic Shock [4]. Throughout the hospitalization period, two attending physicians assessed the occurrence of SA-AKI after admission every 24h according to the electronic medical record system. AKI diagnosis adhered to the KDIGO guidelines [14]. We used the blood Cr level as the only criterion for determination because there were no reliable urine output data within 24 h in patients admitted to ED. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) cases without continuous Cr value monitoring information after admission; (2) admission time <24 h; (3) diagnosis of end-stage renal disease (ESKD) before or at the time of admission or undergoing hemodialysis; (4) recent use of nephrotoxic medications; (5) AKI caused by factors other than sepsis (including coronavirus disease-19, cardiac surgery) (6) currently undergoing antiplatelet drugs or anticoagulant therapy; (7) presence of autoimmune disease; (8) cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma; (9) hematologic disorders. Pursuant to the established inclusion and exclusion criteria, from 553 patients with sepsis, we included 391 patients in this study (Figure 1).

The ethics committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University approved this study’s design and protocol (Approval No.: Min Medical University Attached Ethics Medical Research [2022] 244). The retrospective for informed consent was exempted because of the retrospective nature of this study.

Diagnostic criteria

We defined AKI based on the following KDIGO criteria: (1) an increase in the Cr concentration by ≥0.3 mg/dL (≥26.5 µmol/L) within 48 h; (2) an increase in the Cr concentration to ≥ 1.5 times the baseline level after kidney injury known or presumed to have occurred within the previous 7 d; or (3) a urine volume <0.5 mL/kg/h over a 6 h. Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) was diagnosed based on the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis criteria. The scoring was based on the following parameters: (1) platelet count (PLT), (2) D-dimer or fibrin degradation product concentration, (3) prothrombin time (PT), and (4) fibrin level. A score of ≥5 indicated overt DIC [15]. Multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) was defined as an acute but potentially reversible dysfunction of two or more organ systems [16]. Major adverse kidney events by 30 days (MAKE30) is a composite outcome comprising death, newly initiated renal replacement therapy (RRT), or a diagnosis of primary renal disease (defined as a final inpatient serum Cr concentration ≥200% of the baseline concentration), censored on hospital discharge or 30 days after inclusion in the study, whichever occurred first [17,18]. The baseline value was the first Cr level measured during the exposure period. If any Cr measurements were missing during this period, the most recent Cr level determined within the 1 year before admission was selected. In approximately seven patients, where either of these methods could not determine baseline Cr level, baseline Cr was estimated in women and men using this formula [19]: In women: [Cr] (μmol/L)=88.4 × (0.74 − 0.2 + 0.003 × age) In men: [Cr](μmol/L)=88.4 × (0.74+0.003 × age).

Relevant variables and study endpoints

In this investigation, the analyzed variables encompassed the following categories: (1) demographic and initial presentation data such as age, sex, MAKE30 outcomes, and admission scores; (2) factors pertaining to clinical incidents, including instances of septic shock, MODS, and DIC; (3) criteria for assessing patient status at admission, including Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) scores, and the Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II); (4) considerations of preexisting health conditions-namely, hypertension, diabetes, malignancies, and cardiovascular and respiratory diseases; (5) therapeutic interventions, applied, such as RRT, antibiotics, vasopressor or sedative medications, and mechanical ventilation; and (6) laboratory data measured within 24 h of admission; including white blood cell and neutrophil counts PLT; levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, urea, Cr, N-terminal brain natriuretic peptide-precursor, D-dimer, and fibrinogen; the PT, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), thrombin time; and the international normalized ratio (INR). The earliest data set was prioritized for patients having multiple measurements for a given parameter within 24 h of admission.

The primary outcome of interest focused on the incidence of SA-AKI, while secondary outcomes encompass mortality within 30 d and MAKE30 events.

Statistical analysis

In this study, case allocation was performed by randomized stratified sampling. First, all cases were stratified according to key clinical characteristics (age, sex, and occurrence of kidney injury). Then, within each stratum, cases were proportionally assigned to the training and validation cohorts by random number generation method. Ultimately, 75% of the patients were assigned to the training cohort for the predictive model development, and the remaining 25% were assigned to the validation cohort for evaluating the model’s efficacy.

Normality assessments were performed for all continuous variables. Data that conformed to a normal distribution are depicted as the mean ± standard deviation, while those not adhering to normal distribution are displayed as the median and the interquartile range. Counts are denoted as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). We utilized either one-way analysis of variance or t-tests to compare normally distributed continuous variables between groups. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for continuous variables lacking normal distribution between groups. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

In the univariate analysis, continuous variables were analyzed either through the Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test, depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test based on their appropriateness. In addition, we performed an age-adjusted multivariate logistic regression analysis employing the forward logistic regression method to pinpoint the independent predictors of SA-AKI.

To enhance the clinical utility of the predictive models, we constructed three logistic regression models based on the independent predictors discovered in the training cohort. This development employed three approaches: the registration method, the minimum Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) method, and the AIC in conjunction with the Clinical Practice Rule. Distinctions among these models were established through the DeLong test. In instances where the differences were insignificant, emphasis was placed on the simplest model for a more focused clinical applicability assessment, and then further evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test to confirm model fit [20]. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to assess the SA-AKI prediction model’s performance within the training and validation groups. Optimal thresholds in the ROC analysis were determined using the Youden index. We evaluated the models’ discriminative power and calibration in the training and validation cohorts using the concordance index (C-index) and calibration curves. Moreover, the practical relevance of the SA-AKI nomograms was examined through decision curve analysis (DCA) and clinical impact curve (CIC) analysis [21].

In this dataset, <5% of the covariates exhibited missing entries. Given the low proportion of missing data, no imputations were deemed necessary; therefore, these data were simply excluded. All further analyses utilized R software version 4.2.2 (http://www.r-project.org/) and Statistical Package for Social Sciences Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA), with p-values less than 0.05 denoted as statistically significant.

Table 2 compares the demographic and clinical data between patients with and without SA-AKI in the training (n = 294) and validation (n = 97) cohort. The patients with sepsis who developed AKI required significantly more clinical interventions than did patients who did not. The interventions required included sedative medications (training cohort: 41.9 vs. 17.5% and validation cohort: 47.1 vs. 19.0%, respectively), mechanical ventilation (training cohort: 43 vs. 35% and validation cohort: 19 vs. 11%, respectively), and vasoactive medications (training cohort: 68.6 vs. 26.5% and validation cohort: 76.5 vs. 28.6%, respectively). Patients with SA-AKI had a greater prevalence of concurrent hypertension (training cohort: 57.1 vs. 40.2% and validation cohort: 58.8 vs. 39.7%). In addition, patients with sepsis faced an elevated risk of AKI in case involving septic shock (training cohort: 70.5 vs. 34.4% and validation cohort: 79.4 vs. 19.0%, respectively) and MODS (training cohort: 61.0 vs. 13.8% and validation cohort: 35.3 vs. 11.1%, respectively). Compared with the laboratory indices values of patients without SA-AKI, patients with SA-AKI exhibited higher procalcitonin (PCT), urea, Cr, cystatin C (CysC), uric acid (UA), lactate (Lac), and D-dimer levels; greater PT, APTT, and INR values; and a lower PLT (p < 0.001).

Based on the notable independent predictors discerned through univariate and multivariate regression analyses, three logistic regression models were devised to forecast SA-AKI incidence within the training cohort (Table 6). The predictive Model 2 was constructed by incorporating the statistically significant factors in the multifactorial regression analysis (age, vasopressor drug use, hypertension; and PCT, PLT, and D-dimer levels). Considering the wide age distribution of patients admitted to the ED and the nonuniversality of patients with comorbid hypertension, we excluded hypertension from Model 3, which incorporated age, vasopressor drug use, and PCT, PLT, and D-dimer levels as the SA-AKI predictors.

Association of the nomogram with 30-day mortality and MAKE30 outcomes

The MAKE30 composite endpoint occurred in 154 out of 391 patients (39.4%). Among the individual components, 127 patients (32.5%) experienced 30-day mortality, 64 patients (16.4%) required RRT, and 27 patients (7.1%) had persistent renal insufficiency. ROC curve analysis demonstrated that the predictive model had moderate ability to predict 30-day mortality (AUC = 0.706) and MAKE30 outcomes (AUC = 0.757) in sepsis patients admitted to the ED. The model showed comparable predictive power to SOFA and APACHEII scores for 30-day mortality prediction in both the training set (p = 0.932 vs. SOFA, p = 0.446 vs. APACHEII) and the validation set (p = 0.656 vs. SOFA, p = 0.214 vs. APACHEII). Similarly, it exhibited comparable predictive performance for MAKE30 outcomes in both the training set (p = 0.941 vs. SOFA, p = 0.127 vs. APACHEII) and validation set (p = 0.767 vs. SOFA, p = 0.315 vs. APACHEII) (Figure 7). Discussion

This study found a 35.5% prevalence of AKI in patients with sepsis, consistent with previous research on the epidemiology of SA-AKI [5]. Additionally, results from a national survey on AKI showed that 72% of AKI cases occurred in patients not admitted to the ICU, highlighting the need for increased surveillance and timely intervention for AKI cases outside the ICU [6].

While most research has focused on ICU settings [22–24], early identification of SA-AKI in the ED remains crucial yet understudied. To address this gap, we developed and validated a nomogram incorporating five easily accessible clinical parameters—age, vasopressor use, PLT, PCT, and D-dimer levels—within the first 24 h of ED admission. Although factors such as reduced urine output, elevated SAPS II, and dysnatremia are recognized contributors to AKI risk, they were not fully integrated into our model due to challenges with accurate urine collection in acute care and their delayed manifestation in early sepsis [25,26]. Urine volume typically reflects late-stage renal injury, while SAPS II, although useful for assessing overall disease severity, may not capture subtle changes of early renal dysfunction. Despite the exclusion of these variables, our nomogram, based on age, PCT, PLT, and D-dimer levels, demonstrated superior predictive power for AKI, significantly outperforming traditional markers such as serum creatinine, as well as the SOFA, APACHE II, and SAPS II scores. The model is straightforward to use, highly applicable in the ED setting, and offers a valuable tool for early risk stratification and timely intervention.

Traditional theories of SA-AKI pathogenesis suggest that its development is driven by excessive inflammation, altered intrarenal hemodynamics, dysregulated coagulation, microvascular endothelial dysfunction, and injury to renal tubular epithelial cells. However, the precise mechanisms remain unclear [15]. Identifying biomarkers indicating inflammatory activation is crucial for predicting SA-AKI. While some evidence supports PCT as a marker for AKI onset and prognosis, its reliability as an early predictor of SA-AKI remains debated [27,28]. In this study, PCT was identified as an independent predictor of SA-AKI, possibly due to its cytotoxic properties and involvement in inducing apoptosis in adherent cells, contributing to disease progression [29]. Additionally, elevated APTT, PT, and D-dimer levels, alongside reduced PLT levels in the SA-AKI group, align with previous findings [30–34]. These findings emphasize the key role of PCT, PLT, and D-dimer in SA-AKI pathogenesis. The model based on these biomarkers is consistent with AKI mechanisms and demonstrates strong predictive value.

Taking into account the effects of age-related renal decline, preexisting conditions such as hypertension, and individual treatment modalities on baseline renal function, we included age and hypertension as adjusting factors in our prediction model. This adjustment mitigates the confounding effects of baseline renal function variability across patients, thereby enhancing the model’s accuracy and applicability. In previous studies [25], hyponatremia was considered an important factor in the development of SA-AKI. This may be because hyponatremia affects and aggravates renal injury by causing hemodynamically unstable renal perfusion. However, in our cohort, we did not observe a significant correlation between hyponatremia and SA-AKI, which may be due to the exclusion of patients with chronic kidney disease and the relatively small proportion of patients with severe uremia in our sample.

In sepsis, AKI is induced by the interaction of multiple factors. Given this complexity, it is challenging predict the risk of SA-AKI in sepsis patients using only a single clinical indicator. Therefore, this study included the patients’ treatment modalities after admission in the prediction model. In this study, 27.6 and 42.5% of sepsis patients were on mechanical ventilation and vasopressors within 24 h of admission, respectively. Patients requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation and vasopressor support typically exhibit deterioration in microcirculatory perfusion, further exacerbating renal injury. This highlights the complex relationship between therapeutic modalities and AKI risk. The current Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines recommend a new 1-h bundle of therapies that includes using of vasopressor medications to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg when patients become hypotensive during or after fluid resuscitation [35]. Different vasopressor drugs did not show a significant difference in renal prognosis in this study, but maintaining MAP above this level is a common goal to ensure adequate organ perfusion pressure [36]. This observation is consistent with our findings that the inability to maintain mean arterial pressure (MAP) above the 65 mmHg threshold significantly increases the risk of AKI in septic patients. However, aggressive fluid resuscitation may mask early indicators of renal insufficiency, delaying diagnosis and adversely affecting renal prognosis. Additionally, our findings suggest that patients treated with vancomycin and polymyxin are at a significantly higher risk of developing AKI, consistent with the recognized nephrotoxicity of these drugs. Vancomycin can accumulate in renal proximal tubular cells, leading to acute tubular necrosis, while polymyxins can cause direct tubular toxicity. These associations emphasize the importance of close monitoring of renal function in patients treated with these antibiotics, especially in the intensive care setting. However, other antibiotic classes, such as fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, cephalosporins, and carbapenems were not significantly associated with AKI in our cohort. This suggests that, although nephrotoxicity is a concern, the effect of specific drugs on the AKI risk may vary, possibly due to differences in dose, treatment duration, and patient susceptibility. As most patients in our study were admitted general wards, they may have had less exposure to nephrotoxic drugs or received less intensive monitoring, and the true impact of these drugs may not have been fully realized. Therefore, future studies incorporating fluid resuscitation volume over 24 h, exposure to nephrotoxic drugs, and treatment modalities duration will help in SA-AKI risk prediction.

Despite the accuracy of the nomogram constructed in this study in predicting AKI risk in sepsis patients, the role of novel biomarkers in the early diagnosis of AKI should be considered [37]. Different injury sites are associated with different injury markers. NGAL, as a novel marker for diagnosing AKI, is available from three main sources: serum, plasma and urine. In a meta-analysis of NGAL [38], urinary NGAL had the highest accuracy, with an AUC of 0.92, a sensitivity of 0.87, and a specificity of 0.84. The specificity was higher than that of the prediction model in the present study but the accuracy and sensitivity were similar [38]. NephroCheck [39], which detects TIMP-2 and IGFBP7, was recently approved for predicting moderate-to-severe AKI within 12 h in high-risk patients [40] and can also serve as a prognostic marker for kidney disease and the necessity for RRT. However, some studies have noted that NephroCheck is not yet commercially available outside of the United States. It is currently difficult to extend NephroCheck to patients from general wards with relatively mild renal injury [11]. In contrast, elevated urinary TIMP2 and IGFBP7 may be associated with other organ damage or physiological states. Since sepsis is a multi-organ damage syndrome, their accuracy as SA-AKI-specific biomarkers is affected [41,42]. Applying novel markers in clinical intervention has limitations; most of these tests are performed after the diagnosis of AKI and are challenging in emergency rooms and primary care hospitals due to their cost and operational complexity. Therefore, compared with these novel clinical markers, the model constructed in this study has the advantages of simplicity, ease of application within 24 h of admission, and good sensitivity and specificity for AKI of any injury level.

Given the nomogram’s demonstrated efficacy in forecasting SA-AKI, this study further explored its utility in assessing the imminent risk of mortality and the likelihood of MAKE30 events among this patient population. A total of 154 patients with sepsis experienced MAKE30 composite outcomes, with mortality being a key component. Stratifying patients with sepsis into subgroups based on whether they exhibited an elevated or diminished risk of AKI development revealed that patients within the high-risk category had a particularly pronounced risk of 30-day mortality. Additionally, the ROC curve analysis indicated that the nomogram exhibited moderate predictive performance for 30-day mortality (AUC = 0.706) and MAKE30 composite outcomes (AUC = 0.757). Compared to the SOFA and APACHE II scores, which are used clinically to evaluate patient prognosis, this predictive model demonstrated comparable predictive power. These results underscore the nomogram’s clinical utility in assisting ED physicians in prognostic assessment, thereby facilitating more accurate stratification and clinical decision-making. Implementing this nomogram is anticipated to enhance the efficiency of managing sepsis patients by streamlining treatment approaches through the early recognition of high-risk individuals, thereby improving prognostic outcomes.

Implementing the SA-AKI prediction model developed in this study is feasible from a clinical practice perspective. First, post-hospitalization test results routinely provide the clinical data required for all the nomogram’s variables. Second, DCA demonstrated that the nomogram yielded a favorable net benefit over a threshold probability range of 5–90%, and the CIC analysis confirmed the real-world applicability and value of the nomogram in clinical settings. Third, the predictive model demonstrated significant predictive utility for 30-day short-term prognostic outcomes. It also facilitated more accurate discrimination of the likelihood of MAKE30 events in sepsis patients and revealed an association between indices of inflammation, coagulation, and MAKE30 outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, urine output, an important indicator of renal dysfunction, was not fully incorporated due to inconsistent monitoring in the emergency department, which may have limited the model’s ability to detect early AKI. Second, fluid resuscitation, which can obscure early signs of kidney injury, was not explicitly considered, and the lack of detailed data on resuscitation volumes may have impacted the model’s predictive accuracy. Additionally, baseline Cr values were unavailable for 2% of patients, potentially affecting the accurate assessment of renal function and AKI diagnosis. The retrospective design carries an inherent risk of bias, and the general ward setting, with less exposure to nephrotoxic agents and less intensive monitoring, may understate the impact of drugs like vancomycin and polymyxins. Finally, while biomarkers such as PCT, D-dimer, and platelet count were included, newer biomarkers like NGAL and TIMP-2/IGFBP7, which may provide additional diagnostic value, were not evaluated. Future studies incorporating standardized urine output, detailed fluid management, baseline Cr levels, and additional biomarkers could enhance the model’s accuracy and generalizability.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate that factors such as age, PLT, PCT, and D-dimer levels, alongside the use of vasopressor medications and a history of hypertension, are crucial predictors of AKI in patients with sepsis. Notably, the nomogram incorporating these parameters proved most effective and user-friendly for estimating the likelihood of AKI development in individuals with sepsis. In addition, this nomogram holds clinical significance for forecasting the 30-day prognosis of patients and the occurrence of MAKE30 events. Therefore, this nomogram offers novel insights and tools for emergency physicians to personalize the early-stage management of renal injury in patients with sepsis.
