2‐aminobutyrate mediates the impact of air pollution on blood biomarkers of Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Anum Saeed, Chris Mckennan, Jane E Clougherty, Peidi Zhou, Sheila Tripathy, Ellen J Kinnee, Jiaxuan Duan, Kevin Kip, Xuemei Zeng, Victor Villemagne, Tharick Pascoal, Mark Mapstone, Eric A Whitsel, Oscar I Lopez, Christie Ballantyne, Thomas K Karikari, Bing Yu, Steven Reis, Annie Cohen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe examined the impact of mid‐life metabolites on late‐life Alzheimer's disease (AD) plasma biomarkers and whether these metabolites mediate air pollution effects.METHODSIn the Heart Strategies Concentrating on Risk Evaluation (Heart SCORE) cohort, we applied high‐dimensional regression and mediation models, adjusting for observed and latent confounders, with replication in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study. Exposures included mid‐life fine particulate matter (PM2.5), black carbon, metals, and metabolites. Outcomes were: late‐life plasma p‐tau(181, 217, and 231) and amyloid beta 42/40 (Simoa assays).RESULTSIn the Heart SCORE study, seven lipid‐ and amnio acid‐pathway metabolites measured in mid‐life were significantly inversely associated with late‐life p‐tau181 levels (false discovery rate ≤ 15%) and showed a nominal inverse association with p‐tau217 and 231 (P < 0.05). 2‐aminobutyrate was associated with mid‐life PM2.5 concentration (µg/m3; β = −0.047; P = 4.0 × 10−4) and mediated the effect of PM2.5 on p‐tau181 (P = 0.0057). Independent replication in the ARIC study confirmed the inverse association.DISCUSSIONMid‐life metabolite profiles, particularly 2‐aminobutyrate, may predict and mediate air pollution–related AD risk.Highlights Seven mid‐life metabolites were inversely linked to late‐life plasma phosphorylated tau (p‐tau)181.2‐aminobutyrate was tied to fine particulate matter (PM2.5), black carbon, and heavy metal exposure in two independent cohorts.2‐aminobutyrate may link pollution to Alzheimer's disease (AD) via oxidative stress pathways.This effect is specific to p‐tau181, a marker of early AD changes.This is the evidence of a metabolic mediator between air pollution and AD risk. Keywords: air pollution, Alzheimer's disease, Alzheimer's disease prevention, phosphorylated tau, PM2.5

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology can begin decades before the onset of clinical symptoms, making the identification of early biological indicators critical for intervention. 1 , 2 Early biomarkers of AD (such as plasma phosphorylated tau [p‐tau] and amyloid beta [Aβ]) and neurodegeneration (namely neurofilament light chain [NfL]), may be associated with altered metabolic profiles alterations in mid‐life. 3 Metabolic alterations and profiles are shaped by both lifestyle factors and environmental exposures.

Recent data suggest that long‐term exposure to air pollution ‐particularly fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and heavy metal components thereof ‐may be linked to AD pathology 4 and dementia. 5 , 6 Accumulating mechanistic evidence suggests that PM2.5 and associated metal components promote redox imbalance, systemic inflammation, and blood–brain barrier dysfunction, all of which have been associated with AD‐related processes. 4 , 5 , 6 . Metabolites may, therefore, represent intermediate biological signatures of these pathways, plausibly linking environmental exposures to downstream AD biomarkers. Yet, the extent to which metabolome‐wide signatures predict and/or mediate the relationship between environmental AD risk factors‐such as air pollution‐and downstream pathological processes has not been clearly elucidated.

In this study, we examined the association of mid‐life metabolic markers with late‐life AD plasma biomarkers and evaluated their role as mediators of air pollution on AD risk in the Heart Strategies Concentrating on Risk Evaluation (Heart SCORE) cohort. We independently validated these associations in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study.

METHODS

Study population

Heart SCORE study

Details of the Heart SCORE study have been published. 7 , 8 , 9 Briefly, the Heart SCORE study began in 2003 as a community dwellers study of race‐related disparities in cardiovascular diseases conducted in Allegheny County, Pennsylvania. 9 , 10 The study enrolled 2000 participants ages 45 to 75 years excluding those with a life expectancy < 5 years or an inability to undergo annual follow‐up visits. A subgroup of participants > 70 years from Heart SCORE study (Montreal Cognitive Assessment score > 26) underwent blood draw, brain positron emission tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging in late‐life (2020 onward) for the Heart SCORE brain study. 11

ARIC study (validation cohort)

The ARIC study is a prospective community‐based study of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) incidence in 15,792 middle‐aged adults (45–64 years at visit 1) adults recruited from four US communities from 1987 to 1989. A detailed description of the ARIC study design and methods has been published elsewhere. 12 , 13 Validation analysis for this study was conducted on metabolomic samples from participants in ARIC visit 5 (age 69–88 years), conducted between June 1, 2011, and August 30, 2013.

Assessment of covariates

Detailed methods for the assessment of baseline covariates in the Heart SCORE and ARIC studies have been published previously. 9 , 14 , 15 Briefly, baseline evaluation included assessment of demographics, psychosocial characteristics, anthropometric measurements, exercise and dietary habits, traditional ASCVD risk factors, highest education levels, and sleep disturbances. Diabetes mellitus was defined as a fasting serum glucose level ≥ 126 mg/dL or non‐fasting serum glucose level ≥ 200 mg/dL, a self‐reported physician diagnosis of diabetes, or use of hypoglycemic medication. Participants were classified as “never,” “former,” or “current” smokers from self‐reported information. Validated measures of blood pressure (BP) were collected by trained technicians following a standard protocol and have been validated before. 16 Fasting venous lipid profile was measured using standard enzymatic methods 17 at the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center clinical laboratory. Measurement of high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (hsCRP) was performed using an immunoturbidimetric assay on the Roche P Modular system (Roche Diagnostics), using reagents and calibrators from DiaSorin. Serum interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) concentrations were measured using commercially available enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (RD Systems). Measurements were performed using a standard curve provided with the ELISA kits and a random subset of samples (10%) was assayed in duplicate to assess reproducibility. 10 , 18

Air pollution exposure

Methods for chronic exposures to urban concentration (in ug/m3) of ambient particulate matter < 2.5 um in diameter (PM2.5) and black carbon (BC) have been detailed in prior publications. 19 , 20 , 21 Briefly, hybrid land use regression (LUR) models (including AERMOD dispersion model terms) were derived from a spatial monitoring campaign including 37 sampling sites distributed across metropolitan Pittsburgh during summer (June 5 to July 26, 2012) and winter (January 8 to March 10, 2013) 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 and models were adjusted for temporal variation using daily regulatory data from a centrally located US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Air Quality System (AQS) monitor. 26 , 27

As previously described in the Heart SCORE cohort, 21 period‐specific exposure estimates were developed to account for temporal variation in regional air pollution levels. Sensitivity analyses using alternative temporal exposure windows (up to 5 years prior) were also conducted using data from EPA AQS monitors in the Pittsburgh region, which exhibited consistent temporal trends across sites.

Literature searches were conducted in major biomedical databases (e.g., PubMed, Embase, Web of Science) using keywords such as metabolomics, Alzheimer's disease biomarkers, air pollution, PM2.5 , and p‐tau181. Studies were selected if they examined longitudinal relationships between metabolites and AD biomarkers, or evaluated the role of environmental exposures—particularly fine particulate matter—in neurodegenerative processes.

This review informed our hypothesis that mid‐life metabolic profiles could serve as early predictors of AD risk and potential mediators of environmental impacts.

Interpretation: Our analyses identified seven mid‐life amino acid and lipid metabolites that were significantly inversely associated with late‐life plasma phosphorylated tau (p‐tau)181 levels, a biomarker of AD pathology. Among these, 2‐aminobutyrate levels were significantly correlated with fine particulate matter (PM2.5) exposure and mediated the association between PM2.5 and elevated p‐tau181 levels. This mediation effect was replicated in an independent cohort, strengthening the evidence base. These findings suggest that specific metabolic signatures in mid‐life may predict late‐life AD biomarker levels and act as biological intermediates linking environmental exposures—such as air pollution—to neurodegenerative risk. This expands the existing body of knowledge by integrating metabolomics and environmental epidemiology to explain a mechanistic pathway for AD risk.

Air pollution (urban PM2.5 and BC) and other exposure assessments were completed in the Heart SCORE study in mid‐life (Figure S1 in supporting information). Heavy metal concentrations exhibited skewing and were log‐transformed (Figure S2 in supporting information). Geocoding for participant addresses was completed using a composite address locator in ArcGIS 10.8.2 to generate point features of residential locations. Pollution estimates from the models were then used to estimate mean annual concentrations of PM2.5 and BC for the 300 m buffer surrounding each participant's residential address. Similarly, monthly PM2.5 (µg/m3) concentrations were averaged to compute annual mean conentrations 26 as previously described. 28 , 29

Metabolite profiling

Untargeted discovery metabolomic profiling was completed on plasma samples obtained in fasting state at the mid‐life visit (Figure S1) and stored at −80°C. Duplicate technical replicates were done for 99 participants for validation purposes, for which one replicate was chosen at random to be used in all downstream analyses. Equal volumes of all samples were extracted and run across the Metabolon Inc. Precision Metabolomics discovery platform 30 , 31 and previously applied in Heart SCORE. 32 Samples were extracted and split into equal parts for analysis on four liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry platforms. Proprietary software was used to match ions to an in‐house library of standards for metabolite identification and for metabolite quantitation by peak area integration, for which a metabolite's abundance was defined as its peak area. Metabolites with > 10% missing data were discarded to avoid missing data–related biases. Remaining missing data was imputed as the metabolite's minimum observed abundance. Metabolomics profiling and air pollution exposure assessments (PM2.5 and BC) were completed in the Heart SCORE study in mid‐life (Figure S2).

Blood‐based markers of AD

Plasma p‐tau181 and p‐tau231 concentrations were measured using validated single molecule array (Simoa) methods on an HD‐X instrument (Quanterix). 33 , 34 Plasma p‐tau217 concentration was quantified using the p‐tau217+ assay 35 (Johnson & Johnson, formerly referred as Janssen). Plasma glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), NfL, and Aβ 42 and Aβ40 concentrations were measured using the Simoa Neuroplex 4E four‐plex assay from Quanterix. 36 , 37 Plasma p‐tau, NfL, and GFAP concentrations were log‐transformed to address skewness (Figure S3 in supporting information).

Metabolite–biomarker associations

Latent factors that might confound the relationship between metabolite levels and p‐tau181 and p‐tau217 levels were estimated using the method described by McKennan and Nicolae. 38 For each metabolite, we used linear regression to regress blood biomarker levels onto the metabolite's log abundance while controlling for latent factors and baseline covariates (age, sex, race, hypertension medication use, systolic BP, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, high‐density lipoprotein [HDL] cholesterol, total cholesterol, and the log‐concentrations of IL‐6 and hsCRP). We used the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure 39 to control the false discovery rate (FDR) at 15%. We performed a sensitivity analysis that additionally adjusted for PM2.5 and BC to ensure our results were not confounded by pollution markers. This was not our main analysis because our sample size decreased by 17% (n = 115 to n = 96) due to missing pollution data.

Metabolite–pollution associations

We regressed the log abundance of metabolites passing the FDR threshold onto each pollution marker while adjusting for latent confounds, 38 observed baseline covariates included in the above regressions, education (# years of post‐high school education), and income (≥ $80k or < $80k). Education and income were included because of prior association with pollution levels. 40 , 41 We used a Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple testing (P < 0.05/#{FDR‐significant metabolites identified above} = 0.05/7).

Mediation analysis

We used the output of the above two analyses to test whether a metabolite identified as being significantly associated with p‐tau181 levels mediated the effect of PM2.5 on p‐tau181. Our estimate for the indirect effect was the product of the regression coefficient estimates from the above two analyses and we derive its standard error in Supplemental Methods in supporting information. Because the literature suggests exposure to PM2.5 increases AD risk, 42 we computed one‐sided P values to test the null hypothesis of no indirect effect against the alternative that the indirect effect was positive. We used Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple testing (P ≤ 0.05/{#p‐tau 181‐related metabolites} = 0.05/7). An identical statistical analysis was used to assess replication of the metabolites–pollution relationship in the ARIC study.

To ensure mediation results were not biased by participants who moved during the course of the study, we performed a sensitivity analysis by re‐computing the association between 2‐aminobutyrate and p‐tau181 levels after removing the 17 subjects (out of n = 115) that changed their primary residence prior to p‐tau measurement. Note that all 17 subjects moved to another location within the greater Pittsburgh area.

Amino acid and lipid metabolites are inversely associated with AD biomarkers

In fully adjusted regression models (Methods), seven metabolites in the amino acid (2‐aminobutyrate, isoleucine, lysine, choline phosphate and cysteine‐glutathione disulfide) and lipid (lignoceroyl sphingomyelin [d18:1/24:0], tricosanoyl Sphingomyelin [d18:1/23:0]) pathways were inversely associated with p‐tau181 at an FDR of 15% (Table 3). Our sensitivity analysis that also adjusted for pollution produced nearly identical results (Figure S4 in supporting information). The levels of these seven metabolites were also inversely associated with p‐tau231 and p‐tau217 levels, with all metabolites reaching nominal significance (P < 0.05) in the association with p‐tau231 and 2‐aminobutyrate and lysine reaching nominal significance in the association with p‐tau217 (Table 3). Plasma Aβ42 and GFAP were both associated with the xenobiotic metabolite salicylurate (Table S1 in supporting information). We did not pursue these associations further because these associated metabolites were likely derived from exogenous sources such as medications (Table S1).

2‐aminobutyrate may mediate the impact of air pollution on p‐tau181

PM2.5 was significantly inversely associated with 2‐aminobutyrate after adjusting for multiple testing (P ≤ 0.05/7 = 0.0071 [Methods]; Table 4). This, as well as the above‐described inverse relationship between 2‐aminobutyrate and p‐tau181 levels, suggests 2‐aminobutyrate may mediate an adverse effect of PM2.5 on p‐tau 181 (Figure 1A). A mediation analysis corroborated this, for which the indirect effect of PM2.5 on p‐tau181 was positive after adjusting for multiple testing (P = 0.0057 < 0.05/7; Figure 1B). A sensitivity analysis that excluded study subjects that changed their primary residence during the course of the study indicated mediation results were robust to movers (Methods; Table S2 in supporting information). Mediation analyses involving all other pollution markers also had positive indirect effect estimates with nominally significant P values (P ≤ 0.05; Table S3 in supporting information). To assess effects beyond ambient PM2.5, we re‐ran the metal models adjusting for PM2.5. Although associations were directionally similar, they lost statistical significance (P = 0.50–0.94), indicating limited power to resolve metal‐specific effects.

DISCUSSION

Examining the association of mid‐life metabolites with plasma biomarkers of AD risk can provide valuable insights into the biochemical changes that occur before the onset of neurocognitive decline. Furthermore, identifying whether these metabolic alterations mediate the association between environmental risk factors of AD like air pollution could inform risk mitigation strategies needed to prevent or delay AD. In this study, we identified seven mid‐life metabolites within amino acid and lipid pathways that were inversely associated with late‐life plasma p‐tau181 levels, but not with p‐tau231 or p‐tau217. Among these, only 2‐aminobutyrate was significantly influenced by mid‐life exposure to environmental pollutants, including PM2.5, BC, and airborne heavy metals. The association between 2‐aminobutyrate and PM2.5 was independently replicated in the larger ARIC study cohort. To our knowledge, this study is the first to identify a significant metabolic mediator‐2‐aminobutyrate‐ linking mid‐life air pollution exposure to plasma AD biomarkers, thereby expanding current understanding of how environmental factors may contribute to AD risk.

Alterations in plasma metabolites associated with AD pathology are previously reported. 13 , 43 Indeed, young individuals exposed to high PM2.5 burden have shown increased indices of neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and AD‐associated pathology, including hyperphosphorylated tau and diffuse Aβ plaques. 44 However, no studies describe mid‐life metabolic dysregulations associated with late‐life novel blood‐based markers of AD in individuals without neurocognitive decline. Emerging evidence also indicates a causal link between air pollution exposure and dementias 45 including AD. Our results suggest that such an effect of air pollution on AD pathology may be mediated specifically through alterations in 2‐aminobutyrate, an amino acid metabolite involved in glutathione metabolism. 46 Feng et al. have previously found that air pollutant exposure (characterized by ambient PM2.5 and BC) was significantly associated with changes in serum levels of several amino acids, suggesting that air pollution may prompt vascular dysfunction through perturbing amino acid metabolism. 47

The mechanisms of air pollution in AD pathogenesis are less studied but increased oxidative stress, redox machinery dysregulation, and thus neuroinflammation are plausible mechanisms. 48 , 49 PM2.5‐associated transition metals such as iron, nickel, and vanadium can generate reactive oxygen species through redox cycling, promoting oxidative stress and glutathione depletion, 50 consistent with the observed reduction in 2‐aminobutyrate. These metal components have also been implicated in tau phosphorylation and neuroinflammatory signaling in experimental models, aligning with our findings while extending them to human metabolic mediators. 51

Beyond its role in the glutathione mechanism, 2‐aminobutrate is also involved in the regulation of macrophage polarization and function. 52 The interaction between microglia and astrocytes is influenced by microglial polarization in which M1‐polarized microglia promote a pro‐inflammatory environment. The M1 phenotype is involved in the inflammatory response to Aβ and hyperphosphorylated tau— a key pathology in AD. 53 Targeting macrophage polarization may have therapeutic potential in AD by promoting anti‐inflammatory effects and tissue repair—mitigating neuroinflammation. 54

Our results observing the specificity of 2‐aminobutyrate's association with p‐tau181, but not with p‐tau231 or p‐tau217, likely reflect differences in the biochemical and phosphorylation characteristics of tau at specific sites in the context of AD pathology in the brain. 55 P‐tau181 is a sensitive marker of early AD changes and is known to be influenced by systemic metabolic and oxidative stress—processes in which 2‐aminobutyrate likely plays a key role as a byproduct of glutathione synthesis. Additionally, p‐tau181 is one of the most well validated and robustly detectable amyloid plasma biomarkers currently available, potentially contributing to the strength of the observed association.

LIMITATIONS

This study has several limitations. While metabolite concentrations and pollution exposures can vary over time, our analysis relied on single time‐point measurements. Although regional PM2.5 concentrations declined during the study period, prior Heart SCORE work incorporated period‐specific exposure estimates, 21 and evidence from Pittsburgh suggests that relative spatial pollution contrasts remain stable over tim.e 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 Nevertheless, some exposure misclassification may remain when applying spatial models derived from specific monitoring campaigns. Further, while the mid‐life metabolite measures preceded biomarker assessments by ≈ 15 years, additional work is needed to evaluate causal pathways and predictive performance. However, the independent validation of the PM2.5–2‐aminobutyrate association in the ARIC study strengthens the validity of this specific finding. Additionally, we were unable to independently validate associations between PM2.5 and novel plasma biomarkers of AD (e.g., p‐tau181 and Aβ) due to limited data availability and acquisition delays. Nonetheless, a robust body of literature supports the role of PM2.5 in increasing AD risk. Future studies leveraging longitudinal data and external validation cohorts are needed to further corroborate and expand upon these results.

CONCLUSIONS

2‐aminobutyrate, an amino acid metabolite, may mediate the adverse impact of air pollution on AD risk. Prospective studies should assess the impact of air pollution variation on 2‐aminobutryate level and prevention of AD.
