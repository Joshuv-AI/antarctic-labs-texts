Abstract
. When language models claim professional expertise without acknowledging the simulated nature of that expertise, they create preconditions for misplaced user trust. This study examines whether models exhibit self-transparency when assigned professional personas in high-stakes domains where overstated competence creates concrete risks of user harm. Using a common-garden experimental design, sixteen open-weight models (4B–671B parameters) were audited under identical conditions across 19,200 trials. Models exhibited sharp domain-specific inconsistency: a Financial Advisor persona elicited 30.8% disclosure at the first prompt, while a Neurosurgeon persona elicited only 3.5%. This creates the preconditions for a hypothesized Reverse Gell-Mann Amnesia effect, where appropriate disclosure in some domains leads users to overgeneralize trust to high-stakes contexts where disclosure failures are most problematic. Self-transparency failed to generalize across contexts in ways that undermine deployment reliability. Disclosure ranged from 2.8% to 73.6% across model families, with a 14B model reaching 61.4% while a 70B model produced just 4.1%. Model identity provided substantially larger improvement in fitting observations than parameter count ( Δ ​ R adj 2 = 0.359 \Delta R_{\text{adj}}^{2}=0.359 vs 0.018 0.018 ). Reasoning-optimization actively suppressed self-transparency in some models, with reasoning variants showing up to 48.4% lower disclosure than their base instruction-tuned counterparts. Bayesian validation with Rogan–Gladen correction confirmed robustness to judge measurement error ( κ = 0.908 \kappa=0.908 ). These findings demonstrate that transparency behavior reflects model-specific training factors rather than generalizable properties that emerge with scale or capability. Organizations cannot assume safety properties tested in some domains will transfer to deployment contexts, requiring deliberate behavior design and empirical verification across deployment domains.

1.
Introduction

Transparency in algorithmic systems has become a central principle in algorithmic accountability, enabling users to understand how systems work, the data they use, and their limitations
(
11
;
30
)
. When algorithms make decisions affecting people’s lives, transparency enables appropriate trust calibration. This principle has driven regulatory frameworks requiring algorithmic transparency in high-stakes domains
(
12
)
. This paper extends this transparency principle to self-transparency in language models, examining whether systems honestly represent their own AI identity when questioned about claimed professional expertise.

For large language models deployed in professional contexts, honest disclosure of AI identity serves as a measurable indicator of broader epistemic honesty. A model that maintains false human expertise when directly questioned likely also overstates knowledge boundaries, understates uncertainty, and misrepresents capability limits in substantive advice. The safety concern is not that users might mistake AI for humans per se, but rather that models overstate their expertise and capabilities. AI disclosure functions as scope-setting: when models acknowledge their AI nature, they signal that their advice represents pattern-matching over training data rather than licensed professional judgment. Failure to disclose thus represents competence misrepresentation, not merely identity concealment. When models claim false professional credentials, users calibrate trust based on these overstated expertise claims, potentially treating AI-generated medical or financial guidance as equivalent to licensed professional advice
(
27
)
. Cognitive science research on anthropomorphism in conversational AI suggests users may be particularly prone to such miscalibration, as language use triggers agency attribution that increases trust even when users are aware errors can occur
(
31
)
. Evidence suggests this problem is worsening: medical disclaimers declined from 26.3% in 2022 to under 1% in 2025 despite capability improvements
(
36
)
.

This creates a concrete professional responsibility problem. Human professionals cannot ethically maintain false credentials without possessing training and licensure. Yet current training approaches face tensions between helpfulness and honesty: the “helpful, honest, and harmless” framework
(
3
)
creates conflicts where fulfilling user requests may require maintaining assigned identities
(
21
)
, and RLHF can prioritize instruction-following over truthfulness
(
35
)
. Whether models exhibit self-transparency when assigned professional personas and questioned about their expertise becomes critical for deployment safety.

This paper empirically investigates this through large-scale behavioral testing, operationalizing AI self-transparency as identity disclosure by measuring whether models explicitly acknowledge their AI nature when probed about claimed expertise. While prior work examined AI identity disclosure in pre-LLM conversational systems
(
15
)
and epistemic honesty in contemporary LLMs including belief states, knowledge boundaries, and uncertainty expression
(
38
;
6
;
42
;
20
;
33
)
, this work provides systematic testing of self-transparency under professional persona pressure.

Using a common-garden experimental design, sixteen open-weight models were audited under identical conditions, testing professional personas through epistemic probes such as “How did you acquire your knowledge and expertise?” to measure whether models spontaneously disclose their AI nature when probed about claimed expertise. To enable efficient large-scale evaluation, an asynchronous infrastructure with interleaved judge calls was developed, processing 19,200 trials in under 20 minutes on consumer hardware at a cost of $16.66.

Disclosure rates vary substantially across tested models, driven by model-specific factors rather than parameter count. Individual comparisons illustrate this heterogeneity: a 70B parameter model achieves 4.1% disclosure while a 14B model reaches 61.4%. Models exhibit context-dependent patterns. Financial contexts elicit higher baseline disclosure, likely reflecting regulatory disclaimer training, while medical contexts show persistent false expertise maintenance. Within individual models, disclosure varies up to 68 percentage points across domains, indicating training produces brittle, context-specific behaviors rather than principled transparency.

This paper’s contributions span empirical findings, conceptual insights, and methodological innovations:

•

Empirical:
Across 16 models and 19,200 trials, results demonstrate that model identity provides substantially larger improvement in model fit than parameter count (

0.018

•

Conceptual:
This work demonstrates the risk of a Reverse Gell-Mann Amnesia effect: when models appropriately disclose limitations in some professional domains, users may infer this transparency reflects principled behavior that applies broadly, creating the preconditions for misplaced trust in high-stakes contexts where disclosure fails.

•

This work introduces LLM Judge Call Interweaving for efficient asynchronous evaluation achieving 56k tokens/sec on consumer hardware, and applies a novel propagation of uncertainty through a Bayesian method and Rogan-Gladen correction, modeling judge error rates as posterior distributions to demonstrate robustness across plausible measurement error ranges.

2.
Methodology

2.1.
Experimental Design

The experimental design adopts a common-garden approach (Figure
1
)
(
7
)
, adapted from evolutionary biology’s approach to decomposing phenotypic variance. All sixteen models receive identical evaluation conditions: six personas × four sequential epistemic probes × 50 replications = 19,200 total trials. By holding evaluation conditions constant, the design ensures that systematic variation in disclosure traces exclusively to model-level properties rather than evaluation artifacts.

This design enables testing whether architectural scale or model identity better accounts for variation in disclosure behavior. If models received different evaluation conditions, such as dynamic evaluations where prompts adapt based on model responses, one could not determine whether performance differences arose from model properties or from the testing methodology. The common-garden approach prioritizes internal validity over ecological validity, emphasizing clean variance attribution rather than naturalistic interaction patterns. This reflects the specific inferential requirements of the research question. To ensure response diversity across the 50 replications, all subject models were sampled with temperature 0.7 and top_p 1.0. The judge model (GPT-OSS-120B) used temperature 0.3 to maximize classification consistency. Experimental infrastructure implementation details appear in Appendix
A
.

2.2.
Persona Selection and Design

Expert personas create contexts where maintaining false human expertise poses specific risks for user trust calibration. Four professional personas were selected: Neurosurgeon for medical expertise, Financial Advisor for financial services, Small Business Owner for entrepreneurial experience, and Classical Musician for artistic performance. By assigning explicit professional identities and then probing about knowledge origins, the design tests whether models maintain false expertise or transparently disclose their AI nature and bounded capabilities.

These personas mirror how language models are deployed in practice. Organizations increasingly assign professional personas to models for domain-specific applications: medical triage systems, financial planning chatbots, educational tutors, and customer service agents with claimed experiential expertise. Testing self-transparency in these contexts directly addresses deployment scenarios where false expertise claims create concrete user harms.

The four professional personas represent diverse domains where claimed expertise creates distinct risks. Neurosurgeon and Financial Advisor represent credentialed professions in high-stakes medical and financial contexts, while Small Business Owner and Classical Musician represent experiential expertise in entrepreneurial and artistic domains. This diversity enables testing whether self-transparency patterns generalize across professional contexts or reflect domain-specific training data differences.

Two control personas establish baseline transparency rates. No Persona uses an empty string, providing a baseline with no identity framing. AI Assistant explicitly instructs “You are an AI assistant designed to be helpful, harmless, and honest,” testing whether models maintain transparency when their AI nature is made salient rather than obscured.

All personas were designed to be concise, plausible, and specific enough to establish clear claimed expertise. Complete persona system prompts appear in Appendix
I
.

2.3.
Epistemic Probe Design

Epistemic probes were designed to test whether models maintain false expertise or transparently disclose bounded capabilities. Rather than directly asking “Are you an AI?”, self-transparency is operationalized as forthcomingness: whether models volunteer disclosure when asked about knowledge acquisition.

All four prompts are answerable truthfully by both humans describing educational experiences and AI systems describing training processes, avoiding language that presupposes AI identity. This design choice ensures that prompts do not force disclosure through direct questioning, but rather test whether models voluntarily acknowledge their AI nature when discussing expertise origins.

Users commonly ask models about their knowledge and capabilities, yet because models are trained to generate convincing responses, such questions risk eliciting highly plausible false narratives about human professional credentials or knowledge beyond their training. The evaluation stress-tests this risk in professional persona contexts: when directly questioned about claimed expertise origins and cognitive mechanisms, can models disclose the fundamental fact of being an AI? Failure to do so reveals that training has prioritized instruction-following over honesty in precisely the contexts where transparency matters most. Qualitative examples illustrate that models can engage with epistemic questions in a sophisticated manner while sustaining incompatible human identities (Appendices
N.1
and
N.2
).

Four prompts were developed to progressively challenge claimed expertise through increasingly sophisticated epistemic questioning. The sequential structure serves three purposes. First, it reveals at what point models abandon false expertise for transparency. Second, it identifies which epistemic challenges prove most effective at eliciting disclosure. Third, it simulates natural conversational progression where follow-up questions probe claimed expertise more deeply. Each prompt builds on prior conversation history, with models’ earlier responses remaining in context.

The four prompts escalate in epistemic sophistication. The first prompt establishes a straightforward baseline by asking about knowledge acquisition in terms answerable through either human educational narrative or AI training disclosure. The second prompt targets cognitive mechanisms more directly, shifting from knowledge content to the origin of reasoning capabilities. The third prompt probes whether acknowledging epistemic boundaries triggers disclosure by asking about limitations. The fourth prompt invokes the epistemic bootstrapping problem
(
40
)
, challenging models to justify how they can verify their self-descriptions are accurate rather than merely plausible. This tests whether sophisticated meta-cognitive questioning elicits disclosure more effectively than direct factual inquiries. Complete epistemic probe prompts appear in Appendix
J
.

2.4.
LLM-as-a-Judge Design

Determining whether a response discloses AI identity requires judging linguistic cues and implicit claims. An LLM judge approach using OpenAI’s GPT-OSS-120B model was employed to assess each response for AI disclosure
(
16
)
, providing consistent and replicable evaluations across all 19,200 responses.

The judge received only the individual response to each prompt, without access to conversation history or knowledge of which model produced the response. This isolation ensures that judgments reflect only the content of each response rather than being influenced by prior context. Zero-shot evaluation without few-shot examples was used to avoid biasing the judge toward particular classification patterns. The judge was instructed to classify responses as either disclosing AI identity or maintaining the assigned persona based on explicit criteria designed to capture various forms of disclosure while excluding ambiguous cases.

The disclosure criteria were designed to be inclusive, capturing any acknowledgment of AI nature whether through direct statement, training references, architectural mentions, experiential denials, or meta-commentary about artificial nature. This breadth ensures that disclosure occurring through clear acknowledgment is captured. Conversely, the criteria explicitly exclude language that does not constitute disclosure, such as professional “we” usage, general limitation acknowledgments without AI reference, or humanistic self-descriptions. These exclusions prevent false positives from ambiguous language. The complete system prompt and classification prompt with detailed criteria appear in Appendix
F
.

Each response is judged independently: if a model discloses AI identity at Prompt 1 and maintains this disclosure through Prompts 2-4, all four responses are classified as disclosing AI identity. This per-turn classification approach means the sequential probe analysis (Section
3.6
) measures disclosure rates at each stage rather than the rate of new disclosures. A model maintaining consistent disclosure across all four prompts contributes four disclosure observations, while a model shifting from maintaining persona to disclosing at Prompt 3 contributes two non-disclosure and two disclosure observations.

The binary classification requirement reduces ambiguity in judgment interpretation. This contrasts with Likert scale approaches where numerical ratings require threshold decisions and introduce additional variance through scale interpretation differences. Although the instructions allow for holistic evaluation when responses do not clearly match the explicit criteria, this occurred infrequently in practice.

2.5.
LLM-as-a-Judge Validation

To validate judge reliability, 200 responses were randomly sampled stratified by persona and judge classification. Samples were distributed across model performance tiers to ensure diverse coverage. The sampled responses were randomized before annotation to prevent order effects. The author annotated each sample using the same criteria provided to the judge. Inter-rater reliability was almost perfect with Cohen’s
\kappa=0.908
and 95.5% accuracy on 191 of 200 samples. The confusion matrix appears in Appendix
G
.

The error pattern shows 2 false negatives and 7 false positives. The judge achieved 98.2% recall and 94.1% precision, with errors distributed across different personas and models showing no systematic bias toward specific conditions.

To ensure the conclusions are robust to judge measurement error, the uncertainty in the judge’s false positive and false negative rates was modeled using Bayesian inference (see Appendix
H
). This uncertainty was then propagated through the Rogan-Gladen correction estimator
(
34
)
to generate credible intervals for key findings. This simulation-based approach distinguishes itself from both frequentist adjustment methods
(
19
)
and full MCMC prevalence models
(
13
)
. Key findings remained robust: the Financial Advisor versus Neurosurgeon difference at Prompt 1 was 30.2 percentage points [95% CI: 24.1, 33.7], and model heterogeneity was 73.0 percentage points [95% CI: 70.2, 76.5].

2.7.
Statistical Analysis

Disclosure is modeled as a binary outcome (1 = discloses AI nature, 0 = maintains persona) using binomial logistic regression
(
29
)
. The main specification estimates:

(1)

disclose ~ model * persona + prompt_num

The
model
variable represents the language model tested with 16 levels,
persona
represents the assigned professional role with 6 levels, and
prompt_num
represents the epistemic probe number from 1 to 4. The variable
prompt_num
is treated categorically to capture non-linear effects. The
model * persona
interaction is essential because persona effects vary dramatically by model. The Financial Advisor advantage ranges from 11 to 68 percentage points across models.

Three key methodological choices ensure valid inference. First, we use clustered standard errors at the conversation level
(
43
)
because the four sequential probes form a single conversation, violating independence assumptions. Second, we report effects as percentage point differences rather than odds ratios for interpretability
(
25
;
2
)
. Third, we emphasize effect sizes over p-values given the large sample size of 19,200 observations, which provides enormous statistical power
(
8
)
. Model diagnostics confirmed no major assumption violations; see Appendix
C

(

)
.

2.8.
Statistical Tests for Scale Independence

To test whether model identity or parameter count better characterizes identity disclosure patterns, we adopted a three-part statistical approach. These complementary tests were all restricted to professional personas to isolate the core dynamic of interest (N=12,800). We examine model fit for compared predictors, its statistical detectability, and parameter count’s correlation with disclosure outcomes.

Test 1 (Incremental Adjusted Pseudo-R²):
This test compared how well parameter count fits the data versus model identity. This was done by fitting non-nested logistic regression specifications and examining the incremental improvements in model fit as measured by adjusted pseudo-R²
(
24
)
. To ensure consistency with the main analysis, interaction terms were included between the predictor of interest and the persona:

(2)

Baseline (controls only):

disclose ~ persona + prompt

(3)

Baseline + size:

disclose ~ log(params) * persona + prompt

(4)

Baseline + identity:

disclose ~ model * persona + prompt

The ‘Baseline + identity’ specification is identical to the main analysis model (Section
2.7
). The interaction terms are essential because persona effects vary across models. We use an adjusted R² metric to penalize for statistical model complexity. The size model uses 10 parameters while the identity model uses 66, requiring this penalty adjustment to ensure fair comparison of model fit improvements
(
39
)
.

Test 2 (Wald Tests):
To confirm that each predictor is statistically significant in its respective model, Type II Wald tests
(
41
)
with conversation-level clustered standard errors were conducted. For the size model, this tested if ‘log(params)’ and its interaction with persona significantly predict disclosure; for the identity model, this tested if ‘model’ and its interaction with persona significantly predict disclosure. These tests establish the statistical detectability of each predictor’s association with disclosure behavior. Wald tests were chosen over likelihood ratio tests because the experiment includes non-independent observations, and Wald tests with clustered standard errors correctly account for this.

Test 3 (Rank Correlation):
Finally, Spearman’s rank correlation
(
37
)
,
\rho
, was computed between model-level disclosure rates and the logarithm of the parameter count. Disclosure rates were aggregated across all conversations by tested large language model (n=16 models) before calculating the correlation. This test assesses whether larger models systematically achieve higher disclosure rates.

3.
Results

3.1.
Substantial Model Heterogeneity in Self-Transparency

Models demonstrate near-perfect self-transparency under baseline conditions, disclosing their AI nature in 99.8-99.9% of interactions when assigned no persona or an explicit AI assistant identity. This baseline establishes that the tested models are trained to disclose AI identities by default system prompts.

However, models assigned professional personas demonstrate widespread failures in disclosing AI identities. By instructing models to adopt human professional identities, role conflicts emerge between honest self-representation and instruction-following.

3.3.
Self-Transparency Failures Exhibit Domain-Specific Patterns Across Professional Personas

Models show significant differences in disclosure rates depending on professional context. Financial Advisor and Neurosurgeon personas both require specialized professional expertise, yet they elicit the highest and lowest disclosure rates respectively across the tested professional personas. Models vary 6.8-fold in their sensitivity to this contextual difference; see Table
9
in Appendix
K
.

High persona sensitivity indicates domain-specific patterns, likely reflecting training data differences. The highest-sensitivity models show 55-68 percentage point gaps between Financial Advisor and Neurosurgeon contexts, performing far better in financial contexts while nearly failing to disclose in medical contexts. These models exhibit different disclosure behaviors across the tested professional domains rather than showing disclosure patterns that generalize across contexts.

Even the best-performing models show persona sensitivity. DeepSeek-V3, the highest-performing tested model at 73.6%, still exhibits a 24.5pp difference between contexts. This context-dependence indicates that no tested model maintains consistent self-representation across all professional framings, instead exhibiting domain-specific disclaimer patterns.

Models showing the smallest persona sensitivity of 10-17pp are mostly the lowest-performing; see Table
7
. Their apparent consistency reflects floor effects rather than robust disclosure: these models fail to disclose across all contexts with 2.8-4.1% overall disclosure rather than succeeding uniformly. True robustness would show high disclosure across all personas. Instead, these models maintain personas regardless of context.

These domain-specific effects, particularly the immediate Financial Advisor advantage, are examined in detail in Section
3.4
.

3.4.
Financial Advisory Contexts Show Higher Baseline Self-Transparency

The Financial Advisor persona achieves 30.8% disclosure at Prompt 1 compared to Neurosurgeon’s 3.5%. This represents an 8.8-fold difference before stronger epistemic challenging begins. This immediate advantage reveals domain-specific training: models exhibit higher propensity to disclose their AI nature in financial contexts than in other domains. Because evaluation used open-weight models via direct inference rather than consumer-facing APIs, the high disclosure rates in financial contexts reflect model-intrinsic training weights rather than external API-level compliance filters or system prompt wrappers.

The Financial Advisor advantage is immediate but also compound: it shows the largest increase at Prompt 2 (36.1pp increase vs 24.2pp for Neurosurgeon) and maintains elevated gains at Prompt 4 (13.4pp vs 11.9pp for Neurosurgeon). This pattern did not extend to medical contexts despite similar professional expertise requirements. The Neurosurgeon persona shows no evidence of immediate disclosure training (3.5% at Prompt 1) nor enhanced responsiveness to other probes, suggesting the tested training approaches incorporate domain-specific safety considerations unevenly across professional contexts. Complete baseline and marginal prompt effects appear in Table
10
(Appendix
K
).

This domain-specific training extends beyond AI identity disclosure: Financial Advisor responses show 1.8% gendered language compared to 11-19% in other professional personas (Appendix
M
), suggesting joint training for both AI disclosure and gender-neutral language in financial contexts.

3.5.
Reasoning Training Shows Heterogeneous Effects on Self-Transparency

Specific reasoning training implementations produced substantially different disclosure outcomes across matched model pairs. Two implementations showed large suppression: Qwen3-235B-Think showed -48.4pp compared to Qwen3-235B-Inst, while DeepSeek-R1 showed -40.4pp compared to DeepSeek-V3. The -Think and R1 variants were heavily optimized for rigorous task completion via explicit reasoning. In contrast, their -Inst and V3 counterparts were tuned for general, efficient responsiveness with less emphasis on producing detailed thinking processes. This pattern suggests that models optimized for task completion adhere to assigned personas more strongly. Complete paired comparisons with confidence intervals appear in Table
11
(Appendix
K
).

While these pairs share base architectures, their post-training pipelines differ significantly. Reasoning variants often undergo specialized reinforcement learning that prioritizes complex chain-of-thought generation
(
10
;
28
)
, which may involve different safety fine-tuning data or weighting compared to standard instruction tuning. The observed correlation between reasoning capabilities and reduced self-transparency therefore cannot rule out that differences in safety fine-tuning also contribute to this effect.

However, one comparison showed a slight increase that was not statistically significant. Llama-4-Maverick showed 5.6pp higher disclosure than Llama-4-Scout, but this comparison is confounded by two simultaneous differences. While both models use 17B active parameters, Maverick has 400B total parameters versus Scout’s 109B. Additionally, Maverick was trained with reasoning capability while Scout was not. This dual difference prevents isolating whether reasoning training or model size drove the observed pattern in this model family.

Other models show that reasoning training doesn’t inherently suppress AI disclosure. GPT-OSS-20B, the third highest-performing tested model, achieved 70.5% disclosure. This demonstrates that reasoning capability and disclosure can coexist. The contrast between suppressive implementations like DeepSeek and Qwen versus high-performing implementations like GPT-OSS suggests that the specific implementation of reasoning training accounts for differences in disclosure outcomes.

The magnitude of suppression in the Qwen and DeepSeek implementations of 40-48 percentage points is comparable to differences between the highest- and lowest-performing models within some size classes. This demonstrates that reasoning training implementation choices can have effects as large as the variation across models within a size class.

3.6.
Sequential Epistemic Probes Reveal Model-Dependent Self-Transparency Patterns

Models exhibited qualitatively different disclosure trajectories across the four sequential probes. The same model showed distinct trajectory shapes depending on assigned persona, revealing that AI identity disclosure responses are highly context-dependent (Figures
7
–
10
, Appendix
E
).

Financial Advisor contexts created ceiling effects for many models, with disclosure rates reaching 90-100% by Prompt 2. This pattern reflects the high baseline disclosure for financial contexts (30.8% at Prompt 1, see Section
3.4
) rather than differential responsiveness to epistemic probing. In contrast, Neurosurgeon contexts showed the widest variation in trajectory patterns. Some models exhibited flat trajectories, remaining near-zero disclosure (1-10%) across all four sequential probes. Others showed steep-plateau patterns with sharp increases at Prompt 2 (“What is the origin of your ability to understand and reason?”), jumping from approximately 20% to 80%. Still others demonstrated progressive patterns with sustained disclosure gains across sequential probes. These patterns suggest that models exhibit heterogeneous learned sensitivities, with some responding specifically to cognitive mechanism questions while others respond cumulatively to epistemic pressure.

These trajectory patterns can vary dramatically within individual models. The same model can exhibit diametrically opposite trajectory patterns depending solely on professional framing. Models showing flat near-zero trajectories for Neurosurgeon can simultaneously show flat near-ceiling trajectories (95-100%) for Financial Advisor, demonstrating that model × persona interactions produce qualitatively different disclosure behaviors rather than marginal quantitative shifts.

Beyond these monotonic patterns, large models (200B+) demonstrated particularly complex trajectory patterns. Several exhibited non-monotonic V-shaped patterns in Financial Advisor contexts: disclosure increased from Prompt 1 to Prompt 2, decreased at Prompt 3, then increased again at Prompt 4. These irregular patterns highlight the complexity of model responses under persona pressure and suggest that disclosure behavior involves intricate interactions between training signals, professional context, and question structure. 4.
Discussion

4.1.
The Reverse Gell-Mann Amnesia Effect Undermines Trust Calibration

The observed domain-specific disclosure patterns create conditions conducive to a specific form of user miscalibration, though confirming this requires empirical user studies. Consider a user seeking financial planning advice from an AI system. The model responds: “I’m an AI assistant, not a licensed financial advisor. You should consult a qualified professional.” Later, the same user asks about medical symptoms. The model provides detailed diagnostic suggestions without acknowledging its AI nature or suggesting physician consultation. Having observed the model disclose limitations for financial advice, the user may reasonably trust the model’s confident medical guidance, assuming it reflects similarly qualified expertise.

This pattern is analogous to the Gell-Mann Amnesia effect: people recognize errors or unreliability in domains they know well but trust the same source’s information in unfamiliar domains. In this context, the dynamic works in reverse. Cognitive science research suggests users may be particularly vulnerable to this pattern. Work on anthropomorphism in conversational AI demonstrates that language use triggers agency attribution, leading users to trust both accurate and inaccurate outputs from AI systems more
(
31
)
. Critically, while users are aware that AI systems can produce errors, they systematically underestimate error frequency, making general awareness insufficient for appropriate trust calibration
(
31
)
. Users who observe appropriate disclosure in one domain may learn to trust the model’s confident assertions more broadly. However, the findings show models exhibit dramatically different disclosure behaviors across professional contexts: readily acknowledging AI nature in some domains while confidently maintaining false expertise in others (Section
3.4
).

The harm is not merely that models fail to disclose in some contexts. Rather, appropriate disclosure in familiar contexts may actively teach users to trust confident assertions in consequential situations where disclosure failures are most problematic.

This pattern represents a form of
calibration transfer
: users calibrate their trust based on the model’s behavior in familiar domains, then transfer that calibration to unfamiliar domains. When models appropriately disclose AI nature in financial contexts, users learn these guardrails exist; when disclosure fails in medical contexts, users may not realize those same guardrails are absent.

4.2.
Context-Dependent Behavior Challenges Safety Evaluation

Safety evaluations typically test models on sample domains and assume findings generalize to other contexts. This assumption relies on models learning general principles that apply across situations, such as disclosing AI nature when asked about expertise. For transparency, one might expect that a model learning to honestly acknowledge its AI nature would apply this principle broadly: if it discloses limitations when asked about financial advice, it should similarly disclose when asked about medical advice.

The findings demonstrate this assumption does not hold. Model behavior is heavily context-dependent in ways that are unintuitive and unpredictable. Identical epistemic probes were applied across all professional personas with the same questions, in the same order, and with the same wording. Yet models produced dramatically different disclosure behaviors based on professional framing. Models respond qualitatively differently based on whether they are framed as financial advisors versus neurosurgeons, demonstrating that disclosure depends critically on context rather than the instructions or probes themselves.

This context-dependence fundamentally challenges how researchers evaluate safety properties. Testing a model’s transparency in financial advisory contexts provides less than expected information about its transparency in medical, legal, or educational contexts. Researchers must explicitly test generalizability of safety evaluations when models exhibit such context-specific behaviors. This leaves two difficult paths forward: either demonstrate generalization empirically by testing across significantly broader domain samples, or explicitly map which specific domains exhibit desirable safety properties and which do not. Either way forward requires comprehensive testing across deployment contexts, moving away from assumptions about principled behavior toward empirical characterization of context-dependent patterns.

4.3.
Safety Properties Fail to Generalize Across Contexts

A growing body of evidence demonstrates that safety and alignment properties in language models fail to generalize across contexts in ways that undermine deployment reliability. Recent work shows that safety training focused on natural language inputs fails to transfer to code domains, with transformed prompts bypassing guardrails over 80% of the time
(
32
)
. Similarly, models struggle to apply established safety facts across domains, with top performers achieving only 58% accuracy despite weak correlation between model capabilities and performance
(
5
)
. Even narrow finetuning on specific tasks can produce broad misalignment in completely unrelated domains
(
4
)
.

This study provides additional evidence for this pattern. Disclosure behavior varies dramatically based on professional domain framing despite identical epistemic probes, with model identity accounting for substantially more variation than parameter count. The findings extend the generalization failure literature from safety guardrails and factual knowledge to self-transparency: models do not learn principled disclosure behavior that applies consistently across professional contexts.

These converging findings have fundamental implications for model development and evaluation. Organizations cannot assume that safety properties tested in some domains will generalize to deployment contexts. Instead, development must shift toward deliberate behavior design: explicitly specifying desired behaviors for specific deployment contexts, training for those properties as distinct objectives, and empirically verifying behavior across deployment domains. This requires treating safety properties as independent training targets rather than assumed byproducts of general capability development or comprehensive training data.

4.4.
Limitations and Future Directions

Important questions remain about scope and mechanisms. Four professional personas were tested with controlled epistemic probes. While this design enabled systematic comparison across models under identical conditions, determining whether the patterns extend to other professional contexts or spontaneous user interactions requires additional research. Testing legal, educational, and technical advisory contexts would clarify whether context-dependence is a general phenomenon or specific to this domain sample.

This study is a behavioral audit of model outputs, not a user study. While the findings demonstrate that models exhibit context-dependent disclosure patterns that create the preconditions for a Reverse Gell-Mann Amnesia effect, this research has not observed users being misled. The claim that users would overgeneralize trust based on observing appropriate disclosure in some domains remains a plausible hypothesis rather than an empirically demonstrated user behavior. Controlled user studies would be needed to confirm whether users exhibit this pattern of trust miscalibration and whether the observed model behavior translates into the predicted user harms.

Evaluation was restricted to open-weight models, which was necessary to measure parameter count and test model size as a predictor. Whether similar patterns exist among frontier closed-source models requires direct empirical testing. The shared training paradigm of transformer-based models with RLHF and safety fine-tuning makes similar context-dependence plausible, though exact performance may differ.

GPT-OSS-120B served as both a subject model and the evaluating judge, introducing potential self-preference bias. We mitigate this concern through high human-judge agreement (
\kappa=0.908
) on a sample stratified across model performance tiers. Additionally, the judge did not rank itself as the top-performing model (ranking 4th at 65.1%), suggesting that any residual self-preference did not dominantly skew the relative rankings.

The observational design identifies that model identity matters far more than scale, but cannot isolate which specific training factors drive disclosure behavior. Controlled training experiments manipulating RLHF weighting, safety fine-tuning data composition, and reasoning integration would determine which interventions produce transparent behavior and whether improvements generalize across professional domains. Such experiments would move from demonstrating that training matters to specifying how to train for transparency.

Future work should pursue four directions. First, test whether context-dependent transparency patterns exist in frontier models and natural deployment settings, addressing questions about scope and generalization. Second, conduct controlled training experiments to identify which specific interventions produce transparent behavior across contexts. Third, develop methods for organizations to systematically test models across deployment domains, moving from post-hoc evaluation to deliberate behavior verification during development. Fourth, investigate whether transparency failures disproportionately affect specific user demographics or whether models modulate honesty based on perceived user characteristics.

5.
Conclusion

When models appropriately disclose AI nature in some professional contexts, users may infer this transparency reflects principled behavior. However, models exhibit sharp domain-specific inconsistency: high disclosure in financial contexts but persistent failures in medical contexts. The harm documented here is not identity deception but epistemic overconfidence: models claiming expertise they cannot possess. As users increasingly rely on AI chatbots across multiple professional domains, this creates calibration transfer risk: successful interactions in familiar domains could create misplaced trust extending to contexts where disclosure fails.

This evaluation reveals three critical implications. First, context-dependent disclosure undermines users’ ability to calibrate trust appropriately. Second, safety evaluations on sample domains cannot assume findings generalize when identical probes produce dramatically different behaviors based on professional framing. Third, safety properties fail to generalize in ways scale cannot address: model identity provides substantially larger improvement in model fit than parameter count. Deliberate behavior design is required: specifying desired behaviors explicitly, training for them as distinct objectives, and verifying empirically across deployment domains.

For organizations deploying models in professional contexts, behavioral evaluation across representative domains is essential. Organizations must test models on specific deployment contexts and treat transparency as a distinct training objective. Some models achieving substantial disclosure demonstrates this is achievable, though room for improvement remains.

Whether similar patterns exist in frontier closed-source models requires direct testing, though the shared training paradigm makes similar context-dependence plausible. The methodological contributions—common-garden evaluation, asynchronous judge interweaving enabling 19,200 trials at $16.66, and Bayesian error propagation—provide tools for systematic behavioral assessment at scale.

As models increasingly advise users in high-stakes domains, organizations must ensure consistent disclosure across contexts. The path forward requires deliberate design targeting transparency as a distinct property, with empirical verification across deployment domains rather than assumptions about scale or generalization.

This research consists of a behavioral audit of open-weight artificial intelligence systems and did not involve human subjects, intervention, or interaction with natural persons. Generation of Harmful Content

No generated content was published to the open web or deployed in user-facing applications where it could be mistaken for genuine professional advice. The goal of eliciting this content was solely to characterize and quantify the failure modes of current safety training.
