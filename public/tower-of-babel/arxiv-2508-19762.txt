BuzzSet v1.0: A Dataset for Pollinator Detection in Field Conditions
Ahmed Emam +, Mohamed Elbassiouny +, Julius Miller, Patrick Donworth, Sabine Seidel, Ribana Roscher

Abstract
Pollinator insects such as honeybees and bumblebees are vital to global food production and ecosystem stability, yet their populations are declining due to increasing anthropogenic and environmental stressors. Supporting scalable, automated pollinator monitoring in real agricultural environments remains an open challenge due to the difficulty of detecting small, fast-moving, and often camouflaged insects. To facilitate research in this area, we introduce BuzzSet v1.0 , a large-scale dataset of high-resolution pollinator images collected under real field conditions. BuzzSet contains 7,856 manually verified and labeled images, with over 8,000 annotated instances across three classes: honeybees, bumblebees, and unidentified insects. Initial annotations were generated using a YOLOv12 [ 1 ] model trained on external data and subsequently refined via human verification using open-source labeling tools. All images were preprocessed into 256 × \times 256 tiles to enhance the detection of small insects. We provide strong baselines using the RF-DETR [ 2 ] transformer-based object detector. The model achieves strong classification accuracy with F1-scores of 0.94 and 0.92 for honeybee and bumblebee classes, respectively, with confusion matrix results showing minimal misclassification between these categories. The unidentified class remains more challenging due to label ambiguity and lower sample frequency, yet still contributes useful insights for robustness evaluation. Overall detection performance (mAP@0.50 of 0.559) illustrates the challenging nature of the dataset and its potential to drive progress in small object detection under realistic ecological conditions. Ongoing work focuses on expanding the dataset to version 2.0 with additional annotations and evaluating further detection strategies. Beyond its immediate ecological applications, BuzzSet establishes a challenging benchmark for small object detection and ecological computer vision. The primary difficulty lies in reliably detecting insects that are frequently camouflaged within natural vegetation, highlighting an open problem for future research to address. Methodology

Data Collection.
We collected RGB images of potential insect pollinators in natural field conditions using static camera setups positioned within various treatments, testing the effect of intercropping maize with flowers attractive for pollinators. The data was recorded across multiple days and included various treatments such as phacelia, crops, and flower-enriched maize intercrops. The cameras captured images at regular intervals with a resolution of 4608
\times
3456 pixels, yielding a ground sampling distance of approximately 0.5  mm. Data acquisition took place during peak foraging hours (11:00–15:00), ensuring high pollinator activity
[
12
]
.

Annotation Pipeline.
To generate initial labels for our dataset with minimal manual effort, we used a weak-labeling approach. Specifically, we trained a YOLOv12 model on publicly available pollinator data from Chong et al.
[
10
]
and applied it to our newly collected images. This model produced weak annotations, which we then systematically verified. We used open-source labeling software,
OpenLabeling

[
9
]
, to inspect and refine the annotations. Each image was manually reviewed: if pollinators were missed, misclassified, or inaccurately localized by the model, we corrected the bounding boxes and class labels accordingly. This ensured that the final annotations were both complete and spatially accurate.

Dataset Statistics.
The final version of the BuzzSet dataset consists of 7856 annotated images, split into 6575 training and 1281 validation images. The dataset includes a total of 6853 honeybee instances, 2129 bumblebees, and 1152 unidentified insects, all annotated with bounding boxes and class labels. To address class ambiguity and improve robustness, visually uncertain instances are grouped into a generic “unidentified” category. This helps balance the dataset and reduce misclassification between closely related insect types.

Model Training.
We trained RF-DETR on the BuzzSet dataset using the annotated training split, which consists of 6575 images. The remaining 1281 images were used for validation. To improve detection of small insects, we preprocessed all images by tiling the original high-resolution inputs (4608
\times
3456 pixels) into smaller 256
\times
256 patches using the SAHI (Slicing Aided Hyper Inference) library
[
13
]
. No additional data augmentation techniques were applied.

Training was performed using the AdamW optimizer with a fixed learning rate of 1e
-4
. A batch size of 3 was used with gradient accumulation over 4 steps, resulting in an effective batch size of 12. No learning rate scheduler was employed. The model was trained for 200 epochs on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU and evaluated on the validation set after convergence.

3
Results

The RF-DETR model was evaluated using mean Average Precision (mAP) and per-class metrics. At IoU=0.50 (VOC-style), the model achieved mAP of 0.559; at 0.75, 0.327; and COCO-style mAP@0.50:0.95 was 0.319, indicating strong detection at moderate thresholds and reasonable performance under stricter criteria.
