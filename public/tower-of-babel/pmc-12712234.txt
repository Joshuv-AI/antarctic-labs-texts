Effects of Multiple Wildfire Smoke Pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, and Ozone) on Respiratory and Cardiovascular Hospitalizations in California (2006–2019)
Authors: Rosana Aguilera, Noemie Letellier, Rupa Basu, Ambarish Vaidyanathan, Joan A Casey, Alexander Gershunov, Minghui Diao, Tarik Benmarhnia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe intensity and frequency of wildfire events in the United States are increasing, with wildfire emissions serving as a major contributor to air pollution, particularly through airborne particulate matter and ground‐level ozone (O3). Wildfire smoke detrimentally impacts human health, and evidence has shown that wildfire fine particulate matter (PM2.5) might be more harmful than other sources of PM2.5 for respiratory conditions. Epidemiological research on health effects of wildfire smoke has mainly focused on PM2.5 and there is limited evidence on the impacts of particulate matter less than or equal to 10 μm in aerodynamic diameter (PM10) and O3. PM2.5, PM10, and O3 might have differential effects on human health based on the source of emissions (smoke vs. non‐smoke air pollution). Here, we first quantified the contribution of wildfires to PM10 and O3 concentrations in California, USA for years 2006–2019, using a statistical modeling framework. R 2 for our PM10 predictive model ranged between 0.68 and 0.78 for prediction using testing and all monitoring sites involved, respectively. Similarly, R 2 values for our ozone predictive model ranged between 0.78 (hold‐out sites) and 0.89 (all sites). Second, we quantified the short‐term impacts of wildfire‐specific PM10 and O3 concentrations, along with wildfire‐specific PM2.5, on hospital admissions for respiratory and cardiovascular illnesses during three different time periods and at various spatial extents (all California ZIP codes and regional analyses). We found heterogeneous effects of wildfire smoke pollutants on different health outcomes and across different regions and periods considered in our study.Keywords: wildfire, air pollution, public health, particulate matter, tropospheric ozone

Introduction

Wildfires are becoming more severe and frequent (Goss et al., 2020; Williams et al., 2019), currently contributing to poor air quality in fire‐prone regions in the United States (McClure & Jaffe, 2018) and beyond (Xu et al., 2023). Impacts of wildfire smoke on air pollution are projected to continue in the context of climate change, fire suppression practices, and the expansion of the wildland‐urban interface (Ford et al., 2018; Neumann et al., 2021; Radeloff et al., 2018; Williams et al., 2019). Wildfire smoke is a complex mixture of pollutants that can trigger rapid‐onset respiratory and cardiovascular symptoms, particularly among sensitive populations (Gould et al., 2024). These acute responses are driven by mechanisms such as airway inflammation, oxidative stress, and vasoconstriction, among others (Hughes et al., 2024; Lei et al., 2024). Evidence has shown that fine particulate matter (PM2.5) related to wildfire events is associated with increased hospitalization rates for respiratory conditions such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (Aguilera et al., 2021; Gan et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2017; Reid et al., 2016; Stowell et al., 2019). The current evidence for cardiovascular morbidity from wildfire smoke exposure remains mixed and mainly focused on PM2.5 (Chen et al., 2021). Further, some studies do not quantify the specific contribution of PM2.5 generated from wildfires, and their results may not represent wildfire‐specific impacts (Jiao et al., 2024).

In addition to PM2.5, wildfire emissions lead to the formation of air pollutants of concern for public health that are regulated under the National Ambient Air Quality Standards, such as airborne particulate matter of different diameter sizes and ground‐level ozone (Brey & Fischer, 2016; Jaffe et al., 2008; Kunzli et al., 2006; Lee & Jaffe, 2024; Pfister et al., 2008; Tarín‐Carrasco et al., 2021). Compared to wildfire PM2.5, significantly less is known about the impacts on human health of other smoke‐ associated air pollutants like O3 and PM10. A few toxicologic studies comparing the physicochemical and toxicological characteristics of PM10 and PM2.5 pollution have suggested that the biological mechanisms of wildfire‐specific particulate matter (PM, both PM10 and PM2.5) and all‐source PM may differ from each other (Verma et al., 2009; Wong et al., 2011).

Materials and Methods

Our study region is the state of California, located on the West Coast in the United States. The data and methodology used in the estimation of daily ZIP code‐ level wildfire‐specific concentrations of air pollutants such as PM2.5 were detailed by Aguilera et al. (2023). The same methodology, a set of machine learning techniques briefly described in the following section and summarized in Figure S1 of the Supporting Information S1, was applied here to estimate daily wildfire‐specific PM10 and ozone concentrations across ZIP codes in California spanning over 14 years (2006–2019).

Wildfire‐Specific Pollution Estimation: PM10 and Ozone

Daily, ZIP Code Level PM10 and Ozone Concentrations: All Sources (Non‐Smoke and Smoke)

We used in situ PM10 and ozone measurements (2006–2019) from the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Air Quality System (AQS) (https://www.epa.gov/aqs). The AQS network includes both continuous daily monitoring and 24‐hr sampling on a 1‐in‐6 days, 1‐in‐3 days, and everyday schedule. Measurements were taken from California monitoring sites (n = 190 for PM10 and n = 240 for ozone). These daily concentrations, coming from monitoring station data, represent PM10 and ozone from all sources, including traffic and industrial emissions, wildfire smoke, among and other emissions.

We considered several potential explanatory variables for the estimation of PM10 and ozone in our study region (Table S1 in Supporting Information S1). Time‐varying variables such as satellite‐derived aerosol properties and fixed properties such as elevation are described in detail in Aguilera et al. (2023). All values for response and explanatory variables were extracted at the location of AQS monitoring sites, and at population‐weighted centroids for the estimation of ZIP code level pollution. In addition, we included information for explanatory variables such as county, air basin within California (https://ww2.arb.ca.gov/applications/emissions‐air‐basin), day of the week, month, season, and latitude and longitude coordinates.

We assembled daily values for the response variables (observed PM10 and ozone) along with explanatory variables at each air quality monitoring site across California. We first set aside data from four monitoring sites (three in common between PM10 and ozone), selected to represent distinct areas of the study region, as an independent test set to evaluate our model's ability to generalize to new locations. From the remaining monitoring sites, we used between 70% and 75% of the data for model training and 30%–25% for validation. This validation subset supported 10‐fold cross‐validation, which involved repeatedly splitting the training data into 90% for fitting and 10% for tuning the model's hyperparameters. The 10‐fold random cross‐validation approach has been widely adopted in previous research, including studies by Reid et al. (2019, 2021), Wang et al. (2022), K. Do et al. (2024), and V. Do et al. (2024) demonstrating its utility in model evaluation across different contexts. In addition, we also tested sub‐setting all data from available monitoring sites into 70% for training and 15% for validation and testing, respectively.

We ran three base learner algorithms (Deep Learning, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting; described in Aguilera et al., 2023) and then stacked these into an ensemble model to generate estimates of PM10 and ozone for prediction testing and to eventually estimate daily concentrations at the ZIP code level. To run the machine learning algorithms, we used H2O (Cook, 2016), an open‐source big data platform, to achieve higher performance and reduce processing time in our analysis using R software (version 4.0.3; R Core Team, 2020). Specifically, training and data processing is done in the high‐performance H2O cluster rather than in R memory on a local computer.

We trained these models individually on all response (PM10 and ozone) and explanatory variables, with the selection of optimal parameters of each machine learning algorithm guided by both cartesian grid‐search and prior expert knowledge. We validated our base learner models with k‐fold cross‐validation (k = 10), a standard method for estimating the performance of a machine learning algorithm on a data set. In k‐fold cross‐validation, the original data set is divided into groups of observations (or folds) of approximately equal size, whereas the first fold is treated as a validation set (i.e., the set that is held out), while the remainder constitutes the training set (James et al., 2013). The prediction error is estimated on the held‐out folds.

As a sensitivity test, we implemented a spatial cross‐validation strategy, consistent with recommendations in previous studies (Childs et al., 2022; van Donkelaar et al., 2024). We first randomly selected 5% of monitoring sites to serve as a distinct test set, ensuring that no data from these sites contributed to model training. The remaining 95% of sites were used to create the training set. To further enforce spatial independence during model evaluation, we applied 10‐fold spatial cross‐validation on the training data, grouping folds by site so that all observations from a given site were assigned to the same fold. This approach avoids inflated performance estimates associated with random cross‐validation by ensuring complete site‐level separation between folds.

Daily, ZIP Code Level PM10 and Ozone Concentrations: Contribution of Wildfire Smoke

Similarly to our previous work on wildfire smoke PM2.5 (Aguilera et al., 2023), once we obtained the PM10 and ozone concentrations above, which represent emissions from all sources, we isolated the wildfire‐specific concentrations in California ZIP codes and days exposed to smoke. First, exposure to smoke was determined by the overlap of a given ZIP code area on a given day with smoke plume polygons obtained from the NOAA Hazard Mapping System (HMS; Ruminski et al., 2006). The HMS product uses visible satellite imagery and trained satellite analyst skills to estimate the spatial extent of smoke, though it cannot discern whether a given plume is at ground level or higher in the atmosphere (Rolph et al., 2009). However, HMS smoke plumes remain a common binary metric used to determine if smoke is present in the atmospheric column on a given day (Lipner et al., 2019).

Second, we applied a multiple imputation approach using the R‐package missRanger (Mayer & Mayer, 2019), which iteratively fits random forest models to impute non‐smoke concentrations for a given ZIP code and day categorized as exposed to wildfire smoke. This step provided estimates of background air pollution (for PM10 and ozone, individually) unrelated to wildfire smoke. We imputed the non‐smoke (background) concentrations by using the concentrations available for unexposed ZIP code days and covariates such as day of the week, month of the year and year. Lastly, we subtracted the imputed counterfactual, non‐smoke concentrations (i.e., what would be measured in the absence of wildfire smoke) from the all‐source PM10 or ozone concentrations estimated in the first step (Section 2.1.1; Figure S1 in Supporting Information S1).

Respiratory and Cardiovascular Health Outcomes

We calculated the ZIP code‐specific daily sum of unscheduled hospitalizations by cause using the Patient Discharge Data and Emergency Department Data collected by the California Department of Health Care Access and Information (HCAI; https://hcai.ca.gov/). The data set provided ZIP code information only, though based on the patients' residential address (unavailable due to privacy constraints). We estimated the counts of respiratory (n = 18,185,219) and cardiovascular (n = 8,619,101; Table S2 in Supporting Information S1) hospitalizations based on primary diagnosis International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes (see Table S3 in Supporting Information S1 for the list of codes included). Counts were converted to rates of hospital admissions by dividing the counts by the ZIP code population (based on Census 2010 data). Table S4 in Supporting Information S1 further characterizes the respiratory and cardiovascular health outcomes described above by aggregating across our study regions as well as population characteristics such as age, sex and race/ethnicity.

Fixed Effects Regression Model

We used ZIP code fixed effects Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression models to quantify the individual effects of the association between daily wildfire PM2.5, PM10, and ozone, separately, and daily rates of respiratory and cardiovascular hospital admissions. The following controls were included: daily wind velocity, daily minimum and maximum temperature, specific humidity, day‐of‐week effects, month‐of‐year effects, a linear time trend, and ZIP code fixed effects. Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regressions were implemented with the plm R‐package for panel data (Croissant & Millo, 2008).

Each model was run separately for three different time periods: (a) all months of the year, (b) all months excluding summer (defined as June, July and August, or JJA) and (c) summer months only. In addition, models were also run for different regions considering all ZIP codes in California (where population ≥1,000 inhabitants, n ZIP codes = 1,417) and those contained within individual air basin groups (Figure S2 in Supporting Information S1). As expected, sample size varied not only by the time periods considered but also by region (i.e., air basin grouping). By decreasing order, the number of ZIP codes comprised in each region were: SoCal Coastal (600), NorCal Coastal (339), Central Valley (303), Desert/Inland (92), and NorCal Inland (83). As a sensitivity analysis, we fit ZIP code fixed effects generalized linear models with a quasi‐Poisson distribution using the feglm function from the fixest R package (Bergé, 2018). The log of ZIP code population was included as an offset. As in the case of the OLS models above, these models were applied to assess associations between wildfire smoke pollutants and hospitalization rates across all ZIP codes in California. Results are presented in Supporting Information S1.

Results

Estimated Wildfire PM10 and Ozone Concentrations

Stacking all three base learners in our ensemble model for PM10 resulted in a prediction R 2 of 0.78 for all sites and 0.68 for the testing data set (Table 1). Model performance metrics for base learners for PM10 and ozone are shown in Tables S4 and S5 of the Supporting Information S1, respectively. Similar results were obtained when choosing a hold‐out testing data set of 4 sites (Table S7 in Supporting Information S1). The ensemble model appears to underpredict high PM10 concentrations (roughly those >300 μg m3), as seen in the comparison between observed and predicted PM10 at monitoring sites across California (Figure S3 in Supporting Information S1). However, like in most statistical approaches, it is expected to underpredict at very high values (Reid et al., 2021), particularly since these PM10 concentrations might be associated with episodic events such as wildfires or wind‐related resuspension and transport of fine and coarse particles. The ensemble model for ozone concentrations, on the other hand, provided a higher prediction capability, with a prediction R 2 of 0.89 for all sites and 0.78 for the hold‐out test data set (Table 1, Figure S3 in Supporting Information S1). Nearly identical results were obtained when using a randomized testing data set (Table S7 in Supporting Information S1). On the other hand, spatial cross‐validation generally resulted in lower performance compared to random cross‐validation approaches across most base learner models, with increased RMSE and decreased R‐squared values, particularly for PM10 (Table S7 in Supporting Information S1). However, the ensemble model performed relatively consistently with both random and spatial cross‐validation approaches, with only a slight decrease in RMSE for PM10 (from 11.2 to 10.35 μg m−3) and a small R‐squared improvement from 0.78 to 0.81.

The highest mean concentrations for wildfire‐specific PM10 are observed in central California and in desert areas in the southern part of the state. In addition, maximum wildfire‐specific PM10 concentrations reflect the major wildfire events that took place in Northern California and in coastal areas in Southern California. The seasonality of wildfire‐specific concentrations differs between PM10 and ozone and varies across regions in California. Figure S4 in Supporting Information S1 shows mean concentrations throughout the year within our 14‐year study period. Mean wildfire PM10 concentrations peaked during late fall and early winter months in central and northern regions of the state, while mean wildfire PM10 was higher in coastal Southern California in early spring (March and April). In contrast, the seasonality of peak wildfire‐specific ozone concentrations appears to be consistent across regions, with higher values occurring mainly during the warmer months (May through September).

Impacts on Health Outcomes

Respiratory Hospital Admissions

Table 2 summarizes our main results for the effects of wildfire air pollutants (PM10, PM2.5, and ozone) on respiratory hospital admissions in California over 14 years (2006–2019). Positive associations (i.e., increasing rates of hospital admissions with increasing concentration of wildfire smoke pollutants) were found primarily during the period that excluded the summer months (June, July, August) for wildfire‐specific PM10 and PM2.5. On the other hand, during the summer period, wildfire‐specific ozone was positively associated with respiratory admissions (rates per 100,000) in all California ZIP codes considered. Similar results were obtained when running our sensitivity analysis using generalized linear models with a quasi‐Poisson distribution and respiratory hospital admissions (counts) as the outcomes (Table S6 in Supporting Information S1), though positive associations were found for more periods for wildfire PM10 and PM2.5.

Discussion

Wildfire severity and risk in California and beyond will likely intensify and seasonally broaden in a warming future (Dong et al., 2022; Goss et al., 2020; Williams et al., 2019), further contributing to elevated air pollution levels, including PM2.5, PM10, and ozone in California and other fire‐prone regions. Therefore, isolating the contribution of wildfire smoke from other sources, such as traffic and industrial emissions, on air pollution is helpful to quantify wildfire‐specific health impacts. Using multisource and readily available data integrated into an ensemble machine learning framework that allowed us to capture temporal and spatial variability of daily PM10 and ozone that was generated by wildfires across California ZIP codes.

Our findings showed a heterogeneous response of the effects of air pollutants (PM2.5, PM10 and ozone) attributable to wildfire smoke across regions in California and during different time periods. Regional variation in the health impacts of wildfire smoke can be attributed to factors such as differential exposure, population vulnerability, and resource availability (Thilakaratne et al., 2023). Overall, we have observed that wildfire smoke pollution beyond PM2.5 impacts public health beyond PM2.5 emissions. Our results also reflected impacts on both respiratory and cardiovascular hospital admissions for wildfire PM10, and for respiratory admissions in the presence of wildfire ozone. Population characteristics such as age distribution, socioeconomic status, and the prevalence of pre‐existing health conditions may influence how individuals and communities respond to wildfire smoke exposure (K. Do et al., 2024; V. Do et al., 2024). These factors, along with differences in healthcare access and hospital utilization patterns, likely contribute to the observed variation in public health impacts among regions and time periods in our study. Finally, differences in exposure measurement accuracy, driven by factors like monitor density, may further explain spatial variation in the observed associations. These factors likely interact in complex ways, influencing the health burden of wildfire smoke in diverse ways across California.

Wildfire regimes vary by region across California (Dong et al., 2022; Kalashnikov et al., 2022; Swain, 2021; Turco et al., 2023), and the wildfire season is not limited exclusively to the warmer months (May–October), the typical period considered in many epidemiological studies (Heaney et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2017). The largest wildfire that occurred in SoCal in recorded history was the Thomas Fire that burned for an entire month through most of December 2017 into January 2018 (https://www.fire.ca.gov/incidents/2017/12/4/thomas‐fire/). Our heterogeneous findings on the effects of wildfire smoke pollutants across different regions and time season periods of year suggests that restricting epidemiologic analyses to a hypothetical the wildfire season or considering to a global period, as well as considering only entire regions like California or the Western United States, might result in missed opportunities to observe more local public‐health impacts of different pollutants attributable to wildfire smoke on public health.

Though the focus of previous epidemiological studies has been PM2.5, a few studies have examined the impacts of wildfire PM10 on public health. A retrospective cohort study in Canada, and a time‐series study in Australia, both reported associations with all‐cause respiratory admissions and wildfire PM10 (Henderson et al., 2011; Morgan et al., 2010). More recently, Tarín‐Carrasco et al. (2021) found a positive correlation between burned area and PM10 related to wildfires in Portugal and mortality. In our current work, we detected impacts of wildfire‐specific PM10 on both respiratory and cardiovascular outcomes (i.e., hospital admissions). Regarding ozone in wildfire smoke, several studies have shown the effects on public health. For instance, Reid et al. (2019) reported an association between ozone and respiratory emergency visits during a wildfire, though associations became statistically non‐significant when adjusted for PM2.5. A recent global study by Chen et al. (2024) observed an increase in all‐cause and respiratory mortality associated with short‐term exposure to wildfire‐related O3. Overall, the current evidence for cardiovascular morbidity from exposure to wildfire smoke remains mixed and mainly focused on PM2.5 (Chen et al., 2021).

Our current study estimates, for the first time, the health effects of wildfire smoke considering multiple pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, and ground‐level ozone). We focused on these pollutants given the data availability across California and since they are regulated under the Clean Air Act. Our results show that by focusing on PM2.5 only, researchers may not capture the full extent of the health burden caused by wildfire emissions. On the other hand, focusing solely on PM and ground‐level ozone still does not capture the complexity of what constitutes wildfire smoke and its potentially harmful effects on humans. Further scientific examination, especially where more components of wildfire smoke are considered, can help with understanding impacts on public health. Studies examining the composition of wildfire smoke have shown that wildfire smoke can be composed of several chemicals and microorganisms (Cascio, 2018; Kobziar et al., 2022). For instance, the chemical composition of PM2.5 found in wildfire smoke emissions differs from PM2.5 from other sources (Black et al., 2017; Verma et al., 2009). Sparks and Wagner (2021) showed that higher concentrations of heavy metals such as Cu, Zn, and Pb were found in wildfire PM, compared to non‐wildfire PM, and Alves et al. (2011) identified an increase in organic carbon in wildfire PM. Other studies have found that wildfire smoke contributes to exposure to polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) even at large distances from the wildfire location (Ghetu et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2024). This is particularly relevant to the densely populated California coast, where downslope winds blow smoke into the sloping backcountry (Abatzoglou et al., 2023). In addition, a growing literature documents the importance of bioaerosols, associated with wildfire smoke, in the context of impacts on ecosystems and public health (Kobziar & Thompson, 2020; Kobziar et al., 2022). Therefore, including bioaerosols and other pollutants (besides PM and ground‐level O3) in epidemiological studies of wildfire smoke effects becomes imperative.

We acknowledge that our approach to quantify concentrations of air pollutants attributable to wildfire smoke has limitations. For instance, the number and extent of smoke plumes used to categorize exposed ZIP code days represent a conservative estimate due to the limitations of visible satellite data (e.g., impacts of cloud cover on smoke detection and consideration of the entire atmospheric column). In addition to all the above, the way we defined definition of wildfire smoke exposure may have caused some misclassification, to a small extent, of smoke concentrations as non‐smoke concentrations and vice versa. Related issues that can arise are the occurrence of negative values in the estimation of wildfire‐specific concentrations, which can occur when most ZIP codes on a given day (or consecutive days) are covered by smoke (though these cases are rare). In the case of ground‐level ozone estimation, we acknowledge the omission of precursors such as NO2 or VOCs as predictors, mainly due to data availability. While satellite‐derived and reanalysis products for NO2 and VOCs have improved substantially in recent years, their temporal coverage and spatial resolution did not align with those in our study. Future work may benefit from incorporating these data sets as retrospective products mature, or in shorter‐term modeling efforts.

Other study limitations include using patient residential ZIP code to estimate exposures and using community‐level air pollution concentrations to assess and quantify individual wildfire pollution exposures. We used hospitalizations as our outcome, representing a severe outcome following wildfire smoke exposure. Results may differ when considering urgent care or emergency department visits or a less severe end point, such as rescue inhaler use. In addition, it is also important to explore whether associations between wildfire smoke exposure and health outcomes differ by key demographic or clinical characteristics, such as age, sex, or the presence of pre‐existing cardiorespiratory conditions. Identifying potentially vulnerable subgroups could help inform targeted public health interventions during wildfire events. We also did not consider any lagged effects, though emerging literature suggests that the health impacts of smoke exposure, particularly respiratory and cardiovascular outcomes, may not be immediate (Doubleday et al., 2023; Magzamen et al., 2021). While our analysis primarily focuses on same‐day associations, future work should incorporate distributed lag models to better capture the temporal dynamics of these potential delayed health effects (Gould et al., 2024). This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the full burden of wildfire smoke‐related hospitalizations.

Lastly, we evaluated the association between each pollutant individually, acknowledging that correlations between pollutants, particularly PM10 and PM2.5, may complicate interpretation. Future work should consider multi‐pollutant models or mixture methods to assess the joint impacts of wildfire‐specific PM2.5, PM10, and ozone, helping to better isolate their unique contributions to health outcomes.

As wildfire smoke becomes more prevalent and health burden from smoke exposure continues to increase (Gould et al., 2024; Kobziar et al., 2024), considering the complex nature of wildfire smoke composition in future epidemiological studies becomes increasingly important. Here, we examined the impacts of three pollutants typically associated with wildfire emissions and found a heterogeneous response on different health outcomes and across different time periods and regions in our study area (California, USA). Moving forward, studies should include a more diverse range of pollutants and aeroallergens that have been associated with wildfire smoke, to unravel any compound or differential effects on ecosystem and human health in fire‐prone regions and beyond.

Wildfire‐specific PM2.5 concentrations for California ZIP codes used in this study are publicly available (Aguilera et al., 2023; https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envint.2022.107719). Estimated wildfire‐specific PM10 and O3 concentrations can be accessed in this repository (Aguilera & Benmarhnia, 2025; https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/FYW1VU). Respiratory and cardiovascular hospital admissions data are not publicly available to protect patient privacy, but access to the health outcome data can be requested directly from the California Department of Health Care Access and Information website (HCAI, https://hcai.ca.gov/).
