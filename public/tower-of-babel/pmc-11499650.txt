Automated deep learning-based bone mineral density assessment for opportunistic osteoporosis screening using various CT protocols with multi-vendor scanners
Authors: Heejun Park, Woo Young Kang, Ok Hee Woo, Jemyoung Lee, Zepa Yang, Sangseok Oh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This retrospective study examined the diagnostic efficacy of automated deep learning-based bone mineral density (DL-BMD) measurements for osteoporosis screening using 422 CT datasets from four vendors in two medical centers, encompassing 159 chest, 156 abdominal, and 107 lumbar spine datasets. DL-BMD values on L1 and L2 vertebral bodies were compared with manual BMD (m-BMD) measurements using Pearson’s correlation and intraclass correlation coefficients. Strong agreement was found between m-BMD and DL-BMD in total CT scans (r = 0.953, p < 0.001). The diagnostic performance of DL-BMD was assessed using receiver operating characteristic analysis for osteoporosis and low BMD by dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and m-BMD. Compared to DXA, DL-BMD demonstrated an AUC of 0.790 (95% CI 0.733–0.839) for low BMD and 0.769 (95% CI 0.710–0.820) for osteoporosis, with sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of 80.8% (95% CI 74.2–86.3%), 56.3% (95% CI 43.4–68.6%), and 74.3% (95% CI 68.3–79.7%) for low BMD and 65.4% (95% CI 50.9–78.0%), 70.9% (95% CI 63.8–77.3%), and 69.7% (95% CI 63.5–75.4%) for osteoporosis, respectively. Compared to m-BMD, DL-BMD showed an AUC of 0.983 (95% CI 0.973–0.993) for low BMD and 0.972 (95% CI 0.958–0.987) for osteoporosis, with sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of 97.3% (95% CI 94.5–98.9%), 85.2% (95% CI 78.8–90.3%), and 92.7% (95% CI 89.7–95.0%) for low BMD and 94.4% (95% CI 88.3–97.9%), 89.5% (95% CI 85.6–92.7%), and 90.8% (95% CI 87.6–93.4%) for osteoporosis, respectively. The DL-based method can provide accurate and reliable BMD assessments across diverse CT protocols and scanners.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73709-w.

Introduction

Osteoporosis, characterized by reduced bone mass and microarchitecture deterioration, increases fragility and fracture risk1, particularly in the elderly, including postmenopausal women and men2. This poses global health concerns among the aging population. Clinical screening for osteoporosis is crucial, and one approach to enhance osteoporosis evaluation involves the opportunistic use of computed tomography (CT) scans obtained during routine patient care3. Dual X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and quantitative CT (QCT) are commonly used for bone mineral density (BMD) measurements4. DXA, the global standard, provides areal density results in two dimensions1, while QCT measures volumetric trabecular bone density without superimposition, offering advantages in early osteoporosis screening without oral contrast or specific positioning2. However, QCT has limitations such as the use of standardized software and a dedicated phantom, the need for strict calibration, and the requirement of manual input and a higher radiation dose compared to DXA.

Deep learning (DL) is becoming prevalent in medical imaging analysis, and it is currently undergoing rapid advancement5–8. In terms of osteoporosis, recent efforts have explored the application of artificial intelligence (AI) solutions, especially for opportunistic screening of osteoporosis9–14. Several studies have employed DL techniques for BMD measurements using opportunistic CT images with QCT values as the reference standard11,13–15. Pan et al. developed a U-net model and linear function for automatic BMD measurement using low-dose chest CT scans11. Fang et al. presented a deep learning model for automatic segmentation and BMD calculation of lumbar vertebral bodies of abdomen or spine CTs15. Oh et al. examined the diagnostic efficacy of DL-based automated BMD measurements for opportunistic osteoporosis screening using routine CT scans16. The findings exhibited high diagnostic performance and excellent agreement with QCT values on L1 and L2 vertebrae across various clinical CT scans. However, this DL model evaluation was limited to data from a single CT scanner and a single institution. While many studies have explored AI applications for detecting or classifying osteoporosis, most of them were limited to single-center designs, restricted patient samples, and a lack of validation in real clinical settings. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the performance of a DL model for osteoporosis assessments employing diverse CT protocols with multiple scanners across different medical centers.

Results

Patient characteristics

In this study, 420 patients (mean age, 60.32 ± 15.43 years) with 422 clinical CT scans (159 chest CT, 156 abdomen CT, 107 spine CT) were included, as presented in Table 1. Based on the DXA value, the patients with DXA (N, numbers = 241) in Guro Hospital (N = 189) and Ansan Hospital (N = 52) were classified into normal (64, 32.2%), osteopenia (124, 62.6%), and osteoporosis (53, 26.8%). Table 2 (Supplement 3) presents the BMD data based on both DL-BMD and central DXA. Among the 241 CT scans, 61 were classified as normal, 91 as osteopenia, and 89 as osteoporosis, with a total of 180 as low BMD based on DL-BMD.

Performance of DL-based automated (QCT) volumetric BMD measurement

In the comparison of m-BMD and DL-BMD values in the total CT data, the Pearson coefficient and ICC value were 0.953 (p < 0.001) and 0.972 (p < 0.001), respectively. This strong agreement was confirmed by the individual vendor and CT protocols with Pearson coefficient (r min = 0.855, r max = 0.993, p < 0.001) and ICC value (r min = 0.859, r max = 0.995, p < 0.001) (Table 3). In the ROC analysis using DXA as the reference standard, the AUC of m-BMD for the low-BMD and osteoporosis patients was 0.795 (95% CI 0.739–0.844) and 0.755 (95% CI 0.696–0.808), respectively (Fig. 1; Table 4). The sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of m-BMD for low BMD were 79.1% (95% CI 72.4–84.8%), 64.1% (95% CI 51.1–75.7%), and 75.1% (95% CI 69.1–80.4%), and those for osteoporosis were 59.6% (95% CI 45.1–73.0%), 76.2% (95% CI 69.5–82.1%), and 72.6% (95% CI 66.5–78.1%), respectively (Table 4, Supplement 4). Similarly, the AUC of DL-BMD for the low-BMD and osteoporosis patients was 0.790 (95% CI 0.733–0.839) and 0.769 (95% CI 0.710–0.820), respectively (Fig. 1). The sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of DL-BMD for low BMD were 80.8% (95% CI 74.2–86.3%), 56.3% (95% CI 43.4–68.6%), and 74.3% (95% CI 68.3–79.7%), and those for osteoporosis were 65.4% (95% CI 50.9–78.0%), 70.9% (95% CI 63.8–77.3%), and 69.7% (95% CI 63.5–75.4%), respectively (Table 4, Supplement 5). The difference in the AUC between m-BMD and DL-BMD for diagnosing low-BMD and osteoporosis was not statistically significant, with p = 0.677 and p = 0.246, respectively (Fig. 1). When considering m-BMD as the gold standard, the AUC of DL-BMD for low BMD and osteoporosis diagnoses was 0.983 (95% CI 0.973–0.993) and 0.972 (95% CI 0.958–0.987), respectively (Fig. 2; Table 5). The sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of DL-BMD for low BMD were 97.3% (95% CI 94.5–98.9%), 85.2% (95% CI 78.8–90.3%), and 92.7% (95% CI 89.7–95.0%), and those for osteoporosis were 94.4% (95% CI 88.3–97.9%), 89.5% (95% CI 85.6–92.7%), and 90.8% (95% CI 87.6–93.4%), respectively (Table 5). Such results were observed not only over all vendors, but also by individual vendor.

Discussion

This study was motivated by paucity of multicenter and multivendor studies despite the abundance of recent research on osteoporosis employing DL techniques. Our results demonstrated that the automated DL-based BMD measurement system was highly correlated and concordant with BMD values derived from QCT by multiple vendors from different hospitals, regardless of the type of clinical CT. Furthermore, based on BMD values derived from QCT, the DL system exhibited high diagnostic performance and accuracy in the evaluation of osteoporosis, consistent with the findings of Oh et al.16.

As with the prior study by Oh et al., the diagnostic performance of m-BMD was relatively low when using the central DXA value as the baseline, especially when diagnosing osteoporosis rather than low BMD. This could be attributed to the limitations of DXA, which is influenced by soft tissue overlay, artifacts from medical devices, and especially degenerative change including lumbar spondylosis or abdominal aortic calcification, contributing to the overestimation of osteoporosis diagnosis and reduction of the sensitivity of DXA. In our 241 CT scans obtained with concurrent DXA data, 45 scans (18.7%) indicated osteoporosis based on m-BMD but not with central DXA. 42 scans (93.3%) indicated lumbar spine (L1–L4) osteophytes and facet arthrosis, with 19 scans (42.2%) revealing endplate sclerosis, 12 scans (26.7%) presenting abdominal aortic calcifications anterior to the lumbar vertebral body, and 1 scan (2.2%) displaying focal sclerosis. Among these, 41 CT scans (91.1%) exhibited concordant diagnoses with DL-BMD. This has been corroborated in earlier studies1,17–20 and indicates that QCT is more sensitive in evaluating osteoporosis in patients with factors leading to overestimation of BMD on DXA.

According to a recent systematic review, osteoporosis classification with AI using CT images revealed a wide range of diagnostic accuracy and performance, with AUCs ranging from 0.582 to 0.994 21. In this study, we opted for QCT as the reference standard for BMD measurements instead of CT numbers because CT numbers could be affected by scanner characteristics, scan parameters, and other factors22. We established a conversion model designed for transformation of CT numbers into BMD values adjusted by tube voltage, kernel, and contrast material, resulting in strong correlation and high similarity16. In addition, recent studies using QCT as the reference value demonstrated consistent results. Niu et al. utilized a 3D DL system to predict BMD values, achieving an R² of 0.967 and AUC values of 0.984 and 0.993 for osteoporosis and low BMD, respectively, similar to this study13. In the multicenter and multivendor study of Peng et al. using DL, Pearson’s correlation test and ROC analysis also exhibited excellent correlation and diagnostic performance, concordant with the results of this study14.

This study has several strengths. We applied a U-Net network to analyze routine CT data collected from different vendors and centers with diverse scanning protocols in the presence or absence of intravenous contrast agents, emulating the inherent diversity encountered in routine clinical scenarios. Using this DL-based technique, we could reproduce consistent results in a larger sample size with high variabilities. This reinforces the potential of generalizability of the DL-based method across diverse institutions, encompassing heterogeneous samples and imaging protocols and facilitating the implementation of opportunistic osteoporosis screenings.

There are limitations to this retrospective study. First, because the L2 vertebral body is frequently excluded in routine clinical chest CT scans, most QCT values of chest CT scans were calculated by averaging T12 and L1 instead of L1 and L2. Sebro et al. reported that the CT attenuation of T12 highly correlated with that of lumbar vertebra, and the CT attenuation of T12 was anticipated to serve as a predictive marker of osteoporosis23. Second, while chest CT and spine CT scans were conducted using non-enhanced data, abdominal CT scans from one institution used contrast media, potentially introducing variability in the QCT values even though we adjusted for the impact of the contrast agent using the HU-BMD conversion model. Third, we were able to obtain DXA results for only a subset of patients in Ansan dataset. As a result, the findings may not fully capture the variability in diagnostic outcomes that could occur with complete comparative data. Future research should focus on acquiring complete comparative DXA results across multiple scanners and hospitals to enhance the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Finally, the reduced sample size may limit the statistical power of our findings and potentially affect the generalizability of the study results. However, the equal representation from each subgroup ensures that our findings, while based on a smaller dataset, are reflective of a broader, heterogeneous population.

In conclusion, the automated DL-based method can achieve accurate and reliable BMD assessments in multivendor datasets using diverse CT protocols in different medical centers. Therefore, the DL tool provides high consistency and diagnostic performance, making it suitable for broad application in opportunistic osteoporosis screenings.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Korea University Guro and Ansan Hospitals (2021GR0370 and 2021AS0253), which waived the requirement for written informed consent. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the committee of Korea University Guro and Ansan hospitals, and with the Declaration of Helsinki.

In this study, we collected CT scans of patients using data from four vendors in two medical centers. The CT protocols included non-enhanced chest CT, abdominal CT with or without contrast enhancement, and non-enhanced spine CT, all of which encompassed images from the L1 to L2 vertebrae.

In a query of patients registered between October 2021 and April 2022 in the two vendors at the database of Korea University Guro Hospital, we identified 503 individuals with available DXA within a maximal interval of 12 months from CT examination. These images were acquired using a 320 slice CT scanner (Aquilion ONE, Canon) and a 384 slice CT scanner (SOMATOM Force, Siemens Healthcare). The abdominal CT images all were contrast enhanced. For the data from the two vendors in the database of Korea University Ansan Hospital from October 2022 to January 2023, 620 patients with CT images were identified. These images were acquired using a 256-slice CT scanner (Revolution CT ES; GE Healthcare) or a 256-slice CT scanner (iQon; Philips Healthcare). Among these datasets, 52 individuals who underwent DXA within a maximum interval of 12 months from the CT examination were identified. Exclusion criteria were patients who had undergone operations, those with compression fractures, and those with osteolytic lesions such as bone metastasis or focal sclerotic lesions at the L1 or L2 vertebrae, retrieving from the patient’s record. From the data from each hospital, subjects for this analysis were selected using a structured sampling method to ensure representativeness across different CT scanners and protocols. A final total of 420 patients with 422 CT scans was enrolled in this study (Fig. 3). The scanning parameters for each CT protocol are outlined in Supplement 4.

Central DXA measurement

Central DXA measurements were conducted using a Hologic Horizon W scanner (S/N 301367 M) from Hologic Inc., Bedford, MA, USA, equipped with APEX 13.6.0.5 software. Scans of the L1 to L4 vertebrae and the hip were performed with patients in the supine position. T-scores were calculated by comparing the results to a reference population of healthy young women at their peak bone mass. Osteoporosis was defined as T-score ≤ − 2.5 SD, and osteopenia was defined as − 2.5 < T-score < − 1 SD24. Central DXA measurements were conducted for all 189 included data points at Guro Hospital, while DXA data were available for 52 individuals at Ansan Hospital.

Conventional QCT BMD measurements

Volumetric BMD was extracted from clinical CT scans in two vertebrae from T12 to L2, and the measurements were averaged. The average of L1–L2 is the standard reportable metric. The T12 vertebra was used only in situations where the L2 vertebra was not included in chest CT scans. This practice was derived from a previously published study that presented T12 as the reference value in instances where lesions, such as tumors or sclerosis, were observed in the L1 or L2 vertebrae11.

QCT software (QCT Pro Version 6.1, Model 4 CT Calibration Phantom; MindWays Software Inc, Austin, TX, USA) was utilized for the post-processing of CT images to perform asynchronous measurements of volumetric BMD. Following the guidelines outlined by the American College of Radiology (ACR)25, osteoporosis, osteopenia, and normal were characterized by v-BMD values < 80 mg/cm3, 80–120 mg/cm3, and > 120 mg/cm3, respectively. Each hospital was equipped with only a single unit. This restriction necessitated a sequential scanning approach, where the software was moved from one CT scanner to another, allowing the collection of data across different machine types and protocols in a phased manner. QCT BMD measurements were conducted for all included data in Guro and Ansan Hospitals.

DL-BMD software development

The DL model for Automatic Bone Mineral Density (DL-BMD) measurements operated in two phases: segmenting the spine and identifying the region of interest (ROI) to calculate BMD16. Unlike a previous study, which used only lumbar spine segmentation, we needed to measure to the thoracic spine region, so a new DL model was implemented to segment the thoracic spine. In addition, since it had to operate on data from four vendors, we collected additional training data and preprocessed the training data with CT denoising (ClariCT.AI, ClariPi.INC) and kernel normalization techniques with various filters.

Datasets for model training and validation

We employed 579 cases consisting of 239,368 axial reconstructed CT images, each accompanied by associated binary spine masks. For thoracolumbar spine segmentation, these images were allocated into training and validation datasets, with the training dataset comprising 70% of the images (167,558 slices) and the validation dataset consisting of the remaining 30% (71,810 slices).

The study utilized 324 chest and abdominal CT scans extracted from the VerSe2020 public dataset26. Theses scans were obtained using the Philips Brilliance 64, iCT, and IQon, as well as the Siemens SOMATOM Definition AS and AS + CT scanners. The imaging parameters included an exposure of 120 kVp, a slice thickness ranging between 0.9 mm and 2.0 mm, and a pixel spacing that varied from 0.81 × 0.81 to 0.88 × 0.88. The cases were categorized based on DXA results into three groups: 83 cases (25.6% of the total) were classified as normal, 57 cases (17.6%) as osteopenia, and 184 cases (56.8%) as osteoporosis.

We also utilized 39 non-enhanced chest CT images, 66 abdominal CT images, and 150 contrast-enhanced abdominal CT images from the CTSpine1K open dataset27. These images varied in exposure from 100 to 140 kVp. The slice thickness of these scans was between 1.0 mm and 7.5 mm, with pixel spacing ranging from 0.77 × 0.77 to 1.55 × 1.55.

Preprocessing for two spine segmentation models

We used two segmentation networks: one for the thoracic spine (T-spine) and another for the lumbar spine (L-spine). The T-spine segmentation model was employed for the T12 vertebra, while the L-spine segmentation model was utilized for the L1 and L2 vertebrae. For all training datasets, the L-spine region was originally labeled in a way that the labels increased one by one, such as L1 = 20, L2 = 21, and so on. Conversely, the T-spine region was labeled in the opposite manner, with labels decreasing one by one, such as T12 = 19, T11 = 18, and so forth. In the T-spine segmentation, lumbar vertebrae were re-labeled as background (0), and all thoracic vertebrae were modified to the label of 1. Conversely, in the L-spine segmentation, thoracic vertebrae were re-labeled as 0, and lumbar vertebrae were modified to the label of 1 (Fig. 4). We took advantage of binary segmentation because it was easy to train with a limited amount of training data. Additionally, we could easily find the T12 and L1 levels by using two separate models to determine the end slices of each model’s output mask.

Model training

In this study, a 2D U-Net architecture with encoder and decoder modules connected via concatenated skip-connections was employed for CT image analysis using a dice loss function. Pre-processing included adjustment of image values using window level 300 and width settings 850 and normalization 0 to 1. These ‘windowing’ methods was useful for training models efficiently because they limit the range of pixel values before inferring each pixel. The study also incorporated basic data augmentation techniques (smooth/sharpness filters, translation, rotation), AI-powered denoising (ClariCT.AI, ClariPi.INC), and FOV adjustments, with the latter involving resizing of the scaled spinal area and corresponding CT images to 512 × 512 dimensions. All processes aimed at promoting generalized learning and consistent segmentation performance across CT scan parameters from different manufacturers.

Localization of the thoracolumbar spine and BMD calculation

For comprehensive thoracolumbar segmentation, we applied these two models in tandem. In the segmentation process, the final object delineated by the T-spine model was identified as T12, and the initial two consecutive objects in the mask were identified by the L-spine model as L1 and L2, respectively. For example, in certain slices of the L-spine segmentation results, a mask was present, and in the corresponding location in the T-spine segmentation results, a more complete mask appeared. In such cases, that position could be deemed a false positive, indicating inaccuracies in the L-spine segmentation results. If the L-spine segmentation output an error mask for T12, we could not obtain a consecutive mask on the T12 level slices. However, the output mask of the T-spine segmentation at the T12 level would be better than the error mask of the L-spine segmentation. Therefore, we calculated the connected components in adjacent consecutive mask slices and labeled the spine level accordingly.

The ROI was positioned to avoid both the cortical bone and the basivertebral region and was calculated from the center of mass in the binary mask of the spine. The elliptical ROI was placed using the erosion kernel of a morphological algorithm to encompass only the inner trabecular bone region. When calculating the average directly from the distribution of Hounsfield Unit (HU) values within the ROI, errors could occur if high HU values, like those of calcification due to degeneration, were mistakenly included within the ROI. Therefore, to mitigate this issue, the data distribution, specifically the extreme 1% of values that can potentially be outliers in the distribution of HU values within the ROI, was trimmed. After removing these extreme values, the 50th percentile (median) of the remaining data distribution was calculated and designated as the representative HU value. This value was then applied to the HU-BMD conversion formula to obtain the final BMD value16.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics and continuous variables were reported as counts (percentages, %) and as means with standard deviations (SD), respectively. We explored the correlation between automated volumetric BMD measurements (DL-BMD) on L1 and L2 vertebral bodies obtained from DL-BMD software and manually measured volumetric BMD (m-BMD) from a conventional asynchronous QCT using Pearson’s correlation and absolute ICCs. The diagnostic performance of DL-BMD was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis not only for the osteoporosis group, but also for the low BMD group, encompassing both osteoporosis and osteopenia as determined by DXA and m-BMD. The evaluation of performance was carried out for the entire dataset and sub-groups based on chest, spine, and abdominal CTs. This evaluation was based on 2 × 2 contingency tables derived from a one-sided comparison of DL-BMD test results with central DXA and between DL-BMD and m-BMD. We calculated 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for both sensitivity and specificity. DeLong’s test was performed to evaluate the difference in diagnostic performance between m-BMD and DL-BMD for diagnosing osteoporosis and low BMD. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics (version 25.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and MedCalc (Version 20.217, MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium), with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
