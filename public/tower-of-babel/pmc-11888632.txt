Severe Hypersensitivity Reactions with Oral Manifestations: Case Reports on Stevens-Johnson Syndrome and DRESS Syndrome
Authors: Sura A. A. Fuoad Al-Bayati
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) and Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms (DRESS) syndrome are rare conditions, representing severe hypersensitivity reactions with significant oral presentations. SJS is an uncommon but severe variant of erythema multiforme, presenting skin and mucosal eruptions that can be potentially fatal. DRESS syndrome, similarly, involves a severe hypersensitivity reaction characterized by fever, rash, and organ involvement. DRESS syndrome is a rare but severe drug-induced hypersensitivity reaction characterized by fever, rash, eosinophilia, and internal organ involvement. The condition often presents with elevated liver enzymes and systemic symptoms that necessitate prompt diagnosis and treatment. This report discusses a case of DRESS syndrome in a 41-year-old patient, highlighting the clinical presentation, investigations, differential diagnosis, and treatment approach. Both conditions require prompt diagnosis and treatment, often involving hospitalization. This report discusses two cases of severe hypersensitivity reactions with oral manifestations.

INTRODUCTION

Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) and Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms (DRESS) syndrome are both severe, potentially life-threatening adverse drug reactions that primarily affect the skin and mucosa but can also involve multiple organ systems. Despite their differences, both syndromes share underlying immune mechanisms and require prompt recognition and management. Recent research has highlighted the complex immune mechanisms underlying both SJS and DRESS. For instance, the pathogenesis of DRESS involves a multifaceted interaction between drug-specific immune responses and viral reactivations, particularly HHV-6. This complex interaction also contributes to the severity and multisystem involvement seen in these conditions. Both CD4 and CD8 T cells are implicated in these processes, with distinct roles in initiating drug allergies and targeting virus-infected cells, respectively.[1]

SJS is an immune-mediated disorder that primarily affects the skin and mucous membranes. It is typically induced by medications such as antiepileptics, sulfonamides, and certain antibiotics. SJS is characterized by the detachment of less than 10% of the body surface area, with symptoms that include painful blistering, purpuric macules, and atypical target lesions. Mucosal involvement is common, affecting areas like the eyes, mouth, and genitals, impairing daily activities, and leading to significant complications, if not managed properly and promptly. The pathogenesis of SJS is thought to involve a CD8+ T cell-mediated response that leads to widespread keratinocyte apoptosis, resulting in characteristic muco-dermatological lesions. Mortality rates in SJS can be as high as 15–20%, particularly in older adults or those with comorbidities.[23]

DRESS syndrome, also known as Drug-Induced Hypersensitivity Syndrome (DIHS), is another SCAR characterized by extensive skin eruptions, hematologic abnormalities such as eosinophilia and multi-organ involvement, including the liver, kidneys, and lungs. The onset of DRESS typically occurs two to six weeks after exposure to the offending drug. Unlike SJS, DRESS syndrome is often associated with a protracted clinical course and a higher risk of long-term complications, such as the development of autoimmune diseases. Both SJS and DRESS are part of a broader category of severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs), which also include toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) when the skin detachment exceeds 30% of the body surface.[4] The pathogenesis of DRESS involves a complex, multifaceted interaction between drug-specific immune responses, immune system activation, and genetic predispositions, including specific HLA alleles. This complex interaction also contributes to the severity and multisystem involvement.[56] Both CD4 and CD8 T cells are implicated in these processes, with distinct roles in initiating drug allergies and reactivation of latent viruses, such as human herpesvirus-6 (HHV-6), which has also been implicated in the syndrome’s development.[1]

Despite their differences, both SJS and DRESS syndromes require immediate cessation of the causative drug, supportive care, and, in many cases, systemic corticosteroids or other immunomodulatory treatments to control the inflammatory response. Recent research highlights the importance of personalized medicine approaches, including genetic screening for HLA risk alleles, to prevent these severe reactions. However, much remains to be understood about the underlying mechanisms and optimal treatment strategies for these complex conditions.[25]

SJS and DRESS syndrome are both considered rare conditions. The rarity is reflected in their incidence rates, with SJS affecting approximately 1 to 6 people per million per year, and DRESS syndrome having an estimated incidence of 1 in 1,000 to 10,000 drug exposures. These hypersensitivity reactions are life-threatening and require prompt diagnosis and intervention. The manuscript details two such cases, emphasizing the significance of their rarity and the need for immediate medical attention to prevent severe complications.

CASE 1

A 39-year-old female attending the Thumbay Hospital, her chief complaint was throat pain for the last 4 days, high-grade fever, skin rash for 2 days, vomiting (6 episodes), diarrhea (4 episodes), pain during urination, and burning sensation. History showed that the patient was on ibuprofen 400 mg two times a day for knee pain. Patient was presented in a wheelchair, with a sore throat, high-grade fever, and a painful, polymorphic rash involving the skin and mucous membranes, including the oral cavity. Examination revealed painful acute blisters and ulcerations on less than 10% of the body surface area, consistent with the features of SJS Figure 1. Blood crustation on the lip, halitosis, and thick sticky saliva. Intra-oral examination could not be done due to the limitations in mouth opening and painful intra-oral ulceration. Laboratory investigations done and the results revealed that C-reactive protein (CRP): 33.6 mg/L (elevated), Mycoplasma pneumonia IgM: 4.7 (positive), RBC count: 5.1 × 10^6/μL (normal). Based on all the previous, the differential diagnosis was SJS, erythema multiforme, drug-induced rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS syndrome).

Patient presented with polymorphic rash involving the skin and mucous membranes, including the oral cavity

Based on the analysis of the collected data by the collaborated team of specialists—ENT, internal medicine, oral medicine, and dermatologist, SJS was the final diagnosis. Treatment was decided as follows: First, stop the ibuprofen, and prescribe these medications.

Dexamethasone: Intravenous, 4 mg twice daily, Levocetirizine: Oral tablet, 5 mg once daily, Azithromycin (Zithromax): Oral, 500 mg once daily, Orofar buccal spray: 4 times daily, Xylocaine gel: Oral application as needed, Refresh Tears (eye drops): 2 drops 5–6 times daily, Liporoic eye gel, Topical treatment: Fucidin cream applied to affected areas, Supportive care: Ringer’s solution and normal saline for hydration.

The patient was hospitalized for 2 weeks and received comprehensive supportive care. She was discharged with instructions for continued use of topical treatments and hydration.

CASE 2

A 41-year-old male was admitted to Thumbay Hospital complaint form fever and generalized rash, malaise, and signs of systemic involvement in addition to oral ulceration and burning sensation, including gastrointestinal symptoms and oral ulcerations. patient gave a recent history of antibiotic use. Clinical examination revealed a widespread rash, facial edema, and severe malaise, oral ulcerations, and burning sensation as shown in Figure 2. The rash extended to more than 30% of the body’s surface area. Despite the long list of differential diagnoses including DRESS syndrome, SJS and TEN, AGEP, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), sepsis or systemic infections, serum sickness-like reaction, drug-induced lupus erythematosus, raising suspicion for DRESS syndrome, especially given the recent history of drug exposure.

Generalized skin rash with itching, dryness, and burning in some areas

To achieve the correct diagnosis several laboratory investigations revealed the following results: RBC count was 5.8 106/UL. Routine urine analysis was clear. IGE was 431 (normal less than 100). Oral swab microbiology report was negative. Serum creatinine was normal but liver enzymes were raised AST 134 U/L, ALT was 221 U/L (both normal value less than 41), C-reactive protein 7.4 mg/L (normal less than 5.0). Increased liver enzymes; AST 157, ALT 227 U/L (normal should be less than 41), ALP 162 U/L (normal up to 129), Gamma-glutamyl transferase GTT 295 U/L (normal up to 71). Antistreptolysin ASO 165 IU/ml (normal less than 20, C-reactive protein 66 mg/L (normal value less than 5). WBC count 11.7 103/UL, eosinophils 9.3% (normal 1.09). All kidney function test values are within normal range. Brain MRI with and without contrast showed no abnormality. The patients were consulted with team of general medicine, dermatologists, and oral medicine specialists whom analyzed his records and investigation results, reaching the final diagnosis of DRESS.

The decided treatment was: Calamine lotion topical twice a day, Miconazole oral gel at bed time, Medigel at bed time orally, Fucidin cream, TAB Concore 25 mg oral, Hydrocortisone 100 gm IV, Amoxcycline 1 gm for 7 days, Ceftriaxone 1 gm IV.

The patient showed significant improvement following the administration of appropriate treatment. The previously severe symptoms, including widespread rash, fever, and organ involvement, began to resolve rapidly. The patient’s skin lesions healed, systemic inflammation subsided, and overall well-being improved markedly. Continued monitoring and supportive care ensured a steady recovery, ultimately leading to the patient’s return to normal health. This outcome highlights the importance of timely diagnosis and tailored therapy in managing complex conditions like DRESS syndrome.

DISCUSSION

The cases presented in this report underscore the complex nature of severe hypersensitivity reactions such as SJS and DRESS syndrome, particularly when they manifest with significant oral manifestations. Both cases emphasize the importance of rapid diagnosis and the initiation of appropriate treatment to prevent severe complications and ensure a positive outcome.

In the first case, a 39-year-old female presented with classic signs of SJS, including a polymorphic rash, painful blisters, and ulcerations involving less than 10% of the body surface area, with significant mucosal involvement with oral ulcerations, and systemic symptoms following the use of ibuprofen. This aligns with current literature that describes SJS as a predominantly drug-induced reaction, often triggered by medications such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), antiepileptics, and antibiotics.[23] The diagnosis was confirmed through a combination of clinical findings and laboratory investigations, which ruled out other potential diagnoses such as erythema multiforme and DRESS syndrome. The patient responded well to corticosteroids and supportive care, consistent with existing literature on SJS management approach, which included the immediate cessation of the offending drug, early intervention with systemic steroids can mitigate the progression of skin detachment and reduce mortality rates associated with SJS, which are reported to be between 15 and 20%.[23]

In contrast, the second case involved a 41-year-old male who developed DRESS syndrome following antibiotic therapy. Unlike SJS, DRESS syndrome is characterized by a more delayed onset, extensive systemic involvement, and a higher likelihood of long-term complications such as liver dysfunction and eosinophilia. This patient’s clinical presentation, including a generalized rash, facial edema, and elevated liver enzymes, aligned with the typical features of DRESS syndrome. The elevated eosinophil count further supported the diagnosis, as eosinophilia is a hallmark of DRESS.[5] The successful management of this case with systemic corticosteroids and other supportive treatments as antihistamines, and antibiotics underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in addressing the complex interplay of immune responses in DRESS syndrome. This is consistent with recent findings suggesting that such interventions can be effective in managing the complex inflammatory response associated with DRESS.[78]

The differential diagnosis in both cases was extensive, reflecting the broad range of conditions that can mimic SJS and DRESS syndrome. For instance, viral exanthems, autoimmune disorders, and other drug reactions such as acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP) can present with similar dermatological and systemic symptoms, making clinical judgment and thorough investigation crucial in establishing the correct diagnosis. The use of specific diagnostic markers, such as the Mycoplasma pneumonia IgM in the first case and elevated eosinophil count and liver enzymes in the second, was crucial in narrowing down the differential diagnoses and confirming the final diagnosis.[9]

Recent advancements in the understanding of the pathogenesis of both SJS and DRESS have highlighted the importance of genetic predispositions, particularly the role of specific HLA alleles, in the development of these conditions. This suggests a potential future role for genetic screening in preventing these severe drug reactions by identifying at-risk individuals before initiating therapy with high-risk medications.[37] Additionally, the role of viral reactivation, especially with HHV-6, has been increasingly recognized in the pathogenesis of DRESS, providing further insight into the complex interplay of immune responses in this syndrome.[78]

The outcomes of these two patients highlight the effectiveness of early intervention and the tailored use of immunosuppressive therapy. While both SJS and DRESS syndrome remain challenging to treat due to their severity and potential for rapid progression, these cases demonstrate that with prompt and appropriate management, patients can achieve full recovery without significant long-term sequelae. Future studies should continue to explore the underlying mechanisms of these conditions to refine treatment strategies and improve patient outcomes.

CONCLUSION

These cases illustrate the complexity and severity of hypersensitivity reactions like SJS and DRESS syndrome. Early recognition, prompt intervention, multidisciplinary, and tailored treatment strategies management are crucial in improving patient outcomes. The patient’s recovery following the discontinuation of the offending drugs and administration of corticosteroids emphasizes the importance of timely intervention in preventing severe complications associated with DRESS syndrome. Both conditions necessitate a tailored treatment approach to address systemic involvement and prevent long-term complications.
