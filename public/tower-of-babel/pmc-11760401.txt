An In Vivo Evaluation of Changes in Salivary pH in 3–9-year-old Children Using Chewing Gums (Xylitol) and Lollipops (Xylitol + Erythritol)
Authors: Yash S Latkar, Sowndarya Gunasekaran, Veena Arali, Pradeep D Gainneos, Reshma E Rajan, Karteek Eswara
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the change in salivary pH using xylitol- and erythritol-containing lollipops and chewing gums, and assess the correlation between sugar substitutes and change in pH levels of saliva.Materials and methodsThe present crossover study was conducted in 60 children between 3 and 9 years of age from a private school who had not received any meal for 2 hours prior to examination. There were three groups in this study: group I, control group (rinsing with tap water); group II, experimental group (with sugar substitute lollipops); and group III, experimental group (with sugar substitute chewing gums). The saliva sample was collected at three different time intervals, and the pH of saliva was determined using a universal pH indicator.ResultsThere was a highly significant difference observed in the pre- and postsalivary pH in all three groups, with the maximum increase seen in the lollipop group and the least increase seen in the control group.ConclusionLollipops can be used as a preventive measure in decreasing dental caries by preventing the salivary pH from falling below the critical level.Clinical significanceThe use of sugar-substituted lollipops and chewing gums in raising pH levels was evaluated. Introduction

Sixty-nine point four percent of children and adults worldwide are affected by dental caries.1 It impacts day-to-day life, nutrition, school attendance in children, and the quality of life of caregivers; many parents seek treatment for their children to manage the burden of the disease.2 Caries is a multifactorial disease in which saliva and its constituents play an important role. Saliva, which is composed of 99% water and 1% electrolytes and proteins, plays a significant role in balancing the disease process and thereby maintaining the health of oral tissues. Reduced salivary flow decreases these beneficial constituents, thus contributing to dental caries, oral infections, periodontal problems, etc.3 Modernization and trends in the current generation have led to increased consumption of hard candies and soft chewable toffies containing high levels of sucrose. The maturation of the plaque biofilm and its cariogenicity are enhanced by the presence of sucrose, which favors the increase of cariogenic microbiota, such as mutans streptococci (MS) and lactobacilli, within the biofilm.4

Early caries detection, reduced sugar intake, use of nonfermentable sugar substitutes, pit and fissure sealants, and silver diamine fluoride, etc., are some of the preventive modalities being recently emphasized to reduce the incidence of caries. Noncaloric sweeteners, such as sorbitol and xylitol, are used in multiple products like baked goods, candies, yogurts, chewing gums, etc.5 Chewing gums bring about a masticatory action that increases the salivary flow rate.6 Salivary Streptococcus mutans levels can be significantly reduced by using gums or candies containing sugar substitutes, such as probiotic bacteria or xylitol. The increased salivary flow rate due to chewing gums significantly improves oral health through enhanced oral clearance of dietary sugars and plaque acids from the tooth surface.7

Xylitol acts as an oral prebiotic, decreasing cariogenic MS without affecting the oral microbiota.7 Erythritol, a noncaloric, four-carbon polyol, not only has sweetness equivalent to 60–80% of sugar but also has good digestibility and does not influence blood glucose and insulin levels. Erythritol, along with xylitol, has been shown to decrease plaque levels as a result of reduced S. mutans levels.8 Erythritol can easily pass through cell membranes, thereby suppressing growth through a pathway unlike xylitol, sorbitol, or maltitol. The key contributors to the acceptance of erythritol for day-to-day use are its zero-calorie sweetness and pleasant mouthfeel.

Utilizing sugar substitutes in lollipops and chewing gums can effectively replace the sucrose found in other hard candies, offering a healthier alternative without compromising on sweetness. The aim of this study is to assess the change in salivary pH in 3–9-year-old children using chewing gums (xylitol) and lollipops (xylitol + erythritol).

Materials and Methods

Ethical Clearance and Informed Consent

This crossover trial was conducted in a private school in XXX, YYY District after obtaining clearance from the Institutional Review and Ethics Board (IEC No.: VMSDC/IEC/Approval No. 288) and Trial Acknowledgment Number REF/2023/04/065481. Written and informed consent from the parents and assent from the children, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, were received prior to the start of the study.

Study Participants

The required sample size was estimated from a study done by Jain and Mathur (2022) as 60 children, with the power of the study at 95%, and the level of significance set at p < 0.05.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

A total of 172 children from a private school, between the ages of 3 and 9 years, were screened, out of which 112 children were excluded. Sixty children with a def (d = decayed, e = extracted, f = filled) score between 2 and 4, and those with no history of systemic disease or developmental anomalies of teeth that could affect dietary patterns or caries susceptibility, were included. Children with special health care needs, those not willing to participate, and those who had been treated for dental issues in the last 2 weeks were excluded from the study. Children were instructed not to have any meals for 2 hours prior to the examination.

Procedural Methodology

The study employed a crossover design with a 2-week washout period and consisted of three groups, each comprising 60 children.

The interventions included mouth rinsing with tap water (group I), consumption of Zollipops (Zolli Candy, United States) (xylitol + erythritol) for group II, and chewing HappyDent Wave chewing gums (xylitol) for group III.

Unstimulated saliva samples were collected from all groups at three time points:

T1: Baseline.

T1: Baseline.

T2: 15 minutes after consuming a caloric drink (Frooti).

T2: 15 minutes after consuming a caloric drink (Frooti).

T3: 15 minutes after providing the intervention (Zollipops, chewing gums, or mouth rinsing).

T3: 15 minutes after providing the intervention (Zollipops, chewing gums, or mouth rinsing).

During saliva collection, children were instructed to hold their heads slightly down, refrain from swallowing, and allow saliva to accumulate in their mouths for 2 minutes before spitting it into Eppendorf tubes (Pratap-MCT 2 mL, India), filling up to the 2 mL mark.

For group II (Zollipops), children were directed not to chew or crush the lollipop, while for group III (chewing gums), children chewed the gum for 15 minutes.

In group 1 (tap water rinsing), children were instructed to gargle and spit after rinsing their mouths. After collection, salivary samples were transferred to small containers and transported to the lab within 2 hours.

A universal pH indicator digital meter (Konvio RO Systems Pvt. Ltd.) was used to measure the salivary pH at each time point following sample collection. The pH meter's bulb was fully submerged in the saliva samples, and readings were recorded.

Statistical Analysis

Data were collected and results were statistically tabulated using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Software 23.0. To find the significant difference in the multivariate analysis, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post hoc test was used, and ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was used for repeated measures.

Results

The mean pH of saliva at baseline in group I (control group) was 6.67 (Fig. 1), in group II (lollipop group) was 6.70 (Fig. 1), and in group III (chewing gum group) was 6.66 (Fig. 1). After providing the caloric drink, there was a significant drop in pH to 5.8 in group I (control group), 5.8 in group II (lollipop group), and 5.78 in group III (chewing gum group) (Fig. 1). After providing the intervention, there was an increase in salivary pH in all groups, with 6.1 in group I (control group), 6.5 in group II (lollipop group), and 6.40 in group III (chewing gum group), respectively (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In recent times, the prevalence of tooth loss is rapidly increasing due to dental caries or dental erosion.9 The key factors that have been perceived to increase caries activity are the duration and frequency of oral exposures to carbohydrates. The bacteria that adhere to the teeth degrade simple sugars via biochemical glycolytic pathways and produce energy and lactic acid as byproducts. This leads to a decrease in the pH of saliva, which results in demineralization of the enamel and dentin, followed by cavitation.10

Salivary pH

The pH of human saliva is considered normal when values are between 6.0 and 7.5. The demineralization of teeth by fermentable carbohydrates results in an acidic pH, which, when it drops below 5.5 (critical pH), leads to demineralization. An increase in salivary pH from using bicarbonate chewing gums was reported by Ballal et al.11 This can be ascribed to the fact that the frequency of demineralization is directly proportional to the absolute decline in pH, and the duration of time for which pH is lower favors the dissolution of enamel.11 As seen in this study, the initial pH dropped after providing a sucrose-based fruit drink to the children. It was established that the decrease in pH was due to an increase in microbial activity, leading to the fermentation of sugars.

A study carried out by Macpherson and Dawes (1993) on chewing gums with and without sucrose showed that the pH was similar to or above the pH of unstimulated saliva (pH: 6.3–6.8) after intervention.12 This was in agreement with the present study, in which the salivary pH decreased to 5.78 from 6.67 in group I (control group), 5.82 from 6.70 in group II (lollipop group), and 5.78 from 6.65 in group III. A study conducted by Vantipalli et al. (2017) reported an increase in pH after providing the intervention (chewing gums).13 This was in accordance with the current study, in which the salivary pH increased to 6.10 from 5.78 in group I (control group), 6.50 from 5.82 in group II (lollipop group), and 6.40 from 5.78 in group III. Salivary pH and flow showed statistically significant differences.

Chewing gum stimulation leads to increased salivary flow velocity due to an increased flow rate, facilitating better oral clearance and also an increase in salivary buffering capacity.14 Lollipops seemed to mimic the same gustatory stimulation in this study, which may be responsible for the increase in pH.15

Sugar Substitutes

Sugar alcohols (or polyols) are naturally occurring sweet-tasting organic compounds that can be used to replace sucrose. Many sugar alcohols, for example, maltitol, lactitol, sorbitol, mannitol, erythritol, and xylitol, are used in the food industry. In vitro, animal, and in vivo cariogenicity tests have shown the polyols to be either nonacidogenic or hypo-acidogenic, and therefore are classified as low or noncariogenic. The possible mechanism by which xylitol-containing products may reduce caries16 include a passive substitution of cariogenic free sugars (for example, sucrose, fructose, etc.).17

Secondly, chewing polyol-containing products may reduce caries by stimulation of saliva, which further inhibits caries in four ways: (1) mechanical cleansing or flushing action, (2) distributing calcium, phosphate, and fluoride ions for the remineralization of enamel, (3) buffering action to plaque acids via carbonate, phosphate, and protein, and (4) by specific antibacterial properties.16 Replacement of sucrose and free sugars by polyols will help in the reduction of overall dental caries.

Sugar polyols are accumulated and absorbed intracellularly and hence cannot be readily metabolized into energy by S. mutans. Unlike sucrose, in which the net energy is metabolized and promotes S. mutans growth, sugar polyols create a net energy loss. Sugar substitutes compete with sucrose for its intracellular metabolic process and its cell wall transporter. This eventually results in the starvation of the microorganisms, leading to their overall reduction.18

Erythritol (sugar alcohol) is used as a bulk sweetener in foods. It has the smallest molecular size and is widely available in microorganisms, algae, fermented foods, lichens, mushrooms, many fruits and vegetables, and also animal and human tissues.18 Laxative effects and bloating are not seen with erythritol, making it advantageous over xylitol. The weight of dental plaque and the adherence of common streptococcal oral bacteria to tooth surfaces were reduced in a study conducted by De Cock.18 Additionally, the expression of bacterial genes, along with the growth and metabolic activity of associated bacteria such as S. mutans, was decreased, leading to the overall decrease in the number of dental caries.19

The stimulation of salivary flow, the regulation of pH levels, and the reduction in levels of S. mutans in saliva can be achieved by chewing gums and lollipops.20 Similar results were reported in the present study in both the lollipop and chewing gum groups, where there was an increase in pH postintervention.

Conclusion

Lollipops containing xylitol and erythritol, as well as xylitol chewing gums, can be used effectively in increasing salivary pH as a preventive measure. The appeal of lollipops to children, combined with the reduced risk of ingestion compared to chewing gums, motivates us to prioritize lollipops as a preventive measure for inhibiting dental caries. Caries reduction can be ascribed to mechanical saliva stimulation and the inability of oral microbiota to metabolize polyols into acids. These salient features promote the usage of sugar substitutes as replacement therapy to prevent early childhood caries.
