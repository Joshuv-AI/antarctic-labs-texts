On vertices and inflections of singular plane curves
J. W. Bruce, M. A. C. Fernandes, F. Tari

Abstract
Given the germ of a smooth plane curve ( { f ( x , y ) = 0 } , 0 ) ⊂ ( 𝕂 2 , 0 ) , 𝕂 = ℝ , ℂ (\{f(x,y)=0\},0)\subset(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}^{2},0),\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$} , with an isolated singularity, we define two invariants I f I_{f} and V f ∈ ℕ ∪ { ∞ } V_{f}\in\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{N}$}\cup\{\infty\} , which count the number of inflections and vertices (suitably interpreted in the complex case) concentrated at the singular point. The first is an affine invariant, while the second is invariant under similarities of ℝ 2 \mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{2} , and their analogue for ℂ 2 \mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2} . When the curve has no smooth components, these invariants are always finite and bounded. We illustrate our results by computing the range of possible values for these invariants for Arnold’s 𝒦 {\cal K} -simple singularities. We also establish a relationship between these invariants, the Milnor number of f f and the contact of the curve germ with its ‘osculating circle’.

1
Introduction

Let
C
be germ of a plane curve in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{2}
. The dual and evolute of
C
are associated curves of significant interest. The dual has singularities corresponding to inflections and bi-tangencies, while the evolute has singularities related to vertices and bi-osculating circles. Evolutes can also be interpreted as the caustics of the wavefront generated by
C
. Moreover, when
C
is a projective plane curve, the dual of the dual is
C
.

When the curve is singular, we can still define both the dual and the evolute, and it is natural to ask what happens at the singular points. In particular, if we replace the singular curve with a nearby generic one, how many inflections and vertices emerge from the singularity? The answer naturally depends on how we define a generic deformation. If the curve is given parametrically, we deform the parametrisation to obtain an immersed curve with normal crossings. If it is given implicitly by an equation, we deform the equation to obtain a smooth curve. The question, in each case, is how many inflections and vertices emerge under these respective deformations.
These questions have been addressed in
[
5
]
for parametrised curves, and in
[
16
]
for inflections, both in the case of parametrised curves and curves defined by equations. In this paper we give a self-contained account of all cases.

While primarily motivated by the real case, it is most efficient to start with germs of (possibly singular) holomorphic curves, defined either by a parametrisation
\gamma:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)
, or as a zero set of a germ
f:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},0)
, which we denote respectively by
(C_{\gamma},0)
and
(C_{f},0)
.

Inflections are points where some line has order of contact at least
3
; vertices where the order of contact with some circle or line is at least
4
. We define invariants
I_{\gamma},I_{f}
and
V_{\gamma},V_{f}
in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{N}$}\cup\{\infty\}
, which count the number of inflections and vertices concentrated at the singular point.

We show how to compute these numbers in terms of the corresponding invariants for the irreducible branches of
f
. Given a parametrisation
\gamma
of an irreducible germ defined by
f=0
, we establish relations between
I_{\gamma},I_{f},\mu(f)
,
V_{\gamma},V_{f},\mu(f)
, where
\mu(f)
is the Milnor number of
f
. We also relate
I_{\gamma},V_{\gamma}
and the contact between
\gamma
and its osculating circle.

When
C_{f}
has no smooth components, we show that
I_{f}
and
V_{f}
are bounded, and we provide detailed information for the range of values they can attain. As an illustration, we determine the possible values of
I_{f}
and
V_{f}
when
f
has one of Arnold’s simple singularities.

This paper is organized as follows:

–

In §
2
, we define the notion of vertices of regular complex analytic curves, discuss multiplicity and intersection numbers, and establish some results on the contact between germs of singular curves and smooth curves, which we use throughout the paper.

–

In §
3
, we define
I_{\gamma}
,
I_{f}
,
V_{\gamma}
and
V_{f}
, and describe some of their properties. In particular, we relate
I_{\gamma}
and
V_{\gamma}
to the order of contact of the curve with its osculating line and osculating circle, respectively.

–

In §
4
, we relate
I_{\gamma}
to
I_{f}
, and
V_{\gamma}
to
V_{f}
, when the curve is irreducible, that is, when
(C_{\gamma},0)=(C_{f},0)
. We also determine the range of values that
I_{f}
and
V_{f}
can attain.

–

In §
5
, we carry out explicit calculations, including the ranges of values of
I_{f}
and
V_{f}
for curves with simple singularities.

–

Finally, we return to the real case in §
6
, deducing our results from the previous sections using finite determinacy results.

Throughout, all our defining functions
f:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},0)
are
{\cal K}
-finite, that is
f
has no repeated factors.

2
Preliminaries

We begin with some basic notions from singularity theory (see, for example,
[
15
]
). Two germs
f_{i}:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}^{n},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}^{p},0),i=1,2,
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}
or
\mathbb{R}
, are said to be
\mathcal{R}
-
equivalent
if there exists a germ of a diffeomorphism
\phi:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}^{n},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}^{n},0)
such that
f_{2}=f_{1}\circ\phi^{-1}
.
They are
\mathcal{C}
-
equivalent
if there exists a smooth family of invertible
p\times p
matrices
A:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}^{n},0)\to GL(p,\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$})
such that
f_{2}(x)=A(x)f_{1}(x)
.
They are
\mathcal{K}
-
equivalent
if there exist
\phi
and
A
as above such that
f_{2}(x)=A(x)(f_{1}\circ\phi^{-1})(x)
.

Two germs
f_{i}:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}^{n},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}^{p},0)
, with
i=1,2
, are said to be
{\mathcal{A}}
-
equivalent
if there exist germs of diffeomorphisms
\phi:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}^{n},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}^{n},0)
and
\psi:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}^{p},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}^{p},0)
such that

f_{2}=\psi\circ f_{1}\circ\phi^{-1}
.

If
{\cal G}
is one of the above groups (
\mathcal{R},\mathcal{K},\mathcal{A}
), a germ
f
is said to be
k
-
{\cal G}
-
determined
if any germ
g
with
j^{k}g=j^{k}f
is
{\cal G}
-equivalent to
f
, where
j^{k}f
denotes the
k
-jet of
f
at the origin (i.e., the Taylor polynomial of degree
k
, without the constant term). A germ
f
is said to be
{\cal G}
-
finite
if it is
k
-
{\cal G}
-determined for some
k
. There are algebraic criteria for determining whether a germ is
{\cal G}
-finite; see, for example,
[
15
]
.

In what follows, we consider germs of plane curves
(C,0)\subset(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}^{2},0)
, where the curves may be defined either by parametrisations or by equations.

A germ of a parametrised curve
\gamma:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)
is
{\cal A}
-finite if and only if
\gamma
is an injective immersion away from the origin. Any such map can be written in the form
(t^{m},y(t))
, where
\textrm{ord}(y)>m
. Among the powers appearing in the series expansion of
y(t)
, the lowest exponent not divisible by
m
is called the
first Puiseux exponent
of
\gamma
, denoted
\beta(\gamma)
. If
\gamma
is singular and
{\cal A}
-finite, then the first Puiseux exponent is finite (indeed, since

m>1
, if all exponents in

y(t)
were divisible by

m
, then

y(t)=y_{1}(t^{m})
for some

y_{1}
, and
\gamma
would fail to be injective). Clearly,
\beta(\gamma)
is an analytic invariant of the curve
\gamma
.

Let
\mathcal{O}_{s}
denote the ring of germs of holomorphic functions
(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{s},0)\to\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}
.
A germ
f\in\mathcal{O}_{2}
is
{\cal K}
-finite if and only if
f
has an isolated singularity at the origin, which is equivalent to
f
having no repeated factors.
Such a germ decomposes into a product of irreducible factors in
\mathcal{O}_{2}
, namely
f=f_{1}\cdots f_{n}
. Clearly, the associated curves satisfy
C_{f}=C_{f_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup C_{f_{n}}
, where each
C_{f_{i}}
is referred to as a
branch
or
component
of
C_{f}
.

If
\gamma_{j},j=1,2,
are
{\cal A}
-finite germs with reduced defining equations
f_{j}=0
, then
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are
{\cal A}
-equivalent if and only if
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are
{\cal K}
-equivalent; see
[
2
]
.

If we write
f=F_{r}+F_{r+1}+\cdots,

where each
F_{i}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
i
in the variables
x
and
y
, and
F_{r}\neq 0
, then
r
is the
multiplicity
of
f
, denoted
\textrm{mult}(f)
, and
C_{F_{r}}
is the
tangent cone
of
C_{f}
.

The order of
\rho=\sum a_{k}t^{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{1}
, denoted by
\textrm{ord}(\rho)
, is the smallest positive integer
k>0
such that
a_{k}\neq 0
. Equivalently,

\textrm{ord}(\rho)=\dim_{\mathbb{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}}}\frac{\mathcal{O}_{1}}{\mathcal{O}_{1}\langle\rho\rangle},

where
\mathcal{O}_{1}\langle\rho\rangle
denotes the ideal in
\mathcal{O}_{1}
generated by
\rho
. Clearly, for any
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{1}
, we have
\textrm{ord}(\rho\sigma)=\textrm{ord}(\rho)+\textrm{ord}(\sigma)
. Moreover, if
\rho(0)=0
, then
\textrm{ord}(\rho^{\prime})=\textrm{ord}(\rho)-1
.

2.1
Vertices and inflections of regular curves

Recall that two regular curves in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2}
, one parametrised by
\gamma:U\to\mathbb{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}}^{2}
and the other given by the equation
g=0,
with
g:V\to\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}
, where
U
and
V
are open subset of
\mathbb{C}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2}
respectively, have
k
-point contact
(
k\geq 1
) at
t_{0}
if the following conditions are satisfied:

\gamma(t_{0})\in V
,
(g\circ\gamma)(t_{0})=0
,
(g\circ\gamma)^{(i)}(t_{0})=0
, for
i=1,\ldots,k-1,
and
(g\circ\gamma)^{(k)}(t_{0})\neq 0
.
Switching the roles of the two curves gives the same integer for the contact. The curves are said to have
at least

k
-point contact
if we drop the last condition.

We are interested in the local contact between a plane curve and lines and circles. For this purpose, it is convenient to both projectivise and complexify. A complex circle in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2}
is defined as a conic in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{P}$}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2}
with an equation of the form

A(x^{2}+y^{2})+2Bxz+2Cyz+Dz^{2}=0.

Such a conic passes through the circular points at infinity,
(1:\pm i:0)
. Its centre and radius are defined analogously to the real case, with the degenerate case
A=0
corresponding to lines.

Given a point
p\in\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{P}$}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2}
not on the line joining the circular points at infinity, we may choose a projective change of coordinates that maps
p
to
(0:0:1)
, and then work in the affine chart with
z=1
. In this chart, the definitions of inflection and vertex, dual and evolute and results on local structures for smooth complex curves proceed in the usual way; see
[
3
]
.
In the real case (
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{K}$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}
), the group preserving the circles is the group of similarities. In the complex case, if we projectivise, it is the group preserving the circular points at infinity.
We may think of the subgroup fixing those points as analogous to the orientation preserving transformations, while the coset that exchanges them corresponds to orientation reversing transformations.
In the affine chart
z=1
, one can check that the group fixing both the circular points at infinity and the origin consists of the linear maps of the form
(x,y)\mapsto(ax+by,-bx+ay),
with
a,b\in\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},a^{2}+b^{2}\neq 0
.

2.2
Multiplicity and intersection number

Given
f,g\in\mathcal{O}_{2}
the
intersection number
of
f
and
g
, denoted by
m(f,g)
, is the codimension of the ideal
\mathcal{O}_{2}\langle f,g\rangle
in
\mathcal{O}_{2}
. That is,

m(f,g)=\dim_{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}}\frac{\mathcal{O}_{2}}{\mathcal{O}_{2}\langle f,g\rangle}.

Further details about the intersection multiplicity (or the intersection index) can be found in
[
10
]
. We will use the following properties:

1.
m(f,g)=m(g,f)
;

4.
m(f,g+fh)=m(f,g)
;

2.
m(uf,vg)=m(f,g)

(for units
u,v\in\mathcal{O}_{2}
)
;

5.
m(f\circ\Phi,g\circ\Phi)=m(f,g)
.

3.
m(f,gh)=m(f,g)+m(f,h)
;

Here,
f,g,h\in\mathcal{O}_{2}
, and
\Phi:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)
is a bi-holomorphic map-germ.

Note that if
f
and
g
are irreducible, then
m(f,g)
is non-finite if and only if
f
and
g
define the same curve. The above properties follow directly from the definition. However, what is not simple to prove is that
m(f,g)
is the local number of inverses images of a regular value of
(f,g):(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)
; see
[
1
]
, Chapter 5. While the following results are standard, we outline their proofs to keep the presentation relatively self-contained.

Proposition 2.1

(1)
Let
\gamma:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)
be a parametrisation of a germ of an irreducible curve
C_{f}
. Then
m(f,g)=\mbox{\rm{ord}}(g\circ\gamma)

(
[
10
]
)
. If
f=0
is reducible with
r
branches parametrised by
\gamma_{j}
, then

m(f,g)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mbox{\rm{ord}}(g\circ\gamma_{j}).

(2)
Suppose that
C_{f}
is irreducible with parametrisation
\gamma(t)=(t^{m},y(t))
, where
\mbox{\rm{ord}}(y(t))>m
. Then one can write the equation of the curve in the form
y^{m}+g(x,y)=0
, where
g\in{\cal O}_{2}^{m+1}
.

(3)
If
f
and
g
are irreducible and their tangent cones are transverse, then
m(f,g)=\mbox{\rm{mult}}(f)\,\mbox{\rm{mult}}(g)
.

(4)
If
f=0
is irreducible, then
m(f,g)=\infty
if and only if
f=0
is a component of
g=0
.

(1) It is enough to prove the result when
f=0
is irreducible, by property (3) of the multiplicity stated earlier. So suppose that we have a parametrisation
\gamma:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)
of
f=0
. Consider the function
G=g\circ\gamma:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},0)
and let
c
be a regular value of
G
near
0\in\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}
. Then the set
G^{-1}(c)
will (locally) consist of
\textrm{ord}(G)
points.
On the other hand,
t\in G^{-1}(c)
if and only if
p=\gamma(t)\in g^{-1}(c)\cap f^{-1}(0)
.
It remains to show that
(0,c)
is a regular value of
(f,g):(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)
. If it were not, then for some
t\in G^{-1}(c)
with
p=\gamma(t)
, the differentials
df(p)
and
dg(p)
would be linearly dependent, implying that
t
is a critical point of
G
, contradicting the regularity of
c
.

(2) We may write

y(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}t^{j}\alpha_{j}(t^{m})=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}t^{j}\alpha_{j}(x).

Consider
t^{r}y(t),0\leq r\leq m-1
, replacing
t^{m}
by
x
at each stage.
Rearranging, we obtain
A(x,y)T=0
, where
A
is an
m\times m
matrix whose entries are holomorphic functions in
(x,y)
and
T
is the transpose of
(1,t,\ldots,t^{m-1})
. Taking the determinant gives the equation for
\gamma(t)
with the required property.

(3) By a diffeomorphism, we may suppose that the tangent cones of
f
and
g
are given by

x=0
and
y=0
, respectively. Then we can parametrise
g=0
as
\gamma(t)=(t^{m_{1}},y(t))
.
By part (2), we may write
f(x,y)=x^{m_{2}}+f_{1}(x,y)
, where both
y(t)
and
f_{1}(x,y)
have orders greater than
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
, respectively. Then
\textrm{ord}(f\circ\gamma)=m_{1}m_{2}
, as required.

(4) Parametrising
f=0
by
\gamma
, we have
m(f,g)=\infty
if and only if
g\circ\gamma
vanishes identically, i.e., if and only if
f=0
is a component of
g=0
.

2.3
Contact between singular and smooth curves

Suppose we are given the germ of a plane curve
(C_{\gamma},0)
realised by a parametrisation
\gamma:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)
. Given any germ of a submersion
h:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},0)
, we consider the order of
h\circ\gamma
. Varying over all such submersions, we obtain a set of natural numbers, which we denote by
{\cal I}(\gamma)
.

Similarly, suppose we are given a plane curve
(C_{f},0)
defined by the equation
f=0
, where

f:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},0)
. Given any germ of an immersion
\alpha:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)
, we consider the order of
f\circ\alpha
. Varying over all such immersions, we again obtain a set of natural numbers, which we denote by
{\cal I}(f)
.

We could naturally extend the notion of
{\cal I}(\gamma)
to multi-germs
\gamma:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},S)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)
, where
S\subset\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}
is a finite set, in order to maintain the symmetry between the two approaches. We leave the details to the reader.

The results that follow are related to those in
[
12
]
, where the maps
\alpha
and
h
are referred to as
probes
. Clearly, if
\gamma
is an immersion and
f
a submersion, then
{\cal I}(\gamma)={\cal I}(f)=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{N}$}
.

Theorem 2.2

(1)

{\cal I}(\gamma)
is an
{\cal A}
-invariant of
\gamma
, and
{\cal I}(f)
is a
{\cal K}
-invariant of
f
.

(2)
If
(C_{f},0)
and
(C_{\gamma},0)
define the same curve, then
{\cal I}(\gamma)={\cal I}(f)
.

(3)
If
f
is irreducible and singular, then

{\cal I}(f)=\{km:1\leq k<\frac{\beta(f)}{m}\}\cup\{\beta(f)\},

where
m=\mbox{\rm{mult}}(f)
, and
\beta(f)
is the first Puiseux characteristic exponent of
f
. In particular,
{\cal I}(f)=\{m,\beta(f)\}
has exactly two elements if and only if
\beta(f)<m
, with
m
occurring when the the curve representing the probe is transverse to the tangent cone of
f
, and
\beta(f)
occurring when it is not.

(4)
If
f
and
g
are irreducible and their tangent cones are transverse, then

{\cal I}(fg)=\left(\mbox{\rm{mult}}(f)+{\cal I}(g)\right)\cup\left(\mbox{\rm{mult}}(g)+{\cal I}(f)\right).

(5)
If
f_{i},i=1,\ldots,k,
are irreducible, then

{\cal I}(f_{1}\cdots f_{k})\subset{\cal I}(f_{1})\cup\cdots\cup{\cal I}(f_{k}).

In particular, if the
f_{i}
are irreducible and singular, then
{\cal I}(f_{1}\cdots f_{k})
is finite.

(6)
The set
{\cal I}(f)
is unbounded if and only if
f=0
contains a smooth component.

(7)

Suppose
f:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},0)
is a
{\cal K}
-finite germ with decomposition into irreducible factors
f=\Pi_{1\leq j\leq k,1\leq i\leq r_{j}}f_{ij}
, where for each
j
,
f_{ij},i=1,\ldots,r_{j},
share the same tangent direction
L_{j}
, and the directions
L_{j}
are pairwise distinct. Assume also that that
\beta(f_{ij})<\mbox{\rm{mult}}(f_{ij})=m_{ij}
for all
i,j
. Let
M_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{j}}m_{ij}
,
\beta_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{j}}\beta(f_{ij})
and
M=\sum_{j=1}^{s}M_{j}
.
Then

{\cal I}(f)=\{M,M-M_{1}+\beta_{1},\ldots,M-M_{k}+\beta_{k}\}.

(1), (2) are straightforward.

For (3), suppose that
\gamma(t)=(t^{m},y(t))
with
\textrm{ord}(y(t))>m
. Write
f=ax+by+O(2)
.
Then
\textrm{ord}(f\circ\gamma)=m
if
a\neq 0
. If
a=0
then
b\neq 0
, and by a change of coordinates of the form
(x,y)\mapsto(x,y/b+A(x,y)),
where
A\in{\cal M}_{2}^{2}
, we can reduce
f
to the form
y
,
while
\gamma
remains in the same form
(t^{m},y(t))
. Thus,
{\cal I}(\gamma)
consists of the set
\{\textrm{ord}(y(t))\}
, as
(t^{m},y(t))
ranges over all parametrisations
{\cal A}
-equivalent to
\gamma
. The result now follows.

For (4), if the tangent cones are transverse, then we can write the product as
(x^{r}+F(x,y))(y^{s}+G(x,y))
, where
F\in{\cal M}^{r+1},G\in{\cal M}^{s+1},
and
r=\textrm{mult}(f),s=\textrm{mult}(g)
. Any germ of an immersion can be written in the form
(t,y(t))
or
(x(t),t)
. In the former case, substitution gives an expression with order
r+\textrm{ord}(g\circ\gamma)
, and in the latter case,
s+\textrm{ord}(f\circ\gamma)
.

For (5), clearly
\textrm{ord}((f_{1}\cdots f_{k})\circ\gamma)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\textrm{ord}(f_{i}\circ\gamma)
.

For (6), if all the irreducible components of
f=0
are singular, then
{\cal I}(f)
is finite by (5).
On the other hand, if
f=0
has a smooth component, say
y=0
, consider the immersion
\alpha(t)=(t,t^{n})
. Clearly,
\textrm{ord}(f\circ\alpha)=n
, so
{\cal I}(f)
is unbounded.

Finally, for (7), there are only
k+1

As in earlier discussions, it is crucial to distinguish whether the curve is defined by a parametrisation or by an implicit equation, since the generic deformations in these two cases differ—even topologically.

\displaystyle f_{x}x^{\prime\prime\prime}+f_{y}y^{\prime\prime\prime}+f_{xxx}(x^{\prime})^{3}+3f_{xxy}(x^{\prime})^{2}y^{\prime}+3f_{xyy}x^{\prime}(y^{\prime})^{2}+f_{yyy}(y^{\prime})^{3}

\displaystyle+3\left(f_{xx}x^{\prime}x^{\prime\prime}+f_{xy}(x^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime}+x^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime})+f_{yy}y^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}\right)=0.

Here the derivatives of
f
are evaluated at
\gamma(t)
, and the derivatives of
x
and
y
are taken with respect to
t
.

From the first equation, we deduce that there exists
u:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$},0)\to\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}
,
with
u(0)\neq 0
, and an integer
k\geq 0
, such that:

f_{x}(x(t),y(t))=u(t)t^{k}y^{\prime}(t)\quad\textrm{and}\quad f_{y}(x(t),y(t))=-u(t)t^{k}x^{\prime}(t).

(6)

Now observe that
f(0,y)=y^{m}
for
y\neq 0
small, so by Teissier’s Lemma (see
[
4
]
):

m(f(x,y),f_{y}(x,y))=\mu(f)+m(f(0,y),x)-1=\mu(f)+m-1.

(7)

Using (
4
), (
7
) and (
6
) we get:

\textrm{ord}(f_{y}(x(t),y(t)))=\mu(f)+m-1=k+m-1,

hence
k=\mu(f)
.

Multiplying the second equation of (
5
) by
u(t)^{2}t^{2k}
and using (
6
), we find:

\left(f_{xx}f_{y}^{2}-2f_{xy}f_{x}f_{y}+f_{yy}f_{x}^{2}\right)(x(t),y(t))=-u(t)^{3}t^{3k}(x^{\prime\prime}(t)y^{\prime}(t)-y^{\prime\prime}(t)x^{\prime}(t)),

that is,
i_{f}(\gamma(t))=-u(t)^{3}t^{3k}i_{\gamma}(t)
. Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}I_{f}&=&m(f,i_{f})=\textrm{ord}(i_{f}\circ\gamma)=\textrm{ord}(-u(t)^{3}t^{3k}i_{\gamma}(t))\\
&=&\textrm{ord}(i_{\gamma})+3k=I_{\gamma}+3k=I_{\gamma}+3\mu(f).\end{array}

(8)

Multiplying the third equation of (
5
) by
(f_{x}^{2}+f_{y}^{2})u(t)^{3}t^{3k}
, and using (
6
) and the second equation of (
5
), it follows that
v_{f}(\gamma(t))=-u(t)^{6}t^{6k}v_{\gamma}(t)
.
Using calculations similar to those in (
8
), we conclude that

V_{f}=V_{\gamma}+6k=V_{\gamma}+6\mu(f)
.

Part (3) follows from parts (1) and (2) above, together with Theorem
3.4
and Theorem 6.5.1 of
[
17
]
, which shows that

\mu(f)=\displaystyle{\sum_{i}}\mu(f_{i})+2\displaystyle{\sum_{i<j}}m(f_{i},f_{j})-n+1.

Vertices and inflections of curves in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{2}

Given a real analytic curve
f(x,y)=0
in the Euclidean plane
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{2}
, we can consider its complexification

f_{c}(x,y)=0
in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{C}$}^{2}
, and define
I_{f}
(resp.
V_{f}
) as
I_{f_{c}}
(resp.
V_{f_{c}}
).
Then
I_{f}
(resp.
V_{f}
) provides an upper bound on the number of inflections (resp. vertices) concentrated at the singularity.
Denote by
{\mathscr{R}}I_{f}
(resp.
{\mathscr{R}}V_{f}
) the maximum number of real inflections (resp. vertices) concentrated at the singular point that can appear when deforming the curve. Thus,

{\mathscr{R}}I_{f}\leq I_{f}
and
{\mathscr{R}}V_{f}\leq V_{f}
. If
f
is merely smooth, then provided
(f,i_{f})
(resp.
(f,v_{f})
) is a finite mapping, we still obtain a well-defined upper bound, and these are finite maps for all

off a set of infinite codimension.

It is shown in
[
6
,
8
]
that for an
A_{1}^{+}
-singularity (i.e.,
f\sim_{\mathcal{R}}\pm(x^{2}+y^{2})
),
{\mathscr{R}}I_{f}=0
and
{\mathscr{R}}V_{f}=4
, and
for an
A_{1}^{-}
-singularity (i.e.,
f\sim_{\mathcal{R}}\pm(x^{2}-y^{2})
),
{\mathscr{R}}I_{f}=2
and
{\mathscr{R}}V_{f}=6
.
Since we have
I_{f_{c}}=6
and
V_{f_{c}}=12
for an
A_{1}
-singularity (following the arguments
in Example
4.2
), this implies that for each double point, we need to remove at least 4 inflections and at least 6 vertices.
This suggests that for an irreducible germ
f
,

{\mathscr{R}}I_{f}\leq I_{f}-4\delta=I_{f}-2\mu
and
{\mathscr{R}}V_{f}\leq V_{f}-6\delta=V_{f}-3\mu
.

There is a well-defined notion of the degree of a real map germ
F:(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{2},0)\to(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{2},0)
, which is given by the topological degree of the mapping
F/||F||:S^{\epsilon}\to S^{1}
, where
S^{1}
is the oriented unit circle, and
S^{\epsilon}
is the positively oriented circle of radius
\epsilon
, both oriented consistently with
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. If
F
is differentiable, the degree can be computed as the sum of the signs of the Jacobian determinant of
F
at all preimages of a regular value sufficiently close to the origin. For an explicit formula, see
[
7
]
.

If we consider the map-germ
F=(f,i_{f})
, then an ordinary inflection has degree (or index)
\pm 1
.
More precisely, if we choose a local frame given by the tangent and normal vectors to the curve, the is index
+1
(resp.
-1
) if the curve near the inflection lies in the first and third (respectively, second and fourth) quadrants; see Figure
1
.

For vertices, considering the map-germ
F=(f,v_{f})
, an ordinary vertex also has index
\pm 1
.
In
[
13
]
, the notions of inward and outward vertices are defined based on the relative position of the evolute; see Figure
1
. A vertex is inward if
\kappa\kappa^{\prime\prime}>0
, and outward otherwise.
If we choose a frame given by the tangent and normal vectors to the curve, then the index is
+1
at an inward vertex and
-1
at an outward vertex when
\kappa(0)>0
; the signs are reversed when
\kappa(0)<0
.

For a singular germ, the degree of
(f,i_{f})
(resp.
(f,v_{f})
) gives the sum of the indices of the ordinary inflections (resp. vertices) that appear in a deformation of
f
.
Of course the degree does not give information about the number of inflections or vertices, only their net contribution via their index.
