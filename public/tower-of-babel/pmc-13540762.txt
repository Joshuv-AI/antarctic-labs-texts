Possible delayed neuronal death after seemingly successful evacuation of traumatic acute subdural hematoma: Image report
Authors: Sota Wakahara, Hiroki Ushita, Joji Inamasu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: The term delayed neuronal death (DND) was originally used to describe the histopathological sequence of neuronal injury following severe global brain ischemia. Owing to the lack of histopathological verification, it is technically impossible to confirm DND in clinical settings.Case Description: A 22-year-old military serviceman presenting with clinical signs of impending uncal herniation was found to have a traumatic acute subdural hematoma (ASDH) over the left cerebral hemisphere. Following emergency hematoma evacuation, signs of brainstem dysfunction rapidly resolved, and brain computed tomography (CT) obtained the following day appeared unremarkable. However, a low-density area (LDA) emerged on CT obtained 4 days postoperatively in the left temporal lobe, which did not exhibit interval change in the extent and degree. Although the LDA eventually resolved, atrophy of the left temporal lobe was observed 1 month after injury.Conclusion: Among various possible reasons for the delayed emergence of the LDA, we hypothesized that DND might have played a role based on its unique chronological change in the imaging studies. Awareness of the possibility of DND may help neurosurgeons facilitate outcome prediction in patients with severe traumatic brain injury who exhibit initially unremarkable postoperative imaging.

INTRODUCTION

The phenomenon of delayed neuronal death (DND), which manifests several days after severe global brain ischemia, has been recognized for decades.[5] It has been consistently demonstrated through histopathological studies on animals.[5] Although numerous experimental studies have investigated the pathomechanisms of DND, imaging findings suggestive of DND have rarely been reported in clinical settings.[9] Here, we report a case of traumatic acute subdural hematoma (ASDH) in which a diffuse low-density area (LDA) on the brain computed tomography (CT) emerged several days after seemingly successful surgical evacuation of the ASDH. We list several possible causes for the delayed appearance of LDA and explain why DND might have played a role in the findings we observed.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 22-year-old military serviceman suddenly collapsed during a judo practice match, 30 min after being thrown to the ground by his opponent. He was immediately transported to our institution by ambulance. Upon arrival at the emergency department, he was comatose with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of E1V1M4, and his pupils were bilaterally dilated and nonreactive to light. His vital signs, including blood pressure and respiratory pattern, were stable, and an arterial blood gas analysis showed oxygen saturation of 98% and intact acid-base balance on room air.

Brain CT revealed a left-sided ASDH causing severe compression of the brainstem [Figure 1a], which was considered responsible for his symptoms. He was immediately taken to the operating theater and underwent hematoma evacuation via a left frontotemporal craniotomy. No brain swelling or cortical surface damage was observed intraoperatively, and neither placement of an intracranial pressure probe nor a decompressive craniectomy was considered necessary. The bleeding source was identified as a rupture of a bridging vein. Intraoperative blood loss was 150 g, and there was no episode of hypotension or hypoxia during surgery.

(a) A left-sided traumatic acute subdural hematoma (ASDH) causing severe compression of the brainstem. (b) A brain computed tomography (CT) immediately after surgery showing restoration of brainstem configuration with seemingly intact gray-white matter border. (c) A CT on postoperative day (POD)-1 showing no marked interval change with Hounsfield Unit (HU) value of 32 (white asterisk). (d) A POD-4 CT showing the emergence of a low-density area (LDA) in the left temporal lobe with an HU value of 24 (in the center of LDA, white asterisk). (e) A POD-5 diffusion-weighted imaging demonstrating moderately increased signal intensity in the left temporal lobe and hippocampus. (f) While apparent diffusion coefficient was mildly decreased, (g) fluid attenuated inversion recovery images were considered unremarkable. (h) A POD-10 CT showing no marked interval change in the extent and degree of LDA in the left temporal lobe with an HU of 23 (white asterisk). (i) A POD-20 CT showing resolution of the LDA with an HU value of 34 (white asterisk), in addition to mild enlargement of the temporal horn and atrophy of the left frontal lobe. (j) A POD-30 CT with an HU value of 36 (white asterisk) showing slight progression in the degree of enlargement of the temporal horn.

Brain CT immediately after surgery demonstrated restoration of brainstem configuration and preservation of the gray– white matter differentiation in the left cerebral hemisphere [Figure 1b]. The operative time was 55 min, and the interval between preoperative and the brain CT was <3 h. No interval worsening was identified on the CT obtained the following day [Day 1; Figure 1c].

Although pupillary size normalized and light reflexes recovered promptly after surgery, the patient remained comatose. A serial electroencephalogram (EEG) did not reveal epileptiform waves except for the presence of a low-voltage α-wave in the left cerebral hemisphere. CT obtained on postoperative day 4 [Figure 1d] revealed the emergence of an LDA in the left temporal lobe with loss of gray–white matter differentiation. Magnetic resonance imaging on postoperative day 5 demonstrated mildly high signal intensity in the left temporal lobe and hippocampus on diffusion-weighted imaging [Figure 1e] and mildly decreased apparent diffusion coefficient [Figure 1f]. Fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images [Figure 1g] were considered unremarkable by our radiologists.

CT on postoperative day 10 [Figure 1h] showed no marked interval change in either the extent or degree of the LDA. By postoperative day 20 [Figure 1i], the LDA had mostly resolved, accompanied by mild enlargement of the temporal horn and atrophy of the left frontal lobe. On postoperative day 30 [Figure 1j], enlargement of the temporal horn had progressed slightly.

The patient was transferred to a rehabilitation facility 83 days after surgery with a modified Rankin Scale Score of 3. While he became fully ambulatory, he required assistance for daily activities because of moderate cognitive impairment, which would likely preclude him from returning to military service. Hounsfield Unit (HU) values were measured retrospectively on serial brain CTs at the center of LDA in the left temporal lobe (white asterisks). The HU values were: 32 on day 1, 24 on day 4, 23 on day 10, 34 on day 20, and 36 on day 30 [Figure 1c, d, h-j]. The same CT scanner had been used throughout his hospitalization.

DISCUSSION

Although the patient initially presented in a comatose state with bilaterally dilated pupils indicative of impending uncal herniation due to traumatic ASDH, pupillary reactivity returned immediately after surgery. Furthermore, no LDA was observed in the left occipital lobe on postoperative day 1 CT, suggesting that the left posterior cerebral artery remained patent despite compression by the displaced left cerebral hemisphere.[2,11] Given the prompt surgical intervention, we anticipated optimistically that the patient could have been treated within a critical therapeutic time window for severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) and might recover without marked neurological sequelae.[7] Therefore, the subsequent emergence of LDA in the left temporal lobe, followed by progressive cortical atrophy, was unexpected.

The cause of the delayed emergence of LDA remains unknown in the present case. Because of the absence of systemic hypoxia, circulatory disturbance, or acid-base imbalance during the perioperative period, posttraumatic metabolic brain injury might be excluded. The absence of epileptiform waves on serial EEG implies that excitotoxic neuronal injury has not played a major role.[1] The seemingly unremarkable FLAIR images [Figure 1h] imply that vasogenic edema and venous infarction have not played a major role, either.[3,4,6] After excluding those possibilities, we hypothesized that the delayed emergence of the LDA might be explainable, at least in part, by DND.

DND was originally defined as a histopathological sequence of neuronal injury following severe global brain ischemia.[5] In addition to its characteristic delayed neuronal loss, DND is notable for minimal edema formation around dying neurons.[5] The delayed emergence of a diffuse yet moderate LDA on serial CT without marked interval change in its extent and degree of LDA in the present case suggests a neuronal injury process compatible with DND. The LDA observed in Figure 1d and h eventually became isodense in Figure 1i and j, implying that not all cells within the affected region had undergone cell death. Having said that, definitive diagnosis of DND in clinical settings – including in the present case – is not feasible due to the absence of histopathological confirmation. Furthermore, detection of the delayed LDA in the present case might have been made possible simply because we had acquired brain CT repeatedly within a short interval.[10]

Considering that neurons are more vulnerable to severe ischemia than astrocytes,[8] it is plausible that selective neuronal loss might have occurred following transient severe brain ischemia secondary to the ASDH in the present case. We acknowledge that our hypothesis is based on the assumption that the clinical scenario was relatively close to the settings of the global brain ischemia models: In other words, we assumed that a rapid increase in the intracranial pressure had only been transient and that cortical injury by the initial impact had been negligible. Those assumptions, however, are unverifiable in reality.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, awareness of the DND-like phenomenon may help facilitate clinical prediction of outcomes in patients with severe TBI who initially demonstrate unremarkable postoperative imaging.
