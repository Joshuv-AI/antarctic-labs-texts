Prehospital extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation in refractory out-of-hospital cardiac arrest: retrospective eligibility verification should not be conflated with prospective triage performance
Authors: Ming-Hui Hung, Shu-Yueh Cheng
Published: 2026

We read with interest the RECA study by Lunz et al., which appears to be the largest prospective cohort of prehospital extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) reported to date [1]. The study provides real-world data on the feasibility of integrating ECPR into a regional emergency medical system.

We would welcome clarification on one numerical inconsistency. The Results state that 67 of 137 retrospectively verified eligible patients survived to hospital discharge, corresponding to 48.9%. The Discussion states that restricting ECPR to this cohort would have yielded survival “at more than 60%” [1]. This difference affects how protocol adherence is interpreted.

The apparent survival advantage among retrospectively verified eligible patients should be interpreted with caution. Verification of key eligibility items, including witnessed arrest, bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) within 10 min, and absence of major premorbid multimorbidity, relied on information obtained during hospitalization rather than at the scene [1]. Retrospective verification is not the same as real-time prehospital triage.

This matters because post hoc information may not have been equally complete across outcome groups. Survivors had longer hospital stays than non-survivors (median 22 vs. 3 days). This allowed more time for family interviews, chart review, and recovery of prehospital details [1]. Table 2 is consistent with this concern: only 4 of 74 survivors (5.4%) were classified as unwitnessed, compared with 47 of 139 non-survivors (33.8%) [1]. This difference may reflect true prognostic enrichment. It may also reflect survival-related differences in documentation. The reported association between retrospectively verified eligibility and survival (odds ratio 4.34) should therefore not be assumed to reflect the effect size achievable when the same criteria are applied prospectively in the field.

A survival advantage seen after retrospective eligibility verification does not necessarily predict the same benefit from stricter exclusion at the scene, where information is incomplete. Conflating retrospective enrichment with prospective triage performance may overstate the value of post hoc verified criteria.

We therefore suggest that future analyses of this dataset test whether a triage model based only on variables available in real time can identify a group with similar survival. These variables may include initial cardiac rhythm, pupillary status, CPR-to-ECMO interval, and point-of-care cerebral oximetry [2–5]. This would provide a stronger basis for refining prehospital ECPR selection criteria and help separate true selection effects from information bias related to post hoc verification.

We look forward to the authors’ response regarding both the reported survival figure and the interpretation of retrospectively verified eligibility.
