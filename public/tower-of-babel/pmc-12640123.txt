Bioprospecting and phylogenetic analysis of Priestia flexa AW3: an industrially significant amylase-producing bacterium from unexplored contaminated soil in Layyah
Authors: Sobia Ramzan, Musrat Shaheen, Mohsin Khurshid, Farhat Jabeen, Sara Mahmood, Ayesha Sarwar, Shahzad Ahmad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn spite of wonderful industrial applications of microbial enzymes, still most of habitats in various parts of world are unexplored for bioprospecting of industrial potent microbes. This study represents the first bioprospecting effort in Layyah district to explore indigenous bacterial diversity, enzymatic potential, and phylogenetic relationships in untapped contaminated soil habitat. Contaminated soils serve as reservoirs of industrially significant bacteria with unique enzymatic degradation capabilities, offering solutions for sustainable industrial applications and environmental remediation. An effort for comparative bioprospecting-based study for bacterial diversity exhibiting amylase potential across unaddressed contaminated soil samples [industrial, household, poultry, and animal waste (AW)] using qualitative and quantitative methods, was conducted. AW-contaminated soil exhibited the highest bacterial load (2.51 × 1010 CFUs) and amylase activity (51, amylase zones), whereas industrial waste soil showed the lowest CFUs (1.24 × 1010). Household waste soil, however, displayed the greatest Shannon diversity index (H’= 2.192262) for amylase-producing bacteria. Among isolates, Priestia flexa AW3 (OQ446563) demonstrated exceptional amylase production, forming 30 mm hydrolysis zones on starch agar and achieving optimal activity (1.76 ± 0.05 OD; 1.23 ± 0.03 AU/mL) at pH 7 and 37°C after 48 h. Notably, the strain retained enzymatic stability under extreme conditions, temperature up to 50°C, NaCl concentrations (0.5%–10%), and a broad pH range. Phylogenetic analysis via 16S rRNA sequencing confirmed its identity as P. flexa. This study underscores the untapped potential of contaminated soils in Layyah as sources of robust industrial microbes and highlights the value of bioprospecting in discovering novel bacterial strains for biotechnology and environmental sustainability. One-Sentence Summary: Bioprospecting for industrially important bacteria with unique enzymatic potential from untapped habitats is highly needed to solve sever environmental problems.

Introduction

Bioprospecting is increasingly critical for exploring unknown or untapped habitats to identify indigenous microbes with industrial potential, addressing rising industrial demands, environmental challenges, and antimicrobial resistance issues. Indigenous bacteria serve as key drivers of adaptation in harsh environments such as contaminated soils. Consequently, bioprospecting unexplored habitats is essential to catalog microbial diversity and understand their unique consortia interactions, particularly as factors like rapid industrialization, population growth, and climate change glacial melting, and extreme weather which accelerate ecological disruption (Bhutani et al., 2021; Hosseini et al., 2022; Shintani et al., 2021; Stocka et al., 2024; Vuong et al., 2022). To survive these abrupt changes, microbes rapidly evolve metabolic adaptations, including structural and functional modifications mediated by enzymes, their primary biochemical tools (Bhimani et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2023).

While diverse microbes have been documented to produce enzymes such as proteases, lipases, amylases, xylanases, laccases, invertases, and phosphatases (Pathade, 2024), few exhibit multienzymatic potential. Microbial enzymes are preferred in industrial processes due to their stability, specificity, and cost-effectiveness (Chia et al., 2024; Muhammad et al., 2024, Alonazi et al., 2021). Environments including soils (leguminous plant-associated, agricultural, contaminated), industrial effluents, municipal waste, wastewater, and extreme habitats (glaciers, deep-sea sediments, thermal vents) have been bio-prospected for industrially relevant microorganisms (Obi et al., 2019; Timilsina et al.,2020; Seiyaboh et al., 2020; Ginting et al., 2021; Munawar et al., 2023). Under varying conditions, these bacteria demonstrate robust enzymatic capabilities (e.g. amylases, proteases, lipases) that can be harnessed for industrial applications (Fasiku et al., 2020; Mahmood et al., 2018). Approximately 88 % industrial enzymes are produced from microbial sources including fungi, bacteria and yeast 60%, 24%, and 4% respectively (Fasim et al.,2021), among various enzymes proteases cover 65, amylase 30 percent and rest of lipases, cover global enzyme market and are in high demand due to their versatility (Banerjee et al., 2017, Kholikov et al., 2025).

Soil microorganisms, particularly those in contaminated environments, play pivotal roles in ecosystem functioning through enzymatic activity. Amylase, a starch-hydrolyzing enzyme produced by soil bacteria and fungi, is of significant industrial interest. Soil serves as a proven reservoir for isolating amylolytic bacteria, with starch-rich soil offering ideal substrates for these microbes (Balakrishnan and Shilpa, 2020; Luang-In et al., 2019). Microbial amylases are favored industrially for their rapid microbial growth, scalability, genetic manipulability, and energy-efficient production compared to chemical methods (Balakrishnan and Shilpa, 2020; Yao et al., 2021). Key amylase producers include Bacillus licheniformis, B. subtilis, B. megaterium, B. stearothermophilus, Lactobacillus spp., Pseudomonas spp., Aspergillus spp., and Thermomyces lanuginosus (Mahmood et al., 2018; Rehman et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2021).

Enzyme production is influenced by physicochemical factors such as pH, temperature, substrate availability, and salinity. Most industrially relevant amylases from Bacillus spp. exhibit optimal activity at pH 6.0–7.0 and mesophilic temperatures, though thermophilic and psychrophilic variants exist (Kherouf et al, 2021; Abootalebi et al., 2021; Kiran et al., 2018; Luang-In et al., 2019). Amylases are classified into α-, β-, and γ-types based on cleavage specificity, α-amylase (endo-acting, random starch chain cleavage), β-amylase (exo-acting, releasing maltose), and γ-amylase (exo-acting, cleaving both α-1–4 and α-1,6 glycosidic bonds). Bacillus species, including B. stearothermophilus, B. subtilis, and B. licheniformis, are prominent α-amylase producers, with ongoing research targeting strains exhibiting raw starch degradation and industrial robustness (Ahmed et al., 2019; Obi et al., 2019; Saleh et al., 2020).

In spite of remarkable applications of microbial enzymes, still most of habitats in various parts of world are unexplored for bioprospecting of industrial potent microbes. This study mainly aims to investigate the untapped contaminated soil of Layyah in terms of bioprospecting for industrially important enzyme producing bacteria under various wide range of culturing conditions for both growth and enzyme production including pH, temperature, substrates, NaCl and incubation period and their diversity and phylogenetic analysis of potent isolate from this contaminated habitat.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection

Total (35) soil samples, from five distinct contaminated soil sites [animal waste (AW), fruit waste (FW), household waste (HW), poultry waste, industrial waste) were collected, in Layyah city, Punjab, Pakistan. Samples were obtained using sterile spatulas, 15 mL Falcon tubes, and polyethylene bags, collected from one inch from the surface of the soil. Samples were transported to the research laboratory (Dept. of Zoology, GCUF, Faisalabad) and kept at 4°C, −20°C for subsequent analysis.

Isolation, Screening, and Selecting of Amylase Producing Bacteria

Amylase potent bacteria were isolated using the serial dilution technique. Ten test tubes, each containing 9 mL of (D.W), were sterilized at (121°C, 15 psi) for 20 min. One (g) soil from each sample, mixed to 9 mL of sterile distilled water. Serial dilutions (up to 109) were prepared by transferring 1 mL aliquots between tubes under sterile conditions in a laminar airflow chamber.

Media plates, containing (2.8 g) nutrient agar, 1% substrate (starch or lactose) in 100 mL distilled water within a flask, were prepared. The mixture was stirred thoroughly, autoclaved, and dispensed (20 mL per plate) into sterile petri dishes. After solidification, plates were placed for incubation at 37°C for (24 h) to confirm sterility.

For bacterial isolation, 0.1 mL aliquots from diluted samples were spread onto nutrient agar medium (NAM) using the spread-plate technique. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Colony-forming units, colony morphology (color, size, shape, arrangement), and amylase activity (hydrolysis zone diameter in mm) were recorded. Distinct colonies were sub-cultured on starch-plates, incubated at 37°C, for 24 h. Pure cultures, stored at 4°C for further utilization. The isolate with the largest hydrolysis zone was selected for optimization. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Basic Medium Preparation: nutrient broth (2.5 g), 1% starch, or 1% lactose was dissolved in 100 mL distilled water in flasks, autoclaved, and inoculated with a 10% (v/v) bacterial culture, incubation done in a shaker at150 rpm, at 37°C. Five ml of culture broth from shaking incubator, were collected at interval of 24–72 h and centrifuged (10 000 rpm, 15 min), supernatants were strained and preserved at 4°C and used for assessment of amylase efficacy and stability under different conditions.

Influence of pH (6–8) on isolate (AW3) development and amylase synthesis in starch and lactose, were observed separately by varying the pH of the medium. An isolate (AW3), cultured in 100 ml medium with 1 percent, starch and lactose, incubated at 37°C, 150 rpm for 3 days.

The temperature impact on isolate (AW3) and amylase production, was determined by variable temperature of medium (25°C, 37°C, and 50°C). Isolate (AW3), grown in flask with (100 ml) basic media having 1% starch and 1% lactose one at a time, for seventy-two hrs.

The effect of substrates was determined on the growth of bacterial strain (AW3) and production of amylase by using 1% starch and 1% lactose one by one in production medium.

Isolates from the 35 soil samples exhibited distinct starch hydrolysis zones ranging from 1 to 30 mm in diameter. Optimization of Culture Conditions for Amylase Production

Cultural conditions such as pH, temperature, incubation time, substrate type, and salinity significantly influence microbial growth and enzyme production. Optimizing these parameters is essential for scaling up the production of industrially relevant bacterial enzymes (Pinjari and Kotari, 2018; Ravishankar et al., 2024).

Effect of pH on Amylase Production

The influence of pH (6–8) on AW3 growth and amylase production (amylase activity) was evaluated. It was found that amylase from AW3 was able to work under wide range of pH but significant differences (p = 0.00000386, F = 72.463) in quantity of amylase has been found with the change in time from initial to 72 h period. Maximum bacterial growth (1.76 ± 0.05 OD) and amylase activity (1.23 ± 0.03 AU/mL) occurred at pH 7 using starch as a substrate, while the lowest values (0.69 ± 0.02 OD and 1.1 ± 0.02 AU/mL, respectively) were observed at pH 6 (Fig. 3a, b). These results align with Rehman et al., (2019), Awasthi et al., (2018) and Hasan et al., (2017), who identified pH 7 as optimal for amylase activity in Brevibacillus borstelensis and B. licheniformis. In contrast, Rathour et al., (2020) and Bhatt et al., (2020) reported optimal production at pH 8. Variations in pH disrupt hydrogen bonding critically for maintaining enzyme tertiary structure and alter cellular morphology (e.g. cell shape, mass, and moisture content), directly affecting enzymatic function (Nimisha et al., 2019; Pinjari and Kotari, 2018).

Effect of Cultivation Temperature on Amylase Production

AW3 exhibited optimal growth (1.76 ± 0.05 OD) and amylase activity (1.23 ± 0.03 AU/mL) at 37°C in starch-supplemented medium (Fig. 4a, b). Minimal growth (0.97 ± 0.05 OD) and activity (1.01 ± 0.01 AU/mL) were observed at 50°C. These findings differ from studies reporting higher optimal temperatures by Hasan et al., (2017) and identified 50°C as optimal for Chryseobacterium sp., while Aladejana et al., (2020), Arekemase et al., (2020) and Rehman et al., (2019) reported peak amylase production at 45°C. Similarly, Mahmood et al., (2018) and Dutta et al., (2016) noted optimal activity at 40°C. Psychrophilic α-amylases, such as those reported by Rathour et al., 2020), function optimally at 4°C, whereas Bacillus subtilis and Paraclostridium benzoelyticum exhibit maximal amylase production at 55°C (Afridi et al., 2020). Notably, some studies observed peak amylase activity at 60°C (Kherouf et al, 2021; Kiran et al., 2018), highlighting the thermophilic adaptability of certain strains. Overall, AW3 amylase was active under good range of temperature with minor difference but enzyme quantity significantly increased with interval of time (P = 0.000, F = 27.31).

Effect of Substrates and Incubation Time on Amylase Activity

Substrates starch and lactose were slightly found differ in terms of enzyme production, but incubation duration highly significantly (p = 0.00, F = 17.03) influenced AW3 growth and amylase production. Maximum growth (1.63 ± 0.02 OD) and amylase activity (1.35 ± 0.07 AU/mL) occurred with 1% starch (Fig. 5a, b), while in lactose media, following observations were found (1.51 ± 0.06) and (1.28 ± 0.02 AU/mL) after 48 h at pH 7 and 37°C, respectively. The superiority of starch as a substrate aligns with studies on Aspergillus niger BTM26, Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, and other species (Aladejana et al., 2020; Dutta et al., 2016; Luang-In et al., 2019; Rehman et al., 2019; Saleh et al., 2020; Trabelsi et aal., 2019; Ubi et al., 2024). However, Rathour et al., 2020) reported peak extracellular α-amylase activity at 12 h of incubation, while Ahmad et al., (2021) observed maximal activity after 24 h, underscoring strain-specific variations.

Molecular Identification and Phylogenetic Analysis

16S rRNA sequence (BLAST) analysis of isolate AW3 revealed 99% similarity to P. flexa (family Bacillaceae). A neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree (MEGA X, 1000 bootstrap replicates, p-distance method) confirmed its evolutionary relationships with 15 reference strains (Fig. 7). Despite the industrial potential of P. flexa, only few studies detail about this strain and its enzymatic capabilities have been reported (Bhimani et al., 2024; Ravishankar et al., 2024). Recent work highlights its role in raw starch digestion and bioethanol production (Saleh et al., 2020; Ubi et al., 2024), emphasizing the industrial potential of this isolate from contaminated soils.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that contaminated soil ecosystems in Layyah, Pakistan, harbor diverse indigenous bacteria with significant potential for industrial amylase production. Among the five contaminated waste soil sites analyzed, AW soil exhibited the highest bacterial load (2.51 × 1011 CFUs) and amylase activity (51 hydrolysis zones), while HW soil displayed the greatest Shannon diversity (H’= 2.14) of amylolytic bacteria. The isolate P. flexa AW3 (OQ446563), selected for its exceptional amylase production (30 mm hydrolysis zone), demonstrated robust enzymatic activity under a broad range of conditions; optimal pH (7), temperature (37°C), and NaCl tolerance of AW3 (up to 10%). Notably, AW3 retained stability at elevated temperatures (50°C) and maintained activity across pH 6–8, underscoring its adaptability to industrial-scale processes.

These findings highlight the untapped potential of contaminated soils as reservoirs of industrially relevant microbes. The phylogenetic identification of AW3 through 16S rRNA sequencing not only expands the known biogeography of P. flexa but also positions this strain as a candidate for applications in biotechnology, such as starch processing, biofuel production, and waste management. The ability of this strain to thrive under variable physicochemical conditions aligns with the growing demand for robust, cost-effective enzymes in sectors requiring high-temperature or saline environments.

This study underscores the critical need for bioprospecting unexplored habitats to uncover microbial diversity with unique enzymatic capabilities, especially in this region, due to narrow spectrum of research. Future research should focus on molecular characterization of this strain for the amylase genes to elucidate their catalytic mechanisms and scaling enzyme production using fermentation technologies. By linking microbial diversity to sustainable industrial practices, this study advocates for the strategic exploitation of extremotolerant bacteria to address global challenges such as waste degradation, renewable energy production, and environmentally friendly manufacturing.
