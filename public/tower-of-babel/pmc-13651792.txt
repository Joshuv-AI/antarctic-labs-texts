Intermembrane coupling between Bcl-xL and the IP3 receptor supports local Ca2+ transfer at ER-mitochondrial contacts
Authors: Arijita Ghosh, David Weaver, Chi Li, György Hajnóczky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYBcl-xL, an anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family protein, engages laterally with Bak/Bax in the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) to inhibit apoptosis and interacts with the IP3 receptor Ca2+ channels (IP3Rs) in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane to control Ca2+ release. It is unknown if OMM-localized Bcl-xL can also interact in trans with IP3Rs at ER-mitochondrial contacts to form a tethering complex that supports IP3R-mediated local Ca2+ transfer from ER to mitochondria. We establish that IP3R-mitochondria Ca2+ signal propagation depends on Bcl-xL. By targeting Bcl-xL specifically to different subcellular compartments, we find that OMM-localized Bcl-xL increases the efficacy of ER-mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer without changing ER Ca2+ release, despite attenuating mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake. We find interaction between Bcl-xL and each IP3R isoform occurring at the mitochondria and a complex formed by OMM-localized Bcl-xL and IP3Rs. OMM Bcl-xL interacts with IP3Rs in trans at ER-mitochondrial contacts to optimize local Ca2+ signal propagation into the mitochondria.

INTRODUCTION

Mitochondria are central to many cellular processes including energy metabolism, redox signaling, and apoptosis; mitochondrial matrix [Ca2+] ([Ca2+]m) elevations contribute to the underlying mechanisms of all these functions. The main sources for [Ca2+]m increases are uptake of Ca2+ released from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Ca2+ entering through the plasma membrane.1,2

Regions of the ER membrane are closely associated with the mitochondria, forming ER-mitochondria contacts (ERMCs), which have been implicated in a wide range of mitochondrial functions including calcium signaling.3,4 ERMCs have been reported in many cell types and tissues, and their disruption has been linked to a broad range of diseases.5–7 The close membrane associations of the ERMC set the stage for local Ca2+ transport from ER to the mitochondria involving the ER-resident inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptors (IP3Rs) and outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) localized voltage-dependent anion channels (VDACs).8,9 Upon stimulation, IP3 binds to IP3Rs, leading to Ca2+ mobilization from ER into the cytosol and across the OMM via VDAC. Local micromolar [Ca2+] at ERMCs supports the opening of the mitochondrial calcium uniporter (mtCU) in the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) to relay Ca2+ into the mitochondrial matrix.10,11 The resulting matrix [Ca2+]m rise drives ATP production, ROS generation and/or mitochondrial membrane permeabilization in synergy with other factors dictating cell fate.

IP3R channel function is modulated by a host of interacting proteins, including members of the Bcl-2 family.12 Bcl-2 family proteins, along with their canonical roles as apoptosis regulators, have emerged as intracellular Ca2+ modulators. The anti-apoptotic Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, Mcl-1, and Bcl-2 ovarian killer (BOK) have all been reported to modulate Ca2+ signals by interacting with IP3Rs.13–15 In cancer, the frequently aberrant expression of Bcl-2 proteins can influence cell survival via altered IP3R-mediated Ca2+ signaling and has been explored as a therapeutic target.16–18 Bcl-2, primarily localized to the ER, inhibits Ca2+ release via its BH4 domain interaction with IP3R1.13,19,20 However, Bcl-2-IP3R interaction is also associated with increased IP3R phosphorylation and Ca2+ leak.21,22 BOK was reported to regulate ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer because of a cis-interaction with IP3R in the ER.23 Another Bcl-2 family protein, Bcl-w, interacts with the ARM2 domain of IP3R1 via its BH4 domain, resulting in neuroprotection in sensory axons treated with a chemotherapeutic agent.24

Bcl-xL, such as Bcl-2, has been shown to interact with IP3Rs in multiple ways, but, unlike Bcl-2, the majority of Bcl-xL is localized to the OMM.25 ER-localized Bcl-xL has been shown to interact with both the N-terminal ligand-binding domain and the central modulatory domain of IP3R1 with inhibitory effects on channel function.26 Interaction with the C terminus of IP3R1 at two different BH3-like motifs results in sensitization of IP3R1 to IP3, leading to Ca2+ release that generates pro-survival signals promoting mitochondrial energetics.27,28 The same group reported that, at high expression, Bcl-xL interacts with the central coupling domain of IP3R1, leading to inhibition of IP3R.28 In the OMM, Bcl-xL was reported to interact with VDAC through its BH4 domain to reduce channel activity of VDAC and inhibit mitochondrial Ca2+ signals. Notably, these results were observed in peptide-based studies.29 Interestingly, the interaction of Bcl-xL with VDAC1/3 in mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells has been correlated with increased ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer.30

Studies with Bcl-xL targeted exclusively to the ER or OMM showed that ER-localized Bcl-xL impacted Ca2+ homeostasis without any effect on apoptosis, whereas maximal effect on apoptosis was observed when Bcl-xL was present at both ER and mitochondria.31 In a recent study, MEF cells expressing ER Bcl-xL exhibited protection against ER stress by inhibiting IP3R-mediated ER Ca2+ release and were more susceptible to apoptosis compared to cells expressing mitochondria-targeted Bcl-xL.32 Mitochondria-specific Bcl-xL was also shown to regulate mitochondrial respiration and ATP generation.33

Until now, the focus of research was on the lateral, intramembrane interactions of ER-localized Bcl-2/Bcl-xL with IP3R and the OMM-localized Bcl-2/Bcl-xL with VDAC1, and at both locations with the proapoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins, BAX and BAK. However, the potential of a trans interaction between OMM Bcl-xL and the IP3Rs to regulate local Ca2+ transfer from IP3Rs to the OMM at the ERMC has never been considered. There is ample evidence that Bcl-2/Bcl-xL affect both ER Ca2+ release and mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake. Moreover, sub-cellular fractionation studies have found Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL at ERMCs,29,34 where the ER membrane and the OMM can be within 10 nm. IP3Rs support the formation of these tight ERMCs.8 Several partners have been described: Grp75 binds to IP3R1/3 and VDAC19 and, more recently, FUNDC1,35 TOM7036 and IRE1α37 were also linked to IP3R and VDAC1. Here we test the hypothesis that mitochondrial Bcl-xL and its proximity to both the IP3R and VDAC put it in an advantageous configuration to strengthen local Ca2+ communication between ER and mitochondria.

RESULTS

Bcl-xL facilitates IP3R-mediated local ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer

We postulated a trans interaction between the ER-localized IP3R and OMM-localized Bcl-xL supporting the ER-mitochondrial communication. To investigate the role of Bcl-xL in ER-mitochondrial local Ca2+ transfer, we first subjected WT (black) and Bcl-xLKO (gray) MEFs to carbachol (Cch) (100 μM), which stimulates IP3 formation through the muscarinic receptors. Muscarinic receptors, which were otherwise absent in MEFs, were introduced to the cells by co-transfection with the mitochondrial Ca2+ reporter. We monitored in single cells both the cytosolic ([Ca2+]c) and mitochondrial matrix ([Ca2+]m) rise tracking Fura-2 and mitochondrial matrix-targeted RCaMP (mtRCaMP) fluorescence, respectively (Figures 1A and 1B). In alignment with previous literature, on inhibition of the IP3R-mediated Ca2+ release by Bcl-xL,27,31,38,39 the [Ca2+]c peak was higher in Bcl-xLKO (gray) than in WT cells (black, Figures 1A–1C). Despite a larger [Ca2+]c increase in the Bcl-xLKO, the accompanying [Ca2+]m rise was not significantly different from that in WT (Figures 1A and 1B pink traces, Figure 1D). This indicated that there could be a difference in the [Ca2+]c signal propagation into the mitochondria between WT and Bcl-xLKO, which was difficult to ascertain accurately amidst the differences in the [Ca2+]c increases. We reasoned that the [Ca2+]c signal differences might reflect differences in the Cch-induced IP3 increases between WT and Bcl-xLKO, and Bcl-xL-mediated inhibition of the IP3R activity.

To have a level ground in terms of IP3 and the ensuing [Ca2+]c in WT and Bcl-xLKO, we preloaded with caged IP3 (10 μM) and then subjected these cells to photolysis stimulation. Because UV photolysis interfered with Fura-2 imaging, Cal520 was used to measure [Ca2+]c together with mtRCaMP for recording [Ca2+]m (Figures 1E and 1F). In response to IP3-uncaging, similar [Ca2+]c amplitudes (normalized to the baseline) were obtained in both WT (black) and Bcl-xLKO (gray) cells, respectively (Figures 1E–1G). At the same time, Bcl-xLKO cells showed significantly lower [Ca2+]m elevations (Figures 1E, 1F, and 1H). As a measure of the efficacy of the ER-mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer, we also determined the coupling time between ER Ca2+ release and mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake. This was calculated as the time difference between the half-max of [Ca2+]c and [Ca2+]m peaks and was found to be longer in the absence of Bcl-xL (Figure 1I), indicating more effective Ca2+ signal propagation to the mitochondria in WT in the presence of Bcl-xL. As an additional measure of the mtCU activity, the instantaneous rate of [Ca2+]m was calculated and compared for both WT and Bcl-xLKO, which revealed higher [Ca2+]m increase flux rates of WT as opposed to Bcl-xLKO (Figure 1J). When plotted as a function of the [Ca2+]c signal (Figure S1), the [Ca2+]m started increasing earlier in WT cells than in Bcl-xLKO. Together, all these results point to a potential role of Bcl-xL in promoting IP3-induced ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer.

We next checked if the ER-mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer was restored in Bcl-xLKO cells rescued acutely with full-length Bcl-xL. The rescued cells were identified by expression of mtRCaMP that was co-transfected with the Bcl-xL plasmid. In acutely rescued cells (cyan), the [Ca2+]c rise was unaltered in comparison to WT (black) and Bcl-xLKO (gray) (Figures 2A–2C, and 2D), whereas the [Ca2+]m signal was higher in acute rescue than that in the Bcl-xLKO but still lesser than WT upon photolysis of caged IP3 (Figures 2A–2C and 2E). A similar difference was found for the total area under the curve for [Ca2+]c and [Ca2+]m traces; in the presence of similar [Ca2+]c signals, the accompanying [Ca2+]m signal was least in Bcl-xLKO, followed by acute rescue and WT (Figure 2G). The coupling time between ER Ca2+ release and mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake also tended toward shorter values in the Bcl-xL rescued cells (n = 12) than that in the Bcl-xLKO (Figure 2F). Validation of Bcl-xL expression with immunoblotting in the rescue condition revealed the appearance of the specific band that was weaker than in the WT, consistent with low efficiency of transfection (2–5%) in the MEFs (Figure 2H). Together, these results showed that acute rescue with full-length Bcl-xL can restore the ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer in the Bcl-xL-deficient cells.

OMM-targeted Bcl-xL restores IP3-mediated local ERMC Ca2+ transfer

To test if Bcl-xL had to reside at a particular intracellular location to support ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer, we investigated what happens when Bcl-xLKO cells are substituted with differentially localized Bcl-xL, specifically cytoplasmic, devoid of a membrane targeting signal (cyto Bcl-xL, blue), ER membrane with ER targeting sequence cb5 (ER Bcl-xL, green), and OMM targeting sequence ActA conferring strict mitochondria localization (Mito Bcl-xL, burgundy) in stable rescue MEFs.31 Photolysis of caged IP3 was followed by a similar [Ca2+]c signal among the three groups (Figures 3A–3D) but the [Ca2+]m signal recorded in the case of both ER (green) and Mito Bcl-xL (burgundy) was significantly higher (p < 0.0001) than that of in cyto Bcl-xL (Figures 3A–3C and 3E). Coupling time analysis showed shorter time or effective cyto-mito coupled Ca2+ responses for ER and Mito Bcl-xL compared to cyto Bcl-xL (Figure 3F). [Ca2+]m signal plotted against [Ca2+]c signal increased earlier in the case of Mito Bcl-xL than for ER Bcl-xL, while the response in cyto Bcl-xL was the most delayed (Figure 3G). Moreover, the [Ca2+]m peak and the [Ca2+]m at the time of [Ca2+]c peak were plotted as a function of [Ca2+]c peak, which revealed higher [Ca2+]m signals for mito Bcl-xL (burgundy) as well as for WT (black) and acute rescue (cyan) compared to Bcl-xLKO (gray) (Figure S2A). These results verify that ERMC local Ca2+ transfer is supported by membrane-targeted Bcl-xL, with OMM-localized Bcl-xL having an advantage in this process. The similar effect of OMM-localized Bcl-xL and ER-localized Bcl-xL on local Ca2+ transfer in terms of [Ca2+]m peaks and coupling time (Figures 3E and 3F) prompted further investigation into the expression and localization of organelle-targeted Bcl-xL. An immunofluorescence (IF) experiment with the MEF cells stably expressing ER- and mitochondria-targeted Bcl-xL showed reticular localization along with mixed brighter signals around the nucleus (possibly mitochondria) in the case of ER Bcl-xL vs. well-defined mitochondrial localization of mito Bcl-xL, like the mitochondrial marker MitoBrilliant (Figure S2B). Bcl-xL immunoblotting using whole cell lysates confirmed expression of the differently targeted Bcl-xL constructs (Figure S2C). However, upon cell fractionation followed by immunoblotting, it was revealed that a significant amount of Bcl-xL was found in the mitochondrial fraction in MEF cells expressing ER-targeted Bcl-xL (Figure S2D). These results indicate that the observed [Ca2+]m signal and coupling time in ER Bcl-xL (Figures 3E and 3F) could be contributed by the mitochondrial fraction of Bcl-xL in these cells.

Bcl-xL is dispensable for mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake stimulated by global Ca2+ rise

Next, we checked if the lack of Bcl-xL attenuates uptake of Ca2+ by mitochondria from any origin. WT and Bcl-xLKO were subjected to a SERCA inhibitor, Thapsigargin (Tg, 2 μM), to deplete ER stores and then replenished with extracellular Ca2+ (2 mM), evoking store-operated Ca2+ entry (SOCE) on a global scale. Both WT and Bcl-xLKO cells showed a [Ca2+]m rise; the Bcl-xLKO cells displayed even a significantly larger than the WT (Figures 4A–4D). Thus, Bcl-xLKO mitochondria seem to be fully competent to take up Ca2+ when the global [Ca2+]c rises. A remote possibility remains that mitochondria are more closely positioned to the plasma membrane in the case of Bcl-xLKO cells. To test this, we monitored Ca2+ uptake in cells lacking a plasma membrane barrier next.

We assessed mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake in permeabilized WT and Bcl-xLKO cells upon direct exposure to Ca2+ bolus additions. ER Ca2+ uptake was inhibited by Tg and mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake was measured as the clearance of the extramitochondrial [Ca2+] with fura-FF, simultaneously with ΔѰm using TMRM (Figures 4E–4G). The Ca2+ clearance was abolished by the Ca2+ uniporter inhibitor, Ru265 (1 μM), validating mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake as the sole source of Ca2+ clearance (Figure 4E). The clearance rates were calculated for a dose of Ca2+ (15 nmol) increasing [Ca2+]c to approx. 5 μM and, in the case of more Ca2+ (75 nmol), increasing [Ca2+]c to approx. 20 μM. In summary, Bcl-xLKO mitochondria were more effective in Ca2+ clearance (Figures 4E and 4F) and showed better preservation of the ΔѰm than WT (Figure 4G). Thus, mitochondrial bioenergetics cannot explain the dependence of effective ERMC Ca2+ transfer on Bcl-xL.

Interaction of Bcl-xL and IP3R in the context of OMM localization and ERMC maintenance

Having shown that Bcl-xL is required for the propagation of the IP3R-mediated [Ca2+]c signal to the mitochondria but is not indispensable for IP3R-mediated Ca2+ release and mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake, we reasoned that Bcl-xL might help to connect IP3Rs to the mitochondria. It has also been shown previously that Bcl-xL interacts with the central modulatory region and C-terminal region of IP3R with varying affinities modulating its function14,27,28 but a trans interaction between OMM-localized Bcl-xL and the ER-localized IP3R has not been considered. We set out to explore the physical interaction of Bcl-xL with functionally active IP3R close to the mitochondria and to verify that if any, the interaction specifically due to gain-of-function of IP3R and not due to other off-target effects.

We rescued IP3R triple KO (TKO) HEK and HeLa cells with FLAG-tagged IP3R1 (blue Figure 5A), TKO HEK cells with IP3R2 (burgundy Figure 5B), and GFP-tagged IP3R3 (green Figure 5C) to look at their interaction with endogenous Bcl-xL by proximity ligation assay (PLA). The FLAG IP3R1 and GFP IP3R3 constructs and IP3R2 stable HEK cells were functionally validated by measuring the Cch-induced [Ca2+]c signal (Figures 5A–5C). Expression of a mitochondria tracer, mtBFP (for both IP3R1 and IP3R3) and staining with Mito Brilliant 646 (for IP3R2) helped quantify the PLA signal specifically in the mitochondria. The PLA signal was higher in R1/R3 transfected (blue) and IP3R2 stably rescued cells (burgundy) as opposed to TKO cells (black). Moreover, much of the PLA signal came from the mitochondrial region (cyan/orange, Figures 5A–5C), indicating mitochondria-specific interaction between Bcl-xL and IP3R. The table in Figure 5D depicts the descriptive analysis of total and mitochondria-specific PLA signal in the form of number of dots per 100 μm2 obtained between IP3R and Bcl-xL. The antibodies used with respective fixation strategies (4% PFA in the case of R1/R3 and 100% methanol for R2) against FLAG, IP3R2, GFP, and Bcl-xL were validated by IF (Figure S4).

PLA experiments were then performed in MEFs to investigate mitochondria-specific interaction of endogenous wild-type and stably expressed organelle-targeted Bcl-xL with endogenous IP3R2/IP3R3 using Bcl-xLKO MEFs and mutant Bcl-xLK87D deficient at IP3R binding as negative controls. Figures S3A–S3C show stronger mitochondria-specific PLA signal in cells expressing mitochondria-localized Bcl-xL compared to the rest of the conditions. Antibody incompatibility in the case of IP3R1 and Bcl-xL was the reason that the interaction between them could not be checked by PLA.

We also checked the interaction between Mito Bcl-xL and IP3R1 biochemically by crosslinking followed by immunoprecipitation in MEFs stably expressing Bcl-xL localized in mitochondria (Figure 5E). IP3R1 band was detected in the crosslinked IP sample when the IP was done using the Bcl-xL antibody. However, this could not be shown in reverse order using IP3R1 antibody for the pulldown due to limitations with the antibody and detection of low amounts of endogenous IP3R1. Limitations with the IP3R2 antibody also prevented the verification of interaction between Bcl-xL and IP3R2 using chemical crosslinking. In summary, these studies provided evidence for an inter-organellar interaction formed by IP3R and Bcl-xL localized in the ER membrane and OMM, respectively.

Because IP3Rs are required to maintain the contacts between ER and mitochondria,8 we also asked the question if Bcl-xL deletion and the ensuing loss of IP3R-Bcl-xL inter-organellar interaction alter the closeness between ER and OMM. We found that the OMM fraction closely associated with the ER membrane decreased in Bcl-xL KO MEFs in transmission electron microscopy images, where we measured the % of mitochondrial perimeter within 0–10 nm proximity of ER segments (Figure S3D). This indicates the relevance of the Bcl-xL-IP3R coupling in ERMC structure maintenance.

Possible interplay between mitochondrial apoptosis and local ERMC Ca2+ transfer

Bcl-xL-induced sensitization of the IP3R to IP3 through its C terminus was reported to be competitively inhibited by pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family members Bax or truncated Bid (tBid), which is primarily produced upon death receptor engagement.27 We tested if tBid can suppress the Bcl-xL-mediated enhancement of the IP3R-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer in Bax Bak double knockout (DKO) MEF cells, in which tBid does not induce apoptosis (Figure S4). Our hypothesis was that in the presence of tBid (a known BH3-binding interactor of Bcl2/Bcl-xL) Bcl-xL will be unavailable to bind IP3R and to exert its effect on ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer. Upon tBid overexpression, there were some hints of less efficient IP3-induced local Ca2+ transfer with longer coupling time and equal [Ca2+]m signal as control cells despite a higher [Ca2+]c signal (Figures S5A–S5D and 5G). However, the results did not show significant suppression of the [Ca2+]m signal, which might have been partly because of the very low transfection efficiency in DKO MEFs (Figure S5I). Thus, further studies will be needed to validate if ERMC Ca2+ transfer might be negatively affected by pro-apoptotic stimuli via limiting the ability of OMM Bcl-xL to bind to the IP3R.

DISCUSSION

Anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins have been historically implicated in cell survival, with ER and mitochondrial calcium being an important component of that process. Bcl-xL so far has been highlighted as a cis interactor and modulator of IP3R and VDAC individually. We found that Bcl-xL, localized in the OMM, also interacts with the IP3R, supports the ERMC, and promotes [Ca2+]c signal propagation from the IP3R to the mitochondria. This is due to an enhanced local Ca2+ transfer because neither ER Ca2+ release nor mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake is intrinsically stimulated. Thus, our work uncovers a new role for mitochondrial Bcl-xL in in-terorganellar contact site formation, which might have relevance in fundamental ERMC functions beyond local calcium signaling.

Upon stimulation of the cells with an IP3-linked agonist, we documented an enhanced [Ca2+]c increase in Bcl-xLKO cells. However, no significant difference between WT and Bcl-xLKO cells occurred when endogenous IP3 production was bypassed, and IP3 was directly presented by uncaging. We aimed to stimulate the IP3Rs by a saturating dose of IP3 because the inhibitory/stimulatory effects of Bcl-2/Bcl-xL on IP3R activity were confined to suboptimal stimulation, and maximal IP3R activation was not sensitive to Bcl-xL, consistent with previous studies.27,30,38 Still, it remains possible that opposing effects of Bcl-xL on ER Ca2+ store loading and on various binding sites of the IP3R Ca2+ channel14,27,28,39 balanced out each other to produce similar [Ca2+]c increases in WT and Bcl-xLKO cells, at least when maximal IP3 was directly presented. In any case, ER Ca2+ release seemed to be unaffected by the lack of Bcl-xL, enabling us to specifically examine the ER-mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer and the resulting mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake.

While the maximal IP3-induced ER Ca2+ release was unaffected in the Bcl-xLKO cells, the corresponding [Ca2+]m rise was decreased and was normalized upon acute rescue of Bcl-xL. Thus, mitochondria likely accumulated less effectively the Ca2+ presented by the IP3R in the absence of Bcl-xL. This could have happened because the mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake mechanism (VDACs and mtCU) or the driving force of Ca2+ uptake (mostly ΔѰm) was frail in the absence of Bcl-xL but our studies of the [Ca2+]m signal upon global Ca2+ increases during SOCE and mitochondrial Ca2+ clearance of directly presented Ca2+ showed unaffected or enhanced response when Bcl-xL was absent. Furthermore, the ΔѰm was also unattenuated in Bcl-xLKO. The most likely alternative explanation for the weakened IP3-linked [Ca2+]m signaling was that the local transfer of Ca2+ from IP3R to the mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake sites was ineffective in the absence of Bcl-xL. In summary, though Bcl-xL does not enhance the Ca2+ release by maximally activated IP3Rs and acts as a suppressor of mitochondrial uptake, it quickens and enhances the mitochondrial response to IP3R-mediated Ca2+ release, providing evidence for Bcl-xL’s role in local Ca2+ transfer at ERMC. It has been shown before that there is no sealed Ca2+ tunnel between ER and mitochondria40 but a physical interaction of IP3R with OMM proteins might create an effective and short path for Ca2+ transfer. Originally, IP3R1 was described to bind VDAC with the assistance of GRP759 but later several IP3R binding partners were proposed in the OMM, including TOM70,36 FUNDC1,35 Sigma 1 receptor,41 DJ1,42 transglutaminase type 2,43 IRE1a,37 and a-synuclein.44 However, OMM Bcl-xL has never been considered as a binding partner.

Previously, the effects of Bcl-xL on IP3R and ER Ca2+ were attributed to ER membrane-localized Bcl-xL, but Bcl-xL has preferential distribution in the OMM.25,45 Bcl-xL in the OMM was described to interact with VDAC to attenuate Ca2+ passage46 and staurosporine-induced apoptosis-related Ca2+ transfer to the mitochondria.29 Differently, Bcl-xL-VDAC interaction was also linked to effective mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer in response to agonist stimulation of intact MEFs and direct Ca2+ exposure of permeabilized MEFs.30 However, we did not see a reduced mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake in permeabilized Bcl-xLKO cells when exposed to either low or high doses of Ca2+. In the light of these results and considering that the IP3R-mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer takes place where the ER and mitochondria are tightly apposed, we reasoned that OMM Bcl-xL might engage the IP3Rs in intermembrane interaction (see speculative scheme in Figure 6). This idea gained support when we rescued Bcl-xLKO cells with cytosol-, ER membrane- and OMM-targeted Bcl-xL constructs, respectively, and found that the OMM-targeted Bcl-xL supported the IP3-induced [Ca2+]m signal most effectively. Notably, ER membrane-targeted Bcl-xL also had a positive outcome on the [Ca2+]m signal, which is likely a result of the fraction of Bcl-xL ending up in the mitochondria, as found in both immunocytochemistry and cell fractionation results (Figure S2C). Alternatively, it might also be because of an effect on ER Ca2+ transport or because ER Bcl-xL can bridge to an unidentified OMM protein. Further evidence for OMM Bcl-xL-IP3R interaction came from the co-immunoprecipitation studies and from the PLA studies where Bcl-xL displayed the most interaction with each IP3R isoform in the mitochondria. PLA with organelle-targeted Bcl-xL showed enhanced mitochondria-specific interaction between IP3R2 and mito Bcl-xL compared to ER Bcl-xL. Collectively, these findings provide considerable support for OMM Bcl-xL forming a trans interaction with the IP3R to promote ER-mitochondrial local Ca2+ transfer.

Based on the 3D structural models of IP3R that have been published,47,48 the sheer size of the tetrameric structure would limit the possible interaction sites of Bcl-xL bound to either the OMM or ER membrane. The last helix of the C-terminal domain (C4) has been proposed to bind Bcl-xL in a similar manner as the BH3 helix of pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins.28 Considering the structural constraints, this interaction seems much more feasible in a trans configuration (Figure S6A), though from the extant models that resolve this helix, it is not clear that the putatively important residues are, in fact, accessible. Using Alphafold2 multimer,49 we found a possible interaction between the final, C-terminal flexible domain and the BH3-binding pocket of Bcl-xL (Figure S6B).50 A possible arrangement for the protein-protein interactions is that the Bcl-xL molecule that has a trans interaction with the IP3R also has a cis interaction with VDAC, creating a ternary complex that brings the Ca2+ source and Ca2+ acceptor of the OMM into close connection with each other (see speculative scheme in Figure 6).

Based on our electron micrographs showing decreased presence of tight ERMC upon Bcl-xL deletion, the Bcl-xL-dependent mechanism might contribute to tethering OMM to the ER. Trans interactions of Bcl-xL at ERMC might support a range of functions that depend on close associations of the ER and OMM. Our findings highlight in this regard the potential relevance of OMM Bcl-xL. One can speculate if ER Bcl-xL also reaches out to OMM partners. Notably, for Bcl-xL to have an inhibitory effect on apoptosis, ER targeting failed, and mitochondrial localization of Bcl-xL was required.31 This outcome is not surprising because apoptotic OMM permeabilization mediated, for example, by Bak and Bax is unlikely to be confined to the ERMC regions of the OMM only. However, more investigation is needed to discern the connection, or the lack of it, between Bcl-xL’s role in ER-mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer and apoptosis inhibition. With the ERMCs being implicated in multifarious cancers and a history of Bcl2 family proteins explored in cancer therapeutics, it would be interesting to further study the implications of the effect of Bcl-xL in ER-mitochondria local Ca2+ transfer in the context of apoptosis and cancer progression as well as therapeutics.

Limitations of the study

Low transfection efficiency of the MEF models used posed a limitation in evaluating the effect of Bcl-xL in acute rescue conditions, which was dealt with by stably rescuing MEFs expressing organelle-targeted Bcl-xL. However, it is of note that the passages for these stably rescued cells are different from those of the WT and Bcl-xLKO MEFs. Also, low transfection efficiency of Bax-Bak DKO MEFs posed a limitation to compare competitive binding between Bcl-xL and tBid with IP3R. Lack of IP3R isoform-specific dependence information in crosslinking studies limits insights into interaction between IP3R and Bcl-xL, although we tried to incorporate all three IP3R isoforms in combined PLA studies to compensate for this. The modeling studies were done only using IP3R1 and can be potentially strengthened with inclusion of other isoforms. The modeling prediction was not tested experimentally e.g., by using truncated or mutant IP3R1, which can be an interesting future extension of the study.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Cells

Wild-type (WT) and IP3R-deficient (TKO) HeLa cells were provided by Katsuhiko Mikoshiba (RIKEN Brain Science Institute, Saitama, Japan). Wild-type (WT) and IP3R-deficient (TKO) HEK cells were procured from David Yule.51 Wild-type (WT), Bcl-xL-deficient (Bcl-xLKO), cytoplasm, mitochondria and ER targeted Bcl-xL expressing stable mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) were obtained from Chi Li and generated as previously described.31 Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), with 4.5g/L glucose (Gibco, Cat# 11965118) supplemented with 10% FBS, 2mM glutamine (Invitrogen), 100 units/ml penicillin/streptomycin (Lonza™, Biowhittaker™) in 5% CO2 and 95% air at 37°C. Cell line authentication was not performed. All cells were periodically checked for Mycoplasma contamination and they tested negative.

METHOD DETAILS

Transfection and transduction

Cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher) with 1-2mg total DNA according to the manufacturer’s protocol. WT, Bcl-xLKO MEFs were transfected with muscarinic receptor and matrix targeted RCaMP for simultaneous cytoplasmic and mitochondrial Ca2+ measurements. Bcl-xLKO MEFs were co-transfected with full-length Bcl-xL and RCaMP for acute rescue experiments. TKO HEK and TKO HeLa cells were transfected with IP3R1/3 constructs for proximity ligation assay. WT, Bcl-xLKO and organelle targeted Bcl-xL expressing stable MEFs were seeded 24 h prior to infection with matrix targeted RCaMP adenovirus at 50, 200 and 75 pfu respectively followed by imaging post 24-48hrs.

Fractionation of mef cells

Cells grown overconfluent in T150 flasks (~7 × 107 cells) were harvested using trypsin and washed with Na-Hepes-EGTA (120 mM NaCl, 20mM Na-Hepes, 5mM KCl, 1mM KH2PO4, 100μM EGTA/Tris pH7.4). All further steps were carried out at 4°C. The cells were subjected to hyposmosis for 10 min (20% ICM: 120mM KCl, 1mM KH2PO4, 10mM NaCl, 20mM Tris-Hepes and 1μg/mL each of pepstatin, leupeptin and antipain, pH7.4; added to 80% distilled water) supplemented with 5mM MgCl2 and 200μM EGTA. Cells were then homogenized on ice using IsoBiotec cell cracker (~11 passes). To restore osmolarity, 3x volume of ICM supplemented with 125mM sucrose, 1μg/mL protease inhibitors and 200μM EGTA pH7.4 were added. To eliminate unbroken cell and nuclei, the cell homogenate was centrifuged at 900g for 10 min. The supernatant was further spun at 2,500g for 10 min for a crude fraction of mitochondria in pellet (M1) followed by centrifugation at 10,000g for 15 min for remaining mitochondrial fraction in pellet (M2), resuspended in 200μL ICM supplemented with 20μM EGTA and protease inhibitors. The supernatant was further centrifuged at 40,000g for 45 min to obtain the ER pellet.

Western blot

Reagents for SDS-PAGE were procured from Invitrogen and Bio-Rad. Followed by harvesting, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and solubilized in 1X RIPA lysis buffer (Sigma Aldrich cat# R0278) supplemented with protease inhibitors cocktail (Roche). Lysates were incubated on ice for 20 min followed by centrifugation at 13000xg for 15 min at 4°C. Protein concentrations were estimated with a DC protein assay kit (Bio-Rad). Proteins in cell lysates were resolved on 4–12% SDS-PAGE gels (Invitrogen) and then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (0.45mm, BioRad). Intercept blocking buffer (LICORbio™) was used as blocking reagent. Rabbit monoclonal antibodies against Bcl-xL (1:1000 Cell Signaling #2764), rabbit polyclonal antibodies against IP3R1 (1:5000 custom made, Pocono Rabbit Farm and Laboratories), calnexin (1:1000 Enzo #ADI-SPA-865) and TOM20 (1:1000 Proteintech #11802-1-AP), mouse monoclonal antibodies against cytochrome c (1:1000 BD Biosciences #556433), BiP/GRP78 (1:1000 BD Biosciences #610979), Hsp70 (1:1000 Thermofisher #MA3-006) and GAPDH (1:1000 Proteintech #60004-1) were used as primary antibodies. IP3R1 was detected by chemiluminescence using an HRP substrate for CCD imaging (Azure Biosystems). Rabbit HRP-linked secondary antibody (Cell Signaling #7074) was used in the case of IP3R1. For others, corresponding mouse and rabbit LICOR secondary antibodies were used (LICORbio™).

Cross-linking and co-immunoprecipitation

To determine protein interactions, chemical crosslinking followed by co-immunoprecipitation and western blot were performed. Co-immunoprecipation was performed using Pierce™ Classic Magnetic IP/Co-IP kit (Thermofisher #88804). Cell lysates were prepared using the IP lysis buffer provided along with the kit. Chemical crosslinking was induced by treating cell lysates with 200mM bismaleimidohexane (BMH, Thermofisher #22330) for 30 min at room temperature under continuous agitation. The reaction was quenched by treatment with 50mM DTT for 15 min. Crosslinked and control lysates were then incubated with Bcl-xL antibody (1:200) overnight at 4°C under constant agitation. Magnetic beads were added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature under continuous agitation followed by elution according to manufacturer’s protocol (Thermofisher #88804). Elute and corresponding inputs from lysates were subjected to western blot.

Intracellular Ca2+ measurements

MEFs, HEK and HeLa cells were plated on Poly-D-Lysine coated round (25mm) coverslips which were subjected to loading with 2mM Fura-2-AM (Invitrogen) in a serum-free extracellular medium (ECM) comprising of 121 mM NaCl, 5mM NaHCO3, 10mM Na-HEPES, 4.7 mM KCl, 1.2 mM KH2PO4, 1.2mM MgSO4, 2mM CaCl2, and 10mM glucose, pH 7.4, containing 2% BSA (Roche) along with 0.003% Pluronic F-127 and 100 mM sulfinpyrazone for 12–15 min at 37°C to record changes in [Ca2+]c. Following dye loading, cells were washed with fresh ECM with 0.25% BSA and transferred to thermostatic stage (37°C) of the microscope and imaging was continued in 0.25% BSA devoid of Ca2+. For intracellular Ca2+ imaging the EMCCD camera (Hamamatsu) was fixed to an Olympus IX81 microscope with Lambda DG4 light source controlled by Metamorph (Molecular Devices). The setup used the UV optimized Olympus UAPO/340 x40/1.35 N.A. oil immersion objective. To simultaneously measure [Ca2+]c and [Ca2+]m Fura-2 fluorescence was recorded at 340 and 380 nm, mitochondrial matrix-targeted RCaMP at 577/20 nm excitations, using dual-band dichroic and emission filters (Chroma, set 73100/59022 respectively). Images were acquired ranging from every 0.1–2 s. Fura-2 was calibrated in vitro by adding 1 mM CaCl2 followed by 10 mM EGTA/Tris, pH 8.5. [Ca2+]c was estimated using the following equation, [Ca2+] = Kd * (Sf2/Sb2) * (R-Rmin)/(Rmax-R), where Kd is the Ca2+ dissociation constant (0.224 mM), R is the ratio of fluorescence intensities at 340/380 nm excitation, Rmin and Rmax are the fluorescence ratios in Ca2+ free and Ca2+ saturated conditions, respectively.

Photorelease of caged IP3

mtRCaMP infected cells were incubated with membrane permeant IP3 (10μM, D-2,3-O-Isopropylidene-6-O-(2-Nitro-4,5-Dimethoxy)benzyl-myo-Inositol 1,4,5 Trisphosphate-Hexakis(Propionoxymethyl) ester, SiChem GmbH #1009832-82-9) for 30 min at 37°C in ECM supplemented with 0.003% Pluronic F-127 and 100mM sulfinpyrazone and 5μM Cal520 (AAT Bioquest #21130) to measure [Ca2+]c. Cells were then washed with fresh ECM containing 0.25%BSA and transferred to thermostatic stage (37°C) of the microscope where imaging was executed in 0.25% BSA devoid of Ca2+. After recording a baseline for 20 s, photo release of caged IP3 was performed with a 365nm UV laser for a pulse of 250–350 msecs. Cal520 and mtRCaMP fluorescence signals were recorded using 480 nm and 577 nm excitations respectively and depicted as F/F0. The background signal recorded in cell free regions was employed to correct the fluorescence intensities.

Fluorometric Ca2+ measurements

[Ca2+]c and Ѱm was measured using a fluorometric assay previously described.52,53 Saponin permeabilized MEFs (2*10 ^6) were resuspended in 1.5 mL of intracellular medium (ICM) containing 120 mM KCl, 10 mM NaCl, 1 mM KH2PO4, 20 mM Tris-HEPES at pH 7.2, augmented with protease inhibitors (leupeptin, antipain, pepstatin at 1μg/mL each), 2mM Mg-ATP, 2μM thapsigargin and maintained in a thermostatic cuvette with continuous stirring at 35°C. Assays were conducted in presence of 20 μM CGP-37157 and 1mM succinate using a multiwavelength-excitation dual-wavelength-emission fluorimeter (DeltaRAM, PTI). The extramitochondrial Ca2+ concentration ([Ca2+]c) was assessed using the ratiometric Ca2+ probe Fura-FF (1 mM, AAT Bioquest). Ѱm was measured with 1.5 mM TMRM (Invitrogen). Fura and TMRM fluorescence were captured simultaneously using 340–380 nm excitation and 500 nm emission, 545 nm excitation and 580 nm emission, respectively. Complete depolarization (maximum de-quench of TMRM fluorescence) happened on application of protonophore FCCP (5 mM). Calibration of the Fura signal was done at the end of each measurement, adding 1 mM CaCl2, followed by 10 mM EGTA/Tris, pH 8.5.

Proximity ligation assay

Duolink PLA technology incorporated Duolink In Situ Red/Green Starter Kit Mouse/Rabbit (MilliporeSigma, cat# DUO92101, DUO92014) was employed to study the interaction of IP3R and Bcl-xL. TKO HEK and TKO HeLa cells, rescued with IP3R1FLAG and IP3R3GFP were seeded on 10mm coverslips. 24–48 h post transfection, cells were fixed in prewarmed 4% PFA for 20 min at RT and washed with PBS (X 3 times). Triton X-100 (0.1%) was then used for 1 min to permeabilize cells, subsequently washed with PBS and incubated with AF488-Phalloidin (in case of IP3R1, diluted in 1% FBS/PBS) for 1 h in dark. HEK cells stably rescued with IP3R2, MEFs expressing wilt-type Bcl-xL and stably expressing organelle specific Bcl-xL along with Bcl-xLKO cells, Bcl-xLKO cells acutely expressing mutant BclxLK87D were stained with MitoBrilliant 646 (Tocris, cat#7700) for 30 min, IP3R2 stable and TKO HEK cells were treated with Alexa Fluor 555 conjugated Wheat Germ Agglutinin (WGA) (Invitrogen, cat# W32464), MEFs with Alexa 488 conjugated WGA (Invitrogen, cat# W11261) for 10mins followed by fixation with chilled 100% methanol for 10 min at −20°C. Cells were then washed with PBS and incubated with Duolink blocking buffer for 1 h followed by incubation with primary antibodies (diluted in Duolink antibody diluent) for 2 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies (1:250) used were anti-FLAG (Sigma Cat# F1804), anti-GFP (Invitrogen #A11120), anti-IP3R2 (1:50, Santa Cruz #398434), anti-IP3R3 (1:50, BD Biosciences #610312) and Bcl-xL (Cell Signaling #2764). Cells were then washed with 5% BSA (15 min X 3) followed by consequent incubation with Duolink secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37°C. After that cells were washed with Duolink wash buffer A (5 min X 3) and incubated with Duolink ligase for 30 min at 37°C. Thereafter cells were washed with Duolink wash buffer A (2 min X 3) and incubated with Duolink polymerase and oligonucleotides for 90 min at 37°C. Finally, cells were washed with Duolink wash buffer B (10 min X 3) and mounted on glass slides using ProLong Glass antifade mountant (Invitrogen, cat# P36980). Images were captured within 24 h.

Immunocytochemistry

Primary Antibodies used in PLA studies were validated by immunocytochemistry. HEK and MEF cells on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton or fixed with 100% chilled methanol. Samples were blocked with 10% FBS for 60 min at room temperature, then incubated with mouse monoclonal primary antibodies against FLAG, IP3R2, IP3R3, GFP and rabbit polyclonal anti-Bcl-xL antibody in a wet chamber for 90 min followed by incubation for 60 min with Alexa Fluor 488, AlexaFuorPlus405 and Alexa Fluor 647 anti-mouse and Alexa Fluor 568 anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (Thermofisher).

Alpha fold prediction

AlphaFold2-Multimer predictions of the interaction of the C-terminal tail of IP3R1 and Bcl-xL were performed using the Cosmic2 platform.54 Mouse IP3R1 (Uniprot P11881; 2717-end) and Bcl-xL without its C-terminal membrane binding domain (Q64373; 1–186) were co-folded. 5 predictions were generated from each of 5 separate models and the overall best ranked prediction is shown in Figure S6A.
