Successful repair of a penetrating right atrial gunshot injury via right posterolateral thoracotomy in a resource-limited hospital: a case report
Authors: Issam Alkhayer, Mohammad Alaa Aldakak, Youssef Abbas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Penetrating cardiac injuries are uncommon but frequently fatal, particularly after gunshot wounds. Diagnosis may be challenging when pleural bleeding predominates, and pericardial fluid is minimal because blood may decompress through a pericardial defect into the thoracic cavity.Case presentation:A 25-year-old man sustained a penetrating chest gunshot wound during wartime and presented to a resource-limited hospital in hemorrhagic shock with a massive right hemothorax. Right tube thoracostomy immediately drained approximately 900 mL of blood, but he remained hemodynamically unstable. Computed tomography demonstrated a large retained right hemothorax with only a small pericardial effusion, raising suspicion of cardiac injury with decompression into the pleural space. Emergency right posterolateral thoracotomy was selected because the injury pattern was predominantly right-sided and sternotomy equipment was unavailable. Exploration revealed a 3-cm laceration of the anterior right atrial wall near the base of the right atrial appendage, together with injuries to the right middle and lower lobes. Atrial bleeding was controlled with digital pressure and repaired using a running 4-0 polypropylene suture. The pulmonary injuries were sutured, the pericardial space was evacuated and left open for pleural drainage, and a chest tube was placed. Recovery was uncomplicated, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 6.Discussion:Minimal pericardial effusion does not exclude major cardiac injury when a pericardial breach permits decompression into the hemithorax. Persistent shock and a compatible wound trajectory should prompt urgent operative exploration.Conclusion:Right posterolateral thoracotomy may provide effective exposure for definitive right atrial repair when sternotomy is not feasible.

Introduction

Penetrating cardiac injury is frequently lethal, with a reported incidence of 0.16% of admissions to trauma centers and survival rates ranging from 3% to 84%[1]. In a retrospective review of 302 patients with penetrating intrapericardial cardiac wounds requiring emergency thoracotomy, overall survival was 41%, with lower survival after gunshot wounds compared with stab wounds (23% vs 58%)[2]. Mortality varies by mechanism and anatomic involvement; gunshot wounds are generally more fatal, and the right ventricle is reported as the most frequently injured chamber in penetrating trauma[1,2]. Prognosis is influenced by admission physiology, the presence of tamponade, the number of chambers involved, and whether thoracotomy is performed in the emergency department or the operating room[2]. Clinically, cardiac trauma may present as hemorrhagic shock, pericardial tamponade, or cardiac failure[3]. Injuries within the “cardiac box” should heighten suspicion for penetrating cardiac trauma; however, wounds outside this region, particularly gunshot injuries, still warrant vigilance[3]. Initial evaluation relies on physical examination and evidence of chest wall injury; hypotension, jugular venous distension, and muffled heart sounds may suggest tamponade and possible cardiac injury[1]. Bedside ultrasonography is commonly used to assess for pericardial fluid in trauma, yet sonographic assessment may be limited by concurrent hemothorax, and pericardial blood can decompress into the pleural cavity through a pericardial defect, reducing the apparent pericardial fluid burden despite clinically significant cardiac injury[3,4]. In hemodynamically stable patients with persistent diagnostic uncertainty, options include a pericardial window and/or computed tomography (CT) to assess projectile trajectory, and multidetector CT may demonstrate indirect signs such as pneumopericardium or hemopericardium, prompting further evaluation[1,3]. Management is guided primarily by hemodynamic status, ranging from subxiphoid pericardial window to urgent operative exploration or emergency department thoracotomy, with the surgical approach tailored to injury location and available resources[3,5,6]. Here, we report the successful management of a penetrating gunshot cardiac injury in a wartime, resource-limited hospital. The patient presented with hemorrhagic shock, a massive right hemothorax, and only a small pericardial effusion. Emergency right posterolateral thoracotomy revealed a right atrial laceration, which was successfully repaired with definitive surgical hemostasis, followed by an uneventful postoperative recovery. The main educational value of this case lies in recognizing the diagnostic pitfall of minimal pericardial effusion despite major cardiac injury and in demonstrating a resource-adapted operative approach when standard sternotomy equipment is unavailable. This case has been reported in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guidelines[7].

Case presentation

A 25-year-old man presented to the emergency department of a wartime, resource-limited hospital after sustaining a penetrating gunshot wound to the chest. The entry wound was located to the right of the manubrium, and the exit wound was at the level of the seventh intercostal space along the right scapular line. He arrived approximately 30 minutes after the injury. On initial assessment, he was conscious but in hemorrhagic shock, with a heart rate of approximately 110 beats per minute, a blood pressure of approximately 90/60 mmHg, and a respiratory rate of approximately 22 breaths per minute. Physical examination and an initial supine chest radiograph demonstrated a right-sided pneumothorax with pleural fluid, raising suspicion of a traumatic hemopneumothorax (Fig. 1). Immediate resuscitation was initiated with two bags of isotonic crystalloid and one unit of packed red blood cells. An emergency right-sided tube thoracostomy yielded approximately 900 mL of blood immediately, followed by continued bloody drainage; the exact additional volume was not documented. Despite pleural drainage and ongoing resuscitation, the patient remained hemodynamically unstable. An urgent chest CT scan subsequently demonstrated a large retained, clotted right hemothorax occupying more than one-third of the right hemithorax, together with a small pericardial effusion (Fig. 2). In the context of the projectile trajectory and persistent shock, these findings raised suspicion of a cardiac injury with decompression of pericardial blood into the right pleural cavity; however, the pericardial breach itself was confirmed intraoperatively rather than directly demonstrated on CT. Because hemodynamic stability could not be restored, the patient was transferred urgently to the operating room approximately 30 minutes after completion of the CT assessment. A right posterolateral thoracotomy was selected to provide wide exposure of the right pleural cavity, the associated lung injury, pericardium, and right-sided cardiac structures. Median sternotomy was not feasible because essential sternotomy equipment was unavailable in this austere setting.

Upon entering the right hemithorax, more than 1500 mL of retained blood and clots were evacuated. Exploration revealed a 3-cm tangential laceration of the anterior wall of the right atrium extending along the base of the right atrial appendage. The injury was anatomically distant from the tricuspid valve and did not represent a through-and-through transcardiac wound. Hemorrhage was initially controlled using direct digital pressure, followed by definitive closure with a continuous 4-0 polypropylene suture (Fig. 3). The projectile tract had also caused a lung laceration involving the right middle lobe and the superior segment of the right lower lobe (S6). The injury measured approximately 4 cm in length and extended to a depth of approximately 3 cm, with moderate active bleeding. It was repaired using continuous 2-0 polyglactin sutures, and satisfactory hemostasis was achieved.

The pre-existing pericardial defect was slightly enlarged, and the pericardial space was completely evacuated. The pericardium was left open to maintain free drainage into the right pleural cavity; therefore, a separate pericardial drain was not placed. A pleural chest tube was inserted prior to closure of the thoracotomy.

The operation lasted approximately 2 hours. The documented hemorrhagic burden exceeded 3 L, including approximately 900 mL drained immediately through a tube thoracostomy, more than 1500 mL of retained blood and clots evacuated from the right hemithorax, and an estimated intraoperative blood loss of approximately 700 mL. The patient received approximately four units of packed red blood cells and six units of plasma during resuscitation and surgery.

Postoperatively, the patient was monitored in the intensive care unit for 1 day. His recovery was uncomplicated, without recurrent bleeding or clinical evidence of cardiac tamponade. Cardiac examination and heart sounds remained normal. Postoperative echocardiography was not performed because echocardiographic equipment and appropriately trained personnel were unavailable. A chest radiograph obtained during the postoperative hospital stay showed satisfactory findings, although the exact postoperative day on which it was performed was not documented. The chest tube was removed on postoperative day 4, and the patient was discharged in good general condition on postoperative day 6. No post-discharge follow-up data were available.

Discussion

Clinical presentation and prognostic considerations

Penetrating cardiac injury is associated with high mortality, partly because many patients die before reaching definitive care. Outcomes vary according to the mechanism of injury, anatomic involvement, physiological condition at presentation, and timing of definitive operative control, with gunshot wounds generally carrying a worse prognosis than stab wounds[1,4]. Large-series data demonstrated substantially higher survival when thoracotomy was performed in the operating room rather than in the emergency department (74% vs 8%)[2]. Outcomes following emergency-department thoracotomy also improved stepwise with better physiological status at presentation, underscoring the prognostic importance of admission physiology[2].

Clinical presentation may range from hemorrhagic shock and massive hemothorax to cardiac tamponade, while classic findings such as Beck’s triad are frequently absent[1,3]. Although tamponade has been associated with improved survival in some patients, this potential protective effect appears to be more relevant to stab wounds and less pronounced in gunshot injuries[2]. In our patient, the dominant presentation was hemorrhagic shock with a massive right hemothorax rather than classic tamponade physiology. He arrived with signs of life but remained hemodynamically unstable despite initial resuscitation and tube thoracostomy, supporting rapid progression to definitive operative hemorrhage control[2,3,5,6].

Diagnostic approach and comparison with our findings

Bedside ultrasonography is commonly used to assess for pericardial fluid in penetrating thoracic trauma; however, its diagnostic yield may be reduced in the presence of a concurrent hemothorax[3]. Furthermore, blood from a cardiac injury may decompress through a pericardial defect into the pleural cavity rather than accumulating under pressure within the pericardial space. Consequently, only a small amount of pericardial fluid may be visible despite a clinically significant cardiac wound[3,4].

In selected patients, multidetector CT can support decision-making by clarifying the projectile trajectory, identifying associated thoracic injuries, and demonstrating indirect signs of cardiac injury, such as hemopericardium or pneumopericardium[1]. In our patient, CT demonstrated a massive retained right hemothorax with only a small pericardial effusion. This imaging pattern, together with the projectile trajectory and persistent hemodynamic instability, raised suspicion of decompression of pericardial blood into the right pleural cavity. The presence of a pericardial breach, however, was confirmed intraoperatively rather than being directly demonstrated on CT.

Management and operative strategy

Current management frameworks emphasize that treatment should be guided primarily by hemodynamic status, with urgent operative exploration indicated when instability persists despite resuscitation and pleural drainage[3,5,6]. Injury complexity also affects outcome, with multiple intrapericardial injuries carrying a poorer prognosis than isolated single-chamber injuries[2]. Initial control of bleeding from right atrial injuries may be achieved with direct digital pressure or tangential clamping, followed by definitive polypropylene suture repair, with pledgets or layered closure used when tissue quality requires additional support[4,8–10].

Operative exposure should be selected according to the suspected injury location, associated thoracic injuries, physiological urgency, and available resources. Median sternotomy is commonly used for central cardiac injuries, whereas thoracotomy may be more appropriate when pleural hemorrhage and associated pulmonary injury are predominantly unilateral[5]. Although right anterolateral thoracotomy is frequently used for rapid emergency or resuscitative access, a right posterolateral thoracotomy was selected in this patient, because the operation was performed in the operating room and the projectile trajectory, retained hemothorax, lung injury, and suspected cardiac injury were all predominantly right-sided. This approach provided wide exposure for evacuation of blood and clots, repair of the associated lung laceration, assessment and evacuation of the pericardial space, and direct access to the right atrium. Median sternotomy was not feasible because the required sternotomy equipment was unavailable in this limited setting.

Our operative management followed established principles of rapid hemorrhage control and definitive cardiac repair. The right atrial bleeding was initially controlled with direct digital pressure, followed by closure with a running polypropylene suture. The associated pulmonary laceration was repaired, and adequate hemostasis was achieved. The pre-existing pericardial defect was slightly enlarged, and the pericardial space was completely evacuated. The pericardium was left open to permit free drainage into the right pleural cavity; therefore, a separate pericardial drain was not placed. This case supports the feasibility of a right posterolateral thoracotomy for definitive right atrial repair when the injury pattern is predominantly unilateral and logistical constraints preclude standard sternotomy[4–6].

Postoperative considerations and limitations

Survivors of penetrating cardiac trauma may develop delayed structural complications, including pericardial effusion, valvular injury, intracardiac shunting, and ventricular dysfunction. Postoperative echocardiographic surveillance is therefore advisable when feasible[3,11]. In our patient, the postoperative course was uncomplicated, with no recurrent bleeding or clinical evidence of cardiac tamponade. Cardiac examination and heart sounds remained normal, and a postoperative chest radiograph obtained during hospitalization showed satisfactory findings. The chest tube was removed on postoperative day 4, and the patient was discharged in good general condition on postoperative day 6.

Postoperative echocardiography was not performed because echocardiographic equipment and appropriately trained personnel were unavailable at the treating hospital. Furthermore, no post-discharge follow-up data could be retrieved. These limitations prevented objective evaluation of ventricular function and the exclusion of delayed structural cardiac sequelae. Nevertheless, the patient’s stable clinical condition at discharge supports a favorable early postoperative outcome following definitive surgical repair.

Conclusion

Penetrating cardiac gunshot injuries may present predominantly as exsanguinating hemothorax rather than classic cardiac tamponade, particularly when a pericardial defect permits decompression of blood into the pleural cavity, resulting in minimal pericardial effusion despite a clinically significant cardiac wound. Recognition of this diagnostic pitfall is essential, and management should remain physiology-driven, with prompt progression to definitive operative hemorrhage control when hemodynamic instability persists. In austere settings where standard sternotomy equipment is unavailable, right posterolateral thoracotomy may provide adequate exposure for definitive repair of a right atrial injury when the traumatic findings and associated injuries are predominantly right-sided.
