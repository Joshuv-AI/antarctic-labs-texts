IVUS-Guided Orbital Atherectomy and Intravascular Lithotripsy for Severely Calcified Coronary Lesions
Authors: Saroj Kumar Sahoo, Rajesh Vijayvergiya, Ramachandra Barik, Vikas Kadiyala, Anindya Banerjee, Debasis Panda, Prabhat Kumar Singh, Md Rubel Hossain, Melvin Baby, Mohan Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeverely calcified coronary lesions complicate percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) by impairing device delivery and stent expansion, often necessitating advanced plaque-modification strategies.Case SeriesThree patients with calcified left anterior descending artery disease underwent intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)-guided PCI with orbital atherectomy (OA), intravascular lithotripsy (IVL), and balloon dilation. In patient 1, a balloon-uncrossable lesion was treated with OA, followed by IVL when balloon dog-boning suggested residual deep calcium. In patient 2, OA-related dissection and slow flow were managed with provisional stenting, followed by IVL for persistent proximal calcium-related resistance. In patient 3, Ellis type III coronary perforation during postatherectomy balloon dilation was sealed with a covered stent, followed by IVL for proximal lesion preparation. Angiographic and IVUS assessments showed stent expansion and apposition in patients, with no adverse events during the 5-month follow-up.DiscussionThese cases illustrate the feasibility of an IVUS-guided escalation strategy for resistant calcified coronary lesions.Take-Home MessageThis case series suggests that a stepwise, IVUS-guided multimodality strategy may facilitate successful outcomes and aid optimal stent deployment in complex calcified PCI.Key words: balloon uncrossable lesion, coronary calcification, coronary perforation, intravascular lithotripsy, intravascular ultrasound, orbital atherectomy, percutaneous coronary intervention

IVUS-Guided Multimodality Calcium Modification Using Orbital Atherectomy and Intravascular Lithotripsy in Severely Calcified Coronary Lesions

A stepwise strategy combining orbital atherectomy for superficial calcium modification and intravascular lithotripsy for deep calcium fracture facilitated optimal lesion preparation, stent expansion, and apposition in 3 patients with complex calcified LAD disease. IVUS guidance enabled procedural decision-making, escalation of therapy in cases of persistent resistance, and management of complications, resulting in favorable mid-term outcomes with no adverse events during 5-month follow-up. IVUS = intravascular ultrasound; LAD = left anterior descending artery; MLA = minimal lumen area; IVL = intravascular lithotripsy; PCI = percutaneous coronary intervention; MSA = minimal stent area.

Coronary artery calcification remains a major determinant of procedural complexity during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Severe calcification impairs device delivery, limits stent expansion, and increases the risks of restenosis and stent thrombosis, often necessitating dedicated plaque-modification strategies before stent implantation. Contemporary consensus documents emphasize the importance of intravascular imaging for accurate characterization of calcium morphology and selection of appropriate calcium-modification devices.1, 2, 3

Take-Home Messages

•In severely calcified coronary lesions, IVUS-guided escalation from orbital atherectomy to intravascular lithotripsy may facilitate lesion preparation when significant calcium-related resistance persists after atherectomy, as indicated by persistent balloon dog-boning, inadequate balloon expansion, calcium-related stent underexpansion, or IVUS-confirmed residual circumferential calcium.

In severely calcified coronary lesions, IVUS-guided escalation from orbital atherectomy to intravascular lithotripsy may facilitate lesion preparation when significant calcium-related resistance persists after atherectomy, as indicated by persistent balloon dog-boning, inadequate balloon expansion, calcium-related stent underexpansion, or IVUS-confirmed residual circumferential calcium.

•Intravascular imaging can guide selection, sequencing, and optimization of calcium-modification strategies by identifying residual calcium burden and treatment effect; preprocedural IVUS assessment for morphological features associated with perforation risk, including thin-walled contralateral plaque in heavily calcified segments, may inform balloon-sizing strategy.

Intravascular imaging can guide selection, sequencing, and optimization of calcium-modification strategies by identifying residual calcium burden and treatment effect; preprocedural IVUS assessment for morphological features associated with perforation risk, including thin-walled contralateral plaque in heavily calcified segments, may inform balloon-sizing strategy.

•Major procedural complications, including coronary perforation, require prompt bailout management with immediate covered stent availability; after successful stabilization, completion of lesion preparation and definitive stenting may remain feasible in selected, hemodynamically stable patients.

Orbital atherectomy (OA) and intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) modify calcified plaque through complementary mechanisms. OA preferentially modifies superficial and mid-wall calcium while facilitating device delivery, whereas IVL fractures deeper calcium deposits and improves vessel compliance. Consequently, combined use of these technologies has emerged as a potential strategy for lesions in which a single calcium-modification modality may be insufficient.4,5

Although increasing reports have described combined atherectomy and lithotripsy, practical guidance regarding intraprocedural escalation between calcium-modification modalities remains limited. Contemporary imaging-guided frameworks provide recommendations for device selection according to lesion morphology, but evidence defining when operators should transition from atherectomy to lithotripsy is sparse.1, 2, 3

In this case series, we present 3 patients with severely calcified left anterior descending artery (LAD) disease managed using an intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)-guided escalation strategy. Rather than proposing a novel treatment algorithm, this series illustrates how specific procedural findings—including balloon uncrossability, persistent balloon dog-boning after atherectomy, residual calcium-related resistance on IVUS assessment, and management of major procedural complications—can inform selection and sequencing of complementary calcium-modification technologies.

Case Presentations

Patient 1

A 62-year-old man presented with exertional angina (NYHA functional class II) and inducible ischemia on treadmill testing. He had no history of diabetes or hypertension. Electrocardiography was unremarkable; transthoracic echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular systolic function (ejection fraction 60%) without regional wall motion abnormalities.

Coronary angiography revealed a severely calcified lesion in the LAD (Figure 1A, Video 1). The lesion was uncrossable with a 1.5-mm balloon, and the IVUS catheter could not pass the stenosis. Proximal IVUS revealed extensive calcification (Figure 1B). Cross-sectional IVUS demonstrated concentric calcium with a minimal lumen area (MLA) of 1.7 mm2 and an IVUS calcium score of 2, consistent with severe calcific disease (Figures 1C and 1D).

Baseline Assessment of the LAD Lesion in Patient 1

(A) Coronary angiography demonstrating severe calcification in the LAD. (B) IVUS longitudinal view showing extensive calcification. (C and D) Cross-sectional IVUS images demonstrating concentric calcium with an MLA of 1.7 mm2. IVUS = intravascular ultrasound; LAD = left anterior descending artery; MLA = minimal lumen area.

Given the balloon-uncrossable morphology and severe concentric calcification, OA was selected as the initial strategy using a 1.25-mm crown at 80,000 and 120,000 rpm (Figure 2A). Repeat IVUS showed improved lumen geometry but persistent circumferential calcium (Video 2). Subsequent dilation with a 3.5-mm noncompliant (NC) balloon resulted in persistent dog-boning, suggesting inadequate modification of deeper calcium layers. Accordingly, adjunctive IVL was performed using a 3.5 × 12-mm balloon delivering 80 pulses (Figures 2B and 2C).

Lesion Preparation in Patient 1

(A) Orbital atherectomy performed using a 1.25-mm crown. (B and C) Intravascular lithotripsy with a 3.5 × 12-mm balloon following persistent balloon dog-boning after atherectomy. (D) Angiographic appearance after calcium modification. (E and F) IVUS demonstrating calcium fracture following IVL. IVL = intravascular lithotripsy; IVUS = intravascular ultrasound.

Angiography and repeat IVUS demonstrated increased lumen area, calcium fracture, and improved vessel compliance (Figures 2D to 2F, Videos 3 and 4). A 3.5 × 44-mm drug-eluting stent (DES) was implanted in the LAD. Sequential postdilation used 3.5-, 4.0-, and 4.5-mm NC balloons from distal to proximal. Final angiography showed TIMI III flow (Figure 3A, Video 5). Final IVUS confirmed satisfactory stent expansion and apposition, with minimal stent areas (MSAs) of 8.1 mm2, 9.0 mm2, and 11.3 mm2 in the distal, mid, and proximal segments (Figure 3B to 3E, Video 6).

Final Procedural Result in Patient 1

(A) Final angiography demonstrating TIMI flow grade III. (B) Longitudinal IVUS view. (C to E) Cross-sectional IVUS images showing satisfactory stent expansion and apposition with MSAs of 8.1, 9.0, and 11.3 mm2. IVUS = intravascular ultrasound; MSA = minimal stent area; TIMI = thrombolysis in myocardial infarction.

Patient 2

An 81-year-old man with diabetes and hypertension presented with exertional angina (NYHA functional class III). Electrocardiography showed normal sinus rhythm; echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular systolic function.

Coronary angiography revealed diffuse, calcified disease in the LAD and left circumflex artery. PCI of the circumflex was successful with a 3.0 × 23-mm DES. Angiography of the LAD showed a long, calcified lesion (Figure 4A, Video 7). IVUS confirmed extensive concentric calcification with an MLA of 2.9 mm2 and calcium score of 3 (Figures 4B to 4D, Video 8).

Baseline Assessment of the LAD Lesion in Patient 2

(A) AP cranial view of the LAD showing a fluoroscopically calcified long-segment lesion. (B) IVUS longitudinal view showing a long segment of calcification. (C and D) Cross-sectional IVUS images demonstrating concentric calcification with MLA 2.9 mm2. IVUS = intravascular ultrasound; LAD = left anterior descending artery; MLA = minimal lumen area.

Lesion preparation used OA with a 1.25-mm crown at 80,000 rpm (distal) and 120,000 rpm (mid/proximal) (Figures 5A and 5B), followed by a 2.5 × 12-mm NC balloon (Figure 5C). During preparation, distal LAD dissection and slow flow developed. To seal the dissection and restore flow, a 2.75 × 38-mm DES was implanted.

Lesion Preparation in Patient 2

(A and B) Orbital atherectomy using a 1.25-mm crown at 80,000 rpm in the distal LAD and 120,000 rpm in the proximal LAD. (C) Further plaque modification using a 2.5 × 12-mm NC balloon. (D) Intravascular lithotripsy using a 3.5 × 12-mm balloon in the mid-to-proximal LAD (80 pulses). (E and F) IVUS demonstrating calcium fracture following IVL and residual distal stent underexpansion and malapposition after provisional stenting. IVL = intravascular lithotripsy; IVUS = intravascular ultrasound; LAD = left anterior descending artery; NC = noncompliant.

Repeat IVUS showed persistent calcium-related resistance and incomplete preparation despite a satisfactory angiographic appearance, prompting adjunctive IVL with a 3.5 × 12-mm balloon (80 pulses) in the proximal LAD (Figure 5D, Video 9). Repeat IVUS showed calcium fracture proximally and residual distal stent underexpansion and malapposition (Figure 5E and 5F, Video 10).

Treatment concluded with a 3.5 × 44-mm DES from mid to proximal LAD. Sequential postdilation used 3.0 × 10 mm (distal) and 3.5 × 12 to 4.0 × 10 mm (proximal) NC balloons. Final angiography showed TIMI flow grade III (Figure 6A, Video 11). Final IVUS confirmed satisfactory stent expansion and apposition, with MSAs from proximal to distal segment: 11.9, 8.3, and 5.1 mm2. (Figures 6B to 6E, Video 12).

(A) AP view of the LAD post-DES showing TIMI flow grade III. (B) IVUS longitudinal view showing satisfactory stent apposition. (C-E) Final MSA from proximal to distal segment: 11.9, 8.3, and 5.1 mm2. AP = anteroposterior; DES = drug-eluting stent; IVUS = intravascular ultrasound; LAD = left anterior descending artery; MSA = minimal stent area.

Patient 3

A 72-year-old woman presented with 10 days of exertional chest pain (NYHA class III). Electrocardiography showed T-wave inversion in precordial leads. Echocardiography revealed anterior wall hypokinesia, reduced left ventricular systolic function (ejection fraction ∼40%), and mild mitral regurgitation.

Coronary angiography demonstrated diffuse, long-segment, severely calcified disease in the LAD (Figures 7A and 7D, Videos 13 and 14). The dominant circumflex artery lacked significant obstructive disease. Baseline IVUS confirmed extensive calcification (calcium score 3). The MLA measured 1.9 mm2 in the distal LAD and 2.9 mm2 in the proximal LAD (Figures 7B to 7E, Video 15).

Baseline Assessment of the LAD Lesion in Patient 3

(A) AP cranial view of the LAD showing a fluoroscopically calcified long-segment lesion. (B) IVUS longitudinal view showing a long segment of calcification. (C) Cross-sectional IVUS image in the distal LAD with MLA 1.9 mm2. (D and E) RAO caudal view of the LAD showing significant proximal disease with corresponding MLA on IVUS of 2.9 mm2. IVUS = intravascular ultrasound; LAD = left anterior descending artery; MLA = minimal lumen area; RAO = right anterior oblique.

Lesion preparation used OA with a 1.25-mm crown at 80,000 rpm (distal) and 120,000 rpm (proximal) (Figures 8A and 8B). Subsequent dilation used 2.5-mm NC balloons distally and 3.0-mm NC balloons proximally (Figure 8C). During distal LAD dilation, an Ellis type III coronary perforation occurred (Figure 8D, Video 16).

Lesion Preparation and Perforation Management in Patient 3

(A and B) Orbital atherectomy using a 1.25-mm crown at 80,000 rpm in the distal LAD and 120,000 rpm in the proximal LAD. (C) Further plaque modification using a 2.5-mm NC balloon distally and a 3.0-mm NC balloon proximally. (D) Ellis type III coronary perforation of the distal LAD. (E) Deployment of a 2.8 × 19-mm covered stent (Graftmaster; Abbott Vascular) to seal the perforation. (F) Intravascular lithotripsy using a 3.0 × 12-mm balloon in the mid-to-proximal LAD (80 pulses). LAD = left anterior descending artery; NC = noncompliant.

Balloon tamponade was established while maintaining guidewire position. A 2.8 × 19 mm covered stent (Graftmaster, Abbott Vascular) was deployed across the perforation (Figure 8E). Immediate echocardiography showed no hemodynamically significant pericardial effusion.

Following perforation sealing and hemodynamic stability, IVL was performed in the remaining calcified mid-to-proximal LAD using a 3.0 × 12-mm balloon (80 pulses) (Figure 8F). Definitive treatment involved a 3.0 × 48-mm DES from the distal to ostial LAD, overlapping the covered stent. Postdilation used 2.75- and 3.0-mm NC balloons in the distal and proximal segments.

Final angiography showed successful revascularization with TIMI III flow and no residual perforation (Figure 9A, Video 17). Final IVUS confirmed satisfactory stent expansion and apposition (Figure 9B), with MSAs of 4.5 mm2 and 6.2 mm2 in the distal and mid LAD (Figures 9C and 9D, Video 18).

Final Procedural Result in Patient 3

(A) AP view of the LAD after covered stent implantation and definitive DES implantation showing TIMI III flow and no residual perforation. (B) IVUS longitudinal view showing satisfactory stent apposition. (C and D) Final MSA in the distal and mid LAD: 4.5 and 6.2 mm2, respectively. DES = drug-eluting stent; IVUS = intravascular ultrasound; LAD = left anterior descending artery; MSA = minimal stent area.

All patients remained free from death, myocardial infarction, target lesion revascularization, and recurrent angina during the 5-month follow-up.

Discussion

Severe coronary calcification remains a major challenge during PCI, and no single plaque-modification device effectively addresses all patterns of coronary calcium. Contemporary consensus documents therefore advocate an imaging-guided approach in which device selection is tailored to lesion morphology and calcium characteristics.1,3 The present series illustrates how IVUS findings informed escalation from OA to IVL across 3 distinct clinical scenarios (Table 1).

Although this series was not designed to establish a formal treatment algorithm, several procedural observations consistently informed escalation between calcium-modification modalities (Supplemental Figure 1). Balloon-uncrossable lesions or inability to advance intravascular imaging catheters favored an atherectomy-based strategy to facilitate device delivery. Following atherectomy, persistent balloon dog-boning, inadequate balloon expansion, residual circumferential calcium, or calcium-related stent underexpansion suggested incomplete modification of deeper calcium and prompted adjunctive IVL. Repeat IVUS assessment was then used to confirm calcium fracture, improved vessel compliance, and adequacy of lesion preparation before stent implantation. These triggers are broadly consistent with contemporary imaging-guided recommendations and illustrate how established principles can be applied in real-world practice.1, 2, 3

The first patient demonstrates the value of a stepwise approach in a balloon-uncrossable lesion. Failure to advance both a low-profile balloon and an IVUS catheter suggested severe mechanical resistance and favored atherectomy. Persistent balloon dog-boning after OA indicated residual deep calcium, prompting adjunctive IVL, which improved vessel compliance and enabled satisfactory stent expansion. The second patient highlights the importance of repeat intravascular imaging after lesion preparation. Despite an acceptable angiographic appearance, IVUS demonstrated persistent calcium-related resistance, residual stent underexpansion, and malapposition, prompting further optimization and influencing the final treatment strategy. Together, these cases underscore the limitations of angiography alone and the importance of IVUS-guided decision-making.1,3

The third patient represents the most instructive scenario in this series. Coronary perforation remains an uncommon but potentially catastrophic complication of PCI and occurs more frequently in heavily calcified lesions requiring aggressive plaque modification, oversized balloons, atherectomy, or high-pressure dilation.6 Several factors likely contributed to perforation risk in this patient, including diffuse severe calcification, reduced vessel compliance following atherectomy, long-segment disease, and the need for high-pressure balloon expansion. Moreover, the perforation occurred during balloon dilation rather than during OA or IVL itself. This distinction is noteworthy because pooled analyses of the DISRUPT CAD studies reported no IVL-related coronary perforations, although isolated case reports have subsequently been described.7,8 Operators should be aware that the combination of extensive circumferential calcification and prior atherectomy-induced loss of vessel wall compliance may heighten perforation risk during subsequent balloon dilation; the C-CAT sign—defined as a C-type eccentric approximately 270° calcified arc with a thin-walled contralateral plaque segment on IVUS—has been proposed as a preprocedural imaging predictor of perforation in such settings and should prompt conservative balloon sizing.9

Management followed contemporary principles for treatment of Ellis type III coronary perforation, including immediate balloon tamponade, preservation of guidewire position, rapid deployment of a covered stent, and continuous hemodynamic monitoring.6,10 Ellis type III perforations carry a high risk of cardiac tamponade and mortality, and covered stent implantation achieves hemostasis in 73% to 94% of cases.10 Following successful covered-stent implantation and confirmation of hemodynamic stability without significant pericardial effusion, completion of lesion preparation and definitive PCI remained feasible. Although conclusions cannot be drawn from a single patient, this experience suggests that successful perforation sealing does not necessarily preclude completion of revascularization when vessel integrity has been restored, and the patient remains clinically stable.

This patient also highlights several practical considerations. First, intravascular imaging should be used whenever possible to optimize device sizing and identify preprocedural morphological features that may predispose to vessel injury, including the C-CAT IVUS pattern in diffusely calcified lesions.1, 2, 3,9 Second, operators undertaking complex calcium modification should maintain immediate access to covered stents and be familiar with contemporary bailout algorithms before initiating treatment.6,10,11 Finally, procedural flexibility remains essential when treating severely calcified lesions, particularly when unexpected complications arise.

An important observation across all 3 cases was the central role of IVUS throughout the procedure. Preprocedural imaging defined calcium severity and lesion morphology; repeat imaging identified residual calcium-related resistance after atherectomy, and post-IVL imaging confirmed treatment effect and guided stent optimization. Thus, IVUS functioned not only as a diagnostic tool but also as an integral component of procedural decision-making.

The complementary mechanisms of OA and IVL provide a rationale for their combined use. OA facilitates device delivery and modifies superficial calcium, whereas IVL fractures deeper calcium and improves vessel compliance. Previous reports have demonstrated the feasibility of sequential atherectomy and IVL in resistant calcified lesions.4,5 However, the optimal indications, sequencing strategies, and imaging thresholds for escalation remain uncertain and warrant further investigation.

Nevertheless, this case series has some limitations. It describes only 3 patients from a single center, limiting generalizability. All lesions involved the LAD, and the observations may not apply to other coronary territories. The nonconsecutive nature of the series introduces potential selection and reporting bias. Follow-up was limited to 5 months and does not permit assessment of long-term outcomes, including restenosis, target lesion failure, or stent thrombosis. Finally, the imaging findings that prompted escalation from OA to IVL reflect operator judgment rather than prospectively validated thresholds. Accordingly, the procedural approach described should be considered illustrative and hypothesis-generating rather than prescriptive.

Collectively, this case series illustrates the feasibility of integrating OA and IVL within an IVUS-guided calcium-modification strategy for severely calcified coronary lesions. The 3 cases demonstrate how lesion behavior, balloon response, and intravascular imaging findings may inform escalation between complementary calcium-modification modalities in distinct procedural scenarios, including management of major complications. Rather than proposing a treatment algorithm, these observations provide practical procedural insights and generate hypotheses for future study. Larger prospective studies are needed to define optimal sequencing strategies and validate imaging-guided decision pathways.
