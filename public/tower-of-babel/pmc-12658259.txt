Associations of adverse childhood experiences and depressive symptoms among children and adolescents: the mediating effect of resilience and sleep quality
Authors: Wenping Ji, Lewei Liu, Pei Tang, Lili Zhao, Zhaoyan Wen, Guangbo Qu, Yu Chen, Huanzhong Liu, Yehuan Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdverse childhood experiences (ACEs) may precipitate depressive symptoms among children and adolescents, yet whether resilience and sleep quality jointly mediate this link remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate these potential mediating pathways. From April to June 2023, a total of 3258 children and adolescents were included for this cross-sectional study from 11 primary and secondary schools in Chaohu City, Anhui Province, China. We used a series of scales to assess their depressive symptoms, ACEs, resilience, and sleep quality. To explore the potential mediating pathways between depressive symptoms, ACEs, resilience, and sleep quality, the chain mediation analysis was conducted using the PROCESS macro (Model 6). The prevalence of depressive symptoms was 30.1% among children and adolescents. The multivariate logistic regression analyses showed that females, ACEs total score, resilience, and poor sleep quality were independent correlates of depressive symptoms. When the dimensions of ACEs were involved in the regression model, emotional abuse, physical abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect and mother suffered from domestic violence were also independent risk factors. Notably, resilience and sleep quality had a chain-mediated effect between ACEs and depressive symptoms. A high proportion of children and adolescents reported depressive symptoms. Moreover, resilience and sleep quality may have a chain-mediated effect between ACEs and depressive symptoms. Hence, the impact of ACEs, resilience and sleep quality on depressive symptoms among children and adolescents should be highlighted and targeted interventions should be implemented.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25994-2.Keywords: Depressive symptoms, Adverse childhood experiences, Resilience, Sleep quality, Children, AdolescentsSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Psychology, Psychology, Risk factors

Introduction

Depressive disorder, a mental condition typified by persistent low mood and anhedonia, is projected to be one of the leading contributors to the global disease burden by 20301. Notably, with the increase of social pressure and lifestyle changes, the incidence of depressive disorder among children and adolescents has shown a continuous climb globally2. In the United States, the detection rate of adolescent depressive episodes rose from 8.1% in 2009 to 15.8% in 20193. Comorbid depressive symptoms not only have a profound impact on cognitive development, academic achievement, and social functioning among children and adolescents, but also significantly elevate suicide risk4,5. Therefore, investigating the factors influencing depressive symptoms in this population and their underlying mechanisms holds significant public health importance.

Family environmental risk factors play a critical role in the development of depressive symptoms, particularly adverse childhood experiences (ACEs). ACEs are defined as stressful or traumatic events occurring before age 18 years and include abuse (emotional, physical, or sexual), neglect (emotional or physical), and family dysfunction (e.g., family member’s substance abuse, mental illness, criminal behaviors, domestic violence, parental divorce or separation, etc.)6. Data from the 2021 China School Adolescent Health Survey showed that 47.3% of adolescents have experienced at least one ACE7. ACEs can have significant negative effects on the mental health of children and adolescents. For example, a national study from the United States has found that the more adolescents aged 12–17 years were exposed to ACEs, the higher their risk of developing mental health symptoms such as depression, anxiety, and behavioral problems8. However, the specific pathways through which ACEs influence depressive symptoms among children and adolescents, particularly their mediating mechanisms, are still not fully understood.

Resilience and sleep quality may serve as key mediating variables in the association between ACEs and depressive symptoms. Resilience refers to an individual’s capacity to recover and maintain psychological equilibrium in the face of adversity, trauma, stress, or other significant life challenges, and its development is characterized by considerable dynamism and plasticity9. According to psychosocial development theory, childhood is a critical window for the formation of resilience, with factors such as family environment quality and social support systems playing pivotal roles in shaping its trajectory10. A systematic review indicated that ACEs may diminish resilience among children and adolescents11. And lower levels of resilience may, in turn, contribute to the onset of depressive symptoms among children and adolescents. For instance, a study conducted in Hong Kong, China, identified low resilience as a strong predictor of depressive symptoms among adolescents12. Furthermore, Chen et al. proposed that resilience moderates the association between ACEs and depressive symptoms13.

Moreover, sleep quality, an important physiological indicator of mental health, may also act as a mediator in the association between ACEs and depressive symptoms. Numerous clinical studies have shown that children and adolescents exposed to ACEs face a significantly elevated risk of developing various types of sleep disorders14–16. During adolescence, a critical period for the establishment of sleep-wake rhythms17, sleep problems are closely linked to several adverse mental health outcomes such as anxiety and depression18,19. A recent study further found that childhood family dysfunction undermines resilience, which in turn precipitates poor sleep quality and subsequent depressive symptoms20. These findings suggest that ACEs may affect depressive symptoms through the chain pathway of “impaired resilience → reduced sleep quality”. Yet, this mechanism remains underexplored among children and adolescents. Adolescence is a key sensitive period when psychological resilience is reshaped and sleep rhythms become consolidated, and both processes show marked plasticity and can be modified. Clarifying whether the proposed chain of effects operates in adolescents would therefore help to trace, moment by moment, how ACEs evolve into depressive symptoms and would point to new targets for preventive clinical interventions.

This study aimed to explore the epidemiological characteristics of depressive symptoms among children and adolescents, with a focus on the combined effects of socio-demographic characteristics, ACEs, resilience, and sleep quality. It also sought to validate the mediating mechanism of the chain “ACEs → Resilience → Sleep quality → Depressive symptoms”. Accordingly, we hypothesized that: (1) there is a significant burden of depressive symptoms in Chinese children and adolescents; (2) depressive symptoms are significantly associated with socio-demographic characteristics, ACEs, lower resilience, and poorer sleep quality; (3) resilience and sleep quality play a chain-mediated role between ACEs and depressive symptoms.

Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional survey was conducted from April to June 2023. Using convenience sampling, we selected 11 primary and secondary schools in Chaohu City, Anhui Province, that together cover urban, suburban, and rural areas; although not randomly drawn, the frame maximises regional diversity. The study population consisted of school students, with the following inclusion criteria: (1) age ≤ 18 years; and (2) voluntary participation in the study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) those who are unable to understand the questionnaire; and (2) subjects or their guardians who refused to sign the informed consent form. A total of 3596 children and adolescents were initially recruited. Of these, 213 explicitly declined to participate, leaving 3383 who provided informed consent and completed the questionnaire. After quality control, 125 questionnaires with missing key variables were excluded. Consequently, 3258 valid questionnaires were retained for statistical analysis, yielding an effective response rate of 90.6% (Fig. 1). Among these children and adolescents, the mean age was 13.01 ± 1.61 years (range: 9–18 years), with 52.1% being male (Table 1).

This study’s data collection was conducted by uniformly trained investigators, utilizing a standardized questionnaire administration process. Before the formal survey, the researchers provided participants with a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose, the method of completing the questionnaire, and the principle of confidentiality. All participants and their legal guardians provided informed consent by signing the consent form. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (KYXM-202210-010). It also followed the relevant ethical requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 version) (https://www.wma.net/policies-post/wma-declaration-of-helsinki/) and the STROBE checklist (see the attachment).

Measuring instruments

Socio-demographic characteristics

The socio-demographic characteristics of the children and adolescents, including their age, gender, body mass index (BMI) and family economic status, were collected using a self-designed questionnaire.

Depressive symptoms

The Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) was utilized to assess subjects’ depressive symptoms21. This scale comprises 20 items rated on a 4-point scale, with total scores ranging from 0 to 60. A higher total score indicates more severe depressive symptoms. According to the original scale, the criteria for depressive symptoms are as follows: total score ≤ 15 indicates no depression, 16–19 indicates mild depressive symptoms, and ≥ 20 indicates moderate to severe depressive symptoms. Referring to previous research on Chinese children and adolescents22, this study defined the presence of depressive symptoms as a CES-D total score > 15. Currently, the Chinese version of the CES-D has demonstrated good reliability and validity and is widely used among Chinese children and adolescents23. In our study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for CES-D was 0.907. It is important to note that although the CES-D is a widely used screening tool for depressive symptoms, it does not provide a clinical diagnosis of depression. Our study measured the prevalence of depressive symptoms, not clinically diagnosed depression.

ACEs

In this study, ACEs were comprehensively assessed across 10 key dimensions24. The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) was utilized to evaluate experiences of abuse and neglect among children and adolescents25. The CTQ comprises 28 items rated on a 5-point scale and includes five subscales: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect. Higher CTQ scores indicate a higher level of abuse or neglect experienced by the individual. For this study, each subscale was categorized into two groups (yes and no) based on established cut-off points: 13 for emotional abuse, 10 for physical abuse, 8 for sexual abuse, 15 for emotional neglect, and 10 for physical neglect26. In addition to the CTQ, 5 other dimensions of family dysfunction were assessed, drawing on previous research: (1) family members were addicted to gambling, drugs or alcohol; (2) parents had been separated or divorced; (3) mother suffered from domestic violence; (4) family members had mental disorders; and (5) family members jailed for crimes24. Each of these 10 dimensions was scored as 1 ACE if present. Consequently, the ACEs total score ranged from 0 to 10, with higher scores reflecting a greater accumulation of ACEs.

Resilience

The Child Youth Resilience Measurement Scale (CYRM) was used to assess the level of resilience27. This scale comprises 28 items rated on a 5-point scale, with total scores ranging from 28 to 140. Higher scores indicate more resilient individuals, who are better able to demonstrate adaptability and resilience when facing life’s challenges and dilemmas. The reliability and validity of this scale have been thoroughly established in numerous studies28,29. And the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for CYRM was 0.961.

Sleep quality

In this study, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was utilized to evaluate sleep quality30. The PSQI includes seven distinct components: sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep quality, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medications, and daytime dysfunction. Each of these components is rated on a scale from 0 to 3, resulting in a total score that ranges from 0 to 21. A higher total score is indicative of worse sleep quality. And the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for PSQI was 0.701.

Statistical analysis

This study employed SPSS 23.0 for data analyses. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (%). The normality of continuous variables was evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Variables that followed a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). In the univariate analyses, independent samples t-tests or chi-square tests were utilized to compare the difference of demographic characteristics, ACEs, resilience, and sleep quality between the depressive symptoms group and the non-depressive symptoms group. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analyses were included in a multivariate logistic regression model (Enter method) to identify independent predictors of depressive symptoms. And variance inflation factor (VIF) was employed to test the covariance problem of each variable. The results showed that the VIF values of all independent variables were below 5.0, indicating acceptable multicollinearity levels. This study used the Box-Tidwell method to test the linearity between continuous independent variables and the logit of the dependent variable. The results showed no significant nonlinear trends for any of the continuous predictors (all Box-Tidwell tests, P > 0.05), satisfying the linearity assumption of logistic regression. The model’s calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess its discriminative ability. Additionally, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to assess the correlations between ACEs, depressive symptoms, resilience, and sleep quality. Based on existing empirical evidence and the theoretical assumptions proposed in this study, ACEs may indirectly aggravate depressive symptoms through the pathway of “impaired resilience → decreased sleep quality”. To test this potential chain-mediation mechanism, the present study employed the PROCESS macro (Model 6). A 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated using 5000 Bootstrap samples, and a mediation effect was deemed significant if the interval did not include zero. In the mediation analysis, potential confounding factors, including age, gender, and BMI, were controlled for. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed P < 0.05.

Results

Comparison of socio-demographic and psychological health data in the depressed and non-depressed groups

In our study, the prevalence of depressive symptoms was 30.1% among children and adolescents. Univariate analyses revealed that compared to the group without depressive symptoms, children and adolescents with depressive symptoms were more likely to be females (χ2 = 27.161, P < 0.001) and older (t = 5.371, P < 0.001). They were more likely to experience ACEs (t = 19.180, P < 0.001), including sexual abuse (χ2 = 41.045, P < 0.001), emotional abuse (χ2 = 293.223, P < 0.001), physical abuse (χ2 = 138.100, P < 0.001), emotional neglect (χ2 = 265.023, P < 0.001), physical neglect (χ2 = 209.255, P < 0.001), family members were addicted to gambling, drugs or alcohol (χ2 = 66.491, P < 0.001), parents had been separated or divorced (χ2 = 35.584, P < 0.001), mother suffered from domestic violence (χ2 = 121.044, P < 0.001), family members had mental disorders (χ2 = 39.841, P < 0.001), family members jailed for crimes (χ2 = 7.618, P = 0.006), and family economy was extremely hard (χ2 = 62.266, P < 0.001). In addition, they had lower levels of resilience (t = -23.842, P < 0.001) and poorer sleep quality (t = 24.675, P < 0.001) (Table 1). Additionally, regarding the risk of cumulative ACEs levels, we found that the risk of depressive symptoms gradually increases with the accumulation of ACEs scores.

Independent factors associated with depressive symptoms

When ACEs total score involved in the multivariate logistic regression model (Model 1), females (OR = 1.322, 95% CI 1.103–1.586, P = 0.003), ACEs total score (OR = 1.453, 95% CI 1.351–1.562, P < 0.001), resilience (OR = 0.973, 95% CI 0.969–0.978, P < 0.001), and poor sleep quality (OR = 1.322, 95% CI 1.276–1.369, P < 0.001) were independent correlates of depressive symptoms (Table 2). When the dimensions of ACEs involved in the regression model (each CTQ dimension treated as a continuous variable) (Model 2), females (OR = 1.256, 95% CI 1.040–1.516, P = 0.018), age (OR = 1.066, 95% CI 1.002–1.133, P = 0.041), emotional abuse score (OR = 1.209, 95% CI 1.160–1.259, P < 0.001), emotional neglect score (OR = 1.034, 95% CI 1.008–1.061, P = 0.011), physical neglect score (OR = 1.070, 95% CI 1.025–1.118, P = 0.002), family members jailed for crimes (OR = 0.467, 95% CI 0.222–0.982, P = 0.045), resilience (OR = 0.976, 95% CI 0.972–0.981, P < 0.001), and poor sleep quality (OR = 1.279, 95% CI 1.233–1.326, P < 0.001) were independent correlates (Table 2). When the dimensions of ACEs involved in the regression model (each CTQ dimension treated as a binary variable) (Model 3), females (OR = 1.313, 95% CI 1.092–1.580, P = 0.004), emotional abuse (OR = 2.998, 95% CI 1.989–4.518, P < 0.001), physical abuse (OR = 1.702, 95% CI 1.129–2.567, P = 0.011), emotional neglect (OR = 1.463, 95% CI 1.145–1.869, P = 0.002), physical neglect (OR = 1.455, 95% CI 1.140–1.857, P = 0.003), mother suffered from domestic violence (OR = 1.496, 95% CI 1.068–2.095, P = 0.019), family members jailed for crimes (OR = 0.445, 95% CI 0.214–0.925, P = 0.030), resilience (OR = 0.974, 95% CI 0.969–0.979, P < 0.001), and poor sleep quality (OR = 1.316, 95% CI 1.270–1.364, P < 0.001) were independent correlates (Table 2). When ACEs accumulation category involved in the multivariate logistic regression model (Model 4), ACEs accumulation category also emerged as an independent risk factor for depressive symptoms (P < 0.001) (Table 2). Compared to individuals with no ACEs, those with 1, 2, and 3 or more ACEs had 1.623 times, 2.431 times, and 4.461 times higher odds of depressive symptoms, respectively.

Model evaluation

The calibration of the four models was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The χ2 values for Models 1, 2, 3 and 4 were 8.800, 5.390, 11.502 and 2.516, respectively, with corresponding p-values of 0.359, 0.715, 0.175 and 0.961 (all P > 0.05) (Table 3). These results indicate that no systematic discrepancy exists between the predicted probabilities and the observed events for any model, suggesting that all four models exhibit good calibration. And the AUCs for Models 1, 2, 3 and 4 were 0.827, 0.842, 0.830 and 0.827, respectively (all P < 0.001).

Correlations between depressive symptoms, ACEs, resilience and sleep quality

Correlation analyses showed that depressive symptoms were significantly correlated with ACEs total score, resilience, and sleep quality, respectively (all P < 0.05) (Table 4). As well, ACEs total score was also significantly correlated with resilience and sleep quality, respectively (all P < 0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

In our study, depressive symptoms were detected in 30.1% of children and adolescents, consistent with previous findings31–33. A survey of Chinese school adolescents revealed that about 34% of adolescents had depressive symptoms32. Another study using the CES-D scale among 32,389 Chinese children and adolescents also found that the overall prevalence of depressive symptoms was as high as 39.9%33. A meta-analysis further revealed that the prevalence of depressive symptoms in this population has gradually increased over time in recent years, reaching a current prevalence of 26.3%31. Notably, the data-collection window (April–June) overlapped with the peak examination period in the latter half of the semester; academic stress may therefore have inflated the observed prevalence of emotional problems. Future studies can systematically incorporate “exam season” as a seasonal stressor to clarify its true contribution to fluctuations in depressive symptoms. Additionally, this study found that the risk of depressive symptoms increased with age, a conclusion that previous research had repeatedly confirmed34,35. On one hand, the sharp fluctuations of sex hormones and cortisol after puberty directly disrupt emotion regulation pathways, making adolescents (especially girls) more vulnerable to emotional lows36,37. On the other, the mounting academic workload, peer comparisons, entrance exam competition, and family expectations that come with each additional year create chronic psychological stress, further magnifying the probability that depressive symptoms will surface38–40. Also, our study indicated that girls were significantly more likely than boys to report depressive symptoms. Sun et al. found that female adolescents exhibited a higher prevalence of depression and more severe depressive symptoms at all ages compared to males41. This gender difference may be related to the following reasons. First, the onset of the menstrual cycle and fluctuations in hormone levels can significantly affect the mood of female children and adolescents42,43. For example, during the premenstrual period, female adolescents may experience emotional problems such as irritability and depression, which may gradually develop into depressive symptoms if they are not addressed and alleviated in a timely manner44,45. Moreover, female children and adolescents tend to exhibit higher levels of rumination and anxiety when faced with negative events, and these unique psychological developmental characteristics make them more vulnerable to depressive symptoms46,47. Taken together, these findings suggest that a significant proportion of Chinese children and adolescents (especially girls) exhibit depressive symptoms, a problem that cannot be ignored. Therefore, parents, school staff, and the community should closely monitor the mental health status of this population and proactively explore the risk factors associated with depressive symptoms, so that effective interventions can be implemented in a timely manner.

In-depth analyses found that ACEs were central to the development of depressive symptoms in youth. A series of previous clinical studies have elucidated the strong and complex association between ACEs and depressive symptoms8,48,49. For instance, a 12-year longitudinal study demonstrated that ACEs, including childhood abuse and physical violence victimization, were significantly and positively linked to increased depression from adolescence to early adulthood, with females showing higher vulnerability49. The link between ACEs and depressive symptoms may involve multiple mechanisms. At the biological level, ACEs may increase the likelihood of depression among children and adolescents through specific epigenetic changes (e.g., in NR3C1, SLC6A4, BDNF, FKBP5), dysregulated cortisol secretion rhythms, elevated levels of inflammatory markers (such as IL-6, CRP, and TNF-α), and by disrupting brain structures and functional connectivity involved in emotion regulation50–54. At the psychosocial level, a cross-sectional study found that ACEs can contribute to depressive symptoms by interfering with an individual’s normal ability to regulate emotions55. Another study from China found that ACEs can promote the development of borderline personality traits and reduced self-control in adolescents, which are strongly associated with depression56. Furthermore, the present study also found that emotional abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect, and mothers suffering from domestic violence appeared to be more strongly associated with depressive symptoms among children and adolescents. This may suggest that these specific subtypes of ACEs have a more pronounced impact on the emergence of depressive symptoms in this population. However, further longitudinal and foundational research is essential to elucidate how these ACE types specifically influence depressive symptoms among children and adolescents. This study further revealed that, in terms of cumulative effects, compared to those who had not experienced ACEs, children and adolescents who had experienced one, two, and three or more ACEs were at 1.623, 2.431, and 4.461 times higher risk of developing depressive symptoms, respectively. It indicated that the more ACEs children and adolescents accumulated, the more severe the impact on their mental health. Therefore, schools and social workers were advised to identify ACEs exposure in that population as early as possible and provide early intervention.

In mediation analyses, resilience mediated the association between ACEs and depressive symptoms among children and adolescents. Indeed, the formation of resilience can be traced back to individuals’ childhood stages, and early encounters with ACEs can disrupt the normal trajectory of psychological development, consequently increasing the risk of low resilience10. A study of patients with mood disorders revealed that individuals presenting with childhood trauma (especially emotional abuse and emotional neglect) generally exhibit low resilience57. Furthermore, prior research has confirmed the association between resilience and depressive symptoms58,59. A cross-sectional study focusing on the adolescent population showed that reduced resilience was significantly and positively associated with current depressive symptoms58. Elmore et al. noted that children with ≥ 4 ACEs and low resilience were 8.75 times more likely to be depressed59. On the one hand, individuals with low resilience tend to have significant deficits in their ability to cope with stress, which can be manifested in difficulties in seeking support from others and coping effectively with emotions, and this can significantly increase individuals’ risk of falling into poor mental health states, including depression60. On the other hand, children and adolescents with low resilience tend to have difficulty viewing problems and setbacks in a positive light and are more likely to develop negative cognitive patterns, which can increase susceptibility to depression61,62. Similarly, such mediating effects have been tentatively confirmed in previous studies. A survey of children from Slovakia also showed that resilience mediated the associations between ACEs and emotional and behavioural problems63. Another meta-analysis further showed that childhood trauma, resilience, and depression were significantly associated, with resilience playing a mediating role between the other two64. In summary, this study underscored the need to consider resilience as an underlying factor of interventions for depressive symptoms among children and adolescents, and actively developing more targeted psychological interventions. For example, a randomised controlled trial demonstrated that a resilience-themed curriculum significantly improved children’s resilience and mental health65.

Also, the study identified sleep quality as an additional mediator linking ACEs to depressive symptoms among children and adolescents. The robust associations between ACEs and sleep disturbances have been widely studied. A prospective cohort study found that ACEs experienced during youth significantly predict poor sleep outcomes later on14. Such associations involve multiple mechanisms. Studies have shown that adolescents experiencing ACEs often face significant psychological stress (e.g., hypervigilance, anxiety), which can markedly disrupt the sleep-wake cycle66,67. In terms of neurobiochemistry, ACEs also affect the balance of neurotransmitters associated with sleep regulation, such as by reducing serotonin and dopamine levels68,69. Furthermore, our data further showed that poor sleep quality was independently linked to depressive symptoms, aligning with previous research findings70–72. For example, a prospective study spanning nearly 30 years demonstrated a significant bidirectional association between sleep disorders and depressive symptoms in childhood and adolescence through cross-lagged modelling70. Another human experimental study found that sleep deprivation heightens inflammatory markers, thereby increasing susceptibility to depressive symptoms71. As well, neuroimaging studies have further revealed that sleep disorders may induce depressive-like manifestations in adolescence by affecting the integrity of brain structures such as the cingulate bundle72.

Finally, this study provided preliminary evidence that resilience and sleep quality may have a chain-mediated effect between ACEs and depressive symptoms. This sequential pathway was consistent with stress-coping theory73. Specifically, ACEs act as chronic stressors that persistently impact individuals’ psychological and physiological well-being. According to stress-coping theory, individuals employ various coping strategies to regulate the impact of stress74. Resilience plays a key mediating role in this process, serving as a positive psychological resource that helps individuals cope more effectively with stress and mitigates its negative impacts on sleep quality and mental health24,75. Conversely, if individuals have lower resilience, stress may be more associated with poor sleep quality and, in turn, exacerbate depressive symptoms76. Chang et al. found that ACEs significantly undermine individuals’ resilience, which in turn disrupts sleep quality20, thereby supporting our findings. These findings provided a new perspective for understanding the complex association between ACEs and depressive symptoms. They also offered a theoretical basis for developing interventions that enhance resilience to improve sleep quality and alleviate depressive symptoms. Certainly, the above pathways are only hypothetical paths supported by cross-sectional mediation analysis, and future longitudinal studies are still needed for validation. Moreover, subsequent research should also consider the possibility of reverse pathways—for example, depressive symptoms may in turn affect sleep quality and resilience.

Undeniably, the present study has some limitations: (1) This study was cross-sectional; thus, the mediation findings rest on the strong assumption of sequential ignorability, and the results should therefore be interpreted as statistical mediation rather than causal mediation. Future research should adopt a longitudinal follow-up design with multiple time points to verify the mediation model of this study. (2) This study was conducted only in Chaohu, Anhui Province, China, which may limit the external validity of the findings. It is recommended that subsequent studies expand the sampling scope by adopting a multicentre, multiregional stratified sampling strategy and consider the inclusion of samples from different cultural backgrounds to improve the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, because students were nested within schools, standard errors were likely to be underestimated, increasing the risk of Type I error. Interpretations of these findings should thus be made with caution and, whenever possible, validated in future studies using clustering-aware methods such as multilevel linear models or cluster-robust standard errors. (3) The main variables of this study were collected using self-report scales, which may introduce recall bias. Additionally, the reliability of some scales was relatively low in this study; for example, the Cronbach’s α for the PSQI was only 0.701, which could affect the stability and reliability of the findings. Therefore, it is necessary to introduce objective measures (e.g., sleep monitoring devices) in future studies to improve the reliability of the findings. (4) Depression was identified solely based on the CES-D cutoff score, without confirmation through standard clinical diagnosis. Consequently, the reported prevalence may not accurately reflect the true rate of clinical depression and could be either overestimated or underestimated. (5) The present study did not measure or control for potential moderators (e.g., cultural background) or confounders (e.g., social support, parent-child relationship quality, current stress levels), which may limit the reliability of the findings. Future research should therefore incorporate a broader set of variables to fully examine the associations among depressive symptoms, ACEs, resilience, and sleep quality among children and adolescents.

Conclusion

In this study, a high proportion of school children and adolescents exhibited depressive symptoms, which were significantly associated with ACEs, low levels of resilience, and poor sleep quality. Further analyses indicated that resilience and sleep quality may have a chain-mediated effect between ACEs and depressive symptoms. Therefore, parents and school staff should pay attention to ACEs among children and adolescents and implement timely interventions to improve resilience, prevent sleep disorders, which may be associated with a reduced risk of depression. Concrete steps include designing and delivering school-based programs aimed at strengthening youth resilience and developing evidence-based interventions to improve sleep quality as complementary strategies for alleviating depressive symptoms.
