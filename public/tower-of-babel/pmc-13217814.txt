Psychological and emotional aspects in people with diabetic foot disease: a systematic scoping review
Authors: Alice Cardon, Tue Helms Andersen, Abhilasha Akerkar, Mette Due-Christensen, Olivia Braad Honoré, Anne Rasmussen, Peter Rossing, Bryan Cleal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeveral psychological and emotional aspects have been identified in people with diabetic foot disease (DFD). Currently, the scope of evidence on the topic is unclear. The aim of this scoping review is to identify and map the literature on psychological and emotional aspects of DFD.MethodsA systematic search was conducted on the 6th of May 2024, in MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL and PsycInfo. Search results were screened based on predefined eligibility criteria. Data charting followed predefined extraction sheets, adapted for quantitative or qualitative studies.ResultsOut of 1,838 unique records identified from the database search and 1,819 from backwards citation search, 118 studies were deemed eligible for inclusion. Five categories of foot outcomes were identified. Qualitative studies revealed psychological and emotional impacts, primarily through individual interviews and various methods of analysis. Quantitative studies reported 25 different psychological and emotional concepts, utilizing 53 distinct tools. Most common concepts were depression, anxiety, and illness perception.ConclusionExisting research on psychological and emotional aspects of DFD is diverse, using a wide variety of study designs, concepts, and tools. Most studies focus on quantitative psychological factors. This highlights the need for further exploration of other relevant aspects to enhance patient support.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04545-y.Keywords: Scoping review, Psychological, Emotional, Psychosocial health, Diabetic foot disease, Amputation

Introduction

Diabetic foot disease (DFD) is a common and serious diabetes-related complication, with significant health, social, and economic implications. It is defined as “an infection, ulceration, or destruction of foot tissues linked with neuropathy and/or peripheral artery disease in diabetes patients” [1]. Diabetic foot and lower limb complications affect 40 to 60 million people with diabetes globally [2], with lifetime incidence of diabetic foot ulcers (DFU) between 19 and 34% [3]. DFU are strongly associated with lower extremity amputations (LEA), with 80% of LEA among people living with diabetes being preceded by foot ulcers. The five-year mortality rate of individuals with DFU is estimated at 30.5% [4].

People living with DFD have poorer psychosocial health. For example, people with DFU have lower health-related quality of life compared with the diabetes population without DFU [5–7], and individuals without diabetes [6]. The number and severity of DFU are associated with poorer health-related quality of life [8]. Furthermore, people with DFU have a higher risk of suffering from mental health conditions such as anxiety [9] and depression [10–12]. Moreover, complications of DFD are known predictors of depression: diabetes-related LEAs are associated with an increased risk of depression and anxiety [13], with prevalence of depression post LEA being 33.8% [14]. Other psychological and emotional aspects have been studied in people with DFD, such as fear of amputation: in this population, LEA is feared more than death [15]. Recent qualitative studies showed that people with DFU experience psychological distress related to the condition and its impact on the daily life [16–18].

Various psychological and emotional aspects have been identified in studies as associated to the presence and progression of DFD. Several literature reviews have been conducted on this subject: a 2019 narrative review highlighted that existing studies predominantly focused on quality of life and the role of depression in the development of DFU [19]. Another narrative review provided an overview of the wellbeing domains studied in people with DFU, finding that “psychological wellbeing” was addressed in four studies [20]. A 2022 meta-synthesis of qualitative studies described the perceptions of people with current DFU, and dedicated a sub-theme to “Emotional burdens” [21]. A 2020 systematic review including quantitative designs found moderate evidence of an association between psychosocial and behavioral factors, and DFU [22]. In terms of a possible effect of psychological interventions in the population, a 2021 systematic review highlighted that counseling, cognitive behavioral therapy and relaxation lead to improved healing, though the certainty of evidence was very low [23].

The previous reviews all address psychological and emotional factors to some extent, albeit without it ever being exclusively the primary focus. In recent years, the importance of psychological and emotional aspects in individuals living with DFD has gained increased attention, leading to more studies conducted in this area. It is essential, therefore, to identify and synthetize the existing literature, including the most recent publications on the subject. Moreover, most literature reviews focus on depression in a quantitative design, which highlights the need for a comprehensive review including both quantitative and qualitative studies, to explore the latest and broader scope of psychological and emotional research in the population of interest. This will provide a foundation for future research and a better understanding of the psychological and emotional characteristics that have been studied in this population. Profiling these characteristics in individuals with diabetic foot disease is particularly important, as they are known to influence how individuals are able to engage in foot care [24]. Psychological and emotional aspects are here defined as any individual aspect related to a person’s range of emotions and psychological functioning, either positive or negative. This broad definition aims to capture the diverse dimensions that may be studied in the literature, reflecting the multifaceted nature of the topic.

The aim of this scoping review is to identify and map the literature on psychological and emotional aspects of diabetic foot disease.

Methods

Scoping reviews aim to “systematically identify and map the breadth of evidence available on a particular topic, field, concept, or issue, often irrespective of source within or across particular contexts […]. Scoping reviews can […] identify key characteristics or factors related to a concept, including those related to methodological research” [25]. A scoping review design was chosen to identify the width of existing studies, and to examine how research has been conducted on the topic [26]. This design allows inclusion of heterogeneous studies with a diversity in concepts, populations, and measurement tools. After an initial search, a protocol was developed and registered on Research Registry on the 31 st of July 2024 under the number reviewregistry1867 to ensure transparency of the review objectives and methods. The reporting of this review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension (PRISMA-ScR) checklist (Supplementary material 1) [27].

Sources of information and search strategy

The databases MEDLINE, Embase, CINALH, and PsycInfo were searched on the 7th of May 2024. A comprehensive search strategy was developed, combining the concepts `psychological or emotional aspects´ and `people with DFD´. Broad terms were used to capture the variety of research conducted on the topic, including relevant free-text words and Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms. The search was developed in MEDLINE with an information specialist and subsequently adapted to the other databases. The search string was peer reviewed by another information specialist and evaluated by testing retrieval of nine key articles known to the research team, all of which were successfully retrieved. The full search strings are available in Supplementary material 2. Search results were imported into EPPI-Reviewer 6 [28], and duplicates were removed. A backwards citation search retrieving the references of the included studies was conducted using the software tool citationchaser [29].

Eligibility criteria

Eligible records had to have primary data on psychological or emotional aspects in individuals with DFD. Criteria for inclusion were: i) a description or focus on a psychological or emotional aspect (quantitative or qualitative), ii) from adults with type 1 diabetes (T1D) or type 2 diabetes (T2D), iii) with (a history of) DFD or DFU, or Charcot neuroarthropathy (Charcot foot), or amputation preceded by DFD. Exclusion criteria were: i) no separate data on the population of interest (e.g. studies on diabetes complications without distinct data for DFD; or studies on amputations but no separate data on the participants with diabetes), ii) data exclusively on severe mental disorders other than depression or anxiety such as eating disorders, personality disorders, psychotic disorders, post-traumatic disorders (a systematic review protocol on mental health issues has been published in 2022 [30]), iii) data exclusively on quality of life (covered in a 2019 meta-analysis [31]), iv) non-primary journal articles (in case of conference abstract or poster, a potential matching article was looked for and added if applicable), v) reports in languages other than English, French, Danish, Swedish, or Norwegian (languages understood by the research team). In case an article was eligible by title and abstract but only available in another language, this was listed in Supplementary material 3 for others to analyze. No restrictions were applied regarding study date, location, or design. Longitudinal studies or randomized controlled trials focusing on risk factors or studying the effect of an intervention had to indicate psychological or emotional characteristics at baseline.

Study selection

The study selection followed a two-step screening process based on the eligibility criteria. First, a screening of the title and abstract was conducted, followed by a full-text screening. Two reviewers independently performed the screening using EPPI-Reviewer. Screening inter-rater reliability percent agreement was 92.9% for title and abstract screening, and 84.0% for full text screening. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus. If an abstract or full text was unavailable, the information specialist was contacted, and if needed, the authors and journals were contacted.

Data extraction and synthesis

For each included report, data were systematically extracted using a predetermined form tested beforehand by the research team. Extracted data included details about the study characteristics (author, year of publication, country of data collection, study design, study objective), population details (type of diabetes), and type of DFD studied (categorized in DFU, history of DFU, amputation, Charcot foot, and general diabetic foot disease when this term was directly used or when no specific “diabetic foot” definition was indicated). The data extraction process varied based on study design. For quantitative studies, psychological or emotional concepts and data collection tools were described. Quality of life was not considered, as it encompasses items beyond psychological or emotional aspects. For qualitative studies, the methods of data collection and analysis were extracted. In mixed-methods studies, both the quantitative and qualitative sections were examined, and both were extracted using their respective methods if relevant. One author extracted the data from all included studies. To ensure accuracy, a second author independently extracted data from a randomly selected 10% of studies. The extracted data were then compared, and agreement was 100%. Because no discrepancies were identified in this sample, no additional consensus procedures or corrections were required.

Consistent with the descriptive and mapping objectives of this scoping review, which aimed to characterize study designs, populations, and psychological and emotional concepts rather than synthesize study outcomes, no critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence was conducted.

The data were synthetized as follows: diabetic foot outcomes are presented in tabular form. Qualitative and quantitative studies are separated, with qualitative methods presented narratively, and quantitative psychological and emotional concepts tabulated along with the data collection tools.

Results

The search identified 1,838 unique records, after removing 351 duplicates. After initial title and abstract screening, 165 reports were sought for retrieval, and 164 of them assessed for eligibility, as one report could not be retrieved after contacting both journal and authors. 53 reports were excluded in full text screening leaving 111 to be included. The backward citation search retrieved 1,819 unique records after removing 1,048 duplicates. After screening of title and abstract, and full texts, seven reports were added, totaling 118 reports for inclusion in the review (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The included studies varied in design: 22 studies were qualitative (Table 1), two were mixed-methods studies (Table 1 and 2), and 94 were quantitative (Table 2). The studies were conducted in 34 different countries, across six continents, with two being multicenter studies. Publication years ranged from 1996 to 2024, with more than a third of the studies published between 2020 and 2024. No study had a population of only T1D participants, 33 studies included a population of only T2D participants, 41 studies combined T1D and T2D participants, and 44 studies did not indicate the type of diabetes. Supplementary material 4 presents a stratification of the included studies by type of diabetes.

Aims of included studies

The investigation of DFD in relation to psychological or emotional aspects was diverse, as shown by the objectives of the included papers. The main objective of 74 studies was the direct relationship between a relevant psychological or emotional aspect and the population of interest. In contrast, the other studies had an indirect objective, where the psychological or emotional aspect in the population was not the main focus of the study but was still described.

Synthesis of qualitative studies

Nineteen studies collected data through individual semi-or-unstructured in-depth interviews, conducted face-to-face or via telephone. Two studies combined interviews with field notes, and three studies conducted focus groups. The studies employed a variety of data analysis methods. Thematic analysis was used in 16 studies, phenomenological methods in five studies and other uncategorized methods in five studies. A psychological or emotional theme was described in the results of the qualitative studies, however, not always as a distinct theme. Twenty studies had a results section explicitly including a psychological or emotional concept. These themes can be categorized into two main areas: general psychological and emotional impact (e.g. “Psychological” and/or “Emotional” “impact” [33, 34, 36, 52], “factors” [42], or “effects” [17, 47]); and more specific themes (e.g. “A loss of control and fear about the future” [51], “Experiencing discredited definitions of self” [49]).

Synthesis of quantitative studies

Twenty-five different psychological or emotional concepts were used in the 94 quantitative studies and one quantitative section of a mixed-methods study (Table 4). Depression (mentioned in 71 studies), and anxiety (mentioned in 45 studies) were the most used concepts. This was followed by illness perception (11 studies), perceived stress (8 studies), coping methods (7 studies), diabetes distress (6 studies), general wellbeing (4 studies), personality characteristics (3 studies), and body image (3 studies). Fear of complications, health status including psychological status, illness acceptance, psychological adjustment, general psychological characteristics, powerlessness and self-esteem were reported in two studies. Some concepts were only found in one study, such as fear of diabetic foot, fear of falling, health control, mood, resilience, self-compassion, self-destruction, self-harm, and stigma. Fifty-three different data collection tools were identified among the studies (Table 4). Some tools were used to assess several concepts, for example, the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was used to assess both depression and anxiety. The tools used most frequently were the HADS (32 studies), the Patient Health Questionnaire (11 studies) to assess depression, and the Illness Perception Questionnaire, used in ten studies. Assessment of depression was the most diverse in terms of the number of different measurement tools.

Discussion

This systematic scoping review maps and describes the existing literature on psychological and emotional aspects among people with DFD. The review highlights the diversity of research on the topic and how it has been studied. A total of 118 studies were included, with various study designs, data collection methods, analysis techniques, and populations. The majority of quantitative studies focused on depression or anxiety. Five other psychological and emotional concepts were frequently identified, while the others appear only in three or less studies. There was a high diversity in the tools used to assess the concepts. Qualitative studies were fewer, and all described negative experiences or perceptions in the population, with varying levels of specificity and detail.

The large proportion of studies focusing on depression and/or anxiety was also noted in the 2020 systematic review by Westby et al., which examined psychosocial and behavioral prognostic factors for DFU development and healing, focusing on longitudinal, case–control studies, and randomized controlled trials. While most included studies focused on behavioral factors, the psychosocial factors identified were exclusively depression, and anxiety [22]. This emphasis on depression was also highlighted in the 2019 narrative review by Vileikyte et al. This review primarily discussed the role of depression, and briefly mentioned psychological stress as well [19]. In the wider context of diabetes complications and more generally living with diabetes, depression is a common focus in research, with numerous studies addressing this topic [145, 146]. Over half of the 94 quantitative studies used a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to assess causal or directional associations between psychosocial factors and DFD. Among the longitudinal studies, most focused on depression or anxiety. However, a six-month survival analysis found that illness perception predicted DFU healing [114]; and a prospective study identified confrontation coping style as another predictor [132].

Over one third of the studies did not specify participants’ diabetes type, although this is relevant given potential differences in psychological and emotional characteristics between T1D and T2D. A 2022 systematic review estimated a depression prevalence of 22% in people with T1D and 19% in T2D [147]. Regarding diabetes distress, 36% of people with T2D experience significant distress [148], compared to 20–30% in TD1 [149]. No studies focusing specifically on a T1D population were identified, despite a reported DFU prevalence of 5.5% [150].

A 2022 scoping review on psychosocial aspects of adult-onset T1D also identified a wide range of outcome measures, with many only being measured in one study. Notably, concepts such as denial and confidence in managing diabetes were identified. The need to explore which outcomes and measures are relevant and useful to assess within its population of interest was also emphasized [151].

Although 25 psychological and emotional concepts were identified in this scoping review, more than half of the occurrences were related to depression or anxiety, which are well-established concepts in clinical settings. However, exploring other concepts may be relevant in the diabetic foot field. A 2024 study on psychosocial outcome preferences of people with diabetes and healthcare professionals found that “[they] agreed on the importance of regularly assessing psychological well-being, diabetes distress and diabetes-specific quality of life, while they regarded it as less important to monitor depression, anxiety, eating problems, social support and sexual health.” [152]. Additionally, a 2023 Delphi study on standardized person-reported outcomes recommended assessing general psychological wellbeing, including depression, anxiety, diabetes distress, but also stress and positive wellbeing [153]. Qualitative research further highlights that the lived experiences of people with diabetic foot disease, together with fears of developing further complications, are often central to their daily emotional responses, yet these dimensions are rarely captured in quantitative measures [18]. Thus, there is a need to further explore which psychological and emotional aspects are relevant to study within a diabetic foot population, and what measures are appropriate to capture them. Moreover, it is unclear whether the tools identified have been psychometrically tested in this population.

A systematic approach was employed throughout the review, with at least two people involved in each stage to ensure consistency and accuracy in the search strategy, study selection, and data extraction. A comprehensive database search using broad terms was conducted, enabling the capture of the topic’s diversity. In addition, backward citation searching was performed to enhance search sensitivity and minimize the risk of missing eligible studies, particularly given the wide terminology used to describe psychological and emotional concepts in diabetic foot disease. Various diabetic foot-related terms were used to encompass qualitative studies that may not provide a specific clinical definition of the population. No restrictions were applied to the date of publication, study design, or country. However, limitations should be noted. The search strategy may have missed relevant terms related to psychological and emotional aspects due to the exploratory nature of the research question, and the lack of precise definitions. The search was conducted on May 7th, 2024, and was not updated since. Therefore, more recent relevant publications may not have been captured. Due to the descriptive and mapping aims of this scoping review, no quality appraisal of the included studies was conducted. As a result, the methodological quality of individual studies may vary substantially, and the findings should not be used to inform clinical recommendations or to guide the use of specific tools. Conducting quality appraisal could be considered in future research to help identify and prioritize psychological and emotional research gaps in diabetic foot disease.

Depression and anxiety are important factors in the field of DFD, but it is also relevant to study other outcomes such as diabetes distress or positive psychological wellbeing, particularly with longitudinal designs. A well-described population is essential, as sociodemographic and clinical factors, such as type of diabetes, may have a role in both diabetic foot and psychological and emotional outcomes. Future studies should explore what psychological and emotional aspects are relevant in people with DFD and determine the appropriate measurement tools. This would enhance patient support and contribute to better prevention and management of the condition.

Conclusion

The scoping review identified 118 studies that explored psychological or emotional aspects within a diabetic foot population. It revealed significant diversity in the research on the topic, in terms of study designs, concepts investigated, and tools used. The results indicate a strong focus on quantitative designs, particularly depression and anxiety. The findings also highlight the limited research on other potentially relevant psychological and emotional concepts, such as diabetes distress, as well as the importance of employing more longitudinal designs. Improving clinical practices in DFD requires a deeper understanding of the psychological and emotional profiles of individuals, as well as the potential impact these may have on the prevention, progression, and management of the condition.
