Challenges faced and lessons learnt while trying to review CHD care in India from parents’ perspective
Authors: Suchismita Halder, Jonali Choudhury, Sajani Nair T.R.
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital heart disease (CHD) remains a major pediatric health challenge in India, yet the lived experiences of families navigating public schemes such as Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK) and Ayushman Bharat are largely absent from research and policy discussions. Motivated by wide variations in access, referral pathways, financial protection, and follow-up support across states, we attempted a parent-led, multi-state review to understand how these schemes function for individuals with CHD. This commentary reflects on that attempt and highlights gaps not captured in administrative datasets, which typically report aggregate counts rather than information meaningful to end users. As parents without institutional affiliation, we quickly encountered structural barriers that made even a low-risk qualitative study difficult to initiate. ICMR provides for “Independent ECs”, but independent researchers have no clear way to access or identify committees that can review qualitative, parent-led work. This limits practical feasibility. These challenges illustrate that community-initiated research is difficult to undertake without support or affiliation from researchers or clinicians within established institutions. Reflecting on this journey, we suggest that India needs dedicated mechanisms for community-led and parent- or patient-led research, clearer publishing guidelines, and national oversight structures (for policy implementation) that include patient and parent (or caregiver) representatives. Incorporating lived experience into CHD policymaking is essential for creating pathways that are realistic, equitable, and aligned with the everyday challenges faced by the CHD families.

Introduction

In India, nearly 200,000 babies are born annually with congenital heart defects (CHDs) [1]. Although survival has improved significantly, with almost 95% of children with CHD now reaching adulthood [2, 3], access to timely treatment continues to vary widely. For many families, this means travelling long distances and incurring considerable out-of-pocket expenses to secure appropriate care [4, 5]. Although public investment has reduced out-of-pocket health spending from 62.6% to 39.4% [6], and schemes like Ayushman Bharat (AB-PMJAY) [7], Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK) [8], and Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK) [9] have improved access for some, but implementation varies widely across states due to decentralized health governance [10], with differences in state-level capacity and priorities shaping access, information flow, and referral pathways. These differences directly influence whether families can access timely diagnosis and treatment, and whether public schemes translate into meaningful support.

Against this backdrop, we recognised that the lived experiences of parents navigating these programs remain largely undocumented. This commentary reflects on the motivation for undertaking a parent-led review across multiple states, highlighting gaps in publicly available data, the structural and ethical barriers to community-initiated research, and the need to integrate lived experience into health policy evaluation. Through this commentary, we aim to draw attention to the challenges faced by parent-, patient-, and community-led qualitative research, advocate for the inclusion of caregiver perspectives in policy planning and assessment, and encourage discussion on strengthening equity, transparency, and a more patient- and family-oriented approach within India’s health system.

Why we undertook a parent-led review

Despite systemic challenges, parent and patient perspectives are largely absent from scholarly and policy evaluations. Reporting for RBSK and Ayushman Bharat relies on administrative or hospital data, presenting only aggregate figures of children screened, identified, or treated, without specifying birth defects or CHD. Platforms like HMIS capture institutional indicators rather than condition-level detail [11]. Public RBSK updates focus on budgets and infrastructure, with no CHD-specific outcomes available (12–14]. Apart from isolated initiatives such as Kerala’s Hridyam program [15], West Bengal’s Sishu Saathi [16], and Assam’s Free Operations for Children with Congenital Heart Disease scheme [17], and a collaborative Tamil Nadu–Kerala study [18], India lacks a national dataset showing how many CHD cases are detected, referred, or treated. Only one study from an Ahmedabad urban primary health centre (UPHC) has explored patient experiences within the scheme [19].

The idea for a multistate review emerged organically from conversations within caregiver groups, where parents frequently shared divergent experiences with RBSK, PMJAY, and state programs or basic availability of standard infrastructure. Some families described seamless support, while others faced overwhelming logistical, financial, and bureaucratic hurdles. We felt compelled to document these experiences systematically, recognising that without lived-experience evidence, policy discussions risk becoming technocratic and detached from reality.

How we planned the review

Our initial objective was straightforward: to document parent and patient experiences with CHD treatment under public schemes across states and to understand how these programs function in practice from the family’s perspective. As three mothers of children living with CHD, we felt uniquely positioned to undertake this work. Our motivation came not only from our personal journeys but also from our longstanding involvement in advocacy groups, which kept us closely connected to diverse patient and parent communities.

We began by outlining a simple review framework informed by recurring themes in support group discussions. This included examining publicly available program documents, state health department portals, and government reports to understand how different states structure CHD services. To complement this review, we planned to gather perspectives from parents through structured conversations that could illuminate operational gaps such as referral delays, accessibility challenges, and uneven scheme awareness.

Our aim was never to generate statistical estimates but to synthesise insights that reflected ground realities. By bringing together program information and lived experience, we hoped to highlight variation in implementation, identify gaps that are invisible in administrative data, and offer evidence that could inform more responsive and equitable policy decisions.

Systemic and ethical barriers faced by parent-researchers

As we moved from planning to execution, we encountered several barriers that are rarely acknowledged in the context of public participation in research, particularly when the research is community-initiated and led.

Rationale for an independent parent-led study

Our decision to conduct this study independently was guided by practical needs as well as our own lived experiences as caregivers. As parents who have navigated CHD care pathways ourselves, we were uniquely positioned to create a non-hierarchical and safe environment that encouraged open dialogue. During initial informal conversations, families expressed that they felt more at ease sharing their experiences with peers rather than with clinicians, researchers, or representatives of formal institutions.

Maintaining independence enabled us to engage with other parents in a manner that felt natural, familiar, and free from the formalities often associated with institutional settings. Many participants spoke more openly because the conversations took place between peers with shared lived experiences rather than within a clinical or organisational framework. At the same time, we realised that conducting research without institutional affiliation raises unresolved questions about ethics approval, methodological rigour, and adherence to accepted standards, issues we discuss in the next section.

Ethical review constraints for parent-led research in India

This independence, however, placed us at the margins of India’s current ethics governance system. Under the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) National Ethical Guidelines, all biomedical and health-related research involving human participants, regardless of risk, discipline, or method, must undergo prior review by a duly constituted Ethics Committee (EC). This includes qualitative, policy, behavioral, and implementation studies, even when no clinical records, biological samples, or identifiable medical data are collected. Accordingly, our experiential, interview-based study falls under the category of human-participant health research and therefore requires ethics approval.

In India, most ethical committees (ECs) are housed within institutions such as hospitals, medical colleges, research centers, or non governmental organisations (NGOs), and they typically review only those projects affiliated with their host organisation. Although the ICMR guidelines allow for Independent Ethics Committees (Ind-ECs) for researchers without institutional ties, these entities must meet strict requirements, such as functioning as registered legal bodies, conducting continuing oversight, undertaking site visits, and maintaining access to study records [20].

In practice, the majority of Ind-ECs listed as “independent” are embedded within small private companies or clinical trial support organisations. Their primary mandate is industry-sponsored research, and they rarely engage with zero-funding, low-risk, community-based, qualitative studies. We are still exploring whether any Ind-ECs are both appropriately constituted and willing to review parent-led study in which both the parent-investigators and the families are spread across several states.

Together, these conditions illustrate a structural gap: although EC review is mandatory for all forms of health-related research, India’s ethics review pathways are not designed to support independent parent-led studies. This leaves such community-driven inquiries without a viable or accessible mechanism for ethical approval, despite their value in highlighting lived experiences and gaps in care.

Instrument validation constraints

Even though our semi-structured questionnaire is descriptive and collects no clinical identifiers, we recognized early on that question clarity, relevance, and flow critically influence the quality of participants’ responses [21, 22]. Developing a feasible plan required careful review of methodological literature and reflection on our unique constraints. Ideally, new instruments undergo expert review, pre-testing, and pilot phases to ensure content and construct validity.

For our independent, non-institutional team, formal psychometric validation is not feasible. After studying the literature and considering practical limitations, we identified a workable approach: seek feedback from volunteer experts (clinicians, social scientists, or experienced caregivers) to assess content relevance; conduct a small set of trial interviews with participants similar to our target population to refine wording and prompts; and, where possible, run a brief pilot to test logistics. All changes will be documented to enhance transparency and credibility. This plan represents the most viable way forward given our resources and context.

Emotional, practical, and perceptual barriers

Caregiving for a child with CHD involves ongoing medical appointments, close monitoring, and considerable emotional strain. Many caregivers also balance employment and household responsibilities. In planning our study, we were mindful that families should not feel overwhelmed or overburdened by participation. We therefore anticipated that interviews might need to be flexible, spread out over longer periods, or conducted across multiple short meetings with the same parent or family.

During our preliminary discussions about the idea of conducting interviews, caregivers raised concerns about how their experiences might be portrayed. There was apprehension that openly discussing systemic gaps could be misunderstood or lead to unintended repercussions from institutions. These perceptions made caregivers cautious about the prospect of participating in formal interviews. To address this, we planned clear assurances of confidentiality, anonymity, and removal of identifying details; however, this concern remained an important contextual barrier as we designed our research process.

Insights gathered from our journey so far

Taken together, these barriers reveal an unexpected reversal in our journey: while we initially set out to document the challenges that patients and families face when navigating the healthcare system, we now find ourselves working to overcome the structural, ethical, and methodological obstacles required simply to begin the research itself. This experience highlights the limited pathways available for independent, community-led research in India. Ensuring rigour, transparency, and participant protection therefore becomes not just a requirement, but a barrier to entry in itself. Below, we outline key areas that clinicians, policymakers, researchers, and patient–parent communities must collectively address to make such participatory research possible.

Creating an enabling environment for community-led research

Our journey underscored a critical gap: India lacks formal pathways for independent, community-initiated research. It is important to distinguish between patient and public involvement (PPI) within institutional research and truly community- or patient-led research. While existing frameworks increasingly encourage the inclusion of patients and caregivers as participants, advisors, or collaborators, they typically operate within institution-led structures where study design, ethics approval, and dissemination remain centrally controlled. In contrast, our work intended to initiate a caregiver-led inquiry, where autonomy, ownership, and agenda-setting rest with the community itself. Our experience highlights that current research and ethics systems provide limited pathways for such independent initiation, revealing a structural gap that extends beyond participation to questions of who is able to generate and lead knowledge in health research. Without institutional affiliation, parent-led or patient-led studies struggle to obtain ethical approval, conduct validation, or meet publishing requirements.

Why co-design is essential for CHD policy and care pathways

Co-design goes beyond gathering feedback, making patients and parents full partners in shaping health policies and services. In lifelong CHD care, families’ first-hand experiences of diagnosis, surgery, follow-up, and daily management reveal gaps often invisible to clinicians or administrators and help make care pathways more practical. Co-design also addresses whose voice counts, ensuring policies reflect lived realities. Studies from India, Nepal, and Sri Lanka on neurodevelopmental delays [23], and another from India and Nepal addressing multiple long-term conditions [24] show that stakeholder engagement improves relevance, accessibility, and acceptability. Earlier work in Jaipur highlighted that transdisciplinary approaches responsive to local cultural needs foster ownership, accountability, and trust, strengthening collaboration and enhancing the sustainability of co-produced interventions [25]. Similar approaches have been seen in other disease areas, such as pediatric oncology, where organizations like CanKids have developed structured patient-led initiatives that support engagement in research and care processes, including mechanisms for ethical oversight. Such pathways remain limited in the CHD context [26]. Together, these findings underscore that meaningful parent, patient, and community involvement is not only feasible but essential for developing CHD services that are practical, equitable, and sustainable over the lifespan.

Concluding reflections: lessons, gaps, and the imperative for parent-led research

Our preliminary understanding, based on lived experiences and background exploration that informed the design of this proposed study, suggests that publicly available information on CHD services is limited and often misses what matters most to families. Administrative data capture budgets and utilisation but rarely show how children access care, including registration, diagnostics, surgeries, follow-up, or duration of support. Families face inconsistent access, unclear processes, and ongoing financial and emotional burdens, while state-to-state variation in coverage and quality highlights the need for stronger national oversight and patient and parent representation in policy decisions.

As parent-researchers, we also encountered barriers in ethics review, tool validation, and research pathways that limit independent community-led inquiry. These challenges show how difficult it is for lived experience to inform policy, despite its practical relevance.

Our reflections underscore that parent and patient voices are essential to shaping CHD policy and care pathways. Families reveal gaps invisible to administrative reporting and provide insights into navigating fragmented systems. Conducting this parent-led study is therefore crucial to document experiences, inform equitable and transparent policies, and support interventions designed around the realities of lifelong care. Enabling structures in ethics, institutions, and policy are needed to support community-led research and integrate caregiver perspectives at every stage.

While grounded in the Indian context, these experiences may reflect broader structural patterns in settings where research and ethics systems are primarily designed for institution-led studies, potentially limiting pathways for community-initiated inquiry. This has implications for equity, as it shapes whose perspectives are ultimately represented in evidence generation and policy discussions.
