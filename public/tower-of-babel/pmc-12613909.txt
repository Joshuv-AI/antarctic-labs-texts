Enhancing continuing nursing education: a participatory action research approach in Iran
Authors: Hojat Mohsen, Karimyar Jahromi Mahdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContinuing nursing education is a crucial tool for advancing the nursing profession. Numerous studies have highlighted the suboptimal quality of continuing education programs. The purpose of this study was to identify the problems in continuing nursing education and develop solutions and implement them through active stakeholder engagement.MethodsThe Kemmis and Mactaggart action research model was implemented at Jahrom University of Medical Sciences in Iran from 2017 to 2020. For the quantitative part of the study, convenience sampling was employed, with a total of 200 participants, including 126 nurses, 44 head nurses, and 30 nursing trainers. For the qualitative part of the study, purposive sampling was used, with a total of 114 participants.Participants engaged in one cycle of reflection, and data were collected and analyzed using both qualitative and quantitative methods. Qualitative data were gathered from 14 semi-structured interviews and 10 focus group discussions. A Satisfaction Questionnaire and Quality Assessment of Continuing Nursing Education were utilized. Special techniques such as Train The Trainer, education booster doses, and evaluating outcomes (clinical indicators) of education were used during the action phase. Data analysis was performed using MAXQDA software version 8 and SPSS software version 16.ResultsThe initial qualitative analysis during the assessment phase identified three overarching themes: planning challenges, implementing challenges, and evaluation challenges. Similarly, the final phase revealed three refined themes: planning in recovery, fixed implementation, and improving the evaluation.The total average satisfaction score of participants in continuous nursing education programs significantly improved after the actions (Before action: 19.66 ± 3.96, during action: 27.12 ± 3.64, one month after action: 23.74 ± 3.7, P < 0.001). The total quality score of continuous education programs also increased significantly post-action (Before action: 97.55 ± 23.1, after action: 197.29 ± 13.55, P < 0.001).Clinical trial numberN/ASupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08195-3.Keywords: Educational measurement, Nursing, Continuing education, Needs assessment

Introduction

Continuing education (CE) is an essential function for developing the capabilities of human resources. Nurses constitute one of the most critical human resources in hospital settings, as their performance has a significant impact on hospital indicators(Faramarzi, Dehghani, & Hojat [1]). One of the best ways to improve nurses’ performance is through Continuing Nursing Education (CNE) (Raofi, Talaghani, Hojat, & Shahriari [2]). Research has shown that CNE leads to improved professional performance, reduced occupational hazards, decreased medical errors, fewer client complaints, and increased patient satisfaction (Badiyepeymaiejahromi, Faselehjahromi, Rahmanian, & Hojat [3]). Studies such as Behzadifar et al.‘s study in Iran and Mansour’s study in Bangladesh emphasize the important and effective role of continuing nursing education in increasing nurses’ satisfaction with these programs. Although CNE offers substantial benefits, it faces several persistent challenges [4]. Taxtsoglou, as Quoted by Heidenthal, it was stated; that, Neither organizations nor nurses are satisfied with the results of CNE programs [5]. One of the major barriers to CNE is a lack of time. Other barriers include staff shortages, inconvenient locations or times, fatigue, family or personal commitments, lack of interest in topics, negative past experiences, lack of program variety, poor planning, inappropriate teachers, and lack of administrative support [6]. Nalle, Wyatt, & Myers [7]. Tsirigoti [8]. Most studies suggest that CNE barriers are primarily organizational [9]. Dyck & Kim [9]. Hassanpour, Batmani, & Bolandhematan [10]. Raofi, Talaghani, Hojat, & Shahriari [11]). Research in countries such as Saudi Arabia, Malaysia, Thailand, China, and Iran has also confirmed the inadequacy of CNE programs [12]. Since the challenges of CNE are international in nature, failing to address them could have negative consequences for both patients and nurses. Therefore, this study utilized a participatory action research approach to identify and implement necessary changes.

The history of CE in Iran dates back to 1958-59, with CNE starting from 2003 to 2004. The activities considered acceptable as CNE in Iran are similar to those in most countries, and are associated with a clinical competency license. Each nurse is required to earn 15 points per year, which is equivalent to 25 h of CNE [13]. This action research was conducted from 2017 to 2020 (for 31 months) at Jahrom University of Medical sciences, located in south of Iran. Jahrom University of Medical Sciences has four general hospitals and two sub-specialized clinics that cover, more than 250,000 people. Each of these hospitals has general departments (emergency, surgery, medical, pediatric, maternity, OPD clinic) and specialized units (ICU, CCU, NICU, Dialysis, Thalassemia, Chemotherapy, Angiography). More than 700 nurses work in these hospitals. Initial studies have indicated a low level of satisfaction with CNE, and the effectiveness of the program has not been assessed. A review study also found that most of the issues were not unique to this university, but rather common throughout the country.

The objective of this study is to design, implement, and evaluate a continuing nursing education program based on a participatory action research approach. The aim is to enhance educational quality, increase nurse satisfaction, and improve clinical performance indicators within a hospital setting. By identifying existing challenges in the planning, execution, and evaluation phases of continuing education, and through active stakeholder engagement, the study seeks to develop and assess the effectiveness of targeted interventions in practice.

Methods

The participatory action research approach, based on the Kemmis and MacTaggart model, was utilized. This approach involves assessing the current situation, planning, taking action, and evaluating the results (Fine, Torre, Oswald, & Avory [14]. McTaggart, Nixon, & Kemmis [15]).

Quantitative methods

Trained facilitators conducted a cross-sectional descriptive study, which involved a Quality Assessment Questionnaire of CNE for the assessment and evaluation phases, along with qualitative research. The questionnaire was customized to address contextual issues, using insights from the qualitative study and a narrative review. Out of 135 items analyzed, 45 questions were chosen and organized into planning (8 questions), implementation (31 questions), and evaluation (6 questions) categories. In the psychometric testing phase, 200 participants completed the questionnaire. All invited participants filled out the questionnaire, resulting in a 100% response rate.

The exploratory factor analysis capability was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin index (KMO = 0.786) and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity (p = 0.001) (Fig. 1). The Varimax method was employed to examine the factor structure. Eigenvalues, percentage of variance, cumulative variance, and their validity coefficients were analyzed (Tables 1 and 2). With a KMO value of 0.786, it was determined that the sample size was sufficient and the tool could perform factor analysis at a significance level of p < 0.0001. Subsequently, items were grouped into three distinct factors based on their Eigenvalue. Any items with a factor loading below 0.4, those loading on multiple factors, or those placed on an unrelated factor were removed. This resulted in the exclusion of 6 items. Together, the three factors explained 78.91% of the total variance.

The questionnaire’s reliability was assessed through internal consistency (0.88) and test-retest methods (Pearson correlation coefficient = 0.736). The Content Validity Rate (CVR = 0.79), Content Validity Index (CVI = 0.88), and Face Validity Index (FVI = 1.92) were all calculated.

To evaluate the face validity of the questionnaire, 10 experts in the field of educational assessment rated each item based on clarity and relevance using a 4-point Likert scale. The FVI was determined using the formula: FVI = (Number of experts rating item as 3 or 4)/(Total number of experts). The average FVI across all items was 0.92, indicating excellent face validity. Each individual item received an FVI score above 0.80, meeting the acceptable threshold for content clarity and relevance.

For the calculation of CVI, experts used a 4-part Likert scale to determine the relevance of each item: unrelated, Need for a major overhaul, related but needs revision, totally relevant.

Confirmatory factor analysis confirmed acceptable construct validity (χ2 (df) = 941.64, p < 0.001, RMSEA = 0.071, TLI = 0.951, CFI = 0.906) as shown in Fig. 2. The Pearson inter-correlation test between the three subscales revealed values of planning (0.734), implementation (0.852), and evaluation (0.744) (Table 3). The internal consistency values were as follows: planning (0.744), implementation (0.889), and evaluation (0.924).

The questionnaire used a 5-point Likert scale (very low: 1, low: 2, moderate: 3, high: 4, very high: 5), where higher scores indicated a better situation in continuing education (CE). The range of scores was 45 to 225. To evaluate satisfaction with CE programs, we employed a questionnaire from the national CE system. The questionnaire consisted of 12 questions with a 3-point Likert scale. The range of scores was 12 to 36.

Data analysis

A proactive approach was taken in handling missing data. Attendance records, survey responses, and performance metrics were monitored regularly. Any incomplete entries were either followed up with participants or excluded based on predefined criteria. The number of missing data cases was minimal and did not have a significant impact on the integrity of the findings.

Qualitative data analysis

For qualitative data analysis, we utilized conventional content analysis as suggested by Graneheim and Lundman [16]. Graneheim & Lundman [16]. Lindgren, Lundman, & Graneheim [17]). The interviews were listened to and reviewed multiple times by facilitators after recording. Three facilitators then transcribed them immediately, identified key phrases, and assigned meaning units a code. Three transcripts were returned to participants for comments. To ensure the accuracy of the results, the coded data was assessed by five participants and two professors who are experts in qualitative data analysis. Data analysis was conducted using MAXQDA software version 8.

Quantitative data analysis

For quantitative data analysis, the Paired T-test and Repeated Measures analysis were conducted using SPSS software version 16.

Ethical considerations

The project was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jahrom University of Medical Sciences (IR.JUMS.REC.1393.102). Participants were informed about the objectives, methods, anonymity, recording of the interviews, confidentiality of data, and their right to exclude themselves from the study. The facilitator’s contact information was provided to the participants. Written and oral consent were collected in both the quantitative and qualitative parts of the study.

Validity

Participants were actively involved in every stage of the study, from assessment to reflection. Decisions were made by consulting with them and gathering their opinions. We utilized constant reflection in each phase, as well as triangulation, prolonged engagement, diverse experiences, and peer checking to enhance the validity of our study.

Generalizability

The facilitators made efforts to ensure transparency throughout all phases of the study. They attempted to clearly explain the methods, sampling, data gathering, analysis, and implementation. The results of the study were presented in multiple sessions at the Deputy of Nursing in the Ministry of Health in Iran.

Action research steps

Step one (assessment)

This step lasted for eight months. During this stage, the action research team, consisting of participants and facilitators, attempted to identify and understand the problems related to CNE, as well as determine their underlying causes. To achieve this, they utilized observation techniques, literature review, individual and group interviews (Tables 4 and 5), the satisfaction questionnaire of CNE programs (Table 6), and the Quality Assessment questionnaire of CNE programs (Table 7).

A total of 14 individual interviews and 10 FGDs were conducted with 114 stakeholders.

After analyzing 410 initial codes, 48 subcategories, 9 categories, and three themes were identified through the qualitative data analysis (Table 4).

The results of the qualitative research identified the causes of the issues and traced them back using the “Tree Structure Analysis,” “Why? Why?” and “Problem Analysis Flowchart” (PAF) models. Subsequently, the main causes were prioritized based on indicators such as importance, prevalence, solvability, cost, and urgency. These indicators and their respective importance grades were determined based on the perspectives of stakeholders and participants in the FGDs.

The prioritization process ensured that the selected indicators were not only theoretically sound but also practically applicable within the organizational context.

We utilized a weighted scoring method to prioritize the quality indicators. Each participant in this step assigned scores from 1 to 5 based on importance, prevalence, solvability, cost, and urgency. The final scores were averaged, and indicators with mean scores above 4.0 were chosen for inclusion.

Participants

For the qualitative part of the study, purposive sampling (n = 114) was conducted. All participants were involved in every phase of the study without any attrition. Eight participants held a Master of Science degree, while six had a Ph.D. in Nursing. Each participant actively engaged in all study phases based on their respective roles, with all communication conducted face-to-face. The mean age of participants was 35.67 ± 10.62 years, and the mean work experience was 15.13 ± 6.43 years. Four faculty members, including two assistant professors, a full professor, and an associate professor, participated as facilitators. Additionally, the university president, hospital managers, and clinical supervisors were involved in the project, providing support for the action research and assisting in the implementation and review of plans.

In the prioritization phase, a total of fifteen individuals participated: 8 nurses, 5 nursing managers, and 2 university continuing education managers.

Data collection

A male faculty member with a Ph.D. in nursing, an expert researcher in qualitative studies, conducted semi-structured interviews, Focus Group Discussions (FGDs), field notes, and participatory observation to gather data. Prior to the study, facilitators attended most CNE sessions in hospitals or at the university and recorded their observations. A total of 114 participants took part in fourteen semi-structured interviews and ten FGDs. Some interview questions focused on assessing the current state of CNE, identifying necessary changes, describing these changes, discussing effective implementation strategies, and sharing expectations for the future of CNE. Interviews were transcribed, coded, and listened to multiple times promptly. Interview sessions were scheduled 3–5 days in advance based on participants’ preferences, with confirmation calls made the day before. Individual interviews lasted an average of 80.50 ± 10.51 min, while group interviews lasted an average of 116.9 ± 8.33 min. Interviews continued until data saturation was reached.

Four interviews were repeated to ensure transparency. Facilitators made an effort to create homogeneous focus groups (FGD) based on education level and organizational position.

Efforts were made to ensure homogeneity within each FGD in terms of education level and organizational role. However, the overall sample across all FGDs included participants with diverse experiences, backgrounds, and perspectives. This balance allowed for both focused discussions within groups and broader triangulation across the dataset.

The performance reports of CNE, including costs, credits, hours, special programs, and duplicate programs, were reviewed to support the qualitative data. Performance reports for the past three years were collected by both researchers with the assistance of the university’s continuing education manager.

Step two (planning)

This stage lasted for six months. The outcome of this phase was a short-term action plan that was agreed upon operationally by all participants. After reviewing the results of the initial stage, the research team, participants, and policymakers agreed that change was necessary. Seven FGDs were also held with stakeholders to identify potential solutions. Iranian studies were reviewed. Participants were prioritized based on criteria such as cost, capability, context capacity, rationality, and durability of results. All solutions (35 actions) were presented to the university council of CNE by the facilitators based on prioritization. The CNE Council approved 26 actions (Table 5). The CNE Council was comprised of two researchers, five university nursing directors, the university continuing education officer, the university dean, the university vice chancellor for education, and five volunteer staff nurses. The council played an advisory and participatory role in this phase of the research effort.

The action plan, which included goals, the role of participants, timeline, monitoring, and evaluation, was written and approved by the participants in four FGDs. The plan was uploaded to a social media application (WhatsApp) to ensure that everyone had access to it. Base on stakeholders’ feedback, the plan underwent some changes.

Participants

Eighty four nurses participated in a (FGD). The average age was 33.17 ± 8.22, and the average work experience was 12.43 ± 4.22 years. Out of the participants, 70 were female, 80 had a bachelor’s degree in nursing, and 60 were staff nurses.

Data collection

Seven FGDs were conducted with a total of 84 participants. The average duration of the FGDs was 67.10 ± 8.50 min. The groups were attempted to be homogeneous in terms of experience and age. The meetings were held in person and formally. The interviewer acted as both a facilitator and guide. The questions used were the same as those in the individual interviews. Data analysis was also conducted using the Grenheim and Lundman method.

Step three (action)

The action research project lasted for 12 months. The approved plan was implemented gradually, with sixteen workshops held on educational principles, education planning, and educational evaluation using the Train The Trainer method. The focus during the empowerment stage was on educational supervisors in hospitals. At the conclusion, each educational supervisor conducted a course on needs assessment, implementation, and evaluation using the scientific method under the guidance of facilitators. In one hospital (Motahari), the Delphi method was utilized for needs assessment, identifying Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation training as a top educational priority. Subsequently, CPCR workshops were conducted and additional booster education sessions were provided.

The results of daily and weekly observations were presented to the facilitators, and plans were reviewed or amended accordingly. Additionally, each month, a meeting was held with the participants and stakeholders to review the implementation (continuous reflection). All participants were encouraged and supported by facilitators and managers.

Motahari Hospital is a public government hospital with over 50 years of experience. It has 192 active beds and approximately 100 nursing staff.

Participants

In this stage, 200 nurses participated. The mean age was 40.07 ± 10.92, and the mean work experience was 18.83 ± 6.55 years. Out of the participants, 170 were female and 180 had a bachelor’s degree in nursing. Additionally, 175 were staff nurses.

Data collection

Hospital indicators of Return Of Spontaneous Circulation (ROSC) were assessed three months before (group A) and three months after the CPCR workshops (group B).

Out of the 80 patients included in the study, 24 achieved ROSC, resulting in a rate of 30%. In group B (n = 40), ROSC was achieved by 16 patients (40%), while in group A (n = 40), ROSC was observed in 8 patients (20%). The odds ratio for ROSC in group B compared to group A was 2.67 (95% CI: 0.725–9.80), indicating a higher likelihood of ROSC, although the result did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.054). These findings align with previous studies suggesting that early intervention may enhance ROSC rates, but further research with larger sample sizes is necessary to confirm the effect.

Step Four (Evaluation).

This was the final phase of the first cycle, which lasted five months. Both qualitative and quantitative methods were used for evaluation.

Reflection took place during the action and at the end of the first cycle, focusing on reasons for actions, how actions were carried out, and the results. The experiences of participants were analyzed during the reflection process. Based on the results, participants and policymakers agreed that the second cycle of action must be implemented.

Participants

200 nurses participated in the quantitative stage. The mean age was 29.66 ± 5.12, and the mean work experience was 13.49 ± 3.15 years. 180 were female, and 200 had a bachelor’s degree in nursing. 195 were staff nurses.

88 nurses participated in 11 focus group discussions (qualitative stage). The mean age was 28.09 ± 4.82, and the mean work experience was 11.07 ± 5.65 years. 80 were female, and 85 had a bachelor’s degree in nursing. 88 were staff nurses.

Data collection

In the quantitative stage, the Satisfaction Questionnaire was completed by 200 stakeholders. The highest mean satisfaction was found in the CNE programs (Table 6).

During the qualitative stage, semi-structured group interviews were conducted. A total of 285 initial codes, 29 subcategories, 7 categories, and three themes were identified through the analysis of the qualitative data (Table 8). The questions remained consistent with the first round, and all steps, including the analysis method, were carried out similarly to the initial step. The average duration of interviews was 96.04 ± 05.71 min. Interviews continued until data saturation was reached for both the data and the researcher (Table 9).

Discussion

The aim is to enhance educational quality, increase nurse satisfaction, and improve clinical performance indicators within a hospital setting. The most crucial aspect of action research is its initial phase [13, 18]. In this study, qualitative and quantitative methods were used to determine CNE challenges. One of the key challenges identified was related to planning. Educational planning is essential for the success and sustainability of CNE. By addressing the challenges in educational planning, the CNE plan can effectively achieve its goals [6]. During the evaluation phase, it was noted that there was a shift from an inappropriate needs assessment to a more suitable one. Additionally, the overall satisfaction mean of stakeholders regarding the needs assessment showed improvement, which was deemed acceptable. One of the difficulties encountered in the quantitative and qualitative evaluation of the initial phase was associated with the needs assessment, although there was some improvement in this area. Ensuring that the needs assessment is conducted accurately will address other issues in educational planning, such as selecting empowered teachers, valid content, and suitable training locations and schedules throughout the curriculum process. The curriculum guides the teaching process, with educational planning being a specialized field [13]. Continuing education planning should be overseen by an education specialist or conducted by them [2]. In this study, principles of education were taught to continuing education experts using various techniques, resulting in improved e conditions for continuing education.

The challenges of planning and implementation accounted for the largest volume of codes and subcategories, indicating that existing programs were not running smoothly. Improving the quality of continuing education programs requires commitment from policymakers. Therefore, the (Train The Trainer) technique was used to ensure that continuing education managers had a sufficient mastery of theoretical issues in needs assessment, implementation, and evaluation, and were committed to acting according to their own words.

A study by Taxtsoglou et al. [5] demonstrated that lifelong learning programs in nursing are most effective when they align with nurses’ clinical needs and learning preferences. The research emphasized that nurse satisfaction is closely linked to participatory design and practical content. Findings revealed that interactive methods such as hands-on workshops and simulation-based training significantly enhance program effectiveness. Therefore, educational programs should be developed collaboratively with nurses and tailored to the realities of clinical practice [5].

In a study conducted by Behzadifar et al. [19] in Iran, the effectiveness of continuing nursing education was evaluated in terms of service quality and nurse satisfaction. The results indicated that programs designed collaboratively with hospital administrators and based on performance indicators led to higher levels of satisfaction among nurses. The study also emphasized the importance of continuous evaluation and feedback from participants. Implementing participatory models in the design and delivery of educational programs plays a significant role in improving quality [19].

Changes in behavior and speech of the participants were observed in this study. The observed changes varied in intensity and did not manifest simultaneously. Some of the more visible and prominent changes included moving from not accepting the problem to accepting and trying to solve it, from viewing education as a job to engaging in result-based educational activities, from considering training costs to viewing it as an investment, from cross-sectional CNE to process CNE, and finally from feeling helpless to confident in accepting educational responsibility at the university.

It should be noted that organizational change results from changing organizational behavior and individual behavior within the organization. Observed cases included a change in organizational climate from a neutral space to an applicant space, and from the disintegration of educational officials to integration. Initially, findings were based on the lack of motivation and unwillingness of the CNE, with managers not expecting any changes to occur. The separation of education from management was also a problem identified in the first phase of the study.

Throughout the implementation of this study, the roles of facilitator and observer were carried out in a dynamic and interactive manner. The facilitator had the responsibility of guiding sessions and creating a safe space for open participation, while also maintaining neutrality, encouraging critical thinking, and fostering self-reflection among participants. The observer played a crucial role in documenting interactions, responses, and decision-making processes, contributing to the analysis of organizational transformation. Experiencing these dual roles highlighted the importance of professional distancing, active listening, and refraining from direct intervention in group decisions. These elements are essential for maintaining research credibility and reinforcing collective ownership of the change process. Additionally, the facilitator’s personal reflections throughout the study, such as navigating resistance, communication challenges, and breakthrough moments, added depth to the analysis. They provided a more nuanced understanding of organizational dynamics.

During the feedback phase, issues were identified for the next cycle of the study. These included a lack of team training, training overload, lengthy training programs, and a shortage of training rounds in hospitals. Suggestions for the next study involve enhancing continuing education programs for other disciplines and improving the structure of continuing education.

In this study, qualitative data derived from FGDs, interviews, and participant reflections were analyzed alongside quantitative data related to performance indicators and satisfaction surveys. This complementary use of data enabled a deeper understanding of the impact of the implemented interventions. In particular, qualitative findings played a crucial role in explaining the observed changes in quantitative metrics. Such methodological integration not only enhanced the credibility of the results but also provided a multidimensional evidence base for practical recommendations.

Despite the efforts of all participants, some problems still persist. Some limitations and issues of this study include: the participants having multiple responsibilities, resistance stemming from fear of losing organizational position in CME, lack of financial resources, and hidden structures within education.

One limitation of this study is the lack of in-depth reflexive analysis regarding the roles of the facilitator and observer within the action research process. Although these roles contributed meaningfully to the design and implementation of improvement strategies, potential biases, power dynamics, and the positionality of the researcher in shaping data and interpreting findings were not fully explored. Future studies are encouraged to incorporate structured reflexive approaches to critically examine the researcher’s influence and assess its impact on the credibility and coherence of the results.

Another limitation of this study is that the same dataset was used for both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, which may affect the generalizability and robustness of the factor structure. Future research should consider using independent samples for CFA to validate the findings.

Conclusion

The present study, utilizing a participatory action research approach, successfully identified and analyzed the key challenges in continuing nursing education related to planning, implementation, and evaluation. By engaging stakeholders in a full cycle of action research, the study was able to develop and implement corrective actions that notably enhanced satisfaction and quality indicators in continuing education programs. Therefore, it can be concluded that designing and implementing continuing nursing education programs based on participation, accurate needs assessment, and continuous evaluation not only increases nurses’ satisfaction, but also helps improve hospital performance indicators.

This participatory model offers a replicable framework for improving the quality of continuing education across healthcare domains.
