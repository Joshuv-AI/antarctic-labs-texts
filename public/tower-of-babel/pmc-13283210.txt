Multi expert integrated algorithm for kidney biopsy triage
Authors: Hae-Ryong Yun, Nak-Hoon Son, Gyubok Lee, Hyung Woo Kim, Hyoungnae Kim, Tae Ik Chang, Jung Tak Park, Seung Hyeok Han, Shin-Wook Kang, Tae-Hyun Yoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clinical decision-making often exhibits substantial inter-physician variability when evaluating identical patient data, limiting the reliability of conventional one data–one outcome clinical decision support systems. We developed and validated a Multi Expert Integrated Algorithm (MEIA) designed to preserve and integrate diverse expert decision patterns for kidney biopsy triage. The study included 9598 patients across three cohorts, comprising a developmental cohort of 8228 patients and two external validation cohorts. Three board-certified nephrologists independently annotated biopsy decisions, and expert-specific machine learning models were trained using identical feature sets to replicate each physician’s labeling pattern. These models were integrated through a predefined majority voting framework. Individual models closely reproduced expert decisions in internal validation, while MEIA demonstrated strong performance (accuracy 95.3%, F1-score 84.4%). In external validation, MEIA achieved an AUC of 0.933, with significantly higher discrimination than Expert model C (P< 0.001) and comparable performance to Expert models A and B. SHAP analysis revealed heterogeneity in feature importance across experts. In a pathology-confirmed cohort, all MEIA-recommended cases demonstrated histopathological abnormalities. MEIA provides a structured framework for modeling expert variability; prospective validation is required to confirm clinical utility.

Introduction

In recent years, Clinical Decision Support Systems (CDSS) powered by artificial intelligence (AI) have drawn significant attention due to their potential to enhance clinical decision-making1,2. By analyzing large-scale patient data, these systems aim to improve diagnostic accuracy, optimize treatment strategies, and streamline clinical workflows3. However, despite their potential, medical AI technologies often face critical challenges in real-world settings, including performance degradation and limited generalizability4.

Traditionally, CDSS development has relied on supervised learning approaches that map input features to clinical outcomes based on statistical associations. While effective for large-scale pattern recognition, these models often fail to reflect the nuanced and context-dependent nature of physician decision-making. Many existing CDSS have been developed under a “one data–one decision” paradigm, assuming a single, uniform decision can be derived from a given clinical scenario5,6. In practice, however—particularly in complex or ambiguous cases—the same data may lead to multiple reasonable decisions depending on the physician’s training, experience, and clinical judgment. Rather than resolving such disagreement into a single latent label, which may obscure clinically meaningful differences in risk tolerance and judgment, preserving this variability may be clinically informative7–9.

Kidney biopsy exemplifies this inherent variability in clinical decision-making. Although it remains an essential diagnostic tool in nephrology10, the decision to perform a biopsy depends not only on objective clinical parameters but also on individual nephrologists’ interpretation of clinical context, risk–benefit assessment, and clinical experience. Consequently, different physicians may reach different conclusions when presented with identical patient information11–13.

To address this variability, we developed a Multi-Expert Integrated Algorithm (MEIA), a clinically oriented framework that preserves and integrates expert-specific decision patterns. We trained individual machine-learning models as expert-specific proxy models based on biopsy decisions from three experienced nephrologists and integrated their outputs within a unified framework. We evaluated MEIA using internal and external validation datasets to assess its ability to reflect expert-level decision patterns and assist in structured triage decision-making alongside clinical oversight in real-world settings.

Of the 8228 patients from Severance Main Hospital, nephrologists A, B, and C recommended biopsy in 1290 (15.6%), 1261 (15.3%), and 1106 (13.4%) cases, respectively. Despite generally similar clinical profiles of recommended patients (younger age, lower albumin, higher cholesterol, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio; Supplementary Table 1), unanimous agreement for biopsy across all three nephrologists was observed in only 614 cases (7.4%). The remaining cases were predominantly characterized by partial agreement (2:1 splits), indicating substantial inter-physician variability in clinical judgment. Pairwise agreement analysis further demonstrated heterogeneous concordance among experts, with high agreement between Experts A and B (κ = 0.93) but only moderate agreement involving Expert C (κ = 0.45–0.48). Detailed agreement statistics are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Performance of the individual algorithm

The conceptual development of MEIA is shown in Fig. 2. The performance of individual algorithms (Expert model A, Expert model B, Expert model C), trained separately on each nephrologist’s biopsy decisions, is summarized in Table 2. On the internal test set, all three models achieved perfect performance across all evaluated metrics (accuracy, PPV, NPV, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score: 100 [100–100]). This result reflects the ability of each model to closely replicate the labeling patterns of the corresponding nephrologist within the same source population, rather than indicating prediction of an objective clinical ground truth. To confirm the absence of data leakage, we verified that train-test partitioning was performed strictly at the patient level with no overlap, and that all model inputs were restricted to baseline clinical variables available at the time of the biopsy decision, with no downstream pathology or outcome data included. The substantial performance decrease observed upon external validation further corroborates the integrity of the internal evaluation, as data leakage would be expected to preserve artificially high performance across independent cohorts. SHAP analysis identified proteinuria-related features as top contributors across models, though their relative importance varied among nephrologists (Fig. 3), suggesting differences in individual clinical reasoning patterns. Validation of MEIA recommendations in a biopsy-confirmed cohort

The Severance Pathology dataset (n = 738) included 720 cases (97.6%) with pathological kidney disease and 18 cases (2.4%) with normal findings. Nephrotic syndrome was the most common diagnosis (n = 309, 41.9%), followed by glomerulonephritis (n = 168, 22.8%), other non-primary kidney diseases including diabetic kidney disease and hypertensive nephrosclerosis (n = 182, 24.7%), tubulo-interstitial disease (n = 53, 7.2%), and inherited kidney disease (n = 8, 1.1%) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Because all patients in this cohort underwent histological evaluation, this dataset was primarily used to assess the positive predictive value of MEIA. The algorithm achieved a PPV and specificity of 100%, indicating that all patients for whom MEIA recommended biopsy were subsequently confirmed to have pathological kidney disease (Supplementary Table 6). These results provide supplementary rule-in evidence that MEIA-recommended cases were consistently associated with underlying renal pathology. However, given the highly selected nature of this cohort—in which 97.6% of cases already had confirmed pathological findings—the reported PPV and specificity should be interpreted cautiously, and do not substitute for pre-biopsy discrimination performance evaluated in unselected clinical populations.

Discussion

This study developed and validated a MEIA-based CDSS for kidney biopsy that integrates expert-specific decision patterns. Unlike conventional approaches that aggregate multiple expert judgments into a single consensus, which may obscure clinically meaningful variability, MEIA preserves inter-physician differences in decision-making. By training individual machine-learning models on biopsy decisions from three experienced nephrologists, we characterized internally consistent, expert-specific labeling patterns. Integration of these models into a unified framework resulted in stable internal performance. MEIA demonstrated favorable discrimination performance compared with individual expert-based models in external validation.

Our findings, highlight a “one data–multiple decision” design principle in CDSS development, which assume that aggregating multiple expert opinions into a single label yields stable and generalized clinical recommendations. In our study, unanimous biopsy decisions among nephrologists observed in only approximately 7% of cases, indicating substantial inter-physician variability. This level of disagreement complicates the identification of consistent relationships between clinical inputs and decision outcomes, thereby challenging traditional single-label modeling strategies. Within a conventional supervised learning framework, such variability may be interpreted as label uncertainty, making it difficult for algorithms to learn stable decision boundaries8. As a result, single-outcome models may exhibit limited robustness when applied to clinically ambiguous cases. In this study, MEIA was designed to preserve expert-specific decision patterns by developing separate models for each nephrologist prior to integration, rather than collapsing expert judgments into a single aggregated outcome at the labeling stage. To contextualize the integration strategy and provide a clinically interpretable point of comparison, we additionally evaluated a conventional consensus-based baseline model using a simple majority voting rule, which reflects routine multidisciplinary decision-making in real-world clinical practice.

As shown in Supplementary Table 3, the consensus-based baseline model, trained on majority-vote labels using a single XGBoost classifier, demonstrated markedly lower sensitivity across both external validation cohorts, despite maintaining high specificity and positive predictive value. These findings suggest that early aggregation of expert decisions into a single consensus label may obscure clinically meaningful heterogeneity in expert judgment, thereby increasing the risk of false-negative predictions. In contrast, MEIA preserved expert-specific decision patterns prior to integration, resulting in substantially improved sensitivity while maintaining high positive predictive value and robust external performance. Although alternative ensemble strategies, such as weighted voting, stacking, or threshold optimization, may further enhance predictive performance, reliable estimation of expert-specific confidence weights was not feasible in the present retrospective setting, as independent ground truth for calibrating expert reliability was unavailable. Accordingly, we deliberately avoided introducing additional assumptions regarding expert weighting to prevent potential bias. Future studies incorporating prospective designs or external benchmarks of expert performance may enable more advanced integration strategies and further refine ensemble decision-making frameworks.

The MEIA approach addresses these limitations by adopting a “one data–multiple decision” design principle that preserves expert-specific decision patterns. This approach involves the development of separate models tailored to each expert’s judgment criteria, resulting in individualized algorithms that closely replicated expert labeling patterns in internal validation. These individualized models functioned as expert-specific proxy representations of each nephrologist’s decision process.The subsequent integration of these individual algorithms into a unified model maintained stable predictive performance and provided a structured representation of diverse clinical judgments observed in real-world practice.

To further examine expert-specific decision logic, SHAP analysis was used to characterize patterns of clinical reasoning underlying expert judgments14. Although core clinical variables such as proteinuria-related metrics consistently appeared influential across all models, the relative importance of other clinical factors varied substantially among experts. These results indicate that MEIA captures heterogeneity in clinical reasoning rather than reducing decisions into a single aggregated pattern. Such representation of expert variability may inform the design and implementation of future CDSS in settings characterized by complex and context-dependent clinical decision-making. Together, these findings underscore the importance of integrating global distributional analyses with local explanation methods to improve the clinical interpretability of machine-learning–based risk models. In this context, representative case-level SHAP visualizations illustrate how localized feature contributions can diverge across clinically plausible scenarios, thereby linking global expert-specific decision patterns with individualized model behavior. Consistently, the moderate inter-expert agreement observed in pairwise κ analyses further underscores the clinical reality that biopsy decision-making is inherently heterogeneous, highlighting the limitations of single-consensus labeling and reinforcing the need for decision-support systems, such as MEIA, that explicitly model expert-specific reasoning. In the context of existing machine learning applications in nephrology, prior studies have demonstrated the utility of XGBoost-based frameworks combined with SHAP interpretability for kidney-related clinical decision support. Li et al. demonstrated strong discriminative performance in predicting acute kidney disease risk and prognosis in critically ill elderly patients using XGBoost and LightGBM with SHAP-based feature attribution15. Yang et al. proposed an automated XGBoost-based framework using 3D-CT anatomical features to support surgical decision-making in renal cell carcinoma, validated across multicenter cohorts16. MEIA builds upon this foundation by extending XGBoost-based modeling to the multi-expert integration setting, explicitly addressing inter-physician variability as a core design consideration—a challenge not addressed in prior single-model approaches.

Despite these strengths, external validation—particularly with a biopsy-confirmed pathological dataset—revealed performance variations. MEIA demonstrated high PPV when recommending biopsy, whereas sensitivity varied across pathological subtypes and was lower for less common or subtle conditions, such as tubulo-interstitial and inherited kidney diseases. The detailed clinical review of discordant cases provided additional context for these findings (Supplementary Fig. 3). In false-positive cases, biopsy was often deferred because of patient-specific factors, including advanced age or patient preference, rather than clear algorithmic misclassification. The observed variation in subgroup-specific AUCs is clinically plausible and likely reflects differences in diagnostic complexity across patient strata. In particular, lower discrimination in older patients with reduced kidney function may reflect greater clinical ambiguity in biopsy decision-making, rather than intrinsic model instability. These results emphasize the importance of high-quality reference labeling, sufficiently diverse training datasets, and careful consideration of clinical context when developing and applying CDSS. At the same time, the observed performance differences in external cohorts reflect the inherent complexity and variability of real-world clinical practice.

Clinically, the implications of our findings extend beyond kidney biopsy decision making. The MEIA framework provides a generalizable approach for integrating diverse expert judgments across medical domains. For instance, in chronic disease management, such as type 2 diabetes, MEIA could support individualized treatment planning by incorporating opinions from multiple specialists. Similarly, in radiological interpretation, multi-expert integration may help reduce unnecessary follow-up examinations and non-diagnostic procedures. Potential applications may also include complex clinical settings, such as oncology treatment selection and surgical planning. In particular, MEIA may be relevant in primary care and resource-limited settings, where access to subspecialty expertise is limited. By providing structured representations of specialist decision patterns, such systems may support more timely and consistent clinical decision-making across diverse healthcare environments.

Nonetheless, there are several limitations of our study warrant consideration. First, while the SHAP analysis provided insights into feature importance, MEIA does not offer explicit causal or counterfactual explanations for individual clinical decisions. Future studies incorporating advanced explainable AI techniques such as counterfactual explanations or concept-based visualization may further improve model interpretability and clinical acceptability17,18. Second, the current integration strategy relies on simple majority voting and does not incorporate expert-specific confidence weighting or advanced ensemble methods such as stacking. Although this choice was motivated by clinical interpretability and the lack of independent reliability references, more sophisticated integration strategies may further improve performance and should be explored in future studies. Third, model recalibration was based on a conservative definition of biopsy indication focused primarily on primary glomerular diseases. In clinical practice, however, secondary glomerular and non-glomerular pathologies may also warrant biopsy, and this assumption may not fully reflect real-world decision-making. In addition, we performed an exploratory sensitivity analysis under a conservative clinical assumption regarding biopsy indication. Fourth, most validation data originated from a single healthcare system, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. In addition, the external validation cohorts exhibited imbalanced outcome distributions, which may affect the stability of certain performance metrics. In exploratory subgroup analyses stratified by age and baseline kidney function, the diagnostic performance of MEIA appeared broadly consistent across major subgroups; however, subgroup-specific estimates were limited by relatively small sample sizes and should be interpreted with caution. Further validation across diverse institutions and patient populations is therefore needed. Lastly, although this study provides retrospective evidence supporting MEIA’s feasibility, prospective studies and randomized clinical trials are required to evaluate its real-world effectiveness and clinical utility. To facilitate future clinical translation, we additionally provide a schematic implementation framework illustrating potential deployment architecture and workflow integration (Supplementary Fig. 4). Future studies should also incorporate comparisons against additional ensemble strategies with calibrated expert weighting, and head-to-head evaluation against existing clinical prediction models for kidney biopsy indication, in order to more rigorously establish the clinical utility and generalizability of the MEIA framework.

In conclusion, this study presents and evaluates the MEIA framework for developing clinical decision support systems that integrate diverse expert judgment patterns. By addressing inter-physician variability through a “one data–multiple decision” design principle, MEIA provides a structured approach for complementing physician judgment through integrated algorithmic decision support. This framework may contribute to the development of more transparent decision-support systems that reflect the complexity of real-world clinical practice, though prospective validation is needed before clinical deployment.

Methods

Ethical approval and data governance

This study was approved by the Yonsei University Health System (YUHS) Institutional Review Board (Approval No. 4-2022-1449). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the YUHS Institutional Review Board due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Study population and dataset partitioning

This retrospective study included 8228 patients who visited the nephrology outpatient clinic at Severance Main Hospital (2010–2018) due to proteinuria, hematuria, or unexplained renal dysfunction, after excluding 1369 patients with incomplete clinical information. Biopsy decisions made by board-certified nephrologists served as the ground truth labels. The dataset was randomly divided into two subsets, with 80% allocated for model development—including both training and calibration—and the remaining 20% reserved for internal validation, such that no patient contributed records to both subsets. Because kidney biopsy decisions often involve clinically ambiguous cases without a single objectively correct answer, classical multi-rater learning approaches aiming to infer a latent “true” label were not applied; instead, each expert’s annotations were modeled separately as internally consistent decision patterns.

External validation was performed using two additional datasets: 632 patients from Yongin Severance Hospital (2020–2023) with similar clinical indications, and 738 biopsy-confirmed cases from Severance Main Hospital (2020–2023) to assess concordance between algorithm recommendations and pathological diagnoses (glomerular, tubulo-interstitial, or inherited kidney diseases). The patient flow and dataset partitioning for algorithm development and validation are summarized in Fig. 1.

Expert annotation of biopsy decision

Biopsy necessity was independently assessed by three board-certified nephrologists, each with experience performing over 500 biopsies at tertiary hospitals in South Korea. To minimize bias, nephrologists were blinded to biopsy results and to each other’s assessments. All nephrologists reviewed identical clinical data (demographics, laboratory results) without cross-referencing, with provided variables summarized in Supplementary Table 7. Each nephrologist’s biopsy decision was recorded independently as binary labels: “do biopsy” (1) or “do not biopsy” (0), resulting in three separate expert labels per patient. This structured approach facilitated capturing inter-physician variability, establishing the foundation for a “one data–multiple decision” algorithm. The conceptual development process of the MEIA-based CDSS, incorporating expert-specific models, is illustrated in Fig. 2. Explicit modeling of differential expert reliability was not performed, as all participating nephrologists were board-certified specialists with comparable clinical experience and underwent standardized annotation procedures.

Development of individual machine learning algorithm

To capture the distinct decision-making patterns of each physician, we developed three separate supervised machine-learning models using each nephrologist’s labeled data combined with clinical variables from Severance Main Hospital. The Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm, an ensemble learning method based on decision trees, was selected for its proven robustness, computational efficiency, and regularization capabilities to mitigate overfitting19. Each model was trained to predict biopsy necessity (binary outcome), mapping clinical input features to expert decisions. All expert-specific models were trained using identical feature sets, architectures, and hyperparameter configurations to isolate variability arising from expert judgment rather than algorithmic differences. To enhance interpretability, Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) was used to identify and visualize the features most influential in each model’s predictions14. Technical details of the XGBoost model, including hyperparameter configurations, optimization procedures, cross-validation strategy, and SHAP analysis, are described below. Three expert-specific models were developed using XGBoost 1.7.5. All models shared identical feature sets and architecture to isolate variability attributable to expert labeling.

Overall, the system architecture of MEIA follows an input–learning objective–output framework. Model inputs consisted of baseline demographic and laboratory variables routinely used in clinical assessment, as summarized in Supplementary Table 7. For each expert-specific model, the learning objective was to predict the binary biopsy decision of the corresponding nephrologist (do biopsy vs. no biopsy) using supervised classification. Model outputs were probabilistic predictions that were subsequently converted into binary recommendations and integrated through majority voting to generate the final MEIA output.

Hyperparameter tuning and cross validation

Hyperparameters were optimized using stratified five-fold cross-validation at the patient level to prevent data leakage. To prevent data leakage, dataset partitioning was performed strictly at the patient level, ensuring that no patient contributed records to both the development and internal test sets. Model inputs were limited to baseline demographic and laboratory variables available prior to the biopsy decision. No pathological findings, biopsy outcomes, or post-decision clinical information were used at any stage of model development or internal evaluation. The objective was to maximize mean AUC across folds. Early stopping was applied to reduce overfitting. Tuned parameters included learning rate, maximum tree depth, number of boosting rounds, subsampling ratio, column subsampling ratio, and L1/L2 regularization. All expert-specific models shared identical architectures and hyperparameter configurations to ensure that performance differences reflected expert labeling rather than algorithmic variation. Final hyperparameter values are provided in Supplementary Table 8.

Explainability analysis

Model interpretability was evaluated using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values, a game-theoretic framework that quantifies the marginal contribution of each input feature to individual model predictions20. For each expert-specific proxy model and for MEIA, SHAP values were computed using the TreeExplainer algorithm, which is optimized for tree-based models such as XGBoost and enables exact and computationally efficient attribution of feature contributions. Global feature importance was summarized using mean absolute SHAP values across all predictions, and beeswarm plots were generated to visualize the direction and magnitude of each feature’s contribution across the full patient cohort. In addition, case-level SHAP waterfall plots were constructed for representative false-negative and false-positive cases to illustrate how individual feature contributions drove specific model decisions, providing mechanistic insight into misclassification patterns. All SHAP analyses were performed using the shap library (version 0.44.0) in Python 3.10.

Integration into multi-expert integrated algorithm

Individual model outputs were integrated into the MEIA using a consensus-based ensemble strategy. For each patient case, predictions from the three independently trained expert-specific models were aggregated into a unified binary decision. Specifically, the MEIA utilized a majority voting rule: a final ‘do biopsy’ recommendation (1) was generated only if two or more of the three expert-specific models predicted ‘do biopsy’9. By explicitly incorporating the variability in expert judgment, this integration was designed to generate balanced and clinically robust recommendations. MEIA’s predictive performance and its agreement with individual expert decisions were subsequently evaluated against individual models. Majority voting was adopted to ensure interpretability and clinical usability and to avoid introducing unstable expert-specific weights in the absence of an independent reliability reference.

Performance metrics and evaluation strategy

Model performance was evaluated using standard classification metrics derived from confusion matrices, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and F1-score. True positive (TP), false positive (FP), true negative (TN), and false negative (FN) cases were systematically recorded and analyzed to assess predictive accuracy against expert-labeled reference standards.

Statistical analysis

Model performance was summarized using descriptive statistics. The discriminative power of the models was compared using the DeLong test for the area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUCs)21. When multiple pairwise comparisons were performed, Bonferroni correction was applied to control for type I error. Differences in classification metrics (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and F1-score) between the MEIA and individual models were analyzed using Chi-square tests. All statistical comparisons were considered significant at a two-sided P< 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata, version 15.1 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX). Model development was performed in Python 3.10, using scikit-learn 1.5.1 and XGBoost 1.7.5.
