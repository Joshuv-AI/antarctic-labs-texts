Transcriptome predictors of second breast events in ductal carcinoma in situ: a case‒control study including Black and White women
Authors: Aditi Hazra, Emma Berdan, Thomas Walsh, Sarah Humble, Jen Tappenden, Katherine DeSchryver, Deborah Veis, Kiran Vij, Shannan Ho Sui, Graham A. Colditz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlack women diagnosed with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) experience disproportionately higher risk of second breast cancer events (SBEs), including both ipsilateral and contralateral recurrences, yet remain underrepresented in molecular biomarker studies compared to non-Hispanic White women.MethodsWe performed RNA sequencing on 200 archival DCIS samples from the Resource of Archival Breast Tissue cohort, including 33% self-reported Black women. We correlated transcriptomic and clinical data to identify predictors of SBEs. Cases (n = 100) who developed SBEs between 1999 and 2019 were matched 1:1 to controls (n = 100) on age, race, and margin status, who remained event-free during a comparable follow-up period in this observational study. RNA-seq was conducted using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, which yielded high-quality transcriptomic profiles (13,460 protein-coding genes) from 141 out of 200 samples. Differential gene expression and pathway analyses were used to identify molecular predictors of SBE risk.ResultsPrincipal component analysis demonstrated that transcriptomic variance was associated with race and follow-up duration. Four genes, CHGB, RBM20, SYP, and SYNJ2BP, were significantly associated with ipsilateral SBEs (FDR P value < 0.05). Interferon-alpha signaling was the most significantly enriched pathway in DCIS cases compared with controls. Gene signatures varied by recurrence site (e.g., contralateral SBEs) and covariates, including self-reported race. Our findings demonstrate the feasibility and value of RNA-seq from diverse archival DCIS specimens.ConclusionWe identified novel transcriptomic alterations associated with second breast cancer events in DCIS. Our results support the inclusion of racially diverse biospecimens in biomarker discovery. These findings warrant validation in independent cohorts. These molecular insights have the potential to refine risk prediction tools and inform equitable strategies for DCIS management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-026-02257-0.

Background

Management of ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), a nonobligate precursor to invasive breast cancer, presents a clinical challenge in balancing overtreatment with precision care for 56,500 U.S. women diagnosed annually [1, 2]. Black women with DCIS experience disproportionately higher rates of second ipsilateral and contralateral breast events [3], lymph node involvement, and breast cancer mortality compared to non-Hispanic White (NHW) women [4–8]. However, Black women remain underrepresented in precision oncology research.

Current clinicopathologic risk prediction models for DCIS [9] that incorporate pathology [10] and/or screening mammogram data show moderate predictive performance for DCIS outcomes (areas under the curve, AUCs: 0.63–0.79) [10–16]. Molecular biomarkers such as HER2 and Ki-67, along with commercial gene expression assays including the Oncotype DX DCIS Score and the PreludeDx DCISionRT biosignature, have shown prognostic potential [17–25]. However, these multigene signatures were predominantly validated in women of European ancestry, limiting their generalizability to more diverse populations.

For example, the OncotypeDx DCIS score, validated in a cohort comprising 97% NHW women diagnosed with low-risk DCIS (defined by size and nuclear grade according to the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) E5194: ≤ 2.5 cm low- or intermediate-grade DCIS or ≤ 1.0 cm high-grade DCIS), demonstrated independent prognostic potential for local recurrence in 327 patients [26]. A larger, population-based validation of 718 DCIS patients from multiple centers in Ontario confirmed its utility but noted the limited racial and ethnic diversity in existing studies [27]. The ECOG-ACRIN 4112 prospective trial, which included only 45 self-reported Black patients out of 316, supported the omission of radiation treatment for patients with low Oncotype DX DCIS scores. Similarly, the PreludeDx DCISionRT immunohistochemistry biosignature, combining seven proteins plus four clinicopathologic factors, was prognostic for ipsilateral invasive breast recurrence and radiation treatment (RT) benefit compared to clinicopathologic factors alone [28–31]. Women classified as low risk by this biosignature show no significant RT benefit [31], whereas HER2-positive patients (defined as HER2 3 +) identified through the 7-protein immunohistochemistry biosignature exhibited higher in-breast recurrence rates after breast-conserving surgery and RT [32]. The PREDICT study, which included 11% self-reported African American patients and 81% White DCIS patients, reported an increased likelihood of RT recommendations for African American patients (OR = 1.8). Despite these advances, both Oncotype DX (AUC values of ~ 0.65–0.70 for predicting local recurrence) and DCISionRT (AUC values of ~ 0.75–0.79 for SBE) show limited generalizability due to the underrepresentation of diverse populations. Furthermore, significant knowledge gaps remain in the understanding of the distribution of molecular risk profiles across racial and ethnic groups. These limitations demonstrate the need to discover and validate new DCIS biomarkers in racially and ethnically representative cohorts to improve DCIS [33] risk stratification and address systemic disparities in testing and access.

New technologies now facilitate unbiased biomarker discovery in DCIS [34, 35]. Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) [34, 35] has traditionally been limited by RNA degradation in archival, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue [36] with long-term follow-up outcomes [37–40]. Advances in RNA hybrid exome capture sequencing have improved accuracy, unlocking the use of archival samples. Recent studies have successfully identified gene signatures from FFPE DCIS samples [41–45]. Large consortia, such as the Human Tumor Atlas Network (HTAN) and the PRECISION Grand Challenge, have characterized transcriptomic signatures in DCIS tissue and their relationship with ipsilateral invasive events. These studies demonstrate feasibility but remain limited by sample composition, sequencing depth, and/or racial diversity.

HTAN analyzed 677 DCIS samples from 481 patients with a median follow-up of 7.1 years from the Translational Breast Cancer Research Consortium (TBCRC) 038 study and the Resource of Archival Breast Tissue (RAHBT) cohorts [19, 20, 46], identifying an 812-gene classifier predictive of ipsilateral SBEs within 5 years [19–21]. However, these archival samples exhibited limited sequencing depth, with only 1.11 million uniquely aligned reads using SMART-3SEQ [19, 20]. A European study of 143 women with DCIS used the Oncomine Immune Response Research Assay (395 genes) to identify predictors of SBE, including MAGEA10 overexpression and elevated levels of interferon alpha 17 (IFNA17) and CBLB levels [47].

The Grand Challenge PRECISION consortium examined clonal relationships in 24 DCIS samples and matched invasive recurrence samples in populations of European ancestry, reporting that 75% of invasive recurrences were clonally related to the index DCIS, while 18% of ipsilateral subsequent invasive cancers were new independent lineages [48–51]. However, biomarkers in pure DCIS tissue that predict SBEs in diverse populations remain unknown.

Disparities persist despite evidence that Black women with DCIS are more likely to have high-risk recurrence scores [52] and higher rates of breast cancer death [8]. Moreover, Black women diagnosed with breast cancer are less likely than eligible White women to receive biomarker testing [53] and report lower levels of informed decision-making regarding DCIS treatment (mean difference: −5.2; 95% CI −0.18–10.3) [54]. Accordingly, the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force has prioritized research on “biomarker patterns that confer greater risk” in Black women and prognostic “indicators to distinguish DCIS that is unlikely to progress to invasive breast cancer” [33].

To address these gaps, we conducted an observational case–control study, using hybrid capture RNA-seq of DCIS specimens from the racially diverse RAHBT cohort. Our study systematically investigates candidate genes, identifies novel transcripts, and pathways associated with ipsilateral and contralateral SBEs, and evaluates associations with clinical covariates [19].

The updated STEEP 2.0 criteria provide standardized definitions for all breast cancer endpoints [55]. Although the STEEP primarily focuses on invasive breast cancer, we applied these rigorous standards to our DCIS observational study by systematically capturing both ipsilateral and contralateral SBEs. Our precision health equity approach centers on patient-relevant outcomes and integrates emerging molecular [56] and genetic [57] evidence, advancing understanding of SBE biology and informing personalized risk stratification. By adhering to STEEP 2.0 criteria for comprehensive endpoint reporting [55] and incorporating a precision health equity framework, our study enhances the rigor and translational impact of DCIS biomarker discovery.

Materials and methods

Study design

Pipeline for archival DCIS specimen preparation

We conducted a retrospective observational case–control study of DCIS (International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) oncology code D05)in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

RAHBT study population

The Resource of Archival Breast Tissue (RAHBT) from the St. Louis Breast Tissue Repository is a cohort established by Dr. Allred and Dr. Colditz in 2008. Uniform coding of premalignant lesions ensures greater consistency and use of research. Follow-up through hospital records documented second breast events. The RAHBT study population included women aged 18 years and older with documented cases of premalignant breast disease (including carcinoma in situ). Consent for participation in molecular and gene expression studies was obtained. Institutional Review Board (IRB Protocol ID #: 201,707,090) approval for the RAHBT study was obtained from Washington University, St. Louis. IRB approval for the RNA sequencing study was obtained from Brigham and Women’s Hospital (Boston, Massachusetts, IRB Protocol 2022P002940).

Women were identified as eligible through seven primary sources: the Washington University School of Medicine Departmental databases (Surgery, Radiation Oncology, Pathology, and Radiology), the Siteman Oncology Services Database (local tumor registry), the St. Louis Breast Tissue Repository, and the Women’s Health Repository. We reviewed all the records, excluded women with cancer prior to qualifying premalignant lesions, and identified 1831 unique women with DCIS or DCIS and second recurrence. A common, real-world dataset with pathological details, risk factor data, treatment, and unique identifiers was created and used to follow these women for second breast lesions. A central pathology review confirmed 174 cases of DCIS with recurrent lesions.

Formal power calculations included specimens and data from RAHBT and Dana Farber Cancer Institute (DFCI IRB 93–085). However, DFCI specimens with outcomes, and covariate data were not accessible during the study period.

Cases and controls

For each case (with second ipsilateral or contralateral breast events), we matched one control who remained free from second breast events based on self-reported race, year of diagnosis (± 5 years), and age at diagnosis (± 5 years). For each DCIS diagnosis, we retrieved slides and blocks for macrosectioning and prepared them for laboratory processing. A total of 100 cases and 100 controls were identified. Breast pathology review was completed by Drs. DeSchryver, Veis, and Vij.

Cases were defined as women diagnosed with pure DCIS from 1999 to 2019 with second breast events (SBEs), including invasive breast cancer or ipsilateral or contralateral local DCIS events. Controls were defined as women diagnosed with pure DCIS from 1999 to 2019 without SBEs during a similar follow-up time and matched 1:1.

Case–control analyses are presented stratified by SBE location (ipsilateral and contralateral SBEs). The Standardized Definitions for Efficacy End Points (STEEP), recommend a composite endpoint of ipsilateral breast recurrence (IBR), encompassing subsequent invasive breast cancer or DCIS in the ipsilateral breast or chest wall, to standardize outcome reporting and facilitate cross-trial comparisons. The updated STEEP 2.0 criteria emphasize standardized reporting of all breast events to harmonize clinical trial endpoints [55]. Although STEEP focuses on invasive disease, our observational RNA sequencing study extends this rigor to DCIS by assessing both ipsilateral and contralateral events, thereby reflecting real-world patient concerns and mitigating technical bias.

Covariate analyses used the overall dataset of 200 women diagnosed with DCIS. Covariates age, body mass index, menopausal status, DCIS lesion grade, surgery type (lumpectomy or mastectomy), margin status, radiation therapy, hormone therapy, follow-up time from DCIS diagnosis, and self-reported race were evaluated [58, 59]. We use the descriptor “self-reported race” in accordance with the National Academies of Science, Engineering, and Medicine guidelines on using population descriptors in genetics and genomics research. Registry data on estrogen receptor status are presented in the Supplementary Table S2.

Methodologic comparison

The initial comparison of coring versus macrosectioning and RNA extraction methods were used to determine the optimal procedures for archival FFPE in situ tissue. We compared DCIS specimen preparation procedures (coring vs. macrosection), RNA extraction kits (Qiagen AllPrep vs. RNeasy), and sequencing technology (SMART-Seq vs. Total RNA vs. hybrid exome capture; Supplementary Materials). The normalized data were quantified as transcripts per million (TPM) values. On the basis of hybrid capture efficiency, we developed a data-driven pipeline to conduct macrosection, extraction using the Qiagen RNeasy Kit, and hybrid exome capture RNA sequencing for archival DCIS samples.

Specimen preparation and RNA extraction

We identified DCIS specimens collected in 1999 or later and conducted macrosection (also known as scraping) of 5 µm whole sections from unstained slides to enrich for the DCIS epithelial cells of interest (macrosectioning was conducted at Washington University School of Medicine Anatomic and Molecular Pathology Core (AMP) in St. Louis, MO or at Dana Farber/Harvard Cancer Center (DF/HCC) Specialized Histopathology Core in Boston, MA). RNA was extracted from DCIS macrosections using the Qiagen RNeasy FFPE Kits at the DF/HCC Genotyping & Genetics for Population Sciences Facility.

RNA quality control and library prep

The quality and quantity of purified total RNA from 200 DCIS specimens were evaluated using the Agilent Fragment Analyzer RNA assay. RNA samples with DV200 scores of 40% or greater (13 samples were excluded for failing the DV200 cutoff) were prepared using Roche Kapa RNA Hyper reagents without any prior enrichment or depletion according to the manufacturer’s protocol on a Beckman Coulter Biomek i7. Following library preparation, the samples were evaluated for quality and quantity by a Qubit fluorometer and an Agilent TapeStation 4400.

Hybrid capture RNA sequencing

Libraries were pooled in 8plex reactions and hybridized to Twist Exome 2.0 probes for target enrichment. Post capture library pools were quantified by a Qubit fluorometer and an Agilent TapeStation 4400.

Uniquely dual-indexed libraries were pooled in an equimolar ratio and shallowly sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to further evaluate library quality and pool balance. The final library pools were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform targeting more than 30 million paired end reads of 150 bp per library at the DFCI Molecular Biology Core Facilities.

Statistical analysis

Alignment and Quality control analysis

All sequencing reads were processed using the bcbio RNA-seq pipeline implemented in bcbio-nextgen v1.2.9-8eb78b7 (https://bcbio-nextgen.readthedocs.org/en/latest/). The reads were aligned to the GRCh38/hg38 genome version 1000 g-20150219_1, using STAR v2.6.1d [60]. We optimized the process by specifying that at least 40% of the reads must match to align. This allowed for shorter read lengths to align. Alignments were checked for evenness of coverage, rRNA content, genomic context of alignments (for example, alignments in known transcripts and introns), complexity, and other quality checks using a combination of FastQC, Qualimap [61]. MultiQC (https://github.com/ewels/MultiQC) and custom tools. Transcripts Per Million (TPM) measurements per isoform were generated by quasialignment using Salmon v1.6.0 [62] and transformed to counts per gene estimated by tximport [63]. Quantifying at the isoform level has been shown to produce more accurate results at the gene level.

Differential expression of genes in DCIS

Differential expression at the gene level was called using counts per gene estimated by tximport with DESeq2 v1.38.3 [64] (DEGreport: Report of DEG analysis. R package version 1.13.8.10.18129/B9.bioc.DEGreport). QC and differential expression analyses were performed via the R programming language v.4.2.2.

Candidate gene model for differential expression

Based on prior literature of ipsilateral recurrence, we selected 16 candidate genes for a targeted analysis. We tested the association of these genes with the risk of an ipsilateral SBE by restricting the analysis to cases who developed an ipsilateral event.

Discovery model for differential expression

The full model for 141 out of 200 DCIS specimens with high-quality data was defined as ~ Case/control status + Self-reported race + Age + Diagnosis follow-up time + Grade + Intronic rate + GC Percentage. Both nonclinical covariates (intronic rate and GC percentage) were included in the model because they were significantly correlated with principal components of transcriptomic variance and thus could be confounding factors. To remove noise, log2fold changes were shrunken using the apeglm method [65]. Statistical significance for all the models was determined using a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p value < 0.1. Volcano plots were created using EnhancedVolcano 1.16. To determine whether related genes presented differential expression trends, we used gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). We ranked our genes by log2-fold change and ran a GSEA enrichment test using the MSigDB hallmark and reactome gene sets [66–68]. Statistical significance was determined using a Benjamini‒Hochberg adjusted p value < 0.05. The study follows the STROBE guidelines.

CIBERSORTx analysis

We used CIBERSORT X (https://cibersort.stanford.edu; detailed methods in Supplementary Materials) to conduct immune cell deconvolution [69, 70].

Validation analysis using HTAN

We downloaded Bulk RNA Seq Assay files from the Human Tumor Atlas Network (HTAN) Duke University dataset (Strand et al. 2024 [71]). The primary objective was to validate our strongest differentially expressed genes in each self-reported racial group (Black versus NH White). We evaluated 216 DCIS patients in the TBCRC set of the HTAN Duke University dataset, comprising 66 self-reported Black women diagnosed with DCIS (ipsilateral events) using the DESeq2 package in R. TBCRC patients with missing race data (n = 23) were excluded from the analysis.

Results

Study population

We analyzed 200 DCIS patients, including 100 cases with SBEs and 100 matched controls. Cases and controls were matched for age at diagnosis (mean 55.8 years), race (33% Black, 67% non-Hispanic White), and margin status (93% negative) (Tables 1 and 2). A higher proportion of Grade III lesions was observed among cases (45% vs 38% controls). Surgical profiles (63–66% lumpectomy), with no significant differences in BMI (29.6) or follow-up duration (~ 145–151 months) between cases and controls, were comparable. No statistically significant differences were observed for immunohistochemistry-based ER-status or PR-status by self-reported race (Supplementary Materials, Table S2). High-quality sequencing metrics

We compared SMART-seq and hybrid capture RNA-seq to identify the optimal method for archival, FFPE tissue. Hybrid capture consistently detected a higher number of read counts and transcripts per million per sample (TPM, mean = 14,380). In addition, hybrid capture technology demonstrated greater on-target efficiency and improved detection of OncotypeDx score genes compared to SMART-seq (Supplementary Table S1).

Hybrid capture RNA sequencing

We performed hybrid capture RNA-seq on 200 DCIS specimens. Samples with low total RNA yields (n = 44) or DV200 scores (n = 13) were excluded. Of the 143 high-quality samples, 137 samples generated over 20 million total reads. Two samples with < 15 million total reads and < 16,000 genes detected were excluded from the analysis. The hybrid capture data were zero-inflated, i.e., many genes had zero values for large numbers of samples. We retained genes with no more than 25 zero counts across all samples. After filtering, approximately 10,000 genes were removed, providing 13,460 protein-coding genes for analysis.

Our differential gene expression analyses included 141 women diagnosed with DCIS and with RNA-seq data generated from tissue samples averaging 20 million reads. First, we evaluated the differential gene expression of candidate genes between patients and controls. Second, we identified four genes significantly associated with the risk of ipsilateral SBEs in a discovery model among cases and controls. We further stratified by case‒control analysis by SBE location (Table 2). Third, we tested a discovery model for differentially expressed genes associated with invasive second breast events in a case-only analysis. Finally, we evaluated differential expression by covariates in the well-annotated RAHBT population.

After quality control, we used principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering analyses to examine general patterns in our data and identify critical covariates. We subsequently used these results to build a model in DESeq2 encompassing all 141 samples.

PCA and covariate analysis of 13,460 protein-coding genes were conducted on the 141 DCIS samples with high-quality hybrid capture RNA-sequence data. PC1, representing 9.67% of the variation, was significantly correlated with self-reported race (Fig. 1). Significant correlations were also observed for PC3 (representing 4.14% of the variance) with follow-up time (Dx_FUtime) and grade. Several sequencing metrics were also correlated with the top PCs (Fig. 1). We included both the intronic rate and percent GC in our model to account for this variation. Discovery model among DCIS cases with ipsilateral second breast events and controls

We then conducted PCA in the ipsilateral case‒control subgroup and examined differential expression by ipsilateral case‒control status and adjusted for the following clinical covariates: total reads, percent GC, self-reported race, follow‒up time, and grade (modeled continuously) [59]. Our full ipsilateral case‒control discovery model was ~ Case–control status + Total Reads + Percentage GC (modeled as continuous covariates) + Self-reported race + Follow-up time + Grade. Case–control status and self-reported race were modeled as categorical covariates. We considered any gene with an adjusted P value < 0.05 to be significant.

Validation analysis using HTAN

To validate our findings across diverse DCIS populations, we analyzed bulk RNA-sequencing data from the HTAN Translational Breast Cancer Research Consortium (TBCRC) 038 cohort (Strand et al. 2024 [71]), focusing on 216 DCIS patients with self-reported race data (66 Black, 127 NH White; 23 excluded due to missing race metadata). The differential expression of LRRC37A2 was not statistically significant in the HTAN-TBCRC data (FDR p value = 0.69). Nevertheless, consistent with our RAHBT data, the expression of LRRC37A2 was lower among self-reported Black women than among NH White women diagnosed with DCIS in the TBCRC. As described above, SYNJ2BP was significantly associated with ipsilateral SBE risk in our case‒control analysis. We observed nonsignificant trends for SYNJ2BP-COX16 in HTAN-TBCRC patients (p value = 0.07 and FDR p value = 0.57).

Discussion

Our study, using a racially diverse cohort, identifies novel transcriptomic predictors of SBEs and demonstrates the role of interferon signaling in SBE risk. Recent large-scale studies by Rohan and colleagues did not find an association with clinicopathological variables or candidate immunohistochemistry biomarkers [79, 80]. Our study differs by employing an unbiased transcriptome-wide RNA-seq approach. Our methodological comparison demonstrated that hybrid capture RNA seq outperformed SMART-seq, evidenced by higher alignment rates and more consistent mRNA transcript distributions (lower variability) in archival, FFPE DCIS samples (Supplementary Materials). By implementing sample quality criteria such as a minimum RNA DV200 requirement of 40 and an RNA input of 50 ng for hybrid capture sequencing, we achieved robust gene expression profiles from diverse FFPE samples, supporting the feasibility of high-quality transcriptomic studies using archival tissue.

Case–control candidate gene analyses for ipsilateral SBEs (Table 3) did not distinguish SBE risk in this cohort, consistent with a recent large-scale study using protein biomarkers [80], emphasizing the need for more inclusive biomarker discovery.

Novel genes were associated with the risk of SBEs in women with DCIS

Our RNA-sequencing analyses of DCIS tissue elucidated novel transcripts associated with SBEs (Tables 4 and 5) and aligned with the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force call for research on “biomarker patterns that confer greater risk” in Black women and prognostic “indicators to distinguish DCIS” progression [33].

Transcriptomic analysis of 13,460 genes in DCIS specimens identified CHGB, SYP, RBM20, and SYNJ2BP as molecular drivers of ipsilateral SBEs in DCIS cases with 145 months of follow-up, offering potential new insights into DCIS biology. CHGB (chromogranin B), a neuroendocrine marker, is expressed at reduced levels in DCIS cases with ipsilateral SBE, potentially reflecting secretory signaling [81, 82]. Mouse models have shown that chronic stress promotes breast cancer proliferation through the ERK/CHGB pathway [83]. SYP encodes an integral membrane protein and is a tumor suppressor gene [84]. RBM20, previously identified in an 812-gene DCIS ipsilateral risk signature [20], regulates extracellular matrix gene splicing, suggesting RNA splicing dysregulation contributes to SBE risk. A study showed that SUMOylation of the SYNJ2BP-COX16 fusion protein promotes breast cancer progression by facilitating mitochondrial fission and thereby enhancing mitochondrial function. SYNJ2BP elucidates novel DCIS biology, with downregulation (log2FC = – 2.18) correlated with ipsilateral SBE risk, potentially through PTEN degradation [85], mitochondrial fission [85, 86], or AMPK-regulated tethering of Pink1 mRNA to mitochondria [87]. Studies showing increased expression of SYNJ2BP in malignant breast carcinoma provide mechanistic evidence for SYNJ2BP’s relationship with epithelial-mesenchymal transition and metastatic progression.

In DCIS cases with contralateral SBEs [56, 88] compared with DCIS controls, we identified significant differential expression of PEG10 and APC2. APC2, a tumor-suppressor in Wnt signaling, was downregulated, consistent with its role in inhibiting tumor progression [89, 90]. PEG10’s retrotransposon-like activity may drive genomic instability [91, 92].

Gene expression differs by covariates

In our novel covariate findings, LRRC37A2 was expressed at significantly lower levels in Black women with DCIS than in NHW women with DCIS (Fig. 13). LRRC37A2 (leucine-rich repeat containing 37 member A2, NCBI Gene: 474,170; HGNC ID: 32,404), located on 17q21.31, encodes a protein predicted to facilitate protein‒protein interactions and membrane function. Emerging evidence suggests that LRRC37A2 is associated with invasive breast cancer and racial disparities [93, 94], with lower expression correlating with worse survival in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) patients [94]. A transcriptome-wide association study found LRRC37A2 had a Z score of −1.8 (p value 3.7 × 10–2) in African ancestry participants from the Komen Tissue Bank, inversely correlated with invasive breast cancer risk [95]. Mendelian randomization analysis identified plasma protein levels of LRRC37A2, with higher levels linked to older age at menarche [93]. Furthermore, a neoantigen candidate study identified an NSFP1-LRRC37A2 fusion [96] as a potential immunogenic vaccine target. However, LRRC37A2 was not detected in the 812 gene classifier identified using SMART-3SEQ technology in the HTAN data [19, 71]. Our data suggest that LRRC37A2 may represent an early biomarker of breast cancer.

Another gene, arylamine N-acetyltransferase 1 (NAT1) gene, is a phase II metabolic enzyme that has been linked to breast cancer [97, 98]; this gene was associated with invasive second breast events but was underpowered in the subgroup analysis of invasive ipsilateral second breast events. NAT1 is upregulated in hormone receptor-positive breast cancer compared with hormone receptor-negative breast cancer, and studies suggest NAT1-overexpressing tumors may influence tamoxifen responsiveness [98].

Validation analysis using HTAN

We examined our strongest differentially expressed genes in the independent HTAN TBCRC dataset. Analysis of SYNJ2BP-COX16 in the HTAN-TBCRC dataset showed nonsignificant trends (p value = 0.07 and FDR p value = 0.57). Validation was limited by the use of different sequencing platforms. The trend in the TBCRC data and our association data on SYNJ2BP and breast cancer progression warrants further investigation in larger cohorts.

Pathway analysis

Pathway analysis, using MSigDB and Reactome, identified significant enrichment of interferon alpha (IFN-α) signaling in DCIS cases with ipsilateral SBE compared with controls (Figs. 6, 7). This result is consistent with recent findings in Black women with recurrent DCIS by Strand et al. (2024 [71]), using SMART-3SEQ RNA sequencing (2024 [71]). Notably, IFNA17 was upregulated in DCIS cases with invasive SBE in a study of immune-related genes [47]. Our Reactome data further demonstrated increased involvement of gene sets associated with IFNγ signaling [99]. Mechanistically, interferon alpha signaling modulates tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) effector functions and PD-L1 regulation in invasive triple-negative breast cancer [100]. DCIS cases with contralateral SBE compared with controls were enriched for cell cycle checkpoint pathways (Figs. 8, 9). Combined analysis of ipsilateral and contralateral case‒control datasets identified enrichment of the interleukin 6 (IL6)/JAK/STAT pathway (Hallmark Gene Set), the IL10 pathway (Reactome; Fig. 10) [101, 102], estrogen response, and PD-1 pathways. The IL6/JAK/STAT pathway is associated with SBEs among self-reported NH White women with DCIS [71]. The estrogen response pathways [71] (Hallmark Gene Set) and the programmed cell death 1 (PD-1, PDCD1) pathway were identified across all DCIS cases compared with controls. Strand et al. (2024 [71]) reported that the estrogen response pathway was enriched in White controls and Black cases with SBEs.These pathways suggest immune regulation and tumor-stromal interactions in SBE risk [103, 104].

Clinical evidence supports the tumor microenvironment in high-risk DCIS: intralesional PD-1 inhibition (pembrolizumab), inducing robust CD8 + T-cell responses [105] and PD-1/PD-L1 + tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in HER2 + DCIS predicting breast cancer mortality (OR 2.21; 95% C.I. 1.01–4.84) [106, 107]. Overall, these findings suggest that active immune surveillance in cases compared to controls. Future mechanistic and spatial transcriptomic studies in representative DCIS samples are needed to confirm these observations.

Our study has several strengths and limitations. A key strength is its emphasis on health equity, highlighted by the inclusion of a racially diverse cohort (33% Black women). Larger, representative studies [108] and inclusive study designs are needed to improve the performance of gene signatures across populations and advance precision management and shared decision making. Additional strengths include the use of hybrid capture sequencing, which provides high-quality transcript data for archival FFPE samples; a well-annotated cohort with 1:1 matched DCIS cases stratified for ipsilateral and contralateral SBE type, extended follow-up, and alignment with patient-centric rationales and with STEEP guidelines.

Limitations include the sample size for subgroup analyses, the absence of covariate data on reproductive exposures, including oral contraceptive and postmenopausal hormone replacement therapy use, and the use of a composite in situ and invasive SBE endpoint. Rohan et al. (2025) reported null associations between clinicopathological factors and risk of invasive cancer among women with DCIS (79). The Standardized Definitions for Efficacy End Points (STEEPs) recommend a composite endpoint of ipsilateral breast recurrence (IBR), defined as second invasive breast cancer or DCIS in the ipsilateral breast or on the chest wall [55]. Comparing the breast quadrant locations of the index DCIS and second IBR will help distinguish between recurrences within or outside of the tumor bed, providing clear insights into recurrence patterns. While we did not perform formal power calculations for subgroup analyses, the limitation in sample size comparing Black versus NHW women was partially addressed by validation using the HTAN TBCRC data. Subgroup analyses should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating. Future meta-analyses of high-quality bulk RNA-seq data, integration with trials like NSABP-43, along with DCIS specimen sharing, may enable validation of genes differentially expressed by self-reported racial groups and help clarify the contribution of covariate and treatment-related exposures.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate the relevance of including Black women and high-quality transcriptomic profiling in elucidating novel biology in DCIS. Larger, more representative studies are needed to validate these results and accelerate the development of inclusive, clinically actionable biomarkers that can guide personalized DCIS management.
