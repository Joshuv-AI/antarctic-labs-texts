Background

Palliative care has significantly improved in recent decades and is now an integral part of healthcare systems in many countries [1]. It focuses on providing care and support for people with a life-threatening condition or significant frailty, aiming to ensure the best possible quality of life for patients and relatives [2]. Timely identification of the palliative care phase allows people to benefit from appropriate palliative care over an extended period, sometimes lasting weeks, months or even years. For the general population, improvements in the organization and accessibility of palliative care have contributed to better outcomes [1, 2].

In contrast, people with intellectual disabilities (ID) continue to experience major disparities in both access to and the high-quality palliative care, both nationally and internationally. These barriers relate to limited education and training among professionals, which may result in difficulties in recognising symptoms and providing appropriate treatment. In addition, communication difficulties can hinder adequate symptom assessment, particularly when patients are unable to verbally express pain or distress [3–5]. Multiple studies highlighted the complexity of care for people with ID, emphasizing the significant disadvantages they experience regarding both access to and the quality of palliative care [3, 6–11].

In the Netherlands, the Quality Framework for palliative care (QF), outlines the standards for high-quality palliative care, addressing physical, psychological, social, and spiritual aspects of care, while also emphasizing the importance of research and care quality [2]. Additionally, a dedicated guideline for palliative care for people with intellectual disabilities exists and provides tailored recommendations for care provision in this population [12]. Nevertheless, the implementation of this guideline remains inconsistent, and systematic evidence on effective care delivery in this group is scarce [3].

In the Dutch context, a substantial proportion of people with ID receive lifelong support from care organizations across different domains [13]. In many cases, palliative care is delivered in residential ID care settings, where interdisciplinary teams, are responsible for identifying and addressing palliative care needs [14, 15]. While these teams often provide strong relational, psychosocial, and daily living support, formal training in palliative care may be limited, and structural involvement of specialized palliative care teams is often lacking. In addition, these settings are typically organised from a social care perspective, with less continuous on-site availability of healthcare professionals with a medical background [16–18].

Recognition of palliative care needs in people with ID is hampered by several factors. Symptoms may present atypically, communication options may be limited, and behavioural changes may be subtle or misinterpreted [19–21]. Diagnostic overshadowing, which may lead to missed or delayed recognition of underlying health problems, is a significant contributor to misdiagnosis and undertreatment [22, 23]. Staff members frequently rely on a tacit knowledge rather than clear clinical signs when identifying signs of deterioration [24]. Additionally, shared decision-making with family and legal representatives can be challenging, especially when preferences are difficult to interpret or communicate [25–27].

People with ID also show accelerated and atypical ageing. Multimorbidity and geriatric syndromes can occur in people with ID aged 50 years and older, corresponding to conditions typically seen in the general population aged 75–80 [25–27]. However, commonly used frailty measures and prognostic tools remain limited in this population, leading to either underestimation or overestimation of life expectancy and potential treatment effects [20, 26, 28].

Despite the growing recognition of these challenges, there is a lack of national or international studies that systematically examine how key symptoms develop and are managed in people with ID in the palliative phase, including critical decisions such as the initiation of palliative sedation [15, 21, 29, 30]. In the Netherlands, the national guideline stipulates that continuous palliative sedation is indicated when the patient has a life expectancy of less than two weeks and is suffering from refractory symptoms [31, 32]. How these decisions are made in people with ID remains unclear.

This study aims to gain insight into the occurrence, progression, and management of symptoms during the palliative phase in people with ID living in residential care settings. Using a mixed-methods design with repeated symptom measurements, the study explores how symptoms develop over time, how healthcare professionals recognise and interpret these symptoms, and how treatment decisions are made in practice.

By providing a detailed study protocol, this paper aims to inform future studies and contribute to the development of more evidence-based recommendations tailored to the needs of people with ID in palliative care settings. The study is guided by the following central research question: How do symptoms evolve over time and how do treatment strategies relate to symptom burden in people with ID in the palliative phase?

Methodology

Study design

The study will have a descriptive observational design with repeated measurements. A mixed-methods approach will be used to comprehensively answer the research questions, combining both quantitative and qualitative data [33]. The quantitative part involves regular symptom assessments using electronic questionnaires, while the qualitative part consists of semi-structured interviews with professionals to gain in-depth insight into decision-making and (considerations in) symptom management.

Data will be collected over a period of approximately 18 months. The feasibility and structure of the study design have been tested in a pilot study, which demonstrated the acceptability and feasibility of the procedures in daily care practice [34].

Study population and setting

A total of 50 people with ID living in residential care facilities and a hospice will be included, with at least six measurements per participant. Participants will be recruited from three Dutch care organizations, one organization also includes their hospice. While the organizations differ in size, the sample is not intended to be statistically representative of the entire population of people with ID receiving palliative care in the Netherlands.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for the quantitative part was determined using G*Power (version 3.1), based on repeated measures design with three time points. The calculation aimed to detect a small effect size (f = 0.2) on the primary outcome of symptom severity, as measured with the USD-z. Parameters were set at a statistical power of 0.80 and a significance level (α) of 0.10 [35]. The analysis indicated that a minimum of 34 respondents would be required to meet these statistical parameters under the assumption of complete data, defined as participants providing information at least six measurement points. However, given the likelihood of missing data in a real-world setting, a total of 50 respondents is deemed sufficient to ensure robust analyses and meet the study’s objectives.

For the qualitative part of the study, the goal is to achieve data saturation. Semi-structured interviews will be conducted with a purposive sample of professionals directly involved in the care of the selected participants. The sampling strategy aims to ensure variation in professional background (e.g., physicians, nurses, social workers), level of experience, and degree of involvement in end-of-life decision-making. The exact number of interviews will depend on the richness and variation of the data, and on whether additional interviews continue to yield new insights. Data saturation is reached when no new themes emerge from subsequent interviews. Based on prior research, this is expected to occur between 12 and 20 interviews [36, 37].

Involvement of professionals

This study includes a broad group of professionals who contribute to the palliative care of people with ID. Given the multidisciplinary nature of care in this context, the term “professionals” refers to both healthcare professionals (HCPs) and social workers (SW). These may include ID physicians, general practitioners, nurse practitioners, nurses, psychologists and social care professionals, among other professionals who are involved in symptom management, decision-making, and overall care coordination.

To ensure a wide range of relevant perspectives, we aim to include professionals from various disciplines across all three participating care organizations. Within each organization, we will collaborate with internal coordinators or managers to identify professionals who had a coordinating or overseeing role during the palliative care phase of included persons with ID. These professionals are expected to have been responsible for key decisions or to have closely followed the participant’s symptom progression and overall care process. During this selection process, we explicitly aim for a broad and relevant professional perspective, rather than relying solely on formal roles.

Since responsibilities in the care for people with ID are often interdisciplinary, this process allows us to flexibly identify the professional who can best represent the participant’s care and disease trajectory. To ensure diversity across cases, we will aim for a relatively equal distribution of interviews across the three organizations. The interviews will be limited to professionals. Although family members or legal representatives often play an essential role in decision-making, this study focuses on the perspectives of professionals involved in the delivery and coordination of care. Data collection

A pilot study conducted in 2023 in a Dutch hospice setting confirmed the feasibility of the data collection protocol, showing that the procedures yielded high-quality and complete data with minimal missing values. Although the pilot was performed in a specialized setting, the general positive experiences with the Utrecht Symptom Diary, caregiver version (USD-z) support its broader applicability [38]. The USD-z (English version) is included in Appendix A. The pilot data are not part of the current study due to minor adjustments in the protocol based on pilot findings.

In participating organizations where the palliative phase is not formally registered in electronic health records, professionals will be asked to identify participants based on indicators described in the Dutch guideline Palliative Care for People with Intellectual Disabilities [12]. These include frailty, comorbidities, frequent hospitalizations, and professional intuition. Instruments such as the Surprise Question (SQ) or the PALLI checklist may support this process but are not consistently implemented across organizations. Therefore, identification will be done by a healthcare professional directly involved in the participants care. After identification, the potential participant or his/her legal representative will be approached for informed consent.

Qualitative data collection

Semi-structured interviews will be conducted with a professional after the final questionnaire is completed. The interviews will be used to elaborate on the provided end-of-life-data-questionnaire, on symptom management approaches during the whole study period (indications for treatment and care and their effectiveness), decision-making and use of palliative sedation from the perspective of caregivers. For participants who are still alive at the end of the data collection period, professionals will also be invited for an interview to reflect on the care process, symptom management, and treatment considerations during the observation period.

A semi-structured interview, based on predefined themes and guiding questions, will be used to ensure that all relevant aspects are addressed while allowing flexibility. The full interview guide is included in Appendix B.

Associations between symptom burden and interventionsTo examine how treatments relate to symptom severity (sub-questions 3 and 5), we will perform repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Handling missing data

Missing data will be systematically examined for both extent and pattern. Where applicable, Little’s MCAR test will be used to assess whether missing data are completely at random. Given the relatively small sample size (N ≈ 50), multiple imputation (e.g., predictive mean matching) will only be considered if data are missing at random and the proportion is limited. In such cases, results from both complete case and imputed datasets will be compared [41]. If the missing data appear to be non-random or substantial, alternative strategies will be explored with caution, and sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of the findings.

Qualitative data management and analysis

Sub-questions 2, 4, and 5 will be addressed through thematic analysis of interviews. This study follows the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) guidelines to ensure transparency and rigor in the description of the data collection and analysis [42].

All interviews will be audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Thematic analysis will be applied to the transcripts using Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework [43]. This process includes familiarization with the data, generating initial codes, clustering codes into themes, reviewing and refining themes, and defining them to capture their essence. Findings will be presented narratively. If relevant, a graphical representation of relationships between themes will be included (e.g., thematic maps).

Coding will be facilitated using ATLAS.ti software. To enhance reliability, a peer-review process will be implemented: a second researcher will review a subset of the coded transcripts to assess consistency. Any discrepancies in coding or theme development will be discussed, and if necessary, a third researcher will mediate to reach consensus [44].

The primary researcher will conduct all interviews and lead the data analysis. If additional researchers become involved in the project, their roles in data collection and validation will be explicitly defined.

Combined analysis

The mixed-methods design of this study was informed by the framework of Venkatesh et al. [45], which outlines 14 key properties of mixed-methods research. A sequential design was chosen, with quantitative symptom tracking followed by qualitative interviews to explore underlying care practices and decision-making. This approach enables complementary insights, where quantitative trends are enriched and contextualized by qualitative narratives. While the study begins with a quantitative phase, analytic depth is achieved through the qualitative strand, allowing for a deeper understanding of decision making and system-level dynamics within palliative care for people with ID [45].

Data from both methodologies will be triangulated to provide a comprehensive understanding of symptom management, treatment patterns, and outcomes in the palliative care timeline for people with ID.

Discussion

Awareness of the importance of high-quality palliative care for people with ID is growing, both in research and policy. Yet despite this increasing attention, people with ID still face persistent inequities in access to appropriate end-of-life care. These differences arise from challenges specific to this population, such as atypical symptom expression, multimorbidity, and limitations in verbal communication, which complicate timely diagnosis, symptom recognition, and appropriate treatment planning during the palliative phase [46, 47].

In many healthcare systems, including the Netherlands, palliative care for people with ID is not yet fully integrated into existing care structures. A substantial proportion of people with ID live in residential care settings, where multidisciplinary teams are responsible for providing daily support as well as identifying emerging palliative care needs. While these systemic challenges point to the need for improved integration and staff training, they also underscore a more fundamental gap: the lack of evidence based knowledge on how symptoms develop, how treatment decisions are made in practice, and how effective those treatments are in reducing symptom burden for people with ID in the palliative phase [3, 48, 49]. This study aims to address that gap by generating empirical evidence on the course of key symptoms, the timing and type of treatments provided, and how palliative sedation is applied in practice.

Strengths

A key strength of this study is the collaboration with care organizations for people with ID (Prisma, Prinsenstichting, and Ipse de Bruggen). These organizations provide long-term residential and care to a wide range of people with ID across different regions in the Netherlands, which supports the contextual relevance and potential generalisability of the findings within the Dutch care setting.

Another methodological strength lies in the study’s prospective, longitudinal design. By following participants across the different phases of the palliative lifespan and collecting repeated symptom data using the USD-z, the study provides insight into how symptoms develop over time, including during the terminal and dying phases. The structured monitoring protocol allows for detailed tracking of symptom progression and care interventions, including the timing and nature of decisions such as the initiation of palliative sedation.

In addition, the mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative data with qualitative input from healthcare professionals, enables a nuanced understanding of how care is delivered in everyday practice. This includes exploring the timeliness and perceived appropriateness of symptom management, in relation to clinical guidelines and professional reflections. These insights may help identify both good practices and areas for improvement in the provision of palliative care for people with ID.

The study also makes use of real-world clinical data. This enhances the external validity. The structure of repeated symptom monitoring, in as well the palliative, terminal, and dying phases, enables detailed insight into symptom progression and care needs. Importantly, data collection imposes no direct burden on participants, which is especially relevant given their vulnerability and the complexity of care needs in the final stages of life.

This study addresses a gap in the palliative care literature by focusing specifically on people with ID, a group whose needs are underrepresented in empirical research. By documenting symptom progression and care responses in real-world settings, the study contributes to an evidence base that may inform tailored guidance for both generalist and specialist care contexts.

Challenges and limitations

A potential challenge in this study is ensuring that the professionals complete the symptom assessments (USD-z) in a timely manner. Since the data collection relies on professionals’ ‘input, delayed or missing entries could impact the completeness of the dataset. Additionally, accurate documentation of pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments in the electronic patient record (EPR) is essential for evaluating symptom management. Variability in documentation practices across different care organizations may pose a challenge in obtaining consistent data, which could affect the comparability and interpretation of treatment decisions [4].

The observational nature of the study also brings certain limitations. Participation and data quality depend on healthcare providers’ willingness and capacity to document symptoms and treatments consistently. Missing data may therefore limit the robustness of findings. In addition, the absence of standardized, widely used criteria for distinguishing between different palliative phases in people with ID may lead to variability in how transitions, such as from the palliative to the terminal phase, are identified and acted upon. This variability could affect both care decisions and data collection timing [50], and it underscores the need for more operationalized definitions of palliative phases in people with ID. This issue is also reflected in recent qualitative work in ID-care, where professionals reported uncertainty in recognizing transitions from one stage to another due to the complexity of symptom expression and overlapping phases [51]. As such, better guidance is needed to support consistent and person-centred care across settings.

Another limitation relates to differences between care settings. While the initial pilot study was conducted in a specialized hospice environment, the current study includes a general care setting with a hospice and two general care settings. This expansion increases the ecological validity of the research but may also introduce practical challenges. For example, staff in general care settings may have less expertise in palliative care, which could affect the implementation of the structured study protocol.

Finally, the study highlights a tension between best practices in clinical care and practical considerations in research. While early identification of the palliative phase is recommended to enable proactive care planning and symptom management, not all people with ID in a palliative care trajectory show predictable progressions. For some, the palliative phase may last for months or even years without clear signs of deterioration. In such cases, frequent monitoring can become burdensome for professionals. This observation calls for further reflection on how to balance clinical needs with research feasibility in longitudinal studies in this population.

Declarations

Ethics approval and consent to participate

According to Dutch law (Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act, WMO), this study is not subject to extended ethical review, as participants are not exposed to medical interventions and they are also not subordinated to behavioural rules.

However, the study was reviewed and approved as non-WMO-study by the ethics committee of Rotterdam University of Applied Sciences (approval number 20240702). All participants will receive written information about the study’s objectives, the nature of their participation, and their rights, including the ability to withdraw at any time without consequences. This information will be distributed via the participating care organizations. The researcher will not be in direct contact with the participant.

Before participation, informed consent will be obtained from either the participant (if capable to decide) and/or their legal representative, in accordance with ethical guidelines. By signing the consent form, the participants and/or their representatives confirm that:

1. They agree that symptom questionnaires (USD-z) will be completed at regular intervals (monthly in the palliative phase, weekly in the terminal phase, and daily in the dying phase) until either the participant's death or the termination of data collection;

2. They understand the information provided and have had the opportunity to ask questions;

3. They are informed that the study is observational in nature and is not intended to alter treatment or care. Participation involves only symptom monitoring, interviews with professionals, and analysis of medical records;

4. In case the legal representative provides consent, (s)he did so on behalf of the participant but does not personally participate in the study.

All collected data will be pseudonymised. Participants’ names and identifying details will be replaced with a unique code, which will be securely stored in an encrypted database. The password-protected file will be accessible only by the research team, ensuring compliance with General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) standards. In all future publications and reports, only these codes will be used to protect participants' anonymity and privacy.
