A meta‐refined human Alzheimer's disease‐associated gene subset shows partial mouse‐model pathway correspondence and limited cross‐cohort machine‐learning transportability
Authors: Linsong Chai, Yunshi Huang, Jinglei Ni, Shuang Zuo, Jia Huang, Bingbing Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONCommon Alzheimer's disease (AD) mouse models are widely used, but their molecular correspondence with human AD remains uncertain.METHODSWe analyzed 15 post mortem human brain datasets, four human non‐brain or in vitro sensitivity datasets, and nine AD‐related mouse‐model molecular‐profiling datasets; GSE222494 was analyzed separately for single‐nucleus localization. Refined107, a meta‐refined 107‐gene human AD‐associated gene subset, was evaluated using recurrence‐matched control sampling, independent pathway‐level comparison, within cohort out‐of‐fold (OOF) classification, and ordered cross‐cohort transportability.RESULTSRefined107 comprised 37 Tier 1 and 70 Tier 2 genes and showed greater statistical consistency than recurrence‐matched controls. Functional annotation implicated inflammatory, blood–brain barrier, metabolic, neuronal, and developmental programs. Among 636 recurrent human pathways, 226 showed same‐direction support in at least one evaluated mouse‐model family, indicating partial and model‐dependent human–mouse correspondence. Post‐selection within‐cohort OOF areas under the curve (AUCs) were 0.833 to 0.980, whereas ordered cross‐cohort AUCs were 0.288 to 0.720.DISCUSSIONRefined107 represents a statistically consistent, meta‐refined human AD‐associated gene subset rather than a comprehensive disease signature. Human–mouse pathway correspondence was partial and model‐dependent, and within‐cohort classification did not translate into robust cross‐cohort transportability.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, cross‐cohort transportability, cross‐species transcriptomics, mouse models, pathway correspondence

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia worldwide and is characterized by progressive cognitive decline, amyloid beta (Aβ) deposition, tau pathology, neurodegeneration, and complex inflammatory and vascular alterations. 1 , 2 , 3 Despite major advances in defining AD biology, translation from preclinical discovery to effective disease‐modifying therapy remains challenging. Many therapeutic strategies that showed promise in experimental systems have failed to produce comparable clinical benefit in human trials, underscoring a persistent gap between model‐based discovery and human disease biology. 4

Transgenic mouse models have been central to AD research. Amyloid‐focused models, tauopathy models, and combined models such as amyloid precursor protein/presenilin 1 (APP/PS1), 5xFAD, and 3xTg‐AD have enabled mechanistic studies of amyloid deposition, tau pathology, synaptic dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and neurodegeneration. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 However, these models are built around selected genetic drivers and reproduce only part of the human AD spectrum. Human AD arises in a broader biological context that includes aging, polygenic risk, vascular and immune alterations, cell‐state changes, and heterogeneous regional vulnerability. As a result, molecular programs that are prominent in human AD may be incompletely represented in mouse models. 5 , 6 , 11

Public molecular‐profiling and transcriptomic datasets provide an opportunity to evaluate this translational gap directly. Prior studies profiled AD‐related gene expression changes in human brain tissue and in mouse models, but systematic cross‐species analyses remain limited by differences in tissue source, platform, disease stage, gene annotation, and cohort structure. 11 , 12 In particular, non‐brain or in vitro datasets can introduce tissue‐origin confounding, and single‐cohort differential expression results may not distinguish robust human disease‐state signals from dataset‐specific effects. A brain‐prioritized, multi‐cohort framework is therefore needed to identify human AD‐associated gene subsets and assess how well they are represented in mouse models.

Machine‐learning approaches can further test whether such gene subsets capture disease‐state information within human cohorts. 13 However, high within‐cohort predictive performance does not necessarily imply cross‐cohort transportability. 13 Cohort, platform, and brain‐region differences can substantially influence transcriptomic profiles and limit cross‐cohort performance. 14 , 15 Thus, within‐cohort out‐of‐fold (OOF) classification should be interpreted together with transportability testing and cohort‐shift analysis, especially when evaluating signatures derived from heterogeneous public datasets. 13 , 14

Here, we performed a brain‐prioritized cross‐species transcriptomic analysis integrating 15 post mortem human brain datasets and nine AD‐related mouse‐model datasets. Using effect‐size meta‐analysis, we derived Refined107, a meta‐refined 107‐gene human AD‐associated subset, and evaluated its statistical consistency, biological annotation, pathway‐level correspondence with mouse models, within‐cohort classification performance, cross‐cohort transportability, and single‐nucleus localization. We hypothesized that Refined107 would show reproducible support across human brain cohorts, partial and model‐dependent correspondence in mouse models, and measurable within‐cohort disease‐state signal but limited cross‐cohort transportability.

Data processing and statistical analyses were conducted in R and Python, as appropriate. GEO metadata and expression matrices were curated using GEOquery and custom scripts. 17 Samples were classified according to the original study annotations, and gene identifiers were mapped to official gene symbols before downstream analysis.

Differential‐expression analysis and cross‐species comparison

Differential‐expression analysis was performed independently within each dataset. For RNA‐sequencing (RNA‐seq) datasets with raw count matrices, lowly expressed genes were filtered and differential expression was assessed using DESeq2. 18 For microarray datasets or datasets provided as normalized expression matrices, differential expression was assessed using limma. 19 Within each dataset, AD or AD‐model samples were compared with the corresponding control samples. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini‐Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. 20 Genes with FDR‐adjusted p < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change > 0.58 were considered differentially expressed.

To assess analytical robustness, six recoverable mouse count‐like RNA‐seq datasets were compared across DESeq2, edgeR quasi‐likelihood, 21 and limma‐voom 22 (Table S2, Figure S1).

Human discovery‐layer threshold sensitivity was assessed across alternative FDR and absolute log2‐fold‐change cutoffs while the mouse differentially expressed gene (DEG) union, ortholog mapping, Refined107, and tier assignments were held fixed (Table S3).

For cross‐species analysis, mouse gene symbols were mapped to human orthologs using biomaRt and curated ortholog annotations. 23 Human AD‐associated genes with significant corresponding differential expression in the mouse‐model layer were classified as shared cross‐species signals. Genes without significant corresponding representation in the mouse‐model layer were provisionally classified as mouse‐non‐overlapping human candidates. Cross‐dataset recurrence was defined as the number of human brain datasets in which a gene was differentially expressed.

The original gene‐level human‐mouse comparison was descriptive and partly selection‐conditioned because significant mouse‐model differential expression contributed to upstream candidate exclusion.

Refined107 construction and statistical‐consistency analysis

To refine the broad mouse‐non‐overlapping human candidate space, standardized effect sizes were estimated across human brain cohorts. For each candidate gene, Cohen's d was calculated within each available cohort and integrated across cohorts using random‐effects meta‐analysis in R. 24 Between‐cohort heterogeneity was quantified using the I 2 statistic. 2 5 Genes were prioritized according to meta‐analytic support, effect‐direction consistency, cross‐cohort recurrence, and heterogeneity, yielding Refined107, a meta‐refined 107‐gene human AD‐associated subset. A higher‐confidence Tier1_37 subset was defined using the original multi‐criterion refinement workflow, whereas the remaining genes were classified as Tier 2.

Statistical consistency was further evaluated using leave‐one‐dataset‐out analysis for genes available in all three meta‐refinement cohorts and 1000 recurrence‐matched control sets with the same two‐ and three‐cohort composition as Refined107. Evaluated metrics included heterogeneity and complete effect‐direction concordance (Tables S4 and S5, Figure S2). Table S4 separately reports discovery‐layer significant‐DEG recurrence, direction‐concordant significant‐DEG datasets, and meta‐refinement direction concordance. Directional replication was assessed in GSE203206, GSE138260, and GSE122063, three additional human‐brain cohorts not used in effect‐size meta‐refinement. Refined107 replication rates were compared with recurrence‐matched control gene sets, with effect‐size matching performed as a sensitivity analysis (Table S6, Figure S3).

Functional annotation, human–mouse comparison, and sensitivity analyses

Functional annotation of Refined107 and Tier1_37 was performed in R using clusterProfiler and curated AD‐relevant marker and pathway panels. 26 Gene‐set overlap analyses were used to evaluate associations with immune, vascular, blood–brain barrier, metabolic, neuronal, developmental, and major brain‐cell‐type‐related categories, without assigning causal or definitive cell‐type specificity.

Descriptive gene‐level mouse‐model status was classified as no usable mouse‐model result, detected but not significant, or significantly differentially expressed. Because significant mouse‐model differential expression contributed to upstream candidate exclusion, this comparison was partly selection‐conditioned and was not interpreted as independent evidence of cross‐species generalizability.

Independent pathway‐level comparison was based on pathways with a FDR‐adjusted p value < 0.05 and concordant normalized enrichment‐score direction in at least two of three human cohorts – GSE203206, GSE122063, and GSE138260. 27 The human pathway set was fixed before mouse‐model evaluation. Mouse evidence was assessed only in the four datasets with non‐empty comparable pathway outputs: GSE270100, GSE269314, GSE254970, and GSE125957. Pathways were classified as having same‐direction support, opposite‐direction support only, no significant support, or insufficient coverage; same‐direction support in one model family did not exclude an opposite‐direction result in another family (Table S7, Figure S4 Data S1). Tissue‐origin sensitivity analyses comprised exclusion of GSE231341 and, separately, restriction to the 15 human brain datasets, with changes in candidate layers, Refined107, Tier1_37, and machine‐learning cohort selection recorded.

Within‐cohort OOF classification and cross‐cohort transportability

Random‐forest models were implemented using a scripted machine‐learning workflow. 28 All formal analyses used 500 trees, a maximum depth of 8, random seed 42, balanced class weighting, and a predicted‐probability threshold of 0.5, without resampling or additional hyperparameter tuning. Within‐cohort performance was evaluated as a post‐selection within‐cohort OOF classification audit using stratified five‐fold prediction; for GSE278723, all observations from the same participant were assigned to the same fold. OOF prediction separated model fitting from prediction across folds but did not eliminate optimism associated with upstream Refined107 derivation.

Post‐selection ordered cross‐cohort transportability was evaluated by training the model in one cohort and testing it in another. Feature intersection and preprocessing parameters, including the means and standard deviations used for standardization, were derived from the training cohort only before application to the test cohort. Performance was summarized using area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), balanced accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, with class metrics calculated at a predicted‐probability threshold of 0.5. ROC AUCs and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using pROC. 29 Feature‐overlap sensitivity analyses were repeated using common59, the 59 genes shared across the three machine‐learning cohorts.

Cohort‐shift and single‐nucleus analyses

Cohort‐shift analysis was performed using the common59 genes shared across the machine‐learning cohorts. Principal component analysis was used to visualize cohort and diagnosis structure. PERMANOVA was performed using the vegan package to estimate the variance explained by cohort identity and diagnosis. 30 Gene‐level cohort effects were evaluated across the common59 feature space.

Single‐nucleus localization was assessed using the localization‐only GSE222494 frontal brain single‐nucleus data. 31 Cells from AD and control donors were retained according to the original annotations and aggregated into donor‐by‐cell‐type pseudo‐bulk profiles. Refined107 and Tier1_37 coverage was calculated for each cell type, and donor‐level module scores were compared between AD and control donors. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini‐Hochberg method. 20 Background‐corrected enrichment analysis was used to determine whether Refined107 genes were enriched beyond the all‐gene detection background within each cell type. This analysis was interpreted as localization evidence rather than evidence of independent generalizability.

RESULTS

Study design and identification of Refined107

We analyzed 15 human post mortem brain transcriptomic datasets and nine AD‐related mouse‐model datasets to assess the extent to which human AD‐associated transcriptional alterations were represented in commonly used transgenic mouse models (Figure 1, Table 1). The four human non‐brain or in vitro datasets were retained exclusively for tissue‐origin sensitivity analyses. In the brain‐restricted cross‐species analysis, the human DEG union comprised 43,872 genes, and the mouse‐model DEG union comprised 13,459 genes; 6604 genes overlapped between the two layers, leaving 37,268 genes in the broad mouse‐non‐overlapping human candidate set. Cross‐dataset recurrence reduced this candidate space to 15,383 genes recurrent in at least two human brain datasets, 6585 recurrent in at least three datasets and 1208 recurrent in at least four datasets. Across the nine mouse datasets, sex metadata were confirmed at the sample level for four datasets and at the study level for three, whereas two were unavailable or not reliably mappable.

From this candidate space we derived Refined107, a meta‐refined 107‐gene human AD‐associated gene subset supported by meta‐analytic evidence across human brain cohorts (Figure 2). Refined107 consisted of 37 Tier 1 and 70 Tier 2 genes, and 85 of 107 genes showed low between‐cohort heterogeneity, with I 2 < 25%. Leading Tier 1 effects included genes with both positive and negative meta‐analytic effect directions, including MSX1, CDA, TEX22, S100A12, CLDN3, CCR7, SLC6A3, CDH17, EDARADD, CYP24A1, and HLA‐H. Per‐cohort effect estimates generally preserved the principal meta‐analytic direction across the three meta‐refinement cohorts (GSE261775, GSE261050, and GSE278723), supporting the statistical consistency of Refined107. Refined107 included 48 two‐cohort genes and 59 three‐cohort genes. Leave‐one‐dataset‐out analysis preserved the meta‐analytic direction for 59/59 three‐cohort genes, with a median maximum absolute Cohen's d change of 0.086. Compared with 1000 exactly k‐matched recurrence‐only control sets, Refined107 showed lower median I 2 (0.0% vs 58.3%) and complete direction concordance (100.0% vs 0.0%; empirical p = 0.001). These results support incremental statistical consistency rather than functional superiority (Tables S4 and S5; Figure S2).

Across alternative thresholds, Refined107 retention ranged from 59.8% to 100.0% in the broad candidate layer and from 46.7% to 100.0% in the recurrence ≥2 layer, while direction agreement among shared recurrent genes remained high (93.8% to 99.3%; Table S3).

In three additional human‐brain cohorts not used in effect‐size meta‐refinement, Refined107 showed higher directional replication than recurrence‐matched controls (64.8% vs a median of 52.4%; empirical p = 0.001), with consistent findings in the effect‐size‐matched sensitivity analysis (Table S6, Figure S3). These cohorts were not independent of all upstream discovery steps.

Biological interpretation and human–mouse comparison

Functional annotation linked Refined107 and Tier1_37 to AD‐relevant biological themes, including neuroinflammation or innate immune processes, blood–brain barrier and adhesion dysfunction, metabolism or lipid‐vitamin signaling, synaptic or neuronal signaling, and developmental reactivation (Figure 3). Cell‐type marker‐panel overlap further implicated microglial/myeloid, endothelial/blood‐brain barrier, neuronal/synaptic, and ependymal/choroid plexus‐related signals. Representative biological anchors included MSX1, CDA, S100A12, CLDN3, CCR7, GPR152, SLC6A3, CDH17, EDARADD, PAX2, CYP24A1, and HLA‐H.

The original gene‐level mouse comparison descriptively summarized mouse‐model result availability and differential‐expression status for Refined107 (Figure 3). Among Tier 1 genes, 17 had no usable mouse‐model result and 20 were detected but not significant. Among Tier 2 genes, 47 had no usable mouse‐model result and 23 were detected but not significant. Detailed mouse‐model gene‐level effects are shown in Figure S6. Because significant mouse‐model differential expression contributed to upstream candidate exclusion, this analysis was partly selection‐conditioned and was not interpreted as independent evidence of cross‐species generalizability. These findings provide a descriptive, selection‐conditioned gene‐level comparison.

DEG method‐robustness analyses showed high consistency in gene ranking and effect direction across DESeq2, edgeR quasi‐likelihood, and limma‐voom, whereas threshold‐defined DEG overlap was more variable (Table S2; Figure S1).

Independent pathway‐level analysis identified 636 recurrent human pathways. Of these, 226 showed same‐direction support in at least one evaluated mouse‐model family, 140 showed opposite‐direction support only, 258 showed no significant support in evaluated mouse outputs, and 12 had insufficient mouse coverage. Among the 216 pathways recurrent in all three human cohorts, the corresponding counts were 67, 72, 77, and 0. These results indicate partial and model‐dependent pathway correspondence (Table S7, Figure S4, Data S1).

We next assessed whether the principal human AD‐associated signal was influenced by inclusion of non‐brain or in vitro datasets. In the broader descriptive five‐cohort gene‐level audit, the proportion of evaluable Refined107 genes showing same‐direction support ranged from 38% to 57% across datasets (Figure 4, Figure S6). However, excluding the four non‐brain datasets had no effect on the broad mouse‐non‐overlapping human candidate union, which changed by 0 genes. Recurrence‐filtered layers changed modestly, with the recurrent ≥2, ≥3, and ≥4 layers changing by −1, −16, and −23 genes, respectively. Removing the iPSC‐derived neuron sensitivity dataset did not alter the refined gene sets used downstream: Refined107 remained 107/107 unchanged, Tier1_37 remained 37/37 unchanged, and all three machine‐learning cohorts remained human brain cohorts (Figure 4).

Within‐cohort OOF classification and cross‐cohort transportability

In the post‐selection within‐cohort OOF classification audit, Refined107 showed disease‐state separation in the three human brain machine‐learning cohorts, with AUCs of 0.833 for GSE261775, 0.907 for GSE261050, and 0.980 for GSE278723 (Figure 5, Table S8). Subject‐grouped OOF sensitivity analysis in GSE278723 retained high performance, with an AUC of 0.939 compared with the sample‐level OOF estimate of 0.980. Sample‐level gene‐set screening further showed that predictive performance varied by cohort and gene‐set definition, indicating that the Refined107 signal was informative but cohort dependent. Feature‐level and robustness diagnostics supporting the machine‐learning analyses are summarized in Figure S5.

In the post‐selection ordered cross‐cohort transportability audit, performance was weaker and inconsistent (Figure 6, Table S8). Cross‐cohort AUCs were 0.288 to 0.720, with a median of 0.446, including 0.599 for GSE261775 to GSE261050, 0.288 for GSE261775 to GSE278723, 0.720 for GSE261050 to GSE261775, 0.448 for GSE261050 to GSE278723, 0.407 for GSE278723 to GSE261775, and 0.444 for GSE278723 to GSE261050. Transportability operating points showed marked sensitivity–specificity imbalance across several transfer directions. Principal component analysis of the common59 feature space showed clear separation by cohort, and PERMANOVA indicated that cohort identity explained substantially more variance than diagnosis. Cohort explained 21.1% of variance (R2 = 0.211), whereas diagnosis explained 1.9% (R2 = 0.019), indicating a substantially larger cohort effect. Common59 restriction did not materially improve transportability (ΔAUC, −0.004 to +0.003), and 53/59 common59 genes showed significant cohort effects. These results indicate that Refined107 carries disease‐state information but that direct cross‐cohort transportability is constrained by cohort shift (Tables S8 and S9, Figure S7).

Single‐nucleus localization

Finally, we examined Refined107 in the localization‐only GSE222494 single‐nucleus dataset 31 to assess cell‐state localization and background‐corrected enrichment (Figure 7). Refined107 genes were detectable across major brain cell populations, with detectable gene counts ranging from 13/107 in other neurons to 62/107 in excitatory neurons. Donor‐level AD‐control module‐score effects were not significant after multiple‐testing correction, with all cell‐state FDR values > 0.05. Background‐corrected cell‐state testing also did not identify significant enrichment after accounting for all‐gene detection background, with all enrichment FDR values equal to 1.0. These findings indicate that Refined107 is broadly detectable across brain cell populations but should not be interpreted as specific to a single dominant cell type under the current single‐nucleus analytical framework or as evidence of independent generalizability.

DISCUSSION

In this cross‐species molecular‐profiling study, Refined107 emerged as a meta‐refined human AD‐associated gene subset with cross‐cohort statistical support and inflammatory and barrier‐related biological annotation. The independent pathway‐level comparison showed partial and model‐dependent human–mouse correspondence. Post‐selection, within‐cohort, OOF classification identified AD disease‐state signal, whereas ordered cross‐cohort transportability remained limited and strongly influenced by cohort shift. The separate single‐nucleus analysis provided localization evidence without demonstrating formal cell‐type enrichment. Together, these findings define the biological relevance and translational boundaries of Refined107.

Threshold‐sensitivity analyses supported the stability of the principal Refined107 signal, although membership of the broader candidate layers remained dependent on the selected differential‐expression cutoffs.

A central implication of our study is that human AD‐associated transcriptional programs are not uniformly represented in commonly used mouse models. 11 , 12 , 32 Transgenic mouse models have been indispensable for studying amyloid deposition, tau pathology, neuroinflammation, and neurodegeneration. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 However, they are typically built around selected genetic perturbations 33 and cannot fully reproduce the polygenic, age‐related, vascular, immune, and regional complexity of human AD. 34 , 35 , 36 In our analysis, many Refined107 genes either lacked usable mouse‐model results or were detected but not significantly altered in the mouse comparison layer. These findings do not argue against the value of mouse models but rather identify molecular domains in which their correspondence with human AD may be limited. 11 , 12 Such domains may be particularly relevant when prioritizing human‐relevant molecular targets for subsequent experimental evaluation.

A key analytical boundary is that the gene‐level mouse comparison was partly selection‐conditioned because significant mouse‐model differential expression contributed to upstream candidate exclusion. The independently constructed pathway‐level analysis addressed this issue by defining recurrent human pathways before evaluating mouse‐model evidence. Accordingly, the absence of significant support in the evaluated mouse outputs should not be interpreted as evidence of biological absence. 32 , 33

The biological annotation of Refined107 was consistent with established AD‐related biological processes. 36 Both Refined107 and the higher‐confidence Tier1_37 subset were linked to inflammatory, myeloid/microglial, endothelial, and blood–brain‐barrier‐related programs. Immune activation, glial responses, 37 , 38 , 39 vascular dysfunction, 40 , 41 and blood–brain barrier disruption 42 are increasingly recognized as important components of AD pathophysiology and neurodegeneration. The presence of genes such as CCR7, HLA‐H, PI3, S100A12, CDH17, CLDN3, and EDARADD among the strongest candidates suggests that Refined107 may capture immune–vascular and barrier‐related disease processes. However, these associations do not establish causality. Functional studies are required to determine whether individual Refined107 genes contribute directly to AD pathogenesis or represent downstream disease states. Similarly, the advantage over recurrence‐matched control sets supports greater statistical consistency, rather than functional or causal superiority. Because Refined107 was derived using stringent recurrence, ortholog‐mappability, and meta‐analytic criteria, biologically relevant genes that did not satisfy one or more of these criteria may have been excluded.

Post‐selection machine‐learning audits indicated that Refined107 captured AD disease‐state information within individual human brain cohorts, but this signal did not translate into stable cross‐cohort performance. 13 Moderate to‐ high within‐cohort OOF AUCs were observed across the three machine‐learning cohorts, including after subject‐grouped OOF analysis of GSE278723. However, ordered cross‐cohort transportability remained limited, and restriction to common59 did not improve performance. Cohort identity explained substantially more variance than diagnosis, consistent with the recognized effects of study source, platform, and brain region on transcriptomic structure. 14 , 15 These findings distinguish within‐cohort disease‐state signal from cohort‐invariant diagnostic performance. OOF prediction separated model fitting from prediction across folds but did not eliminate optimism associated with upstream Refined107 derivation. 43

The separate single‐nucleus analysis characterized Refined107 detectability across annotated cell populations in the GSE222494 frontal brain dataset. 31 However, donor‐level AD‐control module score analyses did not identify significant Refined107 cell‐type effects after multiple‐testing correction, and all‐gene background‐corrected enrichment did not support formal cell‐type enrichment. Thus, the single‐nucleus results should be interpreted as evidence of detectability and localization, not as proof that Refined107 is specifically enriched in a particular cell type. Future analyses using larger single‐cell 44 , 45 or spatial transcriptomic datasets may clarify whether subsets of Refined107 map to disease‐associated cell states 46 , 47 , 48 or regional microenvironments. 49 , 50

This study has several strengths. First, the primary discovery analysis was brain‐prioritized, reducing the risk that tissue‐origin differences would drive the main findings. Second, the gene subset was refined using cross‐cohort effect‐size evidence rather than relying solely on thresholded differential expression. Third, the analytical framework combined biological annotation, descriptive gene‐level mouse assessment, independent pathway‐level comparison, within‐cohort OOF classification, cross‐cohort transportability testing, cohort‐shift analysis, and separate single‐nucleus localization. This design allowed us to evaluate not only whether Refined107 carried disease‐state information but also where its translational and generalizability boundaries lay.

Several limitations should also be considered. Public molecular‐profiling datasets vary in platform, tissue region, processing pipeline, sample composition, and clinical annotation, all of which may influence cross‐cohort comparisons. 13 , 14 , 15 Sample‐size imbalance across cohorts may also have affected statistical power and the stability of cross‐cohort comparisons. Although we used a brain‐prioritized design and sensitivity analyses, residual heterogeneity could not be eliminated, and regional or source‐sensitivity analyses remained descriptive rather than causal. The additional cohorts used for directional replication were not included in effect‐size meta‐refinement but contributed to upstream discovery procedures; these results should therefore be interpreted as post‐selection directional replication rather than fully independent confirmation. The mouse‐model layer included commonly used AD‐related models but did not capture the full diversity of available models, disease stages, or experimental conditions, and only four mouse datasets had non‐empty comparable pathway outputs. 5 , 33 Refined107 was derived from observational molecular associations, and the gene‐level mouse comparison was partly selection‐conditioned and should not be interpreted as a causal gene set without functional validation. The machine‐learning analyses were retrospective post‐selection audits based on public datasets; OOF prediction separated model fitting from prediction across folds but did not eliminate optimism associated with upstream Refined107 derivation, and the destination cohorts in ordered transportability analyses were not fully independent of upstream gene‐set derivation. These analyses therefore do not establish clinical diagnostic utility. 13 , 43 Mouse sex metadata were confirmed at the sample level for four datasets and at the study level for three, whereas two were unavailable or not reliably mappable. A harmonized sex‐stratified reanalysis was not performed because sex was intertwined with genotype, age, batch, tissue fraction, repeated measurements, or unresolved sample labels in the available datasets; sex effects therefore were not controlled or separated. Other unavailable donor and clinical metadata were not inferred. Finally, the single‐nucleus analysis supported detectability but did not demonstrate significant background‐corrected cell‐type enrichment.

In conclusion, Refined107 represents a meta‐refined human AD‐associated gene subset with brain‐cohort support, inflammatory and barrier‐related biological annotation, partial and model‐dependent pathway correspondence, and measurable within‐cohort disease‐state signal. Its limited cross‐cohort transportability underscores the need to distinguish biologically informative disease‐state gene subsets from deployable diagnostic classifiers. These findings provide a framework for prioritizing human‐relevant AD molecular programs for further evaluation across model systems and in larger multi‐omics, spatial, and functional studies.
