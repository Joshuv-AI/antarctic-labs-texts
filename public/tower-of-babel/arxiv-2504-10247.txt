Exponentially Decaying Quantum Simulation Error with Noisy Devices
Jue Xu, Chu Zhao, Junyu Fan, Qi Zhao

Abstract
Quantum simulation is a promising way toward practical quantum advantage, but noise in current quantum hardware poses a significant obstacle. We prove that not only the physical error but also the algorithmic error in a single Trotter step decreases exponentially with the circuit depth. This theoretical finding is validated by our numerical results over various Hamiltonians, initial states, and noise channels. Furthermore, we derive the optimal number of Trotter steps and the noise requirement to guarantee total simulation precision. To explicitly show the requirements for robust quantum simulation, we plot a phase diagram of the accumulated error in terms of circuit depth and noise rate. At last, we demonstrate that our improved error analysis leads to significant resource-saving for fault-tolerant Trotter circuits. By addressing these aspects, this work provides fresh and systematic insight on the practical quantum advantage through quantum simulation.

The accuracy of a noisy Trotter simulation task is affected by two competing factors, i.e., circuit depth and noise rate.
Specifically, increasing the Trotter steps (circuit depth) suppresses the Trotter error (algorithmic error) but introduces more physical error induced by circuit noise.

Our work demonstrates the robustness of Trotter simulation with noisy circuits through several novel contributions.

Finally, we present a phase diagram of the total error in a noisy Trotter circuit,

Setting: noisy Trotter circuit

We embark on studying the robustness of noisy Trotter simulation with the following setting.

II.2
Exponentially decaying one-step errors in noisy Trotter circuit

Roughly, the goal of a quantum simulation experiment is to achieve a certain simulation precision
\varepsilon
given the system Hamiltonian,
with maximal simulation time and minimal circuit depth.
Ideally, without physical noise, the algorithmic (Trotter) error can be arbitrarily suppressed by increasing the Trotter number
r

Despite the presence of noise, physical errors tend to accumulate as circuit depth increases.
Therefore, it is essential to analyze the tradeoff between algorithmic and physical errors, as well as the impact of noise on algorithmic error.
In this context, errors need to be formally defined to evaluate the robustness of a noisy Trotter circuit.

First, the one-step
physical error
is defined as the trace distance between the state
\rho

\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{n}

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:phy_{e}rr}\epsilon_{p,\gamma}^{\textup{phy}}(d):=\norm{ \rho_d - \E^n_\gamma(\rho_d) }_{1},

(5)

where
\rho_{d}
is the state after the
d
th noisy
p
th-order Trotter step and
d\in[r]
.
In the state-independent (worst-case) analysis,
the physical error induced by the one-qubit
\gamma
depolarizing channel
\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{n}
on
n
qubits has the upper bound

\norm{\E_{\gamma}^{n} - \IC}_{\diamondsuit}\leq 2n\gamma
.
However, this upper bound by the diamond norm
\norm{\cdot}_{\diamondsuit}
is pessimistic
because it overlooks that the state
\rho
in a noisy Trotter circuit is getting close to the maximally mixed state
I/2^{n}
.

Conventionally, the algorithmic error of the
p
th-order product formula is defined as

\norm{U - \pf_p}_{\mathrm{\infty}}

where the spectral norm captures the worst-case (initial state) analysis.
To analyze the effect of a general mixed state, we define the one-step

as the trace distance between the mixed state
\rho_{d}

after an ideal one-step evolution

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:alg_{e}rr}\epsilon_{p,\gamma}^{\textup{alg}}(d):=\norm{ \mathcal{U}(\rho_d) - \tilde{\mathcal{U}}_p(\rho_d)}_{1},

(6)

\epsilon_{p,\gamma}^{\textup{alg}}(d)\leq 2\norm{U - \pf_p}_{\mathrm{\infty}}

II.3
Upper bound of physical error

We first sketch the proof of the exponential decay of physical error.
The intuition is that the depolarizing channel renders the evolved state close to the maximally mixed state
such that the effect of noise becomes attenuated.
Since the local depolarizing channel transforms the state into a mixture of locally depolarized states,
we quantify the physical error by measuring the distance between the evolved state and the set of locally depolarized states
i.e.
\sum_{F:\absolutevalue{F}=1}\mathcal{D}\quantity(\rho\| \frac{1}{n} \rho_{\overline{F}}\otimes\frac{I}{2})
.
This distance is further bounded by the distance between the evolved state and the global maximally mixed state
\mathcal{D}\quantity(\rho\| \frac{I}{2^n})
,
which has been proved to decay exponentially with the noisy circuit depth
[
53
]

i.e.,

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{C}_{p,\gamma,r}(\rho)\|\frac{I}{2^{n}})\leq(1-\gamma)^{r}\mathcal{D}(\rho\|\frac{I}{2^{n}})

(7)

where
\mathcal{C}_{p,\gamma,r}
is the channel of
Definition 1

(Noisy Trotter)
.
,
and
\mathcal{D}(\cdot\|\cdot)
is the relative entropy.
Then, we have the following proposition.
The detailed proof can be found in

Section

(Exponential decay of physical error)
.

Given a one-qubit local depolarizing channel

\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{n}

\gamma

\gamma=o(n^{-1/2})

where
\rho_{d}
is the state of the
d

p

Further, assume the evolved state during the evolution satisfies

\sum_{F:\absolutevalue{F}=1}\mathcal{D}\quantity(\rho\| \frac{1}{n} \rho_{\overline{F}}\otimes\frac{I}{2})=\Theta\quantity(\frac{1}{n})\mathcal{D}\quantity(\rho\| \frac{I}{2^n})

where

II.4
Upper bound of algorithmic error

This section gives an exponentially decaying upper bound
on the algorithmic (Trotter) error of the noisy circuit by considering state information affected by the depolarizing noise.
Here, we outline the main idea of the proof.
Given the state
\rho_{d}
of the
d

\displaystyle\epsilon_{p,\gamma}^{\textup{alg}}(d):=\norm{U\rho_d U^{\dag}-\pf_p\rho_d \pf_p^{\dag}}_{1}=\norm{ [\rho_d, M_p] }_{1},

II.7
Optimal Trotter number and noise requirement

Ideally, the algorithmic (Trotter) error can be infinitely suppressed by increasing the Trotter number
r
or the Trotter order
p
.
However, this is not the case in the practical scenario.
For example, considering the overhead of circuits in high-order Trotter, Ref.
[
31
]
studied the optimal Trotter order to minimize the gate counts in the noiseless case and Ref.
[
61
]
studied the optimal Trotter order with noisy circuits.

On the other hand, due to the accumulation of the physical error with increasing circuit depth,
there exists an optimal Trotter number to minimize the accumulated error as shown in
Fig.

6
.
Previous works
[
48
,
49
,
32
,
50
]
analyzed the accumulated error of the noisy first-order Trotter (PF1) circuits by simply multiplying the worst-case errors with the Trotter number
r
, that is,

\tilde{\epsilon}_{1,\gamma}^{\textup{acc}}(r)\approx r\quantity( C \gamma+ B \frac{t^{2}}{r^2})
.
Then,
the optimal Trotter number of PF1 has the form

\tilde{r}_{\mathrm{opt}}\sim t\sqrt{\frac{B}{C\gamma}}
.
However, this upper bound by the triangle inequality is not tight
because it only considers the worst-case for each step

[
32
,
61
]
,
ignoring the evolved state information.

Therefore, given the immense reduction in Trotter error by higher-order product formula, it is natural to ask
how to balance two sources of errors by the number of high-order Trotter steps.
To analytically derive the optimal high-order Trotter number that minimizes the total error,
we assume the accumulated error of the
p
th-order noisy Trotter has the following form
(supported by
Theorem

1
and the numerical results),

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:empirical_{a}cc_{e}rror}\epsilon_{p,\gamma}^{\textup{acc}}(r)=\sum_{d=1}^{r}C\gamma\Upsilon e^{-c\gamma\Upsilon d}+B_{p}\frac{t^{p+1}}{r^{p+1}}e^{-b\gamma\Upsilon d},

(14)

where
\Upsilon=\mathcal{O}(2^{p})
is the number of layers implemented in one Trotter step in high-order product formulas.

Proposition 3

(Optimal Trotter number for noisy Trotter)
.

Consider a
p
th-order product formula circuit with local depolarizing noise rate
\gamma
and
the accumulated error formula
Eq.

14
.
Assume
c<C
and
b\approx c
.
The optimal number of Trotter steps is

r_{\mathrm{opt}}(\gamma)=\quantity(\frac{pB_p}{C\gamma\Upsilon})^{\frac{1}{p+1}}t.

(15)

To give an explicit asymptotic scaling,
assume
C=\Theta(n)
and
B_{p}=\Theta(n)
,
then
r_{\mathrm{opt}}(\gamma)=\Theta\quantity( \gamma^{-\frac{1}{p+1}} t)
.

Since there exists a minimal accumulated error
\varepsilon
achieved by the optimal Trotter number
r_{\mathrm{opt}}
for specific noise rate,
we derive the following
noise rate requirement

\gamma^{*}
necessary for robust quantum simulation with noisy high-order Trotter circuits.

Corollary 1

(Noise rate requirement for robust noisy Trotter simulation)
.

Consider the
p
th-order Trotter circuit with local depolarizing noise rate
\gamma

and
assume the accumulated error formula
Eq.

14
.
If the noise rate is larger than

\gamma^{*}=\frac{1}{C\quantity(B_p)^{\frac{1}{p}}}\quantity(\frac{\varepsilon}{t})^{1+\frac{1}{p}}\frac{p}{\Upsilon(p+1)^{1+\frac{1}{p}}},

(16)

for system size
n
and evolution time
t
,
it is impossible to guarantee the accumulated error within simulation precision
\varepsilon
.

To give a concrete asymptotic scaling, we could assume

C=\Theta(n)
and
B_{p}=\Theta(n)

supported by the numerical results,
then
\gamma^{*}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{O}\quantity(\qty(\frac{\varepsilon}{nt})^{1+\frac{1}{p}})
.
We postpone the detailed proof to

Section

A.4
,
where we also give examples of the requirement
\gamma^{*}
and optimal Trotter number
r_{\mathrm{opt}}
for the first and the second-order noisy Trotter.
This asymptotic scaling (inverse polynomial in
n
) requires quantum devices at least in the weak noise (
\gamma\sim 1/n
) regime
[
63
]
,
which is a good approximation of the noise present in some relatively small, fixed-sized experimental systems as
[
64
,
65
]
.
In contrast, the circuits with constant noise rate (independent of the system size
n
) are regarded in a strong noise regime.
Since Trotter algorithm typically requires at least
\textup{poly}(n)
circuit depth and
the state after
\Omega(\log(n))
depth with constant depolarizing noise rate becomes meaningless (i.e., very close to the maximally mixed state)
[
51
]
,
the constant noise circuits are not robust for demonstrating advantage by Trotter quantum simulation.

II.9
Common incoherent noise channels

While we use the depolarizing noise for analyses due to its simplicity,
the general Pauli noise channel
\mathcal{E}_{\gamma_{x},\gamma_{y},\gamma_{z}}(\rho)
of the form

(1-\gamma_{x}-\gamma_{y}-\gamma_{z})\rho+\quantity(\gamma_x X\rho X + \gamma_y Y \rho Y + \gamma_z Z\rho Z)\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:pauli_{n}oise}

(18)

is more realistic.
For example, depolarizing noise is with
\gamma_{x}=\gamma_{y}=\gamma_{z}
,
while the dephasing noise has
\gamma_{x}=\gamma_{y}=0
.
On the other hand, for the typical non-unital noise
[
51
,
63
,
72
]
,
the amplitude damping channel has the Kraus form,

\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{amp}}(\rho)=A_{0}\rho A_{0}L^{\dagger}+A_{1}\rho A_{1}^{\dagger}

where

A_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{1-\gamma}\end{pmatrix}

and

A_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{\gamma}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
.

shows that
the algorithmic errors always share the similar decaying behaviors with the physical errors,
while three typical noise channels have three distinct decaying behaviors.
Specifically, the one-step error with the depolarizing noise channel decays fastest,
while the amplitude damping one decays the most slowly and oscillates.
These different error decay rates qualitatively match the maximal computation time of circuits with different noise channels as indicated in
[

.

II.10
Error per-time model

In Trotter circuits, the gates are usually Pauli rotations
e^{-\textup{i}P\textup{d}t}
for certain local Pauli operator
P
with rotation angle
\textup{d}t=t/r
.
In our previous analyses, the noise rate
\gamma
is constant regardless of the rotation angle.
However, an alternative model called
error-per-time
model in which the noise rate is proportional to the angle,
i.e.,
\gamma\sim\textup{d}t
,
is also considered in
[
56
]
.
Both of these error models are idealizations but reasonable for different scenarios.
For example, if each rotation gate is implemented via a pulse,
the error probability will naturally be proportional to the gate duration (rotation angle).
This model is supported by experiments
[
73
,
74
]
,
where shorter pulse implementations achieve higher fidelities than standard longer ones.
Consequently, if we adopt this model,
the total physical error of the noisy Trotter circuit would be bounded by the total evolution
t
,
independent of the number of Trotter steps
r
.
Nevertheless, for quantum devices operated digitally where Pauli rotations are compiled into native elementary gates, the constant-noise model is more reasonable.
Then, the total accumulated error would still be one-step error times
r
as in previous sections.

III
Discussion

In this work, we quantitatively investigated the algorithmic and physical errors of the Trotter circuit with depolarizing noise.
Considering the evolved state information, our exponentially decaying error upper bound provides a more accurate estimation of the optimal number of Trotter steps and an explicit requirement on noise rate for robust noisy Trotter simulation.
In consequence, our analysis offers substantial reduction in circuit depths and number of physical qubits for fault-tolerant Trotter circuits.
Concretely, our findings delineate parameter regimes where Trotter simulation may demonstrate practical and robust quantum advantage despite noise.
To substantiate the theoretical proofs, various numerical results investigate the impacts, such as initial states, observables, and noise channels.
Therefore, by enhancing the understanding of the interplay between noise and algorithmic errors,
our work is an important step toward practical quantum advantage via quantum simulation.

Besides the Trotter error of states as in this work and Ref.
[
33
]
,
the Trotter errors in expectation values of local observables
[
60
]
could be more optimistic.
For instance,
Refs.
[
58
,
75
]
indicated
that quantum localization keeps Trotter errors in local observables bounded
if the Trotter step size stays below a critical threshold,
unlike state errors which grow with time and system size.
Analogous to our result, Ref.
[
59
]
showed
that the errors in local observables are much lower than expected.
On the other hand, the observable information not only makes Trotter error smaller,
but at the same time renders classical simulation more efficient
[
76
]
.
Therefore, it is crucial to understand the robustness of Trotter error in local observables with noisy quantum circuits.

Meanwhile, there are still many potential directions to pursue with the noisy Trotter simulation.
First, it would be desirable to combine our analysis with error mitigation techniques such as Refs.
[
49
,
77
]
.
In the other direction, one major open problem is whether we could propose low-overhead error correction schemes
[
78
]

or partially fault-tolerant schemes
[
79
,
80
]
tailored for Trotter simulation.
Additionally, for the robustness of post-Trotter quantum simulation algorithms such as Linear Combination of Unitaries
[
21
]
and Quantum Signal Processing
[
23
]
, other error sources should be considered seriously, e.g., the state-preparation-and-measurement (SPAM) errors, and the coherent errors.

In this section, we sketch the proof of Propositions 1 and 2 in the main text,
i.e., the exponential decay of physical and algorithmic errors.

IV.1
Sketch proof of Proposition 1 (Physical error decay)

The
d
-th step state
\rho_{d}
applying the one-qubit depolarizing noise channel
\mathcal{E}_{\textup{depo},\gamma}^{n}
can be written as

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline ethod:eq:channel_{e}xpand}\mathcal{E}_{\textup{depo},\gamma}^{n}(\rho_{d})=\sum_{F}\gamma^{|F|}(1-\gamma)^{n-|F|}\rho_{\overline{F}}\otimes\frac{I}{2^{|F|}},

(19)

where
F
takes all subsets of qubits representing the depolarized subsystems,

\overline{F}
is the complement of
F
and
\rho_{\overline{F}}
is the reduced density matrix of
\rho
on subsystem
\overline{F}
.
The one-step physical error is defined as the trace distance between the depolarized state and the original state.
Since the noise rate
\gamma
is small, we approximate the physical error to the first-order,

\displaystyle\norm{ \mathcal{E}_{\depo, \gamma}^{n}(\rho_d) - \rho_d }_{1}=

\displaystyle\norm{ -n\gamma\rho_d + \gamma\sum_{F:\abs{F}=1} \rho_{\overline{F}}\otimes\frac{I}{2}}_{1}+o(n\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n\gamma\sqrt{2\mathcal{D}\quantity( \rho_d \Vert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{F:|F|=1} \rho_{\overline{F}} \otimes\frac{I}{2})}+o(n\gamma).

The second line above is by the Pinsker’s inequality
that upper bounds the trace distance by the relative entropy.
Then, we bound the distance to the locally mixed state by the distance to the globally maximally mixed state
\frac{I}{2^{n}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\quantity( \rho_d \Vert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{F:|F|=1} \rho_{\overline{F}} \otimes\frac{I}{2})\leq\mathcal{D}\quantity(\rho_d \Vert\frac{I}{2^n} )\leq\mathcal{D}\quantity(\rho_0 \Vert\frac{I}{2^n} )e^{-\gamma d}.

The last inequality above introducing exponential decay factor
e^{-\gamma d}
is by the entropy contraction
[
53
]

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline ethod:eq:contraction}\mathcal{D}\quantity((\pfc\circ\E_{\gamma}^n)^r(\rho_0) \Vert\sigma)\leq(1-\gamma)^{r}\mathcal{D}(\rho_{0}\|\sigma).

(20)

where
\rho_{0}
is any initial state and
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
be the channel of one step Trotter unitary.
Since the maximum relative entropy to the maximally mixed state
\mathcal{D}\quantity(\rho_0 \Vert\frac{I}{2^n} )\leq n
, we have
Proposition

1
.
If the system holds to satisfy

\sum_{F:\absolutevalue{F}=1}\mathcal{D}\quantity(\rho\|\frac{1}{n} \rho_{\overline{F}}\otimes\frac{I}{2})=\Theta\quantity(\frac{1}{n})\mathcal{D}\quantity(\rho\| \frac{I}{2^n})
,
the upper bound of the one-step physical error can be improved to
\Theta(n)\gamma e^{-\frac{1}{2}\gamma d}
.

IV.2
Sketch proof of Proposition 2 (Trotter error decay)

First, we rewrite the
p
th-order Trotter unitary
\tilde{U}_{p}=U(I+M_{p})
in terms of its multiplicative error operator
M_{p}
.
Then, the one-step algorithmic error can be written as the trace norm of the commutator between
\rho
and the Trotter error operator
M_{p}

\displaystyle\norm{U\rho U^{\dag}-\pf_p\rho\pf^{\dag}_p}_{1}

\displaystyle=\norm{U\rho U^{\dag}-U(I+M_p)\rho(I+M_p^{\dagger}))U^{\dag} }_{1}

\displaystyle=\norm{\rho M_p -M_p\rho}_{1}\equiv\norm{[\rho, M_p]}_{1},

by the unitary invariance of trace norm.
Further, the multiplicative Trotter error operator
M_{p}
can be written as the sum of the leading-order local error terms as
M_{p}:=\delta t^{p+1}\sum_{j}E_{j}^{(p)}

where
E_{j}^{(p)}
acts on
w
qubits and
\delta t=t/r
.
For simplicity, we omit the subscript
d
as well as
p
.

\norm{U\rho U^{\dag}-\pf\rho\pf^{\dag}}_{1}=\norm{[\rho, M]}_{1}=\delta t^{p+1}\norm{\qty[\rho, \sum_j E_j]}_{1}

(21)

Then, by inserting cross terms of
\rho^{\prime}:=\rho_{\overline{F}}\otimes I/2^{w}
,
the sum of commutator norms
\sum_{j}\norm{ [\rho, E_j] }_{1}
becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\norm{\rho E_j - \rho' E_j + \rho' E_j - E_j \rho' + E_j\rho' -E_j \rho}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\norm{ \rho E_j - \rho' E_j }_{1}+\norm{\rho' E_j -E_j \rho'}_{1}+\norm{ E_j\rho' - E_j\rho}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j}2\norm{ \rho- \rho' }_{1}\norm{E_j}_{\mathrm{\infty}}+\norm{[\rho',E_j]}_{1}

(Hölder’s ineq)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\norm{I/2^w\otimes\rho_{\overline{F}}-\rho}_{1}\sum_{j}\norm{E_j}_{\mathrm{\infty}}

(locality of
E_{j}
)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Theta(n^{1/2})e^{-\frac{1}{2}\gamma d}\sum_{j}\norm{E_j}_{\mathrm{\infty}}

(Proposition 1)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Theta(n^{1/2})B_{p}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\gamma d}

(worst-case Trotter error)

The last third line uses
\norm{[\rho', E_j]}_{1}=0
because the error operator
E_{j}
acts on identity.
The exponential decay factor in the last second line is the same as the one of the physical error proved in Proposition 1.
In the last line, the factor

is from the upper bound of the worst-case Trotter error which is the
p
th-order nested commutator norm
[
33
]
.
So far, we have the exponential decay of the one-step Trotter error

2
.

A.1
Preliminaries

In this paper, different norms are used to quantify the errors in different cases.
In general, these norms can be defined from different perspectives.
Here, we adopt the notion of Schatten norms.

Definition 2

(Schatten norm)
.

The Schatten
p
-norm of a matrix
A
is defined as

\norm{A}_{p}=[\Tr(\abs{A}^p)]^{1/p}
.
Three commonly used Schatten norms are:

•

The Schatten 1-norm (also called trace norm or nuclear norm)
\norm{A}_{\tr}\equiv\norm{A}_{1}:=\Tr(\sqrt{AA^\dagger})=\sum_{j}\absolutevalue{\lambda}_{j}

that is the sum of the absolute value of eigenvalues of
A
.
It is commonly used to measure the distance between two density matrices, e.g.
A:=\rho-\tilde{\rho}
.

•

The Schatten
\infty
-norm (also known as spectral norm or operator norm, induced-2 norm)
\norm{A}_{\infty}
of an operator
A
is the largest singular value of
A
,
i.e.,
\norm{A}_{\infty}\equiv\norm{A}_{\mathrm{op}}:=\max_{\ket{\psi}}\norm{A\ket{\psi}}_{2}
where
\norm{\cdot}_{2}
is the
l_{2}
-norm for vectors.
So, it corresponds to the operator error induced by the worst input (state).

•

The Schatten 2-norm (also known as Frobenius or Hilbert-Schmidt norm) is
\norm{A}_{\mathrm{F}}\equiv\norm{A}_{2}:=\sqrt{\Tr(AA^\dagger)}

which is the (root mean square) eigenvalue.
Intuitively, it captures the average-case analysis.

Definition 3

(Diamond norm)
.

For a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
, diamond norm is the trace norm of the output of a trivial extension of
\mathcal{E}
,
maximized over all possible inputs with trace norm at most one

\norm{\E}_{\diamondsuit}:=\sup_{A}\quantity{\trnorm{(\E\otimes\mathbb{I}_n)(A)}: \trnorm{A}\le 1}\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:def_{d}norm}

(22)

where
A\in L(\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})
.

A.2
Upper bounds of physical error

A.2.1
The worst-case (state-independent) analysis

We first derive the worst-case upper bound of the physical error by the diamond norm of channels.

Lemma 1

(Diamond distance between Pauli channels
[

Given two

\norm{\E_{\vb{q}}-\E_{\vb{r}}}_{\diamondsuit}=\norm{\vb{q}-\vb{r}}_{1}=\sum_{i=0}^{4^{n}-1}\absolutevalue{p_i-r_i}.

Consider the one-qubit depolarizing channel

Corollary 2

(Diamond distance of 1-qubit depolarizing channel)

The diamond distance between a one-qubit depolarizing channel

and the identity channel

\norm{\E_{\depo,\gamma}^{1}-\IC}_{\diamondsuit}=2\gamma

The worst-case, state-independent bound is as follows.

Lemma 2

(Diamond norm upper bound of physical error)

The physical error induced by the tensor product of one-qubit depolarizing channels with noise rate

Nevertheless, the diamond norm bound is too pessimistic because it is considered the worst state in an extended Hilbert space.
Therefore, we derive a refined upper bound using the trace norm.

The worst-case (state-independent) upper bound on Trotter error is as follows.

Lemma 8

(Trotter error with commutator scaling

Lemma 9

(Trace norm of algorithmic error)
.

Given a state
\rho

p

\displaystyle\norm{U\rho U^{\dag}-\pf\rho\pf^{\dag}}_{1}=\norm{ [\rho, M] }_{1},

\displaystyle\norm{U\rho U^{\dag}-\pf_p\rho\pf^{\dag}_p}_{1}

\displaystyle=\norm{U\rho U^{\dag}-U(I+M_p)\rho(I+M_p^{\dagger}))U^{\dag} }_{1}

The accumulated error we bound by triangle inequality as the sum of the errors of all steps, i.e.,

(48)

Since

by the assumption.

B.1
Impact of noise channels

Besides the depolarizing channel that we have discussed in the main text,
there are two widely used noise channels for modeling realistic noise, i.e.,
the dephasing noise channel and the amplitude damping channel.
The dephasing noise channel on a single qubit is

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\textup{deph}}_{\gamma}(\rho)

\displaystyle:=(1-\gamma)\rho+\gamma Z\rho Z.

(59)

The one-qubit (local) dephasing noise channel on one layer of the
n
-qubit circuit is the tensor-product of
Eq.

59
.

On the contrary to unital noise channels (i.e.
\mathcal{E}(I)=I
), the typical non-unital noise channel is the amplitude-damping channel:

\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\textup{damp}}(\rho)=E_{0}\rho E_{0}^{\dagger}+E_{1}\rho E_{1}^{\dagger}

where the Kraus operators are

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:single_{a}mp_{d}amping}E_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{1-\gamma}\end{pmatrix},\quad E_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{\gamma}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(60)

In
Fig.

11
, we compare the one-step (physical and algorithmic) error decay of the (local) depolarizing, dephasing, and amplitude-damping noise channel.
It can be expected that the errors of the depolarizing channel decay much faster than the dephasing channel.
On the other hand, due to the non-unital property of the amplitude-damping channel, the errors of this channel do not exhibit apparent exponential decay but have large fluctuations.
