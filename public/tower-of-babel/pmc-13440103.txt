Lived experiences of women prescribed low-dose aspirin for preeclampsia prevention: a phenomenological study
Authors: Prisca Asiimwe, Grace Nambozi, Laura Brennaman, Joseph Ngonzi, Gad Ruzaaza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreeclampsia is a prevalent issue causing significant suffering and deaths in Uganda. The Ministry of Health has introduced a policy mandating the use of low-dose aspirin for its prevention. Being a new intervention, the acceptability of this drug among the women for whom it is prescribed is still unexplored in Southwestern Uganda. This study aimed to explore the lived experiences of women on low-dose aspirin during their most recent pregnancy, identify any challenges encountered and recommend appropriate solutions.MethodsWe used a descriptive phenomenological design to explore the lived experiences of women. Data was collected using purposive sampling from women in the antenatal clinic and postnatal units of a tertiary hospital in Southwestern Uganda. A self-developed interview guide and an audio recorder were used to collect data. Data collection continued until saturation was reached at 16 participants. Participants provided narratives about their knowledge of preeclampsia, their perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers and their experiences with aspirin prescription (use, or non-use). Analysis was done using the Colaizzi’s’ method of analyzing qualitative data. Data was collected in two months between the 4th of October and the 20th of December 2025.ResultsThe study included 16 participants (mean age, 31 years; SD, 6.3; range, 22–43). Most participants were married, residents of Mbarara city and had attained secondary education. The parity ranged from 1 to 8 (mean = 4, SD = 2). Half of the participants had experienced an abortion, and 5 (31.3%) had lost 75% or more of their previous pregnancies. Aspirin 75mg and 150 mg were initiated at a mean gestation age of 20 weeks (SD 1.8), and 81% (95% CI: 0.53-0.94) of those who used aspirin progressed to preeclampsia. This study generated five themes: perceived clinical benefit and self-efficacy, suboptimal provider guidance, delayed LDA initiation, resilient therapeutic optimism and side effects-based non-adherence.ConclusionThis study highlights perceived barriers and implementation challenges that may contribute to suboptimal uptake of aspirin prophylaxis and the high prevalence of preeclampsia in Southwestern Uganda. Mitigating these challenges requires consistent capacity building for maternal healthcare providers, robust community sensitization, and incorporating a screening tool within the antenatal card. In addition, improving medication adherence through active follow-up and strengthening health systems are essential to promote early antenatal care utilization. Future research can establish the population-specific risk factors for southwestern Uganda to adjust the general screening tool provided by the Ministry of Health so as to address preeclampsia at the regional level.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09324-6.Keywords: “Preeclampsia aspirin perceptions”, “Preeclampsia aspirin attitudes”, “Preeclampsia aspirin adherence”

Contributions to the literature

What is known

Preeclampsia is a significant factor in maternal morbidity and mortality in Uganda, with the southwestern region alone reporting a prevalence of 22%. In response to this serious public health burden, the Ministry of Health has implemented a policy mandating the use of low-dose aspirin for its prevention.

What this paper adds

Given the recent introduction of low-dose aspirin as a preventative measure for preeclampsia in Uganda, this paper explores maternal perspectives and adherence experiences regarding its use during pregnancy. The paper also identifies important challenges to the preventive therapy and offers actionable strategies to improve patient adherence. Ultimately, these findings aim to inform a robust framework for addressing the high prevalence of preeclampsia in the study setting.

Background

Preeclampsia (PE) is a serious, pregnancy-specific syndrome that causes significant ill health and death in both women and newborns [1]. This is hypertension that starts after 20 weeks of gestation, which destroys vital organs, leading to signs of end-organ dysfunction [2]. The end-organ dysfunction is evident through signs such as proteinuria, impaired liver function (epigastric pain), renal insufficiency (reduced urine output), pulmonary edema (shortness of breath), new onset of cerebral or visual symptoms (severe headache/blurred vision), and edema [3]. The condition continues as a significant clinical challenge due to its elusive etiology. This, therefore, complicates the development of definitive preventive and treatment strategies making it one of the most serious complications of pregnancy [4]. The etiology of preeclampsia is multifactorial, involving an interplay of genetic, immunological, and environmental factors, as well as abnormal placental implantation [5]. The placenta is thought to play a pivotal role, making its removal the only definitive cure [6]. In severe cases, this translates into termination of pregnancy regardless of the gestation age, often leading to delivery of premature babies with all the accompanying challenges of prematurity [7].

PE is an important health concern, affecting approximately 15% of all pregnant women annually worldwide [8, 9]. This condition is responsible for an estimated annual loss of 76,000 women’s lives and 500,000 babies’ lives globally [10, 11]. Although the disease affects all women worldwide, Sub-Saharan Africa alone accounts for 56% of the global burden [12]. Notably, the case fatality rates are often underestimated in many African countries, as many women die before reaching health facilities [12]. The burden of preeclampsia is especially pronounced in Uganda, where it is responsible for a quarter of all maternal deaths nationwide [13] and represents 22% of maternal morbidity and mortality in the current study area [14]. This is far from the target for Sustainable Development Goals of at most 70 deaths per 100,000 live births [15]. Culturally, some women in Sub-Saharan Africa who experience the initial symptoms of PE interpret them as the work of witchcraft or evil spirits, often leading them to seek help from spiritualists instead of medical professionals [16]. This often leads to late medical interventions with resulting poor outcomes. Therefore, achieving the third Sustainable Development Goal requires a strong focus on mass education and prevention strategies.

Inadequate management of preeclampsia precipitates grave risks to both the mother and baby. Maternal issues include life-threatening complications such as placental abruption, acute renal failure, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and Hemolysis Elevated Liver Enzymes Low Platelet (HELLP) syndrome [17, 18]. In addition, the condition can progress to eclampsia, a major cause of maternal mortality. While most mothers recover post-delivery, long-term maternal sequelae remain, including chronic hypertension, ischemic stroke, cardiovascular disease, venous thromboembolism, kidney disease, and memory impairment [19, 20]. Further still, the condition endangers the fetus, resulting in distress, growth restriction, prenatal death, and high blood pressure [21]. The affected children further face higher risks of obesity, delayed physical development, cognitive impairment, and PE later in life [22]. This makes prevention a critical priority in obstetric care.

While research on preeclampsia prevention is ongoing, aspirin has shown a significant effect in preventing its occurrence. Aspirin is thought to improve the placentation process when initiated early in pregnancy by stabilizing the maternal endothelium and inhibiting platelet aggregation, hence lowering the levels of placental infarcts [23]. The high-quality evidence of its effectiveness when initiated before 12 weeks of gestation and taken daily at bedtime until the 37th week is the basis for its recommendation by the World Health Organization [24, 25]. The commendation is centered on the theory that aspirin greatly reduces the levels of thromboxane when taken at night, with the anticipation that the individual will follow the dose with sleep [26]. With this cascade, Low-Dose Aspirin (LDA) has proven high effectiveness in reducing blood pressure, which is the overall aim in preeclampsia prevention. The 12-week recommendation is grounded on the fact that around that time, the process of placentation completes [27]. Therefore, aspirin given at this time aids the remodeling of the uterine arteries and prepares the placental bed [23].

Based on this evidence, the Ministry of Health, Uganda, passed a policy in 2022 requiring that aspirin 75 mg be prescribed for all women at risk of preeclampsia at 12 weeks of gestation, to be taken until 36 weeks [28]. Despite this recommendation, it was unclear if women in the region had fully accepted this intervention without any challenges. PE rates were anticipated to continue rising if women did not see the benefits of LDA use or if their specific challenges remained unaddressed.

Previous studies have reported a lack of acceptance by the at-risk women and uptake by health workers, which has led to the inability to achieve the desired outcomes. For example, Vinogradov, Smith [29] found that women lacked adequate information about the safety of aspirin to the unborn baby. Therefore, they used the drug amidst fear. Negative opinions, attitudes, and perceptions toward medication use during pregnancy have been cited to influence the experiences of women on LDA. Some women have been found to believe that pregnancy is a natural process that should not be supported by medications [30, 31]. In some of the studies, women expressed fear of unknown side effects the drug could cause them or their babies [29, 31, 32]. Therefore, they could silently decline to swallow it. Although some studies indicate that aspirin can cause congenital abnormalities [33, 34], some of these studies experimented on small samples and therefore could not produce conclusive evidence. Subsequent and more recent studies have found that LDA does not increase the risk of congenital abnormalities, suggesting that it is safe to use during pregnancy [35, 36]. Other scholars have noted complacency in using aspirin due to a lack of pain or symptoms at the time the drug is prescribed [37]. Therefore, women find it simply not logical or necessary to take medicines when they are not sick [29]. On the other hand, health workers have also been noted to downplay the use of aspirin in pregnancy. Despite substantial evidence on the safety of LDA in pregnancy [38], some health workers believe that aspirin makes women susceptible to postpartum hemorrhage while others simply don’t know how it works to prevent preeclampsia [29, 37]. This lack of knowledge and trust is reported to influence women’s acceptance and proper use of the medicine. In addition, some health workers have maintained a perception that preeclampsia is only treatable but not preventable [39]. This knowledge gap subsequently limits their confidence in emphasizing the benefits of the drug, hence affecting its positive reception by the users. On the contrary, increased acceptance has been noted among women who are given adequate information on the potential risk of not using a preventive medication and the benefits of use [40]. Respectively, in countries where this preventive intervention is still new, the WHO emphasizes making significant efforts to help women conceptualize their individual risk and accept the therapy [38].

Guided by the Health Belief Theory (HBT), a social-psychological model valued for explaining health actions in situations of uncertainty and for illuminating complex diseases with undefined causes [41], we explored critical insights from women who had been prescribed aspirin prophylaxis during a recent pregnancy. The HBT posits that individuals will take a preventive action, such as aspirin prophylaxis, only if they believe that they are susceptible, consider the disease to be severe, believe that the disease can be avoided by using the medicine and if they feel personally capable of acting. This, therefore, requires having the necessary knowledge and a supportive environment. This paper, therefore, shares the lived experiences of women prescribed LDA in pregnancy and provides practical measures against the challenges expressed by women who participated in this study.

Methods

Study design

We used a descriptive phenomenological design. The design was chosen to get a rich and detailed description of the women’s experiences of using LDA during pregnancy [42]. We interviewed women in the antenatal clinic to obtain their experiences with aspirin. This was done to assess whether they were taking it as prescribed, not taking it, the reasons for each response, and the effects so far. We also interviewed women admitted to the maternity ward of the same hospital to explore how aspirin was used and the overall outcome.

Study setting

This study was conducted in Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH), a government hospital, located about 260 km from the capital, Kampala. The hospital provides preventive, diagnostic, curative and rehabilitation services to patients referred from over 12 districts in the West of the country. It has highly specialized services, making it a referral centre for the other regional referral hospitals in western Uganda. This hospital was used because it is the center for health business and social services in southwestern Uganda, and also the central point where most of the PE and eclampsia cases in southwestern Uganda are referred to and managed [43]. The hospital conducts approximately 9,000 deliveries annually [43]. It has an obstetric referral annual load of about 1,400 patients from peripheral health facilities (Hospital records, unpublished).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included antenatal and postpartum women who were prescribed LDA during pregnancy for PE prevention. Only those who consented to participate in the study were included in the sample. Women who were too weak to participate in the study were excluded.

Sample size determination

Data was collected until saturation occurred, the point at which no new data emerged from the interviews. This was reached at the 16th participant.

Sampling procedure and data collection procedure

Purposive sampling was used to select women with a prescription of LDA during pregnancy. This ensured that the participants who were approached had rich knowledge of the subject. Potential participants were approached by the principal investigator, a female master’s degree critical care nurse with special training in ethics of responsible conduct of research. They were briefed on the purpose of the study and asked to participate in it. For those who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to take part in the study, written informed consent was obtained. Data collection was subsequently conducted in a private, quiet environment to ensure confidentiality and minimize distractions. Data was collected using a self-developed interview guide and an audio recorder. One-on-one, face-to-face in-depth interviews were conducted in a quiet place free of interruptions. The interviews were conducted in the local language (Runyankore). The interview guide was pilot-tested on four women at a neighboring hospital, and subsequent modifications were made to suit the study objectives. The participants were asked to consent to have their voices recorded. Non-verbal cues and keynotes were noted down during the interviews to allow for double-checking at the end. The interviews were conducted by a single experienced investigator who was not involved in the clinical care of these women during their pregnancy to avoid bias. Women were asked to provide a narrative regarding their knowledge of preeclampsia, perceived susceptibility, LDA prescription and experiences of use or non-use. Risk at prescription was mainly determined based on a previous history of PE, chronic hypertension, recurrent abortions and a current blood pressure reading of ≥ 140/90 mmHg. The researcher guided the participants in discussing specific aspects of their experiences accordingly. Participants were given a verbal summary of the interview and requested to be contacted for amendments to the interpretations. Field notes were collected and used to assist the researchers in data immersion. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim for analysis. Transcribed interviews underwent participant checks and were anonymized. Follow-up interviews were conducted to verify that the transcribed data accurately reflected the participant’s lived experiences. Data was collected in two months between the 4th of October and the 20th of December 2025. On average, the interviews lasted 30 min. Data saturation was assessed by monitoring repetition in themes using 3 consecutive interviews. 22 participants were approached, and all of them were eligible. However, only 19 consented and 16 were enrolled. One was excluded due to a language barrier, one declined to participate, and three were dropped because their responses were already mentioned.

Quality control

For data quality assurance, the interview guide was translated from English to the local language and then back to English to ensure consistency.

Rigor of research

Trustworthiness and believability of this study were ensured by following Othmans’s guiding principles of credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability [44].

Credibility

Credibility refers to the degree of trust that participants and other observers have in the genuineness of the findings [45]. This was achieved by using purposive sampling to select the participants, sticking to the inclusion criteria, giving participants sufficient time to express themselves and concentrating on questions that were relevant to the study objectives. The participants were given adequate information on the background of the study to ensure acquaintance with the study aims. The interviewer also used prompts that facilitated the expansion of information and requested a chance to ask for additional information in case it was necessary.

Dependability

Dependability refers to data consistency and the ability of future researchers to replicate the study using the provided methodological details [46]. This was ensured by accurately recording the information in its original form, double-checking it with the participants and documenting the process of data collection precisely.

Confirmability

Confirmability denotes the quality of the research in terms of the objectivity of the data in the analysis and interpretation [47]. This was achieved by including the participant’s illustrative quotes in the themes developed. No preconceived codes were used.

Transferability

Transferability refers to how the study findings can be applied to different settings or contexts so that other researchers can access and use the methods [48]. A description of the study methods, participants and the site where data was obtained is indicated in this article to achieve transferability.

Data analysis

The transcribed data was analyzed using the Colaizzi’s technique of analyzing phenomenological data. This method is known for its efficiency in analyzing descriptive phenomenological data [49]. The method involves four stages of bracketing, intuiting, analyzing and describing. In bracketing, the researcher identified and held in abeyance their own preconceived opinions and beliefs about the phenomenon under study. In intuiting, the investigator remained open to the meanings recognized in the phenomena by those who had experienced it. Based on this, the researcher proceeded to the phase of analyzing the data by extracting significant statements, categorizing them and making sense of the phenomenon. Two investigators (PA, GN) independently coded the transcripts, subsequently triangulating their findings to reach a consensus on the most prominent themes. The researchers ensured that they familiarize with the data and developed a codebook under the constructs; perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, cues to action and self-efficacy. In the final phase, the researcher described the phenomenon in accordance with the concepts of the participant’s views. Constant comparison techniques were used to ensure the rigor of this qualitative data analysis.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

The study included 16 participants (mean age: 31 years, SD 6.3; range: 22–43). Most participants were married, residents of Mbarara city and had attained secondary education (see Table 1). Parity ranged from 1 to 8 (mean = 4, SD 2) (see Table 2). Half of the participants had experienced a spontaneous abortion, and 5 (31.3%) had lost 75% or more of their previous pregnancies. Aspirin was initiated at a mean gestation age of 20 weeks (SD 1.8), and 81% (95% CI: 0.53–0.94) of those who used aspirin progressed to preeclampsia.

Additional findings on aspirin use

Distribution by residence

The women in this study came from 4 districts of Isingiro, Kitagwenda, Mbarara district and Mbarara city. The majority of the participants were residents of Mbarara city (n = 10, 62.5%) and Isingiro district (n = 3, 18.8%).

Theme 1: Perceived clinical benefit and self-efficacy

The women generally termed preeclampsia as “pregnancy pressure,” with their knowledge rooted in firsthand experience or relatives/friends. They viewed the condition as a dangerous threat linked to past miscarriages and severe complications. Consequently, they saw aspirin as a crucial lifeline, a ray of hope and a vital intervention to mitigate such experiences. This perception of therapeutic benefit likely served as a primary motivator for the participant’s positive attitude towards the preventive therapy.

“Yes, I know it because I have suffered from it before. I think it can happen again on the next pregnancy because I had it on my previous pregnancy and now it is back… It could make me feel weak, wanting to sleep all the time and fire in my chest. I was feeling like I was going to die… I think it can kill me and my baby. It killed my previous baby. So when they gave me this medicine, I felt relieved because I thought I was going to be fine, may be my baby will survive this time”. P12, 36

“Yes, I know this disease. I have been suffering from it on each of my pregnancies… I don’t know whether it can come back because I don’t know what brings it… I think it is this disease which has been killing my babies from inside. I have only one child out of five. It can also kill me… when I was given those tablets, I did not feel worried. I got some hope.” LDA9,36

Women with healthy past pregnancies and no positive family history were less familiar with preeclampsia. However, their current physical discomfort made the danger feel immediate and life-threatening. This distress fostered a deep motivation to take aspirin; they viewed the medication as a source of relief and a necessary step toward the survival of their babies.

“No. I don’t know this disease. I don’t know whether I can suffer from it again because this is the first time it has attacked me… It has made me very weak; I can’t do my work, yet I have to spend a lot of money… When I was given the medicine, I wasn’t worried because I knew that the medicine would help me and my baby.” LDA7,37

“No. I don’t know much about this disease. I have never experienced it before. I have been having my pregnancies normally…. I don’t know where it came from. So it can happen again maybe….my heart was beating very fast. I think this disease can stop it from working and I die. It has even killed my baby. It is a very dangerous disease… When I was given these tablets, I wasn’t worried because I was already taking tablets for blood. So. I could take all of them at once. Besides, they are very small tablets, easy to swallow.” LDA4,40

Women who had experienced prior health complications showed a strong internal drive to be in control of their health without much external support. For those with a history of successful aspirin use, the proven effectiveness built a foundation of trust. This directly enhanced their self-efficacy and commitment to the therapy. What is worth noting is that the willingness to use the medicine was noted both in women who perceived themselves as being at high risk of future recurrence and those who perceived themselves to be at low risk.

“I wanted to have a normal pressure. I wanted to have a baby, a healthy baby because 3 of my pregnancies had come out. I had no child so I was determined to do anything it took to have a baby.” LDA7,37

“I wanted my baby to survive. I also wanted to be fine. I needed a baby. I have been losing all my pregnancies. My marriage is not okay.” LDA8,27

“I wanted to get rid of the headache and the palpitations, so I didn’t need anyone to remind me.” LDA6,22.

Theme 2: Suboptimal provider guidance

The majority of the women used the medicine, though not as per the standard recommendations. This was facilitated by the explanations provided by the health workers. Most women lacked detailed guidance on the drug. Consequently, despite taking the medication, the majority of the participants still progressed to preeclampsia.

“They told me that the drug will prevent recurrent pressure…I was taking 2 tablets at night after eating food…I did not meet any challenges…the doctor could take his time to know how I was doing with the medicine. That doctor in the high-risk room.” LDA14,30

“They did not tell me the reason. They just told me to go and take the medicine and come when it is finished…I was taking 2 tablets at night every day. I used them at first, and they caused me a severe headache. I went back to the doctor, and he told me to stop them. Then the headache stopped. I just continued with the other tablets and the headache stopped.” LDA2,35

Theme 3: Delayed LDA initiation

Most participants reported being prescribed aspirin on the day hypertension was diagnosed. This was also documented in their antenatal records. The majority of these women started taking aspirin therapy at or after 12 weeks of gestation.

“I started taking the medicine when my pregnancy was 8 months old. That is when the doctor found out that my pressure was high”. LDA3,24

“I started taking those tablets when I was 4 months pregnant”. LDA7,37

Theme 4: Resilient therapeutic optimism

Most women maintained a positive attitude towards aspirin, especially those who faced no challenges with the drug. Remarkably, even those who experienced treatment difficulties, disease progression, or pregnancy loss remained open to the therapy. These participants expressed a clear intent to use aspirin again in future pregnancies, highlighting a persistent trust in its preventive value.

“It changed the color of my urine to a dark color. It looked like it had blood in it. I also got pain in my upper abdomen, but I continued taking it. I went back to the hospital, and they gave me more medicine to treat the pain… Yes, I would take it again because it helped me to remain well and my baby too”. LDA5,23

“I could get numbness in my feet. I don’t know whether they were the ones causing it. But I continued taking it…Yes, I would take it again for the sake of my baby”. LDA9,36

Theme 5: Side effect-based non-adherence

For some women, the unusual discomfort of side effects outweighed the perceived benefits of the therapy. Because the medication was unfamiliar, participants were quick to blame it for any new physical sensations, leading them to stop the treatment prematurely.

“The medicine caused me severe headache. Then I had to stop taking it. I just continued with the other tablets and the headache stopped… I went back to the doctor who gave them to me and he told me to stop taking them because I had too much headache. When I stopped, the headache stopped also… I stopped taking them after one week at around five months, because they caused me too much headache… I wouldn’t take them again. They didn’t treat me well.” LDA2,35

“They woke up my ulcers, so I stopped using them after a few days. When I stopped them, the ulcers improved… No I wouldn’t take them again. They cause ulcers.” LDA3,24

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the lived experiences of women on LDA during their most recent pregnancy. We also sought to identify any challenges encountered with this therapy and recommend appropriate solutions. This study generated five themes: perceived clinical benefit and self-efficacy, suboptimal provider guidance, delayed LDA initiation, resilient therapeutic optimism and side effects-based non-adherence.

Perceived clinical benefit and self-efficacy

Despite the various challenges encountered during LDA therapy, a high level of participant acceptability remained. Although many women did not perceive an immediate clinical benefit, they maintained a positive attitude toward the treatment. Their willingness to use aspirin in future pregnancies suggested that the perceived benefits of preventing complications outweighed the immediate barriers experienced during the therapy. These results contrast with the findings of Shanmugalingam, Mengesha [50], where participants expressed reservations regarding sustained aspirin use during pregnancy. This discrepancy suggests that if women hold a high perceived benefit through effective health education and receive support to mitigate perceived barriers, their tendency to trust may increase. Therefore, provider support would act as an important cue to action, ensuring that women remain compliant despite clinical challenges.

Suboptimal provider guidance

The drawback in the information and instructions given to the women is consistent with previous research [51]. These scholars observed that when health workers provide inadequate information, it creates a cognitive barrier for patients. Without clear communication, women fail to recognize their perceived susceptibility to complications or the potential severity of their condition. Consequently, they remain unconvinced of the perceived benefits of the medication, viewing the preventive intervention as unnecessary. The gaps in information provision may be due to gaps in the health workers’ knowledge about the condition and the drug. Earlier studies have found that health workers who lack knowledge of the facts about aspirin prophylaxis therapy avoid giving detailed information to the mothers [31]. However, the challenge may also be due to a lack of time for thorough explanations, as hospitals in Western Uganda have been reported to have a high workload and insufficient staffing [52]. The findings imply that significant reductions in disease incidence could be achieved if women receive sufficient information on the benefits, the correct timing (start/stop), and side-effect management. This premise is supported by the Health Belief Theory, which posits that a patient’s motivation for preventive action is crucial for good health outcomes [53].

Delayed LDA initiation

The study noted that the majority of women started LDA on the same date their blood pressure reached hypertensive levels. This was evidenced by antenatal records showing aspirin being introduced alongside antihypertensive drugs and an abnormal blood pressure recording. On average, women started the prophylaxis therapy at 20 weeks of gestation, a time that research has found to be inappropriate [51, 54]. This result is consistent with studies by other researchers, demonstrating that the effectiveness of aspirin is significantly diminished when the therapy is commenced after the first trimester [55, 56]. According to the World Health Organization, the optimum timing should be at 12 weeks of gestation [24], which corresponds to the time around which placentation completes [57]. This notwithstanding, some investigations have reported tremendous success when aspirin was initiated before eight weeks [29, 58] or before conception [59]. The delayed initiation could either be a result of late antenatal care attendance by the mothers, as previously noted in the region [60], or the absence of the necessary screening tools within the antenatal clinics of the study site, as noted by the investigators of this study. Therefore, building the capacity of the Community Health Extension Workers (CHEWs) and Village Health Teams (VHTs) in sensitizing and mobilizing pregnant women for early antenatal care and incorporating the screening tool on the antenatal card can help to address the problem.

Resilient therapeutic optimism

Despite using LDA, the study found that the majority of women progressed to pre-eclampsia. Only two of the women were successful with the therapy. Nonetheless, most of them showed willingness to use the drug again in subsequent pregnancies. Their willingness to use aspirin in future pregnancies suggested that the perceived benefits of preventing complications outweighed the immediate barriers experienced during its use. It is worth noting that the two women who were successful with the therapy had unique characteristics from the rest. They had used a higher dose of 150 mg daily at night, started early at 12 and 14 weeks, received comprehensive counselling and were followed up for adherence support. On the other hand, most of the women who developed preeclampsia had used 75 mg of aspirin, which has been reported to have a low effectiveness in some populations [25]. Several other studies have found the effectiveness of aspirin to be dose-dependent, with doses ≥ 100 mg producing better effects [61, 62]. The high failure rate may create a barrier to aspirin use if not investigated and addressed, since existing theories indicate that individuals only use preventive therapies if they believe that they will help to prevent the potential problem [41]. Therefore, future studies should investigate the effectiveness of the 75 mg dosage in the population of Southwestern Uganda.

Side effects-based non-adherence

The study found that side effects prompted women to stop using aspirin, representing a significant barrier to the successful utilization of the therapy. Similar to the findings of Shanmugalingam et al. (2020), this study noted that side effects such as epigastric pain, palpitations, and breathlessness discouraged women from using aspirin. Although low doses of aspirin, such as 75 mg, have been reported to be safe [63], some studies have reported similar doses to cause injury to the gastric mucosa in some populations [64–66]. Nonetheless, Helicobacter pylori infection (the common cause of peptic ulcer disease) is very common in Uganda and has been reported in almost half of all pregnant women attending the antenatal clinic at the study site [67]. The high prevalence of peptic ulcer disease among pregnant women in the region likely accounts for the frequent reports of discomfort while using aspirin. Because aspirin can aggravate gastrointestinal distress, it is crucial to identify women with active peptic ulcer disease before initiating the therapy. Managing these gastric symptoms and other related concerns is vital to reducing the physical barriers that may prevent women from completing their prescribed regimen.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study highlights perceived barriers and implementation challenges that may contribute to suboptimal uptake of aspirin prophylaxis and the high prevalence of preeclampsia in Southwestern Uganda. These include limited education of at-risk women on aspirin prophylaxis, late initiation of the preventive therapy and side effects of the drug. These challenges can be overcome by regular capacity building of health workers who care for pregnant women, community education, incorporating a screening tool in the antenatal card, following up women to ensure medication adherence and strengthening the health systems to foster early antenatal care for all pregnant women. Future research should establish the population-specific risk factors for southwestern Uganda to help adjust the national screening tool to address preeclampsia at regional level.

Limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first investigation into the experiences of women prescribed low-dose aspirin during pregnancy in Southwestern Uganda. However, its single-center design imposes inherent sampling limitations. Therefore, its ability to represent other facilities could be limited. Given that low-dose aspirin is a new intervention in the country, further studies across multiple facilities are necessary to gain a broader understanding of women’s experiences with the therapy.
