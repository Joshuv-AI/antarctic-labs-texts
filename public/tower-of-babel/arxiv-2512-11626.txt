Abstract
. In a previous work, the regular cosmological volume function τ V \tau_{V} was introduced as an alternative to the regular cosmological time function of Andersson, Galloway, and Howard. In this paper, we show that in many cases of interest, τ V \tau_{V} is a continuously differentiable temporal function. This leads to a canonical splitting of the metric tensor, and induces a canonical “Wick-rotated” Riemannian metric. We also provide some further results and examples related to the cosmological time and volume functions.



1.
Introduction

Two characteristic features of General Relativity are the lack of an observer independent notion of time, and the central role played by singularities. Of these, the second can sometimes relieve the first. For instance, Wald and Yip
[
21
]
proposed that inside of a black hole, one can assign a natural time coordinate to any event via the
maximum lifetime function
, given by the maximum proper time that an observer starting at the event can live, before falling into the singularity. Time-dually, Andersson, Galloway, and Howard
[
1
]
proposed to measure time on a cosmological spacetime
(M,g)
as the maximum proper time that an observer at a given point
p
can have experienced since the Big Bang. This yields the
cosmological time function

\tau(p):=\sup\{L_{g}(\gamma)\mid\gamma\text{ a past-directed causal curve starting at }p\}.

Both constructions rely on the relevant spacetime singularity manifesting itself through (future or past) timelike geodesic incompleteness. Other notions of incompleteness exist, such as bounded acceleration incompleteness, and volume incompleteness. The latter was recently introduced by the author in
[
13
]
, and is defined as the presence of points
p\in M
with
\operatorname{vol}_{g}(I^{\pm}(p))
arbitrarily small (in particular, finite). Here
\operatorname{vol}_{g}
is the canonical volume measure induced by the metric
g
, and
I^{\pm}(p)
the chronological future/past of
p
. On past-volume incomplete spacetimes, it is then natural to define the
cosmological volume function

[
13
, Def. 6.1]

\tau_{V}(p):=\operatorname{vol}_{g}(I^{-}(p)).

The idea of defining time by measuring
I^{-}(p)
goes back to Geroch
[
14
]
, and is, by now, standard. In general, however, the canonical volume measure
\operatorname{vol}_{g}
can be infinite. This is usually circumvented by choosing a different measure (e.g. by adding a weight), but the choice of such a finite measure is completely arbitrary. Chruściel, Grant, and Minguzzi
[
7
]
showed that one can always find a (still highly non-unique) weighted measure
\mu
such that
p\mapsto\mu(I^{-}(p))
is a
C^{1}
temporal function. Simply choosing the weight to be equal
1
does not always work, however, even if
\tau_{V}
is finite.

In this paper, we identify two settings of interest where
\tau_{V}
is a
C^{1}
temporal function, and hence a canonical one at that, since its definition only involves notions directly derived from the metric
g
.

(i)

When
(M,g)
is the future Cauchy development of some initial data. In this case, time is measured from the initial data, rather than from the Big Bang.

(ii)

A cosmological setting, where
(M,g)
is past volume incomplete and has no past observer horizons. The second assumption means that
I^{+}(\gamma)=M
for every past inextendible causal curve
\gamma
.

See Example
3.7
for a concrete model where (ii) is satisfied. The precise statements of the results are as follows, and the proofs rely on adapting the arguments in
[
7
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(M,g)
be a spacetime and
\mathcal{S}
a future Cauchy surface. Then the cosmological volume function
\tau_{V}
of the spacetime
(I^{+}(\mathcal{S}),g)
is regular and is a
C^{1}
temporal function.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
(M,g)
be a causal spacetime with no past observer horizons, and let its cosmological volume function
\tau_{V}
be finite. Then
\tau_{V}
is regular and is a
C^{1}
temporal function.

Note that
\mathcal{S}
being future Cauchy implies that it is a codimension
1
submanifold of class
C^{0}
, and we are requiring no further differentiability. That
\tau_{V}
is
C^{1}
implies that
\mathrm{d}\tau_{V}
is a continuous one-form field, and we can thus rewrite the metric tensor
g
as

g=-\beta(\mathrm{d}\tau_{V})^{2}+h,

where
\beta
is a
C^{0}
function, and
h
induces a
C^{0}
Riemannian metric on the level sets of
\tau
and vanishes on their
g
-orthogonal complement. We can thus flip the minus sign to obtain the Wick-rotated or Riemannianized metric

g_{R}:=\beta(\mathrm{d}\tau_{V})^{2}+h.

Continuous metric tensors have been extensively studied. In the Lorentzian case, causal bubbles can appear
[
8
]
, but this is not a concern here, since the causal structure is that of the smooth metric
g
. In the Riemannian case, the usual notions of length and distance are still well-behaved
[
5
]
.

One can find many Riemannian metrics on any manifold, and even require properties such as smoothness of completeness, which
g_{R}
generally lacks. The important point here is that
g_{R}
is constructed only out of the Lorentzian metric
g
, without making any arbitrary choices. This makes it well suited for the study of convergence of sequences
(M_{i},g_{i})
of spacetimes via any of the established notions of convergence for metric spaces (e.g. Gromov--Hausdorff). A related approach is that of the null distance by Sormani and Vega
1
1

1

See
[
20
]
and later works. In particular,
[
18
, Rem. 2.15]
explains that the original idea was to perform Wick rotation with respect to the cosmological time function
\tau
. In general, however, given that
\tau
is only differentiable almost everywhere, the resulting
g_{R}
is also only defined almost everywhere.
. The null distance
\hat{d}_{\tau}
also depends on a choice of time function
\tau
, but is able to deal with non-differentiable ones, and can encode the causality of the spacetime in certain cases
[
17
,
4
]
. Even if
\tau
is temporal,
\hat{d}_{\tau}
does not coincide with the distance induced by
g_{R}
, but the two are closely related. Returning to Riemannianized metric tensors
g_{R}
, a study of their convergence has recently been performed by Burgos, Flores, and Sánchez
[
3
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout the paper,
(M,g)
will be a
(n+1)
-dimensional time-oriented Lorentzian manifold. Except where specified otherwise, we assume the metric tensor
g
to be of regularity
C^{2,1}
, so that all of standard causality theory and the results of
[
7
]
are applicable. We denote by
L_{g}
and
d_{g}
the Lorentzian length and distance, respectively. By
\operatorname{dvol}_{g}
or
\operatorname{dvol}_{g}^{n+1}
we denote the canonical volume
(n+1)
-form associated to
g
, and by
\operatorname{vol}_{g}(A):=\int_{A}\operatorname{dvol}_{g}
the volume of a set
A\subset M
.

We assume some familiarity with spacetime geometry, but recall the following, perhaps less well-known definition.

Definition 2.1
.

A closed, acausal, edgeless subset
\mathcal{S}\subset M
is called a
future Cauchy surface
if every past-directed past-inextendible causal curve
\gamma\colon[0,\infty)\to M
with
p\in I^{+}(\mathcal{S})
intersects
\mathcal{S}
exactly once.

The defining condition can be equivalently stated as the future Cauchy horizon
H^{+}(\mathcal{S})
being empty. The usual Cauchy surfaces are a special case (they are exactly those sets which are simultaneously future and past Cauchy).

Recall that the
cosmological time function
of Andersson, Galloway, and Howard is defined as

\tau(p):=\sup\{L_{g}(\gamma)\mid\gamma\text{ past-directed causal starting at }p\},

and called
regular

[
1
, Def. 1.1]
if it satisfies:

•

For all points
p\in M
,
\tau(p)<\infty
.

•

For all past-directed past-inextendible causal curves
\gamma\colon[0,\infty)\to M
,
\tau\circ\gamma(s)\to 0
as
s\to\infty
.

It enjoys the following properties.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
1
, Thm. 1.2 & Cor. 2.6]
)
.

Suppose
(M,g)
is a spacetime with regular cosmological time function
\tau
. Then the following properties hold:

(i)

(M,g)
is globally hyperbolic.

(ii)

\tau
is continuous and is strictly increasing along future directed causal curves.

(iii)

The level sets of
\tau
are future Cauchy.

(iv)

For each
p\in M
there is a past-directed unit-speed timelike geodesic
\gamma_{p}:[0,\tau(p))\to M
such that

\gamma_{p}(0)=p,\qquad\tau(\gamma_{p}(t))=\tau(p)-t,\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,\tau(p)).

(v)

The tangent vectors
\dot{\gamma}_{p}(0)
are locally bounded away from the lightcones. More precisely, if
K\subseteq M
is compact then
\{\dot{\gamma}_{p}(\tau(p)):p\in K\}
is compact in the tangent bundle
TM
.

(vi)

\tau
is locally Lipschitz and its first and second derivatives exist almost everywhere.

A similar result exists for
regular cosmological volume functions

[
13
, Def. 6.1]
,

\tau_{V}(p):=\operatorname{vol}_{g}\left(I^{-}(p)\right),

regularity
again meaning that
\tau_{V}
is finite and
\tau_{V}\to 0
along all past-inextendible causal curves. On globally hyperbolic spacetimes, the second requirement is redundant.

Proposition 2.3

(Prop. 1 in
[
10
]
)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a globally hyperbolic spacetime with
\tau_{V}(p)<\infty
for every
p\in M
. Then
\tau_{V}
is regular.

The following parts of Theorem
2.2
hold also for
\tau_{V}
.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
13
, Thms. 6.3 & 6.4]
)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a spacetime with regular cosmological volume function
\tau_{V}
. Then

(i)

(M,g)
is globally hyperbolic.

(ii)

\tau_{V}
is continuous and is strictly increasing along future directed causal curves.

(iii)

The level sets of
\tau_{V}
are future Cauchy.

Points (i)-(iii) of both theorems are identical. Points (iv) and (v) do not make sense for the cosmological volume function, since
\tau_{V}(p)
is, by definition, realized by the set
I^{-}(p)
. Example
3.9
below has a non-Lipschitz
\tau_{V}
. Thus (vi) is not true for
\tau_{V}
in general, but Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2
establish that
\tau_{V}
is even
C^{1}
and temporal in many cases of interest. Recall that any function satisfying (ii) is called a
time function
, while the notion of
temporal function
is stronger, requiring differentiability with past-directed timelike gradient.

3.
Differentiability of the cosmological volume function

In this section, we prove the main result and some corollaries, and provide related examples.

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Recall that we want to study
\tau_{V}
on
I^{+}(\mathcal{S})
for a Cauchy surface
\mathcal{S}
, so in other words, the function

\tau_{V}(p)=\operatorname{vol}_{g}(I^{-}(p)\cap I^{+}(\mathcal{S}))=\int_{I^{-}(p)\cap I^{+}(\mathcal{S})}\operatorname{dvol}_{g}.

We may restrict our spacetime to the interior of the domain of dependence
2
2

2

See e.g.
[
16
, Chap. 6]
for details on domains of dependence.
of
\mathcal{S}
, without altering
\tau_{V}
. This way, without loss of generality, we assume that
(M,g)
is globally hyperbolic and
\mathcal{S}
is a true Cauchy surface. Regularity of
\tau_{V}
is immediate: for every
p\in I^{+}(p)
, the set
I^{-}(p)\cap I^{+}(\mathcal{S})
is compact, hence of finite volume, and on globally hyperbolic spacetimes, finiteness of
\tau_{V}
already implies regularity by Proposition
2.3
.

Our proof of differentiability relies heavily on that of the related result for weighted measures by Chruściel, Grant, and Minguzzi
[
7
]
. Since the goal is to differentiate an integral, one can guess the expected outcome using the Leibniz integral rule
3

3

This is not exactly how it is presented in
[

[

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\biggr|_{s=0}\int_{\Omega(s)}\omega(s)=\int_{\Omega(0)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\biggr|_{s=0}\omega(s)+\int_{\partial\Omega(0)}V\lrcorner\omega(0).

Here
V
denotes the “velocity” of the boundary. In our case,
\omega=\operatorname{dvol}_{g}
is independent of the parameter
s
, so only the boundary term is relevant, and
\Omega(s)=I^{-}(\gamma(s))
for some curve
\gamma
. Since
\partial I^{-}(\gamma(s))
is ruled by null geodesics emanating from
\gamma(s)
, we may obtain the velocity
V
as a Jacobi field, given that the solutions of the geodesic equation depend smoothly on initial data. Care has to be taken when dealing with the null cut locus, but since it has measure zero, it ultimately does not contribute
4

4

This is in contrast with the regular cosmological time function

.

Let us begin by introducing some notation, following
[
7
]
. We fix an auxiliary complete Riemannian metric
h

M

N_{h}^{-}I^{+}(\mathcal{S})

h

Lemma 3.1
.

The function
t_{\mathcal{S}}\colon N_{h}^{-}I^{+}(\mathcal{S})\to[0,\infty)
given by

(1)

t_{\mathcal{S}}(\gamma):=\{t\in[0,\infty)\mid\gamma(t)\in\mathcal{S}\}

is continuous.

Proof.

Let
\gamma_{i}\colon[0,\infty)\to M
be a sequence of past-directed null half-geodesics parametrized by
h
-arclength such that
\gamma_{i}(0)\to\gamma(0)
and
\dot{\gamma}_{i}(0)\to\dot{\gamma}(0)
as
i\to\infty
. First, we show that
t_{\mathcal{S}}
is uniformly bounded above along the sequence
\gamma_{i}
. To this end, let
p
be such that
\gamma(0)\in I^{-}(p)
. Then, since
I^{-}(p)
is open, also
\gamma_{i}(0)\in I^{-}(p)
for all
i
large enough. By global hyperbolicity, the set
K=J^{-}(p)\cap J^{+}(\mathcal{S})
is compact, and
(M,g)
is non-totally imprisoning. Hence, the
h

K
is uniformly bounded above. This puts an upper bound on
t_{\mathcal{S}}
.

Continuity now follows if we show that for every subsequence
\gamma_{i_{j}}
, such that
t_{j}:=t_{\mathcal{S}}(\gamma_{i_{j}})
converges as
j\to\infty
, we have
t_{\infty}:=\lim_{j\to\infty}t_{j}=t_{\mathcal{S}}(\gamma)
. It is easy to see that for such a subsequence
\gamma_{i_{j}}(t_{j})\to\gamma(t_{\infty})
as
j\to\infty
. Since
\mathcal{S}
is closed, and by definition of
t_{j}
,
\gamma_{i_{j}}(t_{j})\in\mathcal{S}
, also
\gamma(t_{\infty})\in\mathcal{S}
. Therefore
t_{\infty}=t_{\mathcal{S}}(\gamma)
.
∎

Next, we define the candidate derivative, and prove that it is continuous. For this, some more notation is needed. Let
E^{-}(p):=J^{-}(p)\setminus I^{-}(p)
. It is well-known that
E^{-}(p)
is ruled by past-directed maximizing null geodesics
\gamma
starting from
p
. For these, the map

t_{-}(\gamma):=\sup\{s\in\mathbb{R}\mid d_{g}(\gamma(s),\gamma(0))=0\}

indicates until which parameter value
\gamma
stays length-maximizing. Moreover, we denote by

\mathring{E}^{-}(p):=\{\gamma(s)\mid\gamma\text{ a past-directed null geodesic, }\gamma(0)=p,s\in(0,t_{-}(\gamma))\}

the set of all points in
E^{-}(p)
that are not a past-endpoint of one of the ruling null geodesics (in other words, we remove the null cut locus). For a vector
Z\in T_{p}M
, we define
L(Z)
to be the vector field on
\mathring{E}^{-}(p)
obtained by solving the Jacobi equation along each null geodesic generator, with initial value
Z
and vanishing initial derivative. Since the Jacobi equation is linear,
L
is a linear map. Finally, let
\pi\colon TM\to M
denote the base-point projection from the tangent bundle to
M

\hat{L}

Lemma 3.2
.

The map

\displaystyle\hat{L}\ \colon\ TI^{+}(\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle\longmapsto\int_{\mathring{E}^{-}(\pi(Z))\cap I^{+}(\mathcal{S})}L(Z)\lrcorner\operatorname{dvol}_{g}

is continuous.

Proof.

Note that
\mathring{E}^{-}(\pi(Z))
is ruled by null geodesics, and that the null cut locus has
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure zero. Hence, up to a set of measure zero, we may parametrize the domain of integration on a subset of
\mathbb{R}\times N^{-}_{h}(\pi(Z))
. For each initial velocity in the
h
-unit past lightcone
N^{-}_{h}(\pi(Z))
, we need to follow the corresponding null geodesic
\gamma\colon[0,\infty)\to M
(parametrized by
h
-arclength) until it reaches the null cut locus or the Cauchy surface
\mathcal{S}
, i.e. until parameter value
b(\gamma)
, where

b\colon N_{h}^{-}I^{+}(\mathcal{S})\to[0,\infty),\quad\gamma\mapsto\min\{t_{-}(\gamma),t_{\mathcal{S}}(\gamma)\}.

Since
t_{-}
and
t_{\mathcal{S}}
are continuous by
[
7

3.1

Z\in TM

N^{-}_{h}I^{+}(\mathcal{S})
on a relatively compact neighborhood
U

\pi(Z)

N^{-}_{h}U\longrightarrow S^{n-1}\times U,\quad\gamma\longmapsto\left(\frac{\dot{\gamma}(0)}{h_{\gamma(0)}(\dot{\gamma}(0),\dot{\gamma}(0))},\gamma(0)\right).

We can thus write
b=b(\theta,p)
as a function of variables
\nu\in S^{n-1}

p\in U

U

S^{n-1}

b

\pi^{-1}(U)

L(Z)

\hat{L}(Z)=\int_{S^{n-1}}\int_{0}^{b(\nu,\pi(Z))}(L(Z)\lrcorner\operatorname{dvol}_{g})_{1...n}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\nu,

\hat{L}

∎

Finally, we show that the candidate derivative is, indeed, the derivative.

Lemma 3.3
.

The function

\tau_{V}(p):=\int_{I^{-}(p)\cap I^{+}(\mathcal{S})}\operatorname{dvol}_{g}

is a
C^{1}
temporal function with differential

d\tau_{v}(Z)=\hat{L}(Z).

Proof.

Given points
p\in M
,
p^{\prime}\in I^{+}(p)
and a continuous function
f\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
such that
f(x)=1
if
x\leq 1
and
f(x)=0
if
x\geq 2
, we define

\phi_{\varepsilon}(q):=f\left(\frac{d_{h}\left(q,I^{-}(p^{\prime})\cap I^{+}(\mathcal{S})\right)}{\varepsilon}\right).

Here
d_{h}
denotes the distance w.r.t. the complete Riemannian metric
h
. Then, for all
\varepsilon>0
small enough,
\phi_{\varepsilon}
has compact support, so by
[
7

\tau_{\phi_{\varepsilon}}(q):=\int_{I^{-}(q)}{\phi_{\varepsilon}}\operatorname{dvol}_{g}

are

\mathrm{d}\tau_{\phi_{\varepsilon}}(Z)=\int_{\mathring{E}^{-}(\pi(Z))}\phi_{\varepsilon}L(Z)\lrcorner\operatorname{dvol}_{g}.

Hence, for any curve
\gamma
with
\gamma(0)=p
and
s_{1}<s_{2}
small enough so that
\gamma([s_{1},s_{2}])\subset I^{-}(p^{\prime})
, we have

\tau_{\phi_{\varepsilon}}(\gamma(s_{2}))-\tau_{\phi_{\varepsilon}}(\gamma(s_{1}))=\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}\left(\int_{\mathring{E}^{-}(\gamma(s))}\phi_{\varepsilon}L(\dot{\gamma}(s))\lrcorner\operatorname{dvol}_{g}\right)\mathrm{d}s.

Take the limit

and that

\tau_{V}(\gamma(s_{2}))-\tau_{V}(\gamma(s_{1}))=\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}\left(\int_{\mathring{E}^{-}(\gamma(s))\cap I^{+}(\mathcal{S})}L(\dot{\gamma}(s))\lrcorner\operatorname{dvol}_{g}\right)\mathrm{d}s.

By Lemma

∎

3.2.
Corollaries

The first corollary implies the ones further below, and is also interesting on its own.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
(M,g)
be a spacetime with regular cosmological time
\tau_{V}
. If
p\in M
is a point such that
I^{-}(p)
contains a future Cauchy surface
\mathcal{S}
, then
\tau_{V}
is
C^{1}
with timelike gradient in a neighborhood of
p
.

Proof.

Since
I^{-}(p)\cap\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}
is compact, and chronological pasts are open, it is easy to infer the existence of a neighborhood
U
of
p
such that
\mathcal{S}\subset I^{-}(q)
for every
q\in U
. Then

\tau_{V}(q)=\operatorname{vol}_{g}(I^{-}(q)\cap I^{+}(\mathcal{S}))+\operatorname{vol}_{g}(I^{-}(\mathcal{S})),

where the first summand is
C^{1}
by Theorem
1.1
, and the second summand is constant.
∎

In order to prove Corollary
1.2
, we show that every point has an entire Cauchy surface in its past. This is a consequence of the characterization of the No Past Observer Horizons (NPOH) condition

\tau_{V}
of the spacetime

Proof.

By the time-reverse of Lemma

We start with an example to motivate the relevance of Corollary

Example 3.7

Let

4.
Comparison of cosmological time and volume functions

In this section, we provide further results and examples to compare the properties of
\tau
and
\tau_{V}
.

4.1.
Restriction to subsets

The cosmological time function
\tau
behaves well with respect to restriction to certain subsets of
M
. In particular, if
\mathcal{S}=\{\tau=c\}
for some
c>0
, and
\tilde{\tau}
is the cosmological time function of
I^{+}(\mathcal{S})
(viewed as a spacetime of its own), then it is easy to prove that
\tilde{\tau}
is also regular, and

\tilde{\tau}(p)=\tau(p)-c\quad\text{for all }p\in I^{+}(\mathcal{S}).

An analogous result does not exist for the cosmological volume function, since the portion of the volume of
I^{-}(p)\cap J^{-}(\mathcal{S})
varies with
p
even if
p\in I^{+}(\mathcal{S})
(except in the special case when
\mathcal{S}\subset I^{-}(p)
, which is treated in Corollary
3.4
).

4.2.
Regularity

Recall that regularity of the cosmological time or volume function means that it is finite and tends to zero along all past-inextendible causal curves. It is a crucial property needed to ensure that these are even time functions in the usual sense (i.e. continuous and strictly increasing along all future-directed causal curves). Using volume comparison techniques recently developed by Cavalletti and Mondino
[
6
]
, we identify conditions such that regularity of
\tau
implies regularity of
\tau_{V}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(M,g)
have a regular cosmological time function
\tau
, compact Cauchy surfaces, and
\operatorname{Ric}\geq-\kappa g
in timelike directions, for some constant
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
(M,g)
has a regular cosmological volume function
\tau_{V}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.3
below, there is some
b>0
such that
\mathcal{S}:=\{p\mid\tau(p)=b\}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}:=\{p\mid\tau(p)=b/2\}
are Cauchy surfaces. Let
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{t}:=\{p\mid d(p,\mathcal{S})=t\}
. The function
d(\cdot,\mathcal{S})
is continuous (see
[
11
, Lem. 3.4]
, time-reversed), and hence reaches a minimum value
a>0
on the compact set
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. Thus
D:=\{p\mid 0\leq d(p,\mathcal{S})\leq a\}
is contained between
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, and is compact. Denote by
\operatorname{vol}_{g}^{n}
the
n
-dimensional measure of a hypersurface. We apply the coarea formula to obtain

\int_{I^{-}(\mathcal{S}_{b})}\operatorname{dvol}_{g}^{n+1}=\int_{0}^{a}\operatorname{vol^{n}_{g}}(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{a}^{b}\operatorname{vol^{n}_{g}}(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t.

The first term on the right hand side is finite, because it is simply the
n+1
-dimensional volume of the compact region
D
. To show that also the second term is finite, we use
[
6
, Thm. 5.1]
, which states that

t\longmapsto\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{g}^{n}(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{t})}{\mathfrak{s}_{\kappa}(t)}

is non-increasing, for

\mathfrak{s}_{\kappa}(t):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\sin(\sqrt{\kappa}t)&\text{if }\kappa>0,\\
t&\text{if }\kappa=0,\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{-\kappa}}\sinh(\sqrt{-\kappa}t)&\text{if }\kappa<0.\\
\end{cases}

It follows that

\int_{a}^{b}\operatorname{vol^{n}_{g}}(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{g}^{n}(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{a})}{\mathfrak{s}_{\kappa}(a)}\int_{a}^{b}\mathfrak{s}_{\kappa}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

is finite. Since
(M,g)
is globally hyperbolic, finiteness of
\tau_{V}
implies regularity by Proposition
2.3
.
∎

4.3.
Cauchyness

Theorem
2.2
(iii) establishes that the level sets
\mathcal{S}_{t}:=\{\tau=t\}
of the cosmological time function are future Cauchy (Definition
2.1
). The question of when they are Cauchy was first investigated in
[
10
]
.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
10
, Thm. 5]
)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a future timelike geodesically complete spacetime satisfying at least one of the following:

(i)

(M,g)
contains a compact Cauchy surface.

(ii)

The future causal boundary of
(M,g)
is spacelike.

If the cosmological time function
\tau\colon M\to(0,\infty)
is regular, then its level sets are Cauchy surfaces.

It remained open if assumptions (i)-(ii) can be removed. Example
4.4
below gives a negative answer. Example 1 in
[
10
]
illustrates that future timelike completeness can also not be removed. The next proposition, however, shows that if we are only interested in small times, then spatial compactness alone suffices. This is also true for the cosmological volume function. The proof is adapted from that of
[
12
, Thm. 4.2]
.

Proposition 4.3
.

If
(M,g)
is globally hyperbolic with compact Cauchy surfaces and admits a regular cosmological time or volume function
\tau
. Then there exists
a>0
such that
\{\tau=t\}
is a Cauchy surface for every
0<t\leq a
.

Proof.

Let
S
be a Cauchy surface. By compactness,
\tau
achieves a minimum value
a
on
S
. Since every inextendible causal curve, say
\gamma\colon I\to M
, intersects
S
, there is
s\in I
such that
\tau\circ\gamma(s)\geq a
. By regularity,
\tau\circ\gamma\to 0
, and it follows that all values in
(0,a]
are achieved. Hence the corresponding
\tau
-level sets are Cauchy surfaces.
∎

The following example is adapted from a very similar one in
[
1

Let

4.4.
Cosmological cut locus

Given a smooth, spacelike Cauchy surface
\mathcal{S}
, the Lorentzian distance to
\mathcal{S}
coincides with the cosmological time function of the spacetime
I^{+}(\mathcal{S})
, and is regular. Hence
\tau
is smooth in a vicinity of
\mathcal{S}
, to be precise, away from the cut locus. See
[
15
]
for further discussion of cosmological time functions that arise in this way. Note also that Theorem
1.1
deals with the cosmological volume function in the same setting.

Now, let
(M,g)
be an arbitrary spacetime with regular cosmological time
\tau
. One can try to think of
\tau
as the distance to some “initial singularity”. In particular, one may define a set analogous to the cut locus to a hypersurface, as follows.

Definition 4.5
.

We define the subset
\mathcal{I}\subset M
by declaring that
p\in\mathcal{I}
if there is
\varepsilon>0
and a past-directed unit-speed timelike geodesic
\gamma_{p}\colon(-\varepsilon,\tau(p))\to M
with
\gamma_{p}(0)=p
such that
\tau(\gamma(s))=\tau(p)-s
for all
s\in(-\varepsilon,\tau(p))
. We define the
cosmological cut locus
as
\mathcal{C}:=M\setminus\mathcal{I}
.

Theorem
2.2
(iv) tells us that a geodesic
\gamma_{p}
as in the definition can always be defined on
[0,\tau(p))
, so the non-trivial requirement for
p\in\mathcal{I}
is that
\gamma_{p}
can be prolonged for some short time
\varepsilon
while retaining the maximization property. The following facts are easy to establish:

•

\mathcal{I}
is dense in
M
.

•

If
p\in\mathcal{I}
, then the geodesic
\gamma_{p}\colon[0,\tau(p))\to M
provided by Theorem
2.2
(iv) is unique.

To prove the first statement, take any point
p\in M
, and the curve
\gamma_{p}
given by Theorem
2.2
(iv). Then
\gamma_{p}((0,\tau(p))\subset\mathcal{I}
, so
p\in\overline{I}
. The second statement is a direct consequence of the fact that geodesics in smooth spacetimes cannot branch. A related result proven by Sormani and Vega
[
20
, Prop. 5.3]
is the following:

•

If
\tau
is differentiable at
p
, then its gradient vector equals
\dot{\gamma}_{p}(0)
for
\gamma_{p}
as in Theorem
2.2
(iv), and moreover,
\gamma_{p}
is unique.

In particular, it follows that the gradient of
\tau
is past-directed unit timelike wherever it exists. Note that the gradient of
\tau_{V}
is also past-directed timelike where defined (see proof of Theorem
1.1
and
[
7
, p. 2810]
), but it will not be unit in most cases.

The above discussion raises two natural questions:

•

Is there an implication between
p\in\mathcal{I}
and differentiability of
\tau
at
p
?

•

Is
\mathcal{C}
closed?

Recall that the usual cut locus is closed. We leave the first question open, and give a negative answer to the second one, via Example
4.7
. We first give a preliminary example where
\mathcal{I}\neq M
.

Example 4.6
.

Consider a function of one variable of the form

f(x):=\begin{cases}2-\sqrt{1+x^{2}}&\text{if }0\leq|x|\leq 1,\\
0&\text{if }2\leq|x|,\\
\text{smoothly interpolated}&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Let
M:=\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1,1}\mid t>f(x)\}
be equipped with the Minkoswki metric
-\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\mathrm{d}x^{2}
. The graph of
f
for
0\leq|x|\leq 1
coincides with the hyperboloid of points that have time separation
1
from
p:=(2,0)
. From this, it is easy to deduce that for the curve
\gamma\colon s\mapsto(s,0)
, we have the following behavior:

•

For
0<s<1
,
\gamma(s)\in\mathcal{I}
and
\gamma
is the unique
\tau
-realizing curve.

•

For
s=1
,
\gamma(1)\not\in\mathcal{I}
and there are infinitely many
\tau
-realizers (including
\gamma
).

•

For
s>1

\gamma(s)\not\in\mathcal{I}
,
\gamma
is not a
\tau
-realizer, and there exist two
\tau
-realizers (one in the left and one in the right half-plane).

\mathcal{C}

for all
