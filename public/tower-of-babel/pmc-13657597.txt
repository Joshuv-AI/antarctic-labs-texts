Introduction

Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST) is a rare, life-threatening cause of stroke predominantly affecting young women. While pregnancy induces a hypercoagulable state, the incidence of pregnancy-associated CVST remains low, estimated at 1 to 12 per 100 000 deliveries. Most cases occur in the third trimester or postpartum; first-trimester presentations are particularly uncommon[1].

Despite its clinical significance, pregnancy-specific epidemiological data and consensus guidelines remain limited[2]. The literature often groups pregnancy-associated cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT) with other forms of venous thromboembolism, obscuring the unique pathophysiology and therapeutic requirements of this population[3]. Anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin is the established first-line therapy, yet optimal dosing and management strategies for refractory cases remain uncertain[2].

Case presentation

A 20-year-old primigravida at 6 weeks’ gestation presented with a 3-day history of 3–4 episodes of non-bilious, non-bloody vomiting. This was followed by episodes of generalized stiffening of the body with upward rolling of the eyes, each lasting approximately 10 seconds, without loss of consciousness. She experienced four such episodes over 30 minutes, followed by a generalized headache. There was no prior history of seizures, fever, trauma, or substance use. There was no significant past medical or drug history. No family history of cerebrovascular events was elicited.

On examination, her weight was 42 kg and her Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 14/15 (Eye: 3, Verbal: 5, Motor: 6). Vital signs were stable. Neurological examination revealed reduced power in the right lower limb (3/5). Plantar responses were down-going bilaterally. There were no cranial nerve deficits, cerebellar signs, or dysarthria. Given the acute focal deficit with seizures, the stroke code was activated.

Baseline laboratory investigations–including complete blood count, renal and liver function tests, electrolytes, and lipid profile–were within normal limits. Transthoracic echocardiography and Doppler ultrasonography of the carotid and vertebral arteries bilaterally revealed no hemodynamically significant stenosis or occlusion.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain with venography demonstrated superior sagittal sinus thrombosis with multiple venous infarcts and areas of hemorrhagic transformation (Fig. 1). Autoimmune workup, including antinuclear antibody (ANA) and antiphospholipid antibody (APLA) panels, was negative. Figure 1.MRI with venography before thrombectomy.

A multidisciplinary team comprising a neurologist, an interventional radiologist, and an obstetrician was involved in the management. Therapeutic anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin at 40 mg twice daily (1 mg/kg twice daily) was used as a practical therapeutic dose, based on her 42 kg body weight and normal renal function. Hypertonic saline (3% NaCl) was administered three times daily to control cerebral edema.

The patient was counseled regarding the disease, maternal and fetal risks, and treatment options. As the pregnancy was unplanned, a medical termination of pregnancy (MTP) was advised; however, it was delayed by 2 weeks due to patient-related factors.

Given the extensive superior sagittal sinus clot burden, hemorrhagic venous infarction, and neurological deficit, mechanical thrombectomy was performed as rescue therapy after initiation of therapeutic anticoagulation. Multiple thromboaspiration passes retrieved approximately 25 cm of thrombus, resulting in successful recanalization of the superior sagittal sinus (Figs 2 and 3). Post-procedure, the patient was monitored in the intensive care unit for 24 hours, with an uneventful course. A repeat non-contrast CT scan of the brain showed heterogeneous residual hyperdensities in the superior sagittal sinus, right transverse sinus, and cortical veins, with bilateral frontal lobe white matter edema and small haemorrhagic foci.

She was discharged on day-3 post-thrombectomy on twice-daily LMWH and oral levetiracetam 1 g twice daily. At the 2-week follow-up, the patient demonstrated complete neurological recovery, with full return of muscle strength and no residual deficits. LMWH (enoxaparin 40 mg twice daily) was continued until MTP. After the pregnancy was terminated, it was planned to continue prophylactic anticoagulation at the adult CVST dose because mechanical thrombectomy removes the existing clot burden but does not eliminate the need for ongoing anticoagulation to prevent recurrent thrombosis. Thrombophilia screening could not be performed at that time because of financial constraints.

Discussion

This case report describes superior sagittal sinus thrombosis occurring in early pregnancy, a relatively uncommon presentation, as pregnancy-associated CVST more frequently occurs in late gestation or the postpartum period[4].

Prompt diagnosis was achieved using MRI with venography, enabling early initiation of appropriate therapy. Management was undertaken through a multidisciplinary approach involving neurology, interventional radiology, and obstetrics, which is essential in the care of complex cerebrovascular conditions during pregnancy. Importantly, this case contributes to the limited existing evidence supporting the feasibility and safety of mechanical thrombectomy during pregnancy[5]. The achievement of successful superior sagittal sinus recanalization with complete neurological recovery supports consideration of endovascular intervention in carefully selected patients.

As a single-patient case report, the findings are inherently limited in generalizability and cannot establish causality or treatment superiority. A complete thrombophilia workup and screening for occult malignancy, which could have identified potential causes of thrombosis, were not performed in this case due to patient-related factors. In addition, termination of pregnancy was planned during the disease course, which may have influenced both maternal outcomes and anticoagulation decisions, limiting extrapolation to cases in which pregnancy continues.

CVST results from thrombus formation within the cerebral venous sinuses, leading to impaired venous outflow, increased intracranial venous pressure, and subsequent cerebral edema or hemorrhage[6].

Pregnancy and the postpartum period are well-recognized risk factors for stroke due to the physiological hypercoagulable state that develops during gestation and the puerperium, caused by elevated fibrinogen levels, reduced protein S activity, and haemostatic changes related to labor, trauma, or hemorrhage[7,8]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis examining CVST during pregnancy and the postpartum period found that the pooled incidence of CVST was approximately seven cases per 100 000 deliveries, with significant regional variability, underscoring its rarity even in high-risk populations. Moreover, the incidence remains highest in the postpartum period compared with pregnancy itself[2]. Seventy per cent of pregnant patients with CVST in the systematic review were primigravid, as was our patient[9]. This suggests that the risk of CVST might be increased during the first pregnancy.

CVST during pregnancy most commonly presents with nonspecific symptoms such as headache, seizures, or focal neurological deficits – features that may overlap with other pregnancy-related conditions and contribute to diagnostic delays[10]. Early recognition with advanced neuroimaging modalities, such as MRI with venography, is critical, as illustrated by our case in which prompt imaging led to timely diagnosis of superior sagittal sinus thrombosis with hemorrhagic infarcts.

Anticoagulation with LMWH remains the first-line therapy for CVST in pregnancy due to its favorable safety profile and lack of teratogenicity. Several observational studies and systematic reviews support therapeutic anticoagulation as the cornerstone of management, with most pregnant patients responding favorably when it is instituted early[9]. However, a subset of patients, particularly those with extensive clot burden, progressive neurological deficits, or evidence of raised intracranial pressure, may not improve sufficiently with anticoagulation alone.

The role of endovascular therapy, including mechanical thrombectomy, for CVST is evolving. While randomized controlled trials in the general CVST population have not demonstrated definitive superiority of endovascular intervention over anticoagulation alone, existing guidelines acknowledge that the overall evidence base is limited and of low quality and recommend that mechanical thrombectomy and other endovascular modalities be considered only in selected patients who fail to respond to adequate anticoagulation or who are at risk of clinical deterioration, as a rescue therapy. Importantly, these recommendations are derived from aggregated data from non-pregnant populations, and pregnancy-specific evidence is scarce[11].

In the context of pregnancy-associated CVST, published case reports and small series suggest that mechanical thrombectomy may be a feasible and potentially beneficial adjunctive therapy when standard anticoagulation fails or when there are severe clinical manifestations. For example, a pregnant patient with superior sagittal sinus and transverse sinus thrombosis treated with stent retriever devices achieved complete neurological recovery after mechanical thrombectomy following an inadequate response to anticoagulation[12].

These reports, while limited in number, support individualized, multidisciplinary decision-making in complex cases such as ours, in which extensive thrombosis, neurological deficits, and hemorrhagic transformation posed a significant risk to maternal neurological function. The most likely cause of CVST in this patient was pregnancy-induced hypercoagulability, given the absence of structural cardiac disease, vascular abnormalities, autoimmune disorders, or antiphospholipid antibodies.

The decision to proceed with mechanical thrombectomy was scientifically justified by the presence of extensive superior sagittal sinus thrombosis, progressive neurological deficits, and hemorrhagic venous infarction, all of which are associated with poor outcomes on anticoagulation alone. Successful mechanical clot retrieval restored venous outflow, reduced intracranial venous pressure, and facilitated rapid neurological recovery, supporting the rationale that timely recanalization can reverse the underlying pathophysiology of venous congestion and edema.

CVST should be considered in pregnant women presenting with seizures, headache, or focal neurological deficits, even in early gestation. Prompt neuroimaging and early anticoagulation are critical for favorable outcomes. This case demonstrates that, in selected patients with extensive thrombosis and neurological deterioration, mechanical thrombectomy can be a safe and effective adjunct to medical therapy during pregnancy. Individualized, multidisciplinary decision-making is essential, and this report contributes to the growing evidence supporting endovascular intervention as a potential option in severe pregnancy-associated CVST. Larger prospective studies are needed to define clear evidence-based recommendations, especially for procedures such as thrombectomy in pregnancy.

Conclusions

CVST in early pregnancy is a rare but serious condition requiring a high index of suspicion in young women presenting with seizures and focal neurological deficits. This case illustrates that timely diagnosis and multidisciplinary, individualized management are essential for favorable outcomes. Although low-molecular-weight heparin remains the cornerstone of therapy, mechanical thrombectomy may serve as a safe and effective adjunct in selected patients with extensive thrombosis, neurological deterioration, or hemorrhagic venous infarction. This report supports the feasibility of endovascular intervention in pregnancy-associated CVST and highlights the need for clearer evidence-based management guidelines.
