The effects of parity on the maternal microbiome - challenges associated with mouse models
Authors: Sondra Turjeman, Soha Zgairy, Omry Koren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding how parity shapes the maternal microbiota is critical for advancing maternal and offspring health. We investigated the effects of parity and spacing between births on the maternal microbiota in mice. While maternal age strongly influenced microbiota diversity and composition, parity and lengths of gaps between births showed minimal impact. These findings highlight key model constraints that must be considered when designing longitudinal microbiome studies within and beyond the context of reproduction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-026-00582-5.

The microbiome fluctuates throughout pregnancy, with decreased alpha diversity being a hallmark of human microbiota dynamics as pregnancy progresses [1–4]. While parity effects have been studied in reproductive and metabolic health [5], their influence on the microbiota remains underexplored.

Recent research has shown that nulliparous women have higher gut microbiota diversity than multiparous women [6], implying that microbiota changes during pregnancy may persist beyond delivery. Furthermore, parity can modulate effects of maternal BMI on the pregnancy microbiota with higher pre-pregnancy BMI linked to greater microbiota stability in pregnancy and postpartum [7]. Despite these insights, few studies explicitly examined, or at least published, effects of parity on the microbiota, and investigations of post-pregnancy microbiota recovery are even rarer. We suggest that the dearth of research may stem from technical constraints of studying long-term host-microbiota phenomena in model systems.

To date, implications for offspring microbiota and development have only been reported in non-murine models: In vervet monkey mother-offspring dyads, low-parity mothers had offspring with lower gut microbiota diversity and higher colonization of maternal milk microbiota taxa, coupled with accelerated infant growth [8]. In contrast, a study in sows reported that offspring of multiparous animals had lower richness than those of primiparous sows [9].

We aimed to investigate how parity with varying gaps between births influences maternal gut (fecal) microbiota; if an effect was found, we planned to examine subsequent consequences for offspring microbiota and development. While previous studies in humans have suggested parity-related microbiota dynamics, findings remain inconsistent and lack rigorous testing.

To address this, we developed a controlled breeding experiment using Swiss Webster mice as our model (all methods are detailed in the online supplement). Specific-pathogen-free (SPF) dams were repeatedly mated with germ-free (GF) males, according to the experimental timeline outlined in Fig. 1a. We used this set-up to minimize the confounding effects of cage-mate coprophagy on maternal microbiota. Specifically, dams were randomly assigned to a baseline group (no intervention) or to be bred repeatedly, post-weaning, with varying gap lengths between litters.

We collected samples from 32 mice over the course of a year, including monthly fecal samples from eight mice, which were never impregnated (Fig. 1a, b “Baseline”). The remaining 24 mice were sampled weekly and mated according to the scheme in Fig. 1a (see online methods). Successful fertilization/live birth did not always follow the experimental design: dams had 1–4 births across the experiment, at various intervals, so we retrospectively considered birth gaps as a continuous variable rather than as an experimental group. To assess how parity (rather than gestation) affected the maternal microbiome, monthly fecal samples from baseline dams and samples collected prior to conception from impregnated dams were characterized using 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and following filtering, 139 samples from 32 dams were included in analyses (Fig. 1b). In all modeling, mouse ID was included as a random factor, and two-tailed tests were used.

While we observed an apparent correlation between alpha diversity and number of previous births (Fig. 1c, Supp Fig. 1a), when utilizing a linear mixed model (LMM) that included both maternal age and previous births, we found that only maternal age (Fig. 1d, Supp Fig. 1b) significantly explained the variance observed in species richness (Fig. 1e). Visualization of alpha diversity trajectories of all mice with 3 previous births further supported the lack of effect of parity on the microbiome (Supp Fig. 2). Comparisons of community composition (beta-diversity, multivariate PERMANOVAs) also revealed significant differences explained by maternal age (Fig. 1f). There was also a smaller effect of number of previous births on composition (Bray-Curtis (BC), Jaccard, and unweighted UniFrac, but not weighted UniFrac): post-hoc testing (FDR-corrected pairwise PERMANOVAs) revealed significant differences between 0 and 1 previous births (BC) and between 0 and 2 previous births (BC, Jaccard, unweighted UniFrac; Fig. 1f). No other post-hoc comparisons were significant (q > 0.05). There were no differences in dispersion of communities by previous births (betadisper, p > 0.05). Differential abundance analyses revealed one unidentified genus “17” in the Rikenellaceae family associated with number of births and 16 taxa associated with maternal age (Supp Fig. 3). These findings led us to conclude that in our model system, age-related effects on the microbiome are stronger than those of parity.

We next examined whether days since the last birth affected the gut microbiota. We did not find a significant difference between number of previous births and days since last birth (LMM; Fig. 2a), but there was an effect of age on gap length (Spearman’s rho; Fig. 2b), suggesting that as age (and parity) increases, there may also be a correlation between parity and gap length. When we examined the correlation between species richness and days since the last birth, no correlation was observed (Spearman’s rho, Fig. 2c).

Our multivariate modeling demonstrated that maternal age, rather than parity, was the primary driver of microbiota diversity and composition in Swiss Webster mice, though small community differences associated with parity were also observed. Additionally, we did not find a correlation between time since the last birth and microbial richness, suggesting that neither parity nor the length of the gap between births influence maternal gut microbiota, despite effects of age on gap length. These findings underscore the dominant role of age in shaping the maternal microbiome within this model system, which may mask smaller, but biologically relevant effects of parity (but see Supp Fig. 2).

In the context of previous research, our findings diverge from patterns in humans, described above. However, in sows, parity was not associated with changes in microbial diversity or community composition, but rather with the rate of community change during pregnancy [10]. Similar to our findings, this study noted that microbial composition at the final pregnancy stage was comparable across nulliparous and multiparous animals, but contrasting our research, age effects on the maternal microbiota were not observed.

While work in animal models allows for tight control of environmental effects, the above study in pigs, alongside mounting evidence in humans and our results, suggests that cross-species generalizations may not always be relevant when considering parity effects on maternal microbiota and offspring microbiota and development. Accordingly, we highlight the notable limitations of murine models for studying parity effects, and other lifetime processes, on the microbiota. First, age effects on the microbiota in mice are known to be particularly strong compared to humans [11], complicating the disentanglement of parity and age. Second, the constraints of mating only post-weaning limit the number of pregnancies a dam can have before reaching non-reproductive age. Introducing males earlier or keeping them in the cage post-weaning is problematic, though, due to the strong influence of coprophagy, which can alter the maternal microbiota and potentially mask parity effects.

To address these challenges, alternative experimental designs could be considered. For example, adding a cohort of mice that begin reproduction later, at 22 or even 34 weeks of age, could help decouple the effects of parity and age. Additionally, modifying husbandry practices to minimize coprophagy (e.g., using mesh-bottom cages) and extending reproductive windows may further improve the relevance of this model. Artificial insemination could also be considered for future research, though associated stress of this procedure and, in some cases, hormone supplementation, could confound microbiota-relevant results. Ultimately, the gold standard would be to track women throughout their reproductive years. However, such studies are very costly and logistically challenging due to the large number of potentially confounding environmental variables, the substantial sample size required, and the long period needed to capture multiple pregnancies.

Our research underscores the limitations of murine models in host-microbiota research, particularly in reproductive biology, and highlights the broader challenge of translating microbiome research from mice to humans and back again [12]. Our results suggest that even well-designed, highly controlled murine studies may be inherently limited when investigating long-term host-microbiota interactions. By sharing these findings, we aim to inform model selection and experimental design in future studies and emphasize the value of publishing negative results as a means of strengthening reproducibility, transparency, and appropriate resource allocation in preclinical and translational microbiome research.
