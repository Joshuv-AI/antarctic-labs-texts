Arthroscopic double-screw Latarjet versus arthroscopic button-fixed Latarjet with Bankart repair
Authors: Shinji Imai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe arthroscopic double-screw (DS) Latarjet procedure has been associated with low recurrence rates, a high sports return rate (SRR), and favorable clinical scores. Arthroscopic button-fixed Latarjet with Bankart repair (BB Latarjet) has been reported recently. Our hypthesis was that there were no statistically significant differences in the graft union rate, SRR, and complication rate between the arthroscopic BB Latarjet and the arthroscopic DS Latarjet, which has been considered advantageous because of its higher initial fixation strength using 2 screws.MethodsBetween 2019 and 2023, 32 patients underwent the arthroscopic BB Latarjet procedure (mean follow-up: 2.5 ± 0.5 years), and 39 patients underwent the arthroscopic DS Latarjet procedure (mean follow-up: 4.4 ± 1.4 years). Clinical outcomes, including visual analog scale scores for pain and instability, Rowe score, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score, University of California, Los Angeles score,Subjective Shoulder Value, and ranges of external rotation, incidence of graft malpositioning, neurological complications, and progression of osteoarthritis, were compared between the 2 groups at the 2-year post-operative time point. Graft union rate and SRR were compared at 3-month, 6-month, 9-month, and 2-year post-operative time point.ResultsNeither group experienced redislocation by the 2-year post-operative time point. No significant differences were observed at 2 years in visual analog scale scores for instability (P = .102) or pain (P = .50), Rowe score (P = .428), American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score (P = .677), or University of California, Los Angeles score (P = .433). At 3 months post-operatively, 32 cases (82.1%) achieved graft union in the DS group, whereas only 11 cases (34.4%) achieved graft union in the BB group (P < .01). However, computed tomography-based evaluation at the 2-year post-operative time point demonstrated no significant difference in graft union rate between the DS group (92.3%) and the BB group (81.3%) (P = .716). At 6 months post-operatively, the SRR in the BB group remained lower (43.8%, 14 cases), whereas the SRR in the DS group reached 76.9% (30 cases) (P = .159). Nevertheless, no significant difference in SRR was observed between the DS group (94.9%) and the BB group (93.8%) at the 2-year post-operative time point (P = .847). In contrast, the BB group exhibited fewer cases of vertical graft malpositioning (P = .049) and fewer neurological complications (P = .049).ConclusionThere were no significant differences between BB Latarjet and DS Latarjet in terms of osseous union rate, SRR, recurrent instability rate, and overall complication rate at 2-year post-operative time point.

The Latarjet procedure is widely accepted for the treatment of anterior shoulder instability.9,24,37 It has been shown to provide greater initial stability than Bankart repair, particularly in cases involving glenoid bone loss exceeding 15% of the original glenoid width.1,17,2334

However, the Latarjet procedure is associated with a relatively higher complication rate compared with Bankart repair.14,15,24 Most complications are related to the fixation devices and the graft, including screw breakage or deviation and graft malposition or fracture. Post-operative bone resorption and secondary osteoarthritis (OA) of the shoulder joint also remain concerns.11

In the Latarjet procedure, appropriate graft positioning is essential to prevent post-operative redislocation.19,2233 Arthroscopic Latarjet procedures have been reported to result in fewer graft malpositions, possibly because of improved intraoperative visualization of graft placement compared with the open Latarjet procedure.926

Some graft-related complications may be associated with the specific osseous anatomy of the coracoid process, not only its length but also its thickness.11 In the Latarjet procedure, a coracoid graft of at least 25.0 mm in length is required when 2 3.5-mm-diameter screws are used.8,10,35 The length of the coracoid process available for grafting is defined as the distance from the tip of the coracoid process to the lateral attachment of the coracoclavicular ligament.28 It has been reported to be as long as 28.5 ± 5.1 mm (range, 22.9-36.8 mm)10 or as short as 19.0 mm (range, 13.5-30 mm) in another study.2 These findings indicate substantial interindividual variability.2,10,27

Post-operative outcomes of the arthroscopic Latarjet procedure have predominantly been reported using the double-screw (DS) technique.20,21 When the graft is excessively long, passage through the subscapularis split can be difficult.7 Conversely, when the graft is too small, secure fixation may be challenging because smaller grafts are at increased risk of fracture due to the relatively large screw holes in proportion to graft length.8

Arthroscopic button-fixed Latarjet with Bankart repair (BB Latarjet) has recently been introduced.3, 4, 5, 6 Arthroscopic BB Latarjet is particularly applicable for short grafts because it requires a single 3.2-mm bone tunnel, through which a single suture loop is secured at both ends with buttons. The author selected either the DS technique20,21 or the BB technique3, 4, 5, 6 according to the intraoperatively measured coracoid length. Thus, this is a comparative cohort study across anatomically distinct 2 groups.

The author's aim is to propose a reliable method to provide joint stability in patients with shoulder instability and a glenoid bone defect combined with a short coracoid process. Our hypethesis was that there were no statistically significant differences in the graft union rate, sports return rate (SRR), and complication rate between the arthroscopic BB Latarjet and the arthroscopic DS Latarjet, which has been considered advantageous because of its higher initial fixation strength using 2 screws.

Patients and methods

The present study included 32 patients in the BB group (mean follow-up [FU] period, 2.5 ± 0.5 years; range, 2.0-3.0 years) and 39 patients in the DS group (mean FU period, 4.4 ± 1.4 years; Fig. 1). A total of 78 patients met the study criteria, with a mean FU period of 3.3 ± 1.3 years (range, 2.0-6.0 years). Seven patients were lost to FU, resulting in an attrition rate of 9.0% (4 in the DS group and 3 in the BB group). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) recurrent anterior shoulder dislocation with glenoid bone loss exceeding 15% of the original glenoid width or (2) recurrent dislocation following previous Bankart repair. The exclusion criteria included uncontrolled epilepsy, multidirectional shoulder instability, and other concurrent shoulder pathologies.

For measurement of the glenoid bone defect, the width of the glenoid was defined as the length of the largest equatorial diameter on pre-operative computed tomography (CT) images. The difference between the diseased glenoid (d) and the intact glenoid (D) was defined as the width of bone loss. The percentage of bone loss was calculated as follows: bone loss = (D − d)/D × 100 (%). The mean glenoid bone defect was 18.7% in the DS group and 17.5% in the BB group (P = .18).

Patients underwent arthroscopic treatment between January 2019 and April 2023 using either a 3.2-mm-diameter bone tunnel fixed with buttons combined with Bankart repair (BB method) (Fig. 2, A–C) or the DS Latarjet technique using 2 3.5-mm-diameter screws (Fig. 2, D–F). Surgical procedures

When the intraoperatively measured coracoid length was >25 mm, a 25-mm coracoid graft was harvested, and the DS method was undertaken. When the coracoid length was <24 mm, a 15-mm coracoid graft was harvested, and the BB method was performed. The diameter of the end button was 5.0 mm, and a 5.0-mm margin was considered necessary on both sides of the 5.0-mm end button.13 Thus, the total graft length was calculated as 5.0 mm + 5.0 mm + 5.0 mm = 15.0 mm. Briefly, the SaSumata (SS) guide (Zimmer Biomet Japan, Tokyo, Japan) was positioned on the coracoid process as previously described.18 A two-hole SS guide with a flat head was used for the DS technique, and a one-hole SS guide with a beaked head was used for the BB technique. The SS guide was provisionally fixed with 2.8-mm prefix screws, and the coracoid process was then osteotomized. Care was taken to avoid injury to the axillary and musculocutaneous nerves during the horizontal split of the subscapularis.

The SS guide was then pushed medially and downward to allow the coracoid graft to pass through the subscapularis split. To achieve temporary fixation of the graft, 1.5-mm Kirschner wires were inserted through the cannulated prefix screws. A 3.5-mm cannulated drill (for the DS method) or a 3.2-mm cannulated drill (for the BB method) was used to create bone tunnels of the corresponding diameters through the graft and the scapular neck.

In the DS method, the bone graft (arrowhead, Fig. 2D) was fixed using 2 3.5-mm cortical screws (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA; arrowheads, Fig. 2E). In the BB method, the graft was secured using ToggleLoc constructs (Zimmer Biomet; arrowhead, Fig. 2A). After the toggle buttons (arrow, Fig. 2B) and the end button (arrowhead, Fig. 2B) were positioned, final tensioning was achieved by pulling each end of the white suture strand. A tension gauge was used to standardize the applied force at 100 N. Bankart repair was performed according to the method described by Boileau et al.3 JaggerTape anchors (Zimmer Biomet) were placed above and below the secured bone graft (arrowheads, Fig. 2C). All BB procedures involved Bankart repair in addition to button fixation (Video 1), whereas the DS method have been a standard Latarjet without Bankart repair.

Post-operative rehabilitation and sports permission

Both patient groups followed the same post-operative rehabilitation protocol. The shoulder was immobilized in internal rotation using a conventional sling for 2 weeks. Active motion was initiated without restriction after 2 weeks.

Permission for return to sports was granted either after confirmation of osseous union on CT examination at either 3 months or 6 months post-operatively. At 9 months post-operatively, permission for return to sports was granted regardless of union status. Sports return was assessed at 6, 9, and 24 months, and it was categorized as follows: (1) sports return confirmed at 6 months post-operatively; (2) sports return confirmed at 9 months post-operatively; and (3) sports return confirmed as 2 years post-operatively.

Clinical evaluation

Patient demographics, pre-operative baseline characteristics, the number of previous dislocations, and sports-related outcomes were obtained from medical records. All patients were scheduled for physical examination, questionnaires, and CT scans pre-operatively at 3 months, at 6 months, and at the 2-year post-operative time point. The graft was considered united when no radiolucent zone was observed between the graft and the recipient site on CT images.

Post-operative clinical outcomes included SRR, visual analog scale scores for pain and instability, Rowe score, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score, University of California, Los Angeles score, and Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV). Post-operative recurrence of instability was defined as at least one episode of anterior dislocation. In addition to overt dislocation that limited shoulder joint function, subjective feelings of subluxation that caused hesitation to return to sports were also considered post-operative recurrences.

Range of motion

Post-operative range of motion was measured using a manual goniometer and expressed as external rotation at rest (ER0) and ER at 90° of shoulder abduction (ER90).

Radiological evaluation

All patients underwent CT scans pre-operatively at 3 months, at 6 months, and at the 2-year post-operative time point. The ideal position of the coracoid graft was defined as placement between the 3 o'clock and 5 o'clock positions on the axial view and flush with the anterior glenoid rim.25

Pseudoarthrosis was defined as a visible radiolucent line of ≤5 mm; if the radiolucent line exceeded 5 mm, the graft was considered to have been displaced. Bone resorption, as assessed on the final CT scan, was classified as follows: grade 0, no resorption; grade 1, resorption limited to the area near the screw head; grade 2, resorption involving most of the graft; and grade 3, complete resorption.343536

Shoulder OA was evaluated based on the size of the humeral osteophyte: <3 mm was classified as mild OA; 3-7 mm as moderate OA; and ≥7 mm or the presence of marked joint sclerosis as severe OA.16,29 Radiographic evaluations and clinical assessments were performed independently by 2 shoulder surgery fellows.

For the interobserver reliability for (1) CT-based determinations of graft position, (2) union, (3) resorption, and (4) OA grading, each Cohen k was (1) 0.767, (2) 0.878, (3) 853, and (4) 0.754, respectively.

Data analyses

A paired t-test was used to compare pre-operative and post-operative outcomes. Mean values of continuous variables were compared using the Student t-test, whereas the chi-square (χ2) test was used for categorical variables. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Return to sports

Thirty patients (76.9%) had returned to sports by 6 months post-operatively in the DS group, whereas 14 cases (43.8%) returned in the BB group (P = .159; Table II). Between 6 and 9 months, 7 (17.9%) return in the DS group, whereas 16 patients (50%) had returned in the BB group (P = .041; Table II). At the 2-year post-operative time point, no significant difference in the SRR was observed between the DS group (37 cases, 94.9%) and the BB group (30 cases, 93.8%) (P = .847; Table II).

Complications and reoperations

No recurrent dislocation was observed in either group. Post-operative apprehension tests were negative in all patients. At the 2-year post-operative time point, 2 patients of the DS group and 2 of the BB group did not return to sports due to the subjective feelings of subluxation causing hesitation to return to sports. In the DS group, one patient required reoperation for removal of a protruding screw due to increasing pain and progression of OA. Neurological complications occurred in 7 cases in the DS group and in 1 case in the BB group (P = .049; Table III). Of the 7 cases in the DS group, 4 involved transient paresthesia or hypesthesia and 2 involved motor dysfunction. At 2 years post-operative time point, there were no persistent deficits beyond the one permanent motor dysfunction. Graft position and osteoarthritis progression

There were no significant differences in horizontal malposition between the DS and BB groups (Table III). In the BB group, one graft (3.1%) exhibited vertical malposition at the initial post-operative assessment. In contrast, 7 of 39 grafts (17.9%) in the DS group demonstrated vertical malposition (P = .049). Three grafts (7.7%) were placed below the 5 o'clock position, and 4 (10.3%) were placed above the 3 o'clock position (Table III). At the 2-year post-operative time point, there was a trend toward a 1-grade progression of OA in the DS group; however, this difference was not statistically significant (P = .274; Table III).

Graft union and bone resorption

At 3 months post-operatively, 32 patients (82.1%) in the DS group showed evidence of bony graft union, compared with 11 patients (34.4%) in the BB group (P < .01; Table IV). Discussion

This study demonstrated that arthroscopic DS Latarjet resulted in earlier graft union and earlier SRR. However, the ultimate osseous union rates were similar between the DS and BB techniques at the 2-year post-operative time point. It would be inappropriate to conclude that the DS technique was superior to the BB technique based solely on early (<3 months post-operatively) osseous union (82.1% vs. 34.4%, P = .037, for the DS and BB groups, respectively). At the 2-year post-operative time point, the osseous union rate in the BB group eventually reached a value comparable to that of the DS group (92.3% vs. 81.3%, P = .716, for the DS and BB groups, respectively). Similarly, the overall SRR in the BB group reached a comparable level at the 2-year post-operative time point (94.9% vs. 93.8%, P = .847, for the DS and BB groups, respectively). The achievement of osseous union and successful return to sports indicates that the initial treatment goal was accomplished with either method.

Both arthroscopic BB Latarjet3, 4, 5, 6 and arthroscopic DS Latarjet20,21 have been reported to yield low recurrence rates, high SRRs, and favorable clinical outcomes. Graft union is a key parameter in evaluating the success of the Latarjet procedure because nonunion is a primary cause of redislocation and a major indication for reoperation.19,2233 At 3 months post-operatively, the graft union rate was significantly higher in the DS group (82.1%) than in the BB group (34.4%) (P < .01). This finding that osseous union occurs more promptly with arthroscopic DS Latarjet is consistent with the report by Tanaka et al,31 who observed that 97.1% of patients undergoing open DS Latarjet achieved osseous union at 3 months.

In the present study, the graft union rate in the BB group improved over time, reaching 81.3% at the 2-year post-operative time point (Table IV). Some patients who were classified as having nonunion at the end of the 2-year study period (7.7% in the DS group and 18.7% in the BB group) continue to be followed. However, it has not yet been fully analyzed whether those with persistent nonunion achieved acceptable levels of return to sports.

The delayed union observed in the BB group may be explained by several factors. First, the DS method provides stronger initial fixation, whereas BB fixation may offer comparatively less initial stability. Second, the longer graft used in the DS Latarjet provides a larger contact area, which may facilitate earlier union. Third, in the BB Latarjet, the use of 2 suture anchors to secure the labrum to the glenoid may help prevent graft rotation but could also influence the timing of union.

Vertical malposition of the graft was more frequent in the DS Latarjet group (P = .049; Table III), likely because of the larger graft size and the technical difficulty associated with passing it through the horizontal split of the subscapularis muscle. Consistent with this finding, the mean operative time was significantly longer in the DS group (153.0 ± 15.7 minutes) than in the BB group (138.1 ± 15.8 minutes) (P < .001).

Neurological complications were more frequent in the DS Latarjet group (P = .049; Table III), possibly because repeated attempts to pass the larger graft through the relatively narrow subscapularis split may increase the risk of nerve irritation.

Return to sports is an important parameter in assessing the effectiveness of the Latarjet procedure. Girard et al12 compared early outcomes of open DS Latarjet and arthroscopic BB Latarjet and reported that the open DS technique resulted in less restriction of ER (−33° vs. −18°; P = .01) and a higher SRR than the arthroscopic BB approach.

After graft union at 6 months, a significantly higher proportion of patients in the BB group returned to sports between 6 and 9 months. By the 2-year FU, the overall SRR was comparable between the 2 groups (Table II). The study by Girard et al13 reported outcomes at 12 months. Thus, the results may differ with a longer FU period.

The incidence of OA following anterior shoulder dislocation and surgical stabilization is known to be higher than that in healthy shoulders.13,32 In the present study, the DS Latarjet group demonstrated OA progression by one grade in 10.3% of patients and by 2 grades in 5.1%, resulting in an overall progression rate of 15.4%. In turn, OA progression was only 6.2% in the BB group (Table III). However, because of the short FU period of 2 years, this difference should be considered as preliminary. Some patients continue to be followed; however, it has not yet been fully analyzed whether they will demonstrate further OA progression.

A comparison of the 2 groups over time provides additional insight. Arthroscopic DS Latarjet demonstrated faster graft healing at 3 months (P < .01; Table IV) and tended to allow earlier return to sports (P = .159; Table II). In contrast, arthroscopic BB Latarjet was associated with fewer cases of vertical graft malposition (P = .049; Table III) and fewer neurological complications (P = .049; Table III). No significant differences were observed between the 2 groups in terms of osseous union rate (P = .716; Table IV), SRR (P = .847; Table II) at the 2-year post-operative time point, or redislocation rate (0 cases in both groups).

It is important to note that the glenoid bone defects in this study were not large in either group. The mean glenoid bone defect was 17.5% in the BB group and 18.7% in the DS group (Table I). In contrast, graft lengths were set at 15.0 mm in the BB group and 25.0 mm in the DS group. In a cadaveric study, a coracoid graft with a mean length of ∖1.9 mm between the graft lengths used in the 2 groups achieved a 100% filling ratio for glenoid bone defects of 10%-20%; however, the filling ratio decreased to 96% for 25% bone defects and to 76% for 30% bone defects.9 In the present cohort, in which the mean bone defect was <20% in both groups, both BB and DS Latarjet demonstrated effective prevention of redislocation. However, in cases of massive glenoid bone defects exceeding 30%, the shorter graft used in the BB method may be associated with an increased risk of redislocation.

A recent report, comparing arthroscopic Latarjet and arthroscopic Bristow procedures found no cases of recurrent dislocation in either group.30 However, the mean glenoid bone defect was 22.3% in the Latarjet group and 15.7% in the Bristow group; therefore, it could not be concluded that the smaller graft with single-screw fixation used in the Bristow group would prevent recurrent dislocation to the same extent as the Latarjet procedure for similarly sized bone defects.

The limitations of this study include its retrospective design and relatively short FU period. Treatment allocation was based on intraoperative measurement of coracoid process length, which introduced selection bias because the groups inherently differed in anatomical characteristics. The author acknowledges that a randomized design would have strengthened the study. For example, patients with a coracoid length >25 mm could have been randomized to undergo either procedure.

Conclusion

DS Latarjet was characterized with early graft healing, early SRR, and higher SSV scores as compared with BB Latarjet. However, there were no statistically significant differences between BB Latarjet and DS Latarjet with respect to graft union rate and SRR at 2 years post-operative time point. BB Latarjet was associated with fewer complications than DS Latarjet. BB Latarjet may be useful particularly in patients with shoulder instability who have a glenoid bone defect and a short coracoid process.
