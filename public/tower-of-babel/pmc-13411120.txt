The relationship between frailty, health literacy, and medication adherence in older adults
Authors: Elaheh Dadashi, Pouya Farokhnezhad Afshar, Hossein Matlabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty is a significant syndrome in aging, influenced by multiple factors. Timely identification of older adults at risk of frailty, along with appropriate interventions, can mitigate their adverse health consequences. This study examined the relationship between frailty, health literacy, and medication adherence among older adults.MethodThis cross-sectional study included 210 older adults (aged 60 and above) recruited from the Tabriz Education and Training Center for Retirees. Data were collected using the Tilburg Frailty Index (TFI), Test of Functional Health Literacy in Adults (TOFHLA), the Morisky, Green, and Levine (MGL) adherence scale, and a general information questionnaire. Data analysis was performed in SPSS v.27 using Spearman’s correlation tests, independent t-tests, ANOVA, and logistic regression.ResultsThe mean age of the participants in this study was 68.92 ± 6.42. Of the participants, 125 (59.5%) were female and 85 (40.5%) were male, with 74.5% being married. Approximately 42% of the older adults were frail, and about 95% of them had high medication adherence. Also, 49.5% of the participants had sufficient health literacy. The findings of logistic regression analysis showed health literacy also had an apparent protective effect on frailty (OR = 0.024, P < 0.001), and an unhealthy lifestyle was significantly associated with frailty (OR = 51.896, P < 0.001).ConclusionThere is a relationship between unhealthy lifestyle, health literacy, and frailty in older adults. Health literacy may serve as an apparent protective factor against frailty. An unhealthy lifestyle could exacerbate frailty. Therefore, healthcare providers should focus on enhancing health literacy and a healthy lifestyle to prevent frailty in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07723-0.

Background

Aging involves the gradual decline of physiological reserves and functional abilities due to age-related changes over time [1]. The World Health Organization estimates the rapid increase of the global older adult population; their projections indicate a rise from 840 million older adults in 2013 to two billion by 2050 [2]. Approximately two-thirds of the 150,000 daily deaths worldwide occur among individuals aged 60 and older [3]. This demographic shift significantly increases the incidence and prevalence of geriatric syndromes, notably frailty. This shift is particularly accelerated in Iran, necessitating context-specific research to address its unique public health implications [4].

Frailty is a common geriatric syndrome [2]. Prevalence statistics vary depending on how researchers define and measure frailty, but systematic reviews show a prevalence range of 12% to 24% among community-dwelling older adults [5], and researchers observe it exceeding 40% in hospitalized older adults [6]. However, studies within the Iranian context report a wide and often higher prevalence, influenced by socioeconomic and healthcare access factors, highlighting a critical national health concern [7]. Factors such as multimorbidity and declining physical, mental, and social capacities increase the likelihood of frailty [8]. Timely identification of at-risk older adults and appropriate interventions based on predictive factors can reduce the adverse outcomes of frailty syndrome [2]. Experts consider frailty the endpoint of the health-instability spectrum [9, 10]. Frailty, especially in older age, indicates compromised health [11]. Among the factors that affect health, we can point to health literacy [12].

Health literacy refers to an individual’s capacity to acquire, interpret, and understand health information for appropriate decision-making [13]. Studies show that health literacy is often insufficient in older adults [14, 15]. In Iran, research indicates that low health literacy among older adults is a prevalent issue, compounded by cultural and educational barriers [16]. Older adults with insufficient health literacy are one of the at-risk groups [17]. Health literacy, by definition, means the ability to read, comprehend, and act on health and medical recommendations and to adhere to treatment [18].

Treatment adherence describes the extent to which a patient’s behavior matches their medical recommendations, including medication adherence [19]. Medication adherence is a complex behavior that depends on many factors, such as individual, socioeconomic, and healthcare system factors [20]. Patient medication adherence means the patient’s commitment to following the doctor’s prescribed medication instructions, including the timely and correct administration of medications [21]. Since older adults are prone to multiple diseases, they face the risk of polypharmacy and may therefore encounter a higher risk of non-adherence compared to younger populations [22]. This is especially relevant in Iran, where polypharmacy and non-adherence pose significant challenges for geriatric care [23].

Studies indicate that a significant number of older adults show poor to moderate medication adherence [24, 25]. Because non-adherence brings multiple complications, individuals with inappropriate medication adherence are likely to experience frailty to a greater extent. Despite the recognized global links, the interplay between health literacy, medication adherence, and frailty remains underexplored in Iranian gerontology, creating a clear rationale and a gap for this study [26]. Therefore, we must examine these factors together to provide healthcare providers with a comprehensive understanding of an older adult’s health status and needs. This study tests two hypotheses within an older Iranian population: first, that health literacy is associated with medication adherence, and second, that both health literacy and medication adherence are independently associated with frailty. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between frailty, medication adherence, and health literacy in older adults.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is a descriptive-analytical, cross-sectional study.

Sample

We entered Participants using convenience sampling who attended the Education Retirement Center in Tabriz between June and December 2024. Inclusion criteria were: age over 60 years, literacy, and Normal cognitive status (based on Mini-Cog test [27] and score: 3). The exclusion criterion was acute illness or severe frailty, and incomplete questionnaire completion (The number of these cases was zero). The required sample size was calculated using the following formula, with r = 0.2, and α = 0.05 [28], yielding a result of 194 participants. To achieve a statistical power of 80% with a 95% confidence level, assuming a minimum correlation coefficient of 0.2 for statistical significance, and accounting for a potential 10% rate of incomplete questionnaires, the final sample size was set to 210 participants.

Data collection

Data were collected using the Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI), a Health Literacy Assessment, the Morisky, Green, and Levine adherence scale (MGL), and a demographic questionnaire. The demographic questionnaire gathered information on participants’ age, sex, education level, marital status, monthly income, and lifestyle (Selfreport).

Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI): The TFI was used to assess frailty in older adults [29]. This tool consists of two parts: Part A, which includes ten questions about determinants of frailty, and Part B, which includes 15 questions about the main components of frailty across three domains: social, psychological, and physical. Each question is scored as either zero or one. The physical domain contains eight questions, the psychological domain contains four questions, and the social domain contains three questions. The total score ranges from 0 to 15, with a cutoff of 5 indicating frailty (a score of 5 or higher) [30]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Persian version of this questionnaire was reported as 0.81, and its validity was confirmed using construct validity [31]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the TFI was 0.76.Morisky, Green, and Levine adherence scale (MGL): The MGL is a four-item questionnaire designed to assess medication adherence. MGL has adequate reliability and internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.61) [32]. MGL includes four dichotomous questions (with “yes” or “no” answers). Its total score ranges from zero to four. Lower scores indicate a higher level of adherence. Patients’ total scores can be categorized into high adherence (0 items with a “yes” answer), moderate adherence (1–2 items with a “yes” answer), and low adherence (3–4 items with a “yes” answer) [33]. The reading comprehension section tests the patient’s ability to read real-world health care texts and consists of 50 questions. The computational section includes a series of explanations regarding prescribed medications, appointment times, steps for obtaining financial assistance, and an example of a medical test result. After these explanations are provided to each individual in the form of cards, relevant questions (17 in total) are asked. Each person’s health literacy score is a number from zero to one hundred, divided into three levels: inadequate, marginal, and adequate, based on cutoff points of 59 and 74. The reliability and validation of the Persian version of the tool showed that the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this questionnaire is 0.84 [35]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.87.

Data analysis

Data analysis included descriptive statistics (frequency and percentage frequency of variables, mean, and standard deviation) and inferential statistics ((Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and Histogram chart), Spearman correlation analysis, and logistic regression analysis). The collected data were analyzed using SPSS v.27. We tried to test the initial model based on past studies and a purposeful approach. The results of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test on all three variables: frailty (K-S = 0.15, P < 0.001), medication adherence (K-S = 0.11, P < 0.001), and health literacy (K-S = 0.17, P < 0.001) showed that the variables did not have a normal distribution.

Results

The participants in this study included 125 women and 85 men. The majority of participants in the research were in the 60–70 age range, and the mean age of participants was 68.92 ± 6.42. There were 123 (58.6%) non-frail and 87 (41.4%) frail. Regarding medication adherence, a large number of participants (98.5%) had high medication adherence. 59 (28.1%) participants had inadequate health literacy, 42 (19.1%) had marginal health literacy, and 109 (49.5%) had adequate health literacy (Table 1).

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that lower health literacy and unhealthy lifestyle patterns are significantly associated with increased odds of adverse health outcomes, including frailty and functional decline. Individuals with inadequate health literacy and unhealthy lifestyle behaviors exhibited markedly elevated risk ratios, which were statistically significant. Notably, the logistic regression model quantified these associations, revealing that adequate health literacy was associated with a reduction in the odds of being frail. These results are consistent with existing evidence suggesting that health literacy mediates self-care capacity, while lifestyle factors such as diet, physical activity, and smoking directly influence physiological resilience and aging trajectories.

The results also indicate a significant and negative relationship between medication adherence and frailty. Most participants demonstrated high medication adherence. One study was not consistent with our findings and did not confirm this relationship [30]. Another study similarly contradicted our results [36]. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in the studied populations and the instruments used. However, other studies were consistent with our findings and reported a direct correlation between frailty and medication adherence [37, 38]. Failure to take medications correctly, completely, and accurately can lead to disease recurrence or lack of improvement. Moreover, improper medication use may result in side effects that impact physical and mental health, thereby influencing frailty in older adults. The high adherence observed in our participants may be related to the fact that they belonged to the younger segment of the older adult population (60–70 years old).

Health literacy showed a significant negative correlation with frailty in our study, and logistic regression analysis indicated that health literacy appears to be a protective factor against frailty. These findings are supported by previous studies [39, 40]. Health literacy affects individuals’ health-related decision-making by enhancing their understanding of health information and access to medical services factors that are closely linked to frailty status. The relatively high average health literacy in our sample may be explained by the predominance of female participants and the inclusion of younger older adults.

According to the findings, demographic variables such as marital status, lifestyle, and the presence of two or more chronic diseases were significantly associated with frailty. This is consistent with previous studies [41, 42]. Our descriptive analysis (Table 1) underscores this, showing that 57.6% of participants with multimorbidity were frail, compared to 34.0% of those without, and that 70% of those reporting an unhealthy lifestyle were frail. Marital status and living arrangements can affect the physical, psychological, and social dimensions of frailty through their impact on social support, mental state, and caregiving. Lifestyle also showed a strong association with frailty. A study consistent with our findings reported that lifestyle is related to frailty [43]. Lifestyle factors including nutrition and physical activity can influence overall health and, consequently, frailty. Another study in line with our findings stated that frailty is associated with chronic diseases [44]. In our study, lifestyle was self-reported; using more precise instruments might have allowed for better identification of its contributing dimensions. Chronic diseases can influence frailty by affecting various physical and mental aspects of health.

According to the results presented in Table 1, sex and living arrangements were not significantly associated with frailty, medication adherence, or health literacy. However, marital status was significantly related to both frailty and health literacy. This may be due to the reduced exchange of ideas among individuals living alone compared to those in group settings, which can influence health literacy. Similarly, lifestyle was significantly associated with both frailty and health literacy. An individual’s ability to comprehend and apply health information plays a critical role in shaping lifestyle choices and vice versa. It is important to differentiate between bivariate and multivariate associations. While Spearman’s test showed significant correlations, the final logistic regression model highlights that health literacy and lifestyle retained strong, independent associations with frailty even after adjusting for other demographic and clinical variables, reinforcing their pivotal role.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between the studied variables. Second, data were collected from a single center using a convenience sampling method, which may restrict the generalizability of the results to the broader population of older adults, particularly those in other settings, of different age groups (e.g., the oldest-old), or with different sociodemographic characteristics. Third, The exceptionally high level of self-reported medication adherence in our sample, coupled with the low Cronbach’s alpha for the MGL scale, suggests potential measurement issues such as a ceiling effect or social desirability bias. These factors may have influenced the observed correlation. Fourth, the majority of participants were “young-old” adults (aged 60–70 years).

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study found significant associations between higher levels of frailty and lower health literacy, poorer medication adherence, and less healthy lifestyle patterns. The analysis suggests that health literacy may act as a protective factor, while modifiable behaviors like medication adherence and lifestyle are key correlates of frailty status. These findings underscore the potential of integrated geriatric care strategies that simultaneously address knowledge, self-management, and healthy behaviors.
