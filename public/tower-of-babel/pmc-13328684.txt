Hate speech detection using pretrained DeBERTaV3 optimized with Grey Wolf Optimizer: a hybrid approach for social media content moderation
Authors: Manas Kamal Das, Basil Skaria, Argha Auddy, M. Vidhyalakshmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The proliferation of hate speech on social media poses a significant challenge to maintaining safe and inclusive online environments, necessitating accurate and scalable automated detection systems. However, the performance of transformer-based models for hate speech detection is highly sensitive to hyperparameter configurations, making manual and conventional tuning strategies inefficient in high-dimensional search spaces.To address this challenge, this study proposes a hybrid optimization framework that integrates the DeBERTaV3 transformer model with the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO) for automated hyperparameter tuning. The proposed approach enables efficient exploration of complex hyperparameter spaces by balancing global search and local refinement. The framework optimizes eight critical hyperparameters, including learning rate, weight decay, and dropout rates, to enhance convergence stability and generalization performance. The proposed method is evaluated on the Davidson et al. (2017) dataset, consisting of 24,783 labeled tweets. Experimental results demonstrate that the GWO-DeBERTaV3 model achieves a peak accuracy of 97.72% and a macro F1-score of 97.71%, with statistically significant improvements over baseline and conventional tuning approaches.These findings highlight the effectiveness of metaheuristic-based optimization for transformer fine-tuning and demonstrate its potential for improving robustness and performance in real-world hate speech detection systems.

Introduction

The exponential proliferation of user-generated content on social media platforms has fundamentally transformed digital communication, while simultaneously creating unprecedented challenges for maintaining safe online environments. Among these, hate speech detection has emerged as one of the most critical and technically demanding problems in modern Natural Language Processing (NLP). Hate speech characterized by language that systematically targets individuals or groups based on protected characteristics such as race, religion, ethnicity, gender, or sexual orientation, poses significant threats not only to individual well-being but also to the fabric of democratic discourse and social cohesion.

The scale of this challenge is staggering; billions of posts, comments, and messages are generated daily across platforms, making manual content moderation practically impossible. Traditional rule-based approaches and keyword filtering systems have proven to be inadequate against linguistic creativity and evolving tactics employed by users seeking to circumvent detection mechanisms. This reality has driven the urgent need for sophisticated automated systems capable of understanding nuanced contextual cues, identifying implicit threats, and distinguishing between legitimate discourse and harmful content, tasks that remain challenging even for human moderators.

Recent advances in transformer-based architectures1–3 have revolutionized NLP tasks, with models such as BERT4 demonstrating remarkable capabilities in language understanding. DeBERTaV3 (Decoding-enhanced BERT with Disentangled Attention)5 represents the current state-of-the-art in this evolution, incorporating innovative architectural improvements including disentangled attention mechanisms and enhanced mask decoders that significantly improved performance on complex language understanding benchmarks.Compared to alternative transformer architectures such as RoBERTa-large and ELECTRA, DeBERTaV3’s disentangled attention mechanism enables improved separation of positional and content representations, which is particularly advantageous in intricate tasks such as hate speech detection where contextual refinements are critical. However, despite these architectural innovations, a critical bottleneck persists: the optimal configuration of the model However, despite these architectural advancements, the effectiveness of transformer-based hate speech detection systems is highly dependent on hyperparameter configuration. In high-dimensional settings, manual tuning and conventional search strategies become inefficient, often leading to suboptimal performance. This limitation motivates the need for automated and intelligent optimization strategies capable of efficiently exploring complex hyperparameter spaces.

The hyperparameter optimization challenge in deep learning extends far beyond simple parameter adjustment—it represents a complex, high-dimensional optimization problem where subtle interactions between parameters can significantly impact model performance. Traditional approaches such as grid search, random search, and manual tuning are computationally expensive and often fail to effectively explore the vast parameter space, potentially leaving performance improvements undiscovered.

Recent research in hybrid metaheuristic optimization has focused on addressing premature convergence and exploration–exploitation imbalance in classical swarm-based algorithms. For example, Fast-flying PSO (FF-PSO) introduces quantization-based velocity updating mechanisms to mitigate oscillatory behavior and enhance convergence stability6. Similarly, Quantized Orthogonal Experimentation SSA (QOX-SSA) improves population diversity and global search capability through structured experimentation strategies, demonstrating strong performance in feature selection and neural network training tasks7. While these approaches achieve promising results on benchmark optimization problems, their integration with transformer-based hyperparameter optimization in NLP applications remains largely unexplored.

Although significant progress has been made in transformer-based hate speech detection, systematic integration of population-based metaheuristic optimization for transformer fine-tuning remains limited. Existing studies predominantly rely on manual tuning or conventional search strategies, resulting in inadequate exploration of the high-dimensional hyperparameter space. This gap motivates the need for a structured and automated optimization framework tailored to transformer architectures.

To address this identified research gap, this study introduces a novel hybrid optimization framework that synergistically combines the linguistic sophistication of DeBERTaV3 with the intelligent search capabilities of the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO)8.Unlike particle swarm optimization (PSO) or genetic algorithms (GA), GWO requires fewer control parameters and indicates stable convergence behavior through its hierarchical leadership mechanism. Compared to Bayesian optimization, GWO avoids surrogate modeling overhead and is computationally simpler to combine within deep learning training pipelines, making it well-suited for large-scale transformer optimization. This integration represents a paradigm shift from ad-hoc hyperparameter tuning towards systematic, automated optimization that can unlock the full potential of advanced transformer architectures.

Our approach was rigorously evaluated using the widely-adopted Davidson et al.9 hate speech dataset, which has become the de facto benchmark for comparing hate speech detection methodologies. The three-class taxonomy of this data set, which distinguishes between hate speech, offensive language, and neutral content, presents nuanced classification challenges that mirror real-world content moderation scenarios.

Literature review

Automated detection of hate speech has emerged as a critical research area in natural language processing, driven by the exponential growth of user-generated content on social media platforms and the urgent need for scalable content moderation systems. This section provides a comprehensive review of the evolution of hate speech detection methodologies, with particular emphasis on the approaches applied to the Davidson et al. dataset, which has become a de facto benchmark in this domain.

Foundational work and dataset establishment

The seminal work by Davidson et al.9 established the foundation for hate speech detection research by introducing a large-scale, systematically annotated dataset comprising 24,783 tweets categorized into hate speech, offensive language, and neutral content. This three-class taxonomy addressed the critical distinction between hate speech (targeted attacks on protected groups) and general offensive language (profanity without targeted malice). The baseline performance using Logistic Regression with TF-IDF features achieved an F1-score of 0.90, establishing the initial benchmark for subsequent research. The dataset’s class imbalance, with offensive language comprising 77.4% of the data, highlighted inherent challenges that would influence future methodological developments.

Early deep learning architectures

Convolutional neural networks

Park and Fung10 pioneered the application of CNNs to hate speech detection by employing a novel dual-level approach that combined character-level and word-level convolutional layers. Their character-level CNN component proved particularly effective in handling the noisy, informal nature of Twitter data, including deliberate misspellings, leetspeak, and other obfuscation techniques commonly used to evade detection systems. The word-level component captured higher-level semantic patterns, while the character-level analysis provided robustness against spelling variations and novel word formations.

This approach achieved an accuracy of 89.00% and an F1-score of 0.87, demonstrating significant improvements over traditional methods. The success of this dual-level architecture highlighted the importance of multi-granular text analysis in social media content, where users frequently employ creative linguistic variations to express hateful sentiments while attempting to circumvent automated detection.

Recurrent neural networks and sequential modeling

The sequential nature of text data naturally led researchers to explore RNN-based architectures, which could model temporal dependencies and contextual relationships within tweets. Badjatiya et al.11 made substantial contributions to this area by developing an ensemble approach that combined LSTM and GRU models with gradient-boosted decision trees, achieving a notable F1-score of 0.93 and accuracy of 92.00%.

Their work demonstrated several key insights: (1) the effectiveness of ensemble methods in capturing diverse aspects of hate speech patterns, (2) the importance of combining neural approaches with traditional machine learning for robust performance, and (3) the value of bidirectional processing to capture both forward and backward contextual dependencies. The integration of gradient-boosted decision trees provided interpretability benefits while maintaining high performance, addressing a common criticism of deep learning approaches in sensitive applications like content moderation.

Kumar et al.12 further advanced sequential modeling by incorporating self-attention mechanisms into BiLSTM architectures. This innovation allowed the model to dynamically weight the importance of different words within a tweet, effectively identifying key terms and phrases that most strongly indicated hateful content. The attention mechanism proved particularly valuable for handling the variable-length nature of tweets and the tendency for hate speech indicators to appear in specific positions within the text.

The transformer revolution

BERT-based approaches

Qian et al.4 were among the first to successfully adapt BERT for hate speech detection, achieving remarkable results with an F1-score of 0.94 and accuracy of 95.00% on the Davidson dataset. Their work demonstrated the power of transfer learning from large-scale pretrained language models, showing that contextual representations learned from massive text corpora could be effectively fine-tuned for the specific nuances of hate speech detection.

The success of BERT-based approaches stemmed from several key advantages: (1) bidirectional context encoding, which captured both left and right context for each word, (2) attention mechanisms that could identify complex dependencies between distant words, and (3) rich pretrained representations that encoded semantic and syntactic knowledge from diverse text sources.

Advanced transformer variants and robustness studies

ElSherief et al.13 extended this line of research by fine-tuning RoBERTa, a more robustly trained variant of BERT, while introducing crucial explainability components. Their work, “Hate Lingo: A Target-based Linguistic Analysis of Hate Speech in Social Media,” provided not only performance improvements but also valuable insights into the linguistic patterns and mechanisms underlying hate speech. By incorporating explainability methods, they addressed a critical gap in understanding how transformer models make decisions in hate speech detection tasks.

Mathew et al.14 conducted comprehensive comparative analyses of various BERT-based models, providing the research community with detailed performance benchmarks and identifying the relative strengths and weaknesses of different transformer architectures. Their introduction of the HateXplain benchmark, which extended existing datasets with human-provided rationales for each annotation, represented a significant step towards explainable AI in hate speech detection.

The HateXplain dataset addressed a fundamental limitation in existing benchmarks by providing not just labels but also explanations for why content was classified as hateful. This addition facilitated the development of models that could not only achieve high accuracy but also provide interpretable justifications for their decisions—a crucial requirement for deployment in real-world content moderation systems where transparency and accountability are essential.

Recent developments and specialized architectures

Recent studies have explored increasingly sophisticated transformer-based approaches. Shojaee et al.15 investigated the application of various transformer architectures specifically for hate speech detection while emphasizing explainable AI components. Their work demonstrated that model transparency could be achieved without significant performance degradation, addressing concerns about the “black box” nature of transformer models.

Cheng et al.16 pioneered the application of prompt-based learning to hate speech detection, showing how task-specific prompts could guide pretrained language models to better understand the nuances of hateful content. This approach proved particularly effective for few-shot learning scenarios and demonstrated the potential for more efficient fine-tuning strategies.

Bias, fairness, and robustness considerations

Recent research has increasingly focused on the fairness and robustness of hate speech detection systems. Pavlopoulos et al.17 conducted important analyses of sensitivity, skewness, and preference biases in hate speech datasets, revealing systematic biases that could lead to unfair treatment of certain groups or topics. Their work emphasized the need for more careful dataset curation and bias-aware model development.

Guzman et al.18 introduced causal approaches to bias mitigation in hate speech models, providing theoretical frameworks and practical techniques for developing fairer detection systems. Their work addressed concerns about models that might inadvertently discriminate against certain demographic groups or linguistic patterns.

Data augmentation and ensemble methods

To address the persistent challenge of limited training data and class imbalance, researchers have explored various data augmentation strategies. Srivastava et al.19 developed comprehensive approaches combining data augmentation with ensemble learning, achieving improved performance while enhancing model robustness against adversarial examples and edge cases.

Saleh et al.20 investigated self-supervised learning approaches for hate speech detection, exploring how models could leverage unlabeled data to improve performance on this challenging task. Their comprehensive study demonstrated the potential for reducing dependence on manually annotated data while maintaining high detection accuracy.

Metaheuristic-based hyperparameter optimization

Recent advancements in swarm intelligence have focused on improving convergence stability, population diversity, and exploration–exploitation balance. For instance, the Fast-Flying Particle Swarm Optimization (FF-PSO) algorithm6 addresses oscillatory behavior and premature convergence in standard PSO by employing a quantization-based velocity update mechanism and dynamic search dimension adaptation, leading to refined convergence and improved solution quality.

Similarly, the Quantized Orthogonal Experimentation Salp Swarm Algorithm (QOX-SSA)7 enhances the standard SSA by integrating orthogonal experimentation strategies to improve population diversity and balance global exploration with local exploitation. Building upon this, Adaptive Dimensional Search-based Orthogonal Experimentation SSA (ADOX-SSA)21 introduces adaptive dimensional search and quantization-based operators to further strengthen convergence speed and robustness across complex optimization landscapes.

Recent hybrid variants22,23 especially Local Search SSA (LS-SSA) have demonstrated improved hyperparameter tuning capability in deep learning models, particularly CNNs24 for medical imaging tasks, by enhancing local exploitation and avoiding premature convergence. Additionally, quantum-inspired approaches such as Quantum Adaptive Mutation Operator PSO (QAMO-PSO)25 incorporate qubit dynamics and adaptive mutation mechanisms to expand the search space and improve convergence reliability in high-dimensional optimization problems.

Despite these advancements, most of these approaches26–29 are primarily evaluated on numerical benchmarks, feature selection, or neural network training tasks. Their direct application to transformer-based hyperparameter optimization in Natural Language Processing (NLP) remains limited. Unlike these methods, which introduce additional hybrid operators30 and increased algorithmic complexity, the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO) employed in this work provides a simpler yet effective optimization mechanism with fewer control parameters and stable convergence behavior.

Therefore, this work extends the application of metaheuristic optimization to transformer fine-tuning, addressing a critical gap in adapting swarm intelligence techniques to large-scale NLP models characterized by high-dimensional, computationally expensive, and highly sensitive hyperparameter spaces.

Hyperparameter optimization gap

A persistent limitation across the majority of studies is their reliance on manual, trial-and-error, or computationally expensive grid-search-based hyperparameter tuning. While architectural innovations have received substantial attention, the systematic optimization of model hyperparameters—which can significantly impact performance—remains largely unexplored. This represents a critical gap, particularly for complex models like DeBERTaV3, which have numerous sensitive hyperparameters that interact in non-obvious ways.

Scalability and efficiency concerns

Most existing approaches focus primarily on maximizing accuracy without adequate consideration of computational efficiency and scalability requirements for real-world deployment. The computational cost of training and inference for large transformer models poses practical challenges for content moderation at social media scale.

Adversarial robustness

Limited attention has been paid to adversarial robustness, despite the fact that users actively attempt to circumvent detection systems through various obfuscation techniques. The arms race between hate speech propagators and detection systems requires more robust, adaptive approaches.

Evaluation methodology limitations

Many studies rely on single-dataset evaluations without adequate consideration of generalization across different platforms, domains, or temporal periods. The dynamic nature of online language and hate speech patterns requires more comprehensive evaluation frameworks.Furthermore, limited reporting of hyperparameter configurations, optimization strategies, and training reproducibility settings across prior studies inhibit fair comparison and replicability, highlighting the need for more systematic and transparent optimization frameworks.

Motivation for current work

The comprehensive review of existing literature reveals that while significant progress has been made in architectural innovation for hate speech detection, a critical gap exists in systematic hyperparameter optimization. The majority of high-performing transformer-based approaches rely on ad-hoc hyperparameter tuning, potentially leaving substantial performance improvements unexplored.Our work directly addresses this gap by introducing a metaheuristic optimization approach to systematically fine-tune a state-of-the-art transformer architecture (DeBERTaV3).Among available metaheuristic strategies, the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO) offers a good balance between exploration and exploitation, minimal control parameters, and stable convergence characteristics. These properties make it particularly suitable for optimizing transformer hyperparameters, where the search space is high-dimensional and computationally expensive to evaluate. By automating the hyperparameter optimization process using the Grey Wolf Optimizer, we aim to unlock the full potential of advanced transformer models while establishing a replicable framework for systematic optimization in hate speech detection tasks.The integration of metaheuristic optimization with transformer architectures provides a structured and systematic framework that addresses both performance and methodological limitations identified in the literature.This approach promises to advance the state-of-the-art while providing a principled framework for hyperparameter optimization in similar NLP tasks.Despite significant advancements in metaheuristic optimization, their application to transformer-based NLP models remains limited. Existing approaches are primarily evaluated on benchmark optimization problems or traditional machine learning models, with minimal focus on large-scale transformer architectures. This gap motivates the proposed GWO-based framework for efficient and systematic hyperparameter optimization in transformer-based hate speech detection.

DeBERTaV3 model architecture

The core of our classification model is the DeBERTaV3-base architecture, a state-of-the-art transformer model that builds upon the successes of BERT4 and RoBERTa31. DeBERTaV35,32 introduces several key innovations that make it particularly effective for nuanced language understanding tasks.Disentangled Attention Mechanism: Unlike traditional self-attention mechanisms that combine word content and position embeddings, DeBERTaV3 uses two separate vectors to represent each token—one for its content and one for its relative position. This disentanglement allows the attention mechanism to better model the syntactic and semantic relationships between tokens5.Enhanced Mask Decoder: During pre-training, an absolute position embedding is incorporated into the final softmax layer. This provides the model with additional positional information, improving its ability to predict masked tokens in the language modeling task32.ELECTRA-style Pre-training: Instead of just predicting masked tokens like BERT, DeBERTaV3 uses an ELECTRA-inspired replaced token detection (RTD) objective, where a small generator predicts substitutes for masked tokens and the main model (discriminator) learns to detect whether each token is original or replaced—making training more sample-efficient and improving language understanding.For the downstream task of hate speech classification, we augment the pretrained DeBERTaV3-base model with a custom classification head. This head consists of a linear layer that maps the contextualized representation of the [CLS] token from the final hidden layer of the transformer to a vector of three neurons, corresponding to our three target classes. A softmax activation function is then applied to these logits to produce a probability distribution over the classes.

Discussion of findings

Convergence Analysis: Figure 6 illustrates the convergence behavior of the proposed GWO-based hyperparameter optimization. The best training cross-entropy loss decreases rapidly from approximately 0.5658 in the first iteration to below 0.30 within six iterations, demonstrating effective global exploration. As the optimization progresses, the rate of improvement gradually reduces, and the loss stabilizes around 0.1613 after the 14th iteration. The smooth monotonic decrease without significant oscillations indicates stable convergence and a well-balanced exploration–exploitation mechanism.The convergence behavior can be theoretically justified by the linear reduction of control parameter ‘a’, which transitions the search from exploration (—A— ?‘ 1) to exploitation (—A— !‘1), ensuring stability and preventing oscillatory divergence.The absence of abrupt fluctuations confirms stable convergence characteristics of GWO Metrics Curves: The curves for accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and loss of the proposed model on the test dataset have been plotted against all the epochs and displayed in Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7. The curves indicate stable learning behavior with optimal generalization achieved at Epoch 9. After this point, a slight increase in validation loss suggests the onset of overfitting, which was reduced by selecting the best-performing epoch.

Confusion Matrix Analysis: The confusion matrix in Fig. 8 generated from the test set predictions revealed good classification performance across all three classes. The model showed very few instances of misclassifying hate speech as neutral, which is a critical requirement for any practical moderation system. Furthermore, it demonstrated a strong ability to distinguish between the nuanced categories of hate speech and offensive language, a primary challenge of this dataset. This indicates that the fine-tuned DeBERTaV3 model successfully learned the subtle semantic cues that differentiate these classes.

Statistical Significance: All experiments were conducted using fixed random seeds to ensure reproducibility. Statistical significance between GWO-DeBERTaV3 and baseline models was evaluated using a paired t-test on matched test-set predictions, as both models were assessed on identical samples. The observed performance improvements were statistically significant (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.001$$\end{document}), indicating that the improvements are unlikely to have occurred by chance under identical evaluation conditions. Statistical significance was assessed at a significance level of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha = 0.05$$\end{document}.To further assess training stability, the final GWO-selected configuration was retrained in three independent random seeds (42, 123, 2024). The mean and standard deviation of the performance metrics were computed across these runs to quantify variability due to stochastic training effects.

Sensitivity Analysis:A localized sensitivity analysis was conducted around the optimal hyperparameter configuration identified by GWO. Each hyperparameter was perturbed individually while others were kept fixed. The learning rate exhibited the highest sensitivity, with performance degrading outside the optimal region. The Dropout showed moderate influence on the generalization stability, whereas the weight decay demonstrated comparatively lower sensitivity.

Multi-Objective Optimization: Extending to simultaneously optimize accuracy, speed, and fairness metrics would enhance practical deployment. The fusion of advanced transformer architectures with sophisticated optimization algorithms represents a powerful and promising direction to tackle the persistent challenge of online hate speech detection. The automated hyperparameter optimization framework presented in this work achieves very good competitive performance compared to recent state-of-the-art methods but also provides a foundation for future research in optimized deep learning for content moderation tasks.

However, this study is limited to a single benchmark dataset. Future work should evaluate the proposed optimization framework across multiple datasets and multilingual settings to further validate its generalizability and robustness.

Conclusion and future work

In this paper, we introduced a hybrid framework that integrates the DeBERTaV3 transformer model with the Grey Wolf Optimizer to address the task of hate speech detection. Our approach successfully automated the critical process of hyperparameter tuning, leading to a model with highly good performance compared to recent state-of-the-art methods on the Davidson et al.9 benchmark dataset.The improvements obtained were further validated through statistical significance testing in multiple independent runs, confirming that the performance gains are consistent and not due to random variation.
