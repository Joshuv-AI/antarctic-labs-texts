C onversations over Clicks: Impact of Chatbots on Information Search in Interdisciplinary Learning
Hannah Kim, Sergei L. Kosakovsky Pond, Stephen MacNeil

Abstract
This full research paper investigates the impact of generative AI (GenAI) on the learner experience, with a focus on how learners engage with and utilize the information it provides. In e-learning environments, learners often need to navigate a complex information space on their own. This challenge is further compounded in interdisciplinary fields like bioinformatics, due to the varied prior knowledge and backgrounds. In this paper, we studied how GenAI influences information search in bioinformatics research: (1) How do interactions with a GenAI chatbot influence learners’ orienteering behaviors? ; and (2) How do learners identify information scent in GenAI chatbot responses? We adopted an autoethnographic approach to investigate these questions. GenAI was found to support orienteering once a learning plan was established, but it was counterproductive prior to that. Moreover, traditionally value-rich information sources such as bullet points and related terms proved less effective when applied to GenAI responses. Information scents were primarily recognized through the presence or absence of prior knowledge of the domain. These findings suggest that GenAI should be adopted into e-learning environments with caution, particularly in interdisciplinary learning contexts.

We investigate the impact of GenAI on e-learning in an interdisciplinary context, using information seeking as the theoretical framework. Information seeking is a purposive human information behavior centered on the acquisition of information

Concepts such as orienteering and information scent build upon this foundation. Orienteering is an information search theory inspired by a sport of the same name, in which participants navigate from a shared starting point to a common destination via multiple potential intermediate locations
[
14

[
14

[
15

[
15
,
16

[
15
]

RQ 1:

How do interactions with a GenAI chatbot influence learners’ orienteering behaviors?

RQ 2:

How do learners identify information scent in GenAI chatbot responses?

[
17

Our team was particularly well-positioned for this work due to its diverse interdisciplinary expertise and academic hierarchy, which helped mitigate potential biases. Hence, we provided detailed snapshots of interactions with GenAI, revealing experiential insights that may be generalizable to similar learning contexts.

Our contributions are empirical and opinion-based

We examine how a learner-practitioner interacts with GenAI in an interdisciplinary context. We identify ways in which GenAI supports (e.g., code debugging and document formatting) and distracts (e.g., a heavy verification load, misaligned goals and misplaced anthropomorphic credibility) from orienteering. We also observe a decrease in the learner’s reliance on traditional contextual cues, marked by changes in information diet and increased dependence on the presence or absence of prior knowledge to detect information scent in GenAI responses.

3.

II
Methods

We adopted an autoethnographic approach
[
17
]
to conduct a formative study on interdisciplinary learning and human-AI interaction. We explored our own experiences engaging with a GenAI tool in the context of bioinformatics. The following sections describe the research team, the GenAI system, the experimental design, and the types of interactions analyzed.

II-A

Team Position

In this study, we positioned ourselves not only as researchers but also as stakeholders in bioinformatics. This positioning was made possible by our team’s integration of expertise–an intellectual introgression
[
21
]
–across bioinformatics and human-computer interaction (HCI). The first author is a bioinformatics and HCI researcher pursuing a PhD. They routinely build bioinformatics pipelines and refer to tool documentations. The second author is in bioinformatics, and the last author is in HCI. Both are experienced researchers and teachers at the same university who serve as the first author’s academic advisors. The team was formed around the first author’s interest in interdisciplinary learning. This dynamic reflects the hierarchical structure commonly observed in academic research and education. It is important to note that the first author also assumed the role of a bioinformatics practitioner in the study, due to their intersectional position across both domains. In summary, the second and last authors functioned as researchers and teachers, while the first author occupied a hybrid role as researcher, learner, and practitioner.

The first author is AI-literate: they can critically assess AI, are proficient at communicating with AI, and are frequent users of AI tools
[
11
]
. They have taken multiple AI-related courses and have read papers on protein structure prediction models such as AlphaFold
[

and ESMFold
[

. They also lead the development of an in-house GenAI chatbot and frequently use ChatGPT for tasks such as language translation, grammar checking, and code debugging. Despite this familiarity, the first author had not used GenAI for research purposes prior to the study and instead primarily turned to traditional e-tutorials, motivated by a preference for independent effort and a sense of pride in self-reliance. Throughout the study, they approached the chatbot’s responses with considerable caution, understanding that its training data had an earlier knowledge cutoff date and that the most recent advancements are typically found in academic literature.

The first author explored the task through interactions with the GenAI chatbot and subsequently devised and followed an action plan to produce a task report. Potential bias due to prior topic familiarity was effectively mitigated: the first author found the complexity of the assigned task entirely unexpected but accepted it as a natural aspect of bioinformatics research. III-A

RQ1: Traction for Orienteering

The GenAI chatbot demonstrated potential for supporting orienteering in two key areas: topic exploration and plan execution. These two search modes thematically dominated the information-seeking process of the first author. The complex yet loosely defined task scenario afforded them the flexibility to independently design and pursue an itinerary involving a unique combination of search modes. Interaction with the GenAI chatbot served as the primary mechanism for triggering subsequent information-gathering steps. The first author experimented with the chatbot in various ways to gauge its capabilities in the topic domain (See Table
I
). An inflection point in search modes occurred between query-response exchanges 51 and 52, when the action plan to produce a task report was established (See Figure
3
). The distinctive trend shift is evident in the top three most frequent query intents before (exploring; Table
I
a–c) and after (following a plan; Table
I
b, 1g–h) developing the plan. The most common trigger for search modes after plan development was the pursuit of that plan. Consequently, queries following the inflection point were not only more predictable but also more focused in intent.

Briefly, assisted by the GenAI chatbot, the first author produced a report that met the second author’s standard for task completion. The chatbot played a supportive role across various stages of the task report preparation. The final report includes: a refined task definition; an overview of the prospective advantages and limitations of comparative sequence analysis models; and a list of attributes required to install, run and understand the models. A list of transformer-based protein language models was compiled using the GenAI chatbot’s response as a starting point (See Code 5). Each model was examined with respect to its original publication, source code availability, runnability on a local or institutional computing environments, architectural design, expected inputs and outputs, empirical validation methods, and key applications. After setting up the overall structure, the remaining time was spent filling in details for each model, with the GenAI chatbot effectively supporting code debugging and document formatting beyond occasional queries related to technical concept exploration. As a result, many post-planning queries became more narrowly focused in intent.

Some inherent behavioral features of the GenAI chatbot were viewed positively in the context of orienteering. Though aware of the risk of anthropomorphizing, the first author described the GenAI chatbot in person-oriented terms in the reflective journals (See Table
II
). In summary, while they held negative views about the chatbot’s style and content, they found its attitude pleasing.

III-B

RQ1: Distraction for Orienteering

The GenAI chatbot was helpful for orienteering with narrow focuses after a structured plan established; however, it proved less effective for exploration prior to that point. A recurring theme in the reflective journals was the distress caused by distractions during pre-planning exploration. Although the original orienteering theory does not inherently account for adversarial situations resulting from low-performing search assistants, this study surfaced several forms of distractions that were antithetic to effective orienteering. While quantitative measures revealed the prevalence of certain patterns, the nuances of relying on GenAI for orienteering were more clearly illuminated through qualitative analysis. Codes 1-3 capture user responses to GenAI-induced distractions.

The theme of distress emerged repeatedly throughout the study. One contributing factor was the heavy load of verification. As an AI-literate bioinformatician aware of GenAI’s limitations (See Section IIA), the first author was cautious about accepting information from the GenAI chatbot at face value, so they frequently verified GenAI responses with alternative sources. However, when not explicitly prompted to summarize and unconstrained by context length, the chatbot’s responses tended to be verbose. Across 74 responses that were neither length-limited nor empty, the average response contained 207.68 words, compared to an average of 32.11 words per corresponding query. Thus, each instance of exchange-based information retrieval required the first author to verify a
“wall of text”
six times longer than the original query. Moreover, each response functioned as a singular, self-contained summary, offering no alternative. This was frustrating for the first author, as it contrasted sharply with the dynamic nature of human conversation–something GenAI ostensibly aims to replicate. Due to these characteristics, the first author experienced verification fatigue (See Code 1). The sheer frequency with which information had to be validated was unhumanly rapid and dehumanizing.

Code 1. Verification fatigue in evaluating GenAI content

“When [GenAI] gave me something more specific, I was busy verifying facts. I was correcting and testing the GenAI so much that the entire conversation was moving away from the main task at one point. It was a fight between me and the GenAI without necessarily getting the task done. … However, it overall felt very forced to ask followup questions because it was generating information at a superficial level, and as of now, the most frequent follow up has been about information verification.”

Another source of distress during orienteering with the GenAI chatbot was the goal misalignment. Establishing mutual understanding is critical for progressing in a coherent direction and pursuing in-depth knowledge. However, the chatbot’s tendency to
“generat[e] information at a superficial level”
while
“fight[ing the user] … without necessarily getting the task done”
(See Code 1) signaled a lack of agreement. The first author encountered multiple instances of such misalignment and attempted to mitigate the issue by providing additional prompts and context (See Code 2). Despite these efforts, repeated failures to reach an agreement left them feeling disoriented. As the first author struggled to steer the chatbot toward task-relevant directions, they frequently found themselves a
“passive recipient”
with limited agency over the direction of the exchange.

Code 2. Unsuccessful attempts to refine GenAI responses

“[I am the one who] forced the GenAI to re-word the task, so it is not like I did not know our task was worded imprecisely, but there was an odd satisfaction in the re-wording process in the style of discussion. ‘Hopefully the GenAI has enough context for what we are about to do’, I thought. … There was one brief moment of excitement about what it generated for a question, but that did not really lead to anything interesting on google. [The information] felt like empty calories.”

The first author was distracted from orienteering as the chatbot interface made it easy to misplace anthropomorphic credibility onto GenAI. Because GenAI mimics patterns of human behavior learned from its training data, the first author struggled to describe its behavioral features without relying on person-oriented terms (See Table
II
), despite conscious efforts to avoid doing so. We identified an instance in which the first author’s early misplacement of anthropomorphic credibility onto the GenAI chatbot resulted in misknowledge that persisted and propagated throughout the journaling process. “ensure”
or
“rest assured”
) and humor (e.g.,
“neigh Oh, hello there!”
), a common rhetorical strategy used for improving persuasion. However, the information it provided was inaccurate: the actual knowledge cutoff date for the model was December 2023
[

The first author only recognized this error during the later stage of data analysis. In the retrospect, they were able to recall subtle hints pointing to the truth. For example, while GenAI responses predominantly contained information from 2019 to 2021 (See Code 5), certain fragments included information that could only have originated in 2023. The realization caused the first author considerable distress, as the chatbot had subtly diverted their focus from distinguishing reliable information without their awareness.

III-C

RQ2: Traditional Information Diet Declining in Value

In traditional e-tutorials, the information diet is composed of instructional structures implemented by teachers to highlight the value of knowledge. These structures serve as landmarks where information scent appears with sufficient frequency to become an integral part of learners’ information diet. Common examples include bullet points and emphasized related terms. However, in GenAI responses, the typical information diet diminished in value (See Codes 4-6).

The presence of bullet points in GenAI responses did not improve information seeking. The first author found the GenAI chatbot’s overall content either overly obvious (See Table
II
) or demonstrably inaccurate. For instance, in Code 4, the GenAI response offered a simple four-step plan for completing the task scenario within a maximum of 33 hours. However, the plan lacked realistic time estimates and sufficient detail to effectively support task completion. In Code 5, the information conveyed through the bullet points was only partially valuable–presumably because software names were individually treated as tokens–but the details were largely nonsensical.

IV
Interpretation

This formative study revealed key user behaviors during interaction with GenAI in the context of interdisciplinary learning. Using an autoethnographic approach allowed us to capture fine-grained details through first-hand experiences. As a team of researchers, teachers, learners, and practitioners, we designed a study based on a realistic task scenario from the field of bioinformatics. Notably, the first author’s AI literacy was not treated as a bias, but as a user trait expected to become more prevalent in the near future. In the remainder of the section, we leverage concepts from prior research to contextualize our findings and outline future directions.

IV-A

RQ1: How do interactions with a GenAI chatbot influence learners’ orienteering behaviors?

Orienteering with GenAI proceeded smoothly for narrowly focused information needs, where the cost of verification was low; however, the cognitive effort required for verification increased significantly in exploratory search. While the need for information verification has long existed
[
38
]
, the generative nature of AI presents a new challenge by inflating the volume of content to be validated, driven by its tendency to produce plausible-sounding nonsense
[
19
]
and excessive verbosity.

Verification fatigue
[
39
]
emerged as a distinct outcome of bearing cognitive load
[
40
]
in this study, characterized by the cumulative stress and eventual exhaustion caused by the continuous need to verify information. This cognitive load stemmed from the first author’s awareness of GenAI limitations and its potential to generate misleading or incorrect content. Although information verification is often framed to be user responsibility
[
41
]
, our analyses indicated that verifying every step in the orienteering process was not only impractical but also unsustainable.

Using a tutorial is inherently a cost-saving
[
15
]
strategy, an attempt to obtain a condensed summary of knowledge rather than search through an overwhelmingly large knowledge space. If a well-established traditional e-tutorial meeting an information need is present, even conducting a web search across the vast online knowledge space may be less cost-efficient by comparison. Although GenAI can produce similar-looking, readily available e-tutorials, the cost may quickly become exorbitant if users must constantly verify the information. However, because users have the convenient, no-cost option of skipping verification, the heavy verification load does not always fall on GenAI’s immediate users.

The GenAI verification burden that learners do not carry eventually shifts to teachers or evaluators, who are relatively fewer in number. In fact, those who are more likely to propagate the burden are the ones who lack the resources (i.e., time or ability) to sensitively detect issues in problematic content. AI literacy is widely promoted as a promising strategy for navigating the AI era, and our findings support this perspective. IV-B

RQ2: How do learners identify information scent in GenAI chatbot responses?

Generally, information scent conveyed through structural cues is the among the most readily recognized by learners. Such structures typically follow both contextual and visual
[
45
]
organization. Reliance on these cues is also habitual; learners taught in traditional settings are familiar to them. Consequently, structured content serves as a reasonable information diet. In traditional e-tutorials, the order of content reflects a teacher-designed curriculum maintained through linear navigation such as scrolling. However, in the GenAI chatbot responses observed in our study, such information scent was significantly weaker, as the order of appearance was no longer guaranteed. Although GenAI simulated structural cues like bullet points and related terms, these provided only an illusion of value; the true density of useful information in these structures had diminished.

The frequency with which the first author failed to forage value-rich information in GenAI responses was alarmingly high. Overall, the first author found GenAI responses to be bland and overly obvious (See Table

). Much of the text they consumed lacked informational value, being either self-evident, inaccurate, or off-topic. The quote
“[the information] felt like empty calories”
from Code 2 directly invokes the concept of empty calories in nutrition; both cases give the impression of meaningful consumption, but provide little in terms of actual value. Prather et al.’s recent work on the
“illusion of competence”
induced by GenAI chatbots
[

]
offers a glimpse into how misleading representations of informational value may influence learners. The traditional information diet, rooted in traditional e-tutorial experiences, was unsustainable in the context of GenAI responses.

Most information scents detected in this study were based on prior knowledge of the learner. These are contextual cues shaped by each individual’s existing knowledge landscape. They rely heavily on the learner’s subjective perception of informational value. We identified two types of prior knowledge-based information scents, shaped by the presence or absence of prior knowledge. Most information scents emerged to fill an information gap when content piqued the first author’s interest. Information scents shaped by the presence of prior knowledge were rarer; they emerged when the first author, as a learner-practitioner, recognized a familiar concept in a less familiar context. The first author leveraged this knowledge to further request examples related to that concept. This latter approach was naturally more suitable for advanced learners or practitioners, as it required some domain knowledge. Although information scents were generally scarce in GenAI responses, prior knowledge–based scents hold potential to improve information seeking if increased in the information diet.

Our work offers directions for personalization in GenAI from the perspective of diverse stakeholders in bioinformatics-HCI. We hope it invites continued exploration of interdisciplinary learning to better reflect complex viewpoints.

V
Conclusion

Introduction of GenAI brought a paradigm shift in both industry and academia. It is understandably a subject of excitement. In this autoethnography, we systematically conducted a formative study of human-AI interactions. We identified several challenges in orienteering with a simple GenAI system. Notably, we observed that issues such as misplaced anthropomorphic credibility can arise even for an AI-literate researcher working at the intersection of bioinformatics and HCI. Our study shows that information scents in GenAI responses differ significantly from those in traditional e-tutorials. We anticipate these findings will become increasingly relevant as GenAI adoption expands and AI literacy continues to grow. In the future, we aim to apply insights from this study to investigate how information-seeking behavior is influenced when additional context is made available to the GenAI system.

VI
Limitations

While we have captured many interesting human-AI interactions in this study, we are aware that some of the inconveniences listed above is not universal. Some models may have longer context lengths, may have been retrieval-augmented beyond knowledge cutoff date, or may have a higher number of parameters. However, we believe that much of our experience will remain relevant in the GenAI landscape as long as there is information outside of the model vocabulary.
