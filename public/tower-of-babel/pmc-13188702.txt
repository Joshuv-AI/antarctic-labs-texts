Pilot study of the DeStress for Health Program: a community-engaged stress reduction and cancer prevention intervention for rural communities
Authors: Devon Noonan, Amnazo Muhirwa, Elaine Sang, Nicole Calhoun, Ardis Crews, Kellen Peter, Henry Crews, Lucette Mercer, Lisa Macon Harrison, Shauna L Guthrie, Kathryn I Pollak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStress related to personal, social, and environmental factors has been linked to engaging in unhealthy behaviors. These behaviors may increase cancer risk. Most current prevention programs do not address stress related to social factors and related stresses that are driving unhealthy behaviors. The purpose of this study was to pilot the DeStress for Heath Program.MethodsWe conducted a pilot randomized controlled trial. Community members from two rural counties were randomized to the DeStress for Health Program group or a waitlist control group. The DeStress for Health Program, developed using a community engaged approach, is a 4-class, in-person, nurse delivered evidenced-based intervention that uses both positive psychology and health behaviour goal setting to address stress reduction and cancer risk behaviours. We offered the program to the waitlist control group after completion of all assessments. Self-report surveys were completed at baseline and 30 days post-intervention in the DeStress arm to assess feasibility and acceptability and 30 days post 4-week wait time in the control. Study outcomes for feasibility included recruitment, retention, and engagement, and acceptability included positive endorsement of the intervention and changes in stress and health behaviours.ResultsWe recruited 29 participants in 6 months, missing our feasibility recruitment benchmark. We did not meet our feasibility benchmark of having 75% of participants attend all 4 classes, with 69% of residents attended at least 2 classes. We retained 21 of 29 (72%) participants at follow-up. Acceptability results include the DeStress group reported that the intervention reduced daily stress (n = 13, 81.3%), helped encourage better nutrition (n = 15, 93.8%) and physical activity (n = 15, 93.8%), and would recommend the program to a friend (n = 15, 93.8%). DeStress improved scores on the Perceived Stress Scale over time compared to the waitlist control.DiscussionThe DeStress intervention is acceptable and demonstrates improvements in stress. Feasibility benchmarks were not all met, and suggestions for future adaptations to improve feasibility are discussed. Background

Most Americans (75%) living in rural populations have health behaviors that increase their risk for chronic disease including smoking, insufficient physical activity, insufficient sleep, alcohol consumption, and poor nutrition [1, 2]. As a result, rural residents self-report worse health outcomes than urban residents [3]. In addition to poor self-reported health, studies show that rural residents have worse actual health. Rural populations experience poorer health outcomes, higher risk for chronic disease, and higher rates of premature death compared to their urban counterparts [2].

In Granville (Rural Urban Continuum Code, RUCC = 4) and Vance (RUCC = 4) counties, two rural counties in the North Piedmont region of North Carolina, levels of cancer mortality are among the highest in the state [4, 5]. Many cancers with high incidence rates (e.g., lung, breast, prostate, and colorectal cancer) have been linked to modifiable risk behaviors such as tobacco use, physical inactivity, and poor nutrition or obesity [2]. Residents in both counties have high rates of these health risk behaviors [4].

We learned through prior work that included community focus groups and surveys that the residents in these counties are less concerned about their cancer risk and more concerned about stress related to high poverty rates, high crime rates, lack of community cohesion, and lack of health care access and that this stress affects their engagement in health promoting behaviors [6, 7]. Stress related to personal, social, and environmental factors has been linked to unhealthy behaviors that may increase cancer risk [8–10]. The National Cancer Institute (NCI) has designated poverty as a risk factor for cancer [11]. Public health efforts aimed at reducing unhealthy behaviors should take a multifactorial approach, considering stress related to personal, social, and environmental factors that are driving these behaviors.

To do so, in partnership with the Granville Vance Health Department and The Green Rural Redevelopment Group (GRRO), a local community-based micro-farming organization, we conducted a formative mixed methods study using a 23-question survey (n = 50) and three focus groups (n = 17) to explore residents’ experiences of stress, their stress-related cancer risk behaviours, and potential community interventions to address these behaviours. Residents identified multiple sources of environmental and psychosocial stressors as well as positive health behaviours that could lower cancer risk (e.g., exercise to reduce stress) and negative behaviours associated with cancer risk (e.g., overeating, smoking, or alcohol use as coping mechanisms for stress). Interventions suggested by residents to promote stress reduction included building social connections within the community, teaching coping skills, and providing programmes delivered through in-person or online modalities (e.g., positive thinking exercises, physical activity programmes). Based on the results of our formative work, in collaboration with our partners, we designed “DeStress for Health,” an intervention to reduce stress through positive psychology.

Positive Psychosocial Well-Being (PPWB) interventions may represent an effective approach to reduce stress and improve health behaviors. PPWB interventions include gratitude exercises, and practicing optimism, savoring, and mindfulness. Accumulating evidence suggests that PPWB interventions reduce stress, increase positive emotion, and reduce disease risk [12]. Research has shown that individuals who engage in PPWB exercises are more likely to engage in healthy behaviors, such as healthy eating and exercise, have lower levels of depressive symptoms, and have greater resilience [12–14]. PPWB is postulated to help individuals deal with stress and better self-regulate health promoting behavior [13].

Combining PPWB with behaviour change techniques, such as goal setting, may further increase engagement in health promotion behaviours. Goal setting is an important facilitator of behaviour change [15]. Goal setting helps to improve self-efficacy through problem solving barriers and increases knowledge to improve health risk behaviours [15–17]. Yet, there are few empirical studies that address both health behaviour change and stress reduction concurrently using a PPWB approach. Among the few studies available, most have focused on addressing cardiovascular risk reduction and these studies have been found to be feasible, acceptable, and have shown promising findings in improving health behaviours [16, 18]. However, there remain few studies that address stress and cancer risk reduction behaviours concurrently in the community and even fewer that have adopted a true community engaged approach for intervention development [14].

To fill this gap, the aim of the DeStress for Health Program pilot was to assess the feasibility and acceptability of a nurse-delivered, evidence-based intervention that uses both positive psychology and health behavior goal setting to address stress and cancer risk behaviors. We conducted a randomized clinical trial to assess (1) the feasibility of the program including recruitment of participants to the trial over a 6-month period, retention, participant engagement/attendance at classes, and changes in individual outcomes including stress and health behaviors and (2) acceptability including positive endorsement of the intervention. The DeStress for Health Program is one of the first cancer prevention programs that focus on stress as a major driver of unhealthy behaviors that increase cancer risk, making the study innovative. Results of this study will be used to inform the feasibility of our methods for a future large evaluation of the DeStress for Health Program.

Materials and methods

Design

This study was a pilot randomized controlled trial comparing DeStress to a wait-list control conducted in 2019 with recruitment from May through November. We surveyed participants in the intervention group at baseline and 30 days after the end of the intervention, and those in the waitlist control at baseline and 8 weeks post-enrollment. This trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov in December 2018 and can be accessed at https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT03776890.

Ethical considerations

We informed participants they could withdraw from the study at any time. This study received Duke University Institutional Review Board (Pro00100183) approval and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Sample

Inclusion criteria were (1) 18 years of age or older, (2) resident in one of the two rural counties of focus, Granville or Vance counties (Rural Urban Continuum Code = 4), (3) interested in participating in a stress reduction study, and (4) English language speaker. A permuted block randomization was used to assign participants to the DeStress group or the waitlist control group. A a priori sample size of 30 was determined to be sufficient to analyze feasibility outcomes in the trial [19].

Study procedure

Participants were recruited via the community (e.g., at community events, by word of mouth from our partners, with flyers) and social media (Facebook). For community recruitment, we placed flyers that included a contact telephone number for the study in various community agencies (e.g., health department, primary care offices, libraries). For recruitment at events, we used a sign-up sheet and called interested individuals who provided contact information. For Facebook recruitment, we created an advertising profile for the study and linked the advertisement to a secure Qualtrics survey where participants could enter their name and contact phone number. Participants were consented over the phone prior to attending class and randomized to the DeStress group or the waitlist control by the research staff. The statistician provided a randomization table, consisting of a computer-generated simple random sampling table, which was kept in a secure folder and accessed for randomization assignment only after consent. Neither the participants nor the research staff were blinded to the study arm. For participants randomized to the DeStress group, we obtained the baseline assessment and immediately placed them in the DeStress class. We obtained follow-up data 30 days after class completion. We chose the 30-day time frame to match the short-term assessment time point in health behaviour trials. For the waitlist control group, baseline assessments were conducted at enrollment. Participants then completed a 4-week waiting period (to approximate the class duration), followed by a follow-up assessment 30 days later, approximately 8 weeks after enrollment. After the 30-day follow-up, those participants were offered the DeStress Program. Participants received $20 for completing all surveys ($10 for baseline, $10 for end of intervention or 8 weeks post-enrollment).

Intervention group

DeStress for Health Program

The DeStress for Health Program consisted of four consecutive classes focused on positive psychology exercises (e.g., three good things, gratitude, and savoring) and goal setting and action planning related to healthy eating, increased physical activity, and tobacco cessation. The DeStress Program is theoretically grounded in Goal Setting Theory [20] employing structured goal formulation and action planning as mechanisms to facilitate behaviour change. Setting specific goals via action plans also provides a strategy for organising health behaviour information and skills into practical and manageable steps that can be acted on [21]. Goal setting facilitates self-regulation, as goals define achievable action and allows people to develop self-control skills to meet their goals [15, 16, 18]. This foundation is complemented by key constructs from Social Cognitive Theory [22], which are integrated to address psychological and environmental barriers to change, enhance behavioural capacity for goal initiation, and strengthen self-efficacy.

Our intervention conceptual framework posits that health behaviour goal setting combined with positive psychology exercises within the DeStress intervention will help improve self-regulation, which should lead to improvements in select cancer risk behaviours (see Fig. 1).

Conceptual model for DeStress for Health intervention

A treatment manual was developed to ensure intervention fidelity, and the 4-week class block was chosen based on feedback from our community partners about keeping the class length reasonable. Specifically, each week a nurse provided participants with instructions for completing positive psychology exercises on their own as well as counseling and goal setting regarding fruit and vegetable intake, physical activity, and smoking cessation. Program participants chose weekly goals related to one of the behaviors. The Green Rural Redevelopment Group partnered with our program and provided a bag of fresh fruit and vegetables to all participants at each class. Specifics about each of the four classes are detailed in a Supplemental Table. Classes were held once a week in the evenings at one of two community locations. Class size was capped at 15 people.

Waitlist control

Participants randomized to the waitlist control completed all assessments prior to attending DeStress classes. At the completion of the 8-week post-enrollment assessment, participants were signed up to attend class and receive the intervention.

Study measures

Feasibility

Feasibility was defined by the ability to (a) recruit 30 participants to the study in 6 months, (b) retain 70% of participants in the study, and (c) ensure their attendance at most (over 50%) intervention classes. We also examined stress and health behaviours and feasibility was defined as any reduction in stress and improvement in at least one health behaviour over the intervention period. To evaluate changes in health behaviours and stress, we used the following measures:

Fruit and vegetable intake. We measured fruit and vegetable intake using a question from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance Survey (BRFSS) [23]: “During the past month, not counting juice, how many times per day, week, or month did you eat fruit? Count fresh, frozen, or canned fruit.” We used the number of days for analysis. We measured vegetable intake using a similar question: “During the past month, not counting juice, how many times per day, week, or month did you eat vegetables? Count fresh, frozen, or canned vegetables.” We used the number of days for analysis.

Acceptability

To determine acceptability for the DeStress group, we asked participants to indicate on a 5-point Likert scale (Not at all–Extremely) how useful the intervention had been and whether they would recommend the intervention to a friend. The intervention was deemed acceptable if most participants (over 75%) indicated that they would be very or extremely likely to recommend it to a friend and rated the intervention components as very or extremely useful. Feasibility and acceptability definitions were adapted from published criteria [27, 28].

Analytic strategy

Descriptive statistics were used to examine demographics, feasibility, and acceptability outcomes including percent and medians. In exploratory analysis to determine the preliminary influence of the DeStress Program on stress, distress, exercise, and diet, we started with straightforward tests for differences between the two groups (DeStress Program vs. waitlist control). Independent samples t-tests were used to test for differences among continuous variables, while chi-squared, Fisher exact tests and Wilcoxon methods (when appropriate based on data distribution) were used to test for differences among categorical variables. To examine change in outcomes over time (from baseline to follow-up), separate mixed-effects general linear models were developed for each outcome. The DeStress Program and the waitlist control educational modalities were analyzed as fixed effects, and time (baseline vs. follow-up) was analyzed as a repeated measures effect. All analysis included completers of baseline and follow-up assessments.

Feasibility of recruitment, class attendance, and retention

We recruited 29 participants in 6 months, missing our feasibility recruitment benchmark. We recruited 24 participants (82%) from community-based recruitment and 5 (17%) from social media. We did not meet our feasibility benchmark of having 75% of participants attend all 4 classes. In the intervention arm, 69% of residents attended at least 2 classes. We retained 21 of 29 (72%) participants at follow-up.

Feasibility outcomes from baseline to follow-up

As seen in Table 2, DeStress improved scores on the Perceived Stress Scale more over time when compared with the wait-list control group. In fact, scores on the Perceived Stress Scale improved (e.g., stress decreased) over time for those in the DeStress group (6.8 vs. 5.9), while they regressed (e.g., stress increased) for those in the waitlist control group (6.2 vs. 7.0). Similarly, those in the DeStress group saw a greater increase in the number of days of moderate exercise per week, increasing by 0.6 more days per week, compared to 0.3 more days per week in the wait-list control group. Finally, in the DeStress group, two participants (12.5%) reported having smoked in the past 7 days at baseline, with only one participant reporting cigarette use at follow-up (4.5%) (not tabled). Conversely, no participants in the control arm reported smoking at baseline, yet one participant (4.5%) reported smoking at follow-up (not tabled).

Discussion

This pilot randomized controlled trial evaluated the feasibility and acceptability of the DeStress Program compared to a wait-list control. We were just under our recruitment benchmark as we were able to recruit 29 of the 30 participants planned into the study within 6 months. For this study, community-based recruitment proved to be most successful. We recruited most participants from community events co-facilitated by our partners, highlighting the importance of including community partners in the research process when developing and implementing community-based interventions to eliminate research mistrust, a common barrier to recruitment [29]. We met our acceptability benchmark with most participants rating the intervention as useful. We held our classes at local community organizations to encourage attendance, yet we did not reach our attendance feasibility benchmark as only 69% of participants attended at least two classes. In further exploring attendance records, we found a seasonal effect to attendance: classes were held in the evenings from July to December, and we noticed anecdotally that attendance dropped over time as the evenings became darker, possibly due to safety, weather, and transportation issues. Reasons for lack of attendance should be assessed in possible future studies. In future programs, providing participants with multiple reminders and boosters between classes may be beneficial. Further, given the current increase in telehealth or text-based approaches to delivering interventions, it may be beneficial for future DeStress classes to be held using a hybrid model to increase attendance.

We found changes in health behaviours, most notably with a significant increase in days of moderate activity in the DeStress group compared to the control. Given the small number of people that use tobacco, it is hard to draw conclusions based on the data; however, it is noteworthy that one person reported having quit smoking during the intervention period. Overall, health behaviour results suggest that the DeStress Program has the potential to make meaningful positive changes in healthy food intake, physical activity, and possibly tobacco use, and should be tested in future research to determine efficacy. Finally, it is noteworthy that the waitlist control saw an increase in vegetables before interacting with the intervention. This may be due to higher levels of general motivation to improve cancer risk behaviours that were acted during the waitlist period before they received the intervention.

PPWB interventions have been shown to decrease stress; however, meta-analyses suggest that the effect of these interventions is small [30]. It is, therefore, noteworthy that the DeStress Program improved scores on the Perceived Stress Scale more over time when compared with the waitlist control group, providing a signal that the intervention has the potential to decrease stress when delivered with a health behaviour change intervention. These results need to be confirmed in a fully powered study designed to take into account the feasibility challenges we faced.

There are limitations of this current study that must be considered. The majority of our small sample were African-American females, limiting generalizability in general and to other groups. We randomized at the individual level, and therefore, contamination of the intervention is a possibility. However, given we were recruiting broadly from the community, the risk was deemed to be low. Health behaviour and stress outcomes need to be interpreted with caution, as we are not powered to detect differences. Reliance on self-report data also has the potential to bias our results and our lack of masking may have increased bias. Attendance at class sessions was low, and we did not collect data to gain an in-depth understanding of this using qualitative data. Our study is dated as it was conducted starting in 2019; given the proliferation of telehealth and other hybrid approaches to increase reach of health care, future studies should employ these approaches.

Conclusion and implications

It is imperative to recognize the relationship between stress and health behaviors, especially for residents in rural communities given the link between stress and cancer risk behaviors and the lack of prevention resources [31]. However, most health promotion and cancer risk reduction programs in rural communities do not take a more holistic approach and include interventions to address stress as a major component driving engagement in unhealthy behaviors, making the DeStress Program unique and innovative. It is important to incorporate a more holistic approach to cancer risk reduction and health behavior change given the clear association between stress and engaging in unhealthy behaviors [8–10]. It is critical that health promotion programs be co-designed with communities to make sure these programs address the unique needs of the communities they are intended for and to ensure acceptability and sustainability. For example, without the extensive formative work done as part of this study to understand stress was the main concern of the community and how stress related to risk behaviors, our program would not have been designed the way it was. Further, the DeStress Program underscores the importance of leveraging community partnerships and linking intervention programs with community resources, particularly in rural communities where health-related resources can be scarce. In conclusion, we found the DeStress Program to be acceptable; however, we did not meet our feasibility benchmarks. We found that for in-person classes, attendance was problematic, suggesting that we may want to consider hybrid models that combine in-person and remote classes to increase engagement and limit potential barriers of attendance. Remote options have potential to reach many more residents to address stress and cancer risk, and they should be tested in future studies.
