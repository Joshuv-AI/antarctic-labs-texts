An end-to-end deep learning framework with nonstationary weighting strategy for enhancing reservoir landslide displacement prediction
Authors: Ningxin Weng, Lei Fan, Lihuang Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of temporal reservoir landslide displacement using key environmental variables, such as rainfall depth, reservoir water level, and groundwater level, is crucial for early warning. These variables exhibit distinct, time-varying influences on displacement, yet conventional deep learning models often overlook such dynamics by adopting stationary sliding windows and assigning equal importance to all variables and timesteps, thereby limiting forecasting accuracy. To address this limitation, we propose a novel Dynamic Weight Generator (DWG) that learns per-variable, per-timestep weights within a user-defined historical window. DWG generates a weight map applied element-wise to normalized inputs before they are fed into a prediction backbone, enabling the model to emphasize critical variables and influential timesteps while down-weighting less relevant ones. DWG is integrated into representative deep-learning models, including convolutional neural networks, long short-term memory networks, transformers, and Mamba architectures. All models are trained end-to-end, allowing the weight map to be optimized directly for displacement prediction. Compared with conventional stationary approaches, the proposed framework consistently improves accuracy across all backbones. Overall, DWG effectively captures dynamic, variable-dependent lag effects, enhancing temporal landslide displacement prediction.Keywords: Landslide, Displacement, Prediction, Deep learning, NonstationarySubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Mathematics and computing, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Landslide displacement prediction plays a crucial role in landslide early warning systems and hazard mitigation strategies, particularly in reservoir landslide areas where slope instability can result in severe threats to human safety and infrastructure stability1–3. Given the increasing frequency of extreme climate events and intensified human activities, the need for accurate prediction methods has become more urgent4,5. Achieving accurate predictions of temporal displacement requires accurately modeling the complex, dynamic and nonlinear contributions from various environmental variables, including rainfall depth (RD), reservoir water level (RWL), and groundwater level (GWL)6,7.

Various methodologies have been employed for predicting temporal reservoir landslide displacement, ranging from traditional statistical techniques and physics-based modeling to modern machine learning and deep learning approaches. Conventional statistical methods, such as regression analyses8 and autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models9, often use historical displacement data coupled with a single environmental variable (typically either RWL or GWL), to predict future landslide displacement10. Physics-based numerical modeling has also been employed for analyzing reservoir landslide behavior. For instance, finite element methods (FEM) and coupled hydro-mechanical models can simulate slope deformation driven by hydrological processes, including reservoir water level fluctuations, groundwater seepage, and pore water pressure changes11,12. These approaches provide physically grounded deformation predictions by solving governing equations of solid mechanics and fluid flow. While physics-based methods offer mechanistic insight, their application is constrained by the need for detailed geotechnical characterization and substantial computational resources, particularly when modeling complex, transient hydrological boundary conditions over extended time periods. More recently, machine learning and deep learning frameworks, including long short-term memory (LSTM)13 and convolutional neural network (CNN)14, have demonstrated considerable performance enhancement in landslide displacement prediction, thanks to their superb abilities to capture complex, nonlinear relationships between displacements and associated contributing environmental variables.

Despite these advancements, many existing studies on prediction of temporal reservoir landslide displacement exhibit significant limitations. When incorporating the lagged effect of an environmental variable on landslide displacement, existing models usually employ an equal-weight to all environmental variables within a sliding window prior to the timestep of prediction. This conventional approach (equal-weight) is straightforward and simple to implement. However, since it treats all timesteps and all variables within a sliding, user-defined temporal window as equally informative at the input stage, this approach can limit a model’s predictive accuracy. This is because the contributions of the input environmental variables vary across different variables as well as different timesteps. For example, impacts of hydrological and meteorological drivers on reservoir landslide displacements often evolve differently15,16. Therefore, incorporating a non-stationary lag strategy that adapts to temporal variability is essential, seeking to better align model inputs with the displacement response.

To address this limitation, we develop a Dynamic Weight Generator (DWG) in this study. The DWG learns a weight for each variable at each timestep for each displacement prediction. In our approach, the historical time series of three considered environmental variables, namely RD, RWL, and GWL, form a multi-channel temporal sequence. The DWG processes this sequence and outputs an optimized weight map to reflect distinct contributions of each variable at each timestep. The DWG and the displacement prediction backbone are trained jointly in an end-to-end manner with a goal of minimizing the prediction error in displacement.

The main contributions of this study are threefold. First, we propose a novel DWG that learns per-variable, per-timestep importance weights within a historical observation window, addressing the limitation of conventional equal-weight approaches that ignore the temporal variability of environmental contributions. Second, DWG is designed as a model-agnostic, plug-in module that can be integrated with any deep learning backbone and trained end-to-end, providing a flexible and general framework applicable across diverse architectures. Third, we provide a comprehensive evaluation across four representative deep learning architectures (CNN, LSTM, Transformer, and Mamba) with ablation studies on window length and regularization coefficients, an analysis of the learned gating coefficients, and computational cost reporting, offering insight into how different architectures interact with the proposed dynamic weighting mechanism.

Study area and data

The Daping landslide is located in Zigui County, Hubei Province, on the left bank of the Yangtze River, approximately 68.09 km from the Three Gorges Dam17. The front edge of the landslide mass has been partially submerged below the RWL of the Three Gorges Reservoir, while the elevation of the rear edge is 365 m. The landslide has a longitudinal length of 380 m, a transverse width of 1200 m, and an average thickness of 70 m. In plain view, the landslide exhibits a tongue-shaped morphology. The overall slope angle is about 175° on the western side and 170° on the eastern side. As for cross-section, the landslide profile is generally convex, with relatively steeper terrain in the rear zone. Several seasonal gullies trending from north to south have developed within the landslide body. The left and right boundaries are delineated by gullies. The landslide is classified as a rock landslide. The sliding mass primarily consists of fragmented rock blocks mixed with soil.

The data used in this research comprise hourly RD obtained from a single-element rain gauge, hourly RWL from a hydrological station upstream of the Yangtze River, hourly GWL from the groundwater level measurements, and hourly displacements monitored by a global navigation satellite system (GNSS) sensor within the Daping landslide. The spatial distribution of these monitoring instruments is presented in Fig. 1. The observation period extends from September 7, 2022, to April 6, 2024, as displayed in Fig. 2. During this period, the highest hourly rainfall intensity reached 23 mm on July 4, 2023. The RWL ranged from 146 m to 175 m, whereas the GWL varied between 9.9 m and 38.2 m. Overall, the temporal variations of RWL and GWL exhibited broadly consistent trends.

The adoption of hourly temporal resolution in this study is motivated by the operational needs of early warning systems for reservoir landslides in the Three Gorges Reservoir area. Rapid reservoir water level fluctuations (up to several meters within days during scheduled drawdown periods) and intense rainfall episodes can trigger accelerated displacement within hours, necessitating predictions at sub-daily intervals for timely hazard response. Hourly monitoring data from GNSS sensors and hydrological stations are increasingly available for major landslides in this region, making hourly-scale prediction both feasible and practically relevant.

Methodology

Data pre-processing

Data pre-processing steps include sorting data chronologically, standardizing using Z-score normalization, and partitioning the dataset into training and testing subsets with a 70/30 ratio, respectively. Data pre-processing begins with organizing the raw dataset chronologically to preserve the inherent temporal relationships among observations. Subsequently, each environmental factor (hourly RD, RWL, and GWL) and the hourly measured displacements undergo standardization through the Z-score normalization. Specifically, each raw observation \:x is transformed into a normalized value \:z using the formula , where and denote the mean and standard deviation computed from the full observation dataset for each corresponding variable. The same standardization parameters are applied to all data partitions, ensuring consistent scaling across training and testing. In this way, all input variables are rescaled to a common scale with zero mean and unit variance, ensuring numerical stability and facilitating faster convergence during training. The normalized dataset is then systematically divided into training and testing subsets, with the first 70% of the temporal sequence allocated to training and the remaining 30% used for testing. The training subset, comprising earlier data points, is used to train the model for adapting to the realistic prediction scenario. In contrast, the testing subset, containing later data points, serves to evaluate the model’s performance on previously unseen data.

Displacement prediction models

To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed DWG, this study considers four representative deep learning models for landslide displacement prediction, including CNN, LSTM, Transformer, and Mamba. These models are selected for their strong performance in time series prediction and ability to model temporal dependencies.

CNN: CNN is a well-established feed-forward neural architecture originally developed for spatial feature extraction, which has also demonstrated effectiveness in capturing short-term temporal patterns in sequential data14. In this study, the CNN model consists of three one-dimensional convolutional layers, each with a kernel size of 5 and padding of 2 to preserve the temporal dimension. Each convolutional layer is followed by batch normalization and a ReLU activation function. The number of output channels is set to 32 for all convolutional layers. A dropout layer (rate = 0.2) is applied after the convolutional stack to mitigate overfitting. Adaptive average pooling then aggregates the temporal dimension into a fixed-length representation, which is fed into a two-layer fully connected head with dimensions 32, 64, and 1 for the final displacement prediction.

Baseline prediction with conventional stationary approach (equal-weight)

Baseline predictions of landslide displacement are obtained using selected models with conventional approach (equal-weight), where all environmental variables (RD, RWL, and GWL) at all timesteps within a historical window are treated with the same weight at the input stage. In this approach, a suitable historical window length needs to be determined. In this study, the prediction model utilizes a historical window consisting of the most recent \:N=168 hours (7 days) of observations prior to time \:t, to predict the landslide displacement at the time \:t. Using 168 h (i.e., 7 days) as the historical window length is primarily informed by empirical findings documented in existing literature as well as our parametric study, as discussed as follows.

Several previous studies have reported that incorporating one-week historical observations effectively captures predominant short-term interactions between environmental triggers and landslide displacement responses, thus providing a meaningful temporal window for baseline comparisons14,21. Moreover, authors22,23 have also observed weekly cyclic behaviors of reservoir landslide displacement responses driven by environmental triggers. To further justify our chosen window length, we have also performed a parametric investigation on the historical window length ranging from 8 h to 240 h. The relevant results are reported in “Selected window length in the conventional stationary approach”.

Figure 3 provides an overview of the pipeline for generating baseline predictions. Initially, the raw hourly time-series data (including RD, RWL, GWL, and displacement) are normalized as described in “Displacement prediction models”. The normalized data are reshaped into input tensors of shape (B, 168, 3), where B denotes the mini-batch size (a training hyperparameter, see Table 1) and 168 is the sequence length representing the number of hours in the historical window, representing the measured data of environmental variables (i.e., RD, RWL, and GWL) in the past 168 h prior to the prediction point in time . This set of data (model input), and the displacement value at (model output) are used to train the selected prediction models. Training proceeds for a maximum of 300 epochs. After each epoch, the validation loss is evaluated. If the validation loss does not improve for a specified number of consecutive epochs (the patience parameter; see Table 1), training is terminated via early stopping, and the model checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is restored for testing. This early stopping mechanism takes precedence over the epoch limit and ensures that the final model generalizes well to unseen data. The prediction backbones (CNN, LSTM, Transformer, and Mamba) are used as stated in “ Displacement prediction models”. The trained models are then used to predict displacements using the testing data and compared the predicted displacement with as-measured to assess the model’s predictive accuracy.

Performance evaluation metrics

Since displacement data are normalized during data pre-processing, all predicted displacement values are inverse-transformed back to their original scales (i.e., millimeters). This post-processing step ensures that prediction results remained interpretable and directly comparable to as-measured displacements. The performance of the adopted models using our proposed DWG strategy is assessed using four widely adopted metrics, including Root Mean Squared Error (), Mean Absolute Error (), Mean Absolute Percentage Error (), and coefficient of determination () metrics.

Results

Selected window length in the conventional stationary approach

Because the conventional stationary sliding-window approach serves as the baseline for comparison, establishing an appropriate window length is critical to ensuring a fair evaluation between models. An optimal window must balance predictive accuracy with computational efficiency. To determine this, we conducted ablation experiments using multiple window sizes ranging from 8 to 240 h. As summarized in Table 2, prediction performance was highly sensitive to the historical window span, with accuracy varying considerably across both temporal lengths and model architectures. Table 3 reports the window-length ablation results for all four DWG-equipped models, complementing the equal-weight baseline results in Table 2. The results show that DWG-equipped models follow the same general trend as the equal-weight baseline: prediction accuracy improved monotonically with increasing window length. Short windows (8 to 40 h) likely provided insufficient temporal context for DWG to learn meaningful lag structures, resulting in high RMSE values. Performance improved substantially at 168 h and further at 240 h. The 168-h window remains a reasonable choice that balances accuracy and computational cost (for the Mamba model as reported in “ Computational efficiency”), consistent with the baseline analysis. This confirms that the selection of 168 h as the working window length is appropriate for both the baseline and DWG settings.

For all four models, short window lengths (e.g., 8–40 h) consistently led to higher prediction errors and lower coefficients of determination (), reflecting insufficient temporal context to capture the delayed impact of environmental variables. However, when a window length of 168 h was tested, the RMSE dropped modestly (e.g., from 9.3615 to 8.5371 for CNN, from 10.0401 to 7.7199 for LSTM) or significantly (e.g., from 10.0447 to 1.6010 for Mamba). These results affirmed the importance of incorporating reasonably longer historical window lengths in landslide forecasting when the conventional stationary approach is adopted.

While extending the window length to 240 h generally led to further enhancements. However, these marginal accuracy improvements come at a substantial computational cost. In attention-based models like transformer and mamba, longer sequences increase the complexity of self-attention mechanisms quadratically, resulting in higher memory consumption and slower training convergence. Similarly, convolution-based models (e.g., CNN) require more layers and wider kernels to maintain effective receptive fields, leading to heavier parameter loads and extended inference time.

Considering the trade-off between accuracy improvements and computational cost, a 168-hour (7-day) input window was selected as the optimal configuration for the conventional stationary approach. This window length was also adopted for the DWG scenarios to ensure direct comparability between the baseline and nonstationary weighting strategies.

Performance under the conventional equal-weight approach

As summarized in Table 2, model performance under the conventional sliding-window configuration (168 h; equal weighting) varied significantly across architectures. Both the CNN and LSTM models, although widely used in sequence modeling tasks, exhibited relatively limited effectiveness in this context. Specifically, CNN yielded a of 8.5371 and a of 0.8411, while the LSTM performed only marginally better with a of 7.7199 and a of 0.8701. These results indicate that convolutional feature extraction alone, as well as recurrent memory mechanisms, were insufficient for capturing the complex dependencies present in geophysical time series when all timesteps are treated uniformly.

However, its effectiveness may still be constrained by the equal-weight assumption, which prevents the model from emphasizing salient time periods within the input window.

This suggests that Mamba’s selective state-space representation is particularly well suited to model long-range dependencies and intrinsic temporal dynamics in displacement data, outperforming both convolutional and recurrent networks.

Performance of models using the DWG strategy

Table 4 demonstrates that applying the DWG strategy, where a unique weight is assigned to each variable (RD, RWL, and GWL) at every timestep, markedly improved model performance across all prediction models. The enhancement was particularly substantial for models (i.e., CNN and LSTM) that performed poorly under the conventional stationary approach.

CNN, which exhibited the lowest predictive accuracy under the conventional stationary approach, benefited most from DWG. Its was substantially reduced from 8.5371 to 1.4527, accompanied by a sharp increase in from 0.8411 to 0.9954. Although CNN excels at learning local patterns, its fixed receptive fields and lack of intrinsic temporal memory limit its capacity to model irregular geophysical dynamics14,24. The DWG strategy was likely to mitigate this limitation by directing the network’s attention toward the most informative timestamps, effectively reducing noise from irrelevant intervals.

Similarly, LSTM demonstrated notable gains, with reduced from 7.7199 to 2.7472 and improved from 0.8701 to 0.9835. LSTM is prone to vanishing gradient issues over long sequences and often struggles with irrelevant long-range dependencies18. The DWG likely helped the LSTM network mitigate its sensitivity to long sequences with irrelevant temporal noise, improving predictive accuracy.

Although the Transformer already performed reasonably well in the conventional stationary approach setting, the introduction of DWG strategy further improved its from 0.9749 to 0.9911, along with a slight decrease in to 2.0258 compared to its baseline (i.e., 3.3942). Although its self-attention mechanisms inherently model temporal importance, externally supplied adaptive weights by DWG appeared to reinforce attention to critical timesteps, reducing dependence on positional encoding alone.

Mamba, despite its already exceptional baseline performance, achieved modest further performance gains. The was reduced from 1.601 to 1.1789, while from 0.9944 to 0.9970. These incremental improvements confirm that DWG remains beneficial even for state-space architectures highly optimized for temporal representation.

Overall, these results reinforce the hypothesis that equal-weight inputs limit model flexibility and may obscure meaningful signal variations. By enabling models to focus on temporally and feature-specific information dynamics, the DWG approach provides more accurate, context-adaptive landslide displacement predictions.

Discussion

Converged gating coefficient α for each DWG-equipped model

Table 5 reports the converged gating coefficient α (after sigmoid transformation) for each DWG-equipped model and each input channel in our experiments. These α values suggest that DWG provides differential benefits across architectures. The most substantial improvements were observed for CNN and LSTM, which lack internal mechanisms for adaptive temporal weighting. In contrast, the Transformer and Mamba models, which already possess internal temporal modelling capabilities through self-attention and selective state-space mechanisms respectively, showed more modest gains from DWG. This pattern suggests a ceiling effect: when the backbone architecture already incorporates mechanisms functionally similar to dynamic temporal weighting, the additional benefit of an external DWG module is reduced. The learned α values (Table 5) support this interpretation, as Transformer and Mamba exhibit lower α values (0.582 and 0.668) compared to CNN (up to 0.903), indicating comparatively less reliance on the external dynamic weighting. For instance, Mamba’s selective state-space mechanism incorporates input-dependent selection matrices that modulate the influence of different time steps during sequence processing, which is functionally analogous to a learned dynamic weighting scheme. Consequently, the external DWG module may provide partially redundant information for Mamba, limiting the scope for additional gains.

The substantial improvements observed for CNN and LSTM could arise from both noise suppression and genuine dynamic lag capture. CNN relies on fixed-size convolutional kernels that treat all positions within the receptive field equally, making it sensitive to irrelevant temporal noise. LSTM is prone to gradient attenuation over long sequences, which can cause it to weight recent inputs disproportionately regardless of their informational content. In both cases, DWG helps by selectively emphasizing informative timesteps and down-weighting uninformative ones. However, the weight analysis in Fig. 7 provides evidence favouring the latter interpretation. The learned weight maps show variable-specific temporal structures that differ across channels. These patterns are consistent with the distinct physical timescales of rainfall infiltration and reservoir water level response. If DWG were merely acting as a noise filter, we would expect more uniform attenuation rather than these structured, variable-dependent profiles.

Future work

Despite the promising outcomes, our study also highlights inherent limitations associated with purely data-driven learning strategies. Our study uses three directly monitored variables (RD, RWL, GWL) that are widely recognized as primary triggers of reservoir landslide displacement in the Three Gorges Reservoir area. Other potentially relevant factors, such as soil moisture, evapotranspiration, and pore water pressure distribution, were not included due to data availability constraints at the study site. The DWG weights therefore reflect the optimal allocation of importance among the available inputs, rather than a complete characterization of all physical drivers. Incorporating additional environmental variables may reveal richer weight structures and further improve prediction accuracy, which we consider a promising direction for future work.

A key observation is that different models, even when trained on the same dataset under identical learning conditions, often converged to markedly different sets of optimal lag weights (i.e., producing substantially large and inconsistent values at individual prediction steps). This lack of consistency indicates that the estimated weights may suffer from limited interpretability—a well-recognized issue in many deep learning applications. Such variability raises concerns regarding the physical plausibility and practical transferability of the learned temporal lags, particularly for landslide early warning systems where interpretability and reliability are as critical as predictive accuracy27.

To overcome this challenge, future research should explore the integration of physics-informed constraints or causal structure priors into the lag-weight generation process. Doing so may enhance both the stability and physical credibility of the learned temporal relationships. Recent studies28,29 demonstrate that embedding geophysical knowledge within neural networks can significantly improve model generalization and ensure consistency with known physical processes. Moreover, incorporating uncertainty quantification into both lag-weight estimation and model predictions will be essential, especially in noisy or data-sparse operational environments commonly encountered in landslide monitoring.

Finally, due to data availability constraints, the proposed DWG strategy has been validated using observations from a single monitored landslide site. Future work should evaluate its scalability and robustness across multiple geographic settings with varying triggering mechanisms. Integrating multi-source remote sensing products (e.g., InSAR displacement measurements, satellite-derived rainfall estimates) and longer historical records may further enhance the model’s capacity to capture complex temporal dependencies and improve displacement prediction accuracy.

Conclusion

This study introduced and evaluated a novel DWG strategy designed to enhance reservoir landslide displacement prediction. The DWG mechanism is integrated directly with prediction models and trained jointly in an end-to-end manner, enabling the automatic generation of optimal variable-specific weights at each timestep. We assessed the effectiveness of this framework using four representative deep learning architectures (i.e., CNN, LSTM, Transformer, and Mamba), and benchmarked their performance against the conventional stationary approach in which all environmental variables and timesteps within the input window are treated equally.

Our results show that the DWG strategy substantially improved prediction accuracy across all models and all performance metrics considered (RMSE, MAE, MAPE, and R²) relative to the stationary equal-weight baseline. The largest performance gains were observed for CNN and LSTM, which performed comparatively poorly under the conventional approach. Mamba remained the top-performing model under both weighting schemes, though its improvement with DWG was more modest, likely because it already achieved strong accuracy using the equal-weight configuration. Beyond numerical metrics, visual comparisons between predicted and measured displacements also confirmed that DWG consistently reduced deviation from ground-truth observations, further validating the advantages of the proposed approach.

The learned α values confirm that all models actively utilize the dynamic weighting mechanism, with CNN showing the strongest reliance on DWG for the reservoir water level channel (α = 0.90). The weight interpretability analysis reveals that different backbone architectures learn qualitatively distinct weighting strategies that align with known hydrological response timescales, supporting the physical plausibility of the learned representations.

Our work advances landslide displacement forecasting by establishing a more accurate and temporally adaptive prediction strategy. Nonetheless, the variability observed in learned weight distributions across models highlights an important limitation of purely data-driven weighting schemes. Future research should incorporate physics-informed constraints, causal priors, or uncertainty quantification to strengthen interpretability and reliability.
