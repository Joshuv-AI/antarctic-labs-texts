Random matrix theory of charge distribution in disordered quantum impurity models
Maxime Debertolis, Serge Florens

Abstract
We introduce a bare-bone random matrix quantum impurity model, by hybridizing a localized spinless electronic level with a bath of random fermions in the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble (GOE). While stripped out of correlations effects, this model reproduces some salient features of the impurity charge distribution obtained in previous works on interacting disordered impurity models. Computing by numerical sampling the impurity charge distribution in our model, we find a crossover from a Gaussian distribution (centered on half a charge unit) at large hybridization, to a bimodal distribution (centered both on zero and full occupations of the charge) at small hybridization. In the bimodal regime, a universal ( − 3 / 2 ) (-3/2) power-law is also observed. All these findings are very well accounted for by an analytic surmise computed with a single random electron level in the bath. We also derive an exact functional integral for the general probability distribution function of eigenvalues and eigenstates, that formally captures the statistical behavior of our model for any number N N of fermionic orbitals in the bath. In the Gaussian regime and in the limit N → ∞ N\to\infty , we are able to solve exactly the random matrix theory (RMT) for the charge distribution, obtaining perfect agreement with the numerics. Our results could be tested experimentally in mesoscopic devices, for instance by coupling a small quantum dot to a chaotic electronic reservoir, and using a quantum point contact as local charge sensor for the quantum dot occupation.



I
Introduction

Disorder plays a central role in condensed matter physics, due to the obvious
randomness induced by electronic doping in real materials. The conjunction of
disorder with interaction effects opens however many challenges, both conceptually
and for numerical modeling. For this reason, the study of disordered quantum
impurities offers a simpler vista into such questions. Quantum impurities are
typically realized by localized electronic levels, traditionally associated to Kondo
magnetic impurity
Kondo 1964
that are diluted in a host material
Hewson 1993
,
but they can be engineered using various nanoscale platforms, for instance
using semiconducting quantum dots
Goldhaber-Gordon et al. 1998
;
Cronenwett et al. 1998
.
These quantum impurities display an internal dynamics, complicated by the potentially strong internal
Coulomb interaction, and acquire at first glance a many-body character due to their hybridization
to the metallic states in the host material
Wilson 1975
;
Bulla et al. 2008
.
In fact, these minimalistic many-body problem turn out to hide a few-body character once expressed
in a dressed one-particle basis
He and Lu 2014
;
Debertolis et al. 2021
;
Debertolis et al. 2022
, which led recently
to the design of ultra-efficient and numerically exact quantum impurity solvers
Núnez-Fernández et al.
.
In the case of single quantum impurities in disordered hosts, these natural-orbital methods have opened
the way to simulate accurately large numbers of random samples
Debertolis et al. 2022
.
In previous studies
Dobrosavljević et al. 1992
;
Zaránd and Udvardi 1996
;
Cornaglia et al. 2006
;
Martin et al. 1997
;
Kaul et al. 2003
;
Lewenkopf and Weidenmüller 2005
;
Zhuravlev et al. 2007a
;
Kettemann and Mucciolo 2007
;
Zhuravlev et al. 2007b
;
Bedrich et al. 2010
;
Camjayi and Arrachea 2012
;
Liu et al. 2012a
;
Miranda et al. 2014
;
Bergmann and Thompson 2015
;
Slevin et al. 2019
,
calculations were mostly relying on mean-field approximations and scaling arguments, or
on more expensive numerical simulations like Quantum Monte Carlo, but in any case, very rich physics was uncovered,
especially in the
statistics
Liu et al. 2012b
. These random single-impurity models
can also be the backbone for disordered Kondo alloys
Kettemann and Raikh 2003
, vacancies
in Moiré materials
Chang et al. 2024
or quasicrystals
Andrade et al. 2015
;
Wu et al. 2022
,
leading potentially to non Fermi liquid physics
Miranda et al. 1996
;
Castro Neto and Jones 2000
;
Kettemann 2023
.
Typically, distribution functions of various observables show multi-modal character
due to the conflict between Kondo screening and Anderson localization.
Clearly, solving the underlying quantum impurity problem (by either controlled or
approximate methods), and then performing disorder averaging leads little room for
mathematical understanding of these problems.

In this work, we propose an analytical approach to random quantum impurity problems,
by attacking the simplest model of a quantum impurity in a disordered host. The first
simplification consists in considering a spinless localized fermionic impurity, which
can be experimentally realized under a strong in-plane magnetic field in quantum dot devices.
This single-level quantum dot is tunnel coupled by an hybridization
V
to a disordered
environment, see Refs.
Beenakker 1997
;
Ullmo 2008
for general reviews on the statistical
properties of chaotic electronic devices. Electron-electron interaction effects in the leads will
also be neglected altogether.
A second crucial simplification into the problem amounts to model the host as
a random matrix fermionic Hamiltonian under the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble (GOE)
with
N
electronic orbitals
Mehta 2004
, instead of taking the tight-binding form with
local Anderson disorder
Hetterich et al. 2017
.
Our random matrix quantum impurity model is easily simulated by solving the one-body impurity problem
for each disorder realization, and then averaging the statistics.

We will mostly consider here the probability distribution function
P(n_{d})
of the local charge

n_{d}\in[0,1]
on the impurity quantum dot, which is a very sensitive measure of its environment.
Our numerical computations show that
P(n_{d})
displays very rich behavior. For hybridizations
V

that are comparable or larger to the electronic half-bandwidth
2\sqrt{N}\sigma
, the distribution
takes an unspectacular Gaussian form:

P(n_{d})=A\exp\left[-\frac{N(n_{d}-\frac{1}{2})^{2}}{2(\sigma_{n_{d}})^{2}}\right],

(1)

with
A
such that the distribution is normalized, and the width
\sigma_{n_{d}}
is found
to decrease in a non-trivial manner with
V/(2\sqrt{N}\sigma
, and barely depends on
N
. However, for
hybridizations
V
that are much smaller than the half-bandwidth
2\sqrt{N}\sigma
,
the distribution
P(n_{d})
surprisingly turns out
to be bimodal, with tails diverging as
n_{d}^{-3/2}
for
n_{d}
close to 0, and as
(1-n_{d})^{-3/2}
for
n_{d}
close to 1.
This type of non-Gaussian distribution was reported previously by us, based on advanced numerical calculations
performed on a model with more realistic Anderson disorder, and including Coulomb
interaction effects as well. The main goal of the present paper is to provide a general framework to attack
random matrix quantum impurity models, allowing us to rationalize by analytical means all these interesting
numerical observations.

II
Summary of results

The paper is organized in several distincts sections where the main results are obtained,
and we summarize them here. After introducing the random quantum impurity model in Sec.
III
,
we perform its numerical solution in Sec.
IV
, and obtain the data for the impurity charge
distribution function
P(n_{d})
, which displays the Gaussian form (
1
) at strong hybridization.
At small hybridization, a bimodal form with universal scaling exponent
(-3/2)
is found numerically.
In Sec.
V
, we demonstrate that the whole crossover between both types of distributions can
be very well fitted by a simple “surmise” form:

P(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})=\frac{V}{\sqrt{8\pi}\sigma}\text{exp}\left(-\frac{2V^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}-\frac{1}{2})^{2}}{n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}(1-n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})}\right)\left[n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}(1-n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})\right]^{-\frac{3}{2}}\!\!\!,

(2)

that is obtained by replacing the whole electronic bath by a single random level with
mean energy
\sigma
.
At large
V/\sigma
, the Gaussian function peaked at
n_{d}=1/2
dominantes the distribution, but
at small
V/\sigma
, the
(-3/2)
power-law divergence becomes prominent near
n_{d}=0
and
1
.
This power-law is ultimately cut off by the diverging term in the exponential as

n_{d}\to 0
or
n_{d}\to 1
, so that the distribution function is normalizable.
Then, in order to obtain analytical results for a finite number of fermions
in the bath, we derive in Sec.
VI
an exact probability distribution function:

P\left[E_{\alpha},z_{\alpha}\right]=\delta\left(V^{2}-\sum_{\gamma=0}^{N}E_{\gamma}(E_{\gamma}-\epsilon_{d})z_{\gamma}\right)\delta\left(\epsilon_{d}-\sum_{\gamma=0}^{N}E_{\gamma}z_{\gamma}\right)\delta\left(1-\sum_{\gamma=0}^{N}z_{\gamma}\right)e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\sum_{\gamma=0}^{N}E^{2}_{\gamma}}\!\!\!\!\!\prod_{0\leq\gamma<\delta\leq N}{}\!\!\!\!\!|E_{\delta}-E_{\gamma}|\!\!\!\prod_{0\leq\alpha\leq N}\!\!\frac{1}{\sqrt{z_{\alpha}}},

(3)

where
E_{\alpha}
are the exact eigenenergies of the random matrix impurity model,

z_{\alpha}
are the squared wave function amplitudes of the impurity level in the eigenstates
of the problem, and
\epsilon_{d}
is the (non-random) energy of the impurity level.
While the right-most terms ressemble the usual random matrix theory distribution, we emphasize
that the energy levels
E_{\alpha}
combine both the impurity degree of freedom and
the set of
N
electronic orbitals, as also seen by the fact hat all Greek indices here
run from 0 to
N
.
In addition, a product of three delta functions impose exact constraints on the exact spectrum
and eigenstates, that are mediated via the impurity.
The probability distribution function of the impurity charge at zero temperature can then be computed
formally by counting only the occupied energy levels:

P(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})=\!\!\int\limits^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\!\!\mathcal{D}E_{\alpha}\!\!\!\int\limits^{+\infty}_{0}\!\!\mathcal{D}z_{\alpha}P[E_{\alpha},z_{\alpha}]\delta\left(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}-\sum\limits_{\gamma=0}^{N}\Theta(-E_{\alpha})z_{\alpha}\right)\!,

(4)

where
\mathcal{D}E_{\alpha}=\prod^{N}_{\alpha=0}\text{d}E_{\alpha}
is written for
readability, and the zero temperature Fermi function was replaced by a theta function.
In contrast to previous RMT studies, which focused mostly on spectral properties,
the computation of impurity distributions such as Eq. (
4
) requires to mix
spectral information with eigenstates distributions, a new step that we take in this
paper.
Finally, in Sec.
VII
, we solve the large
N
limit of Eq. (
4
)
in the Gaussian regime, and find an analytical expression for the width
\sigma_{n_{d}}

of
P(n_{d})
that agrees very well with the width extracted from the numerics.
The expression is not very compact, but can be well approximated by
\sigma_{n_{d}}\simeq\sqrt{2/\pi-1/2}\sqrt{N}\sigma/V
, and is valid for
V<\sqrt{2N}\sigma
. Indeed,
the large
N
solution breaks down for
V>\sqrt{2N}\sigma
, and we find that this critical
value of
V
corresponds to the point where the impurity is no more hybridized with
the band and exits above the band edge located at energy
E=2\sqrt{N}\sigma
.
An appendice describes some of the computations in more details.

III
Random matrix quantum impurity model

Interacting quantum impurity models can be tackled by a variety of analytical
and numerical techniques. They can also be approximately solved by a non-interacting theory
with renormalized parameters (slave bosons). For this reason, it is not surprising
that general qualitative features of disordered interacting quantum impurities can be
present in the case of non interacting fermions. In that case, the spin degree
of freedom can be discarded also. Considering a non-interacting impurity model of spinless fermions will
thus be our first simplification to the full problem. In addition, disorder in the bath surrounding
the impurity can be introduced by a large choice of random distribution functions, but the one that
allows easiest analytical progress is to take a purely random single-particle Hamiltonian, in the
Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble. We thus arrive at our random matrix quantum impurity model:

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{imp}}=\epsilon_{d}d^{\dagger}d+V\left(d^{\dagger}c_{1}+\mathrm{H.c.}\right)+\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{N}G^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{ij}c^{\dagger}_{i}c^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{j},

(5)

with
\epsilon_{d}
the chemical potential of the impurity level,
V
the tunneling rate
of the impurity to a single site of the bath,
N
the number of electronic orbitals in the bath,
and
G_{ij}
a
N\times N
hermitian matrix with entries taken as independent random variables
following a centered Gaussian distribution with variance
\sigma
:

P\left(G_{ij}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}G^{2}_{ij}\right).

(6)

Our model has hence two competing energy scales,
\sigma
that controls the strength
of the disorder, and
V
which is the tunneling rate between the impurity and the bath.
In the present context of quantum impurities, this model can be realized by designing a small
single-level quantum dot, associated to the
d^{\dagger}
fermions, inside a spin polarized two-dimensional
electron gas under a large in-plane magnetic field.
The dot is hybridized via a tunable tunnel barrier into a larger chaotic billiard, whose geometry
is controlled in-situ via electrostatic gates. The charge of the quantum dot can be monitored
via a nearby quantum point contact, acting as a sensitive charge sensor. To the best of our
knowledge, such a simple experiment has not been conducted, but it is clear that maintaining
the quantum dot half-filled may require fine-tuning from a local plunger gate, due to cross-talk
effects induced on the impurity when other electrostatic gates are activated.
In a different context, where quantum statistics is not relevant, a similar model was proposed
to describe Mie scattering of light from disordered granular
media
Elattari et al. 1996
.

In a mean-field treatment of an interacting quantum impurity model, the hybridization
V
would be
a renormalized parameter, and would take sample dependent values, due to self-consistency.
Such effects have been previously considered in Ref.
Liu et al. 2012b
,
but we will keep here
V
as a fixed number, in order to ease the analytical computations.
Note also that the impurity level is totally deterministic, namely
\epsilon_{d}
is not a random variable.
This is important, otherwise the limit
V/\sigma\ll 1
would be trivially dominated by
local disorder broadening, and would lead to a Gaussian distribution of the impurity charge.

In absence of the impurity (corresponding to the strict
V=0
limit), it is known that the
eigenvalues
E
of the matrix
G_{ij}
are distributed for
N
large enough
according to Wigner’s semi-circle law
Mehta 2004
:

W(E)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{4N\sigma^{2}-E^{2}}\;\Theta(2\sqrt{N}\sigma-|E|),

(7)

which is normalized to the number of states
N
.
Hamiltonian (
5
) was previously studied
Elattari et al. 1996

using standard RMT techniques
Efetov 1983
;
Verbaarschot et al. 1985
;
Verbaarschot and Zirnbauer 1984
,
and it was found that the energy level distribution of the coupled system was only affected at order

1/N
by the presence of the impurity.
However, the charge distribution
P(n_{d})
is expected to remain finite for all
N
values, and it
will be now investigated from the full numerical solution of the random Hamiltonian (
5
).

IV
Charge distribution from numerical sampling

The charge distribution
P(n_{d})
computed by solving model (
5
) and averaging over
many disorder realizations is shown in the lower panels of Fig.
1
, together with
the corresponding eigenenergy distritributions
P(E)
(upper panels).
These calculations are performed by aligning the impurity level to the Fermi level, namely
\epsilon_{d}=0
,
and at zero temperature. Hence, for a given realization, the impurity charge is computed from:

n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}=\langle d^{\dagger}d\rangle=\sum\limits_{E_{\alpha}<0}z_{\alpha}=\sum\limits_{E_{\alpha}<0}|\psi_{d}(\alpha)|^{2},

(8)

where
z_{\alpha}
is the squared amplitude of the exact eigenstate
\psi_{d}(\alpha)
on the impurity level
associated to the occupied energy level
E_{\alpha}
.
In contrast to previous RMT studies of impurity models,
P(n_{d})
requires knowledge of both energies
and wave functions.
In the numerical simulations, we used
N=300
electronic states in the bath, and the electronic
half-bandwidth is
2\sqrt{N}\sigma
.
Three different regimes are identified as a function of hybridization
V
from our data.

For

the band. For

to the formation of a local bound state between the impurity and the
c^{\dagger}_{1}

fermion on site
j=1
.

(i) Weak coupling regime:

for
V\propto\sigma/\sqrt{N}=\delta E
, with
\delta E
the typical energy level spacing,
the impurity is very slightly coupled to its environment, and the square amplitude of the wave function
on the impurity is close to one, namely
z_{\alpha}\simeq\delta_{\alpha,0}
. As a result, an extra
impurity state near zero energy clearly emerges in the energy distribution on top of the semi-circle,
see the upper left panel in Fig.
1
.
In this extreme regime, the impurity is easily polarized by its environment, so that
n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}
is most
likely found close to either
0
or
1
, depending on the sign of the energy level
E_{0}
that is the closest
to the Fermi level, see the lower left panel in Fig.
1
.

(ii) Diluted regime:

for
V<\sqrt{2N}\sigma
, the impurity remains in the band and is well diluted in
the bath. The impurity is coupled to a macroscopic fraction of bath states, and the square amplitudes
z_{\alpha}

become of order
1/N
.
For this reason, the impurity level is diluted over
N
states and becomes invisible in the
energy distribution at large
N
, which remains very close to the semi-circle law (see the upper middle panel
in Fig.
1
).
The distribution
P(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})
is now simply centered around
n_{d}=1/2
(see the lower middle panel), since one has
now reached the strong coupling
regime, and the electrons can efficiently tunnel back and forth from the impurity to the bath.

(iii) Bound state regime:

for
V>\sqrt{2N}\sigma
, an impurity bound state emerges out of the electronic band, as seen from the two outliers
outside the semi-circle distribution, see the upper right panel in Fig.
1
.
The escape outside the band is better seen by plotting the distribution of the maximum eigenvalue
Max
(E_{\alpha})
, see Fig.
2

For large
V\gg\sqrt{2N}\sigma
,
these bound states at energy
E_{\pm}\simeq\pm V
have nearly equal square amplitudes

z_{\pm}\simeq 1/2
, and for this trivial reason,
n_{d}
becomes closer and closer to
1/2
.
Due to the larger value of
V/(2\sqrt{N}\sigma)
, fluctuations into the rest of the bath become rare,
so that the charge distribution
P(n_{d})
has drastically narrowed down compared to the diluted regime.
In the limit
V\to\infty
, one should obtain
P(n_{d})=\delta(n_{d}-1/2)
, as the wavefunction becomes
equal to the quantum superposition
(d^{\dagger}|0\rangle-c^{\dagger}_{1}|0\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
,
independently of the disorder values in the bath.

The three regimes presented above can be characterized quantitatively by the
participation ratio (PR) of the impurity wave function, defined as:

\text{PR}=\overline{\left(\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{N}z^{2}_{\alpha}\right)^{-1}}=\overline{\left(\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{N}|\psi_{d}(\alpha)|^{4}\right)^{-1}},

(9)

which counts the number of eigenstates that effectively hybridize to the impurity,
averaged over all realizations disorder.
This quantity is reported with respect to
V
in Fig.
3
, each
point being averaged here over
200
realizations of the random matrix.
When
V\simeq\sigma/\sqrt{N}=\delta E
, the impurity is almost isolated from
the bath, and the PR equals
1
since
z_{0}\simeq 1
, as indeed argued above.
When
V
increases, more and more states become hybridized, and the PR keeps steadily
increasing to large values of order
N
, confirming that the impurity is diluted,
so that all
z_{\alpha}\propto 1/N
.
After
V
has passed the band edge
2\sqrt{N}\sigma
, the PR drastically falls
down to the value
2
, indicating the presence of the two outliers, whose weight is about

1/2
each. This confirm the interpretation made above of our numerical data.
We now investigate a simple analytical approach of the random matrix quantum impurity
model.

V
Surmise for the bimodal charge distribution

Although the mechanisms behind each of the three regimes found in Fig.
1
are
driven by different behavior of the hybridization process between the impurity and the bath,
it turns out that the distribution of the occupation
P(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})
can be obtained by a simple approach.
Our the numerical results were obtained for fixed and large value
N=300
, in order to reach
the self-averaging state. Remarkably, we have observed in our simulations that
the shape of
P(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})
is very little dependent on
N
, even down to
N=1
(the width of
the charge distribution may however depend on
N
).
We now derive an analytical form of this surmise, by solving the
N=1
random matrix
impurity model (
5
):

\hat{H}_{N=1}=V\,(d^{\dagger}c^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{1}+\mathrm{H.c.})+\epsilon_{1}c^{\dagger}_{1}c^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&V\\
V&\epsilon_{1}\end{pmatrix},

(10)

with
\epsilon_{1}=G_{11}
a random potential following the normal distribution

P(\epsilon_{1})=(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{-1}e^{-\epsilon_{1}^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}
.
We have again fixed
\epsilon_{d}=0
for the impurity level.
The matrix on the right hand side of Eq. (
10
) is readily found
in the Fock basis with one electron in total in the system.

The eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}
and eigenvectors
\psi_{\pm}
of
\hat{H}_{N=1}
are readily obtained as:

\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}^{2}+4V^{2}},\quad\psi_{\pm}=\frac{1}{(V^{2}+\lambda^{2}_{\pm})^{1/2}}\begin{pmatrix}V\\
\lambda_{\pm}\end{pmatrix},

(11)

Using Eq. (
8
) for the impurity charge
n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}
, its distribution reads:

P(n_{d})=\int\limits^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\text{d}\epsilon_{1}P(\epsilon_{1})\,\delta\left(n_{d}-\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\Theta\big(-\lambda_{\alpha}\big)|\psi_{\alpha}|^{2}\right).

(12)

Inserting expressions (
11
) and computing the integral in Eq. (
12
) leads
to the desired distribution of
n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}
:

P(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})=\frac{V}{\sqrt{8\pi}\sigma}\text{exp}\left(-\frac{2V^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}-\frac{1}{2})^{2}}{n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}(1-n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})}\right)\left[n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}(1-n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})\right]^{-\frac{3}{2}}\!\!\!.

(13)

This expression shows the competition of two terms.
At strong coupling, namely
V/\sigma\gg 1
, the exponential term dominates
and
P(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})\sim\text{exp}[-\frac{2V^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}-\frac{1}{2})^{2}]
which is a
Gaussian of mean
1/2
and of deviation
\sigma/2V
. Clearly, it will fit the bell-shaped
charge distributions obtained in the diluted and bound state regimes (bottom center and bottom
right panels of Fig.
1
), upon ajusting the width
\sigma
of the two site
model, since the actual width does depend on
N
.
At weak coupling, namely
V/\sigma\ll 1
, we find
P(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})\sim(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}(1-n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}))^{-\frac{3}{2}}
,
leading to an universal (-3/2) power laws for
n_{d}
close to
0
or
1
.
The
1/{n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}(1-n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})}
factor in
the exponential term prevents the distribution to ultimately diverge for
n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}\to 0
or
n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}\to 1
.
In Fig.
4
, we compare the analytical surmise (
13
) to the charge
distribution obtained for
N=300
orbitals in the
V/\sigma\ll 1
regime (left panel) and

V/\sigma\lesssim 1
regime (right panel). The agreement is remarkably good, despite the fact
that the analytical calculation corresponds to the strict
N=1
limit. In particular,
for
V/\sigma\ll 1
(left panel), the power law
(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}(1-n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}))^{-3/2}
is well observed
in the numerics, making it a robust universal feature of the full model. Slight quantitative
deviations near the edges are however seen, unsurprisingly. In addition, for
V/\sigma\lesssim 1
,
we recover the long tails yielding the previously observed plateau of
P(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})
around
n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}=1/2
.

VI
Exact formula for the probability distribution functions

This section aims to find the exact joint probability distribution of the set of eigenvalues

E_{\alpha}
and eigenvectors
\psi_{\alpha}
of the random matrix impurity model.
The starting point is the distribution of the unperturbed eigenvalues
e_{\alpha}
and
eigenvectors
\phi_{\alpha}
of the random hermitian matrix
G_{ij}
of the bath,
that is known to obey the random matrix Gaussian orthogonal ensemble (GOE)
Mehta 2004
:

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{RMT}}[e_{\alpha},r_{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\prod\limits_{1\leq\alpha<\beta\leq N}\!\!\!\!\!|e_{\alpha}-e_{\beta}|\;\;\!e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}e_{\beta}^{2}}\prod\limits_{1\leq\alpha\leq N}\frac{1}{\sqrt{r_{\alpha}}}

(14)

\displaystyle\times\delta\left(\sum\limits_{\beta=1}^{N}r_{\beta}-1\right),

where
r_{\alpha}=|\phi(\alpha)|^{2}
is the square amplitude, and the delta distribution
imposes the normalizationn of the wave function.
A similar study to ours was performed for a random matrix model with a local
defect
Aleiner and Matveev 1998
;
Bogomolny 2017
.
The perturbation corresponded there to a deterministic potential acting on a given entry of
the random matrix, leading to a rank-one perturbation of the random matrix of rank
N
.
In our random matrix quantum impurity system, the perturbation is of rank two, as it adds
an extra physical orbital into the problem.
The matrix model (
5
) can be reformulated in a more convenient way using a matrix form

H_{ij}
, with indices
i,j=0,\ldots,N
, such that:

\begin{cases}H_{00}=\epsilon_{d},\\
H_{0j}=H_{j0}=V\quad&\forall j\neq 0,\\
H_{ij}=G_{ij}\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(15)

The entries
G_{ij}
for
i,j>0
are distributed following a Gaussian distribution of zero mean
and of deviation
\sigma
, and are real and symmetric, in order to work in the GOE.
If we add the impurity site
i=0
with energy
e_{0}
and square amplitude
r_{0}
, we thus obtain
in the decoupled limit
V=0
a new distribution:

P[e_{\alpha},r_{\alpha}]=P_{\mathrm{RMT}}[e_{\alpha},r_{\alpha}]\delta\left(e_{0}-\epsilon_{d}\right)\delta\left(r_{0}\right),

(16)

due to the constraints required in Eq. (
15
).

We want to compute the charge distribution of the impurity
P(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})
, expressed
as
n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}=\sum_{\alpha}|\psi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)|^{2}\Theta(-E_{\alpha})
, where
\psi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)
is the site
0

component of the eigenvector
\alpha
of the full matrix
H_{ij}
, and
E_{\alpha}
is its
associated eigenvalue. We will denote
z_{\alpha}=|\psi_{0}(\alpha)|^{2}
the square amplitude
of this eigenvector. In order to obtain the joint probability distribution function of the
exact energies and amplitudes for the coupled system, denoted

P[E_{\alpha},z_{\alpha}]
, we need to perform a change of variables between the
N^{2}

variables (
e_{\alpha},r_{\alpha}
) of the decoupled problem to the
N)^{2}
variables
(
E_{\alpha},z_{\alpha}
) of the coupled problem. There are however
(N+1)^{2}
entries in
H_{ij}

according to Eq. (
15
), but since the first line and the first column are known,
they are not associated to random variables, and are fixed by the two constraints imposed on
the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
H
. The details of this calculation are presented
in Appendix
A
, and the final expression for the eigenenergy and eigenstate distribution
function reads:

P\left[E_{\alpha},z_{\alpha}\right]=\delta\left(V^{2}-\sum_{\gamma=0}^{N}E_{\gamma}(E_{\gamma}-\epsilon_{d})z_{\gamma}\right)\delta\left(\epsilon_{d}-\sum_{\gamma=0}^{N}E_{\gamma}z_{\gamma}\right)\delta\left(1-\sum_{\gamma=0}^{N}z_{\gamma}\right)e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\sum_{\gamma=0}^{N}E^{2}_{\gamma}}\!\!\!\!\!\prod_{0\leq\gamma<\delta\leq N}{}\!\!\!\!\!|E_{\delta}-E_{\gamma}|\!\!\!\prod_{0\leq\alpha\leq N}\!\!\frac{1}{\sqrt{z_{\alpha}}},

(17)

This equation is the main result of this section, and is exact (up to a norm) for any value
of the hybridizationparameter
V
, and for all system sizes
N
.
We have also checked from the numerics performed in Sec.
IV

that, for each disorder realization, the three delta functions
in Eq. (
17
) above are exactly obeyed, confirming the
correctness of our analytical result.
The first constraint,
V^{2}=\sum_{\gamma=0}^{N}E_{\gamma}(E_{\gamma}-\epsilon_{d})z_{\gamma}
, corresponds actually to the constraint seen in Eq. (
14
), re-expressed
with the new variables. The two other constraints are imposed to reduce the number of
(N+1)^{2}
variables
in the new basis, to the
N^{2}
random variables of the original problem.

The impurity charge distribution can be computed formally from:

P(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})=\!\!\int\limits^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\!\!\mathcal{D}E_{\alpha}\!\!\!\int\limits^{+\infty}_{0}\!\!\mathcal{D}z_{\alpha}P[E_{\alpha},z_{\alpha}]\delta\left(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}-\sum\limits_{\gamma=0}^{N}\Theta(-E_{\alpha})z_{\alpha}\right)\!,

(18)

where
\mathcal{D}E_{\alpha}=\prod^{N}_{\alpha=0}\text{d}E_{\alpha}
is written for
compactness.
The main difficulty at this point is how to deal correctly with the different non-linear
constraints in Eq. (
17
).
In the upcoming and final section, exact analytical calculations of the charge distribution function
are presented, using the large
N
limit.

VII
Large N solution

We finally compute analytically the charge distribution (
18
) in the large
N
limit,
assuming the potential acting on the impurity to be unbiased, namely
\epsilon_{d}=0
.
The first step is to Fourier transform the three delta distributions in Eq. (
17
)
and the fourth one in Eq. (
18
), using

\delta\left(x\right)=\int^{+\infty i}_{-\infty i}\frac{\text{d}\lambda}{2\pi}\;\text{exp}\left[\lambda x\right]

where
\lambda
was written as an purely imaginary variable.
Integrating over the
z_{\alpha}
variables in Eq. (
17
) can then be done
exactly, and we obtain a formally exact expression:

P(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})=\int\limits^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\mathcal{D}E_{\alpha}\int\limits_{-\infty i}\hskip-5.69046pt\int\hskip-2.84544pt\int\hskip-5.69046pt\int\limits^{+\infty i}\frac{\text{d}\lambda\,\text{d}\nu\,\text{d}\rho\,\text{d}\mu}{(2\pi)^{7/2}}e^{-F[E_{\gamma},\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho]},

(19)

with the “free energy” functional:

\displaystyle F[E_{\gamma},\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\gamma=0}^{N}\frac{E^{2}_{\gamma}}{4\sigma^{2}}\;\;-\!\!\!\sum\limits_{0\leq\delta<\gamma\leq N}\text{ln}\left|E_{\delta}-E_{\gamma}\right|

(20)

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt+\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{\gamma=0}^{N}\,\text{ln}\left|\lambda E^{2}_{\gamma}+\nu E_{\gamma}+\rho+\mu\Theta(-E_{\gamma})\right|

\displaystyle-\lambda V^{2}-\rho-\mu\,n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}.

In the limit of large
N
, the Laplace method is used to compute the integrals
over the energies
E_{\alpha}
. We follow here Ref.
Brézin et al. 1978
,
in which a random matrix model perturbed by a
\phi^{4}
bulk interaction was solved.
In our impurity model, the scaling of each terms has to be carefuly accounted for. We know that
energies of the random matrix scale as
E_{\alpha}\propto\sqrt{N}
, and we want to
consider the diluted regime with
V\propto\sqrt{N}
, see middle panels of Fig.
1
.
In order for all the terms inside the logarithm in Eq. (
20
) to be of comparable
size, we thus need to scale the Lagrange multipliers as

\lambda\propto 1
,
\nu\propto\sqrt{N}
,
\rho\propto N
, and
\mu\propto N
.
With these scalings, the saddle-point equations
\partial_{E_{\alpha}}P(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})=0
read for

E_{\alpha}>0
:

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq\beta\leq N,\\
\beta\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}\frac{2}{E_{\alpha}-E_{\beta}}=\frac{E_{\alpha}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{2\lambda E_{\alpha}+\nu}{\lambda E^{2}_{\alpha}+\nu E_{\alpha}+\rho},

(21)

and it is easy to check that the second term in the right hand side, induced by the impurity,
is
1/N
smaller than the other two terms, and can be neglected when
N
is large (the same
argument applies for
E_{\alpha}<0
). Unless the density of state is strongly peaked
near
E_{\alpha}=0
(weak coupling regime), we deduce that the density of states is
barely affected by the impurity at large
N
, and this corresponds to the diluted regime
shown in the top middle panel of Fig.
1
.

Going to the continuous limit
E_{\alpha}\rightarrow E(\alpha/N)
,
and
1/N\sum^{N}_{\alpha}\rightarrow\int^{1}_{0}\text{d}x
, we obtain the
expression
Brézin et al. 1978
:

\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{2.91664pt}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hskip 4.26773pt\rule{4.16669pt}{0.7pt}}}\hss}\int\limits^{1}_{0}\frac{\text{d}y}{E(x)-E(y)}=\frac{1}{4N\sigma^{2}}E(x),

(22)

where
\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{2.91664pt}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hskip 2.56064pt\rule{4.16669pt}{0.7pt}}}\hss}\int
represents the principal part of the integral. At this point,
the density of states
u(E)=\text{d}y/\text{d}E(y)
is introduced, normalized on an interval

[-2a,2a]
which gives:

\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{2.91664pt}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hskip 6.82881pt\rule{4.16669pt}{0.7pt}}}\hss}\int\limits^{2a}_{-2a}\text{d}F\,\frac{u(F)}{E-F}=\frac{1}{4N\sigma^{2}}E,\quad\forall|E|\leq 2a.

(23)

It is direct to check that the density of states solving this equation
Mehta 2004
is given by a semi-circle
law in the support
[-2\sqrt{N}\sigma,2\sqrt{N}\sigma]
, namely:

u(E)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{4N\sigma^{2}-E^{2}}\;\Theta(2\sqrt{N}\sigma-|E|).

(24)

At saddle-point level, we can remove all the integrals over
E_{\alpha}
in Eq. (
19
):

P(n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d})=\int\limits_{-\infty i}\hskip-5.69046pt\int\hskip-2.84544pt\int\hskip-5.69046pt\int\limits^{+\infty i}\frac{\text{d}\lambda\,\text{d}\nu\,\text{d}\rho\,\text{d}\mu}{(2\pi)^{7/2}}\;e^{-F[\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho]},

(25)

and express the energy dependence as a single integral over the density of states:

\displaystyle F[\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-2\sqrt{N}\sigma}^{0}\hskip-8.5359pt\text{d}E\,u(E)\frac{1}{2}\,\text{ln}\left(\lambda E^{2}+\nu E+\rho+\mu\right)

(26)

\displaystyle+\!\!\!\int\limits^{2\sqrt{N}\sigma}_{0}\hskip-8.5359pt\text{d}E\,u(E)\frac{1}{2}\,\text{ln}\left(\lambda E^{2}+\nu E+\rho\right)

\displaystyle-\lambda V^{2}-\rho-\mu\,n^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}_{d}.

From the scaling of the parameters
V,\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho
, we see that
this free energy is proportional to
N
, so that at large
N
the integration
over the four Lagrange multipliers can be treated
by steepest descent,

\partial F[\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho]/\partial x=0
, with
x=\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho
,
which gives:

\displaystyle V^{2}\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\int\limits_{-2\sqrt{N}\sigma}^{0}\hskip-11.38092pt\text{d}E\,\frac{u(E)E^{2}/2}{\lambda E^{2}+\nu E+\rho+\mu}\,+\hskip-5.69046pt\int\limits^{2\sqrt{N}\sigma}_{0}\hskip-8.5359pt\text{d}E\,\frac{u(E)E^{2}/2}{\lambda E^{2}+\nu E+\rho},

\displaystyle n_{d}\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\int\limits_{-2\sqrt{N}\sigma}^{0}\hskip-11.38092pt\text{d}E\,\frac{u(E)/2}{\lambda E^{2}+\nu E+\rho+\mu},

(27)

\displaystyle 0\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\int\limits_{-2\sqrt{N}\sigma}^{0}\hskip-11.38092pt\text{d}E\,\frac{u(E)E/2}{\lambda E^{2}+\nu E+\rho+\mu}+\hskip-5.69046pt\int\limits^{2\sqrt{N}\sigma}_{0}\hskip-8.5359pt\text{d}E\,\frac{u(E)E/2}{\lambda E^{2}+\nu E+\rho},

\displaystyle 1\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\int\limits_{-2\sqrt{N}\sigma}^{0}\hskip-11.38092pt\text{d}E\,\frac{u(E)/2}{\lambda E^{2}+\nu E+\rho+\mu}+\hskip-5.69046pt\int\limits^{2\sqrt{N}\sigma}_{0}\hskip-8.5359pt\text{d}E\,\frac{u(E)/2}{\lambda E^{2}+\nu E+\rho}.

After some manipulations, and using the fact that the density of states
u(E)
is normalized to
N
,
we find the symmetrized system of equations:

\displaystyle\lambda V^{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\rho+\mu n_{d}=N/2,

\displaystyle n_{d}\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\int\limits_{0}^{2\sqrt{N}\sigma}\hskip-8.5359pt\text{d}E\,\frac{u(E)/2}{\lambda E^{2}-\nu E+\rho+\mu},

(28)

\displaystyle 0\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\int\limits^{2\sqrt{N}\sigma}_{0}\hskip-8.5359pt\text{d}E\,\frac{(\mu-2\nu E)u(E)E/2}{(\lambda E^{2}+\nu E+\rho)(\lambda E^{2}-\nu E+\rho+\mu)},

\displaystyle 2n_{d}-1\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\int\limits^{2\sqrt{N}\sigma}_{0}\hskip-8.5359pt\text{d}E\,\frac{(\mu-2\nu E)u(E)/2}{(\lambda E^{2}+\nu E+\rho)(\lambda E^{2}-\nu E+\rho+\mu)}.

These non-linear equations do not have explicit analytical solution in general.
However, we can make further progress by analyzing them in the vicinity of
n_{d}=1/2
,
where a Gaussian peak was found to describe
P(n_{d})
in the diluted regime of the impurity,
see top middle panel in Fig.
1
.
Indeed, due to particle-hole symmetry (in average) for
n_{d}=1/2
, we see that
\mu=\nu=0
satisfies
automatically the last two of the saddle point equations in Eqs. (
28
), and therefore
we have simply

\rho=N/2-\lambda V^{2}
, with

\int^{2\sqrt{N}\sigma}_{0}\text{d}E\,\frac{u(E)/2}{\lambda E^{2}+\rho}=1/2
to be solved for
\lambda
.
Performing the integration over
E
exactly, we obtain the equation:

\sqrt{1+4\lambda\sigma^{2}/(1/2-\lambda V^{2}/N)}=1+4\lambda\sigma^{2},

(29)

recalling that
V\propto N
and
\lambda\propto 1
, for the saddle point to be valid
at large
N
. Squaring Eq. (
29
), we readily find the simple solution

\lambda=N/(2V^{2})-1/(2\sigma^{2})
, that applies for
n_{d}=1/2
.
Noting that the left hand side of Eq. (
29
)
must be positive, we obtain the condition that
1+4\lambda\sigma^{2}>0
, which implies

|V|<\sqrt{2N}\sigma
. This is precisely the condition that the impurity level
remains inside the electronic band, as seen in Fig.
2
, and in agreement
with our initial hypothesis that the impurity is diluted.

The final trick to find
P(n_{d})
is to express it in the close vicinity of
n_{d}=1/2
. At
the saddle point level, we have
P(n_{d})=Ae^{-F[\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho]}
, where
the saddle point values satisfying Eq. (
28
) must be inserted, and
A
is
a normalization factor. Due to the fact that

\partial F[\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho]/\partial x=0
, with
x=\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho
,
and using
\mu=0
for
n_{d}=1/2
, a second order Taylor expansion gives a Gaussian
charge distribution:

P(n_{d})=P(n_{d}=1/2)\exp\left[-\frac{N(n_{d}-1/2)^{2}}{2(\sigma_{n_{d}})^{2}}\right],

(30)

with the width expressed as

\sigma_{n_{d}}=\left[-\frac{\partial\mu(n_{d}=1/2)}{\partial n_{d}}\right]^{-1/2}.

(31)

It remains to compute
\partial\mu/\partial n_{d}
for
n_{d}=1/2
. Taking the derivative
of the saddle point equations (
28
), we can simplify them by inserting

\mu=\nu=0
at
n_{d}=1/2
, and we find:

\displaystyle-I_{2}\frac{\partial\nu}{\partial n_{d}}+\frac{1}{2}I_{1}\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial n_{d}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(32)

\displaystyle-I_{1}\frac{\partial\nu}{\partial n_{d}}+\frac{1}{2}I_{0}\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial n_{d}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2,

(33)

with
I_{n}=\int^{2\sqrt{N}\sigma}_{0}\text{d}E\,\frac{u(E)E^{n}}{(\lambda E^{2}+\rho)^{2}}
.
Clearly we have
the equality
\lambda I_{2}+\rho I_{0}=N
using the saddle point
equation (
28
) for
n_{d}=1/2
.
We obtain finally:

\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial n_{d}}=\frac{4I_{2}}{(I_{1})^{2}-I_{0}I_{2}},

(34)

where the remaining integral are exactly computed:

\displaystyle I_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{32\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}\epsilon^{3/2}\sqrt{1+\epsilon}},

(35)

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{N}}{8\pi\lambda^{2}\sigma^{3}}\left[\frac{1}{\epsilon}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon}}\mathrm{arctanh}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon}}\right)\right],

(36)

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[N-(N/2-\lambda V^{2})I_{0}]/\lambda.

(37)

The saddle point solution
\lambda=N/(2V^{2})-1/(2\sigma^{2})
and

\epsilon=(1/2-\lambda V^{2}/N)/(4\lambda\sigma^{4})
are both finite when
N\to\infty
,
since
V\propto\sqrt{N}
.
Inserting Eqs. (
35
)-(
37
) into Eq. (
34
) provides an analytical
expression for the width
\sigma_{n_{d}}
of the charge distribution
P(n_{d})
, given
by Eq. (
31
). We find that this lengthy expression is very well approximated
in most of the range
V<\sqrt{2N}\sigma
,
by the simple formula
\sigma_{n_{d}}=\sqrt{2/\pi-1/2}\sqrt{N}\sigma/V
, that
is asymptotically exact for
V\to\sqrt{2N}\sigma
.
The analytical form for the width
\sigma_{n_{d}}
is compared in Fig.
5

to the width extracted from the sampling method of Sec.
IV
, for several
data as the one shown in the lower middle panel of Fig.
1
, and
excellent agreement is found. In the weak coupling regime where
V\simeq\sigma/\sqrt{N}
,
deviations from the numerical data start to be seen, as expected since the large
N
limit is
only valid provided
V\propto\sqrt{N}
.

N
analytical expression (

) for the width

VIII
Conclusion

Recently, Bogomolny has considered a random matrix model modified by a local static potential
Bogomolny 2017
,
corresponding to a rank-1 perturbation of the RMT. In this work, we have solved the problem of a random matrix
coupled to a dynamical quantum impurity (resonant level), which corresponds to a rank 2 perturbation
of the RMT.
In contrast with previous literature, we have considered typical observables of a fermionic system,
by investigating the electron charge distribution of the quantum impurity. We have first attacked
the problem using numerics (exact diagonalization of the impurity model and disorder averaging),
and we found rich physical regimes.
When the impurity is weakly coupled to the band, a bimodal charge distribution with universal
(-3/2) power law was found. Although disorder is symmetrically centered around energy zero,
the impurity has a tendency to polarize near zero or full occupation, due to its high sensitivity
to charge noise in the weak coupling regime.
At intermediate coupling, the impurity becomes diluted in the bath due to efficient hybridization,
and a broad Gaussian charge distribution emerges. At strong coupling, the impurity forms a bound states
with a single fermionic orbital of the bath, and the Gaussian distribution rapidly narrows down.
All these observations are fully accounted for by analytical insights, obtained from the limit of either
small or large number of sites in the bath. A general probability distribution functional was also exactly
derived for the random matrix quantum impurity model.

Perspectives of this work are manifold. First, there are still some open questions on the
simple spinless random quantum impurity model investigated in our manuscript, for instance regarding
the statistics of the highest energy level in the band. Indeed, at small hybridization
V
, the
last level is unperturbed and follows the Tracy Widom distribution. However, at large hybridization

V
, the level exits the band and displays Gaussian statistics. Describing the transition between
the two regimes would be interesting. Also at a theoretical level, it would
be more realistic to extend the calculations to the case of spinful fermions, and investigate
spin and charge distribution functions of the impurity level, depending also on the type of disorder
in the bath (random charge potentials, or random local magnetic fields). Since interactions are crucial
to induce spin/charge separation (such as the phenomena of Kondo effect and Coulomb blockade), this study
could be performed by a combination of analytical methods, as proposed here, and many-body
numerics, such as based on natural orbitals
Debertolis et al. 2022
.
Although our model based on Random Matrix Theory is over-simplified, we found by numerical analysis of a more
realistic disordered tight-binding Hamiltonian that all our results were robust (not shown).
Thus, on a more experimental side, our study could be designed in artificial nanostructures, using a
small spin-polarized quantum dot coupled to a large chaotic billiard. Biasing electrostatic gates to modify
the billiard shape, and a quantum point contact to measure the charge on the quantum dot impurity would allow
to build the statistics necessary to confirm the predictions made in our paper. Careful calibration
of the potential acting on the quantum dot would however be required to avoid trivial polarization effects.

Appendix A
Derivation of the probability distribution functions

In this section, we derive the main result Eq. (
17
) of Sec.
VI
, namely
the full probability distribution function of the eigenenergies and eigenstates of the
random matrix quantum impurity model.

A.1
Definition of the eigenvalue problem

We recall that our quantum impurity model is given by the
(N+1)\times(N+1)
hermitian random matrix

H_{ij}
in Eq. (
15
), which is a rank 2 perturbation to a standard
N\times N
GOE
random matrix
G_{ij}
. The latter is
extended here to size
(N+1)\times(N+1)
in order to accomodate the decoupled impurity.
We introduce
\phi_{i}\left(\alpha\right)
(resp.
\psi_{i}\left(\alpha\right)
) as the
i\text{th}
component
of the
\alpha\mathrm{th}
eigenvector of
G_{ij}
(resp.
H_{ij}
) with energy
e_{\alpha}

(resp.
E_{\alpha}
), which obey:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=0}^{N}H_{ij}\psi_{j}\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;E_{\alpha}\psi_{i}\left(\alpha\right),

(38)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=0}^{N}G_{ij}\phi_{j}\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;e_{\alpha}\phi_{i}\left(\alpha\right),\,\text{with}\,e_{0}=\epsilon_{d},

(39)

where the decoupled impurity wave function

\phi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)=\delta_{0\alpha}
with energy
e_{0}=\epsilon_{d}
was added
on the component
i=0
of the
G_{ij}
matrix.
The orthonormality of the eigenvectors
\phi
reads:

\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N}\phi_{i}\left(\alpha\right)\phi_{i}\left(\beta\right)=\delta_{\alpha\beta},\\
\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{N}\phi_{i}\left(\alpha\right)\phi_{j}\left(\alpha\right)=\delta_{ij},\end{cases}

(40)

and similarly for
\psi
.
An orthogonal transformation matrix
C_{\alpha\beta}=C^{-1}_{\beta\alpha}
changes
between the
new
basis
\psi
and the
old
basis
\phi
as:

\displaystyle\psi_{j}\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\beta}^{N}C_{\alpha\beta}\,\phi_{j}\left(\beta\right),

(41)

\displaystyle\phi_{j}\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\beta}^{N}C^{-1}_{\alpha\beta}\,\psi_{j}\left(\beta\right).

(42)

The problem is now completely defined, and we seek now explicit relations between the old set of
variables and the new ones in order to express the problem in the eigenbasis of Hamiltonian
H_{ij}
.
The probability distribution function being defined through integrals over the old variables, the determinant
of the Jacobian associated to the change of basis needs to be calculated. This derivation is now presented step
by step, in the spirit of Ref.
Bogomolny 2017
, which developed a similar yet simpler calculation
for a perturbation of rank 1 to a random matrix.

A.2
Change of basis

A.2.1
From old eigenvectors
\{\phi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)\}
to new eigenvalues

\{E_{\alpha}\}

The desired change of variables from old to new variables,
\left(\{e_{\alpha}\},\{\phi_{\alpha}\}\right)\rightarrow\left(\{E_{\alpha}\},\{\psi_{\alpha}\}\right)
, cannot be done in one shot, and hence will be
done in two steps. In this first section, eigenvectors of the decoupled impurity problem will be expressed through
the eigenvalues of the perturbed problem, this choice being relevant given the form of the
equations. We first start with the full eigenvalue problem (
38
) in the new basis,
and express it with the old basis:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=0}^{N}\left[G_{ij}+V(\delta_{i0}\delta_{j1}+\delta_{i1}\delta_{j0})\right]\psi_{j}\left(\alpha\right)=E_{\alpha}\psi_{i}\left(\alpha\right),

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}C_{\alpha\beta}\left[E_{\alpha}\phi_{i}\left(\beta\right)-\sum\limits_{j=0}^{N}G_{ij}\phi_{j}\left(\beta\right)\right]=V[\delta_{i1}\psi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 193.47882pt+\delta_{i0}\psi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)],

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}C_{\alpha\beta}\left(E_{\alpha}-e_{\beta}\right)\phi_{i}\left(\beta\right)=V[\delta_{i1}\psi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)+\delta_{i0}\psi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)].

(43)

Multiplying the last equation by
\phi_{i}(\alpha)
, summing over
i
, and using orthogonality, we get
a simple expression for the transformation matrix:

\displaystyle C_{\alpha\beta}=V\frac{\phi_{1}\left(\beta\right)\psi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)+\phi_{0}\left(\beta\right)\psi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)}{E_{\alpha}-e_{\beta}}.

(44)

This last equality is injected in the change of basis (
41
):

\displaystyle\psi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)=V\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}\frac{\phi_{1}\left(\beta\right)\phi_{0}\left(\beta\right)\psi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)+|\phi_{0}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}\psi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)}{E_{\alpha}-e_{\beta}},

\displaystyle\psi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)=V\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}\frac{\phi_{0}\left(\beta\right)\phi_{1}\left(\beta\right)\psi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)+|\phi_{1}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}\psi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)}{E_{\alpha}-e_{\beta}}.

Using the wave function of the decoupled impurity problem, namely
\phi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)=\delta_{\alpha 0}

and
\phi_{1}\left(0\right)=0
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\psi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)=\frac{V}{E_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{d}}\;\psi_{1}\left(\alpha\right),

(46)

\displaystyle\psi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)=\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}\frac{V}{E_{\alpha}-e_{\beta}}|\phi_{1}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}\psi_{0}\left(\alpha\right),

(47)

which are consistent provided that:

\left(E_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{d}\right)=\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}\frac{V^{2}}{E_{\alpha}-e_{\beta}}|\phi_{1}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}.

(48)

Since
\phi_{1}\left(0\right)=0
is fixed, there are
N
old wave function variables on one side
(|\phi_{1}\left(1\right)|^{2},\ldots,|\phi_{1}\left(N\right)|^{2})
,
that are expressed in terms of the
N+1
new energy variables
(E_{0},\ldots,E_{N})
, thus one of the variables
E_{\alpha}

is not independent. For the moment, this extra variable is not explicitely determined, and is written

E_{0}=f(\{E_{\alpha\geq 1}\},\{\psi_{i\in\{1,0\}}\left(\alpha\right)\})
. The change of variables

\phi_{1}\left(\alpha>0\right)\rightarrow E_{\alpha}
is done first, so that
e_{\alpha}
and

\psi_{i\in\{1,0\}}\left(\alpha\right)
are supposed to be fixed for now. The system to be
solved Eq. (
48
) can be rewritten in matrix form
\hat{A}\;\vec{b}=\vec{\Lambda}
,
where we note the vector

{}^{t}\vec{\Lambda}=\big((E_{1}-\epsilon_{d}),\ldots,(E_{N}-\epsilon_{d})\big)
, the
vector
{}^{t}\vec{b}=V^{2}\big(|\phi_{1}\left(1\right)|^{2},\ldots,|\phi_{1}\left(N\right)|^{2}\big)
,
and
\hat{A}
is a Cauchy matrix defined as
A_{ij}=\frac{1}{E_{i}-e_{j}}
.
Cramer’s rule is used to determine the solutions
|\phi_{1}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}
through determinants of
matrices:

V^{2}|\phi_{1}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}=\frac{\det(\hat{A}_{\beta})}{\det(\hat{A})},

(49)

with
\hat{A}_{\beta}
a
N\times N
matrix defined by the subsitution of the column
\beta
of
\hat{A}
by
\vec{\Lambda}
:

\hat{A}_{\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{E_{1}-e_{1}}&\ldots&E_{1}-\epsilon_{d}&\ldots&\frac{1}{E_{1}-e_{N}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{E_{N}-e_{1}}&\ldots&E_{N}-\epsilon_{d}&\ldots&\frac{1}{E_{N}-e_{N}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(50)

The determinant of
\hat{A}_{\beta}
is calculated with the Laplace expansion of the
\beta\mathrm{th}
column:

\det(\hat{A}_{\beta})=\sum\limits_{\alpha\geq 1}^{N}(-1)^{\alpha+\beta}(E_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{d})\det(\hat{A}_{\beta\alpha}),

(51)

where
\hat{A}_{\beta\alpha}
is a matrix of rank
N-2
, defined by
\hat{A}
without its row
\alpha
and its column
\beta
.
The determinant of
\hat{A}
is also computed:

\det(\hat{A})=\det\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{E_{1}-e_{1}}&\ldots&\frac{1}{E_{1}-e_{N}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{E_{N}-e_{1}}&\ldots&\frac{1}{E_{N}-e_{N}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(52)

The column
\beta
is subtracted to each column
j\neq\beta
, which leaves the determinant unchanged:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{E_{i}-e_{j}}-\frac{1}{E_{i}-e_{\beta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{E_{i}-e_{\beta}-E_{i}+e_{j}}{(E_{i}-e_{j})(E_{i}-e_{\beta})}

(53)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(e_{j}-e_{\beta})}{(E_{i}-e_{\beta})(E_{i}-e_{j})}.

Each column is factorized by the term
(e_{j}-e_{\beta})
, and each row by the
factor
\frac{1}{E_{i}-e_{\beta}}
, so that:

\displaystyle\det(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}\frac{1}{E_{\gamma}-e_{\beta}}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\beta\end{subarray}}(e_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})

(54)

\displaystyle\times\det\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{E_{1}-e_{1}}&\ldots&1&\ldots&\frac{1}{E_{1}-e_{N}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{E_{N}-e_{1}}&\ldots&1&\ldots&\frac{1}{E_{N}-e_{N}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\!\!.\,\,

Now the row
\alpha
has to be eliminated, and it is subtracted to each row
i
except
i=\alpha
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{E_{i}-e_{j}}-\frac{1}{E_{\alpha}-e_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{E_{\alpha}-e_{j}-E_{i}+e_{j}}{(E_{\alpha}-e_{j})(E_{i}-e_{j})}

(55)

\displaystyle=\frac{(E_{\alpha}-E_{i})}{(E_{\alpha}-e_{j})(E_{i}-e_{j})},\;\;\;\;

and as before each column (except column
\beta
) is factorized by

\frac{1}{E_{\alpha}-e_{j}}
, and each row (except row
\alpha
)
by
(E_{\alpha}-E_{i})
:

\displaystyle\det(\hat{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\beta\end{subarray}}(e_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}(E_{\alpha}-E_{\gamma})}{\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}(E_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\beta\end{subarray}}(E_{\alpha}-e_{\gamma})}

(56)

\displaystyle\times\det\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{E_{1}-e_{1}}&\ldots&0&\ldots&\frac{1}{E_{1}-e_{N}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\ddots&1&\ddots&1\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{E_{N}-e_{1}}&\ldots&0&\ldots&\frac{1}{E_{N}-e_{N}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\!\!,\,\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\beta\end{subarray}}(e_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}(E_{\alpha}-E_{\gamma})}{\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}(E_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1\end{subarray}}(E_{\alpha}-e_{\gamma})}(E_{\alpha}-e_{\beta})(-1)^{\alpha+\beta}

\displaystyle\times\det(\hat{A}_{\beta\alpha}),

(57)

using again Laplace expansion.
Putting everything back into (
49
), this equation is now fully expressed in terms
of the new energies
E_{\alpha}
:

V^{2}|\phi_{1}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}=\frac{\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}(E_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\beta\end{subarray}}(e_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})}\!\sum\limits_{\alpha\geq 1}\!\frac{(E_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{d})}{(E_{\alpha}-e_{\beta})}\frac{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1\end{subarray}}(E_{\alpha}-e_{\gamma})}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}(E_{\alpha}-E_{\gamma})}.

(58)

The sum (denoted
\mathbb{S}
) in the right hand side of Eq. (
58
) can be expanded:

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\alpha\geq 1}\;\frac{(E_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{d})}{(E_{\alpha}-e_{\beta})}\frac{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1\end{subarray}}(E_{\alpha}-e_{\gamma})}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}(E_{\alpha}-E_{\gamma})}

(59)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\alpha\geq 1}\;\frac{(E_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{d})\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\lx@nobreakspace\gamma\neq\beta\end{subarray}}(E_{\alpha}-e_{\gamma})}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}(E_{\alpha}-E_{\gamma})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\alpha\geq 1}\frac{E^{N}_{\alpha}-\left[\sum\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}^{N}e_{\gamma}-e_{\beta}+\epsilon_{d}\right]E^{N-1}_{\alpha}+P(E_{\alpha})}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}(E_{\alpha}-E_{\gamma})}.

Simple arguments from complex analysis and contour integration can be used to compute such sums
Knuth 1997
as:

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\frac{x_{j}^{r}}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{N}(x_{j}-x_{i})}=\begin{cases}0,\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq r<N-1,\\
1,\quad\text{for}\quad r=N-1,\\
\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}x_{k},\quad\text{for}\quad r=N,\end{cases}

(60)

The expression for
\mathbb{S}
is split into the sum of three terms: the first two are respectively proportional
to
E^{N}_{\alpha}
and
E^{N-1}_{\alpha}
, and the last one corresponds to a polynomial
P(E_{\alpha})
of
degree strictly smaller than
N-1
. Using the simplifications of Eq. (
60
), Eq. (
58
) becomes:

\displaystyle V^{2}|\phi_{1}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}(E_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\beta\end{subarray}}(e_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})}\;\sum\limits_{\alpha\geq 1}\left(E_{\alpha}-e_{\alpha}+e_{\beta}-\epsilon_{d}\right)

(61)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}(E_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\beta\end{subarray}}(e_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})}\;\left(e_{\beta}-E_{0}\right),

where, to obtain the last line, we used
e_{0}=\epsilon_{d}
, as well as the trace property:

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N}e_{\alpha}=\text{Tr}(G)=\text{Tr}(H)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N}E_{\alpha}.

(62)

The eigenvectors of the decoupled impurity problem are now totally determined in terms of the old
and new eigenvalues. Denoting the square amplitudes

r_{\beta}=|\phi_{1}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}
,
the determinant of the Jacobian for this change of variables is now computed for

\alpha,\beta\geq 1
:

\big[\hat{J}_{\phi\rightarrow E}\big]_{\alpha,\beta}=\frac{\partial\,r_{\beta}}{\partial E_{\alpha}}.

(63)

Computing the derivative, we get:

\det\left(\hat{J}_{\phi\rightarrow E}\right)=\det\left(\frac{r_{\beta}}{E_{\alpha}-e_{\beta}}+\frac{\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}(E_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})}{V^{2}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\beta\end{subarray}}(e_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})}\right),

(64)

using the fact that
\partial E_{0}/\partial E_{\alpha}=-1
for
\alpha\geq 1
from the
trace formula (
62
).
The second term is identified from Eq. (
61
):

\frac{\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}(E_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})}{V^{2}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\beta\end{subarray}}(e_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})}=\frac{-r_{\beta}}{E_{0}-e_{\beta}},

(65)

so that the determinant is straightforwardly calculated:

\displaystyle\det\left(\hat{J}_{\phi\rightarrow E}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det\left(\frac{r_{\beta}}{E_{\alpha}-e_{\beta}}-\frac{r_{\beta}}{E_{0}-e_{\beta}}\right)

(66)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det\left(\frac{r_{\beta}(E_{0}-E_{\alpha})}{(E_{0}-e_{\beta})(E_{\alpha}-e_{\beta})}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}\left(\frac{E_{0}-E_{\gamma}}{E_{0}-e_{\gamma}}\right)\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}r_{\gamma}\det\left(\frac{1}{E_{\alpha}-e_{\beta}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}\left(\frac{E_{0}-E_{\gamma}}{E_{0}-e_{\gamma}}\right)\frac{(-1)^{N}}{V^{2N}}\frac{\prod\limits_{\delta,\gamma\geq 1}(E_{\gamma}-e_{\delta})}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta,\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\delta\end{subarray}}(e_{\gamma}-e_{\delta})}

\displaystyle\times\prod\limits_{\delta\geq 1}(E_{0}-e_{\delta})\frac{\prod\limits_{1\leq\gamma<\delta}(E_{\delta}-E_{\gamma})(e_{\gamma}-e_{\delta})}{\prod\limits_{\gamma,\delta\geq 1}(E_{\gamma}-e_{\delta})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V^{2N}}\prod\limits_{1\leq\gamma<\delta}\frac{(E_{\delta}-E_{\gamma})}{(e_{\gamma}-e_{\delta})}\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}\left(E_{\gamma}-E_{0}\right),

where we used standard property of the Cauchy determinant.
We thus have all the needed elements to replace the old eigenvectors of the decoupled impurity
problem by the new eigenvalues of the perturbed problem in the probability distribution function.
In order to close the problem in the complete set of new variables, we will now replace the
old eigenvalues of the decoupled problem by the perturbed new eigenvectors, using the same strategy.

A.2.2
From old eigenvalues
\{e_{\alpha}\}

to new eigenvectors
\{\psi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)\}

The orthogonality of the matrix

As before, the previous equation is reinjected in (
42

\displaystyle\phi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}\frac{|\psi_{0}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}\phi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)+\psi_{0}\left(\beta\right)\psi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)\phi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)}{E_{\beta}-e_{\alpha}},

\displaystyle\phi_{1}\left(\beta\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}\frac{\psi_{0}\left(\beta\right)\psi_{1}\left(\beta\right)\phi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)+|\psi_{1}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}\phi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)}{E_{\beta}-e_{\alpha}}.

Using
\phi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)=\delta_{\alpha 0}
,
\phi_{1}\left(0\right)=0
,
and the previous relation
\psi_{0}\left(\alpha\right)=\frac{V}{E_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{d}}\;\psi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}|\psi_{0}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,

(69)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}\frac{E_{\beta}-\epsilon_{d}}{E_{\beta}-e_{\alpha}}|\psi_{0}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,

(70)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}\frac{E_{\beta}-\epsilon_{d}}{V}|\psi_{0}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(71)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}\frac{V}{E_{\beta}-e_{\alpha}}|\psi_{0}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(72)

As before, there are

new variables

and only

old variables

. Conditions on

to

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\beta\geq 0}|\psi_{0}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}=1,

(73)

where we have used Eq. (

) to simplify Eq. (

69
) is the same equation as Eq. (

) for

\sum\limits_{\beta\geq 0}\frac{E_{\beta}-\epsilon_{d}}{E_{\beta}-e_{\alpha}}|\psi_{0}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}=1,\quad\forall\alpha\geq 0.

(75)

We thus found a similar equation to Eq. (
48
), although it involves here a square
(N+1)\times(N+1)
matrix

z_{0}

\displaystyle z_{\beta}

We thus obtained the change of variable from the

,

\det(\hat{J}_{e\rightarrow\Psi})=\det\left(\frac{\partial z_{\beta}}{\partial e_{\alpha}}\right)^{\!\!-1}\!\!\!\!\!\!=(-1)^{N}\prod\limits_{\gamma}\frac{1}{z_{\gamma}}\det\left|\frac{1}{E_{\beta}-e_{\alpha}}\right|^{-1}\!\!\!\!\!\!,

\displaystyle\det(\hat{J}_{e\rightarrow\Psi})

A.2.3
Sum of squared eigenvalues

We aim here to express, in terms of the new variables, the sum of the old eigenvalues squared (decoupled
impurity problem) which appears in the exponential in Eq. (
14
).
This term is easily derived using the fact that the trace of the square matrix
\hat{G}
does not to
depend on the basis choice. We define
W_{ij}=V(\delta_{i1}\delta_{j0}+\delta_{i0}\delta_{j1})
, so that:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{N}e^{2}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}(G^{2})=\text{Tr}(H-W)^{2}=\text{Tr}(H^{2}-2WH+W^{2})

(83)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{N}E^{2}_{\alpha}+2V^{2}-4V\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{N}E_{\alpha}\psi_{0}(\alpha)\psi_{1}(\alpha),

where we have inserted
H_{ij}=\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{N}E_{\alpha}\psi_{i}(\alpha)\psi_{k}(\alpha)
.
From Eq. (
46
), the sum is expressed with the new variables:

\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{N}e^{2}_{\alpha}=\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{N}E^{2}_{\alpha}-4\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{N}E_{\alpha}(E_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{d})z_{\alpha}+2V^{2},

(84)

in terms of the amplitudes
z_{\alpha}=|\psi_{0}(\alpha)|^{2}
.

A.2.4
Sum of squared eigenvectors

The sum of the amplitudes of the all old eigenvectors appears
in Eq. (
14
) inside a Dirac delta function, to ensure the normalization of

r_{\beta}=|\phi_{1}\left(\beta\right)|^{2}
.
This sum must also be expressed as a function of the new variables.
Using the property that
r_{0}=0
(decoupled impurity) and Eq. (
61
),
the wanted term reads:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}r_{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\beta=1}^{N}r_{\beta}=\sum\limits_{\beta=1}^{N}\frac{1}{V^{2}}\frac{\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}(E_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\beta\end{subarray}}(e_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})}\left(e_{\beta}-E_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{V^{2}}\sum\limits_{\beta=1}^{N}\frac{\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 0}(E_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\beta\end{subarray}}(e_{\gamma}-e_{\beta})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{V^{2}}\sum\limits_{\beta=1}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{N}}{(-1)^{N-1}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\beta\end{subarray}}(e_{\beta}-e_{\gamma})}\Bigg[-e^{N+1}_{\beta}

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{N}E_{\alpha}e^{N}_{\beta}-\sum\limits_{0\leq\alpha<\delta}^{N}E_{\alpha}E_{\delta}e^{N-1}_{\beta}+P(e_{\beta})\Bigg]

where
P(e_{\beta})
is a polynomial of order
N-2
, such that its sum vanishes
due to Eq. (
60
). From this equation, we also get the simplification:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}r_{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{V^{2}}\sum\limits_{\beta=1}^{N}\frac{e^{N+1}_{\beta}}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\beta\end{subarray}}(e_{\beta}-e_{\gamma})}+\frac{1}{V^{2}}\left(\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{N}E_{\alpha}\right)\left(\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{N}e_{\alpha}\right)

(85)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{V^{2}}\left(\sum\limits_{0\leq\alpha<\delta}^{N}E_{\alpha}E_{\delta}\right).

However, the sum involving the power
e^{N+1}_{\beta}
remains to be calculated.
For this purpose, the following complex valued function
f(z)
is defined:

f(z)=\frac{z^{N+1}_{{\phantom{\dagger}}}}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}(z-e_{i})}.

(86)

Using the residue theorem, this function can be integrated over a contour
\mathcal{C}
enclosing
every simple pole
e_{i}
:

\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\text{d}z}{2\pi i}\,f(z)\,\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\lim\limits_{z\rightarrow e_{j}}(z-e_{j})f(z)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\frac{e^{N+1}_{j}}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{N}(e_{i}-e_{j})},

(87)

that is in fact the sum that needs to be computed. The function
f(z)
is
analytic, so that a Laurent series can be defined on the contour
\mathcal{C}
that will
converge to
f(z)
:

f(z)=z\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\left(1-\frac{e_{i}}{z}\right)}=z\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sum\limits_{j=0}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{e_{i}}{z}\right)^{j}\right).

(88)

The terms at first order in
\frac{1}{z}
are developed
explicitely to compute the integral, which gives:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\text{d}z}{2\pi i}\,f(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\text{d}z}{2\pi i}\,z\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}\Bigg[1+\frac{e_{i}}{z}+\left(\frac{e_{i}}{z}\right)^{2}+o\left(\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right)\Bigg]

(89)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\text{d}z}{2\pi i}\,\Bigg[z+\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}e_{i}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\sum\limits_{1\leq i<j}^{N}e_{i}e_{j}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}e^{2}_{i}\right)\frac{1}{z}+o\left(\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum\limits_{1\leq i<j}^{N}e_{i}e_{j}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}e^{2}_{i}\right),

where the residue theorem was used to obtain the final result (all other terms obviously cancel).
Comparing this last equation to Eq. (
87
), the desired result reads:

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\frac{e^{N+1}_{j}}{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{N}(e_{i}-e_{j})}=\left(\sum\limits_{1\leq i<j}^{N}e_{i}e_{j}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}e^{2}_{i}\right).

(90)

From Eq. (
85
), the sum of square amplitudes is thus:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}r_{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{V^{2}}\Bigg[\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}e^{2}_{i}+\sum\limits_{1\leq i<j}^{N}e_{i}e_{j}-\left(\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{N}E_{\alpha}\right)\left(\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{N}e_{\alpha}\right)

(91)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{0\leq\alpha<\delta}^{N}E_{\alpha}E_{\delta}\Bigg].

Further simplifications can be done using the rearrangement

\sum\limits_{1\leq i<j}^{N}x_{i}x_{j}=\Big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\Big)^{2}/2-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}x^{2}_{i}/2

into the sums including
E_{\alpha}E_{\delta}
and
e_{i}e_{j}
,
leading to the following:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}r_{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{V^{2}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}e^{2}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}e_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{N}E_{\alpha}\right)\left(\sum\limits_{\alpha=1}^{N}e_{\alpha}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N}E_{i}\right)^{2}\!\!-\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N}E^{2}_{i}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{2V^{2}}\left[\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}e^{2}_{i}-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N}E^{2}_{i}\right)+\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}e_{i}-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N}E_{i}\right)^{2}\right]\!.

We inject Eq. (
84
) in the first term under parenthesis of (
), and
the second term under parenthesis cancels out due the trace formula (
62
), so that
the normalization of the old wavefunction in terms of the new variables reads:

\sum\limits_{\beta=0}^{N}r_{\beta}=\frac{2}{V^{2}}\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{N}E_{\alpha}(E_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{d})z_{\alpha}-1.

(93)

A.3
Full probability distribution

Every part of the change of variables being now defined, the joint distribution of eigenvalues and
eigenvectors of the random impurity matrix
H
can be derived:

P\Big(\{E_{\alpha}\},\{z_{\alpha}\}\Big)=|\det(\hat{J}_{e\rightarrow\Psi})\det(\hat{J}_{\phi\rightarrow E})|P\Big(\{e_{\alpha}\},\{r_{\alpha}\}\Big),

(94)

with the original GOE random matrix distribution given in Eq. (
14
).
From the Jacobian (
66
) and the expression (
61
) for the amplitudes,
the first change of variables reads:

\displaystyle P\Big(\{e_{\alpha}\},\{E_{\alpha}\}\Big)=|\det(\hat{J}_{\phi\rightarrow E})|\,P\Big(\{e_{\alpha}\},\{r_{\alpha}\}\Big)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\propto\prod\limits_{1\leq\gamma<\delta}\frac{(E_{\delta}-E_{\gamma})}{(e_{\gamma}-e_{\delta})}\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}\left(E_{\gamma}-E_{0}\right)\prod\limits_{1\leq\alpha<\beta}^{N}(e_{\alpha}-e_{\beta})

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\times\frac{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq 1,\\
\gamma\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}(e_{\gamma}-e_{\alpha})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(e_{\alpha}-E_{0})^{\frac{1}{2}}\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}(E_{\gamma}-e_{\alpha})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\delta\!\left(\sum\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}^{N}r_{\gamma}-1\right)\!e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\sum\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}^{N}e_{\gamma}^{2}}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\propto\frac{\prod\limits_{1\leq\gamma<\delta}(E_{\delta}-E_{\gamma})(e_{\delta}-e_{\gamma})}{\prod\limits_{\delta,\gamma\geq 1}(E_{\gamma}-e_{\delta})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}\frac{\left(E_{\gamma}-E_{0}\right)}{\left(e_{\gamma}-E_{0}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\times\delta\left(\sum\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}^{N}r_{\gamma}-1\right)\!e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\sum\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}^{N}e_{\gamma}^{2}}.

(95)

Using Jacobian (
) for the second change of variables:

\displaystyle P\Big(\{E_{\alpha}\},\{z_{\alpha}\}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\det(\hat{J}_{e\rightarrow\Psi})|P\Big(\{e_{\alpha}\},\{E_{\alpha}\}\Big)

(96)

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\frac{\prod\limits_{1\leq\gamma<\delta}(E_{\delta}-E_{\gamma})(e_{\delta}-e_{\gamma})}{\prod\limits_{\delta,\gamma\geq 1}(E_{\gamma}-e_{\delta})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,\!\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}\frac{\left(E_{\gamma}-E_{0}\right)}{\left(e_{\gamma}-E_{0}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\delta\left(\sum\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}^{N}r_{\gamma}-1\right)\!\!\prod\limits_{1\leq\delta<\gamma}\frac{(E_{\delta}-E_{\gamma})}{(e_{\gamma}-e_{\delta})}\prod\limits_{\delta\geq 1}(E_{\delta}-E_{0})e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\sum\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}^{N}e_{\gamma}^{2}}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\frac{\prod\limits_{1\leq\gamma<\delta}(E_{\delta}-E_{\gamma})^{2}}{\prod\limits_{\delta,\gamma\geq 1}(E_{\gamma}-e_{\delta})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}\frac{\left(E_{\gamma}-E_{0}\right)^{2}}{\left(e_{\gamma}-E_{0}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\delta\left(\sum\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}^{N}r_{\gamma}-1\right)e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\sum\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}^{N}e_{\gamma}^{2}},

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{1\leq\gamma<\delta}(E_{\delta}-E_{\gamma})\,\prod\limits_{\gamma\geq 1}\frac{\left(E_{\gamma}-E_{0}\right)^{3/2}}{\left(e_{\gamma}-E_{0}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\sum\limits_{\gamma=0}^{N}E^{2}_{\gamma}}\prod\limits_{1\leq\alpha}\frac{1}{(z_{\alpha})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\delta\left(\sum\limits_{\gamma=0}^{N}E_{\gamma}(E_{\gamma}-\epsilon_{d})z_{\gamma}-V^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\times e^{\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\left[4\sum\limits_{\gamma=0}^{N}E_{\gamma}(E_{\gamma}-\epsilon_{d})z_{\gamma}-2V^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{0\leq\gamma<\delta}(E_{\delta}-E_{\gamma})\prod\limits_{0\leq\alpha}\frac{1}{(z_{\alpha})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\delta\left(\sum\limits_{\gamma=0}^{N}E_{\gamma}(E_{\gamma}-\epsilon_{d})z_{\gamma}-V^{2}\right)e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\sum\limits_{\gamma=0}^{N}E^{2}_{\gamma}},

(98)

by inserting Eq. (
