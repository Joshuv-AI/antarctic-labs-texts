Assessment of internal limiting membrane flap and autologous whole blood techniques in complicated macular hole surgery
Authors: Büşra Köse, Hidayet Erdöl, Dilek Uzlu, Murat Günay, Mehmet Kola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate and compare the outcomes of internal limiting membrane (ILM) flap and autologous whole blood application for treatment of complicated macular holes (CMHs).MethodsA retrospective analysis of medical records was performed for patients who underwent vitrectomy for CMHs, including preoperative and postoperative evaluations at 1, 3, and 6 months. Patients were divided into two groups: Group 1 autologous whole blood and Group 2 ILM flap application. The hole parameters were measured using preoperative OCT images. The integrity of the external limiting membrane (ELM)-ellipsoid zone (EZ) and anatomical closure patterns was evaluated on postoperative OCT images. Closure patterns were scored.ResultsThe study included 37 eyes: 17 (45.9%) in Group 1 and 20 (54.1%) in Group 2. The postoperative closure rate was 94.1% in Group 1 and 100% in Group 2, with an overall rate of 97.3%. No significant differences were found between Groups 1 and 2 in OCT parameters, best-corrected visual acuity, ELM–EZ recovery, or macular edema reduction at any follow-up time point (p > 0.05). The optimal closure score was higher in group 2 at the first month postoperatively (p = 0.036), but no difference was detected at the third and sixth months (p = 0.870 and p = 0.780, respectively).ConclusionsThis study demonstrated that both autologous whole blood and ILM flap techniques are effective in the surgical management of CMHs, achieving high closure rates. Given its comparable postoperative results to the ILM flap, autologous whole blood may be a beneficial option in CMH surgery.

Introduction

The gold standard for full-thickness macular hole (MH) repair is posterior cortical vitreous removal combined with internal limiting membrane (ILM) peeling and gas tamponade [1, 2]. The relatively lower success rates of conventional pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) with ILM peeling in large or complicated macular holes (CMHs) led to the development of the ILM flap technique [3]. Müller cell remnants in the ILM release cytokines, promoting retinal gliosis, while the ILM flap aids gliosis and blocks fluid entry into the MH [4].

Various alternative techniques, mainly case series, include autologous lens capsule, amniotic membrane, retinal flaps, temporal arcade retinotomy, MH hydrodissection, and autologous blood or platelet-rich plasma (PRP) [1, 4, 5]. Blood derivatives support healing by supplying growth factors and cytokines and forming a fibrin scaffold [6, 7].

The success rates of these techniques are reported differently in various publications. There is no consensus in the literature on which technique should be used in which situation. In our study, autologous whole blood was selected because it is a simple and readily available biological material capable of providing growth factors and cytokines and forming a fibrin scaffold that may facilitate MH closure. In this context, the present study aimed to compare autologous whole blood application with the widely accepted ILM flap technique in the management of CMHs, with a particular focus on anatomical closure and functional visual outcomes.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study reviewed medical records of patients undergoing vitrectomy for CMHs at our Retina Clinic (June 2018–June 2023). Only patients with at least 6 months of follow-up were included. The study received ethics committee approval, and all participants provided written informed consent. It complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Preoperative systemic and ocular histories were documented. Best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), anterior and posterior segment examinations, and optical coherence tomography (OCT) were assessed preoperatively and at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively. BCVA was measured using the Snellen chart and converted to logMAR for statistical analysis.

Patients undergoing ILM flap or autologous blood application during PPV for CMHs were included. CMHs were defined as MHs associated with factors known to reduce surgical success, including large idiopathic holes (≥ 400 μm), persistent or recurrent MHs, and secondary MHs related to trauma, degenerative myopia, diabetic retinopathy, intraocular surgery, or macular telangiectasia, based on previously reported classifications [1, 5, 7, 8].

All surgeries used 23-gauge PPV (Stellaris, Bausch & Lomb, USA) under general anesthesia, performed by two experienced retinal surgeons. After cortical vitreous clearance, residual vitreous and posterior hyaloid were visualized with diluted triamcinolone and separated. Epiretinal membrane (ERM) was peeled if present. ILM was stained with Brilliant Blue, and in the first patient group, it peeled circumferentially over ~ 2 optic disc diameters. If the MH edges appeared widely separated during fluid–air exchange, gentle approximation was performed using a Tano’s sweeper without diamond dusters. This maneuver was applied when necessary and could be repeated until satisfactory edge apposition was achieved intraoperatively. Fresh venous blood was obtained intraoperatively from the left antecubital vein and applied as a single drop through the surgical port, with excess blood passively aspirated using a 25-gauge flute needle. Finally, an air-gas (12–14% C₃F₈) exchange was performed.

In the second group, the ILM in the inferior half was peeled (~ 2 optic disc diameters) and removed, while the superior half remained attached at the MH border, inverted, and placed over the hole. A small amount of viscoelastic was employed to stabilize the flap. Subsequently, air-gas (12–14% C₃F₈) exchange was carried out.

Patients with peripheral retinal degeneration or tears received endolaser photocoagulation. For multiple, large, and inferior half tears and degeneration, silicone oil was used as an endotamponade. All patients maintained a prone position for at least one week post-surgery.

OCT images were obtained preoperatively and at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively using the spectral-domain OCT device (Solix Optovue—Hannover, Germany). MHs were classified per Gass and the International Vitreomacular Traction Study (IVTS) Group [8, 9]. Full-thickness MHs were categorized as small (≤ 250 μm), medium (250–400 μm), or large (≥ 400 μm) based on their narrowest horizontal diameter. Vitreomacular traction (VMT) and ERM presence were recorded. The bases of the holes were evaluated for retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) abnormalities and deposit accumulation. If macular edema was present, its type was noted. Using the OCT software, the hole’s maximum and minimum diameters and base width were manually measured and were not adjusted for axial length. Maximum hole height was determined as the vertical distance between the apex and RPE. The detached photoreceptor arm length was measured obliquely in nasal and temporal areas. Preoperative prognostic indices, including macular hole index, diameter hole index, tractional hole index, and macular hole closure index, were calculated (Fig. 1a). To assess measurement reliability, intraobserver reproducibility of OCT parameters was evaluated in a subset of cases by repeating all manual measurements at a separate time point by the same observer.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 28 for Windows. Because this was a retrospective study, no formal sample size calculation was performed. All consecutive eligible patients treated during the study period were included. To control for potential confounding arising from retrospective consecutive inclusion, the preoperative characteristics of the two groups — including demographic data, ocular comorbidities, OCT parameters, and visual acuities — were compared prior to further analysis. As no statistically significant differences were identified between groups, the cohort was considered sufficiently comparable for subsequent analyses. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normal variables were shown as median (min-max). Dependent group comparisons used the matched t-test for normal data and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normal. Repeated measurements were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA for parametric data and the Friedman test for nonparametric data; sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test, and when the assumption of sphericity was violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. Kendall’s W was calculated to quantify the effect size associated with the Friedman test, with values interpreted as small (W < 0.30), moderate (0.30 ≤ W < 0.50), or large (W ≥ 0.50). Significance values have been adjusted by the Bonferroni correction for multiple tests. Postoperative OCT parameter differences were assessed with the chi-square test. Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation analyses examined variable relationships, while the Wilcoxon signed-rank test evaluated postoperative recovery grading. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Intraobserver agreement was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement (ICC [3, 1]). Agreement between repeated measurements was further evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis.

Results

Intraobserver reproducibility of OCT measurements was excellent, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.998 (95% CI: 0.996–0.999). Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated a mean difference (bias) of − 0.88, with limits of agreement ranging from − 20.6 to 18.9, indicating minimal systematic bias and a high level of agreement between repeated measurements.

At the sixth postoperative month, anatomical closure was achieved in 97.3% of patients (as a case sample, Fig. 2), except for one patient in the autologous blood group who developed recurrent MH after a 2D closure pattern at the first postoperative month and underwent reoperation after the third-month follow-up; therefore, this case was excluded from the sixth-month analyses. One ILM flap patient did not achieve closure at the first month but showed closure at the third and sixth months with a 1 C pattern. The 1 A pattern, the optimal anatomical and functional closure outcome, was seen in 18.9%, 43.2%, and 51.4% at the first, third, and sixth months postoperatively, respectively.

Mean preoperative BCVA was 1.20 ± 0.52 logMAR, and postoperative BCVA improved to 0.93 ± 0.44 at the first month, 0.83 ± 0.52 at the third month, and 0.72 ± 0.46 at the sixth month in all patients (p < 0.001). Significant differences were found between preoperative and postoperative first, third, and sixth months in BCVA (Friedman χ² = 49.88, p < 0.001). The medium-to-large effect size indicated by Kendall’s W (W = 0.536) suggests that the surgery had a substantial and clinically meaningful impact on functional outcomes. Post-hoc Wilcoxon analyses revealed significant improvements at all postoperative time points compared with the preoperative (all Bonferroni-adjusted p < 0.01). While the change in BCVA from the first to the third postoperative month was not statistically significant (p = 0.065), a significant additional improvement was detected between the third and sixth postoperative months (p = 0.017).

The distribution of BCVA according to closure pattern at postoperative months 1, 3, and 6 is presented in Fig. 3. There were no significant differences between the two groups in BCVA at the first, third, and sixth postoperative months (p = 0.997, p = 0.120, and p = 0.307, respectively). Likewise, we evaluated postoperative visual improvement (ΔBCVA = preoperative BCVA – postoperative BCVA, calculated separately for each time point) and found no significant differences between the groups (p > 0.05 for all time points). At the third postoperative month, a greater numerical improvement was observed in Group 1; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.064).

A strong correlation was found between BCVA at six months and preoperative BCVA (r = 0.639, p = 0.001). At sixth month, BCVA showed a positive correlation with base diameter (r = 0.398, p = 0.040). No significant associations were found between BCVA and other preoperative OCT parameters, including maximum diameter, minimum diameter, maximum hole height, macular hole index, diameter hole index, tractional hole index, and macular hole closure index (r = 0.218, p = 0.150; r = 0.276, p = 0.163; r = 0.185, p = 0.357; r = -0.371, p = 0.057; r = 0.017, p = 0.934; r = -0.300, p = 0.128; r = -0.274, p = 0.166, respectively).

A significant correlation was observed between BCVA and ELM recovery in the first month (r = 0.434, p = 0.024). At the third month, BCVA correlated with both ELM and EZ recovery (r = 0.605, p = 0.001; r = 0.626, p = 0.001, respectively). At sixth month, similar to the first month, BCVA correlated with ELM recovery only (r = 0.624, p = 0.001). No correlation was found between BCVA and macular edema at any time (1st month: r = 0.049, p = 0.750; 3rd month: r = 0.18, p = 0.261; 6th month: r = 0.095, p = 0.560). ELM and EZ recovery showed a strong correlation at all time points (1st month: r = 0.837, p < 0.001; 3rd month: r = 0.851, p < 0.001; 6th month: r = 0.601, p < 0.001).

Postoperative closure patterns were evaluated for “optimal closure.” Optimal closure scores showed a statistically significant improvement across the first, third, and sixth postoperative months (Friedman χ² = 13.52, p = 0.001) (Fig. 4). Post-hoc Wilcoxon analysis demonstrated significant increases from the first to the third month (p < 0.001) and from the first to the sixth month (p < 0.001), whereas the change between the third and the sixth month was not statistically significant (p = 0.170).

The sixth month optimal closure scores showed a positive correlation with the macular hole index, tractional hole index, and macular hole closure index but a negative correlation with minimum and maximum diameter. No correlations were found with base diameter, maximum hole height, or diameter hole index. A notable negative correlation was also observed with preoperative BCVA; however, given the inverse relationship of logMAR, higher optimal closure scores at six months were associated with better preoperative BCVA. A positive correlation was identified between the third-month closure scores and both the macular hole index and the tractional hole index; however, no associations were observed with other OCT parameters or preoperative BCVA. Furthermore, the first-month closure scores showed no relationship with preoperative BCVA or any OCT parameter (Table 5).

Discussion

Vitrectomy with conventional ILM peeling is the most effective surgical approach for MH treatment [5, 11], but closure rates for large holes remain lower [3, 12]. Numerous studies show that ILM flap techniques achieve higher functional and anatomical success in large or refractory MHs [7, 13–16]. Alternative techniques, including multilayer free ILM application, autologous retinal transplantation, amniotic membrane transplantation, autologous platelet concentrate application, platelet-rich plasma application, and lens capsule application, show high anatomical success but limited functional recovery in case series [15, 17, 18]. There is currently insufficient data to make a comparison between these techniques and ILM flap surgery. Furthermore, these studies do not standardize the etiology of the patients or the characteristics of the holes. As a result, it cannot be concluded which surgical technique is superior to the others.

Our study achieved a 97.3% closure rate—94.1% in Group 1, and 100% in Group 2. Although the technique of autologous whole blood application has been limitedly documented in the literature, our study demonstrated that, in the treatment of CMHs, this technique is comparable to the widely accepted ILM flap procedure, with no statistically significant differences observed in anatomical or functional outcomes. This similarity in outcomes may be explained by shared biological mechanisms. The ILM flap acts as a physical barrier that prevents fluid from re-entering the hole while providing a scaffold that supports cellular migration and tissue repair [4]. Similarly, autologous whole blood forms a fibrin matrix that may serve as a temporary scaffold and prevent fluid contact with the hole. In addition, blood-derived cytokines and growth factors may stimulate Müller cell activation and retinal gliosis, thereby facilitating tissue repair and hole closure [6, 7]. It is important to distinguish autologous whole blood from platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and other platelet concentrates used in macular hole surgery. Unlike PRP or platelet concentrates, which are prepared through centrifugation to selectively enrich specific cellular and growth factor components, autologous whole blood is unseparated and thus contains the full complement of cellular elements—including erythrocytes, leukocytes, platelets, plasma proteins, and clotting factors. This unprocessed composition allows it to form a stable fibrin clot at the hole site while simultaneously delivering a broad spectrum of growth factors and cytokines. The relative simplicity of whole blood preparation, requiring no specialized centrifugation equipment or processing steps, may offer a practical intraoperative advantage over PRP in resource-limited settings.

During the postoperative follow-up period, rhegmatogenous retinal detachment developed in one patient in Group 1 at 3 months, and cataracts were observed in one patient in Group 1 and two patients in Group 2. The rhegmatogenous retinal detachment was considered a possible postoperative complication related to the surgical procedure and pre-existing peripheral retinal pathology; however, a direct causal relationship could not be established with certainty due to the retrospective nature of the study. The cataract cases were judged to be consistent with the natural progression of lens opacity accelerated by intraocular surgery and gas tamponade rather than a direct complication of the macular hole repair technique itself.

The MHs studied were secondary, persistent, recurrent, or large, all of which contribute to lower anatomical and functional success [7, 19]. Additional procedures such as phacoemulsification, photocoagulation, and silicone oil removal may impair closure or cause recurrence due to postoperative inflammation and cystoid macular edema [7, 20]. In our study, phacoemulsification was performed in 27%, photocoagulation in 29.7%, silicone oil removal in 10.8%, detachment surgery in 2.7%, and intraocular lens extraction in 2.7%. Despite these, all patients who underwent these adjunctive procedures achieved anatomical closure with no recurrence over six months.

Although high surgical closure rates are well-documented, BCVA improvement remains limited in the literature [19, 21]. Studies have identified preoperative BCVA, ELM deterioration, minimum hole diameter, and base diameter as significant predictors of postoperative visual acuity [19, 22, 23]. The macular hole index is also considered a predictor [22–24]. In our study, BCVA at the sixth month showed a strong positive correlation with preoperative BCVA and a positive correlation with base diameter; however, given the inverse nature of the logMAR scale, this indicates that larger base diameters were associated with worse visual acuity. In line with existing literature, preoperative BCVA and base diameter were the most prominent predictors of postoperative visual acuity, while the macular hole index was unreliable.

The ELM and EZ demonstrated a marked improvement over time, reaching 67.6% and 59.5%, respectively, in the first month. By the third month, these values had increased to 78.4% and 73%, respectively, and continued to show steady progress, reaching 81.1% for both in the sixth month. Anatomical closure was achieved within the first month, while outer foveal layers continued improving through six months. The previous studies showed ELM and EZ healing correlate with better visual outcomes [5, 25]. In our study, BCVA correlated with ELM healing at all time points, but with EZ healing only at the 3rd month. These results indicated that ELM healing is a strong anatomical indicator of functional success after surgery. We observed improvement in both groups on the ELM and EZ and found no statistically significant difference between them, and BCVA improvement did not differ between the two groups, reflecting that the functional outcomes of both approaches were comparable.

In our study, closure patterns were classified using Rossi et al.‘s system to evaluate foveal anatomy restoration. Their study showed the greatest visual acuity gain in type 1, followed by type 2, with type 0 having the poorest outcomes [10]. Based on these findings, the closure patterns were graded in a manner that assigned the highest score to the pattern that most appropriately restored normal foveal anatomy. An improvement in the scores was observed up to the sixth month, which is indicative of ongoing anatomical healing. Preoperative MH morphology demonstrated a progressively stronger association with optimal closure outcomes over time. At the first postoperative month, no significant correlations were identified. However, both the macular hole index and the tractional hole index showed moderately positive correlations with closure scores in the third month. At six months, minimum and maximum diameters showed significant negative correlations, while the macular hole index, tractional hole index, and macular hole closure index displayed strong positive correlations with optimal closure scores. The findings indicate that preoperative structural configuration is a significant predictor of long-term anatomical recovery after CMH surgery. The significant negative correlation between preoperative BCVA and the six-month closure score suggests that patients with superior preoperative visual acuity achieve higher closure scores at six months.

Comparing anatomical healing between groups, the ILM flap group had higher optimal closure scores at the first month, but no significant differences at the third and sixth months. The relatively faster early anatomical restoration observed in the ILM flap group may be related to the immediate structural support provided by the ILM tissue itself. In contrast, the scaffold formed by autologous whole blood develops as the fibrin clot organizes, which may explain the slightly delayed early healing despite similar outcomes. Functionally, no BCVA differences were observed at any time point. These results indicate that both methods had comparable BCVA and overall functional success.

The use of platelet concentrates in various studies has been documented in MH surgery since 1995 with high success rates [6, 26, 27]. The literature, including case reports and case series-based studies, suggests autologous whole blood after ILM peeling yields favorable outcomes [28–31]. The present study is of particular significance as it is the first direct comparison of autologous whole blood and ILM flap techniques in CMHs. The results indicated that both methods achieved comparable anatomical and functional success. The present findings indicate that autologous blood application may present itself as a promising alternative to the widely accepted ILM flap. It offers advantages such as being faster, simpler to perform, less invasive to the retina, and requiring less surgical expertise compared to the ILM flap [6]. However, larger prospective studies with more homogeneous patient groups are needed to confirm these findings.

Our study concluded at the sixth month post-surgery; however, anatomical and functional improvements were observed to persist at this time point, highlighting the need for studies assessing longer-term outcomes. The retrospective design, small sample size, and follow-up period limited to six months represent the primary limitations of this study. Long-term prognosis (≥ 12 months) remains unknown, and the possibility of late recurrence or delayed functional decline cannot be excluded. Furthermore, due to the retrospective nature of the study, functional outcomes were evaluated solely based on BCVA; subjective visual outcomes such as postoperative metamorphopsia and contrast sensitivity were not formally assessed. Prospective multicenter randomized longitudinal studies with large patient cohorts, extended follow-up periods, and inclusion of patient-reported subjective visual outcomes are warranted to more comprehensively evaluate the functional efficacy of both surgical techniques. Additionally, although intraobserver reproducibility of OCT measurements was excellent, interobserver reliability could not be assessed as all measurements were performed by a single observer; future studies incorporating multiple independent observers would further strengthen measurement validity. Since CMHs have different etiologies, these factors may influence the success of surgery and the prognosis for recovery. To minimize this effect, the distribution of preoperative etiologies, OCT parameters, and visual acuities was compared between Group 1 and Group 2. As no statistically significant differences were observed, further evaluations were subsequently performed. Future studies may be designed with groups exhibiting more homogeneous etiological distributions.

Conclusions

The anatomical and functional outcomes of the ILM flap technique and the autologous whole blood technique for MH surgical procedures are favorable, with no statistically significant difference observed between them. Therefore, the autologous whole blood technique may be considered a viable alternative in the surgical management of CMHs. However, it is difficult to determine the surgical technique that should be preferred to achieve optimal anatomical and functional results in the treatment of CMHs. Multicenter, prospective, randomized, controlled studies with large patient numbers are needed for a standardized or generalized approach for achieving optimal anatomical and functional outcomes in the treatment of large, persistent, and recurrent MHs.
