With qualitative research, the risks of data sharing can outweigh the rewards
Authors: Jessica Calarco
Published: 2026

PNAS is among the many journals that have adopted data sharing policies, (1) with the goal of “promoting transparency, reproducibility, and accountability in research.” However, PNAS editors recently made an exception to journal guidelines—setting a precedent with important implications for the viability of qualitative research, particularly in the context of social media, surveillance culture, and artificial intelligence (AI).

Current policies state that authors must make all data (including raw visual data), materials, and code available to peer reviewers, and that, postpublication, authors must share all data, materials, and code in public repositories, as well. The policies also state that failure to comply “may result in manuscript rejection… and may preclude future publication in the journal.” Now, the policies do, in theory, permit exceptions, noting that, “requests for legal, ethical, and logistical (e.g., size) exceptions must be noted at submission.” However, the guidelines do not clarify the grounds for such requests.

Wading through this murkiness, PNAS editors agreed to exempt Kang et al.’s, “Social Cognition and Interpersonal Violence (2),” which is based on retrospective, in-depth interviews with 99 young Black and Hispanic men who participated in a Chicago-based violence prevention program. The project’s data are highly sensitive, exploring not only interviewees’ histories of violence but also their life experiences (including victimization and incarceration) more generally. Take a participant with the pseudonym Sidney. The paper recounts how, after being shot seven times as a teenager, Sidney became focused on “protecting myself by any means necessary,” even when that meant striking offensively, such that, “If I feel like you gonna hurt me, I’m gonna hurt you first.”

Now, you might think—If the paper quotes participants like Sidney, then what is the harm in publicly sharing full interview transcripts, as long as they are pseudonymized? And yet, as I will explain—after a brief primer on qualitative literacy (3)—exempting in-depth interviews from data sharing requirements is essential for legal, ethical, and logistical reasons, and for ensuring the future viability of qualitative research.

Quantitative and experimental research typically aim to accurately and precisely estimate some number that exists in the real world, such as the prevalence of a particular outcome—like violence—or the effect that some factor or intervention will have on that outcome—like with past victimization, or participation in a violence prevention program. This aim is at the heart of the push for data sharing, because transparency helps to ensure that a study exhibits the validity, reliability, and generalizability needed to produce accurate and precise results.

Qualitative research, by contrast, aims to make sense of the real world. Kang et al., for example, aim to make sense of why some people participate in violence while others from similar backgrounds do not. Given these aims, the quality of qualitative research is determined not by the data’s validity, reliability, or generalizability, (1) but rather by the extent to which the data demonstrate what my coauthor Mario Small and I call cognitive empathy, which is the result of the researcher’s efforts to understand “how those interviewed or observed view the world and themselves—from their perspective” (3). Achieving cognitive empathy helps to ensure that qualitative researchers are not simply imposing their own preconceived assumptions when interpreting data—because they have taken steps to understand and contextualize participants’ perspectives first. Those steps include generating data that are highly palpable, such as by asking follow-up questions to gather concrete details about specific situations participants have encountered, rather than just accepting the abstract generalizations that participants might initially offer when asked about their lives.

Strictly enforced data sharing requirements thereby threaten the quality and the viability of qualitative research. These threats stem not only from logistical challenges but also, more gravely, from data sharing’s legal and ethical risks (1).

Legally speaking, sharing high-quality qualitative data risks running afoul of federal laws and Institutional Review Board (IRBs) requirements regarding the protection of participant confidentiality, even if pseudonyms are used. In Kang et al.’s case, for example, readers are told that the interviewees are young Black or Hispanic men recruited from the Rapid Employment and Development Initiative Chicago (READI) violence prevention program. These details, coupled with full transcript data, would make it difficult to ensure “internal confidentiality”—i.e., to prevent program participants or staff from identifying each other (4). Meanwhile, and even for outsiders, access to full transcripts would arguably make it possible to identify specific participants based on what could be gleaned from web searches and gumshoe reporting alone. That is essentially what happened with Alice Goffman’s book, On the Run. In 2015, journalist Jesse Singal was able to unmask otherwise anonymous participants based on published details from Goffman’s fieldwork (5).

Today, confidentiality risks are also amplified by social media, surveillance, and AI tools, which make ample data about people publicly available, thereby making deidentification less effective. In the United States, more than 70 percent of adults and roughly 95 percent of US teens have social media accounts, and roughly half of those post content at least occasionally (6, 7). Smartphones are also nearly ubiquitous among US teens and adults (8, 9), and are often equipped with apps that record users’ data (10). These data are sold to advertisers (11) and used to train AI tools (12, 13), including for use in stylometry—the identification (including forensic identification) of anonymous authors from samples of text (14). More reference text increases the accuracy of these identifications (15), but technologies have advanced to the point where 1,500 to 8,000 characters is sufficient to identify an author (16). In-depth interview transcripts can easily exceed 100,000 characters, which means that sharing them, even pseudonymously, puts participant confidentiality at risk. Of course, AI is not foolproof (17), but this creates a new risk for people who are not study subjects—the risk of being misidentified as a participant in research.

That risk brings me to the ethical problems with sharing high-quality qualitative data, which hinge on the harm of being named. These harms are particularly acute for marginalized and vulnerable populations (18–20), including immigrants (21), people with histories of arrest or incarceration (22), and victims of discrimination and abuse (23). While members of these groups often appreciate the opportunity to share their stories with researchers (24), they rightly worry about how their stories could be used against them—i.e., making them targets for retaliation, deportation, or legal prosecution, or making it more difficult to secure a job, a home, a loan, or even a romantic partner. These worries shape people’s decisions about whether and how to participate in research (25), which is why IRBs typically require that researchers inform participants, in advance, of plans to share data beyond the research team.

Now, researchers have begun to develop “data capsules” and other tools that might eventually allow for secure sharing and analysis of text-based data (26). However, there are still logistical problems to overcome. High-quality qualitative projects are often immense in size, including hundreds (or thousands) of pages of transcripts and fieldnotes, hundreds of hours of audio and/or video files, background surveys, analytic memos, data matrices, and other output from coding tools. Certainly, qualitative researchers could—and often do—share their interview protocols and coding schemes (1), but expecting qualitative researchers to share raw data will be size and cost prohibitive, especially given that qualitative researchers face substantial funding barriers and rarely receive large grants to support their work.

Ultimately, then, and given the legal and ethical risks and logistical challenges at play, I would argue not only that the PNAS editors were right in granting an exception for Kang et al.’s study but also that such exemptions should be broadly applied to ethnographic and in-depth interview data. Put differently, concerns about reproducibility should not trump concerns about confidentiality or harm to participants, particularly when efforts to ensure reproducibility have the potential to place inequitable burdens on qualitative researchers and to further erode people’s willingness to participate in research (27).
