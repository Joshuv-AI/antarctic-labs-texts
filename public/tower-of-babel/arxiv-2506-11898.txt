Scalable Generalized Bayesian Online Neural Network Training for Sequential Decision Making
Gerardo Duran-Martin, Leandro Sánchez-Betancourt, Álvaro Cartea, Kevin Murphy

Abstract
We introduce scalable algorithms for online learning and generalized Bayesian inference of neural network parameters, designed for sequential decision making tasks. Our methods combine the strengths of frequentist and Bayesian filtering, which include fast low-rank updates via a block-diagonal approximation of the parameter error covariance, and a well-defined posterior predictive distribution that we use for decision making. More precisely, our main method updates a low-rank error covariance for the hidden layers parameters, and a full-rank error covariance for the final layer parameters. Although this characterizes an improper posterior, we show that the resulting posterior predictive distribution is well-defined. Our methods update all network parameters online, with no need for replay buffers or offline retraining. We show, empirically, that our methods achieve a competitive tradeoff between speed and accuracy on (non-stationary) contextual bandit problems and Bayesian optimization problems.

Introduction

In sequential decision-making problems,
such as Bayesian optimization and contextual bandits,
uncertainty quantification and efficient belief updates
are essential for balancing exploration and exploitation
[
15
]
.
A standard approach to this is to use Bayesian neural networks (BNNs)
[
47
]
. However,
exact posterior inference in BNNs, through methods such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC),
is computationally expensive,
limiting its usage in time-sensitive applications like recommender systems, adaptive control, and financial forecasting

[
56
,
43
,
45
,
8
,
62
]
.

A popular alternative to HMC is the use of variational Bayes methods
[
57
,
6
,
31
]
.
Most work uses a Gaussian approximation to the posterior. However, this requires
O({D_{\bm{\theta}}}^{2})
space to store the covariance (or precision matrix), and

O({D_{\bm{\theta}}}^{3})
time to update it at each step,
where
{D_{\bm{\theta}}}
is the number of parameters in the network.
One solution is to approximate
the posterior precision matrix with a diagonal-plus-low-rank structure
(see, e.g., the
LoFi
method
[
10
]
),
which takes

O({D_{\bm{\theta}}}\,d^{2})
time to update, where
d
is
the low-rank value.
Another approach is to use
last-layer Bayesian methods,
that use a full covariance Gaussian over the final layer weights, but use a point estimate for the hidden layer weights (see e.g., the
VBLL
method
[
7
]
).
These methods take
O({D_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}^{3}+{D_{{\bm{h}}}})
time per update, where
{D_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}
is the number of parameters in the final layer and
{D_{{\bm{h}}}}
is the number of parameters in the hidden layers. However, they do not capture uncertainty in the hidden layer parameters (which are usually the majority of the network). See Section
6
for further discussion of related work.

In this work, we propose a methodology that combines recursive frequentist inference, for efficient full-rank/low-rank parameter updates,
with a generalized Bayesian posterior predictive that we use for sequential decision making.
The frequentist part finds estimates of the parameters and quantifies the error variance of these estimates, which is simpler than characterizing densities. More precisely, we do not need to ensure that the updates we make yield a proper target density.
However, following the principles of the Martingale posterior
[
22
]
, we can still construct a valid posterior predictive distribution without invoking the prior-likelihood-posterior update.
In summary, our
probabilistic method to train neural networks
enjoys these properties:
(i) efficient closed-form updates (Monte Carlo sampling is not required);
(ii) fully online (it avoids replay buffers, which can be problematic in non-stationary problems);
(iii) delivers closed-form uncertainty-aware predictions through the posterior predictive.

We evaluate the performance of our methods across various
sequential decision-making tasks.
In non-stationary neural contextual bandits, our method achieves the highest performance with lowest computational cost compared to baselines.
In stationary settings such as Bayesian optimization,
our method matches the performance of replay-buffer approaches while achieving a range of
Pareto-efficient tradeoffs between runtime and performance.
Our code is implemented in Jax and it is publicly available at

github.com/gerdm/low-rank-last-layer-inference

Consider a sequence of output

In our method,

,

,

.

1

1

For notational simplicity we focus on regression tasks, where the observation model is Gaussian with fixed variance; in this case,
we only need to model the observation mean, given by the function
f
. However, we can easily generalize our method to other noise models, such as classification problems; see Appendix

Our goal is to efficiently compute best linear unbiased predictions (BLUPs)

In this sense, we combine frequentist recursive parameter estimation with Bayesian decision making via the posterior predictive.

3
Background

3.1
Tools for sequential decision making

A key challenge in sequential decision making is the exploration-exploitation tradeoff,
in which an agent must balance the use of its current knowledge of the environment (exploitation)
with acquiring new information to improve future decisions (exploration)
[
4
]
.
For example consider
the case of a
K
-armed contextual bandit.
Let the input (context) be
{\bm{x}}_{t}
,
and the predicted mean reward for arm
a
be

{f}({\bm{\theta}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{t},a)
,
where

a\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
.
A simple approach — which we use as a baseline — is the
\epsilon
-greedy exploration,
in which
the next action is
a_{t+1}=\argmax_{a}{f}(\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{t},a)
with probability
1-\epsilon
,
and uniformly at random with probability
\epsilon
,
where
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and

\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t}
is a point estimate.

A more sophisticated approach is known as Thompson sampling (TS)
[
52
]
,
where an agent samples the parameters from the posterior distribution
(or approximate posterior

[
50
]
)
and then makes a greedy decision based on a plugin estimate of the reward given the sampled parameter.
This approach is studied in the context of Bayesian neural bandits in
[
51
]
.
In this work, however, we focus on uncertainty in predictions rather than uncertainty in parameters.
For example,
for the case of the
K
-armed contextual bandit,
our approach works as follows:
at each timestep
t
,
estimates of the reward are sampled from the posterior predictive

\hat{y}_{a}\sim p({\bm{y}}_{t+1}\,|\,{\bm{x}}_{t+1},a,\mathcal{D}_{1:t})
,
after which an action is selected as
a_{t+1}=\argmax_{a\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}\hat{y}_{a}
.
2
2

2

An alternative design is for
{f}
to output a reward for each action, i.e.,

{f}:\mathbb{R}^{D_{\bm{\theta}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{K\,{D_{\bm{x}}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}
.
In this case,
{\bm{x}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{K,{D_{\bm{x}}}}
represents the concatenated features for all arms.
Both configurations are explored in our experiments.

This predictive-based approach to TS is also considered in the context of Bayesian optimization in
[
7
]
.
It is particularly relevant in our setting
because our parameter estimates resemble degenerate multivariate Gaussians,
due to the positive semi-definiteness of the covariance matrices
[
54
]
.
Despite this, we show that the posterior predictive distribution is a valid density,
(cf.
[
22
]
).

3.2
Extended Kalman filtering for online learning

We model observations as
{\bm{y}}_{t}={f}({\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{t-1},{\bm{h}}_{t-1},{\bm{x}}_{t})+{\bm{e}}_{t}
, where
{f}
is a neural network and
{\bm{e}}_{t}
is zero-mean noise with covariance
\mathbf{R}_{t}\in{\mathcal{M}^{++}_{{D_{\bm{y}}}}(\mathbb{R})}
;
see Appendix
A
for notation.
Next, assume that the latent parameter vector
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=[{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{t}^{\intercal},{\bm{h}}_{t}^{\intercal}]^{\intercal}

evolves as
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}={\bm{\theta}}_{t-1}+{\bm{u}}_{t}

where
{\bm{u}}_{t}
is a zero-mean dynamics noise process with variance
\mathbf{Q}_{t}\in{\mathcal{M}^{+}_{{D_{\bm{\theta}}}}(\mathbb{R})}
.
Following the frequentist interpretation of the Kalman filter
[
28
,
21
]
,
we aim to recursively compute the best linear unbiased predictor (BLUP)
{\bm{\theta}}_{t|k}=\mathbf{A}_{t|k}{\bm{y}}_{1:k}

and its associated error variance-covariance (EVC) matrix

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|k}={\rm Cov}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}-{\bm{\theta}}_{t|k})
, for
k=t
and
k=t-1
, under a local linearization of the model.
Here,
\mathbf{A}_{t|k}\in{\mathcal{M}_{{D_{\bm{\theta}}}\times(k\,{D_{\bm{y}}})}(\mathbb{R})}
is the best linear mapping (in
L^{2}
)
from the collection of the first
k
observations to the space of parameters at time
t
.

To obtain closed-form solutions for the BLUP and EVC at time
t
,
we linearize the observation model
{f}
around
{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1|t-1}=({\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{t-1|t-1},{\bm{h}}_{t-1|t-1})

to obtain

{\bm{y}}_{t}\approx{f}({\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{t-1|t-1},\,{\bm{h}}_{t-1|t-1},\,{\bm{x}}_{t})+\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{t}({\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{t-1|t-1})+\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{t}({\bm{h}}_{t}-{\bm{h}}_{t-1|t-1})+{\bm{e}}_{t},

(1)

where

\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{t}=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}{f}({\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{t-1|t-1},\,{\bm{h}}_{t-1|t-1},\,{\bm{x}}_{t})

and

\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{t}=\nabla_{{\bm{h}}}{f}({\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{t-1|t-1},\,{\bm{h}}_{t-1|t-1},\,{\bm{x}}_{t})
.
It then follows that
{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t}
can be written as a function
of
{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}
,
which can be written as a function of
{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1|t-1}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t-1|t-1}
and so on.
As a consequence,
these updates enable efficient sequential (single-pass) online learning.
This procedure is known as the extended Kalman filter (EKF).

Now, assume that

p({\bm{\theta}}_{0})={\cal N}({\bm{\theta}}_{0|0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})
,

p({\bm{e}}_{t})={\cal N}({\bm{e}}_{t}\,|\,\boldsymbol{0},\mathbf{R}_{t})
, and

p({\bm{u}}_{t})={\cal N}({\bm{u}}_{t}\,|\,\boldsymbol{0},\mathbf{Q}_{t})
,
then, the EKF yields updates for the BLUP and EVC which could be interpreted as Gaussian posteriors
with mean
{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t}
and covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t}
, i.e.,

p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\,|\,{\bm{y}}_{1:t})={\cal N}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t|t},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t})

[
58
]
.
This interpretation gives rise to the posterior predictive

p({\bm{y}}_{t}\,|\,{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})={\cal N}({\bm{y}}_{t}\,|\,\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{t}\,{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1},\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{t}^{\intercal}+\ \mathbf{R}_{t}),

with Jacobian

\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{t}&\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{t}\end{bmatrix}
,
and with mean prediction

{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1}=\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{t|t-1}^{\intercal}&{\bm{h}}_{t|t-1}^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}
.
(Full derivations, proofs, and the frequentist and Bayesian interpretation of these updates are in Appendix
B
.)

4
Method

The standard EKF strategy for online learning presented in Section
3.2
does not typically scale to large neural networks
because of the memory cost is
O({D_{\bm{\theta}}}^{2})
and the compute cost is
O({D_{\bm{\theta}}}^{3})
.
A number of approaches are proposed to tackle this issue
(see Appendix
6
for details).
Our method leverages the frequentist perspective of filtering to derive low-rank updates
and the Bayesian interpretation of filtering to make sequential decisions through the posterior predictive.

Like the Cholesky factor, the surrogate covariance in (i) reduces the computational cost by ignoring the dynamics noise
However, we retain some information from the dynamics noise to update the hidden layer parameters.
Step (ii) enables a fast update rule.

, and

\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k={d_{{\bm{h}}}}+1}^{D_{{\bm{h}}}}
are smallest
({D_{{\bm{h}}}}-{d_{{\bm{h}}}})
eigenvalues of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t}
.

Remark 4.2
.

Proposition
4.1
, whose proof is in Appendix
F.4
, shows that the per-step error in the
HiLoFi
approximation
is bounded by:
(i) the neglected terms by each surrogate matrix,
(ii) the low-rank approximation for the covariance of the hidden layers,
and
(iii) the cross-terms from the blocks in the off-diagonal.
(Note, however, that it does not account for error due to linearization.)
One could minimize the above lower bound by setting
q_{{\bm{h}},t}=q_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}},t}=0
.
However, this may result in lower per-step performance.
See Appendix
G.1.2
for an example of the main sources of error.

5
Experiments

In this section, we experimentally evaluate our method.
We start by considering a 1d example of
"in-between" uncertainty, to qualitatively
illustrate the behavior of the method. We then quantitatively compare its performance to other methods
across the following sequential decision-making tasks:

(I)

MNIST for classification and bandits
:
This is an online high-dimensional classification problem, which we also study as a contextual bandit problem.

(II)

Recommender systems
:
This is a challenging real-world problem
with non-stationary data.
Here, tackling exploration-exploitation tradeoff is key.

(III)

Bayesian optimization
:
The goal of this task is to find the maximum of an unknown blackbox function

g:[0,1]^{D_{\bm{x}}}\to\mathbb{R}
.
This is a static inference problem
where sample efficiency
and uncertainty quantification are crucial.

For each of the above tasks, we assess the various methods on
predictive performance and wall-clock time.
All experiments were run on a TPU v4-8.

We consider the following variants of our framework.

High-rank low-rank filter

(
HiLoFi
):
Our main method
which uses full rank for last layer
and low rank for hidden layers.

Low-rank low-rank filter

(
LoLoFi
):
A version of our method that uses low-rank matrices for last and hidden layers.
We only consider
LoLoFi
in task
(II)
, due to the relatively large dimension of the last layer.

Low-rank square-root Kalman Filter

(
LRKF
):
This is a special case of our method, described in Appendix
E.3
,
that uses a single low-rank covariance
for all the parameters.
This serves to illustrate the gains from modeling the final layer separately.

As existing baselines, we consider the following.

Diagonal plus low-rank precision
(
LoFi
)
[
10
]
:
A fully online method that models the precision matrix using a low-rank plus diagonal matrix, and uses the same linearization scheme as us.

Gaussian processes
(
GP
)
[
64
]
:
A standard approach to modeling predictive uncertainty.
We only include GPs in the low-dimensional tasks in
(III)
because of scalability limitations.

Variational Bayesian last layer
(
VBLL
)
[
7
]
:
A partially Bayesian method that requires access to the full dataset (or a replay buffer),
and performs multiple optimization steps per update.
See Appendix
E.4
for details.

Online variational Bayesian last-layer
(
OnVBLL
):
A straightforward modification of the
VBLL
method with a first-in-first-out (FIFO) buffer and no regularization.

Last-layer Laplace approximation with FIFO buffer
(
LLL
)
[
13
]
:
A method that uses a Laplace approximation on the last layer,
with a FIFO replay buffer and multiple inner updates per time step.
Unless otherwise stated, we train the parameters of the neural network using the AdamW
optimization algorithm
[
44
]
.

As an even simpler baseline, which does not model uncertainty,
we consider
Muon
(
muon
)
[
32
]
,
which a special case of the Shampoo optimizer
[
26
]
.
This is
a quasi second-order optimization method that is scalable and shows strong empirical performance.
We use the implementation in the Optax library
[
14
]
.
Unlike the other methods, it only computes a point estimate, so we cannot use it for computing the posterior predictive.
We use this method for the
\epsilon
-greedy bandit and as a choice of optimizer for

in Section
5.2
.

6
Related work

Here we briefly discuss additional related work, beyond those in Section
1
. We focus on deterministic methods for approximate online parameter inference.
One line of work approximates the posterior using a diagonal plus low-rank (DLR) representation
for the precision matrix,
which allows for low-rank updates.
This includes the L-RVGA method of
[
36
]
,
which applies a factor-analysis perspective to recursive updates, as well as SLANG
[
46
]
and
LoFi

[
10
]
.
Lastly, efficient low-rank Kalman filtering has been explored for state-space models in
[
53
]
. However, their method assumes that the dimension of the low-rank component is smaller than the dimension of the measurements, an assumption that is not required in our case.
Going beyond low-rank approximations,
the FOO-VB method
[
66
]
scales online BNN inference by
considering a Kronecker product decomposition of the posterior (error-variance) covariance.
In the offline setting, scaling BNNs using various low-rank assumptions has been studied in, e.g.,
[
41
,
59
,
35
]
.

Another class of methods draws on the lottery ticket hypothesis
[
38
,
42
]
, which suggests that sparse subspaces can yield performant models.
Techniques such as the subspace neural bandit
[
17
]
and PULSE
[
9
]
restrict learning to a subset of weights represented as a linear subspace.
However, these methods require offline learning of the projection matrix from the linear subspace to the space of weights before online inference begins.

7
Conclusion and future work

We introduced novel methods for efficient online training of neural networks.
Our approach is based on linearization and optimal low-rank approximations of error covariance matrices. We proposed three variants:
HiLoFi
,
LoLoFi
, and
LRKF
.
These variants offer a good trade-off between performance and runtime in static settings, and achieve highest performance and efficiency in high-dimensional, non-stationary scenarios.

One limitation of our methods is that it does not support
fixed-lag smoothing,
which can be important for sample efficiency in problems with low signal-to-noise ratio.
In addition, our theory does not capture errors due to linearization.
Additional limitations include sensitivity to outliers
[
1
,
16
,
37
]
and reliance on hyperparameters.
In the future, we aim to extend our approach to fully-online reinforcement learning
[
20
]
.

We view our contributions as primarily methodological, with no particular ethical concerns.

Proposition C.2

Next, our update step for the BLUP resembles an EKF update, while the EVC update approximates a block-diagonal covariance:

D.2
Computational complexity

We now compare the computational complexity of the algorithms we employ.
Recall that
{D_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}
,
{D_{{\bm{h}}}}
, and
{D_{\bm{y}}}
are the number of parameters in the last layer, hidden layers, and the size of the output layer, respectively. As before,
{D_{\bm{\theta}}}={D_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}+{D_{{\bm{h}}}}
. The dimensions
{d_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}},{d_{{\bm{h}}}}
, with

{d_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}\ll{D_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}
and
{d_{{\bm{h}}}}\ll{D_{{\bm{h}}}}
, are the low-dimensional subspaces for the last and hidden layers, respectively,
and
d={d_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}+{d_{{\bm{h}}}}
.

The computational complexity of our algorithm when using full-rank in the last layer is

O({D_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}\,({D_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}+{D_{\bm{y}}})^{2}+{D_{{\bm{h}}}}\,({d_{{\bm{h}}}}+{D_{\bm{y}}})^{2})
.
For low-dimensional outputs, this is

O({D_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}^{3}+{D_{{\bm{h}}}}{d_{{\bm{h}}}}^{2})
.
For high-dimensional outputs, we may choose to use
a low-rank approximation for the last layer (which we call
LoLoFi
)
this reduces the cost to

O({D_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}\,({d_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}+{D_{\bm{y}}})^{2}+{D_{{\bm{h}}}}\,({d_{{\bm{h}}}}+{D_{\bm{y}}})^{2})
,
which is linear in
{D_{\bm{\theta}}}

The primary computational bottlenecks in our method are the calculations for the Kalman gain of
the hidden and last layers, with costs

O(2\,{D_{{\bm{h}}}}\,{D_{\bm{y}}}^{2})
and

O(2\,{D_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}\,{D_{\bm{y}}}^{2})
respectively.
This efficiency arises because

\mathbf{S}_{t}^{1/2}
is upper triangular,
allowing the system

\mathbf{S}_{t}^{\intercal/2}\,\mathbf{S}_{t}^{1/2}\,\mathbf{V}=\tilde{\mathbf{J}}
for
\mathbf{V}

to be solved in

O(2\,{D_{{\bm{h}}}}\,{D_{\bm{y}}}^{2})
as opposed to

O({D_{{\bm{h}}}}\,{D_{\bm{y}}}^{3})
.
Additionally, approximating the covariance matrix via truncated SVD incurs a cost of

O({D_{{\bm{h}}}}\,({d_{{\bm{h}}}}+{D_{\bm{y}}})^{2}+({d_{{\bm{h}}}}+{D_{\bm{y}}})^{3}+{d_{{\bm{h}}}}\,({d_{{\bm{h}}}}+{D_{\bm{y}}})\,{D_{{\bm{h}}}})
,
as detailed in Figure 8.6.1 in
[
25
]
.
Finally, the corresponding cost for the last layer is

O({D_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}\,({D_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}+{D_{\bm{y}}})^{2})
.

Among related methods,
the closest to ours in terms of computational costs are variational Bayes approaches
such as the
Slang
method
[
46
]
, the
L-RVGA
method
[
36
]
, and the
LoFi
method
[
10
]
.
In particular,
LoFi
uses the linearized Gaussian updates (similar to the ones in this paper),
but
approximates the precision matrix using
a diagonal-plus-low rank (DLR) form.
The appeal for DLR precision matrices is twofold.
First, they enable the application of the Woodbury identity, leading to a predict step of cost

O({D_{\bm{\theta}}}\,d+d^{3})

and an update step of

O({D_{\bm{\theta}}}\,(d+{D_{\bm{y}}})^{2})=O({D_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}\,(d+{D_{\bm{y}}})^{2}+{D_{{\bm{h}}}}\,(d+{D_{\bm{y}}})^{2})
.
Second,
incorporating positive diagonal terms ensures the matrix is positive definite,
so that a valid posterior Gaussian density is defined.

Although
HiLoFi
and
LoFi
have the same asymptotic complexity,

LoFi
incurs additional practical overhead due to three key operations absent in
HiLoFi
:
(1) the inversion of a
(d+{D_{\bm{y}}})
rectangular matrix,
(2) the inversion of a
d
rectangular matrix, and
(3) the Cholesky decomposition of a
d
rectangular matrix.
These operations increase the actual per-step computational time, which in our example,
scales at about one second per additional rank.
This behaviour is shown in the empirical comparison across
HiLoFi
,
LRKF
, and
LoFi
in the online classification setting
(Figure
6
, Appendix
G.1
).

E.3
Low-rank Kalman filter (LRKF)

Inspired by
[
60
]
,
we derive a novel version of the low-rank Kalman filter
which is
(i) more computationally efficient,
(ii) simple to implement, and
(iii) provably optimal (in terms of Frobenius norm).
We call this
the
LRKF
method.
Instead of keeping track
of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t}
or
\mathbf{S}_{t}
,
we keep track of a low-rank factor for the covariance,
and compute the Cholesky factor for the innovation variance
\mathbf{S}_{t}^{1/2}
.
Algorithm
4
summarizes the predict and update steps for
LRKF
.
Next we turn to the derivation.

We assume the model parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
and the observations
{\bm{y}}
follow

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}_{t}

\displaystyle={\bm{\theta}}_{t-1}+{\bm{u}}_{t},

(50)

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}_{t}\,{\bm{\theta}}_{t}+{\bm{e}}_{t},

where
{\rm Var}({\bm{e}}_{t})=\mathbf{R}_{t}
is known and
\mathbf{H}_{t}
is the known projection matrix.
Both,
{\bm{e}}_{t}
and
{\bm{u}}_{t}
are zero-mean random vectors.

In the Kalman filter update equations, the so-called posterior mean and covariances are given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t-1}+\mathbf{K}_{t}\,({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})\,,

(51)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}

\displaystyle={\rm Var}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}),

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}
is given,

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t|t-1}=\mathbf{H}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t-1|t-1},

(52)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{t}

\displaystyle={\rm Cov}({\bm{\theta}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}){\rm Var}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t})^{-1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{S}_{t}^{-1},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}

\displaystyle={\bm{y}}_{t}-\mathbf{H}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t|t-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}+\mathbf{R}_{t},

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{t}

\displaystyle={\rm Var}({\bm{e}}_{t}).

The memory requirements of the posterior covariance is
O(D^{2})
, with
D
number of parameters.
This makes it unfeasible to store for moderately-sized neural networks.

Thus, to reduce the computational cost, we maintain the best rank
d
approximation of the covariance matrix by

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t}\approx\mathbf{W}_{t}^{\intercal}\,\mathbf{W}_{t},

(53)

where
\mathbf{W}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
is the best rank-
d
approximation to
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}
(in a Frobenius norm sense).
Furthermore, to maintain a numerically stable method, we work with the Cholesky of the innovation variance.
In effect, this is a variant square-root low-rank Kalman filter method.
We explain the details of this method below.

The predict step equations are given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t|t-1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t-1},

(54)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}_{t-1}^{\intercal}\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+q_{t}\mathbf{I}.

(55)

Proposition E.1

(Kalman gain and innovations)
.

The variance
\mathbf{S}_{t}
of the innovation is

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}+\mathbf{R}_{t}

(56)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}_{t}\,(\mathbf{C}_{t-1}^{\intercal}\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+q_{t}\mathbf{I})\,\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}+\mathbf{R}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}_{t}\,\mathbf{C}_{t}^{\intercal}\,\mathbf{C}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}+q_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}+\mathbf{R}_{t}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}_{t}\,\mathbf{C}_{t}^{\intercal}&\sqrt{q_{t}}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}&\mathbf{R}_{t}^{\intercal/2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\\
\sqrt{q_{t}}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{R}_{t}^{1/2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}_{t}^{\intercal/2}\mathbf{S}_{t}^{1/2}\,,

with
\mathbf{S}_{t}^{1/2}
given by

\mathbf{S}_{t}={\cal Q}_{R}(\mathbf{C}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal},\,\sqrt{q_{t}}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\,,\,\mathbf{R}_{t}^{1/2}),

(57)

where
{\cal Q}_{R}
returns the
R
matrix from the QR decomposition row-stacked arguments and
\mathbf{R}_{t}^{1/2}
is the upper-triangular Cholesky decomposition of
\mathbf{R}_{t}
.
The Kalman gain is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\,\mathbf{S}_{t}^{-1}

(58)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{S}_{t}^{-1}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\right)^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{S}_{t}^{-1}\,\mathbf{S}_{t}^{-\intercal}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\,\left(\mathbf{C}_{t-1}^{\intercal}\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+q_{t}\,\mathbf{I}_{{D_{\bm{\theta}}}}\right)\right)^{\intercal}.

Proposition E.2

(Update step)
.

The updated mean is

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{K}_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}).

(59)

The update covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t}
,
approximated through a two-step procedure:
first, a surrogate covariance that ignores the cross-term effect of
\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}
on the artificial noise covariance
q_{t}
, and
second, a best rank-
d
approximation (in Frobenious norm) to the surrogate matrix
takes the low-rank form

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t|t}=\mathbf{C}_{t}^{\intercal}\,\mathbf{C}_{t},

(60)

with

\mathbf{C}_{t}={\cal P}_{d}\left(\mathbf{C}_{t-1}\,\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)^{\intercal},\,\mathbf{R}_{t}^{1/2}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)\in{\mathcal{M}_{d\times{D_{\bm{\theta}}}}(\mathbb{R})},

(61)

the low-rank (rectangular decomposition) matrix.

Proof.

The updated covariance mean follows directly from (
13
).

The updated covariance is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\,\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)^{\intercal}+\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{R}_{t}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}

(62)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)\,\left(\mathbf{C}_{t-1}^{\intercal}\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+q_{t}\,\mathbf{I}_{D_{\bm{\theta}}}\right)\,\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)^{\intercal}+\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{R}_{t}^{\intercal/2}\,\mathbf{R}_{t}^{1/2}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)\,\left(\mathbf{C}_{t-1}^{\intercal}\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+q_{t}\,\mathbf{I}_{D_{\bm{\theta}}}\right)\,\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)^{\intercal}+\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{R}_{t}^{\intercal/2}\,\mathbf{R}_{t}^{1/2}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1}^{\intercal}&\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{R}_{t}^{\intercal/2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}_{t-1}\,\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{R}_{t}^{1/2}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}.\end{bmatrix}+q_{t}\,\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)^{\intercal}.

Next, we consider the
surrogate
covariance

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1}^{\intercal}&\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{R}_{t}^{\intercal/2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}_{t-1}\,\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{R}_{t}^{1/2}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}.\end{bmatrix}+q_{t}\mathbf{I}_{D_{\bm{\theta}}}

(63)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{t}^{\intercal}\,\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{t}+q_{t}\mathbf{I}_{D_{\bm{\theta}}},

where

\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}_{t-1}\,\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{R}_{t}^{1/2}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+o)\times D}.

(64)

Next, the best rank-
d
matrix is

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:{\rm rank}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=d}\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t|t}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}

(65)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:{\rm rank}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=d}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{t}^{\intercal}\,\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{t}+q_{t}\,\mathbf{I}_{D_{\bm{\theta}}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}

where

(66)

is the best
d

\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{t}
and the dynamics covariance
q_{t}

(67)

where

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t}

Proof.

We seek to bound

(68)

where

(69)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t|t}
is the surrogate covariance matrix (

Then,

\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t|t}\|_{\rm F}\leq\|\underbrace{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t|t}}_{=\mathbf{E}_{\rm surr}}\|_{\rm F}+\|\underbrace{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t|t}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t|t}}_{=\mathbf{E}_{\rm proj}}\|_{\rm F}

The norm for

Let

and

be the SVD decomposition of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t|t}

Here,

\mathbf{S}={\rm diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{D_{\bm{\theta}}})

and

\mathbf{S}_{:d}={\rm diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d},0,\ldots,0)

are the singular values (eigenvalues) of the matrices
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t|t}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t|t}
respectively,
ordered in descending order.
Then,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{E}_{\rm proj}\|_{\rm F}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{V}^{\intercal}-\mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{S}_{:d}\,\mathbf{V}^{\intercal}\|_{\rm F}

(71)

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{U}\left(\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}_{:d}\right)\mathbf{V}^{\intercal}\|_{\rm F}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{{\rm Tr}[\left(\mathbf{U}\left(\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}_{:d}\right)\mathbf{V}^{\intercal}\right)\left(\mathbf{U}\left(\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}_{:d}\right)\mathbf{V}^{\intercal}\right)^{\intercal}]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{{\rm Tr}[\mathbf{U}\left(\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}_{:d}\right)\mathbf{V}^{\intercal}\mathbf{V}\left(\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}_{:d}\right)\mathbf{U}^{\intercal}]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{{\rm Tr}[\left(\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}_{:d}\right)\mathbf{V}^{\intercal}\mathbf{V}\left(\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}_{:d}\right)\mathbf{U}^{\intercal}\mathbf{U}]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{{\rm Tr}[\left(\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}_{:d}\right)^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{k=d+1}^{D_{\bm{\theta}}}\lambda_{k}^{2}},

where
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=d+1}^{D_{\bm{\theta}}}
are the bottom
({D_{\bm{\theta}}}-d)
eigenvalues of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t|t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t|t}\|_{\rm F}

(72)

\displaystyle=\|q_{t}\,(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t})\,(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t})-q_{t}\,\mathbf{I}\|_{\rm F}

\displaystyle=q_{t}\,\|(\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t})\,(\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t})^{\intercal}-(\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t})-(\mathbf{K}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t})^{\intercal}\|_{\rm F}
