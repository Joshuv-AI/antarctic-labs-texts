Novel Adaptive Schemes for Hyperbolic Conservation Laws
Shaoshuai Chu, Pingyao Feng, Vadim A. Kolotilov, Alexander Kurganov, and Vladimir V. Ostapenko

Abstract
We introduce new adaptive schemes for the one- and two-dimensional hyperbolic systems of conservation laws. Our schemes are based on an adaption strategy recently introduced in [ S. Chu, A. Kurganov, and I. Menshov , Appl. Numer. Math., 209 (2025)]. As there, we use a smoothness indicator (SI) to automatically detect “rough” parts of the solution and employ in those areas the second-order finite-volume low-dissipation central-upwind scheme with an overcompressive limiter, which helps to sharply resolve nonlinear shock waves and linearly degenerate contact discontinuities. In smooth parts, we replace the limited second-order scheme with a quasi-linear fifth-order (in space and third-order in time) finite-difference scheme, recently proposed in [ V. A. Kolotilov, V. V. Ostapenko, and N. A. Khandeeva , Comput. Math. Math. Phys., 65 (2025)]. However, direct application of this scheme may generate spurious oscillations near “rough” parts, while excessive use of the overcompressive limiter may cause staircase-like nonphysical structures in smooth areas. To address these issues, we employ the same SI to distinguish contact discontinuities, treated with the overcompressive limiter, from other “rough” regions, where we switch to the dissipative Minmod2 limiter. Advantage of the resulting adaptive schemes are clearly demonstrated on a number of challenging numerical examples.



Key words:
Adaptive schemes; low-dissipation central-upwind numerical fluxes; smoothness indicator; overcompressive and dissipative
limiters; quasi-linear fifth-order finite-difference scheme; Euler equations of gas dynamics.

AMS subject classification:
65M08, 65M06, 76M12, 76M20, 76L05, 35L65.

1
Introduction

We consider hyperbolic systems of conservation laws, which in the one-dimensional (1-D) and two-dimensional (2-D) cases, read as

\bm{U}_{t}+\bm{F}(\bm{U})_{x}=\bm{0},

(1.1)

and

\bm{U}_{t}+\bm{F}(\bm{U})_{x}+\bm{G}(\bm{U})_{y}=\bm{0},

(1.2)

respectively. Here,
x
and
y
are spatial variables,
t
is the time,
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a vector of unknown functions, and

\bm{F}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bm{G}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are fluxes.

It is well-known that solutions of (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) can produce extremely complex wave structures including shocks, rarefactions,
and contact discontinuities even when the initial data are smooth. This makes it quite challenging to develop accurate and reliable
numerical methods for (
1.1
) and (
1.2
). Such methods are typically based on equivalent integral forms of (
1.1
) and (
1.2
)
for the time evolution of the computed solution and on nonlinear limiters, designed to prevent spurious oscillations. For a variety of
existing numerical methods for hyperbolic systems of conservation laws, we refer the reader to the monographs and review papers

[
17
,
42
,
27
,
21
,
2
,
39
]
and references therein.

In this paper, we focus on scheme adaption methods, which are based on automatic detection of smooth and nonsmooth (“rough”) parts of the
computed solutions and on applying different numerical methods in the corresponding parts of the computational domain. The main advantage of
such an approach is related to the fact that special nonlinear limiting techniques can be used to accurately capture shock waves and contact
discontinuities only, while smooth parts of the solution may be accurately and efficiently computed by much simpler, possibly higher-order,
(quasi-linear) unlimited methods.

A success of adaptive schemes hinges on an accuracy and robustness of the smoothness indicator (SI) used to detect “rough” parts of the
computed solution. A wide variety of different SIs has been developed; see, e.g.,

[
12
,
11
,
1
,
14
,
16
,
15
,
20
,
31
,
33
,
43
,
13
,
44
,
22
,
30
,
45
,
6
]
. Another critical component
of a successful adaptive scheme is a very sharp method used to capture shock and contact discontinuities inside the identified “rough”
parts of the solution. In this paper, we follow
[
8
]
and implement the SI proposed in
[
30
]
along with the second-order
semi-discrete finite-volume low-dissipation central-upwind (LDCU) schemes from
[
10
]
combined with the overcompressive and
dissipative limiters introduced in
[
28
]
.

The novelty of the proposed adaptive schemes is twofold. First, we treat the smooth parts of the computed solutions in a different way.
Instead of using the second-order LDCU scheme with the dissipative Minmod2 limiter (see
[
28
]
for the classification of different
limiters) as it was done in
[
8
]
, we increase the resolution by switching to the recently proposed quasi-linear fifth-order (in
space and third-order in time) finite-difference scheme recently introduced in
[
24
]
. Following
[
26
]
, we call a numerical
scheme
quasi-linear
if it becomes linear when applied to linear hyperbolic systems. As it was shown in
[
24
]
, the fifth-order
quasi-linear scheme is very accurate and like the third-order Rusanov-Burstein-Mirin scheme
[
4
,
34
]
, it achieves
increased accuracy in shock influence regions. This scheme, however, does not contain any nonlinear stabilization mechanism and thus cannot
be used in the “rough” areas. Moreover, as we show in §
2.3
, it can produce spurious oscillations near the interfaces between the
“rough” and “smooth” regions. To suppress these oscillations, one can “enlarge” the identified “rough” areas, but in this case, the
overcompressive limiter would affect the smooth parts of the solution causing development of artificial kinks and staircase-like structures
or even nonphysical jump discontinuities there.

In order to address these issues, we introduce an alternative approach, which is the second novel component of the proposed adaptive
schemes. To this end, we use the same SI, but applied to different fields to automatically distinguish between the contact waves and other
“rough” parts of the solution. For example, in the case of Euler equations of gas dynamics, we take advantage of the fact that while the
density jumps at the contact surfaces, the pressure remains continuous there. After identifying the locations of contact surfaces, we apply
the second-order LDCU scheme with an overcompressive limiter in their vicinities only, while utilizing the dissipative Minmod2 limiter in
the rest of the “rough” areas. In addition, the overcompressive and Minmod2 limiters are applied to the local characteristic variables
using the local characteristic decomposition (LCD), which is often used in the context of high-order schemes
[
32
]
to suppress
spurious oscillations, but can also be implemented to enhance the resolution of second-order schemes; see, e.g.,
[
9
,
7
]
. This
way, we construct efficient and robust adaptive schemes, which achieve a very high resolution of both jump discontinuities and smooth parts
of the computed solutions.

The paper is organized as follows. In §
2
, we review the 1-D LDCU scheme from
[
10
]
, the fifth-order finite-difference
scheme from
[
24
]
, and then introduce a 1-D adaptive scheme based on the scheme adaption strategy from
[
8
]
. We also
demonstrate that the obtained adaptive scheme is not robust and thus it has to be modified. In §
3
, we present a new 1-D adaptive
scheme, which is based on the dynamical splitting of the computational domains into three parts: vicinities of the contact discontinuities,
other “rough” parts of the computed solutions, and smooth parts of the computed solutions. In §
4
, we extend the new adaptive
scheme to the 2-D case. In §
5
, we test the developed schemes on a number of 1-D and 2-D numerical examples for the Euler equations
of gas dynamics. The obtained numerical results demonstrate that the adaptive schemes contain a substantially smaller amount of numerical
dissipation and achieve higher resolution compared with the adaptive schemes from
[
8
]
. Finally, in §
6
, we provide
concluding remarks.

2
1-D Scheme Adaption Algorithm

In this section, we consider the 1-D hyperbolic system (
1.1
) and describe a scheme adaption algorithm similar to the one recently
introduced in
[
8
]
. The novel feature here is in the treatment of smooth parts of the computed areas detected by the SI; see
§
2.2
and §
2.3
.

2.1
1-D Low-Dissipation Central-Upwind (LDCU) Schemes

In this section, we briefly review the second-order semi-discrete LDCU schemes recently introduced in
[
10
]
.

Assuming that the computational domain is covered with the uniform cells
I_{j}:=\big[x_{j-\frac{1}{2}},x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big]
with
x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-x_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\equiv\Delta x

centered at
x_{j}=\big(x_{j-\frac{1}{2}}+x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big)/2
, we suppose that the computed cell averages,

\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j}(t):\approx\frac{1}{\Delta x}\int\limits_{I_{j}}\bm{U}(x,t)\,{\rm d}x,

are available at a certain time level
t\geq 0
. Note that
\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j}(t)
like many other indexed quantities, which will be introduced
below, are time-dependent, but from here on, we suppress their time-dependence for the sake of brevity.

The computed cell averages are evolved in time by numerically solving the following system of ODEs:

\frac{{\rm d}\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j}}{{\rm d}t}=-\frac{\bm{{\cal F}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}[\bm{U}]-\bm{{\cal F}}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}[\bm{U}]}{\Delta x},

(2.1)

where
\bm{{\cal F}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}[\bm{U}]
are the LDCU numerical fluxes given by (see
[
10
]
)

\bm{{\cal F}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}[\bm{U}]=\frac{{a_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+}}\bm{F}\big(\bm{U}^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big)-{a_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{-}}\bm{F}\big(\bm{U}^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big)}{{a_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+}}-{a_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{-}}}+\frac{{a_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+}}{a_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{-}}}{{a_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+}}-{a_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{-}}}\Big(\bm{U}^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-\bm{U}^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)+\bm{q}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}.

Here:

\bullet

\bm{U}^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
are the left- and right-sided point values of
\bm{U}
at the cell interface
x=x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
reconstructed out of the given
set of cell averages
\{\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j}\}
using a proper nonlinear limiter; see Appendix
A
;

\bullet

a^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
are the one-sided local speeds of propagation, which can be estimated using the largest and smallest eigenvalues of
the Jacobian
A(\bm{U}):=\frac{\partial\bm{F}}{\partial\bm{U}}(\bm{U})
;

\bullet

\bm{q}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
is a “built-in” anti-diffusion term, which needs to be derived for a particular system at hand.

In this paper, we consider the Euler equations of gas dynamics, which, in the 1-D case, read as (
1.1
) with

\bm{U}=\big(\rho,\rho u,E\big)^{\top}\quad{\rm and}\quad\bm{F}=\big(\rho u,\rho u^{2}+p,u(E+p)\big)^{\top}.

(2.2)

Here,
\rho
,
u
,
p
, and
E
are the density, velocity, pressure, and total energy, respectively, and the system is closed through the
following equations of state (EOS) for ideal gases:

p=(\gamma-1)\Big[E-{\frac{1}{2}}\rho u^{2}\Big],

(2.3)

where the parameter
\gamma
represents the specific heat ratio. For the Euler system (
1.1
), (
2.2
)–(
2.3
), the
one-sided local speeds of propagation can be estimated by

a^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\max\Big\{u^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}+c^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}},u^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}+c^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}},0\Big\},\quad a^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\min\Big\{u^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-c^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}},u^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-c^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}},0\Big\},

where

u^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{(\rho u)_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{\pm}}{\rho^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}},\quad p^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=(\gamma-1)\Big[E^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-{\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big(u^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big)^{2}\Big],\quad c^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma p^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}{\rho^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}},

and the anti-diffusion term
\bm{q}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
is given by (see
[
10
]
)

\bm{q}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\alpha^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}q^{\rho}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\left(1,u^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}},{\frac{1}{2}}\big(u^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big)^{2}\right)^{\top}.

Here,

q^{\rho}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}={\rm minmod}\Big(\big(u^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-a^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big)\big(\rho^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-\rho^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big),\big(a^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-u^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big)\big(\rho^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-\rho^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big)\Big),

u^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=(\rho u)^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}/\rho^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
with
\rho^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
and
(\rho u)^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
being the first and second components of

\bm{U}^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{a^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\bm{U}^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-a^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\bm{U}^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-\Big[\bm{F}\big(\bm{U}^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big)-\bm{F}\big(\bm{U}^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big)\Big]}{a^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-a^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}},

\alpha^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{a^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}{a^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-u^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}&&\mbox{if}~u^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}<0,\\
&\frac{a^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}{a^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-u^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}&&\mbox{otherwise},\end{aligned}\right.

and the minmod function is defined by
{\rm minmod}(a,b):=\frac{{\rm sgn}(a)+{\rm sgn}(b)}{2}\min(|a|,|b|)
.

Remark 2.1

The ODE system (
2.1
) has to be numerically integrated using a sufficiently accurate and stable ODE solver. In the numerical examples
reported in this paper, we have used the three-stage third-order strong stability preserving (SSP) Runge-Kutta method, which reads as (see

[
19
,
18
]
)

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{\rm I}_{j}=\bm{U}_{j}(t)-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\bm{{\cal F}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}[\bm{U}(t)]-\bm{{\cal F}}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}[\bm{U}(t)]\right),

(2.4)

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{\rm II}_{j}=\frac{3}{4}\,\bm{U}_{j}(t)+\frac{1}{4}\left[\bm{U}^{\rm I}_{j}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\bm{{\cal F}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm I}\big]-\bm{{\cal F}}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm I}\big]\right)\right],

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{j}(t+\Delta t)=\frac{1}{3}\,\bm{U}_{j}(t)+\frac{2}{3}\left[\bm{U}^{\rm II}_{j}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\bm{{\cal F}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm II}\big]-\bm{{\cal F}}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm II}\big]\right)\right].

In (
2.4
), the time step
\Delta t
is selected adaptively based on the following CFL-based stability restriction:

\Delta t\leq\frac{\Delta x}{2a},\quad a:=\max_{j}\Big\{\max\big(a^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}},-a^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big)\Big\}.

Notice that in (
2.4
) we have replaced the cell averages of
\bm{U}
with its point values (
\bm{U}_{j}(t):\approx\bm{U}(x_{j},t^{n})
). This
does not affect the accuracy of the second-order scheme as the difference between the cell averages and point values is proportional to

{\cal O}((\Delta x)^{2})
for smooth solutions. On the other hand, switching to the finite-difference evolution is advantageous as the LDCU scheme
is going to be combined with the quasi-linear fifth-order finite-difference scheme from
[
24
]
, for which the point values of the
solution are to be evolved in time.

2.2
1-D Quasi-Linear Fifth-Order Scheme

In this section, we describe the quasi-linear fifth-order finite-difference scheme recently introduced in
[
24
]
. As this scheme is
going to be implemented in our adaption algorithm, we write it in the fully discrete flux form (
2.4
).

Assuming the point values
\bm{U}_{j}
are available at a certain time level
t
, the solution is evolved to the next time level
t+\Delta t

according to (
2.4
) with the following numerical fluxes:

\bm{{\cal F}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}[\bm{U}(t)]=\bm{{\cal L}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}[\bm{U}(t)],\quad\bm{{\cal F}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm I}\big]=\bm{{\cal L}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm I}\big],\quad\bm{{\cal F}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm II}\big]=\bm{{\cal L}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm II}\big]-\bm{\omega}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}(t),

where

\displaystyle\bm{{\cal L}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}[\bm{U}]:=\frac{1}{60}\big[\bm{F}(\bm{U}_{j+3})-8\bm{F}(\bm{U}_{j+2})+37\bm{F}(\bm{U}_{j+1})+37\bm{F}(\bm{U}_{j})-8\bm{F}(\bm{U}_{j-1})+\bm{F}(\bm{U}_{j-2})\big],

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}(t):=\frac{3\Delta x}{128\Delta t}\big[\bm{U}_{j+3}(t)-5\bm{U}_{j+2}(t)+10\bm{U}_{j+1}(t)-10\bm{U}_{j}(t)+5\bm{U}_{j-1}(t)-\bm{U}_{j-2}(t)\big].

In the next numerical example, we demonstrate that the described 1-D quasi-linear scheme achieves the fifth order of accuracy in capturing a
smooth solution provided
\Delta t\sim(\Delta x)^{\frac{5}{3}}
.

Example.

We consider the following smooth initial data
[
23
]
:

[0,10]
. We compute the numerical solution until the final time

t=0.1
on a sequence of uniform meshes with
\Delta x=1/10
,
1/20
,

,

2.3
1-D Adaptive Scheme

In this section, we turn to the description of the adaptive scheme from
[
8
]
, in which we modify the treatment of the smooth part
of the computed solution: we will evolve it using the quasi-linear fifth-order scheme from
[
24
]
instead of the LDCU scheme with the
dissipative Minmod2 limiter. The “rough” parts of the computed solution will be still integrated using the LDCU scheme with the
overcompressive (
\tau=-0.25
) limiter described in Appendix
A
.

In order to implement this scheme adaption strategy, we use the SI proposed in
[
30
]
(see also
[
8
, §2.2]
), which is based
on the quantities

{\cal E}_{j}^{\rho}=\frac{|\rho_{j+1}-2\rho_{j}+\rho_{j-1}|}{|\rho_{j+1}-\rho_{j}|+|\rho_{j}-\rho_{j-1}|+\varepsilon\big(\rho_{j+1}+2\rho_{j}+\rho_{j-1}\big)},

(2.5)

which we smooth out in the following way:

\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\cal E$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j}^{\,\rho}=\frac{1}{6}\big[{\cal E}_{j+1}^{\rho}+4{\cal E}_{j}^{\rho}+{\cal E}_{j-1}^{\rho}\big].

(2.6)

The term with
\varepsilon
in (
2.5
) plays the role of a “noise” filter, which is added in order not to refine “wiggles” or “ripples”
that may appear due to loss of monotonicity; see
[
30
]
. The value of
\varepsilon
depends on the problem at hand and in all of the numerical
examples presented in this paper, we use the same value
\varepsilon=0.2
as in
[
30
,
8
]
.

As in
[
8
]
, we use the following simple way to detect “rough” parts of the computed solutions: If

\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\cal E$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j}^{\,\rho}\geq\texttt{C}_{1}
, the cell
I_{j}
is marked as “rough”. Here,
\texttt{C}_{1}
is a positive tunable constant to
be selected for each problem at hand. The key component in making the adaptive scheme robust is the ability of finding a reliable range of
constant
\texttt{C}_{1}
values. As shown in
[
8
]
, the value of
\texttt{C}_{1}
can be tuned on a coarse mesh and then the same
values can be used for finer mesh simulations.

We have tested the resulting adaptive scheme on a number of numerical examples and obtained fairly accurate and non-oscillatory results in
some of them. However, there were some examples, in which this adaptive scheme was unable to produce satisfactory results. We now show one
of such examples.

Example—Shock-Density Wave Interaction Problem.

In this example taken from
[
41
]
, we consider the shock-density wave
interaction problem. The initial data,

(\rho,u,p)\Big|_{(x,0)}=\begin{cases}\bigg(\dfrac{27}{7},\dfrac{4\sqrt{35}}{9},\dfrac{31}{3}\bigg),&x<-4,\\[3.44444pt]
(1+0.2\sin(5x),0,1),&x>-4,\end{cases}

are prescribed in the computational domain
[-5,15]
subject to the free boundary conditions.

We first compute the numerical solutions on a uniform mesh with
\Delta x=1/40
until the final time
t=5
by the adaptive scheme from

[
8
]
, which will be referred to as the OLD scheme, and the adaptive scheme introduced in this section (this scheme will be
referred to as the MODIFIED scheme). For both schemes, we use the adaption constant
\texttt{C}_{1}=0.004
. We present the obtained densities
in Figure
2.1
together with the reference solution computed by the LDCU scheme (with
\tau=0.5
in (
A.3
)) on a much finer mesh
with
\Delta x=1/800
. One can see that compared with the OLD scheme, the MODIFIED one produces a more accurate result; see Figure
2.1

(right).

Density
\rho
computed with
\Delta x=1/40

x\in[9,10.2]
(right).

We then refine the mesh to
\Delta x=1/200
and compute the numerical solutions until the same final time
t=5
by both the OLD and MODIFIED
schemes. The obtained densities are presented in Figure

, where one can clearly see that there are “stair-like” structures in

(right). The appearance of these “stair-like” structures
suggests that the value
\texttt{C}_{1}=0.004
is too small and hence the identified “rough” areas, where the overcompressive limiter is
applied, are too wide. We therefore take a larger value
\texttt{C}_{1}=0.1
and repeat the same computations on the same two meshes. For this
value of
\texttt{C}_{1}
, no “stair-like” structures are formed even when the mesh is refined. However, the obtained densities, plotted in

, contain oscillations in the coarse mesh solution (see the top right panel in Figure

3
Novel 1-D Adaptive Schemes

In this section, we propose novel 1-D adaptive schemes, which achieve higher resolution than the adaptive schemes from
[
8
]
and at
the same time do not suffer from the occurrence of either “stair-like” structures or large spurious oscillations. To this end, we first
compute the pressure-based SI, which is calculated using the following quantities, which are similar to (
2.5
)–(
2.6
):

{\cal E}_{j}^{p}=\frac{|p_{j+1}-2p_{j}+p_{j-1}|}{|p_{j+1}-p_{j}|+|p_{j}-p_{j-1}|+\varepsilon\big(p_{j+1}+2p_{j}+p_{j-1}\big)},\quad\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\cal E$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j}^{\,p}=\frac{1}{6}\big[{\cal E}_{j+1}^{p}+4{\cal E}_{j}^{p}+{\cal E}_{j-1}^{p}\big].

We then take advantage of the fact at the contact discontinuities
\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\cal E$\kern 0.0pt}}}^{\,\rho}_{j}
is large, but
\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\cal E$\kern 0.0pt}}}^{\,p}_{j}
is
small and design the following strategy to distinguish the neighborhoods of contact discontinuities from the other parts of “rough” areas:

\bullet
Select a tunable constant
0<\texttt{C}_{1}<1
and mark the cells
I_{j}
, in which
\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\cal E$\kern 0.0pt}}}^{\,\rho}_{j}>\texttt{C}_{1}
, as
“rough” cells;

\bullet
Select a constant
0<\texttt{C}_{2}<1
and mark those “rough” cells
I_{j}
, in which
\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\cal E$\kern 0.0pt}}}^{\,p}_{j}<\texttt{C}_{2}
, as
“contact” cells.

After identifying three different parts of the computed solution, we design a novel scheme adaption approach as follows:

Area A
(neighborhoods of the contact discontinuities): Use the second-order LDCU scheme (§
2.1
) with the overcompressive
SBM limiter (Appendix
A
) with
\tau=-0.25
in (
A.3
);

Area B
(the rest of the “rough” parts of the computed solution): Use the second-order LDCU scheme (§
2.1
) with the
Minmod2 limiter, which is, in fact, a dissipative SBM limiter (Appendix
A
) with
\tau=0.5
in (
A.3
);

Area C
(smooth parts of the computed solution): Use the quasi-linear fifth-order scheme (§
2.2
).

4.1
2-D Low-Dissipation Central-Upwind (LDCU) Schemes

In this section, we briefly review the second-order semi-discrete 2-D LDCU schemes introduced in
[
10
]
.

We assume that the computational domain is covered with uniform cells
I_{j,k}:=\big[x_{j-\frac{1}{2}},x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big]\times\big[y_{k-\frac{1}{2}},y_{k+\frac{1}{2}}\big]
with

x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-x_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\equiv\Delta x
and
y_{k+\frac{1}{2}}-y_{k-\frac{1}{2}}\equiv\Delta y
centered at
(x_{j},y_{k})
with
x_{j}=\big(x_{j-\frac{1}{2}}+x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big)/2
and

y_{k}=\big(y_{k-\frac{1}{2}}+y_{k+\frac{1}{2}}\big)/2
, and that the cell averages

\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k}:\approx\frac{1}{\Delta x\Delta y}\iint\limits_{I_{j,k}}\bm{U}(x,y,t)\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y

are available at a certain time
t\geq 0
.

According to
[
10
]
,
\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k}
are evolved in time by numerically solving the following system of ODEs:

\frac{{\rm d}\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k}}{{\rm d}t}=-\frac{\bm{\mathcal{F}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}[\bm{U}]-\bm{\mathcal{F}}_{{j-\frac{1}{2}},k}[\bm{U}]}{\Delta x}-\frac{\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}[\bm{U}]-\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{j,{k-\frac{1}{2}}}[\bm{U}]}{\Delta y},

(4.1)

where
\bm{{\cal F}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}[\bm{U}]
and
\bm{{\cal G}}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}[\bm{U}]
are the
x
- and
y
-directional LDCU numerical fluxes given by

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{F}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}[\bm{U}]

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{+}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\bm{F}\big(\bm{U}^{-}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\big)-a^{-}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\bm{F}\big(\bm{U}^{+}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\big)}{a^{+}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}-a^{-}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}}+\frac{a^{+}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}a^{-}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}}{a^{+}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}-a^{-}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}}\Big(\bm{U}^{+}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}-\bm{U}^{-}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\Big)+\bm{q}^{x}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}[\bm{U}]

\displaystyle=\frac{b^{+}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\bm{G}\big(\bm{U}^{-}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\big)-b^{-}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\bm{G}\big(\bm{U}^{+}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\big)}{b^{+}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}-b^{-}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}}+\frac{b^{+}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}b^{-}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}}{b^{+}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}-b^{-}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}}\Big(\bm{U}^{-}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}-\bm{U}^{+}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\Big)+\bm{q}^{y}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}.

Here:

\bullet

\bm{U}^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}
and
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}
are the one-sided point values of
\bm{U}
at the midpoints of the cell interfaces

(x_{j+\frac{1}{2}},y_{k})
and
(x_{j},y_{k+\frac{1}{2}})
, respectively. As in the 1-D case, these one-sided point values are reconstructed out of the given set of
cell averages
\{\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k}\}
using a proper nonlinear limiter; see Appendix
B
;

\bullet

a^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}
and
b^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}
are the one-sided local speeds of propagation in the
x
- and
y
-directions, respectively.
They can be estimated using the largest and smallest eigenvalues of the corresponding Jacobians

A(\bm{U}):=\frac{\partial\bm{F}}{\partial\bm{U}}(\bm{U})
and
B(\bm{U}):=\frac{\partial\bm{G}}{\partial\bm{U}}(\bm{U})
;

\bullet

\bm{q}^{x}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}
and
\bm{q}^{y}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}
are “built-in” anti-diffusion terms, which need to be derived for a particular system
(
1.2
) at hand.

As in the 1-D case, we focus on the Euler equations of gas dynamics, whose 2-D version reads as (
1.2
) with

\bm{U}=\big(\rho,\rho u,\rho v,E\big)^{\top},\quad\bm{F}=\big(\rho u,\rho u^{2}+p,\rho uv,u(E+p)\big)^{\top},\quad\bm{G}=\big(\rho v,\rho uv,\rho v^{2}+p,v(E+p)\big)^{\top}.

(4.2)

Here,
v
is the
y
-velocity and the other variables are the same as in the 1-D case. The system (
1.2
), (
4.2
) is closed through
the following EOS for ideal gases:

p=(\gamma-1)\Big[E-\frac{\rho}{2}(u^{2}+v^{2})\Big].

(4.3)

For the Euler system (
1.1
), (
4.2
)–(
4.3
), the one-sided local speeds of propagation can be estimated by

\displaystyle a^{+}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}=\max\Big\{u^{+}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}+c^{+}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k},u^{-}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}+c^{-}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k},0\Big\},~\,a^{-}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}=\min\Big\{u^{+}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}-c^{+}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k},u^{-}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}-c^{-}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k},0\Big\},

\displaystyle b^{+}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}=\max\Big\{v^{+}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}+c^{+}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}},v^{-}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}+c^{-}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}},0\Big\},\quad b^{-}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}=\min\Big\{v^{+}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}-c^{+}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}},v^{-}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}-c^{-}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}},0\Big\},

where

\displaystyle u^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}=\frac{(\rho u)_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{\pm}}{\rho^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}},\quad u^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}=\frac{(\rho u)_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}^{\pm}}{\rho^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}},\quad v^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}=\frac{(\rho v)_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{\pm}}{\rho^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}},\quad v^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}=\frac{(\rho v)_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}^{\pm}}{\rho^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}},

\displaystyle p^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}=(\gamma-1)\Big[E^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}-{\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\Big(\big(u^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\big)^{2}+\big(v^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\big)^{2}\Big)\Big],\quad c^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma p^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}}{\rho^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}}},

\displaystyle p^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}=(\gamma-1)\Big[E^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}-{\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\Big(\big(u^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\big)^{2}+\big(v^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\big)^{2}\Big)\Big],\quad c^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma p^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}}{\rho^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}}}.

Finally, a description of the anti-diffusion terms
\bm{q}^{x}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}
and
\bm{q}^{y}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}
can be found in
[
10
]
, and here, we
omit the details for the sake of brevity.

As in the 1-D case, we numerically integrate the ODE system (
4.1
) using the three-stage third-order SSP Runge-Kutta method:

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{\rm I}_{j,k}=\bm{U}_{j,k}(t)-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\bm{{\cal F}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}[\bm{U}(t)]-\bm{{\cal F}}_{{j-\frac{1}{2}},k}[\bm{U}(t)]\right)-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta y}\left(\bm{{\cal G}}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}[\bm{U}(t)]-\bm{{\cal G}}_{j,{k-\frac{1}{2}}}[\bm{U}(t)]\right),

(4.4)

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{\rm II}_{j,k}=\frac{3}{4}\,\bm{U}_{j,k}(t)+\frac{1}{4}\left[\bm{U}^{\rm I}_{j,k}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\bm{{\cal F}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm I}\big]-\bm{{\cal F}}_{{j-\frac{1}{2}},k}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm I}\big]\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta y}\left(\bm{{\cal G}}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm I}\big]-\bm{{\cal G}}_{j,{k-\frac{1}{2}}}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm I}\big]\right)\right],

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{j,k}(t+\Delta t)=\frac{1}{3}\,\bm{U}_{j,k}(t)+\frac{2}{3}\left[\bm{U}^{\rm II}_{j,k}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\bm{{\cal F}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm II}\big]-\bm{{\cal F}}_{{j-\frac{1}{2}},k}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm II}\big]\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta y}\left(\bm{{\cal G}}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm I}\big]-\bm{{\cal G}}_{j,{k-\frac{1}{2}}}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm I}\big]\right)\right].

In (
4.4
), the time step
\Delta t
is selected adaptively based on the following CFL-based stability restriction:

\Delta t\leq\min\left\{\frac{\Delta x}{2a},\frac{\Delta y}{2b}\right\},\quad a:=\max_{j,k}\Big\{\max\big(a^{+}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k},-a^{-}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\big)\Big\},\quad b:=\max_{j,k}\Big\{\max\big(b^{+}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}},-b^{-}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\big)\Big\}.

As in the 1-D case, we have replaced in (
4.4
) the cell averages of
\bm{U}
with its point values
(
\bm{U}_{j,k}(t):\approx\bm{U}(x_{j},y_{k},t^{n})
). This does not affect the accuracy of the second-order scheme as the difference between the
cell averages and point values is proportional to
{\cal O}((\Delta x)^{2}+{\cal O}((\Delta y)^{2})
for smooth solutions.

4.2
2-D Quasi-Linear Fifth-Order Scheme

In this section, we extend the 1-D quasi-linear fifth-order scheme described in §
2.2
to the 2-D case. The extension is performed in
a “dimension-by-dimension” manner.

Assuming the point values
\bm{U}_{j,k}
are available at a certain time level
t
, the solution is evolved to the next time level
t+\Delta t

according to (
4.4
) with the following numerical fluxes:

\displaystyle\bm{{\cal F}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}[\bm{U}(t)]=\bm{{\cal L}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{x}[\bm{U}(t)],

\displaystyle\bm{{\cal F}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm I}\big]=\bm{{\cal L}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{x}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm I}\big],

\displaystyle\bm{{\cal F}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm II}\big]=\bm{{\cal L}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{x}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm II}\big]-\bm{\omega}^{x}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}(t),

\displaystyle\bm{{\cal G}}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}[\bm{U}(t)]=\bm{{\cal L}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{y}[\bm{U}(t)],

\displaystyle\bm{{\cal G}}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm I}\big]=\bm{{\cal L}}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}^{y}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm I}\big],

\displaystyle\bm{{\cal G}}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm II}\big]=\bm{{\cal L}}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}^{y}\big[\bm{U}^{\rm II}\big]-\bm{\omega}^{y}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}(t),

where

\displaystyle\bm{{\cal L}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{x}[\bm{U}]:=\frac{1}{60}\big[\bm{F}(\bm{U}_{j+3,k})-8\bm{F}(\bm{U}_{j+2,k})+37\bm{F}(\bm{U}_{j+1,k})+37\bm{F}(\bm{U}_{j,k})-8\bm{F}(\bm{U}_{j-1,k})+\bm{F}(\bm{U}_{j-2,k})\big],

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{x}(t):=\frac{3\Delta x}{128\Delta t}\big[\bm{U}_{j+3,k}(t)-5\bm{U}_{j+2,k}(t)+10\bm{U}_{j+1,k}(t)-10\bm{U}_{j,k}(t)+5\bm{U}_{j-1,k}(t)-\bm{U}_{j-2,k}(t)\big],

\displaystyle\bm{{\cal L}}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}^{y}[\bm{U}]:=\frac{1}{60}\big[\bm{G}(\bm{U}_{j,k+3})-8\bm{G}(\bm{U}_{j,k+2})+37\bm{G}(\bm{U}_{j,k+1})+37\bm{G}(\bm{U}_{j,k})-8\bm{G}(\bm{U}_{j,k-1})+\bm{G}(\bm{U}_{j,k-2})\big],

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}^{y}(t):=\frac{3\Delta y}{128\Delta t}\big[\bm{U}_{j,k+3}(t)-5\bm{U}_{j,k+2}(t)+10\bm{U}_{j,k+1}(t)-10\bm{U}_{j,k}(t)+5\bm{U}_{j,k-1}(t)-\bm{U}_{j,k-2}(t)\big],

We now turn to the description of the proposed 2-D adaptive schemes.

5
Numerical Examples

In this section, we apply the developed adaptive schemes to both the 1-D and 2-D Euler equations of gas dynamics and compare the performance
the adaptive schemes from
[
8
]
(OLD schemes) and novel adaptive schemes, which will be referred to as NEW schemes. We take

\gamma=1.4
in Examples 1–7 and
\gamma=5/3
in Example 8. In all of the examples, we use the CFL number
0.4
.

All of the numerical experiments have been conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-9750H CPU at 2.6 GHz and 32 GB
of RAM. The simulations were conducted in FORTRAN using GCC version 14.2.0 compiler suite. The reported CPU times were averaged over thirty
independent runs to ensure reproducibility and minimize variability due to system processes.

6
Conclusion

We have introduced novel adaptive schemes for hyperbolic systems of conservation laws. The new schemes are based on a scheme adaption
strategy recently introduced in
[
8
]
. Like any adaptive scheme, the proposed one utilizes a smoothness indicator (SI) to
automatically detect “rough” and smooth parts of the computed solution at each time-step. We then use the same SI to identify vicinities
of contact discontinuities within the detected “rough” areas. In the case of Euler equations of gas dynamics, this is achieved by applying
the SI to the density field, which is discontinuous at contact waves, and to the pressure, which is continuous there.

After splitting the computational domain into the corresponding three areas—“contact”, “rough”, and “smooth”—we apply different
schemes inside each of them. In the “contact” areas, we use the low-dissipation central-upwind (LDCU) scheme with the overcompressive SBM
limiter, which helps to extremely sharply resolve contact discontinuities. In the rest of the “rough” areas, we use the LDCU scheme with
the dissipative Minmod2 limiter, which helps to both capture shock waves in a non-oscillatory manner and prevent appearance of artificial
staircase-like structures, which may be developed when the overcompressive limiter is used. Finally, in the “smooth” area, we use a
recently proposed very simple quasi-linear fifth-order finite-difference scheme, which helps to extremely accurately capture smooth parts of
the computed solution.

The new adaptive schemes have been tested on a number of one- and two-dimensional numerical examples, which clearly demonstrate the ability
of the proposed schemes to achieve very high resolution, while being robust. We also show that the new schemes clearly outperform the
adaptive schemes from
[
8
]
, while being substantially more efficient thanks to a very low computational cost of the quasi-linear
fifth-order schemes.

Appendix A
1-D LCD-Based Piecewise Linear Reconstruction

In this appendix, we briefly describe a family of SBM limiters introduced in
[
28
]
, which are applied to the local characteristic
variables to compute the one-sided point values
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
. To this end, we first introduce the matrices

\widehat{A}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=A\big(\widehat{\bm{U}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big)
, where
\widehat{\bm{U}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\big(\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j}+\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j+1}\big)/2
, and compute the
matrices
R_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
and
R^{-1}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
such that
R^{-1}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\widehat{A}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}R_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
are diagonal. We then introduce the local characteristic
variables
\bm{\Gamma}
in the neighborhood of
x=x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
:

\bm{\Gamma}_{k}=R^{-1}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{k},\quad k=j-1,j,j+1,j+2.

Equipped with the values
\bm{\Gamma}_{j-1}
,
\bm{\Gamma}_{j}
,
\bm{\Gamma}_{j+1}
, and
\bm{\Gamma}_{j+2}
, we compute the slopes

(\bm{\Gamma}_{x})_{j}=\phi^{\rm SBM}_{\theta,\tau}\left(\frac{\bm{\Gamma}_{j+1}-\bm{\Gamma}_{j}}{\bm{\Gamma}_{j}-\bm{\Gamma}_{j-1}}\right)\frac{\bm{\Gamma}_{j}-\bm{\Gamma}_{j-1}}{\Delta x}

(A.1)

and

(\bm{\Gamma}_{x})_{j+1}=\phi^{\rm SBM}_{\theta,\tau}\left(\frac{\bm{\Gamma}_{j+2}-\bm{\Gamma}_{j+1}}{\bm{\Gamma}_{j+1}-\bm{\Gamma}_{j}}\right)\frac{\bm{\Gamma}_{j+1}-\bm{\Gamma}_{j}}{\Delta x},

(A.2)

where the two-parameter SBM function

\phi^{\rm SBM}_{\theta,\tau}(r):=\begin{cases}0,&r<0,\\
\min\{r\theta,1+\tau(r-1)\},&0<r\leq 1,\\
r\phi^{\rm SBM}_{\theta,\tau}(\frac{1}{r}),&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(A.3)

is applied in the component-wise manner. Equipped with (
A.1
) and (
A.2
), we evaluate

\bm{\Gamma}^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\bm{\Gamma}_{j}+\frac{\Delta x}{2}(\bm{\Gamma}_{x})_{j}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\bm{\Gamma}^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\bm{\Gamma}_{j+1}-\frac{\Delta x}{2}(\bm{\Gamma}_{x})_{j+1},

and then obtain the corresponding point values of
\bm{U}
by
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=R_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\Gamma}^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
.

Remark A.1

The parameters
\theta\in[1,2]
and
\tau
in (
A.3
) can be used to adjust the amount of numerical dissipation present in the resulting
scheme. Generally, larger values of
\theta
lead to sharper but potentially more oscillatory reconstructions. In all of the numerical
examples presented in this paper, we have taken
\theta=2
.

According to
[
28
]
, the behavior of the SBM limiter depends on
\tau
in the following manner:

\bullet
When
\tau\geq 0.5
, the limiter is dissipative, which often leads to strong smearing of contact discontinuities over time;

\bullet
For
0\leq\tau<0.5
, the limiter is compressive, sharply resolving contact waves within few grid points; however, smooth
extrema may be slightly compressed, introducing small kinks in otherwise continuous profiles;

\bullet
If
\tau<0
, the limiter is overcompressive, preserving contact discontinuities sharply over long times but overcompressing
smooth regions, possibly producing artificial
{\cal O}(1)
jump discontinuities there.

Remark A.2

The matrices
R_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
and
R^{-1}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
in the case of the Euler equations of gas dynamics can be found in
[
5
, Appendix A]
.

Equipped with the values
\bm{\Gamma}_{j-1,k}
,
\bm{\Gamma}_{j,k}
,
\bm{\Gamma}_{j+1,k}

\bm{\Gamma}_{j+2,k}

(\bm{\Gamma}_{x})_{j,k}=\phi^{\rm SBM}_{\theta,\tau}\left(\frac{\bm{\Gamma}_{j+1,k}-\bm{\Gamma}_{j,k}}{\bm{\Gamma}_{j,k}-\bm{\Gamma}_{j-1,k}}\right)\frac{\bm{\Gamma}_{j,k}-\bm{\Gamma}_{j-1,k}}{\Delta x},

and

(\bm{\Gamma}_{x})_{j+1,k}=\phi^{\rm SBM}_{\theta,\tau}\left(\frac{\bm{\Gamma}_{j+2,k}-\bm{\Gamma}_{j+1,k}}{\bm{\Gamma}_{j+1,k}-\bm{\Gamma}_{j,k}}\right)\frac{\bm{\Gamma}_{j+1,k}-\bm{\Gamma}_{j,k}}{\Delta x},

where the SBM function, defined in (
A.3
), is applied in the component-wise manner. We then use these slopes to evaluate

\bm{\Gamma}^{-}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}=\bm{\Gamma}_{j,k}+\frac{\Delta x}{2}(\bm{\Gamma}_{x})_{j,k}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\bm{\Gamma}^{+}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}=\bm{\Gamma}_{j+1,k}-\frac{\Delta x}{2}(\bm{\Gamma}_{x})_{j+1,k},

and finally obtain the corresponding point values of
\bm{U}
by

\bm{U}^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=R_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\bm{\Gamma}^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}.

and

), (

)–(

) can be found

.
