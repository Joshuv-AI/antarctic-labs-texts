Loop anchor tension band wiring for olecranon fractures reduces Kirschner wire migration rate: a retrospective comparative study
Authors: Wei Ho, Chang-Hao Lin, Shu-Hsin Yao, Chun-Ho Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe loop anchor tension band wiring (TBW) technique is employed for olecranon fractures and aims to reduce the Kirschner wire (K-wire) migration rate. This study compared the postoperative outcomes and complication rates of olecranon fractures treated with Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen (AO)-modified TBW versus the loop anchor TBW. This study enrolled 83 olecranon fractures treated with AO-modified TBW (n = 62) or loop anchor TBW (n = 21). K-wire migration, implant removal, time to fracture union, operation time, and subjective functional outcomes including the Mayo Elbow Performance Score and Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand Score (Quick-DASH). Subgroup analysis by fracture classification (Mayo IIA and IIB) was performed. The follow-up duration was 10.2 ± 5.6 (6.0–43.6) months. The incidence of posterior migration of K-wires was 61.3% and 4.8% in the traditional and loop anchor TBW groups, respectively (P < .001). The operation time was 50.3 ± 6.5 and 36.2 ± 3.2 min in the traditional and loop anchor TBW groups, respectively (P < .001). The posterior elbow irritation rate was significantly lower in the loop anchor TBW group (23.8%) than in the traditional group (50.0%; P = .036). Other relevant parameters were not significantly different between the groups during the follow-up period. The loop anchor tension band technique results in a lower K-wire migration rate, a lower posterior elbow irritation rate, and shorter operation duration than does AO-modified TBW while achieving similar functional outcomes.Keywords: Olecranon fracture, Tension band wiring, Loop anchor tension band technique, Implant migrationSubject terms: Medical research, Outcomes research

Introduction

Olecranon fractures are common proximal ulnar fractures with an incidence of 12/100,000 per year1. Despite the favorable outcomes of locking plate osteosynthesis, tension band wiring (TBW) is a simple and widely used procedure for treating uncomplicated olecranon fractures classified as Mayo IIA or Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen (AO)/Orthopaedic Trauma Association 2U1B12.

The TBW technique that was originally introduced is the combination of 2 parallel intramedullary Kirschner wires (K-wires) with figure-of-eight metal wiring. Because of the frequent posterior K-wire migration problem—which can lead to skin irritation, secondary displacement, or malunion—the AO-modified TBW technique has been advocated; it decreases the K-wire migration rate through anterior ulnar transcortical fixation of the distal ends of the K-wire3. However, various complications such as neurovascular injury, restricted forearm range of motion, and radioulnar synostosis have been reported after anterior ulnar transcortical fixation4–6. To avoid such complications, some researchers have suggested the intramedullary K-wire placement of TBW7–9. Nevertheless, anterior ulnar transcortical fixation is challenging when the optimal entry point is compromised, as is the case for some fracture patterns (Fig. 1).

(B) Open reduction and internal fixation with an intramedullary placed TBW was conducted due to the compromised entry point. (C) Posterior K-wire migration with implant exposure through the skin was observed at the postoperative 2-week follow-up. (D) Revision with a loop anchor tension band wiring technique was performed, and union was achieved 3 months later.

The loop anchor TBW technique is employed for patella and olecranon fractures and offers lower K-wire migration rates than those achieved with other techniques10,11. It prevents K-wire migration even when the K-wires are placed in an intramedullary position in the ulnar canal. Simple tools are used to bend the proximal K-wires into a loop, and figure-of-eight metal wires are locked within the loop (Fig. 2). Accordingly, we hypothesized that the risk of K-wire migration following the loop anchor TBW technique would be superior to AO-modified TBW. This study compared the clinical and radiological outcomes between these two techniques.

(A) Schematic of the loop anchor TBW technique. (B) Clinical photo of the K-wire bent into a loop shape.

Materials and methods

This study was performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study design was approved by Ditmanson Medical Foundation Chia-Yi Christian Hospital institutional review board (approval no.: 2021056), and the Ditmanson Medical Foundation Chia-Yi Christian Hospital institutional review board waived the requirement to obtain the informed consent.

Patients and Surgical procedures.

This retrospective comparative study consecutively enrolled patients who underwent TBW for olecranon fractures between January 2011 and February 2021 at a single hospital. Patients were categorized into 2 groups on the basis of which of 2 tension band techniques they received. The control group comprised patients who underwent the AO-modified tension band technique, which involves anatomical reduction of the articular surface, transcortical or intramedullary K-wire fixation, and figure-of-eight wiring (Fig. 3A and B). In this group, intramedullary K-wires were routinely advanced as far as possible toward the ulnar isthmus to reduce the incidence of K-wire migration8, with the distal ends bent to optimize insertion depth; however, the exact insertion depth was not objectively measured or strictly standardized. By contrast, the patients in the loop anchor tension band group received a standardized surgical procedure; after anatomical reduction and provisional fixation, 2 intramedullary K-wires were placed in the ulnar medullary canal. The reduction was carefully maintained during loop bending. Loss of reduction occurred infrequently, and supplementary K-wire fixation was generally not required. The proximal ends of the intramedullary K-wires (typically 1.0 mm, or 1.25 mm depending on bone size) were bent into a loop shape only after confirming satisfactory fracture reduction. The loops were carefully adjusted to closely approximate the ulnar cortex and sized just slightly larger than the figure-of-eight metal wire to avoid excessive tension or instability. Figure-of-eight metal wires were locked in the loops and tightened after the full approximation of the loop to the ulnar cortex (Fig. 3A and B). Further intraoperative details, including representative illustrations of loop creation and fixation, are provided in our previously published technique note10. In cases with multiple small fragments or articular surface depression, supplementary fixation such as transosseous sutures or additional transcortical K-wires was occasionally applied at the surgeon’s discretion. Otherwise, all fractures were treated using either the AO-modified TBW or the loop anchor TBW techniques. All procedures were performed by attending orthopedic surgeons with substantial experience in managing elbow trauma. Surgical procedures and postoperative rehabilitation protocols were relatively consistent across surgeons.

Representative case of loop anchor tension band wiring for a Mayo IIB olecranon fracture. (A) Postoperative anteroposterior view showing anatomical reduction after bone grafting. (B) Lateral view demonstrating stable fixation using the loop anchor construct.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: associated injury of the clavicle, scapula, or humerus on the affected side; additional fixation with other implants; and a follow-up period of less than 6 months. Olecranon fractures were evaluated through radiography and were categorized using the Mayo classification12. The postoperative care and rehabilitation protocols were identical in both groups. After the index surgery, the patients were clinically followed up for at least 6 months.

Outcome evaluation

Pain and functional recovery were assessed 6 months after surgery by using the Mayo Elbow Performance Score (a 100-point scoring system) and the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand Score (Quick DASH; a 100-point scoring system). Medical charts and radiographs were reviewed to assess posterior migration of wires, revision surgery, implant removal, time to fracture healing, hospitalization duration, and operation time. Posterior migration of a wire was defined as a backward displacement of the wire tip ≥ 3 mm on the lateral radiograph, measured as the change in distance between the wire tip and its entry point into the bone, comparing the immediate postoperative image (baseline = 0 mm) with the first follow-up radiograph showing definite posterior displacement. Fracture healing was defined radiographically as the presence of trabeculae connecting the 2 major fragments of the bone. All radiographic evaluations were performed by a single orthopedic surgeon who was not involved in the surgical procedures.

Statistical analysis

Intergroup differences in categorical and continuous variables were determined using the χ2 test, Fisher’s exact test, and Mann–Whitney U test. In addition, a subgroup analysis was performed to compare outcomes between Mayo IIA and IIB fractures using the same statistical tests. All analyses were performed using SPSS (version 28.0.1.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

The operation time was significantly shorter in the loop anchor tension band fixation group than in the traditional group (36.2 ± 3.2 vs. 50.3 ± 6.5, P < .001). Regarding the posterior migration of wires, the incidence was 61.3% and 4.8% in the traditional and loop anchor tension band fixation groups, respectively (P < .001). Among cases with posterior migration, the mean migration distance was 4.6 ± 1.8 mm in the traditional group and 3.9 mm in the single affected case of the loop anchor group. No significant differences were discovered between the 2 groups in terms of revision surgery, implant removal, fracture healing time, pain assessment, or functional scores (Table 2). Among patients who underwent implant removal, posterior elbow irritation was the primary indication in the traditional group (61.5%) but was uncommon in the loop anchor group (18.2%), with a statistically significant difference (P = .011). All revision surgeries occurred in the traditional group (5 elbows) and were due to posterior migration of K-wires.

Complications were comprehensively assessed in both groups. In the loop anchor TBW group, one case of wire migration occurred due to an oversized loop with incomplete cortical engagement, which subsequently required implant removal. One patient had permanent extension lag related to pre-existing elbow osteoarthritis, and two patients reported temporary extension lag during early follow-up. No cases of nerve irritation, restricted forearm rotation, nonunion, or infection were observed in either group.

Discussion

In this study, bone union and good to excellent clinical outcomes were achieved for all patients. Compared with those in the traditional group, the patients in the loop anchor tension band fixation group exhibited almost no posterior migration, and few patients developed posterior elbow irritation.

TBW is a simple and effective treatment for most olecranon fractures. Afra et al. found no significant difference in functional outcomes between simple and comminuted olecranon fractures treated with TBW13. In 2023, similar clinical and functional outcomes were found in comminuted Mayo IIB olecranon fractures treated with TBW or a plate construct14. However, a high reoperation rate of 71–84% was reported for hardware removal, mostly due to posterior wire migration (40%) and subcutaneous prominence or discomfort (61.3–79%)13,15.

The appropriate placement of the distal ends of K-wires has been debated. Biomechanics studies have established that anterior ulnar transcortical fixation is favorable, finding increased pull-out strength and superior fixation after cyclic loading tests16,17. The AO-modified TBW technique consists of several steps. K-wires are initially inserted into the anterior ulnar cortex; the wires are drawn back a few millimeters, cut and bent 180°, and then reinserted into the anterior cortex. In this way, tricortical fixation can be achieved, resulting in the maximal pullout strength18. Despite the biomechanical advantages of the anterior ulnar transcortical fixation of TBW, mixed results have been reported regarding clinical K-wire migration. The average K-wire migration distance was found to be significantly smaller after transcortical TBW fixation (average, 1–1.53 mm) than after intramedullary TBW fixation (average, 3.0–5.5 mm)8,16,19. Some researchers have mentioned 3 times lower incidence of prominent K-wire migration observed in transcortical TBW group versus intramedullary TBW group (11.4% vs. 40%)16. However, no significant difference was discovered in another review article2. Overall, the K-wire migration rate after transcortical fixation is up to 11.4–43.7%7,8. Moreover, meticulous calculation and increased radiation exposure are required to ensure favorable end positions of K-wires. Excessive protrusion of K-wires over the anterior ulnar cortex may damage the anterior interosseous nerve. According to an anatomic study conducted by Prayson et al., a K-wire should not be placed 1.5 cm distal to the coronoid process or 10 mm beyond the anterior cortex to prevent injury to the median nerve and ulnar artery20. Catalano et al. found that 20°–30° projection of K-wires in the lateral view was safe because it resulted in the lowest risk to the osseous nerve21. However, the intramedullary placement of K-wires was more favorable for some fracture patterns, such as fractures with small proximal fragments or dorsal corner comminution.

For most olecranon fractures, this study recommends long intramedullary K-wire placement to prevent neurovascular injury and rotation limitation. Some researchers have proposed similar concepts, with different K-wire configurations and special implant designs. Huang et al. reported a decreased complication rate when using long intramedullary K-wires distal to the ulnar canal, for which the wire migration rate was similar to that in the transcortical group (14%)8. In 2013, Kim et al. treated 44 patients using K-wires with eyelets (Tension Band Pin System; Acumed, Hillsboro, OR); no pin migration, restriction of forearm rotation, or neurovascular injury occurred in these patients9. In a retrospective comparative study conducted in 2019, Francia et al. employed “expulsion-proof pins” (France) and found that they resulted in significantly less material discomfort, secondary displacement, wire migration, and hardware removal surgery7. These results are compatible with the present study’s findings of almost no wire migration, no transcortical complications, and a decreased implant irritation rate. In the present study, wire migration occurred in one patient in the loop anchor tension band group due to an oversized loop and incomplete proximal ulnar cortex engagement. In most cases, the proximal ends of the intramedullary K-wires were bent into loops only after confirming satisfactory fracture reduction, with reduction temporarily maintained using an additional bicortical K-wire when necessary. Smaller-diameter intramedullary K-wires (1.0–1.25 mm) were selected to facilitate controlled loop formation, and the loops were carefully adjusted to be only slightly larger than the figure-of-eight wire while approximating the ulnar cortex. This approach maintained fracture reduction and minimized the risk of instability or posterior irritation. The rare occurrence of migration emphasizes the importance of proper loop sizing and careful cortical engagement during the procedure. The wire removal rate in this study was not significantly lower in the loop anchor TBW group compared to control group, which may have been due to the subcutaneous nature of the materials and practice-specific factors. In the traditional TBW group, most removals were driven by symptomatic posterior irritation, whereas in the loop anchor group, the majority were performed either at the recommendation of the surgeon or at the patient’s request despite minimal symptoms. This reflects a relatively low threshold for implant removal in our practice environment, where secondary surgery is readily accessible and often undertaken to preempt potential hardware-related discomfort. These contextual factors may explain why the overall removal rates remained comparable despite the lower irritation rate observed in the loop anchor group.

Posterior prominence of the metal construct after migration may cause symptomatic hardware prominence that warrants implant removal. In various reviews, the rate of symptomatic hardware prominence was lower in the transcortical TBW group (10.7–79.2%) than in the intramedullary TBW group (33.3–88.8%)8,13,15. In 2019, Francia et al. found that the material discomfort rate was significantly lower for fractures treated using the “expulsion-proof pin” than for fractures in the traditional TBW group (24.5% vs. 79.2%)7. Similar findings were obtained in the loop anchor TBW group in this study, with a posterior irritation rate of 23.8% compared with the rate of 50% in the control group; the lower posterior irritation rate may have been related to the lower K-wire migration rate. A lower K-wire migration rate may contribute to a lower irritation rate.

The shorter operation time observed in the loop anchor TBW group may be related to less adjustment of K-wires, fewer fluoroscopy confirmations, and the relative technical simplicity of the procedure. Compared with the traditional intramedullary TBW, the loop-anchor configuration theoretically prevents posterior migration of the K-wires and eliminates the need for forceful insertion into the isthmus. Regardless of the fixation method, careful attention to wire tension remains important, as it may influence bone healing. However, these factors were not directly measured, and thus the observed difference should be interpreted as a hypothesis.

In the subgroup analysis stratified by fracture classification, the differences between Mayo IIA and IIB fractures provided additional insights. In the traditional TBW group, revision surgery was more frequent in IIB fractures than in IIA (20.0% vs. 2.4%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance. This trend suggests that the loop anchor configuration may offer greater benefit in comminuted IIB patterns, where wire migration is otherwise more common. Notably, the operative time advantage of the loop anchor technique observed in the overall cohort was not evident when only IIA and IIB fractures were compared, indicating that the inclusion of Mayo IIIA and IIIB fractures, though limited in number, may have influenced the overall time difference. Therefore, while the loop anchor technique appears particularly advantageous for Mayo IIB fractures, outcomes in IIA fractures were comparable between techniques, and conclusions regarding IIIA and IIIB remain limited by the small sample size.

Notably, AO-modified TBW cases were performed between 2011 and 2021, whereas loop anchor TBW cases were performed between 2015 and 2023. Although there is temporal overlap, we cannot completely exclude the potential influence of surgeon experience, team efficiency, or perioperative improvements over time. Therefore, the observed difference in operation time should be interpreted with caution.

The loop anchor tension band technique offers advantages in terms of the ease of replication, accessibility of materials, and cost-effectiveness. To minimize variability, the wire diameter was chosen according to bone size (usually 1.0–1.25 mm), and the loops were made only slightly larger than the figure-of-eight wire while remaining closely approximated to the proximal ulnar cortex before tightening. The wire combination can be customized on the basis of the fracture pattern and bone size. In most cases, a 1.25-mm-long intramedullary wire in combination a 1.25-mm figure-of-eight metal wire is recommend to minimize the loop size. One biomechanical study revealed that figure-of-eight wiring provided fixation strength comparable to screw fixation and superior to circular wiring in olecranon fracture models22. Further biomechanics studies are required for the identification of the most effective combination of wire diameters and even the combination of the wire with high-strength polyester and polyethylene suture.

This study has several limitations. The insertion depth of intramedullary K-wires in the AO-modified TBW group was not objectively standardized. Although the surgical intention was to advance the wires toward the ulnar isthmus and bend the distal ends to optimize fixation, variability in individual anatomy, radiographic projection, and intraoperative technique may have contributed to the relatively high rate of migration observed in this group. Additionally, multiple surgeons performed the procedures, which could introduce variability in surgical technique; however, all surgeries were performed by attending orthopedic surgeons with substantial experience in managing elbow trauma, and the relative simplicity and reproducibility of the loop anchor TBW technique likely minimized the potential impact of individual surgeon variability on outcomes. The absence of a matched-group design may have reduced group comparability; however, the data of consecutive patients were analyzed to enhance the representativeness of the study population. Furthermore, the loop anchor TBW included a smaller number of cases, reflecting its later introduction at our institution, which limits the statistical power to detect subtle differences. Notably, the distributions of most baseline characteristics and fracture types were similar between the two groups. Nevertheless, the small sample size prevented further analyses of interaction and potential confounding effects, and a larger cohort would be needed to more definitively confirm the benefits of the technique. Finally, the loop anchor construct was created intraoperatively by bending standard K-wires, which may introduce some variability in loop size and wire strength. Although our standardized approach of selecting appropriate wire diameters and keeping loops small helped reduce variability, reproducibility remains a potential limitation of the technique. Due to these limitations, caution should be exercised when interpreting the results. Therefore, further studies employing robust matching methods and larger samples are necessary to validate the present findings.

Conclusion

For Mayo IIA and IIB olecranon fractures, the loop anchor tension band technique can address the problem of K-wire migration and results in less implant irritation and shorter operation times than does the AO-modified TBW technique while achieving similar functional outcomes. Subgroup analysis indicates that the loop anchor technique may offer particular advantages in preventing revision for comminuted IIB fractures, whereas outcomes for IIA fractures are similar between techniques.
