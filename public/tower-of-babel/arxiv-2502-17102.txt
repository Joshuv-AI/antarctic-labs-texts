Abstract
. Lotuses are certain types of finite contractible simplicial complexes, obtained by identifying vertices of polygons subdivided by diagonals. As we explained in a previous paper, each time one resolves a complex reduced plane curve singularity by a sequence of toroidal modifications with respect to suitable local coordinates, one gets a naturally associated lotus, which allows to unify the classical trees used to encode the combinatorial type of the singularity. In this paper we explain how to associate a lotus to each constellation of crosses , which is a finite constellation of infinitely near points endowed with compatible germs of normal crossings divisors with two components, and how this lotus may be seen as a computational architecture . Namely, if the constellation of crosses is associated to an embedded resolution of a complex reduced plane curve singularity A A , one may compute progressively as vertex and edge weights on the lotus the log-discrepancies of the exceptional divisors, the orders of vanishing on them of the starting coordinates, the multiplicities of the strict transforms of the branches of A A , the orders of vanishing of a defining function of A A , the associated Eggers-Wall tree, the delta invariant and the Milnor number of A A , etc. We illustrate these computations using three recurrent examples. Finally, we describe the changes to be done when one works in positive characteristic.

The computations performed using lotuses are based on the fact that all these invariants of plane curve singularities may be seen also as invariants of the associated lotuses, measuring the sizes of suitable parts of them. We illustrate each type of computation using three recurrent examples, one of which is the lotus shown in Figure

The simplest of those sublotuses is associated to the standard cusp singularity, which is the fundamental example on which any general method of singularity theory is usually first illustrated. We recommend to the reader who wants to learn performing such computations to treat each example as an exercise: just make the computations on the corresponding lotus and compare the result with our figure.

Plane curve singularities remain an important experimental ground for studying phenomena concerning singularities of arbitrary dimension or codimension. Our experience taught us that lotuses are very practical for experiments and may lead to discoveries. For instance, the main theorems of Castellini’s thesis

We tried to make this text self-contained, in order to help learning from it a way of thinking geometrically about the basic invariants of plane curve singularities. For this reason, we recall the definitions of the combinatorial invariants mentioned above and of their properties which are useful in the computations using lotuses. We also indicate some proofs, when we find them particularly enlightening.

2.
Actions of proper morphisms on effective divisors

In this section we recall basic facts about effective divisors on smooth complex analytic surfaces:
intersection numbers
,
direct images
and
total transforms
by proper morphisms (see Definition
2.1
), the
projection formula
(see Theorem
2.2
) and an application to
blowups
(see Proposition
2.4
).

Excepted in the last two sections
16
and
17
of the paper, we work in complex analytic geometry, speaking for instance of
biholomorphisms
and
bimeromorphic maps
, but our considerations may be transposed without difficulties to formal geometry over an arbitrary algebraically closed field of characteristic zero.

If
\Sigma
is a smooth complex analytic surface, a
divisor
on
\Sigma
is a finite linear combination with integral coefficients of closed irreducible curves on
\Sigma
.
Its
support
is equal to the union of the irreducible curves appearing with non-zero coefficients in this linear combination. A divisor is called
compact
if its support is compact. It is called
effective
if its coefficients are non-negative. If
h
is a holomorphic function on
\Sigma
, we denote by
\boxed{Z(h)}
its associated effective divisor, which is the zero locus of
h
, each of its irreducible components being counted with its multiplicity.

Recall that a continuous map
\pi
between Hausdorff spaces is called
proper
if the inverse image
\pi^{-1}(K)
of any compact subset
K
of the target is again compact. Divisors may be pushed forward and pulled back by proper morphisms between smooth surfaces:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\pi:\Sigma_{1}\to\Sigma_{2}
be a proper morphism between two smooth complex analytic surfaces and let
D_{1},D_{2}
be two divisors on
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
respectively.

\bullet

If
D_{1}
is irreducible, its
direct image

\boxed{\pi_{*}(D_{1})}
is:

\deg(\pi|_{D_{1}}:D_{1}\to\pi(D_{1}))\ \pi(D_{1}).

Here
\pi(D_{1})
is seen as a reduced subspace of
\Sigma_{2}
, which is either a point or a closed irreducible curve, by the properness of
\pi
, and
\boxed{\deg(\pi|_{D_{1}}:D_{1}\to\pi(D_{1}))}\ \in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
denotes the degree of the restriction of
\pi
to
D_{1}
. Then
\pi_{*}
is extended by linearity to arbitrary effective divisors on
\Sigma_{1}
.

\bullet

The
pull-back
or
total transform

\boxed{\pi^{*}(D_{2})}
of
D_{2}
by
\pi
is defined by gluing the divisors
Z(\pi^{*}h)
of local defining functions
h
of
D_{2}
. Here
\boxed{\pi^{*}h}:=h\circ\pi
is the
pull-back of
h
by
\pi
.

If
D_{1}
is irreducible and
\pi(D_{1})
is a point, then
\deg(\pi|_{D_{1}}:D_{1}\to\pi(D_{1}))=0
, therefore
\pi_{*}(D_{1})=0
. The direct image of any compact divisor is compact. Similarly, the properness of
\pi
implies that the pull-back of any compact divisor is also compact.

If
D,E
are two divisors on a smooth surface
\Sigma
and at least one of them is compact, then one may associate to them an
intersection number

\boxed{D\cdot E}\in\mathbb{Z}
(see
[
7
, Section II.10]
for the complex analytic setting and
[
32
, Theorem V.1.1]
for the algebraic geometric setting).

Direct images and total transforms of divisors are connected by the following
projection formula
(see
[
24
, Proposition 8.3]
for a more general statement):

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\pi:\Sigma_{1}\to\Sigma_{2}
be a proper morphism between two smooth complex analytic surfaces and
D_{1},D_{2}
be two divisors on
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
respectively, at least one of which is compact. Then:

D_{1}\cdot\pi^{*}(D_{2})=\pi_{*}(D_{1})\cdot D_{2}.

We will apply this theorem in the proofs of Propositions
2.4
and
9.4
.

For us, particularly important proper morphisms are the
blowups
of points of smooth surfaces (see
[
9
, Section 8.4]
,
[
62
, Section 3.2]
,
[
27
, Section 1.2.4]
):

Definition 2.3
.

Let
P
be a point of the smooth surface
\Sigma
. Its associated
blowup morphism
is the unique proper bimeromorphic morphism
\boxed{\pi_{P}}:\boxed{\Sigma_{P}}\to\Sigma
which is an isomorphism above

and which is defined in local coordinates

at

of the closure of the graph of the projectivisation map
\mathbb{C}^{2}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Its

\boxed{E_{P}}
is the preimage
\pi_{P}^{-1}(P)\hookrightarrow\Sigma_{P}
. If

is a closed reduced complex curve, its

\boxed{C_{P}}
by the blowup morphism
\pi_{P}
is the closure of
\pi_{P}^{-1}(C\smallsetminus\{P\})
inside
\Sigma_{P}
.

By working in local coordinates
(x,y)
, one may see that the second projection
\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
establishes an isomorphism
E_{P}\simeq\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Keeping the notations above, one has the following behaviour
of intersection numbers, which will be essential for us in Section
6
:

Proposition 2.4
.

Assume that the reduced curve
C\hookrightarrow\Sigma
is
compact
and
smooth at
P
. Then the self-intersection number
C\cdot C
of
C
in
\Sigma

(1)

E_{P}\cdot E_{P}=-1
.

(2)

C_{P}\cdot C_{P}=C\cdot C-1

Proof.

We give the proof of this basic property of blowups in order to illustrate the power of the

(see Theorem

We will use the following consequences of the

(2.1)

\pi_{P}^{*}(C)=E_{P}+C_{P}.

(2.2)

E_{P}\cdot C_{P}=1.

Both equalities are in fact equivalent to the smoothness of

(1)

Therefore, by (

By applying the projection formula to the same morphism, but replacing

Using again the equality (

3.
Plane curve singularities and their embedded resolutions

In this section we recall the notions of

A

The lotus of a finite active constellation of crosses may be also described as follows:

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
be a finite active constellation of crosses. Then its lotus is the simplicial complex obtained in the following way:

•

Its vertices are the irreducible components of

•

•

If three points are pairwise joined by edges in the resulting graph, then they are the vertices of a unique two-dimensional simplex.

Corollary 5.5

The lotus of a finite active constellation of crosses is a space-time representation of the evolution of the dual graphs of total transforms of the crosses of the constellation, as one performs the blowups leading to its model.

7.
Log-discrepancies and orders of vanishing of the first axis

We start this section by recalling basic facts about
semivaluations
and
valuations
of commutative rings (see Definition
7.1
), illustrated by the semivaluations of
\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
which will be important for us. We define then the notion of
log-discrepancy
of a branch on a model of
(S,O)
(see Definition
7.5
). Finally, we explain how to compute on the lotus
\Lambda(\hat{\mathcal{C}})
of a finite active constellation of crosses
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
both the log-discrepancy
\lambda(E)
and the order of vanishing
\mathrm{ord}_{E}(L)
corresponding to each vertex
E
of the lotus (see Corollary
7.9
).

In the sequel we consider the standard addition and total order of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}=[0,+\infty)
to be extended as usual to the set
[0,+\infty]:=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{+\infty\}
.

The following notion of
semivaluation
will allow us to look at branches on
(S,O)
, at infinitely near points of
O
and at irreducible components of exceptional divisors of models of
(S,O)
as particular cases of a single concept (see Remark
7.3
):

Definition 7.1
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring. A
semivaluation of

R
is a function
\nu:R\to[0,\infty]
such that:

(1)

For every
f,g\in R
,
\nu(fg)=\nu(f)+\nu(g)
.

(2)

For every
f,g\in R
,
\nu(f+g)\geq\min\{\nu(f),\nu(g)\}
.

(3)

\nu(1)=0
.

(4)

\nu(0)=+\infty
.

A semivaluation
\nu
is called a
valuation
if
0
is the only element
f
of
R
such that
\nu(f)=+\infty
.

As a first manipulation of these axioms, let us show that if
u\in R
is a unit and
\nu
a semivaluation of
R
, then
\nu(u)=0
. By the definition of units, there exists
v\in R
such that
1=u\cdot v
. Therefore, by conditions (
3
) and (
1
):

0=\nu(1)=\nu(u)+\nu(v).

As
\nu
takes values in
[0,\infty]
, we deduce that both
\nu(u)
and
\nu(v)
vanish. In particular,
\nu(u)=0
, as announced.

Let now
\nu
be a semivaluation of the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
. Consider a plane curve singularity
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
. Define:

\boxed{\nu(A)}:=\nu(f_{A})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},

where
f_{A}\in\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
is a defining germ of
A
. This real number is independent of the choice of the defining germ
f_{A}
. Indeed, if
g_{A}
is a second such germ, then
g_{A}=u\cdot f_{A}
, where
u
is a unit of the ring
\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
. Therefore, by condition (
1
) of Definition
7.1
:

\nu(g_{A})=\nu(u)+\nu(f_{A})=\nu(f_{A}),

where the second equality is a consequence of the fact that
u
is a unit of
\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
.

Definition 7.2
.

Let
\pi:(S_{\pi},E_{\pi})\to(S,O)
be a model of
(S,O)
in the sense of
Definition
3.2
and let
C
be a branch on
S_{\pi}
at a point of
E_{\pi}

(the branch
C
may be included in
E_{\pi}
). We denote by:

\boxed{\mathrm{ord}_{C}}:\mathcal{O}_{S,O}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\{+\infty\}

the
divisorial valuation
of
\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
which associates to
f\in\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
the order of vanishing on
C
of the pull-back
\pi^{*}f
. In other words,
\mathrm{ord}_{C}(f)
is the multiplicity of
C
as a component of the principal effective divisor on the surface
S_{\pi}
defined by the function
\pi^{*}f
.
Notice that if
f\neq 0
, then
\mathrm{ord}_{C}(f)
is a nonnegative integer.
This shows that
\mathrm{ord}_{C}
is indeed a valuation.

If
C
is a germ at some point of
E_{\pi}
of an irreducible component
E
of the exceptional divisor
E_{\pi}
, then
\mathrm{ord}_{C}(f)
depends only on
E
and not on the point at which the germ
C
is taken. For this reason, it will be also denoted
\boxed{\mathrm{ord}_{E}(f)}
. This integer is unchanged if one replaces
E
by a strict transform on any other model on which
E
appears (in the sense of Definition
4.1
(
1
)). Therefore, the knowledge of the valuation
\mathrm{ord}_{E}
is equivalent to the knowledge of
E
and of its strict transforms on all the models on which it appears. It is also equivalent to the knowledge of the infinitely near point from which this class of irreducible exceptional divisors arises by blowup.

Similarly, if
C
is not included in the exceptional divisor
E_{\pi}
, then it is the strict transform of the branch
\pi(C)
on
S
and
\mathrm{ord}_{C}(f)=\mathrm{ord}_{\pi(C)}(f)
.

Remark 7.3
.

We have reached the announced viewpoint from which one may look at branches, at irreducible exceptional divisors (more precisely, at bimeromorphic classes of such branches and divisors on the various models of
(S,O)
) and at infinitely near points of
O
as particular cases of the same concept: that of semivaluation of the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
. For a thorough study of the space of semivaluations of
\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
, one may consult Favre and Jonsson’s book
[
23
]
. In this paper, that space will appear in the statement of Proposition

.

For any branch
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
, besides the divisorial valuation
\mathrm{ord}_{A}
of Definition
7.2
, one has also an associated semivaluation which is not a valuation:

Definition 7.4
.

Let
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
be a branch.
The
intersection semivaluation

\boxed{I_{A}}:\mathcal{O}_{S,O}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\{+\infty\}

is defined by
I_{A}(D):=(A\cdot D)_{O}
for any curve singularity
D\hookrightarrow(S,O)
(see Definition
9.1
).

The semivaluation
I_{A}
is not a valuation because
I_{A}(A)=\infty
. Intersection semivaluations will be used in the statement of Proposition

.

Let us define now the notion of
log-discrepancy
of a divisorial semivaluation. This important concept appears for instance in the definition of the
topological zeta function
of a plane curve singularity (see Wall

or Veys

and
10.8

Definition 7.5
.

Let
\pi:(S_{\pi},E_{\pi})\to(S,O)
be a model of
(S,O)
and let
C\hookrightarrow S_{\pi}
be a branch at a point of
E_{\pi}
.
Let
\omega
be a germ of non-vanishing differential form of degree
2
on
(S,O)

\mathrm{div}(\pi^{*}\omega)
of its pull-back to the model

\boxed{\lambda(C)}
of

is defined as:

1+\mathrm{ord}_{C}(\mathrm{div}(\pi^{*}\omega)).

Note that
\lambda(C)
depends only on the valuation
\mathrm{ord}_{C}
, that is, it is independent of the choice of non-vanishing differential form
\omega
. One has
\lambda(C)\geq 1
, with equality if and only if

is not contained in the exceptional divisor

, that is, if and only if the valuation
\mathrm{ord}_{C}
is defined by a branch on
(S,O)
.

The following statement may be proved by working in local coordinates. It shows that the orders of vanishing of the smooth branch
L
and the log-discrepancies of the various branches on models of
(S,O)
may be computed in the same way recursively during a process of blowups of points, the only difference lying in their values taken on branches already present on
(S,O)
:

Proposition 7.6
.

Let
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
be a finite active constellation of crosses over

, with initial cross
X_{O}=L+L_{1}

Let

(1)

\mathrm{ord}_{E_{P}}(L)=\mathrm{ord}_{F_{1}}(L)+\mathrm{ord}_{F_{2}}(L),

(2)

\lambda(E_{P})=\lambda(F_{1})+\lambda(F_{2}).

Moreover, for every branch

on
(S,O)
, one has:

(3)

\mathrm{ord}_{C}(L)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\mbox{ if }L\neq C,\\
1&\mbox{ if }L=C.\end{array}\right.

(4)

\lambda(C)=1.

8.
Multiplicities of strict transforms

In this section we explain how to compute on an associated lotus
\Lambda(\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A})
the multiplicities of a reduced plane curve singularity
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
at all infinitely near points lying on it (see Corollary
8.6
). This computation is based on the
proximity equalities
of Theorem
8.5
.

Let us recall the definition of the
multiplicity
of a plane curve singularity:

Definition 8.1
.

Let
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
be a plane curve singularity, with defining function

f_{A}\in\mathfrak{m}_{S,O}
. By choosing local coordinates
(x,y)
on
(S,O)
,
f_{A}

becomes a series in
\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
. The
multiplicity

\boxed{e_{O}(A)}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}

of
A
at
O
is the smallest degree of the monomials appearing in
f_{A}
.

For instance, one has
e_{O}(A)=2
for the cusp singularity
A
of Example
3.4
, defined by
f_{A}=y^{2}-x^{3}
.

Remark 8.2
.

Definition
8.1
is independent of the choices of defining function
f_{A}
and of local coordinates. This notion may be thought as a local analog of the degree of an effective divisor in a complex projective plane. Indeed, both have similar geometric interpretations: the degree is the intersection number of the projective curve with a line not contained in it and the multiplicity of a plane curve singularity
A
in
(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
is the intersection number of
A
at
O
with a line not tangent to
A
.

Generalizing equation (
2.1
), multiplicities at
O
may also be computed as orders of vanishing, as may be readily seen by working in local coordinates:

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
be a plane curve singularity. Consider the blowup morphism
\pi_{O}:S_{O}\to S
of
S
at
O
, with exceptional divisor
E_{O}
. Then:

e_{O}(A)=\mathrm{ord}_{E_{O}}(A).

The notion of multiplicity may be extended to infinitely near points of
O
:

Definition 8.4
.

Let
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
be a plane curve singularity and
P
be an infinitely near point of
O
. The
multiplicity

\boxed{e_{P}(A)}
of
A
at
P
is the multiplicity at
P
of the strict transform of
A
on the minimal model
S_{P}
containing
P
.

The
proximity equalities
of the following theorem (see
[
14
, Theorem 3.5.3]
) relate the multiplicities of a plane curve singularity at their various infinitely near points:

Theorem 8.5
.

Let
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
be a plane curve singularity and
P
be an infinitely near point of
O
lying on
A
in the sense of Definition
4.1
(
4
). Then, with the notations of Definition
4.1
(
5

e_{P}(A)=\sum_{Q:Q\to P}e_{Q}(A).

Theorem
8.5
shows that the multiplicities of the strict transforms of a plane curve singularity

\Lambda(\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A})
of any active constellation of crosses
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A}
adapted to it in the sense of Definition
4.10

on the set of edges appearing in Remark

:

Corollary 8.6
.

\Lambda(\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A})
be the lotus of a finite active constellation of crosses adapted to

9.
Orders of vanishing and intersection numbers

In this section we explain how to compute the orders of vanishing
\mathrm{ord}_{E}(A)
of a reduced plane curve singularity
A
at the vertices
E
of an associated lotus, as well as the intersection numbers
(A_{l}\cdot A_{m})_{O}
of pairs of distinct branches of
A
either from the multiplicities of those branches at infinitely near points or as particular orders of vanishing (see Corollaries
9.3
and
9.6
).

We start recalling the definition of the intersection number of two plane curve singularities at the same point:

Definition 9.1
.

Let
A,B\hookrightarrow(S,O)
be two plane curve singularities. Let
f_{A},f_{B}\in\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
be defining functions for them and let
\boxed{(f_{A},f_{B})}
be the ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
generated by
f_{A}
and
f_{B}
. The
intersection number of
A
and
B
at
O
, denoted
\boxed{(A\cdot B)_{O}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
is the dimension of the quotient vector space

\mathcal{O}_{S,O}/(f_{A},f_{B}).

Note that
(A\cdot B)_{O}=\infty
if and only if
A
and
B
have at least one common branch.

As the ideal
(f_{A},f_{B})
does not depend on the choice of defining germs of
A
and
B
, the intersection number
(A\cdot B)_{O}
is well-defined. It is the local contribution to the computation of the global intersection number of two effective divisors without common irreducible components in a smooth surface, at least one of which is non-compact (see Section
2
). Indeed, if
A
and
B
are two such divisors in a smooth surface
\Sigma
, then:

A\cdot B=\sum_{P\in\Sigma}(A\cdot B)_{P}.

Bézout’s theorem for curves
A,B
in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
, which states that:

A\cdot B=\deg(A)\cdot\deg(B),

has the following local analog for
A,B\hookrightarrow(S,O)
:

(A\cdot B)_{O}\geq e_{O}(A)\cdot e_{O}(B),

with equality if and only if
A
and
B
are transversal, in the sense that they have no common tangent line. This disymmetry between Bézout’s theorem in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
(which is an equality) and its local analog (which is an inequality) may be repaired by looking also at the infinitely near points common to the two curve singularities (see
[
9
, Section 8.4, Theorem 13]
):

Theorem 9.2
.

Let
A,B\hookrightarrow(S,O)
be two plane curve singularities. Then their intersection number
(A\cdot B)_{O}
may be computed as follows:

(A\cdot B)_{O}=\sum e_{P}(A)e_{P}(B),

the sum being taken over the infinitely near points
P
common to (the strict transforms of)
A
and
B
.

This theorem gives a first method of computation on a lotus of the intersection number of two branches
A_{l}
and
A_{m}
of
A
:

Corollary 9.3
.

Let
\Lambda(\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A})
be the lotus of a finite active constellation of crosses adapted to
A
, in the sense of Definition
4.10
. Once the multiplicities of the strict transforms of two distinct branches
A_{l}
and
A_{m}
of
A
are computed as edge decorations (see Corollary
8.6
), the intersection number
(A_{l}\cdot A_{m})_{O}
is the sum of the products of multiplicities associated to the same edge.

Proposition 9.4
.

Let

be a curve singularity on
(S,O)
, which does not contain

in its support. Consider an embedded resolution

of

such that the strict transforms
A_{\pi}
and
D_{\pi}
of

and

are disjoint. Denote by
\boxed{E_{A}}
the unique irreducible component of the exceptional divisor of

which intersects the strict transform
A_{\pi}
of

by

(A\cdot D)_{O}=\mathrm{ord}_{E_{A}}(D).

Proof.

Using the projection formula (see Theorem
2.2
), we get:

\displaystyle(A\cdot D)_{O}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi_{*}(A_{\pi})\cdot D=A_{\pi}\cdot\pi^{*}(D)=A_{\pi}\cdot\left(\sum_{E\subseteq E_{\pi}}\mathrm{ord}_{E}(D)\ E+D_{\pi}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{\pi}\cdot(\mathrm{ord}_{E_{A}}(D)\ E_{A})=\mathrm{ord}_{E_{A}}(D).

∎

8.3

10.
The Eggers-Wall tree associated to a lotus

In this section we explain following
[
27
, Section 1.6.1]
how to associate an
Eggers-Wall
tree

\Theta_{L}(A)
to a reduced plane curve singularity
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
, relative to a smooth branch
L
(see Definition
10.3
). Then, we describe the main properties of the natural embedding built in
[
26
, Section 8]
of
\Theta_{L}(A)
in the space of semivaluations of
(S,O)

(see Proposition
10.7
). Finally, we show how to construct
\Theta_{L}(A)
from an associated lotus
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A}
, decorated by log-discrepancies
\lambda(E)
and orders of vanishing
\mathrm{ord}_{E}(L)
, whose computation was presented in Section
7
(see Proposition
10.8
).

We denote by

\boxed{\mathbb{C}\{x^{1/\mathbb{N}}\}}:=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{C}\{x^{1/n}\}

the ring of convergent
Newton-Puiseux series
and by
\boxed{\nu_{x}}
its
x
-adic valuation
, defined on a nonzero series
\xi=\sum c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}\{x^{1/n}\}
by:

\boxed{\nu_{x}(\xi)}:=\min\{\alpha\mid c_{\alpha}\neq 0\}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

Consider a
reduced
curve singularity
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
. Let
L
be a smooth branch on
(S,O)
and
(x,y)
a local coordinate system on
(S,O)
such that
L=Z(x)
. We assume that
L
is not a component of
A
. Let
f\in\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
be a defining function of
A
in this coordinate system. As a consequence of the
Newton-Puiseux Theorem
(see
[
27
, Theorem 1.2.20]
and the references preceding it), there exists a finite subset
\boxed{\mathcal{Z}_{x}(A)}\subset\mathbb{C}\{x^{1/\mathbb{N}}\}
of
zeros of
f
relative to
x

and a unit
u(x,y)
of the local ring
\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
, such that:

(10.1)

f(x,y)=u(x,y)\prod_{\xi\in\mathcal{Z}_{x}(A)}(y-\xi).

The set
\mathcal{Z}_{x}(A)
is equal to the disjoint union of the
sets
\mathcal{Z}_{x}(A_{l})
, when
A_{l}
varies among the branches of
A
. In turn, each set
\mathcal{Z}_{x}(A_{l})
is an orbit of the action on
\mathbb{C}\{x^{1/\mathbb{N}}\}
of the Galois group of the field extension induced by the ring extension
\mathbb{C}\{x\}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}\{x^{1/\mathbb{N}}\}
. Namely, if
\xi\in\mathbb{C}\{x^{1/\mathbb{N}}\}
belongs to
\mathcal{Z}_{x}(A_{l})
then, after writing
\xi=\eta(x^{\frac{1}{n}})
with
\eta\in\mathbb{C}\{t\}
and
n\geq 1
, we get:

(10.2)

\mathcal{Z}_{x}(A_{l})=\{\eta(\rho\cdot x^{\frac{1}{n}});\ \ \rho\in\mathbb{C},\rho^{n}=1\}.

That is, the Galois conjugates of
\eta(x^{\frac{1}{n}})
are simply obtained by replacing
x^{\frac{1}{n}}
by all possible
n
-th roots of
x
. Note that the elements
\eta(\rho\cdot x^{\frac{1}{n}})
of the set
\mathcal{Z}_{x}(A_{l})
are pairwise distinct if and only if
n
is the lowest common denominator of the exponents of
\xi
.

The elements of
\mathcal{Z}_{x}(A)
allow to define the following important
invariants
(see Remark
10.5
) of
A
relative to the smooth branch
L
:

Definition 10.1
.

The set of
characteristic exponents
of a branch
A_{l}
of
A
relative to
L
is

\boxed{{\mathrm{Ch}}_{L}(A_{l})}:=\{\nu_{x}(\xi-\xi^{\prime}),\>\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}_{x}(A_{l}),\>\xi\neq\xi^{\prime}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}_{>0}.

The
order of coincidence
of two distinct branches
A_{l}
and
A_{m}
of
A
relative to
L
is

\boxed{k_{L}(A_{l},A_{m})}:=\max\{\nu_{x}(\xi-\xi^{\prime}),\>\xi\in\mathcal{Z}_{x}(A_{l}),\>\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}_{x}(A_{m})\}\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}.

Example 10.2
.

Assume that
A=A_{1}+A_{2}
, where

of

.

Definition 11.3

We need also the notion of
trunk decomposition
of a complete Eggers-Wall tree:

Definition 12.3
.

Let
A
be a reduced plane curve singularity and
L
be a smooth branch on
(S,O)
such that
\Theta_{L}(A)
is complete. A
trunk decomposition
of
\Theta_{L}(A)
is a finite set
\boxed{\mathfrak{D}}
of compact subsegments of
\Theta_{L}(A)
called
trunks
, such that:

(1)

The union of the trunks is equal to
\Theta_{L}(A)
.

(2)

For every trunk
\tau\in\mathfrak{D}
, one may denote its ends by
R_{\tau},F_{\tau}
, such that

R_{\tau}\prec_{L}F_{\tau}
.

(3)

For every trunk
\tau\in\mathfrak{D}
, the index function
\mathbf{i}_{L}
is constant on the half open segment
(R_{\tau}F_{\tau}]
and
F_{\tau}
is a leaf of
\Theta_{L}(A)
.

Let
\tau=[R_{\tau}F_{\tau}]
be a trunk of the trunk decomposition
\mathcal{D}
of a complete Eggers-Wall tree
\Theta_{L}(A)
. Its
marked points
are
R_{\tau},F_{\tau}
and the marked points of
\Theta_{L}(A)
which belong to
\tau
. We consider on
\tau
the
renormalized exponent function

\mathbf{e}_{\tau}:\tau\to[0,\infty]
defined by

(12.1)

\boxed{{\mathbf{e}}_{\tau}(P)}:={\mathbf{i}}_{L}(F_{\tau})\cdot({\mathbf{e}}_{L}(P)-{\mathbf{e}}_{L}(R_{\tau})).

We denote by
\boxed{\mathcal{E}_{\tau}}\subset\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
the set of values of the marked points of
\tau
by the renormalized exponent function
\mathbf{e}_{\tau}
.

Notice that
0={\mathbf{e}}_{\tau}(R_{\tau})
,
\infty={\mathbf{e}}_{\tau}(F_{\tau})
, and
\{0,\infty\}\subset\mathcal{E}_{\tau}
.

We are ready to introduce the notion of
lotus associated to a trunk decomposition
of a complete Eggers-Wall tree:

Definition 12.4
.

Let
A
be a reduced plane curve singularity and
L
a smooth branch on
(S,O)
. Assume that the Eggers-Wall tree
\Theta_{L}(A)
is complete. Let
\mathfrak{D}
be a trunk decomposition of
\Theta_{L}(A)
.
Then, the
lotus
\boxed{\Lambda_{\mathfrak{D}}}

associated with the trunk decomposition

\mathfrak{D}
is constructed as follows:

(1)

By definition, there is a canonical bijection between the marked points of a trunk
\tau
in the decomposition and the marked vertices of the abstract lotus
\Lambda(\mathcal{E}_{\tau})
. We label corresponding marked points and vertices with the same labels.

(2)

Identify all the vertices of
\bigsqcup_{\tau\in\mathfrak{D}}\Lambda(\mathcal{E}_{\tau})
which have the same label.
The result of this identification is a two dimensional simplicial complex
\Lambda_{\mathfrak{D}}
.
For each trunk
\tau\in\mathfrak{D}
, we keep the same labels when taking the
images of the marked vertices of
\Lambda(\mathcal{E}_{\tau})
in the lotus

\Lambda_{\mathfrak{D}}
.

Therefore, to each trunk
\tau
of
\mathfrak{D}
corresponds a membrane
\Lambda(\mathcal{E}_{\tau})
of
\Lambda_{\mathfrak{D}}
.

By applying the algorithm of Corollary
10.10
to the lotus
\Lambda_{\mathfrak{D}}
, we get:

Proposition 12.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{D}
be a trunk decomposition of a complete Eggers-Wall tree
\Theta_{L}
. Consider its associated lotus
\Lambda_{\mathfrak{D}}
in the sense of Definition
12.4
. Then the Eggers-Wall tree associated to
\Lambda_{\mathfrak{D}}
in the sense of Definition
10.11
is isomorphic to the starting Eggers-Wall tree
\Theta_{L}
.

\begin{array}[]{ll}x^{\tfrac{3}{2}}+x^{\tfrac{7}{3}}+x^{\tfrac{29}{12}}&=x^{\tfrac{3}{2}}\left(1+x^{\tfrac{7}{3}-\tfrac{3}{2}}+x^{\tfrac{29}{12}-\tfrac{3}{2}}\right)=x^{\tfrac{3}{2}}\left(1+x^{\tfrac{5}{6}}+x^{\tfrac{11}{12}}\right)=x^{\tfrac{3}{2}}\left(1+x^{\tfrac{5}{6}}\left(1+x^{\tfrac{11}{12}-\tfrac{5}{6}}\right)\right)\\
&=x^{\tfrac{3}{2}}\left(1+x^{\tfrac{5}{6}}\left(1+x^{\tfrac{1}{12}}\right)\right)=x^{\boxed{\tfrac{3}{2}}}\left(1+x^{\tfrac{1}{2}\boxed{\tfrac{5}{3}}}\left(1+x^{\tfrac{1}{6}\boxed{\tfrac{1}{2}}}\right)\right).\end{array}

13.
Computation of intersection numbers from Eggers-Wall trees

In Section
9
we explained one method of computation of the intersection numbers of pairs of branches of a plane curve singularity
A
, using a corresponding lotus. In this section, we explain how to compute them using the Eggers-Wall tree associated to the lotus in Section
10
. Besides the exponent function
\mathbf{e}_{L}
and the index function
\mathbf{i}_{L}
, the Eggers-Wall tree
\Theta_{L}(A)
of a reduced plane curve singularity
A
is endowed with a third function, the
contact complexity function

\mathbf{c}_{L}
, which is determined by the first two functions. It is
\mathbf{c}_{L}
which allows to determine easily the intersection numbers of the branches of
A
(see Theorem
13.4
).

Definition 13.1
.

Let
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
be a reduced plane curve singularity and
L
be a smooth branch on
S
.
The
contact complexity
function

{\mathbf{c}}_{L}:\Theta_{L}(A)\to[0,\infty]

is defined at a point
P\in\Theta_{L}(A)
by the formula

(13.1)

\boxed{{\mathbf{c}}_{L}(P)}:=\int_{L}^{P}\frac{d\>{\mathbf{e}}_{L}}{{\mathbf{i}}_{L}}.

One may express the value of the integral (
13.1
) in terms of the characteristic exponents of the branches of
A
and of the exponent
{\mathbf{e}}_{L}(P)
. Take a branch
A_{l}
of
A
such that
P\in[LA_{l}]
. Denote by
\boxed{\alpha_{1}}<\dots<\boxed{\alpha_{g}}
the characteristic exponents of
A_{l}
. We set
\boxed{\alpha_{0}}:=0
and
\boxed{\alpha_{g+1}}:=\infty
. Define
P_{j}:=\mathbf{e}_{L}^{-1}(\alpha_{j})
, for each
j\in\{0,\dots,g+1\}
. The index function
{\mathbf{i}}_{L}
has value
\boxed{{\mathbf{i}}_{j}}
on the half-open segment
(P_{j}P_{j+1}]
. Denote by
\boxed{r}\in\{0,\dots,g\}
the unique integer such that
P\in[P_{r}P_{r+1})
. Then the contact complexity
{\mathbf{c}}_{L}(P)
becomes, by formula (
13.1
):

(13.2)

{\mathbf{c}}_{L}(P)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{j-1}}{{\mathbf{i}}_{j-1}}\right)+\frac{{\mathbf{e}}_{L}(P)-\alpha_{r}}{{\mathbf{i}}_{r}}.

Example 13.2
.

We give the value of the contact complexity function
\mathbf{c}_{L}
on the ramification point

of the tree

on the interval

) we have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}_{L}(E_{5})

In order to compute the intersection number
(A_{l}\cdot A_{m})_{O}
of two branches of

, we need the notion of
center of a tripod
:

Definition 13.3
.

Let
A_{l}
and
A_{m}
be two branches of
A
different from
L
. The
center
\boxed{\langle L,A_{l},A_{m}\rangle}
of the tripod
determined by

and

\Theta_{L}(A_{l}+A_{m})
if

and
A_{m}
are different. If instead

, we set
\langle L,A_{l},A_{m}\rangle:=A_{l}
.

The center
\langle L,A_{l},A_{m}\rangle
of the tripod determined by the points
A_{l},A_{m},L
of
\Theta_{L}(A)
may be also seen as the infimum of the points
A_{l},A_{m}
with respect to the partial order relation
\preceq_{L}
determined by the choice of the root
L
of
\Theta_{L}(A)
(see Definition
10.3
). The following
tripod formula
, which in different language goes back at least to works of Smith, Stolz and Max Noether, allows to determine the intersection numbers using Eggers-Wall trees (see
[

):

Theorem 13.4
.

If
A_{l}
and
A_{m}
are two branches of

different from
L
, then:

(13.3)

(A_{l}\cdot A_{m})_{O}=\mathbf{i}_{L}(A_{l})\cdot\mathbf{i}_{L}(A_{m})\cdot{\mathbf{c}}_{L}(\langle L,A_{l},A_{m}\rangle).

Notice that if
A_{i}
is a branch of

\mathbf{i}_{L}(A_{i})=(L\cdot A_{i})_{O}
. Using this observation, one can reformulate (

) as:

(13.4)

{\mathbf{c}}_{L}(\langle L,A_{l},A_{m}\rangle)=\frac{(A_{l}\cdot A_{m})_{O}}{(A_{l}\cdot L)_{O}\cdot(A_{m}\cdot L)_{O}}.

Remark 13.5
.

Płoski proved in
[

]
that for any three pairwise distinct branches

,
A_{m}
and

, in the sequence
\frac{(A_{l}\cdot A_{m})_{O}}{(A_{l}\cdot L)_{O}\cdot(A_{m}\cdot L)_{O}}

\frac{(A_{l}\cdot A_{k})_{O}}{(A_{l}\cdot L)_{O}\cdot(A_{k}\cdot L)_{O}}
and
\frac{(A_{m}\cdot A_{k})_{O}}{(A_{m}\cdot L)_{O}\cdot(A_{k}\cdot L)_{O}}
there are two terms equal and the third one is not less than the two equal terms (see also
[
29
]
, in which these numbers were called
logarithmic distances
). Płoski’s result may be also expressed as the statement that the function

(13.5)

\boxed{U_{L}}(A_{l},A_{m}):=\frac{(A_{l}\cdot L)_{O}\cdot(A_{m}\cdot L)_{O}}{(A_{l}\cdot A_{m})_{O}}

defines an ultrametric on the set of branches on
(S,O)
different from
L
. In
[
26
]
and
[
28
]
analogous maps were studied in the broader context of branches traced on arbitrary normal surface singularities and those surface singularities for which such maps define ultrametrics were identified.

14.
The minimal generating set of the semigroup of a branch

In this section we recall the definition of the
semigroup
of a branch (see Definition
14.1
) and we explain how to compute its minimal generating sequence using a lotus associated to the branch (see Corollary
14.4
).

The following notion goes back at least to Arf’s paper
[
6
]
(see also
[
21
]
and
[
38
]
):

Definition 14.1
.

Let
A
be an abstract branch. Denote by
\tilde{A}\to A
a normalization morphism and by
\boxed{\nu_{A}}
the restriction to the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{A}
of the canonical valuation of
\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{A}}
(which computes the order of vanishing at the marked point of
\tilde{A}
). The
semigroup
\Gamma(A)
of
A
is defined by:

\boxed{\Gamma(A)}:=\{\nu_{A}(f),\ f\in\mathcal{O}_{A}\smallsetminus\{0\}\}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

The set
\Gamma(A)
is a subsemigroup of
(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},+)
, because for every
f,g\in\mathcal{O}_{A}\smallsetminus\{0\}
:

\nu_{A}(fg)=\nu_{A}(f)+\nu_{A}(g),

by the definition of valuations (see Definition
7.1
(
1
)). In fact
\Gamma(A)
is a submonoid of
(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},+)
, because the neutral element
0\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
is achieved as
\nu_{A}(u)
, for every unit
u
of
\mathcal{O}_{A}
. It is nevertheless traditional in singularity theory to speak of the
semigroup
of a branch instead of its
monoid
.

Remark 14.2
.

When
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
is a
plane
branch, there is an alternative interpretation of the semigroup
\Gamma(A)
as the set of intersection numbers of
A
with the plane curve singularities which do not contain
A
:

\Gamma(A)=\{(A\cdot Z(f))_{O},\ f\in\mathcal{O}_{S,O},\ f|_{A}\neq 0\}.

Every subsemigroup
\Gamma
of
(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},+)
is finitely generated and has a unique minimal generating set with respect to inclusion. It consists of the indecomposable elements of
\Gamma
. By ordering them increasingly, one gets the
minimal generating sequence
of
\Gamma
.

When
\Gamma
is the semigroup of a plane branch
A
, this sequence may be expressed in terms of the characteristic exponents of
A
. In order to explain this, we need more notations. Denote by
\boxed{\alpha_{1}}<\dots<\boxed{\alpha_{g}}
the
generic
characteristic exponents of
A
, that is, the elements of the set
{\mathrm{Ch}}_{L}(A_{l})
of Definition
10.1
, where
L
is a smooth branch
transversal
to
A
. This means that
1<\alpha_{1}
. Using Zariski’s notations in
[
65
, Chapitre II.3]
, define successively:

(14.1)

\displaystyle\boxed{\beta_{0}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mbox{the minimal common denominator of }\ \alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{g}

(14.2)

\displaystyle\boxed{\beta_{i}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\beta_{0}\alpha_{i},\ \mbox{ for all }i\in\{1,\dots,g\},

(14.3)

\displaystyle\boxed{e_{i}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\gcd(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{i}),\ \mbox{ for all }i\in\{0,\ldots,g\},

(14.4)

\displaystyle\boxed{n_{i}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{e_{i-1}}{e_{i}},\ \mbox{ for all }i\in\{1,\ldots,g\},

(14.5)

\displaystyle\boxed{\overline{\beta}_{0}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\beta_{0},

(14.6)

\displaystyle\boxed{\overline{\beta}_{1}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\beta_{1},

(14.7)

\displaystyle\boxed{\overline{\beta}_{i}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle n_{i-1}\overline{\beta}_{i-1}+\beta_{i}-\beta_{i-1},\ \mbox{ for all }i\in\{2,\ldots,g\}.

One has the following result (see Zariski’s
[
65
, Theorem 3.9]
):

Proposition 14.3
.

Assume that
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
is a plane branch. In terms of the previous notations,
the minimal generating sequence of its semigroup
\Gamma(A)
is:

(\overline{\beta}_{0},\dots,\overline{\beta}_{g}).

This proposition indicates the following procedure to compute the minimal generating sequence of
\Gamma(A)
:

Corollary 14.4
.

Let
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
be a plane branch.
Start from the lotus
\Lambda(\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A})
of a finite active constellation of crosses such that the branch
L
corresponding to the initial vertex is transversal to
A
.

•

Construct the Eggers-Wall tree
\Theta_{L}(A)
from
\Lambda(\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A})
, as explained in Corollary
10.10
.

•

If
P_{1},\dots,P_{g}
are the points of discontinuity of the index function
\mathbf{i}_{L}
, define
\alpha_{i}:=\mathbf{e}_{L}(P_{i})
, for
i=1,\dots,g
.

•

Transform the sequence
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{g})
into the sequence
(\overline{\beta}_{0},\dots,\overline{\beta}_{g})
using formulae (
14.1
)–(
14.7
).

On the other hand, by combining all the formulae (
14.7
), we get for all
i\in\{1,\dots,g\}
(see also
[
65
, Chapitre II.3, Théorème 3.9 (a)]
):

(14.8)

\overline{\beta}_{i}=(n_{1}-1)n_{2}\cdots n_{i-1}\beta_{1}+(n_{2}-1)n_{3}\cdots n_{i-1}\beta_{2}+\cdots+(n_{i-1}-1)\beta_{i-1}+\beta_{i}.

If
P_{0}=L,P_{1},\ldots,P_{g},P_{g+1}=A
are the marked points of
\Theta_{L}(A)
, with
P_{i}\preceq_{L}P_{i+1}
for
0\leq i\leq g
, then:

(14.9)

\beta_{0}={\mathbf{i}}_{L}(A),\;\;\;\beta_{i}={\mathbf{i}}_{L}(A)\mathbf{e}_{L}(P_{i}),\;\;\hbox{\rm for all $i\in\{1,\dots,g\}$}

and the relation (
14.8
) becomes

(14.10)

\overline{\beta}_{i}={\mathbf{i}}_{L}(A)\left(\mathbf{e}_{L}(P_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(\frac{{\mathbf{i}}_{L}(P_{i})}{{\mathbf{i}}_{L}(P_{j})}-\frac{{\mathbf{i}}_{L}(P_{i})}{{\mathbf{i}}_{L}(P_{j+1})}\right)\mathbf{e}_{L}(P_{j})\right),\;\;\hbox{\rm for all $i\in\{1,\dots,g\}$}.

This formula allows to compute the minimal generating sequence of
\Gamma(A)
directly from
(\Theta_{L}(A),\mathbf{i}_{L},\mathbf{e}_{L})
.

) and we recall a formula expressing the Milnor number of a plane branch in terms of its characteristic exponents (see Proposition
15.13
).

The following definition applies to any
abstract
reduced curve singularity (that is, a germ of a reduced complex analytic space of pure dimension one), not necessarily embeddable in a smooth germ of surface:

Definition 15.1
.

Let
A
be a reduced curve singularity. Consider a normalization morphism
\tilde{A}\to A
, where
\tilde{A}
is a finite disjoint union of smooth branches. It corresponds to an embedding
\mathcal{O}_{A}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{A}}
of the local ring of
A
into the multi-local ring of
\tilde{A}
. The
delta invariant

\boxed{\delta(A)}
of
A
is the dimension of the quotient complex vector space
\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{A}}/\mathcal{O}_{A}
.

When the reduced curve singularity is a plane one, there is a formula expressing its delta invariant in terms of its multiplicities at its infinitely near points in the sense of Definition
8.4
(see
[
14
, Theorem 3.11.12]
or
[
18
, Exercise 5.4.16]
):

Theorem 15.2
.

Let
(S,O)
be a smooth germ of surface and let
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
be a reduced plane curve singularity. Then:

\delta(A)=\sum_{P}\frac{e_{P}(A)\cdot(e_{P}(A)-1)}{2},

the sum being taken over the infinitely near points
P
of
O
belonging to the strict transforms of
A
.

Remark 15.3
.

In
[
57
]
, Stevens generalized this formula to arbitrary (not necessarily plane) curve singularities. In particular, in the Corollary on page 98 of his paper, he characterized plane curve singularities through the previous formula: if
A
is not planar, then
\delta(A)
is strictly less than the second member of the equality.

As may be deduced from Theorem
15.2
, the delta invariant of a reduced plane curve singularity with several branches is determined by the delta invariants of its branches and their mutual intersection numbers (see
[
14
, Corollary 3.11.13]
):

Theorem 15.4
.

Let
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
be a reduced plane curve singularity, whose branches are denoted
(A_{l})_{1\leq l\leq r}
. Then:

\delta(A)=\sum_{l=1}^{r}\delta(A_{l})+\sum_{l<m}(A_{l}\cdot A_{m})_{O}.

Remark 15.5
.

In
[
37
, Proposition 4]
, Hironaka generalized Theorem
15.4
to reduced curve singularities
of arbitrary embedding dimension
. Namely, if
A=A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{r}
is a decomposition of such a singularity into not necessarily irreducible singularities, pairwise without common branches, he proved that:

\delta(A)=\sum_{l=1}^{r}\delta(A_{l})+\sum_{l=1}^{r-1}A_{l}\cdot(A_{l+1}\cup\dots\cup A_{r}).

In this formula appears the following notion of
intersection number
of two germs
B
and
C
of reduced curves without common branches, contained in an ambient smooth germ
(M,O)
, in terms of their defining ideals
I_{B}
and
I_{C}
inside the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{M,O}
of
(M,O)
(see
[
37
, Definition 1]
):

B\cdot C:=\dim(\mathcal{O}_{M,O}/(I_{B}+I_{C})).

The
Milnor number
of a plane curve singularity is a particular case of the general notion defined by Milnor
[
45
]
for germs of complex algebraic hypersurfaces with isolated singularities. As for the multiplicity (see Definition
8.1
) one defines it first for elements of the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
:

Definition 15.6
.

Let
(S,O)
be a smooth germ of surface and
f\in\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
be a reduced germ. Consider a local coordinate system
(x,y)
of
(S,O)
. Let
(\partial_{x}f,\partial_{y}f)
be the ideal generated by the partial derivatives
\partial_{x}f,\partial_{y}f
in the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
. The
Milnor number

\boxed{\mu(f)}
of
f
is the dimension of the quotient

\mathcal{O}_{S,O}/(\partial_{x}f,\partial_{y}f)

seen as a vector space over the base field.

It is a direct consequence of the change of variables formulae that the
Jacobian ideal

\boxed{J(f)}:=(\partial_{x}f,\partial_{y}f)
of the ring
\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
is independent of the choice of local coordinates
(x,y)
. Therefore
\mu(f)
depends indeed only on
f
and not on those local coordinates. But one has a stronger invariance property:

Proposition 15.7
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
be reduced. The Milnor number
\mu(f)
depends only on the plane curve singularity defined by
f
.

Proof.

We explain an ingenious algebraic proof given by Płoski
[
49
, Property 2.2]
. His main idea was to use the identity

(15.1)

Z(f)\cdot Z(\partial_{y}f)=\mu(f)+Z(f)\cdot Z(x)-1

which is a special case of Teissier’s
[
59
, Proposition II.1.2]
. Here and in the rest of this proof, we write simply
A\cdot B
instead of
(A\cdot B)_{O}
.

Before proving equation (
15.1
), let us see how it implies the assertion of the proposition. Equation (
15.1
) shows that:

(15.2)

\mu(f)=Z(f)\cdot Z(\partial_{y}f)-Z(f)\cdot Z(x)+1=\dim\frac{\mathcal{O}_{S,O}}{(f,\partial_{y}f)}-\dim\frac{\mathcal{O}_{S,O}}{(f,x)}+1.

Consider a second defining function of
Z(f)
. It is of the form
uf
, where
u
is a unit of the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
, that is, a germ which does not vanish at
O
. We have the following equalities of ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{S,O}
:

\begin{array}[]{c}(uf,\partial_{y}(uf))=(uf,(\partial_{y}u)f+u(\partial_{y}f))=(f,(\partial_{y}u)f+u(\partial_{y}f))=(f,u(\partial_{y}f))=(f,(\partial_{y}f)),\\
(uf,x)=(f,x).\end{array}

Combining the resulting equalities of ideals with equation (
15.2
), we get the desired equality
\mu(uf)=\mu(f)
.

Let us prove now equation (
15.1
). The main idea is to write the divisor
Z(\partial_{y}f)
as a sum
\sum_{i\in I}A_{i}
of branches
A_{i}
and to parametrize each branch as
t\mapsto(x_{i}(t),y_{i}(t))
in order to get a normalization of it. If
h
is a series in the variable
t
, denote by
\nu_{t}(h)\in\mathbb{Z}
its order. Then:

\displaystyle Z(f)\cdot Z(\partial_{y}f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}Z(f)\cdot A_{i}=\sum_{i\in I}\nu_{t}(f(x_{i}(t),y_{i}(t)))=\sum_{i\in I}\left(1+\nu_{t}(\frac{d}{dt}f(x_{i}(t),y_{i}(t)))\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}\left(1+\nu_{t}((\partial_{x}f)(x_{i}(t),y_{i}(t))\frac{d}{dt}x_{i}(t))\right)=\sum_{i\in I}\nu_{t}((\partial_{x}f)(x_{i}(t),y_{i}(t))x_{i}(t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}\nu_{t}((\partial_{x}f)(x_{i}(t),y_{i}(t))+\sum_{i\in I}\nu_{t}(x_{i}(t))=\sum_{i\in I}Z(\partial_{x}f)\cdot A_{i}+\sum_{i\in I}Z(x)\cdot A_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z(\partial_{x}f)\cdot Z(\partial_{y}f)+Z(x)\cdot Z(\partial_{y}f)=\mu(f)+\nu_{y}((\partial_{y}f)(0,y))=\mu(f)+\nu_{y}(f(0,y))-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(f)+Z(f)\cdot Z(x)-1.

∎

Remark 15.8
.

The proof of Proposition
15.7
cannot be as easy as that of the independence of
\mu(f)
from the choice of local coordinate system for fixed
f
, because the Jacobian ideal
J(f):=(\partial_{x}f,\partial_{y}f)
may depend on the choice of defining function
f
. Assume for instance that
g:=(1+x)\cdot f
is a second such defining function. Therefore:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{x}g=f+(1+x)\cdot\partial_{x}f\\
\partial_{y}g=(1+x)\partial_{y}f\end{array}\right..

This shows that one has the equality
J(f)=J(g)
if and only if
f\in J(f)
. Zariski showed in
[
63
, Theorem 4]
that whenever
f
is irreducible, this happens if and only if the branch
Z(f)
is analytically equivalent to a branch of the form
Z(y^{n}-x^{m})
, for coprime positive integers
m
and
n
(a result which was generalized to arbitrary dimensions by Saito
[
56
]
). In particular, this never happens when
Z(f)
has more than one characteristic exponent.

The higher dimensional analog of Proposition

15.7
is also true and may be proved similarly. An alternative proof may be found in
[
18
, Corollary 6.4.10]
.

Proposition
15.7
allows to speak about the
Milnor number

\boxed{\mu(A)}
of a reduced plane curve singularity
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
.
The following theorem relates the delta-invariant and the Milnor number of a plane curve singularity. It was proved topologically by Milnor
[
45
, Theorem 10.5]
and extended in
[
10
, Proposition 1.2.1]
by Buchweitz and Greuel to reduced curve singularities of arbitrary embedding dimension:

Theorem 15.9
.

Let
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
be a reduced plane curve singularity. Then:

\mu(A)=2\delta(A)-r(A)+1,

where
\boxed{r(A)}
denotes the number of branches of
A
.

Corollary 15.10
.

Let
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
be a reduced plane curve singularity and let
\Lambda
be the lotus of an active constellation of crosses
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A}
adapted to it. Decorate its edges by the multiplicities of
A
at the infinitely near points of
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A}
, as explained in Corollary
8.6
. Then
\delta(A)
and
\mu(A)
may be computed from those multiplicities using the formulae of Theorems
15.2
and
15.9
.

Example 15.11
.

Consider the branch
A_{1}
which has a lotus decorated with the multiplicities of
A_{1}
at its infinitely near points as shown in Figure

. Using Theorem
15.2
, we get:

\delta(A_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(6\cdot 5+3\cdot 2+3\cdot 2+3\cdot 2\right)=24.

Therefore, by Theorem
15.9
we get:

\mu(A_{1})=2\delta(A_{1})-r(A_{1})+1=2\cdot 24-1+1=48.

Example 15.12
.

Consider the plane curve singularity
A:=A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}
which has a lotus decorated with the multiplicities of
A
at its infinitely near points as shown in Figure

15.2
, we get:

\delta(A)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(20\cdot 19+10\cdot 9+10\cdot 9+10\cdot 9+5\cdot 4+3\cdot 2+2\cdot 1\right)=339.

Therefore, by Theorem
15.9
we get:

\mu(A)=2\delta(A)-r(A)+1=2\cdot 339-3+1=676.

One may compute as follows the Milnor number of a
branch
from its characteristic exponents (see
[
65
, Chapitre II.3]
):

Proposition 15.13
.

Let
A\hookrightarrow(S,O)
be a plane branch. Then, with the notations (
14.1
)–(
14.7
), we have:

\mu(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{g}(n_{i}-1)\overline{\beta}_{i}-\beta_{0}+1=n_{g}\overline{\beta}_{g}-\beta_{g}-\beta_{0}+1.

Using (
14.9
) and (
14.10
), the formula of Proposition
15.13
may be rewritten as follows in terms of the Eggers-Wall tree
(\Theta_{L}(A),\mathbf{e}_{L},\mathbf{i}_{L})
:

(15.3)

\mu(A)={\mathbf{i}}_{L}(A)^{2}\left(\frac{\mathbf{e}_{L}(P_{g})}{{\mathbf{i}}_{L}(P_{g})}+\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}\left(\frac{1}{{\mathbf{i}}_{L}(P_{j})}-\frac{1}{{\mathbf{i}}_{L}(P_{j+1})}\right)\mathbf{e}_{L}(P_{j})\right)-{\mathbf{i}}_{L}(A)(\mathbf{e}_{L}(P_{g})+1)+1.

Example 15.14
.

Consider the Eggers-Wall tree obtained in Figure

15.13
, we get
\mu(A_{1})=3\cdot 22-13-6+1=48
.

We may proceed alternatively using formula (

):

\mu(A_{1})=6^{2}\left(\frac{13}{6}\cdot\frac{1}{2}+\left(\frac{1}{1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\cdot\frac{3}{2}\right)-6\left(\frac{13}{6}+1\right)+1=48.

Happily, we get the same result as in Example

.

16.
Extensions to algebraically closed fields of positive characteristic

In this section, we discuss extensions of the previous uses of lotuses as computational architectures to the case of a formal germ
A
defined by reduced non-zero element
of
\mathbb{K}[[x,y]]
, where
\mathbb{K}
is an algebraically closed field of characteristic
p>0
. The essential point is that in this new setting we can still consider the lotus
\Lambda(\hat{\mathcal{C}})
associated with an active constellation of crosses
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
. This fact allows us to introduce in Definition
16.2
a notion of Eggers-Wall tree of
A
even if some of the branches of
A
do not have Newton-Puiseux roots.

Let
A
be the formal plane curve singularity defined by a reduced and non-zero element of the maximal ideal
(x,y)\mathbb{K}[[x,y]]
of the local ring
\mathbb{K}[[x,y]]
. The notion of active constellation of crosses
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A}
adapted to
A
from Definition
4.10
may be defined in positive characteristic without any change. The determination of the weighted dual graph from the lateral boundary of the lotus explained in Proposition
6.2
remains unchanged.
We can still compute from the lotus
\Lambda(\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A})
the log-discrepancies and the orders of vanishing of the first axis as explained in Corollary
7.9
and the multiplicities of the strict transforms of the branches of
A
as explained in Corollary
8.6
. The intersection multiplicities of pairs of branches of
A
and the orders of vanishing of the branches of
A
along the exceptional divisors corresponding to active points of the constellation may still be determined from the lotus
\Lambda(\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A})
as described in Corollaries
9.3
and
9.6
.

The notion of Eggers-Wall tree is subtler in positive characteristic, due to the absence or the different behaviour of associated Newton-Puiseux series when they exist.

Example 16.1
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic
\boxed{p}>0
. As explained by Kedlaya in
[
40
, Section 1]
, Chevalley
[
16
, Page 64]
showed that the Artin-Schreier polynomial

\boxed{g_{p}(y)}:=y^{p}-y-x^{-1}\in\mathbb{K}((x))[y]

has no root in the fraction field
\mathbb{K}((x^{1/\mathbb{N}}))
of the ring
\mathbb{K}[[x^{1/\mathbb{N}}]]
of formal Newton-Puiseux series in
x
. Later, Abhyankar
[
1
, Section 2]
noticed that the roots of
g_{p}(y)
are of the form

\xi_{j}:=j\ +\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}x^{-\frac{1}{p^{r}}},

for
j
varying in the prime field
\mathbb{F}_{p}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{K}
. Notice that the series
\xi_{j}
are not Newton-Puiseux series, since their exponents have not bounded denominators. For an example in
\mathbb{K}[[x]][y]
, set

\boxed{f_{p}(y)}:=x^{p}g_{p}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)=y^{p}-x^{p-1}y-x^{p-1}.

Using the relation between
f_{p}(y)
and
g_{p}(y)
we see that the roots of
f_{p}(y)
are

\eta_{j}:=x\cdot\xi_{j}=jx\ +\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}x^{1-\frac{1}{p^{r}}}.

Therefore,
f_{p}(y)
has no Newton-Puiseux root, similarly to
g_{p}(y)
. Another change of variable was performed by Abhyankar
[
1
, Section 2]
, in order to get the example

\boxed{h_{p}(y)}:=y^{p}-x^{p-1}y-1

without Newton-Puiseux roots. Let us mention that Kedlaya described in
[
40
]
and
[
41
]
the algebraic closure of the fraction field of the ring
\mathbb{K}[[x]]
in terms of generalized power series, when
\mathbb{K}
is a field of positive characteristic.

We will characterize in Corollary

17.7
below those monic irreducible polynomials in the ring
\mathbb{K}[[x]][y]
which admit Newton-Puiseux roots in
x
.

Because, as shown in Example
16.1
, Newton-Puiseux series do not necessarily exist expressing the roots of the elements of
\mathbb{K}[[x]][y]
, it is more convenient to define the Eggers-Wall tree directly from the lotus, without passing through the consideration of Newton-Puiseux series:

Definition 16.2
.

Consider a reduced formal germ
A
defined by a power series
f\in(x,y)\mathbb{K}[[x,y]]\smallsetminus\{0\}
. Take any finite constellation of crosses
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A}
adapted to
A
. Denote as before
\boxed{X_{O}}:=L+L_{1}
and by
\boxed{\hat{A}}
the
completion of
A
determined by
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A}
, in the sense of Definition
4.10
. Consider the associated lotus
\Lambda(\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A})
, in the sense of Definition
5.2
. The
Eggers-Wall tree

\boxed{\Theta_{L}(\hat{A})}
, endowed with the
index function

\boxed{\mathbf{i}_{L}}
and the
exponent function

\boxed{\mathbf{e}_{L}}
, is the tree rooted at
L
obtained by the construction performed on the lotus
\Lambda(\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{A})
using Definition
10.11
. Therefore,
\Theta_{L}(A)
is the union of segments
[LA_{l}]
joining
L
with the branches
A_{l}
of
A
. The
characteristic exponents
of a branch
A_{l}
of
A
are the values of the exponent function
\mathbf{e}_{L}
on the points of discontinuity of the index function
\mathbf{i}_{L}
in restriction to the segment
[LA_{l}]
. The
contact complexity function

\boxed{\mathbf{c}_{L}}
is defined from

and
\mathbf{e}_{L}

13.1
.

Since the intersection numbers of pairs of branches of

(16.1)

U_{L}(A_{l},A_{m})=\frac{1}{\mathbf{c}_{L}(\langle L,A_{l},A_{m}\rangle)}.

This implies that
\Theta_{L}(A)
is equal to the unique end-rooted tree associated with the ultrametric distance

defined on the set of

different from

(see
[

).

Remark 16.4
.

If
f\in\mathbb{K}[[x,y]]
is irreducible and
L:=Z(x)
is transversal to the branch
A
defined by
f
, then we can also determine the Eggers-Wall tree
\Theta_{L}(A)
using the minimal generating sequence of the semigroup of
A
. Notice that we can always find a
good parametrization
of
A
thanks to the
Normalisation theorem
(see
[
50
, Theorem 2.1]
). Now, from this good parametrization we can construct key polynomials for
A
and obtain the minimal generating sequence of the semigroup
\Gamma(A)
(see for example
[
29
]
). One may define the
characteristic exponents
of
A
to be equal to the characteristic exponents of a complex branch
A^{\prime}
such that
\Gamma(A)=\Gamma(A^{\prime})
. The multiplicity sequence of the infinitely near points of the strict transforms of a branch
A
by the successive blowups in its resolution process is known to be equivalent to the sequence of characteristic exponents of a Newton-Puiseux series associated to
A
, whenever the field
\mathbb{K}
is of characteristic zero. When
\mathbb{K}
is of positive characteristic, the characteristic exponents of
A
, or equivalently the characteristic pairs of
A
, were defined by Moh
[
46
]
and Angermüller
[
5
]
as the result obtained from the multiplicity sequence by the numerical algorithm used in the case of characteristic zero. A different approach valid in arbitrary characteristic was given in terms of
Newton coefficients
, defined in terms of Newton polygons and maximal contact curves by Lejeune-Jalabert in her PhD Thesis and was published in
[
42
]
. A systematic presentation of the equisingularity theory of algebroid branches defined over an algebraically closed field of arbitrary characteristic was given in Campillo’s book
[
11
]
, by using
Hamburguer-Noether expansions
. It included a comparison with the previous approaches in the literature. The Eggers-Wall tree
\Theta_{L}(A)
of a branch
A
relative to a transversal smooth branch
L
in the sense of Definition
16.2
gives rise to the same characteristic exponents as in the previous approaches, because the computation of the multiplicity sequence from the lotus explained in Section
8
is independent of the characteristic. Since the smooth branch
L
is transversal to
A
, the Eggers-Wall tree
\Theta_{L}(A)
is the same as the generic Eggers-Wall tree of a complex branch
A^{\prime}

10.5
).

If

is transversal to all of them,
then we can use Remark

of

A_{l}
and
A_{m}

from the intersection multiplicities

,
(L\cdot A_{m})_{O}
and
(A_{l}\cdot A_{m})_{O}
by the tripod formula (

):

\mathbf{c}_{L}(\langle L,A_{l},A_{m}\rangle)=\frac{(A_{l}\cdot A_{m})_{O}}{(A_{l}\cdot L)_{O}(A_{m}\cdot L)_{O}}.

This determines the bifurcation point
\langle L,A_{l},A_{m}\rangle
on the tree
\Theta_{L}(A_{l}+A_{m})

(
13.2
) to find the value

is determined by applying this process for any pair of branches of

17.
Positive characteristic singularities with Newton-Puiseux roots

As in the previous section, we assume that
\mathbb{K}
is an algebraically closed field of characteristic
\boxed{p}>0
. By analogy with the case of characteristic zero considered in Section
10
, we may introduce the local ring

\boxed{\mathbb{K}[[x^{1/\mathbb{N}}]]}:=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{K}[[x^{1/n}]]

of
formal Newton-Puiseux series with coefficients in the field
\mathbb{K}
.
In this section we characterize the monic irreducible polynomials in the ring
\mathbb{K}[[x]][y]
which admit Newton-Puiseux roots in
x
(see Corollary
17.7
).
Then, we introduce a version of Eggers-Wall tree for a plane curve singularity
A
whose branches have associated Newton-Puiseux series (see Definition
17.9
) and we compare it with the Eggers-Wall tree of Definition
16.2
(see Corollary
17.16
).

Let us define first the
characteristic exponents
of a series
\xi\in\mathbb{K}[[x^{1/\mathbb{N}}]]
. In the case of positive characteristic, we cannot proceed as in the characteristic zero Definition
10.1
.
Instead, we define them
by looking at the jumps of the minimal denominators of fractional exponents in the expansion of
\xi
(compare with formulae (
14.1
)–(
14.7
)).

Definition 17.1
.

Let
\xi=\sum_{j}c_{j}x^{\frac{j}{n}}\in\mathbb{K}[[x^{1/n}]]\smallsetminus\{0\}
, where
n
is the minimal common denominator of the exponents appearing in the expansion of
\xi
(that is, of the elements of the support of
\xi
). We set
\boxed{e_{0}}:=n
,
\boxed{b_{1}}:=\min\lx@nobreakspace\{j:c_{j}\neq 0,n\nmid j\}
and
\boxed{e_{1}}:=\mathrm{gcd}\{e_{0},b_{1}\}
. Assume that
b_{r}
and
e_{r}
are defined and that
e_{r}\neq 1
. Define then
\boxed{b_{r+1}}:=\min\lx@nobreakspace\{j:c_{j}\neq 0,e_{r}\nmid j\}
and
\boxed{e_{r+1}}:=\mathrm{gcd}\{e_{r},b_{r+1}\}
. Since
n
is the minimal common denominator of the fractional exponents of
\xi
, there is an integer
h\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
e_{h}=1
and the process stops. The
set of characteristic exponents
of the series
\xi
is then:

(17.1)

\boxed{\mathrm{Ch}(\xi)}:=\left\{\frac{b_{i}}{n}:i=1,\dots,h\right\}.

Set
\boxed{n_{j}}:=e_{j-1}/e_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,h
. We denote by convenience
\boxed{b_{0}}:=0
and
\boxed{b_{h+1}}:=\infty
. The positive integer
\boxed{m_{j}}
is then defined by the equality
\frac{b_{j}}{n}=\frac{m_{j}}{n_{1}\cdots n_{j}}
, for every
j\in\{1,\dots,h\}
.

It is a consequence of the previous definitions that
m_{j}
and
n_{j}
are coprime, for every
j\in\{1,\dots,h\}
.

Remark 17.2
.

If
\xi\in\mathbb{K}[[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]]
is a Newton-Puiseux root of a formal branch
A
and if
L
is the smooth germ defined by
x
, then the characteristic exponents of
\xi
according to Definition

17.1
do not always coincide with the characteristic exponents of
A
with respect to
L
from Definition
16.2
. For instance, Campillo considered in
[
11
, Example 3.5.4]
the branch
A
with Newton-Puiseux root

\xi=x^{\frac{p+1}{p}}+x^{\frac{p^{3}+p^{2}+p+1}{p^{3}}}\in\mathbb{K}[[x^{\frac{1}{p^{3}}}]]

and computed data equivalent to the minimal set of generators of the semigroup
\Gamma(A)
:

\overline{\beta}_{0}=p^{3},\quad\overline{\beta}_{1}=p^{3}+p^{2},\quad\overline{\beta}_{2}=p^{4}+p^{3}+p^{2}+p,\quad\overline{\beta}_{3}=p^{5}+p^{4}+p^{3}+p^{2}+p+1.

It follows that the series
\xi
has two characteristic exponents according to Definition
17.1
, while the branch
A
has three characteristic exponents according to Definition
16.2
. Teissier mentioned this example in
[
60
, Section 1]
and studied this phenomenon in terms of an
overweight deformation
of the monomial curve defined by the semigroup
\Gamma(A)
. Similarly, in the case of a formal germ with branches
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
admitting Newton-Puiseux roots, the exponent of the ramification point of the tree
\Theta_{L}(A_{1}+A_{2})
of Definition
16.2
may be different from the order of coincidence
k_{L}(A_{1},A_{2})
of the Newton-Puiseux roots
of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
of Definition
17.9
(see also Example
17.17
).

We need the following elementary number-theoretical definition:

Definition 17.3
.

We denote by
\boxed{\nu_{p}}
the
p
-adic valuation
on the ring
\mathbb{Z}
of integers. That is, if
n
is a nonzero integer, then
\nu_{p}(n)
is the largest power of
p
which divides
n
. We denote by

\boxed{{n[:p]}}:=n\cdot p^{-\nu_{p}(n)}\in\mathbb{Z}

the
part of
n
coprime to
p
.

By construction,
n[:p]\not\equiv 0
(mod
p
). Notice that if
n
and
m
are nonzero integers, then:

(17.2)

(nm)[:p]=n[:p]\cdot m[:p].

For every
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, denote by
\boxed{\mathbb{U}_{n}}\subset\mathbb{K}^{*}
the multiplicative group of
n
th roots of unity of
\mathbb{K}
. Its order depends on the characteristic
p
of
\mathbb{K}
:

Lemma 17.4
.

Consider
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.

(1)

The group
\mathbb{U}_{n}
has order
n[:p]
and
\mathbb{U}_{n}=\mathbb{U}_{n[:p]}
.

(2)

The extension
\mathbb{K}((x))\subset\mathbb{K}((x))[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]
is Galois if and only if
n=n[:p]
, that is,
p
does not divide
n
.

Proof.

Notice that
x^{n}-1=(x^{n[:p]}-1)^{p^{\nu_{p}(n)}}
and that the polynomial
x^{n[:p]}-1
has
n[:p]
different roots in
\mathbb{K}^{*}
, since
p
does not divide
n[:p]
. This proves the first assertion.

Any element of the automorphism group of the field extension

\mathbb{K}((x))\subset\mathbb{K}((x))[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]

is determined by the image of
x^{\frac{1}{n}}
, which is of the form
\rho\cdot x^{\frac{1}{n}}
, for some
\rho\in\mathbb{U}_{n}
. This automorphism group is therefore of order
n[:p]
. The field extension is Galois if and only if this order is equal to the degree
n
of the extension, that is, if and only if
n[:p]=n
.
∎

Proposition 17.5
.

Assume that
\xi\in\mathbb{K}[[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]]\smallsetminus\{0\}
and that
n
is the minimal common denominator of the exponents of
\xi
. Then:

(1)

There are exactly
n[:p]
different conjugates of
\xi
under the action of the group
\mathbb{U}_{n}
.

(2)

The minimal polynomial
f
of
\xi
over
\mathbb{K}((x))
belongs to
\mathbb{K}[[x]][y]
and has degree
n
.

(3)

\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi]=\mathbb{K}((x))[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]
.

(4)

The series
\xi
is a root of multiplicity
p^{\nu_{p}(n)}
of the polynomial
f
.

Proof.

(
1
)
In order to prove the first assertion, it is enough to verify that if
\rho\in\mathbb{U}_{n}
is such that
\xi(x^{\frac{1}{n}})=\xi(\rho\cdot x^{\frac{1}{n}})
, then
\rho=1
. Consider the set
{\mathrm{Ch}}(\xi):=\left\{\frac{b_{i}}{n}:i=1,\dots,h\right\}
of characteristic exponents of
\xi
(see Definition
17.1
). We must have
x^{\frac{b_{i}}{n}}=(\rho\cdot x^{\frac{1}{n}})^{b_{i}}
, therefore
\rho^{b_{i}}=1
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,h\}
. Since
n
and
b_{1},\dots,b_{h}
are coprime, there exist
a_{0},\dots,a_{h}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that one has the Bézout identity:

a_{0}\cdot n+a_{1}\cdot b_{1}+\cdots+a_{h}\cdot b_{h}=1.

Therefore:

\rho=\rho^{a_{0}\cdot n+a_{1}\cdot b_{1}+\cdots+a_{h}\cdot b_{h}}=(\rho^{n})^{a_{0}}\cdot(\rho^{b_{1}})^{a_{1}}\cdots(\rho^{b_{h}})^{a_{h}}=1.

(
2
)
Let us prove the second assertion. Denote
\xi=\sum_{j}c_{j}x^{\frac{j}{n}}
. Set
q:=p^{\nu_{p}(n)}
. As
q
is a power of the characteristic
p
of
\mathbb{K}
, we get:

(17.3)

\xi^{q}=\sum_{j}c_{j}^{q}\ x^{\frac{jq}{n}}=\sum_{j}c_{j}^{q}\ x^{\frac{j}{n[:p]}}=\tau(x^{\frac{1}{n[:p]}}),

where

\tau(t):=\sum_{j}c_{j}^{q}\ t^{j}\in\mathbb{K}[[t]].

The hypothesis that
n
is the minimal common denominator of the exponents of
\xi
implies that
n[:p]
is the minimal common denominator of the exponents of
\xi^{q}
. By Lemma
17.4
, the extension

(17.4)

\mathbb{K}((x))\subset\mathbb{K}((x))[x^{\frac{1}{n[:p]}}]

is Galois of degree
n[:p]
. By the first assertion of the present proposition applied to
\xi^{q}=\tau(x^{\frac{1}{n[:p]}})
, there are exactly
(n[:p])[:p]=n[:p]
different conjugates of
\xi^{q}
under the action of
\mathbb{U}_{n[:p]}
. The polynomial

(17.5)

g(y):=\prod_{\rho\in\mathbb{U}_{n[:p]}}(y-\tau(\rho\cdot x^{\frac{1}{n[:p]}}))\in\mathbb{K}[[x^{\frac{1}{n[:p]}}]][y]

belongs to
\mathbb{K}[[x]][y]
, since its coefficients are invariant by the action of the Galois group
\mathbb{U}_{n[:p]}
of the extension (
17.4
). Since
\xi^{q}
belongs to
\mathbb{K}((x))[x^{\frac{1}{n[:p]}}]
and the extension (
17.4
) is Galois, it follows that
g(y)
is the minimal polynomial of
\xi^{q}
over the field
\mathbb{K}((x))
. This implies that

(17.6)

\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi^{q}]=\mathbb{K}((x))[x^{\frac{1}{n[:p]}}]

and

(17.7)

[\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi^{q}]:\mathbb{K}((x))]=n[:p].

Hence, if
p
does not divide
n
, the statement (
2
) is proved.

Therefore, in what follows we assume that
p
divides
n
.

We claim that the series
\xi^{q}
has no
p
-th root
\gamma
in the ring
\mathbb{K}((x))[x^{\frac{1}{n[:p]}}]
. If this were the case, writing
\gamma=\sum_{j}d_{j}\ x^{\frac{j}{n[:p]}}
, we obtain that
\gamma^{p}=\sum d_{j}^{p}\ x^{\frac{jp}{n[:p]}}=\xi^{q}
. In particular, the support of the series
\xi^{q}
is contained in
\frac{p}{n[:p]}\mathbb{Z}
. It follows that the support of the series
\xi
is contained in
\frac{p}{q\cdot n[:p]}\mathbb{Z}=\frac{1}{p^{\nu_{p}(n)-1}\cdot n[:p]}\mathbb{Z}
. This would contradict the hypothesis that
n=p^{\nu_{p}(n)}\cdot n[:p]
is the minimal denominator of the exponents of
\xi
.

Combining the previous claim and equality (
17.6
), we see that the series
\xi^{q}
has no
p
-th root in the field
\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi^{q}]
. Since
q
is a power of
p
, this implies that the polynomial
y^{q}-\xi^{q}\in\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi^{q}][y]
is irreducible (see
[
64
, Ch.II, §5, Th.7]
). This means that
y^{q}-\xi^{q}
is the minimal polynomial of
\xi
over
\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi^{q}]
. Thus:

(17.8)

[\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi]:\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi^{q}]]=q.

Then, the tower formula for the extensions

\mathbb{K}((x))\subset\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi^{q}]\subset\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi],

and the equations (
17.7
) and (
17.8
) imply that

[\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi]:\mathbb{K}((x))]=[\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi^{q}]:\mathbb{K}((x))]\cdot[\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi]:\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi^{q}]]=n[:p]\cdot q=n.

As a consequence, the minimal polynomial
f
of
\xi
over
\mathbb{K}((x))
has degree
n
.

In order to get the whole assertion (
2
), it remains to prove that
f\in K[[x]][y]
. We set
G(y):=g(y^{q})\in\mathbb{K}[[x]][y]
. Notice that
G(\xi)=g(\xi^{q})=0
, by equations (
17.3
) and (
17.5
). Since
f
and
G
are monic and have the same degree, this implies that
f=G
, therefore
f\in\mathbb{K}[[x]][y]
.

(
3
)
In order to prove the third assertion, notice that
\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi]
and
\mathbb{K}((x))[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]
are extensions of degree
n
of
\mathbb{K}((x))
.
Since
\xi\in\mathbb{K}((x))[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]
,
we deduce that
\mathbb{K}((x))[\xi]=\mathbb{K}((x))[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]
.

(
4
)
The last assertion is a consequence of the first and the second one.
∎

By Definition
17.1
, all the Newton-Puiseux roots of the irreducible polynomial
f
in Proposition
17.5
have the same characteristic exponents. This holds because those roots are conjugated under the action of
\mathbb{U}_{n}
, which implies that they are power series with the same support.

Remark 17.6
.

We speak here of the characteristic exponents of a Newton-Puiseux series
\xi
using Definition
17.1
. They do not coincide in general with the characteristic exponents of the branch
A
admitting
\xi
as a Newton-Puiseux root, which are those obtained from the Eggers-Wall tree
\Theta_{L}(A)
of Definition
16.2
(see Example
17.17
below).

The following consequence of Proposition
17.5
provides a characterization of the polynomials in
\mathbb{K}[[x]][y]
which have a root in the ring
\mathbb{K}[[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]]
of Newton-Puiseux series. One may compare it to the characterization given in
[
11
, Remark 2.1.11]
in terms of the order of inseparability of a field extension (see
[
64
, Chapter 2, §16]
).

Corollary 17.7
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{K}[[x]][y]
be a monic irreducible polynomial of degree
n\geq 1
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

The polynomial
f
has a root in the ring
\mathbb{K}[[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]]
of formal Newton-Puiseux series.

(2)

The polynomial
f
belongs to the ring
\mathbb{K}[[x]][y^{q}]
, where
q:=p^{\nu_{p}(n)}
.

Proof.

The implication (
1
)
\Longrightarrow
(
2
) is a consequence of the proof of Proposition
17.5
.

Let us prove the implication (
2
)
\Longrightarrow
(
1
). By hypothesis, there exists a monic polynomial
G\in\mathbb{K}[[x]][y]
such that
f(y)=G(y^{q})
. Notice that
G
is irreducible, as
f
is irreducible. As the degree
n[:p]
of
G
is not divisible by
p
,

[
50
, Corollary 2.4]

implies that there exists a Newton-Puiseux series
\tau=\sum_{j}a_{j}\ x^{\frac{j}{n[:p]}}\in\mathbb{K}[[x^{\frac{1}{n[:p]}}]]
such that
G(\tau)=0
. Since
\mathbb{K}
is an algebraically closed field, for every
j\geq 1
there exists
b_{j}\in\mathbb{K}
such that
b_{j}^{q}=a_{j}
. Consider the series

\xi:=\sum_{j}b_{j}x^{\frac{j}{q\cdot n[:p]}}\in\mathbb{K}[[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]].

As
q
is a power of
p
, one has
\xi^{q}=\sum_{j}b_{j}^{q}\ x^{\frac{j}{n[:p]}}=\tau
. Therefore:

f(\xi)=G(\xi^{q})=G(\tau)=0.

∎

Corollary
17.7
is a generalization of
[
50
, Corollary 2.4]
, where it is proven that a monic irreducible polynomial
f\in\mathbb{K}[[x]][y]
of degree
n
coprime to
p
has Newton-Puiseux roots satisfying (
10.1
), the set (
10.2
) has
n
elements and we have a Galois extension, as in the case of a base field of characteristic zero. This is related to the fact that if
n
is coprime to the characteristic
p
of the algebraically closed field
\mathbb{K}
, then the polynomial
x^{n}-1
has
n
pairwise distinct roots in
\mathbb{K}
.

Example 17.8
.

Using Corollary
17.7
, we deduce that the polynomials
f_{p}
and
h_{p}
considered in Example
16.1
do not have roots in the ring
\mathbb{K}[[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]]
of formal Newton-Puiseux series in
x
.

Next we introduce a version
\Theta_{L}(A)[:p]
of Eggers-Wall tree based on Definition
10.3
, when all the branches of the reduced plane curve singularity
A
admit Newton-Puiseux roots. Then we compare it to the tree
\Theta_{L}(A)
introduced in Definition

starting from the lotus.

Definition 17.9
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic

and

. Denote by

the branch

Assume that all the irreducible factors of

admit Newton-Puiseux roots
.

\bullet
The

\boxed{\Theta_{L}(A_{l})[:p]}
of a branch

of

is a compact segment endowed with a homeomorphism
\boxed{\mathbf{e}_{L}[:p]}:\Theta_{L}(A_{l})[:p]\to[0,\infty]
called the
exponent function
, and with

, which are the preimages by the exponent function of the characteristic exponents of any Newton-Puiseux root of
A_{l}
relative to
L
. The point
(\mathbf{e}_{L}[:p])^{-1}(0)
is
labeled by
L
and
(\mathbf{e}_{L}[:p])^{-1}(\infty)
is
labeled by

. The tree
\Theta_{L}(A_{l})[:p]
is also endowed with the

\boxed{\mathbf{i}_{L}^{(a)}}:\Theta_{L}(A_{l})[:p]\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, which is defined exactly as the index function in Definition
10.3
by considering the variation of the smallest denominators of the characteristic exponents. The

\boxed{\mathbf{i}_{L}[:p]}:\Theta_{L}(A_{l})[:p]\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
is specified using Definition

by:

\mathbf{i}_{L}[:p](P):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}(\mathbf{i}_{L}^{(a)}(P))[:p]&if&P\neq A_{l},\\
(L\cdot A_{l})_{O}&if&P=A_{l}.\end{array}\right.

\bullet

The
Eggers-Wall tree

\boxed{\Theta_{L}(L)[:p]}
is a point labeled by
L
, at which
\mathbf{e}_{L}[:p](L)=0
and
\mathbf{i}_{L}{[:p]}(L)=1
.

\bullet

of

. The

is defined as in the case of characteristic zero, by comparing the Newton-Puiseux roots of the branches (see Definition
10.1
). The
exponent function

\boxed{\mathbf{e}_{L}[:p]}:\Theta_{L}(A)[:p]\to[0,\infty]
and the

\boxed{\mathbf{i}_{L}[:p]}:\Theta_{L}(A)[:p]\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}

\Theta_{L}(A_{l})[:p]
, for

marked points
and the

.

The following proposition is crucial in order to understand the properties of the index function
\mathbf{i}_{L}[:p]
. It is analogous to the description of the index function in the case of characteristic zero given in Remark
10.6
.

Proposition 17.10
.

Let
\xi=\sum_{j}c_{j}x^{\frac{j}{n}}\in\mathbb{K}[[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]]
be a Newton-Puiseux series associated to a branch
A
. Assume that
n
is the least common denominator of the exponents of
\xi
. Take
P\in(LA)
and set
\beta:=\mathbf{e}_{L}[:p](P)\in(0,\infty)
. Consider the truncation

\xi_{P}:=\sum_{\frac{j}{n}<\beta}c_{j}x^{\frac{j}{n}}\in\mathbb{K}[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]

of the series
\xi
. Then,
\mathbf{i}_{L}[:p](P)
is equal to the number of different conjugates of
\xi_{P}
under the action of
\mathbb{U}_{n}
.

Proof.

Consider the characteristic exponents
\frac{b_{1}}{n},\dots,\frac{b_{h}}{n}
of the series
\xi
(see Definition
17.1
). Let
i\in\{0,\dots,h\}
be the unique integer such that
\frac{b_{i}}{n}\leq\beta<\frac{b_{i+1}}{n}
, where we denote by convenience
b_{0}:=0
and
b_{h+1}:=\infty
. Set
m:=\mathbf{i}_{L}^{(a)}(P)
. Definition
17.9
implies that
\xi_{P}\in\mathbb{K}[[x^{\frac{1}{m}}]]
and
m
is the minimal common denominator of the exponents of
\xi_{P}
. In particular,
m
divides
n
. By Proposition
17.5
, there are exactly
m[:p]
different conjugates of
\xi_{P}
in
\mathbb{K}[[x^{\frac{1}{m}}]]
, which are the different roots of the minimal polynomial of
\xi
over
\mathbb{K}((x))
. The proposition follows, since
m[:p]=\mathbf{i}_{L}[:p](P)
and
m
divides
n
, which implies that
\mathbb{U}_{m}\subset\mathbb{U}_{n}
.
∎

Remark 17.11
.

Let
A
be a branch which has a Newton-Puiseux root
\xi\in\mathbb{K}[[x^{1/\mathbb{N}}]]
. Notice that the index function
\mathbf{i}_{L}[:p]
may be continuous at a marked point
P
of
\Theta_{L}(A)[:p]
and the end
A
of
\Theta_{L}(A)[:p]
is a point of discontinuity of
\mathbf{i}_{L}[:p]
if

divides
(L\cdot A)_{O}
(see Example
17.17
). These phenomena cannot happen for the index function in the case of characteristic zero.

Next, we define a contact complexity function on the tree
\Theta_{L}(A)[:p]
in the same way as we did in the case of characteristic zero (compare with Definition
13.1
).

Definition 17.12
.

The
contact complexity
function
{\mathbf{c}}_{L}[:p]:\Theta_{L}(A)[:p]\to[0,\infty]
is defined at every point
P\in\Theta_{L}(A)[:p]
by the formula

(17.9)

\boxed{{\mathbf{c}}_{L}[:p](P)}:=\int_{L}^{P}\frac{d\>{\mathbf{e}}_{L}[:p]}{{\mathbf{i}}_{L}[:p]}.

One may express the value of the integral (
17.9
) in terms of the characteristic exponents of any branch
A_{l}
of
A
such that
P\in(LA_{l}]
. Denote by
\xi\in\mathbb{K}[[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]]
a Newton-Puiseux series associated to

A_{l}
, where we assume that
n
is minimal. We use the notations of Definition
17.1
for the characteristic exponents of
\xi
. Define
\boxed{P_{j}}:=\mathbf{e}_{L}^{-1}[:p](b_{j}/n)
, for each
j\in\{0,\dots,h+1\}
. The index function
{\mathbf{i}}_{L}[:p]
has value
(n_{1}\cdots n_{j})[:p]
in the half-open segment
(P_{j}P_{j+1}]
. Denote by
r\in\{0,\dots,h\}
the unique integer such that
P\in(P_{r}P_{r+1}]
and
\alpha=\mathbf{e}_{L}[:p](P)
.

Formula (
17.9
) implies that:

(17.10)

{\mathbf{c}}_{L}[:p](P)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{1}{(n_{1}\dots n_{j-1})[:p]}\frac{b_{j}-b_{j-1}}{n}\right)+\frac{1}{(n_{1}\dots n_{r})[:p]}\left(\alpha-\frac{b_{r}}{n}\right).

In the next two lemmas, we use the notations of Definition
17.1
for the characteristic exponents of the Newton-Puiseux series
\xi
and the notion of order of coincidence from Definition
10.1
.

Lemma 17.13
.

Let
\xi\in\mathbb{K}[[x^{\frac{1}{n}}]]
be a Newton-Puiseux series. Assume that
n
is the least common denominator of the exponents of
\xi
. Let
\zeta\in\mathbb{K}[[t]]
be such that
\xi=\zeta(x^{\frac{1}{n}})
. Then, for every
j\in\{1,\dots,h\}
, we have:

(1)

\#\{\rho\in\mathbb{U}_{n}:\nu_{x}({\zeta}(\rho\cdot x^{\frac{1}{n}})-{\zeta}(x^{\frac{1}{n}}))\geq\frac{b_{j}}{n}\}=e_{j-1}[:p]
,

(2)

\#\{\rho\in\mathbb{U}_{n}:\nu_{x}({\zeta}(\rho\cdot x^{\frac{1}{n}})-{\zeta}(x^{\frac{1}{n}}))=\frac{b_{j}}{n}\}=e_{j-1}[:p]-e_{j}[:p]
.

Proof.

For each
j\in\{1,\dots,h\}
, the number of different coefficients of the term
x^{\frac{b_{j}}{n}}
in the series
{\zeta}(\rho\cdot x^{\frac{1}{n}})
, when
\rho
varies among the elements of the group
\mathbb{U}_{n}
, is equal to
n_{j}[:p]
.

If
j=1
, then the equality in Formula (
1
) is clear, as Proposition
17.5
implies that
e_{0}[:p]=n[:p]
is equal to the number of different conjugates of
{\zeta}(x^{\frac{1}{n}})
by the action of
\mathbb{U}_{n}
. Next, take
1<j\leq h
. If
\rho\in\mathbb{U}_{n}
and if
\nu_{x}({\zeta}(\rho\cdot x^{\frac{1}{n}})-{\zeta}(x^{\frac{1}{n}}))\geq\frac{b_{j}}{n}
, then the coefficients of the terms
x^{\frac{b_{1}}{n}},\dots,x^{\frac{b_{j-1}}{n}}
in the series
{\zeta}(x^{\frac{1}{n}})
and
{\zeta}(\rho\cdot x^{\frac{1}{n}})
coincide. This implies that the number of elements in the set defined by the left-hand side of Formula (
1
) is equal to
e_{0}[:p]/(n_{1}[:p]\cdots n_{j-1}[:p])=e_{j-1}[:p]
, an equality which follows from equality (
17.2
) and the notations of Definition
17.1
.

Finally, Formula (
2
) is a consequence of Formula (
1
).
∎

Lemma 17.14
.

Let
f,g\in\mathbb{K}[[x]][y]
be two distinct monic irreducible polynomials. Assume that
f
and
g
have Newton-Puiseux roots
\xi
and
\tau
, respectively. Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be the least common denominator of the exponents of
\xi
.
Denote by
\alpha
the order of coincidence of the branches
Z(f)
and
Z(g)
relative to
L=Z(x)
. Let
r\in\{0,\dots,h\}
be the unique integer such that
\frac{b_{r}}{n}<\alpha\leq\frac{b_{r+1}}{n}
. Then, we have:

(1)

\#\{\eta\in\mathcal{Z}_{x}(f):\nu_{x}(\eta-\tau)=\frac{b_{j}}{n}\}=e_{j-1}[:p]-e_{j}[:p]
, for all
j\in\{1,\dots,r\}
.

(2)

\#\{\eta\in\mathcal{Z}_{x}(f):\nu_{x}(\eta-\tau)=\alpha\}=e_{r}[:p]
.

Proof.

Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be the least common denominator of the exponents of
\tau
.
Since the roots of
g
are conjugated under the action of
\mathbb{U}_{m}
, we get that

(17.11)

\alpha=\max\{\nu_{x}(\eta-\tau):\eta\in\mathcal{Z}_{x}(f)\}.

Fix now
j\in\{1,\dots,r\}
.
As in Lemma
17.13
, let
\zeta\in\mathbb{K}[[t]]
be such that
\xi=\zeta(x^{\frac{1}{n}})
. The roots of
f
being conjugated under the action of
\mathbb{U}_{n}
, the equality (
17.11
) for the order of coincidence implies that

\displaystyle\#\left\{\eta\in Z_{x}(f):\nu_{x}(\eta-\tau)=\frac{b_{j}}{n}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\#\left\{\rho\in\mathbb{U}_{n}:\nu_{x}({\zeta}(x^{\frac{1}{n}})-{\zeta}(\rho\cdot x^{\frac{1}{n}}))=\frac{b_{j}}{n}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{j-1}[:p]-e_{j}[:p],

where the last equality is a consequence of Lemma
17.13
. Equality (
1
) is proved.

Equality (
2
) results from the fact that the set whose cardinal is to be computed is the complement of the disjoint union of the sets whose cardinals were computed at point (
1
), and from the fact that the total number of roots of
f
is equal to
n[:p]=e_{0}[:p]
.
∎

The following theorem shows that an analog of the
tripod formula
of Theorem
13.4
holds on the Newton-Puiseux-Eggers-Wall tree
\Theta_{L}(A)[:p]
when
all the branches of
A
have associated Newton-Puiseux roots in
\mathbb{K}[[x^{1/\mathbb{N}}]]
:

Theorem 17.15
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{K}[[x,y]]\smallsetminus\{0\}
be a reduced power series defining the plane curve singularity
A
. Denote by
L
the smooth branch
(Z(x),O)
. Assume that all the branches of
A
different from
L
have associated Newton-Puiseux roots in
\mathbb{K}[[x^{1/\mathbb{N}}]]
.
If
A_{l}
and
A_{m}
are two branches of
A
different from
L
, we consider the center
P:=\langle L,A_{l},A_{m}\rangle
of the tripod determined by
A_{l}
,
A_{m}
and
L
on the Newton-Puiseux-Eggers-Wall tree
\Theta_{L}(A_{l}\cup A_{m})[:p]
. Then:

(A_{l}\cdot A_{m})_{O}=\mathbf{i}_{L}[:p](A_{l})\cdot\mathbf{i}_{L}[:p](A_{m})\cdot\mathbf{c}_{L}[:p](P).

In particular:

(17.12)

\mathbf{c}_{L}[:p](\langle L,A_{l},A_{m}\rangle)=\frac{(A_{l}\cdot A_{m})_{O}}{(A_{l}\cdot L)_{O}\cdot(A_{m}\cdot L)_{O}}.

Proof.

Let us fix Newton-Puiseux roots
\xi,\tau\in\mathbb{K}[[x^{1/\mathbb{N}}]]
corresponding to
A_{l}
and
A_{m}
respectively. We denote by
f,g\in\mathbb{K}[[x]][y]
the minimal polynomials of
\xi
and
\tau
respectively (see Proposition
17.5
). Let
\boxed{\mathrm{Res}_{y}(f,g)}
be the
resultant in
y
of the polynomials
f
and
g
. Then:

(A_{l}\cdot A_{m})_{O}=\nu_{x}(\mathrm{Res}_{y}(f,g))=\nu_{x}\left(\prod_{\tau_{j}\in Z_{x}(g)}\prod_{\xi_{s}\in Z_{x}(f)}(\xi_{s}-\tau_{j})\right)

where the first equality follows from
[
33
, Theorem 4.17, Section 4.2]
. Using the action of the roots of unity, we get

(17.13)

(A_{l}\cdot A_{m})_{O}=(\deg g)\cdot\nu_{x}\left(\prod_{\xi_{s}\in Z_{x}(f)}(\xi_{s}-\tau)\right).

Notice that
\deg g=(L\cdot A_{m})_{O}
. We use now the notation introduced in Definition
17.1
for the characteristic exponents of
\xi
. By Proposition
17.5
, we have the following formula for the previous intersection multiplicity

(17.14)

(L\cdot A_{l})_{O}=n=e_{0}[:p]\cdot p^{\nu_{p}(n)}.

Denote by
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
the order of coincidence of
A_{l}
and
A_{m}
relative to
L
. By (
17.10
) and (
17.14
), we get:

(17.15)

(L\cdot A_{l})_{O}\cdot{\mathbf{c}}_{L}[:p](P)=p^{\nu_{p}(n)}\left((e_{0}[:p]-e_{1}[:p])\frac{b_{1}}{n}+\cdots+(e_{r-1}[:p]-e_{r}[:p])\frac{b_{r}}{n}+e_{r}[:p]\alpha\right).

The result follows by comparing Formula (
17.15
) with Formula (
17.13
) and by applying Lemma
17.14
, which describes the orders of the series
(\xi_{k}-\tau)
, for the
n[:p]
different roots
\xi_{k}
of
f
. Notice that we have to count each root of
f
with multiplicity
p^{\nu_{p}(n)}
(see Proposition
17.5
).
∎
