ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo present the Artificial Intelligence-Ready Mount Sinai (AIR·MS) platform-unified access to diverse clinical datasets from the Mount Sinai Health System (MSHS), along with computational infrastructure for AI-driven research and demonstrate its utility with 3 research projects.Materials and MethodsAIR·MS integrates structured and unstructured data from multiple MSHS sources via the OMOP Common Data Model on an in-memory columnar database. Unstructured pathology and radiology data are integrated through metadata extracted from and linking the raw source data. Data access and analytics are supported from the HIPAA-compliant Azure cloud and the on-premises Minerva High-Performance Computing (HPC) environment.ResultsAIR·MS provides access to structured electronic health records, clinical notes, and metadata for pathology and radiology images, covering over 12M patients. The platform enables interactive cohort building and AI model training. Experimentation with complex cohort queries confirm a high system performance. Three use cases demonstrate, risk-factor discovery, and federated cardiovascular risk modeling.DiscussionAIR·MS demonstrates how clinical data and infrastructure can be integrated to support large-scale AI-based research. The platform’s performance, scale, and cross-institutional design position it as a model for similar initiatives.ConclusionAIR·MS provides a scalable, secure, and collaborative platform for AI-enabled healthcare research on multimodal clinical data.

Background and significance

Over the past decades, the availability of electronic medical data has expanded significantly, including structured data such as electronic health records (EHRs) and genome sequences, as well as unstructured data like clinical texts and medical images. Researchers often need to conduct exploratory research by interactively querying and analyzing this combined data volume.1 Exploring the data efficiently with ad hoc queries (as opposed to predefined queries or database views) is beneficial across the research lifecycle. Interactive question-and-answer exploration helps develop an understanding of the types and amounts of data relevant for a given research question. During the stage of building and refining the required patient cohorts for analysis, it allows the effects of different parameters to be more quickly evaluated. Finally, it makes it possible to dynamically retrieve data from the warehouse based on complex criteria as part of a larger data analysis or artificial intelligence (AI) model training process.

In parallel, recent advances in AI applications in healthcare2 include the domains of medical image analysis3,4 and natural language processing (NLP) of clinical texts.5–8 The availability of data is a crucial precondition for the success of AI-based approaches: experience from large language models (LLMs) suggests that sufficiently large datasets are central for adequately training complex AI models.9,10 Safe, unbiased clinical tools based on AI require models to be trained on sufficiently large and diverse datasets that capture the spectrum of variation of both the underlying diseases and the full patient population.11–13

Effectively serving these 2 requirements requires integrating comprehensive datasets and robust infrastructure for access and analysis. AI techniques are particularly suited for uncovering insights from such diverse data but need harmonized systems that support storage, efficient querying, and model development across modalities.1,14 Through the integration of clinical care and medical research, academic medical centers can play a key role in this effort. First, the clinical departments collect large numbers of patient records that reflect both the interaction of distinct aspects of a disease, and its occurrence and progression over time. Second, as part of their research work, academic medical centers also maintain more specialized data sources, such as biobanks and medical imaging repositories. This allows them to collect and integrate data that cover key qualitative aspects, sometimes referred to as the axes of healthcare data: heterogeneity, longitudinally, data linkage, and standardization.15 This combination of data depth and breadth, along with the close research integration, makes data repositories maintained at such centers an important complement to broader cross-institutional data networks such as PCORnet,16 the All of Us research program,17 or the Observational Health Data Sciences and Informatics (OHDSI) data network.18

The AI-Ready Mount Sinai (AIR·MS) platform was launched in 2020 to support AI-driven clinical research at scale and advance towards a learning health system.19 Integrating data from the Mount Sinai Health System (MSHS, further described in Supplementary Materials, Section A) and the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai (ISMMS), it leverages institutional expertise in clinical AI and builds on existing infrastructure including the Mount Sinai Data Warehouse (MSDW)20 and the Minerva high-performance computing (HPC) cluster.21

AIR·MS is an initiative of the Hasso Plattner Institute for Digital Health at Mount Sinai (HPI·MS), a collaboration between ISMMS in New York, United States, and the Hasso Plattner Institute (HPI) in Potsdam, Germany, in cooperation with Data4Life, a nonprofit health research IT organization. Drawing on the expertise of distinct groups within all 3 organizations, this multi-institutional setup brings together scientific expertise in medicine and data science, as well as technological expertise in data management and infrastructure. By bridging multiple institutions and countries, it also opens the door to cross-institutional and multinational cooperation on digital health research while upholding strict data privacy and security measures.

Materials and methods

AIR·MS was developed to provide streamlined access to heterogeneous clinical data for analysis (Figure 1). The platform supports querying across structured data (whether EHRs or metadata for unstructured data) and unstructured datasets (in the respective raw data formats) via a unified interface, enabling systematic exploration and multimodal analysis. Structured EHR data and extracted metadata are centralized in an in-memory database (DB), while unstructured raw data remain in separate stores linked through metadata. The platform provides access to over 12M patient records.

Data sources and ingestion

Electronic health records

The integrated clinical information system (Epic EHR) at MSHS went live in May 2011; other MSHS facilities went live in later years, as shown in Table 1. In addition, some historical data from Mount Sinai’s former TDS hospital information system were backfilled into Epic, and although not complete, these historical data date back to 2000. In total, the system currently contains over 12M patient records; more than 5M outpatient visits were captured alone in 2024.22

EHR data are extracted daily from Epic’s Caboodle system (including clinical notes), mapped to MSDW using an extract-transform-load (ETL) procedure, and later imported into the AIR·MS DB. The OHDSI Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model (CDM)18,23 is used alongside the OHDSI Standardized Vocabularies,24 as this enables a standardized SQL-based querying, also for multi-site studies.25

The adoption of the OMOP-CDM required addressing various challenges. To begin, the process to map and validate Mount Sinai’s Epic master file records and category lists to standard healthcare codes in OHDSI’s ATHENA repository required support from informaticists with clinical subject matter expertise. For MSDW, this time consuming and expensive work was achieved with support from a vendor, Clinical Architecture, starting in April 2022. It took 16 months to map approximately 1.5 million codes out of the 2 million codes provided (cf. Table 2 for the mapped files/domains). Second, the process to map source to target elements (the selection of columns in Epic’s Caboodle system and their mapping to OMOP-CDM columns) required extensive investigations and decisions, as OHDSI guidance at the time was not specific to Epic. While certain cases were simple (eg, vital signs, lab results, and diagnoses) due to clear equivalents, others were more complex (eg, social history and family history), as there was no table in Caboodle for what OMOP-CDM expected. Here, helper columns were built into Caboodle that facilitated the extraction. Third, the OMOP-CDM was designed as a standardized container for research rather than a longitudinal data warehouse. However, in our research context where reproducibility and incremental updates are important, we decided to add columns to every table to support data lineage and tracking of record loading and updates. Lastly, certain data elements did not have equivalents in OMOP-CDM. To avoid loss of important information and expand the range of data available for specialized studies, data elements not supported by OMOP-CDM are stored in custom elements (both tables and columns).

Data extraction leverages Epic’s Caboodle data warehouse as primary source. We perform additional transformations, filtering, and cleanup to align Caboodle tables with target OMOP-CDM tables. We derive very few of our own research variables from Epic’s source data; instead, and due to resource constraints, we chose to present data records from Caboodle almost entirely “as is” in our OMOP-CDM. The AIR·MS DB is updated monthly from both the full and the de-identified datasets of MSDW. This frequency is a balance between the time it takes our ETL to update data and the requirements from research use cases. A delta-load procedure is used to only transfer updates made since the last load.

De-identified dataset

We generate a de-identified dataset regularly from the PHI instance. Integer surrogate OMOP-CDM identifiers (eg, VISIT_OCCURRENCE_ID) are first assigned to all records. For each patient, a random salt and a date-shift value (1–366 days) are created and securely maintained by the data warehouse team. Each OMOP-CDM identifier is concatenated with the salt and hashed using the SHA-256 1-way cryptographic function26 to yield a 32-byte masked identifier, in accordance with HIPAA 45 CFR § 164.514 [Ref.27, part (c)]. The date-shift is then applied to all timestamps, and records with shifted dates in the future are excluded following the Shift-and-Truncate (SANT) method.28 The method is designed to comply with HIPAA’s Safe Harbor [Ref.27, part (b)(2)], yet still allow for incremental data loads. So far, we have focused on deidentifying data that are stored in structured or “discrete” fields. We do not have the ability to de-identify free-text clinical notes or other unstructured data, so we exclude those data from our de-identified datasets. In the future, we will expand deidentification to other data modalities.

Pathology, radiology, electrocardiogram, and genomic data

Pathology data are extracted from a specialized laboratory information system (PowerPath) including reports and digitized slides. Metadata as well as reports are ingested into the AIR·MS DB, also using a delta-load procedure, while pathology images are copied to on-premises storage accessible from the Minerva HPC cluster (see above). Metadata for radiology images are also transferred from MSDW to AIR·MS after being extracted from an XNAT platform. The corresponding full-resolution DICOM images are being copied into on-premises storage in the Minerva HPC cluster. Electrocardiogram (ECG) data, including waveform files, are retrieved from a Cardiology Information System (GE Healthcare MUSE), linked and loaded. Metadata from the genomic data being collected in the genetic sequencing programs BioMe29 and Mount Sinai Million Health Discoveries30 are also loaded into the database.

ETL tooling

To manage the ingestion process, AIR·MS uses an orchestration tool called datapilot. Built on the open-source Apache Airflow and NiFi tools, datapilot enables custom orchestration logic, extensive logging, and validation checks while ensuring consistent data loading, monitoring, and error reporting. Datapilot is currently used for both OMOP-CDM datasets as well as radiology metadata, and will be extended to other modalities.

AIR·MS database and raw data storage

The AIR·MS DB for structured and metadata fields uses SAP HANA,31 an in-memory columnar database that enables fast SQL-based querying. It currently handles up to 2 TB of compressed data in random access memory (RAM), allowing for high-performance exploration of large-scale datasets. In-memory data are compressed column-by-column, making it possible to handle datasets with an on-disk size of multi terabytes.

While OMOP-CDM is used for structured EHR and clinical notes, metadata describing unstructured data from pathology, radiology, ECG and biobank data, which are not yet part of the OMOP-CDM standard, are stored using custom data models.

The custom pathology model captures >60 data elements related to, eg, the patient, pathologist, specimen, stain information, and full-text pathology reports. The medical image metadata model includes DICOM study and series identifiers, as well as DICOM tags (∼100 per image series). Images are linked to patients via the patient medical record number (MRN) from Epic EHR. The genetic sequencing metadata, available in both identifiable as well as de-identified forms, helps to quickly refine patient cohorts. Finally, the ECG data span more than 10 tables; waveform data are also held in-memory.

MRNs are stored in an extension column of the OMOP-CDM PERSON table. This enables cross-modality queries that, eg, combine features from the core medical history, features of medical images, and pathology results, including word occurrences in the written pathology reports or availability of digitized slides.

Data quality management

AIR·MS targets high data quality across modalities, an ambitious goal due to the different aspects of quality and to the difficulty of systematically quantifying them. Beyond core aspects such as accuracy, completeness, and consistency, further (and more informally defined) aspects such as trustworthiness or accessibility of data can also form important components of the overall notion of quality.32 For structured data, a self-built business rules framework enabled us early on to define and execute data quality checks on the data loaded to our OMOP-CDM tables. More recently, we began running OHDSI’s Data Quality Dashboard (DQD), a tool based on Kahn et al.’s framework.33 Issues related to completeness, consistency, and transformation fidelity34,35 are checked regularly. Quality efforts are ongoing with tools under development, eg, to support cohort-specific checks, and extensions for unstructured data.

Computational infrastructure

Researchers can access the data through the Minerva HPC cluster.21 Minerva provides over 2 petaflops of compute power, drawn from ∼25 000 compute cores distributed across hundreds of computing nodes, including large memory and GPU nodes, suitable for model training while maintaining on-premises data security.

AIR·MS integrates a platform for custom applications called AppTier, which runs on Azure infrastructure. It includes Kubernetes for containerized applications, and virtual machines (VMs) for tailored environments. Kubernetes offers advanced capabilities for monitoring applications, high availability, and dynamic horizontal scaling, while VMs offer more tailored servers with a high degree of application isolation. Centralized user authentication and security checks ensure compliance and robustness. The integration of a container registry ensures that only trusted and signed images are deployed. Developers can test applications in a staging environment before deployment is granted in production.

Data protection and compliance

AIR·MS upholds the highest standards of data security and compliance. It adheres to HIPAA regulations and is approved by Mount Sinai’s institutional review board (IRB) with project id STUDY-20-01288. Strict access controls, encryption, and audits are used throughout the system. PHI access is limited to researchers with IRB-approved protocols. Required training covers the handling of electronic PHI and the importance of maintaining data confidentiality, integrity, rigor, reproducibility, and availability, as well as ethical behavior in biomedical research. Researchers must sign and submit an IRB-approved data use agreement (DUA) before accessing the platform. There are separate DUAs for deidentified and for PHI data. Once requirements are met, researchers can request access to the Minerva HPC. MSHS-employed researchers are granted access through laptops that have been approved by Mount Sinai’s security team Digital and Technology Partners (DTP), external researchers are instead provided with access to a Virtual Desktop Infrastructure to work on AIR·MS.

Compliance assurance processes run continuously, with logs and audits contributing to data protection. Dedicated training programs promote responsible data use and foster a compliance culture. The corresponding principal investigator is responsible for reviewing research outputs such as aggregated tables, figures, and statistical results, prior to release to ensure compliance with privacy and disclosure control standards.

For uses such as training or hackathons, a dataset with no restriction beyond platform access is also available (the DE-SynPUF dataset, freely available via the US Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services36). In Section F of the Supplementary Materials. we describe documentation and training resources offered by the project team.

The AIR·MS platform is developed and operated by a dedicated team with members from both HPI·MS and Data4Life. This team is responsible for the daily operation of the platform, for updating existing data and integrating new data modalities, and for the development of tools and infrastructure for research use. This is done in close cooperation with experts in the Mount Sinai Scientific Computing and Data team (a description of the project team is included in Section E of the Supplementary Materials).

Results

The AIR·MS platform brings together large sets of data of different modalities from the clinical and research ecosystem of MSHS and ISMMS (a characterization of the patient population is included in Supplementary Materials, Section B), making them available to researchers from these institutions and HPI. By providing dedicated infrastructure and tools, it supports researchers during the different stages of data-driven clinical research: data exploration and selection, patient cohort building, and statistical analysis or AI model training.

Performant data exploration and patient cohort building

By containing both the full structured EHR and clinical note data as well as the metadata of the linked unstructured data, AIR·MS offers a comprehensive view of the patient population. We have observed that the use of a column-oriented, in-memory database for the AIR·MS DB supports well the interactive querying of the full AIR·MS data, as this technology allows performant analytical database queries.37 To evaluate the user-facing query performance across a range of realistic queries, we created a test suite consisting of 11 cohort definitions from the OHDSI Phenotype Library (release v3.32.0).38 This library contains over 500 standardized cohort definitions (given as standardized SQL templates). SQL code was generated from the templates using the SqlRender R package,39 plus additional changes to adapt the specific SQL dialect to that of the AIR·MS DB (SAP HANA). The test cohorts were selected to span a range of complexities in terms of their definition. Whereas the simplest cohorts were based solely on the presence of one or a few codes in a single OMOP-CDM table, the most complex ones defined cohort entry criteria across multiple tables, involved large sets of codes, and defined additional inclusion and exclusion criteria. The generated SQL consisted of a standardized sequence of (potentially) complex queries. For instance, the final SQL code for the OHDSI cohort with phenotype ID 741 involves 50 individual SQL queries that use 11 temporary tables as well as 66 table joins and 16 table unions on up to 4 OMOP-CDM tables, resulting in more than 1,200 lines of formatted SQL code. Clinical notes and full text search

With clinical notes being an integral part of OMOP-CDM, these are stored directly in the AIR·MS DB. Combined with full-text indices, this allows flexible text searches beyond exact word matches or fixed patterns, including tunable fuzziness (misspellings) and linguistic analysis such as stemming and stopword filtering. Preliminary experiments using fuzzy text queries on the set of 86M notes typically resulted in execution times <1 s, suggesting that an interactive exploration of basic aspects of clinical notes can also be conducted interactively. More sophisticated NLP workflows can extract notes for external analysis.

Use case 1: Crohn’s disease phenotyping

Identifying clinically meaningful patient subgroups is a foundational step in real-world studies based on EHRs. The AIR·MS platform has supported several studies in this domain, particularly focusing on inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), by enabling the integration of structured EHR data with information extracted from clinical narratives. In one study, cohorts of interest were identified and then validated and refined by clinician-scientists through manual chart review, with a focus on pregnant patients who had undergone proctocolectomy with ileal pouch-anal anastomosis.41 In another effort, computable phenotyping algorithms for Crohn’s Disease (CD) were developed and validated. These included both a rule-based approach using the spaCy framework and an LLM-based method leveraging GPT-4 to extract disease-specific features—such as complications (disease behavior) and age at diagnosis—from clinical text.42 The age-at-diagnosis information was subsequently used to refine the phenotyping algorithm for identifying incident cases of CD.43 Incorporating information from clinical notes alongside structured EHR data improved both the precision of the computable phenotyping algorithms and the performance of related clinical prediction models.

Use case 2: Dysmenorrhea and heart disease

Dysmenorrhea is a common albeit under-researched condition, affecting up to 90% of menstruating women. Despite its prevalence, it is rarely captured in longitudinal cohort studies, making it difficult to assess potential long-term health risks associated with the condition. EHRs, on the other hand, provide a valuable yet underutilized source of real-world, longitudinal patient data, including information gathered during routine gynecological visits. A key challenge is that dysmenorrhea is often not coded as a formal diagnosis for billing, necessitating the use of clinical notes to reliably identify affected individuals. In a recent study presented at the American Heart Association Annual Symposium,44 we showcased how the AIR·MS platform can be used to detect dysmenorrhea cases by combining SNOMED-coded entries with information extracted from clinical notes linked with gynecologic encounters, using a domain-adapted clinical language model.45 This approach enabled construction of a cohort of 56 799 patients with up to 20 years of follow-up—one of the largest dysmenorrhea cohorts reported to date. Cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses on this population revealed dysmenorrhea as a previously unrecognized risk factor for ischemic heart disease (IHD), with significant implications for risk stratification in young women—a demographic that has seen rising IHD incidence and for whom traditional risk models often fall short.

Use case 3: PreCareML

Cardiovascular diseases are the leading cause of death globally. Early identification of high-risk individuals could enable preventative lifestyle or medication changes. However, current risk assessment tools are limited, considering only a handful of known risk factors and often being tailored to specific regions and populations.46 To improve their performance and robustness, in the ongoing PreCareML47 project, we utilize de-identified, longitudinal, structured EHR data from AIR·MS to build a representative patient cohort. This cohort is then used to build ML models for 5-year major adverse cardiovascular event risk assessment by utilizing AIR·MS data alone and through distributed learning strategies such as federated learning with 3 healthcare institutions across 3 continents. Finally, having access to these diverse cohorts allowed us to evaluate model performance across subgroups and to more thoroughly understand any performance degradation during external validation. We are currently investigating the performance of models trained with federated learning.

AppTier and applications

Although the AppTier is a recent addition to the AIR·MS platform, it is already used for deploying custom applications and data analysis tools (cf. Figure 4). Data2Evidence48 enables the interactive exploration of structured datasets and integrates the DQD tool. Visian enables viewing and annotating DICOM & NIfTI medical images, as part of an AI-based image segmentation project.49 Lastly, TURTLE50 is a conversational interface to convert natural language questions about the AIR·MS data into SQL queries using an underlying LLM.

Discussion

Many academic medical centers now operate platforms that consolidate clinical data for research (cf. Ref.51 for an overview and Refs52–57 for examples from the United States). While varying in scale and scope, these systems often employ standards like OMOP-CDM and offer de-identified data with analytic tools. AIR·MS differs in that it integrates diverse modalities—structured EHR, notes, imaging, pathology—and offers performant, interactive data exploration, patient cohort building, and database-based analysis across these modalities via a powerful in-memory database. A further strength of the integrated data is the large patient base with over 12M patient records and the availability of almost 2M digitized pathology slides. Further key differences are discussed in Section D of the Supplementary Materials.

The usage of an HPC environment flexibly supports researchers’ workflows, from very initial data explorations to handling compute-intensive AI training. Custom tools can be deployed on the AppTier’s dedicated infrastructure, enhancing the platform’s versatility.

The binational, multi-institution structure of AIR·MS enables international collaboration between research groups in both the United States and Germany. This is reflected in its joint development model and user base, integrating expertise in clinical research, data science, computer science, and software engineering.

Integration of previously disparate data sources and software components into a single platform is a complex task that requires multiple iterations and ongoing adjustments. Incorporating new data modalities involves not only clearing the technical, organizational, and legal hurdles for accessing the new data sources from AIR·MS but also designing appropriate OMOP-CDM extensions that allow incorporating the associated metadata into the central DB. However, as more modalities are added, infrastructure matures, and the platform team broadens their integration experience, onboarding new sources is expected to become more efficient.

As the number of available data sources grows, additional data owners may choose to integrate their datasets and gain the ability to study it in connection with other data modalities. To face the researchers’ challenge of understanding an ever-broader palette of datasets and their connections, efforts go into extending the documentation of the platform data and functionality and making it available through a central portal.

Even with large datasets, extracting relevant data remains challenging. We have observed that the structured, clinical EHR data are unevenly distributed across patients. While most >99.9% of patients have at least one observation, only 43% have measurements and 29% have medication records. This likely reflects real-world ways in which patients interact with healthcare providers (depending on age, health history, place of residence, etc.), as well as the complicated nature of clinical data capture.1,58 It highlights the importance of a nuanced understanding of the data in any research DB that is based on clinical data. Studies that require rich longitudinal data must account for such variation when defining cohorts. To support researchers in getting a better overview of the generic characteristics of the AIR·MS dataset, new tools are currently being developed.

Conclusion

With the rapid progress in AI and medical data science, the need for large, multimodal, and systematically organized clinical datasets is more critical than ever. AIR·MS demonstrates how such infrastructure can be developed at an academic medical center, enabling scalable, secure, and collaborative research across institutions and countries. By integrating diverse data types and providing robust computational environments, AIR·MS supports the development of AI tools that are both clinically relevant and broadly applicable. These experiences may inform the construction of similar platforms to advance data-driven healthcare research.
