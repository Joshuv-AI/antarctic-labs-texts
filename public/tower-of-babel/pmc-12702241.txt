Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium infections in children in the Oceania region: review of the epidemiology, antimicrobial availability, treatment, clinical trial and pharmacokinetic data and key evidence gaps
Authors: Amanda Gwee, Sarah Bannister, Emma Best, Jeremy Carr, Kiera Harwood, Tony Lai, Alice Lei, Flora Lutui, Brendan McMullan, Mona Mostaghim, Lesley Voss, Heather Weerdenburg, Phoebe Williams, Amanda Wilkins, Daniel Yeoh, KIDS DOSE group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAntimicrobial resistance poses a significant threat to children's health, with up to 20% of 1.27 million deaths attributable to bacterial AMR annually, occurring in children <5 years. The WHO 2024 Bacterial Priority Pathogens List identifies methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium (VRE) as critical pathogens. This review examines the epidemiology, treatment recommendations, dosing strategies, efficacy, and safety data for antibiotics targeting MRSA and VRE infections in children in Oceania. Paediatric MRSA infections are prevalent (13–43%) across Oceania, while VRE infections remain uncommon (3–5%). Disparate access to recommended treatments, particularly in Pacific Island Countries and Territories, highlights the need for paediatric licensing. Paediatric trials primarily assess safety, with efficacy data limited to vancomycin, teicoplanin, and daptomycin. Pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic studies show standard dosing in children under 12 years often fails to achieve therapeutic targets, highlighting the need for dedicated dosing studies. Addressing these gaps is essential to advancing paediatric access to optimal treatment for drug-resistant infections in the region.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a significant threat to the health of children worldwide. Each year, of the 1.27 million deaths attributable to AMR, up to 20% are in young children aged less than 5 years.1 In response, the World Health Organization (WHO) has published the Bacterial Priority Pathogens List 2024, highlighting pathogens on which to focus research, development, and prevention strategies.2 Among the critical and high priority pathogens are two Gram-positive bacteria that are important pathogens in children: methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and to a lesser extent vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium (VRE). Notably, antimicrobial-resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae, Group A and B streptococci identified as a medium priority by the WHO are also important paediatric pathogens, however, are beyond the scope of this review.2

The WHO recognises that the “development of medicines for children lags unacceptably behind that for adults by nearly a decade”.3 This is attributable to fewer industry-sponsored paediatric drug trials relative to adults, greater ethical, financial and logistical challenges in conducting clinical trials in children, and the limited availability of child-friendly formulations.4,5 Furthermore, existing frameworks for regulatory approval for the use of newer antibiotics in children present an additional barrier to timely licensing, contributing to high rates of off-label medication use in children.6

The Oceania region sits within the WHO Western Pacific Region, encompassing Australia, Aotearoa New Zealand (AoNZ) and the Pacific Island countries and territories. In contrast to countries such as China, Malaysia, Vietnam, and the Philippines, local epidemiological AMR data for countries within Oceania are limited.7,8 This review summarises the epidemiology of MRSA and VRE infections in children in Oceania, outlines current treatment recommendations, and presents available paediatric dosing and clinical trial data, with a focus on agents recommended for bloodstream infections. It also reviews the safety profiles of these antibiotic options and highlights regional challenges (see Supplementary Material for the search methodology and search strategy).

What is the epidemiology of drug-resistant gram-positive infections in children in the Oceania region?

MRSA

S. aureus is a leading cause of death and disability in children.9 MRSA has emerged as a significant pathogen in children, and community transmission has become an important driver for the high-burden of disease in the Oceania region, particularly in settings of household crowding and socioeconomic deprivation.10 Children present most commonly with skin and soft tissue infections and osteoarticular infections. However, a range of other severe presentations including bacteraemia and/or toxic shock syndrome, infective endocarditis, pneumonia and device-related infections may also occur.11 In infants, MRSA bacteraemia is more commonly nosocomial and associated with device-related infections.11

The epidemiology of MRSA in children varies across the Oceania region, with a notable paucity of paediatric-specific data from the Pacific Island countries and territories (PICT). In a recent report by the Australian Group on Antimicrobial Resistance for Kids (‘AGAR-Kids’), 13% of 607 S. aureus bloodstream isolates from 38 laboratories were methicillin-resistant.12 Clindamycin resistance was also present in 19% of MRSA isolates, and no trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole resistance was reported.12 Within Australia, the proportion of MRSA has remained stable over the last 10 years, and no vancomycin-intermediate or resistant isolates have been identified.13 Despite inherent cultural strengths, due to longstanding inequities and social determinant disparities, First Nations children from Australia are disproportionately impacted by MRSA. In a multicentre prospective study of paediatric S. aureus bacteraemia, 45% of Australian MRSA isolates occurred in children who identify as Aboriginal, despite these children representing only 4% of the total Australian population.11 Similarly, the highest proportion of MRSA isolates in the AGAR-Kids report was seen in the Northern Territory, where First Nations Australians represent 30% of the population.12 This high burden of MRSA amongst First Nations children is consistent with previous reports of invasive S. aureus disease in this region.14

In Aotearoa New Zealand (AoNZ), point prevalence surveillance data from 2017 of all clinical specimens collected over one month across the country showed an MRSA prevalence of 19.9 per 100,000, almost double that reported in 2009 (10.2/100,000) with the highest prevalence in children 0–4 years of age (54.6/100,000).15 Further, the aforementioned multicentre prospective study of S. aureus bacteraemia in children, found 17% (22/130) of isolates from the greater Auckland region in AoNZ were MRSA.11 Similar to Australia, First Nations children from AoNZ are disproportionately represented in these data, with 73% of AoNZ MRSA isolates from Māori and children of Pacific Island peoples living in AoNZ (despite these populations representing only 25% of the total population).11

For the PICT regions, there is a paucity of published paediatric epidemiological data, with a recent systematic review identifying a single paediatric study reporting S. aureus susceptibility.16 This study found S. aureus was the predominant pathogen causing acute osteomyelitis in children in Papua New Guinea, with 40/47 (85%) of these isolates methicillin-resistant.17 However, clindamycin (6%) and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (4%) resistance was uncommon.17 Recently, published data from microbiological reference laboratories (not specific to children) found varying proportions of MRSA across the PICT region: 43% in Samoa, 43% in Tonga, 26% in Kiribati, 12% in Fiji, 8% in Cook Islands, and 3% in Vanuatu.18,19

VRE

Clinically significant vancomycin-resistant enterococcal infections are largely due to E. faecium.20 Most vancomycin resistance gene clusters are vanA, associated with vancomycin and teicoplanin resistance, and vanB, which remains susceptible to teicoplanin.20 VRE infections commonly manifest as nosocomial bloodstream infections, particularly in children admitted to intensive care, those with an underlying haematology/oncology diagnosis, and with indwelling catheters.20 Another paediatric presentation of VRE infection is urinary tract infections (UTIs), particularly in younger children and those with renal tract anomalies.21

Vancomycin-resistant enterococcal infections remain relatively uncommon in children. In Australia, AGAR-Kids reported vancomycin resistance in 5% and teicoplanin resistance in 2% of enterococcal bloodstream isolates from 2020 to 2021, whilst 20% were ampicillin resistant, including 78% of E. faecium isolates.12 Notably, in the Australian Enterococcus Surveillance Outcome Program 2022 report combining adult and paediatric isolates, 47% of E. faecium were resistant to vancomycin, substantially higher than reported in Europe, with resistance also increasing over time.22 In AoNZ, all clinical Enterococcus spp. isolates from a tertiary paediatric referral centre in 2022 were susceptible to vancomycin (unpublished data). A scoping review from 2020 of studies from the PICT region identified only two reports comprising four VRE isolates in total.16 In a recent study of adults and children in Fiji (2019–2022), 3% of 1415 enterococcal isolates were vancomycin-resistant.19

What are the current treatment recommendations for MRSA and VRE infections in children?

MRSA

Treatment guidelines for MRSA infection in children are primarily extrapolated from adult recommendations, with limited supporting pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) data (See Table 1)). For MRSA bacteraemia and other infections where intravenous therapy is indicated, vancomycin remains the recommended first-line agent despite the difficulty in attaining therapeutic concentrations and concern regarding nephrotoxicity.23, 24, 26 Of note, some studies suggest improved treatment outcomes using alternative agents for MRSA bacteraemia in adults.27,28 Alternative treatment options for invasive MRSA infections include daptomycin (although this is not efficacious for pneumonia caused by MRSA due to inactivation by surfactant), linezolid and ceftaroline.23,24 Ceftaroline has limited paediatric evidence for skin and soft tissue infections (SSTI) and pneumonia and is currently considered as salvage therapy for paediatric MRSA bacteraemia.29,30 Clindamycin is recommended in osteoarticular infections due to MRSA. However, it has not been studied in MRSA bacteraemia due to increased relapse rates reported in endocarditis.26 Oral treatment recommendations for SSTI and osteoarticular infections are generally selected based on considerations around local antimicrobial susceptibility patterns, and may include trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, clindamycin, and linezolid. VRE

There is limited evidence for antimicrobial regimens to treat VRE infections, particularly in children. Recommended treatments for bloods stream infections include daptomycin, oxazolidinones (linezolid and tedizolid), and oritavancin (Table 1).25 Combination therapy with a β-lactam antibiotic may be indicated as it may restore the efficacy of daptomycin in critically ill patients, and in patients with a daptomycin MIC of 3–4 μg/mL.32 For vanB VRE, teicoplanin is also a treatment option. VRE infections confined to the renal tract may be treated with ampicillin or nitrofurantoin (for isolated cystitis).33,34 Fosfomycin may also be considered for lower UTI due to VRE, although clinical data in children are limited.35

Vancomycin and teicoplanin

The most extensively studied agent was vancomycin. Across 10 RCTs, vancomycin was administered empirically for various indications (febrile neutropenia, bacterial sepsis). The five RCTs that reported clinical efficacy endpoints found comparable treatment responses to mixed comparators, which in the majority was a β-lactam antibiotic, teicoplanin or an alternative vancomycin dosing regimen. Of the four RCTs of teicoplanin, two were in children with confirmed gram-positive bacteraemia: all reported no significant difference in efficacy compared to the control, which in two trials, was vancomycin.46, 47, 52

Linezolid

Linezolid was studied in three large RCTs (two specifically in children), none of which were of MRSA infection.48, 49, 50 There was no significant difference in clinical cure in the one trial that compared linezolid to vancomycin to treat various infections. However, significantly fewer adverse effects were observed. The remaining two trials of skin and soft tissue infection showed that clinical cure occurred in >90% of children.

Daptomycin

Daptomycin was studied in three RCTs of children with skin and soft tissue infections, osteomyelitis and S. aureus bacteraemia.52,53,51 In all studies, there was no significant difference in clinical efficacy nor adverse effects between daptomycin and standard of care (vancomycin, clindamycin).

Ceftaroline

There are three published trials of ceftaroline in children, all which evaluated safety as their primary outcome.54, 55, 56 Children had high rates of clinical cure (81–94%) for pneumonia and soft tissue infections in all studies.

Other agents

A single RCT was identified for each of tedizolid and dalbavancin.31,57 For treating skin and soft tissue infections (tedizolid, n = 120; dalbavancin, n = 191), each agent had similar clinical success compared to each comparator.

What paediatric dosing and pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) data are available for the antimicrobials recommended for the treatment of resistant gram-positive infections?

For drugs where therapeutic drug monitoring is not routinely performed, there is a paucity of PK/PD data to inform optimal paediatric dosing strategies. Several challenges contribute to this gap. Additional blood tests are a major barrier to paediatric enrolment, and limited funding (since many of these studies are investigator-led rather than industry-sponsored) further restricts feasibility. Moreover, wide variability in pharmacokinetics across the paediatric age range arises from developmental changes in organ function and body composition, adding complexity to study design and interpretation.

Vancomycin

Vancomycin has a narrow therapeutic index and high inter-individual variability in children and neonates, resulting in challenges in achieving therapeutic drug exposures. Vancomycin PK has been well studied in neonates and children, with 64 population PK models reported in a systematic review.58 In adults with S. aureus bacteraemia, the recommended PD target for vancomycin is a ratio of the area under the concentration–time curve over 24 h (AUC24) to MIC >400 for S. aureus infections with an MIC of ≤1 mg/L.59 A similar PK/PD target has been reported in children, with one study finding an AUC24/MIC <300 was associated with persistent bacteraemia at 48–72 h.60 Due to limited access to reliable paediatric AUC calculators, trough concentrations are still used to guide treatment in many paediatric settings. In young infants aged 0–90 days, the trough concentrations that correlate with an AUC24 >400 mg h/L depend on the dosing interval: for 6-hourly and 12-hourly dosing, this correlates with trough concentrations of 15–20 mg/L and 8–15 mg/L, respectively.42,61 In children aged 3 months to 18 years receiving 6-hourly dosing, the same AUC24 target correlates with trough concentrations of 6–10 mg/L.62 From a toxicity perspective, one study in children showed an AUC24 >800 was associated with a 2.5-fold increase in the risk of nephrotoxicity,63 while another in the paediatric intensive care setting reported increased odds of nephrotoxicity with an AUC0–24 >537 mg h/L (OR 1.005, 95% CI 1.003–1.008).64 In neonates, an AUC24 >600 mg h/L (OR 9.008, CI 2.7–29.983, P < 0.001) was associated with nephrotoxicity.65

Teicoplanin

Several studies of teicoplanin pharmacokinetics in children showed that postmenstrual age, weight and creatinine significantly affected pharmacokinetics.66, 67, 68 Like vancomycin, efficacy correlates with the AUC24/MIC; however, studies in children primarily evaluate trough concentrations. Although the recommended target trough concentration is > 10 mg/L (>15 mg/L for severe infections), several studies have shown that the standard dosing regimen of 3 loading doses of 10 mg/kg every 12 h followed by 10 mg/kg daily fails to achieve this.67,69,70 One study suggested that maintenance doses of up to 18 mg/kg/day in children aged 2–5 years, 14 mg/kg/day in children 6–11 years and 12 mg/kg/day in children 12–16 years were required to achieve trough target concentrations of 15–30 mg/L for severe infections.68 Only one study assessed the probability attainment of achieving an AUC24/MIC target, finding that the current dosing strategy is adequate for moderate infections (AUC24/MIC ≥125) but not for severe infections (target AUC24/MIC ≥345).66

Linezolid

One study in adults has shown the PK/PD target for linezolid is an AUC24/MIC >80–120 mg/L h for staphylococcal infections.71 Several studies have shown that the current recommended paediatric dosing regimen is predicted to achieve this target in 72–81% of children when the MIC is 2 mg/L and only 30–35% when the MIC is 4 mg/L.72,73 When the MIC is > 2 mg/L (or in some studies >1 mg/L), studies have shown that higher doses of up to 20 mg/kg 8 hourly may be required.73,74 Also, higher clearance has been reported in children aged <12 years compared to adults supporting the higher recommended daily dose (e.g. 10 mg/kg three times daily) in this age group.75 The exception is in neonates, where clearance may be reduced and dosing similar to older children is recommended.76 Based on studies in adults, a causal relationship between linezolid trough concentrations and haematological toxicity has been established, with increased risk of toxicity with trough concentrations >7 mg/L and/or AUC24 >300 mg/L h.77

Tedizolid

Data on the pharmacokinetics of tedizolid in children are mainly for those aged ≥12 years,78,79 with only one study of children 2–12 years.80 The PD target is the free AUC24/MIC ≥3, derived from murine studies of S. aureus, with no published PK/PD studies in children.79,81 In adolescents receiving 200 mg oral/intravenous daily PK is similar to adults with a 100% probability of attaining the target free AUC24/MIC ≥3 for S. aureus with a MIC ≤0.5 mg/L with an oral or intravenous dose of 200 mg daily.78 A PK study of young children (aged 2–12 years) showed a single dose of 6 mg/kg (6–12 years) or 3 mg/kg (2–6 years) provided comparable exposure to adults.80

Daptomycin

A systematic review (2016) of daptomycin PK in children included 153 children across 11 studies.82 Clearance is higher in children <12 years, with one study of a 4 mg/kg dose in children aged 3 months to 17 years reporting lower serum drug exposure than in adults receiving the same dose.83,84 The PK/PD target is both the AUC24/MIC and peak concentration to MIC ratio (Cmax:MIC),85,86 with an AUC24/MIC <666 associated with increased mortality in adults with Gram-positive infections (OR 1.24; 95% CI, 1.18–1.3; P = 0.032).87 Current dosing recommendations (7–12 mg/kg every 24 h) are predicted to result in subtherapeutic exposures, with one study in children aged 2–6 years with suspected or proven Gram-positive infection receiving 8 or 10 mg/kg reporting a mean AUC24 of 429 and 550 mg h/L, respectively.88 Two simulation studies found that doses of 17–20 mg/kg once daily in children <12 years old and 10–14 mg/kg once daily in adolescents ≥12 years old are required to achieve an AUC/MIC >666, assuming a daptomycin MIC of 1 mg/L.89,90 Studies in young infants aged up to 3 months with a gestational age ranging from 23 to 40 weeks have shown clearance is similar to young children, highlighting the need for higher doses in this population compared with older children and adults. However, the optimal dosing regimen in this age group has not been established.91

Safety profile of the antimicrobial agents recommended in children (Supplementary Table S3)

Vancomycin

From the paediatric trials of vancomycin identified (Table 3), vancomycin was most commonly associated with infusion-related reactions comprising flushing, erythema, and pruritus and occurring in 2–14% of children. The incidence was higher in those who received a loading dose (48% vs 24%, P = 0.06).39 The incidence of renal impairment in children is 12% (range 2–27%), higher with concomitant use of multiple nephrotoxic agents and higher daily doses.92 Vancomycin-related ototoxicity in children is rare, although a randomised trial of vancomycin in young infants reported a higher proportion of infants failing their newborn hearing test when receiving an optimised dosing regimen with a loading dose.43

Teicoplanin

From the four trials of teicoplanin, adverse effects occurred in 7–48%. Most adverse events were nephrotoxicity, although the reported incidence was lower than that of vancomycin (4–5%).46 The frequency of nephrotoxicity with teicoplanin is lower than vancomycin in children (5%). It has also been associated with thrombocytopenia (15%) and hepatotoxicity (6%), with higher rates of these adverse reactions reported in neonates (14–20%).93 However, its overall safety is considered comparable to that of adults.47

Linezolid

The three RCTs of linezolid reported a frequency of adverse effects between 19 and 45%. The majority were mild to moderate and included diarrhoea (3–12%), vomiting (2–7%), nausea (4–7%), headache (4–7%), and neutropenia (6%).48, 49, 50 Other adverse effects include glossitis (2%), anaemia (5%), thrombocytopenia (2–5%), elevated liver function tests (2–4%), lactic acidosis (16%), and peripheral and optic neuropathy, necessitating careful monitoring of haematological parameters, drug interactions, and neurological manifestations, especially during prolonged treatments (>14 days).48,94 In young infants or non-verbal children receiving prolonged treatment, routine surveillance for neuropathy should be considered, including regular ophthalmologic exams and monitoring for subtle neurological signs.

Tedizolid

Safety data for tedizolid in children are limited to one RCT of children aged >12 years in whom it is licensed. Reported adverse effects include phlebitis (3%), and increased blood creatinine phosphokinase (2.2%).57,80

Daptomycin

Severe adverse reactions to daptomycin are rare in children, with diarrhoea (4–9%) and a raised creatine phosphokinase (4–11%) most frequently reported. Myopathy is a rare side effect, with monitoring of creatine kinase, including a baseline measurement, essential due to this risk.52,95,96

Ceftaroline

In the three paediatric RCTs of ceftaroline, adverse effects were observed in 22–45% of children of which the majority were mild. Anaemia (10%), nausea and vomiting (2–8%), pruritus (10%), rash (5–8%), and eosinophilia (5%) were most frequently reported.29,56

Regional challenges

Despite the recognised burden of drug-resistant pathogens throughout the Oceania region, there are few readily available treatment options for resistant Gram-positive pathogens approved by local regulatory agencies for use in children, and particularly neonates. Currently approved paediatric treatment options in Australia and AoNZ include vancomycin, linezolid, daptomycin, and ceftaroline. Tigecycline and teicoplanin are approved only for adults in both countries, with teicoplanin also approved for use in children aged ≥2 months in AoNZ. Daptomycin is licensed for those aged ≥12 months while high-dose ceftaroline is approved for children aged ≥2 months with complicated skin and soft tissue infections with S. aureus MICs of 2–4 mg/L. In neonates, only vancomycin, linezolid and standard doses of ceftaroline are approved. Newer agents such as tedizolid, dalbavancin, and oritavancin have not been licensed in this region and are only accessible after obtaining approval from national regulatory authorities. In the PICT region, treatment options are severely limited, with vancomycin the only readily available option in Vanuatu, Samoa, Tonga, and Solomon Islands (Personal Communication: Solomon Bosa, Director of National Pharmacy Services Division, Ministry of Health and Medical Services, Solomon Islands 31/05/2024; Leva'itai ‘Asaeli, Pharmacy Department, Ministry of Health, Nuku'alofa, Tonga 05/06/2024).97, 98, 99 IV clindamycin is available and licensed for paediatric use from 1 month of age in Australia and New Zealand and is included in formularies in the Solomon Islands and Tonga however, is not on the Vanuatu essential medicines list. IV cotrimoxazole is licensed for children over 2 months in Australia and New Zealand but is inconsistently available across the Pacific: it is not on the essential medicines list in Vanuatu (2024) or Solomon Islands (2023) (Personal Communication: Solomon Bosa, Director of National Pharmacy Services Division, Ministry of Health and Medical Services, Solomon Islands 31/05/2024).97,98

Paediatric licensing constraints are further amplified by the lack of suitable, palatable oral formulations for children. Linezolid and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole are both approved for paediatric use and available as child-friendly suspensions throughout Australia and AoNZ, however, these are not routinely available in the PICT region. The high cost of linezolid suspension frequently restricts its use, leading to the common practice of crushing tablets as a cost-saving measure, despite the potential impact on pharmacokinetics and palatability. Clindamycin, known for its poor palatability, is only available as a single strength capsule throughout Australia and AoNZ.100 Oral clindamycin is not available in all countries and territories throughout the PICT region.101

Summary & future directions

Our review highlights the significant burden of drug-resistant Gram-positive bacterial infections in children in Oceania as well as the disparate access to the recommended antimicrobial agents in the PICT region (where vancomycin is the only available intravenous treatment option for MRSA) in comparison to Australia and AoNZ. Several research areas on which to focus future efforts have been identified to improve equity and access to appropriate antibiotic treatment for children.

Regional data demonstrate the urgent need for investment in improved laboratory infrastructure and surveillance systems for AMR, particularly in the PICT region. This will enable the identification of emerging drug-resistance and inform treatment guidelines and public health policy.102 MRSA prevalence among S. aureus infections in children in the Western Pacific varies significantly, ranging between 13% in Australia to 43% in Samoa and disproportionately affects First Nations populations of Australia and AoNZ. Whilst the available data suggest that VRE infections remain rare in children, published data from the PICT region are lacking. Recent increases in VRE observed in the adult population, along with the ongoing high resistance rate amongst E. faecium isolates in Australia demonstrate the importance of ongoing robust surveillance to inform local treatment guidelines and research prioritisation.22

Although our review identified several paediatric clinical trials that demonstrated the safety of the recommended agents for MRSA and VRE, only nine included participants with MRSA infections (vancomycin, teicoplanin, and daptomycin), and none involved VRE infections. Further efficacy studies for VRE infections in children, as well as on the use of linezolid, ceftaroline, tedizolid, and dalbavancin for MRSA, are still needed. Although there are robust data from trials in adults, these findings cannot directly inform paediatric practice due to several differences between adults and children: altered pharmacokinetics in children of different ages as highlighted in our review resulting in variable attainment of the pharmacodynamic target; differences in clinical phenotypes and lower mortality from bloodstream infections as compared to adults.103 Robust data on the safety and efficacy of newer antibiotics in large paediatric cohorts remains a critical need, requiring coordinated multicentre and multinational collaboration.

As novel agents for the treatment of resistant Gram-positive bacterial infections emerge, advocacy for early paediatric studies is vital to drive regulatory approval and enable access for children. Limited access to treatment options for drug-resistant Gram-positive infections in the PICT region is likely driven by the high cost of newer antibiotics for both governments and patients, alongside constrained regulatory capacity. Advocacy for mechanisms such as tiered pricing and patent pooling may help address these barriers and improve access. There also remains an essential need for dedicated paediatric PK/PD studies to define dosing across the paediatric age spectrum and identify appropriate therapeutic drug monitoring targets for drugs such as linezolid, where adult therapeutic targets are extrapolated to children.

This review has some limitations. Firstly, the included studies were not specifically assessed for quality or risk of bias. Secondly, as noted in the introduction, the focus of this review was on WHO critical and high priority pathogens, and as such data on other important Gram-positive pathogens in children including antibiotic-resistant S. pneumoniae, Group A and B streptococci were not assessed. Finally, as data on availability of agents in the PICT region was limited, a comprehensive assessment of drug availability by individual country was not possible.

Drug-resistant Gram-positive infections pose significant future challenges for children across Oceania, particularly in the PICT region, where access to recommended antimicrobial agents for MRSA and VRE are limited. Urgent investment and advocacy to generate early paediatric-specific data to drive the licensing of these medications in children are needed. Such studies will improve treatment access and inform the appropriate use of current and novel agents for drug-resistant infections in children.
