NFATc1 activates the Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK pathway to promote the progression of lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Aijuan Sun, Jiayu Yang, Yanhua Chang, Shudong Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence rate and mortality rate of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) are in the forefront of malignant tumors in recent years. Due to its atypical early manifestations, many patients are diagnosed at advanced stages and miss the opportunity for timely intervention, resulting in a poor 5-year overall survival rate. Therefore, identifying specific targets in LUAD is essential for molecular precision therapy. This study integrates clinical and basic research to elucidate the diagnostic value and clinical significance of nuclear factor of activated T-cells cytoplasmic 1 (NFATc1) in LUAD.MethodsImmunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) were first employed to detect the protein expression and localization of NFATc1 in LUAD and adjacent tissues. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was then performed to evaluate the diagnostic potential of NFATc1 for LUAD, and to analyze the correlation between NFATc1 expression and clinical data of LUAD patients. Subsequently, NFATc1 messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein expression in A549 LUAD cells were measured using real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and Western blot assays. The effects of NFATc1 silencing on cell proliferation, migration, and invasion were assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), colony formation, wound healing, and Transwell assays. Finally, the signaling pathway, underlying mechanisms and gene alterations types through which NFATc1 influences LUAD progression were predicted and validated.ResultsNFATc1 expression was higher in LUAD tissues than in adjacent normal tissues and was localized in both the cytoplasm and nucleus. Area under the curve (AUC) of NFATc1 in diagnosing LUAD was 0.8020. NFATc1 expression was related to clinical stage, T stage, N stage, pathological grading, and maximum diameter of LUAD. NFATc1 expression in human LUAD (A549) cell line was significantly higher than in human normal lung epithelial BEAS-2B cell line. Silencing NFATc1 reduced the proliferation, migration, and invasion of A549 cells. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis suggested that NFATc1 may be associated with the Ras/Raf/p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway. Pretreatment with the inhibitor SB202190 decreased the expression of proteins related to Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK and significantly suppressed the proliferation, migration, and invasion of A549 cells. Public database analyses revealed that the NFATc1 alterations in LUAD primarily involve gene deletions and gene mutations.ConclusionsNFATc1 is highly expressed in LUAD tissues, and its expression level is closely related to clinical characteristics. NFATc1 may promote the proliferation, migration, and LUAD cell invasion via the Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK pathway, providing a new therapeutic target for LUAD.Keywords: Nuclear factor of activated T-cells cytoplasmic 1 (NFATc1), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK

Highlight box

Key findings

• Nuclear factor of activated T-cells cytoplasmic 1 (NFATc1) can promote the proliferation, migration, and invasion of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) cells via the Ras/Raf/p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, providing a new therapeutic target for LUAD.

What is known and what is new?

• NFATc1 is upregulated in lung cancer cells and participates in various biological behaviors.

• NFATc1 can promote the proliferation, migration, and invasion of LUAD cells via the Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK pathway

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• NFATc1 has great potential for clinical treatment of LUAD and may be used as a therapeutic target. In the future, further validation should be conducted on animal models to lay the foundation for the development of targeted drugs.

Introduction

Lung cancer is a malignant tumor with high mortality, and its incidence rate and death rates are among the highest of all cancers, especially in male patients (1,2). In 2020, there were approximately 2.2 million new cases of lung cancer and 1.8 million deaths worldwide (3). Lung cancer can be classified into two types: small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with NSCLC accounting for more than 85% (4,5); lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common subtype of NSCLC. Treatment of LUAD depends on the patient’s condition and individual differences, and includes surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy (6,7). Due to its atypical early manifestations, many patients are diagnosed at advanced stage and miss the opportunity for timely intervention, resulting in a poor 5-year overall survival rate (8). Therefore, identifying specific targets in LUAD is essential for molecular precision therapy.

Activated T-cell nuclear factor (NFAT) was initially identified as a critical transcription factor for T cell activation and differentiation (9). In the human genome, the NFAT family comprises five different gene subtypes: NFATc1 (also known as NFAT2), NFATc2, NFATc3, NFATc4, and NFAT5 (also known as TonEBP and OREBP), each family member being a subtype produced by different alternative splicing (10). Ren et al. found that NFATc1 is upregulated in lung cancer cells, and its silencing can significantly inhibit cell proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion, while promoting apoptosis (11). Similarly, He et al. also found similar results (12). Another study found that targeting programmed death-1 (PD-1) induces NFATc1 in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells of mouse models for lung cancer and is associated with enhanced anti-tumor cytotoxicity (13). Additionally, three recent studies have reported that DDIAS and NFATc1 can bind and regulate each other, affecting lung cancer cell proliferation and gefitinib resistance (14-16). Liu et al. also found that NFATc1 plays a key role in promoting cell proliferation, overcoming carboplatin-induced DNA damage, and cell cycle arrest, suggesting that NFATc1 may serve as a therapeutic target for overcoming carboplatin resistance in lung cancer (17). These studies indicate that NFATc1 is closely associated with the progression, prognosis, and drug resistance mechanisms of lung cancer. However, there have been no reports on the correlation between NFATc1 expression and the clinical characteristics of LUAD patients, and the underlying mechanisms by which NFATc1 affects the phenotypes of LUAD cells remain unclear. This study aims to explore the correlation between NFATc1 expression and the clinical characteristics of LUAD patients, as well as to clarify the underlying mechanisms by which NFATc1 affects the phenotypes of LUAD cells. This study aims to explore the expression and diagnostic value of NFATc1 in LUAD, and further investigate the mechanism by which NFATc1 affects the progression of LUAD. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1481/rc).

Methods

Collection of patient tissue specimens

A total of 127 patients who provided LUAD tissues and adjacent normal tissues from January 2022 to December 2023 in our hospital were randomly selected. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Wuxi People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University granted approval for the study (No. KY25021) in December 2021. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. All cases were diagnosed with LUAD by three senior pathologists who jointly reviewed the images. None of the patients received any treatment, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or biological therapy, prior to surgery, and there was no statistical difference in clinical basic data (P≥0.05). Based on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and under the guidance of a pathologist, the sampling site was identified to make a tissue microarray. Selected tissue samples were subsequently used for immunohistochemical staining and immunohistochemical fluorescence staining. Inclusion criteria: (I) patients diagnosed with LUAD through histopathological diagnosis; (II) patients with LUAD whose cancer tissue specimens have been obtained and preserved intact; (III) patients who had no relevant treatments such as surgery, radiotherapy or chemotherapy before admission; (IV) patients with LUAD at stages I to III; (V) patients with LUAD who have complete clinical data. Exclusion criteria: (I) those with heart, lung or kidney function disorders; (II) those with blood system diseases; (III) those with infectious diseases; (IV) those with other malignant tumors; (V) those with cognitive impairment or abnormal mental and behavioral conditions; (VI) patients with excessive tissue bleeding and necrosis.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Tissue slices were first baked in a constant-temperature oven at 56 °C for at least 1 hour for dewaxing. The slices were then sequentially immersed in xylene I for 30 minutes, xylene II for 30 minutes, 100% ethanol for 10 minutes, 95% ethanol for 10 minutes, 90% ethanol for 10 minutes, 80% ethanol for 10 minutes, and 70% ethanol for 10 minutes. The slices were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 3 minutes each and rinsed in distilled water for later use. The following day, the slices were placed in a microwave and heated at medium power for 3 minutes, followed by a 10-minute standing period to allow antigen retrieval. The slices were then incubated in 0.1% to 0.3% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 25 minutes and washed three times with PBS for 5 minutes each. Subsequently, the slices were incubated at room temperature with 4% hydrogen peroxide for 10 minutes to block endogenous peroxidase. The slices were then incubated at 37 °C and blocked with 10% normal goat serum for 60 minutes, followed by three washes with PBS for 5 minutes each. The slices were incubated with NFATc1 antibody (Invitrogen, PA5-79730, Waltham, USA.) at 37 °C for 30 minutes, and then washed three times with PBS for 5 minutes each. Next, the slices were incubated with the Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP) polymer anti mouse/rabbit IHC kit (MXB, KIT-5010, Fuzhou, China) at 37 °C for 30 minutes, followed by three PBS washes for 5 minutes each. The slices were then stained with 0.01% diaminobenzidine (DAB) at room temperature for 5 minutes and washed three times with PBS for 5 minutes each. Subsequently, the slices were counterstained with hematoxylin at room temperature for 3 minutes and washed three times with PBS for 5 minutes each. Finally, the slices were dehydrated in ethanol, mounted, and imaged using a standard optical microscope. For each tissue slice, six representative fields were photographed, and the average optical density (AOD = IOD/area) was calculated using ImageJ software. The average value was used to reflect the expression level of NFATc1 protein. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Tissue slices were first baked in a constant-temperature oven at 56 °C for at least 1 hour for dewaxing. The slices were then sequentially immersed in xylene I for 30 minutes, xylene II for 30 minutes, 100% ethanol for 10 minutes, 95% ethanol for 10 minutes, 90% ethanol for 10 minutes, 80% ethanol for 10 minutes, and 70% ethanol for 10 minutes. The slices were washed three times with PBS for 3 minutes each and rinsed in distilled water for later use. On the following day, the slices were placed in a microwave and heated at medium power for 3 minutes, followed by a 10-minute standing period for antigen retrieval. The slices were then incubated in 0.1–0.3% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 25 minutes and washed three times with PBS for 5 minutes each. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation with 4% hydrogen peroxide at room temperature for 10 minutes. Non-specific binding sites were blocked by incubating the slices with 10% normal goat serum at 37 °C for 60 minutes, followed by three PBS washes of 5 minutes each. The slices were incubated with NFATc1 primary antibody (Invitrogen, PA5-79730) at 37 °C for 30 minutes, followed by three PBS washes of 5 minutes each. Fluorescent secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, A-11034) were then applied at 37 °C for 30 minutes, followed by three additional PBS washes of 5 minutes each. The slices were subsequently stained with 0.01% DAB at room temperature for 15 minutes and washed twice with PBS for 5 minutes each. Finally, the slices were dehydrated in ethanol, mounted with 10 µL of sealing solution per slice, and imaged using a fluorescence microscope. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

BEAS-2B and A549 cells were seeded at 2×104 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured for 12 hours. Total RNA was extracted using RNA/DNA extraction kit (Beyotime, R0017S, Shanghai, China). Reverse transcription of RNA into cDNA was performed using the BeyoRT™ II First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Beyotime, D7168S) following instructions. RT-qPCR was performed as follows: 2 minutes of preheating at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 seconds and 55 °C for 30 seconds. The primer sequences were as follows: GAPDH forward 5'-GCACCGTCAAGGCTGAGAAC-3'; GAPDH reverse 5'-TGGTGAAGACGCAGTGGA-3'; NFATc1 forward 5'-CCATGAAGTCAGCGGAGAA-3'; NFATc1 reverse 5'-GAGGTCTGAAGGTGTGGCA-3'. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Western blot

Cells were collected by scraping and lysed in 500 µL of RIPA buffer. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Concentration Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0009). Samples were boiled in water for 3 minutes and slowly added onto the gel hole for constant pressure electrophoresis. Proteins were then transferred to membranes at 200 mA, with the specific time adjusted according to the size of the target protein. After membrane transfer, membranes were immediately placed in 5% blocking buffer for 1 hour. Membranes were then incubated overnight with the corresponding antibody in a 4 °C chromatography cabinet for binding. GAPDH (Invitrogen, MA5-35235) or β-actin (Invitrogen, PA5-11570) were used as internal references. After the first antibody binding, membranes were washed three times with TBST (TBS with Tween-20) for 10 minutes each. They were then incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) (Invitrogen, 31460) at room temperature for 1 hour, followed by three washes with TBST for 10 minutes each. Finally, protein bands were visualized using BeyoECL Star (Beyotime, P0018AS) on a chemiluminescence imaging system and quantified using ImageJ software. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cell culture and transfection

The BEAS-2B cells [American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), CRL3588] and A549 cells (ATCC, CCL185) used in this study were obtained from the ATCC cell bank. BEAS-2B cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) containing 10% FBS at 37 °C in 5% CO2. A549 cells were cultured daily in DMEM/F-12 medium containing 10% FBS under the same conditions. siRNA NC and siRNA NFATc1 were both designed and acquired from Qiagen, a German company. The specific sequences were: sense strand 5'-CCCGUUCACGUCAGUCUACGUCU-3', antisense strand 5'-AGACGUAGAAACUGUGAACGG-3'. The pCDNA3.1-NFATc1 plasmid design was acquired from Shanghai Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). To investigate the effect of NFATc1 on the proliferation of LUAD cells, A549 cells were seeded at 3×103 cells per well in 96-well plates (100 µL per well). After 6 hours, 100 ng siRNA-NC or siRNA-NFATc1 and 0.2 µL Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000075) were added to each well for transfection. To further clarify the specific mechanism by which NFATc1 promotes LUAD proliferation, A549 cells were seeded at 1×103 cells per well in 96-well plates (100 µL per well). After 6 hours, the experimental group was pretreated with a final concentration of 50 µM p38 MAPK specific inhibitor SB202190 (Beyotime, SC0380-5 mg) for 24 hours, while the corresponding control group was treated with an equal amount of PBS. Subsequently, both groups were transfected with 100 ng pCDNA3.1-NFATc1 plasmid and 0.2 µL Lipo3000 for subsequent testing. To further clarify the effect of NFATc1 on LUAD cell migration, A549 cells were plated in six-well plates at 5×105 cells per well with 2 mL complete medium. After 6 hours, 3 µg siRNA-NC or siRNA NFATc1 and 3 µL Lipo3000 were added to each well for transfection. The medium was replenished every 7 days, and subsequent assays were performed. To further clarify the specific mechanism by which NFATc1 promotes LUAD proliferation, A549 cells were first plated in six-well plates at 1.5×105 cells per well with 2 mL complete medium. After 6 hours, the experimental group was pretreated with a final concentration of 50 µM p38 MAPK specific inhibitor SB202190 for 24 hours, while the control group received an equal volume of PBS. Both groups were then transfected with 3 µg pCDNA3.1-NFATc1 plasmid and 3 µL Lipofectamine 3000 for subsequent testing. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)

CCK-8 (Beyotime, C0037) was purchased from Biyun Tian Biotechnology Company (Shanghai, China). According to the experimental design, CCK-8 detection was conducted at 0, 24, and 48 hours. A total of 3×103 cells per well were seeded into 96-well plates. After 6 hours of cell adhesion, the cells were transfected with 100 ng of plasmid and cultured for the predetermined time. Add 10 µL of CCK-8 solution to each well, incubate at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 1 hour, after which absorbance was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader at 450 nm. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Colony formation assay

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested to prepare a cell suspension. The cell suspension was diluted in a gradient multiple, and cells were seeded at a density of 100 cells per well in 10 mL of preheated (37 °C) culture medium. Cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 according to the experimental requirements. At the end of cultivation, the supernatant was discarded and the cells were gently washed with PBS three times. The cells were fixed with an appropriate amount of 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 minutes, stained with an appropriate amount of GIMSA for 20 minutes, rinsed with running water, dried, and counted by visual inspection. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Wound healing

Horizontal lines were evenly drawn on the back of a six-well plate with a marker, ensuring that at least three lines crossed each well. A sterile toothpick was then used to scratch the cell layer along the marked lines with uniform pressure. Cells were washed in the well twice with PBS to remove non-adherent cells, after which the appropriate fresh serum-free medium was added according to the cell culture conditions. The cells were then placed in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator for continued culture. At designated time points, the width of the scratches was observed and measured, and images were captured. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Transwell assay

Matrigel was first applied to coat the substrate membrane. The specific steps were as follows: Matrigel was mixed with a pre-cooled pipette until uniform, then diluted with serum-free medium to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. A total of 60 µL of the mixture was added vertically to each Transwell chamber and evenly spread at the bottom, avoiding bubble formation. Chambers were then incubated at 37 °C for 1–3 hours, and unbound Matrigel was carefully aspirated. Subsequently, 100 µL of serum-free medium was added and incubated in a 37 °C incubator for 30 minutes for hydration. The liquid was then removed, and the chambers were checked to ensure no leakage into the lower chamber before cell seeding. Cells were resuspended in serum-free medium and adjusted to 8×104 cells/mL, and 100 µL of cell suspension was added to each chamber. After incubation for the required time, the medium from the upper chamber was aspirated, and the chamber was transferred into a 24-well plate containing 600 µL of 4% paraformaldehyde for fixation (25 minutes). The fixative was discarded, and membranes were stained with approximately 800 µL of 0.1% crystal violet for 15 minutes, rinsed with running water, and dried. The membrane was then removed from the chamber with the bottom surface facing upward and allowed to dry. It was then mounted onto a glass slide for sealing. Ten random fields of view were selected under the microscope, positive cells were counted, and statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis is a bioinformatics method used to analyze gene functions and biological processes. Genes are classified based on molecular function (MF), cellular component (CC), and biological process (BP) using the GO database. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) is a large database manually developed by Kanehisa Laboratories (Kyoto, Japan), integrating literature evidence on signaling pathways, genes, diseases, and drugs. KEGG can be used to predict potential key pathways involving specific genes. GO and KEGG analyses were conducted using R language for analysis. Gene lists were first obtained from relevant database and gene IDs were converted. Entrez IDs were generated using the “org.Hs.eg.db” package, and GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were performed using the “clusterProfiler” package in R. The adjusted P and q values are both <0.05.

Public database

cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/) is an open platform containing multidimensional cancer genomics data. In this study, the Cancer Types Summary and Mutations modules were used to obtain detailed information on the frequency and type of NFATc1 changes across cancers as well as LUAD changes.

Statistical analysis

Data were organized, analyzed, and plotted using SPSS 19.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0. Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), and comparisons between two groups were performed using the independent samples t-test. Count data were presented as frequencies, and comparison between two groups was performed using the Chi-squared test. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to analyze the diagnostic value of NFATc1 for LUAD. A P value <0.05 is considered statistically significant.

NFATc1 expression was significantly higher in LUAD tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues (Figure 1A,1B). NFATc1 was widely localized in the cytoplasm and nucleus (Figure 1C), which provides the possibility for further exploration of the mechanism of action of NFATc1 in LUAD.

Clinical value of NFATc1 in LUAD

The diagnostic value of NFATc1 in LUAD was evaluated. The area under the curve (AUC) of NFATc1 for diagnosing LUAD was 0.802 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.744–0.860], which was statistically significant (P<0.001). The optimal cutoff value was 11.01, corresponding to a sensitivity of 0.6772 and a specificity of 0.8819, indicating that NFATc1 possesses certain clinical diagnostic value for LUAD (Figure 2A). To explore the role of NFATc1 in LUAD progression, patients were divided into high and low expression groups based on the median AOD of NFATc1 expression, and the relationship between NFATc1 and clinical data of LUAD patients was analyzed. The results showed that NFATc1 expression was not significantly correlated with gender (P=0.33) and age (P=0.17) of LUAD patients, but was correlated with clinical stage (P<0.001), T stage (P=0.01), N stage (P<0.001), pathological grading (P<0.001), and maximum tumor diameter (P<0.001) (Table 1 and Figure 2B-2G).

NFATc1 affects the proliferation, migration, and invasion of LUAD cells

NFATc1 expression was evaluated in normal human lung epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) and LUAD cells (A549) at the messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein levels. NFATc1 mRNA expression was higher in A549 cells than in BEAS-2B cells (t=0.8213, P=0.001) (Figure 3A). Similarly, protein levels of NFATc1 in A549 cells were higher than in BEAS-2B cells, and the differences were statistically significant (t=10.56, P<0.001) (Figure 3B,3C). The effect of NFATc1 on the proliferation of LUAD cells was assessed using CCK-8 and colony formation assays. The results showed that compared with A549 (siRNA NC), A549 (siRNA NFATc1) exhibited reduced proliferation, and the differences were statistically significant (Figure 3D-3F). The effect of NFATc1 on LUAD cell migration was assessed using a wound healing assay. The results showed that the 48-hours wound closure of A549 (siRNA-NFATc1) cells was significantly lower than that of A549 (siRNA-NC), indicating impaired migratory capacity (Figure 3G,3H). In addition, the effect of NFATc1 on the LUAD cell invasion was detected using Transwell invasion assay. The results showed that the number of cells in the field of view of A549 (siRNA-NFATc1) cells at 24 and 48 hours was significantly lower compared to A549 (siRNA-NC) (Figure 3I,3J).

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of NFATc1

The GO database was used to analyze the biological functions of NFATc1 at three levels: CC, MF, and BP. The results showed that the top ten biological functions associated with NFATc1 included binding to mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) p38 (Figure 4A). In parallel, the KEGG database was used to analyze NFATc1-related signaling pathways. All statistically significant pathways were selected, ranked, and visually represented according to color intensity. The results showed that NFATc1 had the strongest correlation with the MAPK signaling pathway (Figure 4B). Based on the GO analysis, we speculate that NFATc1 may influence the biological functions of LUAD cells via the Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK pathway.

NFATc1 upregulates the expression of Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK pathway

Overexpression of NFATc1 increased the expression of p-p38 (Thr180/Tyr182)/p38, Ras, and p-c-Raf (Ser338)/c-Raf to varying degrees, with all changes being statistically significant. Subsequently, A549 cells were pretreated with p38 MAPK-specific inhibitor SB202190 (final concentration, 50 µM) and transfected with the pCDNA3.1-NFATc1 plasmid to assess the expression changes of p38, p-p38 (Thr180/Tyr182), Ras, c-Raf, and p-c-Raf (Ser338). The results showed that after pretreatment with SB202190, the expression of p-p38 (Thr180/Tyr182)/p38, Ras, and p-c-Raf (Ser338)/c-Raf decreased to varying degrees, with all differences statistically significant (Figure 5A-5D). These results suggested that NFATc1 may upregulate the expression of the Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK pathway.

NFATc1 activates Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK pathway to promote proliferation of LUAD cells

The specific mechanism by which NFATc1 promotes LUAD proliferation was further investigated using CCK-8 and colony formation assays. The results showed that, compared with the A549 (pCDNA3.1-NFATc1) group, the proliferation of the A549 (SB202190+pCDNA3.1-NFATc1) group was reduced, and the difference was statistically significant (Figure 6A-6C). These findings suggested that NFATc1 may promote LUAD cell proliferation via the Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK pathway.

Discussion

In recent years, numerous studies have found that NFATc1, as a transcription factor that regulates downstream gene expression, not only plays an important role in maintaining cellular homeostasis but also possesses strong carcinogenic potential (18-21). NFATc1, as a key factor in various intracellular signaling pathways, plays a critical role in the occurrence and progression of malignant tumors. Abnormal NFATc1 expression can dysregulate downstream target genes, activate related oncogenes, and promote cellular carcinogenesis (21,22). Moreover, abnormal NFATc1 expression enables cancer cells to resist autonomous apoptosis, affect the tumor microenvironment, degrade extracellular matrix, and promote cancer cell infiltration and metastasis (23,24). Clearly, NFATc1 is of great significance for clinical targeted therapy of malignant tumors.

This study focused on NFATc1 and first explored its expression and clinical significance in LUAD tissues and cells. The results showed that NFATc1 was highly expressed in both LUAD tissues and cells, and was widely localized in the cytoplasm and nucleus. ROC curve analysis further indicated that NFATc1 has significant diagnostic value for LUAD. Consistently, Ren et al. and Liu et al. found similar results, laying the foundation for further exploration of the mechanism of action of NFATc1 in LUAD (11,17).

To date, no studies have reported the relationship between NFATc1 expression and clinical progression in LUAD patients. We analyzed the relationship between NFATc1 expression in clinical LUAD tissues and clinical data of LUAD patients. The results showed that NFATc1 expression was related to pathological stage, T stage, N stage, pathological grading, and maximum tumor diameter of LUAD patients. Subsequently, we further analyzed the intergroup relationship between NFATc1 expression and clinical data of LUAD patients. The results showed that NFATc1 expression was higher in stage II and III compared with stage I. In terms of T staging, NFATc1 expression was higher in the T2, T3, and T4 groups compared with the T1 group, and higher in the N2 group than in the N0 group. In terms of pathological grading, expression levels were higher in G2 and G3 than in G1, with G3 exceeding G2. Tumors with a maximum diameter >3 cm exhibited higher NFATc1 expression than those with a diameter ≤3 cm. These findings suggested that NFATc1 expression increases with disease severity.

To further clarify the role of NFATc1 in LUAD, a series of cellular experiments was conducted to evaluate its effects on the proliferation, migration, and invasion in the A549 LUAD cell line. Specifically, CCK-8 and colony formation assays demonstrated that NFATc1 can promote the proliferation of LUAD A549 cells compared to the control group. Wound healing assay further showed that NFATc1 can promote the migration of LUAD A549 cells compared to the control group. Transwell assays indicated that NFATc1 can promote the invasion of LUAD A549 cells compared to the control group. Studies have shown that NFATc1 can promote proliferation and epithelial-mesenchymal transition in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (12,17). A study indicates that NFATc1 can promote the migration and invasion of lung cancer cells, supporting the conclusions of this study, potentially by modulating related signaling pathways (11).

To explore the potential mechanism by which NFATc1 promotes the proliferation, migration, and LUAD cell invasion, we hypothesized—based on GO and KEGG enrichment analysis—that NFATc1 may affect the proliferation, migration, and LUAD cell invasion via the Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK pathway. Mitogen activated protein kinases (MAPKs) are intracellular kinases that serve as focal points for various extracellular stimuli and regulate various cellular processes (25,26). The MAPK pathway consists of a three-tiered signaling cascade: MAPK, MAPK kinase (MEK or MKK), and MAPK kinase kinase (MEKK or MKKK) (27,28). Studies have shown that p38 MAPK, one of the four main branch pathways of the MAPK pathway, possesses a cascade effect that can be activated by various pro-inflammatory and stress stimuli, and plays an important role in immune inflammation, cell proliferation, differentiation, and carcinogenesis processes (29,30). Therefore, we focused on the signaling pathway and utilized the p38 MAPK specific inhibitor SB202190 along with the pCDNA3.1-NFATc1 plasmid to further explore the intrinsic molecular mechanisms by which NFATc1 promotes proliferation, migration, and LUAD cell invasion. Firstly, pretreatment with SB202190 led to a decrease in the expression of p-p38 (Thr180/Tyr182)/p38, Ras, and p-c-Raf (Ser338)/c-Raf to varying degrees, indicating that NFATc1 may affect the expression of the Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK pathway. Subsequently, compared with A549 (pCDNA3.1-NFATc1), A549 (SB202190 + pCDNA3.1-NFATc1) shows slower proliferation, indicating that NFATc1 may promote LUAD cell proliferation via the Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK pathway. Similarly, compared with the A549 (pCDNA3.1-NFATc1) group, the migration of the A549 (SB202190 + pCDNA3.1-NFATc1) group was reduced, indicating that NFATc1 may promote LUAD cell migration via the Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK pathway. We also found that, compared with A549 (pCDNA3.1-NFATc1), A549 (SB202190 + pCDNA3.1-NFATc1) exhibited reduced invasion, indicating that NFATc1 may promote LUAD cell invasion via the Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK pathway.

Studies have shown that NFATc1 plays a critical role in the activation, survival, proliferation, and differentiation of T cells, particularly in the differentiation of T helper 17 cells (Th17), T follicular helper cells (TfHs), and regulatory T cells (Tregs) (31-33). Kostel Bal et al. found that mutations in the NFATc1 protein site can lead to congenital immunodeficiency, primarily manifested as impaired CD8+ T cell function and glycolytic disorders, as well as recurrent infections, hypogammaglobulinemia, and decreased antibody response (34). Based on these findings, we explored NFATc1 mutations in LUAD using the cBioPortal database. The results showed that the main types of NFATc1 gene alterations in LUAD were gene deletions, mutations, and amplifications, with mutations predominantly occurring in the RHD_DNA-bind and TIG domains.

Conclusions

In summary, we found that NFATc1 may promote proliferation, migration, and LUAD cell invasion via the Ras/Raf/p38 MAPK pathway. This indicates that NFATc1 may serve as a new potential target for LUAD, laying a theoretical foundation for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of LUAD.
