Pronounced temporal variability of plant–pollinator networks in response to changing precipitation in the southern Chihuahuan Desert
Authors: Andrés Pereira-Guaqueta, Vinicio J Sosa, Alberto Búrquez, María C Mandujano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant–pollinator interactions are essential for maintaining biodiversity and ecosystem functioning. Their analysis through ecological networks enables the assessment of the structure, resilience, and temporal dynamics of these mutualistic systems. However, research on the temporal variation of pollination networks is scarce for arid and semi-arid environments, where biological communities are highly dynamic due to climate variability. Here, we characterized the temporal variation of plant–pollinator networks in a semi-arid scrubland of the southern Chihuahuan Desert across two years with contrasting precipitation. We conducted monthly surveys of pollination interactions, reconstructed temporally discrete networks for three seasons of each year, and evaluated intra- and inter-annual changes in network composition, structure, and robustness to species loss. Interaction variability was modulated by precipitation dynamics, more pronounced among seasons of the same year than between years, and mainly driven by temporal turnover of the plant species in bloom. Greater rainfall was linked to higher plant and pollinator richness and a greater number of connectors, module hubs, and network hubs, especially herbs, dipterans, and lepidopterans. We recorded substantial temporal variation in species’ structural roles, yet highlight the importance of generalist bees for network cohesion over time. Seasonal networks showed a persistent compound topology, where nestedness occurs within modules rather than at the whole-network scale. Specialization and modularity tended to increase under drought conditions, accompanied by a decrease in network robustness to species loss. Our results underscore the remarkable diversity and plasticity of plant–pollinator networks in semi-arid environments and provide valuable insights for their conservation under climate change scenarios.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00442-026-05886-6.Keywords: Community dynamics, Ecological interactions, Pollination, Seasonality, Semi-arid ecosystem

Introduction

Ecological interactions between plants and pollinators are crucial for the persistence of biological communities, terrestrial ecosystem functioning, and the delivery of ecosystem services (Jordano et al. 2009; Burkle and Alarcón 2011; Elle et al. 2012). About 90% of all angiosperms are estimated to rely on animals for pollination (Tong et al. 2023), and more than 350,000 arthropods and over 1400 vertebrate species have been documented using flowers regularly (many of which closely depend on their resources and function as pollinators; Regan et al. 2015; Wardhaugh 2015). Moreover, pollination-derived seeds and fruits represent vital food sources for a great diversity of animals, including humans (Jordano et al. 2009).

Network analysis has contributed to our understanding of plant–pollinator communities (Jordano et al. 2009; Vizentin-Bugoni et al. 2018), offering enriching perspectives on biodiversity, e.g., how species interact with each other, and what is the hierarchical contribution of particular taxa and traits in structuring communities and ecosystems (Danieli-Silva et al. 2012; Elle et al. 2012). Several emergent properties of plant–pollinator interactions at the community level have been recognized through network analysis. For example, many pollination networks exhibit a nested pattern in which specialist species mainly interact with generalist partners, while generalists also interact with each other, resulting in species’ functional redundancy and very few specialist-to-specialist interactions (Jordano et al. 2009; Elle et al. 2012). Several plant–pollinator networks are also organized in subgroups of tightly linked species called modules, which have been related to species functional diversity, pollination syndromes, and niche partitioning processes (Olesen et al. 2007; Danieli-Silva et al. 2012; Vizentin-Bugoni et al. 2018; Ballarin et al. 2026). Moreover, it is now recognized that ecological networks often exhibit compound topologies, in which the nested pattern mostly takes place within modules rather than at the whole-network (Dormann et al. 2009; Pinheiro et al. 2019; Lampo et al. 2024). These structural attributes influence the responses of plant–pollinator communities to ecological disturbance and network robustness to species loss, which can be quantified and modeled through network analysis (Burgos et al. 2007; Jordano et al. 2009). Hence, this approach has become a valuable tool for ecosystem management, conservation, and restoration (Elle et al. 2012; Vizentin-Bugoni et al. 2018).

Research on the temporal dynamics of plant–pollinator networks is relatively scarce, especially in tropical and subtropical regions, although previous studies have proved them to be remarkably variable across temporal scales (reviewed in Vizentin-Bugoni et al. 2018; Burkle and Alarcón 2011; CaraDonna et al. 2021). Much of this temporal variability has to do with changes in the identity and functional position of network nodes and links, driven by two main processes: species turnover and interaction rewiring (CaraDonna et al. 2021; Fründ 2021). In turn, these are determined by the phenology, relative abundance, and interacting plasticity of plants and pollinators over time (Burkle and Alarcón 2011; CaraDonna et al. 2017; Fründ 2021). Whether this variation is reflected in network topological changes appears to depend on the temporal scale of analysis (CaraDonna et al. 2021). While properties like nestedness and modularity tend to remain stable between years for annual networks (Burkle and Alarcón 2011), they have been reported to vary widely at other temporal scales: throughout the day (Ratoni et al. 2025), across weeks, months, and climatic seasons (e.g., Souza et al. 2018; Rabeling et al. 2019; Argueta-Guzmán et al. 2022; Tavera et al. 2023; Ballarin et al. 2026), and even along successional stages (e.g., Cortés-Flores et al. 2023).

Arid environments are highly dynamic due to their strong seasonality, which is not only related to temperature variance but also to their notable water limitation (Noy-Meir 1973). The scant precipitation of arid and semi-arid ecosystems is irregularly distributed throughout the year and highly variable among years (e.g., Martínez-Falcón et al. 2011; Wright et al. 2015; Rowe et al. 2024). Such variability has marked effects on the flowering phenology of plants and the emergence times of pollinators, modulated by their life history strategies (e.g., annual or perennial plants, polylectic or oligolectic animals; Pavón and Briones 2001; Crimmins et al. 2011; Minckley et al. 2013; Kazenel et al. 2020). Thus, pollination networks of arid and semi-arid ecosystems are expected to be especially dynamic at various temporal scales, but there is a paucity of research on this subject (Lance et al. 2017; Vizentin-Bugoni et al. 2018; Argueta-Guzmán et al. 2022). The arid zones of North America are changing toward drier conditions and more severe droughts (Zhao et al. 2023), posing a threat to the enormous diversity they harbor (Crimmins et al. 2011). Understanding the temporal variation of plant–pollinator networks becomes especially relevant for proper management and conservation under such climate change scenario (Burkle and Alarcón 2011; Elle et al. 2012; CaraDonna et al. 2021).

The objective of this study was to characterize the temporal variation of plant–pollinator networks in a semi-arid locality of the southern Chihuahuan Desert within and between two years with contrasting rainfall levels. We specifically aimed to (1) assess the intra- and inter-annual variability of network composition and the relative contribution of species turnover and interaction rewiring, (2) evaluate changes in network structure and robustness to species loss across both temporal scales, (3) identify plant and pollinator taxa that play key structural roles for the networks over time, and (4) explore the influence of precipitation dynamics on the observed temporal network variability.

Materials and methods

Study site

This study was conducted in a 7.3-hectare fragment of xeric scrubland preserved by the Jardín Botánico Regional de Cadereyta (Fig. 1), located in the municipality of Cadereyta de Montes, Querétaro, Mexico (20º 41′ 15.8" N, 99º 48′ 17.7"W, 2046 m asl). This site belongs to the Queretano-Hidalguense Arid Zone, a subregion of the southernmost portion of the Chihuahuan Desert recognized for its high diversity and endemism of plants and some pollinator groups, especially cacti (Zamudio et al. 1992; Hernández-Magaña et al. 2012; Aranda-Pineda et al. 2025) and bees (Michener 2007; Argueta-Guzmán et al. 2022). It is characterized by geological formations of volcanic origin and well-drained, shallow, rocky soils (Zamudio et al. 1992; Hernández-Magaña et al. 2012). The vegetation exhibits strongly seasonal dynamics and is dominated by Opuntia spp. (Cactaceae) and shrubs of the Fabaceae and Asteraceae (Zamudio et al. 1992). Climate is semi-dry temperate with summer rains, BS1kw(w) according to Köppen classification modified by García Amaro (2004). Average annual temperature ranges between 15 °C and 18 °C, and average annual precipitation between 400 mm and 600 mm (Hernández-Magaña et al. 2012). This study was carried out during two consecutive years with highly different precipitation amounts (Online Resource 1): a particularly dry year with 262.6 mm of annual rainfall (2023, hereafter ‘dry year’), and an average year with 588 mm of total precipitation (2024, hereafter ‘wet year’).

Sampling of plant–pollinator interactions

We sampled plant–pollinator interactions monthly, from February to November 2023, and from March to May and July to November 2024. The study area has pre-established fire trails that were used as transects; we delimited two fixed sampling trails, one 850 m long and the other 1000 m long. A single observer walked along a given trail each day from 08:00 to 18:00 h, randomly selecting blooming plants to record their floral visitors. Each observation event lasted 20 min, after which the walk was resumed to continue sampling. Observations were conducted on clear days without rain, either directly on the flowers (for insects) or using binoculars (for birds and big-bodied insects). We recorded the number of visits of each animal species to the floral units observed; floral units were defined as individual flowers or easily distinguishable inflorescences where average-sized floral visitors foraged without requiring flight. We only considered legitimate interactions, i.e., when the animal physically interacted with the flowers’ anthers or stigmas. Sampling effort varied among plant species according to their monthly occurrence (Souza et al. 2018). In total, we conducted 212.7 h of observation (107.7 h in 2023 and 105 h in 2024).

We further used video recording as a complementary method to assess plant–pollinator interactions. For insects, standardized observers recorded five-minute videos on random sets of floral units along the sampling trails. We also installed one GoPro Hero9 camera occasionally near flowering plants for vertebrate recording. A total of 8.7 h of video were recorded on 17 plants species (4.3 h in 2023, 4.4 h in 2024).

We identified plants and floral visitors to the lowest taxonomic level possible. Several taxa were identified directly in the field or by experts using photographs and video (see Acknowledgements). Reference specimens of some bees and wasps were collected and identified using the guides by Michener (2007) and Fernández and Sharkey (2006), and by comparing them to reference collections (Munguia-Soto et al. 2023); they were deposited in the National Insect Collection of the Institute of Biology at the National Autonomous University of Mexico, UNAM. We used the photo guides of Miranda et al. (2013) and Dankowicz and Dankowicz (2025) for the identification of syrphids and bombyliids, and the field guide by Maruri Aguilar et al., (2013) for birds. Scientific names were standardized following World Flora Online (WFO 2025) for plants and Catalogue of Life (CoL; Bánki et al. 2025) for most pollinators; we used the Integrated Taxonomic Information System (ITIS 2025) for some Coleoptera and Hymenoptera not included in CoL, and Butterflies of America (Warren et al. 2024) for diurnal Lepidoptera. Plants were further categorized according to their growth habit and life cycle as either annual herbs, perennial herbs, shrubs, trees, or succulent perennials.

Data analysis

Sampling completeness

To assess sampling completeness of pairwise interactions and plant and animal species, we built sample-size-based rarefaction and extrapolation curves from the direct observation data, calculated sample coverage sensu Chao and Jost (2012), and computed the Chao1 richness estimator with the iNEXT package in R (Hsieh et al. 2016; R Core Team 2024), both for pooled data and for each year separately. For interaction richness, each combination of a given plant and pollinator taxa (network link) was considered as equivalent to “species” (Jordano et al. 2009). The index of sample coverage estimates the proportion of the total number of individuals (or individual interactions) in a given community that belongs to the species (or links) represented in the sample (thus accounting for differences in occurrence frequency; Chao and Jost 2012).

Interaction matrices

We constructed weighted bipartite interaction matrices for network analysis, with each matrix cell containing the total number of visits observed from a given pollinator to each plant species. It has long been recognized that the analysis of year-long interaction networks should be done at shorter, ecologically relevant periods that reflect the time scale of the system (i.e., flowering phenology and pollinator flight periods; Burkle and Alarcón 2011). Therefore, along with annual matrices, we constructed a matrix for each of the following periods of each year: spring, from February to April (t1), foresummer–rainy season, from May to August (t2), and autumn, from September to November (t3). These periods were defined upon hierarchical cluster analyses of the sampling months, using the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity index based on the monthly registration frequency of plant and pollinator species, reflecting their phenology (see Online Resource 2). From the interaction matrices, we reconstructed a total of two annual networks and six seasonal networks (three for each year), plotted with the circlize package in R (Gu et al. 2014).

Temporal variation in network composition

We applied the methodology of Fründ (2021) to evaluate temporal changes in network link composition using the bipartite package in R (Dormann et al. 2008). For the two annual networks, the networks of consecutive periods within each year, and networks of the same season in different years, we calculated the Sørensen-based index of overall network dissimilarity (βWN) and its two components: dissimilarity due to species turnover (βST) and dissimilarity due to interaction rewiring among shared species (βOS). Values for these indices range from 0 to 1, with higher values denoting higher network dissimilarity. The relative contribution of plant and pollinator turnover to βST was also quantified.

Rainfall is considered the master input of arid ecosystems, a paradigm that has changed little in 50 years (Noy-Meir 1973). In our study site, precipitation variation was pronounced both within years (e.g., from 0 to 186 mm monthly in 2024) and between years (e.g., from 86 to 186 mm in both years’ rainiest months; Online Resource 1). Therefore, we aimed to explore the influence of temporal precipitation variability on the observed variation of network composition. To do so, we fit two simple linear regression models with (1) the overall dissimilarity among compared seasonal networks (βWN) and (2) the relative contribution of species turnover (βST βWN−1) as response variables, respectively, and precipitation change among the compared periods as the predictor variable in both cases. Due to our limited number of seasonal networks, we run the regression analyses with the pooled data of both intra- and inter-annual comparisons (see Online Resource 3). Moreover, our network dissimilarity values were not independent since they involve comparisons of the same periods, rendering the p-values of the regression models biased. To overcome this, we used a Mantel-like permutation procedure to estimate the significance of the observed slope coefficients of both fitted models (Rabeling et al. 2019). Specifically, we re-fitted each regression model 999 times, randomly shuffling the predictor variable among observations; we then compared the observed regression slopes with the null distribution of the permutation models’ slopes to obtain empirical p-values (Hassler et al. 2010).

Network structure and robustness to species loss

We used the bipartite package in R to characterize the following structural properties of each network. (1) Connectance (C), the proportion of all possible interactions that were observed (ranging from 0–1; Jordano et al. 2009). (2) Network specialization (H2’), which indicates if species restrict their interactions compared to those expected from partners’ availability; it scores from 0 to 1 as specialization increases (Blüthgen et al. 2006). (3) Modularity (Q), the degree to which different sets of plants and pollinators interact within tightly linked modules, with fewer links outside of them; it ranges from 0 to 1 with increasing compartmentalization (Beckett 2016). (4) Nestedness, which indicates to what extent the interactions of specialized nodes represent a subset of the interactions of more generalist nodes from the same trophic level. We used a weighted variant of NODF index (weighted nestedness based on overlap and decreasing abundance, wNODA; Pinheiro et al. 2019). NODF is not biased by network dimension (Dormann et al. 2009), and this variant is less sensitive to weak links than the binary one (Pinheiro et al. 2019); it scores from 0 to 100, with 100 representing completely nested networks. The significance of network specialization, modularity, and nestedness was assessed by comparing the observed values with those expected from null model networks. We used the type I null model sensu Dormann et al. (2009) based on Patefield algorithm, which builds randomized interaction matrices with the same dimension and same marginal totals as the empirical matrices. 1000 null matrices were computed for each network. Observed values were considered significant when they differed from the distribution of modeled values with 95% confidence.

To test for a compound topology, we computed the modules of all seasonal networks that showed significant modularity levels, using the DIRTLPAwb + algorithm of Beckett (2016) available in the bipartite package. We then calculated the within-module nestedness degree of each network (wNODAsm) by computing its pairwise nestedness scores only between species that belonged to the same modules (following Pinheiro et al. 2019). A paired samples t-test was used to compare each network’s wNODAsm to its whole-network wNODA value (which averages the pairwise nestedness scores among all pairs of species irrespective of module affiliation); we expected wNODAsm to be significantly higher than wNODA in networks with a compound topology (Pinheiro et al. 2019).

We also used module reconstruction to identify the topological role of all plant and pollinator nodes in each seasonal network, based on their within-module degree (z) and their among-module connectivity (c), following Olesen et al. (2007). They were classified as either peripheral nodes (when z ≤ 2.5 and c ≤ 0.62), module hubs (when z > 2.5 and c ≤ 0.62), connectors (when z ≤ 2.5 and c > 0.62) or network hubs (when z > 2.5 and c > 0.62); the latter three are considered key roles for the structure of modular pollination networks (Olesen et al. 2007). Since the module-computing algorithm can render slightly different module configurations between trials, we run it 100 times for each seasonal network and only considered species to perform a key structural role when their z and/or c values were higher that the pre-defined thresholds in at least 95% of the iterations; for those species, we report the most frequently assigned role across trials.

We further applied the robustness index of the bipartite package for all networks (Burgos et al. 2007). It calculates the area under an extinction curve that describes the rate of cumulative secondary extinction expected in a certain guild (plants or pollinators) as species from the other guild are lost in a sequenced manner. We did 1000 simulations of random stepwise elimination of each trophic level for each network. This index ranges from 0 to 1 with increasing robustness to species loss (hereafter robustness). Finally, we used Spearman correlation tests to explore the relationship between precipitation levels and the structural properties and robustness of each seasonal network, as well as plant and pollinator species richness.

Results

We recorded a total of 19,500 single floral visits across the two years, representing 1040 different pairwise interactions (392 in the dry year, 761 in the wet year) between 120 plant species and 270 pollinator morphospecies. Species richness was higher in the wet year than in the dry year (Table 1) and positively correlated with seasonal precipitation (plants: Spearman’s rho = 0.886, p = 0.019, n = 6; pollinators: Spearman’s rho = 0.943, p = 0.005, n = 6; Online Resource 4). Plants belonged to 39 different families, the most representative being Asteraceae (31 species), Cactaceae (10 species) and Fabaceae (6 species; Online Resource 5). Pollinators belonged to five insect orders (84 morphospecies of Hymenoptera, 81 of Diptera, 77 of Lepidoptera, 15 of Coleoptera, and 5 of Hemiptera) and four bird families (5 species of Trochilidae and one species of Icteridae, Picidae, and Troglodytidae each). The best represented families were Syrphidae (34 species), Apidae (26 species), Hesperiidae (19 species), and Bombyliidae (15 species; Online Resource 6). Across both years, the estimated sample coverage was 62.7% for pairwise interactions and around 95% for plant and pollinator species. According to the Chao1 estimator, we recorded approximately 42.6% of the predicted interaction richness, 83.3% of the plant species, and 73.4% of the animal species potentially involved in the pollination networks (Online Resources 7 and 8).

Temporal variation in network composition

The two annual networks shared 54 plant and 104 pollinator species (Online Resources 5 and 6), and had an overall dissimilarity of βWN = 0.817, mainly due to interaction rewiring (βOS = 0.510) and less so to species turnover (βST = 0.307); 61% of the latter dissimilarity component was due to changes in flowering plant species. Regarding seasonal networks, variation in link composition tended to be greater within than between years (Fig. 2). Only two plant species and 15 pollinator species were common to the three seasonal networks of the dry year, and only five plant and 25 pollinator species for the wet year (Online Resources 5 and 6). Intra-annual network dissimilarity was more pronounced in the dry year and highest between the dry spring (t1) and the foresummer–rainy season (t2) in both years. Species turnover was more important than interaction rewiring for within-year network variation, especially floral turnover; rewiring was relatively more important in the wet year than in the dry year. Interannual dissimilarity of seasonal networks increased from spring (t1) to autumn (t3; Fig. 2). The main cause of between-year variation was also species turnover for summer and autumn networks, but rewiring for spring; floral turnover made a greater contribution than pollinator turnover to the inter-annual dissimilarity of spring and autumn networks. The rainier year and periods showed a notably higher diversity and frequency of herbaceous plants and dipteran and lepidopteran pollinators (Fig. 2).

Network structure and robustness to species loss

All networks showed low connectance and were significantly specialized and modular (Table 1). Values of whole-network nestedness were minimal and significantly lower than those expected from null models in all cases. For all seasonal networks, within-module nestedness was significantly higher than whole-network nestedness (paired t-test: t 5 = 10.32, p < 0.001; Online Resource 9). Both annual networks were very similar in terms of topology (Table 1). Regarding seasonal networks, connectance was the only attribute that was significantly correlated with precipitation; it tended to decrease with greater rainfall (Spearman’s rho = -0.986, p < 0.001, n = 6; Online Resource 4). Network specialization and modularity increased greatly throughout the dry year (Table 1). In the wet year, network specialization decreased over the seasons, while modularity remained constant in spring (t1) and autumn (t3), reaching its lowest value in the foresummer–rainy season (t2). Robustness values for plants and pollinators tended to be greater in the wet year, both for the annual and the seasonal networks (Table 1). Robustness decreased throughout the dry year, while it reached its highest values in the autumnal period of the wet year.

The identity of nodes with key structural roles varied widely across years and seasons (Online Resources 5 and 6), with no plants and only five pollinator species common to both years (the bees Anthophora californica, Apis mellifera, Augochlorella sp., and Lasioglossum (Dialictus) sp., and the butterfly Dione vanillae). One tree species (spring-flowering Neltuma laevigata) and 11 pollinator species acted as module connectors or network hubs in the seasonal networks of the dry year, mainly generalist bees (e.g., Megachile zapoteca in summer, Melissoptila otomita in autumn). During the wet year, one shrub species (Perymenium mendezii) functioned as module hub and 16 other plant species as connectors of the summer or autumn networks, mainly herbs of Asteraceae (e.g., Tridax rosea, Bidens pilosa) and shrubs of several families (e.g., Mimosa aculeaticarpa, Buddleja perfoliata). Moreover, two coleopteran, eight lepidopteran, and 13 dipteran species acted as module connectors in the summer and/or autumn networks of the wet year, along with two flies (Syritta pipiens, Peleteria sp. 1) and two butterflies (Libytheana carinenta, Nathalis iole) that served as network hubs (Online Resources 5 and 6).

Discussion

Research on the temporal dynamics of comprehensive pollination networks is scarce (CaraDonna et al. 2021), especially in the tropics (Vizentin-Bugoni et al. 2018). We aimed to address this gap by characterizing the intra-and inter-annual variation of a plant–pollinator network in the Queretano-Hidalguense Arid Zone, an understudied subregion of the southern Chihuahuan Desert (Argueta-Guzmán et al. 2022; Aranda-Pineda et al. 2025). We documented a remarkable richness of flowering plants and pollinators, and pronounced changes in network composition, structure, and robustness across seasons and years. Our results further show that network variability is strongly modulated by temporal precipitation dynamics, primarily through phenology-mediated changes in species diversity and composition. The effects of rainfall regimes on pollination networks have been documented in other highly seasonal, tropical and subtropical ecosystems (Rabeling et al. 2019; Flórez-Gómez et al. 2020; Cortés-Flores et al. 2023), but only in few arid ones (Lance et al. 2017; Argueta-Guzmán et al. 2022).

Network composition was highly variable between years, yet each annual cycle was so dynamic that most dissimilarity indices were higher at the intra-annual scale. Species turnover was the main driver of interaction variability when analyzing seasonal networks across temporal scales, showing that node composition can change considerably among years, and especially among seasons, under contrasting precipitation levels (as in Lance et al. 2017; Rabeling et al. 2019; Flórez-Gómez et al. 2020; Cortés-Flores et al. 2023; Tavera et al. 2023). In close localities of the Queretano-Hidalguense Arid Zone, Argueta-Guzmán and colleagues (2022) likewise found little overlap of flowering plants and bees between different seasons of the year. Interaction rewiring among shared species was more important for inter-annual variation when comparing whole-year instead of seasonal networks, and more pronounced between seasons in the wet year than in the dry year. This is probably related to inter-annual changes in the phenology of multiple plants and pollinators (like those reported by Wright et al. 2015; Rowe et al. 2024), some of which had extended flowering and foraging periods in the wet year (including structurally keystone species like Perymenium mendezii, Bombus sonorous, and Anthophora californica).

Turnover of the plant species in bloom was particularly important for network variability across temporal scales (as in Rabeling et al. 2019). In arid zones, flowering phenologies vary in onset, intensity, and duration tracking climatic signals over time and depending on the species’ life cycles and growth habits (Bustamante and Búrquez 2008; Crimmins et al. 2011). In both years, many plants blooming during the dry spring were succulent perennials (mainly Cactaceae), whose flowering phenologies are decoupled from precipitation thanks to their water storage capacity (Pavón and Briones 2001; Bustamante and Búrquez 2008). A similar pattern occurs in some woody species capable of accessing deep water reserves (e.g., Neltuma laevigata, Pavón and Briones 2001; Rabeling et al. 2019). In contrast, the flowering phenologies of herbs and several shrubs were coupled to rainfall (Hassler et al. 2010; Minckley et al. 2013; Flórez-Gómez et al. 2020). Some shrub species were able to bloom rapidly following small rain pulses, though precipitation levels appeared to influence their flower abundance (Minckley et al. 2013; Rabeling et al. 2019). Most herbaceous species bloomed only during the foresummer–rainy season or the subsequent autumn, and annual herbs were nearly absent from the networks of the dry year. Each year, annuals require a minimum amount of water in shallow soil layers to achieve germination, establishment, and hence flowering; the same applies to growth reactivation and blooming of many perennial herbs (Noy-Meir 1973; Crimmins et al. 2011; Wright et al. 2015). Accordingly, herbs and shrubs were found to play key structural roles only in the summer and autumn networks of the wet year, predominantly species with generalized floral morphologies.

The contribution of pollinator turnover to network variation was greatest when comparing the foresummer–rainy and autumnal periods between years. This is largely attributable to the remarkable increase in the diversity and frequency of dipterans and lepidopterans during those periods of the wet year compared to the dry year. In other arid regions of North America, rainfall has been reported to regulate the richness and abundance of butterflies and syrphids due to their habitat requirements for larval development (e.g., host plants green-up for caterpillars, humid microhabitats for fly larvae; Martínez-Falcón et al. 2011; Rowe et al. 2024). Indeed, lepidopteran and dipteran pollinators played key structural roles mainly during those wet periods of our wet year. Bee richness remained relatively stable between years though we observed inter-annual changes in the frequency and flight periods of some taxa, probably modulated by rainfall variation and functional traits (e.g., nesting site, sociability, and diet breadth; Minckley et al. 2013; Flórez-Gómez et al. 2020; Ballarin et al. 2026). In both years, bee frequency declined in autumn despite marked inter-annual precipitation differences, which could be related to seasonal temperature declines (Kazenel et al. 2020; Munguia-Soto et al. 2023). Four of the five nodes that played key structural roles in both years were common generalist bees, the exotic Apis mellifera being the only species to do so in all seasons, likely due to its hyper-generalist foraging habits and the abundance of workers in its eusocial colonies (Elle et al. 2012; Souza et al. 2017; Cappellari et al. 2019). However, pollinators’ roles were based solely on their visitation frequency, and their pollination efficiency for different floral hosts has yet to be evaluated (Lance et al. 2017; Argueta-Guzmán et al. 2022).

In terms of structure, all reconstructed networks showed a consistent pattern of low connectance, significant levels of modularity and specialization, and a compound topology where nestedness occurs within modules rather than at the whole-network scale. Such pattern is likely shaped by the major diversity of plants and pollinators in the community, both taxonomic and functional (e.g., diversity of floral morphologies and pollination syndromes; Danieli-Silva et al. 2012; Vizentin-Bugoni et al. 2018; Ballarin et al. 2026). The paradigm of overall nestedness in plant–pollinator networks has been strongly supported by studies of less diverse temperate communities (Vizentin-Bugoni et al. 2018) and partial networks in the tropics (e.g., Flórez-Gómez et al. 2020; Argueta-Guzmán et al. 2022). Yet, increasing modularity is structurally incompatible with whole-network nestedness (Jordano et al. 2009), compound topologies are thought to be prevalent in many ecological networks (Pinheiro et al. 2019), and they have been proven to provide plant–pollinator communities with a convenient balance of stability and feasibility (Lampo et al. 2024).

As previously reported, structural variation was minimal between cumulative annual networks but more pronounced at a finer, more biologically relevant scale (Burkle and Alarcón 2011; CaraDonna et al. 2021). Connectance was the only metric directly correlated with seasonal precipitation, as rainier periods had higher species richness, and connectance tends to decrease exponentially with network size (Dormann et al. 2009). This is primarily due to morphological mismatches among certain flowers and pollinators, which constrain the number of realized legitimate interactions (Jordano et al. 2009). Network specialization and modularity increased markedly throughout the dry year, while they either declined or remained more stable over the periods of the wet year. The reduced diversity and abundance of potential partners in the foresummer–rainy and autumnal seasons of the dry year, especially floral resources, might have led to greater competition and changes in animal foraging that resulted in higher degrees of specialization (as reported by Souza et al. 2018; Rabeling et al. 2019; Tavera et al. 2023). This may have also induced finer niche partitioning and more restricted modules (Lance et al. 2017; Vizentin-Bugoni et al. 2018), which is supported by the lower number of module connectors and network hubs during the summer and autumn seasons of the dry year compared to the wet year.

Robustness was consistently higher in the wet year than in the dry year for both the annual and seasonal networks, especially those of the foresummer–rainy and autumnal seasons (similar to Lance et al. 2017; Rabeling et al. 2019; Argueta-Guzmán et al. 2022). Negative effects of drought on pollination networks are expected to be more acute when communities are dominated by species whose presence, abundance or blooming intensity depend on water availability (like most herbs and several shrubs, and many Diptera and Lepidoptera in our study site; Rabeling et al. 2019). Robustness decreased throughout the dry year as specialization and modularity increased, unlike in the wet year; the drought-induced impoverishment of interaction partners and stronger mutual reliance within the community can potentially hinder its ability to recover from disturbance (Dormann et al. 2009; Elle et al. 2012). Conversely, increased size and relative connectance can confer higher network resilience due to greater redundancy among species (Elle et al. 2012). For instance, the autumn network of the wet year had the same value of connectance as that of the dry year despite its remarkably larger size, and it showed the highest robustness values of all seasonal networks. These findings are particularly important given the future predictions of intensified drought conditions in the arid regions of southern North America (Zhao et al. 2023), which could lead to a greater vulnerability of plant–pollinator communities and interactions.

Overall, our findings highlight the remarkable diversity and plasticity of pollination systems in semi-arid environments and help bridge significant gaps in the ecological knowledge of the Queretano-Hidalguense Arid Zone, an understudied region increasingly threatened by anthropogenic pressure (Argueta-Guzmán et al. 2022; Aranda-Pineda et al. 2025). Despite the limitations of our regression analyses, our findings show that precipitation is a key driver modulating the temporal dynamics of these interaction networks, which are primarily driven by floral turnover, with seasonal variation often being more pronounced than inter-annual differences. Community robustness decreases under drought conditions, associated with higher network specialization and lower species diversity and connectivity, underscoring the vulnerability of pollination mutualisms in the face of climate change. We identify key plant and pollinator taxa that play critical structural roles in contrasting seasons and years, which should be prioritized in local conservation efforts. Although ecological network research is often limited by sampling completeness (Dormann et al. 2009; Vizentin-Bugoni et al. 2018), our major temporal patterns are unlikely to result from sampling effects (CaraDonna et al. 2021), and our sampling completeness estimates align with those previously reported for pollination networks (e.g., CaraDonna et al. 2017; Souza et al. 2018; Argueta-Guzmán et al. 2022). This study emphasizes the importance of considering temporal variability in network research, especially in seasonal tropical and subtropical ecosystems, where plant–pollinator communities are particularly diverse and active year-round. We further encourage future studies to assess pollination effectiveness for a more comprehensive understanding of network structure, functioning, and dynamics.
