Robust, partially alive particle Metropolis-Hastings via The Frankenfilter
Chris Sherlock, Andrew Golightly, Anthony Lee

Abstract
When a hidden Markov model permits the conditional likelihood of an observation given the hidden process to be zero, all particle simulations from one observation time to the next could produce zeros. If so, the filtering distribution cannot be estimated and the estimated parameter likelihood is zero. The alive particle filter addresses this by simulating a random number of particles for each inter-observation interval, stopping after a target number of non-zero conditional likelihoods. For outlying observations or poor parameter values, a non-zero result can be extremely unlikely, and computational costs prohibitive. We introduce the Frankenfilter, a principled, partially alive particle filter that targets a user-defined amount of success whilst fixing lower and upper bounds on the number of simulations. The Frankenfilter produces unbiased estimators of the likelihood, suitable for pseudo-marginal Metropolis–Hastings (PMMH). We demonstrate that PMMH with the Frankenfilter is more robust to outliers and mis-specified initial parameter values than PMMH using standard particle filters, and is typically at least 2-3 times more efficient. We also provide advice for choosing the amount of success. In the case of n n exact observations, this is particularly simple: target n n successes.

Introduction

Consider a hidden Markov model for a hidden process,
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
,
X_{t}\in\mathcal{X}

(t\in[0,T])
, parameterised by
\theta
and observed at times
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
with observations
y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
,
i=1,\dots,n
. The likelihood for the data is

P(y_{1:n}|\theta)=\int f(y_{1:n}|x_{[0,T]},\theta)~\mathsf{d}\widetilde{P}(x_{[0,T]}|\theta),

where
\widetilde{P}
is the law of
x_{[0,T]}
,
and the conditional likelihood is

f(y_{1:n}|x_{[0,T]},\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(y_{i}|x_{t_{i}},\theta).

Suppose that, conditional on
X_{t_{i}}
, the likelihood,
f(y_{i}|X_{t_{i}}=x,\theta)=0
if some criterion on the closeness of
X_{t_{i}}
to
y_{i}
is not satisfied. A special case of particular note is when each conditional likelihood is an indicator function (typically on the closeness of
x
to
y_{t}
):
f(y_{t}|X_{t}=x):\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
. Our interest in this phenomenon arises from exact or partial but noiseless observations of a Markov jump process, which has a discrete statespace,
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
; alternative scenarios include, for any Markov process, approximate Bayesian computation with a kernel such as
1(\|y_{i}-x_{t_{i}}\|\leq\epsilon)

(
Sisson et al., 2018
, e.g.,)
.

The usual collection of particle filters, including the bootstrap filter
Gordon et al., 1993
and the auxiliary particle filter
Pitt and Shephard, 1999
fix the number of particles (simulations) from one observation time,
t_{i-1}
to the next,
t_{i}
. Thus, in the scenario where the conditional likelihood for a simulation can be
0
, it is possible that the conditional likelihood of each of the particles is also
0
; in this case, none of the particles at time
t_{i}
can be resampled, and the particle filter ‘dies out’. Consequently, it is not possible to estimate the filtering distribution and, within a particle MCMC or SMC
2
scheme, the estimate of the likelihood is
0
.

When a traditional particle filter is used within particle Metropolis–Hastings, the ideal number of particles can vary from one parameter value to another; for example, a parameter value that fits less well with the data than the posterior mode, will lead to simulations from the prior of the process that fit less well with the data, and this can lead to more Monte Carlo variability in the estimate of the log likelihood for that particular simulation. Since in real scenarios (nearly) all models are wrong, even for the posterior modal parameter value, a few outlying process values or outlying observations could suggest an ideal number of particles for the related inter-observation intervals that is much larger than is required for the rest of the intervals. All of these points have motivated the development of the alive particle filter.

The alive particle filter and compromises in practice

LeGland and Oudjane, 2004
;
LeGland and Oudjane, 2005
propose and analyse a particle filter which, for each inter-observation interval, simulates repeatedly until a prespecified amount of ‘success’,
\mathfrak{s}

\mathfrak{s}

1
s) and the estimate of the likelihood for
n

\widehat{P}_{LO}:=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\mathfrak{s}}{M_{t}}.

where

Whilst

Subject to strong conditions,

Each

Motivated by a desire for the robustness and adaptivity of the alive particle filter and the practical necessity of bounding the amount of computational work, all whilst producing an unbiased estimator of the likelihood, we present the Frankenfilter: a particle filter that is almost fully alive, but not quite.

1.2
Our contribution and the structure of this article

The Frankenfilter corrects the alive filter with hard threshold so that the likelihood estimator is unbiased, and generalises the applicability in several directions. In particular, the Frankenfilter allows:

•

specification of a minimum number of simulations,
m_{-}
, as well as a maximum,
m_{+}
;

•

the conditional likelihood to be any non-negative weight rather than an indicator; in particular, this permits the use of informed transition proposals, so called ‘bridges’
Golightly and Wilkinson, 2015
;
Golightly and Sherlock, 2019
;

•

any non-negative measure of success, rather than an indicator; this permits the amount of success to depend on the likelihood, for example.

We also derive tuning advice for the alive particle filter and the Frankenfilter. Simulation studies demonstrate the bias inherent in Algorithm
1
, validate the tuning advice and compare PMMH using the Frankenfilter with an equivalent static particle filter, exploring the relative robustness and efficiency.

The Frankenfilter can be applied to any discretely observed Markov process observed at a discrete set of times, where observations
y_{t}
have a known conditional likelihood,
f(y_{t}|X_{t}=x_{t})
, given the state of the hidden process,
X_{t}=x_{t}
,
t=1,\dots,T
. We will repeatedly refer to two cases of particular interest, where the conditional likelihood is an indicator function:

•

Complete, exact observations
: At each observation time, the complete state vector is observed;
i.e.
, we observe
X_{t}=x_{t}
,
t=1,\dots,T
.

•

Partial, exact observations
: At each observation time, a subset of the components of the state vector are observed. Let
X_{t}=(X^{*}_{t},Z_{t})
; we observe
X^{*}_{t}=x^{*}_{t}
,
t=1,\dots,T
.

Section
2
presents the Frankenfilter in several stages of increasing generality and proves the unbiasedness of the likelihood estimator. Section
3
derives advice on tuning the alive particle filter and the Frankenfilter and Section
4
presents the simulation studies. The article concludes in Section
5
with a discussion.

2
The Frankenfilter

To maximise the accessibility of the key ideas, we start with algorithms suitable for complete, exact observations. In this case, the likelihood for the data is a product of individual transition probabilities, and independent estimates of each transition probability may be obtained.

We first present the simplest Frankenfilter, Algorithm
2
, which estimates
p=\mathbb{P}\left({X=x}\right)
for some specific
x
by sampling repeatedly from the distribution of
X
and counting the number of successes, where a success is defined by
\{X=x\}
. The algorithm stops when either the number of successes reaches a threshold,
\mathfrak{s}
, or a maximum number of iterations,
m_{+}
, has been reached. In the case of complete, exact observations of a Markov jump process and a known initial condition, Algorithm
2
would be applied seperately for the initial interval and then each inter-observation interval.

Algorithm
3
generalises Algorithm
2
, by allowing for a minimum number of simulations, for importance-based proposals and for an arbitrary (non-binary) measure of success; it also separates the measure of success from the quantity that we are aiming to estimate. It is still only applicable in the case of complete, exact observations. The proof that the estimator produced by Algorithm
3
is unbiased is included in the main text as the intuition is helpful for understanding the structure of the estimator.

Finally, Algorithm
5
generalises Algorithm
3
to incomplete and/or inexact observations. The proof of unbiasedness is longer and is included in the appendix.

2.1.2
General weights and measure of success

We generalise the previous algorithm to allow for a specified minimum number of simulations, for importance proposals and for the measure of success to be different from the estimate of the quantity of interest.

Pathak, 1976
describes an unbiased estimator when samples of a real-valued variate are taken sequentially and each sample has an associated cost, which can only be ascertained after the sample has been taken. Sampling stops when a specific cost budget is reached.
Kremers, 1987
considers Bernoulli variables and implicity assumes a fixed, constant cost for samples, but allows upper and lower bounds on the number of samples taken. Combining these ideas, we consider real-valued variables of interest,
w^{j}
, and specify upper and lower bounds,
m_{+}
and
m_{-}
, on the number of samples. We relabel “cost” as “amount of success”,
s^{j}
, where each simulation brings a certain amount of success (some increasing function of the conditional likelihood of the simulation, for example) and the simulation stops if a success threshold
\mathfrak{s}
is reached or

simulations have been performed. We summarise the relevant notation:

•

:

, or estimate of the quantity of interest, from the

•

:

for the

th simulation. This could be

•

3
, one might reasonably consider specifying different budgets for each inter-observation interval,
\mathfrak{s}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{s}_{T}
, with
\mathfrak{s}_{t}
conceivably depending on a prior estimate of
p_{t}
, for example. This would still lead to an unbiased estimator of the likelihood. In Section
3
, we show that with complete exact observations it is reasonable to set
\mathfrak{s}_{1}=\mathfrak{s}_{2}=\dots=\mathfrak{s}_{T}=\mathfrak{s}
. Simulations in Section
4
demonstrate that this is still a viable strategy, even with partial observations.

3
Tuning

Until recently, advice for tuning algorithms based on particle Metropolis–Hastings has been to choose the computational cost so that at some particular, representative parameter value,
\widehat{\theta}
,
\mathsf{Var}\left[{\log\widehat{P}(Y_{1:T})}\right]\approx 1

Pitt et al., 2012
;
Sherlock et al., 2015
;
Doucet et al., 2015
.

Sherlock, 2024
points out that in the case where
\mathbb{P}\left({\widehat{P}=0}\right)>0
,
\mathsf{Var}\left[{\log\widehat{P}(y_{1:T})}\right]
is not even defined. This particular case occurs with exact or partial observations of an MJP where the likelihood is zero when the state does not fit with the observation. More generally,
Sherlock, 2024
shows that tuning so that the
relative variance
,

V_{\mathrm{rel}}:=\frac{\mathsf{Var}\left[{\widehat{P}(y_{1:T})}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\widehat{P}(y_{1:T})}\right]^{2}}=\frac{1}{P(y_{1:T}^{2})}\mathsf{Var}\left[{\widehat{P}(y_{1:T})}\right]\approx 1,

(1)

or a little above
1
,
is a more robust approach as it can highlight potential large or infinite polynomial moments of
\widehat{P}
. In the case where
\widehat{P}
is well behaved and strictly positive, the delta method (first-order Taylor approximation) shows that the two approaches are essentially equivalent.

We will typically set
m_{-}=0
, and
m_{+}
sufficiently large that its existence only rarely comes into play. Thus, some of our analysis is for the Alive particle filter, anticipating any resulting guidance will also apply to the Frankenfilter. We then examine the effect of
m_{+}
on the overall variance and derive guidance on setting
m_{+}
so that its impact on the variance is small.

We will show that in the case of exact observations, where the algorithm in Section
2.1
is applicable, to target a relative variance of
V
, one should set
\mathfrak{s}=\lceil 2+T/\log(1+V)\rceil
. In the case of partial observations, where our general algorithm of Section
2.2
applies, but with
s^{j}=w^{j}\in\{0,1\}
for all
j
,
\mathfrak{s}
should be larger and we provide a natural formula for the relative variance, that demonstrates this.

All of the advice cited above assumes that
\mathsf{Var}\left[{\widehat{P}}\right]
or
\mathsf{Var}\left[{\log\widehat{P}}\right]
is inversely proportional to the computational cost. In the case of a standard particle filter, the cost is proportional to the number of particles. For the Alive particle filter the expected cost is proportional to the expected number of simulations, which is proportional to
\mathfrak{s}
. In the case of the alive particle filter with exact observations, a first-order Taylor expansion of (
2
) (appearing in Section
3.1
), shows that, at least approximately, the variance is proportional to
1/\mathfrak{s}
, as required.

3.1
Tuning for complete, exact observations with binary weights

Initially, we allow for different success thresholds for each observation,
\mathfrak{s}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{s}_{T}
. In the case of exact observations, the estimator of the transition probability from time
t-1
to time
t
is

\widehat{P}_{t}=\frac{\mathfrak{s}_{t}-1}{M_{t}-1},

where
M_{t}
is the number of attempts needed to obtain
\mathfrak{s}_{t}
successes.

In the case of complete, exact observations,

V_{\mathrm{rel}}=\frac{1}{\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{t=1}^{T}\widehat{P}_{t}^{2}}\right]-1=\left\{\prod_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{p_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\widehat{P}_{t}^{2}}\right]\right\}-1.

The following result, proved in Appendix
B
, enables us to progress the analysis of
V_{\mathrm{rel}}
.

Proposition 2

Let
P
be the random variable, with
\mathbb{E}\left[{P}\right]=p
, returned by Algorithm
2
with
m_{+}=\infty
, and define
E_{rel}:=\mathbb{E}\left[{P^{2}}\right]/p^{2}
.
For
\mathfrak{s}=2
,

E_{rel}=-\frac{\log p}{1-p},

for
\mathfrak{s}=3
,

1+\frac{1-p}{3}\leq E_{rel}=\frac{2}{1-p}+\frac{2p\log p}{(1-p)^{2}}\leq 2-p,

and for
\mathfrak{s}\geq 4
,

1+\frac{1-(1+\frac{2}{\mathfrak{s}-3})p}{\mathfrak{s}-2}<\frac{1}{p^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[{P^{2}}\right]<1+\frac{1-p}{\mathfrak{s}-2}.

First, it is clear that when
\mathfrak{s}=2
, we cannot control the relative variance as
p\downarrow 0
. For
\mathfrak{s}\geq 3
, however, the relative variance is bounded, and for large
\mathfrak{s}
, the relative variance behaves as
(1-p)/(\mathfrak{s}-2)
.

Using Proposition
2
, then that each
\mathfrak{s}_{t}
is typically large, and finally that in the cases where one might consider using the alive particle filter,
p_{t}
is typically small,

\left\{\prod_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{p_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\widehat{P}_{t}^{2}}\right]\right\}\approx\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left\{1+\frac{1-p_{t}}{\mathfrak{s}_{t}-2}\right\}\approx\exp\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1-p_{t}}{\mathfrak{s}_{t}-2}\right)\approx\exp\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{\mathfrak{s}_{t}-2}\right).

Fixing this, the total number of successes required,
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathfrak{s}_{t}
, is minimised by setting
\mathfrak{s}_{1}=\dots=\mathfrak{s}_{T}=\mathfrak{s}
. Then,

V_{\mathrm{rel}}\approx\exp\left(\frac{T}{\mathfrak{s}-2}\right)-1.

(2)

Rearranging, for a target relative variance of
V_{\mathrm{rel}}
, we should set

\mathfrak{s}\approx 2+\frac{T}{\log(1+V_{\mathrm{rel}})}.

This is, typically, non-integer. Taking
\mathfrak{s}
slightly too high simply increases the computational cost slightly; taking
\mathfrak{s}
too low can negatively impact the mixing of the algorithm, potentially substantially. Hence, we recommend taking the ceiling of the expression. Note that, requiring
V_{\mathrm{rel}}=e-1
suggests setting
\mathfrak{s}=2+T\approx T
.

It is worth emphasising that this tuning advice does
not
require a choice of a representative parameter value. The guidance is valid whatever the system and whatever the true (small) transition probabilities are.

If, on the other hand, we
did
have a representative parameter value and we
could
somehow estimate each
p_{t}
for this parameter, then we could choose to try to minimise the expected number of simulations,
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathfrak{s}_{t}/p_{t}
, subject to obtaining a target
V_{\mathrm{rel}}
;
i.e.
, subject to
\sum_{t=1}^{T}(1-p_{t})/(\mathfrak{s}_{t}-2)=\log(1+V_{\mathrm{rel}})
. The method of Langrange multipliers gives a solution of
\mathfrak{s}_{t}-2\propto\sqrt{p_{t}(1-p_{t})}
. Of course, we could also use a standard particle filter, aiming to minimise the total number of simulations
\sum_{t=1}^{T}n_{t}
, subject to a fixed relative variance
\approx\exp\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(1-p_{t})/(n_{t}p_{t})\right\}-1
, which suggests setting
n_{t}\propto\sqrt{(1-p_{t})/p_{t}}
. These observation-specific sets of tunings are exactly the kind of user-unfriendly course of action that the Frankenfilter is designed to obviate, so we do not pursue this possibility any further.

3.2
Tuning for partial, exact observations

We consider the alive particle filter in the case of partial, exact observations and multinomial resampling, and anticipate similar behaviour for the Frankenfilter provided
m_{+}
is large.

Let the full state at time
t
be
X_{t}=(X^{*}_{t},Z_{t})
, where
X^{*}_{t}
is observed precisely and
Z_{t}
is unobserved.
At time
t-1
there are
\mathfrak{s}-1
particles (with values
z_{t-1}^{1},\dots z_{t-1}^{\mathfrak{s}-1}
). We sample
Z_{t-1}
uniformly at random from these and propagate
X_{t-1}=(x^{*}_{t-1},Z_{t-1})
forwards until time
t
, registering the simulation as a success if
X^{*}_{t}=x^{*}_{t}
, and a failure otherwise. This is repeated until we achieve
\mathfrak{s}
successes at time
t
. The first
t-1
successes provide
\underline{z}_{t}:=(z_{t}^{1},\dots,z_{t}^{\mathfrak{s}-1})
.

Define
p_{t}(z)=\mathbb{P}\left({X_{t}=x_{t}|X_{t-1}=(x^{*}_{t-1},z)}\right)
and for
j=1,2,\dots,\mathfrak{s}-1
, let
\overline{p}_{t}=\frac{1}{\mathfrak{s}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathfrak{s}-1}p_{t}(z_{t-1}^{j})
; for the random variable, we write
\overline{P}_{t}=\frac{1}{\mathfrak{s}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathfrak{s}-1}p_{t}(Z_{t-1}^{j})
.

For
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
be the results of all simulations up to and including time
t
(the filtration at time
t
). All the relevant information from
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
that will inform the simulated values and estimated success probability at time
t
, as well as the evolution beyond time
t
is contained in
\underline{Z}_{t}
.

There are two special aspects of our scenario that facilitate helpful analytical insight.

S1
: Because we are choosing the ancestor of the
j
th simulation uniformly at random, when simulating
\widehat{P}_{t}
, the distribution of the number of trials until
\mathfrak{s}
successes is indistinguishable from the distribution of the number of trials if each were a simple Bernoulli trial with success probability
\overline{P}_{t}
.

S2
: Conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{t-2}
,
\widehat{P}_{t-1}
and
(\overline{P}_{t},\dots,\overline{P}_{T})
are independent. This is because the forward simulations from time
t-2
to time
t-1
are independent of each other. In particular, the number of failures,
F_{t-1}:=|\{i:{X^{*i}_{t-1}}\neq x^{*}_{t-1}\}|
is independent of the state values at the successes, and, therefore, of
\underline{z}_{t-1}
. Now,
\widehat{P}_{t-1}=(\mathfrak{s}-1)/(\mathfrak{s}+F_{t-1}-1)
, whereas
\overline{P}_{t-1}
is a deterministic function of
\underline{Z}_{t-1}
and the subsequent evolution only depends on
\underline{Z}_{t-1}
, not
F_{t-1}
.

As before,
V_{\mathrm{rel}}
is as defined in (
1
);
now, however, we only know that
p=\mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{t=1}^{T}\widehat{P}_{t}}\right]
.
Using S1 and Proposition
2
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[{\widehat{P}_{t}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\widehat{P}_{t}^{2}|\underline{Z}_{t-1}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\widehat{P}_{t}^{2}|\overline{P}_{t}}\right]\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{\mathfrak{s}-2}\right)\overline{P}_{t}^{2},~~~t=1,\dots,T,

(3)

and, again from Proposition
2
, when
\mathfrak{s}
is large and each
p_{t}
is small, we expect this bound to be close to an equality. Theorem
2
, below, is proved in Appendix
C
.

Theorem 2

In the case of the alive particle filter on partial, exact observations with the same target number of successes,
\mathfrak{s}
, for each observation,

V_{\mathrm{rel}}\leq\exp\left\{\frac{T}{\mathfrak{s}-2}\right\}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{t=1}^{T}\overline{P}_{t}^{2}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{t=1}^{T}\overline{P}_{t}}\right]^{2}}-1.

(4)

When
\mathfrak{s}
is large, we expect (
4
) to be close to an equality. Rewriting this gives,

V_{\mathrm{rel}}\approx\exp\left\{\frac{T}{\mathfrak{s}-2}\right\}-1+\exp\left\{\frac{T}{\mathfrak{s}-2}\right\}\frac{\mathsf{Var}\left[{\prod_{t=1}^{T}\overline{P}_{t}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{t=1}^{T}\overline{P}_{t}}\right]^{2}}.

We see that for a given
\mathfrak{s}
, the relative variance is larger than in the case of exact observations. So, to achieve a given target relative variance,
\mathfrak{s}
must increase. Since each
\overline{P}_{t}
is an average of
\mathfrak{s}-1
(albeit correlated) probabilities, when starting from
\mathfrak{s}\approx T
, we anticipate
\mathsf{Var}\left[{\prod_{t=1}^{T}\overline{P}_{t}}\right]
decreasing roughly in proportion to
1/\sqrt{\mathfrak{s}}
as
\mathfrak{s}
increases.

In the case of very large
\mathfrak{s}
, each
\underline{z}_{t}
,
t=1,\dots,T
is, essentially,
\mathfrak{s}-1
independent draws from the corresponding filtering distribution, which we denote by
g_{t}(z_{t}|x^{*}_{1:t})
. For moderately large
\mathfrak{s}-1
, we expect this to hold approximately, so that

\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{t=1}^{T}\overline{P}_{t}^{2}}\right]&\approx\prod_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[{\left(\frac{1}{\mathfrak{s}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathfrak{s}-1}p_{t}(Z_{t}^{j})\right)^{2}}\right]=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{p_{t}(Z_{t})}\right]^{2}+\frac{1}{\mathfrak{s}-1}\mathsf{Var}\left[{p_{t}(Z_{t})}\right]\right)\\
&=\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[{p_{t}(Z_{t})}\right]\right)^{2}\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left\{1+\frac{1}{\mathfrak{s}-1}\frac{\mathsf{Var}\left[{p_{t}(Z_{t})}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[{p_{t}(Z_{t})}\right]^{2}}\right\}\\
&\leq\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[{p_{t}(Z_{t})}\right]\right)^{2}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\mathfrak{s}-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\mathsf{Var}\left[{p_{t}(Z_{t})}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[{p_{t}(Z_{t})}\right]^{2}}\right\}\end{aligned},

where, for each
t=1,\dots,T
,
Z_{t},Z_{t}^{1},\dots,Z_{t}^{\mathfrak{s}-1}
are independent draws from
g_{t}(\cdot|x^{*}_{1:t})
.
Noting
\mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{t=1}^{T}\overline{P}_{t}}\right]\approx\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[{p_{t}(Z_{t})}\right]\right)
leads to an approximate bound on
V_{\mathrm{rel}}
of

\exp\left\{\frac{T}{\mathfrak{s}-2}+\frac{1}{\mathfrak{s}-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\mathsf{Var}\left[{p_{t}(Z_{t})}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[{p_{t}(Z_{t})}\right]^{2}}\right\}-1.

When compared with (
2
), the additional term might be expected to dominate if the sum of the relative variances of the
p_{t}(Z_{t})
is larger than
T
.

3.3
Choice of
m_{+}

We now show that choosing
m_{+}
such that
m_{+}p=\kappa\mathfrak{s}
for some moderately sized
\kappa
, such as
\kappa=10
, is sufficient to control the additional extra variance that comes from the introduction of
m_{+}
(and
m_{-}
) into the alive particle filter. We restrict attention to the most obvious application of Algorithm
3
, where
s^{j}=w^{j}
,
j=1,2,\dots
. Propositions
3
and
4
are proved in Appendix
B
.

Proposition 3

Let
P
be the random variable, with
\mathbb{E}\left[{P}\right]=p
, returned by Algorithm
3
in the special case that
S^{j}\equiv W^{j}
,
j=1,2,\dots
. If
0\leq W^{j}\leq w_{*}
,
j=1,2,\dots
and
\mathfrak{s}>2w_{*}
,

\frac{1}{p^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[{P^{2}}\right]\leq 1+\frac{w_{*}}{\mathfrak{s}-2w_{*}}+\frac{\mathfrak{s}}{m_{+}p}\mathbb{P}\left({\sum_{j=1}^{m_{+}}W^{j}<\mathfrak{s}}\right).

The term
1+w_{*}/(\mathfrak{s}-2w_{*})
is a slight loosening of the upper bound in Proposition
2
, while the final term relates to the probability that the bound
m_{+}
will make a difference to the output.
The result gives an upper bound on the relative variance, generalising the approximation in (
2
):

V_{\mathrm{rel}}\leq\exp\left(\frac{Tw_{*}}{\mathfrak{s}-2w_{*}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\mathfrak{s}}{m_{+}p_{t}}\mathbb{P}\left({\sum_{j=1}^{m_{+}}W^{j}_{t}<\mathfrak{s}}\right)\right)-1.

(5)

The additional variance comes from the second term in the sum.
Applying Bernstein’s inequality to the probability in Proposition
3
leads to the following bound.

Proposition 4

If
m_{+}p=\kappa\mathfrak{s}
for some
\kappa\geq 7/4
then

\mathbb{P}\left({\sum_{j=1}^{m_{+}}W^{j}<\mathfrak{s}}\right)<\exp\left\{-\frac{3\left(\kappa-\frac{7}{4}\right)\mathfrak{s}}{8w_{*}}\right\}.

Combining with Proposition
3
, we obtain

\frac{1}{p^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[{P^{2}}\right]<1+\frac{w_{*}}{\mathfrak{s}-2w_{*}}+\frac{1}{\kappa}\exp\left\{-\frac{3(\kappa-\frac{7}{4})\mathfrak{s}}{8w_{*}}\right\}.

In the typical regime where
\mathfrak{s}/w_{*}>>1
, the bound on the probability that
m_{+}
actually plays a role in the form of
P
, is controlled with relatively small values of
\kappa
, so this is sufficient to control the whole final term.

Of course, in reality, we do not know each
p_{t}
; however, only a very approximate lower bound on each
p_{t}
is needed to set a reasonable
m_{+}
. In Section
4
, to verify our tuning advice, we use a preliminary run of the alive particle filter at the true parameter value. Alternatively, a rough estimate could be supplied by the Linear Noise Approximation
(
Golightly and Sherlock, 2019
, e.g.)
or simpler, single-step Gaussian or Poisson-based approximations.

4
Simulations

In this section we empirically validate the tuning advice of Section
3
and evaluate the performance of the Frankenfilter inside pseudo-marginal Metropolis-Hastings (PMMH) by considering four applications based upon Markov jump processes and an approximation thereof.

To fix notation, consider a
d_{x}
-dimensional latent Markov process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with state-space
\mathcal{X}

\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and index set
\mathcal{T}
(which may be discrete or continuous) and a discrete-time,
d_{y}
-dimensional observation process
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with state-space
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d_{y}}
. Suppose that
observations
y=(y_{t_{1}}^{\prime},\ldots,y_{t_{T}}^{\prime})^{\prime}
are available, where
y_{t_{i}}=F^{\top}x_{t_{i}}
for some constant
d_{x}\times d_{y}
matrix
F
. We consider two specific Markov process models as follows. In the applications in Sections
4.1
and
4.4
, the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is modelled as a Markov jump process (MJP). Given the state of the system at time
t
as
x_{t}
, the process transitions to time
t+\tau
via

X_{t+\tau}=x_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}S^{i}\mathcal{P}_{i}\left(\int_{t}^{t+\tau}h_{i}(x_{t^{\prime}},\theta)dt^{\prime}\right),

(6)

where
\mathcal{P}_{i}(t)
,
i=1,\ldots,r
are independent, unit rate Poisson processes,
S^{i}
is the
i
th row of a
d_{x}\times r
scaling matrix
S
whose elements are non-negative integers and the hazard function
h(x_{t}):=(h_{1}(x_{t},\theta),\ldots,h_{r}(x_{t},\theta))^{\top}
is a vector of event rates or hazards parameterised by
\theta
. MJPs are routinely used to model reaction networks consisting of
r
reaction channels; in this case,
X_{t}
represents a vector of specie counts,
\theta
is a vector of reaction rate constants and
S
is known as the stoichiometry matrix, which describes the effect of each reaction on each component of
X_{t}

(
Wilkinson, 2018
, see e.g.)
. Exact simulation of the MJP is straightforward via Gillespie’s direct method
(
Gillespie, 1977
)
. For the applications in Sections
4.2
and
4.3
where simulating the MJP exactly is likely to be computationally preclusive, we consider a tau-leap approximation
(
Gillespie, 2001
)
. In this case, the state
x_{t}
is assumed constant over the time interval
[t,t+\tau)
so that (
6
) becomes

X_{t+\tau}=x_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}S^{i}\mathcal{P}_{i}\left(h_{i}(x_{t},\theta)\tau\right).

(7)

Tau-leaping permits an adaptive time-step
\tau

(
Cao et al., 2007
, see e.g.)
. In what follows, for simplicity, we assume a constant
\tau
.

All simulators were coded in
C++
and implemented in R via the package
Rcpp
. All runs were performed on a desktop computer with an Intel Core i7-10700 processor and a 2.90GHz clock speed. For PMMH, a Gaussian random walk proposal was applied to the natural logarithm of the parameter (vector), with innovation variance
\gamma\mathsf{Var}\left[{\log\theta|y_{1:T}}\right]
, and the scaling
\gamma
chosen to meet a desired acceptance rate
(
Schmon et al., 2021
, see Table 1 of)
. Code to implement the Frankenfilter and competing methods for each application is available from https://github.com/AndyGolightly/Frankenfilter.

Throughout, we abbreviate the bootstrap particle filter to BSPF, the Frankenfilter to FF and the alive particle filter to APF. When tabulating results, for the BSPF,
m_{+}
represents the actual number of particles used and for the APF it represents the hard threshold, after which the algorithm is terminated and returns an estimated likelihood of 0. Effective sample size is abbreviated to ESS and effective samples per second to ESS/s.

corresponding to a single reaction channel of the form

demonstrating this bias at the ground truth
\theta
). A

interval for the posterior expectation of
\theta/\theta_{\text{true}}
using the direct method is
(1.083,1.093)
which does not overlap with the same interval under APF, even for
m^{+}=10^{4}
. Both BSPF and FF on the other hand, give inferences consistent with the direct method. For D50mod, the outlying final two observations have transition probabilities (likelihoods), at the ground truth

, of

and
1.5\times 10^{-4}
, necessitating a much larger value of
m_{+}
for BSPF, and, in order to obtain accurate inferences, for APF. Consequently, FF outperforms BSPF and APF by factors of 10 and 6-30 respectively. The particularly small ESS obtained for APF with

was due to the failure of the algorithm to generate a non-zero estimate of the likelihood for most iterations. Whereas

5
Discussion

We have introduced the Frankenfilter, an almost-fully alive particle filter that adapts the number of simulations to the difficulty of the problem whilst employing a principled mechanism for bounding the total amount of compute.

In Section
3
, we derived advice for choosing the total number of successes,
\mathfrak{s}
, required over each inter-observation interval; in the special case of
T
exact observations, this simply involves setting
\mathfrak{s}\approx T
. This advice was derived for the alive particle filter, but we also showed that choosing
m_{+}=\kappa\mathfrak{s}/p
for a moderately sized
\kappa
controls the impact of
m_{+}
and
m_{-}
on the variance of the estimator. Section
4
validated our the advice on the Frankenfilter.

We also expect our advice to apply to PMMH using the alive filter with a large hard threshold. Values used for
\mathfrak{s}
in the literature back up the suggestion of choosing
\mathfrak{s}\approx T
.

Drovandi et al., 2016
uses the alive particle filter with a hard threshold of
10^{5}
to perform inference via particle Metropolis–Hastings. Preliminary runs allow tuning parameters to be set to approximately optimise efficiency. Where the values of
\mathfrak{s}
are reported, we can see that: in Experiment 1,
\mathfrak{s}=101
and
T\approx 135
(before the observations are uninformative zeros), in Experiment 3,
\mathfrak{s}=201
and
T\approx 240
and for Experiment 4,
\mathfrak{s}=101
and
T=100
. Using the same hard threshold,
Golightly and Sherlock, 2019
examines a data set with
T=7
and finds
\mathfrak{s}=8
to be optimal.

Comparisons in Section
4
demonstrated the bias of the alive particle filter with a hard threshold, even when that threshold is high; highlighting the need for the Frankenfilter. The studies tackled a variety of Markov-jump-process and filtering scenarios and demonstrated the greater robustness and efficiency of the Frankenfilter when compared with an equivalent standard filter with a fixed number of particles; in some cases the improvement in efficiency was of over an order of magnitude.

All of the examples in Section
4
used the number of observation matches as the measure of success, with
\mathfrak{s}
being the target number of matches; however, Algorithms
3
and
5
permit a more general measure of success. In the case of
noisy
observations a natural candidate is the conditional likelihood
s^{j}=f(y_{t}|x_{t}^{j},\theta)
, so a simulation that fits better with the data contributes a larger amount to the total success. The difficulty is in specifying the target total success,
\mathfrak{s}
. Depending on
\theta
,
f
might be broad or narrow;
f
might also be a density, in which case
s^{j}
may be unbounded (over
\theta
, or even for a fixed
\theta
) or a mass function. Whatever the case, it is not obvious in advance of running the simulations what a typical ”average” amount of success should be. For Gaussian observation noise, one possibility, is to combine the Kalman filter with a continuous-statespace Gaussian approximation to the transition density such as that employed in Section
4.3
, to estimate
p(y_{t}|y_{1:t-1},\theta)\approx\frac{1}{\mathfrak{s}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathfrak{s}-1}f(y_{t}|x_{t}^{j},\theta)
, when
x_{t}^{j}
is sampled from
p(\cdot|x_{t-1}^{j})
.

The Frankenfilter Algorithm
5
could also be applied within a correlated pseudo-marginal MCMC scheme
Deligiannidis et al., 2018
. In particular, Algorithms
2
and
3
could potentially be employed within the augmented correlated pseudo-marginal approach of
Golightly and Sherlock, 2022
, potentially reducing the required number of successes considerably.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
1

Our proof is inspired by
Pitt et al., 2012
and
Kudlicka et al., 2020
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
comprise all of the random variables simulated up to and including time
t
. We define
\widehat{P}(y_{1})
to be the random variable whose realisation is
\widehat{p}_{1}
. For
t\geq 2
, we define
\widehat{P}(y_{t-h:t}|y_{1:t-h-1})
to be the random variable whose realisation is
\prod_{j=t-h}^{t}\widehat{p}_{j}
, with
\widehat{P}(y_{t}|y_{1:t-1}):=\widehat{P}(y_{t:t}|y_{1:t-1})
. We also define
I_{t}=1
if
K_{t}=1
and
I_{t}=0
if
K_{t}\in\{0,2\}
.

Analogously to the single-observation case, if
I_{t}=1
then
(W_{t}^{1},S_{t}^{1}),\dots,(W_{t}^{M_{t}-1},S_{t}^{M_{t}-1})
are exchangeable, and if
I_{t}=0
,
(W_{t}^{1},S_{t}^{1}),\dots,(W_{t}^{M_{t}},S_{t}^{M_{t}})
are exchangeable.

Lemma 1

B.1
Proof of Proposition
2

Firstly, let
X=M_{t}-1
. We are interested in
\frac{1}{p^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\{(s-1)/X\}^{2}}\right]=\frac{(s-1)^{2}}{p^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[{1/X^{2}}\right]
.

The random variable
X
is a shifted version of a negative binomial random variable, and so it has a probability mass function of

\mathbb{P}\left({X=x}\right)=\frac{x!}{(\mathfrak{s}-1)!(x+1-s)!}p^{\mathfrak{s}}(1-p)^{x+1-\mathfrak{s}},~~~(x=\mathfrak{s}-1,\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{s}+1,\dots),

(9)

and
\mathbb{P}\left({X=x}\right)=0
otherwise. Thus

\mathbb{E}\left[{\frac{1}{X^{2}}}\right]=\frac{p^{\mathfrak{s}}}{(1-p)^{\mathfrak{s}-1}(\mathfrak{s}-1)!}\sum_{x=\mathfrak{s}-1}^{\infty}\frac{(x-1)!}{x(x+1-\mathfrak{s})!}(1-p)^{x}.

First case
(
\mathfrak{s}=2
): here

\mathbb{E}\left[{\frac{1}{X^{2}}}\right]=\frac{p^{2}}{1-p}\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x}(1-p)^{x}=-\frac{p^{2}}{1-p}\log(p),

by matching the infinite sum to the Taylor expansion of
-\log[1-(1-p)]
. Multipying by
(\mathfrak{s}-1)^{2}/p^{2}
gives the required result.

Second case
(
\mathfrak{s}=3
): here

\mathbb{E}\left[{\frac{1}{X^{2}}}\right]=\frac{p^{3}}{2(1-p)^{2}}A,

where

A=\sum_{x=2}^{\infty}\frac{x-1}{x}(1-p)^{x}=\sum_{x=2}^{\infty}(1-p)^{x}-\sum_{x=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x}(1-p)^{x}=\frac{(1-p)^{2}}{p}-\{-\log p-(1-p)\},

where, for the second sum, we have matched the same Taylor expansion as when
\mathfrak{s}=2
. Multiplying by
(\mathfrak{s}-1)^{2}/p^{2}\times p^{3}/\{2(1-p)^{2}\}=2p/(1-p)^{2}
gives the exact sum.

To obtain the bounds, let

S:=\sum_{x=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x}(1-p)^{x}.

Then, for
0<p\leq 1
,

\frac{(1-p)^{2}}{2}\leq S\leq\frac{(1-p)^{2}}{2}+\frac{(1-p)^{3}}{3}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-p)^{j}\right]=\frac{(1-p)^{2}}{2}+\frac{(1-p)^{3}}{3p}.

Thus

\frac{(1-p)^{2}}{p}-\frac{(1-p)^{2}}{2}-\frac{(1-p)^{3}}{3p}\leq A\leq\frac{(1-p)^{2}}{p}-\frac{(1-p)^{2}}{2}.

Multiplying by
2p/(1-p)^{2}
gives the bounds.

General case
. Now,

\frac{1}{X(X-1)}-\frac{a}{X(X-1)(X-2)}-\frac{1}{X^{2}}=\frac{(1-a)X-2}{X^{2}(X-1)(X-2)}.

Since
X\geq s-1
, setting
a=1
and then
a=1-2/(s-1)
,

\frac{1}{X(X-1)}-\frac{1}{X(X-1)(X-2)}<\frac{1}{X^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{X(X-1)}-\frac{1-\frac{2}{s-1}}{X(X-1)(X-2)}.

(10)

In particular, therefore, as well as
\mathbb{E}\left[{1/X}\right]=p/(s-1)
, which must be true for unbiasedness of the Alive Particle Filter, for
s\geq 3
,

\mathbb{E}\left[{\frac{1}{X(X-1)}}\right]=\frac{p^{2}}{(s-1)(s-2)}=\frac{p^{2}}{(s-1)^{2}}\left(1+\frac{1}{s-2}\right).

Also, for
s\geq 4
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\frac{1}{X(X-1)(X-2)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{3}}{(s-1)(s-2)(s-3)}=\frac{p^{2}}{(s-1)^{2}}\times\frac{p(s-1)}{(s-2)(s-3)}

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{2}}{(s-1)^{2}}\times\frac{p}{s-2}\left(1+\frac{2}{s-3}\right).

Thus, taking expectations in (
10
) gives,

1+\frac{1-p(1+\frac{2}{s-3})}{s-2}<\frac{(s-1)^{2}}{p^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\frac{1}{X^{2}}}\right]<1+\frac{1}{s-2}-\frac{p}{s-2}\left(1-\frac{2}{s-1}\right)\left(1+\frac{2}{s-3}\right),

which simplifies to the required expression.
\square
