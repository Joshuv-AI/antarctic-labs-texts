Inflammation-based phenotyping and prognostic implications in patients with non-obstructive coronary artery disease
Authors: Yutao Liu, Shuyi Hu, Ruobin Wu, Tao Fu, Zhiyong Li, Jingyi Zhang, Ming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-obstructive coronary artery disease (NOCAD) represents an increasing proportion of patients presenting with angina, yet these patients face significant cardiovascular risk. This study aimed to explore distinct classes in NOCAD patients based on demographic characteristics, clinical comorbidities, and inflammatory biomarkers, and to investigate their associations with prognosis.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed patients diagnosed with NOCAD by coronary angiography for angina between January 1, 2023, and December 1, 2024, with follow-up until August 1, 2025. Latent Class Analysis (LCA) was employed to identify distinct classes based on demographic features, inflammatory markers, including 8 composite inflammatory indices. The primary endpoint was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs). Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the associations between different classes and individual inflammatory markers with clinical outcomes after multivariate adjustment. Prognostic discrimination compared with traditional risk factors was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve.ResultsAmong 342 NOCAD patients, LCA identified three distinct classes: low-inflammation with few comorbidities (n = 156, 45.6%), elderly with multiple comorbidities (n = 126, 36.8%), and high-inflammation subgroup (n = 60, 17.5%). Over a median follow-up of 363 days (interquartile range, 193–613 days), 89 patients (26.0%) experienced MACEs. In fully adjusted models, the high-inflammation class was the strongest predictor of MACEs (HR 3.090, 95% CI: 1.614–5.919), followed by the elderly with multiple comorbidities class (HR 2.181, 95% CI: 1.224–3.886). Among 8 composite inflammatory indices evaluated, three demonstrated significant associations with MACEs: the C-reactive protein-albumin-lymphocyte (CALLY) index (per unit increase in Z-score, HR 0.402, 95% CI: 0.230–0.703), neutrophil-to-albumin ratio (NAR) index (per unit increase in Z-score, HR 1.234, 95% CI: 1.002–1.522), and aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI) index (Z-score > 0, HR 1.635, 95% CI: 1.007–2.655). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated significant differences in MACE-free survival across the three classes (log-rank p = 0.001), with CALLY, NAR, and AISI also showing significant prognostic discrimination. Component analysis revealed the high-inflammation phenotype was independently associated with a 2.44-fold increased risk of hospitalization for unstable angina or heart failure (HR = 2.44, 95% CI 1.230–4.846), accounting for 60.67% of total MACEs. These findings were consistently reproduced in a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients without a history of myocardial infarction.ConclusionInflammatory and clinical phenotyping identifies distinct risk subgroups in NOCAD. While the high-inflammation phenotype is associated with the highest overall MACEs risk, this prognostic signal is predominantly driven by hospitalizations for unstable angina or heart failure. These findings suggest that inflammation-based phenotyping could serve as an exploratory complementary tool for risk stratification, requiring further external validation before any routine clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06337-z.Keywords: Non-obstructive coronary artery disease, Inflammatory indices, Latent class analysis, Major adverse cardiovascular events, Prognosis

Introduction

Ischemic heart disease remains the leading cause of adult mortality and years of life lost globally [1]. Invasive coronary angiography effectively diagnoses obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD). However, among patients with angina undergoing elective coronary angiography for ischemic symptoms and/or signs, up to half demonstrate no obstructive epicardial coronary artery disease, termed non-obstructive coronary artery disease (NOCAD), defined as stenosis < 50% in diameter [2, 3]. This condition has historically been considered benign, yet contemporary evidence demonstrates otherwise [4, 5]. In the Women’s Ischemia Syndrome Evaluation (WISE) cohort, nearly two-thirds of women undergoing clinically indicated coronary angiography for suspected ischemic cardiomyopathy had NOCAD, with 5-year major adverse cardiac events (MACE) rates exceeding 2.5% per year and 12.8% of patients experiencing cardiovascular death or myocardial infarction at 10 years, while repeat angiography prompted by persistent symptom burden significantly increased healthcare utilization [6]. Contemporary clinical frameworks have further reinforced the prognostic significance of this population: angina with non-obstructive coronary arteries (ANOCA) and ischemia with non-obstructive coronary arteries (INOCA), now formally recognized in the 2024 ESC Guidelines for the Management of Chronic Coronary Syndromes [7], explicitly acknowledge that the absence of flow-limiting epicardial stenosis does not preclude myocardial ischemia, persistent symptoms, or adverse outcomes, underscoring the urgent need for improved risk stratification in this population. Despite guideline-directed medical therapy, NOCAD patients continue to experience persistent symptoms and adverse cardiovascular events, highlighting substantial gaps in current management approaches [8].

Inflammation plays a crucial role in atherogenesis, plaque progression, and destabilization, with anti-inflammatory therapy reducing cardiovascular events independent of lipid effects [9, 10]. Recent comprehensive reviews have further framed inflammation as a central and unifying mechanism across the spectrum of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, with implications for prognosis and therapeutic targeting that extend beyond lipid lowering alone [11, 12]. The pathophysiological significance of inflammatory processes in NOCAD is multifaceted, encompassing their role in mediating coronary microvascular dysfunction commonly observed in these patients, promoting vulnerable plaque characteristics despite minimal epicardial stenosis, and providing independent prognostic value through systemic inflammatory markers [13–15]. Inflammatory biomarkers, including neutrophils, C-reactive protein, interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), have been significantly associated with impaired microvascular dilatation in women with angina but no significant CAD [16]. Novel composite inflammatory indices, including the C-reactive protein-albumin-lymphocyte (CALLY) index, systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), integrate multiple aspects of the inflammatory response and have demonstrated superior prognostic performance compared to traditional markers in various cardiovascular conditions [17–20].

The substantial heterogeneity within the NOCAD population suggests that distinct phenotypes may exist with different underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and prognostic implications. Latent class analysis (LCA) offers a robust statistical approach to identify naturally occurring subgroups within complex clinical populations, having been successfully applied in heart failure and atrial fibrillation [21, 22]. However, inflammation-based phenotyping in NOCAD remains largely unexplored despite its potential to improve risk stratification and identify therapeutic targets [23]. Importantly, while CT-derived markers of coronary phenotyping have shown promise in characterizing inflammatory phenotypes, these techniques require dedicated imaging protocols, specialized expertise, and are not routinely applicable to all patients undergoing invasive angiography in real-world practice. In contrast, blood-based inflammatory biomarkers are universally accessible, low-cost, and routinely collected as part of standard clinical assessment, rendering them ideally suited for large-scale phenotyping in unselected NOCAD populations. Beyond risk stratification, inflammatory phenotyping based on routinely available biomarkers may serve as a practical triage tool to identify high-risk patients who would benefit most from further functional evaluation, including coronary flow reserve or index of microcirculatory resistance assessment, or advanced imaging, thereby bridging the gap between biomarker-based stratification and precision cardiovascular management.

In this study, we applied LCA to identify distinct phenotypes within a NOCAD cohort based on demographic characteristics, comorbidity profiles, and inflammatory markers. We hypothesized that inflammation-dominant phenotypes would emerge and associate with worse cardiovascular outcomes. Through comprehensive characterization and follow-up, we aimed to determine whether inflammation-based phenotyping provides additional prognostic information beyond traditional risk factors and to identify specific inflammatory markers that might serve as potential therapeutic targets in this challenging patient population.

Methods

Study population

We retrospectively analyzed patients who consecutively underwent coronary angiography for suspected angina at the Department of Cardiology, Hubei No.3 People’s Hospital of Jianghan University, between January 1, 2023, and December 1, 2024. Non-obstructive coronary artery disease (NOCAD) was defined as the presence of at least one coronary lesion with luminal stenosis ≥ 1% but < 50% in any major epicardial vessel on invasive coronary angiography. Inclusion criteria consisted of age between 18 and 80 years, presentation with angina symptoms requiring coronary angiography, and coronary angiographic evidence of NOCAD (< 50% stenosis). We excluded patients with (1) prior history of percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass grafting, (2) significant valvular heart disease (moderate or severe), (3) acute myocarditis, (4) hypertrophic or infiltrative cardiomyopathy, (5) active malignancy or life expectancy < 1 year, (6) severe renal dysfunction (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²), (7) active autoimmune disease or current use of immunosuppressive therapy, (8) pregnancy or lactation, (9) recent acute infection (within 4 weeks), or (10) insufficient clinical or follow-up data.

Data collection and quality control

Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and imaging data were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records by trained research personnel using standardized data collection forms. All data extractors were blinded to the study outcomes, and two independent cardiologists reviewed all patient records to ensure data accuracy and completeness. The data were classified into five categories:

Demographic characteristics and clinical data

Demographic information included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status (never/former/current smokers), and drinking status (never/former/current drinkers). Clinical history was documented, including comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, etc.), old myocardial infarction (OMI), old cerebral infarction (OCI), and prior medical conditions. Medication history at the time of angiography and during follow-up was recorded from pharmacy records and clinical notes.

Laboratory assessments

Laboratory data collected included routine blood tests performed as part of the mandatory pre-procedural clinical assessment protocol at our institution, obtained prior to coronary angiography either during the index hospitalization or within 14 days before the scheduled procedure. In accordance with institutional protocol, all laboratory measurements, including inflammatory biomarkers, were collected before the angiographic procedure, ensuring that all inflammatory indices reflect each patient’s baseline pre-procedural status and are not subject to confounding by procedure-related transient inflammatory changes. All laboratory tests were performed as part of routine clinical care using standardized methods in our hospital’s central laboratory. Standard laboratory measurements included complete blood count, hepatic function markers (alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, total bilirubin, serum albumin), renal function indicators (serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, estimated glomerular filtration rate), lipid profile (total cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol), high-sensitivity cardiac troponin, and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP). We also extracted data on several inflammatory biomarkers. Single inflammatory markers included high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), serum amyloid A (SAA) and white blood cell count with differential. Composite inflammatory indices were calculated from available complete blood count data and included the CRP-albumin-lymphocyte (CALLY) index, systemic immune inflammation index (SII), platelet-to-albumin ratio (PAR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), neutrophil-to-albumin ratio (NAR), albumin-to-platelet ratio (AIP), platelet to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (PHR), and aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI). Detailed calculation formulas for all composite inflammatory indices are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Imaging assessments

Coronary angiograms performed during the study period were retrospectively analyzed by two experienced interventional cardiologists who were blinded to patient outcomes. Discrepancies in stenosis assessment were resolved by consensus. Transthoracic echocardiography data were extracted from clinical reports, with assessment of cardiac structure and function according to current guidelines. For patients who underwent cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, reports were reviewed for evidence of myocardial fibrosis or microvascular dysfunction. The history of myocardial infarction (MI), defined as OMI in this study, was defined as a documented prior episode of anginal symptoms accompanied by troponin elevation, in the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease. Brain CT/MRI scans obtained within 6 months prior to enrollment were evaluated for old cerebral infarction (OCI), defined as well-demarcated hypodense lesions on CT or T1-hypointense/T2-hyperintense areas on MRI with no corresponding acute neurological deficits. Patients with a documented history of stroke were also classified as having OCI. Carotid atherosclerosis (CAS) was defined as intima-media thickness ≥ 1.0 mm or presence of plaques by carotid ultrasonography. Significant carotid stenosis was defined as luminal narrowing ≥ 50% according to velocity criteria and B-mode imaging. Beyond assessing coronary stenosis severity, coronary artery spasm, myocardial bridging, and coronary slow flow were also evaluated during angiography. All imaging analyses were independently reviewed by two experienced interventional cardiologists blinded to clinical outcomes.

Follow-up and outcome measures

Follow-up data were collected through August 2025 from electronic medical records, hospital readmission databases, and death registries. All patients undergoing coronary angiography at our department routinely receive scheduled follow-up at 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months. For patients who missed scheduled clinic visits, telephone follow-up was conducted to ascertain vital status and cardiovascular events. All follow-up data, including vital status and event occurrences, were systematically recorded and stored in the hospital’s electronic medical record system. The primary endpoint was the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), defined as a composite of (1) cardiovascular death, (2) nonfatal myocardial infarction, (3) unplanned revascularization, (4) hospitalization for unstable angina or heart failure, and (5) stroke or transient ischemic attack. Events were identified through systematic review of hospital records and death certificates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) based on Shapiro-Wilk normality tests, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. All variables with missing data (none exceeding 20%) were imputed using multiple imputations to ensure a consistent analytical approach throughout the study. Z-score standardization was applied to all inflammatory indices. Latent class analysis (LCA) was conducted using the poLCA package to identify distinct phenotypic clusters within the NOCAD population, incorporating demographic, clinical, laboratory, and angiographic parameters. Model selection (2–4 classes) was based on Bayesian Information Criterion, Akaike Information Criterion, entropy measures, and clinical interpretability. Each participant was assigned to the latent class with the highest posterior membership probability (modal assignment), yielding a categorical class membership variable for use in downstream analyses; mean posterior probabilities for each class are reported to indicate the reliability of class assignments, with values exceeding 0.75 considered indicative of adequate classification certainty. Baseline characteristics were compared across latent classes using ANOVA/Kruskal-Wallis or chi-square/Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests were used to assess differences in MACE-free survival between LCA-derived phenotypes and between groups with high versus low inflammatory marker levels. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models evaluated associations between identified phenotypes and MACEs, using the lowest-risk group as reference. Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression analyses examined MACE components (cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, hospitalization for unstable angina or heart failure, and stroke or transient ischemic attack). A sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding patients with a history of OMI, replicating the primary Cox models and Kaplan-Meier analyses to assess the robustness of findings. To evaluate the added prognostic value of the LCA inflammatory classification, we built two Cox models for MACE. Model 1 contained traditional clinical variables plus LCA class membership. Two comparisons were performed: (1) Model 1 versus a model with the clinical factors only, and (2) Model 1 versus a model with clinical factors plus hs‑CRP (replacing LCA classes). Model discrimination was compared using Harrell’s C‑index (95% CI via 1000 bootstrap samples) and DeLong’s test. The net reclassification improvement (NRI) was calculated to quantify correct reclassification of risk categories when adding LCA classification. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by The Medical Ethics Committee of Hubei No.3 People’s Hospital of Jianghan University (approval number: KY2025015). Given the retrospective design involving analysis of de-identified medical records and the absence of patient contact or intervention, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for reporting cohort studies.

Results

Latent class-derived clinical subtypes and baseline characteristics

Latent class analysis and phenotype identification

In this study, 1532 consecutive patients who underwent coronary angiography were initially screened. After applying predefined inclusion criteria, 565 patients were preliminarily eligible. Of these, 223 patients were excluded according to the exclusion criteria. The final analysis included 342 NOCAD patients (Fig. 1). Latent class analysis based on clinical characteristics and inflammatory markers identified three optimal clinical phenotypes among NOCAD patients, with model fit statistics shown in Supplementary Table 2. As shown in Fig. 2, the three distinct phenotypes were: low-inflammation with few comorbidities (n = 156, 45.6%), elderly with multiple comorbidities (n = 126, 36.8%), and high-inflammation (n = 60, 17.5%). The high-inflammation phenotype (Class 3) was characterized by elevated levels of systemic inflammatory markers, particularly SII (96.67%), AISI (88.33%), NAR (81.67%), and PLR (76.67%). The elderly with multiple comorbidities phenotype featured the highest prevalence of hypertension (82.54%), old cerebral infarction (30.95%), and type 2 diabetes mellitus (44.44%). In contrast, the low-inflammation with few comorbidities phenotype demonstrated lower inflammatory marker levels and fewer cardiovascular comorbidities, though it had the highest proportion of patients with coronary vasospasm (5.77%) and coronary bridging (12.82%) compared to other phenotypes.

Baseline characteristics across phenotypes

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the entire NOCAD population and the three identified phenotypes. Significant differences were observed across the three phenotypes in demographic features including age (p < 0.001), gender (p = 0.03), and BMI (p < 0.001). The elderly with multiple comorbidities phenotype (Class 2) had the highest mean age (68.75 ± 9.00 years) and male proportion (58.7%), while the high-inflammation phenotype (Class 3) showed the lowest BMI (23.45 ± 1.21 kg/m²). Notably, inflammatory markers demonstrated significant differences among the phenotypes, with Class 3 exhibiting substantially elevated levels of CRP, SAA, SII, PLR, NAR, and AISI compared to other phenotypes (all p < 0.001). The standardized Z-scores of inflammatory markers further supported these differences, with Class 3 showing consistently higher values, particularly for SII (Z-score = 1.38 ± 1.69) and AISI (Z-score = 1.54 ± 1.43).

In Table 1, the overall MACE rate in the total cohort was 26.02% (89/342), with a significant difference across the three latent inflammatory classes (p < 0.001). Class 1 exhibited the lowest event rate (16.67%), whereas Class 2 and Class 3 showed progressively higher rates of 32.54% and 36.67%, respectively. This gradient was primarily driven by cardiovascular death (0.64% vs. 6.35% vs. 5.00%, p = 0.03) and hospitalization for unstable angina or heart failure (11.54% vs. 16.67% vs. 25.00%, p = 0.05). In contrast, nonfatal myocardial infarction and stroke or transient ischemic attack were numerically low across all classes and did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.42 and p = 0.50, respectively).

Survival analysis across clinical subtypes and inflammatory indices

Cox proportional hazards analysis of MACEs

Cox proportional hazards models were constructed to evaluate the prognostic significance of clinical subtypes, composite inflammatory indices, and traditional inflammatory markers (SAA and CRP) across three sequential adjustment models (Table 2). Compared to Class 1 (reference), both Class 2 and Class 3 demonstrated significantly elevated risk of MACEs across all models, with Class 3 exhibiting the highest hazard ratios (Model 3: HR = 3.090, 95% CI 1.614–5.919, p = 0.001 for Class 3; HR = 2.181, 95% CI 1.224–3.886, p = 0.008 for Class 2).

Prognostic value of inflammatory indices

Among the inflammatory indices, CALLY exhibited significant protective effects when analyzed both as a continuous variable (HR = 0.402, 95% CI: 0.230–0.703, p = 0.001) and as a dichotomized variable (Z-score > 0: HR = 0.411, 95% CI: 0.210–0.806, p = 0.010). Conversely, elevated NAR and AISI indices were associated with adverse outcomes. Specifically, patients with NAR Z-score > 0 showed significantly increased risk of MACEs (HR = 1.632, 95% CI: 1.027–2.592, p = 0.038), as did those with AISI Z-score > 0 (HR = 1.635, 95% CI: 1.007–2.655, p = 0.047). Notably, traditional inflammatory markers including SAA and CRP failed to demonstrate significant prognostic value in any model configuration (p > 0.05). Supplementary Table 3 further presents the Cox proportional hazards analysis of inflammatory markers stratified by quartiles for predicting MACEs.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated significant stratification of patients into three distinct prognostic classes (Class 1–3, p = 0.0011; Fig. 3A). The CALLY index exhibited protective prognostic value, with patients harboring positive Z-scores demonstrating markedly improved MACE-free survival compared to those with negative Z-scores (p = 0.023; Fig. 3B). Conversely, the adverse inflammatory indices NAR and AISI both effectively discriminated between high-risk and low-risk patients, where individuals with positive Z-scores for either marker displayed significantly poorer MACE-free survival compared to their counterparts with negative Z-scores (p = 0.011 and p = 0.028, respectively; Fig. 3C and D).

Subgroup analysis

To assess the consistency of the prognostic impact of Class 3 (high-inflammation group) on MACEs across different patient populations, subgroup analyses were performed and presented as a forest plot (Fig. 5). Overall, Class 3 was associated with a significantly increased risk of MACEs (HR 1.79, 95% CI 1.08–2.96, P = 0.023). The association remained generally consistent across subgroups, with no significant interactions detected for gender, OMI, heart failure (HF), hypertension, OCI, hyperlipidemia, CAS, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), or chronic kidney disease (CKD) (all P for interaction > 0.05). Notably, the prognostic effect was particularly pronounced in patients with hypertension (HR = 2.14, 95% CI 1.17–3.92, P = 0.013), hyperlipidemia (HR = 2.60, 95% CI 1.21–5.57, P = 0.014), and CAS (HR = 5.09, 95% CI 1.26–20.67, P = 0.023), although none of the interaction terms reached statistical significance. Among patients without HF, the phenotypic classification remained a significant predictor of MACEs (HR = 2.13, 95% CI 1.10–4.11, P = 0.025), whereas this association was attenuated in those with established HF (HR = 1.29, 95% CI 0.58–2.83, P = 0.531). Similarly, among patients without CKD, a significant association was retained (HR = 1.75, 95% CI 1.03–2.98, P = 0.040), while the effect was not significant in those with CKD (HR = 1.33, 95% CI 0.22–7.87, P = 0.753).

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of our primary findings, a pre-specified sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding patients with a history of OMI (n = 35 excluded, n = 307 remaining). Latent class analysis of this restricted cohort again identified three new optimal latent classes, with a composition and distribution of defining characteristics that closely mirrored those of the primary analysis (Supplementary Tables 4–5). The prognostic superiority of Class 3 over Class 1 remained statistically significant in this restricted cohort (HR = 2.203, 95% CI 1.188–4.085, p = 0.012), consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Table 6). Similarly, for hospitalization due to unstable angina or heart failure, Class 3 remained a significant predictor across all models (HR = 2.262, 95% CI 1.085–4.716, P = 0.030; fully adjusted), whereas Class 2 did not reach significance. Cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and stroke or transient ischemic attack showed numerically elevated but non-significant hazard ratios for both classes, likely reflecting limited event numbers within each subcategory. Kaplan–Meier curves for total MACEs and individual components in the non-OMI cohort, are presented in Supplementary Figure S1.

Incremental prognostic value of LCA-derived inflammatory phenotypes

Receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated that the incorporation of LCA-derived class membership significantly enhanced prognostic discrimination for MACE prediction beyond traditional clinical variables alone (Supplementary Figure S2). Model 1 (traditional clinical variables plus LCA-derived class membership) achieved an AUC of 0.730 (95% CI: 0.663–0.797), representing a substantial improvement over Model 2 (traditional clinical variables alone; AUC = 0.645, 95% CI: 0.575–0.716). Similarly, when compared against traditional clinical variables supplemented with hs-CRP (AUC = 0.646, 95% CI: 0.575–0.716), the LCA-enhanced model maintained superior discriminatory performance, with DeLong’s test confirming statistically significant differences in both comparisons (p < 0.05). For prognostic validation, the LCA-derived phenotypes demonstrated significant net reclassification improvement (continuous NRI = 0.554, 95%CI 0.274–0.851), primarily through accurate risk down-classification in non-MACE patients (NRI-:0.292, 95%CI 0.155–0.516) with a positive trend in MACE patients (NRI+:0.262, 95%CI 0.030–0.443).

Discussion

In this study, we employed LCA to identify three distinct phenotypes among patients with NOCAD: a low-inflammation phenotype characterized by few comorbidities, an elderly phenotype marked by multiple comorbidities, and a high-inflammation phenotype. These phenotypes carry significantly different prognostic implications, with the high-inflammation phenotype associated with the highest risk of MACEs. While the high-inflammation phenotype is associated with the highest overall MACEs risk, this prognostic signal is predominantly driven by hospitalizations for unstable angina or heart failure. Furthermore, we demonstrated that novel composite inflammatory markers, particularly CALLY index, NAR and AISI, provide superior prognostic information compared to traditional inflammatory markers in this patient population.

Inflammatory phenotyping as a novel dimension in NOCAD classification

The recognized heterogeneity of NOCAD necessitates meaningful patient subgroups to improve risk stratification. Previous classifications predominantly focused on coronary anatomical or functional abnormalities without comprehensively integrating clinical and inflammatory profiles. Ong et al. proposed a classification based on coronary vasomotor abnormalities, identifying epicardial and microvascular vasospasm as distinct pathophysiological entities [24]. Similarly, Ford et al. stratified patients according to coronary function test results, demonstrating that tailored therapy based on these endotypes improved angina and quality of life [25]. The WISE study investigators identified a dysfunctional microvessel phenotype with low coronary flow reserve, linked to a higher risk of major adverse outcomes [26]. Meanwhile, Bairey Merz et al. proposed a comprehensive taxonomy for INOCA, which includes both structural and functional abnormalities [6]. These approaches emphasize that phenotype-specific characterization beyond conventional ischemia assessment is essential.

Our LCA revealed three distinct clusters with markedly different prognostic profiles. The low-risk phenotype (Class 1), comprising the largest proportion of our cohort, was characterized by a female predominance (57.05%), the highest prevalence of functional coronary abnormalities—including coronary bridging (12.82%), coronary slow flow (17.31%), and coronary vasospasm (5.77%)—the lowest rate of prior myocardial infarction (5.77%), and preserved left ventricular systolic function (LVEF 66.62 ± 3.73%). This profile aligns with established observations that women are disproportionately affected by NOCAD and coronary microvascular dysfunction compared with men [27, 28]. These patients typically manifest endothelial dysfunction and vasomotor abnormalities rather than significant atherosclerotic burden, which may explain their relatively favorable prognosis.

The elderly phenotype (Class 2), characterized by advanced age, male predominance, and high cardiometabolic burden, represents patients with established atherosclerosis at substantial risk for adverse outcomes. This profile is consistent with the “inflamm-ageing” framework [29], wherein immunosenescence and chronic low-grade inflammation promote endothelial injury independently of obstructive lesions. Although Class 2 did not exhibit the markedly elevated acute-phase inflammatory indices observed in Class 3, the clustering of age-related comorbidities reflects the cumulative inflammatory burden inherent to this phenotype, potentially explaining the intermediate yet significantly elevated MACE risk, especially cardiovascular death. These findings support the concept that inflamm-ageing constitutes an independent cardiovascular risk pathway in older adults, mechanistically distinct from overt systemic inflammation [30].

Most notably, the high-inflammation phenotype (Class 3, 17.5% of patients) conferred a 3.09-fold elevated MACE risk compared to Class 1. This classification remained robust in a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with a history of prior myocardial infarction. Component-level analysis of individual MACE endpoints further revealed mechanistically distinct risk profiles between Class 2 and Class 3. Class 2, characterized by advanced age and cumulative cardiometabolic burden, was predominantly associated with higher rates of cardiovascular death, findings consistent with progressive structural disease and long-standing risk-factor accumulation. Class 3, by contrast, was distinguished by markedly elevated acute-phase inflammatory indices and a substantially higher incidence of hospitalization for unstable angina or heart failure, reflecting an overtly dysregulated systemic inflammatory state that dynamically destabilizes coronary physiology and predisposes to recurrent ischemic decompensation rather than acute fatal events. Our finding aligns with accumulating evidence implicating inflammation as a central driver in the development and progression of coronary atherosclerosis, independent of traditional risk factors and stenosis severity [31]. The adverse prognosis associated with NOCAD, particularly in the high-inflammation phenotype, challenges the traditional lumen-centric paradigm of coronary disease assessment. Cohort studies have demonstrated that NOCAD patients with stable angina experience 52–85% increased risk for major adverse cardiovascular events and 29–52% elevated all-cause mortality compared with reference populations [5].

In our study, endpoint-specific associations suggest that distinct inflammatory pathways may drive different cardiovascular manifestations. Specifically, elevated PLR and NAR independently predicted cardiovascular death, while NAR also correlated with hospitalization for unstable angina or heart failure. Additionally, SII was uniquely associated with stroke or transient ischemic attack. The lack of significant predictors for nonfatal myocardial infarction likely reflects limited statistical power. Ultimately, these heterogeneous risk profiles indicate that utilizing complementary inflammatory indices, rather than a single marker, is essential for comprehensive prognostic assessment across the MACE spectrum.

Another notable finding was the paradoxically lower BMI in Class 3 despite peak inflammatory indices, seemingly challenging the conventional obesity-inflammation paradigm [32, 33]. However, Class 3 simultaneously exhibited the lowest prevalence of T2DM (21.67%) and dyslipidemia (33.33%), clearly dissociating metabolic burden from inflammatory intensity. This suggests a non-metabolic inflammatory pathway, potentially involving coronary microvascular or CKD-associated uraemic inflammation [34]. The neutrophil-predominant signature, characterized by the highest NAR and suppressed lymphocyte count, further indicates innate immune activation with adaptive immune suppression, a profile distinct from adipokine-mediated obesity inflammation and independently associated with adverse outcomes in NOCAD [35, 36]. Since BMI does not capture visceral fat distribution [37], our findings suggest that clinically significant systemic inflammation in NOCAD may arise through non-obesity-dependent mechanisms.

The concept of residual inflammatory risk [10, 38, 39] extends well to this NOCAD population without prior acute coronary events. In this primary prevention context, the high-inflammation phenotype conferred markedly elevated MACE risk independent of conventional risk factors. Incorporating LCA class membership into the predictive model effectively improved discrimination (AUC increased from 0.645 to 0.730, NRI = 0.554). This is consistent with recent evidence from the ORFAN study demonstrating that coronary inflammation predicted cardiac mortality and MACE in patients without obstructive CAD, accounting for the majority of total events in absolute terms [36]. Importantly, to further approximate a primary prevention population, we conducted a pre-specified sensitivity analysis excluding patients with a history of prior OMI. The prognostic superiority of a high-inflammation phenotype remained robust in this restricted cohort, supporting this inflammation-driven risk gradient reflects a genuine inflammatory pathway rather than the residual effect of prior injury.

It is important to note that the prognostic value of the high-inflammation phenotype in the context of composite endpoint heterogeneity should be interpreted cautiously. Our component-level analysis showed that the statistically significant association with overall MACEs was primarily driven by softer endpoints, specifically hospitalizations for unstable angina and heart failure, which accounted for over 60% of total events. In contrast, associations with hard endpoints, such as cardiovascular mortality and myocardial infarction, were not statistically significant and exhibited wide confidence intervals. This pattern is well-recognized in cardiovascular research, where more frequent but less severe events often determine the significance of composite outcomes. The wide confidence intervals observed for individual hard endpoints, such as cardiovascular death, reflect limited statistical precision driven by low event rates. Consequently, our fully adjusted models fall below the traditional events-per-variable (EPV) ratio of 10, potentially resulting in unstable estimates. Therefore, the associations regarding these rare endpoints are not definitive and must be interpreted strictly as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. These potential signals require rigorous validation in larger, appropriately powered cohorts.

Composite inflammatory index demonstrates enhanced stratification capability

We found that composite inflammatory indices exceeded the prognostic capabilities of traditional single markers in NOCAD. Previous research established hs-CRP’s prognostic value across the coronary disease spectrum, from stable angina to acute myocardial infarction [40–42]. The CANTOS trial validated this further by showing cardiovascular benefits from targeted anti-inflammatory therapy guided by hs-CRP reduction [10, 43]. Similarly, the WISE study demonstrated that both hs-CRP and SAA predict adverse outcomes in women with ischemic symptoms, non-obstructive CAD, and preserved ejection fraction [44, 45]. Despite previous evidence, hs-CRP and SAA demonstrated limited predictive efficacy as continuous variables in our NOCAD cohort. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. SAA lacked prognostic significance entirely, while hs-CRP was significant only when stratified by quartiles (Supplementary Table 2), indicating a potential threshold effect. Therefore, the lack of overall independent significance for continuous hs-CRP likely reflects limited statistical power in our specific cohort rather than a true absence of association. Furthermore, the suboptimal performance of these single markers is likely compounded by the substantial heterogeneity of our population, which dilutes individual predictive signals and suggests varying biomarker efficacy across specific NOCAD subgroups.

In contrast, composite indices (CALLY, NAR, AISI) served as decisive parameters in phenotypic stratification. Among the eight indices evaluated, the CALLY index exhibited significant protective effects, whereas elevated NAR, AISI, SII and PLR values were associated with worse prognosis. These findings align with previous studies across diverse coronary disease settings. Higher CALLY index values (> 1.13) were associated with lower risk of severe coronary lesions in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) patient [20]. The NAR index has been shown to be superior to other inflammatory ratios in predicting the severity of coronary disease in non-STEMI patients [46]. Similarly, higher AISI levels were independently associated with increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in CAD patients, regardless of whether they received percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) treatment [47, 48].

The improved performance of composite indices stems from several key advantages. First, they capture multiple aspects of inflammation simultaneously rather than isolated snapshots. By integrating different inflammatory cascade components, these indices better reflect the complex immune dysregulation and metabolic disturbances driving atherosclerosis [49]. Take albumin, for example—it appears in both CALLY and NAR because it does double duty: protecting blood vessels through antioxidant effects while serving as a marker of nutritional status [50]. This dual role means composite indices can assess both inflammatory stress and the body’s capacity to handle it. Second, composite indices may enhance signal-to-noise ratio by consolidating information from multiple inflammatory pathways, which could be especially valuable given the unique characteristics of NOCAD. The AISI index, for instance, averages information from four different white blood cell types, making it less susceptible to transient fluctuations that might skew any single cell count. Third, from a statistical standpoint, composite indices also help address practical analytical challenges. By condensing multiple correlated inflammatory markers into fewer parameters, they mitigate multicollinearity issues that can complicate multivariate models. By condensing multiple correlated inflammatory markers into fewer parameters, they reduce multicollinearity issues that can plague multivariate models [51]. Dimension reduction enhances statistical power, particularly valuable in NOCAD cohorts with lower event rates than obstructive coronary disease, maximizing predictive signal utilization.

Notably, composite indices such as CALLY, NAR, and AISI appear to function primarily as integrated markers of overall inflammatory burden, with predictive strength most evident for composite rather than individual endpoints. This pattern is consistent with the possibility that the prognostic signal is driven predominantly by the composite outcome rather than by uniform associations across specific event types, a phenomenon likely attributable to reduced statistical power following event-level stratification, given the relatively low incidence of individual MACE components in a NOCAD population. Nevertheless, certain indices demonstrated specific associations with distinct MACE components: PLR with cardiovascular death, NAR with hospitalization for unstable angina or heart failure, and SII with stroke or transient ischemic attack, reflecting heterogeneous pathophysiological pathways underlying different adverse outcomes. These findings suggest that composite and individual inflammatory indices play complementary rather than competing roles. Integrating multiple composite indices through LCA therefore offers a meaningful strategy for capturing broader inflammatory phenotypes that single-marker analyses may overlook, though validation in larger prospective cohorts remains warranted.

Strengths and limitations

Our findings have important clinical implications. First, these observations suggest that inflammatory markers should be incorporated into NOCAD risk assessment, complementing traditional anatomic evaluation with biological profiling. Second, composite inflammatory indices such as CALLY, NAR, and AISI may better reflect the complex inflammatory processes in NOCAD compared to single biomarkers, helping to identify high-risk patients more accurately. Finally, our data emphasize that NOCAD is not a benign condition but rather represents diverse disease mechanisms that require personalized diagnostic and treatment strategies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this single-center retrospective study may have introduced selection bias, though we implemented rigorous statistical adjustments including multivariable models to minimize confounding. Our findings from this real-world community-based cohort require validation in other populations. Notably, our exclusively Chinese cohort exhibited an atypical male predominance, contrasting with the classic female predominance typically observed in NOCAD populations, which may limit generalizability to younger, female, or healthier populations. Second, the robustness of our LCA phenotypes requires cautious interpretation. The smallest subgroup (Class 3, n = 60) falls below the optimal sample size for highly stable LCA solutions, introducing a potential risk of model overfitting. Since internal sensitivity analyses cannot substitute for formal external validation, reproducibility in independent populations remains to be validated. Third, although the relatively short follow-up period may not fully capture long-term outcomes, it is acknowledged that the assertion regarding better medication adherence during this timeframe remains speculative in the absence of direct adherence data; this interpretation should therefore be regarded as a possible contributing factor rather than an established finding. The evolution of cardiovascular risk in NOCAD, particularly for low-inflammatory phenotypes, may extend beyond our observation window. Fourth, the prognostic associations observed for LCA membership and individual inflammatory markers should be interpreted with caution, as composite MACE findings likely reflect the predictive signal for hospitalization due to unstable angina or heart failure, the most frequent component in this cohort. Given the low EPV ratio and severe statistical imprecision, component‑level estimates for hard endpoints are strictly exploratory and hypothesis‑generating rather than definitive. The absence of significant discrimination for cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and stroke at the component level reflects statistical underpowering rather than a true absence of association, and composite inflammatory indices should not be assumed to predict specific event types. Finally, inflammatory markers were measured at a single time point and may not reflect their dynamic nature. Although strict criteria were applied to minimize acute conditions that could artificially elevate biomarkers, serial measurements would better capture temporal fluctuations and provide deeper insights into the inflammation-outcome relationship. Therefore, external validation in diverse, multicenter, and multi-ethnic cohorts is essential before these inflammatory phenotypes can be confidently considered for clinical application.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that distinct phenotypes exist within the NOCAD population, and the high-inflammation phenotype is associated with an elevated risk of composite adverse cardiovascular outcomes. However, this prognostic signal is predominantly driven by hospitalizations for unstable angina or heart failure rather than consistent effects across individual hard endpoints. Novel composite inflammatory indices appear to offer incremental prognostic value compared to traditional single markers. These findings suggest that inflammation-based phenotyping might serve as a complementary tool for risk stratification in NOCAD, though clinical application requires cautious interpretation. Future prospective, multicenter studies are necessary to externally validate these exploratory phenotypes in diverse populations and to determine their practical utility in guiding clinical management.
