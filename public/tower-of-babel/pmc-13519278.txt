The Lived Experiences of Parents of Children Diagnosed With Congenital Heart Disease: Challenges, Emotional Responses, and Sources of Strength
Authors: Abedallah Kasem, Nadin M Abdel Razeq, Jumana Al‐Omari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis study explored the lived experiences, emotional responses, and caregiving challenges of parents of children under five years old diagnosed with congenital heart disease.DesignAn exploratory descriptive qualitative design was employed to gain in‐depth insight into parents' caregiving experiences, emotional responses and challenges.MethodsData were collected between January and February 2022. Ten parents (six mothers and four fathers) of children younger than 5 years diagnosed with congenital heart disease were purposively recruited from two public hospitals in Jordan. Semi‐structured interviews were conducted either face‐to‐face or via smartphone video call, using a flexible interview guide. Data were analysed thematically following Braun and Clarke's six‐step process. Reporting adhered to COREQ guidelines.ResultsThree overarching themes emerged: (1) The immense burden of care, including physical exhaustion, nutritional challenges, financial strain, and difficulties navigating healthcare; (2) Life as a journey on a thorny road, encompassing emotional and psychological struggles such as intense negative emotions, protective caregiving, disturbed family dynamics, and societal stigma; and (3) Strength amid challenges, highlighting spirituality, trust in divine will, and faith‐inspired charitable acts as vital sources of resilience.ConclusionParents face a heavy caregiving burden, emotional and social challenges, and financial and healthcare barriers. Yet, spirituality and faith serve as vital sources of resilience. Supporting parents requires addressing these practical demands, emotional struggles, and coping resources in a holistic way.Implications for the Profession and/or Patient CareHealthcare professionals should offer tailored emotional support, enhance communication, and receive training on the psychosocial impact of caring for a child with congenital heart disease. Family‐centred care and accessible educational resources are essential for enhancing outcomes for children and their families.Patient or Public ContributionPatients and members of the public were not directly involved in the design or conduct of this study.Reporting MethodThis study adhered to the relevant EQUATOR reporting guidelines, specifically the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).ImpactWhat problem did the study address? The study addressed the limited understanding of the emotional, social, and caregiving burdens experienced by parents of young children with congenital heart disease, particularly within local cultural and healthcare contexts.What were the main findings? Parents faced significant physical, emotional, financial, and healthcare‐navigation challenges, alongside social stigma and family strain. Spirituality and faith emerged as key sources of resilience and coping.Where and on whom will the research have an impact? The findings may inform paediatric and cardiology practice by helping nurses and other healthcare professionals provide family‐centred, culturally sensitive emotional and educational support to affected children and their families.

Introduction

In 2021, congenital heart defects (CHD) were the most common congenital birth defect and posed the highest disease burden worldwide (Bai et al. 2024). They represent a leading cause of death among individuals under 20 years, with global deaths in 2019 estimated at 217,000 (95% uncertainty interval: 177,000–262,000) (Su et al. 2022). The global prevalence of congenital heart anomalies in infants has been reported at approximately 27.8 per 10,000 births (Salari et al. 2024), with health inequalities remaining especially pronounced in countries with lower socioeconomic development (Bai et al. 2024).

The considerable burden of CHD is also reflected in the lived experiences of families, who face complex emotional, social, and financial challenges in caring for affected children (Delaney et al. 2024; Holstad et al. 2025; Kankaya et al. 2025). The emotional and financial burdens associated with caregiving can significantly affect parents' well‐being. Studies have shown that parents, particularly mothers, of children with CHD often experience mental health challenges, including stress, anxiety, depression, post‐traumatic stress disorder, and feelings of caregiving burden (Alonso Lloret et al. 2023; Besley et al. 2025; Davey et al. 2023; Demianczyk et al. 2022; Lisanti et al. 2024). These mental health challenges often begin at the time of diagnosis, persist through surgical interventions (Alonso Lloret et al. 2023), and may extend beyond hospital discharge (Lisanti et al. 2024). In a cohort study, at least one in five parents experienced clinically significant depressive or post‐traumatic stress symptoms three months after discharge, while one in four parents exhibited clinically elevated anxiety, highlighting the enduring psychological impact of caring for a child with CHD (Lisanti et al. 2024). Parental experiences of caring for a child with CHD are influenced by socio‐cultural norms, family roles, and healthcare access.

Jordan, like many countries with constrained healthcare resources, continues to face health inequalities in CHD care. Three single‐centre studies have reported variable incidence rates of CHD in infants, ranging from 12.3 to 25 per 1000 live births in Jordan (Al‐Ammouri et al. 2017; Hasan et al. 2023; Khasawneh et al. 2020). Although findings varied across centres and time periods, these studies collectively highlight a considerable burden of CHD in Jordan. Although the burden of CHD in Jordan is substantial, research exploring the lived experiences of parents remains extremely limited. Only one small‐scale qualitative study had focused on mothers' lived experiences of caring for infants with CHD (Almomani 2020), leaving fathers' perspectives and broader parental experiences largely unexplored. Since completion of the present study, Alfraheed et al. (2026) have reported findings from another qualitative investigation of Jordanian parents raising children with CHD in a different healthcare setting. While this recent study expands the available evidence, qualitative research examining parental experiences across diverse contexts in Jordan remains limited.

This gap reflects a broader trend in Arab countries, where research productivity on CHD, specifically, is significantly lower than in Europe, Canada, and the United States, with an approximate 24‐fold lower research output per capita (Bitar et al. 2024). Moreover, research output within the region is concentrated in a few countries, predominantly clinical, and published in regional journals, highlighting the need for contextually relevant studies (Bitar et al. 2024). Such low research productivity on CHD in Arab countries may contribute to gaps in understanding how local sociocultural norms shape parental experiences. Although recent qualitative evidence has begun to emerge from Jordan, the available literature remains sparse and insufficient to capture the diversity of parental experiences across healthcare settings and family contexts. Taken together, existing evidence highlights a substantial clinical burden of CHD but shows limited integration of parental lived experiences, particularly within Arab sociocultural contexts. This indicates a gap in how parents interpret, cope with, and navigate the condition in their daily lives beyond clinical descriptions.

This study addresses this gap by providing an in‐depth qualitative exploration of both mothers' and fathers' lived experiences in Jordan, with a focus on emotional, social, and cultural dimensions of caregiving. Accordingly, the study aimed to explore the lived experiences, emotional responses, and challenges faced by parents of children under five diagnosed with CHD in Jordan. By including both mothers and fathers, this research provides a comprehensive understanding of parental perspectives and informs culturally appropriate, family‐centred care strategies. The rationale for studying the first five years of children's life is that this represents a critical period of rapid growth and development, during which children with CHD are particularly vulnerable to growth failure, feeding difficulties, recurrent infections, developmental delays, and increased healthcare needs. Early identification of clinical outcomes and risk factors in this age group is essential for timely intervention, optimizing nutritional status, supporting normal development, and improving long‐term health outcomes. Therefore, focusing on children younger than five years provides clinically meaningful evidence to guide paediatric healthcare professionals in the early management of this high‐risk population.

Methods

Design

An exploratory descriptive qualitative (EDQ) design was employed to obtain in‐depth insights into parents' personal and emotional experiences of caring for a child with congenital heart disease. This design is appropriate for capturing participants' accounts with the aim of describing their experiences as reported. It is suited to generating a comprehensive description of caregiving experiences and contextual challenges within real‐world healthcare settings (Willis et al. 2016). Reporting adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines (Tong et al. 2007) to ensure rigour.

Setting and Participants

Data were collected from two leading public hospitals in Jordan that provide paediatric cardiology care: one in the capital and the other in the northern region. Purposive sampling was used to recruit parents of children under five years old diagnosed with CHD, ensuring inclusion of participants with direct lived experience relevant to the study aim. Eligibility criteria included being Jordanian nationals aged 18 years or older and having the capacity and willingness to provide informed consent. Participants were excluded if they declined participation, were unable to provide informed consent, or were unable to complete the interview due to emotional or clinical instability at the time of data collection. One investigator identified potential participants using clinic attendance records, in collaboration with paediatric cardiologists and nursing staff. Only one parent per child was included to ensure independent perspectives and avoid duplication of family experiences. Sampling continued until thematic saturation was achieved, with no new meanings or substantial variations emerging in the final interviews.

Data Collection

Semi‐structured interviews facilitated open‐ended dialogue, enabling participants to share detailed reflections while allowing flexible probing of relevant issues (Hunter et al. 2019). This approach effectively uncovered both emotional and practical caregiving challenges related to their child's CHD diagnosis and cardiology services. Most interviews were conducted face‐to‐face or via smartphone video call. No substantive differences in the richness, depth, or quality of data were observed across interview modes. Each participant took part in a single interview. Each session began with an open‐ended prompt: ‘Can you describe your experience of having a child diagnosed with congenital heart disease?’ Subsequent questions were tailored responsively to deepen understanding. Interviews were conducted in private, comfortable settings, including participants' homes and hospital consultation rooms, to promote openness. All interviews were audio‐recorded after obtaining participants' informed consent for both participation and audio recording and conducted over a two‐month period (January–February 2022). The average interview duration was 70 min. The study prioritized depth of understanding over sample size or representativeness; therefore, data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, at which point no new themes or conceptual insights emerged from the interviews.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Jordan university of Science and Technology and the ethical committees of the king Abdullah University Hospital ‘Ref.: 8/142/2021, date 05.08.2021’. Informed consent was obtained before participation, with assurances of confidentiality, anonymity, and the voluntary nature of participation. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without repercussion. For smartphone video call interviews, verbal consent was documented in accordance with ethical protocols.

Data Analysis

Original data were analysed using thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's (2006) six‐phase framework: (1) data familiarization, (2) generation of initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes, and (6) producing the report. All interview transcripts were read repeatedly to achieve familiarity with the data before coding commenced. Coding was conducted independently by the authors, who systematically coded the transcripts line by line. The researchers then met to compare codes, discuss areas of disagreement, and reach consensus. Codes with conceptual similarities were grouped into preliminary categories, which were iteratively refined into broader themes through repeated comparison with the original transcripts to ensure that the themes accurately reflected participants' experiences. Colour‐coding was used throughout the analytic process to facilitate the organization and visualization of codes and themes. Reflexivity was maintained throughout the analysis to document analytic decisions, emerging interpretations, and researchers' reflections on how their perspectives might influence data interpretation. The credibility of the findings was supported through investigator triangulation during the coding and theme development process, iterative discussions among the research team, maintenance of an audit trail of coding decisions and analytic memos, and adherence to established criteria for qualitative rigour. The first and second authors conducted initial coding independently.

Trustworthiness

To enhance trustworthiness, the study adhered to Lincoln and Guba (1985) criteria encompassing credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. Credibility was strengthened by building rapport with participants, thereby enabling candid discourse. Dependability was maintained through consistent data‐collection procedures and iterative consultation with qualitative research experts. An audit trail documenting methodological decisions, coding processes, and theme development was maintained throughout the analysis to enhance the transparency and dependability of the findings. Confirmability was supported by maintaining a reflective journal to document the researcher's assumptions, methodological decisions, and reflections throughout the research process, and by using rich verbatim quotations to substantiate analytic interpretations. Transferability was addressed through detailed contextual descriptions of the setting and participant characteristics, allowing readers to assess applicability to similar contexts.

Theme 1. The Immense Burden of Care

This theme captures the tangible, practical, and physical demands parents face in caring for a child with CHD. Parents described exhaustive efforts in managing their child's health needs, including constant vigilance, managing nutrition, navigating restrictions to protect their child's health, and enduring societal judgement or stigma. The parents illustrated parenting as a continuous state of responsibility in which caregivers become the primary managers of an unpredictable medical condition.

Subtheme 1.1: Continuous Vigilance of Child Healthcare

Parents’ narratives described an ongoing state of emotional and physical alertness in which parents continuously monitor their child's health, anticipate deterioration, and remain prepared for unpredictable medical developments, often without clear time boundaries. Parents described that this state of living in a constant state of alertness regarding their child's health became both physically and emotionally exhausting over time. This vigilance extended beyond daily care to include prolonged and often uncertain waiting periods for critical treatments. Several parents spoke of the mental strain of delayed interventions, such as one parent who recalled, ‘The surgery was delayed because of COVID; we waited and waited, but the doctor didn't come’ (P1). Others described being told to wait in the hope of natural recovery, yet seeing no progress: ‘The doctors told us to wait for the hole to close by itself, but it didn't’ (P2), and ‘The hole has been the same for two years’ (P5).

The need to postpone surgery until the child reaches a certain age or condition added another layer of stress. As one parent expressed, ‘We must wait until the child becomes older for surgery’ (P4). In some cases, medical decisions were contingent on prolonged pre‐treatment, further extending the period of uncertainty: ‘The doctor said the child needs medication for two years before deciding what to do’ (P9). For others, even the preparatory steps toward treatment were hindered by the child's fragile health, as illustrated by the parent who shared, ‘The doctor needs to do catheterization, but she is not gaining weight’ (P8).

Collectively, these accounts depict a relentless cycle of monitoring, waiting, and coping with unpredictable medical timelines. This awareness shows not only attention to the child's condition but also the emotional strain of always being ready for possible changes without knowing when they might happen.

Subtheme 1.2: Challenges Navigating Healthcare

This subtheme focused on parents' interactions with the healthcare system. Logistical, practical, and relational obstacles within the healthcare system marked parents' journeys. Administering medication was a recurring challenge, with one mother saying, ‘She took diuretics, and we struggled to give her tablets; I wish there was syrup’ (P2) and another adding, ‘We face difficulties giving medication; she always refuses to take it’ (P6). Geographic barriers also played a role: ‘I live 30 to 40 kilometers away from the hospital’ (P5) and ‘I have to take many types of transportation to get to the hospital’ (P7).

Parents often encountered systemic inefficiencies, such as ‘You need to know someone to get the appointment’ (P6) and dissatisfaction with medical communication, with one lamenting, ‘The doctor didn't give us all the attention; he treats us like a number’ (P5). Conflicting medical advice further eroded trust: ‘I don't believe the doctors; each tells me a different diagnosis’ (P10). These difficulties showed how, in addition to coping with their child's illness, parents were navigating a healthcare system that often felt unresponsive, fragmented, and exhausting. Beyond logistical barriers, the parents' experiences suggest that they often perceive themselves as positioned outside the centre of care and decision‐making, requiring them to actively negotiate access, communication, and trust within the healthcare system.

Subtheme 1.3: Continuous Search for Answers

This subtheme focused on parents managing uncertainty through information seeking. Uncertainty surrounding the child's condition, coupled with fear of misdiagnosis or inadequate care, led parents to engage in a persistent search for information and medical validation. This process was not a one‐off occurrence but rather an ongoing journey marked by active questioning, repeated consultations, and constant cross‐checking of medical advice.

Parents described how the initial shock of diagnosis often triggered a sense of urgency to ‘make sure nothing is missed’. Many sought second, third, or even multiple medical opinions, visiting different healthcare facilities or consulting specialists recommended by relatives and friends. For some, this search was a source of reassurance, while for others it prolonged anxiety as conflicting information emerged.

Several parents used online sources, particularly social media, to explore possible explanations for their child's symptoms, treatment options, and potential side effects of prescribed medications. While this was sometimes helpful, it also risked amplifying worry due to exposure to unverified or alarming content.

One parent reflected on the emotional toll of this habit, noting that ‘the worst thing is looking for the disease on social media’ (P1).

Others relied on their social networks to seek advice or confirm a diagnosis: ‘I asked relatives and hospitals multiple times to confirm diagnosis’ (P4).

For some, this process eventually reinforced trust in their chosen treatment path: ‘I took a second opinion that confirmed the treatment’ (P9).

The act of information‐seeking also served as a way for parents to feel more in control and engaged in the care process, as one mother explained, ‘I read about symptoms and medication side effects to be oriented’ (P10).

This continuous search for answers reflected the parents' underlying need for certainty and empowerment in the face of an unpredictable illness trajectory. It also underscores the importance of timely, clear, and empathetic communication from healthcare providers to reduce the emotional burden and potential misinformation arising from unregulated information sources. The parents described how uncertainty drove them to actively construct understanding and regain a sense of agency in managing their child's condition.

Subtheme 1.4: Bearing the Financial Sacrifices of Care

The economic toll of CHD care was profound. Parents struggled with the high costs of treatment, surgical interventions, and follow‐up, often compounded by inadequate insurance coverage. One participant recounted, ‘The surgery expense was too much; we couldn't afford it… I had to sell land to cover the cost’ (P1). For some, insurance offered minimal relief—‘Insurance only covers 30% of the surgery cost’ (P5)—forcing families to make difficult sacrifices: ‘We gave up on buying a car to cover the surgery cost’ (P7). Others faced more severe financial collapse: ‘We had to sell the house, cars, even gold to cover the surgery cost’ (P10). Lack of spousal financial support further compounded the strain, as one mother shared, ‘My husband didn't give me money; my mother gave me money to go to the clinic’ (P8). These accounts illustrate how financial hardship was not an occasional challenge but an ongoing source of stress, influencing medical decisions and daily life. These sacrifices and economic strains reflected the extent to which parents prioritized their child's survival and treatment over their own and their family's financial stability.

Subtheme 1.5: Nutrition: A Concern and A Responsibility

Feeding and nutrition emerged as a persistent challenge for many caregivers, often intertwined with the child's poor appetite, selective eating, and concerns about growth and weight gain. Parents described the daily struggle to encourage their child to eat, frequently expressing frustration and worry over inadequate intake. One mother explained, ‘The disease affected her appetite; she doesn't ask for food’ (P2), while another recounted, ‘We tried seven types of milk before he accepted one, adding calorie‐dense complementary infant food to support weight gain’ (P3).

These difficulties were not limited to food preferences; they were compounded by slow or insufficient weight gain, which heightened parental anxiety, particularly when adequate weight was required for surgical readiness. As one caregiver noted, ‘My child only eats a little, like an egg; the doctor said she must gain weight before surgery’ (P10). Others described their children as ‘not gaining weight as expected’ (P8) or ‘fragile’ (P9), despite careful monitoring and efforts to provide nutritious meals. One parent emphasized, ‘I make sure everything he eats is healthy’ (P7).

For these families, feeding difficulties were not merely a practical problem but a persistent emotional stressor. Caregivers constantly navigated the balance between medical recommendations, the child's preferences, and the pressure to ensure adequate growth. This positions children's diet and nutrition as an ongoing caregiving responsibility that extends beyond feeding into monitoring survival readiness and perceived child fragility.

Theme 2. Life as a Journey on a Thorny Road

This theme reflects the profound emotional, psychological, social, and financial challenges parents face. It captures the internal and external struggles that make parenting a child with CHD a difficult, often painful journey. These burdens are not limited to the medical dimension—they permeate parents' emotional states, social relationships, and daily living, often reshaping family dynamics and priorities. For these parents, caregiving was an all‐encompassing lived experience shaped by emotional distress, protective responsibility, and social negotiation under conditions of uncertainty.

Subtheme 2.1: Navigating Negative Emotional Reactions

Parents described a spectrum of powerful emotions—sadness, shock, fear, denial, and feelings of fragility—triggered by the diagnosis and intensified by the ongoing care demands. Many recalled the moment of diagnosis as shattering. One parent said, ‘After we went from the hospital, we felt devastated, especially for a girl’ (P1), while another added, ‘When we first learned about the diagnosis, I felt sad and stressed; I even cried a lot’ (P4).

Several shared the persistent grief of seeing their child suffer, such as, ‘I may cry just talking about this; when I look at her, I look with a sad look’ (P2) and ‘I developed hypertension because of my sadness on him; it's a heartbreaking experience’ (P9). The awareness of possible mortality weighed heavily: ‘It's a difficult feeling knowing that your child may not live’ (P3).

Tears were a standard outlet, with many stating simply, ‘When we knew about the diagnosis, we cried’ (P6), ‘We both cried when we were informed about her disease’ (P10), or ‘I cry a lot, and my spirit is crushed’ (P8).

Shock was an immediate reaction for many. One mother admitted, ‘I was shocked; I tried not to believe what I was told about the diagnosis—it was like a nightmare’ (P7), while another described it as simply, ‘It was shocking for us’ (P10), with one father illustrating the news about the diagnosis as ‘a bullet in the head’ (P5).

For some, denial emerged as a defence mechanism. As one participant shared, ‘My husband always says he is fine, nothing wrong with him, that I am being extra worried’ (P7), and another recounted, ‘Her father's reaction was that nothing is wrong with her’ (P8).

Feelings of fragility and weakness permeated their caregiving identity. Parents expressed moments when their emotional reserves were depleted: ‘I sometimes got weak and can't stand what is going on, but she (her child) gives me strength’ (P2); ‘I always try to be strong but sometimes I got weak and I cry’ (P7); and ‘We felt weak and hopeless because we are dealing with her like a fragile glass’ (P10).

Fear was constant and multifaceted, encompassing the child's survival, ability to live an everyday life, and social acceptance. Some lived in a state of perpetual anxiety—‘We are always afraid about her… we are always living terrified’ (P1)—while others worried about functional limitations, as in ‘We are worried that he may struggle to join in school play and that something difficult or embarrassing might happen in front of his classmates’ (P4).

Parents feared both the immediate risks, ‘I was afraid of surgery’ (P9), and the uncertain future, ‘I am even afraid of tomorrow from everything’ (P7). The possibility of loss was a silent shadow, with some admitting, ‘We are afraid of losing him’ (P3) or ‘I was afraid that she would not survive’ (P8). The parents' emotional responses reflected a sustained psychological burden shaped by uncertainty, anticipatory fear, and perceived vulnerability across the caregiving trajectory.

Subtheme 2.2: Protective Caregiving and Lifestyle Restrictions

Parents described imposing significant lifestyle changes to safeguard their child's health and prevent potential complications. These restrictions extended beyond medical care to daily routines, social interactions, and the child's physical activities. For many, this vigilance was constant, reflecting both the fragility they perceived in their child's condition and their deep sense of responsibility.

Protective behaviours included limiting the child's exposure to environmental hazards and public settings. For example, some parents restricted visitors' behaviours: ‘We prevented smoking around him and anyone who comes to our house’ (P3), ‘I don't let family members smoke around her’ (P6), while others avoided taking the child out in public: ‘I don't go outside often with her; I try to protect her from people’ (P5).

Parental overprotection also shaped decisions about the child's educational and social environment. One mother described being ‘strict with nursery directions and [paying] more to avoid upset’ (P4), while another requested that her child be placed in a smaller class at school to minimize risks: ‘I didn't want her to run or be hurt at school’ (P8).

Parents also sought additional attention from educators, as illustrated by ‘We gave him whatever he wanted and asked the teacher to be extra caring’ (P9).

In many cases, this vigilance extended to limiting physical exertion and emotional distress: ‘She is not allowed to cry or play; at the slightest illness, we take her to the hospital’ (P10). For some, heightened caregiving became a central part of their identity and daily life: ‘I wanted to be with him 24 hours; he is everything’ (P7).

Others acknowledged increased indulgence, often as a way of compensating for the child's health challenges: ‘Her father spoiled her more than her brothers’ (P6). The parents' perceived fragility transformed into continuous protective regulation of the child's environment, behaviour, and social exposure.

Subtheme 2.3. Family Support as a Source of Strength and Struggle

Family support emerged as a double‐edged factor—either a critical source of emotional strength or an added layer of distress. In many accounts, supportive relatives became pillars of resilience. For example, one parent expressed gratitude: ‘They are standing with us; they give us positive energy more than you think’ (P2).

Others highlighted practical assistance: ‘My mother always helps me with my children when I got tired’ (P4) and the affectionate bonds: ‘Her uncles love her so much’ (P5).

Spousal support could also strengthen marital relationships: ‘My husband was very supportive’ and ‘Our relationship became closer’ (P7). Even couples who had separated found renewed connection during the crisis: ‘We left each other and got back; our families were very supportive’ (P10).

However, the absence of support—or worse, hostility—added to parents' emotional burden. Some faced neglect, as one mother revealed, ‘My husband didn't give me any money; my mother used to give me money’ (P8). Others endured verbal cruelty: ‘My husband's family always try to make me down and upset me with cruel words’ (P8) and ‘His mother always hurt me with her words’ (P9). These contrasting experiences highlight family support as a dynamic relational resource that can either buffer or intensify caregiver burden. These negative interactions intensified feelings of isolation and vulnerability during an already challenging caregiving journey.

Subtheme 2.4. Facing Societal Judgement and Stigma

Beyond the immediate challenges of caregiving and medical management, parents also faced societal judgement and stigma associated with their child's congenital heart defect. This external scrutiny added a layer of emotional burden, as families navigated not only the illness itself but also misconceptions and blame within their communities.

Some parents described a need to conceal the diagnosis to avoid gossip or negative labelling. One parent shared, ‘No one knows about our child's illness except my wife's brother because people say this family has heart problems’ (P1), reflecting the fear of being stereotyped or singled out.

Another explained how family members urged secrecy: ‘My parents told me not to tell anyone because people say things that are not true’ (P6), highlighting concerns about misinformation and damaging rumours.

The stigma sometimes extended to harsh personal accusations and relational strain. One mother recounted painful blame: ‘I have been told I transferred the disease to my child, and my husband said he can't stand having ill children from me’ (P8). Such judgements not only exacerbated parents' emotional distress but also isolated them further, as they felt misunderstood or unfairly targeted by those around them.

This societal dimension marked the broader psychosocial challenges families endured, complicating their experience of caregiving and amplifying feelings of vulnerability and loneliness. The parents' experiences illustrate how social meanings attached to illness extend the burden of CHD beyond the family unit, shaping identity, disclosure decisions, and emotional well‐being.

Theme 3. Strength Amid Challenges

This theme illuminates how parents find meaning, hope, and resilience amid the trials of caring for a child with a congenital heart defect. Despite the intense emotional and practical challenges, many parents draw strength from their faith, spiritual beliefs, and charitable acts, which sustain them and help them endure the journey. Spirituality and relational support function as key resilience resources that enable parents to reframe suffering and maintain emotional endurance.

Subtheme 3.1: Drawing Strength from Faith and Meaning

For many parents, spirituality and faith serve as a vital source of inner strength. They interpret their child's illness as part of a divine test or plan, which helps them accept the hardship with patience and hope. This belief offers a framework for making sense of suffering and uncertainty, enabling parents to cope by placing trust in a higher power. As one parent expressed, ‘We have only our relationship in God, and I didn't do anything except return to God’ (P3). Others echoed this deep surrender and reliance on divine will: ‘It's up to God; I pray day and night to keep her safe for me’ (P5), and ‘It's ok to do, you know why? Because it's in the hand of God’ (P6).

Parents described how this faith influenced their outlook: ‘Especially when my child came after two years of marriage, I let everything on God, and I know that he gives it to me and he is more merciful with him than me’ (P7). This acceptance of God's mercy and plan helped alleviate some of the emotional burden, allowing them to care for their child with hope rather than despair. One parent summarized this trust: ‘I put all my hope in God, and leave it in God's will’ (P9). Another reinforced this belief in divine control: ‘We only pray; after all, it's God's will, nothing we can do, we did everything we can do—everything is up to God’ (P10). Across parents' accounts, faith functioned as an active coping framework that transformed uncertainty into acceptance and emotional endurance.

Subtheme 3.2: Faith‐Inspired Acts of Charity and Prayer

Faith also translated into concrete actions that helped parents cope emotionally. Engaging in charity was a means of expressing gratitude, seeking blessings, and finding spiritual solace. One parent shared, ‘Other than praying, I give away charity’ (P2), highlighting how giving back nurtured a sense of purpose and connection beyond their own struggle. Another described balancing sorrow with positive acts: ‘When upset, we give through charity or entertain ourselves with religious gatherings’ (P4), indicating that ritual and generosity served as coping mechanisms. The spiritual rhythm of religious occasions also offered comfort, as one participant said, ‘In Ramadan, we gave away and left it to God’ (P7), connecting personal hardship with communal faith practices. These practices demonstrate how spirituality is enacted through behaviour that reinforces meaning‐making and emotional regulation.

Subtheme 3.3: Trusting in God's Plan and Mercy

Underpinning the above experiences is a profound trust in God's plan and mercy. This trust represents a conscious decision to surrender control and anxiety, transferring the weight of uncertainty to divine care. Many parents described this surrender as essential to their emotional survival. The repeated emphasis on leaving outcomes ‘in God's hands’ signals a deep spiritual resilience. For example, the words of several parents harmonize in this theme: ‘We have only our relationship in God and I did not do anything except return to God’ (P3), ‘It's up to God’ (P5), ‘It is in the hand of God’ and ‘everything is from God’ (P6).

This trust alleviated fears about their child's future, providing hope amid uncertainty: ‘I let everything on God, and I know that he gives it to me and he is more merciful’ (P7). It framed the difficult caregiving journey not merely as a trial but as a meaningful experience grounded in divine compassion. The theme culminates in the acceptance that ‘We only pray… it's God's will… nothing we can do… everything is up to God’ (P10), demonstrating how faith underpins hope, resilience, and peace.

This profound trust in God's plan and mercy ultimately provided parents with a sustaining source of peace and resilience. It transformed the ongoing challenges of caring for a child with congenital heart defects into a journey of spiritual meaning, enabling them to endure hardship with hope and courage grounded in their faith. Overall, parents' experiences reflect strong trust in God's will and mercy, which help them accept uncertainty and reduce fear about their child's future.

Discussion

This study explored the multifaceted experiences and challenges encountered by parents caring for children under five diagnosed with CHD in Jordan, revealing three overarching themes: the immense burden of care, the emotional and psychosocial challenges, and the sources of strength and resilience.

Our findings support previous findings regarding the psychological strain and immense burden created by parenting a child with CHD. Parents experienced intense emotions, including shock, grief, denial, uncertainty, and ongoing fear, consistent with prior findings on the psychological impact of paediatric CHD on families (Ay et al. 2023; Harris et al. 2023; Lee and Ahn 2020). These findings are also consistent with the recent qualitative study by Alfraheed et al. (2026), which similarly described profound emotional responses following diagnosis and the substantial psychological burden experienced by Jordanian parents of children with CHD. The immense burden is characterized by daily hands‐on care, where parents must be meticulous and cautious, sometimes going beyond what is typically expected in parenting (Ay et al. 2023; Kankaya et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2023). Similarly, Alfraheed et al. (2026) described parents' continuing adaptation to the challenges of caring for a child with CHD, reinforcing the substantial caregiving and emotional burden experienced by families. For families of children with CHD, normative family tasks become abnormally stressful when compounded by the demands of caring for a child with CHD, resulting in heightened emotional and caregiving burdens (Zhang et al. 2023).

Parents expressed this burden through physical and emotional exhaustion from childcare, painful waiting for medical procedures, the demands of ongoing treatment, and the relentless search for the best available treatments and opinions (Ay et al. 2023; Kankaya et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2023), and the relentless search for the best treatments and opinions (Kankaya et al. 2025). Parents not only had to maintain strict attention to their child's physical condition but also endured the emotional toll of stalled progress and deferred hope, reinforcing the constant vigilance that defined their caregiving role. This ongoing vigilance reflects what Alfraheed et al. (2026) described as parents' ongoing adjustments to the challenges of caregiving while navigating the complexities of raising a child with CHD.

Our findings resonate with prior studies showing families' profound uncertainty and persistent fear of losing their child, with anxiety about surgery, mortality, and the future consistently shaping parents' lived experiences (Ay et al. 2023; Harris et al. 2023; Kankaya et al. 2025; Lee and Ahn 2020). Similarly, Alfraheed et al. (2026) reported that Jordanian parents experienced profound emotional changes after diagnosis, persistent uncertainty, and emotional distress while navigating their child's illness, highlighting the psychological impact of CHD on families during the caregiving journey. These findings provide essential insights for healthcare providers on the importance of offering clear communication and integrated emotional support to help families manage uncertainty and fear alongside medical care.

However, our study revealed that such support and information were often perceived as insufficient. Parents frequently reported gaps in communication, inadequate guidance about their child's condition, and a lack of emotional support, which compounded their uncertainty and distress. This finding is consistent with Alfraheed et al. (2026), who identified parents' need for information, guidance, and active meaningful involvement in their child's care as an essential component of coping and adaptation. Findings from other research show that when families receive timely information, reassurance, and practical guidance, they are better able to cope with the stressors following diagnosis, support their adaptation, and reduce their distress (Catapano et al. 2024; Kankaya et al. 2025).

The parents often adopted intensive caregiving practices and significant lifestyle modifications, shaped by a combination of medical advice, personal fears, and the desire to preserve their child's well‐being. Parents of children with CHD engage in highly vigilant caregiving, including nutrition, medication, sleep, and complex care coordination. Their uncertainty around feeding and technical tasks, such as formula choice and nasogastric tube management (Ay et al. 2023; Nayeri et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2023). While such measures provided a sense of control, they also reflected the underlying anxiety and hypervigilance that accompanied daily life for many families.

Our findings indicate that financial hardship is not a secondary concern but a central component of caregiver burden. Evidence suggests that financial burden is deeply intertwined with emotional distress, caregiving capacity, and family functioning, particularly among families with limited socioeconomic resources (de Silva and Gamage 2024; Delaney et al. 2024; Kankaya et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2023). Consistent with our findings, Alfraheed et al. (2026) identified financial challenges related to children's CHD treatment costs, medications, and travel as part of the Heavy Burden of Survival. Families are confronted not only with the direct costs of medical treatment, such as surgery, hospitalization, and medications, but also with substantial indirect and often overlooked expenses, including transportation, accommodation, food, and daily living costs during prolonged hospital stays (Zhang et al. 2023); the economic strain extends beyond the financial hardship of children's treatments to affect parents' emotional well‐being and family functioning and dynamics (Alfraheed et al. 2026).

On the other hand, caregiving demands actively disrupt employment through job instability, unpaid leave, and underemployment, shifting the interpretations from financial stress as a consequence to employment disruption and a driver of financial burden (de Silva and Gamage 2024; Delaney et al. 2024). The evidence from these studies and ours suggests that without adequate financial, employment, and social support, caregivers—especially mothers—face compounded stress that may undermine both their well‐being and the quality of care provided to their children (de Silva and Gamage 2024; Delaney et al. 2024; Kankaya et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2023).

An important dimension of the findings relates to societal stigma and judgement surrounding children with CHD. Parents described concerns about disclosure, fear of gossip, and the need to conceal the diagnosis to avoid negative labelling within their communities. This reflects a broader psychosocial burden extending beyond the immediate caregiving role, where illness becomes socially visible and potentially stigmatized. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting negative societal perceptions of CHD and its impact on families' social participation and sense of belonging (Al Obieat et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023). Such stigma was not only directed at the child but also extended to the family unit, contributing to feelings of isolation, vulnerability, and emotional distress. In some cases, stigma shaped disclosure decisions and reinforced protective behaviours, such as secrecy, to preserve social standing and avoid discrimination. Within this cultural context, stigma operates as a powerful social force that amplifies the overall burden of CHD beyond its clinical implications.

The findings emphasized the central role of culturally and religiously grounded spirituality in shaping emotional resilience and sustaining parents throughout the caregiving journey. Faith was both a coping resource and a meaning‐making framework for parents. Parents described engaging in religiously grounded practices such as prayer, charitable giving, and participation in religious gatherings as intentional strategies to manage distress and restore emotional balance. These practices reflect not only individual coping responses but also culturally embedded forms of resilience through which suffering is reframed within a spiritual context. In this study, faith went beyond passive acceptance to include active expressions of spirituality, such as charity and collective religious practices, illustrating how belief systems are translated into lived, practical coping behaviours. This is consistent with previous research showing that religion and spirituality are frequently used coping mechanisms among parents of children with CHD, contributing to emotional adjustment and psychological relief (Davey et al. 2023; Demianczyk et al. 2022). Parents of children with CHD in Jordan described spirituality as a source of refuge, where practices such as prayer, supplication, and religious recitation provided emotional stability, acceptance, and hope while navigating the uncertainty associated with their child's illness (Alfraheed et al. 2026). Prior studies have similarly highlighted that turning to religion provides a cognitive framework for interpreting illness and enhancing acceptance, hope, and perceived control in situations of uncertainty (de Silva and Gamage 2024; Nayeri et al. 2021).

The findings emphasize that optimal care for children with CHD requires holistic, family‐centred approaches that integrate medical, emotional, social, and spiritual support. Paediatric nurses are essential in implementing family‐centred care by fostering open communication, building trusting relationships, and tailoring interventions to each family's unique context (IPFCC 2023). Training healthcare providers to recognize the multidimensional needs of parents and to collaborate effectively with families can improve outcomes and reduce caregiver burden (Çetintaş et al. 2023; Seniwati et al. 2023).

Implications for Nursing Practice

Paediatric nurses who work with children diagnosed with cardiac problems, as frontline providers, are uniquely positioned to assess emotional well‐being and provide psychosocial support or referrals to counselling services. For healthcare providers, particularly paediatric nurses who maintain close contact with families, recognizing these burdens is crucial for delivering empathetic, individualized care. Paediatric nurses play a crucial role in educating families, coordinating care, and assisting with practical problem‐solving, such as administering medication and providing nutritional support. Addressing communication barriers with healthcare providers can further reduce caregiver stress. The adoption of protective caregiving behaviours and lifestyle restrictions, while understandable, can isolate families, underscoring the need for nurses and multidisciplinary teams to promote balanced caregiving that supports child development and social inclusion. The variable presence of family support and the added strain of societal stigma further complicate parents' experiences. Paediatric nurses are also well positioned to identify and address social stigma experienced by families by providing stigma‐sensitive counselling, encouraging social inclusion, and promoting balanced caregiving practices that support the child's developmental and social needs, as well as the family's spiritual and cultural values. Nurses can advocate for and facilitate access to social, financial, and community resources and referrals to support groups to help mitigate these challenges.

The severity of congenital heart disease (CHD) is an important determinant of both clinical outcomes and parental psychological well‐being. Children with complex or severe CHD, particularly those requiring multiple surgical interventions, prolonged hospitalization, or intensive follow‐up, place substantial emotional and caregiving demands on their families. Previous studies have demonstrated that parents of children with more severe forms of CHD report significantly higher levels of stress, anxiety, and psychological distress than parents of children with less complex cardiac conditions (Demianczyk et al. 2022; Jackson et al. 2015). Furthermore, research has consistently identified differences in emotional responses between mothers and fathers. Mothers generally report greater levels of anxiety, depression, caregiving burden, and emotional distress, likely reflecting their more frequent role as the primary caregiver, whereas fathers often report lower levels of emotional symptoms but may experience distress through concerns about financial responsibilities, family stability, and supporting their partner. These findings highlight the importance of characterizing the severity of children's CHD using medical records and considering both maternal and paternal emotional responses when evaluating the psychosocial impact of paediatric CHD, as these differences may influence family adaptation, coping strategies, and the design of family‐centred interventions.

Healthcare organizations may consider integrating routine psychosocial assessment of parents into paediatric cardiology services and establishing structured family‐centred support programs that include caregiver education, counselling, and peer support. At the policy level, strengthening access to multidisciplinary family support services and incorporating psychosocial care into standard CHD care pathways may help address the emotional, social, and practical needs of families. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of nurse‐led and multidisciplinary interventions to reduce caregiver burden, improve family coping, and enhance psychosocial outcomes across diverse healthcare settings.

Conclusion

Parents caring for children with CHD face multifaceted challenges shaped by emotional, spiritual, financial, and social factors. Their caregiving burden includes physical exhaustion, emotional distress, and systemic obstacles. Psychological struggles and social stigma further affect family dynamics and well‐being. Despite these hardships, many parents find resilience through spirituality, trust in divine will, and faith‐inspired acts. Addressing these intertwined challenges is essential to providing holistic support that meets both practical and emotional needs. Creating a compassionate healthcare environment with empathetic providers and accessible resources can help alleviate some of the pressures parents face in managing their child's condition.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths, including purposive sampling that ensured inclusion of parents with relevant caregiving experience for children with CHD. Semi‐structured interviews facilitated rich, in‐depth data, providing a comprehensive understanding of parents' challenges. Methodological rigour, including thematic analysis conducted in accordance with established protocols, enhanced the credibility, dependability, and confirmability of the findings. However, we acknowledge some limitations that could guide future studies. This study focused on parents as primary caregivers; however, other family members or caregivers may have different perspectives that were not captured. Future research may focus on including a broader range of caregivers, such as grandparents or other family members involved in the child's care, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the caregiving experience. Although our study prioritized depth of understanding rather than sample size or representativeness, limitations include a relatively small sample size from a narrow region of Jordan and data collection from only two public hospitals, which may limit transferability to other regions or settings. Accordingly, the findings can be interpreted as reflecting the experiences of parents within these specific healthcare settings and sociocultural contexts, rather than as representative of all parents of children with CHD in Jordan or elsewhere. Moreover, our findings may inform the development and delivery of family‐centred nursing care and provide insights for future intervention research, rather than implying demonstrated effectiveness. Additionally, Logistical challenges limited fathers’ participation, potentially resulting in a gendered perspective. Future studies involving more diverse geographical regions, healthcare settings, and caregiver groups would help further examine the transferability of these findings across different contexts. As data were collected in 2022, some contextual aspects of care delivery may have evolved; therefore, findings should be interpreted with consideration of their temporal context and assessed for transferability to current practice. Finally, the severity of the child's condition was not detailed, which may have influenced interpretation of caregivers' emotional responses. Future research is needed to give more insight about intervention effectiveness, which was not tested in this study. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable insights into the unique needs and challenges of parents caring for children with CHD, informing healthcare practices and policies to better support families.
