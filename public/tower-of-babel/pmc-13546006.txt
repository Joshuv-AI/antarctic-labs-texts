Depletion of Soluble PD-L1 with Engineered Nanoparticles Promotes Antitumor Immunity and Tumor Control
Authors: Michelle A Hsu, Jacob B Hirdler, Roxane Lavoie, Jacob J Orme, Louis Hawthorne, Curtis L Ruegg, John Dodgson, Haidong Dong, Sean S Park, Fabrice Lucien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeProgrammed Cell Death Protein 1 (PD-1) and Programmed Cell Death Ligand 1 (PD-L1) checkpoint blockade has led to improvements in clinical outcomes for various advanced cancers. However, response rates remain low, and most patients present with intrinsic resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. Circulating soluble PD-L1 (sPD-L1) has emerged as a driver of resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. Elevated levels of sPD-L1 can be detected in peripheral blood in patients with cancer and are associated with poor prognosis and resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 therapy, highlighting the need to find strategies to remove sPD-L1 from circulation. Here, we evaluated the efficacy and immunological responses of using NaNots®A, a type of engineered nanoparticle that has been designed to capture sPD-L1, as a therapeutic agent to treat cancer.MethodsDifferent biological samples containing sPD-L1 were tested pre- and post-treatment to determine the capturing efficiency of NaNots. To evaluate the therapeutic potential of NaNots in vivo, a humanized PD-L1 mouse model was used with an sPD-L1 secreting tumor model to assess tumor growth and to immunophenotype antitumor responses.ResultsNaNot treatment successfully depleted sPD-L1 from multiple sources, including patient and mouse plasma. NaNot treatment resulted in substantial tumor growth delay and increased proportions of effector CD8 T cells, concurrent with decreased immunosuppressive regulatory T cells, in tumor and spleen tissues.ConclusionOverall, this preclinical work demonstrates that selective capture of sPD-L1 in vivo with a novel nanotherapeutic platform can reduce immune suppression concurrent with greater immune activation, thereby enabling tumor growth control.Keywords: nanoparticles, nanomedicine, soluble PD-L1 depletion, immune checkpoint, antitumor immunity, humanized mouse model

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) targeting the Programmed Cell Death Protein 1/Programmed Cell Death Ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) pathway have revolutionized the treatment of cancers, by providing unprecedented survival rates in patients with historically negative prognoses and high rates of resistance to conventional treatments.1 These therapeutics work specifically to block the interaction of PD-L1 with its cognate receptor, PD-1, preventing the inhibition of T cell responses. Currently, there are 11 antibody therapeutics targeting PD-1 or PD-L1 that are Food and Drug Administration approved to treat patients with a variety of cancers (fda.gov). However, one decade following the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of the first PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) inhibitor, Pembrolizumab (an anti-PD-1 antibody), it is now clear that most patients either do not respond to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition or acquire resistance after initial responses, with less than 30% of patients responding to treatment.2 Overcoming resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition remains a significant challenge. Another concerning issue in patients treated with ICI is immune-related adverse events (irAEs), as these are common, affecting approximately 40% of all patients treated, and can be fatal.3 These challenges provide a framework to understand further the mechanisms of primary and adaptive resistance to ICI and to develop rational therapies for improving the clinical outcomes of patients with cancer.

Originally described as membrane-bound proteins, immune checkpoint molecules can be found circulating as soluble forms.4 Soluble PD-L1 (sPD-L1) was initially reported in cell culture media of tumor cells and mature dendritic cells.5,6 sPD-L1 can be generated by proteolytic cleavage of the extracellular domain from the cell surface in a mechanism dependent on metalloproteases of a disintegrin and metalloproteinase (ADAM) and Matrix metalloproteinases (MMP) families.7–9 A second mechanism of sPD-L1 biogenesis involves alternative splicing of PD-L1 transcripts resulting in a truncated form of PD-L1 lacking the transmembrane domain.10–12 By carrying an intact extracellular domain, sPDL1 retains its functional activity through binding of its receptor, PD-1, thereby inhibiting T cell cytotoxic function and mediating immunosuppression.5,10,11 Additionally, sPD-L1 can act as a decoy during treatment by sequestering anti-PD-L1 antibodies and allowing more opportunity for membrane-bound PD-L1 to engage with PD-1.13 In murine tumor models, secretion of sPD-L1 has been associated with resistance to therapeutic PD-L1 antibodies.12,14 Consistent with this finding, elevated concentrations of sPD-L1 have been reported in several cancers, and in most cases, high sPD-L1 concentrations are associated with poor prognosis.12,15 Furthermore, numerous studies have shown that high pre-treatment levels of blood-derived sPD-L1 have been associated with lower response rates to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors.4,16–20 Treatment of patients with anti-PD-L1 mAbs, such as atezolizumab, generates very significant (25–30-fold) accumulations of circulating sPD-L1 even after the first dose. This is due to the complexation of sPD-L1 with mAbs, resulting in the extension of sPD-L1’s natural half-life.21–23 This phenomenon is widely recognized across many soluble targets for which blocking mAbs are used therapeutically.24 The binding between a mAb and its target, PD-L1 in this case, is reversible. As a result, sPD-L1 accumulates and has the potential to amplify the suppressive effect on PD-1+ T cells, thus representing a significant limitation of existing anti-PD-L1 mAbs.

Preclinical and clinical evidence supports the concept that targeting soluble PD-L1 (sPD-L1) specifically may enhance local and systemic antitumor immunity and improve responses to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. However, no clinically established therapeutic strategy selectively targeting sPD-L1 has been developed. One approach is to reduce sPD-L1 production by interfering with mechanisms that regulate PD-L1 processing and release. For example, inhibition of MMP and ADAM proteases can reduce proteolytic cleavage of membrane-bound PD-L1,7,25,26 while inhibition of PD-L1 palmitoylation promotes lysosomal degradation of PD-L1 and may consequently decrease sPD-L1 release.27 Although these strategies are biologically relevant, they are not specific to sPDL1 and may broadly affect PD-L1 biology or other protease-regulated pathways, potentially leading to irAEs and limiting their translational potential. A second approach is to remove sPD-L1 from circulation using therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE), a clinical procedure in which plasma containing pathogenic soluble factors is removed and replaced. Traditionally used in autoimmune and neurological disorders, TPE has more recently been explored in cancer as a means to eliminate tumor-promoting circulating factors, including sPD-L1.28,29 While TPE combined with radiation has been shown to resensitize patients with immunotherapy-refractory melanoma, this approach is inherently nonselective and removes many plasma proteins and soluble mediators that may also contribute to the observed clinical responses.30,31 Thus, there remains a need for a selective strategy capable of depleting circulating sPD-L1 while preserving other components of plasma immunity.

A large body of work has focused on developing nanoparticles (NPs) mostly as a drug delivery system, and some NP-based therapeutics have been approved by the FDA for the treatment of various cancers.32 NPs have also been engineered to be used in proof of-concept experiments to bind and neutralize circulating cytokines selectively in acute inflammatory disorders33,34 and as a static adsorbent in the joint compartment in arthritis.35 Due to their size, therapeutic NPs are cleared from circulation by the liver where they are phagocytosed and degraded by liver-resident macrophages (Kupffer cells).36 In preclinical models of systemic inflammation, cell-derived NPs decorated with decoy receptors against IL-6, GM-CSF or TNF-α have been shown to capture circulating cytokines and suppress immune-related inflammation.33,37 In contrast, the therapeutic potential of NPs to selectively target and deplete soluble immune inhibitory molecules, such as sPD-L1, and to restore antitumor immunity has not been investigated yet.

In this study, we developed NaNots®A (ANaNots is a registered trademark of NaNotics LLC), engineered and functionalized NPs with an anti-PD-L1 mAb as a capture agent, shielded with polymer molecules, with the intention of selectively depleting circulating sPD-L1. Using transgenic mouse models, we evaluated the efficacy of NaNots in depleting sPD-L1 in vivo and enhancing immune-mediated tumor clearance. Our studies demonstrate depletion of sPD-L1 by NaNots as an innovative approach to treat sPD-L1-secreting cancers. Additionally, this treatment has the potential to improve historically poor response rates of patients with solid tumors treated with PD-1/PD-L1 antibody inhibitors.

Materials and Methods

The main objective of this study was to assess the efficacy and therapeutic potential of depleting sPD-L1 with NaNots using a tumor model known for secreting high levels of sPD-L1.

Generation of sPD-L1-Depleting NaNots

NaNots are functionalized, adsorptive therapeutic NPs that selectively deplete circulating soluble protein targets without engaging membrane target forms or other cell surface proteins. Fabrication of NaNots begins with silica NP (100 nanometer (nm) diameter) made according to previously published procedures followed by bi-orthogonal chemical functionalization of the NP surface.38,39 To functionalize NaNots for binding/depletion of human sPD-L1, the previously described anti-PD-L1 mAb clone H1A was used.40,41 A polyethylene glycol (PEG) polymer – affixed in a manner that excludes membrane protein engagement – and the anti-PD-L1 mAb were covalently conjugated to the NP to yield the finished NaNots (150 nm hydrodynamic diameter as measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS)). NaNots without anti-PD-L1 mAb capture agent but otherwise polymercoated in parallel (“Empty NaNots”) were prepared as control. NaNots were prepared and tested by nanoComposix (San Diego) under supervision by, and to specifications of, NaNotics.

Tumor Cell Lines

E0771hPD-L1 tumor cells were gifted by Dr. Kun Ling40 (Mayo Clinic) and were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Corning Catalog# 10–013-CV) + 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco Catalog# A5256701) + 1% penicillin/streptomycin (ThermoFisher Catalog# 15140–122) under antibiotic selection with puromycin (1µg/mL) (Invivogen Catalog# ant-pr-1). MC38hPD-L1 tumor cells were generated within the Dong lab as previously described42 and cultured in RPMI 1640 + 10% FBS + 1% penicillin/streptomycin under antibiotic selection with G418 (0.3 mg/mL) (Gibco Catalog# 10131027). B16F10hPD-L1 tumor cells were generated within the Dong Lab as previously described43 and were cultured in RPMI 1640 + 10% FBS + 1% penicillin/streptomycin under antibiotic selection with G418 (0.8 mg/mL).

Human Blood Samples

Blood samples were collected at baseline from patients with metastatic melanoma progressing on immunotherapy enrolled in the RECIPE-M1 trial (NCT04581382).30 Platelet-poor plasma was obtained from blood by dual centrifugation at 2,500 x g for 15 minutes at room temperature. Plasma was aliquoted in cryovials and stored at −80°C until use. This study was conducted in accordance with the criteria set by the Declaration of Helsinki. We conducted the study under a protocol approved by the Mayo Clinic institutional review board (IRB#19-008055).

In vitro sPD-L1 Depletion Efficiency of NaNots

To measure the capture performance of NaNots, sPD-L1 protein (Fc-dimeric, R&D Systems, Catalog# 156-B7 and monomeric, His-tagged, Abcam Catalog# ab167713) was spiked into phosphate buffered saline (PBS) + 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at varying concentrations and was incubated with NaNots at a concentration of either 6 µg/mL or 24 µg/mL for 5 or 30 minutes. Samples were then spun at 14,000 × g for 1 minute, and supernatants were tested by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) as described below. Capture performance is reported as a percentage of the initial sPDL1 concentration. To measure the depletion efficiency of NaNots against sPD-L1 in biological media in vitro, NaNots were incubated with the following samples: recombinant sPD-L1 spiked into sPD-L1-poor human plasma, plasma from melanoma patients, plasma from huPD-H1 mice and conditioned media from cultured E0771hPD-L1 cells. E0771hPD-L1 cells were cultured for 48 hours in medium supplemented with extracellular vesicle (EV)-depleted FBS. Conditioned media was collected and centrifuged at 2,500 × g for 15 minutes to remove aggregates and cell debris. Conditioned media was concentrated using centrifugal filters with a Molecular Weight Cut-Off (MWCO) of 10 kilodalton (Amicon Catalog# UFC9010) and stored at −80°C until use. Previously frozen conditioned media and plasma samples were thawed at 37°C for 2 minutes. NaNots were added to undiluted plasma and conditioned media at final concentrations of 6 µg/mL and 24 µg/mL and incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature. Samples were spun at 14,000 × g for 10 minutes to pellet the NaNots and the supernatant was collected for sPDL1 ELISA. The resulting sPD-L1 concentration was measured by ELISA as described below.

Soluble PD-L1 ELISA

For detection of sPD-L1 in biological samples, ELISA plates (White 96-well Immuno Plates, Thermo Scientific Catalog# 7905) were coated with monoclonal antibody against PD-L1 (H1A clone, Mayo Clinic) at 2 μg/mL in PBS (100 μL/well) for 2 hours at room temperature, followed by multiple washing steps with TBS + 0.05% Tween. Free binding sites were blocked with 200 μL of SuperBlock (ThermoFisher Catalog# 37515) for 1 hour at room temperature. Plasma and conditioned media samples were prepared and incubated with NaNots as previously described. After incubation, plates were washed multiple times. 50 μL of prepared plasma or conditioned media samples were mixed with PBS + 1% BSA and added to each well in duplicates. Recombinant human PD-L1 protein (Fc-tagged, R&D Systems, Catalog# 156-B7 or His-tagged, Abcam Catalog# ab167713) were used to make the standard curve ranging from 0.3125 to 10 ng/mL and were plated accordingly in duplicates. After overnight incubation at 4°C, plates were washed multiple times. Biotinylated monoclonal PD-L1 antibody (B11 clone, Mayo Clinic) was diluted to a concentration of 4 μg/mL in PBS + 0.1% BSA and was added to each well for an incubation of 1 hour at room temperature. After incubation, plates were washed multiple times. Streptavidin conjugated to horseradish-peroxidase (SA-HRP) (BD Biosciences Catalog# 554066) was diluted at 1:10,000 in PBS. 100 μL of diluted SA-HRP was added to each sample and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. Plates were developed with SuperSignal ELISA Pico Substrate (ThermoFisher Catalog# 37069), per manufacturer’s protocol. Plates were measured by luminescence at 425 nm with a plate reader (BioTek Synergy HTX Multi-Mode Microplate Reader).

In vitro NaNot Shielding Assay Using Beads with Surface-Bound PD-L1

To demonstrate shielding of antibody capture agents by surface-coupled PEG, simulated PD-L1 presenting cell structures were produced by coupling PD-L1 protein to 3 µm diameter polystyrene beads using SoluLINK™ hydrazone chemistry (Vector Labs). PD-L1 protein (Abcam Catalog# ab167713) was functionalized with a heterobifunctional Sulpho-4FB linker (Vector Labs Catalog# S-1008-105) in 100 mM potassium phosphate (KPhos) buffer pH 8, per manufacturers’ protocol and then purified (Zeba filters, 7000 MWCO) into 100 mM KPhos buffer pH 6. Polyscience (PS) beads (Polyscience aminefunctionalized polybead, Catalog# 17145–5) were coupled with 0.9 µg/mL Sulfo S-HyNic (Vector Labs Catalog# S-1011-010) in 100 mM KPhos buffer pH 8 for 2 hours at room temperature and resuspended in 20 mM KPhos buffer pH 6. S-HyNic coupled beads and functionalized PD-L1 were conjugated overnight at room temperature, washed and resuspended in 10 mM KPhos buffer pH 7.2 ready for use as simulated cell structures in shielding experiments. NaNots were prepared with anti-PD-L1 antibody capture agents. Unshielded particles were prepared identically to NaNots with the exception that shielding PEG was not coupled. NaNots, unshielded particles (unshielded NaNots) and empty NaNots were each incubated at 0.1 mg/mL with simulated cell structures at 2.0 mg/mL in KPhos buffer pH 7.2 for 30 minutes at room temperature with intermittent vortexing, washed in PBS pH 7.4 + 0.2% Tween20 and then twice in DI water. Samples were spun at 2,000 × g and resuspended in 50 µL DI water. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) grids were prepared using 3 µL of the sample and 3 µL EtOH and TEM images were made. Experiments were repeated as above but with incubation in matrix comprising 1% or 50% mouse serum (Abcam Catalog# ab7486) in 10 mM KPhos buffer pH 7.2. TEM images were assessed by eye for degree of association with the simulated test structures. For quantitative assessment, NaNots, unshielded particles or empty NaNots associated with the surface of the simulated cell structures were counted manually after incubation with Empty NaNots (n=2), unshielded NaNots (n=6) or NaNots (n=6).

Dynamic Light Scattering

To assess the size distribution of NaNots, DLS was used to determine their hydrodynamic diameter (DLS, Malvern ZetaSizer Nano ZS, ZEN3600, Malvern, UK). The NaNots were diluted to a target concentration of 50 µg/mL using PBS. The colloidal stability of the NaNots in buffer over time was determined by DLS. The stock solution of the NaNots was diluted to a target concentration of 5 mg/mL in PBS and held for 0-, 20-, 40- or 60-minute incubation periods. After the incubation time, 4 µL of the solution was diluted with 196 µL of deionized water and placed into a low volume cuvette for immediate characterization. To assess long-term storage stability, NaNots were reassessed again by DLS using the same methods as previously described at 4 and 9 months.

Storage Stability of NaNots by Functional Test (Capture of sPD-L1 in vitro)

sPD-L1 capture functional testing was conducted as described above with in vitro incubations for 5- and 30-minutes followed by separation of NaNots from solution via centrifugation and quantitation of remaining sPD-L1 in solution via ELISA. This assay was performed similarly to that described above using PBS + 1% BSA as the medium for capture and sPD-L1 as target. The ELISA assay used for quantitation was a similar sandwich style assay but sourced commercially (DuoSet Kit for human PD-L1, R&D Systems Catalog# DY156) and performed at nanoComposix.

Murine Tumor Models

Animals used throughout all experiments followed the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) to ensure the welfare of laboratory animals. Humanized PD-1/PD-L1 C57BL/6 transgenic mice (huPD-H1 mice) were generated as previously described42 and housed in animal facilities located at Mayo Clinic, Rochester, Minnesota, USA, under Dr Haidong Dong’s protocol (A00002530-17R23), which was approved by Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were housed under pathogen-free and temperature-controlled conditions. Mice were fed PicoLab® Rodent Diet 20 (LabDiet Catalog# 5053). Six-to-eight-week-old male and female mice weighing approximately 27–32 grams were used in all experimental studies. Based on preliminary studies, we calculated (using the stat.ubc.ca online tool) that given an average tumor volume size of 747 mm3 in controls and 283 mm3 in treated mice of E0771hPD-L1 tumors, with a sigma value of 343, we would require a sample size of 7 mice per group to achieve 95% confidence with 80% power. The in vivo experiments outlined in the main figures detail two separate experiments with the data compiled into one graph with total n = 16 for PBS control and n = 19 for NaNot treatment. Experiments were performed twice to confirm the therapeutic effects of treatment in vivo and account for variation in quality of cultured cells and tumor cell injections. Further, mice were added to each initial group of n = 7 based on availability of huPD-H1 mice from the Mayo breeding program that were age-appropriate resulting in higher final number per group.

Because E0771hPD-L1 tumor cells were derived from breast cancer, male mice were excluded, and only female mice were used in studies requiring this cell line. For studies using MC38hPD-L1 and B16F10hPD-L1 tumor cells, approximately equal numbers of male and female mice were used. For each study, tumor cells were trypsinized and washed with PBS twice. The following concentrations of tumor cells were prepared in PBS for subsequent tumor inoculation: MC38hPD-L1 at 0.75×106 per mL, E0771hPD-L1 at 1.0×106 per mL, and B16F10hPD-L1 at 0.5×106 per mL. 1 day before injection, mice were shaved, followed by subcutaneous tumor cell injection the following day on the right hind flank of huPD-H1 mice. Each mouse received 100 µL per tumor injection. Once palpable, around Day 7–13, tumors were measured, and mice were randomized into groups using minimization methods based on tumor size such that the average tumor volume was similar across groups. Tumor measurements of each individual mouse were taken three times per week by calipers. Tumor volume was calculated based on the following formula: Tumor volume = 0.5 x Length x Width2. In order to minimize potential confounders in all experiments, mice of different treatment groups were co-housed (mixed among the cages) and the order in which the cages received treatments varied each week. Laboratory members performing caliper measurements of mouse tumors were blinded to treatment group. Only laboratory members who did not perform caliper measurements were permitted to know assigned treatment conditions and dose mice accordingly. Mice were euthanized if they reached endpoint (tumor volume ≥ 1500 mm3) or if tumors became ulcerated. Euthanasia was performed by inhalation of CO2 for 5 minutes, followed by secondary measure of cervical dislocation. Probability of survival was reported using Kaplan–Meier survival curves.

The dose of NaNots used for this study was based on prior work performed with NaNots conjugated with other capture agents.44 These studies have shown that NaNots exhibit a circulating half-life of ~6 hours in mice. Estimating ~1.2 mL total plasma volume for a 30 gram mouse, the 200µg/mouse dose should provide for a NaNot plasma concentration in vivo of ~170µg/mL at dosing and after 5 half-lives (~1.25 days of 2-day dosing interval with expected plasma concentration of 10µg/mL) remain active in depleting sPD-L1 (>80% depletion based on in vitro depletion at 6 and 24 µg/mL; Figure S1D and S1E). It should be noted that H1A antibody conjugation to NaNots is approximately 10% mass/mass and thus a 200µg/mouse dose of NaNots contains approximately 20 µg of H1A or 10% of the amount of H1A (200µg/mouse) dosed in tumor therapy experiments with free H1A antibody.45 Mice received 200μg/mouse of NaNots diluted with PBS into a final total volume of 100μL per dose per animal. Empty NaNots or PBS were included as negative controls. Mice were dosed intravenously (i.v.) via tail vein injection every two days for a total of 5 doses. Prior to tail vein injection and blood collection, mice were heated under a lamp for 3–4 minutes to ensure proper vasodilation of the tail vein.

Blood collection was completed by lateral tail artery nick sampling. Mice were not fasted prior to blood collection. Due to the amount of blood loss caused by this method, each bleed completed was a terminal bleed and resulted in immediate euthanasia as described previously. As such, for detection of sPD-L1 in mouse plasma, one cohort of mice were designated as baseline (i.e received no NaNot injection) and another cohort of mice served as the NaNot-treated group. The mean value of the baseline group was used to determine the approximate efficiency of sPD-L1 depletion. Blood collection was completed 1 hour after the initial tail vein dose and in 200μL volumes. Blood was diluted in 50μL of EDTA and centrifuged at 2,500 × g for 15 minutes at room temperature. Plasma was collected and stored at −80°C until use.

Mouse Tumor Infiltrating Lymphocyte (TIL) Isolation

At the designated study day, mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation, followed by cervical dislocation. Tumors were removed from mice and cut into smaller pieces for tumor digestion. Cut tumor pieces were then transferred into gentleMACS™ C Tubes (Miltenyi Catalog# 130–093-237) containing an enzyme mix, using a mouse tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Catalog# 130–096-730). Samples were then loaded onto gentleMACS Octo Dissociator with Heaters (Miltenyi Biotec), per manufacturer’s protocol. Once samples were processed into single cell suspensions, TILs were isolated using EasySep Mouse TIL (CD45) Positive Selection kits (StemCell Catalog# 100–0350), per manufacturer’s protocol. After TIL isolation, samples were resuspended in PBS and counted for subsequent flow analysis.

Mouse Splenocyte Isolation

At the designated study day, mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation, followed by cervical dislocation. Whole spleens were excised from mice and were mechanically digested by grinding spleen tissue between two frosted microscope slides in 5 mL of RPMI media, followed by filtration with a 70 μm strainer. Samples were then washed with PBS. Red blood cells were lysed using ACK lysis buffer, followed by multiple wash steps with PBS. Splenocytes were then resuspended in PBS and counted for subsequent flow analysis.

Flow Cytometry

Samples were plated at 1×106 cells in 100 µL of PBS in a 96-well U-bottom plate, followed by staining with fixable Live/Dead Zombie UV dye (Biolegend Catalog# 423107) at a 1:1000 dilution for 30 minutes at 4°C in the dark. Samples were washed twice with FACS Buffer (PBS + 2% FBS + 2mM EDTA). Samples were then incubated with TruStain FcX™ (anti-mouse CD16/32) (Biolegend Catalog# 101319) to block any unbound Fc receptors, for 5 minutes at 4°C in the dark, per manufacturer’s protocol. Cell surface marker flow antibodies were directly added to samples and incubated for 30 minutes at 4°C in the dark, followed by two washes with FACS buffer. Samples were then fixed using eBioscience Intracellular Fixation and Permeabilization buffer set (Invitrogen Catalog# 88–8824-00) for 30 minutes at room temperature in the dark, per manufacturer’s protocol. Intracellular flow markers were then added to the samples and incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature in the dark. Samples were then washed twice with FACS buffer and resuspended in a final volume of 200 µL of FACS buffer. Fluorescence minus one (FMO’s) were used as negative controls for gating purposes. Single color stained UltraComp eBeads™ Compensation Beads (Invitrogen Catalog# 01–2222-42) were used for unmixing. Samples were read on Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer running SpectroFlow software. Subsequent flow analysis and gating were completed using FlowJo™ v10.9 Software (BD Life Sciences).

Statistical Analysis

Paired t-test (parametric) and unpaired Welch’s t-test (parametric) were employed to compare two groups. To perform a parametric test, data sets were evaluated for a Gaussian distribution. If the data were not normally distributed, a nonparametric test was used (Mann–Whitney for unpaired and Wilcoxon for paired). For average tumor growth curve comparisons, a mixed-effects model analysis was performed. For probabilities of survival in the mouse experiments, a Log rank test was performed to compare two groups. Results were considered significant for p-values < 0.05. All data were analyzed and plotted in GraphPad Prism version 9.3.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

NaNots Deplete sPD-L1 from Various Biological Fluids

NaNots used here were developed to selectively deplete sPD-L1 that may contribute to T cell inhibition locally and systemically by binding its cognate PD-1 receptor. To test the capture efficiency of NaNots, we first evaluated the secretion of sPD-L1 across three murine cell lines, B16-F10hPD-L1 (melanoma), MC38hPD-L1 (colorectal carcinoma) and E0771hPD-L1 (triple-negative breast cancer/carcinoma). Tumor cell lines were genetically silenced for murine PD-L1 and replaced with the human isoform of PD-L1 (hPD-L1) to allow testing of human PD-L1 targeting therapies,42 including NaNots. All three cell lines had confirmed expression of hPD-L1 (Figure 1A) with E0771hPD-L1 having the highest expression and B16-F10hPD-L1 having the lowest (Figure 1B). We standardized a sensitive sandwich ELISA assay to measure sPD-L1 concentration in biological samples (Figure S1A). Using standards of recombinant Fc-tagged PD-L1 protein spiked into PBS and sPD-L1-poor plasma, the lower limits of detection (LoD) for sPD-L1 were 9.76 pg/mL and 19.5 pg/mL respectively (Figure S1B). The lower limits of quantification (LoQ) were 19.5 pg/mL and 78.1 pg/mL for sPD-L1 in PBS and plasma, respectively. We first analyzed the secretion of sPD-L1 by the previously mentioned tumor cell lines expressing hPD-L1. While conditioned media of B16-F10hPD-L1 and MC38hPD-L1 showed no detectable level of sPD-L1, E0771hPD-L1 was positive for sPD-L1, identifying this cell line as a suitable tumor model to test the efficacy of NaNots (Figure 1C). Soluble PD-L1 can be functional in its monomer and dimer forms.10,12,14,46 We evaluated the capture efficiency of NaNots on Fc-tagged recombinant sPD-L1 (recPD-L1-Fc) which forms dimers, and monomeric His-tagged sPD-L1 (recPD-L1-His). Using the established ELISA assay, we confirmed similar detection of both forms of sPD-L1 (Figure S1C). NaNots and Empty NaNots were then diluted at 6 µg/mL or 24 µg/mL and incubated for 5 or 30 minutes with 5 ng/mL of His-tagged monomeric or Fc-tagged dimeric sPD-L1 protein in PBS containing 1% BSA. 6 µg/mL concentration was used in earlier testing of NaNots, and 24 µg/mL concentration was used to better recapitulate the dosing used in the following in vivo studies. In both forms of sPD-L1, NaNots at 24 µg/mL captured more than 80% of sPD-L1 at both incubation times, with the 30-minute incubation resulting in more than 95% capture of sPD-L1 (Figure S1D and S1E). NaNots were tested at a lower concentration of 6 µg/mL and still demonstrated high capture efficiency of both forms of sPD-L1 after 30-minute incubations with more than 70% capture. Empty NaNots captured neither form of sPD-L1 at all conditions tested. Finally, we tested whether NaNots at 24 µg/mL with an incubation period of 30 minutes could capture sPD-L1 at varying concentrations. NaNots successfully captured more than 95% of either form of sPD-L1 at 1.25, 2.5, and 5.0 ng/mL concentrations (Figure S1F and S1G). To test the depletion efficiency of NaNots for tumor cell derived sPD-L1, NaNots were incubated with E0771hPD-L1 cell culture supernatant for 30 minutes and processed for assessment by the sPD-L1 ELISA. Approximately 95% of tumor cell derived sPD-L1 was depleted by NaNots (Figure 1D and E). Similar results were obtained with plasma from metastatic melanoma patients where sPD-L1 was depleted to undetectable levels (Figure 1F and G). Finally, we tested the in vivo depletion efficiency of NaNots. Following a single i.v. injection of 200 µg of NaNots in E0771hPD-L1 tumor-bearing mice, sPD-L1 levels were reduced by 61% at 1-hour post-treatment, compared to mice that did not receive NaNot treatment (Figure 1H). Usually, NaNots are capable of >90% depletion of targets from circulation for a period of several hours post-injection. Therefore, this lower capture percentage may result from an exosomal component of PD-L1 in the mouse model,47 detectable in ELISA but not captured by present generation NaNots. Overall, these results confirm and validate the ability of NaNots to deplete soluble hPDL1 across multiple protein forms.

NaNots are Prevented from Binding Surface Bound PD-L1

NaNots are configured to specifically bind to and deplete soluble pathogenic forms of target from blood and other fluids without binding and interfering with healthy functions of cell-surface membrane forms of the same target. To accomplish this, polyethylene glycol (PEG) polymer shielding was designed to prevent the capture agent conjugated to the NaNot (eg, an anti-target mAb) from accessing and binding to such cell surface forms while still permitting diffusion of soluble target through the polymer layer with subsequent binding and ultimate depletion of the target. To confirm successful shielding in vitro, a simulated cell structure bearing surface-bound PD-L1 was generated by covalently attaching sPD-L1 to a 3-µm diameter polystyrene bead. These simulated cell structures were then tested for binding to NaNots in comparison with unshielded NP conjugated with anti-PD-L1 mAbs and with Empty NaNots as a background control. After incubation and washing/removal of unbound species, simulated cell samples were prepared for transmission electron microscopy to visualize and quantitate the degree of NaNot and unshielded NP species binding to the simulated cells. Empty NaNots lacking the anti-PDL1 mAb were incapable of any significant degree of binding to the surface-bound sPD-L1 whereas unshielded NP bearing anti-PD-L1 mAb exhibited a high degree of binding (Figure S2A and S2B). NaNots bearing both the anti-PD-L1 mAb and the PEG shield were inhibited from binding surface-bound PD-L1 essentially to background levels (Figure S2C and S2D). Incubation in buffer alone, in 1% serum and in 50% serum spiked into buffer all gave similar results. These results confirm that PEG shielding prevents NaNot binding to surface bound PD-L1. It is acknowledged that the cell structure bead proxy is a well-controlled system and a limitation of this study. However, due to the complexity of cell membrane dynamics, including membrane-bound PD-L1 cis-interactions and varying expression of PD-L1 across cell types that may introduce confounding factors to the experimental design, stable attachment of sPD-L1 on the bead surface was an alternative solution that provided clear evidence of NaNot binding to only the soluble form of PD-L1 and not to the surface form of PD-L1.

NaNots Maintain Physical and Functional Integrity After Long-Term Storage

To ensure the storage stability of NaNots over time, two lots of NaNots (referred to as TDT0631 and TDT0688) were each evaluated at time of production (baseline) and again following storage at 2–8°C in potassium phosphate buffer for either 4 months (TDT0688) or 9 months (TDT0631). NaNot batches were evaluated for physical integrity by DLS. At baseline, DLS analysis revealed similar hydrodynamic diameters (Z-average) for both lots (146 nm and 148 nm, respectively) (Table 1). Storage of NaNots at 4°C did not impart meaningful change in hydrodynamic diameter after either 4 months (155 nm) or 9 months (156 nm) (<10% variation, within expected variability of the method), nor in colloidal stability (no meaningful change or trend in size over 60-minute time course). The samples exhibited excellent quality with polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.02–0.11, indicating monodisperse populations. Representative correlation curves of the raw DLS spectral data of both lots at baseline and end of storage testing (with y-intercept > 0.7 and smooth, steep curve shape without steps, falling to flat clean baselines at x-axis) indicate high‑quality samples that are monodisperse without interfering substances, thus confirming that the reported Z-average values for hydrodynamic diameter of the NaNot preparations are representative of the intact NaNot nanoparticles (Figure S3A and S3D). Physical characterization by DLS confirmed that the NaNot lots were stable during refrigerated storage of 4 to 9 months.

NaNots Reduce Tumor Growth in a Murine Model of Triple-Negative Breast Cancer

Previous studies have reported the inhibitory activity of sPD-L1, notably by blocking T-cell proliferation and cytotoxic function leading to tumor progression.10,11 In order to test the therapeutic efficacy of NaNots in inhibiting tumor growth, E0771hPD-L1 cells were inoculated into the flank of huPD-H1 mice (Figure 2A). On Day 9, huPD-H1 mice were randomized into treatment groups such that tumor volumes at baseline were similar between control and NaNots groups (Figure 2B). Mice were then dosed with 200 µg of NaNots diluted in 100 µL of PBS or 100 µL PBS control i.v. via tail vein injection every two days for a total of 5 doses. Compared to control, treatment with NaNots resulted in significant tumor growth delay (p<0.001) (Figure 2C and D). Forty-eight hours after the last injection (Day 19), tumors in the NaNots group were significantly smaller than those in the control group (mean: 313.4 vs 808.2 mm3 p<0.001) (Figure 2E). We calculated progression-free survival as the length of time required for a tumor to reach 1,000 mm3 from the volume on Day 9. Progression-free survival was prolonged with NaNot treatment (Log rank test p=0.017) (Figure 2F). A subset of mice was euthanized on Day 19 after receiving all 5 treatments and tumor tissues were stained for CD8. We observed an increase in CD8+ T cell focal infiltrates in NaNot-treated tumors, suggesting the presence of primed antitumor immune responses (Figure 2G).

Due to their physical and biochemical characteristics, engineered NPs, including NaNots, can become coated by a corona of various host-derived molecules such as proteins and nucleic acids that can trigger an immune response.48 Therefore, we tested the activity of Empty NaNots and their potential for promoting an antitumor immune response independently of sPD-L1 depletion. E0771hPD-L1 tumor growth curves were identical in mice treated with PBS and Empty NaNots, thereby confirming that NaNots lacking PD-L1 antibody do not induce any immunological responses against the tumor (Figure S4A and S4B). This suggests that the NaNot mechanism relies on specific sPD-L1 depletion in driving antitumor activity. To further justify that the antitumor effect observed in NaNot-treated mice is due to the depletion of sPD-L1, we treated mice inoculated with B16-F10hPD-L1 and MC38hPD-L1 tumors, which we previously demonstrated do not secrete sPD-L1. NaNots did not show evidence of antitumor activity in either of these tumor models, further confirming that NaNots likely mediate their therapeutic activity specifically by depletion of sPD-L1 (Figure S4C and S4F). Together, these results demonstrate NaNots’ capacity to induce an antitumor immune response in mice bearing tumors secreting measurable levels of sPD-L1.

NaNots Promote the Accumulation of Effector CD8 T Cells and Reduce Levels of Regulatory T Cells in Tumors

To understand the immune-related changes supporting the antitumor response elicited by NaNots, we analyzed the composition of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and splenocytes after 5 doses of treatment in E0771hPD-L1 tumor bearing mice (Figure 3A). Because we previously observed CD8+ staining accumulation by IHC, we prioritized assessing relevant T cell subsets in our TIL analysis by flow cytometry (Figure S5A and S5B).

While NaNots did not result in significant changes to the frequencies of total CD8+ T cells (CD3+ CD8+) (Figure 3B) and PD1+ CD8+ T cells (CD3+ CD8+ PD1+) (Figure 3C), higher frequencies of CD8+ T cells with an effector phenotype were noted (CD3+ CD8+ PD1+ Granzyme B+ TCF1−)49 (Figure 3D). Additionally, NaNots significantly reduced frequencies of regulatory T cells (Tregs) (CD3+ CD4+ FoxP3+ CD25+), compared to PBS control (Figure 3E) (Figure S6A and S6B). These Tregs can express PD-1, which can promote their immunosuppressive activity50,51 upon binding to membrane-bound PD-L1 or sPD-L1. Concurrently, NaNot treatment led to elevated levels of FoxP3− CD4+ helper T cell populations (CD3+ CD4+ FoxP3−) that were trending towards significance (Figure 3F), further supporting the idea that NaNots can reduce immunosuppressive cell populations and instead promote immune activating ones. It has been previously reported that high ratios of CD8+ T cell to Treg cells are an indicator of good prognosis in cancer patients.52 In our mouse model, we observed significantly increased ratios of CD8+ T cell/Treg cells in the NaNot treated group compared to PBS controls (Figure 3G), which further indicates a positive adaptive immune response resulting from sPD-L1 depletion.

Due to the i.v. dosing route of NaNots, we next characterized the spleen to evaluate the activity of NaNots on systemic immunity. Similar to our TIL analysis data, spleens of NaNot-treated mice had reduced populations of Tregs compared to PBS treated mice (Figure 3H) (Figure S7A and S7B). Additionally, there was a significant increase in total CD8+ T cell (CD3+ CD8+) populations in spleens of NaNot-treated mice (Figure 3I) (Figure S8A and S8B). Interestingly, NaNot treatment also resulted in significantly increased populations of proliferating, activated natural killer (NK) cells (CD3− CD49b+ NKG2D+ Ki67+)53 (Figure 3J) (Figure S9A and S9B), another important antitumor effector immune cell type that expresses PD-1 and can be suppressed by sPD-L1. Overall, our TIL and splenocyte data demonstrate the ability of NaNots to reduce immunosuppressive Treg populations intratumorally and systemically, while also promoting antitumor immune cell populations.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that NaNots, adsorptive NPs containing an antibody against PD-L1, shielded against membrane protein interactions, effectively captured sPDL1 from circulation, and significantly reduced its levels in biological fluids. NaNot treatment led to marked tumor growth delay in a sPD-L1-secreting murine tumor model, demonstrating its ability to mitigate sPD-L1-mediated immunosuppression. Furthermore, this treatment improved intratumoral and systemic immune profiles, evidenced by increased effector CD8+ and CD4+ helper T cell populations and decreased Treg levels within the tumors and spleens. These results provide a first-ever proof-of-concept that sole depletion of a soluble form of an immune checkpoint protein, such as sPD-L1, can result in reduction of tumor-mediated immune suppression and can mediate antitumor immune responses that ultimately lead to delay in tumor growth.

We recently conducted a clinical study of advanced melanoma patients using a therapeutic sequential regimen of stereotactic body radiotherapy, therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) and immune checkpoint blockade which resulted in strikingly high clinical response (61% objective response).30 From the combined therapeutic study design, it is not clear to what degree each component therapy contributed to the overall outcomes. However, these patients had received and failed prior checkpoint blockade, and it is known from prior similar trials of radiotherapy regimens in combination with checkpoint blockade that such a dual treatment does not evoke such a magnitude of treatment response.54 TPE had been shown previously to reduce sPD-L1 levels as well as other soluble factors in patient plasma. In this combination regimen, it was shown that those patients with greater sPD-L1 depletion had greater overall survival compared with those patients experiencing higher residual sPD-L1 levels. Likewise, patients with higher levels of tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells, as well as patients with lower levels of Treg cells, experienced statistically longer overall survival. When patients were divided into subsets by high/low levels of both sPD-L1 and tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells, the patient subset with low sPD-L1 and high tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells had the greatest and significantly longer survival than the other subsets. These efficacy-associated biomarker results in advanced melanoma patients are in alignment with those observed in the current preclinical study wherein sPD-L1 depletion alone drove antitumor efficacy in mice along with increases in tumor-infiltrated CD8+ effector T cells and reduction of Tregs, together suggesting promise of future clinical benefit of sPD-L1 depletion by NaNots in cancer patients.

NaNots present distinct advantages over other soluble factor-depleting technologies such as TPE and mAb drugs recognizing soluble factors. TPE, while effective in removing pathogenic factors, is invasive and resource intensive. Another major challenge is the simultaneous removal of both harmful and protective substances, which may lead to unwanted outcomes. mAbs have the disadvantage of induced accumulation of soluble target leading to therapeutic escape as described earlier. In contrast, NaNots offer specificity for the soluble form of a target protein, rapid and robust target depletion and targeted elimination via hepatic clearance, enhancing therapeutic efficacy with the potential for reduced systemic side effects.

Despite the promising findings observed with NaNots, we acknowledge several limitations in this study. While NaNots can induce immunological responses through the depletion of sPD-L1 in vivo, further testing is needed to confirm that other immunosuppressive soluble proteins besides sPD-L1, such as PD-1 and CTLA-4, are not also reduced in concentration as these factors could also have an effect on immune responses.4 Mice treated with empty NaNots did not show differences in tumor growth, suggesting no off-target effects on antitumor immunological responses and further justifying the specificity of NaNots for sPD-L1. We also acknowledge an absence of direct comparison with other sPD-L1 depleting methods such as TPE. TPE has shown to be effective in removing sPD-L1 and other soluble factors from patient blood. However, mouse models of TPE are lacking. Another limitation is the use of only one main sPD-L1 secreting tumor model. This is due to resource availability as the cell lines used with our huPD-H1 mouse model require additional genetic modification by replacing the murine PD-L1 protein with the human isoform to allow direct testing of the humanized PD-L1 targeting antibody that is paired with NaNots. We generated 3 cell lines to express human PD-L1 with only E0771hPD-L1 secreting sPD-L1. Future experiments will explore additional sPD-L1-secreting tumor models that would further validate the utility of NaNots. The degree and durability of therapeutic efficacy mediated by NaNot treatment may be underrepresented in this study due to practical constraints with the mouse model in terms of repeated i.v. dosing of mice, limited here to five injections spaced at 2-day intervals, resulting in limited dosing duration. This dosing does not span the full treatment window and potentially restricts longitudinal assessments of response sustainability and the possible emergence of resistance mechanisms to NaNots, though it is expected that NaNot shielding of the antibody capture agents will mitigate B-cell driven development of anti-drug antibodies and associated loss of response.55 Additionally, we acknowledge the ex vivo testing of sPD-L1 depletion in huPD-H1 mouse plasma covers only a short postinjection window. Future studies will include full pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic analyses to assess the durability of the sPD-L1 depletion mediated by NaNots. Clinical response to ICIs is uncommonly complete, and partial response may indicate sPD-L1 independent mechanisms of resistance. The partial response to NaNots observed in this study may be attributable to the presence of extracellular vesicle (EV)-bound PD-L1, another immunosuppressive factor linked to tumor progression and resistance against PD-1/PD-L1 blockade.47,56–58 The capability of modified NaNots to capture or neutralize EV-bound PD-L1 remains unexplored. However, to address this limitation, we note that the size of the NaNot is equivalent to the size of an EV. Therefore, it is unlikely that the EV can penetrate the PEG shield and engage the capture agent. Overall, addressing these limitations in future studies will be crucial for translating NaNots into a viable clinical intervention wherein dosing interval and treatment duration will not be restricted as is the case for mice in this study.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that NaNots effectively capture sPD-L1, alleviating tumor-mediated immunosuppression and significantly delaying tumor growth in a preclinical model. These results provide first-time proof-of-concept evidence that pharmaceutically mediated depletion of only the soluble form of an immune checkpoint protein can result in substantial antitumor immunity and efficacy. These results support further development of NaNots to selectively deplete circulating immunosuppressive factors, opening new therapeutic opportunities for the treatment of human cancers.
