1.
Introduction

In
[
5
]
Douglas established the result (
0.1
) that was used as the main technical tool to solve the minimum surface problem of Plateau. He proved the identical relation between the two quantities through a third equivalent quantity in terms of the Fourier coefficients of the boundary function
u
, namely
4\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k|c_{k}|^{2},
where
c_{k}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}u(e^{it})e^{-ikt}dt.
This third quantity is, in fact, closely related to the norm of the Dirichlet space function induced by the boundary function
u.

In
[
1
]
, Ahlfors presented an alternative proof based on differentiation of the Schwarz kernel. Although Ahlfors’ proof is not be as direct as Douglas’s, it provides a fourth equivalent quantity, namely,

(1.2)

\displaystyle A(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{|z|=1}\overline{f}f^{\prime}\frac{dz}{i},

where
f
is the holomorphic function obtained through adding the normalized conjugate harmonic part
V
:

f(z)=(U+iV)(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{e^{i\phi}+z}{e^{i\phi}-z}u(\phi)d\phi,\quad V(0)=0.

There then followed, besides the application to minimum surface problem, other relevant studies as well, including capacity of sets in planar domains (
[
1
]
). Enhancing the quantitative equivalence relations in domains other than the unit disc, there recently arouse studies of Wei and Zinsmeister (
[
16
]
) on domains defined through chord-arc curves and on the related Dirichlet functional spaces. As a very interesting result they show that those quantitative equivalence results stand as a characteristic property of rectifiable Jordan chord-arc curves. The wide and deep connections and applications of the equivalence relations to minimum surfaces, operator and function space theories, as well as in Teichmüller theory (see e.g.
[
7
]
) naturally motivate great interest in the related research. This includes the question that in higher dimensions in what extent similar quantities can be defined and whether they have the sort of quantitative equivalence. To our knowledge implementation of the Douglas’ program has not been carried out to contexts other than one complex variable. The present paper, as a start, establishes fundamental results in the several real variable cases. We show that in the unit ball context all the four quantities are well defined and equivalent. Furthermore, we show that there exist counterpart results in the quaternionic and Clifford algebra contexts as well. With the Clifford algebra case there exists an exception (see §4).

In the sequel the writing of the paper is divided into three sections. §2 deals with the forms of
A(u)
in the harmonic function setting. Pursuing Douglas’s idea, the section proceeds fundamental computations based on the spherical Fourier-Laplace expansions. §3 gives the forms of
A(u)
in the quaternionic setting. The quaternionic setting is different from
{\mathbb{R}}^{4},
for itself possesses a regular function theory, or a Cauchy-Riemann structure, as in the one-complex variable case. In §4 the forms of
A(u)
in the Clifford algebra setting is presented. The Clifford setting is different from the harmonic, and different from the quaternionic one either, for the domain of the functions in the case is the linear Euclidean space, not an algebra, and the range of the functions, although being an algebra, is not divisible, and nor commutative either. Clifford algebra provides Euclidean space with a Cauchy-Riemann structure as well. The Clifford Cauchy-Riemann equations, however, are not as convenient as for the quaternionic case. For our purpose some critical computations concerning monogenic functions and the Clifford Schwarz kernel are spelt out that would be new in the foundation of Clifford analysis.

2.
The forms of
A(u)
in the harmonic analysis setting

Definition 2.1
.

For real-valued function
u\in L^{2}(S^{n-1}),n\geq 2,
define the functional

(2.3)

\displaystyle A(u):=\frac{1}{\omega_{n-1}}\int_{S^{n-1}}\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{|u(\underline{\eta_{1}})-u(\underline{\eta_{2}})|^{2}}{|\underline{\eta_{1}}-\underline{\eta_{2}}|^{n}}dS_{\underline{\eta_{1}}}dS_{\underline{\eta_{2}}}.

Clearly, the above is an improper double integral that has a determinate positive value, finite or
+\infty
.

Let
U
be the harmonic extension of
u
into the unit ball
B_{n}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. It is well known knowledge that the function values of
u
are the non-tangential boundary limits of
U
on the sphere
S^{n-1}
a.e. As main result of this section, we have

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Then

\displaystyle A(u)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n-1}}\int_{S^{n-1}}\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{|u(\underline{\eta_{1}})-u(\underline{\eta_{2}})|^{2}}{|\underline{\eta_{1}}-\underline{\eta_{2}}|^{n}}dS_{\underline{\eta_{1}}}dS_{\underline{\eta_{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{n}}|\bigtriangledown U|^{2}dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}k\sum_{j=1}^{a^{n}_{k}}|b_{j}|^{2},

where
b_{j}
’s are the coefficients of the Fourier-Laplace series expansion of
u
which are given by formula (
2.9
).

When
n=2
, denote by
\mathcal{D}=B_{2}
the unit disk, and
T=S^{1}
the unit circle. Then there exists the classical result as cited in
[
16
]
:

Corollary 2.3
.

(2.4)

\displaystyle A(u)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{T}\int_{T}\frac{|u(z_{1})-u(z_{2})|^{2}}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}}|dz_{1}||dz_{2}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{D}}|\bigtriangledown U|^{2}dxdy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}k(a_{k}^{2}+b_{k}^{2}),

where
a_{k}
’s and
b_{k}
’s are the coefficients of the Fourier series expansion of
u
given by formula (
2.10
).

Based on the Divergence Theorem, we have

(2.5)

\displaystyle{}\int_{B_{n}}|\bigtriangledown U|^{2}dV=\int_{S^{n-1}}U\frac{\partial U}{\partial{\bf n}}dS.

Next, we perform homogeneous spherical harmonic decomposition on the sphere.

It is well known that
L^{2}(S^{n-1})=\oplus\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{H}}^{n}_{k}
,
where
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}_{k}
is the
a^{n}_{k}
-dimensional linear space of all the
k
-spherical harmonics of
n
variables,

\displaystyle a^{n}_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\vskip 5.69046pt1,&&\mbox{if }k=0,\\
(n+2k-2)\frac{(n+k-2)!}{k!(n-2)!},&&\mbox{if }k\in{\mathcal{N}}^{+}=\{1,2,\cdots\}.\end{array}\right.

If
u\in L^{2}(S^{n-1})
, then in the
L^{2}
sense,
f
has the Laplace-Fourier series expansion:

u(\underline{\xi})=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}Y_{k}(u)(\underline{\xi}),

where

Y_{k}(u)(\underline{\xi})=c_{n,k}\int_{S^{n-1}}u(\underline{\eta})P_{k}^{n}(\underline{\xi}\cdot\underline{\eta})dS_{\underline{\eta}}

stands for the projection of
f
onto
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}_{k},

where
P_{k}^{n}
are the Gegenbauer polynomials and

(2.7)

\displaystyle c_{n,k}=\frac{1}{\omega_{n-1}}\frac{(n+2k-2)\Gamma(n+k-1)}{(n+k-2)k!\Gamma(n-1)},

\omega_{n-1}=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}
is the surface area of
S^{n-1}
.

For
0<r<1
, there hold the relations

(2.8)

\displaystyle U(r,\underline{\xi})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}r^{k}Y_{k}(u)(\underline{\xi})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}r^{k}c_{n,k}\int_{S^{n-1}}u(\underline{\eta})P_{k}^{n}(\underline{\xi}\cdot\underline{\eta})dS_{\underline{\eta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}u(\underline{\eta})\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}r^{k}c_{n,k}P_{k}^{n}(\underline{\xi}\cdot\underline{\eta})dS_{\underline{\eta}}.

Since we also have the Poisson kernel integral representation

\displaystyle U(r,\underline{\xi})=\frac{1}{\omega_{n-1}}\int_{S^{n-1}}u(\underline{\eta})\frac{1-r^{2}}{|\underline{\eta}-r\underline{\xi}|^{n}}dS_{\underline{\eta}},

we have the spherical representation of the Poisson kernel of
B_{n}
at
r\underline{\xi}:

P_{r}(\underline{\eta},\underline{\xi})=\frac{1}{\omega_{n-1}}\frac{1-r^{2}}{|\underline{\eta}-r\underline{\xi}|^{n}}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}r^{k}c_{n,k}P_{k}^{n}(\underline{\xi}\cdot\underline{\eta}),

which, as harmonic by itself, gives rise to the harmonic extension
U
of
u
into the unit ball
B_{n}.

Before proving Theorem 1, we prove several technical Lemmas.

Lemma 2.4
.

[
15
]

Let

,
k\in{\mathcal{N}}^{+}
. For any orthonormal base
\{y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{a^{n}_{k}}\}

\displaystyle P^{n}_{k}(\underline{\xi}\cdot\underline{\eta})=\frac{1}{c_{n,k}}\sum_{j=1}^{a^{n}_{k}}\overline{y_{j}(\underline{\xi})}y_{j}(\underline{\eta})=\frac{1}{c_{n,k}}\sum_{j=1}^{a^{n}_{k}}\overline{y_{j}(\underline{\eta})}y_{j}(\underline{\xi}).

The proof is simple. The same type expression holds, in fact, for all Hilbert spaces with an orthonormal basis.

Using Lemma
2.4
, we give another form of the Laplace-Fourier series:

\displaystyle u(\underline{\xi})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}c_{n,k}\int_{S^{n-1}}u(\underline{\eta})P_{k}^{n}(\underline{\xi}\cdot\underline{\eta})dS_{\underline{\eta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\int_{S^{n-1}}u(\underline{\eta})\sum_{j=1}^{a^{n}_{k}}\overline{y_{j}(\underline{\eta})}y_{j}(\underline{\xi})dS_{\underline{\eta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{a^{n}_{k}}b_{j}y_{j}(\underline{\xi}),

where

(2.9)

b_{j}=\int_{S^{n-1}}u(\underline{\eta})\overline{y_{j}(\underline{\eta})}dS_{\underline{\eta}}.

Remark 2.5

Particularly, under the inner product defined by (

Then

If

Lemma 2.7
.

[

]

Let

When

\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n+k-2)}{k!\Gamma(n-2)}x^{k}P^{n}_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-2tx+x^{2})^{\frac{n-2}{2}}}.

When

\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{k}x^{k}P^{2}_{k}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\ln(1-2xt+x^{2}).

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
-1<t<1
,

, then

(2.11)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}kr^{2k+n-2}c_{n,k}P^{n}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n-1}}r^{n}\mbox{ }\frac{nt-(n-4)tr^{4}-(n+2)r^{2}+(n-2)r^{6}}{(1-2tr^{2}+r^{4})^{\frac{n}{2}+1}}.

We denote

J(r,t)=-r^{n}\mbox{ }\frac{nt-(n-4)tr^{4}-(n+2)r^{2}+(n-2)r^{6}}{(1-2tr^{2}+r^{4})^{\frac{n}{2}+1}}.

Proof:

When
n>2,
taking derivative with respect to

on both sides of the equality in Lemma
2.7

\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n+k-2)}{k!\Gamma(n-2)}kx^{k-1}P^{n}_{k}(t)=\frac{(n-2)(t-x)}{(1-2tx+x^{2})^{\frac{n}{2}}}.

Then

\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n+k-2)}{(k-1)!\Gamma(n-1)}x^{k-1}P^{n}_{k}(t)=\frac{t-x}{(1-2tx+x^{2})^{\frac{n}{2}}}.

Letting

and multiplying

\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n+k-2)}{(k-1)!\Gamma(n-1)}r^{n+2k-2}P^{n}_{k}(t)=\frac{tr^{n}-r^{n+2}}{(1-2tr^{2}+r^{2})^{\frac{n}{2}}}.

Taking derivative with respect to

\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n+k-2)}{(k-1)!\Gamma(n-1)}(n+2k-2)r^{n+2k-3}P^{n}_{k}(t)=\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{tr^{n}-r^{n+2}}{(1-2tr^{2}+r^{2})^{\frac{n}{2}}}\right).

By replacing the expression of

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}kr^{n+2k-2}c_{n,k}P^{n}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n+k-1)(n+2k-2)}{(n+k-2)(k-1)!\Gamma(n-1)}r^{n+2k-2}P^{n}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n-1}}r\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{tr^{n}-r^{n+2}}{(1-2tr^{2}+r^{2})^{\frac{n}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n-1}}r^{n}\mbox{ }\frac{nt-(n-4)tr^{4}-(n+2)r^{2}+(n-2)r^{6}}{(1-2tr^{2}+r^{4})^{\frac{n}{2}+1}}.

When

\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{k}x^{k}P^{2}_{k}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\ln(1-2xt+x^{2}).

3.
The forms of
A(u)
in the Quaternionic analysis setting

It is well known that if
U
is harmonic in
\mathcal{D}
, then there exists a canonical conjugate harmonic function
V
in
\mathcal{D}
, such that
F=U+iV
is holomorphic in
\mathcal{D}
.

Using the Cauchy-Riemann equation, we have

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\vskip 5.69046pt\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial V}{\partial y}\\
\frac{\partial U}{\partial y}=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}.\end{array}\right.

Therefore,

F^{\prime}(z)=\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}+i\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}-i\frac{\partial U}{\partial y}.

Then there exists another form of
A(f)
in 2-dimensional case, namely

A(u)=\int_{\mathcal{D}}|\bigtriangledown U|^{2}dxdy=\int_{\mathcal{D}}|F^{\prime}(z)|^{2}dxdy,

which is essentially (
1.2
) through using Green’s formula.

In the quaternionic four dimensional case, we will be able to get a similar counterpart form, that is, specifically,

(3.18)

\displaystyle A(u)=\int_{B_{4}}|\bigtriangledown U|^{2}dV=\int_{B_{4}}|\bar{D}F|^{2}dV,

where
F
is the associated quaternionic regular function. Also see the reasoning given after Lemma
3.5
.

To deduce the above formula we recall some basic knowledge about quaternion algebra and quaternionic analysis. For more details, see
[
14
]
.

The real quaternions constitute a four-dimensional algebra which is non-commutative but associative and divisible.

We choose a basis
1,\mbox{ }\bf i,\mbox{ }\bf j,\mbox{ }\bf k
such that the multiplication is given by the rules as follows:

\bf i^{2}=\bf j^{2}=\bf k^{2}=-1,

\bf i\bf j=-\bf j\bf i=\bf k,\mbox{ }\bf j\bf k=-\bf k\bf j=\bf i,\mbox{ }\bf k\bf i=-\bf i\bf k=\bf j.

A typical quaternion is denoted as

q:=x_{0}+x_{1}{\bf i}+x_{2}{\bf j}+x_{3}{\bf k},

where
x_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}},k=0,1,2,3.

Denote

{\mathbb{H}}:=\{q\ |\ q=x_{0}+x_{1}{\bf i}+x_{2}{\bf j}+x_{3}{\bf k},x_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}},k=0,1,2,3\}

the set of quaternions.

Let
q=x_{0}+x_{1}{\bf i}+x_{2}{\bf j}+x_{3}{\bf k}\in\mathbb{H}
, define
\bar{q}:=x_{0}-x_{1}{\bf i}-x_{2}{\bf j}-x_{3}{\bf k}
to be the conjugate of
q
. Then we have

q\bar{q}=\bar{q}q=\sum_{k=0}^{3}x_{k}^{2}.

We define
|q|:=\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{3}x_{k}^{2}}
.

Definition 3.1
.

For any
q\in\mathbb{H}
,
q=x_{0}+x_{1}{\bf i}+x_{2}{\bf j}+x_{3}{\bf k}
, we call
x_{0}
the real part of
q
, also denoted as
Re(q),
and
x_{1}{\bf i}+x_{2}{\bf j}+x_{3}{\bf k}
the non-real part of
q
, denoted as
{\rm{NRe}}(q)
.

Define the Cauchy-Riemann-Fueter operator

D=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}{\bf i}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}{\bf j}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{3}}{\bf k})

and its conjugate operator

\bar{D}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}{\bf i}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}{\bf j}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{3}}{\bf k}).

Clearly, the Laplace operator in the 4-D space
\Delta_{q}=4D\bar{D}=4\bar{D}D
.

Let
g(q):{\mathbb{H}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{H}}
be a quaternionic valued function.

Denote
g(q)=g_{0}(q)+g_{1}(q){\bf i}+g_{2}(q){\bf j}+g_{3}(q){\bf k}
, where
g_{k}(q),k=0,1,2,3
are real-valued functions.

Definition 3.2
.

We call
g(q)
left-regular in domain
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}
, if
g\in C^{1}(\Omega)
and
Dg=0
. If
g
is left-regular, then we call
\bar{D}g
the left-derivative of
g
.

Remark 3.3
.

(1). If
g
is left-regular, then

(3.19)

\bar{D}g=\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{0}}.

(2). If
g
is left-regular in
\Omega
, then
g,
and each
g_{i},i=0,1,2,3,
as well, is harmonic in
\Omega
.

Lemma 3.4
.

[
14
]

\displaystyle Dg=0\Longleftrightarrow\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\vskip 5.69046pt\displaystyle\frac{\partial g_{0}}{\partial x_{0}}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial x_{k}}\\
\vskip 5.69046pt\displaystyle\frac{\partial g_{0}}{\partial x_{k}}=-\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial x_{0}},k=1,2,3\\
\vskip 5.69046pt\displaystyle\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial x_{j}}=-\frac{\partial g_{j}}{\partial x_{k}},j\not=k.\end{array}\right.

Lemma 3.5
.

[
14
]

Let
U
be a real-valued function defined on a star-shaped open set
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}
. If

is harmonic, then the unique left-regular function

on

such that

and normalized by

is given by

In general, denote by
F(q)=U(q)+U_{1}(q){\bf i}+U_{2}(q){\bf j}+U_{3}(q){\bf k}
a left-regular function on the ball whose real part is the harmonic function
U
and the non-real part is normalized to be zero at the origin. Using the formulas in (

), we have
\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_{k}}=-\frac{\partial U_{k}}{\partial x_{0}},k=1,2,3.

By virtue of (

) we have

Therefore, (

) holds.

Furthermore, in the quaternion algebra setting, we also can obtain a form of

similar to (

) given by Alfors for one complex variable.
That is

where

Lemma 3.6
.

(Stokes’ formula)

If
g\in C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
, then

\int_{\Omega}dw=\int_{\partial\Omega}w.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let

. If
F(q)
is left-regular in

, then we have

where

is the volume element in

Proof:
Using Stokes’ formula, we have

Then,

\displaystyle d(\bar{F}d\sigma{\bar{D}}F)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(\bar{F}d\sigma){\bar{D}}F+\bar{F}d(d\sigma{\bar{D}}F)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\bar{F}\wedge d\sigma{\bar{D}}F-\bar{F}d\sigma\wedge d({\bar{D}}F)

\displaystyle\left[2(\bar{F}D){\bar{D}}F+2\bar{F}(D{\bar{D}}F)\right]dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left[(\bar{F}D){\bar{D}}F+\bar{F}(\frac{1}{4}\Delta_{q}F)\right]dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2|{\bar{D}}F|^{2}dV.

This completes the proof.

Using formula (

) and (

4.
Forms of
A(u)
in the Clifford analysis setting

The quaternionic four-dimensional case is different from the case where functions are defined on the span of the basic elements
{\bf e}_{0}=1,{\bf e}_{1}
and
{\bf e}_{2}
and take values in the whole algebra generated by those basic elements. We regard the latter as the standard Clifford analysis setting in which functions are defined in the Euclidean space but with one more dimension the range of the functions possesses a Cauchy-Riemann structure. In the case we will show that, unlike the complex and the quaternionic case, in the Clifford analysis setting, when
n>2
we have

(4.26)

\displaystyle A(u)=\int_{B_{n}}|\bigtriangledown U|^{2}dV\not=\int_{B_{n}}|\bar{D}F|^{2}dV=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Sc}\int_{S^{n-1}}\bar{F}d\sigma{\bar{D}}F.

Clifford analysis has close connections with harmonic analysis. For instance, a harmonic conjugate system (
[
13
]
) is just the components of a Clifford monogenic function, and Hilbert transform of a function in Euclidean space is the
{\bf e}_{j}
-multiple-sum of the Riesz transforms (
[
3
]
). Clifford algebras structure has been well adopted into contemporary harmonic analysis (
[
6
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
), and helps to solve deep analysis problems.

Next, we will introduce some basic knowledge about Clifford algebra and Clifford analysis. For details, see
[
4
]
.

Let
{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{2},\cdots,{\bf e}_{n-1}
be the basic elements satisfying
{\bf e}_{j}{\bf e}_{k}+{\bf e}_{k}{\bf e}_{j}=-2\delta_{jk}
, where
\delta_{jk}=1
if
j=k
, and
\delta_{jk}=0
otherwise,
j,k=1,2,\cdots,n-1
. Let

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}=\{x\ |\ x=x_{0}+x_{1}{\bf e}_{1}+\cdots+x_{n-1}{\bf e}_{n-1}:x_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}},k=1,2,\cdots,{n-1}\}

be the n-dimensional non-homogeneous Euclidean space.

Let
{\mathcal{CL}}_{0,n-1}
denote the real
Clifford algebra generated by
{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{2},\cdots,{\bf e}_{n-1}
. The linear basis for the
Clifford algebra is given by
{\bf e}_{A}
, where
A
runs over all the
ordered subsets of
\{0,1,\cdots,n-1\}
, namely,

A=\{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{l}\leq n-1\},\mbox{ }1\leq l\leq n-1.

We identify
{\bf e}_{0}={\bf e}_{\emptyset}=1.

A general element
x
of
{\mathcal{CL}}_{0,n-1}
can be represented in the form
x=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}[x]_{k}
, where

[x]_{k}=\sum\limits_{A}x_{A}{\bf e}_{A},{\bf e}_{A}={\bf e}_{i_{1}}{\bf e}_{i_{2}}\cdots{\bf e}_{i_{k}},1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq{n-1}.

When
x=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}[x]_{k}\in{\mathcal{CL}}_{0,n-1}
, then x consists of a scalar part and a non-scalar part,
denoted, respectively, by

x_{0}={\rm{Sc}}(x),\quad\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}[x]_{k}={\rm{NSc}}(x).

We defien the norm of
x\in{\mathcal{CL}}_{0,n-1}
to be
|x|=\sqrt{{\rm Sc}(x\bar{x})}=\sqrt{{\rm Sc}(\bar{x}x)}=\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{A}x_{A}^{2}}
.

Define the generalized Cauchy-Riemann operator as

D=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}{\bf e}_{k})

and the related conjugate operator

\bar{D}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}}-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}{\bf e}_{k}).

Clearly, the Laplace operator
\Delta_{x}
in the n-D space satisfies
\Delta_{x}=4D\bar{D}=4\bar{D}D
.

We will involve the radial decomposition of
D
. Let, as in
[
4
]
,
x=r\xi
with
r=|x|
and
\xi\in S^{n-1}.
Then
D
can be expressed as

D=\frac{1}{2}(\xi\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\xi})=\frac{1}{2}\xi({\partial_{r}}+\Gamma_{\xi}),

where
\Gamma_{\xi}=\bar{\xi}\partial_{\xi}
.

(4.27)

{\bar{D}}=\frac{1}{2}({\partial_{r}}+\Gamma^{*}_{\xi}){\bar{\xi}},

where
\Gamma^{*}_{\xi}=\bar{\partial_{\xi}}\xi
. We note that the action of
\Gamma^{*}_{\xi}
on a function
f
is proceeded as
\Gamma^{*}_{\xi}f=\bar{\partial_{\xi}}(\xi f)
.

As is well known, the Laplacian

(4.28)

\Delta_{x}=\partial^{2}_{r}+\frac{n-1}{r}\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{\xi},

where
\Delta_{\xi}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operaor on
S^{n-1}
.

While we also have

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\bar{D}D

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\Gamma^{*}_{\xi})\bar{\xi}\xi(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\Gamma_{\xi})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\Gamma^{*}_{\xi})(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\Gamma_{\xi})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{r}+\frac{\partial_{r}}{r}(\Gamma^{*}_{\xi}+\Gamma_{\xi})+\frac{1}{r^{2}}(\Gamma^{*}_{\xi}\Gamma_{\xi}-\Gamma_{\xi}).

With formula (
4.28
), we obtain

(4.29)

\Gamma^{*}_{\xi}+\Gamma_{\xi}=(n-1){\bf I},

where

is the identity operator.

Remark 4.1
.

For the one complex variable case treated in

B_{n}
are, respectively,

P_{r}(\xi,\eta)=\frac{1}{\omega_{n-1}}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty}r^{|k|}P^{(k)}(\eta^{-1}\xi),

and

Q_{r}(\xi,\eta)=\frac{1}{\omega_{n-1}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{k}{n+k-2}r^{k}P^{(k)}(\eta^{-1}\xi)-\sum_{k=-\infty}^{-1}r^{|k|}P^{(k)}(\eta^{-1}\xi)\right].

Using Lemma
4.6
and formula (
4.30
), for
r<1
, we get cancelation for the negative index summation part and have

(4.31)

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n-1}}\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{n+2k-2}{n+k-2}r^{k}\int_{S^{n-1}}P^{(k)}(\eta^{-1}\xi)u(\eta)dS_{\eta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n-1}}\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{n+2k-2}{n+k-2}r^{k}\int_{S^{n-1}}C_{n,k}^{+}(\xi,\eta)u(\eta)dS_{\eta}.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
F(x)\in C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
. If
F(x)
is left-monogenic in
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then we have

(4.32)

\int_{\Omega}|\bar{D}F|^{2}dV=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Sc}\int_{\partial\Omega}\bar{F}d\sigma{\bar{D}}F.

Proof:
Using Stokes’ formula, we have

\int_{\partial\Omega}\bar{F}d\sigma{\bar{D}}F=\int_{\Omega}d(\bar{F}d\sigma{\bar{D}}F).

There holds

\displaystyle d(\bar{F}d\sigma{\bar{D}}F)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(\bar{F}d\sigma){\bar{D}}F+\bar{F}d(d\sigma{\bar{D}}F)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\bar{F}\wedge d\sigma{\bar{D}}F+(-1)^{n-1}\bar{F}d\sigma\wedge d({\bar{D}}F)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[2(\bar{F}D){\bar{D}}F+2\bar{F}(D{\bar{D}}F)\right]dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left[(\bar{F}D){\bar{D}}F+\bar{F}(\frac{1}{4}\Delta_{x}F)\right]dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left[(\bar{F}D){\bar{D}}F\right]dV.

By taking scalar part on both sides, we get (
4.32
). This completes the proof.

If, in particular,
F(x)
is left-monogenic in a ball
B_{n}(r)
, then
F(x)
has the form (
4.31
).
Using the radial decomposition (
4.27
) of
\bar{D}
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{D}F(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\omega_{n-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{n+2k-2}{n+k-2}r^{k-1}[k\int_{S^{n-1}}C_{n,k}^{+}(\xi,\eta)u(\eta)dS_{\eta}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}\Gamma^{*}_{\xi}C_{n,k}^{+}(\xi,\eta)u(\eta)dS_{\eta}]\bar{\xi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{n+2k-2}{n+k-2}(k+\frac{n-1}{2})r^{k-1}\int_{S^{n-1}}C_{n,k}^{+}(\xi,\eta)u(\eta)dS_{\eta}\bar{\xi}.

Using the orthogonality between
C_{n,k}^{+}(\xi,\eta)
and
C_{n,j}^{+}(\xi,\eta)
when
k\not=j,
we have

(4.33)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{n}(r)}|\bar{D}F|^{2}dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\rm Sc}\int_{S_{r}^{n-1}}\bar{F}d\sigma{\bar{D}}F

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\omega_{n-1}}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}(\frac{n+2k-2}{n+k-2})^{2}(k+\frac{n-1}{2})kr^{n+2k-2}\int_{S^{n-1}}\int_{S^{n-1}}u(\eta_{1})u(\eta_{2})dS_{\eta_{1}}dS_{\eta_{2}}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle{\rm Sc}\int_{S^{n-1}}\overline{C_{n,k}^{+}(\xi,\eta_{1})}\xi C_{n,k}^{+}(\xi,\eta_{2})\bar{\xi}dS_{\xi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}(k+\frac{n-1}{2})r^{2k+n-2}c_{n,k}\int_{S^{n-1}}\int_{S^{n-1}}u({\eta_{1}})u({\eta_{2}})P^{n}_{k}({\eta_{1}}\cdot{\eta_{2}})dS_{{\eta_{1}}}dS_{{\eta_{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}(k+\frac{n-1}{2})r^{2k+n-2}\sum_{j=1}^{a^{n}_{k}}|b_{j}|^{2}.

Taking limit
r\rightarrow 1^{-}
on both sides of (
4.33
), we have

\int_{B_{n}}|\bar{D}F|^{2}dV=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Sc}\int_{S^{n-1}}\bar{F}d\sigma{\bar{D}}F=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}(k+\frac{n-1}{2})\sum_{j=1}^{a^{n}_{k}}|b_{j}|^{2}\not=\int_{B_{n}}|\bigtriangledown U|^{2}dV.

Then formula (
4.26
) is proved.

In summary, in the Clifford algebra setting of the n-dimensional Euclidean space we have

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
F
be left-monogenic in
B_{n}

(n>2)
and
U
its scalar part with square-integrable boundary value

in

\displaystyle A(u)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{n-1}}\int_{S^{n-1}}\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{|u({\eta_{1}})-u({\eta_{2}})|^{2}}{|{\eta_{1}}-{\eta_{2}}|^{n}}dS_{{\eta_{1}}}dS_{{\eta_{2}}}

\displaystyle\int_{B_{n}}|\bigtriangledown U|^{2}dV

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}k\sum_{j=1}^{a^{n}_{k}}|b_{j}|^{2}

\displaystyle\int_{B_{n}}|\bar{D}F|^{2}dV

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\rm Sc}\int_{S^{n-1}}\bar{F}d\sigma{\bar{D}}F
