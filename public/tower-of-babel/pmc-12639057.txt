Comparison of metaheuristic algorithms set-point tracking-based weight optimization for model predictive control
Authors: Kawsar Nassereddine, Marek Turzynski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Traditional controllers frequently perform poorly when managing complex industrial systems because they must balance multiple goals like cost, pollution, and efficiency. For higher performance and efficiency, model predictive controller addresses this but necessitates efficient cost function tuning, which is currently frequently enhanced utilising metaheuristic algorithms. This work aims to develop and validate a data-driven weight optimisation method for multivariable model predictive controller in a DC microgrid comprising photovoltaic panels, battery, supercapacitor, grid, and load—by systematically balancing control effort and accuracy. Four algorithms—particle swarm optimization, genetic algorithm, pareto search, and pattern search-selected for their capacity to resolve complex, multi-objective problems are used in this study’s automated, set-point tracking-based weight optimisation approach. The results show that incorporating parameter interdependency reduces genetic algorithm’s power load tracking error from 16% to 8%, while particle swarm optimization achieves an error of under 2% even without considering interdependency. Fast convergence and trade-offs are supported by pareto and pattern search, but they are less responsive to sudden changes. These methods provide a feasible, repeatable way to boost model predictive controller performance.

Introduction

Managing complex systems in industrial processes like power generation, chemical manufacturing, and oil refining requires addressing the issue of optimisation under several, sometimes conflicting goals, like lowering emissions without sacrificing product quality or minimising energy consumption while increasing efficiency. To discover a fair trade-off between objectives including cost, efficiency, safety, and environmental effect, these situations call for multi-objective optimisation strategies. For instance, speeding up production in chemical plants may result in increased energy consumption and safety hazards; in power generating, maximizing output might interfere with fuel economy. To solve this problem, a Model Predictive Controller (MPC) can be a useful tool for managing these trade-offs.

MPCs are advanced management strategies that forecast future behaviour and identify the best course of action by using a model of the system. MPC facilitates proactive decision-making that improves overall system performance by carefully handling competing goals. Although MPC is good at handling complicated systems, adjusting the cost function is one of its biggest problems. The cost function’s ability to effectively weight different goals is crucial to MPC efficiency, and striking the correct balance frequently necessitates thorough research and might take a lot of effort. This tuning problem is still very important and requires careful and effective techniques to solving it1.

Numerous metaheuristic optimisation techniques have been employed in recent research to optimise or adjust a MPC’s cost function. Techniques like Genetic Algorithms (GA), Particle Swarm Optimisation (PSO), Salp Swarm Optimisation, and Differential Evolution systematically investigate the weighting-matrix space, surpassing manual trial-and-error in closed-loop tasks ranging from industrial process control to arterial blood pressure regulation. Due to their ability to balance computing efficiency and search width, comparative surveys show that PSO and GA are the most used techniques; nevertheless, newer swarm-based approaches are being used more for complex, nonlinear plants2.

The problem of effectively MPC tuning is not new, and some approaches have been proposed and described in the literature. For example, in3 a PSO-based tuning approach for MPC under model uncertainty was discussed. Solution was validated on the Shell heavy oil fractionator process and optimized for handling multiple control objectives. This approach is flexible, robust, and can be applied to various MPC formulations. Another PSO-based method for tuning networked MPC systems in large-scale networks like electricity grids and enabling a balance between performance and communication overhead was described in4.

In5, GA and multi-objective fuzzy decision-making method was introduced to optimize MPC tuning parameters based on user-defined control preferences, and it was validated through a mixing tank case study. The technique demonstrated its flexibility, adapting well to different preference sets. Similarly, in cement kiln control, GA-based MPC tuning was used to minimize tracking error and energy consumption, with an interactive decision tree involving operator input for real-time adjustments. This operator-driven approach was more effective and faster than multi-objective techniques, outperforming them in a variety of contexts by 23.74% in various settings6.

Another interesting solution was described in7, where a multi-objective optimization method was applied to adjust MPC in a Crude Distillation Unit, optimizing weight matrices either sequentially or simultaneously to balance output tracking and input movement, yielding lower computational costs compared to traditional methods. Likewise, two multi-objective optimization techniques were presented for MPC tuning: lexicographic optimization for square systems and compromise optimization for non-square systems, demonstrating reduced commissioning time and less computational effort. These methods were successfully applied to the Shell Heavy Oil Fractionator subsystem8. A machine learning architecture was introduced for automating the tuning of many inputs and many outputs MPC in a diesel engine air path, optimizing tuning time and cost by approximating the cost function9. Additionally, an adaptive MPC tuning method was proposed, using online identification and Linear Matrix Inequalities with Recursive Least Squares for parameter updates. This method is effective for managing unknown or time-varying parameters and is more efficient than conventional techniques10.

In11 an application of Bayesian Optimization for tuning MPC controllers in Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning systems was proposed. This approach enables elimination of the need for lengthy closed-loop simulations, what results in better performance, lower computational cost compared to traditional methods and offers higher efficiency for MPC applications. Described solution was adapted and tested to complex building control.

Similarly, Bayesian Optimization may be also employed for autonomous vehicle lateral control, adjusting to various driving scenarios and successfully completing lane-keeping maneuvers in simulations. This tuning technique optimized MPC gains based on user-specified standards12.

A multi-objective optimization procedure for Nonlinear MPC to balance reference tracking and control effort was proposed in13. This approach was validated on the Van de Vusse reactor and Tennessee Eastman plant, demonstrating robustness to disturbances and model mismatch. Another method for NMPC systematically determines tuning parameters through multi-objective optimization, ensuring strong closed-loop performance despite disruptions. This technique was applied to three chemical process examples described in14.

In15, an analytical tuning technique for multivariable MPC was proposed for square First-Order Plus Dead Time systems with inactive constraints, focusing on decoupling and achieving the intended performance through closed-loop transfer functions. Additionally, a technique for MPC tuning in autonomous quadrotors used machine learning to adjust objective function weights, compensating for nonlinearities and modifying the system to avoid obstacles during flight, demonstrating the potential of machine learning for MPC in Aerial vehicule operations16; Although it requires a large amount of training data and adds additional processing cost, this data-driven approach learns and adjusts dynamically to complicated nonlinear dynamics.

Another approach was presented in17, where a simplified method for tuning MPC by dynamically adjusting output setpoints rather than internal weights was described. This technique offers desired control performance with minimal computational effort and supports zone control and real-time optimization.

An MPC tuning is used to build thermal control18, achieving up to 5.2% energy savings and improved comfort by adjusting prediction and control horizons, outperforming conventional PID control; however, the approach demands higher computational resources and accurate system models, which can limit deployment in legacy building‑management systems.

And to automate MPC tuning, reinforcement learning (RL) may be also used. For example, in19 this approach was adapted to control aerial robot trajectory tracking. The proposed solution, validated through real-world experiments, significantly reduced the time and effort typically required for weight tuning in MPC. Nevertheless, RL’s high computational cost and training phase instability are significant drawbacks, particularly when used in safety-critical systems.

In20 An offline technique for adjusting MPC parameters in limited multi-inputs multi-outputs systems was presented. This approach enables to reach a balance between the accuracy and response speed while ensuring closed-loop stability. Using exponential data weighting allowed long time horizons prediction without sacrificing computational efficiency. Achieved results demonstrated its superiority over existing techniques in terms of both performance and speed. However, the approach’s primary drawbacks are limited capacity to adjust to changes in real time and the requirement for precise prior system modelling.

Another solution was discussed in21, where a robust closed-loop performance tuning method for MPC was proposed. Described approach was validated through a steering control case study and focused on resilience in the face of disturbances. To ensure robustness and anticipation in control design matrix-based methods were used. In another approach, robustness-oriented tuning for AutoRegressive with eXogenous input model (ARX) based offset-free MPC was demonstrated for multivariable systems such as cement mills and distillation columns22. Nevertheless, this approach’s primary drawback is its dependence on precise ARX model identification.

In a review of MPC, tuning techniques was presented. Authors discussed a specialized methods like Max-Plus-Linear MPC and general approaches at the importance of accurate process models for effective tuning. A usefulness of Kalman filtering, state estimation, and auto-tuning were also discussed. Authors also noticed that advancements in subspace identification and autocovariance least-squares had made MPC tuning more cost-effective23.

In24 a unique technique for improving MPC weights in motion cueing algorithms for driving simulators was proposed. This approach utilizes a multi-objective genetic algorithm to optimize the cost function weights for objectives such as minimizing motion inputs and improving realism. The technique was further refined using an interactive genetic algorithm (IGA) to maximize user satisfaction. Simulation results indicated that the method enhanced the realism of driving simulators and reduced motion inaccuracies compared to previous tuning techniques. However, it has limited scalability to real-time or large-scale applications, relies on subjective user feedback for IGA, and has a high computational cost.

Similarly, a strategy for tuning MPC in automated and connected vehicles (CAVs) in mixed traffic was described in25. Bayesian optimization and inverse reinforcement learning were applied to adjust weights online, achieving a 20% improvement in performance at unsignalized crossings, showcasing its effectiveness in real-world traffic scenarios. However, this strategy necessitates sufficient information availability, which reduces its efficacy in situations with limited or extremely variable data.

A novel tuning strategy for nonsquare systems with more outputs than inputs was introduced in26. Proposed method is based on analysis of set points or ranges for controlled variables. The methodology includes determining the best attainable closed-loop performance, calculating optimal scaling, and tuning the controller weights. The method was successfully applied to the Shell heavy oil fractionator benchmark, demonstrating steady performance and robust disturbance handling without excessive modification of manipulated variables.

To optimize the trade-off between fatigue loads and power generation Pareto curves may be effectively used to assess a model predictive control (MPC) strategy for wind turbines. By using this technique, MPC weights can be adjusted to lower loads without compromising power production27,28. As it is presented in29, pattern search methods for linearly constrained problems can provide global convergence to Karush-Kuhn-Tucker points without the need of gradients. This might be further simplified when the constraints are restricted to variable bounds.

Recent research demonstrates a clear trend toward hybrid and intelligent optimization methods for modern energy systems. Hybrid schemes such as GOA-PSO–based PID controllers have achieved substantial improvements in settling time and overshoot reduction in renewable-rich scenarios30, while Adaptive MPC frameworks enhanced with reinforcement learning and optimizers like COA-jDE have improved frequency stability and grid code compliance in low-inertia systems31. Similarly, approaches such as MGOA-tuned PD-PIDA controllers and GOA-PSO–tuned STATCOMs have been shown to strengthen fast frequency response, LVRT compliance, and post-fault stability in microgrids32,33. In the PV domain, fuzzy logic–based MPPT strategies, including modified and hybrid variants, have enhanced tracking accuracy and speed, though at the cost of added complexity34, while comparative analyses of FLC and MPC highlight FLC’s superior dynamic response against MPC’s long-term stability35. Together, these works reflect the field’s growing emphasis on sophisticated, hybrid, and adaptive optimizers to address the nonlinear and highly variable challenges of renewable-dominated power networks, providing essential context for our algorithmic comparison.

An example of the system that requires multi-criteria optimization is also a microgrid power system. In suc h systems (e.g. DC microgrids), energy is generated by various distributed power sources (wind generators, PV, etc.) - Fig. 1, whose level of power generation depends on many factors. Mutual correlation of these factors (e.g. irradiance, wind speed) is often difficult to predict in wider time horizon, what complicates system stabilization. As a result, in microgrid power systems use of additional energy storages (batteries, super capacitors etc.) is required. Hence, a precise prediction of energy storages state of charge depending on consumers’ energy consumption and weather conditions plays an important role in system optimization and stabilization. On the other hand, microgrid power systems usually cooperates with system power grids, thus the bidirectional energy flow occurs between these systems. It must be noted that energy cost is not constant, and it varies in dependency on the time of day and year season. Consequently, an economic aspect must be also considered in a microgrid power system control process. To solve microgrid optimization problem, an approach based on application of MPC may be effectively adopted36.

Custom cost function

Key components of DC microgrid operation, including precise power tracking, smooth energy flow, and battery life preservation, are optimised using the cost function in this work. It includes terms that reduce variations from the battery’s and supercapacitor’s reference state of charge, hence extending storage life. Other terms encourage system stability and energy efficiency by penalising big or sudden variations in power flow as well as deviations between the desired and actual load power.

This cost function (1), is based on MATLAB’s default MPC structure37, and has been altered to incorporate demand monitoring error and grid power use penalties, making it more appropriate for actual microgrid scenarios with intermittent renewable energy sources. A binary switching variable adjusts the weightings during low-generation periods—such as early morning or late afternoon—to prioritize energy saving and reliability.

Loss function

The loss function evaluates the performance of a given set of weights by running the MPC with the current parameters, constraints, initial conditions, and system data. The main component of the loss is the tracking error’s squared norm, and to avoid overfitting, a regularisation term penalises high weight values. The final loss is the sum of the tracking error and the regularization term, with the regularization scaled by eta = 5 to appropriately balance its impact relative to the tracking error. Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)

Every potential weight vector is represented by PSO as a “particle” in a multidimensional search space. These particles travel across space by adjusting their position and velocity in response to the swarm’s global best solution as well as their own best past performance. Without the need for gradient information or an explicit system model, this collective learning behaviour aids in determining the optimal weight configuration. The search space research and the exploitation of suitable regions are encouraged by this hybrid movement. Using random components helps prevent local minima and guarantees diversity in the search. Each particle’s position and velocity are randomly produced within predetermined bounds during the initialisation phase of the PSO method. The objective function is used to determine each particle’s initial cost, and the best-known position is then determined for each particle. Subsequently, the particle having the lowest cost among all is determined to be the global best answer. The cost function, the number of particles, the number of dimensions (i.e., the weights being adjusted), the maximum number of iterations, and the algorithm-specific parameters—inertia weight and acceleration constants—are key inputs for the algorithm.

Each particle modifies its position and velocity according to three guiding terms during the main loop. The inertia term keeps the particle moving in the same direction (exploration), the cognitive component draws it in the direction of its most well-known position (exploitation), and the social component draws it in the direction of the swarm’s global best solution. Particles are reassessed after updating and compared to boundary limitations. The particle’s own best is updated if a new position results in a lower cost. In the same way, the personal best of any particle is updated if it surpasses the global best. PSO is particularly useful for adjusting complicated cost functions in control applications such as DC microgrids because of its capacity to strike a balance between exploration and exploitation. Genetic algorithm

Inspired by natural selection, a genetic algorithm is a population-based metaheuristic optimisation technique that finds optimal or nearly optimal solutions by evolving a population of candidate solutions, also known as chromosomes or individuals, over many generations; The algorithm assesses each individual’s fitness at each iteration (generation), chooses the most fit individuals for reproduction, applies crossover (recombination of parent solutions) and mutation (random alterations) to create new offspring, and then replaces some or all of the old population with the new one41. The primary loop of the GA starts with a fitness function evaluation of each individual. Using a tournament-style selection process, the most fit members of randomly selected subgroups are selected to become parents. A scattered crossover approach uses a crossover probability to make children by randomly combining genes from both parents. Then, with a specific probability, a mutation is performed, changing a gene in the descendants by appending a value selected from a power distribution. Elite preservation guarantees that the top individual or individuals (E = 1) are passed on directly to the following generation. Then, the newly produced descendants and elites are used to replace the existing individuals, updating the population.

MATLAB’s default settings were used to establish the initial population for the Genetic Algorithm. These settings randomly create individuals between predetermined lower and higher bounds for each cost function weight. In addition to ensuring adequate coverage of the search field, this approach offers a wide range of potential options to begin the optimisation process. It is common practice to use the default initialisation, particularly in situations where no prior knowledge favours certain beginning locations43.

Pattern search

Pattern search is a derivative-free optimisation method that progressively explores the search space by evaluating the objective function at several points organised in a pattern around the current answer. Pattern search is particularly useful for optimising functions that are noisy, discontinuous, or non-differentiable since, in contrast to gradient-based algorithms, it does not require any knowledge of the derivatives of the objective function. The algorithm repeats the procedure until a stopping criterion is met, iteratively expanding or contracting its search pattern to identify better solutions based on the results.

Results

Sensitivity analysis of optimization parameters

The reliability of these algorithms-based tuning technique is further evaluated by a sensitivity analysis that concentrates on key algorithm parameters. By systematically changing these settings, the study discovers combinations that ensure accurate tracking outcomes, with performance assessed using the tracking error between the load power Pload and the demand reference Pdem, defined as Error% = (Pdem – Pload)/Pdem. This phase is crucial to prevent overfitting and ensure that the controller functions dependably in a range of operating scenarios.

Particle swarm optimization

Effect of initial weight values on tracking performance

Richards and Ventura46 state that the particles should be initialized to ensure broad coverage of the search space, so that they are distributed as evenly as possible throughout the space. The standard method of initialization fails to accomplish this goal, especially in high-dimensional spaces.

We began our work using the default settings, adopting MATLAB’s default parameters for PSO. The only modification was reducing the swarm size from 100 to 2 to significantly reduce computation time. After this, we ran the initial simulation. As shown in the top Fig. 5, the early stage results initially deviated significantly from the reference demand. To address this, we introduced initial values for the weights.

MATLAB’s default settings, which produce individuals at random between predetermined lower and higher bounds for each cost function weight, were used to establish the initial population for the Genetic Algorithm. This approach helps guarantee enough coverage of the search field and offers a varied collection of potential options to begin the optimisation. When no prior knowledge favours certain starting positions, it is usual practice to use the default initialisation. The outcome, illustrated in the bottom figure, shows improved tracking performance, with values more closely aligned with the reference.

Inertia range, social adjustment, self adjustment

We begin by varying the inertia range (to explore how different levels of inertia affect the system’s performance and to identify which range helps balance exploration and exploitation effectively). The inertia weight controls the impact of the previous velocity on the current velocity, influencing how much the system explores new solutions versus exploiting known good ones. Choosing an appropriate range is crucial because too high inertia can cause the system to overshoot optimal solutions, while too low inertia might lead to premature convergence49. After that, we identify the range that yields the smallest error.

Next, we fine-tune the social adjustment weights. Social weights determine how much influence the best solutions found by the group have on individual adjustments. By tuning these weights, we control how strongly particles (or agents) are attracted towards the global best position. As illustrated in Fig. 8(a), the error does not consistently increase with higher social weight values, so we select the value corresponding to the lowest error. This observation suggests that increasing social weight doesn’t always lead to better or worse performance in a straightforward way; instead, there’s an optimal value that minimizes error50,51.

Finally, using the previously determined inertia and social weight values, we adjust the self-adjustment weight. Adjusting the cognitive weight after fixing inertia and social weights can fine-tune the balance between individual exploration and social learning, further minimizing error52. The self-adjustment weight controls the particle’s tendency to rely on its own past best position.

In Fig. 8(b), we examined parameter interdependencies specifically, how the optimal value of one parameter (inertia, social, or self) depends on other parameter, and to confirm whether considering their combined effects could lead to improved optimization results. To capture these interactions, we used nested loops to evaluate the effect of each value of one parameter on the others.

Although no new minimum error below 1.96% was found, the analysis was essential to confirm that PSO parameters do not exhibit hidden interdependencies that could otherwise lead to unexplored improvements. Without this verification, one might wrongly assume that parameter tuning is fully independent, risking suboptimal settings or overfitting to specific scenarios. By confirming the absence of significant interdependencies, the analysis strengthens confidence in the reported optimal solution, demonstrating that the tuning reflects a thorough and reliable search rather than an incomplete or biased outcome.

Discussion

Key PSO factors that impact load tracking was investigated in this sensitivity analysis, including swarm size, initial weights, iterations, swarm span, inertia, and social and self-adjustment weights. While 200 iterations and a swarm span over 100 offered a decent mix between accuracy and variety, setting starting weights greatly enhanced performance. Due to their non-linear impacts, optimal inertia and social weights needed to be carefully adjusted. Robustness was confirmed by parameter interactions, which revealed no superior combinations than sequential tuning. And finally larger swarms increased computation time, but a small swarm size of 2 offered acceptable results.

Genetic algorithm

Upper bounds

The genetic algorithm’s capacity to efficiently explore the solution space is strongly impacted by the selection of upper and lower constraints on the decision variables. While loose boundaries raise the complexity of the search space and may hinder convergence, tight bounds may limit exploration and result in premature convergence. In GA, establishing these limits appropriately is essential to striking a balance between exploration and exploitation54.

When the upper bound is 30 or higher, tracking performance is acceptable during the curtailment period but poor outside of it. In contrast, with an upper bound of 20, tracking is better during non-curtailment periods but worsens during curtailment. An upper bound of 20 was chosen to maintain balanced tracking performance across all periods. While tracking during curtailment is not optimal at this setting, it can be improved by tuning other parameters.

Population size

The error is significantly high when the population size is set to 2. As the population size increases from 2 to 10, the error decreases, reaching 16%. However, beyond a population size of 10, the error starts to rise again as shown in Fig. 9. Therefore, a population size of 10 was chosen as the optimal point55. Increasing the size further than 20 is not feasible, as it would significantly extend the runtime at the cost of other critical performance factors.

Other parameters

In contrast to Particle Swarm Optimization, the Genetic Algorithm requires interdependency among parameters such as the selection function, crossover function, and mutation function to achieve lower error. Selection chooses individuals for reproduction based on their fitness; crossover combines them to generate offspring, while mutation introduces random variations to preserve diversity. Tournament selection offered a more balanced distribution, power mutation enabled efficient exploration, and scattered crossover helped reduce the error effectively56. This contrasts with PSO, which primarily depends on velocity and position updates without such operator interdependencies.

Before applying this interdependency according to the Fig. 10, the lowest error achieved was 15%. However, with the introduction of interdependent parameters, the error was reduced to 13%. This interdependency was further enhanced by adjusting additional parameters such as Elite Count and Crossover Fraction. Accounting for their interaction led to a new minimum error of around 8% in Fig. 11, a notable improvement over the 16% error.

Discussion

The boundaries placed on choice variables have a significant impact on the speed of the genetic algorithm; tight restrictions run the risk of premature convergence, while loose bounds complicate searches and prevent convergence. To balance tracking accuracy during restriction and normal periods, an upper bound of 20 was selected. Error is also affected by population size, with 10 particles offering the optimal balance between computational cost and exploration. The interaction of selection, crossover, and mutation operators determines GA’s efficacy in contrast to PSO; integrating these interdependencies decreased error from 15% to 13%, and fine-tuning elite count and crossover percentage further decreased it to roughly 8%. This emphasises how crucial it is to optimise GA parameters collaboratively to attain better outcomes.

By striking a balance between exploration and variety, GA parameters such as selection, crossover, and mutation can be tuned simultaneously to greatly improve performance. PSO parameters, on the other hand, behave more autonomously and are not as sensitive to these kinds of interactions.

Load demand tracking performance using optimization algorithms

PSO provided the most dependable load power tracking out of all the studied algorithms, retaining high accuracy both during curtailment and non-curtailment times. GA performed moderately well; under typical circumstances, its tracking was adequate, but during more dynamic phases, it lacked precision, which made it less appropriate for crucial energy management. Due to its trade-off balancing between various aims, Pareto optimisation performed almost as well as PSO during non-curtailment periods but became much less successful during curtailment. Like GA, pattern search produced average outcomes but was unable to adjust to the drastic changes in curtailment times. PSO is the recommended option for reliable load tracking in varied grid settings due to its overall consistent and adaptable conduct as can be shown in Fig. 12.

Accurate tracking becomes harder during curtailment because the load drops abruptly to much lower, highly fluctuating values, which rapidly reshapes the cost landscape (shifted optima, steeper gradients, increased multimodality and noise). Algorithmically this penalizes methods that rely on local polling or slow generational adaptation: Pattern Search performs local polls and progressively reduces its step size, so when the optimum moves far from the current iterate it needs many iterations and step–size reductions to escape the old basin; Pareto-style multiobjective evolutionary methods preserve diversity and rebalance trade-offs across generations, so a sudden change forces them to re-establish dominance relations and rebuild the Pareto front, delaying focused exploitation. By contrast, PSO’s velocity-based, population-wide updates (personal-best + global-best guidance) allow larger, directed jumps and therefore faster recovery after abrupt shifts. These differences explain why Pattern Search and Pareto methods typically underperform during curtailment.

The comparative evaluation of optimization algorithms highlights distinct strengths and limitations across the tested methods, as shown in Table 6. The Pareto-based optimizer achieved an average error of 13.7% with an average computation time of 372 s, demonstrating strong capability in handling multi-objective problems and constraints while effectively exploring trade-offs. The Pattern search algorithm, with an average error of 13.9% and computation time of 64.2 s, proved to be fast and efficient for problems with simple or well-defined constraints, making it suitable for straightforward optimization tasks. However, Pareto and Pattern adaptability are limited in curtailment or fast-changing scenarios, reducing responsiveness under dynamic conditions.

The GA recorded an average error of 8.7% and computation time of 366 s, showing good global search performance and strong handling of complex, nonlinear problem spaces. Nonetheless, its low population size may restrict search diversity, and it typically exhibits slower convergence than PSO, requiring careful parameter adjustment for effective performance; plus, independent genetic algorithm relies on subjective user feedback which can limits its effectives.

Finally, the PSO algorithm achieved the lowest average error of 1.9% and an average computation time of 150 s, confirming its fas. convergence, robust global search ability, and effective balance between exploration and exploitation. Despite these advantages, PSO may stall or converge prematurely if not properly tuned, and a small swarm size can further reduce its exploration capability, which is acknowledged as a limitation in the study.

This study relies on offline tuning, which may not accurately reflect the dynamics of real-time MPC operation. The findings are derived from a particular microgrid setup including a battery, supercapacitor, grid, PV generation, and load. Moreover, the effectiveness of the algorithms depends on hyperparameter selection (such as population size, mutation rate, and search strategies). And also the proposed framework performs best under conditions where accurate forecasting and system information are available.

It should be noted that the execution time is reported as the average of 10 runs, since it can fluctuate due to system-related factors independent of the random seed.

Conclusion

This paper presents a practical off-line approach for tuning Model Predictive Control’s cost function with constraints. These weights determine how the MPC prioritizes tracking performance, control effort, and other objectives. It assesses four optimisation algorithms for automated MPC weight adjusting based on set-point tracking: PSO, GA, Pareto Search, and Pattern Search. The approach ensures successful regulation even under numerous interacting variables and system limits by concentrating on how well the system tracks desired set points. This aligns controller behaviour with operational goals in the actual world. With a 1.9% error rate and quick convergence, PSO performed the best, making it suitable for offline applications. Although GA needed more work and fine-tuning, it demonstrated a robust worldwide search. Pareto Search was good at handling multi-objective trade-offs, but it was less flexible and took a long time to calculate. The quickest method, Pattern Search, was vulnerable to local minima. Sensitivity analysis made clear how crucial it is to adjust parameters unique to each algorithm. Future research will concentrate on applying these optimised weights in the Microgrid model simulation.
