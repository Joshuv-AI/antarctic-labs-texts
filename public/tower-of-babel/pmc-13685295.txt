From caregiver burden to psychological resilience and positive emotional outcomes: the effectiveness of innovative psychiatric nursing intervention among family caregivers of patients with bipolar disorder
Authors: Rania Maher Alhalawany, Yahya Mubark Khatatbeh, Ola Zaher Mansour, Shereen Roushdy Hashem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFamily caregivers of people with bipolar disorder (BD) often experience substantial caregiver burden (CB), lower psychological resilience (PR), and fewer caregiving rewarding feelings (CRF), which generally affect caregiver well-being and patient care. In this study, we assessed the effectiveness of an innovative psychiatric nursing intervention in reducing CB, improving PR, and fostering CRF among family caregivers of patients with BD.MethodsA two-arm randomized controlled pre- and post-test study was conducted with 70 family caregivers recruited from a psychiatric hospital in Egypt. Eligible family caregivers were recruited using convenience sampling and were subsequently randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group (n = 35) or the control group (n = 35). The intervention ran for eight weeks and was a structured nurse-led psychiatric telenursing program that blended psychoeducation, stress control, coping ability training, mindfulness practices, communication skills, and resilience-building strategies. Outcomes were measured using validated tools for CB, PR, and CRF, both before and after the program. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, independent-samples t-tests, separate repeated-measures ANOVAs, separate ANCOVAs adjusted for baseline scores, Pearson’s correlation analysis, and multiple linear regression.ResultsParticipants in the intervention group demonstrated significantly lower CB and significantly higher PR and CRF than those in the control group (all p < 0.001). Separate repeated-measures ANOVAs demonstrated significant main effects of time and group, together with significant group × time interactions across all three outcome variables. Separate ANCOVAs further confirmed that these differences remained statistically significant after adjusting for baseline scores. In addition, PR and CRF were identified as significant independent predictors of CB in multiple linear regression analysis.ConclusionsOverall, the findings suggest that structured nurse-led psychiatric telenursing interventions may be linked to decreased CB along with improved PR and CRF among family caregivers.RecommendationsIn practical terms, structured nurse-led psychoeducational sessions may function as an important part of family-centred psychiatric care. To confirm the durability of the benefits and determine their applicability in broader settings, further multicenter studies with longer follow-up periods are needed to assess the sustainability and generalizability of these effects.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT07548359, registered on April 22, 2026. Background

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a chronic mood disorder that causes repeated cycles of manic or hypomanic episodes, alternating with periods of depression, resulting in serious functional disability. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that approximately 40 million people worldwide live with BD, making it a major public health concern [1]. Families play a significant role in supporting individuals with BD throughout the recovery process, including relapse prevention, treatment adherence, and psychosocial rehabilitation [2]. Family caregiving in BD has consequently emerged as an important focus of psychiatric and mental health nursing research, particularly because of the sustained emotional and practical demands placed on family caregivers [3]. Family caregivers provide continuous emotional and practical support, assist with the early recognition of relapse warning signs, encourage treatment adherence, and contribute to long-term recovery and community functioning [3].

Family caregivers of individuals with BD frequently experience substantial caregiving burdens because of the chronic and recurrent nature of the illness, unpredictable mood fluctuations, repeated hospitalizations, and long-term caregiving responsibilities [3, 4]. Recent evidence has demonstrated that caregiver burden (CB) is associated with poorer psychological well-being, increased emotional distress, and unmet support needs, highlighting the need for effective family-centred psychiatric nursing interventions to improve caregiver outcomes [3, 5]. Psychological resilience (PR) functions as a vital protective element that helps caregivers cope with their caregiving responsibilities while maintaining psychological well-being [5, 6]. Caregiving rewarding feelings (CRF) refer to the positive aspects of the caregiving experience, including personal satisfaction, sense of accomplishment, emotional closeness, and perceived meaning derived from the caregiving role [6]. Positive aspects of caregiving enable caregivers to manage stress better while increasing their happiness and protecting them from the harmful effects of their caregiving responsibilities [7, 8].

The training of patients with their caregivers brings benefits to both groups through the implementation of coping skills training and resilience-building programs [2, 9]. Family education should be regarded as an essential component of care because families play a central role in BD treatment [10]. Telenursing provides a remote platform for delivering psychoeducation and caregiver support and may improve access to structured nursing interventions when face-to-face participation is difficult [11]. Psychological nursing treatment includes psychoeducation, coping skills, stress management, and a coordinated care system for family support [2]. Recent evidence suggests that family-focused psychiatric nurse-led programs may contribute to improved psychological and functional outcomes among individuals with BD, supporting the role of psychiatric nursing in family-centred mental healthcare [12]. Psychiatric nursing interventions that reduce caregiver strain, boost PR, and improve emotional outcomes are clinically and scientifically necessary [8, 12]. Given the inverse relationship between CB and PR, interventions that strengthen caregivers’ coping capacities may help reduce burden and improve psychological outcomes [4, 5].

Contemporary management of BD increasingly extends beyond symptom control to emphasize functional recovery, quality of life, and sustained psychosocial functioning [1]. Psychoeducation for relapse prevention is an important component of evidence-based care [10]. It helps caregivers identify early warning signs, support treatment adherence, and encourage sustained recovery [13]. Hence, psychiatric nursing interventions that combine recovery-oriented principles and family psychoeducation can improve the outcomes of both caregivers and patients [12]. Previous evidence indicates that family-focused interventions can improve clinical and psychosocial outcomes in individuals with BD [2]. Family-focused interventions incorporating psychoeducation, communication enhancement, and problem-solving skills have been associated with improved illness management and family functioning in BD [2]. However, comparatively less attention has been directed toward structured nurse-led telenursing interventions that simultaneously address CB, PR, and CRF among family caregivers of individuals with BD. This gap is particularly relevant because available studies have generally examined selected caregiver outcomes or individual intervention approaches rather than these three caregiver outcomes within a single structured nursing intervention [2, 14, 15].

Higher-level evidence supports the potential value of caregiver-focused interventions in BD while also revealing important limitations in the existing evidence base. A systematic review and meta-analysis of psychological interventions for caregivers found a large post-treatment reduction in CB following psychoeducation (Hedges’ g = − 0.80, 95% CI − 1.32 to − 0.27), together with substantial improvements in illness-related knowledge [16]. However, the burden effect was not maintained at follow-up, and considerable methodological heterogeneity and small sample sizes limited the strength of the evidence. Similarly, a randomized controlled trial demonstrated that brief group psychoeducation produced significant reductions in CB and improvements in illness knowledge and self-efficacy [14]. Family-focused interventions have also been associated with reductions in caregivers’ depressive symptoms and improvements in coping-related outcomes [18]. Collectively, these findings support the clinical relevance of structured caregiver interventions but also indicate that evidence remains insufficient regarding integrated programs that simultaneously address CB, PR, and CRF [9, 16]. This gap provides a clear rationale for evaluating a structured, theory-informed psychiatric nursing intervention that targets complementary caregiver outcomes.

The intervention was designed to reduce CB and improve PR and CRF among family caregivers of individuals with BD. It consisted of psychoeducation, cognitive-behavioral techniques, stress-management strategies, communication-skills training, mindfulness exercises, and ongoing telenursing support delivered according to a standardized intervention protocol.

Recent evidence indicates that family caregivers of individuals with BD experience substantial CB and that PR represents an important adaptive resource for coping with caregiving demands. Specifically, Mirhosseini et al. [4] reported moderate CB and low PR among family caregivers of individuals with bipolar I disorder, highlighting the need for interventions that address caregivers’ psychosocial needs. Earlier evidence has similarly documented substantial burden and adverse health and psychological consequences among caregivers of individuals with BD [17].

Previous caregiver interventions have commonly focused on specific therapeutic approaches or selected caregiver outcomes, although evidence supports the potential value of psychoeducation, cognitive-behavioral approaches, and mindfulness-based strategies for improving caregiver resilience and related psychosocial outcomes [9, 16]. Therefore, the present study developed a structured, theory-informed psychiatric nursing intervention integrating these evidence-based components within a standardized and culturally adapted telenursing protocol targeting CB, PR, and CRF. The intervention was standardized using an intervention manual, fidelity-monitoring procedures, and reporting according to the TIDieR checklist to enhance intervention transparency and facilitate replication [18].

The present intervention was theoretically grounded in the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping and complemented by principles of cognitive-behavioral theory, positive psychology, and family-centred care [19–21]. From a stress–coping perspective, CB is influenced not only by objective caregiving demands but also by caregivers’ appraisal of these demands and their available coping resources [19]. Accordingly, psychoeducation, cognitive-behavioral strategies, stress-management techniques, and coping skills training were incorporated to modify maladaptive appraisals and strengthen adaptive coping [19, 20]. PR was conceptualized as a protective resource that may facilitate adaptation to persistent caregiving demands [9, 22]. Positive psychology principles inform activities aimed at recognizing the personal strengths, meaning, gratitude, and rewarding aspects of caregiving [21, 23]. Together, these complementary theoretical perspectives provide a rationale for simultaneously targeting CB, PR, and CRF within an intervention.

Research hypotheses

H1

Family caregivers who receive the innovative psychiatric nursing intervention will report significantly lower CB and significantly higher PR and CRF post-intervention than those who receive routine care.

H2

The innovative psychiatric nursing intervention will produce significant changes over time in CB, PR, and CRF, as reflected by significant time, group, and group × time interaction effects.

H3

CB, PR, and CRF will be significantly associated with one another.

H4

PR and CRF will be significant independent predictors of CB among family caregivers of patients with BD.

Materials and methods

Design

This study employed a two-arm randomized controlled pre-test/post-test design to evaluate the effectiveness of an innovative psychiatric nursing intervention on CB, PR, and CRF among family caregivers of patients with BD. Eligible family caregivers were recruited from the study setting and underwent baseline assessment before random allocation to either the intervention or control group. Outcome measures were assessed at baseline (pre-test) and immediately after completion of the eight-week intervention (post-test) to evaluate changes in the study outcomes over time.

Setting

The study was conducted at a psychiatric and mental health hospital in Meet-Khalf, Menoufia Governorate, Egypt, from September to December 2025. The hospital is a public tertiary mental health facility that provides comprehensive psychiatric treatment services for patients with various mental health conditions. It includes inpatient wards, outpatient psychiatric services, and follow-up treatment programs and serves patients from both urban and rural areas. The study was conducted within the hospital’s organized psychiatric nursing services, which provided the setting for recruitment, assessment, and delivery of the intervention.

Participants

Eligible family caregivers of BD patients were recruited from the study setting. Participants were eligible if they: (1) were the primary family caregiver of a patient diagnosed with BD; (2) had provided continuous care for at least six months; (3) were 18 years of age or older; and (4) had access to a mobile phone and the internet for communication. Participants were excluded if they: (1) had a diagnosed psychiatric disorder; (2) had sensory impairments that could interfere with participation; or (3) had experienced major psychological stress during the previous six months.

Sample size determination

The target sample size was 70 family caregivers, with equal allocation to the intervention and control groups (35 participants per group). The sample-size planning specified a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and statistical power of 90%. However, the original planning records did not retain sufficient documentation of the effect size assumption used to determine the target sample size. Therefore, no retrospective effect-size assumption was introduced for the present report. As a sensitivity analysis, with 35 participants per group, a two-sided α of 0.05, and 90% power, the minimum detectable standardized between-group difference was approximately d = 0.78. The final enrolled sample comprised 70 eligible family caregivers.

Recruitment and allocation

Potentially eligible family caregivers attending the psychiatric and mental health hospital during the study period were approached consecutively and screened according to predefined eligibility criteria. Following the completion of informed consent procedures and baseline assessment, eligible participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group (n = 35) or the control group (n = 35). The random allocation sequence was generated using a computer-generated random-number generator by a researcher who was not involved in participant recruitment or baseline assessment. Group assignment was implemented only after completion of the baseline assessment and according to the predetermined randomization sequence. The allocation sequence was concealed from those involved in participant recruitment and baseline assessment until assignment, thereby reducing the risk of selection bias.

Intervention

The intervention was designed as a structured, nurse-led psychoeducational and psychosocial support program theoretically grounded in the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping and complemented by principles of cognitive-behavioral theory, positive psychology, and family-centred care. These complementary frameworks have guided the development and integration of intervention components that target CB, PR, and CRF. Accordingly, the program aimed to (1) reduce CB, (2) enhance CRF, (3) strengthen PR, and (4) improve adaptive coping strategies and caregiving skills.

The intervention incorporated multiple evidence-based therapeutic components designed to address the three primary study outcomes: CB, PR, and CRF. Psychoeducation, cognitive-behavioral strategies, stress-management techniques, and coping skills training primarily targeted CB and adaptive appraisal. Resilience-building, mindfulness, and problem-solving activities were incorporated to strengthen PR, whereas strengths-based activities, communication-skills training, and recognition of positive caregiving experiences were used to enhance CRF [20, 24].

The intervention was delivered according to a standardized protocol that incorporated structured session plans and fidelity monitoring procedures to promote consistency across participants. The program was culturally adapted for Egyptian family caregivers and delivered using structured session materials and procedures designed to support consistent implementation throughout the intervention period.

The intervention was delivered by a blended telenursing model featuring weekly Zoom sessions, telephone follow-up calls, and continuous support through WhatsApp. To make it more accessible and interesting to the participants, educational materials were prepared in Arabic in multimedia forms. The intervention included examples of caregiving situations typical of Egyptian families that care for individuals with BD to ensure cultural relevance.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 Statement [25]. The intervention was described according to the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist to improve completeness, transparency, and replicability [18]. The intervention was delivered by the principal investigator, a psychiatric nursing researcher experienced in mental health nursing and caregiver psychoeducation. Participants attended eight weekly telenursing sessions, each lasting 60–90 min, delivered via Zoom. Between scheduled sessions, WhatsApp was used exclusively to reinforce educational content, distribute session materials, send reminders, and respond to participants’ questions; it was not intended as an independent therapeutic intervention.

Intervention fidelity was ensured using a standardized intervention manual, structured session plans, and consistent delivery of all intervention components across participants. Adherence was assessed by recording session attendance by participants during the intervention period. The intensity of participant engagement was assessed based on their active involvement in group discussions, completion of session activities, response to follow-up telephone calls, and frequency of their interaction on WhatsApp. These monitoring procedures were developed to ensure consistency in the delivery of the intervention protocol and to minimize variation in the delivery of the intervention among participants.

Control condition

Participants in the control group were provided with the hospital’s usual psychiatric care during the eight weeks of the study. The usual care involved standard outpatient psychiatric follow-up, medication management by the treating psychiatrist, and nursing care following the hospital’s usual clinical procedures. The control group did not receive any structured psychoeducational sessions, telenursing follow-up, stress management training, coping skills training, mindfulness activities, resilience-building strategies, or additional psychosocial support during the study period. Outcome assessments were administered at the same pre-test and post-test time points as for the intervention group.

Outcome measures/instruments

Data collection was performed using the following four primary tools:

Caregiver socio-demographic and caregiving-related characteristics

A structured questionnaire was developed based on relevant literature [5, 26] to assess caregivers’ characteristics, including age, sex, educational level, occupation, income, marital status, duration of caregiving, and relationship to the patient.

Caregiver burden (CB)

CB was assessed using the Modified Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI), originally developed by Zarit et al. [26] and subsequently translated into Arabic by El Razek et al. [27] The scale consists of 10 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (nearly always), with total scores ranging from 0 to 40; higher scores indicate greater perceived CB. Previous research has demonstrated good internal consistency for this scale [28]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.87.

Psychological resilience (PR)

PR was assessed using the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) developed by Connor and Davidson [22]. The scale consists of 25 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4, with total scores ranging from 0 to 100; higher scores indicate greater PR. The scale has demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency in previous research, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89 [29]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.90.

Caregiving rewarding feelings (CRF)

CRFs were assessed using the Positive Aspects of Caregiving Questionnaire (PACQ) [23]. The scale consists of ten items rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4, with total scores ranging from 0 to 40; higher scores indicate stronger positive caregiving experiences, including personal satisfaction, a sense of accomplishment, and emotional closeness. Previous research has demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha of approximately 0.82 [6]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.85.

Validity and reliability of the tools: All study instruments were reviewed for face and content validity by a panel of five experts from the psychiatric nursing department. The expert panel evaluated the clarity, cultural appropriateness, and relevance of the tools used for the target population, and necessary modifications were made before finalizing the instruments. The tools were considered valid based on expert evaluations. The tools were evaluated for reliability using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. Internal consistency reliability was assessed in the present study using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients, which were 0.87 for the Modified ZBI, 0.90 for the CD-RISC, and 0.85 for the PACQ.

Pilot study

A pilot study was conducted on seven patient caregivers (10% of the calculated sample size) to examine the feasibility, clarity, and applicability of the study tools. Participants in the pilot study were selected from the chosen hospital and were not included in the main sampling frame. The pilot was used to determine the time required to administer the questionnaire and to uncover confusion in the items. Some questions were reworded and reordered for clarity based on feedback. The pilot study revealed that the study tools were clear, understandable, and applicable.

Data collection procedures

Data collection was conducted in two phases. At baseline (pretest), eligible participants completed a structured questionnaire and standardized assessment instruments to measure CB, PR, and CRF before the intervention was initiated. Following completion of the baseline assessment, participants were randomly allocated to the intervention or control groups. The intervention group then received an eight-week psychiatric nursing intervention, while the control group received usual psychiatric care. Immediately after completion of the eight-week intervention period, the same assessment instruments were administered to both groups as a post-test to assess changes in the study outcomes. Standardized data collection procedures were used at both assessment points to maintain consistency across the two groups.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Statistics version 26.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics and study variables. Post-test differences between groups were evaluated using the independent samples *t* test. Separate repeated-measures ANOVAs were conducted for CB, PR, and CRF to examine the effects of time, group, and group × time interaction. Separate ANCOVAs were performed for the three post-test outcomes, with the corresponding baseline scores entered as covariates. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships between the study outcomes, and multiple linear regression was conducted to identify independent predictors of CB. Before the regression analysis, assumptions of linearity, normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, independence of errors, and multicollinearity were assessed. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle; because no participant withdrew, the analyzed and randomized samples were identical.

The primary statistical analysis was a repeated-measures ANOVA, which was used to evaluate the effects of time, group, and the group × time interaction on the three study outcomes: CB, PR, and CRF. Independent-sample t-tests and ANCOVAs were performed as secondary analyses to compare post-intervention outcomes and adjust for baseline scores, whereas Pearson’s correlation analysis and multiple linear regression were conducted as exploratory analyses to examine the associations among the study variables and identify independent predictors of CB.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Nursing, Menoufia University, Egypt (Approval No. ERCNMA 1000/6/8/153/25). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment. Participation was voluntary, confidentiality and anonymity were maintained, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty.

Results

A total of 70 eligible family caregivers were enrolled and randomized, with thirty-five participants allocated to each study group. All participants completed the pre- and post-test assessments, and no withdrawals occurred.

Discussion

This study evaluated a structured psychiatric nursing intervention delivered through a telenursing model to family caregivers of individuals with BD. Caregivers in the intervention group reported significantly lower CB and higher PR and CRF at post-test than those receiving routine care. These findings are consistent with previous evidence indicating that structured psychoeducational and family-focused interventions can reduce CB and strengthen psychological well-being and adaptive coping [7, 10–12]. Repeated-measures ANOVAs showed significant effects of time and group as well as significant group × time interactions across CB, PR, and CRF. ANCOVA findings further indicated that between-group differences in all three outcomes remained statistically significant after adjustment for their respective baseline scores. In addition, multiple regression analysis identified PR and CRF as significant independent predictors of post-test CB. Collectively, these findings suggest that the intervention was associated with improvements across complementary caregiver outcomes and support the relevance of addressing CB, PR, and CRF within an integrated psychiatric nursing framework.

The reduction in CB observed in the intervention group is consistent with previous research indicating that structured psychoeducational and family-focused interventions can reduce CB and strengthen caregivers’ knowledge, coping resources, and confidence in managing caregiving demands [3]. From a theoretical perspective, this finding is consistent with the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping, in which the psychological impact of caregiving demands depends partly on caregivers’ appraisal of stressors and the coping resources available to manage them [21]. Cognitive-behavioral strategies may also support more adaptive appraisal and responses to caregiving challenges, thereby contributing to a lower perceived burden [20]. However, previous studies have reported variability in the magnitude and persistence of intervention effects [30, 31], potentially reflecting differences in intervention content, intensity, duration, participant engagement, follow-up periods, and professional support. Accordingly, the present reduction in CB should be interpreted as the effect of the integrated intervention as a whole, rather than attributed to any single therapeutic component.

The improvement in PR observed in the intervention group is consistent with evidence suggesting that structured psychosocial and psychoeducational interventions may strengthen caregivers’ adaptive coping and emotional well-being [32, 33]. From a theoretical perspective, resilience can function as a psychological resource that supports adaptation to prolonged caregiving demands [19, 21]. The intervention incorporated stress management, cognitive-behavioral, mindfulness, and resilience-building strategies intended to strengthen adaptive coping and caregivers’ capacity to manage caregiving-related stress. Nevertheless, the present findings reflect outcomes assessed immediately after the eight-week intervention and should not be interpreted as evidence of sustained improvement in PR. Previous studies have reported variability in intervention effects [5, 11, 17, 34]. Direct comparisons should be made cautiously because of differences in intervention content and intensity, caregiver populations, participant engagement, and follow-up duration.

The increase in CRF observed in the intervention group suggests that positive caregiving experiences may be strengthened along with reductions in perceived burden. This finding is consistent with previous evidence indicating that psychosocial interventions may promote positive caregiving experiences by strengthening coping resources, perceived meaning, and emotional support [7]. From a positive psychology perspective, recognizing the personal strengths, meaning, gratitude, and positive aspects of caregiving may help caregivers identify the rewarding dimensions of their role despite ongoing caregiving demands [21]. Previous research has similarly shown that positive caregiving experiences can coexist with CB, indicating that burden and reward represent related but distinct dimensions of caregiving experience [3, 33, 35, 36]. Accordingly, the improvement in CRF observed in this study should be interpreted as an outcome of the integrated intervention, rather than being attributed to any individual intervention component.

Correlation and regression analyses further demonstrated significant relationships among the three caregiver outcomes. CB was negatively correlated with both PR and CRF, whereas PR was positively correlated with CRF. In the multiple regression analysis, PR and CRF were significant independent negative predictors of post-test CB, indicating that higher levels of resilience and more rewarding caregiving experiences were associated with lower perceived burden. These findings are consistent with previous evidence suggesting that resilience may function as a protective psychological resource that supports adaptive coping among family caregivers [7, 33]. However, because these analyses were associative, they should not be interpreted as establishing causal or mediating mechanisms between PR, CRF, and CB. Rather, the findings suggest that CB, PR, and CRF represent interrelated dimensions that may warrant simultaneous consideration in comprehensive caregiver support interventions.

The present findings suggest that structured telenursing may be a useful component of family-centred psychiatric nursing care. Nevertheless, given the methodological limitations of the present study, further multicenter trials with larger and more diverse samples and extended follow-up periods are required to determine the sustainability, generalizability, and practical applicability of the observed effects.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including its randomized controlled design with concealed allocation, a theory-informed and manualized intervention reported according to the TIDieR checklist, the use of validated outcome measures, and complete follow-up of all participants. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, participants were recruited using convenience sampling from a single psychiatric hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other caregiver populations and healthcare settings. Second, the study outcomes were assessed using self-report measures and may therefore be subject to response and social desirability biases. Third, outcomes were assessed immediately after the intervention without long-term follow-up; consequently, the durability of the observed effects could not be established. Fourth, although random allocation strengthened internal validity, the relatively small sample size and single-center design warrant caution when generalizing the findings. Fifth, the intervention was delivered by the principal investigator, and outcome assessment was not blinded, which may have introduced performance and detection bias. Sixth, the control group received usual care without an attention-control condition, so non-specific effects of contact and attention cannot be excluded. Finally, the trial was registered retrospectively, and the effect-size assumption underlying the original sample-size calculation was not documented. Future multicenter randomized controlled trials with larger and more diverse samples and extended follow-up periods are needed to confirm these findings and evaluate their sustainability and broader applicability.

Conclusions & recommendations

This study found that a structured, theory-informed psychiatric nursing intervention delivered through a telenursing model was associated with lower CB and higher PR and CRF among family caregivers of individuals with BD compared to routine care. The findings support the potential value of integrating psychoeducation, coping skills training, stress management strategies, mindfulness practices, communication skills, and structured telenursing follow-up within caregiver support programs.

These findings should be interpreted in light of the study’s single-center setting, convenience sampling, reliance on self-report measures, and absence of long-term follow-up. Accordingly, the results support further evaluation of structured nurse-led telenursing interventions rather than definitive conclusions regarding their routine implementation. Multicentre randomized controlled trials with larger and more diverse samples and extended follow-up periods are needed to evaluate the sustainability, generalizability, cost-effectiveness, and feasibility of implementing this approach across different mental healthcare settings.

Nursing implications

The results of this study have important implications for psychiatric nursing practice. The observed improvements in CB, PR, and CRF suggest that structured nurse-led psychoeducational and psychosocial interventions could be integrated into routine family-centred mental health services to better support family caregivers of individuals with BD. This study presents a practical, user-friendly method of enhancing caregiver support beyond face-to-face services through a standardized telenursing model, particularly in areas with limited mental health resources.

Psychiatric nurses should be well-trained in psychoeducation, coping skills, stress management, mindfulness-based strategies, and effective therapeutic communication to enhance caregivers’ adaptive coping and psychological well-being. Structured caregiver support programs in routine psychiatric care can help caregivers cope with the burden of long-term caregiving and foster more positive caregiving experiences.

Healthcare organizations should consider integrating evidence-informed telenursing interventions into regular psychiatric nursing services and creating training programs to help nurses provide standardized family-focused interventions with high fidelity. Such initiatives may improve the quality, availability, and continuity of caregiver support within comprehensive mental healthcare systems.
