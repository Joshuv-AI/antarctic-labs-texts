Reasoning-Driven Retrosynthesis Prediction with Large Language Models via Reinforcement Learning
Situo Zhang, Hanqi Li, Lu Chen, Zihan Zhao, Xuanze Lin, Zichen Zhu, Bo Chen, Xin Chen, Kai Yu

Abstract
Retrosynthesis planning, essential in organic synthesis and drug discovery, has greatly benefited from recent AI-driven advancements. Nevertheless, existing methods frequently face limitations in both applicability and explainability. Traditional graph-based and sequence-to-sequence models often lack generalized chemical knowledge, leading to predictions that are neither consistently accurate nor easily explainable. To address these challenges, we introduce RetroDFM-R , a reasoning-based large language model (LLM) designed specifically for chemical retrosynthesis. Leveraging large-scale reinforcement learning guided by chemically verifiable rewards, RetroDFM-R significantly enhances prediction accuracy and explainability. Comprehensive evaluations demonstrate that RetroDFM-R significantly outperforms state-of-the-art methods, achieving a top-1 accuracy of 65.0% on the USPTO-50K benchmark. Double-blind human assessments further validate the chemical plausibility and practical utility of RetroDFM-R ’s predictions. RetroDFM-R also accurately predicts multistep retrosynthetic routes reported in the literature for both real-world drug molecules and perovskite materials. Crucially, the model’s explicit reasoning process provides human-interpretable insights, thereby enhancing trust and practical value in real-world retrosynthesis applications.



Introduction

Retrosynthetic analysis, formalized by Corey
[
1
]
, has long been a cornerstone of molecular synthesis. This strategy systematically deconstructs target molecules step by step into feasible precursors, enabling chemists to design efficient synthetic routes. Retrosynthetic analysis is especially significant in drug discovery and materials science, where it accelerates innovation by streamlining the synthesis of complex natural products and novel compounds. Over the decades, numerous efforts have been made to automate retrosynthesis using computers
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. Traditional approaches primarily rely on manually curated reaction rules and expert knowledge. However, these methods are labor intensive, struggle to generalize across the vast chemical reaction space.

The advent of artificial intelligence (AI) technology has gradually overcome the limitations of manual rule-based approaches that rely solely on expert knowledge. Deep learning-based methods have demonstrated remarkable success in single-step retrosynthesis, the foundational building block of retrosynthetic analysis. Based on the input representation, these methods are typically categorized into two paradigms: graph-based and sequence-based methods. However, each paradigm faces distinct challenges, such as low prediction accuracy or limited interpretability, hindering reliable, explainable, and practically useful retrosynthetic predictions.
Graph-based methods for single-step retrosynthesis treat molecules as graphs, where atoms are nodes and chemical bonds are edges. These approaches leverage graph neural networks (GNNs) to capture the topological and chemical structure of molecules, enabling more accurate modeling of reaction mechanisms. Typically, graph-based methods focus on identifying the reaction center. By preserving rich structural information, these methods closely align with chemists’ intuition and often produce interpretable predictions. Notably, Dai et al.
[
5
]
proposed GLN, which employs a conditional graphical model to learn the rules for applying reaction templates, thereby providing clear interpretability for its predictions. MEGAN
[
6
]
employed an encoder-decoder graph attention model, expressing the retrosynthesis task as a sequence of graph edits. Similarly, Graph2Edits
[
7
]
employed a graph neural to sequentially predict the edits of the productgraph and obtain final reactants according to the predicted edits sequence. G
2
Retro
[
8
]
defined a comprehensive set of reaction center types. It first predicts reaction centers in a target molecule to generate synthons, then sequentially attaches small substructures to those synthons to form reactants. However, graph-based methods often suffer from limited scalability, as GNNs can be computationally intensive and less efficient when applied to large datasets or complex molecular graphs
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
. Additionally, some approaches still rely on reaction templates or heuristic rules, which limits their ability to generalize to a broad range of reaction types
[
5
,
12
]
.

Sequence-based methods approach retrosynthesis as a sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) task
[

,

,

,

,

[
21

.
Liu et al.
[
13

was the first to frame retrosynthesis as a seq2seq problem using long short-term memory (LSTM) networks
[
22

, where the model predicts the SMILES of possible reactants given the SMILES of a target product. With the advent of Transformer-based architectures
[
23
]
, sequence-based methods achieved significant performance improvements. For example, Karpov et al.
[

introduced the Transformer model for retrosynthesis prediction, while Tetko et al.
[

further boosted accuracy through SMILES augmentation and beam search. R-SMILES
[

proposed an aligned SMILES representation between reactants and products, reducing the edit distance and further enhancing the efficiency of Transformer-based retrosynthesis models. The current state-of-the-art method, EditRetro
[
20
]
, reformulates the retrosynthesis task as a string editing problem, iteratively refining SMILES strings to achieve superior performance. However, current sequence-based methods, which rely on end-to-end, fully data-driven learning, often lack explainability in their prediction process. This makes it difficult to analyze the chemical plausibility of the generated reactants.

In recent years, large language models (LLMs) have been widely applied across various domains, breaking through the traditional boundaries of sequence-based models
[
24
,
25
,

,
27
]
. With their vast number of parameters and extensive training data, LLMs encode rich knowledge and demonstrate strong generalization capabilities. Notably, advancements in Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning
[
26
,
28
]
, have enabled LLMs to achieve impressive results in complex tasks like code generation and mathematical problem solving, sometimes even surpassing human performance. These strengths underscore the potential of LLMs to advance retrosynthesis beyond the capacity of smaller models. LLMs can integrate extensive knowledge acquired from large-scale pretraining, while their Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning makes complex retrosynthetic predictions more transparent and explainable. However, general-purpose LLMs still struggle to understand chemical syntax, such as SMILES notation, and to reason effectively in chemical tasks. Recent efforts to develop chemical LLMs
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
, including ChemDFM
[
29
]
and ChemLLM
[
31
]
, have enhanced domain knowledge by leveraging large-scale pretraining on chemical domain texts. However, these chemical LLMs still perform far below smaller, specialized models on retrosynthesis benchmarks. For example, even the best-performing model, ChemDFM, achieves only 17.9% accuracy on the USPTO-50K dataset. Moreover, existing chemical LLMs approach retrosynthesis as an end-to-end prediction task, lacking both human-readable explanations and the ability to provide practical chemical insight.

To address the limitations of previous methods and existing LLMs in retrosynthesis, we propose
RetroDFM-R
, a reasoning-driven LLM specifically tailored for chemical retrosynthesis.
RetroDFM-R
integrates chemical domain knowledge with advanced reasoning capabilities to deliver accurate, explainable retrosynthetic predictions. By explicitly harnessing the reasoning potential of LLMs through reinforcement learning and long-CoT strategies, our model effectively bridges the gap between general-purpose LLMs and specialized chemical retrosynthetic analysis.

The core design of
RetroDFM-R
comprises two main components. First, inspired by recent advances in general-domain reasoning models
[
28
,
34
,
35
]
, we propose a three-stage training paradigm integrating knowledge distillation with reinforcement learning: (1) continual pretraining on retrosynthesis-specific chemical data to enrich domain-specific knowledge and explicitly capture structural and functional insights; (2) supervised fine-tuning on distilled reasoning data from general-domain models to establish an initial chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning foundation; and (3) reinforcement learning to further improve accuracy and promote sound and step-by-step reasoning. Second, we notice that while chemical reactions are primarily described in text, SMILES representations are essential for input and output molecules in LLMs. To bridge the gap between the chemical knowledge in text embedded in LLMs and SMILES representations, we employ targeted SMILES-IUPAC conversion training. This is crucial because IUPAC names inherently encode rich structural and functional group information, which is particularly valuable for retrosynthetic analysis and reasoning
[
1
]
. The complete training framework is illustrated in
Figure

1
(a).

This integration of domain-specific chemical knowledge with CoT reasoning not only enables the model to better utilize its internal chemical knowledge but also introduces transparency into the prediction process, offering explainability and clearer guidance for chemists. Overall,
RetroDFM-R
bridges the gap between chemical knowledge and CoT reasoning, yielding a scalable and explainable solution for retrosynthesis prediction. We evaluate
RetroDFM-R
on two widely used public datasets, USPTO-50K and USPTO-FULL. Our results show that
RetroDFM-R
consistently outperforms both the strongest sequence-based method, EditRetro, and the leading graph-based method, Graph2Edit. Notably,
RetroDFM-R
achieves a top-1 accuracy of 65.0% on USPTO-50K, and delivers substantial improvements in challenging cases involving chirality and ring structures. Double-blind AB tests further indicate that our model’s predictions are generally preferred by human experts. Moreover,
RetroDFM-R
can be applied to multi-step retrosynthesis scenarios, closely aligning with real-world synthesis planning. Taken together, these results highlight
RetroDFM-R
’s practical value and research potential in advancing both the accuracy and explainability of retrosynthetic prediction.

Results

1.1
Overview of
RetroDFM-R

Inspired by the expert reasoning strategies employed by chemists
[
1
]
, we introduce
RetroDFM-R
, a Transformer-based large language model (LLM) specifically designed to leverage advanced chain-of-thought reasoning for retrosynthetic analysis. By emulating the stepwise logic of expert chemists,
RetroDFM-R
first conducts a thorough structural analysis of the target molecule and then identifies plausible retrosynthetic disconnections. The core innovation of
RetroDFM-R
is its ability to substantially improve both prediction accuracy and explainability through explicit CoT reasoning.

To develop
RetroDFM-R
, we built upon ChemDFM
[
29
]
, a state-of-the-art open-source chemical LLM that serves as the foundational architecture for our approach. To equip large language models with fundamental knowledge of chemical retrosynthesis and enhance their understanding and generation of SMILES, we employ a three-stage training strategy: (1) continual pretraining, (2) cold-start reasoning distillation, and (3) large-scale reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, as illustrated in
Figure

1
(a). To incorporate specialized retrosynthesis knowledge, we first perform continual pretraining on retrosynthesis-related chemical data. This stage includes training on SMILES-IUPAC name conversion pairs sourced from the PubChem database
[
36
]
and retrosynthesis predictions derived from the USPTO-FULL
[
5
]
training set. Much of the chemical knowledge learned during pretraining is encoded in human-readable formats such as IUPAC names, which provide detailed information about molecular structure and functional groups. To better align these insights with the SMILES format used for model input and output, we incorporate SMILES-IUPAC conversion tasks. This approach explicitly transfers the structural and functional group information embedded in IUPAC names to the SMILES representations required for retrosynthetic prediction. Next, to kickstart the model’s reasoning abilities, we employ a cold-start distillation process powered by synthetically generated rationales. In this stage, we leverage large general-domain reasoning models that, while lacking domain-specific chemical expertise, excel at step-by-step logical reasoning. We employ an
answer-conditioned distillation
strategy, wherein the model is provided with both the input question and the correct answer, along with explicit instructions to generate a coherent, stepwise reasoning chain leading from problem to solution. In doing so, the model generates a reasoning process as if independently deriving the answer reactants, thereby emulating the analytical thinking of a human expert. The resulting reasoning data is then used to distill and initialize our model’s reasoning capabilities. Finally, we apply large-scale reinforcement learning using the DAPO algorithm
[
34
]
to encourage the generation of detailed, chemically sound retrosynthetic reasoning, guided by verifiable, rule-based reward modeling. This comprehensive strategy enables
RetroDFM-R
to deliver accurate predictions accompanied by interpretable, human-readable rationales for each retrosynthetic decision.

For inference,
RetroDFM-R
takes as input the SMILES representation of the target product molecule. The model first generates an explicit, detailed reasoning sequence, encapsulated within
<think>…</think>
tags. This is followed by the final predicted reactants, presented in SMILES format within
<answer>…</answer>
tags. During its reasoning process,
RetroDFM-R
closely mirrors the approach of expert chemists by first thoroughly analyzing the molecular structure and then identifying plausible retrosynthetic disconnections. As shown in
Figure

1
(b), the model systematically predicts key reaction sites and proposes breakdown strategies based on well-established reaction types, such as named reactions or classic synthetic methods.
RetroDFM-R
also evaluates the chemical feasibility of the proposed transformation and considers the accessibility of the corresponding reactants. This structured and interpretable reasoning not only enhances prediction accuracy but also provides clear, human-understandable rationales—offering a critical advantage in real-world applications where chemists demand transparency and trust in model predictions.

1.2
Datasets

We evaluate our method’s performance on retrosynthesis tasks using two well-established chemical reaction datasets: USPTO-50K
[
37
]
and USPTO-FULL
[
5
]
, both derived from U.S. patent records spanning 1976 to 2016. USPTO-50K contains 50,016 manually curated organic reactions represented in SMILES notation, offering high-quality labeled data with accurate atom mappings between products and reactants. In contrast, USPTO-FULL comprises approximately 1 million reactions, which inherently include noise due to the raw extraction process from patent documents.

For both datasets, we adopt the same train/validation/test splits as previous works
[
3
,
5
,
19
,
7
,
20
]
. USPTO-50K is partitioned into 40K/5K/5K for training, validation, and testing, respectively, while USPTO-FULL is split into 800K/100K/100K. Due to its rigorous curation, USPTO-50K serves as the primary benchmark for single-step retrosynthesis evaluation. Meanwhile, the larger and more diverse USPTO-FULL dataset provides a valuable benchmark for assessing model scalability and generalization. Together, these complementary datasets support a comprehensive evaluation of model performance across different aspects of retrosynthesis prediction. To prevent test-set leakage, we carefully cleaned our training dataset by removing all reactions whose products appear in the test sets of USPTO-50K
[
37
]
, USPTO-MIT
[

]
, and USPTO-FULL

5
]

RetroDFM-R
’s reasoning process provides explainability and insights into chemical retrosynthesis

A significant advantage of
RetroDFM-R
over previous retrosynthesis models is its ability to not only generate chemically feasible precursor reactants, but also provide clear and detailed rationales for its synthetic decisions. Leveraging the advanced reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs),
RetroDFM-R
provides human-readable explanations, allowing chemists to understand the model’s thinking process clearly. This approach enables trustworthy AI-assisted retrosynthesis, offering practical insights for chemists.

Similar to expert chemists,
RetroDFM-R
first thoroughly analyzes the target molecule’s structure and then identifies plausible retrosynthetic breakdown strategies, often referencing established reaction types such as named reactions or classical synthetic methods. Moreover, the model may propose multiple viable synthetic routes, along with potential catalysts and suitable reaction conditions required to realize these transformations.

We illustrate the reasoning capabilities and advantages of
RetroDFM-R
through two representative cases selected from the USPTO-50K dataset.
Figure

To rigorously evaluate the quality of retrosynthetic predictions from

For each reaction pair, two experts independently assessed the feasibility and chemical quality of the proposed retrosynthetic routes, choosing from four evaluation categories: “A is better”, “B is better”, “Both are equally good” or “Both are equally bad/unclear”. Detailed evaluation instructions are provided in Supplementary Section E. The AB tests were conducted in a double-blind manner, such that neither the annotators nor the experimenters knew the origin (model-generated or ground truth) of each retrosynthetic solution. In cases of disagreement between the first two evaluators, the third expert provided the final decision.

2
Discussion

We present
RetroDFM-R
, a reasoning-driven large language model designed for chemical retrosynthesis.
RetroDFM-R
bridges the gap between general-purpose, large-scale pretrained LLMs and the specialized requirements of retrosynthetic analysis. Adopting a step-by-step chain-of-thought reasoning approach,
RetroDFM-R
mirrors the workflow of expert chemists. It thoroughly analyzes molecular structures, identifies plausible reaction sites and retrosynthetic disconnections, and can propose appropriate reaction conditions or alternative synthetic routes. Importantly, the model not only generates chemically feasible precursor reactants, but also provides clear, detailed rationales for its synthetic decisions. This results in human-readable explanations that enable chemists to understand the model’s thought process and gain actionable insights.

To train
RetroDFM-R
, we leverage large-scale reinforcement learning to incentivize the model’s reasoning capabilities. Our three-stage training recipe consists of (1) continual pretraining to build domain-specific knowledge tailored for retrosynthesis tasks; (2) cold-start reasoning data to instill correct reasoning patterns in the model’s outputs; and (3) reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards to further enhance the model’s reasoning ability and encourage the discovery of diverse retrosynthetic strategies.

We conducted comprehensive experiments on the USPTO-50K benchmark. The results demonstrate that
RetroDFM-R
significantly outperforms the previous state-of-the-art, achieving a top-1 exact match accuracy of 65.0% and a top-10 accuracy of 92.5%.
RetroDFM-R
also substantially surpasses both leading proprietary LLMs and existing chemical-specific LLMs. The model also maintains strong performance on the larger, noisier USPTO-FULL dataset, highlighting its robustness and generalizability.

A unique strength of
RetroDFM-R
lies in its explicit reasoning ability. The model not only predicts feasible retrosynthetic routes, but also provides recommendations for reagents, reaction conditions, and considers alternative strategies. In multi-step retrosynthesis planning scenarios, including recent real-world medicinal chemistry cases,
RetroDFM-R
consistently delivers accurate and reliable predictions. These results underscore
RetroDFM-R
’s potential to advance AI-driven chemical synthesis planning in medicinal and pharmaceutical applications.

Figure 7:
Illustration of hallucination in the reasoning process of
RetroDFM-R
. Hallucinated contents are highlighted in red.

Although
RetroDFM-R
demonstrates the promise of reasoning-enabled LLMs for retrosynthesis tasks, several important challenges remain. First, similar to other large language models,
RetroDFM-R
may generate chemically invalid or hallucinated content. For example, as illustrated in
Figure

7
, the model occasionally references a chlorine atom that does not exist in the product compound. In fact, while Vilsmeier–Haack aldehyde formation commonly employs phosphoryl chloride to activate dimethylamide, phosphoryl chloride is notably absent from the model’s reasoning process. It may also produce reasoning steps that are irrelevant or disconnected from the final predicted reactants. Furthermore, as a sequence generation model,
RetroDFM-R
may occasionally output infeasible reaction pathways. Incorporating cheminformatics validation tools or expert rules into the reasoning workflow could help further improve reliability and synthetic plausibility. Secondly,
RetroDFM-R
does not yet fully utilize the extensive chemical knowledge available in reaction databases and literature. Incorporating retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) or similar methods could allow the model to dynamically access and integrate information from external sources, further enhancing its accuracy and practical value.

In summary,
RetroDFM-R
demonstrates the power of reasoning-driven LLMs to enhance both the accuracy and interpretability of retrosynthetic predictions. With continual advancements in AI and cheminformatics, we hope our approach will inspire further progress toward fully automated, reliable, and explainable systems for chemical synthesis planning.

Methods

We first present the detailed pipeline for
RetroDFM-R
, covering data preparation, and the overall workflow. Next, we describe the large-scale reinforcement learning algorithm employed and the model architecture. Finally, we outline the inference procedure used by
RetroDFM-R
. An overview of the training and prediction workflow is illustrated schematically in
Figure

1
.

3.1
Pipeline for
RetroDFM-R

As illustrated in
Figure

1
(a), we adopt a three-stage training pipeline to develop
RetroDFM-R
: (1) continual pretraining, (2) cold-start reasoning distillation, and (3) large-scale reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards.

In the continual pretraining stage, we assemble a large-scale dataset encompassing three primary tasks: SMILES-to-IUPAC translation, IUPAC-to-SMILES translation, and end-to-end retrosynthesis prediction. The name conversion tasks bridge the knowledge gap between SMILES and IUPAC representations, recognizing that much of the chemical knowledge learned during large-scale pretraining is embedded in the human-readable IUPAC format, whereas SMILES strings are essential for model input and output. To this end, we curated over 10 million canonicalized SMILES–IUPAC pairs from PubChem
[
36
]
, creating a robust dataset for training accurate molecular translation capabilities. For retrosynthesis prediction, we curated reactions from the USPTO-FULL dataset, implementing a rigorous cleaning pipeline to filter out reactions with invalid atom mappings, abnormal product sizes, or extraction artifacts. To enhance model robustness, we adopt the same pretraining and data augmentation strategies from previous work
[
16
,
19
,
20
]
, applying 10-fold R-SMILES augmentation
[
19
]
. This technique aligns the starting atoms of reactants and products, facilitating enumeration and effectively reducing the complexity of learning reaction patterns. To prevent test-set leakage, we excluded all reactions whose products appear in the test sets of USPTO-50K
[
37
]
, USPTO-MIT
[
38
]
, and USPTO-FULL
[
5
]
. The resulting pretraining dataset comprises approximately 24 million examples.

In the cold-start reasoning distillation stage, we sample a subset of reactions from USPTO-FULL and leverage DeepSeek-R1
[
28
]
, a large general-domain reasoning model. Using answer-conditioned distillation, we provide DeepSeek-R1 with both the product SMILES, IUPAC name, and the corresponding ground-truth reactants, along with explicit instructions to generate detailed, stepwise reasoning. This reasoning covers structural analysis, identification of key reaction sites, and proposed reaction conditions, enabling the model to articulate logical pathways from product to reactants as if reasoning independently. By training on this carefully curated set of 165,000 high-quality reasoning examples,
RetroDFM-R
achieves notable gains in both interpretability and chemical reasoning performance.

For the reinforcement learning stage, we further augment the dataset by enumerating different SMILES roots, generating diverse representations and prompts for each product molecule. Following previous studies
[
19
,
20
]
, we apply 20-fold augmentation to USPTO-50K and 5-fold to USPTO-FULL. All augmented SMILES are canonicalized using the RDKit toolkit
[
56
]
to ensure chemical validity and consistency. Datasets generated during pretraining and cold-start distillation are used to train
RetroDFM-R
via supervised fine-tuning (SFT), while reinforcement learning is performed using the DAPO algorithm.

3.2
Reinforcement learning with DAPO

We train
RetroDFM-R
using large-scale reinforcement learning, optimizing the model with rule-based, verifiable rewards to ensure training robustness and reliability. The reward function consists of two components: a correctness reward
R_{\text{correct}}
, which verifies whether the predicted reactant SMILES matches the ground truth, and a format reward
R_{\text{format}}
, which checks if the output follows the required format. For a given retrosynthesis question, we define the reward with ground-truth reactant
a
and model outputs
o
with

R(o,a)=R_{\text{correct}}(o,a)+\lambda R_{\text{format}}(o),

(1)

where
\lambda
is a coefficient that weights the two parts of rewards. To calculate the correctness reward, we extract the content between the special tags
<answer>
and
</answer>
from the model’s output, canonicalize both the predicted and ground-truth SMILES using RDKit, and assign
R_{\text{correct}}(o,a)=1
if they match and
0
otherwise. The format reward
R_{\text{format}}(o)
is determined by applying regular expressions to verify compliance with the prescribed output structure "
<think>…</think>\n<answer>…</answer>
". A correctly formatted output yields
R_{\text{format}}(o)=1
, otherwise
0
.

We adopt the Dynamic sAmpling Policy Optimization (DAPO) algorithm
[
34
]
for reinforcement learning. DAPO is a policy-gradient method that improves upon the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm
[
57
]
. For each input question
q
, ground-truth answer
a
from the data distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and a group of
G
sampled outputs
\{o_{i}\}^{G}_{i=1}
, DAPO optimizes the following objective to improve the policy.

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\text{DAPO}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(q,a)\sim\mathcal{D},\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot|q)}\left[\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{G}|o_{i}|}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_{i}|}\min\left(r_{i,t}(\theta)\hat{A}_{i,t},\>c\cdot\hat{A}_{i,t}\right)\right]

(2)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=\text{clip}\left(r_{i,t}(\theta),1-\epsilon_{\text{low}},1+\epsilon_{\text{high}}\right)

(3)

s.t.

\displaystyle 0<\left|\left\{o_{i}\mid\text{is\_equivalent}(a,o_{i})\right\}\right|<G,

where
\epsilon
denotes the clipping range for the importance sampling ratio
r_{i,t}(\theta)
. The advantage
\hat{A}_{i,t}
for the
i
-th response is computed by normalizing the group-level rewards
{R}_{i=1}^{G}
. The formulas for
r_{i,t}(\theta)
and
\hat{A}_{i,t}
are as follows:

r_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})},\hat{A}_{i,t}=\frac{R_{i}-\text{mean}(\{R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G})}{\text{std}(\{R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G})}.

(4)

DAPO advances classical Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
[
58
]
and GRPO by introducing asymmetric clipping ranges. This strategy promotes the exploration of low-probability tokens, thereby increasing output diversity. Additionally, DAPO improves training efficiency by filtering out prompts for which all group outputs receive identical rewards, thus avoiding ineffective or redundant training samples.

3.3
Model architecture and training

We build
RetroDFM-R
on top of ChemDFM-v1.5
[
29
]
, which shares the same architecture as Llama3-8B
[
59
]
. The model is a decoder-only transformer
[
23
]
comprising 32 layers, with a model dimension
d_{\text{model}}=4096
and a feed-forward dimension
d_{\text{ffn}}=14,336
. It uses a vocabulary size of 128,000 and employs group query attention (GQA)
[
60
]
with 8 key/value heads and 32 query heads. We train
RetroDFM-R
using the OpenRLHF framework
[
61
]
with full-parameter tuning. The continual pretraining stage is trained for one epoch, cold-start distillation for three epochs, and reinforcement learning for one epoch. Additional details are provided in the Supplementary Section H.

3.4
Inference augmentation

To generate diverse predicted reactants for each input SMILES, previous end-to-end sequence-based approaches typically apply beam search directly to the model’s output of reactant SMILES
[
16
,
19
,
20
]
. However, since
RetroDFM-R
outputs both an explicit reasoning process and the final answer, directly applying beam search often yields identical reactant predictions, differing only in the reasoning text rather than the reactant prediction. To address this, we adopt a two-stage approach that combines repeated sampling with partial beam search, enabling the generation of varied and plausible reactant predictions.

During the repeated sampling phase, we set the model’s sampling temperature to a non-zero value (empirically chosen as 1.0) and sample
k_{s}
outputs to obtain diverse reasoning paths. In the partial beam search phase, we then initiate beam search from the
<answer>
tag for each sampled reasoning path, generating
k_{b}
distinct reactant SMILES predictions per path. This two-stage inference procedure ultimately produces
k_{s}\times k_{b}
responses.

Following the augmentation strategy in Augmented Transformer
[
16
]
and R-SMILES
[
19
]
, for each product in the test set, we select different root atoms and traversal orders to obtain
k_{a}
distinct representations of the same product molecule. This results in a total of
k_{a}\times k_{s}\times k_{b}
predictions for each product, with many predictions being duplicates. To rank the predictions, we aggregate the beam search rankings and occurrence frequencies for each unique reactant prediction
y
. Specifically, for each unique canonical reactant
y
, we compute a score:

\text{Score}(y)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{y}}\frac{1}{1+\alpha\cdot(i-1)},

(5)

where
\mathcal{I}_{y}
denotes the set of all ranking positions from the partial beam searches in which reactant
y
appears,
i
is the rank within each beam, and
\alpha
is a decay parameter (set to 1.0 in our experiments). The final ranking of all unique reactant predictions is obtained by sorting them in descending order according to their aggregated scores:

\text{Ranking}=\text{argsort}\left\{{\text{Score}(y):y\in\mathcal{Y}}\right\},

(6)

where
\mathcal{Y}
is the set of all unique canonical reactant predictions obtained from the augmented inference. Using these rankings, we can then calculate the top-
k
accuracy metrics. This approach is specific to sequence-based methods. Supplementary Figure 1 provides an example illustrating the workflow of the inference module.

RetroDFM-R

Answer-conditioned distillation prompt

You are an expert in organic chemistry, specifically skilled in retrosynthetic analysis. Your goal is to carefully analyze a provided product molecule and identify the most likely reactants using rigorous retrosynthetic reasoning. Your response should demonstrate logical, detailed, and scientifically accurate analysis.

Instructions:

1. Product Structure Analysis:

- Clearly identify and describe key functional groups within the provided molecule.

- Highlight any reactive sites, electron-withdrawing/electron-donating groups, and resonance or steric effects that may influence its reactivity.

2. Retrosynthetic Analysis:

- Break down the molecule into simpler precursors using retrosynthetic analysis, clearly indicating the bonds targeted for disconnection.

- Propose reactants that are chemically feasible, stable, and commercially available or readily synthesized.

- Provide a comprehensive explanation of your proposed reaction pathway, specifying the reaction type (e.g., nucleophilic substitution, cycloaddition), roles of each reactant, and required catalysts or reaction conditions.

- Justify the feasibility of your proposed synthesis by addressing thermodynamic and kinetic favorability, potential side reactions, and expected reaction yield and selectivity.
