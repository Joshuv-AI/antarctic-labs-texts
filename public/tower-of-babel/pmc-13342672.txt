NETosis activity in patients with glioblastoma multiforme compared to healthy controls
Authors: Jakub Stawicki, Edyta Wawrzyniak-Gramacka, Barbara Morawin, Aleksandra Grzmil, Anna Tylutka, Agnieszka Zembron-Lacny, Pawel Jarmuzek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neutrophil extracellular traps derived from NETosis are present in various clinical conditions characterized by elevated concentrations of circulating DNA (cirDNA), including glioblastoma multiforme (GBM). Therefore, the study was designed to explain whether activated neutrophils are the primary source of cirDNA release into the bloodstream and to explore the associations between the cirDNA fragments and clinicopathological characteristics. Preoperative plasma samples from 50 patients (GBM 65.7 ± 10.5 years) and 80 healthy individuals (HC 70.3 ± 5.2 years) were used to determine the levels of total cirDNA, methylated cirDNA, oxidative cirDNA, and myeloperoxidase (MPO). The GBM patients demonstrated significantly higher cirDNA and MPO compared to HC. In GBM, methylated cfDNA represented 7% of total cirDNA whereas in HC it accounted for 12%. The Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis showed a higher mortality risk with cirDNA of 958–2540 ng/mL. The ROC analysis indicated the optimal cirDNA threshold of 930 ng/mL for cfDNA (AUC = 0.951, p < 0.001), indicating a very high diagnostic value (sensitivity 88% and specificity 91.2%). Elevated plasma concentrations of total cirDNA and MPO may reflect the intensity of NETosis, with high MPO level supporting the concept that activated neutrophils contribute substantially to the pool of circulating cirDNA pool in GBM.

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most common malignant primary brain tumour in adults, characterised by an aggressive course with a median survival of 217 days and median age of onset of 63 years for the Polish population. With regard to the age, the survival probability was reported to decrease by 50% in patients aged 63 years and the disease is considered incurable with a 5-year survival rate of only 7.2%. The 5-year survival rate for the overall population is around 5.5%1,2. Age is a well-established negative prognostic factor for GBM, with younger patients generally experiencing longer survival than older individuals. This association is attributed to such factors as increased co-morbidities and frailty in older adults which reduce treatment tolerance and recovery following surgery and other therapies. Although the mechanisms underlying the impact of chronological age are not fully understood, age consistently emerges as a significant factor in multivariate analyses, where increased age is consistently associated with a higher risk of mortality3. Our retrospective study showed that the survival probability declines considerably faster in older patients (63–90 years) than younger (23–63 years) with high-grade glioma1. Diagnosis and monitoring of GBM patients typically rely on imaging techniques which cannot reliably discriminate between true tumour progression and pseudoprogression—treatment-related changes that mimic tumour progression4. This can lead to misinterpretation of therapy response and delay clinical interventions. Therefore, the assessment of molecular markers at the tumour tissue is necessary for accurate GBM diagnosis, prognostication and prediction of treatment response5–7.

Some studies have highlighted potential benefits of the measurement of circulating cell-free DNA (cirDNA) fragments which are released from the tumour and healthy cells into the bloodstream as a result of cell death such as necrosis, apoptosis, neutrophil extracellular trap formation (NETosis) or active secretion via extracellular vesicles7–12. According the American Society of Clinical Oncology and the College of American Pathologists, the largest fraction of cfDNA in patients with cancer is derived from the tumour tissue of origin13. The extracellular DNA pool includes total circulating DNA, circulating tumour-derived cell free DNA (ctDNA) and circulating mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). Under normal conditions, the concentration of cirDNA is typically low (~ 100 pg/µL) but it significantly increases in pathological conditions, including cancer, where levels can exceed 1000 pg/µL14. The first study carried out in our laboratory demonstrated a higher concentration of circulating 50-700 bp DNA fragments which were indicated as the largest fraction of DNA fragments derived from the tumour tissue15. Surprisingly, 50–700 bp DNA fragments constituted a lower percentage of total cirDNA in GBM patients (74.38%) compared to healthy controls (86.87%). Therefore, we hypothesise that DNA fragments > 700 bp (~ 25% of the total cirDNA content) may have a higher diagnostic value in brain tumours15.

Longer DNA fragments could be the products of NETosis associated with activated neutrophils in peripheral blood. During NETosis, the release of nuclear DNA from neutrophils requires chromatin decondensation via PAD4-mediated histone citrullination16, nuclear envelope rupture through phosphorylation of PKCα-mediated lamin B and CDK4/6-mediated lamin A/C17,18, and, finally, cytoskeleton-related plasma membrane breakdown19. Understanding the mechanistic drivers of NETosis may be helpful in elucidating the role of neutrophils in glioblastoma and in developing therapeutic strategies targeting neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)20. Interestingly, NETs are found in the same pathological conditions where high concentrations of circulating cirDNA have been reported, including autoimmune diseases, inflammatory disorders, sepsis, thrombotic illnesses, and cancers12. Pastor et al.12 postulated that in cancer patients, higher neutrophil counts and their prolonged lifespan, particularly when activated in tumour microenvironment, could enhance the formation of NETs and their by-products. These could play a crucial role in circulating DNA production, notably in the degradation of the web-like chromatin derived from NETs in blood. Previously, we observed that the release of cirDNA into the circulation is proportional to the severity of inflammaging21 and neutrophilia in glioblastoma15. In GBM patients, an increase in neutrophils was associated with poor prognosis1. NETs promote the occurrence and invasion of GBM by expressing NET-related proteins. Neutrophil-derived NETs in cancer can act as a physical blockade, preventing immune cell infiltration and restricting the interaction between lymphocytes T and cancer cells22.

This study was designed 1) to explain whether activated neutrophils and NETosis are the main source of release of cirDNA into the bloodstream in comparison with healthy individuals, 2) to explore the associations between the circulating cirDNA fragments, including methylated and oxidized DNA fractions, and pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV), along with clinicopathological characteristics. Furthermore, performing a detailed comparative power analysis between cirDNA and other immunoinflammatory indices to determine their prognostic and predictive value in GBM could enhance risk stratification and treatment selection and ultimately improve the care and prognosis of patients with this aggressive form of brain tumour.

Results

White blood cell count-derived inflammation marker

White blood cells were found to fall within the referential ranges and were significantly higher in GBM than HC. The counts of neutrophils and lymphocytes were significantly elevated in GBM whereas monocyte and platelet counts did not differ between groups. This suggests that the main source of extracellular traps is neutrophils, not monocytes. Among the investigated immune cells, neutrophils exceeded reference values and showed the most pronounced changes reflected by alternations in PIV (Table 1). Changes in white blood cells resulted in a sixfold increase in PIV in GBM compared to HC. PIV achieved values above 545, which, according to Kavak et al.23, indicates a critical role of this systemic inflammation marker in disease progression. CRP, a commonly used inflammation marker, did not differ between GBM (2.978 ± 2.348 mg/L) and HC (2.372 ± 1.661 mg/L) groups. The ROC analysis indicated that the optimal PIV threshold value corresponded to 339 (AUC = 0.769, p < 0.001). The values of sensitivity (68%) and specificity (86.2%) for PIV indicated a low level of false positive results during its diagnostic procedure. DNA analysis

GBM patients displayed a fourfold higher concentration of total circulating cell-free DNA (Table 2) which was more scattered than in HC (Fig. 2). No significant differences in cirDNA level were observed between females and males both in GBM and HC groups. The majority of the GBM patients (74%) demonstrated a high concentration of cirDNA > 1000 pg/µL, which may aggravate immunoinflammatory reactivity. In HC group, cirDNA > 1000 pg/µL occurred only in 4%. Methylated cirDNA tended to increase in GBM but it was present in a lower percentage (7%) in contrast to HC where methylated cirDNA accounted for 12% of the total peripheral cirDNA content. This resulted in a two-fold higher concentration of non-methylated cirDNA in GBM patients. The level of oxidised cirDNA, as a marker of oxi-inflammation, did not differ between patients and healthy individuals (Table 2), suggesting no significant changes in oxidants generation such as reactive oxygen species. Discussion

Chronic inflammation contributes to the development and progression of cancer through multiple mechanisms, including DNA damage and mutational changes, inhibition of apoptosis by inflammation-activated cytokines and growth factors, promotion of angiogenesis via mediators such as prostaglandins, inhibition of immune recognition, and altered immune responses that reduce cancer cells clearance. Additionally, inflammation supports tumour growth and spread through microenvironmental changes26. Owing to their critical roles in the inflammatory response, platelets, neutrophils, monocytes, and lymphocytes—each exhibiting distinct characteristics that influence the immune system- constitute the primary components of peripheral blood elements in this process27. Consequently, these immune-inflammatory factors have been correlated with an elevated risk of tumour progression and adverse prognostic outcomes in GBM28. The results of our previous study and meta-analysis, wherein combinations of these markers were measured through peripheral blood, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), consistently indicate that these inflammation-related indices exhibit significant prognostic and predictive capabilities in the management of GBM1,29,30. The findings from these investigations further corroborate the concept that scores derived from quadruple-marker formulation, such as the PIV, offer more robust prognostic and predictive insight into glioblastoma survival and clinical outcomes than indices generated from single systemic inflammatory markers, such as CRP, neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes and platelets. PIV was sixfold higher in patients with glioblastoma, and demonstrated the ability to independently stratify newly diagnosed GBM patients into different groups based on their predicted survival outcomes after treatment. However, Kaplan–Meier survival curves analysis did not reveal the clinical utility of PIV for estimating overall survival in glioblastoma. This contrasts with our previous study, where patients aged 63 years (cut-off value) with inflammatory marker NRL at values ranging from 4.56 to 26.72 exhibited a significantly shorter overall survival time1.

Circulating cell-free DNA has emerged as a promising biomarker in oncology, reflecting both tumour burden and host-derived inflammatory processes. In our study, GBM patients displayed a four-fold increase in cirDNA compared with healthy controls, with the majority (74%) exceeding the threshold of 1000 pg/µL, while only 4% of controls reached this value. The findings are consistent with previous reports that cirDNA concentrations rise markedly in pathological conditions, particularly in cancer, where necrosis, apoptosis, and tumour–host interactions drive the release of DNA fragments into the bloodstream13,14. The elevated cirDNA observed here may therefore reflect both direct tumour cell turnover and systemic immunoinflammatory activity. Interestingly, while total cirDNA was substantially higher in GBM patients, its composition differed from that of controls. We observed a predominance of non-methylated cirDNA in GBM, whereas a higher proportion of methylated cfDNA was detected in healthy individuals. This shift suggests that tumour- or inflammation-derived cirDNA in GBM is more frequently unmethylated, which is in line with the reports that aberrant cirDNA methylation patterns can serve as hallmarks of malignancy8,9. Furthermore, the absence of significant changes in oxidized cirDNA between GBM patients and healthy controls indicates that reactive oxygen species–mediated DNA damage may not be a major driver of cirDNA release in glioblastoma. Survival analysis revealed that higher cirDNA concentrations (958–2540 ng/mL) were associated with an increased risk of mortality; however, overall survival times did not differ significantly between patients with high and low cirDNA levels. This paradox highlights the complexity of cirDNA as a biomarker in GBM, suggesting that while cirDNA levels may reflect disease activity, they may not independently predict long-term outcomes. Similar observations have been reported in other malignancies, where cirDNA has demonstrated stronger utility for diagnosis and disease monitoring than for survival prognostication7.

High neutrophil counts in GBM patients indicate that these immune cells are the main source of cirDNA and the cause of systemic inflammation. The progression-free survival (PFS) in cancer treatment is often influenced by systemic inflammatory status with high neutrophil counts generally correlating with shorter overall survival (OS)31–34. PFS in glioblastoma is typically short due to the disease’s aggressive nature, with a median PFS of approximately 5.3 months for newly diagnosed patients. PFS measures the time from treatment initiation to tumor progression, which is often rapid, with 12-month OS typically being a key endpoint35. Previously, we determined significant relationships of high neutrophil–lymphocyte ratio (NLR) with the survival rate in GBM patients. A poorer prognosis was observed in the patients with NLR ≥ 4.56 × 103/µL when compared with the patients with NLR < 4.56 × 103/µL. NLR showed the greatest for predicting survival rate in GBM patients compared to other markers of inflammatory status31,36.

NETosis has been increasingly recognized as a contributor to tumor progression, acting not only as an antimicrobial defence mechanism but also as a source of pro-tumorigenic factors. In our cohort, NETosis markers, including complexes of cirDNA and MPO, were approximately two-fold higher in GBM patients than in healthy controls. According to the classification proposed by Zhang et al.24, healthy individuals in our study displayed MPO levels within the “low” range, while GBM patients reached “medium” levels, underscoring the disease-associated increase in neutrophil activation. These results are consistent with prior experimental and clinical evidence suggesting that NETs accumulate in the tumour microenvironment and may support glioblastoma growth and immune evasion12,22. The role of MPO in glioblastoma is currently unclear, as it has been postulated to be both pro- and anti-tumorigenic. Although MPO promotes GBM growth under chronic inflammatory conditions, its role in the context of the acute inflammation following radiotherapy remains unclear. It is possible that by creating a highly oxidative and inflammatory microenvironment, MPO may contribute to the innate immune response that helps restrain tumour growth37. Notably, MPO levels were higher in older GBM patients, in line with earlier observations that MPO activity increases with age38. This finding supports the notion that age-related changes in neutrophil biology may further enhance NETosis and its downstream effects in elderly glioblastoma patients, potentially contributing to their poorer prognosis. Receiver operating characteristic analysis revealed that cirDNA exhibited an outstanding diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.951) with high sensitivity (88%) and specificity (91.2%), outperforming MPO (AUC = 0.790). These results highlight cirDNA as a clinically superior marker for distinguishing GBM patients from healthy individuals, whereas MPO may reflect broader age- and inflammation-related changes rather than serving as a tumour-specific biomarker. Importantly, this aligns with reports that cirDNA provides a direct link to tumour biology, while NET-associated proteins such as MPO may act as supportive but less specific indicators of systemic inflammation12,13. Based on these findings, we suggest that increased cirDNA release is a prominent feature of GBM pathophysiology, which may indicate NETosis-related processes. The data further support a model in which neutrophil activation contributes to the cirDNA pool, thereby linking inflammation, NETosis, and tumour biology in glioblastoma.

In summary, our findings highlight the multifaceted role of systemic inflammation and neutrophil activity in glioblastoma, linking PIV and cirDNA as interconnected biomarkers that shape disease biology. While PIV confirmed the presence of heightened systemic inflammation in GBM patients, it did not provide prognostic value for overall survival, suggesting that composite blood indices may capture the inflammatory milieu but do not fully translate into clinical outcomes. In contrast, cirDNA emerged as both a sensitive marker of disease activity and a robust discriminator between GBM patients and healthy individuals, particularly when considered in the context of its methylation status. Elevated cirDNA levels may also reflect increased NETosis, with neutrophil-derived MPO supporting the concept that activated neutrophils contribute substantially to the circulating DNA pool. Collectively, these results point toward cirDNA, rather than classical inflammatory indices, as a clinically meaningful biomarker with potential diagnostic and monitoring applications in GBM. Future studies should aim to validate these findings in larger, longitudinal cohorts and explore whether cirDNA and NETosis-related markers can be integrated into multimodal biomarker panels, alongside imaging and molecular profiling, to improve diagnosis, treatment monitoring, and risk stratification in glioblastoma. Additionally, mechanistic investigations into the interplay between neutrophil biology and tumour–immune interactions may uncover novel therapeutic strategies targeting inflammation-driven pathways in this highly aggressive brain tumour.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, circulating DNA was quantified using a fluorometric method that measures total cirDNA and does not distinguish between nuclear-derived and mitochondrial-derived DNA fragments. As mitochondrial DNA may also be released during inflammatory processes and cellular stress, its contribution to the circulating DNA pool in glioblastoma cannot be excluded. Second, the study design did not include more specific assays for NET formation, such as detection of citrullinated histone H3-DNA complexes or neutrophil elastase-DNA complexes. Therefore, plasma cirDNA and MPO should be interpreted as indirect indicators potentially associated with neutrophil activation and NETosis-related processes, rather than definitive markers of NET formation. Moreover, the lack of a comparison group of patients with lower-grade gliomas limited our ability to assess the specificity and clinical relevance of circulating DNA across different stages of glioma progression. Nevertheless, all samples were processed using a standardized protocol, which minimizes methodological variability between groups, and this study provides a comprehensive assessment of circulating DNA and NETosis-related markers in glioblastoma, contributing to a better understanding of their potential role in tumour-associated inflammation.

Material and methods

Study population

The study was carried out in fifty patients aged 65.7 ± 10.5 years (females n = 32, males n = 18) with newly diagnosed high-grade gliomas (GBM) who underwent an operation in Neurosurgery Centre University Hospital in Zielona Gora (Table 4). GBM diagnosis was established by an experienced neuropathologist based on WHO criteria, including immunohistochemical labelling for IDH1 and IDH2 (isocitrate dehydrogenases), and Ki-67 (proliferative index) in accordance with the criteria for diagnosing glioblastoma set by the oncological standards of the National Health Fund Poland. The overall survival was defined as the interval between the diagnosis and death. For the patients who had not died prior to the last follow-up, the overall survival was censored at the date of the last follow-up. All patients underwent a craniotomy on GBM with either total or subtotal resection. The following exclusion criteria were used: biopsy only, age below 18 years, no definite diagnosis, incomplete baseline clinical data, adjuvant therapy like chemotherapy or radiotherapy received before operation, malnutrition and perioperative mortality (survival < 20 days). Importantly, every patient diagnosed with a primary brain tumour and enrolled in our study had been diagnosed only recently and no prior specific treatment, including glucocorticoids, had been applied. The GBM group was compared to eighty healthy individuals (healthy control HC) aged 70.3 ± 5.2 years (females n = 62, males n = 18) recruited from the University of the Third Age in Zielona Gora (UTA, Poland). UTA is an organization which encourages the adults over 60 years of age to stay pro-healthy active. The current health status of the control group was assessed on the basis of medical records at a routine follow-up visit to a primary care physician. On the basis of the medical interview the following exclusion criteria were applied: acute infectious and autoimmunological diseases, uncontrolled hypertension and/or diabetes, oncologic diseases and neurodegenerative diseases. The study design received approval from the institutional review board at University Zielona Gora (No. 16/2022). Clinical assessment

Medical records were reviewed and the following clinical data were collected: gender, age at operation, hemisphere and locations of tumours, pathological diagnoses and histopathological, molecular and genetic biomarkers according to the protocol described in our previous study30. Ki-67 proliferation index was expressed as the percentage of cells with Ki-67-positive immunostained nuclei using the Ventana BenchMark GX (Ventana Medical Systems Inc., Tucson, USA). The expression of Ki-67 was categorised into two groups: low and intermediate (Ki-67 < 30%), and high (Ki-67 ≥ 30%) according to the recommendations by Chen et al.40. The data on postoperative adjuvant therapies and survival time were collected through documentation analysis.

Blood samples collection

The blood samples of patients with GBM were collected in S-Monovette-K2 tubes (Sarstedt AG & Co. KG, Germany) prior to the initiation of any treatment, including corticosteroid therapy (e.g., dexamethasone), to avoid potential confounding effects on leukocyte counts and inflammation-related parameters. Blood samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for approximately 20–30 min and then centrifuged at 500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The plasma was immediately apportioned into 0.5 mL aliquots and were transferred into clean polypropylene tubes. The plasma aliquots were stored at -80 °C until the time of analysis according to the protocol of our previous study30.

White blood cell count-derived inflammation marker

Haematological parameters including WBC, platelet count and differential WBC were determined by Sysmex XN-1000 (Sysmex Europe Gmbh, Norderstedt, Germany). The PIV, as a novel blood-based biomarker of survival in GBM patients23, was calculated according to the following formula: PIV = [neutrophil count (103/µL) × platelet count (103/µL) × monocyte count (103/µL)]/lymphocyte count (103/µL) proposed by Fuca et al.41. C-reactive protein (CRP) was measured using a high sensitivity commercial kit from DRG International (Springfield Township, Cincinnati, OH, USA) with the detection limit of 0.001 mg/L.

DNA analysis

DNA methylation (metDNA) was determined using ELISA kit from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA) with detection limit of 3 ng/mL. Non-methylated DNA (non-metDNA) was calculated by subtracting methylated DNA from total cirDNA. Oxidative cfDNA, as 8-hydroxy-2-deoxyguanosine, was determined using ELISA kits from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA) with detection limit of 50.9 pg/mL. The average intra-assay coefficients of variation (intra-assay CV) for the used enzyme immunoassay tests (ELISA) were < 4%. All samples were analyzed in duplicate in a single assay to avoid inter-assay variability.

Neutrophil activity

The total circulating fragments of DNA (cirDNA) were measured directly in plasma using a Quant-iTTM DNA high-sensitivity assay kit and a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The intra-assay CV for the Quant-iTTM DNA high-sensitivity assay is < 2%. Myeloperoxidase (MPO), as a protein associated with DNA fragments, was determined using ELISA kits from ThermoFischer Scientific (Invitrogen, USA) with detection limits of 26 pg/mL. The average intra-assay coefficient of variation for the used enzyme immunoassay test (ELISA) was < 10%. All samples were analysed in duplicate or triplicate in a single assay to avoid inter-assay variability according to the protocol of our previous study30.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.2.1 software [R Core Team (2022). R: A language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria. URL https://www.R-project.org/]. The variables were reported as mean values ± standard deviation (SD) and median (Me). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The significant differences in mean values between HC and GBM group were evaluated by the one-way ANOVA. If the normality and homogeneity assumptions were violated, the Mann–Whitney nonparametric test was used. The diagnostic values of NETosis and PIV were performed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and the area under the curve (AUC). The optimal threshold value for clinical stratification (cut-off value) was obtained by calculating the Youden index. Relative risk (RR) was performed for univariate logistic models. The Kaplan–Meier method was used for survival analysis.
