Delivering national immunisation programmes – a blanket opportunity for the pharmacy profession or a case of horses for courses?
Authors: Christina Wilson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis opinion piece explores the opportunities and challenges from increased utilisation of pharmacists and pharmacy technicians in delivery of the UK immunisation programme, within the recent legislative change enabling pharmacy technicians to administer Prescription Only Medicines (POMs) under Patient Group Directions (PGDs).Keywords: Immunisation, Medicines governance, Pharmacy, Patient group directions

The potential contribution that pharmacists in the UK are able to make to the healthcare landscape has been a long time coming in its realisation and a huge source of frustration to me on a professional level, particularly in my early career years. At that time of my life, while holidaying in Egypt, a community pharmacist saved my life, not to mention thousands of pounds in emergency hospital fees (I didn't have travel insurance). The topline detail of that trip goes as follows: while horse trekking in the Mount Sinai foothills, I developed an anaphylactic reaction, which fortunately developed insidiously over a few hours. En route to the community pharmacy premises near my resort, I could feel and hear my chest tightening with audible stridor. Within 10 min and for the equivalent of less than £10, I received treatment with intramuscular (gluteal) dexamethasone and some over-the-counter chlorpheniramine tablets. The next morning, I was as right as rain. Recounting the previous day’s events with some of the other guests, I sardonically commented how that would never happen in a pharmacy back home. An enquiring mind might have asked, ‘Why not?’. The cynic, least of all within myself, would point out that the divergent career paths of pharmacists make benchmarking of the clinical skills and competence of a pharmacist a challenge. Very much, therefore, a case of horses for courses (all puns intended).

When I first qualified as a pharmacist, the career options were fairly linear: community, hospital or industry. Independent prescribing didn’t exist during my undergraduate years. Fast forward to the present day and the inclusion of both pharmacists and pharmacy technicians within the Additional Roles Reimbursement Scheme (ARRS),1 coupled with the now firmly established CPPE Primary Care Pharmacy Education Pathway (PCPEP)2 enables assurance of set standards and acts as a gateway for pharmacy professionals to contribute towards an ever-spiralling workload in GP practices. A workload which (unenviably) includes an 18% increase in the average number of patients managed by a single GP, a loss of 5,512 full-time equivalent GP partners since September 2015 and more GPs electing to work part-time hours.3

Introduction of the COVID-19 vaccination programme introduced a further springboard for pharmacy to step up and deliver. In April 2021, community pharmacies were brought onboard to support greater ease of access for the general public, having successfully delivered the seasonal flu immunisation programme for many years prior. From data supplied by Community Pharmacy England (CPE), the exponential contribution of the sector in delivering the Advanced Flu Service, particularly during the COVID years, is plain to see. Nearly 3.6 million doses (Fig. 1) were administered by the sector during 2023/2024,4 representing 19.6% of the total doses given in England.5

In respect of COVID-19, this proportion jumps to nearly 25% (24.85%). In December 2023, 62% of all COVID-19 vaccines delivered in England were by the community pharmacy sector (Fig. 2), with a cumulative total of over 40 million doses given since the introduction of the programme in December 2020.6

Percentage (in blue) of cumulative COVID-19 doses delivered by the community pharmacy sector: February 2021 to December 2023. (Source: Company Chemists’ Association).

Development of the Pharmacy First scheme as a nationally commissioned framework in England has erased inequalities in clinical frontline care, further recognising the ability of community pharmacies to contribute meaningfully to existing NHS pressures.7

Given this growing contribution by the pharmacy sector, the recent public consultation recommending inclusion of pharmacy technicians as a professional group able to supply and administer medicines under a Patient Group Direction (PGD) came as no surprise.8 This recommendation was accepted and came into effect on 25 June 2024, culminating in the amendment to the Human Medicines Regulations (HMR) 2012.9 Perhaps in reflecting on my own professional transition into a public health role, I consider both the risks as well as the opportunities that such change introduces.

True, pharmacists are widely acknowledged as medicines experts. Across the main pharmacy sectors, both technicians and pharmacists are comfortable having face-to-face interactions with the public. However, as the adage goes, you don’t know what you don’t know.

The more technical language in the world of immunology and vaccination is specialised, not generalised. Antigens. Vaccine efficacy. Safety signals. Reassortment. The knowledge – again something that’s acquired over time and with experience: recommended dosing intervals between live and inactivated vaccines. DTaP/IPV versus dTaP/IPV. What to do if an individual has missed doses from the primary course. Demonstrating confidence in the principles of consent, assuring a worried parent about the safety of the vaccine and being able to manage safeguarding issues are further examples of the specialist communication skills that may as yet be alien to some pharmacists and technicians. Do they know where to seek specialist advice, outside the perimeter of their employer’s walls? For example, how many pharmacy professionals are aware of the support available via the Screening and Immunisation Team (SIT) networks, let alone know how to contact them?

It’s important not to conflate my opinion with the view that there’s not already the existence of the baseline skills within the profession, having had the privilege of working with some truly gifted and driven individuals, both pharmacy technicians and pharmacists alike. Neither do I have any doubts of the potential that pharmacy as a whole can offer, particularly in the arena of national immunisation delivery. Being able to confidently assure the public that a minimum expected, yet safe and effective standard of knowledge and skill has been achieved and ensure that the individual has been given the headspace and support to do so, before picking up that first syringe, would be my desired aim. I would urge caution with the advent of these new possibilities and my ask would be that wider education bodies, professional pharmacy bodies, integrated care boards (ICBs) and NHS England ensure that minimally recommended training standards are promoted, supported and upheld at every opportunity. Agreeing how assurance of the standards will be obtained is also vitally important. Recalling my semi-unannounced visits to community pharmacy premises and GP practices who’d opted into providing a COVID-19 service in autumn 2022 revealed the wide disparity of quality standards and system readiness, demonstrating the importance for an informal but supportive and accessible network of knowledge and support at an ICB, if not place, level. The anticipated change in vaccination commissioning from a centralised to devolved ICB responsibility13 perhaps further underlines the need for ICBs to take the initiative and expand their accountability to one of assurance.

My overriding concern at this juncture is that the expectations of employers and the wider healthcare system, perhaps keen to recognise efficiencies, place unreasonable or unrealistic demands upon their pharmacy professionals – technicians in particular – which potentially exceed the limitations of their own clinical competence. It’s vitally important that pharmacy colleagues are acutely aware of the training and support opportunities out there, in the guise of immunisation conferences, courses, the SIT coordinators. Moreover, that employers seek to provide practical shadowing opportunities with existing practice staff to assure that patient safety will be upheld.

Perhaps one day in the near future, when I’m asked what I do for work, someone will subsequently tell me how a pharmacist also saved their life. Or at the very least, explain how their healthcare needs were met when all other avenues to access were seemingly closed off.

Like other invested individuals and groups, I watch the proverbial space with interest.
