An XGBoost-based cellular automata for modeling PM₂.₅ concentration using ground and satellite data
Authors: Sajad Farokhi, Jamshid Maleki, Ehsan Foroutan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAir pollution is one of the major environmental issues in Iran, driven by industrial greenhouse gas emissions, vehicle exhaust, power plants, and natural sources such as dust storms. Among various pollutants, PM₂.₅ is especially concerning due to its high prevalence in urban areas. Governments have utilized air quality monitoring stations to measure PM₂.₅ concentration levels to implement policies aimed at controlling this pollutant. Fixed monitoring stations, which operate for regulatory purposes, are typically located in urban areas where a large portion of the population resides. However, the measurements obtained from these stations are limited due to their insufficient number, uneven distribution, and inadequate spatial coverage, which prevent continuous pollutant estimation over large areas and the generation of comprehensive risk maps. To address these limitations, this study introduces a novel hybrid modeling framework for modeling the spatiotemporal distribution of PM₂.₅ across Iran. The proposed approach integrates Cellular Automata with the XGBoost algorithm, leveraging both satellite-based products and ground observations. Within this framework, XGBoost generates continuous transition potentials for each neighborhood window, enabling dynamic representation of spatial variations in PM₂.₅ levels. The resulting model achieved an adjusted R² of 0.88, demonstrating strong performance in capturing regional and temporal variability. A key strength of the method is its ability to localize the influence of predictor variables across different regions, allowing the development of region-specific models within each neighborhood window and improving both interpretability and predictive accuracy.Keywords: PM₂.₅ concentration, Cellular automata, Spatiotemporal modeling, Regional air pollution, Ground-based data, Satellite dataSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Air pollution is a pressing global challenge, particularly in rapidly industrializing economies; Iran exemplifies this pattern, as an aging, high-emitting vehicle fleet increases exposure and risk1,2. Since industrialization began in the 1970 s, ambient pollutant levels have risen steadily, and over the past two decades, concentrations have reached hazardous levels in several major cities, including Tehran, Mashhad, Tabriz, Isfahan, Ahvaz, Arak, and Karaj. A 2016 World Bank assessment estimated the annual economic damage from air pollution in Iran at roughly US$13 billion—about 2.2% of GDP3. Among regulated pollutants, fine particulate matter ≤ 2.5 μm in aerodynamic diameter (PM₂.₅) warrants particular attention because it penetrates deep into the lungs and is strongly associated with cardiopulmonary morbidity and mortality4. Accordingly, modeling PM₂.₅ concentrations is critical for targeting mitigation and evaluating policy in Iran.

The main PM₂.₅ emission sources in Iran include urban traffic, industrial facilities (e.g., refineries and power plants), residential fuel combustion, and transboundary dust storms entering the country from western and southeastern borders1. Studies and national assessments indicate that many urban areas routinely record PM₂.₅ concentrations and Air Quality Index (AQI) values that exceed health-based guidelines, underscoring substantial population exposure and public-health risks1. According to1, in the 2019 revision of Iran’s National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS), the annual permissible limit for PM₂.₅ is 12 µg m⁻³; however, many metropolitan areas frequently exceed this threshold throughout the year. Moreover, the national air quality monitoring network, consisting of approximately 160–200 active stations in recent years, exhibits strong spatial imbalance, with most stations concentrated in large urban centers while vast rural, mountainous and border regions remain unmonitored1,2. In this context, satellite remote sensing combined with data-fusion and advanced machine-learning models provide a practical route to generate spatially continuous PM₂.₅ fields and to mitigate the biases introduced by uneven monitoring coverage2.

Given the limited coverage of fixed monitoring stations, air pollution modeling is indispensable for estimating pollutant concentrations across entire urban and regional domains. To date, three principal approaches have been employed in air pollution modeling: deterministic models, statistical models, and machine learning–based non-deterministic models. Deterministic models rely on classical mathematics, using aerodynamic theories and physical-chemical processes to model pollutant dispersion and emission3. However, the practical application of these models is often restricted due to their reliance on numerous highly accurate parameters, the need for large input datasets, computational complexity, and extended modeling timeframes4. Moreover, the accuracy of deterministic models is strongly influenced by the spatial extent of the study area and the quality of the input data.

Statistical models and machine learning models are among the most widely used approaches in air pollution modeling5–8. Statistical models typically operate without considering the complex physical-chemical reactions occurring in the atmosphere and are based solely on data-derived relationships9. However, these models often assume linear relationships, whereas most real-world relationships are non-linear. Machine learning models have been extensively used to explore non-linear and complex relationships between observations and predictive variables5,10–14. Nevertheless, machine learning, particularly deep learning models, requires large volumes of labeled data and relatively long training times. Moreover, deep learning models exhibit a high dependency on the network architecture designed for neural layers, and the black-box nature of these hidden layers adds to their complexity.

To overcome these limitations, cellular automata (CA) models can be employed to model changes in pollutant concentrations influenced by multiple sources and factors15–19. CA models can simulate complex processes using simple mathematical operations20,21. The CA models are discrete, grid-based computational models composed of individual cells, each of which updates its state over time based on a set of transition rules and the states of neighboring cells. These components enable CA to capture spatial and temporal dynamics in complex systems such as air pollution dispersion. However, the development of an efficient CA model requires sufficiently accurate and reliable data, which fixed monitoring stations alone cannot provide due to their limited spatial coverage. Therefore, accurate and spatially detailed PM₂.₅ concentration measurements at regional or urban scales are essential. To address this gap, satellite remotely sensed data have been widely used to retrieve continuous, spatially resolved PM₂.₅ measurements at regional and urban scales22–24, providing critical information for estimating exposure levels25.

In a recent study, a pixel-level PM₂.₅ projection method was employed using Landsat imagery, monitoring data, and historical PM₂.₅ grids16. Tang et al. (2023) developed a pixel-level PM₂.₅ projection method that integrated Landsat imagery, monitoring data, and historical PM₂.₅ grids. Applied to the Yangtze River Delta, the approach combined CA-simulated land-use changes with a spatial lag model to estimate future PM₂.₅ distributions, revealing fine-scale spatiotemporal trends that can inform sustainable policy planning16. In another study, a hybrid model that integrates land-use regression and CA to simulate real‑time NO₂ dispersion across urban environments by accounting for on‑road emissions, meteorological, and morphological factors17. Their model achieves an external R² of 0.57 and successfully replicates NO₂ reductions observed during the first COVID‑19 lockdown.

Integrating data from fixed monitoring stations with ground-based and satellite data can potentially enhance the development of CA models for continuous regional-scale PM₂.₅ concentration modeling. CA models have demonstrated robust performance in spatiotemporal modeling applications, such as land use change and urban development monitoring19,26, air quality simulation and analysis17,18,27, forest fire monitoring28,29, and more. However, limited studies have focused on modeling PM₂.₅ concentrations using integrated ground, satellite, and reanalysis data to achieve countrywide fields in Iran, a prerequisite for robust policy evaluation2. While the model proposed in2 demonstrated acceptable performance in capturing large-scale spatial variations, its ability to represent localized dynamics and neighborhood-level interactions remained limited. To overcome these limitations, the present study introduces a hybrid framework that integrates CA with the XGBoost algorithm. This combination enables dynamic spatial transition modeling and improves predictive accuracy, providing a more realistic representation of PM₂.₅ spatiotemporal variability across Iran.

The main objective of this study is to develop a hybrid modeling framework that integrates XGBoost with CA to simulate the spatiotemporal distribution of PM₂.₅ concentrations across Iran. The study primarily focuses on methodological innovation rather than policy assessment, aiming to enhance prediction accuracy by incorporating both spatial neighborhood interactions and nonlinear relationships among multiple predictor variables derived from ground-based and satellite data. This hybrid XGBoost–CA approach is designed to localize the effects of independent variables, capture spatial heterogeneity, and provide a reliable, high-resolution PM₂.₅ estimation model suitable for large-scale environmental analysis.

Materials and methods

Study area

Iran, with a population exceeding 87 million and a total area of approximately 1,648,000 square kilometers, is located in Western Asia between latitudes 25° and 40° North and longitudes 44° and 63° East. Geographically, Iran is situated in a region highly susceptible to dust storms. These dust storms, often originating from neighboring countries such as Iraq and Saudi Arabia to the west, and Afghanistan and Pakistan to the east, carry fine particles and pollutants that significantly deteriorate air quality.

Despite Iran’s unique situation of being surrounded by some of the most polluted countries in the world, the fixed air quality monitoring stations established for regulatory purposes are limited in number and concentrated mainly in urban areas. As a result, the PM₂.₅ data they provide suffer from sparse coverage, uneven spatial distribution, and restricted geographic reach, making them inadequate for capturing large-scale pollution dynamics or producing reliable exposure assessments. In this study, ground-based observations were obtained from 162 air quality monitoring stations distributed across Iran, operated by the Department of Environment (DOE) of Iran30. However, their distribution is uneven, with a stronger concentration in urban areas compared to rural or remote regions. Proposed method

This section begins with a brief explanation of the basic concepts of CA and XGBoost, which serve as the foundations of the proposed model. Following that, the structure and execution process of the integrated XGBoost-CA model are described, including its input components and implementation flowchart.

CA basic concepts

Cellular Automata (CA) are discrete dynamic systems composed of a grid of cells, each holding a specific state at any given time. The state of each cell evolves through a set of transition rules that consider the states of neighboring cells. CA models are particularly effective for simulating spatial and spatiotemporal phenomena due to their local interaction mechanisms and parallel updating structure. The essential components of a CA model include:

Cell Space: A two-dimensional grid that represents the geographical or spatial domain of the system.

Cell Space: A two-dimensional grid that represents the geographical or spatial domain of the system.

Neighborhood Configuration: Defines which surrounding cells influence the central cell. In this study, a 5 × 5 extended Moore neighborhood was employed, which includes 24 neighboring cells.

Neighborhood Configuration: Defines which surrounding cells influence the central cell. In this study, a 5 × 5 extended Moore neighborhood was employed, which includes 24 neighboring cells.

Transition Rules: Deterministic or probabilistic rules that update each cell’s state based on its current state and those of its neighbors.

Transition Rules: Deterministic or probabilistic rules that update each cell’s state based on its current state and those of its neighbors.

Boundary Conditions: Define how edge cells are handled. Custom boundary conditions were implemented to exclude missing data and to limit predictions within Iran’s national borders.

Stochastic Factor: A random factor was incorporated into the model to account for uncertainties and unknown disturbances in the PM₂.₅ estimation process.

Stochastic Factor: A random factor was incorporated into the model to account for uncertainties and unknown disturbances in the PM₂.₅ estimation process.

Overall, CA is well-suited for modeling environmental phenomena such as air pollution due to its ability to simulate local interactions across space and time.

XGBoost basic concepts

XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting) is a high-performance machine learning algorithm based on the gradient boosting framework. It builds an ensemble of decision trees sequentially, where each new tree attempts to correct the prediction errors made by the previous trees. Key features of XGBoost include:

Gradient Boosting Mechanism: Trees are added one at a time, each minimizing a defined loss function (e.g., mean squared error) by following the gradient of that loss.

Regularization: Incorporates both L1 (Lasso) and L2 (Ridge) regularization terms to prevent overfitting and improve model generalization.

Efficient Handling of Missing Data: XGBoost can natively process missing values and determine optimal splits accordingly.

Tree Pruning: Uses a depth-first strategy with a pruning mechanism based on a minimum loss reduction threshold to avoid over-complex trees.

In the context of the proposed model, XGBoost acts as a spatial rule engine that learns weighted relationships between independent variables and PM₂.₅ concentrations within the CA framework.

XGBoost-CA model description

The proposed method is a continuous CA model that utilizes the XGBoost ensemble learning algorithm as the core mechanism for estimating the spatial transfer potential of PM₂.₅ concentrations. This integrated model is referred to as the XGBoost-CA model. A key advantage of this approach is its ability to develop region-specific sub-models that address the heterogeneous behavior of independent variables across different areas of Iran. This helps capture the complex spatial dynamics and variability of PM₂.₅ concentrations.

In general, the inputs of the proposed model are divided into three categories: dependent variables, independent variables, and CA variables. These three categories of inputs were prepared through a series of processes to be entered into the model, and a brief description of each is provided below.

The dependent variable of this model corresponds to the measured PM₂.₅ data with a three-hour average obtained from the National Air Quality Monitoring System of the Department of Environment. After performing data engineering steps, the data were prepared as monthly averages throughout the year 2021 to serve as the dependent variable for the model.

The independent variables of the proposed model were obtained from relevant sources. After processing steps such as masking cloud-covered values in some cases and interpolating missing data, they were integrated at a spatial resolution of 5 km and represented as monthly averages for the year 2021.

Before finalizing the independent variables, a feature selection and multicollinearity analysis were performed to improve model efficiency and avoid redundant predictors. First, the pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients among all variables were computed, and those with strong correlations (|r| > 0.85) were excluded from the final feature set. Next, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was calculated for the remaining predictors, and all variables with VIF values greater than 5 were removed. After this two-step screening process, 18 out of the initial 21 independent variables were retained for model development, ensuring that only statistically independent and relevant predictors were used. Overall, the monthly values of the dependent and independent variables were organized into a four-dimensional matrix of size 330 × 432 × 12 × 19. The first two dimensions represent the size of the cellular grid used, the third dimension corresponds to the months of the year, and the fourth dimension includes the dependent variable as the first entry, followed by the eighteen independent variables.

The CA variables, which are also known as the fundamental parameters of the CA model, include neighborhood configuration, boundary conditions, a stochastic factor, and CA rules. The neighborhood used in this study is a five-by-five extended Moore neighborhood, allowing for the localization of the modeling process by employing 24 neighboring cells to estimate the value of the central cell. The boundary conditions were custom-designed so that cells were considered from the top-left corner to the bottom-right, excluding missing data in the estimation process and using the boundaries of Iran as a limiting factor to prevent estimations beyond national borders.

To account for uncertainties and unknown disturbances in the PM₂.₅ concentration estimation process, a stochastic factor generating random values between zero and one was applied. The rule used in this study was a weighted averaging rule defined by XGBoost within the specified neighborhood. According to this rule, the eight cells within a Moore neighborhood of radius R = 1 were assigned a weight of 1.0, while the remaining cells within the neighborhood—excluding those with missing values—were assigned a lower weight of 0.9 during the averaging process used to estimate the value of the central cell.

Results

In the following section, the findings and results of the study are presented exclusively based on the test and cross-validation data, which were not involved in the model training process during any month. The results are provided in the form of figures, tables, and charts.

Hyperparameter tuning of the model

In this study, a Grid Search algorithm with Cross-Validation (GS-CV) was employed to determine the optimal values for tuning the hyperparameters of the XGBoost model. The main hyperparameters tuned in this study are briefly described below. n_estimators defines the number of boosting trees in the ensemble. eta (learning rate) controls how much each tree contributes to the model, where smaller values yield smoother convergence. max_depth specifies the maximum depth of trees, regulating model complexity. min_child_weight determines the minimum sum of instance weights required in a child node, helping to prevent overfitting. subsample and colsample_bytree denote the fractions of samples and features randomly used for each tree, which improve generalization. Finally, lambda and alpha are regularization parameters that control model complexity and enhance stability. The cross-validation of hyperparameters was conducted using a five-fold approach, and the final results obtained from this process are presented in Table 2. In addition, to evaluate the model’s predictive performance over time, a temporal cross-validation scheme was employed. Specifically, a leave-one-month-out strategy was implemented, where the model was trained on data from eleven months and validated on the remaining month in a rotating manner. This design ensured temporal independence between training and testing samples and provided a robust assessment of the model’s generalization capability across different time periods.

Discussion and conclusion

Given the unique conditions of Iran regarding the widespread presence of particulate matter (PM) emission sources—such as consecutive droughts, excessive use of fossil fuels, the distribution of factories and thermal power plants across the country, and its specific geographical location surrounded by the most polluted countries in the region in terms of particulate matter emissions—the need for air pollution modeling in this region has become essential. Moreover, air pollution modeling, due to its specific nature, requires models that accurately capture the spatiotemporal behavior of independent variables.

This study introduces two novel models with acceptable and distinct performances, named LR-CA and XGBoost-CA. These models aim to generate high-accuracy PM₂.₅ concentration maps over a wide and regional area covering the entire territory of Iran. The core of the proposed models is based on the CA framework. By localizing the behavior of LR and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) models, the CA model addresses the spatial heterogeneity of independent variables influencing PM₂.₅ concentration changes as the dependent variable. Consequently, the proposed models exhibit stronger performance in modeling PM₂.₅ concentration variations.

Additionally, the CA model utilizes a stochastic factor, which introduces random values at the local level within each neighborhood window. This approach mitigates overfitting to a reasonable extent and ensures high accuracy and precision in predictions. Essentially, the LR and XGBoost models serve as generators of the potential transformation or transition for the CA.

Spatial heterogeneity in regional air pollution modeling is a crucial factor that has received limited attention. To deal with spatial heterogeneity, CA is an appropriate model. On the other hand, XGBoost was selected as an appropriate choice for generating continuous air pollution transition potential maps for the CA model. This selection was based on XGBoost’s efficiency in handling large datasets and its ability to model nonlinear behaviors of the phenomena. As a result, the proposed model, by incorporating this capability into the air pollution modeling process, addresses a critical need on a large regional scale, marking the primary innovation of this study.

To model monthly PM₂.₅ concentrations across Iran, four different models were used for performance comparison, utilizing available ground-based and satellite data. Among these, the primary proposed model, XGBoost-CA, consistently outperformed the other three models in all months. The XGBoost-CA demonstrated the highest predictive performance among all benchmark models, achieving an adjusted R² of 0.89, RMSE of 3.88 µg/m³, MAE of 2.33 µg/m³, and an MBE of −0.32 µg/m³ across the test folds. Additionally, the LR-CA model showed significant improvements in accuracy and performance compared to the base LR model. These results clearly indicate that incorporating spatially localized transition potentials within the CA structure substantially enhances model accuracy compared to standalone machine learning approaches. The consistency of these improvements across all months further highlights the robustness and stability of the proposed method.

The results of this study are consistent with previous research that employed machine learning techniques to model PM₂.₅ concentrations; however, the proposed XGBoost-CA model offers significant improvements in both spatial resolution and predictive accuracy2,37. For example, a prior study that used a random-forest model reported an R² of 0.65 at the monthly scale across large, spatially heterogeneous regions37. In contrast, the integration of CA enabled our model to capture localized dynamics, while XGBoost provided a robust mechanism for handling complex nonlinear interactions among predictors. With an adjusted R² of 0.88, our hybrid model outperformed comparable studies and demonstrated enhanced capability in producing spatially continuous PM₂.₅ concentration estimates2. This performance gap underscores the value of combining spatial modeling frameworks with advanced ensemble learning algorithms in air quality assessment.

This study provides several key insights that can inform environmental policy and urban air quality management in Iran. First, the demonstrated ability of the XGBoost-CA model to produce localized predictions underscores the importance of region-specific strategies for pollution control, rather than relying on uniform national policies. Second, the integration of remotely sensed satellite data with machine learning algorithms enables the generation of continuous pollution maps in areas where ground monitoring is sparse or unavailable, providing valuable support for early warning systems and public health planning. Lastly, the spatial modeling approach adopted here can be extended to other environmental variables or pollutants such as NO₂ or O₃, supporting a more comprehensive air quality management framework. Policymakers and environmental managers are encouraged to invest in both data infrastructure and computational modeling capacity to leverage such tools for targeted, evidence-based interventions.

Future research should aim to address several key limitations identified in the current study. One major limitation is the spatial bias introduced by the concentration of air quality monitoring stations in urban areas, which restricts the representativeness of ground-truth data in rural or remote regions. This urban-centric sampling structure can introduce spatial bias into the training dataset, potentially reducing the model’s ability to fully characterize pollution dynamics in regions with limited or no ground observations. Although the integration of satellite-derived predictors and the global/regional PM₂.₅ model partially alleviates this imbalance by providing spatially continuous inputs, uncertainties may still propagate into the XGBoost-CA predictions in areas with low station density. Therefore, inferred concentrations in such regions should be interpreted with caution, and future work would benefit from expanding the monitoring network or incorporating additional independent validation sources to improve spatial representativeness and reduce this source of uncertainty.

Another methodological limitation of this study concerns the temporal and spatial resolution of the datasets used for model development. Because both the fused ground–satellite PM₂.₅ product and several meteorological and geophysical predictors were only available as monthly composites at a 5 km spatial resolution, the XGBoost-CA model operates at a regional scale rather than capturing fine-scale temporal fluctuations or sub-kilometer spatial heterogeneity. As a result, rapid pollution dynamics associated with short-term meteorological events, localized emission spikes, or topographically driven gradients may not be fully reflected in the model outputs. Future studies could benefit from integrating higher-resolution datasets, such as daily PM₂.₅ retrievals or 1 km satellite products, to enhance the model’s capability for capturing local-scale variations. Moreover, incorporating meteorological forecast data and land-use dynamics may further improve model generalizability and allow for near-real-time prediction capabilities.

Furthermore, an examination of the spatial performance maps (Fig. 11) reveals that the model exhibits relatively lower accuracy in certain regions of northern and western Iran. These areas are characterized by complex topography, strong local meteorological gradients, and sparse ground-based monitoring coverage, all of which can reduce the reliability of spatially continuous predictions. In the northern regions, proximity to the Alborz mountain range and frequent inversion events may introduce localized variability that is not fully captured by the monthly 5 km input datasets. Similarly, parts of western Iran experience intermittent dust intrusions and rapidly shifting synoptic conditions, contributing to higher prediction uncertainties. Acknowledging these region-specific limitations helps prevent overgeneralization and highlights the need for additional high-resolution observations to further enhance spatial model performance. These enhancements would significantly strengthen the model’s utility for public health applications and environmental policy planning.
