Neural architecture search using network embedding and generative adversarial networks
Authors: Morteza Yousefi, Vahid Mehrdad, Mohammad Bagher Dowlatshahi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Surrogate models are used by recently proposed algorithms as a means of forecasting neural architecture performance. Rather than training the network from scratch, which speeds up evaluation of performance in the search for neural architecture. However, collecting a sufficient number of labeled architectures for training surrogate models is a time-consuming process. We suggest a surrogate-assisted swarm optimization algorithm with network embedding for neural architecture search, as well as a generative adversarial networks for augmentation data (GNE-NAS) to improve the performance of surrogate models with limited training data. In this case, each architecture is meaningfully represented using an unsupervised learning technique. In the embedding space, architectures with a greater degree of structural similarity are positioned closer together. This proximity facilitates the training of surrogate models. Prior to training the surrogate model, we employ a generative adversarial network for data augmentation. This approach enhances the robustness of the surrogate model and concurrently reduces the need for a large number of real evaluations. The surrogate model achieves comparable or better performance when network embedding is applied, as demonstrated by experimental results on two distinct NASBench search spaces. Our proposed method, GNE-NAS, has been shown to outperform other state-of-the-art neural architecture search algorithms.

Introduction

Deep neural networks (DNNs) have achieved remarkable success in a wide range of artificial intelligence tasks, including image classification, object detection, and natural language processing1–3. Their performance depends not only on the optimization of network weights but also critically on the underlying architecture design. Traditionally, creating high-performing architectures has required substantial domain expertise and iterative experimentation. To reduce this dependence on manual design, Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has emerged as a powerful paradigm, formulating architecture design as an optimization problem over a predefined search space and aiming to discover network structures that maximize validation performance after training.

Existing NAS approaches can be broadly grouped into three categories: reinforcement learning RL-based, evolutionary algorithm EA-based, and gradient-based methods. RL-based NAS employs a controller that samples candidate architectures and learns to improve its proposals based on performance rewards. EA-based NAS treats architectures as individuals in a population, generating new ones via evolutionary operations like selection, crossover, and mutation. In contrast, gradient-based NAS relaxes the discrete nature of the search space into a continuous form, allowing architecture parameters and network weights to be optimized jointly via gradient descent. While EAs are known for their strong exploration capabilities4, their reliance on full training of each candidate makes them computationally costly, for instance, LargeEvo5 requires more than 3000 GPU-days for a single search.

Surrogate-Assisted Evolutionary Algorithms (SAEAs) address this computational bottleneck by replacing expensive full training with performance prediction through surrogate models. Examples include end-to-end performance predictors6, adaptive switching between multiple surrogates7, and LSTM-based estimators8. Although such strategies reduce computational expense, supervised surrogate models still require large sets of well-trained architectures as labeled data, limiting their scalability. To address this, recent methods have incorporated unsupervised or self-supervised learning to obtain architecture embeddings that reduce labeled data demands, such as Arch2vec9, NASGEM10, and SS-RL/SS-CCL11. However, these works often impose restrictive assumptions on the embedding distribution or neglect operation-level details that significantly influence performance.

Alternative embedding techniques, such as graph convolutional networks, contrastive graph objectives, and transformer-based graph encoders, have also been explored to produce richer representations of architectural graphs12–14. Another promising direction is online learning with infill criteria, in which the surrogate model is updated incrementally as new evaluations are obtained. Infill criteria select candidate architectures that balance exploration of new regions with exploitation of promising areas, guided by predicted performance and uncertainty15,16. In parallel, environmental selection strategies help maintain diversity in the search population while preserving high-quality solutions, supporting both efficient convergence and robustness against premature stagnation.

This improves prediction robustness and accuracy, especially under limited real-evaluation data.Online learning-based infill criterion: We design a dual-objective infill criterion that considers both predicted accuracy and model uncertainty, guiding the search toward promising architecture regions while preserving exploration. This criterion is integrated into the swarm search to dynamically update and improve the surrogate.

Related works

In recent years, EAs have gained popularity in NAS. Among these, the Genetic Algorithm (GA) is the most popular optimizer in EA-based NAS. Additionally, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) has emerged as another prominent algorithm in this field. LargeEvo5 is an evolutionary algorithm used in NAS. It employs binary tournament selection for mating, each pair of architectures compete, and the better-performing one becomes a parent. Genetic operations like crossover and mutation create new architectures in each generation. The algorithm maintains diversity in the population and removes older individuals to introduce fresh genetic material. Genetic CNN17 utilizes genetic operations to evolve and create new neural network architectures in each generation. The fixed-length binary representation allows for efficient manipulation of the architecture’s components. This approach aims to automatically discover architectures that excel in specific tasks, eliminating the need for manual design.

REA4 maintains population diversity by eliminating the oldest individuals, which contributes to achieving state-of-the-art performance. Cartesian genetic programming for convolutional neural networks, CGP-CNN18,19, introduces an innovative approach to architectural exploration in Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). Utilizing the capabilities of Cartesian genetic programming, this method employs an evolutionary search algorithm to generate and optimize diverse CNN architectures iteratively. The distinctive strengths of CGP-CNN lie in its ability to navigate a vast architectural space with flexibility and efficiency while minimizing computational overhead. AutoML-Zero20, employs a distinctive approach in NAS by utilizing fundamental mathematical operations as the foundational building blocks for designing neural network architectures. In contrast to traditional methods that rely on expert-crafted layers, AutoML-Zero starts with elementary operations and leverages an evolutionary process to generate and refine architectures iteratively. This unique strategy demonstrates the algorithm’s ability to autonomously discover architectures from scratch, significantly reducing reliance on pre-defined, human-designed components.

DE-NAS21 utilizes Differential Evolution (DE) in NAS, adapting it to handle the discrete and categorical nature of architectures. Building on this line of research, Han et al.22 introduced SaDENAS, a self-adaptive differential evolution algorithm that tunes its control parameters based on the search stage. This strategy accelerates convergence while maintaining strong exploration capability, and their experiments on multiple NAS benchmarks reported clear gains over conventional DE-NAS variants.

In a related hybrid direction, Xue et al.23 proposed GENAS, a gradient-guided evolutionary NAS framework. By incorporating gradient information into evolutionary operators, GENAS effectively navigates toward promising regions of the search space while preserving population diversity. Their results show consistent improvements compared with both pure evolutionary and pure gradient-based approaches. Extending recent advances to gradient-based NAS, Xue et al.24 developed a differentiable NAS method with a self-adaptive weighting scheme based on dual-attention mechanisms. This approach dynamically balances the influence of candidate operations during architecture search, yielding more stable convergence and better performance on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet than standard DARTS.

Within the domain of NAS, PSO can be used to optimize neural network architectures by representing the network design as a particle in the search space. The algorithm iteratively updates the position of each particle (network architecture) by evaluating its performance relative to the best-performing particle of the swarm, this process aims to identify the architecture that achieves the best validation performance25. The application of PSO in NAS is valuable due to its ability to handle the vast search space and complex objective functions involved in designing neural networks. It balances exploration and exploitation, crucial for avoiding local optima and ensuring convergence to a global optimum26. Furthermore, the flexibility of PSO allows it to be adapted for various NAS tasks, such as feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, and layer configuration, making it a versatile tool for improving neural network performance across different datasets and problem domains27. In summary, PSO is a promising approach for NAS, offering a systematic method to explore and optimize neural network architectures, which can result in the development of more efficient and accurate models for a wide range of applications25.

The application of Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) to NAS is promising because it can potentially discover high-performing architectures that might not be easily found through manual design or other search strategies. The algorithm can gradually search for the best architectures by covering the entire search space in tiny, incremental steps28. The Firefly Optimization Algorithm (FA) has been applied in various domains, including optimizing Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) structures for classification tasks. For instance, a Variable Step Size Firefly Optimization Algorithm (VSFA) has been employed to optimize the weights and biases of neurons in an MLP model, with the goal of minimizing the mean square error of the network output29.

Encoding schemes

The impact of encoding schemes on search performance is a complex topic that has been explored in various research contexts. Encoding schemes play a vital role in representing data in a storage-efficient format and conducive to fast retrieval and processing. In the context of search performance, encoding schemes can significantly affect the speed and accuracy of search algorithms. Adjacency matrix encoding is a straightforward representation of graph data, where an edge in a graph is represented as either present or absent by a two-dimensional matrix. This encoding is particularly useful for dense graphs and allows quick lookups of edge existence. However, the adjacency matrix can be space-inefficient for sparse graphs, as it requires storage for all possible node pairs, regardless of whether an edge exists30. Path-based encodings, on the other hand, represent paths or sequences in a graph, which can be more space-efficient for sparse graphs. These encodings can be optimized for specific types of searches, such as shortest-path queries in transportation networks31. In NAS, path-based encoding encodes every path that connects an input to an output by treating the neural network as a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG). This method contrasts with adjacency matrix encoding and has been shown to reduce feature dependency and improve the performance of neural predictors32. BANANAS33 is one of the algorithms that adopts path-based encoding, and it is noted for its innovative approach in this area.

The research on path-based encoding and its advantages over traditional adjacency matrix encoding is supported by various studies. For instance, the Directed Acyclic Graph Variational Autoencoders (D-VAE) encode an input graph using a graph neural network, which computes encodings by following the structure of the graph, a method similar to the recursive neural networks employed by RTG-AE34. Moreover, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are highly relevant to the discussion of path-based encoding. To process complex graph-structured data, GNNs, a class of deep learning algorithms, iteratively update the representation of each node by aggregating information from its neighboring nodes35. This iterative process is similar to path-based encoding, as it approaches emphasizes the exploration of paths within the graph. The effectiveness of path-based encoding in NAS is further highlighted by the use of surrogate models in NAS. These models, such as GATES, process neural architecture information and map it into a continuous embedding space, potentially representing the networks data36.

The concept of Position-Aware Path-based Encoding (PAPE) in NAS represents a novel approach to encoding neural network architectures. Unlike traditional path-based encoding, which may only consider the type of operations in a path, PAPE also records the position of each operation. This allows for a more detailed representation of the architecture’s structure, potentially leading to more effective search and optimization in NAS. Within the domain of NAS, encoding schemes like PAPE are crucial because they influence how architectures are represented and manipulated during the search process. The encoding needs to capture the essence of the architecture topology and operations to allow for efficient search and performance prediction37.

Inspired by biological processes, Action Command Encoding (ACEncoding) has been proposed38, which encodes these commands into a variable-length sequence using seven action commands made up of three integers. This technique enables the representation of more intricate neural architectures, suggesting a parallel with the gene expression process in biological systems. The ACEncoding scheme is particularly notable for its ability to encode a sequence of architectural decisions, similar to how gene expression encodes the development of biological organisms. The flexibility and depth of representation offered by this method could potentially lead to the discovery of more nuanced and effective neural network architectures, especially when considering the vast and complex search spaces involved in NAS39.

Performance estimation strategy

NAS is critical in automated machine learning, particularly for designing efficient neural networks. Evaluating new architectures is a significant bottleneck in NAS due to the high computational cost of training and validating each candidate model. To address this, numerous techniques have been developed to accelerate the evaluation process. One such method is low-fidelity estimation, which involves training models on a subset of the data or for fewer epochs to quickly estimate their performance40,41. Although this method can drastically reduce the computational resources requirements, the resulting performance estimations might be less precise5,42. Another method is the weight-sharing mechanism, which entails training a supernet that includes every possible architecture in the search space. Individual architectures can be evaluated without training from scratch by inheriting weights from the supernet. This method can lead to faster evaluations but may introduce biases due to the shared weights43. Surrogate model techniques involve training a predictive model, such as a Gaussian process or a neural network, to predict the performance of architectures without actually training them. This approach can be very efficient, especially when combined with transfer learning or meta-learning techniques, but the accuracy of the predictions depends on the surrogate model’s quality44. One important tactic to lower computational costs and expedite the evaluation of novel neural network architectures is the weight-sharing mechanism in one-shot architecture search. The literature provides various approaches to implementing weight sharing, each with advantages and potential drawbacks. SMASH45 is an early approach that utilizes a hypernetwork to dynamically generate weights for different architectures, which are then evaluated based on their performance46. This method allows for a rapid assessment of numerous architectures without needing to train each individually.

One-shot NAS methods, such as the one proposed by47, employ dynamic dropout rates during supernet training to mitigate the weight co-adaptation problem. This involves randomly zeroing out a subset of operations, which encourages the network to become more robust to architectural changes. SPOS48 assumes that each architecture is a single path within the supernet and uses uniform path sampling during training. This method aims to alleviate the weight co-adaptation issue by ensuring that each path is treated equally during the training process46. FairNAS49 addresses the inherent unfairness in supernet training by ensuring that each block within the network is activated and updated only once, promoting fairness in evaluating candidate architectures46. Landmark Regularization50 introduces a regularization term that leverages stand-alone performance to guide the supernet training, providing a way to incorporate external knowledge into the training process51. DARTS52 adopts a gradient-based optimization method that simultaneously optimizes the weights and the architecture, allowing for a more integrated and efficient search process53.

Preliminaries: graph2vec

Graph representation learning has emerged as a crucial area of research in machine learning, particularly for tasks involving graph-structured data such as social networks, molecular structures. Traditional approaches, such as graph kernels, rely on handcrafted features to measure graph similarity, which often suffer from poor generalization and high computational costs. To address these limitations, graph2vec54 introduces a novel unsupervised framework for learning distributed representations of entire graphs, enabling efficient and scalable graph analytics.

The core idea behind graph2vec is inspired by document embedding models in NLP, particularly the doc2vec model55. In this analogy, an entire graph is treated as a document, and its rooted subgraphs are considered as words that compose the document. By leveraging this analogy, graph2vec extends the skip-gram model to learn embeddings for graphs in an unsupervised manner. Specifically, the algorithm samples rooted subgraphs around each node in a graph, assigns unique labels to these subgraphs using the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) relabeling strategy, and then applies a skip-gram model to learn graph embeddings. This process ensures that structurally similar graphs are mapped to nearby points in the embedding space, preserving their inherent properties.

One of the key advantages of graph2vec is its ability to capture structural equivalence in graphs. Unlike traditional methods that rely on linear substructures such as random walks or shortest paths, graph2vec considers non-linear substructures (i.e., rooted subgraphs), which provide a richer representation of the graph topology. This approach allows graph2vec to outperform existing substructure-based embedding methods and achieve competitive results compared to state-of-the-art graph kernels. Moreover, the embeddings produced by graph2vec are task-agnostic, meaning that they can be applied to various downstream tasks such as graph classification, clustering, and even as initializations for supervised learning models.

The training process of graph2vec involves two main steps: (1) extracting rooted subgraphs using the WL relabeling strategy, and (2) learning graph embeddings through a skip-gram model with negative sampling. Negative sampling is employed to reduce computational complexity by updating only a small subset of negative samples during each training iteration. This makes graph2vec computationally efficient while maintaining high accuracy. The resulting embeddings are optimized using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), ensuring that the learned representations are both meaningful and scalable.

The generator wants to create data indistinguishable from real data, while the discriminator wants to tell real data from generated data56. Goodfellow et al. (2014) recommended implementing a non-saturating loss for the generator, which provides much stronger gradients56.

Motivation of using PSO

Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) is a population-based stochastic optimization technique inspired by the social behavior of birds flocking. Since its introduction by Kennedy and Eberhart in 1995, PSO has gained significant popularity due to its simplicity, efficiency, and ability to solve complex optimization problems across various domains. The primary motivation for using PSO in this study stems from its robust performance in handling high-dimensional, non-linear, and multi-modal optimization problems, which are often encountered in real-world applications.

The PSO algorithm offers several advantages compared to other optimization algorithms, contributing to its popularity as a solution for various uses. The main benefits of PSO are as follows:

Easy implementation: PSO is relatively easy to understand and implement compared to other optimization algorithms. Its straightforward concept and low coding effort requirements make it accessible to many users.

Few algorithm parameters: PSO has a small number of algorithm parameters that need to be tuned, such as the population size and the inertia weight. This simplicity facilitates the application of the algorithm to various problems and reduces the complexity of parameter tuning.

Effective parallel operations: The PSO algorithm is well-suited for parallel computing because it operates without gradient information. The particles in the swarm can be updated independently, allowing for efficient utilization of parallel processing capabilities. This makes PSO well-suited for high-performance computing environments.

Efficient global exploration: PSO is known for efficiently conducting global search space exploration. Collaboratively, the particles search the solution space, exchanging information and adjusting their positions in response to the best solutions so far discovered. This exploration feature improves the algorithm chances of locating the global optimum and prevents it from becoming stuck in local optima.

Compared to genetic algorithms, PSO has advantages in terms of easy implementation, fewer parameters to adjust, and less computational complexity. Genetic algorithms typically require more genetic operators and involve higher computational costs due to operations like crossover and mutation. Therefore, in scenarios where simplicity, efficiency, and parallelization are important factors, PSO may be considered a favorable choice over genetic algorithms59.

The PSO algorithm operates by iteratively updating the positions and velocities of a swarm of particles in the search space. Each particle represents a potential solution to the optimization problem, and its movement is influenced by its own best-known position (personal best) and the best-known position of the entire swarm (global best). The adoption of PSO in our NAS framework is motivated by several key properties of the architecture search space. First, PSO is inherently compatible with ordinal architectural parameters, such as the indices of layers or the number of filters, where preserving ordering constraints is crucial. Its smooth, momentum-based velocity–position update rules maintain these natural order relations, unlike abrupt mutations in genetic algorithms which may break them.

Second, each candidate architecture in our method is represented as a fixed-length layer-index vector tailored to the search space, jointly encoding macro-level topology (e.g., layer sequence and connectivity) and micro-level configurations (e.g., operation types, kernel sizes, and pooling strategies). While some prior works use explicit two-level particle encoding, our representation implicitly covers both levels in a unified vector, enabling PSO to optimize them simultaneously.

Finally, it should be emphasized that graph embeddings generated via graph2vec are employed exclusively for training the surrogate model, and PSO operates directly on the fixed-length layer-index vectors rather than on the graph embeddings. This clear distinction separates the embedding stage from the PSO-driven search process, preventing confusion about their respective roles, and highlights the suitability of PSO for exploring high-dimensional, non-convex architecture spaces60.

Neural predictor provides the ability to rank neural architectures, which helps to determine the relative merits of different architectures. Instead of calculating the accuracy of each architecture, neural predictor can use the ranking score of the architectures, which is a different and more suitable criterion for evaluating different architectures in the neural architecture search environment. The procedure is as follows, when we apply two inputs to the neural predictor, it compares the score values of each input sample to determine which is higher. Implementation detailed of the surrogate model

The surrogate predictor adopts a RankNet-style feedforward neural network architecture. The input to the network is a 128-dimensional graph2vec embedding of an architecture, and the output is the predicted probability that the first architecture in a pair outperforms the second. The network consists of six fully-connected layers: 128 → 256 → 256 → 128 → 64 → 32 → 1 neurons, with ReLU6 activations and a final sigmoid output. Weights are initialized using Kaiming uniform initialization and biases set to zero.

Training samples are created pairwise from architectures in the current population, labeled according to the sign of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F=100 - val\_loss$$\end{document}. The training set is shuffled before learning. The network is trained for 40 epochs with a batch size of 512, using the Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.001) and a StepLR scheduler (step size 15, gamma 0.5). Binary cross-entropy loss is minimized. All training is performed on GPU.

The graph2vec embeddings are generated from the cell DAGs using a pretrained Doc2Vec model (dimension 128). The surrogate model is integrated in the ENAS search loop after the initial archive is populated with architectures from a random sampling of the NASBench search space.

Uncertainty estimation

To estimate the uncertainty associated with a candidate architecture, the network embedding procedure is repeated multiple times on the same graph representation with identical parameter settings. Although the input and parameters remain fixed, the stochasticity inherent in the embedding process produces slightly different vector representations in each repetition. These embeddings are evaluated individually by the surrogate performance predictor, and the standard deviation of the predicted performance values is reported as the uncertainty measure.

Particle swarm optimization algorithm

To avoid the completely random selection of individuals, the PSO algorithm is employed. The process begins with the random generation of particles, which are then updated in each generation using the PSO algorithm. In the early stages, the neural predictor network might not be fully trained, increasing the risk of incorrectly identifying the best architecture during the search process. Consequently, entering the evaluation phase prematurely could result in particles being prematurely excluded from the search process.

To mitigate this, a secondary PSO algorithm is executed separately. With each global search, the value obtained is added to the evaluation list for the next stage. This secondary search process is then terminated to allow a new search for another optimal value. The values discovered are subsequently entered into the environmental selection phase, ensuring a more thorough and accurate evaluation.

Architecture representation in search vs. surrogate phases

In our framework, the PSO algorithm operates over a search-specific parameterization of architectures, where each particle’s position vector directly encodes the indices of operations and node connections in a cell. The continuous position update from Eqs. (4) and (5) is followed by a rounding and mapping step that deterministically yields a valid discrete neural architecture.

In contrast, the 128-dimensional embeddings obtained via graph2vec are used exclusively for surrogate model training and prediction. These embeddings provide a continuous, learned representation of graph structure but are not decoded back to architectures during the search. This separation ensures that PSO search dynamics remain transparent and reproducible while the surrogate benefits from the rich structural information captured by embeddings.

Environmental selection

To simultaneously maintain high solution quality and promote diversity within the evolving population, we employ an environmental selection mechanism centered on a set of reference architectures. At each generation, all newly generated candidate architectures are first embedded and normalized, and the cosine distance between every offspring and each member of the current reference population is calculated. This yields a distance matrix that succinctly encodes structural similarities across the search space.

Using this matrix, each offspring is assigned to the closest reference (i.e., the one with which it shares the minimum cosine distance), resulting in the formation of clusters, each anchored around a distinct reference. This assignment procedure is designed to ensure that candidates are distributed across different regions of the embedding space and that no single reference is overwhelmed by excessively many assignments.

Within each cluster, only the most promising candidate, identified by its predicted fitness score, is selected to represent that region in the next generation’s population. This selection protocol is applied with further safeguards: depending on the current generation, either all reference members are forcibly replaced by their respective cluster’s best candidate, or replacement occurs only if the new candidate demonstrates higher predicted fitness. By repeating this process for every reference, we construct a new population that is both diverse (covering a range of architectural motifs) and comprised of strong performers. In this way, our approach not only minimizes the risk of premature convergence, but also prevents the loss of diversity caused by the population collapsing into a limited region of the search space or being overly attracted to dominant solutions61.

Overall, this reference-based clustering and selection scheme provides an efficient, scalable method for preserving diversity without the need for explicit diversity metrics or post-hoc adjustments.

Infill sampling

To judiciously select candidate architectures for real evaluation, a two-objective criterion is adopted at each generation. Specifically, for every architecture in the candidate pool, the surrogate model provides a prediction of fitness \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{f}(x)$$\end{document}. Along with this, the model’s predictive uncertainty is estimated for each architecture, representing the degree of confidence in its prediction.

To ensure balanced progress, candidates are prioritized using a non-dominated sorting approach on these two objectives: higher predicted fitness and higher uncertainty. An architecture is considered preferable if it either promises superior expected performance or if its evaluation could provide new information to further refine the surrogate model. The candidate pool is thus ranked into Pareto fronts, architectures in the leading fronts exhibit either high predictive scores or high uncertainty, or strike a balance between the two62.

In implementation, architectures from the highest-ranked fronts are incrementally chosen until the desired number of samples (K, e.g., 10) is reached. If the final selected front includes more candidates than needed, its members are further ordered by predicted fitness, and those with highest predicted values are selected to fill the remaining slots. This method ensures that both exploration (sampling less certain regions of the search space) and exploitation (focusing on promising candidate solutions) are effectively maintained throughout the search. The chosen candidates subsequently undergo true evaluation, updating the surrogate for the next generation.

Since this procedure is carried out after diversity-preserving environmental selection, the resulting sample set maintains architectural variety in every iteration.

Experiments

In this section, evaluations were conducted on three common search spaces from NASBench. The performance of the proposed method is examined on three NASBench search spaces and compared with existing NAS algorithms. All experiments were performed using an NVIDIA Geforce 1060 GTX GPU and Intel Core i7 8700 CPU.

NASBench search space

Three common search spaces in NASBench include NASBench101, NASBech201 and NASBench301. In the following, we will elaborate on these three search spaces in the NAS domain.

NASBench-10163 is a benchmark for NAS, specifically designed to evaluate the effectiveness of various neural network topologies. It consists of 423,000 distinct convolutional architectures, each trained and evaluated three times with different random initializations on the CIFAR-10 dataset. Architectures are constructed by stacking cells, which form the search space. Each cell is represented as a directed acyclic graph with five nodes, where each node corresponds to an operation, and the adjacency matrix indicates the connections between these operations. To manage the complexity of the search space, only three types of operations are permitted: 3 × 3 convolution, 1 × 1 convolution, and 3 × 3 max-pooling. Additionally, the maximum number of edges in the adjacency matrix is limited to 9, further constraining the possible architectural configurations.

NASBench-20164 serves as a comprehensive benchmark for NAS, comprising 15,625 unique architectures. Each architecture is built using four nodes and five possible operations: zeroize, skip-connect, 1 × 1 convolution, 3 × 3 convolution, and 3 × 3 average pooling. These architectures are represented graphically, where nodes correspond to feature maps and edges represent operations. NASBench-201 covers a wide variety of architectures, offering a diverse range of configurations for evaluation. It employs multiple datasets, including CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet-16–120, to assess the architectures. For each architecture, test accuracy, validation, and training metrics are reported, providing a comprehensive performance assessment.

NASBench-30165 is a surrogate benchmark tailored for large-scale neural architecture search, specifically extending to the DARTS search space. Unlike NASBench-101 and NASBench-201, which provide pre-computed performance metrics for all architectures, NASBench-301 employs a high-fidelity surrogate model trained on a subset of real evaluated architectures to predict performance for unseen ones. The search space consists of convolutional and reduction cells, each represented as a directed acyclic graph with seven nodes: two fixed input nodes, four intermediate nodes each with two incoming edges, and one output node that concatenates the outputs from the intermediate nodes. Edges represent operations selected from a predefined set, including separable convolutions (3 × 3, 5 × 5), dilated convolutions (3 × 3, 5 × 5), skip-connections, and zero-operations. The DARTS-like topology allows for a vast number of possible configurations, making exhaustive evaluation infeasible. NASBench-301 uses its surrogate predictor to provide fast accuracy estimates, enabling efficient benchmarking of NAS algorithms on large, continuous search spaces while maintaining a close approximation to real training results.

Top Architectures from NAS-Bench-201: For NAS-Bench-201, three datasets, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet-16–120, were examined. The optimized architectures in this space consist of a combination of 3 × 3 and 1 × 1 convolutions along with skip connections. Depending on the complexity and size of the dataset, these architectures achieved varying accuracies, ranging from 91.61% to 46.77%. For instance, in the case of the CIFAR-100 dataset, the architectural combination of multiple 3 × 3 convolutions and skip connections yielded an accuracy of 73.49%.

These results clearly demonstrate that selecting the best architecture for each dataset requires a deep understanding of the data’s characteristics and the model’s complexities. The optimized architectures in NAS-Bench-201 have shown superior performance across various datasets, particularly due to their flexibility in combining different layers and utilizing skip connections. This is especially evident in more complex datasets like ImageNet-16–120.

Evaluation of GAN module and infill criterion

To rigorously assess the contribution of other key components of GNE-NAS, supplementary experiments were conducted on NASBench-201 with the CIFAR-10 dataset, focusing on two elements: (i) the GAN-based augmentation module, and (ii) the uncertainty term in the infill criterion. In the first case, the GAN module, responsible for generating synthetic architectures during the search process, was removed. As shown in Table 5, this modification resulted in a notable decrease in standard deviation of accuracy by 0.06 compared to the full configuration. This outcome indicates that the GAN module plays a crucial role in training the surrogate model. In the second case, the uncertainty term was removed from the infill criterion, and only the predicted mean performance was used as the basis for selecting candidate architectures. This change also led to a drop in validation accuracy (− 0.02%) and a reduction in the STD by about 0.07%, representing a substantial decrease in the framework’s ability to explore promising yet uncertain regions, along with a decline in the quality of uncertainty estimation. Conclusions

This paper aims to approximate the true accuracy of trained neural networks using a neural predictor as a surrogate model. Utilize the graph2vec model to represent each neural network into 128-dimensional vectors. Additionally, for enhancing the search capability and effective updating of the surrogate model, the WGAN-GP network is employed to generate similar samples. Furthermore, online learning significantly contributes to keeping the surrogate model up to date. Moreover, using the particle swarm optimization algorithm in the optimal architecture search process enables the GNE-NAS algorithm to achieve competitive performance in the NasBench101, NasBench201 and NasBench301 search spaces.

Future research is needed to apply the GNE-NAS method to other tasks like segmentation, object detection, digital image denoising, and Natural Language Processing (NLP). Moreover, a promising research direction involves developing complementary sampling techniques and adaptive environmental selection strategies to align with diverse search spaces.
