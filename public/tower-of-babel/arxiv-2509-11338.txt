Next-Generation Reservoir Computing for Dynamical Inference
Rok Cestnik, Erik A. Martens

Abstract
We present a simple and scalable implementation of next-generation reservoir computing for modeling dynamical systems from time series data. Our approach uses a pseudorandom nonlinear projection of time-delay embedded input, allowing an arbitrary dimension of the feature space, thus providing a flexible alternative to the polynomial-based projections used in previous next-generation reservoir computing variants. We apply the method to benchmark tasks — including attractor reconstruction and bifurcation diagram estimation — using only partial and noisy observations. We also include an exploratory example of estimating asymptotic oscillation phases. The models remain stable over long rollouts and generalize beyond training data. This framework enables the precise control of system state and is well suited for surrogate modeling and digital twin applications.

I
Introduction

Reservoir computing (RC) has emerged as a powerful and versatile machine learning framework for processing temporal data, particularly in tasks involving time series prediction and the inference of latent variables in dynamical systems
Jaeger 2001
;
Maass et al. 2002
;
Lukoševičius and Jaeger 2009
.
As
Lu et al. 2018
describe,
“Reservoir computing is a machine-learning approach that has demonstrated success at a variety of tasks, including time series prediction and inferring unmeasured variables of a dynamical system from measured variables”
.
In its classical form, RC leverages a high-dimensional, nonlinear dynamical system — referred to as the
reservoir
— which is driven by an input signal. The reservoir’s response is then post-processed with a linear map to obtain the desired output, commonly referred to as
readout
. For a general overview of the theory and state of the art, see also Refs.
Tanaka et al. 2019
;
Nakajima and Fischer 2021
.

A key advantage of RC lies in its simplicity: the reservoir itself operates as a static component that does not require training, while only the output layer (readout) is optimized, typically through linear regression.
This design allows for implementations of the reservoir in both software — often utilizing recurrent neural networks — and physical systems. Such flexibility has led in a diverse array of physical implementations, including water buckets
Fernando and Sojakka 2003
, optical systems
Appeltant et al. 2011
, electronic delay circuits
Haynes et al. 2015
, and photonic devices
Larger et al. 2017
.
The capacity to treat the reservoir as a black box, without needing a detailed model or adjustable internal parameters, sets RC apart from other machine learning paradigms, such as deep learning.

While these systems have demonstrated impressive capabilities
Jaeger and Haas 2004
;
Parlitz and Hornstein 2005
;
Wyffels and Schrauwen 2010
;
Pathak et al. 2017
;
Lu et al. 2017
;
Zimmermann and Parlitz 2018
, classical RC has limitations, particularly in terms of interpretability and control. The internal state of the reservoir is often opaque, and perturbing or probing the system in a controlled manner can be challenging. These limitations have motivated the development of a new paradigm:
next-generation reservoir computing (NGRC)

Gauthier et al. 2021
.

NGRC departs from the traditional architecture by eliminating the
explicit
reservoir altogether. Instead, it constructs a high-dimensional feature space of dimension,
M
, (corresponding to an
implicit
reservoir) through a deterministic projection of the input signal. This results in a simple, fully transparent mapping from one time step to the next, where the system state is entirely known and controllable. Letting
u(t)
denote the input and
r(t)
the projected state, the evolution becomes a direct function
r(t+1)=F(r(t),u(t))
, enabling precise interventions and measurements. This is particularly advantageous in applications requiring system perturbation and response analysis, such as the computation of phase response curves
Smeal et al. 2010
;
Wedgwood et al. 2013
;
Pietras and Daffertshofer 2021
. See Sec.
1(b)
and Fig.
1
for a more detailed comparison of the two methods.

When using NGRC a challenge remains to design a feature projection algorithm that is independent of the observation dimension, and the desired number of final features.
For example, suppose the observation is
N
-dimensional with a time-embedding of
H
steps.
The dimension of the input to the reservoir is then
NH
. Further, suppose we use a feature expansion built on pair-wise combinations, we end up with
(NH)^{2}
features. It is desirable that changing the observation dimension,
NH
, does not require changing the reservoir dimension
M
, i.e., the method is
scalable
.

Our work adopts the next-generation reservoir computing framework with an emphasis on scalability and interpretability.
In particular, we introduce a pseudorandom nonlinear projection of time-delay embedded input as a simple and dimension-independent alternative to the polynomial-based projections used previously.
This projection maintains flexibility across different system sizes while avoiding the need to manage opaque internal reservoir states. The approach is illustrated on benchmark tasks such as attractor reconstruction, bifurcation diagram estimation, and phase recovery, showing stable long rollouts and robustness to partial or noisy data. We further explore asymptotic phase estimation, which to our knowledge has not yet been demonstrated in this context.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In Sec.
II
, we briefly review classical RC and NGRC approaches.
Sec.
III
explains our methods and introduces the projection method used to lift the input into a high-dimensional space (Sec.
III.2
), followed by details on training the linear model (Sec.
III.3
) and tracking prediction errors (Sec.
III.4
).
In Sec.
IV
we present three example applications to demonstrate the method’s capabilities: the reconstruction of a chaotic attractor, the estimation of a bifurcation diagram, and the recovery of asymptotic phases of an oscillatory process.
Finally, we close with a discussion of the implications and limitations of our approach in Sec.
V
.

II.0.1
Traditional Reservoir Computing (RC)

We first review the classical reservoir computing (RC) framework (Lu
et al.

Lu et al. 2018
), summarized diagrammatically in Fig.
1(a)
.
Let
\mathbf{u}(t)
denote the input (or measurement) vector and
\mathbf{r}(t)
the internal state of the reservoir. The reservoir evolves according to the dynamical system,

\dot{\mathbf{r}}(t)=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{r}(t),\mathbf{u}(t)),

(1)

where
\mathbf{f}
is an appropriately chosen function that is differentiable and typically high-dimensional.

A common choice for the
reservoir function

\mathbf{f}
is

\mathbf{f}=\gamma\left(-\mathbf{r}+\tanh(\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{r}+\sigma\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{in}}\cdot\mathbf{u})\right).

(2)

Thus, updating the reservoir
\mathbf{r}
involves a nonlinear transformation (e.g.,
\tanh
) of a linear combination of the reservoir state
\mathbf{r}
and the input signal
\mathbf{u}
. The input signal,
\mathbf{u}
, is linearly mapped into the reservoir via weights,
\mathbf{W}_{\text{in}}
, and the internal reservoir dynamics,
\mathbf{r}
, is governed by a (possibly random) matrix,
\mathbf{M}
. In practice, the exact form of
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{W}_{\text{in}}
does not need to be known, however, they need to remain unchanged throughout the phases of training and prediction.

Reservoirs of this type are commonly referred to as “echo state networks” (ESNs)
Hermans and Schrauwen 2010
;
Hermans and Schrauwen 2012
;
Grigoryeva and Ortega 2018
;
Grigoryeva et al. 2021
;
Jaeger and Haas 2004
. A key requirement is the
echo state property

Jaeger 2001
;
Manjunath and Jaeger 2013
;
Yildiz et al. 2012
, which ensures that the reservoir state
\mathbf{r}(t)
in the asymptotic time limit does not depend on initial conditions but only on the input history. That is, the reservoir must effectively “forget” its initial state in order to respond reliably to the input.

The output of the reservoir, i.e., the estimated system state
\mathbf{u}(t)
, is obtained as a readout from the reservoir state via a linear output layer,

\mathbf{u}(t)=\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}\cdot\mathbf{r}(t),

where
\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}
is trained, e.g., via linear regression, to learn the mapping
\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}
based on time series predictions.
Often, the output layer uses a concatenation of
\mathbf{r}(t)
with its element-wise square,
\mathbf{r}\odot\mathbf{r}
, to break linear symmetries (e.g., sign reversals) and capture nonlinear structure; for further details on RC refer to, e.g., Ref.
Lu et al. 2018
.

In reservoir computing, the equations governing the reservoir dynamics and output are used during both the training and predicting phases. During the
training phase
, the reservoir state
\mathbf{r}(t)
evolves according to the input signal
\mathbf{u}(t)
, and the output
\mathbf{u}(t)=\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}\cdot\mathbf{r}(t)
is used for learning the mapping represented by the weight matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}
. In the
prediction phase
, the trained output layer
\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}
is applied to the reservoir state to determine the new input signal
\mathbf{u}(t)
, which then in turn continues drive the evolution of the reservoir. (We note that some literature distinguishes variables for the training and prediction phases, but for our purposes we maintain the same notation for
\mathbf{r}(t)
and
\mathbf{u}(t)
, regardless of whether they represent values for training or prediction phases.)
In this context, readout weights are parameters optimized during the training phase, whereas the projection dimension
M
and other process defining quantities often are referred to as
hyperparameters
, are set before training and held fixed during prediction.

One of the main advantages of RC over traditional recurrent neural networks is that training is simple: only the output layer
\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}
needs to be trained (learned), while the reservoir dynamics itself remains fixed and untrained. For the readout, linear mapping to be expressive enough, the reservoir dimension must be sufficiently high. In effect, RC transforms a low-dimensional nonlinear problem into a high-dimensional linear one.

Moreover, a notable strength of reservoir computing is that the reservoir function
\mathbf{f}
can be chosen quite freely — it does not even need to be precisely known. This flexibility enables the use of physical systems as reservoirs, particularly in photonic or optical platforms, which offer high bandwidth and fast computation speeds
Larger et al. 2012
.

II.0.2
Next Generation Reservoir Computing (NGRC)

Next-generation reservoir computing (NGRC) is a simplified alternative to traditional RC. Instead of relying on an explicitly simulated dynamical system as the reservoir, NGRC uses a static, implicit reservoir structure based on nonlinear vector autoregression
Gauthier et al. 2021
. The central idea is to take the time-delay embedded state vector,

\mathbf{u}_{H}(t):=[\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{u}(t-\tau),\mathbf{u}(t-2\tau),\dots,\mathbf{u}(t-H\tau)],

and map it nonlinearly into a high-dimensional space,

\mathbf{r}(t)=P(\mathbf{u}_{H}(t)),

as illustrated in Fig.
1(b)
.
The projected state
P(\mathbf{u}_{H}(t))
serves as the reservoir state
\mathbf{r}(t)
, forming the implicit reservoir.
Prediction of the next state
\mathbf{u}(t+\tau)
is then performed — as before in RC — using a simple linear model,

\mathbf{u}(t+\tau)=\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}\cdot P(\mathbf{u}_{H}(t)),

(3)

which constitutes the full one-step prediction mechanism of the NGRC approach.
The only trainable parameter is the output weight matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}
which can be learned by fitting the linear relation (
3
) to a training data set.

This approach closely aligns with Koopman operator theory
Koopman 1931
;
Mezić 2005
;
Brunton et al. 2022
, which asserts that the dynamics of a nonlinear system can be represented by a linear operator acting on a suitably chosen set of observables.
In NGRC, the delay-embedded state vector
\mathbf{u}_{H}(t)
is mapped into a higher-dimensional space, resulting in the state
\mathbf{r}(t)=P(\mathbf{u}_{H}(t))
, which serves as such a set of observables, effectively lifting the system into a higher-dimensional function space where its evolution becomes approximately linear.
Rather than explicitly constructing the Koopman operator, NGRC learns a linear mapping
\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}
between
\mathbf{r}(t)
and the predicted next state
\mathbf{u}(t+\tau)
. The linearity of
\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}
is consistent with the Koopman framework, but NGRC sidesteps the need to estimate the full operator. Finally, we note that, while this operation often is referred to as a “projection” in literature, we may more aptly think of this operation as a “lifting”, as it maps the state into a higher-dimensional space rather than projecting onto a subspace.

An advantage of the NGRC architecture is that the time-delay embedding is explicit, eliminating the long transients required in traditional RC to forget intitial conditions.
This simplification makes NGRC more practical for manipulating and initializing states, particularly in digital twin settings, where constructing meaningful initial conditions is often intractable due to the “black box” nature of the reservoir.

Despite its simplicity, NGRC has demonstrated strong performance on several challenging tasks
Gauthier et al. 2021
, including (1) short-term forecasting, (2) attractor reconstruction, and (3) generalization to unseen regions of the state space.

III
Methods

III.1
Scope of This Work

We introduce a simple, scalable projection scheme to construct the high-dimensional feature space used in next-generation reservoir computing.
This projection involves pseudo-random mixing of the delay-embedded measurement vector
\mathbf{u}_{H}
, and is detailed in Sec.
III.2
.

Using this projection, we train NGRC models on several representative dynamical systems, leveraging both complete and partial observations.
We demonstrate that these models accurately reproduce short-term trajectories, long-term attractors, asymptotic phases, and bifurcation diagrams.
Notably, we show that bifurcation diagrams can be inferred even when the bifurcation parameter varies in time as a correlated stochastic process
— a scenario often arising in non-stationary data collection conditions, similar to the recurrent neural network approach in
Cestnik and Abel 2019
.

Our NGRC models remain stable over long prediction horizons and do not exhibit any transversal instabilities – it seems that even asymptotically the integrator does not drift away and remains on the same attractor
Ratas and Pyragas 2024
.
This stability enables us to estimate bifurcation structure directly from a single long trajectory, employing a quasi-continuation approach.

III.2
Projection
P

In Ref.
Gauthier et al. 2021
, the projection is achieved by constructing the Volterra series of the measurements
\mathbf{u}_{H}
, a series which contains monomials formed by multiplying the components of the input signal. In Ref.
Ratas and Pyragas 2024
, Chebyshev polynomials were used instead.
Various processes may thus be employed for this projection, but a limitation of such polynomial-based expansions is that the projection dimension grows with the size of the input vector, so increasing the observation dimension or embedding depth directly increases the number of features. To avoid this dependency, we adopt a pseudorandom projection scheme based on pairwise mixing of input components. This approach makes the feature dimension
M
a tunable parameter independent of the input size, thereby offering scalability across different system dimensions.

The projection
P
embeds (lifts) the state vector
\mathbf{u}_{H}
into a higher-dimensional space, with
P(\mathbf{u}_{H})=[p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},...,p_{M}]
, where the dimension
M\gg 1
is a free parameter, typically chosen to be sufficiently large.
For this task, we construct the projection using a pseudo-random shuffled sequence of indices, which is deterministic and reproducible.

First, we normalize the values of the input vector
\mathbf{u}_{H}
to lie within the unit interval
(0,1)
.
To maintain numerical stability, a small positive margin
\epsilon>0
is introduced, ensuring the values fall within (
\epsilon,1-\epsilon
) and never leave the unit interval during computation.

The measurement vector
\mathbf{u}
is
N
-dimensional, and the time-delay embedded vector
\mathbf{u}_{H}
contains
H
delayed samples, resulting in a complete input vector of size
N\times H
components:

\displaystyle\begin{split}[&u_{1}(t),u_{1}(t-\tau),\ldots,u_{1}(t-H\cdot\tau),\\
&u_{2}(t),u_{2}(t-\tau),\ldots,u_{2}(t-H\cdot\tau),\\
&\quad\quad\quad\vdots\\
&u_{N}(t),u_{N}(t-\tau),\ldots,u_{N}(t-H\cdot\tau)].\end{split}

(4)

These initial values form the starting point for constructing the projected vector
P(\mathbf{u}_{H})
.
To distinguish them from the new features generated during the projection process, we assign non-positive indices:

p_{-N\cdot H},\ p_{-N\cdot H+1},\ \ldots,\ p_{-1},\ p_{0},

where, for example,
p_{-N\cdot H}=u_{1}(t)
and
p_{0}=u_{N}(t-H\cdot\tau)
.

Next, we generate the projected vector of size
M
(with
M\gg N\times H
), by iteratively creating new values, i.e., incrementing
m=1,\ldots,M
, from component pairs
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
with
i,j<m
.
Each new component is defined by the nonlinear combination,

p_{m}=(1-p_{i})^{p_{j}}.

(5)

where the indices
i
and
j
are chosen from a pseudo-random but deterministic sequence, seeded for reproducibility.
Any duplicate values are discarded to prevent degeneracy and to ensure that feature matrix reamins full-rank during training.

To illustrate this process, the first new value,
p_{1}
, is created by combining two pseudo-randomly selected elements from the initial set of values with non-positive indices (
p_{i},p_{j}
where
i,j\leq 0
).
The next value,
p_{2}
, can now incorporate
p_{1}
along with the original components, and this process continues.
Consequently, the pool of available elements grows with each step, rapidly increasing the diversity of possible pairings used in the projection.

Importantly, the map (
5
) preserves the unit interval: if
p_{i},p_{j}\in(0,1)
, then
p_{m}
will also remains within that range.
This process is repeated until
M
distinct components are obtained. In practice, we exclude the original components (
4
) from the final projected vector, although this step is not strictly necessary.
The resulting
M
-dimensional vector
[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{M}]
is then used as the projected state
P(\mathbf{u}_{H})
.

A notable property of this projection scheme is that it preserves the unit interval: all projected components lie within
(0,1)
, are typically of the same order, and do not aggregate around particular values.
Figure
2
shows the distribution of
p_{m}
values for for a trajectory of the Lorenz system.
The values are distributed smoothly and densely across the interval, contrasting monomial-based projections which often produce extreme values, either very small (near zero) or excessively large, depending on the scaling of the input signal.

Figure 2
:
Distribution of
p_{m}
values generated by the projection
P
for a long time series of the Lorenz system (
10
).
The values remain bounded within the unit interval and are smoothly distributed, reflecting the stable and well-scaled nature of the projection scheme.

III.3
Optimizing the linear model

Assuming that the projection
P
has been chosen appropriately, we can expect that the linear relation (
3
) provides a good approximation of the system dynamics and can be fitted directly to data.
Given the observed states
\mathbf{u}(t)
at several time points, we construct their delay-embedded forms
\mathbf{u}_{H}(t)
, compute the corresponding projections
P(\mathbf{u}_{H}(t))
, and then optimize the relation (
3
) over all available training time points.
Fitting the model corresponds to finding the optimal output weight matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}
.
Since the relation is linear, the optimization is straightforward and can be performed using standard techniques such as least squares regression or singular value decomposition
Press 2007
.

The state vector
\mathbf{u}
has dimension
N
, while the projected vector
P(\mathbf{u}_{H})
is a vector of size
M
, where typically
M\gg 1
and
N
may be small (even
N=1
).
Thus, the output matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}
has dimensions
N\times M
.
Each time point contributes one instance of the relation (
3
), and the entire training dataset over
T
time points can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle=[\mathbf{u}(t),\ \mathbf{u}(t-\tau),\ \mathbf{u}(t-2\tau),\ \ldots,\ \mathbf{u}(t-T\tau)]

(6a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=[P(\mathbf{u}_{H}(t)),\ P(\mathbf{u}_{H}(t-\tau)),\ \ldots,\ P(\mathbf{u}_{H}(t-T\tau))]

(6b)

which yields the matrix relation:

\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}\mathbf{P}.

(7)

The optimal output weights
\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}
are then obtained via least squares minimization:

\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}^{T}\left(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{P}^{T}\right)^{-1}.

(8)

III.4
Tracking errors

Once the model
\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}
has been determined, we evaluate its accuracy by tracking the mean squared error of the one-step prediction. For each time point
n
, we compare the true next state
\mathbf{u}((n+1)\tau)
with the predicted state
\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}P(\mathbf{u}_{H}(n\tau))
, where
P(\mathbf{u}_{H}(n\tau))
is the projected feature vector constructed from the previous
H
delay-embedded states. The mean squared error is then

E=\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{T}\left\|\mathbf{u}((n+1)\tau)-\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}\,P(\mathbf{u}_{H}(n\tau))\right\|^{2},

(9)

where
||\cdot||
denotes the Euclidean norm and
T
is the number of prediction steps.
This error can be computed both on the training dataset and on a separate validation set that was not used during fitting.

III.5
Validation

Validation is the assessment of model performance on data not used during training, providing a measure of how well the model generalizes to new, unseen inputs.

The projection dimension
M
is a free parameter and can be treated as a hyperparameter of the method.
While the training error (
9
) typically decreases with increasing
M
, monitoring the validation error is essential to avoid overfitting (i.e., as
M
becomes larger, the model can fit the data, including fluctuations or noise, too closely, effectively “remembering” and thus performs poorly on unseen data in the validation set).

Additionally, the least squares solution relies on the invertibility of the matrix
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{P}^{T}
, which can become problematic for simple systems or when the projection dimension
M
is large.
A practical way to mitigate this issue is to add a small amount of measurement noise to the original state vectors
\mathbf{u}
, which improves numerical stability and can also act as a form of regularization.

Figure
3
shows the training and validation errors (
9
) as a function of
M
, for two different levels of Gaussian measurement noise.
In the noise-free case, the error decreases only up to about
M=200
, after which it increases sharply, leading to models that are effectively unusable.
In contrast, adding 1% Gaussian noise stabilizes the inference and prevents this error blow-up for moderate values of
M
.
However, excessive noise can also degrade model quality, so we selected the 1% level as a reasonable compromise — at which point the training and validation errors remain comparable across a range of
M
. We consistently use 1% measurement noise for all examples going further.

Figure 3

Mean squared error for one-step prediction as a function of the projection dimension
M
, shown for two different measurement noise levels: 0% (top) and 1% (bottom).
Without measurement noise (top), the training error decreases initially but diverges from the validation error as
M
increases, indicating overfitting and numerical instability — particularly beyond
M=300
.

M
.
This motivates our choice to use 1% noise in all subsequent experiments, as a compromise between stability and predictive accuracy.
Too much measurement noise, however, degrades performance, so a trade-off must be considered.
All results are computed using long time series of length
T=10^{5}
for both training and validation.

IV
Examples

IV.1
Reconstruction of attractor from partial observations

We train the reservoir model (
3
) using the random projection
P
described in Sec.
III.2
. The training data comes from two standard chaotic systems, the Lorenz system and the Rössler system, which are three dimensional models formulated in variables
x(t),y(t),z(t)
see Eqs. (
10
) and Eqs. (
11
), respectively. We generate long trajectories from each system, but we only use the scalar variable
x(t)
for training. The model is trained for one-step prediction. After training, it can be used to predict long-term evolution by iterating its own outputs.

First, we compare short-time predictions from the reservoir model with the true trajectories. Next, we let the model run freely for a long time and plot the time-embedded trajectory alongside one from the true system.

For the experiments, we fix some (hyper-)parameters:
M=1000

IV.3
Estimation of asymptotic phases

We again consider the noisy Rössler system (
12
), however, now in a parameter regime where the deterministic system exhibits a stable period-1 limit cycle:
a=0.2
,
b=1.6
,
c=5.7
.
We train a reservoir model on time series of the variables
x(t)
,
y(t)
, and
z(t)
. Unlike in the bifurcation diagram experiment (Sec.
IV.2
), we do not provide the noise signal
\eta(t)
as an explicit input to the reservoir.
Instead,
\eta(t)
only perturbs the training trajectory (and is thus hidden from the reservoir input), introducing variability that helps the data explore the state space more broadly. The reservoir is expected to “average out” this noise and approximate the dynamics of the unperturbed Rössler system over a wider range of states.

We then evaluate the model’s ability to recover asymptotic phase. This is a hard task to accomplish, as it requires coherent long-term prediction and a globally accurate model of the evolution of the system.
To test this, we generate a new trajectory from the noisy Rössler system (
12
) — one that has not been used in the training phase — to use as test data. For each point along this trajectory, we evolve the system forward using the reservoir model until it converges to the limit cycle, and then estimate its asymptotic phase from the position on the cycle. The same is done with the true ODEs for comparison.
We visualize the estimated phases along the test trajectory using a color palette. The reservoir accurately recovers the phase structure, except in regions of state space that were underrepresented in the training data; see Fig.
10
.

V
Discussion

In our work, we built on the next-generation reservoir computing framework (NGRC) and proposed a projection method based on pseudorandom nonlinear features. An advantage of this approach is that the number of projected features
M
can be set independently of the input dimension, whereas in polynomial-based projections the feature space typically depends on the input size. We demonstrated the utility of this scalable scheme across several canonical examples, including attractor reconstruction, bifurcation diagram estimation, and asymptotic phase inference. The models showed robust performance even when trained on limited or non-stationary data, and they could generalize to parameters and dynamics not seen during training. These features suggest that next-generation reservoir computing may be a practical tool for applications ranging from digital twins and surrogate modeling to problems in climate science, finance, and biology.

One of the central advantages of NGRC over classical reservoir computing lies in its transparency and manipulability.
Because NGRC evolves entirely in the space of observable inputs, it allows direct and global perturbations of the system state within this observable space (i.e., by modifying the delay-embedded measurement vector
\mathbf{u}_{H}
).
This feature is particularly valuable for digital twin and surrogate modeling tasks, where probing system responses to hypothetical inputs or counterfactual states (i.e., hypothetical system states not visited in the observed trajectory) is essential.
In contrast, classical RC relies on evolving a high-dimensional reservoir state
\mathbf{r}(t)
, which is difficult to interpret or initialize, making it challenging to explore off-attractor or perform controlled interventions.

Another strength of NGRC is its use of explicit time-delay embedding, which eliminates the need for long transients to “wash out” initial conditions, as required in classical RC.
This approach makes it easier to analyze the model, set initial states, and perform controlled simulations.
Even though the projection
P(\mathbf{u}_{H})
may involve a black-box transformation, the evolution remains anchored in physically meaningful variables, enhancing interpretability and stability.

A known challenge in NGRC, highlighted in Ref.
Ratas and Pyragas 2024
, is the risk of transversal instability, where small perturbations orthogonal to the attractor direction can grow, degrading long-term predictions.
Interestingly, we did not observe such instabilities in any of the examples tested.
The models remained stable over long trajectories, including during bifurcation diagram estimation via quasi-continuation.
Although we did not conduct a systematic study of this effect, we speculate that adding small amounts of measurement noise during training acts as a form of implicit regularization and may contribute to improved transversal stability.

One promising direction for future work is to apply NGRC to network inference.
By training NGRC models on multivariate time series from interacting systems, one can construct a complete model of the network, which can then be interrogated using surrogate modeling or digital twin techniques to estimate both the intrinsic dynamics of individual units as well as their functional connectivity patterns.
