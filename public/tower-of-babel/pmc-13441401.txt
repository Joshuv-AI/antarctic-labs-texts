The 2025 ATS/ERS update of the international multidisciplinary classification of the interstitial pneumonias: implications for the pathologist
Authors: Andrew G Nicholson, Wendy A Cooper, Aurelie Fabre, William Travis, Lida P Hariri, Ayodeji Adegunsoye, Sara Piciucchi, Christopher J Ryerson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA consensus classification for idiopathic interstitial pneumonias was first published in 2002, providing terminology for clinicians, radiologists and pathologists as well as highlighting that a multidisciplinary approach was best clinical practice. This comprised seven histological patterns that had corresponding idiopathic multidisciplinary diagnoses. This classification was updated in 2013, with the addition of an eighth histological pattern. Since 2013, there have been further advances in our understanding of interstitial pneumonias, in particular relating to idiopathic versus secondary disease, advances in molecular pathology and recognition of progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF), which have led to a further update in 2025. This review highlights the changes relevant to pathologists reporting interstitial pneumonias. Major changes include (1) expansion beyond idiopathic interstitial pneumonias to also include secondary causes, (2) subclassification as interstitial (fibrotic vs non‐fibrotic) and alveolar filling disorders, (3) expansion to include additional patterns (e.g. bronchiolocentric interstitial pneumonia), (4) improved terminology that better reflects histogenesis and (5) consideration of diagnostic confidence in biopsy evaluation. Pathologists also need to be aware of the advantages and limitations of cryobiopsy interpretation and the importance of reporting features that point towards a secondary cause rather than idiopathic disease. The 2025 classification provides a framework for a methodological approach to reporting biopsies in patients with interstitial pneumonia, which should be used prospectively for both diagnosis and research.Keywords: classification, diagnosis, interstitial lung disease, pathology, pulmonary fibrosis

Introduction

A consensus classification for idiopathic interstitial pneumonias was first published in 2002, providing terminology for clinicians, radiologists and pathologists, as well as highlighting that a multidisciplinary approach was the best clinical practice. 1 The 2002 classification comprised seven histological patterns, with each having a corresponding idiopathic multidisciplinary diagnosis. This classification was updated in 2013, including formal recognition of nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) as a major pathological pattern with a corresponding idiopathic multidisciplinary diagnosis, and the addition of pleuroparenchymal fibroelastosis (PPFE) as an eighth entity. From a histological standpoint, there was also proposed inclusion of bronchiolocentric interstitial pneumonia (BIP) and acute fibrinous organising pneumonia (AFOP) as histological patterns, although without sufficient evidence to warrant their inclusion as individual multidisciplinary entities. 2 Since 2013, there have been further advances in our understanding of interstitial pneumonias, in particular relating to idiopathic versus secondary disease, advances in molecular pathology and recognition of progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF) as an overarching term relating to potential anti‐fibrotic therapies. 3 , 4 This led to a further international multidisciplinary initiative sponsored by the European Respiratory Society (ERS) and American Thoracic Society (ATS) to review the literature since the 2013 publication, resulting in a third update in 2025. 5 The histological patterns from this 2025 classification are presented in Table 1, highlighting the key histological features and their clinical correlates. This review discusses the major changes and then highlights their potential impact for pathologists when reporting or researching interstitial pneumonias.

Major changes

The five major changes in the 2025 update are listed below and then reviewed further in the context of individual histological patterns.

Expansion beyond Idiopathic Interstitial Pneumonias to Also Include Secondary Causes

Since the first classification in 2002, there has been increasing realization that all histological patterns can be secondary as well as idiopathic, albeit with secondary disease being more common in some patterns (e.g. lymphoid interstitial pneumonia [LIP], organising pneumonia [OP], diffuse alveolar damage [DAD]) than others (e.g. usual interstitial pneumonia [UIP]). It was therefore decided that the word ‘idiopathic’ would be removed from the heading of this document to better reflect the appropriateness of including all interstitial pneumonias within a single classification framework. Despite including secondary and idiopathic entities within a single framework, there remains clinical relevance in identifying secondary causes. Therefore, when assessing biopsies, pathologists should look for histological features that may identify a potential cause (see the individual sections below).

Subclassification as Interstitial (Fibrotic Vs Non‐fibrotic) and Alveolar Filling Disorders

The 2025 classification proposes separating patterns into interstitial and alveolar filling disorders (i.e. ‘pneumonias’) based on the predominant location of lung injury, recognising that pure interstitial or pure alveolar disorders are uncommon and that both compartments are frequently affected in any given disease entity. Interstitial disorders are further subgrouped into fibrotic and non‐fibrotic (or cellular) presentations, which is particularly important for NSIP and BIP patterns that can present across this spectrum. This advances the primarily clinical subcategorisation proposed in 2013 of chronic fibrosing, smoking‐related and acute/subacute subtypes, and highlights the importance for pathologists of reporting the presence of interstitial fibrosis in the context of any interstitial pneumonia pattern. Reporting the presence of fibrosis is relevant in the context of clinical trials demonstrating the benefit of multiple anti‐fibrotic medications in patients with PPF, regardless of underlying diagnosis. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

Expansion to Include Additional Patterns

Including secondary causes of interstitial pneumonias mandated the inclusion of hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) and other disorders associated with bronchiolocentricity in the classification. ‘BIP’ was coined as the term for these patterns that are characterised not only by bronchiolocentricity within lung biopsies as proposed in 2013, 2 but also with corresponding imaging features and a provisional idiopathic multidisciplinary entity. This approach improves consistency of the ontologic framework, whereby pathology and imaging provide a diagnostic morphological pattern (e.g. BIP pattern) and a corresponding differential diagnoses, but the final multidisciplinary diagnosis (e.g. HP) is determined by multidisciplinary discussion. There was considerable debate as to whether HP should be a major category separate from BIP or whether HP should be a subcategory of BIP, with the latter being the eventual but not unanimous consensus. The majority of the classification update committee prioritised the evidence that some bronchiolocentric cases with imaging typical of HP are due to other causes, such as CTD‐ILD, aspiration and drug‐induced ILD. 10 , 11 If there is no identifiable cause after multidisciplinary review, then cases are suggested to be provisionally classified as ‘idiopathic BIP’, which is intended to highlight the importance of ongoing active surveillance for an underlying cause. The updated classification approach similarly included other patterns that are more often associated with a clear cause and were thus not included in previous documents focusing on idiopathic diagnoses (e.g. eosinophilic pneumonias, lipoid pneumonia). These updates better incorporate these and other patterns and multidisciplinary diagnoses to provide a more comprehensive classification framework.

Improved Terminology that Reflects Histogenesis

A major goal of the updated classification document was to use terms that better reflect histogenesis. ‘Idiopathic diffuse alveolar damage’ (DAD) therefore replaces acute interstitial pneumonia (AIP), which was considered an imprecise term given the potential for acute presentations of other interstitial pneumonias. ‘Alveolar macrophage pneumonia’ (AMP) is introduced to replace the pathologically inaccurate term of desquamative interstitial pneumonia (DIP), which is not the result of alveolar pneumocyte desquamation but the accumulation of macrophages within alveoli. These changes in terminology aim to reduce confusion and better reflect the underlying pathology and aetiology of these diseases.

Consideration of Diagnostic Confidence in Biopsy Evaluation

The past decade has seen recommendations within consensus statements and clinical practice guidelines to report diagnostic confidence in the context of patients with suspected clinical diagnoses of UIP/IPF 3 , 12 , 13 and HP. 14 , 15 The ramifications for pathologists are discussed in more detail in relation to individual patterns below.

General approach to biopsies

The role of the pathologist in diagnosing interstitial pneumonia has evolved considerably over the past three decades. Advances in imaging and other non‐invasive techniques have reduced the number of patients in which a biopsy is required for confident diagnosis. This is particularly true for patients with typical/probable UIP on imaging. 16 There have been periods when biopsy rates temporarily increased in relation to specific conditions; for example, there was an influx of biopsies for suspected PPFE when it was added to the classification in 2013, which soon reduced once clinical and imaging parameters were better defined and more widely accepted. Biopsies are performed now mainly when there is either a lack of confidence in the diagnosis after multidisciplinary review, or unexpected clinical behaviour that questions a previous diagnosis. Biopsy may be particularly helpful if there is uncertainty about the extent of fibrosis and/or cellular disease; therefore, informing the optimal management strategy.

The classification is primarily for adults. The same criteria can be used in interstitial lung disease in children (chILD), but their interpretation is considerably different in a paediatric setting 17 and is outside the scope of this document. Similarly, data from bronchoalveolar lavage can be of considerable value in the diagnosis of interstitial pneumonia 18 but are outside the scope of the document.

Radiological–Pathological Correlation

Interpreting lung biopsies is best done when the pathologist is aware of CT findings. It is particularly important to consider incongruous CT and biopsy findings, including the potential for sampling error for biopsied sites. For example, there may be completely discordant radiological–pathological findings or a sampling problem where the biopsy clearly does not represent the lesions seen by CT, or there may be features that might suggest underlying conditions, such as CTD (pleural effusions, pleural thickening or a patulous oesophagus) that might not be apparent on a biopsy.

Use of Cryobiopsy in the Context of the 2025 IP Classification

Cryobiopsies as an alternative to surgical lung biopsies have increased since the 2013 classification, with sampling using surgical lung biopsies now rare in many ILD units. 19 , 20 Published literature reports a diagnostic yield of 80%–90% and moderate‐to‐good interobserver agreement, which are both similar, although slightly lower than surgical lung biopsies. 21 , 22 , 23 Importantly, the complication rate of cryobiopsy is lower than that of surgical lung biopsy, and some patients not fit for a surgical lung biopsy are able to tolerate a cryobiopsy. 24 Despite some advantages, the use of cryobiopsy is dependent on the availability of the necessary hardware and clinicians with relevant expertise.

There are important considerations for pathologists regarding cryobiopsies in the updated classification. Firstly, not all the histological features required for a specific pattern may be present in these smaller samples. Also, the volume of tissue may be insufficient to see the distribution of disease within lobules. For UIP, recognition of ‘probable UIP’ within guidelines for assigning a level of confidence is often valuable in this scenario, particularly when some but not all of the main criteria for UIP are present, or when a biopsy sample shows only honeycomb change. 3 , 12 In these situations, multidisciplinary review can overcome the less conclusive cryobiopsy findings to still arrive at a confident diagnosis. 23 This is particularly relevant with cryobiopsy yielding smaller samples and highlights the importance of correlating histological and CT findings in relation to disease distribution.

Secondly, cryobiopsies, by the nature of the procedure, may comprise more bronchiolocentric than subpleural or paraseptal tissue, especially if the samples are small. This means that there may be bias towards reporting BIP as a histopathological pattern, and in fibrotic cases, distinguishing UIP from fibrotic BIP may be problematic. A multidisciplinary review of such cases is critical for determining whether bronchocentricity is present on imaging to support a diagnosis of either secondary or idiopathic BIP. Similarly, distinguishing RB from more diffuse AMP may be difficult due to insufficient tissue volume to assess macrophage distribution within lobules.

Finally, there is little data on how many cryobiopsy samples are needed for optimal diagnostic yield. Sampling of multiple sites is ideal, given that combinations of patterns are often seen, similar to recommendations for surgical lung biopsy. An increased number of cryobiopsy samples is associated with higher diagnostic concordance with paired surgical lung biopsy samples, reaching diagnostic agreement of 80% when five cryobiopsy samples are obtained. 25 Multiple deeper sections may be helpful if feasible. 19

Despite these limitations, all histological patterns of interstitial pneumonias can be diagnosed on cryobiopsy if appropriate areas are sampled.

Approach to Reporting

With the formal distinction between idiopathic and secondary patterns, the approach to reporting biopsies has also evolved. There is no set methodology, but it is often valuable to first identify the primary histological pattern (Table 1) and then search for features that might point to a secondary cause. An example of this might be ‘The features are those of UIP with the presence of an occasional bronchiolocentric granuloma suggesting this may be UIP in the context of fibrotic HP (UIP/fHP) rather than UIP/IPF’. As described above, it is important to convey confidence in these findings in the final report, ideally within the context of available clinical and radiological findings.

Combined Patterns

The 2025 update also highlights the recognition that some biopsies may show more than one histological pattern, especially if multiple sites have been sampled, or can show disease in anatomic compartments other than the interstitium, such as the pleura, vasculature or airways. Identifying such combined patterns should make the pathologist consider connective tissue disease (CTD), drug toxicity or familial disorders.

When present, all observed findings should be documented as a multidisciplinary review can often reconcile multiple patterns to a single unifying diagnosis, for example, ‘The features are a combination of fibrotic NSIP and OP, with NSIP predominating’ or ‘The features are those of fibrotic NSIP with coexistent chronic pleuritis’, with both examples suggesting a CTD cause. If one pattern predominates, then this should also be documented, noting that any area showing a pattern of UIP will have prognostic significance in terms of disease progression. 26 , 27

Combined patterns are further discussed in relation to individual histopathological patterns below.

Unclassifiable

Interstitial pneumonias are unclassifiable in a substantial minority of patients; a systematic review of 1,060 patients from 88 studies reported a 9.5% prevalence even after multidisciplinary review. 28 However, classification as ‘unclassifiable’ on biopsy should be avoided whenever possible, with the term ‘unclassifiable’ ideally limited to disease classification after all investigations are completed and multidisciplinary review. If a sample is inadequate for diagnosis, it should be reported as such rather than unclassifiable. If a biopsy shows honeycomb change only, it should be reported as ‘probable UIP’ and then reviewed to ensure that the CT features are compatible with UIP. 3 , 12 If there are combined patterns, then they should all be itemised (see section above).

Individual histopathological patterns

Interstitial Patterns

The most common interstitial patterns are UIP, NSIP and BIP. UIP is fibrotic by definition, while NSIP and BIP are frequently associated with PPF and a poorer prognosis than non‐fibrotic disease, so documentation of any fibrosis is a key feature to document in reports. Staining using an Elastic van Gieson (EVG) technique is often invaluable in helping identify both fibrosis and elastosis. Of note, although PPFE and DAD are not always predominantly interstitial in certain phases of disease progression, these are included in the interstitial category.

Usual interstitial pneumonia

UIP was originally defined histologically as a specific pattern on surgical lung biopsy, most commonly seen in IPF. However, a UIP pattern is not specific for IPF and can be seen in other fibrosing interstitial pneumonias, albeit less commonly and often with additional features suggesting a diagnosis other than IPF. Interstitial pneumonias that may show a UIP pattern include fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis (fHP), CTD‐associated interstitial lung disease (CTD‐ILD), ILD secondary to drug toxicity and some familial forms of pulmonary fibrosis. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 In pneumoconioses, there may be extensive fibrosis but there remains contention over whether a pattern of UIP can be seen in patients with asbestosis. 36 From a practical perspective, consensus guidelines for asbestosis should be followed, namely the presence of interstitial fibrosis independent of pattern, with sufficient asbestos bodies or fibres present. 32

Independent of aetiology, the histological features remain unchanged with the key features of patchy dense collagenous interstitial fibrosis with architectural distortion and remodelling (honeycombing), in a subpleural or paraseptal predominant distribution (Figure 1). 1 , 3 , 12 , 37 In addition to the dense fibrosis, there are scattered ‘fibroblastic foci’—small subepithelial zones of more cellular, loose fibrosis forming flat to sessile foci that are often located near the interface of normal lung and dense fibrosis (temporal heterogeneity). 38 , 39 Areas of uninvolved normal lung parenchyma are present in more centrilobular areas and typically show a sharp transition with areas of fibrosis (spatial heterogeneity). Honeycomb change may be present, but while honeycomb change is characteristic of UIP, it is not specific for the diagnosis as it represents an end stage of pulmonary fibrosis that can be seen in a variety of fibrosing interstitial pneumonias. Mild chronic inflammation, or scattered neutrophils or eosinophils, may be seen within areas of established fibrosis but should not be conspicuous in non‐fibrotic interstitium. 40 , 41 Focally, inflammation can be more marked, typically in relation to areas of honeycomb change where mucostasis may elicit local inflammatory changes.

Clinical guidelines now recommend using ‘UIP’ and ‘probable UIP’, based on whether all or some key features are present, which is especially useful when assessing cryobiopsies. 3 , 12 Terms such as ‘indeterminate for UIP’ are more useful at MDT review. Such cases histologically show a fibrotic pattern of IP other than UIP, but where UIP cannot be absolutely excluded. Using ‘UIP/IPF’, ‘probable UIP/IPF’ and ‘indeterminate UIP/IPF’ as suggested in the Fleischner white paper on the diagnosis of IPF can be useful on biopsy to describe cases that have a UIP pattern without additional features favouring an alternative clinical diagnosis to IPF such as fibrotic HP (UIP/fHP) or a CTD (UIP/CTD). 12

Histological clues to secondary UIP. Fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis with a UIP histological pattern (UIP/fHP). In many cases of fibrotic HP with a histological pattern of UIP, histological clues of HP are present even in the absence of a clinical source of antigen exposure and histology may be the only route to a correct diagnosis. 45 Features suggesting HP, even with a background UIP pattern include bronchiolocentric chronic inflammation, interstitial granulomas or giant cells, particularly those that are peribronchiolar in location, coexistent airway centred fibrosis, peribronchiolar metaplasia (especially if extensive) or bridging fibrosis. 37 , 43 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 Giant cells within alveoli are less specific for HP as they may be seen secondary to honeycomb changes or in chronic aspiration but still require documentation and clinical correlation. Biopsies showing features of UIP together with coexistent features of BIP (fibrotic or non‐fibrotic) are strong indicators of fHP but more subtle features in an otherwise typical UIP case may include the presence of only focal bronchiolocentric changes. Identification of bronchiolocentric changes in addition to a UIP pattern should always prompt consideration of fibrotic HP, although minor changes do not exclude an idiopathic UIP/IPF, highlighting the importance of pathologists providing the predominant histological pattern(s) and differential diagnoses for optimal multidisciplinary consensus decision‐making. One study of patients with a UIP pattern on histology showed that bronchiolocentric fibrosis was not specific for secondary causes of UIP and could be identified in one third of biopsies showing UIP confirmed as IPF at multidisciplinary review. 44 The extent of bronchiolocentric changes acceptable in UIP/IPF is contentious. One study suggested peribronchiolar metaplasia in >50% bronchioles favoured a diagnosis of HP over UIP/IPF. 43 In UIP/IPF, giant cells or granulomas with foreign material indicating aspiration may be seen as chronic aspiration is associated with IPF. 50 Distinction of fibrotic HP from UIP/IPF is important as treatment differs between these entities and avoidance of the inciting antigen may improve disease progression.

Connective tissue disease‐associated ILD with a UIP histological pattern. CTD‐ILD may present with a UIP pattern, especially in rheumatoid arthritis, although it can also occur in other CTDs. 29 , 51 Histological clues to CTD‐ILD rather than UIP/IPF include overlapping features of UIP and NSIP, 52 lymphoid hyperplasia (around airways, in fibrosis or pleura), lymphoid follicles with germinal centres, interstitial chronic inflammation with prominent plasma cells, OP, chronic pleuritis or overlapping acute, subacute and chronic changes. 35 , 39 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 Coexistent rheumatoid nodules are rarely seen in these biopsies but support a pattern of UIP/CTD if present. 52

Familial ILD, pneumoconioses, drug toxicity. The histological features of familial forms of pulmonary fibrosis with a UIP pattern may be indistinguishable from sporadic forms of UIP/IPF and a high clinical suspicion is required for identification of familial disease including attention to patient age and family history. 55 Drug toxicity can occasionally lead to marked pulmonary fibrosis, with reported drugs including methotrexate, bleomycin, amiodarone and many newer biologic and immune therapies. 33 , 56 , 57 Clues to the aetiology of the fibrosis may be very limited but a drug reaction should be considered if there are prominent interstitial eosinophils or poorly formed granulomas or giant cells. 52

Importantly, there may be no histological clues to a secondary cause of UIP and the ultimate multidisciplinary diagnosis may still be a confident secondary UIP. 14 , 34 , 58 Pathologists should provide the histological pattern and list possible differential diagnoses based on pathological findings; for example, ‘UIP pattern with no features to suggest a secondary cause; favour IPF but secondary causes of UIP should also be considered’.

UIP combined with other patterns. When a UIP pattern is present in combination with another pattern, it is the UIP pattern that portends a poor prognosis. In patients with IPF, discordant findings in biopsies taken from different lobes, with UIP in one lobe and NSIP in another, are considered more in keeping with UIP as the prognosis is the same as UIP alone. 26 , 27 Combinations can be part of the same disease process: focal NSIP pattern can be seen in patients with UIP/IPF. In some patients, the additional pattern may be part of a different disease in the same patient (UIP combined with PPFE), 59 , 60 , 61 or the additional pattern can be incidental (UIP/IPF in a patient with subclinical smoking‐related pathology [RB]) (Table 2).

Nonspecific interstitial pneumonia

NSIP encompasses a spectrum of non‐fibrotic (i.e. cellular) and fibrotic patterns. NSIP most often presents with underlying causes (CTD, drug toxicity, HP, smoking‐related or following cryptogenic or secondary OP), and is rarely idiopathic. 62 Based on a recent systematic review of 122 publications that included 8,266 patients, fibrotic NSIP is the most common ILD pattern in CTD‐ILD (range 27%–76% of all CTD‐ILD), excluding RA‐ILD, where UIP is slightly more common at 46% of all cases. 10 , 63

Histologically, NSIP is characterised by homogeneous, uniform thickening of alveolar walls, with varying amounts of interstitial inflammation and/or fibrosis. Cellular NSIP consists of interstitial infiltrates of lymphocytes with or without plasma cells. Fibrosing NSIP can have a mixture of interstitial fibrosis and inflammatory cells, or it may be mostly fibrotic with little inflammation. The architecture of the lung is generally preserved. In its pure form, honeycombing, OP, bronchiolocentricity, granulomas and fibroblastic foci are usually absent in the setting of pure NSIP (Figure 2). 1 , 62

The architecture of the lung is preserved, and there is no fibrosis. (B) Fibrotic NSIP: There is diffuse thickening of alveolar walls by fibrosis with minimal architectural remodelling, no bronchiolocentricity and no honeycombing. Fibroblastic foci are absent. (C) Cryobiopsy shows fibrotic and cellular NSIP. [Colour figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

Biopsies are becoming less frequent for patients with a CT pattern of NSIP and are unlikely to be taken in typical cases linked to a known underlying cause such as a CTD or high‐risk medication exposure. However, NSIP can be radiologically indistinguishable from other patterns, particularly UIP, 64 , 65 , 66 and lung biopsy may be warranted in rare situations. 20

Histological clues to secondary NSIP (differential diagnosis of NSIP pattern). The majority of cases with an NSIP pattern on biopsy will ultimately be shown to be secondary rather than idiopathic, so it is especially important to document any individual histological features or coexistent histological patterns, additional to those that define the histological pattern of NSIP. Bronchiolocentric interstitial pneumonia

The current definition of BIP was proposed as one of two rare histological patterns in the 2013 classification of idiopathic interstitial pneumonias for biopsies showing a bronchiolocentric distribution of interstitial fibroinflammatory abnormalities. 2 Since 2013, additional papers have been published supporting the inclusion of this pattern. 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 Expansion of the 2025 classification beyond idiopathic cases mandated inclusion of HP, 14 , 15 as discussed above and in the primary paper. The majority consensus was to introduce BIP as a major pattern, within which many cases will be HP, some will have other causes (e.g. CTD‐ILD, aspiration and inhalational or medication exposures), and some will remain idiopathic. 78 However, data on idiopathic BIP are sufficiently sparse that this requires further study, 5 as was the case for idiopathic NSIP in 2002. 1

BIP is described as a histological and/or radiological pattern of predominantly airway‐centred interstitial pneumonia. BIP is therefore similar to other major interstitial patterns, such as UIP and NSIP, in that they can occur in a variety of clinical settings, be present as a sole pattern or be present in combination with other patterns. The multidisciplinary approach to BIP is therefore the same as any other interstitial pneumonia pattern.

BIP consists of an inflammatory and/or fibrotic interstitial process centred on bronchioles that extends into the surrounding peribronchiolar interstitium (Figure 3). The histological features of non‐fibrotic (or cellular) BIP include chronic lymphocyte‐predominant inflammation surrounding bronchioles that may also include non‐necrotising granulomatous inflammation. 14 , 15 , 79 Fibrotic BIP is characterised by bronchiolocentric fibrosis (± chronic inflammation), often with peribronchiolar metaplasia in adjacent airspaces consisting of ciliated, pseudostratified respiratory epithelium or rarely squamous metaplasia in cases with prominent distal airway injury. The lightly pigmented macrophages characteristic of RB are either absent or minimal, unless the patient has a history of smoking. 14 , 15 , 48 , 74 , 75 , 76 , 77

Architectural remodelling with airway narrowing and/or bronchiolectasis with mucostasis are common sequelae of fibrotic BIP. Non‐fibrotic and fibrotic BIP often exhibit interstitial involvement of adjacent alveolar walls, driven by inflammation or fibrosis, respectively. Foci of OP and/or foamy macrophage accumulation within alveolar airspaces likely reflect proximal airway injury (Figure 3).

If fibroblastic foci and/or honeycomb changes are present, careful consideration is required to determine whether selective sampling has occurred in a patient with UIP/IPF, or whether the patient has a combination of UIP and BIP patterns in the context of fHP or a CTD‐ILD. This is particularly important when assessing cryobiopsies as these may contain a predominance of bronchocentric alveolar parenchyma, leading to potential bias towards histological reporting of pattern of BIP. Multidisciplinary review is therefore critically important. If there is doubt when reporting, documentation as a combination of BIP and probable UIP should be considered, raising the possibility that this may reflect disorders such as fHP or CTD. These data should then be considered in a multidisciplinary discussion to determine whether the disease is overall bronchocentric on CT (Figure 4).

Diffuse alveolar damage

DAD is a pattern of lung injury histologically characterised by exudative and organising phases, with initial phases primarily affecting the entire alveolar septum, septa thickened by interstitial oedema, pneumocyte hyperplasia and surface hyaline membranes. Pneumocyte hyperplasia can be exuberant and may exhibit atypical cytological features. The acute phase can progress to an organising phase characterised by loose connective tissue within alveolar walls. The loose connective tissue can also be intraluminal, as in OP. If the patient survives the acute episode, organising DAD can progress to interstitial fibrosis that may show a pattern of NSIP. 81

DAD is the cardinal histological pattern in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and is caused by a variety of infections, drugs, inhalational injuries, CTDs (especially inflammatory myopathy) and other lung insults. Patients with idiopathic disease, now termed ‘idiopathic DAD’ (which replaces the clinical term ‘acute interstitial pneumonia’), remain rare, with most cases being secondary (Figure 5). Patients with DAD are rarely biopsied due to the accuracy in non‐invasive diagnosis and the risk of biopsy, 82 other than when a specific disease is suspected that requires tissue for diagnosis (underlying cancer, some infections). Cryobiopsies may be preferred over surgical lung biopsy if a biopsy is considered required in this scenario. 83 , 84

DAD. Lung biopsy shows alveolar walls diffusely thickened by loose organising connective tissue with hyaline membranes present, indicating the exudative phase of DAD. [Colour figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

Combinations of patterns with DAD. DAD may also be seen in combination with other patterns, such as UIP and fNSIP, usually in the context of an ‘acute exacerbation’, 85 which is often the terminal event. When such cases are found at autopsy, these changes should not be misreported as infection (Table 5). 86 , 87

Pleuroparenchymal fibroelastosis (PPFE)

PPFE was introduced into the classification in 2013 as a ‘rare’ entity, on the basis that very few cases had been reported. 2 , 88 , 89 However, PPFE had been reported with much greater frequency in the Japanese literature, originally as Amitani's disease, 90 and the last decade has seen many more cases being identified globally, with increasing recognition that PPFE can be secondary or familial, 91 , 92 , 93 , 94 as well as idiopathic. 91

The key histopathological features of PPFE include intra‐alveolar fibrosis and elastosis (IAFE) with or without pleural fibrous thickening. EVG staining is again useful as it highlights both the elastosis as well as the established collagenisation (Figure 6). However, it is important to recognise that IAFE is a pattern of lung injury seen in several disorders and is not specific to PPFE, so multidisciplinary review remains key to diagnosis. In particular, the histological features can overlap with the localised subpleural fibroelastotic scarring seen in apical caps, which are quite common. This contrasts with PPFE, which is relatively rare.

Combined patterns with PPFE. It is also important to consider combined patterns with PPFE, especially when multiple biopsies are performed, with combinations of PPFE and UIP being the most commonly seen. 59 , 60 , 61 Some of these cases are likely driven by chronic or previous infection, which is important to identify as it may suggest the benefit of specific therapy. Lymphoid interstitial pneumonia. Cases of LIP without a recognised cause or association are exceedingly rare. The histopathological criteria remain unchanged from 2002, although it should be noted that there is poor correlation between cases diagnosed on imaging as LIP and those diagnosed on biopsy as LIP. 95 To this end, usage of the overarching term ‘reactive pulmonary lymphoid hyperplasia’ may be of value when managing these patients. LIP is characterised by a dense diffuse infiltrate of lymphocytes and plasma cells expanding and distorting the alveolar septa, often accompanied by reactive lymphoid follicles in a bronchovascular or lymphatic distribution (Figure 7). 1 , 96

LIP. The surgical lung biopsy shows diffuse, marked interstitial infiltration with lymphoid cells. [Colour figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

From a histopathological perspective, distinguishing LIP from both neoplastic (MALT lymphomas) and other reactive lymphoproliferative disorders (e.g. IgG4‐related disease, Rosai–Dorfman disease, granulomatous lymphocytic‐interstitial lung disease [GL‐ILD]) is the most important consideration, which typically requires immunohistochemistry and molecular studies to look at cell subtypes and clonality, imaging (diffuse vs localised/nodular), serology (IgG4 levels), presence of common variable immune deficiencies (CVID) in GL‐ILD and multidisciplinary review. Multidisciplinary assessment should always include a thorough review for CTD and both acquired and congenital immunodeficiency disorders.

Alveolar Filling Patterns

The proposal to subdivide the IPs into those primarily interstitial and those primarily intra‐alveolar is not new. Although there is sometimes overlap between these major categories, this division should help pathologists decide which pattern is present in a biopsy when first assessing at low power, with additional benefits from a clinical and radiological perspective (e.g. more frequent utility of bronchoalveolar lavage in patients with alveolar filling disorders).

There are also changes to terminology with alveolar macrophage pneumonia (AMP) replacing DIP, which is not, as originally thought, the result of alveolar pneumocyte desquamation. In addition to OP, RB‐ILD and AMP, the 2025 classification has included additional histological patterns: acute and chronic eosinophilic pneumonia (AEP and chronic eosinophilic pneumonia [CEP], respectively), lipoid pneumonia (LP) and pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP). Overall, biopsy is less frequently required for alveolar filling patterns compared to interstitial patterns, given the often characteristic clinical, radiological and laboratory findings, particularly including bronchoalveolar lavage.

Organising pneumonia

Considerations for pathologists with regard to OP are a greater awareness of secondary causes and the histological subgroup of AFOP, which is the pathological manifestation of OP in a more acute setting. AFOP was increasingly identified as a manifestation of COVID‐19 infection. One paper proposed an additional category of ‘granulomatous organising pneumonia’ for cases of granulomatous inflammation that are combined with OP and are predominantly intra‐alveolar. 97 However, as above, it is preferable to report such cases as a combination of OP and granulomatous inflammation, with each component further investigated to identify an underlying cause.

Histologically, OP is characterised by polypoid plugs of loose fibroblastic connective tissue within the alveoli and alveolar ducts (Figure 8). Distribution is patchy and with preserved lung architecture, although some cases progress to established fibrosis. 1 , 98 , 99 , 100 OP can be identified on a transbronchial forceps biopsy, cryobiopsy, 101 , 102 or transthoracic needle biopsy; however, OP can also be a secondary reaction to an underlying pathology such as vasculitis, neoplasm, abscess or infarct, or can be superimposed on another form of IP, including UIP, NSIP and BIP, usually related to an additional acute process. 103 Careful clinical and radiological correlation is critical, particularly with small biopsies, to ensure the presentation is consistent with OP and not secondary changes adjacent to unsampled pathology.

OP. (A) Low power shows filling of alveolar spaces, correlating with consolidation on imaging. (B) At high power, polypoid intraluminal plugs of loose organising connective tissue are present in alveolar ducts and alveoli. [Colour figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

Acute fibrinous and organising pneumonia (AFOP) is a histological pattern that can be seen in patients who present with a spectrum of clinical and radiological manifestations ranging from OP to DAD (Figure 9). 104 , 105 , 106 , 107 Most cases of AFOP are also secondary, manifesting as predominantly patchy mass‐like lesions or GGO on imaging and prominent intra‐alveolar fibrin‐forming balls with varying amounts of OP on histology. 104 OP can also be a component of a mixed pattern of lung injury, often with NSIP or can progress from typical OP to a fibrotic phase more characteristic of NSIP. In this situation, classification as a combination of fibrotic NSIP and OP is preferred histologically to ‘fibrosing OP’. To confirm progression of typical OP to a fibrotic pattern, radiological–pathological correlation with serial CTs is needed. In addition, this needs to be distinguished from the more common presentation of combined NSIP and OP patterns that represent a primary NSIP pattern with secondary OP. The term ‘cicatricial OP’ (Figure 9) may also be used to describe OP when the polypoid plugs comprise dense established fibrous tissue, sometimes with dendriform ossification and often in a perilobular distribution (Table 7). 99 , 100 The clinical setting of chronic acid aspiration in elderly males is a known association. 108

Eosinophilic pneumonia. Acute eosinophilic pneumonia (AEP) and CEP are lung‐limited disorders characterised by eosinophilic accumulation in distal airways and alveoli, often as micro‐abscesses, with BAL eosinophilia typically >25% of nucleated cells. Histologically, AEP typically has additional features of DAD and/or AFOP and/or OP, 109 while CEP often can show scattered non‐necrotising granulomas and rarely granulomas surrounding necrotic eosinophilic microabscesses (Figure 9). Any increase in eosinophils should raise consideration of a drug‐related cause and their presence also warrants close assessment of the vasculature for a vasculitis (as in EGPA), as well as fungal infection and underlying LCH (Table 7).

Respiratory bronchiolitis‐interstitial lung disease (RB‐ILD)

The histopathological pattern of respiratory bronchiolitis (RB) comprises increased accumulation of lightly pigmented macrophages within respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts and peribronchiolar alveoli. There may be an associated chronic peribronchiolar inflammatory cell infiltrate with or without mild peribronchiolar and/or alveolar wall fibrosis (Figure 10). However, classification as the clinical entity RB‐ILD requires clinical and imaging correlation with ensure the patient's symptoms are caused by the RB and are not incidental coexistent smoking‐related changes. Nearly all cases are smoking‐related, with only a minority being due to other inhalational exposures and exceptionally being idiopathic. 110 , 111 , 112

Alveolar macrophage pneumonia

As above, AMP now replaces the term DIP, but the criteria for histopathological diagnosis remain the same, namely a uniformly distributed accumulation of lightly pigmented macrophages within alveoli. Alveolar walls may be fibrotic and variably inflamed, including eosinophils and lymphoid aggregates (Figure 10). As with RB, most cases of AMP are smoking‐related, but around 20% may be due to other causes (CTD, dust inhalation, surfactant protein disorders [children/young adults]) or rarely idiopathic. 110 , 113 , 114

There is no difference in the morphology of the macrophages in relation to smoking‐related and other causes of RB/AMP, in particular, the finely granular haemosiderin pigmentation that is often present can be seen in the macrophages of never smokers. 110 The quantity of the haemosiderin deposition is, however, much less marked, and the quality is far more finely granular than the pigment seen in haemosiderosis secondary to haemorrhage.

Combinations of patterns with RB and AMP. RB and AMP often coexist, sometimes as close as adjacent lobules and the most important consideration is multidisciplinary review correlation to determine if the histological changes are the cause of disease or incidental (e.g. smoking‐related changes are frequently seen in the background of cancer resections or coincidental with other ILDs). RB and/or AMP also frequently exist in Langerhans cell histiocytosis, which is unsurprising given the common association with exposure to cigarettes. 115 RB and/or AMP may also coexist with emphysema and fibrotic NSIP, reported by some as smoking‐related interstitial fibrosis (SR‐IF) 116 or air space enlargement with fibrosis (AEF), 117 although the preference is to report individual interstitial pneumonia patterns with coexistent emphysema. In this setting, the fibrosis has a dense eosinophilic hyalinised appearance that can usually be distinguished from fibrotic NSIP caused by, or associated with, other aetiologies. The terms smoking‐related interstitial fibrosis (SR‐IF) or smoking‐related interstitial lung disease (SR‐ILD) are clinicopathological terms where clinical correlation is recommended when reporting these pathological findings, in order to confirm the smoking causation, with discussion via multidisciplinary review where appropriate (Table 8). 118

Pulmonary alveolar proteinosis

Biopsy is typically not necessary or recommended in previous guidelines for the diagnosis of pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP) but, when performed, shows alveolar accumulation of amorphous, amphophilic material that typically stains with a periodic‐Schiff (PAS) stain. Foamy macrophages with cytoplasmic inclusions, cholesterol crystals, scattered hyperplastic type II pneumocytes and mature lymphocytes are also typically present. The interstitial space is mostly normal, with lymphocytic infiltrate absent or minimal. (Figure 11). The main cause of PAP is autoimmune and secondary to anti‐GM‐CSF antibodies (90% of all PAP cases), with other causes being inhalational exposures (e.g. silica, aluminium and titanium), haematological disorders such as malignancy, myelodysplastic syndrome, GATA2 deficiency, allogeneic haematopoietic cell transplantation and some infections. Distinction from idiopathic cases is mainly through clinical data and/or imaging, although any case of PAP should have additional staining undertaken to look for causative or associated infection (Gram and Grocott). Also, coexistence with other patterns should be documented, particularly including HP. 119

Lipoid pneumonia

Lipoid pneumonias are subcategorised as exogenous when due to aspiration or inhalation of foreign material, or endogenous when a consequence of the accumulation of lipid created by cells within the body. The lipid material often washes out of the tissue during processing, so all that remains is empty rounded spaces in the tissue, although residual lipid droplets sometimes remain in the tissue. If diffuse and endogenous, inborn errors of metabolism should be considered. 120 Lung biopsy is infrequently required, given its characteristic imaging features. When performed, histology of exogenous lipoid pneumonia comprises larger and more variably sized droplets in the interstitium, often associated with histiocytic giant cells, while endogenous lipoid pneumonia comprises macrophages with foamy cytoplasm filling alveoli (Figure 11).

Other alveolar filling disorders. There are other alveolar filling diseases that were not included in the 2025 updated classification as they were considered outside the classification's remit, but that are still relevant to pathologists involved in the diagnosis of interstitial pneumonias.

Haemosiderosis comprises increased haemosiderin‐laden macrophages in the alveoli, sometimes with a minor component in the interstitium. Endogenous pneumoconiosis may be present. As with the interstitial pneumonias, the role of the pathologist is to identify the haemosiderosis and then assess for any potential cause, such as an underlying neoplasm, vasculitis or pulmonary veno‐occlusive disease. If none are present, cases can be reported as ‘haemosiderosis, aetiology not apparent’.

Alveolar microlithiasis comprises abundant calcific nodules within alveolar spaces caused by a mutation in the SLC34A2 gene. Most cases are diagnosed in childhood and biopsy is rarely performed due to the characteristic imaging features. 121 , 122

Molecular advances in interstitial pneumonias

There have been considerable molecular advances over the past decade as reviewed in the primary update document. 5 However, molecular testing is not routine in the current management of interstitial pneumonias and, when undertaken, is usually blood or serum‐based. Nevertheless, the presence of certain histological patterns should prompt the pathologist to suggest specific testing (e.g. endogenous lipid pneumonia, AMP in a young adult never‐smoker).

Conclusion

The 2025 update on the classification of interstitial pneumonias provides a framework for classifying these disorders that is anchored on histological pattern and integrated with clinical, radiological and laboratory data to arrive at a multidisciplinary diagnosis. This framework has multiple implications for practising pathologists who report biopsy findings in patients with interstitial pneumonia, which should be considered for both clinical use and future research.
