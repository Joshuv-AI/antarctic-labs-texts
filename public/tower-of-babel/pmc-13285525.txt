Experiences of discrimination, paternal support, and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) in the MIEHR study, Houston, Texas
Authors: Tahereh Alinia, Elaine Symanski, Juan Manuel Alvarez, Kristina Walker Whitworth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiscrimination is a psychosocial stressor that, through activation of stress-response pathways, may influence risk of adverse perinatal health outcomes. However, few studies have evaluated the association between discrimination and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), and none have explored effect modification by race or paternal support, which may buffer the adverse effects of discrimination.MethodsWe included 1,847 non-Hispanic Black and White participants from the Maternal and Infant Environmental Health Riskscape (MIEHR) study. Krieger’s validated Experiences of Discrimination (EOD) scale was used to assess occurrence (yes/no) and frequency (once, 2–3, ≥ 4 times) of discrimination in nine situations. We created continuous scores representing total situations and frequency and also categorized (0, 1–2, ≥ 3) the situations score. Using electronic health records, we identified participants with HDP (hypertension in pregnancy, preeclampsia, eclampsia, and hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count (HELLP) syndrome). We applied log-binomial regression to evaluate associations between experiences of discrimination and HDP, adjusted for maternal age, pre-pregnancy body mass index, income, and race. Analyses were stratified by race and self-reported paternal support (none/a little vs. good/excellent) as potential effect modifiers; due to small numbers, the situation score was dichotomized (0, ≥ 1) for these analyses.ResultsHDP occurred in 37.5% (n = 430) of Black and 26.0% (n = 182) of White participants. Compared with participants reporting no discrimination, the adjusted risk ratio (aRR) was 1.13 (95% CI: 0.96, 1.33) and 1.11 (95% CI: 0.94, 1.30) for those reporting discrimination in 1–2 and ≥ 3 situations, respectively. By setting, the strongest associations were observed for discrimination in getting a job (aRR: 1.22; 95% CI: 1.14, 1.43), by police (aRR: 1.21; 95% CI: 1.01, 1.44), and getting medical care (aRR: 1.17; 95% CI: 0.94, 1.44). Stratified analyses revealed the strongest associations between experiencing discrimination and HDP among Black participants and those with lower perceptions of paternal support.ConclusionsWe found associations between discrimination and HDP among Black participants, who bear the greatest burden of HDP, as well as a potential buffering effect of paternal support. Future research is needed to investigate associations between discrimination and HDP and evaluate whether enhancing paternal support can mitigate the impact of discrimination on HDP risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-026-02864-5.Keywords: Discrimination, Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, Paternal support, Race

Background

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) is a composite outcome that includes hypertension in pregnancy, preeclampsia, eclampsia, hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count (HELLP) syndrome. Women with HDP have elevated risks of adverse pregnancy outcomes like small-for-gestational age, preterm birth, and stillbirth [1]. Racial disparities in the prevalence of HDP in the United States (U.S.) are well-documented, with non-Hispanic Black and American Indian and Alaskan Native women disproportionately impacted by both HDP and associated complications as compared to women from other racial and ethnic groups [2]. Studies have reported that psychosocial stress may contribute to the etiology of HDP [3–5], perhaps through hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and sympathetic nervous system activation [6–9].

One major source of psychosocial stress experienced by pregnant women is discrimination based on gender, age, disability, race or ethnicity [10–12]. In the U.S., although racial and/or ethnic discrimination has been evaluated with regard to adverse pregnancy outcomes such as preterm birth, its association with HDP is less well characterized [5, 13, 14], and findings have been equivocal. Caplan et al. [5] found that higher levels of reported discrimination (assessed using Williams’ Everyday Discrimination scale) were associated with higher odds of HDP (adjusted Odds Ratio [aOR]: 1.16, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 0.94, 1.44). In contrast, Grobman et al. [13] reported an inverse association (aOR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.62, 1.06) between experiencing discrimination in three or more situations (assessed using Krieger’s Experiences of Discrimination (EOD) scale [15]) and HDP. Using data from the Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS), Lee et al. [14] reported an aOR of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.74, 1.20) for the association of racism or discrimination and HDP.

While previous studies assessed discrimination as a global or summary measure, discrimination occurs within specific social contexts [16], and context-specific assessments may better identify targets for intervention. Furthermore, to our knowledge, no prior studies have assessed the association between experiences of discrimination and HDP by race and thus, potential heterogeneity in the impact of discrimination across racial and ethnic groups remains poorly understood. Finally, despite evidence that social support is a key protective factor for pregnancy health [13, 17, 18], there is limited evidence of its potential role in buffering the association between discrimination and pregnancy outcomes. To address limitations of the prior literature, the present study examines the association between experiences of discrimination and HDP, both overall and within specific settings and evaluates potential effect modification of this association by both race (i.e., non-Hispanic Black vs. non-Hispanic White; hereafter referred to as ‘White’ or ‘Black’), and perceived paternal support.

Methods

Study population

This investigation included 1,847 participants from the Maternal and Infant Environmental Health Riskscape (MIEHR) study for which the overall goal was to examine individual and neighborhood-level risk factors that contribute to Black-White disparities in perinatal health outcomes [19]. In brief, MIEHR enrolled participants around the time they delivered their infants at three major academic hospitals in the Texas Medical Center in Houston, Texas between April 2021 and December 2023. Eligible participants were non-Hispanic, self-identified as Black or White, were at least 18 years old, delivered a singleton liveborn infant with no major congenital anomalies, and resided in the greater Houston area (Brazoria, Chambers, Fort Bend, Galveston, Harris, Liberty, Montgomery, and Waller counties). All participants had to be proficient in English and be cognitively able to give informed consent. Prior to participating in the study, participants provided written informed consent, including consenting to the abstraction of information from their electronic health records (EHRs). The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards at Baylor College of Medicine and the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston.

Data collection

After obtaining informed consent, research coordinators administered questionnaires electronically through Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap). The questionnaire gathered detailed information including self-identified race (Black; White), maternal age (in years), nativity (U.S.-born; foreign-born), employment status (works at home; works outside the home; does not work), education (less than high school; high school/general educational development exam; some college; college degree; master’s degree; doctoral/professional degree), marital status (married or living with partner; single, never married; separated; divorced; widowed), household income (less than $34,999; $35,000–$74,999; $75,000–$99,999; $100,000 or more), smoking during pregnancy (yes; no), alcohol use during pregnancy (yes; no), and gestational age at the first prenatal visit (in weeks). Paternal assistance and support were assessed via self-report; mothers were asked, “How would you rate the amount of assistance and support you received during your pregnancy from the father of your baby?” Responses were recorded on a four-level scale (none; a little; a good amount; an excellent amount). Additionally, information on participants’ pre-pregnancy weight and height, diagnosis of pre-pregnancy chronic conditions (diabetes or hypertension), the name of the participants’ health insurance carrier, diagnosis of gestational diabetes during the index pregnancy (yes; no), and parity (number) were abstracted from EHRs. We also identified from the EHR if the following conditions were present in the index pregnancy: ‘hypertension in pregnancy,’ ‘preeclampsia,’ ’eclampsia,’ or ‘HELLP syndrome’. These conditions were combined to create a single variable representing HDP. Participants were identified as having ‘hypertension in pregnancy’ regardless of whether the diagnosis occurred prior to or during pregnancy.

The MIEHR study questionnaire included Krieger’s EOD scale [15] a validated scale to assess lifetime experiences of discrimination. The administration and scoring of this scale, and subsequent creation of three EOD metrics was conducted according to the methodology outlined by Krieger et al. [15]. Briefly, the EOD scale asks participants: “Have you ever experienced discrimination, been prevented from doing something, or been hassled or made to feel inferior…because of your race, ethnicity, or color?” Participants responded (yes; no) to this prompt in relation to nine situations: at school; getting hired or getting a job; at work; getting housing; getting medical care; getting service in a store or a restaurant; getting credit, bank loans or a mortgage; on the street or in a public setting; and from the police or in courts. Affirmative responses to the nine situations were summed to create the EOD situations score (range = 0–9) and, as recommended [15], this score was also classified into three categories: 0, 1–2, and ≥3 situations. Participants who responded affirmatively to any of the situations were asked how many times this had happened in their lifetime: once, 2–3 times, or 4 or more times. These responses were assigned scores of 1, 2.5, and 5, respectively, and summed across all reported situations to create the EOD frequency score (range = 0–45). Higher situation or frequency scores indicate greater experiences of discrimination.

Statistical analysis

For analyses, we collapsed categories of maternal age (< 24; 25–29; 30–34; and ≥ 35 years); employment status (employed vs. not employed), education (college degree or higher vs. high school or less); marital status (married/living with partner vs. single/separated/widowed/divorced); and household income (≥$75,000; $35,000–$74,999; and <$35,000). Paternal support was dichotomized as none/a little vs. good/excellent. We classified specific insurance carriers as either private or public (e.g., Medicaid, children’s health insurance plan [CHIP]) and created a dichotomous insurance variable (private/self-pay vs. public/no insurance). We used information on participants’ self-reported date of the first prenatal care appointment to classify prenatal care initiation as either early (i.e., before 13 weeks) or late (i.e., > 13 weeks) and created a dichotomous prenatal care initiation variable (early vs. late/no prenatal care). Parity was categorized as 1, 2, or ≥ 3 prior livebirths. Pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by height (m) squared and categorized as: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 30 kg/m²). Missing data were minimal for most covariates (< 1% for education, paternal support, insurance and 5.3% for household income). A separate missing category for BMI was retained due to the high proportion (31%) of missing values (primarily for pre-pregnancy weight) for this variable, minimizing data loss in adjusted models. For all other variables, we imputed missing values using multiple imputations by chained equations with ten imputations and a fully conditional specification using a logit link for binary variables (i.e., smoking during pregnancy, insurance, education, alcohol use during pregnancy, and prenatal care initiation) and discriminant function methods for income as it had more than two levels. Fully conditional specification is an iterative multiple imputation approach in which each variable with missing data is imputed using a regression model conditional on all other variables in the dataset. The imputation model included maternal age, race/ethnicity, parity, nativity, employment, marital status, pre-pregnancy chronic conditions, gestational diabetes, pre-pregnancy BMI, smoking during pregnancy, insurance, education, alcohol use during pregnancy, household income, and prenatal care initiation.

Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, experiences of discrimination, and HDP in the study population. Differences in characteristics between Black and White participants were assessed using chi-squared tests for categorical variables and t-tests (or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, as appropriate) for continuous variables. Using log-binomial regression, we examined associations between experiences of discrimination overall, as well as in each of the nine situations, and HDP. We report adjusted risk ratios (aRRs) with 95% CIs; adjusted models included a priori covariates (maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, income, and race). We additionally evaluated potential effect modification by race (Black; White) and paternal support (none/a little, good/excellent) using stratified analyses. Due to small numbers, for the stratified analyses, we further categorized the EOD situation score as 0 vs. ≥1. Finally, we conducted two sensitivity analyses: (1) adjusting for education instead of income to assess whether our results were sensitive to how socioeconomic status was operationalized and (2) restricting the outcome to preeclampsia, eclampsia, and HELLP syndrome, clinically well-defined and more severe subtypes of HDP. Because multiple hypothesis tests were conducted, we applied a Bonferroni correction to control the family-wise error rate. The significance level (α = 0.05) was divided by the number of tests performed to determine a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold (α = 0.05/69 ≈ 0.0007). All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4.

Results

Table 1 shows that 1,148 (62.2%) MIEHR participants identified as Black and 699 (37.8%) as White. Overall, HDP was more prevalent among Black than White (37.5% vs. 26.0%, p < 0.001) participants, as were hypertension in pregnancy (21.9% vs. 18.2%, p = 0.050) and preeclampsia (21.9% vs. 11.6%, p < 0.001). HELLP syndrome was rare and the prevalence was similar between Black and White participants (0.3% vs. 0.4%, p = 0.539); no cases of eclampsia were observed in this study. There were notable racial differences in most sociodemographic variables: a college degree or higher was less common among Black than White participants (51.9%, vs. 87.8% p < 0.001) and the proportion of Black participants reporting household income ≥$75,000 was substantially lower than among White participants (14.5% vs. 75.7%, p < 0.001).

Table 2 summarizes self-reported experiences of lifetime racial discrimination. Only 48.3% of Black participants reported never experiencing racial discrimination compared with 83.6% of White participants. Black participants reported having experienced discrimination in a greater number of situations as compared to White participants (mean ± standard deviation (SD): 1.7 ± 2.2 for Black participants vs. 0.3 ± 0.8 for White participants, p < 0.001). There were also differences in the frequency with which participants reported ever experiencing discrimination (mean ± SD: 5.4 ± 8.2 for Black participants vs. 0.9 ± 2.9 for White participants, p < 0.001). Both Black and White participants most commonly reported discrimination occurring “on the street or in a public setting” (30.8% vs. 8.5%, p < 0.001) and “at work” (29.0% vs. 8.2%, p < 0.001). Black participants also frequently reported discrimination when “getting service in a store or restaurant” (29.8%).

As shown in Table 4, the association between experiencing discrimination and HDP differed by race. Among Black participants, discrimination was associated with a higher risk of HDP (aRR: 1.13; 95% CI: 0.98, 1.32) whereas little evidence of an association was observed among White participants (aRR: 1.02; 95% CI: 0.64, 1.12). The number of situations in which a participant experienced discrimination was positively associated with HDP among Black participants (aRR: 1.04; 95% CI: 0.98, 1.10) and inversely associated with HDP among White participants although the CI for this estimate was relatively wide (aRR: 0.94; 95% CI: 0.77, 1.16). In our analysis of racial differences in the associations between discrimination in specific settings and HDP (see Supplementary Table S1), the largest RR was observed for non-Hispanic Black participants experiencing discrimination when getting hired or getting a job (aRR: 1.24; 95% CI: 1.06, 1.46). For other settings, RRs ranged between 0.83 and 1.19 and were not statistically significant. Results are also shown in Supplementary Table S1 for White participants, but the estimates were imprecise due to small sample sizes.

While the overall patterns of association between experiences of discrimination and preeclampsia (including HELLP syndrome) were similar to the results for HDP, there were a few notable differences worth highlighting (Table 6). Although there was no evidence of an association between experiencing discrimination in 1–2 settings and preeclampsia (aRR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.74, 1.25), the association between experiencing discrimination in three or more settings was slightly stronger for preeclampsia (aRR: 1.16; 95% CI: 0.92, 1.47) than for HDP. Importantly, discrimination in getting medical care (aRR: 1.37; 95% CI: 1.02, 1.84) and when getting hired or getting a job (aRR: 1.32; 95% CI: 1.04, 1.66) was more strongly associated with preeclampsia than with HDP. The results from the sensitivity analysis evaluating the inclusion of education rather than income (as a proxy for socioeconomic status) in the adjusted models did not differ meaningfully from the main findings (see Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

We observed a relatively high prevalence of HDP in our study that likely arose in part because the study population included a higher proportion of non-Hispanic Black participants compared with many prior studies, a population with a higher risk of HDP [5, 13, 14]. Our classification of HDP also included (among other disorders) individuals with chronic hypertension diagnosed prior to, but extending into, pregnancy. Not surprisingly, Black participants reported a greater number of experiences and greater frequency of discrimination than White participants. While we found that associations between discrimination and HDP were modest overall, the strongest associations emerged when examining discrimination in specific settings; namely, discrimination related to being hired for a job, from the police and courts, and in getting medical care. Stratified analyses indicated that the association between discrimination and HDP was stronger among Black than White participants and that paternal support served to buffer this relationship.

Exposure to psychosocial stressors in pregnancy, including discrimination, may induce inflammation that impairs placental function and promotes endothelial dysfunction [7, 20], providing a plausible biological mechanism linking these exposures with HDP. In our study, over half of Black participants reported experiencing racial discrimination in at least one setting and far fewer White participants did so. Several other studies have assessed racial discrimination using Krieger’s EOD among Black women although we are unaware of studies that have administered this scale in non-Hispanic White populations. While Giurgescu et al. [21] report that the prevalence of discrimination among pregnant women in Chicago, IL, US was similar to that in our study (53.1%), the prevalence of discrimination was much higher among Black women in Project Viva (Boston, MA, US) (80.4%) [22] and in a convenience sample of women in Oakland, CA, US (92.1%) [23]. Notwithstanding these differences, the most common settings in which Black women report experiencing discrimination are consistent across studies, including ours. For example, in the study by Giurgescu et al. [21], getting service in a store or restaurant (34%), getting hired or getting a job (20%), and on the street or in a public setting (20%) were the most frequently reported settings in which women reported discrimination. Similarly, among Black women in Project Viva, the most common settings in which women reported discrimination were also when getting service in a store or restaurant (58%) and on the street or in public (44%) [22].

In the only study to administer Krieger’s EOD scale in examining the impact of discrimination on HDP, Grobman et al. [12] evaluated data from a large cohort of approximately 10,000 women from eight sites in the U.S. and, unlike our study, found no association between experiencing discrimination in three or more situations and HDP (aOR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.62, 1.06). Two other studies evaluated the impact of discrimination on HDP using measures other than the EOD scale and reported equivocal results. Using Williams’ Everyday Discrimination Scale, Caplan et al. [5] reported a positive association with HDP in a cohort of pregnant women recruited from multiple U.S. sites (aOR: 1.16; 95% CI: 0.94, 1.44) whereas Lee et al. [13] relied on responses to questions about discrimination and stress in an analysis of PRAMS data from 17 states in the U.S. and found no association (aOR: 0.94; 95% CI: 0.74, 1.20). These mixed results may not only reflect differences in the measurement tools for discrimination but also in the racial makeup of the study populations; for example, the proportion of Black participants ranged from 13.8% [13] to 27.2% [14] in prior studies versus 62.2% in the present study. The prevalence of HDP also varied, namely, 11.4% in Caplan et al. [5], 14% in Grobman et al. [13], 20.2% in Lee et al. [14], and 33.1% in our study. These differences in the racial/ethnic composition of the study populations and in the prevalence of both discrimination and HDP may have limited the ability of prior studies to detect associations. Based on our stratified analyses, it appears that the association observed in our study was driven by this association in Black participants. Given the racial breakdown of our population, our findings help to fill an important gap in the literature regarding the role of discrimination in shaping racial disparities in HDP.

In particular, our findings point to employment, the criminal justice system, and the healthcare system as particularly important settings in which discrimination may adversely impact maternal health. We are unaware of previous studies that have evaluated associations of racial discrimination in specific settings with HDP. However, in an analysis of data from the Black Women’s Health Study, a prospective study of U.S. Black women, Rosenberg et al. [24] found that women who reported unfair treatment at their job had 30% increased odds of preterm birth (95% CI: 1.1, 1.6), compared with women who did not report unfair treatment. Employment-related discrimination can limit women’s access to income and health insurance, which in turn reduces access to prenatal care and increases psychosocial stress during pregnancy, both key risk factors for adverse pregnancy outcomes [25]. Rosenberg et al. [24] also reported a modest association (albeit not statistically significant) between reporting unfair treatment by the police and preterm birth (OR: 1.1; 95% CI: 0.9, 1.4) [24]. Negative interactions with the police or courts reflect broader exposure to systemic racism, leading to chronic stress, and heightened allostatic load, all of which have been implicated in the pathogenesis of preeclampsia and other pregnancy complications. Anticipation of police brutality is a source of stress and fear during pregnancy, which may negatively influence maternal and infant health outcomes [26]. It is possible that experiences of discrimination when seeking medical care can undermine trust in healthcare providers, delay the start of prenatal care, and discourage women from seeking care altogether. These effects may result in missed opportunities for prevention of adverse pregnancy outcomes and early treatment [27–29].

To our knowledge, ours is the first study to evaluate the potential buffering role of paternal support on the adverse impact of a specific psychosocial stressor (i.e., discrimination) and HDP. However, a population-based case-control study conducted among Latina women in Los Angeles County (U.S.) found that among women lacking support, those experiencing moderate-to-high levels of chronic stress had higher risk of preterm birth (aOR 2.15; 95% CI 0.92, 5.03) compared to those with low stress, whereas lower evidence of an association was observed among women with moderate-to-high support (aOR: 1.13; 95% CI: 0.94, 1.35) [18]. While effect modification was not examined, a cross-sectional study from Iran reported higher levels of perceived stress and lower social support among women with preeclampsia compared to healthy pregnancies, though it relied on the Social Support Appraisal (SSA) scale, which does not explicitly measure paternal support, making a comparison more difficult [30]. Evidence on the mechanisms by which paternal support may modify the relationship between discrimination and pregnancy outcomes, particularly HDP, remains limited. Several studies have suggested that social support alleviates physiological stress responses and reduces cortisol levels [31]. Furthermore, support from the baby’s father has been associated with better maternal engagement in health-promoting behaviors (e.g., timely prenatal care visits, avoidance of smoking) [32]. In the present study, we did not assess specific aspects of paternal support and instead relied on women’s perceptions of support, which may operate both through psychosocial pathways (e.g., stress buffering [31], emotional reassurance) and through structural pathways, as paternal support is often correlated with cohabitation, relationship stability, and greater access to financial and material resources [32, 33].

Our study relied on data from a large sample of pregnant women in Houston that included a substantial number of Black women to perform robust race-stratified analyses. Another strength of our study was the use of Krieger’s EOD scale [15], which provides a reliable measure of perceived discrimination across multiple settings. Using EHRs to discern HDP diagnoses also minimizes the probability of information bias. Our study also had few missing observations (< 6%) for all covariates except pre-pregnancy BMI; however, we retained a separate missing category for this variable to minimize data loss and preserve sample size. Our examination of paternal support as a potential buffer of the association between discrimination and HDP adds new evidence by explicitly identifying a specific type of social support, support from the baby’s father, that can mitigate the harmful health effects of discrimination during pregnancy. This finding is novel, as prior research has generally examined broad categories of social support in relation to maternal stress or pregnancy outcomes, but not as a modifier of the discrimination-HDP relationship. These results offer potential for intervention by informing strategies that promote paternal engagement during pregnancy, such as father-focused prenatal programs or counseling interventions, especially among populations exposed to high levels of discrimination.

Several limitations should be considered. While the EOD scale is a well-validated tool for assessing experiences of discrimination [15], it relies on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall bias or underreporting, particularly when discrimination occurs in indirect, less obvious, or more socially accepted ways [34]. This misclassification is likely non-differential since any misreporting of discrimination would likely occur similarly among those with and without HDP; hence, if present, biasing associations towards the null. A further limitation is that paternal support was assessed based on participants’ self-reported perceptions and thus, our results cannot inform specific aspects of support that may operate to buffer the negative impacts of discrimination. Each participant may have interpreted this construction differently, depending on her unique situation. However, our intent was to assess each participant’s subjective experience and perception of support, that may itself be a meaningful determinant of maternal health [35]. Finally, it is worth noting that the target population of our study sample were women who delivered an infant at one of three large hospitals in the Texas Medical Center, not all women who delivered an infant in the 8-county study area. The Texas Medical Center is the largest medical center in the world and the population of women who seek antenatal care at these hospitals is enriched for high-risk pregnancies, as indicated by the higher prevalence of HDP that we observed as compared to national estimates.

Conclusion

In this large sample of women in greater Houston, Texas, racial discrimination, particularly in contexts such as employment, interactions with law enforcement, and seeking medical care, was modestly associated with increased risk of HDP, particularly among Black participants, who reported both higher levels and greater frequency of discrimination. Notably, perceived paternal support appeared to mitigate the adverse impact of discrimination on HDP risk, highlighting the importance of social support as a potential protective factor. Future research is needed to investigate associations between discrimination and HDP and evaluate whether enhancing paternal support can mitigate the impact of discrimination on HDP risk.
