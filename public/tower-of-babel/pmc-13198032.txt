Cryptosporidiosis at the human–ruminant interface in Aswan, Egypt: a one health epidemiological study using microscopy, immunofluorescence, and PCR
Authors: Ahmed Gareh, Nady Kh Elbarbary, Marwa O Abd El-Halim, Marwa A Ali, Laila A AL-Essa, Ramadan Taha, Yehia Hazzazi, Mari Sumayli, Ahmed E Ahmed, Mohammed F Ragab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCryptosporidiosis is a significant zoonotic enteric infection at the human–livestock interface. In Egypt, Cryptosporidium spp. has been widely reported in both humans and domestic animals, with prevalence varying across regions and host species, highlighting its importance as a public health and veterinary concern. This study investigated the prevalence and epidemiological correlates of Cryptosporidium spp. infection among domestic ruminants and closely exposed humans in Aswan Governorate, Egypt, using parasitological and molecular approaches.MethodsA total of 870 fecal samples were collected, including 684 from domestic ruminants (cattle calves n = 238, buffalo calves n = 186, lambs n = 127, and goat kids n = 133) and 186 from humans (1–65 years) in close contact with the examined animals. Samples were initially examined using microscopy and an immunofluorescence assay (IFA). PCR was subsequently performed on IFA-positive samples to confirm the presence of Cryptosporidium DNA. Associations between infection and potential risk factors were assessed using logistic regression analysis, and results were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsThe prevalence of Cryptosporidium spp. infection in ruminants was 34.5% (236/684; 95% CI: 30.9–38.2) and 31.6% (216/684; 95% CI: 28.1–35.2) as determined by microscopic and immunofluorescence examination, respectively. Among humans in close contact with the examined ruminants, the prevalence was 25.3% (47/186; 95% CI: 19.4–32.0) and 18.8% (35/186; 95% CI: 13.5–25.2), respectively. Nested PCR analysis of immunofluorescence-positive samples confirmed infection in 89.8% (194/216; 95% CI: 85.0–93.4) of ruminant samples and 91.4% (32/35; 95% CI: 76.9–98.2) of human samples. In ruminants, univariable analysis showed that infection was significantly associated with sex (OR = 1.76; 95% CI: 1.28–2.42; p < 0.05), age (highest in 1–3 months: OR = 4.58; 95% CI: 2.66–7.88; p < 0.001), season (spring: OR = 2.64; 95% CI: 1.59–4.38; p < 0.001), and fecal consistency (liquid feces: OR = 8.32; 95% CI: 3.75–18.47; p < 0.001). Multivariable analysis identified age and fecal consistency as independent predictors. In humans, age was the only significant factor, with higher odds in children aged 1–10 years (OR = 6.44; 95% CI: 1.75–23.77; p = 0.0412), remaining significant after adjustment.ConclusionCryptosporidiosis is highly prevalent in Aswan, particularly among young ruminants, with prevalent infection observed among closely exposed humans—especially children. These findings highlight the importance of improving hygiene in young livestock, managing diarrheic animals, and reducing environmental contamination of manure and water sources. Species-level identification was not performed; therefore, zoonotic transmission could not be confirmed. Further molecular characterization studies are needed to determine circulating Cryptosporidium spp. and better understand transmission dynamics within a One Health framework.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05519-3.Keywords: Cryptosporidium, Domestic ruminants, Fecal–oral transmission, One Health, Risk factors, Zoonotic transmission

Introduction

Cryptosporidiosis is an enteric disease caused by Cryptosporidium spp. and represents an important contributor to diarrheal morbidity, particularly among young children and immunocompromised individuals. In low-resource settings, it is consistently reported among the leading causes of moderate-to-severe childhood diarrhea, underscoring its public health significance [1]. The parasite’s biological characteristics further enhance its epidemiological importance, as environmentally resistant oocysts are shed in feces, remain immediately infectious upon excretion, and can persist under diverse environmental conditions [2].

Cryptosporidium spp. infect a wide range of vertebrate hosts, including mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, and fish, with more than 50 species described to date [3]. In both humans and domestic ruminants, infections have been reported across diverse geographical settings, reflecting the broad host range of the genus [4, 5]. In humans, infection occurs via person-to-person and environmental exposure, whereas in livestock, it is most common in young animals and frequently associated with gastrointestinal disease [5].

Cryptosporidium spp. have a direct life cycle within the gastrointestinal epithelium of infected hosts. Infection occurs through ingestion of immediately infectious oocysts in contaminated food, water, or environmental surfaces [1]. After ingestion, sporozoites invade intestinal epithelial cells and undergo asexual and sexual development, resulting in the production of new oocysts shed in feces [6]. Control in farm environments is difficult due to the parasite’s low infectious dose and the high environmental resistance of its oocysts to stress and commonly used disinfectants [5].

From a One Health perspective, domestic ruminants represent an important interface in the epidemiology of cryptosporidiosis. Infected animals can contribute to environmental contamination by shedding large numbers of oocysts, particularly in settings where livestock are kept close to households or share water sources with humans [7]. However, confirmation of transmission pathways requires molecular characterization and species-level identification.

Young ruminants are particularly susceptible to infection, which is commonly associated with diarrhea, dehydration, impaired growth, and economic losses due to reduced productivity [8]. In contrast, adult animals often remain asymptomatic carriers, contributing to the persistence of infection. High levels of oocyst shedding in young animals can increase environmental contamination of farm environments and water sources, potentially enhancing indirect exposure risks in livestock-associated settings, especially where close human–animal contact and shared resources are common [9, 10].

Detection of Cryptosporidium infection relies on parasitological, immunological, and molecular methods [11]. Microscopy, commonly using modified Ziehl–Neelsen (MZN) or auramine–phenol staining, remains widely used [12, 13] but is limited by low sensitivity and operator dependence, particularly at low oocyst shedding levels [14]. Immunofluorescence improves sensitivity and specificity, while PCR provides highly sensitive detection and enables species-level identification when sequencing is applied [5, 15].

In Egypt, Cryptosporidium infection has been reported in both humans and livestock across multiple regions, with prevalence varying by host species, age, management practices, and diagnostic methods [16, 17]. Recent studies in Egypt have demonstrated the presence of Cryptosporidium oocysts in various environmental matrices, including surface water, irrigation water, and drinking water systems, with variable prevalence, in some cases reaching up to 25% or higher, indicating a significant environmental reservoir [18].

However, data from Aswan Governorate remain limited, particularly studies assessing both animal and human populations within the same ecological setting. In this region, livestock are commonly raised in smallholder systems with frequent human contact, which may increase exposure risk. Most previous studies have examined either animal [19, 20] or human [21, 22] populations separately, often using a single diagnostic method, highlighting the need for integrated investigations at the human–livestock interface.

Although Cryptosporidium is recognized as a zoonotic pathogen, confirmation of transmission pathways requires species-level molecular characterization and subtyping. In the absence of such data, the present study does not attempt to establish transmission but instead describes the co-occurrence and epidemiological patterns of Cryptosporidium spp. infection among domestic ruminants and humans within the same ecological setting in Aswan Governorate.

Accordingly, this study investigated the prevalence and associated risk factors of Cryptosporidium spp. infection in domestic ruminants and closely exposed humans within a One Health framework. Microscopy and immunofluorescence were used for primary detection, while PCR was applied only to immunofluorescence-positive samples as a confirmatory step rather than for full diagnostic comparison.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted in Aswan Governorate, located in southern Egypt along the eastern bank of the Nile River near the First Cataract and just north of the Aswan High Dam. The governorate covers approximately 62,726 km², of which about 12,203 km² are inhabited. The region has a hot desert climate characterized by extremely hot, dry summers with temperatures often exceeding 41 °C and relatively mild winters with average temperatures around 26 °C [23, 24].

Livestock production in Aswan is predominantly based on smallholder mixed farming systems, in which cattle, buffaloes, sheep, and goats are raised in proximity to human households. According to the latest available statistics, the livestock population in Aswan Governorate is estimated at approximately 32,185 cattle, 10,187 buffaloes, 23,358 sheep, 5,874 goats, and 3,718 camels. These animals are mainly concentrated in rural settlements along the Nile Valley, resulting in relatively high livestock density in irrigated areas [25].

The agro-ecological conditions of the region are strongly influenced by the Nile River, with agriculture relying primarily on perennial irrigation systems. Crop–livestock integration is common, where agricultural residues are used as animal feed and livestock manure is recycled as organic fertilizer. These practices, together with the use of shared irrigation canals and water sources, may facilitate the environmental dissemination of Cryptosporidium oocysts [18].

Moreover, the close spatial overlap among livestock, agricultural land, and human dwellings increases the risk of zoonotic transmission via contaminated water, soil, and fomites. Such interactions highlight the importance of environmental pathways in the epidemiology of cryptosporidiosis and support the application of a One Health approach.

Samples were collected from four districts in Aswan Governorate: Edfu (24°58′42.77″ N, 32°52′32.95″ E), Kom Ombo (24°27′8″ N, 32°55′42″ E), Daraw (24°24′24.35″ N, 32°55′7.96″ E), and Aswan City (24°5′20.1768″ N, 32°53′59.3880″ E) (Fig. 1).

Study population/animals

The study population consisted of domestic ruminants and humans living in close contact within smallholder farms and household settings in the selected districts of Aswan Governorate.

The animal population included young domestic ruminants, recognized as key epidemiological reservoirs for Cryptosporidium infection. The sampled animals comprised cattle calves, buffalo calves, lambs, and goat kids. Animals were categorized into four age groups: <1 month, 1–3 months, 3–5 months, and > 5 months, based on farm records and owner-reported birth history, with confirmation by dentition and general physical development when required. These categories were selected in accordance with commonly applied age classifications in epidemiological studies of juvenile ruminants, where susceptibility to Cryptosporidium infection is highest during early life stages [17, 19].

Body condition was assessed using a standardized Body Condition Score system appropriate for each species, based on a 1–5 scale (1 = poor, 5 = excellent), using visual inspection and palpation of anatomical landmarks (ribs, lumbar area, and tail head) [26].

The human population included individuals aged 1–65 years who resided in the same households as the examined animals. All participants were household contacts, selected by convenience sampling from farms included in the animal sampling. No hospital-based or unrelated populations were included in the study. For analytical purposes, participants were categorized into the following age groups: 1–10, 11–20, 21–40, 41–60, and > 60 years.

Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants, while consent for minors (< 18 years) was obtained from their parents or legal guardians, in accordance with approved ethical guidelines [27].

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted between March 2024 and April 2025. The study aimed to generate epidemiological and parasitological data from both animal and human populations within the same geographic setting.

Sample size

The required sample size was calculated using Thrusfield’s formula [28], assuming an expected prevalence of 33.6% for ruminants [19] and 10.7% for humans [29], with a 95% confidence level and a precision of 5%.

n = required sample, Z = standard normal deviation at 95% confidence (1.96), Pexp = expected prevalence, and d = desired precision (0.05).

Based on this formula, the minimum required sample sizes were 343 animals and 147 human participants. In the present study, more samples were collected in both groups to increase the likelihood of detecting positive cases and to improve the precision of the prevalence estimates [30]. It should be noted that the expected prevalence used for human sample size estimation (10.7%) was derived from a pediatric diarrheal population, which may not fully represent the broader population with livestock contact and may have led to an underestimation of the required sample size. However, the number of human samples included in the present study exceeded the calculated minimum, which partially mitigates this limitation.

Data collection

Data on potential epidemiological risk factors were collected during sampling. For animals, information recorded included sex (male or female), age group (< 1 month, 1–3 months, 3–5 months, and > 5 months), season (autumn, winter, spring, and summer), and fecal consistency (normal/no diarrhea, pasty, liquid, or liquid with blood or intestinal mucus) [31].

Basic information was collected from human participants, including demographic characteristics (age and sex), clinical symptoms (e.g., diarrhea), and exposure-related factors such as contact with animals and involvement in animal husbandry activities [16, 32].

Animal sampling

A total of 684 fecal samples were collected from domestic ruminants, including 238 cattle calves, 186 buffalo calves, 127 lambs, and 133 goat kids. Approximately 3–5 g of fecal samples were collected directly from the rectum by a veterinarian using digital stimulation to minimize environmental contamination [20].

Animals were selected from smallholder households and private farms based on availability at the time of the visit. Due to the absence of a complete sampling frame and variability in herd size, a formal random sampling method (e.g., simple or systematic random sampling) was not applied. Instead, animals were selected non-systematically, without prior consideration of clinical status or other characteristics, to reduce selection bias under field conditions. Animals that were inaccessible or whose owners declined participation were excluded.

Human sampling

A total of 186 fecal samples were collected from individuals aged 1–65 years living in the same households as the examined animals.

Participants were included based on their direct exposure to livestock, defined as regular involvement in animal husbandry activities such as feeding, milking, cleaning animal shelters, or handling manure. Individuals who declined consent or were unable to provide samples were excluded. No restrictions were applied based on sex or clinical status.

All animal and human fecal samples were collected in clean, sterile, labeled containers and transported to the Department of Parasitology, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Aswan University, within 2 h, under cooled conditions (approximately 4 °C) without the use of chemical preservatives. Samples were processed as soon as possible after collection to maintain integrity [20].

Preparation of fecal samples

Each fecal sample was divided into two portions. One portion was used for direct examination, while the second portion was filtered through two layers of gauze to remove coarse debris. The filtered material was preserved in an equal volume of 2.5% potassium dichromate solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Canada) and stored at 4 °C until further analysis [27].

Macroscopic examination

Fresh fecal samples were examined macroscopically on the day of collection. Parameters assessed included consistency, color, degree of digestion, and the presence of blood, mucus, or other abnormal constituents [33, 34].

Laboratory examination

All fecal samples were examined for Cryptosporidium oocysts using a stepwise diagnostic approach. Initial screening was performed using microscopy and an immunofluorescence assay (IFA). Samples that tested positive by immunofluorescence were subsequently subjected to molecular analysis to confirm the presence of Cryptosporidium DNA. PCR was not performed on samples that were negative by microscopy and immunofluorescence; therefore, a full diagnostic comparison across all methods was not performed.

Microscopical examination (Modified Ziehl Neelsen method)

Smears were prepared from fecal samples, fixed in absolute methanol for 2 min, and air-dried. Slides were stained using Kinyoun’s modified acid-fast technique by flooding with Carbol-Fuchsin for 5 min, followed by rinsing with 50% ethanol (5 s), washing with water, and decolorization with 1.5% sulfuric acid for 2 min. Slides were then counterstained with methylene blue for 5 min, washed, and air-dried.

Stained smears were examined at ×400 and ×1,000 magnifications. Cryptosporidium oocysts appeared as pink to red spherical or ovoid bodies against a blue background [35].

Immunofluorescence assay (IFA)

All fecal samples were examined using immunofluorescence as a parallel diagnostic method. Before staining, oocysts were concentrated using the formalin–ethyl acetate sedimentation technique [36]. The concentrated samples were then processed using a commercial direct immunofluorescence assay (Aqua-Glo™ G/C Direct Reagent Kit, A100FLR-20X, Waterborne, Inc., New Orleans, LA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Briefly, aliquots of the concentrated fecal suspension were applied to microscope slides, air-dried, and stained with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled monoclonal antibodies specific for Cryptosporidium oocysts. The slides were examined under an Accu-Scope EXC-350 fluorescence microscope equipped with FITC and Texas Red LED filters (USA). Cryptosporidium oocysts were identified by their characteristic size, shape, and bright apple-green fluorescence. The assay has been reported to exhibit high diagnostic performance, with sensitivity and specificity generally exceeding 90% under optimal laboratory conditions. However, performance may vary depending on sample quality and processing procedures [36].

An estimated oocyst count (EOC) was determined by examining 25 randomly selected microscopic fields. Infection intensity at 400× magnification was classified as high (> 10 oocysts), moderate (5–10 oocysts), low (< 5 oocysts), or negative (0 oocysts) [37].

Molecular detection

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from fecal samples that tested positive by immunofluorescence microscopy. PCR was applied as a confirmatory method within a stepwise diagnostic workflow rather than as a primary screening tool for all samples, due to practical logistical and resource constraints considerations.

Before extraction, each sample was thoroughly homogenized, and 200 µL was suspended in 1.4 mL of Buffer ASL (lysis buffer). To disrupt the oocysts, samples were subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles: freezing at − 40 °C followed by thawing at 90 °C in an incubator.

Genomic DNA was subsequently extracted from fecal samples using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen Inc., USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with isopropanol used during the precipitation step. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were measured using a microvolume spectrophotometer (Quawell Q9000, USA) [38].

Nested PCR procedure

Nested PCR (nPCR) was performed as previously described by Mensah et al. [14]. Two sets of primers (Willowfort Co., UK) were used in this study (Table 1), generating an expected amplicon size of 655–667 bp. The primary PCR reaction was performed in a total volume of 20 µL, containing 2 µL of template DNA. For the secondary PCR, 5 µL of the primary PCR product was used as the template for re-amplification. Non-acetylated bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Invitrogen, USA) was added to each reaction at a final concentration of 400 µg/mL. Locally derived isolates containing previously confirmed Cryptosporidium DNA from the Animal Health Research Institute, Egypt, served as the positive control, and PCR-grade water served as the negative control.

Both primary and secondary PCR amplifications were performed under the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min; 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, annealing at 55 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Amplifications were conducted using a BIO-RAD Thermal Cycler (BIO-RAD, Singapore). PCR products were resolved by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel stained with SYBR Safe DNA gel stain (Invitrogen, USA), run at 100 V for 30 min, and visualized under a UV transilluminator (GEL DOC XR).

Statistical analysis

All data were entered, cleaned, and validated using Microsoft Excel and subsequently analyzed using SPSS version 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the prevalence of Cryptosporidium infection among the studied populations.

Associations between Cryptosporidium infection status (positive/negative) and potential risk factors were assessed using logistic regression analysis. Initially, univariable logistic regression was performed to evaluate the association between each independent variable and infection status. Variables with p < 0.20 in the univariable analysis were subsequently included in multivariable binary logistic regression models to identify independent predictors while controlling for potential confounding effects.

Associations between Cryptosporidium infection status (positive/negative) and potential risk factors were assessed using logistic regression analysis. Initially, univariable logistic regression was performed to evaluate the association between each independent variable and infection status. Variables with p < 0.20 in the univariable analysis were subsequently included in multivariable binary logistic regression models to identify independent predictors while controlling for potential confounding effects.

Separate multivariable models were constructed for ruminants and humans. For ruminants, the explanatory variables included age, sex, season, and fecal consistency, while for humans, age, sex, and season were included. The strength of association was expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), with the lowest-risk category as the reference group.

Risk factor analyses were conducted based on infection status determined by microscopy and IFA. PCR was used as a confirmatory diagnostic method and was not included as a primary outcome variable in the epidemiological analysis.

Results

Clinical assessment indicated that young ruminants commonly exhibited gastrointestinal signs, particularly diarrhea. These observations represent general clinical findings and are not specific to Cryptosporidium spp. infection. The association with diarrheic fecal consistency is epidemiological and does not imply causation, as other enteric pathogens were not investigated. Conversely, several host and environmental factors were significantly associated with Cryptosporidium spp. infection in ruminants (Table 3). Sex, age, season, and fecal consistency showed statistically significant associations (p < 0.05), whereas species and study area did not.

Age-related patterns indicated higher infection in younger animals, with decreasing prevalence as age increased. Seasonal variation was also observed, with higher detection during cooler and transitional periods. Fecal consistency showed the strongest association with infection, with higher positivity in animals exhibiting diarrheic feces. Fecal consistency was analyzed as an epidemiological indicator of gastrointestinal status, not as a confirmed clinical outcome of Cryptosporidium spp. infection. Associations do not imply causation, as other enteric pathogens were not investigated.

Additionally, the use of multiple unadjusted statistical tests may increase the risk of type I error, particularly for variables with borderline significance, and the results should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Risk factor analysis of Cryptosporidium spp. infection in ruminants and humans

In ruminants, logistic regression analysis showed that infection was significantly associated with fecal consistency, age group, season, and sex (Fig. 5). The highest odds of infection were observed in animals with liquid feces (OR = 8.32; 95% CI: 3.75–18.47), followed by animals aged 1–3 months (OR = 4.58; 95% CI: 2.66–7.88). Increased odds were also observed during spring (OR = 2.64; 95% CI: 1.59–4.38) and in female animals (OR = 1.76; 95% CI: 1.28–2.42). No significant associations were found for study area or host species (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Cryptosporidium spp. are ubiquitous enteric protozoa infecting a wide range of vertebrate hosts and represent a recognized One Health concern at the human–animal–environment interface [39]. The present study provides epidemiological data on Cryptosporidium spp. infection at the human–ruminant interface in Aswan Governorate, where integrated investigations remain limited. Unlike previous studies focusing on either livestock or humans, this work simultaneously assessed infection in multiple ruminant species and in humans with close livestock contact within the same ecological setting using microscopy, IFA, and PCR-based detection. However, molecular analysis was limited to PCR without species identification or genotyping. Therefore, the findings indicate co-occurrence of infection in humans and ruminants but do not allow conclusions regarding species distribution or transmission pathways.

Clinical examination of sampled animals revealed common gastrointestinal signs in young ruminants, including diarrhea, dehydration, emaciation, and pale mucous membranes, with more pronounced symptoms in younger animals. These observations reflect general clinical findings at the time of sampling. The association between Cryptosporidium spp. detection and diarrhoeic fecal consistency in this study is epidemiological and does not imply causation. Other enteric pathogens (e.g., rotavirus, coronavirus, Escherichia coli, and Giardia duodenalis) were not investigated and may have contributed to the observed signs; therefore, coinfections or alternative etiologies cannot be excluded. Similar clinical patterns have been reported in gastrointestinal disease complexes of young ruminants [40, 41].

The prevalence observed in ruminants in this study falls within the range reported in previous investigations from Upper Egypt, particularly among young animals, which are more susceptible and tend to shed higher numbers of oocysts [4, 19, 40]. Young ruminants may therefore contribute to environmental contamination in livestock-associated settings, especially during diarrheic episodes [41]. This may increase the likelihood of human exposure to environmental contaminants in close contact with livestock.

Previous studies in Upper Egypt reported prevalence rates of approximately 33.6% in cattle and buffalo calves using coproscopic methods with molecular confirmation [19], which are comparable to the findings of the present study. This supports the recognized contribution of young ruminants to environmental contamination with Cryptosporidium oocysts. In contrast, lower prevalence (18.84%) has been reported in cattle calves from the Nile Delta [4], suggesting that regional differences in management, hygiene, climate, and sampling strategies may influence infection dynamics.

Higher prevalence rates have been reported in other Egyptian regions. For example, a survey in Beni-Suef Governorate detected Cryptosporidium spp. in 42.68% of cattle by microscopy, indicating that elevated infection levels may occur under conditions of high environmental contamination or in populations dominated by young calves [40]. Similarly, Taha et al. [42] reported prevalence rates of 53.85% by Ziehl–Neelsen staining and 43.59% by nested PCR, with variation across species, including cattle, goats, and sheep.

International studies have likewise demonstrated substantial variability in reported prevalence. Rates in calves have ranged from 2.6% in China [7], 4.4% in South Korea [43], and 15.7% in Bangladesh [9] to 83.8% in Japan [44], 55.4% in Austria [45], and 28.97% in northern China [46]. Such variation is expected because Cryptosporidium spp. prevalence is influenced by multiple epidemiological factors, including age structure (particularly pre-weaned animals), fecal consistency, stocking density, environmental hygiene, and the clinical status of sampled animals.

Host composition may contribute to differences between studies. The present investigation included multiple ruminant species, whereas some Egyptian studies have focused only on small ruminants, reporting a higher microscopy-based prevalence (≈ 42.7%) has been reported [47]. These differences highlight the influence of host species, age distribution, and management conditions on infection patterns. In addition, variation in study design—such as sampling diarrheic, healthy, or mixed populations—can further affect reported prevalence estimates [48].

The molecular prevalence observed in humans in this study indicates a notable burden of Cryptosporidium spp. infection among individuals with close contact with livestock in Aswan Governorate. This prevalence is lower than that reported in high-risk Egyptian cohorts, particularly among immunocompromised children. For example, studies conducted among oncological children in Egypt reported prevalence rates of 29.7% and 27.8% by microscopy and 25.5% and 23.7% by nested PCR, respectively [21, 22], likely reflecting increased susceptibility and hospital-based sampling of symptomatic individuals.

In community-based or One Health settings, prevalence estimates similar to those observed in this study have been reported. For example, a study in Menoufia Governorate documented 19.2% human positivity by microscopy, with higher infection rates in calves within the same households [17]. Differences between studies may reflect variation in population characteristics, livestock exposure, environmental conditions, and diagnostic methods. In the present study, participants were recruited from households with regular livestock contact, representing higher exposure than general community surveys but differing from hospital-based cohorts of symptomatic or immunocompromised individuals.

These differences highlight the influence of sampling frameworks and diagnostic methods on reported human cryptosporidiosis prevalence. Studies focusing on high-risk or clinical populations typically report higher prevalence, whereas community-based investigations reflect broader environmental exposure patterns in livestock-associated settings. Globally, previous investigations conducted in different epidemiological settings have documented prevalence ranging from low levels in some community-based surveys such as 0.9% in China [7], 2.3% in Bangladesh [9], 2.0% in China [49], and 29.0% in Ethiopia [50] to substantially higher rates in specific high-risk or outbreak-associated populations such as 94% in Ireland [51].

A notable feature of this study is the stepwise reduction in apparent prevalence from microscopy to immunofluorescence and PCR in both ruminants and humans. This pattern should be interpreted cautiously and does not represent a formal comparison of diagnostic performance. It may reflect differences in method sensitivity and specificity, reader subjectivity in microscopy, and technical limitations of PCR in fecal samples (e.g., inhibitors or DNA degradation). In addition, PCR was performed only on immunofluorescence-positive samples, limiting molecular testing to a subset of the dataset. Therefore, the lower molecular prevalence partly reflects the diagnostic workflow rather than true differences in test performance.

Previous methodological studies indicate that PCR offers highly sensitive detection and enables species-level characterization, while immunofluorescence improves specificity and reduces operator dependence compared with routine staining methods [15, 52]. However, diagnostic performance depends on specimen quality, laboratory protocols, and the testing workflow [9]. From a surveillance perspective, these findings support a tiered approach in which microscopy or immunofluorescence is used for initial screening, followed by molecular confirmation for enhanced epidemiological interpretation.

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of the diagnostic approach used. Although Cryptosporidium spp. infection was detected in both ruminants and humans within the same ecological setting, molecular analysis was limited to PCR-based detection without species identification or genotyping. Therefore, the results indicate co-occurrence of infection but do not allow determination of circulating species or transmission pathways. These considerations are particularly relevant in Upper Egypt, where close human–livestock contact, manure handling, and shared water resources may increase opportunities for environmental exposure [53]. Evidence of Cryptosporidium spp. contamination in irrigation and agricultural water further supports the role of environmental pathways in shaping infection patterns [40]. Accordingly, the relative contributions of zoonotic, anthroponotic, and environmental transmission routes cannot be determined from the present data. Further studies incorporating molecular characterization and environmental sampling are required to clarify transmission dynamics.

Epidemiologically, Cryptosporidium spp. infection in the examined ruminants was associated with host- and management-related factors (age, sex, season, and fecal consistency) rather than species or study area. The lack of a significant species effect suggests that different ruminant species were exposed to similar contamination pressures, such as shared housing, bedding, manure handling, and water sources within the same production systems. This interpretation is consistent with evidence from Upper Egypt indicating that both cattle and buffalo calves can sustain infection and contribute to transmission cycles, even when risk patterns vary between host groups [19].

The present study identified a significant association between sex and Cryptosporidium spp. infection in ruminants (p = 0.0147), with higher prevalence in females. Similar findings in Egyptian studies have attributed this difference primarily to management practices rather than biological susceptibility [40]. Female animals are typically retained for breeding longer and maintained in closer contact with other livestock, increasing their exposure to contaminated environments. In contrast, male calves are often raised for shorter periods under more controlled conditions [23]. The lower number of male samples in this study also reflects common dairy management practices [52]. However, some studies report weak or non-significant associations after accounting for confounding factors, suggesting that sex may act as a proxy for management conditions rather than an independent risk factor [4].

Age was the most influential factor associated with Cryptosporidium spp. infection, with the highest prevalence in animals aged 1–3 months and a marked decline in older groups. This pattern aligns with the epidemiology of cryptosporidiosis as an early-life infection in pre-weaned ruminants, where immunological immaturity and close confinement facilitate fecal–oral transmission [54]. Similar associations with young age have been reported in previous studies [55], although variations in peak age may reflect differences in study design and management practices [17, 40].

A significant seasonal effect was also observed, with higher prevalence during winter and spring. Comparable patterns have been reported in Egypt [40]. They may be linked to environmental conditions that favor oocyst survival, as well as seasonal management practices such as increased housing density and hygiene constraints during cooler months [17].

These findings are consistent with Egyptian studies reporting higher Cryptosporidium spp. prevalence during cooler seasons, particularly winter and spring, with lower rates in summer and autumn [19]. However, some studies have reported different patterns, such as peak prevalence in autumn, likely reflecting differences in study design, management practices, and regional climatic conditions [40].

Seasonal variation has also been observed in broader surveillance systems; for example, increased human cryptosporidiosis during spring in the United Kingdom has been linked to livestock-related activities and environmental exposure [56]. Together, these observations suggest that seasonal environmental conditions and livestock management practices may influence infection patterns at the human–animal interface.

Fecal consistency showed the strongest association with Cryptosporidium spp. detection (p = 0.0002), with higher prevalence in diarrheic animals than in those with normal feces. This finding is consistent with previous studies linking infection to neonatal diarrhea in young ruminants [4, 17, 19].

In humans, age was the only significant factor, with higher prevalence in children and adolescents and lower rates in adults. This pattern aligns with the established epidemiology of cryptosporidiosis, in which younger age groups are more susceptible due to behavioral and environmental exposure factors [56]. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies [57], although variability across age groups may occur depending on study design and diagnostic methods [29].

These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that children bear a disproportionate burden of Cryptosporidium spp. infection, although prevalence varies by region and study design. Reported rates among children with diarrhea typically range from ~ 9–15%, influenced by hygiene, seasonality, and diagnostic methods [55, 58]. The relatively higher prevalence observed in this study may reflect increased exposure associated with frequent livestock contact.

No significant associations were observed with study area, sex, or season, consistent with previous reports. This may be explained by the sampling framework, which focused on individuals with regular livestock contact, resulting in relatively homogeneous exposure across groups. In such settings, shared environmental and behavioral factors may reduce detectable differences between demographic categories [59].

Similar findings have been reported in a One Health study from Menoufia Governorate, which identified age as a significant factor but found no association with sex [17, 22]. However, that study reported stronger seasonal effects, whereas seasonality was not significant in the present dataset. These differences may reflect regional variation in climate, sampling distribution, and local exposure conditions [49].

Seasonal variation in human cryptosporidiosis has been widely reported and may be influenced by environmental conditions, agricultural activities, human–animal contact, and childcare exposure [17]. These factors affect oocyst survival and transmission, with increased infection often linked to rainfall or water contamination events [55]. Thus, seasonal patterns likely reflect both environmental conditions and livestock management practices.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the human cohort was limited to individuals with close contact with ruminants, which supports a One Health framework but restricts generalizability and may introduce selection bias toward higher-exposure groups. Second, molecular detection was performed only on IFA-positive samples, introducing potential verification bias and preventing an unbiased comparison of diagnostic methods. In addition, PCR was used solely for confirmatory detection without sequencing or species-level identification; therefore, molecular results should be interpreted as confirmatory rather than epidemiological. Another limitation is the absence of environmental sampling (e.g., water or soil), which represents a key component in the transmission cycle of Cryptosporidium spp., particularly in settings where humans and animals share water sources and living environments. The lack of environmental data limits the ability to assess environmental transmission pathways directly within a One Health framework.

Future studies should incorporate molecular characterization, including sequencing and subtyping, to better define species distribution and transmission dynamics. In addition, longitudinal studies integrating animal, human, and environmental sampling across diverse settings are recommended to improve understanding of temporal patterns and ecological drivers of infection.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the occurrence of Cryptosporidium spp. infection in both domestic ruminants and humans in Aswan Governorate, with ruminants likely contributing to environmental contamination in livestock-associated settings. Infection patterns were associated with age, sex, and fecal consistency, with higher prevalence in young animals and in children aged 1–10 years. Although species and study area were not significant predictors, the findings are consistent with shared environmental exposures, including livestock contact and potential water contamination. Overall, the results indicate co-occurrence of infection in ruminants and humans within the same ecological setting.

These findings emphasize the importance of integrated One Health interventions, including improved hygiene, better animal husbandry, and protection of water resources, to reduce Cryptosporidium spp. transmission at the human–animal interface. However, without molecular characterization, the relative contributions of zoonotic and anthroponotic transmission remain unknown. Future studies incorporating molecular typing and environmental surveillance are needed to clarify transmission dynamics and support evidence-based control strategies.
