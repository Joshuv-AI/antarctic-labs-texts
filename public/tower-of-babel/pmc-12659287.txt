Protective effects of mouse bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles on radiation-induced epididymal cells damage via USP44/RBM14 axis
Authors: Lingfeng Zhu, Jianping Zhang, Yaoyao Liang, Yuanhang Cheng, Na Lin, Wenyi Deng, Lingling Lin, Jin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiation-induced damage to reproductive tissues presents a major clinical concern with potentially lasting functional consequences.MethodsHere, we investigate the protective function of extracellular vesicles derived from mouse bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (mBMSCs-EVs) in mitigating ionizing radiation-induced injury in murine epididymal cells (mPC-1).ResultsWe demonstrate that mBMSC-EVs significantly enhance cell viability and reduce apoptosis, oxidative stress, and DNA damage in irradiated mPC-1 cells. Besides, we identify the deubiquitinase USP44 as a key mediator enriched in mBMSC-EVs, which confers radioprotection by stabilizing the RNA-binding protein RBM14 through deubiquitination for mPC-1 cells. Silencing USP44 in mBMSCs or their EVs abrogates these protective effects, leading to impaired DNA repair, increased apoptosis, oxidative stress, and disrupted cell cycle progression. Conversely, RBM14 overexpression partially restores cell survival and function, supporting its downstream role in this pathway.ConclusionOur findings reveal a novel USP44/RBM14 axis mediated by mBMSC-EVs that counteracts radiation injury, and suggest this mechanism as a promising therapeutic approach for protecting reproductive tissues from radiation-induced damage. Notably, these findings are primarily grounded in in vitro experiments utilizing an immortalized epididymal cell line; further in vivo validation and evaluation of clinical translatability are required prior to therapeutic application.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04782-9.Keywords: Mesenchymal stem cells, Extracellular vesicles, Radioprotection, USP44/RBM14 axis, DNA repair, Epididymal cells, Reproductive toxicity

Introduction

Ionizing radiation is a widely used therapeutic modality, but it inevitably causes collateral damage to healthy tissues [1, 2]. Among the radiosensitive organs is the epididymis, which can sustain significant functional impairment at low radiation doses [3]. Such adverse effects frequently result in infertility that substantially diminishes the quality of life of patients [4, 5]. Therefore, strategies to protect radiosensitive organs during radiotherapy are urgently needed.

A promising strategy to achieve this involves the integration of radioprotective agents that selectively modulate tissue responses, safeguarding normal cells without compromising therapeutic efficacy [6, 7]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as promising radioprotective candidates due to their remarkable regenerative and immunomodulatory capabilities [8–10]. These effects are largely mediated by the secretion of extracellular vesicles (EVs), which facilitate intercellular communication and deliver bioactive molecules to damaged tissues [11, 12]. Recent evidence suggests that MSC-derived EVs can replicate many of the beneficial actions of their parent cells, including reducing radiation-induced tissue injury and promoting repair via regulating various biological processes, including mitochondrial homeostasis and oxidative damage [7, 13–16]. However, the specific molecular cargos in MSC‑EVs responsible for radioprotection and their mechanisms of action remain incompletely defined [17].

Ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs), a family of deubiquitinating enzymes that regulate protein stability and cellular signaling, have garnered attention for their involvement in DNA damage response pathways and cellular resilience to radiation [18, 19]. For example, USP15 regulates DNA repair and influences the progression of radiation-induced intestinal injury [20], while USP47 modulates oxidative skin damage by maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis through degradation control of NNT [21]. These studies suggest that specific USPs could mediate extracellular vesicle-delivered radioprotective signals, but this possibility has not been systematically explored.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the molecular mediators within EVs from mouse bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (mBMSC-EVs) that exert radioprotective effects on irradiated murine epididymal cells (mPC-1). We utilized a combination of in vitro co-culture systems, molecular assays, and gene expression analyses to elucidate the roles of mBMSC-EVs and the underlying mechanism in mediating radioprotection. Our findings reveal a critical role for the USP44/RBM14 pathway, demonstrating that USP44 is enriched in mBMSC-EVs and enhances DNA repair while inhibiting apoptosis through the stabilization of RBM14, an RNA-binding protein essential for coordinating the DNA damage response. Our findings provide a mechanistic link between MSC-EVs cargo and tissue radioprotection and suggest a potential avenue for preserving fertility and tissue function during radiotherapy.

Methods

Cell culture

Mouse proximal caput epididymal epithelial cell line (mPC-1) cells were purchased from Immocell Biotechnology Co.,Ltd and maintained in PriGrow V medium (Abm, #TM015) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone, #SH30406.05), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Pen-Strep; Gibco, #15,140–148), and incubated at 33 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. mBMSCs were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, #C11995500BT) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO₂.

mBMSC isolation and identification

mBMSCs were isolated from the femora and tibiae of 6–8-week-old C57BL/6 mice following aseptic procedures. Cells were cultured using Mesenchymal Stem Cell Culture Medium (Immocell, #IMC-393) and purified by differential adherence. Flow cytometry was used to characterize the phenotype: cells were stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against hematopoietic markers CD14 (eBioscience, #17–0141-82), CD19 (eBioscience, #11–0193-82), CD34 (BioLegend, #119,307), CD45 (eBioscience, #48–0451-82) and mesenchymal markers CD29 (BioLegend, #102,207), CD44 (BioLegend, #103,021), CD73 (eBioscience, #12–0731-82), CD90 (Beyotime, #AC1153), incubated at 4 °C for 30 min, washed twice with PBS, and analyzed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer. Cells were gated to exclude debris and doublets, and viable single cells were used for analysis. Marker positivity was determined relative to the corresponding isotype control: a population was considered positive when its fluorescence intensity was shifted above the isotype control peak. Reported values represent the percentage of positive cells for each marker.

MSC differentiation

mBMSCs were induced to differentiate along osteogenic, chondrogenic, and adipogenic lineages. For osteogenic differentiation, mBMSCs were cultured in osteogenic induction medium (Procell, #PD-005) containing 0.1 μM dexamethasone, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid for 21 days, and mineralized matrix was assessed by Alizarin Red S staining. Chondrogenic differentiation was induced using chondrogenic medium containing 10 ng/mL TGF-β3 (Procell, #PD-003) for 21 days and evaluated by Alcian Blue staining. Adipogenic differentiation was performed in adipogenic induction medium (Procell, #PD-004) containing 0.5 mM isobutylmethylxanthine (IBMX), 1 μM dexamethasone, and 10 mg/mL insulin for 14 days, and lipid accumulation was assessed by Oil Red O staining.

EVs isolation

EVs were isolated as previously described [22]. Briefly, mBMSCs at passage 3–4 were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% exosome-depleted FBS (VivaCell Biosciences, #C3801) for EVs isolation. Upon reaching ~ 80% confluence, cells were cultured in serum-free medium for an additional 48 h. The conditioned medium was then collected and centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 10 min to remove cells and large debris. The supernatant was then transferred to fresh tubes and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 45 min at 4 ℃ to remove larger vesicles. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane (Millipore, #SLGVM33RS), and the filtrate was ultracentrifuged at 100,000 ×g for 60 min at 4 ℃ (Beckman Coulter Optima L-100XP). The pellet was resuspended in 10 mL pre-cooled 1 × PBS and ultracentrifuged again at 100,000 ×g for 70 min at 4 ℃. The final pellet was resuspended in 100 µL pre-cooled 1 × PBS, of which 20 µL were used for transmission electron microscopy (TEM), 10 µL for nanoparticle tracking analysis, and the remainder was aliquoted and stored at − 80 ℃ until further use.

EVs characterization

Morphological analysis was performed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi, HT-7700) by placing 10 µL of the sample onto a copper grid for 1 min, removing excess liquid with filter paper, staining with 10 µL of 2% uranyl acetate for 1 min, air-drying at room temperature, and imaging at 100 kV. Particle size distribution and concentration were measured by NTA using a Zetaview instrument (Particle Metrix) after instrument performance verification with standards. Samples were tested with serial dilutions, and the original sample concentration was calculated from measured values. Exosomal protein markers CD9 (Proteintech, #20,597–1-AP, 1:2000), CD81 (Proteintech, #27,855–1-AP, 1:2000) and CD63 (Proteintech, #67,605–1-Ig, 1:5000) were detected by Western blotting.

mPC-1 cell irradiation and mBMSC co-culture or EVs exposure

When mPC-1 cells reached ~ 70% confluence, they were irradiated at room temperature using a 6 MV X-ray linear accelerator (Elektra precise) with doses of 2, 4, 6, or 8 Gray (Gy) delivered at a dose rate of 300 cGy/min. After irradiation, the irradiated mPC-1 (mPC-1/IR) cells were either indirectly co-cultured with mBMSCs (shNC or shUSP44) at a ratio of 5:1 (mPC-1: mBMSC) using a Corning Transwell system (#07–200-154), or treated with mBMSC-EVs at a previously optimized concentration of 20 μg/mL. The cells were then cultured for an additional 24 h at 33 °C in 5% CO₂ atmosphere to allow subsequent assays. In specific groups, to inhibit EV secretion, GW4869 (10 μM, an exosome secretion inhibitor) was added into the mBMSC compartment 24 h before co-culture to block EV release.

shRNA-mediated knockdown

Three lentiviral shRNAs targeting USP44 (shUSP44-1, -2, -3) and a non-targeting control shRNA (shNC) were used (GenePharma). shRNA oligonucleotides targeting mouse USP44 were cloned into pLKO.1 (sense sequences: shNC: TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTT; shUSP44‑1: CGGATGATGAACTTGTGCAAT; shUSP44‑2: GCACAGGAGAAGGATACTAAT; shUSP44‑3: GAATTGGAGTATCAAGTTAAAC). Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells and concentrated by ultracentrifugation. mBMSCs were transduced at an MOI of 10 in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene, followed by puromycin selection (2 μg/mL). Knockdown efficiency was evaluated by qRT-PCR using SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, A25776) and normalized to 18S.

Overexpression vectors and transfection

Full-length cDNAs of USP10, USP29, USP32, USP44, USP45, and RBM14 were cloned into pcDNA3.1 vectors with HA or Flag tags. mPC-1 cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L2000075) per the manufacturer's protocols. Experiments were performed 48 h post-transfection.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Cells were lysed on ice using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, #P0013B) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Yeasen, #20124ES03) and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; Yeasen, #58076ES01). Protein quantification was performed by BCA assay (Thermo, #23,227). Thirty micrograms of protein were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk in TBST, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-USP44 (1:500, Immunoway, #YT4841), anti-USP10 (1:500, Immunoway, #YN1464), anti-USP29 (1:500, Immunoway, #YN1469), anti-USP32 (1:500, Immunoway, #YT4835), anti-USP45 (1:500, Immunoway, #YT4842), anti-RBM14 (1:1,000, Abcam, ab70636), anti-Rad51 (1:1,000, Abcam, ab133534), anti-γH2AX (1:1,000, CST, #9718), anti-PCNA (1:1,000, CST, #13,110), anti-Bax (1:1,000, Abcam, ab32503), anti-Bcl2 (1:1,000, CST, #3498), anti-cleaved caspase-3 (1:1,000, CST, #9664), and anti-ACTB (1:5,000, Sigma, A2228). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (HRP-Anti-Rabbit IgG, Proteintech, #SA00001-2, 1:10,000; HRP-Anti-Mouse IgG, Proteintech, #SA00001-1, 1:10,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Thermo Scientific, #34,580). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ (NIH) software. Briefly, band intensities were measured, and background subtraction was applied. Target protein expression was normalized to the corresponding loading control (actin) in the same lane to account for loading variability. Relative protein levels were calculated as fold changes compared to the control group.

Co-immunoprecipitation and GST pull-down

Cells transfected with HA- or Flag-tagged constructs were lysed in ice-cold IP lysis buffer (Yeasen, #20119ES60) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation and precleared with protein A/G agarose beads. For Co‑IP, clarified lysates were incubated with anti‑HA (Proteintech, #51,064–2-AP, 1:1000) or anti‑FLAG (DYKDDDDK) (Proteintech, #20,543–1-AP, 1:1000) antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by addition of protein A/G agarose beads and incubation for 2 h at 4 °C. Beads were washed four times with IP lysis buffer, boiled in SDS sample buffer, and the eluted proteins were analyzed by SDS–PAGE and immunoblotting.

For GST pull-down assays, recombinant GST and GST-USP44 fusion proteins and His-RBM14 were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3). Bacterial lysates were clarified, and GST fusion proteins were affinity‑purified on glutathione‑Sepharose beads while His‑RBM14 was purified on Ni‑NTA agarose according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Equal amounts of GST or GST-USP44 immobilized on glutathione‑Sepharose beads were incubated with purified His-RBM14 at 4 °C for 3 h with gentle rotation. Beads were then washed with binding buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP‑40), boiled in SDS sample buffer, and bound proteins were resolved by SDS–PAGE and detected by Western immunoblotting.

Protein stability and ubiquitination assays

For degradation assays, treated cells were exposed to cycloheximide (CHX, 30 μM) to inhibit protein synthesis for the times indicated (0, 6, 9, 18 h). For proteasome inhibition, cells were pretreated with MG132 (10 or 20 μM) 6 h before CHX addition. For ubiquitination detection, cells transfected with Flag-RBM14, HA-USP44, and His-tagged ubiquitin were lysed in 1% SDS lysis buffer, boiled, diluted, and then immunoprecipitated with anti-Flag antibody overnight at 4 °C. Ubiquitinated proteins were detected by Western blot analysis using an anti-His antibody.

Cell cycle analysis

Cells were fixed in 70% ethanol at − 20 °C overnight, washed, and stained with 50 μg/mL 7-AAD containing RNase A (100 μg/mL) for 30 min at room temperature before analysis by flow cytometry.

Apoptosis assay

Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) staining were performed using commercial kits (BD Biosciences) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Data were immediately acquired on a flow cytometer, quantifying early and late apoptotic cells.

ROS measurement

Intracellular reactive oxygen species levels were detected by staining cells with 10 μM DCFH-DA (Beyotime, #S0034) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After washing, fluorescence intensity was measured by flow cytometry.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT kit (Yeasen, #0206ES76). Briefly, cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were irradiated at a dose of 6 Gy, and viability was evaluated 24 h post-irradiation. Following treatment as previously, 10 μL of MTT reagent (5 mg/mL) was added to each well, and cells were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h to allow formazan crystal formation. The crystals were dissolved by adding 100 μL of solubilization solution, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 4 h. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek). Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the untreated control group. All experiments were performed in triplicate and independently repeated at least three times.

Measurement of oxidative stress and cytotoxicity

Oxidative stress and cytotoxicity were assessed using commercial kits. Nitric oxide (NO) production in cell culture supernatants was measured using a Griess reagent assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, #A012-1). Briefly, 500 μL of supernatant was mixed with assay reagents, incubated at room temperature for 10 min, and the absorbance was read at 550 nm. Concentrations were determined from a standard curve. Tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) levels were quantified using an ELISA kit (Yueteng Biological, #YT-M30398). Supernatants were centrifuged (1000 ×g, 20 min, 4 °C), immediately deproteinized with perchloric acid, supplemented with dithiothreitol (DTT), and stored at − 80 °C in the dark to minimize oxidation. Samples were analyzed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with assays performed promptly after thawing and under reduced light. Cytotoxicity was evaluated by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release using a detection kit (Solarbio, #BC0680). Cells were pelleted, lysed in extraction buffer (5 × 106 cells/mL) via sonication (200 W, 3 s pulse, 10 s interval, 30 cycles) on ice, and centrifuged (8000 ×g, 10 min, 4 °C). Supernatants were incubated with assay reagents, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm after 3 min. A standard curve was used for quantification. All assays were performed in triplicate and repeated in at least three independent experiments.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using RNA Isolater Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Vazyme, #R401-01) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, #R222-01). Quantitative PCR was carried out with ChamQ SYBR Color qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, # Q431-02) on an Applied Biosystems real-time PCR system (ABI 7300 Plus). Primer sequences used were listed in the supplemental table (Supplemental Table 1). Relative expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct (ΔΔCt) method with 18S as the internal control. All reactions were run in technical triplicate, and experiments were repeated at least three times.

Morphological analysis

Cell morphology was observed and photographed using an inverted phase-contrast microscope (Olympus CKX41) at 24 h post-treatment.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in at least three biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were calculated using GraphPad Prism 9. Differences between groups were evaluated using unpaired Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Significance thresholds are indicated in figure legends.

Result

Characterization of mBMSCs and their EVs

We first confirmed the identity and multipotency of the isolated mBMSCs and validated the morphology, size distribution, and marker profile of their secreted EVs. The cells were negative for hematopoietic markers CD14 (0.38%), CD19 (0.43%), CD34 (0.46%), and CD45 (0.35%), indicating a lack of hematopoietic lineage contamination. High expression levels of mesenchymal stem cell markers CD29 (98.39%), CD44 (98.54%), CD73 (98.46%), and CD90 (98.30%) confirmed the mBMSCs phenotype (Fig. 1A). Differentiation potential of the mMSCs was evaluated by lineage-specific staining. Alizarin Red staining confirmed osteogenic differentiation through calcium deposition (Fig. 1B, top), Alcian Blue staining demonstrated chondrogenic differentiation by detecting proteoglycans (Fig. 1B, middle), and Oil Red O staining revealed lipid droplets indicative of adipogenic differentiation (Fig. 1B, bottom).

Gray shading represents isotype controls; red lines represent specific antibody staining. EVs derived from the mBMSCs were visualized by transmission electron microscopy, showing typical cup-shaped morphology with sizes consistent with exosomes (Fig. 1C). Nanoparticle tracking analysis revealed a predominant size distribution around 100 nm and a particle concentration, consistent with robust exosomes secretion (Fig. 1D). Western blot analysis further confirmed the presence of classical exosome markers CD9, CD63, and CD81 (Fig. 1E), validating their exosomes identity. Collectively, these data confirm that the isolated mBMSCs are bona fide mesenchymal stem cells and that they release well-characterized exosomes suitable for downstream functional studies.

Effects of ionizing radiation on mPC-1 cells

We further examined how ionizing radiation affects mPC-1 cell viability, DNA damage, and apoptosis. Radiation exposure significantly decreased the viability of mPC-1 cells in a dose-dependent manner, with cell viability markedly reduced at 6 and 8 Gy doses compared to control (Fig. 2A), and we chose 6 Gy for the downstream mechanistic study. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated elevated apoptosis rates in irradiated cells at 6 Gy (Fig. 2B). Western blotting further demonstrated the increased γH2AX in 6 Gy-ionized cells relative to controls (Fig. 2C). Collectively, radiation induced loss of viability, DNA damage, and apoptosis.

B Flow cytometry and quantification bar plots showing apoptotic cell populations stained with Annexin V-FITC/PI at 0 Gy and 6 Gy. C Representative Western blotting picture and quantification of γH2AX protein expression (mean ± SD, n = 3)

Role of mBMSCs and EVs in DNA repair and apoptosis of mPC-1/IR cells

We next investigated whether the protective effects of mBMSCs against radiation-induced DNA damage and apoptosis are mediated through their secreted EVs. Compared to the mock group, co-culture with mBMSCs significantly increased the DNA repair protein Rad51 and the proliferation marker PCNA but decreased the DNA damage marker γH2AX. Additionally, pro-apoptotic protein Bax and cleaved-caspase 3 levels decreased, while anti-apoptotic protein Bcl2 increased, indicating reduced apoptosis. However, treatment with the exosome secretion inhibitor GW4869 reversed the protective effects of mBMSCs, leading to lower Rad51 and PCNA and higher γH2AX, Bax, and cleaved-caspase 3 levels. Treatment with isolated mBMSC-EVs mimicked the effects of mBMSCs, increasing Rad51 and PCNA and decreasing γH2AX, Bax, and cleaved-caspase 3 levels (Fig. 3A, B). These results suggest that mBMSC-EVs can serve as major mediators delivering mBMSC-driven enhancement of DNA repair and inhibition of apoptosis to mPC-1/IR cells.

Effects of mBMSCs and mBMSC-EVs on cell viability, cell cycle, apoptosis, and oxidative stress in mPC-1/IR cells

We next assessed how mBMSCs and mBMSC-EVs influence viability, cell cycle distribution, apoptosis, membrane integrity, and oxidative stress in mPC-1/IR cells. Co-culture of mPC-1/IR cells with mBMSCs or treatment with mBMSC-EVs significantly enhanced cell viability compared to mock treatment, while the addition of the exosome inhibitor GW4869 diminished this protective effect (Fig. 4A). Cell cycle analysis revealed that mBMSCs and mBMSC-EVs decreased the proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase and increased those in the S phase, effects reversed by GW4869 treatment (Fig. 4B, C). Apoptosis assays demonstrated reduced apoptotic percentages in the mBMSC and mBMSC-EV groups relative to mock and GW4869 groups (Fig. 4D, E). Morphological observations corroborated these findings, showing improved cell morphology with mBMSCs and mBMSC-EVs (Fig. 4F).

Furthermore, LDH release, indicative of cell membrane damage, was also decreased following mBMSC or mBMSC-EV treatment (Fig. 4G). mBMSCs and mBMSC-EVs significantly diminished intracellular ROS levels, as measured by DCF fluorescence, while GW4869 inhibited this effect (Fig. 4H, I). Correspondingly, levels of BH4 and NO were increased in the mBMSC and mBMSC-EV treatment groups relative to controls (Fig. 4J, K). These data indicate that mBMSCs and their EVs play a critical role in protecting mPC-1 cells from irradiation-induced oxidative stress, cell cycle arrest, and apoptosis.

Differential expressions of USPs in mPC-1/IR cells and functional screening of candidate USPs

To identify deubiquitinases potentially involved in the radiation response, we profiled the expression of USP family members in mPC-1 cells after irradiation. RT‑qPCR revealed that USP32, USP45, USP29, USP10, and USP44 were significantly downregulated in mPC-1/IR cells compared with mock-treated controls (Fig. 5A). Western blot analysis further confirmed the marked downregulation of USP44 at the protein level (Fig. 5B, C). We hypothesized that the downregulation of these USPs reflects irradiation-induced damage and that their restoration via mBMSCs or EVs could alleviate radiation toxicity. To assess the functional relevance of these candidates, each USP was overexpressed in mPC-1/IR cells. Overexpression of all five USPs significantly enhanced cell viability compared to vector controls, with USP44 overexpression showing the most pronounced protective effect (Fig. 5D). Based on consistent downregulation after irradiation and a strong functional rescue phenotype, USP44 was selected for further investigation. We also confirmed the presence of USP44 protein in both mBMSCs and mBMSC-derived EVs (Fig. 5E), supporting its potential for intercellular transfer. Collectively, these results identify USP44 as a radiation-responsive deubiquitinating enzyme (DUB) and a candidate mediator underlying mBMSC-conferred radioprotection.

USP44 expression regulates DNA repair and apoptosis in mPC-1/IR cells via mBMSCs and EVs

We next tested whether USP44 in mBMSCs and mBMSC-EVs modulate DNA repair and apoptosis in mPC-1/IR cells. The efficiency of USP44 knockdown in mBMSCs was confirmed by RT-qPCR, showing significant mRNA reduction with three different shRNAs compared to the negative control (shNC) (Fig. 6A). where we selected shUSP44-2 for downstream application. Similarly, USP44 mRNA was significantly reduced in mBMSCs and in isolated mBMSC-EVs after shUSP44 transduction (Fig. 6B). Depletion of USP44 in mBMSCs (shUsp44) or inhibition of EV release (GW4869) reduced USP44 levels in cocultured mPC-1/IR cells, compared with the mBMSC or mBMSC-EVs transfected with the shNC vector group (Fig. 6C).

C USP44 mRNA expression in mPC-1/IR cells after co-culture or treatment with mBMSCs or mBMSC-EVs transduced with shNC or shUSP44, with or without GW4869 inhibitor (mean ± SD, n = 3). D, E Representative Western blot images and relative quantifications showing protein levels of USP44, Rad51, γH2AX, PCNA, Bax, Bcl2, cleaved-caspase 3, and actin in mPC-1/IR cells under indicated treatments (mean ± SD, n = 3). Statistical significance is indicated with p-values. P < 0.05 is considered significantly different

Western blot analysis further revealed that knockdown of USP44 in mBMSCs significantly decreased expression of DNA repair and proliferation markers Rad51 and PCNA, while increasing DNA damage marker γH2AX and pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved-caspase 3, with a concomitant reduction in the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl2 in mPC-1/IR cells (Fig. 6D, E). Treatment with GW4869 can reverse the effects of mBMSCs with intact USP44 expression (Fig. 6D, E). These results reveal that USP44 carried by mBMSC-EVs promoted DNA repair and cell survival in mPC-1/IR cells, and inhibition of USP44 diminishes these protective effects.

USP44 knockdown in mBMSCs/mBMSC-EVs impairs viability, promotes apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, oxidative stress, and cytotoxicity in mPC-1/IR cells

We further evaluated the functional consequences of USP44 loss in mBMSC-EVs on multiple injury endpoints in mPC-1/IR cells. Knockdown of USP44 in mBMSCs significantly reduced the protective effects against irradiation in mPC-1 cells. Cell viability assays showed decreased cell survival in mPC-1/IR cells with USP44 knockdown mBMSCs or mBMSC-EVs, with persistent impairment seen upon co-culture with the exosome inhibitor GW4869-treated mBMSCs (Fig. 7A). Consistent with the pro-proliferative effect observed previously (Fig. 4B, C), in which mBMSCs or mBMSC-EVs increased the S-phase population, further analysis confirmed that this effect was reversed by USP44 knockdown in mBMSCs or abrogated by GW4869 treatment (Fig. 7B, C).

Apoptosis assays demonstrated elevated apoptotic rates in mPC-1/IR cells co-cultured with mBMSCs or mBMSC-EVs lacking USP44 compared to controls (Fig. 7D, E). Morphological changes consistent with reduced cell health were confirmed by light microscopy (Fig. 7F). Besides, ROS levels were increased following USP44 knockdown mBSMCs or mBMSC-EVs treatment, indicating higher oxidative stress, while GW4869 treatment compounded these effects (Fig. 7G, H). Furthermore, the levels of NO and BH4 were reduced in USP44-deficient mBMSCs/EVs treated groups (Fig. 7I, J), with increased ROS levels; these results suggested USP44 deficiency aggravated redox imbalance after irradiation. LDH release level converted by mBMSC-EVs, indicating protective effects of mBMSC-EVs (Fig. 7K). These findings demonstrated that loss of USP44 functions in mBMSCs or mBMSC-EVs impairs radioprotective effects in mPC-1 cells.

USP44 interacts with RBM14 and regulates its ubiquitination and stability in mPC-1 cells

To identify proteins that specifically interact with USP44, we conducted immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry analysis. Proteins uniquely detected in the USP44 immunoprecipitates were ranked by total peptide counts in descending order (Fig. 8A). Based on literature related to radiation damage (Supplemental Table 2), the top three candidates were selected for further investigation. To validate these interactions, co-immunoprecipitation assays were performed focusing on USP44 and the proteins RBM14, Hnrnpu, and Hnrnpk. HA-tagged USP44 was found to co-immunoprecipitate with Flag-tagged RBM14, but not with Flag-tagged Hnrnpu or Hnrnpk (Fig. 8B). This interaction was further confirmed by reciprocal co-IP and GST pull-down assays, demonstrating a direct physical binding between USP44 and RBM14 (Fig. 8C, D).

Next, we assessed the protein degradation levels of USP44 and RBM14 using Cycloheximide (CHX) combined with proteasome inhibition assays. Treatment with CHX led to decreases of USP44 and RBM14 protein levels, but RBM14 protein level was significantly enhanced by the proteasome inhibitor MG132 in a dose-dependent manner, suggesting proteasomal degradation of RBM14 (Fig. 8E, F). Ubiquitination assays were conducted to further elucidate how USP44 influences RBM14. Immunoprecipitation analysis showed knockdown of USP44 increased RBM14 ubiquitination, whereas overexpression of USP44 reduced ubiquitinated RBM14 levels (Fig. 8G). Correspondingly, CHX assays showed that USP44 knockdown accelerated RBM14 degradation, while USP44 overexpression prolonged its half-life (Fig. 8H, I). These findings suggest that USP44 binds to and stabilizes RBM14.

Modulation of USP44 and RBM14 expression in mPC-1/IR cells by mBMSC or mBMSC-EVs influences DNA damage response and apoptosis-related proteins

We further investigate whether changes in USP44 and RBM14 delivered by mBMSC-EVs alter DNA repair and apoptotic signaling in mPC-1/IR cells. Knockdown of USP44 in mBMSCs or mBMSC-EVs resulted in a significant decrease in USP44 and RBM14 protein levels in mPC-1/IR cells, which coincided with increased expression of DNA damage markers γH2AX and reduced Rad51 and PCNA levels (Fig. 9A, B). Conversely, overexpression of RBM14 partially rescued these effects when co-treated with shUSP44-expressing mBMSCs or mBMSC-EVs (Fig. 9A, B). Additionally, the apoptotic marker Bax and cleaved caspase 3 were elevated upon USP44 knockdown, while the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl2 decreased, indicating enhanced apoptosis in mPC-1/IR cells. RBM14 overexpression counteracted these changes with decreased Bax and cleaved caspase 3 levels, and increased Bcl2 expression (Fig. 9A, B). These data suggest that USP44 from mBMSC-EVs positively regulates RBM14 in mPC-1/IR cells, thereby affecting DNA repair capacity and apoptosis.

USP44 knockdown in mBMSCs reduces viability and promotes apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, oxidative stress, and cytotoxicity in mPC-1/IR cells

Finally, we examined whether USP44 deficiency in donor mBMSCs or their EVs leads to irradiated damage of recipient cells, and whether RBM14 can rescue these defects. Cell viability assays revealed that mPC-1/IR cells co-cultured with mBMSCs or treated with mBMSC-EVs with shUSP44 exhibited significantly decreased viability compared to controls (mBMSCs/shNC or mBMSC/shNC-EVs). RBM14 overexpression can rescue the decreased viability caused by USP44 knockdown (Fig. 10A). Cell cycle analysis by flow cytometry showed that USP44 knockdown induced G0/G1 phase arrest and reduced S phase population in mPC-1/IR cells; these effects were reversed by RBM14 overexpression (Fig. 10B, C). Correspondingly, Annexin V/PI staining demonstrated increased apoptosis in mPC-1/IR cells treated with shUSP44-expressing mBMSCs or their EVs, which was attenuated upon RBM14 overexpression (Fig. 10D, E).

Moreover, cell morphology displayed cytotoxic changes consistent with apoptosis upon USP44 silencing, which were alleviated by RBM14 upregulation (Fig. 10F). Measurement of oxidative stress markers revealed decreased ROS levels (Fig. 10G, H), elevated NO and BH4 content in mPC-1/IR cells treated with RBM14 overexpressed mBMSCs or their EVs (Fig. 10I, J). LDH release was also rescued by RBM14 overexpression (Fig. 10K). These findings confirm that the USP44-RBM14 axis plays a critical role in protecting mPC-1/IR cells.

Discussion

This study investigates the protective role of mBMSC-EVs against irradiation-induced damage in epididymal cells. Mechanistically, we identify the deubiquitinase USP44, elucidate its function in DNA repair and cell survival, and reveal its regulation of the substrate RBM14. Our results suggest that mBMSC-EVs, enriched in USP44, promote radioprotection and reproductive preservation by directly stabilizing RBM14 and enhancing DNA repair. These data support a model in which EVs‑mediated delivery or induction of USP44 reduces ubiquitin‑dependent turnover of RBM14, thereby promoting more efficient resolution of DNA lesions and reducing residual cell damage after radiation exposure..

Our data show that mBMSC-EVs reduce IR‑induced damage in mPC‑1 cells, consistent with prior reports that EVs recapitulate key tissue‑repair functions of their parent cells [11, 23, 24]. EVs exert anti‑inflammatory, pro‑repair, and immunomodulatory effects across radiation‑injured tissues [25] and ameliorate radiation injury via mitochondrial transfer and mitophagy [26]. Beyond direct, cell‑intrinsic DNA‑repair support, MSC‑derived EVs can carry proteins, lipids, and miRNAs that may shift immune responses toward tissue repair, promoting M2‑like macrophage polarization and suppressing proinflammatory cytokines, thereby limiting secondary inflammation and fibrosis after IR. Future studies should: (a) evaluate the effects of EVs on immune‑cell phenotypes in vitro (e.g., macrophage polarization and cytokine assays), and (b) quantify local inflammatory mediators, immune infiltration, and fibrosis in vivo following IR with or without EV-based therapy.

Our data indicated that mBMSC-EVs mitigated IR-induced damage in mCP-1 cells, at least in part through upregulation of USP44 and downstream stabilization of DNA-repair processes. Consistently, USP44 has been reported to form a complex with BRCA2 in neuroblastoma cells, where it is required to prevent chromosome breakage [27]. USP44 also stabilizes DDB2 to facilitate nucleotide excision repair induced by DMBA or UV light [28] and counteracts the DNA double-strand break response mediated by ubiquitin ligases RNF8 and RNF168 [29]. Moreover, USP44 has been linked to cell viability by attenuating c-MYC-mediated transactivation of LDHA transcripts, inhibiting glycolysis and subsequent cell proliferation[30]. Engineered EVs for the targeted delivery of USP44 could be developed to protect normal tissues (testis, epididymis, possibly ovaries) from off-target radiation during pelvic/abdominopelvic radiotherapy [31], thereby preserving fertility and endocrine function. However, further work is needed to identify direct substrates and to demonstrate benefit in vivo.

We identified RBM14 as a novel substrate of USP44 in irradiated cells, with emerging evidence pointing to its critical role in regulating homologous recombination (HR). Previous studies have primarily linked RBM14 to the non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) pathway through interactions with CtIP and its ability to stimulate DNA-PK-dependent repair, facilitate KU protein release [32, 33], and promote recruitment of XRCC4 and XLF to damage sites [34]. RBM14 stimulates DNA repair by controlling the DNA-PK-dependent NHEJ pathway [35]. Recent findings, including our observation of Rad51 upregulation and enhanced Rad51 foci formation, strongly suggest an additional role in HR [36]. RBM14’s recruitment to DNA damage sites is known to be PARP- and RNA polymerase II-dependent, and it contributes to genome integrity through transcription-coupled repair mechanisms and regulation of alternative splicing [37]. It also maintains telomere stability by modulating TERRA levels and R-loop dynamics, and has been implicated in epigenetic regulation of cell growth and apoptosis [38]. The dual involvement in both HR and NHEJ may reflect RBM14’s role as a scaffold protein that integrates transcriptional and repair machineries at sites of DNA damage.

Our screening also revealed several USP family members with altered expression after IR, pointing to potential redundancy and overlap among DUBs in the DNA damage response. Many DUBs restore the function or availability of repair proteins by removing ubiquitin chains that mark proteins for degradation or alter their signaling properties [39, 40]. Further Functional overlap among USPs could mean that multiple family members cooperate or compensate to regulate shared substrates.

For reproductive tissues, preserving the appropriate proliferative capacity of germ cells and supporting niche cells is crucial for functional recovery [41]. EV-based intervention must therefore be shown to restore healthy proliferative dynamics in normal cells without promoting proliferation or survival of co-located tumor cells [42]. Our finding reveals that the USP44-RBM14 axis influences cell viability. Apart from reflecting cell-cycle distribution, PCNA, which also marks proliferation [43], showed altered expression in our samples. From a translational perspective, this finding identifies a promising strategy to preserve reproductive function during pelvic or abdominopelvic radiotherapy [44]. An EV‑based countermeasure that selectively protects testes, epididymis, or ovaries could mitigate infertility and endocrine dysfunction in cancer survivors.

Although our study offers novel mechanistic insights into how exosomal signaling mitigates IR-induced damage, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we relied on the mPC-1 epididymal cell line as a model, which may not fully recapitulate the physiological complexity of reproductive tissue in vivo. In addition, although we assessed cell viability and DNA damage, further assays, such as senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining, would help clarify whether mPC-1/IR cells undergo senescence in response to irradiation, as has been reported in other transformed cell models. Moreover, while GW4869 was used to inhibit exosome release, its potential off-target effects necessitate complementary approaches, for example, genetic perturbation of EV biogenesis pathways or antibody-based EV depletion, to confirm that the observed effects are specifically EV-mediated. The translational relevance of our findings awaits further validation through in vivo models and fertility outcome studies. Furthermore, while our study focused on USP44, the complex composition of EVs implies that other cargo molecules (proteins, RNAs, lipids) likely contribute to the functional outcomes. The observed discrepancies between mRNA and protein levels of certain USPs may reflect post-transcriptional regulation and differences in protein half-life [45], further underscoring the importance of multilevel validation in mechanistic studies.

Future work should focus on validating these findings in vivo, employing more definitive EV manipulation tools, and systematically characterizing EV cargo via proteomic, transcriptomic, and lipidomic analyses. Such efforts will help identify the active components within mBMSC-EVs and solidify the mechanistic basis of their radioprotective function.

Conclusion

In summary, this study characterizes how mBMSC-EVs mitigate irradiation injury in epididymal cells through a novel USP44/RBM14-dependent mechanism, highlighting mBMSC-EVs as a stem cell-derived therapeutic for radiation-induced reproductive damage.
