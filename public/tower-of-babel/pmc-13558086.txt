The Mediating Role of Perceived Social Support in the Association Between Perceived Stress and Self-Management Behaviors Among Patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus and Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease
Authors: Ling Ma, Yaling Song, Wei Long, Aiqun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo examine whether perceived social support mediates the relationship between perceived stress and self-management behaviors in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) complicated by metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), and to provide a theoretical basis for targeted psychosocial interventions in clinical practice.MethodsBy convenience sampling, patients with T2DM and MASLD were recruited from a tertiary hospital in Changsha between April 2024 and September 2025. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, the Chinese Perceived Stress Scale, the Perceived Social Support Scale, and the Chronic Disease Self-Management Behavior Measurement Scale.ResultsThe mean scores for perceived stress, perceived social support, and self-management behaviors were 25.66 ± 8.02, 56.05 ± 10.28, and 27.63 ± 8.62, respectively. Pearson correlation analysis indicated that dimensions such as loss of control and tension were significantly associated with both social support and self-management behaviors (all P < 0.01). Perceived stress was negatively correlated with perceived social support (r = −0.534, P < 0.01) and self-management behaviors (r = −0.391, P < 0.01), whereas perceived social support showed a positive correlation with self-management behaviors (r = 0.471, P < 0.01). Mediation analysis demonstrated that perceived social support partially mediated the association between perceived stress and self-management behaviors, accounting for 50.2% of the total effect.ConclusionPerceived social support functions as a key mediator linking perceived stress and self-management behaviors in patients with T2DM and MASLD. These findings highlight the importance of integrating psychological assessment into routine nursing care. Interventions such as family-involved nursing care, peer support groups, and the utilization of community-based healthcare resources may help establish a coordinated support system that connects the hospital, family, and society. The proposed approach could mitigate the adverse impact of stress on self-management behaviors and ultimately improve patient outcomes.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance and/or impaired insulin secretion.1 Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) refers to a clinicopathological syndrome featuring excessive lipid accumulation within hepatocytes.2 The condition is recognized as a chronic, progressive liver condition closely associated with insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, and T2DM.3 MASLD and T2DM are pathophysiologically intertwined. Specifically, mechanisms such as insulin resistance and dysregulated lipid metabolism reinforce one another, creating a bidirectional, self-perpetuating cycle that accelerates disease progression.4,5 Evidence has shown that the global prevalence of MASLD among individuals with diabetes mellitus (DM) is approximately 65.04%.6 In China, the prevalence of MASLD among middle-aged and older adults with T2DM exceeds 60%.7 Moreover, patients with this comorbidity tend to exhibit more pronounced insulin resistance and a heightened risk of cardiovascular complications,8 underscoring the substantial clinical burden. At present, no curative therapy is available for this combined condition. Management strategies primarily focus on lifestyle modification and pharmacological treatment to control disease activity and delay progression,9 necessitating sustained, lifelong self-management on the part of patients.

Effective self-management plays a central role in achieving glycemic control, reducing the risk of complications, and slowing disease progression in patients with T2DM.10 Nevertheless, preceding research suggests that patients with concurrent T2DM and MASLD often demonstrate limited awareness of health management and suboptimal adherence to recommended self-management practices.11,12 Perceived stress reflects an individual’s subjective appraisal of internal and external stressors.13 Elevated levels of perceived stress may trigger both physiological and psychological stress responses in patients with T2DM, leading to reduced motivation and capacity for effective self-management.14 Perceived social support, defined as an individual’s internalized perception and evaluation of support received from external sources, reflects the extent and quality of their social connectedness.15 Existing evidence has identified perceived social support as a positive predictor of self-management behaviors among patients with T2DM.16

Although both perceived stress and perceived social support have been shown to play crucial roles in shaping self-management behaviors, the underlying pathways linking these variables remain insufficiently explored. Notably, several recent studies have examined mediation models involving social support in related populations. A structural equation modeling (SEM) study based on 302 young and middle-aged patients with multimorbidity confirmed that perceived social support plays a significant mediating role between perceived stress and self-management behaviors.17 A sample study of 772 adults with type 1 diabetes found that the impact of diabetes-related stress on health-related quality of life is partially mediated by perceived social support.18 Similarly, Sun’s research indicated that in older patients with T2DM and chronic complications, chronic disease resources can indirectly influence self-management behaviors through diabetes-related distress, with this mediating effect accounting for 28.89% of the total effect.19 Chen’s study also confirmed that in patients with type 2 diabetes, social support serves as a mediator between diabetes-related distress and self-management, with a total mediating effect reaching 56.32%.20

Against this background, this study aimed to examine the interrelationships among perceived stress, perceived social support, and self-management behaviors in this population. Furthermore, we tested the hypothesis that perceived social support mediates the association between perceived stress and self-management behaviors. By approaching the issue from a psychosocial perspective, the present study seeks to offer an additional avenue for improving self-management in patients with this comorbidity. While the mediating role of perceived social support between stress and self-management has been established in populations such as patients with multiple chronic conditions and those with T2DM alone, the present work represents is a preliminary attempt to quantitatively validate such a mediation model specifically within the T2DM- MASLD comorbid population.

Subjects and Methods

Study Subjects

This cross-sectional study was conducted between April 2024 and September 2025. By convenience sampling, patients with T2DM complicated by MASLD who were hospitalized in the Department of Endocrinology at a tertiary hospital in Changsha were recruited. While convenience sampling was selected for its practicality and efficiency, efforts were made to minimize potential selection bias by applying consistent inclusion and exclusion criteria and enrolling eligible patients consecutively wherever possible.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) diagnosis of T2DM in accordance with established criteria,21 with a disease duration of at least 6 months, and concurrent diagnosis of MASLD based on diagnostic guidelines;22 (3) provision of informed consent and voluntary participation; and (4) ability to communicate effectively. Patients were rendered ineligible if they met any of the following criteria: (1) presence of severe comorbid conditions affecting major organs, including heart disease (New York Heart Association functional class ≥III), recent cerebrovascular events (eg, stroke), or severe renal impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min/1.73 m2); and/or (2) cognitive impairment or diagnosed psychiatric disorders.

The prolonged data collection period in this study was primarily driven by three factors. First, the strict exclusion criteria screened out approximately 35–40% of hospitalized patients with type 2 diabetes, significantly narrowing the eligible pool. Second, given the short average length of stay in the endocrinology department (only 5–7 days), many eligible patients could not complete all questionnaires during their hospitalization; consequently, a substantial portion of the data had to be collected via outpatient visits or telephone follow-ups. Finally, to minimize potential bias from seasonal fluctuations in metabolic markers and emotional states, participant recruitment was intentionally distributed across different seasons to enhance the temporal representativeness of the sample. Collectively, these factors contributed to the extended timeline for data collection.

The required sample size was estimated as 10 times the number of independent variables, including both demographic and core study variables (a total of 18 predictors). Allowing for a potential attrition rate of 10%–20%, the minimum sample size was calculated to be 198–216 participants. This estimation was further supported by power analysis using the software G*Power (assuming a medium effect size, f2 = 0.15, α = 0.05, β = 0.20).23 Ultimately, 299 participants were included in the final analysis. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No. LY-2024-061).

MASLD severity was assessed by abdominal ultrasonography (Logiq E9, GE Healthcare, USA) performed by experienced radiologists with at least 10 years of clinical experience. Hepatic steatosis was graded according to standard ultrasonographic criteria: mild (grade 1)—slight diffuse increase in hepatic echogenicity with normal visualization of diaphragm and intrahepatic vessels; moderate (grade 2)—moderate diffuse increase in echogenicity with slightly impaired visualization of diaphragm and intrahepatic vessels; and severe (grade 3)—marked increase in hepatic echogenicity with poor or no visualization of diaphragm, intrahepatic vessels, and posterior right liver lobe. This grading system has been widely adopted in clinical research and is consistent with the recommendations of the Chinese guidelines for the prevention and treatment of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (2024 edition).

Instruments

General Information Questionnaire

The general information questionnaire was developed by the research team based on a review of relevant literature and consultation with nursing and endocrinology experts.24,25 Content validity was evaluated by three specialists in nursing and endocrinology, yielding a content validity index of 0.92. A pilot test was conducted with 30 patients, and test–retest reliability assessed after a two-week interval demonstrated good stability (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.89 for items regarding lifestyle habits and self-management behaviors). The questionnaire collected data on sex, age, marital status, body mass index (BMI), educational level, living arrangement, place of residence, monthly personal income, employment status, type of medical insurance, duration of DM, presence of DM-related complications, current primary treatment modality for DM, types of medications used, and severity of MASLD.

Chinese Perceived Stress Scale (CPSS)

The Chinese version of the Perceived Stress Scale, translated and validated by Yang,26 was used to assess individuals’ perceptions and appraisal of stressful life events over the preceding month. The scale comprises 14 items across two dimensions, namely, perceived uncontrollability and perceived tension. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“always”), with a total score ranging from 0 to 56. Items 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, and 13 belong to the perceived uncontrollability dimension and are reverse-coded. Items 1, 2, 3, 8, 11, 13, and 14 constitute the perceived tension dimension and are scored in the forward direction. Higher total scores indicate greater perceived stress sensitivity. In the present study, the CPSS yielded a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.926, confirming its reliability in the study cohort.

Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS)

The Chinese version of PSSS, translated by Jiang,27 was employed to assess the level of social support perceived by individuals from both family and external sources. The scale consists of three dimensions: family support (items 3, 4, 8, and 11), friend support (items 6, 7, 9, and 12), and other social support (items 1, 2, 5, and 10). Responses are rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”), yielding a total score between 12 and 84. Higher scores reflect greater perceived social support. Scores of 12–36 indicate low support, 37–60 moderate support, and 61–84 high support levels.28 In this study, the PSSS demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.887.

Chronic Disease Self-Management Behavior Management Scale (CDSMBMS)

The CDSMBMS was originally developed by the Stanford University Patient Education Research Center29 and subsequently translated and culturally adapted into Chinese by Fu Dongbo of Fudan University.30 The scale comprises three dimensions: exercise, cognitive symptom management, and communication with physicians, totaling 15 items. The exercise dimension includes six items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“always”), with a total score of 0–24. The remaining dimensions are assessed using a 6-point Likert scale, from 0 (“never”) to 5 (“always”). The total score ranges from 0 to 69, with higher scores indicating stronger self-management ability in chronic disease contexts. Based on total scores, self-management levels are categorized as low (<20.7), moderate (20.7–45.5), and high (>45.5).31 In this study, no modifications were made to the original items. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.833.

Data Collection and Quality Control

Questionnaire data were collected by two nurses who received standardized training. The training program lasted four academic hours and covered the study objectives, inclusion and exclusion criteria, interpretation of measurement scales, and survey administration techniques. Consistency between the two investigators in guiding questionnaire completion was high, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.91 based on simulated assessments. Before data collection, both investigators received additional instruction on questionnaire administration procedures. Eligible patients who provided informed consent were given questionnaires along with standardized written instructions. Participants completed the questionnaires independently, after which the forms were collected on-site and checked for completeness. Any missing responses were promptly clarified and mented. A total of 319 questionnaires were distributed. Of these, 11 were excluded due to incomplete responses or evident response patterns, yielding 299 valid questionnaires and an effective response rate of 93.73%. During the data collection process, a small number of eligible participants who initially agreed to participate were unable to complete the survey due to time constraints, changes in health status, or loss to follow-up.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0. Categorical data were described using frequencies (n) and percentages (%). The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to assess normality. Continuous data conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x±s). Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships among perceived stress, perceived social support, and self-management behaviors. Mediation analysis was performed using the PROCESS macro (version 4.1) in SPSS, with Model 4 specified. Bootstrap resampling (5000 iterations) was applied, and core variables were standardized using Z-scores before analysis to evaluate the mediating effect.

Univariate Analysis of Subgroup Differences

Further univariate analyses were performed to examine differences in key study variables across demographic and clinical subgroups. For perceived social support, one-way analysis of variance revealed a statistically significant difference across educational levels (F = 5.32, P = 0.005), with post-hoc comparisons indicating that patients holding a junior college degree or above reported greater levels of perceived social support compared with those with a secondary education or below. For self-management behaviors, independent-samples t-tests showed that patients with a monthly income ≥5,000 yuan scored significantly higher than those earning <5,000 yuan (t = 2.89, P = 0.004). Similarly, patients with a junior-college education or above demonstrated significantly better self-management behaviors than those with lower educational attainment (t = 3.15, P = 0.002).

Mediating Effects of Perceived Social Support on Perceived Stress and Self-Management Behaviors in Patients with T2DM and MASLD

Given the potential for common method bias in questionnaire-based studies, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted across all measurement items. Six factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, and the first factor accounted for 20.76% of the total variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%.22 This result suggests that common method bias was not a serious concern in the present study. Discussion

This study confirms the hypothesis that perceived social support partially mediates the relationship between perceived stress and self-management behaviors in patients with T2DM and MASLD.

Status of Perceived Stress, Perceived Social Support, and Self-Management Behaviors in Patients with T2DM and MASLD

The observed mean perceived stress score (25.66 ± 8.02) was notably higher than that reported in T2DM-only populations,15 a difference we attribute to the compounded disease burden inherent in MASLD comorbidity. Compared with individuals with T2DM alone, those with concomitant MASLD typically present with more pronounced insulin resistance,3,8 greater difficulty in achieving glycemic control, and poorer clinical prognosis. These factors may, in turn, influence neuroendocrine and immune system functioning, thereby exacerbating negative emotional states, such as anxiety, and depression, and heightening perceived stress. In addition, patients with T2DM and MASLD must simultaneously manage multiple aspects of their condition, including glycemic control, dietary fat intake, monitoring of liver function, and avoidance of hepatotoxic medications. This multidimensional management burden may further intensify the demands of self-management. These findings highlight the need for healthcare providers to strengthen psychological assessment and targeted health education for this population. Establishing a more robust social support system may also help alleviate perceived stress.

The moderate level of perceived social support (56.05 ± 10.28) in our cohort was lower than in previous studies of younger T2DM populations.32 This discrepancy highlights the critical role of sociodemographic factors. We found that patients with higher educational attainment reported significantly greater perceived social support. Older adults and those with lower education levels may have limited access to digital health resources and peer networks, and may lack the skills to actively mobilize external support.30 From a clinical standpoint, this suggests that simply having a support network is insufficient; patients’ ability to perceive and effectively utilize available support is equally crucial, and this ability may be contingent on health literacy and social engagement.

In our cohort, the mean self-management behavior score was 27.63 ± 8.62, indicating a moderate level with considerable room for improvement among T2DM and MASLD patients.33,34 This suboptimal performance may partly reflect the sample’s socioeconomic and educational profile; those with higher monthly incomes or at least a college degree showed significantly better self-management,34 suggesting that lower education and income constrain effective self-management. In this study, 52.5% earned <5,000 RMB per month; such financial insecurity may undermine confidence in coping with chronic illness,35 hindering self-management. Moreover, 54.9% had a high school education or below. Limited education not only restricts disease-related knowledge36 but also impairs the ability to obtain, comprehend, and apply health information, potentially compromising adherence and proactiveness in self-management activities like medication adherence, blood glucose monitoring, and dietary control.

Relationships Among Perceived Stress, Perceived Social Support, and Self-Management Behaviors in Patients with T2DM and MASLD

Our findings reveal a significant negative correlation between perceived social support and perceived stress in patients with T2DM and MASLD, indicating that greater support is associated with lower stress, consistent with prior studies.17,37 The social buffering model38 suggests that strong social support facilitates cognitive reappraisal of stressors, allowing them to be viewed as manageable challenges rather than threats, thereby reducing stress. Perceived support from family, friends, and healthcare professionals provides emotional reassurance, practical guidance, and tangible assistance, alleviating loneliness, helplessness, and anxiety, and ultimately lowering stress perception during disease management. Moreover, encouragement from family, peer support, and professional guidance can improve treatment adherence and self-management confidence. As effective management leads to improved glycemic control and liver function, a positive feedback loop may emerge, reinforcing self-efficacy and diminishing feelings of uncertainty and loss of control.

These findings also support the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory,39 which holds that individuals strive to protect their limited psychological resources. Chronic comorbidity acts as a persistent stressor that continuously drains patients’ mental and emotional reserves, and elevated perceived stress signals awareness of this ongoing resource loss. To avert further depletion, patients may reduce investment in health behaviors such as regular physical activity and strict dietary control, directly impairing self-management. Aligned with this framework, our results showed a negative correlation between perceived stress and self-management, meaning lower stress was linked to better self-management, as previously reported.17,40 Several mechanisms may account for this. First, sustained high stress depletes already limited psychological resources, weakening self-regulation and the ability to maintain disciplined disease routines. Second, high stress is often accompanied by anxiety and depression, which can undermine confidence and cause disease management tasks to be seen as burdensome, promoting avoidance or passive coping. Third, stress-induced physiological responses may worsen insulin resistance and hepatic lipid accumulation, making disease control more difficult and further hindering effective self-management.

Mediating Role of Perceived Social Support in the Relationship Between Perceived Stress and Self-Management Behaviors in Patients with T2DM and MASLD

Mediation analysis showed that perceived social support partially mediated the relationship between perceived stress and self-management behaviors in patients with T2DM and MASLD, accounting for 50.2% of the total effect. This clinically significant proportion suggests that enhancing perceived social support may be more effective in improving health behaviors than strategies focused solely on stress reduction or direct self-management instruction; thus, social support interventions should be prioritized for this population.41 Perceived stress exerts both a direct negative effect and an indirect effect through perceived social support on self-management, a mechanism that can be understood through the COR theory and the social buffering model.

Within the COR framework, comorbid T2DM and MASLD acts as a chronic, compounded stressor that continuously drains physiological and psychological resources. High perceived stress signals ongoing or imminent resource loss, prompting patients to scale back investment in health behaviors, which weakens confidence and reduces adherence. In parallel, the social buffering model holds that perceived social support mitigates stress’s adverse effects by improving psychological well-being, buffering the impact of stress on health. When patients perceive and effectively utilize informational, emotional, and instrumental support, such external resources compensate for depleted internal reserves, enhancing motivation to cope and sustaining self-management behaviors.42

In light of these findings, healthcare providers should closely monitor patients’ psychological experiences and integrate psychosocial assessment into follow-up care. For patients with elevated perceived stress, targeted interventions such as mindfulness-based stress reduction and cognitive behavioral therapy may alleviate stress. Concurrently, strengthening social support systems through family involvement, peer support programs, and health education can enhance the perception and utilization of support, ultimately fostering sustained improvements in self-management.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, convenience sampling was employed, and participants were recruited from a single tertiary hospital in Changsha, which may limit the representativeness of the sample and constrain the generalizability of the findings. Second, although this study confirmed the overall mediating role of perceived social support, it did not further differentiate the effects across specific dimensions (eg, family, friends, and other sources of support). Subsequent research could examine potential heterogeneity in these pathways to identify more precise intervention targets. In addition, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Longitudinal or interventional studies are warranted to clarify the directionality of relationships among variables and to evaluate the effectiveness of individualized social support interventions based on identified mediation pathways. Comparative studies involving other comorbid conditions, such as T2DM with hypertension, may also help determine whether the observed psychosocial mechanisms are disease-specific or more broadly applicable.

Conclusion

Perceived social support partially mediated the relationship between perceived stress and self-management behaviors in patients with T2DM and MASLD, supporting the social buffering model and enriching psychosocial understanding of chronic disease management. These findings highlight the need for a coordinated, multi-level support system—integrating institutional programs, family caregiver training, and community resources—to alleviate perceived stress and strengthen self-management capacity.
