Sodium thiosulfate for treatment of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis with metastatic pulmonary calcification in a patient undergoing peritoneal dialysis: a case report
Authors: Qiqi Yan, Kun Xu, Mingjiu Zhu, Deguang Wang, Guiling Liu, Dandan Li, Jingjing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (NSF) is a rare systemic disease characterized by widespread, progressive fibrosis of the skin and connective tissue. We report a rare case of NSF and metastatic pulmonary calcification coexisting in a patient undergoing peritoneal dialysis (PD). The patient had received two exposures to gadodiamide 11 weeks prior to admission. Subsequently, he developed skin sclerosis in the lower limbs, which gradually spread to the waist and back, accompanied by limited joint movement in the lower extremities, resulting in an inability to walk. After the patient was ​transitioned​​ to hemodialysis, received low-dose methylprednisolone and intravenous sodium thiosulfate, and underwent infrared radiation therapy, his skin sclerosis and limited joint movement ​​improved significantly, indicating that the treatment was effective.

Introduction

Nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (NSF) is a systemic disease characterized by widespread, progressive fibrosis of the skin and connective tissue. Its primary manifestations include skin sclerosis and joint contractures. In severe cases, patients may experience difficulty ambulating and/or joint restriction. When fibrosis involves the heart, lungs, or diaphragm, it may lead to death. Histologically, NSF is marked by proliferation of the following: (a) Densely packed collagen bundles within the dermis; (b) Spindle-shaped cells, likely fibroblasts; and (c) Increased mucin deposits in the dermis. NSF is typically associated with exposure to gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCAs) during contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in patients with kidney failure.

Following the black box warning issued by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2007 regarding the use of GBCAs in patients with kidney failure, the incidence of NSF has significantly decreased. A systematic review including patients with biopsy-confirmed NSF between 2000 and 2019 reported that 525 patients had received GBCAs prior to developing NSF, whereas only 7 patients were reported after 2008 [1].

Case description

A 52-year-old male with end-stage kidney disease caused by chronic glomerulonephritis, who had been treated with peritoneal dialysis (PD) for 7 years, was admitted to the Department of Nephrology at our hospital on November 29, 2024. Eleven weeks prior to admission, the patient underwent a contrast-enhanced cardiac MRI using gadodiamide, a Group Ⅰ GBCA. Due to an unsuccessful scan, gadodiamide was re-administered the following day, which confirmed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Shortly thereafter, the patient developed bilateral lower limb swelling with pain. Following adjustments to the PD prescription, the pain subsided and the edema improved. However, he subsequently developed skin sclerosis in the lower limbs, which gradually spread to the waist and back, accompanied by limited joint movement in the lower extremities, leading to an inability to walk. He had a personal history of poorly controlled hypertension and a family history of the disease. At admission, physical examination revealed skin sclerosis in both lower limbs and the waist, limited joint movement in the lower extremities, and symmetrical pitting edema in both lower limbs (Fig. 1). Laboratory tests showed a serum calcium level of 2.44 mmol/L, a serum phosphorus level of 2.80 mmol/L, and an intact parathyroid hormone level of 735 pg/mL. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed scattered abnormalities suggestive of metastatic pulmonary calcification (Fig. 2). A skin biopsy of the right thigh revealed hyperkeratosis of the epidermis, pigment deposition in basal cells, and fibrous tissue proliferation with glassy degeneration and collagen bundles in the dermis, as well as fat necrosis with calcification in the subepidermal layer. Immunohistochemical analysis showed positive staining for cluster of differentiation (CD) 38, CD34, and CD163 (Fig. 3). Therefore, our final diagnosis was NSF related to gadodiamide exposure. During hospitalization, the patient was transitioned to maintenance hemodialysis (three sessions per week), administered low-dose methylprednisolone (4 mg twice daily for two weeks), and given intravenous sodium thiosulfate (3.84 g, post dialysis), along with infrared radiation therapy (twice daily for 20 min, at a distance of approximately 40 cm from the skin, for 10 days). After slight improvement in lower limb skin sclerosis, the patient was discharged. He continued regular hemodialysis and sodium thiosulfate therapy, ​​with​​ infrared radiation therapy discontinued. The dose of methylprednisolone was reduced to 4 mg once daily and was discontinued after one month. Three months after discharge, the patient’s skin sclerosis and limited joint movement improved significantly, allowing him to stand and walk with assistance, indicating that the treatment was effective. No adverse events were observed during the treatment period.

Discussion

This patient undergoing PD was exposed to gadodiamide twice and subsequently developed progressive skin sclerosis of the lower limbs and limited joint movement. A skin biopsy revealed fibrous tissue proliferation with glassy degeneration and collagen bundles in the dermis. Chest CT showed scattered pulmonary abnormalities. The final diagnosis was NSF and metastatic pulmonary calcification. The pathogenesis of NSF may involve the release of trivalent gadolinium ions from the chelate complex, leading to the deposition of free gadolinium in tissues and triggering a fibrotic response. In patients with kidney failure, the slow clearance of GBCAs increases the likelihood of gadolinium release and deposition. Notably, the incidence of NSF is higher in patients undergoing PD [2], likely due to the lower clearance rate of GBCAs in this population. Gadodiamide is classified as a Group I agent by the American College of Radiology and is associated with the greatest number of reported NSF cases. Other agents in this group include gadopentetate and gadoversetamide. Most contrast-enhanced MRIs are performed with Group II or Group III agents. A pharmacokinetic study in patients receiving kidney replacement therapy found that a single hemodialysis session removed approximately 65% of gadodiamide, whereas 22 days of continuous ambulatory PD achieved only 69% clearance [3]. Additionally, Saitoh et al. reported that 73.8%, 92.4%, and 98.9% of gadodiamide was eliminated after the first, second, and third hemodialysis sessions, respectively, when hemodialysis was performed routinely three times per week for 3 to 4 h per session [4].

To date, various treatments have been attempted for NSF, including kidney transplantation, plasma exchange, phototherapy, imatinib, sirolimus, methotrexate, glucocorticoids, and sodium thiosulfate. Marckmann et al. reported increased dialysate gadolinium concentrations with sodium thiosulfate therapy, suggesting that it may facilitate gadolinium excretion [5]. Moreover, its antioxidant properties may contribute to reduced inflammation, improved endothelial function, and decreased tissue fibrosis [6]. Sodium thiosulfate may also inhibit tissue fibrosis by interfering with transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-beta 1) and mothers against decapentaplegic homolog (Smad) signaling [6, 7]. To assess the effectiveness of sodium thiosulfate for the treatment of NSF, a comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed on July 31, 2025, using the search terms (‘nephrogenic systemic fibrosis’ OR ‘nephrogenic fibrosing dermopathy’) AND ‘sodium thiosulfate’. A total of 10 cases were identified, and their outcomes are summarized in Table 1. Although sodium thiosulfate has shown potential for the treatment of NSF, no standardized dosing regimen has been established, and outcomes have varied. Sodium thiosulfate is also a calcium chelator that promotes calcium excretion by binding to it in tissues. Studies have shown that it can effectively treat metastatic pulmonary calcification [8]. In this case, chest CT revealed scattered pulmonary abnormalities suggestive of metastatic pulmonary calcification, and sodium thiosulfate may treat both NSF and this condition simultaneously.

In this case, the patient also received infrared radiation therapy and steroids as adjunctive treatments for NSF. To date, there is no established standard treatment for NSF, and combination therapies are commonly used. Extracorporeal photopheresis may be effective [9], but it was not available at our hospital. Therefore, infrared radiation therapy was chosen as an alternative, given its potential to improve circulation and promote tissue repair. As this therapy was administered only during hospitalization, we speculate it was not the primary contributor to symptom improvement. The effectiveness of steroids in NSF remains controversial. Yu Jeong Lim reported that combination therapy, including six months of steroid use, halted the systemic progression of NSF [10]. However, R. Kintossou reported that a one-month course of prednisone (1 mg/kg/day) was ineffective [9], and Herwig Pieringer also pointed out that corticosteroid treatment does not appear to benefit NSF [11]. In our case, the patient received a relatively low dose of steroids for a short period. Notably, continued improvement in skin sclerosis and joint mobility was observed even after steroids were discontinued, with only sodium thiosulfate continued. Therefore, we consider sodium thiosulfate to have played the primary role in alleviating NSF symptoms, while infrared radiation therapy and steroids may have served as complementary treatments enhancing its therapeutic effects.

Given the lack of effective treatments for NSF, prevention is of utmost importance. Kidney function should be carefully evaluated before using GBCAs. In patients with severe kidney dysfunction, GBCAs should be avoided whenever possible. If it is clinically in the best interest of the patient, Group II or III agents should be used, as they are associated with fewer reported cases of NSF. Additionally, for patients undergoing PD, due to the low clearance of GBCAs, immediate hemodialysis is recommended [12].

The main strength of this case report lies in the successful use of sodium thiosulfate for the treatment of NSF and metastatic pulmonary calcification in a patient undergoing PD—a clinical scenario that, to the best of our knowledge, has not been previously reported. The favorable outcome provides valuable insight into potential therapeutic strategies for such complex conditions. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single case report, the findings may not be generalizable. Second, the lack of long-term follow-up constrains the ability to fully assess the sustained efficacy of sodium thiosulfate. Finally, the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of sodium thiosulfate remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

In summary, this case demonstrates the successful use of sodium thiosulfate for the treatment of NSF and metastatic pulmonary calcification in a patient undergoing PD. Given the rarity and complexity of this comorbidity, sodium thiosulfate may represent a promising therapeutic option. Further studies are warranted to establish standardized treatment regimens and clarify the underlying mechanisms.
