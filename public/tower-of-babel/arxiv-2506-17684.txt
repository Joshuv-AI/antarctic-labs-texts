But first we will prove a common but somewhat peculiar characteristic of
the Fermat quotient matrix.
We call the first line of
{\mathtt{FQM}}(p)

Fermat quotient point
.
Then the next theorem shows that the average distance of the Fermat quotient point
to a straight line is, in the limit, equal to

Note that in the mod

equally spaced parallel segments, and their number increases as the slope of the line
increases (see Figure

Theorem 1
.

Let
\{C_{p}\}_{p\in\mathcal{P}}
and
\{D_{p}\}_{p\in\mathcal{P}}

be two sequences of integers indexed by prime numbers.
Suppose
C_{p}\neq 0

C_{p}=o(p^{1/12}\log^{-2/3}p)

Then

\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}$}\sum_{b=1}^{p-1}\Big|\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b)}{p}$}-\Big\{C_{p}\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{b}{p}$}+D_{p}\Big\}\Big|=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}$}+O\Big(C_{p}^{3/5}p^{-1/20}\log^{2/5}p\Big)\,,

(2)

where
\big\{C_{p}b/p+D_{p}\big\}
denotes the fractional part of

C_{p}b/p+D_{p}
.

In Theorem
1
, we allowed the slopes of the lines to change as

with the result remaining valid even when the slopes increase
together with the number of the segments that compose the lines.

The common version, with the distance between the Fermat quotient point
and any fixed line, is the object of the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Let
C\neq 0
and

\sum_{b=1}^{p-1}\Big|\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b)}{p}$}-\Big\{C\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{b}{p}$}+D\Big\}\Big|=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{p}{3}$}+O_{C}\Big(p^{19/20}\log^{2/5}p\Big)\,.

{\mathtt{FQM}}(p)

The elements of the Fermat quotient matrix are related to each
other through various basic relations, including some that are indicated in the next proposition (see also
[
14
]
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
A_{a,b}
be the elements of
{\mathtt{FQM}}(p)
, for
0\leq a<p

and
1\leq b<p
. Then, with any indices and entries taken modulo
p
, we have

(i)

A_{a,b}=\mathfrak{q}_{p}(ap+b)
;

(ii)

A_{a,b}=A_{p-1-a,p-b}
;

(iii)

A_{a,b}=\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b)-ab^{-1}
;

(iv)

A_{a+s,b}=A_{a,b}-sb^{-1}
;

(v)

e_{p}\big(A_{a,b_{1}b_{2}}\big)=e_{p}\big(A_{a,b_{1}}\big)e_{p}\big(A_{a,b_{1}}\big)e_{p}\big(a(b_{1}+b_{2}-1)b_{1}^{-1}b_{2}^{-1}\big)
,
where
e_{p}(x):=e^{\frac{2\pi ix}{p}}
.

Proof.

Part (
i
) is listed as the definition and the others are straightforward
calculations using

(ap+b)^{p-1}\equiv b^{p-1}-apb^{p-2}\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p^{2}\right)
.
Then, via the translation of the notation

\mathfrak{q}_{p}(n)=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{n^{p-1}-1}{p}$}\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right)
, this particularly implies

\mathfrak{q}_{p}(ap+b)\equiv\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b)-ab^{-1}\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right)
.
Also, note that
A_{a+s,b}
is equal to

\begin{split}\mathfrak{q}_{p}\big({(a+s)}p+b\big)&=\frac{\big((a+s)p+b\big)^{p-1}-1}{p}\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right)\\
&=\frac{(p-1)(a+s)pb^{p-2}+b^{p-1}-1}{p}\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right),\end{split}

which further leads to

\begin{split}A_{a+s,b}=-(a+s)b^{-1}+\frac{b^{p-1}-1}{p}\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right)=-(a+s)b^{-1}+\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b).\end{split}

The proof of part (
v
) yields as follows. Using part (
iii
), we have:

e_{p}\big(A_{a,b_{1}b_{2}}\big)=e_{p}\big(\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b_{1}b_{2})-ab_{1}^{-1}b_{2}^{-1}\big)=e_{p}\big(\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b_{1})\big)e_{p}\big(\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b_{2})\big)e_{p}\big(-ab_{1}^{-1}b_{2}^{-1}\big),

(9)

because
\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b_{1}b_{2})=\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b_{1})b_{2}^{p-1}+\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b_{2})
, which implies,

\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b_{1}b_{2})\equiv\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b_{1})+\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b_{2})\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right)

for
\gcd(b_{1}b_{2},p)=1
.
Then, the sequence of equalities in (
9
) is further continued by

\begin{split}e_{p}\big(A_{a,b_{1}b_{2}}\big)&=e_{p}\big(\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b_{1})-ab_{1}^{-1}\big)e_{p}\big(\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b_{2})-ab_{2}^{-1}\big)e_{p}\big(ab_{1}^{-1}+ab_{2}^{-1}-ab_{1}^{-1}b_{2}^{-1}\big)\\
&=e_{p}\big(A_{a,b_{1}}\big)e_{p}\big(A_{a,b_{2}}\big)e_{p}\big(a(b_{1}+b_{2}-1)b_{1}^{-1}b_{2}^{-1}\big).\end{split}

∎

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
C,D>0
be real numbers and let
g(x):=\{Cx+D\}
. Then

I(g):=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|y-g(x)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}$}\,.

Proof.

Let us note that the integrand function is piecewise continuous and bounded,
thus Fubini’s theorem can be applied. Then, since
0\leq g(x)<1
, we have

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}|y-g(x)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}&=\int_{0}^{g(x)}(g(x)-y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}+\int_{g(x)}^{1}(y-g(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}\\
&=\big(g(x)\big)^{2}-\left.\frac{y^{2}}{2}\right\rvert_{0}^{g(x)}+\left.\frac{y^{2}}{2}\right\rvert_{g(x)}^{1}-g(x)\big(1-g(x)\big)\,,\end{split}

and, as a result,

I(g)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\big(g(x)\big)^{2}-g(x)+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}.

(10)

Remark 2.1
.

We can simplify the calculation of the integral noticing that the function

x\mapsto\{Cx+D\}
is periodic of period
1/C
. Indeed, we have

\Big\{C\Big(x+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}$}\Big)+D\Big\}=\big\{Cx+1+D\big\}=\{Cx+D\}\,.

Next, one checks that if
x\in[0,1/C)
, then

\{Cx+D\}=\begin{cases}Cx+D&\text{ if $0\leq x\leq\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1-D}{C}$}$,}\\[6.0pt]
Cx+D-1&\text{ if $\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1-D}{C}$}\leq x\leq\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}$}$}.\end{cases}

(11)

Then, on combining (
10
) and (
11
), we have

\begin{split}I\big(g\big)&=C\int_{0}^{1/C}\left(\{Cx+D\}^{2}-\{Cx+D\}+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\\
&=C\int_{0}^{\frac{1-D}{C}}\left((Cx+D)^{2}-(Cx+D)+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\\
&\phantom{(Cx+D)^{2}-(Cx+D)}+C\int_{\frac{1-D}{C}}^{\frac{1}{C}}\left((Cx+D-1)^{2}-(Cx+D-1)+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}.\end{split}

Summing the terms alike in the two integrals, yields

\begin{split}I\big(g\big)&=C\int_{0}^{1/C}\left((Cx+D)^{2}-(Cx+D)+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\\
&\phantom{(Cx+D)^{2}-(Cx+D)}+C\int_{\frac{1-D}{C}}^{\frac{1}{C}}\big(-2(Cx+D)+1+1\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\\
&=\left.C\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{(Cx+D)^{3}}{3C}$}-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{(Cx+D)^{2}}{2C}$}+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{x}{2}$}\right)\right\rvert_{0}^{1/C}+\left.C\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{-2(Cx+D)^{2}}{2C}$}+2x\right)\right\rvert_{(1-D)/C}^{1/C}.\end{split}

Next, a straightforward calculation leads to the conclusion that

I\big(g\big)=1/3
, which concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

For any integer

\big|\Discrepancy(\mathcal{S})\big|\leq\frac{|\mathcal{S}|}{K}+3\sum_{m=1}^{K}\frac{1}{m}\bigg|\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}e(ms)\bigg|\,,

The next lemma gives the volume of the hyper-polyhedron.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\sigma
be a permutation of size
N\geq 1

and let
T(\sigma,N)\subset[0,1]^{N}
be the polyhedron defined by

T(\sigma,N):=\big\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in[0,1]^{N}:x_{\sigma(1)}<x_{\sigma(2)}<\cdots<x_{\sigma(N)}\big\}.

Then the Lebesgue measure of
T(\sigma,N)
is

\mu\big(T(\sigma,N)\big)=\frac{1}{N!}.

Proof.

The result follows by noting the following recursion when we reduce the dimension:

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{z_{N}}\cdots\int_{0}^{z_{3}}\int_{0}^{z_{2}}1\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{2}\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{N}&=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{z_{N}}\cdots\int_{0}^{z_{4}}\int_{0}^{z_{3}}z_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{3}\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{N}\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{z_{N}}\cdots\int_{0}^{z_{5}}\int_{0}^{z_{4}}\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{z^{2}_{3}}{2}$}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{3}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{4}\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{N}\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{z_{N}}\cdots\int_{0}^{z_{6}}\int_{0}^{z_{5}}\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{z^{3}_{4}}{2\cdot 3}$}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{5}\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{N}\,.\end{split}

Differently, the same result is a consequence of the observation
that
\mu\big(T(\sigma,N)\big)
is the same for any
\sigma
.
∎

We will also need a basic tool analogous to the
Erdős-Turán inequality, which is useful for classifying sequences in
multi-dimensional spaces as uniformly distributed.
For this, consider the sequence
\bm{x}=\{\bm{x}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset[0,1)^{N}
and denote
by
\mathscr{B}
a generic box in the unit cube:

\mathscr{B}=\prod_{1\leq j\leq N}[a_{j},b_{j})\subset[0,1)^{N}.

Then the
extreme discrepancy
of
\bm{x}
with respect to
\mathscr{B}
is defined by

\DiscrepancyN(\bm{x},R):=\sup_{\mathscr{B}\subset[0,1)^{N}}\bigg|\mu(\mathscr{B})-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{R}$}\#\big\{n\leq R:\bm{v}_{n}\in\mathscr{B}\big\}\bigg|\,,

where
\mu
denotes the Lebesgue measure.

The next lemma gives the Koksma–Szüsz inequality
[
20
,
40
]
,
which bounds the extreme discrepancy in terms of the level of cancellation in the
exponential sums associated to the sequence in question.
It shows that if the sequence
\bm{x}=\{\bm{x}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset[0,1)^{N}

is uniformly distributed at random in
[0,1)^{N}
and independently for any
n\geq 1
,
then
\mu(\mathscr{B})
approximates effectively the proportion
of the elements
\bm{x}_{n}
that belong to
\mathscr{B}
.

Lemma 2.5

(Koksma–Szüsz)
.

For any integer
H>1
and any sequence

\bm{x}=\{\bm{x}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset[0,1)^{N}
, we have

\DiscrepancyN(\bm{x},R)\leq C_{N}\left(\frac{2}{H+1}+\sum_{0<\|\bm{h}\|_{{\infty}\leq H}}\frac{1}{r(\bm{h})}\left|{\frac{1}{R}}\sum_{j=1}^{R}e\Big(2\pi i\langle\bm{h},\bm{x}_{j}\rangle\Big)\right|\right),

(14)

where

r(\bm{h})=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}\max\{1,|h_{i}|\}

for

\bm{h}=(h_{1},\dots,h_{N})\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle

denotes the inner product, and
C_{N}
denotes an absolute constant depending on the dimension
N
.

The following lemma estimates the exponential sum in (
14
)
for the sequence of elements in the Fermat quotient matrix translated by the
displacements
\mathcal{V}=\{\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{N}\}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
p
be prime,
H,M,N\geq 1
, and let

\bm{v}_{j}=(s_{j},t_{j})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
for
1\leq j\leq N
be fixed and chosen such that

\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq N}\|\bm{v}_{j}\|_{\infty}\leq M
.
Assume that one of the integers
t_{j}
is different from all the others,
and denote by
J
its index.
Let

\mathcal{G}_{N}(p):=\bigcap_{j=1}^{N}\Big\{(a,b)\in\Big([0,p)\times[1,p)\Big)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}:0\leq a+s_{j}<p,\ 1\leq b+t_{j}<p\Big\}\,,

and let
\bm{x}_{a,b}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
, where

x_{j}:=\mathfrak{q}_{p}\big((a+s_{j})p+(b+t_{j})\big)/p
for
1\leq j\leq N

and
(a,b)\in\mathcal{G}_{N}(p)
.
Then, for any
\bm{h}=(h_{1},\dots,h_{N})\in\big([-H,H]\cap\mathbb{Z}\big)^{N}
,
for which
h_{J}\neq 0
, we have

S(p):=\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal{G}_{N}(p)}e(\langle\bm{h},\bm{x}_{a,b}\rangle)=O_{M,N}(p)\,.

(15)

Proof.

First, let us complete the sum
S(p)
to a sum over the entire torus
[0,p)^{2}
.
Let

\begin{split}S_{0}(p):=&\sum_{(a,b)\in[0,p)\times[1,p)}e(\langle\bm{h},\bm{x}_{a,b}\rangle),\end{split}

where
\bm{x}_{a,b}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
for
(a,b)\not\in\mathcal{G}_{N}(p)

are defined by the same formula in the lemma
except that the arguments are replaced by
the representatives of
a+s_{j}
and
b+t_{j}
modulo
p

taken from
\{0,\dots,p-1\}
.
Then, if
p
is sufficiently large, the sums
S(p)
and
S_{0}(p)

coincide except for at most
4Mp
new terms added in
S_{0}(p)
, that is,

\begin{split}S(p)=S_{0}(p)+O_{M}(p)\,.\end{split}

(16)

The complete sum is

\begin{split}S_{0}(p)&=\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\sum_{b=1}^{p-1}e({h_{1}x_{1}+\cdots h_{N}x_{N}})\\
&=\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\sum_{b=1}^{p-1}e\bigg(\frac{h_{1}\mathfrak{q}_{p}\big((a+s_{1})p+(b+t_{1})\big)+\cdots+h_{N}\mathfrak{q}_{p}\big((a+s_{N})p+(b+t_{N})\big)}{p}\bigg)\,.\end{split}

By Proposition
2.1
, we know that for
1\leq j\leq N
we have

\begin{split}\mathfrak{q}_{p}\big((a+s_{j})p+(b+t_{j})\big)=\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b+t_{j})-(a+s_{j})(b+t_{j})^{-1}.\end{split}

Then,
S_{0}(p)
can be written as

\begin{split}S_{0}(p)&=\sum_{b=1}^{p-1}e\bigg(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{h_{1}\big(\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b+t_{1})-s_{1}(b+t_{1})^{-1}\big)+\cdots+h_{N}\big(\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b+t_{N})-s_{N}(b+t_{N})^{-1}\big)}{p}$}\bigg)\\
&\phantom{\sum_{b=1}^{p-1}\quad}\times\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}e\bigg(\frac{aR(b;\bm{h},\mathcal{V})}{p}\bigg)\,,\end{split}

(17)

where
X\mapsto R(X;\bm{h},\mathcal{V})
is a rational function
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}(X)
.
More explicitly,

R(X;\bm{h},\mathcal{V})=\frac{P(X,\bm{h},\mathcal{V})}{Q(\bm{h},b,\mathcal{V})},

where
P(X;\bm{h},\mathcal{V})
and
Q(X;\bm{h},\mathcal{V})
are polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
of degrees

0\leq\deg(P)<\deg(Q)\quad\text{ and }\quad 1\leq\deg(Q)\leq N.

Note that the roots of
Q(X;\bm{h},\mathcal{V})
belong to the set

\{-t_{1},\dots,-t_{N}\}
, but not all elements of the set are always roots,
as certain terms may cancel each other through addition.
Nevertheless, the requirement in the hypothesis, which
implies that one of the roots is distinct from all the others,
ensures that
\deg(Q)\geq 1
, so that, in particular,

R(X;\bm{h},\mathcal{V})
is not identically zero.

Then, for any
b
with
1\leq b\leq p-1
, there are two possibilities:
either
b
is a root of
P(X;\bm{h},\mathcal{V})
or
b
is not a root of
P(X;\bm{h},\mathcal{V})
.
In the first case, in the sum over
a
in (
17
)
all terms are equal to
1
, while in the second case,
the terms run over the set of all roots of unity of order
p
. Therefore

\begin{split}\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}e\bigg(\frac{aR(b;\bm{h},\mathcal{V})}{p}\bigg)=\begin{cases}p&\text{ if $R(b;\bm{h},\mathcal{V})\equiv 0\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right)$,}\\[5.69054pt]
0&\text{ if $R(b;\bm{h},\mathcal{V})\not\equiv 0\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right)$}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(18)

Then, since
P(X;\bm{h},\mathcal{V})
has at most
N
roots modulo
p
,
on inserting (
18
) in (
17
),
yields

\begin{split}\big|S_{0}(p)\big|\leq Np\,.\end{split}

(19)

The lemma then follows on combining (
16
) and (
19
).
∎

The next lemma can be deduced from the general literature
(see Niederreiter
[
30
]
or Dick and Pillichshammer
[
12
]
),
but for completeness we will include it along with a brief justification.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
H,N\geq 1
be integers,
let
\bm{h}=(h_{1},\dots,h_{N})\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
,
and let
r(\bm{h})
be the ‘appended volume’ volume of the associated hypercube defined by

r(\bm{h}):=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\max\{1,|h_{j}|\}
.
Then

\sum_{0<\|\bm{h}\|_{\infty}\leq H}\frac{1}{r(\bm{h})}=O_{N}\big(\log^{N}H\big).

(20)

Proof.

If we assume exactly
\ell
entries of
\bm{h}
are nonzero, then the corresponding sum is bounded by

\ll\binom{N}{\ell}\sum_{1\leq|h_{1}|\leq H}\cdots\sum_{1\leq|h_{\ell}|\leq H}\frac{1}{|h_{1}|\cdots|h_{\ell}|}\ll\binom{N}{\ell}(\log H)^{N}.

Hence, as
\ell
ranges over
1,\ldots,N
, we have

\sum_{0<\|\bm{h}\|_{\infty}\leq H}\frac{1}{r(\bm{h})}\ll\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\binom{N}{\ell}(\log H)^{N}<2^{N}\log^{N}H.

∎

3.
Average distances from the the Fermat Quotients to a line

Let
p
be a large prime number. Let
L
be a positive integer less than
p
whose value will be chosen later. Define

\mathcal{T}_{jk}=\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{j-1}{L}$},\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{j}{L}$}\right]\times\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{k-1}{L}$},\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{k}{L}$}\right],\quad 1\leq j\leq L,~2\leq k\leq L,

while, for
k=1
, we keep the left end of the second interval closed, that is,

\mathcal{T}_{j1}=\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{j-1}{L}$},\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{j}{L}$}\right]\times\left[0,\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}$}\right],\quad 1\leq j\leq L.

These rectangles are disjoint and form the set partition

(0,1]\times[0,1]=\bigcup_{j=1}^{L}\bigcup_{k=1}^{L}\mathcal{T}_{jk}\,.

Next, consider the set of normalized points based on the
Fermat quotient matrix:

\mathcal{B}_{jk}:=\Big\{1\leq b\leq p-1:\Big(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{b}{p}$},\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b)}{p}$}\Big)\in\mathcal{T}_{jk}\Big\}\ \ \text{ for $1\leq j,k\leq L$}.

Note that, since
b/p\neq 0
, each
b\in\{1,\ldots,p-1\}
lies in exactly one of the sets
\mathcal{B}_{jk}
.

Now, let
C,D>0
be fixed real numbers and
let

f(x,y):=\big|y-\{Cx+D\}\big|\,.

In order to prove Theorem
1
, we need to estimate the average

M(p,C,D):=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{b=1}^{p-1}f\left(\frac{b}{p},\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b)}{p}\right).

(21)

With the notations of the partition above, this is

M(p,C,D)=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{jk}}f\left(\frac{b}{p},\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b)}{p}\right).

(22)

Note that for each
b\in\mathcal{B}_{jk}
, we have

f\left(\frac{b}{p},\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b)}{p}\right)=f\left(\frac{j}{L},\frac{k}{L}\right)+O\left(\frac{C}{L}\right).

(23)

Next we estimate the size of the sets
\mathcal{B}_{jk}
.
Note that
b\in\mathcal{B}_{jk}
if and only if both of
the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{j-1}{L}$}p<b\leq\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{j}{L}$}p
,

(ii)

\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{k-1}{L}$}p<\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b)}{p}$}\leq\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{k}{L}$}p

for
1\leq k\leq L
.

The size of
\mathcal{B}_{jk}
will follow if we know the discrepancy of the sequence

\mathcal{S}_{p}(j)=\left\{\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b)}{p}:\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{j-1}{L}$}p<b\leq\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{j}{L}$}p\right\}\subset[0,1]\ \ \text{ for $1\leq j\leq L$.}

According to Erdős-Turán inequality
[
28
, Corollary 1.1]
,
the discrepancy is bounded by

D\big(\mathcal{S}_{p}(j)\big)\leq\frac{p/L}{K+1}+3\sum_{m=1}^{K}\frac{1}{m}\bigg|\sum_{\frac{j-1}{L}p<b\leq\frac{j}{L}p}e\left(\frac{m\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b)}{p}\right)\bigg|.

The exponential sum above is bounded by Heath-Brown’s Lemma
2.3
:

\sum_{\frac{j-1}{L}p<b\leq\frac{j}{L}p}e\left(\frac{m\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b)}{p}\right)\ll\left(\frac{p}{L}\right)^{1/2}p^{3/8}=L^{-1/2}p^{7/8},

for all integers
m
relatively prime to
p
.
Therefore, it follows that

\begin{split}D\big(\mathcal{S}_{p}(j)\big)&\ll\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{p}{L(K+1)}$}+3\sum_{m=1}^{K}\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}$}L^{-1/2}p^{7/8}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\left(\frac{p}{K+1}+3p^{7/8}\log K\right),\end{split}

for any integer
K\geq 1
. To balance the terms,
we choose
K=\lfloor p^{1/8}\rfloor
, and obtain

\begin{split}D\big(\mathcal{S}_{p}(j)\big)&\ll L^{-1/2}p^{7/8}\log p\,.\end{split}

Thus, by the definition of the discrepancy,

|\mathcal{B}_{jk}|=\frac{p}{L}\cdot\frac{1}{L}+O\left(L^{-1/2}p^{7/8}\log p\right),

(24)

for
1\leq j,k\leq L
.

Now we can estimate the average defined by (
21
).
For this, we use the decomposition in rectangles (
22
),
where the values of
f
can be approximated by those on the
corners (
23
):

\begin{split}M(p,C,D)&=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{jk}}f\left(\frac{b}{p},\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{p}(b)}{p}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}|\mathcal{B}_{jk}|\bigg(f\Big(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{j}{L}$},\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{k}{L}$}\Big)+O\Big(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{C}{L}$}\Big)\bigg)\,.\end{split}

On inserting the size of
|\mathcal{B}_{jk}|
from (
24
), yields

\begin{split}M(p,C,D)=&\frac{1}{p}\left(\frac{p}{L^{2}}+O\left(L^{-1/2}p^{7/8}\log p\right)\right)\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\left(f\left(\frac{j}{L},\frac{k}{L}\right)+O\left(\frac{C}{L}\right)\right)\\
=&\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}f\left(\frac{j}{L},\frac{k}{L}\right)+O\bigg(\frac{\log p}{L^{1/2}p^{1/8}}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}f\left(\frac{j}{L},\frac{k}{L}\right)\bigg)\\
&+O\bigg(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{C}{L^{3}}$}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}1\bigg)+O\left(CL^{1/2}p^{-1/8}\log p\right).\end{split}

(25)

Next, to continue, it should be noted that, by Lemma
2.1
, the first term
on the right-hand side of (
25
),
which is a Riemann sum, tends to
1/3
if
L\to\infty
,
because
C\neq 0
.
Furthermore, counting on the maximal possible offset makeing use of (
23
), we find that

\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}f\left(\frac{j}{L},\frac{k}{L}\right)=\frac{1}{3}+O\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{C}{L}$}\right).

(26)

Using (
26
) again, it follows that the sum of the three
error terms in (
25
) is

O\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{C}{L}$}+(L+C)L^{1/2}p^{-1/8}\log p\right)=O\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{C}{L}$}+L^{3/2}p^{-1/8}\log p\right),

(27)

because we need to choose
L
such that
C=o(L)
,
in order to have a proper estimate with the error term that is not
larger than the main one, which is equal to
1/3
, as we have
learned from (
26
).

Now we choose
L=C^{2/5}p^{1/20}\log^{-2/5}p
,
so that the terms in (
27
) have equal contributions.
Then, under the assumption that
C=o\big(p^{1/12}\log^{-2/3}p\big)
,
from (
25
),(
26
), and (
27
)
it follows

M(p,C,D)=\frac{1}{3}+O\big(C^{3/5}p^{-1/20}\log^{2/5}p\big)\,,

with an absolute constant in the error term.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
1
.

4.
Distribution of Fermat quotients in geometric configuration respecting relative size conditions. Proof of Theorem
2

The proof of Theorem
2
goes through the following steps.
First, we decompose the unit cube that contain the
N
-dimensional
vectors
\bm{x}_{a,b}
into boxes of a generic size and
then we estimate the number of vectors
\bm{x}_{a,b}
in each box.
Next, we count the number of boxes that contain “good” vectors,
meaning those that follow the increasing order given by
\sigma
.
Finally, we add up all the numbers of good vectors and choose the
optimal size value of the boxes for which the error term is smaller.

4.1.
Breaking the unit cube into pieces

Let
L>1
, whose precise value will be chosen later, be fixed.
We divide the unit cube into
L^{N}
boxes:

\mathscr{B}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}=\Big[\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{i_{1}}{L}$},\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{i_{1}+1}{L}$}\Big)\times\cdots\times\Big[\frac{i_{N}}{L},\frac{i_{N}+1}{L}\Big),\quad 0\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}\leq L-1.

Let
\mathcal{V}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{2})^{N}
be the ordered set of vectors

\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{N}
that defines the pattern
of displacement vectors and let
\mathcal{G}_{N}(p)
be the set of admissible positions in
the Fermat quotient matrix
{\mathtt{FQM}}(p)
defined by (
3
), positions that
still remain in the matrix after translations by any
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}
.

To evaluate the spread of vectors

\bm{x}_{a,b}=\frac{1}{p}(A_{(a,b)+\bm{v}_{1}},\dots,A_{(a,b)+\bm{v}_{N}})
with

A_{a,b}=\mathfrak{q}_{p}(ap+b)
and

(a,b)\in\mathcal{G}_{N}(p)
, we will use the fact that their set is uniformly distributed in
[0,1]^{N}
.
In order to verify this fact, let us estimate the discrepancy of

\mathcal{X}=\{\bm{x}_{a,b}:(a,b)\in\mathcal{G}_{N}(p)\}.

The Koksma–Szüsz inequality (
2.5
) imply that for any
integer
H>1
, we have

\frac{\#\big(\mathcal{X}\cap\mathscr{B}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}\big)}{|\mathcal{X}|}=\mu(\mathscr{B}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}})+O_{N}\bigg(\frac{1}{H}+\sum_{0<\|\bm{h}\|_{{\infty}\leq H}}\frac{1}{r(\bm{h})}\bigg|{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}}\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal{G}_{N}(p)}e\Big(2\pi i\langle\bm{h},\bm{x}_{j}\rangle\Big)\bigg|\bigg),

On using (
4
) and the bound (
15
) for the exponential sum, it follows

\#\big(\mathcal{X}\cap\mathscr{B}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}\big)=\frac{p^{2}}{L^{N}}+O_{M,N}\bigg(\frac{p^{2}}{H}+\frac{p}{L^{N}}+p\sum_{0<\|\bm{h}\|_{{\infty}\leq H}}\frac{1}{r(\bm{h})}\bigg)\,.

It should be noted that we were able to apply (
15
)
because the condition
0<\|\bm{h}\|_{{\infty}}
in the summation
ensures that there exists
j
for which
h_{j}\neq 0
,
call
J
this index, and from the hypothesis of the theorem we know that

t_{J}
is different from each other
t_{j}
with
1\leq j\leq N
and
j\neq J
.

Bounding the sum over
\bm{h}
by (
20
) and taking
H=p
, we conclude

\#\big(\mathcal{X}\cap\mathscr{B}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}\big)=\frac{p^{2}}{L^{N}}+O_{M,N}\big(p\log^{N}p\big)\,.

(28)

4.2.
Counting on the boxes that contain ordered vectors

Let
\sigma
be a permutation of
N
elements and let

T(\sigma,N)
be the polyhedron

T(\sigma,N)=\big\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in[0,1]^{N}:x_{\sigma(1)}<x_{\sigma(2)}<\cdots<x_{\sigma(N)}\big\},

which we know from Lemma
2.4
that has volume

\mu\big(T(\sigma,N)\big)=1/N!
.

Note that some of these boxes are completely included in
T(\sigma,N)
,
others are only partially, intersecting the boundary of
T(\sigma,N)
,
while others are outside of
T(\sigma,N)
.
Precisely, if

i_{\sigma(1)}<\cdots<i_{\sigma(N)}
, then

\mathscr{B}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}\subseteq T(\sigma,N)
. If any of the inequalities becomes an equality, then points in
\mathscr{B}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}
may or may not lie in
T(\sigma,N)
. If any of the inequalities gets reversed, then points in
\mathscr{B}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}

do not lie in
T(\sigma,N)
.

We need to know the number of boxes included in
T(\sigma,N)
,
respectively the ones that have a non-empty intersection with
T(\sigma,N)
.

Remark 4.1
.

1.
Let
N,L
be integers such that
1\leq N\leq L
. Then

B^{*}(N,L):=\#\big\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{N})\in\{1,\dots,L\}^{N}:i_{1}<\cdots<i_{N}\big\}=\binom{L}{N}.

(29)

This follows by an induction argument by counting the numbers of tuples with
i_{N}
on all possible positions and observing that

\binom{L-1}{N-1}+\binom{L-2}{N-1}+\cdots+\binom{N-1}{N-1}=\binom{L}{N},

equality known as the “hockey stick formula”.

2.
Let
N,L
be integers such that
1\leq N\leq L
. Then

B(N,L):=\#\big\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{N})\in\{1,\dots,L\}^{N}:i_{1}\leq\cdots\leq i_{N}\big\}=\binom{L+N-1}{N}.

(30)

The exact equality above follows by induction
in the same way as (
29
),
the only difference in the counting being the allowance for
i_{N}

to be equal to
i_{N-1}
.

Then, (
29
) and (
30
) imply that the number of
boxes
\mathscr{B}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}
included in
T(\sigma,N)
and the number of boxes
that intersect
T(\sigma,N)
are approximately equal as
L
becomes sufficiently large:

B^{*}(N,L)=\frac{L^{N}}{N!}+O_{N}\big(L^{N-1}\big)\ \ \text{ and }\ \ B(N,L)=\frac{L^{N}}{N!}+O_{N}\big(L^{N-1}\big).

(31)

4.3.
Completion of the proof of Theorem
2

Knowing, on the one hand, the cardinality of ‘good’ vectors

\bm{x}_{a,b}
in a box
\mathscr{B}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}
,
which is given by (
28
), and, on the other hand, the number of boxes
that intersect the polyhedron
T(\sigma,N)
, we find that

\begin{split}\#\left\{(a,b)\in\mathcal{G}:\bm{x}_{a,b}\in T(\sigma,N)\right\}&=\#\big(\mathcal{X}\cap T(\sigma,N)\big)\\
&=\left(\frac{L^{N}}{N!}+O_{N}\big(L^{N-1}\big)\right)\left(\frac{p^{2}}{L^{N}}+O_{M,N}\big(p\log^{N}p\big)\right)\\
&=\frac{p^{2}}{N!}+O_{M,N}\left(pL^{N}\log^{N}p+\frac{p^{2}}{L}\right).\end{split}

To balance the error terms, we choose

L=\lfloor p^{1/(N+1)}\log^{-N/(N+1)p}\rfloor
and obtain

\#\left\{(a,b)\in\mathcal{G}:\bm{x}_{a,b}\in T(\sigma,N)\right\}=\frac{p^{2}}{N!}+O_{M,N}\left(p^{(2N+1)/(N+1)}\log^{N/(N+1)}p\right).

This concludes the proof of Theorem
2
.

∎
