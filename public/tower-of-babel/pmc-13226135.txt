Direct puncture and NBCA embolization for refractory lymphatic leakage after inguinal lymphadenectomy in the absence of puncturable lymph nodes guided by lymphoscintigraphy
Authors: Hirokazu Sawamura, Hiroyuki Kimura, Rio Kou, Akari Nishida, Kango Kawase, Naotoshi Hatsuda, Toshiyuki Sato, Kotaro Shimada, Kenta Fujiwara, Mitsunori Kanagaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lymphatic leakage is a recognized complication of lymphadenectomy. Intranodal lymphangiography is commonly used to identify lymphatic leakage; however, the procedure may not be feasible in cases where no puncturable lymph nodes remain following inguinal lymphadenectomy. We report a case of refractory lymphatic leakage occurring after inguinal lymphadenectomy that was managed by direct puncture and N-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA) embolization of a lymphocele and lymphopseudoaneurysm with lymphoscintigraphy employed for localization.A 70-year-old woman experienced persistent lymphatic leakage following left inguinal and external iliac lymphadenectomy for melanoma. Lymphoscintigraphy with single photon emission computed tomography/computed tomography revealed 2 disrupted lymphatic pathways along the great saphenous and femoral veins. Intense tracer accumulation at the inguinal stump along the great saphenous pathway indicated fluid collection, whereas tracer presence along the femoral pathway extended near the drainage catheter without focal pooling suggested communication with the drain tract. Due to the absence of puncturable lymph nodes, intranodal lymphangiography was not feasible. We punctured and embolized the fluid collection along the great saphenous pathway using an NBCA-ethiodized oil mixture. Despite this intervention, lymphatic leakage persisted; therefore, we accessed another small fluid collection along the femoral vein. Ethiodized oil injection demonstrated continuity with the drainage catheter which was consistent with a lymphopseudoaneurysm. Following embolization, the external lymphatic leakage resolved within several days.This case illustrated that lymphoscintigraphy can effectively identify major sites of leakage and guide intervention; however, intense tracer accumulation does not necessarily indicate the sole source of leakage when multiple lymphatic pathways are disrupted.

Introduction

Lymphatic leakage is a recognized complication of lymphadenectomy. Traditionally, pedal lymphangiography was used to directly opacify lymphatic vessels. Since the introduction of ultrasound-guided intranodal lymphangiography in 2011 [1], intranodal lymphangiography has largely supplanted pedal lymphangiography in clinical practice due to its technical simplicity and lower invasiveness. However, in patients experiencing lymphatic leakage following inguinal lymphadenectomy, the absence of puncturable inguinal lymph nodes can render intranodal lymphangiography technically unfeasible.

We report a case of postoperative lymphatic leakage after inguinal lymphadenectomy, wherein lymphoscintigraphy was used to localize the suspected site of leakage. A lymphopseudoaneurysm—defined as a small extravasated lymphatic fluid collection contained by the surrounding tissue before draining into larger spaces [2]—was directly punctured and embolized with N-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA), resulting in the resolution of refractory leakage.

Case report

A 70-year-old woman had undergone surgical resection and sentinel lymph node biopsy for malignant melanoma of the left thigh 1 year prior and was diagnosed with pT4aN0M0 stage IIB disease. Subsequently, she was administered adjuvant pembrolizumab therapy. A follow-up 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG-PET/CT) indicated increased uptake in the left inguinal and external iliac lymph nodes, raising suspicion for nodal metastases. She then underwent left inguinal and external iliac lymphadenectomy. Persistent lymphatic drainage from the left inguinal drain was still observed 17 days postoperatively (100-200 mL/day). Lymphoscintigraphy and single photon emission computed tomography/computed tomography (SPECT/CT) using 99mTc–human serum albumin diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid (99mTc-HSA-D) demonstrated 2 lymphatic pathways: one along the left great saphenous vein and another along the left femoral vein (Figs. 1A and B). Radiotracer uptake along the left femoral vein pathway extended to the area near the drainage catheter but did not form a focal hotspot (Fig. 1B). The absence of hotspot formation and lack of pelvic continuity suggested potential communication with an alternative extrapelvic route. The presence of radiotracer within the catheter suggested that lymphatic flow from the left femoral vein pathway drained into the catheter and subsequently into the drainage bag.

An ultrasound performed on postoperative day 20 corroborated the SPECT/CT findings revealing fluid collection adjacent to the tip of the drainage catheter. No puncturable lymph nodes or dilated lymphatic vessels were visualized; therefore, intranodal lymphangiography was not feasible. Under local anesthesia, the fluid collection was punctured with a 23-gauge needle under ultrasound guidance. A total of 2.3 mL of NBCA (Histoacryl; B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany) mixed with ethiodized oil (Lipiodol; Guerbet, Tokyo, Japan) in a 1:2 ratio was injected for embolization (Fig. 2). Due to the lack of reduction in drainage output, a second embolization was performed on postoperative day 22. Ultrasound revealed another fluid collection near the drainage tube corresponding to the interruption site of the left femoral lymphatic pathway identified on lymphoscintigraphy. The fluid collection along the left femoral vein was accessed with a 23-gauge needle, and ethiodized oil injection demonstrated communication with the peridrain space. Subsequently, NBCA mixed with ethiodized oil (1:2) was injected (Fig. 3). The embolic agent extended along the drain tract, with a total of 2.4 mL administered. This procedure confirmed continuity of lymphatic flow from the left femoral vein pathway to the drainage catheter, which is consistent with the scintigraphic findings. Drainage output exhibited considerable daily fluctuations before and after embolization. Collection intervals were not strictly standardized at exactly 24 hours because drainage was measured as part of routine clinical practice. Although an immediate reduction in volume was not observed, cutaneous leakage resolved within 8 days following the second embolization. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 30.

Follow-up CT performed 28 days after the second embolization confirmed persistent ethiodized oil retention within the treated fluid collections but revealed a new lymphocele at a different site in the left thigh (Fig. 4). Due to the absence of associated symptoms, conservative management was selected. Discussion

Postoperative lymphatic leakage may manifest as lymphocele, chylous ascites, or chylothorax. Lymphangiography using ethiodized oil alone has been reported to resolve leakage in 46%-89% of cases [2]. This efficacy is attributed to inflammatory and granulomatous reactions induced by ethiodized oil at the leakage site [3]. The average interval from lymphangiography to resolution of lymphatic leakage is approximately 5 days [2]. In the present case, cutaneous lymphatic leakage resolved 8 days after embolization, which is generally consistent with prior reports.

Various interventional approaches for persistent lymphatic leakage including direct puncture and NBCA embolization of a lymphopseudoaneurysm, embolization of afferent lymphatic vessels, transnodal embolization via upstream lymph nodes, balloon-occluded retrograde abdominal lymphatic embolization, and drain-based ethanol sclerotherapy, have been documented [2,[4], [5], [6]]. In our case, intranodal lymphangiography was not feasible due to the absence of puncturable inguinal lymph nodes. It has also been reported that lymphatic leakage following inguinal lymphadenectomy can be managed through surgical exposure of a lymphatic vessel via a lower-leg skin incision, followed by the insertion of a 29-gauge catheter into the vessel under microscopic guidance for embolization [7]. However, this technique necessitates surgical exposure and microsurgical expertise. Pedal lymphangiography is considered technically challenging and more invasive. Therefore, lymphoscintigraphy with SPECT/CT was used to localize the suspected leakage site.

Although the first treated fluid collection associated with the left great saphenous pathway was morphologically consistent with a lymphocele on CT, it can also be interpreted to be a lymphopseudoaneurysm because it presented as focal lymphatic extravasation from the disrupted lower-extremity lymphatic pathway on lymphoscintigraphy. In practice, lymphoceles and lymphopseudoaneurysms likely present as different manifestations along a spectrum of lymphatic leakage rather than entirely distinct entities because lymphopseudoaneurysms may gradually enlarge and develop to form lymphoceles. Fluid collection associated with the left great saphenous pathway exhibited intense tracer accumulation and was initially believed to be the primary source of lesion formation. However, embolization of this lesion alone proved insufficient, as it behaved as a contained lymphocele without direct communication with the drain tract. During the first procedure, no afferent lymphatic vessels were visualized, and reflux toward the upstream lymphatics did not occur during NBCA injection into the lymphocele. This observation may be attributed to the physiological characteristics of the lymphatic flow, which directs lymph cranially, with intraluminal valves in lymphatic vessels preventing retrograde propagation of the embolic material.

During the second procedure, continuity between the leakage along the left femoral pathway and the drain tract was established. The punctured site during the second embolization exhibited direct communication with the drain cavity, functioning as a larger extraluminal space. This configuration corresponds with previously reported definitions of a lymphopseudoaneurysm rather than a simple lymphocele. Continuous drainage likely prevented focal tracer pooling at the lymphopseudoaneurysm.

Interestingly, a new lymphocele developed after second embolization but resolved spontaneously. This observation suggests that complete occlusion of all leakage sites may not always be necessary. Rather, reducing leakage below the absorptive capacity of the surrounding tissues may facilitate spontaneous healing and permit drain removal [2].

Lymphoscintigraphy serves as a valuable tool for identifying regions of significant leakage and assisting in procedural planning. However, given that multiple lymphatic pathways may be disrupted after lymphadenectomy, potentially leading to multiple leakage sites, imaging findings should be interpreted judiciously. Intense tracer accumulation does not necessarily indicate a singular source of leakage.

Conclusion

In this case of lymphatic leakage following inguinal lymphadenectomy, direct puncture and embolization of the lymphocele and lymphopseudoaneurysm with an NBCA-ethiodized oil mixture effectively managed refractory leakage in the absence of puncturable lymph nodes. Lymphoscintigraphy with SPECT/CT provided important information for treatment planning; however, intense tracer accumulation did not necessarily represent the sole leakage site when multiple lymphatic pathways are disrupted.
