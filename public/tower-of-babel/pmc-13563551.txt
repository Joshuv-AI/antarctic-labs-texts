Primary care physician documentation in oncology electronic health records: prognostic marker or immortal-time bias?
Authors: PE Heudel, J-Y Blay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStructured administrative fields in oncology electronic health records (EHRs) are used in studies but their interpretation may depend on when they are completed. We assessed whether dated primary care physician (PCP) documentation was associated with survival after accounting for documentation timing.Materials and methodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using routinely collected oncology EHR data from a single French comprehensive cancer centre (ConSoRe platform), including patients treated for cancer between 2015 and 2017. Survival was analysed from cancer care initiation to death from any cause or administrative end of follow-up; no patients were censored before 12 months. Conventional analyses compared patients with or without an exploitable dated PCP declaration using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) targeting the average treatment effect based on age, sex, metastatic status, and tumour location. Temporally aligned analyses included baseline-available exposure definitions, a time-dependent Cox model, and a 6-month landmark analysis.ResultsAmong the 15 216 patients, 10 418 had a dated PCP declaration (68.5%), 4757 had a declared PCP but no exploitable date (31.2%), and 41 had no PCP declared (0.3%). During the median follow-up of 102.1 months, 7175 deaths occurred. In conventional IPTW analysis, dated PCP documentation was associated with improved survival [hazard ratio (HR) 0.86, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.82-0.90, P < 0.001]. Patients with declarations recorded after 6 months had the most favourable survival, consistent with immortal-time bias. In the time-dependent Cox model, dated documentation was not protective (HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.10-1.21, P < 0.001). In the 6-month landmark analysis, unadjusted postlandmark survival did not differ significantly (log-rank P = 0.140), whereas the adjusted landmark Cox model showed a small association in the opposite direction to the conventional analysis (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.89-0.99, P = 0.011).ConclusionsThe apparent survival benefit associated with dated PCP documentation was explained by documentation timing rather than a robust prognostic effect. Time-dependent EHR fields require temporally aligned analyses in real-world oncology research.Key words: electronic health records, immortal-time bias, landmark analysis, oncology, real-world data, survival analysis

Introduction

Real-world oncology research increasingly relies on routinely collected electronic health record (EHR) data to study outcomes, care pathways, and organisational features of cancer care outside clinical trials.1,2 In addition to tumour characteristics and treatments, EHRs contain administrative fields that may appear clinically meaningful but often reflect local documentation workflows.

Primary care physicians (PCPs) contribute to symptom assessment, comorbidity management, treatment tolerance monitoring, supportive care, survivorship, and end-of-life coordination.3,4 In oncology EHRs, the presence of a declared PCP may therefore represent care coordination. However, absence of a structured or dated field does not necessarily mean absence of primary care involvement.

A key methodological issue is that administrative information may be recorded after the start of follow-up. Survival analyses treating such time-dependent fields as fixed baseline exposures—including Kaplan–Meier analyses, Cox proportional hazards models, and logistic regression—may be affected by immortal-time and documentation-time bias. This form of bias has been documented in pharmacoepidemiology and oncology observational studies, where it can substantially inflate clear treatment or organisational benefits.5, 6, 7, 8 We used dated PCP documentation as a case study to demonstrate how such bias operates in routinely collected oncology EHR data and to show that temporally aligned analyses resolve the spurious association.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a retrospective real-world cohort study using routinely collected oncology EHR data queried through ConSoRe, a federated French oncology data-mining platform.9 This study was conducted at a single participating centre (Centre Léon Bérard, Lyon, France). ConSoRe integrates structured EHR data with standardised variable definitions and quality-control procedures; mortality data are derived from EHR death records supplemented by linkage with national civil registration data. Eligible patients were adults treated for cancer with a recorded date of cancer care initiation between 2015 and 2017. Cancer care initiation was the index date, defined operationally as the date of the first therapeutic act recorded in the oncology EHR (first consultation, treatment initiation, or diagnostic procedure leading to a therapeutic plan); this date determined the start of follow-up and the baseline from which PCP documentation timing was classified. Patients with missing or inconsistent dates precluding survival estimation were excluded.

The study was reported in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology, REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected Data, and European Society for Medical Oncology Guidance for Reporting Oncology real-World evidence principles for observational studies using routinely collected data.10,11

Exposure, outcome, and covariates

The exposure of interest was the presence and timing of a dated PCP declaration in the oncology EHR. A dated declaration required both a recorded PCP and an exploitable declaration date (DT_CREA field in ConSoRe). DT_CREA corresponds to the date of creation or last modification of the PCP field in the oncology EHR, not necessarily the date of onset of the patient–PCP relationship. Patients were classified into three mutually exclusive groups: (group 1) PCP declared with an exploitable date (n = 10 418), (group 2) PCP declared but no exploitable date (n = 4757), and (group 3) no PCP declared (n = 41). The primary exposure contrast was between group 1 and groups 2 and 3 combined (n = 4798).

Three exposure approaches were used. Firstly, a conventional ever-dated exposure classified patients according to whether a dated declaration existed at any time. Secondly, documentation timing was described as on or before 6 months, after 6 months, or no exploitable date. Thirdly, temporally aligned definitions used only information available at baseline or by a predefined landmark. Overall survival was measured from cancer care initiation to death from any cause or last follow-up. Covariates included age, sex, metastatic status, and tumour location.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised overall and by dated documentation status. Conventional overall survival was estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves and an inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) approach. A logistic regression model was used to estimate propensity scores for the probability of having a dated PCP declaration. Covariates were selected on the basis of their established independent associations with overall survival in oncology cohorts and their potential to confound the documentation-outcome relationship: age at diagnosis, sex, metastatic status, and tumour location. Stabilised IPTW weights were computed targeting the average treatment effect in the full study population. Weights were truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles to limit the influence of extreme values. Covariate balance was assessed using standardised mean differences, with values <0.10 considered acceptable.

To investigate time-related bias, we fitted logistic models for 6- and 12-month mortality using baseline-available documentation status and a Cox model restricted to the first 12 months. We also fitted a counting-process Cox model in which dated PCP documentation was coded as time-dependent: unexposed before the declaration date and exposed thereafter. In the logistic regression analyses—which estimate the probability of death by a fixed time horizon and do not require the proportional hazards assumption—no patient was censored before 12 months: all patients had an observed outcome by the administrative end of follow-up (25 May 2026). A 6-month landmark analysis included patients alive and uncensored at 6 months and defined exposure using information available up to that date.12 The 6-month threshold was selected because it corresponds to a standard oncological assessment interval, encompasses 79.1% of all dated PCP declarations, and captures most early deaths while preserving adequate postlandmark sample size. Landmark sensitivity analyses at 3 (n = 14 779) and 12 months (n = 13 278) are presented in Supplementary Tables S1, S2 and Supplementary Figure S1 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100773). Multivariable models adjusted for age, sex, metastatic status, and tumour location. Interaction with metastatic status (P-interaction = 0.263) and exploratory tumour-site analyses were prespecified as sensitivity analyses. Analyses were carried out in Python version 3.12 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE).

Ethics and data governance

The study used retrospectively collected, deidentified routine-care data. In accordance with European and French legislation on data protection, the use of deidentified routine-care data for research does not require individual patient consent. Data processing was declared to the institutional data-protection officer according to applicable Commission Nationale de l'Informatique et des Libertés (French data protection authority) requirements (R201-000-041).

Results

Study population

After the exclusion of 27 patients with missing or inconsistent survival data, the final cohort included 15 216 patients. Median age was 62 years [interquartile range (IQR) 50-71], 6844 patients were male (45.0%), and 2601 had metastatic disease (17.1%). During a median observed follow-up of 102.1 months (IQR 27.8-118.1), 7175 deaths occurred. Overall, 10 418 patients (68.5%) had a dated PCP declaration (DT_CREA present and exploitable), 4757 (31.2%) had a declared PCP but no exploitable date, and 41 (0.3%) had no PCP declared (Table 1). The primary exposure contrast was between patients with a dated declaration and the remainder (n = 4798). Among patients with a dated declaration, DT_CREA preceded cancer care initiation in 74.2% of cases (n = 7730). Among all 10 418 patients with a dated declaration, 7730 had a DT_CREA strictly before the index date and 2 were recorded on the index date itself, for a total of 7732 (74.2%) with documentation at or before cancer care initiation. The median DT_CREA delay relative to cancer care initiation was −55.9 months (IQR −122.3 to +0.6) across all 10 418 dated declarations, confirming that most PCPs were already documented before the oncological episode. The remaining 25.8% (n = 2688) had a postindex DT_CREA, representing entries updated during oncological follow-up and thus subject to documentation-time bias.

Conventional and timing-based survival analyses

In the conventional time-fixed IPTW analysis, the presence of a dated PCP declaration (compared with no exploitable dated declaration) was associated with longer overall survival [hazard ratio (HR) 0.86, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.82-0.90, P < 0.001; Figure 1A]. For comparability with temporally aligned analyses—which report the absence of declaration as the exposure—this estimate can equivalently be expressed as HR 1.16 (95% CI 1.11-1.23) for absence compared with presence. The propensity model including age, sex, metastatic status, and tumour location achieved good balance, with the maximum absolute standardised mean difference decreasing from 0.197 before weighting to 0.006 after weighting.

When documentation timing was examined, 8237 patients had a dated declaration on or before 6 months after cancer care initiation and 2181 after 6 months, and 4798 had no exploitable dated declaration. Overall survival differed across groups (global log-rank P < 0.001), with the most favourable profile among patients documented after 6 months (Figure 1B). Because this group is defined using future information, this pattern is consistent with documentation-time bias rather than a causal protective effect.

Temporally aligned analyses

Using baseline-available information, 7731 patients had a dated PCP declaration at or before cancer care initiation and 7486 did not. Absence of baseline-dated documentation was not associated with increased 6-month mortality (odds ratio 0.88, 95% CI 0.77-1.01, P = 0.067). At 12 months, point estimates were below unity for both logistic regression and the restricted Cox model (Table 2), indicating that the direction of association depended on exposure timing.

In the time-dependent Cox model, coded using a counting-process formulation, dated PCP documentation was not protective when exposure status was updated over time (HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.10-1.21, P < 0.001). This result contrasted markedly with the conventional IPTW analysis (HR 0.86), confirming that the apparent protective association was driven by documentation timing.

Six-month landmark analysis

The 6-month landmark population included 14 287 patients alive and uncensored at the landmark: 7716 had dated PCP documentation within 6 months and 6571 did not. Postlandmark survival curves largely overlapped and the unadjusted log-rank comparison was not statistically significant (P = 0.140; Figure 1C).

In the adjusted landmark Cox model, the absence of dated PCP documentation within 6 months had a small association in the opposite direction to the conventional analysis (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.89-0.99, P = 0.011). The full model coefficients are presented for transparency in Figure 2. Covariate estimates should not be interpreted as independent prognostic associations in this multivariable context. Discussion

In this large real-world oncology cohort, dated PCP documentation appeared protective when analysed as a conventional fixed exposure. That association persisted after IPTW adjustment for key baseline prognostic factors, including tumour location. However, the apparent benefit did not withstand analyses that were respected when the documentation became available. The most informative finding was that patients documented after 6 months had the best survival profile in a descriptive analysis. This is not plausible as a causal effect of late data entry. Rather, it indicates that the date field encodes survival-conditioned documentation: patients must remain alive and under observation long enough for late documentation to occur.

Temporally aligned analyses supported this interpretation. Baseline-defined models did not show increased early mortality among patients without a dated declaration. The time-dependent Cox model reversed the conventional protective association and the landmark curves were largely overlapping. Together, these results indicate that the dated PCP field is primarily a documentation feature, not a robust independent prognostic marker. The apparent discrepancy between the time-dependent Cox (HR 1.15) and landmark Cox (HR 0.94) reflects three distinct mechanisms. Firstly, the two models address different estimands: the time-dependent Cox model includes all patients from time zero and estimates the instantaneous effect of transitioning from undocumented to documented status; the landmark model is restricted to survivors at 6 months. Secondly, survivor selection bias: patients dying early are predominantly undocumented and their exclusion mechanically attenuates the contrast in the landmark population. Thirdly, the HR above unity in the time-dependent Cox model most likely reflects time-varying confounding or reverse causation—patients whose condition deteriorates may be more likely to trigger an administrative update of their EHR record—rather than documenting that the time-dependent model ‘corrects’ immortal-time bias. The time-dependent Cox model correctly reallocates person-time but does not control for time-varying confounding or informative documentation. Neither estimate should be interpreted as a causal effect of PCP documentation on mortality.

The clinical interpretation should remain narrow. These findings do not imply that primary care involvement is unimportant in oncology. They show that a dated administrative field is not a validated proxy for effective care coordination. More specific measures, such as documented provider communication, shared care plans, comorbidity management, unplanned hospital use, or patient-reported continuity of care, are needed to evaluate primary care integration.13,14

This study highlights a general challenge for oncology real-world evidence. Structured EHR variables are attractive because they are extractable at scale but their meaning depends on how and when they are generated. Analogous documentation-time biases may arise when using treatment completion flags as predictors of survival, when coding supportive care referrals entered during follow-up as baseline covariates, or when extracting administrative comorbidity codes without anchoring to a defined baseline window.8,15, 16, 17, 18 Variables completed after cohort entry should not automatically be treated as baseline covariates. Baseline-restricted definitions, time-dependent modelling, landmark analyses, and target-trial thinking can help prevent self-inflicted time-related biases. Target-trial emulation provides a coherent causal framework when a modifiable intervention can be specified. In the present study, PCP documentation is not a directly modifiable intervention and the objective was to demonstrate bias rather than estimate a causal effect, thus this framework was not applicable here.5,19, 20, 21, 22

Strengths include the large, unselected cohort, long follow-up, substantial number of deaths, and availability of declaration dates enabling direct assessment of documentation timing. Limitations include the retrospective design and residual confounding. The propensity model was restricted to age, sex, metastatic status, and tumour location. Performance status, a major prognostic determinant likely correlated with documentation completeness, was unavailable. Comorbidity burden, treatment intent (curative compared with palliative), socioeconomic status, and health care utilisation frequency are similarly likely to confound the exposure–outcome relationship and could not be adjusted for. These variables represent the primary source of residual confounding and may partly explain the HR of 0.94 in the landmark model. The meaning of missing or undated declarations could not be determined and the DT_CREA date represents the creation or last modification of the EHR field rather than the clinical onset of primary care involvement. Finally, landmark results apply only to patients surviving event-free for 6 months.

Conclusion

The apparent survival benefit associated with dated PCP documentation was largely explained by documentation timing. Time-dependent administrative EHR fields can generate misleading survival associations when treated as fixed baseline exposures in oncology real-world studies.
