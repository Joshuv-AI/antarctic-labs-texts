Dynamics of autophagy in response to high linear energy transfer radiation and cancer
Authors: Mitu Lal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Autophagy process is important in the removal of damaged organelles following radiation interaction. Autophagy maintains stability or balance between intracellular environments at the cellular level. High linear energy transfer radiation is a complex and under-researched field of cancer and radiation biology. In-depth investigation in this area is necessary because autophagy has both protective and detrimental effects on cells, and the specific response can vary depending on factors like the type of radiation, the cell type, and the stage of cancer. Ionizing radiation exposure caused significant alterations in the autophagy genes (ATG3, ATG5, ATG7, ATG8, ATG12). Autophagy blocker could prevent cells from autophagic cell death in response to high linear energy transfer radiation. We have outlined how autophagy can reverse complications of serious diseases in tumor cells and tumor microenvironment, which will contribute to a new direction for better treatment of diseases.

INTRODUCTION

Ionizing radiation exposure is a risk factor for colorectal cancer reported in atomic bomb survivors and radiological workers [1,2]. High linear energy transfer (LET) radiation is lethal because it has sufficient energy to break DNA molecules, causing clustered DNA damage, which can kill cells. This can lead to various health problems ranging from acute effects to long-term effects. Exposure to high-LET radiation depends on various factors such as the altitude of the spacecraft, amount of shielding from the spacecraft or spacesuit, length of the mission, duration and intensity of exposure, and type of radiation. Astronauts working and travelling on long-duration space missions “mission to Mars” are exposed to high energy-charged particle radiation such as protons and heavy ions (12C, 56Fe, 28Si); about 30% cells of the astronauts are hit by heavy ions during space missions [3–5]. Heavy ions are high-LET radiation that is densely ionizing. The most important heavy ion is carbon (12C) because its specific energy is higher; hence, carbon ion is preferred more due to its high relative biological effectiveness and superior dose distribution and deposit higher energy in tissues used for cancer tumor therapy. Autophagy (auto means “self” and phagy means to “to devour”) is induced by high-LET radiation. There are autophagy blockers (chloroquine) that block the process of autophagy (early phase–autophagy biogenesis) and the late phase (autolysosomal fusion). These agents have the potential to block autophagy genes in tumor cells and alter the autophagy pathway inside the cells. Autophagy is a cell-type-dependent process; too much autophagy is also bad for cells. We are inspired by the work of “Yoshinori Ohsumi (Japanese cell biologist),” who discovered the process that cells use to destroy and recycle cellular components. The main function of autophagy is that cells degrade and recycle their own components: proteins, mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum (cargo), so that a new and healthy version can be built. Autophagy induced by high linear energy-charged particle radiation with anticancer drugs is a less explored area in research. However, there are several strategies that can help in improving the efficacy of heavy-ion radiotherapy in combination with lysosomal drugs. Such type of studies is consistent with the possibility of how lysosomotropic compounds help in inhibiting autophagy when tumor cells are exposed to radiation. Deep experimental research with autophagy genes will further investigate the understanding of the pathways regulating autophagy. The present-day research in radiation biology is focused on radiation induced DNA damage, gene expression, and cell death. Radiation induced autophagy by high-LET radiation with different LETs is an unexplored area. The detailed molecular mechanism of heavy ion irradiation induced autophagy would help in better cancer treatment (Figs. 1–4).

Autophagy is regulated by autophagy (ATG) proteins (Fig. 4); ATG5, ATG12, and LC3B (ATG8) all participate in the formation of the autophagosome. ATG5 forms a complex with ATG12 and ATG16L at the membrane of the evolving autophagosome. The 18-kDa isoform LC3-I is localized in the cytosol and after conjugation to phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), a multifunctional phospholipid found in all eukaryotes, is directly involved in autophagy by covalently attaching to LC3-I to form LC3-II, which is important for autophagosome formation, which is a crucial step for the formation of an autophagosome [6]. The activated 16-kDa isoform LC3-II is situated within the autophagosomal membrane. Autophagy is an evolutionarily conserved catabolic process that protects cells from a wide variety of stresses, such as nutrient starvation, organelle damage, and protein aggregation [7–9]. Beclin 1 is a well-established core mammalian autophagy protein. Autophagy has various applications for cellular responses to DNA damage (pathways), such as cell cycle (G1–G2S-M) regulation and apoptosis. One study showed that the deletion of beclin 1 in vitro did not block an autophagy response in human cells but did reduce the ability of cells to undergo DNA repair when exposed to ionizing radiation [10]. The crucial roles that autophagy plays in maintaining cellular homeostasis have been well established, and autophagy dysfunction might result in human diseases, such as cancer. The function of autophagy in cancer is typically considered a double-edged sword in tumorigenesis and therapy [11]. On one hand, autophagy enables cancer cells to survive under some stress conditions, such as starvation, hypoxia, exposure to chemotherapy, and radiotherapy [12]. On the other hand, autophagy acts as a tumor suppressor that induces autophagic cell death [13]. This divergent role is dependent on various factors, such as the genetic context, tumor type, and disease stage. Although much is unknown regarding the exact role of autophagy in cancer, abnormal control of autophagy is one of the key hallmarks of cancer. ATG3, an autophagy-related protein, is the most common homolog gene among eukaryotes. During the process of autophagy, the ATG3 protein acts as an E2 like ubiquitin-like conjugating enzyme in the ATG8 conjugation system, contributing to the formation of phagophore. This gene has been involved in many pathogenic processes, most likely in tumor occurrence and progression, clearance of PE microorganisms, organelle homeostasis, cell differentiation, and mitosis (M). Here we have concluded the importance of the ATG3 function and role in autophagosome formation (binding sites). ATG has been known to regulate all types of cancers as well as in solid tumors (non-small cell lung cancer). ATG has been upregulated significantly and increased autophagic flux; this means it has advantages for survival and resistance to solid tumor cells [14–16]. It is evident that ATG5 and ATG7 are involved in autophagosome formation and fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes subsequently through endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-phagy, which contains a variety of processes that are both mechanistically different and regulate the delivery of ER fragments or their luminal content within lysosomes. LC3 (ATG8) plays an important role in autophagosome formation by enabling membrane elongation and conjugated to PE, perhaps by enabling membrane elongation. Whereas ATG16L complex is required for LC3 lipidation which is determined by membrane localization. ATG16L interacts with ATG12, with ATG3, and the E2 enzyme helps in the LC3 lipidation process.

Autophagy function is associated with cell differentiation, cell death, and aging. Macroautophagy is the best characterized type of autophagy. In this case, the cell forms a double-membrane sequestering compartment called a phagophore (Fig. 5), which is a schematic depiction of the autophagy pathway including p62 and LC3. The first step involved the formation and elongation of the isolated membrane or phagophore. The second step entails the expansion and sequestering of the cytoplasm and the formation of a double-membrane autophagosome. During this step, the LC3 protein is recruited to the autophagosome and p62 along with the attached ubiquitin cargo attached to the LC3 protein. The fusion of lysosome with autophagosome generates the autolysosome. At this step, LC3 dissociated and p62 and the attached ubiquitin cargo are degraded. Autophagosomes are double-membrane structures that engulf the cytoplasm and organelles, deliver material to the lysosomes to be catabolized and recycled. LC3, also known as microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (MAP1LC3/LC3), was first identified in rats. There are two forms of LC3 (I) cytoplasmic, LC3-I (18 kDa) and LC3-II (16 kDa), which are associated with the autophagosome membrane. LC3-I is a membrane-bound protein, LC3-II (a LC3-phospholipid conjugate), by mammalian autophagy, which is associated with ATG7 and ATG3. During macroautophagy (self-eating) process portion of the cytoplasm, cellular organelles are engulfed in autophagosomes (double-membrane structure) which subsequently fuse with lysosomes, resulting in cellular degradation and recycling of intracellular components. Autophagy is activated by extracellular stimuli, starvation, DNA damage, or reactive oxygen species. Till date, 42 (ATG) autophagy-related genes have been identified. Autophagy initiation starts with a nascent autophagy vesicle which is formed upon activation of the ULK complex. This nucleation event is driven by the Vps34 complex. p62 in humans is encoded by the (SQTM1 gene), also known as the ubiquitin-binding protein, which has a short LC3B interacting site that facilitates binding to LC3B, thereby inducing p62 degradation by autophagy. When autophagy is inhibited, p62 levels are increased, and when autophagy is induced, p62 levels decrease. This upregulation and downregulation of p62 are autophagic flux. While on the other side, p62 induced by oncogene can lead to overexpression, and therefore the abundance of p62 in cancer cells does not generally indicate a blockade of autophagy. Function of autophagy is to deliver cytoplasmic materials like cellular proteins, cell organelles, for degradation with the help of hydrolytic enzymes. This segregation of autophagy is healthy for cellular function. Failure of autophagy or lysosomal machinery may lead to cell death or aging. Under cellular stress, over 35 proteins join as autophagy and undergo various stages starting from initiation (induction) followed by vesicle nucleation, (E) elongation, and completion. Later, docking and fusion of lysosome, and the last stage is degradation and recycling. Enzyme responsible for digestion is hydrolase, and p62 is a well-known marker for autophagy. Too much accumulation of p62 leads to inhibition as well as degradation of autophagy. Defects in autophagy can lead to life threatening diseases such as cancer, various muscular disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and pathogen infections.

ROLE OF AUTOPHAGY IN CANCER

Differential response of autophagy in tumor promotion and suppression can lead to cancer cell development and proliferation. Different types of anticancer drugs can regulate autophagy. Hence, autophagy-regulated chemotherapy can be involved in cancer-cell survival or cell death. The mutation in ATG genes suppresses the development of tumors. In addition, autophagy regulation contributes to the expression of tumor suppressor proteins or oncogenes. Bax interacting factor-1 (BIF-1) proteins which associate with beclin 1 (BECN1) gene function as tumor suppressor and positively regulate autophagy. Numerous studies reported that depletion in the autophagy gene, UV radiation resistance-associated gene (UVRAG) suppresses tumor progression in a variety of cancers (colon, gastric, breast, and prostate cancers) [17–20]. Many studies have shown that autophagy acts to promote tumor survival and growth in advanced cancers [21,22]. Tumors are exposed to stressful conditions (hypoxia and nutrient deprivation). Autophagy helps cells to overcome these stresses; under hypoxia, autophagy is activated in solid tumors. Autophagy recycles intracellular components to supply metabolic substrates to proliferating tumors. Hence, the autophagy mechanism contributes to tumor-cell survival by enhancing stress tolerance and the recycling process to meet the metabolic demands of tumors, and therefore, the inhibition or knockdown of ATG genes can result in tumor-cell death [23–26]. Some studies have revealed autophagy is increased in RAS-mutated cancer cells that maintain a high basal level of autophagy. RAS are small GTPases, an enzyme that binds and hydrolyzes guanosine triphosphate (GTP) to guanosine diphosphate (GDP), acting as molecular switches in cellular signaling and other processes. RAS is involved in a wide range of cellular functions, including signal transduction pathways involved in cell survival, metabolism, protein synthesis, cell growth, and cell proliferation. The inhibition of autophagy-related protein enhances the accumulation of damaged mitochondria and decreases cell growth. These studies indicate that autophagy plays an important role in cell survival of several tumors that depend on RAS activation [27–33].

ROLE OF AUTOPHAGY IN CANCER METASTASIS

Autophagy may serve as both pro-metastatic and anti-metastatic functions depending on the contextual demands placed on tumor cells throughout the metastatic process. Autophagy indirectly inhibits inflammation at the primary site of tumor cells required for the initiation of metastasis. Autophagy promotes metastasis by enhancing tumor-cell fitness in response to environmental stresses, such as anoikis, during metastatic progression [34]. One study has shown that autophagy is upregulated in cancer cells that are resistant to TNF-related apoptosis inducing ligand (TRAIL), serving a cytoprotective function. TRAIL has a critical role in regulating the suppression of metastasis by natural killer (NK) cells and T (lymphocyte) cells. In addition, tumor cells may acquire TRAIL resistance via autophagy upregulation and go on to metastasize [35–38].

Metastases arise from residual disseminated tumor cells (DTCs), several years after primary tumor treatment because DTCs cells can enter dormancy and evade therapies [38]. Autophagy is induced by hypoxia and protects against necrosis in primary tumor cells. During Metastasis, tumor cells at the site of origin increased motility to migrate to secondary sites [34,38,39]. The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling induces autophagy cell death and inhibits metastasis in gastric cancer cells in patients with advanced gastric carcinoma; blocking of the mTOR signaling pathway could be an effective strategy for gastric cancer [40]. The inhibition of autophagy not only reduced invasion and migration but also reduced anoikis resistance and lung metastasis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells [41]. Differential response of autophagy as tumor-suppressing and tumor-promoting functions has been reported, decrease in ATG5 expression, reduced survival rate in 158 primary melanoma patients, and decreased ATG5 expression promotes cancer-cell proliferation and is associated with the progression of early-stage cancer [42].

ROLE OF AUTOPHAGY INHIBITORS IN CANCER THERAPY

Many studies have indicated autophagy plays crucial roles in anticancer therapy, including the acquisition of resistance for anticancer therapy. Autophagy is also enhanced by chemotherapy as it decreases cell death, increases cancer-cell survival, and is associated with drug resistance in cancer. In addition, many studies have confirmed that the suppression of autophagy promotes apoptosis [43–46]. The autophagy regulators like rapamycin and rapamycin water-soluble derivatives such as temsirolimus and everolimus, chloroquine (CQ) is an antimalarial drug, hydroxychloroquine (HCQ); a CQ derivative, are used in cancer therapy. Temsirolimus and everolimus are approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for cancer therapy because they inhibit mTORC1 and induce autophagy. Everolimus is used to treat progressive neuroendocrine tumors of pancreatic origin and breast cancer [47]. Both CQ and HCQ are lysosomal inhibitors and directly inhibit autophagy by altering lysosomal pH, inhibiting autophagy degradation, and accumulation of autophagosomes [48–50]. Some studies have indicated that CQ or HCQ can suppress cancer-cell growth via the inhibition of autophagy in bladder cancer and pancreatic adenocarcinoma [51]. In addition, many studies have found that these reagents enhance the effects of chemotherapy by inhibiting autophagy-mediated resistance to cancer therapy [52].

Another autophagy inhibitor, spautin-1, inhibits autophagy and leads to the induction of proteasomal removal of class III PI3K kinase complexes [53]. Additionally, Lys05 (water-soluble analog of HCQ), a new lysosomal autophagy inhibitor, has the potential to be developed further into a drug for cancer and other medical applications [54]. Hence, the development of autophagy-specific inhibitors is a new therapeutic strategy for anticancer therapy. SAR405 is a kinase inhibitor of vacuolar protein sorting-associated protein 18 (Vps18), and Vps34 has essential roles in autophagy, endocytosis, heart, and liver function [55] and impairs lysosomal function as well as influences the interaction between the late endosome and lysosome combined study with SAR405 and everolimus, which enhances the inhibition of cancer proliferation in renal cancer cell lines [56,57]. These results indicate that SAR405 has anticancer effects as a Vps34 inhibitor.

AUTOPHAGY IS A NOVEL TARGET FOR CANCER THERAPY

Autophagy, which is a type II dependent cell death, affects cell proliferation and survival. Therefore, various clinical trials have been conducted to test the potential applications or mechanisms of modulating autophagy for improving better cancer treatment. Several studies demonstrated that high-LET radiation can induce autophagy. Studies on the effect of autophagy in radiation sensitization of cancer cells for improving radiotherapy have shown controversial results. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate before the modulation of autophagy can be applied in clinical manifestation. Some studies reported that modulating autophagy could modify the radiosensitivity of tumor cells to the high-LET radiation. The autophagic level in the irradiated cells HeLa, MCF-7, and MDA-MB-231, increases in a dose- and LET-dependent manner, and the ability of carbon ions to inhibit the activation of the phosphoinositide 3-kinases pathway rises with increasing their LET. Inhibition of autophagy made tumor cells more sensitive to high-LET radiation [58]. In another study, oxaliplatin increased the rate of autophagy in neutrons irradiated in U-87 cells. Combining oxaliplatin with high-LET radiation represents a fruitful approach to unravel cell death mechanisms involved in tumor response to such treatments [59]. In addition, high-LET neutron exposure had a weaker effect on cell survival than low-LET gamma-rays. The induction of autophagy was examined in gamma-ray-treated KHOS/NP and MG63 osteosarcoma cells along with exposure to high-LET neutrons. The dose response relationship between radiosensitivity and autophagy was assessed by colony formation assay, plotting the cell surviving fractions against autophagy levels. Radiotherapy by high-LET radiation results in effectively killing tumor cells by increasing radiosensitivity. High-LET radiation lowers cell survival per absorbed dose than low-LET radiation. Neutron treatment increases autophagy rates in KHOS/NP and MG63 cells; neutrons combined with high-LETs showed more effective inhibition compared to gamma-rays [60]. All these studies demonstrate that autophagy is very important in radiosensitivity, cell survival, and cellular resistance against high-LET radiation.

ROLE OF AUTOPHAGY IN HUMAN DISEASES

Parkinsons disease

Autophagy has implications in the pathogenesis of Parkinson disease; some studies have demonstrated the molecular mechanism by which mitochondria are selectively targeted. Autophagy in mitochondria is known as mitophagy. These studies show that defects in mitochondria can lead to familial Parkinson disease [61]. Two key players PINK1 (mitochondrial kinase) and Parkin (E3 ubiquitin ligase) are linked to Parkinson disease and mitophagy pathway that prevents mitochondria dysfunction and damage. When mitochondria are damaged and lose their membrane potential (depolarization), PINK1 becomes stabilized and recruits parkin to the damaged mitochondria [62]. Mitophagy is triggered by parkin upon ubiquitination with various outer membrane of mitochondrial proteins. Mutations in PINK1 and parkin are linked with Parkinson disease [63]. Too much accumulation of damaged mitochondria can affect neuronal cells and generate oxidative stress inside cells.

Alzheimer disease

Alzheimer disease is the most common cause of progressive dementia in humans (elderly people). It is mainly caused by accumulation of proteinaceous aggregates (intracellular MAPT/tau tangles) and extracellular beta amyloid peptide (irreversible loss of cognitive abilities/formation of senile plaques). Which leads to neuronal cell death and declines important cognitive functions of the brain, the metabolism of amyloid-β (Aβ) and tau proteins is a crucial step and it is influenced by autophagy. It is evident that autophagy is a lysosomal-dependent, homeostatic process where organelles and proteins are continuously degraded and recycle nutrients into energy. Hence, dysfunction/cell death of autophagy is suggested to lead to growth or increase of noxious proteins in the Alzheimer disease brain [64]. In a recent study, multilayer brain proteomics analysis revealed that accumulation of autophagy substrate SQSTM1/p62 at different stages of Alzheimer disease in humans suggests impaired autophagy flux [65].

Cancer and Alzheimer disease are two common disorders for which the final pathophysiological mechanism is yet to be identified. To understand the biological pathways between Alzheimer disease and cancer could lead to novel therapeutic targets. The key findings of an inverse association between cancer and Alzheimer disease open several avenues of investigation. Reducing radiation exposure from medical imaging and other sources is important for both cancer prevention and Alzheimer disease risk reduction. The exact mechanisms by which radiation impacts Alzheimer’s are still being investigated. High-LET radiation used in radiotherapy has been shown to promote the clearance of amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques from the brain. These plaques are a key feature of Alzheimer disease, and their reduction can potentially slow down or even reverse the progression of the disease [66–68].

Polyglutamine diseases

Polyglutamine diseases occur when there is an accumulation of intracellular aggregates that lead to the formation of misfolded proteins (abnormally elongated polyglutamine tract). Some experimental evidence has highlighted the role of autophagy in disorders caused by expansion of polyglutamine, including Huntington disease, and other forms of spinocerebellar ataxias. In Huntington disease, the expansion of polyglutamine length is an etiological driver of Huntington. Most important part of HTT is that there is a significant contrast between wild-type and mutated HTT in the regulation of the autophagic process [69]. In HTT wild-type functions in regulation of basal autophagy due to its role in the selection of the autophagic cargo [70,71]. Whereas, mutant Huntington expression leads to altered cargo recognition and autophagy failure [72].

Research suggests that Huntington disease patients might be more sensitive to the harmful effects of radiation, especially high-LET radiation, which is used in cancer treatment. However, Huntington disease itself may be associated with a reduced cancer risk; the use of high-LET radiation in Huntington disease patients with cancer needs more evaluation due to potential increased radiosensitivity [73].

Neuropathic disorders

Neuropathy disorders like autonomic neuropathy (HSAN) and hereditary sensory are caused by degeneration and death of peripheral sensory and spastic paraplegia type 49 (SPG49) and hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP) (motor neurons). Mutations in several genes encoding several ER proteins which are involved in (ER-remodeling) are well associated with neuropathies [74]. Hence, hereditary neuropathies like loss-of-function mutations in ER (reticulon type) membrane protein RETREG1/FAM134B (reticulophagy regulator 1) are linked with the development of HSAN type II (HSAN2) [75,76], and mutations in reticulon 2 (RTN2) are linked with HSP (SPG12) [77]. The first mammalian receptor identified for selective ER autophagy (reticulophagy) is RETREG, involved in the delivery of ER fragments via autophagosomes for lysosomal degradation [78].

CONCLUSION

Today, there are many outstanding questions related to autophagy in cancer that need to be addressed. What are the exact mechanisms behind autophagy in the mammalian system? How does autophagy machinery function in clearance of cargo (cytoplasmic materials) stabilized with synthesis and up folding (proteins) through the proteostasis network? How exactly does a human cell respond with autophagy inhibitors, and what are distinct autophagic pathways yet to be discovered? How does autophagy function in regulating genes (ATG), and how does autophagy signaling activation promote/suppress in human disease? Are some aspects of mammalian autophagy more important and vary in an age-cell and/or tissue-dependent manner? All these unanswered things need to be explored in the future.
