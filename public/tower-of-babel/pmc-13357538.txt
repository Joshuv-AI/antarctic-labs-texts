A supramolecular non-mesogenic route towards autonomous liquid crystal elastomer soft robots
Authors: Changfei He, Yufan Yang, Hong Wan, Ning Zheng, Yibao Li, Tao Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Liquid crystal elastomers are unique choices for designing the next generation soft robots. Their synthesis relies exclusively on the polymerization/crosslinking of reactive mesogens with high molecular rigidity, necessitating high energy input to disrupt the molecular order for their actuation function. We report a non-mesogenic route towards designing liquid crystal elastomers. The monomeric precursors are non-mesogenic, but the supramolecular interactions are amplified in the polymerized network due to their cooperative nature, leading to liquid crystallinity. This allows resolving the common conflict between high actuation strain and low energy input, making it possible to design soft robots that can operate autonomously by harnessing the serendipitous energy from natural environments. Our molecular design broadens the scope of liquid crystalline materials, with potential implications beyond elastomeric actuators.

Introduction

The muscle-like actuation of liquid crystal elastomers (LCE) has made them promising candidates for many applications, including soft robotics1–5, medical devices6,7, and human-machine interfaces8–11. An ideal LCE should exhibit high actuation response with minimal input energy required. This is challenging for classical LCE, which are generally formed through the polymerization and crosslinking of reactive mesogens. Typically, the strong interactions between their mesogens necessitate large energy input to disrupt the structural ordering, resulting in high actuation temperatures. Delicate designs of LCE with reduced mesogenic interactions12,13 or content14,15 can reduce the actuation temperature, but the actuation performance (strain) is inevitably compromised. Here, we report a non-mesogenic route towards the synthesis of LCE actuators. The monomeric precursors exhibit no liquid crystallinity, but a smectic phase emerges as the polymer network is formed. We attribute this surprising finding to the π-interactions which are too weak at the monomeric state, but become sufficiently strong to induce the liquid crystallinity in the network due to their cooperative nature. The unique supramolecular mesogens result in a low actuation temperature at or well below physiological temperature. Meanwhile, the high ordering of the smectic phase enhances the actuation performance relative to typical LCE that commonly rely on less ordered nematic phases for actuation. Consequently, a large actuation response is achieved with very little thermal energy input. Our approach expands the design scope for LCE, with potential implications for the broader class of liquid crystal materials.

The concept of reactive mesogens16 has been fundamental to the field of LCE since its initial discovery. Reactive mesogens exhibit two key molecular characteristics: (i) rod/disc-like rigid molecular segments composed of π-conjugated aromatic moieties that self-organize into ordered mesogens through typically π-π stacking intermolecular interactions; (ii) flexible segments responsible for the mobility of the mesogens17,18. Despite the vast chemical library, the range of chemistries capable of forming LCE remain surprisingly limited. Common mesogenic units (Fig. 1a) are typically composed of two or more 1,4-phenylenes connected either directly or through very short spaces such as esters, oxymethylenes, imines, or methylene groups13,14,19,20. A non-intuitive question arises as to whether, using non-mesogenic monomers, a mesophase can be formed for a polymerized network. If the molecular segments possessing distinct structures and shapes could self-organize into short ordered nanostructures through weak intramolecular interactions, the resulting material may exhibit an intermediate order between the long-range ordered crystalline state and the disordered liquid state. This would allow creation of a mesophase as “something from nothing”. In this regard, jacketed liquid-crystalline polymers provide a useful hint, for which side-group crowdedness around the polymer main backbone can induce liquid crystallinity, without employing any mesogenic monomers21,22. Such a mechanism, however, cannot be extended to LCE since the entire macromolecule becomes too stiff. Nevertheless, we hypothesize that, by rational design of a non-mesogenic precursor, it is possible to induce phase separation in its polymerized state in which the rigid segments aggregate to form a mesophase and the flexible segments provide the molecular mobility, meeting the pre-requite for an LCE.

Results

We achieve this goal using the oxime-ester monomer shown in Fig. 1b. Collectively, an oxime-ester and the directly connected benzene ring forms a π-conjugating moiety. The polarized optical microscopic image (POM in Supplementary Fig. 1) of the oxime-ester monomer suggests that it is non-mesogenic. Presumably, this is because the two π conjugating moieties in each monomer are separated by a long flexible aliphatic spacer (–(CH2)n–). After polymerization to form a network, however, these weak molecular interactions can work cooperatively to facilitate self-assembly to form a mesophase.

A series of network samples are synthesized via a radical-mediated “thiol-ene” click reaction using the oxime-ester monomer, an aliphatic decanedithiol as the chain extender, and a pentaerythritol tetra(3-mercaptopropionate) (PETMP) as a crosslinker (Fig. 2a). Here, the thiols and double bonds are always maintained at a stoichiometric balance, with the dithiol to PETMP molar ratio fixed at 2.37 unless otherwise noted. The formulations containing a photo-initiator (0.5%) are photo-polymerized for 2 min in dichloromethane. We focus primarily on POE-6 as a representative sample unless otherwise noted. Its photo-polymerization yields a transparent gel (Fig. 2b). The material becomes opaque (Fig. 2c) after solvent evaporation, thermal annealing at 80 °C for 1 h, and cooling to 20 °C. The network gel fraction is 97.4%. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis suggests the completeness of the polymerization process, as evidenced by the disappearance of infrared peaks characteristic to the double bond (=C-H at 3054 cm⁻¹) and thiol (S-H stretching at 2560 cm⁻¹) (Fig. 2d) in POE-6. Under POM, the unaligned POE-6 exhibits a focal conic fan-shaped textures, while the aligned sample displays a well-organized layered pattern (Fig. 2e), indicating the formation of a smectic A phase. Both the one-, and two-dimensional wide-angle X-ray diffraction (WAXD) patterns (Fig. 2f, g) display inner halos at approximately 0.17 Å⁻¹, suggesting further that liquid crystalline phase is smectic23,24. The smectic layer spacing is calculated as 36.9 Å according to Bragg’s equation23. By comparison, the molecular length of the nonmesogenic monomer is 29.4 Å (calculated via ChemBio 3D simulation).

The DSC curves of the oxime-ester-6 monomer, the linear polymer (LPOE-6, with no cross-linker in the formulation), and the cross-linked network (POE-6) are presented in Fig. 3a. For the oxime-ester monomer and the linear polymer, they exhibit crystalline melting peaks at 101.4 °C and 85.7 °C, respectively, with no observable liquid crystalline phase transition. The absence of peaks at smectic regions and short-range ordered scattering peaks of π-π stacking further verifies the non-liquid-crystalline nature of the monomer. (Supplementary Fig. 1). The latter is also confirmed as non-liquid crystalline by POM analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). Interestingly, the crystalline melting peak disappears for POE-6, but a smaller transition peak emerges at 33.6 °C. We attribute this peak to the transition from its smectic phase to an isotropic phase, which is verified by the 2D WAXD patterns recorded at various temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 2). The small-angle scattering ring forms two symmetrical arcs is perpendicular to the aligned direction after alignment (Supplementary Fig. 3). Based on this, the smectic phase is assigned as smectic A. The essential role of crosslinking in the mesophase formation is intriguing. We believe that this is because crosslinking restricts long-range segmental movement that suppresses crystallization. The chemical structure of the oxime-ester monomer, particularly the molecular spacing between the oxime-ester groups as defined by m, significantly influences the mesophase formation. The DSC curves in Fig. 3b compare the phase transition temperatures of four cross-linked networks with varied m. With the shortest spacing, the smectic-isotropic transition temperature (TSI) of POE-4 is barely detectable. Conversely, with the longest spacing, POE-12 shows a pronounced enthalpy peak, indicative of a crystalline melting transition rather than a liquid crystalline transition. With intermediate spacings, both POE-6 and POE-10 possess notable smectic-to-isotropic transition peaks. The physical states of these samples are consistent with the above analyses, with POE-4 being soft and transparent, POE-6 and POE-10 being soft and opaque, whereas POE-12 is rigid and opaque (Fig. 3c). The stress-strain curves of these samples (unaligned polydomain states) at 20 °C are provided in Fig. 3d, showing that POE-6 and POE-10 both exhibit a soft-elastic plateau characteristic of LCE25, whereas POE-4 and POE-12 display stress-strain behaviors consistent with typical rubber and crystalline polymers, respectively. These results highlight the strong impact of the spacing length, with mesogen forming only within a specific molecular length range. When the spacing is too short, the drive force for self-assembly-induced phase separation is insufficient, prohibiting mesogen formation. Conversely, if the spacing is too long, the π conjugating moieties become too independent, leading to high segmental mobility. This promotes long-range ordering, not only generating layered smectic phases but also facilitating crystallization (Supplementary Fig. 4). We note that solvent evaporation during thermal annealing is crucial for the formation of mesophase (Supplementary Fig. 5). The liquid crystalline order in POE-6 is formed only after the polymerization and solvent evaporation (80 °C, 1 h). By comparison, if the POE-6 is polymerized in the molten state (without a solvent), it fails to form the layered Smectic A phase (Supplementary Fig. 6). Therefore, solvent evaporation plays an essential role. Mechanistically, our non-liquid-crystalline monomer is a rod-coil-rod tri-block molecule containing a flexible aliphatic spacer, an allylic terminal segment, and a rigid unit of π-conjugating moieties. The interactions between the solvent and the three segmental blocks differ. Consequently, microphase separation occurs as the solvent evaporates because of the distinct interactions between the solvent and polymer segments. Kinetically, this process is aided by the dynamic exchange of the oxime-esters that provides the necessary network mobility.

The characteristics of the mesophase are affected by the thermal annealing condition. Figure 3e shows that annealing at a higher temperature (from 25 to 80 °C) for a fixed 1 h time promotes forming higher content of the mesophase as implied by the higher enthalpy and the sample optical opaqueness. In this annealing process, the TSI peak is mostly unaffected within a range of 31 to 33 °C. We note, however, further raising the annealing temperature to 120 °C results in no mesogen formation (Supplementary Fig. 7). At the optimal annealing temperature of 80 °C, prolonging the annealing time from 1 h to 4 h yields no noticeable difference other than slight changes in TSI (Supplementary Fig. 8). The strong impact of annealing temperature sheds light on the mechanism of mesogen formation. While crosslinking is essential to prohibit long-range crystalline order, it also constrains the self-assembly to form the mesophase especially for a system like ours with a high crosslinker content. The dynamic covalent nature of the oxime-ester bonds26 plays an intriguing role in resolving this dilemma. During the thermal annealing process, the polymer network undergoes bond exchange that provides extra molecular segmental mobility. When the annealing temperature is too high, however, the rate of bond exchange far exceeds the speed of the self-assembly process, inhibiting mesophase formation. Mechanically, the LCE shows strong mechanical hysteresis originated from the soft elasticity27. When it is stretched to different strains (Fig. 3f), the material exhibits low levels of strain recovery long after the stretching force is released. This is consistent with the stress relaxation curves in Supplementary Fig. 9. At 25 °C (below TSI), the material shows rapid stress relaxation due to the soft elasticity. In contrast, no stress relaxation is observed at 60 °C (above TSI) within the same timescale since the material is at an isotropic rubbery state.

To achieve actuation under stress-free condition, mesogen alignment is required. For our LCE, the mesogens are generated after the material synthesis. This makes it impossible for mesogen alignment during the synthesis step, as is often the case for conventional LCE. However, the dynamic oxime-ester bonds present in the LCE offers the possibility to align the mesogens after material preparation20,27. Figure 4a shows two parallel routes to achieve mesogen alignment. The first route is conducted above TSI and the programming force is maintained during the process. The second route is to utilize the mechanical hysteresis described in Fig. 3f by simply stretching/deforming the LCE without heating (below TSI) and let it rest under a stress-free condition for three days. Figure 4b compares the 2D WAXD pattern of an LCE before and after programming (50% programming strain at 40 °C for 18 h), showing the transition from an isotropic ring to two distinct arcs that confirms the mesogen alignment. The alignment is further verified by POM images and the degree of orientation is 0.70 (Supplementary Fig. 10). For an aligned LCE, its actuation strain can be quantitatively assessed as (L − L0)/L0 × 100%, where L0 and L represent the sample lengths above and below the TSI, respectively. Accordingly, the photographic images in Fig. 4b show a high actuation strain of 118%. In dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) tests, the actuator shows high cyclic stability with consistent actuation strains at around 132 ± 1.1% even after 10 cooling-heating cycles (Fig. 4c). Here, the higher actuation strain in the DMA tests is because DMA allows extending the lower test temperature well below ambient temperature to achieve maximum performance. Compared with nematic and smectic liquid crystal elastomer, despite the smectic ordering, the modulus and the threshold stress are close to nematic liquid crystal elastomer, which is beneficial for actuation efficiency (Supplementary Table 1).

For both the two programming routes, the dynamic oxime-ester bond exchange is the essential mechanism for the mesogen alignment. However, the drastically different bond exchange speeds due to the temperature difference require different programming times to achieve similar actuation (Supplementary Fig. 11). Although the second route takes much longer time, it is much easier to operate because it does not require maintaining the programming force during the entire process. This makes it particularly suited for geometrically complexed actuators as exemplified by the photographic images in Fig. 4d and Supplementary Movie 1, which reflect two characteristics distinct from other LCE actuators: its low actuation temperature and the high actuation strain. This is further highlighted in Fig. 4f, showing its advantage over existing LCE12,13,28–36 (Supplementary Table 2). The reason why our LCE provides reversible actuation without strain loss is mainly due to the reduction of TSI without sacrificing the liquid crystalline order. For conventional LCE, reducing the transition temperature is typically accomplished by introducing structural defects. This inevitably compromises the degree of ordering, consequently the actuation strain. In contrast, our supramolecular approach leads to reduced transition temperatures while maintaining high smectic ordering.

We note that the reversible actuation behavior of LCE have been used for designing autonomous soft robots3,32,37–39. For instance, LCE robots can be built to self-roll or even self-jump by converting the thermal energy from the environment into mechanical energy utilizing the thermal actuation of LCE39. Typically, however, the thermal energy required is high because of the high actuation temperature of the LCE. Thus, typical autonomous LCE robots that only operate on high temperature hotplates (above 100 °C)3,4,39. In contrast, the advantages of our LCE make them suited for designing autonomous robots that can operate under natural conditions with very low obstructive thermal energy present. For instance, our LCE actuator can operate around human body temperature (Supplementary Movie 2), achieving self-rolling on human opisthenar (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Movie 3). This, coupled with its high actuation strain relative to other low temperature LCE12,13,32, suggests its vast potential for device applications under physiological conditions. For our LCE system, the TSI can even be further lowered to 11.5 °C by employing a different dithiol extender (Supplementary Fig. 12). This makes it possible to fabricate an autonomous soft robot that can self-roll continuously on an ice surface (Fig. 4g) at a speed of 0.74 mm/s (Fig. 4h, Fig. 4i, and Supplementary Movie 4). We emphasize here that there is no purposely introduced energy. The LCE robot simply captures the environmental thermal energy naturally exists due to the temperature gradient between the ice surface and the surrounding air. Compared to most LCEs reported in the literature, POE-6 exhibits a relatively high work capacity of 386 kJ m−3, attributable to its elevated actuation strain. Concurrently, POE-6 actuates at a very low actuation temperature, indicating that minimal energy input suffices to achieve its high-work-capacity actuation (Supplementary Fig. 13 and Table 4).

Discussion

Our non-mesogenic self-assembly approach towards designing highly ordered smectic LCE stands in sharp contrast to current methods that rely on mesogenic monomers to access less ordered nematic LCE. The resulting LCE actuators can be triggered with low energy inputs, yet simultaneously exhibit high actuation strains, which resolves a common conflict for LCE design. The simplicity in mechanical programming of our LCE opens up new opportunities for making geometrically complex actuators that are otherwise difficult to program. In addition, our non-mesogenic monomers are much simpler to synthesize and tailor relative to many mesogenic monomers. Collectively, these benefits markedly expand the scope of LCE. Importantly, there is ample space for further molecular engineering following this or similar principles, for instance, by introducing additional stimuli-responsiveness to produce more intelligent LCE beyond thermotropic LCE. More generally, our study offers potentially useful hints for other fields in which liquid crystallinity is relevant.

Methods

Materials

4-hydroxy acetophenone (99%, TCI), Allyl bromide(98%, Macklin), potassium carbonate (K2CO3, 99%, Macklin), Sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3, 99.5%, Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd), Magnesium sulfate (MgSO4, AR, Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd), Hydroxylamine hydrochloride (NH2OH·HCl, 98.5%, J&K), Sodium acetate (NaOAc, 99% AR, Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd), pyridine (99.5%, J&K), succinyl chloride (95%, Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd), adipyl chloride (98%, TCI), sebacoyl chloride (99%, Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd), 1,10-decanedicarboxylic (99%, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd), 4-(dimethylamino)pyridine (DMAP, 99%, J&K), and N,N-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC, 99%, J&K), 1,10-decanedithiol (97%, Macklin), α,ω-Dithiopolyethylene glycol (SH-PEG-SH, Mw = 1000, Macklin) pentaerythritol tetrakis(3-mercaptopropionate) (PETMP, 95%, Energy Chemical), 2,2-bimethoxy-2-phenylacetophenone (DMPA, 98%, Energy Chemical), All solvents (dichloromethane (DCM, AR, Guangdong Guanghua Sci-Tech Co., Ltd), acetonitrile (MeCN, AR, Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd), Hexane(AR, Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd), ethyl acetate(EA, AR, Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd) were purchased commercially and used without purification.

Synthesis of oxime-ester-m

The synthesis of oxime is according to the earlier paper26. All the oxime ester monomers were prepared with the same procedure except that the chloride was replaced with chlorides with different carbon chain lengths. The chemical structures of oxime esters were characterized using 1H NMR and 13C NMR (Supplementary Fig. 14-Fig. 18).

Synthesis of linear POE-6 and POE-m

For example, to synthesize cross-linked POE-6, oxime ester-6, decanedithiol, and PETMP were dissolved into 2 mL DCM, and then, DMPA (0.5%) was added as a photo initiator. The solution was firstly pre-polymerization by UV light (365 nm, 1 mW/cm2) for few seconds in order to increase the viscosity. Then the mixtures were placed between two glass plates separated by a silicone rubber spacer with different thickness and irradiated under UV light (365 nm, 1 mW/cm2) for 1 min for each side. After that, the fresh POE films were peeled off from glass and then annealed at 80 °C for 2 h. The linear POE-6 was prepared by photo-polymerization a DCM solution with same equivalent of oxime etster-6 and decanedithiol. Other cross-linked POE follow the same procedure except using different oxime-ester monomers and chain extenders. The formulations for preparing different POE samples are shown in the Supplementary Table 3.

Characterization

The 1H NMR spectra were collected using Bruker Avance 400 MHz and 101 MHz spectrometer at room temperature with Chloroform-d as a solvent. FTIR spectra were recorded using a Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrometer. The transition temperatures were tested using DSC-60Plus (SHIMADZU Instruments) with a heating rate of 20 °C min−1 and cooling rate of 5 °C min−1. POM images were measured using a ZEISS Axiola 5 polarizing optical microscope. WAXD patterns were collected via Bruker (Xenocs Xuess3.0) X-ray diffractometer (Cu anode, wavelength = 1.54 Å). Stress-relaxation experiments were carried out on dynamic mechanical analyzer (DMA, TA Q800) in a stress relaxation mode. The Cyclic stability of actuation strain was monitored under control force mode with a negligible applied force (0.01 N). The mechanical properties were performed on an Instron 5965 instrument at room temperature (~20 °C), which was used to obtain stress-strain and strain-recovery curves. Gel content were measured by extracting a sample into DCM at room temperature for 24 h.

Programming of POE-6

When the temperature higher than TSI, a stripe is stretched to a length of Lx (below the maximum elongation achievable at the current temperature, for 40 °C is 50% and for 60 °C is 20%). Via maintaining the applied strain, the samples were annealed for different programming times. Programmed POE-6s were subsequently obtained after removing the external force. When temperatures below TSI, the procedure is similar; however, in this case, the stripe was stretched to a length of Lmax and released, then let it stay at 25 °C for different times. The actuation strain of the programmed sample was calculated as follows: actuation strain = (Lcold − Lheat)/Lheat × 100%, where Lcold and Lheat are the sample lengths at 20 °C and 40 °C, respectively.
