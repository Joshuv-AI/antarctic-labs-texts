Double-antigen sandwich ELISA based on S1 for cross-species detection of Porcine deltacoronavirus antibodies
Authors: Yingjie Bai, Ruiming Yu, Liping Zhang, Zhongwang Zhang, Ya Liu, Dongsheng Wang, Peng Zhou, Zhangfu Pei, Huichen Guo, Li Pan, Xinsheng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPorcine deltacoronavirus (PDCoV) is an emerging enteric coronavirus causing high mortality in neonatal piglets and posing a significant threat to the swine industry. Evidence indicates that PDCoV has cross-species transmission potential and may pose a zoonotic risk, emphasizing the need for reliable serological tools for epidemiological surveillance and vaccine evaluation. Here, we developed a double-antigen sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (DAgS-ELISA) based on the PDCoV S1 protein, expressed in CHO cells and used as both coating and HRP-conjugated antigen. The assay reliably detected PDCoV-specific antibodies in sera from pigs, chickens, rabbits, and mice, showing high sensitivity (92.86%) and specificity (99.11%) as determined by receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, with excellent reproducibility. No cross-reactivity was observed with antibodies against other common swine pathogens. Concordance with indirect immunofluorescence assay was 96.18% (kappa = 0.923), and assay results correlated strongly with neutralizing antibody titers (Pearson r = 0.865). Overall, this S1-based DAgS-ELISA provides a sensitive, specific, and cross-species applicable method for PDCoV serological detection, supporting its use for epidemiological surveillance and evaluation of vaccine-induced neutralizing antibodies.Key points• A novel S1-based double-antigen sandwich ELISA was established for PDCoV detection.• The assay shows high sensitivity, strong specificity, and broad cross-species applicability.• ELISA results strongly correlate with neutralizing antibody titers, aiding vaccine evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-025-13671-3.

Introduction

Porcine deltacoronavirus (PDCoV) is an emerging swine enteric coronavirus belonging to the genus Deltacoronavirus within the Coronaviridae family (Ma et al. 2015; Jung et al. 2016). PDCoV infection can cause acute gastrointestinal disease in pigs, characterized by diarrhea, vomiting, dehydration, and lethargy. The disease is particularly severe in suckling piglets, with mortality rates ranging from 30 to 80% depending on region and outbreak severity (Jung et al. 2015; Sun et al. 2023). First detected in Hong Kong in 2012, PDCoV was reported in the United States in 2014 and has since been identified in China, North America, Southeast Asia, and other regions, resulting in considerable economic losses in the swine industry (Woo et al. 2012; Hu et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2015; Duan 2022).

The PDCoV genome is a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA approximately 25.4 kb in length, comprising untranslated regions at both termini and seven open reading frames encoding structural and nonstructural proteins (Woo et al. 2009; Marthaler et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2015). The spike (S) protein, located on the viral envelope, mediates host cell entry (Shang et al. 2018). It consists of two subunits: the N-terminal S1 subunit, which binds host receptors, and the C-terminal S2 subunit, which facilitates membrane fusion (Li 2016). The S1 subunit is rich in neutralizing epitopes and is therefore a prime target for vaccine development and serological assays (Bosch et al. 2003; Li et al. 2018; Du et al. 2024). Notably, in studies on SARS-CoV-2, S1-specific antibodies have been reported to persist for at least 7 months, highlighting the suitability of S1 as a robust serological target (den Hartog et al. 2021).

PDCoV has been reported to infect various economically important animals, including pigs, calves, and poultry, and is also capable of infecting canine cells (Jung et al. 2017; Li et al. 2018; Liang et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2025). Although its infectivity in calves and chickens appears limited, pigs and poultry are in frequent contact with humans and other mammals, raising concerns about potential cross-species transmission (Wang et al. 2019; Poudel et al. 2020). Notably, PDCoV RNA was detected in serum from Haitian children in 2021, highlighting its zoonotic potential and the need for surveillance (Lednicky et al. 2021).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) are widely used for pathogen detection due to their speed, sensitivity, and low equipment requirements. Among them, double-antigen sandwich ELISA and competitive blocking ELISA are suitable for cross-species applications (Watcharatanyatip et al. 2010; Cordero-Ortiz et al. 2023; Yu et al. 2025). However, competitive ELISAs require blocking monoclonal antibodies, the production of which is labor-intensive. In contrast, double-antigen sandwich ELISAs can be rapidly developed using recombinant antigens and offer high specificity and sensitivity. This format has been successfully applied to the serodiagnosis of several zoonotic pathogens (Sas et al. 2018; Cordero-Ortiz et al. 2023). Moreover, it can serve as a surrogate for virus neutralization tests, facilitating antibody monitoring with reduced technical complexity (Bonifacio et al. 2022).

In this study, the PDCoV S1 protein was expressed using a CHO cell system and subsequently conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP). We then developed a double-antigen sandwich ELISA optimized for detecting total antibody responses in serum from multiple species. The assay demonstrated high reproducibility, sensitivity, and specificity, with 96.18% concordance with indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA). ELISA optical density (OD) values correlated strongly with neutralizing antibody titers (Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.865), supporting its use in PDCoV serosurveillance, diagnostic applications, and evaluation of vaccine-induced immunity.

Materials and methods

Materials

LLC-PK cells (porcine kidney, ATCC No. CL-101) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, USA) and cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM; Gibco, Waltham, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; BBI Life Sciences, Shanghai, China). The codon-optimized S1 gene, designed based on the sequence of the PDCoV strain previously isolated and preserved in our laboratory (GenBank accession no. MN064712), was synthesized and cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector by Genscript (Nanjing, China). The full optimized sequence is included in Supplementary File 1. A total of 112 PDCoV-negative, 56 PDCoV-positive, and 157 porcine serum samples of unknown status were collected from commercial pig farms with or without documented PDCoV outbreaks. Positive sera for porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV), pseudorabies virus (PRV), porcine rotavirus (PoRV), foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV), classical swine fever virus (CSFV), porcine circovirus type 2 (PCV2), porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV), and transmissible gastroenteritis virus (TGEV) were collected from clinical pig farms, validated by indirect ELISA, and stored in our laboratory at the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. These sera have also been used in previous studies, supporting their reliability as reference materials (Yu et al. 2025). Positive sera (n = 3 per species) from pigs, chickens, mice, and rabbits, obtained after PDCoV vaccination or infection, were validated by established methods (Yu et al. 2025).

Expression, purification, and HRP labeling of the S1 protein

The pcDNA3.1-PD-S1 plasmid was transfected into ExpiCHO-S cells using ExpiFectamine™ CHO reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), and cultures were maintained at 37℃ with 8% CO₂ and ≥ 80% relative humidity. At 24 h post-transfection, ExpiFectamine™ CHO Enhancer and ExpiCHO™ Feed (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) were added, and supernatants were harvested on day 12 by centrifugation (5,000 × g, 30 min, 4℃) and filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane. The S1 protein was purified by incubation with Ni–NTA agarose (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) at 4℃ for 12 h, followed by washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and elution with PBS containing 500 mM imidazole. Protein purity and identity were confirmed by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), western blotting using PDCoV-positive serum as the primary antibody, and indirect ELISA. Protein concentration was determined with a Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and purified proteins were stored at − 80℃. For HRP conjugation, purified S1 was adjusted to 2 mg/mL in PBS and labeled with activated HRP using a commercial kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 37℃ for 3 h in the dark. After termination with stop solution, the conjugates were mixed with stabilizing buffer and stored at − 20℃ until use.

Establishment and optimization of DAgS‑ELISA

A checkerboard titration was performed to optimize the antigen coating concentration and dilution of the HRP-conjugated antigen. The antigen was coated at six concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 1.2 µg/mL, and the HRP-conjugated antigen was tested at seven dilutions ranging from 1:100 to 1:6400. Serum samples were diluted from 1:2 to 1:10 across five gradients using a designated serum diluent. To determine the optimal coating conditions, five time–temperature combinations were evaluated, including incubation at 37 °C for 1 to 4 h and at 4 °C for 12 h. For blocking buffer selection, the blocking efficacy of six different solutions was assessed: 1%, 3%, and 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Solarbio, Beijing, China); 5% skim milk; a commercial ELISA plate blocker; and PBST (PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20). Blocking was performed under five different conditions, including incubation at 37 °C for 1 to 4 h and at 4 °C for 12 h. The incubation times for both serum and HRP-conjugated antigen were evaluated in five gradients ranging from 30 to 90 min. Finally, optimal color development time was assessed across four time points ranging from 5 to 20 min. The optimal parameters were determined based on the highest P/N (positive/negative) value.

Determination of cut-off value, diagnostic sensitivity, and specificity by ROC analysis

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of the DAgS-ELISA, 56 PDCoV-positive and 112 PDCoV-negative porcine serum samples were tested. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA). The optimal cut-off value, as well as diagnostic sensitivity and specificity, were determined based on the ROC analysis.

Evaluation of detection limit, cross-reactivity, and reproducibility of the DAgS-ELISA

To assess the detection limit, cross-reactivity, and reproducibility of the DAgS-ELISA, serial two-fold dilutions of PDCoV-positive serum were used to determine the minimum detectable level. Cross-reactivity was evaluated using positive sera against other common swine pathogens, including PEDV, PRV, PoRV, FMDV, CSFV, PCV2, PRRSV, and TGEV. Samples were diluted 1:20 in sample diluent and processed exactly as described for DAgS-ELISA. A result was considered negative if the OD₄₅₀ was below the cut-off. Reproducibility was assessed using six serum samples tested in triplicate. Intra-assay variation was determined within a single plate, and inter-assay variation was evaluated across three independent experiments conducted on different days. The intra- and inter-assay reproducibility of the DAgS-ELISA was evaluated by calculating the coefficients of variation (CV).

Comparison with indirect immunofluorescence assay

To assess consistency with established serological methods, 157 PDCoV serum samples of unknown status were tested using both the DAgS-ELISA and IFA. IFA was performed on LLC-PK1 cells infected with PDCoV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1. Cells were fixed 24 h post-infection with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100. After blocking with 5% skim milk, serum samples diluted 1:200 were added and incubated at 37℃ for 45 min. Following washing, cells were incubated with fluorescein isothiocyanate-conjugated goat anti-pig IgG (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 30 min at 37℃ and observed under a fluorescence microscope. IFA is widely recognized as a reliable reference method for evaluating PDCoV serological status and has been extensively applied in the development and validation of PDCoV serological assays (Okda et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2022).

Neutralization assay

To evaluate the correlation between ELISA-detected antibody levels and virus-neutralizing activity, neutralization assays were performed on 48 PDCoV-positive sera. Sera were serially diluted twofold and mixed with equal volumes of PDCoV containing 100 TCID₅₀ (50% tissue culture infectious doses). After incubation at 37℃ for 1 h, the mixtures were added to LLC-PK1 monolayers in 96-well plates. Cytopathic effects (CPEs) were assessed after 3 days, and the neutralizing titer was defined as the reciprocal of the highest dilution that inhibited ≥ 50% of CPEs.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software was used to display data. Each sample was tested at least three times, and the results are presented in the form of average (X) ± SD. Reproducibility was evaluated by the CV and the degree of agreement between different methods was assessed using the kappa statistic (κ). Kappa values were interpreted as follows: κ > 0.9, excellent agreement; 0.7 ≤ κ ≤ 0.9, strong agreement; 0.5 ≤ κ < 0.7, moderate agreement; and κ < 0.5, poor agreement.

The recombinant PDCoV S1 protein was purified using Ni-affinity chromatography. Western blotting with a monoclonal antibody specific for the PDCoV S protein confirmed the identity of the purified product, showing a single immunoreactive band of the expected size, indicating successful expression of PDCoV S1 (Fig. 1B). The purified S1 protein was then immobilized on ELISA plates as the coating antigen. Establishment of the S1-based DAgS‑ELISA

Checkerboard titration was performed to determine optimal assay parameters. The ideal coating concentration of the S1 antigen was 0.6 μg/mL, and the optimal dilution of HRP-conjugated S1 protein was 1:400 (Fig. 2A). A serum dilution of 1:2 produced the highest P/N ratio (Fig. 2B). The optimal coating condition was overnight incubation 12 h at 4 °C (Fig. 2C), followed by blocking with 3% BSA at 4 °C for 12 h (Fig. 2D, E). A serum incubation period of 60 min yielded the highest P/N ratio; however, the difference between 30 and 60 min was negligible. Therefore, 30 min was selected to shorten the assay time (Fig. 2F). The optimal incubation time for the HRP-labeled antigen was 45 min (Fig. 2G), and a 10 min chromogenic reaction was sufficient for signal development (Fig. 2H). Determination of cut-off value, diagnostic sensitivity, and specificity by ROC analysis

ROC curve analysis was used to assess the diagnostic performance of the DAgS-ELISA. As shown in Fig. 3A, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.995, indicating excellent discrimination between PDCoV-positive and PDCoV-negative sera. Based on the ROC analysis, the optimal OD₄₅₀ cut-off value was 0.16, which yielded a diagnostic sensitivity of 92.86% and specificity of 99.11% (Fig. 3B). Detection limit, cross-reactivity, and reproducibility of the DAgS‑ELISA

The detection limit of the DAgS-ELISA was assessed using serial two-fold dilutions of PDCoV-positive serum. The assay could reliably detect antibodies at dilutions up to 1:2048 (OD450 = 0.23), demonstrating high sensitivity (Fig. 4A). Cross-reactivity was evaluated using positive sera against other common swine pathogens, including PEDV, PRV, PoRV, FMDV, CSFV, PCV2, PRRSV, and TGEV. All non-PDCoV samples yielded OD₄₅₀ values below the cut-off, with the highest value observed for PRRSV-positive serum (OD450 = 0.13), indicating no cross-reactivity and confirming high assay specificity (Fig. 4B). Reproducibility was assessed using six serum samples with varying OD levels, each tested in triplicate. As shown in Table 1, the mean OD values ranged from 0.29 to 3.15, with intra-assay CVs between 1.58% and 4.73% and inter-assay CVs between 2.75% and 5.00%. Concordance between ELISA and indirect immunofluorescence assay

A total of 157 serum samples were evaluated by both ELISA and IFA. Among them, 88 were IFA-positive, showing strong specific fluorescence, and 69 were IFA-negative. Using the established ELISA method, 86 samples tested positive and 71 tested negative. Of these, 84 samples were positive and 67 were negative by both methods. The overall concordance rate between ELISA and IFA was 96.18%, with a Cohen’s kappa value of 0.923, indicating excellent agreement (Table 2). Application of the ELISA in multiple animal species

As PDCoV is capable of infecting multiple species, species-specific secondary antibodies are typically required for antibody detection. However, the double-antigen sandwich ELISA is not limited by host species. To validate cross-species applicability, the assay was used to detect PDCoV antibodies in sera from pigs, chickens, mice, rabbits. The results showed that all positive sera from different species yielded OD values above the cutoff, with the lowest positive OD (rabbit serum) being 0.98, while negative sera remained below the cutoff, with the highest negative OD (chicken serum) being 0.12, indicating that the assay is broadly applicable across species and suitable for serological surveillance of PDCoV in various hosts (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Cross-species detection of PDCoV antibodies using the DAgS-ELISA. Correlation between ELISA OD values and neutralizing antibody titers

As the S1 protein contains major neutralizing epitopes of PDCoV, we investigated the correlation between ELISA OD values and virus neutralization titers using 48 PDCoV-positive sera. As illustrated in Fig. 6A, the antibody levels determined by DAgS-ELISA exhibited a significant positive correlation with the neutralizing antibody titers obtained by virus neutralization tests (VNT). Discussion

PDCoV was first identified in avian fecal samples in Hong Kong in 2012. Since then, its prevalence in pig populations has been reported in several countries, severely hindering sustainable development of the swine industry (Ma et al. 2015; Kong et al. 2022). Notably, PDCoV demonstrates cross-species transmission potential, infecting not only pigs but also chicks, calves, and cells derived from companion animals such as dogs (Kong et al. 2022; Tian et al. 2025). It has been hypothesized that PDCoV originated in wild birds, subsequently spread to sparrows, then to mammals, and may ultimately spill over to humans (Niu et al. 2021; Vlasova et al. 2021). Ji et al. (2022) resolved the crystal structures of the receptor-binding domains of the PDCoV spike protein in complex with both human aminopeptidase N (hAPN) and porcine APN (pAPN). Structural comparison revealed a high degree of similarity, with PDCoV exhibiting stronger binding affinity to hAPN than to pAPN, suggesting a considerable risk of zoonotic spillover.

The double-antigen sandwich ELISA (DAgS-ELISA), which uses both a coating antigen and an HRP-conjugated antigen, facilitates the detection of total antibodies irrespective of host species (Cordero-Ortiz et al. 2023). Compared to indirect ELISA, DAgS-ELISA generally provides enhanced specificity and sensitivity and has been widely applied in the diagnosis of infectious diseases in both humans and animals (Wang et al. 2023, 2024). In this study, we developed a DAgS-ELISA based on the S1 subunit of the PDCoV spike protein. The S1 protein was chosen due to its ability to induce more durable antibody responses and exhibit minimal cross-reactivity with other coronaviruses (Poudel et al. 2020; den Hartog et al. 2021; Yen et al. 2022). To evaluate the cross-species applicability of the assay, PDCoV-positive sera from pigs, chickens, rabbits, and mice were tested. The assay successfully detected PDCoV-specific antibodies in all tested species, supporting its utility for epidemiological surveillance and interspecies transmission studies.

Neutralizing antibodies (nAbs) play a critical role in disease progression and vaccine efficacy assessment. Although VNTs are considered the gold standard, they are time-consuming, technically demanding, and require biosafety containment, limiting their widespread use in field surveillance. ELISA offers a practical alternative for nAb assessment (Meyer et al. 2014). Amanat et al. (2020) reported a strong correlation between SARS-CoV-2 S protein-specific ELISA titers and nAb titers, underscoring the important role of ELISA in neutralizing antibody detection, particularly for highly pathogenic pathogens. Most existing ELISA assays for detecting PDCoV antibodies use the nucleocapsid protein as the target antigen, which makes them unsuitable for evaluating serum neutralizing antibody titers (Okda et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2022). Although various S protein-based ELISA methods have been developed, few studies have explored the correlation between ELISA OD values and nAb titers (Lu et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2023). Given the presence of multiple neutralizing epitopes on the S1 subunit and the strongest correlation between anti-S1 antibody levels and PEDV neutralization titers (Song et al. 2023), this study used Pearson correlation analysis to associate the ELISA results with nAb titers. A correlation coefficient of 0.865 (n = 48) was obtained, consistent with the results from a nanobody-based competitive ELISA established by Yu et al. (2025). This suggests that the DAgS-ELISA could serve as a rapid alternative to the traditional virus neutralization test for assessing neutralizing antibody levels.

The S1 protein is highly glycosylated, and proper glycosylation is essential for maintaining its native structure and antigenicity. Therefore, we expressed the S1 protein in CHO-S cells for both plate coating and HRP conjugation and optimized the DAgS-ELISA accordingly. The assay was validated using 157 porcine serum samples, showing 96.18% concordance with the IFA and a kappa value of 0.923, indicating strong agreement. The assay detected positive sera at dilutions of up to 1:2048, demonstrating high sensitivity and potential for early outbreak detection, while the absence of cross-reactivity with sera positive for other common porcine pathogens further confirmed its high specificity.

In conclusion, we developed a DAgS-ELISA based on the PDCoV S1 protein, which enables serological detection across multiple species with high sensitivity, specificity, and reproducibility. Moreover, this assay serves as a practical alternative to VNT for evaluating neutralizing antibody levels in porcine sera, providing a valuable tool for PDCoV epidemiological surveillance and vaccine efficacy assessment. Nevertheless, due to the diversity of PDCoV-susceptible species and the varying severity of infection, further validation is required before applying this assay to other species, such as wild birds, sparrows, or even humans.
