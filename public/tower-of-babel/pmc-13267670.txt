Parasympathetic overdrive as a cause of overactive bladder in a high‐fat‐diet‐induced obesity mouse model
Authors: Ayu Sugiura, Retsu Mitsui, Kyoko Miwa‐Nishimura, Hikaru Hashitani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractOveractive bladder (OAB) is a highly prevalent condition characterized by urinary urgency that negatively affects quality of life. Because obesity and metabolic syndrome (MetS) are known as major risk factors for OAB, we explored the mechanisms underlying obesity‐/MetS‐associated OAB using a high‐fat‐diet (HFD)‐induced obesity mouse model. Four‐week‐old male C57BL6 mice were subjected to either a HFD (60 kcal% fat) or a normal diet (ND, 10 kcal% fat). Cystometry and bladder afferent activity recordings in anaesthetized mice, isometric tension recordings in detrusor smooth muscle (DSM) strips and intravesical pressure recordings in isolated whole bladders were performed after 12 weeks of ND or HFD feeding. Bladder morphology was examined using histochemistry. HFD mice exhibited a MetS phenotype characterized by abdominal obesity, hyperglycaemia and insulin resistance. HFD mice developed enlarged non‐voiding contractions (NVCs) during bladder filling associated with enhanced afferent activity. Both were attenuated by intravenous administration of atropine (1 mg/kg) or pyridoxalphosphate‐6‐azophenyl‐2′,4′‐disulfonic acid (PPADS), a P2X purinoceptor antagonist (10 mg/kg), or by bilateral pelvic nerve transection. In ND mice these drugs attenuated NVCs but not afferent activity. Spontaneous or nerve‐evoked DSM contractions or transient pressure increases in isolated whole bladders were unaltered in HFD mice. DSM thickness, collagen deposition or parasympathetic nerve density in HFD bladders was unaltered. Thus the OAB phenotype in HFD mice appears to primarily results from the increased parasympathetic neural activity during the bladder storage phase. These results provide the basis of the clinical effectiveness of anti‐muscarinic agents on OAB as well as the enhanced purinergic transmission in OAB patients known as ‘atropine resistance’. Key points High‐fat‐diet (HFD)‐induced obese mice exhibiting metabolic syndrome phenotype display overactive bladder (OAB) phenotype characterized by enlarged non‐voiding contractions (NVCs) associated with bladder afferent overactivity.Both enlarged NVCs and afferent overactivity in HFD mice are reversed by intravenous administration of atropine or the P2X receptor antagonist pyridoxalphosphate‐6‐azophenyl‐2′,4′‐disulfonic acid (PPADS), or by bilateral pelvic nerve transection.Detrusor muscle strip contractility, corresponding spontaneous pressure increases in isolated whole bladder and bladder morphology are unaltered in HFD mice.The OAB phenotype in HFD‐induced obese mice primarily results from excessive parasympathetic activity during the bladder storage phase.These findings provide a basis for the clinical effectiveness of anti‐muscarinic drugs on bladder storage symptoms as well as the relevance of enhanced purinergic transmission in OAB patients known as ‘atropine resistance’. Keywords: afferent activity, bladder, obesity, overactive bladder

Introduction

Overactive bladder (OAB) characterized by urinary urgency, urinary frequency and urge incontinence is a highly prevalent condition that substantially limits work and social activity of patients. In the elderly reduced social or physical activity due to OAB symptoms would worsen sarcopenia or cognitive impairment. OAB is a multifactorial in both aetiology and pathophysiology, and therefore its causes are undetermined in many cases and patients are often diagnosed as idiopathic OAB, although comorbidity of neurogenic disorders or benign prostate hyperplasia can be defined as a cause in some cases. Nevertheless ischaemia and oxidative stress leading to whole‐body inflammation (Powers et al., 2019) have now been shown as major risk factors for lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTSs), including OAB, highlighting the importance of obesity and metabolic syndrome (MetS) in the pathogenesis of OAB (Elbaset et al., 2020; Laven et al., 2008; Richter et al., 2010).

Because high‐fat diet (HFD) consumption and reduced energy expenditure are major contributors to the development of human abdominal obesity (Galgani & Ravussin, 2008), HFD‐fed mice have been used as an obesity‐driven MetS model exhibiting abdominal obesity hyperglycaemia and hyperinsulinaemia (Surwit et al., 1995). It was demonstrated that the HFD‐induced obesity mouse model develops OAB phenotype exhibiting increased voiding frequency accompanied with augmented non‐voiding contractions (NVCs) during bladder storage phages (Leiria et al., 2012). Because NVCs arising from spontaneous phasic contractions (SPCs) of detrusor smooth muscle (DSM) mechanically stimulate intramural afferent nerves to increase their firing frequency (Heppner et al., 2016; Sugiura et al., 2022), the augmented NVCs could cause urinary urgency. Thus afferent hyperexcitability originating within the bladder wall has been suggested as a primary cause of OAB (Grundy et al., 2018; Grundy et al., 2024). β3‐adrenergic agonists that are originally designed to relax DSM also attenuate afferent nerve activity to relieve OAB symptoms (Kwon et al., 2024). In other animal models of OAB, such as partial bladder outlet obstruction (PBOO) or spinal cord injury model, increased NVC amplitude has commonly been demonstrated (Drake et al., 2003). However the pathophysiological mechanisms of obesity‐driven OAB still remain to be explored.

The augmented NVCs in obese mice could result from changes in the properties of several cell types, including DSM cells themselves, urothelium or efferent nerves releasing humoral factors or neurotransmitters that modulate DSM contractility. Increased contractility of DSM muscle is involved in the development of OAB in the PBOO model (Zheng et al., 2020), spinal cord injury model (Ikeda & Kanai, 2008) or rat chronic bladder ischaemia model (Akaihata et al., 2018), in which morphological changes in DSM such as DSM hypertrophy or fibrosis develop concurrently. Mechanical stimulation of the urothelium, for example, increased intravesical pressure or bladder overdistension, triggers the release of several urothelium‐derived substances such as ATP or ACh directly by stimulating the afferent nerves (Cockayne et al., 2000) or indirectly by enhancing DSM contractility (Birder et al., 2001), leading to afferent hyperactivity. Parasympathetic efferent nerves release ACh and ATP as co‐transmitters in small mammals to induce powerful contractions for bladder emptying (Chakrabarty et al., 2022) but also continuously release small amounts of these transmitters during the storage phase that may be involved in generating the basal DSM tone (Zagorodnyuk et al., 2009). Efferent parasympathetic input would be enhanced by increasing afferent activity in the late phase of bladder filling that enhances NVCs in normal rats (Sugiura et al., 2022). Therefore identifying the cell type which is primarily responsible for the enhanced NVCs in obesity/MetS mouse models would greatly facilitate our understandings of the pathophysiology of the resulting afferent overactivity.

This study developed the HFD‐induced obesity/MetS model mice that predominantly exhibited an OAB phenotype characterized by enlarged NVCs and increased afferent nerve activity. It was explored if anti‐cholinergic drugs or P2X purinergic receptor antagonists may ameliorate the OAB phenotype. To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying increased NVC, functional properties of contractile elements in the bladder wall of obese/MetS mice were investigated by evaluating the contractility of DSM strips or in ex vivo whole bladders where urothelium‐derived factors may modulate DSM contractility. Morphological changes in the bladders, namely DSM hypertrophy or fibrosis, if any, were also examined.

Methods

Ethical approval

The procedure described has been approved by the animal experimentation ethics at the Nagoya City University Medical School (no.: 23‐005). The authors acknowledge the ethical principles of The Journal of Physiology described in Grundy (2015) and confirm that the procedures and experiments involving animals comply with these principles. Mice were anaesthetized with urethane (0.8–1.0 g/kg i.p.; Ito et al., 2020) during the in vivo experiments. After experiments, mice were killed by cervical dislocation.

General

Male C57/BL6 mice were housed in a special temperature‐controlled room under a 12‐h light–dark cycle and had free access to food and water.

Four‐week‐old mice were divided into two groups: one group fed a normal diet (ND) containing 10% kcal as fat (Oriental Yeast, Tokyo, Japan) and the other fed a HFD containing 60% kcal as fat (D12492, Research Diet, North Brunswick, NJ, USA) groups. Their body weight and blood glucose level were measured every 4 weeks. After 12 weeks of HFD or ND feeding, mice were used for either in vivo cystometry and multiunit afferent nerve recordings or in vitro/ex vivo experiments, including isometric DSM tension recordings, intravesical pressure recordings of isolated whole bladder preparations or histological examinations. After the mice were killed bladder weight and epididymal fat mass were measured.

Measurement of metabolic parameters

Blood samples were taken from the tail vein after a 6‐h fast, and serum glucose levels were measured (using Roche Diagnostics, Tokyo, Japan). Blood samples collected were centrifuged and stored at −80°C. Serum insulin levels were determined using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay using an insulin assay kit (FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals). The homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) (serum insulin * serum glucose/22.5) index was calculated (Oliveira et al., 2021).

In vivo voiding cystometry

In vivo cystometry was conducted as previously described (Sugiura et al., 2022). Briefly under urethane (Wako) anaesthesia (0.8–1.0 g/kg i.p.; Ito et al., 2020), a poly‐ethylene catheter was inserted in the bladder in which bilateral ureters were ligated. The catheter was connected to both a pressure transducer (DX‐100, Nihon Kohden, Tokyo, Japan) and a filling pump (KDS100, KD Scientific, MA, USA). Changes in the intravesical pressure were recorded, while the bladder was filled with saline at 0.6 mL/h continuously. At the end of experiments mice were killed by decapitation.

The following parameters were measured: voiding interval (s), compliance at the start of voiding contractions (mL/mmHg), maximum voiding pressure (mmHg), threshold pressure (mmHg), NVC amplitude (mmHg) and averaged NVC frequency (min−1) over 30 min of filling phase.

In vivo bladder afferent nerve recording

In vivo bladder afferent nerve recordings were conducted simultaneously with cystometry as previously described (Sugiura et al., 2022). Briefly the left pelvic multifibre nerves containing both sensory and motor fibres proximal to the main pelvic ganglion were transected near the left L6 dorsal root and placed on a bipolar extracellular electrode for multiunit afferent nerve recording. The right pelvic nerves were also transected proximal to the pelvic ganglion in some HFD mice (‘centrally disconnected bladder’), whereas the nerves remained intact in other mice (‘centrally connected bladder’). The afferent nerve activity presented as neurogram was acquired at 20 kHz, preamplified using a low‐noise AC differential amplifier (10×), filtered between 60 and 5000 Hz and finally amplified (10,000×). Multiunit afferent nerve discharge frequency during saline‐induced bladder filling was determined using Spike2 impulse‐matching software (CED, Cambridge, UK). Action potentials that emerged above the threshold of background electrical activity were discriminated, and their frequency for 1 s was calculated as afferent firing frequency (Hz). Averaged afferent nerve firing (imp.s−1) was measured over 30 min of filling phase.

In vivo non‐voiding cystometry

Because atropine or pyridoxalphosphate‐6‐azophenyl‐2′,4′‐disulfonic acid (PPADS) prevented complete bladder emptying by attenuating voiding contractions, it was not possible to evaluate the effects of the drugs on NVCs during consecutive cycles of voiding cystometry.

To evaluate the effects of the drugs on enlarged NVCs and associated afferent bursts in HFD mice, in vivo non‐voiding cystometry and afferent recording were simultaneously carried out. The bladder was filled with saline at 0.6 mL/h for 7 min, while changes in the intravesical pressure were recorded at a sampling rate of 100 Hz and then manually evacuated by opening the catheter. Bladder filling‐evacuation cycle was set at the interval similar to that observed during voiding cystometry in HFD mice (6.2 ± 1.9 min). The cycle was consecutively repeated at 5‐min intervals until reproducible intravesical pressure changes were produced, and then atropine (1 mg/kg; Lluel et al; 2002), PPADS (10 mg/kg; McCord et al., 2008) or nifedipine (1 mg/kg; Guarneri et al., 1993) was intravenously administered via a poly‐ethylene catheter placed in the left femoral vein.

The following parameters were measured: NVC amplitude (mmHg), NVC frequency (min−1) and afferent firing (imp.s−1), which was averaged over 6 min during filling.

Isometric tension recording

Isometric tension recording was conducted as previously described (Sugiura et al, 2022). The mice were exsanguinated after cervical dislocation. The urinary bladder was removed and cut open, and the mucosa was dissected from the DSM. DSM strips were suspended in the organ baths (2 mL volume) that were superfused with warmed (36°C), oxygenated (95%O2, 5%CO2) physiological salt solution (PSS) at a flow rate of 2 mL/min. After SPCs were produced electrical field stimulation (EFS) (1 s, 10 Hz, 50 µs pulse duration) was applied every 3 min. At the end of the experiments, 40 mm KCl‐containing solution induced a maximal contraction, and the amplitude of SPCs and EFS‐induced contractions was normalized to this maximal force induced by the 40 mm KCl solution.

The following parameters of SPCs and/or nerve‐evoked contractions were measured: baseline tension (mg), peak amplitude (mg) and frequency (min−1). All of these parameters were averaged over 5 min for SPCs and 15 min for nerve‐evoked contractions.

Ex vivo intravesical pressure recording

Isolated whole bladders with ligated bilateral ureters were cannulated by a poly‐ethylene catheter through the bladder dome and placed in the organ bath (2 mL volume) that was superfused with warmed (36°C), oxygenated (95% O2, 5% CO2) PSS at a flow rate of 2 mL/min. The catheter was connected to both a pressure transducer (DX‐100, Nihon Kohden) and a filling pump (KDS100, KD Scientific). The bladder was filled with PSS at a rate of 0.06 mL/min (Daly et al., 2014), until an intravesical pressure of ∼10 mmHg was established, equivalent to the threshold voiding pressure in in vivo cystometry. After 1 h of equilibration period, small transient pressure rises (TPRs) were developed and recorded at a sampling rate of 100 Hz.

Histochemistry

The bladder tissues fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4°C were washed in PBS. The fixed bladder was cut along the midline; one half was embedded in paraffin for haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson's trichrome staining (collagen staining), and the other half was embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and frozen at −80°C to make cryosections for immunohistochemistry. Conventional methods of H&E staining and Masson's trichrome staining were used. The median sagittal sections of bladder (3 µm thickness) were used for these staining protocols. ImageJ was used to analyse the ratio of collagen‐stained areas in the detrusor.

The cholinergic nerves in the detrusor were visualized using immunohistochemistry for vesicular acetylcholine transporter (VAChT) and α‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA), using the median sagittal cryosections of the bladder (10 µm thickness). The protocol of immunohistochemical staining was described previously (Sugiura et al., 2022). Antibodies and related reagents used were as follows: rabbit anti‐VAChT antibody (1:400, Almone Labs, Jerusalem, Israel), mouse anti‐α‐SMA antibody (1:1000, clone 1A4, Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), biotinylated swine anti‐rabbit IgG antibody (1:300, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark), Alexa488‐conjugated streptavidin (10 µg/mL, Molecular Probes) and Cy3‐conjugated goat anti‐mouse IgG antibody (2.5 µg/mL, Merck‐Chemicon, Darmstadt, Germany). A confocal laser scanning microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was used to obtain stacked images (4 micrographs with 1 µm intervals). ImageJ was used to analyse the area of VAChT immunoreactivities. Three sets of images were taken from each mouse, and the averaged values were used for statistical analysis.

Solution and drugs

The composition of PSS was as follows (in mM): Na+, 137.5; K+, 5.9; Ca2+, 2.5; Mg2+, 1.2; HCO−, 15.5; H2PO4 −, 1.2; Cl−, 134; glucose, 11.5. The pH of the PSS was 7.2 when bubbled with 95% oxygen and 5% carbon dioxide, and the measured pH of the organ bath solution was ∼7.4. The drugs used were atropine (Sigma), PPADS (Sigma) and nifedipine (sigma). Atropine and PPADS were dissolved in distilled water, whereas nifedipine was dissolved in 100% ethanol. The final concentration of these solvents did not exceed 1:1000.

Data and statistical analysis

Measured values were expressed as mean ± SD (n = number of animals). Statistical significance was tested using paired or unpaired Student's t test or one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparison. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical association was tested using Pearson's correlation analysis.

Results

Changes in body weight, blood glucose and insulin resistance

HFD mice (n = 27) had a higher body weight compared to ND mice (n = 25) (at 4 weeks: P = 0.273, at 8 weeks: P < 0.001, at 12 weeks: P < 0.001, at 16 weeks: P < 0.001; Fig. 1A ; Table 1). Blood glucose level was also higher in HFD mice than ND mice (at 4 weeks: P = 0.849, at 8 weeks: P < 0.001, at 12 weeks: P < 0.001, at 16 weeks: P < 0.001; Fig. 1B ; Table 1). In addition HFD mice (n = 9 at 16 weeks) had a larger amount of epididymal fat mass (P < 0.001), a higher serum insulin level (P < 0.001) and a HOMA‐IR index (P < 0.001) compared to ND mice (n = 7) (Table 1). However bladder weight of HFD mice was not significantly different from that of ND mice (P = 0.549) (Table 1), indicating that HFD feeding for 12 weeks developed MetS phenotype without bladder hypertrophy.

HFD mice exhibited OAB phenotype and afferent overactivity

In both ND and HFD mice, a cycle of bladder filling and voiding was induced by continuous saline infusion into the bladder. NVCs defined as TPRs exceeding 0.1 mmHg were developed in the filling phase. Unlike simultaneous cystometry and bladder afferent recording in normal rats in which individual NVCs were associated with afferent firing bursts (Sugiura et al., 2022), NVCs in ND mice did not correspond to afferent surges (Fig. 2A and C ). Larger NVCs in HFD mice had a temporal correlation with afferent surges (Fig. 2B and D ).

The histograms of NVC amplitudes of HFD mice contained more large‐amplitude events compared with that of ND mice (Fig. 3A ). The averaged amplitude of pooled NVCs was greater in HFD mice (n = 499 NVCs in 9 animals) than in ND mice (n = 401 NVCs in 6 animals) (Fig. 3B ). Because the proportion of NVC ≧1 mmHg per individual in HFD mice (39.51 ± 19.74%, n = 9) was significantly higher than that in ND mice (15.87 ± 15.17%, n = 6) (P = 0.0113), normal bladder phenotype (average of NVCs < 1 mmHg) and OAB phenotype (average of NVCs ≥1 mmHg) were defined. Normal bladder phenotype was also characterized by regular voiding intervals (Fig. 2A ), whereas voiding intervals were irregular and shortened in the OAB phenotype (Fig. 2B ). ND mice predominantly exhibited normal bladder phenotype (n = 5/6), whereas OAB phenotype was dominant in HFD mice (n = 8/9).

Effects of atropine and PPADS on OAB phenotype in HFD mice

Because atropine or PPADS prevented complete bladder emptying by attenuating voiding contractions during consecutive cycles of voiding cystometry, it was not possible to assess the effect of the drugs on NVCs. Therefore the roles of endogenous ACh and ATP in developing enlarged NVCs in HFD mice were investigated using in vivo ‘non‐voiding’ cystometry. In HFD mice exhibiting OAB phenotype (n = 9), large NVCs were produced during bladder filling (Fig. 5A ). Each large NVC was associated with a surge in afferent nerve firing in the late phase of bladder filling as reported previously (Heppner et al., 2016; Sugiura et al., 2022; Fig. 5A ).

Atropine (1 mg/kg body weight) suppressed NVCs and afferent surges (Fig. 5A ), reducing NVC amplitude (P = 0.0029; Fig. 5B ) without changing their frequency (P = 0.631; Fig. 5C ). Atropine also attenuated afferent nerve firing (P = 0.00100; Fig. 5D ). Subsequent administration of the P2X purinoceptor antagonist PPADS (10 mg/kg body weight) further reduced NVC amplitude (P = 0.0237 vs. atropine, P = 0.00200 vs. control; Fig. 5B ) without changing their frequency (P = 0.717 vs. atropine, P = 0.287 vs. control; Fig. 5C ). Subsequent administration of PPADS also attenuated afferent firing (P = 0.0258 vs. atropine, P = 0.000300 vs. control; Fig. 5D ). These results suggested that ACh released from parasympathetic efferent nerves and/or urothelium accounts for the enlarged NVCs involved in the development of OAB phenotype in HFD mice.

PPADS (10 mg/kg body weight) alone suppressed NVCs and afferent surges (Fig. 6A ). PPADS reduced the amplitude of NVCs (P = 0.00930; Fig. 6B ) without changing their frequency (P = 0.186; Fig. 6C ) and diminished afferent nerve firing (P = 0.00530; Fig. 6D ). Subsequent administration of atropine (1 mg/kg body weight) further reduced the NVC amplitude (P = 0.0331 vs. PPADS, P = 0.000400 vs. control; Fig. 6B ) without changing their frequency (P = 0.267 vs. PPADS, P = 0.676 vs. control; Fig. 6C ). Subsequent administration of atropine also attenuated afferent firing (P = 0.0230 vs. PPADS, P < 0.0001 vs. control; Fig. 6D ). These results suggested that ATP released from the parasympathetic efferent nerves and/or urothelium was also capable of enhancing NVCs to develop OAB phenotype in HFD mice.

Because ACh released from the urothelium and/or intramural parasympathetic nerves appears not to enhance DSM contractility in HFD bladder, the fundamental roles of parasympathetic efferent input originating from the spinal cord in the development of enlarged NVCs in HFD mice were investigated using in vivo ‘non‐voiding’ cystometry.

In HFD mice exhibiting OAB phenotype (n = 5; Fig. 11A ), right pelvic nerve transections greatly suppressed the enlarged NVCs and associated afferent nerve surges (Fig. 11B ). Afferent nerve firing was also attenuated in ‘centrally disconnected’ HFD bladders (P = 0.00481; Fig. 11E ).

Discussion

In this study a HFD‐induced obese mouse model exhibiting OAB phenotype was established without any evidence of morphological changes within the bladder wall. HFD mice exhibited enhanced NVCs and associated afferent nerve overactivity. Both enhanced NVCs and afferent overactivity were attenuated by the intravenous administration of atropine or PPADS, or bilateral pelvic nerve transections. However in vitro studies revealed that neither spontaneous nor nerve‐evoked contractions in DSM strips or transient bladder pressure increases in isolated whole bladder preparations were altered in HFD mice. Thus the OAB phenotype in HFD‐induced obese mice appears to primarily result from an excessive activation of parasympathetic efferent nerves. Parasympathetic overdrive would enlarge NVCs to trigger afferent hyperactivity, whereas afferent hyperactivity could induce parasympathetic overdrive via the spinal micturition centre, forming a self‐perpetuating efferent‐DSM‐afferent cycle.

Characteristics of HFD‐induced obese OAB model

Consistent with previous studies of HFD‐induced obesity models (Leiria et al., 2012), HFD mice in the present study developed increases in body weight, epididymal fat mass and fasting blood glucose levels associated with insulin resistance and therefore well‐representing human MetS. Other OAB models, including the rat bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) model (Zheng et al., 2020), rat spinal cord transection (SCT) (Ikeda & Kanai, 2008) or rat chronic bladder ischaemia (Akaihata et al. 2018), exhibited extensive bladder remodelling, for example, smooth muscle hypertrophy or increased connective tissue deposition, suggesting that they represent relatively severe pathological changes in the bladder. In the present study there is no significant difference in bladder weight between ND and HFD mice. Moreover neither detrusor muscle hypertrophy nor fibrosis was evident in the bladders of HFD mice. Thus HFD‐induced obese OAB can be considered as a milder OAB model that may represent the pathogenies of a larger population of OAB patients who have no comorbidity such as BOO or spinal code injury.

Roles of NVCs in HFD‐induced OAB phenotype

Epidemiological studies in Sweden have demonstrated a strong correlation between human obesity and OAB (Laven et al., 2008; Richter et al., 2010; Elbaset et al., 2020). HFD mice exhibited enlarged NVCs, shortened voiding intervals and reduced bladder compliance, whereas maximal voiding pressure or voiding threshold pressure was unaltered, indicating that bladder storage and not voiding function was preferentially disturbed. Because NVCs are associated with bursts of afferent nerve activity (Heppner et al., 2016; Sugiura et al., 2022), enlarged NVCs would play a critical role in developing OAB. In this study NVCs were classified into two types. Averaged NVCs <1 mmHg were considered as ‘normal phenotype’, whereas averaged NVCs >1 mmHg were defined as ‘OAB phenotype’. Based on this classification it was revealed that ND mice predominantly exhibited normal bladder phenotype (n = 5/6), whereas OAB phenotype was dominant in HFD mice (n = 8/9). This is consistent with previous reports of increased NVCs and voiding frequency under urethane anaesthesia in C57/BL6 mice after 10 weeks of HFD feeding (Leiria et al., 2012). In contrast a previous study reported no difference in bladder function in conscious C57/BL6 mice after 20 weeks of HFD feeding (Aizawa et al., 2013), although NVCs as an indicator of OAB were not evaluated. We have no clear explanation for the different bladder phenotypes among HFD‐induced obesity mouse models. The HFD used in the present study (D12492, Research Diets, North Brunswick, NJ, USA) and the one utilized by Aizawa et al. (2013) (HFD32, CLEA Japan) had similar fat energy percentages, but their primary fat sources were different, lard (D12492) compared with powdered beef tallow and safflower oil (HFD32). Therefore the difference in fat composition may account for variations in lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses.

Roles of parasympathetic overdrive in HFD‐induced OAB

Unlike the human bladder where parasympathetic transmission is exclusively mediated by ACh, parasympathetic nerve excitation of other species induces both ATP‐mediated P2×1 receptor activation and ACh‐mediated M3 receptor activation in their DSM (Chakrabarty et al., 2022). Therefore both atropine and PPADS attenuated the enlarged NVCs and reduced the frequency of afferent firing in HFD‐induced OAB mice. Both atropine and PPADS alone almost halved the NVC amplitudes, suggesting that enhanced cholinergic and purinergic inputs nearly equally contribute to OAB development in HFD mice. In ND mice in which small NVCs and low‐frequency afferent firing were generated, atropine or PPADS were still capable of attenuating NVCs, suggesting that ACh and ATP enhance intrinsic DSM contractility even under normal physiological conditions. However consistent with the lack of temporal correlation between small NVCs and afferent surges, neither atropine nor PPADS diminished afferent nerve activity. Because no difference in contractility of DSM strips was observed between ND and HFD in vitro, the enhanced NVCs observed in vivo appears to result from changes in parasympathetic nerve activity but not in the intrinsic contractile properties of DSM. This notion was further supported by the finding that bilateral pelvic nerve transections attenuated enhanced NVCs and afferent overactivity to levels similar to those in ND mice, indicating that the enlarged NVCs in HFD mice are primarily attributable to excessive parasympathetic release of ACh and ATP.

Parasympathetic nerve activity is often considered to be largely attenuated during bladder storage phases; however it has been suggested that ACh and ATP are released to maintain basal DSM contractility (Zagorodnyuk et al., 2009). In accordance with this notion our previous study in normal rats demonstrated that NVCs are greatly suppressed by atropine (Sugiura et al., 2022). In the present study NVCs in HFD mice were only halved by atropine, suggesting that neurotransmitters enhancing NVCs may vary among species. Alternatively the relative contribution of ATP to NVC enhancement may be increased in pathological bladders as in human OAB bladders in which ‘atropine resistance’ become evident.

Roles of neuronal and urothelial ACh in HFD‐induced OAB

In vivo studies show that atropine suppressed NVCs without changing bladder compliance (0.00756 ± 0.00157 mL/mmHg in control vs. 0.00830 ± 0.00129 mL/mmHg in the presence of atropine, P = 0.0987). In contrast in isolated whole bladder preparations, atropine did not change TPR amplitudes but reduced the basal tone in both ND and HFD bladders. Urothelial‐derived non‐neuronal ACh is thought to be involved in the pathogenesis of OAB (Hanna‐Mitchell et al., 2007). Because TTX does not significantly affect basal tone or phasic contractions in DSM strips (Heppner et al., 2024), urothelial ACh may predominantly contribute to the DSM basal tone in whole bladder preparations. Nevertheless TPRs in isolated whole bladders were not different between ND and HFD mice, suggesting that neither ACh nor ATP released from the urothelium or intramural nerves can account for the enlarged in vivo NVCs.

The DSM receives dense parasympathetic innervation (Zagorodnyuk et al., 2009). In the pig bladder the inner DSM muscle bundles develop tonic contractions with low force, whereas the outer DSM muscle bundles produce phasic contractions with higher force (Pel JJ, et al., 2005). Inner and outer smooth muscle layers can also be defined in the mouse bladder and may have different contractile properties. Presumably the urothelium‐derived ACh would preferentially act on the inner DSM layer due to its anatomical proximity (Hanna‐Mitchell AT et al., 2007), and thus may predominantly contribute to the basal tone in the whole bladder preparations during bladder filling. Neurally released ACh would be larger in vivo compared to ex vivo isolated bladders due to a functional innervation to the bladder. It is likely that neuronal ACh‐induced enhancement of DSM contractility is more evident in the outer DSM due to its higher intrinsic phasic contractility compared to the inner DSM, resulting in the preferential suppression of in vivo NVCs with atropine.

Changes within the bladder wall in HFD‐induced OAB mice

In vitro studies demonstrated no significant differences between ND and HFD groups in their DSM contractions, be they spontaneous or induced by EFS or high KCl saline. This is in contrast to recent studies reporting enhanced DSM contractility in HFD‐induced obese animals due to an increased protein kinase C (PKC) activity and altered large conductance calcium‐activated potassium channel (BK channel) properties (Leiria et al., 2012; Li et al., 2019). HFD feeding may have different effects on detrusor muscle contractility depending on feeding duration, housing environment, animal species or strains. In addition individual parameters of obesity could differently affect bladder function. In obese humans focal bladder wall fat distribution, higher visceral fat surface area and higher visceral fat index have a higher correlation with total Overactive Bladder Symptom Score (OABSS) and urodynamic findings compared with measures of body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC) (Elbaset et al., 2020).

Unaltered DSM contractility in HFD‐induced OAB bladder also differed from the findings in the rat BOO model (Zheng et al., 2020) and rat SCT (Ikeda & Kanai, 2008) exhibiting enhanced DSM contractility or rat chronic bladder ischaemia (Akaihata et al., 2018) showing weakened DSM contraction (in chronic bladder ischaemia). It is known that obesity/MetS significantly increases the risk of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) (Parsons et al., 2009), a risk factor for OAB due to outlet obstruction. However the lack of a significant difference in maximal voiding pressure in obese mice in the present study suggests that their bladder did not suffer from outlet obstruction. Thus HFD‐induced OAB may primarily result from enhanced parasympathetic activity without inducing other causes of OAB.

Causes of parasympathetic overdrive

Because obesity is known to be associated with excessive sympathetic nerve activation causing damage to various organs, particularly those of the cardiovascular system (Grassi et al., 2019), parasympathetic overdrive as a cause of OAB in HFD mice is apparently contradictory. However heart rate variability (HRV) analysis has demonstrated increased parasympathetic activity in adult male OAB patients (Karasu & Oruç, et al., 2023) and paediatric OAB patients (Demir AD, et al., 2016). Moreover dynamic pupillometry has demonstrated excessive parasympathetic activation in female patients with idiopathic OAB (Aydogmus, et al., 2017).

Chronic inflammation and ischaemia associated with obesity may alter the functional properties of peripheral afferent nerves, promoting excessive efferent parasympathetic activation. For example spinal cord infarction in cats increases nerve growth factor (NGF), sensitizing C‐fibres and strengthening the micturition reflex pathway, leading to neurogenic detrusor overactivity (NDO) (de Groat et al., 1983). NGF is also known to increase in cystitis and ischaemia models, where it sensitizes afferent nerves and contributes to the development of OAB (Ochodnický et al., 2011). A previous study reported that NGF was increased in male Wistar rats after 12 weeks of HFD consumption (Adedeji & Olapade‐Olaopa, 2018), suggesting that NGF‐mediated changes in afferent nerves may contribute to OAB in obesity. Furthermore it has been shown that HFD‐fed rats have increased expression of TRPV1 channels in C‐type primary afferent nerves (Rahman et al., 2007) that plays an important role in pain perception and micturition reflex control, suggesting that the TRPV1/C fibre pathway may play a potential role in OAB. Thus in addition to systemic parasympathetic activity, the enhanced bladder afferent observed in this study may locally enhance parasympathetic efferent activity at the spinal level, leading to a vicious cycle of efferent‐DSM‐afferent activation.

Limitations and future directions

In the present study intravesical pressure seldom exceeded 10 mmHg. Thus it is likely that only signals from low‐threshold afferent fibres that are activated below 15 mmHg (Daly et al., 2014) were detected; even afferent nerve activity was subclassified into two to four individual afferent nerve signals in both ND mice and HFD mice. Therefore additional experiments beyond the scope of this study involving non‐physiological filling conditions to develop higher intravesical pressure would be required to investigate if properties of high‐threshold afferent fibres may also be changed in HFD mice.

Unlike in vivo rat bladder where a tight correlation between NVCs and afferent firing was evident (Sugiura et al., 2022), only the larger NVCs and not the smaller NVCs were associated with afferent bursts in mouse bladders. This is presumably because afferent nerve activity generated in response to small NVCs was not efficiently detected by the bipolar extracellular electrode due to a small interface between afferent fibres and the electrodes. Nevertheless a correlation between the average NVC amplitude and afferent activity was demonstrated. Ex vivo afferent activity recordings using suction electrodes (Heppner et al., 2016) have an advantage when examining the relationship between NVCs and afferent nerve activity more precisely. However we employed in vivo bipolar electrode recordings to evaluate the influence of efferent input from the spinal cord as well as the effects of systemic administration of the drugs.

Increased insulin resistance can lead to ischaemic damage due to endothelial dysfunction and reduced nitric oxide release from the endothelium (Hill et al., 2021). The possibility of ischaemic changes affecting bladder function in HFD mice needs further evaluation by assessing vascular contractility. Ex vivo whole bladder preparations may not fully replicate the urothelial function observed under in vivo conditions, because the urothelial barrier function can be affected by mechanical or chemical injury, hormonal changes and changes in blood flow (Anderson et al., 2003). Previous studies have shown that large NVCs were observed in rat bladders where the micturition reflex pathway was intact, but not in bladders where both pelvic nerves were transected (Sugiura et al., 2022), suggesting that the potentiation of NVCs is attributable to efferent input.

Therapeutic potential

Micturition in mice and rats are induced by activation of ACh and ATP (Yoshida, et al., 2001), whereas micturition in normal humans is produced almost completely by the activation of muscarinic receptors (Bayliss et al., 1999). This differing response can be explained by the high activity of ecto‐ATPase, which degrades ATP, near the neuromuscular junction in normal adult bladder tissue, rather than by variations in ATP release (McCarthy et al., 2019). However in humans a purinergic (atropine‐resistant) component of detrusor contraction emerges in the elderly (Yoshida et al., 2001) and in patients with idiopathic OAB (Chakrabarty et al., 2022) due to reduced ecto‐ATPase function. In the OAB treatment anti‐muscarinic agents exert their actions by antagonizing DSM muscarinic receptors that are vigorously activated by neurally released ACh during voiding (Andersson, 2004). However despite the common belief that parasympathetic activity is greatly suppressed or even absent during the storage phase (Morrison J, et al., 2002), anti‐muscarinic agents should primarily act during the storage phase to reduce urinary urgency and increase bladder capacity. This study revealed that parasympathetic activity increases during the bladder filling phase in obesity‐associated OAB, providing experimental evidence for the effectiveness of anti‐cholinergic agents as well as purinergic receptor antagonists for the pharmacological treatment of bladder storage symptoms. Because anti‐muscarinics have several adverse effects such as constipation or dry mouth (Andersson, 2004) resulting in a diminished adherence, purinergic receptor antagonists may hold potential as a therapeutic alternative.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the HFD‐induced obesity/MetS mouse model exhibited an OAB phenotype. The enlarged NVCs and increased afferent nerve activity appear to result from an excessive parasympathetic efferent input from the spinal cord, rather than enhanced DSM contractility or excessive releases of DSM‐contracting substances from either the urothelium or intramural efferent nerves. Our findings may bring a renewed recognition of targeting the parasympathetic overdrive as a convincing therapeutic strategy for bladder storage symptoms in obesity‐related OAB.
