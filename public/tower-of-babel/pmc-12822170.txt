Comparison of transanal total mesorectal excision and robotic total mesorectal excision for rectal cancer: an inverse probability of treatment weighting analysis
Authors: Guan-Yu Chen, Yen-Lin Yu, Yueh-Chen Lin, Yu-Jen Hsu, Yih-Jong Chern, Pao-Shiu Hsieh, Jy-Ming Chiang, Jeng-Fu You, Chun-Kai Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBoth transanal total mesorectal excision (TaTME) and robotic total mesorectal excision (robotic TME) have been developed to optimize mesorectal dissection quality in rectal cancer surgery. However, current evidence remains inconclusive regarding the comparative advantages between these two minimally invasive approaches.Materials and methodsPatients who underwent curative TaTME or robotic TME for rectal cancer at our institution from 2015 to 2021 were retrospectively reviewed. The primary outcomes were circumferential resection margin (CRM) involvement and oncological outcomes (local recurrence, disease-free survival, and overall survival). Secondary outcomes included operative time, blood loss, conversion rate, postoperative complications, and functional recovery. To adjust for baseline differences, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was employed.ResultsA total of 174 patients were included in the final analysis, comprising 110 who underwent TaTME and 64 who underwent robotic TME. While operative duration was comparable, the TaTME group exhibited increased intraoperative blood loss and higher rates of diverting stoma (72.6% vs. 51.7%, p < 0.001) and hand-sewn anastomosis (43.6% vs. 24.1%, p < 0.001) than the robotic TME group. Minor complications (Clavien-Dindo grade I) were more frequent in the TaTME group, though major morbidity was similarly low in both cohorts. Pathologically, TaTME was associated with a greater incidence of positive circumferential resection margins (CRM) (6.1% vs. 0%, p = 0.001) and shorter distal margins. Long-term oncological endpoints, including local recurrence, distant metastasis, overall survival, and disease-free survival were statistically similar.ConclusionsDespite certain technical drawbacks observed in TaTME, such as increased blood loss and CRM positivity, both techniques demonstrated equivalent long-term oncologic safety, indicating their appropriateness in selected patients undergoing rectal cancer resection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04156-8.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common malignancy worldwide, with over 1.9 million new cases and 904,000 deaths reported in 2022, accounting for nearly 10% of all cancer diagnoses and deaths. Rectal cancer represents about 38% of CRC cases [1]. Treatment of rectal cancer requires a multifaceted approach that includes radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and surgery. Although recent data have revealed that total neoadjuvant therapy has become the new standard of care, surgery remains the primary treatment for most cases of locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) [2–4]. Total mesorectal excision (TME) has become the standard approach for rectal cancer treatment since it was introduced by Dr. Heald in 1986 [5]. Achieving a high-quality TME specimen with an adequate circumferential resection margin (CRM) is critical for oncological outcomes [6]. With advances in minimally invasive surgery (MIS), laparoscopic TME has become widely adopted, offering faster recovery and reduced morbidity without compromising cancer control [7–10]. However, performing TME can be more challenging in patients with obesity, a narrow pelvis, male sex, bulky tumors, or low-lying tumors, potentially resulting in suboptimal specimen quality and an increased surgical conversion rate [11]. To overcome these difficulties, transanal TME (TaTME) was developed as a “bottom-up” approach. In recent decades, TaTME combined with MIS has shown promising results and gained increasing popularity [11–13]. Large-scale observational studies have shown that TaTME leads to better short-term patient outcomes, yields high-quality specimens, and ensures oncological safety. This is evidenced by 2-year local recurrence rates of 4.0–4.8%, with most recurrences being unifocal [14, 15]. Although some reports have indicated an unexpectedly higher local recurrence rate and instances of multifocal growth patterns following TaTME, two recent randomized controlled trials and a well-conducted phase II study have demonstrated its safety and found it to be non-inferior to laparoscopic TME when performed by experienced surgeons [16–18].

Robotic surgery, with its three-dimensional vision and articulated instruments, offers another solution to the technical challenges of pelvic dissection. The international multicenter ROLLAR trial, which compared robotic and laparoscopic rectal cancer surgeries, did not demonstrate the advantage of the robotic approach in terms of conversion rates, short-term outcomes, or functional outcomes. However, one criticism of this trial was that some surgeons had not yet achieved the same level of mastery in laparoscopic surgery. The advantage of reducing the risk of conversion to open surgery could have been more substantial if the trial accounted for learning effects [19, 20]. A recent randomized controlled trial conducted in China showed that robotic surgery led to better CRMs and a lower conversion rate than conventional laparoscopic surgery, with fewer complications and improved postoperative recovery [21]. Updated reports have also indicated reduced locoregional recurrence and improved disease-free survival in the robotic surgery group [22]. These findings support the effectiveness of robotic surgery for the treatment of rectal cancer. Despite these advances, direct comparisons between laparoscopic TaTME and robotic TME remain scarce. To address this gap, we evaluated the clinical and oncological outcomes of TaTME versus robotic TME in patients with rectal cancer treated at a high-volume referral center.

Materials and methods

Ethics statements

The study protocol received approval from the institutional review board (IRB number: 202200885B0, dated June 20, 2022). Given the retrospective design and use of anonymized data, the need for informed consent was waived. All procedures complied with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design and population

This retrospective study included patients with rectal cancer who underwent restorative proctectomy at Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (Linkou), a high-volume tertiary center performing over 200 rectal resections each year, between January 2015 and December 2021.

The study population was defined on the basis of the following inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible participants had histologically confirmed rectal adenocarcinoma with tumors located within 15 cm of the anal verge as determined by sigmoidoscopy. Our institution adopted laparoscopic surgery in 2008, with surgeons in training following a structured program to acquire step-by-step experience [23]. Robotic surgery was introduced in 2010, and pure TaTME was implemented in 2014. In the year preceding the adoption of TaTME, transanal abdominal transanal (TATA) procedures were commonly performed for patients with difficult rectal cancers. During the study period, laparoscopic TME remained the most frequently performed procedure at our institution. However, the present analysis focused on TaTME and robotic TME, as these represent contemporary minimally invasive techniques with distinct technical advantages in challenging rectal cancer cases. To minimize potential confounding from historical practice patterns and learning curve effects, this study was restricted to comparisons between robotic TME and TaTME. The exclusion criteria were patients requiring open surgical approaches, those not undergoing tumor resection, those who underwent elective proctectomy via conventional laparoscopic TME, those with preoperative stage IV disease, and those undergoing elective proctectomy using robotic TaTME. Assessment and treatment protocol

All patients underwent standard preoperative assessments, including digital rectal examination, colonoscopy with biopsy, computed tomography (CT) of the chest and abdomen or pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Staging was determined using the 8th edition of the TNM classification by the Union for International Cancer Control. The tumor location was measured by rigid sigmoidoscopy as the level of distal tumor from the anal verge. Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels were measured routinely. Multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussions guided the decision-making process for neoadjuvant therapy. TaTME was preferentially performed for very low tumors (< 5 cm from the anal verge), bulky lesions, challenging pelvic anatomy, or situations in which enhanced distal visualization and control were anticipated to provide an advantage. Robotic TME was generally selected for standard mid-low rectal tumors, particularly when the benefits of minimally invasive pelvic dissection were expected. Laparoscopic TME remained the institutional default during the earlier study years and was performed when robotic systems were unavailable. All procedures were carried out by 11 fellowship-trained colorectal surgeons. TaTME was exclusively performed by 4 surgeons who had completed formal certification, including structured training and proctored cases, whereas most surgeons performed robotic TME, and all had substantial experience in minimally invasive rectal surgery. Operations involving trainees were included only when performed under direct supervision. A dual-team approach was utilized during TaTME, with a specialized team managing the transanal phase.

For the anastomosis, a double-stapling technique (DST) was used as the standard approach. In lower rectal tumors where distal transection with a linear stapler was challenging and required transanal division, a single-stapling technique (SST) was performed when the rectal stump could be closed securely with a purse-string suture. If the rectal stump was too short to permit purse-string placement, a hand-sewn coloanal anastomosis was performed. Our institutional policy was to consider a diverting stoma when the anastomosis was ≤ 5 cm from the anal verge, when the total mesorectal excision was technically difficult, after neoadjuvant therapy, or when the operating surgeon judged the risk of anastomotic leakage to be increased. However, diverting stomas were not used routinely and were largely determined by surgeon experience and preference. No patients received immunotherapy during the study period.

Data collection

Electronic medical records were reviewed to extract demographic and clinical data. Preoperative variables included age, gender, body mass index (BMI), serum albumin, and CEA levels. Surgical characteristics, such as blood loss, stoma formation, anastomotic technique, and conversion to open surgery, were recorded. Pathological assessments included tumor stage, histologic subtype and grade, lymphovascular and perineural invasion, margin status, and lymph node yield. A positive margin, for both the CRM and DRM, was defined as a tumor-to-margin distance of less than 0.1 cm. Postoperative outcomes encompassed complication rates (graded via the Clavien-Dindo system), length of hospital stays, mortality, and oncologic endpoints: overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), local recurrence (LR), and distant metastasis (DM). The primary outcomes were CRM involvement and oncological outcomes (local recurrence, disease-free survival, and overall survival). Secondary outcomes included operative time, blood loss, conversion rate, postoperative complications, and functional recovery.

Follow-up

Patients were followed according to National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines [24]. Outpatient visits were scheduled every 3 to 6 months during the first 3 years following primary tumor resection, then annually thereafter. Each visit included a physical examination, CEA testing, and appropriate imaging studies. Annual surveillance also included chest-abdomen-pelvis CT scans and colonoscopies for up to 5 years, with earlier evaluations performed if recurrence was suspected. All follow-up data were recorded in the Colorectal Section Tumor Registry. The date of first recurrence was defined as the earliest confirmed diagnosis of LR and/or DM, based on histopathological findings, subsequent surgical procedures, or radiological imaging. OS was calculated from the date of surgery to death or the last follow-up. DFS was defined as the time from surgery to the first documented recurrence, death, or last follow-up. The final follow-up date was May 18, 2024.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as median with interquartile range and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequency and percentage, and comparisons were made using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. To account for baseline imbalances between the TaTME and robotic TME groups, we first applied inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on the propensity score to estimate the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) across the entire study population [25]. This approach provides a population-level comparison reflecting the expected effect if all patients had equal probability of receiving either treatment. Propensity scores were calculated via logistic regression, incorporating relevant preoperative variables, such as the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, sex, tumor location, pathological T stage, N stage, and whether neoadjuvant therapy was administered. Covariate balance in the weighted sample was assessed using standardized mean differences, with a target threshold of ± 0.10 [26]. The T stage was the only covariate that did not meet this threshold; however, the chi-square test revealed no significant difference between the groups, allowing it to be included in the analysis (supplement Fig. 1). Recognizing that TaTME is primarily indicated for low rectal cancers, we additionally performed a propensity-score matching (PSM) analysis as a sensitivity test to estimate the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT). A 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement and a caliper width of 0.2 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score were used. The PSM results are presented in Supplementary Tables 1–5. Survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan–Meier curves, and the effect of the surgical procedure was estimated using Cox proportional hazards models weighted by IPTW. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and all analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (version 26; IBM Corp., New York, NY, USA) and R Statistical Software (version 4.4.1; R Core Team 2024, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics before and after IPTW adjustment

In total, 174 patients who underwent restorative surgery for rectal cancer during the study period were included in the analysis. Of these, 110 patients underwent TaTME and 64 underwent robotic TME. Table 1 presents the patients’ baseline characteristics and preoperative clinicopathological results before and after IPTW adjustment in the two groups. Notably, the TaTME group had a higher proportion of patients with lower rectal cancer and those who received neoadjuvant therapy than the robotic TME group. Following IPTW adjustment, 179 TaTME events were compared with 174 robotic TME events, which resulted in well-balanced baseline characteristics between the groups. Except for the type of neoadjuvant treatment (p = 0.003), there were no significant differences in age, sex, BMI, ASA scores, CEA levels, albumin levels, or tumor characteristics between the groups.

Operative parameters and postoperative outcomes

The operative parameters are presented in Table 2. The median operative times were similar between the TaTME and robotic TME groups. However, TaTME was associated with significantly higher intraoperative blood loss, with 33.5% of patients in the TaTME group experiencing blood loss ≥ 100 mL compared with 16.1% in the robotic TME group. A greater proportion of patients in the TaTME group underwent diverting stoma creation (72.6% versus [vs.] 51.7%, p < 0.001) and natural orifice specimen extraction procedures than those in the robotic TME group (74.3% vs. 46%, p < 0.001). Additionally, hand-sewn anastomosis was more frequently performed in the TaTME group than in the robotic TME group (43.6% vs. 24.1%, p < 0.001). There were no conversions in the TaTME group, and only one case of conversion in the robotic LAR group was due to severe adhesion. The anastomotic outcomes by anastomotic technique before and after IPTW adjustment was shown in supplement Table 6. Patients who required a hand-sewn anastomosis were more likely to receive a diverting stoma at the time of surgery. Although their anastomotic leakage and local recurrence rates were comparable to other techniques, they had a higher likelihood of retaining a permanent stoma during follow-up.

Postoperative outcomes are presented in Table 3. The median hospital stay was comparable between the groups (8 vs. 7.4 days, p = 0.057). The time to the first flatus passage, first stool passage, and tolerance to liquid and soft diets were similar between the groups. There was a significant difference in the postoperative complications between the groups, with a trend toward more grade I complications in the TaTME group than in the robotic TME group. However, only two patients (1.1%) in the TaTME group and six patients (3.4%) in the robotic TME group had complications classified as Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 3. Overall, no surgical mortalities were observed. The rates of anastomotic leaks were similar between the TaTME and robotic TME groups (2.2% and 3.4%, respectively, p = 0.539), as were the rates of intra-abdominal infections (13.4% and 11.4%, respectively, p = 0.573). The reoperation rates for various indications, including anastomotic leakage and bowel obstruction, were comparable between the groups. Regarding blood analysis results at 3 days postoperatively, the TaTME group showed significantly higher C-reactive protein (CRP) levels than the robotic TME group (CRP ≥ 100 mg/L: 43.7% vs. 29.9%, p = 0.01), although there was no difference in white blood cell counts between the groups.

Histopathological parameters

The histopathological outcomes are summarized in Table 4. After IPTW adjustment, a pathological complete response was observed in 13 patients (7.3%) in the TaTME group and in six patients (3.4%) in the robotic TME group (p = 0.112). TaTME was associated with a significantly higher rate of positive CRM (6.1% vs. 0%, p = 0.001) and overall R1 resection (6.7% vs. 0%, p = 0.001) than the robotic TME group. The median DRM was shorter in the TaTME group than in the robotic TME group (1.2 cm vs. 1.6 cm, p = 0.021). The robotic TME group had a higher incidence of signet ring cell or mucinous-type cancers as well as more grade III differentiated tumors than the TaTME group. There were no significant differences between the groups in terms of lymphovascular invasion (p = 0.933), perineural invasion (p = 0.489), or lymph node yield (p = 0.29).

Long-term outcomes

As shown in Table 5, the median follow-up durations were 56 months in the TaTME group and 63.8 months in the robotic TME groups, respectively. LR occurred in 18 (16.4%) and seven patients (10.9%) in the TaTME and robotic TME groups, respectively (p = 0.325), with median times to LR of 22.7 months and 17.0 months, respectively (p = 0.468). DM was observed in 23 (20.9%) and 17 patients (26.6%) in the TaTME and robotic TME groups, respectively (p = 0.393), with median times to DM of 14.8 months and 19.4 months, respectively (p = 0.389). Nineteen (17.3%) and 13 (20.3%) patients in the TaTME and robotic TME groups, respectively, died (p = 0.618).

Discussion

Our study highlights a complex and multifaceted approach to treating rectal cancer, particularly LARC, where surgical techniques such as TME are crucial in achieving optimal oncological outcomes. We compared two advanced, minimally invasive approaches, TaTME and robotic TME, to assess their impact on clinical and oncological results. The findings revealed that both approaches offered similar outcomes in many aspects, although there were some notable differences in the operative parameters and histopathological outcomes that may influence surgical decision-making.

Regarding operative parameters, the TaTME group had more hand-sewn anastomoses and diverting stomas than the robotic TME group; however, median operative time was comparable, consistent with previous reports [27]. The use of a two-team approach in some TaTME procedures may have contributed to maintaining operative efficiency [28]. Intraoperative blood loss was significantly greater in the TaTME group, a finding also reported in prior studies [21, 29]. This may reflect the technical demands of the “bottom-up” approach, especially in anatomically challenging cases such as a narrow pelvis. By contrast, robotic surgery offers enhanced precision, ergonomics, and stability, facilitating accurate dissection and better preservation of neurovascular structures, thereby reducing blood loss [30–32]. Baseline differences between groups also influenced outcomes. Before IPTW adjustment, the TaTME cohort had a higher proportion of low rectal cancers (70.9% vs. 31.3%), which increased surgical complexity and contributed to blood loss. Moreover, TaTME is associated with a steeper learning curve, estimated at 40–51 cases to achieve proficiency, compared with 15–25 cases for robotic surgery [33–35]. Surgeons in training may face challenges in visualization and hemostasis during TaTME, particularly in confined pelvic spaces, which can exacerbate blood loss. Despite these challenges, both techniques demonstrated low conversion rates, with only one robotic TME case converted to open surgery because of severe adhesions. The higher frequency of hand-sewn anastomoses in the TaTME group likely explains the increased use of diverting stomas, reflecting the greater proportion of low rectal cancers. Although IPTW adjustment was applied, residual differences between groups cannot be entirely excluded. Importantly, with a median follow-up of five years, permanent stoma rates were comparable (18.2% for TaTME vs. 17.0% for robotic TME, Table 5), suggesting that the type of anastomosis did not significantly influence long-term stoma outcomes.

Postoperative recovery parameters, including hospital stay, time to first flatus, and time to tolerate liquid or soft diets, were comparable between the groups, indicating that both techniques provide effective minimally invasive approaches with similar short-term recovery profiles. Although the overall rate of postoperative complications differed significantly, the incidence of major complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III) was similar. Common adverse events such as ileus, anastomotic leakage, and intra-abdominal infection showed no significant differences, consistent with previous reports [27, 35–37]. Importantly, comparable anastomotic leak rates suggest that both techniques allow safe anastomosis. A trend toward more minor complications (grade I) was observed in the TaTME group (3.9% vs. 0%), aligning with prior findings [38]. Most of these were superficial surgical site infections managed successfully with conservative treatment. This may be explained by the higher proportion of patients in the TaTME group who had advanced tumors and received neoadjuvant therapy, both of which may predispose to complex wound conditions and higher infection risk. In contrast, robotic surgery, with its smaller incisions, enhanced hemostasis, and reduced tissue trauma, likely lowers the risk of wound complications [31]. The significantly lower postoperative CRP levels in the robotic group further support this interpretation.

Histopathological outcomes showed a CRM positivity rate of 6.1% in the TaTME group, consistent with large series and the international TaTME registry (1.5–8.1%) [27, 39–41]. The transanal approach facilitates clear visualization of the presacral and perirectal spaces, while carbon dioxide insufflation expands tissue planes and enhances perineal dissection, allowing precise manipulation via the transanal platform [42, 43]. Meta-analyses have reported lower positive CRM rates with TaTME compared to laparoscopic TME (odds ratio 0.39, 95% CI 0.17–0.86, p = 0.02) [28]. In our study, no CRM involvement occurred in the robotic TME group, likely reflecting the benefits of stable three-dimensional visualization and tremor filtration offered by the robotic system [44]. Although both groups achieved oncologically adequate resection margins, the TaTME group demonstrated a shorter median DRM, consistent with the precise distal dissection afforded by the transanal approach, without an increase in DRM positivity [45]. Notably, R1 resections were more frequent in the TaTME group (7.3% vs. 0%), likely reflecting the higher technical complexity and more advanced tumors treated in this cohort. Importantly, the observed differences in CRM positivity between TaTME and robotic TME may, in part, be attributable to a higher incidence of preoperative mesorectal fascia (MRF) involvement among TaTME patients, suggesting that local tumor extension influenced both the choice of surgical approach and the risk of positive margins. Despite these differences, the lymph node yields were comparable, underscoring the overall oncological adequacy of both techniques.

Only a limited number of studies have directly compared the long-term outcomes of TaTME and robotic TME (Supplement Tables 7, 8, 9 and 10). In our analysis, 5-year oncological outcomes, including local recurrence (LR), distant metastasis (DM), overall survival (OS), and disease-free survival (DFS), were comparable between the two groups after IPTW adjustment. Although the robotic TME group showed a slightly higher incidence of distant metastases, this difference was not statistically significant. These findings indicate that both surgical techniques achieve similar long-term oncological results. Our results are consistent with prior studies reporting comparable rates of LR, systemic recurrence, and OS between TaTME and robotic TME [46]. A systematic review and network meta-analysis by Chen et al. also found no significant differences in CRM involvement or mesorectal completeness among TaTME, robotic, and laparoscopic approaches [47]. Other meta-analyses also reported no differences in 5-year OS, DFS, or LR between TaTME and laparoscopic TME [29], and no differences in 3-year survival, recurrence, or metastasis between robotic and laparoscopic TME [48]. Taken together, current evidence suggests that long-term outcomes are largely equivalent across minimally invasive techniques, and the choice of approach may depend more on surgeon expertise, patient factors, and institutional resources. Importantly, both TaTME and robotic TME require overcoming distinct learning curves [49]. In our study, despite differences in operative and histopathological parameters, these did not translate into different long-term oncological outcomes, underscoring the safety and feasibility of both procedures for rectal cancer.

The present study has some limitations. First, this study is limited by its retrospective design and incomplete data on preoperative clinical staging and MRF involvement, which restricted a detailed evaluation of these potentially influential factors. In this study, pathological T and N stages were used in the propensity-score model because complete clinical staging data were unavailable for all patients. We acknowledge that this may violate the temporal assumption of propensity-score estimation, as only preoperative variables should ideally be included. Second, surgeons have been performing TaTME for rectal cancer at our hospital since 2015, and case collection started in January 2015. We did not exclude surgical cases performed by surgeons who were still in the learning phase; however, this may reflect real-world conditions. Third, there were important differences between patients who underwent TaTME and those who underwent robotic TME, with a higher proportion of advanced cancer and worse baseline conditions in the TaTME group. The present study used IPTW to provide an overall population-level comparison (ATE) between TaTME and robotic TME. While this approach allows broad generalizability, it may include patients with higher rectal tumors who would not typically be candidates for TaTME. To address this, we conducted an additional PSM-based sensitivity analysis focusing on the treated population (ATT), which better reflects the real-world clinical indication of TaTME. The PSM results were consistent with the IPTW analysis, reinforcing the robustness and external validity of our findings. Also, given the retrospective nature of this study, detailed operative records were not uniformly accessible, which precluded a comprehensive assessment of intraoperative injuries across all patients. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the comparative outcomes of these two approaches for rectal cancer. It is important to note that our study’s extended follow-up period provided a more comprehensive assessment of long-term oncological outcomes than previous studies, offering a more accurate evaluation of late recurrences and metastases, which may not have been captured in studies with shorter follow-up durations. Our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence on these novel surgical techniques and offer important information to clinicians and patients when considering treatment options.

Conclusions

Both TaTME and robotic TME are viable minimally invasive options for rectal cancer, achieving comparable overall clinical and oncological outcomes. However, TaTME was associated with greater blood loss and a higher rate of CRM positivity, whereas robotic TME provided more consistent margin control, a key determinant of long-term oncological safety. Although our series did not show increased local recurrence in the TaTME group, this may be related to the overall low rate of CRM involvement. These findings suggest that robotic TME may offer an oncological advantage, particularly in securing negative margins. Further high-quality studies, especially randomized controlled trials, are warranted to confirm these observations and refine surgical decision-making.
