Heme as a versatile signaling molecule: Coordinating mitochondrial metabolism with global cellular responses
Authors: Iva Chitrakar, Breann L. Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heme is an essential iron-containing cofactor that supports diverse biological processes, including oxygen transport, mitochondrial respiration, and xenobiotic metabolism. Beyond these canonical functions, accumulating evidence has established heme as a dynamic signaling molecule that couples mitochondrial metabolism to cellular stress responses, gene expression, and metabolic adaptation. Heme biosynthesis is compartmentalized between mitochondria and the cytosol, requiring tightly coordinated synthesis, trafficking, sensing, and degradation to maintain cellular homeostasis and prevent heme toxicity. In this review, we examine mechanisms by which heme regulates mitochondrial protein quality control, respiratory chain assembly, and metabolic feedback to integrate organellar function with cellular energy demands. We further discuss how heme is trafficked to extramitochondrial compartments, where it modulates cytoplasmic stress signaling, iron homeostasis, transcriptional networks, and metabolic programs through interactions with proteins, including the BACH1 transcription factor, REV-ERB nuclear receptors, and the glycolytic enzyme GAPDH. We also highlight the ongoing discourse surrounding mitochondrial heme trafficking and identify critical unanswered questions regarding the identity of intracellular heme chaperones and mitochondrial heme sensors. Finally, we discuss how dysregulation of heme metabolism contributes to diverse pathologies. Collectively, recent advances establish heme as a central regulator of mitochondrial communication and cellular homeostasis, underscoring the therapeutic potential of targeting heme signaling pathways in human disease.

Heme mediates a variety of cellular processes in many organisms, from bacteria to vertebrates. In mammals, heme is widely known for its role in oxygen transport in red blood cells, serving as the prosthetic group of globin to form hemoglobin. In addition to this ubiquitous function, heme plays other roles in maintaining cellular homeostasis. For example, heme mediates drug metabolism, serving as a fixed prosthetic group of hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes (1). Reversible heme binding to proteins such as the circadian regulators CLOCK and the REV-ERBα nuclear receptor (2, 3, 4), as well as the transcription factor BACH1, can determine protein activity, stability, or localization (5). We refer to heme signaling as the dynamic interaction between heme and various biomolecular targets that conveys information about cellular metabolic status. This review curates myriad heme–protein interactions to highlight how heme acts as a cellular chameleon, activating and regulating several biological programs.

The heme molecule consists of a porphyrin scaffold that coordinates an iron ion in the center. In non-plant eukaryotes, heme synthesis begins with the production of aminolevulinic acid by the enzyme aminolevulinic acid synthase (ALAS) in the mitochondrial matrix (Fig. 1) (6, 7). The next four enzymatic steps occur in the cytoplasm, ensuring proper porphyrin condensation and cyclization. The final three reactions occur back inside the mitochondrion, ultimately terminating with the insertion of iron into protoporphyrin IX to form mature heme by Ferrochelatase (FECH) (8, 9, 10). Heme is then shuttled to other parts of the cell, where it performs various functions before being degraded by heme oxygenase (HO) (11, 12). Importantly, heme-mediated signal transduction extends outside of mitochondria. While this cofactor is indispensable, heme overload can lead to inflammation, organ injury, and death (13). Therefore, heme availability is tightly buffered in the cell through constant binding and exchange via hemoproteins and the labile heme pool. Its shuttling between subcellular compartments enables heme to link the metabolic status of the mitochondrion, cytoplasm, and nucleus, influencing multiple biological pathways. Thus, our increasing knowledge of heme-mediated signaling is revealing new and intricate links between this molecule and many areas of human physiology. We first examine mitochondrial heme signaling before discussing how heme is trafficked to extramitochondrial compartments, where it regulates cytoplasmic stress responses, nuclear gene expression, metabolism, and ultimately human disease. Understanding these regulatory mechanisms offers promising targets for therapeutic intervention in diseases ranging from anemia and neurodegeneration to cancer and inflammatory disorders.

Mitochondrial heme signaling

Heme is not only synthesized in mitochondria but also required for proper electron transport and mitochondrial physiology. However, these organelles lack an internal mechanism to degrade heme. The absence of heme oxygenase mandates tight regulation of heme synthesis, hemoprotein maturation, and export to prevent toxic heme accumulation. Protein quality control

Because mitochondrial function depends on the import and maturation of more than 1500 proteins, maintaining protein quality control (PQC) is essential for both respiratory function and heme homeostasis (14). The overwhelming majority of mitochondrial proteins are nuclear-encoded and must be imported into the mitochondrion either co-translationally or post-translationally (15). Proper protein import, processing, and folding are ensured by the mitochondrial PQC network (16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21). In many instances, ATP-dependent HSPA9 and translocase of the inner mitochondrial membrane 23 (TIM23) translocase transfer nascent polypeptides into the matrix while shielding their hydrophobic regions to prevent aggregation (22, 23). Generally, once inside the mitochondrial matrix, the targeting sequence is removed, and the imported protein undergoes chaperone-mediated folding (24, 25). This network also supports proper metalation and iron–sulfur (Fe-S) cluster biogenesis by transferring the ISCU Fe-S cluster assembly scaffold to glutaredoxin-5, thereby sustaining enzymes such as FECH and respiratory chain complexes (26, 27, 28). When proteins fail to achieve their native conformation, specialized proteases recognize and degrade the misfolded polypeptides (29).

Loss of PQC can induce the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt), which is a stress-response network that activates the transcription and import of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial chaperones to support proteostasis (30, 31, 32). This stress response includes the upregulation of several mitochondrial PQC players, such as the HSPA9 and HSP60 chaperones and the CLPP, LONP1, and YME1L proteases, which work in concert to refold or remove unfolded and damaged mitochondrial proteins (18, 21, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39). Accordingly, changes in heme levels affect mitochondrial chaperone and protease activities via UPRmt activation. Elevated heme concentrations activate stress-responsive transcription factors, upregulating HSPA9 and HSP60 expression to alleviate proteotoxic stress (40, 41, 42, 43, 44). Conversely, heme deficiency can directly disrupt heme-binding chaperones in the mitochondrial inner membrane that are critical for the import and stability of certain electron transport chain proteins (45). The resulting accumulation of these misfolded proteins also activates the UPRmt (46). Thus, mitochondrial proteostasis is unsustainable when heme pools fluctuate significantly. Interestingly, despite the swift response of mitochondrial heme levels to maintain PQC, direct heme binding to soluble chaperones in the mitochondrial matrix is yet to be identified.

A more direct mechanism linking heme signaling to PQC is the heme-dependent proteolytic degradation of ALAS. In mammals, two isoforms of ALAS exist (47, 48, 49, 50, 51, 52, 53). ALAS1 is ubiquitously expressed to support basal heme production in most tissues. ALAS2 is an erythroid-specific isoform responsible for heme synthesis needed to produce hemoglobin during red blood cell differentiation. Both isoforms possess multiple heme regulatory motifs (HRMs), composed of cysteine-proline signatures, that mediate reversible heme binding. One outcome of this interaction is the proteolytic degradation of ALAS, thereby decreasing heme production. Heme binding to an N-terminal HRM in ALAS1 recruits the ATP-dependent proteases LONP1 and CLPXP, leading to ALAS1 degradation (54, 55). Similarly, early studies suggest that heme-induced degradation of the ALAS2 isoform is mediated by CLPXP (56). This heme-dependent degradation of both ALAS isoforms establishes a negative feedback loop that prevents excess heme accumulation when its cellular levels are sufficient. Together, these mechanisms position heme as both a substrate and a regulator of mitochondrial proteostasis, ensuring that protein import, folding, and heme biosynthesis remain tightly coordinated.

Respiratory chain assembly

Despite the fact that mitochondrial PQC preserves proteome integrity, successful protein folding alone is insufficient. Many mitochondrial proteins also require heme incorporation, making respiratory chain assembly another major point at which heme regulates mitochondrial homeostasis. Post-translational heme insertion into Complex II, Complex III, and Complex IV functions as a critical structural checkpoint that determines whether newly synthesized respiratory complexes are stable or targeted for degradation upon failure to fold properly (57, 58, 59, 60, 61). In this way, heme availability synchronizes mitochondrial translation with respiratory complex assembly, ensuring that synthesis of electron transport chain components proceeds only when cofactors are available and preventing toxic accumulation of orphan proteins (62, 63). This synchronization is especially evident during the biogenesis of Complex IV or cytochrome c oxidase, which requires the heme-binding chaperone mitochondrial translation regulation assembly intermediate of cytochrome c oxidase (MITRAC) complex to orchestrate translation with cofactor insertion (64). MITRAC assembly factors such as SURF1 and COA2 promote Complex IV maturation by facilitating proper heme insertion and stabilizing assembly intermediates (65). Notably, emerging evidence indicates that mitochondrial heme-binding assembly factors, including COA7, participate directly in Complex IV biogenesis (66). Through this proteostatic control, heme contributes to respiratory chain assembly and efficient oxidative phosphorylation.

An intriguing possibility is that this coordination is reinforced by the spatial organization of the respiratory complex assembly within a mitochondrial metabolon, a transient multi-protein complex in which enzymes and assembly factors associate to channel reactive intermediates directly between active sites (67, 68). The heme biosynthetic metabolon has been proposed to include FECH, along with inner-membrane translocases and scaffold proteins, raising the possibility that it could transiently interact with Complex IV assembly factors such as COX10, COX15, SURF1, or COA2 to facilitate the efficient delivery of newly synthesized heme to nascent respiratory complexes. Although direct evidence for such a supercomplex is lacking, this arrangement would be energetically favorable, preventing diffusion of heme and toxic precursors, limiting oxidative damage, and ensuring that heme insertion occurs only when apoproteins are properly folded and ready for maturation. Thus, heme availability not only supports respiratory complex maturation but also couples electron transport chain biogenesis to mitochondrial metabolic state, establishing the basis for broader metabolic feedback.

Metabolic feedback

Beyond supporting respiratory complex maturation, heme synthesis is itself integrated with central carbon metabolism, potentially allowing mitochondrial metabolic flux to regulate heme production and vice versa. The first committed step of heme biosynthesis requires succinyl-CoA, a TCA cycle intermediate, as a precursor. ALAS condenses glycine and succinyl-CoA to produce ALA in the mitochondrion. Succinyl-CoA is generated primarily by the oxidative decarboxylation of α-ketoglutarate by the α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex and can also arise from propionyl-CoA metabolism via the methylmalonyl-CoA pathway. In addition, the reversible succinyl-CoA synthetase reaction can interconvert succinyl-CoA and succinate, however, the physiological direction in the TCA cycle is typically toward succinate (69). Prior studies implicated multiple TCA cycle enzymes in binding to ALAS2, presumably delivering the required succinyl-CoA precursor (70, 71, 72). Speculatively, elevated ALAS activity may lower mitochondrial succinyl-CoA levels, limiting NADH and FADH2 generation, and directly linking heme synthesis to respiratory output and mitochondrial redox balance.

At the same time, heme itself serves as a signaling molecule to regulate its own production. For example, heme binding to the HRMs present in both ALAS1 and ALAS2 mitochondrial targeting sequences blocks mitochondrial import, providing rapid post-translational control to prevent toxicity (73, 74). Beyond this mechanism, we recently reported that within the matrix, heme acts as a reversible inhibitor by transiently binding allosteric sites on mature ALAS2, potentially stabilizing an inactive enzyme conformation (75). These mechanisms establish a local feedback system in which heme synthesis and availability influences metabolic flux. Furthermore, heme’s regulatory functions extend well beyond the organelle.

Outer-mitochondrial heme signaling

After export from mitochondria, heme coordinates cellular adaptation by regulating cytoplasmic stress responses, nuclear transcription, and systemic metabolism (Fig. 3). Because heme is hydrophobic and cytotoxic at high concentrations, signaling depends on dedicated trafficking pathways to prevent nonspecific membrane insertion and limit oxidative damage. Seminal studies identified the membrane transporter Feline Leukemia Virus subgroup C Receptor 1b (FLVCR1b) as a mitochondrial heme exporter required for erythroid differentiation, as depletion of FLVCR1b resulted in mitochondrial heme accumulation and impaired erythropoiesis (76, 77, 78). Subsequent work identified the protein transport and Golgi organization 2 (TANGO2) as a putative heme chaperone that facilitates heme transfer from mitochondria to the cytosol (79). A bacterial TANGO2 homolog was likewise shown to support cytochrome c biosynthesis and catalase activity, suggesting that a heme-binding function may be evolutionarily conserved (80). Together with later studies demonstrating heme transfer from FLVCR1b to the cytosolic chaperone GAPDH, these findings support a model in which FLVCR1b, TANGO2, and GAPDH function sequentially to export, buffer, and deliver newly synthesized heme to downstream hemoproteins and signaling targets (76, 81, 82, 83, 84, 85, 86, 87, 88).

More recent studies, however, have challenged aspects of this model. (89). Structural and functional analyses indicate that FLVCR1 and FLVCR2 transport choline and ethanolamine across the plasma membrane rather than heme (90, 91, 92, 93). Likewise, several reports suggest that many phenotypes associated with TANGO2 deficiency arise from defects in coenzyme A, lipid, or mitochondrial metabolism rather than impaired heme trafficking (89). TANGO2 is implicated in binding both the small heat shock protein crystallin alpha B and acyl-coenzyme A (94, 95) and may also play a critical role in vitamin B5 metabolism (96). These observations raise the possibility that previously observed alterations in heme homeostasis may represent indirect consequences of broader metabolic dysfunction rather than defective heme transport itself.

Rather than being mutually exclusive, these findings may reflect the complexity of intracellular heme homeostasis. FLVCR1b and TANGO2 could possess context-dependent or tissue-specific functions, participate indirectly in heme allocation by regulating mitochondrial metabolism, or act as multifunctional proteins whose roles in heme signaling vary across developmental or physiological states. Alternatively, heme export may involve multiple partially redundant pathways that compensate for one another under different conditions. Resolving these possibilities will require direct measurements of heme flux in vivo together with structural and biochemical characterization of candidate transporters under physiological conditions.

Additional proteins have also been implicated in intracellular heme trafficking. The membrane-associated protein PGRMC1 localizes to mitochondria–ER contact sites and interacts with several heme biosynthetic enzymes, suggesting a role in coordinating heme transfer between organelles (97, 98). PGRMC1 also associates with PGRMC2, which has been proposed to facilitate nuclear heme delivery for transcriptional regulation (99). Collectively, these studies support the existence of a highly regulated intracellular trafficking network, although the identities, mechanisms, and relative contributions of its individual components remain active areas of investigation.

Cytoplasmic stress response

Cells rapidly adapt to mitochondrial dysfunction by reprogramming protein synthesis through the integrated stress response (ISR) (100). These changes in translation are mediated by the phosphorylation of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 alpha (eIF2α) by one of four eIF2α kinases, including the Heme-regulated eIF2α kinase (HRI). HRI receives signals directly from stressed mitochondria through the OMA1-DELE-HRI relay (20, 21, 38, 101, 102). OMA1 is an inner mitochondrial membrane metalloprotease that is activated under conditions that compromise mitochondrial PQC, such as arrested protein import, accumulation of misfolded proteins, loss of membrane potential, or defects in mitochondrial metabolism. Upon activation, OMA1 cleaves the mitochondrial protein DELE1 (DAR2-binding cell death enhancer 1). Cytosolic accumulation of processed DELE1 directly activates HRI, prompting ISR signaling even when cytosolic heme levels are sufficient (103). This pathway enables mitochondrial dysfunction and iron deficiency to rapidly reprogram cytoplasmic protein synthesis. In parallel, HRI activity is inhibited when heme is bound to the N-terminal domain or the kinase insertion domain (104, 105). This inhibition is reversed in response to low cytosolic heme levels or by HSP70 sequestration of misfolded or aggregated proteins during proteotoxic stress. Both effectively release HRI, which phosphorylates eIF2α to halt global protein synthesis while also inducing translation of stress-responsive transcription factors to adapt to oxidative and proteotoxic stress (106).

Iron and heme availability operate in parallel with HRI, providing an additional layer of regulation. This adaptation is fulfilled by iron regulatory proteins (IRP1 and IRP2), bifunctional cytosolic RNA-binding proteins that post-transcriptionally regulate cellular iron metabolism. Under basal iron conditions, IRP functions as cytosolic aconitase (107, 108). During mitochondrial dysfunction or iron deficiency, the heme-IRP switch prioritizes cellular survival over heme and hemoglobin production (109). Loss of its 4Fe-4S cluster converts IRP into a translational repressor that blocks ALAS2 translation while stabilizing transferrin receptor mRNA to increase iron uptake and prevent toxic accumulation of porphyrin intermediates (110). Heme also directly controls IRP activity by sterically blocking IRP binding to iron-responsive elements (IREs) located in the 5′ untranslated region of ALAS2 mRNA, and by serving as a signal to recruit E3 ubiquitin ligases for subsequent IRP proteasomal degradation (111, 112, 113). One exception to this model is during terminal erythroid differentiation, where the IRP system is less responsive to cellular iron levels to ensure robust heme synthesis for hemoglobinization (114).

While IRP1 suppresses heme synthesis, HRI decreases protein synthesis to prevent heme accumulation (115). Because IRP1 activation depends on mitochondrial 4Fe-4S cluster biogenesis, it is plausible that proteotoxic stress that sequesters mitochondrial chaperones such as HSPA9 could indirectly suppress heme biosynthesis. Collectively, these pathways allow cytoplasmic translation to rapidly respond to changes in both mitochondrial function and heme availability prior to a transcriptional response.

Nuclear gene expression

Whereas cytoplasmic heme signaling enables rapid translational adaptation, this molecule also elicits longer-lasting responses by directly regulating nuclear transcription factors and receptors. One well-characterized pathway is mediated by the transcriptional repressor BACH1, which binds antioxidant response elements and suppresses genes such as heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) when heme is low. When heme levels rise, its binding promotes BACH1 nuclear export and proteasomal degradation (116), relieving repression of NRF2-dependent antioxidant genes (117). NRF2, normally restrained by KEAP1 and BACH1, then induces targets including HO-1, which catabolizes excess heme into biliverdin, iron, and carbon monoxide (CO) to limit oxidative damage (118, 119, 120). Thus, BACH1 degradation and NRF2-dependent activation of antioxidant and heme-degradation genes coordinate mitochondrial respiration and reactive oxygen species production with nuclear antioxidant gene expression.

In addition to stress-responsive transcription factors, heme also regulates nuclear receptors that synchronize metabolism and circadian rhythms. REV-ERB nuclear receptors (REV-ERBα/NR1D1 and REV-ERBβ/NR1D2) are ligand-dependent transcriptional repressors (4). Heme-bound REV-ERBs repress genes involved in gluconeogenesis, lipid metabolism, and circadian regulation (2, 4, 121, 122, 123). Furthermore, despite the oxidative state of heme-bound REV-ERBs, they act as gas sensors, with CO and nitric oxide (NO) modulating their transcriptional activity (124). Along with heme-binding circadian regulators, these pathways convey the mitochondrial metabolic state to the nucleus (125, 126). Accordingly, REV-ERB signaling provides a direct mechanism by which mitochondrial heme availability reshapes long-term metabolic gene expression.

Heme-nuclear communication is particularly evident in erythroid cells, where heme and globin synthesis must remain tightly synchronized to minimize pathophysiologic conditions such as Thalassemia, a blood disease in which globin production outweighs heme availability. Erythroid cells maintain this balance through a transcriptional circuit centered on GATA1, BACH1, and heme. GATA1 is the master transcription factor that drives erythroid differentiation by activating genes required for erythrocyte maturation, including those involved in heme biosynthesis (such as ALAS2) and hemoglobin synthesis (48, 127). Genome-wide studies have identified BACH1 as a direct or indirect component of the GATA1 transcriptional network during erythropoiesis (128, 129). BACH1 subsequently functions as a heme-sensitive transcriptional repressor of the β-globin locus and other heme-responsive genes by binding MAF recognition elements as a heterodimer with small MAF proteins, thereby antagonizing transcriptional activation by NRF2 (119, 120). This regulatory architecture forms a coherent feed-forward circuit in which GATA1 promotes erythroid differentiation while simultaneously inducing a transient repressor that restrains globin gene expression until intracellular heme levels are sufficient. Heme-mediated inhibition and degradation of BACH1 subsequently relieve this repression, ensuring that globin synthesis is tightly coordinated with heme availability (116). Unlike the rapid translational response mediated by HRI and IRPs, these heme-responsive transcriptional circuits provide a mechanism for sustained adaptation to changes in heme availability.

Metabolic signaling

The transcriptional programs initiated by heme sensing ultimately converge on broader metabolic adaptations that coordinate mitochondrial activity with global cell physiology. In addition to a multitude of biological events dictated by heme-activated REV-ERB nuclear receptors, it also influences the REV-ERB/PGC-1α feedback loop. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1α, PGC-1α, is a transcriptional coactivator that promotes mitochondrial biogenesis, oxidative metabolism, and adaptive energy homeostasis. REV-ERBα represses transcription of PGC-1α when bound to heme, while PGC-1α is also a positive regulator of hepatic ALAS1 expression (121, 130). By responding to heme, this pathway forms a feedback loop, such that this cofactor represses its own synthesis. Through this mechanism, mitochondrial heme production is synchronized with mitochondrial biogenesis and energy metabolism. Moreover, REV-ERB/PGC-1α signaling extends to broader lipid metabolic networks. REV-ERBα also regulates the promoters of lipid transport proteins such as apolipoprotein CIII (131, 132).

Heme also controls metabolism through post-translational signaling pathways. For instance, heme activation of soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC), the primary receptor for NO in vascular signaling, is essential for converting apo-sGC into an NO-responsive holoenzyme. NO promotes heme insertion, directly activating apo-sGC, creating a circuit by which NO signaling enhances maturation of its own receptor (133). Recent theories suggest NO plays a role in heme allocation beyond sGC (134). Several hemoproteins are known to be only partially saturated with heme in the cell, which would allow for graded regulation of protein activity via heme signaling without requiring changes in protein expression. Since NO has a concentration-dependent effect on cell heme allocation, stimulating heme delivery at low NO concentrations, it could serve as a master regulator of heme signaling via altered incorporation into myriad hemoproteins (135).

Further, heme facilitates microRNA (miRNA) stability and processing, both directly and indirectly. First, heme directly binds the RNA-binding protein DiGeorge critical region-8 (DGCR8), leading to its dimerization and activation of miRNA primary transcript (pri-miRNA) processing (136). Additionally, ALAS1 was recently shown to repress cytosolic miRNA accumulation in a heme-independent manner (137). Together, these mechanisms suggest heme signaling operates across multiple regulatory layers, with changes in heme pool impacting transcription, protein activity and RNA processin.

Heme and pathogenesis

The signaling pathways described above normally align mitochondrial function with cellular adaptation. Consequently, disruption of heme synthesis, trafficking, sensing, or degradation compromises these homeostatic networks, promoting numerous human diseases (Fig. 4). Defects in heme synthesis are known to cause several types of porphyrias, anemia, and Friedreich's ataxia. In hepatic porphyrias, insufficient enzyme activity leads to the accumulation of toxic porphyrin precursors, causing acute neurovisceral attacks characterized by severe abdominal pain and neurologic symptoms and/or cutaneous photosensitivity with blistering skin lesions (130, 138, 139). Mutations in erythroid-specific ALAS2 impair heme synthesis, leading to mitochondrial iron overload and ineffective erythropoiesis in X-linked sideroblastic anemia (140). In another example, mutations in the FXN gene cause nerve damage underlying Friedreich's ataxia, a rare inherited disease that affects the nerves and heart, leading to problems with balance, coordination, walking, speech, and muscle strength (141, 142). Although earlier studies reported that Frataxin directly delivers iron to FECH, recent work describes the primary role of Frataxin in Fe-S cluster biogenesis (143), which, in turn, may affect FECH stability and heme synthesis. Moreover, mitochondrial protein quality control malfunctions, such as defective cytochrome assembly due to mutations in the heme delivery protein SURF1 and cytochrome delivery factors COX10 and COX15, could destabilize respiratory complexes (144, 145, 146, 147, 148, 149). Since heme trafficking is also critical, disruption in heme export leads to numerous pathologies. Mutation of the FLVCR1 transporter impacts the nervous system, leading to various diseases, including retinitis pigmentosa (150, 151), and developmental syndromes, such as Diamond-Blackfan Anemia (152, 153). In parallel, free heme acts as a damage-associated molecular pattern leading to pro-inflammatory responses. Hemolytic disorders, such as sickle cell disease, highlight how extracellular heme can activate innate immune pathways and induce vascular inflammation when its buffering systems are overwhelmed. Oxidative stress and protein misfolding are hallmarks of multiple neurodegenerative disorders, such as Parkinson’s, Alzheimer’s, and Huntington’s diseases (154, 155). Tumor cells frequently reprogram heme biosynthesis to co-opt heme signaling to support proliferation and metastasis, altering heme transport (156, 157, 158) and transcription factors such as BACH1 and NRF2 (159, 160). Finally, aberrant heme degradation also drives pathogenesis. Prior studies report that the levels of both heme oxygenase and bilirubin increase in patients with Alzheimer’s disease (161, 162, 163). This pathological regulation also extends to the products of heme degradation, including iron and CO, which can generate reactive oxygen species or bind ion channels, altering their function (164, 165, 166, 167). Together, these disorders underscore that heme is far more than a metabolic cofactor; its synthesis, trafficking, sensing, and degradation form an integrated signaling network whose disruption factors into a broad spectrum of human disease.

Outlook

Despite major advances in identifying heme-responsive signaling pathways, fundamental questions remain regarding how cells spatially organize, traffic, and sense heme. Addressing these questions represents the next frontier in understanding heme as a signaling molecule.

One of the most pressing challenges is identifying the proteins responsible for intracellular heme trafficking. The recent discovery that GAPDH functions as a cytosolic heme chaperone transformed our understanding of heme allocation by demonstrating that enzymes with established metabolic functions can also participate directly in heme signaling. This finding raises the possibility that additional multifunctional proteins remain undiscovered. The ongoing controversy over the identities of mitochondrial heme transporters, including FLVCR1b and TANGO2, further underscores the evolving discourse on the mechanisms governing intracellular heme transport. Likewise, no high-resolution structure of a eukaryotic heme chaperone bound to its hemoprotein target has yet been reported, leaving fundamental questions regarding substrate recognition and specificity unanswered.

These uncertainties also raise broader conceptual questions about mitochondrial heme signaling itself. Despite the mitochondrion being the exclusive site of heme synthesis, comparatively few mitochondrial heme-sensing pathways have been identified relative to those operating in the cytoplasm and nucleus. One possibility is that eukaryotic cells restrict mitochondrial heme signaling to a small number of tightly regulated processes, such as ALAS feedback inhibition and heme-dependent proteolysis, thereby minimizing fluctuations in labile heme. Alternatively, heme distribution within mitochondria may occur through a transient metabolon that channels newly synthesized heme directly between biosynthetic enzymes, assembly factors, and respiratory complexes. The proposed association of FECH with inner membrane scaffold proteins provides an attractive framework for such a mechanism (68, 168), although direct experimental evidence remains limited. An equally intriguing possibility is that mitochondrial heme sensors have escaped identification because they moonlight as proteins with established metabolic or chaperone functions, analogous to GAPDH in the cytosol. Distinguishing among these models will require identifying bona fide mitochondrial heme-binding proteins, determining their heme affinities and regulatory thresholds, and defining how heme is selectively retained, trafficked, or exported from the mitochondrial matrix.

Addressing these gaps will depend on continued technological innovation. Genetically encoded fluorescent heme sensors have already begun to reveal the spatial and temporal dynamics of labile heme in cells. When combined with genome-wide genetic perturbation approaches such as CRISPR interference, targeted protein degradation systems, and quantitative proteomics, these sensors should enable systematic identification of new components of the heme signaling network (88). Integrating these approaches with structural biology and quantitative measurements of compartment-specific labile heme will provide a more complete understanding of how cells regulate heme allocation under both physiological and pathological conditions.

Finally, translating these mechanistic insights into therapeutic strategies remains an important long-term goal. Cellular concentrations of labile and protein-bound heme vary substantially across tissues, developmental stages, and metabolic states, yet the thresholds that distinguish physiological signaling from toxicity remain poorly defined (62, 169, 170, 171). Because dysregulated heme signaling triggers disorders ranging from porphyrias and mitochondrial disease to neurodegeneration, inflammation, and cancer, future therapies must restore heme homeostasis without disrupting essential hemoprotein biogenesis. Achieving this balance will require a detailed understanding of when, where, and how heme functions as a signaling molecule. As the molecular framework of heme trafficking and sensing continues to emerge, targeting these pathways may provide new opportunities to selectively modulate mitochondrial function and cellular adaptation in human disease.
