Rigidity of anti-de Sitter (2+1)-spacetimes with convex boundary near the Fuchsian locus
Roman Prosanov, Jean-Marc Schlenker

Abstract
. We prove that globally hyperbolic compact anti-de Sitter (2+1)-spacetimes with strictly convex spacelike boundary that is either smooth or polyhedral and whose holonomy is close to Fuchsian are determined by the induced metric on the boundary.

1.
Introduction and results

1.1.
Motivation

A deep geometric result states that the shape of a convex body in Euclidean 3-space is completely determined by the intrinsic geometry of its boundary
[
50
]
. Using this fact as a foundation for our study, we aim to investigate, to which extent it can be generalized. Standard proofs of this result rely significantly on the fact that a convex body has the topology of a 3-ball. However, it was observed that this phenomenon holds also in nontrivial topology, provided that we stay in the setting of homogeneous geometry. In the case of
hyperbolic geometry
, which is the richest of Riemannian homogeneous geometries in dimension 3, manifestations of this were established, e.g., in the papers
[
57
,
51
]
.

In the present paper, we are concerned with
anti-de Sitter geometry
, which is a Lorentzian cousin of hyperbolic geometry. Namely, we deal with
globally hyperbolic Cauchy compact anti-de Sitter (2+1)-spacetimes
. They appear, e.g., in models of quantum gravity, see the fundamental paper of Witten
[
68
]
. They are reminiscent in their properties to
quasi-Fuchsian hyperbolic 3-manifolds
. In particular, it is conjectured that globally hyperbolic compact anti-de Sitter (2+1)-spacetimes with spacelike convex boundary are determined by the intrinsic geometry of the boundary. See, e.g.,
[
7
, Questions 3.3–3.6]
. Every such spacetime has two boundary components and can be canonically extended beyond the boundary to a maximal spacetime. Fundamentally, the conjecture means that the geometry of the whole spacetime is determined by the intrinsic geometry of two of its spacelike slices provided that the slices are convex in the opposite directions. In this article, we prove the conjecture among spacetimes whose holonomy is close to Fuchsian and whose boundary is either smooth or polyhedral. In the works
[
14
,
54
]
, some other partial cases of the conjecture are established, see below for details.

1.2.
Parallels between hyperbolic and anti-de Sitter geometry

For the whole manuscript,
S
is a closed orientable surface of genus
k\geq 2
. In what follows, we take
GHC AdS (2+1)-spacetimes
to mean globally hyperbolic compact anti-de Sitter (2+1)-spacetimes with spacelike convex boundary and
GHMC AdS (2+1)-spacetimes
to mean globally hyperbolic maximal Cauchy compact anti-de Sitter (2+1)-spacetimes. The exact definitions are given in Section
2.3
. The former ones are homeomorphic to
S\times[-1,1]
and the latter ones are homeomorphic to
S\times\mathbb{R}
.

The similarity of GHMC AdS (2+1)-spacetimes to quasi-Fuchsian hyperbolic 3-manifolds was discovered by Mess in a groundbreaking paper
[
46
]
. In particular, Mess proved that the marked moduli space of GHMC AdS structures on
S\times\mathbb{R}
is parameterized by the product of two copies of the Teichmüller space of
S
, see Section
2.3
. This is strongly reminiscent of the Bers Double Uniformization Theorem for quasi-Fuchsian hyperbolic 3-manifolds
[
9
]
.

The GHMC AdS (2+1)-spacetimes and quasi-Fuchsian hyperbolic 3-manifolds are also similar in that in both cases they contain a nonempty inclusion-minimal compact totally convex subset,
the convex core
. Here, a subset is
totally convex
if it contains every geodesic segment between every pair of its points. The convex core is either a totally geodesic surface or it has nonempty interior and its topological boundary is the disjoint union of two closed surfaces whose intrinsic metric is hyperbolic and that are “pleated” along a measured geodesic lamination.

In the hyperbolic case, Thurston conjectured that the corresponding marked moduli space can be parameterized both by the induced metrics on the boundary of the convex core and by the pleating laminations. These questions have two aspects: the realization part – to describe the image of the respective map from the marked moduli space, and the rigidity part – to show that the map is injective. For the first conjecture, the realization part follows, e.g., from the work of Labourie
[
42
]
; the rigidity is only known to hold for an open dense set, as shown in the work
[
51
]
of Prosanov. For the second conjecture, the image was described in
[
11
]
by Bonahon–Otal; the rigidity has been obtained only recently in
[
23
]
by Dular–Schlenker. (An important earlier advance was made by Bonahon in
[
10
]
, where he proved the rigidity in a neighborhood of the Fuchsian locus.) Prior to that, it was only known in a related, though slightly different setting when
S
is the once-punctured torus, due to Series
[
60
]
.

Mess conjectured that similar parameterizations hold for GHMC AdS (2+1)-spacetimes. In this case, the realization of the induced metrics was established by Diallo, published as an appendix to paper
[
13
]
. The realization of pleating laminations was shown in
[
15
]
by Bonsante–Schlenker, where the rigidity near the Fuchsian locus was also deduced. Except that, the rigidity parts of these problems remain open.

1.3.
Manifolds with boundary

As we saw, geometric rigidity questions are interconnected with the corresponding realization questions. In the case of Euclidean convex bodies, the relevant realization questions were answered by Nirenberg
[
48
]
in the smooth case, and by Alexandrov
[
1
]
in the polyhedral and, furthermore, in the general case of metric structures of curvature
K\geq 0
in the sense of Alexandrov, without any additional regularity assumption.

The analogy of Section
1.2
suggests exploring to what extent other remarkable properties of hyperbolic 3-manifolds extend to AdS (2+1)-spacetimes. In the hyperbolic case, Thurston also conjectured the following beautiful fact, established by Labourie in
[
42
]
: given a 3-dimensional manifold
M
that admits a (non-complete) hyperbolic metric
m
for which the boundary is locally convex, then any Riemannian metric on
\partial M
of curvature
\kappa>-1
can be realized as the induced intrinsic metric for some choice of
m
. In
[
57
]
, Schlenker proved that
m
is uniquely determined by the boundary metric, provided that the boundary is smooth and strictly convex, as is the case for Labourie’s realizations. Here, the boundary is said to be
strictly convex
if the shape operator is positive definite.

From now on,
M
is a 3-manifold diffeomorphic to
S\times[-1,1]
. (The diffeomorphism is fixed for the whole paper.) The anti-de Sitter analog that we are interested in is the following question.

Question 1
.

Let
s_{+},s_{-}
be two Riemannian metrics on
S
of curvature
\kappa<-1
. Is there a unique GHC AdS structure on
M
such that the boundary is smooth and strictly convex, the induced metric on its future boundary is
s_{+}
, and the induced metric on its past boundary is
s_{-}
?

The existence part of this statement was proved by Tamburelli
[
63
]
. However, the uniqueness remains elusive in general. In
[
14
]
, Bonsante–Mondello–Schlenker showed the uniqueness for the case when
s_{+}
,
s_{-}
have constant curvatures
\kappa_{+},\kappa_{-}<-1
respectively, satisfying
\kappa_{+}=-\kappa_{-}/(\kappa_{-}+1)
.

In fact, a key open question is, given such a spacetime
M
, whether any first-order deformation of the AdS structure on
M
that leaves invariant the induced metric on the boundary is trivial. We will discuss this further below.

There is a polyhedral counterpart to this story, when
s_{+},s_{-}
are so-called
cone-metrics
. There are, however, some special peculiarities, since the most natural notion of polyhedral boundary (locally modeled on polyhedral sets) turns out to be insufficient to capture all the picture of what is happening. Some singularities, similar to those of pleating laminations, may appear, which obstruct the analysis of this case. We refer to
[
54
]
of Prosanov for a description, and to
[
51
]
also of Prosanov for a discussion of these phenomena in the hyperbolic setting. In
[
54
]
, a polyhedral realization result for all pairs of cone-metrics satisfying the appropriate curvature condition is derived. Furthermore, the rigidity is established, provided that the cone-metrics are sufficiently small in some sense.
In
[
51
]
, the corresponding hyperbolic realization statement is obtained, and the rigidity is shown in a generic case. Luckily, the mentioned singularities lie outside of the scope of the present article, since they happen away from the Fuchsian locus.

Note that both this paper and
[
54
]
exploit the fact that anti-de Sitter geometry is a subgeometry of projective geometry. However, the other tools are quite different. In the present article, we apply the infninitesimal Pogorelov map and obtain local rigidity near the Fuchsian locus. In
[
54
]
, the technique of geometric transitions is employed, which allows to deduce global rigidity for small enough metrics on the boundary.

1.4.
Our results

Let us fix some notations to state the main results below. Let
\mathscr{M}
be the space of GHC AdS structures on
M
with smooth strictly convex boundary, considered up to isotopy. (The topology on
\mathscr{M}
is discussed in Section
2.4
.) There is a distinguished subspace
\mathscr{M}_{0}\subset\mathscr{M}
of
Fuchsian
structures, i.e., the ones with Fuchsian holonomy. We will give an exact definition in Section
2.4
. For now, we remark that, equivalently,
m
is Fuchsian if and only if
(M,m)
contains an embedded totally geodesic surface isotopic to
S\times\{0\}
. We also denote by
\mathscr{S}
the space of smooth metrics on
S
with curvature
\kappa<-1
, considered up to isotopy.

The Gauss equation in anti-de Sitter 3-space
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
shows that a smooth spacelike strictly convex surface in
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
has an induced metric with curvature
\kappa<-1
. Therefore, there is a natural map
\mathscr{I}:\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{S}\times\mathscr{S}
that sends a GHC AdS structure on
M
to the induced metrics on the two connected components of its boundary. A strengthening of Question
1
is whether
\mathscr{I}
is a homeomorphism. The following statement is a partial answer to it in a neighborhood of Fuchsian structures.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists a neighborhood
U
of
\mathscr{M}_{0}
in
\mathscr{M}
and an open subset
U^{\prime}\subset\mathscr{S}\times\mathscr{S}
, which contains the diagonal, such that
\mathscr{I}|_{U}
is a homeomorphism from
U
to
U^{\prime}
.

We want to stress the rigidity part of Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 1.2
.

There exists a neighborhood
U
of
\mathscr{M}_{0}
in
\mathscr{M}
such that, for any
m_{1},m_{2}\in U
, if there exists a map
f:(M,m_{1})\rightarrow(M,m_{2})
isotopic to the identity such that
f|_{\partial M}
is an isometry, then
f
is isotopic to an isometry.

In Section
5
we consider analogous statements for GHC AdS (2+1)-spacetimes with strictly polyhedral boundary. Here, the notion of being
strictly polyhedral
is a polyhedral analog to strict convexity, see Section
5
for the definitions. We denote by
V
a finite subset of
\partial M
, and denote by
V_{+}
and
V_{-}
its subsets belonging to each boundary components. We assume that both
V_{+}
and
V_{-}
are nonempty. We then denote by
\mathscr{M}(V)
the space of GHC AdS structures on
M
with strictly polyhedral boundary and with vertices at
V
. We also denote by
\mathscr{M}_{0}(V)
the subset of Fuchsian structures, defined the same as in the smooth case.

For each of those AdS structures in
\mathscr{M}(V)
, the induced metric on
\partial M
is hyperbolic, with cone-singularities of angle larger than
2\pi
at the points of
V
. We consider
V_{\pm}
as subsets of
S
and denote by
\mathscr{S}(V_{\pm})
the spaces of hyperbolic metrics with cone-singularities at
V_{\pm}
respectively of angles
>2\pi
. We have the induced metric map
\mathscr{I}_{V}:\mathscr{M}(V)\rightarrow\mathscr{S}(V_{+})\times\mathscr{S}(V_{-})
. We then have the following results.

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists a neighborhood
U
of
\mathscr{M}_{0}(V)
in
\mathscr{M}(V)
and an open subset
U^{\prime}\subset\mathscr{S}(V_{+})\times\mathscr{S}(V_{-})
, which contains the diagonal, such that
\mathscr{I}_{V}|_{U}
is a homeomorphism from
U
to
U^{\prime}
.

Corollary 1.4
.

There exists a neighborhood
U
of
\mathscr{M}_{0}(V)
in
\mathscr{M}(V)
such that for any
m_{1},m_{2}\in U
, if there exists
f:(M,m_{1})\rightarrow(M,m_{2})
isotopic to the identity such that
f|_{\partial M}
is an isometry, then
f
is isotopic to isometry.

1.5.
A word on the proofs

The main issue in answering Question
1
lies in an
infinitesimal rigidity
statement, which can be formulated as follows: Is the differential of
\mathscr{I}
always injective? This can also be stated as follows.

Question 2
.

Let
m\in\mathscr{M}
, and
(s_{+},s_{-})=\mathscr{I}(m)
be the pair of the induced metrics on its boundary. Let
\dot{m}
be a non-trivial first-order deformation of
m
. Is the corresponding variation
(\dot{s}_{+},\dot{s}_{-})
of the induced metrics on the boundary necessarily nonzero?

Note that a first-order deformation
\dot{m}
can include a deformation of the boundary of
(M,m)
, but also a deformation of its holonomy representation.

Unfortunately, we do not know how to prove this infinitesimal rigidity statement in general. For spacetimes with Fuchsian holonomy, however, there is a “trick” that makes it possible. Namely, we use the infinitesimal Pogorelov map (see Section
3.2
) to “translate” this infinitesimal rigidity question into another one concerning globally hyperbolic Minkowski spacetimes, also with Fuchsian holonomy.

We are then in a position to use infinitesimal rigidity statements proved in this Minkowski setting, by Smith
[
62
]
when the boundary is smooth and by Fillastre–Prosanov
[
31
]
when the boundary is polyhedral.

In
[
10
]
, Bonahon proved the rigidity part of Thurston’s pleating lamination conjecture for quasi-Fuchsian hyperbolic 3-manifolds near the Fuchsian locus. A similar strategy was later applied by Bonsante–Schlenker in
[
15
]
in the respective anti-de Sitter situation. There, the infinitesimal rigidity at the Fuchsian locus was translated to a statement in the language of pure Teichmüller theory; no use of the Pogorelov map was necessary. The rest in
[
10
,
15
]
was accomplished by interpreting the infinitesimal rigidity as a statement on transversality of some associated manifolds and then by exploring the special structure of the space of measured laminations. In our smooth case, the last part has to be replaced with some arguments from functional analysis and an application of the Nash–Moser inverse function theorem.

1.6.
Related results

There is a vast body of related results in this area of geometry. Here we survey some of them that we have not mentioned yet, though our exposition does not aim to be exhaustive. For other versions of such a survey, we refer to
[
30
]
by Fillastre–Smith and to
[
59
]
of Schlenker.

We rely on a resolution of Question
1
for Fuchsian structures due to Labourie–Schlenker
[
43
]
in the smooth case and due to Fillastre
[
26
]
in the polyhedral case. The hyperbolic polyhedral case was resolved by Fillastre in an earlier paper
[
25
]
, while the realization part of the hyperbolic smooth case is due to Gromov
[
35
]
. We note that the methods of these papers do not allow to step into a neighborhood of the Fuchsian locus.

It is natural to wonder whether there exists a common generalization of the smooth and the polyhedral cases. For convex bodies in Euclidean 3-space, this question was a starting point for the development by Alexandrov of theory of Alexandrov spaces
[
1
]
, now a cornerstone of modern geometry. The rigidity problem for convex bodies with general boundary was resolved by Pogorelov
[
50
]
, and is known to be notoriously difficult. In the setups relevant to us, some realization results for general convex boundary are
[
61
,
29
,
41
]
by Slutskiy, Fillastre–Slutskiy and Labeni respectively. The only known rigidity result for nontrivial topology and general convex boundary is
[
52
]
by Prosanov for Fuchsian hyperbolic 3-manifolds.

Up to scaling, there are three types of spacetimes of constant curvature: anti-de Sitter, Minkowski and de Sitter. (Note that in dimension (2+1), solutions of Einstein’s equations necessarily have constant curvature.) Mess also classified GHMC Minkowski (2+1)-spacetimes in
[
46
]
, while the classification of the de Sitter ones was completed by Scannell in
[
56
]
. Questions similar to ours can also be formulated in these settings, though they have to be stated not in the sense of extending metrics from the boundary but rather in the sense of simultaneous isometric embeddings of pairs of surfaces into pairs of spacetimes with the same holonomy. In the Minkowski setting, the smooth case is due to Smith
[
62
]
and the polyhedral case is due to Fillastre–Prosanov
[
31
]
. The de Sitter setting turns our to be dual in some sense to the hyperbolic setting and is covered by the papers
[
57
]
and
[
53
]
of Schlenker and Prosanov. It is interesting to note that the toolboxes used differ from setting to setting and between the smooth and the polyhedral cases.

Question
1
and its hyperbolic counterpart can be interpreted as a realization and rigidity statements for convex domains in anti-de Sitter 3-space or in hyperbolic 3-space that are invariant with respect to a discrete and faithful representation of a surface group. It is natural to wonder about the validity of universal versions of such results, i.e., realizations of non-invariant metrics without group actions. Clearly, for the rigidity to hold, one must impose a kind of gluing condition at infinity. We refer to a survey
[
58
]
of Schlenker and to papers
[
13
,
20
,
44
,
45
]
of Bonsante–Danciger–Maloni–Schlenker, Chen–Schlenker, Merlin–Schlenker and Mesbah for some advances on the respective realization problems. The rigidity, while expected to hold, remains widely open.

The classical result of Bers
[
9
]
concerns the prescription of conformal structures at infinity for quasi-Fuchsian hyperbolic 3-manifolds. One can, however, define also measured foliations at infinity, which are analogs at infinity of the pleating laminations of the convex core. In this setting, an analog of Bonahon’s result
[
10
]
was obtained in
[
21
]
by Choudhury.

Question
1
is also equivalent to a question of simultaneous isometric embeddings of two metrics on
S
into GHMC AdS (2+1)-spacetimes. Another interesting problem is to attempt to embed only one metric into a spacetime with prescribed left or right metric in the sense of Mess. This problem in the smooth case was fully resolved in
[
63
]
by Tamburelli (realization) and in
[
19
]
by Chen–Schlenker (rigidity). In the polyhedral case, the realization was shown in
[
54
]
by Prosanov, as well as the rigidity for sufficiently small metrics. In the Minkowski setting, instead of left or right metric it is natural to prescribe the linear part of the holonomy. In the smooth case, this follows from the work
[
65
]
by Trapani–Valli, while in the polyhedral case, it is proved in
[
31
]
by Fillastre–Prosanov. Actually, we will employ some of these results in our proof. We note that analogous questions in the de Sitter and in the hyperbolic settings (when the left or right metric is replaced by a conformal structure at infinity) are fully open.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Anti-de Sitter geometry

The main reference for us is the excellent survey
[
17
]
by Bonsante–Seppi. Other good sources include the original paper of Mess
[
46
]
, an accompanying paper
[
2
]
, a survey
[
5
]
of Barbot and a survey
[
30
]
of Fillastre–Smith.

First we recall the quadric model for anti-de Sitter 3-space. Let
\mathbb{R}^{2,2}
be the 4-dimensional real vector space equipped with a symmetric bilinear form of signature
(2,2)
:

\langle x,y\rangle=x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}-x_{3}y_{3}-x_{4}y_{4}.

Anti-de Sitter 3-space is modeled on the following quadric

\mathbb{H}^{2,1}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2,2}:~\langle x,x\rangle=-1\}.

The induced metric is a Lorentzian metric of constant curvature
-1
.

We will, however, consider as the main model for anti-de Sitter 3-space the projective quotient of
\mathbb{H}^{2,1}
. We will denote it by
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
and consider it as a subset of
\mathbb{RP}^{3}
. Recall that it is a minimal model space for anti-de Sitter geometry, i.e., any other Lorentzian 3-space of curvature
-1
with maximal isometry group is a covering of
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
, see
[
17
]
.

We also briefly recall the
{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
-model for
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
, which is a special feature of dimension 3. Note that on the space of real 2
\times
2-matrices the determinant is a quadratic form of signature
(2,2)
. Considering the polarization of the minus determinant as a bilinear form, we can identify this space with
\mathbb{R}^{2,2}
. Under this identification,
{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
gets identified with
\mathbb{H}^{2,1}
, and
{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
gets identified with
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

This model admits a straightforward description of the isometry group of
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. We are interested only in its identity component, which we denote by
G
. Since for
A,B,X\in{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
we have
\det(X)=\det(AXB^{-1})
, the left and right multiplications by the elements of
{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
are isometries. This produces a map
{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})\times{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})\rightarrow G
. A dimension count shows that this is actually an isomorphism

(2.1)

G\cong{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})\times{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R}).

We fix an orientation and a time-orientation on
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. The latter means that we pick a (nowhere zero) timelike vector field
\xi
on
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
to define future and past. Namely, for every
x\in\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
the subset of timelike vectors of
T_{x}\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
has two connected components. The vectors from the same component as
\xi(x)
are called
future
vectors, the others are called
past
.

2.2.
Quasi-Fuchsian representations

To proceed further, we need to recall the basics of Teichmüller theory. The
Teichmüller space

\mathscr{T}
is the space of isotopy classes of hyperbolic metrics on
S
. Denote by
\mathbb{H}^{2}
the hyperbolic plane and by
G_{F}
the identity component of its isometry group. Every hyperbolic metric on
S
is a
(G_{F},\mathbb{H}^{2})
-structure in the sense of Thurston. For an introduction to Thurston’s approach to geometric structures (later we will also apply this framework to anti-de Sitter structures), we refer to Thurston’s notes
[
64
]
and an accompanying paper
[
18
]
. Provided that
S
and
\mathbb{H}^{2}
are considered oriented, the holonomies of orientation-preserving hyperbolic structures form a connected component in the representation space of
\pi_{1}S
to
G_{F}
. We call such representations
Fuchsian
and denote by
\mathcal{R}_{F}
the quotient of this component by conjugation of
G_{F}
, the component itself is denoted by
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{F}
. In terms of notation, we will not distinguish between representations and their classes. It is well-known that the map sending a hyperbolic structure to its holonomy provides an identification
\mathscr{T}\cong\mathcal{R}_{F}
. See, e.g., the book of Farb–Margalit
[
24
]
for an introduction.

Consider
G_{F}\cong{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
as the diagonal subgroup of
G\cong{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})\times{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
. We call a representation
\rho:\pi_{1}S\rightarrow G

quasi-Fuchsian

if it is a deformation of a Fuchsian representation.
By definition, quasi-Fuchsian representations form a connected component of a representation space. We denote by
\mathcal{R}
the quotient by conjugation of
G
, the component itself is denoted by
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
. The projections of a quasi-Fuchsian representation to both factors in (
2.1
) are discrete and faithful. Hence, we have
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\cong\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{F}\times\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{F}
and

(2.2)

\mathcal{R}\cong\mathcal{R}_{F}\times\mathcal{R}_{F}\cong\mathscr{T}\times\mathscr{T}.

For a given
\rho\in\mathcal{R}
, identification (
2.2
) produces two hyperbolic metrics on
S
(given up to isotopy). They are called
the left and the right metrics
of
\rho
.

The quasi-Fuchsian representations in this sense are exactly the holonomies of GHMC AdS (2+1)-spacetimes, as we explain in the next subsection. We point out a slight discrepancy with the classical setting of 3-dimensional hyperbolic geometry that was discussed in the introduction, where
G_{F}\cong{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
is considered naturally embedded into
{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
and only quasi-conformal deformations of Fuchsian representations are called quasi-Fuchsian. They then form the interior of the respective component of the representation space. There are also representations at the boundary, not quasi-Fuchsian in this sense, which do not have counterparts in the anti-de Sitter setting.

2.3.
Globally hyperbolic anti-de Sitter spacetimes

An
anti-de Sitter (2+1)-spacetime
is a 3-manifold equipped with an anti-de Sitter metric, i.e., with a Lorentzian metric of curvature
-1
. Provided that it is oriented and time-oriented, it is equipped with a
(G,\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3})
-structure in the sense of Thurston. Hence, every anti-de Sitter (2+1)-spacetime
M
is equipped with a holonomy representation
\rho:\pi_{1}M\rightarrow G
and a
\rho
-equivariant developing map
f:\widetilde{M}\rightarrow G
, which are determined up to an isometry of
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. A (2+1)-spacetime is
globally hyperbolic
if it admits a
Cauchy surface
, i.e., a surface that is intersected exactly once by every inextensible causal curve. All Cauchy surfaces in a spacetime are homeomorphic to each other. See, e.g., the book
[
49
]
by O’Neill for an introduction to Lorentzian geometry. By a result of Geroch
[
33
]
, any globally hyperbolic (2+1)-spacetime admits a parametrization
S\times\mathbb{R}
, in which all fibers
S\times\{r\}
are Cauchy surfaces.

A globally hyperbolic spacetime
N
is
maximal
if every isometric embedding
N\rightarrow N^{\prime}
that sends a Cauchy surface of
N
to a Cauchy surface of
N^{\prime}
is onto. It is
Cauchy compact
if its Cauchy surfaces are compact. Recall that we refer to globally hyperbolic maximal Cauchy compact anti-de Sitter spacetimes as GHMC AdS for short. Any GHMC (2+1)-spacetime
N
is diffeomorphic to
S\times\mathbb{R}
, and hence
\pi_{1}N=\pi_{1}S
. Let now
N
be a manifold diffeomorphic to
S\times\mathbb{R}
, and let
\mathscr{N}
be the space of isotopy classes of GHMC AdS structures on
N
, with the topology induced by uniform
C^{\infty}
-convergence of developing maps on compact subsets of
\widetilde{N}
.
Mess showed in
[
46
]
that the holonomy of every GHMC AdS (2+1)-spacetime is quasi-Fuchsian. Furthermore, it follows from his work that the map
\mathscr{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}
sending a GHMC AdS structure to its holonomy is a homeomorphism.
Any spacelike surface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
invariant with respect to
\rho\in\mathcal{R}
belongs to the
domain of dependence

D(\rho)\subset\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
, a convex open
\rho
-invariant set, whose quotient by
\rho
produces the respective GHMC AdS spacetime.

2.4.
Spacetimes with boundary and spaces of embeddings

Recall that we use GHC AdS (2+1)-spacetimes to mean globally hyperbolic compact anti-de Sitter (2+1)-spacetimes with spacelike convex boundary.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be the space of GHC AdS structures on
M
with smooth strictly convex boundary. We pick up the viewpoint of locally homogeneous geometry, already mentioned above, and consider elements of
\mathcal{M}
as
G
-conjugacy classes of pairs
(\rho,f)
of holonomies
\rho:\pi_{1}M=\pi_{1}S\rightarrow G
and of
\rho
-equivariant developing maps
f:\widetilde{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. We endow it with the topology of uniform
C^{\infty}
-convergence on compact subsets of
\widetilde{M}
. Let
\mathscr{M}
be the quotient of
\mathcal{M}
by isotopies. A structure is
Fuchsian
if its holonomy is conjugate into
G_{F}
.

We will, however, choose a different space to focus our consideration on. To this purpose, we will now notice that an element of
\mathscr{M}
is determined by the “full” structure of the boundary, that is, by a lift of its two boundary components to equivariantly embedded complete surfaces in
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Our eventual goal in this paper is to show that, in certain cases, only a part of this data, namely the induced metrics, is sufficient to determine
\mathscr{M}
.

We say that an immersion of a surface to
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
is
future-convex
(resp.
past-convex
) if it is smooth, spacelike, locally convex, and for each point its future cone (resp. its past cone) locally belongs to the convex side. A strictly future-/past-convex immersion is a one that is future-/past-convex and strictly convex.
Let
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
be the space of strictly future-convex orientation-preserving immersions
f:\widetilde{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
equivariant with respect to a representation
\rho:\pi_{1}S\rightarrow G
. We endow it with the topology of uniform
C^{\infty}
-convergence on compact subsets of
\widetilde{S}
. As it follows from the work of Mess
[
46
]
, any arising representation is quasi-Fuchsian and any such immersion is an embedding. Let
\mathcal{E}_{+}
be the quotient of
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
by
G
. Similarly, let
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{-}
and
\mathcal{E}_{-}
be the spaces of equivariant strictly past-convex orientation-preserving embeddings and of their classes. Denote by
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}
the subset of
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{+}\times\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{-}
consisting of pairs of embeddings that are equivariant with respect to the same representation, and by
\mathcal{E}
denote its quotient by
G
. Finally, let
\mathscr{E}
be the quotient of
\mathcal{E}
by isotopies (actually, by pairs of isotopies of
S
, as for this quotient we consider pairs of maps
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
, representing
e\in\mathcal{E}
, separately).

We claim that
\mathscr{M}
is naturally homeomorphic to
\mathscr{E}
. Indeed, from the description of the spaces it follows that there is a natural continuous injective map
\iota:\mathscr{M}\rightarrow\mathscr{E}
, sending a GHC AdS structure on
M
with smooth and strictly convex boundary to the (equivalence class under
G
of the) pair of equivariant embeddings
f_{\pm}:\widetilde{S}\to\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
of its boundary components. It is also clear that
\iota
is surjective. It only remains to check that
\iota^{-1}
is continuous.

Let
(\rho_{i},f_{i,+},f_{i,-})
be a sequence in
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}
converging to
(\rho,f_{+},f_{-})
. The convergence of
\rho_{i}
implies that
\rho_{i}
and
\rho
determine on
N
GHMC AdS structures
n_{i}
uniformly
C^{\infty}
-converging on compacts to a GHMC AdS structure
n
. Furthermore, the convergence of
f_{i,+}
and
f_{i,-}
implies that the pairs of surfaces in
N
corresponding to
(f_{i,+},f_{i,-})
converge to the ones corresponding to
(f_{+},f_{-})
. By associating the part of
N
bounded by these pairs of surfaces with
M
, one can deduce that
(\rho_{i},f_{i,+},f_{i,-})
determine on
M
GHC AdS structures that converge to such a structure determined by
(\rho,f_{+},f_{-})
.

Hence, we see that
\iota
is a homeomorphism. From now on we forget about the spaces
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathscr{M}
and work only with
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathscr{E}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{S}
the space of Riemannian metrics on
S
of curvature
<-1
, and denote by
\mathscr{S}
its quotient by isotopies.
We have the respective induced metric map
\mathcal{I}:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}
, descending to a map
\mathscr{E}\rightarrow\mathscr{S}\times\mathscr{S}
, which from now on we will denote by
\mathscr{I}
. We call
Fuchsian
elements of
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}
whose representation is in
G_{F}
. This notion descends to elements of
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathscr{E}
. We denote by
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{F}\subset\widehat{\mathcal{E}}
the subspace of Fuchsian elements, and denote by
\mathcal{E}_{F}
its quotient by conjugation. What we will actually prove is

Theorem 2.1
.

There exists a neighborhood
U
of
\mathcal{E}_{F}
in
\mathcal{E}
such that the image of
U
by
\mathcal{I}
is open, contains the diagonal and
\mathcal{I}|_{U}
is a homeomorphism onto its image.

Note that the map
\mathcal{I}
is equivariant with respect to isotopies. Thus Theorem
2.1
implies a similar result for the map
\mathscr{I}
and, through our discussion, implies Theorem
1.1
. From now on we will actually also forget about the spaces
\mathscr{E}
and
\mathscr{S}
.

2.5.
Reminder on group cohomology

In the next section, we discuss the differentiable structure on our spaces. We will then use basic properties of representation varieties, which we recall here. We briefly describe a construction that we will use several times. Let
\rho:\pi_{1}S\rightarrow G
be a representation into a Lie group
G
and
\mathfrak{m}
be a
G
-module (most frequently we use
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{g}
, though not only). It becomes a
\pi_{1}S
-module via the representation
\rho
. We denote by
Z^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{m})=Z^{1}_{\rho}(\pi_{1}S,\mathfrak{m})
the vector space of (first)
\mathfrak{m}
-valued
cocycles
of
\pi_{1}S
, i.e., maps
\tau:\pi_{1}S\rightarrow\mathfrak{m}
for all
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\pi_{1}S
satisfying

\tau(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2})=\tau(\gamma_{1})+\rho(\gamma_{1})\tau(\gamma_{2}).

A cocycle is called
coboundary
if for some
x\in\mathfrak{m}
and all
\gamma\in\pi_{1}S
we have

\tau(\gamma)=\rho(\gamma)x-x.

Denote the subspace of coboundaries by
B^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{m})=B^{1}_{\rho}(\pi_{1}S,\mathfrak{m})
and define the (first)
\mathfrak{m}
-valued cohomology of
\pi_{1}S
as

H^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{m})=H^{1}(\pi_{1},\mathfrak{m}):=Z^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{m})/B^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{m}).

In particular, if
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
is the representation variety of
\pi_{1}S
into
G
, it is convenient to perceive its Zariski tangent space at
\rho
as
Z^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{g})
, where
\mathfrak{g}
is considered as a
G
-module via the adjoint representation. This is a construction going back to Weil
[
67
]
. See also the book
[
55
]
for details. Then
B^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{g})
is the Zariski tangent space to the scheme associated to the
G
-orbit of
\rho
under conjugation, and
H^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{g})
can be interpreted as the Zariski tangent space to the scheme associated to the quotient of
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
by conjugation of
G
. In our cases, the components of the representation variety under our consideration are smooth, the action of
G
on it is free and proper, and all these can be thought as the standard tangent spaces.

2.6.
Differentiable structures on spaces of embeddings

As a main reference for Fréchet spaces and Fréchet manifolds we use the excellent survey
[
36
]
of Hamilton.

Consider a smooth path
(\rho_{t},f_{t})\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
with
(\rho_{0},f_{0})=(\rho,f)
. As recalled in Section
2.5
, a tangent vector to
\rho_{t}:\pi_{1}S\rightarrow G
at
t=0
can be described by a cocycle
\dot{\rho}\in Z^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{g})
, which is a map
\dot{\rho}:\pi_{1}S\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}
. A tangent vector to
f_{t}
at
t=0
is a vector field
\dot{f}
defined on
f(\widetilde{S})
. An easy computation shows that the condition of
\rho_{t}
-equivariance of
f_{t}
translates at the first order into the condition of
automorphicity
with respect to
\dot{\rho}
:

(2.3)

\dot{f}(\rho(\gamma)x)=d\rho(\gamma)(\dot{f}(x))+\dot{\rho}(\gamma)(\rho(\gamma)x)~.

Here elements of
\mathfrak{g}
are interpreted as Killing fields on
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. An automorphic vector field is
trivial
if it is the restriction of a Killing field of
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Note that this in particular implies that
\dot{\rho}
is a coboundary.

Pick
(\rho,f)\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
. Denote by
\widetilde{V}_{f}
the restriction of
T\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
to
f(\widetilde{S})
. Denote by
C^{\infty}_{\rho}(\widetilde{V}_{f})
the space of vector fields that are automorphic with respect to some
\dot{\rho}\in Z^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{g})
. This is a Fréchet space, and
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
can be considered as a Fréchet manifold with charts in
C^{\infty}_{\rho}(\widetilde{V}_{f})
. In particular,
T_{(\rho,f)}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{+}\cong C^{\infty}_{\rho}(\widetilde{V}_{f})
. There is a natural fibration
\widehat{\pi}_{+}:\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{+}\rightarrow\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
, i.e., a surjective submersion. The group
G
acts on
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
freely and properly, and so it does on
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
. The quotient
\mathcal{E}_{+}
is also naturally a Fréchet manifold, and there is a surjective submersion
\pi_{+}:\mathcal{E}_{+}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}
. The same relates to the spaces
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{-}
,
\mathcal{E}_{-}
,
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}
and
\mathcal{E}
. It is also standard that
\mathcal{S}
is a Fréchet manifold.

2.7.
Gauss-Codazzi fields on surfaces

Pick
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Thanks to the fundamental theorem of surfaces, the space of its isometric embeddings to
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
has a nice parametrization in terms of
GC-fields
.
Denote the curvature of
s
by
\kappa:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and its Levi-Civita connection by
\nabla
. A
GC-field
for
s
is an
s
-symmetric operator field
b:TS\rightarrow TS
satisfying the Gauss–Codazzi equations:

(Gauss)
\det(b)=-\kappa-1
;

(Codazzi)
d^{\nabla}b=0
.

Denote by
\mathcal{GC}(s)\subset C^{\infty}(T^{1}_{1}S)
the space of GC-fields on
(S,s)
. It is known from
[
63
, Lemma 3.2]
of Tamburelli that it is a finite-dimensional submanifold of dimension
6k-6
. Denote by
\mathcal{GC}_{+}(s)
and
\mathcal{GC}_{-}(s)
the subspaces of those
GC
-fields that have positive (resp. negative) eigenvalues. The fundamental theorem of surfaces (see, e.g.,
[
3
, Section 7]
) allows to define two maps

\psi_{s,\pm}:\mathcal{GC}_{\pm}(s)\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{\pm}~

so that a GC-field is the shape operator of the respective embedding. An examination of the proof of the fundamental theorem allows to ensure that

Theorem 2.2
.

\psi_{s,\pm}
are
C^{1}
-immersions.

We will benefit from employing the dual viewpoint on the tangent vectors to
\mathcal{GC}_{\pm}(s)
: as tensor fields and as automorphic vector fields.

For a given
s\in\mathcal{S}
with the almost-complex structure
j
and a GC-field
b
, consider the following two metrics

(2.4)

s(({\rm id}+jb)\cdot,({\rm id}+jb)\cdot),~~~s(({\rm id}-jb)\cdot,({\rm id}-jb)\cdot)),

where
{\rm id}
is the identity operator.
It was shown by Krasnov–Schlenker
[
39
]
that those are hyperbolic metrics on
S
. Furthermore, they represent the left and the right metrics of the corresponding quasi-Fuchsian representation. By taking the isotopy classes, we obtain two smooth maps

\phi_{s,\pm}:\mathcal{GC}_{\pm}(s)\rightarrow\mathscr{T}\times\mathscr{T}\cong\mathcal{R}

b\mapsto\Big(\big[s(({\rm id}+jb)\cdot,({\rm id}+jb)\cdot)\big],\big[s(({\rm id}-jb)\cdot,({\rm id}-jb)\cdot))\big]\Big).

Note that
\phi_{s,\pm}
coincide with
\pi_{\pm}\circ\psi_{s,\pm}
. It was shown by Chen–Schlenker
[
19
]
that

Theorem 2.3
.

For every
s\in\mathcal{S}
the compositions of
\phi_{s,\pm}
with the projection to any of the factors in
\mathscr{T}\times\mathscr{T}
is a diffeomorphism.

We will need from this a weaker fact that both
\phi_{s,\pm}
are immersions. Curiously, we do not know a proof of this not relying on Theorem
2.3
.
We will also rely on the work of Labourie–Schlenker
[
43
]
, which implies

Theorem 2.4
.

The restriction
\mathcal{I}|_{\mathcal{E}_{F}}
is a homeomorphism onto the image.

3.
Infinitesimal rigidity

This section is devoted to a proof of

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
e\in\mathcal{E}_{F}
. Then
d_{e}\mathcal{I}
is injective.

To this goal, in the next subsection we deal first with equivariant convex surfaces in Minkowski 3-space
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
. We deduce from the work of Smith
[
62
]
that a pair of strictly future-convex and past-convex surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
, equivariant with respect to the same quasi-Fuchsian representation, is rigid with respect to the simultaneous automorphic infinitesimal isometric deformations. After that, in the next subsection we introduce the infinitesimal Pogorelov map from anti-de Sitter 3-space to Minkowski 3-space, and conclude Proposition
3.1
from the corresponding Minkowski result.

3.1.
Equivariant surfaces in Minkowski 3-space

Let
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
be Minkowski 3-space with the origin
o
. Denote the identity component of its isometry group by
G^{0}
. We consider here
G_{F}
as a subgroup of
G^{0}
stabilizing
o
. We have

(3.1)

G^{0}\cong G_{F}\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{2,1}.

As previously, we call a representation
\rho:\pi_{1}S\rightarrow G^{0}

quasi-Fuchsian
if it is a deformation of a Fuchsian representation
\pi_{1}S\rightarrow G_{F}
. They form a component of the representation variety. We denote by
\mathcal{R}^{0}
the quotient of this component by conjugation of
G^{0}
, while we denote the component itself by
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{0}
. They and the associated spacetimes were actively studied also by Mess
[
46
]
, as well as by Barbot, Bonsante, Seppi and others
[
4
,
12
,
6
,
16
]
.

For
\rho\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{0}
, its linear part, i.e., the projection to the
G_{F}
-factor in (
3.1
), is a Fuchsian representation
\rho_{F}\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{F}
. It is twisted by a cocycle
\tau\in Z^{1}_{\rho_{F}}(\mathbb{R}^{2,1})
, where
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
is considered as a
G_{F}
-module via the standard action by isometries. This means that for
\gamma\in\pi_{1}S
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
we have

\rho(\gamma)x=\rho_{F}(\gamma)x+\tau(\gamma).

The space
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{0}
is a (trivial) vector bundle over
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{F}
with the fiber at
\rho_{F}\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{F}
identified to
Z^{1}_{\rho_{F}}(\mathbb{R}^{2,1})
. The conjugacy action of
G^{0}
, in particular, contains an action by coboundaries on each fiber, and
\mathcal{R}^{0}
is a (trivial) vector bundle over
\mathcal{R}_{F}
with the fiber at
\rho_{F}\in{\mathcal{R}}_{F}
identified to
H^{1}_{\rho_{F}}(\mathbb{R}^{2,1})
.

The Minkowski cross-product allows to construct a canonical isomorphism between
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{F}
as
G_{F}
-modules. See, e.g.,
[
28
, Section 3]
of Fillastre–Seppi. Thereby, we have identification
H^{1}_{\rho_{F}}(\mathfrak{g}_{F})\cong H^{1}_{\rho_{F}}(\mathbb{R}^{2,1})
. Furthermore, we have

(3.2)

\mathcal{R}^{0}\cong T\mathcal{R}_{F}\cong T\mathscr{T}.

We can repeat some definitions from Section
2.4
for immersions of surfaces into Minkowski 3-space.
Let
s
be a negatively curved metric on
S
and
\kappa:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be its curvature. Denote by
\mathcal{GC}^{0}_{+}(s)
and
\mathcal{GC}^{0}_{-}(s)
the spaces of GC-fields on
(S,s)
that have both eigenvalues of the respective sign. (For Minkowski 3-space the Gauss equation is
\det(b)=-\kappa
.) Similarly to the anti-de Sitter situation, they are endowed with topologies of smooth manifolds of dimension
6k-6
. The fundamental theorem of surfaces allows to interpret each element of
\mathcal{GC}^{0}_{\pm}(s)
as a spacelike equivariant surface in
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
. By considering the respective representation, we obtain two smooth maps

\phi_{s,\pm}^{0}:\mathcal{GC}^{0}_{\pm}(s)\rightarrow T\mathscr{T}.

Denote by
\chi_{s,\pm}^{0}:\mathcal{GC}^{0}_{\pm}(s)\rightarrow\mathscr{T}
the compositions of
\phi_{s,\pm}^{0}
with the projection to
\mathscr{T}
.
For
b\in\mathcal{GC}^{0}_{\pm}(s)
, the element
\chi_{s,\pm}^{0}(b)
is given by the isotopy class of the metric
s(b\cdot,b\cdot)
, see, e.g.,
[
62
]
.

Note that in contrast to the anti-de Sitter situation, a pair of a future-convex and a past-convex surfaces in Minkowski 3-space, equivariant with respect to the same representation, never bound together a subset, on which the action of the representation is free and properly discontinuous. A quasi-Fuchsian representation into
G^{0}
has two disconnected maximal convex domains of discontinuity in
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
, one is future-convex and one is past-convex.

The following result is a corollary of the work of Trapani–Valli
[
65
]
, as was noticed by Smith in
[
62
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

For any negatively curved metric
s
,
\chi_{s,\pm}^{0}
are diffeomorphisms.

In particular, the maps
\phi^{0}_{s,\pm}
are immersions. For
\sigma\in\mathscr{T}
define

x_{s}(\sigma):=\phi_{s,+}^{0}\circ(\chi_{s,+}^{0})^{-1}(\sigma).

This gives us a vector field
x_{s}
on
\mathscr{T}
defined by
s
. If
f:\widetilde{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
is a
\rho
-equivariant future-convex isometric embedding for a quasi-Fuchsian
\rho
with cocycle
\tau
, then
-f
is a past-convex isometric embedding equivariant with respect to a quasi-Fuchsian representation with the same linear part and
-\tau
as the cocycle. Hence,

-x_{s}(\sigma)=\phi_{s,-}^{0}\circ(\chi_{s,-}^{0})^{-1}(\sigma).

There is another related vector field over
\mathscr{T}
that we associate to
s
.
To proceed, we need to recall that due to the uniformization theorem, the Teichmüller space
\mathscr{T}
can be also considered as the space of isotopy classes of conformal structures. See, e.g.,
[
66
]
for details.
Let
\mathcal{S}^{*}
be the space of all Riemannian metrics on
S
. For
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
we denote by
\mathcal{O}_{\sigma}
the space of
\sigma
-symmetric operator fields on
S
. Any
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
determines an isomorphism between the subspace of positive-definite elements of
\mathcal{O}_{\sigma}
and
\mathcal{S}^{*}
by
a\mapsto\sigma(a.,.)
. Denote the differential at the identity of this map by
\zeta_{\sigma}:\mathcal{O}_{\sigma}\rightarrow T_{\sigma}\mathcal{S}^{*}
, which is also an isomorphism. By taking the conformal class of a metric, we get the projection
\mathcal{S}^{*}\rightarrow\mathscr{T}
. We compose the differential of this projection at
\sigma
with
\zeta_{\sigma}
and obtain a map

\eta_{\sigma}:\mathcal{O}_{\sigma}\rightarrow T_{\sigma}\mathscr{T}.

Now define another vector field
x_{s}^{\prime}
on
\mathscr{T}
by

x_{s}^{\prime}(\sigma):=\eta_{\sigma}\circ{\rm inv}\circ(\chi_{s,+}^{0})^{-1}(\sigma),

where
{\rm inv}
is the operation of taking the inverse on
C^{\infty}(T^{1}_{1}S)
, i.e.,
{\rm inv}(b)=b^{-1}
.

Recall that there is a canonical complex structure on
\mathscr{T}
, which we denote by
\mathscr{J}
. Bonsante–Seppi showed that
\mathscr{J}x^{\prime}_{s}=x_{s}

[
16
, Theorem B]
.

A Riemannian metric
\sigma
on
S
determines an
L^{2}
-scalar product on all tensor fields over
S
. For operator fields
a_{1},a_{2}
, which are
(1,1)
-tensor fields, it is expressed as

\langle a_{1},a_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\int_{S}{\rm tr}(a_{1}a_{2})d{\rm area}_{\sigma}.

The element

is the hyperbolic representative of the conformal class, the

-scalar product produces the Weil–Petersson scalar product on the Teichmüller space, up to a universal constant depending on a convention. It also follows from the discussion that if

and

,

Now return to an equivariant isometric immersion
(\rho,f)
with induced metric

be the linear part of

be the hyperbolic metric on

[

of Bonsante–Seppi. Define

Due to Theorem

is indeed a well-defined function of a negatively curved metric

,
\sigma

as a function

over the Teichmüller space

that

Lemma 3.3
.

Pick

. If

Corollary 3.4
.

The vector field

is the Weil–Petersson gradient field of
F_{s}
and
-x_{s}
is the symplectic Weil–Petersson gradient field of

.

Furthermore, Smith proved
[
62
, Lemma 5.6]

is strictly convex with respect to the Weil–Petersson metric.

From this we can deduce

.

For any two negatively curved metrics

and

, the vector fields

and
-x_{s_{-}}^{\prime}

and

If at a point

∎

Note that Smith also proved in

All the discussion allows us to obtain

Lemma 3.7

Let

Here

,

to

Proof.

Let

and

and

3.2.
The infinitesimal Pogorelov map

Recall the definition of
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
from Section
2.1
. We now pick a convenient affine chart for it, given by the projection to the plane
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2,2}:x_{4}=1\}
. This plane is also a model for Minkowski 3-space, and we will consider it endowed simultaneously with both geometries. We denote it now by
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
, and by the origin we will mean the point
o=(0,0,0,1)
. The part of
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
that is captured in this plane is the interior of a one-sheeted hyperboloid

A:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2,1}:x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-x_{3}^{2}<1\}.

Let
C
be the union of the future- and the past-cones of
o
. (Note that the future- and the past-cones of
o
coincide both in Minkowski and in anti-de Sitter geometries.) Denote by
r:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
the Minkowski distance to
o
on
C
, considered as a positive real number.

There is an (infinitesimal) Pogorelov map
\Phi:TC\rightarrow TC
defined as follows. For
x\in C
and
v\in T_{x}C
let
v_{r}
be its radial component, i.e., parallel to the direction of the ray
ox
, and
v_{l}
be its lateral component, i.e., orthogonal to the radial direction. (Note that the orthogonality to the radial directions is the same both in Minkowski and in anti-de Sitter geometries.) Then

\Phi(v):=\frac{v_{r}}{1-r^{2}(x)}+v_{l}.

The map
\Phi
is an automorphism of
TC
. Its key property is

Lemma 3.8
.

The map
\Phi
sends anti-de Sitter Killing fields to Minkowski Killing fields. The inverse
\Phi^{-1}
sends Minkowski Killing fields to anti-de Sitter Killing fields.

Here, for convenience, we only consider the restrictions of the Killing fields to
C
, though one can define
\Phi
over the whole tangent bundle
TA
of
A
, see, e.g.,
[
22
]
.
See
[
26
, Lemma 11]
of Fillastre for a proof of Lemma
3.8
, which is based on
[
57
, Lemma 1.10]
of Schlenker. In particular,
\Phi
determines an isomorphism
\Psi:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}^{0}
of Lie algebras, considered as vector spaces. Furthermore, every
\gamma\in G_{F}
preserves the decomposition into radial and lateral parts, as well as the function
r
. Hence,
\Phi
is
G_{F}
-equivariant. In turn, this implies

Lemma 3.9
.

\Psi
is an isomorphism of
G_{F}
-modules.

For a Fuchsian
\rho:\pi_{1}S\rightarrow G_{F}
, Lemma
3.9
establishes a canonical isomorphism

\Psi_{\rho}:Z^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{g})\rightarrow Z^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{g}^{0}),

which sends coboundaries onto coboundaries, and hence also induces an isomorphism of cohomologies. Now we also note

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\Sigma\subset C
be a submanifold and
v
be an infinitesimal isometric deformation of
\Sigma
with respect to the anti-de Sitter metric. Then
\Phi(v)
is an infinitesimal isometric deformation of
\Sigma
with respect to the Minkowski metric.

A proof follows word by word the proof of Lemma 1.10 in
[
57
]
, which deals with the Pogorelov map from hyperbolic space to Euclidean space. The role of relevant metric computations is played by Lemmas 12 and 13 from
[
26
]
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\rho\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{F}
,
\Sigma\subset C
be a
\rho
-invariant submanifold and
v
be a vector field over
\Sigma
, automorphic with respect to
\dot{\rho}\in Z^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{g})
. Then
\Phi(v)
is automorphic with respect to
\Psi_{\rho}(\dot{\rho})\in Z^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{g}^{0})
.

Proof.

For all vector fields over
\Sigma
we use subscripts
r
,
l
to denote the radial and the lateral components. The key point is that Fuchsian representations preserve the function
r
, and hence the Pogorelov map is well-behaved. Here is the computation. For
x\in\Sigma
we have

d\rho(\gamma)\big(\Phi(v(x))\big)+\Psi_{\rho}(\dot{\rho})(\gamma)(\rho(\gamma)x)=\frac{d\rho(\gamma)(v_{r}(x))+\dot{\rho}(\gamma)_{r}(\rho(\gamma)x)}{1-r^{2}(x)}+

+d\rho(\gamma)(v_{l}(x))+\dot{\rho}(\gamma)_{l}(\rho(\gamma)x)=\frac{v_{r}(\rho(\gamma)x)}{1-r^{2}(\rho(\gamma)x)}+v_{l}(\rho(\gamma)x)=\Phi(v(\rho(\gamma)x)),

which verifies the automorphicity condition (
2.3
).
∎

Now we have all in our hands to conclude a proof.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Consider
e\in\mathcal{E}_{F}
. Lift it to a triple
(\rho,f_{+},f_{-})\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{F}
of a representation
\rho:\pi_{1}S\rightarrow G_{F}
, where
G_{F}
is considered as a subgroup of
G
, and of
\rho
-equivariant developing maps
f_{+},f_{-}:\widetilde{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. We assume that
o
is the fixed point of
G_{F}
as a subgroup of
G
. Then the images of
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
are contained in
C
. Pick
\dot{e}\in T_{e}\mathcal{E}
such that
d_{e}\mathcal{I}(\dot{e})=0
. It produces a cocycle
\dot{\rho}\in Z^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{g})
and a
\dot{\rho}
-automorphic vector field
v
on
f_{+}(\widetilde{S})
and
f_{-}(\widetilde{S})
, isometric with respect to the anti-de Sitter metric.

Apply the Pogorelov map
\Phi
to

. The combination of Lemma
3.11
and Lemma
3.10
implies that
\Phi(v)
is a
\Psi_{\rho}(\dot{\rho})
-automorphic vector field on
f_{+}(\widetilde{S})
and

,

.
∎

Remark 3.12
.

It is interesting to notice that even though one can define the infinitesimal Pogorelov map outside of
C
, this proof works only when the pair of embeddings is Fuchsian. The reason is that for a general equivariant embedding, its image in the projective model will not be equivariant with respect to some Minkowski representation. There is a nice way to do a rescaling, to obtain quasi-Fuchsian Minkowski spacetimes from anti-de Sitter ones (more precisely, from the components of the complements to the convex core), well-behaved on holonomies, see
[
8
]
by Benedetti–Bonsante. However, this is not a projective map, and hence does not have a related infinitesimal Pogorelov map. (An infinitesimal Pogorelov map can be associated to any projective map between ambient spaces, see
[
27
]
).

4.
Rigidity near the Fuchsian locus

4.1.
A bit of functional analysis

For a bounded operator
\mathcal{L}
between two Banach spaces
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
, we define its reduced minimum modulus

\gamma(\mathcal{L}):=\inf\{\|\mathcal{L}a\|:a\in\mathcal{A},d(a,{\rm Ker}\mathcal{L})=1\}.

When the range of
\mathcal{L}
is closed,
\gamma(\mathcal{L})>0
. See, e.g.,
[
38
, Chapter IV, Theorem 5.2]
.

For two subspaces
A,B
of a Banach space, we define their distance as

(4.1)

d(A,B):=\sup\{d(a,B):a\in A,\|a\|\leq 1\}.

We will need the following

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A
be a finite-dimensional subspace in a Banach space, and
A_{i}
be a sequence of subspaces, converging to
A
in the distance (
4.1
). Let
a_{i}\in A_{i}
be a uniformly bounded sequence of vectors. Then, up to subsequence,
a_{i}
converges to
a\in A
.

Proof.

Since
A
is finite-dimensional, for every
a_{i}
there is
a^{\prime}_{i}\in A
realizing the distance from
a_{i}
to
A
. The sequence
a^{\prime}_{i}
is uniformly bounded. Since bounded subsets of
A
are precompact, a subsequence of
a^{\prime}_{i}
converges to some
a
. It is easy to see that the same subsequence of
a_{i}
also converges to
a
.
∎

4.2.
Local infinitesimal rigidity

We show now the following result.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let

. There exists a neighborhood

of

such that for every

The first point is that the infinitesimal rigidity can be expressed in terms of transversality of submanifolds. (We already used this idea in the proof of Lemma

Consider

. Let

,

,

and

be the respective induced metrics and shape operators on

and

\phi_{s_{+},+}(b_{+})=\phi_{s_{-},-}(b_{-})

Proof.

Fix a lift

of

. Pick
\dot{e}\in T_{e^{\prime}}\mathcal{E}
. If
d_{e^{\prime}}\mathcal{I}(\dot{e})=0

can be represented by

∎

Now we need to show that if
s_{i}
converge to

. Let

be a sequence of GC-fields for

For every

(\mathcal{R}_{i})_{*}\mathcal{L}_{i}
and
\mathcal{L}
have the same dimension of kernel and zero cokernel. Denote the kernels of
(\mathcal{R}_{i})_{*}\mathcal{L}_{i}
by

. For every

we have

d(a^{\prime},X)\gamma(\mathcal{L})\leq\|\mathcal{L}a^{\prime}\|.

Since
\mathcal{L}
is Fredholm, its range is closed, thus
\gamma(\mathcal{L})>0
. It follows that for
a^{\prime}\in Y_{i}
,
\|a^{\prime}\|\leq 1
, we get

d(a^{\prime},X)\leq\gamma^{-1}(\mathcal{L})\|(\mathcal{L}-(\mathcal{R}_{i})_{*}\mathcal{L}_{i})\|.

Thus,
Y_{i}
converge to
X
. In turn,
\mathcal{R}^{-1}(Y_{i})=X_{i}
also converge to
X
.
∎

We can now prove Proposition
4.2
.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Suppose the converse. Then there exists a sequence
e_{i}\in\mathcal{E}
converging to
e
such that the differentials
d_{e_{i}}\mathcal{I}
are not injective. Let
(s_{i,+},s_{i,-})
and
(b_{i,+},b_{i,-})
be the corresponding induced metrics and shape operators on
S
. Similarly, let
(s_{+},s_{-})
and
(b_{+},b_{-})
be induced by
e
. By Lemma
4.3
, the intersection of the images of
\phi_{s_{i,+},+}
and
\phi_{s_{i,-},-}
is not transverse at
\phi_{s_{i,+},+}(b_{i,+})=\phi_{s_{i,-},-}(b_{i,-})
. Thus, there are sequences of tangent vectors
\dot{b}_{i,+}\in T_{b_{i,+}}\mathcal{GC}_{+}(s_{i,+})
and
\dot{b}_{i,-}\in T_{b_{i,-}}\mathcal{GC}_{-}(s_{i,-})
such that

(4.2)

d_{b_{i,+}}\phi_{s_{i,+},+}(\dot{b}_{i,+})=d_{b_{i,-}}\phi_{s_{i,-},-}(\dot{b}_{i,-}).

We can assume that the sequences
\dot{b}_{i,+}
,
\dot{b}_{i,-}
are uniformly bounded. From the mentioned results of Tamburelli,
T_{b_{+}}\mathcal{GC}_{+}(s_{+})
,
T_{b_{-}}\mathcal{GC}_{-}(s_{-})
are finite-dimensional.
Hence, by a combination of Lemma
4.4
and Lemma
4.1
, these sequences subconverge to
\dot{b}_{+}\in T_{b_{+}}\mathcal{GC}_{+}(s_{+})
and
\dot{b}_{-}\in T_{b_{-}}\mathcal{GC}_{-}(s_{-})
. Due to the definition of the maps
\phi_{s_{\pm},\pm}
via formula (
2.4
), equation (

) implies that

d_{b_{+}}\phi_{s_{+},+}(\dot{b}_{+})=d_{b_{-}}\phi_{s_{-},-}(\dot{b}_{-}).

Hence, Lemma
4.3
implies that
d_{e}\mathcal{I}
is not injective, which contradicts to Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Proof of Proposition

4.4.
Proof of the main result

Now we can prove Theorem
2.1
.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Both spaces
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}
are nuclear, hence paracompact
[
40
, Theorem 16.10]
. Since they also are Hausdorff and locally metrizable, they are metrizable by the Smirnov metrization theorem
[
47
, Theorem 42.1]
. Choose a metric
d_{S}
on
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}
and consider its restriction to
\mathcal{S}_{F}:=\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{E}_{F})
. By Labourie–Schlenker
[
43
]
,
\mathcal{I}|_{\mathcal{E}_{F}}
is a homeomorphism onto the image, we can pullback
d_{S}
from
\mathcal{S}_{F}
to
\mathcal{E}_{F}
. The subset
\mathcal{E}_{F}
is closed in
\mathcal{E}
and it is a result of Hausdorff
[
37
]
that a metric on
\mathcal{E}_{F}
extends to a metric
d_{E}
on
\mathcal{E}
compatible with the topology of
\mathcal{E}
.

Now from Proposition
4.5
, for every
e\in\mathcal{E}_{F}
there is
r(e)>0
such that
\mathcal{I}
is a homeomorphism onto the image when restricted to the closed ball
B(e,r(e))
. We can pick a neighborhood
U_{e}
of
e
such that
U_{e}\subset B(e,r(e)/3)
and
\mathcal{I}(U_{e})\subset B(\mathcal{I}(e),r(e)/3)
.
We claim that
\mathcal{I}
is injective on
U=\bigcup_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{F}}U_{e}
, which is then a desired neighborhood of
\mathcal{E}_{F}
. Indeed, suppose that
h=\mathcal{I}(e_{1}^{\prime})=\mathcal{I}(e_{2}^{\prime})
for
e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime}\in U
,
e_{1}^{\prime}\in U_{e_{1}}
,
e_{2}^{\prime}\in U_{e_{2}}
with
e_{1},e_{2}\in\mathcal{E}_{F}
, and that
r_{1}:=r(e_{1})\leq r_{2}:=r(e_{2})
. We claim that then
e_{1}^{\prime}\in B(e_{2},r(e_{2}))
. Indeed,

d_{E}(e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2})\leq d_{E}(e_{1}^{\prime},e_{1})+d_{E}(e_{1},e_{2})\leq r_{1}/3+d_{S}(\mathcal{I}(e_{1}),\mathcal{I}(e_{2}))\leq

\leq r_{1}/3+d_{S}(\mathcal{I}(e_{1}),h)+d_{S}(h,\mathcal{I}(e_{2}))\leq r_{1}/3+r_{1}/3+r_{2}/3\leq r_{2}.

This means that both
e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime}\in B(e_{2},r(e_{2}))
, which is a contradiction, as
\mathcal{I}
is injective when restricted to
B(e_{2},r(e_{2}))
.
∎

5.
Spacetimes with polyhedral boundary

We now prove the polyhedral version of our result, Theorem
1.3
. In this case the proof is quite parallel to the smooth one, thus we will give a bit less details in this setting.

5.1.
Anti-de Sitter spacetimes with marked points

Every GHC AdS (2+1)-spacetime
M
admits a canonical extension to a GHMC AdS (2+1)-spacetime, which we denote for now by
N
. The spacetime
N
has a
convex core
, which can be defined, e.g., as the inclusion-minimal closed totally convex subset of
N
. See details, e.g., in
[
17
]
. In particular, the convex core is contained in
M
(considered here as a subset of
N
). We say that
M
has
strictly polyhedral
boundary if it is locally modeled on convex polyhedral subsets of
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
and the boundary does not intersect the convex core. The last condition ensures a reasonable behavior of the combinatorics of the boundary as we vary the metric on
M
. See
[
51
, Section 3]
on the description of this in the hyperbolic case, as the anti-de Sitter case is the same in this regard. By a
vertex
of
M
we mean a point on
\partial M
at which
M
is locally modeled on a convex cone different from a half-space or from the wedge of two half-spaces.

The space
\mathscr{M}(V)
, defined in the introduction, is endowed with the topology of uniform convergence of developing maps on compact subsets of
\widetilde{M}
. As well as in the smooth case, we actually care only about the structures on the boundary. However, in this case the objects should be thought in a slightly different fashion.

Pick
\rho\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
, recall that
D(\rho)\subset\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
is the maximal open
\rho
-invariant convex set, on which the action of
\rho
is free and properly discontinuous. It also contains the
convex core

C(\rho)
, which is the inclusion-minimal closed
\rho
-invariant convex set. Alternatively, it can be defined as the convex hull of the limit set
\Lambda(\rho)
at the boundary at infinity of
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. (Recall that the
\rho
-quotient of
D(\rho)
is a GHMC AdS spacetime. Then the convex core of this spacetime, mentioned above, is exactly the quotient of
C(\rho)
.) There are two connected components of
D(\rho)\backslash C(\rho)
, corresponding to the future-convex and past-convex components of
\partial D(\rho)
. We denote these components of
D(\rho)\backslash C(\rho)
by
D_{+}(\rho)
and
D_{-}(\rho)
.

For now, let
V\subset S
be a finite subset and
\widetilde{V}\subset\widetilde{S}
be its full preimage, equipped with the natural
\pi_{1}S
action. Let
\rho\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
and let
f:\widetilde{V}\rightarrow D_{+}(\rho)
be a
\rho
-equivariant map. Denote by
\overline{\rm conv}(f)
the closed convex hull of
f(\widetilde{V})
in
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Only one of its boundary components has nonempty intersection with
D_{+}(\rho)
. This component is spacelike and future-convex. We denote it by
\Sigma(f)
. We say that
f
is
future-convex
if no point of
f(\widetilde{V})
is a convex combination of others and
strictly future-convex
if, in addition,
\Sigma(f)
is disjoint from
C(\rho)
. Note that then
\Sigma(f)\subset D_{+}(\rho)
,
f(\widetilde{V})\subset\Sigma(f)
and
\Sigma(f)
has a a decomposition into vertices, edges and faces, where the set of vertices is exactly
f(\widetilde{V})
, every edge is a geodesic segment between two vertices, and every face is isometric to a convex hyperbolic polygon. For more details on this decomposition, we refer to
[
54
, Section 3.4.5]
.

Denote the space of quasi-Fuchsian equivariant strictly future-convex maps by
\widehat{\mathscr{E}}_{+}(V)
. By picking a lift in
\widetilde{V}
for each
v\in V
, the space
\widehat{\mathscr{E}}_{+}(V)
can be considered as a subset of
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\times(\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}^{3})^{V}
, from which it inherits a topology. This is an open subset, hence
\widehat{\mathscr{E}}_{+}
becomes a smooth manifold. Denote by
\mathscr{E}_{+}(V)
its quotient by
G
. It is a smooth manifold of dimension
6k-6+3n
, where
k
is the genus of
S
and
n:=|V|
. Similarly, we define quasi-Fuchsian equivariant strictly past-convex maps and denote the respective spaces by
\widehat{\mathscr{E}}_{-}(V)
and
\mathscr{E}_{-}(V)
.

Remark 5.1
.

We use here the notation
\mathscr{E}
rather than
\mathcal{E}
, following our convention to use the first font for spaces up to isotopy (cf. the smooth case). An element
f\in\widehat{\mathscr{E}}_{+}(V)
extends to an equivariant homeomorphism from
\widetilde{S}
onto
\Sigma(f)
, but it is determined only up to isotopy. There is a way to consider polyhedral equivariant embeddings of
S
as the main objects, but some things are more peculiar in such setting.

A tangent vector to
\widehat{\mathscr{E}}_{+}(V)
at
(\rho,f)
is a vector field
v
on
f(\widetilde{V})
satisfying the automorphicity condition (
2.3
). Again, we say that
v
is
trivial
if it is the restriction of a global Killing field. Pick an arbitrary triangulation of
\Sigma(f)
subdividing its face decomposition. We say that
v
is
isometric
if it induces zero variation on each edge-length of the triangulation. Note that this is independent on the chosen triangulation.

Let
\mathscr{S}(V)
be the space of isotopy classes of hyperbolic cone-metrics on
S
with cone-angles
>2\pi
with vertices at
V
. Here we mean that every point of
V
has cone-angle
>2\pi
in such metrics. Every such metric admits a geodesic triangulation and
\mathscr{S}(V)
is endowed with a topology of a smooth manifold of dimension
6k-6+3n
by considering as charts the edge-length maps for varying triangulations. See, e.g.,
[
53
, Section 2.2]
for details.

Return to the space
\mathscr{M}(V)
defined in the introduction. Denote by
V_{+}
and
V_{-}
the intersections of
V
with both boundary components of
M
, considered as subsets of
S
via an identification
M\cong S\times[-1,1]
, and redefine
V:=\{V_{+},V_{-}\}
.
Denote by
\widehat{\mathscr{E}}(V)
the subset of
\widehat{\mathscr{E}}_{+}(V_{+})\times\widehat{\mathscr{E}}_{-}(V_{-})
consisting of the pairs of maps that are equivariant with respect to the same representation, and by
\mathscr{E}(V)
denote its quotient by
G
.
We have a natural continuous injective map
\mathscr{M}(V)\hookrightarrow\mathscr{E}(V)
. Imitating the proof from the smooth case, we obtain that this map is a homeomorphism. We denote by
\mathscr{E}_{F}(V)
the subspace of Fuchsian pairs of maps, i.e., the ones that have a lift in
\widehat{\mathscr{E}}_{F}
with representation into
G_{F}
.

Pick
e\in\mathscr{E}(V)
represented by
(\rho,f_{+},f_{-})\in\widehat{\mathscr{E}}(V)
. We have two associated
\rho
-invariant spacelike surfaces
\Sigma(f_{+})
and
\Sigma(f_{-})
. They are endowed with hyperbolic cone-metrics with cone-angles
>2\pi
. Consider their
\rho
-quotients. We have a canonical isomorphism of the fundamental group of each of these quotients with
\pi_{1}S
. This allows to define homeomorphisms from them to
S
, determined up to isotopy. Furthermore, the markings of vertices given by
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
allow to associate the induced metrics with elements of
\mathscr{S}(V_{+})
and
\mathscr{S}(V_{-})
respectively. This defines the induced metric map

\mathscr{I}_{V}:\mathscr{E}(V)\rightarrow\mathscr{S}(V_{+})\times\mathscr{S}(V_{-}).

The respective reformulation of our polyhedral result is

Theorem 5.2
.

There exists a neighborhood
U
of
\mathscr{E}_{F}(V)
in
\mathscr{E}(V)
and an open subset
U^{\prime}\subset\mathscr{S}(V_{+})\times\mathscr{S}(V_{-})
, which contains the diagonal, such that
\mathscr{I}_{V}|_{U}
is a homeomorphism onto
U^{\prime}

The proof is based on the following facts.

Lemma 5.3
.

The map
\mathscr{I}_{V}
is
C^{1}
.

The proof is identical to the proof of respective results in other settings, see, e.g.,
[
31
, Lemma 2.13]
for a Minkowski version or
[
51
, Lemma 2.17]
for a de Sitter version.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
e\in\mathscr{E}_{F}(V)
. Then
d_{e}\mathscr{I}_{V}
is injective.

The proof is given in the next subsection.

Theorem 5.5
.

The restriction
\mathscr{I}_{V}|_{\mathscr{E}_{F}(V)}
is a homeomorphism onto the image.

This is the content of article
[
25
]
of Fillastre.

Proof of Theorem
5.2
.

Because
\mathscr{E}(V)
and
\mathscr{S}(V_{+})\times\mathscr{S}(V_{-})
are finite-dimensional manifolds of the same dimension, Proposition
5.4
actually means that for each
e\in\mathscr{E}_{F}(V)
,
d_{e}\mathscr{I}_{V}
is an isomorphism. Together with Lemma
5.3
, the inverse function theorem implies that
\mathscr{I}_{V}
is a local homeomorphism around
\mathscr{E}_{F}(V)
. Using Theorem
5.5
instead of Theorem
2.4
, the proof is finished in the same way as the proof of Theorem
2.1
.
∎

5.2.
Minkowski spacetimes with marked points and infinitesimal rigidity

In this section we prove Proposition
5.4
. As in the smooth case, the proof is done by using the Pogorelov map. We will rely on
[
31
]
by Fillastre–Prosanov instead of
[
62
]
. We now recall the basics of polyhedral surfaces in Minkowski 3-space
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
. We adapt some notions from the previous subsection to this setting.

Pick
\rho\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{0}
. Associated with it are two maximal open
\rho
-invariant convex sets, on which the action of
\rho
is free and properly discontinuous, one is future-complete and one is past-complete. We denote them by
D_{+}^{0}(\rho)
and
D_{-}^{0}(\rho)
. See
[
46
]
of Mess for details.

Again, let
V\subset S
be a finite subset and
\widetilde{V}\subset\widetilde{S}
be its full preimage, equipped with the natural
\pi_{1}S
action. Let
\rho\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{0}
and let
f:\widetilde{V}\rightarrow D_{+}^{0}(\rho)
be a
\rho
-equivariant map. Denote by
\overline{\rm conv}(f)
the closed convex hull of
f(\widetilde{V})
. In this setting, it is a future-complete set with one boundary component. Furthermore, this component is future-convex and spacelike. We denote it by
\Sigma(f)
. We say that
f
is
future-convex
if no point of
f(\widetilde{V})
is a convex combination of others. (An interesting difference with the anti-de Sitter situation is that here is no notion of strict convexity for our maps due to the lack of convex cores in this setting.) Note that then
f(\widetilde{V})\subset\Sigma(f)
. The surface
\Sigma(f)
also has a a decomposition into vertices, edges and faces, where the set of vertices is exactly
f(\widetilde{V})
, every edge is a geodesic segment between two vertices, and every face is isometric to a convex Euclidean polygon.

Denote the space of quasi-Fuchsian equivariant future-convex maps by
\widehat{\mathscr{E}}_{+}^{0}(V)
. By picking a lift in
\widetilde{V}
for each
v\in V
, the space
\widehat{\mathscr{E}}_{+}(V)
can be considered as a subset of
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{0}\times(\mathbb{R}^{2,1})^{V}
, from which it inherits a topology. This is an open subset, hence
\widehat{\mathscr{E}}_{+}^{0}(V)
becomes a smooth manifold. Denote by
\mathscr{E}_{+}^{0}(V)
its quotient by
G
. It is a smooth manifold of dimension
6k-6+3n
, where
k
is the genus of
S
and
n:=|V|
. Similarly, we define quasi-Fuchsian equivariant past-convex maps and denote the respective spaces by
\widehat{\mathscr{E}}_{-}^{0}(V)
and
\mathscr{E}_{-}^{0}(V)
. The notions of automorphic, trivial and isometric vector fields extend to this setting accordingly.

Let
\mathscr{S}^{0}(V)
be the space of Euclidean cone-metrics with cone-angles
>2\pi
and vertices at
V
, considered up to isotopy. For
e\in\mathscr{E}_{+}^{0}(V)
lifting to
(\rho,f)
, the induced metric on
\Sigma(f)
can be associated with an element in
\mathscr{S}^{0}(V)
. This defines the induced metric map

\mathscr{I}_{V,+}^{0}:\mathscr{E}_{+}^{0}(V)\rightarrow\mathscr{S}^{0}(V).

It was shown in
[
31
, Lemma 2.13]
that this map is
C^{1}
. Moreover, the authors proved

Theorem 5.6
.

The map
\mathscr{I}_{V,+}^{0}
is a surjective submersion.

This is a direct corollary of
[
31
, Theorem I]
. See also the discussion in
[
31
, Section 4.1]
. For
s\in\mathscr{S}^{0}(V)
denote
(\mathscr{I}_{V,+}^{0})^{-1}(s)
by
\mathscr{E}_{+}^{0}(s)
. Due to Theorem
5.6
, this is a
C^{1}
-submanifold of
\mathscr{E}_{+}^{0}(V)
. We now define a map

\phi_{s,+}^{0}:\mathscr{E}^{0}_{+}(s)\rightarrow T\mathscr{T},

which sends a
\rho
-equivariant map to
\rho
(recall that
\mathcal{R}^{0}\cong T\mathscr{T}
). It is also
C^{1}
, see
[
31
, Section 4.1]
. We define similarly the space
\mathscr{E}^{0}_{-}(s)
and the map
\phi^{0}_{s,-}
. It is another direct corollary of
[
31
, Theorem I]
that

Theorem 5.7
.

For any Euclidean cone-metric
s
with cone-angles
>2\pi
, the compositions of
\phi_{s,\pm}^{0}
with the projection to
\mathscr{T}
are
C^{1}
-diffeomorphisms.

In particular, the maps
\phi^{0}_{s,\pm}
are
C^{1}
-immersions.
From
[
31
, Theorem II]
, we have

Theorem 5.8
.

Pick two finite subsets
V_{+},V_{-}\subset S
and metrics
s_{+}\in\mathscr{S}^{0}(V_{+})
,
s_{-}\in\mathscr{S}^{0}(V_{-})
. Then the intersection of
\phi^{0}_{s_{+},+}
and
\phi^{0}_{s_{-},-}
is unique and transverse.

Note that in
[
31
]
Theorem
5.8
is formulated in the language of vector fields given by Theorem
5.7
.
Now we can show

Lemma 5.9
.

Pick two finite subsets
V_{+},V_{-}\subset S
.
Consider
e_{+}=(\rho,f_{+})\in\mathscr{E}_{+}^{0}(V_{+})
,
e_{-}=(\rho,f_{-})\in\mathscr{E}_{-}^{0}(V_{-})
and
\dot{\rho}\in Z^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{g}^{0})
. Let
v
be a
\dot{\rho}
-automorphic isometric vector field on
f_{+}(\widetilde{V}_{+})
and
f_{-}(\widetilde{V}_{-})
. Then
v
is trivial.

Proof.

Let
s_{+}
and
s_{-}
be the induced metrics.
If
v
is nontrivial, then it defines a nonzero tangent vector
\dot{e}_{+}
to
\mathscr{E}_{+}(s_{+})
at
s_{+}
and
\dot{e}_{-}
to
\mathscr{E}_{-}(s_{-})
at
e_{-}
. We then have

(5.1)

d_{e_{+}}\phi^{0}_{s_{+},+}(\dot{e}_{+})=d_{e_{-}}\phi^{0}_{s_{-},-}(\dot{e}_{-}).

Due to Theorem
5.7
, expression (
5.1
) is nonzero. But this contradicts Theorem
5.8
.
∎

We can now prove Proposition
5.4
.

Proof of Proposition
5.4
.

As in the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we exploit the simultaneous model for anti-de Sitter and Minkowski 3-spaces as well as the Pogorelov map. Recall the model from Section
3.1
, particularly the point
o
and the set
C
. Pick
e\in\mathscr{E}_{F}(V)
and lift it to a triple
(\rho,f_{+},f_{-})\in\widehat{\mathscr{E}}_{F}(V)
, where
\rho:\pi_{1}S\rightarrow G_{F}
and
G_{F}
fixes
o
. Then the images of
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
are contained in
C
. Pick
\dot{e}\in T_{e}\mathscr{E}(V)
such that
d_{e}\mathscr{I}_{V}(\dot{e})=0
. Then it is represented by a
\dot{\rho}
-automorphic isometric vector field on
f_{+}(\widetilde{V}_{+})
and
f_{-}(\widetilde{V}_{-})
for
\dot{\rho}\in Z^{1}_{\rho}(\mathfrak{g})
.

Recall the Pogorelov map
\Phi
and the associated map
\Psi_{\rho}
. From Lemma
3.11
,
\Phi(v)
is a
\Psi_{\rho}(\dot{\rho})
-automorphic vector field. Also, if
v
induces a zero variation on the distance between two points, then this means that its restriction to these two points coincides with the restriction of a Killing field. (Indeed, by subtracting a Killing field, one can assume that at one point
v
is zero. Then at the second point
v
is orthogonal to the segment between the points and any such
v
is induced by an infinitesimal rotation around the first point.) Hence, Lemma
3.8
implies that
\Phi(v)
is isometric. By Lemma
5.9
, it is trivial, thereby so is
v
. It follows that
\dot{e}=0
.
∎
