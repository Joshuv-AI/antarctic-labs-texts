A hybrid large language model framework for structured data entry from code-switched persian clinical speech
Authors: Mohammad Tanhaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConverting spoken clinical dictation into structured electronic health record (EHR) entries poses significant challenges in low-resource languages. These difficulties are primarily driven by phonetic complexity and the prevalent phenomenon of code-switching with English medical terminology, both of which severely degrade the performance of automatic speech recognition (ASR) models. This paper presents a practical hybrid framework that combines ASR, Persian-specific normalization of code-switched and orthographically ambiguous terms, metadata-constrained field mapping, and uncertainty-triggered human review for structured EHR entry. Leveraging large language model (LLM) prompting and specialized bilingual glossaries, this module normalizes code-switched terms and disambiguates Persian-specific homophones. The proposed method employs embedding-based field mapping guided by form metadata and existing clinical annotations, supplemented by selective human-in-the-loop validation for high-stakes fields. Evaluation within the BioArc EHR system demonstrates substantial improvements in transcription quality and structured data accuracy. Experimental results and granular ablation studies confirm that explicitly addressing linguistic challenges such as code-switching and homophone variability is critical for improving field detection rates and supporting data integrity. Our findings underscore that while the framework serves as a robust assistive tool–achieving high field detection accuracy in controlled evaluations–a hybrid workflow remains essential to address residual error rates (approximately 5–10% in complex forms) and support the precision required for medical documentation.Keywords: Electronic Health Records, Voice-to-Form, LLM, Data Extraction, Automated Data Entry, Persian Clinical Speech, Low-Resource Languages, Code-SwitchingSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

The increasing adoption of voice assistants and advanced natural language processing technologies has facilitated the transformation of spoken input into structured data formats. Structured forms remain essential for data collection across numerous domains, including healthcare, legal, and administrative services, where precision and standardized handling are critical. Existing voice-to-form systems, however, frequently suffer from transcription inaccuracies, loss of contextual information, and misalignment with target fields–challenges that are particularly pronounced in low-resource languages characterized by complex phonetics, diverse dialects, and limited training datasets.

In English-speaking clinical environments, ambient documentation systems and speech-to-text integrations in electronic health records (EHRs) have demonstrated substantial reductions in administrative burden, allowing providers to devote more time to patient care1,2. These advances rely on robust automatic speech recognition (ASR) models trained on extensive English corpora. In contrast, low-resource languages such as Persian face significant barriers: higher word error rates due to phonetic variability, informal speech patterns, scarcity of domain-specific medical audio data, and frequent code-switching with English medical terminology. Few documented voice-driven EHR systems exist for such contexts, leaving a gap in scalable, accurate documentation tools for non-English clinical settings.

This paper presents a practical hybrid framework designed to address these challenges through the integration of ASR, large language model (LLM)-driven text refinement, metadata- and annotation-guided field mapping, and targeted human-in-the-loop validation. Applied to the BioArc EHR system–a comprehensive Persian-language platform supporting over 100 medical registries and used daily by more than 3,000 physicians–the framework converts spoken medical dictation into structured form entries while leveraging existing system annotations to constrain extraction and enhance reliability. Unlike standard ASR-to-Mapping pipelines that often fail in non-English clinical settings, our technical novelty lies in the dual-layer normalization architecture. This includes a specialized module for code-switched medical term recovery and a context-aware homophone disambiguation layer, which together bridge the gap between noisy Persian acoustic output and structured EHR metadata requirements.

Objectives

The primary objectives of this work are to develop a metadata-driven system capable of autonomously converting speech input to structured text using ASR; refining raw transcripts with LLMs to correct errors and enrich content, including normalization of code-switched medical terms; mapping refined text to predefined form fields guided by schema metadata and existing annotations; incorporating feedback mechanisms for error handling and quality control; and enabling cross-domain adaptability, with particular emphasis on low-resource language challenges in healthcare.

Significance

Efficient voice-to-form conversion has the potential to streamline data collection across diverse linguistic contexts, particularly in medical and administrative fields where rapid and accurate entry is essential. In low-resource language settings with prevalent code-switching, such systems can significantly enhance data quality, reduce administrative workload, and minimize human transcription errors. Research on hybrid human–AI systems indicates amplified benefits in high-stakes domains like hospitals, where precision is paramount3. By addressing gaps in non-English clinical documentation, this framework supports more equitable deployment of voice-enabled EHR tools globally.

Related work

Voice-to-text conversion

Voice-to-text systems form the foundation of spoken data processing. Models such as OpenAI’s Whisper (2022) provide robust multilingual support4, while Google Speech-to-Text and older frameworks like DeepSpeech offer varying degrees of accuracy and language coverage5,6. Deep learning advancements enable adaptation to noisy environments and accents, though challenges persist in healthcare settings with background noise and domain-specific speech7. Fine-tuning on specialized data improves performance, but low-resource languages remain underserved.

Text refinement

Post-transcription refinement enhances clarity and usability. Pretrained models like BERT (2019) excel in contextual understanding8, while generative approaches such as GPT-3 (2020) and newer models like Gemma support coherence and grammar correction9,10. Iterative techniques, including self-instruction loops, further improve output quality11.

Data extraction and mapping to forms

Extracting structured information from unstructured text relies on named entity recognition and information extraction techniques12,13. Multilingual models like XLM-R facilitate cross-lingual tasks14, and generative AI streamlines complex extractions15.

Hybrid approaches in high-stakes domains

Hybrid human–AI workflows gain traction in precision-critical fields like healthcare, where automated transcription benefits from expert oversight2,18–20.

Multilingual and low-resource language challenges

Most ambient clinical documentation systems remain English-centric, with limited support for low-resource languages4. Multilingual ASR models like Whisper offer broad coverage but exhibit degraded performance in domain-specific low-resource settings due to data scarcity, phonetic complexity, and insufficient fine-tuning on non-English medical speech.

Code-switching challenges in low-resource clinical speech

Code-switching–the intrasentential mixing of languages–is common in multilingual clinical environments, where local language discourse frequently incorporates English/Latin medical terminology. This phenomenon significantly degrades ASR performance in low-resource languages, as models trained primarily on monolingual data misrecognize or mistransliterate switched terms21.

Recent works have addressed code-switching in low-resource ASR, including fine-tuned models for Bengali-English medical transcription22, Hindi-English code-mixed speech23, and clinical nursing documentation24. However, to our knowledge, systematic handling of these challenges in Persian clinical speech remains largely unaddressed in the current literature, particularly regarding the integration of LLM-guided normalization within an operational EHR pipeline. To our knowledge, prior studies have not reported a Persian clinical dictation pipeline that explicitly combines LLM-guided normalization of code-switched medical terms with glossary-based standardization.

Persian-specific orthographic and homophone challenges

A distinct challenge in Persian ASR is the presence of homophonous letters that are acoustically indistinguishable in spoken form, leading to inconsistent orthography in transcriptions. This issue compounds code-switching errors and contributes to elevated WER in Persian clinical speech (30–40% for Whisper in real-world settings25,26). While Gemma 3 models demonstrate strong contextual performance for Persian text correction, we did not identify prior Persian clinical voice-to-EHR studies that jointly addressed homophone disambiguation and code-switch normalization within the same operational pipeline.

Voice-to-text conversion with ASR

The proposed framework initiates the processing pipeline by capturing audio input from the clinician. This audio is subsequently transcribed using an automatic speech recognition (ASR) system, primarily leveraging the Whisper model, which offers robust multilingual capabilities and reasonable performance across diverse accents. Despite these strengths, residual transcription errors persist due to factors such as background noise, rapid speech, and domain-specific challenges. Recent advancements in noise-robust ASR techniques are incorporated to mitigate these issues27.

On the evaluation dataset, the Whisper model achieved a Word Error Rate (WER) of 18.4% on standard Persian clinical speech, increasing to 28.7% in segments exhibiting heavy code-switching. In comparison, Google Speech-to-Text produced a higher WER of 32.1%, primarily attributable to suboptimal handling of Persian phonetic characteristics.

Audio preprocessing

Prior to transcription, audio signals undergo preprocessing steps including noise reduction and signal enhancement to improve overall speech quality. These procedures enhance ASR robustness in noisy clinical environments and accommodate variations in speaker characteristics. Segmentation into shorter utterances further facilitates parallel processing while maintaining transcription accuracy.

Challenges in non-English language transcription

Transcription of non-English languages, particularly Persian, presents distinct challenges owing to phonetic complexity, regional dialectal variations, and limited domain-specific training data. Although state-of-the-art ASR systems perform adequately on English clinical speech, their accuracy on Persian remains lower due to these linguistic factors and the prevalence of informal speech patterns in clinical settings.

Improving ASR for non-English languages

Performance on non-English languages can be enhanced through targeted fine-tuning on domain-specific corpora that encompass regional accents, idiomatic expressions, and medical terminology. Such adaptations yield measurable improvements in transcription accuracy for specialized applications.

The refined transcript is subsequently mapped to the target structured form, which is defined by schema metadata specifying field types (e.g., free text, date, numeric), validation rules (e.g., required fields, value ranges), and inter-field dependencies. Field Identification: Individual segments of the refined text are matched to candidate form fields using cosine similarity computed over multilingual sentence embeddings derived from a pretrained SBERT model.

Error handling and quality control with embedding feedback

The framework incorporates a closed-loop feedback mechanism that enables clinicians to review and correct automatically populated form fields. Each mapped field is assigned a confidence score derived from embedding similarity and metadata compliance. Fields with scores below 0.75 or those violating schema constraints are automatically flagged for manual review or supplementary validation. An embedding-based error detection component identifies potential logical or semantic inconsistencies in the extracted values, facilitating rapid correction. Within the BioArc ecosystem, existing functionality allows annotations and comments to be associated with specific fields, thereby reducing ambiguity in subsequent processing and enabling continual improvement of model performance through clinician feedback.

Model evaluation and improvement

The system’s efficacy is assessed using real-world clinical datasets comprising diverse medical forms and corresponding structured extractions. Performance is benchmarked against established baselines, with primary evaluation metrics including Field Detection Accuracy, Missed Fields Percentage, and Incorrectly Filled Fields Percentage. Particular emphasis is placed on hybrid configurations that integrate automated population with targeted human validation, which is especially critical in healthcare domains requiring exceptional reliability. Empirical results demonstrate superior accuracy when leveraging structured annotations and human oversight. Opportunities for further enhancement are identified, particularly in handling large, complex forms with numerous interdependent fields. Prior research on human-AI collaboration in critical domains similarly underscores the value of such hybrid approaches in optimizing data workflow efficiency28. Integration with platforms like BioArc enhances practical utility, facilitating smoother adoption in clinical settings, reducing data entry time, and supporting high accuracy standards while acknowledging residual challenges in complex scenarios.

Experimental design and test setup

To rigorously evaluate the proposed hybrid framework, we conducted a comprehensive experimental study using 1,000 distinct speech inputs collected within the BioArc ecosystem. This section details the data characteristics, participant demographics, and the technical environment.

Dataset and participant profile

The evaluation dataset comprises 1000 audio recordings (totaling approximately 15 hours of clinical dictation). These recordings were obtained from 20 unique clinicians (12 male, 8 female) aged between 30 and 55 years, ensuring a diverse range of vocal pitches and speaking rates. All participants are native Persian speakers who routinely utilize English/Latin medical terminology in their professional discourse.

Unlike simulated datasets, these inputs were recorded in actual clinical environments–including outpatient clinics and emergency departments–to capture realistic acoustic characteristics such as background medical equipment noise, ambient conversations, and varying microphone distances. The speech patterns exhibit high variability, ranging from formal dictation to rapid, informal clinical notes with significant code-switching.

To ensure unbiased evaluation, a strict data split was maintained: 200 recordings were reserved exclusively for threshold tuning and calibration, while the remaining 800 recordings were dedicated to final evaluation; no recording or clinician-specific audio appeared in both splits.

Gold-standard annotation process

To establish a ground-truth for evaluation, the 1000 speech inputs were manually transcribed and annotated by three senior medical residents with over five years of experience in clinical documentation.

Human-in-the-loop review process

In hybrid scenarios, flagged fields (confidence < 0.75 or constraint violation) were reviewed by three clinical interns (medical residents with 3–5 years experience). Reviewers were blinded to system configuration and received training on the interface (30-minute session). Disagreements were resolved by majority vote; inter-reviewer agreement (Fleiss’ kappa) was 0.82. Average review time per flagged form was 45 seconds, demonstrating scalability (only 12% of fields typically flagged).

Total processing time for hybrid scenarios was calculated as the sum of automated system latency (ASR, normalization, and mapping) and the active time spent by clinicians on the review interface. To provide a granular view, these components are reported separately as and in our results.

Discussion

The empirical results indicate that the highest performance across nearly all metrics is achieved by the hybrid approach incorporating form annotations. Benefits are particularly pronounced in more complex scenarios. For medium-sized forms, field detection accuracy increases by approximately 2 percentage points when moving from annotated autonomous processing to the full hybrid configuration, with larger forms exhibiting a more modest 1 percentage point gain. Reductions in missed fields are comparable across form sizes, averaging approximately 3 percentage points in the hybrid setting. The hybrid configuration consistently improves suggestion correctness, while the most substantial error mitigation is observed in the Incorrectly Filled Fields Percentage, which declines markedly–from 10% to 3% in medium forms and by 5 percentage points in large forms.

To precisely quantify the human-in-the-loop time savings, we analyzed the workload distribution between the AI and clinicians. While manual entry for a large BioArc form requires minutes, our hybrid framework reduces the active human involvement to an average of only 0.2 minutes (approx. 12 seconds) per form across the dataset. This efficiency is achieved because human intervention is triggered only for the 12% of fields that fall below the 0.75 confidence threshold. Consequently, even with the 45-second overhead required for each flagged review session, the clinician achieves a total time saving of approximately 55–65% compared to traditional manual documentation, without compromising the safety and precision of the EHR record.

The sample size (1000 inputs, 200 for ablation) provides sufficient statistical power for the observed effects, although broader generalizability to additional low-resource languages warrants future investigation.

A key aspect of the framework’s reproducibility is its modular design, but its transferability is partial rather than universal. While the metadata-mapping layer is tailored to the BioArc schema, the core architectural components–including the dual-layer normalization, the iterative LLM refinement prompts, and the confidence-based human-in-the-loop escalation policy–are domain-agnostic. These modules can be directly integrated into other EHR systems or low-resource language frameworks with minimal adaptation of the underlying medical glossaries.

Safety, governance, and ethical considerations

To ensure responsible deployment in clinical environments, the framework incorporates a multi-layered Safety and Governance Funnel (illustrated in Fig. 12). This architecture is designed to enforce clinical data integrity through a combination of deterministic schema constraints and probabilistic uncertainty filters, strictly mitigating the risk of LLM-generated hallucinations.

The multi-layered Safety and Governance Funnel: A sequence of deterministic schema constraints and probabilistic uncertainty thresholds (e.g., cosine similarity> 0.75) used to safeguard clinical data integrity in the BioArc system.

The safety funnel subjects every potential field entry to three rigorous validation layers:

Layer 1: Schema-Constrained Extraction: The framework retrieves field-specific metadata directly from the BioArc EHR schema (e.g., data types such as numeric, date, or categorical). During the mapping process, these constraints are injected into the LLM prompts to narrow the generation space. A post-extraction validation script ensures that a numeric field, for instance, does not contain textual noise.

Audit Logging and Clinician Override: All pipeline stages are logged with timestamps and versioned transcripts for full traceability. The user interface features a Clinician Override UX with visual indicators (e.g., colored borders and tooltips showing violation details), allowing physicians to modify or reject suggestions without disrupting the clinical workflow. This feedback loop ensures that automated efficiency never compromises the stringent safety standards required for EHR documentation.

Conclusion

The proposed hybrid framework demonstrates substantial improvements in voice-driven structured data entry within the evaluated Persian clinical context using the BioArc EHR system. Although residual error rates of approximately 5–10% persist in complex forms, the approach significantly reduces documentation burden and supports data integrity when augmented with selective human oversight.

These results suggest that modular hybrid AI systems can improve structured clinical documentation in low-resource settings, although broader validation across additional institutions, EHR schemas, and languages is still needed.

Future work

Future research directions focus on refining the hybrid methodology and extending its applicability across diverse domains. Priority areas include the following:

Improved Error Detection and Contextual Understanding: Advanced techniques for identifying and resolving errors in challenging acoustic conditions are needed. Enhanced handling of ambiguous or degraded inputs would further elevate performance in both autonomous and hybrid configurations. Targeted fine-tuning of automatic speech recognition models on domain-specific corpora–encompassing regional accents, colloquial variants, background noise, and code-switched medical terminology in languages such as Persian–represents a promising avenue.
