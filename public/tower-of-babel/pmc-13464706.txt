Phage-encoded NARP3 system rebuilds NAD+ to subvert bacterial immunity
Authors: Jia Yan, Siyu Liu, Zhaorong Luo, Zhi Su, Gaoqin Teng, Mengyuan Li, Jiangang Ma, Rongsui Gao, Jiuxin Qu, Man Huang, Jianhua Gan, Youjun Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phages deploy diverse countermeasures to evade bacterial nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)–directed immunity in a perpetual arms race. Known pathways include NAD+ reconstitution pathway 1 (NARP1), which recycles adenosine 5′-diphosphate–ribose, and NARP2, which converts nicotinamide into NAD+ via nicotinamide mononucleotide ligation. Whether additional NAD+-restoring strategies exist has remained unclear. Here, we identify NARP3, a conserved two-gene NAD+-restoring phage pathway widespread in Enterobacteriaceae-infecting phages. NARP3 encodes a pyridine nucleoside uptake system C (PnuC)-like nicotinamide riboside transporter (Bas30_87) and a bifunctional NAD+ biosynthesis regulator (NadR)-like enzyme (Bas30_86). Bas30_87 imports nicotinamide riboside, and Bas30_86 converts it to NAD+ through sequential phosphorylation and adenylation. NARP3 fully restores NAD+ pools depleted by Sir2-HerA defenses, enabling robust phage replication. We solve x-ray crystal structures of Bas30_86 alone and bound to NAD+, revealing coordinated substrate capture, intermediate handling, and product formation. Mutational analyses confirm that both transport and enzymatic activities are essential. NARP3 functions as a metabolite-centered countermeasure, expands the phage arsenal, and underscores NAD+ metabolism as a central battlefield in host-phage conflicts. Its discovery provides a blueprint for engineering phages to bypass NAD+-dependent bacterial immunity and offers a mechanistic framework to harness metabolite-guided viral strategies for biotechnological applications.

INTRODUCTION

As Earth’s most abundant biological entities with an estimated 1031 particles (1), bacteriophages (phages) have profoundly shaped microbial diversity and driven global biogeochemical cycles over billions of years (1, 2). The coevolutionary conflict between bacterial hosts and phages has fueled a perpetual molecular arms race (3), fostering an arsenal of innovations in both anti-phage defense systems (4, 5) and phage-encoded countermeasures (6, 7). Bacterial defense is generally organized into a multilayered architecture that includes, but is not limited to, (i) innate immunity, such as restriction-modification (RM) systems (8); (ii) adaptive immunity mediated by CRISPR-Cas (clustered regularly interspaced palindromic repeats and CRISPR-associated nucleases) (9); and (iii) metabolite- or signal-directed strategies (10, 11), exemplified by the cyclic oligonucleotide-based anti-phage signaling system (CBASS) (12, 13). The explosive growth of bacterial genome sequencing has revealed an extraordinary diversity of antiviral machineries (10, 14, 15), far exceeding the classical RM and CRISPR-Cas paradigms, with 150 to 200 distinct families of anti-phage defense systems now recognized (10, 14–16). Mobile genetic elements (MGEs), such as plasmids and prophages, function as genetic “arms bazaars,” promoting the dissemination and evolution of defensive weapons (17–19). Historically, RM laid the foundation for molecular biology by offering restriction enzymes (8), while CRISPR-Cas revolutionized biotechnology through precise gene editing (9). Following this trajectory of milestone breakthroughs (8, 9), recently discovered anti-phage systems may be repurposed as molecular tools for biotechnology and synthetic biology, although their potential awaits experimental validation.

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD), in its oxidized (NAD+) and reduced (NADH) forms, is a universally conserved metabolite essential across all domains of life (20, 21). Its importance derives from dual functions: as a cofactor in core redox reactions, such as glycolysis (22), and as a cosubstrate in signaling pathways, including adenosine 5′-diphosphate (ADP) ribosylation (23, 24). Cellular NAD+/NADH homeostasis is maintained through coordinated de novo biosynthesis and salvage pathways (Fig. 1, A and B) (25, 26). Nudix-type hydrolases regulate NAD turnover via cleavage reactions (27, 28), while NrtR-family transcription factors couple metabolic state to gene expression by sensing ADP-ribose (ADPR), a central product of NAD+ hydrolysis (29–31). Beyond metabolism, NAD+ serves as a central target of bacterial immune systems defending against phage infection (5, 32). NAD+-directed prokaryotic immunity encompasses at least five mechanistic classes: (i) NAD+-consuming signaling modules, such as SMODS-associated and fused to various effector domains (SAVED) proteins in CBASS (13, 33); (ii) NAD-dependent toxin-antitoxin systems, exemplified by DarTG (34); (iii) NAD+-depleting strategies mediated by Toll/interleukin-1 receptor (TIR) domains in Thoeris ThsAB (14, 35–37); (iv) Sir2-linked enzymes, including short pAgo (38), defense-associated sirtuins 1/2 (DSR1/2) (39) plus Sir2-HerA (10, 14); and (v) NAD+-utilizing effectors, such as sirtuins and CBASS enzymes (40). These systems use distinct mechanisms. CBASS generates cyclic ADPR via NAD cyclase activity to trigger programmed cell death (13). In DarTG, DarT ADP-ribosylates invading DNA, whereas DarG reverses this modification, leading to abortive infection (34). Type I Thoeris degrades NAD+ into variant metabolites via TIR domains, rapidly depleting cellular NAD+ and halting phage replication (14, 36). Sir2-linked modules further expand defense capacity: DSR2 functions as a defined antiviral NADase (39), DSR1 remains poorly characterized, and Sir2-HerA couples NAD hydrolysis to nucleic acid processing (41, 42). Together, these observations establish NAD+ as a central hub integrating metabolism, regulatory signaling, and bacterial immunity, highlighting its multifaceted roles in cellular physiology and constant host-phage metabolic conflicts.

In general, NAD+-directed systems do not act in isolation. Because NAD+ degradation products often serve as secondary messengers [e.g., v-cADPR; (43)], NAD+-centric defenses are thought to cross-talk with other bacterial immunity modules, generating a multilayered “protection web” (36, 44). The evolutionary advantage of such cross-communication lies in redundancy: Even if a phage evades one NAD+-based pathway, NAD+ signaling can still activate additional systems. Persistent host-phage metabolic conflict has, however, driven the evolution of diverse viral countermeasures that inhibit or bypass NAD+-guided defenses. Three kinds of phage-encoded inhibitors have been characterized to date, each acting through direct binding to bacterial defense proteins. Central to DarT toxin of DarTG1 TA system, T4-like phages encode (i) anti-DarT factor A1 (AdfA1) (34) and (ii) anti-DarT factor NADAR (AdfN) (45); and Vibrio ICP1 phage also has an anti-DarT mimic, AdfB (46). The rare phage protein DSR anti-defense 1 (DSAD1) binds the tetrameric DSR2 system and blocks its NADase activity, neutralizing its antiviral function (39). Likewise, phage Φ3T produces Nip, a dedicated inhibitor of the TIR-domain NADase SpbK, ensuring phage replication (47, 48). Beyond direct inhibition, two recently identified NAD+ reconstitution pathways (NARP1 and NARP2, in Fig. 1A) restore NAD+ levels rather than interfering with host defense machinery (49). NARP1 encodes an ADPR-pyrophosphate synthetase (ADPS) that phosphorylates ADPR to form ADPR-pyrophosphate (ADPR-PP), which is subsequently coupled to nicotinamide (NAM) by nicotinamide ADPR-transferase (NAMAT) to regenerate NAD+ (49). By contrast, NARP2 uses NAM phosphoribosyl-transferase (NAMPT) to ligate PRPP (5-phosphoribosyl-1-pyrophosphate) and NAM, generating nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), which is then adenylated by NMN adenylyl-transferase (NMNAT) with adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) to yield NAD+ (49). The growing arsenal of mechanistically diverse counterdefenses underscores the dynamic and ongoing nature of the phage-host arms race.

In this study, we uncover a conserved “two-gene” module (exemplified by Bas30_86/87) present in a substantial fraction of Escherichia/Salmonella-infecting phages, encoding an NAD+ biosynthesis enzyme/regulaor (NadR)-type enzyme and a PnuC-like transporter predicted to mediate nicotinamide riboside (NR) salvage. Functional analyses establish that these genes act together to define a previously unrecognized phage NAD+ reconstitution pathway, which we designate NARP3. Mechanistically, NARP3 operates through three sequential steps: uptake of NR via PnuC/Bas30_87, conversion of NR into an NMN intermediate by NadR/Bas30_86, and subsequent synthesis of NAD+ from NMN by NadR/Bas30_86. NARP3 restores NAD+ levels depleted by the Sir2-HerA defense system and thereby neutralizes host immunity. Thus, the discovery of NARP3 expands the repertoire of phage-encoded countermeasures, underscoring molecular evolution of “phage-bacterial host” arms race.

RESULTS

Identification and characterization of NARP3

Cellular NAD+ metabolism is sustained by three de novo pathways (Fig. 1A)—two producing quinolinic acid (QA) from tryptophan (Trp) or l-aspartate (Asp), and a third “C3N” route from chorismate to nicotinic acid (NA) (50). These pathways are complemented by salvage routes that recycle NA and NAM, ensuring rapid NAD+ replenishment (Fig. 1A). Phages have recently been shown to encode two enzymatic NAD+ reconstitution pathways: NARP1, which converts ADPR into NAD+, and NARP2, which channels NAM through NMN formation and adenylation (Fig. 1A) (49). Consistent with phage pan-genomic analysis of NAD+ utilization (11), Escherichia phage TrudiRoth features a “two gene” cassette of Bas30_0086/Bas30_0087 (hereafter abbreviated as Bas30_86/87) that programs a putative NAD+ salvage route (49). Bas30_87, a PnuC-type transmembrane protein, likely functions in NR uptake (fig. S1, A and B). Bas30_86 encodes a truncated NadR-like enzyme lacking HTH domain at N terminus (fig. S2A), in contrast to canonical NadR (51), which contains HTH, NMNAT, and ribosyl-nicotinamide kinase (RNK) domains (fig. S2, A and B). This genomic organization suggests that Bas30_87-mediated NR import is functionally coupled with Bas30_86-driven NR metabolism, consistent with a novel salvage route (Fig. 2, A and B). We designate this system NARP3, representing a third phage-encoded NAD+ regeneration strategy (Fig. 1B). However, this postulate requires experimental validation.

To assess the defensive role of NARP3, we engineered Escherichia coli strains carrying two distinct NAD+-depletion systems, EcSir2-HerA from E. coli and AbSir2-HerA from Acinetobacter baumannii (10), and challenged them with phages T7 and λ (Fig. 1C). Expression of Bas30_86/87 operon imposed minimal metabolic cost on the host (fig. S3), resembling NARP1, rather than the more burdensome NARP2 (49). Plaque assays showed that neither Bas30_86 nor Bas30_87 alone could rescue infection, but coexpression of the operon increased phage sensitivity by ∼103- to 104-fold compared to vector controls, consistent with a synergistic requirement of both components (Fig. 1D and fig. S4). In control strains, extensive plaque formation reflected efficient lytic activity, whereas introduction of EcSir2-HerA or AbSir2-HerA reduced lysis by ∼103- to 104-fold, indicating robust anti-phage defense (fig. S4, A and B). Similarly, we also noticed that NARP3 substantially counteracts DSR1 defensive system by restoring host NAD+ level (fig. S5, A and B). Metabolite profiling confirmed that Sir2-HerA systems depleted NAD+ by 7- to 17-fold and NADH by three- to fivefold, while coexpression of Bas30_86/87 fully restored both pools to parental levels (Fig. 1E). These data demonstrate that Bas30_86/87 constitutes NARP3, an NR-based salvage operon that neutralizes NAD+ depletion and restores phage infectivity. By coupling transport and enzymatic functions into a compact module, NARP3 provides phages with a streamlined, infection-optimized pathway that complements NARP1 and NARP2 as part of a diverse arsenal to subvert NAD+-guided bacterial immunity.

Evolutionary distribution of NARP3

Approximately 7% of sequenced bacterial genomes encode NAD+-centered defense systems (49, 52), highlighting the central role of NAD+ metabolism in host-phage conflicts. Among the two NAD+-targeted countermeasures described to date, NARP1 occurs in ∼4.3% of sequenced phage genomes (859/20,000) from the BASEL (BActeriophage SElection for your Laboratory) collection (53), whereas NARP2 is rare (∼1.1%) (49). To assess the prevalence of a potential third variant, NARP3, we queried the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database with the Bas30_86/87 operon, identifying 124 homologous two-gene modules (Fig. 2A). These operons are largely restricted to Enterobacteriaceae-infecting phages, most frequently in Salmonella (75%) and Escherichia (15%), with occasional occurrences in Yersinia, Klebsiella, Citrobacter, and Enterobacter (∼1% each). The conserved two-gene architecture across otherwise unrelated viral lineages suggests functional coupling and selective maintenance as a molecular evolutionary unit.

Phylogenetic analyses reveal that both NARP3 components have broad evolutionary distributions beyond Enterobacteriaceae phages. Bas30_86, a NadR-like enzyme, clusters with phage homologs from Aeromonas, Vibrio, Escherichia, Salmonella, and Yersinia, as well as chromosomal loci from distant bacteria such as Brucella and Clostridia. Similarly, Bas30_87, a PnuC-type transporter, aligns with phage homologs and bacterial and archaeal sequences from Bacillus, Cytobacillus, Clostridia, and Methanobrevibacter (Fig. 2D). Their dual presence in phage and bacterial genomes indicates repeated horizontal transfer and recurrent mobilization (Fig. 2, C and D). Mechanistically, NARP3 constitutes a distinct NAD+ regeneration pathway: Imported NR via Bas30_87/PnuC is converted intracellularly by Bas30_86/NadR, contrasting with NARP1 (ADPR recycling) and NARP2 (NMN ligation) (49). This modular arrangement exemplifies phage reconfiguration of host-derived metabolites into streamlined infection-optimized pathways. Together with NARP1 and NARP2, NARP3 highlights convergent phage strategies to overcome NAD+-targeted bacterial defenses, illustrating metabolic ingenuity of viral adaptation.

Bas30_87 acts as an NR transporter

To probe its biochemical role in NARP3 counterdefense pathway (Fig. 1A), we first overexpress Bas30_87, a predicted inner-membrane protein (fig. S1), and purify it to homogeneity. The yielded Bas30_87 migrates as a single band of ∼24 kDa on SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), consistent with its expected monomeric mass (Fig. 3A). Gel filtration chromatography on a Superdex 200 shows that the recombinant Bas30_87 elutes at a volume of ∼15 ml, corresponding to an apparent molecular mass of ∼70 kDa. This observation is consistent with a stable trimer complex, as further confirmed by chemical cross-linking assays with ethylene glycol bis(succinimidyl succinate) (EGS) (Fig. 3A). Structural predictions generated by AlphaFold 3 suggest that Bas30_87 adopts a helical bundle architecture consisting of nine transmembrane α-helices and resembles Neisseria mucosa PnuC [Protein Data Bank (PDB): 4QTN], forming a trimer through extensive protomer-protomer interactions (Fig. 3B and fig. S6A). Docking simulations identify a central cavity within each Bas30_87 protomer, capable of accommodating an NR molecule (Fig. 3B). Structural alignment with PnuC yields a root mean square deviation (RMSD) of 0.89 Å, highlighting conservation of the NR-recognition fold (fig. S6D). While PnuC engages four residues for NR coordination (Q124 on α5-helix, Q196 on α7-helix, N214 on α8-helix, and Y242 on α9-helix; fig. S6, B and E), Bas30_87 only relies on three residues (N45/α3-helix, Q94/α5-helix, and N180/α8-helix; fig. S6, C and F), indicating a reduced but efficient pattern for NR entry.

We then exploit multiple approaches to test this hypothesis. Consistent with the isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) results (fig. S7A), microscale thermophoresis (MST) assays demonstrate that wild-type Bas30_87 binds NR with a dissociation constant of 664.8 ± 201.9 nM (Fig. 3C). Alanine substitution of each NR-coordinating residue (N45, Q94, and N180) completely abolishes NR binding, confirming their functional importance (Fig. 3C). To assess its physiological relevance of NR transport by Bas30_87, we evaluate the impact of NR-binding mutations on NAD+ metabolism and phage counterdefense using engineered E. coli strains expressing diverse variants of NARP3 (Bas30_86/87) pathway (figs. S8 and S9). As expected, the wild-type NARP3 efficiently restores intracellular NAD+ and NADH levels in E. coli following depletion by EcSir2-HerA or AbSir2-HerA systems. In contrast, the three single-point mutants of Bas30_87 (N45A, Q94A, and N180A) uniformly disable NARP3 in restoring cytosolic NAD+ pools (fig. S8, A and B). Consistent with these metabolic defects, NR-binding mutants of Bas30_87 markedly attenuate an ability of NARP3 to rescue T7 and λ phage propagation (fig. S9, A and B). Beyond the single-point mutants that display a substantial loss of counterdefense activity, double- and triple-point mutants further exacerbate this defect (Fig. 3D and fig. S9), emphasizing the cooperative role of the three residues in functional NR transport. Therefore, these findings define Bas30_87 as a trimeric NR transporter whose conserved binding pocket is indispensable for NARP3 strategy to neutralize bacterial NAD+-centered immunity (Fig. 3D).

Bas30_86 catalyzes NR-to-NAD+ conversion

Having established that Bas30_87 imports NR for the NARP3 countermeasure (Fig. 3), we next investigate how the imported NR is enzymatically transformed into NAD+ within the infected cell. We focus on Bas30_86, a NadR-like bifunctional enzyme that is localized immediately upstream of Bas30_87 (Fig. 2B) and hypothesized to catalyze the stepwise synthesis of NAD+ from NR (Figs. 4 and 5). Bas30_86 exhibits selective recognition of three metabolites along the NAD+ salvage route—NR, NMN, and NAD+—as illustrated in the proposed scheme (Fig. 4A). Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) analyses confirm their molecular identities, detecting NR at a mass/charge ratio (m/z) of 255.10 (theoretical, 255.70) (Fig. 4B), NMN at m/z 335.06 (predicted, 334.22) (Fig. 4D), and NAD+ at m/z 664.12 (Fig. 4F). In general agreement with the ITC results (fig. S7B), MST assays reveal that Bas30_86 efficiently binds NR [dissociation constant (Kd) = 33.9 ± 20.2 nM] (Fig. 4C) and NAD+ with comparable strength (Kd = 55.8 ± 30.5 nM) (Fig. 4G), while its interaction with NMN is substantially weaker (Kd = 6.8 ± 2.6 μM) (Fig. 4E). This hierarchy of affinities indicates that Bas30_86 tightly captures both substrate and product while transiently engaging the intermediate, a pattern typical of certain bifunctional enzymes that couple substrate sensing with catalysis. Thus, these results suggest that Bas30_86 of NARP3 appears as a dual-function NAD+-interacting enzyme capable of recognizing and processing the full sequence of NAD+ metabolites.

To elucidate its catalytic mechanism, we reconstitute the Bas30_86-driven conversion of NR to NAD+ in vitro and monitor reaction products by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Fig. 5A). Comparison with authentic standards establishes distinct retention times for NR (2.407 min), NMN (2.937 min), and NAD+ (5.943 min) (Fig. 5B). In the first catalytic step, Bas30_86 phosphorylates NR to generate NMN, accompanied by ADP formation indicative of ATP consumption (Fig. 5, C and D). Although NMN represents the expected intermediate in the “first-step” reaction, co-occurrence of the downstream NAD+ product is captured here, likely due to continued turnover by the intrinsic NMNAT activity of Bas30_86. We therefore interpret the simultaneous detection of NMN and NAD+ as mechanistically consistent with the bifunctional nature of Bas30_86, rather than indicative of contamination or product misassignment (Fig. 5, C and D). In the second step, Bas30_86 adenylates NMN using ATP to produce NAD+, confirmed by its characteristic elution profile (Fig. 5, E and F). The sequential reactions demonstrate that Bas30_86 catalyzes the two-step conversion “NR → NMN → NAD+”, efficiently coupling ATP hydrolysis to product formation (Fig. 5, A to F). In sum, Bas30_87 and Bas30_86 define NARP3, an integrated NR transport–biosynthesis module that counters NAD+ depletion under phage attack.

Crystal structure of Bas30_86

We express and purify the Bas30_86 enzyme for subsequent structural analyses. The SDS-PAGE profile shows that the Bas30_86 migrates as a single band at ∼40 kDa, consistent with its expected monomeric mass (fig. S10A). Size exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a Superdex 200 column reveals that recombinant Bas30_86 elutes at the volume of ∼16.6 ml, slightly later than the bovine serum albumin standard (∼66 kDa, ∼16.0 ml), indicating that this enzyme exists as a monomeric state in solution (fig. S10B). The concentrated Bas30_86 protein is subjected to a crystallization screen, yielding two structures: the apo-form at 2.7-Å resolution and the NAD+-liganded complex at 3.4-Å resolution (Table 1). Both crystals belong to the P43212 space group, with four protomers per asymmetric unit (Table 1). Each protomer consists of two α/β-folded domains (figs. S2 and S10, B to D): an N-terminal NMNAT domain (residues 1 to 166) and a C-terminal RNK domain (residues 167 to 351). The NMNAT domain contains eight α-helices and five parallel β-strands forming a central twisted β-sheet, whereas the RNK domain features nine α-helices and five β-strands arranged into a similar α/β topology (fig. S10D). Structural alignment among the four apo-form protomers reveals highly conserved overall folds (RMSD of 0.35 ∼ 0.50 Å), with the NMNAT core displaying the greatest stability (RMSD of 0.27 ∼ 0.38 Å). Notably, the β3-α5 linker (residues P84 to G89) exhibits partial disorder in protomers A and B and adopts distinct conformations in protomers C and D, suggesting intrinsic flexibility. These structural insights establish Bas30_86 as a monomeric NadR-type bifunctional enzyme with a modular “NMNAT-RNK” organization, poised for dynamic interdomain communication.

S11B), structural comparison with the cryo–electron microscopy structure of E. coli NadR (PDB: 8X7F) nevertheless reveals strong conservation of the NMNAT and RNK folds in Bas30_86, with RMSD values of 1.12 to 1.32 Å (fig. S12, A to C). The NMNAT domain of KVP40 NatV could likewise be aligned with those of Bas30_86 and E. coli NadR, with RMSD values of 2.12 to 2.30 Å (fig. S12D). Relative to Bas30_86, KVP40 NatV contains a shorter NMN-binding loop and lacks aromatic residues corresponding to Y86 and W90 that stabilize the NMN moiety. Nonetheless, residues D78, Y79, and W85 of KVP40 NatV occupy positions analogous to E80, P84, and Y93 of Bas30_86, suggesting partial conservation of NAD+ recognition (fig. S12E). By contrast, the ATP-binding loops differ substantially in both sequence and conformation, indicating divergent mechanisms for adenyl-moiety recognition.

NAD+-induced conformational flexibility of Bas30_86

To probe NAD+ recognition, we align the apo-structure of Bas30_86 with its NAD+-bound complex (fig. S13, A to D). Although the overall folds of the NMNAT and RNK domains remain conserved (fig. S13, A and B), pronounced conformational rearrangements are observed in the β3-α5 linker region of the NMNAT domain upon ligand binding (fig. S14A). In the apo structure, protomers C and D display “open” and “closed” loop states (fig. S13E), while protomers A and B lack traceable electron density in this region (fig. S13A). Following NAD+ binding, the link in all four protomer (A′-D′) adopts well-defined “closed” or “open” configurations, capturing distinct states of a dynamic gating cycle (fig. S13, B to D). In the NAD+-bound protomers A′ and B′, the linker adopts nearly identical “closed” conformation (fig. S13B), while the ligand-free protomer C′ exhibits a different “closed” conformation, indicating subtle variations in the linker’s position (fig. S13F). Rotation between the “closed” and “open” states of the linker ranges from 55° to 79° (fig. S13, E, H to J, M, and P), with minimal rotation observed between the “open” linkers in the two structures (fig. S13N). The β3-α5 linker contains a central Tyr residue (Y86), which forms hydrophobic interaction with NAD+ in the ligand-bound protomers (fig. S13, K and L). In the apo-form, the chain of Y86 is rotated ∼76° in protomer D (fig. S13, K and L), while in protomer C′ of the NAD+ complex, Y86 adopts a distinct conformation (figs. S13O and S14B). These observations support the hypothesis that the β3-α5 linker functions as a gatekeeper in regulating NAD+ synthesis and release. In addition, key residues at site 2, including K10, H18, E80, L114, and D115 (fig. S14B), are critical for efficient NAD+ synthesis, emphasizing the importance of both structural and functional flexibility in Bas30_86’s catalytic mechanism (Fig. 6).

Mechanistic insights into stepwise catalysis of Bas30_86

Structural and biochemical analyses of Bas30_86 reveal a two-step, domain-coordinated mechanism for NAD+ regeneration, involving its RNK and NMNAT domains (Figs. 4 to 6). Structural modeling using AlphaFold 3 elucidates the architecture of two distinct catalytic pockets within Bas30_86: the NR-binding pocket (site 1) in the RNK domain (Fig. 6A) and the NAD+-synthesizing pocket (site 2) in the NMNAT domain (Fig. 6C). Site 1, dedicated to NR binding, contains key residues K163, R167, K168, E230, and D275, which form a positively charged cavity conducive to NR recognition (Fig. 6A). Docking studies suggest that site 1 also accommodates the NMN intermediate, facilitated by a conformational change in K163 to D164 (Fig. 6B). At site 2, within the NMNAT domain, distinct residues—K10, S37, E80, P84, G89, and D115—coordinate interactions with the phosphate and ribose moieties of NMN (Fig. 6C). NAD+ binds at site 2 more tightly than NMN, stabilized through extensive hydrophobic and aromatic interactions (Fig. 6D and fig. S10B), confirming that this site is the product-forming pocket. We successfully determine the Bas30_86/NAD+ complex structure at 3.4-Å resolution (Table 1), which corroborates the binding mode of NAD+ and supports the mechanistic model (fig. S14, A to D).

The stepwise mechanism of NAD+ synthesis is orchestrated by these two catalytic sites (Fig. 6E). NR binds at site 1 and is converted into NMN, which is then transferred to site 2 for ATP-dependent adenylation to produce NAD+ (Fig. 6E). Structural and biochemical data support this two-site model. In the NAD+-liganded Bas30_86 crystal structure, the final product exclusively occupies site 2, confirming its role in NAD+ synthesis (Fig. 6D and fig. S14). Mutational analysis of key residues in either site provides further evidence of the importance of both domains in NAD+ regeneration (Fig. 7 and fig. S15, A and B). Mutations in site 1/RNK domain (e.g., E230A and D275A) or site 2/NMNAT domain (e.g., K10A/S37A and E80A/P84A/G89A) result in substantial reductions in NAD+ regeneration, disabling Bas30_86/87 NARP3 to restore NAD+ levels and to support phage propagation during phage infection (Figs. 6F and 7, A to C). These findings highlight the indispensable roles of both the RNK and NMNAT domains in the activity of Bas30_86 primed for sequential NR-to-NAD+ regeneration that counteracts NAD+ depletion by Sir2-HerA catalysis observed in vitro and in vivo (Figs. 5 to 7).

In vivo experiments further emphasize the functional role of Bas30_86 in counteracting bacterial anti-phage defenses. In engineered E. coli strains expressing NAD+ depleting systems (Fig. 7A), the NARP3 pathway carrying the wild-type Bas30_86 efficiently restores intercellular NAD+ and NADH levels despite their hydrolysis by EcSir2-HerA (Fig. 7B) or AbSir2-HerA (Fig. 7C). This replenishment of redox balance supports continued phage replication even in the presence of NAD+ depletion. In contrast, mutants with defective catalytic activity—whether due to impaired NR/NMN-binding (e.g., E230A and D275A) or product formation (e.g., F110A)—fail to recover NAD+ pools, resulting in significantly reduced phage fitness (Fig. 6F). Mutational analysis of key residues, including single (F110A, E230A, and D275A), double (K10A/F11A and S112A/D115A), and triple (E80A/P84A/G89A and K10A/V139A/I141A) substitutions, progressively diminishes NAD+ regeneration (Fig. 7 and fig. These results not only validate the structural predictions but also establish the physiological relevance of Bas30_86’s catalytic mechanism in subverting bacterial immunity. The correlation between catalytic defects and decreased phage propagation underscores the vital contribution of both RNK and NMNAT domains for efficient phage survival and replication in hostile bacterial environments.

Efficacy of NARP3 in ESKAPE pathogen

Given that NARP3 depends on exogenous NR availability, we ask whether precursor abundance constrains phage rescue under nutrient-limited conditions. In M9 medium, EcSir2-HerA strongly restricted both T7 and λ phages, whereas coexpression of NARP3 progressively restores productive infection as NR concentrations increased (Fig. 8, A to C). Quantitative analysis reveals low-micromolar median effective concentration (EC50) values for rescue of T7 and λ propagation (Fig. 8D), indicating that NARP3 efficiently couples environmental NR salvage to intracellular NAD+ replenishment. The results establish that phage-encoded NARP3 remains active even under certain metabolic competitions. We next investigate whether this mechanism overcomes antiviral immunity in Klebsiella pneumoniae, a major ESKAPE pathogen (Fig. 9, A to D). Sir2-HerA defensive systems from K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, and E. coli each confer broad resistance against a panel of environmental K. pneumoniae phages (Fig. 9E and table S1), highlighting the conserved role of NAD+ metabolite in abortive immunity. Mechanistically, productive infection requires maintenance of intracellular NAD+ pools, whereas activation of Sir2-HerA redirects infected cells into an abortive state characterized by catastrophic NAD+ hydrolysis and failure of phage replication (Fig. 9, A and B). NARP3 reverses this metabolic blockade by restoring NAD+ homeostasis to permit viral productive infection (Fig. 9C). Consistent with this model, introduction of NARP3 broadly restores plaque formation across all tested phages and genetic defense sets (Fig. 9F). Together, these findings support that NARP3 functions as an engineerable anti-defense module, by rewiring host NAD+ metabolism to bypass Sir2-HerA defense. More broadly, this framework suggests the feasibility of engineering therapeutic phages carrying NARP3-like pathways to target multidrug-resistant ESKAPE pathogens (Fig. 9D).

DISCUSSION

The diversification of bacterial defense mechanisms [≥152 systems; (15, 52)] has driven the evolution of an expanding repertoire of phage anti-defense systems, currently encompassing more than 156 subtypes (7, 54). Among them, most countermeasures either directly target the defensive components—for example, Anti-CRISPR [AcrF variants; (55–57)] and Gabija anti-defense 1 [Gad1; (58, 59)]—or act by sequestering or even degrading defense-associated signaling molecules, such as Thoeris anti-defense 1 (Tad1) (60), Anti-CBASS [Acb1 (61) and Acb2 (62, 63)], and Anti-Pycsar (Apyc1) (61). NARP1 and NARP2 exemplify a distinct metabolic evasion strategy that enables phages to resist multiple bacterial NAD+-depleting systems, typified by Sir2-HerA (49). The discovery of NARP3, a two-gene module described here, expands this repertoire by uncovering a previously unrecognized mechanism that mitigates the metabolic consequences of bacterial immunity rather than directly antagonizing its core machinery. Consistent with NARP1 and NARP2 (49), this finding highlights an emerging theme in the phage-host arms race: Anti-defense systems can act not only by disabling immune effectors but also by buffering the physiological stress imposed by host defenses. NAD+ has emerged as a central metabolic target for diverse bacterial immunity pathways (37–39, 49, 64–67). Similarly, the other nucleoside pools are disrupted by distinct systems, including (i) ATP hydrolysis by CBASS (68) or RADAR (69) effectors, (ii) 2′-deoxycytidine 5′-triphosphate deamination by cytosine deaminases (70, 71), and (iii) 2′-deoxyguanosine 5′-triphosphate degradation by guanosine triphosphatase (71). The presence of three NAD+ reconstitution pathways (NARP1 to NARP3) across diverse phages (49) suggests that nucleotide regeneration may constitute a broader viral strategy for maintaining metabolic activity under immune attack—an intriguing possibility that warrants further mechanistic investigation (72). In addition, we acknowledge a limitation of this study: The in vivo function of NARP3 could not be examined in its native phage context, likely due to the limited accessibility of this rare phage TrudiRoth (Bas30) within the Chinese phage research community.

The structural and mechanistic features of NARP3, exemplified by Bas30_86 and Bas30_87, underscore this restorative logic (Figs. 1 and 3 to 6). The NadR-type Bas30_86 retains a canonical NMNAT fold and catalytic mechanism conserved across cellular NAD+-synthesizing enzymes but has been repurposed for a viral metabolic context. The conservation of substrate recognition and product formation between phage and bacterial NMNATs suggests that phages have selectively integrated host-like enzymatic architectures to achieve efficient NAD+ restoration. Its physical and functional coupling with the PnuC-like NR transporter Bas30_87 forms a co-optimized system that links substrate uptake to catalytic turnover (Fig. 5), allowing rapid replenishment of NAD+ pools depleted by bacterial NADases (Fig. 7). In contrast to this tightly coupled phage arrangement (Fig. 2), pnuC (b0751) and nadR (b4390) in E. coli are encoded at separate chromosomal loci, reflecting the dispersed organization of bacterial NAD+ metabolic functions. Notably, E. coli NadR retains an additional winged helix-turn-helix, DNA binding domain (fig. S2), consistent with its role in transcriptional regulation and tight modulation of NAD+ homeostasis. This regulatory complexity may explain why E. coli NadR alone cannot counteract NAD+ hydrolysis by Sir2-HerA defense system in the absence of NARP3 (Fig. 7).

Comparative analyses reveal that while NARP1, NARP2, and NARP3 share the goal of NAD+ regeneration, they use distinct biochemical strategies (Fig. 1). NARP1 uses ADPS and NAMAT to convert ADPR to NAD+, NARP2 relies on NAMPT and NMNAT to convert NAM to NAD+, and NARP3 uniquely combines an RNK-NMNAT enzyme (Bas30_86) with a dedicated NR transporter (Bas30_87). This configuration likely benefits metabolic efficiency, enabling phage replication under harsh NAD+-limiting conditions. In contrast to NARP2, which begins with PRPP—a key precursor in purine and pyrimidine metabolism (73)—NARP3 uses NR as its priming substrate (Figs. 1 and 5). This distinction likely explains why NARP2 imposes a substantial fitness cost on E. coli host (49), whereas NARP3 does not (Fig. 3). Structural comparison of Bas30_86 with homologous NMNATs from Haemophilus influenzae and Methanococcus jannaschii further confirms conservation of the active-site architecture and NAD+-binding mode (fig. S14), underscoring the evolutionary stability of this catalytic framework. From an evolutionary standpoint, NARP3 exemplifies a metabolite-centered counterdefense paradigm in which phages rebuild key biochemical nodes disrupted by host immunity. The presence of NARP3-like modules across unrelated phage taxa implies recurrent horizontal transfer and convergent selection for NAD+ resilience (Fig. 2), suggesting that NAD+ metabolism represents a universal point of vulnerability—and innovation—in phage-bacterial host conflict. Functionally, such systems sustain phage infectivity within NAD+-depleting bacterial populations (Figs. 3 and 6), thereby influencing microbial community dynamics and horizontal gene flow. Beyond its evolutionary implications, NARP3 provides a conceptual and practical framework for engineering metabolically robust phages. Incorporating metabolite-restoring modules such as NARP3 into synthetic phage genomes could enhance resistance to bacterial immunity and enable precision control of intracellular metabolism in therapeutic or biotechnological settings.

Collectively, these findings establish NARP3 as a model for viral metabolic adaptation, capable of restoring host-derived cofactors (such as NAD+) to support phage replication. By coupling immune evasion with targeted metabolite regeneration, NARP3—together with NARP1/2—illuminates generalizable design principle that could guide the engineering of metabolically robust therapeutic phages. Harnessing such systems may enable precise modulation of intracellular metabolism in microbial engineering and provide optimized strategies for phage therapy against top-priority ESKAPE pathogens with extensive antimicrobial resistance.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Phages, bacterial strains, and growth conditions

Eight bacteriophages and two bacterial species were used in this study (table S1). The short-tailed phage T7 (74) and the long-tailed phage λ (75) were propagated using E. coli MG1655. Six K. pneumoniae phages, designated FKP1, FKP2, FKP4, FKP7, FKP10, and FKP11, were propagated using K. pneumoniae American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) 43816. All phages were stored as lysates in SM buffer (100 mM NaCl, 8 mM MgSO4, and 50 mM tris-HCl; pH 7.5) at 4°C. For infection assays, phage lysates were added at a 1:50 (v/v) ratio to early-exponential E. coli MG1655 or K. pneumoniae ATCC 43816 cultures [optical density at 600 nm (OD600) ≈ 0.2] and incubated at 37°C with shaking (200 rpm) until complete lysis was observed. All bacterial strains used in this study were derived from either E. coli or K. pneumoniae (table S1). E. coli DH5α was used for plasmid construction, E. coli BL21(DE3) for recombinant protein expression, and E. coli MG1655 for phenotypic characterization and phage-resistance assays. Unless otherwise specified, bacterial strains were cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth or on LB agar plates supplemented with appropriate antibiotics: ampicillin (100 μg/ml), kanamycin (50 μg/ml), chloramphenicol (34 μg/ml), or rifampicin (50 μg/ml).

Plasmids and molecular manipulations

Six plasmid types were used in this study: pLG024 (10), pLG022 (10), pSGKP (76), pBAD24 (77), pET28a (78, 79), and pET21a (78, 79). To evaluate the NAD+-regeneration role of the NARP3 system, two pLG024-derived constructs were generated to express the Sir2-HerA anti-phage systems that mediate NAD+ depletion upon phage infection, yielding pLG024::Ecsir2-herA and pLG024::Absir2-herA. In addition, three pSGKP-derived constructs expressing the Sir2-HerA systems were created in K. pneumoniae ATCC 43816, producing pSGKP::Ecsir2-herA, pSGKP::Absir2-herA, and pSGKP::Kpsir2-herA. Furthermore, one recombinant plasmid pLG022::Ecdsr1 was introduced in E. coli MG1655 to express the DSR1 anti-phage system. The bas30_86 gene (acc. no.: YP_011992783) was synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China) with codon optimization for E. coli expression, and bas30_87 (acc. no.: YP_011992784) was synthesized similarly. Both genes were cloned into the pBAD24 vector to generate pBAD24::bas30_86/87, which was coharbored with either pLG024::sir2-herA or pSGKP::sir2-herA (table S1). To assess the effects of individual NARP3 components on counteracting Sir2-HerA or DSR1-mediated NAD+ depletion, coexpression strains harboring pLG024::sir2-herA, pSGKP::sir2-herA, or pLG022::Ecdsr1 together with either pBAD24::bas30_86 or pBAD24::bas30_87 were also developed.

The full-length bas30_86 was chemically synthesized and directionally cloned into the pET28a vector via Bam HI and Xho I restriction sites, producing pET28a::bas30_86 that encodes an N-terminal 6 × His-tagged Bas30_86 protein. Similarly, bas30_87 was cloned into pET21a using the same restriction sites to generate pET21a::bas30_87, encoding a C-terminal 6 × His-tagged Bas30_87 protein. To investigate interactions between Bas30_87 and its substrate NR, site-directed mutagenesis was performed using the pET21a::bas30_87 plasmid as the template and a variety of specific primers (table S2), yielding three single mutants (N45A, Q94A, and N180A).

To evaluate functional importance of the substrate-binding pockets of Bas30_86 and Bas30_87 in the NARP3 system, site-directed mutagenesis was carried out using the templates of pBAD24::bas30_86/87 along with a set of specific primers (table S2). For Bas30_86, nine mutants were constructed, comprising three single mutants (F110A, E230A, and D275A), three double mutants (K10A/F11A, K10A/S37A, and S112A/D115A), and three triple mutants (E80A/P84A/G89A, K10A/V139A/I141A, and K10A/R167A/K168A). For Bas30_87, seven mutants were created, which included three single mutants (N45A, Q94A, and N180A), three double mutants (N45A/Q94A, N45A/N180A, and Q94A/N180A), and one triple mutant (N45A/Q94A/N180A).

Phage infection and plaque assays

Phage titers were determined with a modified small-drop plaque assay (80). A total of 54 strains (FYJ6494 to FYJ6498, FYJ6500 to FYJ6540, and FYJ6591 to FYJ6598; table S2) were involved in phenotypic observation, and 46 strains (FYJ6494 to FYJ6498 and FYJ6500 to FYJ6540; table S2) were involved in plaque assay. Single colonies of tested strains were inoculated into 300 μl of LB, with appropriate antibiotics added to ensure plasmid selections, and grown overnight at 37°C with shaking (200 rpm) to produce seed cultures. Seed cultures were diluted 1:100 into 2 ml of LB and grown at 37°C, 200 rpm until OD600 ≈ 1.0. Double-layer agar plates were prepared by pouring 15 ml of 1.5% LB agar into 9 cm–by–9 cm square plates (bottom layer). The soft agar layer (13 ml of 0.8% LB agar maintained at 45°C) was mixed with 1.5 ml of corresponding culture (OD600 ∼ 1.0) and immediately overlaid onto the bottom layer. Phage samples were filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane and serially diluted 10−1 to 10−7 in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). For phenotypic observation, 4 μl of each phage dilution was spotted onto the bacterial lawn, and plates were incubated at 30°C for 10 hours to assess phage infection phenotypes. For plaque enumeration, 10 μl of each dilution was spotted onto the bacterial lawn. After the spots had dried, plates were incubated at 30°C for 10 to 12 hours, and plaques from countable spots were used to calculate phage titers. NR-dependent phage rescue

To assess whether NARP3-mediated phage rescue is dependent on NR concentration, phage spot assays were performed using M9 minimal medium supplemented with varying levels of the NR precursor (81–83). Bacterial strains carrying the corresponding plasmids were grown in LB medium containing appropriate antibiotics at 37°C until the culture reached an OD600 of ∼0.8. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, at 8000g and 4°C for 5 min, washed three times with sterile PBS, and resuspended in PBS. The cell suspension was adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0 prior to use. M9 double-layer agar plates were prepared with a bottom agar layer containing 1.5% agar. For each plate, 15 ml of M9 bottom agar was poured and allowed to solidify.

The top agar was prepared with M9 medium containing 0.8% agar and supplemented with NR at varying concentrations (0, 5, 10, 20, and 30 μM). For each plate, 14 ml of molten M9 top agar was cooled to ∼50°C and mixed with 1 ml of the adjusted bacterial suspension. The mixture was immediately poured onto the bottom agar layer and allowed to dry. T7 and λ phage lysates were serially diluted 10-fold, and 4.5 μl of each dilution was spotted onto the dried bacterial lawn. After absorption of the droplets, the plates were kept at 37°C for 10 hours. Plaque formation was then examined to evaluate NR concentration-dependent rescue of phage propagation by the NARP3 system.

Protein expression and purification

A total of four proteins were overexpressed and purified: Bas30_86, the membrane protein Bas30_87, and three Bas30_87 single-point mutants (N45A, Q94A, and N180A). E. coli BL21(DE3) strains harboring respective expression plasmids were cultured to OD600 of 0.6 to 0.8 and induced with 0.3 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside. Expression of Bas30_86 was induced at 16°C for 12 hours, whereas Bas30_87 and its mutants were induced at 37°C for 4 hours, following a protocol initially established by Jaehme et al. (84). Cells were harvested by centrifugation (6000 rpm, 20 min, 4°C) and washed once with lysis buffer. Bas30_86-producing bacterial cells were resuspended in lysis buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride]. Whereas for Bas30_87, the buffer lacked 10 mM imidazole. Cell disruption was performed using a mini–French press (JNBIO, Guangzhou, China) with four to five passes. For Bas30_86, cell debris was removed by centrifugation (13,800 rpm, 50 min, 4°C). The clarified lysate was incubated with Ni-NTA agarose (QIAGEN) for 1 hour, washed with buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, and 50 mM imidazole], and eluted with buffer containing 300 mM imidazole. The eluate was dialyzed overnight against 20 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 150 mM NaCl. For Bas30_87, lysates were clarified by ultracentrifugation (150,000g, 60 min, 4°C) to collect the membrane fraction. Membrane pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer containing 1% (w/v) n-dodecyl-β-d-maltoside (DDM) and solubilized overnight at 4°C with gentle rotation. After removal of insoluble material by centrifugation (13,800 rpm, 50 min, 4°C), the supernatant was incubated with equilibrated Ni-NTA Beads 6FF for 2 hours at 4°C. The resin was washed three times with buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, and 0.03% DDM] containing increasing imidazole concentrations (20, 50, and 100 mM), and target proteins were eluted with 300 mM imidazole. Eluted proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and further purified by SEC on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare). Peak fractions containing the target protein were pooled, concentrated, analyzed by SDS-PAGE, and stored at −80°C until use.

Measurement of intracellular NAD+ and NADH

Phage preparation and titration were as described above. E. coli MG1655 cultures were grown to OD600 of ∼0.25 [∼2 × 108 colony-forming units (CFU)/ml], and viable counts were confirmed by serial dilution plating to ensure consistent multiplicity of infection (MOI). For each strain (FYJ6500 to FYJ6540), cultures adjusted to OD600 ≈ 0.25 were infected with T7 phage at an MOI of 2 and incubated at 37°C with shaking (180 rpm) for 0 min (control) or 20 min (infected). Each culture (2 ml) was harvested by centrifugation, and the pellets were washed twice with sterile PBS. Viable counts were determined by CFU plating. Each pellet was split into two equal aliquots and resuspended in 1 ml of acidic or alkaline extraction buffer. Cells were lysed by ultrasonication (20% amplitude; 2 s on, 1 s off; total of 1 min), boiled for 5 min in tightly sealed tubes to prevent evaporation, cooled on ice, and centrifuged (10,000g, 10 min, 4°C). A 200-μl aliquot of supernatant was mixed with 200 μl of the corresponding neutralization buffer and centrifuged again (10,000g, 10 min, 4°C). Final supernatants were kept on ice for subsequent assays. Intracellular NAD+ and NADH levels were quantified using the Coenzyme I NAD(H) Content Assay Kit (Micromethod; Sangon Biotech) following the manufacturer’s instructions (85). This assay was based on methylthiazolyldiphenyl-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) colorimetric method (86, 87). Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. Concentrations were calculated from standard curves and normalized to bacterial counts, expressed as nanomoles per 109 CFU.

MST analysis

MST assays were used to determine protein-ligand binding as described previously (88). Bas30_86 was tested against NR (Sigma-Aldrich), NMN (MCE), and NAD+ (Sigma-Aldrich). Bas30_87 and its mutants (N45A, Q94A, and N180A) were assayed against NR. Proteins were fluorescently labeled with the Monolith RED-NHS Second Generation Protein Labeling Kit (NanoTemper Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Labeling and binding analyses were conducted in protein-specific MST buffers: Bas30_86 buffer [20 mM Hepes (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Tween 20] and Bas30_87 buffer [20 mM Hepes (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 0.03% DDM, and 0.05% Tween 20]. Ligands were prepared in the corresponding MST buffer to avoid buffer mismatch. Measurements were performed at 25°C on a Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies) with RED/LED excitation at 20% and laser power at 60%. Labeled samples were loaded into MST Premium Coated capillaries to minimize nonspecific adsorption. Each binding curve represents the mean of three independent biological replicates. Dissociation constants (Kd) were calculated using MO.Affinity Analysis v2.3 (NanoTemper Technologies).

ITC measurement

As Huang et al. (88, 89) performed with little changes, ITC assays were performed using a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC instrument (Malvern Panalytical) to determine the binding affinities of Bas30_86 and Bas30_87 toward their respective ligands (90). Bas30_86 protein was prepared in buffer containing 20 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 150 mM NaCl. Bas30_87 protein was prepared in buffer containing 20 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, and 0.03% DDM. NAD+ and NR were prepared in the same buffer as Bas30_86 and Bas30_87, respectively.

For the Bas30_86-NAD+ binding assay, NAD+ was loaded into the syringe at a concentration of 1 mM and titrated into Bas30_86 protein in the sample cell at a concentration of 20 μM. For the Bas30_87-NR binding assay, NR was loaded into the syringe at a concentration of 1 mM and titrated into trimeric Bas30_87 in the sample cell at a concentration of 13.3 μM. All titrations were performed at 25°C. Each experiment consisted of 19 injections, with an initial injection of 0.4 μl over 0.8 s, followed by 18 injections of 2 μl over 4 s. The interval between injections was set to 150 s. The raw heat signals were integrated and analyzed using the MicroCal PEAQ-ITC analysis software. Binding parameters, including the dissociation constant (Kd), binding stoichiometry (N), and enthalpy change (ΔH), were obtained by fitting the integrated heat data to an appropriate binding model. Each ITC experiment was independently repeated three times.

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry was used to identify substrates of Bas30_86 and Bas30_87, including NR, NMN, and NAD+. Stock solutions (10 mM) were prepared in deionized water and diluted to 1 mM in 20 mM tris and 300 mM NaCl. For analysis, each sample (100 μl) was mixed with 100 μl of acetonitrile and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min), and the supernatant were analyzed on an Agilent 6460 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies) equipped with an electrospray ionization source operated in negative ion mode using multiple reaction monitoring. Chromatographic separation proceeded on a Zorbax SB C18 column using methanol with 0.1% formic acid (95:5, v/v) as the mobile phase at 0.3 ml/min. Data acquisition and processing were conducted using Agilent MassHunter Workstation software.

High-performance liquid chromatography

HPLC was applied to assay two enzymatic activities of Bas30_86 consisting of NMNAT and RNK (91). Standard 200-μl reactions contained 100 mM Hepes-KOH (pH 7.5), 10 mM MgCl2, and 20 μM Bas30_86. For NMNAT assays, substrates were 2 mM NR and 4 mM ATP; for RNK analysis, substrates were 2 mM NMN and 2 mM ATP. Reactions were incubated at 37°C for 3 hours with control reactions lacking enzyme. Proteins were precipitated by adding an equal volume of acetonitrile, and samples were centrifuged (12,000 rpm for 10 min). Supernatants were analyzed on a Dionex Ultimate 3000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a Welch Ultimate C18 column (4.6 mm by 250 mm, 3 μm). Isocratic elution was performed using 50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 5.5), 8 mM tetrabutylammonium bromide, and 8% methanol (v/v) at 1.0 ml/min. Analytes were detected at 254 nm. Five standards (ATP, ADP, NR, NMN, and NAD+) were separated under identical conditions. ATP was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich; ADP, NR, NMN and NAD+ were obtained from MedChemExpress.

Crystallization, data collection, and structure refinement

Purified Bas30_86 protein was concentrated to 15 mg/ml in buffer containing 10 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, and 2 mM dithiothreitol. For the Bas30_86/NAD+ complex, NAD+ was added to a final concentration of 5 mM prior to crystallization. Crystals of both apo and NAD+-bound Bas30_86 were obtained at 18°C using the hanging-drop vapor-diffusion method, with drops composed of equal volumes of protein solution and reservoir solution (1.0 M magnesium sulfate hydrate and 0.1 M bis-tris propane, pH 7.0). Crystals appeared within 2 days and reached full size after ∼2 weeks. Crystals were cryoprotected in reservoir solution supplemented with 25% (v/v) glycerol and flash-cooled in liquid nitrogen. Diffraction data were collected at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility beamlines BL19U1 and BL18U1 and processed using autoPROC or HKL3000 (Table 1) (92). Structures of apo and NAD+-liganded Bas30_86 were solved by molecular replacement using Phaser (CCP4 suite) (93) with an AlphaFold 3–predicted model as the search template (94). Electron density maps (2Fo−Fc and Fo−Fc) were calculated and manually inspected in COOT for model building (95). Final refinement was carried out by using phenix.refine (PHENIX suite) (96). Refinement statistics are summarized in Table 1.

Bioinformatics analyses

Phylogenetic and molecular docking analyses were performed to characterize Bas30_86 and Bas30_87, the two core components of NARP3. Homologs of both Bas30_86 and Bas30_87 were retrieved from the NCBI nonredundant protein database using BLASTP. Fifty homologs for each protein were selected based on sequence coverage and identity thresholds of >80%, and only sequences with significant similarity (E value ≤ 1 × 10−5) were retained. Multiple sequence alignments were generated using ClustalW in MEGA7 v7.0.26 with default parameters (gap opening penalty = 10, gap extension penalty = 0.1) (97). Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the maximum-likelihood method in MEGA7 v7.0.26. The best-fit substitution model was selected according to the Bayesian Information Criterion using the “Model Selection – Maximum Likelihood” function in MEGA7. The LG + G model, which accounts for among-site rate variation using a gamma distribution, was identified as the optimal model and subsequently used for tree reconstruction. Branch support was evaluated using the bootstrap method with 1000 replicates. Gaps and missing data were treated by partial deletion with a site coverage cutoff of 95%. Tree topology was optimized using the nearest-neighbor interchange algorithm with default convergence settings. Resulting trees were visualized as rectangular cladograms and annotated with taxonomic information.

Subsequently, molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina v1.1.2 program (98, 99). Receptor and ligand structures were prepared using AutoDockTools v1.5.7 (100) by removing water molecules, adding polar hydrogens and Gasteiger charges, and defining ligand rotatable bonds. Docking calculations were carried out using a grid box encompassing the predicted active site (74 × 78 × 68 Å; center coordinates: X = −69.23, Y = 6.321, Z = −12.285). The exhaustiveness parameter was set to 8, with a maximum of 100 output poses and an energy range of 3 kcal mol−1. Docking conformations were ranked using the AutoDock Vina scoring function (98, 99), and the top-scoring pose was selected for interaction analysis and visualization.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates unless otherwise stated. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Statistical significance was determined using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
