Cross-sectional study of the association between pain and depression in patients with brachial plexopathy
Authors: Erik Michael Tito-Huaraqui, Larissa Silveira-Machado, Salomon Huancahuire-Vega, David R Soriano-Moreno
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the association between pain and depression in patients with brachial plexopathy.MethodsAnalytical cross-sectional study based on an online survey of patients with a diagnosis of brachial plexopathy in Latin America. Pain was measured using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Depression was measured with the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). To evaluate the association between both variables, Poisson regression was used to calculate crude (cPR) and adjusted (aPR) prevalence ratios.Results102 patients were included with a mean age of 33.9 years and the majority were male (78.4%). The median pain according to VAS was 8 [IQR: 5–9] and 65.7% presented severe pain. The prevalence of depression was 59.8%. Each point increase in VAS pain was associated with a 13% higher prevalence of depression (aPR: 1.13; 95% CI 1.04–1.23). Other factors associated with an increased prevalence of depression were female sex (aPR: 1.65; 95% CI 1.24–2.19) and previous depressive disorder (aPR: 1.46; 95% CI 1.05–2.03).ConclusionPatients with brachial plexopathy suffer mostly from severe pain and the prevalence of depression is high. Greater pain is associated with higher prevalence of depression. Screening and a multidisciplinary approach, including mental health care, should be considered in recovery programs for these patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-025-00322-2.Keywords: Depression, Pain, Brachial plexus neuropathies

Introduction

Depressive disorders affect patients’ quality of life, mood, motivation, and daily functionality. Globally, they have a prevalence of 3440 cases per 100 000 inhabitants [1], with an increasing trend in recent years [2]. Additionally, depression is a significant risk factor for suicide [3]; however, only 22% of patients in low-income countries receive treatment for this condition [4].

The risk of developing depression is higher in patients suffering from musculoskeletal trauma, such as those with brachial plexopathy, affecting approximately 50% of patients [5]. Brachial plexus injuries represent one of the most severe forms of peripheral nerve damage, primarily affecting young men due to their greater exposure to high-risk activities, such as motorcycle accidents [6, 7]. These injuries, which can be of traumatic, iatrogenic, or idiopathic origin, lead to functional disability, chronic neuropathic pain, and psychological issues such as anxiety and depression. Previous research has indicated that up to 95% of patients with brachial plexus injuries experience neuropathic pain, which can progress to a chronic and difficult-to-treat condition, severely impacting their quality of life [8].

A systematic review has shown that chronic pain is closely linked to depression, negatively affecting the physical, mental, and social functioning of patients [9]. From a neurobiological perspective, depression is associated with alterations in the serotonergic and dopaminergic systems, as well as prolonged activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, which increases pain sensitivity [10]. This interaction suggests a bidirectional relationship in which both conditions mutually reinforce each other [11].

However, few studies address this relationship in patients with brachial plexopathy. Most do not comprehensively analyze the intensity and characteristics of pain, often relying on limited sample sizes [5, 12, 13]. Additionally, there is a clear lack of research focused on middle- to low-income populations, such as those in Latin America, where cultural and socioeconomic factors could significantly influence the dynamics between these two conditions. Understanding this relationship is essential for developing intervention strategies that integrate multidisciplinary approaches, combining pain management with mental health interventions, which could significantly improve patients’ quality of life and functionality. Furthermore, the findings of this study could be key to designing psychological and social support programs tailored to the specific needs of this population.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the association between pain and depression in patients with brachial plexopathy.

Methodology

Design and population

We conducted an analytical cross-sectional study. The study population consisted of patients with brachial plexopathy who were members of WhatsApp or Facebook groups dedicated to individuals with the same condition across Latin America. These groups were created by patients with brachial plexopathy to provide mutual support among members. Participants were native Spanish or Portuguese speakers. We included patients with a self-reported diagnosis of brachial plexopathy, aged 18 years or older, who provided informed consent to participate in the study. We excluded individuals unable to understand or complete the survey due to cognitive, language, or technological limitations. A non-probabilistic convenience sampling method was used to maximize survey dissemination. Unfortunately, because the questionnaire was distributed through online groups and the total number of individuals who received the survey link was unknown, a response rate could not be calculated.

Variables

The independent variable was pain, measured using the question “average pain level during the last month” based on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), which ranges from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain). This recall period was chosen to capture a representative estimate of ongoing pain while reducing recall bias and is consistent with approaches used in clinical and epidemiological research on chronic pain and previous similar studies [13, 14]. This method has been previously validated, showing no differences when applied via mobile phones, computers, or paper [15]. This variable was analyzed as a numerical score and descriptively categorized as follows: no pain (score of 0), mild pain (1–3), moderate pain (4–6), and severe pain (7–10). Additionally, we collected data on pain duration, type of pain (burning, cold pain, electric shocks, tingling, pins and needles, numbness, itching, or other types of pain), location (hand, forearm, arm, and shoulder), and use of analgesics (paracetamol or NSAIDs, opioids, cannabinoids, pregabalin or gabapentin, amitriptyline, or others).

The dependent variable was depression, measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), which includes nine items based on the DSM-IV criteria for major depression reported over the past two weeks. This questionnaire has been validated in the general population in both Spanish (alpha: 0.87) and Portuguese [16, 17]. Each item has four possible responses scored from 0 to 3: 0 “Not at all” 1 “Several days” 2 “More than half the days” and 3 “Nearly every day” with a total score ranging from 0 to 27, where higher scores indicate greater depression symptoms. Participants were considered to have depression if they scored 10 or higher [18].

Covariates included sex (male, female), age (years), educational level (secondary or lower, higher), marital status (single, married, cohabiting, divorced, or separated), employment status (unemployed, employed), prior anxiety disorder (no, yes), prior depressive disorder (no, yes), current use of psychiatric medication (no, yes), time since injury (months), type of injury (partial, total, unknown), and surgical treatment for the injury (no surgery, received surgery).

Procedures

Data was collected using online questionnaires hosted on Google Forms platform. Surveys were distributed in both Spanish and Portuguese to participants and shared within WhatsApp and Facebook groups. For participants who did not initially respond, the survey was resent a second time. Data collection took place between February and March 2025. The questionnaire was divided into the following sections: 1) Informed consent, 2) General characteristics (age, sex, educational level, marital status, employment status, time since injury, injury level, surgical treatment for the injury, previous psychiatric conditions, current use of psychiatric medication), 3) Pain intensity based on the VAS, 4) Pain characteristics for patients reporting a VAS score of 1 or higher (type of pain, location, use of analgesics), and 5) Depression measured using the PHQ-9 questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

The results from the Google Forms questionnaire were exported to Microsoft Excel, where the database was cleaned. Subsequently, the database was imported into the statistical software STATA V17.0 for analysis. Categorical variables were presented as absolute and relative frequencies. Numerical variables were presented as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on whether they met normality criteria. Differences in sample characteristics based on depression status were assessed using Chi-square tests for categorical variables and T student or Mann–Whitney tests for numerical variables. The association between VAS pain scores and depression was evaluated using Poisson regression analysis with robust variance to calculate crude (cPR) and adjusted prevalence ratios (aPR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Covariates were considered confounders and included in the adjusted analysis following an epidemiological model. Multicollinearity between variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a VIF greater than 10 indicating strong correlation. As a result, the age variable was recategorized into categories to avoid multicollinearity in the adjusted regression model. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The research protocol for this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Universidad Peruana Unión (approval code: 2023-CE-FCS-UPeU-067). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent to participate. The survey was anonymous, and data was kept confidential.

Results

A total of 102 participants with brachial plexopathy were included, with no exclusions due to incomplete data. Regarding the country of birth, the participants were from Brazil (38.2%), Mexico (16.7%), Colombia (16.7%), Peru (4.9%), Argentina (3.9%), Guatemala (2.9%), Honduras (2.9%), and Others (13.9%). The mean age of participants was 33.9 years. The majority were male (78.4%), had a higher education level (53.9%), were single (48.0%), and were not currently employed (56.9%). Regarding prior conditions, a considerable proportion of participants reported a history of anxiety disorder (24.5%) or depressive disorder (26.5%) and were currently taking psychiatric medication (20.6%). The median time since injury was 29.5 months, with most participants reporting a partial brachial plexus injury (52.0%) and having undergone surgery for the injury (79.4%) (Table 1).

The median pain score on the VAS was 8 [IQR: 5–9], with 94.1% of participants reporting some degree of pain and 65.7% reporting severe pain (VAS 7 to 10). The most common types of pain were burning (80.2%), electric shocks (70.8%), tingling (70.8%), numbness (67.7%), and pins and needles (61.5%). The most frequent pain locations were the hand (84.4%) and forearm (59.4%). The most used medications were pregabalin or gabapentin (60.4%) and opioids (35.4%) (Supplementary material 1).

The mean depression score on the PHQ-9 scale was 11.7. The items with the highest combined proportion of responses indicating “more than half the days” or “nearly every day” were feeling tired (45.1%), poor appetite or overeating (44.2%), feeling bad about oneself (46.1%), and difficulty concentrating (45.1%) (Supplementary material 2).

The prevalence of depression was 59.8%, significantly higher among females (86.4%, p = 0.004), those with a prior depressive disorder (81.5%, p = 0.007), and those with a higher median pain score on the VAS (8 [7 to 9], p = 0.002) (Table 2).

Discussion

Main results

In this study, patients with brachial plexopathy were included and we found that the median pain score according to the VAS was 8, with a prevalence of severe pain (VAS 7 to 10) of 65.7%. The prevalence of depression was high, reaching 59.8%. For each additional point of pain on the VAS, the prevalence of depression increased by 13%.

The high prevalence of pain in this study contrasts with a previous study conducted in China involving 510 surgically treated patients with brachial plexus injuries, which found that 67.0% experienced mild pain (VAS 1 to 3), 23.9% moderate pain (VAS 4 to 6), and only 9.1% severe pain (VAS 7 to 10) [19]. Similarly, another study conducted in Serbia with 128 surgically treated patients reported that 76.0% experienced regular pain, of which only 5.8% had severe pain (VAS 8 to 10) [20]. The higher prevalence of severe pain in our study may be due to differences in populations, as we included patients from Latin America who may have limited access to specialized pain treatment (both medical and surgical). Additionally, not all patients who responded to the survey had undergone surgery, which may have also increased the prevalence of pain.

The prevalence of depression was also higher compared to a previous study that included 1843 patients with brachial plexus injuries, which found a pre-surgical depression prevalence of 38%, based on medical records, and a post-surgical incidence of 142 cases per 1000 patients [21]. Another study in patients with brachial plexopathy and neuropathic pain found that the prevalence of depression, according to the “Beck Depression Inventory,” was 48% [5]. The higher prevalence in our study may be explained by the use of the PHQ-9, a sensitive tool that may have identified more cases of depression. Additionally, the studied population may face additional challenges such as limited mental health services, socioeconomic stress, and cultural factors that could exacerbate depressive symptoms. Another factor is that individuals were included regardless of their surgical status, potentially capturing a more vulnerable group.

Another relevant finding was that greater pain was associated with a higher prevalence of depression. On the other hand, a study conducted in Indonesia found that, through logistic regression analysis, the pain score according to the VAS was not associated with depression [12]. Another study found that patients with neuropathic pain had lower scores on the mental health scale measured with the SF36 questionnaire (p < 0.001) [5]. Conversely, a systematic review in patients with multiple sclerosis found that the prevalence of depression was higher in those with neuropathic pain, although the pathophysiology, disease course, and treatment context differ from brachial plexopathy [22]. These findings may be explained by the fact that persistent pain can lead to greater stress, depression, and a sense of losing control over one’s life [23]. Additionally, this relationship can be bidirectional, with evidence showing that neuropathic pain and depression are intimately connected, mutually influencing each other in a negative cycle that can progressively worsen the organism [24]. Nonetheless, due to the cross-sectional design of our study, it is not possible to establish temporality and direction between pain and depression.

On the other hand, the exact pathophysiological mechanism of depression induced by neuropathic pain is still not fully understood; however, it has recently been identified that certain monoaminergic neurotransmitters, such as serotonin and dopamine, are necessary for the development of both neuropathic pain and depression [25, 26]. Furthermore, it has been described that activated microglia may also increase the deleterious effects of depression associated with neuropathic pain through inflammatory pathways [27]. Finally, more and more studies are showing that certain brain regions, such as the anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, etc., may function as common regulatory regions between depression and chronic pain [28].

Implications and recommendations

The high prevalence of severe pain and depression underscores the need for screening and a multidisciplinary approach to care that integrates pain management with mental health interventions. Early identification in high-prevalence groups, particularly women and individuals with a history of depressive disorders, could lead to targeted interventions that improve outcomes. Furthermore, these findings provide valuable evidence for populations in low- and middle-income countries, where cultural and socioeconomic factors may influence the relationship between pain and depression. Future research is recommended to explore longitudinal designs to better understand causality and evaluate the effectiveness of integrated care models in improving the quality of life for these patients.

Limitations and strengths

Several limitations of this study should be considered. The cross-sectional design prevents establishing causal relationships between pain and depression or determining the direction of the association. The use of convenience sampling through social media groups may limit the external validity of the results, as the sample is not representative of the entire Latin American population of patients with brachial plexopathy. The survey method may have introduced reporting bias, as participants who chose to respond may have identified with the study topic and belonged to social media groups for mutual support, potentially overestimating the prevalence of severe pain and depression. Additionally, the PHQ-9 depression scale is designed to assess depressive symptoms but does not provide a clinical diagnosis. Also, residual confounding cannot be ruled out because some relevant variables, such as substance and alcohol use, were not assessed. However, the study also has several strengths, such as the use of validated tools (VAS and PHQ-9) in both Spanish and Portuguese. Furthermore, adjusted regression analysis was performed to reduce confounding bias.

Conclusion

Most patients with brachial plexopathy experience severe pain and depression, with greater pain being associated with a higher prevalence of depression. These findings highlight the importance of systematic screening for mental health disorders and the implementation of a multidisciplinary approach in the care of these patients, combining pain management with mental health interventions to improve their quality of life.
