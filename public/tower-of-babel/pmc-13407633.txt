Prestin as a potential biomarker for cochlear injury: Current evidence and future directions
Authors: Nienke Streefkerk, Regina Timmer, Alice I. E. Larsson, Kim E. de Jager, Alexander E. Hoetink, Wilbert P. Vermeij, Alwin D. R. Huitema, Martine van Grotel, Marry M. van den Heuvel-Eibrink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePrestin has been identified as a potential biomarker for cochlear damage and consequent ototoxicity. Although several preclinical and clinical studies have investigated prestin, a comprehensive overview of the evidence regarding its diagnostic value across different types of auditory damage and its use for cancer treatment-related ototoxicity is currently lacking.MethodsA structured literature review, based on a systematic PubMed search, was conducted to summarize preclinical and clinical studies that evaluated serum and/or plasma prestin levels in association with cochlear damage. Studies were categorized by etiology, moment of biomarker assessment, and correlation with functional hearing outcomes.ResultsTwenty-one studies (six animal, 15 human) were included. In animal models, prestin levels increased within hours to days after cochlear injury. Clinical studies showed elevated prestin levels within 30 days after acute damage, due to cisplatin exposure, noise exposure, or surgery. Higher prestin levels correlated with elevated hearing thresholds in six studies, whereas findings were more variable in age-related or chronic hearing loss. So far, no pediatric populations have been studied, and reference values or diagnostic thresholds for prestin are lacking.ConclusionPrestin shows potential for use as a biomarker for acute cochlear damage, with time-dependent patterns observed in both animal and human studies. However, heterogeneity in study design, patient populations, and measurement methods limits current general conclusions and recommendations for its use in the clinical field. Further standardized and longitudinal studies, including pediatric and cancer-survivor cohorts, are needed to determine its clinical utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00405-026-10236-6.

Introduction

Hearing loss, tinnitus, and vertigo are phenotypes of cochlear damage that may occur as adverse effects of cancer treatment, collectively referred to as ototoxicity. Among the various cancer treatment regimens, cisplatin and cranial radiotherapy are most commonly associated with irreversible hearing damage [1, 2]. In adult patients receiving cisplatin, hearing loss occurs in 35–50%, whereas in pediatric cancer patients, it affects up to 70% [3, 4]. While young age and higher cumulative doses of cisplatin are known risk factors, interindividual differences also suggest a role for an underlying (genetic) susceptibility [1, 5–8].

Cisplatin-induced ototoxicity typically manifests as direct, irreversible sensorineural hearing loss due to cochlear damage, particularly of the outer hair cells (OHCs), which play a critical role in sound amplification through electromotility. This electromotility requires metabolic activity, which makes the cells more vulnerable to oxidative stress. Cisplatin induces OHC death (1) through DNA-crosslinking, thereby blocking transcription, inducing apoptosis, and (2) by disrupting the cell’s antioxidant defense, leading to apoptosis, induced by oxidative stress [9–11]. Supporting cochlear structures, such as the spiral ganglion and stria vascularis, are also affected by these mechanisms [10]. This ototoxicity, especially in children, leads to impaired language acquisition, reduced academic performance, significant developmental and psychosocial consequences, and mental health challenges [12–15].

Recent developments have introduced systemic sodium thiosulfate (STS) as a protective agent against cisplatin-induced hearing loss (CIHL), particularly in pediatric patients with non-disseminated malignancies [16–19]. However, due to economic challenges, the early identification of patients at the highest risk of developing CIHL could be attractive to guide the potential selective and effective use of STS.

Hence, a reliable biomarker for early detection of cochlear damage would improve the ability to personalize otoprotective interventions. Prestin, a motor protein located in the lateral membrane of OHCs, is a promising candidate. As a member of the SLC26A5 transporter family, prestin is essential for OHC motility. Its expression and role in electromotility make it a plausible indicator of OHC viability status. In addition, prestin is believed to be highly specific to the inner ear tissue, although, to a lesser extent, it has also been described to be present in cardiac smooth muscle cells [20–24].

Prestin has been detected in the bloodstream, even in healthy individuals, suggesting that a baseline level of homeostatic secretion exists, possibly via proteomic recycling across the blood-labyrinth barrier [25, 26]. Elevated serum prestin levels may reflect OHC damage, especially when caused by necrosis, when cellular contents, including prestin, are released into the circulation [25, 27, 28]. This dual mechanism raises the hypothesis that systemic prestin levels could distinguish between physiological regulation and pathological cochlear injury.

While several preclinical and clinical studies have focused on prestin, an up-to-date comprehensive overview of the evidence of the diagnostic value of this biomarker for identifying diverse types of auditory damage occurring from various etiologies is currently lacking. Here, we summarize current literature evaluating prestin as a potential biomarker for cochlear damage.

Methods

A structured literature search of the PubMed database was conducted in February 2025 and covered publications from 2000 to February 2025. The search strategy included combinations of the terms prestin, ototoxicity, hearing loss, tinnitus, and vertigo; the complete search strategy is listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Titles and abstracts were screened, followed by a full-text assessment. Study selection was performed independently by two reviewers. A flow diagram summarizing the study selection process is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. Preclinical and clinical studies investigating circulating prestin levels in peripheral blood in relation to cochlear impairment were included. Reviews, book chapters, and non-English publications were excluded. To facilitate comparison across studies, prestin concentrations reported in different units (pg/mL and ng/mL) were converted to a uniform unit (ng/mL).

For clinical studies, methodological quality was appraised using selected domains of the QUIPS (Quality In Prognosis Studies) framework [29]. The following domains were evaluated: study participation, prestin measurement (including assay platform and reporting transparency), outcome measurement, and confounding and statistical analysis. Related QUIPS domains were combined (e.g., study participation and attrition, and confounding and statistical analysis) to improve applicability to the heterogeneous study designs included in this review. Given the heterogeneity in study designs and endpoints, a formal quantitative scoring system was not applied; instead, studies were qualitatively assessed as having low, moderate, or high risk of bias within each domain, and these assessments informed the narrative synthesis.

As QUIPS was developed for human prognostic studies, formal risk-of-bias assessment was restricted to clinical studies. Preclinical studies were critically appraised narratively, with attention to experimental design, sample size, timing of prestin measurements, and methodological transparency.

Results

Study selection

The search identified 477 publications, of which 21 were included in this review after title, abstract and full text screening (Supplementary Fig. 1). These studies consisted of six animal studies and 15 human studies in a clinical setting. Extracted data are summarized in Tables 1 and 2 for animal and clinical studies, respectively.

Preclinical evidence of prestin as a biomarker for hearing loss

Table 1 provides an overview of the six animal studies, published between 2016 and 2022, investigating prestin as a biomarker for cochlear damage. A total of 192 animals with induced auditory damage and 58 healthy controls were included. Three studies utilized rats as an animal model [20, 30, 31], one investigated mice [32], one guinea pigs [33], and one included both mice and guinea pigs [34].

In four studies, ototoxicity was induced via intraperitoneal administration of cisplatin [30, 32–34]. The remaining two studies employed noise trauma, using sound intensities between 110 and 121 decibels to elicit auditory damage [20, 31]. The time between damage induction and prestin measurement varied considerably. Prestin levels were assessed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits targeting the SLC26A5 protein. ELISA kits from My BioSource (San Diego, CA, USA) [20, 31–34] and Elabscience (Houston, TX, USA) [30] were used in five and one study, respectively. Prestin was measured in serum in four studies [30, 32–34] and in plasma in two [20, 31]. In CIHL models, blood was sampled between 0 and 56 days post-cisplatin administration. In noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL) models, samples were collected between 0 and 14 days after noise exposure. Mean prestin levels in controls were 0.18–8.05 ng/mL, and values in cisplatin and noise-exposed animals ranged from 0.15 to 24.83 ng/mL.

All four CIHL studies demonstrated an initial increase in prestin levels in serum following cisplatin administration. In one study, prestin levels were elevated at days 5–14 (15.18 ± 1.89 ng/mL), continued to rise until days 33–42 (24.83 ± 2.56 ng/mL), and declined at day 56 (9.00 ng/mL), in one study [32]. Another study examined the effect of cisplatin (n = 19) or amikacin (n = 16) at high and low doses in rats [30]. Mean prestin levels in the control group were 0.38 ± 0.14 ng/mL. Following cisplatin administration, prestin levels showed a dose-dependent increase (low dose: 0.41 ± 0.07 ng/mL; high dose: 0.56 ± 0.05 ng/mL). A comparable dose-dependent pattern was observed after amikacin (low dose: 0.41 ± 0.07 ng/mL; high dose: 0.51 ± 0.11 ng/mL). A third study assessed cisplatin in combination with either saline or diltiazem in 20 guinea pigs [33]. Diltiazem was evaluated as a potential otoprotective agent. Prestin increased in the saline group within the first two days (+ 25% from baseline) but returned to baseline thereafter, whereas no significant change was observed in the diltiazem group. The fourth study observed elevated prestin levels in both mice (n = 5, 2.5 ± 0.33 ng/mL) and guinea pigs (n = 10, 5.85 ± 0.14 ng/mL) up to day 7, followed by a decline to nearly baseline levels at day 14 post-cisplatin (1.33 ± 0.08 ng/mL and 4.97 ± 0.32 ng/mL, respectively) [34].

Auditory function was measured in all CIHL studies, predominantly via click-evoked auditory brainstem response (ABR) [32–34]. In half of all studies, ABR thresholds closely mirrored prestin dynamics [32, 34]. In one of these studies, this correlation was evident in the five mice, but not in the ten guinea pigs [34]. Another study in ten guinea pigs reported that prestin levels declined by day 3, while ABR thresholds only began to improve by day 7, suggesting prestin preceded the audiological testing [33]. One study used distortion product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAE), revealing reduced amplitudes with increasing drug dosage, which corresponded with the dose-dependent rise in prestin levels [30].

The two NIHL studies, performed in rats (n = 41 in total), showed a more rapid decline in prestin levels in plasma after an initial rise [20, 31]. One study observed decreased levels 14 days post-exposure (0.15 ng/mL) compared to controls (0.26 ng/mL) [20]. Another study reported an increase 4 h post-exposure (+ 0.001–0.016 ng/mL from baseline), followed by a decrease at 24 h (−0.008 to −0.030 ng/mL from baseline) [31]. Both studies used tone pip ABR and demonstrated decreasing thresholds over time.

Hence, five of the six animal studies showed an initial rise in prestin levels following injury. While prestin levels tended to remain elevated in CIHL cases, a more transient pattern was observed following acoustic trauma. Animal studies that investigated long-term prestin levels as a biomarker for permanent drug- or noise-induced hearing loss are not available.

Clinical evidence of prestin as a biomarker for hearing loss

Prestin levels were evaluated in 15 clinical studies published between 2018 and 2024, involving a total of 681 patients and 458 healthy controls (Table 2). Study populations included individuals with confirmed or suspected hearing impairment, as well as patients assessed in the postoperative setting, following lead exposure, or in the context of vertigo.

All studies included adults, with seven reporting a mean age above 50 years [35–41]. Hearing loss was assessed using pure tone audiometry (PTA) or DPOAE. Blood samples were analyzed using ELISA kits from My BioSource (San Diego, CA, USA) (n = 10), Bioassay Technology Laboratory (Shanghai, China) (n = 4), and SunRedBio (Shanghai, China) (n = 1). Prestin levels were measured in serum in 13 studies and in plasma in two studies. Samples were taken between minutes to several weeks after either detectable cochlear damage or after damaging treatment. Mean prestin levels in controls ranged between 0.150 and 1.162 ng/mL, whereas values in patients varied from 0.110 to 1.956 ng/mL.

One clinical study investigated CIHL in adult patients (n = 52). Patients were followed for three weeks from the start of treatment, with weekly blood sampling. Prestin levels increased from 0.132 ng/mL at baseline to 0.529 ng/mL at week 3, correlating with aggravating hearing loss, as confirmed by elevated PTA thresholds (p < 0.002) [37]. Studies on longer-term (months to years) evaluation of prestin in patients with persistent CIHL are not yet available.

Two studies assessed prestin dynamics around surgical interventions [41, 42]. In patients undergoing myringoplasty (n = 21), prestin levels were 0.556 ± 0.259 ng/mL pre-operatively, increased to 0.671 ± 0.353 ng/mL shortly after surgery, declined to 0.620 ± 0.360 ng/mL on day 3, and rose again to 0.766 ± 0.399 ng/mL on day 7 [42]. In mastoidectomy patients (n = 21), levels were 0.646 ± 0.516 ng/mL at baseline, increased to 0.745 ± 0.500 ng/mL shortly after surgery, declined to 0.687 ± 0.480 ng/mL on day 3, and reached 0.799 ± 0.607 ng/mL on day 7. In both groups, prestin levels were significantly higher on day 7 compared to baseline (p = 0.001). These changes paralleled increasing PTA thresholds. Similarly, in another study involving surgery for inner ear tumors (n = 42), prestin levels increased intraoperatively (from 0.59 ng/mL to 1.32 ng/mL) and decreased postoperatively (0.86 ng/mL), although these differences were not statistically significant [41].

One study examined the effect of different levels of lead exposure on hearing loss and prestin levels [43]. In the low-lead group (< 10 ug/dL), patients with hearing loss ≥ 25 dB (n = 21) showed lower prestin levels (0.63 ng/mL) than controls without hearing loss (n = 79) (11.06 ng/mL). In the high-lead group (> 10 ug/dL), prestin levels were similar between patients (n = 47) (0.14 ng/mL) and controls (n = 136) (0.15 ng/mL). Prestin levels were significantly lower in the high-lead group compared to the low-lead group.

Six other studies investigated patients with sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL), including idiopathic and age-related forms. In studies where prestin was measured within 30 days of symptom onset, mean levels were higher in patients (0.174–1.500.174.500 ng/mL) than in controls (0.118–1.163 ng/mL) [35, 40, 44]. In the studies assessing prestin levels after a longer interval – often without a clearly defined onset of damage – findings were more variable, and even in some cases, prestin levels were lower in patients (0.080–0.630 ng/mL) than in controls (0.150–1.106 ng/mL) [36, 43]. When comparing mild versus severe SNHL, prestin levels did not differ between subgroups (both 1.500 ng/mL), although PTA thresholds increased [45]. Age-stratified studies indicated that prestin levels increased with age until approximately 50 years, after which prestin levels declined despite worsening hearing outcomes [36, 39]. In patients treated with corticosteroids for sudden SNHL, prestin levels decreased from 0.2277 to 0.1801 ng/mL in plasma, paralleled by a reduction in PTA thresholds [40, 44].

Four studies investigated prestin levels in the context of NIHL. In one study measuring prestin levels within hours of acute noise exposure, an initial increase was observed (from 0.140 ng/mL to 0.158 ng/mL), followed by a decrease after 24 h (0.149 ng/mL), returning to baseline level after one week (0.139 ng/mL) [46]. Changes in PTA thresholds at these time points followed a similar pattern. Two studies examined groups with varying levels of occupational noise exposure (0–95 dB). In one, measured in serum, no significant differences in prestin levels were found between exposure groups (office workers 0.269 ± 0.042 ng/mL, construction workers 0.234 ± 0.029 ng/mL, pilots 0.211 ± 0.027 ng/mL) [47]. In the other, measured in plasma, the lowest prestin levels were observed in participants with moderate noise exposure (560 ± 25 ng/mL), while the highest values were found in individuals with NIHL or self-reported hearing loss (728 ± 129 ng/mL) [48]. The fourth study compared individuals with low-risk and high-risk noise exposure [25]. Although the high-risk group showed only slightly elevated PTA thresholds, prestin levels were significantly lower compared to the low-risk group (0.110 ng/mL vs. 0.260 ng/mL, p = 0.042).

Two studies investigated prestin levels in patients with presbycusis or vertigo. In one study including 19 patients with Meniere’s disease (2.33 ng/mL) and 11 patients with vestibular migraine (0.64 ng/mL), no significant differences in prestin levels were found between the two diagnostic groups [38]. Another study, including 30 patients aged 20–55 years with SNHL and 30 patients ≥ 55 years with presbycusis, did not show distinct prestin patterns related to the presence of presbycusis (presbycusis group: 0.07954 ng/mL, SNHL group: 0.12364 ng/mL) [36]. Long-term studies, regardless of recovery status, have not yet been conducted.

Using an adapted QUIPS-based appraisal for clinical studies, the overall methodological quality was judged to be moderate (see Supplementary Table 2 for domain-level ratings). Most studies clearly defined their study populations and applied standardized audiometric outcome measures. However, small sample sizes were common, and the majority of studies employed exploratory or case-control designs without prospective validation cohorts. Adjustment for confounders such as age, cumulative noise exposure, or comorbidities was inconsistent. In addition, heterogeneity in sampling intervals and analytical methodologies limited the robustness of reported prestin measurements. Collectively, these methodological constraints reduce comparability across studies and restrict conclusions regarding reproducibility and clinical applicability.

In summary, clinical studies show that prestin levels tend to increase shortly after acute cochlear damage, while findings in chronic or long-term hearing loss are more variable. Dynamic changes have been observed in surgical settings, and external factors such as lead exposure have been shown to affect prestin levels. However, given the heterogeneity in study design, sample size, and analytical methodology, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

This review provides an updated overview of current evidence on the potential of prestin as a serum/plasma biomarker for cochlear damage [49]. Elevated prestin levels were frequently observed shortly after acute auditory injury, while findings in chronic or more long-term hearing loss were more variable. Dynamic changes were also reported in surgical contexts, and external factors such as lead exposure were shown to affect prestin levels. Together, these findings suggest a physiological response to cochlear stress, though variation exists across study designs and populations. These observations prompt further reflection on the potential clinical relevance, interpretative challenges, and future applicability of prestin as a biomarker for cochlear damage. In addition to prestin, other circulating biomarkers such as thiol-disulfide balance and stereocilin have been proposed in the context of cochlear injury and ototoxicity [50, 51]. However, these markers have been far less extensively investigated and currently lack sufficient clinical validation, limiting their applicability at present.

The observed increase in prestin levels shortly after cochlear injury indicates that prestin release in the bloodstream reflects cochlear cell damage. In both animal models and acute clinical settings, such as after cisplatin administration or noise exposure, prestin levels rise within hours to days [33, 34, 37, 42]. This suggests that prestin could serve as an early indicator of OHC stress, prior to irreversible structural damage. In line with this, the temporal relationship between prestin levels and functional hearing assessments following cochlear injury suggests that prestin may hold potential as a dynamic blood-based biomarker for early detection and monitoring of auditory damage. Prestin may therefore reflect early cochlear stress, although its role in complementing or preceding audiometric testing in clinical settings remains hypothetical. The potential use of prestin to guide the initiation of otoprotective strategies remains unclear, as no studies to date have prospectively evaluated prestin-guided interventions.

While acute increases in prestin levels are consistently reported [37, 40, 44], results in chronic hearing loss or after prolonged damaging exposure are more heterogeneous [35, 43, 48]. In two studies, prestin levels were even reduced, which may reflect different stages of cochlear pathology, ranging from acute cellular stress to potential OHC loss [31, 34, 37]. These findings suggest that the effect of cochlear injury on prestin levels may depend on the underlying etiology, with more consistent elevations observed in acute conditions such as sudden SNHL or ototoxic exposure, and more variable findings in age-related or chronic NIHL [35, 43, 48]. Collectively, this indicates that prestin may be most informative during early, dynamic phases of cochlear injury, whereas its diagnostic value may be limited in advanced or degenerative stages. However, due to inconsistencies in measurement intervals and the limited availability of longitudinal human data, it remains unclear how these changes relate to the progression or resolution of auditory damage. Consequently, interpretation of prestin levels may require context-specific reference ranges and cut-off values that take disease etiology and timing into account. Further studies with standardized protocols and repeated longitudinal sampling are necessary to clarify the temporal course of prestin secretion into the bloodstream in relation to specific types and phases of cochlear injury.

Several studies reported associations between prestin levels and audiometric outcomes, but findings were not uniform. In most studies, prestin mirrored changes in hearing thresholds [37, 40, 44], whereas in others, no correlation was found [34, 45]. A possible explanation is that prestin reflects subclinical cochlear stress that is not yet detectable by audiometry. Determining whether prestin can function as an early surrogate marker, a longitudinal monitoring tool, a predictor of treatment response or late cochlear damage, will require studies that systematically integrate biological, functional, and temporal outcome measures.

As reflected in the methodological appraisal, interpretation of existing prestin data is complicated by several methodological limitations that constrain reproducibility, comparability, and clinical translation. The human studies to date are generally characterized by small sample sizes and a predominance of case-control designs rather than adequately powered prospective cohorts. These features increase susceptibility to selection bias, thereby reducing confidence in the generalizability of reported findings. In addition, substantial heterogeneity exists in the timing of prestin measurement relative to the auditory damage, ranging from hours to weeks after exposure. As prestin levels vary over time after cochlear injury, heterogeneity in sampling intervals hinders direct comparison between studies and limits insight into temporal patterns.

An additional unresolved question concerns the intra-individual stability of baseline prestin levels over time. While several studies report considerable inter-individual variability in control populations, it remains unclear whether prestin levels are stable within the same individual in the absence of cochlear injury or ongoing exposure. This has important implications for interpretation, as it is currently unknown whether a single measurement reflects a true baseline value or is subject to physiological fluctuation and analytical variability [52]. Longitudinal studies in healthy individuals are therefore needed to determine the temporal variability of prestin levels and to inform the development of reliable reference ranges.

Further methodological heterogeneity arises from differences in biological matrices and assay platforms. Prestin levels were measured in either serum or plasma, which represent distinct biological matrices: serum is obtained after blood coagulation, whereas plasma is collected in the presence of anticoagulants and retains clotting factors [53]. These differences may influence measured prestin levels, for example, through protein binding, degradation, or matrix-specific interference, although direct comparative data are currently lacking [54]. Prestin levels were also reported using different units in the original clinical studies, with values predominantly expressed in pg/mL (n = 11) and less frequently in ng/mL (n = 4), requiring conversion by a factor of 1,000 to enable comparison. Although this difference is primarily technical, it increases the risk of misinterpretation and complicates comparison across studies. Moreover, assay sensitivity, specificity, and detection limits varied across ELISA platforms. Importantly, analytical performance characteristics were rarely independently validated and were predominantly based on manufacturer-provided data. As a result, uncertainty remains regarding the robustness of these measurements, which may partly explain the variability in findings across studies [55]. Together, these factors preclude direct comparison of absolute prestin values across studies and currently prevent the establishment of reliable reference ranges of cut-off values.

Taken together, the current body of evidence supports the biological plausibility of prestin as a circulating marker of cochlear stress, particularly in the context of acute auditory damage. At the same time, substantial methodological heterogeneity, limited sample sizes, and the predominance of exploratory study designs currently restrict the interpretability and broader applicability of prestin measurements in human studies. Addressing these challenges will require larger, prospectively designed studies employing harmonized sampling strategies, validated assay platforms, and clearly defined analytical and clinical endpoints. Such efforts are necessary to clarify the conditions under which prestin may provide meaningful biological or translational information, while recognizing that its role remains exploratory at present and confined to research settings.

Within this context, an important and largely unaddressed gap concerns pediatric populations. Despite the high clinical relevance of ototoxic hearing loss in children undergoing cancer treatment, prestin has not yet been evaluated in pediatric clinical studies. Given the limitations of behavioral audiometry in infants and toddlers [56–59], pediatric ototoxicity monitoring represents a priority area for future research, in which objective biomarkers such as prestin may hold particular promise if appropriately validated.
