Stopping Rules for Stochastic Gradient Descent via Anytime-Valid Confidence Sequences
Liviu Aolaritei †, Michael I. Jordan †‡

Abstract
We study stopping rules for stochastic gradient descent (SGD) for convex optimization from the perspective of anytime-valid confidence sequences. Classical analyses of SGD provide convergence guarantees in expectation or at a fixed horizon, but offer no statistically valid way to assess, at an arbitrary time, how close the current iterate is to the optimum. We develop an anytime-valid, data-dependent upper confidence sequence for the weighted average suboptimality of projected SGD, constructed via nonnegative supermartingales and requiring no smoothness or strong convexity. This confidence sequence yields a simple stopping rule that is provably ε \varepsilon -optimal with probability at least 1 − α 1-\alpha , with explicit bounds on the stopping time under standard stochastic approximation stepsizes. To the best of our knowledge, these are the first rigorous, time-uniform performance guarantees and finite-time ε \varepsilon -optimality certificates for projected SGD with general convex objectives, based solely on observable trajectory quantities.



1
Introduction

Stochastic gradient methods are the workhorses of modern optimization and machine learning. Their appeal lies in a rare combination of simplicity, scalability, and robustness to noisy information. Yet, despite decades of research, a persistent gap remains between theory and practice: during an optimization run, practitioners have no data-dependent, statistically valid indication of how close the current iterate is to optimality.

Classical analyses provide valuable insights into the behavior of stochastic gradient descent (SGD), including convergence in expectation, asymptotic rates, and fixed-horizon high-probability bounds. However, these guarantees typically rely on problem-dependent constants, such as smoothness parameters, strong convexity coefficients, and variance bounds, which are unknown in practice, and they offer no protection under adaptive monitoring or early stopping. Thus, when training a model or tuning a hyperparameter, a user is left with the fundamental but unanswered question:

How close am I to the optimum right now?

In practical settings, such as large-scale training, reinforcement learning, scientific computing, or any computation where time or data is limited, this question is more than academic. Stopping too early risks under-optimization, while stopping too late wastes considerable resources. Yet most stopping rules used in practice are heuristic: they monitor validation curves, impose fixed budgets, or rely on experience rather than statistical guarantees.

In this paper, we revisit this classical challenge from a sequential-inference perspective. The key idea is to view the progress of SGD as a stochastic process that can be monitored through anytime-valid confidence sequences (CSs). A confidence sequence is a sequence of intervals that remains valid uniformly over time, even under continuous monitoring and adaptive stopping. By adapting this framework to stochastic optimization, we obtain fully
data-dependent, time-uniform upper bounds on the suboptimality gap
, requiring neither knowledge of the optimization horizon nor precise knowledge of problem-dependent parameters. These guarantees immediately yield
statistically valid, data-dependent stopping rules
that certify
\varepsilon
-optimality under arbitrary adaptive monitoring.

1.1
Problem formulation

To formalize the problem, we consider optimization over a closed and convex feasible region. Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a nonempty compact convex set, and let
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex objective function. We denote an arbitrary minimizer of
f
over
\mathcal{X}
by

x^{\star}\in\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x).

We study the classical projected variant of stochastic gradient descent, defined recursively by

x_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t}-\eta_{t}g_{t}),

(1)

where
\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}
denotes Euclidean projection onto
\mathcal{X}
and
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
is a predictable stepsize sequence. Formally, we define the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma(x_{1},g_{1},\ldots,x_{t},g_{t})
, for
t\geq 1
, and assume that
\eta_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
measurable. Because
x_{1}\in\mathcal{X}
and each update projects back onto
\mathcal{X}
, we have
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
for all
t\geq 1
. In particular, all iterates lie in a bounded region around
x^{\star}
:

\|x_{t}-x^{\star}\|\;\leq\;R_{x}:=\textrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})<\infty.

The stochastic gradients
g_{t}
are assumed to satisfy the following three conditions.

Assumption 1.1

(Stochastic gradients)
.

For all
t\geq 1
, the stochastic gradient
g_{t}
satisfies:

(i)

Unbiasedness:

\mathbb{E}[g_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\nabla f(x_{t})
, and

(ii)

Conditional second moment bound:

\mathbb{E}[\|g_{t}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq G^{2}
almost surely.

(iii)

Conditional sub-Gaussian noise:
there exists a constant
\sigma^{2}>0
such that for
\xi_{t}\coloneqq g_{t}-\nabla f(x_{t})
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\big(\lambda\langle u,\xi_{t}\rangle\big)\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\leq\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}\|u\|^{2}}{2}\right)\qquad\forall u\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ \forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

(2)

Assumption
1.1
(i) is the standard unbiasedness condition used in stochastic approximation. Assumption
1.1
(ii) requires a uniform bound on the conditional second moment of the stochastic gradients, which is classical in nonasymptotic analyses of SGD and ensures that the quadratic term
\|g_{t}\|^{2}
remains controlled. Finally, Assumption
1.1
(iii) postulates that the gradient noise
\xi_{t}\coloneqq g_{t}-\nabla f(x_{t})
is conditionally sub-Gaussian given the past. This condition provides a quantitative tail bound on the one-step fluctuations of the stochastic gradients and is natural in time-uniform concentration arguments. Moreover, it is satisfied by a broad class of noise models, including bounded, Gaussian, and many light-tailed stochastic gradients frequently encountered in practice. We operate in the standard stochastic approximation regime:

Assumption 1.2

(Stepsizes)
.

The predictable stepsizes
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
satisfy

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{t}=\infty,\qquad\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{t}^{2}<\infty,\qquad 0<\eta_{t}<\infty.

(3)

These conditions ensure that the algorithm continues making progress in early iterations while the cumulative noise remains controlled. It is convenient to introduce two standard cumulative quantities from stochastic approximation,

S_{t}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s},\qquad V_{t}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}^{2},

which will appear naturally in our analysis. To measure optimization progress, we focus on the weighted average suboptimality,

\bar{F}_{t}:=\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}\big(f(x_{s})-f(x^{\star})\big)}{S_{t}}.

(4)

This statistic is classical in stochastic approximation theory and has a direct optimization interpretation. Indeed, defining the corresponding weighted average iterate

\bar{x}_{t}:=\frac{1}{S_{t}}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}x_{s},

(5)

convexity of
f
implies

f(\bar{x}_{t})-f(x^{\star})\;\leq\;\bar{F}_{t}.

Thus
\bar{F}_{t}
simultaneously controls the performance of the averaged iterate
\bar{x}_{t}
and serves as the natural target for our anytime-valid analysis.

1.2
Contributions

Our goal is to provide
anytime-valid, data-dependent guarantees
for the progress of projected stochastic gradient descent. Specifically, given the stochastic gradients
\{g_{t}\}
, the predictable stepsizes
\{\eta_{t}\}
, and the observed iterates
\{x_{t}\}
, we construct an upper confidence sequence for the weighted average suboptimality
\bar{F}_{t}
in (
4
), which holds uniformly over all times
t\geq 1
. This confidence sequence depends on trajectory-dependent quantities: the stochastic gradient norms, the stepsizes, and the squared-distance process
\|x_{t}-x^{\star}\|^{2}
. While the latter is not directly observable, it is uniformly bounded by
R_{x}^{2}
due to compactness of
\mathcal{X}
, which immediately yields a fully implementable bound depending only on the realized gradient norms, the stepsizes, and
R_{x}
. To state our contributions precisely, we recall the formal notion of an anytime-valid upper confidence sequence.

Definition 1.3

(Anytime-valid upper confidence sequence)
.

Let
\{\bar{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
be as in (
4
). A sequence of random variables
\{U_{t}(\alpha)\}_{t\geq 1}
is an
anytime-valid upper confidence sequence
for
\{\bar{F}_{t}\}
if

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\forall t\geq 1:\ \bar{F}_{t}\leq U_{t}(\alpha)\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

(6)

These uniform-in-time bounds enable a natural data-dependent stopping rule. Given a target accuracy
\varepsilon>0
and confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we define the stopping time

\tau_{\varepsilon}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 1:U_{t}(\alpha)\leq\varepsilon\right\},

(7)

which returns the first iterate that is
certified
to be
\varepsilon
-optimal.

Main contributions.

Our main contributions can be summarized as follows.

(i)

Anytime-valid confidence sequence.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,\tfrac{2}{\mathrm{e}})
. Theorem
2.1
provides an upper confidence sequence
\{U_{t}(\alpha)\}_{t\geq 1}
for the weighted average suboptimality
\bar{F}_{t}
satisfying

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\forall t\geq 1:\ \bar{F}_{t}\leq U_{t}(\alpha)\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

The bound
U_{t}(\alpha)
is
trajectory-adaptive
and remains valid uniformly over time, including under adaptive monitoring and data-dependent choices of stepsizes. Moreover, a fully observable version of the confidence sequence, suitable for online implementation, is given in Corollary
2.4
. Finally, under standard stochastic approximation stepsizes,
U_{t}(\alpha)
decays at the rate
1/S_{t}
almost surely; see Proposition
2.2
and Remark
2.3
.

(ii)

Anytime-valid
\varepsilon
-optimal stopping rule.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
\alpha\in(0,\tfrac{2}{\mathrm{e}})
. Theorem
3.1
proves that the stopping time
\tau_{\varepsilon}
defined in (
7
) is almost surely finite, and the returned iterate
\bar{x}_{\tau_{\varepsilon}}
satisfies

\mathbb{P}\!\left(f(\bar{x}_{\tau_{\varepsilon}})-f(x^{\star})\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

Thus projected SGD, when equipped with the confidence sequence from (i), becomes an anytime-valid optimization procedure with a rigorous finite-time certificate of
\varepsilon
-optimality. Explicit bounds on the stopping-time complexity for standard stepsize schedules are given in Propositions
3.4
and
3.5
.

1.3
Related Work

Conceptually, our work lies at the intersection of two developments:
(i)
time-uniform inference, which provides a framework for hypothesis tests and confidence intervals that yields valid control over errors uniformly in time, and
(ii)
stochastic approximation, which delivers nonasymptotic convergence guarantees for stochastic gradient methods in convex optimization. The present paper combines these perspectives by adapting nonnegative supermartingale techniques from time-uniform inference to the projected SGD setting. This yields a fully data-dependent, anytime-valid upper confidence sequence for suboptimality, together with a stopping rule that is provably
\varepsilon
-optimal with high probability and almost surely finite under standard stochastic approximation conditions. In what follows, we position our contributions within the broader literature on stochastic approximation, averaging and weighted iterates, optimization stopping criteria, time-uniform concentration, and confidence-sequence methodology.

Stochastic approximation and asymptotic normality.

Stochastic approximation provides the theoretical foundation for stochastic gradient methods. The seminal paper
[
26
]
established almost sure convergence of iterative schemes under diminishing stepsizes, while
[
17
]
developed a comprehensive asymptotic theory based on ordinary differential equations, covering stability, asymptotic normality, and efficiency. A key advance showed that appropriately averaged iterates achieve asymptotically optimal covariance, providing a principled justification for studying
weighted averages
(which we adopt in this paper) of SGD trajectories
[
22
]
. This asymptotic perspective was sharpened in
[
9
]
, which characterized minimax and asymptotically efficient rates for stochastic optimization, including constrained formulations, which was further extended to nonsmooth stochastic approximation in
[
5
]
. Together, these results motivate the use of averaged iterates and enable asymptotic confidence regions for optimal solutions based on limiting normality. However, such guarantees are inherently asymptotic: they do not yield finite-time confidence sets, nor do they provide time-uniform validity under continuous monitoring or adaptive stopping.

Fixed-horizon high-probability guarantees for SGD.

A vast literature develops
nonasymptotic
convergence guarantees for stochastic gradient (and mirror-descent-type) methods that hold with high probability at a
pre-specified
iteration horizon. Early robust frameworks for stochastic convex optimization established high-probability bounds under bounded or sub-Gaussian noise assumptions, providing optimal oracle complexity guarantees for stochastic approximation and stochastic programming
[
21
,
19
]
. Related work also studied statistical validation of mirror-descent–type stochastic approximation methods, providing confidence bounds for the optimal objective value at a fixed iteration horizon rather than under adaptive or continuously monitored stopping rules
[
18
]
. Parallel developments in machine learning emphasized refined nonasymptotic analyses of stochastic approximation algorithms, including explicit bias–variance tradeoffs and finite-time rates for averaged iterates
[
20
,
2
,
8
]
. Subsequent work sharpened fixed-horizon high-probability bounds across a range of regimes, including nonsmooth and strongly convex objectives, and clarified when optimal rates can be achieved by averaging versus last-iterate solutions
[
23
,
28
,
12
,
13
,
16
]
. Complementary information-theoretic lower bounds characterize the fundamental limits of stochastic convex optimization at fixed confidence levels
[
1
]
. More recent contributions extend fixed-horizon high-probability guarantees beyond light-tailed noise models, including heavy-tailed settings handled via gradient clipping and related robustification techniques
[
11
,
3
]
, as well as general boosting and robust-estimation wrappers that convert low-probability guarantees into high-confidence ones
[
6
]
. Together, these works provide sharp finite-time guarantees at prescribed horizons and confidence levels. However, these guarantees are intrinsically
fixed-time
: they do not yield confidence sets or optimality certificates that remain valid uniformly over time under continuous monitoring or adaptive stopping without additional union bounds or restarts, motivating the time-uniform approach pursued in this paper. Viewed differently, the present work does not seek to refine convergence rates for SGD, but rather to introduce a complementary, time-uniform notion of statistical validity for stochastic optimization algorithms, in which stopping times are treated as random, data-dependent objects subject to formal inference.

Time-uniform inference and confidence sequences.

Time-uniform (also called anytime-valid) inference provides uncertainty bounds that remain valid simultaneously over all times and are therefore robust to continuous monitoring and optional stopping. A foundational tool is Ville’s inequality for nonnegative supermartingales
[
29
]
, which converts supermartingale constructions into time-uniform deviation guarantees. Early statistical instances of this idea include classical confidence sequences for basic parameters
[
4
]
, as well as boundary-crossing results for martingales
[
27
]
. Modern developments systematize these principles through nonnegative supermartingale constructions and, in particular, mixture and stitching arguments, yielding sharp time-uniform concentration inequalities that adapt to variance-type processes
[
15
]
; see also related time-uniform Chernoff bounds based on exponential supermartingales
[
14
]
and the broader literature on self-normalized processes
[
7
]
. More recently, these ideas have been reframed through e-values and game-theoretic statistics, providing a unified perspective on optional-stopping-safe inference and a comprehensive entry point to the literature
[
24
,
25
]
. While this framework has been developed primarily for sequential testing and estimation, it has begun to influence learning and decision-making problems, including anytime-valid inference for bandits and betting-based estimation
[
31
,
30
]
. Our work leverages this time-uniform machinery in an optimization context, using supermartingale mixtures to construct suboptimality certificates that remain valid under arbitrary, data-dependent stopping.

Stopping rules and certificates in optimization.

Stopping criteria play a central practical role in stochastic optimization, yet they remain poorly understood from a statistical perspective. In practice, stochastic gradient methods are typically terminated using heuristic rules, such as monitoring validation loss, gradient norms, iterate differences, or pre-assigned computational budgets. While these rules are often effective empirically, they lack formal guarantees: they do not provide statistically valid certificates of
\varepsilon
-optimality, and their use under adaptive monitoring is generally incompatible with fixed-horizon analyses. Classical convergence results for SGD (whether asymptotic or nonasymptotic) are typically stated either in expectation or at a predetermined iteration horizon, and therefore do not justify early stopping based on observed trajectories. As a result, there is a gap between the widespread use of early stopping in optimization practice and the lack of rigorous, time-uniform performance certificates. The confidence-sequence framework developed in this paper directly addresses this gap by providing a fully data-dependent stopping rule with provable
\varepsilon
-optimality guarantees that remain valid under arbitrary, adaptive monitoring.

Closest related work.

The works most closely related to ours study anytime or time-uniform guarantees for stochastic approximation, but differ substantially in assumptions and in the form of the resulting guarantees. Classical results such as
[
23
]
establish high-probability convergence bounds for SGD under strong convexity and bounded stochastic gradients, with guarantees stated uniformly over iterations up to a pre-specified horizon. As such, they do not support data-dependent stopping times. More recent work
[
10
]
derives anytime high-probability bounds for stochastic gradient methods with momentum under convexity and smoothness assumptions, using algorithm-specific dynamics and stepsize choices motivated by continuous-time ODE analysis. While these results control the optimization error uniformly over time, they do not provide explicit constants or trajectory-observable confidence
sequences, and therefore cannot be used to derive certified stopping rules. Finally,
[
32
]
studies one-dimensional stochastic root finding and proposes a bisection-based procedure that yields time-uniform confidence
intervals for the unknown root, but their guarantees concern inference on the unknown root rather than certificates of optimization suboptimality for projected SGD. In contrast to these works, our results apply to plain projected SGD under general convexity, yield fully explicit and trajectory-dependent confidence sequences for suboptimality, and lead to provably correct stopping rules that remain valid under arbitrary adaptive monitoring.

2
Anytime-Valid Confidence Sequences

In this section we derive an adaptive, anytime-valid confidence sequence for the weighted average suboptimality of projected SGD. Our goal is to construct, for each confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, an upper confidence sequence
\{U_{t}(\alpha)\}
in the sense of Definition
1.3
such that
\{\forall t\geq 1:\ \bar{F}_{t}\leq U_{t}(\alpha)\}
holds with probability
1-\alpha
, where
\bar{F}_{t}
is the weighted suboptimality defined in (
4
). A central feature of our construction is
adaptivity
: the bound adjusts to the realized optimization trajectory through the distances
\|x_{t}-x^{\star}\|
and the squared stochastic gradient norms
\|g_{t}\|^{2}
, rather than relying solely on global parameters such as
R_{x}
and
G
. A simple corollary will later convert this adaptive bound into a fully observable version involving only
R_{x}
, the realized quantities
\|g_{t}\|^{2}
, and the stepsizes.

To state the main result, we introduce the squared distance process

Z_{t}:=\|x_{t}-x^{\star}\|^{2},

and a trajectory-dependent quantity that enters the width of the confidence sequence: for each
t\geq 1
, define

\sigma_{t}^{2}:=\sigma^{2}\eta_{t}^{2}Z_{t},\qquad\Sigma_{t}^{2}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sigma_{s}^{2},\qquad\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}:=\max\!\left\{\Sigma_{t}^{2},\;2\Big(\log\tfrac{2}{\alpha}+1\Big)\right\}.

(8)

where
\sigma^{2}>0
is the sub-Gaussian variance proxy appearing in Assumption
1.1
(iii). The quantity
\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
will be referred to as the
effective cumulative variance proxy
. It coincides with the true cumulative variance proxy
\Sigma_{t}^{2}
once the latter exceeds a fixed threshold, and serves to prevent small-variance degeneracies in the time-uniform concentration argument. Larger values of
\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
correspond to trajectories with larger stepsizes or larger distances to the optimum, and therefore lead to wider confidence sequences. Although
Z_{t}
is not directly observable, it is always bounded by
R_{x}^{2}
, and keeping it explicit highlights which components of the trajectory influence the final bound. A fully observable version of the confidence sequence is provided in Corollary
2.4
by replacing
Z_{t}
with
R_{x}^{2}
.

Our confidence sequence will be stated in terms of the following data-dependent upper boundary:

U_{t}(\alpha):=\frac{1}{2S_{t}}\Bigg(C\,\sqrt{\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\Big(\log\tfrac{2}{\alpha}+\log\log(\mathrm{e}+\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2})\Big)}+Z_{1}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}^{2}\|g_{s}\|^{2}\Bigg),

(9)

where
C=C(\alpha)>0
is an explicit constant, provided below. This boundary combines three sources of variability: the effective cumulative variance proxy
\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
, the initial distance
Z_{1}
, and the accumulated squared gradient steps
\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}^{2}\|g_{s}\|^{2}
. The leading term involving
\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
captures the stochastic fluctuations of the process and stems from a time-uniform concentration inequality applied to an adapted sequence satisfying conditional sub-Gaussian tail bounds (see the proof of Theorem
2.1
). In contrast, the terms
Z_{1}
and
\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}^{2}\|g_{s}\|^{2}
come directly from the deterministic structure of projected SGD, and appear when the distance recursion is telescoped. Before proceeding, we specify the constant
C
appearing in
U_{t}(\alpha)
:

C\;:=\;6\sqrt{\max\!\left\{1,\;\frac{\log\Big(\log\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}+1\Big)}{\log\Big(\log(\mathrm{e}+1)\Big)}\right\}},

(10)

with
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
defined as follows

V_{\infty}\coloneqq\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\eta_{s}^{2},\qquad\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty}^{2}:=\sigma^{2}R_{x}^{2}V_{\infty},\qquad\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}:=\max\!\left\{\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty}^{2},\;2\Big(\log\tfrac{2}{\alpha}+1\Big)\right\}.

(11)

Since
V_{\infty}<\infty
, it follows that
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}<\infty
, ensuring that the constant
C
is finite. With the definition of
C
, the confidence sequence
U_{t}(\alpha)
in (
9
) is now fully specified. The next theorem establishes that
U_{t}(\alpha)
forms an anytime-valid upper confidence sequence for the weighted suboptimality process
\{\bar{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
.

Theorem 2.1

(Adaptive anytime-valid suboptimality bound)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1.1
and
1.2
hold. Then, for every
\alpha\in(0,\tfrac{2}{\mathrm{e}})
, the process
\{U_{t}(\alpha)\}_{t\geq 1}
defined in (
9
), with constant
C
defined in (
10
), satisfies

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\forall t\geq 1:\ \bar{F}_{t}\leq U_{t}(\alpha)\right)\;\geq\;1-\alpha,

where
\bar{F}_{t}
is the weighted suboptimality in (
4
). In particular,
\{U_{t}(\alpha)\}_{t\geq 1}
is an anytime-valid upper confidence sequence for
\{\bar{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
in the sense of Definition
1.3
.

A noteworthy feature of the bound in Theorem
2.1
is its rate-controlled decay. Under the stochastic approximation stepsize conditions in Assumption
1.2
, we have
S_{t}\to\infty
and
\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
uniformly bounded, since the trajectory remains in a compact set and
\sigma_{t}^{2}=\sigma^{2}\eta_{t}^{2}Z_{t}
is summable. Moreover, by Lemma
A.1
, the partial sums
\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}^{2}\|g_{s}\|^{2}
converge almost surely to a
finite
random limit. Consequently, we have the following result.

Proposition 2.2

(Rate-controlled almost-sure decay of the confidence sequence)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
2.1
, for any fixed

\alpha\in(0,\tfrac{2}{\mathrm{e}})
,

U_{t}(\alpha)=O\!\left(\frac{1}{S_{t}}\right)\quad\text{almost surely},

and in particular
U_{t}(\alpha)\to 0
almost surely as
t\to\infty
.

This proposition ensures that projected SGD, equipped with our anytime-valid bound, provably converges to the optimum with a data-dependent, high-probability certificate of progress.

Remark 2.3

(Optimality of the
1/S_{t}
rate)
.

The
1/S_{t}
rate in Theorem
2.1
cannot, in general, be improved, even in a very simple one-dimensional deterministic setting. Fix any stepsizes
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
with
\eta_{t}>0
,
\sum_{t}\eta_{t}=\infty
, and
\sum_{t}\eta_{t}^{2}<\infty
, and consider the quadratic model

f(x):=\frac{\mu}{2}x^{2},\qquad\mu\in(0,1),

with minimizer
x^{\star}=0
,
f^{\star}=0
, and deterministic oracle
g_{t}=\nabla f(x_{t})=\mu x_{t}
. Starting from
x_{1}=x_{0}\neq 0
, the projected SGD recursion reduces to the deterministic update
x_{t+1}=x_{t}-\eta_{t}g_{t}
, and the weighted cumulative suboptimality

A_{t}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}\Big(f(x_{s})-f^{\star}\Big)

is nondecreasing with

A_{t}\;\geq\;A_{1}=\eta_{1}\frac{\mu}{2}x_{1}^{2}\;>\;0\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 1.

For any anytime-valid upper confidence sequence
\{U_{t}(\alpha)\}_{t\geq 1}
over a model class containing this example, validity on this deterministic path forces
A_{t}\leq S_{t}U_{t}(\alpha)
for all
t
, and hence

S_{t}U_{t}(\alpha)\;\geq\;A_{1}\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 1.

Thus, even in this benign one-dimensional model, no anytime-valid confidence sequence can shrink uniformly faster than order
1/S_{t}
. This example illustrates that the
1/S_{t}
rate in our upper bound is essentially optimal from a time-uniform perspective.
\clubsuit

Before turning to the proof, we record an immediate consequence of Theorem
2.1
. The theorem itself produces an adaptive confidence sequence that depends on the (unobservable) distances
\|x_{t}-x^{\star}\|
and on the realized stochastic gradients. In practice, one often wishes to work with fully observable bounds, expressed only in terms of the known constraint radius
R_{x}
, the realized squared gradient norms
\|g_{t}\|^{2}
, and the predictable stepsizes
\{\eta_{t}\}
. The following corollary provides such a variant.

Corollary 2.4

(Observable anytime-valid suboptimality bound)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
2.1
, suppose in addition that
\mathcal{X}
is known to satisfy
\|x-x^{\star}\|\leq R_{x}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Define

\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}:=\sigma^{2}\eta_{t}^{2}R_{x}^{2},\qquad\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{2}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\widetilde{\sigma}_{s}^{2}=\sigma^{2}R_{x}^{2}V_{t},\qquad\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}:=\max\!\left\{\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{2},\;2\Big(\log\tfrac{2}{\alpha}+1\Big)\right\}.

Then for every
\alpha\in(0,\tfrac{2}{\mathrm{e}})
the process

U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha):=\frac{1}{2S_{t}}\Bigg(C\sqrt{\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\!\left(\log\tfrac{2}{\alpha}+\log\log(\mathrm{e}+\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2})\right)}+R_{x}^{2}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}^{2}\|g_{s}\|^{2}\Bigg)

is an anytime-valid upper confidence sequence for
\{\bar{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
.

Note that
U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable and can be computed online at each iteration using only observable information.

2.1
Proof of Theorem
2.1

In this subsection, we unpack the proof of Theorem
2.1
through a sequence of conceptual steps. Rather than presenting all technical details at once, we isolate the key ideas that transform the projected SGD recursion into an anytime-valid confidence sequence. We adopt this layered presentation, along with detailed explanations, deliberately for its didactic value. We first construct a process whose one-step evolution compares the actual suboptimality to what is explained by the geometry of projected SGD and the stochastic gradients, and show that this process enjoys a trajectory-dependent sub-Gaussian control. We then present a time-uniform concentration lemma for martingale sums, after which a simple telescoping argument and normalization by
S_{t}
yield the desired bound on
\bar{F}_{t}
.

Step 1: Constructing a sub-Gaussian process

We begin by identifying a natural potential function that tracks the progress of projected SGD toward the minimizer. A standard choice in convex optimization is the squared distance to the optimum,
Z_{t}=\|x_{t}-x^{\star}\|^{2}
. Because the update
x_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t}-\eta_{t}g_{t})
is a Euclidean projection onto a closed convex set, the evolution of
Z_{t}
can be controlled using the non-expansiveness of projection:

Z_{t+1}\;=\;\|x_{t+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\;\leq\;\|x_{t}-\eta_{t}g_{t}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\;=\;Z_{t}-2\eta_{t}\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},g_{t}\rangle+\eta_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}.

(12)

Rearranging this gives the pathwise lower bound
Z_{t}-Z_{t+1}\geq 2\eta_{t}\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},g_{t}\rangle-\eta_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}
. To connect this distance recursion to the suboptimality we care about, we package the suboptimality, the distance decrement, and the squared gradient step into a single quantity

X_{t}\;:=\;2\eta_{t}\big(f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star})\big)-(Z_{t}-Z_{t+1})-\eta_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2},

(13)

with
X_{0}\coloneqq 0
. The next lemma shows that
X_{t}
has two crucial properties: it has nonpositive conditional drift, and its conditional moment generating function is dominated by that of a sub-Gaussian variable with variance proxy
\sigma_{t}^{2}=\sigma^{2}\eta_{t}^{2}Z_{t}
, as defined in (
8
). This is where convexity of
f
, the unbiasedness assumption, and the conditional sub-Gaussian noise assumption enter.

Lemma 2.5

(Conditional sub-Gaussian control)
.

Let
X_{t}
be defined as in (
13
). Under Assumption
1.1
, the following hold for all
t\geq 1
:

(i)

Drift negativity:

\mathbb{E}[X_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\;\leq\;0.

(14)

(ii)

Conditional sub-Gaussian bound:
for all
\lambda\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\big(\lambda X_{t}\big)\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\;\leq\;\exp\!\big(2\lambda^{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}\big).

(15)

Proof.

We first derive a convenient upper bound on
X_{t}
. From (
12
) and the definition (
13
),

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=2\eta_{t}\big(f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star})\big)-(Z_{t}-Z_{t+1})-\eta_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\eta_{t}\big(f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star})\big)-\big(2\eta_{t}\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},g_{t}\rangle-\eta_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}\big)-\eta_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\eta_{t}\Big(f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star})-\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},g_{t}\rangle\Big).

(16)

Recalling that
\xi_{t}:=g_{t}-\nabla f(x_{t})
, we can further decompose
\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},g_{t}\rangle=\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},\nabla f(x_{t})\rangle+\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},\xi_{t}\rangle
. Substituting this into (
16
) yields

X_{t}\leq 2\eta_{t}\Big(f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star})-\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},\nabla f(x_{t})\rangle\Big)-2\eta_{t}\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},\xi_{t}\rangle.

(17)

We now prove assertion (i). By convexity of
f
,

f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star})\leq\langle\nabla f(x_{t}),x_{t}-x^{\star}\rangle,

so the first term in (
17
) is nonpositive:
f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star})-\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},\nabla f(x_{t})\rangle\leq 0
. Taking conditional expectations in (
17
) and using the unbiasedness in Assumption
1.1
(i), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\eta_{t}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star})-\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},\nabla f(x_{t})\rangle\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]-2\eta_{t}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},\xi_{t}\rangle\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=2\eta_{t}\Big(f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star})-\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},\nabla f(x_{t})\rangle\Big)-2\eta_{t}\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},\mathbb{E}[\xi_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\rangle

\displaystyle=2\eta_{t}\Big(f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star})-\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},\nabla f(x_{t})\rangle\Big)\;\leq\;0,

which proves (
14
).

We now prove assertion (ii). From (
17
) and the convexity inequality just used, we also have the pathwise bound

X_{t}\;\leq\;-2\eta_{t}\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},\xi_{t}\rangle.

(18)

Fix
\lambda\geq 0
. Multiplying (
18
) by
\lambda
and taking conditional expectations,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\big(\lambda X_{t}\big)\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\big(-2\lambda\eta_{t}\langle x_{t}-x^{\star},\xi_{t}\rangle\big)\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right].

Applying Assumption
1.1
(iii) with

u:=-2\eta_{t}(x_{t}-x^{\star})\ \implies\ \|u\|^{2}=4\eta_{t}^{2}\|x_{t}-x^{\star}\|^{2}=4\eta_{t}^{2}Z_{t},

we obtain

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\big(\lambda X_{t}\big)\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\leq\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}\|u\|^{2}}{2}\right)=\exp\!\big(2\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}\eta_{t}^{2}Z_{t}\big)=\exp\!\big(2\lambda^{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}\big),

which is exactly (
15
).
∎

The relevance of the process
X_{t}
emerges once we consider its partial sums

\bar{X}_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{s=1}^{t}X_{s},

(19)

which admit a direct algebraic link to the cumulative weighted suboptimality:

\bar{X}_{t}=2\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}\big(f(x_{s})-f(x^{\star})\big)-(Z_{1}-Z_{t+1})-\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}^{2}\|g_{s}\|^{2}.

Since
Z_{t+1}\geq 0
, it follows that

2\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}\big(f(x_{s})-f(x^{\star})\big)\;\leq\;\bar{X}_{t}+Z_{1}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}^{2}\|g_{s}\|^{2}.

Dividing by
2S_{t}
and recalling the definition of
\bar{F}_{t}
in (
4
), we obtain

\bar{F}_{t}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2S_{t}}\left(\bar{X}_{t}+Z_{1}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}^{2}\|g_{s}\|^{2}\right).

This inequality already mirrors the structure of the confidence sequence
U_{t}(\alpha)
defined in (
9
): the first term comes from an upper bound on
\bar{X}_{t}
, while the remaining two terms carry over unchanged. Thus, controlling
\bar{X}_{t}
uniformly over time is the key step in constructing
U_{t}(\alpha)
.

Step 2: A time-uniform concentration bound for
\bar{X}_{t}

Our goal is now to show that the partial sums
\bar{X}_{t}
admit a time-uniform upper bound in terms of the effective cumulative variance proxy
\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
defined in (
8
). This is where the main probabilistic machinery enters: we construct an exponential supermartingale for each fixed
\lambda
, mix over a geometric grid of
\lambda
’s, and apply Ville’s inequality to obtain a high-probability bound that holds simultaneously for all
t
.

To state the result, define

c_{1}\;:=\;\max\!\left\{1,\;\frac{\log\Big(\log\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}+1\Big)}{\log\Big(\log(\mathrm{e}+1)\Big)}\right\},

(20)

with
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
defined in (
11
), and set
\lambda_{0}:=\sqrt{c_{1}/\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}}
. Moreover, for
k=0,1,2,\ldots
, define

\lambda_{k}:=\lambda_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-k/2},\qquad w_{k}:=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}(k+1)^{2}}.

Note that
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}w_{k}=1
. We are now ready to state the time-uniform concentration result for
\bar{X}_{t}
.

Lemma 2.6

(Time-uniform bound for
\bar{X}_{t}
)
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,\tfrac{2}{\mathrm{e}})
, and let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the process defined in (
13
). For
t\geq 0
, define the mixture process

\bar{E}_{t}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}w_{k}\exp\!\left(\lambda_{k}\bar{X}_{t}-2\lambda_{k}^{2}\,\Sigma_{t}^{2}\right),

with
\Sigma_{t}^{2}
defined in (
8
). Then
\{\bar{E}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a nonnegative supermartingale with
\bar{E}_{0}=1
. Moreover, on the event
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}:=\bigl\{\forall t\geq 1:\bar{E}_{t}<\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\bigr\}
, which satisfies
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\alpha})\geq 1-\alpha
, the following holds:

\forall t\geq 1:\ \bar{X}_{t}\leq C\sqrt{\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\Big(\log\frac{2}{\alpha}+\log\log(\mathrm{e}+\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2})\Big)}.

with constant
C=6\sqrt{c_{1}}
and
c_{1}
defined in (
20
).

Proof.

For clarity, we divide the proof into six steps.

Step (i):
\{\bar{E}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a nonnegative supermartingale.

For any fixed
\lambda\geq 0
, define

E_{t}(\lambda):=\exp\!\left(\lambda\bar{X}_{t}-2\lambda^{2}\Sigma_{t}^{2}\right)\qquad\forall t\geq 0,

with the convention
\bar{X}_{0}=0
and
\Sigma_{0}^{2}=0
, so
E_{0}(\lambda)=1
. Using
\bar{X}_{t}=\bar{X}_{t-1}+X_{t}
and
\Sigma_{t}^{2}=\Sigma_{t-1}^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}
, we compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[E_{t}(\lambda)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\lambda(\bar{X}_{t-1}+X_{t})-2\lambda^{2}(\Sigma_{t-1}^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})\right)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\!\left(\lambda\bar{X}_{t-1}-2\lambda^{2}\Sigma_{t-1}^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\lambda X_{t}-2\lambda^{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}\right)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=E_{t-1}(\lambda)\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\lambda X_{t}-2\lambda^{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}\right)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right].

By the conditional sub-Gaussian assumption (
15
), the conditional expectation in the last line is at most
1
. Hence

\mathbb{E}[E_{t}(\lambda)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq E_{t-1}(\lambda),

so
\{E_{t}(\lambda)\}_{t\geq 0}
is a nonnegative supermartingale with
E_{0}(\lambda)=1
. Finally, since each
E_{t}(\lambda_{k})
is a nonnegative supermartingale and
(w_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is a fixed sequence of nonnegative weights summing to one,
\{\bar{E}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is also a nonnegative supermartingale with
\bar{E}_{0}=\sum_{k\geq 0}w_{k}=1
.

Step (ii): A first upper bound on
\bar{X}_{t}
.

By Ville’s inequality applied to the nonnegative supermartingale
\bar{E}_{t}
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{t\geq 1}\bar{E}_{t}\geq\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\leq\alpha.

Equivalently, the event

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}:=\Big\{\forall t\geq 1:\ \bar{E}_{t}<\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\Big\}

satisfies
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\alpha})\geq 1-\alpha
. Fix
\omega\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
and a time
t\geq 1
with
\Sigma_{t}^{2}>0
. For notational simplicity, we suppress the dependence on
\omega
in what follows. On
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
we have

\bar{E}_{t}=\sum_{k\geq 0}w_{k}\exp\!\left(\lambda_{k}\bar{X}_{t}-2\lambda_{k}^{2}\Sigma_{t}^{2}\right)<\frac{1}{\alpha}.

Hence for every
k\geq 0
,

w_{k}\exp\!\left(\lambda_{k}\bar{X}_{t}-2\lambda_{k}^{2}\Sigma_{t}^{2}\right)<\frac{1}{\alpha},

which implies

\lambda_{k}\bar{X}_{t}-2\lambda_{k}^{2}\Sigma_{t}^{2}<\log\frac{1}{\alpha w_{k}}.

Rearranging this inequality yields, for each
k\geq 0
,

\bar{X}_{t}<\frac{\log(1/(\alpha w_{k}))}{\lambda_{k}}+2\lambda_{k}\Sigma_{t}^{2}=:B_{k}(\Sigma_{t}^{2}).

Noticing that
B_{k}(\cdot)
is nondecreasing and
\Sigma_{t}^{2}\leq\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
, we further have

\bar{X}_{t}<B_{k}(\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2})\qquad\forall k\geq 0.

and since this holds simultaneously for all
k\geq 0
, we obtain the bound

\bar{X}_{t}\leq\inf_{k\geq 0}B_{k}(\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}).

(21)

It remains to upper bound the infimum in terms of
\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
and
\alpha
.

Step (iii): Explicit form of
B_{k}(\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2})
.

For notational simplicity, set

v:=\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2},\qquad L_{\alpha}:=\log\frac{2}{\alpha}\geq\log\frac{\pi^{2}}{6\alpha}.

Using
w_{k}={6}/(\pi^{2}(k+1)^{2})
and
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-k/2}
, we have

\log\frac{1}{\alpha w_{k}}=\log\frac{\pi^{2}}{6\alpha}+2\log(k+1)\leq L_{\alpha}+2\log(k+1),

and

\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{k/2}}{\lambda_{0}}.

Hence, for
v>0
,

B_{k}(v):=\frac{\log(1/(\alpha w_{k}))}{\lambda_{k}}+2\lambda_{k}v\leq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{k/2}}{\lambda_{0}}\big(L_{\alpha}+2\log(k+1)\big)+2\lambda_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-k/2}v.

Step (iv): Choose
k
as a function of
v
.

Define

L_{v}:=\log\log(\mathrm{e}+v)>0.

We will pick an integer
k=k(v)
such that:

1.

\mathrm{e}^{k(v)/2}
is within a constant factor of

z_{v}:=\sqrt{\frac{v}{L_{\alpha}+L_{v}}},

2.

\log(k(v)+1)
is bounded by a constant multiple of
L_{v}
.

One convenient choice is

k(v):=\left\lfloor\log\frac{v}{L_{\alpha}+L_{v}}\right\rfloor\qquad\text{for }v>0,

which immediately implies that
\mathrm{e}^{k(v)/2}\leq z_{v}\leq\mathrm{e}^{(k(v)+1)/2}
. Since
\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\geq 2(\log\tfrac{2}{\alpha}+1)
by definition, Lemma
A.3
guarantees that
k(v)\geq 0
. For the lemma, we note that
L_{\alpha}\geq 1
holds since
\alpha\leq\tfrac{2}{\mathrm{e}}
. Therefore, for this choice, we have that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}}\,z_{v}\leq\mathrm{e}^{k(v)/2}\leq z_{v},\qquad\log(k(v)+1)\leq c_{1}L_{v},

(22)

with the constant
c_{1}\geq 1
defined in (
20
). Note that the inequality
\log(k(v)+1)\leq c_{1}L_{v}
follows from Lemma
A.2
, using the fact that
L_{\alpha}+L_{v}>L_{\alpha}
. For this
k=k(v)
, let

D_{v}:=L_{\alpha}+2\log(k(v)+1).

Using (
22
), we have
2\log(k(v)+1)\leq 2c_{1}L_{v}
, so

D_{v}\leq L_{\alpha}+2c_{1}L_{v}\leq c_{2}\big(L_{\alpha}+L_{v}\big)

for the constant
c_{2}\coloneqq\max\{1,2c_{1}\}=2c_{1}
.

Step (v): Bounding
B_{k(v)}(v)
.

Using (
22
) again, we have

\mathrm{e}^{k(v)/2}\leq z_{v}=\sqrt{\frac{v}{L_{\alpha}+L_{v}}},\qquad\mathrm{e}^{-k(v)/2}\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}\sqrt{\frac{L_{\alpha}+L_{v}}{v}}.

Therefore,

\frac{\mathrm{e}^{k(v)/2}}{\lambda_{0}}D_{v}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{v}{L_{\alpha}+L_{v}}}\,c_{2}\big(L_{\alpha}+L_{v}\big)=\frac{c_{2}}{\lambda_{0}}\sqrt{v\big(L_{\alpha}+L_{v}\big)},

and

2\lambda_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-k(v)/2}v\leq 2\lambda_{0}\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}\sqrt{\frac{L_{\alpha}+L_{v}}{v}}\,v=2\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}\lambda_{0}\sqrt{v\big(L_{\alpha}+L_{v}\big)}.

Summing these two bounds and recalling that
\lambda_{0}=\sqrt{{c_{2}}/({2\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}})}
, we obtain

B_{k(v)}(v)\leq 2\sqrt{2\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}c_{2}}\sqrt{v\big(L_{\alpha}+L_{v}\big)}\leq c_{3}\sqrt{v\big(L_{\alpha}+L_{v}\big)},

for the constant
c_{3}\coloneqq 4\sqrt{c_{2}}\leq 6\sqrt{c_{1}}=:C
.

Finally, since
\inf_{k}B_{k}(v)\leq B_{k(v)}(v)
for each fixed
v>0
, we conclude that
for all
v>0
,

\inf_{k\geq 0}B_{k}(v)\leq C\sqrt{v\big(L_{\alpha}+\log\log(\mathrm{e}+v)\big)}=C\sqrt{v\big(\log\tfrac{2}{\alpha}+\log\log(\mathrm{e}+v)\big)}.

Step (vi): Conclude the time-uniform bound.

Combining this with (
21
), we obtain, on the event

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
,

\bar{X}_{t}\leq C\sqrt{\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\Big(\log\tfrac{2}{\alpha}+\log\log(\mathrm{e}+\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2})\Big)}\qquad\forall t\geq 1,

which gives the stated bound.
∎

This lemma is the probabilistic engine of the paper: whenever one can certify a conditional sub-Gaussian inequality of the form (
15
), one obtains a time-uniform concentration inequality for the partial sums, with a variance scale
\Sigma_{t}^{2}
and only a mild iterated-logarithm correction. In particular,
\bar{X}_{t}
remains controlled simultaneously over all
t\geq 1
with probability at least
1-\alpha
.

Step 3: From martingale sums to cumulative suboptimality.

Using the definition of
X_{t}
, we now rewrite
\bar{X}_{t}
in terms of the cumulative weighted suboptimality, the distance terms
Z_{t}
, and the squared gradient terms. As explained at the end of Step 1, a simple telescoping argument yields the desired upper bound on the numerator of
\bar{F}_{t}
.

Lemma 2.7

(From
\bar{X}_{t}
to cumulative suboptimality)
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,\tfrac{2}{\mathrm{e}})
, with probability at least
1-\alpha
, the following holds simultaneously for all
t\geq 1
:

\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}\big(f(x_{s})-f(x^{\star})\big)\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(C\,\sqrt{\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\Big(\log\tfrac{2}{\alpha}+\log\log(\mathrm{e}+\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2})\Big)}+Z_{1}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}^{2}\|g_{s}\|^{2}\Bigg),

(23)

where
C>0
is the constant from Lemma
2.6
.

Proof.

From Lemma
2.6
we know that for any
\alpha\in(0,\tfrac{2}{\mathrm{e}})
, there exists an event
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
with
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\alpha})\geq 1-\alpha
such that, on
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
,

\forall t\geq 1:\qquad\sum_{s=1}^{t}X_{s}\leq C\sqrt{\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\Big(\log\tfrac{2}{\alpha}+\log\log(\mathrm{e}+\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2})\Big)}.

(24)

Next, we express
\sum_{s=1}^{t}X_{s}
in terms of the cumulative suboptimality. Summing the definition (
13
) from
s=1
to
t
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{t}X_{s}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}\big(f(x_{s})-f(x^{\star})\big)-\sum_{s=1}^{t}(Z_{s}-Z_{s+1})-\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}^{2}\|g_{s}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}\big(f(x_{s})-f(x^{\star})\big)-(Z_{1}-Z_{t+1})-\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}^{2}\|g_{s}\|^{2}.

Rearranging this identity, we obtain

2\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}\big(f(x_{s})-f(x^{\star})\big)=\sum_{s=1}^{t}X_{s}+(Z_{1}-Z_{t+1})+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}^{2}\|g_{s}\|^{2}.

(25)

On the event
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
, we may bound
\sum_{s=1}^{t}X_{s}
using (
24
). Moreover,
Z_{t+1}=\|x_{t+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\geq 0
, so we can drop it. Plugging these bounds into (
25
), we find that on
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
, for all
t\geq 1
,

2\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}\big(f(x_{s})-f(x^{\star})\big)\leq C\sqrt{\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\Big(\log\tfrac{2}{\alpha}+\log\log(\mathrm{e}+\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2})\Big)}+Z_{1}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}^{2}\|g_{s}\|^{2}.

Dividing both sides by
2
yields (
23
), and the bound holds simultaneously for all
t\geq 1
on the event
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
, which has probability at least
1-\alpha
.
∎

This lemma is the bridge from the martingale world back to optimization: it translates the time-uniform control of the process
\bar{X}_{t}
into a time-uniform upper bound on the cumulative weighted suboptimality
\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}(f(x_{s})-f^{\star})
. Finally, dividing both sides by
S_{t}
yields a time-uniform bound for the weighted average suboptimality
\bar{F}_{t}
, completing the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

2.2
Clarifying remarks and intuition

The proof of Theorem
2.1
relies on several ideas from time-uniform sequential analysis that may be unfamiliar to readers with a background in stochastic optimization. In this subsection we collect three clarifying remarks intended to illuminate the construction. First, we explain the role of the mixture supermartingale
\bar{E}_{t}
and why a single-parameter exponential martingale cannot yield a time-uniform bound without sacrificing optional stopping. Second, we provide a heuristic “continuous optimization” argument that motivates the geometric grid over
\lambda_{k}
and the emergence of an iterated-logarithm term in the boundary. Third, we discuss why our bound does not contain a
\log\log t
factor typical in classical anytime concentration results, and how the square-summable stepsize condition fundamentally alters the variance accumulation in the stochastic approximation setting.

Remark 2.8

(Why the mixture
\bar{E}_{t}
is necessary)
.

To understand the need for mixtures in Lemma
2.6
, it is instructive to redo the key step with a
single
exponential supermartingale. For any fixed
\lambda>0
, define

E_{t}(\lambda):=\exp\!\big(\lambda\bar{X}_{t}-2\lambda^{2}\Sigma_{t}^{2}\big),

as in Step (i) of Lemma
2.6
. By the conditional sub-Gaussian property (
15
),
\{E_{t}(\lambda)\}_{t\geq 0}
is a nonnegative supermartingale with
E_{0}(\lambda)=1
. Ville’s inequality therefore gives

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{t\geq 1}E_{t}(\lambda)\,\geq\,\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\,\leq\,\alpha.

(26)

Equivalently, with probability at least
1-\alpha
,

\forall t\geq 1:\ E_{t}(\lambda)<\frac{1}{\alpha}\ \iff\ \lambda\bar{X}_{t}-2\lambda^{2}\Sigma_{t}^{2}<\log\frac{1}{\alpha}.

Rearranging yields the deterministic upper bound

\bar{X}_{t}<\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{\lambda}+2\lambda\Sigma_{t}^{2}\leq\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{\lambda}+2\lambda\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\qquad\forall t\geq 1.

(27)

Now fix a time
t
and, for that
t
, treat the right-hand side as a function of
\lambda
:

B(\lambda,t):=\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{\lambda}+2\lambda\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2},\qquad\lambda>0.

(28)

This function is minimized explicitly at

\lambda^{\star}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{2\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}}},

(29)

with minimum value

B\big(\lambda^{\star}(t),t\big)=2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\log\frac{1}{\alpha}}\leq 2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\log\frac{2}{\alpha}}.

(30)

Thus, for a
fixed
time
t
, a single exponential supermartingale produces an upper bound with the scaling
\sqrt{\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\log(2/\alpha)}
. This matches the leading term that appears in Theorem
2.1
, but
without
the additional iterated-logarithm factor that arises when enforcing time-uniform validity.

The problem arises from the fact that
\lambda^{\star}(t)
in (
29
) depends on
\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
and is therefore
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable: it is chosen
after observing the data up to time
t
. Substituting this data-dependent
\lambda^{\star}(t)
back into
E_{t}(\lambda)
destroys the supermartingale structure, so Ville’s inequality cannot be applied time-uniformly. With a single exponential process one faces a choice: either fix
\lambda
in advance (yielding time-uniform but suboptimal bounds for many
t
), or optimize
\lambda
at each
t
(yielding sharp fixed-time bounds but no optional-stopping guarantee).

The mixture construction in Lemma
2.6
reconciles these goals by fixing a countable family of parameters
a priori
and combining the corresponding exponential supermartingales. This “method of mixtures” (also called pseudo-maximization) goes back to Robbins and Siegmund’s classical work on sequential boundary crossing and is developed systematically in the theory of self-normalized processes (see
[
7
]
). Modern confidence-sequence constructions build on this principle in various forms, including explicit mixture and stitching frameworks such as the sub-
\psi
approach in
[
15
]
, as well as betting- and e-process-based methods that aggregate families of nonnegative supermartingales; see
[
30
]
.
\clubsuit

Remark 2.9

(Intuition for the geometric grid
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
)
.

The mixture construction in Lemma
2.6
hinges on choosing a
geometric
grid of tuning parameters
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-k/2}
. Here we provide intuition for this choice by examining how the envelope
B_{k}(\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2})
is minimized, and how the geometry of the grid matches the natural scale of the problem. Let
v\coloneqq\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
and recall the upper envelope

B_{k}(v):=\frac{\log(1/(\alpha w_{k}))}{\lambda_{k}}+2\lambda_{k}v,\qquad\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-k/2},\quad w_{k}=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}(k+1)^{2}}.

Momentarily regard
k
as a continuous variable and write
z=\mathrm{e}^{k/2}>0
. Then

B_{k}(v)=\frac{D(z)}{\lambda_{0}}\,z+2\lambda_{0}\,\frac{v}{z},

where

D(z):=\log\frac{\pi^{2}}{6\alpha}+2\log(k+1)=\log\frac{\pi^{2}}{6\alpha}+2\log\!\Big(1+2\log z\Big).

The key observation is that
D(z)
varies
very slowly
in
z
: once
z
is chosen near its optimal scale,
D(z)
grows only like
\log\log(\mathrm{e}+z)
, a negligible rate compared to any power of
z
. As a heuristic, we therefore
treat
D(z)
as approximately constant
and consider the simplified objective

B(z,v):=\frac{D}{\lambda_{0}}\,z+2\lambda_{0}\,\frac{v}{z},

where
D>0
is held fixed. The function
B(z,v)
is strictly convex in
z>0
, and its minimum is attained at

z^{\star}=\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda_{0}^{2}v}{D}}\ \implies\ \lambda^{\star}=\frac{\lambda_{0}}{z^{\star}}=\sqrt{\frac{D}{2v}}.

Substituting
z^{\star}
back into
B(z,v)
yields

\min_{z>0}B(z,v)=2\sqrt{2Dv}.

Heuristically, since
D(z)\approx\log\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{6\alpha}+\log\log(\mathrm{e}+z)
, which at the relevant scale is equivalent up to constants to
\log\tfrac{2}{\alpha}+\log\log(\mathrm{e}+v)
, this suggests the leading-order behavior

\inf_{k\geq 0}B_{k}(v)\;\lesssim\;\sqrt{v\Big(\log\tfrac{2}{\alpha}+\log\log(\mathrm{e}+v)\Big)}.

The slowly varying term
\log(k+1)
introduces an iterated-logarithm correction, where the
\log\log(\mathrm{e}+v)
factor arises precisely from the slow growth of
\log(k+1)
on this geometric grid. In particular,
for a fixed time
t
, the optimal value of
\lambda
is of order
v^{-1/2}
, where
v=\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
controls the variance scale of the martingale sum. Since the scale
\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
is not known in advance and may vary with the trajectory up to the a priori bound
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
, a
geometric
grid of the form
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-k/2}
, with
\lambda_{0}
chosen as a function of
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
, is a natural discretization: consecutive grid points differ by a constant factor, ensuring that for every
t
there exists a
\lambda_{k}
within a constant factor of the ideal choice
\lambda^{\star}\approx\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}
.

In short, the grid is chosen so that: (i) it spans the relevant scales of
\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
via exponentially spaced search points, and (ii) at least one
\lambda_{k}
is guaranteed to be within a constant factor of the ideal minimizer, thereby achieving near-optimal behavior across all times
t
. This “scale-matching” intuition for geometric discretizations is common in mixture and stitching arguments for martingale concentration; see, for example, the heuristic discussion surrounding Theorem 1 in
[
15
]
and the broader treatment of pseudo-maximization via mixtures in self-normalized processes
[
7
]
.
\clubsuit

Remark 2.10

(Why no
\log\log(\mathrm{e}+t)
term appears)
.

A recurring feature in time-uniform martingale concentration is the appearance of
iterated logarithm
corrections, such as
\log\log(\mathrm{e}+t)
; see, for example, classical boundary-crossing analyses of Robbins and Siegmund
[
27
]
and modern confidence-sequence treatments such as
[
15
]
. At first glance, one might therefore expect a confidence sequence for stochastic optimization to include a term of the form
\sqrt{\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\log\log(\mathrm{e}+t)}
rather than the
\sqrt{\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\log\log(\mathrm{e}+\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2})}
term appearing in Theorem
2.1
. The difference comes from the
square-summable stepsize condition
in Assumption
1.2
and the resulting definition of
\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
. In most sequential testing settings,
\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
grows
linearly
with
t
, so controlling
k
in Step (iv) of Lemma
2.6
ultimately leads to iterated-logarithm terms in
\log(\mathrm{e}+t)
. Here, however,
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
is square summable, so
\Sigma_{t,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
is
uniformly bounded
by
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}<\infty
. Consequently, the relevant “scale” for the confidence sequence is the variance process itself rather than the time index. This variance-based iterated logarithm therefore remains bounded and is substantially smaller than the classical time-based correction that arises when the variance grows linearly with
t
.

This phenomenon is well known in the martingale boundary literature: confidence sequences expressed in terms of data-dependent variance proxies (such as predictable quadratic variation, conditional variance processes, or self-normalized sums) adapt to the realized variability of the process and can be substantially tighter when the variance grows slowly or remains bounded, compared to bounds written purely in terms of time. See, for example,
[
7
]
and the discussion in
[
15
, Section 3]
on expressing time-uniform bounds in terms of intrinsic variance processes rather than the time index.
\clubsuit

3
Anytime-Valid Stopping Rules

A central motivation for our confidence-sequence approach is to turn projected SGD into an
anytime-valid
optimization procedure: instead of committing to a fixed horizon in advance, the user would like to stop the algorithm based on the information actually observed along the trajectory. In practice, most stopping rules for SGD are heuristic (i.e., they monitor validation loss, gradient norms, or empirical convergence patterns) and their guarantees, when available, typically hold only for a pre-specified time or for a specific deterministic stopping rule. In contrast, a confidence sequence is designed precisely to support
optional stopping
: it provides a high-probability guarantee that remains valid under arbitrary data-dependent monitoring and stopping strategies.

Corollary
2.4
provides an observable anytime-valid upper confidence sequence
\{U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)\}_{t\geq 1}
for the weighted average suboptimality
\bar{F}_{t}
in (
4
). Combining this with the convexity bound
f(\bar{x}_{t})-f^{\star}\leq\bar{F}_{t}
, we obtain, for any fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\forall t\geq 1:\ f(\bar{x}_{t})-f^{\star}\leq U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)\right)\;\geq\;1-\alpha,

(31)

where
\bar{x}_{t}=S_{t}^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}x_{s}
is the weighted average iterate, with
S_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}
.

Given a target accuracy
\varepsilon>0
and confidence level
\alpha\in(0,\tfrac{2}{\mathrm{e}})
, it is therefore natural to stop as soon as the certificate
U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)
falls below
\varepsilon
. As already foreshadowed in the introduction, we formalize this through the stopping time

\tau_{\varepsilon}:=\inf\big\{t\geq 1:U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)\leq\varepsilon\big\},

(32)

with the convention
\inf\emptyset=\infty
. Since
U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable for each
t
,
\tau_{\varepsilon}
is a proper stopping time with respect to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
generated by the iterates and gradients.

In the remainder of this section we show that, under the stochastic approximation conditions on the stepsize in Assumption
1.2
, the stopping time
\tau_{\varepsilon}
enjoys two key properties: (i) it is almost surely finite for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, and (ii) the returned iterate
\bar{x}_{\tau_{\varepsilon}}
is
\varepsilon
-optimal with confidence
1-\alpha
. Together, these facts certify projected SGD as an anytime-valid
\varepsilon
-optimization algorithm driven entirely by observable quantities along the run.

Theorem 3.1

(Adaptive anytime-valid stopping rule)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1.1
and
1.2
hold, and let
\{U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)\}_{t\geq 1}
be the anytime-valid upper confidence sequence from Corollary
2.4
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
\alpha\in(0,\tfrac{2}{\mathrm{e}})
, and define the stopping time
\tau_{\varepsilon}
by (
32
). Then:

(i)

\tau_{\varepsilon}<\infty
almost surely;

(ii)

the stopped iterate
\bar{x}_{\tau_{\varepsilon}}
satisfies:

\mathbb{P}\Big(f(\bar{x}_{\tau_{\varepsilon}})-f^{\star}\leq\varepsilon\Big)\;\geq\;1-\alpha.

(33)

In particular, projected SGD equipped with
\tau_{\varepsilon}
yields a data-dependent stopping strategy that guarantees
\varepsilon
-optimality with probability
1-\alpha
, without requiring a predetermined time horizon.

Proof.

We first show that
\tau_{\varepsilon}
is almost surely finite. Following the same reasoning as in Proposition
2.2
, for any fixed
\alpha\in(0,\tfrac{2}{\mathrm{e}})
, we have

U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)\to 0\quad\text{almost surely as }t\to\infty.

Let

\Omega_{0}:=\Big\{\lim_{t\to\infty}U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)=0\Big\},

so that
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0})=1
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
. By the definition of convergence, there exists a (random, finite) index
T_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\in\mathbb{N}
such that

U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)(\omega)\;\leq\;\varepsilon\qquad\forall t\geq T_{\varepsilon}(\omega).

By the definition (
32
) of
\tau_{\varepsilon}
,

\tau_{\varepsilon}(\omega)=\inf\big\{t\geq 1:[U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)](\omega)\leq\varepsilon\big\}\;\leq\;T_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\;<\;\infty.

Since this holds for every
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
and
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0})=1
, we conclude that
\tau_{\varepsilon}<\infty
almost surely.

For part (ii), let

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}:=\Big\{\forall t\geq 1:\ f(\bar{x}_{t})-f^{\star}\leq U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)\Big\}.

By (
31
),
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\alpha})\geq 1-\alpha
. On the event
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
, the inequality
f(\bar{x}_{t})-f^{\star}\leq U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)
holds for all
t\geq 1
. In particular, on
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
we have

f(\bar{x}_{\tau_{\varepsilon}})-f^{\star}\;\leq\;U_{\tau_{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha).

By definition of
\tau_{\varepsilon}
,
U_{\tau_{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)\leq\varepsilon
, so on
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
,

f(\bar{x}_{\tau_{\varepsilon}})-f^{\star}\;\leq\;\varepsilon.

Thus
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\subseteq\{f(\bar{x}_{\tau_{\varepsilon}})-f^{\star}\leq\varepsilon\}
, and taking probabilities yields

\mathbb{P}\Big(f(\bar{x}_{\tau_{\varepsilon}})-f^{\star}\leq\varepsilon\Big)\;\geq\;\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\alpha})\;\geq\;1-\alpha,

which is exactly (
33
). This proves part (ii) and completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
3.1
shows that projected SGD equipped with the stopping rule
\tau_{\varepsilon}
is a
data-dependent

\varepsilon
-optimal procedure: one may monitor
U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)
online and stop as soon as it falls below
\varepsilon
, without specifying a horizon in advance or invalidating the
1-\alpha
guarantee. This yields fully implementable stopping rules based only on running sums of
\eta_{t}
,
\eta_{t}^{2}
, and the observed gradient norms, with the usual tradeoff between adaptivity and reliance on global problem parameters.

In the next subsection we study the stopping-time complexity associated with the rule
U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)\leq\varepsilon
. We present explicit bounds with
deterministic
constants via a strengthening of our assumptions on the stochastic gradients.

3.1
Stopping-time complexity

In this subsection we derive explicit bounds on the stopping time
\tau_{\varepsilon}
under standard stepsize schedules for stochastic approximation. Our assumptions on the stochastic gradients are as follows.

Assumption 3.2

(Stochastic gradients (strengthened))
.

For all
t\geq 1
, the stochastic gradient
g_{t}
satisfies:

(i)

Unbiasedness:

\mathbb{E}[g_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\nabla f(x_{t})
, and

(ii)

Boundedness:

\|g_{t}\|\leq G
almost surely.

Note that Assumption
3.2
(ii) implies Assumption
1.1
(ii), and moreover implies the conditional sub-Gaussian Assumption
1.1
(iii) with variance proxy
\sigma^{2}:=4G^{2}
. Indeed, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

|\big\langle g_{t}-\mathbb{E}[g_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}],u\big\rangle|\leq 2G\|u\|\qquad\text{almost surely},

so by Hoeffding’s lemma,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\lambda\,\langle g_{t}-\mathbb{E}[g_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}],u\rangle\right)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\leq\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\,4G^{2}\,\|u\|^{2}}{2}\right),

establishing conditional sub-Gaussianity.

Before quantifying the dependence of
\tau_{\varepsilon}
on the target accuracy
\varepsilon
, it is convenient to reduce the stopping criterion
U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)\leq\varepsilon
to a simple threshold condition on the accumulated stepsizes
S_{t}
. The following corollary makes this reduction precise.

Corollary 3.3

(General certified stopping bound)
.

Let Assumptions
3.2
and
1.2
hold, and let
\{U_{t}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\alpha)\}_{t\geq 1}
be the anytime-valid upper confidence sequence from Corollary
2.4
. Moreover, define

K_{\alpha}:=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(C\sqrt{\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\Big(\log\tfrac{2}{\alpha}+\log\log\!\big(\mathrm{e}+\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\big)\Big)}+R_{x}^{2}+G^{2}V_{\infty}\Bigg),

(34)

with constant
C
defined in (
10
) and
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}:=\max\{4G^{2}R_{x}^{2}V_{\infty},2(\log\tfrac{2}{\alpha}+1)\}
. Then, for every target accuracy
\varepsilon>0
,

\tau_{\varepsilon}\;\leq\;\inf\!\left\{t\geq 1:\;S_{t}\geq\frac{K_{\alpha}}{\varepsilon}\right\}\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Corollary
3.3
shows that the stopping time
\tau_{\varepsilon}
is controlled entirely by the growth of
S_{t}
: any deterministic lower bound on
S_{t}
immediately yields an almost-sure upper bound on
\tau_{\varepsilon}
. The constant
K_{\alpha}
depends only on the global problem parameters
(G,R_{x})
, the confidence level
\alpha
, and the stepsize schedule
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
. In what follows, we apply Corollary
3.3
to several standard choices of stepsizes to obtain certified stopping-time complexity guarantees.

(1) Polynomially decaying stepsizes.

Consider

\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}t^{-\gamma},\qquad\eta_{0}>0,\ \ \gamma\in\Big(\frac{1}{2},1\Big).

(35)

Since
\gamma>1/2
, this family satisfies
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{t}=\infty
and
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{t}^{2}<\infty
. Lemma
A.4
implies that for all
t\geq 1
,

S_{t}\geq\frac{\eta_{0}}{1-\gamma}\Big(t^{1-\gamma}-1\Big).

Using Corollary
3.3
, it suffices that

\frac{\eta_{0}}{1-\gamma}\Big(t^{1-\gamma}-1\Big)\ \geq\ \frac{K_{\alpha}}{\varepsilon}.

Solving for
t
yields:

\tau_{\varepsilon}\ \leq\ \Biggl(1+\frac{(1-\gamma)K_{\alpha}}{\eta_{0}\,\varepsilon}\Biggr)^{\!\!\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}\qquad\text{almost surely}.

(36)

We summarize this in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.4

(Certified stopping complexity for
\gamma\in(1/2,1)
)
.

Under the conditions of Corollary
3.3
and for the stepsize schedule (
35
), the stopping time
\tau_{\varepsilon}
defined in (
32
) satisfies

\tau_{\varepsilon}=O\!\Big(\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}\Big)\qquad\text{almost surely}.

In particular,
\tau_{\varepsilon}
scales polynomially in
1/\varepsilon
with an exponent determined by the decay rate
\gamma
.

(2) Harmonic stepsizes.

Now consider the boundary case

\eta_{t}=\frac{\eta_{0}}{t},\qquad\eta_{0}>0.

(37)

Then
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{t}=\infty
and
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{t}^{2}<\infty
, so Assumption
1.2
holds. Lemma
A.5
gives
S_{t}\geq\eta_{0}\log t
. By Corollary
3.3
, it suffices that

\eta_{0}\log t\ \geq\ \frac{K_{\alpha}}{\varepsilon},

which implies

\tau_{\varepsilon}\ \leq\ \exp\!\Big(\frac{K_{\alpha}}{\eta_{0}\,\varepsilon}\Big)\qquad\text{almost surely}.

(38)

We summarize this in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.5

(Certified stopping complexity for
\gamma=1
)
.

Under the conditions of Corollary
3.3
and for the stepsize schedule (
37
), the stopping time
\tau_{\varepsilon}
defined in (
32
) satisfies

\tau_{\varepsilon}=\exp\!\Big(O(\varepsilon^{-1})\Big)\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Thus harmonic stepsizes yield certified stopping but with very slow complexity in
1/\varepsilon
.

Remark 3.6

(Practical implications)
.

The polynomial regime
\gamma\in(1/2,1)
produces certified complexity
\tau_{\varepsilon}=O\Big(\varepsilon^{-1/(1-\gamma)}\Big)
, which matches the classical stochastic approximation tradeoff between stepsize decay and variance control. The harmonic schedule
\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}/t
, although valid for confidence-sequence stopping, leads to exponential complexity in
1/\varepsilon
and can therefore be unattractive in practice. Combined with Theorem
3.1
, these results show that the stepsize schedule entirely governs the certified stopping-time rate (up to problem-dependent constants), and that the confidence-sequence approach preserves standard stochastic approximation complexity guarantees while remaining valid under arbitrary, data-dependent optional stopping.

Appendix A
Technical lemmas

Lemma A.1
.

Let Assumptions
1.2
and
1.1
(ii) hold. Then,

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}<\infty\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Proof.

Define the nonnegative partial sums

Y_{n}:=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\eta_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}\qquad\forall n\geq 1,

and let
Y:=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}Y_{n}
(possibly taking values in
[0,\infty]
). Since the sequence
\{Y_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is nondecreasing and nonnegative, we may apply the monotone convergence theorem:

\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\lim_{n\to\infty}Y_{n}\Big]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[Y_{n}].

For each
n
, we have

\mathbb{E}[Y_{n}]=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\eta_{t}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}[\|g_{t}\|^{2}].

Using the tower property and Assumption
1.1
(ii),

\mathbb{E}[\|g_{t}\|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[\|g_{t}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\big]\leq\mathbb{E}[G^{2}]=G^{2},

so that

\mathbb{E}[Y_{n}]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\eta_{t}^{2}G^{2}=G^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\eta_{t}^{2}.

Letting
n\to\infty
and using Assumption
1.2
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[Y]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[Y_{n}]\leq G^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{t}^{2}<\infty.

Thus
Y
is a nonnegative random variable with finite expectation. If
\mathbb{P}(Y=\infty)>0
, we would have
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\infty
, a contradiction. Hence

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}<\infty\right)=1,

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}>1
and define

c_{1}\;:=\;\max\!\left\{1,\;\frac{\log\Big(\log\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}+1\Big)}{\log\Big(\log(\mathrm{e}+1)\Big)}\right\}.

Fix any
L_{\alpha}\geq 1
. Then, for all
v
with
L_{\alpha}<v\leq\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
,

\log\Big(\log(v/L_{\alpha})+1\Big)\;\leq\;c_{1}\,\log\Big(\log(v+\mathrm{e})\Big).

Proof.

First note that for
v>L_{\alpha}/\mathrm{e}
we have
v/L_{\alpha}>1/\mathrm{e}
, hence
\log(v/L_{\alpha})>-1
, so
\log(v/L_{\alpha})+1>0
and
\log(\log(v/L_{\alpha})+1)
is well-defined. Moreover,
v+\mathrm{e}>\mathrm{e}
implies
\log(v+\mathrm{e})>1
and thus
\log(\log(v+\mathrm{e}))>0
. We now prove the inequality. Since
L_{\alpha}\geq 1
and the logarithm is an increasing function,

L_{\alpha}\leq v\leq\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\ \implies\ \log(v/L_{\alpha})+1\leq\log\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}+1\ \implies\ \log\Big(\log(v/L_{\alpha})+1\Big)\;\leq\;\log(\log\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}+1).

Similarly, since
v\geq L_{\alpha}\geq 1
, we have

v\geq 1\ \implies\ \log(v+\mathrm{e})\geq\log(\mathrm{e}+1)>1\ \implies\ \log(\log(v+\mathrm{e}))\geq\log(\log(\mathrm{e}+1)).

Now, by the definition of
c_{1}
,

c_{1}\;\geq\;\frac{\log(\log\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}+1)}{\log(\log(\mathrm{e}+1))}\ \implies\ \log(\log\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}+1)\;\leq\;c_{1}\,\log(\log(\mathrm{e}+1)).

Combining the last three displays, we obtain for all

L_{\alpha}\leq v\leq\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
,

\log\Big(\log(v/L_{\alpha})+1\Big)\;\leq\;\log(\log\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty,\mathrm{eff}}^{2}+1)\;\leq\;c_{1}\,\log(\log(\mathrm{e}+1))\;\leq\;c_{1}\,\log(\log(v+\mathrm{e})),

which is the desired inequality.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Fix any
L_{\alpha}\geq 1
and define
\nu_{0}:=2(L_{\alpha}+1)
. Then, for all
v\geq\nu_{0}
,

v>L_{\alpha}+\log\Big(\log(\mathrm{e}+v)\Big).

Proof.

Since
L_{\alpha}\geq 1
, we have
\nu_{0}=2(L_{\alpha}+1)\geq 4
. For any
v\geq 4
, we have
\mathrm{e}^{v}\geq\mathrm{e}+v
, which implies that
\log(\mathrm{e}+v)\leq v
. Since
\log(\mathrm{e}+v)>1
, and therefore
\log\Big(\log(\mathrm{e}+v)\Big)\leq\log v
for all
v\geq 4
. Thus, for
v\geq\nu_{0}
, we have

v-L_{\alpha}-\log\Big(\log(\mathrm{e}+v)\Big)\geq v-L_{\alpha}-\log v=:g(v)

Since
g^{\prime}(v)=1-\tfrac{1}{v}\geq 0
for all
v\geq 1
, we have that
g
is increasing and

g(v)\geq g(\nu_{0})=L_{\alpha}+2-\log\Big(2(L_{\alpha}+1)\Big).

Since
\log(2x)\leq x
for all
x\geq 1
, we obtain

g(\nu_{0})\geq L_{\alpha}+2-(L_{\alpha}+1)=1>0.

Therefore
v-L_{\alpha}-\log(\log(\mathrm{e}+v))>0
for all
v\geq\nu_{0}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
and define

S_{t}^{(\gamma)}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}s^{-\gamma}\qquad\forall t\geq 1.

Then, for all
t\geq 1
,

S_{t}^{(\gamma)}\;\geq\;\frac{t^{1-\gamma}-1}{1-\gamma}.

Proof.

The function
x\mapsto x^{-\gamma}
is positive and strictly decreasing on
[1,\infty)
, for any
\gamma>0
. Therefore, for each integer
t\geq 1
,

\sum_{s=1}^{t}s^{-\gamma}\;\geq\;\int_{1}^{t+1}x^{-\gamma}\,dx.

Evaluating the integral for
\gamma\in(0,1)
gives

\int_{1}^{t+1}x^{-\gamma}\,dx\;=\;\left[\frac{x^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\right]_{x=1}^{x=t+1}\;=\;\frac{(t+1)^{1-\gamma}-1}{1-\gamma}.

To conclude, note that
(t+1)^{1-\gamma}\geq t^{1-\gamma}
for all
t\geq 1
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
.
∎

Lemma A.5
.

Define

H_{t}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{1}{s},\qquad\forall t\geq 1.

Then for all
t\geq 1
,

H_{t}\;\geq\;\log(t).

Proof.

The function
x\mapsto 1/x
is positive and strictly decreasing on
[1,\infty)
. Hence, for each integer
t\geq 1
,

\sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{1}{s}\;\geq\;\int_{1}^{t+1}\frac{dx}{x}\;=\;\log(t+1),

The final statement follows from the monotonicity of the logarithm:
\log(t+1)\geq\log t
for all
t\geq 1
.
∎
