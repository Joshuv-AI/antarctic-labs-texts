Association between muscle-specific strength and inflammatory biomarkers in community-dwelling older women: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Leonardo Augusto da Costa Teixeira, Luana Aparecida Soares, Maria Clara de Moura Oliveira, Maria Fernanda dos Santos Mourão, Juliana Nogueira Pontes Nobre, Pedro Henrique Scheidt Figueiredo, Henrique Silveira Costa, Adriana Netto Parentoni, Vanessa Amaral Mendonça, Redha Taiar, Ana Cristina Rodrigues Lacerda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Global Leadership Initiative on Sarcopenia (GLIS) has released recommendations on the use of muscle-specific strength (MSS) in the assessment of sarcopenia. MSS is considered the measurement of force per unit of muscle mass and has been recommended for comprehensive assessment of muscle tissue and assessment of sarcopenia in the older. While biomarkers have been associated with sarcopenia’s pathophysiology and its diagnostic, the association between inflammatory markers and MSS in older people remains understudied. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the association between MSS and the concentrations of a panel of inflammatory biomarkers in community-dwelling older women. Seventy-one older women participated in this study, with Dual X-ray Absorptiometry used to assess lean muscle mass and the Jamar dynamometer employed to measure handgrip strength. The concentrations of IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, adiponectin, leptin, resistin, IFN, TNF, sTNFr-1, and sTNFr-2 were measured using flow cytometry and ELISA. Spearman correlation and linear regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the relationships between the variables, with a significance level set at 5%. The analyses revealed significant correlations between muscle-specific strength and the concentrations of IL-5 (ρ = -0.408), IL-6 (ρ = -0.258), IL-8 (ρ = -0.309) and sTNFr-2 (ρ = -0.390) in this sample. The association analysis showed the optimal model explaining variations in muscle-specific strength included IL-5 and sTNFr-2 (R: 0.47; R² 0.20; p = 0.02). These findings suggest a pro-inflammatory component contributing to the loss of muscle-specific strength in community-dwelling older women. Furthermore, they provide insights into the pathophysiology of sarcopenia and identify promising biomarkers that may be useful in screening strategies and early diagnosis of this condition in older adults.Keywords: Sarcopenia, GLIS, Inflammation, sTNFr-2, IL-5 SDG 3 and 10Subject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Recommendations and algorithms for diagnosing sarcopenia are periodically updated to refine the assessment and diagnosis of sarcopenia in the older people1,2. Several consensuses, methods, and instruments are currently used to assess sarcopenia1,2, with the EWGSOP2 being one of the most adopted in the literature, stating that the first indicator of the disease is low muscle strength, the second is low muscle mass, and physical performance is the factor defines its severity2,3. Recently, the Global Leadership Initiative on Sarcopenia (GLIS) consensus released adjustments to the definition of sarcopenia, which is now considered by the loss of muscle-specific strength (MSS) in older people4.

Although sarcopenia is a known condition and widely studied5, and assessment and diagnostic refinements are proposed2, there are considerable gaps in the literature about its diagnosis and classification6–8. The evidence demonstrates that the main clinical tools recommended for the diagnosis of sarcopenia by EWGSOP2 are not in agreement with one another6. Investigations comparing the diagnosis of sarcopenia using different consensus and algorithms reveal substantial differences in the number of older people diagnosed as sarcopenic or not1,8–11. In this context, according to GLIS, the MSS expressed by the muscle quality index has gained emphasis as a promising measure for understanding and diagnosis of sarcopenia in older people4,12.

Muscle-specific strength in older people refers to the force generated by a specific amount of muscle mass, represented as force per unit of muscle mass, and has been recommended for the diagnosis of sarcopenia12 and sarcopenic obesity in older13. This measurement considers muscle quality, that is, how efficient a person’s muscle mass is in generating force12,14, since it serves multiple roles: not only does it assess the ability to generate force, but it also has substantial predictions value for mortality across different clinical populations13,15. Even if muscle mass remains unaltered, its capacity to produce force may be compromised by changes in muscle composition, fat infiltration, neuromuscular and inflammatory changes associated with aging4,12,14.

Inflammation is previously recognized as part of the pathophysiology of sarcopenia16. Studies are consistent in demonstrating higher levels of pro-inflammatory mediators contributing to increased inflammatory response and muscle catabolism16–20. In this context, some biomarkers have been proposed to characterize low appendicular muscle mass, low muscle strength and sarcopenia in community-dwelling older people19,21–24.

Considering that several studies demonstrate significative associations between inflammatory markers (such as adiponectin, BDNF, IL-6, IL-8, sTNFr-1, and sTNFr-2) and sarcopenia, as assessed by low strength and muscle mass algorithms17,19,22,24, it is possible to hypothesize that significant associations will be found between inflammatory biomarkers and MSS. In view of the new recommendations (GLIS), it is imperative that new studies be conducted to explore the factors associated with MSS in the older.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the association between MSS and the plasma concentrations of adiponectin, BDNF, IFN, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, leptin, resistin, TNF, sTNFr-1, and sTNFr-2 of community-dwelling older women.

Methods

This is an observational, exploratory cross-sectional study carried out between June 2016 and June 2017 in Diamantina, MG, Brazil. These are secondary analyses from the sarcopenia and inflammatory biomarkers study published elsewhere19,25,26. The research was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Vales do Jequitinhonha e Mucuri (UFVJM) under protocol number 1,461,306. All participants signed an informed consent form, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national and institutional guidelines and regulations.

Sample

Community-dwelling older women were recruited from their records at the Basic Health Units (UBS), invited in public places, and by announcements in geriatric clinics in Diamantina, Minas Gerais, Brazil. All participants were visited in their homes and answered a questionnaire with information about identification, medical history and lifestyle habits. The inclusion criteria were accepting to take part in the research and being a woman over 65 years of age.

The exclusion criteria were: Those with cognitive deficits, detected by the Mini Mental State Examination, according to their level of education; unable to walk independently; who had been hospitalized or had suffered fractures in the last 3 months; with active neoplasia in the last five years; those in palliative care; those who used anti-inflammatory and corticosteroid medication; who performed physical activity regularly (at least three times a week); those with metabolic disease in the acute phase; those with severe visual and hearing impairment, those with cardiorespiratory diseases in the acute phase; and any other impediment to carrying out the proposed assessments.

Procedures

The assessments were conducted over three pre-scheduled meetings. In the first session, carried out at the participant’s home, informed consent was obtained, and a clinical health questionnaire was completed to ensure compliance with the exclusion criteria. During the second session, performed in the morning at the Exercise Physiology Laboratory (LAFIEX) of UFVJM, body composition was measured after an 8-hour fast from both food and medication. Following 15 min of rest, participants received a standardized snack and subsequently performed the physical tests. In the third session, 24 h after the DXA assessment, blood samples were collected at the Inflammation and Metabolism Laboratory (LIM/UFVJM) after a 10-hour fast.

Anthropometrics assessment

The volunteers’ body mass was measured while they were fasting, using a digital scale (Welmy, model 110, precision of 0.1 kg and resolution of 0.1 kg) with an attached stadiometer (precision of 0.5 cm). The older women remained without shoes and wore light clothing. The body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing the body mass by the height squared (kg/m2).

Body composition assessment

Body composition was assessed using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA; model DPX, Lunar Radiation Corporation, Madison, WI, USA). Participants wore light clothing and removed all metallic objects prior to the scan. Standardized positioning was adopted, with the sagittal axis aligned to anatomical reference points (skull, spine, pelvis, and lower limbs). Velcro straps were used to stabilize the legs, knees, and feet to ensure proper alignment. The parameters extracted for analysis were appendicular lean mass (ALM) and total body fat percentage (%). All assessments were performed in the morning (8:00 a.m.) by the same trained evaluator, with participants in a total fasting state. Following the scan, participants rested for 15 min and received a standardized snack before proceeding to the subsequent assessments3.

Muscle strength assessment

Handgrip strength (HGS) was assessed using a Jamar® dynamometer. This measurement was in kilograms of force (kgf), using an isometric contraction with the dominant hand applied to the dynamometer handles. The participants were instructed to remain seated, with her shoulder in neutral position, her elbow at 90 degrees flexion, and her wrist in neutral position3. Standardized verbal commands were offered to stimulate greater handgrip force production by the participant, and three measurements were taken, and the average was used for analysis.

Muscle-specific strength assessment

MSS was calculated by normalizing HGS to ALM, providing an estimate of muscle quality27. HGS was measured in kilograms-force (kgf) and ALM in kilograms (kg). The MSS value was obtained by dividing HGS by ALM using the following formula: MSS = HGS/ALM. This measure offers a more accurate indicator of strength per unit of muscle mass and reflects the functional muscle capacity13,14,27.

Functional assessment

Physical function was assessed using the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) and the 6-meter gait speed (GS) test. The SPPB evaluates lower-extremity function through three components: static balance, habitual gait speed, and lower limb muscle strength. Each component was scored from 0 to 4, and the total SPPB score ranged from 0 to 12, with higher values indicating better physical performance. Performance categories were defined as follows: 0–3 (poor), 4–6 (low), 7–9 (moderate), and 10–12 (good). A cutoff of ≤ 8 points was used to identify low physical performance3,10.

Gait speed was also assessed independently using the 6-meter walk test. Participants were instructed to walk at their usual pace along a straight walkway. To ensure reproducibility, a 2-meter acceleration zone and a 2-meter deceleration zone were established before and after the central 6-meter timed section, minimizing the influence of start/stop effects. Time was recorded with a digital stopwatch, and gait speed was calculated in meters per second (m/s)3,10.

Biomarker analysis

On the third day of assessments, blood samples were drawn at 8 a.m. (10 mL from the antecubital fossa of the upper limb using disposable materials) after participants fasted from food, liquid, and medication for 10 h. The samples were collected in vacutainer tubes with heparin under sterile conditions. Immediately after collection, the samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting plasma was extracted and stored at -80 °C for six months before analysis.

Plasma levels of IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-10, TNF, adiponectin, leptin, resistin, BDNF, sTNFr-1, and sTNFr-2 were analyzed using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) technique (Duo-Set, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA). While plasma levels of IL-6, IL-8, and IFN were measured using the cytometric bead arrays kit (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The samples were acquired with a FACSCanto flow cytometer (BD Bioscience) and analyzed using FCAP array v1.0.1 software (Soft Flow).

Sample classification

The sample was evaluated according to the Global Leadership Initiative on Sarcopenia (GLIS) recommendations. As there is currently no established cutoff point for defining sarcopenia based on MSS, a percentile-based approach was adopted. This method was chosen due to the absence of reference values or diagnostic thresholds for MSS in existing literature. Descriptive frequency distributions of MSS values were analyzed, and the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles were used to classify participants into four groups based on MSS. The cutoffs were defined as follows: (1) very low MSS: ≤1.13, (2) low MSS: >1.13 to 1.42, (3) intermediate MSS: >1.42 to 1.65, and (4) high MSS: ≥1.66.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistic 22 software and G*Power version 3.1. The sample size was estimated based on data from a previous study that investigated the association between plasma adiponectin levels and muscle mass in older women (R² = 0.14)21. Assuming an effect size of 0.16, a significance level (α) of 0.05, a statistical power of 0.95, and three potential predictors in the regression model, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 64 participants. To account for an anticipated 10% attrition rate, the final estimated sample size was 71 participants. Data normality was verified using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables were described using mean and standard deviation (± SD) or minimum and maximum values [min-max], depending on data distribution. The Kruskal–Wallis or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc, depending on the distribution of the variables, were used to compare the mean differences of continuous variables between groups.

Spearman’s correlation (ρ) analysis was performed to verify the relationship between MSS and biomarkers. Univariate and stepwise multivariate linear regression were used to identify the determinants of MSS. In each univariate model adjusted for age and % of fat was made, and variables correlated with MSS in the univariate analysis (ρ < 0.1 and p < 0.05) were included. Four assumptions were employed in the linear regression analysis: linearity, residual distribution, homoscedasticity, and the absence of multicollinearity. Scatter plots were used to assess the linearity of the independent variables and residuals, and a histogram was used to verify the distribution of residuals. The scatter plot confirmed homoscedasticity, which was defined by the evenly distributed residuals in regression line. The variance inflation factor (VIF) values below 10.0 were used to define the absence of multicollinearity. Finally, the autocorrelation of the variables was verified by the Durbin–Watson test and the values between 1.5 and 2.5 showed that there was no autocorrelation in the data. Statistical significance was set at 5%.

Results

A total of 411 older women were identified through registration records at the Basic Health Units (BHU). However, 110 addresses were unavailable, either due to relocation or because they could not be located, and 31 individuals did not meet the minimum age criterion of 65 years. Thus, 270 older women were interviewed in their homes. Of these, 114 did not meet the inclusion or exclusion criteria, resulting in a final sample of 156 older women eligible for the evaluation procedures. Among the 156 participants, 85 did not participate in blood sample collection, leaving a final sample of 71 (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of sample recruitment. BHU = basic health units.

The mean (± SD) age of the participants was 75 ± 7 years, with a mean body weight of 59 ± 11 kg, height of 1.49 ± 0.05 m, and BMI of 26 ± 4 kg/m². Mean handgrip strength was 20 ± 6 kgf, appendicular lean mass (ALM) was 14 ± 2 kg, and muscle-specific strength (MSS) was 1.37 ± 0.35. Participants with very low MSS did not significantly differ from those with normal MSS in terms of age, BMI, body fat percentage, ALM, gait speed, or SPPB scores. However, significative differences were found in their inflammatory profile, as plasma concentrations of IL-5, IL-8, and sTNFr-2 were significantly elevated in the very low MSS group. When MSS was examined as the dependent variable, in models adjusted for age and body fat percentage, higher concentrations of IL-5 were strongly associated with lower MSS, as indicated by the unstandardized coefficient (b = − 0.42, p < 0.001). This value means that for each unit increase in IL-5 concentration, MSS decreased by 0.42 units, representing the strongest biomarker effect and explaining 19% of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.19). IL-6 also showed a significant negative association with MSS (b = − 0.02, p = 0.03), meaning that each unit increase in IL-6 was related to a 0.02-unit reduction in MSS, with an adjusted R² of 0.15.

In contrast, IL-8 demonstrated no meaningful association (b = − 0.003, p = 0.57), indicating that changes in IL-8 had a negligible effect on MSS, accounting for only 9% of the variance. Finally, sTNFr-2 showed a moderate inverse association with MSS, with a standardized coefficient of β = −0.34 (p = 0.007) and an adjusted R² of 0.18, indicating that higher sTNFr-2 levels were independently associated with lower MSS, even though the unstandardized coefficient was close to zero due to the biomarker’s measurement scale. Overall, increases in IL-5, IL-6 and sTNFr-2 were associated with reductions in MSS (Table 3).

A stepwise multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to identify the best predictors of MSS, with MSS as the dependent variable and age, body fat percentage, and the plasma concentrations of IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, and sTNFr-2 as independent variables. In the final adjusted model, sTNFr-2 and IL-5 remained as significant predictors of MSS, collectively explaining 20% of its variance [β (SE) = -0.35; b (SE) = -0.26; R = 0.47; adjusted R² = 0.20; F = 5.67; p = 0.02].

The unstandardized coefficient (b = − 0.26) indicates that, for each one-unit increase in the combined effect of these biomarkers, MSS is expected to decrease by 0.26 units, holding all other variables constant. This value expresses the association in the original measurement scale of MSS, allowing a direct interpretation of how changes in biomarker concentrations translate into changes in MSS. The corresponding standardized coefficient (β = −0.35) reinforces that this combined inflammatory profile has a moderate inverse impact on MSS.

Discussion

In this study, levels of inflammatory mediators were associated with muscle-specific strength in community-dwelling older women. Participants with very low MSS exhibited significantly higher plasma concentrations of IL-5, IL-8, and sTNFr-2, even though age, BMI, body fat percentage, ALM, gait speed, and SPPB scores did not differ between groups. The correlation analyses demonstrated significant negative associations between MSS and age, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, and sTNFr-2. Moreover, IL-5 and sTNFr-2 remained in the final regression model, explaining approximately 20% of the variance in MSS, and indicates that each unit increase in the combined concentrations of IL-5 and sTNFr-2 corresponds to a reduction of 0.26 in MSS, reinforcing the meaningful impact of these inflammatory pathways on muscle function in older women.

With the new recommendations from the Global Leadership in Sarcopenia Studies (GLIS), measures such as the SPPB and gait speed are now considered consequences of sarcopenia rather than diagnostic components4. In this context, the assessment of MSS has emerged as a promising indicator for evaluating sarcopenia in older adults4,13. In the present study, no significant associations were observed between MSS and the physical performance tests. This finding suggests that the development of sarcopenia, characterized by low MSS, may primarily reflect the force generated per unit of muscle mass and may not directly capture functional domains such as agility, mobility, and balance, which are essential for the execution of tests like gait speed and the SPPB. Importantly, these results also raise the possibility that declines in MSS may precede physical-functional impairments, being accompanied instead by alterations in the systemic inflammatory environment, characterized by elevated concentrations of pro-inflammatory markers such as IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, and sTNFr-216,23,24,28,29.

TNF-α is an inflammatory biomarker that interacts with its receptors, including sTNFr-2, to modulate several inflammatory responses30. The sTNFr-2 is an important inflammatory mediator that may modulate, neutralize, or extend the effects of TNF-α30. There is already evidence that suppression of sTNFr-2 contributes to an inflammatory environment unfavorable to the formation and progression of tumors and metastases in some types of cancer31. In clinical research, sTNFr-2 is frequently associated with inflammation in chronic conditions and has been studied in various populations, including women with fibromyalgia32, older adults with chronic low back pain33, knee osteoarthritis34, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease35, sarcopenia19, respiratory sarcopenia36 and sarcopenic obesity25 in older person.

Especially in sarcopenia, a previous study conducted by our research group demonstrated that plasma concentrations of sTNFr-2 above 2280 pg/mL are effective in discriminating older women with sarcopenia, as diagnosed by the EWGSOP2 algorithm26. Elevated sTNFr-2 levels may reflect an exacerbated inflammatory response, which is closely associated with muscle catabolism and the progression of sarcopenia in older adults19,37. Therefore, it is not surprising that higher plasma sTNFr-2 concentrations are associated with worse MSS in our results.

Traditionally, IL-5 is a cytokine that plays a crucial role in regulating the production and activation of eosinophils, which are important cells in the allergic response and in some chronic inflammatory conditions38–40. Although L-5 is mainly associated with allergic diseases, such as asthma and allergic rhinitis, and may be related to certain parasitic infections38,40, is also involved in the regulation of the inflammatory response, and its alterations may influence chronic inflammatory conditions, as it can promote the activation and infiltration of immune cells, such as eosinophils, into skeletal muscle tissue41.

Long-term eosinophilia may result in tissue damage by mechanisms that are not fully understood38. The eosinophils participate in the modulation of hypersensitivity reactions related to the degradation or deactivation of mediators released by mast cells, such as histamine, leukotrienes and heparin, which alter blood viscosity and the vasodilation state of tissue blood vessels, consequently affecting blood supply and adequate tissue perfusion38,40,42. Therefore, our results allow us to speculate that the association found between IL-5 and MSS can involve vascular phenomena of the blood supply to skeletal muscle affecting MSS, through specific mechanisms that remain unknown.

In addition to acting on eosinophils and their precursors, IL-5 plays a crucial role in the production of IgA in human mucosal tissues, which contributes to adequate tissue remodeling38,42. In the blood, IgA interacts with the FcαRI (or CD89)-like receptor expressed on effector immune cells to initiate inflammatory responses42. However, direct evidence of IL-5 in sarcopenia is less established compared to other cytokines such as adiponectin, TNF-α and IL-622,43,44.

Although the relationship between sTNFr-2 and IL-5 is not widely documented in the literature, is possible to discuss some of its aspects to understand how they may interact in the context of chronic inflammation40,45. Both are involved in inflammatory processes, although in different contexts30,38. There is no clear direct interaction between them, but both may coexist in chronic inflammatory environments17,45. For example, in conditions where there is systemic inflammation, there may be a co-regulation of different inflammatory cytokines and receptors45,46. In a chronic inflammatory environment, such as in sarcopenia, the increase in sTNFr-2 may be associated with the modulation of other cytokines, including IL-5, although IL-5 is not primarily related to sarcopenia.

In our analysis, IL-5 concentrations were associated with MSS, and the best association model indicated that IL-5 and sTNFr-2 explained approximately 20% of the variations in MSS in this sample. These results allow us to speculate that IL-5 and sTNFr-2 may be interrelated, exacerbating the negative effects on muscle mass and strength, amplifying the inflammatory response and consequently the loss of MSS observed in sarcopenia16,17,30,38,45. Furthermore, they can influence remodeling, adequate perfusion, and the supply of nutrients and oxygen to the functional unit of skeletal muscle, reinforcing potential vascular mechanisms contributes to pathophysiology of sarcopenia38,47. However, advances in research are needed to explore and clarify the role of IL-5 and its relationship with vascular mechanisms that contribute to sarcopenia characterized by low MSS.

Since its identification as a myokine, IL-6 has been widely explored for its role in the pathogenesis and diagnostics of sarcopenia43,48–50. Some studies indicate that IL-6 is significantly increased in older with sarcopenia43,50. However, our results agree with Chen et al. (2024), who suggested that although increased, IL-6 is not associated with indicators of sarcopenia in the older, disagreeing with the hypothesis of a causal factor51. Likewise, IL-8, a pro-inflammatory mediator that promotes neutrophil chemotaxis to inflammatory sites, which despite being increased in sarcopenia, was not significantly associated with MSS in these analyses16,17,19.

Since it is already known that inflammation is part of the pathophysiology of sarcopenia16,17, it is important that the evaluation of inflammatory aspects be considered for these patients. Either as a diagnostic element and monitoring of the course of the disease, or to understand the pathophysiology of muscle dysfunction and catabolism in these patients23,24. It is important to expand the understanding of a disease considered multifaceted and multifactorial, and that can involve structural aspects of muscle tissue, the neuromuscular function and adequate blood supply18,23,47.

As with other studies involving sarcopenia and biomarkers, these results should be interpreted with caution and considered initial, as they have several limitations. A cross-sectional design does not allow inferences of cause and effect between the variables. The isokinetic assessment, which is the gold standard for assessing muscle strength, was not available. The sample size is modest, so these investigations should be replicated in nationally representative samples. The sample consisted exclusively of women, which can be considered a weakness and a strength of these analyses, since inflammation in aging is different between men and women52. However, we provide a comprehensive view of the relationship of MSS and inflammatory mediators, can be useful for future research and clinical practice aimed to prevent and understand the mechanisms involved in loss of MSS or sarcopenia in older women. Finally, this study presents important strengths, including a narrow and methodologically robust design, the use of high-accuracy instruments for assessing body composition (ALM and body fat percentage), and the systematic evaluation of inflammatory parameters, all of which contribute to the methodological rigor and reliability of our findings.

Conclusion

This study offers a comprehensive understanding of the relationship between MSS and inflammatory mediators in community-dwelling older women, highlighting IL-5 and sTNFr-2 as significant biomarkers associated with reduced MSS. Beyond reinforcing the biological plausibility linking chronic low-grade inflammation to declines in muscle function, these findings underscore the potential clinical value of incorporating inflammatory profiling into strategies for early detection, monitoring, and risk stratification of sarcopenia. Importantly, studies of this nature align with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals 3 (Good Health and Well-Being) and 10 (Reduced Inequalities), as they promote early identification of vulnerability in older populations, support actions aimed at reducing health disparities, and provide evidence to guide public health strategies that foster functional longevity and improved quality of life.
