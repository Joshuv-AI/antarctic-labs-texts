Targeted facet joint surface remodeling for lumbar facet syndrome: A retrospective case series
Authors: Nikou Nikmaram, Keyvan Mostofi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Lumbar facet joint syndrome is a common cause of chronic axial low back pain. Current interventional treatments such as medial branch radiofrequency denervation may provide temporary relief, with frequent symptom recurrence. Direct surgical treatment of the facet joint surface itself has rarely been reported.Objective:To evaluate the clinical outcomes of targeted facet joint surface remodeling in patients with lumbar facet joint syndrome.Materials and Methods:This retrospective monocentric case series included 32 patients treated between January 2022 and March 2025. All patients presented with chronic axial low back pain and a positive response to diagnostic facet joint injection. The surgical procedure consisted of targeted facet joint surface shaving and selective osteophyte removal, performed through an open or endoscopic approach, without decompression or fusion. Pain and functional outcomes were assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) preoperatively, and at 1, 3, 6, and 9 months postoperatively.Results:The cohort included 18 women and 14 men, with ages ranging from 39 to 69 years. Mean preoperative VAS was 7.34 and improved to 4.12 at 1 month, 3.99 at 3 months, 3.95 at 6 months, and 3.92 at 9 months postoperatively. Mean ODI decreased from 39 preoperatively to 24 at 1 month, 21 at 3 months, 20 at 6 months, and 19 at 9 months. No intraoperative or postoperative complications were observed.Conclusion:Targeted facet joint surface remodeling appears to be a safe and effective motion-preserving surgical option for carefully selected patients with lumbar facet joint syndrome. This proof-of-concept study demonstrates encouraging short-to mid-term clinical outcomes and supports further prospective evaluation.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic low back pain is one of the leading causes of disability worldwide and represents a major public health and socioeconomic burden.[1] Among its various etiologies, lumbar facet joint syndrome is a well-recognized source of axial low back pain, particularly in patients with degenerative changes of the zygapophyseal joints.[23] These posterior spinal joints play a crucial role in segmental stability and motion and are richly innervated by nociceptive fibers, making them a frequent pain generator in degenerative spinal conditions.[4] The diagnosis of lumbar facet joint pain remains challenging and is based on a combination of clinical features, imaging findings, and, most importantly, the response to diagnostic facet joint injections, which are considered the most reliable method to identify facet-mediated pain.[56] Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may demonstrate facet joint effusion, hypertrophy, osteophyte formation, and inflammatory changes; however, imaging findings alone correlate poorly with symptoms and must be interpreted in conjunction with clinical and diagnostic data.[26] Current treatment strategies for lumbar facet joint syndrome include conservative management, intra-articular injections, and medial branch radiofrequency denervation.[78] While radiofrequency ablation has been shown to provide significant pain relief in selected patients, its effectiveness is often time-limited, with symptom recurrence commonly reported within 6–12 months, frequently necessitating repeat procedures.[89] Surgical treatment for isolated facet-mediated pain remains controversial, and most surgical interventions indirectly address facet pathology by limiting motion or treating associated conditions. In contrast, direct surgical treatment of the facet joint surface itself has rarely been described. Targeted facet joint surface remodeling, consisting of limited articular surface shaving and selective osteophyte removal without decompression or fusion, aims to reduce mechanical micro-conflicts and intra-articular inflammation while preserving physiological segmental mobility.[34] The purpose of the present study is to report our retrospective experience with targeted facet joint surface remodeling in a cohort of patients with lumbar facet joint syndrome, to evaluate short-term clinical outcomes at 1 and 3 months postoperatively, and to present this technique as a proof of concept for a motion-preserving surgical approach in carefully selected patients. To date, very few studies have specifically explored direct surgical treatment of the facet joint surface in the absence of instability or neural compression. This highlights the need to investigate alternative approaches targeting the structural origin of facet-mediated pain.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective monocentric observational study was conducted between January 2022 and March 2025. The study was approved by the institutional review board, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients for the use of their clinical data for research and publication purposes. All data were anonymized prior to analysis. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they presented with chronic axial low back pain lasting more than 6 months, a clinical presentation consistent with lumbar facet joint syndrome, a positive response to diagnostic facet joint injection defined as significant pain relief during standing or walking, and failure of conservative treatment including analgesics and physical therapy. Patients were excluded in cases of typical radiculopathy or neurological deficit, severe degenerative disc disease associated with sciatica, symptomatic lumbar spinal canal or foraminal stenosis, indication for decompression or fusion surgery, or involvement of more than three symptomatic lumbar levels. The importance of strict patient selection based on clinical and diagnostic criteria has been emphasized in previous studies addressing facet-mediated pain.[56] A total of 32 patients were included, comprising 18 women and 14 men, with ages ranging from 39 to 69 years. The duration of symptoms ranged from 7 months to 5 years. Among these patients, 28 had a positive diagnostic facet joint injection, and 17 had previously undergone medial branch radiofrequency denervation with recurrence of pain within 6 to 12 months. Ten patients presented with disc bulging on imaging without associated radicular symptoms. All patients underwent preoperative lumbar MRI [Figure 1]. Radiological findings supporting the diagnosis of facet joint pathology included facet joint effusion, facet hypertrophy with osteophyte formation, and inflammatory signal changes of the facet joints, in the absence of significant disc–nerve root conflict. Although imaging findings alone are insufficient for diagnosis, MRI was used to support clinical suspicion and to exclude alternative pain generators.[26] Surgical procedures were performed under general anesthesia [Figure 2]. Twenty-seven patients were treated using an open posterior approach through a midline incision, while five patients underwent a minimally invasive endoscopic procedure using two paramedian incisions. In the endoscopic group, three patients were operated at the L4–L5 level and two at L5–S1. In the open surgery group, fifteen patients underwent two-level procedures, including nine patients at L4–L5 and L5–S1 and six patients at L3–L4 and L4–L5. Patients requiring three-level surgery were excluded from the study. The surgical technique consisted of a targeted remodeling of the facet joint articular surfaces. After exposure of the posterior elements, the facet joints were identified and inspected. Selective shaving of the degenerated articular surfaces was performed using a high-speed burr, combined with careful removal of marginal osteophytes when present. The procedure was strictly limited to the articular surfaces and periarticular osteophytes, without opening the interlaminar space or addressing the spinal canal or neural foramina. Particular care was taken to preserve the facet joint capsule as much as possible in order to maintain segmental stability. No decompression, foraminotomy, discectomy, or fusion was performed. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for pain intensity and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) for functional impairment, both of which are validated tools widely used in the assessment of chronic low back pain.[1011] Assessments were performed preoperatively and at 1, 3, 6, and 9 months postoperatively. Given the descriptive nature of this retrospective case series, statistical analysis was limited to descriptive statistics, with continuous variables reported as means and ranges. No comparative or inferential statistical testing was performed.

RESULTS

A total of 32 patients were included in the study. The cohort consisted of 18 women (56.3%) and 14 men (43.7%), with ages ranging from 39 to 69 years. All patients presented with predominant axial low back pain consistent with lumbar facet joint syndrome, with a symptom duration ranging from 7 months to 5 years. Preoperatively, 28 patients (87.5%) demonstrated a positive response to diagnostic facet joint injection, defined as significant pain relief during standing or walking. Seventeen patients (53.1%) had previously undergone medial branch radiofrequency denervation, with recurrence of pain within 6 to 12 months. Lumbar disc bulging without radicular symptoms was observed in 10 patients (31.3%). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Regarding the surgical approach, 27 patients were treated using an open posterior midline approach, while 5 patients underwent a minimally invasive endoscopic procedure. In the endoscopic group, three patients were operated at the L4–L5 level and two at L5–S1. In the open surgery group, 15 patients underwent two-level procedures, including nine patients at L4–L5 and L5–S1 and six patients at L3–L4 and L4–L5. No patient required a three-level procedure, as these cases were excluded according to the study criteria. Surgical approaches and operated levels are detailed in Table 2. Pain intensity, assessed using the VAS, showed a clinically meaningful improvement following surgery. The mean preoperative VAS score was 7.34 (range 5–9). At 1 month postoperatively, the mean VAS score decreased to 4.12. This improvement was maintained at 3 months (mean VAS 3.99), 6 months (mean VAS 3.95), and 9 months (mean VAS 3.92). Functional outcomes, measured using the ODI, also demonstrated significant postoperative improvement. The mean preoperative ODI score was 39 (range 26–45). At 1 month postoperatively, the mean ODI score improved to 24, with further improvement observed at 3 months (mean ODI 21), 6 months (mean ODI 20), and 9 months (mean ODI 19). Specifically, there were no neurological deficits, no surgical site infections, and no need for early revision surgery. All patients completed the scheduled follow-up at 1, 3, 6, and 9 months.

DISCUSSION

The present study reports a retrospective case series evaluating targeted facet joint surface remodeling as a surgical treatment for lumbar facet joint syndrome. The results demonstrate a clinically meaningful reduction in pain intensity and functional disability at short-term follow-up in a carefully selected patient population, with no observed perioperative complications. Lumbar facet joints are true synovial joints and a well-established source of axial low back pain due to their rich nociceptive innervation and susceptibility to degenerative changes. Degenerative facet arthropathy is characterized by cartilage degeneration, osteophyte formation, joint surface irregularities, and inflammatory synovitis, all of which may contribute to chronic pain through mechanical micro-conflicts and persistent nociceptive stimulation. The surgical concept evaluated in this study directly targets the presumed pain generator by selectively smoothing degenerated articular surfaces and removing marginal osteophytes, thereby reducing abnormal mechanical stress and intra-articular inflammation. Unlike denervation techniques, this approach preserves neural pathways and aims to maintain physiological segmental motion. Medial branch radiofrequency denervation remains one of the most commonly used treatments for facet-mediated pain. Although its short-term efficacy has been reported, symptom recurrence within 6 to 12 months is frequent, often necessitating repeated procedures. In addition to medial branch radiofrequency denervation, several interventional and surgical strategies have been described for the management of facet-mediated pain in similar clinical scenarios. Intra-articular facet joint injections with corticosteroids may provide short-term relief, although their long-term efficacy remains limited and inconsistent across studies. Repeated injections are often required, raising concerns regarding cumulative effects and diminishing returns. Minimally invasive techniques such as endoscopic facet joint debridement or partial facetectomy have been reported in selected cases, primarily in association with foraminal stenosis or nerve root compression. However, these approaches are generally not designed to specifically address isolated facet-mediated axial pain and may involve partial destabilization of the segment. More extensive surgical options, including spinal fusion, indirectly address facet joint pathology by eliminating motion at the affected level. While fusion may be effective in selected patients with instability or advanced degeneration, it is associated with higher morbidity and the risk of adjacent segment disease, making it less suitable for isolated facet joint syndrome without instability. In this context, targeted facet joint surface remodeling represents a more conservative and motion-preserving alternative, aiming to directly treat the articular source of pain while avoiding denervation or fusion. By focusing on selective surface regularization and osteophyte removal, this technique may bridge the gap between purely interventional treatments and more invasive surgical procedures. In the present cohort, more than half of the patients had previously undergone radiofrequency denervation with subsequent recurrence of pain, highlighting the need for alternative strategies. In contrast to denervation, targeted facet joint surface remodeling addresses the structural substrate of pain rather than pain transmission alone. The sustained clinical improvement observed up to 9 months suggests that modifying the articular surface itself may offer a more durable benefit, although longer follow-up is required to confirm this hypothesis. Clinically, mean VAS scores decreased from 7.34 preoperatively to 3.99 at 3 months, while mean ODI scores improved from 39 to 21. These improvements exceed commonly accepted thresholds for minimal clinically important differences in chronic low back pain populations. Importantly, no neurological complications, infections, or early revision surgeries were observed, suggesting that the procedure is safe when performed in appropriately selected patients. Careful patient selection appears to be a key factor in achieving favorable outcomes. All included patients presented with predominant axial low back pain, a positive diagnostic facet joint injection, and imaging findings consistent with facet joint pathology, without significant radiculopathy, instability, or indication for decompression or fusion surgery. Patients requiring multilevel or destabilizing procedures were intentionally excluded, reinforcing the concept that this technique is intended for a specific and well-defined clinical scenario. This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design and relatively small sample size limit the generalizability of the findings, and the short follow-up period does not allow assessment of long-term durability or recurrence of symptoms. The absence of a control group prevents direct comparison with other interventional or surgical treatments, and the inclusion of both open and endoscopic approaches introduces some heterogeneity, although the underlying surgical principle was identical across techniques. Despite these limitations, this case series provides preliminary evidence supporting targeted facet joint surface remodeling as a safe, motion-preserving surgical option for lumbar facet joint syndrome. By directly addressing the presumed facet joint pain generator through limited articular surface shaving and selective osteophyte removal, this approach represents a novel alternative to purely denervative or motion-limiting strategies. These findings support its role as a proof of concept and justify further prospective comparative studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up to better define its indications and long-term effectiveness.

CONCLUSION

Targeted facet joint surface remodeling appears to be a safe and effective motion-preserving surgical option for carefully selected patients with lumbar facet joint syndrome. In this retrospective case series, the procedure was associated with a clinically meaningful reduction in pain intensity and functional disability at short-term follow-up, with no observed perioperative complications. These results suggest that directly addressing degenerative facet joint surfaces may alleviate axial low back pain while preserving segmental mobility. By focusing on limited articular surface shaving and selective osteophyte removal, this technique offers a novel alternative to purely denervative or motion-limiting strategies. Although the present findings are preliminary and limited by the retrospective design and short follow-up, they support the concept of targeted facet joint remodeling as a potential surgical option in a well-defined patient population. Further prospective comparative studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are warranted to better define its indications, durability, and long-term clinical effectiveness.
