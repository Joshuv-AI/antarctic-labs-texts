Expanding the genetic spectrum of autosomal recessive microcephaly in Pakistani families
Authors: Bilal Ahmad, Matthew Adams, Iram Javed, Muhammad Tariq, Anees Muhammad, Laura Harrold, Rebecca J. Almond, Nishanka Ubeyratna, Muhammad Tahir Sarwar, Joseph S. Leslie, Andrew H. Crosby, Emma L. Baple, Lettie E. Rawlins, Muhammad Qasim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAutosomal recessive microcephaly encompasses a group of rare neurogenetic disorders in which microcephaly presents at birth or postnatally as part of a syndromic disorder. Autosomal recessive microcephaly is both clinically and genetically heterogeneous, with numerous genes associated. In the present study, we investigated the genetic basis of likely autosomal recessive microcephaly in a cohort of five unrelated consanguineous and non-consanguineous Pakistani families presenting with microcephaly, intellectual disability, and developmental delay.MethodsWhole-exome sequencing was performed on one affected individual from each family. Candidate variants identified by WES were subsequently validated by Sanger sequencing, and segregation analysis was performed in all available affected and unaffected family members.ResultsWe identified five homozygous pathogenic variants, including three novel variants: ASPM (NM_018136.5:c.1669_1670del p.(Ser557Leufs*2)), CDK5RAP2 (NM_018249.6:c.199del p.(Ile67Serfs*4)), and VPS13B (NM_152564.5:c.8230 C > T (p.Gln2744*)). Alongside, two previously reported homozygous variants in ASPM (NM_018136.5:c.9190 C > T p.(Arg3064*) & NM_018136.5:c.3978G > A p.(Trp1326*)). All variants matched a suspected autosomal recessive inheritance pattern, segregated within their respective families, and were absent or very rare in proxy population genetic databases.ConclusionThe results expand the genetic spectrum of autosomal recessive microcephaly within the Pakistani population and highlight the importance of whole exome sequencing in diagnosing rare neurodevelopmental disorders and understanding genetic diversity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-05027-9.

Introduction

Autosomal recessive primary microcephaly (MCPH) is a rare inherited congenital disorder affecting brain growth. MCPH is defined by an occipito-frontal circumference (OFC) more than or equal to 3 standard deviations (SD) below the mean at birth, due to reduced cerebral cortical development [1]. Affected individuals typically exhibit simplified gyral patterns, reduced cerebral cortical volume, and varying degrees of intellectual disability, language delay, and motor impairment [2]. Genetically, MCPH is highly diverse and most often results from biallelic loss-of-function variants in genes involved in neurogenesis, cell cycle progression, centrosome integrity, and DNA repair [3].

To date, more than thirty genes have been implicated in the etiology of MCPH, with ASPM (abnormal spindle-like, microcephaly-associated) being the most frequently reported [4]. ASPM alone accounts for approximately 40% of MCPH cases and is highly expressed in the ventricular zone during fetal brain development [5]. Biallelic pathogenic variants in CDK5RAP2 (cyclin-dependent kinase 5 regulatory subunit-associated protein 2) are also associated with MCPH, although these disorders are comparatively rarer than the ASPM-associated primary microcephaly 5 (MCPH5) [6]. These genes play critical roles in regulating mitotic spindle dynamics and maintaining the symmetric division of neural progenitors during corticogenesis. CDK5RAP2 is essential for centrosomal cohesion, centriole duplication, and microtubule stabilization [7]. Disruption of these mechanisms leads to reduced neuronal output, either through premature differentiation or apoptosis of neural stem cells [8].

However, MCPH is not the only group of disorders in which microcephaly is a feature. There are numerous further disorders in which microcephaly presents as part of a syndromic presentation. Biallelic pathogenic VPS13B (vacuolar protein sorting 13 homolog B) variants, for example, are classically associated with Cohen syndrome, which is characterized by post-natal acquired microcephaly, intellectual disability, retinal degeneration, and neutropenia [9]. Although alongside this syndromic presentation, there have also been reports of isolated forms of microcephaly [10].

Recent studies have further expanded the genetic spectrum of these disorders. Erdogan et al. described four novel frameshift variants in CDK5RAP2 [11], while Almeida et al. reported novel ASPM variants in siblings affected by MCPH [12]. Such discoveries underscore the continuing identification of pathogenic alleles underlying this disorder. Whole exome sequencing (WES) has proven to be a powerful approach for detecting candidate variants, particularly in consanguineous populations where recessive inheritance is common [13]. Despite these advances, South Asian cohorts remain underrepresented in genetic research, leading to reduced rates of definitive diagnosis, limiting access to further genomic healthcare services, which exacerbates healthcare inequalities [14]. Nearly 60% of marriages in Pakistan are consanguineous unions, leading to a high prevalence of autosomal recessive conditions, and facilitating the identification of novel pathogenic variants and further delineation of recurrent pathogenic variants in specific ethnic groups [15].

In the present study, five Pakistani families comprising multiple individuals with primary microcephaly underwent genomic studies with WES and segregation analysis to identify disease-associated causative variants. Three novel and two previously reported biallelic pathogenic variants in ASPM, CDK5RAP2, and VPS13B were identified, expanding the genetic landscape of primary microcephaly in the Pakistani population.

Methods

Ethical approval and consent to participate

Study protocols received approval from the Ethical Review Committee of Government College University Faisalabad, Pakistan (Approval No. GCUF/ERC/307), and from the Ethical Committee and Advanced Studies and Research Board at Khyber Medical University, Pakistan (IRB00013852). Written informed consent was obtained from all participating individuals or their legal guardians before their inclusion in the study. For participants younger than sixteen years of age, informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians. All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Families were recruited through local clinicians, who conducted a detailed clinical assessment.

Clinical assessment

Clinical evaluation comprised recording detailed pedigrees and medical histories for all family members. Local clinicians carried out comprehensive clinical and neurological examinations of affected individuals, including OFC measurement. Blood samples were taken by trained phlebotomists.

Whole-exome sequencing, variant annotation, and filtering

Whole exome sequencing was performed on one affected individual from each family. Candidate variants identified by WES were subsequently validated by Sanger sequencing, and segregation analysis was performed in all available affected and unaffected family members.

Initially whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on one affected individual from each family (NovaSeq X; Illumina, Twist Human Core Exome targeting). Analysis involved read alignment (Sentieon BWA-MEM v202308.02) to human reference genome assembly GRCh38, duplicates removed and base quality recalibration (Sentieon v202308.02), single nucleotide variant and InDel detection (Sentieon DNAseq v202308.02), variant annotation (VEP v113), and read depth assessment (GATK 3.8 Depth of Coverage). Copy number variants (CNVs) were detected using SavvyCNV. For all platforms, a minimum read depth and coverage of 20x was achieved for 95–98% of the exome. Variants with 1% in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD v3.1.2) were excluded (Figure S1). Candidate variants identified by WES were subsequently validated by Sanger sequencing, and segregation analysis was performed in all available affected and unaffected family members. The pathogenicity of the detected variations was assessed using the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria and Association for Clinical Genomic Science (ACGS) guidelines [16, 17]. The ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) database was also checked to support the clinical relevance of the discovered variations.

Results

Clinical findings

Five families (11 affected individuals) with likely autosomal recessive microcephaly were recruited from the Punjab and Pashtun population of Pakistan (Fig. 1). All affected individuals had microcephaly with OFC measurement − 3 SD below the mean, accompanied by global developmental delay and intellectual disability ranging from mild to severe. Additional features included facial dysmorphism, behavioral problems, and seizures. Detailed clinical evaluation for families 1–5 is described in Table 1 and Supplementary data 1.

Genetic findings

WES in families 1–5 identified five homozygous variants in three genes as the likely cause of MCPH (Table 1). In families 1–3, homozygous pathogenic truncating variants in ASPM were identified as the cause of microcephaly in these families, confirming a diagnosis of MCPH5. This included one novel (NM_018136.5:c.1669_1670del p.(Ser557Leufs*2)), and two previously reported (NM_018136.5: c.9190 C > T p.(Arg3064*), NM_018136.5:c.3978G > A p.(Trp1326*)) variants. A novel homozygous CDK5RAP2 frameshift variant (NM_018249.6:c.199del p.(Ile67Serfs*4)) was identified as the cause of microcephaly in family 4, confirming a diagnosis of primary microcephaly 3 (MCPH3). A novel pathogenic nonsense variant in VPS13B (NM_152564.5:c.8230 C > T p.(Gln2744*)) was ascertained in family 5, and is associated with Cohen syndrome, in which microcephaly is a clinical feature. Segregation analysis using dideoxy sequencing confirmed these variants in probands and demonstrated co-segregation within families as expected for an autosomal recessive disorder (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Primary autosomal recessive microcephaly represents a heterogeneous group of rare neurodevelopmental disorders, clinically characterized by reduced head circumference, varying degrees of intellectual disability, and underlying defects in neurogenesis and neuronal growth [18]. In this investigation, we examined five consanguineous Pakistani families with multiple affected individuals and identified five pathogenic variants in ASPM, CDK5RAP2, and VPS13B, including two novel and three previously reported variants. Our findings highlight the importance of WES in expanding the mutational spectrum of MCPH-associated genes.

Affected individuals exhibited classical MCPH features [18]. Neuroimaging data were not available for all probands; however, the absence of major structural brain malformations in most individuals from families 1–4 is consistent with the classical definition of MCPH as a disorder of reduced neuronal production rather than gross brain malformation. However, the presence of additional neurological symptoms, such as seizures, points to potential overlaps with other neurodevelopmental pathways, underscoring the need for detailed phenotypic characterization alongside genetic testing [19].

ASPM encodes the 3477 amino acid protein Abnormal spindle-like microcephaly-associated (ASPM). ASPM is highly expressed in the ventricular zone during brain development, where it regulates mitotic spindle orientation and ensures the symmetric division of neural progenitor cells [20]. Loss-of-function variants in ASPM are well-established in cases of MCPH across populations worldwide [21]. Amongst MCPH cases, biallelic pathogenic ASPM variants are the most commonly ascertained, accounting for around ~ 40–60% of reported cases [22]. Three homozygous ASPM variants were identified in this study. In Family 1, a novel frameshift (NM_018136.4:c.1669_1670del p.(Ser557Leufs*2)) was ascertained as the likely cause of disease, whilst previously reported nonsense variants were identified in families 2 and 3 respectively (NM_018136.4:c.9190 C > T p.(Arg3064*), NM_018136.5:c.3978G > A, p.(Trp1326*)). All ASPM variants identified here are predicted to undergo nonsense-mediated decay, leading to loss of function. The ASPM p.(Ser557Leufs*2) was absent from proxy populational genomic database gnomAD (V4.1), whilst the p.(Arg3064*) variant and p.(Trp1326*) variant are present at low frequencies (35 heterozygotes, 0 homozygotes, allele frequency (AF) 0.00002171; 6 heterozygotes, 0 homozygotes, allele frequency (AF) 0.000003719, respectively).

Both the p.(Arg3064*) and p.(Trp1326*) ASPM variants have been previously reported in the Pakistani population in association with MCPH5 [23–27]. However, the p.(Arg3064*) variant has also been observed in association with MCPH5 in an Egyptian family and is harbored in a heterozygous state by individuals of both European and African/African American ancestries in gnomAD V4.1, potentially representing a recurrent variant across populations [28]. The p.(Trp1326*) ASPM variant is a known founder variant within the Pakistani population, with numerous unrelated families from a variety of provinces described across the literature. This variant has been observed in association with MCPH5 in both consanguineous and nonconsanguineous families, as observed in family 3, underscoring the p.(Trp1326*) frequency within the Pakistani population [25–27, 29].

All individuals with ASPM variants showed typical MCPH5 features. The seizures observed in both affected individuals from families 1 and 3 have been reported in MCPH5 previously. Interestingly, however, a large-scale review of MCPH5 cases in the literature observed no seizures before 6 months of age, whereas seizures were observed two days after birth in III:4 [30].

The novel frameshift variant in CDK5RAP2 (NM_018249.6:c.199del, p.(Ile67Serfs*4)) identified in family 3 underscores the gene’s essential role in centrosome cohesion, microtubule organization, and as a spindle checkpoint control [31]. Biallelic pathogenic variants in CDK5RAP2 are associated with MCPH3 [32, 33]. The CDK5RAP2 p.(Ile67Serfs*4) variant was absent from gnomAD V4.1 and had not been reported in ClinVar or HGMD Professional.

WES of consanguineous families has vastly expanded the genetic spectrum of MCPH3, identifying numerous novel loss-of-function variants [11, 34]. The presentation of family 3 is consistent with other MCPH3 cases described in the literature, typically presenting with severe microcephaly, mild-moderate intellectual disability, and non-distinct dysmorphic features [34].

ASPM and CDKRAP5 are expressed and function at the centrosome, underlying the crucial role centrosomal function plays in typical brain development [35, 36]. CDK5RAP2 is involved in cortical development as a pericentriolar material scaffolding protein during the G2 phase. Downregulation of CDK5RAP2 in mouse embryonic stem cells has shown reduced proliferation of undifferentiated cells, alongside mitotic spindle defects and disrupted γ-tubulin localization, which has been confirmed in MCPH3 patient-derived cells [37, 38].

Notably, ASPM has been shown to function redundantly with CDK5RAP2 during mitotic metaphase, playing a role in spindle pole organization [39]. Knockout of ASPM in mice is associated with decreased brain volume and cortical thickness [40]. Interestingly, further study of developing mouse brains shows no disruption of neural progenitor cell proliferation or aberration of recently generated neurons, instead observing increased apoptosis within the cerebral cortex throughout cortical development [41].

Biallelic pathogenic VPS13B variants are classically associated with Cohen syndrome but have also been described in cases of non-syndromic microcephaly. The novel VPS13B p.(Gln2744*) nonsense variant identified in this study is rare in gnomAD v4.1 (5 heterozygotes, 0 homozygotes, AF 0.000003098) and predicted to undergo nonsense-mediated decay. This variant has not been reported in prior literature.

Affected individuals in family 5 harboring the homozygous pathogenic VPS13B variant (NM_152564.4:c.8230 C > T, p.(Gln2744*)), presented with microcephaly, intellectual disability, and hypotonia but lacked many common features of Cohen syndrome, such as the typical progressive visual deterioration. This lack of retinal involvement may reflect the wide heterogeneity in this disorder. Recent reports have described patients with atypical clinical presentations for Cohen syndrome and biallelic VPS13B variants [10]. Further work is required to elucidate the cause of this apparent variable expressivity. However, this study provides further evidence for the consideration of biallelic VPS13B variants in cases where the classical features of Cohen syndrome are not present.

VPS13B encodes vacuolar protein sorting 13 homolog B (VPS13B), a large protein that localizes to the Golgi apparatus and lysosome, where it acts as a lipid transfer and tethering protein [42, 43]. VPS13B is crucial for Golgi apparatus integrity and intracellular trafficking. Knockdown of which leads to fragmentation of the Golgi apparatus into ministacks [42, 44]. The disruption of the Golgi ribbon by VPS13B knockout results in inhibited neurite outgrowth, and investigations using Cohen syndrome patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells demonstrate neurospheres of a reduced size when compared to controls, which, taken together, may indicate the pathomolecular basis for this disorder [43, 45].

Our findings have broader genetic and epidemiological implications for the wider Pakistani population. Consanguineous marriages, which are common in Pakistan, occur in more than half of marriages [15], significantly increasing the prevalence of autosomal recessive disorders such as MCPH [46]. By identifying both novel and known variants, our study contributes to a growing body of evidence that highlights the unique mutational landscape of South Asian populations, some of which may reflect founder effects. Importantly, these populations remain underrepresented in global reference databases like gnomAD, emphasizing the value of region-specific genetic studies for closing diagnostic gaps.

From a public health perspective, our findings underline the importance of early molecular diagnosis and genetic counseling. Accurate genetic testing facilitates timely clinical management, informed reproductive decisions, and targeted carrier screening in high-risk families [47]. In low-income countries like Pakistan, where awareness of rare genetic diseases remains limited, whole-exome sequencing can play a transformative role by identifying at-risk families and enabling preventive strategies such as prenatal or preimplantation genetic diagnosis [48]. Additionally, contributing novel variants to international databases will enhance future diagnostic yield not only for Pakistani families but also for other underrepresented groups globally.

Conclusion

Our study underscores the powerful role of WES in uncovering the genetic basis of highly heterogeneous recessive disorders, particularly in populations with a high prevalence of consanguinity, such as Pakistan. Through the identification of pathogenic variants in known genes and the characterization of candidate genes in consanguineous families, WES enables more accurate molecular diagnoses and facilitates the discovery of shared mutations across different populations. A major strength of this work lies in the integration of detailed clinical profiles with genetic findings, consolidating previously reported genotype-phenotype correlations associated with MCPH genes. Affected individuals with ASPM and CDK5RAP2 biallelic loss-of-function variants displayed typical clinical MCPH5 and MCPH3 phenotypes, respectively. These comprise severe microcephaly, variable intellectual disability, and craniofacial dysmorphism consistent with microcephaly. Although the two affected siblings were biallelic for the VPS13B p.(Gln2744*) variant, presented with microcephaly, intellectual disability, neurobehavioral manifestations, and hypotonia consistent with Cohen syndrome, they lacked key common features of the disorder, in particular progressive visual impairment due to high myopia and retinal dystrophy, reported to be present in 90–100% of cases [49]. In conclusion, we report two novel and three previously reported pathogenic variants in ASPM, CDK5RAP2, and VPS13B among Pakistani families affected by primary microcephaly. These findings expand the mutational spectrum of MCPH-associated genes and further support the loss-of-function mechanism underlying disease pathogenesis. Importantly, this study contributes to the growing evidence that population-specific genetic studies are essential for closing diagnostic gaps, refining variant interpretation, and improving genetic counseling in underrepresented communities.
