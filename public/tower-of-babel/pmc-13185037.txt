Comparison of iloprost therapy versus non-iloprost therapy for severe frostbite: a retrospective cohort study of two Canadian cities
Authors: Domhnall O’Dochartaigh, Matthew J Douma, Christopher Picard, Efrem Violato, Alexander Poole, Josianne Gauthier, Alexis Armour, Daniel Tiwana, Ted Harrigan, Vincent Gabriel, Jevon Brown, S Monty Ghosh, Ian MacNairn, Jaspreet Khangura, Heather Boucher, Lindsay Robinson, Lisa Watt, Jacob Van Nest, Johnathan l Bown, Alexis Mageau, Sean Crooks, Yvonne Efegoma, Kimberly Byrnes, Stacey Middleton, Jake Hayward, Brian Whiteside, Kathryn Dong, Elaine Hyshka, Michael Zakhary, Scott Maclean

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesFrostbite is a common reason for emergency department (ED) presentations in Canada. Iloprost, a prostacyclin analogue, has been investigated to reduce the risk of amputation with its use expanding. Two Canadian cities implemented iloprost over different times leading to a practice variation that allowed for treatment comparison. Our objective is to evaluate the effectiveness of iloprost compared with non-iloprost treatment. Secondary objectives include assessing the impact of iloprost dosage and homelessness.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on adult severe frostbite cases presenting to EDs in Calgary and Edmonton between November 2021 and April 2024. Data were abstracted from clinical databases and analysed for demographic and injury characteristics, treatment and amputation outcomes.ResultsOf 1812 total ED encounters for frostbite, 257 patients with grades 2–4 extremity frostbite were included for analysis. Logistic regression found that overall patients receiving iloprost were associated with reduced likelihood of any amputation (OR=0.49, 95% CI 0.25 to 0.96) and fewer digit amputations (p<0.001). In particular, iloprost use was associated with reduced amputation rates in grade 3 injuries compared with non-iloprost care (30% vs 52%, p=0.042). Although over 80% of patients with grade 4 frostbite required amputations regardless of iloprost treatment, its use was associated with improved digit salvage, with 10% fewer digit segments requiring amputation in treated patients in both hands (p=0.049) and feet (p=0.003). When assessing 65 patients who received iloprost for limb frostbite, higher iloprost doses were associated with a lower likelihood of amputation (OR=0.35; 95% CI 0.13 to 0.91; p=0.03). In the patient subgroup who underwent at least one amputation, total iloprost dosage was inversely related to outcome (p=0.033, βST=−0.26). Adverse events were reported in 61% of iloprost-treated patients, with headache being the most common. Homelessness was found to be a significant predictor of delays in arrival to the ED after injury (p<0.001); OR=2.90 (95% CI 1.73 to 4.91).ConclusionsIloprost infusion was associated with a reduction in amputation rates in grade 3 and 4 frostbite with the greatest association seen in grade 3 cases. Greater iloprost dosage was associated with improved digit salvage. Homelessness was associated with delayed ED presentation.Keywords: INTERNAL MEDICINE, ACCIDENT & EMERGENCY MEDICINE, PLASTIC & RECONSTRUCTIVE SURGERY, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

Frostbite is a severe cold-induced injury with potentially devastating physical and psychosocial consequences. Severe frostbite injuries occur when skin temperatures fall below −4°C triggering ice crystal formation with subsequent rewarming causing reperfusion injury.1 2 Although uncommon in temperate climates, frostbite disproportionately affects vulnerable populations—particularly those experiencing homelessness—during subzero temperatures.3 4 A recent Canadian study reported frostbite across all income and marginalisation quintiles, with a trend towards more severe injuries among people with lower incomes and those experiencing homelessness.5 Initial frostbite management includes external rewarming and classification to determine further treatment.6 Frostbite is classified into four grades proposed by Cauchy et al7 to correlate with predicted amputation rate.

To mitigate post-rewarming negative effects, thrombolytics and prostacyclin analogue treatments (eg, iloprost) have been investigated to reduce the risk of amputation.8 Each therapeutic option or combination thereof has patient-specific considerations, contraindications and route of administration. For example, availability of interventional radiology is necessary for intra-arterial thrombolytic therapy and patient blood pressure monitoring is required during iloprost administration.9 Health Canada’s Special Access Program has authorised the use of iloprost for severe frostbite treatment. Iloprost, a synthetic prostacyclin analogue, has shown promise in mitigating the progression of frostbite injury.10 Its vasodilatory, antithrombotic and endothelial-protective properties offer a plausible mechanism for salvaging ischaemic tissue when administered early. Although small trials and case series have suggested benefit,10 widespread adoption of iloprost remains limited, and evidence supporting its real-world effectiveness and optimal dosing remains sparse.

Frostbite management and protocols vary from one setting to another. In the Canadian province of Alberta, Calgary hospitals implemented a care pathway incorporating iloprost and alteplase in 2019 but until November 2024, iloprost was not used routinely in Edmonton hospitals. This natural experiment created an opportunity to evaluate clinical outcomes associated with iloprost treatment, including amputation rates, dose–response effects and equity of access. This retrospective cohort study aims to describe the characteristics, treatments and outcomes of patients treated for frostbite over a 3-year period, with a particular focus on the impact of iloprost therapy.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in large academic and small urban hospitals in Calgary and Edmonton between November 2021 and April 2024. This study received approval from the University of Alberta Research Ethics Board (RES 0113907) and was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement (see online supplemental material for STROBE checklist).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and public were not involved during the research study design, process or plans to disseminate the study results to participants or wider patient communities. Research questions and outcome measures were informed by patient/public priorities, experience and preferences through consultation with subject matter experts in Primary Care Alberta’s Vulnerable Population Support Program. The results of this study highlighted gaps in timely access to care as well as public policy limitations, which impede equitable access to available treatment that may optimise health outcomes. These study results informed further investigation as part of quality improvement (QI) work involving the patient population in identifying gaps and barriers to accessing care; as well as systemic barriers to frostbite injury prevention. This QI work included interviews with patients that have a documented frostbite injury and were living homeless at the time of incidence. Key learnings from this QI work have been implemented into revised screening tools and treatment pathways, as well as informing best practice recommendations for healthy public policy on this topic. Further investigation and publication of this follow-up work is currently under careful consideration.

Case identification and inclusion criteria

All adult patients with severe (grade 2–4) acute frostbite who presented to emergency departments (EDs) were identified through diagnostic coding of frostbite and verified through detailed chart review. Inclusion criteria were documented frostbite injury with grading by the treating physician and presentation within the study timeframe.

Data collection and abstraction

Data abstraction from the provincial electronic medical record (Connect Care, Epic, Madison, Wisconsin, USA) was conducted using a standardised chart review tool11 by a trained investigator (DT) and verified by a second researcher (DOD). Data collection fields are included along with details on specific variable collection (online supplemental material 3). Grade of injury was assessed independently by two investigators from the documented time point of post rewarming and before medical therapy. This was done through independent review of the clinical note and confirmed with photograph assessment, where present, to assess cyanosis level per digit. In rare cases where there was clear photographic evidence of digit circumferential cyanosis that differed from the clinical note, this level was used to code for digit grade level. Reviewers were not blinded to the treatment notes or treatment received. While photographing the injury was common practice, in some cases, clear photographic evidence was not present, in which case the clinical note was used to document the injury grade. Amputation rate was assessed with a minimum chart follow-up time of 6 months.

Iloprost protocol

For patients prescribed iloprost, it was administered intravenously daily for five consecutive days using a standardised protocol. The initial infusion was started at 2 mcg/hour and titrated upward every 30 min to a goal target rate of 8 mcg/hour or 10 mcg/hour for those weighing 50–74 kg or >74 kg, respectively. Dose modifications were made in response to adverse effects such as hypotension or headache. Regardless of patient weight, adverse effects or cumulative dose, each daily infusion was capped at a fixed duration of 6 hours. On subsequent days, the infusion was started at the highest tolerated rate. This fixed-duration approach may have limited total drug delivery in patients who required slower titration or who were in higher weight categories. No dose adjustments were made to ensure a minimum total dose per day. Patients who received fewer than 5 days of therapy were included in the analysis if treatment had been initiated.

Housing status classification

Housing status was classified into three categories: experiencing homelessness, not experiencing homelessness or unknown. Status was determined based on ED documentation, social work assessments or inpatient notes. No assumptions were made in the absence of documentation. For all subgroup and regression analyses involving housing status, cases with unknown status were excluded. No imputation was performed for missing housing data.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was any amputation attributable to frostbite during the index admission or follow-up period of 6 months. Secondary outcomes included the number and level of amputated segments, time to presentation from exposure and healing without surgical intervention. Outcomes were stratified by frostbite grade, anatomical location, housing status and iloprost dose.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis frequencies and percentages were used for categorical variables, and measures of central tendency were used for continuous variables. Proportions were compared using χ2 tests. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify predictors of amputation, reporting ORs with 95% CIs. Multivariable linear regression was used to evaluate for predictors of the number of digits and segments amputated, reporting standardised coefficients (βST) with 95% CIs. Within the grade 4 frostbite cohort, χ2 and t-test were used for comparison of amputation and number of digits amputated between iloprost alone and the combination therapy groups. Additionally, within the iloprost-treated cohort, frequencies and types of adverse reactions were documented and compared within dosing weight bands. In people experiencing homelessness (PEH), ordinal logistic regression was performed to examine the effect of delay of presentation (<12 hours, 12–24 hours, 24–48 hours, 48–72 hours and >72 hours) and injury severity on frostbite grade. No imputation was used for missing data. Data were analysed using jamovi V.2.3.26 (2022).12 A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Among the 257 included patients with grade 2–4 frostbite, 80 received iloprost and 177 did not. Logistic regression showed that iloprost treatment was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of any amputation (OR=0.49, 95% CI 0.25 to 0.96; p=0.038). Covariates included in the logistic regression indicated admission was a predictor of amputation (p<0.006, OR=2.89, 95% CI 1.35 to 6.2), though time from injury to ED presentation was not predictive of the risk of amputation (p>0.05). Iloprost was also associated with fewer digit amputations (βST=–0.6, 95% CI –0.91 to –0.3; p<0.001), with admission being a predictor of amputation (βST=0.87, 95% CI 0.58 to 1.15; p<0.001), while time of injury to ED arrival was not a significant predictor of the number of digits amputated (p>0.05). The amputation rate per patient was significantly lower among patients with grade 3 injuries who received iloprost therapy compared with those who did not (30% vs 52.3%, p=0.042; see table 3). No statistically significant differences in amputation rates were observed in grade 2 (3.2% vs 3.3%; p=0.977) or grade 4 frostbite (84.2% vs 81%; p=0.787) between iloprost and non-iloprost groups.

Grade 4 analysis of iloprost and alteplase

In a separate subgroup analysis using χ2 of 19 patients with grade 4 injuries, there was no significant difference in the rate of amputation for those who received iloprost, alteplase and heparin (6/7, 85%) compared with those who received iloprost alone (10/12, 83%; χ²=0.0188, p=0.891). There was also no significant difference in the number of digits amputated based on receiving iloprost, alteplase and heparin versus iloprost alone (p=0.33, mean difference=0.43, d=0.47 95% CI 0.49 to 1.42).

Iloprost dosage impact

Among 65 patients admitted for a 5-day iloprost protocol for digit frostbite, (excluded non-digit cases) higher total iloprost dose (mcg/kg) was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of amputation (OR=0.35, 95% CI 0.13 to 0.91; p=0.03), no effect was found for time from admission to first dose (p=0.238). Adverse effects

Of the 80 patients who received iloprost, 49 (61.3%) experienced adverse effects, with 44 (55%) experiencing multiple reactions (online supplemental material). The most common was headache (n=25, 23.3%). No significant correlation was found between adverse effects and patient weight (r=–0.016, p=0.894) or dose per kg (r=0.004, p=0.975).

Homelessness and frostbite outcomes

PEH accounted for 140 cases (54.5%). Homelessness was not associated with increased frostbite severity but was strongly associated with delayed ED presentation (OR=2.90, 95% CI 1.73 to 4.91; p<0.001).

Analysis of cold weather response systems showed that 22 (35%) of frostbite cases among PEH in Edmonton and 6 (18%) in Calgary occurred when municipal cold weather responses were not in effect.

Temperature ranges

Reported cases of severe frostbite occurred across a wide range of documented ambient temperatures, from –5°C to –44°C, though charted temperatures may be subject to recall or documentation variability (see online supplemental material 2).

Discussion

Statement of principle findings

We examined 257 cases of severe frostbite injury, with 80 cases receiving iloprost. Iloprost therapy was associated with a significant reduction in amputation rates, with the greatest benefit observed in grade 3 injuries. Moreover, a higher cumulative iloprost dose was associated with lower likelihood of amputation and less extensive tissue loss. PEH did not have significantly different amputation rates with noted limitations below. However, almost half of all cases were associated with homelessness and those individuals were also three times more likely to have delayed ED presentation.

Strengths and weaknesses in relation to other studies

Iloprost has been used for frostbite treatment alone or in combination with other therapeutics, but studies have been limited by small numbers of participant recruitment.3 10 13 14 The results of our cohort study provide support for the use of iloprost as a treatment that may be superior or equal to other treatments to reduce the risk of amputation following frostbite injury. Comparing our cases with Crooks et al Calgary study,15 we found higher grade 4 amputation rates in our cohort (84% vs 46% in Crooks et al), and grade 3 amputation rates (30% and 18%, respectively). The reasons for this are unclear, but might include small patient numbers in the former study, increased injury burden or an increasing number of PEH.

Increased patient weight has previously been associated with lower iloprost infusion rates and higher iloprost-related adverse events in patients with systemic sclerosis.16 Although our study did not report higher adverse iloprost effects with increased patient weight, these patients were noted to receive less overall dose than lighter patients. Importantly, the assessment of dose impact was conducted in a population that came from the same health region with a single standardised care standard.

Time to presentation and warm ischaemia time (time from rewarming to iloprost) was not associated with changes in amputation rate in our study. This unexpected finding is not in keeping with previous work. Nygaard et al study of thrombolytic therapy found an increase in amputation rate as time between rewarming and thrombolytic therapy increased.17 Several reasons might explain this difference. Warm ischaemia time data were missing in 38% of our study. In some cases, injury time was missing or estimated in the clinical note and was overall underpowered. Data were also not collected and assessed as a continuous variable. In addition, immersion rewarming, which is the recommended rewarming standard, was only documented in 24% of non-iloprost presentations and 45% of iloprost presentations and the impact of this was unknown.

We found a delayed frostbite presentation among PEH. It is possible that insufficient awareness of impending cold temperatures and frostbite injury treatment contributes to delayed presentation among PEH.5 18 Enhancing at-risk group understanding of the necessity to seek shelter, and to seek medical attention promptly is a key preventive measure.5 9 18 Raising awareness about early signs and symptoms of frostbite and the significance of early medical treatment can assist in minimising delays in obtaining care, and hence, improving outcomes.5 9 18 Finally, a major potential barrier might be a lack of shelter spaces and housing that meet the needs of the community. The education of first responders is also crucial to enable the responding teams to promptly identify and address frostbite, thereby reducing delays in treatment and ensuring timely access to medical assistance.19 Furthermore, social services are essential in offering counselling and shelter to PEH during cold weather.20

City departments responsible for cold weather response should consider their current cold response activation threshold given our findings that PEH incur severe frostbite injuries starting at −5°C. In British Columbia, it is recommended that response plans be implemented when temperatures are 0°C or below.21 Thresholds for activation in Edmonton during the study period were 3 days of −20°C (including windchill), and in Calgary, the threshold changed to support transit shuttle service from −20°C in the 2022/2023 to every day regardless of temperature from December to March in 2023/2024. PEH are significantly/rapidly impacted by a broader spectrum of weather conditions than those who are sheltered.9 22 Consequently, it is essential for cold weather response plans to incorporate early warning systems and outreach strategies, grounded in trauma-informed practices and emergency preparedness, response and recovery strategies that foster safety, collaboration and empowerment for this population.9 22

Future directions

The evidence for the use of iloprost in patients experiencing frostbite is limited. However, there are limited to no treatment alternatives, especially in patients with grade 2 and 3 frostbite, in patients with contraindication to thrombolytics or in settings where catheter-directed thrombolysis or intravenous thrombolysis is unavailable. System consideration should be given to improving access to iloprost and streamlined clinical pathways, such as early recognition of frostbite, appropriate triage and early rewarming to reduce injury severity23; while also examining system factors that may impact the frequency of patients who have a significant injury and may not remain in hospital for assessment or treatment. Perhaps most impactfully, most frostbite might be preventable, so strong consideration could be given to primary prevention strategies.

The ideal dose of iloprost is not yet defined. Future studies may consider total dose and infusion characteristics to optimise outcomes and minimise adverse events. Several treatment regimens (eg, different daily infusion duration and number of treatment days) have been used, but they have not been formally studied or compared. Many questions remain. For example, whether infusing iloprost for 6 hours daily or until achieving a ‘target’ dose daily is most important. Answering this question is challenging because iloprost adverse reactions are associated with faster infusion rate, and tolerance improves with slower infusion rate, gradual rate increase and with time. In this study protocol, the iloprost infusion was discontinued after 6 hours, irrespective of the total dose administered and this may have contributed to the lower total dose administered and negative outcomes observed. In other centres, such as Whitehorse, Yukon, the daily infusion is continued beyond 6 hours until a total of one ampoule (50 mcg) is administered (https://yukon.ca/en/whitehorse-frostbite-protocol). It is unknown what the optimal weight-based dose is, and there is a wide dosing range used (0.5–2 ng/kg/min or 2–10 mcg/hour).10 A higher daily target dose or a specific weight-based daily dose may be warranted, but this will affect hospitalisation and nursing care time.

Limitations

This study has notable limitations. It was limited to two urban Canadian centres with access to tertiary care facilities, iloprost, angiography and other therapeutics that may not be available in other regions. Data were retrospectively collected and, in some cases, were missing. Due to the variable-specific and non-systematic patterns of missing data, listwise deletion was applied, and no imputation methods were used. This approach may have reduced statistical power, particularly in subgroup analyses, and should be considered when interpreting null findings. Grading of digit injury relied on clinical chart notes and, where present, photographs. We attempted to mitigate this using independent abstraction and review of the injury grade; however, in a small number of cases where detailed photographs were not available, independent checking could not be completed so the chart note was abstracted as the documented grade for analysis. Although not ideal, the use of the clinical note as a standard was considered reasonable given the teams’ 4–5 years of experience with routine grading and using iloprost. Iloprost administration was not routinely individually customised beyond the 6-hour infusion time. ‘Non-iloprost therapy’ was not standardised. Because patients were not randomised to either iloprost or non-iloprost treatment, and that the groups predominantly came from separate cities, the groups may differ in important ways that are not possible to determine on retrospective review. Non-operative wound care and surgical procedures were not standardised nor compared.

Temperature estimates at the time of injury were derived retrospectively using a combination of clinical documentation and historical weather data. In many cases, the exact time of exposure was unclear or described imprecisely (eg,‘overnight exposure’), and minimum or average daily temperatures were substituted. As a result, estimates of the environmental conditions under which frostbite occurred, including the observation that injuries began at –5°C should be interpreted with caution. These limitations may have introduced misclassification bias in the assessment of temperature thresholds relevant to policy recommendations.

Appropriately capturing all adverse effects of iloprost and its effect on iloprost infusion rates was challenging, as noted by not finding an impact between adverse effects and dose delivered. The medication infusion instructions were standardised in the computer order set with a slow starting rate titrated every 30 min until the goal rate and maintained or decreased if adverse effects occur. In some cases, multiple adverse events were documented with little apparent effect on iloprost dose. Whereas, in other cases, a single adverse effect was noted during one infusion, and all subsequent infusions remained below the target rate. We believe the primary reason for reducing dose was due to medication intolerance that was not clearly documented or abstracted.

Regarding amputations, 6 months was selected based on previous studies methodology to allow for comparison, and to capture those cases where amputation was delayed. The determination of the index case of frostbite causing the amputation was made based on a detailed chart review of all hospital presentations. If subsequent frostbite injury occurred without hospital presentation, this would not have been accounted for; however, we feel this would have been unlikely due to the patient seeking care for their index case, and in many cases, repeat ED visits were noted without the presence of a subsequent injury. Some amputations were not captured. This occurred with PEH. Feedback from community partners reports that some PEH have their amputations occur outside of planned and documented healthcare procedures in the form of autoamputation or self-amputation. The exact number of such amputations is unknown. We acknowledge the large reported proportion of indigenous community members that make up the cities’ homeless populations;24 25 however, Indigenous-specific analysis was out of scope, and no identification of Indigenous patients formally occurred in this study, which is a limitation. This could be an area of future inquiry.

Conclusions

In severe frostbite presentations over three consecutive winters in two Canadian cities, the use of iloprost was associated with reduced amputations, with those who received a larger dose showing the most benefit. PEH were overrepresented in the study cohort and were at increased risk for delayed ED presentation. Severe frostbite was noted, starting at −5°C, supporting broader preventative measures from community and social support systems.
