Improved Trial and Error Learning for Random Games
Jérôme Taupin, Xavier Leturc, Christophe J. Le Martret

Abstract
When a game involves many agents or when communication between agents is not possible, it is useful to resort to distributed learning where each agent acts in complete autonomy without any information on the other agents’ situations. Perturbation-based algorithms have already been used for such tasks [ 1 , 2 ] . We propose some improvements based on practical observations to improve the performance of these algorithms. We show that the introduction of these changes preserves their theoretical convergence properties towards states that maximize the average reward and improve them in the case where optimal states exist. Moreover, we show that these algorithms can be made robust to the addition of randomness to the rewards, achieving similar convergence guarantees. Finally, we discuss the possibility for the perturbation factor of the algorithm to decrease during the learning process, akin to simulated annealing processes.

Introduction

Consider a game where multiple agents choose an action among a set of possible actions and observe a reward as an outcome, called utility in this paper.
We are interested in the study of algorithms that aim at finding a choice of strategies that maximizes the average utility of all agents in a distributed manner when playing the same game repeatedly.
By distributed, it is meant that each agent only has knowledge of its own action and its perceived outcome when choosing the next action.
Despite this strong constraint, perturbation-based algorithms such as
tel
(
tel
)
[
1
]
and
odl
(
odl
)
[
2
]
are shown to converge to some sense towards action profiles maximizing welfare.
These algorithms rely on a fixed perturbation factor
\varepsilon>0
. It is proven
[
1
]
that when choosing
\varepsilon
close to
0
, the asymptotic behavior of
tel
is to almost only visit Nash equilibria maximizing welfare.
This result is proven by showing that the process induced by
tel
adheres to a kind of perturbed Markov processes, for which the convergence is linked to a notion of potential over an appropriate resistance graph
[
3
]
.

1.1
Problem Setup

Let

n

Given an action profile

As there is a finite amount of action profiles, the set of all possible utilities is finite and we assume without loss of generality that utilities are bounded within

The quality of an action profile is measured via its global welfare

W(\boldsymbol{a})\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}\sum_{i}U_{i}(\boldsymbol{a})\penalty\ .

The aim of the algorithms discussed in this paper is to push agents to autonomously act in a way that maximizes this global quantity. Precisely, the proposed algorithm are shown to favor optimal states where all agents have a utility of
1

These statements are formalized by our main results

In this paper vectors with coordinates over all agents are written in bold font. Given a vector

Theoretical Framework

2.1
Regular Perturbed Markov Processes and Potential

This section recalls useful results from

First, let us describe the regularity condition required on

Definition 2.1

(Regular Perturbed Markov Process)

A family

\displaystyle 0<\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-r}X^{\varepsilon}<{+\infty}.

Intuitively, the regularity condition implies that

The study of the resistance graph allows to describe the behavior of the perturbed process when

Definition 2.2

(Resistance Graph)
.

Let

be a

.

•

The resistance of a transition

•

Let

•

•

The resistance of a path

The resistance

The tendency of the process to remain in a state

Definition 2.3

(Potential)

•

A

•

The

2.2
Perturbed Distributed Learning

Let us introduce the notion of
pdl
(
pdl
), which encompasses the general reasoning of all the following algorithms.
The main idea of a
pdl
algorithm is to attribute moods to each agent along with a reference—or benchmark—action and utility. Agents behave differently based on their state, i.e., their mood
m
, benchmark action
\overline{a}
and benchmark utility
\overline{u}
.
In a
discontent
(denoted
D
for short) state, an agent systemically explores a new strategy and may accept it depending on the quality of the outcome.
In a
content
(
C

Two intermediate moods,
hopeful
(
H
) and
watchful
(
W

tel
and its variants discussed here and allow to identify Nash equilibria, which is not done in

A step of any
pdl

1.

Chooses an action
a
to play based on an
action policy
taking into account its state
(\overline{m},\overline{a},\overline{u})
,

2.

Observes a utility
u
resulting from the choice of all actions
\boldsymbol{a}
,

3.

Updates its state according to an
update policy
taking into account its state
(\overline{m},\overline{a},\overline{u})
along with
a
and
u
.

Regarding the update policy, we list here the different behaviors an agent can adopt to update its state
(\overline{m},\overline{a},\overline{u})
:

•

Accept : Go to state
(\texttt{C},a,u)
, i.e., accept the outcome and commit to the observed action and utility.

•

Revert : Go to state
(\texttt{C},\overline{a},\overline{u})
, i.e., remain in the previous state. This is only used from content states.

•

Reject : Go to state
(\texttt{D},a,u)
, i.e., reject the outcome and become discontent.

•

Adopt intermediate mood

(m,\overline{a},\overline{u})
, i.e., keep the same benchmark and wait for another round before making a decision. The intermediate mood

is either

or

Deterministic Games – ITEL

In this section, perceived utilities

are assumed to be deterministic and bounded within

We introduce

Let us first discuss a necessary assumption on the underlying game for our analysis to hold.
In a general game, it is possible that an agent
i
maximizes its utility by playing an action that is detrimental to the other agents. If the other agents have no way to influence
i
out of playing this action, then it is not possible to design a distributed algorithm that does not get stuck in a sub-optimal state in these situations, without forcing agents to possibly leave a seemingly optimal situation from their point of view. For this reason, the following condition is introduced in

1
]
and is also used in this work.

Assumption A1

(Interdependence)
.

The game is

, that is, given any action profile
\boldsymbol{a}
and any proper subset
\emptyset\subsetneq J\subsetneq I
of agents, there exists an agent

and a choice of actions

such that
U_{i}(a^{\prime}_{J},a_{-J})\neq U_{i}(\boldsymbol{a})
.

The interdependence assumption ensures that for any partition of the agents into two groups, there exists a way for the agents of the first group to play that influences the utilities of at least one agent in the second group.

In the update policy of

The probability of accepting an observation from a discontent state is clipped at

The behaviors of agents under

•

A discontent agent explores at random any action. Depending on the outcome

•

A content agent explores an action different from its benchmark with resistance

•

When a content agent does not explore, it may still notice a different utility than its benchmark due to other agents exploring different actions. In this case, the agent becomes hopeful or watchful depending on the change being an improvement or a deterioration.
If its utility goes back to normal on the following round, the agent reverts back to its original state. Else, if an improvement is confirmed the agent accepts it and update its benchmark. Finally, if a deterioration is confirmed the agent rejects it and become discontent.

Notice that a content agent with optimal utility is bound to its state as it never explores. We call such agents

This behavior is not present in the original formulation of

4
Random Games – RITEL

In this section we consider random games. For a given action profile
\boldsymbol{a}
, the utility
U_{i}(\boldsymbol{a})
is a random variable that is bounded
as
in
[0,1]
. Apart from this assumption, utilities may follow any probability distribution: continuous, discrete or even deterministic.
The introduction of noisy utilities yields the following issues, both from a practical standpoint as it can confuse agents, and from a theoretical standpoint as it prevents us from using the
rpmp
framework.

Instability

When no agent explores, different payoffs may still be observed due to the random nature of utilities. Under
itel
, those differences would be wrongly interpreted as changes of behaviors from other agents.

Infinite space state

In
itel
, the proof of convergence is based on the study of the
rpmp
induced by the algorithm. This process acts over the space of all states
(\boldsymbol{\overline{m}},\boldsymbol{\overline{a}},\boldsymbol{\overline{u}})
of possible moods, actions, and utilities for each agents.
If the support of the random utilities is infinite, which would be the case for continuous distributions, then the state space becomes infinite and the theory of
rpmp
no longer applies.

Non-Regularity

The randomness of utilities must be taken into account when computing the resistance of paths. It is not true in general that transition probabilities are regular as in
Definition

2.1
.

We introduce
ritel
in
Section

4.1
.

Sections

4.2
and
4.3
adapts the
rpmp
framework discussed in
Section

2.1
in order to fit the
ritel
process and overcome the issues mentioned above. The
ritel
process is then analyzed in
Section

4.4
following the same reasoning as for
itel
in
Section

3.2
with the necessary adaptations, eventually leading to our convergence statement in
Theorem

4.22
.

4.1
Definition of RITEL

In this section we introduce
ritel
, an adaptation of
itel
in the random setup to tackle the issues mentioned above. As for
itel
, we aim at converging to optimal states, then to equilibria with maximal welfare, then to action profiles with maximal trade-off between welfare and stability.
By welfare, we refer to the sum of the mean utilities of all player.

Let us first describe informally the differences between
itel
and
ritel
.
First, iterations are divided in periods of length
\tau
. Within a period, each agent commits to a fixed action so that the chosen action profile remains constant. This way, the variations in utility an agent may observe along the period are solely due to noise, so that it can estimate more reliably the average utility it should receive in this action profile.
When the period ends, each agent compares the estimate of its average utility to its benchmark utility and update their state with a policy similar to
itel
.

Second, in order to consider only a finite amount of possible states, benchmark utilities are restricted to a finite set of bins by slicing the utility range
[0,1]
into bins
[0,\delta),[\delta,2\delta),\dots,[1-\delta,1),{1}
for some
\delta>0
chosen as the inverse of an integer. The highest bin
\{1\}
may also be referred to as
[1,1+\delta)
for consistency.
This implies a loss of accuracy that eventually appears in our main result:

ritel
is not able to discriminate two action profiles when their welfare are too close. Choosing smaller
\delta
allows finer results, however this requires longer periods to ensure convergence.
Precisely, we shall see that choosing a period length depending on the perturbation factor
\varepsilon
of the form
\tau=\lceil\tau_{0}\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\rceil
for some constant
\tau_{0}
ensures that the randomness of utilities is negligible in the overall process. The particular choice of
\tau_{0}
is guided by a condition which essentially boils down to the fact that better accuracy, i.e., smaller
\delta
, requires higher reliability on the samples, i.e., higher
\tau_{0}
.

Third, when comparing utilities to choose a behavior, an agent actually compares the utility bins. Furthermore, it is important for stability to avoid the situation where an agent whose mean utility is close to the border between two bins constantly switches between both bins. For this reason, agents consider a change of utility significant enough to act on it only when the new utility is neither in the benchmark bin, nor in an adjacent bin, which implies a gap in utilities of at least
\delta
.

4.1.3
Theoretical Framework

Let us discuss the conditions needed to use the
rpmp
framework given by
Definitions

1.2
and
2.1
.
Since the states of the algorithm are updated at the end of each period, we define the perturbed process
P^{\varepsilon}
to model the transitions from one state to another after a full period of sampling, for which we recall the length
\tau=\lceil\tau_{0}\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\rceil
.
Notice that
P^{\varepsilon}
also corresponds to the process without periods—or equivalently with a period length of
1
—but where each utility distribution
U
is replaced with the distribution
U^{(\tau)}
of its empirical average.
Indeed, the only role of the periods is to reduce the variance of observations.
When
\varepsilon\to 0
,
\tau\to{+\infty}
and
U^{(\tau)}
converges as a distribution towards
\mu
.

The unperturbed process
P^{0}
can therefore be defined by taking the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
in
Table

3
and replacing each observed utility
U^{(\tau)}
with
\mu
.
Formally, let us check that the transition probabilities indeed converge to such process.
Consider an agent in a state
(\overline{m},\overline{a},\overline{v})
who observes
u\sim U^{(\tau)}
, the probability that the agent chooses a given behavior is a combination of probabilities of
u
belonging to specific bins and of probabilities of choosing a behavior given the observed bin.
For example, the probability that a content agent who explored accepts its exploration with new benchmark bin
v
is
\mathbb{P}\big(U^{(\tau)}\in v\big)\varepsilon^{G(\overline{v},v)}
.
The probability that a hopeful agent becomes watchful is
\sum_{v\leq\overline{v}-2\delta}\mathbb{P}\big(U^{(\tau)}\in v\big)
.

Since
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tau(\varepsilon)={+\infty}
, the law of large numbers implies that as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the quantities
\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\in v)
converge to
1
when
\mu=\mathbb{E}[U]\in v
, else converge to
0
, except for one case. Indeed, if
U
is not deterministic and its mean
\mu=v^{-}
is exactly on the edge between two bins
v
and
v-\delta
, then both bins are observed with probability
\frac{1}{2}
when
\tau\to{+\infty}
as the distribution of the empirical mean becomes symmetric due to the central limit theorem.
Regardless of the case, it always holds that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{P}\big(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\in v\big)
exists, which in turn implies that all transition probabilities
P^{\varepsilon}_{x,y}
converge to a limit
P^{0}_{x,y}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Moreover, in
P^{0}
the observed utilities are deterministic or equally distributed between two adjacent bins in the special case discussed above.

Regarding the regularity condition given in
Definition

2.1
, we can see from the above discussion that this condition would be satisfied if and only if
\mathbb{P}\big(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon)}\in v\big)
is regular for all random utilities
U
and all bins

.
This is unfortunately not true in general. However, a slightly weaker condition is satisfied by any distribution.

From there we are able to analyze the convergence of

4.2
Almost Regularity and Large Deviations

In this section we explain why for any utility distribution
U
and bin
v
, the probability
\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon)}\in v)
satisfies the following definition, which follows from Cramér’s theorem.

Definition 4.1

(Almost Regularity)
.

A family
(X^{\varepsilon})_{0\leq\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}
of non-negative real numbers is
almost regular
if there exists
r\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-r^{\prime}}X^{\varepsilon}=0&\text{for all $r^{\prime}<r$,}\\
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-r^{\prime}}X^{\varepsilon}={+\infty}&\text{for all $r^{\prime}>r$.}\end{cases}

(3)

r
is unique and called the
resistance
of
X^{\varepsilon}
.
r
can be equal to
{+\infty}
, which is the case in particular when
X^{\varepsilon}=0
.

It is immediate that regularity as defined in
Definition

2.1
implies almost regularity, and that both notions of resistance coincide in this case.
Almost regularity suggests that
X^{\varepsilon}
behaves somewhat like
\varepsilon^{r}
but in a broader sense.
For instance, the sequence
\big(\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\big)^{\alpha}\varepsilon^{r})_{\varepsilon>0}
is almost regular with resistance
r
regardless of
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, although
\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{\alpha}
may converge to
0
or to
{+\infty}
.
In particular, if the resistance of
X^{\varepsilon}
is positive then
X^{\varepsilon}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
by applying the definition with
r^{\prime}=0
.
Let us now recall Cramér’s theorem.
For a real-valued random variable
U
, define its
lmgf
(
lmgf
)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{U}:t\mapsto\log(\mathbb{E}[\exp(tU)])

along with its Legendre transform

\displaystyle\Lambda^{*}_{U}:x\mapsto\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}(tx-\Lambda_{U}(t))\penalty\ .

Recall a few elementary properties of the function
\Lambda^{*}_{U}
along with Cramér’s theorem.

Lemma 4.2
.

The function
\Lambda^{*}_{U}
takes the value
0
at the mean
\mu
of
U
. It is also decreasing over
({-\infty},\mu]
and increasing over
[\mu,{+\infty})
.
Moreover, if
x>\mu
then
\mathbb{P}(U\geq x)=0
if and only if
\Lambda^{*}_{U}(x)={+\infty}
, and if
x<\mu
then
\mathbb{P}(U\leq x)=0
if and only if
\Lambda^{*}_{U}(x)={+\infty}
.

Lemma 4.3

(Cramér
[
5
, Theorem 23.3]
)
.

Let
U
be any non-degenerate random variable with expectation
\mu
and finite
lmgf
.
Then, the empirical mean
U^{(\tau)}
satisfies a large deviation principle with rate function
\Lambda^{*}_{U}
, that is:

•

For all
x>\mu
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to{+\infty}}\frac{1}{\tau}\log\big(\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau)}\geq x)\big)=-\Lambda^{*}_{U}(x)\penalty\ .

•

For all
x<\mu
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to{+\infty}}\frac{1}{\tau}\log\big(\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau)}<x)\big)=-\Lambda^{*}_{U}(x)\penalty\ .

In
Lemma

4.3
, writing
\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau)}\geq x)
or
\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau)}>x)
does not influence the result, and similarly for the lower tail. The inequalities are stated in a way that is compatible with the form of the bins as
[v^{-},v^{+})
.
Using
Lemma

4.3
, the probability of observing a given bin is made almost regular by having
\tau
diverge to
{+\infty}
when
\varepsilon\to 0
at an appropriate rate.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\tau(\varepsilon)=\lceil\tau_{0}\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\rceil
for some constant
\tau_{0}>0
. Under the same conditions as in
Lemma

4.3
, the following holds:

•

For all
x>\mu
,
\big(\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\geq x)\big)_{\varepsilon>0}
is almost regular with resistance
\tau_{0}\Lambda^{*}_{U}(x)
.

•

For all
x<\mu
,
\big(\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}<x)\big)_{\varepsilon>0}
is almost regular with resistance
\tau_{0}\Lambda^{*}_{U}(x)
.

Proof.

Let us show that if
(X^{\tau})_{\tau}
satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to{+\infty}}\frac{1}{\tau}\log\big(X^{\tau}\big)=-r

(4)

for some
r\geq 0
, then
(Y^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}\big(X^{\tau(\varepsilon)}\big)_{\varepsilon>0}
is almost regular with resistance
\tau_{0}r
as defined in
Definition

4.1
.
Indeed, if
Equation

4
holds then for all
r^{\prime}\geq 0

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau(\varepsilon)}\log\left(e^{\tau(\varepsilon)r^{\prime}}X^{\tau(\varepsilon)}\right)=r^{\prime}+\frac{1}{\tau(\varepsilon)}\log\left(X^{\tau(\varepsilon)}\right)\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\to}r^{\prime}-r

as
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tau(\varepsilon)\to{+\infty}
.
From there,

•

If
r^{\prime}<r
,
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\log\big(e^{\tau(\varepsilon)r^{\prime}}X^{\tau(\varepsilon)}\big)={-\infty}
, hence
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}e^{\tau(\varepsilon)r^{\prime}}Y^{\varepsilon}=0
.

•

If
r^{\prime}>r
,
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\log\big(e^{\tau(\varepsilon)r^{\prime}}X^{\tau(\varepsilon)}\big)={+\infty}
, hence
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}e^{\tau(\varepsilon)r^{\prime}}Y^{\varepsilon}={+\infty}
.

Now, since
\tau(\varepsilon)=\lceil\tau_{0}\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\rceil
is bounded between
\tau_{0}\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
and
\tau_{0}\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})+1

and replacing

with
\varepsilon^{-\tau_{0}r^{\prime}}
in the above statements yields the same limits.
We conclude that
(Y^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
is almost regular with resistance
\tau_{0}r
.
Replacing
X^{\tau}
with
\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau)}\geq x)
or
\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau)}<x)
and using

4.3
concludes the proof.
∎

Recall that we are ultimately interested in
\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\in v)
for any bin

. This is deduced directly from the above using

4.2
.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
\tau(\varepsilon)=\lceil\tau_{0}\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\rceil
for some constant
\tau_{0}>0
. Then for all random distribution
U
supported within
[0,1]
and bin
v
,
\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\in v)
is almost regular.
Moreover, denoting

the corresponding resistance, we have

\displaystyle r_{U}(v)=\begin{cases}\tau_{0}\Lambda^{*}_{U}(v^{-})&\text{if $\mu<v^{-}$,}\\
0&\text{if $\mu\in v$,}\\
\tau_{0}\Lambda^{*}_{U}(v^{+})&\text{if $\mu\geq v^{+}$.}\end{cases}

Moreover,
\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\in v)
converges to a positive limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
if

and is equal to
0
if
r_{U}(v)={+\infty}
.
If
U
is deterministic, the result remains true except that when
\mu=v^{+}
,
r_{U}(v)
is equal to
{+\infty}
instead of
\tau_{0}\Lambda^{*}_{U}(\mu)=0
.

Proof.

If
U
is deterministic, everything follows from the fact that
\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\in v)=\mathds{1}_{v}(\mu)
and
\Lambda^{*}_{U}(x)={+\infty}
for
x\neq\mu
.
Assume that
U
is not deterministic.
If
v^{-}\leq\mu\leq v^{+}
, we already know that this probability converges to a positive limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
(precisely, to
1
if the inequalities are strict, else to
\frac{1}{2}
), which implies that
\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\in v)
is almost regular with resistance
0
. This concludes the case where
\mu\in v
and the case where
\mu=v^{+}
for which
\Lambda^{*}_{U}(v^{+})=0
.
If
\mu<v^{-}
, one can write

\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\in v)=\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\geq v^{-})-\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\geq v^{+})

and both terms are almost regular with resistance
\tau_{0}\Lambda^{*}_{U}(v^{-})
and
\tau_{0}\Lambda^{*}_{U}(v^{+})
respectively, according to

.
Since
\Lambda^{*}_{U}
is increasing over

, the second term is dominated by the first one, therefore
\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\in v)
is equivalent to
\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\geq v^{-})
, hence almost regular with resistance
\tau_{0}\Lambda^{*}_{U}(v^{-})>0
.
If
\mu>v^{+}

\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\in v)=\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}<v^{+})-\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}<v^{-})

is almost regular with resistance
\tau_{0}\Lambda^{*}_{U}(v^{+})>0
using the same reasoning.
We have identified the value of

for all cases and proven that it is equal to

if and only if
\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\in v)
converges to a positive limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Moreover, the fact that

if and only if
\mathbb{P}(U^{(\tau(\varepsilon))}\in v)=0
stems from the second part of
Lemma

.
∎

Beyond the theoretical requirement of regularity, it is also needed to lower bound the resistance of offset observations in order to ensure the stability of aligned states.
For common distributions, the
lmgf
may be explicitly computed. For instance, given a gaussian variable

, it holds that for
x>\mu
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z^{(\tau)}>x)\underset{\tau\to{+\infty}}{\sim}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi\tau}(x-\mu)}e^{-\tau\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}

hence
\Lambda^{*}_{Z}(v^{-})=\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}
.
Note that this example shows that the introduction of almost regularity is necessary. Indeed, here
\mathbb{P}(Z^{(\tau)}>x)
is of order
\tau^{-1/2}e^{-\tau r}
whereas regularity as defined in
Definition

2.1
would impose an equivalent of order
e^{-\tau r}
.
In general, knowing that utilities are bounded
as
within
[0,1]
, Hoeffding’s inequality implies that for all

\displaystyle\Lambda^{*}_{U}(x)\geq 2(x-\mu)^{2}\penalty\ .

(5)

If it is known that the variance of utilities is upper bounded by
\sigma^{2}>0
, then using Bernstein’s inequality instead yields

\Lambda^{*}_{U}(x)\geq\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}+2|x-\mu|}\penalty\ ,

which may be tighter when

is known to be small.
Precise knowledge of the utility distributions or more specific inequalities may improve the above lower bounds. This discussion falls under the theory of large deviations and is independent from our work.
Ultimately,

allows to lower bound the resistance of offset observations in the most general case and shall be used in the following.
Eventually, this inequality translates to a condition between
\tau_{0}
and

to ensure that such events are negligible in the

4.3
Almost Regular Perturbed Markov Processes

In this section we show that the theory of
rpmp
can be extended by replacing the regularity condition with almost regularity.

Theorem

4.7
generalizes
Theorem

2.4
to this larger class of processes.

Definition 4.6

(Almost Regular Perturbed Markov Process)
.

Let
(P^{\varepsilon})
be a
pmp
over a state space
\mathcal{X}
.
(P^{\varepsilon})
is said to be an
arpmp
(
arpmp
) if all transition probabilities are almost regular, that is

\displaystyle\text{$\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}$, $\exists r\geq 0:$}\begin{cases}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-r^{\prime}}P_{x,y}^{\varepsilon}=0&\text{for all $r^{\prime}<r$,}\\
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-r^{\prime}}P_{x,y}^{\varepsilon}={+\infty}&\text{for all $r^{\prime}>r$.}\end{cases}

As in
Definition

2.1
,
r
is unique if defined, is called the resistance of the transition and can be equal to
{+\infty}
. This is the case in particular for non-existing transitions
P^{\varepsilon}_{x,y}=0
.
Recall that
Equation

2
is stronger then
Equation

3
and both notions of resistance coincide if regularity holds.
We define the notions of resistance
r(x\to y)
, resistance graph
\mathcal{G}
, rooted tree, and potential
\gamma
exactly as in
Section

2.1
.

When the process is almost regular we lose the information on the behavior of
\varepsilon^{-r}P^{\varepsilon}_{x,y}
. In particular we are unable to know how two transitions
P^{\varepsilon}_{x,y}
and
P^{\varepsilon}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}
of equal resistance behave relatively, as their ratio can be any quantity that is sub-polynomial with regards to
\varepsilon
.
This leads to not being able to describe as accurately the behavior of
\pi^{\varepsilon}_{x}
for states
x
minimizing the potential as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
We are however able to adapt
Theorem

2.4
to show that states that do not minimize the potential vanish even under the looser assumption of almost regularity, so that the
sss
are included in
\mathcal{X}^{\star}
although the inclusion may not be an equality.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
(P^{\varepsilon})
be an
arpmp
over a finite space
\mathcal{X}
such that
P^{\varepsilon}
is aperiodic and irreducible for all
\varepsilon>0
and denote
\pi^{\varepsilon}
its stationary distribution.
Then,
\pi^{\varepsilon}_{x}
vanishes as
\varepsilon\to 0
for any state that does not minimize
\gamma
.
In particular, the set of
sss
satisfies

\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{X}^{\star}\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}\big\{x\in\mathcal{X}\penalty\ :\penalty\ \gamma(x)=\min_{\mathcal{X}}\gamma\big\}\penalty\ .

As for
Theorem

2.4
, the potential can be computed over recurrence classes instead of the whole state space.
There is a slight loss of accuracy in
Theorem

4.7
compared to
Theorem

2.4
as the behavior of
\pi^{\varepsilon}_{x}
for an individual state
x\in\mathcal{X}^{\star}
is unknown, whereas it it shown to converge to a positive limit if the process is a
rpmp
.
This is not an issue in our context as we mainly care about the fact that the process asymptotically remains in favorable states, regardless of the distribution within these states.

Proof.

The proof of
Theorem

2.4

[
3
, Lemma 1]
states that
\pi^{\varepsilon}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\pi^{\varepsilon}_{x}=\frac{p^{\varepsilon}_{x}}{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}p^{\varepsilon}_{y}}

(6)

for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, where

\displaystyle p^{\varepsilon}_{x}\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}\sum_{T:x\text{-tree}}\prod_{(y\to z)\in T}P^{\varepsilon}_{y,z}\penalty\ .

Proving
Theorem

2.4
consists in showing that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-\gamma(x)}p^{\varepsilon}_{x}\in(0,{+\infty})
, i.e.,
p^{\varepsilon}_{x}
is regular with resistance
\gamma(x)
.
It follows that the quantities
\pi^{\varepsilon}_{x}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}^{\star}
are all equivalent up to a constant as
\varepsilon\to 0
and that
\pi^{\varepsilon}_{y}
for all
y\notin\mathcal{X}^{\star}
are negligible, hence the
sss
are the states minimizing
\gamma
.
When the process is almost regular instead, we may only show that
p^{\varepsilon}_{x}
is almost regular with resistance
\gamma(x)
. For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-\gamma^{\prime}}P_{x,y}^{\varepsilon}=0&\text{for all $\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma(x)$,}\\
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-\gamma^{\prime}}P_{x,y}^{\varepsilon}={+\infty}&\text{for all $\gamma^{\prime}>\gamma(x)$.}\end{cases}

Indeed, let
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma(x)
. Since
\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma(x)\leq\sum_{(y\to z)\in T}r(y\to z)
for any
x
-tree
T
, there exists a constant
\alpha>0
such that
\gamma^{\prime}=\sum_{(y\to z)\in T}(r(y\to z)-\alpha)
.
Then,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-\gamma^{\prime}}\prod_{(y\to z)\in T}P^{\varepsilon}_{y,z}=\prod_{(y\to z)\in T}\varepsilon^{-(r(y\to z)-\alpha)}P^{\varepsilon}_{y,z}.

Using
Equation

3
with
r^{\prime}=r(y\to z)-\alpha<r(y\to z)
, each term in this finite product goes to
0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Summing over all
x
-tree, of which there is a finite amount, we conclude that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-\gamma^{\prime}}p^{\varepsilon}_{x}=0
.
Let
\gamma^{\prime}>\gamma(x)
and
T
be a minimal
x
-tree. Since
\gamma^{\prime}>\gamma(x)=\sum_{(y\to z)\in T}r(y\to z)
, the exists a constant
\alpha>0
such that
\gamma^{\prime}=\sum_{(y\to z)\in T}(r(y\to z)+\alpha)
.
Then,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-\gamma^{\prime}}p^{\varepsilon}_{x}\geq\varepsilon^{-\gamma^{\prime}}\prod_{(y\to z)\in T}P^{\varepsilon}_{y,z}=\prod_{(y\to z)\in T}\varepsilon^{-(r(y\to z)+\alpha)}P^{\varepsilon}_{y,z}.

Using
Equation

3
with
r^{\prime}=r(y\to z)+\alpha>r(y\to z)
, each term in this finite product goes to
{+\infty}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. We conclude that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-\gamma^{\prime}}p^{\varepsilon}_{x}={+\infty}
.
We have shown that
p^{\varepsilon}_{x}
is almost regular with resistance
\gamma(x)
, and can now rewrite
Equation

6
as

\pi^{\varepsilon}_{x}=\frac{\varepsilon^{-\gamma^{\prime}}p^{\varepsilon}_{x}}{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}^{\star}}\varepsilon^{-\gamma^{\prime}}p^{\varepsilon}_{y}+\sum_{y\notin\mathcal{X}^{\star}}\varepsilon^{-\gamma^{\prime}}p^{\varepsilon}_{y}}

for any
\gamma^{\prime}\geq 0
.
If
x\notin\mathcal{X}^{\star}
, choosing
\gamma^{\prime}
so that
\min\gamma<\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma(x)
makes the numerator go to

and the denominator go to
{+\infty}
, which implies that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\pi^{\varepsilon}_{x}=0
. This holds for all

, therefore
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\pi^{\varepsilon}_{\mathcal{X}^{\star}}=1
.
∎

4.4
Theoretical Analysis of RITEL

The previous sections introduce
arpmp

ritel
adheres to this framework.
We now discuss the convergence properties of
ritel
. This section follows the same outline as
Section

3.2
.
We call state a triplet
(\boldsymbol{\overline{m}},\boldsymbol{\overline{a}},\boldsymbol{\overline{v}})=(\overline{m}_{i},\overline{a}_{i},\overline{v}_{i})_{i\in I}
describing the states of all agents at a given time in the algorithm, and
\mathcal{X}
the set of all possible states.
As for
itel
, we disregard the benchmark action and bin of discontent agents. We also identify the set of all-discontent states to a single state
D
.
Recall that our goal is to identify the
sss

\mathcal{S}
, which are included in the states
\mathcal{X}^{\star}
that minimize the potential
\gamma
according to
Theorem

4.7
in the case where the perturbed process
P^{\varepsilon}
is aperiodic and irreducible.

Recall that
itel
is shown to converge under the interdependence assumption
A1
.
In the context of
ritel
, due to bin quantization and to the fact that offsets of only one bin are dismissed by agents, we require a slightly stronger assumption, that interdependence can always happen with a change of at least
3\delta
in utility.

Assumption A2

(
3\delta
-Interdependence)
.

The game is
3\delta
-
interdependent
, that is, given any action profile
\boldsymbol{a}
and any proper subset
\emptyset\subsetneq J\subsetneq I
of agents, there exists an agent
i\notin J
and a choice of actions
a^{\prime}_{J}
such that
|M_{j}(a^{\prime}_{i},a_{-i})-M_{j}(a)|\geq 3\delta
.

Note that since there is a finite number of agents and action profiles, a game that satisfies interdependence
A1
always satisfies
3\delta
-interdependence

for small enough
\delta
.

4.4.1
Definitions

Recall that when studying
itel
in
Section

3.2
, we defined subsets
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{X}
of the state space
\mathcal{X}
, namely
\mathcal{C}
the subset where all agents are content with a benchmark utility aligned with the benchmark action profile,
\mathcal{E}
the subset of equilibrium states, and
\mathcal{A}
the subset of optimal states.
With each inclusion comes a stronger sense of stability: states in
\mathcal{C}
are absorbing in the unperturbed process
P^{0}
, states in
\mathcal{E}
have a large outward resistance in the perturbed process
P^{\varepsilon}
and states in
\mathcal{A}
are absorbing in
P^{\varepsilon}
.
Regarding
ritel
, similar subsets are defined. However, their definition needs to be adapted to account for the loss of accuracy due to the introduction of random utilities and bin quantization.
Eventually, these subsets come with a “weak” and “strong” variant. Intuitively, the “strong” set contains the states that are ensured to hold the associated property, whereas the “weak” set contains the states that may hold the property and is identified with a subscript
\delta
.
We derive upper bounds on the potential of states that are part of the strong subset, and lower bound on the potential of the states that are not part of the weak subset. The weak set acts as a gray area that cannot be described as accurately.

Let us adapt
Definition

3.1
to introduce the notion of strongly aligned states
\mathcal{C}
and weakly aligned states
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}
in
ritel
.
Since benchmark utilities are quantified into bins, one would intuitively define aligned states as states where the mean utility of each agent is within its benchmark bin. Such states shall be referred to as strongly aligned.
Recall that
ritel
is designed so that agents observing an offset of only one bin from their benchmark disregard the change. For this reason we also introduce a weaker notion of alignment for agents with an offset of at most one bin between their benchmark and their mean.

Definition 4.8

(Aligned States)
.

•

An agent with benchmark bin
\overline{v}
is
strongly aligned
with an action profile
\boldsymbol{a}
if its average utility
\mu
in
\boldsymbol{a}
satisfies
\mu\in\overline{v}
.

•

An agent with benchmark bin
\overline{v}
is
weakly aligned
with an action profile
\boldsymbol{a}
if its average utility
\mu
in
\boldsymbol{a}
satisfies
\mu\in\overline{v}\pm\delta
. If
U(\boldsymbol{a})
is not deterministic, it is also required that
\mu\neq(\overline{v}-\delta)^{-}
.

An all-content state is strongly aligned (resp. weakly aligned) if all agents are strongly aligned (resp. weakly aligned) with the benchmark actions.
The set of weakly aligned states is denoted
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\subset\mathcal{X}
and the set of strongly aligned states is denoted
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{C}_{\delta}
.

Generally speaking, an agent is strongly aligned if its mean utility is contained in its benchmark bin, and weakly aligned if it is contained in its benchmark bin or in an adjacent bin.
There is one exception which is the case where the average utility
\mu
lies exactly at the boundary between two bins
\nu
and
\nu-\delta
and the utility is not deterministic. In this case,
\nu-\delta
is weakly aligned,
\nu
is strongly aligned, but
\nu+\delta
is not weakly aligned. This is due to the fact that as
\tau\to{+\infty}
,
U^{(\tau)}
will tend to be observed in both
\nu
and
\nu-\delta
with equal probability, and the resistance for observing
U^{(\tau)}
in

can be explicitly lower bounded since it requires an offset of at least
\delta
from the true mean.
This technical detail does not influence the overall reasoning.
In any case, given an action profile
\boldsymbol{a}
, there is exactly one strongly aligned state with benchmark actions
\boldsymbol{a}
.
We shall see that states in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}
are absorbing in
P^{0}
, but their outward resistance may be arbitrarily close to
0
, whereas the outward resistance of states in
\mathcal{C}
can be lower bounded.

We now adapt the notion of equilibrium given in
Definition

1.1
.
In
itel
, the notion of equilibrium for aligned states coincides with the notion of equilibrium for their benchmark action profile.
In
ritel
, we need to take into account quantization and weakly aligned states. In particular, the equilibrium inequality itself needs to be relaxed by allowing small improvements.
We first define this notion for action profiles, then for weakly aligned states.

Definition 4.9

(
\rho
-Equilibrium Action Profile)
.

An action profile
\boldsymbol{a}
with resulting average utilities
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is a
\rho
-equilibrium if for all agent
i
,
i
is at a
\rho
-equilibrium, i.e., for any action
a^{\prime}_{i}\neq a_{i}
,

.

An action profile is at a
\rho
-equilibrium if no agent can explore such that it would improve its mean utility by at least

Regarding weakly aligned states, notice that for a given action profile
\boldsymbol{a}
there are several weakly aligned states with benchmark
\boldsymbol{\overline{a}}=\boldsymbol{a}
but with different benchmark bins
\boldsymbol{\overline{v}}
—although only one strongly aligned state. How stable the state is depends on
\boldsymbol{\overline{v}}
since higher benchmarks are harder to leave, so the definition of equilibrium takes both
\boldsymbol{\overline{a}}
and
\boldsymbol{\overline{v}}

Definition 4.10

(
\rho
-Equilibrium State)
.

A weakly aligned state with benchmark actions
\boldsymbol{\overline{a}}
and utility bins
\boldsymbol{\overline{v}}

\rho
-equilibrium if for all agent

,
i

\rho
-equilibrium, i.e., for any action
a_{i}\neq\overline{a}_{i}

When
\rho=\delta
, a state is at a
\delta
-equilibrium if no agent can explore in a way so that it would observe a mean utility high enough that it can accept it, hence the notion of
\delta
-equilibrium state reflects how stable a state it is in

On the other hand, the notion of
\rho
-equilibrium for action profiles is completely unrelated to the

This notion is only used to translate the convergence statement of

Although closely related, both notions of equilibrium do not correspond directly, that is, a

-equilibrium state does not necessarily imply a

-equilibrium action profile. In fact,

It remains to discuss optimal states.
A weakly aligned agent is said to be optimized if its benchmark utility
\overline{v}

\{1\}

\delta

is
\delta

A “strong” set

We now move on to the notions of welfare and stability, along with their virtual counterparts, defined in

F

Definition 4.12

(Welfare and Stability of an Action Profile)

Let
\boldsymbol{a}
be an action profile with mean utilities
\boldsymbol{\mu}

•

W(\boldsymbol{a})\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}\sum_{i}\mu_{i}

•

S(\boldsymbol{a})\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}\max\big\{M_{i}(a^{\prime}_{i},a_{-i})-\mu_{i}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \textrm{$i\in I$ and $a^{\prime}_{i}\neq a_{i}$ such that $M_{i}(a^{\prime}_{i},a_{-i})>\mu_{i}$}\big\}

Definition 4.13

(Virtual Welfare and Stability of an Action Profile)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{a}
be an action profile with mean utilities
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. We define

•

\tilde{W}_{+}(\boldsymbol{a})\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}1-\sum_{i}F(\mu_{i}+\delta)

•

\tilde{W}_{-}(\boldsymbol{a})\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}1-\sum_{i}F(\mu_{i}-\delta)

•

\tilde{S}_{+}(\boldsymbol{a})\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}1-\min\big\{G\big(\mu_{i}-\delta,M_{i}(a^{\prime}_{i},a_{-i})+\delta\big)\penalty\ |\penalty\ \textrm{$i\in I$ and $a^{\prime}_{i}\neq a_{i}$ such that $M_{i}(a^{\prime}_{i},a_{-i})>\mu_{i}$}\big\}

•

\tilde{S}_{-}(\boldsymbol{a})\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}1-\min\big\{G\big(\mu_{i}+\delta,M_{i}(a^{\prime}_{i},a_{-i})-\delta\big)\penalty\ |\penalty\ \textrm{$i\in I$ and $a^{\prime}_{i}\neq a_{i}$ such that $M_{i}(a^{\prime}_{i},a_{-i})>\mu_{i}+3\delta$}\big\}

\tilde{S}_{+}(\boldsymbol{a})
is defined only if
x
is not a
\delta
-equilibrium and
\tilde{S}_{-}(\boldsymbol{a})
only if
x
is not a
3\delta
-equilibrium.

The subscript

(resp.

ritel

x=(\boldsymbol{\overline{m}},\boldsymbol{\overline{a}},\boldsymbol{\overline{v}})\in\mathcal{C}
there may be an offset of
\delta
at most between the utilities
\boldsymbol{\overline{v}}^{-}
used for the functions
F
and

The link between true welfare and stability and these quantities is discussed in the linear case at the end of
Section

.

Regarding the virtual welfare and stability of states, the randomness of the observation makes the analysis of acceptations from a content state more complex. Indeed, an agent of benchmark
\overline{v}
may accept the mean bin
\nu
of its exploration with resistance equal to

when
\nu\geq\overline{v}+2\delta
. It may also accept any higher bin
v
with resistance equal to
r_{U}(v)+G(\overline{v},v)
where
U
is the distribution of the observed utility. This resistance may be lower than that of accepting
\nu
if the non-increasing nature of

compensate for the addition of

.
For this reason, we also introduce two quantities
\tilde{S}_{+}
and

that act as upper and lower bounds on the virtual stability of a state.

Definition 4.14

(Virtual Welfare and Stability of a State)
.

Let
x=(\boldsymbol{\overline{m}},\boldsymbol{\overline{a}},\boldsymbol{\overline{v}})\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}
. We define

•

\tilde{W}(x)\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}1-\sum_{i}F(\overline{v}_{i})
,

•

\tilde{S}_{+}(x)\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}1-\min\big\{G(\overline{v}_{i},N_{i}(a_{i},\overline{a}_{-i})+\delta)\penalty\ |\penalty\ \textrm{$i\in I$ and $a_{i}\neq\overline{a}_{i}$ such that $N_{i}(a_{i},\overline{a}_{-i})\geq\overline{v}_{i}+\delta$}\big\}

•

\tilde{S}_{-}(x)\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}1-\min\big\{G(\overline{v}_{i},N_{i}(a_{i},\overline{a}_{-i}))\penalty\ |\penalty\ \textrm{$i\in I$ and $a_{i}\neq\overline{a}_{i}$ such that $N_{i}(a_{i},\overline{a}_{-i})\geq\overline{v}_{i}+2\delta$}\big\}

is defined only if

is not an equilibrium and
\tilde{S}_{-}(x)
only if
x
is not a
\delta
-equilibrium.

Notice that

when both are defined, i.e., when

\delta
-equilibrium.
The following lemma states the correspondences that are required to convert the convergence result of
ritel
in terms of states and their virtual welfare and stability to a convergence result in terms of action profiles and their welfare and stability.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
x
be a state with benchmark actions
\boldsymbol{\overline{a}}
. The following holds.

(i)

If
x\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}
,
\tilde{W}(x)\leq\tilde{W}_{+}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})
.

(ii)

If
x\in\mathcal{C}
,
\tilde{W}(x)\geq\tilde{W}_{-}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})
.

(iii)

If
x\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
,
\tilde{S}_{-}(x)\geq\tilde{S}_{-}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})
.

(iv)

If
x\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{E}
,
\tilde{S}_{+}(x)\leq\tilde{S}_{+}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})
.

Proof.

Let
x=(\boldsymbol{\overline{m}},\boldsymbol{\overline{a}},\boldsymbol{\overline{v}})\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}
and denote
\boldsymbol{\mu}
the mean utilities of
\boldsymbol{\overline{a}}
.
Recall that
F
and
G
are defined over bins by replacing the bins with their lower edge:
F(v)=F(v^{-})
and
G(\overline{v},v)=G(\overline{v}^{-},v^{-})
.

(i)

If
x
is weakly aligned, then
\mu_{i}\geq\overline{v}_{i}^{-}-\delta
for each agent
i
.
F
is non-increasing, hence

1-\tilde{W}(x)=\sum_{i}F(\overline{v}_{i}^{-})\geq\sum_{i}F(\mu_{i}+\delta)=1-\tilde{W}_{+}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})\penalty\ .

(ii)

If
x
is strongly aligned, then
\mu_{i}\leq\overline{v}_{i}^{-}+\delta
for each agent
i
.
F
is non-increasing, hence

1-\tilde{W}(x)=\sum_{i}F(\overline{v}_{i}^{-})\leq\sum_{i}F(\mu_{i}-\delta)=1-\tilde{W}_{-}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})\penalty\ .

(iii)

If the minimum in the definition of
\tilde{S}_{-}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})
is over an empty set, the result reads
\tilde{S}(x)\geq{-\infty}
which is immediate.
Else, denote
a_{i}\neq\overline{a}_{i}
the action that achieves the minimum.
M_{i}(a_{i},\overline{a}_{-i})>M_{i}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})+3\delta
so
N_{i}(a_{i},\overline{a}_{-i})\geq N_{i}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})+3\delta\geq\overline{v}_{i}+2\delta
since
x
is weakly aligned, which also implies that
M_{i}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})\geq\overline{v}_{i}^{-}-\delta
.
G
is non-decreasing in the first argument and non-increasing in the second, hence

1-\tilde{S}_{-}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})=G\big(M_{i}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})+\delta,M_{i}(a_{i},\overline{a}_{-i})-\delta\big)\geq G\big(\overline{v}_{i}^{-},M_{i}(a_{i},\overline{a}_{-i})-\delta\big)\geq G\big(\overline{v}_{i}^{-},N_{i}(a_{i},\overline{a}_{-i})^{-}\big)\geq 1-\tilde{S}_{-}(x)

where the last inequality holds due to the fact that
N_{i}(a_{i},\overline{a}_{-i})\geq\overline{v}_{i}+2\delta
.

(iv)

Denote
a_{i}\neq\overline{a}_{i}
the action that achieves the minimum in the definition of
\tilde{S}_{+}(x)
and
v_{i}=N_{i}(a_{i},\overline{a}_{-i})
, so that
\tilde{S}_{+}(x)=1-G(\overline{v}_{i}^{-},v_{i}^{-}+\delta)
.
x
is strongly aligned so
M_{i}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})<\overline{v}_{i}^{-}+\delta
.
G
is non-decreasing in the first argument and non-increasing in the second, hence

1-\tilde{S}_{+}(x)=G\big(\overline{v}_{i}^{-},v_{i}^{-}+\delta\big)\geq G\big(M_{i}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})-\delta,v_{i}^{-}+\delta\big)\geq G\big(M_{i}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})-\delta,M_{i}(a_{i},\overline{a}_{-i})+\delta\big)\geq 1-\tilde{S}_{+}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})

where the last inequality holds due to the fact that
v_{i}\geq\overline{v}_{i}+\delta
, hence
M_{i}(a_{i},\overline{a}_{-i})\geq v_{i}^{-}\geq\overline{v}_{i}^{-}+\delta>M_{i}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})
.
∎

4.4.2
Noise Resistance

Let us discuss the resistance associated with an agent’s decision. In
itel
such resistances were derived directly from the policies given in
Table

1
.
Here, the resistance of observing a utility enabling a given decision needs to be added to the resistance of the decision itself which is derived from
Table

3

In the example where a content agent who explored accepts its exploration with new benchmark bin
v
, recall that the probability of such decision given the chosen action profile is
\mathbb{P}\big(U^{(\tau)}\in v\big)\varepsilon^{G(\overline{v},v)}
. The associated resistance is
r_{U}(v)+G(\overline{v},v)
where
r_{U}(v)
is the resistance associated with
U^{(\tau)}
belonging to
v
, defined in
Corollary

4.5
.
In particular, recall that observing the mean bin has no resistance, whereas the resistance of observing other bins is lower bounded using
Equation

5
.
We are particularly interested in lower bounding the resistance of offset observation, which we recall corresponds to observing an average utility that is not weakly aligned with its theoretical mean.

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
U
be a utility distribution with support in
[0,1]
and mean bin
\nu
. Then for all
v\neq\nu\pm\delta
,

\displaystyle r_{U}(v)\geq R_{0}\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}2\tau_{0}\delta^{2}\penalty\ .

Proof.

Let
U
be a utility distribution,
\mu
its mean utility and
\nu=|\mu|
its mean bin.
If
U
is deterministic, the result is immediate since
\mathbb{P}(U\in\nu)=1
.
Else, according to
Corollaries

4.5
and
5
, the resistance associated with observing bin
v\geq\nu+2\delta
is

\displaystyle r_{U}(v)=\tau_{0}\Lambda^{*}_{U}(v^{-})\geq\tau_{0}\Lambda^{*}_{U}(\mu+\delta)\geq 2\tau_{0}(\mu+\delta-\mu)^{2}=2\tau_{0}\delta^{2}

as
\Lambda^{*}_{U}
is increasing over
[\mu,{+\infty})
and
\mu+\delta\leq\nu^{+}+\delta=\nu^{-}+2\delta\leq v^{-}
.
The same reasoning holds when
v\leq\nu-2\delta
and then
r_{U}(v)\geq\tau_{0}\Lambda^{*}_{U}(\mu-\delta)\geq 2\tau_{0}\delta^{2}
.
∎

We now define the noise resistance of a state, which is the resistance associated with one agent observing a utility that is not aligned with its benchmark, in the event where the benchmark action profile is selected.

Definition 4.17

(Noise Resistance)
.

Let
x=(\boldsymbol{\overline{m}},\boldsymbol{\overline{a}},\boldsymbol{\overline{v}})\in\mathcal{X}
. We define the

\displaystyle\tilde{R}(x)\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}\min\big\{r_{U_{i}(\boldsymbol{\overline{a}})}(v)\penalty\ |\penalty\ v\neq\overline{v}_{i}\pm\delta\big\}\penalty\ .

(i).

5.1

In

The results from

A key idea is that two states are merged into a cycle if there is a path going from one to the other and back using only easy edges.

The results are presented using quantities that depend on the cycle decomposition.
In the following, consider a

(P^{\varepsilon})

(

5.2
Application to ITEL

The above results apply to all

.
They mostly serve a theoretical purpose:

hints that the cooling schedule should be of order
\varepsilon_{k}=k^{-1/\Gamma_{0}}
at the fastest.
Moreover, the optimal convergence speed given by

for finite horizon

The precise definition of

and of the optimal cooling schedule are complex and can be found in the proof of

on which

is based.
These definitions involve problem-dependent quantities and their precise computation in the case of

The cooling rate and the convergence speed are both of polynomial order, which is not very fast in practice. In particular, an exponentially decreasing cooling schedule may give better result, although it will theoretically not converge.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a more efficient algorithm for distributed learning in the case where rewards are bounded. We also extended the scope of the algorithm to random games with minor assumptions of the utility distributions and showed that with appropriate parameters the algorithm keeps its convergence properties.
Finally, we briefly discussed the possibility of a dynamic perturbation to obtain a stronger sense of convergence towards optimal states.

\mathcal{E}\neq\emptyset
are included in the ones described in the case
\mathcal{E}=\emptyset
.
Likewise, it is not possible to know whether
\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
or not solely from the mean utilities of action profiles, as this information requires the knowledge of the support of the utility distributions. However, the action profiles described in the case
\mathcal{A}\neq\emptyset
are included in the ones described in the other cases.
This concludes
Theorem

4.22
.
