Completing the referral loop: accountable handovers for social prescribing in Japan
Authors: Yusuke Shono
Published: 2026

In my Osaka outpatient practice, I see up to 150 patients a day. People rarely walk in saying they are socially isolated. Instead, they present with insomnia, non-specific symptoms, or requests for medication. Only after multiple consultations does bereavement or the loss of everyday relationships come into focus. These struggles often become visible in the consulting room before they show up in administrative data. For many, returning to a familiar clinic is easier than navigating a social service directly.

Yamasaki and colleagues rightly warn against medicalising social problems, such as prescribing hypnotics for isolation-driven insomnia.1 Their proposed response is broad: welfare and housing take the lead on social determinants, while social prescribing and community comprehensive support centres connect clinical care to community support.1

What remains unclear is how this link works from the clinician's side and what constitutes a successful handover. Based on my clinical experience, one implementation proposal is a closed referral loop with four components: the clinician would recognise the social need and obtain the patient's agreement to proceed. A specific person or team—with a name and a telephone number, not a leaflet—would receive the referral. In my clinic, a patient handed a leaflet rarely makes the call. The receiving organisation would communicate acceptance, deferral, or refusal. If connection failed, a designated person would follow up, offer an alternative, and update the clinician. These are not established requirements.

Such a model would not make primary care responsible for resolving social isolation. Rather, it would test whether the consultation can start an accountable handover, transferring responsibility instead of returning it to the clinician by default. This model has not been shown to improve outcomes in Japan. Implementation would require adequate staffing and sustainable funding in receiving organisations for coordination and follow-up.

A scoping review found that social prescribing across the Western Pacific is hindered by limited resources, fragmented referral systems, and a lack of standardised evaluation.2 If piloted in Japan, evaluation could examine feasibility and patient outcomes alongside process measures such as disposition, time to first contact, connection to support, and feedback to the clinician.

Such studies could clarify who receives referrals, how quickly, how the pathway is funded, and who remains accountable when connections fail. Their findings could inform a renewed social contract. Otherwise, we risk perpetuating the medicalisation of social distress or leaving clinicians unable to act on what continuity of care has revealed.
