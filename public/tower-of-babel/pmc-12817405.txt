Caregiver awareness and preparedness regarding pediatric foreign body aspiration and emergency response in Poland: a cross-sectional observational study
Authors: Maciej Szczukocki, Andrzej Pogorzelski, Henryk Mazurek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractForeign body aspiration (FBA) is a critical pediatric emergency that poses significant health risks, particularly in children under 7 years old. This cross-sectional observational study aimed to evaluate the knowledge of caregivers in Poland regarding FBA risks, symptom recognition, prevention strategies, and first aid measures. A total of 333 caregivers completed a 23-item questionnaire assessing socio-demographic characteristics and knowledge of FBA and choking management. Associations between demographic variables and knowledge scores were analyzed using multivariable negative-binomial regression. The mean accuracy across all knowledge questions was 80.48%, with the highest proportion of incorrect answers related to recognizing effective and ineffective cough and appropriate actions for infants with ineffective cough. Formal medical education and first aid training were the strongest predictors of higher knowledge scores, increasing the expected number of correct answers by 19–30% and 9–18%, respectively. General education level was not significantly associated with knowledge. Despite generally good overall knowledge, substantial deficits were observed in recognizing and managing time-sensitive choking scenarios. The results highlight the need for targeted, pediatric-specific first aid programs integrated into prenatal, educational, and community health systems to enhance caregivers’ ability to promptly recognize and respond to choking emergencies in young children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06412-y.Keywords: Foreign body aspiration, Knowledge gaps, Pediatric emergency, Prevention strategies, First aid actions

Background

Foreign body aspiration (FBA) is a critical pediatric emergency that poses significant health risks, particularly in children under the age of 7, owing to their developmental stage and exploratory behaviors. It is crucial to understand and address FBA, as failure to promptly recognize and treat it can result in severe respiratory complications or even death. FBA has been reported as one of the leading causes of accidental death in young children, with the highest prevalence among those under 3 years of age [1, 2]. Moreover, delays in diagnosis and inappropriate first responses have been associated with fatal cases [3].

Although FBA is a well-documented medical event, there is a paucity of data concerning parental awareness and ability to prevent and manage such emergencies [4]. While some studies have investigated parental awareness in Asia, Southern Europe, and Africa [5–10], little research has been conducted on Eastern European populations. Despite the frequent occurrence of FBA, with some single-center studies reporting over 1000 pediatric cases [11], comprehensive studies assessing parental knowledge of its risks, symptoms, and effective first aid measures are lacking.

This gap in knowledge is concerning, given that prompt identification and intervention are crucial to prevent complications such as chronic pulmonary infections or irreversible lung damage [12]. Additionally, despite existing guidelines for airway obstruction management [13], parental familiarity with these protocols remains limited, particularly regarding first aid maneuvers, such as the Heimlich technique, and knowing when to seek urgent medical help.

Despite clear epidemiologic evidence that most aspiration events involve familiar, everyday foods and occur under adult supervision [14–16], fatalities continue to be reported worldwide [17].

This study aimed to evaluate the extent of knowledge among parents of children under 7 years of age regarding the risks associated with FBA, as well as symptom recognition, prevention strategies, and first aid measures. In addition, it aimed to identify knowledge gaps by assessing parental knowledge and preparedness in Poland, where distinct cultural, educational, and healthcare contexts may affect parental responses to FBA emergencies. This assessment is crucial for developing targeted educational programs aimed at enhancing child safety.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted in Poland from January to March 2025. The primary aim was to assess the knowledge of caregivers of children aged 0 to 7 years regarding FBA and appropriate first aid responses in choking incidents. This design allowed for data collection at a single point in time, providing an overview of caregivers’ awareness and practices during a defined period.

The survey was conducted in pediatric outpatient settings. Participants were recruited during regular medical consultations and invited to complete the questionnaire using a QR code or direct electronic link. No personal identifiers were collected, and participation was anonymous and voluntary.

Of the 14 knowledge questions, 8 were based on a validated questionnaire developed by Higuchi et al. [18] to assess maternal knowledge of aspiration risks and preventive behaviors in children. The remaining 6 questions were developed by the research team based on current European Resuscitation Council guidelines [13]. Before deployment, the questionnaire was pretested on 10 parents for clarity and technical functionality. An English version of the full questionnaire used in this study is available as Supplementary File 1.

Data collection and eligibility

Participants were recruited during pediatric outpatient visits. Healthcare staff provided access to the survey via a QR code or URL. Respondents completed the questionnaire on their mobile devices.

Of the 357 submitted responses, 24 were excluded due to incomplete data or logical inconsistencies. A total of 333 responses were included in the statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Three prespecified composite outcomes were defined to address multiplicity and to align with the questionnaire structure:

FBA Knowledge Score (items Q1–Q8),

FBA Knowledge Score (items Q1–Q8),

First-Aid Knowledge Score (items Q9–Q14), and.

First-Aid Knowledge Score (items Q9–Q14), and.

Total Knowledge Score (sum of all 14 items).

Total Knowledge Score (sum of all 14 items).

Each score represented the count of correct (“Yes”) answers in the respective item block. Higher values indicated better knowledge. These composite outcomes were treated as the primary endpoints, while item-level analyses were considered secondary/exploratory.

To model the associations between socio-demographic predictors and the composite knowledge scores, multivariable negative binomial regression models were used. This approach was selected because the dependent variables were discrete counts, and the negative binomial distribution accounts for overdispersion that violates Poisson assumptions.

Model coefficients were expressed as parameters (prevalence ratios) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), representing multiplicative changes in the expected number of correct answers per unit change in the predictor.

The following covariates were included based on a priori conceptual reasoning and previous literature: age, sex, medical education, relationship status, number of children in the household, place of residence (rural/<100k/>100k inhabitants), education level, and any formal first-aid training (yes/no).

Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with all values < 5, confirming the absence of collinearity. No missing data were detected; therefore, all analyses were conducted on complete cases (N = 333).

For representativeness and sensitivity, an additional subset analysis was performed among female respondents with university education (n = 263) — the largest homogeneous stratum in the dataset.

All analyses were performed in R version 4.5.0, with two-tailed p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analysis (Mothers with university education)

In the female-only, university-educated subgroup, patterns remained consistent: medical education remained a significant predictor of better first-aid knowledge (+ 29.9%, p < 0.001), while no variable was associated with FBA knowledge or total score.

Knowledge assessment overview

In the Knowledge Assessment section, the mean accuracy (proportion of ‘Yes’ answers) was 80.48%, indicating good general knowledge. The highest proportion of ‘No’ answers was observed for questions regarding the definition of an ineffective cough (41.7%), an effective cough (40.8%), and the appropriate initial action for conscious infants who are not coughing effectively (19.8%).

Exploratory item-level analyses additionally suggested that male caregivers tended to show lower accuracy in recognizing the features of an effective cough, while respondents from large urban areas demonstrated slightly poorer awareness of safe feeding behaviors, such as avoiding walking or laughing during meals. These trends were not statistically significant in the final multivariable models but are consistent with behavioral patterns observed in other populations.

Representativeness and missing data

The sample remained predominantly female (93%) and university-educated (79%), reflecting potential selection bias inherent to outpatient recruitment. The sensitivity analysis restricted to mothers did not materially change results. No missing data were identified in any variable.

Discussion

This study provides an updated and comprehensive overview of caregivers’ knowledge and preparedness regarding pediatric foreign body aspiration (FBA) and first aid for choking in young children. By analyzing composite knowledge domains it offers a broad reliable picture of the factors that determine parental competence in responding to this life-threatening pediatric emergency.

Educational background and training as primary determinants

Formal medical education and structured first-aid training were identified as the strongest independent predictors of higher knowledge levels. Caregivers with a medical background achieved 19–30% more correct responses, while those who had completed a certified first-aid course scored 9–18% higher overall. These findings are consistent with prior reports highlighting the beneficial effect of structured educational programs on preparedness for airway emergencies [19–22]. They also emphasize that practical, scenario-based instruction—rather than general theoretical knowledge—is crucial for competence in real-life choking events.

Although nearly four out of five respondents held a university degree, general education did not translate into adequate procedural knowledge. Similar discrepancies between academic attainment and emergency response competence have been reported in studies from Saudi Arabia [5, 7], and Croatia [9], suggesting that traditional schooling does not necessarily develop the specific cognitive and motor skills needed in acute pediatric emergencies. This distinction between formal schooling and functional health literacy highlights the need for targeted, applied education rather than purely informational campaigns.

Persistent knowledge gaps in critical areas

Despite generally good overall knowledge, substantial deficits were observed in recognizing and managing the most time-sensitive choking scenarios. Over 40% of caregivers could not correctly distinguish an effective from an ineffective cough, and one in five did not know the correct intervention for an infant with an ineffective cough. These findings are clinically meaningful because such recognition is central to determining whether observation, intervention, or emergency response is required. Similar deficits have been reported internationally, with 35–45% of parents unable to identify choking symptoms or correct first-aid actions [5, 8, 9].

Defining the type of cough is a difficult task due to the lack of clear-cut criteria. It has been shown that the seemingly simple distinction between productive and dry cough poses difficulties not only for parents but even for experts. Twenty video clips of children with different types of cough were presented to eight specialists for interpretation. Only 13 clips (50%) were consistent with the experts’ opinions, 5 clips (20%) had differing opinions from one specialist, and 8 clips (32%) had no consensus on the nature of the cough. Next, parents of 48 children with chronic cough were presented with 13 selected clips, on which the experts’ opinions were clear. 52.5% of parents’ opinions on the 13 clear clips were consistent with the doctors’ opinions, but 20% were contradictory. As many as 27.5% selected both the wet and productive cough clips [23].

The context of training was equally important. Caregivers who reported acquiring first-aid information in driving schools showed poorer understanding of the high-risk age group for FBA, a finding that mirrors concerns about the superficial pediatric content of generic first-aid curricula [20]. This suggests that mandatory training platforms, while widespread, may inadvertently reinforce misconceptions if their scope and teaching methods are not standardized. Revising these courses to include pediatric airway management could yield substantial public-health benefits at minimal additional cost.

Sociodemographic patterns and behavioral context

Although composite models did not identify sex or residence as significant independent predictors, exploratory analyses provided relevant insights. Male caregivers were less likely to correctly identify features of an effective cough, and residents of large urban centers demonstrated poorer awareness of safe feeding behaviors—such as avoiding walking or laughing during meals. Comparable patterns have been observed in other cultural contexts, where lower paternal involvement and reliance on informal or online information sources were associated with reduced practical preparedness [24–27]. These findings point to behavioral and sociocultural barriers rather than purely informational gaps, suggesting that awareness campaigns should consider gender roles and urban lifestyles when designing educational strategies.

Bridging awareness and procedural competence

The contrast between declarative knowledge and procedural capability remains a central challenge. While most caregivers recognize that choking is dangerous, many are unable to correctly describe an effective or ineffective cough (approximately 40%) or to perform the appropriate sequence of first-aid actions (around 20%). Effective interventions must therefore move beyond knowledge dissemination to behavioral acquisition—focusing on recognition of signs, confident decision-making, and correct performance of rescue maneuvers under stress. Studies from Turkey and Ethiopia have shown that interactive, demonstration-based education significantly improves both knowledge retention and self-efficacy in pediatric first aid [8, 10]. Embedding such approaches into antenatal courses, daycare inductions, and pediatric visits could help convert awareness into competence.

Public health implications

The results highlight a need to systematize pediatric airway safety education across societal levels. Integrating standardized first-aid training into pregnancy programs, parental schools, and early childhood institutions could ensure coverage during key developmental periods. Brief clinician-led training during well-child visits—where parents can practice choking maneuvers—may represent a cost-effective strategy to reinforce knowledge. Although this study lacked an interventional component, the observed knowledge gaps indicate such educational programs are warranted. Future studies should evaluate the effectiveness of caregiver knowledge following structured pediatric first-aid training, to guide evidence-based public health interventions. International experience confirms that such short, clinician-delivered interventions substantially improve caregiver confidence and skill retention [20, 28].

At the national level, the revision of driving-school curricula offers a unique opportunity to include pediatric first-aid modules within an existing mandatory training framework. Standardizing such courses to align with the European Resuscitation Council (ERC) pediatric life support guidelines [13] could markedly enhance the population’s baseline readiness for pediatric choking incidents.

Conclusions

Substantial and clinically relevant knowledge gaps persist among caregivers of young children, even in a highly educated sample. Medical education and formal first-aid training demonstrably improve preparedness, whereas unstandardized educational contexts, such as driving-school courses, may perpetuate misinformation. These results emphasize the need for structured, pediatric-specific first-aid programs integrated into prenatal, educational, and community health systems. Ensuring that caregivers can promptly recognize and respond to choking remains a critical, achievable objective for pediatric public health in Poland and beyond.
