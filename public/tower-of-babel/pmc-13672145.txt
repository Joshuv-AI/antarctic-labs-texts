Surrogate Scattering Matrix‐Guided Inverse Design of Nanophotonic Neural Networks
Authors: Azka Maula Iskandar Muda, Uğur Teğin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOptical computing offers a route to address the growing computational demands of machine learning, with inverse‐designed nanophotonic media providing a compact path to passive optical operators. Training such devices end to end remains expensive because each geometry update requires full‐wave forward and adjoint evaluations. We introduce a surrogate‐guided inverse‐design framework that separates task learning from electromagnetic realization. Classification is first solved in matrix space using a passive complex operator with bounded singular values, and the resulting target is transferred to a fabrication‐aware freeform device through complex transmission matching and reflection suppression. Because the realization target is fixed, the electromagnetic work per update is determined by the input and output port counts and is independent of training‐set size. A locality‐constrained router composed with a fixed evanescent mixing stage extends the approach to 16‐port transformations. Under the same physical initialization, solver, fabrication map, and 550‐solve budget, operator matching reached 98.34% test accuracy on MedNIST, compared with 89.50% for direct geometry‐to‐task training. On RSSCN7, the combined optical surrogate reached 48.21%, compared with 38.57% for a PCA‐16 ridge classifier. Three Yin–Yang realizations reached 91.44±2.02%. These results establish surrogate‐guided operator matching for compact, fabrication‐aware photonic neural networks.Keywords: adjoint optimization, image classification, inverse design, nanophotonic media, optical computing, photonic neural networks

Introduction

Deep neural networks now drive a broad range of applications, from image recognition to natural language processing. Training these models demands growing amounts of compute, and digital electronics is approaching its efficiency limits. Optical computing offers a hardware route to address this bottleneck. Photonic neural networks perform matrix–vector multiplication at the speed of light with passive components. Several architectures have been demonstrated, including coherent Mach–Zehnder meshes [1, 2, 3], diffractive deep networks [4], phase‐change photonic tensor cores [5], and on‐chip photonic classifiers [6]. The unitary factors in programmable meshes follow the Reck and Clements decompositions [7, 8]; the bounded singular values in Equation (1) extend this representation to passive nonunitary operators. Recent reviews frame these systems as physical neural networks whose capacity is set by the interplay between trainable parameters and hardware physics [9, 10, 11, 12].

Wave‐based analog computing established scattering‐space operator design through metamaterial mathematical operations [13], inverse‐designed equation solvers [14], parallel operators in one structure [15], and inverse‐designed structures combined with reconfigurable couplers [16]. Inverse‐designed nanophotonic media offer a compact route to optical linear operators. The entire passive device is represented as a continuous permittivity distribution and optimized so that its scattering matrix implements the desired map [17, 18]. Gradients are computed with the adjoint method [19]. Khoram et al. showed that freeform nanophotonic media can perform neural inference [20]. Nikkhah et al. experimentally realized vector–matrix multiplication in low‐index‐contrast structures [21]. Zhao et al. and Sved et al. subsequently increased the computational density and scale of these devices [22, 23]. Fabrication‐aware maps, such as conic filtering and subpixel‐smoothed projection [24], impose minimum‐feature constraints during optimization.

Training an inverse‐designed photonic classifier end to end remains expensive because each geometry update requires full‐wave forward and adjoint evaluations. For a linear optical device, field responses can be reused across samples by superposition. The relevant electromagnetic solve count is set by the excited input and adjoint output ports and is independent of dataset minibatch size.

Here, we decouple these roles with a two‐stage surrogate workflow. In the first stage, the trainable optical block is optimized directly in matrix space using a singular‐value‐bounded parameterization. The resulting operator T⋆ is then transferred to a fabrication‐aware freeform device through an adjoint problem. The realization objective is a Frobenius‐norm residual between the simulated transmission matrix and T⋆, together with a reflection penalty. Because T⋆ is fixed, one update uses Nin+Nout source solves and is independent of the training‐set size. The task‐trained target and the local‐router/fixed‐mixing factorization distinguish this method from established prescribed‐operator matching.

Our second contribution addresses a layout bottleneck in inverse‐designed routers. Routing power between ports separated across the device width increases the required propagation distance along the routing axis. We train a banded router through a differentiable power‐concentration term while retaining every complex matrix entry as a trainable variable. The router is then composed with a fixed downstream evanescent‐coupling region that supplies global mixing [25, 26].

We validate the framework on three tasks. For MedNIST [27], operator matching reaches 98.34% test accuracy under the same 550‐solve budget for which direct geometry‐to‐task optimization reaches 89.50%. For RSSCN7 [28], the combined optical surrogate reaches 48.21%, compared with 38.57% for a PCA‐16 ridge classifier. The Yin–Yang dataset [29] tests the same framework with intensity detection and a digital readout, reaching 91.44±2.02% across three physical initializations. Materials and Methods

The workflow has two stages. First, we solve the learning task in a low‐cost surrogate space by representing the trainable optical block directly as a passive matrix. We then transfer the selected surrogate operator to a fabrication‐aware nanophotonic device through adjoint inverse design. This separation removes dataset batching from the expensive electromagnetic loop and lets the full‐wave solver focus on matching a target scattering response.

Preprocessing, Encoding, and Locality

To satisfy the available port budget, we preprocess the MedNIST [27] and RSSCN7 [28] datasets before optical encoding. MedNIST contains 58,954 grayscale 64×64 images in six classes: AbdomenCT, BreastMRI, CXR, ChestCT, Hand, and HeadCT. A deterministic stratified split with Seed 7 assigns 48,000 images to training and 10,954 to testing. We flatten and normalize the images to [0, 1], then reduce them to 16 dimensions by principal component analysis [31],

so that the optical input dimension matches the available number of ports. RSSCN7 contains 400 images in each of seven classes. We use a deterministic stratified 80/20 partition, giving 2240 training and 560 test images. PCA and coordinate scaling are fitted only on the training partition for both datasets.

The study uses two optical encoding families

The former is a real‐amplitude encoding, and the latter is a phase‐only encoding on the unit circle. MedNIST uses the real PCA coordinates as amplitudes. For RSSCN7, each training‐fitted PCA coordinate is scaled and clipped to x∈[−1,1], so exp(−iπx) spans [−π,π]. The Yin–Yang task uses a separate amplitude‐phase encoder described below.

A preliminary surrogate‐only sweep over hard half‐bandwidths w=1,…,15 and a dense matrix used 10 predetermined seeds and training‐only validation accuracy. The mean accuracy was 51.90±0.88% at w=5, compared with 50.80±1.23% at w=4, 51.32±1.26% at w=8, and 52.48±0.72% for the dense matrix. Because a hard w=5 matrix retains 146 of 256 entries, we selected w=5 as an accuracy–locality compromise; the full sweep is reported in Supporting Information S1: Figure S2. The reported RSSCN7 router then uses this width as the neighborhood of a differentiable locality constraint rather than as a hard mask. Every complex entry remains trainable, whereas the surrogate loss assigns a 10% power budget to entries outside |i−j|≤5. The selected target is fully dense, places 89.97% of its power within five port spacings, and has a measured out‐of‐band power fraction of 10.03%. The fixed evanescent stage redistributes this locally concentrated router response across the output ports. The reported end‐to‐end operator is the product ETR, as illustrated in Figure 2.

The three datasets studied here instantiate the same framework in different regimes. MedNIST is an all‐optical example with 16 optical inputs and 6 output classes. RSSCN7 uses 16 optical inputs, a learned 16×16 local router, a fixed 16×16 evanescent array (EVA), and a linear readout on detected intensities. The fixed‐stage response is obtained by FDTD. For the Yin–Yang dataset [29], a seed‐7 rejection sampler generates 6000 balanced points inside the unit‐diameter circle. Each accepted point (x,y) is represented by (x,y,1−x,1−y) and assigned to yin, yang, or dot using the two 0.1‐radius dots and the Yin–Yang regions defined by Kriener et al. A deterministic stratified 80/20 split gives 4800 training and 1200 test samples. Each coordinate v∈[0,1] is encoded as vexp(−i2πv). The four‐input, six‐output optical structure remains linear in the complex field; the input‐dependent encoding, square‐law detection, and digital readout produce the nonlinear decision boundary in the original coordinates.

Results

We evaluate the method on three tasks. MedNIST is an all‐optical classification problem in which the realized operator maps a 16‐dimensional feature vector directly to six class channels. RSSCN7 uses a learned 16×16 local router followed by a fixed 16×16 evanescent array and a digital readout. The Yin–Yang dataset tests the same realization procedure on a nonlinear decision boundary produced by coherent encoding, intensity detection, and a digital readout. Together, these tasks probe transfer of a high‐performing surrogate, locality‐constrained routing with fixed downstream mixing, and nonlinear classification. The surrogate comparisons use fixed protocols and report the final surrogate epoch without test‐set model selection. Chance accuracies are 16.67%, 14.29%, and 33.33%, respectively.

Nonlinear Yin–Yang Task

The Yin–Yang experiment uses a four‐input, six‐output freeform device followed by intensity detection and a digital readout. Three 25‐update realizations start from one uniform density and two random freeform initializations (Seeds 17 and 23). Final fixed‐readout accuracies are 89.17%, 92.17%, and 93.00%, giving 91.44±2.02% and a range of 89.17%–93.00%. The transmission residual is 0.110±0.057, the reflection residual is 0.0147±0.0051, and mean input insertion loss is 4.87±0.40 dB.

Each operator‐matching realization is paired with a direct task‐loss realization from the identical physical initialization. A multinomial readout is fitted separately to the training‐set optical powers of each update‐25 response and evaluated on the held‐out test partition. Operator matching reaches 93.17%, 92.42%, and 92.33% for the uniform, seed‐17, and seed‐23 initializations, compared with 88.50%, 91.25%, and 90.75% for direct task loss. The corresponding means are 92.64% and 90.17%, a difference of 2.47% points, with operator matching higher in all three pairs.

Physical Validation

At 1550 nm, the binary Yin–Yang device obtains 92.58% in the 2D effective‐index model and 81.58% in 3D after response‐specific readout refitting. After fitting output phases and one passive amplitude scale, the 2D‐to‐3D transmission‐matrix difference is 0.453. This difference indicates that the compact device relies on strong local scattering. We expect the discrepancy to decrease for a larger design area, where the transformation can be distributed over a longer propagation distance with weaker local scattering. This expectation is particularly relevant because our device occupies a 10.5 μm square, making it more compact than the 35.4 μm × 29.4 μm region used for the 10×10 design of Nikkhah et al. [21]. Its 3D mean and worst‐input insertion losses are 6.31 and 6.84 dB, and normalized reflection is 0.110. The fixed‐readout 2D wavelength sweep gives 90.33%, 90.75%, and 88.00% accuracy at 1540, 1550, and 1560 nm. For Yin–Yang, 20 nm over‐etch and 20 nm under‐etch give 89.08% and 88.00% accuracy, respectively, after readout refitting. For the fabrication‐refined MedNIST checkpoint with a fixed readout, 20 nm over‐etch and 20 nm under‐etch give 59.05% and 65.02%, respectively, compared with 96.57% at the nominal geometry. The 2D‐to‐3D comparison and wavelength sweep use the same binary geometry but different evaluation protocols. The former retrains the electronic classifier for the stored nominal 2D response, whereas the latter independently simulates each wavelength and keeps one classifier fixed across the sweep. This protocol difference gives 92.58% for the nominal 2D comparison and 90.75% for the 1550 nm wavelength‐sweep point.

Input‐phase sensitivity is evaluated from the stored seed‐23 transmission response without additional electromagnetic simulations. Independent Gaussian phase errors are applied after coherent encoding, whereas the readout remains fixed. Across 100 trials, the selected response changes from 93.00% at σϕ=0° to 91.09±1.38% for a static channel offset and 90.74±0.59% for sample‐dependent jitter at σϕ=10°. At 20°, the corresponding means are 87.43±3.35% and 86.66±0.75%.

Discussion

The two‐stage workflow separates task learning from full‐wave electromagnetic realization. In the matched MedNIST comparison, operator matching gives higher accuracy under the tested 25‐update budget. Both objectives use 550 solve‐equivalents, and their elapsed times are 25.5 and 24.7 min. Complex operator supervision changes the optimization objective while preserving the electromagnetic work per update.

The Frobenius residual specifies the required amplitude and phase at each output port, so the operator‐matching adjoint sources depend on modal scattering quantities. The direct‐task adjoint sources depend on the detected powers and labels in the predetermined 250‐sample batch. Operator matching reaches higher final accuracy in the MedNIST comparison and all three paired Yin–Yang comparisons under common readout refitting.

The existing controls narrow the physical origin of the excess insertion loss. The RSSCN7 power‐concentration term is applied while learning the target and is therefore already contained in T⋆. It does not delete long‐range couplings: every router entry remains trainable, and 10.03% of the selected target power lies outside five port spacings. The target constraint therefore changes the desired power distribution but cannot by itself explain attenuation introduced when the fixed target is transferred to a device. MedNIST provides an independent check because its realized response has 0.91 dB more mean insertion loss than its target even though that architecture has neither a banded target nor the RSSCN7 local‐router/fixed‐mixer factorization. In the matched RSSCN7 control, the factorized branch also has a lower update‐15 end‐to‐end residual than dense direct realization (38.13% vs. 42.37%) and a lower reflection norm (0.0816 vs. 0.0898). These comparisons do not support the routing bandwidth or local factorization as the dominant source of the excess loss.

The response‐level evidence instead points to attenuation introduced by the finite, fabrication‐constrained electromagnetic realization. For RSSCN7, fitting detector‐equivalent output phases and one passive amplitude reduces the relative residual from 0.449 to 0.227. The fitted amplitude of 0.633 corresponds to 40.1% power scaling relative to the target. The matched factorized response has a reflection norm of 0.0816, only 6.0% of its raw transmission‐mismatch norm of 1.3614, so the reported objective discrepancy is not dominated by reflection. We therefore attribute the excess loss primarily to attenuation and scattering incurred while implementing the complex target within the finite footprint under the 200‐nm filter and projection, with the remaining aligned residual measuring operator‐shape error. The stored controls do not vary footprint and projection independently, so they cannot assign separate numerical shares to those two coupled constraints. The 3D comparison additionally measures the response change produced by the extruded cross‐section.

The same constraints suggest operator‐level routes to improve the realizability of future targets without placing the physical‐device model inside the task loss. One option is to construct targets with a strict passivity margin, ‖Ttarget‖2≤ρ<1, rather than permit the largest singular value to reach unity. Nikkhah et al. use singular‐value analysis to rescale a prescribed transmission matrix when its largest singular value exceeds one and account for the known scalar outside the passive block [16]. This scaling makes the matrix compatible with passive transmission; it does not guarantee implementation by our finite device. Modular construction provides a second option. A target can be parameterized as a product of simpler passive stages, such as the local router and fixed mixer used here. Nikkhah et al. likewise combine a reconfigurable coupler network with an inverse‐designed operator block and provide an auxiliary multimode waveguide for power not directed to the monitored outputs. A larger design region supplies more electromagnetic degrees of freedom, as reported by Camacho et al. [15], but increases footprint. These are prospective target‐construction and architecture choices. The reported realizations retain the fixed task‐trained targets without retrospective scaling, projection, or replacement.

For MedNIST, the target matrix predicts a mean insertion loss of 5.81 dB, whereas the realized device gives 6.72 dB. The device therefore has 0.91 dB of additional loss. Expressed as power, it transmits 21.3% of the input power, compared with 26.2% predicted by the target, or 81.1% of the target transmission. We then tested whether this difference arises mainly from a uniform reduction in power or from distortion of the intended input–output transformation. Before comparing the two matrices, we removed one phase offset from each output and fitted one common amplitude factor. The output‐phase offsets do not affect intensity detection, and a common amplitude factor does not change the predicted class because it scales all output intensities equally. The fitted amplitude is 0.897, corresponding to a power factor of 0.8972=80.5%. This value closely matches the independently calculated 81.1% realized‐to‐target power ratio. The relative matrix error also decreases from 0.128 to 0.047 after alignment. These results indicate that most of the MedNIST discrepancy is a nearly uniform loss of optical power; the remaining error represents nonuniform changes in the relative amplitudes and phases of the matrix elements. The same analysis reduces the relative error from 0.449 to 0.227 for RSSCN7 and from 0.041 to 0.028 for Yin–Yang. We nevertheless optimize the complete complex matrix because both amplitude and phase determine the response of subsequent optical stages. Directly rewarding total transmitted power could reduce insertion loss while changing the intended input–output transformation.

Sved et al. [23] also exploit electromagnetic linearity. Each geometry update uses a fixed set of Nin+Nout basis simulations, and superposition reconstructs the training‐sample fields. Their physical optimization remains coupled to the training data: A labeled minibatch of size B=250 supplies the geometry gradient. A complete pass through Ndata samples requires ⌈Ndata/B⌉ minibatch updates. The basis‐solve count per update stays fixed, whereas batch size controls the samples and task‐evaluation work represented in each geometry update.

In our workflow, task learning is completed before electromagnetic realization. The physical optimizer matches a fixed complex scattering matrix and therefore uses no samples, labels, minibatches, or dataset passes. Each realization update requires exactly Nin+Nout solve‐equivalents, and the number of physical updates is independent of Ndata and B. The comparable six‐class example of Sved et al. uses approximately (15+6)×150=3150 solve‐equivalents. The matched MedNIST comparison here uses (16+6)×25=550 solve‐equivalents for each objective. Operator matching reaches higher final accuracy at the matched internal budget and uses fewer physical updates than the published comparison.

The sensitivity studies separate variability in the matrix‐surrogate and electromagnetic‐realization stages. Eight predetermined RSSCN7 surrogate seeds give 50.87±1.15% test accuracy at the fixed final epoch, with a range of 48.75%–52.32%. Three Yin–Yang freeform initializations give 91.44±2.02% fixed‐readout accuracy and a transmission residual of 0.110±0.057. The accuracy is more stable than the realized complex matrix, indicating that different physical local solutions can preserve similar classification performance.

The fixed‐stage study contains 12 FDTD responses spanning three waveguide gaps and four coupling lengths. For each response, the router and electronic readout are trained together using five predetermined seeds. The training‐only validation means range from 54.68% to 55.70%, measuring the coupled response of the fixed stage and learned router across the sampled geometries.

The reported sensitivity estimates apply to a device class with a fixed design‐region size, 25 nm discretization, 200 nm fabrication filter, platform cross section, and locality width w=5. A broader optimization could place these variables in an outer loop using early truncated physical runs, learned geometry representations, and Bayesian optimization. HiLAB combines these elements for nanophotonic inverse design [32], and related representation‐learning strategies are reviewed by Marzban et al. [33].

Conclusions

We introduced a surrogate‐guided inverse‐design framework for compact photonic neural networks. Task training is performed on a passive complex matrix with bounded singular values, and electromagnetic realization is posed as a fixed‐target adjoint problem driven by complex transmission matching and reflection suppression. This separation fixes the electromagnetic work per update through the input and output port counts and makes it independent of training‐set size. In the matched MedNIST comparison, operator matching reaches 98.34% accuracy, compared with 89.50% for direct task optimization at the same 550‐solve budget. The selected RSSCN7 banded router is fully dense and uses a power‐concentration term centered at w=5, with 10.03% measured out‐of‐band power. In the matched seed‐23 physical comparison through Update 15, the factorized branch has lower end‐to‐end transmission residual in 13 of 15 updates and finishes at 38.13%, compared with 42.37% for dense‐direct realization. The Yin–Yang experiments give 91.44±2.02% across three operator‐matching initializations. Under common readout refitting, the three matched objective pairs give 92.64% mean accuracy for operator matching and 90.17% for direct task loss. The physical evaluations cover 2D and 3D responses, wavelength, uniform over‐etch and under‐etch, and phase noise. These results establish surrogate‐guided operator matching for the tested nanophotonic neural networks.
