The transfusion of a single unit of red blood cells significantly increases total hospital costs in adult, non-cardiac surgical patients
Authors: Andrea Saporito, Alberto Pagnamenta, Marco Previsdomini, Davide La Regina, Lorenzo Ruinelli, Paolo Ferrari, Francesco Mongelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe aimed to quantify the financial impact of a single red blood cell (RBC) unit transfusion. We included all adult patients who underwent elective non-cardiac surgery from 2014 to 2021 and received either none or a single RBC transfusion. Clinical and economic data were retrieved from electronic records and billing systems using a bottom-up, micro-costing approach, including 80,944 surgical admissions. Mean age was 60.6 ± 18.1 years, 40,541 (50.1%) were female, and 27,661 (34.2%) were classified ASA score ≥ 3. In total, 2,727 (3.4%) patients received an RBC transfusion. The average length of stay was 6.3 ± 9.2 days. The most common surgical specialty was orthopedics, followed by general surgery. Unadjusted analyses revealed higher costs in the transfused group. After adjusting for age, sex, ASA score, preoperative hemoglobin value, length of stay and hospital site, the hospital costs were USD 11,724 (IQR 3,481 − 21,872) compared to USD 11,233 (IQR 6,736 − 19,336) in the non-transfused group (p = 0.018). RBC transfusions represent a significant economic burden in adult elective non-cardiac surgical patients. Regardless of its appropriateness, each transfusion increased hospital costs by an average of USD 491 compared to non-transfused patients, corresponding to 5.2% of the median total hospital cost. Introduction

Allogeneic red blood cell (RBC) transfusion represents an independent risk factor for morbidity and mortality1–4. In surgical cohorts, RBC transfusion’s immunosuppressive and prothrombotic effects are linked to delayed wound healing, heightened susceptibility to nosocomial infections5,6, and increased perioperative cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events7–9. Furthermore, RBC transfusion has been associated with an increased incidence of postoperative complications, longer hospitalization, heightened rates of unexpected readmissions, and consequent augmented healthcare expenditures3,10–12.

Guidelines commonly recommend RBC transfusion for clinically stable patients with hemoglobin levels below 70 g/L unless they have major comorbidities, and below 80 g/L for those with cardiovascular or respiratory diseases or significant risk factors6,13,14. A growing body of evidence highlights the effectiveness of patient blood management (PBM) programs and a restrictive approach to transfusion practice in diminishing perioperative morbidity and mortality15,16, as well as reducing both the duration of hospitalization and total hospital costs in surgical patients2,11,15,17. RBC transfusion, in fact, significantly affects surgical costs, including both direct and indirect expenditures18, as well as costs associated with prolonged hospital stays, unexpected readmissions, and postoperative complications3,19,20.

Although prior research has consistently shown that transfused patients generate higher overall hospital costs, most existing economic analyses rely on aggregated DRG-based calculations, activity-based costing, or models driven by postoperative complications17,18. These approaches estimate the global economic impact of transfusion but do not isolate the incremental financial burden attributable to the transfusion event itself. Furthermore, available studies evaluating transfusion outcomes (including those assessing single-unit transfusion effects) have focused primarily on clinical endpoints rather than on precise economic quantification12,21, leaving the specific cost associated with a single RBC unit largely undefined.

To date, no study has quantified the cost of a single-unit RBC transfusion using a bottom-up micro-costing methodology, which enables granular and patient-level resource assessment. Clarifying this cost has important clinical and policy implications: it allows hospitals to benchmark transfusion practices, supports economic justification for PBM programs15,22,23, and provides robust inputs for cost-effectiveness analyses of perioperative anemia management strategies. Accurately defining the incremental cost of one RBC unit therefore fills a critical evidence gap in perioperative medicine and healthcare resource allocation.

The aim of this study is to quantify the economic burden related to RBC transfusion in elective surgical patients within a public hospital trust via a micro-costing approach.

Materials and methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study across five acute hospitals belonging to the Ente Ospedaliero Cantonale (EOC), a public hospital trust in Southern Switzerland, over eight years. The EOC routinely conducts an average of 22’000 elective non-cardiac surgical procedures per year. Employing a bottom-up micro-costing technique, we analyzed all costs associated with RBC transfusion. We aimed to quantify the comprehensive economic impact of a single RBC unit transfusion. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the local ethics committee (Comitato Etico Cantonale Ticino; BASEC-2021-00287). Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the complete anonymization of the data, the requirement for informed consent was waived in accordance with national regulations. The preparation of this manuscript adheres to the guidelines outlined in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. S1 Appendix.

Participants

We included all adult patients who underwent elective non-cardiac surgery from January 2014 to December 2021 and received either none or a single unit of RBC transfusion intraoperatively or postoperatively. Exclusion criteria were: pediatric patients (less than 16 years old), cardiac surgery procedures, multiple surgical procedures during the same hospital stay, transfusion of more than one RBC unit, and transfusions that occurred in an emergency setting, such as acute intraoperative hemorrhage. Patients were divided into two groups: patients receiving one unit of RBC and patients receiving no transfusion.

Data sources

Anonymized data were extracted from the EOC electronic PBM database. This system contains epidemiological, clinical and economic data from electronic medical and administrative records, hospital coding and billing systems. The PBM dashboard enables users to filter and extract data to obtain indicators of interest, such as clinical data, preoperative hemoglobin values within one week before surgery, transfusion rates, length of hospital stay, unexpected readmissions, and total hospital costs per case. Financial data are calculated using a bottom-up, micro-costing method. Importantly, the Swiss-DRG–based accounting system used at our institution does not generate tariff payments, but patient-level case costs derived from micro-costing allocations of personnel time, operating room use, diagnostics, ward stay, consumables, laboratory workflows and overhead structures. Although these costs are reported at case level, the underlying accounting architecture is micro-costing–derived and feeds itemized resource inputs into each hospitalization.

Micro-costing is a detailed and comprehensive approach to cost analysis that involves meticulously examining and quantifying individual resources and their associated costs within a specific process, service, or intervention. The methodology consists of breaking down the entire process into its constituent components, identifying and measuring each resource used, and assigning a specific cost to each resource (e.g., personnel, equipment and supplies both direct and indirect costs). Single costs of resources are then summed to calculate the total hospital expenses related to a given healthcare intervention. Micro-costing thus provides a granular and accurate perspective on the economic aspects of a given surgical procedure, allowing one to understand better the financial implications with the highest level of detail. In our setting, however, transfusion-specific items are not stored as an isolated micro-cost table. Transfusion-related activities (laboratory crossmatch, blood bank handling, consumables, staff involvement) are embedded within the same micro-allocation rules used for the overall hospitalization. Therefore, transfusion contributes to the total direct and indirect case costs.

Variables and outcomes

The primary outcome of this study is the incremental total hospital costs in elective surgical patients receiving one unit of RBC compared to non-transfused patients. Secondary outcomes are those transfusion-related incremental total hospital costs in different surgical specialties.

Clinical and financial data were obtained from the electronic EOC PBM database and medical records. A micro-costing approach excluded consumable costs unrelated to transfusions and incorporated expenses such as single units of RBC, direct and indirect costs, such as inventory costs, transport-related costs, processing fees, transfusion-related complications, unexpected readmissions, and proportional fix costs related to length of hospital stay. Direct costs referred to expenses directly associated with patient treatment, such as medication and surgical procedures, while indirect costs encompassed expenses indirectly related to patient care, including administrative overhead and facility maintenance. Further details were reported in S2 Appendix.

Costs were reported in United States dollars (USD) using an exchange rate of 1.1338 from Swiss franc to USD, updated on November 11th, 2023. As the considered period spanned over 8 years, costs were adjusted for national inflation (https://www.bfs.admin.ch/bfs/en/home/statistics/prices/consumer-price-index/indexierung.html).

Statistical methods

Data was expressed with mean and standard deviation or with median and interquartile values (25th and 75th percentiles, IQR) according to the distribution. In the unadjusted analysis, we utilized the independent samples t-test or the Mann-Whitney test to compare continuous variables and the chi-squared test for categorical variables. We assessed the distribution of hospital costs through histograms and Q–Q plots. Although costs were right-skewed, the residuals of the linear model showed an acceptable approximation to normality in a dataset of this size, where the central limit theorem supports the validity of ordinary least squares estimates. Robust standard errors were applied to account for heteroskedasticity. For these reasons, multivariable linear regression was considered appropriate for estimating incremental costs. To assess adjusted hospital costs, we first conducted a linear regression analysis assuming raw hospital costs as dependent variables and age, sex, and the American Society of Anesthesiologists’ (ASA) score assessed during the preoperative anesthesiologic visit, as covariates (independent variables). Beta coefficients with their corresponding 95% confidence interval (95%CI) were then calculated. Missing data were handled by multivariate imputation using chained equations. The predicted values of the regression analysis were saved to run a multiple regression analysis for adjusted costs. All regression models were multivariable and were adjusted for age, sex, ASA score, preoperative hemoglobin value, length of hospital stay and hospital sites. These specific confounders were pre-selected to account for potential variations between transfused and non-transfused patients. Additionally, subgroup analyses were conducted based on surgical specialty and type of surgery. All tests were performed two-sided, and a value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were carried out using R version 4.0.5 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and Stata 15 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Between January 2014 and December 2021, we included 80,944 consecutive adult elective non-cardiac surgical admissions from our five acute hospitals network. Orthopedics was the most common surgical specialty (24,277 cases), followed by general surgery (16,231 cases), urology (10,142 cases), and gynecology (9,229 cases). The mean age was 60.6 ± 18.1 years, with 40,541, (50.1%) female patients and 27,661, (34.2%) classified as ASA score ≥ 3. In total, 2,727 (3.4%) patients received one unit of RBC transfusion. The average length of hospital stay was 6.3 ± 9.2 [median 3.0; IQR 2.0–7.0] days. Readmissions at 30 days were 572 (21.0%) in the transfused group and 6,436 (8.2%) in the non-transfused group (p < 0.001), while readmissions at 90 days were 904 (33.1%) in the transfused group and 12,255 (15.7%) in the non-transfused group (p < 0.001). Significant clinical differences were observed between transfused and non-transfused patients in terms of age (p < 0.001), sex (p < 0.001), ASA scores (p < 0.001), and length of stay (p < 0.001). Details are reported in Table 1. Also, preoperative hemoglobin values significantly differed in the two groups, being 9.5 ± 1.9 g/dL in the transfused group and 13.9 ± 1.9 g/dL in the non-transfused group (p < 0.001). Figure 1. Further analyses were conducted to identify differences in transfusion rates between hospital sites. We found no significant difference between the three low to mid-volume hospitals, while the risks of transfusions significantly increased in the two larger volume centers (OR 1.99, 95% CI 1.73–2.29, p < 0.001 and OR 1.49, 95% CI 1.28–1.72, p < 0.001).

Adjusted hospital costs

Adjusted hospital costs were estimated according to age, sex, ASA score, preoperative hemoglobin value, length of stay and hospital site. The total hospital costs were USD 11,724 (IQR 3,481 − 21,872) in the transfused patient group compared to USD 11,233 (IQR 6,736 − 19,336) in the non-transfused group. The transfusion of a single RBC unit, regardless of its appropriateness, was associated with a difference in adjusted costs between the two groups of USD 491 (p = 0.018). This adjusted difference corresponded to approximately 5% of the median total hospital cost, providing context for the magnitude of the association. Discussion

Our study, conducted on a large cohort of 80,944, patients, revealed that the adjusted cost associated with the transfusion of a single RBC unit, assessed with a bottom-up, micro-costing approach, was USD 491 in elective, non-cardiac adult surgical patients, irrespective of the appropriateness of the transfusion (based on recommended hemoglobin thresholds). This increment corresponded to approximately 5.2% of the median total hospital cost.

Previous research has consistently demonstrated that RBC transfusion is associated with elevated total hospital costs, prolonged length of hospital stays, increased incidence of perioperative complications, and a higher likelihood of unplanned readmission19,20,24. However, to our knowledge, our study is the first to specifically investigate the cost of a single RBC unit transfusion using a bottom-up, micro-costing methodology. These findings are noteworthy due to the common occurrence of transfusions in clinical practice, a substantial proportion of which has been deemed inappropriate or potentially preventable based on current evidence. Understanding the economic impact on total hospital costs related to surgery is crucial for optimizing resource allocation and improving healthcare efficiency.

In our study, we observed that approximately 4% of elective non-cardiac adult surgical patients underwent RBC transfusion, while the rate of patients receiving a single unit of RBC was 3.4%. These findings contrast with those from Houston et al., who reported a baseline RBC transfusion rate in major non-cardiac surgeries of 16%25. Similarly, Blankstein et al. documented an RBC transfusion rate of 10.3% in orthopedic patients, with wide variations depending on the type of surgery. The lower rate observed in our study may be attributed to the inclusion of patients undergoing minor surgery and the implementation of stringent PBM programs in our hospital network over several years.

The current literature strongly supports implementing PBM programs in clinical practice. These programs aim to enforce more restrictive transfusion policies, ultimately reducing complications and costs. Studies have shown that restrictive transfusion practices can reduce RBC transfusion by 40% to 65%19,26. This positive outcome is achieved through various strategies, including educational initiatives, clinical decision support, and a multidisciplinary approach. Frank et al.22 implemented specific clinician education, decision support tools, audits for guideline compliance, and other blood conservation measures to curtail the need for transfusions. Saag et al.23 employed a comprehensive strategy, integrating decision support and targeted education, incorporating mandatory selection of clinical indications in every transfusion order, and providing evidence-based information to hospital services. Additionally, choosing wisely policies highlighted the importance of transfusing a single unit of RBC rather than two (transfusion index) in numerous clinical scenarios, alongside various other recommendations6,14.

Our findings align with the results of other economic evaluations assessing the impact of RBC transfusion on total hospital costs. For instance, when adjusting for patient differences, including DRGs, RBC transfusion is projected to contribute to an increase of 50% to 80% in total hospital costs17,27. In our study, patients who underwent transfusion showed significantly higher total hospital costs, even after adjusting for confounding variables. Those receiving appropriate transfusions were characterized by more comorbidities and lower levels of Hb12. The observed higher expense in transfused patients may be partially justified by the severity of the underlying clinical conditions, even after adjustment for various variables. The large unadjusted cost difference between groups (~ USD 34,800) does not reflect the cost of the transfusion itself, but rather the greater clinical complexity, frailty and markedly longer hospital stays of transfused patients. Our adjusted analysis already accounts for several major indicators of complexity—age, ASA score, preoperative hemoglobin, length of stay and hospital site—providing a more comparable estimate. The resulting incremental cost of USD 491 therefore reflects the association between receiving one RBC unit and total hospital costs after adjustment. However, because Swiss-DRG case-level costs cannot isolate transfusion-specific resource use and we could not adjust for factors such as procedure type or intraoperative bleeding, residual confounding remains possible. For this reason, the adjusted estimate should be interpreted cautiously as an association rather than evidence of a causal effect. It is also likely that part of the remaining cost difference reflects underlying surgical complexity and patient frailty, which may not be fully captured by the available covariates, even after adjustment. These baseline imbalances reinforce that the observed cost difference represents an adjusted association. Therefore, a more comprehensive regression model or propensity-score–matched analyses could help mitigate confounding and allow an even deeper understanding of the various influences. Propensity score matching was considered; however, it would have restricted the analysis to a matched subset of patients and reduced the generalizability of findings in a cohort of this size. As highlighted by Elze et al.28, when key confounders are adequately measured, multivariable covariate adjustment provides estimates comparable to propensity-based methods while preserving the full dataset. Moreover, regression is better suited to estimating marginal incremental effects, which was the primary aim of our cost analysis. For instance, a separate study found that even the transfusion of a single RBC unit heightened the risk of postoperative complications and prolonged length of stay (presumably increasing costs) compared to matched patients who did not receive transfusions21. These findings imply that hospital costs may be higher in transfused patients, irrespective of the appropriateness and the transfusion policy adopted.

RBC transfusion was independently linked to a prolonged hospital stay compared to patients who did not receive transfusions (20.0 ± 18.3 vs. 5.8 ± 8.3 days). This association may be attributed to a heightened occurrence of complications, encompassing both procedural and delayed transfusion-related complications. The latter can be attributed to the recognized effects of allogeneic blood on homeostasis, rheology, and immune system function29,30.

Financial data for our study were extracted from DRG hospital billing registries. Each specific DRG included all costs generated by the treatment, encompassing both direct and indirect costs associated with hospitalization, operations, medications, procedures, and more. The expenses were calculated individually for each patient. Our cost calculation was based on the Swiss-DRG31, yet it is essential to highlight that various DRG systems adhere to the same concept and share many similarities32. Additionally, multiple articles have underscored the comparability of DRG systems in Europe and the United States in terms of cost calculation and DRG-based reimbursement. While actual cost values may vary among countries, the economic analyses consistently apply to countries adopting DRG systems31–34.

One peculiarity of our study is the use of a micro-costing methodology for cost assessment. Micro-costing is a meticulous approach to analyzing the specific resources and expenses associated with individual medical activities35. This method entails a detailed inventory of all resources utilized, including personnel, equipment, and overhead costs, with precise measurement of the quantities of each resource to ensure maximal accuracy. Each resource is assigned a specific fee based on actual data, market prices, or estimations, allowing for the calculation of total costs by summing up the costs of all individual resources, thereby providing a realistic estimate. The analysis of such data offers valuable insights for healthcare decision-makers, facilitating informed policies and resource allocation decisions36. Micro-costing is crucial in conducting precise financial assessments in healthcare, aiding in budgeting, pricing, reimbursement, and resource allocation. Its detailed approach allows for a deeper understanding of the economic impact of medical interventions37.

Furthermore, it is essential to consider that higher hospital costs observed in transfused patients may not be solely attributable to the transfusion event itself but could also reflect underlying differences in surgical complexity and patient condition. Surgical specialties such as neurosurgery, vascular, or thoracic surgery are inherently more invasive and carry greater risk, often requiring additional resources and prolonged hospital stays. Raw costs showed in tables represent total hospital case costs rather than the direct cost of the transfusion. The observed differences therefore reflect broader patterns of resource use in transfused versus non-transfused patients, not the isolated financial impact of a single RBC unit. However, we found in our stratified analysis that transfused patients consistently had higher costs across nearly all surgical specialties, suggesting that transfusion independently contributes to cost. We acknowledge the possibility of residual confounding, as transfusion is often an indicator of surgical difficulty, intraoperative events, or patient frailty. Although our multivariable model adjusted for key covariates including surgical specialty, preoperative hemoglobin, ASA score, and length of stay, unmeasured variables may still influence the association. Future studies should consider propensity score matching or randomized controlled trials to better isolate the causal impact of transfusion on costs. Importantly, medical decision-making should not be based on cost alone but should integrate both economic and clinical outcomes, including transfusion appropriateness and patient benefit.

Our study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment. Firstly, it involved a retrospective analysis of a prospectively collected database. However, all transfusion cases undergo a weekly review at our PBM meeting, which involves a comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment to minimize mislabeling. Additionally, using ASA scores to estimate illness severity, while convenient and universally available, may not fully capture the complexity of specific clinical scenarios. However, the ASA score is a widely adopted and reliable surrogate for assessing illness severity, enhancing our results’ readability and generalizability. To better adjust for severity of illness, also the preoperative hemoglobin value, length of stay and hospital sites were considered in our regression model. Missing hemoglobin values had to be estimated using a multiple imputation model. Further, almost all RBC transfusions in our series were cross-matched, excluding emergency cases. Platelet and plasma transfusions were not included in order to focus specifically on the cost of a single RBC unit transfusion, thereby minimizing potential confounders and allowing for a more precise analysis of its economic impact. The lack of data on these cases represents an unexplored aspect of our study. Furthermore, data on the incidence of RBC transfusion-related complications were not extracted, limiting our ability to draw statistically supported conclusions about their potential causes and financial impact. It should be noted that in the subgroup analysis, the very low incidence of transfusion and the small sample sizes in some subgroups limited the ability to draw meaningful conclusions. Further analyses regarding transfusion appropriateness, adherence to transfusion policies, and their financial implications were not carried out, as they were beyond the aim of the present study. Another limitation of our study is the lack of data on time-to-recovery or quality of life, restricting our ability to assess long-term outcomes.

Regarding cost calculation, our methodology collected the costs of entire hospitalizations while excluding non-attributable consumable costs. While this approach provides a reliable surrogate, it may not fully reflect the precise actual costs associated with transfusions, considering various fix costs, which cannot be otherwise collected. However, our data should be regarded as the most accurate possible as costs accounted for all transfusion-related expenditures on a granular level. The bottom-up approach was challenging and resource-intensive but significantly enhanced the accuracy of the expense estimates. It is important to distinguish the statistical estimate derived from our multivariable model from the direct micro-costing measurement of the transfusion process. The adjusted incremental cost reflects an association between transfusion and total hospital expenditures after controlling for key confounders, and should not be interpreted as a causal effect. In parallel, we conducted a true bottom-up micro-costing of the transfusion workflow, quantifying personnel time, consumables, laboratory handling, and logistics. These raw unit-level components are now fully reported in the supplementary table to ensure transparency. Together, the micro-costing analysis provides the detailed resource breakdown, while the regression model estimates the incremental cost associated with transfusion within the overall hospital cost structure.

These findings have direct policy relevance. Effective PBM programs have consistently demonstrated their ability to reduce unnecessary transfusions, improve postoperative outcomes and lower overall healthcare costs15–17. In parallel, transfusion stewardship frameworks emphasize the need to standardize thresholds, optimize preoperative hemoglobin and minimize avoidable exposure to allogeneic blood, particularly in elective surgery22,23. Our results reinforce these concepts by showing that even a single-unit transfusion is associated with a measurable incremental cost. When considered alongside evidence describing the substantial resource burden of blood products at health-system level18, these findings support the implementation of PBM and stewardship strategies as priority institutional policies. Adoption of such measures may help hospitals reduce variability in transfusion practice, improve resource utilization and ultimately mitigate the economic impact of perioperative transfusion across surgical populations. While these findings provide useful economic context, cost considerations should complement rather than replace clinical judgment in transfusion decisions, which must remain driven by patient safety, clinical appropriateness, and established PBM principles. Further assessments of this topic should focus on conducting randomized trials to evaluate both cost and clinical outcomes (i.e., cost-effectiveness) in the context of standardized elective surgery within optimized patient blood management programs.

Conclusion

Despite the acknowledged limitations, our study shows the considerable economic impact of RBC transfusion in adult elective non-cardiac surgical patients. Regardless of its appropriateness, each transfusion was associated with an average increase in hospital costs of USD 491 compared to non-transfused patients, corresponding to approximately 5.2% of the median total hospital cost. Given these findings, PBM policies should be implemented in hospital trusts to minimize transfusion rates among elective surgical patients. Such measures can potentially mitigate the economic burden of RBC transfusion and optimize resource utilization in healthcare settings.
