Construction duration prediction for ultra-high-rise buildings using PCA and an improved sparrow-search–optimized BPNN
Authors: Fei-yang Yuan, Wen-tong Lyu, Pei-juan Zheng, Hou-jun Li, Yong-qi Liu, Rony Badhon Roy, Xian-chao Yang, Xu Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Construction duration prediction is crucial for the early-stage planning of ultra-high-rise building (UHRB) projects, but its accuracy is often limited by high-dimensional, strongly correlated, and mixed-type engineering features. To overcome these issues, this study develops a hybrid PCA-ISSA-BPNN framework that combines principal component analysis (PCA), an improved sparrow search algorithm enhanced with grey wolf optimization (ISSA), and a back-propagation neural network (BPNN). PCA is used to reduce multicollinearity and redundancy in the quantitative variables, while ISSA performs global hyperparameter optimization for the BPNN. Based on 87 UHRB projects and five-fold cross-validation, the proposed model achieved the best predictive performance among all compared methods, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}^{2}=0.982$$\end{document}, RMSE = 23.496, MAE = 15.742, and MAPE = 2.0%. These results demonstrate that the proposed framework can simultaneously improve accuracy, robustness, and feature representation quality. The PCA-ISSA-BPNN model therefore provides a practical and effective tool for early-stage duration estimation and schedule-risk screening in UHRB projects.

Introduction

The duration of construction projects is a critical consideration factor prior to initiating new projects, as it directly impacts the ultimate success or failure of the project1. For construction contractors, they earnestly seek reliable information regarding construction durations at the initial stages of a project. Such information effectively assists project managers in devising schedules for cash flow and material flow within the allotted timeframe2,3. In comparison to typical buildings, ultra-high-rise buildings (UHRBs) employ more specialized structural designs, higher standards of construction materials, more intricate construction techniques, and pose greater challenges in material transportation, storage management, and construction fund management. Therefore, accurate estimation of construction durations for UHRBs becomes exceedingly crucial.

In order to accurately predict project durations, some researchers utilize linear regression analysis models to initially estimate the durations of construction projects4–6. However, linear regression models face challenges in effectively forecasting and analyzing more complex datasets7,8. In contrast, machine learning demonstrates superior computational efficiency and prediction accuracy in modeling complex nonlinear data, exhibiting significant advantages and broader application scenarios9–11. Back-propagation neural network (BPNN) is a gradient-based feed-forward network that iteratively updates weights and biases to minimize the discrepancy between predicted and actual outputs. After structural tuning or hyper-parameter optimization, BPNN can achieve fast convergence with acceptable accuracy and has been widely applied to complex regression problems12. Nevertheless, the original construction-feature set usually suffers from high dimensionality and multicollinearity; feeding it directly into BPNN enlarges the network, prolongs training, and increases the risk of local minima. Consequently, further preprocessing is still required when BPNN is used for high-dimensional data.

Two further limitations remain. First, BPNN performance is highly sensitive to hyper-parameters, so an efficient optimiser is indispensable for locating optimal network settings13–15. For example, Peng & Li14 employed particle-swarm optimisation (PSO) to tune the hyper-parameters of a grey-BPNN for construction-cost prediction. More generally, recent optimisation-based studies have shown that metaheuristic strategies such as GA and GWO can effectively enhance predictive performance in complex engineering datasets16–18. Although these studies do not focus on UHRB duration prediction, they provide methodological support for the use of hybrid optimisation–learning frameworks in the present study. Second, project duration is driven by critical rather than by all activities, yet standard BPNNs provide no explicit mechanism for identifying those critical items. Leu & Liu19, for instance, applied principal-component analysis (PCA) to an industrial-building database to extract critical factors that were subsequently fed into a BPNN to estimate construction time; however, UHRB projects were not covered.

Considering these factors, this paper proposes a composite model (PCA-ISSA-BPNN) that integrates Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Sparrow Search Algorithm enhanced with Grey Wolf Optimization (ISSA), and BPNN. Initially, the dataset is dimensionally reduced using the PCA algorithm. Subsequently, the Sparrow Search Algorithm is augmented with a hierarchical structure derived from the Grey Wolf Optimization to enhance its global search capability. Finally, an automatic optimization of the hyperparameters of the BPNN model is performed, thereby establishing the PCA-ISSA-BPNN prediction model.

Baseline models

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) reduces dimensionality by finding directions that capture the greatest variance in the data. First, standardize the data and summarize pairwise linear relationships with a covariance matrix. Then perform an eigen-decomposition to obtain eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Sort the eigenvalues in descending order, keep the top \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:k$$\end{document} eigenvectors as the principal components, and project the data onto them to obtain a lower-dimensional representation that preserves as much information as possible20.

In this study, PCA is applied to quantitative features for orthogonalization and dimensionality reduction, mitigating multicollinearity while preserving as much information as possible. The PCA-transformed quantitative vectors are then concatenated with one-hot–encoded categorical features, preserving category semantics and yielding a stable numerical representation. Based on the loading matrix, we further distill a small set of interpretable engineering latent axes as a unified coordinate system to support subsequent visualization, clustering model selection, and group-wise modeling. In this study, interpretability is mainly provided through the PCA loading matrix and the subsequent cluster–category association analysis, which together preserve the engineering semantics of the original variables at a global level. Since the model is trained on PCA-transformed inputs, SHAP-like local explanation methods would mainly attribute importance to principal components rather than to the original engineering variables directly, and were therefore not further introduced in the present work.

Backpropagation neural network (BPNN)

A Backpropagation Neural Network (BPNN) is a multilayer, feed-forward Artificial Neural Network (ANN) trained with the backpropagation algorithm21,22. It uses an input layer, one or more hidden layers with nonlinear activations, and an output layer; during supervised learning it sends the prediction error backward through the network to update weights with gradient-based optimization, enabling it to learn complex nonlinear mappings for regression and classification. The structure of BPNN is shown in Fig. 1.

We implement a BPNN as a multilayer feed-forward model in MATLAB using feedforwardnet with traingdx (gradient descent with momentum and an adaptive learning rate). The input consists of the concatenation of PCA-transformed quantitative variables and one-hot–encoded categorical variables. Model selection follows five-fold cross-validation, with 15% of each training fold reserved for early stopping (max_fail = 8). The hyperparameter setting includes a hidden layer of size 15, a learning rate of 0.01, a momentum coefficient 0.5, an L2 penalty of 1e-4, and 1000 training epochs; the hidden-layer size was empirically specified as 15 based on preliminary trial-and-error experiments and practical considerations of model complexity and training stability, whereas the remaining hyperparameters were retained according to the lowest average RMSE across folds. For comparison, a multilayer perceptron trained with feedforwardnet + trainlm (Levenberg–Marquardt) uses the same input processing and CV/early-stopping protocol, adopts the same hidden-layer size and L2 penalty, runs for 1000 epochs, optimizes MSE, applies tansig activations in hidden layers, and purelin at the output. The selected hyperparameters, hidden size and L2 penalty, and for the BPNN additionally the learning rate and momentum, together with out-of-fold predictions, are recorded in the exported scoring files.

Hyperparameter optimization

Sparrow search algorithm (SSA)

The Sparrow Search Algorithm (SSA) is a population-based swarm intelligence method inspired by sparrows’ foraging and anti-predation behaviors23. Individuals are organized into discoverers (producers) that lead global exploration and set the search direction, followers that exploit neighborhoods by tracking discoverers, and scouts (warners) that monitor risk and trigger escape or expansion when danger is detected. Through adaptive role updating, stochastic perturbations, and greedy selection, SSA balances global exploration and local exploitation with few control parameters and typically rapid convergence. It is widely used for continuous optimization and engineering calibration tasks, such as feature selection and hyperparameter tuning of machine-learning models.

Grey wolf optimization (GWO)

Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO) is a swarm-intelligence metaheuristic that models the social hierarchy and hunting behavior of grey wolves24,25. The algorithm proceeds through encircling, hunting, and attacking phases driven by leader positions, enabling a gradual shift from global exploration to local exploitation. With few control parameters, simple implementation, and competitive convergence on continuous, multimodal landscapes, GWO is widely used in engineering design, feature selection, and machine-learning hyperparameter tuning.

After computation, the Pearson correlation coefficients are depicted in Fig. 4. Among all predictors, the strongest bivariate association with Y is observed for X12 (super-height surcharge): =0.36, <0.001 (***), indicating that higher super-height premiums are associated with longer schedules. X8 (number of above-ground floors) also shows a positive and statistically significant correlation with Y (≈0.24, <0.05, *), suggesting a tendency toward longer durations as storey count increases. Linear correlations between Y and the remaining variables are weak and mostly non-significant. Substantial inter-predictor dependencies are present. Area-related variables X3–X4 are nearly perfectly positively correlated (=0.99) and are both highly correlated with X2. Within the area/height cluster we note X1–X6 =0.80, X1–X7 =0.76, X1–X8 =0.72 r = 0.72, and X6–X7 =0.76. For the cost/floors cluster, X9–X11 yields =0.87. Based on Fig. 5, the variance contributions of PC1, PC2, and PC3 are 37.9%, 20.9%, and 13.5%, respectively, yielding a cumulative contribution of approximately 72.3%. An evident “elbow” appears between PC3 and PC4; adding PC4 lifts the cumulative contribution above 80%, and PC5 raises it beyond 85%. Accordingly, to balance information retention and noise suppression in predictive modeling, we adopt PC1–PC5 as the low-dimensional representation of the quantitative features. For visualization and clustering, we select PC1–PC3 (≈ 72.3%) to form an interpretable three-dimensional subspace for 3D projection (see Fig. 6). In this 3D space, k-means clustering attains good separation at the optimal k = 10 (Silhouette = 0.74, DBI = 0.515, CHI = 105.2), and the clusterwise duration distributions are clearly distinct (see box plots in Fig. 7), indicating that the first three principal components capture the major structure associated with project duration.

Based on the loadings in Fig. 8, PC1 can be interpreted as a “scale/height axis”: it has positive loadings on X1 (base area), X6 (eave height), X7 (total number of floors), X8 (above-ground stories), X9 (below-ground stories), and X10–X11 (cost), contrasted with negative loadings on X3–X4 (floor area), capturing the opposition between vertical organization and plan extent. PC2 functions as a “plan size/foundation depth axis”, dominated by X2–X4 and X5, reflecting the intensity of excavation and underground works. PC3 corresponds to a “super-height premium/special difficulty axis”, with a strong positive loading on X12 (super-height surcharge), negative loadings on X2 and X5, and secondary positive contributions from X9 and X11, indicating additional complexity due to high-altitude operations and special measures. Together, these axes provide a unified, engineering-semantic, low-correlation, and interpretable coordinate system for subsequent clustering visualization and as inputs to the predictive model.

The retained principal components were selected according to the trade-off between information preservation and dimensionality reduction. In the present study, PC1–PC5 were used for predictive modeling because their cumulative explained variance exceeded 85%, indicating that most of the quantitative information was preserved in the reduced feature space. By contrast, PC1–PC3 were used for visualization purposes, since they provide a more intuitive low-dimensional representation of the sample structure while still capturing the dominant variance of the dataset. At the current stage, a more exhaustive sensitivity analysis on the number of retained principal components was not further carried out; therefore, the adopted PC1–PC5 configuration should be understood as a practically motivated choice based on the scree-plot characteristics and cumulative explained variance of the dataset.

To verify the representativeness of the adopted five-fold partition, a fold-level statistical equivalence analysis was performed. The descriptive statistics of the quantitative variables and the target variable were broadly comparable across the five folds. Pairwise Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests showed that 119 out of 130 comparisons (91.5%) were not statistically significant, and the target variable Y exhibited no significant difference across any fold pair. Permutation tests on fold-wise mean differences yielded the same overall pattern, again with 119 out of 130 comparisons (91.5%) being non-significant. In addition, chi-square-based balance analysis for the categorical variables showed no significant fold-level imbalance (X13: p = 0.450; X14: p = 0.200; X15: p = 0.472). These results indicate that the adopted five-fold partition is statistically comparable overall and that no severe fold-level distributional shift exists.

Prediction results and analysis

After PCA based dimensionality reduction, PC1–PC5 are used as model inputs and use ISSA to tune the hyperparameters of a BPNN, yielding the PCA–ISSA–BPNN model. Five baseline models(ANN, BPNN, SSA-BPNN, GWO-BPNN, and ISSA-BPNN) are used for comparison, where ANN and BPNN are tuned by grid search. For the swarm-intelligence-based models, SSA, GWO, and ISSA were applied to optimize the same set of model hyperparameters under a unified optimization framework. To ensure methodological consistency, the three algorithms shared the same population size, iteration number, parameter-search bounds, and objective function. In all cases, the optimization objective was defined as the average RMSE under five-fold cross-validation. This design ensures that the performance differences among SSA-BPNN, GWO-BPNN, and ISSA-BPNN can be attributed to the optimization strategy itself rather than to inconsistent search settings. All predictive models are evaluated under five-fold cross-validation. Although the proposed PCA-ISSA-BPNN framework demonstrated strong predictive capability on the present dataset, caution is still required when extending the model to other geographical regions or construction settings. Construction duration is affected by region-specific factors, including local regulations, labor productivity, construction practices, supply-chain efficiency, climatic conditions, and seismic or safety requirements. Since the current dataset was collected within a single regional context, the external generalizability of the trained model remains uncertain. Therefore, the present study primarily demonstrates the feasibility and effectiveness of the proposed framework within the investigated context. Further studies based on multi-region datasets and external validation are needed to verify its broader applicability.

Conclusion

This study developed a PCA-ISSA-BPNN framework for predicting the construction duration of Ultra-High-Rise Buildings (UHRBs). On 87 projects, PCA was applied to quantitative variables (with categorical attributes one-hot encoded), and an Improved Sparrow Search Algorithm (ISSA) conducted global hyperparameter search for the BPNN. Under five-fold cross-validation, the proposed model achieved the best overall accuracy among all contenders: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}^{2}=0.982$$\end{document}, RMSE \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:=23.496$$\end{document}, MAE \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:=15.742$$\end{document}, and MAPE \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:=2.0\%$$\end{document}.) Relative to the baselines and single-optimizer variants, these gains confirm the complementary roles of PCA (orthogonalization and scale stabilization) and ISSA (balanced exploration–exploitation and robust convergence), which together constitute the main methodological novelty of the proposed framework.

Interpretable structure from PCA. Beyond improving conditioning, PCA distilled engineering-semantic axes that organize duration-relevant variability. Based on the loading structure, PC1 behaves as a “scale/height axis” (positive on X1, X6–X9, X10–X11 and negative on X3–X4), PC2 as a “plan size/foundation depth axis” (dominated by X2–X4 and X5), and PC3 as a “super-height premium/special difficulty axis” (strongly positive on X12, negative on X2 and X5, secondary positives on X9 and X11). PCs 1–5 were adopted as supervised inputs, while the PC1–PC3 subspace supports 3D visualization and unsupervised structure discovery. Cluster–category analyses show strong, significant associations with structure type (X13), seismic class (X14), and usage (X15), indicating that quantitative predictors implicitly encode system/requirement/function semantics which PCA recovers compactly. Practically, these factors and cluster memberships can guide stratified or mixture modeling and targeted feature engineering for schedule risk screening.

Advantages of ISSA

1) Inertia-weight scheduling for discoverers gradually shifts from broad exploration to local refinement, preventing early crowding and improving stability (Eq. (1) and the enhanced update in Eq. (2)).

2) Risk-aware multi-leader guidance embeds a GWO-style tri-leader reference \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\{\alpha\:,\beta\:,\delta\:\}$$\end{document}with adaptive weights (Eqs. (3a)–(3b)), providing multiple reliable escape directions rather than herding the population toward a deceptive local optimum.

3) Greedy-probabilistic acceptance (Eq. (4)) preserves improvements while allowing occasional non-greedy moves to avoid premature convergence; projection/clamping enforces feasibility.

Together these designs strengthen global search, maintain diversity, and accelerate convergence, which is empirically verified: vs. BPNN, ISSA-BPNN cuts RMSE/MAE/MAPE by 62.4%/73.7%/≈75% and lifts \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}^{2}$$\end{document} to 0.905; vs. SSA-BPNN and GWO-BPNN, ISSA further reduces RMSE by 39.5% and 36.1% and roughly halves MAPE, evidencing a more robust exploration–exploitation balance. Building on this, coupling PCA delivers a “second leap,” with PCA-ISSA-BPNN achieving the best scores overall. To reduce the risk of overfitting on the relatively limited dataset, several measures were adopted in this study, including five-fold cross-validation, early stopping, PCA-based dimensionality reduction, and L2 regularization. However, no independent external dataset was available beyond the adopted cross-validation framework. Therefore, while the present results support the effectiveness and internal robustness of the proposed model within the current dataset, its broader external generalizability still requires further validation using independent or cross-regional project data.

Overall, Coupling interpretable PCA factors (PC1–PC3) with ISSA-optimized BPNN (fed by PCs 1–5) delivers both accuracy and insight. The proposed framework proposed a practical tool for early-stage duration estimation in UHRBs, and offers a sound basis for future process-awareand hybrid physics–data extensions.
