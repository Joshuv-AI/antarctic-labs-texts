Patient expectations assessed before randomisation and after the first treatment session, and their associations with pain outcome at 3 months in patients with tennis elbow: a secondary analysis of a randomised controlled feasibility trial in Norwegian secondary care
Authors: Håkon Sveinall, Cecille Røe, Kaia B Engebretsen, Sigrid Skatteboe, Are Hugo Pripp, Jens Ivar Brox, Marianne Bakke Johnsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate patients’ expectations of pain improvement before randomisation (T1) and after the first treatment (T2), and to examine how expectations at these two time points were associated with pain outcome measured at 3 months (T3).DesignExploratory secondary analyses of a three-arm, randomised controlled feasibility trial in patients with tennis elbow comparing heavy slow resistance training, shock wave therapy and advice (1:1:1).SettingOutpatient clinic at Oslo University Hospital.ParticipantsAdults with lateral epicondylalgia, commonly known as tennis elbow.Main outcome measuresExpected pain was rated on a Numeric Rating Scale (NRS, 0–10) at T1 and T2. Present pain (NRS, 0–10) was reported at 3 months (T3). Changes in expectations from T1 to T2 were summarised descriptively. Univariable linear regressions assessed associations between T3 pain and expectations at T1 and T2, treatment group and baseline factors. Explained variance was quantified by R2. Multivariable models including demographics and baseline pain were evaluated via adjusted R2.ResultsFifty-four participants were included. In the shock wave group, nine (47%) came to expect greater improvement from T1 to T2; by contrast, in the advice group, seven (41%) expected less improvement. Expectations at T1 were not associated with T3 pain, whereas expectations at T2 were positively associated with T3 pain (b=0.61, 95% CI 0.33 to 0.89, p<0.01, R2=0.27), suggesting that higher expected pain at T2 was associated with higher reported pain at T3. Adding education and baseline pain increased explained variance modestly (R2 from 0.27 to 0.32).ConclusionIn this study, expectations measured after randomisation and one treatment session were associated with pain at 3 months for patients with tennis elbow. Larger, prospectively designed studies should investigate how postrandomisation expectations relate to clinical outcomes in non-blinded musculoskeletal trials.Trial registration numberNCT04803825.Keywords: Physical Therapy Modalities, Exercise, Tennis Elbow, Elbow Tendinopathy, Randomized Controlled Trial

Introduction

Tennis elbow is characterised by pain over the lateral part of the elbow and is considered a self-limiting condition. However, 66% report incomplete recovery at 1 year and 54% at 2 years.1–3 When persistent, the pain may negatively affect quality of life, cause absence from work, disrupt sleep and social functioning and increase healthcare utilisation.4–6 A wide variety of treatment programmes exist, yet physiotherapy is generally considered the first-line treatment.7–9 However, studies evaluating different treatment programmes often fail to demonstrate additional benefit when compared with alternative interventions or wait-and-see approaches.6 10–13 The limited variation in outcomes across treatments for tennis elbow warrants exploration of other predictors of recovery.

Advice and education are considered key components of optimised physiotherapy.14 Exercise is regarded as first-line treatment, while other physiotherapy modalities, such as radial extracorporeal shock wave therapy, are commonly used.8 Yet, the effectiveness of shock wave therapy for pain relief and functional recovery is debated. Several high-quality trials in tennis elbow and other conditions have found that the response to shock wave therapy is not different from sham shock wave therapy.15–20

In other musculoskeletal pain conditions, patients’ expectations of treatment have been shown to be associated with outcomes.21–24 Healthcare expectations can be defined as beliefs about future outcomes. These beliefs can be created by several factors, including expectations of the treatment effectiveness based on perceived credibility, personal preference and previous or early treatment experiences, as well as expectations regarding future pain and prognosis.25–27 For example, when patients receive their preferred treatment, their specific expectations for that treatment over alternatives have been associated with better outcomes.28 This suggests that expectations may be associated with outcomes in randomised controlled trials (RCTs). Expectations of pain have also been reported to influence the perception of pain, and positive or negative expectations of treatment may contribute to placebo or nocebo responses, respectively.29–35 The study by Dubé et al22 was the first to evaluate the change in expectations over time. In a RCT of shoulder pain, they conducted secondary analyses and concluded that expectations are important indicators of outcome. Similarly, Zheng et al23 analysed expectations at baseline and post-treatment in patients with migraine and found that high post-treatment expectations, but not baseline expectations, were associated with clinical outcomes.

Placebo-controlled randomised clinical trials on tennis elbow have shown that symptom improvement can be attributed to placebo effects.17–19 36–38 Hence, patients’ positive or negative expectations may play a significant role in treatment outcomes. To our knowledge, there are no previous studies investigating the effect of treatment expectations in tennis elbow.

We recently published a randomised controlled feasibility trial comparing heavy slow resistance training, radial extracorporeal shock wave therapy and information and advice in patients with tennis elbow.39 In the present study, we conducted an exploratory secondary analysis in which expectations were assessed with a single item asking participants how they expected their elbow pain to be in 3 months. The main objectives were to: (1) evaluate patients’ expectations of pain improvement both prior to randomisation and after the first treatment, and (2) examine how expectations at these two time points were associated with pain outcome measured at 3 months.

Materials and methods

Participants were recruited from the Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation at Oslo University Hospital outpatient clinic between August 2021 and February 2023. The study was approved by the Regional Committees for Medical and Healthcare Research Ethics (REK109547) and registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04803825).

The trial was designed as a randomised controlled feasibility study and reported according to the 26-item checklist from the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 statement: extension to randomised pilot and feasibility trials.40 Full details of the study have been published previously.39 Participants were randomised by a coinvestigator. The randomisation sequence was computer-generated with blocks of variable size (range 3–9), which was unknown to the treating physiotherapist. At baseline, before randomisation (T1), participants reported their expected pain at 3 months on a 0–10 Numeric Rating Scale (NRS). Approximately 1 week after inclusion, participants were randomised in a 1:1:1 ratio to heavy slow resistance training, radial shock wave therapy or advice, and then received their first treatment. After this appointment, which comprised randomisation and the first treatment (T2), they completed the same NRS question about expected pain at 3 months. Due to the nature of the interventions, blinding of the physiotherapist or the participants was not possible. One postgraduate physiotherapist delivered all interventions.

Inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years and a clinical diagnosis of tennis elbow.41 42 Participants were excluded if they had insufficient language skills to participate, contraindications to shock wave therapy or any suspected serious illness. A detailed list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is described elsewhere.39 All participants provided written informed consent before inclusion.

Patient and public involvement

User involvement was embedded through the Patient Panel at the Research and Communication Unit for Musculoskeletal Health (FORMI), which comprises user representatives with various musculoskeletal disorders. The Patient Panel contributed to the development of study information and materials. The intervention protocols were presented to the Patient Panel, and adjustments were made accordingly.

Treatments

All participants, regardless of treatment group, received the same written and oral information about aetiology, pathogenesis, treatment options and prognosis. Pacing and activity modification were recommended. The importance of using the arm within tolerable pain to regain and maintain function was emphasised. Participants were informed that it is safe to use the arm and that pain does not equal harm. No restrictions were given after any of the treatments.

Heavy slow resistance training

Participants were instructed to perform a home training programme comprising two exercises, carried out three times per week for 12 weeks. Supervision was offered once per week during the intervention period, with attendance voluntary. They received a written protocol based on previous research on heavy slow resistance training and a training diary, which was submitted at follow-up.43–45 A detailed description of the training programme, reported according to the Consensus on Exercise Reporting Template,46 has been described previously.39

Radial shock wave therapy

Following the treatment protocol, participants received three sessions approximately 1 week apart, with 2000 impulses per session at 10 Hz, using low-energy settings between 1.5 and 3 bar.

Information and advice

A single, individual, face-to-face session with a postgraduate physiotherapist, lasting up to 45 min, was provided. In addition to the written and oral information provided to all participants, they were asked to identify challenges and set recovery goals. The natural course and favourable prognosis of the condition were emphasised. Participants’ pain beliefs and behaviour were discussed, and they were reassured about the robustness of the elbow despite pain. Participants were encouraged to use their arm as normally as possible.

Expectations

Expectations of pain improvement were measured using the NRS (0–10) with the question: ‘How do you expect your elbow pain to be in 3 months, on a scale from 0 to 10, where 0 indicates no pain and 10 the worst imaginable pain?’.47

Outcomes

Numeric Rating Scale of pain

The NRS (0–10) was chosen as the outcome measure for current pain at the 3-month follow-up (T3). On the NRS, patients rate their pain from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst possible pain).48 The NRS for pain has been shown to correlate highly with other pain scores, and a change of two points is considered clinically significant.49 50

Data analysis

Data were reported as mean (SD) and frequency (%). Expectations from before randomisation (T1) to after one treatment (T2) were compared using descriptive statistics for the total sample and within each treatment group. Univariable linear regression models with NRS pain (0–10) at 3 months (T3) as the dependent variable, and expectations (at T1 and T2) plus other established predictors from previous research as independent variables, were fitted.2 51 52 Model R2 values were used to describe how the independent variables explained variance in the outcome at T3.53 Multivariable linear regression models with NRS pain (0–10) at T3 as the dependent variable were fitted for exploratory adjustment to the univariable models. Only variables that were significant in the univariable analyses were included in the multivariable models. Adjusted R2 values from the models were evaluated to assess the additional explanatory value provided by the added variables. Participants with missing data at any time point (T1, T2 or T3) were excluded from the analyses.

Patient expectation of elbow pain at 3 months was prespecified in the protocol as a covariate, collected alongside other baseline and follow-up variables. However, the analyses of associations between expectations and 3-month pain were not preplanned and were conducted post hoc as exploratory secondary analyses.

Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata/SE V.17.0 for Windows (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Change in expectations of pain

Nineteen participants (35%) in the total sample did not change expectations of improvement from T1 to T2, 19 (35%) expected greater improvement, and 16 (30%) expected less improvement. Within the shock wave therapy group, nine participants (47%) changed to expect greater improvement at T2, whereas in the information and advice group, seven participants (41%) changed to expect less improvement. Association of expectations and reported pain at 3 months

Higher expected pain at T1 was not associated with pain at 3-month follow-up (b=0.13, 95% CI −0.18 to 0.43, p=0.41). The corresponding regression model explained only 1% of the variance in pain at 3 months (R2=0.01; n=54). In contrast, higher expected pain at T2 was significantly associated with pain at 3-month follow-up (b=0.61, 95% CI 0.33 to 0.89, p<0.01). This model was statistically significant (F(1, 52)=19.28, p<0.01) and explained 27% of the variance in pain at 3 months (R2=0.27). Baseline pain (NRS, 0–10) and education were the only other variables significantly associated with pain at 3-month follow-up (Table 3).

Discussion

In this exploratory secondary analysis of a randomised feasibility trial, expectations measured after one treatment were statistically associated with pain at 3 months and explained more outcome variance than any other variable. These findings generate the hypothesis that post-randomisation expectations may represent an important contextual factor in non-blinded trials. Future studies should prospectively measure expectations at multiple time points to allow for a better understanding of how these factors are associated with clinical outcomes. As these results come from patients with tennis elbow in a musculoskeletal rehabilitation setting, it is unclear whether the same patterns would appear in other patient groups, and further studies in other clinical contexts are warranted.

Results from univariable analyses showed that expectations after one treatment accounted for more variation in 3-month pain than any of the other previously established predictors analysed in this study.2 51 54 An R2 above 15% is often considered clinically meaningful, but in this small sample, the association should be interpreted with caution and evaluated in larger, prospectively designed studies.

Bot et al2 reported a R2 of 0.46 in a predictor study of pain intensity at 3 months among patients with tennis elbow. Their model included nine predictors, such as sociodemographic factors, characteristics of the complaint, comorbidities and psychosocial factors. The multivariable regression models in the current study showed only a small increase in the explained variance when demographic data and baseline severity were included, underscoring the potential role of expectations.

The difference in the association with the outcome between expectations at T1 and T2 is consistent with findings by Zheng et al23 in their study on acupuncture for migraine. In the present study, the association between T2 expectations and 3-month pain may reflect stronger beliefs in the effectiveness of shock wave therapy compared with advice. These findings align with those of Kalauokalani et al28 in patients with low back pain. They examined patients’ expectations of acupuncture and massage before treatment and found that those with higher expectation ratings for the treatment they received had fivefold greater likelihood of substantial improvement. In the present study, expectations at T2 were measured after randomisation and completion of the first treatment, and are likely influenced not only by a priori beliefs and satisfaction with treatment allocation, but also by factors such as therapist communication and perceived early improvement after the first session, which may have contributed to the observed results.25–27 Assessing expectations after randomisation and initial treatment exposure may be particularly important in non-procedural interventions such as physiotherapy, where blinding of participant and therapist is not feasible and the interventions in this trial (shock wave, exercise and advice) differ markedly in their appearance. In this regard, the experimental study by Sánchez Romero et al55 on verbal suggestion and postneedling soreness after dry needling may be relevant. They reported that positive or negative verbal suggestions delivered before the procedure did not alter postneedling soreness or pain-processing, suggesting that attempts to manipulate expectations pretreatment may not translate into outcome differences. This reinforces the importance of assessing expectations both following randomisation and after initial treatment exposure, as these time points are more likely to reflect a broader appraisal of the therapeutic context, patient–clinician interaction and placebo/nocebo-related mechanisms.25–27

The shock wave therapy group was the only group to show a trend towards increased expectations of improvement after randomisation and one treatment session. The reason for this is uncertain. We cannot exclude that the information about treatments given to all patients before treatment favoured shock wave therapy or that the more technically sophisticated treatment appealed more to the patients. Several high-quality RCTs investigating shock wave therapy for tennis elbow and other tendinopathies have failed to show any additional effect over sham shock wave therapy.15–19 56–59 This is consistent with the findings of Holtedahl et al60 in their systematic review and meta-analysis of minimally invasive interventions, where only small differences in effect sizes between active treatment and sham were observed. This suggests that non-specific mechanisms, including placebo, are major contributors to the observed effects of treatments. Placebo responses to shock wave therapy may be driven by cues such as the large, noisy machine and the painful stimuli delivered by the handpiece.61 Prior exposure to positive marketing in the media, or to favourable opinions from physiotherapists, chiropractors, physicians or other patients, may also have influenced expectations of effect.34 In addition, information given to patients about expected pain relief, both verbally and in writing, may have induced positive expectations regarding outcome.

Thirty-eight per cent of participants assigned to heavy slow resistance training reported a decrease in their expectations after one treatment. This aligns with the findings of Lindheimer et al,62 who, in a survey of 966 participants, reported that more than half anticipated increased pain from exercises. However, 33% reported increased expectations of pain improvement after one session of heavy slow resistance training. Previous research on exercise for low back pain also suggests substantial effects of expectations. Rhon et al63 found that high expectations of exercise as a treatment were a predictor of reduced pain intensity, and De Roode et al64 reported in their meta-analysis that 60% of pain reduction after exercise therapy could be explained by contextual factors or the placebo response.

The information and advice group showed a trend towards a negative change in expectations. This contrasts with Dubé et al,22 who found that expectations improved post-treatment across all groups, including the education-only group. However, their education intervention was much more comprehensive than the information and advice provided in the current study.65 In the present study, participants received information about the prolonged course of the condition and were encouraged to use the arm despite pain. This may have reduced confidence in pain improvement within 3 months, as they were told that pain might persist longer. Additionally, communication between therapists and patients may not have been effective for some individuals, resulting in a less successful intervention.

A total of 63% of study participants had received treatment prior to their inclusion in the current study, which may have influenced their expectations of future treatment outcomes. Zerth et al26 reported that prior experiences of treatment-related worsening were associated with heightened expectations of future deterioration, which in turn were linked to poorer treatment outcomes.

Limitations

The results are based on exploratory secondary analyses from a feasibility RCT. The hypotheses regarding expectations and the regression analyses were not prespecified in the original trial protocol but were developed post hoc. Consequently, the study was neither powered nor protocolised to address these expectation-related research questions. This increases the risk of chance findings and overfitting. The generalisability of results is limited because findings derive from a single feasibility-trial.

The small sample size may have contributed to baseline imbalances between groups despite randomisation, which may have influenced the change in expectations between T1 and T2. For example, the advice group had a notably lower mean expectation at T1 than the other groups, and changes to T2 may partly reflect regression to the mean rather than true group effects. Given the small group sizes, these patterns should be regarded as exploratory and descriptive rather than robust treatment-group differences.

All interventions were delivered by a single physiotherapist, meaning that all patients were effectively clustered within one therapist and therapist-level variation could not be accounted for. As a result, it cannot be ruled out that specific aspects of this therapist’s interaction style or communication contributed to the observed associations.

T2 expectations were assessed with a single-item question after the first treatment session, and treatment credibility and preference were not measured separately. This limits construct validity, and the T2 item may partly overlap with early perceived response rather than capturing expectations alone.

Among the 35 (65%) participants who changed their expectations from T1 to T2, some of the observed changes may be attributable to measurement error. However, 15 participants showed a true change greater than the suggested SDC 95% of 2.8 points.66 To reduce the burden of repeated measurement, changes in expectations were not assessed between randomisation and the first treatment. Therefore, we could not distinguish between expectation and experience effects in this study.

Expectations at T2 accounted for approximately one quarter of the variability, meaning that three quarters remained unexplained. However, Gupta et al67 suggested in a narrative review that R2>15% is generally meaningful in clinical medicine.

Conclusion

In this study, expectations measured after randomisation and one treatment session were associated with pain at 3 months for patients with tennis elbow. Larger, prospectively designed studies should investigate how postrandomisation expectations relate to clinical outcomes in non-blinded musculoskeletal trials.
