TinyLLaVA-Video: A Simple Framework of Small-scale Large Multimodal Models for Video Understanding
Xingjian Zhang, Xi Weng, Yihao Yue, Zhaoxin Fan, Wenjun Wu, Lei Huang

Abstract
We present the TinyLLaVA-Video, a video understanding model with parameters not exceeding 4B that processes video sequences in a simple manner, without the need for complex architectures, supporting both fps sampling and uniform frame sampling. Our model is characterized by modularity and scalability, allowing training and inference with limited computational resources and enabling users to replace components based on their needs. We validate the effectiveness of this framework through experiments, the best model achieving performance comparable to certain existing 7B models on multiple video understanding benchmarks. The code and training recipes are fully open source, with all components and training data publicly available. We hope this work can serve as a baseline for practitioners exploring small-scale multimodal models for video understanding. It is available at https://github.com/ZhangXJ199/TinyLLaVA-Video .



1
Introduction

In recent years, the rapid development of LLMs such as GPT-4o and Gemini

Team et al. 2023
has continuously broken through the performance limits of LLM capabilities, attracting a great deal of attention. However, due to their massive size and training data requirements, training and locally deploying such LLMs still demand costly computational resources, making them inaccessible to many researchers.

Meanwhile, language models with sizes of 7B and fewer, such as Qwen
Hui et al. 2024
and Phi
Javaheripi et al. 2023
, have also seen significant performance improvements. Their capabilities even rival those of larger models from earlier generations. Because of requiring fewer computational resources for deployment, they have become highly popular among researchers. As a result, replacing the language model in multimodal models with smaller ones can still achieve impressive performance. Consequently, a surge of small-scale multimodal models has recently emerged
Zhou et al. 2024a
;
He et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024b
. These models utilize encoders like CLIP
Radford et al. 2021
and SigLIP
Zhai et al. 2023
to understand images, excelling in benchmarks such as image-text conversation tasks.

Unlike image understanding, video-based models must address challenges such as handling long visual sequences and temporal comprehension. Due to the input-length limitations of language models, previous approaches often tackled these issues by restricting the number of frames extracted
Liu et al. 2024a
or designing complex token resampling methods
Song et al. 2024
. However, these solutions tend to result in complex model architectures, the increased number of parameters, and the loss of information from video sequences. Furthermore, some outstanding works are not fully open-sourced, making it difficult for practitioners to reproduce results or conduct video understanding research due to limited resources.

To address these challenges, we propose TinyLLaVA-Video, a simple-structured video understanding model with fewer than 4B parameters, capable of training and inference under constrained computational resources. Since TinyLLaVA-Video is derived from TinyLLaVA Factory
Jia et al. 2024
, it retains advantages such as modularity and scalability. The code and training recipes are fully open source, with all components and training data publicly available. In addition, users have the flexibility to replace language models, vision encoders, and training methods as needed. Ultimately, we demonstrate a series of small-scale video understanding models that outperform existing 7B models on multiple video understanding benchmarks.

TinyLLaVA-Video can perform inference on 3090 or 4090 GPUs without quantization, and it offers reproducibility and scalability. We hope this work can assist researchers with limited computational resources and provide a baseline for practitioners exploring small-scale video understanding models.

2
Related Work

Small-scale Language Models.
While the capability improvements of large language models in recent years are widely recognized, small-scale language models have attracted more attention from researchers with limited computational resources due to the high cost of deploying large models. Currently, many high-performing small-scale language models, such as Qwen2.5
Hui et al. 2024
, Phi-2
Javaheripi et al. 2023
, Gemma
Team et al. 2024
and Tinyllama
Zhang et al. 2024a
, are widely used. These models maintain strong performance within constrained computational resources, ensuring the continued development of small-scale multimodal models.

Small-scale Multimodal Models.
Since the introduction of fine-tuning LMMs with visual instruction tuning data by LLaVA
Liu et al. 2024b
, researchers have observed that reducing the size of language models within LLaVA can still yield satisfactory performance. This has led to the emergence of small-scale multimodal models such as TinyLLaVA
Zhou et al. 2024a
, Bunny
He et al. 2024
, and Mini-Gemini
Li et al. 2024b
. These models have achieved competitive results on benchmarks such as GQA
Hudson and Manning 2019
and MMMU
Yue et al. 2024
for image-text understanding. However, most multimodal models with fewer than 4B parameters remain limited to image understanding and struggle to comprehend video sequences.

Video Understanding Models.
The challenge of processing video sequences is constrained by the limited context length of language models, this often limits the number of frames the model can sample. To address it, different approaches have been explored. Video-ChatGPT
Maaz et al. 2023
and VideoLLaMA
Zhang et al. 2023
employ pooling and convolution techniques to reduce the number of visual tokens, MovieChat
Song et al. 2024
adopts a memory mechanism for storage, Video-LLaVA
Lin et al. 2023
designs multimodality encoders to align videos with the textual feature space and LLaVA-NEXT
Liu et al. 2024a
uses linear adjustments to allocate frame and image tokens based on the token limit. However, these methods make the model structure more complex or limit the number of frames that can be extracted from the video. Therefore, resampling visual tokens in a simple manner is crucial for small-scale video-based models.

Concurrent to our approach, LLAVA-MINI
Zhang et al. 2025
uses simple image-level resampling method to reduce visual tokens while incorporating Pre-fusion module to integrate more visual information. TinyLLaVA-Video adheres to the principles of small-scale and model simplification design, employing the video-level resampling method and achieving comparable performance without additional modules.

TinyLLaVA-Video Framework

In this section, we will introduce the overall structure of TinyLLaVA-Video and its training pipeline. The architecture of TinyLLaVA-Video is inherited from TinyLLaVA, retaining modularity and scalability, as shown in Figure

. It enables a straightforward understanding of visual sequences, and the number of extracted video frames is no longer constrained by the input-length limitations of the language model.

3.1
Model Architecture

For video sequences, TinyLLaVA-Video allows customization of the number of extracted frames, supporting both fps sampling and uniform frame sampling and using the extracted sequence
\{{X}_{1},{X}_{2},\dots,{X}_{n}\}
as input.

Vision Encoder
V_{\varphi}
.
Vision encoder
V_{\varphi}
extracts features for each image:
{H}_{i}={V}_{\varphi}({X}_{i})
, producing a visual feature sequence
\{{H}_{1},{H}_{2},\dots,{H}_{n}\}
. Following TinyLLaVA Factory, the visual encoder can be CLIP, SigLIP or Dinov2.

Connector(Resampler)
P_{\phi}
.
Before passing the visual feature sequence
\{{H}_{1},{H}_{2},\dots,{H}_{n}\}
into the connector
P_{\phi}
, it is concatenated into a long tensor:

{H}=Linear(Concat(\{{H}_{1},{H}_{2},\dots,{H}_{n}\})).

(1)

Then, learnable queries Q are initialized to learn the overall features of the visual feature sequence:

\hat{Q}=CrossAttention(\{q=Q,k\&v=H\}).

(2)

This approach extracts global features of the sequence while preserving temporal information. Its simple structure ensures that the number of extracted video frames is not limited by the context length of the language model.

Small-scale LLM
F_{\theta}
.
The input to the small-scale language model consists of text tokens obtained through the tokenizer and embedding module, along with the visual feature sequence Q, both having the same dimensions. After processing through the language model, the output is the response Y.

3.2
Training Pipeline

The base training pipeline of TinyLLaVA-Video is the same as TinyLLaVA, consisting of two stages:

Stage 1: Pre-training for Feature Alignment.
We keep the visual encoder and language model frozen, training only the connector(resampler)
P_{\phi}
to align the visual embedding
\hat{Q}
with the text embedding by video-caption data.

Stage 2: Supervised Fine-tuning.
We only keep the visual encoder frozen, use the video-text pair(X, Y) in multi-turn conversation, fully fine-tuning small-scale LLM
F_{\theta}
and connector(resampler)
P_{\phi}
.

LLaVA-Video-178K contains multiple subsets, with video captions that are highly detailed and rich in content. The length of the captions is positively correlated with the duration of the videos. Due to the selection of data types and the token limit of small-scale language models, we chose videos with durations of no more than 1 minute from the ”academic” and ”youtube” subsets as training data.

Valley is sourced from publicly available video websites. It contains various scene-descriptive video-caption data, and all the videos we use are within 5 minutes in duration.

3.3.2
Data Curation

For LLaVA-Video-178K, we only removed the corrupted videos, resulting in approximately 108k videos. Since each video corresponds to one video-caption pair and a set of multi-turn dialogue data, the dataset includes 108k video-caption pairs for pre-training and 491k dialogue questions for fine-tuning.

For Valley, the original dataset consisted of approximately 1,406k videos. To simplify and refine the dataset, we performed the following steps: first, we removed corrupted videos or those with incomplete captions. Furthermore, we observed that some captions in the dataset included descriptions of filming time, angles, and geographic locations. We considered such data excessive and unhelpful for describing video events, so we filtered them out as well, ultimately obtaining 289k video-caption data for pre-training.

4.1.2
Training Recipes

Our experiments follow the base method of TinyLLaVA for two-stage training. During pre-training, we update only the connector
P_{\phi}
while keeping the rest of the model frozen, maintaining a learning rate of 1e-4 and a batch size of 128. In the fine-tuning stage, we freeze the vision encoder
V_{\varphi}
and update both the connector
P_{\phi}
and the small-scale LLM
F_{\theta}
using a learning rate of 2e-5 and a batch size of 64. As our model is derived from TinyLLaVA Factory, it also supports customized training methods.

4.1.3
Evaluation Benchmark

We selected four widely used video-based benchmarks for evaluation: Video-MME
Fu et al. 2024
, LongVideoBench
Wu et al. 2024
, MVBench
Li et al. 2024a
and MLVU
Zhou et al. 2024b
. These benchmarks cover video lengths ranging from a few seconds to several hours, providing a comprehensive assessment of the model’s ability to understand both short and long videos.

4.2
Experimental Results

4.2.1
Investigating the Effects of Data Quality

We conducted experiments using 397k mixed data and 405k unfiltered Valley data as training data for pre-training. The results are presented in Figure
3
. It can be observed that when using data on the same scale, higher quality training data enables the model to achieve better performance on most benchmarks.

4.2.2
Investigating the Effects of Small-scale LLMs

We conducted experiments using different small-scale LLMs in the same settings. The results are presented in Figure
4
. It can be observed that when Qwen2.5-3B is used as the language model, the model performs better across multiple benchmarks. However, for long video evaluation, such as on the LongVideoBench and the long video subset of Video-MME, the model performs better when Phi-2 is used as the language model. Furthermore, the performance of both Qwen2.5-3B and Phi-2-2.7B surpasses that of Qwen2.5-1.5B and Gemma-2B-it. Therefore, we believe that using larger language models with more computational resources can further enhance performance, which also demonstrates the effectiveness of our framework.

4.2.3
Investigating the Effects of Vision Encoders

We also conducted experiments with different vision encoders. The results are presented in Figure
5
. It can be observed that using SigLIP as the vision encoder achieves the best performance, and the model using Dinov2 outperforms the model using CLIP on most benchmarks. Since video understanding still relies on the extraction of image features, using SigLIP with higher input resolution is more helpful for capturing fine-grained visual information.

4.2.4
Investigating the Effects of sampling

We uniformly used Qwen2.5-3B as the language model and SigLIP as the vision encoder to conducted experiments on different numbers of sampling frames and quantities of learnable queries, with results shown in Figure
6
. Generally, a higher number of learnable queries is more beneficial for the model to understand video information. However, increasing the number of sampling frames may not only extend the model’s processing time but could also lead to performance degradation. Only by maintaining a balance between the number of sampling frames and learnable queries can the model achieve optimal performance. This will also be one of directions for our future research.

4.2.5
Comparison with other Video-based models

Finally, we compared TinyLLaVA-Video with recent advanced multimodal models on multiple benchmarks. Although our model has fewer parameters, as shown in Table
2
and
3
, it still outperforms several 7B models on comprehensive video understanding benchmarks. Although the training data for TinyLLaVA-Video consists mostly of videos under 1 minute, as shown in Table
4
and
5
, our model’s long video understanding ability is comparable to several 7B models in evaluations on long video benchmarks. Moreover, the capabilities of TinyLLaVA-Video are not limited to evaluations, as demonstrated in the example in Figure
2
, our model excels in capturing video information and generates better video understanding than other leading works. It also confirms that model size is not the only factor affecting video understanding ability.

5
Conclusion

We propose the TinyLLaVA-Video framework, which processes video sequences in a simple manner, supporting different sampling methods without the need for complex model architectures. Our best model outperforms existing 7B models on multiple benchmarks and demonstrates exceptional capabilities in capturing and understanding video information. Our work does not require expensive computational resources and offers advantages such as modularity and scalability. The potential of small-scale video-based models still has significant room for exploration, and we hope our work can serve as a baseline for practitioners exploring small-scale video understanding models.
