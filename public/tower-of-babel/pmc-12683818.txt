Efficacy based on dose-response and safety of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells for the treatment of osteoarthritis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Elnaz Lohrasbi, Soraya Babaie, Hadi Hamedfar, Samira Pourzeinali, Vahideh Toopchizadeh, Azizeh Farshbaf-Khalili
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn recent years, the use of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), especially those derived from bone marrow and embryonic sources, has been considered as an emerging therapeutic approach for repairing joint tissue and reducing osteoarthritis symptoms. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BM-MSCs) efficacy, safety, and optimal dosing for osteoarthritis treatment.MethodsThirteen randomized trials of the 1427 articles identified (11 meta-analyzed; n = 1150 patients) were identified via PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and other databases (search updated to May 2025). The primary outcomes were the optimal dose of BM-MSCs in improving pain intensity (VAS), WOMAC scores (pain, stiffness, function, total score), and their safety, analyzed using RevMan 5.4. Cochrane risk-of-bias assessment guided quality appraisal.ResultsBM-MSCs showed no significant pain reduction (VAS) or functional improvement (WOMAC) at 1–3 months’ post-injection. Significant improvements emerged at 6 months, peaking at 12 months: VAS decreased by WMD − 2.31 (95% CI − 3.83 to − 0.80; P = 0.003), and WOMAC total score reduced by WMD − 10.14 (95% CI − 18.26 to − 2.02; P = 0.01). Subgroup analyses indicated optimal doses of ≤ 25 × 10⁶ cells for VAS improvement and ≤ 25 × 10⁶ or 25–50 × 10⁶ cells for WOMAC total score at 12 months. Safety profiles were favorable, with no serious adverse events reported in most studies.ConclusionBased on the findings of this meta-analysis, BM-MSCs, especially in the long term, can be effective in reducing pain, stiffness, and improving the quality of life of patients with osteoarthritis. Also, the favorable safety of these cells makes them a promising option in joint reconstruction treatments. However, due to the heterogeneity of studies and the lack of standardized information on the optimal dose, it seems necessary to conduct prospective studies with careful design and long-term follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04799-0.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Mesenchymal stem cells, Bone marrow, Joint regeneration, Systematic review, Meta-analysis, Joint pain, Treatment safety

Introduction

Osteoarthritis is an inflammatory joint disease characterized by the destruction of cartilage and bone tissue. As a common and chronic disabling musculoskeletal disease worldwide, it imposes a heavy social and economic burden on patients and healthcare systems [1]. To date, nearly 400 million people worldwide have been living with osteoarthritis [2]. Knee osteoarthritis, as the most common site of osteoarthritis, is the most common diagnosis for arthropathy that causes discomfort and disability in older people [3]. Knee osteoarthritis becomes more common in adults with increasing age. Obesity, knee injuries, and a family history of knee osteoarthritis are risk factors for this disease [4]. The global prevalence of knee osteoarthritis is approximately 16% in people aged 15 years and older and 23% in people aged 40 years and older [5]. Epidemiological studies have reported a significant increase in the prevalence of knee osteoarthritis in the last years, highlighting the importance of early diagnosis and management approaches in suspected patients [6]. Knee osteoarthritis patients often suffer from reduced self-care ability, joint pain, and limited mobility [7].

Currently, osteoarthritis is treated using pharmacological and non-pharmacological techniques. Non-pharmacological approaches such as self-management, regular exercise, and weight control are the first-line treatment for osteoarthritis [8]. Pharmacological approaches, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), paracetamol, topical analgesics, intra-articular corticosteroids, duloxetine, and some newer therapies such as nerve growth factor (NGF) antibodies, are recommended to relieve pain and reduce disability in knee osteoarthritis [9]. However, instead of regenerating damaged articular cartilage, the aforementioned therapies aim to reduce discomfort and increase joint mobility [10].

Although various treatment modalities have been proposed for the treatment of this disease, the use of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BM-MSCs) has been considered as a new and novel therapeutic approach [8, 11]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have the potential for self-renewal, differentiation into cartilage, and immune modulation. Injection of MSCs has been demonstrated as an advanced therapeutic approach for the treatment of osteoarthritis by numerous preclinical and clinical studies [12]. The most common method of MSC administration for patients with knee osteoarthritis is intra-articular injection. Intra-articular injection of MSCs is associated with proliferation or differentiation into cartilage [13].

BM-MSCs constitute 0.001–0.01% of the bone marrow mononuclear cells. Due to their low abundance, culture and expansion of these cells under advanced in vitro conditions are required to obtain sufficient numbers for studies or clinical applications [14]. Some of the properties of BM-MSCs, such as ease of isolation from BM without causing immunological problems, ability to expand in vitro in a short period of time, bioavailability for implantation at the desired site with minimal loss of differentiation, and acceptable safety (no serious adverse reactions have been reported during autologous or allogeneic therapy), have made BM-MSCs an important tool in regenerative medicine [14]. Furthermore, BM-MSCs have demonstrated potent therapeutic potential for the treatment of a wide range of debilitating diseases, with more than 130 clinical trials currently registered/ongoing worldwide to elucidate the potential of BM-MSC-based cell therapy in the treatment of a variety of diseases [15].

The remarkable prevalence of osteoarthritis and its significant impact on patients’ quality of life underscore the importance of studies in the field of treatment methods for this disease. The use of new therapeutic methods such as regenerative medicine has been associated with promising results in recent studies. However, determining the optimal dose and safety of BM-MSCs for the treatment of osteoarthritis has not been done in previous studies, and this study was designed and implemented with this aim. This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to determine the appropriate dose, therapeutic effects, and safety of BM-MSCs on patients with osteoarthritis. In this study, scientific resources related to the use of BM-MSCs in the treatment of osteoarthritis was collected, reviewed, and meta-analyzed. By completing this systematic review and meta-analysis, comprehensive and complete information was obtained regarding the optimal and safe dose of BM-MSCs for the treatment of osteoarthritis, which can help improve the treatment of these patients.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was designed and conducted according to the PRISMA 2020 (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines. The study protocol was pre-written and registered in the PROSPERO database (registration ID: CRD420251104404). This study was approved by ethical committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (Ethics number: IR.TBZMED.VCR.REC.1403.135).

Search strategy

Searches were exhaustively conducted on a systematic basis through Web of Science, Cochrane Library, PubMed, Scopus, ScienceDirect, ProQuest, ClinicalTrials.gov and Embase with no time limitation up to May 2025. The following keywords and medical subject headings (MeSH) were combined: (‘osteoarthritis’ OR ‘arthritis, degenerative’ OR ‘arthritis, noninflammatory’ OR ‘arthrosis’ OR ‘degenerative arthritis’ OR ‘degenerative joint disease’ OR ‘noninflammatory arthritis’ OR ‘osteo-arthritis’ OR ‘osteo-arthrosis’ OR ‘osteoarthritis’ OR ‘osteoarthrosis’ OR ‘primary osteoarthritis’ OR ‘rheumatoid arthrosis’) AND (‘mesenchymal stem cell’/de OR ‘mesenchymal progenitor cell’ OR ‘mesenchymal stem cell’ OR ‘mesenchymal stem cells’ OR ‘stem cell, mesenchymal’ OR ‘bone marrow cell’ OR ‘bone marrow cell’ OR ‘bone marrow cells’ OR ‘bone marrow derived cell’ OR ‘bone stem cell’ OR ‘cell, bone marrow’ OR ‘marrow stem cell’ OR ‘myeloid cell’ OR ‘myeloid cells’) AND (‘clinical trial’ OR ‘clinical drug trial’ OR ‘clinical trial’ OR ‘major clinical trial’ OR ‘trial, clinical’ OR ‘controlled clinical trial’ OR ‘clinical trial, controlled’ OR ‘controlled clinical comparison’ OR ‘controlled clinical drug trial’ OR ‘controlled clinical experiment’ OR ‘controlled clinical study’ OR ‘controlled clinical test’ OR ‘controlled clinical trial’ OR rct OR ‘randomized controlled trial’ OR ‘controlled trial, randomized’ OR ‘randomised controlled study’ OR ‘randomised controlled trial’ OR ‘randomized controlled study’ OR ‘randomized controlled trial’ OR ‘trial, randomized controlled’).

A supplementary search of grey literature and reference lists from review articles was conducted using Google Scholar to identify additional relevant sources. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published on investigating effects of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells on human subjects diagnosed with knee osteoarthritis, involving intra-articular injection of BM-MSCs and reporting quantitative data on pain, function, stiffness or whole-organ magnetic resonance imaging score (WORMS), regardless of the study time was reviewed. Studies not being interventional randomized trials, not reporting intended outcomes, or involving composite injections were excluded. All observational (cross-sectional, case-control and cohort), animal studies, case reports, narrative reviews, letters to the editor, protocols, or conference abstracts without full data and systematic reviews were also excluded.

In this study, the primary outcomes were efficacy based on dose-response and safety, and cost-effectiveness was as secondary outcome (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of study process

Study selection

Two reviewers (E.L, S.B) independently screened the titles and abstracts identified in the literature search after ethics committee approval. Both reviewers (E.L, S.B) downloaded the full texts of all potentially relevant studies for independent assessment and data extraction. Any disagreement with article selection was resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (A.F.Kh).

Data extraction

A predesigned extraction form was used by two authors (H.H, S.P) to independently screen the literature and extract the data. The following data were extracted: the first author’s name, journal, year of publication, study design, participants (e.g., number of participants, age, gender), intervention details (e.g., BM-MSC dosage, duration of supplementation), comparator, dose of BM-MSCs, source of BM-MSCs, outcomes assessed (both primary and secondary outcomes), adverse events & safety.

Quality assessment

Bias risk evaluation was conducted through dual independent assessment by two researchers following Cochrane Collaboration guidelines [16]. Domains analyzed included randomization procedures, allocation concealment, participant/personnel blinding, outcome assessor masking, attrition, and reporting biases. Discrepancies in judgments were resolved via third-party adjudication (V.T.T). Visual representation of bias profiles was generated using Review Manager 5.3 (RevMan; Cochrane Collaboration, UK).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative synthesis utilized RevMan 5.4.1 (Nordic Cochrane Centre, Denmark). Treatment effects were quantified as weighted mean differences (WMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) between intervention and control arms. Pre-post intervention changes were derived by subtracting follow-up means from baseline values. Standard deviations (SD) were calculated using:

where SDb, SDf, and r represent baseline SD, follow-up SD, and baseline-follow-up correlation [17]. Median/interquartile range (IQR) data were converted to mean ± SD via validated methods [18]. Standard errors (SE) were transformed to SD using SD = SE×√n. For studies lacking numerical data, Universal Desktop Ruler 3.8 software extracted values from graphical representations. All conversions adhered to standardized methodologies to ensure analytical consistency. The chi-squared distribution and the I² statistic assessed the study heterogeneity. A random effects model was used when I² was greater than 50%; otherwise, a fixed effects model was adopted. If heterogeneity was observed, sensitivity analysis was performed through a sequential exclusion method, and a funnel plot was generated to assess publication bias. In cases of different scoring for Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and Western Ontario and McMaster Universities (WOMAC), they converted to standard and identical scale. For different efficacy outcomes, sub-group analyses were done based on diverse applied BM-MSCs doses as: ≤25 × 106 cells, 25 to 50 × 106 cells, ≥50 × 106 cells, or non-reported dose. Two studies had no reported outcomes similar to other included studies, and so explained only as qualitative data (systematic review) [19, 20].

Results

Study selection

Figure 1 depicts the search and screening process for the literature based on the PRISMA statement. The initial search yielded a total of 1428 articles alongside four additional records identified through other sources. After eliminating duplicate entries (n = 612), 820 publications were subjected to eligibility assessment. Applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria led to the exclusion of 792 articles. Additionally, at final stage, 15 full-text articles excluded due to being not interventional randomized study, not intended outcomes and composite injections. Ultimately, an analysis was performed on 13 eligible publications comprising RCTs and prospective clinical trials were included in systematic review [19–31], and 11 publications entered in meta-analysis [21–31]. The included studies had publication dates ranging from 2015 to 2023 across various countries including the USA, Mexico, India, France, Iran, Brazil, China, and Spain.

Study characteristics

A total of 1150 participants were involved across the 13 studies. Among these participants, 824 were assigned to the intervention group (MSC-treated patients), while 466 were in the control group receiving usual care (In Hernigou et al., each patient served as their own control). From 18 to 480 was the sample sizes range. Participants (both genders) had the mean age between 55 and 75 years. According to Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) evaluations, the osteoarthritis grade usually ranges between II and IV. The intra-articular administration of MSCs were assessed in all included studies. Ex vivo-expanded cell preparations, stromal vascular fractions, or fresh bone marrow aspirate concentrates were used to deliver MSCs. There were significant differences in the dosages, ranging from 6 million to 150 million cells per injection, with most studies administering 25, 40, or 100 million MSCs in a single shot. Multiple trials have combined MSCs with hyaluronic acid (HA) or platelet-rich plasma (PRP). The comparators across studies comprised of placebo (saline or vehicle medium) [21, 23, 24], corticosteroid injections [19, 30], PRP [20], hyaluronic acid [26, 28, 29, 31], and conventional pharmacological therapy (e.g., acetaminophen) [22, 25, 27]. The contralateral knee receiving total knee arthroplasty in one study [25]. Primary and secondary outcomes varied across studies but generally included: pain and function (assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Arthritis Index (WOMAC), Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), Knee Society Scores (KSS), and Lequesne index, structural evaluation (assessed using MRI (T2 mapping, WORMS score), X-ray (joint space width), and quantitative cartilage assessments), and quality of life and global function (assessed using SF-12, SF-36, EQ-5D, PROMIS-29). Follow-up durations ranged from 6 to 24 months, with one study reporting long-term follow-up data up to 15 years [25]. Table 1 presents detailed information about the included studies, including their methodologies, participant characteristics, and key findings. This table serves as a valuable resource for understanding the context and outcomes of the research conducted on MSCs efficacy and safety in treatment of osteoarthritis.

Risk of bias assessments

Risk of bias assessment of the included studies showed that most studies had low to moderate risk of bias in most domains. The highest level of concern was related to “allocation concealment” and “blinding of outcome assessment”. Some studies also had ambiguity in the area of ​​“selective reporting”. In addition, more than 70% of the studies had low risk of bias in the area of ​​randomization. However, in the areas of allocation concealment and blinding, a higher percentage of studies had unclear or high risk of bias (Figs. 2 and 3).

Meta-analysis results

In this section, the results of the meta-analysis of data extracted from RCTs included in the study are presented by outcome variables.

VAS assessment

In the analysis of VAS in the first month after the intervention, 4 studies (with 7 between-group comparisons) were included in the analysis. Heterogeneity analysis showed an I2 level of 52% and a p-value of 0.05. Therefore, a random effects model was applied. As a result of meta-analysis, MSC-treated patients and control groups showed no statistically significant differences (P = 0.44), which indicates there may not be a significant reduction in VAS after using MSCs in osteoarthritis patients [WMD: 0.20 (95% CI − 0.30, 0.70)]. Similarly, sub-group analysis based on dose classification illustrated no significant inter-group diversity in this regard (p > 0.05) (Fig. 4). In evaluating VAS score after 3 months intervention, six RCTs (with nine between-group comparisons) were included. Heterogeneity analysis returned an I2 level of 83% and a p-value less than 0.00001, indicating statistical heterogeneity among five studies. Therefore, the random effects model was applied. As a result of meta-analysis, MSC-treated patients and control groups showed no statistically significant differences (P = 0.40), which indicates there may not be a significant reduction in VAS using MSCs in osteopathists patients after 3 months [WMD: − 0.25 (95% CI − 0.83, 0.33)]. Sub-group analysis based on dose classification indicated no significant between-group differences (p > 0.05) (Fig. 5) In evaluating VAS score after 6 months intervention, eight RCTs (with 11 between-group comparisons) were included. Heterogeneity analysis returned an I2 level of 81% and a p-value less than 0.00001, indicating statistical heterogeneity among eight studies. Therefore, the random effects model was applied. As a result of meta-analysis, MSC-treated patients and control groups showed statistically significant differences (p = 0.01), which indicates there may be a significant reduction in VAS using MSCs in osteopathists patients after 6 months [WMD: − 0.65 (95% CI − 1.16, − 0.13)]. Sub-group analysis according to dose classification indicated a significant difference only in the applied dose less than 25 × 106 cells [WMD: − 1.33 (95% CI − 1.83, − 0.84); p < 0.0001] (Fig. 6a). Potential publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot. It appears that there is no substantial evidence of publication bias based on the distribution of studies on the plot. Although the number of studies is limited, it is difficult to detect asymmetry, particularly among certain subgroups (Fig. 6b). After 12-month intervention based on eight included RCTs (with 12 between-group comparisons), MSC-treated patients and control groups showed statistically significant differences (P = 0.003) using random effect (I2 = 92%), which indicates there may be a significant reduction in VAS using MSCs in osteopathists patients after 12 months [WMD: − 2.31 (95% CI − 3.83, − 0.80)]. Sub-group analysis according to dose classification indicated a significant difference only in the dose less than 25 × 106 cells [WMD: − 5.25 (95% CI − 9.19, − 1.32); p = 0.009] (Fig. 7a). The funnel plot indicated that there is no significant publication bias for VAS after 12-month intervention. The distribution of studies around the mean effect appears to be relatively uniform and symmetrical. Studies with higher precision (top) and lower precision (bottom) are well located on either side of the mean (Fig. 7b).

Discussion

Despite the availability of multiple studies on the use of BM-MSCs for treating osteoarthritis, the optimal dosage and safety of these cells for this treatment have not yet been clarified; therefore, this study provides reliable evidence on the efficacy based on dose-response and safety of MSCs in the treatment of osteoarthritis, especially knee osteoarthritis, utilizing high-quality randomized clinical trials. This study, which was conducted by carefully reviewing 13 RCTs and analyzing data from 11 of them, was able to draw a more accurate picture of the improvement of symptoms over time after stem cell injection. As a chronic, debilitating musculoskeletal disease that mostly affects the knees, hips and hands, osteoarthritis is considered as one of the most common chronic diseases in the world [32]. In elderly people and those with sedentary lifestyles, this disease often decreases motor performance, causes chronic pain, disrupts daily activities, and reduces quality of life [33]. As a result of life expectancy increases and a growing elderly population over the past few decades, osteoarthritis rates have also increased significantly in the term of prevalence and incidence [34]. Nevertheless, health systems are burdened with the costs of treating and managing this disease, including medication, physiotherapy, joint replacement surgeries, and work absences [34]. It is estimated that millions of people suffer from pain and limitations caused by this disease [35]. In severe cases, total joint replacement is the only remaining treatment option, and it can be challenging as well [35]. Current treatments, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroid injections, physiotherapy, and joint supplements, have only been able to provide temporary relief of symptoms, but none of them can stop or reverse the process of articular cartilage destruction [36]. Meanwhile, regenerative therapies, such as MSC therapy, are becoming increasingly popular. Besides inhibiting inflammation and relieving symptoms, these therapies can also stimulate repair processes and regenerate joint tissues, which can have longer-term effects [37]. In this regard, the present study was designed to evaluate the efficacy exploring the optimal dose and safety of bone marrowstem cells in the treatment of patients with osteoarthritis. The focus of this study was on examining clinical outcomes such as pain severity, joint stiffness, and motor function, as well as determining the optimal dose and analyzing safety data, which could help provide more precise evidence about the benefits or limitations of this method. The findings of this study, especially in the long term, will play an important role in clinical decision-making, the design of future trials, and the expansion of the application of cell therapy in degenerative joint diseases.

What is clear from the review of the results is that the effectiveness of stem cells in improving osteoarthritis symptoms is mainly associated with a time delay. The most important outcomes of the intervention group were not significantly different from those of the control group after short-term assessments (such as 1 and 3 months after the intervention). In the long term, particularly after 6 and 12 months, some clinically important outcomes improved significantly. One of the most important pain measurement tools is the VAS scale. While no statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups during the first month and 3 months after the intervention, a significant reduction in pain was reported at the sixth month, which continued to be more pronounced at the twelfth month. The optimal and effective dose among studies according to sub-group analysis was minimal applied dose i.e. ≤25 × 10 cells. This indicates that MSCs, through regenerative and anti-inflammatory mechanisms, begin to exert their effects on pain reduction over time. The decrease in VAS score at the 12th month was clinically significant and indicated significant pain relief for the patients. The assessment of the WOMAC total score, a comprehensive measure of pain, joint stiffness, and motor function, also showed a similar pattern. A modest decrease in the overall WOMAC score was observed at the first and third months, but a more dramatic and statistically significant difference appeared at the 12 month. The optimal and effective dose among studies according to sub-group analysis was ≤ 25 × 106 cells or 25 < cells ≤ 50 × 106 cells. These findings suggest that MSC injection not only helps relieve pain, but also plays an effective role in improving patients’ daily functioning and reducing their physical limitations. An evaluation of the individual components of the WOMAC questionnaire yielded mixed results. There was a significant increment in WOMAC-Pain score at the first month compared to control which was observed in cases of using more than 50 × 106 cells, but this improvement did not continue at the third month, while significant reduction was seen at the 12 month assessment with optimal dose of 25 < cells ≤ 50 × 106 cells. Based on this pattern, it appears that the severity of disease, an individual’s immune response, or other intervening factors may influence pain response to treatment. The WOMAC-Stiffness scores also showed that the effect of stem cells in the short term was not very clear, but at 12 months, a significant improvement was observed with the effective and optimal dose of ≤ 25 × 106 cells. Since joint stiffness is one of the annoying aspects of osteoarthritis that reduces quality of life, especially in the early hours of the day, this finding is of great clinical importance. The WOMAC-Function score also showed a non-significant improvement at most time points, however, a significant decrease in the function score was reported at 12 months, which, although statistically at the threshold of significance, from a clinical perspective indicates an improvement in functional independence and a decrease in motor disability of patients. At 6 and 12 months, no significant differences were found in the WORMS, which measures knee function; this suggests that this scale may be less sensitive to detecting gradual changes due to cell therapy.

In addition to pain-related measures, by analyzing instruments such as KOOS and SF-36, researchers have improved understanding of how interventions affect functional outcomes and quality of life among patients. In three studies, KOOS was used to assess functional outcomes. According to Mautner et al., a phase 2/3 clinical trial with four parallel arms compared changes in KOOS pain scores 12 months after injection, but no significant difference was found between stem cell and control groups (corticosteroid) [30]. Two other studies conducted by Bastos et al., the full version of the KOOS was applied to investigate several functional domains, such as quality of life [19, 20]. In one study, the total KOOS score in both MSC and MSC + PRP groups showed significant improvement at 12 months [20]. Also, in other study, all three treatment groups (MSC, MSC + PRP, and corticosteroids) experienced significant improvement in most KOOS subscales from baseline to 12 months, with the difference that the improvement in the stem cell groups was greater in terms of intensity and durability [19]. In the study by Ho et al., the standard SF-36 instrument was used to assess general quality of life at four time points (3, 6, 9, and 12 months after the intervention). The results showed that in the BM-MSC group, significant improvements were observed in several subscales, including physical function, bodily pain, and physical component, while in the control group (hyaluronic acid), no such improvements were reported. These findings indicate a positive effect of cell therapy on the quality of life of patients with knee osteoarthritis during 1 year of follow-up [26].

According to the study results, using MSCs, whether autologous or allogeneic, poses no serious complications from a safety perspective. A mild and transient local reaction such as inflammation or pain was reported, which resolved spontaneously after a short period of time. The safety of MSCs has been confirmed, and it is now possible to use them more widely in patients who are susceptible and refractory to other treatments. Likewise, in our meta-analysis, we noted that MSC injection did not have a standard dose. A total of 6 to 150 million cells were used in the reviewed studies, and in many cases, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) or hyaluronic acid was added. The optimal dose for improvement of pain and other symptoms in osteoarthritis was achieved by less than 50 × 106 cells (≤ 25 × 106 or 25 < cells ≤ 50 × 106). It seems that using high doses of stem cells may not result in better results and even week improvement compared to lower doses. A review and meta-analysis by Huang et al. [38], provided robust evidence on the efficacy and safety of adipose tissue derived-MSC therapy for osteoarthritis, particularly knee osteoarthritis. This study, by carefully reviewing 13 randomized clinical trials and analyzing data from 11 studies, provides a more detailed picture of the improvement in patients’ symptoms over time after stem cell injection. According to the findings of this study, the efficacy of MSCs is mainly associated with a time lag; in short-term assessments (1 to 3 months after injection), no significant difference was observed compared to the control group, but in longer time periods (6 and 12 months), significant clinical improvements in pain, joint stiffness, and motor function were reported. In terms of cell dose, this study identified doses below 25 million and between 25 and 50 million cells as optimal and effective doses for pain reduction (based on VAS) and functional improvement (based on WOMAC). Interestingly, doses above 50 million cells showed an initial improvement in pain in the first month, but this improvement did not persist, and ultimately the intermediate and lower doses had more sustained clinical efficacy. Also, joint stiffness and daily function symptoms improved significantly with doses of 25 million cells or less at 12 months post-injection. This pattern of results is consistent with the findings of the present study. Specifically, the meta-analysis results showed that high doses (>50 million cells), although they may have better or faster results in the short term, also resulted in more side effects and did not provide more sustained efficacy than intermediate or lower doses. On the other hand, optimal and balanced doses (less than 50 million cells) provide a good balance between efficacy and safety and significantly improve patients’ pain and function over a period of 12 months. It is important to consider the risk of side effects in addition to symptom improvement when choosing a therapeutic dose to achieve the best clinical outcome with the least risk. It should also be kept in mind that several factors such as disease severity, individual immune response, and the concomitant use of complementary therapies (such as platelet-rich plasma or hyaluronic acid) can influence the therapeutic response. The effectiveness of MSCs combined with other therapeutic modalities needs to be evaluated in future clinical trials focusing on dose-response and comparing different cell sources (bone marrow vs. umbilical cord). Moreover, the findings of this study demonstrated that treatment with MSCs for knee osteoarthritis could significantly improve patients’ pain, stiffness, and function over a 1-year period. Currently, conventional treatments mainly aim to relieve symptomatic relief and do not have regenerative properties. MSCs may play a crucial role in therapeutic strategies in the future due to their emerging and promising nature [39].

Despite the significant findings of this study, some limitations should be considered in interpreting the results. First, there was high heterogeneity between the included studies in some outcomes—especially at intermediate time points such as 6 months—which may be due to differences in the dose of stem cells injected, the source of cells (autologous or allogeneic), the concomitant use of complementary interventions such as PRP or hyaluronic acid, and variation in initial disease severity (based on the Kellgren-Lawrence classification). Second, some studies did not provide sufficient information on the method of randomization, blinding of patients and assessors, or allocation concealment, which could introduce bias and reduce confidence in the results to some extent. Third, in many studies, the sample size was relatively small and, in some cases, patients were followed up for only 6 or 12 months, whereas osteoarthritis is a chronic disease and it is important to examine the long-term effectiveness of cellular interventions. A key limitation is the insufficient data within individual dose subgroups. The preponderance of subgroups containing only one to three studies significantly undermines the reliability and generalizability of the dose-specific findings.

In order to address these limitations, MSCs need to be assessed over a longer period of time to assess their long-term effects, especially on joint tissue structure (by using MRIs or arthroscopes). Future studies should have multicenter designs, larger sample sizes, and longer follow-up periods (at least 24 months). The analysis of factors predicting response to treatment (biomarker-based stratification) can also assist in optimizing treatment and optimizing outcomes, including comparing different cell doses, examining response to treatment in different subgroups (e.g., age, gender, disease severity), and comparing response to treatment across subgroups. The future of MSC treatments may benefit from studies that combine MSCs with other novel approaches, such as inflammation inhibitors, bioscaffolds, or gene therapy.

Conclusion

The present study, using a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials, provides acceptable evidence regarding the efficacy based on dose-response and safety of bone marrow-derived in the treatment of osteoarthritis. MSCs can significantly improve pain, reduce stiffness in joints, and improve motor function in patients who receive intra-articular injections, especially for longer periods (6 to 12 months), although a significant effect is not observed for short-term injections. The optimal dose for improvement of pain and other symptoms was achieved by less than 50 × 106 cells (≤ 25 × 106 or 25 < cells ≤ 50 × 106). These results are of great importance given the limitations of current therapeutic methods, which often only have a palliative role, and introduce MSCs as a promising therapeutic option with the potential to regenerate cartilage tissue. On the other hand, the results of this study also confirm the favorable safety of stem cells; no serious complications were reported, and the occurrence of mild and transient complications was limited and not worrisome. It is important to interpret the results of these studies with caution, however, since they differ in methods, doses, and patient characteristics. For these cells to be effective at optimal doses, structurally efficacious, and for long-term clinical use, prospective studies involving careful design, large sample sizes, and long-term follow-up are still necessary. It was concluded that this study is a critical step towards developing and applying cell-based regenerative therapies for osteoarthritis patients, paving the way for individualized, safe, and effective therapies.
