Consensus H3N2 HA immunogens incorporating key antigenic mutations elicit broad-spectrum neutralizing responses
Authors: Yimeng An, Meina Cai, Chenyan Zhao, Mengyi Zhang, Xi Wu, Weijin Huang, Xu Zhou, Youchun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSeasonal influenza A/H3N2 poses a significant public health burden due to its rapid antigenic evolution, necessitating frequent vaccine updates. This study systematically analysed the antigenic evolution of 14 H3N2 vaccine strains (2000–2022) using pseudovirus-based neutralization assays and antigenic cartography. Vaccine strains exhibited cross-neutralization patterns, with higher reactivity among strains from adjacent years, forming six antigenic clusters. Key amino acid mutations (e.g. 144K, 189N) were identified as critical drivers of antigenic variation, with clustering-specific effects on neutralization sensitivity. Based on these findings, a consensus HA immunogen (consensus up-HA) was designed by combining mutations at key sites. This immunogen demonstrated broad-spectrum neutralization against pseudoviruses representing circulating strains from 2011 to 2024, including recent variants. These results highlight the potential of targeted HA modifications to develop broadly cross-reactive H3N2 vaccine candidate, offering broader and more durable neutralizing activity against evolving H3N2 strains.

Introduction

Influenza poses a significant threat to public health due to its high morbidity and mortality. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), there are approximately 3 to 5 million severe cases of influenza and 290,000 to 650,000 influenza deaths worldwide each year [1,2].

Vaccination is the primary preventive measure against influenza [3,4]. Since A/H3N2 influenza virus first emerged in the human population in 1968, the A/H3N2 component of the seasonal influenza vaccine has been updated approximately 40 times to keep pace with viral evolution and preserve vaccine effectiveness [5]. The selection of effective vaccine strains relies on assessing the antigenic match between vaccine and circulating strains, and to rapidly identify emerging variants [6]. Vaccine strain updates are generally deemed essential when hemagglutination inhibition assay results show more than a four-fold disparity between the current vaccine component and prevalent or emergent viral strains [5].

Normally, seasonal influenza vaccine efficacy is approximately 60%, but if the vaccine strain does not match the currently prevalent strain, vaccine protection will be reduced to 10%–20% [7]. To better prevent seasonal influenza epidemics, there is a need to develop universal influenza vaccines with broad-spectrum protection against strains from various branches within the same subtype or even between different subtypes [8,9]. Current universal influenza vaccine designs targeting the hemagglutinin (HA) include headless HAs [10,11], chimeric HAs [12–14], mosaic HAs [15–17], and computationally optimized broadly reactive antigens(COBRA) [18–21]. Universal influenza vaccine designs targeting other proteins, such as the neuraminidase (NA) [22–24] and M2e protein [25], have also been developed. Vaccine strains are generally selected from the epidemic strains that show similar antigenicity to that predicted for next season's epidemic strains. Therefore, systematic analysis of the antigenicity of previously recommended vaccine strains helped us to identify critical amino acid sites that affect antigenicity. These sites are critical for the design of broad-spectrum immunogens.

Pseudoviruses can be rapidly constructed with any desired HA sequence, enabling flexible generation of variant panels that include both historical wild-type strains from distant years and specific mutant variants without requiring virus isolation and adaptation [26]. This advantage makes pseudoviruses particularly suitable for applications in broad-spectrum neutralization assessment and antigenic variation analysis. The luciferase reporter system further provides quantitative and reproducible readouts with high sensitivity for detecting neutralizing antibody responses, and the assay is readily amenable to high-throughput screening, making it well-suited for large-scale antigenic analyses [26,27]. Previous studies have demonstrated strong correlations between pseudovirus neutralization titres and those obtained by traditional microneutralization and HI assays for influenza viruses [26,28–31].

In this study, we constructed pseudoviruses representing the WHO-recommended A/H3N2 vaccine strains from 2000 to 2022 using the HIV system, and identified critical point mutations driving antigenic variation. HA antigens were designed by incorporating critical mutations into the consensus HA sequence derived from the vaccine strains, and their abilities to induce broad-spectrum neutralizing antibodies were evaluated.

Results

Antigenic analysis of the recommended A/H3N2 vaccine strains

All 14 WHO-recommended vaccine strains were successfully constructed as pseudoviruses using the HIV vector system. HA-encoding recombinant plasmids were used for guinea pig immunization. Guinea pigs were immunized with HA-encoding plasmids, and serum samples collected after the third immunization were tested for neutralizing activity against the panel of pseudoviruses (Figure S1a).

Neutralization titre (NT50) of immunized sera against homologous strains were approximately 104–105 (Figure S1b). Cross-neutralization analysis revealed that vaccine strains recommended before 2015 (TE/12, VI/11, PE/09, BR/07, WI/05, CA/04) showed higher cross-neutralization activity. Sera against these strains could neutralize pseudoviruses of at least three other vaccine strains, with sensitivity decreasing as the temporal distance between isolates increased, approximating a normal distribution (Figure S1i-n). In contrast, immunized sera for post-2015 vaccine strains (CA/20, HK/19, KA/17, SI/16, SW/13, HK/14) generally showed lower cross-reactivity (Figure S1c-h). Notably, HK/19 and HK/14 vaccine strains shared high sequence homology with only one amino acid difference in HA. They demonstrated nearly identical antigenicity and high cross-reactivity with each other, but neither was able to cross-neutralize other vaccine strains.

Antigenic distance of the A/H3N2 vaccine strains was further characterized using antigenic cartography based on NT50 values of the serum/variant pairs. This analysis grouped the 14 strains into six distinct antigenic clusters in three-dimensional space: Cluster 1 (CA/20, HK/14, HK/19); Cluster 2 (KA/17, SW/13); Cluster 3 (SI/16, PE/09, TE/12, VI/11); Cluster 4 (BR/07, WI/05, CA/04); Cluster 5 (FU/02); and Cluster 6 (MO/99) (Figure 1(a)). While temporally adjacent strains often clustered together, exceptions were observed (e.g. SW/13 and KA/17). Genetic evolution analysis of the HA protein indicated that antigenic changes were not always synchronous with genetic divergence in cluster 2 and 3 (Figure S1). Comparison of HA amino acid sequences suggested that an identity below 95% for the full-length HA or below 93% for the HA1 subunit typically signifies a significant antigenic difference, placing strains in separate clusters (Tables S1, S2).

To quantify inter-cluster differences, we calculated the fold-reduction in NT50 for cross-neutralization. Neutralization titres between different clusters were typically reduced by more than ten-fold (Figure 1(b)). The exception was between Cluster 2 and Cluster 3, where the reduction was only approximately ten-fold.

In summary, the antigenic evolution of A/H3N2 vaccine strains largely follows a pattern of gradual antigenic drift, resulting in the formation of distinct clusters. However, certain key amino acid substitutions can disproportionately influence antigenicity, leading to deviations from this overall trend.

Key amino acid sites affecting the antigenicity of vaccine strains

To identify key amino acid sites that drive antigenic variation of vaccine strains, we determined the total number of amino acid differences across the full-length HA proteins from the 14 vaccine strains (n = 93). We selected the HK/19 vaccine strain with the lowest cross-neutralization effect on other vaccine strains as the reference strain and statistically analysed the location data for the above amino acid differences. Then, we successfully constructed 76 mutant pseudoviruses based on the HIV system (Table S3).

There are a series of receptor binding sites (RBSs) in the HA1 protein of influenza virus strain A/H3N2, and five classical antigenic epitopes (A, B, C, D, and E) exist in the region adjacent to the RBSs [32]. Antibodies recognizing these epitopes can have a neutralizing effect on the RBS region by making it inaccessible after epitope binding [33]. After screening, we found ten mutant pseudoviruses that reduced serum neutralization sensitivity: S144I at epitope A, and A128T and K189S at epitope B reduced serum neutralization sensitivity by two-fold; S144K and T131K at epitope A, S193F at epitope B, S219Y at epitope D, and N225G in the RBS region 220-loop reduced serum neutralization sensitivity by four-fold; and K135T at epitope A and K189N at epitope B reduced serum neutralization sensitivity by eight-fold (Figures 2 and 3(a), Table S4). While certain positions such as position 144 in HA are known to be associated with N-linked glycosylation in some H3N2 strains, the amino acid substitutions identified in our study do not predominantly involve changes in N-linked glycosylation sequons (N-X-S/T motifs) in the HK/19 genetic background. Thus, the antigenic effects observed are to a large extent attributable to direct alterations of epitope structure. In addition, we also found that the neutralizing activity of immunized guinea pig sera was significantly higher against five of the mutant pseudoviruses, including a two-fold increase in the neutralizing activity of immunized sera against S135K at epitope A, I160K at epitope B, and K171N at epitope D, and a four-fold increase in the neutralizing activity of S138A and G142R mutant pseudoviruses at epitope A (Figures 2 and 3(b), Table S4).

Consistent with prior knowledge that mutations at antigenic epitopes determine antigenicity, we found that the mutations that reduced serum neutralization activity were mostly present in the vaccine strains belonging to clusters 2–6. These results further confirm that amino acid changes at these loci are important contributors 2 strains, in the specific genetic background of our study (HK/19-based mutations), these identified mutation for the differences in antigenicity of vaccine strains between clusters (Table S4). Therefore, we identified key residues with cluster-specific or cluster-predominant patterns: 219Y is predominantly found in cluster 2; 144K is shared between clusters 2 and 3; 189N is specific to cluster 4; 189S and 225G are found in clusters 5 and 6; and 144I is specific to cluster 6.

However, the mutations that enhance serum neutralizing activity do not have a clear clustering profile and occur in almost all antigenic clusters (Table S4). Alteration of these amino acid sites alone is not the main reason for the antigenicity of the vaccine strains, and it is more likely that synergistic or antagonistic effects with other key amino acid sites ultimately cause differences in antigenicity between vaccine strains from different antigenic clusters.

Design and evaluation of broadly neutralizing HA immunogens incorporated with critical mutations

As vaccine strains are representative of circulating strains, to develop a broad-spectrum vaccine immunogen, we first generated a consensus HA sequence derived from the 14 historical WHO-recommended A/H3N2 vaccine strains. Based on our antigenicity data, we formulated two design hypotheses targeting key mutations: Reverting residues associated with reduced serum neutralization might recapture breadth by exposing conserved epitopes; Incorporating residues from recent circulating strains that confer dominant phenotypes could better match contemporary viruses.

Guided by these hypotheses, we designed four immunogens: The unmodified consensus-HA; consensus up-HA: Incorporated the “breadth-enhancing” mutations (138S, 142G, 145S, 160I, 171 K); consensus down-HA: Incorporated the “contemporary-matching” mutations (144S, 193S); consensus up&down-HA: A combination of all aforementioned mutations (Table 1, Figure S3). The four recombinant HA ectodomain proteins were successfully expressed in HEK293F cells and purified by HisTrap affinity chromatography. SDS-PAGE analysis under reducing conditions showed bands migrating at approximately 100 kDa, higher than the theoretical molecular weight (∼65–70 kDa) due to N-linked glycosylation in mammalian cells (Figure S4a). The identity of the purified proteins was further confirmed by Western blot using an anti-His tag antibody (Figure S4b). All constructs contain a C-terminal T4 fibritin foldon trimerization domain to promote stable homotrimer formation [34,35]. SEC-MALS analysis confirmed that all four constructs adopt native trimeric conformations with monodisperse oligomeric states (Table S5 and Figure S5). It was determined that a 1 µg/dose was sufficient for a robust prime-boost humoral immune response in guinea pigs (Figure S6).

Guinea pigs were immunized with each of the four consensus immunogens formulated with BFA03 adjuvant. Archived sera from guinea pigs previously immunized with the monovalent HK/19 vaccine stock, using the same immunization protocol, were included as a reference control. Serum neutralizing activity was then evaluated against a comprehensive pseudovirus library representing high-frequency circulating A/H3N2 strains from 2011 to 2024 (Table S5) [36]. Heatmap results showed that the consensus-based immunogens elicited substantially higher and more uniform neutralizing activity compared to HK/19 sera (Figure 4). Among the four consensus immunogens, consensus up-HA immune sera demonstrated remarkably uniform and high neutralizing activity across the entire pseudovirus panel (Figure 4). While all four consensus based immunogens elicited potent antibodies against strains from 2011–2020, only the consensus up-HA sera maintained high neutralization titres against the more recent 2021–2024 representative strains; the other three immunogen groups showed varying degrees of reduction in NT50 against these contemporary viruses (Figure 5(a)). Further analysis against recent evolutionary branches confirmed the superiority of the consensus up-HA design. Sera from this group neutralized viruses from the currently dominant 3c.2a1b.2a.2 clade and its sub-branches at higher neutralization level than sera from the other three immunogens (Figure 5(b)). Discussion

The continuous antigenic drift of seasonal influenza viruses necessitates the annual update of vaccine strains by the World Health Organization (WHO). Among the four subtypes, A/H3N2 evolves most rapidly, as evidenced by its highest frequency of vaccine strain updates – 14 changes between 2000 and 2022. Although selected based on epidemiological data to match circulating strains, the protective efficacy of these vaccines has varied over the years [37,38]. Since vaccine strains are representative of contemporary epidemics, our systematic analysis of their antigenic evolution provides critical insights for designing broad-spectrum immunogens.

Using the pseudovirus-based neutralization assay with guinea pig immunized sera, we analysed cross-neutralization levels among seasonal influenza H3N2 vaccine strains recommended in the northern hemisphere from 2000–2022. We found that pre-2016 vaccine strains often cross-neutralized those from adjacent years – with activity decreasing in a normal distribution as the temporal gap increased. However, this pattern did not hold for post-2015 strains (e.g. HK/19, KA/17, HK/14). The reduced cross-neutralization among certain post-2015 strains reflects their phylogenetic divergence, particularly the emergence of the 3C.3a clade representatives (KS/17 and SW/13) which are antigenically distinct from the predominant 3C.2a clade strains.

Antigenic cartography further revealed that the evolution of H3N2 vaccine strains is not strictly linear. Instead, strains form distinct antigenic clusters, with members often recommended in proximate years. A new cluster emerges when antigenic drift accumulates sufficiently or when key amino acid substitutions cause significant antigenic alterations. Cross-neutralization titres (NT50) between clusters typically differed by more than ten-fold, whereas within-cluster variations were only about five-fold. This antigenic divergence is closely linked to genetic evolution; we propose that when the HA1 identity falls below 93% (or full-length HA below 95%), significant cross-neutralization is unlikely.

Consistent with immune-driven selection, mutations were enriched in antigenic epitopes, particularly epitopes A and B [32]. We identified 80 mutations in the HA1 region, with 13 in epitope A, 23 in epitope B, 5 in C, 14 in D, and 5 in E; an additional 11 mutations occurred in the receptor-binding site (RBS), which partially overlaps with epitopes. Certain sites, such as 144 and 160, exhibited multiple amino acid variants (e.g. S, I, N, K at 144), highlighting the diversity driven by immune pressure. We also identified key residues where single substitutions significantly alter antigenicity. Most neutralization-reducing mutations showed cluster-specific patterns – e.g. 144K and 219Y were exclusive to cluster 2, while 144I, 189S, 189N, and 225G were found only in cluster 3. In contrast, mutations that enhanced neutralization sensitivity lacked such specificity, suggesting they may function through synergistic or antagonistic interactions with other sites [39]. In addition to amino acid substitutions, glycosylation of the HA head domain plays a significant role in modulating antigenic properties. Lei et al. demonstrated that H3N2 viruses underwent a surge of glycoprotein adaptation between 1997 and 2002, with the acquisition and loss of N-linked glycosylation sites contributing to immune evasion by shielding antigenic epitopes [40]. While certain positions (such as position 144) are known to be associated with N-linked glycosylation sites in other H3N2 strains, in the specific genetic background of our study (HK/19-based mutations), these identified mutations do not predominantly involve acquisition or loss of N-linked glycosylation sequons (N-X-S/T motifs). Therefore, the antigenic effects observed are to a large extent attributable to direct alterations of epitope structure rather than glycosylation-related changes.

It has been shown that computationally optimized broadly reactive antigen (COBRA) generated and expressed based on the HA sequences of a large number of circulating strains can provide broader immunoprotection against the constantly drifting seasonal influenza H1N1 and H3N2 [21]. Allen et al. employed the COBRA methodology, which generates consensus HA sequences derived from large numbers of cocirculating H3N2 isolates and delivered these antigens as virus-like particles (VLPs) in a preimmune ferret model. Their results demonstrated that COBRA H3 HA vaccines could elicit antibodies with neutralizing activity against H3N2 variants spanning approximately 20 years [41]. Our approach differs from the classic COBRA strategy by focusing on experimental characterization of 14 WHO-recommended vaccine strains rather than computational consensus from thousands of sequences. This strategy helps preserve the epitope structures important for clinical strains and validates the role of each key mutation through direct experimental testing. Combined with the results of the key mutation sites obtained, we designed four HA trimer immunogens based on the consensus HA sequences of 2000–2022 H3N2 vaccine strains. The immunization dose was determined to be 1μg, and four immunogens were mixed with BFA03 adjuvant and immunized in guinea pigs in a “prime-boost” immunization protocol. Neutralizing and broad-spectrum properties of the four immunizing sera were tested using a pseudovirus library representing the 2011–2024 H3N2 circulation. One immunogen, termed “consensus-up,” elicited sera with remarkably broad and uniform neutralization against the testing pseudovirus library. Crucially, unlike post-2015 vaccine strains which show limited efficacy [36], the consensus up sera also potently neutralized future circulating strains, including those from after 2022. This demonstrates the potential of our design strategy for eliciting broadly neutralizing antibody responses against both contemporary and future variants.

We acknowledge that HI assays represent the gold standard for influenza antigenic characterization, and our study relied exclusively on pseudovirus neutralization assays. Although we were unable to perform HI validation due to the unavailability of the required reference antigens, our previous comparative studies on seasonal influenza A/H1N1, A/H3N2 and avian influenza A/H7N9 demonstrated strong positive correlations between PN and HI assay results [29,31,42]. Additionally, validation using vaccine strains immunized guinea pig sera against HI antigen A/Hong Kong/2671/2019 (IVR-208) (NIBSC, UK) showed an excellent correlation between PN and HI assays (r = 0.953, P < 0.001, n = 30) (Supplementary Figure S7), further confirming the reliability of PN as a surrogate for HI measurements. Nevertheless, future studies should incorporate HI assays to further validate the broad-spectrum activity of the consensus up-HA immunogen when appropriate HI antigens become available.

TWO limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, in vivo challenge data are absent; while neutralizing antibody titres are a recognized correlate of protection for influenza vaccines, definitive evidence of protective efficacy requires animal challenge studies. Second, all immunogenicity data were generated using guinea pig sera, and validation in ferret or other models that more closely recapitulate human influenza immune responses would strengthen the generalizability of our findings.

In summary, our study reveals that the antigenic evolution of H3N2 vaccine strains is non-linear and cluster-based, driven by both gradual drift and key amino acid changes. By integrating these insights into the design of a consensus HA immunogen, we have developed a candidate that induces broad neutralization against H3N2 strains spanning 2011-2024, offering a promising strategy for a more broadly cross-reactive H3N2 influenza vaccine candidate.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and materials

MDCK (Madin-Darby Canine, Kidney, RRID: CVCL_0422) was obtained from the Japan Research Biological Resources Collection. And 293T (human, kidney, RRID: CVCL_0063) cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. Routine quality control confirmed cell identity, absence of inter-species contamination, freedom from exogenous viral agents, and compliance with sterility and mycoplasma standards [43]. Both cell lines were maintained under standard conditions: DMEM (GIBCO, Grand Island, NY, USA) fortified with 10% FBS (PAN-Biotech, Adenbach, Germany), 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin (GIBCO), and 20 mM HEPES (GIBCO), incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Recombinant protein expression was carried out in Expi293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A14527) grown in Expi293™ Expression Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A1435101) on an orbital shaker (120 rpm, 37°C, 10% CO2) per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Production of A/H3N2 wild-type and mutant pseudoviruses

Target HA sequences were retrieved from GISAID (accession IDs in Table 2 and Table S5), codon-optimized, and custom-synthesized by General Biological System (Anhui, China). Restriction sites for BamHI and XhoI were engineered at the 5’ and 3’ ends respectively, with a Kozak element (GCCACC) placed upstream of the start codon to support efficient translation. After enzymatic digestion and gel purification, inserts were ligated into pcDNA3.1(+) and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. To generate pseudoviral particles, a 1:2 plasmid ratio of HA construct to HIV backbone (pSG3.Δenv-FlucΔnef) was introduced into 293T cells via Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Six hours after transfection, the original medium was replaced with serum-reduced DMEM (1% FBS) supplemented with 5 MU/mL Clostridium neuraminidase (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to promote surface release of HA-bearing pseudoviruses. Supernatants were collected at 48 h post-transfection and clarified through 0.45 µm mixed cellulose ester filters (Millipore, Boston, MA, USA). HA cleavage was performed by incubating pseudoviruses with 20 µg/mL TPCK-trypsin (Aladdin, Shanghai, China) at 37°C under 5% CO2 for 30 min. Pseudovirus aliquots were snap-frozen and held at −80°C. Titration of A/H3N2 pseudoviruses

Infectious titre was established using MDCK cells as target cells. Pseudovirus stocks were serially diluted three-fold across a 96-well plate (100 µL per well; the terminal 50 µL discarded), then left to adsorb for 1 h at 37°C with 5% CO2 before 3 × 104 MDCK cells were seeded per well. Following a further 48-hour incubation, luminescence was quantified with the Britelite Plus reporter system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). TCID50 was derived according to the Reed–Muench endpoint dilution formula as described [44].

Neutralization assay

Working dilutions of each test serum were prepared and then further diluted three-fold in series. All pseudovirus-based neutralization (PN) experiments used a fixed input of 104 TCID50 per well, which corresponded to approximately 107 relative light units (RLU) in virus-only wells. After a 1-hour preincubation step at 37°C to allow antibody–virus complex formation, 100 µL of each mixture was transferred onto 96-well plates carrying 3 × 104 MDCK cells. Plates were returned to 37°C in 5% CO2 for 48 h, after which luciferase-driven chemiluminescence was measured using the Britelite Plus system (PerkinElmer). Neutralization titres were derived from dose–response curves fitted by the Reed–Muench method in PerkinElmer Ensight software. Each serum sample was assayed across 3–5 independent replicates.

Immunized sera of Guinea pigs

Animal procedures were authorized by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of NIFDC, Beijing (Approval No.: 2023(B)053), and conducted in accordance with institutional welfare standards.

Vaccine HA plasmids immunization: Fourteen groups of female guinea pigs (200–220 g, n = 5 per group) were each assigned a single pcDNA3.1-based HA construct from the 14 WHO-recommended vaccine strains. Plasmid solution (1 mg/mL, 200 µL per animal) was delivered by intramuscular injection into the quadriceps femoris, with immediate in vivo electroporation at the same site (BBFG001 electroporator, Shanghai Teresa Bio-Tech Co., Ltd.; Mode II). A three-injection schedule with 14-day intervals was used. Terminal serum collection was performed on day 14 after the final dose.

Dose optimization for HA trimer proteins: To establish the minimally effective immunizing dose, three cohorts (n = 5) received the consensus-HA trimer at 15, 5, or 1 µg per animal, each formulated with BFA03 adjuvant (Recbio Technology, Jiangsu, China) and delivered intramuscularly. Following a prime-boost schedule (interval: two weeks), post-boost sera were collected at day 14 for antibody titre determination.

Immunization with consensus HA trimer immunogens and single vaccine strain: For the evaluation of the four designed consensus immunogens (consensus-HA, consensus up-HA, consensus down-HA, and consensus up&down-HA), guinea pigs (n = 5 per group) were immunized following the established protocol. Each animal received a 1 μg dose of the respective HA trimer protein mixed with an equal volume of BFA03 adjuvant (Recbio Technology, Jiangsu, China) at a final immunization volume of 100 μL via intramuscular injection. Immunizations were performed twice at a two-week interval. Serum samples were collected two weeks after the booster immunization. Additionally, as a reference control, sera from guinea pigs (n = 5) previously immunized with A/Hong Kong/2671/2019 vaccine stock (Hualan Biological Engineering, Inc., Henan Province, China), using the same immunization protocol and adjuvant dose, were employed for comparison.

Stored aliquots (−80°C) were thawed immediately before use, and all sera were heat-inactivated at 56°C for 30 min prior to neutralization testing.

Haemagglutination-inhibition (HI) assay

For method validation, HI assays were performed using WHO-recommended reference standards obtained from the National Institute for Biological Standards and Control (NIBSC, UK). The influenza antigen A/Hong Kong/2671/2019 (IVR-208) (NIBSC code 19/310) was used as the H3N2 reference strain, which is calibrated to contain defined haemagglutination activity for quantitative antibody measurement.

Guinea pig sera were pre-treated with receptor-destroying enzyme (RDE; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at a ratio of 1:4 (serum:RDE) and incubated overnight at 37°C, followed by heat inactivation at 56°C for 30 min. To remove non-specific inhibitors, treated sera were then absorbed with chicken red blood cells (cRBC) overnight at 4°C. HI assays were conducted in 96-well V-bottom plates using standard WHO protocols. Briefly, sera were serially diluted 2-fold in phosphate-buffered saline, and 25 μL of standardized H3N2 antigen containing 4 haemagglutination units was added to each well. After 1-hour incubation at room temperature, 50 μL of 0.5% cRBC suspension was added, and plates were incubated for an additional hour at room temperature. HI titres were recorded as the reciprocal of the highest serum dilution that completely inhibited haemagglutination.

Construction of the phylogenetic tree

The HA sequences of the 14 vaccine strains were subjected to maximum likelihood phylogenetic reconstruction using MEGA software.

Processing of the serum neutralization data

Neutralization titres were processed and visualized in GraphPad Prism 9.2.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Antigenic clustering of strains was carried out by applying hierarchical cluster analysis to the full serum–virus neutralization matrix.

Statistical analysis

In the vaccine-elicited sera neutralization assay, differences between groups were analysed using one-way and two-way ANOVA. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A P value below 0.05 was taken as the threshold for statistical significance (ns: P ≥ 0.05; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.005; ****P < 0.001).

Structural visualization

The three-dimensional structure of the A/H3N2 HA protein (VI/11 vaccine strain) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 4WE8). Structural visualization and analysis were performed using PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (Version 3.1.5.1, Schrödinger, LLC). The reduced and enhanced groups were mapped onto the structure and represented as spheres and solid surfaces coloured in blue and red, respectively. The receptor-binding site was highlighted in yellow, while classical antigenic epitopes A-E were displayed as semi-transparent sticks in distinct colours. All structural figures were exported as high-resolution images (300 DPI) and final composition was prepared using Adobe Illustrator.

Construction of HA trimer protein expression vector

The amino acid sequences of the HA extracellular domains from the 14 vaccine strains were retrieved from NCBI. A C-terminal T4 fibritin foldon trimerization motif and an 8×His tag were appended, and the sequences were codon-optimized, synthesized, and subcloned into the pcDNA3.1 expression vector.

Consensus HA trimer protein synthesis and purification

Expi293F cells (Gibco, A14527) were cultured at 37°C, 120 rpm, 80% relative humidity, and 8% CO2. At a viable cell density of 4.5–5.5 × 106 cells/mL with ≥95% viability, cells were transfected using plasmid DNA (2 μg/106 cells) complexed with PEI Max 40 K (1 mg/mL; Polysciences) at a PEI:DNA ratio of 3:1. PEI–DNA complexes were formed by incubation at room temperature and then added to the culture flasks with gentle shaking. Cells were cultured for 5–7 days post-transfection, with glucose supplemented as needed.

Purification was performed using the Cytiva Avant chromatography system and HisTrap affinity chromatography column, equilibrated with buffer A (10 mM PBS+0.5 M NaCl, pH 7.4). Cell culture supernatant was centrifuged and loaded onto the column, and proteins were eluted with a linear gradient of buffer B (buffer A + 0.5 M imidazole). Elution peaks were collected and desalted using a HiPrep Desalting column, followed by concentration with a 10 kDa molecular weight cut-off ultrafiltration tube.

Concentrations of the purified soluble HA proteins were measured by the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Cat. No. PC0020, Solarbio). The recombinant proteins were analysedby SDS-PAGE under reducing conditions using Precision Plus Protein™ Standards (15–250 kDa, Bio-Rad) as molecular weight markers, with gels stained with Coomassie Blue, confirming protein expression and molecular weight. The identity of the purified proteins was confirmed by Western blot analysis. Briefly, proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE with PageRuler™ Prestained Protein Ladder (10–250 kDa, Thermo Scientific) as molecular weight markers, transferred onto PVDF membranes, and probed with HRP-conjugated mouse anti-His tag monoclonal antibody (Cat. No. 535, ACROBiosystems) at a dilution of 1:5000. Bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence detection system (Cat. No. 1705061, Bio-Rad).

SEC-MALS analysis

Protein samples were analysed by size-exclusion chromatography coupled with multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS) to determine molar mass and oligomeric state. Samples (100 μL, ∼1 mg/mL) were loaded onto a Waters XBridge Premier Protein SEC column (250 Å, 7.8 × 300 mm; Waters Corporation) equilibrated with 20 mM phosphate buffer containing 300 mM NaCl (pH 7.4). The system consisted of an Agilent 1260 Infinity II HPLC instrument (Agilent Technologies) coupled to a Wyatt Technology DAWN HELEOS II eighteen-angle light scattering detector (Model WH2-13, Wyatt Technology; λ = 660.5 nm) and a Wyatt Technology Optilab T-rEX differential refractive index detector (Model WTRX-13, Wyatt Technology). Separation was performed at 0.3 mL/min with UV detection at 280 nm and a total run time of 60 min. Data were collected and analysed using WYATT ASTRA 7 software (Wyatt Technology) with dn/dc set to 0.185 mL/g and UV extinction coefficient set to 1.12 mL/(mg·cm). Molar mass (Mw), polydispersity (Mw/Mn), and root-mean-square radius were determined for each resolved peak using a Zimm fitting model.
