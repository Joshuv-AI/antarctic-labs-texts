Stabilization of fields of meromorphic functions on neighborhoods of a rational curve Thanks: This study was partially supported by the HSE University Basic Research Program and by the project FNEF-2024-0001 (Reg. no. 1023032100070-3-1.2.1)
Serge Lvovski

Abstract
. Suppose that F F is a smooth and connected complex surface (not necessarily compact) containing a smooth rational curve C C with positive self-intersection. We prove that there exists a neighborhood U ⊃ C U\supset C such that any meromorphic function defined on a connected neighborhood of C C in U U can be extended to a meromorphic function on the entire U U .

1.
Introduction

Suppose that
F
is a smooth and connected complex surface containing
a curve
C\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
with positive self-intersection (
\mathbb{P}^{1}
is the
Riemann sphere). It was shown in
[
5
]
that the field of
meromorphic functions
\mathcal{M}(F)
is isomorphic to either
\mathbb{C}
, or the
field
\mathbb{C}(X)
of rational functions in one variable, or the
field
\mathbb{C}(X,Y)
of rational functions. If
U\supset V\supset C
are
connected neighborhoods of
C
, then
\mathcal{M}(U)
is naturally embedded in

\mathcal{M}(V)
.

In this paper we prove that the fields
\mathcal{M}(U)
stabilize as
the connected neighborhoods
U\supset C
narrow down.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
F
is a smooth and connected complex surface containing
a curve
C\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
with positive self-intersection. Then
there exists a connected neighborhood
U\supset C

such that for any connected neighborhood
V\supset C
,
V\subset U
,
the embedding of fields
\mathcal{M}(U)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}(V)
induced by the
inclusion
V\subset U
is an isomorphism.

In other words, there exists a connected neighborhood
U\supset C

such that, for any connected neighborhood
V\supset C
,
V\subset U
,
any meromorphic function on
V
can be extended to a meromorphic
function on
U
.

Notation 1.2
.

If
F
is a smooth and connected complex surface containing
a curve
C\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
with positive self-intersection, then

\tau(F,C)=\max_{U\supset C}\trdeg_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{M}(U),

where maximum is taken over all connected neighborhoods
U\supset C
.

It is clear that
\tau(F,C)\leq 2
and that
\tau(U,C)\leq\tau(V,C)
if

U\supset V\supset C
.

Of course, Theorem
1.1
is trivial if
\tau(F,C)=0
, for in
this case all the fields involved consist entirely of constants. The
overall idea of the proof of Theorem
1.1
is the same for both
nontrivial cases
\tau(F,C)=1
and
\tau(F,C)=2
, but
technicalities differ significantly.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we
prove, for further reference,
a folklore lemma that will be used in the proof for both cases. In
Section
3
we prove Theorem
1.1
in the
case
\tau(F,C)=1
. In Section
4
we recall some known
general properties of meromorphic mappings. In Section
5
we
prove an auxiliary result on birational isomorphisms of rational
surfaces; the content of this section belongs entirely to algebraic
geometry. Finally, in Section
6
we treat the case

\tau(F,C)=2
, thus completing the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

1.1.
Notation and conventions

All topological terms refer to the classical topology unless specified
otherwise.

If
F
is a smooth and connected complex manifold, then
\mathcal{M}(F)
denotes
the field of meromorphic functions on
F
.

By a
curve
on a complex manifold we will mean its closed
one-dimensional irreducible (and reduced) complex subspace.

If
C
is a
compact
curve on a complex manifold
F
,

U_{1},U_{2}\supset C
are its neighborhoods, and if
B_{1}\subset U_{1}
,

B_{2}\subset U_{2}
are two curves meeting
C
, we will say that

B_{1}
and
B_{2}
have the same germ along
C
if there exists a
neighborhood
V\supset C
,
V\subset U_{1}\cap U_{2}
, such that
B_{1}\cap V=B_{2}\cap V
.

The
n
-dimensional complex projective space is denoted by
\mathbb{P}^{n}
; in
particular,
\mathbb{P}^{1}
is the Riemann sphere.

If
P\in\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
is a polynomial in
n
variables, then

V(P)\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is its zero locus.

In diagrams, solid arrows refer to holomorphic mappings
and dashed arrows refer to meromorphic mappings
(see Section
4
).

In arguments where the only complex spaces involved are complex
projectve varieties, the terms ‘meromorphic mapping’ and ‘rational
mapping’ will be used interchangeably, and the same applies to the
pair ‘meromorphic function/rational function’.

If a field
K
contains
\mathbb{C}
as a subfield, then by generators of
K
we
will always mean generators over
\mathbb{C}
.

2.
A simple lemma

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
F
is a smooth and connected analytic surface containing
a curve
C\subset F
,
C\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
, with positive
self-intersection. If
f
is a non-constant meromorphic function
on
F
, then its divisor of zeroes
(
as well as its divisor of
poles
)
has non-empty intersection with
C
.

Proof.

Observe first that a holomorphic function on any connected
neighborhood of
C
must be constant. Indeed, if
U\supset C
is such
a neighborhood and
f\colon U\to\mathbb{C}
is holomorphic, then it suffices
to show that
f
is constant on
V\subset U
, where
V
is a good
neighborhood of
C
in the sense of
[
5
, Definition 4.1]
. The
good neighborhood
V
is swept by curves isomorphic to
C
, so
f
is
contant on each of these curves, and any two such curves have
non-empty intersetion, so all these constants are the same.

Assume now that the divisor of zeroes of
f
does not
intersect
C
; then there exists a connected neighborhood
W\supset C
,
W\subset F
, such that
f
has no zeroes on
W
. Then the
function
1/f
is holomorphic on
W
, hence it is constant
on
W
, hence
1/f
is constant on
F
, hence
f
is
constant on
F
, a contradiction.

The assertion about poles of
f
is obtained by substituting

1/f
for
f
in the argument above.
∎

3.
The
\tau(F,C)=1
case

In this section we account for the case
\tau(F,C)=1
in
Theorem
1.1
. To that end it suffices to proof the following.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that
F
is a smooth and connected complex surface containing
a curve
C\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
with positive self-intersection and that, for
any connected neighborhood
U\supset C
,
\trdeg_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{M}(U)=1
. Then there
exists a connected neigborhood
U\supset C
such that for any
open and connected
V\supset C
,
V\subset U
, the inclusion

V\hookrightarrow U
induces an isomorphism of fields of meromorphic
functions.

Observe that if the hypotheses of Proposition
3.1
are
satisfied, then, according to
[
5
]
, for any open and connected

U\supset C
the field
\mathcal{M}(U)
is isomorphic to the field
\mathbb{C}(T)
of
rational functions in one variable, so
\mathcal{M}(U)
is generated by one
element.

Further on, we will say that a meromorphic function
f
on a
surface
F
is constant on a curve
C\subset F
if either
f
is
constant or there exists an
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
such that the
function
f-\alpha
has a zero along
C
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose that
F
is a smooth and connected complex surface containing
a curve
C\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
with positive self-intersection and that

\trdeg_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{M}(F)=1
. Then the following assertions are equivalent.

(1)

For any meromorphic function
f
that is a generator of

\mathcal{M}(F)
,
f
is
either constant on
C
or has a pole along
C
.

(2)

There exists a generator
f
of
\mathcal{M}(F)
such that
f

has a zero along
C
.

(3)

There exists an
h\in\mathcal{M}(F)
such that
h
has a
pole along
C
.

(4)

There exists a non-constant
h\in\mathcal{M}(F)
such that
h
is
constant on
C
.

Proof.

The implications
\eqref{b1}\Rightarrow\eqref{b2}\Rightarrow\eqref{b3}\Rightarrow\eqref{b4}
are obvious. To prove the
implication
\eqref{b4}\Rightarrow\eqref{b1}
, suppose that the
restriction to
C
of a meromorphic function
h
is identically equal
to
\alpha
. Then
h-\alpha=R(f)
, where
f
is a generator of
the field
\mathcal{M}(F)
and
R
is a non-constant rational function. Thus,

(1)

h-\alpha=\frac{(f-a_{1})\cdot\dots\cdot(f-a_{m})}{Q(f)},

where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
are complex numbers and
Q\in\mathbb{C}[X]
is a
polynomial such that none of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
is its root. (The product in the
numerator in the right-hand side of (
1
) may be empty, in which
case the numerator is meant to be identically
1
.) Since the
left-hand side of (
1
) is identically zero on
C
, the
function
f
is identically equal to
a_{j}
on
C
, for some
j
.

If the numerator in (
1
) is identically
1
, then, obviously,

\alpha=0
and
f
has a pole along
C

∎

Definition 3.3
.

If the equivalent assertions of
Proposition
3.2
are satisfied for a surface
F
and a
curve
C\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
contained in
F
, we will say that the
pair
(F,C)
is
of the first kind
. Otherwise, we will say
that
(F,C)
is
of the second kind
.

Proposition 3.4
.

If, for any neighborhood
U\subset C
, the pair
(U,C)
is of the
second kind in the sense of
Definition
3.3
, and if
\tau(F,C)=1
, then
Proposition
3.1
holds for
(F,C)
.

Proof.

Let
K
stand for the field of meromorphic functions on
C\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
;

K
is isomorphic to the field of rational functions in one variable
over
\mathbb{C}
. Since, by the hypothesis, for any neighborhood
U\supset C
, the pair
(U,C)
is
of the second kind, for any meromorphic function
f\in\mathcal{M}(U)
the
restriction
f|_{C}
is well defined; this restriction induces the
embedding
\mathcal{M}(U)\hookrightarrow K
. Thus, for any connected
neighborhoof
U\supset C
one has

\mathcal{M}(F)\subset\mathcal{M}(U)\subset K.

Since both
K
and
\mathcal{M}(F)
are finitely generated extensions of
\mathbb{C}
, of
transcendence degree
1
, one has
[K:\mathcal{M}(F)]<\infty
, so there exists
a neighborhood
U\supset C
for which
[\mathcal{M}(U):\mathcal{M}(F)]
is maximal. It
is clear that, once
U\supset V\supset C
, where
V
is open and
connected, one has
\mathcal{M}(V)=\mathcal{M}(U)
, so the neighborhood
U
is the
neighborhood the existence of which we are to prove.
∎

In view of what we have just proved, to finish the proof of
Proposition
3.1
it remains to account for the case in
which there is a connected neighborhood
U\supset C
such that the
pair
(U,C)
is of the first kind; this is what we will assume from
now on. It is clear from Proposition
3.2
that in this case
the pair
(V,C)
is also of the first kind for any open and
connected
V
such that
U\supset V\supset C
.

End of the proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Arguing by contradiction, suppose that there is an infinite
sequence of nested connected neighborhoods

U_{0}\supset U_{1}\supset U_{2}\supset\dots\supset U_{n}\supset\dots\supset C

such that
\mathcal{M}(U_{j})\subsetneqq\mathcal{M}(U_{j+1})
for each
j
and all
the
U_{j}
are of the first kind. For each
j
, choose once and for
all a meromorphic function
f_{j}
on
U_{j}
such that
f_{j}
has a zero
along
C
and is a generator of the field
\mathcal{M}(U_{j})
; this is possible
in view of Proposition
3.2
and our assumption.

Each
embedding of fields
\mathcal{M}(U_{j})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}(U_{j+1})
corresponds
to a holomorphic mapping of algebraic curves

\pi_{j}\colon C_{j+1}\to C_{j}
, where
C_{j}\cong C_{j+1}\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}

and
\deg\pi_{j}=[\mathcal{M}(U_{j+1}):\mathcal{M}(U_{j})]>1
. This mapping is defined as follows:
if

f_{j}|_{U_{j+1}}=R(f_{j+1})
, where
R
is a rational function in one
variable, then the mapping
\pi_{j}
is defined by the formula
z\mapsto R(z)
. (I would like to stress that
the curves
C_{j}
are
not
embedded in
F
.)

The choice of
f_{j}
in each
\mathcal{M}(U_{j})
allows one to identify
each
C_{j}
with the Riemann sphere
\mathbb{P}^{1}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
: each
point
x\in C_{j}
is identified with
T_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
, where
T_{j}
is
the generator of the field
\mathbb{C}(T_{j})\cong\mathcal{M}(C_{j})
that corresponds
to
f_{j}\in\mathcal{M}(U_{j})
.

For each
j\geq 0
, put
\deg\pi_{j}=\delta_{j}>1
and

\varphi_{j}=\pi_{0}\circ\dots\circ\pi_{j}
.
Since the field
\mathbb{C}
is uncountable, there exists a point
a\in C_{0}
,

a\neq\infty
, such that, for any
j\geqslant 0
, any point in

(\pi_{0}\circ\dots\circ\pi_{j})^{-1}(a)\subset C_{j+1}
is not a
ramification point of the mapping
\varphi_{j}\colon C_{j+1}\to C_{0}
and is
different from
\infty\in C_{j}
.

Put
m_{j}=\delta_{0}\delta_{1}\dots\delta_{j}
. By our choice of the
point
a
, one has
\card(\varphi_{j})^{-1}(a)=m_{j}
. If

(\varphi_{j})^{-1}(a)=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{m_{j}}\}
, then the image of the element

f_{0}-a
in
\mathcal{M}(C_{j+1})=\mathbb{C}(T_{j+1})
has simple zeroes at the points

b_{1},\ldots,b_{m_{j}}
and only at these points. Recalling that

\mathcal{M}(C_{i})\cong\mathcal{M}(U_{i})
for each
i
and the embeddings

\mathcal{M}(C_{i})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}(C_{i+1})
are induced by the restriction of
meromorphic functions from
U_{i}
to
U_{i+1}
, we conclude that on

U_{j+1}
the identity

(2)

f_{0}-a=\frac{(f_{j+1}-b_{1})\cdot\dots\cdot(f_{j+1}-b_{m_{j}})}{Q(f_{j+1})},

holds, where
Q\in\mathbb{C}[X]
is a polynomial such that none of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{m_{j}}
is
its root.

It follows from (
2
) that for each natural
k\leq m_{j}
, the
divisor of zeroes of the function
f_{0}|_{U_{i+1}}-a
contains the
divisor of zeroes of the function
f_{j+1}-b_{k}
. All these divisors
are different, and
Lemma
2.1

implies that each of them has non-empty
intersection with
C
.

Hence, the divisor of zeroes of the meromorphic function
f_{0}-a

contains at least
m_{j}
different components, and each of these
components has non-empty intersection with
C
. Since
C
is compact,
the number of these components must be finte, but

m_{j}
tends to infinity as
j
tends to
infinity. This contradiction completes the proof.
∎

4.
Generalities on rational and meromorphic
mappings

This section does not contain new results. We just state some
well-known facts about the ramification of meromorphic mappings, in the
form that suits our purposes.

For the definition of meromorphic functions and meromorphic mappings
we refer the reader to
[
1
, Section 3]
.

If
X
is a smooth complex manifold and
f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}
are meromorphic
functions on
X
, then, if not all these functions are identically
zero, the formula
x\mapsto(f_{0}(x):\dots:f_{n}(x))
defines a
meromorphic mapping from
X
to
\mathbb{P}^{n}
.

If
X
is a complex manifold,
Y
is a smooth projective variety, and

\varphi\colon X\rightarrow Y
is a meromorphic mapping, then the
indeterminacy locus (in
[
1
]
, indeterminacy locus is
called just ‘indeterminacy’) of
\varphi
is a complex subvariety
I\subset X
of
codimension at least
2
; the meromorphic mapping
\varphi
induces a genuine
holomorphic mapping from
X\setminus I
to
Y
.

If
X
is a projective variety, then meromorphic functions on
X
are
the same as rational functions on
X
in the sense of algebraic
geometry; if
X
and
Y
are non-singular projective varieties, then
meromorphic mappings from
X
to
Y
are the same as rational mappings
from
X
to
Y
.

Suppose that
X
and
Y
are projective varieties and
\varphi\colon X\dasharrow Y
is a rational, aka meromorphic, mapping from
X
to

Y
; the mapping
\varphi
is called
dominant
if
\varphi(X\setminus I)
, where

I\subset X
is the indeterminancy locus of
\varphi
,
is dense in
Y
in the classical topology (or, which is equivalent,
in the Zariski topology). There is a natural bijection between the set
of dominant rational mappings
\varphi\colon X\dasharrow Y
and the set of
embeddings of fields
\iota=\varphi^{*}\colon\mathcal{M}(Y)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}(X)
as
extensions of
\mathbb{C}
.

If
\varphi\colon X\dasharrow Y
is a dominant meromorphic mapping of
projective varieties of equal dimension, with indeterminacy
locus
I\subset X
, then its
degree

\deg\varphi
is the
cardianlity of a general fiber of the mapping
\varphi|_{X\setminus I}\colon X\setminus I\to Y
. Degree of
\varphi
equals the degree of
the field extension
\mathcal{M}(X)\supset\varphi^{*}\mathcal{M}(Y)
.

From this point and to the end of this section, we will be speaking
solely about complex sufraces, even though much of what follows can be
restated for complex varieties of dimension greater than
2
.

If
X
is a smooth and connected complex surface,
Y
is a projective
variety, and
\varphi\colon X\dasharrow Y
is a meromorphic mapping, then
the indeterminacy locus of
\varphi
is a discrete subset
I\subset X
. If

E\subset X
is a curve (i.e., a one-dimensional irreducible complex
subspace), then by
\varphi(E)
we will mean
\varphi(E\setminus I)
, where
I

is the indeterminacy locus of
\varphi
.

The section of the line bundle
\omega_{X\setminus I}^{-1}\otimes\varphi^{*}\omega_{Y}
which, in local coordinates, is the Jacobian determinant
of
\varphi
, will be denoted by
\Jac(\varphi)
. If
\Jac(\varphi)
is not
identically zero but has zeroes, then the closure of its zero locus is
an analytic subspace in
X
by virtue of the Remmert–Stein theorem
(see for example
[
2
, Section 3]
) and all the irreducible
components of this closure are curves in
X
.

Definition 4.1
.

Suppose that
X
is a smooth and connected complex surface,
Y

is a projective surface, and
\varphi\colon X\dasharrow Y
is a
meromorphic mapping such that
\Jac(\varphi)
is not identically zero.

If
D\subset X
is a curve such that
\varphi(D)
is not a point,
then the
ramification
index
of
\varphi
along
D
is the number
1+\ord_{D}\Jac(\varphi)
.

The ramification index of
\varphi
along
D
will be denoted
by
e(\varphi,D)
.

The following proposition is very well known in the context of
algebraic surfaces.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose that
X
is a smooth complex surface,
Y
and
Z
are
smooth projective surfaces, and
\varphi\colon X\dasharrow Y
and

\psi\colon Y\dasharrow Z
are meromorphic mappings such that neither

\Jac(\varphi)
nor
\Jac(\psi)
is identically zero.

If
D_{1}\subset X
a curve such
that
\varphi(D_{1})
is not a point,
\varphi(D_{1})\subset D_{2}
, where

D_{2}\subset Y
is an irreducible curve, and
\psi(D_{2})\subset Z
is
not a point either, then

e(\psi\circ\varphi,D_{1})=e(\psi,D_{2})\cdot e(\varphi,D_{1}).

Proof.

We begin with a lemma the straightforward proof of which is left to
the reader.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that
X=\{(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\colon|t_{1}|,|t_{2}|<\varepsilon\}
is a
bidisk; put
D=\{(0,t)\}\subset X
. If
\varphi\colon X\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
is a
holomorphic mapping defined by the formula

(z,w)\mapsto(t_{1}^{k}\varphi_{1}(t_{1},t_{2}),\varphi_{2}(t_{1},t_{2})),

where
k>0
is an integer, the function
t\mapsto\varphi_{1}(0,t)
is not
identically zero, and the function
t\mapsto\varphi_{2}(0,t)
is not
constant, then
e(\varphi,D)=k
and
\Jac(\varphi)=t_{1}^{k-1}F(t_{1},t_{2})
,
where
F(0,t)
is not identically zero.
(
Here, by
\Jac
we
mean the ordinary Jacobian determinant.
)

Now put
e(\varphi,D_{1})=e_{1}
,
e(\varphi,D_{2})=e_{2}
.
Since, in local coordinates,
\Jac(\psi\circ\varphi)=\Jac(\varphi)\cdot(\Jac(\psi)\circ\varphi)
, we have, in view of the lemma,

e(\psi\circ\varphi,D_{1})=1+\ord_{D_{1}}\Jac(\varphi)+\ord_{D_{1}}(\Jac(\psi)\circ\varphi)\\
{}=1+e_{1}-1+e_{1}(e_{2}-1)=e_{1}e_{2},

as required.
∎

Definition 4.4
.

Suppose that
X
is a smooth complex surface,
Y
is a
smooth projective surface, and
\varphi\colon X\dasharrow Y
is a
meromorphic mapping such that
\Jac(\varphi)
is not identically zero. We
will say that
\varphi
is
strongly ramified
along an irreducible
curve
D\subset X
if
\varphi(D)
is not a
point and
e(\varphi,D)>1
.

Remark 4.5
.

According to standard definitions, the ramification locus of
\varphi
is
(the closure of) the zero locus of
\Jac(\varphi)
. The aim of
Definition
4.4
is to rule out the components of the
ramification locus that are mapped to points.

5.
An auxiliary result from algebraic geometry

The results of this section are valid, with their proofs, over any
algebraically closed field, of arbitrary characteristic.

First we recall definitions and establish some notation concrning
quadratic transformations. Folowing
[
4
]
, we will say that
the
standard quadratic transformation

T_{\mathrm{st}}\colon\mathbb{P}^{2}\dasharrow\mathbb{P}^{2}
is the birational automorphism of

\mathbb{P}^{2}
defined by the formula

(x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2})\mapsto(x_{1}x_{2}:x_{0}x_{2}:x_{0}x_{1}).

If
(a,b,c)
is any triple of non-collinear points of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, then by

T_{abc}
we will denote the birational automorphism of
\mathbb{P}^{2}

defined by the formula

(3)

T_{abc}=A_{abc}^{-1}\circ T_{\mathrm{st}}\circ A_{abc},

where
A\colon\mathbb{P}^{2}\to\mathbb{P}^{2}
is a linear automorphism of

\mathbb{P}^{2}
mapping the points
a
,
b
, and
c
to
(1:0:0)
,
(0:1:0)
,
and
(0:0:1)
respectively.

In the paragraph above we said “
a
linear automorphism” since
the authomorphism
A_{abc}
is not unique. Thus, the notation

T_{abc}
does not refer to a sole birational authomorphism: any two
such birational authomorphisms differ by a linear authomorphism of

\mathbb{P}^{2}
fixing the points
a
,
b
, and
