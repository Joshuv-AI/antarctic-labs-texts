What the blood knows: predicting atrial fibrillation risk in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy patients
Authors: Gilbert Jabbour, Rafik Tadros, Carol Ann Remme
Published: 2024

This editorial refers to ‘Prediction of new-onset atrial fibrillation in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy using plasma proteomics profiling’, by H.S. Lumish et al., https://doi.org/10.1093/europace/euae267.

Besides its well-known ventricular phenotype, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is also associated with an increased risk for atrial fibrillation (AF) with a prevalence of up to 24%.1 In HCM patients with AF, the risk of thromboembolic events is high, with a prevalence of 27% and an annual incidence of 3%.2 Given the established benefit of anticoagulation in stroke prevention and the increased morbidity and even mortality attributable to AF in HCM patients,3 the prediction and prevention of AF and its complications are paramount. Current practice guidelines recommend initiating oral anticoagulation upon AF diagnosis regardless of thromboembolic risk scores (e.g. CHA2DS2-VA) and advocate for systematic AF screening at HCM diagnosis and regularly thereafter using ambulatory ECG monitoring.4,5 As in the general population, the risk for AF in HCM patients is increased with older age in addition to modifiable factors such as obesity, obstructive sleep apnoea, diabetes, and hypertension.6 However, AF risk prediction tools specifically tailored for HCM patients remain scarce (Figure 1).

Current and potential AF-risk prediction modalities in patients with HCM. Current clinical risk score consists of HCM-AF.7 Explored polygenic scores are PGS-AF and PGS-HCM in sarcomeric variant carriers.8 Proteomics-based prediction is first reported by Lumisch et al. in the current issue of EP Europace.9 Electrocardiogram-based deep learning (ECG-AI) and radiomics (e.g. multimodality imaging biomarkers) are potential AF prediction avenues in HCM patients which are yet to be explored.

Multiple modalities have been explored for AF risk prediction in general or cardiovascular population-based cohorts. Prediction has traditionally relied on clinical risk scores such as CHARGE-AF.10 Recently, novel approaches like polygenic risk scores (PGS) and electrocardiogram-based artificial intelligence (ECG-AI) have emerged as promising tools for predicting incident AF.11 A recent study demonstrated that ECG-AI offers superior predictive performance for incident AF compared with both clinical and polygenic scores in a cardiac centre's patient population.12 Another study developed a risk score based on 47 plasma proteins—including N-terminal prohormone of brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP)—which showed superior predictive performance for AF risk compared with traditional clinical and polygenic risk models in the population-based UK Biobank cohort.13 Risk models based on NT-proBNP were also shown to improve AF prediction over clinical scores in another population-based cohort.14

The application of these incident AF prediction tools in HCM patients has been thus far limited. The HCM-AF score7 is a simple and reliable clinical risk score for AF prediction in HCM patients but has a relatively low specificity and sensitivity. In a prospective, multi-centre cohort study published recently in EP Europace, Lumisch and colleagues employed for the first time plasma proteomics to predict new-onset AF in HCM patients.9 They utilized a proteomics-based machine learning (ML) model to identify a set of 12 plasma proteins from a cohort of 193 HCM patients (the training set). This model successfully predicted new-onset AF with an area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.89 (with a wide 95% CI: 0.78–0.99) when tested on an independent cohort of 91 HCM patients from a different centre (the test set). The proteomics-based model outperformed the clinical HCM-AF score in predicting new-onset AF during follow-up. In a time-to-event analysis, proteomics-model inference of high risk identified 27% of the test set population with a higher rate of developing incident AF (log-rank P = 0.002). The study therefore provides a first demonstration of how molecular parameters may be successfully employed to enhance risk prediction of new-onset AF in the setting of HCM.

The approach employed by Lumisch and colleagues exhibits several key strengths. First, developing a risk model based on a prospective cohort allows for survival analyses and minimizes AF classification bias. This approach is advantageous over retrospective cohorts, where distinguishing between the detection of pre-existing paroxysmal AF and the prediction of truly incident AF is more challenging. Second, the inclusion of an external validation cohort enhances the generalizability of the findings, demonstrating that the proteomics-based model performs consistently in a different HCM cohort. Third, the authors underscore the superior predictive performance of their model by comparing it against the best available clinical model—the HCM-AF score—highlighting its potential clinical utility. Fourth, they conducted several analyses to enhance the explainability and biological plausibility of their risk score, which is particularly important in ML-based models. The use of a Random Forest algorithm provided insights into the relative importance of the proteins included in the model for predicting new-onset AF in patients with HCM. Fifth, in contrast to prediction models based on ECG parameters or other clinical markers, the proteomics-based approach has potential additional benefit in terms of providing novel insight into underlying mechanisms and pathways. The top discriminant proteins have potentially relevant functions, such as the regulation of sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ homeostasis, which make them plausible contributors to the pathophysiology of AF.15 Several of these proteins serve as upstream regulators of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway; accordingly, pathway analysis in the study suggested that dysregulation of the Ras-MAPK pathway is associated with new-onset AF. Additionally, the significant associations between the model's probability outputs and established AF risk factors—such as body mass index, the HCM-AF score, NT-proBNP, and left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) gradient at rest (an indicator of HCM severity)—support the biological plausibility of the ML-based model. Furthermore, adding clinical risk factors from the HCM-AF score into the risk model did not significantly improve prediction performance. This suggests that the selected proteins likely capture information already provided by traditional risk factors. Finally, the authors plan to make their ML model publicly available, which will facilitate broader external validation and model improvement. This openness is crucial for accelerating clinical implementation, as it allows the wider scientific and medical community to test and refine the model.

The study has some limitations that warrant consideration. First, although the authors compared their proteomics-based model with the HCM-AF score, they did not compare it with common circulating biomarkers such as NT-proBNP, or with radiomics biomarkers such as LVOT gradient and LV posterior wall thickness. Those three biomarkers were significantly greater in patients who developed new-onset AF which leaves open the possibility that these patients simply had a more severe/advanced form of HCM and hence a higher overall risk for complications. On the other hand, it remains unknown how the presented model compares with emerging complex prediction modalities like ECG-AI. This head-to-head comparison could provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the model's relative performance compared to the rapidly evolving state-of-the-art in AI-based prediction modelling. Second, while an external validation was conducted, it was limited to another tertiary care institution geographically close to the training population centre. This similarity between both centres may limit the generalizability of the findings to populations with less severe HCM, and different demographic characteristics. Additional external validations, enabled by the public availability of the model, in more diverse cohorts will be eventually necessary to ensure broader applicability. Third, the relatively short average follow-up period of approximately three years may explain the lower incidence of AF observed (10–15%) and could introduce a short-term follow-up bias in incident AF adjudication. This may have potentially underestimated the model's predictive performance. Finally, the proteomics-based model relied on a single time-point blood sample which may be affected by time-of-day or medications. However, AF risk is expected to be dynamic, modulated by changes in the atrial myopathy substrate and acute triggers.15 The incorporation of serial proteomic measurements could provide insights into temporal changes in protein expression related to AF risk.

Building on the promising results of Lumisch and colleagues, future research should explore whether combining proteomics-based models with other novel predictive approaches—such as PGS, which were shown to be associated with lifetime AF risk in sarcomeric variant carriers,8 ECG-AI algorithms, and radiomics—can further improve the accuracy of AF risk prediction in patients with HCM. Integrating multiple modalities may capture a broader spectrum of risk factors, potentially enhancing the predictive performance beyond what each method achieves individually. Moreover, larger studies with extended follow-up periods are necessary to assess the differential accuracy of various prediction models across distinct HCM subgroups defined by disease severity, genotype, age, and sex. Establishing the clinical significance of these predictive models requires prospective trials that not only validate their performance but also consider the cost-effectiveness and potential harms of the screening strategies. It is essential to balance the benefits of early AF detection with the resources required and the risk of overdiagnosis or unnecessary interventions. Furthermore, given the high prevalence of AF and its significant morbidity and mortality in HCM patients, one could argue about the necessity of risk stratification vs. adopting a universal systematic more intensive screening approach. With the increasing availability of consumer-facing wearable technologies capable of continuous cardiac monitoring, a ‘screen-all’ strategy might be justified. However, it is important to determine the clinical relevance of AF detected by such novel screening tools—specifically, whether these detections are akin to clinically significant AF or resemble device-detected subclinical AF, and what their implications are for patient outcome and management in HCM.

In conclusion, the authors should be congratulated for developing a novel proteomics-based AF risk score in HCM patients. The study offers a new avenue for early identification of patients at high risk for AF, potentially enabling timely interventions, and enhancing our ability to prevent AF-related complications in patients with HCM.
