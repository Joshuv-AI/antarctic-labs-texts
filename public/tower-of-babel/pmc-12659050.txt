Inflammatory and neuroimmune imbalance in diabetic incontinence: insights from serum biomarker profiling
Authors: Ali Can Albaz, Funda Kosova, Gökhan Temeltaş, Oktay Üçer, Talha Müezzi̇noğlu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic urinary incontinence is a multifactorial condition involving neuropathy, oxidative stress, and epithelial dysfunction. Serum biomarkers—cathelicidin (LL-37), elafin, vitamin D receptor (VDR), 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), and amyloid-β1–42 (Aβ1–42) may provide insight into underlying mechanisms.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional, single-centre observational study including 120 adults: type II diabetes with urinary incontinence (DI; n = 40), non-diabetic urinary incontinence (Non-DI; n = 40), and healthy controls (n = 40). Serum biomarker levels were measured by ELISA. Group differences were analysed using Kruskal–Wallis, with pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests and Bonferroni adjustment (m = 3) where appropriate.ResultsAmong profiled biomarkers, 4-HNE was lower in DI than in both Non-DI and controls, while LL-37, elafin, VDR, and Aβ1–42 showed no Bonferroni-corrected pairwise differences.ConclusionIn diabetic urinary incontinence, 4-HNE showed the most consistent group-level separation—lower in DI compared with both Non-DI and healthy controls—whereas LL-37, elafin, VDR, and Aβ1–42 did not demonstrate Bonferroni-corrected pairwise differences. Introduction

Urinary incontinence (UI), defined by the International Continence Society as the involuntary leakage of urine, is a prevalent condition that significantly impairs quality of life and imposes a substantial burden on health-care systems worldwide [1]. Its prevalence increases with age, particularly among women, and has been reported in up to 50% of postmenopausal women in population-based studies [2, 3]. Despite often being considered benign, UI carries profound physical, psychological, and economic consequences [4].

Diabetes Mellitus type II (DMII) is a major risk factor for UI through mechanisms such as autonomic neuropathy, microvascular complications, and detrusor dysfunction—collectively described as diabetic cystopathy [5, 6]. Hyperglycaemia-induced glucosuria and increased urinary osmolality may further exacerbate urgency and frequency [7]. Although diagnostic tools such as urodynamics, bladder diaries, and symptom questionnaires are valuable, urodynamics can be invasive and diaries/questionnaires can be time-consuming and variably reflective of underlying biology [8]. This gap motivates evaluation of non-invasive biomarkers to improve phenotyping and monitoring in patients with DMII-associated incontinence.

Emerging evidence indicates that inflammation, oxidative stress, and neurodegenerative processes contribute to lower urinary tract dysfunction [9, 10]. Cathelicidin (LL-37), an antimicrobial peptide with immunomodulatory functions, supports epithelial barrier integrity and innate defence, and its synthesis is regulated via vitamin D–vitamin D receptor (VDR) signalling [11, 12]. Rather than systemic vitamin D levels alone, VDR-mediated signalling may therefore be relevant to epithelial responses in the lower urinary tract [13]. Elafin, a serine protease inhibitor, counteracts neutrophil elastase and contributes to protease/antiprotease balance at mucosal surfaces [14, 15]. Amyloid-β1–42 (Aβ1–42), traditionally investigated in neurodegenerative disease, has been implicated in peripheral neuropathy and diabetic complications, suggesting potential relevance for bladder dysfunction [16, 17]. In parallel, 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE)—a lipid-peroxidation product and marker of oxidative stress—offers complementary insight into oxidative epithelial injury in diabetes-related conditions [9, 10]. While the diagnosis and clinical management of UI primarily rely on clinical history and urodynamics [1, 8], biomarkers may help identify patients with DMII at higher risk of developing UI and inform prognosis [13, 18–20].

Accordingly, this study investigated serum levels of LL-37, elafin, VDR, 4-HNE, and Aβ1–42 in adults with DMII-related and non-diabetic UI compared with healthy controls. We hypothesised that biomarker profiles differ between DMII-related and non-diabetic incontinence, reflecting distinct inflammatory, epithelial, oxidative, and neuroimmune mechanisms underlying DMII-associated UI.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This cross-sectional, single-centre observational study was conducted at Manisa Celal Bayar University, Faculty of Medicine. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval no: 20.478.486/2040; date: 11 October 2023) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was supported by the Scientific Research Projects Coordination Unit of Manisa Celal Bayar University (Project no: 2023 − 111).

Participants and grouping

A total of 120 adults were recruited and assigned to three groups: Diabetes Mellitus type II (DMII) with urinary incontinence (UI) (n = 40), non-diabetic UI (n = 40), and healthy controls without urinary symptoms or chronic disease (n = 40). Groups were frequency-matched for age, sex, and body mass index (BMI). Eligibility criteria included age 18–80 years and ability to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria comprised active infection, pregnancy, malignancy, neurogenic bladder, systemic immunosuppressive therapy, acute urinary tract infection, and severe neurological disease. To avoid pathophysiological heterogeneity, patients with Diabetes Mellitus type I were not included. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Menopausal status, detailed prostate-related symptom burden (e.g., IPSS), and diabetes stage were not consistently captured. Severity measures such as pad use per day were also inconsistently recorded and therefore not analysed. Where available, UI subtype labels (stress, urgency, mixed, overflow) and use of lower urinary tract–related medications (α-blockers/5-ARIs, antimuscarinics/β3-agonists) are summarised descriptively in Supplementary Table S1.

Across groups, overall differences were first evaluated with the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed—when appropriate—by pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni adjustment (m = 3). LL-37, elafin, and VDR did not show significant overall between-group differences (Kruskal–Wallis: LL-37 p = 0.257; elafin p = 0.464; VDR p = 0.905), and no pairwise contrasts survived multiple-comparison correction (Tables 2 and 3; Fig. 1A–C). Aβ1–42 likewise did not demonstrate a significant overall difference (Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.310), and pairwise comparisons were not significant after adjustment (Fig. 1D; Tables 2 and 3). In contrast, 4-HNE exhibited a clear overall group effect (Kruskal–Wallis p = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵), driven predominantly by lower 4-HNE levels in the DI group relative to both controls and Non-DI (Mann–Whitney U with Bonferroni adjustment: Control vs. DI adjusted p = 0.000454; Non-DI vs. DI adjusted p = 0.000062; Control vs. Non-DI adjusted p = 1.000; Fig. 1E; Tables 2 and 3). These patterns are visually consistent with the scatter distributions in Fig. 1, while the exact statistics and effect sizes are tabulated for transparency.

To contextualise biomarker covariation, an unsupervised heatmap of z-scored biomarkers (Supplementary Figure S1) suggested a distinct patterning for DI relative to the other groups, in line with the inferential results primarily driven by 4-HNE. Taken together, the results indicate that, among the profiled markers, 4-HNE shows the most robust group-level separation—particularly distinguishing DI from both Non-DI and controls—whereas LL-37, elafin, VDR, and Aβ1–42 do not demonstrate corrected pairwise differences under the present sample and analytic framework.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated using G*Power (version 3.1; Heinrich-Heine-University Düsseldorf, Germany) with an effect size of 0.25 (Cohen’s f), α = 0.05, and power = 80%, indicating a minimum of 35 participants per group. To ensure adequate power, 40 participants were enrolled per group [21].

Sample collection and storage

Venous blood samples were obtained in the morning after an overnight fast of 8–10 h. Samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min; sera were aliquoted into sterile microtubes and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Each sample was thawed once to minimise protein degradation. Laboratory personnel were blinded to group allocation.

ELISA assays

Serum LL-37, elafin, vitamin D receptor (VDR), 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), and amyloid-β1–42 (Aβ1–42) were quantified using CE-marked sandwich ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Catalogue numbers: LL-37 (E-EL-H2438), elafin (E-EL-H2447), VDR (E-EL-H2043), 4-HNE (E-EL-0128), and Aβ1–42 (E-EL-H0543). All assays were performed in duplicate with calibration curves (four-parameter logistic, 4-PL); absorbance was read at 450 nm on a BioTek microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments, USA). Internal controls were included for assay validation.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normal variables are shown as median (interquartile range, IQR). Group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for overall group differences. When significant, pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests were applied with Bonferroni adjustment (m = 3) to correct for multiple comparisons. Categorical variables were analysed with the chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate). A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Effect sizes (η²_H for Kruskal–Wallis; r = Z/√N for Mann–Whitney U contrasts) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported where relevant to complement p-values [22].

Discussion

This study evaluated serum biomarkers related to epithelial defence, oxidative stress, and neuroimmune signalling in adults with diabetic urinary incontinence (DI), non-diabetic urinary incontinence (Non-DI), and healthy controls. The most consistent finding was a reduction in circulating 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) in the DI group, whereas LL-37, elafin, vitamin D receptor (VDR), and amyloid-β1-42 (Aβ1-42) did not demonstrate significant differences after correction for multiple comparisons. These results suggest that oxidative stress pathways may play a more prominent role in DI than systemic antimicrobial or antiprotease mechanisms. LL-37 is essential for urothelial barrier integrity and innate defence, and impaired cathelicidin function has been linked to increased infection susceptibility and delayed epithelial repair in diabetes (23, 24). Its regulation through vitamin D–VDR signalling is well established (11, 12). In the present study, systemic LL-37 and circulating VDR did not differ significantly across groups, implying that serum levels may not capture tissue-specific regulation at the bladder level. Future work combining systemic 25(OH)D measurements with tissue-level VDR analysis may clarify this pathway. Elafin is an inducible antiprotease that modulates neutrophil elastase activity during epithelial stress (25, 26). In our cohort, elafin levels showed only modest variation between groups and did not reach statistical significance, limiting conclusions about protease–antiprotease balance. Together with the LL-37 findings, this suggests that systemic antimicrobial and antiprotease markers are unlikely to serve as reliable discriminators of DI. Although Aβ1-42 has been implicated in peripheral neuropathy and neuroimmune dysfunction in diabetes (16, 17, 27, 28), our data showed no significant overall or pairwise differences. This may reflect the complexity of amyloid biology, where local tissue or urinary alterations could be more informative than serum concentrations. Reports linking urinary Aβ to lower urinary tract dysfunction (29) support further investigation into this pathway in diabetic uropathy. The most striking observation was the reduction in circulating 4-HNE among DI patients, contrasting with expectations of increased oxidative stress in diabetes. Potential explanations include compartmentalisation of oxidative injury at the bladder level, variability in lipid peroxidation dynamics, or adaptive antioxidant responses that lower circulating aldehydes despite local tissue stress. These results highlight the limitations of single oxidative biomarkers and underscore the need for integrated panels combining epithelial, oxidative, and neuroimmune markers. Taken together, our findings suggest that while systemic antimicrobial and antiprotease markers did not distinguish DI from other groups, oxidative alterations—particularly reduced 4-HNE—may represent a more relevant signal in type II diabetes–associated incontinence. These observations should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, warranting validation in larger, longitudinal cohorts.(30)

Limitations

Several limitations merit consideration. The cross-sectional, single-centre design and relatively modest sample size limit causal inference and generalisability. Although groups were matched for age, sex, and BMI, residual confounding cannot be excluded because clinical variables such as diabetes duration, glycaemic control, and treatment modality were incompletely captured. Incontinence subtype and severity (e.g., pad counts) were inconsistently recorded, precluding subtype-specific analyses and reducing interpretability for sex-stratified outcomes. Important covariates such as menopausal status in women and prostate symptom burden in men were also not systematically assessed. Biomarker measurements were restricted to circulating LL-37, elafin, VDR, Aβ1-42, and 4-HNE; related parameters such as 25(OH)D, tissue-level VDR expression, neutrophil elastase, and broader oxidative stress markers were not evaluated. Finally, the absence of longitudinal sampling prevents assessment of temporal changes and limits conclusions to cross-sectional associations.

Future Directions

Future studies should be multicentre and longitudinal to characterise within-person trajectories of candidate biomarkers. Concurrent assessment of systemic vitamin D, tissue-level VDR, broader oxidative stress panels (e.g., lipid peroxidation and antioxidant indices), and urinary readouts may refine mechanistic interpretation. Stratification should be pre-specified by urinary incontinence subtype (urgency, stress, mixed, overflow), type II diabetes stage/duration, and sex-specific factors, with prospective capture of menopausal status, prostate-related symptom burden, and standardised severity measures (e.g., pad tests, validated scores). Integrating biomarker data with detailed clinical covariates and multi-omics in explainable modelling frameworks may ultimately improve risk stratification and support personalised management. Larger cohorts are also needed to evaluate age- and duration-adjusted associations between biomarkers and clinical phenotypes, and to test external validity across care settings.

Conclusion

In adults with diabetic urinary incontinence, 4-HNE showed the most consistent group-level separation—lower in DI compared with both non-diabetic incontinence and healthy controls—whereas LL-37, elafin, VDR, and Aβ1-42 did not demonstrate Bonferroni-corrected pairwise differences. These findings point to oxidative and neuroimmune pathways as plausible features of DI biology but should be regarded as exploratory. Validation in larger, longitudinal cohorts is required before biomarker signals can inform clinical application.
