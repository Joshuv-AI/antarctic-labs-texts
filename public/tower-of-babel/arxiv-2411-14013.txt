Single-Model Attribution for Spoofed Speech via Vocoder Fingerprints in an Open-World Setting
Matías Pizarro 1,* , Mike Laszkiewicz 1,* , Dorothea Kolossa 2 , Asja Fischer 1

Abstract
As speech generation technology advances, so do the potential threats of misusing spoofed speech signals. One way to address these threats is by attributing the signals to their source generative model. In this work, we are the first to tackle the single-model attribution task in an open-world setting, that is, we aim at identifying whether spoofed speech signals from unknown sources originate from a specific vocoder. We show that the standardized average residual between audio signals and their low-pass filtered or EnCodec filtered versions can serve as powerful vocoder fingerprints. The approach only requires data from the target vocoder and allows for simple but highly accurate distance-based model attribution. We demonstrate its effectiveness on LJSpeech and JSUT, achieving an average AUROC of over 99% in most settings. The accompanying robustness study shows that it is also resilient to noise levels up to a certain degree.



I
Introduction

With the rapid advancement of synthetic data generation technologies, distinguishing between genuine and artificial speech signals has become increasingly challenging.
This issue is particularly relevant today, because public and open-source tools enable even those with minimal expertise to produce highly realistic voices that closely resemble human speech
[
1
,
2
]
.
Although these technologies offer numerous advantages, such as enhancing accessibility for the speech-impaired
[
3
]
and supporting multilingual communication
[
4
]
, they also pose several risks.
These include the potential misuse of generated media for spreading disinformation or fake news
[
5
]
, conducting spoofing attacks on biometric systems that use automatic speaker verification
[
6
]
, misleading juries in criminal trials by presenting forged evidence
[
7
]
, or by cloning voices to impersonate individuals for phishing or other types of fraud
[
8
,
9
]
.
Given these risks, different countermeasures against spoofed speech signals were proposed.

Current research in deepfake detection mainly focuses on binary classification, as mirrored by
well-established challenges like the Automatic Speaker Verification Spoofing (ASVspoof)
[
10
]
and Audio Deepfake Detection (ADD)
[
11
]
.
However, a significant gap remains in the ability to identify from which model a piece of spoofed speech signal was most likely generated—a process known as
model attribution
or
algorithm recognition
.
Identifying the source algorithm of spoofed speech signals is important for several reasons, including supporting intellectual property protection, preventing copyright infringement, and ensuring that publicly shared information remains genuine.
While only a few studies
[
12
,
13
]
have developed multiclass classifiers that aim at predicting which vocoder—a key element of text-to-speech (TTS) systems—was used to generate a given speech sample, these classifiers are unfortunately limited to a fixed set of candidate vocoders known during training, a scenario typically referred to as a closed-world setting.
The downside of models trained in this setting is that they can not be applied to novel vocoders and thus need to be retrained (which is costly) every time novel vocoders are released, which frequently happens.

:
Vocoder fingerprint extraction using the average residuals between audio signals and their EnCodec filtered versions.

To overcome the limitations of existing methods, this paper introduces, to the best of our knowledge, the first single-model attribution technique in an open-world setting.
Here, single-model attribution determines whether a sample was generated by a specific vocoder of interest, and an open-world setting corresponds to the system’s ability to distinguish between speech signals generated by a known vocoder and inputs from any previously unseen model.
Notably, our method requires only training data from a single target vocoder, making it more adaptable and efficient in handling new and diverse spoofed audio sources.
We conduct an empirical analysis using state-of-the-art vocoders and not only demonstrate, building on previous studies, that vocoders leave unique fingerprints on audio signals, but also that we can successfully extract such fingerprints to trace back the specific vocoder that generated them.
Our approach across datasets in two languages achieves outstanding performance in distinguishing spoofed speech signals generated by a specific vocoder from speech signals sourced elsewhere and remains highly robust in the presence of environmental noise.

II
Related Work

In this section, we review previous research on deepfake detection and revisit the concept of generative model fingerprints as they have been proposed in the computer vision domain for generative adversarial networks (GANs).

II-A

Deepfake Detection

The study of deepfake detection has two main fields of research: spoofing detection systems and vocoder attribution methods.
Spoofing detection systems generally consist of two components: an acoustic feature extractor and a classifier.
The acoustic feature extractor is designed to extract relevant characteristics from the raw audio input into a form that a classifier can effectively analyze.
Examples of effective spectral features include Linear-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (LFCC)
[
14
]
, Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC)
[
14
]
, Constant Q Cepstral Coefficients
[
15
]
, and long-term variable Q transform
[
16
]
.
The classifier uses these extracted acoustic features to train a model capable of distinguishing real from spoofed audio samples. Classifier models that have demonstrated effectiveness for this task are based on Gaussian mixture models
[
17
]
or various types of neural networks like CNNs
[
18
,
19
]
, ResNet
[
20
,
21
]
, Res2Net
[
22
]
, SENet
[
23
,
24
]
, GNNs
[
25
,
26
]
, RawNet2
[
27
]
, DARTS
[
28
]
, and Transformers
[
29
]
.

In contrast, vocoder attribution methods aim to determine the specific vocoder that produced the samples.
Frank and Schönherr
[
30
]
observed that the residuals left by vocoder-generated speech, particularly in the higher frequency ranges, can be attributed to the specific type of vocoder used.
Building on this, Yan et al.
[
12
]
developed a vocoder detector by extracting acoustic features with LFCC and training a ResNet-based classifier to identify speech generated from eight distinct vocoders.
Similarly, Li et al.
[
31
]
proposed a method that uses MFCCs to extract acoustic features trains a lightweight CNN model to classify four different vocoders.
During training, for spoofed speech signals, the input is first reconstructed by an autoencoder before extracting the MFCCs.
Additionally, Deng et al.
[
13
]
created a vocoder detector utilizing Mel spectrograms as acoustic features and training a neural network classifier with patch-wise supervised contrastive learning to detect spoofed speech signals coming from one of six vocoders.
Recently, the third track of the ADD 2023 challenge
[
11
]
involves model attribution of speech signals.
While this challenge also includes open-world model attribution, it differs from our investigated setting in the following three points.
First, the attribution techniques were evaluated based on data from 8 different sources, from which 7 were known during training. The existence of multiple models during training allows to leverage latent representations of a trained classifier, as deployed by the winner of the challenge
[
32
]
, or to make use of contrastive learning, as deployed by the runner-up
[
33
]
. In the single-model setting, that is, when restricting the access to data from only a single source during training, these techniques are not applicable.
Secondly, the evaluation in ADD puts more emphasis on the closed-world performance as the dataset contains around
7/8=87.5\%
samples from generative models seen during training.
In contrast, the focus of this paper lies purely on the open-world attribution task.
Lastly, the dataset utilized in ADD is not publicly available and not reproducible.

II-B

GAN Fingerprints

The existence of model-specific fingerprints in images generated by GANs was verified by Marra et al.
[
34
]
.
Their idea is based on the assumption that any artificially generated image
X
decomposes into its content
I(X)
and a fingerprint
F
that is unrelated to the image semantics but specific to the model, that is,
X=I(X)+F
for every
X
generated from the model.
To extract the fingerprint, they assumed that a suitable image filter
f
is capable of removing the fingerprint, such that
f(X)\approx I(X)
, and therefore
R:=X-f(X)\approx F
.
Given a set of generated samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
, the sample-wise residuals are defined by
R_{i}:=X_{i}-f(X_{i})
and
F
is estimated by
\hat{F}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}R_{i}
.
For inference, i.e., for checking whether a test sample
X_{\operatorname{test}}
contains a fingerprint similar to
\hat{F}
, they first compute its residual
R_{\operatorname{test}}
as above and then assign the correlation score
s_{\operatorname{cor}}(X_{\operatorname{test}};\,\hat{F}):=\langle\tilde{R}_{\operatorname{test}}\,,\,\tilde{F}\rangle\in[-1,1]
, where
\tilde{R}_{\operatorname{test}}
and
\tilde{F}
denote the zero-mean and unit-norm versions of
R_{\operatorname{test}}
and
\hat{F}
, respectively.
Having a set of
m
different generative models, and therefore, a set of corresponding fingerprints
\hat{F}_{1},\dots,\hat{F}_{m}
, one attributes a sample to the

j
th model, where
j=\argmax_{i\in[m]}s_{\operatorname{cor}}(X_{\operatorname{test}};\,\hat{F}_{i})
. A recent study
[
35
]
shows that this fingerprinting technique generalizes to diffusion models (e.g.,
[
36
]
) and is applicable in an open-world setting for single-model attribution, which is largely unexplored in the audio domain.

III
Extracting Audio-Fingerprints

In this section, building upon the fingerprinting methodology for GAN-generated images, we present an approach for extracting fingerprints of generative models for audio and employing them for model attribution.
While applying the methodology may sound straightforward at first sight, we are confronted with two major challenges.
First, note that the length of audio waveforms is typically not unified. Since the fingerprint however is based on averaging filter-based residuals,
it is necessary to find a representation of audio waveforms that is independent of the audio length.
To remedy this, we choose the average energy (in dB) per frequency bin
E(X)
of the audio
X
, whose size is given by the number of frequency bins of the spectrogram.
The second challenge is to find a suitable filter capable of removing noise unrelated to the content of the audio.
We found two filters to work particularly well: 1) EnCodec
[
37
]
, which corresponds to a deep autoencoder developed for compressing audio, and 2) a low-pass filter that attenuates signal components above a specified cutoff frequency.
Specifically, we propose to estimate a fingerprint based on samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
by

\hat{F}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}R_{i}\kern 5.0pt,\text{where }R_{i}:=E(X_{i})-f(E(X_{i}))\kern 5.0pt,

where
f
either corresponds to the low-pass filter or the concatenation of the EnCodec encoder and decoder.
Figure
2
shows the fingerprints extracted for some vocoders.

As an alternative to the correlation-based scoring we introduce scoring based on the Mahalanobis distance to the fingerprint, that is defined by

s_{\operatorname{md}}(X_{\operatorname{test}},\hat{F}):=-\sqrt{(R_{\operatorname{test}}-\hat{F})^{\top}S^{-1}(R_{\operatorname{test}}-\hat{F})}\kern 5.0pt,

where
S^{-1}
denotes the inverse of the empirical covariance matrix of the training residuals.
In contrast to
s_{\operatorname{cor}}
,
s_{\operatorname{md}}
incorporates the variance and covariance between different energy bins of the residuals and corrects the score accordingly.
Figure
1
presents the
s_{\operatorname{md}}
values w.r.t. the target WGlow vocoder fingerprint for various unseen inputs generated by multiple vocoders, as well as inputs generated by the same target vocoder.

IV
Experiments and Results

This section details our experiments, including dataset selection, fingerprint extraction, and model attribution in an open-world setting.
We accompany our experiments with a noise robustness analysis to evaluate the effectiveness of our approach in a more realistic scenario.

IV-B

Experimental Setting

We experimented with different feature extraction methods, filter parameters, and score functions to uncover vocoder fingerprints.
For the EnCodec filter, the optimal setting involved Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) with a window size of
2048
and a hop-length of
128
for feature extraction, a compression rate of
24
kbps, and the
s_{\operatorname{cor}}
as the score function.
In contrast, for the low-pass filter, the best results were achieved with an STFT with a window size of
128
and a hop-length of
2
for feature extraction, a cutoff frequency of
1
kHz with a stop band frequency of
1.5
kHz for the filter, and
s_{\operatorname{md}}
as the score function.

To conduct the experiments, we use
80\%
of the data for training and
20\%
for testing.
For each vocoder, we estimate a fingerprint exclusively on data generated by that particular vocoder, as detailed in Section
III
.
For evaluation, we use a dataset composed of
50\%
samples generated by the target vocoder and
50\%
samples from another source (i.e. one other model or real audio) and calculate the attribution score relative to the target vocoder’s fingerprint.
The results are reported by the corresponding AUROC values.

To test the robustness of our approach, we evaluate it in a more realistic scenario by adding noise to the test samples using SpeechBrain’s environmental corruption function
[
2
]
.
We mix noise provided by random samples from the Freesound portion of the MUSAN corpus
[
41
]
(which includes
843
recordings of music, speech, and background noise)
to the test samples at various signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) ranging from
0
dB to
40
dB.

V
Conclusions

Given that speech-generating models are getting increasingly good and easy to exploit, there is a surging need for vocoder attribution methods in an open-world setting.
Our approach, which employs either EnCodec or a low-pass filter for fingerprint extraction, shows excellent performance in distinguishing samples from a target vocoder compared to those from unknown vocoders and real audios, achieving nearly perfect AUROC scores.
Moreover, our single-model attribution framework proves effective in noisy conditions, keeping good performance over
15
dB. We are convinced that our approach adds a simple but highly effective technique to the model attribution and deepfake detection toolbox.

Future research could focus on exploring the explainability of how different vocoders produce distinct audio fingerprints and assessing the robustness of our attribution technique against further types of variations, including compression artifacts, different bandwidths, or bit rates.
