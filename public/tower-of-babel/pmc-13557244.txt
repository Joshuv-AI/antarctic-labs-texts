Primary Nodular Melanomas May Represent Epidermotropic Melanoma Metastases Diagnosed at a Later Stage
Authors: Félix Khoa Pham, Marie Danset, Olivier Harou, Damien Vasseur, Jonathan Lopez, Luc Thomas
Published: 2026

Introduction

Primary nodular melanoma (PNM) is classically defined as a melanocytic tumor with a de novo vertical growth phase and no radial growth phase extending beyond three rete ridges from the dermal component. In contrast, most cutaneous melanoma metastases are confined to the dermis and, when associated with a history of melanoma, are readily identified as secondary lesions. However, epidermotropic melanoma metastases (EMM) can mimic PNM, particularly when no previous primary melanoma has been documented [1,2]. We aimed to compare the clinical, histopathological, and molecular features of PNM and EMM to better distinguish between these entities.

Findings

We retrospectively analyzed all consecutive cases of PNM (N=19) and EMM (N=15) diagnosed at our center between 2008 and 2018. Patients with PNM had no previous history of melanoma, and both cohorts were independent of each other. Samples older than ten years were excluded to ensure DNA quality. Clinical data were extracted from medical records, and all formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumor samples were reviewed by two expert dermatopathologists. DNA was extracted from tumor-rich areas and sequenced using an Illumina NextSeq 500 platform (Illumina, Paris, France). Mutational and copy-number variations in major melanoma oncogenes (BRAF, NRAS, KIT, MAP2K1) and tumor suppressor genes (CDKN2A, NF1, PTEN, BAP1) were analyzed using the SOPHiA GENETICS DDM platform (SOPHiA GENETICS, Saint-Sulpice, Switzerland). Only pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants with a variant allele frequency of >5% were included.

No significant demographic difference was observed between the two cohorts (Table 1). Clinically, PNM lesions were more frequently ulcerated and amelanotic. Histopathologically, PNMs were thicker, more ulcerated, and exhibited higher mitotic activity than EMMs (Table 1). However, the two groups shared remarkably similar genetic alteration profiles, with no specific mutation or copy number pattern distinguishing PNM from EMM (Figures 1 and 2 and Table 1).

Conclusion

Currently, PNM are thought to occur in any of the proposed developmental pathways of melanoma without any clear genomic features distinguishing it from other melanoma histological subtypes (e.g., superficial spreading and lentigo maligna melanomas) [3]. Our results suggest that some PNMs could in fact correspond to epidermotropic metastases from regressed or undetected primary tumors [4], appearing as thick, ulcerated lesions due to a delayed diagnosis. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that PNM cases were often diagnosed de novo, whereas EMM patients were already under surveillance for previous melanoma, facilitating the earlier detection of new lesions.

Although PNM is still considered a distinct clinicopathological entity, our results, consistent with those of prior studies [5,6], demonstrate the absence of a specific molecular signature. This challenges the relevance of maintaining PNM as a separate histopathological subtype and highlights the need for caution in staging and therapeutic decision-making, as potential misclassification may lead to undertreatment in the setting of effective adjuvant therapies for resected metastatic melanoma patients.
