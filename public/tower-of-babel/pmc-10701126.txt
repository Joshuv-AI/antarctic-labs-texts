Prostatic abscess Metastasis: A case report of an unusual complication
Authors: Abdulaziz Althunayan, Mana Almuhaideb
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndogenous endophthalmitis, a rare and severe ocular infection, is typically associated with endogenous infections that reach the eye, while prostatic abscesses pose clinical challenges and are more common in immunocompromised patients. We present a case of a 50-year-old male with endophthalmitis resulting from a prostatic abscess. The patient's journey underscores the need for heightened awareness of prostatic abscess in patients with perineal symptoms, especially those with immunosuppression, and the consideration of endophthalmitis as a potential complication in urologic disease. The case highlights the urgent need for standardized guidelines in managing prostatic abscesses, given their complexity and potential for severe complications.Keywords: Prostate abscess, Endophthalmitis, Prostatic abscess, Metastatic infection

Introduction

Endophthalmitis is a rare infection, where an inflammatory process is present in the vitreous cavity. Endophthalmitis is most commonly exogenous, occurring post intraocular surgery or posttraumatic injury. Endogenous endophthalmitis occurs when an endogenous infection is present, and organisms travel to the eye.1 Endogenous endophthalmitis accounts for only 5–10 % of all endophthalmitis cases. And commonly occurs in the setting of immunosuppression.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7

Prostatic abscesses yet rare, impose a clinical difficulty for urologists. Prostatic abscesses usually occur in immunocompromised patients, e.g. Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, diabetes.8 Due to the plummeting incidence of prostatic abscesses, published data are mostly case reports. Thereafter, guidelines haven't been established. Moreover, prostatic abscess as a diagnosis shouldn't be only limited to adults. Regardless of the patient's age, perineal swelling, and pain should raise the awareness of prostatic abscess, especially in the setting of immunosuppression. Prostatic abscesses in children have been reported in the setting of immunodeficiency.9,10

To the best of our knowledge, few cases of prostatic abscesses metastasizing to the eye and causing endophthalmitis are reported.11,12 We present a case of a 50-year-old gentleman, presenting with right endophthalmitis secondary to a prostatic abscess.

Case presentation

A 50-year-old gentleman presented to the emergency department with right eye vision loss.

20 days earlier, the patient began complaining of progressive perianal pain, swelling, and a small nodule. Moreover, 8 days before presentation the patient started to complain of urinary retention, lower abdominal pain, fever, night sweats, and weight loss. The patient has visited a different institution and was diagnosed to have a prostatic abscess. Subsequently, the patient underwent incision and drainage, and intravenous antibiotics were administered. Antibiotics were switched to oral, and the patient was discharged. The fever has improved but was still present. Follow-up pelvic Computed Tomography (CT) showed a remnant abscess. The CT results indicated another incision and drainage, which was performed under general anesthesia. On the day of the drainage, the patient was still complaining of fever but was told that he did not have bacteremia. The day following the operation the patient developed sudden painless loss of vision in the right eye without discharge.

The following day, the patient was referred to our institution. The patient was still complaining of fever, rigors, chills, and night sweats. Moreover, was still complaining of abdominal pain and perianal pain. The perianal pain was worse when the patient sits. The patient is a known case of type two diabetes mellitus poorly controlled with oral hypoglycemic agents. The patient has never had any ophthalmologic complaints. Prior to the patient's presentation, he had never undergone any surgeries.

On physical examination, the patient was febrile (38.1C), but otherwise vitally stable. Genitourinary examination revealed perineal induration and soft tissue swelling, erythema of the perineum, bladder distention, and scrotal tenderness and swelling, which was most likely attributed to orchitis. Per rectal exam showed tenderness and swelling at the 6 o'clock position. Ophthalmic examination revealed the following abnormalities in the right eye: visual acuity detecting hand motion only, mild injection in conjunctiva and sclera, 2 mm hypopyon in the anterior chamber with floating cells, dense fibrinous reaction on the lens, and inability to view the fundus. The left eye was within normal limits. The rest of the physical examination was normal. Thereafter, the patient had a preliminary diagnosis of endophthalmitis.

Subsequently, the patient was admitted and administered intravenous (IV) antibiotics and intravitreal antibiotics. A Foley catheter was placed, and 1 L of urine was released with signs of fat on the surface. During the admission, the patient underwent an ultrasound of the abdomen and pelvis and chest X-ray, which revealed bilateral hydronephrosis and left middle lobe cystic lesion, respectively. Complete blood count showed leukocytosis (18 × 109/Liter). Random blood glucose was high at 17 mmol/L. Urinalysis was positive for pyuria, leukocyte esterase, and nitrites. The remaining laboratory investigations were unremarkable. Subsequently, the patient underwent CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis. The CT confirmed the diagnosis of Fournier's gangrene, emphysematous cystitis, and septic emboli in the lung. A CT cystogram showed extensive air pockets in the left ischiorectal fossa, perineum extending to the gluteal region. And a few pockets of air seen in the perirectal area. Moreover, it showed a mildly enlarged ill-defined prostate with multiple pockets of air as well as contrast filled pockets. A hyperdense material was visualized in the left perineal region between air pockets that could represent contrast leak.

On the second day, the patient was taken to the operating room for debridement surgery. The patient was placed in a lithotomy position and a vertical perineal incision was done. Pus came out of the incision extensively, the tissue appeared healthy without signs of necrosis. Dissection was continued up to the aforementioned air pockets, and more pus was discharged. Subsequently, we packed the wound, applied pressure dressing, and shifted the patient to the surgical intensive care unit (SICU).

On the third day, Klebsiella pneumoniae was found in the blood, vitreal, corneal, wound, and tissue cultures. Infectious diseases service was consulted, and IV antibiotics were modified based on the patient's cultures. Despite that the patient was clinically improving, on the 2nd day post-op, the patient complained of right-sided headache and orbital pain. An orbital CT confirmed the presence of endophthalmitis and orbital cellulitis. Given, the patient complaints and orbital CT results, he was booked for evisceration of the right eye. On the 5th day after the first presentation, the patient underwent evisceration of the right eye under general anesthesia and was shifted back to the SICU. The following day, the patient was doing well and was shifted to the surgical ward.

On the 6th day post-debridement, a CT abdomen and pelvis demonstrated prostatic multiloculated collections and urinomas with active urinary bladder leak. Thoracic surgery service was consulted for the multiple septic emboli in the lung and recommended medical management. CT abdomen and pelvis was repeated two weeks later and showed significant improvement in the abscess. A month later a CT abdomen and pelvis, and CT cystogram demonstrated further improvement of the multiloculated prostatic collection. Despite negative cultures, infectious diseases service continued the patient on antibiotics for 6 weeks. After two months from the patient's initial presentation, a cystoscopy was done and revealed tissue healing and a normal bladder wall. Thereafter, the patient was discharged. The patient was seen serially in the outpatient department for wound examination and dressing changes. 1 month after discharge a cystogram was done and showed homogenous opacification of the bladder with a smooth wall, with no filling defects or diverticular out pouches or contrast leak.

Discussion

Prostatic abscesses commonly present with dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic or perineal pain, and tenesmus. Urinary retention occurs in almost one-third of the patients.13 Up to one-third of the patients present with systemic symptoms only, such as fever of unknown origin.14 As for the physical examination, almost all patients will have tender prostate on digital rectal examination.13 However, our patient did not present mainly with lower urinary symptoms but presented with urinary retention and perianal pain.

Prostate abscess commonly occurs as a complication of poorly treated bacterial prostatitis, most commonly occurring in men in their 5th or 6th decade of life.8 The most common organism causing prostatic abscess is Escherichia Coli, followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae.15 As aforementioned, the cultures of our patient were positive for Klebsiella in the tissue, wound, blood, and vitreous fluid.

Given the presence of air pockets in the patient's imaging, this could hint to the diagnosis of emphysematous prostatitis. Emphysematous prostatitis is commonly caused by Klebsiella organisms. Delay in the diagnosis can lead to serious sequela such as rupture of the abscess. The abscess could rupture into the ischiorectal fossa or the perivesicle space. This was confirmed in our patient's cultures, with Klebsiella as the main organism. Multiple similar case reports have had similar results to our patient's.16,17

Metastasis of the infection has been attributed to the virulence of Klebsiella pneumoniae.18 Few cases were reported with endogenous endophthalmitis caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae prostatic abscess.12,18 Endogenous endophthalmitis is an emergency that will require eventually evisceration, if not promptly managed. When a patient presents with ophthalmologic symptoms in the presence of a urologic disease, such as a prostate abscess, endogenous endophthalmitis should be kept in mind.18

Conclusion

In the absence of guidelines to treat prostatic abscesses, such a disease creates a dilemma for urologists. We presented a case report of a patient that had a prostatic abscess that has metastasized to the eye and lung. Despite that proposed approaches have been reported, to the best of our knowledge, none were scientifically collected to formulate standardized guidelines.
