Comparative Analysis of the Clinical Performance of Three Different Inlays Based on Fabrication: An In Vivo Study
Authors: MS Kalpana, Sophia Thakur, Benin Paulaian, Nagammai Meyappan, T Shunmuga Priya, S Santhoshi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContext:Novelty in today’s dental practice aims at superiority in aesthetics. Dental practitioners address patient anxiety about retaining teeth while maintaining aesthetic appeal. The debate revolves around the best approach to meet their aesthetic and functional needs, leading to the development of various inlay techniques. Choosing between laboratory-processed composite resins and metal-free ceramic restorations has become increasingly complex. Laboratory-processed composites offer similar aesthetics and physical properties to dental ceramics, but ceramic restorations tend to adapt better to cavity preparation.Aims:This study aimed to compare clinical performance, post-operative sensitivity, marginal discrepancy, interfacial staining, and secondary caries between direct composite inlays, indirect composite inlays, and ceramic inlays, with the null hypothesis being no significant difference.Methods and Material:The study involved 30 patients aged 18-45 from Bapuji Dental College and Hospital in Karnataka who underwent composite inlays for their posterior teeth. The patients were selected based on inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the study was approved by the Ethical Committee. Post-operative pain was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale.Statistical Analysis Used:Statistical analysis was performed using the Chi-square test.Results:Satisfactory results were observed throughout the evaluation, with statistically no significant differences between them but with a more assured performance of the ceramic inlay group and acceptable presentation of the indirect composite inlay group.Conclusions:Posterior tooth-coloured inlays provide acceptable outcomes, making them reliable candidates for clinical use, and ceramic inlays exhibit better performance rates.

INTRODUCTION

The growing demand for aesthetic or metal-free restorations and the dental profession’s interest in bio-aesthetic materials have led to new biomimetic approaches to restorative dentistry.[1] This includes the use of tooth-like restorative materials-like composite resins and porcelain, as well as the generation of adhesive agents. The primary goal of aesthetic dentistry is to achieve functional, natural, and beautiful restorations of teeth while eliminating the appearance of metals-like gold and amalgam.[2]

As patients seek conservative, biocompatible, durable, safe, and aesthetic treatments, the increased utilization of composite resin materials for direct posterior dentition has drawn attention to technological advances.[3] However, their use in posterior regions should be limited to smaller restorations due to polymerization shrinkage, which can lead to microleakage, post-operative sensitivity, and recurrent caries.

Nano-filled composites have shown equivalent mechanical properties to hybrid composites, with high translucency, high polish, and polish retention similar to micro-filled composites. Heat treatment of composite resin materials has been claimed to produce more abrasion-resistant and aesthetic restorations than conventional composite resins.[4]

Indirect composite resin and porcelain systems represent alternative restorative solutions for larger posterior cavities. Laboratory-processed inlays fabricated with porcelain or composite resin restore mechanical and biological function while achieving optimal aesthetics with minimal tooth reduction.[5] Both porcelains and indirect resins provide precise marginal integrity, ideal proximal contacts, wear resistance, reduced polymerization shrinkage, enhanced safety, biocompatibility, and optimal aesthetics. These restorative systems complement and broaden the scope of restorative alternatives available to assist patients, technicians, and dentists in making informed choices for different clinical situations.[6]

AIM

To compare and evaluate post-operative sensitivity, marginal discrepancy, interfacial staining, and secondary caries between direct composite inlays, indirect composite inlays, and ceramic inlays.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

This study was conducted on 30 patients aged 18-45 years. The subjects were in good medical health and required treatment for primary caries or failed restorations. Inclusion criteria included asymptomatic molar and premolar teeth, teeth without active pulp-periapical pathosis, teeth requiring two surface cavity preparations, fractured restorations, and teeth with recurrent caries. Patients with high caries index, poor oral hygiene, periodontitis, occlusal interferences, parafunctional habits, and teeth with internal discolorations were excluded from the study.

Teeth were randomly divided into 3 groups with 10 teeth in each group. In Group I and Group II cavity preparations were made to receive direct and indirect composite inlays, respectively, and in Group III cavity preparations were made to receive ceramic inlays [Figure 1].

Grouping

Cavity preparations were made according to adhesive inlay principles, with an occlusal thickness of at least 2 mm and divergence angles between opposing walls of 8-10°, and undercuts were eliminated by the placement of resin-reinforced glass ionomer (Vitrebond, 3M ESPE).

Direct composite inlays were prepared using a nano-hybrid composite resin, employing a sectional matrix band and light-reflecting wedges for contouring the proximal contact. Restorative material (Synergy D6) was placed as a single increment into the unbonded cavity preparation and light cured for primary polymerization. Then the inlay was placed into a heated oven for secondary curing at 110°C for a 7-minute cycle.[7] Direct composite inlays were cemented according to manufacturers’ instructions, where the cavity walls and the fitting surface were etched for 30 seconds with 37% phosphoric acid. A thin layer of bonding agent was applied onto the cavity walls and cured for 10 seconds. The base and catalyst of the resin-luting cement (Rely X Arc) were mixed and placed into the cavity.[8] The inlay was seated with firm pressure, and the proximal and occlusal aspects were each given a 60-second exposure from the light-curing unit. Finally, the occlusion was refined using articulating paper and polished with the composite polishing kit (Astropol, Ivoclar Vivadent).

For indirect composite inlays, diagnostic casts were initially prepared. Following cavity preparation, impressions were taken using elastomeric material (Aquasil LV, Dentsply). Provisional restorations were secured using eugenol-free temporary cement (RelyX Temp NE, 3M ESPE). Subsequently, all inlays were fabricated by the manufacturers’ instructions in the laboratory. After applying a separating medium, the restorative material (SR ADORO) was placed into the cavity in one or two increments and light-cured for 40 seconds per surface. The inlay was then heat-treated in an oven at 100°C for 15 minutes. Once cooled to room temperature, it was removed from the oven, refined on the die using fine diamond instruments, and polished with a composite finishing kit before cementation.[9]

Once the inlay was prepared, cementation procedures followed the same protocol as the cementation process for a direct composite inlay.

Cavity preparation for ceramic inlays was designed with an occlusal thickness of 2 mm and proximal extension determined by carious tissue, featuring rounded internal angles and 90° cavosurface angles without bevels, with the cervical margin ending in a deep chamfer to avoid occlusal bevels and 10°-12° divergence. After cavity preparation, impressions were taken, and, in the laboratory, ceramic inlays were fabricated according to the manufacturer’s instructions.[10]

During cementation, the cavity was etched with 37% phosphoric acid, rinsed, and dried gently before applying a bonding agent and curing it. Resin cement was mixed and applied, and then the inlay was seated, excess cement was removed, and light-curing was done. After checking the occlusion and removing the rubber dam, final finishing and polishing were performed.

Follow-up and evaluation

Post-operative pain levels were measured using Visual Analog Scale. Restoration evaluations were conducted blinded at baseline, 1 week, 3 months, 6 months, and 9 months. The assessments included examining marginal discrepancies, interfacial staining, secondary caries, and post-operative sensitivity using the modified USPHS criteria, categorising outcomes as Alpha, Bravo, Charlie, and Delta based on clinical criteria.[11]

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Chi-square test.

DISCUSSION

Dental practitioners should be aware of various aesthetic restorative schemes, including composite and ceramic inlays. Adhesive inlay systems offer better proximal contacts, occlusal morphology, and marginal accuracy with minimal shrinkage. Composite inlay systems, which have been in use for almost 20 years, mimic anatomic features and enhance proximal contacts and marginal accuracy.[12]

Nano-filled composites have high translucency, polishability, and retention similar to micro-filled composites, while maintaining physical properties and wear resistance.[13] Ali Riza CETIN and Nimet UNLU concluded that nano-composites could be used in posterior teeth due to their strength and aesthetic properties. In this study, a nano-composite Synergy D6 (Coltene) was used to evaluate the clinical performance of direct composite inlays when heat treated. Synergy D6 is a highly filled nano-hybrid composite for restoration of anterior and posterior teeth, with an average filler particle size of 0.6 μm. Direct composite inlays are prepared in a single visit. Indirect composite inlays, manufactured through laboratory processing, have been developed to improve strength, wear, hardness, colour stability, and optical properties.[14]

This study uses SR Adoro (Ivoclar) as an indirect resin for posterior dentition restorations, which consists of large filler particles combined with micro-fillers using splinter polymers. SR Adoro is less susceptible to water absorption and solubility. Micro-fillers are added to combat the disadvantages of large filler particles, such as resistance to discolouration and plaque formation. High-strength ceramics-like aluminium oxide and zirconium oxide ceramics have high wear resistance but also exhibit abrasiveness.[15] Turkom-Cera, an aluminium oxide ceramic, is used in this study due to its 16 times faster technique and high-quality results. It allows for easy corrections for mistakes and repairs that are not possible in other old techniques and is not fragile or delicate when working with copings/cores.

Turkom-Cera demonstrated equal or higher loads at fracture than currently accepted all-ceramic materials, making it acceptable for fabrication of anterior and posterior ceramic restorations. Indirect composites have advantages such as lower elastic moduli, less brittleness, easy repair, less rigidity, and less abraded opposing dentition.

In this study, RelyX ARC Adhesive Resin Cement was used for luting inlays. This dual-cure is suitable for bonding indirect restorations-like crowns, bridges, inlays, onlays, and endodontic post-cementation. The cement’s rheology is modified by a dimethacrylate polymer, allowing it to flow during seating and be easily cleaned up.

The present study found that 2 out of 10 cases in the direct composite inlay group showed post-operative sensitivity at baseline, which was not statistically significant. This could be due to exposure of dentinal tubules during the prolonged fabrication time. The other two groups did not record any post-operative sensitivity, possibly due to early temporization of the cavities and accurate bonding protocols. At 3, 6, and 9 months, none of the groups recorded any post-operative sensitivity. Interfacial staining and marginal discrepancy did not show significant changes at baseline and 3 months. Secondary caries did not record significant observations throughout the 9-month period, which is not found in controlled clinical trials. No patients reported significant pain in either group. Short-term clinical evidence shows little or no failure for direct inlay fabrications, but composite-dependent and short-lived improvements in physical properties. Indirect laboratory-processed composites showed better clinical performance compared to direct composite inlays, with ceramic inlays providing superior aesthetic results.

CONCLUSION

This study suggests aesthetic inlays as viable alternatives for posterior tooth restoration, ceramic inlays as a promising material for future research, and laboratory-fabricated indirect composite inlays as more viable options.
