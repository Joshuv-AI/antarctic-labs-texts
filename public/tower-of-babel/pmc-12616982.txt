Extreme thrombocytosis and ANCA-associated vasculitis in an adult patient: a case report
Authors: Andrés Hormaza-Jaramillo, Maria J Varela, Daniela Peñaloza Gonzalez, Sara Alejandra Benavides-Ibarra, Carlos Jimenez, David Aguirre-Valencia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe describe the case of a 39-year-old woman who presented with extreme reactive thrombocytosis as a prominent and unusual feature in the context of anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (myeloperoxidase, MPO)-associated vasculitis. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case in an adult patient where marked thrombocytosis was part of the initial presentation of ANCA-associated vasculitis.Case PresentationThe patient presented with a two-month history of constitutional symptoms and polyarticular pain. Laboratory evaluation revealed extreme thrombocytosis, impaired renal function, and positive MPO-ANCA. Renal biopsy demonstrated rapidly progressive pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis consistent with MPA. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of ANCA-associated vasculitis was established. Treatment with high-dose corticosteroids and intravenous cyclophosphamide led to clinical improvement, including resolution of thrombocytosis and recovery of renal function.ConclusionsAlthough the clinical features of ANCA-associated vasculitis were typical, the presence of extreme thrombocytosis may indicate a heightened inflammatory response and deserves careful evaluation. Early recognition and prompt treatment are essential to prevent irreversible organ damage and other complications.Keywords: Thrombocytosis, Immune complex diseases, Glomerulonephritis, ANCA-associated vasculitis, Case report

Introduction

Extreme thrombocytosis, defined as a platelet count exceeding 1,000 × 10⁹/L, is an uncommon hematologic finding in clinical practice. Although most frequently associated with myeloproliferative neoplasms, it can also occur as a reactive phenomenon in the setting of systemic inflammation. Among autoimmune diseases, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody ANCA-associated vasculitis has been identified as a rare cause of reactive thrombocytosis. However, the association between ANCA-associated vasculitis and extreme thrombocytosis is rarely described in the literature, with only a single pediatric case reported to date [1].

Thrombocytosis, defined as a platelet count above 450 × 10⁹/L, is a relatively common laboratory finding and is reactive in over 80% of cases [2]. It typically occurs in response to infections, trauma, malignancy, splenectomy, or chronic inflammatory states such as iron deficiency anemia. In cases of marked or extreme elevation, the differential diagnosis should include both primary (clonal) causes—such as myeloproliferative neoplasms—and secondary (reactive) etiologies, particularly in the context of systemic inflammation [1, 3].

Here, we present the case of a 39-year-old woman who developed extreme reactive thrombocytosis as part of the initial presentation of MPA, a subtype of ANCA-associated vasculitis, associated with pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis. To our knowledge, this represents the first documented case of such a presentation in an adult patient [3, 4].

Case presentation

A 39-year-old Colombian woman with a history of hypothyroidism and no significant family history presented with a two-month history of constitutional symptoms, including fatigue, cough, polyarticular pain in the hands and feet, morning stiffness, and subjective fever. On admission, her vital signs were blood pressure 90/60 mm/hg, heart rate 100 beats per minute, respiratory rate 18 breaths per minute, oxygen saturation 100% and temperature36.3 °C. She also reported numbness in the lower limbs. The patient had not received any prior treatment for her symptoms.

Physical examination revealed two purpuric lesions, 2–3 mm in diameter, on the second toe of the left foot. No signs of joint inflammation were observed, and peripheral pulses were normal.

Initial laboratory tests revealed thrombocytosis (1321 × 10^3 /µL), microcytic anemia (Hemoglobin 6.0 g/dL), iron deficiency (total iron 13.60 µg/dL), elevated ferritin (ferritin 563.0 ng/mL and impaired renal function (serum creatinine 2.45 mg/dL), Urinalysis showed proteinuria 75 mg/dL, Inflammatory markers were elevated, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate (66 mm/hour) and PCR (16.2 mg/dL) (Table 1).

General laboratory findings

During hospitalization, further investigations were performed to assess systemic diseases. Chest and abdominal CT scans showed multiple ill-defined pulmonary nodules and non-specific retroperitoneal lymphdenopathy. PET-CT did not reveal any hypermetabolic lesions. Bone marrow biopsy showed no evidence of neoplastic infiltration, and JAK2-V617F mutation testing was negative. Infectious disease screening was negative, and tumor markers were within normal limits (Table 2).

Based on clinical, laboratory and histopathological findings, a diagnosis of ANCA-associated vasculitis, specifically MPA, was established. Other autoimmune diseases were ruled out.

Treatment was initiated with intravenous methylprednisolone pulses (1 g daily for three days) followed by monthly intravenous cyclophosphamide (750 mg, or 15 mg/kg) for six months (six total doses). Upon completion of the inpatient regimen, the patient’s serum creatinine had decreased to 1.74 mg/dL, and platelet count to 750 × 10^3 µL. She was discharged with rheumatology follow-up and prescribed oral prednisolone (50 mg daily).

At six-month follow-up, the patient showed clinical and laboratory improvement, with normalization of renal function (serum creatinine 1.21 mg/dL), resolution of thrombocytosis (platelet count 393 × 10^3/µL), and negative repeat ANCA testing. No adverse events or treatment-related complications were reported.

Discussion

This case highlights that extreme reactive thrombocytosis may be part of the initial presentation of MPA, potentially serving as an early diagnostic clue in the context of systemic inflammation. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of such a presentation in an adult. The strength of this report lies in the detailed clinical documentation and the systematic exclusion of alternative causes of thrombocytosis.

Severe or extreme thrombocytosis requires a careful differential diagnosis. While clonal hematologic disorders must be considered, RT remains the most frequent cause [3, 4]. In this case, bone marrow biopsy and JAK2-V617F mutation testing were negative, effectively excluding myeloproliferative neoplasm. A diagnosis of ET was deemed unlikely due to the spontaneous normalization of platelet counts without cytoreductive therapy [1, 5]. Although such extensive evaluation is not routinely recommended in ANCA-associated vasculitis, unusually elevated or persistent thrombocytosis may justify further investigation. Longitudinal monitoring of platelet levels can help determine whether thrombocytosis reflects an inflammatory response or an independent hematologic process.

The patient demonstrated a favorable clinical course, with resolution of thrombocytosis, recovery of renal function, and ANCA seroconversion to negative. These findings support the effectiveness of early and intensive immunosuppressive therapy in ANCA-associated vasculitis. The presence of axonal polyneuropathy, observed in up to one-third of MPA cases [6], further reflects systemic involvement and the underlying inflammatory burden. Notably, the spontaneous decline in platelet count without cytoreductive treatment reinforces the reactive nature of thrombocytosis. No treatment-related complications were reported. This case emphasizes the importance of early therapeutic intervention, as delayed treatment in ANCA-associated vasculitis may lead to chronic kidney disease or death due to infection or uremia [7].

Extreme thrombocytosis is rarely observed in inflammatory diseases and is more characteristic of hematologic disorders or post-splenectomy states [8–11]. In most cases of reactive thrombocytosis, platelet counts remain below 800 × 10⁹/L [8], making the level observed in our patient (> 1,300 × 10⁹/L) particularly unusual. Although a Chinese cohort identified reactive thrombocytosis in up to 20% of patients with ANCA-associated vasculitis, specific platelet ranges were not reported [12], and only a single pediatric case has documented this finding in the setting of ANCA-associated glomerulonephritis [1]. Our case suggests that this phenomenon may also occur in adults and should be acknowledged as part of the broader clinical spectrum of ANCA-associated vasculitis.

This case contributes to the limited literature on this association and underscores the importance of considering thrombocytosis as a clinically relevant marker in systemic vasculitis. It also raises the possibility that ANCA and immune complex-mediated inflammation may influence both platelet production and renal injury. Although the exact pathophysiological mechanisms remain unclear, some studies propose that immune complexes may synergize with ANCA to amplify inflammation and glomerular damage, potentially promoting thrombopoiesis [13, 14]. Moreover, platelets themselves may participate in vascular injury through thrombin-PAR signaling and complement activation, contributing to a prothrombotic and proinflammatory state [15]. While thrombocytopenia is more frequently reported in ANCA-associated vasculitis [16, 17], the presence of extreme thrombocytosis, as in our case, may reflect a distinct and robust inflammatory response.

Regarding glomerulonephritis, a few animal studies have explored the relationship between platelet count and nephritis, though their results are inconsistent and not specific to ANCA vasculitis [1, 18, 19]. This case represents only the second reported instance of reactive thrombocytosis as a manifestation of ANCA-associated vasculitis with CGN, and notably, the first reported in an adult. It highlights the potential interplay between immune mechanisms and hematologic responses in ANCA-associated vasculitis, although further studies are needed to elucidate these associations.

Conclusions

This case illustrates that extreme reactive thrombocytosis can occur in association with ANCA-associated vasculitis, particularly MPA. Although rare, its presence may reflect a high degree of systemic inflammation and should not be overlooked. The coexistence of both conditions can complicate early diagnosis and underscores the importance of timely recognition and intervention to prevent irreversible organ damage. Further research is warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms linking ANCA antibodies, immune complex activity, and thrombopoiesis. Reporting such cases may broaden our understanding of the immunohematologic spectrum of ANCA-associated vasculitis.
