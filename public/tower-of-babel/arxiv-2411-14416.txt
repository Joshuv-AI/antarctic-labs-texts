𝖰𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QMA} vs. 𝖰𝖢𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QCMA} and Pseudorandomness
Jiahui Liu, Saachi Mutreja, Henry Yuen

Abstract
We study a longstanding question of Aaronson and Kuperberg on whether there exists a classical oracle separating 𝖰𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QMA} from 𝖰𝖢𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QCMA} . Settling this question in either direction would yield insight into the power of quantum proofs over classical proofs. We show that such an oracle exists if a certain quantum pseudorandomness conjecture holds. Roughly speaking, the conjecture posits that quantum algorithms cannot, by making few queries, distinguish between the uniform distribution over permutations versus permutations drawn from so-called “dense” distributions. Our result can be viewed as establishing a “win-win” scenario: either there is a classical oracle separation of 𝖰𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QMA} from 𝖰𝖢𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QCMA} , or there is quantum advantage in distinguishing pseudorandom distributions on permutations.

Introduction

In 2007, Aaronson and Kuperberg showed that there is a quantum oracle
U
relative to which
\mathsf{QMA}^{U}\neq\mathsf{QCMA}^{U}

[
AK07
]
. Since then, finding a

\mathsf{QCMA}
verifiers:

1.

Fefferman and Kimmel
[
FK18
]

[

]

]
separate
\mathsf{QMA}

2.

Liu, Li, Pelecanos, and Yamakawa
[

]
also obtain the separation relative to a classical oracle, but under the restriction that the quantum verifier’s query register is measured prior to querying the oracle. Ben-David and Kundu
[

]

We continue “chipping” away at the
\mathsf{QMA}

Theorem 1.1

(Main theorem, informal)
.

Assuming a quantum pseudorandomness conjecture (described below), there exists a classical oracle
F
such that
\mathsf{QMA}^{F}\neq\mathsf{QCMA}^{F}
.

In other words, we prove a

separation relative to a standard classical oracle and without any restrictions on the verifier.

1.1
A quantum pseudorandomness conjecture

To explain the quantum pseudorandomness conjecture, we first explain the notion of a
dense distribution
. We say that a random variable
\mathbf{F}
over
\{0,1\}^{N}
is
\delta
-dense
if for all subsets
S\subseteq[N]
of coordinates, the marginal distribution of
F\sim\mathbf{F}
restricted to the coordinates in
S
has min-entropy at least
(1-\delta)|S|
.
1
1

1

In the theoretical computer science literature this condition is often called “blockwise dense,” but we use “dense” for brevity.
In other words, every subset of coordinates has near maximal min-entropy.

We now present a simplified version of our quantum pseudorandomness conjecture.

Conjecture 1.2

(Simplified quantum pseudorandomness conjecture)
.

Let
A
be a quantum query algorithm that makes
T
queries to an oracle
F\in\{0,1\}^{N}
. Let
\mathbf{F}
denote a
\delta
-dense random variable over
\{0,1\}^{N}
. Then

\Big|\Pr_{F\sim\mathbf{U}}[A^{F}=1]-\Pr_{F\sim\mathbf{F}}[A^{F}=1]\Big|\leq\mathrm{poly}(T)\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\delta)

where
\mathbf{U}
denotes a uniformly random
N
-bit string, and the probabilities are over both the randomness of the algorithm
A
as well as the randomness of sampling
F
.

In other words, even though a dense random variable
\mathbf{F}
may be far from uniform
2
2

2

An example of a dense distribution is the uniform distribution over even-parity strings: this is
\frac{1}{N}
-dense, but is
\Omega(1)
far in statistical distance from the uniform distribution over all strings.
, no quantum algorithm can distinguish it from uniform by making few queries to an oracle sampled from it. In other words, the conjecture posits that dense distributions look
pseudorandom
to all polynomial-query quantum algorithms.

The conjecture is true for classical probabilistic query algorithms; this was essentially proved by Unruh
[
Unr07
]
(and later improved by Coretti, Dodis, Guo, and Steinberger
[
CDGS18
]
) in the context of studying the random oracle model (from cryptography) when the adversary is provided some short advice about the random oracle. Guo, Li, Liu, and Zhang
[
GLLZ21
]
first formulated the quantum pseudorandomness conjecture above and showed that it is closely linked to the
Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture
, formulated by Aaronson and Ambainis in
[
AA09
]
:

Conjecture 1.3

(Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture
[
AA09
]
)
.

Let
p:\{0,1\}^{N}\to[0,1]
be a degree-
d
real polynomial. Then there exists a coordinate
j\in[N]
such that
\mathrm{Inf}_{j}(p)\geq\mathrm{poly}(\mathrm{Var}(p)/d)
.

Here,
\mathrm{Inf}_{j}(p)
denotes the influence of the
j
’th variable in
p
and
\mathrm{Var}(p)
denotes
p
’s variance over the uniform distribution.

The link between
Conjecture

1.2
and the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture is that they both imply that every quantum query algorithm can be classically simulated on
most
inputs (with respect to the uniform distribution) with only a polynomial overhead.
Just like the
\mathsf{QMA}
vs.
\mathsf{QCMA}
question, the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture is also one of the central open problems in quantum complexity theory. Despite significant attention and a slew of partial results
[
OZ15
,
ABP19
,
BSdW22
,
Gut23
,
LZ23
,
Bha24
]
, the question remains open.

The difficulty of resolving the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture (and its conclusion about the classical simultability of quantum query algorithms) suggests that proving
Conjecture

1.2
will also be quite challenging. However there is some evidence that the pseudorandomness conjecture is true; in
Section

4
we prove a special case of it when both the density parameter
\delta
and the number of queries
T
is very small.

Lemma 1.4

(Weaker version of
Conjecture

1.2
)
.

For all
\epsilon>0
, for all
T
-query algorithms
A
, and for all
\delta
-dense random variables
\mathbf{F}
over
\{0,1\}^{N}
, we have

\Big|\Pr_{F\sim\mathbf{U}}[A^{F}=1]-\Pr_{F\sim\mathbf{F}}[A^{F}=1]\Big|\leq\sqrt{\delta}2^{O(T)}/\epsilon+O\Big((\delta N)^{T/2}\epsilon\Big)~.

For example, by setting
T=\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\log N}
,
\delta=2^{\sqrt{\log N}}/N
, and
\epsilon=N^{-1/3}
,
Lemma

1.4
implies that
T
-query algorithms cannot distinguish between
\delta
-dense distributions and the uniform distribution with more than
O(\sqrt{\delta}/\epsilon+N^{1/4-1/3})=o(1)
bias.

This lemma is analogous to a weaker version of the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture which establishes the existence of a variable with influence at least
\mathrm{poly}(\mathrm{Var}(p)/2^{O(d)})
, i.e., there is an exponential dependence on the degree of the polynomial. This weaker version was observed by Aaronson and Ambainis in
[
AA09
]
to follow from a powerful Fourier-analytic result of Dinur, Friedgut, Kindler, and O’Donnell
[
DFKO06
]
.

The pseudorandomness conjecture for random permutations.

We now state the version of the quantum pseudorandomness conjecture that is needed for our
\mathsf{QMA}
vs.
\mathsf{QCMA}
separation result. First, recall the
permutation oracle
model, in which the quantum algorithm can query a permutation
P:[N]\to[N]
and its inverse
P^{-1}
; that is, the algorithm can query the unitary operations
U_{P}:\ket{x,y}\mapsto\ket{x,y\oplus P(x)}
and
U_{P^{-1}}:\ket{x,y}\mapsto\ket{x,y\oplus P^{-1}(x)}
.

We furthermore consider the setting where the quantum algorithm can query a number of permutations
P_{1},\ldots,P_{R}
, where each
P_{r}
is a permutation on
[N_{r}]
for some integer
N_{r}
. To query permutation
r\in[R]
(resp., its inverse), the algorithm calls a unitary
U_{P_{r}}:\ket{x,y}\mapsto\ket{x,y\oplus P_{r}(x)}
(resp., the unitary
U_{P_{r}^{-1}}:\ket{x,y}\mapsto\ket{x,y\oplus P_{r}^{-1}(x)}
).

Conjecture 1.5

(Quantum pseudorandomness conjecture for random permutations)
.

Let
A
be a quantum query algorithm that makes
T
queries to a collection of permutations
(P_{1},\ldots,P_{R})
where each
P_{r}
permutes
[N_{r}]
for some integer
N_{r}
. Let
\mathbf{P}
denote a random variable over
S_{N_{1}}\times\cdots\times S_{N_{R}}
such that both
\mathbf{P}
and the inverse permutation
\mathbf{P}^{-1}
are
\delta
-dense. Then

\Big|\Pr_{P\sim\mathbf{Z}}[A^{P}=1]-\Pr_{P\sim\mathbf{P}}[A^{P}=1]\Big|\leq\mathrm{poly}(T,\log N_{1},\ldots,\log N_{R})\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\delta)

where
\mathbf{Z}
denotes a tuple of uniformly random permutations sampled from
S_{N_{1}}\times\cdots\times S_{N_{R}}
. Here the
\mathrm{poly}(\cdot)
notation denotes universal polynomials that are independent of
N_{1},\ldots,N_{r},T,\delta
(but can depend on
R
).

A sample of the random variable
\mathbf{P}
over the product set
S_{N_{1}}\times\cdots\times S_{N_{R}}
can be thought of as a string of length
N_{1}+\cdots+N_{R}
whose coordinates are indexed by pairs
\bigcup_{r=1}^{R}\bigcup_{j=1}^{N_{r}}\{(r,j)\}
. We say that
\mathbf{P}
(resp.
\mathbf{P}^{-1}
) is
\delta
-dense if for every subset
J\subseteq\bigcup_{r=1}^{R}\bigcup_{j=1}^{N_{r}}\{(r,j)\}
, the min-entropy of
\mathbf{P}
(resp.
\mathbf{P}^{-1}
) restricted to the elements in
J
is at least

(1-\delta)\sum_{r=1}^{R}\log\Big(N_{r}(N_{r}-1)\cdots(N_{r}-|J_{r}|+1)\Big),

where
J_{r}=J\cap\{(r,j)\}_{j}
. In other words, the min-entropy is
1-\delta
fraction of its maximal value.

The condition that the inverse permutation
\mathbf{P}^{-1}
is dense is necessary for the conjecture to hold. Consider the following example:
\mathbf{P}
is a uniformly random permutation over
[N]
conditioned on
\mathbf{P}^{-1}(1)
being an even integer. The forward permutation
\mathbf{P}
is
\delta
-dense for
\delta=O(1/N)
, but clearly
\mathbf{P}
is easily distinguishable from a uniformly random permutation by querying
\mathbf{P}^{-1}(1)
.
3
3

3

We are indebted to Mark Zhandry for pointing out this example.
In this example the inverse permutation
\mathbf{P}^{-1}
is not
\delta
-dense for small
\delta
, because the entropy of
\mathbf{P}^{-1}(1)
is
\frac{1}{2}\log N
.

In this conjecture, the number
R
of permutations is treated as a fixed constant, while the
N_{r}
’s are numbers growing to infinity and
T,\delta
are allowed to depend on the
N_{r}
’s.

The main difference from
Conjecture

1.2
is that the algorithms in
Conjecture

1.5
query random permutations, rather than random boolean functions. Furthermore, the model of oracle algorithm is different, in that
A
can compute the permutation both the “forwards” as well as “inverse” directions.
Thus it is not clear if it is comparable with
Conjecture

1.2
(i.e., whether one implies the other).

The main pieces of evidence for
Conjecture

1.5
come from (a) its resemblance to the random oracle model formulation of
Conjecture

1.2
, and (b) a proof for the special case of classical probabilistic query algorithms; this was shown by Coretti, Dodis, and Guo
[
CDG18
, Claim 18]
in the context of studying the power of auxiliary information in the random permutation model in cryptography.
4
4

4

We note that the statement of Claim 18 in
[
CDG18
]
does not explicitly state the condition that the inverse permutation is dense. However, as illustrated by the example above, this is necessary, and their proof implicitly uses this condition.
It is an interesting question to find additional evidence for
Conjecture

1.5
(such as proving a permutation analogue of
Lemma

1.4
) – or refuting it.

Looking ahead, we can interpret our main result (
Theorem

1.1
) as spelling out a “win-win” scenario: either quantum proofs are more powerful than classical proofs (i.e., there is a classical oracle separating
\mathsf{QMA}
from
\mathsf{QCMA}
), or there is quantum advantage in distinguishing between dense permutation distributions from the uniform distribution (or both could be true!). The possibility of quantum advantage for this task would be rather interesting, as it could have implications for the post-quantum (in)security of various ciphers and hashes, which are often modeled with random permutations (see
[
CDG18
]
for more discussion about this).

1.2
The conditional oracle separation

We now describe the oracle problem we use for the conditional
\mathsf{QMA}
vs.
\mathsf{QCMA}
separation. Informally, the oracle is promised to encode an exponentially-large constant-degree graph that has either (at least) two connected components, or is a connected expander graph; the task is to distinguish between the two cases. This is a slight simplification of the problem considered by Natarajan and Nirkhe
[
NN23
]
in their
\mathsf{QMA}
vs.
\mathsf{QCMA}
separation in the randomized oracle model, except they consider distinguishing between having (exponentially) many connected components and having a single connected component.

The graph oracle model.

First, we specify the oracle model. Fix integers
n,R\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
N=2^{n}
. The oracles we consider are functions
F:[R]\times[N]\to[N]
such that for all
r\in[R]
,
F(r,\cdot)
describes a perfect matching on
[N]
:
F(r,x)=y
if and only if
F(r,y)=x
. We call such a function
F
a
graph oracle
because it naturally corresponds to a (multi)graph
G_{F}
with vertex set
[N]
and there is an edge
(x,y)
if there exists an
r
such that
F(r,x)=y
. In fact, the graph oracle
F
encodes more information in that each edge
(x,y)
has a
color
corresponding to the variable
r
such that
F(r,x)=y
.

A quantum query algorithm can query a graph oracle
F
via the unitary
U_{F}:\ket{r,x,u}\mapsto\ket{r,x,u\oplus F_{r}(x)}
where
\oplus
denotes addition over the binary field (we identify
[N]
with binary strings
\{0,1\}^{n}
).

In this paper we think of
R
as a fixed constant (indeed,
R=3
suffices) and
N
as the growing parameter. Let
\Delta>0
be such that a random degree-
R
graph formed by taking the union of
R
random perfect matchings of
[N]
yields a
\Delta
-expander (i.e., the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of the normalized graph Laplacian is at least
\Delta
) with high probability (see
Lemma

2.3
).

The oracle problem.

Now we formally define the oracle problem, which we call
Components
. Consider two sets
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}})
of graph oracles where

•

F\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}
if and only if there exists a subset
S\subseteq[N]
with
|S|=N/2
such that there are no edges between
S
and
\overline{S}
in
G_{F}
.

•

F\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}
if and only if the graph
G_{F}
is a
\Delta
-expander.

The
Components
problem is to distinguish whether a given graph oracle
F
is in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}
or
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}
, promised that one is that case.

Natarajan and Nirkhe
[
NN23
]
show that the
Components
can be solved in the
\mathsf{QMA}
model: given oracle access to an instance
F\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}
, the prover can send the verifier the following
\log N
-qubit state:

\ket{\psi}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}}\sum_{x\in S}\ket{x}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}}\sum_{x\notin S}\ket{x}~.

The quantum verifier performs one of two tests:
(a) check the state is orthogonal to the uniform superposition over
all
vertices, or (b) check the state is invariant under permuting the vertices according to
F
. Since
F\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}
, the verifier will accept with probability
1
. On the other hand, if
F\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}
, then it can be shown that
no
state can cause the verifier to accept with high probability. This verification procedure runs in time
\mathrm{poly}(\log N)
.

Can the
Components
problem be efficiently solved by a quantum verifier given a classical witness?
Natarajan and Nirkhe showed the following “distribution testing” lower bound: all
\mathsf{QCMA}
proof systems must fail at solving (their version of) the
Components
problem when the verifier only makes
\mathrm{poly}(\log N)
queries to the oracle and the witness length is
\mathrm{poly}(\log N)
– under the assumption that the classical witness only depends on the
partition
of vertices of the underlying graph
G_{F}
into connected components, and not on how the vertices are connected (i.e. the edges).

For our conditional separation, we trade the assumption about the witness structure for a plausible assumption about the pseudorandomness of dense distributions.

1.3
Our approach

The proof of
Theorem

1.1
proceeds in two steps. First, assume that there exists an efficient
\mathsf{QCMA}
verifier for the
Components
problem. We show that
Conjecture

1.5
implies that, without loss of generality, the witness specifies a
\mathrm{poly}(\log N)
-sized list of vertices and edges in the graph. That is, the witness has
some
amount of structure, albeit less structure than what is assumed in
[
NN23
]
.

In the second step, we show that such a witness cannot help distinguish between Yes and No instances of
Components
. Intuitively, the verifier should not be able to tell by making
\mathrm{poly}(\log N)
queries to the oracle whether the specified vertices and edges are part of the same component or belong to different components.

Applying the pseudorandomness conjecture.

The connection between our oracle structure and pseudorandomness in the first step of the proof arises as follows. If the classical witness is short (e.g.,
\mathrm{poly}(\log N)
length), then by a standard counting argument, there must exist a set of Yes instances corresponding to a
maximizing witness

w^{*}
, one which is associated with the maximum number of Yes instances such that the
\mathsf{QCMA}
verifier accepts. We call the set of these Yes instances
\mathcal{L}_{w^{*}}
. Now, hardwiring
w^{*}
into the supposed
\mathsf{QCMA}
verifier solving the
Components
problem, we can obtain a
\mathrm{poly}(\log N)
-query algorithm that does
not
use a witness but can distinguish between instances in
\mathcal{L}_{w^{*}}
and instances in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}
. Since the maximizing witness
w^{*}
is short, the set
\mathcal{L}_{w^{*}}
occupies at least
2^{-\mathrm{poly}(\log N)}
fraction of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}
, and therefore the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{L}_{w^{*}}
has min-entropy at least
\log|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}|-\mathrm{poly}(\log N)
. This argument is standard in all previous works studying
\mathsf{QMA}
vs
\mathsf{QCMA}
separations
[
AK07
,
FK18
,
NN23
,
LLPY24
]
.

It is not clear at a first sight why we can apply a conjecture on pseudorandom permutation oracles to this high min-entropy distribution over graph oracles. To do this, we introduce a “raw permutation” model of viewing each graph oracle
G\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}\cup\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}
. In more detail, we show that any algorithm querying a
Components
oracle can be viewed as an algorithm that queries a small number of independently-chosen permutations
X,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{R},Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{R}
. The permutation
X:[N]\to[N]
partitions
[N]=S\cup\overline{S}
by letting
S
be the image of
\{1,2,\ldots,N/2\}
under
X
, and
\overline{S}
the complement. Then, for each
r\in[R]
, the permutations
Y_{r},Z_{r}:[N/2]\to[N/2]
specifies a matching on
S
and
\overline{S}
, respectively.

Furthermore, the uniform distribution over raw permutations induces a uniform distribution over corresponding graph oracles in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}
. Conversely, the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{L}_{w^{*}}
induces a high-min entropy distribution over the set
\mathcal{P}_{w^{*}}
of all “raw permutations”
XY_{1}\cdots Y_{R}Z_{1}\cdots Z_{R}
that give rise to a graph oracle
F\in\mathcal{L}_{w^{*}}
. By a well-known decomposition lemma for high min-entropy distributions from complexity theory
[
GLM+16
,
KMR21
]
and cryptography
[
CDGS18
,
CDG18
,
GLLZ21
]
, the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{P}_{w^{*}}
can be decomposed into a convex combination of distributions over random permutations such that both the forward and inverse distributions are
(k,\delta)
-dense, for
k=\mathrm{poly}(\log N)
and
\delta=\mathrm{poly}(\log N)/N
. Here, a
(k,\delta)
-dense distribution is one that is fixed to a deterministic value in some
k
coordinates, but is
\delta
-dense everywhere else.

Then, using Conjecture
1.5
, each dense source in the convex combination is indistinguishable (by an efficient query algorithm) from a
bit-fixing source
. In the context of raw permutation model, a bit-fixed raw permutation would imply that the
X
,
\{Y_{r}\}_{r\in[R]}
and
\{Z_{r}\}_{r\in[R]}
permutations are fixed in a small number of coordinates in
[N]
or
[N/2]
, and are uniform otherwise. Translated to graph oracles, these fixings correspond to at most
\mathrm{poly}(\log N)
fixed vertices and edges; let
\rho
denote this
graph fixing data
. Let
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}^{\rho}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}^{\rho}
denote the graph oracles in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}
respectively that are consistent with
\rho
. The verifier with the hardwired witness
w^{*}
thus can distinguish between
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}^{\rho}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}^{\rho}
with few queries.

Uselessness of the fixed coordinates.

The second step is to show that revealing
\mathrm{poly}(\log N)
-size graph fixing data
\rho
cannot help a quantum algorithm distinguish between Yes and No instances of the
Components
problem.
We prove via a reduction: we show that if there was an efficient query algorithm
A
that distinguishes between
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}^{\rho}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}^{\rho}
, then it can be transformed into an efficient query quantum algorithm
M
that solves the original
Components
problem (without any witness).

Our reduction takes as input a uniformly random graph oracle
F\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}\cup\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}
, and
plants
the graph fixing data
\rho
in the graph
F
by only making
\mathrm{poly}(\log N)
queries to
F
. Let
F^{\prime}
denote the resulting graph oracle. Then, it simulates the algorithm
A
with the oracle
F^{\prime}
. When
F\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}
, then
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}^{\rho}
and therefore
A
would reject with high probability.

The main challenge is to prove that when
F
is sampled uniformly at random from
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}
, the distribution over
F^{\prime}
is uniform over
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}^{\rho}
. Once this is shown, then we deduce that there is a
\mathrm{poly}(\log N)
-query algorithm for the
Components
problem, which contradicts
(a slightly modified version of) the query lower bound of Ambainis, Childs, and Liu
[
ACL11
]
on distinguishing whether a given graph oracle represents an expander or a graph with two connected components. We note that this part of the proof is directly inspired by the approach of Natarajan and Nirkhe
[
NN23
]
, who also reduce to the same query lower bound of
[
ACL11
]
.

Putting everything together, there cannot be an efficient
\mathsf{QCMA}
verifier for the
Components
problem, assuming the pseudorandomness conjecture.

1.4
An unconditional
\mathsf{QMA}
vs.
\mathsf{QCMA}
separation for interactive games and distributional oracles

In addition to the previous conditional oracle separation result, we
give a simple proof of an
interactive version
of
\mathsf{QMA}/\mathsf{QCMA}
separation, which directly leads to a simplified proof of the distributional oracle separation in
[
NN23
,
LLPY24
]
.
Interactive games are a useful notion in cryptography and complexity theory. The techniques employed here to analyze the lower bound for interactive games have been previously utilized widely in addressing the non-uniformity of algorithms – algorithms that have an unbounded preprocessing stage to produce an advice (in our case, a witness) that helps a bounded second stage algorithm to solve a challenge. This formulation of interactive games offers a clear and alternative perspective on the distributional oracle separation explored in earlier works, highlighting their underlying conceptual similarity.

The oracle has a similar structure to the one used in the
Components
problem, but is slightly different. We then observe that this
\mathsf{QMA}/\mathsf{QCMA}
separation for interactive games almost directly gives a
\mathsf{QMA}/\mathsf{QCMA}
separation with a distributional oracle, reproving the results of
[
NN23
,
LLPY24
]
.

We describe the high level idea of our interactive game separation.
The oracle
F
we give out still represents a disconnected graph with two highly connected components in the Yes-instance and a connected graph in the No-instance. But the difference is that each sub-oracle
F(r,\cdot)
specifies a single-cycle random permutation instead of any permutation and there are exponentially many sub-oracles
F(r,\cdot)
.

We first define an interactive variant of the
\mathsf{QMA}/\mathsf{QCMA}
protocol.
In the interactive game, prover
P
is first given access to the entire oracle
F(\cdot,\cdot)
, where each sub-oracle
F(r,\cdot)
is either a Yes oracle: two disjoint cycles, each on
N/2
vertices (the partitions are fixed for all Yes oracles); or one single cycle on all
N
vertices.
P
then produces a witness. When it comes to a challenge phase, the challenger will sample a random
r\leftarrow[R]
. Now the verifier
V
with the witness should output whether
F(r,\cdot)
is a Yes-oracle or No-oracle.

The
\mathsf{QMA}
upper bound is essentially the same as for the
Components
problem: a uniform superposition over all vertices except vertices in one component has a
1
phase and vertices in the other component has a
-1
phase.

We then give an
\mathsf{QCMA}
lower bound for this interactive variant through a technique that removes the use of witness in our analysis with a sequentially repeated game (multi-instance security). Suppose there is a polynomial-query
\mathsf{QCMA}
verifer that can distinguish two oracles with a some arbitrary polynomial-size classical witness, then we can build a verifier that guesses a witness and succeeds in winning a sequentially repeated version of the interactive oracle distinguishing game, by breaking the sequential repetition soundness. Let us denote the interactive game as
G
and the
k
-sequentially repeated game as
G^{\otimes k}
.

Since the individual games in
G^{\otimes k}
are being played sequentially and each challenge is sampled independently with a fresh choice of
r_{i}
for
F(r_{i},\cdot)
at each round, we can argue that the winning probability decays exponentially by a lemma in
[
CGLQ20
]
.

What’s left is to analyze the probability of success in a single instance
G
. This reduces to the problem of deciding whether a given permutation oracle is a single cycle or a product of two equal length cycles. This should be hard to do using a polynomial number of queries, and thus the success probability should be at most
\frac{1}{2}+\eta

for some negligible quantity
\eta
, through Ambainis’s adversary method
[
Amb00
]
.
Therefore, the success probability in
G^{\otimes k}
is at most
(\frac{1}{2}+\eta)^{k}
.

Finally, we can leverage the loss in guessing the advice.
When we set the repetition number
k
to be a proper superpolynomial in terms of
\log N
, then we can observe that the winning probability in a single-instance interactive game
with advice
is also upper bounded by
1/2+\mathsf{negl}(\log N)
.

In the end, we show a simple reduction from a distributional oracle QMA/QCMA game to our interactive QMA/QCMA game.

1.5
Relation with prior and concurrent work

We briefly compare our results and approach with prior work. The
Components
problem and variants of it were previously studied by Lutomirski
[
Lut11
]
, Fefferman and Kimmel
[
FK18
]
, Natarajan and Nirkhe
[
NN23
]
, and Bassirian, Fefferman, and Marwaha
[
BFM23
]
.
5
5

5

In fact, it was pointed out to us by Kunal Marwaha that Lutomirski had already conjectured that the
Components
problem was a candidate to separate
\mathsf{QMA}
from
\mathsf{QCMA}
in
[
Lut11
]
.

Our
\mathsf{QCMA}
lower bound proof is a marriage of the proof of
[
NN23
]
and the proof approach of Li, Liu, Pelecanos, and Yamakawa
[
LLPY24
]
. The latter prove a
\mathsf{QCMA}
lower bound assuming that the verifier only makes classical queries to the oracle. In this case, the verifier is essentially a classical randomized verifier (because we only consider query complexity, not time complexity). The problem they use is based on the Yamakawa-Zhandry problem, which was originally used to show verifiable quantum advantage in the random oracle model
[
YZ24
]
. They argue that a
\mathsf{QCMA}
verifier for their problem can be converted into an efficient classical query algorithm that distinguishes between a “bit-fixed” distributions over Yes instances from all No instances. This conversion uses the pseudorandomness conjecture for random functions (

) for classical query algorithms, which was proved by
[
CDGS18

. However,
[

shows that such a classical query algorithm does not exist. It is not clear when allowing the

verifier to make quantum queries in their setting, whether a query lower bound can be obtained for their problem, even assuming a quantum bit-fixing conjecture like we do in this work.

Ben-David and Kundu
[
BK24
]
analyze
\mathsf{QCMA}

[
LLPY24
]
does not admit such bounded-adaptivity
\mathsf{QCMA}

Finally, we mention a paper
[
Zha24
]
that recently appeared as we were preparing to post this manuscript on arXiv. Like us, Zhandry also proves a
\mathsf{QMA}
vs.
\mathsf{QCMA}
separation conditional on a conjecture. The conjecture, roughly speaking, stipulates that distributions that are
approximately

k
-wise independent (in a certain multiplicative error sense) can be “rounded” to a nearby distribution that is
exactly

k
-wise independent. Zhandry considers the problem of distinguishing whether a sparse subset

has superpolynomial size or is empty, given membership queries to

shows that there is a
\mathsf{QMA}

\mathsf{QCMA}
verifier for the problem.

Our conjecture and Zhandry’s conjecture share some similarities; his conjecture can also be interpreted as a pseudorandomness statement, as the result of
[

implies that (exactly)

1.6
Next steps

The main contribution of our paper is to connect the
\mathsf{QMA}
vs.
\mathsf{QCMA}
oracle separation question with a question about pseudorandomness against (query-)efficient quantum algorithms. This latter question is in turn deeply related to the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture, which is one of the most important questions in quantum complexity theory.
We believe this connection suggests several immediate avenues for exploration.

1.

What are the relationships between
Conjecture

1.2
(the pseudorandomness conjecture in the random oracle model),
Conjecture

1.3
(the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture), and
Conjecture

1.5
(the pseudorandomness conjecture in the random permutation model)?
For example, does
Conjecture

1.2
imply
Conjecture

1.5
or vice-versa?

2.

Can
Conjecture

1.5
be deduced as a consequence of a simpler version of
Conjecture

1.5
that only refers to a
single
permutation oracle (i.e.
R=1
)?

3.

Can weaker versions of the quantum pseudorandomness conjecture (
Conjecture

1.5
) be proved? For example, in analogy to
Lemma

1.4
, can the conjecture be proved where the dependence on
T
is exponential (rather than polynomial)? Another question is whether the conjecture can be proved if the number of
adaptive rounds
of queries is bounded? This would immediately give an alternate proof of the bounded-adaptivity lower bound of Ben-David and Kundu
[
BK24
]
.

4.

The oracle separations of Li, Liu, Pelecanos, and Yamakawa
[
LLPY24
]
and Ben-David and Kundu
[
BK24
]
are based on the Yamakawa-Zhandry problem
[
YZ24
]
. An attractive feature of these problems is that the Yamakawa-Zhandry problem is based on random boolean functions (rather than on random permutations as the
Components
problem is). Can a full
\mathsf{QMA}
vs.
\mathsf{QCMA}
separation be obtained from a problem inspired by Yamakawa-Zhandry, assuming
Conjecture

1.2
(the pseudorandomness conjecture in the random oracle model)?

5.

Can it be shown that, for the specific oracle problems considered in this paper or in other works (e.g., the
Components
problem), a classical witness in a
\mathsf{QCMA}
protocol effectively fixes a subset of coordinates of the oracle? In our paper we relied on the full power of the pseudorandomness conjecture to argue this, but it is plausible that this can be directly argued for the

problem, thus avoiding having to resolve the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture or related questions (although that is also a worthwhile outcome!).

2.1
Random variables

We will use boldfaced letters
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\ldots
to denote random variables. To denote a specific instantiation of a random variable
\mathbf{X}
, we will use non-boldfaced variables
X
. If
\mathbf{X}
takes values in
\Sigma^{n}
for some finite alphabet
\Sigma

I\subseteq[n]
we write
\mathbf{X}_{I}
to denote the random variable corresponding to the restriction of
\mathbf{X}
to the coordinates in

\mathbf{X}
, denoted by
\mathrm{supp}(\mathbf{X})

of the random variable
\mathbf{X}
over a finite set, denoted by

, is defined to be
\min_{X\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathbf{X})}\log\frac{1}{\Pr(\mathbf{X}=X)}
.

2.2
Graphs and expansion

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph where there can be multiple edges between a pair of vertices. Let
A
denote the adjacency matrix of
G
, i.e.,
A_{uv}
is the number of edges between vertices
u,v\in V
. Suppose that
G
is
R
-regular; i.e., each vertex has
R
neighbors. Then the normalized Laplacian of
G
is given by the matrix
\mathcal{L}=I-\frac{1}{R}A
. The normalized Laplacian is a positive semidefinite matrix and its maximum eigenvalue is at most
1
. The spectral gap
\Delta
of
\mathcal{L}
is its second smallest eigenvalue (which may be
0
if the kernel of
\mathcal{L}
has dimension greater than
1
). We say that a graph
G
is a
\Delta
-expander if its spectral gap is at least
\Delta
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the normalized Laplacian of a graph
G
with spectral gap
\Delta
. Then for all vectors
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{v\in V}\alpha_{v}\ket{v}
, we have

\|\ket{\psi}-\ket{\overline{\psi}}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\Delta}\bra{\psi}\mathcal{L}\ket{\psi}

where
\ket{\overline{\psi}}
denotes the vector where every entry is
\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v}\alpha_{v}
.

The following is a well-known fact that relates the mixing time of a random walk on graph with its spectral gap.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G
be a
R
-regular
\Delta
-expander graph with
N
vertices. Consider the following
t
-step random walk
\mathbf{W}
starting at some vertex
v
of the graph: at each time step stay in place with probability
1/2
, and move to a uniformly random neighbor with probability
1/2
. The probability distribution over the ending vertex of the walk
\mathbf{W}
is
N\Delta^{t}
-close in total variation distance from the uniform distribution over the graph.

Proof.

A proof of this can be founded in, e.g.,
[
Gol11
]
.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
G
be a
R
-regular
\Delta
-expander graph with
N
vertices. Let
K
be an integer, and consider a
Kt
-step random walk
\mathbf{W}
as described in
Lemma

2.1
. Let
\mathbf{E}=(\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{K})
denote a sequence of vertices where
\mathbf{e}_{k}
is the

\mathbf{W}
. Then the distribution of
\mathbf{E}
is
KN\Delta^{t}
-close to the uniform distribution over

Proof.

This follows applying a hybrid argument to

2.1

∎

Lemma 2.3

(Union of

2.3
Quantum query lower bounds

We collect some quantum query lower bounds that will be used to prove both our
\mathsf{QCMA}
lower bound (
Section

6
) and the interactive game lower bound (
Section

7
).

The first lower bound is the so-called “hybrid method” due to Bennett, Brassard, Bernstein, and Vazirani
[
BBBV97
]
; this roughly states that if a quantum query algorithm makes a few queries to an oracle, then the algorithm cannot “notice” small changes to most places in the oracle. We can model the state of a quantum query algorithm right before a query as some superposition

\ket{\phi}=\sum_{b,x,y,w}\alpha_{b,x,y,w}\ket{b,x,y,w}

where
\ket{b}
denotes a single-qubit control register,
\ket{x}
denotes the query input register,
\ket{y}
denotes the query target register, and
\ket{w}
denotes the workspace. Define the following quantity, known as the
query weight
of
A
on input
x
:

W_{x}(\ket{\phi})=\sum_{b,y,w}|\alpha_{b,x,y,w}|^{2}~.

After the query to the (controlled) oracle
cO
the state becomes

cO\,\ket{\phi}=\sum_{b,x,y,w}\alpha_{b,x,y,w}\ket{b,x,y\oplus bO(x),w}

where by
bO(x)
we mean the zero string if
b=0
and otherwise
O(x)
.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
BBBV97
]
)
.

Let
A
denote a
T
-query quantum algorithm. Let
O:[N]\to[M]
be an oracle and let
\ket{\phi_{i}}
denote the intermediate state of
A
right before the
i
’th query to
O
. Let
F\subseteq[N]
be a set of query inputs, and let
\widetilde{O}
denote an oracle that is identical to
O
on all inputs
[N]\setminus F
. Then

\Big\|\ket{\phi_{T+1}}-\ket{\widetilde{\phi}_{T+1}}\Big\|\leq\sqrt{T\sum_{x\in F}\sum_{i=1}^{T}W_{x}(\ket{\phi_{i}})}

where
\ket{\phi_{T}},\ket{\widetilde{\phi}_{T}}
are the final states of the algorithm
A
when querying oracles
O
and
\widetilde{O}
, respectively.

Lower bound for testing expansion.

In our
\mathsf{QCMA}
lower bound proof we convert a hypothetical
\mathsf{QCMA}
verifier for the
Components
problem into a query-efficient algorithm for the
Components
problem (
without
using a witness). However, Ambainis, Childs, and Liu
[
ACL11
]
proved a
\Omega(N^{1/4})
lower bound on quantum algorithms that can test whether an
N
-vertex bounded-degree graph is an expander or far from being an expander. Their lower bound is
almost
what we need: they show that no algorithm making
o(N^{1/4})
queries to the graph can distinguish between whether the graph is drawn from a distribution over two disconnected random graphs, or a single connected random graph.

In more detail, their distribution over random graphs are chosen as follows. Let
M
be divisible by
4
, and let
N
be such that
M=N+\Theta(N^{0.9})
. A Yes instance as sampled in two steps.

1.

Partition
[M]
into 2 equal sets
V_{1},V_{2}
. For each set
V_{j}
, choose
R
random perfect matchings on
V_{j}
and take their union. Let
G^{\prime}
be the resulting graph.

2.

Pick
N
vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}
at random as follows: for each
v_{i}
, pick
j\in\{1,2\}
uniformly at random, and then sample
v_{i}
uniformly at random from
V_{j}
, conditioned on not being sampled before. Let
G
be the induced subgraph of
G^{\prime}
on the vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}
.

A No instance is sampled similarly, except there is only one component.

Without the subsampling step (the second step above), the distributions would be identical to the uniform distributions over
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}
respectively of the
Components
problem. However the subsampling step is helpful for the lower bound proof
[
ACL11
]
, which employs the polynomial method of
[
BBC+01
]
in a sophisticated way.

However it is more useful for us to have a lower bound for distinguishing between (the uniform distributions over)
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}
. We deduce such a lower bound as a corollary of
[
ACL11
]
, using the BBBV theorem (
Theorem

2.4
).

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
A
be a quantum algorithm that makes
o(N^{1/20})
queries. Then

\Big|\Pr_{F\sim\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}}[A^{F}=1]-\Pr_{F\sim\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}}[A^{F}=1]\Big|\leq\frac{1}{1000}.

Proof.

The main idea is that the subsampling step omits so few vertices (at most a
\Theta(N^{-0.1})
fraction) that query-efficient algorithms should not be able to detect if the subsampling step was skipped.

Suppose that any quantum algorithm
A
is given oracle
F^{\prime}
corresponding to graph
G^{\prime}
in one world and given oracle
F
for graph
G
in another world (either in the Yes-instance case or in the No-instance case), we argue that
A
’s states in these two worlds have negligible difference in trace distance at the end of its algorithm. Therefore we can apply the
[
ACL11
]
lower bound to any algorithm with access to
F^{\prime}
.

Ambainis, Childs, and Liu
[
ACL11
]
use the same graph oracle model as we do: the oracle
F(r,u)
returns the vertex that is matched with
u
in the
r
’th matching. When we change an oracle
F^{\prime}
to
F
, we apply the following changes: (1) the vertices
v\in V
are chosen randomly from
V^{\prime}=[M]
in the subsampling step. Let us denote the set of chosen vertices as
V
, where
|V|=N
. We first implement an intermediate oracle
F_{0}
.
For all
v\in V^{\prime},v\notin V
where
V^{\prime}
is the vertex set in
G^{\prime}
,
F_{0}(r,v)=\bot
for all

; for all

such that
u\notin V
, we let
F_{0}(r,v)=\bot
. (2) We rename the vertices
v\in V
in
1,2,\cdots,N

according to the order sampled and call this mapping
\varphi
. For
v\notin V
, we design
\varphi
such that
\varphi(v)
also assigns
v
to some label in
\{N+1,\ldots,M\}
in arbitrary fashion.

We then create oracle

(v,r),v\in[N]
, we map it to
v^{\prime}=\varphi^{-1}(v)
and query oracle
F_{0}
on
(v^{\prime},r)
to get result
u^{\prime}
(or
\bot
) and output
u=\varphi(u^{\prime})
or
\bot
. We can observe that
F_{0}
and

are essentially the same oracle with only renaming on the vertices using oracle

. If there is a quantum algorithm with access to oracle

, one can perfectly simulate its behavior and output with oracle access to

and
\varphi,\varphi^{-1}
. Since

is only a relabeling of all vertices, it will not affect the performance of the algorithm. Therefore, from now on, we analyze the quantum algorithm

We then compute the fraction of changes on the output of oracle

F_{0}
from
F^{\prime}
. These are

(r,v)
satisfies that
F^{\prime}(r,v)\neq\bot,F_{0}(r,v)=\bot
. These correspond to either the case where vertex

is not picked into set

or the case where
v

on matching

is not picked into set
V
in the above oracle change procedure.
The expected fraction of output changes is:

\displaystyle\Pr_{(r,v)}[F_{0}(r,v)=\bot,F^{\prime}[r,v]\neq\bot]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr_{(r,v)}[(v\in V^{\prime},v\notin V)\vee(F^{\prime}(r,v)\in V^{\prime},F^{\prime}(r,v)\notin V)]\leq\frac{2N^{0.9}}{N+\Theta(N^{0.9})}=\Theta\Big(\frac{1}{N^{1/10}}\Big)

Let
T
be the number of queries of

. We then have
\sum^{i=T}_{i,F_{0}(v,r)\neq F^{\prime}(r,v)}W_{r,v}(\ket{\phi_{i}})\leq\Theta(\frac{T}{N^{1/10}})
, where

is the squared amplitude of query weight of the

-th query on input

(r,v)
. Then by

2.4
, we have that
\|\ket{\phi_{T+1}^{\prime}}-\ket{\phi_{T+1}}\|\leq\frac{T}{N^{1/20}}
where
\ket{\phi_{T+1}^{\prime}}
and

are the algorithm’s states in the world using oracle

and

respectively. Since by assumption

is an algorithm that can distinguish between the Yes and No instances of the subsampled distributions described above with advantage

. However,

]
also proved that any algorithm that does so must make at least

3
Min-entropy decompositions for permutations

The quantum pseudorandomness conjectures discussed in the Introduction are centered around notions of “dense” random variables that are either uniform over a product alphabet
\Sigma^{N}
or the set of permutations on
[N]
. Such dense random variables arise as follows: every distribution over strings or permutations with sufficiently high min-entropy can be approximately decomposed into a convex combination of distributions where a small subset of coordinates are fixed to a deterministic value, and the rest of the coordinates form a dense distribution. This decomposition lemma has been used in numerous settings in theoretical computer science, ranging from communication complexity lower bounds
[
GLM+16
,
KMR21
]
to analyzing security in the presence of auxiliary information
[
Unr07
,
CDGS18
,
GLLZ21
]
.

We present a self-contained presentation of the definition of dense distributions over (tuples of) permutations, and the min-entropy decomposition lemma. In what follows, for a function
Z:\Sigma\to\Sigma
and a subset
I\subseteq\Sigma
, we let
Z(I)
denote the vector
\Sigma^{I}
whose coordinates are the evaluations of
Z
on the elements of
I
. We write
\mathrm{image}(Z(I))=\{a\in\Sigma:x(b)=a\text{ for some $b\in I$}\}
to denote the set of evaluations.

We say that a random function
\mathbf{Z}:\Sigma\to\Sigma
for some finite alphabet
\Sigma
is a
random permutation
if every instantiation of
\mathbf{Z}
is a bijection between
\Sigma
and itself. We let
\mathbf{Z}^{-1}
denote the random function induced by sampling
Z\sim\mathbf{Z}
, and then taking the inverse
Z^{-1}
(which is well-defined because
Z
is a permutation).

Definition 3.1

(Nice random permutations)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet.
We say that a random permutation
\mathbf{Z}:\Sigma\to\Sigma
is
nice
if for all disjoint subsets
I,J\subseteq\Sigma
and
z_{J}\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathbf{Z}(J))
(the support of the marginal distribution of
\mathbf{Z}
on coordinates
J
),

A canonical example of a nice random permutation is a uniformly random permutation

.

Definition 3.2

(Dense random permutations)

4
Quantum pseudorandomness conjectures

4.1
A link between
Conjecture

1.2
and the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture

The pseudorandomness conjecture in the random oracle model (
Conjecture

1.2
) is very similar to Conjecture 4 from
[
GLLZ21
]
, which was shown to imply that quantum query algorithms can be efficiently simulated by classical deterministic algorithms over the uniform distribution (this is the main quantum computing consequence of the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture
[
AA09
]
). We present a self-contained proof that
Conjecture

1.2
also implies a particular reformulation of the conjecture about classical simulability of quantum algorithms (i.e. Conjecture 3 of
[
GLLZ21
]
).

Proposition 2
.

Assume that
Conjecture

1.2
is true. Let
A
denote a
T
-query quantum algorithm that queries a uniformly-random
N
-bit string
X\in\{0,1\}^{N}
. Let
\mathrm{Var}(A)
denote

\mathrm{Var}(A):=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim\mathbf{U}}\Big(\Pr[A^{X}=1]^{2}\Big)-\Big(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim\mathbf{U}}\Pr[A^{X}=1]\Big)^{2}

where the expectation is over the uniform random variable
\mathbf{U}
on
\{0,1\}^{N}
, and the probabilities are over the randomness of the algorithm
A
only.
Then there exists a subset
I\subseteq[N]
of size at most
\mathrm{poly}(T/\mathrm{Var}(A))
and a string
W\in\{0,1\}^{I}
such that

\Big|\Pr_{Z\sim\mathbf{Z}}[A^{Z}=1]-\Pr_{X\sim\mathbf{U}}[A^{X}=1]\Big|\geq\Omega(\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(A)})

where
\mathbf{Z}
is a uniformly random string in
\{0,1\}^{N}
conditioned on
\mathbf{Z}_{I}=W
, and the probabilities are both over the randomness of sampling the oracle as well as the randomness of the algorithm.

Proof.

We largely follow the proof of
[
GLLZ21
, Theorem 4]
.
For notational convenience, for all
Q\in\{0,1\}^{N}
, define
A^{Q}:=\Pr[A^{Q}=1]
where the probability is over the randomness of the algorithm.
Let
\mu:=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim\mathbf{U}}[A^{X}]
and let
\sigma:=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(A)}
. From the definition of variance we can write

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim\mathbf{U}}[(A^{X}-\mu)^{2}]\leq\Pr_{X\sim\mathbf{U}}(|A^{X}-\mu|\geq\sigma/2)+(\sigma/2)^{2}~.

Rearranging, we get
\Pr_{X\sim\mathbf{U}}(|A^{X}-\mu|\geq\sigma/2)\geq 3\sigma^{2}/4
. Thus at least one of
\Pr_{X\sim\mathbf{U}}[A^{X}\geq\mu+\sigma/2]
or
\Pr_{X\sim\mathbf{U}}[A^{X}\leq\mu-\sigma/2]
must be at least
3\sigma^{2}/8
. Suppose (without loss of generality) that
\Pr_{X\sim\mathbf{U}}[A^{X}\geq\mu+\sigma/2]\geq 3\sigma^{2}/8
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
denote the set of
X
’s such that
A^{X}\geq\mu+\sigma/2
, and let
\mathbf{V}
denote a uniformly random element of
\mathcal{V}
.
Since
|\mathcal{V}|\geq(3\sigma^{2}/8)\cdot 2^{N}
we have

H_{\min}(\mathbf{V})\geq N-\log\frac{8}{3\sigma^{2}}~.

The min-entropy decomposition lemma in
[
CDGS18
, Appendix A]
then implies that for all
0<\delta,\epsilon<1
, the random variable
\mathbf{V}
is
\epsilon
-close to a convex combination of random variables
\{\mathbf{Y}^{(z)}\}_{z}
that are
(k,\delta)
-dense for
k=\delta^{-1}\Big(\log 8/3\sigma^{2}+\log(1/\epsilon)\Big)
. This implies that
\Big|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{z}\Pr_{Y\sim\mathbf{Y}^{(z)}}[A^{Y}=1]-\Pr_{V\sim\mathbf{V}}[A^{V}=1]\Big|\leq\epsilon
and therefore

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{z}\Pr_{Y\sim\mathbf{Y}^{(z)}}[A^{Y}=1]\geq\mu+\sigma/2-\epsilon~.

By averaging there exists a
z
such that
\Pr_{Y\sim\mathbf{Y}^{(z)}}[A^{Y}=1]\geq\mu+\sigma/2-\epsilon
. For notational convenience we drop the
z
and simply write
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{Y}^{(z)}
.

Since
\mathbf{Y}
is
(k,\delta)
-dense, there is a subset
I
of at most
k
coordinates such that
\mathbf{Y}
is fixed to some string
W\in\{0,1\}^{I}
and the rest of the string
\mathbf{Y}_{\overline{I}}
is a
\delta
-dense random variable over
\{0,1\}^{N-|I|}
. The algorithm
A
making queries to
X
sampled from
\mathbf{Y}
can be viewed as making queries to
\mathbf{Y}_{\overline{I}}
only, since the coordinates
I
are fixed. By
Conjecture

1.2
, letting
\mathbf{Z}
be uniform over
\{0,1\}^{N}
conditioned on
\mathbf{Z}_{I}=W
, we have that

\Big|\Pr_{Z\sim\mathbf{Z}}[A^{Z}=1]-\Pr_{Y\sim\mathbf{Y}}[A^{Y}=1]\Big|\leq\mathrm{poly}(T)\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\delta)~.

Therefore

\Pr_{Z\sim\mathbf{Z}}[A^{Z}=1]\geq\mu+\sigma/2-\epsilon-\mathrm{poly}(T)\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\delta)~.

By setting
\epsilon=\sigma/4
and
\delta=\mathrm{poly}(\sigma/T)
for some universal polynomials

from
Conjecture

1.2
, we get that this is at least
\Omega(\sigma)
. Observing that

, we conclude the proof.

∎

4.2
A weaker version of
Conjecture

1.2

Given that the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture has been open for a while it appears that the quantum pseudorandomness conjectures are also quite difficult to prove. However we also give some evidence that
Conjecture

1.2
is true by proving a weaker version of it.

This is analogous to how Aaronson and Ambainis proved a weaker version of their eponymous conjecture via a Fourier-analytic result of Dinur, et al.
[
DFKO06
]
. Indeed, we also use the same Fourier-analytic tool in our proof.

We first recall a few basic concepts from analysis of Boolean functions and entropy before delving into the proof.

Fourier analysis on the boolean hypercube.

We define inner product between two functions
f,g:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
to be
\left\langle f,g\right\rangle:=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}f(x)g(x)
where the expectation is over uniformly random
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. We define
p
-norms to be
\|f\|_{p}=(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}|f(x)|^{p})^{1/p}
. We observe that the inner product satisfies Hölder’s inequality: for
p,q>0
satisfying
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
, we have
|\left\langle f,g\right\rangle|\leq\|f\|_{p}\,\|g\|_{q}
.

Lemma 4.1

(
(2,q)
-hypercontractivity; Chapter 9 of
[
O’D14
]
)
.

Let
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a degree-
d
function. Then for all integers
q>2
we have
\|f\|_{q}\leq\sqrt{q-1}^{d}\|f\|_{2}
.

We say that a function
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
has a
(k,d,\epsilon)
-junta approximation
if there exists a degree-
d
,
k
-junta function
g:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\|f-g\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
.

Theorem 4.2

(Dinur, Friedgut, Kindler, O’Donnell
[
DFKO06
]
)
.

Let
\epsilon>0
. All degree-
d
polynomials
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
have a
(2^{O(d)}/\epsilon^{2},d,\epsilon)
-junta approximation.

Densities and entropy.

A function
\mu:X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a
density
if
|X|^{-1}\sum_{x\in X}\mu(x)=1
. We say that a density
\mu
has
min-entropy deficit

t
if
\mu(x)\leq 2^{t}
for all
x\in X
. If
X=\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and
S\subseteq[n]
, then we say that the marginal of
\mu
on the subset
S
is the density
\mu_{S}
where for all
x_{S}\in\{\pm 1\}^{S}

\mu_{S}(x_{S})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{-S}}\mu(x_{S},x_{-S})

where the expectation is uniform over bits outside of
S
. We say that a density is
\delta
-dense
if for all subsets
S\subseteq[n]
, the marginal
\mu_{S}
has min-entropy deficit at most
\delta|S|
.

Lemma

1.4
is a consequence of the following lemma about low-degree polynomials.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
be a degree-

0<\epsilon\leq 1
, and let
\mu:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a
\delta
-dense density where
\delta\leq\epsilon^{2}2^{-O(d)}
. Then

|\left\langle f,\mu-1\right\rangle|\leq\sqrt{\delta}2^{O(d)}/\epsilon+O\Big((\delta n)^{d/2}\,\epsilon\Big)~.

Proof.

By
Theorem

4.2
,
f
has a
(2^{O(d)}/\epsilon^{2},d,\epsilon)
-approximation
\tilde{f}
. For convenience let
k=2^{O(d)}/\epsilon^{2}
. Then

|\left\langle f,\mu-1\right\rangle|\leq\left|\left\langle\tilde{f},\mu-1\right\rangle\right|+\left|\left\langle f-\tilde{f},\mu-1\right\rangle\right|~.

We bound each term separately. Let
S\subseteq[n]
denote the
k
variables that
\tilde{f}
is defined on. First, since
\mu
is
\delta
-dense, the marginal
\mu_{S}
has min-entropy deficit at most
\delta k
. This implies that
\mu_{S}
is

-close in total variation distance to the uniform density (i.e. the constant
1

2^{-k}\sum_{y\in\{\pm 1\}^{S}}|\mu_{S}(y)-1|=\frac{2}{2^{k}}\sum_{y:\mu_{S}(y)>1}|\mu_{S}(y)-1|\leq\frac{2}{2^{k}}\sum_{y\in\{\pm 1\}^{S}}|2^{\delta k}-1|=O(\delta k)~.

This follows since
\delta k\leq O(1)
(i.e., it does not grow with
n
), and then
2^{\delta k}-1=O(\delta k)
by Taylor series approximation.
Therefore

\left|\left\langle\tilde{f},\mu-1\right\rangle\right|=\left|\left\langle\tilde{f},\mu_{S}-1\right\rangle\right|\leq\|\tilde{f}\|_{2}\,\|\mu_{S}-1\|_{2}

by Cauchy-Schwarz. Since
\tilde{f}
is
\epsilon
-close to
f
, which has bounded
2
-norm, this is at most

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon)\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}(\mu_{S}(y)-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon)\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}|\mu_{S}(y)-1|\cdot(2^{\delta k}+1)}\leq O(\sqrt{\delta k})

where we used the bound on the total variation distance between
\mu_{S}
and uniform, and that
2^{\delta k}=O(1)
.
For the second term, we use Hölder’s inequality and hypercontractivity (

4.1
):

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle f-\tilde{f},\mu-1\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left\langle f-\tilde{f},\mu\right\rangle\right|+\left|\left\langle f-\tilde{f},1\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{p}\,\|\mu\|_{q}+\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big((p-1)^{d/2}\|\mu\|_{q}+1\Big)\,\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{2}~.

Set
p=\delta n+1
and
q=\frac{p}{p-1}
. Then

\|\mu\|_{q}^{q}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}|\mu(x)|\cdot|\mu(x)|^{1/(p-1)}\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}|\mu(x)|\cdot 2^{\delta n/(p-1)}\leq 2

where we used that the min-entropy deficit of
\mu
is at most
\delta n
.
Therefore

\left|\left\langle f-\tilde{f},\mu-1\right\rangle\right|\leq\Big((\delta n)^{d/2}\,2^{\frac{p-1}{p}}~+1\Big)\,\epsilon\,\leq O\Big((\delta n)^{d/2}\,\epsilon\Big)~.

Putting everything together, we get that

|\left\langle f,\mu-1\right\rangle|\leq O\Big(\sqrt{\delta k}+(\delta n)^{d/2}\epsilon\Big)~.

Plugging our choice of
k
, we get the desired statement.
∎

to obtain the desired conclusion.

\mathbf{P}

\mathbf{P}=(\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2})
over
S_{N}\times S_{N}
: the random variable
\mathbf{P}_{2}
is uniform over
S_{N}
, and
\mathbf{P}_{1}
is uniform conditioned on
\mathbf{P}_{1}(m)=m
where
m
is the length of the longest cycle of
\mathbf{P}_{2}
. The min-entropy of
\mathbf{P}
is
2\log N!-\log N
, and thus is
\delta
-dense for
\delta=\frac{\log N}{2\log N!}
. On the other hand, fixing a suffix
P_{2}
will fix a coordinate of
\mathbf{P}_{1}
, which is no longer dense.

We leave it as an interesting question to explore whether
Conjecture

4.4
can be “boosted” to imply
Conjecture

1.5
.

Proving the conjecture for few queries.

Another interesting question is whether it is possible to prove a permutation analogue of
Lemma

1.4
. The proof of this relied heavily on tools from boolean Fourier analysis, which works for well for studying distributions over product spaces. However, the distribution of random permutations is highly non-product: knowing that a random permutation maps
\pi(i)=j
means that
j
cannot appear in any other coordinate of
\pi
(when viewed as a string in
[N]^{N}
).

While the theory of Fourier analysis of boolean functions has been richly developed (see, e.g.,
[
O’D14
]
), the theory of harmonic analysis of functions on non-product spaces such as the symmetric group is nascent. The recent paper of Filmus, Kindler, Lifshitz, and Minzer
[
FKLM24
]
(and of Keevash and Lifshitz
[
KL23
]
) proved hypercontractive inequalities for functions on the symmetric group. Exploring the utility of these results in harmonic analysis to obtain additional evidence for
Conjecture

1.5
is something we leave for future work.

4.4
A helper lemma

In our
\mathsf{QCMA}
lower bound we deal with distributions over permutations where the permutations are fixed in a few coordinates, and are dense everywhere outside of those fixings. Such distributions do not exactly fulfill the requirements of
Conjecture

1.5
; technically the conjecture only handles random permutations that are dense
everywhere
and do not have any fixed coordinates. We state a variant of
Conjecture

1.5
that handles the broader class of permutation distributions, and argue that it is implied from
Conjecture

1.5
.

Consider a random variable
\mathbf{B}=(\mathbf{B}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{B}_{R})
where each
\mathbf{B}_{r}
is a permutation on
[N_{r}]
. We can equivalently view it as a random permutation
\mathbf{B}
over the alphabet
\Sigma=\bigcup_{r}\{r\}\times[N_{r}]
such that for each
r
,
\mathbf{B}(r,\cdot)
permutes
[N_{r}]
.
Definition

3.2
specifies the notion of
(k,\delta)
-denseness for such random permutations, with respect to the random permutation
\mathbf{Z}_{B}
that is uniform over
S_{N_{1}}\times\cdots\times S_{N_{R}}
conditioned on the same
k
fixed coordinates as in
\mathbf{B}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
Conjecture

1.5
is true, then the following is true. Let
A
be a quantum query algorithm that makes
T
queries to a collection of permutations
(B_{1},\ldots,B_{R})
where each
B_{r}
permutes
[N_{r}]
for some integer
N_{r}
. Let
\mathbf{B}
denote a random tuple of permutations from
S_{N_{1}}\times\cdots\times S_{N_{R}}
, such that both
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{B}^{-1}
are both
(k,\delta)
-dense. Then

\Big|\Pr_{Z\sim\mathbf{Z}_{B}}[A^{Z}=1]-\Pr_{B\sim\mathbf{B}}[A^{B}=1]\Big|\leq\mathrm{poly}(T,\log N_{1},\ldots,\log N_{R})\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\delta)

where
\mathbf{Z}_{B}
denotes a tuple of uniformly random permutations sampled from
S_{N_{1}}\times\cdots\times S_{N_{R}}

conditioned
on the same
k
fixed coordinates as
\mathbf{B}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{Z}_{B},\mathbf{B}
be random variables as specified in the lemma statement. Since
\mathbf{B}
is
(k,\delta)
-dense, for each
r\in[R]
there exists sets
I_{r},J_{r}\subseteq[N_{r}]
such that
\mathbf{B}_{r}
is
not
fixed on
I_{r}
and
\mathbf{B}_{r}(I_{r})=J_{r}
. In particular,
|I_{r}|=|J_{r}|
for all
r
and
\sum_{r}|I_{r}|\geq\sum_{r}N_{r}-k
. For all
r\in[R]
, let
\pi_{r}:J_{r}\to I_{r}
be some fixed bijection.

For all tuples
(B_{1},\ldots,B_{R})
, define the permutations
P_{r}:I_{r}\to I_{r}
as follows:

P_{r}(x)=\pi_{r}(B_{r}(x))~.

Let
\mathbf{P}_{r}
denote the random variable induced by
\mathbf{B}_{r}
, and let
\mathbf{P}
denote the product random variable
\mathbf{P}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbf{P}_{R}
. Let
\mathbf{Z}
denote a tuple of uniformly random permutations
(P_{1},\ldots,P_{R})
sampled from
S_{I_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{I_{R}}
.
For all subsets
T\subseteq\bigcup_{r}\{r\}\times I_{r}
, we have

H_{\min}(\mathbf{P}(T))=H_{\min}(\pi(\mathbf{B}(T)))=H_{\min}(\mathbf{B}(T))\geq(1-\delta)h(T)

where
\pi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{R})=(\pi_{1}(a_{1}),\ldots,\pi_{R}(a_{R}))
and
h
is the entropy associated with
\mathbf{Z}
. The second equality is because the min-entropy is invariant under renaming under the bijections
\pi_{r}
, and the last inequality is because
\mathbf{B}
is
\delta
-dense outside
\bigcup_{r}\{r\}\times I_{r}
. Thus if
\mathbf{B}
is
(k,\delta)
-dense with respect to
\mathbf{Z}_{B}
, then
\mathbf{P}
is
\delta
-dense with respect to
\mathbf{Z}
. Observe that
P_{r}^{-1}(y)=B_{r}^{-1}(\pi_{r}^{-1}(y))
. Then, for all subsets
S\subseteq\bigcup_{r}\{r\}\times I_{r}
,

H_{\min}(\mathbf{P}^{-1}(S))=H_{\min}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}(\pi^{-1}(S)))\geq(1-\delta)h(\pi^{-1}(S))=(1-\delta)h(S).

The last equality follows because
h
only depends on the cardinality of its input.

Define an algorithm
A^{\prime}
that makes
T
queries to the collection of permutations
(P_{1},\dots P_{R})
and simulates
A
as follows:

•

Whenever
A
queries
B_{r}(x)
, check if
x\in[N_{r}]\setminus I_{r}
. If yes, return
B_{r}(x)
. Otherwise, query
y\leftarrow P_{r}(x)
and return
\pi_{r}^{-1}(y)
to
A
.

•

Whenever
A
queries
B^{-1}_{r}(y)
, check if
y\in[N_{r}]\setminus J_{r}
. If yes, return the value
x
such that
B_{r}(x)=y
. Otherwise, query
q\leftarrow\pi_{r}(y)
and return
P_{r}^{-1}(q)
to
A
.

•

Output
A
’s output.

We can conclude that
\Pr_{P\sim\mathbf{Z}}[A^{\prime P}=1]=\Pr_{B\sim\mathbf{Z_{B}}}[A^{B}=1]
, and
\Pr_{P\sim\mathbf{P}}[A^{\prime P}=1]=\Pr_{B\sim\mathbf{B}}[A^{B}=1]
.
Conjecture

1.5
implies that

\Big|\Pr_{B\sim\mathbf{Z}_{B}}[A^{B}=1]-\Pr_{B\sim\mathbf{B}}[A^{B}=1]\Big|\leq\mathrm{poly}(T,\log N_{1},\ldots,\log N_{R})\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\delta)

as desired.
∎

5
\mathsf{QMA}
upper bound

We give a self-contained proof of the
\mathsf{QMA}
upper bound for the
Components
problem. This is essentially the same
\mathsf{QMA}
upper bound given by Natarajan and Nirkhe
[
NN23
]
, with some minor simplifications (for example, we obtain perfect completeness whereas their
\mathsf{QMA}
verifier has imperfect completeness).

Algorithm 1
.

\mathsf{QMA}
verifier for the
Components
problem

Given oracle access to a graph oracle
F
, and an
n
-qubit witness
\mathsf{\psi}
in register
\mathsf{X}
, perform one of the following tests at random:

1.

Balancedness test.
Apply
H^{\otimes n}
to
\ket{\psi}
and check if the result is
\ket{0^{n}}
. If so, reject. Otherwise, accept.

2.

Invariance test.

(a)

Sample a random
r\in[R]
.

(b)

Prepare a control qubit
\mathsf{C}
in the state
\ket{+}
, and a register
\mathsf{R}
in the state
\ket{r}
.

(c)

Controlled on
\mathsf{C}
being in the state
\ket{1}
,
\mathsf{R}
in the state
\ket{r}
, apply the permutation
F(r,\cdot)
to the register
\mathsf{X}
.

(d)

Apply
H
to
\mathsf{C}
and check if the result is
\ket{0}
. If so, accept. Otherwise, reject.

The meaning of “apply the permutation”
F(r,\cdot)
to register
\mathsf{X}
is that the verifier applies the permutation
in-place
, meaning that
\ket{x}
gets mapped to
\ket{F(r,x)}
. This can be done by attaching an ancilla register
\ket{0}
, computing the “forwards” oracle to obtain
\ket{x}\ket{F_{r}(x)}
, uncomputing the first register using the “inverse” oracle to obtain
\ket{0}\ket{F_{r}(x)}
, and then swapping the registers.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
F\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}
, then there exists a witness
\ket{\psi}
such that the
\mathsf{QMA}
verifier
V
accepts with probability at
1
.

Proof.

Since
F\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}
, there exists a unique partition
(S,\overline{S})
of
[N]
such that for all
r\in R
, the oracle
F
simply permutes the vertices within
S
and
\overline{S}
respectively. Define

\ket{\psi}:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}}\sum_{x\in S}\ket{x}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}}\sum_{x\notin S}\ket{x}~.

The probability that witness state
\ket{\psi}
is rejected in the Balancedness test is exactly equal to the squared overlap between
\ket{\psi}
and the uniform superposition which is
0
because
|S|=N/2
. Thus the verifier always accepts in this subtest.

We now compute its success probability in the Invariance test.
After registers
\mathsf{C}
and
\mathsf{R}
are prepared, the state of the algorithm is as follows:

\ket{+}_{\mathsf{C}}\otimes\ket{r}_{\mathsf{R}}\otimes\ket{\psi}_{\mathsf{X}}

Let
\pi
denote the permutation
F(r,\cdot)
.
After applying the oracle
we have (omitting mention of the register
\mathsf{R}
)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{0}_{\mathsf{C}}\otimes\ket{\psi}_{\mathsf{A}}\otimes+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{1}_{\mathsf{C}}\otimes\pi\ket{\psi}_{\mathsf{X}}~.

Hadamarding the control qubit we get

\frac{1}{2}\ket{0}_{\mathsf{C}}\otimes\Big(\ket{\psi}+\pi\ket{\psi}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\ket{1}_{\mathsf{C}}\otimes\Big(\ket{\psi}-\pi\ket{\psi}\Big)~.

The probability of rejecting (conditioned on
r
) is

\frac{1}{4}\Big\|(I-\pi)\ket{\psi}\Big\|^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\Big\|\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}}\sum_{x\in S}\Big(\ket{x}-\ket{\pi(x)}\Big)+\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}}\sum_{x\notin S}\Big(\ket{x}-\ket{\pi(x)}\Big)\Big\|^{2}~.

By the triangle inequality, this is at most

\frac{|S|}{2N}\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{|S|}}\sum_{x\in S}\Big(\ket{x}-\ket{\pi(x)}\Big)\Big\|^{2}+\frac{N-|S|}{2N}\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N-|S|}}\sum_{x\notin S}\Big(\ket{x}-\ket{\pi(x)}\Big)\Big\|^{2}~.

Since
F
is a
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{yes}}
instance, we have that
x\in S
if and only if
\pi(x)\in S
, and thus

\displaystyle\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{|S|}}\sum_{x\in S}\Big(\ket{x}-\ket{\pi(x)}\Big)\Big\|^{2}=\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N-|S|}}\sum_{x\notin S}\Big(\ket{x}-\ket{\pi(x)}\Big)\Big\|^{2}=0~.

Since this holds for every
r
, the probability of rejection in the Invariance test is
0
. The overall acceptance probability is therefore
1
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

If
F\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{no}}
, then for all witnesses
\ket{\psi}
the verifier
V
accepts with probability at most
1-\Delta
, where
\Delta
is the spectral gap of the normalized Laplacian of the graph associated with
F
.

Proof.

Let
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x}
. Suppose that the verifier
V
accepts with probability at least
1-\epsilon
in the Invariance test. From the above, the rejection probability is exactly equal to

\frac{1}{4}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{r}\Big\|\ket{\psi}-\pi_{r}\ket{\psi}\Big\|^{2}\leq\epsilon~.

Expanding, this implies

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{r}\bra{\psi}(I-\pi_{r}^{\dagger})(I-\pi_{r})\ket{\psi}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{r}\bra{\psi}(2I-\pi_{r}-\pi_{r}^{\dagger})\ket{\psi}=\bra{\psi}(2I-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{r}(\pi_{r}+\pi_{r}^{\dagger})\ket{\psi}\leq 4\epsilon.

Notice that for all
r
, the
(x,y)
element of the matrix
\pi_{r}+\pi_{r}^{\dagger}
is
1
if and only if either
\pi_{r}(x)=y
or
\pi_{r}(y)=x
. Thus
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{r}(\pi_{r}+\pi_{r}^{\dagger})
is proportional to the normalized adjacency matrix
\frac{1}{2R}A
of the graph
G
corresponding to
F
. Letting
\mathcal{L}=I-\frac{1}{2R}A
denote the normalized Laplacian of
G
, we have

\bra{\psi}\mathcal{L}\ket{\psi}\leq 2\epsilon~.

On the other hand, the spectral gap
\Delta
of
\mathcal{L}
satisfies

\|\ket{\psi}-\ket{\overline{\psi}}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\Delta}\bra{\psi}\mathcal{L}\ket{\psi}\leq\frac{2\epsilon}{\Delta}

where
\ket{\overline{\psi}}
is the vector where each entry is
\overline{\alpha}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}
.

Now we analyze the success probability of
\ket{\psi}
in the Balancedness test. This is equal to

1-\Big\|\bra{+}^{\otimes n}\cdot\ket{\psi}\Big\|^{2}=1-N\overline{\alpha}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\|\ket{\psi}-\ket{\overline{\psi}}\|^{2}\leq\epsilon/\Delta~.

Therefore the overall probability of success is at most

\frac{1}{2}(1-\epsilon)+\frac{1}{2}\max\Big\{\epsilon/\Delta,1\Big\}\leq\frac{1}{2}(1-\epsilon)+\frac{1}{2}\max\Big\{\epsilon/\Delta,1\Big\}~.

The largest this expression can get is
1-\Delta
, corresponding to when
\epsilon=\Delta
.

∎

Reducing to the Ambainis, Childs, and Liu lower bound.

The techniques we use to analyze the lower bound for interactive games have appeared in the literature for dealing with non-uniformity of algorithms. F_{r}(u_{1})=v_{1},F^{-1}_{r}(v_{1})=u_{1}
in the Yes case, then a No instance is different at entry
F_{r}(u_{1}),F^{-1}_{r},(v_{1})
, then we have
|S|/2
choices for the vertex
u_{2}
such that
F_{r}(u_{1})=u_{2}
. Once
u_{2}
is picked, then we have 2 choices for the vertex
v_{2}
such that
(v_{1},v_{2})
is an edge. So the total choices are
N
, and
l_{x,u_{1},v_{1}}=N
.

4.

For every No oracle and each entry pair
F_{r}(u_{1}),F_{r}^{-1}(v_{1})
in truth table, we consider the Yes instances different
from it at entry
F_{r}(u_{1})
(and accordingly
F_{r}^{-1}(v_{1})
): if
F_{r}(u_{1})\neq v_{1}
in the No instance, now we modify
F_{r}(u_{1})=v_{1},F^{-1}(v_{1})=u_{1}
so that it is a Yes instance. If this is a valid transformation, then we must have that
u_{1}
is
(N/2-1)
vertices from
v_{1}
in the original No instance, on the shorter path, which makes
u_{1}
unique. Now we must remove the edge
(u_{1},u_{2})
(correspondingly, entries
F_{r}(u_{1}),F^{-1}_{r}(u_{2})
) where
u_{2}
is on the longer path from
u_{1}
to
v_{1}
; similarly, we remove edge
(v_{1},v_{2})
where
v_{2}
is on the longer path from
v_{1}
to
u_{1}
; finally, add edge
(v_{1},v_{2})
(correspondingly, entries
F_{r}(v_{1}),F^{-1}_{r}(v_{2})
) to get a Yes instance. Thus there is only one choice and
l_{y,u_{1},v_{1}}^{\prime}=1
.

If we analyze all entries where
F_{r}(u_{1})\neq v_{1}
in the Yes instance and
F_{r}(u_{1})=v_{1}
in the No instance we get a smaller
l_{x,u_{1},u_{2}}\cdot l_{y,u_{1},u_{2}}^{\prime}
.
Thus we take

.

Therefore we have query lower bound
\Omega(\sqrt{\frac{m\cdot m^{\prime}}{l_{max}}})=\Omega(\sqrt{\frac{N^{3}}{N}})=\Omega(N)
for constant success probability

.

If we like to make
\eta

), we have a query lower bound
\Omega(\eta\cdot N)
which is still exponential.
∎

Sequential repetition/Multi-instance security

We show a sequential repetition of the interactive game using

Lemma

7.1
.

Claim 8
.

Let

(i-1)

Proof.

Since each

F(r_{i},\cdot)
are independently prepared. Suppose

i\in[k]

(1-O(\frac{i}{N}))

∎

By combining the above lemma with
