The DoGST18-DoHSP70 module modulates the cadmium tolerance of Dendrobium officinale
Authors: Man Zhang, Xiaojing Duan, Jiadong Chen, Yingying Liu, Zhengming Tao, Wu Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDendrobium officinale is a well-known traditional Chinese medicinal plant in Orchidaceae, and cadmium (Cd) toxicity in the substrate of continuous cultivation has become an important factor limiting cultivation of D. officinale domesticated seedling. Glutathione S-transferase (GSTs) are well-characterized as crucial mediators of plant stress responses. However, their specific functional roles and molecular mechanisms in Cd tolerance remain poorly understood in D. officinale. In this study, we identified a member of the GST gene family, DoGST18, demonstrating its significant involvement in Cd detoxification. DoGST18 expression in yeast increased host tolerance to Cd. In addition, genetic transformation in Arabidopsis revealed that overexpression (OE) lines exhibited enhanced Cd resistance relative to wild-type lines, as evidenced by growth rates and antioxidant enzyme activities. The candidate target S5M5N9/DoHSP70 was identified by pull-down and mass spectrometry. Yeast two-hybrid, bimolecular fluorescence complementation and co-immunoprecipitation experiments confirmed the specific interaction between DoGST18 and S5M5N9 in the plant cytoplasm, which was also proved by further pull-down experiments. Our findings offer a crucial theoretical foundation for revealing the molecular mechanism of DoGST18 in the Cd resistance of D. officinale, and will potentially contribute to cultivating low Cd-accumulating D. officinale germplasm.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12970-x.Keywords: Dendrobium officinale, DoGST18 gene, Cadmium tolerance, Glutathione S-transferase, Molecular interaction

Introduction

Cadmium (Cd) is a highly toxic element that is harmful to plants [1, 2]. In recent years, soil heavy metal contamination has been particularly prominent due to various geological and anthropogenic activities, as well as the difficulty of natural degradation of heavy metals in soil [3]. With the characteristics of easy solubility and high mobility, Cd is easily absorbed by plants, affecting plant cell membranes and enzyme systems, leading to abnormal plant metabolism, hormone disorders, inhibition of root tip cell proliferation, which in turn leads to the elongation of the root system, and a decline in the ability of plants to absorb nutrients [4]. In addition, Cd also disrupts the photosynthetic system, causing changes in the structure of chloroplasts and stomatal dysfunction, reducing photosynthetic efficiency, which is detrimental to plant growth. Redox reactions in plant cells are also affected by Cd, resulting in the accumulation of large amounts of reactive oxygen species, and even in severe cases triggering apoptosis [5]. Soil Cd contamination leads to significant impacts on crop growth resulting in reduced yield and quality, threatening agriculture and food security [6, 7].

Dendrobium officinale Kimura et Migo, a Chinese endemic orchid, is a traditional precious medicinal plant and a medicinal and edible homologous product. It has different enrichment capacities for different heavy metals, with the highest accumulation for Cd [8]. Cd toxicity in continuous cultivation substrates has become an important factor limiting the cultivation and production of D. officinale seedlings. Cd content analysis of 21 cultivated dendrobiums from different production areas found that more than 90% of the tested samples had Cd content far exceeding the limit of 0.3 mg/kg stipulated in the Green Industry Standard for Foreign Trade of Medicinal Plants and Preparations (WM/T2-2004) and Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2025), with a maximum of 6.85 mg/kg [9]. In the D. officinale production area of South Zhejiang, the water samples were generally good, while the single-factor pollution index of heavy metal Cd in 27 cultivation substrates was 1.33, which was mildly polluted [10]. Therefore, the environmental conditions for the development of pollution-free dendrobium need to strictly control the content of heavy metal Cd in the cultivation substrate.

GST protein superfamily plays a key role in detoxification and protection from oxidative damage of plant cells. GSTs catalyze the conjugation of the reduced glutathione (GSH) via its thiol group to electrophilic centers of various hydrophobic substrates to enhance hydrophilicity for detoxification and elimination [11, 12]. In addition, GSTs have important roles in controlling intracellular transport of compounds, signal transduction, and antistress responses. However, the molecular mechanisms by which D. officinale glutathione S-transferase (DoGSTs) regulate Cd stress response are still unclear. Therefore, the mining of Cd-resistant genes in Dendrobium and the further study of Cd toxicity and Cd-tolerance mechanisms in Dendrobium can help to improve the germplasm of D. officinale, enhance its resistance, and promote the sustainable development of the D. officinale industry, etc. It has important theoretical value and application prospects.

In this study, we identified a Cd-responsive GST gene (gene id: LOC110103396) in D. officinale, designated DoGST18, which was overexpressed to determine the mechanisms associated with Cd tolerance. The mechanism of Cd tolerance of candidate genes was studied by heterologous overexpression, gene characterization, subcellular localization, physiological and biochemical determination, interacting protein identification and verification.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Seeds were collected from three-year-old self-pollinated D. officinale plants in Yandangshan national D. officinale industrial base (28.37°N, 121.03°E, Yueqing, China), cultured under sterile conditions under a 14/10 h light/dark photoperiod and 60 µmol·m− 2·s− 1 light intensity at 25 ± 1 °C. After 180 days, six-month-old tissue-cultured seedlings with consistent growth were selected and transferred to MS medium containing CdSO4 for 30 days. CdSO4 concentrations were set at 0, 2, 5, 9 and 14 mg·L− 1, environmental conditions remained consistent with the pre-treatment period. Each cadmium concentration treatment included three biological replicates, and 10 plants were measured per replicate during sampling.

Cloning of the DoGST18 gene and expression analysis

To characterize the DoGST18 gene, we extracted total RNA from D. officinale using the Trizol method and reverse transcribed the extracted RNA to cDNA using a PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara, China). Specific primers were used to amplify the coding sequence (CDS) of DoGST18, and the PCR products were inserted into a pClone007 Blunt Simple vector and then sequenced. The sequences thus obtained were subsequently aligned using DNAMAN software. The nucleotide and encoded amino acid sequences of DoGST18 are shown in Supplementary Text S1. The neighbor-joining method was used for phylogenetic tree construction using MEGA-X software.

To determine the relative expression levels of DoGST18, we PCR amplified these genes using a TB Green® Premix Ex TagFAST qPCR (Takara, China), and calculated expression relative to that of the internal reference DoActin gene using the 2−ΔΔCt method. cDNA was also prepared from the WT lines of D. officinale without Cd treatment using the primer sequences.

Eukaryotic expression analysis

To further evaluate the cadmium tolerance potential of the cloned DoGST18 gene, the coding sequence was constructed into the yeast expression vector pYES2 for expression analysis, and transformed Saccharomyces cerevisiae Δycf1, with the pYES2 empty vector serving as a control. Positive clones were screened using SD-Ura medium and colony PCR. The growth status and colony formation of recombinant Δycf1 (pYES2-DoGST18) and control Δycf1 (pYES2) strains on SG-Ura culture were detected at CdCl2 concentrations of 0, 20, 30, 40 and 50 µM using galactose as an inducer of protein expression.

DoGST18 pull-down assay

In order to further assess the function of the cloned DoGST18 gene, the coding sequence was inserted into pMAL vector (with an MBP-tag) at the BamHI and SacI restriction sites, which was used to transform Escherichia coli (BL21) cells for the prokaryotic expression. The sequences of the primers used for amplification are shown in Table S2. The control and BL21 pMAL-DoGST18 cells were then used to inoculate 2-YT liquid medium containing 1 mM IPTG and 50 mg·L− 1 chloramphenicol, and incubation with shaking (220 rpm) was carried out for 3 h at 37 °C. The cells were harvested and disrupted by sonication. Crude extract (8 mL) from E. coli containing a plasmid that expresses a DoGST18-MBP fusion protein was then loaded onto an Amylose Resin column (1 mL) at 25 °C. The column was washed with column buffer (pH 7.4, 200 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT), and the target protein was eluted with at least 4 mL of column buffer containing 10 mM maltose. 20 µL of the eluate was loaded on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and run at 150 V for 1.5 h followed by silver staining to confirm the successful expression and isolation of the DoGST18-MBP fusion protein. The D. officinale samples treated with Cd were ground in liquid nitrogen, and total protein extraction was carried out using the Plant Total Protein Extraction Kit (Yeasen, China). The 25 µg of DoGST18-MBP fusion protein and 150 µL total protein were incubated with 100 µL amylose resin for 12 h at 4 °C. The resin was then collected by centrifugation at 1000 g and washed three times with MBP column buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, pH 7.5). The resin-complexes were eluted with elution buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 10 mM maltose, pH 7.5) and analyzed by SDS-PAGE. The differential bands were excised from the gel, and in-gel digestion was performed. Then, RPLC-MS was conducted to identify candidate interacting proteins.

Vector construction and genetic transformation

To functionally characterize the DoGST18 gene with respect to Cd tolerance, the coding sequence of DoGST18 was isolated and subsequently inserted into a pCAMBIA1300 vector using BamHI and SalI sites. The plasmid construct was used to transform Agrobacterium cells (GV3101) using the freeze−thaw method. A single positive clone was grown to an OD600 of 0.8 in LB medium. The cells were centrifuged for 15 min at 8000 rpm, and then resuspended in infiltration buffer (½MS, 5% sucrose, 0.03% Silwet L-77, 0.01 µg·mL− 1 6-BA, 20 mg·L− 1 AS, pH 5.7) to a final OD600 of 0.8. Arabidopsis ecotype Col-0 plants were transformed via the floral dip method. Infiltrated plants were covered with a plastic bag to retain humidity and placed under dark conditions for 24 h. Plants were transferred to normal growth conditions at 24 °C, 60% relative humidity, under a 16/8 h light/dark photoperiod with a light intensity of 100 µmol·m− 2·s− 1 to produce seeds. Seeds were surface-sterilized with 5% sodium hypochlorite for 5 min, followed by washing with sterile water. The transgenic lines were selected on ½MS solid medium containing 50 µg·mL⁻¹ hygromycin, vernalized at 4 °C for 3 days, and then transferred to normal growth conditions as described above. Seedlings with two leaves were transplanted into potting soil and labeled as the T1 generation. At the bolting stage, T1 plant leaves were collected for PCR identification. T1 seeds were harvested and screened again under the same conditions. Lines showing an ungerminated: germinated ratio of 1:3 were selected to obtain T2 seeds. These seeds were subsequently screened using the same procedure, and the seeds of T3 homozygous DoGST18 overexpression lines were obtained for future experiments.

Quantification of Arabidopsis physiological and biochemical indices

To investigate the physiological and biochemical differences between WT and OE lines in response to Cd treatment, the surface-sterilized Arabidopsis seeds were plated directly on ½MS solid medium (with 3% sucrose) containing 0 and 60 µM CdCl2, vernalized at 4 °C for 3 days, and then transferred to normal growth conditions (24 °C, 16/8 h light/dark, 100 µmol·m− 2·s− 1 light intensity) for 14 days. Whole seedlings were obtained for the measurement of root length, fresh weight and ROS content. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and superoxide radical (·O2−) contents in Arabidopsis were determined using the respective assay kits (Beyotime, China).

Subcellular localization of DoGST18

The coding sequence of the cloned DoGST18 gene was constructed into the pBWA(V)HS-GLosgfp vector, and transformed into EHA105. A. tumefaciens cells harboring pBWA(V)HS-GLosgfp empty vector, pBWA(V)HS-GLosgfp-DoGST18, and plasma membrane marker pCAMBIA1300-mCherry-OsMCA1 were cultured with shaking in Yeast Mannitol Medium (YEB) at 28 ℃ for 16 h. After centrifugation, Agrobacterium cells were collected and resuspended. The infection solutions were prepared by mixing equal volumes of bacterial suspensions containing the respective constructs, and the mixture was placed in dark for 2 h. The suspensions were injected into Nicotiana benthamiana from the abaxial surface of leaves and the plants were cultured under dark and light conditions successively for one day each, respectively. Fluorescence in N. benthamiana leaves was observed and captured using a Zeiss LSM880 laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany). Signal detection parameters were set with the following excitation/emission wavelengths: 488/509 nm for GFP fluorescence, 587/610 nm for mCherry fluorescence, and 640/681 nm for chloroplast autofluorescence.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay

The CDS of S5M5N9 and DoGST18 were inserted into the pCAMBIA2300S-cYFP and pCAMBIA1300S-nYFP, respectively, and transformed into A. tumefaciens EHA105. All the bacterial fluids were incubated overnight at 28 °C to an OD600 = 0.6. The cells were harvested by centrifuging at 5000 g for 15 min, and then resuspended to a final OD600 of 0.6 in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM AS, pH 5.6). The suspension was mixed according to experimental groupings: DoGST18-nYFP + S5M5N9-cYFP, DoGST18-nYFP + cYFP and S5M5N9-cYFP + nYFP. The mixed suspension was placed in the dark for 2 h, and injected into leaves of 4-week-old N. benthamiana. After infiltration, plants were kept in the dark for 12 h at 24 °C, and then cultured under a 16/8 h light/dark cycle at 24 °C for 3 days. The leaf disc (about 1 cm2) was sectioned from the infection site of N. benthamiana leaves, and immediately transferred to a glass slide with a drop of water in the middle, covered with a cover glass, and the excess liquid was removed with filter paper. Fluorescence signal was detected using a laser scanning confocal microscope Zeiss LSM880 (Zeiss, Germany) with an excitation/emission wavelength of 488/509 nm.

Luciferase (LUC) assay

The CDS of S5M5N9 and DoGST18 were inserted into the pCAMBIA1300-nLUC and pCAMBIA1300-cLUC vector, respectively, and transformed into A. tumefaciens EHA105. All the bacterial fluids were incubated overnight at 28 °C to an OD600 = 0.6. The cells were harvested by centrifuging at 5000 g for 15 min, and then resuspended to a final OD600 of 0.6 in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM AS, pH 5.6). The Agrobacterium resuspensions were mixed in a volume ratio of 1:1 according to experimental groupings: DoGST18-nLUC + S5M5N9-cLUC, DoGST18-cLUC + S5M5N9-nLUC, DoGST18-nLUC + cLUC, DoGST18-cLUC + nLUC, S5M5N9-nLUC+cLUC and S5M5N9-cLUC + nLUC. After Agrobacterium infiltration, plants were kept in dark for 12 h at 24 °C, and then cultured under a 16/8 h light/dark cycle at 24 °C for 2 days. The infected area of N.benthamiana leaves was treated with 1 mM D-luciferin potassium salt and kept in dark for 7 min. The fluorescence was observed using the LB985 NightSHADE evo In Vivo Plant Imaging System (Berthold Technologies, Germany) with the following parameters: exposure time of 3 min, gain set to “high”, and emission filter of 560–660 nm. Each group of samples had three replicates.

Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) experiment

The CDS of S5M5N9 was integrated into the prey vector pGADT7 and the CDS of DoGST18 was inserted into the bait vector pGBKT7, then co-transformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae AH109. Positive co-transformants were initially selected on synthetic defined (SD) minimal medium lacking tryptophan and leucine (SD-TL, DDO). Prior to the interaction test, potential self-activation of each construct (pGBKT7-DoGST18 and pGADT7-S5M5N9) was examined individually by co-transforming it with the corresponding empty vector (pGADT7 or pGBKT7) into the AH109 yeast strain. No growth was observed on the corresponding selection media, confirming the absence of self-activation for both constructs. Subsequently, the yeast lines of identical optical density were spotted onto a series of selective media: SD medium lacking tryptophan, leucine, and histidine (SD-TLH, TDO); SD medium lacking tryptophan, leucine, histidine, and adenine (SD-TLHA, QDO); and SD-TLHA medium supplemented with X-α-gal (SD-TLHA + X-α-gal). Plates were incubated at 30 °C for 3 days and monitored for colony growth and the development of blue coloration. To validate the reliability of the yeast two-hybrid system, standard controls were included in the assay: pGBKT7-largeT + pGADT7-p53 served as the positive control to confirm the system’s capability to detect a known interaction, while pGBKT7-largeT + pGADT7-lam were used as the negative control to rule out false positives caused by nonspecific binding or auto-activation. These control plasmids were transformed and screened simultaneously with the experimental groups.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

The transformed A. tumefaciens cells GV3101 harboring recombinant constructs (pFastR06-DoGST18‐GFP, pCAMBIA1306‐S5M5N9‐MYC) were cultured in lysogeny broth (LB) supplemented with selective antibiotics (50 µg·ml− 1 kanamycin/spectinomycin and 35 µg·ml− 1 rifampicin) at 28 °C with shaking at 200 rpm until the culture reached an OD600 of 0.8. The bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in infiltration medium (MS basal salts medium containing 200 µM acetosyringone) to an OD600 of 0.6, and infiltrated into tobacco leaves. Infiltrated plants were maintained under a 16/8 h light/dark cycle at 24 °C for 48 h. The infiltrated leaves were harvested, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground to a fine powder. Total proteins were extracted by homogenizing the tissue in EXB lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 0.1% Triton X-100, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 5 mM DTT) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The homogenates were centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain clarified supernatants, which were used as input samples.

For co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), clarified lysates were incubated with 15 µL of GFP-Trap magnetic beads in 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes at 4 °C for 4 h with gentle rotation. Subsequently, the beads were collected by centrifugation at 1000 g and washed eight times with washing buffer (50 mM Tris, 0.15 M NaCl, pH7.4) to remove nonspecifically bound proteins. The beads were incubated with elution buffer (0.1 M Glycine-HCl, pH3.0) at 4 °C for 1 h, and then placed on a magnetic rack until the solution was clarified. The supernatant was collected and immediately neutralized by adding one-tenth volume of 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) to adjust the pH to 7.0–8.0. Proteins in total extracts were analyzed by immunoblotting.

Protein samples (20 µL) were separated by SDS-PAGE. Electrophoresis was initially performed at 80 V until the dye front migrated through the stacking gel, followed by 100 V until separation was complete in the resolving gel. The separated proteins were then transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane at 100 V for 90 min. Subsequently, the membrane was washed with TBST buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween-20, pH 7.4) for 10 min and blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk prepared in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. The membrane was incubated with α-MYC antibody (100 ng/mL, diluted in the blocking buffer) for 4 h at room temperature. After three 10-min washes with TBST, the membrane was incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated α-GFP antibody (10 ng/mL, diluted in blocking agent) for 45 min at room temperature. Finally, immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Beyotime, China) and imaged using an iBright FL1500 chemiluminescence detection system (Thermo Fisher, USA) with an exposure time of 3 s.

Cell-free protein expression and pull-down

According to the instruction manual of the cell-free protein expression kit (Kangma-Healthcode, China), DoGST18 and S5M5N9 coding sequences were inserted into pD2P vector, respectively, and reacted with DNA amplifier at 37 °C for 2 h. The amplification products were mixed with 2 mL protein reaction solution and reacted at 20–30 °C and 40–220 rpm for 3–20 h. The reaction solution was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. The protein solution was purified by magnetic beads for WB detection.

Statistical analysis

One-way ANOVA and multiple t-tests were conducted using SPSS 22.0 software. All data were presented as the means ± standard deviations of three replicate treatments for each sample, with a P < 0.05 being considered statistically significant. The figures were generated using GraphPad Prism 7 and Origin 2021 software.

Results

DoGST18gene is highly responsive to Cd and enhances yeast resistance to Cd stress

We cloned the DoGST18 gene and established that the protein-coding sequence of the gene encodes 197 amino acids. In addition, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the expression level of DoGST18 was significantly higher in roots than in stems and leaves. With increased Cd treatment concentration, DoGST18 was significantly upregulated; especially at 14 mg·L− 1, its expression was about 7-fold higher than that of the control group (Fig. 1A). These results indicate that DoGST18 may play an important role in alleviating Cd toxicity in the roots of D. officinale, thereby helping plants alleviate Cd stress.

To investigate the cellular distribution of DoGST18, transient expression was employed in N. benthamiana leaves. Confocal microscopy revealed that the fluorescently tagged protein predominantly localized to the plasma membrane (Fig. 1B). To evaluate its functional role, DoGST18 was cloned into the pYES2 vector and transformed into the cadmium-sensitive yeast mutant strain Δycf1. Transformants were selected on synthetic defined (SD) medium lacking uracil and subsequently exposed to varying concentrations of cadmium (Cd²⁺). Notably, yeast cells expressing DoGST18 exhibited significantly enhanced growth compared to empty vector controls under Cd²⁺ stress, particularly at concentrations ranging from 20 to 50 µM (Fig. 1C). These results suggest that DoGST18 can improve the host’s tolerance to Cd.

Overexpression of DoGST18 enhanced growth and reduced oxidative damage of Arabidopsis under Cd stress

To characterize the role of DoGST18 in Cd stress adaptation, we generated and validated stable transgenic Arabidopsis lines expressing this gene through Agrobacterium-mediated transformation (Fig. 2A-B). Two independent, homozygous transgenic Arabidopsis lines overexpressing DoGST18 (designated DoGST18-OE-1 and DoGST18-OE-2) were used for subsequent analyses. PCR analysis confirmed that the OE lines expressed DoGST18 (Fig. S1). Under 60 µM Cd treatment, the root length and fresh weight of the transgenic lines DoGST18-OE-1 and DoGST18-OE-2 were significantly increased compared to the wild type (WT) Col-0 (Fig. 2C-D). Moreover, no significant differences were observed in phenotype between WT and OE lines under normal conditions. Based on the phenotype results, the ROS content, including H2O2 and ·O2−, was determined in WT and OE lines. Compared with the WT lines, we observed that Cd stress significantly decreased the H2O2 and ·O2− contents in OE lines (Fig. 2E-F), indicating enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity.

S5M5N9 is a candidate target protein of DoGST18

After clarifying the biological function of DoGST18, we obtained MBP-specific proteins by pull-down to further understand the detoxification mechanism of DoGST18 (Fig. 3A). According to the results of protein mass spectrometry sequencing, 50 specific peptides (Table S1) were obtained. Combined with the Unused score, the coverage of peptides with a credibility greater than 95% in the whole protein, the number of peptides with a confidence interval greater than 95%, and the current research progress on the cadmium resistance mechanism of D. officinale, S5M5N9 was selected as the candidate target protein for the next specific interaction verification. In order to verify the interaction between DoGST18 and S5M5N9, multiple sets of controls were set up for Y2H experiments, as shown in Table 1. The pGBKT7-DoGST18 + pGADT7, pGBKT7 + pGADT7-S5M5N9, pGBKT7-largeT + pGADT7-lam were used as negative controls, and pGBKT7-largeT + pGADT7-p53 served as positive control. The results indicated that DoGST18 can directly interact with S5M5N9 (Fig. 3B).

DoGST18 and S5M5N9 interact in cytoplasm and cell membrane of plants

Next, we carried out BiFC and LUC assays. The findings revealed that DoGST18 could interact with S5M5N9 in the cytoplasm and cell membrane (Fig. 4A). In addition, strong fluorescence signals were detected in the tobacco leaves co-expressing DoGST18 and S5M5M9, while almost no fluorescence signals were detected in the negative control leaves, which is consistent with prior observations that DoGST18 and S5M5N9 could indeed interact in plants (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

Overexpression of DoGST18 enhances Cd tolerance and regulates ROS homeostasis

The amino acid sequence encoded by the DoGST18 gene is highly similar to the ZmGSTU6 and PeGSTU18 protein sequences that have been validated to be involved in plant tolerance to Cd stress, indicating that DoGST18 may also play a similar function. Cd treatment significantly increased the expression of DoGST18 gene in the roots of D. officinale, consistent with the expression patterns observed for GmGST1, TaGST25, TaGST26 and ZmGSTU4 [13–15]. The accumulation of Cd is regulated by xylem transport from root to stem [16] and is related to the ability to store Cd in the root system [17]. Cd accumulation in roots was downregulated when GST activity and thiol content increased [18, 19]. More studies have shown that GST proteins can catalyze the formation of complexes between Cd and GSH [20], thereby alleviating the toxic effects of Cd [21]. In order to evaluate the Cd tolerance function of DoGST18, the gene was expressed in brewing yeast. Under Cd stress, the growth of recombinant brewing yeast was better compared to the empty control strain. When Cd was not added, the two showed basically the same growth condition. It can be inferred that DoGST18 can enhance Cd tolerance of host cells.

In addition, by assessing Arabidopsis growth, our study indicated that the transgenic Arabidopsis overexpressing DoGST18 exhibited a significant Cd tolerance phenotype. Excessive ·O2− and H2O2 concentrations mediate cytotoxicity, resulting in irreversible cell viability loss [22, 23]. Further physiological experiments showed that overexpression of DoGST18 could reduce the excessive ROS accumulation induced by Cd stress in plants, thereby reducing plant cell damage and improving plant Cd tolerance. Our previous study showed that the malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration increased significantly under 9 and 14 mg·L⁻¹ Cd stress. Additionally, peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) activities in the roots of D. officinale increased dramatically with increasing Cd concentrations [24]. Those findings provide important clues for understanding the physiological mechanisms underlying D. officinale stress resistance. Furthermore, to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the antioxidant mechanism, future studies should examine a wider range of antioxidant enzyme activities in OE lines, including SOD, POD, and CAT.

Molecular mechanisms ofDoGST18overexpression in Cd tolerance

The interaction between proteins is the basis of all biological reactions. To analyze the detoxification mechanism of DoGST18, a D. officinale HSP70 protein was obtained by pull-down experiment, named S5M5N9. Heat shock proteins (HSP) belong to a class of stress proteins that belong to the molecular chaperone type. They are induced by various stresses such as drought and high temperature in plants, and are highly expressed in adversity environments. They are of great significance for plants to resist adversity and adapt to the environment [25–27].

HSPs are highly conserved and mainly responsible for folding proteins. It can not only play a role in stabilizing membranes and proteins, but also promote better folding of proteins and stabilize proteins even in stressful environments [28]. Among the HSP family proteins, HSP70 accounts for the highest proportion, which can enhance the tolerance of transgenic Arabidopsis to drought, high salt and heat stress [29]. It may improve plant abiotic stress tolerance by reducing the accumulation of excessive ROS induced by abiotic stress. Further experiments using Y2H, LUC, BiFC, pull-down, and Co-IP confirmed that DoHSP70 (S5M5N9) could interact with DoGST18. Here, our findings provide a new research perspective for the regulation of abiotic stress, especially Cd stress, by the DoGST18-DoHSP70 module.

Conclusions

In summary, this article reveals the significant response of DoGST18 to Cd stress in D. officinale through functional analysis, as well as its key role in increasing Cd tolerance and reducing ROS levels in Arabidopsis. The specific interaction between DoGST18 and S5M5N9 was verified through a series of protein interaction experiments, and S5M5N9 was identified as a potential interaction target of DoGST18 in the Cd resistance mechanism. The work described here shows that there may be a synergistic molecular mechanism between them, which affects the physiological and biochemical response of plants to stress by regulating the expression of related genes. This discovery not only helps to understand the adaptation mechanism of D. officinale to heavy metal stress such as Cd, but also provides new ideas for stress resistance breeding of other crops.
