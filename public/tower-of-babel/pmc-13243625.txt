An evaluation of the impact of visual attributes of park landscape spaces on the emotional benefits and behavioral intention of younger older adults
Authors: Yuhui Chen, Mu Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the rising density of the elderly population, community parks, due to their high accessibility and flexibility, have become crucial resources for enhancing the physical activity levels of older adults and promoting inclusive elderly care. However, the relationship between the composition and configuration of landscape spaces and the behavioral intention and emotional benefits of older adults remains unclear. This study investigated the landscape spaces of community parks in three Chinese cities. Real-time psychological and physiological data were collected from participants by viewing sample photos, and physiological data on differential emotional arousal induced by specific visual stimuli were extracted using deconvolution techniques. Through descriptive statistics, differential analysis, correlation analysis, and mediation effect analysis, a mediation model comprising “visual attributes of park landscape space, behavioral intention for active activities, and emotional benefits” was constructed to explore the effective enhancement of the emotional states of older adults by different visual attributes of landscape spaces in community parks. (1) The landscape space visual attributes regarding positive emotional arousal can be ranked as landscape spatial enclosure (LSE) > landscape spatial hue (LSH) > green space accessibility (GSA) > artificiality of landscape space (AoLS) (p < 0.001). (2) Landscape spaces with lower enclosure degrees could significantly improve the emotional benefits of the younger older adults (p < 0.001). However, their behavioral intention was attenuated as the enclosure degree decreased (p < 0.001), which resulted in impaired effectiveness (p < 0.001). (3) For the younger older adults, both GSA and AoLS influenced emotional benefits via behavioral intention (p < 0.001), with GSA showing a partial mediation effect and AoLS exhibiting a suppressor (inconsistent) mediation effect. An increase in green accessibility was correlated with an increase in behavioral intention (p < 0.05), while artificiality was negatively correlated with both behavioral intention (p < 0.001) and emotional benefits. (4) Emotional benefits were higher in cold color landscape spaces compared with warm color ones (p < 0.001). Meanwhile, the indirect effect of LSH via behavioral intention was small and negative; however, this pathway accounted for a relatively minor proportion of the total effect and was outweighed by the significant positive direct effect of cool tones on emotional benefits (p < 0.001). Among the four visual attributes of landscape spaces, landscape enclosure has the most significant impact on the level of positive emotional arousal. However, the mechanism by which visual perception of thermal comfort effectively enhances the role of enclosure in emotional benefits has previously been overlooked by researchers and warrants further investigation in future studies to better understand the role of visual factors in effectively improving the emotional benefits for older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48324-6.

Introduction

The rapid growth of the global elderly population has driven a major strategic shift in healthcare systems worldwide, from a disease-treatment model toward a health-promotion paradigm. In this context, how to help older adults maintain favorable physical and mental well-being in daily life has become a key focus of both public policy and academic research1,2.

China’s policy document, the Opinions on Strengthening Aging Work in the New Era, explicitly underscores that the individuals aged 60 to 69, categorized as the “younger older adults” demographic cohort, frequently serve as the primary participants in social engagement and research activities, attributed to their relatively preserved physical functionality and higher capacity for active societal involvement3. Among many factors affecting public health, green spaces play an important role in generating health and well-being due to humans’ innate affinity for nature4. Residential areas, serving as the immediate physical surroundings for the elderly, play a crucial role in boosting physical activity and social engagement among seniors. These factors contribute significantly to improving their emotional states and physical health, consequently fostering inclusive elderly care5.

Prior research indicates that exposure to natural environments is associated with improvements in health indicators such as cardiovascular health, immune function, and physical functioning, and can also enhance older adults’ overall well-being by alleviating psychological stress and increasing positive affect (e.g., pleasure and vitality)1,6–11. Previous studies indicate that multiple visual attributes of green spaces—such as color characteristics, vegetation enclosure, and green space accessibility—are closely associated with individuals’ emotional states and their behavioral intention to engage in outdoor activities12–14. Nevertheless, existing research on green spaces and elderly emotional states has predominantly focused on the availability and quantity of such spaces, often overlooking the critical aspect of spatial configuration15,16, and thus paying insufficient attention to their internal composition and configuration17. This imbalance may lead to an underestimation of the emotional benefits attributable to green-space structure and visual layout.

More specifically, the differences in the composition and configuration of green spaces may directly affect older adults’ emotional states through visual perception. On the one hand, Chen et al.18 examined single attributes—transparency, enclosure, greenness, temperature, and humidity—and confirmed their close associations with older adults’ emotions, including tension, oppression, and pleasure, indicating that visual landscape composition can directly shape emotional experience. On the other hand, Howell et al.19 further demonstrated that improving neighborhood landscape accessibility promotes physical activity and strengthens activity intention among older adults, thereby benefiting physical health. Additionally, Zhang et al. ascertained that landscape color tone and user preference play important roles in enhancing positive affect and relieving stress11. Collectively, these studies suggest that the visual attributes emerging from green-space composition and configuration are key pathways through which green spaces elicit positive emotions and activity intention in older adults, providing a basis for classifying landscape spaces based on visual perception.

Several studies have examined the effects of landscape visual attributes on older adults’ emotions from a single dimension. Cross-attribute comparisons across key visual dimensions (e.g., color tone, accessibility, enclosure, artificiality) remain limited, and the indirect pathway via older adults’ behavioral intention for active engagement is rarely tested. As a result, it is still unclear which visual attributes most effectively increase behavioral intention and thereby enhance positive emotional experiences, limiting the precision and effectiveness of landscape design.

Most studies on the physical and mental health evaluation of landscape space and visual perception are carried out based on self-assessment reports, questionnaires, and big data surveys20–22. Meanwhile, remote sensing data, ArcGIS, and other methods are also adopted in some studies to statistically quantify the space of visual preferences of the elderly23,24. Although recent research has begun incorporating physiological measurements to assess the emotional benefits of landscape environments, studies specifically examining emotional arousal through physiological data in elderly populations remain scarce25,26. Considering that older adults represent the predominant users of outdoor activity spaces in China, investigating the emotional benefits—and broader physical and mental health impacts—of community park landscapes for this population has clear practical relevance.

In addition, there are various analysis methods for exploring the emotional benefits and behavioral intention in landscape spaces, such as regression analysis, variance analysis, and path analysis27,28. Some scholars have also begun to highlight the mediating roles of factors such as group-level visual preference and nature-related motivation29. In this context, intention for active engagement is a key psychological link between visual environmental perception and health-related behaviors, reflecting older adults’ intention to engage in activities in community parks (e.g., walking, exercise, or social interaction)30,31. Importantly, these mediation pathways should ultimately be evaluated in outcome-oriented terms—namely, whether the environment promotes active participation and whether higher participation is associated with greater emotional benefits—yet systematic evidence remains limited.

Accordingly, this study draws on community park landscape spaces in three Chinese cities (Xiamen, Nanchang, and Yancheng) and focuses on younger older adults aged 60–69 years to examine the mechanism linking visual attributes of park landscape spaces, behavioral intention, and emotional benefits. Specifically, the study (1) compares the effects of different visual attributes on positive affect among younger older adults and (2) tests behavioral intention as a mediator between visual attributes and emotional outcomes. Assessing the strength and stability of this intention helps clarify whether visually induced emotional benefits can be translated into realized outcomes. The findings provide evidence for optimizing community park visual attributes to promote engagement and enhance emotional benefits, informing age-friendly planning and design.

Methodology

Conceptual framework

From the perspective of health promotion for the younger older adults, the hypothetical pathway of “visual attributes of park landscape space -> behavioral intention for active engagement -> emotional benefits” is proposed by integrating psychophysiological theory, landscape perception theory, and cognitive evaluation theory (Fig. 1).

Fig. 1Research conceptual framework.

Research conceptual framework.

Landscape perception theory posits that landscape perception is a process involving the interaction between the objective features of the landscape and an individual’s subjective attitudes, with these attitudes triggering the individual’s emotional activities and behavioral tendencies and outcomes. Concurrently, cognitive evaluation theory suggests that environmental events or situations can affect the emotions of an individual, and the nature and intensity of these emotions depend on the content of the evaluated events and the individual’s evaluation results. Furthermore, psychophysiology theory states that psychology and physiology can interact with each other, positive emotions contribute to health by affecting physiological mechanisms, and the physiological health mechanisms of the younger older adults are determined by such factors as stress hormone levels, immune system function, and cardiovascular function.

The coupling relationship of these three theories and mechanisms has been widely recognized and applied in the fields of environmental psychology, psychophysiology, and ergonomics. This coupling mechanism reveals new pathways through which community landscapes enhance emotional benefits in elderly populations32–34. Specifically, visual perception of landscape features initiates a cognitive-behavioral process wherein individuals assess psychological responses and activity preferences through implicit subjective evaluations. The actualization of emotional benefits can then be objectively verified through corresponding physiological measures of arousal.

Research subjects and sample preparation

This study is situated in a severely ageing city in eastern China, as documented in the Jiangsu Provincial Report on Aging Development (2024), Yancheng City has entered a hyper-aged society (per United Nations criteria: ≥21% population aged ≥ 65), with 22.86% of its permanent resident population aged 65 or above, positioning it among four prefecture-level municipalities in Jiangsu Province that have crossed this demographic threshold35. By the end of 2023, the resident population aged 60+ in Yancheng City reached 2.015 million, corresponding to an aging rate of 30.12%36. This demographic trajectory exhibits regional prototypicality (β = 0.87, p < 0.001 in provincial spatial autoregression models). This regionally representative ageing trajectory helps characterize community park use and related emotional benefits in economically developed areas of eastern China.

To enhance the generalizability of the findings, three cities—Xiamen, Nanchang, and Yancheng—were selected for spatial sampling in this study (Fig. 2). Xiamen represents visual characteristics of low enclosure, such as open lawns and tall trees. Nanchang represents landscape spaces with a medium level of enclosure, where vertical landscape elements are more frequently used. Yancheng features low-intervention landscape spaces, where areas with higher enclosure or lower accessibility are relatively easy to find. The typicality of these landscape spatial forms facilitates the selection of spatial samples with different visual attributes of landscape spaces. Cross-regional sampling has been proven to be a methodological approach to mitigate the moderating effects of place dependence and place identity on emotional responses, thereby reducing environmental familiarity bias37,38.

To further minimize non-target influences (e.g., place attachment and cultural symbols), sampling sites were screened, and selected images were edited in Photoshop to downplay salient regional identifiers and cultural cues, thereby improving the standardization of landscape elements (Fig. 3). This procedure encourages participants to rely more on immediate visual cues (e.g., color tone and enclosure form) rather than prior place-based experience or emotional memory, enabling a clearer comparison of how visual attributes across landscape spaces differentially affect emotional benefits.

In addition, sample photographs were drawn from existing excellent park landscape spaces to ensure ecological validity39. Such spaces typically feature reasonable design and adequate facilities, aligning with Egon Brunswik’s principle of representative design, which emphasizes maintaining representative cue structures and environmental complexity under real-world conditions40. Compared with fully orthogonal, tightly controlled image designs, this approach sacrifices complete independence among variables but preserves holistic perception and naturally co-occurring cue combinations closer to everyday use contexts, thereby strengthening the interpretability of findings in real environments.

Data collection

Landscape space indicator data

Human visual perception endows landscape spaces with unique visual attributes41–44. Therefore, the accessibility of green space, enclosure, color tone, and artificiality of landscape spaces are indicators that are chosen to represent the landscape characteristics of community park spaces (Table 1). In recent years, scholars have highlighted the importance of these attributes and quantified indicators such as color characteristics, enclosure, accessibility, and artificiality to examine their effects on affective outcomes12,31,45. These indicators have been shown to significantly influence visual preference and emotional responses. Building on prior work, this study selected green space accessibility (GSA), landscape spatial enclosure (LSE), landscape spatial hue (LSH), and artificiality of landscape space (AoLS) as key visual-attribute indicators of community park landscape spaces.

Behavioral intention test data

The behavioral intention scale in this study was established based on the tourism participation intention scale with slight simplification and modification50–53. Individual attitudes cannot be directly linked to behavior, and intention plays a key role in the connection between the two. Hence, behavioral intention can be considered an important factor in predicting individual behavioral tendencies and can be used to measure the intention of younger older adults to use the environment54. The simplified scale captured younger older adults’ stated willingness and intention to engage in activities in specific types of landscape spaces; the items are presented in Table 3.

In addition, all items were administered with the sample images as contextual stimuli and rated on a 4-point Likert scale: (1) strongly disagree, (2) disagree, (3) agree, and (4) strongly agree. The simplified format was intended to reduce choice difficulty and response burden, thereby more effectively capturing behavioral intention for community park landscape spaces with different attributes.

Emotional benefit test data

Emotional benefits cannot be measured by a single physiological indicator and require careful consideration. Accordingly, this study combined physiological and psychological measures, operationalizing emotional benefits by integrating ISCR (arousal intensity) and PANAS (positive/negative affect).

Firstly, the physiological indicator used in this study was the integrated skin conductance response (ISCR). ISCR reflects the degree of emotional arousal caused by specific stimuli and is sensitive to subtle emotional changes. Hence, the differences in individual skin conductance level (SCL) can be minimized, thus providing a clearer and stimulus-specific response measure55. It is well-suited for identifying which landscape attributes can significantly affect emotional arousal. Therefore, the degree of emotional arousal in younger older adults caused by landscapes with different attributes can be compared based on this measure. Besides, the Shimmer3 GSR+ skin conductance sensor (Shimmer Research, Dublin, Ireland) was used in this study to collect the electrodermal activity (EDA) signals from the subjects in real time. After data collection, LEDalab software was used to deconvolute EDA signals using a continuous decomposition algorithm to extract ISCR and remove the influence of SCL56,57.

Secondly, the skin conductance physiological data can only serve as an indicator of the degree of emotional arousal. The positive and negative affect schedule (PANAS) was used as a self-assessment questionnaire to determine the positivity or negativity of individual emotions58. The PANAS (Table 4) was administered to older adults for self-assessment of emotional states using a 4-point Likert scale at each phase59, thus serving as an indicator of the positive or negative emotional arousal of the landscape space on younger older adults, and thus to quantify emotional benefits. The four response options were: (1) strongly disagree, (2) disagree, (3) agree, and (4) strongly agree. Each participant selected the option that best reflected their agreement with each affect item. Using a simplified 4-point scale helps reduce choice-related bias and improves data reliability.

Experimental design

Subjects

This study was designed to reduce errors caused by other factors. (1) A total of 52 subjects (with a gender ratio of 1:1), who had lived in both urban and rural areas, were recruited as subjects. These younger older adults were recruited through community visits. (2) The experiment was conducted in a closed and quiet indoor space that was set up as each participant’s home environment. This measure was implemented to ensure physical isolation and control of the acoustic environment, to provide older adults with a familiar and comfortable environment, and to mitigate the influence of external factors on emotional responses. (3) We excluded participants who were currently undergoing treatment for mental illness, had taken medication for allergies, colds, insomnia, stomach discomfort, or glaucoma within the past week, had consumed alcoholic or caffeinated beverages within the past week, or had severe chronic diseases such as heart disease or diabetes. All enrolled participants demonstrated normal cognitive function and adequate physical mobility. (4) All subjects were right-handed with normal vision or corrected normal vision60.

It should be mentioned that all subjects voluntarily participated in this experiment without compensation. Before the experiment, all procedures were explained to the participants in detail, and written informed consent was obtained from all individuals for participation in the study and for the use of their anonymized data in scientific publications. This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Soochow University (Approval No. SUDA20241006H02).

Experimental image preparation

The experimental sample images were collected from well-established community landscape spaces across various regions in China for experimental use. For publication, any persons appearing in the original photographs were removed (no blurring or masking was used); therefore, no identifiable individuals appear in the published figures, and informed consent for publication of identifiable images was not required. To minimize the interference from other visual elements in the landscape spaces, photographs were edited using Photoshop to control potential influencing factors (see section “Research subjects and sample preparation” for details). Besides, the same photograph was not processed repeatedly, with a view to increasing participant engagement, reducing fatigue from overly similar photographs, and maintaining their motivation, thereby improving the validity of emotional responses.

Additionally, the selection of scenes was primarily based on three criteria. (1) Each type of landscape attribute was presented in the same spatial form. (2) The scenes were located near residential areas. (3) Only well-established excellent park landscape spaces were selected. Well-established, excellent park landscape spaces exhibited a high level of resident participation and satisfaction, which was more conducive to the horizontal comparison of the impact of visual characteristics of different community landscapes on emotional benefits.

A total of 200 photographs were collected, and the park landscape spaces represented in these photographs were categorized into four types based on their visual characteristics. Considering the representativeness of the sampling sites and the feasibility of the experiment, a typical sampling method was adopted, with two dimensions set within each category for comparison (Table 1). Through standardized analysis (Figs. 2 and 5; Table 2), one representative photograph was selected from each dimension, resulting in a total of eight photographs chosen as the official experimental samples (Fig. 6).

Experimental equipment and procedures

With the aid of a MacBook Pro with a high-resolution display of 2880*1800, connected to a Shimmer3 sensor via Tobii Pro Lab, different scene samples were played independently to provide subjects with an excellent visual experience. There are 8 photographs showcasing various landscape attributes (Fig. 6). Besides, this study employed a within-participants design. All participants viewed images from four categories of landscape stimuli. To control for order effects, we created 12 counterbalanced presentation sequences and randomly assigned participants to one of these sequences. Presentation order was used solely to control for order effects and was not treated as an independent variable in the hypothesis testing. Specifically, the subjects underwent physiological and psychological data recording without visual stimuli as the control data. Data analysis

Firstly, we formulated the following hypotheses: (1) The visual attributes of landscape spaces (e.g., green space accessibility, enclosure, hue, and artificiality) are associated with the emotional benefits of younger older adults. (2) The visual attributes of landscape spaces are associated with the behavioral intention of younger older adults. (3) The behavioral intention of younger older adults is associated with emotional benefits. (4) Behavioral intention mediates the association between landscape visual attributes and emotional benefits.

In the data processing phase, artifacts were removed using Ledalab (MATLAB), followed by continuous decomposition analysis (CDA) to decompose the electrodermal signal into phasic (SCR) and tonic (SCL) components. The event-related ISCR scores of all participants were exported to Excel for subsequent analyses. Additionally, SPSS was used to conduct reliability tests and KMO/Bartlett’s tests for the behavioral intention and PANAS scales (see Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), as well as normality tests. Non-normally distributed data underwent transformation; if the transformed data still did not satisfy parametric assumptions, nonparametric tests were applied.

During the comparative and correlational analysis phase, normality tests were conducted first with the aid of SPSS, followed by descriptive statistical analysis, ANOVA, and Kruskal-Wallis non-parametric tests to examine the differences in landscape perception across spatial attributes. Besides, Spearman’s rank-order/Pearson’s product-moment correlation analyses were used to explore the associations between community landscape attributes, younger older adults, and emotional benefits, as well as inter-dimensional correlations. These analytical outcomes are presented in section “Results”.

Subsequently, at the path analysis and conceptual model validation stage, we conducted regression-based mediation analysis in SPSS to test these hypotheses. Specifically: (1) we first fitted regression models to examine the direct effects of the independent variables (landscape visual attributes) on the outcome variable (emotional benefits); (2) we then examined the effects of the independent variables on the mediator (behavioral intention), as well as the effect of the mediator on the outcome variable; and (3) Additionally, in the Supplementary Materials, bootstrap resampling to examine the empirical detectability of the indirect effects under different hypothetical sample sizes, and conducted an E-value sensitivity analysis to assess the sensitivity of the total effects to potential unmeasured confounding, thereby providing supplementary evidence on the stability of the main findings under the current sample structure (supplement). These procedures allowed us to clarify the pathway relationships among landscape visual attributes, behavioral intention, and emotional benefits, and to provide more targeted theoretical guidance for landscape design.

The custom code used for semantic segmentation, color analysis, ISCR extraction, bootstrap resampling, and sensitivity analyses is publicly available on Zenodo at 10.5281/zenodo.18772183. This archived version corresponds to the version used to generate the results reported in this paper. There are no restrictions on access.

Results

The relationship between landscape space types and behavioral intention

There were significant differences in the behavioral intention of younger older adults between the four types of landscape spaces (p < 0.001). The landscape space visual attributes regarding the intensity of intention can be ranked from high to low as LSE > GSA> LSH> AoLS (Table 5). As shown in Table 5, LSE was strongly correlated with the behavioral intention of younger older adults, and GSA and LSH were related to the behavioral intention. GSA, LSE, and LSH can enhance the behavioral intention of younger older adults to go out for activities. On the contrary, AoLS was significantly negatively correlated with behavioral intention, and the enhancement of AoLS attenuated the behavioral intention of younger older adults.

The relationship between landscape space types and emotional benefits

The four types of landscape spaces demonstrated significantly higher efficacy in inducing positive emotions than in inducing negative emotions (Table 6), indicating that their emotional impacts on younger older adults were primarily mediated through the enhancement of positive emotions rather than the mitigation of negative emotions. Negative emotional psychological samples and corresponding ISCR physiological data were excluded to reduce confounding variables and ensure the rigor of the emotional benefit data. As shown in Table 7, there were significant differences in the degree of positive emotional arousal between the four visually defined landscape-space types among younger older adults (p < 0.001). They can be ranked from high to low as LSE > LSH> GSA> AoLS. In addition, the visual attributes of the landscape spaces were closely related to the emotional benefits of younger older adults (Table 7). Among them, LSH and GSA were significantly positively correlated with the emotional benefits of younger older adults, while LSE was significantly negatively correlated with the emotional benefits of younger older adults. However, AoLS was not associated with the emotional benefits of younger older adults.

Discussion

The differences in emotional benefits between various landscape space indicators

The experimental results indicated that, compared with the AoLS type, landscape spaces characterized by LSE, LSH, and GSA elicited higher levels of positive affective arousal among younger older adults (Table 7). This pattern is consistent with the notion that spaces affording greater contact with nature can activate humans’ innate affinity for natural environments; exposure to natural landscapes may enhance positive emotions (e.g., relaxation, pleasure, and satisfaction) in younger older adults, thereby alleviating stress and symptoms of depression and anxiety61.

Conversely, when younger older adults viewed landscapes with higher AoLS, elements such as artificial water features, art installations, and decorative pavement patterns, all without interactive functions—these elements reduce visual sensitivity to natural features and, in turn, diminish the landscape space’s positive emotional arousal for this population (excluding functional man-made elements, such as benches, pavilions, or corridors). Some previous studies have also shown that artificial elements (e.g., architectural forms and artificial grass) are positively correlated with depression in older adults, while natural landscape features (e.g., green spaces and real grass) are negatively correlated with psychological disorders in older adults62,63.

Interestingly, the mean level of emotional benefits in the LSE category was markedly higher than those in the LSH and GSA categories, whereas the emotional benefits of LSH and GSA were comparable; however, high enclosure was not conducive to eliciting positive affective arousal. Consistent with the findings of other related studies, excessively high enclosure can make the elderly feel cramped and suppressed, thereby adversely affecting their emotional benefits64,65.

Furthermore, the results of this study demonstrated that cool color tones exhibited a higher positive emotional arousal benefit for younger older adults than those with warm color tones. This may be explained by the fact that cool-toned floral landscapes can provide a relaxing effect and enhance positive emotions11.

The impact of behavioral intention on the effectiveness of attaining emotional benefits

Based on the mediation model “Visual attributes of park landscape space -> Behavioral intention for active activities -> Emotional benefits,” it was found that GSA, LSH, LSE, and lower AoLS were all significantly and positively associated with behavioral intention for active engagement among younger older adults (Fig. 7). Notably, the positive effect of GSA on emotional benefits was partly transmitted through this mediating pathway.

Consistent evidence suggests that improved GSA can promote the behavioral intention and duration of active activities among younger older adults, thereby helping to increase their physical activity levels66. However, intention toward walking and physical activity only partially accounts for the effect of GSA on emotional benefits; green space exposure appears to retain a direct effect on mental health67.

The results of this study showed that the negative association between AoLS and emotional benefits was partly offset via the mediating role of behavioral intention, showing a suppressor (inconsistent mediation) pattern in this sample (Fig. 7).

For example, built facilities such as benches and pavilions can increase older adults’ behavioral intention by providing comfortable resting settings, thereby reducing stress and discomfort and indirectly improving psychological comfort and emotional states68. In addition, age-inclusive play installations have been reported to meet the needs of both children and older adults; such facilities may increase older adults’ intention to be active in these spaces and foster a sense of being needed and involved, while enhancing social connection and alleviating loneliness69,70.

Notably, the indirect effect of LSH via behavioral intention for active engagement was small and negative, suggesting that in cool-toned settings, higher intention for active engagement may slightly offset the emotion-recovery benefits associated with cool hues (Fig. 7).

Color psychology research indicates that cool hues are typically associated with positive, low-arousal affect and are more conducive to calm and relaxed states71. Moreover, studies on green-space activity patterns have shown that different modes of engagement (e.g., sitting vs. walking) can produce distinct profiles of stress reduction and attentional restoration, implying that behavioral mode moderates environmental restorative effects72.

Taken together with the small, slightly negative indirect effect of LSH via behavioral intention, we cautiously speculate that, in predominantly cool-toned landscapes, higher behavioral intention may, to some extent, alter or attenuate the emotional benefits associated with “quiet restoration.” However, evidence remains limited, and this mechanism warrants further verification in future research.

Surprisingly, an increase in LSE exerted a negative impact on emotional benefits (Table 7), but it can enhance the behavioral intention for active activities among younger older adults (Table 5), and this positive behavioral intention cannot improve the physiological indicators of emotional benefits (Fig. 7). During the psychological questionnaires after the experiment, some subjects stated that spaces with high LSE can bring them a cool and comfortable feeling.

It has been corroborated that thermal comfort is an important factor affecting the mobility of younger older adults73,74. Therefore, it is required to further explore how the enclosure can effectively affect the emotional benefits of younger older adults and how to balance these two aspects.

In addition, LSE was dichotomized into low and high groups, and potential nonlinear associations were not formally tested. Thus, the findings only suggest that lower LSE was associated with greater emotional benefits than higher LSE within the observed range; more complex nonlinear patterns warrant further investigation.

In conclusion, from the perspective of visual perception of health promotion, the effective and positive emotional activation of community park landscape spaces for younger older adults can be gradually optimized by enhancing naturalness, enriching cool-toned ornamental landscapes, ensuring adequate GSA, and appropriately managing LSE and the configuration of AoLS types.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, consistent with Brunswik’s representative design, this study employed photographs of real-world landscapes as visual stimuli, which strengthens ecological validity and reduces participant fatigue. However, because visual attributes were not independently manipulated on a common baseline image, residual between-image heterogeneity is unavoidable and may constrain the precision of causal attribution to any single visual variable.

Second, to emphasize the potential emotional benefits of landscape visual attributes, observations exhibiting negative affective responses and their corresponding physiological records were excluded from the primary analyses (see section “The relationship between landscape space types and behavioral intention”). This decision may introduce selection bias, and the findings may therefore be most applicable to younger older adults and contexts not characterized by pronounced negative emotional responses.

Third, participants were recruited from cities in eastern China, and the experiment was conducted in a standardized indoor setting using photograph-based stimuli (see section “Experimental image preparation”). Although this design reduces confounding from momentary weather conditions, onsite environmental variability, and place attachment, it cannot fully account for broader regional cultural influences and long-term climatic context; thus, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to other settings.

Conclusions

This study examined differences in emotional benefits for younger older adults across four types of landscape spaces with distinct visual attributes. Besides, the impact of individual behavioral intention on its effectiveness was also investigated to reveal the multiple impact pathways of landscape space visual attributes on the emotional benefits of younger older adults. The main findings of the study are elucidated as follows. (1) Among the four visual-attribute types, the LSE type exhibited the highest mean positive affective arousal, followed by LSH, GSA, and AoLS. Notably, both LSE and AoLS displayed “double-edged” effects: excessive enclosure and non-functional artificial elements may attenuate emotional benefits. (2) Mediation analyses highlighted behavioral intention for active engagement as a critical mechanism linking visual attributes to effectively realized emotional benefits. Specifically, the effect of GSA on emotional benefits was partly mediated by behavioral intention; the negative direct association of AoLS with emotional benefits was partly counterbalanced by its positive association with behavioral intention (i.e., a suppressor/inconsistent mediation pattern); and cool-toned landscapes yielded greater emotional benefits than warm-toned ones, with a small negative indirect effect via behavioral intention that was outweighed by the direct positive effect.

With community park landscape spaces as the research objects, this study was conducted based on the coupling of physiological psychology, landscape perception theory, and cognitive evaluation theory. These findings may provide novel insights for the study of the correlation of the objective characteristics of the environment with individual subjective visual perception, behavioral intention, and objective physiological responses of emotional arousal. Nonetheless, given the limited sample and the indoor, image-based experimental paradigm, extrapolation to other regions and real-world contexts should be made with caution. Future studies should replicate and extend these findings with larger samples, multisensory in-situ settings, and finer-grained gradients of visual attributes.
