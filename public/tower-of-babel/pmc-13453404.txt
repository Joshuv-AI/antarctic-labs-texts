Regulated Cell Death in Calcium Oxalate Stone Disease: From Tubular Epithelial Injury to Inflammatory Amplification
Authors: Xijie Ding, Chaoyue Ji, Guojun Chen, Jianxing Li, Weiguo Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalcium oxalate (CaOx) stone disease begins with urinary supersaturation. However, crystallization alone does not explain crystal retention, lesion progression, or inflammatory amplification. Current evidence suggests that crystal-exposed renal epithelium undergoes regulated stress responses that alter adhesion, membrane integrity, organellar homeostasis, and inflammatory signaling. These epithelial responses link luminal crystal contact to tissue injury and persistence. This review evaluates regulated cell death pathways in CaOx nephrolithiasis, oxalate nephropathy, and related crystal-induced renal injury, with emphasis on lesion context and evidence depth. Ferroptosis and pyroptosis currently have the strongest direct support in epithelial and preclinical CaOx-related models, but this pattern is not uniform across all lesion settings. Apoptosis is well supported but is best viewed as one output of broader epithelial stress. Regulated necrosis is documented mainly in crystal nephropathy, nephrocalcinosis, and acute oxalate injury. Autophagy requires context- and flux-aware interpretation, whereas mitophagy appears more consistently protective through the removal of damaged mitochondria. Immune-cell membrane rupture and neutrophil extracellular trap formation may add an inflammatory amplification layer. Direct evidence for cuproptosis and PANoptosis remains preliminary. Overall, crystal injury, regulated cell death, and inflammatory responses appear to interact across several CaOx-related lesion settings. Lesion-specific human validation remains limited.Keywords: calcium oxalate stone disease, regulated cell death, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, oxalate nephropathy

Introduction

Calcium oxalate (CaOx) stone disease begins with urinary supersaturation, but supersaturation alone does not determine whether crystals are retained within the kidney, whether papillary lesions progress, or whether local injury becomes sustained. Contemporary models of stone formation therefore distinguish physicochemical driving forces from the biologic events that govern papillary plaque exposure, papillary plugging, tubular crystal attachment, and subsequent stone overgrowth.1,2 Once crystals contact renal epithelium, they can perturb membrane asymmetry, epithelial polarity, redox balance, organellar homeostasis, and inflammatory signaling.3,4 In this sense, CaOx stone disease cannot be understood simply as crystal precipitation in urine; it also depends on how renal tissue responds to crystal exposure and how those responses vary across lesion settings. A simplified conceptual framework linking CaOx crystal exposure, tubular epithelial injury, regulated cell death, and inflammatory amplification is shown in Figure 1.

Here, regulated cell death is used in a mechanistically disciplined sense rather than as a catch-all label for tubular injury. The focus is on pathways for which direct CaOx-, oxalate-, or crystal-injury studies provide at least some execution-level support in stone-relevant renal systems. Broader reviews of regulated cell death in kidney disease are cited mainly for terminology, pathway crosstalk, and interpretation of indirect evidence, not as direct evidence for CaOx stone disease.5–7 This distinction is important because some regulated death pathways are supported by much stronger direct CaOx evidence than others, whereas several newer labels remain preliminary in direct stone-related settings.5,6

Current evidence is uneven not only across regulated death pathways, but also across lesion settings. Within direct epithelial and preclinical CaOx-related models, pyroptosis and ferroptosis currently have deeper mechanistic support than several other pathway labels, but that pattern should not be treated as a universal hierarchy across all stone phenotypes or renal lesions. Apoptosis remains well supported in tubular injury models, but current evidence suggests that it is better viewed as one downstream output of broader epithelial stress rather than as a complete explanation for crystal injury. By contrast, the most direct literature on regulated necrosis is concentrated in acute oxalate nephropathy, nephrocalcinosis, and related acute oxalate injury models, especially for necroptosis and mitochondrial permeability transition-related necrosis. Autophagy likewise requires flux-aware interpretation, and immune-cell membrane rupture or neutrophil extracellular trap formation is better framed as inflammatory amplification than as a core epithelial-autonomous program. That lesion structure should be kept explicit from the outset. Idiopathic CaOx papillary plaque-bearing disease, papillary plugging, nephrocalcinosis, and acute oxalate nephropathy are related crystal-associated settings, but they are not biologically interchangeable. Human studies already show that plaque-rich papillae, plug-dominant lesions, and oxalate nephropathy differ in structural context, inflammatory features, and associations with renal function.34–37 Most mechanistic work, however, still comes from cell systems and animal models rather than human lesion-specific data. For that reason, the following sections prioritize direct stone-related evidence, state lesion context whenever it materially affects interpretation, and avoid treating findings from one crystal-injury setting as though they automatically apply to all others. Why Tubular Epithelial Injury Drives Crystal Adhesion and Retention

One major reason to place tubular epithelial injury near the center of CaOx retention biology is that injured epithelium becomes more adhesive to crystals. In classic oxalate-exposure models, oxalate rapidly redistributed phosphatidylserine (PS) from the inner to the outer leaflet of the epithelial plasma membrane, and annexin V functionally blocked the resulting increase in crystal attachment.38,39 A complementary study in injured collecting duct cells showed that CaOx binding can arise through more than one structural route: loss of lipid asymmetry and loss of epithelial polarity each created a crystal-binding state.40 These experiments remain foundational because they connect epithelial injury to an intrarenal retention step rather than treating cell damage as a passive consequence of supersaturation.

Subsequent in vivo and receptor-level studies sharpened that interpretation. Injured or regenerating rat tubules expressing hyaluronan, osteopontin, and CD44 selectively retained intratubular CaOx crystals, whereas early crystalluria without epithelial injury did not.41 Osteopontin suppression weakened CaOx attachment to renal epithelial cells, and annexin II was identified on the apical epithelial surface as a functional COM-binding protein whose blockade reduced crystal adhesion.42,43 Together, these studies define the epithelial adhesion interface as an injury-induced surface phenotype rather than a purely urinary event.

More recent direct CaOx studies extend the same idea in mechanistic terms. In HK-2 cells, oxalate activates an ER stress-ROS-NF-κB program with mitochondrial injury, placing crystal-exposed tubules in a state compatible with altered membrane behavior and retention.10 In a more specific pyroptosis-oriented setting, Xiang et al13 linked NLRP3-caspase-1-GSDMD activation to increased expression of osteopontin, CD44, MCP-1, and ITGB1, and showed that perturbing GSDMD altered epithelial crystal adhesion and renal crystal deposition. Yang et al14 further showed that GSDMD-mediated membrane injury is counterbalanced by CHMP4B-dependent repair, and that stronger repair reduced tubular injury and crystal burden. These studies help clarify how tubular epithelial injury may promote crystal retention, but they are still based on model systems and should not be generalized to all papillary lesion settings.

Taken together, the evidence supports a lesion-bounded conclusion: tubular epithelial injury can promote crystal retention by increasing surface adhesiveness and by impairing restoration of normal membrane integrity. That inference is strongest for tubular adhesion systems, nephrocalcinosis-type models, and related epithelial crystal-retention settings. It does not, by itself, determine how much of papillary plaque exposure, papillary plugging, or mature stone overgrowth is driven by the same epithelial events. The pathway sections that follow should therefore be read in lesion context, with particular attention to whether a given pathway influences epithelial adhesion, membrane injury, or crystal retention.

Once that injury-retention link is established, the next interpretive question is why broadly similar crystalline insults can produce different regulated death outputs across different models and lesions.

Shared Upstream Stress Architecture

Direct CaOx studies suggest that no single upstream trigger explains the full injury spectrum; instead, several stress responses recur across different model settings. Oxalate and CaOx crystals repeatedly induce ROS accumulation, ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, calcium dysregulation, lysosomal stress, and inflammatory priming in tubular cells, even when downstream phenotypes are classified differently.3,4,10,12,15,18,21,44,45 The same broad stresses can feed distinct execution programs: oxalate activates ER stress markers together with ROS and NF-κB signaling in apoptosis-oriented models;10 COM and COD crystals differentially engage GRP78, CHOP, TXNIP, NLRP3, caspase-1, and GSDMD cleavage in pyroptosis-oriented systems;12 oxalate increases autophagy-linked iron mobilization and can drive an ER stress-ATF4-CHAC1 axis that weakens GPX4-dependent defense in ferroptosis;15,18 and acute oxalate injury couples crystal phagocytosis, lysosomal cathepsin leakage, mitochondrial permeability transition, and ROS generation in regulated necrosis.21

These overlaps help explain why multiple forms of regulated cell death can emerge within the broader CaOx injury spectrum. ER stress is not specific to apoptosis, and ROS is not specific to ferroptosis or inflammasome activation.3,4,10,12,15,18,21,44,45 The downstream phenotype appears to depend on variables such as crystal burden, crystal phase, uptake route, calcium load, repair capacity, cell type, and lesion setting rather than on wholly distinct upstream triggers. Nong et al12 illustrated this point directly by showing that pathogenic calcium oxalate monohydrate (COM) produced stronger ER stress and pyroptotic signaling than physiologic calcium oxalate dihydrate (COD) in size-matched HK-2 experiments. CaOx crystals should therefore not be treated as biologically interchangeable stimuli, and results from one crystal form or injury model should not be generalized without qualification.

This shared-stress framework also helps clarify why not every upstream disturbance should be treated as a separate death modality. In acute oxalate injury, for example, lysosomal destabilization is clearly involved in crystal handling and may lie upstream of mitochondrial permeability transition, but the current stone literature does not justify defining lysosome-dependent cell death as a separate formal modality in this setting.5,21 Calcium fluxes likewise participate in epithelial injury and membrane repair during pyroptosis, yet calcium overload alone does not define an independent pathway category.13,14 Taken together, the current literature suggests that several recurring upstream stresses can feed different downstream death programs, with the balance varying across models and lesion settings. Within this shared-stress framework, apoptosis remains relevant, but it is best interpreted as one regulated output within a broader epithelial stress network rather than as an isolated explanation for CaOx injury.

Apoptosis

Apoptosis remains the longest-recognized regulated death program in CaOx-related tubular injury, and direct support for its involvement is strongest in oxalate-exposed epithelial systems rather than in lesion-resolved human stone tissue. In these models, ROS accumulation, ER stress, CHOP induction, mitochondrial dysfunction, and caspase-associated cell death have all been documented.8–10 Abhishek et al8 linked oxalate-induced oxidative stress to ER stress-mediated apoptosis and showed only partial rescue with antioxidant treatment, supporting ROS-ER stress coupling rather than nonspecific toxicity. Song et al9 likewise showed that melatonin reduced oxalate-induced ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis in HK-2 cells through AMPK-dependent signaling, providing interventional support in a direct tubular oxalate system.

Current evidence suggests that apoptosis is better understood as part of a broader epithelial stress response than as an isolated explanation for CaOx injury. Ming et al10 integrated ER stress, ROS, NF-κB activation, mitochondrial abnormalities, and apoptotic injury in both HK-2 cells and a glyoxylic-acid mouse model, making it difficult to separate apoptosis from the wider epithelial stress network. In this setting, apoptosis is best viewed as a controlled injury output that weakens epithelial integrity and can coexist with other death programs rather than dominating the phenotype.

Additional work points to regulatory layers beyond generalized oxidative stress. Chen et al46 reported that ALKBH5-mediated m6A demethylation destabilized MUC1 and promoted apoptosis through PI3K/Akt-related signaling in renal tubular epithelial cells. This broadens the mechanistic scope of apoptosis beyond generalized oxidative stress, although lesion-level and in vivo evidence remains less extensive than that for pyroptosis or ferroptosis. Apoptosis should therefore not be regarded as a marginal pathway in CaOx-related injury. It may contribute to chronic epithelial injury by promoting epithelial cell loss, impaired epithelial integrity, maladaptive repair, tubular remodeling, and an injury-permissive microenvironment. This role may be particularly relevant in chronic or low-grade lesion settings, where repeated sublethal stress and controlled cell loss can shape tissue remodeling without overt lytic inflammation. Nevertheless, current evidence does not support apoptosis as a single dominant or unifying mechanism across all CaOx-related lesion contexts.

Pyroptosis

Among the lytic death pathways studied in CaOx-related tubular epithelial models, pyroptosis currently has some of the strongest direct mechanistic support. The strongest studies do more than report inflammasome-associated cytokines; they document a canonical NLRP3-caspase-1-GSDMD axis together with membrane injury, ultrastructural change, rescue experiments, or downstream effects on crystal deposition.11–13,47–50 This distinction is important because inflammasome activation alone is insufficient to establish pyroptotic death. In the CaOx field, the most persuasive evidence links inflammasome signaling to gasdermin cleavage and epithelial membrane damage in direct tubular models.11,47,48 Ding et al47 showed that CaOx injury in vivo and in vitro was associated with NLRP3, caspase-1, GSDMD, and IL-1β signaling, and that vitexin suppressed these readouts together with inflammatory infiltration and crystal burden. Gan et al48 then showed that miR-141-3p restrained NLRP3-mediated pyroptotic injury in CaOx-treated HK-2 cells, adding an upstream regulatory layer. A key epithelial study was provided by Chen et al11, who showed that ROS-dependent NLRP3-GSDMD signaling mediated oxalate-induced pyroptotic injury in vitro and in vivo, that GSDMD inhibition preserved membrane integrity, and that GSDMD deficiency reduced crystal formation in glyoxylic-acid mice. That study also reported ultrastructural features compatible with pyroptosis in oxalate-stimulated HK-2 cells, making the case stronger than a marker-only interpretation.

Recent studies further indicate that pyroptosis in CaOx models varies with crystal composition, crystal size, and calcium loading rather than following a single uniform pattern. Nong et al12 showed that COM crystals provoke stronger ER stress and pyroptotic responses than COD crystals in size-matched HK-2 experiments, indicating that crystal phase biases pathway engagement. Chen et al50 further showed that COM crystal size matters: smaller crystals produced higher pyroptotic rates and appeared more dependent on endocytosis, whereas larger crystals were more closely linked to adhesion-associated injury. In other words, both crystal composition and crystal size shape how strongly, and by what route, pyroptosis is engaged.

Pyroptosis is also important because it links inflammatory membrane injury to adhesion and retention. Xiang et al13 showed that high calcium activated ROS-dependent NLRP3-caspase-1-GSDMD pyroptosis while increasing osteopontin, CD44, MCP-1, and ITGB1; GSDMD knockdown reduced epithelial adhesion and crystal attachment, whereas overexpression had the opposite effect. Yang et al14 added an important refinement by showing that GSDMD-mediated injury is dynamically opposed by CHMP4B-dependent membrane repair. Augmenting CHMP4B reduced epithelial injury and crystal deposition, and combined CHMP4B enhancement plus GSDMD inhibition outperformed either maneuver alone. In these models, the extent of pyroptotic injury appears to depend not only on GSDMD pore formation but also on the capacity for membrane repair.

Non-canonical pyroptosis remains relevant but more conditional. Yao et al49 reported that lipopolysaccharide amplified CaOx crystal deposition through caspase-11-related non-canonical inflammasome signaling and that melatonin mitigated this effect, a result best interpreted as a primed inflammatory setting rather than baseline CaOx biology. Overall, the available evidence suggests three main points: direct epithelial pyroptosis is well supported in selected CaOx-related tubular models by GSDMD-centered mechanistic studies; crystal phase, crystal size, calcium load, and ROS signaling can influence the phenotype; and pyroptotic membrane injury may contribute to tubular crystal retention by altering epithelial adhesiveness and repair capacity. None of these observations, however, justifies treating pyroptosis as a universal explanation across papillary plaque, papillary plugging, nephrocalcinosis, and oxalate nephropathy.

Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis currently has one of the broadest direct evidence bases among regulated cell death pathways studied in CaOx-related epithelial and preclinical models. The literature includes canonical machinery, rescue experiments, cell-type localization, and increasingly specific upstream regulation. Across direct oxalate and CaOx systems, investigators have repeatedly documented lipid peroxidation, glutathione depletion, iron overload, GPX4 loss, SLC7A11 suppression, ACSL4 induction, mitochondrial injury, and functional rescue by ferroptosis-directed interventions.15–17,19,51–53 That breadth justifies detailed discussion, but it should still be interpreted in relation to model type and lesion setting rather than as proof of uniform pathway dominance across CaOx disease.54

A major reason ferroptosis stands out in this field is the consistency of functional rescue across direct CaOx models. Song et al15 showed that oxalate activated autophagy and induced ferroptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells, with NCOA4-dependent iron mobilization supporting a ferritinophagy-like amplification mechanism. Xie et al16 then demonstrated that ferrostatin-1 alleviated oxalate-induced tubular injury, interstitial fibrosis, and CaOx stone formation while restoring GPX4/SLC7A11-associated defense and reducing oxidative stress. Dong et al51 extended this intervention literature by showing that Schizandrin B ameliorated nephrolithiasis through GSK3beta/Nrf2-regulated ferroptosis control, again linking ferroptosis rescue to lower crystal burden rather than to marker changes alone. Additional intervention studies reinforce the same direction rather than redefine it: p53 deacetylation limited CaOx deposition-associated fibrosis by suppressing ferroptosis, luteolin attenuated Nr4a1-mediated ferroptotic injury, and both edaravone and paliperidone reduced CaOx-induced tubular damage by suppressing autophagy-linked ferroptosis.55–58 These later studies are supportive rather than equal to the strongest canonical papers because most remain single-laboratory intervention studies, but they point in the same mechanistic direction.

The case is further strengthened by studies that localize ferroptotic injury to specific tubular populations and directly manipulate canonical execution and defense nodes. Duan et al17 used single-cell transcriptomics in CaOx nephrocalcinosis and identified proximal tubular cells as the most severely affected epithelial population, with ferroptosis signals enriched in those cells. They further showed that Sirt1 suppressed CaOx-induced ferroptosis through PGC-1alpha-mediated coactivation of NRF2 and GPX4 transcription, and that tubular Sirt1 deficiency aggravated crystal deposition and kidney injury in vivo. Ye et al19 independently strengthened the canonical core by showing in oxalate-induced AKI that Acsl4 deficiency protected renal tubules whereas Fsp1 deficiency worsened injury; GPX4 or FSP1 overexpression in tubular cells reduced lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. Together, these studies move the field beyond correlative ferroptosis markers and directly manipulate execution and defense nodes in tubular epithelium.

Recent studies also clarify upstream regulation of ferroptosis in CaOx-related injury. Yan et al52 showed that a SOX4/EZH2/SLC7A11 axis promoted ferroptosis in CaOx crystal deposition-induced kidney injury, linking epigenetic repression of SLC7A11 to tubular vulnerability. Dong et al18 then identified ER stress-dependent ferroptosis through the ATF4-CHAC1 axis; in that study, 4-phenylbutyric acid and CHAC1 deficiency restored glutathione, reduced Fe2+ accumulation and lipid peroxidation, improved renal function, and lowered crystal deposition, whereas ATF4 depletion suppressed CHAC1 upregulation. The paliperidone study reaches the same interface from a different angle by placing CHAC1 within an autophagy-linked ferroptotic program.58 Together, these data support an ER stress-ferroptosis link in direct CaOx-related models rather than leaving ferroptosis framed only as a generic oxidative-stress endpoint.

The relationship between autophagy and ferroptosis is also important here, although it should be interpreted within clear boundaries. Song et al15 showed that oxalate-activated autophagy promoted ferroptosis and that NCOA4 knockdown reduced this response, supporting ferritinophagy-like iron mobilization as an amplifier of epithelial damage rather than an independent pathway category. Ferritinophagy is therefore best handled as a ferroptosis-autophagy interface, not as a standalone section and not as evidence that autophagy is uniformly harmful in stone disease. The more recent edaravone and paliperidone studies extend this interface beyond iron mobilization alone by showing that autophagy modulation can alter ferroptotic outcome in direct CaOx models.57,58 At a broader systems level, Liu et al53 linked gut microbiota-bile acid crosstalk to HSP90alpha-mediated ferroptosis and crystal adhesion, suggesting that ferroptotic susceptibility can be shaped beyond the individual tubular cell.

The main uncertainty is not whether ferroptotic signaling exists, but how far these findings translate across lesion settings, especially in human disease. Human support remains limited to tissue, transcriptomic, and metabolic datasets, particularly in the studies by Yan and Liu, and no direct human study shows that ferroptosis is the dominant death program across the full spectrum of CaOx stone phenotypes.52,53 Some of the strongest execution-level evidence, including the ACSL4/GPX4/FSP1 study, comes from acute oxalate injury or nephropathy models rather than idiopathic papillary stone disease.19 Taken together, current evidence supports ferroptosis as a major and comparatively well-developed pathway in CaOx-related crystal injury, while lesion-specific human confirmation remains limited.

Regulated Necrosis: Necroptosis and Mitochondrial Permeability Transition-Related Necrosis

The regulated-necrosis literature in CaOx-related disease is smaller than the ferroptosis or pyroptosis literatures, but it includes landmark studies that are central to crystal nephropathy and acute oxalate injury. Mulay et al20 provided foundational evidence that crystal cytotoxicity can involve RIPK3-MLKL-mediated necroptosis rather than passive necrosis alone. In multiple cell systems and mouse oxalate nephropathy, crystals induced caspase-independent death that was attenuated by necrostatin-1, RIPK3 or MLKL suppression, and genetic deletion of Ripk3 or Mlkl. The same study also identified phosphorylated MLKL-positive tubular cells in human oxalate crystal-related acute kidney injury, making it one of the few direct links between mechanistic crystal injury work and human renal tissue. That study also helps define the lesion context in which regulated necrosis is most relevant, namely crystal nephropathy, acute oxalate injury, and necroinflammation, rather than papillary stone lesions more broadly. MPT-related necrosis is best discussed alongside necroptosis, because current evidence suggests that the two processes overlap in acute crystal injury rather than operating as entirely separate compartments. In their 2019 JASN study, Mulay et al21 showed that crystal phagocytosis led to lysosomal cathepsin leakage, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, ROS generation, and PPIF-dependent mitochondrial permeability transition; Ppif deficiency or mitochondrial permeability transition inhibition attenuated oxalate-induced AKI. Mlkl deficiency was also protective, and dual blockade produced only partly additive effects, supporting the idea that these processes overlap in acute crystal injury and are best interpreted together.

Recent studies extend this framework without changing its phenotype restriction. Hou et al22 showed that Cpd-42 reduced RIPK3-necrosome formation, inflammation, renal injury, and crystal deposition in CaOx nephrocalcinosis, with no additional benefit in RIPK3-deficient settings. Prajapati et al59 likewise showed that 6,7-dihydroxycoumarin reduced MLKL phosphorylation and ameliorated crystal-induced necroptosis, providing supportive intervention evidence at the terminal execution step. Sedmaki et al60 further showed in acute oxalate nephropathy that HDAC6 inhibition reduced IL-1β secretion together with RIP kinase-mediated necroptosis, linking inflammatory signaling to tubular regulated necrosis. A more recent JASN study implicated ZBP1 sensing of mitochondrial Z-DNA in oxalate-induced acute kidney injury, suggesting that upstream danger sensing in necroptosis is becoming more refined.61

Overall, regulated necrosis is best interpreted in crystal nephropathy, nephrocalcinosis, and acute oxalate injury rather than generalized across all CaOx stone lesions. Necroptosis is directly supported in these settings, whereas MPT-related necrosis is better considered alongside it as a related lytic process. By contrast, current evidence is still insufficient to apply the same regulated-necrosis framework to plaque-dominant or other idiopathic papillary CaOx phenotypes. Even the biopsy literature on oxalate nephropathy is more useful for lesion restriction than for universalizing necroptosis across CaOx stone disease.20,21,62

Context-Dependent Autophagy and Protective Mitophagy

Autophagy should not be described as uniformly protective or harmful in CaOx disease. Its apparent role depends on flux status, cargo selectivity, model system, and lesion context. Wu et al23 showed that resveratrol enhanced TFEB-dependent autophagy and reduced oxidative injury, apoptosis, inflammatory cytokines, osteogenic change, and crystal deposition; these effects were blunted by TFEB knockdown, supporting a protective role linked to restored cellular homeostasis rather than to marker accumulation alone. A systematic review reached a similar conclusion at a broader level, noting that studies reporting benefit from either autophagy activation or inhibition may still be internally consistent because they often assess different stages of injury, different systems, or different aspects of autophagic flux.63 In a glyoxylate model, dapagliflozin reduced CaOx nephrolithiasis not by simply increasing autophagy markers, but by restoring impaired autophagic flux, and this protection was lost with hydroxychloroquine co-administration.25 The key question, therefore, is not whether autophagy is beneficial or harmful in the abstract, but whether it restores effective turnover of damaged cellular components or instead contributes to maladaptive stress.

Direct CaOx studies illustrate why autophagy has divergent effects in different models. In the resveratrol study, TFEB-dependent autophagy was associated with reduced oxidative injury, inflammatory signaling, apoptosis, osteogenic change, and crystal deposition.23 In the dapagliflozin study, protection was linked to restoration of impaired autophagic flux rather than to simple accumulation of autophagy markers.25 Similarly, NAT10-ULK1 regulation reduced COM-induced oxidative stress, apoptosis, pyroptosis-associated injury, and crystal deposition.24 These findings support a protective role when autophagy helps restore cellular homeostasis or limits downstream injury. By contrast, autophagy can also amplify epithelial damage. Song et al15 showed that oxalate-activated autophagy promoted ferroptosis through BECN1-dependent signaling and NCOA4-linked iron mobilization. Other studies further suggest that the local microenvironment can shift autophagy toward injury-amplifying effects. CaOx-conditioned macrophage exosomes promoted HK-2 apoptosis through autophagy, and linoleic acid exacerbated COM-induced epithelial injury while enhancing autophagic responses.64,65 Thus, autophagy-related markers such as LC3, BECN1, TFEB, ULK1, or p62 need to be interpreted together with flux status, cargo selectivity, and downstream injury outcomes.

Overall, the current CaOx autophagy literature supports a context-dependent view. These findings are most informative in direct epithelial and preclinical models and should not be generalized into a single hierarchy across all CaOx lesion settings. Within the broader autophagy literature, mitophagy has so far been described more consistently as a protective response in CaOx models. Zhou et al26 showed that melatonin restored AMPK/PINK1-Parkin-mediated mitophagy, reduced ROS accumulation, restrained ferroptosis, and ameliorated crystal-associated renal injury in vitro and in vivo. Sun et al28 likewise reported that urolithin A restored oxalate-suppressed mitophagy through a PCK1-linked mechanism, improved mitochondrial homeostasis, and reduced crystal formation and renal injury. Together, these studies suggest that preserved mitophagy may limit mitochondrial dysfunction and redox imbalance in selected CaOx models, thereby attenuating ferroptotic and related injury. Even so, the current evidence base remains modest and is still dominated by interventional preclinical systems rather than human lesion-specific data. Mitophagy is therefore better viewed as a potentially protective component of the broader autophagy network than as a uniform solution to tubular crystal injury across all CaOx-related lesions.

Immune-Cell Death and Inflammatory Amplification

The epithelial sections above do not fully explain CaOx-related injury, because crystal disease also involves multicellular inflammatory responses. The most direct human evidence in this area comes from acute oxalate nephropathy rather than idiopathic papillary CaOx stone disease. Bao et al66 found increased renal NLRP3, caspase-1, and GSDMD staining together with altered M1/M2 macrophage distribution, and macrophage balance was associated with chronicity at follow-up. These findings suggest that oxalate-related human kidney injury is not purely epithelial-autonomous, but they remain correlative and do not by themselves establish pathway dominance in patients. This human evidence also fits with earlier studies showing that macrophages can phagocytose CaOx crystals and stone fragments, while oxalate exposure can impair monocyte/macrophage bioenergetics, IL-10 signaling, lysosomal function, and autophagic competence.67,68

Preclinical studies add mechanistic depth, but they also highlight model dependence. In chronic oxalate nephropathy, Kusunoki et al69 found that GSDMD deficiency unexpectedly aggravated nephrocalcinosis because macrophages became more vulnerable to necroptosis, and necroptosis inhibition reversed much of this phenotype. Macrophage responses also vary with crystal size and burden: large micro-COM induced stronger macrophage pyroptosis and M1 polarization than nano-COM in a THP-1-derived model.70 Together, these studies suggest that immune-cell death pathways can interact, compete, and alter tissue outcome in different ways depending on the inflammatory setting. What they do not support is a single immune-cell pathway hierarchy that applies across the full range of CaOx lesion settings.

NINJ1-Mediated Macrophage Plasma Membrane Rupture

The strongest direct evidence for immune-cell lytic amplification currently comes from acute oxalate nephropathy rather than idiopathic papillary stone disease. Lin et al29 showed that NINJ1-mediated macrophage plasma membrane rupture promoted HMGB1 release and facilitated neutrophil extracellular trap formation; myeloid Ninj1 deletion attenuated tubular injury and improved renal function. These findings suggest that macrophage membrane rupture can amplify oxalate injury through DAMP release and downstream neutrophil activation. A parallel non-lytic route may also exist, because CaOx-conditioned macrophage exosomes promoted autophagy-dependent HK-2 apoptosis in vitro.64 In this setting, NINJ1 is better viewed as part of downstream immune amplification in myeloid-rich oxalate injury than as a core epithelial-autonomous pathway.

NETosis and Neutrophil-Driven Amplification

NETosis offers a related route by which immune activation may intensify crystal-associated injury. In oxalate nephropathy, Lin et al29 linked macrophage NINJ1-dependent rupture to downstream NET formation, suggesting that macrophage lysis and neutrophil responses can be coupled within the same circuit. Separate nephrolithiasis-model and patient-plasma data from Yang et al30 associated NET-related signals with calcium deposition, tubular injury, apoptosis, and renal dysfunction, while NET suppression improved these phenotypes. Direct neutrophil-crystal studies provide a more proximal mechanistic basis for this interpretation: COM altered neutrophil proteomes, increased ROS production, enhanced degranulation, and promoted NET formation.31 Together, these studies support NETosis as a downstream inflammatory amplifier, although the strength and context of the evidence still vary across CaOx lesion settings. Human support remains indirect. Circulating and tissue NET-associated signals are informative, but they do not yet show that NETosis is a dominant driver across clinical stone phenotypes. Overall, current evidence suggests that NINJ1-associated membrane rupture and NET formation can amplify crystal injury in selected inflammatory settings after epithelial injury has already occurred.

Crosstalk, Pathway Competition, and Lesion-Specific Interpretation

Once this shared stress architecture is recognized, the key question is not simply whether regulated death pathways overlap, but which outputs become more prominent in a given lesion setting. In CaOx disease, crystal phase, injury location, cell type, and intercellular propagation vary across tubular retention models, nephrocalcinosis, acute oxalate nephropathy, papillary plaque exposure, papillary plugging, and mature stone overgrowth. As a result, an intervention that appears pathway-specific in one system may act mainly upstream in another, and similar crystal burdens may resolve into different dominant phenotypes depending on repair capacity, inflammatory context, and the cells involved. The relative importance of any single pathway should therefore be assessed within the lesion and model under discussion rather than generalized across CaOx disease as a whole.

Two findings make that point concrete. Yang et al14 showed that epithelial outcome during pyroptotic stress depends not only on GSDMD pore formation but also on CHMP4B-dependent membrane repair, so lytic injury reflects a balance between damage and repair rather than a binary event. Kusunoki et al69 showed the converse in immune cells: in chronic oxalate nephropathy, GSDMD deficiency rendered macrophages more vulnerable to necroptosis and worsened disease. Together, these studies suggest that targeting a single node may not produce the same outcome across epithelial and immune compartments or across different CaOx lesion settings.

Autophagy-related pathways highlight the same issue in a different way. In direct tubular models, oxalate-activated autophagy can amplify ferroptosis through iron mobilization, whereas TFEB-linked autophagy and mitophagy restoration can be protective when they preserve organellar quality control. Su et al27 added a spatial dimension by showing that exosomes from oxalate-damaged HK-2 cells propagated Ambra1-dependent mitophagy and autophagy-ferroptosis signaling to recipient epithelial cells. This remains preclinical evidence, but it suggests that pathway interactions in CaOx injury may extend beyond single-cell readouts and may vary with the local lesion environment.

This becomes particularly important when papillary pathology is considered. Human papillary studies indicate that idiopathic CaOx stone formers can segregate into plaque-rich and plug-rich phenotypes, and those phenotypes differ in local structure, inflammatory context, and associations with renal function. Taguchi et al71 further reported that helper T-cell signaling and inflammatory pathways were more closely linked to calcium phosphate than to calcium oxalate stone formation on Randall’s plaques. That finding argues against transferring plaque-associated inflammatory biology directly to CaOx stone disease as a whole. Liu et al72 then showed that, in a hydroxyapatite-rich Randall’s plaque microenvironment, fibroblast-derived FASLG promoted detachment-associated death of papillary surface epithelium.72 This is relevant to plaque exposure biology, but it should not be conflated with canonical tubular CaOx crystal toxicity or used to imply the same death program in nephrocalcinosis, oxalate nephropathy, papillary plugging, or mature stone overgrowth. These lesion settings are related within CaOx stone disease, but they represent distinct structural and mechanistic contexts.

Emerging but Still Limited Pathways

Additional regulated-death labels have recently been applied to CaOx injury, but current evidence remains too limited to place them alongside the more established pathways discussed above. By that standard, cuproptosis and PANoptosis remain emerging possibilities rather than established components of CaOx pathway architecture.32,33,73,74 Broader kidney-disease reviews may help explain the conceptual background, but they do not materially strengthen the direct stone evidence base.

Cuproptosis

Cuproptosis has so far been examined directly in only one stone-relevant study. Li et al32 reported that ginsenoside Ro alleviated high-oxalate-induced cuproptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells, implicated ZnT1 in the process, and reduced renal injury and crystal deposition in a glyoxylic-acid model. These findings support brief consideration of cuproptosis as a possible oxalate-related tubular injury pathway, but the evidence remains preliminary and restricted to a narrow experimental context. A broader kidney-disease review is useful for outlining the metabolic logic of cuproptosis,73 yet it does not change the current level of support in CaOx disease.

PANoptosis

PANoptosis requires even greater caution. Li et al33 reported that salidroside attenuated CaOx-induced renal injury together with coordinated changes in apoptosis-, pyroptosis-, and necroptosis-related readouts, and interpreted this pattern within a PANoptosis framework. That study is relevant because it suggests multi-pathway injury, but coordinated marker changes do not establish bona fide PANoptosome assembly or show that PANoptosis operates as a dominant CaOx mechanism. The broader kidney-disease review helps explain why the label exists,74 but it does not substitute for direct assembly-level evidence in stone models. For now, PANoptosis is better viewed as a hypothesis-generating framework than as an established component of CaOx disease.

Biomarkers, Translational Implications, and Unresolved Questions

The current translational value of this literature lies primarily in preclinical target validation rather than in clinically actionable therapy. The strongest intervention evidence comes from ferroptosis-related studies, in which ferrostatin-1, Schizandrin B, CHAC1 modulation, SIRT1-PGC-1alpha signaling, and ACSL4/GPX4/FSP1 manipulation have repeatedly improved epithelial injury, crystal burden, or related renal phenotypes. Pyroptosis- and regulated-necrosis-oriented interventions, including GSDMD inhibition, CHMP4B enhancement, vitexin, melatonin in an LPS-primed setting, and RIPK3-directed approaches, have also shown benefit in preclinical systems. Additional proof-of-concept studies, such as Nr4a1-directed ferroptosis inhibition, autophagy-ferroptosis modulation, lycopene, corn silk polysaccharides, biomimetic NLRP3-targeted nanoparticles, and neutrophil-hitchhiking nanozymes, extend this picture, but most remain limited to single-model settings without lesion-specific validation, long-term safety data, or cross-model confirmation.75–78 Taken together, these findings support continued pathway-directed preclinical investigation, but not therapeutic translation in CaOx disease.

The biomarker literature remains similarly preliminary. Human studies have reported potentially informative signals in papillary or plaque-associated transcriptomics, oxalate-nephropathy tissue staining, urinary extracellular vesicles, urine and stone-matrix proteomics, oxidatively modified urinary proteins, plasma NET-associated markers, and gut microbiota-bile acid associations.79–81 These signals are useful for mechanistic interpretation but remain insufficiently validated for lesion-specific diagnostic or prognostic use. One useful translational bridge comes from the dapagliflozin study, which paired a glyoxylate model with human cohort data and linked nephrolithiasis protection to restoration of autophagic flux rather than to a single death-pathway readout.25 Even so, no currently available marker set has demonstrated lesion specificity, prospective validation, or clear incremental value over conventional clinical assessment.

Several priorities follow from these limitations. First, the field still lacks spatially resolved human studies that compare papillary plaque, papillary plugs, nephrocalcinosis, and acute oxalate nephropathy within a shared mechanistic framework. Second, better flux-aware and cell-type-resolved measurements are still needed, particularly for autophagy and for emerging pathway labels that are easy to overcall from static marker panels. Third, any future therapeutic development will need to account for cross-pathway compensation across epithelial and immune compartments, as illustrated by membrane-repair biology, macrophage necroptotic compensation, and NINJ1-NET amplification. For these reasons, regulated cell death pathways remain biologically informative and preclinically useful in CaOx research, but current evidence is still insufficient to support pathway-based biomarkers or treatment decisions.

Conclusion and Future Priorities

Overall, current evidence supports viewing CaOx stone disease as an interplay among tubular epithelial injury, regulated cell death, and inflammatory amplification rather than as a disorder driven by a single dominant death pathway. Injured tubular epithelium becomes more adhesive to crystals, and shared upstream stresses can give rise to multiple regulated death responses. In direct CaOx-related epithelial and preclinical models, ferroptosis and pyroptosis currently have the strongest mechanistic support, whereas apoptosis, regulated necrosis, autophagy, and mitophagy remain more dependent on lesion context, flux state, or model design. Immune-cell membrane rupture and NET formation may further amplify injury in selected inflammatory settings, especially oxalate nephropathy, but the supporting evidence remains more limited and less generalizable than that for the core epithelial literature.

These lesion boundaries may also guide future research priorities. In idiopathic papillary plaque disease, current human papillary data remain insufficient to assign dominance to a specific regulated cell death pathway. Future studies may first examine low-grade epithelial or ER stress, apoptosis-related epithelial loss, local immune activation, and epithelial detachment at plaque-exposed surfaces, rather than presuming overt lytic pyroptosis as the primary process. By contrast, papillary plugging and tubular retention may be more closely linked to epithelial adhesion and membrane injury, including GSDMD-mediated membrane damage, CHMP4B-dependent repair, and adhesion-related molecules such as osteopontin, CD44, and ITGB1. In nephrocalcinosis and acute oxalate nephropathy, current evidence provides stronger support for prioritizing ferroptosis, necroptosis, mitochondrial permeability transition-related necrosis, and myeloid amplification circuits, including NINJ1-HMGB1-NET signaling. However, direct lesion-resolved human validation remains limited, and these pathway priorities should be tested rather than treated as established hierarchies.

From a methodological perspective, future studies will require lesion-resolved human validation, flux-aware measurement, and cell-type-resolved analysis. Direct comparisons across papillary plaque, papillary plugging, nephrocalcinosis, acute oxalate nephropathy, and tubular retention models are likely to be more informative than continued expansion of pathway labels without stronger contextual grounding. Such work may help define which signals are relevant as biomarkers or therapeutic targets in specific lesion settings, but current evidence remains insufficient to support pathway-based clinical stratification or treatment decisions.
