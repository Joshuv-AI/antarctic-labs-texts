S100A9 regulates eosinophil extracellular trap and activates NF-κB signaling in endometrial cancer: a machine learning-based biomarker discovery
Authors: Juanjuan Ni, Yanan Mao, Qiaofeng Ma, Ziyao Wu, Min Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndometrial cancer (EC) is a leading cause of gynecological malignancy with poor prognosis in advanced stages. This study aimed to identify key eosinophil extracellular trap (EET) regulators involved in EC progression and explore their prognostic value using machine learning-based models. Through differential expression analysis, we identified 108 EET regulators whose expression was significantly altered in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues. Survival analysis further demonstrated that S100A9 and other EET-related genes, such as CCL26 and CD40, were significantly associated with poor patient outcomes. Unsupervised clustering analysis revealed two distinct molecular subtypes of EC, with Cluster A showing upregulation of most EET regulators and worse clinical outcomes. We assessed immune infiltration profiles and found elevated eosinophil infiltration in Cluster A. Machine learning models incorporating S100A9 achieved superior predictive performance, with the Lasso + RSF model demonstrating robust accuracy (C-index = 0.864) for predicting patient survival. Experimental validation of S100A9 function in endometrial cancer cell lines demonstrated that S100A9 knockdown effectively reduced its expression, leading to the disruption of the NF-κB pathway, as confirmed by Western blot analysis. Further, S100A9 overexpression in Ishikawa and KLE cells resulted in increased levels of apoptotic markers, indicating its role in apoptosis regulation. These findings suggest S100A9 as a potential prognostic biomarker for EC, influencing immune infiltration, NF-κB signaling, and tumor progression, with implications for new therapeutic strategies targeting EET-related pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04058-5.Keywords: Endometrial cancer, Eosinophil extracellular trap, S100A9, NF-κB signaling, Machine learning

Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) is one of the most prevalent gynecological malignancies, with incidence rates steadily increasing worldwide [1]. Despite advancements in early detection and treatment, the prognosis for patients diagnosed with advanced or recurrent EC remains poor [2]. Various therapeutic strategies have been explored, including surgical intervention, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy. Additionally, targeted therapies such as GnRH conjugates, which link GnRH analogs to cytotoxic agents like doxorubicin or mitoxantrone, have shown promise in selectively delivering the cytotoxic agents to tumor cells expressing GnRH receptors [3]. This highlights the urgent need for a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving EC progression.

Recent studies have highlighted the critical role of extracellular traps (ETs) in cancer biology [4]. These web-like structures, composed of DNA and antimicrobial proteins, are typically released by immune cells, such as neutrophils and eosinophils, to capture and eliminate pathogens [5]. However, in the context of cancer, the role of ETs extends beyond their antimicrobial function. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) have been shown to promote cancer metastasis by facilitating tumor cell migration and invasion [6]. Furthermore, recent research in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) has illuminated the role of NETs in immune cell communication and disease progression, identifying key hub genes such as CRYBG1, RMM2, MMP1, and SLC19A2 that may serve as potential biomarkers [7]. In fact, NETs have been implicated in the progression of various cancers, including breast, lung, and pancreatic cancers [8–10]. Studies suggest that NETs not only aid in immune evasion but also create a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment, thereby supporting cancer cell survival and spread [11]. Similarly, eosinophil extracellular traps (EETs) have emerged as a novel factor in the tumor microenvironment [12–14]. While much of the research on ETs has focused on neutrophils, recent findings suggest that eosinophils, another class of immune cells, also release extracellular traps in response to various stimuli [15]. EETs share structural similarities with NETs but may have distinct roles in cancer progression. Eosinophils are known to participate in immune regulation and inflammation, and their traps could influence the tumor microenvironment, potentially affecting tumor growth, immune cell infiltration, and metastasis [16]. Given the similarities between NETs and EETs, it is plausible that EETs may also play a crucial role in promoting endometrial cancer progression, further emphasizing the need to explore their mechanistic contributions.

In this study, we aim to investigate the role of EETs in EC by identifying key regulators involved in their formation and exploring their prognostic significance. By leveraging machine learning-based models and experimental validation, we seek to uncover new insights into how EETs contribute to the progression of endometrial cancer, offering potential avenues for novel therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Data collection and preprocessing

Transcriptomic data (TPM format), clinical annotations, and mutation profiles data for Uterine Corpus Endometrial Carcinoma (UCEC) were downloaded from the TCGA database [17]. After excluding repeated samples and those with survival follow-up less than one day, 523 tumor samples were retained. Gene expression values were log2-transformed and normalized for downstream analysis. Additionally, three independent GEO datasets—GSE17025, GSE106191, and GSE146889—were retrieved from the GEO database as external validation cohorts [18]. Batch effect correction across datasets was performed using the “sva” package [19]. To focus on eosinophil extracellular trap (EET)-related processes, we curated 146 genes from previous references [14]. A refined subset of 108 EET regulators was determined based on differential expression between normal and tumor tissues (adjusted p < 0.05, |log2FC| >0), followed by univariate Cox analysis to assess prognostic relevance (p < 0.05).

Construction of EET score and molecular subtyping

Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was applied to compute an EET activity score (EET score) [20] for each tumor sample, reflecting the overall activity level of the EET-related gene set (146 genes). Based on the ssGSEA-derived scores, tumors were stratified into EET-high and EET-low subgroups. Additionally, unsupervised consensus clustering using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package (K-means, 1000 bootstraps) was employed on prognostic EET genes to define molecular subtypes [21]. The stability of clustering was confirmed by PCA and cumulative distribution function (CDF) plots.

Evaluation of the tumor microenvironment and immune infiltration

To investigate immune microenvironment heterogeneity across molecular subtypes and risk groups, six deconvolution algorithms (TIMER, CIBERSORT, QUANTISEQ, EPIC, XCELL, and MCP-counter) were applied using the “IOBR” R package [22]. Stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores were calculated via the “estimate” package [23]. In addition, ssGSEA was used to quantify the enrichment of 24 immune-related cell types. The correlation between risk score and specific immune cell populations (e.g., eosinophils, neutrophils, Tregs) was evaluated using Spearman’s correlation.

Pathway activity and functional enrichment analyses

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) [24] was conducted based on MSigDB “c2.cp.kegg” and “h.all” hallmark gene sets to reveal enriched biological processes and signaling pathways between subtypes. Significantly upregulated pathways were selected based on |log2FC| >0.25 and FDR < 0.05. In addition, the PROGENy algorithm was implemented to infer the activity of 14 key oncogenic pathways from gene expression profiles. Pathways including NF-κB, and estrogen signaling were particularly evaluated due to their known involvement in endometrial cancer biology.

Machine learning model construction and validation

To establish a robust risk stratification model, we systematically tested 101 combinations of machine learning algorithms [25, 26], integrating feature selection (e.g., LASSO, Stepwise Cox, RSF) with survival regression models (e.g., Ridge, GBM, CoxBoost). These were trained on 70% of the TCGA-UCEC cohort (random split, set.seed = 2025) and validated in the remaining 30%. The derived risk scores were calculated using the model coefficients and used to stratify patients into high- and low-risk groups. Time-dependent ROC curves and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted using “timeROC” and “survminer” packages, respectively.

Genomic and mutational analyses

The somatic mutation landscape of TCGA-UCEC samples was analyzed using mutation annotation format (MAF) files via the “maftools” package [27]. Comparisons between high- and low-risk groups were performed to assess mutational enrichment in key genes (e.g., PTEN, PIK3CA, TP53).

External validation and proteomic confirmation

S100A9 expression levels were validated across GEO datasets and the CPTAC proteomic cohort [28]. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence images from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database [29] were used to confirm subcellular localization and expression patterns.

Cell culture and functional experiments

Human endometrial cancer cell lines (Ishikawa, KLE, and HEC-1 A) and the normal endometrial stromal cell line (ESC) were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. The cells were passaged every 2–3 days when they reached approximately 70–80% confluence. For overexpression of S100A9, cells were transfected with the S100A9-expressing plasmid (Addgene) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For S100A9 knockdown, short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting S100A9 (sh-S100A9−1 and sh-S100A9−2) was delivered using RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher, USA), and a negative control shRNA (sh-NC) was used as the control. The cells were transfected for 48 h before harvesting for subsequent experiments.

To assess the effects of S100A9 knockdown or overexpression on protein expression, Western blotting was performed following standard procedures. Briefly, after transfection, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher, USA) containing protease inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher, USA). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used were anti-S100A9 (1:1000, Abcam, UK), anti-p65 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-p-IκBα (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-IκBα (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-NF-κB (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-cleaved PARP (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-Caspase-3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-cleaved Caspase-3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-Caspase-8 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), and anti-cleaved Caspase-8 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Thermo Fisher, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (Thermo Fisher, USA). For detailed catalog numbers and suppliers, please refer to Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.2.2). Wilcoxon rank-sum or t-test was used for continuous variable comparisons; chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Cox regression was used for survival analysis. Correlation analyses employed Spearman or Pearson tests as appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise noted.

Results

Identification of key EET regulators and their prognostic significance in endometrial cancer

Using differential analysis, we identified a set of 108 key eosinophil extracellular trap (EET) regulators, whose expression was significantly altered in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1A). The heatmap clearly demonstrates distinct expression patterns of these genes, with many showing upregulation in tumor tissues, such as CCL26, AGER, and CD40. The prognostic significance of 22 EET-related genes was further explored through a forest plot, which assessed hazard ratios (HR) and p-values in relation to patient survival (Fig. 1B). Several genes, including MMP9, TLR4, and VEGFA, were found to be significantly associated with poor prognosis (p < 0.05), with hazard ratios greater than 1, indicating their involvement in tumor progression. Based on unsupervised clustering analysis (Fig. 1C), the matrix revealed strong correlations among genes within the tumor samples, suggesting shared regulatory pathways or interactions, which led to the identification of two distinct subgroups. Principal component analysis (PCA), shown in Fig. 1D, further confirmed the existence of these distinct tumor subtypes. The separation of these clusters suggests the presence of different molecular subtypes of endometrial cancer, which may be associated with distinct clinical characteristics and prognostic outcomes. The relationship between these clusters and clinical factors such as histological type and clinical stage was further explored in Fig. 1E, with Cluster A predominantly showing upregulation of EET regulators. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed significant differences in survival outcomes between Cluster A and Cluster B, with Cluster A demonstrating poorer survival (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1F). This finding indicates that the gene expression profile associated with Cluster A is predictive of worse prognosis, reinforcing the clinical relevance of these results. Gene expression differences between tumor clusters A and E were further examined through boxplots (Fig. 1G and H). Notably, immune-related genes such as HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C exhibited significant differential expression across clusters, with elevated expression in the more aggressive Cluster A. The upregulation of these immune-related genes in Cluster A may reflect an adaptive response of the tumor to evade immune surveillance.

Immune infiltration profiles and tumor microenvironment characteristics

Using differential analysis, we assessed immune infiltration profiles in tumor clusters A and B (Fig. 2A). Significant differences in the infiltration of various immune cell types were observed. Notably, eosinophil infiltration was significantly higher in Cluster A (p < 0.05), suggesting a pivotal role for eosinophils in this tumor subtype. Other immune cell types, including activated CD4 + T cells, activated CD8 + T cells, and natural killer cells, also exhibited significant differences in their infiltration levels between the clusters, highlighting distinct immune microenvironments across tumor subtypes. Tumor microenvironment (TME) characteristics were further evaluated using stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores (Fig. 2B). Cluster A exhibited significantly higher immune and ESTIMATE scores compared to Cluster B, indicating a more immune-enriched environment in Cluster A. Figure 2C-D presents representative H&E-stained images of Cluster A samples, showing dense immune cell infiltration, particularly eosinophilic staining cells. The histological images further support the immune infiltration findings from Fig. 2A, revealing substantial immune cell aggregation in Cluster A. In Fig. 2E, EET scores were significantly higher in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (p = 0.047). This suggests that EET activation is a prominent feature in endometrial cancer, particularly in tumor tissues, where elevated EET scores may contribute to disease progression. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (Fig. 2F) demonstrated that higher EET scores were associated with significantly poorer clinical outcomes. Patients with high EET scores had reduced disease-free survival (p = 0.007), progression-free survival (p = 0.035), and overall survival (p = 0.028), underscoring the prognostic value of EET activation in endometrial cancer.

Machine learning-based model evaluation and prognostic biomarker discovery

Based on eosinophil extracellular trap (EET) regulators with prognostic value, we evaluated the performance of 101 combinations of machine learning algorithms to identify the optimal model for endometrial cancer prediction. Figure 4A presents a heatmap of performance metrics (C-index) for the different model combinations. The Lasso + RSF models achieved the highest C-index values of 0.854, indicating superior predictive performance. The detailed C-index values of each model can be found in Supplementary Files 1. Figure 4B shows the variable importance ranking, with S100A9 emerging as the most important feature for predicting patient outcomes, followed by ACTN4, ERBB2, and ZAP70. In Fig. 4C-D, the performance of the Lasso + RSF model is evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for 1-year, 2-year, and 3-year survival predictions. The AUC values for these time points were all greater than 0.9 in both the test and training cohorts. Figure 4E shows the distribution of the survival score, where a higher score correlates with worse prognosis. The optimal cutoff for the risk score was 18.17, which divided patients into high-risk and low-risk groups. The model’s ability to discriminate between these two groups is clearly reflected in the survival curves. Figure 4F also presents Kaplan-Meier survival curves, with high-risk and low-risk categories significantly associated with overall survival. The high-risk group exhibited significantly worse survival outcomes (p < 0.0001), further validating the clinical utility of the model. Finally, Fig. 4G shows the survival curves for the Lasso + RSF model applied to a validation cohort, confirming its predictive value over a longer follow-up period. The model’s performance remained significant, with a p-value of 0.03, demonstrating its reliability across different datasets.

Similarly, Fig. 5B demonstrates that advanced disease stages (III and IV) are associated with higher risk scores, whereas early-stage disease (I and II) is linked to lower risk scores (p < 0.05). Figure 5C further highlights that patients with higher tumor grades (G3) exhibit significantly higher risk scores compared to those with lower grades (G1 and G2) (p < 0.0001). Figure 5D presents the risk scores across different histological types, with serous tumors showing significantly higher risk scores compared to endometrioid and mixed tumors (p < 0.01). S100A9 as a key prognostic biomarker in endometrial cancer

As one of the most important eosinophil extracellular trap (EET) regulators in machine learning models, we assessed the expression of S100A9 across various datasets to investigate its potential as a prognostic marker in endometrial cancer. Consistent with the TCGA-UCEC results shown in Figs. 1A and 6A-C presents the expression levels of S100A9 in different datasets (GSE17025, GSE106191, and GSE146889), demonstrating significantly higher expression in tumor samples compared to normal tissues, with Wilcoxon rank sum test p-values of < 0.001, 0.007, and < 0.001, respectively. Figure 6D provides a broader overview of S100A9 expression in the CPTAC protein cohort, showing a distinct shift toward higher expression levels in tumor samples. Protein expression of S100A9 in tumors is further supported by data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA), where the majority of tumor samples show strong cytoplasmic and membranous staining (Fig. 6E). To explore the functional implications of S100A9 in cancer progression, we examined its correlation with various biological processes. The CancerSEA database, which categorizes different functional states of 14 tumor cell types, provided additional insights. Figure 6F shows the correlation between S100A9 expression and multiple oncogenic pathways, with strong positive correlations observed in the apoptosis pathway (R = 0.23, p < 1e-08). To validate these findings, we investigated the expression of S100A9 in different endometrial cancer cell lines. Figure 6G shows that S100A9 is expressed in multiple endometrial cancer cell lines, with significantly higher expression in tumor cell lines (Ishikawa, KLE, and HEC1A) compared to the normal ESC cell line. We selected KLE, which had relatively low expression, and Ishikawa, with the highest expression, for overexpression experiments. Figure 6H presents the results of these experiments, showing that S100A9 overexpression in Ishikawa and KLE cells leads to decreased levels of apoptotic markers, including cleaved PARP, cleaved caspase 3, and cleaved caspase 8, suggesting that S100A9 may influence apoptosis pathways in endometrial cancer cells. Finally, in light of the potential activation of the aforementioned oncogenic pathways, survival analysis in Fig. 6I demonstrates that S100A9 expression is significantly associated with patient outcomes in endometrial cancer. Patients with high S100A9 expression exhibited worse overall survival compared to those with low expression (p = 0.005).

Discussion

In this study, we identified S100A9 as a key eosinophil extracellular trap (EET)-related gene that significantly contributes to the progression and poor prognosis of endometrial cancer (EC). Through an integrated analysis combining machine learning modeling, immune profiling, and experimental validation, we demonstrated that S100A9 is not only a prognostic biomarker but also an active regulator of a tumor-promoting immune microenvironment and NF-κB pathway activation. Importantly, our Lasso + RSF machine learning model exhibited excellent predictive performance for patient prognosis, achieving a C-index of 0.864 and AUCs above 0.9 at 1-, 2-, and 3-year survival intervals. Among all features, S100A9 emerged as the top-ranking predictor, suggesting that EET-related genes, particularly S100A9, may provide robust risk stratification in EC. In addition to survival prediction, the model also effectively delineated immune infiltration patterns, with high-risk groups characterized by elevated eosinophil, neutrophil, macrophage, and dendritic cell scores—highlighting the model’s capacity to reflect TME heterogeneity. These findings reinforce the clinical utility of machine learning-based approaches for EC prognosis and for capturing TME-associated immunological dynamics.

Beyond EC, it is important to contextualize our findings with prior research on EETs in other solid malignancies. Emerging evidence has indicated that EETs contribute to tumor biology across multiple cancers, and our results both align with and diverge from these reports. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, Zhao et al. [14] identified SPINK5 as a critical regulator of EETs, demonstrating that aberrant EET formation enhanced inflammatory signaling and promoted tumor progression through modulation of the immune microenvironment. Mukherjee et al. reviewed that EETs may promote tissue damage and persistent inflammation across multiple cancers [13], while more recent data in lung and colorectal cancers suggest that ETs facilitate extracellular matrix remodeling and metastatic dissemination. Consistent with our observation that S100A9 activates NF-κB signaling in EC cells, previous studies confirm that S100A9 acts as a ligand for pattern recognition receptors such as TLR4 and RAGE, initiating inflammatory cascades that culminate in NF-κB activation and subsequent transcription of pro-inflammatory genes [30–32]. Notably, S100A9 promotes IL-6 and IL-8 production in multiple cancers by engaging NF-κB signaling [33], and this mechanism appears conserved in EC, as supported by our knockdown and overexpression experiments. Furthermore, S100A9-mediated NF-κB activation can enhance immune evasion, tumor cell survival, and matrix remodeling—hallmarks of cancer progression [31, 32, 34].

Our data also show a strong positive correlation between S100A9 expression and eosinophil infiltration, suggesting that S100A9 may regulate EET formation in the tumor microenvironment. In addition to eosinophils, S100A9 has been implicated in the recruitment and activation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and neutrophils [32, 34–36], thereby shaping an immunosuppressive microenvironment conducive to tumor progression. Interestingly, the dual regulation of both NETs and EETs by S100A9 may explain its widespread impact on immune infiltration across various cancers, including breast, colorectal, liver, and lung malignancies [37, 38]. In EC, our analysis of immune signatures revealed significantly elevated eosinophil and neutrophil scores in S100A9-high tumors, suggesting that S100A9 serves as a central modulator of ET-mediated immune activation.

Our experiments further showed that S100A9 overexpression influences apoptosis in EC cell lines, marked by decreased cleaved PARP and caspases. We acknowledge that this finding appears contradictory to reports in the literature where S100A9 has been shown to promote apoptosis in certain cancer types [39, 40]. This discrepancy may arise due to the context-dependent roles of S100A9, which likely vary across different cancer types and cellular environments. Specifically, in some tumors, S100A9 may activate NF-κB signaling pathways [33, 40], thereby suppressing apoptosis and enhancing tumor cell survival. Conversely, in other contexts, S100A9 may exhibit pro-apoptotic effects through distinct mechanisms. Therefore, the dual role of S100A9 in apoptosis regulation across different tumor microenvironments warrants further investigation to clarify the underlying mechanisms and how they might influence tumor progression.”

In addition, S100A9 contributes to extracellular matrix remodeling, fibrosis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), which are essential processes in tumor invasion and metastasis [41, 42]. In EC, our pathway enrichment analysis and histological validation revealed dense stromal and immune cell infiltration in S100A9-high tumors, supporting its role in microenvironmental crosstalk. Mechanistically, S100A9 activates metalloproteinases (e.g., MMP9) and enhances TGF-β and IL-23 signaling, thereby promoting fibrotic remodeling and EMT [41, 43, 44]. Given its robust predictive value in our Lasso + RSF model and consistent overexpression in tumor samples across multiple datasets, S100A9 emerges as a promising biomarker for EC prognosis. Furthermore, targeting S100A9 or its downstream pathways (e.g., NF-κB, TLR4, RAGE) may offer new therapeutic opportunities. Preclinical studies have shown that S100A9 inhibition sensitizes tumors to immunotherapy and reduces immune evasion [32, 45, 46], making it an attractive target for combinatorial treatments in EC. Moreover, S100A9 levels in serum and tissue have been proposed as accessible biomarkers for treatment response monitoring in inflammatory diseases [47–49], which could be extended to EC in future clinical studies.

While our study suggests a potential role for S100A9 in regulating EETs and their associated pathways in EC, it is important to note that direct experimental evidence for S100A9’s involvement in EET formation is currently lacking. EETs, which are web-like structures composed of extracellular DNA and antimicrobial proteins released by eosinophils, have been implicated in modulating immune responses within the tumor microenvironment. In this study, we observed significant eosinophil infiltration in S100A9-high EC samples and a strong association between S100A9 expression and EET-related pathways. However, to strengthen the causal relationship between S100A9 and EET formation, future research should include in vitro experiments aimed at directly visualizing EETs, such as through extracellular DNA trap assays or eosinophil activation markers. These experimental approaches would provide more conclusive evidence of the role of S100A9 in promoting EET formation and clarify its impact on the tumor microenvironment.

Conclusion

In summary, we identified EET-related gene signatures and constructed a high-performing machine learning model (Lasso + RSF) that accurately stratifies endometrial cancer patients by prognosis and reflects distinct tumor immune microenvironments. Notably, tumors with high risk scores exhibited elevated eosinophil infiltration, immune activation, and poor survival, highlighting the clinical relevance of EET activity. Among the predictors, S100A9 emerged as a representative biomarker linked to NF-κB signaling and immune regulation, offering additional mechanistic insights. These findings support the utility of EET-related markers and machine learning tools in improving EC prognosis prediction and guiding immune-targeted strategies.
