Adaptive clinical trial design with delayed treatment effects using elicited prior distributions
James A. Salsbury, Jeremy E. Oakley, Steven A. Julious, Lisa V. Hampson

Abstract
Clinical trials with time-to-event endpoints, such as overall survival (OS) or progression-free survival (PFS), are fundamental for evaluating new treatments, particularly in immuno-oncology. However, modern therapies, such as immunotherapies and targeted treatments, often exhibit delayed effects that challenge traditional trial designs. These delayed effects violate the proportional hazards assumption, which underpins standard statistical methods like the Cox proportional hazards model and the log-rank test. Careful planning is essential to ensure trials are appropriately designed to account for the timing and magnitude of these effects. Without this planning, interim analyses may lead to premature trial termination if the treatment effect is underestimated early in the study. We present an adaptive trial design framework that incorporates prior distributions, elicited from experts, for delayed treatment effects. By addressing the uncertainty surrounding delayed treatment effects, our approach enhances trial efficiency and robustness, minimizing the risk of premature termination and improving the detection of treatment benefits over time. We present an example illustrating how interim analyses, informed by prior distributions, can guide early stopping decisions. To facilitate the implementation of our framework, we have developed free, open-source software that enables researchers to integrate prior distributions into trial planning and decision-making. This software provides a flexible, accessible tool for designing trials that more accurately evaluate modern therapies through adaptive trial designs.

Introduction

Clinical trials with time-to-event endpoints, such as overall survival (OS) or progression-free survival (PFS), are fundamental in evaluating new treatments in many therapeutic areas—especially in immuno-oncology. Randomized clinical trials (RCTs) remain the gold standard for such evaluations, but the advent of modern therapies, including immunotherapies and targeted treatments, has introduced complexities that challenge traditional trial designs. A significant issue arises when treatments exhibit delayed effects, where therapeutic benefits are not immediately apparent but only emerge after a certain period.

Delayed treatment effects (DTEs) pose significant challenges in the design, analysis, and interpretation of clinical trials. One major issue is that they violate the proportional hazards (PH) assumption, which is an assumption of the standard Cox proportional hazards model. Also, as the log-rank test is most powerful when the PH assumption holds, delayed effects can make these methods suboptimal or even inappropriate.

2
Delayed Treatment Effects

In this section, we reintroduce the parameterisation and notation for DTEs, as found in our previous work.
[
13
]
Assume we are planning a survival trial, with two groups: the control group and the experimental treatment group. We denote the hazard function for the control group as
h_{c}(t)
and the hazard function for the experimental treatment group as
h_{e}(t)
. In a typical survival trial, we usually assume that patients in the experimental treatment arm may immediately benefit from the intervention compared to those in the control arm; that is,
h_{e}(t)\leq h_{c}(t)
,
\forall t
.

In a trial in which a DTE is thought likely to occur, we make a different assumption. We assume that the hazard function for the experimental treatment group is the same as that of the control group until a certain time
T
, which represents the delay in the experimental treatment taking effect. After time
T
, we assume that the experimental treatment group starts experiencing some benefit relative to the control group, that is

h_{e}(t)=\begin{cases}h_{c}(t),&t\leq T\\
h^{*}_{e}(t),&t>T\end{cases},

(1)

where
h_{e}^{*}(t)\leq h_{c}(t)
describes the benefit of the experimental treatment relative to control.
[
1
]

2.1
Parameterisation

For purposes of planning such a trial, we suppose that the survival times in the control group follow a Weibull distribution with hazard function

h_{c}(t)=\gamma_{c}\lambda_{c}^{\gamma_{c}}t^{\gamma_{c}-1}

(2)

and corresponding survival function

S_{c}(t)=\text{exp}\{-(\lambda_{c}t)^{\gamma_{c}}\}.

(3)

We assume survival times in the experimental treatment group, after a delay of length
T
, also follow a Weibull distribution with different parameters to the control. This induces the hazard function

h_{e}^{*}(t)=\gamma_{e}\lambda_{e}^{\gamma_{e}}t^{\gamma_{e}-1}

(4)

and corresponding survival function

S_{e}^{*}(t)=\text{exp}\{-(\lambda_{c}T)^{\gamma_{c}}-\lambda_{e}^{\gamma_{e}}(t^{\gamma_{e}}-T^{\gamma_{e}})\}.

(5)

Prior to time
T
, we assume the experimental treatment group has the same survival function as the control (Equation
3
). Thus, the survival function for the experimental treatment group is

S_{e}(t)=\begin{cases}\text{exp}\{-(\lambda_{c}t)^{\gamma_{c}}\},&t\leq T\\
\text{exp}\{-(\lambda_{c}T)^{\gamma_{c}}-\lambda_{e}^{\gamma_{e}}(t^{\gamma_{e}}-T^{\gamma_{e}})\},&t>T\end{cases}.

(6)

The hazard ratio of the two groups (derived from Equations
2
and
4
) is

\text{HR}(t)=\begin{cases}1,&t\leq T\\
\frac{\gamma_{e}\lambda_{e}^{\gamma_{e}}t^{\gamma_{e}-1}}{\gamma_{c}\lambda_{c}^{\gamma_{c}}t^{\gamma_{c}-1}},&t>T\end{cases}.

(7)

Given the parameterisation, we have five unknown parameters to make judgements on:
\lambda_{c}
,
\gamma_{c}
,
\lambda_{e}
,
\gamma_{e}
and
T
. The following sections briefly discuss the different methods we would use to elicit these parameters (either directly or indirectly). We do not discuss the actual method of elicitation, this can be found in Salsbury et al.
[
13
]

2.2
Prior(s) for
\lambda_{c}
and
\gamma_{c}

We first consider the two parameters in the control group;
\lambda_{c}
, also known as the scale parameter, and
\gamma_{c}
, the shape parameter. We assume that there exists some historical data on the control so that we can derive

\pi(\lambda_{c},\gamma_{c}|\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{hist}}),

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{hist}}
is the historical data for the control group intervention.

2.3
Post-Delay Hazard Ratio

Typically, it is assumed that once the treatment begins to take effect, the proportional hazard assumption is valid.
[
2
]
If we let
\gamma_{c}=\gamma_{e}
, then Equation
7
simplifies to

\text{HR}(t)=\begin{cases}1,&t\leq T\\
\bigg(\frac{\lambda_{e}}{\lambda_{c}}\bigg)^{\gamma_{c}},&t>T\end{cases},

(8)

and we see that the hazard ratio is now piecewise constant. Thus, we are able to indirectly elicit judgements about
\lambda_{e}
by asking questions about the (constant) post-delay hazard ratio.

We also need to account for the possibility that the survival curves may not separate at all (i.e. there will be no form of treatment effect). We define
S
to be the proposition that the population survival curves separate, and
P_{S}
to be elicited probability that this proposition is true. Note that the proposition
S
refers to the unobserved true population distributions of survival, and not sampled Kaplan-Meier curves that would be observed in a trial.

Thus, the prior distribution of the post-delay hazard ratio is given by

\text{HR}^{*}\sim\begin{cases}1,&\text{with probability }1-P_{S}\\
D_{\text{HR}^{*}},&\text{with probability }P_{S}\end{cases},

where
D_{\text{HR}^{*}}
is the distribution of the post-delay hazard ratio assuming the survival curves separate.

2.4
Length of Delay

The length of the delay, denoted by
T
, is a parameter that can be intuitively understood and directly asked about during elicitation. It is only relevant when the survival curves are expected to eventually separate; thus, this parameter is considered under the condition that the curves will diverge. We also incorporate the possibility of there being no delay in the separation of the curves. The prior distribution for the length of delay is

T|S\sim\begin{cases}0,&\text{with probability }1-P_{\text{DTE}}\\
D_{T},&\text{with probability }P_{\text{DTE}}\end{cases},

where
D_{T}
is the distribution of the length of delay (assuming there will be a delay) and
P_{\text{DTE}}
is the probability of experiencing a delayed treatment effect.

2.5
Elicited Distributions

Based on the parameterisation outlined above, the values/distributions that are required to plan a clinical trial in this context are:

•

\gamma_{c}
and
\lambda_{c}
: The distributions for the hyperparameters associated with the control group

•

P_{S}
: The probability that the survival curves will separate

•

D_{\text{HR}^{*}}
: The distribution of the post-delay hazard ratio

•

P_{\text{DTE}}
: The probability that the curves will exhibit a delayed treatment effect

•

D_{T}
: The distribution of the length of delay

For the remainder of this paper, we assume that these values/distributions have been obtained, and we use them to plan an adaptive clinical trial.

3
Methods

We anticipate that elicited prior distributions will inform the design and planning of adaptive trials in the following three ways:

1.

Evaluation of a design.
Given a specified trial design, the elicited distributions can be used to assess its operating characteristics. This process can help refine adaptation rules.

2.

Design optimisation.
Elicited distributions can serve as the foundation for trial design optimisation. Rather than committing to a fixed design a priori, a range of plausible designs can be explored via simulation, with their respective operating characteristics evaluated. What constitutes an “optimal” design will be context-dependent, shaped by the sponsor’s priorities and constraints.

3.

Informing decision rules.
Elicited distributions can be used directly during interim analyses. At each such analysis, the prior distributions are combined with the observed data to form posterior distributions, which then are used to guide decision-making.

In all three applications, analytical solutions are generally intractable, making a simulation-based approach essential for estimating key operating characteristics within a structured framework for simulation studies.
[
19
]
The following subsections elaborate on each of the three methodological applications described above.

3.1
Evaluation of a design

When a trial design has already been specified—typically including sample size, number and timing of interim analyses, allocation ratio, and statistical decision rules—elicited prior distributions can be used to evaluate its performance. This involves simulating clinical trial outcomes based on the prior beliefs about the treatment effect and other relevant parameters, followed by an analysis using the pre-specified statistical framework. This process enables estimation of the design’s operating characteristics, such as:

•

Expected sample size and expected trial duration.

•

Probability of early stopping for efficacy or futility.

•

Probability of success (PoS) (also referred to as assurance).

The elicited distributions allow stakeholders to critically assess whether the pre-defined design aligns with their risk tolerance. If key operating characteristics are found to be suboptimal, the design may be revised, or a grid of designs may be considered (see Section
3.2
).

3.2
Design optimisation

In contrast to evaluating a single, pre-specified trial design (as discussed in the previous section), trial design optimisation is a broader, more exploratory approach. Rather than committing to a fixed set of design parameters from the outset, optimisation involves systematically simulating and comparing a range of alternative designs. This process allows trial planners to identify the configuration that best aligns with their objectives and constraints, often formalised through a utility function.
[
21
,
22
,
20
]

This simulation framework allows planners to vary parameters such as sample size, the number and timing of interim analyses, allocation ratios, and stopping rules. By incorporating expert beliefs and comparing the operating characteristics of each candidate design, stakeholders can identify a configuration that offers the optimal trade-off between their risk tolerance, ethical considerations, and logistical constraints. For example, a sponsor prioritising speed may prefer a design with a higher probability of early stopping, whereas another might favour one that maximises the probability of detecting a true treatment effect.

Thus, trial design optimisation builds directly on the evaluation process described previously, but extends it by allowing a more flexible and iterative exploration of the design space.

3.3
Informing decision rules

At an interim analysis, we would like to calculate the probability that the trial will go on to be successful. One approach to calculating such a metric is the Bayesian Predictive Probability (BPP), which is the probability of achieving trial success at the final analysis, given the prior distribution(s) and the interim data.
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
BPP offers an alternative metric to the traditional frequentist metric of
conditional power
, which relies on fixed point estimates.

Mathematically, BPP is defined as:

\text{BPP}=\int P(\text{reject }H_{0}\text{ at the final analysis}\mid\text{interim data})p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\text{interim data})d\boldsymbol{\theta},

where
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\text{interim data})
are the posterior distributions.

This probability is typically computed via simulation: samples are drawn from the posterior distribution of the treatment effect (given the interim data and prior), and then used to project possible future trial outcomes. The BPP is estimated as the proportion of these projected outcomes in which the trial meets its pre-specified success criterion (e.g., statistical significance at the final analysis).

We envisage BPP being applied in two main contexts:

1.

During trial design.
At the design stage, we can simulate clinical trials and then use the elicited distributions to derive posterior distribution(s). The BPP can then be calculated using these posteriors. If we do this for varying information fractions, we can evaluate when such interim analyses are likely to be most informative. Following the framework in Alhussain and Oakley
[
29
]
, we define an interim look as
informative
if the BPP exceeds 0.95 or falls below 0.05. Since informativeness generally increases with the amount of accumulated information, such evaluations can guide the timing of interim analyses so as to facilitate meaningful decision-making.

2.

At an interim analysis.
While the trial is ongoing, the BPP can be calculated using the observed interim data and elicited prior distributions to update the probability of success at the final analysis. For example, a trial could be stopped for futility if the BPP
<
0.10, as per the pre-specified stopping rule in the trial protocol.

4
Example

To illustrate the methodologies introduced, we present a modified version of the example from Salsbury et al.
[
13
]
This example involves a two-arm Phase III superiority trial designed to evaluate whether a new immunotherapy drug is more beneficial than the current standard of care, docetaxel, in patients with advanced non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Given the nature of immunotherapy, we expect a DTE. The primary efficacy endpoint is overall survival (OS) and we assume uniform recruitment over 12 months with a 1:1 allocation.

For the control group, we assume the data will follow an Exponential distribution, with rate parameter
\lambda_{c}=0.08
. For the other parameters, we assume we have used expert elicitation and obtain the following judgements. Suppose the expert believes there is a 90% probability that the survival curves will diverge, that is
P_{S}=0.9
. We then ask for their beliefs about the post-delay hazard ratio, given the survival curves diverge. For this quantity, the expert provides a median of 0.6 and two quartiles (25% and 75%) of 0.55 and 0.7, respectively. Mathematically:

\text{HR}^{*}\mid S=\begin{cases}1,&\text{with probability }0.1\\
\text{Gamma(29.6, 47.8)},&\text{with probability }0.9\end{cases}

We then ask about the length of delay. We assume the expert believes, given the survival curves diverge, there is a 70% probability they will be subject to a delayed treatment effect. Given they will be subject to a delayed treatment effect, the expert provides a median of 4 months and two quartiles (25% and 75%) of 3 and 5 months, respectively, for the length of delay. Again, mathematically:

T\mid S=\begin{cases}0,&\text{with probability }0.3\\
\text{Gamma(7.29, 1.76)},&\text{with probability }0.7\end{cases}

4.1
No Interim Analysis

We recommend first considering the scenario in which no interim analysis is performed. This serves as a baseline for comparison with more complex trial designs. Given the beliefs outlined above and this trial setup, we calculate that with 300 patients in each group, and running the trial until 450 events have been observed, corresponds to an estimated probability of success of 75%, reflecting both rounding and Monte Carlo error. The duration of the trial will be approximately 27 months and the sample size will be 600 (as all the patients will be recruited by the time the trial finishes).

It is important to emphasise that there is no universally optimal design. The choice of interim analysis timing involves a trade-off between the risks and benefits specific to the context of the trial. The framework presented here is intended to support decision-makers by highlighting where potential vulnerabilities may lie. In practice, sponsors may wish to conduct sensitivity analyses to explore the robustness of their design under different assumptions.

This highlights that, in this example, the principal risk associated with early stopping stems from uncertainty in the timing of the treatment effect, rather than its size.

4.3
Bayesian Predictive Probability

As introduced in Section
3.3
, interim data can be simulated at the design stage, and the corresponding Bayesian predictive probability (BPP) can be calculated. This approach allows us to evaluate the usefulness of conducting an interim analysis at a given information fraction. Let
\mathrm{BPP}_{i}
denote the predictive probability associated with the interim look at information fraction
i
.

For this example, we first simulate
N=500
complete trials using the elicited prior distributions. Then, at information fraction
i
, we perform an interim analysis, according to the alpha and beta spending functions in Table 1. We combine the observed interim data with the prior distributions to calculate corresponding posterior distributions. We then calculate the BPP for each simulated trial using these posterior distributions. For
i=0.2,0.4,0.6
and
0.8
, the resulting distribution of BPP values are shown in Figure
4
. This whole process is outlined in Algorithm

In Figure
4
, we see that as the information fraction increases, the histogram shifts more towards the extremes (values close to 0 or 1). This reflects the fact that, as the trial progresses, we are more likely to be well informed about its eventual outcome. Table

provides further support for this observation: the final column shows the informativeness metric introduced in Section

These findings show how BPP can help at the design stage when deciding when to carry out interim analyses. The idea is to time the analysis so that it is as informative as possible and useful for decision-making. In some cases, this might mean delaying the interim look until a sufficient level of informativeness has been reached. However, what counts as “sufficient,” will vary depending on the sponsor’s perspective and should always be weighed against other factors such as expected trial duration and sample size. The type of adaptation also matters: for example, one might want a higher level of informativeness before making an efficacy decision, while a lower level may be acceptable for a futility analysis.

5
Discussion

This paper provides a practical and statistical foundation for designing adaptive clinical trials in the presence of delayed treatment effects (DTEs). Our proposed methods aim to reduce inefficiencies and improve trial outcomes, ultimately enabling more effective evaluations of innovative therapies.

We have extended the assurance framework to incorporate interim analyses in the presence of anticipated delayed treatment effects. The example illustrates how this framework can help practitioners make informed decisions under uncertainty. Sponsors are able to identify the risks of any such design and plan accordingly.

The open-source software developed alongside this work provides a user-friendly tool for researchers and trial designers, supporting the implementation of these methods in real-world settings. While statistical considerations are fundamental in determining the timing of interim analyses, decisions must also integrate clinical relevance, operational logistics, and ethical implications related to early trial termination. Our software’s flexibility enables exploration of diverse scenarios, empowering sponsors to make informed decisions at the design stage.

Looking forward, we anticipate that the methodology presented here will have broad applicability beyond immuno-oncology, contributing to the design of adaptive Phase III trials.
