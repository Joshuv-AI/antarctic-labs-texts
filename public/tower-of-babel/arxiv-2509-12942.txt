Asymmetric Grid Quorum Systems for Heterogeneous Processes
Michael Senn, Christian Cachin

Abstract
Quorum systems are a common way to formalize failure assumptions in distributed systems. Traditionally, these assumptions are shared by all involved processes. More recently, systems have emerged which allow processes some freedom in choosing their own, subjective or asymmetric, failure assumptions. For such a system to work, individual processes’ assumptions must be compatible. However, this leads to a Catch-22-style scenario: How can processes collaborate to agree on compatible failure assumptions when they have no compatible failure assumptions to start with? We introduce asymmetric grid quorum systems that allow a group of processes to specify heterogeneous trust assumptions independently of each other and without coordination. They are based on qualitative attributes describing how the processes differ. Each process may select a quorum system from this class that aligns best with its subjective view. The available choices are designed to be compatible by definition, thereby breaking the cycling dependency. Asymmetric grid quorum systems have many applications that range from cloud platforms to blockchain networks.

Introduction

In resilient distributed systems that use replication, independent processes
jointly perform a task, which can vary from reading and writing data in
storage systems and coordinating computations in the cloud to running smart
contracts on a blockchain.
In order to reason about the behavior of such a system during an execution,
one must first formalize assumptions on the reliability of the processes. It
is common to foresee that processes may crash and stay silent, that they crash
and recover but suffer from amnesia, or that they are outright malicious and
behave arbitrarily. This work addresses the last case and considers

Byzantine
processes.

A wide-spread way to describe which processes may fail at once is by assuming
bounds on their numbers with so-called
threshold failure assumptions
,
which capture that less than a fraction of them, such as one-third, will be
jointly faulty. The more general approach of
(Byzantine) quorum
systems

[
13
,
20
,
23
]

encodes a trust assumption in a set system that specifies collections of
processes that may jointly fail. Quorum systems are the core abstraction that
form the basis of many protocols.

Most of the academic work and many distributed systems deployed in practice
consider this assumption to be a
global
, or
symmetric
, property,
that is shared by all processes. For applications to secure and trustworthy
systems, however, this may be relaxed, in the same way as trust among humans
is subjective because individuals trust others based on personal beliefs and
past experience.

Indeed there are blockchain networks such as the XRP Ledger
(
https://xprl.org
) and
Stellar
[
16
,
22
]

(
https://stellar.org
) which allow their nodes some freedom in choosing
their individual trust assumptions. At the time of writing (August 2025),
these are the third- and eighth-largest cryptocurrencies by market
capitalization, respectively.
Similarly there is work which provides theoretical foundations for

asymmetric
trust

[
1
,
11
,
15
]

and
flexible
trust

[
19
,
24
]
. These models
generalize Byzantine quorum systems and allow processes to pick quorums of
their own. There already exist diverse protocols for this setting, such as
broadcast
abstractions
[
1
,
15
]
, a common
coin
[
1
, Section 7]
or a DAG-based
consensus
[
3
]
.

However, asymmetric trust poses a new challenge. If processes are given the
freedom to choose their trust assumptions, these must be compatible with each
other and the processes should have enough mutual trust so that system can act
coherently.
This is no different from the traditional symmetric case, where one requires,
for example, the overlap of any two quorums to have a certain size. But now we
require processes to agree and compromise on their subjective trust
assumption, so that our distributed system can do some work. This is a
distributed task by itself that requires coordination.

Resilient and highly secure computing systems often exploit diversity of
software, hardware, governance, and other aspects to reduce the
undesirable vulnerabilities inherent in a
monoculture
[
9
,
14
]
.
The processes that comprise such systems are characterized by attributes such
as operating system, CPU model, geographical location, programming language,
virtual machine implementation, jurisdiction and so on, and each attribute can
take on different values. But designing trust assumptions from such attributes
has been done ad hoc, so far.

This paper introduces a new approach to designing trust models for distributed
systems: We introduce
asymmetric grid quorum systems
that characterize
the components or processes in a system with a number of attributes taken from
a predefined set. If such attributes offer sufficient diversity, i.e., take
on enough different values, then each process may subjectively choose the
attributes that matter to itself and define its own quorum system without
coordination in the system. The resulting choices are compatible with each
other by construction — in other words, the assumptions made individually
according to the attribute structure automatically lead to an asymmetric
Byzantine quorum system.

In particular, we allow processes to choose their trust assumption from a
fixed set of options characterized by attributes. We call this a

structured failure assumption
and formalize the corresponding
fail-prone systems. The attributes and their values are common knowledge, but
each member may then select which attributes are relevant for itself. The
attributes span a multi-dimensional grid whose properties we analyze in this
work. We model fail-prone systems with a grid structure and characterize
their necessary and sufficient properties that lead to asymmetric grid quorum
systems.

We remark that real-world processes are rarely homogeneous, hence, this
technique appears to have practical applications. Our running example will
treat the operating system of a process and its geographical location (or the
cloud provider supplying the physical hardware) as two relevant attributes
that form a grid, but our methods generalize to a hyperrectangle structure of
arbitrary dimension.
Intuitively, we want to allow that each process chooses which of the other
processes’ attributes it believes to be the best predictor of future failure.
The process will then be able to tolerate a large number of faults which
correlate with its chosen attribute, plus a smaller number of processes that
are characterized by other attributes. In particular, the failure scenarios
that the resulting asymmetric quorum system can tolerate are strictly larger
than those tolerated by threshold quorum systems.

Related work

Structured symmetric failure assumptions

Constructing resilient systems from redundant components has long been an
important technique when faults occur
independently
[
4
]
.
Threshold-based quorum systems are suitable when one believes faults to be
distributed uniformly at random, with no existing structure on the set of
processes. If one is willing to impose some structure, however, one can design
quorum systems to be more resilient or efficient, at the cost of resilience if
the structure is violated. A prominent example are grid-based quorum systems,
where processes are arranged in a two-dimensional grid, and quorums consist of
one full row and one process from every row
below
[
10
,
18
,
25
]
.
However, these systems do not address the manner by which processes should be
arranged in such a grid.

Earlier work by Cachin
[
6
]
suggests to build
generalized quorum systems based on attributes that distinguish processes,
such that the resulting structures are also suitable for distributed
cryptography.

Whittaker
et al.

[
28
]
introduce quorum
systems which accommodate the fact that processes are heterogeneous when it
comes to their available resources. Their construction defines quorums which
more evenly spread the read and write load over processes, to optimize
performance. However, they restrict themselves to non-Byzantine faults, i.e. to
settings where there are no actively adversarial processes.

Practical systems with asymmetric trust

The Stellar blockchain allows each validator to specify a custom list of other

17

While such an FBQS is no generalization of Byzantine quorum systems, there is

.

Similarly, the XRP Ledger allows each validator to define a

. During execution of the consensus protocol, a validator then only

Communities and companies associated with both the XRPL and Stellar ecosystems

3
System Model

We assume a permissioned setting with a universe of
n
independent processes

\mathcal{P}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. These processes have a known identity and are
able to exchange messages with each other via reliable, authenticated
point-to-point links. In any execution, some of these processes are expected to
be faulty. We model these faulty processes using the notion of
subjective
trust
by Alpos
et al.

[
1
]
. There, each process
has some liberty in choosing its subjective trust assumptions. For context we
first discuss
generalized quorum systems
by Malkhi and
Reiter
[
20
]
.

3.1
Symmetric Trust Assumptions

In the model of symmetric trust, there is a
global
assumption, which all
processes share, about the combinations of processes that may be jointly
faulty. A
failprone set

F\subset\mathcal{P}
is a maximal set of processes
which may jointly be faulty during an execution. The collection of these
failprone sets, the
failprone system

\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{k}\}\subset 2^{\mathcal{P}}
, specifies this global failure assumption. To illustrate, the common

n>3f
threshold failure assumption corresponds to the failprone system
containing all possible failprone sets of cardinality
\lceil n/3\rceil-1
.

While failprone systems formalize failure assumptions, protocols are often
described in terms of
quorum systems
. Those formalize the sets of
processes with which a party must have interacted before proceeding to the next
step of the protocol.

A common way to derive a quorum system from a failprone system is by the
so-called
canonical quorum system
. As will be shown later, the canonical
quorum system describes a convenient relation between failprone systems and
quorum systems where properties of one imply properties of the other.

Definition 1 (Canonical quorum system).

Given a failprone system
\mathcal{F}
, the
canonical quorum system

\mathcal{Q}

of
\mathcal{F}
is defined as the bijective complement
[
20
, Theorem
5.4]

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
of
\mathcal{F}
, i.e.

\mathcal{Q}=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=\{\mathcal{P}\setminus F:F\in\mathcal{F}\}.

Consistency, availability, and resilience

In order to reason about protocol properties, a notion of
consistency

and
availability
of quorum systems is required. Intuitively, consistency
ensures that any two quorums intersect in sufficiently many correct processes,
while availability ensures that at least one quorum consists of exclusively
correct ones.

While usually all quorum systems share the same availability property, they
often differ in their consistency properties. Malkhi and
Reiter
[
20
]
distinguish between
masking
and

dissemination
quorum systems, while Alpos

et al.

[
1
]
use the notion of
Q^{3}
consistency
for the latter, based on earlier work by Hirt and
Maurer
[
13
]
.

Definition 2 (
Q^{3}
-consistent quorum system).

A
Q^{3}
-consistent quorum system

\mathcal{Q}
for a failprone system

\mathcal{F}
is a set system
\mathcal{Q}\subset 2^{\mathcal{P}}
where the following properties
hold:

Consistency

The intersection of any two quorums is not a subset of
any failprone set, that is

\forall Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathcal{Q},\forall F\in\mathcal{F}:Q_{1}\cap Q_{2}\not\subseteq F.

Availability

For any failprone set, there exists a quorum disjoint
from it, i.e.

\forall F\in\mathcal{F}:\exists Q\in\mathcal{Q}:F\cap Q=\varnothing.

We remark here that
Q^{3}
consistency can be generalized to
Q^{k}
consistency
for
k\geq 2

[
11
, Section 1.3]
. This
family of consistency notions imposes a sequence of increasingly stricter
conditions on the overlap of individual quorums. To illustrate, a
n>3f

threshold failure assumption is
Q^{3}
-consistent, while
masking quorum
systems

[
20
]
are
Q^{4}
-consistent.

For this work we focus on
Q^{3}
-consistent quorum systems, which have
been shown to be sufficient for many protocols such as consensus with fast
termination in a crash-fault setting
[
5
]
or
Byzantine consensus
[
7
,
29
]
.

In contrast to the definition above, a quorum system’s consistency can also be
formalized purely in regards to its failprone system, called the failprone
system’s
resilience
.

Definition 3 (
Q^{3}
resilience).

A failprone system
\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{\mathcal{P}}
is
Q^{3}
-resilient
if no union of
three failprone sets covers the universe, that is

\forall F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\in\mathcal{F}:F_{1}\cup F_{2}\cup F_{3}\subsetneq\mathcal{P}.

One can show that a failprone system
\mathcal{F}
is
Q^{3}
-resilient if and only if
its canonical quorum system is
Q^{3}
-consistent
[
20
, Theorem
5.4]
.

Definition 4.

We adopt the notation of Alpos
et al.

[
1
]
to denote by

\mathcal{F}^{*}
the set containing all possible subsets of every
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{F}^{*}=\bigcup_{F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}2^{F^{\prime}}=\{F:F\subseteq F^{\prime}\land F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}\}.

Intuitively, if there is an execution with faulty processes
F
and a failprone
system
\mathcal{F}
, then
F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
simply means that this specific failure mode
was anticipated by the failure assumption, and the system will be able to
handle it.

3.2
Asymmetric Trust Assumptions

We now review the notion of asymmetric trust of Alpos

et al.

[
1
]
based on earlier work by Damgård

et al.

[
11
]
. Here, each process
i\in\mathcal{P}

chooses its subjective trust assumption through its own failprone system

\mathcal{F}_{i}\subset 2^{\mathcal{P}}
. The collection of all such failprone systems then
forms the
asymmetric failprone system

\mathbb{F}=[\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{n}]
.

As in the symmetric case, one can derive a
canonical asymmetric quorum
system
from an asymmetric failprone system.

Definition 5 (Canonical asymmetric quorum system).

Given an asymmetric failprone system
\mathbb{F}
, the
canonical asymmetric quorum system

\mathbb{Q}
of

\mathbb{F}
is defined as the collection of the bijective complement of every

\mathcal{F}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}
, that is

\mathbb{Q}=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{F}_{i}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu:\mathcal{F}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}].

Naive and wise processes

As each process can have its own trust assumption, it is no longer sensible to
believe that every process’ assumption always holds. In any execution with
faulty processes
F\subset\mathcal{P}
, correct processes are either
wise
, or

naive
. A process
i\in\mathcal{P}
is called
wise
if its subjective
trust assumption holds, that is if
F\in\mathcal{F}_{i}^{*}
. Otherwise, it is called
naive. This is a distinction specific to the asymmetric trust setting.

This is relevant as protocols designed for the asymmetric setting may not be
able to guarantee liveness or safety for all correct processes, but only for
wise ones, or even only for a “self-sufficient” subset of wise processes.

Consistency, availability, and resilience

As in the symmetric
setting, an asymmetric quorum system
\mathbb{Q}
must fulfill some properties such
that one can use it in protocols with provable guarantees. This inherently
limits a process’ freedom in choosing its own subjective trust assumption, as
its assumption must not only be internally consistent, but also “compatible”
with those of other processes. Alpos
et al.

[
1
, Definition
4]
adopt the generalization of
Q^{3}
-consistent
quorum systems to the asymmetric setting as originally done by Damgård

et al.

[
11
, Theorem 2]
, and call it

B^{3}
-consistent asymmetric quorum systems.

Definition 6 (
B^{3}
-consistent asymmetric quorum system).

A
B^{3}
-consistent asymmetric quorum system

\mathbb{Q}
for an asymmetric failprone
system
\mathbb{F}
is an array
\mathbb{Q}=[\mathcal{Q}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{Q}_{n}]
, where
\mathcal{Q}_{i}\subset 2^{\mathcal{P}}
, and the following properties hold:

Consistency

The intersection of any two quorums of any two processes
contains at least one process which at least one of the two processes
believes to be correct, that is

\forall i,j\in\mathcal{P},\forall Q_{i}\in\mathcal{Q}_{i},\forall Q_{j}\in\mathcal{Q}_{j},\forall F_{i,j}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}^{*}\cap\mathcal{F}_{j}^{*}:Q_{i}\cap Q_{j}\not\subseteq F_{i,j}.

Availability

For any failprone set of any process, there exists a
quorum for that process disjoint from it, i.e.

\forall i\in\mathcal{P},\forall F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}:\exists Q_{i}\in\mathcal{Q}_{i}:F_{i}\cap Q_{i}=\varnothing.

The availability property is a straightforward generalization of its
counterpart in the symmetric setting. The consistency property is a
generalization in the sense that, if all processes were to pick the same
failprone (and quorum) systems, it would degrade to the symmetric property.

Alpos
et al.
.
[
1
, Definition 5]
further
introduce
B^{3}
resilience of an asymmetric failprone system as a
generalization of
Q^{3}
resilience, and show
[
1
, Theorem
4]
that an asymmetric failprone system
\mathbb{F}
is

B^{3}
-resilient if and only if its canonical asymmetric quorum system is

B^{3}
-consistent.

Definition 7 (
B^{3}
resilience).

An asymmetric failprone system
\mathbb{F}
is
B^{3}
-resilient
if it holds that

\displaystyle\forall i,j\in\mathcal{P},\forall F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i},\forall F_{j}\in\mathcal{F}_{j},\forall F_{i,j}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}^{*}\cap\mathcal{F}_{j}^{*}:F_{i}\cup F_{j}\cup F_{i,j}\subsetneq\mathcal{P}.

We remark that, just like
B^{3}
consistency is a generalization of
Q^{3}

consistency to the asymmetric setting, other forms of
Q^{k}
consistency could
also be adapted to the asymmetric setting. While it has been shown that
B^{2}

consistency, a weak form of consistency in the asymmetric setting, actually
degrades to a symmetric failure assumption
[
26
]
, the
adaption of e.g.
Q^{4}
consistency into the asymmetric setting is an open
problem.

4
Asymmetric Grid Quorum Systems

In this model of asymmetric trust, coordination is required: Processes must
know of each others’ quorum systems before execution of the distributed
protocol can commence, and their choices must be compatible. This is limiting
because learning each others’ assumptions is, itself, a distributed problem. We
propose to solve this by letting processes choose among a set of well-defined
options, which are compatible by construction but are still meaningfully
different. Then, no coordination is required: Processes simply select the
quorum system that aligns most with their subjective belief, and can commence
with the protocol.

To this end, we remark that real-world processes are commonly heterogeneous,
i.e. they differ in attributes such as their operating system, geographical
location, or software version. Which of these attributes meaningfully affects a
process’ trustworthiness is inherently subjective, and so a good starting
point for our quorum system construction.

Heterogeneous Processes

We consider a set of
d
attributes
A_{1},\ldots,A_{d}
. Each attribute
A_{j}
can
take on one of
k_{j}
many distinct values in a set
\mathcal{A}_{j}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k_{j}}\}
. We now consider processes which differ in these attributes.
That is, each process
i\in\mathcal{P}
will take on exactly one attribute value
a\in\mathcal{A}_{j}
for every attribute
A_{j}
.

We specifically limit ourselves to the universe
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{A}_{d}
where there is
exactly one process
for every distinct
combination of attribute values. Hence, there are a total of
n=\prod_{j=1}^{d}k_{j}
processes. These processes can be thought of as a finite

d
-dimensional grid, i.e., a hyperrectangle of attributes, where processes
make up the vertices. A process’ location in the grid depends on the values it
takes on for each of the attributes. An example of such a universe, with
attributes for the operating system (“OS”) and location (“Loc”), is show in

fig.

n\geq 4
processes when considering an

threshold failure assumption.

Consider a subset of

Lemma 2 (Cardinality of universe restricted to set of attribute values).

For any two different attributes

and any two subsets of their

1

\Big\lvert{{\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{P}{\upharpoonright_{A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{i}}}}}\Big\rvert=\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{i}}\Big\lvert{{\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{P}{\upharpoonright_{A_{i}=a_{i}}}}}\Big\rvert=\frac{n}{k_{i}}\cdot\Big\lvert\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{i}\Big\rvert.

The second claim follows by the same argument:

∎

Lemma 4 (Cardinality of failprone set).

The cardinality of any failprone set

Lemma 5 (Cardinality of union of full failures).

Consider any two failprone sets
F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
and
F_{j}\in\mathcal{F}_{j}
for

. Then their respective full failures necessarily overlap, and so

\left|\fullf(F_{i})\cup\fullf(F_{j})\right|=\frac{n}{k_{i}}\cdot\textit{f}_{i}+\frac{n}{k_{j}}\cdot\textit{f}_{j}-\frac{n}{k_{i}k_{j}}\cdot\textit{f}_{i}\cdot\textit{f}_{j}.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{i}\subset\mathcal{A}_{i}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{j}\subset\mathcal{A}_{j}
be the attribute values taken
on by processes in
\fullf(F_{i})
respectively
\fullf(F_{j})
. Then, by the

, and

2

\displaystyle\left|\fullf(F_{i})\cup\fullf(F_{j})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\fullf(F_{i})\right|+\left|\fullf(F_{j})\right|-\left|\fullf(F_{i})\cap\fullf(F_{j})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{{\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{P}{\upharpoonright_{\mathcal{A}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{i}}}}}\right|+\Big\lvert{{\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{P}{\upharpoonright_{\mathcal{A}_{j}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{j}}}}}\Big\rvert-\Big\lvert{{\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{P}{\upharpoonright_{\mathcal{A}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{i}\land\mathcal{A}_{j}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{j}}}}}\Big\rvert

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{k_{i}}\cdot\textit{f}_{i}+\frac{n}{k_{j}}\textit{f}_{j}-\frac{n}{k_{i}k_{j}}\cdot\textit{f}_{i}\cdot\textit{f}_{j}.

Lemma 6 (Union of partial failures).

Let
F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
and
F_{j}\in\mathcal{F}_{j}
be any two failprone sets of
different processes. Then

\left|\partialf(F_{i})\cup\partialf(F_{j})\right|\leq\textit{p}_{i}\alpha_{i}+\textit{p}_{j}\alpha_{j}.

Equality is reached by choosing the partial failures of the two such that
they do not overlap.

Proof.

By definition, the partial failures of any failprone set
F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}

contain up to
\alpha_{i}
processes for each of
\textit{p}_{i}

attribute values. Thus

\left|\partialf(F_{i})\cup\partialf(F_{j})\right|\leq\left|\partialf(F_{i})\right|+\left|\partialf(F_{j})\right|=\textit{p}_{i}\alpha_{i}+\textit{p}_{j}\alpha_{j}.

It is generally easy to pick the two sets disjoint, due to the low number of
partial failures.
∎

Lemma 7 (Intersection of two failprone sets).

Let
F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
and
F_{j}\in\mathcal{F}_{j}
be any two failprone sets of
different processes. Then

\displaystyle\left|\fullf(F_{i})\cap\fullf(F_{j})\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{k_{i}k_{j}}\cdot\textit{f}_{i}\textit{f}_{j},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\left|\partialf(F_{i})\cap\fullf(F_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\textit{p}_{i}\alpha_{i},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\left|F_{i}\cap F_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{k_{i}k_{j}}\cdot\textit{f}_{i}\textit{f}_{j}+\textit{p}_{j}\alpha_{j}+\textit{p}_{i}\alpha_{i}.

Proof.

Recall that by definition the full and partial failures of a failprone
set are a partition of the set. We first consider the intersection of the
full failures of
F_{i}
and
F_{j}
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{A}_{i}
and

\mathcal{A}_{j}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{A}_{j}
be the sets of attribute values of
\fullf(F_{i})

respectively
\fullf(F_{j})
. Then the first expression follows by

lemma

2
as

\Big\lvert\fullf(F_{i})\cap\fullf(F_{j})\Big\rvert=\Big\lvert{{\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{P}{\upharpoonright_{A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\prime}\land A_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{j}^{\prime}}}}}\Big\rvert=\frac{n}{k_{i}k_{j}}\cdot f_{i}f_{j}.

The bound for
\left|\partialf(F_{i})\cap\fullf(F_{j})\right|
is trivial, as
the intersection of two sets is at most as large as either of its
constituents, and
\left|\partialf(F_{i})\right|=\textit{p}_{i}\alpha_{i}
. However, we want to show that the bound is tight. Assume

\fullf(F_{j})
is given, and we place the elements of
\partialf(F_{i})
. For
each attribute value
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\prime}
, we can choose up to

\alpha_{i}
processes from
{{\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{P}{\upharpoonright_{A_{i}=a_{i}}}}}
to include in the
partial failures. We first consider the number of processes which are both in

{{\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{P}{\upharpoonright_{A_{i}=a_{i}}}}}
as well as in
\fullf(F_{j})
, which
by
lemma

2
is

\big\lvert{{\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{P}{\upharpoonright_{A_{i}=a_{i}}}}}\cap\fullf(F_{j})\big\rvert=big\lvert{{\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{P}{\upharpoonright_{A_{i}=a_{i}\land A_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{j}^{\prime}}}}}big\rvert=\frac{n}{k_{i}k_{j}}\cdot f_{j}.

By
lemma

3
this is larger than
\alpha_{j}
,
and so the partial failures of
F_{j}
, rather than the full failures of
F_{i}
,
will limit the size of the intersection, and so all partial failures of
F_{i}

are “spent” on full failures of
F_{j}
and vice-versa. Then, the
intersection
\partialf(F_{i})\cap\partialf(F_{j})
is empty, and thus

\displaystyle\left|F_{i}\cap F_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\fullf(F_{i})\cup\fullf(F_{j})\right|+\left|\partialf(F_{i})\cup\fullf(F_{j})\right|+\left|\fullf(F_{i})\cup\partialf(F_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{k_{i}k_{j}}\cdot\textit{f}_{i}\textit{f}_{j}+\textit{p}_{i}\alpha_{i}+\textit{p}_{j}\alpha_{j}.

While one could place partial failures of
F_{i}
and
F_{j}
such that

\partialf(F_{i})\cap\partialf(F_{j})\neq\varnothing
, this would lower the
cardinality of the intersection of partial- and full-failures by two for
every element added to the intersection of partial failures, and so the bound
for
\left|F_{i}\cap F_{j}\right|
holds.
∎

Lemma 8.

Consider any two different attributes

and
A_{j}

Recall that for such a maximum-cardinality intersection set, the constituent

To maximize the cardinality of this set, we want to minimize the intersection of

with the two failprone sets

and
F_{j}

Theorem 10 (
B^{3}
resilience of
\mathbb{F}
).

The asymmetric failprone system
\mathbb{F}=[\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{d}]
is
B^{3}
-resilient.

Proof.

By
theorem

9
, each
\mathcal{F}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}
is
Q^{3}
-resilient.
Consider thus any two
\mathcal{F}_{i},\mathcal{F}_{j}\in\mathbb{F}
with

. Let
F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
and

be one failprone set of each, and

a joint failprone set of the two. In order to show that

is

-resilient, we show that
\lvert F_{i}\cup F_{j}\cup F_{i,j}\rvert<n
. To do so, we first partition this set and then bound the cardinality of each

\displaystyle\left|F_{i}\cup F_{j}\cup F_{i,j}\right|=

\displaystyle\left|F_{i}\cup F_{j}\sqcup(F_{i,j}\setminus(F_{i}\cup F_{j}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\fullf(F_{i})\cup\fullf(F_{j})\right|+\left|\partialf(F_{i})\cup\partialf(F_{j})\right|

\displaystyle+\left|F_{i,j}\setminus(F_{i}\cup F_{j})\right|

By definition

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{n}{k_{i}}\cdot\textit{f}_{i}+\frac{n}{k_{j}}\cdot\textit{f}_{j}-\frac{n}{k_{i}k_{j}}\cdot\textit{f}_{i}\textit{f}_{j}

\displaystyle+\textit{p}_{i}\alpha_{i}+\textit{p}_{j}\alpha_{j}

\displaystyle+\frac{n}{k_{i}k_{j}}\cdot\textit{f}_{i}\textit{f}_{j}+(\textit{p}_{i}-\textit{f}_{i})\alpha_{i}+(\textit{p}_{j}-\textit{f}_{j})\alpha_{j}

We now insert the expressions of

and
simplify. As all

follows that

\displaystyle\left|F_{i}\cup F_{j}\cup F_{i,j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq n-k_{i}\delta_{i}-k_{j}\delta_{j}-\frac{n}{2k_{i}}\cdot\epsilon_{i}-\frac{n}{2k_{j}}\cdot\epsilon_{j}-3(\epsilon_{i}\delta_{i}+\epsilon_{j}\delta{j})<n.

And so
\mathbb{F}
is

-resilient.

Tightness of the construction

We briefly discuss tightness of the construction in the sense of whether its
parameters can be increased without violating
B^{3}
resilience. The two
parameters to consider are
\textit{f}_{i}
, the ratio of attribute values making
up the full failures of a failprone set, and
\alpha_{i}
, the number of
processes per remaining attribute value which make up the partial failures.

Lemma 11.

The construction is tight with regards to the ratio of full-failure
attribute values it supports.

Proof.

For the ratio of full-failure attribute values we use
\textit{f}_{i}=\left\lceil k_{i}/3\right\rceil
. Assume we instead used some value
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\textit{f}_{i}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu>\textit{f}_{i}
. As these are integers, it must hold that
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\textit{f}_{i}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\geq\textit{f}_{i}+1\geq k_{i}/3
. Let now
F^{\prime}_{1},F^{\prime}_{2},F^{\prime}_{3}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
be three failprone sets of such a hypothetical failprone system. Recall
that we can choose the attribute values of their full failures freely. But
then as
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\textit{f}_{i}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu>k_{i}/3
, it is easy to choose those such that
the union
F^{\prime}_{1}\cup F^{\prime}_{2}\cup F^{\prime}_{3}
covers the universe
\mathcal{P}
, and the
system would not be
B^{3}
-consistent.
∎

For
\alpha_{i}
, the second parameter, the value we chose is
asymptotically required for our proof of consistency in

theorem

10
. However, it is not optimal due to two reasons. First,
it is not possible to simultaneously achieve both a maximum-cardinality
intersection set as in
lemma

7
and a
maximum-cardinality set of partial failures as in

lemma

6
, as a maximum-cardinality
intersection set will inevitably fully cover all processes for some attribute
values, overlapping with the set of partial failures. Empirically, to maximize
the cardinality of a union as seen in the
B^{3}
resilience proof, one will end
up with a tradeoff where the intersection set is chosen slightly non-maximal to
permit making use of all partial failures. Second, for specific choices of
attribute cardinalities, the
\alpha_{i}
parameter can actually be
incremented while remaining compatible with our proof.

Figure

3
(
appendix

A
) visualizes this for the
two-dimensional case, showing that this mainly matters for low-attribute
cardinalities.

5
Comparison with threshold failure assumption

Asymmetric trust models such as the one presented here are
more expressive than traditional symmetric failure assumptions, in that they
allow processes to choose failure assumptions based on personal beliefs.
However, protocols that use them are closely related to the traditional ones,
and so expressiveness it not an end in itself. Rather, processes strive to
perform some task in spite of other processes being faulty. In order to be
useful, asymmetric failure assumptions must be able to model some failure
scenarios — at least for a subset of processes — which symmetric
assumptions could not. We will discuss this by comparing our failprone systems
with the commonly-used
threshold failure assumptions
.

The threshold failure assumption of interest to us is
n>3f
, which states
that strictly less than one third of processes are faulty. This quorum system
is
Q^{3}
-consistent, which is the symmetric analogue of our
B^{3}
-consistent
construction. We denote by
\mathcal{F}_{n>3f}
the failprone system of such a
threshold failure assumption, i.e.,

\mathcal{F}_{n>3f}=\left\{X\subset\mathcal{P}:\left|X\right|=\left\lceil\frac{n}{3}-1\right\rceil\right\}.

Consider an asymmetric grid failprone system
\mathbb{F}
as per

definition

10
. By

lemma

4
, all failprone sets of any given
failprone system
\mathcal{F}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}
have the same cardinality. If these failprone
systems would consist of failprone sets with cardinality at most

\left\lceil\frac{n}{3}\right\rceil-1
, then none of these systems could model a failure
scenario which is not also modeled by
\mathcal{F}_{n>3f}
, and no rational process
would be inclined to use it. We thus only care about asymmetric failprone
systems where there are at least some failprone sets with a cardinality larger
than those of the
n>3f
threshold failure assumption, and call such systems

useful
.

Definition 13 (Usefulness of asymmetric grid failprone systems).

An asymmetric grid failprone system
\mathbb{F}
is
useful
, if there exists
at least one failprone system
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{F}
, whose failprone sets have a
cardinality larger than
\left\lceil\frac{n}{3}\right\rceil-1
.

In the remainder, we characterize which universes permit for
useful

asymmetric grid failprone systems. Many of the involved arguments are
straightforward and have been relegated to the appendix.

Equal-cardinality attributes

Consider first the special case of all attributes having cardinality
k
, for a
total of
n=k^{d}
processes. By
lemma

4
, the
cardinality of any failprone set
F_{i}
is

\Big\lvert F_{i}\Big\rvert=\left\lceil\frac{k}{3}-1\right\rceil\cdot\frac{n}{k}+\left\lfloor\frac{2k}{3}+1\right\rfloor\cdot\bigg\lceil\frac{n}{6k}-1\bigg\rceil.

Since this cardinality approaches
\lvert F_{i}\rvert=\frac{n}{3}+\frac{n}{9}
as

k
grows, it is strictly larger than the asymptotic cardinality
\frac{n}{3}

of the threshold assumption. For smaller values of
k
this may not be the
case, however. We show that for the two-dimensional case, all attribute
cardinalities above
7
, except for
k\in\{9,12\}
, permit for useful
failprone systems to emerge. With the straight-forward optimization of

\alpha_{\cdot}
as discussed in
section

4
, this even extends to

k=5
. Effectively this means that most universes with at least
25

processes are useful for our construction.
For the
d
-dimensional case, the same can be shown for all cardinalities above

4
, except for
k=6
. This covers many possible universes from
64

processes onward. Details, including proofs, can be found in

table

1
(
appendix

B
).

Unequal-cardinality attributes

The analysis is more complex if we allow attributes to differ in their
cardinalities, since failprone sets of two failprone systems may not have the
same cardinality. For a concrete set of attributes it is possible that one
failprone system is useful while another is not. As above, we aim to
characterize which parts of the parameter space permit for useful failprone
systems to exist.

Figure

4
(
appendix

B
) shows
the ratio of grid-style to threshold-style failprone sets for the
two-dimensional case, and highlights some of the patterns which emerge. As an
example, there are already configurations which permit for failprone systems to
be useful with cardinalities as low as
k_{1}=4
and
k_{2}=7
, for a total of

28
processes. If one wants both failprone systems to be useful, then most
configurations where both attributes’ cardinalities are above
7
are suitable,
except some values where one of the two is equal to
8
,
9
or
12
.

For higher dimensions, useful failprone systems emerge for e.g.
d\geq 3
and

k_{i}\in\{4,7,8\}
, as well as for virtually all cases where attributes have
a higher cardinality. We provide more details and proofs in

table

2
in the appendix. A more generic comparison with
threshold quorums is left for future work.

6
Conclusion

We have introduced a quorum system construction for the asymmetric trust
setting with byzantine faults which allows processes to express their
subjective assumptions without the need for coordination. This breaks the
cyclic dependency that is otherwise present in this setting, where
processes need to coordinate among themselves before being able to participate
in the distributed protocol. Our construction offers processes a choice among
different quorum systems based on attributes in which real-world processes
commonly differ. Each process can then pick the one which aligns most with its
subjective view.

We have compared these quorum systems with the threshold failure assumption

n>3f
used commonly, and have shown that they are viable, and can provide
higher resilience, for different configurations of processes, from as little as

25
processes, but especially performing well when the number of processes
increases. There are clear avenues for future work, such as allowing for
“gaps” in the universe of processes, an optimization of parameters, or
practical benchmarks.
