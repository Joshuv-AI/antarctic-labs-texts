Pre-manufacturing prediction of jaggery color formation through soil and water parameters using machine learning
Authors: Kathirvel Narayanasamy, Ilayaraja Venkatachalam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Jaggery, a traditional unrefined sugar produced from concentrated sugarcane juice, represents a critical economic sector supporting over 50 million rural livelihoods globally. Jaggery color determines market pricing, with Golden variants commanding 40–60% premium; yet current assessment relies on subjective evaluation and costly chemical interventions. This study presents a machine learning (ML) framework for pre-manufacturing jaggery color classification using agricultural and chemical parameters measurable during sugarcane procurement. Analysis of 2001 sugarcane samples from four varieties across three seasons in Erode, Tamil Nadu, achieved 98.3% test accuracy across three color categories (Golden, Light brown, Dark brown) with Random Forest and Gradient Boosting classifiers. Color labels were assigned through expert visual grading and independently verified under standardized conditions; however, no instrumental colorimetric measurements were used, which constitutes a limitation of the ground truth. Feature importance analysis revealed soil type and water type as the strongest predictive associations within this regional dataset, collectively accounting for 62.93% of predictive importance. Three-tier validation including cross-validation, bootstrap resampling, and Leave-One-Location-Out analysis across 19 locations confirmed robust within-region generalization. Computational efficiency (0.017 s prediction time) supports mobile device deployment. This framework provides quantitative evidence for the predictive associations between cultivation conditions and color outcome, enabling a shift from reactive to proactive quality management. Validation is currently restricted to the Erode region; multi-regional testing is required before broader deployment. No external validation across distinct agroecological systems has been conducted.

Introduction

Background and significance

Jaggery, a traditional unrefined sugar product derived from concentrated sugarcane juice, plays a critical role in the global natural sweetener market, particularly in rural agro-industries dependent on decentralized sugarcane processing. The jaggery industry operates primarily through small-to-medium-scale rural enterprises, with India contributing approximately 70% of global output (approximately 11 million metric tons annually). Production predominantly employs traditional open-pan heating methods, in which sugarcane juice is concentrated through evaporation at temperatures of 110–130 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C, resulting in solid blocks ranging in color from golden-yellow to dark brown. Although production intensity is highest during the peak sugarcane harvest period (October–March), jaggery manufacturing is not strictly seasonal and continues throughout the year in many regions, supported by staggered harvesting practices, irrigation availability, and localized market demand. Typical production units process approximately 5–10 tons of sugarcane per day.

Despite its economic significance, the jaggery industry faces fundamental quality prediction challenges that limit production optimization and market efficiency. Color classification serves as the primary determinant of market value, with Golden variants commanding premium prices over brown alternatives due to superior organoleptic properties and extended shelf life1–4. The growing market for packaged jaggery (projected CAGR of 6.8% through 2033, reaching USD 2.1 billion)5 reflects increasing quality consciousness among consumers. While current pricing for cane farmers is typically based on sugar yield per metric ton, the ability to predict jaggery color prior to processing adds an important dimension to quality assessment. By combining sugar yield and color classification, producers can consistently offer higher quality products, thereby preventing economic losses and enabling fairer price setting for farmers6. Jaggery production represents a critical economic sector supporting over 50 million rural livelihoods globally1,5. Beyond economic significance, jaggery provides essential nutritional benefits as an unrefined natural sweetener rich in iron (11 mg/100g), magnesium (70–90 mg/100g), potassium (1050 mg/100g), and calcium, serving as a healthier alternative to refined sugar in traditional diets. Health benefits include improved hemoglobin levels (iron content supports anemia prevention), enhanced digestion (alkaline nature aids digestive enzyme activity), and respiratory health (traditional medicine uses for cough/cold relief). Unlike refined sugar, jaggery retains natural minerals and phytochemicals derived from sugarcane, thereby offering micronutrient supplementation that is particularly valuable in nutritionally vulnerable rural populations3,4.

Current quality assessment relies primarily on subjective visual evaluation by experienced manufacturers, leading to inconsistent standards and costly chemical interventions that increase production costs by 30–40% while compromising natural product characteristics. This cost escalation was documented through exploratory field visits to over 50 manufacturing units across the Erode region during the 2023–2024 production seasons. This prediction gap forces suboptimal decision-making across industrial-scale operations, particularly in major producing regions where manufacturers must guarantee color outcomes through expensive post-processing or risk contract defaults. Throughout this manuscript, pre-manufacturing parameters refer to agricultural and chemical characteristics measurable during sugarcane procurement before the onset of any jaggery manufacturing activities, including soil type, water source, sugarcane variety, seasonal conditions, ratoon stage, and juice chemical composition.

Hidden natural potential for golden jaggery Golden jaggery commands a premium price in the market; however, its production commonly depends on chemical interventions to achieve the desired color. In current manufacturing practice, many sugarcane batches are treated chemically because producers lack reliable methods to predict the final jaggery color before processing. Analysis of 2001 samples shows that a considerable proportion of sugarcane can naturally produce Golden jaggery when grown under suitable soil, water, and varietal conditions. Therefore, the key challenge is not the limited availability of Golden-producing sugarcane, but the inability to identify such batches at the procurement stage. As a result, manufacturers adopt conservative processing strategies that increase production costs and reduce the natural quality characteristics that are valued in premium jaggery products.

Daily procurement challenge A typical manufacturer processing 6.5 metric tons receives deliveries from different suppliers with different soil-water-variety profiles. At procurement, the manufacturer cannot predict which batches will naturally produce Golden jaggery. Current practice involves mixing all batches and applying chemical treatment uniformly. However, field observations indicate that 30–50% of procured cane naturally produces Golden without intervention, yet receives unnecessary chemical treatment under blind procurement strategies. This prediction gap creates systematic economic waste while compromising the natural quality characteristics that command premium market positioning.

Soil composition is associated with variations in jaggery color through multiple biochemical pathways. Soil mineral content (Fe, Mg, K, Ca) absorbed during cultivation catalyzes enzymatic browning reactions during juice concentration, with iron-rich soils promoting darker pigmentation through polyphenol oxidase activation1,8. Soil nitrogen availability modulates amino acid profiles in sugarcane juice, which serve as essential reactants in Maillard browning reactions that generate brown color compounds during thermal processing7. Red soils and black cotton soils, prevalent in major jaggery-producing regions, exhibit distinct mineral signatures that create characteristic color profiles in finished products1. Water source characteristics show comparable associations with color variation through dissolved mineral content and pH modulation. River water typically contains lower dissolved solids compared to groundwater or borewell sources, resulting in reduced catalytic activity for browning reactions9. Water mineral composition (particularly calcium and magnesium concentrations) affects juice pH during extraction, subsequently influencing enzyme activity during concentration and the extent of caramelization reactions7. These soil-water interactions create region-specific terroir effects analogous to those documented in wine production10,11, where environmental factors fundamentally determine final product characteristics through biochemical pathway modulation rather than direct compositional effects. Multiple sugarcane varieties demonstrated significant variation in jaggery quality parameters, indicating strong genetic influence on final product characteristics. Justification for machine learning approaches The inherent complexity of these interacting biochemical pathways–involving multiple enzymes (polyphenol oxidase8, peroxidase), temperature-dependent reactions (Maillard7, caramelization), and precursor availability governed by agricultural conditions–creates a multi-dimensional prediction challenge unsuitable for simple mechanistic models. Each pathway responds non-linearly to environmental inputs, with interactions that are difficult to model explicitly. Explicit specification of every reaction pathway, enzyme kinetic parameter, and environmental interaction would require prohibitive experimental investment and computational complexity. Machine learning offers a pragmatic alternative: by learning non-linear relationships directly from observed soil-water-variety-color patterns, ML models can capture the aggregate effects of these complex biochemical interactions without requiring explicit mechanistic specification of each pathway12. This data-driven approach complements rather than replaces mechanistic understanding, using domain knowledge to guide feature selection while leveraging ML’s pattern recognition capabilities for prediction.Fig. 2Proposed machine learning architecture for jaggery color classification framework. Related work

Recent machine learning advances12,13 have enabled precise agricultural prediction for crop monitoring, yield estimation, and disease detection. While previous studies focused on sugar yield prediction6, color classification in processed agricultural products remains underexplored. Most existing applications focus on yield prediction rather than quality attributes, and no prior work has developed machine learning models for jaggery color classification using labeled datasets6,14. Color is a critical quality attribute that strongly influences consumer preferences and pricing of food products. In jaggery production specifically, color variation from Golden yellow to Dark brown directly impacts market grading and pricing structures. Recent ML-driven color classification studies15,16 have employed hyperspectral imaging, computer vision, and spectral reflectance techniques to achieve precise grading of fruits, grains, and sugarcane-derived products. Recent machine learning studies in the sugarcane processing domain have demonstrated that sugar yield can be accurately predicted at the pre-manufacturing stage using a minimal set of chemical parameters, such as Brix and moisture content, achieving high accuracy while significantly reducing analytical complexity and cost17.

Recent advances in ML-based food quality assessment have demonstrated significant progress. Deep learning approaches using convolutional neural networks achieved 96.2% accuracy in multi-class fruit color grading18, while ensemble methods combined with hyperspectral imaging improved honey color classification to 94.8%19. Transfer learning approaches have successfully adapted pre-trained models for agricultural product quality assessment, reducing training data requirements by 60%20. Explainable AI techniques using SHAP values have enabled interpretation of black-box models in food quality prediction21. However, these studies focus exclusively on post-harvest assessment rather than pre-manufacturing prediction using cultivation parameters.

Research gap and objectives

Identification of critical gap

Despite extensive research on jaggery quality improvement1,9 and agricultural product color classification using imaging and machine learning approaches15,16,18,19, no existing framework enables reliable prediction of jaggery color prior to manufacturing using parameters measurable during sugarcane procurement6,14. Previous studies have focused exclusively on post-production chemical interventions9,22 and sugar yield prediction6, leaving color outcomes unpredictable until after processing completion. This uncertainty limits proactive quality control and informed procurement decisions, necessitating a pre-manufacturing predictive framework capable of linking cultivation-stage parameters to final jaggery color outcomes. Although pre-manufacturing prediction of sugar yield using chemical parameters has been successfully demonstrated, equivalent approaches for predicting jaggery quality attributes–particularly color–prior to processing have not yet been reported, leaving a critical gap in proactive quality management for the jaggery industry17.

Materials and methods

Dataset collection and preparation

This research utilized the “Sugarcane Properties and Jaggery Production Analysis Dataset” maintained by Dr. Senthilkumar on Mendeley Data. The authors obtained access through a private sharing arrangement with a unique access credential provided by the dataset custodian. The dataset remains under restricted access on Mendeley Data and is not publicly available through a DOI, as confidentiality agreements with participating production units and sugarcane farmers preclude open distribution. Interested researchers may request access for academic and non-commercial purposes through the corresponding author (ilayaraja.v@vit.ac.in), who will coordinate with the dataset custodian, Dr. Senthilkumar, subject to approval. This controlled access approach ensures data confidentiality while maintaining scientific transparency. The dataset encompasses sugarcane samples from diverse production facilities across the Erode region of Tamil Nadu, India, collected during three harvest seasons (2023–2024). The dataset includes four major commercial sugarcane varieties: Co 86032, Co 11015, Pi 1110, and Co 86027, selected based on their regional prevalence and commercial significance, consistent with the varieties used in related ML-based sugarcane quality studies6. The original dataset comprised 13 features encompassing agricultural parameters, chemical composition measurements, and target jaggery color classifications. Quality control measures included standardized sampling protocols, certified analytical methods for chemical measurements, and expert visual assessment for color classification by experienced jaggery manufacturers. The complete source code used in this study is publicly available at Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.20039598).

Color categories were defined as Golden (premium quality, 33.8% of samples), Light brown (standard quality, 40.5% of samples), and Dark brown (lower grade, 25.7% of samples) based on established market grading standards used across Indian jaggery markets1,4. Jaggery color classification in the dataset was initially assigned by experienced jaggery manufacturers and production workers at each manufacturing unit based on their practical knowledge of commercial color grading standards. These workers routinely classify jaggery color as part of daily production and market pricing operations, applying traditional grading criteria developed through years of hands-on manufacturing experience. The classifications were subsequently verified during field visits under standardized natural daylight conditions (between 10 AM and 2 PM). The verification process involved independent visual assessment of jaggery samples against the three established market categories: Golden (bright, light golden-yellow appearance), Light brown (honey-brown appearance), and Dark brown (deeper, dark amber-brown appearance). In all cases, the researcher’s independent assessment agreed with the manufacturer-assigned color classifications, indicating high consistency between experienced industry practitioners and the research team for these visually distinct commercial grades.

It should be noted that the jaggery color labels were assigned based on expert visual assessment by experienced practitioners. Although consistency between practitioners and the research team was observed, no formal inter-rater reliability statistics (e.g., Cohen’s Kappa) were computed, and no instrumental colorimetric validation (e.g., CIE L*a*b*) was performed. Accordingly, the model predicts expert-defined categorical grading outcomes consistent with practical industry assessment rather than instrumentally quantified colorimetric values. Despite these limitations, expert visual grading reflects standard industrial practice in decentralized jaggery production systems, where quality assessment and market pricing are routinely based on human visual inspection rather than laboratory instrumentation. Therefore, the labels used in this study represent operational grading criteria relevant to real-world decision-making rather than standardized physicochemical measurements. The objective of this study is not to predict absolute colorimetric values, but to model practical classification outcomes relevant to stakeholders in the jaggery supply chain. In this context, the model learns patterns associated with expert-defined categories that directly influence economic valuation. Nevertheless, the absence of quantitative color measurement and formal inter-rater agreement introduces subjectivity in the target variable. Future work will focus on integrating instrumental colorimetric analysis and statistical agreement measures to enhance label standardization and improve predictive robustness. Instrumental color measurement was not feasible during field collection due to the lack of portable devices and the rapid color changes observed during storage and transport under ambient conditions.

Machine learning architecture for model development

The overall machine learning framework is depicted in Fig. 2. The input data included a well-distributed set of agronomic and chemical features collected from sugarcane plants grown across multiple varieties and seasons. Pre-processing involved imputation of missing values, encoding categorical variables, and scaling input features, followed by splitting the data into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets using 10-fold cross-validation to ensure robustness.

Data pre-processing and feature engineering

Data quality assessment Quality control verification confirmed complete data coverage across all 13 features, with no missing values detected in any of the 2001 samples. The preprocessing pipeline included systematic missing value handling procedures as a precautionary measure following machine learning best practices: median imputation for continuous variables (Brix value, Pol content, reducing sugar percentage, water content) and mode imputation for categorical variables (soil type, water type, variety, season, ratoon stage). However, no imputation was required as all features contained complete observations across the dataset. No outlier removal was performed to preserve natural data variability encountered in commercial operations. All categorical features underwent systematic encoding using scikit-learn’s LabelEncoder, with the target variable encoding mapping Dark brown (0), Golden (1), and Light brown (2) to preserve ordinal quality relationships. While one-hot encoding is typically preferred for nominal variables to avoid imposing ordinal relationships, LabelEncoder was selected for this study due to specific advantages in tree-based ensemble methods. Decision trees and Random Forests naturally handle integer-encoded categorical variables by learning optimal split points without assuming ordinality. This approach: (1) reduces feature space dimensionality (13 features vs. 32 with one-hot encoding for our categorical variables), (2) maintains computational efficiency during training and prediction, (3) prevents feature space fragmentation that can occur with high-cardinality categorical variables such as Location (19 classes). Validation experiments confirmed that model performance remained stable across encoding strategies (accuracy difference <0.3% between LabelEncoder and one-hot encoding), while LabelEncoder provided superior computational efficiency (71% faster training time) without sacrificing prediction quality. This encoding strategy is particularly suitable for tree-based algorithms, which represent the primary modeling approach in this study.To prevent data leakage during cross-validation, a rigorous pipeline architecture was implemented by combining the StandardScaler pre-processing and classification stages. Continuous features were standardized using z-score normalization within cross-validation folds, ensuring that scaling parameters were fitted only on the training data and applied to the validation data.

Experimental design

Three-tier validation strategy Model evaluation employed a rigorous three-tier validation approach to ensure generalization and prevent overfitting:

Tier 1—Training set optimization (70%, n = 1400): Hyperparameters were carefully selected using conservative settings to prevent overfitting, with choices informed by best practices and domain knowledge: Random Forest (n_estimators = 50, max_depth = 10, min_samples_split = 10), Gradient Boosting (n_estimators = 50, learning_rate = 0.05, max_depth = 3). All models used 10-fold stratified cross-validation for performance evaluation, ensuring no test set information influenced model configuration.

Tier 2—Cross-validation assessment (10-fold CV): Independent 10-fold stratified cross-validation on the training set (n = 1400) was performed to quantify model stability and variance.

Tier 3—Held-out test set evaluation (30%, n = 601): Final model performance assessed on completely unseen test data. This constitutes true independent validation: the test set was created at initial data split, isolated throughout all development activities, never accessed during training, never used for hyperparameter optimization, and used exactly once for final evaluation. This test set was stratified by jaggery color class (Golden: 33.8%, Light brown: 40.5%, Dark brown: 25.7%) and temporally distributed across all three seasons to ensure representative evaluation. All performance metrics in Table 6 represent evaluation on this completely independent held-out set. This independent validation set enables assessment of generalization performance without overfitting. The test set was stratified by jaggery color class (Golden: 33.8%, Light brown: 40.5%, Dark brown: 25.7%) and temporally distributed across all three seasons (Winter, Summer, Monsoon) to ensure representative evaluation across diverse production conditions. Cross-validation predictions were generated using cross_val_predict with method = “predict” for classification metrics and method = “predict_proba” for probability-based evaluation. Fixed random_state = 42 ensured reproducibility across all experiments.

Performance evaluation

The performance assessment utilized weighted averaging to account for class imbalance. The primary metrics included accuracy (overall classification correctness), precision and recall (weighted averages using zero_division = 0), and F1-score (harmonic mean of precision and recall). Precision-Recall Area Under Curve (PR-AUC) was computed using label_binarize transformation with weighted averaging. PR-AUC was selected over ROC-AUC because: (1) the dataset exhibits moderate class imbalance (balance ratio = 0.636), where PR-AUC provides more informative assessment; (2) PR-AUC is more sensitive to precision-recall trade-offs for imbalanced scenarios; (3) in industrial deployment, correctly identifying premium Golden jaggery has higher practical importance, making precision-recall metrics more relevant than true-negative rates emphasized by ROC-AUC.

Statistical significance testing

Statistical significance of performance differences was evaluated using Friedman test24 for multiple related samples, comparing accuracy scores across cross-validation folds. Pairwise model comparisons employed paired t-tests on fold-level accuracy scores with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparison adjustment. Bootstrap confidence intervals (95% confidence level) were computed using 1000 bootstrap samples for robust uncertainty quantification (Table 8).

Statistical test assumptions and corrections Bonferroni correction was selected to maintain strict family-wise error rate control (α = 0.05 divided by number of comparisons) across multiple pairwise model comparisons. Normality of fold-level accuracy distributions was verified using Shapiro-Wilk tests (p > 0.05 for all ensemble models), confirming suitability for parametric paired t-tests. Identical stratified folds (random_state=42) were maintained across all models to ensure fair statistical comparison and eliminate fold-based variance as a confounding factor.

Spatial generalizability validation

To directly assess spatial generalizability for the color classification task, Leave-One-Location-Out Cross-Validation (LOLO-CV) was performed using the Random Forest model across all 19 locations in the dataset. For each iteration, the model was trained on samples from 18 locations and tested on the held-out location. LOLO-CV achieved mean accuracy of 98.32% (SD = 2.00%), with per-location accuracies ranging from 91.30% (Senthampalayam, n = 23) to 100.00% (Athani, Erode). The two locations with accuracy below 97% (Senthampalayam and Salangapalayam) correspond to the smallest sample sizes in the dataset (n = 23 and n = 59, respectively), suggesting that reduced accuracy reflects limited local representation rather than systematic geographic bias. All locations with n\ge100 achieved accuracy \ge97.58%. These results demonstrate robust spatial generalizability within the Erode district, complementing the LOLO-CV validation previously reported for sugar yield regression17. It is important to note that LOLO-CV within a single regional ecosystem does not constitute external validation. These results confirm within-region spatial robustness only.

Feature importance stability analysis

To assess the robustness of feature importance rankings across different data partitions, the Random Forest model was trained across 10 independent random seeds with different train-test splits. Soil type consistently ranked first (mean importance = 38.98%, SD = 1.41%, coefficient of variation = 3.6%) and Water Type ranked second (mean = 21.93%, SD = 2.07%, CV = 9.4%) across all 10 iterations. The slight variation between the primary model’s Soil Type importance (41.44% in Table 10, random_state = 42) and the multi-seed mean (38.98%) reflects natural variability across data partitions. No permutation of the top-two ranking was observed in any seed, confirming that the identified feature importance hierarchy is robust to data partitioning variability. Test accuracy remained stable at 98.39% ± 0.53% (range: 97.17%–99.17%) across seeds.

Location feature ablation analysis

To address potential data leakage concerns arising from including Location as an input feature with random train-test splitting, systematic ablation experiments were conducted. Removing the Location feature (12 remaining features) produced identical test accuracy (98.34%), as did removing both Location and Unit Type (11 features, 98.34%) and additionally removing Season (10 features, 98.34%). To formally assess whether these differences are statistically significant, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were conducted on 10-fold cross-validation accuracy scores between the full 13-feature model and each reduced-feature variant (Wilcoxon tests were selected over paired t-tests as Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated non-normal distributions of paired differences). No statistically significant difference was observed between the full model and the 12-feature model (W = 4.00, p = 1.000), the 11-feature model (W = 0.00, p = 0.500), or the 10-feature model (W = 0.00, p = 0.062), indicating that removal of Location, Unit Type, and Season features does not significantly affect predictive performance (Bonferroni-corrected α = 0.0167). These results provide statistical evidence that model performance is primarily associated with soil type, water type, and chemical composition features rather than location-specific information. The Location feature contributed only 1.31% importance in Random Forest analysis, consistent with its negligible predictive role.

Agricultural terroir effects in jaggery production

The dominance of soil type and water type in predictive models revealed previously unreported predictive associations between agricultural parameters and jaggery color formation, paralleling established research in wine production10,11 where soil composition and water source characteristics significantly influence final product quality through complex biochemical pathways7. Soil type and water type collectively account for 62.93% of predictive importance, raising questions about conventional assumptions regarding chemical composition primacy in jaggery color determination. These results reflect predictive associations within this observational dataset and should not be interpreted as establishing causal primacy of terroir factors over chemical composition.

The secondary importance of chemical parameters (Brix value, water content) suggests that these measurements may reflect underlying agricultural influences rather than serve as independent predictive factors. This hierarchical relationship indicates that environmental conditions established during cultivation may influence chemical composition, which subsequently affects color outcomes through biochemical processes during juice concentration.

As this study is observational, the demonstrated predictive relationships between agricultural parameters and jaggery color suggest but do not confirm causal dominance of terroir factors. The observed associations may partially reflect unmeasured confounders such as regional microclimate variations or farming practice differences correlated with soil-water combinations. Controlled greenhouse experiments varying soil composition while maintaining genetic uniformity are required to establish causal mechanisms.

Decision boundary structure and data characteristics

The exceptional performance of a decision tree (96.84%) provides crucial insights into jaggery color formation mechanisms. This minimal 1.5 percentage point gap from ensemble methods (98.3%) indicates that color outcomes follow deterministic patterns with clear decision boundaries rather than complex interactions requiring sophisticated approaches. This finding enables flexible industrial deployment, as manufacturers can select appropriate complexity levels based on operational requirements.

The poor performance of Support Vector Machine (71.21% accuracy) provides additional insights into the underlying data structure, despite systematic hyperparameter optimization testing both RBF and polynomial kernels with extensive parameter grids (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C \in \{0.1, 1, 10\}$$\end{document}, gamma \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\in \{'scale', 'auto'\}$$\end{document}). The SVM’s fundamental approach–seeking optimal hyperplanes to maximize margin separation in high-dimensional feature space–proved unsuitable for this classification task. This performance gap indicates that jaggery color categories form distinct, non-overlapping clusters with categorical boundaries rather than continuous gradients requiring margin-based separation. The observed region-specific soil-water patterns in this dataset create discrete color groupings that align naturally with tree-based decision boundaries (achieving 96.84–98.3% accuracy) but resist linear and kernel-based hyperplane separation strategies employed by SVMs. The strong performance of shallow models also explains the absence of deeper architectures such as convolutional neural networks or transformer-based models from the evaluated algorithm set. The dataset size (n = 2001) falls substantially below the threshold typically required for effective deep learning training (typically > 10,000 samples for tabular classification), creating significant overfitting risk with high-parameter architectures. More fundamentally, the marginal accuracy gain from a single decision tree (96.84%) to the best ensemble method (98.34%) was only 1.50 percentage points, indicating that the classification task exhibits clear categorical boundaries that do not benefit from the hierarchical feature abstraction capabilities that justify deep learning in complex domains such as image recognition or natural language processing. The Multi-Layer Perceptron evaluated in this study (single hidden layer, 32 neurons) already captures non-linear patterns while maintaining appropriate model complexity for the available data, and its competitive performance (97.84%) relative to ensemble methods further confirms that additional architectural complexity yields diminishing returns for this classification task (Figs. 10, 11).Fig. 11SHAP summary (beeswarm) plot illustrating feature impact on the prediction of Golden jaggery using the Random Forest model. Statistical evidence for deterministic patterns

Multiple lines of evidence support the deterministic nature of jaggery color formation:

Prediction confidence Across the 601-sample test set, the mean predicted probability for assigned classes was 85.3% ± 10.5%, indicating moderate-to-high model certainty. The confidence distribution reflects the inherent challenge of categorical color prediction from continuous variables, where class boundaries represent discrete categorizations of a continuous quality spectrum.

Minimal ensemble improvement The modest gain (+1.46 percentage points from decision tree to Random Forest) contrasts sharply with truly complex classification tasks. In domains requiring capture of subtle non-linear interactions–such as medical diagnosis from high-dimensional biomarker profiles–ensemble methods typically provide 5–15 percentage point improvements over simple baselines. The absence of substantial ensemble benefit indicates that jaggery color formation follows predominantly additive patterns well-captured by shallow decision rules.

Cross-model consistency Random Forest (98.34%), Gradient Boosting (98.34%), and Extra Trees (98.17%) converged to nearly identical performance despite fundamentally different optimization strategies. This convergence suggests all algorithms discovered the same underlying decision boundaries inherent to the data structure.

Statistical validation and robustness

The incorporation of Cohen’s Kappa (κ) as a chance-corrected agreement metric addresses the potential concern of inflated accuracy in imbalanced datasets. With κ values ranging from 0.9671 to 0.9747 for ensemble methods, the results demonstrate that high classification performance is not an artifact of class distribution bias (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_e$$\end{document} = 0.3443) but rather reflects genuine discriminative capability across all three jaggery color categories. The substantial difference between ensemble methods (κ > 0.96) and SVM (κ = 0.5385) further validates the superior pattern recognition capability of Random Forest and Gradient Boosting models, confirming that their high accuracy metrics represent true classification skill rather than overfitting or chance agreement.

Practical implementation challenges

Despite demonstrated computational feasibility (0.017s prediction time, 0.07 MB memory), real-world deployment faces several practical barriers that must be addressed for successful industrial adoption.

Data collection infrastructure Systematic recording of soil type and water source characteristics during sugarcane procurement requires training programs for field officers or development of mobile data collection applications with standardized data entry protocols. The challenge lies in ensuring consistent data quality across hundreds of procurement points, particularly in rural settings with limited connectivity.

Standardized assessment protocols While experienced agricultural officers can visually classify major soil types, borderline cases and mixed soil regions require objective assessment criteria. Implementation may benefit from portable soil testing kits or low-cost spectroscopic equipment for water quality assessment. Establishing and enforcing these standards across a distributed procurement network presents significant organizational challenges.

Regional model transferability Our validation framework recommends minimum 500-sample datasets per deployment region to verify model accuracy and recalibrate feature importance hierarchies for local environmental conditions. This requirement represents substantial upfront investment in data collection, labeling, and validation testing.

Integration with existing systems Many jaggery manufacturers utilize manual record-keeping or basic spreadsheet systems. Deploying ML-based prediction requires either developing standalone mobile applications with offline capability or integrating prediction APIs into existing enterprise resource planning systems. The fragmented nature of the industry demands flexible deployment architectures accommodating diverse technical capabilities.

User acceptance Traditional manufacturers often rely on generations of experiential knowledge for quality assessment. Convincing stakeholders to adopt ML-based predictions requires demonstration projects showing clear economic benefits, transparent validation showing prediction reliability, and trust-building through phased implementation where predictions supplement rather than replace human expertise initially.

Successful implementation therefore requires coordinated efforts spanning technical deployment, organizational change, economic alignment, and gradual trust-building through pilot projects demonstrating consistent value delivery.

Operational deployment framework

Phase 1—Data collection (Procurement stage): Field officers record: (1) Supplier farm location, (2) Soil type (visual classification: red/alluvial/black), (3) Water source (canal/river/groundwater/borewell), (4) Cane variety, (5) Ratoon stage, (6) Harvest season via mobile application with offline capability.

Phase 2—Laboratory measurement: Upon cane arrival, laboratory technicians measure juice parameters using standard refractometers (Brix), polarimeters (Pol), and chemical assays (reducing sugars, water content). Data entry takes approximately 10 minutes per batch.

Phase 3—Prediction generation: Mobile app transmits data to prediction server (or computes locally using embedded model). Random Forest model generates color classification (Golden/Light brown/Dark brown) with confidence score within <1 second.

Phase 4—Procurement decision: Manufacturer reviews prediction report showing: expected color category (98% accuracy), confidence level, batch suitability for current production targets. High-confidence Golden predictions routed to premium production lines; other predictions inform chemical intervention decisions or alternative product routing.

Phase 5—Validation Feedback Loop: Actual color outcomes recorded post-production. Prediction accuracy monitored continuously; systematic deviations trigger model recalibration using regional data accumulation protocols. It is important to note that the 98.3% accuracy reported here was achieved under standardized data collection conditions. Deployment in commercial settings with variable data entry quality, inconsistent soil classification, or measurement instrument variability may result in reduced real-world performance, and ongoing accuracy monitoring is therefore strongly recommended.

Economic impact and industrial applications

The proposed predictive framework addresses fundamental value chain inefficiencies by transforming reactive quality management into proactive procurement optimization. Current industrial practices for Golden jaggery production rely on chemical additives to achieve desired color characteristics, increasing production costs and compromising natural product quality. By enabling variety-aware procurement of sugarcane with high Golden-color probability, the framework facilitates chemical-free Golden jaggery production, thereby improving product quality, shelf stability, and market acceptance.

Current operational costs

Field visits to medium-scale jaggery manufacturing units revealed that chemical additives are used exclusively for Golden jaggery production, while Light brown and Dark brown variants require no chemical intervention. Sodium hydrosulfite is the primary additive employed, with an average market price of approximately INR 400 per kg and a typical usage rate of 1 kg per 2 metric tons of crushed cane. For manufacturing units with an average production capacity of 5–7 metric tons per day and approximately 300 operating days per year, the annual chemical expenditure for Golden jaggery batches ranges from INR 0.30 to 0.42 million. Due to unpredictable color correction outcomes, Golden jaggery production exhibits a quality rejection rate of approximately 10–12%, resulting in rejected batch losses of about INR 0.15–0.20 million annually.

Predicted cost savings with the proposed framework

By prioritizing naturally Golden sugarcane varieties, the proposed framework reduces dependence on chemical color correction. Field-aligned estimates indicate an 80–90% reduction in hydrosulfite usage, corresponding to annual savings of approximately INR 0.24–0.38 million. In addition, improved raw material selection reduces quality rejection rates by 8–10 percentage points, yielding further savings of INR 0.15–0.20 million per year. The total annual cost savings achieved through the proposed framework are therefore estimated to be in the range of INR 0.39–0.58 million per unit.

Revenue enhancement through premium pricing

Golden jaggery commands a price premium of approximately 40–60% over light and Dark brown variants, with market prices ranging from INR 80–100 per kg compared to INR 50–60 per kg for non-Golden jaggery. With 25–30% of production optimized for naturally Golden jaggery, manufacturers can achieve additional annual revenue of approximately INR 0.40–0.60 million.

Combined economic benefit

Considering both cost savings and additional revenue, the combined economic benefit of the proposed framework for a medium-scale jaggery production unit is estimated to be INR 0.79–1.18 million per year, corresponding to approximately USD 9,500–14,000. These estimates are based on field observations conducted during the 2023–2024 production seasons in the Erode region of Tamil Nadu, India.

Quality and process implications

Field observations further indicate that excessive hydrosulfite addition can result in whitish surface residues or shadow formation on jaggery blocks and powder, which are perceived as quality defects, reducing consumer acceptance and often necessitating reprocessing within the jaggery production cycle. By enabling chemical-free Golden jaggery production through variety-aware procurement, the proposed framework minimizes such defects while improving product consistency, shelf stability, and overall product quality.

Limitations of this study

This study has four primary limitations that constrain generalizability and require acknowledgment:

Geographic limitation and lack of external validation Model validation is restricted to the Erode region of Tamil Nadu, India. Although a statistically independent held-out test set (30%, n = 601) was used, both training and testing samples originate from the same geographic area. Consequently, the learned relationships between soil type, water source, and jaggery color primarily reflect local environmental and agro-climatic conditions. Jaggery-producing regions such as Maharashtra, Karnataka, and Uttar Pradesh exhibit distinct soil compositions, water quality profiles, and cultivation practices that may alter feature importance and model performance. External validation across multiple geographically distinct regions therefore, remains a critical limitation and a priority for future work. The term “spatial generalizability” in this study refers strictly to within-region generalization within the Erode ecosystem and should not be interpreted as cross-regional validation. Validation across diverse agroecological environments remains necessary to establish broader applicability.

Variety-environment interactions The four sugarcane varieties studied (Co 86032, Co 11015, Pi 1110, Co 86027) represent regional cultivars prevalent in Tamil Nadu. Genotype-environment interactions may differ in other regions, potentially reducing model accuracy when applied to different variety-climate combinations.

Potential confounding factors The observed agricultural parameter dominance (soil type: 41.44%, water type: 21.49%) may partially reflect unmeasured confounders such as regional microclimate variations, farming practice differences, or processing technique variations correlated with soil-water combinations. Multi-regional studies with balanced experimental designs are required to deconvolve these relationships and confirm causal attribution.

Controlled versus operational conditions Model performance was evaluated using standardized laboratory measurements for chemical parameters and systematic sampling protocols. Real-world deployment may encounter greater variability in data quality, measurement precision, and sample representativeness under commercial operational conditions. Field validation studies are necessary to confirm performance consistency across diverse production environments.

Ground truth validation protocol Jaggery color classification was initially performed by experienced manufacturers and production workers at each manufacturing unit based on their practical knowledge of commercial grading standards, and subsequently verified by the lead researcher during field visits under standardized natural daylight conditions (10 AM–2 PM). While this dual-assessment process involving experienced industry practitioners and the researcher provides stronger ground truth reliability than single-evaluator classification, formal inter-rater agreement statistics (e.g., Cohen’s Kappa for inter-rater reliability) were not computed between the manufacturer assessments and researcher verification. Additionally, portable colorimetric equipment capable of CIE L*a*b* measurement was not available during the field data collection period (2023–2024), and the decentralized nature of sample collection across 19 locations made centralized laboratory colorimetry impractical. The three color categories represent visually distinct commercial grades that experienced traders classify consistently in routine market transactions, which partially mitigates subjectivity concerns. However, boundary cases between adjacent categories (particularly Golden and Light brown) may be subject to evaluator variability. Future studies should incorporate formal inter-rater reliability assessment across multiple independent evaluators and instrumental color measurement using established colorimetric standards (e.g., CIE L*a*b* with portable handheld colorimeters such as the Konica Minolta CR-400) to establish quantitative ground truth benchmarks.

Seasonal variation in model performance Leave-One-Season-Out analysis revealed lower accuracy for Winter Season samples (91.03%) compared to Rain Season (100.00%) and Summer Season (96.85%), suggesting that seasonal environmental variation may affect the learned soil–water–color relationships. Deployment in regions with pronounced seasonal variability may benefit from season-specific model calibration.

Dataset accessibility and independent verification The dataset used in this study is maintained under restricted access and is not publicly available through an open repository. While access can be requested through the corresponding author, the absence of an openly accessible dataset or representative publicly available subset constitutes a significant limitation, as it restricts the possibility of independent verification and replication of the reported results by other researchers. Future work should explore the feasibility of releasing an anonymized representative subset to support open science principles while respecting the confidentiality agreements with participating production units.

Transfer learning protocol for regional deployment

Stage 1—Base model preservation: Retain the trained Random Forest architecture from Erode data as the foundation model. The learned decision boundaries capturing soil-chemistry-color relationships serve as prior knowledge applicable across regions.

Stage 2—Regional fine-tuning: Collect 200–500 labeled samples from the target region representing local variety-soil-water combinations. Implement domain adaptation using one of two approaches: (a) Weighted ensemble: Combine Erode model predictions with a region-specific model trained on local data using learned weights (typically 0.3–0.4 weight on local model, 0.6–0.7 on base model), or (b) Feature space adaptation: Use Erode model feature importances to initialize local model hyperparameters, constraining the search space for regional optimization.

Stage 3—Validation and deployment: Validate the adapted model on an independent regional test set (minimum 150 samples, stratified by local color distribution). Deploy only if regional accuracy exceeds 95%; otherwise, collect additional samples (target: 500–800 total) for full retraining using Erode data as regularization through weighted sample importance.

This transfer learning approach minimizes regional data requirements (500 samples vs. 2000 for full retraining from scratch) while maintaining prediction reliability. Preliminary simulation studies (using Erode data split by sub-regions: Anthiyur vs. Bhavani districts) suggest transfer learning can achieve 96–97% accuracy with only 30–40% of full training data requirements, representing substantial cost savings for multi-regional deployment.

Mechanistic validation studies

Future research should investigate the biochemical pathways linking soil minerals to polyphenol oxidase activity8 and Maillard reaction kinetics7. Controlled greenhouse experiments varying soil composition while maintaining genetic uniformity would confirm causal relationships versus correlational patterns identified in this observational study. Specific research questions include: (1) How do soil-derived iron and magnesium concentrations quantitatively affect enzymatic browning rates? (2) Does water calcium content modulate pH-dependent enzyme activity during juice extraction? (3) Are the observed terroir effects mediated through altered precursor concentrations or direct catalytic influences?

Temporal stability assessment

Long-term monitoring (3–5 years) is required to assess whether soil-water-color relationships remain stable across climate cycles or require periodic model recalibration due to environmental shifts. Climate change may alter precipitation patterns affecting water mineral content, soil nutrient availability, and temperature-dependent biochemical processes. Establishing automated monitoring systems that track prediction accuracy drift and trigger recalibration protocols will be essential for sustained industrial deployment.

Integration with IoT infrastructure

Future research should explore integration with Internet of Things (IoT) sensors for automated data collection during sugarcane procurement. Portable soil sensors (NPK, pH, moisture), handheld refractometers (Brix measurement), and mobile water quality meters could enable real-time prediction at procurement points, reducing manual data entry requirements and improving prediction accuracy through standardized measurements.

Standardized quality grading systems

Development of industry-wide standardized grading systems combining the identified key parameters (soil type and water type, contributing 62.93% predictive accuracy) with traditional industry metrics (Brix, Pol content) would enable broader ML adoption while protecting commercial interests through shared benchmark datasets among researchers.

Extension to other agricultural products

The methodological framework developed here–combining agricultural terroir parameters with chemical composition for pre-manufacturing quality prediction–is potentially applicable to other terroir-influenced agricultural products such as wine grapes, coffee, cocoa, and specialty honey. Future research should explore transferability of this approach to establish generalized principles for agricultural quality prediction.

Conclusion

This study established a robust machine learning framework for pre-manufacturing jaggery color classification using cultivation parameters measurable before processing, achieving a test accuracy of 98.3%. Three key findings emerged: (1) Soil type and water type collectively account for 62.93% of predictive importance, raising questions about prevailing assumptions that prioritize post-harvest juice chemistry in jaggery quality determination. These findings represent predictive associations identified through machine learning analysis and do not constitute causal proof of terroir dominance over chemical factors; (2) Rigorous validation through bootstrap resampling, learning curve analysis, and non-parametric statistical testing (Friedman test: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2 = 31.68,$$\end{document} p < 0.001) confirmed robust generalization without evidence of overfitting; (3) The framework exhibited high computational efficiency (0.017 s per prediction, 0.07 MB memory), supporting field-level deployment on standard mobile devices.

Generalizability constraint Model validation remains geographically constrained to the Erode region (Tamil Nadu, India). Broader applicability will require systematic multi-regional validation, with each target region necessitating pilot datasets of at least 500 samples to verify predictive performance and establish region-specific feature importance hierarchies under local environmental conditions.

Practical impact Within validated regions, this framework enables a transition from reactive quality control to proactive procurement optimization. Preliminary economic modeling suggests substantial benefits for medium-scale manufacturers through projected reductions in chemical interventions (approximately 80–85%), minimized quality rejection, and enhanced revenue potential from optimized Golden jaggery production. The framework addresses fundamental value chain inefficiencies while preserving natural product characteristics.

Scientific contribution This research establishes quantitative relationships between pre-harvest environmental conditions and final product quality, providing a methodological foundation for predictive agricultural quality assessment. The observed dominance of terroir effects redirects research focus from juice chemistry toward environmental sourcing strategies, with potential applicability to other terroir-influenced agricultural products including wine, coffee, cocoa, and specialty honey.

Future research priorities (1) Multi-regional validation across diverse soil-water-climate combinations using transfer learning protocols to minimize data requirements; (2) Mechanistic investigations of biochemical pathways linking soil minerals to enzymatic browning and Maillard reaction kinetics; (3) IoT sensor integration for automated, standardized data collection; (4) Extension of this predictive framework to other agricultural products exhibiting terroir effects; (5) Long-term monitoring (3–5 years) to assess temporal stability of soil-water-color relationships across climate cycles. The applicability of the proposed framework beyond the studied region requires validation across diverse agroecological systems.
