SP 2 DPO: An LLM-assisted Semantic Per-Pair DPO Generalization
Chaoyue He, Xin Zhou, Di Wang, Hong Xu, Wei Liu, Chunyan Miao

Abstract
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) controls the trade-off between fitting preference labels and staying close to a reference model using a single global temperature β \beta , implicitly treating all preference pairs as equally informative. Real-world preference corpora are heterogeneous: they mix high-signal, objective failures (e.g., safety, factuality, instruction violations) with low-signal or subjective distinctions (e.g., style), and also include label noise. We introduce SP 2 DPO ( S emantic P er- P air DPO ), a generalization that replaces the global temperature with an instance-specific schedule { β i } \{\beta_{i}\} pre-decided offline from structured semantic-gap annotations (category, magnitude, confidence) produced by teacher LLMs. We instantiate this procedure on the UltraFeedback preference corpus ( 59,960 pairs), enabling large-scale construction of an auditable { β i } \{\beta_{i}\} artifact, and incur zero training-time overhead: the inner-loop optimizer remains standard DPO with β ← β i \beta\leftarrow\beta_{i} per pair. We focus our empirical study on AlpacaEval 2.0 (AE2) , reporting both raw win rate and length-controlled win rate. Across four open-weight, instruction-tuned student backbones (4B–8B), SP 2 DPO is competitive with a tuned global- β \beta DPO baseline and improves AE2 length-controlled win rate on two of four backbones, while avoiding per-model β \beta sweeps. All code, annotations, and artifacts will be released.

Introduction: From Static
\beta
to Pre-Decided Semantic Enforcement

The art of progress is to preserve order amid change and to preserve change amid order.

– Alfred North Whitehead

Aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) can be formulated as a preference-learning problem
(
36
)
. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
38
)
has emerged as a stable, reward-model-free alternative to
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
by optimizing policies directly on paired comparisons. Yet, DPO’s standard formulation relies on a significant simplification: it employs a single, global temperature
\beta
to govern the trade-off between reward maximization and reference divergence for
every
preference pair. This implicitly assumes a uniform signal-to-noise ratio across the entire dataset. Real-world alignment corpora violate this assumption because they are heterogeneous mixtures of objective failures (e.g., safety violations, hallucinations), structural constraints (e.g., instruction-following), and subjective distinctions (e.g., prose style), each with varying degrees of noise and importance.

We argue that preference optimization must become
semantically instance-aware
: informative, high-confidence pairs regarding safety or factuality should shape the update more strongly than ambiguous or low-stakes stylistic preferences. To achieve this, we introduce
SP
2
DPO
(
S
emantic
P
er-
P
air
DPO
), a generalization that replaces the global hyperparameter
\beta
with instance-specific temperatures
\beta_{i}
, modulated by the semantic nature of each comparison (
Figure

1
).

SP
2
DPO instantiates a broader methodological paradigm we term
APO
(
Appendix

J
). Rather than complicating the “inner loop” of training with online dynamics, APO shifts alignment control to an offline, auditable
pre-decision
stage. We employ strong teacher LLMs to analyze the dataset
prior
to student training, producing a structured control artifact: a per-instance temperature schedule
{\beta_{i}}
attached to the data. Training then proceeds via standard DPO, simply substituting
\beta\leftarrow\beta_{i}
per pair. This strict separation of
offline semantic judgment
from
online gradient-based fitting
is central to our approach: it transforms temperature regulation from brittle hyperparameter tuning into a robust data annotation task, naturally supporting calibration and ensembling without incurring training-time overhead.

Specifically, we view alignment as a
process design
problem with explicit process variables
\lambda
(e.g., filtering rules, teacher rubrics, or per-example control signals such as
\{\beta_{i}\}
). Student training is formulated as a bilevel optimization:

\displaystyle\theta^{\star}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\theta}\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}(\theta;\lambda),

(1)

\displaystyle\lambda^{\star}

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\;\mathcal{E}\!\left(\theta^{\star}(\lambda)\right),

(2)

where
\theta
denotes the student model parameters,
\lambda\in\Lambda
denotes alignment process variables,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}(\theta;\lambda)
is the alignment training loss parameterized by
\lambda
, and
\mathcal{E}(\cdot)
is a held-out alignment evaluation metric used to select the optimal process configuration
\lambda^{\star}
. In SP
2
DPO,
\lambda
is instantiated as a pre-decided per-pair temperature schedule produced offline by teacher LLM annotation. We provide an expanded APO roadmap and taxonomy in
Appendix

J
.

To construct the schedule
\{\beta_{i}\}
, an annotator LLM evaluates each pair
(x_{i},y_{i}^{w},y_{i}^{l})
to output a structured
semantic gap
tuple containing the dominant gap category, magnitude, and confidence (
Appendix

B
). These signals are deterministically derived to a scalar
\beta_{i}
. We investigate four offline instantiation strategies that trade off computational cost for semantic robustness, ranging from single-pass annotation to multi-prompt, multi-annotator ensembles that isolate prompt sensitivity from model bias (
Section

4
).

A natural question is whether simple instance weighting (e.g.,
w_{i}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}
) is sufficient. We show theoretically that per-pair temperature is
not
equivalent to loss weighting. While weights linearly scale the gradient vector, changing
\beta_{i}
alters the optimization geometry by modifying the sigmoid’s curvature relative to the current margin. This allows SP
2
DPO to concentrate gradient mass near the decision boundary dynamically based on the pair’s didactic value (
Section

4.2
).

The key contributions of this work are:

1.

Paradigm: Alignment Process Optimization.
We formalize the separation of offline enforcement pre-decision from online training, moving control variables from hyperparameters to versioned, auditable data artifacts (
Appendix

J
).

2.

Theory: Semantic per-pair temperature formulation.
We generalize DPO to instance-wise temperatures and show per-pair temperature is strictly
not
equivalent to per-example loss weighting because it changes loss curvature and saturation (
Section

4.2
).

3.

Method: SP
2
DPO via robust teacher ensembling.

We propose an offline pipeline to derive
\beta_{i}
from structured semantic signals (category, magnitude, confidence), spanning single-pass annotation and multi-prompt/multi-annotator ensembling without training-time overhead (
Section

4
).

4.

Empirics (AE2-focused).
We evaluate SP
2
DPO on AlpacaEval 2.0 across four instruction-tuned student backbones and analyze what the current AE2 results imply about semantic enforcement versus global-
\beta
tuning and random per-pair control (
Section

5
).

2
Background and Foundations

We briefly review the standard DPO formulation and isolate the role of the temperature
\beta
, laying the groundwork for its generalization to a semantic per-pair schedule.

2.1
Setup and Standard DPO Objective

DPO trains a policy
\pi_{\theta}
on a dataset of preference pairs
\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\}
, where
y^{w}
is preferred over
y^{l}
given prompt
x
. Let
\pi_{\text{ref}}
denote a fixed reference policy, following
38
, we define the implicit log-ratio reward
r_{\theta}(x,y)
as:

r_{\theta}(x,y)=\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.

(3)

The standard DPO objective minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the preferred completion under a Bradley–Terry model
(
5
)
parameterized by following implicit rewards:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\left(r_{\theta}(x,y^{w})-r_{\theta}(x,y^{l})\right)\right)\right],

(4)

where
\sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}
is the logistic function and
\beta>0
is a scalar temperature hyperparameter.

2.2
Origin and Interpretation of
\beta

DPO is derived as the closed-form solution to a KL-regularized reward maximization objective. In this derivation (detailed in
Appendix

D
),
\beta
acts as the temperature coefficient related to the KL regularization constraint. Practically,
\beta
controls how aggressively
\pi_{\theta}
is pushed away from
\pi_{\text{ref}}
to satisfy preference labels. Larger values of
\beta
sharpen the decision boundary, enforcing stronger updates based on the sign of the preference, while smaller values yield softer updates and tighter anchoring to the reference distribution.

Standard DPO applies a single, global
\beta
across the entire dataset. This implicitly assumes that all pairwise comparisons—whether they represent critical safety violations or minor stylistic preferences—are equally informative and possess uniform signal-to-noise ratios. SP
2
DPO challenges this assumption by treating
\beta
as an instance-specific process variable determined via offline semantic analysis.

3
Related Work

Preference-based alignment is commonly framed as RLHF, where a reward model is learned from comparisons and a KL-regularized policy is optimized against a reference policy
(
6
;
41
;
36
)
. DPO
(
38
)
simplifies this pipeline by optimizing the policy directly under a Bradley–Terry likelihood, with a global temperature
\beta
controlling the strength of deviation from the reference.

A growing line of work modifies DPO-style objectives to improve stability or sample efficiency, including IPO
(
1
)
and SimPO
(
28
)
, as well as online adaptive schemes such as instance-adaptive margins (AlphaDPO;
45
) and temperature calibration (e.g.,
\beta
-DPO;
46
). In contrast, SP
2
DPO pre-decides an
offline
, example-level temperature schedule
\{\beta_{i}\}
via LLM-based semantic auditing, modulating loss curvature at the pair level without altering the inner-loop optimizer.

Our approach is also related to curriculum learning and per-example reweighting, which emphasize how examples are ordered or emphasized during training
(
4
;
16
;
39
)
, and to using LLMs as annotators/judges in alignment pipelines
(
3
;
17
;
8
)
. An extended discussion is provided in
Appendix

I
.

4
SP
2
DPO: Semantic Per-Pair DPO

This section presents SP
2
DPO, a semantic per-pair reformulation of DPO that instantiates the APO paradigm by decoupling enforcement strength from the training loop. Rather than tuning a global temperature during optimization, SP
2
DPO assigns a fixed, per-example temperature
\beta_{i}\in[\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max}]
through a one-time, offline annotation stage, where a teacher LLM estimates the semantic preference gap for each
(x_{i},y_{i}^{w},y_{i}^{l})
pair. These semantic scores are deterministically mapped to temperatures, yielding auditable and reproducible coefficients attached to each training instance. Training then proceeds identically to standard DPO using the same optimizer, learning rate schedules, and compute budget, except that the global
\beta
is replaced by pre-decided per-pair values
\beta_{i}
, allowing semantic judgments to modulate the loss curvature without introducing additional training-time complexity. The full procedure, including robust ensembling strategies for semantic estimation, is in
Algorithm

2
.

4.1
Overview

Our method consists of four main steps (
Section

D.2
):

1.

Annotate semantic gap (offline):
For each pair
(x,y^{w},y^{l})
, teacher LLMs output a structured tuple representing the gap category, magnitude, and confidence
(S_{G}^{ct},S_{G}^{m},S_{G}^{cf})
(
Appendix

B
).

2.

Stabilize the signal:
We reduce prompt sensitivity via multi-prompt self-ensembling within each annotator (
Section

4.3.3
) and reduce teacher bias by ensembling across multiple annotator backbones (
Section

4.3.4
).

3.

Derive per-pair temperature:
We compute a scalar
\beta_{i}\in[\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max}]
deterministically from the effective semantic gap
S_{G}^{\mathit{eff}}
(
Section

4.3.2
).

4.

Train with per-pair temperatures:
Training proceeds via standard DPO updates, substituting
\beta\leftarrow\beta_{i}
per pair, incurring no online computational overhead (
Algorithm

2
).

We explore four instantiations of this pipeline based on the number of annotator backbones (
J
) and prompt variants (

):
(i)
single annotator single prompt (
J{=}1,K{=}1
)
,
(ii)
single annotator multiple prompts (
J{=}1,K{>}1
)
,
(iii)
multiple annotators single prompt (
J{>}1,K{=}1
)
,
and (iv)
multiple annotators and multiple prompts (

. All four strategies produce a fixed schedule

that is used in the general SP

4.2
Theoretical Re-framing: DPO as Semantically-Unaware Optimization

We generalize DPO by replacing the global temperature
\beta
with an
instance-wise temperature
\beta_{i}
attached to each preference pair.
Given an augmented dataset
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}^{w},y_{i}^{l},\beta_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
we minimize the empirical objective

\mathcal{L}_{SP\textsuperscript{2}DPO{}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\sigma\left(\beta_{i}\left(r_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i}^{w})-r_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i}^{l})\right)\right),

(5)

which can equivalently be written as an expectation under uniform sampling from
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
. Standard DPO is recovered when
\beta_{i}\equiv\beta_{\text{static}}
for all preference pairs. In contrast, SP
2
DPO operationalizes the generalized objective by leveraging strong LLM annotators to
pre-decide
a non-uniform, instance-aware schedule
\{\beta_{i}\}
in an offline manner.

4.2.1
SP
2
DPO vs. Per-Example Loss Weighting

A key theoretical distinction is that assigning a per-pair temperature
\beta_{i}
is
not

equivalent to multiplying the DPO loss by a scalar weight
w_{i}
(i.e.,
w_{i}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}
).
To formalize this, consider the per-pair objective

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}(\beta_{i})

\displaystyle=-\log\sigma\!\big(\beta_{i}\Delta\hat{r}_{i}\big),

(6)

\displaystyle\Delta\hat{r}_{i}

\displaystyle=r_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i}^{w})-r_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i}^{l}),

(7)

where
\Delta\hat{r}_{i}
is the implicit margin. Differentiating yields

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{i}=-\underbrace{\sigma(-\beta_{i}\Delta\hat{r}_{i})}_{\text{Saturation}}\cdot\underbrace{\beta_{i}}_{\text{Scale}}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}(\Delta\hat{r}_{i}),

(8)

Thus,
\beta_{i}
influences in two distinct ways:

(i) Scale:
it directly scales the gradient magnitude via the multiplicative factor
\beta_{i}
;

(ii) Curvature/Saturation:
it
modifies the curvature of the sigmoid
through

\sigma(-\beta_{i}\Delta\hat{r}_{i})
.
In particular, larger
\beta_{i}
makes the sigmoid steeper, concentrating gradient mass around small margins (and saturating more aggressively for large margins).

In contrast, per-example loss weighting keeps the loss geometry fixed.
For a weighted objective using a
global
temperature
\bar{\beta}
,

\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\mathrm{LW}}=w_{i}\,\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bar{\beta})
, we have

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\mathrm{LW}}=-w_{i}\,\sigma(-\bar{\beta}\Delta\hat{r}_{i})\,\bar{\beta}\,\nabla_{\theta}(\Delta\hat{r}_{i}),

(9)

which can only rescale gradients via
w_{i}
and cannot reproduce the curvature change induced by placing
\beta_{i}
inside the sigmoid.

Proposition 1

(Strict non-equivalence)
.

Assume the dataset contains at least three examples whose margins
\Delta\hat{r}_{i}(\theta)
attain
three distinct, nonzero values on a set of parameters
\theta
with nonzero measure. Then there exists no choice of
fixed
weights
\{w_{i}\}
and a global temperature
\bar{\beta}

such that

\nabla_{\theta}\sum_{i}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\beta_{i})\;\equiv\;\nabla_{\theta}\sum_{i}w_{i}\,\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bar{\beta})\quad\forall\,\theta

(10)

unless
\beta_{i}
is constant across
i
.

Proof.

If the gradients are identical for all
\theta
, then for each
i
the scalar coefficient on

\nabla_{\theta}(\Delta\hat{r}_{i})
must match:

\beta_{i}\,\sigma(-\beta_{i}x)\;=\;w_{i}\,\bar{\beta}\,\sigma(-\bar{\beta}x)\quad\forall\,x=\Delta\hat{r}_{i}(\theta).

(11)

Rearranging implies

w_{i}\;=\;\frac{\beta_{i}\,\sigma(-\beta_{i}x)}{\bar{\beta}\,\sigma(-\bar{\beta}x)}.

(12)

However, the right-hand side depends on
x
unless
\beta_{i}=\bar{\beta}
, so a
fixed

w_{i}

cannot satisfy the equality simultaneously at multiple distinct nonzero margin values.
With three distinct nonzero values, this dependence cannot be eliminated, implying that equality can
hold for all
\theta
only when
\beta_{i}
is constant across
i
.
We provide the full proof and a geometric discussion in
Section

D.2
.

4.2.2
SP
2
DPO vs. Adaptive-Margin Methods

SP
2
DPO is fundamentally different from adaptive-margin approaches such as AlphaDPO
(
45
)
. Adaptive-margin methods introduce instance-wise target margins by reparameterizing an implicit reference distribution using the current policy and reference likelihoods, causing the effective margin for a given preference pair to evolve over the course of training. Conceptually, such methods ask
what margin should be enforced for this pair at the current stage of training?
In contrast, SP
2
DPO fixes the target margin and modulates the optimization strength via a static, instance-specific temperature
\beta_{i}
, computed
offline
from semantic analysis and independent of the student model’s training state. From this perspective, SP
2
DPO asks
how informative a preference pair is semantically, and how strongly it should be enforced whenever it appears during training
. These two paradigms—dynamic target-margin adaptation and static semantic temperature scheduling—are conceptually orthogonal and could be combined in future work.

4.3
Eliciting Semantic Temperatures via Robust Ensembling

The success of SP
2
DPO depends on eliciting a robust distribution of per-pair temperatures
\{\beta_{i}\}
from teacher LLMs. We treat the annotator LLM as an approximation of a function
\beta_{i}=F_{\text{LLM}}(x_{i},y_{i}^{w},y_{i}^{l})
that maps a preference pair to a scalar temperature based on deep semantic understanding. For completeness and reproducibility,
Appendix

C
consolidates the exact ensembling estimator used in this work, provides a small numeric illustration of its robustness, and documents additional plug-in ensemble operators that we leave to future work.

4.3.1
Structured semantic gap rubric (category, magnitude, confidence)

For each preference triplet
(x_{i},y_{i}^{w},y_{i}^{l})
, teacher annotators output a structured
semantic gap

S_{G}^{i}(x_{i},y_{i}^{w},y_{i}^{l})\triangleq\big(S_{G}^{ct,i}(x_{i},y_{i}^{w},y_{i}^{l}),\;S_{G}^{m,i}(x_{i},y_{i}^{w},y_{i}^{l}),\;S_{G}^{cf,i}(x_{i},y_{i}^{w},y_{i}^{l})\big),

(13)

where
S_{G}^{ct,i}\in S_{G}^{D}
is the dominant semantic category,
S_{G}^{m,i}\in[0,1]
is the gap magnitude, and
S_{G}^{cf,i}\in[0,1]
is the annotator confidence that the gap is real and that the dataset preference direction is objectively justified. Here, we use a fixed category dimension set

S_{G}^{D}=\{\text{Safety},\text{Factuality},\text{Instruction},\text{Reasoning},\text{Helpfulness},\text{Style}\},

(14)

whose semantics are summarized in
Table

1
, with strict priority

\textsc{Safety}\succ\textsc{Factuality}\succ\textsc{Instruction}\succ\textsc{Reasoning}\succ\textsc{Helpfulness}\succ\textsc{Style}
.
If multiple differences exist, annotators must select exactly one dominant category following this order.

Category

Dominant semantic gap meaning

Safety

Harmful, illegal, or unsafe behavior versus safe refusal or mitigation.

Factuality

Incorrect or hallucinated claims versus correct grounded content.

Instruction

Violations of explicit user constraints versus correct constraint following.

Reasoning

Invalid or incoherent reasoning versus sound justification and logic.

Helpfulness

Low utility or clarity versus more actionable and complete assistance.

Style

Tone, verbosity, or polish differences with similar underlying content.

S_{G}^{ct}

throughout all experiments.
These correspond to three rubric-driven prompt variants:

(v1) conservative

(v2) utility-focused

(iii) semantic magnitude

4.3.2
Deriving the Temperature

We define the
effective semantic gap
for a single annotation call as
S_{G}^{\mathit{eff,i}}=S_{G}^{m,i}\cdot S_{G}^{cf,i}
. We then deterministically map this gap to a temperature
\beta_{i}
using a linear scaling within pre-defined bounds
[\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max}]
:

\beta_{i}=\beta_{\min}+(\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min})\cdot\mathrm{clip}(S_{G}^{\mathit{eff,i}},0,1).

(15)

Unless otherwise stated, we use a conservative stability envelope
(\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max})=(0.03,0.3)
, providing a
10\times
dynamic range while avoiding extreme temperatures that could allow individual preference pairs to dominate training. The category variable
S_{G}^{ct}
is used exclusively for auditing, stratified analysis, and interpretability, and does not directly enter the computation of
\beta_{i}
. The rationale for selecting the temperature bounds is detailed in
Section

A.6
.

4.3.3
Robust Multi-Prompt Self-Ensembling

To reduce sensitivity to prompt phrasing, we adopt a
multi-prompt self-ensembling
strategy. For each preference pair
i
and annotator backbone
j
, we query the annotator using
K
prompt variants, yielding a set of
effective semantic gap
estimates
\{S_{G}^{\mathit{eff},ijk}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. These estimates are aggregated into a robust, per-annotator effective semantic gap via the median:

S_{G}^{\mathit{eff},ij}\;\triangleq\;\mathrm{median}_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}}\,S_{G}^{\mathit{eff},ijk}.

(16)

The resulting effective semantic gap
S_{G}^{\mathit{eff},ij}
is then used to compute the annotator-specific temperature
\beta_{ij}
according to
Equation

15
. The categorical field is aggregated analogously using majority voting, with a predefined priority rule applied to break ties.

4.3.4
Robust Multi-Annotator Ensembling

To mitigate bias from any single teacher model, we ensemble across
J
diverse LLM annotator backbones. For each preference pair
i
, each annotator
j
is queried with a single prompt, producing an annotator-specific temperature
\beta_{ij}
. These temperatures are then aggregated via simple averaging:

\beta_{i}\;\triangleq\;\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\beta_{ij}.

(17)

This multi-annotator design isolates annotator-specific bias while holding the prompt fixed, yielding a more stable and auditable per-pair temperature schedule.

4.3.5
Joint Multi-Annotator Multi-Prompt Ensembling

We combine multi-prompt self-ensembling and multi-annotator ensembling into a unified estimator. For each preference pair
i
, annotator
j
, and prompt variant
k
, the annotator produces an
effective semantic gap
estimate
S_{G}^{\mathit{eff},ijk}
. The final per-pair temperature is computed as

\beta_{i}\;\triangleq\;\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}f_{\beta}\!\left(\mathrm{median}_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}}\,S_{G}^{\mathit{eff},ijk}\right),

(18)

where
f_{\beta}(\cdot)
denotes the deterministic gap-to-temperature mapping defined in
Equation

15
. This hierarchical aggregation—median over prompts followed by mean over annotators—suppresses both prompt-induced variance and annotator-specific bias, yielding a stable and auditable per-pair temperature schedule.

6
Scope, Limitations, and Future Directions

6.1
Evaluation Scope and Limitations

AE2-centric evaluation.

Our empirical evaluation focuses on AlpacaEval 2.0 (AE2), which targets general instruction-following and employs length-controlled pairwise judging. This makes AE2 particularly suitable for analyzing semantic enforcement under known judge-length confounds. However, AE2 does not directly evaluate strict constraint satisfaction, tool-use behavior, multi-turn consistency, or domain-specific factual accuracy and calibration. As a result, the reported findings characterize behavior on general instruction-following tasks rather than comprehensive alignment performance across all settings.

Residual judge artifacts.

While length control mitigates a major evaluation confound, AE2 remains a judge-based benchmark and may still reflect preferences correlated with phrasing, style, or presentation. Such artifacts can lead to over- or under-estimation of substantive improvements. Complementary evaluation strategies—such as targeted human assessment on subsets or diversified judge panels—are necessary to validate that observed gains correspond to underlying quality changes.

Teacher quality and signal fidelity.

SP
2
DPO relies on teacher LLMs to generate structured semantic-gap annotations. These teachers may exhibit systematic biases, imperfect calibration, or version drift across providers and time. Multi-prompt and multi-annotator aggregation reduces variance and improves robustness, but cannot guarantee correctness of the semantic signals. This motivates pinning model identifiers, logging raw call-level outputs, and releasing aggregation rules to support auditability and replication.

Annotation cost and design choices.

The approach introduces an upfront offline annotation cost and requires explicit design choices for mapping semantic signals to per-pair enforcement strengths
\beta_{i}
(e.g., bounds
[\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max}]
and the combination of magnitude and confidence). While these choices are transparent and auditable, suboptimal mappings may under-enforce critical errors or over-enforce ambiguous distinctions. Disagreement-aware damping and lightweight human-audit anchors are promising mechanisms to reduce sensitivity to these choices.

Static schedules and adaptivity.

The per-pair enforcement schedule
\{\beta_{i}\}
is fixed prior to training. This improves auditability and cleanly decouples semantic judgment from training dynamics, but prevents adaptation to evolving model competence during optimization. Dynamic or hybrid schemes that combine an offline semantic prior with online adaptation could improve sample efficiency, at the cost of relaxing the strict offline/online separation emphasized by Alignment Process Optimization.

Safety coverage and rare failures.

The semantic taxonomy includes an explicit safety category and allows stronger enforcement for clearly safety-relevant pairs. However, AE2 is not a comprehensive safety benchmark, and rare or adversarial failure modes are not well captured. Dedicated safety evaluations and stress tests are required to substantiate safety-related claims beyond the scope of this work.

6.2
Future Directions

Expert-domain alignment.

We plan to extend semantic enforcement to expert domains such as Legal and ESG. In high-stakes settings, enforcement strengths
\beta_{i}
can be derived from domain-specific notions of severity and material impact (e.g., prioritizing regulatory accuracy over stylistic fluency). This naturally supports retrieval-aware alignment, where update strength depends on the faithfulness and validity of cited evidence.

Richer APO control artifacts.

Beyond per-pair schedules, future work will explore richer offline APO control mechanisms, including category-conditioned enforcement envelopes (e.g., higher
\beta_{\max}
for Safety or Factuality than Style), slice-aware sampling or replay, and learned mappings from
(S_{G}^{ct},S_{G}^{m},S_{G}^{cf})
to enforcement strength. For robustness,
Appendix

C
documents several drop-in ensembling primitives beyond mean and median, including disagreement-aware damping, trimmed or M-estimator aggregation, and lightweight bias-decomposition models (
Section

C.5.5
). Systematically benchmarking these operators and learning reliability weights remains promising.

Human evaluation and judge diversity.

To assess external validity beyond automated metrics, future work will include human evaluation to correlate the proposed semantic-gap signals with human judgments. We also plan to diversify judge panels—further decoupling them from annotator models—to characterize sensitivity to evaluation artifacts and robustness to judge choice.

Online semantic curricula.

A natural extension is moving from static offline schedules to online or hybrid semantic curricula, where
\beta_{i}
adapts to student competence during training. One promising direction treats the offline semantic score as a prior modulated in real time by training dynamics (e.g., model confidence), enabling responsive, compute-efficient adaptation while preserving semantic structure.

7
Conclusion

We presented SP
2
DPO, a semantic, instance-aware generalization of Direct Preference Optimization that replaces the single global temperature
\beta
with a fixed per-pair schedule
\{\beta_{i}\}
derived
offline
from structured semantic-gap annotations. By delegating enforcement decisions to an auditable pre-decision stage—where teacher LLMs estimate gap category, magnitude, and confidence and these signals are deterministically mapped into a conservative envelope
[\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max}]
—SP
2
DPO preserves the standard DPO training loop with
\beta\leftarrow\beta_{i}
per pair and incurs zero training-time overhead. We further showed that per-pair temperatures provide a control mechanism distinct from loss reweighting by altering the curvature and saturation behavior of the logistic objective. Empirically, using a large-scale instantiation on UltraFeedback (59,960 pairs) and AlpacaEval 2.0 as a length-controlled evaluation target, semantic schedules are competitive with tuned global-
\beta
DPO across four open-weight instruction-tuned backbones; notably, strong semantic configurations match or improve AE2 length-controlled win rate on two backbones without per-backbone
\beta
sweeps, while a robust multi-prompt/multi-annotator default (JMAMP) delivers stable performance without changes to training infrastructure. Overall, these results demonstrate that meaningful alignment control can be shifted from fragile hyperparameter tuning into auditable, reusable data artifacts.

Impact Statement

This paper introduces
SP
2
DPO
(
S
emantic
P
er-
P
air
DPO
), a data-centric generalization of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) that replaces a single global temperature
\beta
with a
fixed per-pair
schedule
\{\beta_{i}\}
. Each
\beta_{i}
is
pre-decided offline
from structured semantic-gap annotations (dominant category, magnitude, confidence) produced by an ensemble of teacher LLMs, and then injected into an otherwise unchanged DPO training loop. In our study, the method is instantiated on the public UltraFeedback preference corpus and evaluated primarily on AlpacaEval 2.0’s length-controlled protocol; we do not claim comprehensive alignment or safety coverage beyond this evaluation scope.

Potential positive impacts.

If used responsibly, semantic per-pair enforcement could improve the
auditability
and
robustness
of preference optimization by turning a brittle training hyperparameter (global
\beta
tuning) into a versioned, inspectable data artifact. Practically, this can reduce the need for per-model
\beta
sweeps, enable targeted diagnostics on which preference pairs drive updates, and support more reproducible alignment research when prompts, aggregation rules, and the resulting
\{\beta_{i}\}
schedules are released. By penalizing verbosity-as-a-signal at the annotation stage and emphasizing length-controlled evaluation, the approach may also help reduce incentives to optimize toward superficial correlates (e.g., longer answers) rather than substantive improvements.

Potential negative impacts and misuse risks.

The same mechanism that improves sample-efficiency and steering precision can be
dual-use
: an actor who controls the preference data and/or the
\{\beta_{i}\}
construction pipeline could rapidly tune models toward harmful objectives (e.g., manipulation, disinformation, or other undesirable behaviors), potentially lowering the barrier to harmful fine-tuning.
Because
\beta_{i}
is derived from LLM annotators, systematic biases or normative assumptions in annotators, prompt templates, or the chosen semantic taxonomy can be
inherited and amplified
by the student model (e.g., unequal enforcement across topics, dialects, or cultural styles). The offline annotation stage can also introduce
privacy and confidentiality risks
if sensitive prompts/responses are sent to third-party APIs. Finally, the extra annotation compute can increase
cost and environmental footprint
, and reliance on proprietary annotators may exacerbate
access disparities
between groups with and without such resources.

Mitigations and best practices.

We recommend (i) using conservative temperature bounds (as in our envelope) to prevent extreme enforcement, (ii) employing multi-prompt and multi-annotator ensembling and reporting disagreement as an uncertainty signal, (iii) releasing prompts, aggregation rules, and summary statistics (e.g.,
\beta
distributions by semantic category and sensitive slices) to support independent auditing, and (iv) keeping teacher/annotator models decoupled from benchmark judges to reduce evaluation leakage. For sensitive or regulated data, practitioners should avoid sending content to external services; a safer alternative is to use self-hosted annotators or distill
\beta(x,y^{w},y^{l})
into a local predictor trained only on non-sensitive data. Before deploying models fine-tuned with SP
2
DPO in real applications—especially high-stakes domains—additional domain-specific evaluation, safety testing, and monitoring are necessary beyond the AE2-focused scope of this paper.

Uncertainties and future societal consequences.

The net societal impact depends on how the temperature policy is generated, audited, and governed. LLM annotators and benchmark pipelines can drift over time, and improvements on judge-based benchmarks may not translate uniformly to reduced real-world harms. Continued work on human-audit anchors, slice-based bias evaluation, and dedicated safety/factuality benchmarks will be important to ensure that stronger preference optimization yields reliably beneficial behavior in deployment.

A.7
Role of the
\mathrm{clip}(\cdot)
Operator

In
Equation

26
, the clip operator is defined for a scalar
z
as:

\mathrm{clip}(z,0,1)=\min\big(\max(z,0),1\big).

(27)

In SP
2
DPO, clipping is applied to the
effective semantic gap

S_{G}^{\mathit{eff,i}}
and serves several critical roles:

Numerical Robustness.

Although the constituent terms
S_{G}^{m,i}
and
S_{G}^{cf,i}
are theoretically bounded within
[0,1]
, practical implementation details—such as robust aggregation statistics or prompt ensemble averaging—can introduce minor numerical deviations. Clipping acts as a sanitizer, guaranteeing that the control signal strictly adheres to the domain
[0,1]
before scaling.

Strict Enforcement Bounds.

The operator enforces the hard constraints of the stability envelope:

\beta_{\min}\leq\beta_{i}(x_{i},y_{i}^{w},y_{i}^{l})\leq\beta_{\max}

(28)

for all pairs. This prevents runaway enforcement values where
S_{G}^{\mathit{eff,i}}>1
would otherwise imply
\beta_{i}>\beta_{\max}
, ensuring the training dynamics remain predictable.

Semantic Saturation.

Clipping encodes a “diminishing returns” logic: once a semantic gap is identified as both maximally large and maximally reliable (
S_{G}^{\mathit{eff,i}}\geq 1
), further increases in the raw signal do not yield stronger enforcement. This reflects the intuition that once a preference is “certain,” increasing the penalty further yields marginal gains while risking instability.

Design Choice: Linearity and Transparency.

We purposefully utilize a linear clip rather than a nonlinear activation (e.g., sigmoid). A linear mapping preserves the direct interpretability of the semantic gap signals: a 10% increase in confidence yields a proportional increase in enforcement strength (within the active range). This avoids introducing additional hyperparameters (e.g., temperature/steepness of a sigmoid) that would require tuning and could obscure the relationship between annotation and training.

We intentionally avoid asking annotators to output

C.4
Disagreement diagnostics and uncertainty-aware guardrails

A major benefit of ensembling is that it exposes
uncertainty
. We recommend logging prompt-level and annotator-level disagreement as diagnostics (and potential future control signals).

Prompt disagreement within an annotator.

We use a robust dispersion statistic:

\Delta^{\text{prompt}}_{ij}\;\triangleq\;\mathrm{median}_{k}\left|S_{G}^{\mathit{eff},ijk}-\mathrm{median}_{k^{\prime}}S_{G}^{\mathit{eff},ijk^{\prime}}\right|.

(36)

Large
\Delta^{\text{prompt}}_{ij}
indicates prompt sensitivity for pair
i
under annotator
j

Annotator disagreement.

After prompt aggregation, we quantify cross-annotator uncertainty via the dispersion of per-annotator enforcement strengths. In particular, annotator disagreement for pair
i
can be measured as either the standard deviation

u^{\mathrm{anno}}_{i}\;\triangleq\;\mathrm{Std}_{j}\big(\beta_{ij}\big)

(37)

or

u^{\mathrm{anno}}_{i}\;\triangleq\;\mathrm{median}_{j}\left|\beta_{ij}-\mathrm{median}_{j^{\prime}}\beta_{ij^{\prime}}\right|.

(38)

Optional damping.

A conservative extension is to down-modulate enforcement when disagreement is high:

S_{G}^{\mathit{eff},ij}\;\leftarrow\;S_{G}^{\mathit{eff},ij}\cdot h\!\left(\Delta^{\text{prompt}}_{ij}\right),

(39)

h(u)=\exp(-\lambda u)\ \ \text{or}\ \ h(u)=\frac{1}{1+\lambda u},

(40)

with

. We include this as a
drop-in guardrail
for noisy regimes, but we do not exhaustively evaluate it here; see
Section

6.2
for discussion.

Appendix D
Deriving DPO from KL-Regularized RLHF

This section derives the DPO objective from the standard KL-regularized RLHF formulation, making explicit where the temperature/regularization parameter
\beta
comes from and how it controls deviation from the reference policy.

The derivation follows three standard ingredients:

1.

Solve the KL-regularized reward maximization problem in RLHF to obtain the closed-form optimal policy.

2.

Specify a Bradley–Terry (BT) model that links pairwise preference probabilities to reward differences.

3.

Substitute the RLHF log-ratio reward expression into the BT likelihood to recover the DPO loss.

D.1
Step-by-step derivation

Step 1: KL-regularized RLHF and the optimal policy.

In RLHF, we seek a policy
\pi
that maximizes expected reward under a reward model
r(x,y)
, while
remaining close to a reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)
. This is commonly written as the
KL-regularized objective

\pi_{r}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\pi}\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{x}}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\!\big[r(x,y)\big]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\Big]

(45)

where
\beta
is the coefficient on the KL penalty (equivalently, a temperature/inverse-temperature parameter controlling how strongly the learned policy is anchored to
\pi_{\text{ref}}
).
4
4

4

Here
\mathcal{D}_{x}

denotes the prompt distribution induced by the preference dataset.

This objective has a closed-form solution:

\pi_{r}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)

(46)

with the partition function

Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)

(47)

ensuring normalization.

Rearranging
Equation

46
yields an equivalent expression for the reward (up to an additive, prompt-dependent constant):

r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x).

(48)

This identity is the key bridge to DPO: it expresses reward in terms of a
\beta
-scaled log-probability ratio between the optimal policy and the reference, plus an
x
-only offset.

Step 2: The Bradley–Terry preference model.

The BT model connects latent rewards to observed pairwise preferences. For a prompt
x
and a pair
of completions
(y^{w},y^{l})
, it posits

\begin{split}\Pr(y^{w}\succ y^{l}\mid x)&=\sigma\!\big(r(x,y^{w})-r(x,y^{l})\big)\\
&=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\big(-(r(x,y^{w})-r(x,y^{l}))\big)}\end{split}

(49)

which defines a likelihood over a preference dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\}
.

Step 3: Substitution and the DPO likelihood.

Substitute
Equation

48
into the reward difference
r(x,y^{w})-r(x,y^{l})
. The

\beta\log Z(x)
term cancels because it is shared across completions for the same prompt:

\begin{split}r(x,y^{w})-r(x,y^{l})&=\bigg(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y^{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{w}\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\bigg)\\
&\quad-\bigg(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y^{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{l}\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\bigg)\\
&=\beta\bigg(\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y^{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{w}\mid x)}-\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y^{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{l}\mid x)}\bigg)\end{split}

(50)

Plugging
Equation

50
into
Equation

49
gives

\begin{split}\Pr(y^{w}\succ y^{l}\mid x)&=\sigma\!\bigg(\beta\bigg[\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y^{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{w}\mid x)}\\
&\quad-\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y^{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{l}\mid x)}\bigg]\bigg)\end{split}

(51)

DPO replaces the unknown optimal policy
\pi_{r}
with a parameterized policy
\pi_{\theta}
and directly
maximizes the BT likelihood implied by
Equation

51
. Defining the log-ratio
margin

\Delta_{\theta}(x,y^{w},y^{l})\;\triangleq\;\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{w}\mid x)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{l}\mid x)},

(52)

the resulting DPO loss is the negative log-likelihood:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\!\big(\beta\,\Delta_{\theta}(x,y^{w},y^{l})\big)\Big]

(53)

Equivalently, using
\log\sigma(u)=-\log(1+\exp(-u))
,
Equation

53
can be written in the standard logistic-loss form

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\!\big(1+\exp(-\beta\,\Delta_{\theta}(x,y^{w},y^{l}))\big)\Big]

(54)

This completes the derivation and highlights the operational role of
\beta
inherited from the
KL-regularized RLHF objective: it scales the log-ratio margin and thereby controls how strongly the
policy is encouraged to move away from
\pi_{\text{ref}}
to satisfy preferences. SP
2
DPO generalizes
this view by arguing that this regularization strength should not be static, but should vary across
pairs via
\beta_{i}
, based on the semantic character and reliability of each preference signal.

D.2
Non-Equivalence, Geometry, and Generalization

Set-up.

Let
\Delta\hat{r}_{i}:=r_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i}^{w})-r_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i}^{l})
and
z_{i}(\theta):=\beta_{i}\Delta\hat{r}_{i}
. We compare logistic DPO losses

\mathcal{L}_{\beta_{i}}(\theta):=-\log\sigma(\beta_{i}\Delta\hat{r}_{i})

and loss-weighted DPO

\mathcal{L}^{\text{LW}}_{w_{i}}(\theta):=-w_{i}\log\sigma(\bar{\beta}\Delta\hat{r}_{i})

with a global
\bar{\beta}>0
and weights
w_{i}>0
that do not depend on
\theta
.

Proposition 2

(Strict non-equivalence)
.

Let
\Theta
be the parameter space with measure
\mu
. Define
S\subset\Theta
as the set where indices
i,j,k
exist such that:

\displaystyle S=\Big\{\theta\in\Theta\;\Big|

\displaystyle\{\Delta\hat{r}_{i},\Delta\hat{r}_{j},\Delta\hat{r}_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

\displaystyle\land|\{\Delta\hat{r}_{i},\Delta\hat{r}_{j},\Delta\hat{r}_{k}\}|=3\Big\}

If
\mu(S)>0
, then:

\Big(\exists\{w_{i}\}_{i},\bar{\beta}:\forall\theta\in\Theta,\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\beta_{i}}(\theta)\equiv\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\text{LW}}_{w_{i}}(\theta)\Big)\\
\implies(\exists c\in\mathbb{R},\forall i:\beta_{i}=c)

Proof.

We analyze the gradients in the following steps:

1.

Gradient Definitions:
For a single pair, the gradients are:

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\beta_{i}}=-\sigma(-z_{i})\beta_{i}\nabla_{\theta}\Delta\hat{r}_{i}

while,

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\text{LW}}_{w_{i}}=-w_{i}\sigma(-\bar{\beta}\Delta\hat{r}_{i})\bar{\beta}\nabla_{\theta}\Delta\hat{r}_{i}

2.

Requirement for Equality:
Equality for all
\theta
requires the following identity to hold as a function of
\Delta\hat{r}_{i}
:

\sigma(-\beta_{i}\Delta\hat{r}_{i})\beta_{i}\equiv w_{i}\sigma(-\bar{\beta}\Delta\hat{r}_{i})\bar{\beta}

3.

Conclusion:
Because
\sigma(-\alpha x)\alpha
is not an affine rescaling of
\sigma(-\bar{\alpha}x)\bar{\alpha}
over three or more distinct
x
values (unless
\alpha=\bar{\alpha}
), there is no
\theta
-independent
w_{i}
that matches the curvature modulation induced by
\beta_{i}
unless all
\beta_{i}
are equal.

∎

Optimization geometry.

Define
g_{i}(\theta):=\sigma(-z_{i})\beta_{i}
so that
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}=-\sum_{i}g_{i}(\theta)\nabla_{\theta}\Delta\hat{r}_{i}
.
Then

\small\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial\Delta\hat{r}_{i}}=-\beta_{i}^{2}\sigma(z_{i})\sigma(-z_{i})

(55)

\frac{\partial^{2}g_{i}}{\partial(\Delta\hat{r}_{i})^{2}}=\beta_{i}^{3}\sigma(z_{i})\sigma(-z_{i})(1-2\sigma(z_{i}))

(56)

Larger
\beta_{i}
steepens
g_{i}
around
\Delta\hat{r}_{i}=0
, concentrating gradient mass near decision boundaries and saturating faster for large margins. This
targeted curvature control
is unavailable to loss weighting.

Assumption 1

(Margin-tail condition)
.

There exist constants
c,\tau>0
such that
\Pr(|\Delta\hat{r}|>t)\leq ce^{-\tau t}
under the training distribution.

Theorem 1

(Margin-sensitive generalization (informal))
.

Under
1
and a bounded-gradient condition on
\nabla_{\theta}\Delta\hat{r}
, any schedule that increases
\mathbb{E}[\beta_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{|\Delta\hat{r}_{i}|\leq\epsilon}]
while holding
\mathbb{E}[\beta_{i}]
fixed reduces a uniform upper bound on the expected surrogate risk by tightening the variance of the stochastic gradient around the classification boundary.

E.3
Semantic Gap Annotation Processing

Annotation overview.

We annotate preference pairs using a semantic-gap paradigm to produce instance-wise control signals for preference optimization. For each preference instance, multiple frontier LLM annotators are queried with several prompt formulations that emphasize complementary perspectives on the preference relationship. Each annotation yields four components: (i) a semantic gap category drawn from a fixed taxonomy (Safety, Factuality, Instruction, Reasoning, Helpfulness, Style), (ii) a magnitude score
S_{G}^{m}\in[0,1]
capturing the strength of the semantic discrepancy, (iii) a confidence score
S_{G}^{cf}\in[0,1]
reflecting annotator certainty, and (iv) a derived temperature coefficient
\beta\in[0.03,0.30]
computed as

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\beta_{\min}+(\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min})\cdot\mathrm{clip}(S_{G}^{m}\cdot S_{G}^{cf},\,0,\,1),

(57)

\displaystyle\text{with }\beta_{\min}=0.03,\;\beta_{\max}=0.30.

This construction ensures that semantically larger and more confident gaps exert stronger optimization pressure, while weak or uncertain gaps are softly down-weighted.

Annotation normalization and validation.

Starting from a deduplicated set of preference records, we enforce basic data integrity constraints, including the presence of non-empty
prompt
,
chosen
, and
rejected
fields. Annotator outputs are normalized into a shared schema: category labels are mapped to the approved taxonomy when alternative names are returned by APIs, and all numeric fields are validated to lie within their prescribed ranges. To guarantee internal consistency, all
\beta
values are recomputed from the stored magnitude and confidence scores rather than trusting raw API outputs.

Completeness and robustness handling.

We require each preference instance to contain a complete set of annotations across prompt formulations for every annotator. Incomplete records—typically arising from API failures, malformed responses, timeouts, or unresolved content filtering—are excluded from the released artifact. Where possible, transient failures are mitigated through retries and prompt sanitization, but the final dataset contains only fully validated and internally consistent annotations. For robustness, we additionally aggregate each annotator’s outputs across prompt formulations, producing a single per-annotator ensemble signal for category, magnitude, confidence, and
\beta
.

Released artifact.

The released
final annotated file

prompt_id

Each entry includes complete per-prompt and per-annotator semantic gap annotations, prompt-ensemble aggregates for each annotator, and cross-annotator summary coefficients used as instance-wise temperature schedules during preference optimization. \beta_{i}

and

Appendix I
Extended Related Work

This section extends the condensed related work in the main paper (
Section

3
) with additional discussion and pointers. SP
2
DPO connects data-centric preference optimization with curriculum learning and teacher-guided process design. We situate our contribution within: (i) RLHF and DPO, (ii) instance-adaptive enforcement, weighting, and curricula, and (iii) LLMs as annotators/judges and process designers.

RLHF and preference optimization foundations.

Preference-based RLHF learns from comparative feedback, typically by fitting a reward model and then optimizing a KL-regularized policy
(
6
;
41
;
36
)
. DPO
(
38
)
removes the explicit reward model and optimizes the policy directly via a closed-form objective derived from the Bradley–Terry model. Our work preserves DPO’s reward-free simplicity but revisits a key assumption: heterogeneous preference pairs should not be enforced with a single global temperature
\beta
.

DPO variants.

A growing literature modifies DPO-style objectives to improve stability or sample efficiency. IPO
(
1
)
replaces the logistic objective with a regression-style loss, while SimPO
(
28
)
uses a reference-free, length-normalized margin with optional KL regularization. Other methods adapt the optimization
target
or
signal
online: AlphaDPO
(
45
)
sets instance-adaptive margins, and
\beta
-DPO
(
46
)
calibrates temperature dynamically (often at batch level) and may include filtering. FocalPO
(
25
)
adapts the intuition of focal losses
(
24
)
to concentrate updates. In contrast, SP
2
DPO introduces a teacher-side,
semantic
notion of per-pair importance via an offline temperature schedule
\{\beta_{i}\}
, thereby modulating curvature/saturation rather than only scaling magnitudes or shifting targets. This is complementary to adaptive-margin methods: margins change the
target
, while temperatures change the
geometry
of the loss.

Weighting, filtering, and curriculum over preference pairs.

Curriculum learning argues that optimization depends on how examples are ordered and emphasized
(
4
;
16
)
. Recent work applies curricula and data filtering to preference optimization
(
7
;
18
;
37
;
40
)
, and classic meta-reweighting learns per-example weights to improve robustness
(
39
)
. Our approach is related but distinct: instead of staged sampling or multiplying per-example losses, we set per-pair temperatures that reshape the sigmoid geometry, concentrating gradient mass near the decision boundary for informative pairs while softly saturating on ambiguous/noisy pairs (
Section

4.2
).

LLMs as annotators, judges, and process designers.

Using LLMs to generate feedback or serve as judges has become standard in alignment pipelines, including RLAIF and constitutional-style supervision
(
17
;
3
)
and large-scale feedback datasets such as UltraFeedback
(
8
)
. Benchmarking suites like MT-Bench
(
50
)
and AlpacaEval 2.0
(
21
;
9
)
illustrate both the usefulness and pitfalls of LLM judging, including length and spurious-correlation biases
(
10
)
. SP
2
DPO uses LLMs in a different role: not to replace labels or reward models, but to provide structured, auditable signals that control optimization strength. This aligns with a broader shift toward pipeline-level alignment improvements, including accelerated preference optimization
(
12
)
and self-play style preference pipelines
(
47
)
. We view per-pair semantic temperature schedules as a lightweight, composable instance of this process-optimization direction (
Appendix

J
).

Pre-decided control vs. on-the-fly adaptation.

Many adaptive schedules modify training dynamics based on online signals (e.g., step-wise statistics, evolving margins, or controller-style updates). In contrast, SP
2
DPO fixes
\beta_{i}

before
training via an offline teacher annotation pass: there is no feedback loop from the student model into
\beta_{i}
during optimization. This pre-decision makes the alignment control auditable, reproducible, and easy to integrate into existing DPO pipelines.

Appendix J
Alignment Process Optimization (APO): A Paradigm and Roadmap

This section complements the APO formulation and motivation in the main content and we focus on (i) what constitutes an APO
process variable
in practice, (ii) what artifacts should be logged to make APO decisions auditable and reproducible, and (iii) how SP
2
DPO instantiates APO as a concrete, one-shot pre-decision of per-pair enforcement.

J.1
APO variables as versioned, auditable artifacts

APO treats alignment as a
pipeline design
problem: the most consequential choices often occur

before
gradient-based training begins (data construction, filtering, teacher rubrics, evaluation protocol),
yet these choices are typically documented informally or tuned implicitly. APO makes these upstream
choices explicit by grouping them into a process configuration
\lambda
(see
Equation

1
–
Equation

2
)
and insisting that
\lambda
be stored as a versioned artifact.

Concretely, we recommend treating the following as part of
\lambda
and logging them in the released artifacts:

•

Data decisions:
dataset identifier(s), pinned revisions, preprocessing rules, and any exclusion criteria.

•

Teacher/annotator policy:
annotator backbone IDs, prompt templates, decoding constraints, and parsing rules for structured outputs (e.g., JSON schema).

•

Aggregation operator:
how call-level signals are reduced (e.g., median over prompts, mean over annotators), including any tie-breaking rules (e.g., priority order).

•

Control-signal mapping:
the deterministic mapping from structured supervision to training-time controls (e.g.,
f_{\beta}
and the envelope
[\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max}]
).

•

Evaluation protocol:
judge model choice and prompting (for judge-based metrics), any length-bias mitigation, and explicit decoupling constraints between teacher(s), student(s), and judge(s)
(
50
;
9
;
10
)
.

This “process-as-artifact” view is especially important when LLMs participate upstream (as teachers or judges),
since prompt templates, model versions, and default behaviors can drift over time. Versioning
\lambda

ensures that improvements attributed to an algorithm are not silently caused by untracked pipeline changes.

SP
2

\beta_{i}
)

SP
2
DPO instantiates APO by placing the key enforcement decision—the per-pair temperature schedule

\{\beta_{i}\}
—
outside
the optimizer. The training objective remains standard DPO
(
Equation

5
); the process variable
\lambda
determines the control artifact

\{\beta_{i}\}

prior
to training (Phase 1 of
Algorithm

2
).

Concrete APO artifact in SP
2
DPO

In SP
2
DPO, the process configuration
\lambda
can be viewed as the tuple:

\lambda\equiv\left(\begin{aligned} &\underbrace{\mathcal{D},\;\texttt{revision},\;\texttt{filters}}_{\text{data}},\\
&\underbrace{\{A_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J},\;\{V_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}}_{\text{teacher panel + prompts}},\\
&\underbrace{\texttt{schema},\;\texttt{decode params}}_{\text{structured output control}},\;\underbrace{\texttt{Agg}(\cdot)}_{\text{robust ensembling}},\\
&\underbrace{f_{\beta}(\cdot),\;[\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max}]}_{\text{control mapping}}\end{aligned}\right).

Given
\lambda
, Phase 1 yields the released control artifact

\mathcal{D}^{*}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}^{w},y_{i}^{l},\beta_{i},\text{audit fields})\}_{i=1}^{N}

via the hierarchical estimator in
Equation

18
and
Appendix

C
. Phase 2 then trains with identical inner-loop settings
(optimizer, LR schedule, batch size, compute budget) across all methods, differing only by the injected
per-example coefficient
\beta_{i}
.

This explicit separation is the core APO commitment: (i)
semantic judgment and reliability control
live in
\lambda
and are auditable; (ii) the optimizer remains unchanged and compute-matched to standard DPO.

•

and all exclusion counts.

•

Call-level logs:

•

Aggregation spec:

).

•

Control artifact:
the released
\{\beta_{i}\}
schedule (or sufficient statistics) and distribution summaries (by category, by disagreement, by source).

•

Decoupling:
explicit separation between teacher/annotator models and judge model(s), and a statement of what overlaps are disallowed in the pipeline
(

.

•

J.5
Research opportunities enabled by semantic per-example control

The APO view suggests a natural design space once per-example control signals are treated as first-class artifacts:

•

Category-conditioned envelopes.
Use
S_{G}^{ct}
to set different stability envelopes per semantic slice (e.g., allow stronger caps for Safety/Factuality than Style), while preserving the same within-slice mapping (
Equation

15
)

•

Disagreement-aware enforcement.
Treat prompt/annotator disagreement as an uncertainty signal to down-modulate enforcement (a natural extension of
Appendix

C
diagnostics), improving robustness under label noise.

•

Outer-loop selection over mappings.
Instead of fixing
Equation

15
, treat
f_{\beta}
as a small parametric family and tune it on a held-out alignment set under
Equation

2
, yielding principled “process search” without changing the inner-loop optimizer.

•

Distillation and governance.
Distill
\beta(x_{i},y_{i}^{w},y_{i}^{l})
into a local predictor to amortize API cost and reduce dependence on drifting external annotators, while preserving auditability by releasing calibration diagnostics.

•

Hybrid offline–online curricula.
Use the offline semantic score as a prior and modulate it online by a student-state proxy (e.g., current margin), bridging static APO schedules and online adaptive methods.

Shared training configuration (DPO and SP
2
DPO).

Core settings are:
bfloat16 precision;
paged AdamW (32-bit) optimizer;
cosine LR schedule with 10% warmup;
max sequence length 2048 and max prompt length 1536;
1 training epoch;
and saving the final checkpoint and merged model at the end of training
(no explicit early-stopping or best

We do not explicitly set gradient clipping in the training configs; library defaults apply.

Data loading and efficiency settings.

We enable reference-log-prob precomputation and use worker/prefetch settings for faster I/O.
We also enable gradient checkpointing and length bucketing where supported.
The DPO config includes ‘group_by_length‘, persistent dataloader workers, pinned memory, and prefetching.

Hardware.

Training scripts are configured for 4 GPUs with bf16 mixed precision and CUDA visible devices 0–3.
DPO training is annotated as optimized for 4×A100
