An Unusual Cause of Typical Chest Pain
Authors: Aria Atash, Marish I.F.J. Oerlemans, Arco J. Teske
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This case highlights the prompt use of cardiac computer tomography angiography in a patient with intracardiac localization of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and chest pain in the acute setting. Information on anatomical associations of the metastases with the coronaries serves as the foundation for subsequent therapeutic and diagnostic strategies.

A 54-year-old woman visited the hematologist out-patient-clinic at our hospital after a recently diagnosed diffused large B-cell lymphoma with metastasis in multiple organs including the heart. She was set to receive 6 cycles of chemotherapy (rituximab/cyclophosphamide/doxorubicin/vincristine/prednisolone) with high-dose methotrexate. On presentation, she appeared to have a 3-week history of pressing chest pain that persisted at rest. The electrocardiogram (ECG) showed prominent negative T waves, ST-segment depression, a prolonged corrected QT interval, and a premature ventricular contraction, possibly a manifestation of cardiac infiltration (Figure 1A). The patient was admitted to the emergency department, where, after sublingual administration of nitroglycerine, the chest pain resolved completely. Laboratory test results showed elevated cardiac troponin-I (86 ng/L), negative creatine kinase-MB (<1.0 μg/L), and elevated N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (3,583 pg/mL). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a borderline normal left and right ventricular systolic function, no valvular disease, slight pericardial effusion, and hypokinesia in the apicolateral wall. Cardiac computer tomography angiography (CCTA) imaging showed multiple lymphoma localizations in the right and left ventricle and in the right atrium. Although the distal right coronary artery, right posterior descending artery, and the right posterolateral branch were clearly surrounded by lymphoma, there was no compression of the vascular lumen (Figures 1B and 1C). In addition, the location of the regional wall motion abnormalities does not fit the right coronary artery coronary supply. The clinical presentation is suggestive of a non–ST-segment elevation acute coronary syndrome, as substantiated by the typical symptoms, prominent negative T waves, and troponin release. Considering the resolution of chest pain after nitroglycerine administration, the absence of ST-segment elevations, and the blank cardiac medical history, immediate invasive angiography was not performed. Moreover, this could be a high-risk endeavor without any prior knowledge on the coronary anatomy in relation to the lymphoma localizations. CCTA was chosen as the first imaging modality due to its high spatial and temporal resolution, providing accurate anatomic analysis between the coronary arteries and the lymphoma localizations. Furthermore, CCTA has high negative predictive value to determine acute coronary syndrome or obstructive coronary artery disease1 and is associated with good clinical outcomes.2,3

The differential diagnosis of angina in the presence of cardiac metastases includes pericardial disease and takotsubo syndrome. Laboratory work, ECG, and transthoracic echocardiography/CT findings showed no signs of cardiac tamponade or pericarditis. Elevated N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide levels alongside the prominent negative T waves and prolonged QT interval were indicative of takotsubo syndrome secondary to cardiac lymphoma localizations, without obstructive coronary involvement. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) was performed to evaluate the myocardial involvement at baseline (Figures 1D and 1E). Diffuse elevated T2 mapping values (basal: 65.4 ± 8.80 milliseconds; mid: 76.0 ± 12.1 milliseconds; apical: 71.6 ± 11.4 milliseconds) indicated edema in the acute takotsubo cardiomyopathy phase. To monitor arrythmia, 48-hour telemetric and cardiac troponin follow-up were performed during the first cycle of chemotherapy. The ECG abnormalities initially persisted even as cardiac troponin levels steadily normalized. CMR and fluorodeoxyglucose–positron emission tomography evaluation after 6 months revealed normalization of left ventricular systolic function and complete metabolic remission without any remaining intracardiac lymphoma localizations (Figures 1F and 1G). Furthermore, no detectable damage was observed after resolution of the extensive lymphoma localizations, as evidenced by no late gadolinium enhancement on CMR.
