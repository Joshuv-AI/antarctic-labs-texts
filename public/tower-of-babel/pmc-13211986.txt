Influence of time of day and insecticide treatment on the abundance of Culiseta melanura (Diptera: Culicidae) in resting boxes
Authors: Nicholas Brown, John B Keven, Edward D Walker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrequent use of resting boxes by Culiseta melanura (Coquillett) during the day-time may offer the opportunity for use of these artificial resting sites as delivery substrates for insecticides to manage populations of this eastern equine encephalitis virus (EEEV) vector. In field studies at EEEV-endemic foci in southwestern Michigan, abundance in black-painted resting boxes of adult male and female Cs. melanura varied within the 24-h diel cycle, by whether the interiors of the boxes were treated with a microencapsulated formulation of lambda cyhalothrin (Demand CSTM), and from before to after treatment of boxes at field sites. Abundance was highest at noon, diminished to nil at midnight, and increased by noon the following day. Abundance of Cs. melanura was lower in insecticide-treated boxes compared to untreated boxes, and declined at field sites sampled after treatment of boxes with microencapsulated lambda cyhalothrin, compared to before treatment and to control sites. This population suppression effect lasted at least 30 days and exceeded 90% reduction. Field-caught Cs. melanura males and females experienced high mortality when exposed to lambda cyhalothrin-treated bottles in bottle bioassays and in overnight exposures to treated boxes set in the laboratory. Prolonged daily visits to resting boxes, resulting in exposure to residual insecticide, may explain the sustained, diminishing effect on abundance of field populations of Cs. melanura. Results support use of insecticide-treated boxes distributed into wooded upland edges of natural larval habitats as an intervention against EEEV transmission.Keywords: diel periodicity, resting behavior, lambda cyhalothrin, Culiseta melanura

Introduction

The increase in occurrence of mosquito-borne diseases in the United States has stimulated efforts to improve effectiveness of methods used for vector control (Rosenberg et al. 2018). Mosquito abatement programs in the U.S. face the operational challenge of ensuring that the chemical control methods they use are effective (EPA [Environmental Protection Agency] 2016, Moise et al. 2019, Stoops et al. 2019), yet not disruptive to other forms of life (Rochlin et al. 2022). Effectiveness can be enhanced by knowledge of the particular behaviors of the mosquito vectors, such as resting behavior and selection of resting sites (Jimenez-Vallejo et al. 2025). For example, the use of indoor residual spraying in malaria and dengue control programs takes advantage of the resting behavior of female mosquitoes on the indoor surfaces of human habitations, especially the walls (Samuel et al. 2017, Dunbar et al. 2019, Irish et al. 2024). Female mosquitoes encountering residues on treated surfaces may acquire a lethal dose and die as a consequence, thereby reducing population size, female longevity, and vectorial capacity (Brady et al. 2016).

Eastern equine encephalitis virus (EEEV) (Togaviridae: Alphavirus), causative agent of infection and disease in wild and domestic birds, horses, white-tailed deer, and humans in the eastern United States including Michigan (Scott and Weaver 1989, Lindsey et al. 2018, Oliver et al. 2018, Corrin et al. 2021, Stobierski et al. 2022, Hill et al. 2023), is transmitted enzootically among passerine birds in and around particular swamp and bog settings, primarily by the mosquito Culiseta melanura (Coquillett). Several other species of mosquitoes, such as Aedes canadensis (Theobald) and Coquillettidia perturbans (Walker), may serve as bridge vectors responsible for transmission of EEEV to mammals (Armstrong and Andreadis 2010, Sherwood et al. 2020, Corrin et al. 2021), although Armstrong and Andreadis (2010) have postulated that enough Cs. melanura may feed on mammals that it could be responsible for transmission of EEEV to humans and horses without bridge vectors.

Empirical observations from trapping and sampling studies indicate that Cs. melanura emerges as adults from its swamp and bog larval habitats and moves from those habitats to nearby, peripheral wooded uplands or so-called “edge” sites (Morris et al. 1980, Howard et al. 2025). This dispersal behavior from swamp to surrounding wooded uplands is supported by mark-recapture studies in upstate New York (Howard et al. 1989) and sampling studies in eastern Massachusetts (Nasci and Edman 1981, 1984). Presumably, females move in this way to obtain blood from suitable bird hosts in these uplands, but movement outside of swamp environments also may place them into closer contact with humans and horses, thereby increasing the potential for Cs. melanura to function as direct vectors of EEEV without the requirement of bridge vector species (Nasci and Edman 1984, Armstrong and Andreadis 2010).

Culiseta melanura females and males exhibit a strong behavioral predilection to enter into and rest inside artificial resting stations, a behavior that provides a sampling system informative for population studies and for surveillance for EEEV (Burbutis and Jobbins 1958, Edman et al. 1968, Morris 1981, Nasci and Edman 1984, Komar et al. 1995, Howard et al. 2011, West et al. 2020, Howard et al. 2025). This behavior also presents the opportunity to explore the utility of artificial resting stations, such as small black boxes placed on the ground, as delivery systems for insecticides to the resting fraction of the Cs. melanura population, a concept analogous to a “miniature” indoor residual spraying of insecticides (Brady et al. 2016) that could reduce the vectorial capacity of the population (West et al. 2020).

The purpose of our study was to quantify variation in the abundance of Cs. melanura adults in resting boxes set in the field over the 24-h diel period and to measure the effect of insecticide treatment of those boxes on density in boxes, and population abundance over time post-treatment.

Materials and Methods

Field Sites

This study was conducted in summers of 2021 to 2024 at field sites in southwestern Michigan, where Cs. melanura populations were previously encountered and where EEE is endemic (Stobierski et al. 2022). The six sites for the present study (Fig. 1) included wooded uplands of deciduous trees and shrubs, qualifying as edge sites (sensu Howard et al. 2025), bordering the following sphagnum bog wetlands: (1) the north side of Fair Lake, Barry Township, Barry County, Michigan (“Fair Lake North” site; 42°29’42.68” N, 85°19’52.94” W); (2) the south side of Fair Lake, Barry Township, Barry County, Michigan (“Fair Lake South” site; 42°28’32.13” N, 85°20’09.38” W); (3) the West Lake Nature Preserve, Portage, Kalamazoo County, Michigan (“West Lake” site; 42°11’12.90” N, 85°35’05.82” W); (4) the Eliason Natural Area, Portage, Kalamazoo County, Michigan (“Bishop’s Bog” site; 42°10’48.67” N, 85°36’15.66” W); (5) the Hampton Creek watershed in Portage, Kalamazoo County, Michigan (“Hampton Creek” site; 42°12’08.49” N, 85°38’25.72” W); and (6) the hardwoods surrounding a bog at the northern end of the Gourdneck State Game Area, Portage, Kalamazoo County, Michigan (“Gourdneck” site; 42°11’42.78” N, 85°38’02.96” W).

Resting Boxes and Sampling

Resting boxes used for sampling and insecticide treatment consisted of Western Pulp square pots, 31.5 cm square × 28.3 cm deep (product number CN-WPSQ-1111, Greenhouse Megastore, Danville, IL). These pots, marketed for starter plantings in the horticulture industry, consist of durable, molded pulp (Komar et al. 1995). We coated the exterior and interior of them with flat black, latex interior paint primer (#O55198677593, Glidden, Home Depot, Okemos, Michigan) using an electric spray paint gun and hand brush finish. This paint offers durability and resistance to moisture and mildew. Small drainage holes at the bottom four corners of each pot were closed with pieces of gray, foam pipe insulation material (#K7PXE048058HD1, Everbilt, Home Depot, Okemos, Michigan). Boxes were placed on the ground in the wooded upland areas of the field sites, with the opening typically directed away from the south and in shade, against a tree trunk, under a shrub, or in an area not exposed to direct sunlight. To sample mosquitoes resting inside the boxes, the person doing the sampling directed a collection cup (8.5 cm diam) fixed to the intake tube of a Prokopack aspirator powered with a 12 V battery (Model 1419, John W. Hock, Gainesville, Florida, USA) to the open end of the box. With fan on, the sampler rotated the open end of the cup along the inner surfaces of the box and into the open space between them for approximately 15 s, capturing both resting mosquitoes and those disturbed and flying. The sampler then withdrew the device and capped the cup before switching off the fan. Cups were labeled, placed into insulated cooler boxes containing frozen blue ice packs, returned to the laboratory, and transferred to a −20°C freezer for >1 h. Then, samples were removed and sorted, and mosquitoes identified to species (Wood et al. 1979, Darsie and Ward 2005). Given that the majority of Cs. melanura encountered in this study had pale bands of scales on the dorsal abdominal segments (a confounding diagnostic feature; Andreadis and Munstermann 1997, Darsie and Ward 2005), the confirmation procedure in Andreadis and Munstermann (1997) was followed to confirm identification of Cs. melanura and rule out Culiseta impatiens (Walker).

Field Study on Diel Periodicity of Resting in Black Boxes

In August 2023, 20 black boxes were set on the ground at approximately a 3-m distance apart from each other in a hardwood upland immediately west and adjacent to a sphagnum bog at the Hampton Creek wetland (https://www.portagemi.gov/717/Hampton-Creek-Wetland-Area) in Portage, Michigan, USA (site 6 in Fig. 1). These boxes were part of a linear array of 360 boxes deployed in an approximate distance of 1.3 km along the bog perimeter from the Angling Road entrance to the Borges Integrative Medicine facility (8001 Angling Rd, Portage, MI 49024, USA; 42°12’05.65” N; 85°38’38.10” W) on the southwest to the wooded area immediately to the west of the golf tee box of fairway 17 of the Moors golf course (42°12’18.30” N; 85°38’32.31” W) (Fig. 2A). Mosquitoes were sampled from the boxes, as described above, at noon (1200 h), evening (1800 h), midnight (0000 h), morning (0600 h), and 1200 h the following day; on the following four 24-h occasions (August 2 to 3; August 9 to 10; August 24 to 25; and September 9 to 10). Mosquitoes were identified to sex and species, and counted. On August 15, the interiors of 300 boxes arrayed around these twenty boxes were treated with a wet spray mixture of 0.06% Demand CSTM insecticide (9.6% lambda cyhalothrin in microencapsulated formulation; Syngenta; hereafter referred to as microencapsulated lambda cyhalothrin) using a backpack sprayer with hand pump pressurizer (model DXSP190652; DeWalt).

Maps of (A) the Hampton Watershed site and (B) the West Lake site showing the arrays of treated and sampling resting boxes (white X symbols) in relationship to sphagnum bog wetlands. Both sites are within the city limits of Portage, Michigan. Images are from Google Earth Pro. Scale bars are 200 m.

Repeated-measures negative binomial regression implemented in SASTM 9.4 software with the GENMOD procedure (SAS [computer program] 2014) was used to analyze variation in mosquito counts, using individual boxes as the repeatedly sampled replicates, time intervals as the main treatment effect, the four sampling dates as a categorical variable, and the generalized estimating equations method for estimation of parameters and z-scores for statistical inferences (Zeger et al. 1988, Diggle et al. 1994). Wald statistics for main effects and interactions also were estimated (Lin et al. 2016). Interpretation of coefficients from negative binomial regression were interpreted following guidelines from UCLA: Statistical Consulting Group (2024). Briefly, the exponent (with base e) of the coefficient of the treatment independent variable was used to determine the multiplicative difference in the expected counts of mosquitoes in untreated and treated boxes.

Effect of Insecticide Treatment on Population Abundance in Resting Boxes at Field Sites

Abundance of Cs. melanura Between Treated and Untreated Resting Boxes

To determine if abundance of Cs. melanura differed between resting boxes that were treated with microencapsulated lambda cyhalothrin or not treated, a set of 60 boxes was placed in a linear transect of approximately 180 m in length, in the hardwood upland directly north of a sphagnum bog at the Fair Lake North study site in September 2021. Treated and untreated boxes were placed in pairs along the length of the sampling transect. These boxes were sampled between 1100 and 1400 h on September 16 (high temperature, 26.1°C), September 22 (high temperature, 13.9°C), and October 6 (high temperature, 16.1°C). Mosquitoes from samples were identified to species and sex and counted. Counts of mosquitoes per box were subjected to repeated-measures negative binomial regression implemented in SASTM with the GENMOD procedure, using individual boxes as the repeatedly sampled replicates, insecticide treatment as the main treatment effect, and sampling date as a categorical variable.

Population Decline of Cs. melanura After Insecticide Treatment of Resting Boxes

The following experiment was conducted in summer 2023 to determine if Cs. melanura populations declined at sites after distribution of insecticide treated resting boxes. Three sites (Hampton Creek, West Lake, and Fair Lake North) were designated as treatment sites, and three sites as controls (Fair Lake South, Bishop’s Bog, and Gourdneck). First, 20 untreated resting boxes were distributed to each site for purposes of sampling. On August 15, 300 additional boxes treated with microencapsulated lambda cyhalothrin were distributed to the three treatment sites in a row of approximately 1.3 km in length with approximately 3 m between each box. The other three sites received no additional boxes. Samples were taken from late July to late August on 6 dates, three before and three after deployment of the insecticide treated boxes spanning a 30-day period. Mosquitoes were pooled from the 20 boxes per site, identified and counted. Mosquito counts per 20 boxes per site per collection date were transformed by log10(x + 1) to reduce heteroscedasticity and to improve central tendency. The transformed data were subjected to repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with profile analysis following procedures in von Ende (2001), using the GLM procedure in SAS 9.4 (SAS [computer program] 2014). Long Term Effect of Insecticide Treated Resting Boxes on Abundance of Cs. melanura

A third field experiment was conducted at the Hampton Creek and West Lake sites in 2024 to determine if insecticide treatment resulted in reduced abundance of Cs. melanura over time, but in this case individual sampling boxes were used as the sampling unit. The intention of this design was to determine if the population response to distribution of insecticide treated boxes over time was similar for samples from treated and untreated boxes. A total of 360 boxes was distributed at each site (schematics in Fig. 2A and B). Of these, 20 boxes were designated to be untreated for sampling, and 40 others were designated to be treated and sampled; all these sampled boxes were intermixed with the remainder of the boxes which were designated for treatment but not sampled. Twenty boxes assigned to the sampling array were interspersed with treated boxes at about every tenth box deployed. At the Hampton Creek site, boxes were sampled on 12 dates from July 9 to September 6 with insecticide treatment done on July 15. At the West Lake site, boxes were sampled on 6 dates from July 17 to August 20, with insecticide treatment done on July 23. The resting box was the unit of replication. Counts of mosquitoes were subjected to repeated-measures negative binomial regression implemented in SASTM with the GENMOD procedure, using individual boxes as the repeatedly sampled replicates, insecticide treatment as the main treatment effect, and sampling date as a categorical variable.

Insecticide Bioassays

Culiseta melanura males and females were sampled from untreated black resting boxes set on the ground at the Hampton Creek site, returned to the laboratory in cold boxes with moist towels placed over the collection cups and blue ice blocks underneath. Mosquitoes were held overnight in the original collection cups with 20% sucrose solution in cotton pledgets at room temperature conditions (25 ± 2°C). Approximately 10 adult males and females surviving overnight were transferred by oral aspiration to each of three BugDormTM (BugDorm-2S120 Insect Rearing Cage, Taichung 407008, Taiwan; width 60 cm × length 60 cm × height 60 cm) dome insect cages, into which had previously been placed one black box with open end facing the zippered entrance to the cage. One box had been treated on the interior surfaces the day before with 0.06% dilution of Demand CSTM (9.6% lambda cyhalothrin in microencapsulated formulation; Syngenta), one box had been treated with 0.08% dilution of Mavrik PerimeterTM (22.3% tau-fluvalinate; Zoecon); and a third box was treated with water. Treatments were made with a hand-operated mist sprayer (Zep Commercial Professional Sprayer, #U4300, 946 ml capacity; Atlanta, Georgia USA) until the boxes dripped. Boxes were treated in the afternoon in a vented chemical fume hood, allowed to dry overnight, and were placed in separate dome cages the following morning, after which mosquitoes were added. Two cups containing cotton balls soaked in 20% sucrose solution were placed into each cage. The following morning, live and dead mosquitoes were recovered from the cages and boxes using a mouth aspirator, and were counted. This experiment was replicated three times. Mortality data were expressed as the percentage of the total number of males or females dead after 24 hours. Proportionate mortality data were transformed by the arcsine of the square root because of the wide range of values from 0% to 100% (Warton and Hui 2011) and data were subjected to one way analysis of variance using the ANOVA procedure in SAS, with comparisons of means by Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference procedure (SAS 9.4).

Bottle bioassays using Cs. melanura males and females sampled as above from the Hampton Creek site were conducted as described in Dubie et al. (2022) using a modified CDC bottle bioassay protocol (CDC [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention] 2024) with formulated product diluted to the highest labeled dosage. The liquid formulation of Demand CS, diluted with water to 0.06%, was used as toxicant in single dose/time-dependent observations. Two ml of dilution (or 2 ml of water for controls) was introduced by pipette into each bottle, then the bottles rotated to coat the interiors thoroughly, and allowed to dry in a chemical fume hood. Once dry, mosquitoes (approximately ten per sex) were introduced into bottles, caps applied, and knockdown scored by eye at 5 min intervals for 30 min. Proportionate data of number of mosquitoes of each sex knocked down at each time interval were transformed by the arcsine of the square root, and the data subjected to repeated-measures analysis of variance using the GLM procedure in SAS (SAS 9.4) as in Dubie et al. (2022).

Results

Diel Periodicity of Resting in Black Boxes

A total of 1,253 Cs. melanura males and 1,094 females (sex ratio, 1.14:1) were collected from a total of 400 box-sampling events in the diel periodicity study conducted at the Hampton Creek site in 2023. Of the females, 80.9% were unfed, 11.1% were fully gravid, 2.5% were half-gravid (both eggs and blood were visible in the abdomens), and 5.6% were blood fed (only blood was visible in the abdomens). Other species in the samples included Anopheles quadrimaculatus Say (368 males, 178 females), Anopheles punctipennis (Say) (121 males, 47 females), Culex erraticus (Dyar and Knab) (2 females), Aedes canadensis (1 female), and Cq. perturbans (1 female). Counts of Cs. melanura of both sexes were greatest at noon (1200 h), declined in the evening (1800 h), were lowest at midnight (0000 h), still low at morning (0600 h), and high at the following noon (Fig. 3). Overall, most males (89.2%) and females (93.4%) were collected at the two noon samples. The number of Cs. melanura males and females varied significantly among sampling times (Wald tests from negative binomial regression: females, χ2 = 202.6, df = 4, P < 0.0001; males, χ2 = 671.3, df = 4, P < 0.0001). Tests by z-scores from generalized estimating equations of the parameter estimates across sampling time intervals showed that the two noon sampling times were not statistically significantly different for males or females, whereas the evening, midnight, and morning time periods were significantly less (Table 1, males; and Table 2, females; the second noon sampling interval serving as reference group). The number of Cs. melanura males and females varied significantly among sampling dates as well (Wald statistics from negative binomial regression: females, χ2 = 158.4, df = 3, P < 0.0001; males, χ2 = 253.3, df = 3, P < 0.0001). Tests by z-scores across sampling dates showed that Cs. melanura male and female abundance was higher by approximately ten-fold for the first two sampling dates, which were not different from each other and were different from the second two sampling dates, which were not different from each other (Table 1, males; and Table 2, females; the fourth sampling date serving as reference). Data of An. quadrimaculatus males and females showed similar trends for diel periodicity, but numbers were too few for statistical analysis.

Insecticide Treatment and Cs. melanura Population Responses

Abundance of Cs. melanura Between Treated and Untreated Resting Boxes

Abundance of Cs. melanura males and females was significantly lower in resting boxes treated with microencapsulated lambda cyhalothrin compared to untreated boxes in the experiment conducted at the Fair Lake North site in 2021 (Fig. 4) (Wald statistics from negative binomial regression: females, χ2 = 21.3, df = 1, P < 0.0001; males, χ2 = 26.4, df = 1, P < 0.0001). The parameter estimate for male mosquito abundance in the control boxes relative to treated boxes as the reference was 2.72 (95% confidence interval of 1.99 and 3.46), with z-score of 7.27 (P < 0.0001). The parameter estimate for female mosquito abundance in the control boxes relative to treated boxes as the reference was 3.94 (95% confidence interval of 2.91 and 4.97), with z-score of 7.53 (P < 0.0001). Exponentiation of the regression coefficients indicated that untreated boxes had 15.2 times as many males and 51.9 times as many females as did treated boxes. Of the 230 female and 134 male Cs. melanura sampled across all three dates in 180 box-samples, 225 (97.8%) of females and 125 (94.0%) of males were from untreated boxes. Of the females, 40% were unfed, 29.1% gravid, 22.6% half-gravid, and 8.3% blood fed.

Population Decline of Cs. melanura After Insecticide Treatment of Resting Boxes

In the field experiment conducted in July and August, 2023 to determine if distribution of lambda cyhalothrin-treated resting boxes affected abundance of Cs. melanura over time, a total of 1,393 males and 1,081 females were collected from a total of 720 boxes sampled across 6 sites and 6 dates (three dates pre-treatment, and three dates post-treatment). Of the females, 70.6% were unfed, 11.7% were gravid, 9.3% were half-gravid, and 8.4% were blood fed. Culiseta melanura populations declined at three sites after insecticide treated boxes were distributed, compared to three sites where treated boxes were not distributed (Fig. 5). One of the sites receiving treated boxes (Hampton Creek) had a much higher population than did all the others, skewing the average number of mosquitoes higher for the treatment group. However, it did not affect the analysis due to the scaling effect of the log10-transformation of the sampling data. Repeated measures ANOVA showed a highly significant interaction of treatment and sampling date (F = 18.3, df = 5, P = 0.0003), indicating that the treatment and control sampling series lacked parallelism, ie responded differently to the treatment over time. Significant variation among sampling dates (F = 12.1, df = 5, P = 0.0015) also indicated a strong effect of the box treatment over time (von Ende 2001). Profile analysis showed no differences for the first two pre-treatment pairs of sampling dates, but that the pre- and post-treatment pair of date 3 and date 4 were highly significantly different from each other (Table 3). Thereafter, the pairs of dates were not significantly different, indicating that the treatment remained in effect for the treated boxes (Table 3; comparison of dates 4 and 5; and 5 and 6). When considering untransformed data, the percent reduction (ie decline in the treated populations compared to the controls) was 97.7% (males) and 92.5% (females) one day after treated boxes were distributed at post-treatment sampling date 1, 92.7% (males) and 87.4% (females) three days at post-treatment sampling date 2, and 95.9% (males) and 96.8% (females) seven days afterwards.

Long Term Effect of Insecticide Treated Resting Boxes on Abundance of Cs. melanura

Field experiments during 2024 captured 3,351 male and 1,700 female Cs. melanura at the Hampton Creek site, and 577 male and 290 female Cs. melanura at the West Lake site. Of the females, 81.6% were unfed, 3.6% were gravid, 5.6% were half-gravid, and 9.4% were blood fed. At the West Lake site, Cs. melanura male and female resting counts were significantly lower in insecticide treated boxes compared to untreated ones (negative binomial regression for males: z = 4.17, P < 0.0001; negative binomial regression for females: z = 3.10, P = 0.002) (Fig. 6A and B). The Wald Type 3 tests for males were: main treatment effect, χ2 = 5.41, df = 1, P = 0.02; sampling date χ2 = 37.21, df = 5, P < 0.0001; interaction of main treatment and sampling date, χ2 = 40.3, df = 11, P < 0.0001. The Wald Type 3 tests for females were: main treatment effect, χ2 = 3.75, df = 1, P = 0.05; sampling date χ2 = 39.6, df = 5, P < 0.0001; interaction of main treatment and sampling date, χ2 = 39.81, df = 11, P < 0.0001. At the Hampton Creek site, Cs. melanura male and female resting counts were significantly lower in insecticide treated boxes compared to untreated ones (negative binomial regression for males: z = 2.69, P = 0.0071; negative binomial regression for females: z = 2.73, P = 0.006) (Fig. 6C and D). The Wald Type 3 tests for males were: main treatment effect, χ2 = 3.1, df = 1, P = 0.08; sampling date χ2 = 41.9, df = 11, P < 0.0001; interaction of main treatment and sampling date, χ2 = 48.6, df = 23, P = 0.0014. The Wald Type 3 tests for females were: main treatment effect, χ2 = 3.2, df = 1, P = 0.07; sampling date χ2 = 45.1, df = 11, P < 0.0001; interaction of main treatment and sampling date, χ2 = 52.22, df = 23, P = 0.0005.

Bottle Bioassays

Results of mortality of male (total, 60) and female (total, 48) Cs. melanura when exposed to microencapsulated lambda cyhalothrin in 30-min bottle bioassays are shown in Fig. 8. For mosquitoes of both sexes, the observed knockdown leading to mortality exceeded 50% after 5 min exposure, 90% at 10 min, and 100% in all treatments by 25 min for both sexes. No recoveries were observed. Mortality in control bottles was low. Knockdown was behaviorally evident by mosquitoes ceasing flying, losing normal posture, positioning scutum down on the glass surface, twitching, wing fanning, and spinning in circles before ceasing movement. Repeated measures ANOVA showed a highly significant interaction of treatment and time interval in minutes for males (F = 26.8; df = 6, 24; P < 0.0001) and females (F = 36.0; df = 6, 24; P < 0.0001), indicating that mortality curves followed different trajectories in the insecticide-treated and nontreated bottles. Discussion

Adult male and female Cs. melanura demonstrated marked diel periodicity in utilization of the boxes as resting sites. Mosquito abundance was highest at noon, but declined as evening approached and was lowest at midnight to morning. The periodicity of changes in abundance indicated that repopulation took place over the period between sunrise and mid-day, and that depopulation of the boxes occurred as the evening commenced. The periodicity pattern observed here was similar to that in New York state (Morris 1981) involving visual observations of the number of Cs. melanura resting in boxes over hourly intervals. Similarly, Gray et al. (2011) observed that Cx. erraticus sought resting sites during the few hours before and after sunrise, although repopulation by Cs. melanura based on our sampling data appears to be somewhat later in the morning, an inference supported by the observations of Morris (1981). These observations imply that boxes should be sampled during midday when mosquito resting is highest and not in early mornings, evenings, or night when resting is lowest or nil.

Diel activity patterns of mosquitoes are well known for several different species of mosquitoes (Fritz et al. 2008, 2014, Wilke et al. 2023), whose control relates to entrainment of behaviors into circadian rhythms by environmental cues (Rund et al. 2016, Ajayi et al. 2020, Duffield 2024). Although such mechanisms are unknown in Cs. melanura, the stark diel periodicity of departure from and return to resting boxes observed here indicated that their diel activity was pronounced, and included substantial time allocated for resting during daylight hours. Hess (1958) observed that Cs. melanura males formed mating swarms starting at 1 hour before sunset, consistent with an evening departure time of males from resting boxes noted here. Because swarming continued until about 1 hr after sunset (Hayes 1958), but males did not repopulate resting boxes until the following day (Fig. 3), the males must have rested elsewhere at night, or sought resources such as carbohydrates during the nighttime after swarming. Presumably, females similarly departed from resting boxes in the evening for purposes of mating, to forage for nectar and blood resources, and to locate oviposition sites. Among the Cs. melanura females sampled in resting boxes in southeastern Massachusetts, USA, 48% were positive for nectar by the anthrone test method across all physiological categories (unfed, gravid, blood fed; Nasci and Edman 1984), suggesting a high daily carbohydrate demand regardless of gonotrophic condition.

Laboratory and field experiments demonstrated that Cs. melanura from field sites were highly sensitive to microencapsulated lambda cyhalothrin. Mosquitoes from natural populations showed rapid knockdown leading to death, with no recovery, when exposed to interiors of glass bottles treated with a labeled rate of 0.06% after only a few minutes, with most knockdown and mortality occurring within 10 min of exposure. When resting boxes treated with microencapsulated lambda cyhalothrin (or a long-lasting but non-encapsulated formulation of tau fluvalinate) were placed in dome cages and wild-caught Cs. melanura males and females added to the cages, the majority of the mosquitoes were recovered dead both inside and outside of the treated boxes within 24 h. These findings indicated that the insecticide treatment in boxes did not inhibit mosquito entry, and that after mosquitoes entered, they contacted the formulated toxin, receiving a dose sufficient to cause death. These laboratory findings likely explain the sampling patterns observed in the field, where Cs. melanura counts were much lower in treated boxes compared to non-treated boxes.

Lambda cyhalothrin shows strong, post-contact irritation effects on mosquitoes (Evans 1993), although one study infers pre-contact repellency of lambda cyhalothrin to Anopheles mosquitoes from experimental hut studies (Gunasekaran et al. 2014) and another from excitation test boxes (Potikasikorn et al. 2005). These pre-contact behavioral responses are difficult to explain given the low vapor pressure of lambda cyhalothrin (1.5 × 10−9 mm Hg or 0.0002 MPa at 20°C; He et al. 2008), and consequently its very low tendency to volatilize from treated surfaces. By contrast, initial landing frequency of female Aedes aegypti (L.), Culex quinquefasciatus Say, and An. quadrimaculatus was as high on surfaces treated with analytical grade lambda cyhalothrin as on untreated surfaces, leading to high knockdown and mortality as observed here for Cs. melanura (Cooperband and Allan 2009). However, no studies have examined the contact toxicity vs. excito-repellency effects of lambda cyhalothrin for mosquitoes, when this chemical is incorporated into the commercial formulation Demand CS with its proprietary iCAPTM microencapsulated technology (Syngenta 2023). German cockroaches, Blatella germanica L., equally visited surfaces treated or not treated with microencapsulated lambda cyhalothrin, showing no pre-contact repellency but exhibiting post-contact toxic and behavioral effects (Gaire et al. 2023). Similarly, applications of microencapsulated lambda cyhalothrin did not inhibit imported fire ants (Solenopsis invicta Buren) nor Pharaoh ants (Monomorium pharaonic L.) from visiting treated surfaces nor did it lessen the amount of time moving over them, compared to untreated surfaces (Syngenta 2010). These studies support our conclusion that Cs. melanura males and females are likely to enter treated resting boxes and to encounter lambda cyhalothrin incorporated in the microcapsules comprising the formulation, leading to contact exposure and death. Additional studies of pre- and post-contact behavioral effects of microencapsulated lambda cyhalothrin on Cs. melanura with relationship to resting box interactions are warranted, but our results suggest a mode of action involving direct contact followed by toxicity leading to mortality as opposed to non-contact repellency, or sublethal contact repellency, followed by recovery.

Knowledge of resting behavior of mosquitoes provides opportunity for designing and implementing targeted control measures for vector species of human pathogens (Jimenez-Vallejo et al. 2025). However, resting behavior is a relatively understudied behavior, knowledge of which is heavily dependent upon intensive, comprehensive sampling of microhabitats within larger surroundings in natural environments (Burkett-Cadena et al. 2008, 2013). Directing indoor residual sprays of insecticide to specific indoor resting sites of Ae. aegypti, for purposes of reducing dengue virus transmission, improved population reduction while reducing the amount of insecticide used and the time required to apply it (Vazquez-Prokopec et al. 2017, Dunbar et al. 2019). Targeted indoor residual sprays for control of populations of Anopheles vectors of human malaria, focusing on areas within a house used for resting, can reduce costs of the treatments (Irish et al. 2024). In our study, the small resting boxes we chose to use (Komar et al. 1995) were readily procured, painted, distributed by the hundreds to field sites by field teams within short time intervals, and treated readily in the field. Culiseta melanura males and females show a tendency to move to and rest at peripheries of swamps and bogs (such as the “edge” sites of Morris et al. 1980, Howard et al. 1983, Howard et al. 2025; or the “perimeter” sites of Nasci and Edman 1984). Placement of boxes on terra firma at edge or perimeter sites by bogs, as described here, eliminates the need for field teams to enter wet swamps and bogs to deploy them, and provides ready access for sampling and treatment from nearby deployment areas.

Chemical control of Cs. melanura populations, whether directed against immature or adult populations, has shown limited success with remarkably few published field evaluations (Rathburn et al. 1971, Woodrow et al. 1995, Howard and Oliver 1997, Burtis et al. 2021). In particular, ultralow volume, aerial application of insecticides directed against adult Cs. melanura did not result in population declines (Rathburn et al. 1971, Howard and Oliver 1997) or results were equivocal depending upon site and year of application (MDAR 2020). Results of our studies indicated that resting boxes treated with microencapsulated lambda cyhalothrin and distributed in sufficient numbers to peripheries of larval habitat sites (sphagnum bogs of varied surface area) resulted in rapid and sustained declines in Cs. melanura populations. The rapidity of the response was similar to insecticidal barrier treatments applied to reduce mosquito populations (Stoops et al. 2019). This finding is important because EEEV activity at endemic foci is positively correlated with the density of Cs. melanura at those foci (Edman et al. 1993, Hachiya et al. 2007, Armstrong and Andreadis 2022).

We approached the research problem of effects of insecticide treatment of resting boxes on population abundance of Cs. melanura in two different ways. In the first approach, in a comparison of sites to which treated boxes were distributed or not, and mosquitoes were sampled in untreated boxes at all sites, there was a > 90% decline in the Cs. melanura resting population at treated sites compared to untreated ones within 1 day post-treatment, an effect that continued to 7 days post-treatment, when the sampling was discontinued (Fig. 5).

Our second approach was to use individual boxes as the sampling units within sites, by sampling boxes before and after treatment, while retaining a set of boxes as untreated controls in both the before and after treatment intervals. This design revealed population changes from before to after treatment in a sampling series over time, and allowed the use of untreated boxes as a control on those changes reflected by samples from treated boxes. The results indicated a rapid, sustained decline in the population of Cs. melanura occurred after box treatment, but differed subtly (yet significantly) in samples from untreated and treated boxes, at both sites. The trend over time from before to after insecticide treatment indicated that the effect of treatment of a large number of boxes within the upland wooded “edge” habitat not only killed mosquitoes coming into contact with insecticide in treated boxes, but also caused a rapid and sustained reduction in local population abundance (Fig. 6). A rebound in Cs. melanura abundance in both treated and untreated box samples at the Hampton Creek site by day 38 post-treatment may have been due to both a population increase and a wane in the effect of the insecticide treatment. According to the manufacturer’s information, an outdoor application of microencapsulated lambda cyhalothrin for barrier treatment directed against resting mosquitoes on vegetation or other outdoor surfaces lasts minimally 30 days and as long as 90 days (Syngenta 2023). Our data were consistent with the estimated shorter interval, but more research on duration of insecticide treatment of inner surfaces of painted resting boxes is warranted.

The differential decline of the West Lake and Hampton Creek populations of Cs. melanura from the first two sampling dates (prior to treatment) followed in short order with sampling after treatment of treated and untreated boxes revealed two population processes (refer to Fig. 6). First, the observed rapid reduction in the number of mosquitoes in treated boxes revealed the direct toxicity effect of those treatments. Second, the slower reduction in counts over time in untreated boxes revealed a population level effect. If counts in untreated boxes had not changed from pre-treatment counts, then the logical inference would be that there was a toxic effect due to the boxes, but that the overall population abundance at the study sites was unaffected. However, the opposite was the case, noting as a caveat that the decline in counts in untreated boxes was slower by a lag of a few days compared to treated boxes, a difference substantial enough that the interaction terms of treatment and sampling date were significant for males and females at both sites. A likely explanation for the decline in population abundance as revealed by sampling treated and untreated boxes was that the boxes themselves outcompeted, and/or outnumbered, natural resting sites available within the study areas, resulting in high contact of Cs. melanura adults with insecticide-treated surfaces, followed by their death. Although we did not systematically attempt to locate and sample natural resting sites, we observed male and female Cs. melanura resting under downed trees and within brush piles. Another explanation is the repeated daily visits of Cs. melanura males and females to treated boxes due to the strong diel periodicity of resting behavior documented here, resulted in the rapid exposure to a large proportion of the natural population. Thus, the encounter rate of the mosquitoes in the natural population with toxic surfaces in resting boxes was likely high, despite availability of natural resting sites. Further work on relative utilization of natural resting sites compared to resting boxes could serve to illuminate this matter.
