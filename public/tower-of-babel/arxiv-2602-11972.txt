Splitting schemes for ODEs with goal–oriented error estimation
Erik Weyl, Andreas Bartel, Manuel Schaller

Abstract
We present a hybrid a–priori/a–posteriori goal oriented error estimator for a combination of dynamic iteration-based solution of ordinary differential equations discretized by finite elements. Our novel error estimator combines estimates from classical dynamic iteration methods, usually used to enable splitting–based distributed simulation, and from the dual weighted residual method to be able to evaluate and balance both, the dynamic iteration error and the discretization error in desired quantities of interest. The obtained error estimators are used to conduct refinements of the computational mesh and as a stopping criterion for the dynamic iteration. In particular, we allow for an adaptive and flexible discretization of the time domain, where variables can be discretized differently to match both goal and solution requirements, e.g. in view of multiple time scales. We endow the scheme with efficient solvers from numerical linear algebra to ensure its applicability to complex problems. Numerical experiments compare the adaptive approach to a uniform refinement.

Introduction

In many applications, ordinary differential equations (ODEs) are used to model the temporal evolution of dynamic processes. In numerous real–world phenomena, multiple subsystems of different nature interact; mathematically, this interaction is typically represented by coupling individual models. The resulting coupled systems often exhibit pronounced differences in time scales, degrees of nonlinearity, or dimensionality.

Dynamic iteration, see e.g.
Lelarasmee 1982
, is an approximation method that allows to numerically simulate these models by solving the subsystems in a decoupled, iterative manner. This enables the combination of dedicated solvers and facilitates the use of different time grids for the subsystems that respect the individual scales of the phenomena.

In this work, we derive error estimators for dynamic iteration methods coupled with finite element schemes that allow for quantification of the error in a user–specified Quantity of Interest (QoI). In fact, we seek for a number of dynamic iterations and an (as coarse as possible) time grid such that the numerical solution approximates the true solution up to a given accuracy when measured in the QoI. The error is thereby composed of two different sources, the dynamic iteration error, and the finite element discretization error.

As a first ingredient we apply adaptive finite elements (FE) with independent discretizations for all components of the state. In recent years, it has been shown that these methods are indeed equivalent to certain Runge–Kutta schemes
Muñoz Matute 2019
;
Muñoz-Matute
et al. 2019
. To estimate the error of the solution in a given QoI, and to perform adaptive refinements of the time grid, we use the well–established dual weighted residual (DWR) method
Becker and
Rannacher 2001
. Thereby, one solves an adjoint problem, which yields sensitivity factors for the impact of errors through the lens of the QoI. Since the seminal paper mentioned before, goal-oriented methods have been developed in various directions
Becker et al. 2000
;
Bruchhäuser 2022
;
Estep 1995
;
Meidner 2007
;
Grüne et al. 2022
. As a second component, we develop a goal oriented estimator for the error caused by splitting the problem based on dynamic iteration theory
Burrage 1995
.

We briefly provide an overview of relevant literature and relate it to our novelties.
The combination of multirate in time and DWR based error estimation and refinement has for instance been applied to coupled flow and transport problems
Bruchhäuser 2022
. Here, we employ the more general multiadaptive approach
Logg 2003
in which no similarity for grids of different solution components is proscribed. Moreover, the localized error estimators naturally lead to variable steps sizes for the components of the solution. Controlling the iteration and discretization error has been applied, e.g., in
Becker et al. 1995
, but from the perspective of using the iterative solvers for the discrete problem. Another approach to the goal oriented error estimation of discretization and iteration errors was presented in
Rannacher and
Vihharev 2013
. The difference to our approach is that there the problem was first discretized and the discrete problem solved iteratively. The resulting error estimator was based entirely on DWR theory. In
Chaudhry
et al. 2016
for instance, a two–stage approach was presented in which the error estimators obtained in a first coarse stage were used to design the fine mesh. The fine mesh construction is based on groups of time steps to exploit error cancellation. The application of the DWR method to the problems arising from dynamic iteration has been investigated before to obtain error estimators
Estep et al. 2012
;
Chaudhry
et al. 2013
;
Chaudhry
et al. 2015
.

In this work, by adopting a view of the dynamic iteration process as a single system of ODEs, we obtain a novel error estimator based on a holistic view of the method.

This paper proceeds as follows: the splitting scheme and our interpretation in the light of the DWR method are introduced in Section
2
. In Section
3
, we describe the multirate discretization of the split problems derived before. Subsequently, Section
4
deals with the implementation details and the balancing of error estimators for splitting and discretization. Numerical results are reported in Section
5
. Finally, a conclusion and further possible research direction are given in Section
6
.

2
Global view on dynamic iteration

In this work, we consider the following problem formulation: let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and time interval
\mathcal{I}:=\left[t_{0},t_{n}\right]\subset\mathbb{R}
be given. We seek
U\in\mathcal{U}:=H^{1}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{m}\right)
which satisfies the initial value problem

\begin{split}\dot{U}\left(t\right)+BU\left(t\right)&=Y\left(t\right),\quad t\in\mathcal{I}\\
U\left(t_{0}\right)&=U_{t_{0}},\end{split}

(1)

with given coefficient matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, continuous right–hand side
Y:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
and initial value
U_{t_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Furthermore, to streamline the presentation and to not hide the main novelties behind technical details, we consider a discrete quantity of interest (QoI)

J\left(U\right):=\sum_{r=1}^{R}J_{r}\cdot U\left(\tau_{r}\right)

(2)

with
R\in\mathbb{N}
time points of interest
\tau_{r}\in\mathcal{I}
and given weight vectors
J_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
for
r=1,\ldots,R
. We assume that
\tau_{R}=t_{n}
which can always be done w.l.o.g. by setting
J_{R}=0
.

Next, we introduce the splitting and dynamic iteration (Section
2.1
) and view the whole process of the iterations together as a stacked system (Section
2.2
). This novel view aims at providing the means to estimate the error in the QoI
J
and adaptively refine the discretization in order to achieve accurate results at low cost.

2.1
Construction of splitting schemes

For solving the full system (
1
), we use a dynamic iteration (DI) scheme to iteratively compute an approximation by solving subsystems. Dynamic iteration—also waveform relaxation—was first proposed in
Lelarasmee 1982
. It translates ideas of iterative methods for linear systems to systems of differential equations: some occurrences of the unknown
U_{i}
in (
1
) are replaced with a known guess
\check{U}_{i}
and then the resulting simplified problem is solved. One obtains an approximation
\hat{U}
to
U
. By
iteration, it is possible to construct a sequence, which convergences to the solution
U
, see
Burrage 1995
.

Given an initial waveform
U_{0}\in\mathcal{U}
satisfying
U_{0}\left(t_{0}\right)=U_{t_{0}}
, e.g.
U_{0}\equiv U_{t_{0}}
, a DI splitting scheme for the ODE initial value problem (
1
) may be constructed by choosing a splitting matrix
S\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{m\times m}
and defining

\displaystyle\hat{B}:=S\ast B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},

\displaystyle\check{B}:=B-\hat{B}

(3)

where
\ast
denotes elementwise multiplication. Then, for
k\geq 1
,
U_{k}
is defined from
U_{k-1}
as solution of the initial value problem

\begin{split}\dot{U}_{k}\left(t\right)+\hat{B}U_{k}\left(t\right)&=Y\left(t\right)-\check{B}U_{k-1}\left(t\right),\quad t\in\mathcal{I}\\
U_{k}\left(t_{0}\right)&=U_{t_{0}}.\end{split}

(4)

Notice, if the entry
s_{i,j}
of
S
is 0, the unknown
u_{k,j}
is replaced by the previous iterate
u_{k-1,j}
in
i
–th equation of (
4
). Therefore,
S=1^{m\times m}
corresponds to the original, fully coupled Setting (
1
). Prominent splitting schemes are:

Jacobi splitting.

Let
S=I
. In this case, during each solve, the equations are decoupled from one another making it possible to solve them all in parallel.

Gauß–Seidel splitting.

Let
S
be lower triangular. This yields a sequential scheme where new results are immediately used for the computation of the next equation.

Further splitting methods as block splitting, Picard iteration, underrelaxation and overlapping can be also encoded via a splitting matrix
S
and may be used in our proposed framework. In particular, overlapping can improve convergence rates
Jeltsch and Pohl 1995
.

Considering the split model (
4
) instead of (
1
) results in a splitting error

E_{k}\left(t\right):=U\left(t\right)-U_{k}\left(t\right).

(5)

The following result provides an estimate of the splitting error in the QoI and hence provides a goal oriented perspective. By
\left\|\cdot\right\|
, we denote the Euclidean vector norm and the induced matrix norm.

Proposition 1
.

We consider problem (
1
). Given a splitting matrix
S
with split model (
4
), then
the error
E_{K}
(
5
) after
K
iterations
leads to an error in the QoI (
2
)
J\left(E_{K}\right)
, which is bounded as follows

\nu:=\left|J\left(E_{K}\right)\right|\leq\left(-\frac{L_{2}}{L_{1}}\right)^{K}\sup_{t\in\mathcal{I}}\left(\left\|E^{\left(0\right)}\right\|\right)\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left\|J_{r}\right\|\left(1-e^{L_{1}t_{r}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{\left(-L_{1}t_{r}\right)^{k}}{k!}\right)

(6)

with
L_{1}=\mu_{\max}\left(\frac{\hat{B}+\hat{B}^{T}}{2}\right)
,

L_{2}=\left\|\check{B}\right\|
.

Proof.

Using the triangle and Cauchy–Schwarz inequalities, we get

\left|J\left(E_{K}\right)\right|=\left|\sum_{r=1}^{R}J_{r}\cdot E_{K}\left(t_{r}\right)\right|\leq\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left|J_{r}\cdot E_{K}\left(t_{r}\right)\right|\leq\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left\|J_{r}\right\|\left\|E_{K}\left(t_{r}\right)\right\|.

(7)

It now remains to bound
\left\|E_{K}\left(t_{r}\right)\right\|
. Thereby, we rewrite (
4
) as

\dot{U}_{k}\left(t\right)=F\left(U_{k},U_{k-1},t\right):=Y\left(t\right)-\hat{B}U_{k}\left(t\right)-\check{B}U_{k-1}\left(t\right)

and note that the right hand side
F
satisfies the following Lipschitz conditions:

\displaystyle\left(F\left(U_{a},\cdot,\cdot\right)-F\left(U_{b},\cdot,\cdot\right)\right)\cdot\left(U_{a}-U_{b}\right)

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}\left\|U_{a}-U_{b}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\left\|F\left(\cdot,U_{a},\cdot\right)-F\left(\cdot,U_{b},\cdot\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{2}\left\|U_{a}-U_{b}\right\|

The constants
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
in this case are the logarithmic norm of
\hat{B}
and the matrix norm of
L_{2}
respectively, as given above.

Using
(
Burrage 1995
, Theorem 7.9.3)
, we have for the error in the dynamic iteration

\sup_{t\in\left[t_{0},t_{r}\right]}\left(\left\|E^{\left(k\right)}\left(t\right)\right\|\right)\leq\left(-\frac{L_{2}}{L_{1}}\right)^{k}\left(1-e^{L_{1}t_{r}}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{\left(-L_{1}t_{r}\right)^{j}}{j!}\right)\sup_{t\in\left[t_{0},t_{r}\right]}\left(\left\|E^{\left(0\right)}\right\|\right).

We can use this bound in (
7
) for each
t_{r}
.
Due to
\left[t_{0},t_{r}\right]\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, we have

\sup_{t\in\left[t_{0},t_{r}\right]}\left(\left\|E^{\left(k\right)}\left(t\right)\right\|\right)\leq\sup_{t\in\mathcal{I}}\left(\left\|E^{\left(k\right)}\left(t\right)\right\|\right),

which gives the stated error bound (
6
).
∎

Remark 1
.

(i) In case of a dissipative system, i.e.
L_{1}<0
, we have

1-e^{L_{1}t_{r}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{\left(-L_{1}t_{r}\right)^{k}}{k!}=e^{L_{1}t_{r}}\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\frac{\left(-L_{1}t_{r}\right)^{j}}{j!}\leq e^{L_{1}t_{r}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(-L_{1}t_{r}\right)^{j}}{j!}=1.

If we additionally have
-L_{1}>L_{2}
, then (
6
) implies that the dynamic iteration is contractive. More precisely, by choosing
S
such that the coupling ratio
\left|\frac{L_{2}}{L_{1}}\right|
is minimized, the speed of convergence can be improved.

(ii) In the more general case where
L_{1}
may be positive, it is still possible to obtain proof of asymptotic convergence
(
Burrage 1995
, Theorem 7.9.2)
. However, in practice the error may blow up beyond the maximum machine number during early iterations
Bartel et al. 2023
.

In practice, the number of iterations is limited. In this work, we aim to balance the splitting error estimator with the discretization error estimator introduced below. The details are given in Section
4
(there, we also use the abbreviation
\nu
for the splitting error in
J
).

2.2
A holistic variational formulation for dynamic iteration

For a total number of dynamic iteration steps
K\in\mathbb{N}
, we stack the respective ODEs into a single system:

\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}\dot{U}_{1}\left(t\right)\\
\dot{U}_{2}\left(t\right)\\
\vdots\\
\dot{U}_{K-1}\left(t\right)\\
\dot{U}_{K}\left(t\right)\end{array}\right)+\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\hat{B}&0&\ldots&0&0\\
\check{B}&\hat{B}&\ddots&&0\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\hat{B}&0\\
0&\ldots&0&\check{B}&\hat{B}\end{array}\right]\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}U_{1}\left(t\right)\\
U_{2}\left(t\right)\\
\vdots\\
U_{K-1}\left(t\right)\\
U_{K}\left(t\right)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c@{}l}Y\left(t\right)&-\check{B}U_{0}\left(t\right)\\
Y\left(t\right)&\\
\vdots&\\
Y\left(t\right)&\\
Y\left(t\right)&\end{array}\right)

(8)

with initial waveform
U_{0}\left(t\right)
.
This allows for a more rigorous derivation of the DWR based error estimators later (Section
2.3
). Additionally, it makes the flow of information — forward through iterations for the primal problem and backward in inverse order for the dual — more transparent. It also highlights the influence of the initial waveform. On the other hand, for the practical evaluation of the variational problem, it is better to return to the individual steps.

We rewrite (
8
) more compactly as

\mathbf{\dot{U}}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)+\mathbf{B}^{\left(K\right)}\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)=\mathbf{Y}^{\left(K\right)}\left(U_{0};t\right)

(9)

with initial value

\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t_{0}\right)=\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}_{t_{0}}:=\left(U_{t_{0}},\ldots,U_{t_{0}}\right)^{\top}.

For the goal functional, we apply the QoI defined in (
2
) to the final result, discarding all previous ones:

\mathbf{J}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}\right):=J\left(U_{K}\right).

To obtain a weak formulation of (
8
), we consider the test space
\mathcal{V}\subseteq L^{\infty}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{m}\right)
, use stacked solutions

\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{\left(K\right)}:=\mathcal{U}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{U}
and stacked test functions
\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{V}}^{\left(K\right)}:=\mathcal{V}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{V}
to define

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{\left(K\right)}\!\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)},\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\!:=\!\!\int_{\mathcal{I}}\!\!\left(\mathbf{\dot{U}}^{\left(K\right)}\!\left(t\right)+\mathbf{B}^{\left(K\right)}\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}\!\left(t\right)\right)\cdot\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\textnormal{d}t+\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t_{0}\right)\cdot\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}^{\left(K\right)}\!\left(U_{0};\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\!:=\!\!\int_{\mathcal{I}}\!\!\mathbf{Y}^{\left(K\right)}\left(U_{0};t\right)\cdot\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\textnormal{d}t+\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}_{t_{0}}\cdot\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t_{0}\right).

With these definitions, we have:

Stacked dual variational problem.

Given the initial value problem
(
1
)
.
Let an initial waveform
U_{0}\in\mathcal{U}
be given which satisfies the initial condition in (
1
).
Then, the stacked variational problem is to find
\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{\left(K\right)}
such that for every test function
\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{V}}^{\left(K\right)}
holds

\mathbf{f}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)},\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\right)=\mathbf{g}^{\left(K\right)}\left(U_{0};\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\right).

(10)

This stacked view now provides the basis for the arguments in the following section.

2.3
Dual weighted residual method

Using the variational formulation (
10
), we derive a goal oriented error estimator for the dynamic iteration method (
8
) based on the dual weighted residual (DWR) method
Becker and
Rannacher 2001
.

We briefly provide the derivation, which is standard for goal oriented refinements
Becker and
Rannacher 2001
. To this end, we reinterpret the variational problem as the trivial constrained optimization problem to minimize the quantity of interest
\mathbf{J}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}\right)
for
\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{\left(K\right)}
subject to (
10
). The solution to this problem can be determined by finding a stationary point
\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}_{s},\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}_{s}\right)\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{\left(K\right)}\times\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{\left(K\right)}
of the Lagrangian function

L\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\right)=\mathbf{J}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}\right)+\mathbf{g}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\right)-\mathbf{f}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\right).

Consequently, first–order optimality condition is given by the two equations

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}}\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\right)\right|_{\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}=\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\right)-\mathbf{f}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)},\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\right)=0,

(11)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}}\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\right)\right|_{\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}=\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{J}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)}\right)-\mathbf{f}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\right)=0

(12)

holding for all
\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{\left(K\right)}
and
\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{\left(K\right)}
. We note that (
11
) is the (primal) variational equation (
10
) while (
12
) is the adjoint (or dual) equation for the adjoint (or dual) solution
\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}
.

Stacked dual variational problem.

Find
\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{\left(K\right)}
such that for all
\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{\left(K\right)}
holds

\mathbf{f}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\right)=\mathbf{J}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)}\right)

(13)

with

\mathbf{f}^{\left(K\right)}\!\left(\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)}\!,\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\right)\!:=\!\!\int_{\mathcal{I}}\!\!\left(\mathbf{\dot{W}}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)+\mathbf{B}^{\left(K\right)}\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\right)\cdot\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\!\left(t\right)\textnormal{d}t+\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t_{0}\right)\cdot\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t_{0}\right).

Using integration by parts, we have for
\mathbf{f}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\right)

=\int_{\mathcal{I}}\left(-\mathbf{\dot{Z}}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)+{\mathbf{B}^{\left(K\right)}}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\right)\cdot\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\textnormal{d}t+\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t_{n}\right)\cdot\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t_{n}\right).

This shows that the adjoint problem has a terminal condition given by the weight
J_{R}
at the end of
\mathcal{I}
: specifically
Z\left(t_{n}\right)=J_{R}
. Furthermore since the matrix
\mathbf{B}^{\left(K\right)}
is transposed, the dynamic iteration steps are solved in reverse order.

In practice, we solve discrete versions of (
11
) and (
12
) with solutions in a finite–dimensional subspace. For these approximations, we have the following goal oriented error bound
(
Becker and
Rannacher 2001
, Proposition 2.2)
:

Proposition 2
.

Consider the weakly formulated primal and dual problems (
11
) and (
12
) and their respective analytic solutions
\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}
and
\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}
. Let
\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}
be the solution to a discretized version of the primal problem. Then, the error in the quantity of interest,

\mathbf{J}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{E}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right):=\mathbf{J}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}\right)-\mathbf{J}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right)\leq\mu^{{\Delta t}}

(14)

is bounded from above for any
\mathbf{V}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}
with

\mu^{{\Delta t}}\!:=\!\!\int_{\mathcal{I}}\!\!\left(\mathbf{Y}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\!-\!\mathbf{A}^{\left(K\right)}\mathbf{\dot{U}}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\!-\!\mathbf{B}^{\left(K\right)}\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\right)\!\cdot\!\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\!-\!\mathbf{V}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\right)\textnormal{d}t.

(15)

Proof.

The proposition is proved by
(
Becker and
Rannacher 2001
, Propositions 2.2 and 2.3)
, noting that both the left hand side
\mathbf{f}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
and the right hand side
\mathbf{g}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\cdot\right)
are linear.
In Appendix
7
, the proof specifically for our case is presented.
∎

To make the error estimator in (
15
) sharp,
\mathbf{V}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}
has to be a good approximation to
\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}
. In practice, we use the discrete adjoint result
\mathbf{Z}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}
as
\mathbf{V}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}
. Since the exact adjoint
\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}
is generally unknown, we approximate the adjoint error by the difference between two different interpolations (e.g. linear and quadratic) of
\mathbf{Z}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}
.

2.4
Decoupling

The presented
holistic
formulation (
10
) of variational problem (
8
) is useful for the derivation of the DWR method. Of course, in practice, the iteration steps are usually solved sequentially. More precisely, we may use the block–triangular structure

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{\left(K\right)}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\hat{B}&0&\ldots&0&0\\
\check{B}&\hat{B}&\ddots&0&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ddots&\hat{B}&0\\
0&0&\ldots&\check{B}&\hat{B}\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle{\mathbf{B}^{\left(K\right)}}^{\top}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\hat{B}^{\top}&\check{B}^{\top}&\ldots&0&0\\
0&\hat{B}^{\top}&\ddots&0&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ddots&\hat{B}^{\top}&\check{B}^{\top}\\
0&0&\ldots&0&\hat{B}^{\top}\end{array}\right]

to decouple the unified variational formulations: We define for
U\in\mathcal{U}
and
V\in\mathcal{V}
:

\displaystyle\hat{f}\left(U,V\right)

\displaystyle:=\int\mathcal{I}\left(\dot{U}\left(t\right)+\hat{B}U\left(t\right)\right)\cdot V\left(t\right)\textnormal{d}t+U\left(t_{0}\right)\cdot V\left(t_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{I}}\left(-\dot{V}\left(t\right)+\hat{B}^{\top}V\left(t\right)\right)\cdot U\left(t\right)\textnormal{d}t+V\left(t_{n}\right)\cdot U\left(t_{n}\right),

\displaystyle\check{f}\left(U,V\right)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{I}}\check{B}U\left(t\right)\cdot V\left(t\right)\textnormal{d}t\quad=\quad\int_{\mathcal{I}}\check{B}^{\top}V\left(t\right)\cdot\!U\left(t\right)\textnormal{d}t,

\displaystyle g\left(V\right)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{I}}Y\left(t\right)\cdot V\left(t\right)\textnormal{d}t+U_{t_{0}}\!\cdot V\left(t_{0}\right),\quad\;h\left(U\right):=\int_{\mathcal{I}}J_{1}^{\top}\left(t\right)\cdot U\left(t\right)+J_{2}^{\top}\!\cdot\!U\left(t_{n}\right).

Then, the primal problem reads: find
U_{k}\in\mathcal{U}
such that for all
V\in\mathcal{V}
holds

\hat{f}\left(U_{k},V\right)=g\left(V\right)-\check{f}(U_{k-1},V)\quad(k=1,\ldots,K).

(16)

The dual problem proceeds in reverse order,
k=K,\ldots,1
, and backward in time; this coincides with
Estep et al. 2012
. Thus, we seek
Z_{k}\in\mathcal{V}
such that for all
W\in\mathcal{U}
holds

\hat{f}\left(Z_{k},W\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}h\left(W\right)&\textnormal{ for }k=K,\\
-\check{f}\left(W,Z_{k+1}\right)&\textnormal{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(17)

Discretization

We discuss the discretization of the variational problems (
10
) and (

) via finite dimensional trial and test spaces
\mathcal{U}^{{\Delta t}}
and
\mathcal{V}^{{\Delta t}}

3.1
Multiadaptive discretization schemes

Many naturally occurring problems exhibit multirate behavior, where the components operate on time scales, which differ drastically. This occurs frequently in electrical circuit simulation where the activity in concentrated on a subset of components
Bartel and
Günther 2002
. Another example is in astrophysics where Keplerian motion results in significant velocity differences between orbiting bodies
Engstler and
Lubich 1997
. To efficiently address this, different discretizations tailored to the frequencies of the respective components should be used.

The term
multiadaptive
originates from
Logg 2003
;
Logg 2004
, where it is used to describe methods that allowed each component to have its own time grid, order of method and quadrature. In this work, to concisely illustrate the core of our idea, we restrict ourselves to the first of these three features, while including the other features is straightforward and left for future research.

For each component
i=1,\ldots,m
of the state solving the ODE (
1
), we partition the simulation time interval
\mathcal{I}=[t_{0},t_{n}]
into
n_{i}
cells parametrized by the nodes

t_{0}=t_{i,0}<t_{i,1}<\ldots<t_{i,n_{i}-1}<t_{i,n_{i}}=t_{n}.

Let
N:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}n_{i}
be the total number of cells. For
j=1,\ldots,n_{i}
, we define

\mathcal{I}_{i,j}:=\left[t_{i,j-1},t_{i,j}\right[\quad\text{with}\quad|\mathcal{I}_{i,j}|:=t_{i,j}-t_{i,j-1}.

And for notational ease, we use:

\mathcal{I}_{i,0}=\left]-\infty,t_{t_{i,0}}\right[,\qquad\mathcal{I}_{i,n_{i}+1}=\left[t_{i,n_{i}},\infty\right[.

Furthermore, we define the sets of admissible indices

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle:=\left\{\left(i,j\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\;|\;1\leq i\leq m,\;1\leq j\leq n_{i}\right\},\quad I_{0}:=I\cup\left\{\left(i,0\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\;|\;1\leq i\leq m\right\}.

For the sake of ease and clarity, we will use the same discretization for all dynamic iteration steps (
k
) in one sweep.
This has several practical benefits: as shown later in Proposition
3
, it allows the reuse of computed discrete systems across dynamic iterations; secondly, it also allows to compute error estimators for variable numbers of iteration steps. Consequently, we can dynamically adjust the number of dynamic iteration steps before grid refinements. In principle, our framework does allow using different grids in each step if this is needed.

Now, we introduce three options for the finite dimensional space of the individual components
i=1,\ldots,m
of the state:

(a)

piecewise constant functions:

\mathcal{U}_{a,i}^{{\Delta t}}:=\Bigl\{u\;\Bigl|\;\;\forall j=1,\ldots,n_{i}+1:\;u\bigl|_{\mathcal{I}_{i,j}}\equiv\textnormal{const}\}

with respective indicator functions
\phi_{i,j}
as basis:

\begin{array}[]{r@{}l@{\quad}ll}\phi_{i,0}\left(t\right)&:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&t\in\left]-\infty,t_{i,1}\right[\\
0&\textnormal{ otherwise}\end{array}\right.&\dot{\phi}_{i,0}\left(t\right)\!=-\delta\left(t-t_{i,1}\right)&\\[1.07639pt]
\phi_{i,j}\left(t\right)&:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&t\in\mathcal{I}_{i,j}\\
0&\textnormal{ otherwise}\end{array}\right.&\dot{\phi}_{i,0}\!\left(t\right)\!=\!\delta\left(t\!-\!t_{i,j-1}\right)-\delta\left(t\!-\!t_{i,j}\right),&j\!=\!1,\ldots,n_{i}\!-\!1\\[1.07639pt]
\phi_{i,n_{i}}\!\left(t\right)&:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&t\in\left[t_{i,n_{i}},\infty\right[\\
0&\textnormal{ otherwise}\end{array}\right.&\dot{\phi}_{i,0}\left(t\right)=\delta\left(t-t_{i,n_{i}}\right);&\end{array}

(b)

as a slight modification of (a), we have
\mathcal{U}_{b,i}^{{\Delta t}}
defined as in (a) just the cells are minutely modified
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i,j}:=\left]t_{i,j-1},t_{i,j}\right]
; this could be considered a sort of dual counterpart to
\mathcal{U}_{a,i}^{{\Delta t}}
, where we start at
t_{n}
and go backward in time from there.

(c)

piecewise linear and globally continuous functions

\mathcal{U}_{c,i}^{{\Delta t}}:=\left\{u\in\mathcal{C}^{0}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}\right)\;\Bigl|\;\forall j=1,\ldots,n_{i}:u\bigl|_{\mathcal{I}_{i,j}}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}\left(\mathcal{I}_{i,j}\right)\right\},

where
\mathcal{P}_{1}\left(R\right)
are polynomial of maximal degree one on the domain
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}
; suitable basis functions are the hat functions (for
j=0,\ldots,n_{i}
):

\phi_{i,j}\left(t\right):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\frac{t-t_{i,j-1}}{{\Delta t}_{i,j-1}}&t\in\mathcal{I}_{i,j-1}\;\textnormal{ and }\;j\geq 1,\\[1.07639pt]
\frac{t_{i,j+1}-t}{{\Delta t}_{i,j}}&t\in\mathcal{I}_{i,j}\;\textnormal{ and }\;j\leq n_{i}-1,\\[1.07639pt]
0&\textnormal{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

For
x\in\left\{a,b,c\right\}
, the overall spaces (for all components) are then given by

\mathcal{U}_{x}^{{\Delta t}}:=\left\{U=\left(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\right)\;|\;\forall i=1,\ldots,m:u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{x,i}^{{\Delta t}}\right\}

with the basis functions
\Phi_{i,j}\left(t\right):=e_{i}\phi_{i,j}\left(t\right)
for
\left(i,j\right)\in I_{0}
and canonical basis vector
e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Different FE schemes now arise by choosing
\mathcal{U}^{{\Delta t}}
and
\mathcal{V}^{{\Delta t}}
from
\mathcal{U}_{a}^{{\Delta t}}
,
\mathcal{U}_{b}^{{\Delta t}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{c}^{{\Delta t}}
. Of particular interest to us are the choices
\mathcal{U}^{{\Delta t}}:=\mathcal{U}_{a}^{{\Delta t}}
and
\mathcal{V}^{{\Delta t}}:=\mathcal{U}_{b}^{{\Delta t}}
yields a time–stepping scheme equivalent to the explicit Euler method as well as
\mathcal{U}^{{\Delta t}}:=\mathcal{U}_{c}^{{\Delta t}}
and
\mathcal{V}^{{\Delta t}}:=\mathcal{U}_{b}^{{\Delta t}}
for the Crank–Nicolson scheme. Of course, other options are also possible.

3.2
Solution of the discretized problem

Here, we consider one fixed step
k
(
\in
{1, …, K}
)
of dynamic iteration step.
We seek an approximation
U_{k}^{{\Delta t}}
to the analytic solution
U_{k}
of (
16
):

U_{k}\left(t\right)\approx U_{k}^{{\Delta t}}\left(t\right):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{n_{i}}u_{k,i,j}^{{\Delta t}}\cdot\Phi_{i,j}\left(t\right).

(18)

Likewise, the approximation of the solution of the adjoint equation (
13
) is given by

Z_{k}\left(t\right)\approx Z_{k}t^{{\Delta t}}\left(t\right):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{n_{i}}z_{k,i,j}^{{\Delta t}}\cdot\Phi_{i,j}\left(t\right).

(19)

The unknown coefficients
u_{k,i,j}^{{\Delta t}}
and
z_{k,i,j}^{{\Delta t}}
for
\left(i,j\right)\in I_{0}
are obtained by solving by solving the finite–dimensional discrete primal and dual variational problems.

Discrete primal formulation.

The discrete primal variational problem associated with the ODE (
16
) is to find coefficients
u_{k,i,j}^{{\Delta t}}
for
\left(i,j\right)\in I_{0}
such that for all
\Phi_{\ddot{\imath},\ddot{\jmath}}\in\mathcal{V}^{{\Delta t}}
holds

\hat{f}\left(U_{k}^{{\Delta t}},\Phi_{\ddot{\imath},\ddot{\jmath}}\right)=g\left(\Phi_{\ddot{\imath},\ddot{\jmath}}\right)-\check{f}\left(U_{k-1}^{{\Delta t}},V^{{\Delta t}}\right).

(20)

Using the ansatz for
U_{k}^{{\Delta t}}
(
18
) and linearity, we can write the linear system (
20
) as:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\hat{f}\left(\Phi_{1,0},\Phi_{1,0}\right)&\ldots&\hat{f}\left(\Phi_{m,n_{m}},\Phi_{1,0}\right)\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\hat{f}\left(\Phi_{1,0},\Phi_{m,n_{m}}\right)&\ldots&\hat{f}\left(\Phi_{m,n_{m}},\Phi_{m,n_{m}}\right)\end{array}\right]}_{\hat{F}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u_{k,1,0}^{{\Delta t}}\\
\vdots\\
u_{k,m,n_{m}}^{{\Delta t}}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}g\left(\Phi_{1,0}\right)\\
\vdots\\
g\left(\Phi_{m,n_{m}}\right)\end{array}\right)}_{G}-

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\check{f}\left(\Phi_{1,0},\Phi_{1,0}\right)&\ldots&\check{f}\left(\Phi_{m,n_{m}},\Phi_{1,0}\right)\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\check{f}\left(\Phi_{1,0},\Phi_{m,n_{m}}\right)&\ldots&\check{f}\left(\Phi_{m,n_{m}},\Phi_{m,n_{m}}\right)\end{array}\right]}_{\check{F}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u_{k-1,1,0}^{{\Delta t}}\\
\vdots\\
u_{k-1,m,n_{m}}^{{\Delta t}}\end{array}\right).

Discrete dual formulation.

The discrete adjoint variational problem corresponding to the adjoint problem (
13
) is to find coefficients
z_{k,i,j}^{{\Delta t}}
for
\left(i,j\right)\in I_{0}
such that for all
\Phi_{i,j}\in\mathcal{U}^{{\Delta t}}
holds

\hat{f}\left(\Phi_{i,j},Z_{k}^{{\Delta t}}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}h\left(\Phi_{i,j}\right)&\textnormal{ if }k=K,\\
-\check{f}\left(\Phi_{i,j},Z_{k+1}^{{\Delta t}}\right)&\textnormal{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(21)

By definition of
Z_{k}^{{\Delta t}}
in (
19
) and linearity, we obtain the system of equations for the discrete adjoint problem. for
k=K
, we get:

\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\hat{f}\left(\Phi_{1,0},\Phi_{1,0}\right)&\ldots&\hat{f}\left(\Phi_{1,0},\Phi_{m,n_{m}}\right)\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\hat{f}\left(\Phi_{n,n_{m}},\Phi_{1,0}\right)&\ldots&\hat{f}\left(\Phi_{m,n_{m}},\Phi_{m,n_{m}}\right)\end{array}\right]}_{\hat{F}^{\top}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}z_{k,1,0}^{{\Delta t}}\\
\vdots\\
z_{k,m,n_{m}}^{{\Delta t}}\end{array}\right)=\underbrace{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}h\left(\Phi_{1,0}\right)\\
\vdots\\
h\left(\Phi_{m,n_{m}}\right)\end{array}\right)}_{H}

and for
k=K-1,\ldots,1
:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\hat{f}\left(\Phi_{1,0},\Phi_{1,0}\right)&\ldots&\hat{f}\left(\Phi_{1,0},\Phi_{m,n_{m}}\right)\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\hat{f}\left(\Phi_{n,n_{m}},\Phi_{1,0}\right)&\ldots&\hat{f}\left(\Phi_{m,n_{m}},\Phi_{m,n_{m}}\right)\end{array}\right]}_{\hat{F}^{\top}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}z_{k,1,0}^{{\Delta t}}\\
\vdots\\
z_{k,m,n_{m}}^{{\Delta t}}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=-

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\check{f}\left(\Phi_{1,0},\Phi_{1,0}\right)&\ldots&\check{f}\left(\Phi_{1,0},\Phi_{m,n_{m}}\right)\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\check{f}\left(\Phi_{n,n_{m}},\Phi_{1,0}\right)&\ldots&\check{f}\left(\Phi_{m,n_{m}},\Phi_{m,n_{m}}\right)\end{array}\right]}_{\check{F}^{\top}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}z_{k+1,1,0}^{{\Delta t}}\\
\vdots\\
z_{k+1,m,n_{m}}^{{\Delta t}}\end{array}\right).

Proposition 3
.

Given
\left(i,j\right),\left(\ddot{\imath},\ddot{\jmath}\right)\in I_{0}
,
the entries of
\hat{F}
,
\check{F}
,
G
and

read:

\displaystyle\hat{f}\left(\Phi_{\ddot{\imath},\ddot{\jmath}},\Phi_{i,j}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{I}}\left(\delta_{\ddot{\imath},i}\dot{\phi}_{\ddot{\imath},\ddot{\jmath}}\left(t\right)+\hat{b}_{i\ddot{\imath}}\phi_{\ddot{\imath},\ddot{\jmath}}\left(t\right)\right)\phi_{i,j}\left(t\right)\textnormal{d}t+\delta_{\ddot{\imath},i}\delta_{\ddot{\jmath},0}\delta_{j,0},

\displaystyle\check{f}\left(\Phi_{\ddot{\imath},\ddot{\jmath}},\Phi_{i,j}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{I}}\left(\delta_{\ddot{\imath},i}\dot{\phi}_{\ddot{\imath},\ddot{\jmath}}\left(t\right)+\check{b}_{i\ddot{\imath}}\phi_{\ddot{\imath},\ddot{\jmath}}\left(t\right)\right)\phi_{i,j}\left(t\right)\textnormal{d}t+\delta_{\ddot{\imath},i}\delta_{\ddot{\jmath},0}\delta_{j,0},

\displaystyle g\left(\Phi_{i,j}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{I}}y_{i}\left(t\right)\phi_{i,j}\left(t\right)\textnormal{d}t+\delta_{j,0}u_{t_{0},i},\qquad h\left(\Phi_{\ddot{\imath},\ddot{\jmath}}\right)=\sum_{r=1}^{R}j_{t,\ddot{\imath}}\phi_{\ddot{\imath},\ddot{\jmath}}\left(t_{r}\right).

Proof.

This follows from inserting the appropriate
\Phi_{i,j}=e_{i}\phi_{i,j}
into the functions
\hat{f}
,
\check{f}
,
g
and

as defined in Section
2.4
.
∎

Remark 2
.

Note that
\hat{F}
,
\check{F}
,
G
and
H
do not depend on the dynamic iteration index
k
, hence they need to be computed only once in terms of the basis functions. The right hand sides

an

include the more general functions
Y
and

respectively and so must be approximated using quadrature. In this case, it is advantageous to use an approximation of the integral that is consistent with the left hand side.

4
Goal oriented mesh refinement

We describe the computation of the error estimators and the resulting mesh refinement for the DI method. First, we provide an overview of the proposed error estimation and refinement method.
To this end, we refer to the meshes described in Section
3.1
as
\mathbb{T}^{l}
with the index
l
denoting the refinement level. Thus, from the initially given mesh
\mathbb{T}^{0}
, we obtain iteratively finer meshes using error estimators from the previous level.

We estimate the error as follows:

\left|J\left(U\right)-J\left(U_{K_{l}}^{{\Delta t}}\right)\right|\leq\left|J\left(U\right)-J\left(U_{K_{l}}\right)\right|+\left|J_{K_{l}}\left(U\right)-J\left(U_{K_{l}}^{{\Delta t}}\right)\right|\leq\nu_{l}+\mu_{K_{l}}^{{\Delta t}_{l}}.

Here,
\nu_{l}
and
\mu^{{\Delta t}_{l}}
are estimates of the splitting and discretization errors, respectively. We already have a bound for
\nu_{l}
in Proposition
1
. The basis for evaluating
\mu^{{\Delta t}_{l}}
is provided in Proposition
2
; and in the following, we discuss how to obtain the localized error estimators efficiently.

4.1
Local goal oriented discretization error estimators

The upper bound of the total error
\mathbf{J}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{E}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right)=J\left(E_{k}^{{\Delta t}}\right)
is given by (
14
) and can be split as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{I}}\left(\mathbf{Y}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)-\mathbf{\dot{U}}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)-\mathbf{B}^{\left(K\right)}\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)-\mathbf{Z}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\right)dt

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i,j}}\underbrace{\left(y_{i}\left(t\right)-\dot{u}_{k,i}^{{\Delta t}}\left(t\right)-\hat{B}_{i\cdot}\cdot U_{k}^{{\Delta t}}\left(t\right)-\check{B}_{i\cdot}\cdot U_{k-1}^{{\Delta t}}\left(t\right)\right)}_{\rho_{i}\left(U_{k}^{{\Delta t}},U_{k-1}^{{\Delta t}},t\right)}\underbrace{\left(z_{i}\left(t\right)-z_{k,i}^{{\Delta t}}\left(t\right)\right)}_{e_{k,i}^{*}\left(t\right)}dt.

This partition would allow to compute error estimators for every time step
j
in every component
i
in every DI step
k
. However, since we want to use the same grid for all
k
, we add up the errors over the iteration index
k
for each
\left(i,j\right)\in I
. If a cell is then refined, that refinement effects all iteration steps
k
.

\mu_{i,j}^{{\Delta t}}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i,j}}\rho_{i}\left(U_{k}^{{\Delta t}},U_{k-1}^{{\Delta t}},t\right)e_{k,i}^{*}\left(t\right)dt\right|.

(23)

The use of absolute values is to prevent cancellation effects between the terms for different
k
.
Then, a numerical estimator of the total
J
error is given by

\mu^{{\Delta t}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\mu_{i,j}^{{\Delta t}}.

Remark 3
.

(i)

Note that due to the approximate nature of the computations there is no guarantee that it is actually an upper bound for the error in
J
. However, it can still be useful to guide the refinement.

(ii)

In (
23
), we use the unknown exact solution
z_{i}\left(t\right)
of the adjoint problem which is generally unknown. Therefore, we use different approximations of
z_{i}
in place of the term
\left(z_{i}\left(t\right)-z_{k,i}^{{\Delta t}}\left(t\right)\right)
(see below and cf.
(
Becker and
Rannacher 2001
, Section 5.1)
).

Efficient computation of the adjoint and error estimators.

In theory, adding another DI step changes the stacked problem (
10
) and thus would require evaluating the new adjoint problems for all
\tilde{k}=k,\ldots,1
. However, this can be circumvented by exploiting the structure of the discrete adjoint problem
3.2
. Suppose we have made
K
steps in the process and have solved the discrete adjoint problems

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{T}Z_{K}^{{\Delta t}}

\displaystyle=H,

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{T}Z_{k}^{{\Delta t}}

\displaystyle=-\check{F}^{\top}Z_{k+1}^{{\Delta t}},\qquad k=K-1,\ldots,1.

For the next iteration step, we would have to solve

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{T}\tilde{Z}_{K+1}^{{\Delta t}}

\displaystyle=H,

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{T}\tilde{Z}_{k}^{{\Delta t}}

\displaystyle=-\check{F}^{\top}\tilde{Z}_{k+1}^{{\Delta t}},\qquad k=K,\ldots,1.

By induction, it is easy to see that for
k=K+1,\ldots,2
, we have
\tilde{Z}_{k}^{{\Delta t}}=Z_{k-1}^{{\Delta t}}
. Of the new adjoints, only
\tilde{Z}_{1}^{{\Delta t}}
actually has to be computed.

Furthermore, note from (
22
) that each
\mu_{i,j,k}^{{\Delta t}}
is the integral of a product between the primal residual and the estimate of the dual error. The former only depends on
U^{{\Delta t}}
, the latter only on
Z^{{\Delta t}}
. When approximating the integrals using quadrature, we obtain a formula like

\int_{T_{i,j}}\rho_{i}\left(U_{k}^{{\Delta t}}\left(t\right)\right)e_{i,k}^{*^{{\Delta t}}}\left(t\right)\textnormal{d}t\approx\sum_{j=0}^{n_{i}}c_{j}\rho_{i}\left(U_{k}^{{\Delta t}}\left(t_{i,j}\right)\right)e_{i,k}^{*^{{\Delta t}}}\left(t_{i,j}\right)

with coefficients
c_{j}
. The terms
\rho_{i}\left(U_{k}^{{\Delta t}}\left(t_{i,j}\right)\right)
and
e_{i,k}^{*^{{\Delta t}}}\left(t_{i,j}\right)
only need to be evaluated once and from then, the quadrature coefficients can be computed by multiplying the appropriate terms.

Now, we have all components needed to describe the full goal oriented refinement strategy, which is presented in Algorithm
1
. Fundamentally, it consists of two nested loops:

•

an outer loop over the refinement levels,
l=0,\ldots,L
, with predefined maximal number of refinements
L
;

•

an inner loop over the DI steps on each refinement level for
k=1,\ldots,K_{l}
with
K_{l}
bounded by a given
K_{\max}
and a stopping criterion.

For the stopping criterion of the inner loop, we follow
Rannacher and
Vihharev 2013
and seek to balance

\mu^{{\Delta t}_{l}}\approx\nu_{l}.

Since additional DI steps will mostly decrease
\nu_{K_{l}}
, we are specifically looking for
\geq
in this relation.

After an inner loop, the grid is refined by bisecting the cells with the largest local discretization error given estimators
\mu_{i,j}^{{\Delta t}}
. The number of cells to be refined
is a fixed predefined fraction
p\in\left(0,1\right]
of the total number of cells. The case
p=1
corresponds to uniform refinement.

If
l<L
(outer loop), the computed refinement is used in the next series of dynamic iterations. The final approximation
U_{K_{l}}^{{\Delta t}_{l}}
on the current mesh will serve as the initial waveform
U_{0}^{{\Delta t}_{l+1}}
which allows us to keep the progress of the DI.

Remark 4
.

(i) The error estimators
\mu_{i,j}^{{\Delta t}_{l}}
which were used to decide which cells need to be refined were computed with old iterates, while mesh is used for the new iterates.
This resembles the classical heuristics for time stepping.

(ii) The discretization error estimator is computed for the DI process
up to the last refinement
. One practical consequence for splitting is that enough iteration steps need to be performed to allow information to propagate through the system. For instance if only a single step with Jacobi splitting was performed, no coupling between components has taken place yet. Consequently a component which does not directly impact the quantity of interest will not get any refinement.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have combined results from DI and DWR theories to construct an algorithm for the adaptive solution of time dependent systems of ODEs. Goal oriented error estimators are derived for both the splitting and discretization errors and these are used to control the number of dynamic iteration steps as well as to guide the refinement of the mesh.

We have adopted a view of the entire DI process as a single system. This leads to a rigorous derivation of the adjoint problem where information flows in inverse direction compared to the primal problem. In particular, this means that the iterations of the dual problem have to be computed in reverse order. However, it is possible to significantly reduce the cost of this by reusing certain results.

These first experiments show that the resulting scheme is advantageous compared to a naive uniform refinement. This applies especially to cases where there are large differences in the frequencies of the components and the time points of interest. Investigations of the mesh refinement pursued also reveal results that are intuitively sensible. The error estimators were reliable for the explicit Euler scheme but not fully reliable for the higher order Crank–Nicolson scheme. Nonetheless, they provide useful information for the purposes of mesh refinement.

There are various possible avenues for further research. Besides higher order methods and different splitting methods,
a question of particular interest is whether any information gained by the goal oriented error estimation could be used to determine an optimal splitting.

The discrete goal functional used in the present paper can also be an ideal use case for a windowing technique. In that case, the goal oriented error estimators could be used to find windows that contribute most to the overall error. Moreover, distributed QoIs are still missing in our investigations.
Finally, the method needs to be developed for a wider category of problems including PDEs or nonlinear problems.

Proof of DWR Error Estimator

The proof is classical in goal oriented techniques and follows the arguments of the proof of
(
Becker and
Rannacher 2001
, Proposition 2.2)
. We define the primal and dual residuals for
\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{V}}^{\left(K\right)}
,
\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{\left(K\right)}
:

\displaystyle\rho\left(\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)},\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\right)

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{g}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\right)-\mathbf{f}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)},\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\right),

\displaystyle\rho^{*}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right)

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{J}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)}\right)-\mathbf{f}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right).

Note that the residuals vanish for any
\mathbf{V}^{\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{V}}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}
,
\mathbf{W}^{\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}
.
Using
\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}
and
\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{V}}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}
, we obtain for the difference in the Lagrangian:

\displaystyle L\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\right)-

\displaystyle L\left(\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right)=\mathbf{J}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}\right)-\mathbf{J}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right)

and we have expressed the error in
\mathbf{J}^{\left(K\right)}
as an error in the Lagrangian functional. Consequently, we have in our linear setting:

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\right)-L\left(\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\ \frac{1}{2}\min_{\mathbf{W}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}}\left(\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right)\left[\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}-\mathbf{W}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right]\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\min_{\mathbf{V}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{V}}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}}\left(\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right)\left[\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}-\mathbf{V}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\ \frac{1}{2}\min_{\mathbf{W}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}}\left(\rho^{*}\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}-\mathbf{W}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\min_{\mathbf{V}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{V}}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}}\left(\rho\left(\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}-\mathbf{V}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right)\right).

Thus, for arbitrary
\mathbf{V}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{U}}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}^{\left(K\right)}

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{U}^{\left(K\right)}\right)-\mathbf{J}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad\ \rho\left(\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}-\mathbf{V}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\ \mathbf{g}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}-\mathbf{V}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right)-\mathbf{f}^{\left(K\right)}\left(\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)},\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}-\mathbf{V}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\int_{\mathcal{I}}\!\!\left(\mathbf{Y}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\!-\!\mathbf{\dot{U}}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\!-\!\mathbf{B}^{\left(K\right)}\mathbf{U}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)-\mathbf{V}^{{\Delta t},\left(K\right)}\left(t\right)\right)\textnormal{d}t}={\mu}^{{\Delta t}}.

This completes the proof.
