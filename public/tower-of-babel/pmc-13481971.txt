Association between non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and high-risk intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm
Authors: Yongkang Liu, Weixiao Zhang, Chuangen Guo, Jianhua Wang, Xiao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSerum cholesterol level plays a vital role in cancer development. Several studies have suggested a potential relationship between non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-c) levels and cancer risk. However, little is known about the association between non-HDL-c levels and high-risk pancreatic intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs). This study aimed to explore the relationship between non-HDL-c levels and high-risk IPMNs.MethodsThis retrospective study included 185 patients with IPMNs. Participants were divided into high and low non-HDL-c groups according to the third quartile of non-HDL-c levels. The associations between total cholesterol, non-HDL-c, and malignant IPMN were evaluated with univariate and multivariate logistic regression. In addition, the relationship between non-HDL-c levels and invasive carcinoma was examined. Finally, we explored the associations of the non-HDL-C/total cholesterol and non-HDL-c/HDL-c ratios with high-risk IPMNs.ResultsIn addition to HDL-c levels, the average levels of triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) in patients with high non-HDL-c levels were higher than those in the low non-HDL-c group (P < 0.05). In multivariate logistic regression analysis, non-HDL-c levels were significantly associated with the high-risk IPMNs (P = 0.043), but such an association was not observed between non-HDL-c levels and invasive carcinoma. No such relationship was observed between the non-HDL-c/TC ratio, non-HDL-c/HDL ratio, and high-risk IPMNs (P = 0.43; P = 0.16).ConclusionHigh non-HDL-c levels are associated with high-risk IPMNs. Serum non-HDL-c levels may be a potential associated factor for high-risk IPMNs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05422-1.

Introduction

Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs) are cystic pancreatic lesions regarded as precursors to invasive pancreatic cancer. Histologically, they are classified as low-grade, intermediate-grade, or high-grade dysplasia on the basis of cytological atypia [1]. The most recent diagnostic schema instead conceptualizes IPMNs as a biological continuum that progresses from low-grade dysplasia (LGD) through high-grade dysplasia (HGD) to invasive intraductal papillary mucinous carcinoma (IPMC) [2–4]. The 2023 International Consensus Guidelines recommend surgical resection for HGD and IPMC and surveillance for LGD in the management of IPMN and mucinous cystic neoplasia of the pancreas [5]. Nevertheless, management remains challenging because the International Consensus Guideline (ICG), the European Evidence-based Guideline, and the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) Institute guideline offer divergent recommendations. Moreover, up to 70% of resected IPMNs prove to be benign [6], and pancreatectomy carries substantial morbidity that may compromise long-term prognosis. Accurate pre-operative assessment of malignant potential is therefore essential for optimal clinical decision-making.

Serum lipoproteins and circulating lipids are implicated in both tumorigenesis and cancer progression [7–10]. Cholesterol is essential for cellular homeostasis and intracellular signalling, yet malignant cells rewire lipid metabolism to support aberrant membrane synthesis, energy balance, and lipid-mediated signalling that drive proliferation, differentiation, and motility [7]. Clinical and pre-clinical studies have linked specific lipid fractions to cancer risk: high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c) correlate directly with breast-cancer incidence in women and in murine models [11]; dyslipidaemia predisposes to rectal neuroendocrine tumors [12]; serum HDL-c may indicate nodal stage in gastric cancer [13]; and elevated total cholesterol and HDL-c are associated with increased prostate-cancer risk [14]. Collectively, these data support a cholesterol–cancer axis.

However, HDL-c-another lipoprotein family member-usually protects against cardiovascular disease [15]. HDL-associated apolipoproteins and enzymes can also suppress tumorigenesis by lowering oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory mediators, inhibiting angiogenesis, and countering tumour immune escape [16, 17]. Consequently, the association between non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-c) and cancer has attracted interest. Links between non-HDL-c and risk have been reported for breast, gastric, lung, and oral cancers [18–22], yet its role in IPMNs remains unexplored. In the present study, we examined the relationship between non-HDL-c levels and high-risk IPMNs.

Materials and methods

Patients and data collection

This retrospective study included 185 patients with histopathologically confirmed IPMNs. The study was conducted at our hospital, including patients diagnosed between Sep 2016 and Dec 2019. The patients were selected consecutively. Inclusion criteria: pathologically confirmed IPMNs with available grading information; available preoperative fasting lipid profile. A total of 206 patients were found and 21 were excluded for the following reasons: concurrent other malignancies (n = 1); use of lipid-lowering therapy (n = 5); incomplete medical records (n = 8) and absence of imaging data necessary for IPMN classification (e.g., cyst size, main duct involvement, mural nodules, etc.) (n = 7). Demographics, clinical data, tumour characteristics, and serum lipid levels were extracted from the institutional medical database. Smoking and drinking habits were also recorded. However, missing data was observed in 11.4% (n = 21) for smoking status and 13.0% (n = 24) for alcohol consumption. Dietary habit data were not available for our study population. The following variables were recorded: tumour size, location, type, main-duct diameter, mural nodules, and final histopathology; serum tumour markers (carcinoembryonic antigen [CEA], carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 [CA 19 − 9]); fasting lipids (triglycerides [TG], total cholesterol [TC], HDL-c, LDL-c, non-HDL-c); body mass index (BMI) and history of diabetes mellitus (DM). Blood samples were drawn on admission. DM was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2-h plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or documented diagnosis. Non-HDL-c was calculated as TC minus HDL-c. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine. The Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine waived the requirement for informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. We have de-identified all patient details. The reporting of this study conforms to STROBE guidelines [23].

Definition

Histologically, IPMNs are classified as low-, intermediate-, high-grade dysplasia or invasive carcinoma. According to the 2019 WHO classification of digestive system tumors, all IPMNs were divided into two groups. Low and intermediate dysplasia IPMNs were regarded as low-risk tumors, while high-grade dysplasia and invasive carcinoma were regarded as high-risk IPMNs [24, 25]. The primary outcome was prevalence of high-risk IPMNs. The second outcome was the invasive IPMNs. There were 134 low-risk IPMNs, 22 high-grade dysplasia and 29 invasive carcinoma. The tumor sizes were 3.5 cm, 4.1 cm and 3.9 cm and the duct diameter was 0.48 cm, 0.85 cm and 0.89 cm, respectively. Lymphnode metastasis was observed in 2 cases with invasive carcinoma. Moreover, invasive carcinoma IPMNs were used separately for statistical analyses.

The information of tumor size, duct diameter, tumor location and mural nodule were collected from magnetic resonance imaging report. A mural nodule was defined as a contrast-enhancing solid nodule with a size larger than 5.0 mm. Main-duct (MD-IPMN) was considered when the main pancreatic duct dilation ≥ 5 mm. Branch-duct (BD-IPMN) was defined when cystic lesion communicating with a non-dilated main duct (< 5 mm).

Clinical trial number

Not applicable.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and P < 0.05 was considered statistical significant. Continuous variables were analyzed using the independent-samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. The third quartile of non-HDL-c levels, non-HDL-c/TC ratio and non-HDL-c/HDL-c ratio in all patients was used to define low and high levels, respectively. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to determine the association between non-HDL-c levels and malignant risk of IPMNs. Additionally, the association between the non-HDL-c/TC ratio, non-HDL cholesterol/HDL ratio, and high-risk IPMN was evaluated, and age, sex, tumor location, triglyceride level, and diabetes were considered as confounding factors. The third quartile of non-HDL-c levels, non-HDL-c/TC ratio and non-HDL-c/HDL-c ratio in all patients was used to define low and high levels, respectively. We estimated the sample size using PASS 11.0. If power = 0.8, ɑ = 0.05, P0 (probability in low non-HDL-c group) = 0.24, P1 (probability in high non-HDL-c group) = 0.46, X1 = 30%, the estimated sample size was 174. Sensitivity analyses was performed after excluding those patients without data of smoking and drinking.

Characteristics of IPMN subjects

Table 1 shows the characteristics of the subjects with low (< 3.8 mmol/L) and high levels of non-HDL-c (≥ 3.8 mmol/L). Mean age and sex distribution did not differ between groups. Tumour size, duct diameter, CEA and CA 19 − 9 levels were also similar. Apart from HDL-c, patients with high non-HDL-c had higher TG, TC and LDL-c concentrations (all p < 0.05). No differences were observed in tumour subtype (main-duct vs. branch-duct), location (head-neck, body, tail) or presence of mural nodules. The prevalence of diabetes was comparable between groups. The association between cholesterol, non-HDL-c level and risk of malignant IPMN

Table 2 presents the unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios for cholesterol levels and high-risk IPMNs. In univariable analysis (Model 1), larger tumor size (≥ 3.0 cm; odds ratio (OR) = 2.60, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.17–5.79; P = 0.01), larger duct diameter (≥ 1.0 cm; OR = 6.10, 95% CI: 2.56–14.54; P < 0.001), and the presence of mural nodules (OR = 5.26, 95% CI: 2.11–13.11; P < 0.001) were positively associated with high-risk IPMNs. No significant association was observed between total cholesterol level and high-risk IPMNs (OR = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.88–1.56; P = 0.30). Similar results were found in multivariable analysis (Model 2), which was adjusted for age, sex, BMI, tumor location, triglyceride level, and diabetes.

Table 3 shows the association between non-HDL-c levels and high-risk IPMNs. Larger tumor size, larger duct diameter, and concomitant mural nodules increased the probability of high-risk IPMNs in both univariable and multivariable analyses. Similarly, high non-HDL-c levels were positively associated with high-risk IPMNs in the logistic regression model. Discussion

Accumulating evidence indicates that cholesterol plays an important role in tumorigenesis and cancer progression [7]. The relationship between non-HDL-c and cancer risk has attracted researchers’ attention. Several studies have demonstrated a potential link between non-HDL-c and several individual cancers [18, 19, 26, 27]. To the best of our knowledge, few studies have examined the relationship between non-HDL-c levels and IPMNs, especially high-risk IPMNs and invasive carcinoma. In the current study, multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that high non-HDL-c levels were associated with the probability of high-risk IPMNs; however, no such association was observed between non-HDL-c levels and invasive carcinoma.

Cholesterol is essential for cellular homeostasis. It is a key component of specialized membranes and plays a crucial role in intracellular signal transduction [28]. Cholesterol dysregulation is associated with various cardio-cerebrovascular diseases. Additionally, elevated cholesterol levels are implicated in cancer initiation, progression, and metastasis [29]. This is partly because rapidly proliferating cancer cells require more cholesterol for membrane synthesis and other functional needs [7]. Hypercholesterolemia contributes to tumorigenesis and tumor development through multiple mechanisms. For example, cholesterol metabolites such as 27-hydroxycholesterol can promote tumor growth and metastasis [30]. Increased cholesterol levels can also activate oncogenic pathways, including the Hedgehog signaling pathway, which is involved in the proliferation and migration of cancer stem cells [31]. Moreover, cholesterol can influence the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and exert anti-apoptotic effects [32]. However, the role of cholesterol is complex, as it may also possess tumor-inhibiting properties. Cholesterol and its metabolites can modulate immune responses and potentially exert anti-cancer effects [33]. Therefore, the relationship between cholesterol and cancer risk remains controversial and requires further clarification. Some studies report a positive association between elevated cholesterol levels and cancer risk [29], while others find no significant link [34]. Similarly, our study found no significant association between total cholesterol levels and the likelihood of high-risk IPMNs. A meta-analysis focusing on hormone-related cancers also reported no associations for ovarian and prostate cancers, and limited and conflicting findings for breast cancer [34].

HDL-c is a major lipoprotein that moves cholesterol from the periphery to the liver for chemical clearance [29]. In the past few decades, researchers have found that abnormal HDL-c metabolism is associated with tumorigenesis and progression [35]. Despite some conflicting findings, most studies indicate a negative correlation between HDL-c and risk of tumor. In a systematic review [36], a relatively consistent negative association was observed between HDL-c level and cancer occurrence. Some studies suggest that HDL-c is also associated with cancer progression and prognosis [37]. Interestingly, our previous work showed that low HDL-c levels were associated with high-risk IPMNs in all types of IPMNs and BD-IPMNs, and that HDL-c levels may be a potential predictor for malignant IPMNs [38].

Non-HDL-c, subtracted from TC, is usually not considered good cholesterol. Some studies have documented a potential association between non-HDL-c levels and atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases [39] or new-onset non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [40]. However, only a few studies have focused on the association between non-HDL-c levels and cancer. In a prospective cohort study, researchers found that non-HDL-c was inversely associated with the risk of total cancer and several individual cancers risk [27]. Negatively association was found between non-HDL-c and breast cancer risk in patients not using hormone replacement therapy [17]. Additionally, non-HDL fractions may be linked to the proliferation severity of breast cancer [20]. In another prospective cohort study, no significant associations between non-HDL-c and incident lung cancer risk were observed [20]. Nevertheless, the above-mentioned findings were limited to male or female populations. A recent study showed that LDL-c in cystic fluids was associated with high-risk IPMN [41]. To our knowledge, few studies have reported the role of non-HDL-c in IPMN. In the current study, our findings showed that serum high non-HDL-c levels were positively correlated with high-risk IPMN, suggesting that highly malignant tumors may require more LDL-C or other types of cholesterol.

In addition, we wanted to know whether the relative distribution of lipid fractions provides additional insight beyond absolute concentrations. Composite ratios like non- non-HDL-c/TC or HDL-c/HDL-c may offer a more integrated picture of the lipid metabolism balance. In addition, from a clinical perspective, these ratios are easily calculable from standard lipid profiles without additional cost. If proven to be independently associated with high-risk IPMNs, they could also serve as simple, readily available adjuncts for risk stratification in clinical practice, complementing the information provided by absolute non-HDL-C levels. However, no significant differences were observed between the groups. Although non-HDL-c is associated with high-risk IPMN, the role of non-HDL-c/TC or non-HDL-c/HDL-c in identifying high-risk IPMN may be limited. In addition, our results showed that high non-HDL cholesterol was not associated with high risk of invasive IPMNs. This is possibly attributed to insufficient statistical power, given the small sample size of invasive IPMN patients with high non-HDL-c (n = 7). Additionally, it is plausible that during the progression of IPMNs—from dysplastic changes to high-risk lesions and ultimately to invasive carcinoma—the metabolic microenvironment and lipid requirements may differ. However, there is currently a lack of relevant studies to support this hypothesis.

Why non-HDL-c-rather than total cholesterol or lipid ratios-emerged as the key lipid parameter associated with high-risk IPMNs is not clear. We speculated that non-HDL-c encompasses all atherogenic lipoprotein particles (very low LDL, intermediate density lipoprotein and LDL), which may collectively contribute to a pro-inflammatory, pro-proliferative microenvironment conducive to IPMN progression. TC, by contrast, includes HDL-c, which may have opposing biological effects that dilute any observed association. In addition, non-HDL-c may be more readily utilized by tumor cells to promote their proliferation, differentiation, and growth.

Diet may affect TC levels. However, dietary habit data were not collected in this study population. However, it should be emphasized that patients with pancreatic tumors often present with gastrointestinal symptoms such as anorexia, nausea, and steatorrhea. Therefore, a high-fat diet is uncommon in this patient population. Existing literature also supports that the relative contribution of diet to lipid profiles is significantly reduced in the setting of pancreatic diseases [42]. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that residual confounding due to dietary habits cannot be completely excluded. Given that the strength of the association between diet and lipid profiles is moderate in the general population but is likely weaker in this specific population, we speculate that the bias introduced by the absence of dietary data is small.

Our study had several limitations. (1) This was a single-center retrospective study and the sample size was relatively small. The generalisability of the study results may be limited. This may also lead to insufficient statistical power, making it difficult to detect subtle effects that may truly exist, while also increasing the possibility that the results are influenced by chance or bias. (2) Interval changes in lipid fractions were not analyzed in the cross-sectional study design. (3) Due to the retrospective nature of our study design, complete and reliable data on lifestyle habits and medication history (especially lipid-lowering agent use) were not available for all patients in our cohort. This lack of detailed information may affect cholesterol levels and cancer. (4) Our study was an observational study which implies that the identified associations cannot be directly inferred as causal relationships. (5) Due to the relatively small sample size in our study-particularly the limited number of invasive carcinoma cases (n = 7) and limited high-risk IPMN in men with high non-HDL cholesterol (n = 4), further stratification by sex would result in very small subgroups with insufficient statistical power to draw meaningful conclusions. We did not performed subgroup analysis. In addition, we just showed that non-HDL-c was an associated factor of high-risk IPMNs. The role of non-HDL-c in stratifying IPMNs or the actual application of these results in clinical practice requires further investigation. Finally, since our study is a retrospective analysis, we did not have access to patient prognosis data (including recurrence or survival) and we did not observe any relationship between non-HDL-c levels and IPMN occurrence or prognosis.

In conclusion, in this retrospective cross-sectional study, we found that high non-HDL-c levels may be positively associated with high-risk IPMNs. Non-HDL-c levels may be useful for the management of IPMNs. Our study reported a new associated lipid factor for high-risk IPMNs. Larger multicenter studies are required to confirm our findings.
