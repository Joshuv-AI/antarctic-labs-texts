Cross-cultural differences in students’ reward addiction and punishment oversensitivity, self-regulation, school burnout, and cyber-aggression
Authors: Tomaszek Katarzyna, Muchacka-Cymerman Agnieszka, Ayşe Aypay, Fatma Altınsoy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe general aggression model (GAM) explains youth aggression by integrating individual and environmental factors, but their interplay in the digital context remains unclear. This study examined cross-cultural links between school factors (study reward and punishment oversensitivity), individual traits (self-regulation), school burnout, and cyber-aggression in Polish and Turkish students (N = 959, 51% girls, M = 12.95 years). Path and multigroup analyses revealed higher school burnout and cyber-aggression in Polish students. Direct effects of reward and punishment oversensitivity on cyber-aggression were consistent across cultures, but indirect effects varied. The model explained 23% of cyber-aggression variance in Poland and 34% in Türkiye, highlighting the need for culturally sensitive interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24901-z.Keywords: Study reward and punishment oversensitivity, Self-regulation, School burnout, Cyber-aggression, Cultural differencesSubject terms: Psychology, Health care

Introduction

Cyber-bullying was recognised as one of the most common psychosocial problems in childhood and adolescence, with prevalence ranging between 6% and 72%1. Even though the cyber-aggression literature has been advancing by focusing not only on frequency rates but also on the psychological characteristics and consequences of these harmful online actions, little attention has been paid to the educational predictors (study reward and punishment oversensitivity, and school burnout) of these cyber-behaviours. Moreover, although researchers have confirmed that academic burnout is positively related to cyber-aggression involvement among university students from Poland and Japan2,3, such an association among children and early adolescents has not yet been examined in the context of cultural differences between Polish and Turkish students. Due to the fact that studies on the cross-cultural differences and similarities vis a vis this issue are scarce, along with calls for more thorough investigation, the current research aimed to determine the educational and psychological predictors of cyber-aggression among Polish and Turkish students.

Cyber-aggression in the context of cross-cultural similarities and differences

Cyber-aggression is a broader form of cyber-bullying that includes all online aggressive behaviours occurring in a digitally-mediated context4. Furthermore, this type of aggression tends to be most prevalent among school-aged children and adolescents5. Because young people strongly rely on their electronic devices and the virtual environment, they are more vulnerable to this nefarious cyber-behaviour, and its adverse online (e.g. cyber-abuse, cyber-hate, cyber-gossip, cyberstalking) and mental health consequences (e.g. depression, suicide ideation, and low wellbeing)4,6. Past studies on children’s and adolescents’ cyber-aggression have emphasised that its prevalence and severity are different across countries. In a meta-analysis cyber-bullying perpetration ranged from 6.0 to 46.3%, with the highest prevalence in China and the lowest in Canada and South Korea5. However, recently conducted studies in Poland on 541 adolescents aged 14–15 years revealed that over 50% of them were engaged in online perpetration7, while 35.7% of the 269 Turkish secondary students displayed bullying behaviours8. Noteworthy, a meta-analysis has shown that around one-third of youth victims of bullying are also affected by offenders in cyberspace, whereas the reverse effects (i.e., cybervictims are attacked in real life) were reported by two-thirds of adolescents9. All of these statistics highlight the urgent need for further investigations into the causes and consequences of engaging in online aggression. Importantly, among the reasons for the abovementioned differences, scholars highlighted cultural, educational, technological, legislative, and socioeconomic factors that may contribute to these malicious acts conducted in cyberspace10,11. Although the causes of engaging in cyber-aggression are complex, a comprehensive and valuable theoretical framework for explaining this type of students’ behaviour offers the general aggression model episodic processes (GAM)12. The model describes three stages of the cyclical pattern of interaction between the person and the environment. e.g. input (personal and environmental/situational variables), routes (individuals’ present internal state), and outcomes (actions resulting from appraising the current situation and the selection of the most appropriate)13. The aggressive cycle begins with a process that depends on individual-specific, and situation-specific factors. Both types of factors activate the individual’s hostile attitude, and an output variable process14. In addition, the quadripartite typology of aggression distinguishes four types of aggressive acts considering two above-mentioned dimensions, e.g. Recreation (Impulsive – Appetitive Acts), Rage (Impulsive - Aversive Acts), Reward (Controlled – Appetitive Acts), and Wrath (Controlled – Aversive Acts)15. Past research suggested that high reactive aggression is an effect of perceived hostile intentions of others (perceived threat or punishment), while high proactive aggression is an effect of positive evaluation of aggression as a reasonable path to gain desired outcomes (anticipated reward)16. Notably, these forms of cyber-aggressive acts have been supported by multiple cross-cultural empirical studies employing self-reports among diverse groups of adolescents, including youth from Poland and Türkiye7,17–20. For example, Japanese students tend to ignore others in public and either impulsively or non-impulsively use cyber-aggression, while European students (Poland and the Czech Republic) present such tendencies more frequently if they are addicted to Facebook3. Furthermore, in recently published studies, found that Polish students scored significantly higher in three cyber-aggression types: Wrath, Reward, Recreation, and lower in Rage, than Turkish one19. In addition, the culture as a key risk factor for cyber-violence was also recently proposed in the Barlett Gentile Cyberbullying Model (BGCM). More specifically, the association between positive attitudes towards cyber-bullying and cyber-bullying perpetration was stronger for independent cultures, in which individuals view themselves as independent from others and concentrate on internal goals and attributions (Western cultures)21. In accordance, the role of social context as risk factor for cyber-aggression was investigated by Voulgaridou and Kokkinos22, who found that adolescents in European and American countries reported less relational aggression, while Asian youths demonstrated higher engagement in this type of aggressive behaviour. One of the significant social risk factor analysed by scholars is parental concerns about the Internet and control behaviours over children Internet use11. For example, parents from multicultural societies were found to be less equipped to deal with cyber threats that their children face and were ill-equipped to monitor and mitigate the risks posed23. What is more, Turkish families put greater importance on dependence, emotional closeness, and control over children during the upbringing process, which creates the attitude “to be a good son or daughter” and to behave as required by cultural expectations, which was recognised as a preventive factor of problem behaviours, e.g. aggression and cyber-aggression24.

Based on the above-presented tenets and empirical evidence, we hypothesised significant cultural differences in cyber-aggression frequency, with Polish students will present a higher rate of aggressive behaviours in cyberspace (H1).

Impairment in self-regulation processes as individual-specific factors of cyber-aggression

Past empirical investigations confirmed that people with low self-control are more likely to present problematic behaviours, e.g. aggression and cyber-aggression7,25,26. Researchers have recognised that in collectivistic cultures (Asian), the process of regulation presented by individuals’ behaviours, emotions, and cognitions is subordinated to the preservation of social harmony, whereas in individualistic cultures (European/ American), self-regulation serves to build up autonomy and to widen opportunities to fulfil personal goals27.

Based on these approaches, we posited cultural differences in self-regulation level among Polish and Turkish youth, with stronger self-regulation among students from a more collectivistic culture (H2).

According to a dual-process model of self-control, actions are the result of cooperation between emotional (hot process, impulsive, fast, intuitive, and connection-based) and cognitive (cold process, reflective, slow, thoughtful, and rule-based) systems28,29. Consequently, the imbalance between both processes (the predominance of the affective responses) makes the cognitive effort to regulate the impulse limited, and this causes biased decision-making and problematic behaviours, e.g. aggression26. Several studies have shown that failure in self-control is strongly related to negative emotions, resulting in impulsive actions and choices named by the term “negative urgency”30. In this regard, cyberbullying perpetration may be predicted by a perceived lack of behavioural control over cyber-aggressors’ intentions31. It is worth noticing that during preadolescence (9–12 years), poor executive functioning (problems with planning and low inhibitory control) has been associated with reactive aggression, while being proactively aggressive indicates a better ability to manage and switch between operations in order to achieve goal-directed action32.

Considering the above-presented theories and empirical findings we expect the positive link between cyber-aggression types and dysregulation of one’s emotional states and behaviours, regardless of culture (H3).

Reward and punishment oversensitivity in educational context as environmental-specific predictors of cyber-aggression

Situation-specific variables are defined as all aversive events that initiate the impulse to engage in aggressive behaviours, i.e., dysregulated reward and punishment mechanisms14,33,34. In the literature, little is known about educational-specific reward - punishment oversensitivity, despite the fact that both processes are commonly used practices by parents and teachers to facilitate or mitigate students’ behaviours. The concept of reward - punishment oversensitivity in the educational context stems from Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory (RST)35. Study reward oversensitivity (or study reward dependency) was described as one’s learning that feelings related to school performance and behaviours are strongly reinforced by positive encouragers (high grades and praise from significant others), whereas study punishment oversensitivity results in being over-reactive to stimuli based on parents’ and teachers’ chastisement36. Both processes enlarge the external world’s impact on behaviour because they act as individuals’ filter of perception and interpretation, and weaken their internal locus of control37.

In regards, Gray’s Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory (RST) postulate the three motivational systems that impact individuals’ reactions: the behavioural activation system (BAS), the Fight-Flight-Freeze system (FFFS), and the behavioural inhibition system (BIS)35,37. Notably, individuals with high reactivity to the BAS system are primarily guided by rewards, whereas those with high reactivity to the BIS system are more susceptible to punishment38. Worthy of note, behavioural inhibition was confirmed to be sensitive to cultural factors, i.e., children from Eastern cultures (China) presented more inhibited and socially reticent reactions in novel situations than did their North American counterparts39. Similarly, cross-cultural differences were detected between toddlers from Eastern cultures (Chinese, South Korean)40, and Western cultures (Italian and Australian)41.

Following these results, we hypothesize that more collectivistic Turkish students will be more susceptible to punishment in the educational context, while their Polish counterparts from a more individualistic culture will exhibit higher reward dependency (H4).

Strong pieces of evidence confirm the relationship between aggression and the higher reactivity of the BAS system. Specifically, the reward mechanism is an integral characteristic of compulsive “addiction-like” aggression of the offender, which includes exploitation of the victim, and instrumentally motivated behaviour (proactive aggression)34. This is consistent with the studies in which two controlled types of aggression were both positively associated with the motivation to follow one’s goals (i.e. future rewards)42. Importantly, wrath aggression was also related to higher behavioural inhibition, which indicates that the predator reacts to punishment, non-reward and novelty stimuli, and the aggressive acts are presented to avoid negative consequences. Interestingly, the link between aggression and BIS reactivity remains unclear. For example, some evidence confirmed the positive associations between reactive aggression and the BIS system42, yet higher sensitivity to punishment also acted both as a protective and risk factor for aggression38, and these effects were moderated by emotion self-regulation ability38. Importantly, many past approaches considered reward-punishment mechanisms and emotion to be interrelated. Thus, the ability to regulate own emotional state is recognised to be associated not only with aggression itself, but also with oversensitivity to punishment and certain aspects of reward38.

Based on these findings and theoretical frameworks we posited that the oversensitivity to study reward and punishment will be positively associated with cyber-aggression, regardless of culture (H5), the self-regulation processes will mediate the associations between study reward and punishment oversensitivity and cyber-aggression (H6).

Learning adverse experiences – students’ burnout symptoms as factors of cyber-aggression

The high prevalence and influence of study-related distress and burnout on students are one of the reasons that prompted researchers to investigate their association with youth aggression and cyber-aggression. It is worth adding that the prevalence of study burnout varied in different countries43. These disparities are related to both differences in socially accepted values (collectivistic vs. individualistic cultures)3, and differences in school system programmes for students’ support and well-being in countries that share similar cultural values44.

Accordingly, based on the above evidence, we raised the following hypothesis: Polish and Turkish students significantly differ in their school burnout levels (H7).

The school burnout triadic symptom model (Maslach Burnout Inventory, MBI) is comprised of: (1) study exhaustion, (2) cynical attitude towards school, and (3) decreased personal accomplishment45. However, the Burnout Assessment Tool (BAT) model, theorised that burnout includes exhaustion (loss of energy resulting in chronic mental and physical fatigue), and mental – distancing (reluctant and aversion attitude towards co-workers/classmates and superiors/teachers), it also points to mental dysregulation of key processes i.e. cognitive impairment (i.e. memory deficits, inattention, and poor cognitive performance) and emotional impairment (i.e. intense negative emotional reactions and feeling overwhelmed by one’s emotions)46,47. Student burnout may also manifest itself through anti-social or problematic behaviours (i.e. health-risky behaviours, substance abuse, aggression, and cyber-aggression) or various types of digital addiction48. The mechanism underlying the aforementioned links is explained by impulses triggered by aversive school-related events in combination with low self-control49–52. This way of maladaptive coping with educational distress is associated with the state of frustration, which may lead to aggression expressed indirectly via social media3.

Following the above theoretical frameworks and research findings, we posited that school burnout will be positively associated with cyber-aggression, regardless of culture (H8).

In parallel to the presented results, the Study Demand-Resources burnout Theory (SD-R) locates the origins of school burnout in the imbalance between excessive study demands (students are expected to master certain educational and psychological competencies in order to pass externally created sets of Matura exam criteria in a given period of time), and lack of study resources (the loss of positively valued physical, psychological, social or organisational aspects of the educational process that are inevitable to achieve study goals, meet the study demands, and facilitate personal growth and development)53. Importantly, one of the significant predictors of school burnout is oversensitivity to behavioural stimulation, i.e., reward and punishment36. Both processes may increase chronic school distress and burnout by the direct relationship to overwhelming study demands and the depletion of study resources. These tenets are empowered by the theory of effort-reward imbalance (ERI) (high effort and low reward), one of the predictors of chronic negative emotions, exhaustion of internal resources, and academic pressure as well as student burnout itself54,55.

Accordingly, based on the SD-R framework and empirical evidence provided above, we hypothesized the indirect effects of study reward and punishment oversensitivity on cyber-aggression through poor self-regulation and school burnout (H9).

Materials and methods

Participants and procedure

The participants in this study were 959 early adolescents; of these, 548 (57.1%) were from Poland (47% girls) and 411 (42.9%) were from the Türkiye (57% girls). The Polish students’ mean age was 13.26 years (SD = 0.70), while the Turkish students’ 12.60 years (SD = 0.98).Both samples were recruited through schools, and the assents of school principals, parents and children were obtained. The paper-pencil method was used for data collecting in Poland, while online questionnaires were completed by the Turkish participants. The research project was conducted from September 2022 to March 2023, and the Helsinki Declaration was respected during data collection. Participants from both countries shared similar socio-demographic and educational characteristics: puberty or early adolescence period, and middle school students (7th – 8th grades in Poland, and middle school in Türkiye). The study was reviewed and approved by the University Research Ethics Committee of the National Education Commission in Krakow (approval no. Dna.0046.12.2023). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians prior to participation in the study.

Measures

Study Reward Oversensitivity was assessed with the 10- item Scale of Reward Addiction in an academic context for Middle Schools Students Scale (SRA) by Aypay36 (e.g. I usually study or do homework to get a reward). In the current study, only its subscale High Expectation of Reward was used, as it is the dimension that expresses the actual oversensitivity state. The HRE reliability in the Poland (α = 0.66/ɷ=0.68)) and Türkiye (α = 0.57/ ɷ=0.62) samples. Due to the small number of test items, α values ​​in the range of 0.45 – 0.60 are considered acceptable56.

Study Punishment Sensitivity was estimated by using the Scale of Punishment Sensitivity in an academic context for Middle Schools Students (SPS) developed by Aypay36. This is a 9-item scale (e.g. I usually study not to be punished at home or school). In this tool the most typical behaviours are captured by the 3-item subscale Inhibition due to punishment, with reliability in the Poland (α = 0.69/ɷ=0.72)) and Türkiye (α = 0.67/ɷ=0.68) samples.

Student school burnout was measured with the Elementary Student School Burnout Scale developed by Aypay57. This tool consists 24 items (e.g. School is tiring for me). The reliability was equal to α/ɷ= 0.90 in the Polish sample, and α = 0.92/ɷ=0.94 in the Turkish sample.

Emotional and Behavioural Self-Regulation was measured with the Self-regulation scale short version by Novak and Clayton58. The total scale consists of three aspects of self-regulation: emotional, cognitive, and behavioural (e.g. There are days when I’m on edge all the time). In the current study, we used emotional (Polish sample: α/ɷ= 0.81, Turkish sample: α/ɷ= 0.78), and behavioural (Polish sample: α/ ɷ= 0.75, Turkish sample: α/ɷ= 0.65/0.66) scales.

Students’ Cyber-aggression was measured with the Cyber-aggression types inventory by Runions et al.49. The instrument consists of 29 items (e.g. Sometimes I can be mean to people online to get what I want).The reliability α/ɷ=0.97 in the Polish sample and α/ɷ=0.94 in the Turkish sample.

The composite reliability and validity of all measurements were confirmed, as well as the absence of collinearity problems among the latent constructs in preliminary analysis (see Appendix, Tab. A).

Normal distribution and missing data

The univariate values for asymmetry of data distribution e.g. skewness and kurtosis ranged between − 2 and + 2 were acceptable59. Mardia’s coefficients of multivariate skewness and kurtosis were significant (p < 0.001) (Polish sample: Skewness = 2.14; Kurtosis = 10.15; Turkish sample: Skewness = 2.58; Kurtosis = 11.20). The Mahalanobis-squared method indicate no outliers in the observations (p < 0.05). There were no missing data.

Data analysis

Statistics were carried out using SPSS 22, and Jamovi-2.3.18.0 (2023) free software. The G*Power – free software60 was used to estimate minimum the sample size requirements for sufficient power. 176 subjects (88 per group) was sufficient for Student’s t-test, and 111 for Pearson’s analyses (Conditions: α = 0.05; Power 1- ß = 0.95).The sample size for linear multiple regression model with six predictors (effect size f2 = 0.05 with 95% power) was equal to 218 subjects. Six observed variables were included into path analysis conducted with Gallucci jamovi module61. The multivariate non-normal distribution of variables and no missing data, the overcorrection of standard errors occurs if the sample size for path analysis is > 25062,63. Also, a ratio of 10 cases per indicator (rule of thumb) is recognised as an adequate sample size64. Due to the violation detected in the multivariate data normality we used robust method when estimating path analysis (i.e. weighted least squares (WLS)). Descriptive statistics, e.g. mean, standard deviation, Student’s t-test and independence chi- square test were calculated to test differences between the two cultures. The Pearson correlation analyses was used to evaluate the associations between assessed variables. The multi-group Path analyses was carried out to test equivalence of a construct across groups. To evaluate model fit, the following fit indices were used: the chi-square (χ2 ≥ 0.05) statistic, the comparative fit index (CFI ≥ 0.95), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA ≤ 0.09), and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR ≤ 0.09), as indicators of excellent fit of the model to the data62,65.To examine the measurement invariance of the model across the two samples (e.g. Polish & Turkish), a hierarchical approach was applied by constraining the model parameters (regression coefficients &intercepts) and juxtaposing the model fit indexes. Specifically, the following procedure was applied: (1) we tested the model separately in each culture and then the multigroup analysis was carried out (configural invariance), (2) first the model parameters were set free across the groups, and then the regression coefficients were constrained to be equal across the two groups (metric invariance). The measurement invariance may be confirmed if the multi-group model fit indices after modification are acceptable, and the small and insignificant change of values for fit indices (Δχ2 insignificant, ΔCFI < 0.02, ΔRMSEA < 0.03) is revealed66.

Direct effects of the tested models

Significant direct effects of school burnout and study reward dependency, as well as emotional self-regulation on cyber-aggression were observed, regardless of culture. School burnout was directly predicted by study punishment and self-dysregulation indicators in both cultures. However, higher study reward predicted school burnout only in the Turkish sample. Study punishment and emotional self-regulation predicted behavioural self-regulation, however in the Turkish sample also study reward was a significant predictor of this variable. Similarly, emotional self-regulation was significantly predicted by study punishment in both cultures, but in the Turkish sample also study reward played a significant role in explaining the level of this variable.

Indirect effects of the tested models

The major difference between Polish and Turkish samples was associated with the significance of all indirect effects among students from a more collectivistic culture. In both samples, we detected significant indirect effects of study punishment oversensitivity on cyber-aggression through (1) school burnout, (2) emotional self-regulation, and (3) school burnout accompanied by both self-regulation indicators. Additionally, we confirmed an indirect effects of self-regulation domains on cyber-aggression via school burnout, regardless of culture. In the Turkish sample, study punishment oversensitivity was associated with cyber-aggression via (1) school burnout; (2) emotional and behavioural self-regulation, as well as (3) school burnout and self-regulation indicators altogether. Another difference was related to the insignificance in the Polish sample of the study punishment oversensitivity relationship with cyber-aggression via chain mediation, i.e., behavioural self-regulation together with school burnout (see Appendix, Tab. Discussion

Building on the General Aggression Model (GAM), the present study aimed to examine cross-cultural similarities and differences in the cyber-aggression, individual-specific (emotional and behavioural self-regulation), and environmental-specific (study reward and punishment oversensitivity) factors, as well as present students’ states (student burnout). Based on the literature review we posited nine study hypothesis, of which most were supported by the data. In general, we confirmed significant cross-cultural differences in cyber-aggression, school burnout, behavioural self-regulation and punishment oversensitivity between Polish and Turkish students (H1, H2, H7). Although contrary to our hypothesis, a more individualistic students scored higher in study reward oversensitivity compared to those from more collectivistic culture (H4). We also supported the study hypothesis about the positive relationships between cyber-aggression (dependent variable) and all predictive variables, that is, school burnout, self-dysregulation, oversensitivity to study reward and punishment (H3,H5,H8). Finally, in accordance with our assumptions, we detected the indirect effects of study reward and punishment oversensitivity on cyber-aggression through poor self-regulation and school burnout (H6, H9).

Cross-cultural differences in cyber-aggression, school burnout, self-regulation, and study reward-punishment oversensitivity

Students from Poland were more burned-out and presented cyber-aggressive behaviours more frequently than their counterparts from Türkiye (H1, and H7 were accepted). Furthermore, Polish students were more over-sensitive to learning-punishment and learning-reward (H4 was partially confirmed). By contrast, Turkish students controlled their behaviours to a greater extent than Polish youth, although the expression of emotions was regulated on a similar level in both cultures (H2 was partially accepted). Our results are aligned with the tenet that culture has a significant role in shaping human behaviours in offline and online settings, including different perceptions of virtually committed acts of aggression67. Türkiye is one of the countries with an honour culture, which implies mostly a focus on suitability to social culture, particularly social dignity, is more important than individual preferences68. Considering that cyber-aggression is an act of violating social norms, this may discourage Turkish students from being aggressive in cyber-space. Some scholars argue that differences in cyber-aggression among Turkish individuals lies in the collectivistic nature of their culture, which is related to openness to communication and a higher tendency to forgive67. In this respect, children from collectivistic cultures are more frequently taught by their parents to meet social expectations rather than emphasise individual mental states69, which may encourage them to behave correctly in the virtual space. Furthermore, Turkish students were found to perceive all cyber-bullying scenarios as more severe than adolescents from European countries, and were more sensitive to the imbalance of power between offender and victim70. These characteristics may inhibit them from cyber-aggression.

Our findings are also consistent with a large body of literature that suggests a higher level of cognitive control of behaviours (executive functions) among individuals from collectivistic cultures compared to their Western counterparts71,72. For example, students’ behaviours from individualistic cultures (Canada) were significantly more motivated by introjected rewards and punishment than those from collectivistic cultures (China)73. Moreover, according to some researchers, a self-regulatory system is fostered by cultural norms, and thus individuals’ internal processes and behaviours are modified to reach culture-specific goals27. Hence, children from collectivistic countries may inhibit their inner affections, beliefs and desires not only to save their views but also to maintain relational and social harmony and the alignment of personal and communal goals. Such higher interdependence decreases the tendency to react aggressively in cyberspace but also enhances the tendency to behave obediently and control individuals’ impulses.

Cultural differences in self-control may stem from children’s interpretation of the general notion of free will69. Specifically, in collectivistic cultures, the loss of self-control is perceived as a conflict between internal desires and external social norms, in which a person reduces the value of free will in socially important situations. In the context of our findings, Polish and Turkish students do not differ in their level of emotional self-control. It is worth noting, however, that individualistic cultures prefer emotional expression to regulate emotions while collectivistic ones focus more on expressive suppression to regulate affective states74. Both emotional coping strategies result in the subjective perception of control over inner states, thus although emotional control is achieved through a different mechanism (expression vs. suppression), the effect is similar.

To some extent, the observed cross-cultural differences in school burnout may be explained by diversity in parenting practices applied to manage children’s learning behaviours and teaching-learning environments. In accordance, the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) found that Turkish students positively assessed learning environments75, and presented significantly more participation in the classroom76. By contrast, PISA 2018 studies revealed that only 25% of Polish students consider it worthwhile to work hard at school to get a good job77 and negatively assessed the school climate as annoying and unsatisfactory78.

Predictors of cyber-aggression

The results of the current research suggest that cyber-aggression is significantly predicted by a greater sensitivity to study reward, self-dysregulation, and higher school burnout, regardless of culture (Study hypothesis 3, 5, and 8 are confirmed). These observations are aligned with the prior literature regarding positive links between cyber-aggression and school burnout, as well as dysregulation of one’s inner states3,79. The results are also compatible with findings, indicating that reward oversensitivity may increase the risk of negative emotions (frustration, anger, or sadness) after frustrating non-reward situations, effortful pursuit of goals, a response to negative social events, as well as if an expected rewarding stimulus is not as valuable or large as anticipated80,81. Notably, our results are also consistent with the Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution (I-PACE) model posits that cyber-aggression may increase as a result of interactions between predisposing characteristics of individuals (e.g. affective and cognitive dysregulation, stress perception, and adverse childhood experiences), moderators (internal triggers, e.g. maladjustment passive coping style, negative moods, low inhibitory control, and internet-related cognitive false beliefs), and mediators (contextual factors, e.g. reactions to specific behaviour-stimuli and confrontation to reduced executive functioning)82–84. Moreover, our findings confirmed that the potentially threatening school-situations and adverse outcomes of study-behaviours, accompanied by a strong motivation to avoid such situations and outcomes, make students to be more cyber-aggressive. Notably, school burnout as a result of adverse educational experiences and chronic psychological school distress leads to dysregulation of emotional and cognitive systems, which indicates the process of burnout syndrome development and its adverse behavioural consequences (e.g. absenteeism, low school performance, substance abuse and aggressive behaviours)44,46.

Mediating effect of self-regulation and school burnout on the associations between study reward-punishment oversensitivity and cyber-aggression

Regarding our findings behavioural self-regulation and study-punishment oversensitivity were significant predictors only indirectly via emotional regulation and school burnout. These results are consistent with evidence that the indirect effect of self-control on cyber-aggression via the personal beliefs of perpetrators26. Notably, emotional dysregulation was found to increase the risk of both committing cyber-aggression and cyber-victimisation85. Furthermore, on an emotional level, burn-out can be described as having difficulties with adverse emotional acknowledgment, suppression of negative mood and impulses, and lack of understanding of inner states86, whereas cyber-bullies seem to experience direct problems linked to affective reactions inhibition87. Given our results, aggression in cyberspace may be related to unpleasant emotions of perpetrators with high punishment sensitivity which include fact-based cognitions (i.e. hostile beliefs about a world of injustice and being hurt or excluded) and a cycle of maladaptive reactions and reappraisals of online behaviours, including the feeling of gaining power via cyberspace. In particular, in online social interaction, it is easy for threatened and oversensitive individuals – who perceive the world as an injustice – to unduly categorise ambiguous online messages as threatening, and interpret online cues in a hostile manner26.

In our study we detected the indirect effects of study-punishment on cyber-aggression via school burnout and self-regulation indicators in both countries, whereas indirect effects of study-reward oversensitivity via these variables were only presented in the Turkish sample. Comparing both samples, our studies revealed that Turkish students’ reward conditions directly predict emotional and behavioural self-dysregulation and school burnout. The observed cultural differences may result from both the level of family and teacher support when coping with school distress and failure, which may be recognised by Turkish students as an honour-threat88. More specifically, honour values cultures are recognised to be hypersensitive to reputational threats, which directly leads to mental health problems, and interpersonal violence89,90. Relating these findings to our results, it can be noted that the belief of mistreatment by teachers/parents (i.e. lack of reward when a student has worked hard to meet the study demands) may be perceived as a false accusation of not working hard enough, which in turn may result in aggression. Even so, “honour behaviours highlight the need to maintain group structure”91, thus the aggression may not be directed at injustice authority but against others. However, individuals may also direct aggressive impulses onto themselves89, which may launch the process of struggling with strong negative affect (emotional dysregulation) and increase self-blaming and self-negative thoughts about school performance resulting in higher personal distress and burnout.

Study limitations

Even though this study presents several strengths, such as analysing cyber-aggression and its predictors from the cross-cultural perspective and prospective design of the GAD model, a few limitations should be acknowledged. Thus, the limitations of this study are the following. First, the data collection was made by using self-reports in a cross-sectional study design. However, it is worth adding that the magnitude of the coefficients obtained in this study varies considerably, indicating that the associations among studied characteristics have not yielded bias error by a common underlying process. Nevertheless, further longitudinal study projects are needed in order to gather in-depth evidence that improves our knowledge in this area. Next, two systems underlying reward and punishment oversensitivity were emphasised, namely BAS and BIS36. However, theoretical resources of reward and punishment oversensitivity also include FSSS concepts, which need to be tested in the context of educational performance. Furthermore, despite the fact that the main inclusion criteria were similar in both countries, the Turkish sample was slightly younger. In addition, also different assessment procedures were used, with face-to-face contact in Poland and online assessments in Türkiye. Hence, future studies with more between-countries shared characteristics may be needed to confirm the findings. Lastly, our findings did not support the equivalence of the tested relationships in the multigroup path analysis, thus cross-cultural differences seem to be important factors in cyber-aggression models. However, the observed results may also stem from other relevant variables not included in our model, but mentioned in the discussion section such as coping strategies, self-esteem, family bonds, or teachers’ and classmates’ support. Thus, further analysis should be made to confirm cross-cultural differences in the cyber-aggression model tested in this research project.

Regardless of the limitations mentioned above, we must highlight that the current study investigated constructs that have been rarely analysed by past literature. In addition, one of the major strengths of this study is focusing on the sample – middle school students at an early adolescence period. At the same time, cross-cultural research in this age group is rarely conducted.

Conclusion

Aggressive behaviours manifested in society have become common in everyday life at every developmental stage and are one of the most typical behaviours in middle school. The findings imply that culturally acquired features may be one of the explanatory frameworks for aggressive online actions, along with observations of an individual’s capabilities for self-control and educational problems. The results shed light on middle school students’ mechanism of cyber-aggression. Moreover, our study suggests that high external dependency on reward and punishment in an educational context may increase individuals’ tendency toward increased loss of self-control, which may impair their ability to effectively cope with school-related stress and result in both school burnout and cyber-aggression.
