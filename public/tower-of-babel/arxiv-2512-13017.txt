Hinge-Proximal Stochastic Gradient Methods for Convex Optimization with Functional Constraints
Vaibhav Rajoriya, Prateek Priyaranjan Pradhan, Ketan Rajawat

Abstract
This paper considers stochastic convex optimization problems with smooth functional constraints arising in constrained estimation and robust signal recovery. We operate in the high-dimensional and highly-constrained setting, where oracle access is restricted to one or a few objective and constraint gradients per-iteration, as in streaming or online estimation. Existing approaches to solve such problems are based on either the stochastic primal-dual or stochastic subgradient methods, and require globally Lipschitz continuous constraint functions. In this work, we develop a hinge-proximal framework that utilizes an exact penalty reformulation to yield updates involving only one linearized constraint (and hence accessing one constraint gradient) per-iteration. The updates also admit a novel hinge-proximal three-point inequality relying on smoothness rather than global Lipschitz continuity of the constraint functions. The framework leads to three algorithms: a baseline hinge-proximal SGD (HPS), a variance-reduced HPS version for finite-sum settings, and a nested HPS version whose performance depends on a geometric regularity constant of the constraint region rather than explicitly on the number of constraints, while achieving near-SGD sample complexity. The superior empirical performance of the proposed algorithms is demonstrated on a robust regression problem with noisy features, representative of errors-in-variables estimation.

In this work, we put forth a new hinge-proximal framework for stochastic optimization with smooth functional constraints. The proposed framework utilizes an exact hinge-penalty reformulation to develop a class of primal-domain SGD algorithms for solving (

P

We instantiate this framework in three algorithms: the baseline hinge-proximal SGD (HPS), a variance-reduced HPS (VR-HPS) for finite-sum problems, and a nested HPS (N-HPS) that leverages linear regularity to remove the explicit dependence on the number of constraints

•

The HPS algorithm makes one SFO call per-iteration and achieves an SFO complexity of
{\mathcal{O}\left(m^{2}/\epsilon\right)}
without requiring any regularity condition on the constraints. While this dependence on

is suboptimal, HPS serves as a conceptual and technical baseline for developing the other variants.

•

Building upon the hinge-proximal framework, we next develop the VR-HPS algorithm, which exploits the finite-sum structure of both the objective and the penalty, achieving an SFO complexity of
{\mathcal{O}\left(n+\tfrac{m+\sqrt{m+n}}{\sqrt{{\epsilon}}}\right)}

•

Finally, seeking to remove the explicit dependence on

{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\tfrac{1}{{\epsilon}}\right)}

In summary, for solving (

I-A

Robust Regression

To motivate the usefulness and demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed algorithms, we evaluate its numerical performance on the problem of robust regression under noisy features. Recall that the general regression problem with a linear measurement model entails estimating
{\mathbf{x}}
using

\min_{{\mathbf{x}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell({\mathbf{a}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}},b_{i}).

(1)

where
\ell:{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is a convex loss function. However, in many real world domains, such as in sensor networks, wireless communication, and biomedical signal analysis, the features are often corrupted by complex and poorly understood acquisition noise. Estimation of
{\mathbf{x}}
from noisy feature-vector pairs
({\mathbf{a}}_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
has been widely studied under the umbrella of errors-in-variables and measurement-error regression
[
5
]
.

Some of the optimization-based approaches for handling noisy features include worst-case robust optimization
[
4
]
, distributionally robust optimization
[
28
]
, and total least-squares
[
12
]
. These methods rely on explicit parametric descriptions of the uncertainty and can be overly conservative if the true corruption mechanism is complex or only available only through simulation. On the other hand, Bayesian approaches model measurement noise probabilistically and can flexibly specify parametric noise models, but they also require an explicit noise model while relying on computationally intensive interfaces like Markov Chain Monte Carlo.

Here we consider an alternative framework that retains the loss function in the objective but introduces constraints that keep the per-sample loss small for sampled corrupted features:

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell({\mathbf{a}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}},b_{i})

(2)

\displaystyle\text{s. t.}

\displaystyle\ell(P_{j}({\mathbf{a}}_{i})^{\top}{\mathbf{x}},b_{i})\leq\varepsilon,\quad\forall i,j

where
P_{j}({\mathbf{a}}_{i})
denotes the perturbed realization of the true feature vector
{\mathbf{a}}_{i}
and
\varepsilon
is a user-defined tolerance for prediction error under perturbations. The constraints in (
2
) ensure that even under feature corruption, the prediction loss remains within an acceptable threshold of
\varepsilon
for the considered perturbation realizations. This formulation allows us to incorporate Monte-Carlo models of feature noise, arising from sensor simulators, calibration uncertainty, and adversarial perturbation generators, without an explicit parametric noise model. However, this formulation is less common in the literature because it introduces a large number of constraints. This motivates the proposed stochastic methods that access only a single data record and a single sampled corruption constraint per iteration. In the remainder of the paper, we develop and analyze such methods, and demonstrate their effectiveness on robust regression tasks.

II
Background and Assumptions

In this section, we introduce the exact penalty framework that will serve as the main vehicle for designing our algorithms. We also state the various assumptions required for the proposed algorithms.

The exact penalty method for constrained optimization has been widely studied and incorporates the constraints directly into the objective via a nonsmooth penalty function
[
2
, Sec. 4.3.1]
. Using this approach, we reformulate (

P

) as

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}~F({\mathbf{x}}):=f({\mathbf{x}})+h({\mathbf{x}})+\frac{\gamma}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}[g_{j}({\mathbf{x}})]_{+}

(
\mathcal{P}_{1}
)

where
\gamma>0
is the penalty parameter. Observe that instead of enforcing the constraints via projections onto the feasible region, the penalty reformulation attempts to correct the constraint violations through hinge penalties applied to individual constraints. Remarkably, and unlike various other smooth penalty functions, the exact penalty ensures that any solution to (

P
1

) is also a solution to (

P

), provided that
\gamma
is sufficiently large
[
2
, Prop. 4.3.2]
. We remark that other types of penalty functions, such as
\max_{k}[g_{k}({\mathbf{x}})]_{+}
, have also been used in the literature
[
3
,
20
]
. Here, we utilize a separate penalty for each constraint in (

P
1

), resulting in a finite-sum structure that will be the key to developing stochastic algorithms capable of handling a large number of constraints.

For brevity, we collect the non-smooth terms into

\displaystyle\phi({\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\phi_{j}({\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle\phi_{j}({\mathbf{x}}):=h({\mathbf{x}})+\gamma[g_{j}({\mathbf{x}})]_{+},

(4)

for
1\leq j\leq m
so that
F({\mathbf{x}})=f({\mathbf{x}})+\phi({\mathbf{x}})
. The somewhat redundant appearance of
h
in each
\phi_{j}
is intentional, as it allows us to work with a collection of per-constraint non-smooth components
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
and apply stochastic algorithms that access only one
\phi_{j}
per-iteration.

The feasible region for the
j
-th constraint is denoted by
{\mathcal{X}}_{j}:=\{{\mathbf{x}}\mid g_{j}({\mathbf{x}})\leq 0\}
and the entire feasible region is
{\mathcal{X}}=\cap_{j=1}^{m}{\mathcal{X}}_{j}
. A point
{\mathbf{x}}_{\star}
is optimal if it satisfies the first order optimality condition

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f({\mathbf{x}}_{\star})+\nabla h({\mathbf{x}}_{\star}),\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{\star}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall~\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathcal{X}}

(5)

for some
\nabla h({\mathbf{x}}_{\star})\in\partial h({\mathbf{x}}_{\star})
. Since we consider
f
to be strongly convex, the optimal
{\mathbf{x}}_{\star}
is guaranteed to be unique. For stochastic optimization algorithms, a random point
{\mathbf{x}}
is near-optimal if its mean squared distance from
{\mathbf{x}}_{\star}
is small, i.e.,
{\mathbb{E}}\left\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{\star}\right\|^{2}\leq\epsilon
.

We assume access to a stochastic first order (SFO) oracle that returns
\{\nabla f_{i_{t}}({\mathbf{x}}),\nabla g_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{x}}),g_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{x}})\}
for a given
{\mathbf{x}}
and random indices
i_{t}
and
j_{t}
. The SFO complexity of various algorithms will be measured by the total number of oracle calls required to obtain a near-optimal point.

II-A

Assumptions

We first discuss assumptions that are common to all the algorithms. We begin with the standard assumption regarding the smoothness and convexity of the various functions.

A1
.

The functions
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{g_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
are proper, closed, convex, and
L_{f}
- and
L_{g}
-smooth, respectively. The regularizer
h
is proper, closed, and convex, but may be non-smooth.

Assumption
A1
delineates the structural properties of the problem class under consideration. Note that we do not require
h
to be Lipschitz and hence allow
h
to contain indicator functions of convex sets. Next, we require the Slater condition which is one of the simplest and most widely used constraint qualifications (CQ) for convex optimization problems.

A2
.

Slater condition holds for (

P

), i.e., there exists a feasible
{\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}}
such that

\displaystyle g_{j}({\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}})

\displaystyle\leq-\nu<0

\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq m

(6)

Additionally, we assume that the optimality gap at the Slater point is bounded, i.e.,
f({\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}})+h({\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}})-f({\mathbf{x}}_{\star})-h({\mathbf{x}}_{\star})\leq{\tilde{B}}
.

Assumption
A2
implies the existence of a primal-dual optimum pair
({\mathbf{x}}_{\star},{\bm{\lambda}}_{\star})
with
\left\|{\bm{\lambda}}_{\star}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{{\tilde{B}}}{\nu}
; see
[
20
]
. Further, as shown in Appendix
A
, choosing
\gamma>m\frac{{\tilde{B}}}{\nu}
ensures that a KKT point of (

P

) is also a KKT point of (

P
1

) and hence
{\mathbf{x}}_{\star}=\arg\min_{{\mathbf{x}}}F({\mathbf{x}})
. Under Assumption
A2
, the optimality condition in (
5
) becomes
0\in\partial F({\mathbf{x}}_{\star})
, or equivalently,

\displaystyle\nabla f({\mathbf{x}}_{\star})+\nabla\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{\star})=0.

(7)

for some
\nabla\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{\star})\in\partial\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{\star})
. Next, we have the strong convexity assumption.

A3
.

The objective function
f({\mathbf{x}})
is
\mu
-strongly convex.

In this context, we define the objective condition number
\kappa_{f}:=\frac{L_{f}}{\mu}
and the constraint condition number
\kappa_{g}:=\frac{L_{g}}{\mu}
. Note that for problems satisfying Assumption
A3
, the optimal
{\mathbf{x}}_{\star}
is unique. Combining Assumption
A3
with the bound in Assumption
A2
yields

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{\star}\right\|^{2}\leq f({\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}})+h({\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}})-f({\mathbf{x}}_{\star})-h({\mathbf{x}}_{\star})\leq{\tilde{B}}.

(8)

For vanilla SGD variants, we also need the following bounded variance assumption.

A4
.

The stochastic gradients
\{\nabla f_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{\star})\}_{i=1}^{n}
have bounded variance at
{\mathbf{x}}_{\star}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f_{i_{t}}({\mathbf{x}}_{\star})-\nabla f({\mathbf{x}}_{\star})\right\|^{2}\leq\sigma_{\star}^{2}

where the expectation is with respect to the random index

.

The variance-reduced SGD proposed in Sec.

.

A5
.

The constraint function gradients are mean-square bounded at

.

The constraints satisfy linear regularity with parameter

,

III
Hinge-Proximal SGD

In this section, we introduce the hinge-proximal framework for solving (

P
1

), which exploits the finite-sum structure of
\phi
to access only one constraint per-iteration, while still operating in the primal domain. Our starting point is the stochastic splitting proximal gradient (SSPG) algorithm of
[
18
]
, which also handles stochastic composite problems such as (

P
1

). At iteration
t
, the SSPG algorithm for solving (

P
1

) would involve (a) perform an SGD update to calculate
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}={\mathbf{x}}_{t}-\eta_{t}\nabla f_{i_{t}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})
for a random index
i_{t}
; and then (b) calculate
{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}={\text{prox}_{\eta_{t}\phi_{j_{t}}}\!\left({\mathbf{z}}_{t}\right)}
.

For SSPG to be efficient,
\phi_{j_{t}}
must be proximally tractable, which is often not the case in many instances of (

P

). The proposed HPS algorithm retains the same two-stage structure, but replaces the difficult proximal step with a simpler proximal with respect to a partially linearized penalty
{\tilde{\phi}}_{j}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{x}}_{t})\coloneqq h({\mathbf{u}})+\gamma[g_{j}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})+\langle\nabla g_{j}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}),{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\rangle]_{+}
, resulting in the update

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}={\text{prox}_{\eta_{t}{\tilde{\phi}}_{j_{t}}}\!\left({\mathbf{z}}_{t}\right)}

IV
Variance Reduced Hinge-Proximal SGD

In this section, we construct a variance-reduced version of HPS for the finite-sum setting, i.e., for
f({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}({\mathbf{x}})
. The proposed VR-HPS algorithm achieves an improved
{\mathcal{O}\left(n+\frac{m+\sqrt{m+n}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)}
complexity, which to the best of our knowledge, is the first such rate for solving (

P

) while accessing only one constraint gradient per-iteration. Our design combines SVRG-style variance reduction for the smooth component
f
with a decoupled variance reduction scheme for the hinge penalty
\phi
, adapted from
[
13
]
but modified to work with linearized constraints and without proximal access to each
g_{j}
.

For the smooth term, we use a SVRG-type variance reduced estimator
{\mathbf{v}}_{t}\coloneqq\nabla f_{i_{t}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})-\nabla f_{i_{t}}({\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t})+\nabla f({\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t})
, where
{\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t}
is a checkpoint at which the full gradient
\nabla f({\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t})
is periodically computed and kept fixed between updates. Variance reduction for the nonsmooth term follows the decoupling idea of
[
13
]
; we introduce auxiliary variables
{\mathbf{y}}_{j,t}
that track subgradients of
\phi_{j}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})
, and maintain
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\mathbf{y}}_{j,t}
that approximates the subgradient of
\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{t})
. At the
t
-th iteration, we only update
{\mathbf{y}}_{j_{t},t+1}
to replace
{\mathbf{y}}_{j_{t},t}
for a random
j_{t}
, so that
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
can be maintained as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}_{t+1}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{y}}_{t}+\frac{1}{m}({\mathbf{y}}_{j_{t},t+1}-{\mathbf{y}}_{j_{t},t}).

(23)

If
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
were the exact subgradient of
\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{t})
, the classical stochasic (sub-)gradient update would have involved updating
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
with
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}-\eta_{t}{\mathbf{v}}_{t}-\eta_{t}{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
. Since
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
is only an approximate subgradient, additional correction terms need to be included. The update in
[
13
, Sec. 4]
is motivated by the connection between the subgradient and the proximal operation, and requires each regularizer to be proximally tractable. Here, we use a similar connection, but introduce a key innovation: we replace
g_{j_{t}}
with its linearized version
{\tilde{g}}_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{x}}_{t}):=g_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})+\langle\nabla g_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}),{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\rangle
within the update. Hence replacing
\phi_{j_{t}}
by
{\tilde{\phi}}_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{x}}_{t})=h({\mathbf{u}})+\gamma[{\tilde{g}}_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{x}}_{t})]_{+}
, we propose the update

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}

\displaystyle={\text{prox}_{\eta_{t}{\tilde{\phi}}_{j_{t}}}\!\left({\mathbf{x}}_{t}-\eta_{t}{\mathbf{v}}_{t}-\eta_{t}{\mathbf{y}}_{t}+\eta_{t}{\mathbf{y}}_{j_{t},t}\right)}

(24)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}h({\mathbf{u}})+\gamma[g_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})+\langle\nabla g_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}),{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\rangle]_{+}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\tfrac{1}{2\eta_{t}}\left\|{\mathbf{x}}_{t}-\eta_{t}{\mathbf{v}}_{t}-\eta_{t}{\mathbf{y}}_{t}+\eta_{t}{\mathbf{y}}_{j_{t},t}-{\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}.

(25)

As explained earlier, such a hinge-proximal update can be easily carried out if
h
is proximally tractable. Specifically, we can use a one-dimensional search method to solve (
24
) to accuracy
\epsilon
using at most
O(\log(1/\epsilon))
proximal evaluations of
h
. Interestingly, the linearization step also makes the update for
{\mathbf{y}}_{j_{t},t+1}
different from that in
[
13
]
, which is now written as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}_{j_{t},t+1}={\mathbf{y}}_{j_{t},t}+\frac{1}{2\eta_{t}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}-{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1})-({\mathbf{v}}_{t}+{\mathbf{y}}_{t}).

(26)

Specifically, the factor of 2 in the denominator of the second term on the right of (

[
13

V
Nested Hinge-Proximal SGD

In this section, we consider the regime where both
n
and
m
are large and seek complexity bounds that are effectively independent of both quantities. To remove the explicit dependence on
n
, we revert to vanilla SGD on the smooth component, without variance reduction. However, as discussed earlier, dependence on
m
is unavoidable without additional assumptions on the geometry of the constraint region. We therefore rely on the linear-regularity assumption
A6
, which ensures that the distance of any point to the overall feasible set
{\mathcal{X}}
is controlled by the mean-squared distance to the individual constraint sets
{\mathcal{X}}_{j}

[
19
]
.

A second challenge comes from the penalty parameter
\gamma
in the exact-penalty reformulation, which typically scales as
{\mathcal{O}\left(m\right)}
and thus propagates an
m
-dependence into the complexity results. To address these issues, we consider a nested scheme, where the outer loop performs a standard SGD step
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}={\mathbf{x}}_{t}-\eta_{t}\nabla f_{i_{t}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})
whereas the inner loop computes a constraint-corrected proximal point
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
for a randomly selected constraint
g_{j_{t}}
by solving

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=\arg\min_{{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2\eta_{t}}\left\|{\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}+h({\mathbf{u}})

\displaystyle\text{s.t}\hskip 5.69054ptg_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{u}})\leq 0

(38)

for a randomly selected
j_{t}
. To ensure tractability, we utilize the exact penalty reformulation of (
V

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=\arg\min_{{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tfrac{1}{2\eta_{t}}\left\|{\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}+h({\mathbf{u}})+\gamma_{t}[g_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{u}})]_{+}

(39)

\beta_{t}\eta_{t}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}_{s+1}

\displaystyle={\text{prox}_{\beta_{t}\eta_{t}{\tilde{\phi}}_{j_{t}}(\cdot,{\mathbf{u}}_{s})}\!\left((1-\beta_{t}){\mathbf{u}}_{s}+\beta_{t}{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\right)}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{{\mathbf{u}}}\tfrac{1}{2\beta_{t}\eta_{t}}\left\|(1-\beta_{t}){\mathbf{u}}_{s}+\beta_{t}{\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}+h({\mathbf{u}})

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma_{t}[g_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{u}}_{s})+\langle\nabla g_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{u}}_{s}),{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}_{s}\rangle]_{+}

(40)

As earlier, we define

and
{\tilde{\phi}}_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{x}})=h({\mathbf{u}})+\gamma_{t}[g_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{x}})+\langle\nabla g_{j_{t}}({\mathbf{x}}),{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{x}}\rangle]_{+}

associated with the constraint of (

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\frac{1}{2\eta_{t}}\left\|{\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}}\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\eta_{t}}\left\|{\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\right\|^{2}}{\nu}\leq\frac{\left\|{\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}}\right\|^{2}}{2\eta_{t}\nu}.

Since

Observe that compared to HPS and VR-HPS, the nested scheme above decouples the choice of the penalty parameter
\gamma_{t}
from the number of constraints

is no longer characterized by (

VII
Conclusion

We propose a hinge-proximal framework for solving high-dimensional stochastic strongly convex optimization problems with functional constraints. Unlike existing approaches that require globally Lipschitz continuous constraint functions, the proposed framework utilizes exact penalty reformulation that admits smooth constraint functions that need not have bounded gradients. The three instances of the framework, namely hinge-proximal SGD (HPS), variance-reduced HPS, and nested HPS, achieve state-of-the-art or better oracle complexities, while being computationally efficient by using only one constraint gradient per-iteration. We demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithms on a robust regression problem handling noisy features.

A critical limitation of our current analysis is that it applies only to strongly convex objectives. It remains open whether the hinge-proximal framework can be used to remove the bounded-gradient assumption in the general convex (non-strongly convex) and nonconvex regimes. Beyond the three instances proposed here, the hinge-proximal framework may also be extended to develop distributed variants or projection-free (Frank–Wolfe–type) algorithms.
