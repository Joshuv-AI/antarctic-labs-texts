Diagnostic Concordance and Interobserver Variability Between Tele-Dermatology (Store-and-Forward) and In-Person Evaluation for Inpatient Dermatology Consultations
Authors: Pratibha Gupta, Deepika Pandhi, Chander Grover, Rajeev Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Limited availability of dermatologists, the high burden of skin ailments in hospitalized patients, and lack of access to dermatology training within primary care highlight the need for tele-dermatology. Data about the diagnostic reliability of tele-dermatology and interobserver variability in inpatient settings are limited.Aim and Objectives:To assess diagnostic concordance and interobserver variability between tele-dermatology (store-and-forward) and in-person evaluation for inpatient dermatology consultations.Patients and Methods:One hundred hospitalized patients, sent by referring physicians for dermatology consultations, were evaluated in-person by certified dermatologists (CD) and trainee dermatologists (TrD). Another set of CD and TrD evaluated the store-and-forward case using tele-evaluation. Diagnosis and investigations recommended were recorded. Final diagnosis was made for each case by CD (in-person) after detailed investigations. The final diagnosis was considered the gold standard and used for comparisons. Percentage concordance between the evaluators and kappa values was calculated.Results:CD (in-person) and CD (tele) were found to have maximum partial diagnostic concordance (88%), concordance in biopsy decisions (89%), and dermoscopy recommendations (85.7%) among all groups. Diagnostic categories by CD (tele) that matched with the final diagnosis were infections, pediatric, papulosquamous, and hair and nail disorders. TrDs (tele) were able to diagnose infections, eczematous disorders and papulosquamous disorders correctly. Discordant cases by CD (tele) were drug-induced and contact dermatitis. TrD (tele) had also diagnosed drug-induced and contact dermatitis incorrectly, in addition to lymphatic and vascular disorders. Referring physicians had the lowest diagnostic concordance with CD and TrD.Limitations:It was a single-center study, and dermatological emergency cases were excluded.Conclusion:Teleconsultation by CD and TrD can be an alternative to evaluation by non-dermatologists for providing dermatological services to inpatients. It may further supplement in-person evaluation, especially for complex or high-risk cases.

Introduction

Tele-dermatology (TD) refers to the use of electronic telecommunication tools to provide dermatologic services from a distance. Its utilization in inpatient dermatologic consultations is referred to as inpatient TD.[1] Skin disorders are the fourth leading cause of nonfatal disease burden, affecting around one-third of the world’s population.[2] A recent study reported that 1 in 8 hospitalized patients suffered from skin diseases, indicating a high prevalence of skin disease in inpatient settings.[3] Furthermore, the availability of dermatologists is limited, especially in rural and remote areas.[3] Most of these cases are managed by primary-care physicians. However, limited access to dermatologists may sometimes pose challenges in achieving optimal diagnosis and management.[4] TD increases the probability of making an accurate and prompt diagnosis, facilitating initiation of treatment, hence improving the overall clinical outcome.[5]

TD has been widely studied in outpatient settings, showing promising levels of diagnostic accuracy and concordance with in-person evaluations.[67] Diagnostic accuracy depends on the image quality, history adequacy, and dermatologist experience.[8] A meta-analysis by Bourkas et al.[9] reported an overall moderate to substantial agreement between TD and face-to-face dermatology consultations across various skin conditions. However, there are limited data on the application and challenges of TD in inpatient populations, especially in low-resource settings like India. Our study aims to address this gap by focusing specifically on the diagnostic concordance in hospitalized dermatology referrals using both TD and in-person evaluations.

Patients and Methods

The study, conducted from January 2021 to August 2022, evaluated 100 consecutive dermatology referrals of patients admitted to different clinical departments, excluding emergencies. The study was conducted at the University College of Medical Sciences, Delhi, after approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee–Human Research (IEC-HR) (IECHR/2020/PG/46/57; approved on 21/12/2020), and informed consent was obtained from the parent/legal guardian along with age-appropriate assent from the pediatric patient after explaining the procedure in simple and understandable terms. There were a total of 5 evaluators for each case, including certified dermatologist (CD) (in-person), CD (tele), trainee dermatologist (TrD) (in-person), TrD (tele), and referring physician. CD had a total of 4 years of experience in dermatology, including 3 years of MD degree course. TrD was a resident doctor who was pursuing the course of MD dermatology and had only 1 year of experience. The referring physician was the resident doctor of the referring department and had only a graduation level of knowledge in dermatology. None of the evaluators had any prior experience in teleconsultation.

The diagnosis for the dermatological condition considered by the referring physician was recorded, after which these patients were further assessed by CD and TrD by in-person evaluation independently. Their diagnosis was recorded. TrD also captured clinical images using a Canon EOS M200 digital camera with a 24.1 Megapixel CMOS sensor, following American Telemedicine Association guidelines.[10] The images were captured under standardized lighting conditions. The color and white balance of the camera were calibrated accordingly. Several views were taken covering all the skin lesions, which include far, medium, and close-up views. Complementary views were also included. Image quality was reviewed on site, and suboptimal images were retaken. These images, along with clinical data, formed store-and-forward cases that were assessed remotely by another set of CD and TrD via teleconsultation. The response from all evaluators, including the provisional diagnosis, differential diagnosis, decision to biopsy, and performing dermoscopy, was recorded in the predesigned form. The final diagnosis was made for each case by the same CD who performed the in-person evaluation, after considering the clinical findings and all relevant investigation reports. Treatment was given according to the department protocol by the CD (in-person).

Diagnosis by all five evaluators was compared with the final diagnosis to calculate the diagnostic accuracy. Diagnostic accuracy was categorized as completely accurate (matching the final diagnosis), partially accurate (one differential matching), inaccurate (no match), or indeterminate (not able to make a diagnosis). We also compared the diagnoses among the five evaluators, and diagnostic concordance was calculated. Complete concordance was defined as the same results by both evaluators, partial concordance as a completely accurate diagnosis by one evaluator, and partially accurate diagnosis by the other evaluator. Discordance was defined as an inaccurate diagnosis by any evaluator.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 20.0 Statistics. Quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and qualitative ones as absolute values with 95% confidence interval (CI). The proportion of complete concordance and partial concordance for diagnosis with 95% CI was determined. Concordance was calculated as prevalence-adjusted and bias-adjusted Cohen’s kappa values with 95% CI. The percentage agreement in the decision to biopsy and to perform dermoscopy was also calculated. Chi-square and Fisher’s exact test were used to calculate the significance of the difference between percentages of agreement between different groups. A P value of <0.05 was considered significant.

Diagnostic concordance between in-person evaluation and tele-evaluation

Partial concordance was found to be the highest between CD (in-person) and CD (tele) and was 88%. Complete concordance was 65%. TrD (in-person) and TrD (tele) had the highest complete concordance (69%) but more discordant cases when compared with the final diagnosis.

Other dermatologist groups had lower partial concordance [Table 2], while referring physicians showed the lowest concordance [Table 3]. A statistically significant difference (P < 0.001) was observed in diagnostic concordance between referring physicians and dermatologists (both certified and trainees). The kappa statistics in our study reinforces the diagnostic reliability of dermatologists over referring physicians. CDs achieved substantial agreement in partial diagnoses (κ = 0.76) when comparing in-person and teleconsultations, and trainee dermatologists showed moderate agreement (κ = 0.64). In contrast, referring physicians demonstrated poor or even negative agreement, highlighting a significant gap in diagnostic accuracy.

Diagnostic accuracy of certified dermatologists for various disease categories

Disease categories diagnosed by CD (in-person) that showed a complete match with final diagnosis included vascular/lymphatic disorders (100%), infections (84%), nail/hair (90%), and pediatric cases (89%). Contact dermatitis (75%), drug-induced dermatoses (67%), and eczematous disorders (67%) also had a significant level of accuracy. CD (tele) had the highest accuracy for papulosquamous disorders (80%) and infections (59%). Hair and nail disorders had a 60% match [Table 4]. Among pediatric cases, partial and complete matches were similar to adult cases including for vascular and lymphatic disorders, infections, and hair and nail disorders [Table 5].

Diagnostic accuracy of trainee dermatologists for various disease categories

Disease categories diagnosed by TrD (in-person) that matched completely with the final diagnosis were papulosquamous disorder (80%), infections (62%), and vascular and lymphatic disorders (60%). Disease categories diagnosed by TrD (tele) that matched completely with the final diagnosis were mainly papulosquamous (80%), eczematous disorders (67%), infections (67%), and vascular and lymphatic disorders (60%) [Table 4].

Discordant cases by certified and trainee dermatologists

Evaluation of cases by TrD and tele-evaluation had more discordant cases than CD and in-person evaluations. CD (tele), TrD (in-person), and TrD (tele) misdiagnosed 9, 16, and 15 cases, respectively. CD (tele) had higher errors in drug-induced dermatoses (33%).

TrD (in-person) had discordant diagnoses in pediatric cases (11%), drug-induced dermatoses (17%), contact dermatitis (25%), papulosquamous (20%), and eczematous disorders (17%). TrD (tele) misdiagnosed 11% of pediatric cases, 33% of drug-induced dermatoses, 25% of contact dermatitis, and 20% of vascular/lymphatic disorders. TrD (tele) diagnosed eczematous disorders more accurately compared to TrD (in-person) (67% vs 17%), which is unexpected because eczema is usually better assessed in-person, where touch and texture can be evaluated. This difference may be attributed to the small sample size (n = 6). Most of the cases that were wrongly diagnosed in the pediatric group were drug-induced dermatoses.

Referring physicians had 21% discordance compared to CD (in-person), misdiagnosing 11% of infections like erythrasma, impetigo, and herpes, along with pediatric, eczematous, hair, and nail disorders [Table 4]. Over half (56%) of referrals lacked a diagnosis or used nonspecific terms.

Concordance in biopsy decisions

Biopsy requests were made by CD (tele), TrD (tele), TrD (in-person), and CD (in-person) in 22%, 17%, 15%, and 14% of cases, respectively. Diagnostic concordance in biopsy recommendation between CD (in-person) with CD (tele), TrD (in-person), and TrD (tele) was 89%, 78.5%, and 71.4%, respectively. CD (tele) recommended more biopsies as compared to TrD and in-person evaluations.

Concordance in dermoscopy decisions

Dermoscopy was performed in 30 cases by the TrD (in-person) and in 23 cases by the CD (in-person). Additionally, based on questionnaire response, dermoscopy was requested in 25 cases by the TrD (tele) and in 31 cases by the CD (tele). It was primarily used in cases where clinical images were insufficient to make a confident diagnosis or when the morphology suggested multiple possible differentials or to confirm the diagnosis.

Dermoscopy was most commonly utilized in cases of Bowenoid papulosis, onychomycosis, subungual hematoma, melanocytic nevus, verruca vulgaris, seborrheic keratosis, trichotillomania, lichen sclerosus et atrophicus, tinea capitis, nevus depigmentosus, and nevus sebaceus, where dermoscopic patterns provided diagnostic clues.

Following dermoscopic evaluation, the CD (tele) was able to narrow the differential diagnosis to a single provisional diagnosis in 6 cases, and the TrD (tele) in 4 cases. However, the improvement in diagnostic concordance was not statistically significant.

Discussion

Diagnostic concordance between CD (in-person) and CD (tele)

Our study showed a moderate diagnostic concordance between TD and in-person evaluation, with the highest concordance observed between CD (in-person) and CD (tele) (65% complete, 88% partial). This supports TD as a useful adjunct in dermatological evaluation. Recent meta-analyses have highlighted the growing reliability of tele-dermatology as a diagnostic tool. A review of 44 studies by Bourkas et al.[9] (2023) reported a pooled diagnostic agreement rate of 68.9% and a kappa concordance of 0.67. Similarly, Bastola et al.[11] (2021) found substantial concordance between tele-dermatologists and in-person dermatologists, supporting TD role in contexts where face-to-face evaluations may be limited. Tan et al.[12] found a partial diagnostic concordance of 89.2% in dermatologic consultations in 65 cases of COVID-19. Our study included two cases from the COVID ward (1 each had nail changes in COVID and cutaneous mucormycosis) that were correctly diagnosed by CD (tele), supporting the use of TD in COVID patients while minimizing exposure.[1314]

The diagnosis of CD (tele) was accurate in infections (59%), similar to the findings of Keller et al.[15] and Okita et al.[16] A recent study details the management of a pediatric impetigo outbreak in Haryana between July and September 2023 using the e-Sanjeevani 2.0 telemedicine platform. A total of 165 cases were remotely diagnosed and treated by dermatologists and pediatricians.[17] TD can reduce the burden of inpatient dermatology consultations, as infections constitute the major group of diagnosed dermatoses in dermatology referrals, especially in rural areas.[4] Conditions like dermatophytosis, pityriasis versicolor, molluscum contagiosum, varicella, and herpes zoster were accurately diagnosed. This could be attributed to the characteristic morphological appearance of the lesions in most of these disorders that enables diagnosis only by good-quality photographs of the lesions without much need for any in-person evaluation. However, conditions requiring further tests or mimicking other conditions, such as herpes labialis, cutaneous candidiasis, and scabies, had partial matches. Such cases can still be managed by teleconsultation by providing them basic training.

Teleconsultation also performed well in diagnosing papulosquamous disorders, hair/nail disorders, eczematous conditions, and syndromic diseases like neurofibromatosis, Klippel-Trénaunay syndrome, and tuberous sclerosis that could be explained by the classical morphological appearance. Syndromic conditions have multiple clinical manifestations not shared by any other disorder and are easy to diagnose if photographs of all the involved regions are provided. TD can be helpful in syndromic disorders as the skin manifestation can give clues for these syndromes such as cutaneous neurofibromas, adenoma sebaceum, and shagreen patch.[1819] Among pediatric cases, teleconsultation performed well in infections, hair and nail disorders, and vascular disorders. There was discordance in drug-induced dermatosis and miscellaneous cases such as smegma pearl, nevus depigmentosus, and post-edema exfoliation. Inpatient pediatric TD can also decrease the burden and provide timely access to certified pediatric dermatologists.[20]

CD (tele) had lower diagnostic accuracy for drug-induced dermatoses (33%) and contact dermatitis (25%), similar to Hines et al.’s findings.[21] A suspected toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) case was later confirmed as drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), highlighting the need for in-person evaluation in complex drug reactions. This could be due to lack of adequate information such as detailed drug history, time between drug intake and rash development, inability to do simple bedside tests like Nikolsky’s sign, and lack of results of investigation such as absolute eosinophil count and deranged liver function test. However, tele-triage can help prioritize severe cases and prevent delays in providing care to patients.[22] Video TD is more suitable for such cases.[23] Malignant conditions were challenging, as seen in a misdiagnosed vulval carcinoma. Consistent with Vestergaard et al.,[24] TD had lower accuracy for malignancies, emphasizing the need for in-person evaluation in suspected neoplastic cases. These findings highlight the role of TD as a complementary tool, helpful for initial assessment and triage, but not as a substitute for in-person evaluation in complex or high-risk cases.

Agreement in biopsy decision between in-person and tele-evaluation

In our study, biopsy was recommended more often by CD (tele) (22%) than CD (in-person) (14%), likely due to diagnostic challenges from morphological similarities. This aligns with findings of Khosravi et al.[25] In-person evaluation reduces biopsy need by allowing assessment of finer details like varicosities in venous ulcers or scaling in seborrheic dermatitis or verrucous or filiform surface of verruca which may be missed in photos.

Agreement in dermoscopy decision between in-person and tele-evaluation

CD (tele) recommended dermoscopy in more cases as compared to trainee and in-person evaluations. Dermoscopy helped in distinguishing clinically similar entities. It helped in differentiating genital warts from Bowenoid papulosis by identifying the presence of finger-like structures and dotted vessels favoring a viral etiology. Similarly, seborrheic keratosis was distinguished from melanocytic nevi by identifying comedo-like openings and milia-like cysts. In nail disorders, dermoscopy clarified cases of chronic paronychia by showing periungual inflammation and cuticle disruption. We could not find any study that has used dermoscopy images in inpatient TD consultations. However, this is comparable to multiple studies by authors like Moreno-Ramírez et al.[26] and Markun et al.,[27] who reported that dermoscopic images in a teleconsultation improve the reliability of telediagnosis, improving both sensitivity and specificity. However, most of the studies were for outpatients and for melanocytic and malignant conditions that were not represented in our study.

Effects of years of experience of a dermatologist on diagnostic concordance

The highest diagnostic concordance was between CD (in-person) and CD (tele) who were the most experienced ones. Concordance was slightly lower with TrD; however, it was significantly higher than with referring physicians, highlighting the impact of even 1 year of dermatology training. Our findings aligned with Azfar et al.[28], who reported higher concordance among experienced dermatologists, whereas Gabel et al.[6] found no link between experience and diagnostic concordance.

TrD correctly diagnosed several cases of infections, hair and nail disorders, and vascular and lymphatic disorders but had fewer correct cases than CD. Cases with typical lesion morphology were easy to diagnose. TrD often missed complex cases like dissecting cellulitis, prurigo simplex, pederus dermatitis, and lichen nitidus, particularly in mild or residual presentations. A two-step TD model with initial triage by a trained physician followed by CD review for complex cases, which could reduce unnecessary referrals.

Effect of years of experience of a dermatologist and biopsy recommendation

A biopsy was recommended in 15% of cases by TrD (in-person) as compared to 14% in CD (in-person), which may be because of less diagnostic certainty in a given case by trainees. TrD (tele) recommended biopsy in 17% of cases, indicating an increasing need for biopsy compared to in-person evaluation; however, it was less than CD (tele) (22%). This may be because of less knowledge, and therefore, fewer differentials were given by TrD.

Diagnostic concordance between referring physicians and other evaluators

Our study found the lowest diagnostic concordance between referring physicians and dermatologists [Table 3], consistent with prior studies conducted by Georgesen et al.,[4] Ortiz et al.,[29] and Khosravi et al.[2530] Only Keller et al.[15] reported higher concordance. The kappa values for referring physicians were negative, indicating concordance lower than what would be expected by chance alone. This suggests a significant level of discordance between the referring physicians and the dermatologists. Many referrals used nonspecific terms or provided only a diagnostic category without a specific diagnosis, indicating unfamiliarity of the physician with common dermatoses, highlighting the need for interdepartmental consultation. Typical cases (e.g. varicella, harlequin ichthyosis, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, neurofibromatosis, and venous ulcer) showed full concordance, while tinea and drug reactions were often over diagnosed. Erythrasma, pressure urticaria, seborrheic dermatitis, seborrheic capitis, and hand and feet eczema have been diagnosed incorrectly as tinea in our study. Teleconsultation can guide basic tests like potassium hydroxide mount (KOH) mount to reduce misdiagnoses.

Twenty-one percent of cases were discordant in comparison to CD (in-person). Approximately 11% of infections, including erythrasma, impetigo, and herpes, were misdiagnosed. Other discordant cases included pediatric, eczematous, hair, and nail disorders. Acute cutaneous lupus was incorrectly diagnosed as a drug reaction, genital herpes as vulval carcinoma, congenital melanocytic nevus as a wart, and nevus sebaceous as a wart. Many of these cases were diagnosed correctly by dermatologists by in-person and tele-evaluation in our study.

Limitations

It was a single-center study carried out in tertiary care settings, and dermatological emergency cases were excluded. Another limitation of the study was that the final diagnosis was made by the same CD who performed the in-person evaluation, potentially introducing observer bias; however, the teleconsultation evaluators were different reducing the bias in the comparative arm.

Conclusion

TD showed moderate to substantial concordance with in-person evaluation for many diagnostic categories. TD provides quicker access to specialized dermatological expertise especially in resource-limited settings and increases the probability of making an accurate and prompt diagnosis and hence improves the overall clinical outcome. Teleconsultation can be a good complementary tool for in-person evaluation, particularly in complex or high-risk cases.
