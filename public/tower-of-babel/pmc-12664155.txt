Senior–Løken syndrome with IQCB1/NPHP5 mutation in an adult: a case report
Authors: İrem Demirtas, Sibel Gökcay Bek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSenior–Løken syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive ciliopathy characterized by nephronophthisis and early-onset retinal dystrophy. It is typically diagnosed in childhood, and adult-onset diagnosis is rare and may delay renal-protective interventions. Here, we report a rare case of Senior–Løken syndrome in an adult with an IQCB1/NPHP5 mutation.Case presentationA 20-year-old Turkish male presented with rotatory nystagmus since birth, progressive visual impairment from childhood, and chronic kidney disease that began during adolescence. The patient exhibited polyuria and polydipsia, and the ocular electrophysiology results were consistent with retinitis pigmentosa. Initial laboratory results were as follows: hemoglobin 11.03 g/dL, urea 56.65 mg/dL, creatinine 2.59 mg/dL, estimated glomerular filtration rate 34.14 mL/min/1.73 m2. Renal ultrasonography revealed increased parenchymal echogenicity, and abdominal computed tomography revealed small cystic lesions in both kidneys. Kidney biopsy revealed predominantly tubulointerstitial changes (tubular atrophy, basal membrane thickening, and interstitial fibrosis), and immunostaining was negative. Whole-exome sequencing identified homozygous full-gene deletion of IQCB1/NPHP5, confirming the diagnosis of SLS. The patient was managed with conservative and supportive treatments.ConclusionChronic kidney disease in Senior–Løken syndrome is often diagnosed late, leading to a poor clinical outcome. Early diagnosis and timely management of renal complications can prevent the progression to end-stage renal disease.

Introduction

Senior–Løken syndrome (SLS) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by nephronophthisis (NPHP) and early-onset retinal dystrophy [1]. The clinical features of NPHP are generally nonspecific, and signs of chronic kidney disease (CKD), such as edema and hypertension, are uncommon [2]. In patients with urinary concentration defects, polyuria, polydipsia, and reduced maximum urine concentration are observed, and the condition progressively advances to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [3]. Renal NPHP is a heterogeneous ciliary dysfunction or renal ciliopathy [4]. This condition can lead to cystic kidneys or renal cystic dysplasia and is the most common genetic cause of CKD in the first 20 years of life [5]. Cases are classified according to the age of ESRD onset as infantile, juvenile, adolescent, or late onset [6]. Ultrasonography is the most effective imaging modality for clinical diagnosis of NPHP. Typical features include normal or reduced kidney size, loss of corticomedullary differentiation, increased echogenicity, and the formation of corticomedullary cysts [7]. Histopathology is nonspecific and demonstrates tubular atrophy, thickening or thinning of tubular membranes, interstitial fibrosis, and inflammation [8]. Here, we report a rare case of nephronophthisis with an IQCB1/NPHP5 mutation in an adult.

Case presentation

A 20-year-old Turkish male visited the nephrology clinic with complaints of general discomfort, tiredness, widespread muscle pain, polyuria, irregular kidney function test results, and high blood pressure. He had long-standing visual impairment but no other known diseases, and there was no family history of kidney disease. The family history revealed a consanguineous marriage, but no siblings (four sisters and one brother) were affected by the disease. Since birth, the patient had experienced nystagmus and started wearing corrective glasses at the age of 2 years. He was unmarried, not engaged in sexual activity, and unemployed. Visual impairment was noted at the age of 8 years, and he was diagnosed with retinitis pigmentosa in 2008. Polyuria and polydipsia symptoms appeared at the age of 18 years (2018).

At initial presentation in January 2020, the daily fluid intake was 5 L, and the urine output was 3.5 L. Laboratory results included hemoglobin 11.03 g/dL, urea 56.65 mg/dL, creatinine 2.59 mg/dL, sodium 140 mmol/L, potassium 3.8 mmol/L, albumin 4.26 g/dL, corrected calcium 9.19 mg/dL, phosphorus 3.58 mg/dL, iPTH 226 pg/mL, spot urine protein/creatinine ratio 770.15 mg/g, and eGFR 34.14 mL/min/1.73 m2. Physical examination was unremarkable, except for ocular findings. His height was 180 cm, weight was 72 kg, and blood pressure was 110/80 mmHg (Table 1). A kidney biopsy performed in February 2020 revealed 11 glomeruli, 2 of which exhibited global sclerosis. Focal mesangial cell proliferation was observed in the remaining glomeruli. Focal tubular atrophy, basal membrane thickening, and hyaline casts were observed in the tubule lumens. Interstitial mononuclear infiltration and prominent fibrosis were observed. The vascular structures exhibited myointimal thickening. Immunostaining was negative. These findings were consistent with predominant tubulointerstitial changes.

The patient was managed with conservative and supportive therapy: dietary sodium restriction, sevelamer carbonate 800 mg thrice daily, sodium bicarbonate 500 mg twice daily, allopurinol 300 mg/day, erythropoietin alfa 4000 IU subcutaneously once weekly, and amlodipine 5 mg/day for blood pressure control. Follow-up was performed intermittently thereafter.

At the 2-month follow-up, urea was 60 mg/dL, creatinine 2.74 mg/dL, and uric acid 8.49 mg/dL; treatment was continued. The patient subsequently missed follow-up visits. In August 2021, he presented to the emergency department with nausea and vomiting and was admitted for treatment of hypovolemic hyponatremia.

Genetic analysis was performed because of suspected NPHP. WES performed in December 2021 revealed homozygous full-gene deletion of IQCB1/NPHP5. Three years after diagnosis, the urea, creatinine, and GFR levels were 202 mg/dL, 6.11 mg/dL, and 11.8 mL/min/1.73 m2, respectively, leading to ESRD classification and initiation of peritoneal dialysis in January 2023. The latest follow-up showed urea 119 mg/dL, creatinine 12.9 mg/dL, and GFR 4.72 mL/min/1.73 m. The patient continues to receive follow-up and treatment. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient.

Discussion and conclusions

SLS is a rare syndrome characterized by a combination of NPHP and retinal degeneration, first described by Senior and Løken in 1961 [9, 10]. Its prevalence is approximately 1 in 1,000,000. The genetic cause of NPHP can be identified through biallelic pathogenic variants in any of the 19 known NPHP-related genes. [11]. The NPHP5 gene, located on human chromosome 3, encodes a 69-kDa protein (nephrocystin-5 or IQCB1) that plays a role in the formation of photoreceptor outer segment disk membranes. These mutations affect both renal and retinal functions [12]. Unlike NPHP1–4 mutations, retinal degeneration develops in nearly 100% of patients with NPHP5 mutations. While other nephrocystin genes may involve multiple organs, NPHP5 mutations are exclusively associated with the retinal–kidney phenotype, making it the classic Senior–Løken gene [13].

Although most reported IQCB1 mutations are present in childhood, adult-onset cases are rare (Table 2). Chiu et al. reported a similar phenotype in a Filipino male. Our patient, who received a diagnosis in adulthood, mirrors these uncommon cases in terms of clinical development [14]. Retinitis pigmentosa presents with bone spicule retinal pigmentation and initially manifests as night blindness, which later progresses to loss of daytime vision. In our patient, visual impairment began in early childhood and gradually affected daily activities, which is consistent with the typical disease progression [15]. A major clinical challenge in SLS is delayed diagnosis, which accelerates the loss of renal function. Early genetic diagnosis allows the initiation of renoprotective measures and may delay progression to ESRD. Our patient exhibited rapid renal deterioration due to an adult-onset diagnosis.

Treatment is primarily conservative, aiming to slow the decline in renal function, including fluid and sodium management, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) blockade, anemia management, and treatment of mineral and bone disorders [16]. Innovative treatments, including tolvaptan, mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors, genetic therapy, RNA-based therapies, and stem cell transplantation for retinal changes, show potential; however, experience with these treatments is still limited [17]. Therefore, it is essential to closely monitor patients and tailor treatment plans to individual needs in these situations.

In conclusion, SLS should be considered in young patients with renal impairment and retinitis pigmentosa. Early diagnosis and timely management of renal complications can prevent the progression to ESRD. Genetic testing for IQCB1 mutations is recommended for young adults with similar clinical features.
