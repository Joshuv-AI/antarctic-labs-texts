The Confluent Heun functions in Black Hole Perturbation Theory: a spacetime interpretation
Marica Minucci, Rodrigo Panosso Macedo

Abstract
This work provides a spacetime interpretation of the confluent Heun functions within black hole perturbation theory (BHPT) and explores their relationship to the hyperboloidal framework. In BHPT, the confluent Heun functions are solutions to the radial Teukolsky equation, but they are traditionally studied without an explicit reference to the underlying spacetime geometry. Here, we show that the distinct behaviour of these functions near their singular points reflects the structure of key surfaces in black hole spacetimes. By interpreting homotopic transformations of the confluent Heun functions as changes in the spacetime foliation, we connect these solutions to different regions of the black hole’s global structure, such as the past and future event horizons, past and future null infinity, spatial infinity, and even past and future timelike infinity. We also discuss the relationship between the confluent Heun functions and the hyperboloidal formulation of the Teukolsky equation. Although neither confluent Heun form of the radial Teukolsky equation can be interpreted as hyperboloidal slices, this approach offers new insights into wave propagation and scattering from a global black hole spacetime perspective.

Introduction

Over the past decade, the hyperboloidal framework has established itself as a fundamental approach to black hole perturbation theory (BHPT)
Zenginoglu (2008)
;
Zenginoglu (2011)
;
Jaramillo
et al. (2021)
;
Panosso Macedo (2024)
. One of the central motivations for this formalism is the resolution of a long standing puzzle within this theory
Panosso Macedo and
Zenginoglu (2024)
. In the post-merger dynamics, gravitational waves carry energy toward the black hole and to the infinitely far wave zone until the spacetime settles into a stationary regime. The gravitational waves signal in this ringdown phase has a very particular signature, with oscillations decaying exponentially at characteristic frequencies
\omega\in{\mathbb{C}}
, the so-called quasinormal modes
Kokkotas and
Schmidt (1999)
;
Nollert (1999)
;
Berti et al. (2009)
;
Konoplya and
Zhidenko (2011)
. The quasinormal mode problem is typically formulated as an eigenvalue problem and, associated to each frequency
\omega
, there exists a time-harmonic quasinormal mode function
\Psi
defined on an underlying Riemannian manifold with topology
{\mathbb{R}}\times\mathcal{S}^{2}
. In the traditional mathematical approach, these quasinormal mode eigenfunctions
\Psi
grow exponentially towards the black hole and infinity. It is therefore puzzling that black hole stability implies time decay for linearised perturbations, but the associated time-harmonic perturbations is singular in the asymptotic regions.

A resolution to this conundrum arises when the global structure of the spacetime is fully taken into account. It becomes clear that the singular behaviour of the quasinormal mode eigenfunctions is a mere consequence of the choice of coordinates employed, in particular the notion of constant time hypersurface yielding the Riemannian manifold. The typical choice for a time coordinate
t
is actually singular at the event horizon and at infinity. In other words, surfaces of
t=
constant accumulate at the bifurcation sphere
\mathcal{B}
and at spatial infinity
{i}^{0}
. Reformulating the problem in terms of regular spacelike surfaces extending between the black-hole event horizon
{\cal H}^{+}
and null infinity
{\mathscr{I}}^{+}
yields regular quasinormal mode eigenfunctions, as intuitively expected.

The hyperboloidal framework has led to great advances in the BHPT, see e.g.
Panosso Macedo (2024)
;
Panosso Macedo and
Zenginoglu (2024)
for reviews. All applications focus on scenarios where energy flows
exclusively
across
{\cal H}^{+}
and
{\mathscr{I}}^{+}
, with this property being the core feature defining the asymptotic behaviour of the field. However, there are further scenarios of interest in BHPT for which the field’s behaviour at other geometrical surfaces — such as
{\cal H}^{-}
and
{\mathscr{I}}^{-}
— play an important role.

As a first example, let us consider the traditional definition of quasinormal modes as the poles of the Green’s function associated with the underlying wave operator
Kokkotas and
Schmidt (1999)
;
Nollert (1999)
. Broadly speaking, to construct the correct Green’s function at a non-quasinormal mode frequency, one needs two linearly independent solutions to the homogenous wave equation, with each individual field satisfying the desired boundary conditions. In BHPT, these solutions are referred to as the “in” and “up” solutions, and they contain the field’s behaviour either at
\mathscr{I}^{-}
or
{\cal H}^{-}
, see e.g.
Chandrasekhar (1983)
;
Pound and Wardell (2020)
. For
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the “in” solution describes scattering of monochromatic waves by the black hole, and the corresponding transmission and reflexion coefficients provides valuable information about the spacetime.

Currently, the hyperboloidal framework in BHPT still lacks a comprehensive formalism and numerical infrastructure to tackle problems where these other asymptotic regions are also relevant. Developing this infrastructure is necessary to computationally address open problems in the field such as the relation between the traditional definition of quasinormal modes as the poles of the Green’s
Kokkotas and
Schmidt (1999)
;
Nollert (1999)
function and more recent approaches that interpret quasinormal modes as a formal eigenvalue problem on an appropriate Hilbert space
Gajic and Warnick (2020)
;
Gajic and Warnick (2021)
;
Gajic and Warnick (2024)
; understanding notions of quasinormal modes orthogonality
Zimmerman et al. (2015)
;
Green et al. (2023)
;
London (2023)
from the hyperboloidal perspective; or taking the fully global structure of the spacetime into account when studying wave scattering in black hole spacetimes.

This work presents initial steps towards filling this gap. For this purpose, we consider the Teukolsky equation in the Kerr spacetime, and we review the representation of solutions to the radial equation in terms of the confluent Heun function
Ronveaux (1995)

A decade after Teukolsky’s seminal works
Teukolsky (1972)
;
Teukolsky (1973)

Teukolsky and Press (1974)

In this work, we offer a spacetime interpretation for the Heun’s confluent solution in BHPT by considering its relation with the underlying coordinates system parametrising the spacetime. In a nutshell, the standard theoretical approach to the
confluent Heun equation
(CHE) always focuses on the linear ordinary differential equation (ODE)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{2}Z(z)}{dz^{2}}+\left(\dfrac{\gamma}{z}+\dfrac{\delta}{z-1}+\epsilon\right)\dfrac{dZ(z)}{dz}+\dfrac{\alpha z-q}{z(z-1)}Z(z)=0,

(1)

also known as the CHE in the canonical form. The equation has regular singular points at
z=0
and
z=1

\displaystyle Z(z)\sim 1+{\cal O}\big(z\big),

\displaystyle Z(z)\sim z^{1-\gamma}\left(1+{\cal O}\big(z\big)\right),

(2)

\displaystyle Z(z)\sim 1+{\cal O}\big(z-1\big),

\displaystyle Z(z)\sim(z-1)^{1-\delta}(1+{\cal O}\big(z-1\big)).

(3)

The equation has also an irregular singular point at
z\rightarrow\infty

\displaystyle Z(z)\sim\dfrac{1}{z^{\alpha/\epsilon}}\bigg(1+{\cal O}\big(1/z\big)\bigg),\quad Z(z)\sim\dfrac{e^{-\epsilon z}}{z^{\gamma+\delta-\alpha/\epsilon}}\bigg(1+{\cal O}\big(1/z\big)\bigg).

(4)

The full solution to eq. (
1

2
) or (
3

4

Further representations of the canonical form (

(5)

A useful qualitative description of the effects from transformation (

2

3

\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
, respectively. The asymptotic behaviour (
4

While such approach has its advantages at an analytical level, it misses the geometrical interpretation from the spacetime perspective. Since in BHPT the CHE actually results from projecting a wave equation into the frequency domain via a Fourier (or Laplace) transform

2
The Confluent Heun Equation

Following ref.
Ronveaux (1995)
, we begin with an introductory discussion about the confluent Heun equation and its solutions. The literature and results on this topic are vast, and a detailed analysis goes beyond the scope of this work (see
Ronveaux (1995)
for a complete review). Apart from reviewing some relevant properties and results, this section also serves the purpose of fixing the notation employed in this work.

The Heun equation is a second-order linear differential equation with four regular singular points, with the confluent Heun equation (CHE) arising when two singularities are brought into coincidence. The general form of the CHE reads

\dfrac{d^{2}}{dy^{2}}Y(y)+\left(\sum_{i=0}^{1}\dfrac{A_{i}}{y-y_{i}}+E\right)\dfrac{d}{dy}Y(y)+\left(\sum_{i=0}^{1}\dfrac{C_{i}}{y-y_{i}}+\sum_{i=0}^{1}\dfrac{B_{i}}{(y-y_{i})^{2}}+D\right)Y(y)=0,

(6)

with
y\in[0,\infty)
. The regular singular points
y_{0}
and
y_{1}
are arbitrary, and so are the coefficients
A_{i},B_{i},C_{i}
(
i=0,1
),
D,E
, whereas
y\rightarrow\infty
is an irregular singular point.

The two linear independent solutions to eq. (

, or as an asymptotic expansion around

Y(y)\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\cal K}_{i+}(y-y_{i})^{\varrho^{+}_{i}}+{\cal K}_{i-}(y-y_{i})^{\varrho^{-}_{i}},&{\rm for}&y\rightarrow y_{i}\\
{\cal K}_{\infty+}\,\dfrac{e^{y\varsigma^{+}}}{y^{\eta^{+}}}+{\cal K}_{\infty-}\,\dfrac{e^{y\varsigma^{-}}}{y^{\eta^{-}}}&{\rm for}&y\rightarrow\infty\\
\end{array}\right.

(7)

with the
{\cal K}_{\pm}

\varrho^{\pm}_{i}
,
\varsigma^{\pm}
and
\eta^{\pm}
are given by

\displaystyle\varrho^{\pm}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1-A_{i}}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{1-A_{i}}{2}\right)^{2}-B_{i}},

(8)

\displaystyle\varsigma^{\pm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{E}{2}\pm\dfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{E^{2}-4D},

(9)

\displaystyle\eta^{\pm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{1}\left(\dfrac{A_{i}}{2}\pm\dfrac{C_{i}-EA_{i}/2}{\sqrt{E^{2}-4D}}\right).

(10)

Despite the generic form of eq. (
6

. Alternatively, one may also map the independent variable

3
Kerr spacetime

In this section, we review the common approach to the usage of the confluent Heun equation in the context of black hole perturbation theory. The background spacetime is given by the Kerr solution, and the perturbation dynamics are dictated by the Teukolsky equation
Teukolsky (1973)
.

3.1
Boyer-Lindquist Coordinates

In black hole perturbation theory, the line element for the Kerr spacetime is traditionally expressed in terms of the Boyer-Lindquist (BL) coordinates
x^{\mu}=(t,r,\theta,\phi)
,

\displaystyle\,\textnormal{d}s^{2}=

\displaystyle-f(r,\theta)\,\textnormal{d}t^{2}-\dfrac{4Mar}{\Sigma(r,\theta)}\sin^{2}\theta\,\textnormal{d}t\,\textnormal{d}\varphi+\dfrac{\Sigma(r,\theta)}{\Delta(r)}\,\textnormal{d}r^{2}

(21)

\displaystyle+\Sigma(r,\theta)\,\textnormal{d}\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\left(\Sigma_{0}(r)+\dfrac{2Ma^{2}r}{\Sigma(r,\theta)}\sin^{2}\theta\right)\,\textnormal{d}\phi^{2}

with

\displaystyle\Delta(r)=r^{2}-2Mr+a^{2}=(r-r_{\rm h})(r-r_{\rm c}),

(22)

\displaystyle\Sigma(r,\theta)=r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta,\quad\Sigma_{0}(r)=\Sigma(r,0)=r^{2}+a^{2},

(23)

\displaystyle f(r,\theta)=1-\dfrac{2Mr}{\Sigma(r,\theta)},\quad f_{0}(r)=f(r,0).

(24)

The parameters
M
and
a
relate, respectively, to the black hole’s mass and angular momentum. The condition
\Delta(r)=0
defines the event (
r_{\rm h}
) and Cauchy (
r_{\rm c}
) horizons

\displaystyle\dfrac{r_{\rm h}}{M}=1+\sqrt{1-\dfrac{a^{2}}{M^{2}}},\quad\dfrac{r_{\rm c}}{M}=1-\sqrt{1-\dfrac{a^{2}}{M^{2}}}.

(25)

Some calculations are more convenient performed with dimensional quantities measured in terms of the event horizon length scale
r_{h}
, as opposed to the more common choice using the mass parameter
M
. For that purpose, we introduce a dimensionless black hole spin parameter
\kappa
equivalently defined by

\kappa=\dfrac{a}{r_{h}}=\dfrac{a}{M+\sqrt{M^{2}-a^{2}}},\quad\kappa^{2}=\dfrac{r_{\rm c}}{r_{\rm h}}.

(26)

With this definition, the mass parameter reads
2M=r_{\rm h}\left(1+\kappa^{2}\right)
.

Further important quantities for the Kerr metric are the horizons angular velocities

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm h}=\dfrac{a}{2Mr_{\rm h}}=\dfrac{\kappa}{r_{\rm h}(1+\kappa^{2})},\quad\Omega_{\rm c}=\dfrac{a}{2Mr_{\rm c}}=\dfrac{1}{r_{\rm h}\kappa(1+\kappa^{2})},

(27)

and the horizons surface gravities

\varkappa_{\rm h}=\dfrac{1}{4M}-M\Omega_{\rm h}^{2}=\dfrac{1-\kappa^{2}}{2r_{\rm h}(1+\kappa^{2})},\quad\varkappa_{\rm c}=\dfrac{1}{4M}-M\Omega_{\rm c}^{2}=-\dfrac{1-\kappa^{2}}{2r_{\rm h}\kappa^{2}(1+\kappa^{2})}.

(28)

In terms of these observables, the tortoise coordinate
r^{*}(r)
reads

\dfrac{dr^{*}(r)}{dr}=\dfrac{r^{2}+a^{a}}{\Delta(r)}\quad\Rightarrow\quad r^{*}(r)=r+2M\ln\left(\dfrac{r}{r_{\rm h}}\right)+\dfrac{1}{2\varkappa_{\rm h}}\ln\left(1-\dfrac{r_{\rm h}}{r}\right)+\dfrac{1}{2\varkappa_{\rm c}}\ln\left(1-\dfrac{r_{\rm c}}{r}\right),

(29)

whereas the “tortoise” azimutal angular phase
\chi(r)
reads

\dfrac{d\chi(r)}{dr}=\dfrac{a}{\Delta(r)}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\chi(r)=\dfrac{\Omega_{\rm h}}{2\varkappa_{\rm h}}\ln\left(1-\dfrac{r_{\rm h}}{r}\right)+\dfrac{\Omega_{\rm c}}{2\varkappa_{\rm c}}\ln\left(1-\dfrac{r_{\rm c}}{r}\right)

(30)

3.2
The Teukolsky equation

The equation governing the dynamics of perturbations around this spacetime is given by the (sourceless) Teukolsky equation. When expressed in terms of the Boyer-Lindquist coordinates
(t,r,\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle 0=-\left[\dfrac{(\Sigma_{0}(r))^{2}}{\Delta(r)}-a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\right]{}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\Psi_{,tt}(x^{a})-\dfrac{4Mar}{\Delta(r)}{}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\Psi_{,t\phi}(x^{a})-\left[\dfrac{a^{2}}{\Delta(r)}-\dfrac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}\right]{}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\Psi_{,\phi\phi}(x^{a})

\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}{\cal R}_{\ell m}(\omega;r)
and
\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}S_{\ell m}(\omega;\theta)
, namely the
angular Teukolsky equation

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right)\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}S_{\ell m}(\omega;\theta)+

\displaystyle+\bigg(a^{2}\omega^{2}\cos^{2}\theta-2\mathfrak{s}a\omega\cos\theta-\left(\dfrac{m+\mathfrak{s}\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\right)^{2}+\mathfrak{s}+A_{\ell m}\bigg)\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}S_{\ell m}(\omega;\theta)=0,

(36)

\displaystyle\Delta^{-\mathfrak{s}}(r)\,\dfrac{\,\textnormal{d}}{\,\textnormal{d}r}\left(\Delta^{\mathfrak{s}+1}(r)\,\dfrac{\,\textnormal{d}}{\,\textnormal{d}r}\right)\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}{\cal R}_{\ell m}(\omega;r)+\left(2i\mathfrak{s}\omega r-a^{2}\omega^{2}-A_{\ell m}\right)\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}{\cal R}_{\ell m}(\omega;r)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{(\omega\Sigma_{0})^{2}-4Mam\omega r+a^{2}m^{2}+2i\mathfrak{s}\left[am(r-M)-M\omega(r^{2}-a^{2})\right]}{\Delta(r)}\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}{\cal R}_{\ell m}(\omega;r)=0,

4
A spacetime interpretation to the Confluent Heun Equation solutions

We now present an alternative strategy to obtain the radial Teukolsky equation in the canonical form for the CHE. Instead of employing s-homotopic transformations to the radial ODE, we focus on the spacetime geometry, with particular attention to the role played by the choice of time coordinate. To exemplify the strategy, we begin discussing the well-know representation of the Teukolsky equation in Kerr coordinates
Teukolsky and Press (1974)
, and then we consider generic time slices.

4.1
Outgoing and Ingoing Kerr coordinates

Let us first introduce the outgoing Kerr coordinates
\hat{x}^{\mu}=(u,r,\theta,\hat{\phi})
via

t=u+r^{*}(r),\qquad\phi=\hat{\phi}+\chi(r),

(55)

with the tortoise coordinate
r^{*}(r)
and phase
\chi(r)
defined in eqs. (
29
) and (
30
). The resulting line element is not directly relevant to the discussion, but the expressions for the Kinnersley null tetrad (
33
) plays an important role as they define the Weyl curvature components
\Psi_{0}
and
\Psi_{4}
. In the coordinate system
\hat{x}^{\mu}
, the outgoing and ingoing Kinnersley null vectors read

\ell^{\hat{\mu}}_{+}=\delta^{\hat{\mu}}_{r},\qquad\ell^{\hat{\mu}}_{-}=\dfrac{1}{\Sigma(r,\theta)}\left((r^{2}+a^{2})\delta^{\hat{\mu}}_{u}-\dfrac{\Delta(r)}{2}\delta^{\hat{\mu}}_{r}+a\delta^{\hat{\mu}}_{\hat{\phi}}\right).

(56)

As expected, the Kinnersley null tetrad is adapted to the outgoing Kerr coordinates
\hat{x}^{a}=(u,r,\theta,\hat{\phi})
, and the vectors’ components are well-defined at the horizons, where
\Delta(r)=0
. Moreover, when
\Delta(r)=0
, the ingoing null vector
\ell^{a}_{-}
is the generator of the horizon surfaces, indicating that
r_{\rm h}
corresponds to the white hole horizon.

Because the null tetrad is adapted to the coordinate system, one can identify the Teukolsky master function in outgoing Kerr coordinates
{}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\hat{\Psi}(\hat{x}^{a})
directly with the master function in Boyer-Lindquist coordinates via

{}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\hat{\Psi}(\hat{x}^{a})={}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\Psi(x^{a}),

(57)

where the transformation
x^{a}(\hat{x}^{a})
between Boyer-Lindquist and outgoing Kerr coordinates is understood.

An equivalent procedure applied to eq. (
31
) leads to the following form of the Teukolsky equation

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\hat{\Psi}_{,uu}(\hat{x}^{a})+2a\,\left({}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\hat{\Psi}_{,u\hat{\phi}}(\hat{x}^{a})-{}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\hat{\Psi}_{,r\hat{\phi}}(\hat{x}^{a})\right)-2(r^{2}+a^{2})\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\hat{\Psi}_{,ur}(\hat{x}^{a})+

(58)

\displaystyle-2\bigg({\rm i}a\mathfrak{s}\cos\theta+r(1+2\mathfrak{s})\bigg){}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\hat{\Psi}_{,u}(\hat{x}^{a})+\Delta^{-\mathfrak{s}}(r)\,\partial_{r}\bigg(\Delta^{\mathfrak{s}+1}(r)\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\hat{\Psi}_{,r}(\hat{x}^{a})\bigg)+

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}\bigg(\sin\theta\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\Psi_{,\theta}(\hat{x}^{a})\bigg)+\dfrac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\hat{\Psi}_{,\hat{\phi}\hat{\phi}}(\hat{x}^{a})+2{\rm i}\mathfrak{s}\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin^{2}\theta}\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\hat{\Psi}_{,\hat{\phi}}(\hat{x}^{a})-\mathfrak{s}(\mathfrak{s}\cot^{2}\theta-1)\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}\!\hat{\Psi}(\hat{x}^{a}).

(59)

which yields the radial equation

\displaystyle\Delta^{-\mathfrak{s}}(r)\,\dfrac{d}{dr}\bigg(\Delta^{\mathfrak{s}+1}(r)\,\dfrac{d}{dr}\bigg)\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}{\hat{\cal R}}_{\ell m}(\omega;r)+2{\rm i}\bigg(\omega(r^{2}+a^{2})\dfrac{d}{dr}-am\bigg)\dfrac{d}{dr}\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}{\hat{\cal R}}_{\ell m}(\omega;r)

(60)

\displaystyle-\bigg(A_{\ell m}+a^{2}\omega^{2}-2a\omega m-2{\rm i}r\omega(1+2\mathfrak{s})\bigg)\,{}_{\mathfrak{s}}{\hat{\cal R}}_{\ell m}(\omega;r)=0.

(61)

\displaystyle\hat{C}_{0}=\dfrac{\Omega_{\rm c}}{2a\varkappa_{\rm c}}\bigg(-A_{\ell m}+2ma\omega-a^{2}\omega^{2}+2{\rm i}\,a\omega\kappa(1+2\mathfrak{s})\bigg),

(62)

\displaystyle\hat{C}_{1}=\dfrac{\Omega_{\rm h}}{2a\varkappa_{\rm h}}\bigg(-A_{\ell m}+2ma\omega-a^{2}\omega^{2}+2{\rm i}\,r_{\rm h}\omega(1+2\mathfrak{s})\bigg).

in Table

to input in eq. (

A very similar argument follows if we consider the ingoing Kerr coordinates
\check{x}^{a}=(v,r,\theta,\check{\phi})
, defined by

t=v-r^{*}(r),\quad\phi=\check{\phi}-\chi(r).

(67)

In this case, however, the Kinnersly tetrad is not best suited to define the scalars
\Psi_{0}
and
\Psi_{4}
. In coordinates
\check{x}^{a}
, the outgoing and ingoing Kinnersley null vectors read

\ell^{\check{\mu}}_{+}=\dfrac{2(r^{2}+a^{2})}{\Delta(r)}\delta^{\check{\mu}}_{v}+\delta^{\check{\mu}}_{r}+\dfrac{2a}{\Delta(r)}\delta^{\check{\mu}}_{\check{\phi}},\quad\ell^{\check{\mu}}_{-}=-\dfrac{\Delta(r)}{2\Sigma(r,\theta)}\delta^{\check{\mu}}_{r},

(68)

and hence, ill-defined at the horizons, when
\Delta(r)=0
. Therefore, a new set of null tetrad is needed, boosted as

\check{\ell}^{a}_{+}=\Delta(r)\,\ell^{\check{a}}_{+},\quad\check{\ell}^{a}_{-}=\Delta^{-1}(r)\ell^{\check{a}}_{-}.

(69)

Apart from being regular at the horizons, one can infer that the surface
r_{\rm h}
now corresponds to the black hole horizon because the outgoing null vector
\check{\ell}^{a}_{+}
is the generator of the horizon.

The boost transformation (

\check{\Psi}_{0}(\check{x}^{\mu})=\Delta^{2}(r)\Psi_{0}(x^{\mu})
and
\check{\Psi}_{4}(\check{x}^{\mu})=\Delta^{-2}(r)\Psi_{4}(x^{\mu})

4.2
Heun slices

Outgoing and Ingoing null vectors as horizon generators

The boost transformation (
75

\zeta(r)
as in eq. (

) and values according to Table

, ensures that the outgoing
\tilde{\ell}^{a}_{+}
and ingoing null tetrad

are regular at

and

. When evaluated at a given horizon surface

or

\tilde{\ell}^{a}_{+}
or
\tilde{\ell}^{a}_{-}
will be generating the horizon hypersurface. In practical terms, a null vector, say
\tilde{\ell}^{a}_{-}

\tilde{\ell}^{\tilde{t}}_{-}\neq 0
,
\tilde{\ell}^{r}_{-}=0
and
\tilde{\ell}^{\tilde{t}}_{+}=0

\tilde{\ell}^{r}_{+}\neq 0

\tilde{\ell}^{r}_{-}\neq 0

\tilde{\ell}^{t}_{-}=0
and

,
\tilde{\ell}^{r}_{+}=0
if
\tilde{\ell}^{a}_{+}
is the horizon generator.

For

, the event horizon surfaces to the past (white hole
{\cal H}_{\rm p}
) and to the future (black hole
{\cal H}_{\rm f}
) are generated, respectively, by the ingoing
\tilde{\ell}^{a}_{-}
or outgoing null vector
\tilde{\ell}^{a}_{+}
. Since we are restricting ourselves to one asymptotic flat region in the conformal diagram, all configurations for the Heun slices uniquely identify the white hole horizon with
\mu_{h}^{-}
and the black hole horizon with
\mu_{h}^{+}
, i.e.,
{\cal H}_{\rm p}^{-}
and
{\cal H}_{\rm f}^{+}

\pm
in the parameter
\mu_{h}^{\pm}
identifies the particular null vector
\tilde{\ell}^{a}_{\pm}
generating the event horizon, and as a consequence, the past/future character of the surfaces
{\cal H}
. The same reasoning applies to the Cauchy horizons
{\cal C}
at
r=r_{\rm c}
, where the sign
\pm
in the parameter
\mu_{c}^{\pm}
also identifies the particular null vector
\tilde{\ell}^{a}_{\pm}
generating them. However, they do not determine the past and future character of the surfaces
{\cal C}
.

We can infer a key property of the Heun functions from the fact that all Heun slices are horizon-penetrating. The characteristic exponents associated with the local solutions around the regular singular points always include one value of
\varrho_{i}=0
— cf. (
16
) for the theoretical prediction and Table
2
for the Teukolsky equation. From a spacetime perspective, the finiteness of the Heun functions at the singular points directly results from the underlying time-constant hypersurface being horizon-penetrating. The behaviour arising from the other characteristic exponent,
\varrho_{i}\neq 0
, links the second linearly independent local solution to geometrical information associated with the respective time-symmetric horizon surface, either in the future or the past.

The vector normal to the hypersurface
\tilde{t}=
constant has the norm
||\nabla_{a}\tilde{t}||^{2}=g^{\tilde{t}\tilde{t}}
. Its sign indicates whether the surface is null

or spacelike
(g^{\tilde{t}\tilde{t}}<0)

\tilde{t}=

To study the asymptotic structure of the slice

6
Conclusions

In this work, we reviewed the use of Heun functions in describing solutions to the radial Teukolsky equation. Typically, Heun functions are studied through mappings applied solely at the radial coordinate level, using the so-called s-homotopic transformations. By emphasising the geometrical role played by coordinate transformations at the spacetime level, a key outcome of this study is the introduction of the so-called Heun slices.

Specifically, by defining a new coordinate system
(\tilde{t},r,\theta,\tilde{\phi})
with appropriate transformations of the time and angular coordinates, as well as a particular choice of null tetrad, we demonstrated that the representation of the radial Teukolsky equation as a confluent Heun equation in the canonical form (
1
) naturally follows from a frequency decomposition with the coordinate
\tilde{t}
as a reference. This approach enables us to interpret certain properties of the Heun functions as consequences of the geometrical structure of the hypersurfaces
\tilde{t}=\text{constant}
, without relying on traditional s-homotopic methods for the radial functions. In particular, we paid special attention to the solutions’ properties near the horizons and at asymptotic distances, connecting the local analytical structure of the Heun functions to the geometry of hypersurfaces
\tilde{t}=\text{constant}
.

More specifically, there are a total of eight distinct transformations mapping the radial Teukolsky equation in the traditional Boyer–Lindquist coordinates into the canonical confluent Heun equation. When expressed in terms of spacetime hypersurfaces, these transformations correspond to four distinct pairs of time-reversed configurations, which we labelled as:
(--+)/(++-)
;
(---)/(+++)
;
(-++)/(+--)
; and
(-+-)/(+-+)

+
and
-
signs corresponds to a particular choice of exponents
(\mu_{c},\mu_{h},\nu)
responsible for homotopically transforming the radial Teukolsky equation into the canonical confluent Heun form, cf. eq. (
50

1
. Alternatively, one can interpret these configurations as representing different spacetime foliations and null tetrads, cf. eqs. (
74
), (
75

79

81

3

(--+)
and ingoing
(++-)

We observed that all configurations give rise to horizon-penetrating slices, with the signs associated with the exponents
(\mu_{c},\mu_{h})
directly related to the null vectors generating the horizons. These are the principal null vectors
\ell^{a}_{+}
and
\ell^{a}_{-}
, corresponding to outgoing and ingoing directions, respectively. Thus, the relatively simple behaviour of Heun functions around the regular singular points, expressed by the vanishing characteristic exponents in eq. (
16

\tilde{t}=\text{constant}

Our analysis also revealed that the asymptotic behaviour of radial perturbations is sensitive to the chosen coordinate slicing. Different slicing configurations produce distinct asymptotic behaviours. For instance, in the cases of outgoing
(--+)

In addition to the coordinate transformations associated with Heun solutions, we also explored the use of hyperboloidal coordinates in the study of the Teukolsky equation. Strictly speaking, the radial Teukolsky equation resulting from a hyperboloidal slice does not assume a Heun form. However, when expressed in terms of a compact radial coordinate

Despite the non-Heun form of the hyperboloidal radial equation, the spacetime framework retains a structure that can still be analysed using methods akin to those applied in the Heun function framework. In other words, the choice of a height function in the spacetime coordinate transformation automatically determines the mapping at the level of the radial equation. In the hyperboloidal framework, this mapping remains homotopic, as it continuously maps functions into functions. However, it is more general than s-homotopic transformations, which restrict the mapping to a subspace of the functional space. In particular, one can infer from the Heun function the resulting characteristic exponents (
