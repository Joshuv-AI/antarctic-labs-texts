Identification of Clinical Subtypes of Surgical Sepsis in Critically Ill Patients Based on K-Means Clustering and Development of Parsimonious Classifier Model for High-Risk Subtype
Authors: Xiayan Qian, Huan Ma, Yanping Zhu, Shuhe Li, Saiya Ma, Ruoxu Dou, Zhaoxia Tang, Xiangdong Guan, Ka Yin Lui, Changjie Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdentifying the phenotypes of patients with surgical sepsis is crucial for improving their management. This study aims to identify the clinical subtypes of critically ill surgical sepsis patients and develop parsimonious classifier model for rapid subtype identification.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on 4,027 adult surgical sepsis patients admitted to the Surgical Intensive Care Unit at the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University from 2018 to 2023, with external validation using the MIMIC-IV (the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care) database. We extracted the worst values of clinical variables within 24 hours before and after diagnosis and performed K-means clustering. The elbow method and the silhouette coefficient was used to determine the optimal number of clusters. Survival analysis was used to evaluate the prognostic differences among different subtypes. A multivariate logistic regression model was constructed to identify the subtypes, and the model was evaluated by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), decision curve analysis (DCA), calibration curve and SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations).ResultsThree subtypes were finally determined. Subtype-1 (13%) exhibited severe organ dysfunction and the highest 1-year mortality (73.3%), subtype-2 (59%) had mild organ dysfunction and the lowest 1-year mortality (26.4%), while subtype-3 (28%) was characterized by older age and prevalent comorbidities. External validation confirmed similar subtype distributions and clinical profiles in the MIMIC-IV cohort. A four-variable logistic regression model (including prothrombin time (PT), dosage of norepinephrine, lactate and pH) was developed to predict the high-risk subtype-1. The model demonstrated satisfactory discriminative ability in the derivation cohort (AUC: 0.970) and robust performance in external validation (AUC: 0.889). SHAP analysis highlighted PT, lactate, and dosage of norepinephrine as core predictors of subtype-1.ConclusionThis study identified three clinical subtypes of critically ill patients with surgical sepsis based on early clinical variables. A multivariate logistic regression model was developed to identify the high-risk subtype-1, with core impact factors including PT, dosage of norepinephrine and lactate.Keywords: surgical sepsis, subtypes, K-means clustering, parsimonious classifier model

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome of organ dysfunction caused by the dysregulation of the host’s response to infection.1 Its incidence and mortality remain high.2,3 Sepsis exhibits significant heterogeneity, and different patients may have different response trajectories, pathophysiological mechanisms, and clinical outcomes.4,5 This heterogeneity is believed to make guideline-driven treatment difficult to be effective for all patients.6,7 Therefore, identifying and understanding the subtypes of sepsis is crucial for improving patient management.

Recently, researchers have utilized machine learning in combination with clinical or molecular data to identify various subtypes of sepsis. For instance, the Sepsis Endotyping in Emergency Care (SENECA) study, based on multi-dimensional clinical data, identified four clinical subtypes (α, β, γ, and δ).8 Bhavanide et al developed and validated subtypes based on the evolution trajectory of vital signs.9 These subtypes exhibit differences in clinical characteristics. However, when applied to different cohorts, the distribution frequencies and clinical characteristics of these identified subtypes may not be completely consistent.7 Additionally, patients with surgical sepsis constitute a unique subset of the sepsis population and show significant differences in disease manifestations compared to non-surgical sepsis.10,11 They often accompany systemic stress responses caused by trauma or surgery, leading to postoperative immunosuppression.12,13 When an infection occurs on this immunological disturbance, the body’s response pattern is completely different from that of non-surgical patients. Surgical trauma, intraoperative blood loss and fluid administration, as well as hemodynamic and endocrine changes caused by anesthesia, all may have an impact on the subsequent development of diseases. Studies have shown that the clinical trajectory of surgical sepsis exhibits significant heterogeneity. Some patients can recover quickly, but a considerable proportion will progress to chronic critical illness.11 Therefore, identifying the unique clinical phenotypic characteristics of critically ill patients with surgical sepsis is of great clinical significance. However, current research on their clinical phenotypic features is still insufficient.

Furthermore, some existing subtype classification strategies rely on complex algorithms and a large amount of high-dimensional data,14 such as involving multi-omics analysis,15 blood gene expression profiles,16 or the evolution trajectories of time-series data.9,17–20 This has created obstacles for clinicians to quickly and accurately identify subtypes at the bedside, severely limiting the effective application and promotion of subtype stratification in daily medical practice and clinical trials. To convert the research results on sepsis subtypes into clinical practice and guide the design of future clinical trials, it is urgently necessary to develop simplified and robust classification models. Especially for surgical sepsis, there is still no efficient and feasible model to identify high-risk subtypes to guide treatment.

Therefore, this study aims to employ the K-means clustering, based on early multi-dimensional clinical variables, to explore the clinical subtypes of critically ill patients with surgical sepsis, to reveal their clinical characteristics and outcomes, and to conduct external validation in the large-scale critical illness database MIMIC-IV cohort to ensure the reproducibility and universality of the identified subtypes. At the same time, parsimonious classifier model is developed to determine the rapid and accurate subtype identification methods, providing an important basis for precise clinical treatment.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This study was a retrospective observational study conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (FAH-SYSU) (Guangzhou, China), aiming to identify the clinical subtypes of critically ill patients with surgical sepsis based on early clinical variables and develop a parsimonious classifier model. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The extraction of clinical data has been approved by the Clinical Research and Animal Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University on 23 May 2025 (Approval Number: [2025]384). Subsequently, external validation was carried out using data from the publicly available “Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV” (MIMIC-IV) database (https://mimic.mit.edu), which covers the relevant medical information of patients admitted to the intensive care unit of Boston Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in the United States. One of the authors of this study obtained the permission to access and extract data from this database. The shared nature of this database has been reviewed by the Institutional Review Board of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. Since the analyzed data are anonymous, it has been approved to waive informed consent.

This study included patients with surgical sepsis who were admitted in the Surgical Intensive Care Unit (SICU) of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University from January 2018 to May 2023. The diagnosis of sepsis was based on “Sepsis-3”,21 which stipulates that there is suspected or confirmed infection and an acute increase of more than 2 points in SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment). Exclusion criteria included non-first admission to the ICU, and loss to follow-up. These criteria were applied to both cohorts simultaneously.

Data Extraction, Preprocessing and Clinical Outcomes

This study used PostgreSQL (version 16.10) software to extract information from the established database of critically ill patients in our center and the MIMIC-IV database by running Structured Query Language (SQL). We extracted the following variables: Demographics, age, sex, underlying medical conditions [such as hypertension, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), etc], surgery, infection site, severity scores [SOFA score and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)], vital signs and laboratory test results (including body temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, pulse oxygen saturation, 24-hour urine volume, cumulative dosage of norepinephrine within 24 hours (dosage of NE, mg), blood gas analysis, blood cell count, liver and kidney function, coagulation function indicators totaling 48 variables, as well as survival information. The primary outcome was 1-year mortality, the secondary outcomes were 30-day mortality and length of stay in ICU (ICU-LOS). For the cumulative dosage of NE within 24 hours, it is defined as the cumulative amount of NE administered during the first 24 hours after sepsis onset. Due to the inability to obtain complete weight information for some patients, the NE exposure was not adjusted according to body weight. All patients were uniformly calculated as a common unit of mass (mg). Patients who did not record the administration of NE within the first 24 hours were recorded as a cumulative norepinephrine dose of 0 mg. For longitudinal data with multiple records, the most critical corresponding value within 24 hours before and after the diagnosis of sepsis were collected. Variables with a missing value ratio more than 20% were excluded, and those with ≤20% missing values were processed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE).

Clustering and Subtype Identification

For the extracted variables, we evaluated the strength of correlation between variables using Cramer’s V test, point-biserial correlation, Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis based on the variable type. We calculated VIF (variance inflation factor) to conduct multicollinearity analysis for continuous variables. We excluded highly correlated variables and finally included 36 features for clustering analysis. Through feature standardization processing, all variables were normalized with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. K-means clustering was used for clustering. The elbow method was used to determine the optimal number of clusters (K value), and the silhouette coefficient was used for verification to ensure the reliability of the clustering results. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used to reduce the dimensionality of the dataset and achieve clustering visualization. Finally, a two-dimensional PCA clustering graph was drawn to visually present the distribution characteristics of each cluster. Subsequent analysis compared the clinical characteristics and prognosis among each cluster. Additionally, to ensure the universality of the derived clustering, external validation was conducted using the MIMIC-IV database. To reduce the influence of baseline differences among different populations on the validity of the clustering model. We adopted the feature space alignment, standardized the same feature variables of the MIMIC-IV, mapped them to the feature space of the FAH-SYSU, and based on the clustering centroid of the FAH-SYSU, assigned subtypes according to the minimum Euclidean distance to complete the cross-population clustering label migration.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical data are presented in the form of frequencies and percentages. Continuous data with a normal distribution are presented in the form of mean (standard deviation, SD), while continuous data that do not conform to a normal distribution are presented in the form of median [interquartile range]. We evaluated the distribution of different subtypes in each cohort as well as clinical characteristics and outcomes. Categorical data were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous data with a normal distribution were analyzed using the one-way ANOVA test, while continuous data that do not follow a normal distribution were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Kaplan-Meier curves were plotted and Log rank tests were performed to compare the survival differences among different subtypes.

In order to determine a rapid and accurate subtype identification method, we chose a multivariate logistic regression model to develop a parsimonious classifier. Variables were selected and the model was constructed using the FAH-SYSU cohort. The contribution of each variable to the principal components was calculated based on the loading matrix in principal component analysis. The top 6 variables with the greatest contribution were extracted and used to build the multivariate logistic regression step by step forward. Then, the models were compared and selected based on the AIC (Akaike Information Criterion) and BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion). The model performance was evaluated by AUROC (the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) and accuracy, and a nomogram was drawn to visually display the model. The model performance was then verified in MIMIC-IV. Finally, SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) values were used to explain and evaluate the importance and impact of variables in the machine learning model. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.1.2) or R studio. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Population

After screening according to inclusion and exclusion criteria (Figure 1), a total of 4,027 surgical sepsis patients from the FAH-SYSU dataset were included in this study. The validation cohort consisted of 11,453 patients in the MIMIC-IV dataset. The baseline characteristics of the two cohorts are presented in Supplementary Table 1. In the FAH-SYSU cohort, 69.5% were male, with an average age at admission of 57.88 ± 15.92 and a SOFA score of 7.93 ± 3.54. Among them, 1,579 patients (39.2%) died within one year of being diagnosed with sepsis. In the MIMIC-IV cohort, 54.3% were male, with an average age at admission of 65.38 ± 17.57 and a SOFA score of 3.42 ± 1.86. 4,265 patients (37.2%) died within one year. Overall, the FAH-SYSU cohort had a more severe condition compared to the MIMIC-IV cohort.

Different Subtypes of Surgical Sepsis

Supplementary Figure 1 and Supplementary Figure 2 showed the strength of the correlation between paired variables and the results of the multicollinearity analysis for continuous variables. We excluded the highly correlated variables and finally included 36 variables for cluster analysis. To determine the optimal number of clusters K, we conducted the elbow method, silhouette coefficient analysis, and gap statistics. The elbow method found that the decrease in SSE gradually became more gradual after K > 3 (Supplementary Figure 3A). The silhouette plot indicated that the average silhouette coefficient was the highest at K = 2 (Supplementary Figure 3B). However, this grouping simply divided the patients into two subgroups of low risk and high risk, masking a large amount of important clinical heterogeneity in the cohort. The gap statistic increased across the evaluated range (Supplementary Figure 4 and Supplementary Table 2). Although neither the silhouette coefficient nor the gap statistic identified K=3 as a unique statistical optimum, the K=3 solution retained acceptable cluster separation and yielded additional clinically meaningful heterogeneity. Therefore, considering both statistical adequacy and clinical interpretability, K=3 was selected as the primary exploratory solution.

Figure 2A presented the distribution of the individuals at the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) after dimensionality reduction through PCA, with three distinct clusters marked. A total of 524 patients (13%) were assigned to subtype-1, 2362 (59%) to subtype-2, and 1141 (28%) to subtype-3 (Figure 2B). Table 1 described the clinical characteristics and prognosis of the three subtypes and Supplementary Table 3 reported the effect sizes of all variables to complement the statistical significance p-values. Patients classified as subtype-1 presented the most severe organ dysfunction, including the highest SOFA score (11.89 ± 3.20), the highest dosage of norepinephrine within 24 hours (40.00 [10.58, 84.33] mg), the highest total bilirubin (3.69 [1.70, 10.97] mg/dL), and the poorest coagulation parameter (PT 26.50 [20.60, 35.70] s). Patients in subtype-2 presented the mildest organ dysfunction (SOFA score 7.11 ± 3.12). Patients in subtype 3 were the oldest and had the highest prevalence of underlying conditions, such as hypertension, diabetes, CKD. Supplementary Figure 5A and Figure 2C showed the 30-day and 1-year survival curves of the three subtypes. Subtype-1 had the highest hospital mortality (52.1%, n = 273), 30-day mortality (62.8%, n = 329) and 1-year mortality (73.3%, n = 384), while subtype-2 had the best survival outcomes (1-year mortality: 26.4%, n = 623). The results of the Cox proportional hazards model indicated that after adjusting for confounding factors, subtype-1 remained an independent risk factor for 1-year mortality in surgical sepsis patients (HR 2.56, 95% CI (2.2–2.97), P < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure 6).

External Validation in the MIMIC-IV Dataset

For the MIMIC-IV cohort, the optimal number of clusters K was also found to be 3 based on the elbow method and the silhouette plot (Supplementary Figure 7). To validate the derived clusters, we assigned each patient in the MIMIC-IV cohort to a clinical subtype based on the minimum Euclidean distance from the three cluster centroids of the FAH-SYSU cohort. Figure 2D–F, Supplementary Figure 5B and Supplementary Table 4 respectively showed the subtype distribution, prognosis, and clinical characteristics of the MIMIC-IV cohort. The validation cohort clusters demonstrated consistency with the original cohort: similar subtype distributions and clinical features. Subtype-1 (13%, n = 1544) exhibited the most severe organ dysfunction (SOFA score was 5.04 ± 2.72) and the highest 1-year mortality (54.5%); subtype-2 (51%, n = 5809) had the mildest organ dysfunction (SOFA score was 2.93 ± 1.35) and the lowest 1-year mortality (27.6%); while subtype-3 (36%) was characterized by older age and a higher prevalence of comorbidities.

Parsimonious Classifier Model

Based on the current clustering results, patients in subtype-1 are younger but exhibit the most severe organ dysfunction and the highest mortality. Therefore, accurately identifying subtype-1 for providing timely clinical intervention is of the most significant clinical benefit. To simplify the recognition of subtype-1, we extracted the top 6 variables with the greatest contribution (“lactate”, “PT”, “APTT”, “pH”, “dosage of NE within 24h”, “bicarbonate”) from the load matrix of principal component analysis (Supplementary Figure 8) and constructed a multivariate logistic regression model forward stepwise. The performance of the nested model was evaluated in Supplementary Figure 9 and Supplementary Table 5. It can be seen that the AIC and BIC continuously decreased as the number of variables increased, but the AUC and accuracy gradually stabilized. Considering the goodness of fit and complexity of the model, the 4-variable model (“lactate”, “PT”, “dosage of NE”, “pH”) was finally selected as the optimal model. This 4-variable model was transformed into a visual scoring tool, the nomogram (Figure 3A), where each predictive variable is assigned weighted points based on its regression coefficient, and individualized prediction values are generated for each patient to assist in clinical classification decisions. The parameters and equations of the model are shown in Supplementary Table 6. Clinicians can score patients based on the 4 indicators when diagnosing sepsis to identify patients with subtype-1. Patients with high lactate, severe coagulation disorders, large doses of vasoactive drugs, and significant acidosis exhibit elevated total scores, indicating a markedly increased probability of belonging to subtype-1.

The ROC curve demonstrated that the established model exhibited favorable performance in identifying subtype-1 in the derivation cohort (AUC: 0.967[0.953–0.980], Figure 3B; accuracy: 0.943 [0.925–0.958], Supplementary Table 7). External validation demonstrated that it also had comparable robust discriminatory performance in the MIMIC-IV cohort (AUC: 0.889[0.883–0.895], Figure 3C; accuracy: 0.824[0.817–0.831], Supplementary Table 7), significantly superior to the SOFA score (AUC: 0.725 [0.711–0.740]). The DCA (Decision Curve Analysis) showed that across nearly all high-risk threshold ranges (0.1–0.98), the model yields significantly higher net benefit compared to traditional “SOFA score”, “All intervention”, and “none intervention” strategies (Figure 3D and Supplementary Table 8). The calibration curve showed acceptable agreement between predicted and observed probabilities (Figure 3F). The DCA and calibration curve of the external validation cohort showed the same result (Figure 3E and G), highlighting its potential value in practical clinical applications.

Explanation of the Parsimonious Classifier Model Variables

We conducted SHAP analysis to quantify the contribution of each feature to the identification of the subtype-1 in the parsimonious Classifier model and to perform a global ranking, achieving interpretability of individual-level predictions. Figure 4 showed the global distribution of SHAP feature value for each individual, visually presenting the direction (positive/negative) and intensity (SHAP value magnitude) of the feature’s impact on the model. PT prolongation, high norepinephrine dose, and high lactate all show positive contributions, among which high PT has the most significant contribution to the model output (Figure 4A). The SHAP dependence plots (Figures 4B–G) further revealed the interactions among the four variables. As PT increases, the SHAP value rises significantly, and high norepinephrine doses and high lactate level (red dots) are concentrated in the high SHAP value region (Figure 4B and C), indicating that the large-dose vasoactive drug requirement and elevated lactate substantially amplify the predictive contribution of PT prolongation. Similarly, synergistic interactions exist between high vasoactive drug dosage and low pH with elevated lactate (Figure 4D–F). And pH shows a negative contribution to the model (low pH corresponds to a positive SHAP value), meaning that low pH increases the probability of subtype-1, but its independent distinguishing ability is weak, and its effect is mostly dependent on synergistic interactions with other features. Overall, coagulation dysfunction (PT), tissue hypoperfusion (lactate), and vasoactive drug requirement (dosage of norepinephrine) are the core impact factors for subtype-1, exhibiting significant synergistic effects.

Discussion

This study focused on critically ill patients with surgical sepsis, identifying three clinical subtypes based on early clinical variables. Significant differences were observed in clinical characteristics and outcomes across these subtypes. External validation using the MIMIC-IV cohort demonstrated cross-cohort robustness of this classification. These findings support the view of high heterogeneity in sepsis.22 And we developed a multivariate logistic regression model to identify high-risk subtypes. External validation proved its good and stable discriminatory ability (AUC: 0.889). SHAP analysis revealed that PT, dosage of norepinephrine, and lactate are the main variables influencing the classification, further supporting the potential practicality of this tool in the identification of clinical subtypes.

In the three subtypes identified by the FAH-SYSU cohort, subtype-1 exhibited the most severe organ dysfunction (coagulation, circulation, liver function) and the highest 1-year mortality. This is similar to the most severe δ-type (characterized by abnormal liver function, septic shock, and the highest mortality) identified in the SENECA study.8 Subtype-2 were the youngest with the mildest organ dysfunction, resembling α-type; subtype 3 were characterized by advanced age and multiple underlying diseases, similar to the β-type. This similarity suggested that different populations with sepsis may share several stable clinical phenotypic dimensions rather than being completely distinct disease subtypes. However, the SENECA classification was derived from the clinical data of emergency department (ED) sepsis patients, while this study focused on the distinct clinical subgroup of critically ill surgical sepsis patients, optimizing the existing sepsis phenotype concept to make it specifically applicable to surgical sepsis. Due to inherent differences in the study population, the specific subtype distribution and some clinical characteristics still differ from those of the SENECA classification. For instance, this study did not identify independent subtypes similar to the γ-type of SENECA, which is characterized mainly by respiratory dysfunction. Additionally, some features of the subtypes in the MIMIC-IV cohort differ from those derived by the FAH-SYSU cohort. For example, in terms of inflammatory response, WBC count was the highest in subtypes-1 and 3 in the FAH-SYSU cohort, while the MIMIC-IV cohort exhibited the highest WBC count in subtype-1, followed by subtype-3. Indeed, SENECA classification exhibits certain subtype variations across other sepsis cohorts.7,22 These variations in subtype distribution and characteristics may be related to regional differences in medical practices or baseline population characteristics. This highlights the importance of external validation of sepsis subtypes in different geographical and clinical settings. In this study, the clinical characteristics and prognostic trends of different surgical sepsis subtypes in the external validation MIMIC-IV cohort were consistent with those of the derivation cohort as a whole, further supporting the cross-population applicability of this classification in surgical sepsis patients.

This study selected a multivariate logistic regression model to identify the subtype-1 with the poorest prognosis. Although complex machine learning models may offer superior performance, their complexity limits their application in resource-constrained settings, especially for rapid triage and bedside decision-making. Using easily available parameters, such as biomarker ratios, to stratify the risks of critically ill patients holds significant clinical application value.23 Previous studies have successfully developed parsimonious classifier model using only a few routine variables to identify ARDS subtypes and SENECA δ-type patients in real-time in clinical settings.7,24 Therefore, this study developed a multivariate logistic regression model for subtype identification by integrating several easily accessible important clinical variables. In the nested model with forward stepwise regression, the performance of the model improved with the gradual addition of variables. However, in the external validation cohort, the 4-variable model showed the best discrimination and accuracy (AUC: 0.889), with high sensitivity and specificity. It also avoided the risk of model overfitting. Thus, considering both performance and simplicity, the 4-variable model constructed with lactate, PT, dosage of norepinephrine, and pH was optimal. Furthermore, the performance and net benefits within the wide high-risk threshold of this model are significantly higher than those of SOFA score. This indicates the potential of the model as a precise classification tool for distinguishing subtype-1 and optimizing clinical decision-making. Moreover, compared to unconventional plasma protein markers and time series variables, these parameters are easily accessible in clinical settings, and clinicians can use this model for real-time risk warning and early intervention for subtype-1 patients.

SHAP analysis emphasizes the significance of coagulation dysfunction, vasoactive drug requirements, and tissue hypoperfusion in the identification of subtype-1, which may reflect the unique pathophysiological process of surgical sepsis. During sepsis, the massive release of inflammatory mediators causes endothelial cell damage, activates the coagulation pathway, promotes thrombin production and fibrin deposition, forming thrombotic inflammation, resulting in local tissue hypoperfusion and microcirculatory failure.25,26 In surgical sepsis, in addition to inflammatory stress, surgical procedures or trauma exacerbate vascular endothelial injury, disrupting endothelial glycocalyx integrity. This facilitates activation of the extrinsic coagulation pathway, promoting microthrombus formation.27,28 Subsequent depletion of coagulation substrates results in hypocoagulopathy characterized by bleeding tendencies. Moreover, factors such as intraoperative bleeding and infusion of resuscitative fluids can alter the levels and function of coagulation factors, further exacerbating coagulation dysfunction.29 These changes may have shortened the duration of the early hypercoagulable phase of sepsis, making the hypercoagulable period more concealed and enabling a quicker transition to the hypocoagulable phase, or even presenting as a hypocoagulable state from the very beginning. On the other hand, endothelial cell injury increases the production of vasodilatory factors, reduces vascular tone, and decreases the sensitivity of vascular smooth muscle to vasoconstrictive drugs, leading to hemodynamic instability.30 Additionally, surgical trauma may lead to neuroendocrine system disorders, prolonged increase in catecholamine secretion resulting in desensitization, further affecting vascular tension and reactivity.30 Moreover, excessive catecholamine use may even exacerbate endothelial glycocalyx damage,31 which highlights the synergistic effect between endothelial damage, thrombotic inflammation, and circulatory instability, as revealed by the interactions among variables in the SHAP dependence plot. Patients in subtype-1 are more prominent in coagulation dysfunction and macro- and microcirculatory failure, which may reflect the modifying effect of surgical trauma on the sepsis phenotype. Therefore, based on the clinical characteristics of subtype-1 identified in this clustering analysis, we propose a conceptual framework for future prospective study. Patients with this subtype simultaneously present with coagulation disorders and circulatory failure, suggesting that these aspects may require more intensive monitoring in clinical practice, and even individualized intervention. For example, using thrombelastography (TEG) for dynamic coagulation monitoring to identify the transition process from an early hypercoagulable state to a subsequent hypocoagulable and bleeding-prone state, thereby providing a basis for more personalized treatment (anticoagulation or supplementation of coagulation substrates).32 Additionally, patients with subtype-1 exhibit a higher demand for vasoactive drugs and lactic acidosis, suggesting that the early combined use of non-ephedrine-type vasopressor drugs, such as vasopressin, may reduce the exposure of norepinephrine and alleviate the secondary damage to the endothelial glycocalyx induced by high-dose ephedrine.31 However, these assumptions are based on phenotypic associations and it will be necessary to conduct prospective studies in the future to evaluate whether subtype-specific treatment strategies can bring benefits to this high-risk population.

This study still has some limitations. Firstly, during data preprocessing, variables with a missing value ratio greater than 20% were excluded. For variables with a missing value ratio of ≤ 20%, MICE was used for filling, which may introduce certain bias. To address this, we compared the imputed dataset with a control dataset generated using random forest imputation, observing the similarity of the post-imputation data distributions to confirm the robustness of the imputation results (Supplementary Table 9). Secondly, due to the lack of sufficient weight data, the cumulative exposure of NE within 24 hours was not corrected based on body weight. Although a fixed observation window and a unified common unit of measurement were adopted to enhance the consistency among the data sets, the absolute cumulative dose could still be affected by the patient’s body size, the vascular vasopressin treatment strategy of the medical institution, and the duration of treatment. Lastly, this retrospective analysis relied on data extracted from electronic health records systems. Consequently, it could not draw conclusions about treatment efficacy for high-risk subtypes using the parsimonious classifier. In the future, randomized controlled trials are needed to verify whether subtype-directed intervention strategies can improve prognosis.

Conclusion

This study identified three clinical subtypes of critically ill patients with surgical sepsis based on early clinical variables using K-means clustering. Significant differences were observed in clinical characteristics and outcomes across subtypes, and the classification demonstrated robust cross-cohort validity through validation in the MIMIC-IV cohort. A multivariate logistic regression model was developed to identify the high-risk subtype-1, with core impact factors including PT, dose of norepinephrine and lactate.
