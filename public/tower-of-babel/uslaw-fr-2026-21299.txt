Alaska; Wildlife, Harvest, and Access

AGENCY: National Park Service, Interior.

ACTION: Final rule.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

SUMMARY: The National Park Service (NPS) amends its regulations for 
hunting, fishing, trapping, and other harvest-related activities in 
Alaska and the procedures used to restrict public use and access in 
Alaska park areas, including for subsistence. This rule restores the 
regulatory framework adopted to implement the Alaska National Interest 
Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) in 1981 that guided management 
for more than three decades, with targeted updates to that framework in 
response to public comments and further agency review. The restored 
framework recognizes the complementary and distinct roles of the State 
of Alaska, the Federal Subsistence Management Program, and the NPS in 
managing public uses and resources in Alaska park areas. The rule 
removes regulatory changes made in 2015, 2017, and 2024 that departed 
from that framework. These amendments are consistent with Executive 
Orders 14153 and 14192 and Secretary of the Interior's Orders 3422 and 
3447.

DATES: This rule is effective November 18, 2026.

SUPPLEMENTARY INFORMATION: This final rule updates and restores 
regulations for Alaska park areas to the framework in place from 1981 
through amendments beginning in 2015. A table summarizing this 
restoration is included in the ``Final Rule'' section below. Detailed 
explanations are included in that section and in the ``Background'' and 
``Summary and Responses to Public Comments'' sections below.
    This rule does not alter the federal subsistence priority 
established in ANILCA. Federal Subsistence Management Program 
regulations and the rural preference for qualified users on federal 
public lands in ANILCA Title VIII are unchanged. What this rule means 
for subsistence users:
     Permit requirements for collecting animal parts for 
handicrafts--removed. The 2017 changes added a mandatory permit for 
collecting antlers, bones, and similar materials to create handicrafts 
for barter and customary trade. This rule removes the permit 
requirement; collection for these purposes remains allowed.
     Restrictions for harvesting bears over bait for 
subsistence uses--removed. Bait and other restrictions added in 2017, 
which limited subsistence users to using natural or legally harvested 
remains, are removed; regulations issued by state and federal boards 
govern these uses unless the statutory

[[Page 65986]]

criteria and procedures in ANILCA are met for a closure.
     Public hearings before closures affecting fish and 
wildlife harvest--restored. The requirement to hold a hearing in the 
affected vicinity before closures affecting the harvest of fish and 
wildlife was removed in 2015. This rule restores that local hearing 
requirement.
     Notice requirements for local rural residents--restored 
and updated. The requirement to post signs and/or provide notice in 
local newspapers, radio broadcasts, posted maps, and post office 
notifications was eliminated in 2015. The final rule restores these 
locally focused notice requirements, which remain essential tools for 
reaching remote communities with limited connectivity, and updates the 
regulatory text to ensure posted signs are required in addition to (not 
in the place of) published notice.
    This rule does not expand or restrict NPS closure authorities 
beyond what Congress has provided for Alaska. It does not affect state 
management authority over Alaska's fish and wildlife resources or 
affect the authority of the Secretaries of the Interior and Agriculture 
under Title VIII of ANILCA, including the authority that the 
Secretaries delegated to the Federal Subsistence Board. The rule does 
not reopen areas closed to harvest by statute.

Background

    In 1980, ANILCA established 10 national preserves, two national 
monuments, and eight new or expanded national parks across Alaska. 
Since enactment, the NPS has managed these units of the National Park 
System (System) under a regulatory framework that recognizes multiple, 
complementary state and federal authorities. The State of Alaska 
manages fish and wildlife populations on all lands under state 
regulations and requirements in the Alaska Constitution; the 
Secretaries of the Interior and Agriculture (through the Federal 
Subsistence Management Program) currently administer the subsistence 
taking and uses of fish and wildlife on federal public lands, as set 
forth in ANILCA Title VIII; and the NPS manages park areas in Alaska 
according to ANILCA provisions that expressly allow subsistence and 
sport hunting, fishing, and trapping in national preserves and 
subsistence harvest in certain specified parks and monuments according 
to applicable state and federal law. As part of its management of these 
areas, the NPS may close areas or restrict activities according to 
Alaska-specific criteria and procedures, including consultation and 
public participation requirements. See ANILCA Titles II, VIII, and 
XIII; see also Alaska Dep't of Fish & Game v. Fed. Subsistence Bd., 139 
F.4th 773, 779, 783-84 (9th Cir. 2025).
    These provisions reflect Congress's comprehensive and intentionally 
negotiated balance among competing uses, authorities, interests, and 
values. In enacting ANILCA, Congress affirmed the State of Alaska's 
existing authorities to manage Alaska's fish and wildlife resources and 
the NPS's authority to manage for specific unit purposes, with 
constraints on the discretion each manager has to close or restrict 
uses authorized in ANILCA.
    Implementing regulations adopted in 1981 (46 FR 31836), 1983 (48 FR 
30252), and 1986 (51 FR 31619) reflected this structure and governed 
Alaska park areas for more than three decades, recognizing state 
management authorities, the Secretary of the Interior's (Secretary) 
responsibilities in ANILCA Title VIII, including in parks and monuments 
that are closed to hunting except for subsistence, and NPS's authority 
to close areas and restrict activities in accordance with Alaska-
specific criteria and procedures, one of numerous unique management 
directives for NPS in ANILCA. This final rule restores the regulatory 
framework that governed Alaska park areas from ANILCA's passage until 
the discretionary shift in 2015 to advance administration policy goals. 
The rule reinstates regulations that reflect ANILCA's balanced 
compromise and were designed to implement this unique, Alaska-specific 
approach.

Legal Authority

    The proposed rule outlined the legal authorities supporting this 
action. See, e.g., 91 FR 11483, 11484 (Mar. 10, 2026). In short, ANILCA 
established a clear, Alaska-specific framework in which national 
preserves are managed as units of the System while expressly allowing 
the harvest of fish and wildlife resources under applicable state and 
federal law. Congress affirmed the State of Alaska's responsibility for 
managing fish and wildlife resources except as provided in Title VIII, 
limited the NPS's involvement in regulations implementing Title VIII, 
and set out the statutory criteria and procedures for closures and 
restrictions in Alaska park areas, particularly for subsistence uses 
and other authorized harvest activities.
    This framework guided the regulations adopted in the 1980s that 
successfully carried out ANILCA's unique requirements for more than 
three decades, until policy-driven interpretations in 2015 and 2017 
departed from its balanced mandates for Alaska. This framework 
continues to guide NPS in restoring that proven regulatory approach and 
is the baseline for evaluating each of the amendments.

Regulatory Development

    The proposed rule explores the history that led to this rule in 
significant detail. See, e.g., 91 FR 11484-87. In short, ANILCA 
expanded or established park areas in Alaska within an overarching 
framework for managing federal lands and interests that differs from 
more general and nationwide frameworks in important respects. This 
means System units in Alaska have a unique management structure for 
preserving nationally significant natural, cultural, scenic, and 
wildlife resources as well as continuation of the Alaska way of life, 
including harvesting renewable natural resources for food, clothing, 
shelter, familial and cultural ties; access and transportation for 
traditional activities and connecting communities; long-term use, 
occupancy, and storage; and other uses Congress determined should 
continue in Alaska subject to the provisions in ANILCA.
    Since ANILCA, the Department of the Interior (DOI) and NPS have 
implemented these provisions in regulations at 36 CFR part 13, 43 CFR 
part 36, and 43 CFR part 51 (formerly at 50 CFR part 100) as follows.
    1981-2014 (the established framework): NPS regulations adopted 
shortly after ANILCA's passage included Alaska-specific provisions for 
hunting, fishing, trapping, subsistence, access, and public 
participation. Regulations recognized complementary state and federal 
authorities and provided transparent criteria and procedures for 
closures affecting authorized uses.
    2015: Starting with a proposed rule in 2014, NPS regulations for 
Alaska were amended to depart from the established framework. The 2015 
amendments introduced narrower interpretations of ANILCA's provisions 
to support broader application of System-wide prohibitions; 
recharacterized statutory closure criteria as broader ``factors''; 
reduced public engagement requirements; altered notice and consultation 
procedures; and removed cross-references to applicable state and DOI 
authorities. These changes marked a shift toward greater consistency 
with management practices outside Alaska and broader discretion for 
unit managers, rather than the Alaska-specific approach required by 
ANILCA.
    Public comments on the 2015 changes included significant opposition 
from Alaska Native Tribes, villages,

[[Page 65987]]

corporations, and organizations. The Alaska Federation of Natives 
adopted Resolution 14-42 finding the rulemaking ``overreaching, vague, 
and indiscrimina[te]'' noting it ``would further criminalize Alaska 
Native traditional resource management practices.'' Tanana Chiefs 
Conference also opposed the rule for its potential impacts on 
subsistence, member Tribes, and traditional homelands. Many commenters 
observed there was no consultation or notice before publication and 
procedural amendments received sparse attention.
    2017: The NPS made additional revisions to its Alaska-specific 
regulations to address subsistence uses of renewable natural resources. 
Among other things, these changes added a permit requirement for 
collecting nonedible animal parts for handicrafts; restricted methods 
and types of bait used in harvesting bears for subsistence; and 
prohibited collection of live raptor chicks under a falconry permit 
(preempting state regulation at 5 AAC 92.037).
    2020: In a 2020 rulemaking, NPS removed most of the 2015 
prohibitions on state-authorized activities including harvesting bears 
over bait, which was allowed from 2020 through 2024 after being 
prohibited in 2015; it has been limited for federally qualified 
subsistence users since 2017. In a lawsuit challenging the 2020 
rulemaking, the U.S. District Court noted the 2020 rulemaking 
principally concerned the relationship between the federal and state 
management systems and the scope of NPS's regulatory authority, while 
the administrative record reflected limited evidence of population-
level impacts under state management and the specific harvest practices 
at issue. Alaska Wildlife Alliance v. Haaland, 632 F. Supp. 3d 974, 
996-1002 (D. Alaska 2022). The court affirmed the NPS's conclusion that 
the practices reauthorized in 2020, including bear baiting, would not 
adversely impact populations or interfere with NPS's statutory 
obligations. Id. at 1002. The 2020 changes did not address the 
procedural, interpretive, and administrative changes made in 2015 and 
2017.
    2024: NPS reinstated the prohibition on bear baiting in preserves, 
attempted to further define ``trapping'' in ways that are unnecessarily 
duplicative of the State's trapping regulations, and added an 
unnecessary severability provision.
    Now: Executive Order 14153 Sec. 3(b)(xix) directed rescission of 
the 2024 changes and restoration of the regulations adopted in 2020. 
This rule implements that direction and goes further to address the 
changes in 2015 and 2017 that the 2020 rulemaking did not consider.
    This rule restores the established Alaska-specific framework, 
regulatory language, decision criteria, public procedures, and 
underlying interpretations that implemented Congress's direction for 
Alaska park areas from their establishment until the changes in 2015 
and 2017. The NPS believes a return to this proven framework more 
closely reflects Congress's direction by preserving subsistence uses, 
local participation, and government coordination, and by providing 
greater consistency, clarity, and predictability in park 
administration.

Administrative Direction

    As recognized in the proposed rule, 91 FR 11486, this rule's 
comprehensive and updated restoration of the pre-2015 regulations is 
also consistent with current Executive and Departmental policy 
direction concerning Alaska, regulatory reform, public access, 
subsistence uses, hunting and fishing, and coordination with state and 
local partners in managing public lands.
    Executive Order 14153 directs federal agencies to review and revise 
actions affecting Alaska and specifically directs DOI bureaus to 
consider the cultural significance of hunting and fishing in Alaska, 
the priority for subsistence in ANILCA, meaningful consultation with 
state resource agencies, and consistency between hunting and fishing 
opportunities on federal and state lands. Secretary's Order 3422 guides 
implementation through directed actions affecting Alaska and the 
administration of federal lands and resources. Executive Order 14192 
and Secretary's Order 3447 direct agencies to remove unnecessary 
regulatory burdens, improve regulatory clarity, and strengthen 
coordination with state, tribal, territorial, and local partners. The 
directives in Secretary's Order 3447 are specific to hunting and 
fishing on DOI-managed public lands. Restoring the established Alaska-
specific framework directly advances all of these directives.

Requests for Action

    The proposed rule details the history of requests for action 
related to the changes in the final rule. See 91 FR 11487. In short, 
since the 2015 and 2017 changes, stakeholders have consistently raised 
concerns about those amendments affecting the closure process, public 
participation, consultation requirements, subsistence uses, permitting 
requirements, and the relationship between Alaska-specific regulations 
and System-wide regulations under ANILCA. Questions regarding the 
consistency of these amendments with ANILCA and its unique roles for 
federal and state managers have remained at the forefront of public 
comments, letters, petitions, and litigation since 2015.
    The final rule responds to these concerns through a comprehensive 
review of the procedural, interpretive, and administrative amendments 
adopted in 2015 and 2017 that were not reconsidered in the 2020 or 2024 
rulemakings. In addition to implementing the direction in Executive 
Order 14153 to rescind the 2024 rule and restore the 2020 rule, NPS 
considered, among other things, the May 19, 2025, letter from Safari 
Club International and the October 10, 2025, Petition for Rulemaking 
submitted by the Alaska Outdoor Council, which both requested a broader 
review of the changes that began in 2015 and restoration of the 
established Alaska-specific provisions that endured for decades before 
those changes.
    Consistent with NPS's review of ANILCA's requirements for Alaska, 
regulatory implementation from 1981 to 2015, the operation of NPS 
regulations under subsequent amendments, public comments on the 
proposed rule, and the potential environmental impacts from adopting 
it, the final rule restores the established regulatory provisions that 
implemented Congress's unique direction for Alaska park areas for more 
than three decades, addressing issues previous rulemakings left 
unresolved, with updates and clarifications in response to public 
comments and NPS's further review.

Finalized Changes

    By rescinding these new and modified regulations, the NPS restores 
the Alaska-specific procedures for restricting activities and closing 
park areas to public use, entry, and access to the language and 
procedures in 36 CFR part 13 in effect before the changes made in 2015, 
which further restores alignments with the Alaska-specific procedures 
for access in 43 CFR part 36. This rule removes the changes made 
through the 2015, 2017, and 2024 rulemakings that ultimately reduced 
public engagement, omitted required consultation with the State, and 
allowed for discretionary closures without the unique criteria and 
procedures previously established in the DOI and NPS regulations for 
Alaska park areas to comply with ANILCA. This rule maintains the NPS's 
authority to limit use, entry, and access through those criteria and 
procedures.

    Consistent with FCC v. Fox Television Stations, Inc., 556 U.S. 502, 
515-16 (2009), NPS acknowledges its awareness this rule represents a 
change in position from that taken in the 2015, 2017, and 2024 rules. 
For the reasons discussed here and in detail in the proposed rule, 91 
FR 11489, the NPS concludes this rule: (1) is permissible under ANILCA 
and the NPS Organic Act, which allow for the state-regulated harvest 
activities and procedural protections this rule restores; (2) is better 
than the 2015, 2017, and 2024 rules because it returns to the fixed 
durational limits, consultation requirements, locally targeted notice 
and hearing requirements, and other longstanding, Alaska-specific 
criteria and procedures rather than relying on System-wide standards 
and electronic notifications; and (3) is supported by good reasons, 
including public comments from subsistence users and Alaska Native 
organizations supporting removal of the 2017 permit requirements. The 
NPS has finalized these conclusions after reviewing public comments and 
preparing the final rule.
    With specified exceptions for unrelated changes, the NPS restores 
its long-standing Alaska-specific regulations at 36 CFR part 13 by 
rescinding:
     changes made in 2015 that were not changed in 2020, 
including,
    [cir] changes to the Alaska-specific closure process, including 
reduced public engagement, discretionary timelines, and subjective new 
``factors'' beyond the specified criteria for closure in ANILCA,
    [cir] narrowed interpretations of Congress's balanced mandates for 
Alaska to support broader application of System-wide prohibitions and 
policies,
    [cir] removal of references to applicable state and federal 
authorities, and
    [cir] revisions to Sec.  13.490 that facilitated the 2017

of unlimited duration;
     most changes made in 2017, unless it provides relevant

    [cir] a more appropriately detailed definition of ``customary

    [cir] a definition for ``handicraft'' as a term used in the 
definition of ``customary trade'' with distinguishing features from the 
referenced definition in DOI regulations; and
     changes made in 2024, in their entirety.
    Based on a review of the full record, including comments on the 
proposed rule, the NPS has determined that reverting to its pre-2015 
regulations is a substantial improvement over the changes that began in 
2015 that fundamentally altered the unique management framework 
Alaskans relied on following ANILCA. For the reasons stated above and 
in the proposed rule, 91 FR 11483, the NPS rescinds the regulatory 
changes made in 2015 that were not rescinded in 2020; changes made in 
2017, with the exception of the definitions for ``customary trade'' and 
``handicraft'', which are retained; and the changes made in 2024. The 
rule also makes targeted updates to reflect subsequent developments, 
like the relocation of the Federal Subsistence Management Program to 
the Office of the Secretary and its regulations from 50 CFR part 100 to 
43 CFR part 51, and incorporates clarifying revisions in response to 
public comments.
I. Hunting and Trapping--Sec.  13.40 (Restored) and Sec.  13.42 
(Removed)
    The rule restores pre-2015 language governing the harvest of fish 
and wildlife in national preserves, including the requirement that non-
emergency closures to non-subsistence uses become effective only after 
the superintendent consults with the appropriate state agency and 
representatives of affected users. Section 13.42, added in 2015 and 
amended in 2017 and 2024, is deleted in its entirety.
II. Harvesting Bears Over Bait--Restrictions Removed for All Users
    NPS acknowledges this aspect of the rule represents a change in 
position from the 2015, 2017, and 2024 rules, which prohibited or 
limited using bait to harvest bears. Consistent with FCC v. Fox 
Television Stations, Inc., 556 at 515-16, NPS provides the following 
analysis.
    (a) Authority. Allowing the harvest of bears over bait under state 
regulations in effect since 1982 is permissible under ANILCA and the 
NPS Organic Act. ANILCA expressly allows hunting in national preserves 
under applicable state and federal law. The district court confirmed 
NPS's conclusion that the 2020 allowance would not adversely impact 
wildlife populations or contravene NPS's statutory duties. Alaska 
Wildlife Alliance, 632 F. Supp. 3d at 993. No new data has emerged 
since that decision indicating this regulated harvest activity will 
have population-level effects. The NPS maintains management authority, 
including closure authority under the Alaska-specific criteria and 
procedures restored in this rule, consistent with ANILCA to address any 
issues that might arise.
    (b) Improvements. The 2015 and 2024 prohibitions were based 
primarily on public safety concerns and NPS policy favoring natural 
wildlife behaviors and ecosystem dynamics. On review, neither basis 
supports categorical prohibition. Regarding public safety, NPS 
estimates no more than 10 bears per year (2 black bears, up to 8 brown 
bears) will be harvested over bait across all national preserves, 
consistent with historical levels from 1982 through 2015 and from 2020 
through 2024. State regulations require permits; minimum setbacks from 
roads, trails, dwellings, and public facilities; registration of bait 
stations with the State; and completion of a bear baiting clinic. 5 AAC 
92.044. The Alaska Board of Game has stated it has no information on 
bear baiting causing public safety concerns and found no fact-based 
support for that concern in any state that allows the practice. The one 
study cited in the 2024 rule (Herrero 2018) is, on further review, 
relevant only to human-bear conflicts at campgrounds and other 
congregation points, not remote bait stations. NPS is not aware of 
safety incidents from bait stations established in unauthorized 
locations within Wrangell-St. Elias National Preserve. NPS Management 
Policies Sec.  8.2.5 recognizes that not all hazards in areas managed 
as natural environments can be eliminated; a categorical prohibition is 
not required to manage residual risk. Regarding natural conditions, the 
district court's 2022 decision confirmed that bear baiting does not 
contravene NPS's statutory duties. The Alaska Board of Game has stated 
that bear baiting tends to focus harvest on mature bears while avoiding 
female and immature bears, making it compatible with sustainable 
population management.
    (c) Reliance interests. The 2015 prohibition was in effect from 
2015 to 2020, lifted from 2020 to 2024, and reimposed from 2024 to the 
present. This demonstrates that reliance on any particular rule was 
limited after 2015. Conservation interests supporting the 2015 and 2024 
prohibitions do not have a sufficient reliance interest to override the 
analysis above. The NPS will continue to monitor harvest and visitor 
safety and retains full authority to, among other things, close 
specific areas if a documented concern arises meeting the criteria in 
Sec.  13.50 and Sec.  13.490.
    (d) Factual findings. To the extent the 2015 amendments expressed 
concern that state regulations do not adequately

[[Page 65989]]

address public safety risks from bear baiting, state requirements for 
permits, setbacks, and educational clinics partially mitigate those 
risks. To the extent the 2024 rule relied on the Herrero study and an 
informal survey of bear biologists, the NPS does not rely on those 
findings because: (1) the study is inapplicable to remote bait 
stations; (2) the biologist survey addressed theoretical risks without 
reference to actual documented incidents in Alaska; and (3) the State 
of Alaska's decades of operational experience without documented public 
safety incidents are more probative than speculation without context or 
data.
III. Definition of Trapping--Sec.  13.1 (Restored)
    The definition of ``trapping'' in Sec.  13.1 was amended in 2024 to 
define the circumstances where individuals may use firearms to take 
similarly redefined ``furbearers'' under a state trapping license. 
State regulations at 5 AAC 92.075(d) and 92.095(a)(8) already address 
these circumstances and no basis exists for NPS to impose a duplicative 
and unnecessary regulation. The rule restores the former definition for 
``trapping'' and removes the definition of ``furbearer'' as unnecessary 
under the restoration. This change eliminates a regulatory burden that 
lacks a corresponding management benefit, and is consistent with 
direction in Executive Order 14192 to remove unnecessary regulations 
and in Executive Order 14153 to maximize consistency in harvest 
opportunities across public lands.
IV. Live Wildlife Collection/Falconry--Sec.  13.42(j) (Removed)
    The 2017 rule added Sec.  13.42(j) which prohibited the regulated 
collection of live raptor chicks in national preserves on the basis 
that such activities are not ``hunting or trapping'' and are therefore 
subject to System-wide prohibition at 36 CFR 2.2(a)(2). This conclusion 
is not consistent with Congress's direction for national preserves in 
Alaska. The State of Alaska manages falconry under 5 AAC 92.037, which 
requires permits and applies on all public lands in Alaska. The U.S. 
Fish and Wildlife Service manages migratory raptors under 50 CFR 21.82 
and 21.85, which authorize chick collection under state permits. 
Removing Sec.  13.42(j) restores the pre-2017 framework and recognizes 
the adequate management of falconry activities by the State and U.S. 
Fish and Wildlife Service. The NPS requested input on any documented 
management need for this prohibition, and public comments did not 
identify impacts or concerns to justify retaining it.
V. Subsistence Collection--Sec.  13.482 (Removed) and Sec.  13.485 
(Revised)
    As noted before and elsewhere, this rule does not alter the federal 
subsistence priority established in ANILCA, Federal Subsistence 
Management Program regulations, or ANILCA's rural preference for 
qualified users on federal public lands. The 2017 rule added Sec.  
13.482, which created a mandatory permit system for collecting 
nonedible animal parts for use in handicrafts for barter and customary 
trade. This permit requirement was opposed by nearly every commenter 
representing subsistence users and Alaska Native organizations in the 
2017 rulemaking, who noted the activity was already authorized under 
ANILCA, the permit added burdens for subsistence users who already must 
hold federally qualified status, the most common collection method is 
opportunistic gathering and is incompatible with a permit system 
requiring advance authorization, and subsistence users had been engaged 
for nearly a decade in good faith in the regulatory process only to 
receive additional restrictions rather than the clarification they 
sought.
    Removing Sec.  13.482 does not prohibit the uses it addressed; 
collection of animal parts for handicrafts remains authorized under 
ANILCA Title VIII and applicable federal regulations. The permit 
requirement and any conditions attached to it are removed. The removal 
of this permit requirement is a deregulatory action that will benefit 
subsistence users by removing unnecessary administrative burdens. The 
definition of ``animal parts'' in Sec.  13.420 is likewise removed as 
unnecessary, while the definitions of ``customary trade'' and 
``handicraft'' are retained with minor technical edits to align with 
federal regulations at 43 CFR part 51, with NPS-specific modifications 
including products made from plant materials and excluding trophies or 
European mounts of horns or antlers. Section 13.485(b) is revised to 
restore pre-2017 language authorizing noncommercial gathering of plant 
materials and dead or downed timber for firewood without a permit.
VI. Closure and Restriction Procedures--Sec.  13.50 (Restored)
    The 2015 rule significantly revised closure procedures in ways that 
reduced public engagement, eliminated durational requirements, removed 
specific notice methods essential to local rural residents, and 
recharacterized statutory ``criteria'' as discretionary ``factors.'' 
This rule restores the pre-2015 procedures, as follows.
    (a) Emergency closures: 30-day maximum; may not be extended. 
Emergency closures affecting harvest of fish and wildlife resources 
require notice and hearing before taking effect. Other emergency 
closures take effect on notice.
    (b) Temporary closures: affecting fish or wildlife harvest requires 
notice and hearing in the affected vicinity and other appropriate 
locations before taking effect. Other temporary closures take effect on 
notice. Maximum duration of 12 months may not be extended.
    (c) Permanent closures: published as rulemaking in the Federal 
Register with a minimum 60-day public comment period and public 
hearings in the affected area and other appropriate locations.
    (d) Notice requirements: all closures and restrictions shall be 
published in at least one newspaper of general circulation in the state 
and at least one local newspaper where available; posted at community 
post offices in the affected vicinity; made available for broadcast on 
local radio stations in a manner reasonably calculated to inform 
residents in the affected vicinity; and designated on a map available 
for public inspection at the superintendent's office and other 
convenient locations. The proposed rule language has been revised 
slightly (replacing an ``or'' with ``and') to confirm that all of these 
notice methods must be utilized in addition to the posting of 
appropriate signs.
    (e) Openings: Federal Register notice required with hearings on 
request in the affected vicinity and other appropriate locations before 
a final determination.
    (f) Facility closures and restrictions: restored as a distinct 
category with defined criteria (public health, safety, and protection 
of public property), duration (limited to the circumstance requiring 
closure), and notice requirements.
    These restorations directly respond to concerns from rural Alaska 
residents and communities that the changes in 2015 to rely on website 
postings and discretionary notice were inadequate to inform residents 
of changes affecting subsistence and harvest activities in remote 
areas. Fixed durations and defined criteria provide predictability, 
consultation requirements restore meaningful government engagement, and 
hearing requirements restore opportunities to engage decision-makers 
before closures take effect.

VII. Subsistence Provisions --Sec. Sec.  13.400, 13.470, 13.480, 13.490 
(Restored)
    As stated elsewhere, this rule does not alter the federal 
subsistence priority established in ANILCA, Federal Subsistence 
Management Program regulations, or ANILCA's rural preference for 
qualified users on federal public lands. Section 13.400 is revised to 
add clarifying language confirming that the regulations in subpart F 
governing subsistence uses apply to local rural residents and others 
authorized to conduct those activities, and recognizing that Alaska 
laws relating to the taking of fish and wildlife apply within park 
areas to the extent consistent with applicable federal law, including 
ANILCA and 43 CFR part 51.
    The rule restores references to applicable state law in Sec. Sec.  
13.470 (subsistence fishing) and 13.480 (subsistence hunting and 
trapping) to ensure subsistence users can conduct these activities in 
compliance with applicable state and federal law, as was the case under 
regulations in effect from 1981 through 2015 for park areas.
    Section 13.490 is revised to restore the requirement for adequate 
notice and public hearing in the affected vicinity and other 
appropriate locations before the superintendent temporarily closes a 
park area to subsistence uses of fish and wildlife. Consultation with 
the Federal Subsistence Board and provisions for temporary closures are 
retained, including the duration standard (``only so long as reasonably 
necessary to achieve the purposes of the closure'').
    Section 13.1902(d), which imposed bait restrictions on subsistence 
bear harvest at Wrangell-St. Elias National Park and Preserve, is 
removed.
VIII. Clarifications in Response to Comments--Sec. Sec.  13.35 and 
13.400 (Revised)
    Minor clarifying language is added to structural provisions at 
Sec. Sec.  13.35 and 13.400 to confirm that limitations generally 
applicable to park visitors do not generally apply to subsistence 
users. Alaska park areas operate under ANILCA's balanced mandates in 
addition to System-wide direction. These revisions respond to concerns 
about confusion over which rules apply to subsistence users as they 
cross jurisdictional boundaries.
IX. Severability--Sec.  13.42(k) (Removed)
    The 2024 Rule added a severability provision the NPS has determined 
is unnecessary and it is not retained in this rule. Notwithstanding the 
NPS having determined that a stand-alone severability provision is 
unnecessary to include in the 36 CFR part 13 regulations, the NPS 
intends the provisions of this final rule to be severable. If any 
provision of this rule, or the application of any provision to any 
person or circumstance, is held invalid, the NPS intends that the 
remainder of the rule and its application to other persons or 
circumstances remain in effect to the fullest extent permitted by law.

Summary and Responses to Public Comments.

    On March 10, 2026, the NPS published the proposed rule in the 
Federal Register (91 FR 11483). The proposed rule was open for an 
initial 30-day public comment period. The NPS extended the comment 
period on April 10, 2026, (91 FR 18340) in response to several requests 
for more time to review the proposal. In total, the comment period was 
open for 46 days. The comment period closed on April 24, 2026. The NPS 
invited comments through the mail, hand delivery, and through the 
Federal eRulemaking Portal at https://www.regulations.gov.
    The NPS received 5,172 comments on the proposed rule, including 
submissions from organizations representing more than 48,000 collective 
signatories. A summary of the pertinent issues raised in the comments 
received and NPS responses are provided below.

Statutory Consistency and Priorities

    1. Comment: Several commenters stated the proposed rule and its 
approach to management are inconsistent with NPS's obligations under 
the law, including the NPS Organic Act, Wilderness Act, and ANILCA, 
arguing that National Park System units must be managed to prioritize 
conservation and natural processes over consumptive uses.
    NPS Response: Through ANILCA, Congress mandated that consumptive 
uses such as subsistence and sport hunting, fishing, and trapping be 
allowed in Alaska's national preserves under applicable state and 
federal law and that certain parks and monuments be available for 
subsistence uses. ANILCA Sec. Sec.  203, 1313, and 1314. The 
continuation of these activities is one of the intrinsic values 
Congress saw in establishing preserves in Alaska as distinct from parks 
and monuments, where hunting would only continue for federally 
qualified users in specified areas. In its report on the language that 
became ANILCA through a negotiated compromise with the U.S. House of 
Representatives, the U.S. Senate Committee for Energy and Natural 
Resources explained this intentional approach:

    Like the House-passed bill, the Committee amendment directs that 
a preserve be managed as a national park except that all forms of 
hunting be permitted to continue. This includes sport, subsistence, 
and guided hunting . . . . The Committee also adopted an amendment 
clarifying that trapping shall be allowed in national preserves and 
that the statutory requirement that the taking of fish and wildlife 
be allowed does not deprive the Secretary of his traditional 
authority to close public lands located within conservation system 
units to the taking of fish and wildlife under statutory criteria. 
In allowing trapping to continue within preserves, it was not the 
intent of the Committee to allow exploitative forms of commercial 
trapping to occur. The intent of the committee is to allow 
individual Alaskans to continue to operate their own trap lines 
within the preserves, even though those individuals might not 
qualify as subsistence users . . . . The Secretary, through the 
National Park Service, is expected to monitor trapping operations 
within the preserves and the associated wildlife populations and 
issue appropriate regulations to insure that exploitative forms of 
trapping do not take place and that there is no substantial or 
permanent harm to the wildlife populations. S. Rep. 96-413, 96th 
Cong. 1st Sess. 1979, at 307-308.

    Some concerns with accommodating these and other consumptive use 
mandates rest on the premise that managing for natural processes under 
the NPS Organic Act and Wilderness Act is an exclusive, independent 
standard from other applicable laws. That premise does not hold for the 
areas established and expanded under ANILCA, which referenced both laws 
in specifying which uses are allowed, grounds for restricting them, and 
procedures to enact those restrictions. Regulatory expectations 
discussed by the committee, such as allowing activities necessary to 
conduct consumptive uses and limits only where monitoring shows 
exploitation or substantial harm, are the ways Congress defined 
resource protection for these areas given the unique Alaska context. In 
implementing those expectations, the final rule is not deprioritizing a 
natural processes standard in favor of consumptive uses, it is applying 
the standards Congress wrote for Alaska park areas.
    2. Comment: In Title VIII of ANILCA, Congress established a federal 
priority for customary and traditional uses of fish and wildlife 
resources by rural residents on federal public lands and

[[Page 65992]]

certain waters in Alaska. Commenters raised concerns that proposed 
changes would diminish the federal subsistence priority by allowing 
state-regulated harvest activities in national preserves, potentially 
increasing competition for subsistence resources with insufficient 
attention to subsistence users and their expertise, perspective, and 
needs.
    NPS Response: The federal subsistence priority for rural Alaska 
residents cannot be diminished or overridden by state law or NPS 
regulation. ANILCA Title VIII and Federal Subsistence Board regulations 
are unchanged and addressed in this rulemaking through the restored and 
updated language. The final rule acknowledges subsistence opportunities 
and preferences are available in certain Alaska park areas under both 
federal and state regulations, and that NPS's authority is contingent 
on the Alaska-specific criteria and procedures in ANILCA.
    While the State cannot implement ANILCA's rural preference under 
the state constitution, it remains an important manager of Alaska's 
fish and wildlife resources on federal lands, including harvest 
seasons, methods, means, limits, and other regulations. This authority 
was affirmed in the Alaska Statehood Act, again in section 1314 of 
ANILCA, and is recognized in implementing regulations for wildlife 
refuges at 50 CFR 36.11. On all lands, Alaska law requires that, if a 
portion of a stock or population can be harvested consistent with 
sustained yield principles, a reasonable opportunity will be provided 
with a preference for subsistence uses that applies to all Alaska 
residents.
    The Federal Subsistence Board, in cooperation with Subsistence 
Regional Advisory Councils, Subsistence Resource Commissions, the NPS, 
the public, local experts, Tribes, other federal land managers, and the 
State of Alaska carries out the Secretary's responsibilities under 
Title VIII of ANILCA, which include ensuring: the continuation of 
subsistence uses by rural residents on federal public lands; rural 
residents with personal knowledge of local conditions and requirements 
have a meaningful role in the management of fish and wildlife and of 
subsistence uses on the public lands in Alaska; and rural residents 
engaged in subsistence uses have reasonable access to subsistence 
resources on the public lands. The NPS has the authority to preempt 
state law where necessary to implement the NPS Organic Act, Wilderness 
Act, ANILCA, and other statutory direction. The NPS has not found it 
necessary to exercise that authority in issuing this rule.
    The Secretary's delegation of authority to the Federal Subsistence 
Board is to administer the subsistence taking and uses of fish and 
wildlife on public lands and the related promulgation and signature 
authority for regulations in subparts C and D of 43 CFR part 51 and 36 
CFR part 242. The Board is empowered, to the extent necessary, to 
implement Title VIII of ANILCA, to undertake various actions as set 
forth in 43 CFR 51.10(d)(4) and 36 CFR 242.10(d)(4), including the 
following:
     issue regulations for the management of subsistence taking 
and uses of fish and wildlife on public lands;
     allocate subsistence uses of fish and wildlife populations 
on public lands;
     ensure that the taking on public lands of fish and 
wildlife for nonwasteful subsistence uses shall be accorded priority 
over the taking on such lands of fish and wildlife for other purposes;
     restrict the taking of fish and wildlife on public lands 
for nonsubsistence uses or closing public lands to the take of fish and 
wildlife for nonsubsistence uses when necessary for the conservation of 
healthy populations of fish or wildlife, to continue subsistence uses 
of fish or wildlife, or for reasons of public safety or administration;
     restrict the taking of a particular fish or wildlife 
population on public lands for subsistence uses, closing public lands 
to the take of fish and wildlife for subsistence uses, or otherwise 
modifying the requirements for take from a particular fish or wildlife 
population on public lands for subsistence uses when necessary to 
ensure the continued viability of a fish or wildlife population, or for 
reasons of public safety or administration;
     establish priorities for the subsistence taking of fish 
and wildlife on public lands among rural Alaska residents;
     restrict or eliminate taking of fish and wildlife on 
public lands; and
     determine what types and forms of trade of fish and 
wildlife taken for subsistence uses constitute allowable customary 
trade.
    Of note, however, the Federal Subsistence Board's regulations do 
not authorize subsistence uses in Glacier Bay National Park, Kenai 
Fjords National Park, Katmai National Park, and that portion of Denali 
National Park established as Mt. McKinley National Park prior to 
passage of ANILCA, where subsistence taking and uses are prohibited. 43 
CFR 51.3(a); 36 CFR 242.3(a). Also, the subsistence regulations in 43 
CFR part 51 do not supersede agency-specific regulations. Id. State and 
federal regulations can be in effect in the same area unless the 
Federal Subsistence Board or the NPS have closed or restricted use of 
the area by non-federally qualified users or to all harvest or entry.
    Changes made to NPS regulations in 2015 to support the preemption 
of state law in national preserves removed language recognizing this 
unique partnership of authorities and procedures. The restored and 
updated language in the final rule clarifies that federally qualified 
users can continue subsistence uses of fish and wildlife regulated by 
state and federal boards consistent with NPS regulations, including 
Alaska-specific procedures for area closures and user restrictions. 
Under the final rule, Alaskans and others can continue making board 
proposals and guide the management of customary and traditional uses 
without encountering the discretionary permissions and automatic 
preemptions added for certain park areas in 2015 and 2017. Public use 
will continue to be managed in Alaska park areas in accordance with 
federal law and, under the final rule, more specifically in accordance 
with the criteria and procedures in ANILCA.
    To ensure federally qualified subsistence users are unaffected or 
benefited by these changes, the final rule adds clarifying revisions to 
36 CFR 13.35 and 13.400. These revisions confirm general limitations 
for park visitors do not apply to federally qualified users engaged in 
subsistence, including those defined as ``local rural residents'' in 
Sec.  13.420 and other authorized users (e.g., those with a federal 
harvest permit from another bureau or agency). The rule restores the 
regulatory framework adopted immediately after ANILCA's passage, when, 
as now, the State did not have a program to implement the mandates in 
Title VIII, see 46 FR 31836, 31839, 31860-62 (June 17, 1981). The text 
is updated as needed to reflect the development of a federal program to 
carry out those mandates.
    3. Comment: A number of commenters opposed deferring to the State 
of Alaska in managing fish and wildlife resources, noting authorized 
practices may conflict with federal mandates for the NPS. Conversely, 
comments were received asserting the State is the appropriate and legal 
administrator of fish and wildlife resources on all lands in Alaska, 
with exceptions that do not apply in this

[[Page 65993]]

rulemaking, and that direction to align federal and state regulations 
will have positive effects.
    NPS Response: The NPS does not ``defer'' to the State of Alaska in 
implementing federal mandates. As explained above, the State manages 
fish and wildlife on all lands in Alaska except as specifically 
modified by Congress, and the NPS manages System units consistent with 
the Alaska-specific criteria, procedures, and priorities outlined in 
ANILCA. In adopting the clarifying amendment that became ANILCA Sec.  
1314, the Senate Committee stated:

    This Section, adopted as a Committee Amendment, preserves the 
status quo with regard to the responsibility and authority of the 
State to manage fish and wildlife, and reconciles this authority 
with the Act, including the subsistence title. At the same time, the 
Section confirms the status quo with regard to the authority of the 
Secretary to manage the wildlife habitat on federal lands. The 
Section also clarifies that fishing shall be permitted in national 
parks and monuments and that subsistence uses shall be permitted 
where specifically provided by the Act. S. Rep. 96-413, at 308.

    The ``status quo'' division of state-federal authorities in Sec.  
1314 is primarily about resource management and not about implementing 
Title VIII. It was in effect when the final rule language was initially 
adopted in 1981, a few months after ANILCA's passage; in 1982, when the 
Secretary approved the State's program to administer the federal 
subsistence priority; in 1985, when the Secretary intervened during the 
program's compliance issues (outside of parks and monuments); in 1989, 
when a state court found the State's program violated the Alaska 
Constitution; and in 1990, when the Federal Subsistence Management 
Program was established to administer the priority. Despite these 
shifts, the state-federal authorities Congress established for Alaska 
did not change, including NPS's limited role and responsibilities under 
ANILCA. The final rule acknowledges establishment of the federal 
program, updates references to its newly relocated regulations in 43 
CFR part 51, and restores the ``status quo'' language that provided 
decades of stability for all park users.
    The final rule also improves consistency for hunting and fishing 
across DOI-administered lands in Alaska, as directed in Secretary's 
Order 3447 Sec.  4(b)(3), while preserving the distinctions for 
National Park System units outlined in ANILCA and other laws. In 
enacting a single, comprehensive law governing all the federal lands in 
one state, Congress was able to build consistencies into jurisdictional 
landscapes in Alaska, and, shortly after ANILCA's passage, similar 
regulatory language was adopted for managing public uses in Alaska's 
national preserves and wildlife refuges based on similar or the same 
provisions. See 46 FR 31818, 31819 (June 17, 1981); 46 FR 31836. With 
the Congressionally directed restoration of regulations for wildlife 
refuges in 2017, this final rule solidifies the long-standing 
alignments in Congress' design. Revisions in the final rule will 
simplify compliance and enforcement by making it easier for eligible 
users to cross jurisdictional boundaries while harvesting under state 
or federal regulations in Alaska's wildlife refuges and preserves and 
under federal regulations in other park areas (as noted above, Klondike 
Gold Rush and Sitka national historical parks, Katmai and Kenai Fjords 
national parks, and parts of Denali National Park are closed to harvest 
by law).

Conservation and Public Use

    4. Comment: Several commenters expressed concern the proposed rule 
would harm tourism, asserting that hunting and trapping can reduce 
opportunities for non-consumptive uses and damage Alaska's reputation 
as a destination for wildlife viewing and photography.
    NPS Response: Section 1313 of ANILCA recognizes that, for national 
preserves in Alaska, ``the Secretary may designate zones where and 
periods when no hunting, fishing, trapping, or entry may be permitted 
for reasons of public safety, administration, floral and faunal 
protection, or public use and enjoyment.'' Understanding that all of 
these reasons have implications for tourism, and using the best 
available information, including diverse and meaningful experiences 
shared by park users in their comments on the proposed rule, the NPS 
has not found evidence that regulated harvest impairs or is likely to 
impair opportunities for non-consumptive uses in Alaska's park areas.
    Most areas where harvest occurs are highly remote, with few if any 
overlapping uses. A number of factors affect successful wildlife 
viewing for all user groups in all areas where animals largely dictate 
the encounter. Populations in Alaska park areas have been targeted by 
hunters and trappers for millennia and demonstrate significant 
variations in tolerance levels for humans regardless of their activity. 
Environmental analyses prepared for the 2015, 2020, and 2024 Alaska-
specific rulemakings did not identify a correlation between harvest 
activities addressed in those rules and measurable impacts in wildlife 
viewing opportunities across Alaska park areas. To the extent harvest 
activities affect visitor experiences, those effects are more likely to 
be location-specific rather than indicative of a broader reduction in 
non-consumptive recreational opportunities across Alaska park areas. 
Limiting one user group without impacting other user experiences could 
contribute to the problem those limits are looking to resolve (e.g., 
reducing user conflicts and barriers for allowed uses). The final rule 
maintains ANILCA's balanced mandates for federal public lands in 
Alaska.
    5. Comment: Commenters expressed concerns that harvest methods 
authorized by the State of Alaska and available under state law will 
impact natural ecosystem dynamics, like predator-prey relationships and 
pack formations. A smaller number of commenters opposed all state-
regulated harvest opportunities as potentially incompatible with or a 
violation of conservation purposes and values.
    NPS Response: As explained in the response to Comment 3, Congress 
restricted the NPS's general management authority over hunting, 
fishing, or trapping in Alaska park areas; it affirmed the State's 
existing authority; and it provided that, when NPS uses its management 
authority to close areas to allowed uses, it must meet certain 
requirements, including ``consultation with the appropriate State 
agency having responsibility over hunting, fishing, and trapping 
activities.'' ANILCA Sec.  1313 (cross-referencing Sec.  816, where 
unit manager discretion to restrict regulated harvest by federally 
qualified subsistence users is further restricted).
    State and federal managers have defined roles and enforcement 
obligations that work together for the safe, responsible management of 
Alaska's public lands. Under the final rule, NPS continues to steward 
habitats, protect resources, and implement closures when statutory 
criteria and procedures are met, in consultation with the State, which 
continues to establish harvest methods and other regulations in 
managing populations for sustainability under the Alaska Constitution. 
When the State and NPS work together to meet these interconnected 
obligations, the Federal Subsistence Management Program is more 
effective in exercising its delegated authority to regulate subsistence 
uses under ANILCA Title VIII, including necessary closures. Directing 
comments, proposals, and information on harvest activities to state

[[Page 65994]]

officials and established processes, including the Alaska Boards of 
Fisheries and Game, the Alaska Department of Fish and Game, and the 
Alaska State Legislature, or to the Federal Subsistence Board, will 
ensure that input is considered in the venues where population 
management decisions and policies are made for Alaska.
    Some commenters view this intersectional management collaboration 
using each organization's respective authorities as a prohibited form 
of deference, as noted in Comment 3, or as an abdication of NPS's duty 
to allow for natural processes and ecosystem dynamics and to protect 
fish and wildlife resources for future generations, as noted in Comment 
1. Many argued NPS should take a stronger position in shaping harvest 
regulations that affect Alaska park areas, including through 
discretionary preemption based on administrative criteria and values, 
which the restored and updated language in the final rule would not 
support. The NPS has the authority to preempt state law where necessary 
to implement the NPS Organic Act, Wilderness Act, ANILCA, and other 
statutory direction. The NPS has not found it necessary to exercise 
that authority in issuing this rule.
    As explained in the detailed preamble for changes to NPS 
regulations in 2020, 85 FR 35181 (June 9, 2020), regulated harvest in 
Alaska cannot legally function as a predator control program, which can 
only be conducted under specific requirements in federal and state law 
and have not been authorized in Alaska park areas. The NPS believes the 
final rule language is more consistent with balanced statutory mandates 
for Alaska, for the reasons explained in this rulemaking and based on 
decades of proven workability and cooperation toward science-based, 
user-informed conservation objectives.
    6. Comment: Regarding the NPS's closure authorities, a large number 
of comments specifically opposed the use of bait in harvesting bears, 
sharing ethical concerns and potential risks to wildlife populations 
and public safety.
    NPS Response: Harvest methods are established under applicable 
state and federal regulations that are continuously refined through 
regional input and monitoring. These regulations, and the publicly 
accountable processes that create and rigorously maintain them, define 
ethical practices from a management standpoint. The NPS is committed to 
its ongoing participation in these defined regulatory processes, as a 
stakeholder and a resource manager with independent authorities, and to 
continuing to work collaboratively with the public, subsistence users, 
gateway communities, Alaska Native Tribes and corporations, other 
federal agencies, and the State in executing distinct and shared 
responsibilities in a transparent and consistent manner based on our 
respective authorities.
    Harvesting bears over bait has been regulated across Alaska since 
1982 with no evidence of notable public safety incidents or population-
level impacts. To the contrary, this harvest method can be effective at 
targeting animals with an established tolerance for human activities, 
which are more likely to be involved in an incident, and to close the 
distance and expand the time available for targeting a particular sex 
or age class, which is a key consideration in managing populations for 
sustainability. Analysis supporting the rule's determinations is set 
out in detail in Section II of the ``Finalized Changes'' section above. 
The NPS will continue to monitor and may close areas if a specific 
concern arises using the appropriate criteria and procedures in ANILCA. 
Comments submitted on the proposed rule and in previous rulemakings 
provide detailed data and analyses that support NPS's conclusion this 
state-authorized activity is not detrimental to wildlife conservation 
and public safety at current levels.

Procedural Issues

    7. Comment: Commenters questioned whether the rulemaking process 
meets the procedural requirements in the Administrative Procedure Act, 
National Environmental Policy Act, and section 810 of ANILCA, sharing 
concerns about sufficiency, rationale, and transparency.
    NPS Response: With this final rule and associated decision-making 
materials, including an environmental assessment and section 810 of 
ANILCA analysis, the NPS has fully complied with all applicable 
procedural requirements. The rule is supported by a detailed 
administrative record, including the above-referenced analyses, public 
notice and comment and consultations with state officials, tribal 
governments, and Alaska Native corporations. This is not to imply that 
input provided during the comment period and consultations is uniformly 
supportive of the final rule, from a substantive perspective, only 
that, from a procedural perspective, that input was fully considered in 
developing the final rule.
    8. Comment: Some commenters found the proposed rule unclear and 
potentially misleading without more information, with recurring 
concerns that certain provisions could lead to unintentional loopholes, 
inconsistent application in the field, and challenges in determining 
where rules apply. Commenters did not find the proposed rule made it 
clear what is changing for Alaska park areas.
    NPS Response: The NPS recognizes that confusion can occur in any 
rulemaking process and has endeavored in its outreach efforts to focus 
on providing accurate and detailed information in open dialogue, and 
comments expressing confusion have influenced NPS's approach to 
modifications and explanations in this final rule.
    One area of confusion revolves around the amount of language being 
changed, with a rulemaking format that makes it hard to compare 
versions of the regulation, and which appears to be inconsistent with 
NPS's statement it is making minor changes. Although the proposed and 
final rules contain substantial text showing edits, much of this 
language is not new, not all of it represents a change to current 
language and most of it reflects long-standing regulations in place for 
more than 30 years. The main goal is to make a single change across 
multiple provisions: a return to the framework that more closely 
adheres to ANILCA's requirements in implementing its mandates for 
Alaska park areas. Changes to the restored regulations reflect changes 
in the governing situation, like the creation of the Federal 
Subsistence Board and its relocation into the Office of the Secretary, 
or respond to comments on the proposed rule, which made several 
requests for specific information and ideas for reform. See 91 FR 
11483, 11486, 11488, 11491, and 11492.
    Another identified area of confusion relates to changes in 
definitions, specifically modifying established terms like 
``subsistence uses'' and ``customary trade'' and more recent terms like 
``handicraft'', leading to questions about what activities are allowed, 
who qualifies, and how enforcement will work. Clear definitions can 
help public land users and enforcement officials better understand 
compliance with unique laws like ANILCA.
    One of the requests made in the proposed rule for specific reform 
ideas involves NPS's definition of ``customary trade''. 91 FR 11483, 
11491. Reverting to the pre-2015 language does not seem to cover the 
breadth of activities implicated by this term, and if the current 
definition does or could do that better, retaining or reforming it 
would be an improvement over pre-2015 regulations. One alternative to 
retention would be to adopt the definition used by the Federal 
Subsistence Management

[[Page 65995]]

Program at 43 CFR 51.4, which is very similar to the current NPS 
definition, or to include a cross-reference to that definition in 36 
CFR 13.420 along with any differences, similar to the definition for 
``handicraft''. Without a clear expression of a need for reform in the 
comments, the final rule retains the current definitions of ``customary 
trade'' and ``handicraft'' (a term used in NPS's definition of 
``customary trade'') to provide consistency for subsistence users and 
law enforcement. This change from the proposed rule also reduces the 
amount of edited text in the final rule, addressing the source of 
confusion described earlier.
    The final rule does not retain the recently added definition for 
``animal parts''. As noted above and in the proposed rule, 91 FR 11491, 
this does not mean subsistence use and gathering of animal parts is not 
allowed in Alaska park areas. This definition was added when NPS began 
regulating these activities, primarily due to concerns that general 
rules and policies prohibiting visitors from removing natural objects 
may be interpreted to prohibit subsistence uses. See 82 FR 3626, 3627 
(January 12, 2017). Reflecting on these stated concerns, the 
definition's decade in use addressing them, comments encouraging 
removal of unnecessary barriers to subsistence, and statutory direction 
and guidance in ANILCA Title VIII, NPS does not see the same value in 
retaining the definition for ``animal parts'' as it does for 
``customary trade'' and ``handicraft''. A basic understanding of the 
term should suffice for compliance and enforcement purposes.
    To make the relationship between nationwide rules, administrative 
policies, and ANILCA easier to understand in practice moving forward, 
the final rule adds minor wording changes in 36 CFR 13.35 and 13.400. 
These adjustments are intended to reduce confusion about how System-
wide prohibitions apply to local residents and others authorized to 
conduct subsistence activities. They also clarify that subsistence uses 
are not treated or managed the same way as general public uses, and 
that Alaska park areas operate under ANILCA's balanced mandates rather 
than System-wide direction alone, unless Congress states otherwise. 
These edits and the final rule's restoration of Alaska-specific closure 
procedures in the sections they address (36 CFR part 13, subparts B and 
F) also respond to comments expressing confusion about which 
authorities apply in a given situation and concerns about how closures 
are administered and communicated.
    Several commenters cautioned that greater alignment between state 
and federal regulations in Alaska park areas could unintentionally give 
state managers too much authority over subsistence uses on federal 
public lands. Many expressed frustration about the overlapping state 
and federal systems in park areas and conflicting priorities, even with 
specific roles assigned to each manager. As in Comment 2, commenters 
noted state management actions do not always support customary and 
traditional uses by prioritizing opportunities for all Alaskans, 
marginalizing regional input, and increasing competition and harvest 
pressure on struggling stocks and populations, and that obscure 
phrasing in the proposed rule could expand the reach of those actions. 
Comments frequently referred to direction in ANILCA to ensure rural 
residents are advising in management decisions and prioritized among 
consumptive users of Alaska's fish and wildlife resources.
    Alaska's system of ``dual management'' is a measured and complex 
approach to these overlapping systems to meet constitutional and 
statutory mandates, and commenters are correct that federal and state 
priorities are not always aligned, an issue of particular concern for 
communities that rely heavily on wild food resources. To be clear, this 
rule does not change ANILCA's rural subsistence priority or the intent, 
priorities, or structure of the Federal Subsistence Management Program. 
Under dual management, the State manages fish and wildlife populations 
according to sustained yield principles on all lands and waters unless 
Congress modifies that authority, like in ANILCA Title VIII, and 
federal agencies have their own responsibilities and authorities on 
federal lands to protect public safety, meet statutory purposes, and 
fulfill federal mandates, like the rural subsistence priority. This 
structure is explicitly recognized in ANILCA and the rules and land use 
plans that guide managers and decision-makers in Alaska. As discussed 
in the response to Comment 2, this rule does not change that division 
of authorities. State law will continue to govern harvest activities on 
all lands to the extent permissible under federal law. However, so long 
as the State cannot implement the rural subsistence priority required 
in ANILCA, the Secretaries of the Interior and Agriculture are 
responsible for administering ANILCA's framework for the subsistence 
taking and uses of fish and wildlife on public lands and have delegated 
most of their responsibilities to the Federal Subsistence Board. The 
NPS will carry out its respective duties to manage park areas, 
including consultation with the Federal Subsistence Board under the 
2015 revisions to Sec.  13.490, which is retained in the final rule. 
State and federal managers will carry out their respective duties, and 
the revisions in this rulemaking will better equip unit managers in 
Alaska to meet NPS's obligations within this shared system consistent 
with the balanced mandates in ANILCA. The NPS has the authority to 
preempt state law where necessary to implement the NPS Organic Act, 
Wilderness Act, ANILCA, and other statutory direction. The NPS has not 
found it necessary to exercise that authority in issuing this rule.
    To help limit the potential for other misunderstandings, now or in 
the future, a table is included in the section entitled ``Final Rule'' 
above to show which regulations are changing in a substantive way with 
a short summary explaining the change.
    9. Comment: Some comments supported or opposed changes beyond those

where hunting would still be permitted; we don't do that in the lower

    Adhering closely to the proposed rule, and its close adherence to 
the regulations that guided management of Alaska park areas from 
ANILCA's passage until 2015, will minimize disruption for users and 
managers and restore the Alaska-specific procedures they long relied on 
for responsible and cooperative resource management. The NPS has 
considered the comments encouraging additional or different action and 
determined that changes in the final rule must meet an exacting 
standard with a compelling reason, like the creation of the Federal 
Subsistence Management Program, or make important clarifications, like 
the definition for ``customary trade'' and other reforms to facilitate 
allowed uses and the continuation of subsistence uses. Many comments 
included valuable recommendations for improvements to the framework 
beyond the scope of this rulemaking, which would require or benefit 
from consultation, notice and comment, and environmental analysis in a 
separate rulemaking process.
    In the final rule, definitions are aligned with state and federal 
regulations to the greatest extent practicable to promote consistency 
across jurisdictional boundaries and simplify compliance in the places 
where people hunt, fish, and trap in Alaska. Congress intended for all 
forms of regulated hunting to be allowed in national preserves under 
applicable state and federal law, where relevant distinctions between 
subsistence and ``sport'' hunting are made and enforced in Alaska park 
areas. Consistent with that direction, this rule restores the roles, 
responsibilities, and limitations that governed wildlife management in 
Alaska park areas for decades following their establishment in ANILCA. 
While commenters expressed differing views regarding wildlife 
conservation and access to harvest opportunities, the restored 
regulations reflect the balance Congress directed for Alaska and the 
framework that successfully implemented that balance for decades. The 
NPS has concluded that departures from this long-standing framework, 
whether to expand or restrict authorities, should be supported by 
substantial justification, thoughtful analysis, meaningful public 
engagement, and a demonstrated basis in law for improving upon this 
proven management approach for Alaska park areas.

Changes From the Proposed Rule

    Based on public comments received and NPS's further review, the 
final rule makes the following changes to the proposed rule:
    1. The title of this rule has been changed to reflect its scope 
that addresses hunting and trapping in national preserves, but also 
subsistence harvest of live wildlife and plant materials, and 
procedures used to restrict public use and access to Alaska park areas, 
including for subsistence.
    2. Sec.  13.50(f), notice methods: The proposed rule and pre-2015 
regulations could be interpreted to allow for public notice through the 
posting of appropriate signs, instead of or in addition to newspaper 
publications, radio broadcasts, post office flyers, available maps, and 
other methods likely to inform affected users. The final rule replaces 
``or'' with ``and'' to confirm that all the listed notice methods are 
required, where available, rather than having an option to close areas 
to harvest through the posting of signs alone.
    3. Sec. Sec.  13.35(g) and 13.400(e), authorized users: In response 
to comments and further agency review, the final rule adds language to 
clarify that generally applicable limitations on park visitors do not 
generally apply to those authorized to harvest renewable resources for 
subsistence uses in park areas in Alaska, including local rural 
residents, as defined in Sec.  13.420, and other federally qualified 
users engaged in authorized harvest activities (e.g., subsistence 
permits issued by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service pursuant to the 
Migratory Bird Treaty Act).
    4. Sec.  13.400(e), new citation: A cross-reference to the Federal 
Subsistence Board regulations at 43 CFR part 51 was added in response 
to comments, including from the Alaska Federation of Natives, seeking 
greater specificity for subsistence users than a general reference to 
ANILCA as applicable federal law.
    5. Sec.  13.420, definitions: The proposed rule solicited comment 
on revising the definition of ``customary trade.'' No submissions 
included specific reforms that would improve upon any particular 
definition. Rather than reverting to the pre-2015 language, the final 
rule retains the current definitions of ``customary trade'' and 
``handicraft'' to provide continuity and reduce confusion for 
subsistence users and enforcement personnel.
    6. Sec.  13.420, revised citation: The final rule updates the 
cross-reference in the definition of ``handicraft'' to Federal 
Subsistence Board regulations formerly located at 50 CFR part 100 which 
were transferred to 43 CFR part 51 to align with the transfer of the 
Office of Subsistence Management to the Office of the Secretary of the 
Interior. See 90 FR 34142 (July 18, 2025).
    7. Sec.  13.490, consultation: In response to comments and agency 
review, the requirement to consult with the Federal Subsistence Board 
before temporarily closing a park area to harvest for subsistence uses 
is retained in the final rule as an important update for ensuring rural 
Alaska residents have a meaningful role in the management of fish and 
wildlife and subsistence uses on all public lands in Alaska.

Compliance With Other Laws, Executive Orders and Department Policy

Regulatory Planning and Review Executive Orders 12866 and 14192

    This rule has been determined to be significant for purposes of 
Executive Order 12866 and has been submitted for review by the Office 
of Information and Regulatory Affairs (OIRA) within the Office of 
Management and Budget (OMB). This rule is a deregulatory action under 
Executive Order 14192. It removes the 2017 permit requirement for 
collecting animal parks for handicrafts, removes restrictions for all 
user groups on hunting bears over bait, and removes the 2024 firearm-
related definition of ``trapping'' and its associated compliance 
burdens, without imposing any new permit, reporting, or recordkeeping 
requirements on regulated entities. The rule also restores fixed 
procedural requirements (e.g., durational limits, consultation 
obligations, and notice requirements) that constrain agency discretion 
and reduce regulatory uncertainty for the public, which the 2015 and 
2017 rules had displaced with greater focus on System-wide standards 
and less focus on affected areas and users. Because this rule aligns 
federal and state requirements, rather than adding or maintaining 
conflicting layers of regulation, NPS does not consider it a new 
regulatory burden for purposes of this classification.

exists and that extraordinary measures must be taken for public safety 
or to assure the continued viability of a particular plant population, 
the Superintendent may immediately close all or any portion of a park

shall be effective when made, shall be for a period not to exceed sixty 
(60) days, and may not subsequently be extended unless the 
Superintendent establishes, after notice and public hearing in the 
affected vicinity and other locations as appropriate, that such closure 
should be extended.
    (2) Notice of administrative actions taken pursuant to this 
section, and the reasons justifying such actions, shall be published in 
at least one newspaper of general circulation within the State and at 
least one local newspaper if available, and information about such 
actions and reasons also shall be made available for broadcast on local 
radio stations in a manner reasonably calculated to inform local rural 
residents in the affected vicinity. All closures shall be designated on 
a map which shall be available for public inspection at the office of 
the Superintendent of the affected park area and the post office or 
postal authority of every affected community within or near the park 
area, or by the posting of signs in the vicinity of the restrictions,
