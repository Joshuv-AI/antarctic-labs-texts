Masticatory function, inflammation, and mortality after stroke: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Wenshu Zhang, Yu Wang, Yuli Wu, Feiyu Chen, Xiaoping Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to clarify the relationship between masticatory function and mortality (both all-cause and cardiovascular) in stroke patients and explore the potential mediating role of systemic inflammation. This study utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey cycles spanning 1999 to 2018, comprising 1708 participants aged 20 or older with a history of stroke. Mortality was tracked through December 31, 2018. Masticatory function was assessed based on the count of functional tooth units (FTUs). The associations between masticatory function and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality were evaluated using Cox regression model and subgroup analysis. Mediation analysis was further performed to assess the potential mediating effects of the systemic inflammatory biomarkers, namely the aggregate index of systemic inflammation and the systemic inflammation response index, in the relationship between masticatory function and mortality. Stroke participants with a greater number of FTUs exhibited lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Cox regression model revealed that FTUs were inversely associated with all-cause mortality (hazard ratio = 0.73, 95% confidence interval = 0.57–0.93) but not significantly associated with cardiovascular mortality (hazard ratio = 0.80, 95% confidence interval = 0.54–1.18). Mediation analysis indicated that aggregate index of systemic inflammation and systemic inflammation response index mediated 3.31% and 6.20%, respectively, of the association between FTUs and all-cause mortality, and 2.17% and 4.27%, respectively, of the association between FTUs and cardiovascular mortality. Improved masticatory function is associated with reduced all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in stroke participants. Furthermore, systemic inflammation appears to partially mediate the association between masticatory function and all-cause mortality. There is stronger and consistent evidence for an association with all-cause mortality than with cardiovascular mortality. These findings suggest that masticatory function may serve as a simple and practical prognostic marker for risk stratification in the stroke population.

1. Introduction

Worldwide, stroke is 2nd only to ischemic heart disease as a cause of death,[1] and it also represents the 5th most common cause of combined morbidity and mortality in the United States,[2] with 96.4 cases per 100,000 men and 73.5 cases per 100,000 women.[1] Among these cases, cardiovascular death is particularly common.[3] Hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, kidney disease, smoking, and excessive alcohol consumption are among the most prevalent contributors to stroke risk. However, despite aggressive measures against traditional risk factors, stroke survivors still face “residual risk.”[4] Therefore, it is urgent to explore new and modifiable pathophysiological pathways.

Masticatory function, a modifiable risk factor, is potentially associated with higher mortality risk in stroke patients, as evidence suggests a correlation between diminished masticatory ability and elevated mortality.[5] This link may be explained through several pathways: poor masticatory function may lead to reduced intake of a healthy diet, thereby accelerating biological aging[5]; it may also impair cerebral blood flow stimulation and brain function enhancement provided by masticatory movements.[6–8] Furthermore, systemic inflammation may serve as a core mediator connecting masticatory function to health outcomes,[9,10] as declined masticatory function can promote a pro-inflammatory state that exacerbates inflammatory responses and triggers complications. In cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, systemic chronic low-grade inflammation acts as a primary mediator and is widely recognized as an important pathological mechanism contributing to disease occurrence and patient mortality.[11,12] As a serious cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease, stroke itself involves a strong inflammatory stress response during its onset,[13] and impaired masticatory function may represent a significant factor that aggravates this inflammatory state within the complex pathological process.

Among the numerous composite inflammatory indicators calculated based on blood routine, aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI) and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) have unique indicator integration methods and good clinical application value.[14] In the “chewing function–inflammation–prognosis” pathway of this study, the 2 new composite inflammatory indicators, AISI and SIRI, have significant advantages. They can more stably and comprehensively reflect the changes in the inflammatory state caused by impaired chewing function. Different degrees of impaired chewing function may affect the quantity and proportion of the above blood cells through multiple pathways, thereby causing changes in the values of AISI and SIRI. By monitoring the changes in these 2 indicators, we can more accurately understand the association between chewing function and the inflammatory state, as well as how the inflammatory state affects the prognosis of stroke patients. Compared with traditional inflammatory markers, AISI and SIRI can provide more comprehensive and accurate information,[15,16] providing a powerful tool for in-depth exploration of the mechanism by which chewing function affects the prognosis of stroke patients.

Based on existing scientific evidence, a coherent pathophysiological pathway can be hypothesized linking impaired masticatory function to increased mortality risk: poor chewing capacity may initiate local oral inflammation and systemic dietary alterations, which in turn trigger or amplify systemic inflammation reflected by elevated AISI and SIRI leading to accelerated atherosclerosis, heightened risk of adverse cardiovascular events, and ultimately elevated mortality. However, no prior study has employed prospective cohort data and rigorous mediation analysis to empirically examine the specific mediating roles of AISI and SIRI in the relationship between masticatory function and mortality. Therefore, this study aims to utilize prospective cohort data to investigate the association between masticatory function and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among stroke patients, and to determine whether systemic inflammation as measured by AISI and SIRI mediates this relationship, with quantification of the mediation effects. By revealing the potential “chewing–inflammation–mortality” pathway, this research addresses a critical mechanistic gap and provides high-level pathophysiological evidence linking oral health to overall survival. If mediation is confirmed, AISI and SIRI could serve as actionable biomarkers for risk assessment, offering novel intervention strategies such as improving masticatory function or targeting inflammation to refine prognostic management in stroke patients.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Research design and participants

Participants were selected from the 1999 to 2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database based on the criteria of being at least 20 years of age and having a history of stroke. To ensure the integrity and reliability of the study results, specific exclusion criteria were established, including: individuals with missing survival time data (N = 2); individuals with missing masticatory function (functional tooth unit [FTU]) data (N = 394); and patients with missing systemic inflammation index (AISI) data (N = 92). A total of 1708 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Mortality information of NHANES participants were linked to the National Death Index maintained by the National Center for Health Statistics, and survival duration was measured in person years. During the initial data collection process, written informed consent was secured from all individuals involved. This study was exempt from ethical review by an Institutional Review Board in accordance with National Institutes of Health policies, as it utilized de-identified data without direct contact with participants.

Flowchart of participant selection for the study on functional tooth units (FTUs) and mortality. NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

2.2. FTU assessment

The primary exposure factor in this study was masticatory function, assessed by trained health technologists through calibrated dental examinations conducted during the 2007 to 2008 NHANES survey cycle (the only NHANES cycle with standardized FTU evaluation). All examiners underwent centralized training approved by NHANES and utilized standard dental models for calibration.[17] Masticatory performance was quantified by the number of posterior tooth pairs, namely, all premolars and molars. The count of posterior teeth (comprising natural teeth and implants) defined the FTUs with 1 FTU equivalent to 1 antagonistic pair, and 1 point awarded for each pair of upper and lower teeth. There are 4 teeth in each of the 4 oral compartments (2 premolars 1B and 2B, and 2 molars 1M and 2M, excluding the 3rd molar, and the wisdom tooth). There are 4 pairs on each side, totaling 8 pairs, with FTU ranging from 0 to 8 points. A FTU ≥ 5 points indicates adequate oral chewing ability, while a FTU of 0 to 4 points indicates insufficient chewing ability. Accordingly, subjects were classified into 2 groups as per prior research: the adequate chewing ability group (FTU ≥ 5) and the insufficient chewing ability group (FTU = 0–4).[18,19]

2.3. Inflammatory index measurement (AISI and SIRI)

Data for the inflammatory markers AISI and SIRI originated from standard complete blood count testing. The NHANES study protocol ensured standardized handling of all samples, mandating collection with certified phlebotomy equipment and processing in certified laboratories. From the complete blood count, data on specific white blood cell subtypes – neutrophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes – and platelets were acquired. The integrity of this data was secured through a robust quality control framework that involved standardized operating procedures, the use of validated instruments, and frequent calibration checks. The formulas for calculating the indices are as follows:

SIRI = Neutrophil count × Monocyte count/Lymphocyte count;

SIRI = Neutrophil count × Monocyte count/Lymphocyte count;

AISI = Neutrophil count × Platelet count × Monocyte count/Lymphocyte count.[20,21]

AISI = Neutrophil count × Platelet count × Monocyte count/Lymphocyte count.[20,21]

2.4. Mortality rate

The main outcome of this study was all-cause mortality. The secondary outcome was cause-specific mortality, including cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality. According to the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases codes, specific deaths were defined as the underlying causes. Therefore. For instance, CVD mortality (10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases codes I00 to I99) refers to deaths caused by cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases.[22]

2.5. Covariates

Covariates include socio-demographic variables, lifestyle behaviors, self-reported data and chronic diseases. Socio-demographic variables include the survey year, age, gender, marital status, race/ethnicity, educational level, family annual income and family poverty income ratio. The survey year is an ordinal variable; age is continuous; gender is defined as male and female; marital status is classified as married, single (single status encompass those who are separated, divorced, or widowed) and unmarried. Race/ethnicity is categorized as nonHispanic White, nonHispanic Black, Mexican-American and others.[23] Educational level is divided into below high school, high school or equivalent, and university or above.[24] Family poverty income ratio is classified as <1.3, ≥1.3 to <3.5, ≥3.5. Lifestyle behaviors include smoking history, alcohol consumption and body mass index (BMI). Smoking history is classified as no-smokers (never smoked or smoked <100 cigarettes) and smokers (smoked at least 100 cigarettes).[25] Alcohol consumption is classified as nondrinkers and current drinkers. In addition, based on BMI, the population is divided into underweight (<18.5.0 kg/m2), normal (≥18.5.0 kg/m2, <25 kg/m2), overweight (≥25 kg/m2, <30 kg/m2), and obese (≥30.0 kg/m2). Chronic disease data were collected using patient interviews, including doctor-diagnosed hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia.

2.6. Statistical methods

The relationship between FTU and mortality outcomes was assessed through multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression. We employed 3 models: a crude model (Model 1); a model controlling for socioeconomic and lifestyle factors (Model 2); and a comprehensively adjusted model that also accounted for clinical conditions including hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and BMI (Model 3).To validate the robustness of the primary analysis results, we conducted sensitivity analyses. The binary variable (<60 years vs ≥60 years) was converted into a continuous age variable for the primary association analysis, with results consistent with the original model (Fig. S1A and B, Tables S1 and S2, Supplemental Digital Content). Results are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals. Survival trends over time were analyzed with Kaplan–Meier curves, and the log-rank test was used to evaluate differences. In parallel, the associations of inflammatory indicators with FTU and with mortality were examined using multivariate logistic regression and Cox regression, respectively. The potential mediation effect of these inflammatory markers on the FTU-mortality pathway was tested with a dedicated software package, with adjustments applied as in Model 3. A significant mediation was concluded only when the total effect and the indirect effect were both statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R software (v4.5.1; The Comprehensive R Archive Network, CRAN), EmpowerStats (v2.0; http://www.empowerstats.net/analysis), the rms package, and Zstats (v0.9; www.zstats.net). Specifically, weighted analyses were performed with the survey package, Kaplan-Meier curves were plotted using the jskm package, subgroup forest plots were visualized with the forestploter package, and mediation analyses were conducted using the mediation package. For group comparisons based on FTU status, the chi-square test was used for categorical variables and the Kruskal–Wallis H test for continuous variables, with a 2-sided P value <.05 denoting statistical significance.

To assess the correlation between FTU and all-cause mortality as well as cardiovascular disease mortality, we conducted a subgroup analysis. After adjusting for covariates in the entire population, FTU showed statistically significant differences in all-cause mortality but no statistically significant differences in cardiovascular disease mortality. Moreover, there was no interaction between each subgroup and the exposure.(Fig. 3A and B).

4. Discussion

This prospective cohort study is the 1st time to reveal that better masticatory function is independently associated with significantly reduced risks of all-cause mortality and CVD mortality among stroke survivors. More importantly, this research found that systemic inflammation (characterized by the composite indicators AISI and SIRI) played a significant mediating role in the “masticatory function–all-cause mortality” association, with mediation proportions of 3.31% (for AISI) and 6.20% (for SIRI), respectively. Nevertheless, this mediating effect did not reach statistical significance in the “masticatory function–CVD mortality” pathway.

This study also found that after adjusting for multiple confounding factors, the all-cause mortality risk in the FTU ≥ 5 group was significantly lower by approximately 27% compared to the FTU < 5 group. Although the HRs varied across Model 1, Model 2, and Model 3, they were all significantly below 1, indicating that the association between FTU and reduced mortality risk is robust and not confounded by other factors. This suggests that good masticatory function is beneficial for longevity, which aligns with previous research results.[26] However, this study is the 1st time to focus specifically on stroke patients, a high-risk and special population, thereby confirming this association within this group and providing better intervention measures for the prognosis and survival time extension of stroke patients, which has significant clinical significance. Several mechanisms may explain how good masticatory function reduces mortality. One potential explanation is malnutrition associated with impaired chewing ability.[27] Furthermore, a review article suggests that chewing stimulation can affect central nervous system development via the brain-oral axis, in which hippocampal neurogenesis, neuronal activity, and brain-derived neurotrophic factor expression may play a role within the neural stimulation pathway.[28] Additionally, this study identified the AISI and SIRI as mediating factors in the relationship between FTU and mortality. Therefore, The inflammatory pathway may represent another core mechanism through which good masticatory function reduces mortality.

The research pathway of its mediating effect is as follows: Path a (exposure → mediator): It clearly indicates that “better masticatory function significantly reduces AISI and SIRI levels.” This provides direct evidence for “antiinflammatory effect of mastication.” Path b (mediator → outcome): It confirms that “elevated AISI and SIRI levels independently predict a higher risk of all-cause mortality.” This consolidates the role of inflammation as a driver of mortality risk. From this, it can be seen that the 2 paths constitute a complete biological pathway of “masticatory function → reducing systemic inflammation → lowering mortality risk.” Moreover, AISI and SIRI, as comprehensive inflammatory indicators, are more comprehensive and superior than single indicators and have stability. Nevertheless, in the CVD mortality outcome, the mediating effect was not significant. The possible reasons for this could be attributed to several aspects, including issues related to statistical power: the number of CVD death events might be fewer than the total number of all-cause deaths, resulting in insufficient test power. Specific pathways: The protective effect of chewing function on CVD mortality might be mainly achieved through other more direct mechanisms, such as improving autonomic nerve function, blood pressure regulation, or the stability of atherosclerotic plaques, and these mechanisms were not fully captured by AISI/SIRI. Specificity of inflammatory indicators: AISI/SIRI reflects systemic inflammation, and its prediction of CVD mortality might be less accurate than some more specific cardiovascular inflammatory markers (such as interleukin-6, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein). The underlying mechanism may be related to the following aspects. Firstly, chewing is not merely a simple mechanical action in the mouth; it is also closely related to the physiological functions of the entire body. When the chewing function is impaired, it may affect the digestion and absorption of food, trigger intestinal inflammation, thereby leading to a systemic inflammatory response, or affect the microenvironment in the mouth, increase the risk of oral infections, and trigger or aggravate the systemic inflammatory response, ultimately resulting in death.[29,30] Secondly, AISI and SIRI integrate 4 important blood cell parameters: neutrophils, monocytes, platelets, and lymphocytes. It can more comprehensively assess the overall activation status of the immune and inflammatory systems. It not only reflects the situation of acute inflammatory responses but also takes into account the changes of cells during the immune regulation process and the continuous inflammatory process, thus more accurately capturing the changes in the inflammatory state throughout the process from chronic inflammation to acute inflammation exacerbation.

This study has significant national representativeness and adopts a prospective cohort design, effectively reducing recall bias in retrospective studies and improving the reliability of causal inference. Through strict multivariate adjustment strategies, we controlled a large number of potential confounding factors,[31] including but not limited to age, gender, smoking history, hypertension, and diabetes, thereby enhancing the internal validity of the research results. What is particularly important is that this study is the 1st time to deeply explore the association between chewing function and mortality in stroke patients and innovatively verifies the mediating role of systemic inflammatory indicators (AISI and SIRI) in this association, filling the research gap into this field and providing important theoretical basis for subsequent mechanism research and intervention strategies. Although this study strives for rigor in design and analysis, there are still some inevitable limitations. Firstly, although we adjusted a large number of covariates, there may still be unmeasured confounding factors, such as more detailed indicators of social economic status like income level, comprehensive assessment of dietary quality, etc, which may affect the relationship between chewing function and mortality. Secondly, the measurement of chewing function mainly relies on self-report or relatively simple functional assessment tools, which may lead to measurement errors, especially for those patients with cognitive impairment or communication difficulties. Moreover, the mediation analysis based on cross-sectional baseline data in this study has inherent limitations in causal temporal inference and cannot fully determine the causal relationship between chewing function, systemic inflammation, and mortality. Finally, since this study mainly focuses on a specific population (such as stroke patients in a certain country or region), we need to be cautious when extrapolating its results to other populations or stroke patients in other countries, as there may be significant differences in genetic background, lifestyle, and medical resources among different populations. In conclusion, chewing function is an independent protective factor for the mortality risk of stroke patients, and it partially mediates through systemic inflammation inhibition. Future intervention studies can be conducted to verify whether improving chewing function can directly reduce inflammatory levels and mortality risk. Alternatively, this mediation model can be repeatedly verified in more diverse cohorts. There is an opportunity to explore other possible mediating mechanisms (such as nutritional status, vagal nerve tension, etc).

5. Conclusion

This study confirmed the negative correlation between FTU and the all-cause mortality rate in the stroke population, and also revealed the significant mediating role of inflammation in this association. These findings provide more evidence to support the clinical value of FTU in predicting poor prognosis after stroke, and offer an important reference for the early risk stratification and intervention strategy development of the stroke population.
