“Having Lunch Now” : Understanding How Users Engage with a Proactive Agent for Daily Planning and Self-Reflection
Adnan Abbas, Caleb Wohn, Arnav Jagtap, Eugenia H Rho, Young-Ho Kim, Sang Won Lee

Abstract
. Conversational agents have been studied as tools to scaffold planning and self-reflection for productivity and well-being. While prior work has demonstrated positive outcomes, we still lack a clear understanding of what drives these results and how users behave and communicate with agents that act as coaches rather than assistants. Such understanding is critical for designing interactions in which agents foster meaningful behavioral change. We conducted a 14-day longitudinal study with 12 participants using a proactive agent that initiated regular check-ins to support daily planning and reflection. Our findings reveal diverse interaction patterns: participants accepted or negotiated suggestions, developed shared mental models, reported progress, and at times resisted or disengaged. We also identified problematic aspects of the agent’s behavior, including rigidity, premature turn-taking, and overpromising. Our work contributes to understanding how people interact with a proactive, coach-like agent and offers design considerations for facilitating effective behavioral change.



1.
Introduction

With the presence of large-language models (LLMs), Conversational Agents (CAs) are becoming more human-like, exhibit better conversational intelligence and novelty in interactions
(
Rayan et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Sorin et al. 2024
)
.
LLM-powered CAs can interpret intent, adapt their tone, and maintain contextual continuity across multiple sessions
(
Mahmood et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2024
)
.
Leveraging these capabilities, researchers have studied coaching-style agents: systems designed to advise, persuade, and guide everyday behavior using the breadth of their knowledge
(
Jörke et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Arakawa and Yakura 2024
;
Wilhelm et al. 2025a
)
.
Oftentimes, such agents are
proactive
in a way that they initiate interactions by reminding, suggesting, or even steering users toward specific actions
(
Liu et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2025
;
Baraglia et al. 2016
)
, compared to reactive agents that respond only when prompted.
Studies show that proactivity in CAs has several utilities in human–AI interaction: introducing new topics
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
, offering richer suggestions
(
Peng et al. 2019
)
, and persuading users toward target behaviors
(
Lei et al. 2022
)
.

One domain where proactive CAs have been employed is supporting workers’ productivity and well-being
(
Kimani et al. 2019
;
Ahire et al. 2024
;
Kocielnik et al. 2018a
;
Williams et al. 2018
;
redacted for anonymity [n. d.]
)
.
In these studies, rather than CAs responding to a user’s request, CAs take the role of a productivity coach or well-being coach and regularly prompt workers to articulate plans or reflect on their day, proactively leading the conversation.
By initiating these check-ins, CAs can help make users’ intentions more concrete and actionable, thereby improving adherence to planning
(
Luo et al. 2020
;
Kimani et al. 2019
;
Grover et al. 2020
)
.
Through reflective prompts, these agents can increase users’ awareness of everyday habits and foster behavior change
(
Mols et al. 2016
;
Kocielnik et al. 2018a
;
Ho et al. 2018
)
.
Many of these systems relied on daily check-ins (e.g., morning or evening check-in) with a specific purpose, such as to remind users of and revisit their initial plans or progress
(
Kocielnik et al. 2018a
;
Kimani et al. 2019
;
Ahire et al. 2022
;
redacted for anonymity [n. d.]
)
.
These studies report positive outcomes such as greater engagement with work, improved work–life balance, better physical health, and more structured planning
(
Kocielnik et al. 2018a
;
Grover et al. 2020
;
Kimani et al. 2019
;
Ahire et al. 2024
;
Ahire et al. 2022
;
redacted for anonymity [n. d.]
)
.
However, we still lack an understanding of
how
people actually communicate with CAs, leaving important gaps in knowledge about the mechanisms through which CAs support workers. This gap limits the ability of prior work to inform how CAs should facilitate desirable behavioral changes in the context of productivity and well-being.

To address these gaps, we aim to closely observe and investigate how users communicate with a proactive coach that checks in twice per day to facilitate planning in the morning and self-reflection in the evening.
We use thematic and dialogue act analyses of user interactions to examine how users cooperatively interact with CAs in order to understand the mechanisms by which a proactive agent facilitates desirable changes in productivity and well-being. Other studies have used conversation analysis to provide deeper insight into how CAs and users communicate
(
Rapp et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2020
;
Seering et al. 2020
;
Porcheron et al. 2018
;
Pelikan and Broth 2016
)
. This method is particularly well-suited for uncovering the sequential and interactional dynamics of dialogue
(
Meredith 2019
;
Liddicoat 2021
)
, allowing us to capture subtle cues in how proactivity is received and negotiated in real use.
Specifically, we ask the following research questions:

•

RQ1
: How do users engage with a proactive agent that facilitates daily planning and reflection?

•

RQ2
: What conversational behaviors of the coaching agent cause communication breakdowns in conversational interaction?

To answer these questions, we conducted a two-week field deployment of a proactive, coaching-style agent (PITCH) with 12 graduate students, analyzing 336 conversations (3,181 turns) through thematic and dialogue act analysis.
Our analysis shows that the most common user behaviors were planning upcoming tasks, reporting progress, reflecting on routines, and sometimes ignoring the agent’s prompts.
Users often cooperated by adopting suggestions or providing contextual explanations when advice did not fit, while also refusing or negotiating to assert their own work styles.
Notably, participants complied more often when the agent pursued explicit agendas, but they also refused more often when those agendas felt misaligned.
Beyond scheduled check-ins, users treated the chat as an external memory—recording plans, reflecting on habits, making commitments to change, and even offering unsolicited updates as a running log of their day.
Finally, we identified problematic aspects of the agent’s behavior, including rigidity, premature turn-taking, and overpromising its capabilities, which point to design opportunities for more flexible and context-aware coaching agents.

This study makes four key contributions:

(1)

Empirical insight:
We provide an in-situ account of how users communicate with a proactive coaching agent in daily work contexts, based on a two-week field deployment with graduate students.

(2)

Conversation analysis:
We conduct a fine-grained analysis of 336 interactions (3,181 turns), uncovering how users cooperate, negotiate, or resist agent prompts, and how these behaviors shape productivity and reflection.

(3)

Dataset:
We release an anonymized dataset of 336 conversations (3,181 turns), which can serve as a resource for future research on conversational agents, proactive interaction, and productivity support, available at [redacted for anonymity].

(4)

Design implications:
We identify opportunities (e.g., leveraging explicit agendas and external memory functions) and challenges (e.g., rigidity, premature turn-taking, and overpromising) for designing proactive CAs that better support planning and self-regulation.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Planning and Reflection with Conversational Agents at Work

HCI researchers have explored the role of conversational interaction to facilitate planning and reflection for knowledge workers’ productivity.
Several works developed an agent to collect self-reported tasks from users, manage scheduling, and facilitate work planning
(
Kimani et al. 2019
;
Grover et al. 2020
;
Kocielnik et al. 2018a
;
Marikyan et al. 2022
)
.
For example, Amber
(
Kimani et al. 2019
)
initiated conversations to elicit user’s tasks, provided reminders for task scheduling, suggested breaks, and facilitated work reflections based on user interactions and context sensing.
At the same time, agents also helped with automating some of the knowledge workers’ tasks, such as scheduling meetings
(
Cranshaw et al. 2017
)
, managing teamwork
(
Toxtli et al. 2018
)
, finding work resources
(
Liao et al. 2016
)
, organizing email inboxes
(
Faulring et al. 2010
)
, and managing to-do lists
(
Gil and Ratnakar 2008
)
.
Findings from these works highlight both benefits for productivity and challenges around timing and intrusiveness, with users often perceiving proactive interventions as annoying or misaligned with their needs
(
Cranshaw et al. 2017
;
Bellotti and Edwards 2001
)
.
While these systems improved productivity, they didn’t study how users actually respond to and take up the agent’s suggestions while planning.

Beyond productivity, CAs have also been designed to support knowledge workers’ well-being.
This includes prompting healthy breaks
(
Ahire et al. 2024
;
Cambo et al. 2017
;
Ahire et al. 2022
)
, facilitating reflection on work-life balance
(
Williams et al. 2018
)
, and helping to manage stress
(
Meyer et al. 2019
;
Følstad et al. 2019
)
.
Similar works have explored how workers can be encouraged to take work breaks for their physical and mental well-being to mitigate the negative effects of sedentary lifestyle
(
Ahire et al. 2022
;
Cambo et al. 2017
;
Ahire et al. 2024
)
.
Research shows that engaging in reflective conversations can facilitate self-awareness, helping people be more aware of task switching, track progress, evaluate obstacles, and reflect on higher-level goals
(
Cuadra et al. 2021
;
Tian et al. 2021
;
Marikyan et al. 2022
;
Kocielnik et al. 2018a
;
Kimani et al. 2019
)
.
Such reflection fostered healthier work practices and greater satisfaction, particularly in fast-paced, busy environments where self-reflection is often overlooked
(
Kocielnik et al. 2018a
)
.
Even though reflection improves work habits
(
Kocielnik et al. 2018a
;
Kocielnik et al. 2018b
)
, little is known about what users actually articulate in reflection and how they do so in conversation with an agent. Recent studies suggest that reflective interactions often remain surface-level with LLM-based agents, supporting only single-loop learning
(
Arakawa and Yakura 2024
)
, with participants at times describing the agent’s responses as formulaic or emotionally flat
(
Dechant et al. 2025
)
.
Our study addresses this gap by examining how often users engage in self-reflection, what they share, and how their reflective behaviors unfold in dialogue.

Most of the above studies were conducted in the era before LLMs
(
Kocielnik et al. 2018a
;
Kimani et al. 2019
;
Williams et al. 2018
;
Grover et al. 2020
)
, where the agent’s role was more reactive to user input and limited because of the static, fixed dialogue structures.
In contrast, with the advancement in AI, LLMs exhibit better conversational intelligence
(
Li et al. 2025
;
Sorin et al. 2024
)
, persuasion
(
Karinshak et al. 2023
;
Salvi et al. 2025
)
, and novelty in interactions
(
Rayan et al. 2025
)
.
Building on these capabilities, researchers have begun exploring AI in a coaching role
(
Wilhelm et al. 2025a
;
Arakawa and Yakura 2024
;
Jörke et al. 2025
;
Shin et al. 2025
)
—not only providing information or reminders but scaffolding users’ planning and reflection through prompts, suggestions, and feedback.

Despite this growing body of work, we still know little about how users behave in dialogue with a proactive, LLM-based coach that suggests different productivity or well-being interventions as part of users’ daily planning and self-reflection.
Most studies have focused on outcomes (e.g., productivity, stress reduction, reflection, perceived usefulness) or user perceptions, but have not closely analyzed conversational behaviors such as how people accept, appreciate, negotiate, or disengage from the agent’s suggestions.
Understanding these dynamics is particularly important now that LLM-based agents exhibit greater human-likeness and conversational intelligence
(
Rapp et al. 2025
;
Rayan et al. 2025
;
Sorin et al. 2024
)
.
Our work addresses this gap by examining in-situ conversation logs from a fourteen-day longitudinal study, focusing on the behaviors that emerge when users engage in daily planning and reflection with an LLM-based agent.

2.2.
Human-AI Interaction: Cooperation and Agent Sociality

An important aspect of successful human–AI interaction is cooperation: a mutual process in which both human and system work toward shared goals, supported by grounding and mutual understanding
(
Clark and Brennan 1991
)
.
In task-oriented scenarios, where users interact with AI systems to achieve a concrete outcome (e.g., processing a return with a customer service bot or booking a ticket with an airline virtual agent), cooperation is critical to reaching the goal of the interaction
(
Rapp et al. 2023
;
Laban and Araujo 2019
)
.
Prior work has shown that when conversational agents are perceived as cooperative, users consider their information richer, rate the interaction as higher quality, and report greater satisfaction
(
Laban and Araujo 2019
;
Hoffman and Breazeal 2004
)
.
To cooperate, people often adapt their own behavior: simplifying or reformulating messages, rephrasing to repair breakdowns, or even using gestures with robots to accommodate system constraints
(
Rapp et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2020
;
Myers et al. 2018
;
Porcheron et al. 2018
;
Pelikan and Broth 2016
)
.
However, this process is also challenging because chatbots often display limited intelligence while users bring expectations of human-like competence and conversational norms, leading to mismatches and frustration
(
Luger and Sellen 2016
;
Hill et al. 2015
)
.
Unsatisfactory responses such as turn-taking failures, hallucinations, incorrect information, or lack of flexibility undermine user trust and can ultimately reduce engagement
(
Gkinko and Elbanna 2023
;
Ashktorab et al. 2019
;
Akhtar et al. 2019
;
Dechant et al. 2025
)
.
While we know how cooperation is enacted in task-centric chatbots, we lack a clear account of how it unfolds in the conversational exchanges of an LLM coach that provides productivity and well-being advice.

Beyond cooperation, another thread of work examines how users perceive agents as social partners.
Because conversational interfaces mirror human dialogue
(
Chaves and Gerosa 2021
)
, people often respond to them with politeness, humor, or self-disclosure, consistent with the CASA paradigm
(
Nass et al. 1995
;
Purington et al. 2017
;
Liao et al. 2016
;
Seering et al. 2020
)
.
While other studies show users are reluctant to build bonds with AI agents because “
[they’re] a machine you can’t make an emotional connection with
”
(
Clark et al. 2019
)
(P106).
How people perceive the agent matters: users who see it as a social entity are more willing to sustain engagement and repair breakdowns, while others treat it as a tool and disengage after a single error
(
Gkinko and Elbanna 2023
;
Lee et al. 2010
)
.
Building on the CASA paradigm, researchers have studied how the anthropomorphism influences the experience of interaction
(
Liao et al. 2016
;
Croes and Antheunis 2021
)
.
While some studies show increased satisfaction
(
Purington et al. 2017
)
, other studies suggest that anthropomorphic design choices can frustrate or even unsettle people
(
Grover et al. 2020
;
Rapp et al. 2025
)
.
These tensions suggest that the perception of the agent directly shapes user behavior.

Our study extends this line of work by examining cooperation and sociality in a personal rather than task-oriented setting.
Unlike service chatbots that follow a request–response–confirmation template, PITCH involves users in relatively open-ended planning and reflection.
Its interactions are less about completing a transaction and more about scaffolding self-reflection, prompting users to accept, decline, or negotiate suggestions.
In this context, cooperation involves more than providing slot values—it includes sharing context, disagreeing or negotiating advice, and aligning conversations with one’s preferences.
We also observe how users treated the agent socially:
updating it on progress or providing additional context.
By analyzing these behaviors, we show how users manage cooperation with a coach-like agent and how their actions signal perceptions of the agent’s social presence.

2.3.
Designing Proactive Agents for Behavior Change

A longstanding debate in HCI concerns how much initiative should rest with users versus systems.
Shneiderman argued for direct manipulation interfaces where “users comprehend the display, feel in control, [and] the system is predictable” so they remain responsible for outcomes
(
Shneiderman and Maes 1997
)
.
Maes, by contrast, saw growing complexity as making delegation inevitable: software agents should be personalized, proactive, long-lived, and adaptive, taking initiative on behalf of overloaded users.
Framing these positions as a strict opposition has proven limiting
(
Shneiderman and Maes 1997
)
.
Horvitz later described this as a “false dichotomy” and advanced principles of mixed-initiative interaction, where automated services and direct manipulation are coupled
(
Horvitz 1999
)
.

Horvitz 1999
’s Lookout system, for example, anticipated calendar events from email but always allowed users to confirm or refine results.
Allen extended this view to dialogue, defining mixed-initiative as a flexible strategy in which initiative is dynamically negotiated: sometimes the system reports or clarifies, sometimes the user directs, and sometimes roles shift opportunistically
(
Allen et al. 1999
)
.

Building on this lineage, recent work has asked whether conversational agents should be reactive or proactive.
Reactive agents respond only when the user initiates, giving users more control but limiting the system’s role.
Proactive agents, by contrast, initiate interactions by offering reminders, or suggesting actions
(
Liu et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2025
;
Baraglia et al. 2016
)
.
Studies show that proactivity can introduce new topics
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
, provide richer suggestions
(
Peng et al. 2019
)
, and persuade users toward a target behavior
(
Lei et al. 2022
)
.

Chaves and Gerosa 2021
describes proactivity as sharing initiative with the user, where the agent may start exchanges, propose topics, or offer follow-up questions.
Researchers have also mapped proactivity on a spectrum to show how much initiative rests with the user versus the system.

Isbell and Pierce 2005
outlines an Interface-Proactivity continuum that ranges from the user doing everything on their own to the system acting on their behalf, with several mixed approaches in between where the system may highlight information or suggest options.
Recent efforts to push AI toward agentic behavior, such as the release of the ChatGPT agent
(
OpenAI 2025
)
, illustrate the far end of this spectrum where the system can act on the user’s behalf.
Yet, highly proactive designs can also be perceived as intrusive, disruptive, or controlling
(
Liao et al. 2016
;
Peng et al. 2019
;
Tallyn et al. 2018
)
.
With the rise of LLM-powered agents, the model responses can exhibit agency in unpredictable ways that produce negative emotional effects toward the technology
(
Rapp et al. 2025
)
.

Across these strands of work, the challenge is balancing initiative in ways that adapt to users’ evolving needs.
Proactivity can keep conversations goal-oriented
(
Lei et al. 2022
)
, but it can also constrain flexibility when users’ contexts shift.
Our study extends this literature by examining how users engage with an LLM-based coach that proactively decides planning and reflection goals, highlighting how proactivity can be helpful and when it can go out of context.

Method: Field Deployment Study

In October 2024, we conducted a two-week (14 weekdays) deployment study to investigate how users interact with PITCH, which initiates morning conversations to help externalize their plans and evening conversations to support self-reflection.
The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the researcher’s university approved the procedure of our study, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.
We organized the study procedure into three main phases: an onboarding session, a field deployment, and a post-study interview session.

3.1.
PITCH development

We implemented PITCH as a Slackbot that initiated check-ins with users at their chosen times in the morning and evening.
Morning sessions were designed to help users externalize concrete plans for the day, while evening sessions prompted reflection on those plans by drawing directly on the morning conversation history. This context preservation allowed PITCH to ask targeted follow-up questions.

To keep interactions focused, each conversation was oriented around a goal.
Prior work shows that goal-based dialogue improves adherence in behavior change settings
(
Bickmore and Picard 2005
;
Kocielnik et al. 2018b
;
Grant et al. 2009
;
Mitchell et al. 2022
)
.
In our deployment, the participants used two versions of the system per week in random order
The
fixed
-goal version used a consistent goal (“help workers structure their day to enhance productivity and well-being”), while the
rotation
version cycled through a set of different daily goals drawn from planning and well-being literature (e.g., prioritization, mindfulness, work–life balance).
This rotation aimed to introduce conversational diversity and reduce wear-out effects.

PITCH was implemented with the Slackbolt framework and OpenAI’s GPT-4 API.
A scheduling module delivered prompts at user-specified times, and MongoDB logged conversations and goals.
Further technical details are described in
(
redacted for anonymity [n. d.]
)
.

).

.

3.2.
Study Procedure

We conducted onboarding sessions in person or over Zoom to introduce participants to PITCH.
During onboarding, we demonstrated the Slack-based chatbot, explained the study procedure, and provided written instructions.
Participants were invited to a dedicated Slack workspace and asked to complete a pre-study demographic form (age, gender, race/ethnicity, occupation, daily activities, and time spent).
Following onboarding, participants began a two-week field deployment.
Each day, PITCH initiated check-ins at user-specified times in the morning and evening.
Participants engaged with these prompts and completed a brief daily survey.
All interactions with PITCH were conducted in English.
We collected all conversation interactions between participants and PITCH during the study.
At the end of the two-week study, we did a semi-structured debriefing interview.
Participants received $50 as compensation for their participation.

3.4.
Data Analysis

3.4.1.
Thematic Analysis

We analyzed the conversation logs collected during the field study using thematic analysis
(
Clarke et al. 2015
)
.
The first author conducted an initial round of open coding, noting user and agent behaviors (e.g., “user shared a ranked task list” or “agent suggested user to break down their task”).
These codes were transferred onto a shared Miro board
(
Miro 2025
)
and collaboratively reviewed in a team session, where four researchers grouped codes into preliminary themes and merged overlapping ones.
Drawing on this shared framework, the first author coded the remaining dataset while iteratively refining the codes.
Once the full dataset was coded, the team held additional sessions to review the coding, map codes to themes, and refine the thematic structure through discussion.
This iterative, collaborative process ensured that the final themes captured recurring interactive behaviors across the dataset.

3.4.2.
User Behavior Dialogue Act Analysis

While the thematic analysis allowed us to understand broad recurring patterns across conversations, we also wanted to examine user behaviors at a finer granularity.
To do so, we conducted a dialogue act analysis, drawing inspiration from established taxonomies in conversational research
(
Montenegro et al. 2019
;
Wei et al. 2024
;
Traum 2000
)
.
A dialogue act is the function or purpose behind an utterance in a conversation.
It goes beyond the literal words to capture what the speaker is trying to do with their turn.
From our thematic codes, we inductively derived a set of 17 behavior categories tailored to this dataset.
To establish these categories, two researchers independently coded 12 days of conversation.
Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, which led to clearer boundaries between categories, resulting in a coding framework with detailed definitions.
Table
3
presents the coding framework used for the annotation process.
The first author then applied the finalized scheme to the remaining data.

Note that one response can be labeled under multiple codes to capture overlapping dialogue acts.
For instance, for a user’s response of “
Quick meditation is a great idea. I will try today
”, we coded both
Commend
and
Comply
: the user gave a positive mark on PITCH’s advice while also taking the suggestion.

We analyzed the proportion of each dialog act code using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) in R (package
lme4
with
lmerTest
for significance testing). For each dialog act type, the dependent variable was the proportion of utterances labeled with that act.
The models included Session (Morning vs. Evening), Condition (two chatbots: Fixed vs. Rotation), and Day index (scaled to -1 to 1) as fixed effects. Participant was included as a random intercept to account for repeated measures. Day was scaled to ensure comparability and aid model convergence. 4.
Results

4.1.
Descriptive Statistics

Across 336 conversation sessions (14 days, with two daily check-ins), participants and the system exchanged a total of 3,181 turns (1,423 user turns; 1,758 system turns). On average, a conversation lasted 9.47 turns (
\sigma
= 5.6), though some stretched beyond 30 turns.
Participant responses were typically concise (8.71 words on average), while system turns were longer (23.65 words on average).

To better understand how participants engaged with the system, we annotated each user turn with a behavior category (Table
3
).
The turn ratio for each category was calculated by summing the proportion of turns tagged with that category within each morning or evening conversation and dividing by the total number of conversations (336).
The most common behaviors were Plan (23.52%), where users outlined upcoming actions, and Report (21.33%), where they described progress or outcomes.
Other frequent moves included Ignore (15.11%), Clarify/Contextualize (14.28%), Self-reflect (12.21%), and Comply (10.74%).

4.2.
RQ1: Engagement Patterns in Daily Planning and Reflection Behaviors in Conversations with PITCH

To address RQ1, we examined how users engaged with a proactive agent for daily planning and reflection. The participants exhibited cooperative behaviors by taking suggestions, negotiating, or grounding their mutual understanding.

4.2.1.
Taking PITCH’s Suggestions and Expressing Helpfulness

Participants (10/12) complied with PITCH’s suggestions and adopted them during their day.
PITCH checked in with users each morning and evening, proposing concrete actions (e.g., How about taking 5-minute micro-breaks?)
When a suggestion resonated, users often remarked that it was “
helpful
” (P3) or “
a good question
” (P4).

Comply
behaviors averaged 10.74% across conversations, while related
Commend
behaviors averaged 1.26% (Table
4
), underscoring that acceptance was sometimes paired with explicit praise.
The following examples illustrate how users demonstrated a cooperative stance, either by negotiating the advice offered by PITCH and later appreciating their suggestion in the evening (P1) or by accepting it readily (P3).

These examples highlight users’ cooperative and open attitudes toward a CA, treating the agent as a coach or companion capable of offering concrete, actionable advice in daily life.

4.2.3.

Participants frequently shared personal context or clarified constraints.

Clarify/Contextualize
was one of the more common dialogue acts (14.28%), reflecting that participants explained their situation to PITCH, especially when its suggestions were not feasible for them.
Many participants (7/12) provided such external constraints or personal details before refusing the suggestion rather than dismissing the advice.
39% of
Refuse
responses were also coded as
Clarify/Contextualize
, which suggests that they account for a reason why they had to turn down their suggestions.
Here are some of the conversation examples that show such behaviors of how participants chose to explain their personal context to decline PITCH’s suggestion.

In these examples, PITCH’s prompts were reasonable in general, but the participants’ situations made them impractical.
Instead of ignoring or simply refusing PITCH’s suggestions, participants cooperated by explaining their context as to why they could not take the suggestion.
The result suggests that the conversation with PITCH may reveal various personal and contextual information that can be later useful for the agent to remember and consider, if they not readily available from other systems (e.g., calendar).

When PITCH’s assumptions or strategies were not aligned with participants’ personal productivity habits, they voiced their disagreement to align PITCH with their own practices.
Although less frequent overall (3.0%),
Refuse
behaviors were exhibited by nearly all participants (11/12) atleast once, showing that disagreement was a shared and expected part of using the system.
As a productivity coach, PITCH often encouraged strategies such as writing to-do lists, breaking tasks down, scheduling them with times, or incorporating breaks.
However, in some cases, participants resisted this approach, arguing that it conflicted with their preferred working style:

Rather than disengaging and leaving the chat, they actively voiced disagreement to communicate their own preferences, such as avoiding breaks or resisting time-slotting.
This negotiation can be understood as a form of grounding: participants took the initiative to ensure the agent had the correct understanding of their preferences.
In this sense, cooperation did not only involve acceptance of suggestions but also the expression of disagreement so that both parties shared an understanding of what strategies were meaningful.
In our implementation, PITCH did not have long-term memory beyond a single day to retain this information; therefore, there was a missed opportunity for greater personalization and context awareness.
Overall, the corrective behaviors participants displayed when PITCH did not provide ideal suggestions demonstrate that they perceived PITCH as a conversational partner—one with whom they were willing to share their situation and engage on equal footing.

One interesting behavior we noticed was using the conversation as a to-do list and beyond.
Most participants (11/12) listed their tasks when prompted by PITCH to externalize their plan in the morning session.
Quantitatively,
Plan
was the most frequent behavior (23.52%) across the dataset, suggesting that participants frequently externalized their day through PITCH.

Plan

\beta=0.39

4.2.5.
Participants’ Uncooperative Behaviors

While many participants engaged cooperatively with PITCH, we also observed instances of uncooperative behavior.
Most participants showed these behaviors at least once: 10 of 12 did not fully answer PITCH’s questions, and 9 of 12 ended the conversation abruptly without replying.
Out of 336 total conversations, 109 (32.4%) sessions concluded with a participant leaving the last message unanswered.
In our coding scheme, such cases were labeled as
Ignore
, which averaged 15.11% across conversations.
Other forms of disengagement included
Not Answered
(4.12%, where participants skipped answering a question that PITCH asked or gave an irrelevant answer) and
Not Interested
(3.35%, where participants gave minimal or vague replies).
