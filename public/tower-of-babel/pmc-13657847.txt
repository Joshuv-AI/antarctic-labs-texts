Glaucocalyxin A sensitizes ovarian cancer cells to PARP inhibitors by inducing DNA damage
Authors: Lingling Zhuang, Lanyan Hu, Qi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPoly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) have demonstrated therapeutic advantages in ovarian cancer (OC) patients with homologous recombination (HR) deficiency. However, their effectiveness is limited in HR-proficient or acquired resistance tumors. Glaucocalyxin A (GLA) is an ent-kauranoid diterpene with anti-tumor properties. This study aimed to investigate the role and underlying mechanisms of GLA in OC and its potential as a sensitizing agent for PARPi.MethodsThe anti-proliferative effect of GLA on OC cells was evaluated both in vitro and in vivo. RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed to identify GLA-regulated pathways. DNA damage and cell cycle progression were assessed by immunofluorescence, neutral comet assay, and flow cytometry. The expression and activation of key proteins in the DNA damage response (DDR), HR, and Fanconi anemia (FA) pathways were analyzed by western blotting and immunofluorescence. The synergistic effect of GLA combined with the PARPi talazoparib was evaluated in vitro and in SKOV3 xenografts.ResultsGLA significantly inhibited OC cell proliferation in vitro and in vivo. RNA-seq analysis revealed that GLA induced G2/M cell cycle arrest and DNA damage. Mechanistically, GLA activated the ATM-Chk2 pathway while suppressing the ATR-Chk1 axis within the DDR. Furthermore, GLA inhibited HR and FA pathways, leading to DNA repair deficiency. Notably, a sequential regimen of GLA followed by talazoparib markedly increased drug responsiveness and DNA damage levels, and synergistically enhanced the anti-tumor effect of PARPi in SKOV3 xenografts.ConclusionsThis study identifies GLA as a potential PARPi-sensitizing agent and provides a preclinical rationale for sequential combination therapy to broaden the therapeutic utility of PARPi in HR-proficient OC.Keywords: Ovarian cancer (OC), glaucocalyxin A (GLA), poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARP inhibitors), DNA damage, combination therapy

Highlight box

Key findings

• Glaucocalyxin A (GLA) induces DNA damage and impairs both homologous recombination (HR) and Fanconi anemia (FA) DNA repair pathways.

• GLA exerts a differential effect on the DNA damage response (DDR) by activating the ATM-Chk2 axis while suppressing the ATR-Chk1 axis.

• Sequential combination of GLA with talazoparib synergistically enhances anti-tumor efficacy in SKOV3 xenografts by increasing cellular drug responsiveness and DNA damage.

What is known and what is new?

• Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) are effective in OC patients with HR deficiency, but their efficacy is limited in HR-proficient tumors and in cases of acquired resistance. GLA is an ent-kauranoid diterpene with reported anti-tumor properties in various cancers, but its role in OC and its effect on DNA repair pathways were previously unknown.

• This study identifies a novel biological activity of GLA as a PARPi sensitizer. It reveals that GLA induces G2/M arrest and DNA damage while simultaneously inhibiting HR and FA repair pathway. It also demonstrates that sequential administration of GLA and talazoparib is rational for synergistic anti-tumor efficacy.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The findings provide a preclinical rationale for sequential combination of GLA with PARPi in HR-proficient ovarian cancer. Further research is needed using PARPi-resistant models, patient-derived tumor xenograft (PDX) model and comprehensive toxicological assessment. The identification of direct molecular target of GLA may make future biomarker-driven patient stratification possible.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is a fatal gynecological malignancy often diagnosed at advanced stages, owing to the lack of reliable early screening techniques (1). Over the past two decades, the conventional approach to OC treatment has consisted of surgical cytoreduction followed by platinum/taxane-based cytotoxic chemotherapy (2). Newly diagnosed patients commonly show sensitivity to standard therapies; however, most OC patients eventually develop resistance to treatment, leading to treatment failure (3). Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) have revolutionized OC treatment (4). PARPi have shown synthetic lethality with BRCA1/2 mutations that cause defects in the homologous recombination (HR) pathway. This treatment is approved for various types of cancers, including ovarian, breast, prostate, and pancreatic cancers (5,6). However, the efficacy of PARPi is mainly limited to patients with HR deficiency, and HR-proficient and acquired drug resistance remains a clinical obstacle. Mechanisms of PARPi resistance include HR repair restoration through reversion or secondary mutations in HR repair genes and other HR-independent mechanisms (7-9). Therefore, there is an urgent need for a combined strategy that can sensitize HR-proficient tumors to PARPi.

Natural products are used more frequently in the development of cancer therapeutic drugs because of their remarkable efficacy and safety (10). Glaucocalyxin A (GLA) is an ent-kauranoid diterpene extracted from Rabdosia japonica var, a traditional Chinese herb (11). GLA exhibits a range of biological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anticoagulant, and anti-tumor effects (12,13). For example, GLA induces G2/M cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in human bladder cancer cells (14), melanoma cells (15), and multiple myeloma cells (16) by modulating signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt, NF-κB/p65, and STAT3. Additionally, recent research has greatly expanded the understanding of the anti-tumor mechanism of GLA. These include targeting redox-regulating enzymes such as PRDX1 and TXNRD1 to induce ferroptosis (17), selectively inhibiting thioredoxin reductase to trigger oxidative stress-mediated apoptosis (18). Despite these findings in various cancers, the effects and underlying mechanisms of GLA on OC remain largely unexplored.

In preliminary experiments, we observed a potent inhibitory effect of GLA on a panel of OC cell lines. Moreover, RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis indicated that GLA treatment downregulated pathways related to DNA replication, HR, and Fanconi anemia (FA) repair. The FA pathway is crucial for DNA interstrand crosslinks repair, while HR is essential for double-strand break (DSB) repair, and these pathways exhibit functional crosstalk (19). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that GLA might induce DNA damage and impair DNA repair, thereby sensitizing HR-proficient OC cells to PARPi. This study aimed to investigate the role and mechanisms of GLA in OC and evaluate its potential as a sensitizing agent for PARPi. Our results demonstrate that GLA induces DNA damage and G2/M arrest, inhibits HR and FA repair pathways, and synergistically enhances the anti-tumor efficacy of PARPi when administered in a sequential regimen. These findings identify a novel role for GLA and provide preclinical evidence for further evaluation of its combination with PARPi in HR-proficient OC. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR and ARRIVE reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1260/rc).

Methods

Cells and cell culture

A2780, HOC7, and SKOV3 cell lines were gifted by Professor Mading’s laboratory at Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. The cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), RPMI 1640, and McCoy’s 5A medium (Basal Media, China), respectively. Cells were cultured in a medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sijiqing) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Solarbio) at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2.

Chemicals and antibodies

The GLA used in this study was obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Company (Shanghai, China). GLA (20 or 200 mg) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and stored at concentrations of 50 mM and 40 mg/mL, respectively. Talazoparib, sourced from MedChemExpress, was dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 10 mM. Mitomycin C (MMC) was got from GLPBIO. The compounds were stored at −80 ℃ after aliquoting.

Antibodies against P21 (ab109520), Cdc25C (ab32444), cyclin B1 (ab32053), Chk2 (ab109413), ATM (ab32420), p-ATM (ser1981) (ab81292), p-ATR (ser428) (ab178407), FANCA (ab201457), FANCD2 (ab108928), FANCI (ab245219), MRE11 (ab208020), CtIP (ab155988), RAD51 (ab133534), Ki-67 (ab92742) were purchased from Abcam plc (Cambridge, UK). Antibodies against BRCA1 (YT0519) was purchased from ImmunoWay Biotechnology Company (plano, TX, USA). Antibodies against p-Chk2 (Thr68) (2661T), Chk1 (2360S), p-Chk1 (ser345) (2348T), γH2AX (ser139) (2577S) were sourced from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA). Antibodies against GAPDH (60004-1-Ig), HRP-conjugated affinipure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H+L) (SA00001-2), HRP-conjugated affinipure goat anti-Mouse IgG(H+L) (SA00001-1), CoraLite594-conjugated goat anti-Rabbit IgG(H+L) (SA00013-4) were purchased from Proteintech Group, Inc. (Wuhan, China).

Cell viability and drug combination analyses

The cells were seeded in 96-well plates at an appropriate density and cultured for 12-24 h. The cells were then treated with different concentrations of GLA for 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. For drug combination analysis, cells were pretreated with DMSO or GLA at the specified concentrations for 48 h, followed by talazoparib for 96 h. Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (GlpBio, USA).

Colony formation assay

A2780, HOC7, and SKOV3 cells were cultured in 6-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells/well for 24 h. The cells were continuously treated with different concentrations of GLA for 10–12 days. For the drug combination analysis, the three cell lines were treated with the specified drugs for 12 days. In sequential therapy, the cells were treated with GLA for 6 days, followed by talazoparib for another 6 days. After treatment, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.2% crystal violet for 20 min. The plates were photographed, and the colony numbers were quantified using ImageJ software.

RNA sequencing

The A2780 cells were treated with DMSO or 5 μM GLA for 48 h. After treatment, cells were collected, and RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA quality was evaluated using a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent) and quantified using ND-2000 (NanoDrop Technologies). A library was established using the TruSeqTM RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with 1 μg total RNA. The mRNA levels were assessed using TopHat v2.0.9. RNA-seq data were analyzed using the Majorbio Cloud Platform (www.majorbio.com). The DESeq2 algorithm was used for the differential expression analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were filtered using the criteria |log2(fold change)| >1 and P.adj <0.05. The KEGG pathway analysis was performed using the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/), while the GSEA analysis was conducted using the R package ‘clusterProfiler’.

Cell cycle analysis

The three OC cell lines were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1×106 cells per well. After culturing for 12–24 h, cells were incubated with different concentrations of GLA for 48 h. Afterwards, the cells were collected and fixed with 70% pre-cooled ethanol overnight at −20 ℃. The cells were centrifuged and washed twice with PBS. Next, 500 μL pre-chilled PBS was added to resuspend the fixed cells. 20 μL of RNase A solution was added to the cells and incubated at 37 ℃ for 30 min. The cells were resuspended in 400 μL of Propidium Iodide and stained at 4 ℃ in the dark for 30–60 min. The cell cycles were assessed within 24 h using flow cytometry and the data was analyzed by the FlowJo software.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA was isolated from A2780 cells using the TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using 1 μg total RNA and PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TaKaRa Biotechnology, Otsu, Japan). qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Premix EsTaq (Mei5 Biotechnology, Beijing, China) in a real-time PCR detection system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The primer sequences used in this study were as follows: GAPDH forward, 5'-GTCAAGCTGAGAACGGGAA-3’; GAPDH reverse, 5'-AAATGAGCCCCAGCCTTCTC-3’; FANCI forward, 5'-GCAAGCTGATGTTCGACTCATGC-3'; FANCI reverse, 5'-AGGCAGCAGATCAGGTTTTGGC-3'; FANCD2 forward, 5'-TTCCAGGATGCCTTCGTAGTGG-3'; FANCD2 reverse, 5'-GCAGGAGGTTTATGGCAATCCC-3'; RAD51 forward, 5'-TCTCTGGCAGTGATGTCCTGGA-3'; RAD51 reverse, 5'-TAAAGGGCGGTGGCACTGTCTA-3'; BRCA1 forward, 5'-CTGAAGACTGCTCAGGGCTATC-3'; BRCA1 reverse, 5'-AGGGTAGCTGTTAGAAGGCTGG-3'; CDK1 forward, 5'-GGAAACCAGGAAGCCTAGCATC-3'; CDK1 reverse, 5'-GGATGATTCAGTGCCATTTTGCC-3'; p21 forward, 5'-AGGTGGACCTGGAGACTCTCAG-3'; p21 reverse, 5'-TCCTCTTGGAGAAGATCAGCCG-3'.

Western blotting

Proteins from OC cells were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). After blocking for 1 h in 5% non-fat milk, the transferred membranes were incubated with primary and corresponding secondary antibodies, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The membranes were visualized using an Imaging System.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates at an appropriate density and allowed to adhere overnight. For HR repair analysis, cells were pretreated with DMSO or GLA for 48 h. The medium was then replaced with pre-warmed PBS, and cells were exposed to ultraviolet C (UVC) radiation (254 nm, 100 J/m²) in a UV crosslinker (XEPU, China) with the coverslip lids removed. Cells were incubated for another 6 h. For FA repair analysis, cells were treated with DMSO, MMC (1 μM, 24 h) or GLA combination with MMC. Following fixation, permeabilization, and blocking, cells were incubated with γH2AX antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1,000), RAD51 (Abcam, 1:200) and FANCD2 (Abcam, 1:300) at 4 ℃ overnight. The cells were then washed with PBS and probed with fluorescent secondary antibodies at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. Finally, the cells were stained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min, and captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Neutral comet assays

DMSO was added to the Lysis Solution (cat. # 4250-050-01) at a 1:10 (v/v) ratio and then cooled at 4 ℃ for at least 20 min. The LMAgarose comet was heated to 90–100 ℃ and then cooled to 37 ℃ to prevent heat shock. The three OC cell lines were treated with DMSO or 5 μM GLA for 48 h. After treatment, the cells were rinsed twice with PBS. Then, 250 cells/μL were mixed with LMAgarose at a 1:10 (v/v) ratio and 50 µl of the mixture was immediately pipetted onto CometSlide™ (cat #4250-050-03). The comet slides were covered with glass slides and stored in the dark at 4 ℃ for 30 min. Next, the cover slides were carefully moved aside and immersed in Lysis Solution at 4 ℃ overnight and in Neutral Electrophoresis Buffer for 30 min. Finally, the comet slides were electrophoresed at 21 V for 45 min and stained with Propidium Iodide for 10 min. The images were captured using a fluorescence microscope and analyzed using CASP software.

Mouse xenograft experiments

Animal experiments procedures were performed under a project license (approval number: RYE2025011501) granted by the Animal Research Committee of Nanchang Royo Biotech Co., Ltd., in compliance with the institutional guidelines for the care and use of animals. A protocol was prepared before the study without registration. Five-to-six weeks old female BALB/c nude mice were obtained from Zhejiang Weitong Lihua Co. Ltd. A2780 and SKOV3 xenografts were generated via the subcutaneous injection of cells at densities of 6×106 and1×107 cells/mouse, respectively. The tumor volume was measured by an investigator blinded to group assignment. When their tumor volume reached 50–100 mm3, the nude mice were randomly assigned to control or treatment groups using a computer-generated random number sequence or a random number table, with six nude mice per group. The selection of sample size was based on common practice similar to xenograft studies, providing sufficient capacity to detect significant therapeutic effects while minimizing animal use. The number of animals remained consistent throughout the research. Tumor volume and body weight were measured every alternate day. Tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: tumor volume = (length × width2) × 0.52. At the experimental endpoint, mice were anaesthetized followed by cervical dislocation. Once animals reached the predefined endpoint criteria (e.g., tumor volume >1,500 mm3, body weight loss >20%, or showing severe morbidity), they were euthanized immediately.

For the A2780 in vivo study, mice were randomly assigned to control group (saline containing 6% DMSO, 40% PEG300, and 5% Tween-80) and GLA group (20 mg/kg). The treatments were administered intraperitoneally twice every three days for a total of 18 days.

For sequential therapy in vivo studies with SKOV3 xenografts, animals were randomly divided into four groups: vehicle (saline containing 6% DMSO, 40% PEG300, and 5% Tween-80), GLA (20 mg/kg), talazoparib (0.033 mg/kg), and combination (GLA administered for 6 days followed by talazoparib for 7 days). The total duration of drug intervention was 46 days.

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry

Tumor tissues and tissues from the heart, liver, and kidneys of nude mice were fixed with 4% formalin, followed by paraffin embedding and serial sectioning into 3.5 μm thick slices. H&E staining was performed after dewaxing and dehydration. The slides were subsequently dehydrated and made transparent. For immunohistochemistry, slides underwent dewaxing, dehydration, epitope retrieval, and blocking steps. The slides were incubated with Ki-67 and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) antibodies at 4 ℃ overnight. Next, secondary antibodies were added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The results were visualized and scanned using a section scanner.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and each experiment was repeated at least three times. This study used statistical tests such as Student’s t-test and one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to analyze the data. The threshold for statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

GLA reduces A2780 cell proliferation in vivo

We next investigated the anti-tumor efficacy of GLA in vivo by constructing subcutaneous xenograft tumor models. GLA administration led to a significant reduction in tumor growth compared to the control group (Figure 2A,2B). A significant difference in tumor weight was observed between two groups (Figure 2C). In contrast, tumor tissues from the GLA administration group showed significantly reduced staining for Ki-67 and PCNA (Figure 2D,2E). Additionally, no significant differences were found in body weight (Figure 2F) and H&E staining of the heart, liver, and kidney tissues (Figure 2G) compared to the control group. These findings demonstrated the efficacy of GLA treatment in suppressing OC cell proliferation in vivo. Meanwhile, the administered dose was well-tolerated over the treatment period.

GLA treatment downregulates genes related to the G2/M checkpoint and DNA repair

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the inhibitory effects of GLA, we performed RNA sequencing on A2780 cells. A total of 1,103 upregulated and 657 downregulated DEGs were identified (Figure 3A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs were significantly involved in pathways related to DNA replication, p53 signaling, cell cycle, FA, and HR (Figure 3B), suggesting that GLA may exert antitumor effects by regulating cell cycle and DNA repair. Next, we conducted GSEA to determine the specific effects of GLA on the above pathways. The results revealed that GLA treatment led to a significant decrease in gene signatures associated with the ‘G2/M checkpoint’ and ‘DNA repair’ (Figure 3C). A heatmap illustrating the downregulation of representative genes within the DNA repair signature is shown in Figure 3D. We confirmed that GLA treatment significantly decreased the mRNA expression of key genes involved in G2/M transition (CDK1), HR (BRCA1, RAD51), and FA repair (FANCD2, FANCI), while increasing expression of the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor CDKN1A (p21) (Figure 3E).

GLA causes G2/M phase arrest and DNA damage in OC cells

We evaluated the effects of GLA on cell cycle progression in OC cells using flow cytometry analysis to investigate the protein levels of G2/M transition-related genes. The results revealed that GLA treatment increased the transition of OC cells from G1 and S phases to the G2 phase (Figure 4A and Figure S1A). Consistent with attenuated cell cycle progression, GLA upregulated p21 expression, a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor, and decreased the expression of Cdc25C, CDK1, and Cyclin B1 in a time- and dose-dependent manner in A2780 and HOC7 cells (Figures S1B,S1C,S2). In SKOV3 cells, the protein expression, except for cyclin B1, showed a trend similar to that of A2780 and HOC7 cells (Figure S1B).

G2/M arrest is accompanied by DNA damage (20). Investigation of the effect of GLA on DNA DSBs in three cell lines revealed a significant increase in the percentage of γH2AX positive cells and tail DNA (Figure 4B-4H), confirming that GLA treatment could induce DNA damage in OC cells.

GLA inhibits the HR and FA repair pathways

The DNA damage response (DDR) pathway is crucial for detecting DNA damage and regulating DNA repair (21). ATM and ATR kinases are two master regulators of the DDR pathway that are responsible for the phosphorylation of Chk2 and Chk1, respectively (22). We therefore examined the effect of GLA on DDR signaling. GLA treatment resulted in a dose-dependent increase in γH2AX levels in OC cells (Figure 5A), which was consistent with the results of immunofluorescence staining and the comet assay. Intriguingly, while GLA activated the ATM-Chk2 pathway, as evidenced by increased phosphorylation of ATM (Ser1981) and Chk2 (Thr68), it suppressed the ATR-Chk1 pathway, with decreased phosphorylation of ATR (Ser428) and Chk1 (Ser345) observed in A2780 and HOC7 cells (Figure 5A, Figure S3).

GLA inhibits the HR and FA pathways. WB analysis of the DDR pathway (A), HR, and FA pathways (B) in three cell lines treated with GLA for 48 h. Full-length blots are presented in Figures S3,S4. DDR, DNA damage response; FA, Fanconi anemia; GLA, glaucocalyxin A; HR, homologous recombination; WB, Western blotting.

The ATR-Chk1 pathway plays a critical role in DNA damage repair (23). Specifically, ATR regulates interstrand cross-linking repair by targeting the FA pathway proteins FANCD2 and FANCI (24). ATR-dependent FANCD2 phosphorylation promotes mono-ubiquitination and enhances its localization to DNA injury lesions (25). In addition, the HR pathway is regulated by ATR and Chk1. ATR responds to DNA damage or replication stalling by directly phosphorylating BRCA1 (26). Consistent with our RNA-seq data and observed ATR-Chk1 suppression, FANCA, FANCI, FANCD2, BRCA1, RAD51, and CtIP were markedly decreased by GLA in all three OC cell lines (Figure 5B, Figure S4). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that GLA treatment attenuated the UVC-induced increase in nuclear RAD51 and MMC-induced accumulation of nuclear FANCD2 fluorescence intensity (Figure S5). These findings indicate that GLA treatment effectively impairs both HR and FA-mediated DNA repair.

GLA improves OC cell sensitivity to PARP inhibitors in vitro and in vivo

PARPi causes synthetic lethality in individuals with BRCA1/2 defects or other HR deficiencies (27). Given the inhibitory effects of GLA on the HR and FA pathways, we speculated that the combination of GLA and PARPi may exert potential synergistic effects. First, OC cells were treated with talazoparib and GLA simultaneously. However, we found that varying the GLA concentration did not significantly enhance the sensitivity of SKOV3 cells to talazoparib (Figure S6). In our preliminary experiments, we observed no significant changes in cell cycle distribution or DNA damage after GLA treatment for 24 or 36 h, suggesting the importance of treatment duration. Therefore, a sequential scheme was used (Figure 6A), and the results indicated a significant increase in cellular response when GLA and talazoparib were administered sequentially (Figure 6B). The interaction term was statistically significant in all three cell lines (P<0.001), and Šidák’s post-hoc comparisons confirmed that GLA significantly enhanced the inhibitory effect of talazoparib at most concentration tested (adjusted P<0.01 or P<0.001). Detailed statistical results are shown in Table S1. In addition, combination therapy significantly reduced the number and size of the colonies (Figure 6C,6D) and increased the accumulation of DNA damage, as indicated by γH2AX protein levels (Figure 6E, Figure S7). Subsequently, we assessed the effects of sequential combination therapy in vivo, and the schematic is shown in Figure 6F. Sequential combination therapy remarkably mitigated the growth of tumors in SKOV3 xenografts compared to monotherapy with GLA or talazoparib (Figure 6G-6I). Moreover, there were no significant changes in body weight (Figure S8A) or in the histological analysis of the heart and liver using H&E staining, indicating that it did not cause toxicity in major organs (Figure S8B).

Discussion

Combining PARPi with other HR-inhibiting drugs shows promise in expanding their use beyond HR deficiency and enhancing the duration of response to PARPi (28,29). Our results indicated that GLA effectively inhibited the proliferation of OC cells in vitro and in vivo. GLA treatment induced G2/M cell cycle arrest, DNA damage, and inhibited the HR and FA repair pathways. Additionally, our study demonstrated that the sequential combination of GLA and PARPi exhibited a synergistic effect on OC cells and increased the anti-tumor effects of PARP inhibitors in vivo. Collectively, we disclose a new molecular mechanism of GLA and provide a rational combinatorial strategy in HR-proficient OC.

Previous studies have reported that GLA exerts strong antitumor effects in various tumor models (12). GLA was found to inhibit the proliferation of the three OC cell lines, as evidenced by CCK-8 and colony formation assays. This inhibitory effect was validated in an A2780 xenograft model. Subsequent RNA-seq-based analysis revealed significant downregulation of ‘G2/M checkpoint’ and ‘DNA repair’ gene signatures following GLA treatment. Therefore, we subsequently confirmed the effects of GLA on the cell cycle and DNA damage in the three OC cell lines using appropriate methods. Similar to the findings in other types of cancers (14,15), GLA triggered cell cycle arrest in the G2/M phase in OC cells. Additionally, this study explored the effect of GLA on DNA damage for the first time, revealing a novel molecular mechanism associated with GLA.

DDR signaling plays a crucial role in detecting DNA damage, regulating cell cycle progression, and DNA repair. In this study, GLA treatment increased ATM-Chk2 phosphorylation, but did not have the same effect on p-ATR and p-Chk1. ATM-Chk2 activation can prevent G2/M phase progression by influencing various substrates (30), which is consistent with our findings. The ATR-Chk1 pathway is crucial for DNA damage repair, especially the HR and FA pathways (23). Based on the existing literature and our RNA-Seq findings, we validated that GLA can inhibit the key regulators of the HR and FA pathways, including BRCA1, RAD51, FANCD2, and FANCI, in OC cells. This trend was consistent at both the mRNA and protein levels. These results suggest that GLA induces DNA damage and G2/M arrest, impairs the DDR pathway, and inhibits the HR and FA pathways in OC cells. This provides further insight into the anti-tumor activities of GLA.

PARPi drives synthetic lethality in individuals with BRCA1/2 defects or other HR deficiencies. Based on the above findings, we speculated that GLA could sensitize HR-proficient OC cells to PARPi. The lack of synergy with concurrent administration underscores the importance of treatment scheduling. We hypothesize that GLA pretreatment is necessary to establish a state of HR/FA deficiency and increase the accumulation of DNA damage, upon which PARP inhibition becomes synthetically lethal. The efficacy of this sequential combination in OC cell lines and the SKOV3 xenograft model, provide a preclinical proof for this strategy.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the direct molecular target(s) of GLA responsible for ATR-Chk1 suppression and HR/FA downregulation remain unidentified. Second, the precise mechanism by which GLA downregulates HR and FA proteins, whether at the transcriptional, post-transcriptional, or protein stability level, was not fully elucidated. Third, the rationale for the sequential procedure needs deeper exploration, potentially through detailed pharmacodynamic studies, more clinically relevant models, and PARPi-resistant models. Fourth, the concentrations used in this study were selected based on experimental feasibility and prior literature rather than human pharmacokinetic data; comprehensive pharmacokinetic evaluation will be required before clinical translation.

Conclusions

In summary, our results demonstrate that GLA induces DNA damage and impairs HR and FA repair pathways, thereby sensitizing HR-proficient OC cells to PARPi. This study discloses a new biological activity of GLA and provides evidence for sequential combination strategy to broaden the therapeutic utility of PARPi.
