Displaced minds: ecologies of depression
Authors: Aila Akosua Kattner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This issue of the Biomedical Journal explores emerging perspectives across neuropsychiatry, metabolism, aging, regenerative medicine, artificial intelligence, and chronic inflammatory disease. Featured contributions examine epigenetic and neuroendocrine mechanisms in depression, including the antidepressant-like effects of magnolol in chronic stress-induced models and the potential relevance of short-chain fatty acids as biomarkers and therapeutic targets in major depressive disorder. Additional reviews address evolutionary and metabolic dimensions of liver disease, artificial intelligence-assisted imaging approaches in lymphedema, immunological senotherapeutic strategies, developmental origins of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome, and the risks associated with short-term corticosteroid exposure. Original investigations further examine dopaminergic signaling during acute deprivation, molecular mechanisms of axonal regeneration, lipid peroxidation in pulmonary fibrosis, hypothalamic NPFFR2 signaling in metabolic dysfunction, neurobehavioral consequences of occlusal disharmony, and fall risk following cataract surgery with blue-light-filtering intraocular lenses. A correspondence exchange additionally discusses the diagnostic scope and limitations of elastase pretreatment in ALK fluorescence in situ hybridization analysis for non-small cell lung cancer.

Spotlight

As European settlers embarked on what they referred to as the age of discovery - although the “discovered” lands had long been inhabited by and known to Indigenous peoples1 - they carried with them plants to ensure enough food for survival. Over time, plants were also introduced to colonized lands for aesthetic, nostalgic, scientific, and economic purposes, thereby initiating an expanding global circulation of species. Displacing plants for practical purposes also reflected a broader European ambition, namely to tame the wild and assert dominion over nature. The philosophical foundations of this worldview are often linked to the French philosopher and scientist René Descartes, who argued that practical knowledge would render humans “masters and possessors of nature” [1]. Yet it was the English philosopher Francis Bacon who further developed this idea within the context of colonial expansion, promoting the notion that nature should be subdued through European imperial enterprise, and that it was both literally and figuratively expedient to plant the colony and to uproot and displant the native [2].

Even today, colonial and imperial nostalgia persist, linked to the declining power associated with economic and political hegemony, and with the loss of the colonial lifestyle [3]. The aftermath of colonialism and imperialism continues to shape the present through neoliberalism, racial capitalism, development policies, education, media, and global systems of exchange. Climate colonialism manifests through fossil fuel capitalism, extractive growth models, and hyper-consumptive lifestyles [4], but also through institutional structures and enduring systems of knowledge concerning land, and human relations with nature. The coloniality of nature emerged through interconnected mechanisms including the imposition of a dualist human-nature ontology, the degradation of life and territory through resource exploitation, and epistemic violence against Indigenous knowledge systems [5]. Nature itself became increasingly commodified [6], while the globalization of the world was structured around Eurocentric ideals, including the racial classification of populations [7].

Colonial expansion transformed not only ecosystems and landscapes, but also identities, cultural memory, and psychological experience. Colonial mentality refers to the internalized oppression and perceived ethnic and cultural inferiority experienced by peoples subjected to colonization. This mindset affects individuals on a transgenerational level [8].2 The systematic exercise of economic, political, and cultural violence contributed to the erosion of languages, traditions, and local knowledge across the world, while simultaneously reinforcing Eurocentric standards of beauty, value, and social legitimacy.3 Colonialism thus not only operated as a pervasive territorial or economic project, but moreover entailed ubiquitous psychological consequences [9].

Colonial mentality has been correlated with diminished individual and collective self-esteem, as well as increased anxiety and depression scores [10,11]. Colonial mentality has notably been associated with increased acculturative stress and a higher risk of depressive symptoms, prompting suggestions that it should be considered when conceptualizing depression [12].

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a complex and multifactorial condition shaped not only by genetic predisposition, but also by environmental and metabolic influences. Nutritional status, childhood adversity, and recent life stress have all been implicated in contributing to the risk of major depression [13]. The heritability estimate of MDD is approximately 30-40%, suggesting that factors beyond genetics substantially contribute to disease development. Increasing evidence additionally implicates epigenetic mechanisms in both the pathophysiology and treatment of MDD [14]. In particular, histone deacetylases (HDACs) have emerged as potential therapeutic targets, prompting interest in antidepressant strategies that modulate epigenetic regulation [15].

The large-scale exchange of plant species between the “Old” and “New” worlds intensified particularly during the 19th and 20th centuries. Plants were transported not only for food and fodder, but also for horticulture, medicine, science, and economic exploitation. Botanical gardens and so-called “acclimatization societies” were keen instruments of imperial exchange, contributing to the establishment of many of the “alien floras” that persist today [16].4 Among the species introduced from the Americas to Europe was Magnolia grandiflora, which arrived in France in1711,5 a tree whose bioactive compounds are now being investigated in the context of depression and neuropsychiatric disease.

Magnolol in depression

In this issue of the Biomedical Journal, Jiang et al. evaluate the antidepressant-like effects of magnolol, a bioactive compound derived from the Magnolia tree [17], and its influence on epigenetic regulation of synaptic plasticity in mice exposed to chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS). Conventional antidepressants primarily target monoaminergic signaling pathways, whereas increasing evidence implicates dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis as a central mechanism in depression. Prolonged stress exposure results in sustained HPA axis activation, elevated circulating glucocorticoids, and excessive glucocorticoid receptor (GR) signaling, collectively impairing neurogenesis, synaptic integrity, and neuronal plasticity. Magnolol treatment alleviated depressive-like behaviors and attenuated HPA axis hyperactivity in CUMS mice. Mechanistically, the authors identified modulation of histone acetylation and synaptic architecture as key components of its antidepressant-like action. Magnolol was further shown to interact with histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC2), a regulator elevated in the hippocampus under chronic stress conditions and associated with impaired synaptic plasticity and reduced neurogenesis. Metabolites of melancholy

Long before depression became framed through neurochemistry, epigenetics, or the gut-brain axis, emotional suffering was commonly understood as inseparable from bodily imbalance. Around 400 BC, the Greek physician and “father of medicine” Hippocrates proposed that persistent fear and distress reflected melancholia, a condition rooted not in the soul alone, but in bodily imbalance. According to humoral theory, excess black bile and phlegm altered the brain, darkening both mood and spirit [19]. Greek and later Roman physicians consequently approached melancholia as a physical disorder, prescribing dietary adjustments, exercise, bathing, and herbal remedies to restore equilibrium. Comparable mind-body treatment traditions emerged elsewhere across the globe. In South African traditional medicine, several plants long used for emotional suffering have since demonstrated antidepressant activity [20]. Likewise, a pre-Columbian healing program developed by indigenous Shipibo communities employing ayahuasca has been hypothesized to induce lasting improvements in psychological well-being, including effects on depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, and grief [21].

Le Do and colleagues examine major depressive disorder (MDD) through the modern framework of the gut-brain axis and neuroinflammation. Their meta-analysis of human and murine studies identified a significant and quantifiable deficit in circulating short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in individuals with MDD. The authors therefore propose that SCFAs may hold promise both as peripheral biomarkers as well as therapeutic targets within precision nutritional psychiatry [22].

Review articles

Ice age metabolism

Recent archaeogenetic studies suggest that a major genetic risk variant for fatty liver disease emerged more than 700,000 years ago. Today, under conditions of overnutrition, hepatic fat accumulation ranges from relatively benign steatosis - affecting up to 30% of contemporary populations - to progressive inflammatory steatohepatitis. Beyond dietary and environmental influences, susceptibility is strongly shaped by genetics. In particular, a common variant in the PNPLA3 gene has repeatedly been linked to an increased risk of steatotic liver disease. One proposed explanation for the remarkable persistence of this nowadays metabolically unfavorable variant is that it once conferred an evolutionary advantage. During the late Paleolithic period, and possibly especially under Neanderthal ice-age conditions, enhanced hepatic fat storage may have improved survival and fitness in cold climates and during periods of food scarcity. What was once adaptive may have become detrimental under modern nutritional conditions [23].6

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) likewise has a long history of association with human liver disease. Full HBV genomes have been reconstructed from Neolithic skeletons dating back approximately 5000 to 7000 years, representing the oldest viral genomes recovered to date [24,25].

Intriguingly, hepatic steatosis appears inversely associated with HBV activity. In patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), hepatic steatosis is associated with decreased HBV expression [26]. In their review, Huang and Kao focus on the underlying cellular mechanisms by which lipid accumulation transforms hepatocytes into unfavorable environments for HBV, involving immune modulation, metabolic reprogramming, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and impaired autophagy [27].

From imaging to intelligence

Data annotation forms an essential basis of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning. In supervised learning systems, annotated datasets enable algorithms to recognize complex patterns and generate predictions across applications ranging from autonomous driving to medical diagnostics and natural language processing. Yet the production of high-quality datasets remains constrained by practical and methodological limitations: Manual annotation is costly, time-consuming, and difficult to standardize at scale. Consistency poses an additional challenge, particularly in tasks requiring subjective interpretation. These limitations become even more apparent in specialized disciplines. Areas such as medical imaging or legal document analysis require annotators with extensive domain expertise, substantially increasing both complexity and expense. Human factors also contribute to variability: fatigue, cognitive bias, and differing interpretative frameworks can introduce systematic inconsistencies that ultimately affect dataset quality and model reliability [28,29].

Urooj et al. review established imaging modalities for lymphedema assessment, including indocyanine green lymphography (ICG-L), lymphoscintigraphy, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and computed tomography (CT), while also examining emerging AI-based approaches aimed at improving diagnostic accuracy and treatment planning. The authors likewise discuss current obstacles to clinical implementation, including data heterogeneity, limited interpretability, and regulatory constraints. The team outlines potential strategies for integration through federated learning, explainable AI, and standardized imaging protocols [30].

When cells refuse to retire

Cellular senescence is characterized by a near-irreversible arrest of the cell cycle, and acquisition of a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). Substantial alterations also take place in cell surface protein expression that shape the recognition of senescent cells (SnCs) by immune effectors. The persistence of SnCs contributes to chronic low-grade inflammation and promotes the development of age-associated disorders. Senotherapeutics have shown beneficial effects by reducing senescence-associated dysfunction, while lifestyle interventions including exercise, nutrition, phytochemicals, adequate sleep, and grounding have all been proposed to reduce inflammation and delay aging-related decline [31,32].

Importantly, senescence is not inherently pathological. In transient settings, it can support tissue homeostasis and stress adaptation. However, both excessive accumulation of senescent cells and their insufficient immune-mediated clearance have been implicated in pathological aging processes. A central challenge for immunological anti-senescence strategies therefore lies in selectively targeting pathogenic senescent cells while preserving physiologically beneficial, homeostatic senescence [33].

In their comprehensive review, Bi and Ji examine a broad spectrum of senotherapeutic approaches, including senolytics that eliminate SnCs, senomorphics that suppress SASP activity, immunotherapeutic strategies that enhance immune-mediated clearance, and restoration-oriented interventions designed to recover cellular function while maintaining cellular identity. The authors advocate pragmatic stratification frameworks for senescent states, integrated senescence-targeted therapeutic design, and a strong emphasis on safety, durability, and the minimization of unintended consequences [34].

Lasting consequences

Systemic corticosteroids remain among the most widely prescribed anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive drugs, particularly since the COVID-19 pandemic. While the complications of prolonged high-dose exposure are well established, accumulating evidence suggests that even short courses may not be as benign as previously assumed [35,36].

Commonly used to manage acute exacerbations of chronic inflammatory disease, allergic reactions, respiratory infections, and dermatologic conditions, short-term oral corticosteroids have become routine in everyday clinical practice. Tsai and Yao revisit the safety profile of these corticosteroid bursts and summarize growing evidence linking even brief treatment periods to serious adverse events. Reported complications include sepsis, pneumonia, gastrointestinal bleeding, acute pancreatitis, heart failure, venous thromboembolism, and fracture. Several of these risks emerged within weeks after treatment initiation, including in otherwise healthy populations. The authors emphasize that the widespread use of corticosteroids for self-limiting conditions may contribute to avoidable harm. Their review therefore calls for a more cautious and evidence-based prescribing culture, balancing the undeniable therapeutic benefits of corticosteroids against risks that may previously have been underestimated [37].

Early roots of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome (CKMS), formally recognized in 2023, describes the intertwined progression of cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and metabolic dysfunction. Affecting millions worldwide, CKMS has rapidly emerged as a major driver of premature mortality, with chronic inflammation increasingly identified as a central determinant of both disease development and prognosis [38,39]. Over the past two decades, prevalence patterns and temporal trends of CKMS have further revealed marked differences across age groups and between sexes [40].

Tain and Hsu turn attention to how susceptibility to CKMS may emerge long before clinical manifestation. The authors outline how CKMS, although typically manifesting in adulthood, may originate during critical periods of early-life programming. Their overview integrates a spectrum of developmental and environmental contributors, including oxidative stress, metabolic dysregulation, perinatal complications, epigenetic alterations, and maternal health factors. The review further delineates mechanistic pathways underlying CKMS, particularly through the lens of kidney programming and pediatric precision prevention strategies. Preventive recommendations focus on maternal health, the first 1000 days of life, early nutritional and lifestyle interventions, and pediatric screening for risk factors such as obesity and hypertension. By linking maternal and pediatric care within a unified preventive framework, the work highlights the possibility of interrupting CKMS trajectories before they become clinically entrenched, potentially reducing intergenerational disease burden [41].

Original articles

Hunger, thirst, and dopamine

In 2020, “dopamine fasting” emerged as a popular trend associated with Silicon Valley culture. The idea was to abstain from food and beverages, digital media and screen technology, while also minimizing or avoiding reading, talking, and sexual activity in an attempt to “reset” the brain's dopamine system. However, the concept of “fasting dopamine” by depleting or blocking its activity in the brain through behavioral restriction is misleading, since dopamine signaling cannot simply be switched off through avoidance of these activities.,78

Although framed as a modern neurobehavioral intervention, the broader idea of voluntary sensory and behavioral restraint is far from novel. Comparable concepts have existed for centuries in contemplative and spiritual traditions. In yogic philosophy, for example, brahmacharya refers to restraint from sensual indulgence considered incompatible with wisdom [42], while mauna emphasizes silence as a means of reducing mental activity [43]. The contemporary “dopamine fasting” concept was loosely inspired from principles related to cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), where limiting compulsive or distress-inducing behaviors may help reduce maladaptive reward-seeking patterns linked to dopaminergic signaling and addiction [44]. However, popular reinterpretations conflated this approach with forms of sensory deprivation or extreme asceticism, often detached from established neurobiological evidence.9

In Drosophila, dopaminergic neurons (DANs) innervating the mushroom body (MB) are essential for innate as well as learned thirst- and hunger-dependent behaviors. Lee and Chiang et al. investigated whether acute food or water deprivation alters dopamine production and neural activity in MB-innervating DANs. Interestingly, deprivation did not significantly alter dopamine production or neural activity in these neurons, suggesting that baseline dopaminergic activity in these circuits is largely independent of acute internal hunger or thirst states [45].

The long road back

For millennia, nerve injury was regarded as irreversible. The oldest surviving medical texts from the Babylonian and Egyptian periods, dating to around 1700 BC, make no mention of peripheral nerves or their repair. In Greco–Roman medicine, severed nerves were believed incapable of reunion or restoration, although later Ottoman-era medical writings suggest that Hippocrates or his pupils may already have attempted surgical treatment of nerve injuries [46].

Only over the past two centuries did evidence emerge that neurons possess at least limited capacities for repair and regeneration. A major turning point came with the work of Santiago Ramón y Cajal, Chair of Histology and Pathology at the University of Barcelona in 1888 - then considered among the most important medical departments in Spain. Cajal, building on staining techniques developed by the Italian histologist Camillo Golgi, systematically examined the microscopic architecture of the nervous system. Studying embryonic tissue of birds and small mammals, Cajal challenged the prevailing reticular theory. He proposed instead that the nervous system consists of individual cells linked through highly organized networks. His work laid the foundation for modern neurobiology, including concepts of neurogenesis and neural plasticity [47,48].

Experiences from the world wars moreover demonstrated that peripheral nerves can, under certain conditions, recover function. Crushed nerves generally regenerate more successfully than completely transected ones, and injuries closer to the target tissue often show better outcomes than proximal lesions.10 Yet peripheral nerve injuries (PNI) still frequently result in lasting sensory and motor deficits. Using simplified in vitro axotomy models based on dorsal root ganglia and spinal motor neuron aggregates, Fang et al. demonstrated that axotomy induces regeneration-associated genes and RhoA/ROCK signaling alongside collapse of growth cones. The ROCK inhibitor Y27632 partially restored growth cone morphology and enhanced axonal regeneration, particularly in injured neurons, underscoring the potential of these models for mechanistic studies and regenerative drug screening [49].

Oxidative echoes

Oxidative stress and chronic inflammation are increasingly recognized as central drivers of fibrotic lung disease [50]. Among the reactive byproducts generated during membrane lipid damage, 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) has emerged as a potent signaling molecule capable of amplifying inflammation, epithelial injury, and profibrotic remodeling. Using a bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis model, Liu et al. demonstrated marked upregulation of 4-HNE in fibrotic lung tissue together with increased collagen deposition and oxidative stress markers. Pharmacological inhibition of lipid peroxidation using ferrostatin-1 or deferoxamine reduced fibrosis severity, improved pulmonary function, and suppressed TGF-β signaling through the JNK/c-Jun pathway. The authors also confirmed elevated 4-HNE expression in human IPF tissue, thus supporting a potential translational relevance of lipid peroxidation-targeted interventions [51].

The weight of NPFFR2

Neuropeptide FF receptors 1 and 2 (NPFFR1 and NPFFR2) belong to the RF-amide peptide receptor family and are implicated in the regulation of nociception, opioid tolerance, and metabolic homeostasis [52]. Beyond their established role in pain modulation, NPFFR2 has also been linked to diet-induced thermogenesis and glucose regulation. The receptor is strongly activated by neuropeptides FF (NPFFs) [53] and accumulating evidence suggests that NPFF signaling may influence systemic glucose metabolism, raising interest in its potential relevance to obesity and type 2 diabetes [54].

Hsu et al. examined the metabolic consequences of hypothalamic NPFFR2 overexpression in mice exposed to diet-induced metabolic stress. NPFFR2-overexpressing animals showed accelerated weight gain despite unchanged food intake, accompanied by adipose tissue inflammation, adipocyte hypertrophy, hepatic steatosis, and impaired glucose regulation. The findings suggest that enhanced NPFFR2 signaling favors energy storage over metabolic efficiency. Although receptor overexpression was largely confined to the hypothalamus, the observed effects extended into peripheral tissues, underscoring the context-dependent interaction between central and peripheral NPFFR2 signaling [55].

Bite and brain

Occlusal disharmony (OD) was initially associated primarily with dysfunction of the stomatognathic system and mandibular pain disorders [56]. Subsequent experimental studies, however, have suggested a considerably broader physiological impact. In murine models, OD has been linked to hippocampal morphological alterations as well as osteopenia affecting the lumbar vertebrae and long bones [57]. These changes were accompanied by stress-related responses and an increased incidence of cardiovascular disease, raising the possibility that OD may influence cardiac homeostasis through modulation of the autonomic nervous system [57]. Additional findings from studies in rats and mice indicate that OD may also contribute to neuropsychiatric vulnerability, including depression and anxiety-like states [58,59].

Expanding on these observations, Li et al. demonstrated that mice with OD exhibited pronounced anxiety-like behavior that could be effectively attenuated by administration of mifepristone. Mechanistically, the authors reported that OD reduced GABAergic synaptic transmission within the medial prefrontal cortex, proposing impaired inhibitory signaling in this region as a potential neural substrate underlying OD-associated anxiety [60].

Cataract surgery in a different light

One of the best-preserved legal texts of antiquity is the Code of Hammurabi. Hammurabi, a king and successful military leader, is credited with transforming Mesopotamia - parts of present-day Iraq and Syria - into one of the earliest major urban centers [61]. Inscribed on a two-meter basalt stele and displayed publicly in Babylon, the collection comprises more than 200 case laws addressing many aspects of society in the early second millennium BCE [62].11 The code prescribed severe penalties for professional misconduct, for instance, a physician responsible for a grave medical error could have his fingers severed [63]. Although the phrase “an eye for an eye” is commonly associated with the Old Testament, the considerably older Code of Hammurabi already articulated the principle that punishment should be proportional to the harm inflicted [64].5 Despite ongoing debate surrounding the interpretation of several passages, some scholars suggest that the code may contain one of the earliest written references to cataract surgery [65].

Millennia later, cataract treatment remains linked not only to visual outcomes, but also to broader questions of safety and quality of life. Lee et al. emulated a target trial to examine fall risk among patients receiving bilateral cataract surgery with premium blue-light-filtering intraocular lenses (BF-IOLs), premium non-BF-IOLs, or standard non-BF-IOLs. The authors found no evidence that BF-IOLs increased fall incidence. Interestingly, however, both premium lens groups showed consistently lower fall rates than the standard non-BF-IOL cohort, suggesting that socioeconomic factors, alongside IOL type itself, may contribute to this difference [66].

Correspondence

Beyond autofluorescence

A recent correspondence exchange revisited the diagnostic implications and limitations of elastase pretreatment in ALK fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assays for non-small cell lung cancer. Liu and Xu acknowledged the reduction in retest rates and the improved detection of previously missed ALK translocations reported in the original study, while cautioning that the findings originate from a single-center retrospective cohort and may be influenced by differences in tissue processing and fixation protocols across institutions. The authors emphasized that elastase pretreatment addresses autofluorescence specifically, but does not resolve other major causes of FISH assay failure, including impaired probe penetration, fixation-associated DNA crosslinking, or challenges related to tumor heterogeneity [67].

In their response, Hsu and colleagues agreed with these limitations and clarified that the proposed protocol was intended as a practical approach to reducing background autofluorescence in lung cancer FFPE specimens rather than a universal solution for all technical constraints of FISH analysis [68].
