Characteristics and regional distribution of radiographers in Saudi Arabia: a national cross-sectional study
Authors: Jaber Hussain Alsalah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadiographers play vital roles in diagnostics and therapeutic healthcare. Despite ongoing health sector reforms in Saudi Arabia, comprehensive national data on the radiographer workforce remain limited. This study aimed to examine the characteristics and distribution of radiographers in Saudi Arabia by identifying their demographic patterns, educational qualifications, and employment distribution across healthcare sectors and regions.MethodsA national cross-sectional analysis was conducted using data obtained from the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS) on 11 September 2025. Descriptive statistics were used to analyse practitioners’ demographics, educational qualifications, professional classifications, employment sectors, and geographical distribution in Saudi Arabia to inform workforce planning under the Health Sector Transformation Program.ResultsA total of 22,626 active radiographers were registered with the SCFHS. Approximately 60% were women, and 74% were under 40 years of age (mean 34.3 ± 8.9 years). More than half (52%) held a bachelor’s degree, 43% held a diploma, and 5% held a postgraduate qualification. Most (78%) had completed their education in Saudi Arabia. More than half were employed in the public sector (56.8%) and specialists comprised the largest professional category (52.5%). Workforce distribution was uneven, with over half concentrated in the Central and Western regions, particularly Riyadh and Makkah. The national average density was 640 radiographers per million inhabitants, with substantial regional variations.ConclusionsSaudi Arabia has a relatively young workforce with a clearly defined professional classification system; however, significant regional disparities persist. Background

Diagnostic imaging, an essential component of modern healthcare, is crucial for diagnosis, management, and treatment [1, 2]. Radiography has evolved significantly with advances in imaging technology, requiring practitioners to manage complex diagnostic systems, ensure patient safety, and contribute to multidisciplinary care. As imaging demand increases globally, maintaining an adequately distributed and well-qualified radiography workforce has become a critical health system priority [3, 4].

Recent literature has reported challenges related to radiographic workforce shortages, maldistribution, and retention across both high- and low-income countries [5, 6]. Workforce gaps have been reported in the United Kingdom, Sweden, and Lithuania. These challenges are driven by increasing imaging utilisation, limited training capacity, and uneven geographic distribution of professionals [5, 6]. While this global evidence highlights the importance of workforce planning, national-level data remain essential to inform context-specific policy decisions.

In Saudi Arabia (SA), the healthcare system is undergoing significant reforms within the framework of the Health Sector Transformation Program under Saudi Vision 2030, which aims to enhance healthcare accessibility, quality, efficiency, and workforce localisation. Changes to diagnostic imaging are among these reforms, given its expanding role in disease diagnosis, therapy, and management [7].

As of 2022, the population of SA surpassed 32 million, growing annually at a rate of 2.4% [8]. The country has 20 health clusters, 16 health cities, and 493 hospitals offering 78,440 beds, signifying a 21% surge in total hospital bed capacity over the past decade [7, 9]. The number of medical staff, including physicians and dentists, increased from 80,475 in 2013 to 129,229 in 2022. The number of nurses has increased from 154,568 in 2013 to 200,558. Similarly, the number of allied health professionals, including clinical laboratory specialists, physical therapists, respiratory therapists, and other similar specialists, increased by 51% from 91,781 in 2013 to 138,362 in 2022 [9]. In a recent study, 5150 radiologists were registered with the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS), corresponding to 147 radiologists per 1,000,000 inhabitants [10].

Despite rapid health sector expansion and growth in healthcare infrastructure and personnel, comprehensive and publicly available national data on the radiographer workforce in SA remain limited. This lack of consolidated workforce data constrains evidence-based planning, particularly in the context of SA’s Health Sector Transformation Program under the Saudi Vision 2030 initiative, which both emphasise healthcare accessibility, quality improvement, and workforce optimisation.

Therefore, this study aimed to provide a national assessment of the radiographer workforce in SA using official registry data. Specifically, it examined workforce size, demographic characteristics, professional classifications, educational backgrounds, employment sectors, and geographic distributions. By generating nationally representative workforce evidence, this study sought to inform strategic planning, support equitable service distribution, align workforce supply with growing imaging demands, and contribute to policy development under the Health Sector Transformation Program.

Methods

Data source and study population

This study used a national, cross-sectional design. The current data were sourced from the SCFHS via a formal request to the SCFHS data department. The SCFHS, a government organisation, oversees the classification and registration of all healthcare providers in SA, with mandatory participation [11]. Accordingly, the SCFHS is among the most reliable sources of accurate data in radiology and other healthcare specialties. The dataset comprises data on radiographers actively registered with the SCFHS, defined as those holding a valid professional licence and confirmed to be employed in SA. The inclusion criterion required radiographers to be actively registered with the SCFHS and currently employed at the time of data extraction (11 September 2025). Inactive, expired, non-practising, or part-time registrants who were not confirmed to be employed were excluded from the dataset provided by the SCFHS.

Variables and geographic classification

The analysed variables included demographic and professional characteristics such as specialisation, professional rank, geographic and administrative regions of employment, and educational background. Professional ranks are classified by the SCFHS into five professional ranks based on academic qualifications and clinical training. Practitioners holding an associate’s degree or diploma are designated technicians. Those with a bachelor’s degree are classified as specialists, and those with a master’s degree along with relevant clinical experience (4 years of combined experience and study) are classified as senior specialists. Practitioners with a PhD in radiological technology, coupled with specific clinical experience (a minimum 3 years of experience in radiology), are classified as consultants. Finally, trainee specialists are on the Saudi Board of Radiology Technology and Medical Imaging [12].

Radiographers were categorised based on their distribution across the 13 administrative regions in SA. These administrative regions were grouped into five major geographical regions (Central, Eastern, Western, Northern, and Southern; Additional File 1) [13]. Specialisations were categorised as radiological technology or various subspecialties, and they were coded according to the SCFHS terminology. Furthermore, this study collected information regarding the type of academic institution from which each radiographer obtained their degree, which was categorised as local governmental colleges, local private colleges, and international institutions outside SA (referred to as ‘abroad’). The dataset was reviewed for completeness prior to analysis; however, some variables contained ‘unknown’ or missing values. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of King Abdulaziz University Hospital (Reference No 170 − 25, approval date: 23 April 2025) in Jeddah, SA.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (v26; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). This study was designed as a descriptive, exploratory analysis, and the dataset represents a comprehensive dataset of registered radiographers. Accordingly, descriptive statistics were used to summarise the distribution of radiographers across regions, ranks, and specialties. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages to present the overall patterns and identify regional or specialty-related disparities. Missing or “unknown” values were present for some variables (e.g., employer type, university type, and region). Analyses were conducted using valid cases only, and valid percentages were reported. All the data are presented in tabular and graphical formats.

Results

The study showed that 22,626 active radiographers were registered with the SCFHS as of September 2025, of whom 59.8% were women. The mean age of the workforce was 34.3 years (SD = 8.9), with approximately 74% under the age of 40 and only 7% aged 50 years or older. The largest age group was 25–29 years (n = 5,858; 25.9%). As shown in Fig. 1, age distribution differed by sex. Male radiographers had a higher mean age (37.2 years, SD = 9.1) compared with female radiographers (33.0 years, SD = 8.8).

Educational qualifications

Most radiographers held a bachelor’s degree (52%), followed by diploma qualifications (43%), while only a small proportion had postgraduate qualifications (5%), including master’s degrees, PhDs, or board certification. The majority of radiographers completed their education in Saudi Arabia (78.3%), with 64.5% graduating from public institutions.

Employment sector

More than half of the radiographers were employed in the public sector (56.8%), 33.2% were employed in the private sector, and 10% had an employer classified as ‘unknown’. These cases were retained as a separate category and may have influenced the interpretation of sectoral distribution. Among the private-sector radiographers, 67.3% were specialists. In the public sector, 44.2% were specialists and 50.9% were technicians.

Specialisation

Radiological technology was the predominant specialisation (90%, n = 20,585), followed by ultrasonography and echocardiography (8.4%). As shown in Fig. 2, the distribution of specialisations varied by gender. Women constituted 81.2% of radiographers specialised in ultrasound.

Geographical distribution

The distribution of radiographers varied across geographical regions. The Central (n = 7,431) and Western (n = 5,249) regions together accounted for 56% of the national workforce. At the administrative region level, Riyadh, Mecca, and Eastern Region had the highest numbers of radiographers (Table 2). Radiographer-to-population ratios

Radiographer density varied considerably across the 13 administrative regions of Saudi Arabia [14]. The number of radiographers per million inhabitants ranged from 394 in Tabuk to 1,155 in Najran, with a national average of approximately 640 per million. Most regions, including Mecca, Eastern, Medina, Asir, Baha, Jouf, and Northern Borders, fell below 650 radiographers per million. In contrast, Hail (891), Riyadh (758), and Jazan (725) showed moderate densities (650–900 per million), while Najran exceeded 900 per million. Discussion

This study presents a national analysis of the demographic and professional characteristics and distribution of the radiography workforce in SA based on official data sourced from the SCFHS, addressing a critical gap in workforce evidence. This study has direct policy relevance for workforce planning according to the Health Sector Transformation Program under Saudi Vision 2030, particularly regarding equitable regional distributions, workforce optimisation, and sustainable imaging service delivery.

The findings indicate that SA has a relatively young radiography workforce, with nearly three-quarters under the age of 40 and only 2.4% aged 60 years or older. Compared with data from Europe, where approximately 7% of radiographers are aged ≥ 60 years [6, 7], SA appears to be less exposed to short-term retirement pressure. The mean age (34.3 years) was also lower than that reported in the United Kingdom (approximately 40 years) [15], which may suggest a favourable demographic structure for long-term sustainability, although differences in workforce models should be considered. However, a young workforce alone does not ensure stability; structured career progression pathways and retention strategies remain essential to prevent mid-career attrition, as highlighted in international literature [6, 16–18]. The limited proportion of consultants (0.4%) further indicates a narrow advanced practice tier, which may constrain leadership capacity, mentorship opportunities, and specialist service expansion.

The gender distribution was broadly balanced, with women constituting approximately 60% of the workforce and demonstrating a particularly high representation in ultrasound (81.2%). This aligns with the broader national workforce participation trends under Saudi Vision 2030 [19] and is broadly consistent with patterns reported in the United States of America and Australia, although differences in workforce roles and scope of practice may limit direct comparability [20–24]. While female predominance may enhance patient-centred services, particularly in examinations where gender concordance may influence patient comfort such as breast imaging [25], international evidence suggests that women remain underrepresented in leadership roles [26, 27]. Although the gender distribution among leadership positions was not examined in this study, future workforce monitoring should consider both horizontal and vertical gender equality.

Specialists constituted the largest professional category (52.5%), indicating a relatively advanced qualitative structure. However, the proportion of consultants (0.4%) suggests limited expansion of advanced practice roles. International workforce reports emphasise that strengthening advanced practice pathways can improve service efficiency and professional retention [16–18]. Therefore, the expansion of postgraduate training programmes may enhance long-term workforce sustainability.

Despite a national density of approximately 640 radiographers per million inhabitants, the workforce distribution remains uneven. Radiographers were heavily concentrated in the administrative regions of Riyadh, Makkah, and the Eastern Region, whereas the peripheral and northern regions had substantially lower workforce densities. Although SA’s national radiographer density appears to exceed the reported European average of approximately 385 per million [6], workforce density alone does not confirm its adequacy. Differences in healthcare system structure, imaging utilisation rates, service complexity, and public–private sector balance limit direct comparisons. Moreover, this study did not include workload indicators, vacancy rates, or imaging demand data; therefore, although the workforce appears numerically substantial, its sufficiency relative to service needs cannot be conclusively determined.

Within the Arabian Gulf region, available workforce reports from the United Arab Emirates and Qatar have indicated similar metropolitan concentrations of advanced imaging services. This may suggest that regional maldistribution reflects structural features of national healthcare systems in high-income Arabian Gulf countries rather than absolute workforce shortages [10, 28, 29].

Although cross-professional comparisons were not the primary objective of this study, national data have indicated that other allied health professionals in SA, such as those in Emergency Medical Services, demonstrate different qualification structures and markedly lower female participation [27]. However, similar to radiography, these professions are concentrated in the administrative regions of Riyadh, Makkah, and the Eastern Region, reflecting broader healthcare centralisation patterns.

These findings highlight the need for targeted workforce strategies to ensure the equitable delivery of radiography services across SA. Priority actions include implementing region-specific recruitment and retention incentives for underserved administrative regions, expanding postgraduate and advanced practice pathways to strengthen specialist leadership capacity, and integrating continuous workforce monitoring systems that incorporate indicators of workloads, vacancies, and utilisation rates. Aligning these strategies with the Saudi Vision 2030 objectives is essential for optimising distribution and ensuring equitable access to diagnostic and therapeutic imaging services nationwide.

This study had several limitations. The data reflect the SCFHS registry at a single time point (11 September 2025), and workforce figures may change owing to registration updates and professional mobility. The absence of data on workloads, imaging utilisation rates, and vacancies limits the assessment of workforce adequacy. Additionally, citizenship status (Saudi vs. non-Saudi) was not analysed, restricting the evaluation of workforce nationalisation trends. A proportion of records contained missing or “unknown” data, particularly for employer type and region, which may have influenced estimates of workforce distribution and density. Furthermore, registry-based headcount data do not necessarily reflect full-time equivalent staffing, productivity, skill mix within departments, or actual service capacity. Future longitudinal research that integrates service demand indicators can provide a more comprehensive understanding of workforce sustainability.

Conclusions

Saudi Arabia has developed a relatively young and professionally structured radiography workforce, with national density levels broadly comparable to those reported in some international settings. However, significant regional maldistribution persists, with workforce concentrations in major metropolitan areas. Future workforce planning should prioritise targeted regional redistribution, the expansion of advanced practice roles, strengthened retention strategies, and the integration of workforce surveillance with service demand indicators. Strategic alignment with the Saudi Vision 2030 health sector objectives is essential to ensure equitable access, optimise service delivery, and sustain long-term radiography workforce resilience.
