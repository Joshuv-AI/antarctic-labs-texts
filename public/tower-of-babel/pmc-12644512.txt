Two-dimensional transport study for heavy metal pollutants in a cutoff wall-aquifer system considering both consolidation behavior and Langmuir adsorption features
Authors: Chen Feng, Wenhao Jiang, Hao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Consolidation behavior of the cutoff walls will alters their porosity, thereby influencing their long-term service performance. In this paper, a two-dimensional transport model for heavy metal pollutants (HMPs) in the cutoff wall-aquifer system is built, incorporating both consolidation behavior and Langmuir adsorption features for the first time. The model’s numerical solutions are acquired via the method of finite difference, and its validity is exhibited upon three comparisons. Thereafter, the analyses suggest that the cutoff wall’s anti-fouling performance is improved when the consolidation behavior is considered relative to when it is ignored, which is attributed to porosity reduction induced by effective stress. The relative concentrations of HMPs obtained from the Langmuir adsorption model are consistently higher than those of a linear adsorption model, and such difference shows a growing tendency with concentration. Furthermore, a case study reveals that as the reduction factor of shear strength influencing the effective stress distribution increases from 0.1 to 0.7, the defined breakthrough time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{b}$$\end{document} declines from 88.6 to 71.4 years. This study provides a more realistic basis for evaluating the blocking performance of cutoff walls against HMPs and offers a simplified method for determining their design thickness.

Introduction

Vertical cutoff walls, such as soil-bentonite, soil-cement-bentonite and slag-cement-bentonite cutoff walls, have been widely employed over the past few decades as engineered anti-fouling barriers at contaminated sites1–9. To effectively impede the transport of toxic and hazardous substances, the hydraulic conductivity of these wall materials is typically designed not to exceed 1.0 × 10−9 m/s10–17. Besides, different types of adsorbent additives (e.g., activated carbon, biochar and zeolite) are also commonly added to the cutoff wall materials (e.g., soil-bentonite) to enhance the barrier function17–20. Despite these measures, contaminants at polluted sites may still migrate through the barriers over time via advective and diffusive transport mechanisms, posing potential risks to the surrounding environment21–27. Consequently, studying the transport laws of pollutants is urgent to clarify the containment performance of cutoff walls and to provide guidance for engineering design.

In recent years, numerous models have been developed to investigate the transport of pollutants through cutoff walls and adjacent soils22,25,27–33. For example, Rabideau and Khandelwal34 developed one-dimensional (1-D) models transport for degradable organic substances in vertical barriers, and explored the applicability of multiple boundary conditions under different engineering circumstances. Following the derived analytical solutions, Li et al.35 provided the non-dimensional design charts for pollutant migration through a vertical cutoff wall under the two types of boundary conditions. In light of the presence of an aquifer on the outside of the cutoff wall, Xie et al.24 and Yan et al.36 constructed the analytical theoretical models for one-dimensional and two-dimensional (1-D and 2-D) transport of pollutants in a system comprising a cutoff wall and an aquifer, respectively. These models help reveal the mechanisms and governing principles of pollutant migration in vertical barriers under various conditions. Nevertheless, these studies considered primarily organic substances, and were usually restricted to investigating the transport characteristics with a linear adsorption model27,31–33. In reality, heavy metal pollutants (HMPs) with varying types and concentrations are also commonly found in landfills and other contaminated sites37–46. Moreover, the previous transport models mostly assumed that the cutoff wall was in a homogeneous and isotropic state, ignoring the change of physical and mechanical parameters of the cutoff wall with depth caused by the consolidation behavior47.

Experimental studies have shown that nonlinear adsorption models, such as the Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms, often more accurately represent the adsorption characteristics of HMPs onto cutoff wall materials compared to linear models48–50. For example, Wang et al.48 found that the adsorption of lead ions by soil-bentonite and amended soil-bentonite used as the cutoff wall materials largely fitted the Langmuir adsorption features. Similarly, Hong et al.40 documented that the Langmuir adsorption model can effectively simulate the adsorption behavior of zinc ions by the cutoff wall materials, which was consistent with the results of Yang et al.42,43. Furthermore, Hong et al.23 assessed the containment effectiveness of the cutoff wall against HMPs using a numerical approach, where the Langmuir adsorption features had been incorporated. Unfortunately, most previous simulations have neglected the existence of an aquifer outside a cutoff wall51, which may cause the simulation errors24,25. Furthermore, existing transport studies of HMPs in cutoff walls are predominantly based on 1-D theoretical frameworks23–25,31. However, employing a 2-D model may be more suitable to simulate the transport behaviors of HMPs since the concentration at the pollution source is generally depth-dependent52,53, as illustrated in Fig. 136. Presently, a 2D transport study of HMPs in a cut-off wall-aquifer system that takes into account nonlinear adsorption properties is still lacking.

On the other hand, studies have revealed that the cutoff wall undergoes the non-linear consolidation under its self-weight and the neighbouring soils, which can affect its barrier performance54–59. For instance, Li et al.47 stated that consolidation leads to depth-dependent variations in the porosity of the wall. Such changes not only modify the hydraulic conductivity, thereby influencing advective transport60, but also changed its diffusion coefficient61,62. Long-term field tests performed by Tong et al.63 further confirmed that consolidation affects the engineering properties of cutoff walls. These findings collectively demonstrate that consolidation behavior plays a critical role in the barrier function of cutoff walls. Nevertheless, the influences of this consolidation behavior on the HMPs transport behaviors as well as the cutoff walls’ service performance remain unknown. Moreover, as noted earlier, the study of the 2-D transport of HMPs in a cutoff wall-aquifer system that considers the nonlinear adsorption features (e.g., the Langmuir adsorption features) is lacking. Therefore, it is of considerable theoretical and practical importance to develop a transport model that simultaneously accounts for both consolidation effects and nonlinear adsorption, thereby providing a more realistic representation of field conditions.

In this study, a theoretical model for the 2-D transport of HMPs in a cutoff wall-aquifer system is developed, which for the first time considers the impacts of both consolidation behavior and Langmuir adsorption features. The corresponding numerical solutions are obtained using a finite difference method. Validation against experimental data, an existing analytical model, and COMSOL simulations confirms the rationality and accuracy of the proposed model. On this basis, we systematically analyze the influence of consolidation and Langmuir adsorption on HMP transport behavior. Finally, the containment performance of the cutoff wall is quantitatively assessed through a representative case study.

Then, to present a theoretical model for 2-D transport of HMPs in a cutoff wall-aquifer system, the following assumptions are made in line with previous literatures23,33,36: (1) both a cutoff wall and an aquifer are in saturated state; (2) the HMPs diffusion behavior satisfies Fick’s second law; (3) the seepage behavior in this cutoff wall-aquifer system follows Darcy’s law and has reached a steady state; (4) the adsorption process of HMPs by soil particles has been in an equilibrium state; and (5) the variation in HMPs forms during the transport process is neglected.

Analysis of the consolidation behavior

As the natural aquifers commonly are over-consolidated soils and the primary objective of this study is to investigate the cutoff wall’s containment performance, here it is assumed that the aquifer is in isotropic and homogeneous state33,36. For a vertical cutoff wall, Li et al.47 presented a model to predict its horizontal and vertical effective stresses’ distribution under the impact of consolidation behavior. Compared with other models for predicting the effective stress’s distribution, the model developed by Li et al.47 takes both the arching effect as well as the lateral squeezing effect into consideration, and it fits well with the measured results in the field56,57,64. Hence, this prediction model is adopted to ascertain the corresponding effective stress values within a cutoff wall under the impact of consolidation behavior, where an approximate distribution of horizontal and vertical effective stresses has been illustrated schematically in Fig. 247. It should be stated that the service life of a cutoff wall such as the soil-bentonite cutoff wall is commonly span about 50 years (or more than 50 years)65,66, which is much longer than the time required to complete its primary consolidation process (typically has finished within the first 2 years)63,66,67. Given this, in this study, it is assumed that the consolidation process of the cutoff wall has reached a steady state for various considerations such as convenience and practicality66.

Transport characteristics analyses of HMPs

In the existing theoretical studies of HMPs transport, the consolidation behavior as well as the non-linear adsorption features of the cutoff wall have rarely been considered9,48. It is therefore of interest to explore the influences of these two factors on the HMPs transport characteristics. When conducting the analyses, the parameters shown in Table 2 are used along with other parameters that will be specified.

Comparison with the scenario neglecting the consolidation behavior

Once the consolidation behavior is neglected, the cutoff wall will be regarded to be in a homogeneous state, which means that the cutoff wall’s porosity is constant at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{n}_{w,0}$$\end{document}. Thus, in order to investigate 2-D transport characteristics of HMPs in a cutoff wall-aquifer system without the effects of consolidation behavior, the porosity of this cutoff wall can be taken to be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{n}_{w,0}$$\end{document}.

Figure 6 exhibits the HMPs concentration’s distribution in a cutoff wall-aquifer system at 200 years, where cases A and B stand for the scenarios neglecting and accounting for the consolidation behavior, respectively. The HMPs concentrations are observed to symmetrically distribute along the depth line \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:z/H=0.5$$\end{document} (the depth where the inlet boundary concentration maximum \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{0,m}$$\end{document} is located) in case A (see Fig. 6(a)). In comparison, for case B, the concentrations of HMPs in the upper zone are slightly higher than those in the bottom zone of the whole system (see Fig. 6(b)). Also, the location of the concentration maximum for case A is invariably at the same depth, whereas the location of the concentration maximum for case B shifts slightly upward with an increase in horizontal distance. These results are mainly due to the reality that the upper cutoff wall is subject to a relatively smaller consolidation stress (i.e., the effective stress) and then has a higher porosity, which makes its blocking effectiveness weaker than that of the bottom cutoff wall47. Furthermore, as the cutoff wall’s consolidation behavior reduces its porosity, the concentration in a cutoff wall system is significantly lower in case B compared to case A (see Fig. 6(c)). More specifically, at a time of 200 years, the average concentration on the outside of the cutoff wall in case B is only 30% of that in case A.

In summary, the overall barrier performance of the cutoff wall is enhanced when considering the consolidation behavior compared to neglecting it. Simultaneously, the cutoff wall’s porosity reduces with an increasing depth under the effect of consolidation behavior. Therefore, the bottom zone of the cutoff wall has a stronger barrier effectiveness than its upper zone.

Figure 7 illustrates the distribution of HMPs concentrations in the cutoff wall-aquifer system at 200 years, where case C refers to the scenario incorporating a linear adsorption model (i.e., Eq. (16) is adopted) and case D refers to the scenario considering the Langmuir adsorption model.

Study limitations

For practicality and simplicity, several factors have been neglected to develop a 2-D transport model for HMPs in this research, resulting in a few limitations. One limitation is that a variety of metal ions, such as calcium and zinc ions, are also often present within contaminated sites, which may affect the cutoff wall’s engineering properties46,51. Meanwhile, a cutoff wall’s barrier performance might be further changed with depth by the non-uniform temperature distribution, and can be deteriorated due to the environmental factors (e.g., dry-wet cycles and freeze-thaw cycles)88, but these are not taken into consideration in the proposed 2-D transport model. Also, both the seepage and adsorption processes in the cutoff wall are considered to have reached a steady state, and the cutoff wall-aquifer system is thought to be saturated. These assumptions typically differ slightly from the actual engineering circumstances. Besides, assuming the primary consolidation process of a cutoff wall has reached a steady state may slightly underestimate the pollutant transport rate, with this underestimation being more pronounced in its bottom zone. It is noted that the aquifer usually is non-homogeneous, such that its geotechnical properties vary with spatial position, an effect that is also neglected. For these limitations, it remains necessary to perform corresponding experimental studies in the future, and combine them with the field-measured data to further improve the HMPs transport theory in a system of cutoff wall.

Conclusions

In this study, a 2-D transport model for HMPs in a cutoff wall-aquifer system is established, which for the first time takes the effects of both consolidation behavior and Langmuir adsorption features into consideration. Then, the corresponding numerical solutions are acquired, and the presented model’s correctness has been validated by the three-comparisons. On such basis, the impacts of consolidation behavior as well as Langmuir adsorption features on the 2-D transport characteristics have been explored in detail, and an example-study on the cutoff wall’s containment performance has been conducted.
