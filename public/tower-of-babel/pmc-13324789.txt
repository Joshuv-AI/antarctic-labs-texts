Stable bioreactor control reveals acidic pH–driven metabolic reprogramming and mitochondrial dysfunction in human lymphoblastoid cells
Authors: Samhan Alsolami, Yingzi Zhang, Upendra Singh, Yiqing Jin, Nadia Steiner, Kacper Szczepski, Shannon G Klein, Baolei Yuan, Chongwei Bi, Mengge Wang, Siyi Fu, Juan Carlos Izpisua Belmonte, Pierre J Magistretti, Carlos M Duarte, Lukasz Jaremko, Mo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman cells require pH regulation to maintain physiological function, yet the molecular consequences of acidic environments remain incompletely understood. Here, we employ a gas-only bioreactor to control pH, oxygen, and temperature. Integrated multi-omics analyses reveal that acidic pH induces a glycolytic metabolic shift, suppresses proliferation, and promotes accumulation of lactate and oncometabolites alongside mitochondrial dysfunction. Acidic conditions increase reactive oxygen species (ROS) and activate inflammatory and immune pathways, leading to heteroplasmic enrichment of a pathogenic mitochondrial mutation. Acidic pH depletes intracellular NAD⁺, partly driven by PARP1 activation. Restoring NAD⁺ through nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) supplementation partially rescues proliferation and stress-associated transcription, while elevating NAD+ levels by NMN or PARP1 inhibition reverses heteroplasmic enrichment of mutant mitochondrial DNA. These findings underscore the role of pH homeostasis in coordinating metabolism, redox balance, and immune signaling, and identify NAD⁺ metabolism as a mechanistic link between acidic microenvironments, mitochondrial genome instability, and immune–metabolic remodeling.Subject terms: Homeostasis, Gene expression, Epigenomics, Metabolomics

Introduction

To ensure normal biological function, human cells rely on the tight regulation of their internal environmental factors, including pH1,2. Homeostasis is achieved through mechanisms such as vasodilation and respiratory adjustments, which maintain blood pH within a narrow range of 7.35–7.45 by facilitating the respiratory excretion of carbon dioxide and the renal excretion of non-carbonic acids or bases3. However, with advancing age, blood pH and plasma bicarbonate levels often decline as a result of renal dysfunction and diet-dependent metabolic acidosis4,5. This age-related acid-base imbalance has been implicated in the progression of neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, in part through impaired function of brain-resident immune cells like microglia6.

In the solid tissue microenvironment, the Warburg effect drives localized acidification, which has been shown to impair immune cell function7. The tumor microenvironment is characteristically acidic, with extracellular pH values frequently falling below 6.58. Such acidification has been documented across a wide range of malignancies, including epithelial tumors such as breast cancer and melanoma, as well as non-epithelial tumors such as sarcoma9–11. In melanoma, extracellular acidosis has been shown to enhance pulmonary metastasis by increasing the activity of proteolytic enzymes, including MMP-2, MMP-9, and cathepsin B, as well as pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF-A and IL-812. Together, these findings underscore the critical role of microenvironmental pH in tumor progression.

Mitochondria, present in many copies per cell, play critical roles in health and disease13. In cancer, mitochondrial dysfunction is involved in the Warburg effect, where a shift from oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to aerobic glycolysis is observed14. In aging, mitochondria exhibit functional decline accompanied by increased oxidative damage15, and mitochondrial dysfunction can promote cellular senescence16. Somatic mtDNA mutations are widespread across human cancers17; moreover, recent single-molecule analyses reveal clonal expansion and complex heteroplasmy patterns of mtDNA variants in individual human oocytes and related early lineages18,19, while high burdens of mtDNA deletions accumulate in substantia nigra neurons during aging and Parkinson’s disease20. Together, these observations support a context-dependent contribution of mtDNA mutations to disease pathogenesis. However, the direct influence of acidic pH on mitochondrial DNA remains unexplored.

Among the environmental factors tightly regulated within living organisms, pH and dissolved oxygen are among the most critical, undergoing dynamic fluctuations driven by cellular metabolism. Failure to maintain homeostasis of these parameters can lead to disease and, in severe cases, death21. Despite their importance, precise environmental control has often been overlooked in conventional in vitro cell culture methodologies22. As a result, molecular-level impacts of pH perturbations on human cell physiology remain poorly understood. This gap arises in part because many in vitro studies lack the rigorous environmental monitoring and control that characterize healthy living systems, thereby limiting a comprehensive understanding of pH-driven changes in cellular function22,23. For instance, most studies measure only the initial pH and dissolved oxygen levels and rely on static medium buffers and incubator settings to regulate culture conditions–an approach that has been shown to be insufficient for maintaining environmental stability over time22. Moreover, the widespread use of non-physiological buffering systems, such as the MES/Tris buffer12, deviates from the endogenous bicarbonate-based buffering system and can introduce pH instability, thereby confounding experimental outcomes22,24.

To address these limitations, we developed gas-only bioreactor-based platforms for precise environmental control25,26. These systems regulate pH and oxygen levels using controlled mixtures of N2, O2, and CO2, relying exclusively on the physiological bicarbonate buffering system (Fig. 1A). Using this approach, we investigated the effects of extracellular pH on the human B lymphoblastoid cell line (LCL) GM12878, a widely used model in biomedical research22,23.

In our previous work, we demonstrated that exposure of B LCL cells to stably maintained acidic pH levels was associated with activation of inflammatory pathways25. Building on these findings, the present study focuses on defining the functional consequences of pH perturbation by comparing the physiological norm of pH 7.4 to the acidic pH 6.8. Cells cultured under these tightly controlled conditions were subjected to integrated transcriptomic, epigenomic, and metabolomic analyses. Under acidic stress, cells underwent metabolic reprogramming characterized by a shift toward glycolytic ATP production, suppression of energy-intensive processes such as cell proliferation, and broad perturbations in metabolites involved in energy metabolism, consistent with an underlying cellular energy deficit. These metabolic changes are accompanied by upregulation of inflammatory and immune-response genes, shifts in mitochondrial DNA heteroplasmy, and impaired mitochondrial function. Among the metabolites altered by acidic pH, NAD⁺–a central regulator of cellular energy metabolism, genome maintenance, and mitochondrial integrity–was markedly depleted. Importantly, restoring NAD⁺ availability partially rescued multiple defects induced by acidic pH, providing mechanistic insight into how pH variations reshape human cell physiology.

Results

Human cells cultured under stably controlled pH and physioxia

Our gas-only bioreactor approach maintained stringent control over pH set points of 6.8 and 7.4, along with stable dissolved oxygen (87% ± 0.37) and temperature (37 °C ± 0.0038) regulation throughout the experiment (Fig. 1B–D). The analysis of dissolved oxygen levels revealed reduced oxygen demand at pH 6.8 (Fig. 1E), indicating reduced cellular respiration under acidic pH 6.8 compared with physiological conditions. These data suggest metabolic suppression in cells maintained at acidic pH relative to those cultured at physiological pH (Fig. 1E).

The acidic condition significantly reduced cellular growth (Fig. 1F), consistent with stress-induced metabolic suppression. Mobility is a crucial aspect of B cell functionality, enabling them to migrate, scan for antigens, interact with helper T cells, and perform tissue homing25,27,28. Given the potential metabolic impairment under acidic conditions, we next investigated whether B-cell mobility was also affected. To monitor live cell behavior, we employed label-free NanoLive imaging after 72 h of exposure to pH 6.8 or pH 7.4. Cells cultured under pH 6.8 exhibited significantly reduced mobility compared to those maintained at physiological pH (Fig. 1G; Supplementary Movies 1–2).

Multiomics analysis of cells cultured under acidic pH reveals an inflammatory signature

We employed a multi-omics approach to characterize cellular adaptations to acidic pH, including: (a) RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to investigate transcriptional responses; (b) Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin assays using Sequencing (ATAC-seq) to assess chromatin accessibility and transcription factor (TF) activity; and (c) Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR)-based metabolomics to analyze intracellular and extracellular metabolites (Supplementary Fig. 1A–C).

Dimensionality reduction of transcriptomic, epigenomic, and metabolomic datasets using principal component analysis (PCA) revealed pronounced and consistent shifts in cellular states between bioreactor cultures maintained under stable pH and physioxia and conventional batch cultures lacking environmental control (Fig. 2A–C). Batch culture without control of key parameters such as pH, dissolved oxygen, and temperature exhibited markedly distinct gene expression and chromatin accessibility profiles compared with bioreactor cultures (Fig. 2A–B, D, E). These results confirm that the absence of rigorous environmental control inherent to batch culture compromises reproducibility and interpretability of molecular readouts22,23,25. Consequently, batch culture data were excluded from subsequent analyses.

A comprehensive differential analysis revealed significant differences in the transcriptional, epigenomic, and metabolic states of cells under various pH treatments (Fig. 2D–F and Supplementary Data 1–3). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the RNA-seq data revealed that DEGs upregulated at pH 6.8 were enriched in pathways related to stress and immune responses, such as CR229,30, TCF731–33, and IL3234; whereas DEGs upregulated at pH 7.4 were enriched in pathways associated with normal cellular function and metabolism, such as PSAT135,36 and PHGDH29 (Fig. 2G, H). Carbon metabolism was among the top enriched GO terms in pH 6.8 (Fig. 2G), and most genes associated with this term were related to glycolysis (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further underscored this dichotomy, revealing significant enrichment of pathways such as apoptosis, TNF-α signaling via NF-κB, and interferon-γ response at pH 6.8, indicative of an inflammatory state triggered by the acidic environment (Fig. 2I and S2B). Interestingly, GSEA also revealed that acidic pH induces adipogenesis37 (Fig. 2I). Recent work suggested that acidic pH may act through WNT signaling38—a core developmental pathway governing hematopoietic progenitor specification and maturation39, which may represent an axis through which stress-like states intersect with lineage programs. Additionally, cells exposed to pH 6.8 demonstrated increased activity in the Notch and Hedgehog signaling pathways (Fig. 2I), both of which are often hyperactive in stressed cells, such as cancer cells, conferring resistance to adverse conditions40–42. In contrast, cells cultured at pH 7.4 exhibited GSEA signatures associated with growth and homeostasis. These included enriched activity in mTORC1 signaling40–42, p5343–45, G2M43 pathways, and protein export and processing (Fig. 2J, S2C-D).

In addition to transcriptional differences, TF activity scores inferred from ATAC-seq data revealed increased activity of TFs such as NFKB246–48 and FOS49–51–both established mediators of inflammatory and stress responses–at pH 6.8. In contrast, cells cultured at pH 7.4 exhibited elevated activity of TFs, including IRF652,53 and IRF954, which are associated with normal B cell functions (Fig. 2K). Together, these results indicate that acidic pH drives activation of stress-associated transcriptional programs that diverge from the homeostatic programs under physiological pH.

Acidic pH results in a cellular energy crisis

To further examine whether the reduced oxygen consumption at pH 6.8 reflected a shift in cellular metabolism, we analyzed the expression of key glycolytic enzymes and found that most were upregulated under acidic conditions, consistent with a metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis (Fig. 3A).

Concordantly, the NFE2 TF activity was higher in cells at pH 6.8 (Fig. 2K). NFE2 is known to activate glycolytic genes such as LDHA55, TALDO156, PFKFB356, and HK157, all of which were upregulated at pH 6.8 (Fig. 3B, C).

Consistently, a key glycolytic metabolite, lactate, showed significant intracellular accumulation at pH 6.8 (Fig. 3D, p < 0.0001). This was accompanied by a marked decrease in extracellular lactate secretion, as quantified by NMR analysis (Fig. 3E, p < 0.0029). We further validated this finding using an enzymatic assay, which confirmed the reduction in extracellular lactate levels (Supplementary Fig. 3A, p < 0.0050).

Although it has been reported that intracellular lactate accumulation can act to enhance OXPHOS by activating the electron transport chain58 and leading to chromatin remodeling59,60, our analysis demonstrated a significant decrease in OXPHOS activity in cells at pH 6.8 (Fig. 3F, p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 3B–H), while no differences in extracellular acidification were observed between pH 6.8 and pH 7.4 (Fig. 3G). Intracellular lactate accumulation could be caused by low extracellular pH, which could impair monocarboxylate transporter (MCT) export of lactate61. These observations suggest that cells at physiological pH exhibit metabolic flexibility, efficiently utilizing both OXPHOS and glycolysis. In contrast, cells at low pH preferentially rely on glycolysis, likely due to OXPHOS impairment.

NAD⁺ is a central metabolic cofactor required for cellular energy homeostasis, genomic stability, and stress resilience. NMR analysis showed a significant reduction in NAD⁺ levels at pH 6.8 (Fig. 3H, p < 0.0001), while NADH levels remained comparable between pH 6.8 and 7.4 (Supplementary Fig. 3I). These data suggest an impairment in NAD⁺ regeneration, which is typically associated with OXPHOS dysfunction, consistent with the Seahorse analysis (Fig. 3F). A low NAD⁺/NADH ratio and reliance on glycolysis could be associated with hypoxia or cancer-like metabolism (Warburg effect)62. Since normoxia was consistently maintained throughout the experiment (Fig. 1D), these data strongly suggest a Warburg-like metabolic reprogramming at acidic pH and imply impaired TCA cycle activity and mitochondrial dysfunction.

Indeed, NMR measurements of several cellular metabolites validated these predictions. First, accumulation of inosine monophosphate (IMP)—a marker of energy depletion and metabolic stress—was observed at pH 6.8 (Fig. 3I, p < 0.001). This coincided with a trend toward reduced ATP levels—though not statistically significant—along with a significant reduction in ADP levels (Fig. 3J, p < 0.01; Supplementary Fig. 3J). Second, pyruvate, a key substrate for the TCA cycle, was significantly depleted at pH 6.8 (Fig. 3K, p < 0.0001), potentially impairing ATP production via the TCA cycle. Third, the TCA cycle intermediate fumarate was significantly increased (Fig. 3L, p < 0.0001), suggesting impaired TCA cycle flux with a buildup of intermediates. Notably, fumarate is an oncometabolite implicated in hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell carcinoma, where its pathogenic accumulation shifts metabolism toward glycolysis (the Warburg effect) and promotes mitochondrial dysfunction, including cytosolic release of mtDNA, which activates innate immune responses, and overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS)63,64. Consistently, metabolic reprogramming characterized by enhanced glycolysis and impaired TCA cycle activity under acidic pH was accompanied by enrichment of ROS-related pathway genes (Fig. 3M), indicating elevated oxidative stress. NMR-based metabolomic profiling revealed significant depletion of the metabolite glutathione at pH 6.8 (Fig. 3N, p < 0.0001), accompanied by a significant upregulation of genes in the glutathione antioxidant pathway and other ROS detoxification genes–possibly reflecting a feedback mechanism to counteract oxidative stress (Fig. 3O–R, p < 0.01, p < 0.01, and p < 0.05). Furthermore, the metabolite taurine was significantly elevated at pH 6.8 (Fig. 3S, p < 0.0001). Interestingly, the taurine exporter MCT7 was significantly downregulated at pH 6.8 (Fig. 3T, p < 0.0309). In contrast, the Taurine importer TauT was significantly upregulated under the same condition (Fig. 3U, p < 0.05). These findings are consistent with the known role of taurine in enhancing glutathione regeneration to maintain redox homeostasis and in directly scavenging ROS to protect mitochondrial lipids, proteins, and DNA from oxidative damage65. Therefore, the intracellular accumulation of taurine at low pH likely reflects a coordinated adaptive response to oxidative stress.

Damaged mitochondria are a main source of ROS, which can initiate a vicious cycle of escalating oxidative stress and further mitochondrial damage. This process is mitigated by mitophagy, which selectively removes damaged mitochondria and thereby limits overall ROS accumulation66. Consistent with elevated ROS levels, genes involved in the mitophagy pathway were significantly upregulated at pH 6.8 (Fig. 3V).

Taken together, the cellular response to acidic pH is characterized by reduced respiration, decreased growth, a metabolic shift to glycolysis, upregulation of inflammatory pathways, increased oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction. These complex changes likely reflect cellular adaptations necessary to withstand the stresses of an acidic environment while maintaining integrity and essential functions.

Acidic pH shifts the heteroplasmy level of a mitochondrial mutation in the GM12878 cell line

Accumulation of mtDNA mutations has been implicated in development, cancer, and aging, with substantial evidence linking it to disease pathogenesis17. Given that the metabolic, redox, and mitophagy phenotypes revealed by RNA-seq and metabolite profiling at pH 6.8 suggested mitochondrial dysfunction, we investigated mtDNA variants using the high-depth and uniformly distributed mitochondrial reads captured in our ATAC-seq datasets ( > 20,000× coverage) under both pH conditions. Owing to the full accessibility of mitochondrial DNA during transposition, ATAC-seq provides genome-wide coverage of mtDNA and enables sensitive detection of heteroplasmy changes.

Analysis of mtDNA reads consistently identified 17 single nucleotide variants (SNVs) across all six samples and three culture conditions (batch control, pH 6.8, and pH 7.4). Among these, 15 SNVs were essentially homoplasmic (99–100% variant allele frequency, VAF) and remained unchanged across conditions (Supplementary Fig. 4A). The SNV at position m.309 displayed intermediate heteroplasmy (83–87% VAF) but showed no appreciable difference among conditions (Supplementary Fig. 4A). In contrast, the SNV 16023 G > A–a previously identified pathological mutation67 within the mitochondrial tRNA gene MT-TP–was specifically enriched under acidic conditions (Fig. 4A, B). This finding was validated by Sanger sequencing (Fig. 4C, D). When the VAF (or heteroplasmy level) of the mutation was monitored over six days in culture using droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), it remained stably elevated at pH 6.8 but decreased at pH 7.4 and slightly decreased in batch culture (Fig. 4E, F and S4B).

These findings suggest that an acidic microenvironment may preferentially select for cells harboring high levels of mutant mtDNA that compromise mitochondrial function, whereas physiological pH favors cells with intact mitochondrial function. This finding could have significant implications for understanding the impacts of acidic conditions, such as those found in tumors or inflamed tissues, on mitochondrial function, cellular metabolism, and disease progression. Further studies are needed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying this phenomenon and its potential impact on cellular homeostasis.

Restoration of NAD⁺ levels partially rescues cellular dysfunction under acidic conditions

NMR-based metabolomic analysis revealed that human LCL cells exposed to acidic pH significantly deplete intracellular NAD⁺ levels compared to physiological pH (Fig. 3H). Given the established role of NAD⁺ in supporting cellular function, we hypothesized that restoring NAD⁺ availability—either through nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) supplementation68 or by inhibiting NAD⁺-consuming enzymes—would alleviate phenotypes induced by acidic pH. To test this hypothesis, we designed a rescue experiment with defined molecular, metabolic, and genomic readouts (Fig. 5A).

We first examined the expression of CD38 and PARP1, the major NAD⁺-consuming enzymes. Bulk RNA-seq analysis showed that CD38 was not upregulated at pH 6.8 (Supplementary Fig. 5A), whereas PARP1 was significantly upregulated under acidic conditions, as confirmed at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figures S5B, p < 0.0282; S5C-D, p < 0.0006). To confirm the effect of different treatments on intracellular NAD⁺ availability, we quantified NAD⁺ levels using a biochemical assay. This assay corroborated the NMR findings, demonstrating a significant reduction of NAD⁺ levels under acidic conditions (pH 6.8) relative to physiological pH (Figs. 3H and 5B, p < 0.0001). Both NMN supplementation and PARP1 inhibition–using a well-characterized specific inhibitor Rucaparib69–under acidic conditions significantly increased NAD⁺ levels compared with the pH 6.8 control (Fig. 5B and S5E; p < 0.0001 for both) without altering PARP1 mRNA or protein expression (Supplementary Fig. 5B–D), thereby validating these approaches for restoring NAD+ levels.

Live-cell counts revealed that acidic pH significantly reduced cellular proliferation compared with physiological pH (Fig. 5C; p < 0.0001), consistent with our previous observation (Fig. 1F). Under acidic conditions, NMN supplementation significantly rescued this proliferation defect (Fig. 5C; p < 0.0114), whereas PARP1 inhibition failed to do so (Supplementary Fig. 5F; p = 0.0849).

Acidic pH was associated with a significant enrichment of the mtDNA SNV m.16023 G > A (Fig. 4A–F). To determine whether restoring NAD⁺ levels alters the heteroplasmy level of m.16023 G > A, we quantified this pathogenic mitochondrial variant by ddPCR. As observed previously, this SNV was significantly enriched under acidic pH compared with physiological pH (Fig. 5D; p < 0.003). Importantly, NMN supplementation significantly reduced the abundance of this SNV under acidic conditions (Fig. 5D; p < 0.0031). Similarly, PARP1 inhibition under acidic pH significantly reduced the abundance of this SNV (Supplementary Fig. 5G; p < 0.0002).

To investigate the transcriptomic changes following restoration of NAD⁺ availability, we performed bulk RNA-seq across all treatment groups (Supplementary Data 4). PCA showed that NMN supplementation shifted the transcriptional state under acidic pH toward that of the pH 7.4 condition, indicating partial global transcriptional rescue (Fig. 5E). In contrast, PARP1 inhibition drove the transcriptome to a more distinct state relative to both the pH 6.8 and pH 7.4 conditions, potentially reflecting broader effects of PARP1 inhibition on cellular programs beyond NAD⁺ restoration (Supplementary Fig. 5H). Further studies will be required to elucidate the mechanisms underlying this shift in the future.

To further define how NMN modulates the transcriptional response to acidic stress, we compared gene expression profiles between cells cultured at pH 6.8 with or without NMN. GO analysis of genes upregulated following NMN supplementation revealed significant enrichment of pathways associated with positive regulation of cellular biosynthetic and metabolic processes, response to nutrient levels, regulation of signaling, homeostatic processes, and response to oxygen-containing compounds (Fig. 5F). KEGG pathway analysis further identified enrichment of the cell cycle pathway as well as protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum (Fig. 5F, also enriched at pH7.4 shown in Supplementary Fig. 2D), indicating restoration of transcriptional programs related to biosynthetic regulation, proteostatic capacity, and cellular homeostasis following NMN supplementation.

In contrast, genes downregulated upon NMN treatment under acidic pH were enriched for inflammatory and stress-associated transcriptional programs, including pathways involved in cytokine response and production, immune and defense responses, and type I interferon signaling (Fig. 5F). Consistent with attenuation of stress-responsive signaling, KEGG analysis further revealed significant downregulation of the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway, the AMPK signaling pathway–a central sensor of cellular energy stress–and the NF-κB signaling pathway–a key regulator of immune and inflammatory responses (Fig. 5F). Concordantly, NMN treatment largely restored the expression of genes associated with cellular biosynthetic and metabolic processes, cell cycle regulation, inflammatory response, type I interferon signaling, AMPK signaling, and NF-κB signaling toward the expression patterns observed at pH 7.4 (Fig. 5G).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that restoration of NAD⁺ levels under acidic conditions improves cellular proliferation and reduces mitochondrial pathological mutation burden.

Discussion

Our study investigates the physiological and molecular responses of B LCL cells to acidic pH using a tightly controlled culture system that minimizes confounding environmental variables. We employed the GM12878 cell line as a tractable immune-relevant model to examine how human cells respond to modest extracellular acidification similar to that observed in tumor microenvironments. Cells were cultured at pH 6.8, a condition previously used in vitro25 and commonly reported in solid tumors8.

Cells exposed to pH 6.8 exhibited significantly reduced oxygen consumption compared with those cultured at physiological pH, highlighting a strong influence of extracellular pH on aerobic metabolism. Multi-omics profiling and Seahorse analyses revealed a pronounced metabolic shift toward aerobic glycolysis, accompanied by suppression of energy-intensive processes such as cell proliferation. These changes were associated with elevated intracellular lactate, reduced NAD⁺ levels, accumulation of fumarate, increased ROS, and activation of antioxidant pathways, collectively indicating metabolic and oxidative stress.

Importantly, dissolved oxygen was maintained at ~87% air saturation throughout the experiments, demonstrating that the reduced oxygen demand at pH 6.8 reflects impaired mitochondrial respiration rather than limited oxygen availability. While oxygen and pH are tightly coupled in many physiological and pathological contexts—particularly in tumors and inflamed tissues—our experimental design allowed us to isolate the effects of extracellular pH. Future studies explicitly modeling oxygen–pH coupling will be important to extend these findings. Together, our data indicate that acidic pH induces an oxygen-independent pseudohypoxic state characterized by impaired oxidative phosphorylation, reduced pyruvate availability, and a compensatory shift toward glycolysis. These features are consistent with a Warburg-like metabolic program driven by acidity alone, independent of hypoxia.

Beyond metabolic reprogramming, acidic pH induced shifts in mitochondrial DNA heteroplasmy, altered inflammatory and immune gene expression, and suppressed cell growth and motility. The observed heteroplasmy changes were detected in a single immortalized cell line and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Broader assessment of mitochondrial and nuclear genomic alterations—particularly structural variation and haplotype-resolved changes—would benefit from dedicated long-read sequencing approaches and represents an important avenue for future investigation18,70–77. Nevertheless, these phenotypes collectively reflect a coordinated, energy-conserving response to low-pH stress. Given that this study was conducted exclusively in GM12878 cells, the findings are cell-type specific and may not generalize to primary B cells or other human cell types.

A central mechanistic insight from this work is the marked depletion of intracellular NAD⁺ under acidic conditions. NAD⁺ is an essential cofactor for redox reactions and for enzymes such as PARPs and sirtuins, which regulate DNA repair, mitochondrial function, and cellular longevity78–81. Acidic pH–induced NAD⁺ depletion may therefore contribute directly to mitochondrial dysfunction, genome instability, and aging-like phenotypes. Notably, NAD⁺ levels decline with age78, and supplementation with NAD⁺ precursors has been shown to extend lifespan and improve metabolic function in model organisms78. Consistent with this framework, we demonstrate that restoring NAD⁺ availability—either through NMN supplementation or PARP1 inhibition—partially rescues multiple acidic pH–induced defects, including impaired proliferation, mitochondrial genome instability, and stress-associated transcriptional programs. These findings suggest that targeting NAD⁺ homeostasis may represent a tractable therapeutic strategy for diseases characterized by chronic tissue acidification, such as cancer and metabolic disorders.

Physiological pH is tightly regulated across tissues, yet localized acidification arises in specific microenvironments. In solid tumors, extracellular pH commonly ranges from 6.5 to 7.0 due to high glycolytic flux and limited perfusion, placing our pH 6.8 condition well within a physiologically relevant range for tumor biology8. In contrast, cerebrospinal fluid is strongly buffered, with pH typically maintained near 7.3 and only modest decreases reported in neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease6,82. Thus, while pH 6.8 models an acidic niche relevant to tumors and inflamed peripheral tissues, even smaller pH reductions may be biologically meaningful in the central nervous system. These contextual differences underscore that both the magnitude and consequences of acidification are tissue dependent, and that controlled pH systems provide a powerful framework for dissecting pH-specific cellular responses.

Our findings also have broader implications for immune regulation under acidic conditions. Elevated fumarate levels can drive protein succination, modifying cysteine residues and potentially disrupting inflammatory signaling83. In parallel, the itaconate–fumarate axis is known to regulate macrophage activation and immune responses during infection84, linking metabolic rewiring to immune adaptation. These mechanisms may contribute to the inflammatory and immune signatures observed under acidic stress.

Systemic pH perturbations can arise from metabolic or renal dysfunction, as well as external factors such as diet, medications, or elevated environmental CO₂85,86. Atmospheric CO₂ levels are projected to exceed 700 ppm by the end of the century87, which may lower blood pH via carbonic acid formation21, particularly in individuals with impaired acid-base regulation85,88. Experimental and epidemiological studies have shown that high atmospheric CO₂ can impair mitochondrial function and reach indoor concentrations associated with mild acidosis and cognitive effects7,21. Notably, modest pH reductions of 0.3–0.4 units have been reported in such contexts89–91, underscoring that relatively small acid–base disturbances can engage cellular stress pathways consistent with those identified here.

While the long-term physiological consequences of chronic low-pH exposure in humans remain to be fully elucidated, our findings emphasize the importance of understanding cellular responses to acidification. This study provides a controlled, multi-omics framework for dissecting how defined pH shifts reshape metabolism, redox balance, mitochondrial integrity, and immune signaling, and may inform future strategies to mitigate aging-related decline and disease progression in increasingly acidogenic environments.

Finally, we note that some assays—particularly bioreactor-based experiments and NMR metabolomics—were conducted with a limited number of biological replicates due to the scale and technical demands of these platforms. Nevertheless, the responses to acidic pH were robust, reproducible across independent measurements, and supported by multiple orthogonal assays. Accordingly, we interpret these findings with appropriate caution, while emphasizing that the strong concordance across multi-omics datasets reinforces the overall conclusions.

Methods

Culture conditions

All cell culture and bioreactor operations were performed as previously described in ref. 25. Briefly, GM12878 cells were thawed from the liquid N2 tank and cultured in batch for 40–44 h before transfer into the bioreactor under two pH treatments (6.8 and 7.4) for 72 h, with dissolved oxygen (dO2) maintained at 85% air saturation and a stirring rate of 155 RPM. Cells were inoculated at 200k cells per mL with a total volume of 150 mL. The culture medium was RPMI 1640 supplemented with10% FBS and 1% Pen/strep. All media were prewarmed at 37 °C prior to use. Stirred mini-bioreactors (Eppendorf) equipped with single-use BioBlu vessels (Eppendorf, Cat# 1386100600), pH sensors (Eppendorf, Cat# 78103205), and dissolved oxygen (dO₂) sensors (Eppendorf, Cat# 78108023) were used for all cultures. pH control was achieved by automated sparging of CO₂ or N₂ gases when the pH rose above or fell below the setpoint, respectively. Similarly, dO₂ levels were maintained by sparging pure O₂ gas when dO₂ levels dropped below the setpoint, in response to real-time sensor measurements. All endpoint assays—including RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, NMR metabolomics, Seahorse respiration measurements, viability, and NanoLive imaging—were performed on cells harvested after 72 h under the indicated pH conditions, unless otherwise specified.

Droplet digital PCR

Duplex ddPCR was performed using the Bio-Rad QX200 Droplet Digital PCR System to quantify mitochondrial mutation at position 16023. Primers were designed to amplify the target region, including a forward primer (m.16023_F: 5’-TGAAAACCTTTTTCCAAGGAC-3’) and a reverse primer (m.16023_R: 5’-TTGTTGATGGGTGAGTCAAT-3’). Allele-specific hydrolysis probes were used to discriminate between the wild type and mutant genotypes: a FAM-labeled probe specific for the wild-type 16023 G allele (FAM-ZEN-ACTATTCTCTGTTCTTTCATGGGGA-Iowa Black FQ) and a HEX-labeled probe specific for the 16023 G > A mutant allele (HEX-ZEN-ACTATTCTCTATTCTTTCATGGGGA-Iowa Black FQ). Approximately 18,000–20,000 droplets were generated per reaction using the Bio-Rad QX200 Droplet Generator, and the emulsified reactions were transferred to a thermal cycler for amplification. Thermal cycling was performed with a ramp rate set to 2 °C/s under the following conditions: 3 min at 25 °C, 10 min at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 30 s at 94 °C and 1 min at 60 °C, with a final enzyme deactivation step of 10 min at 98 °C and a hold at 4 °C for 30 min. After amplification, droplets were read using the Bio-Rad QX200 Droplet Reader, and data analysis was performed using QuantaSoft software (Bio-Rad).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells subjected to NMN, PARP inhibitor treatment, or untreated control using the Direct-zolTM RNA MiniPrep Kit (Zymo Research, Catalog #R2052) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was measured by Qubit 4 Fluorometer using the Qubit RNA HS Assay kit, and RNA purity was assessed by the Nanodrop 8000 Spectrophotometer. For each sample, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed using iScript™ Reverse Transcription Supermix for RT-qPCR (Bio-Rad, Catalog #1708841) following the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR primers were designed for PAPR1 (forward primer 5’-TGGAAAAGTCCCACACTGGTA-3’; reverse primer 5’-AAGCTCAGAGAACCCATCCAC-3’) and the reference gene PGK1 (forward primer 5’- GACCTAATGTCCAAAGCTGAGAA-3’; reverse primer 5’- CAGCAGGTATGCCAGAAGCC-3’). qPCR reactions were set up in 10 µL volumes using SsoAdvanced™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Catalog #1725275) on a Bio-Rad C1000 Touch™ Thermal Cycler. Cycling conditions were 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 55 °C for 30 s, with subsequent melt-curve analysis to confirm amplification specificity. Each biological sample was measured with three technical replicates. Ct values were obtained using Bio-Rad CFX Manager 3.1. Relative PARP1 expression was normalized to PGK1 using the ΔCt method, with pH 7.4 serving as the calibrator for ΔΔCt calculations. Statistical analyses were performed on ΔCt values, whereas fold-change values (2⁻ΔΔCt) were used for visualization. Group comparisons were conducted using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Data are shown as mean ± SEM.

Western blot

Cells treated with NMN, PARP inhibitor, or left untreated (control) were lysed on ice for 30 minutes using pre-cooled RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and protein concentration was determined using the Qubit Protein Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, #Q33211). Protein samples were diluted to equal concentrations, mixed with Laemmli buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol, and heated at 95 °C for 5 min. Proteins were separated by electrophoresis on Mini-PROTEAN TGX gels (Bio-Rad, #456-1093) and transferred to PVDF membranes using the wet transfer method. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4° C with primary antibodies (PARP1 Monoclonal Antibody (123), InvitrogenTM, #436400, 1:2000; Anti-GAPDH (6C5), Merck, #CB1001, 1:2000) diluted in 5% BSA. After washing three times for 10 min each with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #7076S, 1:2000) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by additional washes. Protein bands were visualized by developing the membranes with ECL substrate (Thermo Scientific Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate, #32106) and subsequent exposure using an Amersham Imager 600. Band intensities were quantified using Image Lab software. Target protein signals were normalized to the loading control GAPDH and expressed relative to the indicated control condition.

NAD+/NADH assessment

Cellular NAD(H) was quantified using the NAD/NADH Assay Kit (Colorimetric; Abcam, Catalog #ab65348) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Plates were incubated at room temperature for 5 min, then 10 µL Developer Solution was added to each well, and the absorbance at OD450 was checked after 3 hours. To measure NADH, NAD+ was decomposed prior to the cycling reaction by heating extracted samples at 60°C for 30 min, followed by cooling on ice. A fresh NADH standard curve was prepared using the kit NADH standard. Absorbance values were background-corrected by subtracting the mean OD450 of blank wells measured at the same read. NADt and NADH amounts were quantified by interpolation from the linear calibration curve generated from the NADH standards at the matched read. NAD+ was calculated as NADt − NADH. For each condition, replicate wells were quantified in pmol/well. Group comparisons were performed using two-sided t-tests on pmol-based NAD+ values, with multiple-testing correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

Sanger sequencing

The genomic DNA extracted from pH-treated cells was sent to KAUST Genomic Corelabs for sequencing. Subsequently, the data obtained was analyzed using Surveyor software and visualized through GraphPad Prism software. Additionally, Sanger traces were processed and analyzed using Snapgene.

Extracellular lactate measurement

To measure extracellular lactate concentrations produced by the GM12878 cell line, a separate experiment was performed. Extracellular lactate levels at 72 h were determined using a microplate reader colormetric kit (Sigma-Aldrich, cat# MAK064). The experiment included two biological samples, each with two technical replicates. The kit’s enzymatic assay, sensitive from 0.001 mM to 10 mM, was used for lactate determination. Supernatant medium aliquots were collected at each timepoint, centrifuged at 200 g for 5 minutes to clear cells and debris, and then diluted in the provided assay buffer. A standard curve was constructed using lactate standards for serial dilutions (0.0 mM to 0.4 mM). The lactate concentration in unknown samples was calculated using the Excel “trend” function, based on spectrophotometric absorbances of the standards at 570 nm (A570). The results of this assay are presented in Supplementary Fig. 3F.

Seahorse

Cells were tested with the mito stress kit (Agilent). Before the test, the XFe24 cartridge (Agilent) was incubated overnight at 37 °C in the calibrant reagent (Agilent). On the day of the test, fresh assay media was prepared by supplementing the XF Assay Base Medium with 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 10 mM glucose. The medium was warmed to 37 °C and brought to pH 7.4. Prior to seeding the cells, XF24 seahorse plates were coated with poly ornithine 0.1 g/L for 30 min and washed twice with cell culture grade water. Cells were washed twice with the assay medium and seeded at a density of 500,000 per well. The plate was centrifuged at 300 x g for 5 min to promote cell attachment. Cells were then incubated at 37 °C without CO2 for 30–45 min. Meanwhile, oligomycin, Carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP) and rotenone/antimycin-A solutions were prepared and added into the cartridge prior to the start of the assay. A final concentration of 1 µM oligomycin, 1 µM FCCP, and 0.5 µM of rotenone and antimycin A were delivered into each well. The oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured throughout the test. The data were collected and further analyzed.

Metabolomics

For metabolomics studies, we followed an established protocol for 1H NMR–based metabolomics profiling of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs)92. B cells were washed to remove extracellular components by resuspension in 1 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4 or 6.8) and centrifugation at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C92. The final cell pellet was used for metabolite extraction. The extraction was performed by resuspending the washed cell pellet in 1 mL of cold 80% (v/v) methanol in water. The suspension was incubated on a thermomixer at 4 °C with mixing at 1400 rpm for 15 min to facilitate efficient metabolite release. Following incubation, the sample was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 5 minutes at 4 °C, and 900 µL of the supernatant was carefully collected. This extraction step was repeated twice more using the remaining pellet, and the three supernatant fractions (total volume 2.7 mL) were pooled. The combined supernatant was subsequently dried overnight in a Speedvac (CentriVap Concentrator of LABCONCO) until complete removal of the solvent. The resulting dried extract was reconstituted in 550 µL of 50 mM PBS (pH 7.4 or 6.8) in D2O containing 1.0 mM trimethylsilyl propionate (TSP), which served as an internal standard reference. Suspended solution was vortexed till complete dissolution and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C. 500 µL transparent supernatant was transferred into 5 mm NMR tube for NMR analysis on a Bruker 800 MHz AVANACE NEO NMR spectrometer equipped with Bruker TCI (2H/13C/15N) cryogenic probe (BrukerBioSpin, Rheinstetten, Germany) was used to record all NMR spectra. 1D 1H NMR spectra from the noesypr1d experiment were recorded at temperature of 298 K for 128 scans with a 5-second inter-scan delay and a 2-second per-scan acquisition time, spectral width 20 ppm, and processed at data points 64,516. The total acquisition time for each sample was 15 min and 35 s. Topspin 4.1.4 was used for spectral processing following the acquisition. All spectra were zero-filled to 131k points, and a 0.3 Hz line broadening was used. In the case of media, 60 µL of media with high salt was added to dilute in 140 µL of 50 mM PBS (pH 7.4 or 6.8) in D2O containing 1.0 mM trimethylsilyl propionate (TSP) and vortexed it for one minute and transferred in 3 mm NMR tube for NMR analysis on a Bruker 700 MHz AVANACE NEO NMR spectrometer equipped with Bruker TXO (1H/2H/13C/15N) cryogenic probe (BrukerBioSpin, Rheinstetten, Germany) was used to record all NMR spectra. 1D 1H NMR spectra from the noesypr1d experiment were recorded at temperature of 298 K for 256 scans with a 5 second inter-scan delay and a 2-second per-scan acquisition time, spectral width 20 ppm with processed at data points 55,554. The total acquisition time for each sample was 35 minutes and 27 s. Topspin 4.1.4 was used for spectral processing following the acquisition. All spectra were zero-filled to 131k points, and a 0.3 Hz line broadening was used. Identification of metabolites and its quantification were analyzed at Chenomx NMR Suite 9.0 (Chenomx Inc., Alberta, Canada)93, and statistical analyses were performed using the web server Metaboanalyst 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca,)94. 1D 1H NMR spectra with assigned identified metabolites were illustrated in Adobe Illustrator 2022.

Label-free live cell holographic tomography

The cells were cultured in a 35 mm µ-Dish (Ibidi, Cat.# 80136) and subjected to imaging using a 3D Cell Explorer-fluo (Nanolive SA, Switzerland). Throughout the imaging process, conditions such as temperature (maintained at 37 °C), CO2 levels (maintained at 5%), and humidity were meticulously controlled. A total of 96 slides were acquired in z-stack configuration, with images collected at 10 min intervals. Image processing and video generation were carried out using ImageJ software with a custom script provided by the manufacturer. Videos were used to calculate cell displacements using ImageJ.

RNA-seq and ATAC-seq

Total RNA was extracted using Qiagen RNeasy mini kit (cat#74106), and the quality was assessed with the Bioanalyzer 2100. The RNA samples with RIN > 7.0 are outsourced to Novogene and X Genomics for directional library preparation and sequencing. For ATACseq library preparation, the ATACseq kit from Diagenode (cat# C0108002) was used, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The resultant tagmentation product was then single-indexed using the indexing kit (cat# C01011032). The sequencing of the ATACseq library was outsourced to Novogene.

Gene expression and chromatin accessibility analysis

For RNA-seq profiling, gene expression was quantified at the gene level. Fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values were used for quantifying gene expression levels. Genes were filtered to retain those expressed at FPKM ≥ 1 in at least two samples. PCA was performed using the prcomp() function in R. For differential gene expression analysis, raw read counts from RNA-seq datasets were processed using DEseq295. Genes were considered differentially expressed if they exhibited an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and an absolute log₂ fold change > 1, unless otherwise specified. The volcano plot highlighting differentially expressed genes was generated using the EnhancedVolcano package in R.

For chromatin accessibility analysis, the ATAC-seq raw sequencing reads were preprocessed with the default parameters by NGmerge (version 0.3)96 to remove adapters and low-quality reads. Cleaned reads were aligned to the human genome assembly (hg38) using bowtie2 (version 2.3.4.1)97 with the default parameters except for the following options: “-X 2000 --no-unal --very-sensitive”. The hg38 blacklist regions were removed using bedtools (version 2.26.0)98. Peaks were called using MACS2 (version 2.2.6)99. For accessibility profiling, peak counts were filtered to retain peaks with at least 10 reads in two or more samples, normalized using DESeq2 size factors, and log₂-transformed as log₂(normalized counts + 1). PCA was performed on the top 2000 most variably accessible peaks across all samples using the prcomp() function in R. The heatmap was generated based on the top 500 most variable peaks, with peak accessibility values scaled by row before clustering. TF activities were inferred from ATAC-seq footprinting analysis using HINT-ATAC100. Differential TF activity between pH 6.8 and pH 7.4 conditions was assessed, and TFs were considered differentially activated if they exhibited an absolute fold change > 0.1 and a padj < 0.05. The volcano plot for TF activities was generated using the EnhancedVolcano package in R.

NMN supplementation and PARP1 inhibitor intervention

GM12878 cells were thawed from liquid nitrogen stocks and expanded in batch culture until entering logarithmic growth phase. Cells were then transferred to bioreactor culture systems equilibrated to the indicated pH conditions (pH 7.4 or pH 6.8), with dissolved oxygen and pH continuously monitored to ensure stability throughout the experiment. Cells were seeded at a density of approximately 2.5 × 10⁵ cells/mL and cultured for 72 h. For NAD⁺ precursor supplementation, nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN, Abcam; Cat# ab223879) was added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 0.5 mM at the start of the bioreactor culture. For inhibition of NAD⁺ consumption, a PARP1 inhibitor (Rucaparib, Sigma; Cat# AMBH2D6FA67F) was added at a final concentration of 5 μM. NMN-treated and PARP1 inhibitor–treated cultures were performed in parallel with untreated controls under both pH conditions. No medium changes were performed during the intervention period to maintain consistent metabolic conditions. At the end of the 72 h treatment period, cells and conditioned media were harvested for downstream analyses. All intervention conditions were processed in parallel to minimize batch effects.

Cell viability and growth analysis

After NMN supplementation and PARP1 inhibitor intervention, cell growth and viability were assessed by quantifying the number of live cells 72 h after treatment. Statistical comparisons were performed on raw live cell counts. Pairwise two-sided Student’s t-tests were conducted for the following predefined comparisons: pH 6.8 vs pH 7.4, pH 6.8 vs pH 6.8 + NMN, and pH 6.8 vs pH 6.8 + PARP1 inhibitor. Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Adjusted p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Bar plots display the mean live cell number for each condition, with error bars representing SEM.

Statistics and Reproducibility

All analyses were performed in RStudio (R version R 4.4.3) and GraphPad Prism (version 10). Statistical tests were conducted using unpaired two-tailed t-tests, unless otherwise specified. P-values were reported in the corresponding figure legends. Statistical significance was defined as follows: ns, not significant; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001. For each experiment, at least three independent biological replicates were analyzed unless otherwise stated.
