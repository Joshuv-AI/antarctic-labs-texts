Green mycosynthesis of small-sized silver nanoparticles (ss-AgNPs) from endophytic Fusarium solani and their multi-target biomedical profiles
Authors: Tarkeshwari A Madavi, Sachin T Yerpude, Utpal J Dongre, Vijay J Upadhye, Alok R Rai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present work focuses on the mycosynthesis, physicochemical profiling and biological evaluation of small-sized silver nanoparticles (ss-AgNPs) synthesized through a fungal-mediated green approach using endophytic Fusarium solani. The UV–Visible spectrum exhibited a distinct surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peak at 499 nm, indicating the successful reduction of Ag⁺ ions to Ag0, while XRD and TEM analyses revealed crystalline, spherical ss-AgNPs with an average particle size of 6.13 nm and a face-centered cubic (fcc) crystalline structure. FTIR spectra indicated the contribution of biomolecules containing functional groups such as amine, hydroxyl and carbonyl groups as reducing and stabilizing agents. The zeta potential (− 6.9 mV) suggests moderate colloidal stability. Biologically, the ss-AgNPs demonstrated broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, significant anti-biofilm inhibition (up to 73.53%), potent antioxidant potential (IC50 = 35.85 µg/mL), and strong anti-inflammatory activity in protein denaturation (IC50 = 22.90 µg/mL). The nanoparticles also exhibited marked cytotoxicity against A549 lung cancer cells (IC50 ≈ 25–50 µg/mL) and moderate α-amylase inhibitory activity (IC50 = 93.82 µg/mL), suggesting anti-diabetic potential. These findings confirm that fungal-mediated synthesis is a sustainable, eco-friendly, and efficient method for producing multifunctional ss-AgNPs with diverse biomedical applications in antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer therapeutics.Keywords: Mycosynthesis, Endophytic Fusarium solani, Multifunctional ss-AgNPs, Biomedical applicationSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Microbiology, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

In recent years, nanostructures have attracted significant attention encompassing a wide range of advanced fields, including medicine, water and wastewater application, antimicrobial technologies, diagnostics, pharmaceuticals, and the food industry, owing to their unique optical, electrical, chemical, magnetic and mechanical properties1. Among the various nanomaterials, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have gained special prominence because of their remarkable antimicrobial, antifungal and catalytic activities, which have made them indispensable in biomedical and environmental applications2. Conventional physical and chemical approaches for nanoparticle synthesis, while effective, are often constrained by high energy demands, use of hazardous solvents and resulting in the production of toxic residues, thus limiting their suitability for biological applications. These limitations have driven growing interest in environmentally sustainable biogenic synthesis approaches employing organisms like plants, bacteria, algae and fungi as natural reducing and capping agents to produce nanoparticles that are cost-effective, high-yielding, and eco-friendly3,4.

Among biological systems, microorganisms have emerged as efficient nanoscopic synthesis modules capable of creating multiple types of metallic nanoparticles, including silver, gold, and cadmium sulfide, under mild conditions5. Fungal species in particular—owing to their rapid growth, metal tolerance, and robust enzymatic secretion—are considered ideal candidates for large-scale nanoparticle production6. The fungal-mediated synthesis of AgNPs occurs either extracellularly or intracellularly through enzymatic reduction of metal ions, with extracellular synthesis offering advantages such as easier recovery, scalability, and reduced processing cost7. Numerous studies have demonstrated that AgNPs derived from fungal routes exhibit enhanced antimicrobial capability, enhanced by their smaller size, greater surface exposure and improved dispersity, which enable them to penetrate microbial cell walls and impede key biological functions involved in nucleic-acid and protein formation, leading to suppressed microbial development8.

In recent years, endophytic fungi—microbial fungi occurring internally in plants without inducing symptoms have garnered increasing attention as a promising and underexplored biological source for nanoparticle synthesis9. These fungi possess a rich metabolic repertoire, capable of producing diverse bioactive secondary endogenous metabolites that impart essential reducing properties and structural stabilization during nanoparticle development5. Their mutualistic association with host plants often confers enhanced biochemical diversity, enabling endophytes to synthesize nanoparticles with unique size, shape, and functional properties. Recent studies have demonstrated that endophytic fungi such as Aspergillus hiratsukae and Colletotrichum sp. can efficiently biosynthesize silver nanoparticles exhibiting potent antibacterial, antioxidant, and photocatalytic properties5,7. Similarly, marine endophytic fungi have been reported to produce monodispersed AgNPs with improved biocompatibility and performance under stress conditions8.

Endophytic fungi are not only valuable for nanoparticle synthesis but also for their ability to produce pharmaceutically significant compounds like taxol (paclitaxel), camptothecin, and podophyllotoxin, highlighting their potential as sustainable sources for drug discovery and nanomaterial production7,9. Integrating these dual capabilities offers exciting prospects for developing bio functional nanomaterials with synergistic therapeutic and antimicrobial properties. Despite these advances, however, critical gaps remain—particularly concerning mechanistic understanding of endophyte-mediated nanoparticle biosynthesis, optimization of physicochemical parameters, and the correlation between metabolite profile and nanoparticle characteristics. Addressing these challenges could pave the way for scalable, non-toxic, and eco-friendly production of AgNPs for biomedical and environmental applications. Therefore, the current research examines the extracellular synthesis of silver nanoparticles facilitated by an endophytic fungal isolate Fusarium solani (F. solani), emphasizing optimization of synthesis parameters, physicochemical characterization, and evaluation of biological and biomedical efficacy. Through this approach, we aim to contribute to the growing field of green nanotechnology by demonstrating an efficient, sustainable, and biologically driven route for silver nanoparticle production.

Recent work by Ali et al. has advanced understanding of fungal-mediated silver nanoparticle synthesis and associated biological activities. For example, Ali et al. reported the synthesis of AgNPs using fungal extracts and demonstrated enhanced antioxidant properties, highlighting the influence of capping biomolecules on functional performance10. Similarly, Ali et al. investigated AgNP synthesis for biomass valorization with significant antimicrobial outcomes, emphasizing the role of synthesis conditions on nanoparticulate behavior11. However, few studies integrate detailed physicochemical characterization with a comprehensive assessment of antibacterial, antibiofilm, antioxidant, and cytotoxic activities across multiple biological models. The present work builds upon these foundations by correlating multi-technique characterization data with broad biological profiles, thereby providing deeper mechanistic insight into fungal-mediated AgNP synthesis using Fusarium solani.

Although several studies have reported silver nanoparticle synthesis using Fusarium species, most investigations primarily focus on synthesis optimization and basic antimicrobial screening, with limited integration of detailed physicochemical characterization and systematic biological correlation. In contrast, the present study advances the field by (1) providing a comprehensive multi-technique characterization (UV–Vis, XRD, TEM, FTIR, EDS, and ζ-potential) with structured interpretation of surface chemistry and stability, (2) establishing a direct correlation between nanoparticle physicochemical properties and antibacterial, antibiofilm, antioxidant, and cytotoxic activities, and (3) critically evaluating solvent effects and biofilm inhibition mechanisms using clinically relevant Gram-positive pathogens. Furthermore, unlike earlier reports that emphasize only reduction mechanisms, this work discusses reduction, capping, stabilization, and structure–activity relationships in a unified framework. These integrated analyses offer deeper mechanistic insight into fungal-mediated AgNP synthesis using Fusarium solani and enhance the translational relevance of the synthesized nanoparticles for biomedical applications.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and fungal culture of endophytic Fusarium solani (F. solani)

Analytical-grade silver nitrate (CAS No. 7761–88-8) was acquired from Sigma-Aldrich and employed without further treatment. All solutions were formulated using double-distilled water. Potato dextrose agar (PDA) and nutrient agar (NA) media were supplied by HiMedia Laboratories, Mumbai, India. The fungal strain Fusarium solani was acquired from the National Fungal Culture Collection of India (NFCCI), Pune.

F. solani was cultivated and preserved on PDA at the temperature about 25 ± 2 °C. For long-term storage and future use, cultures were preserved at 4 °C. Robust growth of F. solani was observed on PDA plates, indicating successful maintenance of pure culture (Fig. 1).

Growth of F. solani on potato dextrose agar (PDA).

Preparation of ss-AgNPs

The formation of ss-AgNPs was achieved through biosynthesis was initiated by cultivating F. solani in 150 mL of sterilized potato dextrose broth (PDB) within an Erlenmeyer flask. The fungal culture was incubated at 25 °C with continuous agitation (150 rpm) for 20 days. The biomass was then separated using Whatman No. 1 filter paper, washed three times with sterile deionized water, and suspended in 100 mL deionized water for agitation at 26 °C and 150 rpm for 48 h. Subsequently, 30 g of wet biomass was transferred to a 1000 mL flask containing 500 mL of 0.1 M AgNO₃ solution and incubated at 26 °C at 150 rpm for 20 days. Formation of silver nanoparticles was indicated by a color change from pale red to deep wine red indicated the reduction of silver ions. Appropriate controls were maintained—filtrate obtained from biomass without the addition of silver nitrate served as the positive control while silver nitrate solution lacking fungal biomass served as the negative control. After incubation, the precipitate was recovered by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min, washed thoroughly, and dried at 80 °C for 3 h. Subsequent calcination at 500 °C for 3 h resulted in the formation of lustrous grey ss-AgNPs.

An illustrative diagram of the biosynthetic process is presented in Fig. 2.

Diagrammatic overview of ss-AgNPs biosynthesis.

The concentration of AgNO₃ (0.1 M) and extended incubation period (20 days) were selected following preliminary optimization experiments aimed at achieving controlled nucleation and ultra-small particle formation using extracellular fungal metabolites. Fungal-mediated nanoparticle synthesis is often slower than plant-mediated systems due to gradual enzymatic reduction, sustained metabolite secretion, and protein-mediated stabilization mechanisms12,13. Higher precursor concentrations can promote sustained nucleation events when reduction kinetics are slow, allowing the formation of smaller crystallites under controlled growth conditions3,14,15. Previous reports have demonstrated that prolonged incubation (10–21 days) in fungal-mediated synthesis improves structural uniformity and crystallinity due to gradual metal ion reduction and protein-assisted stabilization8,9.

In the present study, these optimized conditions resulted in highly crystalline, predominantly spherical ss-AgNPs with a mean particle size of ~ 6.13 nm (TEM) and a crystallite size of ~ 6.02 nm (XRD), confirming that the selected parameters favored controlled nucleation over uncontrolled growth. Therefore, although longer than some rapid green synthesis protocols, the extended reaction time was essential for achieving nanoscale control and enhanced multifunctional bioactivity.

Characterization of ss-AgNPs

The synthesized ss-AgNPs were characterized using a combination of analytical techniques. UV–Visible absorption spectra were recorded with an Elico SL 210 double-beam spectrophotometer to assess their optical properties. Structural analysis was performed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) on a Bruker AXS D8 Advance diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) over a 2θ range of 20°–80°. Functional group identification was carried out by Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy using a Bruker IFS 66 v spectrophotometer with the KBr pellet technique, scanning within the range of 400–4000 cm−1. Qualitative elemental analysis was carried out using energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) on an Oxford Instruments system. The morphology and particle size were examined through transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEOL JEM-100SX) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JEOL JSM-690LV). Distribution of particle size and zeta potential were measured using a HORIBA SZ-100 analyzer to determine the size dispersion and surface charge of the NPs parameters essential for evaluating their stability and potential applications.

Evaluation of biological activities

Antibacterial activity

Evaluation of the antibacterial potential of the synthesized ss-AgNPs was conducted against seven bacterial strains: Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 27853), Salmonella typhi (ATCC 6539), Proteus vulgaris (ATCC 49990), Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 25923), Staphylococcus saprophyticus (ATCC 15305), and Enterococcus faecalis (ATCC 29212) following previously established protocols16,17.

ss-AgNPs were dispersed in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 1 mg/mL stock solution and sonicated for 15–20 min for uniform dispersion. Working concentrations (100 µg/mL) were freshly prepared by dilution with DMSO. A solvent control containing DMSO without ss-AgNPs was included. Mueller–Hinton (MH) agar plates were prepared, after which 100 µL of overnight bacterial cultures were uniformly spread over the agar surface using a sterile L-shaped spreader. Wells (5 mm in diameter) were then punched into the agar using a sterile well borer. 50 µL of ss-AgNPs (100 µg/mL) suspension prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was introduced into the wells as the test sample, while DMSO alone served as the negative reference. Amikacin and ciprofloxacin (30 µg/mL) were functioned as standard positive reference. The diameter of zones was recorded in millimeters (mm) after 24 h of incubation at 37 °C. The experiment was performed in triplicates (n = 3) and results were reported in mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Anti-biofilm activity

The anti-biofilm activity of ss-AgNPs was evaluated against Bacillus subtilis, Enterococcus faecalis, and Staphylococcus aureus, selected based on their well-documented biofilm-forming ability and clinical significance. S. aureus and E. faecalis are opportunistic pathogens frequently implicated in chronic wound infections, medical device–associated infections, and antimicrobial resistance mediated through robust biofilm formation18,19. B. subtilis is widely used as a model organism for studying biofilm architecture and extracellular matrix production due to its well-characterized biofilm regulatory pathways20. These Gram-positive bacteria form structured, surface-adherent communities that exhibit enhanced tolerance to antibiotics and host immune responses. Therefore, their inclusion enables evaluation of the antibiofilm efficacy of ss-AgNPs against clinically relevant and mechanistically distinct biofilm systems, thereby strengthening the translational relevance of the study.

The anti-biofilm assay was carried out via the tube method, following previously described procedures with slight modifications21–23. A sterile test tubes containing 2–5 mL of tryptone soya broth (TSB) were inoculated with standardized bacterial suspensions and tested with concentrations ranging from 62.5 µg/mL to 1000 µg/mL of ss-AgNPs. A stock solution of ss-AgNPs (1000 µg/mL) was prepared in DMSO, and subsequent dilutions were made to obtain the desired test concentrations. Control tubes containing only bacterial cultures without treatment were also prepared. The biofilm formation was achieved at 37 °C after 24 h of incubation. The broth was cautiously decanted; to remove planktonic cells, the tubes underwent two washes with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Tube-wall biofilms were exposed to 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet for 15 min, gently washed with distilled water, and subsequently air-dried. The intensity of the crystal violet stain on the tube walls indicated the degree of biofilm formation, which was further quantified by solubilizing the bound dye in (DMSO) dimethylsulfoxide measuring the absorbance at 570 nm. The experiment was performed in triplicates (n = 3) and results were reported in mean ± standard deviation (SD). Anticancer Activity (MTT-based cell viability test)

The cytotoxic efficacy of a biosynthesized ss-AgNPs on cell proliferation was determined through an MTT cell viability assay24 employing A549 human lung adenocarcinoma cells sourced from National Cell repository, National Centre for Cell Science (NCCS), Pune, India and transferred into Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium, adjusted to a level of 1 × 105 cells per 200 µL25. A stock solution of ss-AgNPs (1000 µg/mL) was prepared in DMSO, and subsequent dilutions were made to obtain the desired test concentrations. Next, the prepared cell mixture was distributed into the wells of a 96-well plate, after which ss-AgNPs were introduced at nominal concentrations of 5, 12, 25, 50, and 100 µg/mL and the cultured plates were allowed to incubate for 24 h under a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere at 37 °C. Upon completion of the incubation period, the culture medium was carefully removed and 200 µL of fresh medium with 10% MTT reagent (yielding a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL) was introduced into each well. Additional 3-h incubation was performed under the same experimental conditions26. Following, the supernatant was carefully aspirated and 100 µL of DMSO was applied to facilitate dissolution of the formazan. Gentle shaking ensured complete solubilization prior to measuring absorbance at 630 nm. The experiment was performed in triplicates (n = 3) and results were reported in mean ± standard deviation (SD). The absorbance measurements were used to compute the percentage inhibition of cell proliferation, and the IC50 was estimated by analyzing the resulting dose–response curve.

Antioxidant activity (DPPH scavenging assay)

The DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging assay was performed using a 0.3 mM DPPH solution prepared in ethanol and freshly used for each experiment. Although a concentration of 0.4 mM DPPH is commonly reported in the literature, variations between 0.1 and 0.4 mM are widely accepted depending on experimental optimization, absorbance stability, and sample sensitivity27,28. In the present study, 0.3 mM was selected based on preliminary optimization to ensure a stable baseline absorbance at 517 nm and an appropriate linear response range for the tested AgNP concentrations. For the assay, each sample of 2.5 mL solution was treated with 1 mL of ethanolic solution of 0.3 mM DPPH and mixture was kept at room temperature in the dark for 30 min to facilitate the reaction. Anti-inflammatory activity

Protein denaturation

Protein denaturation, a major factor in inflammatory processes, occurs due to the loss of native secondary and tertiary protein conformations under stress conditions such as elevated temperature, extreme pH, or chemical exposure29. The AgNP concentrations evaluated in the assay ranged from 50 to 500 µg/mL. All preparations were made immediately prior to the experiment to ensure consistency. Anti-diabetic activity

Assessment of α-amylase activity suppression by ss-AgNPs

To determine their antidiabetic efficacy, the synthesized ss-AgNPs were subjected to an α-amylase inhibition test as described by30. An ethanolic stock solution of the sample was formulated at a concentration of 1.0 mg/mL and subsequently diluted to obtain the working solutions of 25–125 µg/mL. The reaction was terminated on addition of 450 µL of (DNS) 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid reagent, subsequently heating the tubes for 5 min in a boiling water bath to develop color. Results and discussion

The biosynthesis, characterization, and biological evaluation of ss-AgNPs were systematically carried out to investigate their physicochemical properties and multifunctional bioactivities. The results obtained from various analytical techniques confirmed the successful formation of ss-AgNPs with well-defined structural and morphological characteristics. Subsequent biological assessments, including antibacterial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, cytotoxicity, and enzyme inhibition assays, were conducted to elucidate the potential biomedical and therapeutic applications of the synthesized nanoparticles. The findings are discussed below in relation to previous studies, highlighting the significance of fungal-mediated synthesis as a sustainable and efficient approach for nanomaterial development.

Fungal-mediated synthesis of silver nanoparticles involves a coordinated process of metal ion reduction, nucleation, growth, and stabilization mediated by extracellular biomolecules secreted by the fungus. In the case of Fusarium solani, extracellular enzymes such as nitrate reductase and other reductase systems are believed to facilitate the bioreduction of Ag⁺ ions to metallic Ag⁰ through electron transfer mechanisms. Additionally, quinone compounds and secondary metabolites may serve as electron shuttles, accelerating the reduction process. Following nucleation, proteins, amino acids, and polysaccharides present in the extracellular filtrate adsorb onto the nanoparticle surface, forming a biological corona that acts as a capping layer. This organic layer provides steric or electrosteric stabilization, preventing uncontrolled growth and limiting aggregation. Such biomolecule-assisted stabilization explains the formation of small-sized, predominantly spherical nanoparticles observed in the present study. Similar reduction–capping mechanisms have been reported in recent studies of fungal-mediated AgNP synthesis, where enzyme-assisted electron transfer and protein-based surface stabilization were identified as key contributors to nanoparticle formation and colloidal behavior31–34. This mechanistic framework supports the structural and physicochemical characteristics observed in the synthesized ss-AgNPs.

Characterization of ss-AgNPs

UV–vis spectroscopy of ss-AgNPs

The optical absorption behavior of the synthesized ss-AgNPs as observed through UV–Vis spectroscopy shows three distinct absorption peaks at 359 nm, 499 nm, and 654 nm (Fig. 3a). The primary and most intense band of (SPR) surface plasmon resonance observed at 499 nm is characteristic of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), ensuring the successful reduction of Ag⁺ ions to metallic silver (Ag⁰). The appearance of an SPR band in the visible range arises as a result of synchronized oscillation of conduction band electrons in resonance with the incident electromagnetic field, a unique optical property of metallic nanoparticles35. The peak at 359 nm suggests the presence of smaller AgNPs or possible residual silver oxide species, as reported by Nkosi et al.36, who observed similar pre-plasmon peaks between 340 and 380 nm during early nucleation stages of AgNPs formation. This shorter wavelength absorption can also indicate quantum confinement effects in particles smaller than 20 nm, which aligns well with the XRD-derived crystallite size (~ 6.02 nm) obtained from Scherrer’s analysis.

(a) UV–Vis spectra; (b) XRD & (c) FTIR (d) Zeta potential of ss-AgNPs.

The additional band at 654 nm may indicate particle aggregation or anisotropy (formation of elongated or irregularly shaped nanoparticles). Such secondary red-shifted plasmon peaks are commonly associated with larger particle sizes or surface coupling among closely packed nanoparticles37. The broadness of the absorption profile further suggests a polydisperse distribution of particle sizes within the colloidal system. These spectral characteristics are consistent with recent literature on biosynthesized and chemically reduced silver nanoparticles. For example, Ibrahim et al. reported an SPR maximum around 480–510 nm for monodisperse AgNPs, while extended absorption up to 650 nm was observed for aggregated or anisotropic particles38. Thus, the optical data confirm the synthesis of metallic ss-AgNPs with mixed morphologies and size distributions, supported by the XRD results.

The UV–Vis spectrum of the synthesized ss-AgNPs exhibited a characteristic surface plasmon resonance (SPR) band centered at ~ 420 nm, which is consistent with spherical silver nanoparticles in the size range below 20 nm12,13. In addition to the primary SPR band, a secondary broad absorption feature was observed at ~ 654 nm. The appearance of such red-shifted or additional plasmon bands is commonly attributed to interparticle plasmon coupling, partial aggregation, or the presence of anisotropic nanoparticle domains8,14. When nanoparticles are in close proximity, dipole–dipole interactions induce plasmon hybridization, resulting in broadened or red-shifted absorption bands extending toward the visible–near infrared region. Although TEM analysis predominantly revealed spherical nanoparticles with an average size of ~ 6.13 nm, minor clustering was observed in certain regions, which is consistent with the secondary SPR feature. Furthermore, the relatively low zeta potential (− 6.9 mV) indicates moderate electrostatic stabilization, which may permit limited interparticle interactions in colloidal suspension. Similar dual-band SPR behavior has been reported in fungal-mediated AgNP systems where protein-capped nanoparticles exhibit partial aggregation under prolonged incubation conditions3,10. Importantly, the primary SPR peak remains sharp and well-defined without significant broadening, suggesting that aggregation is limited rather than extensive. Therefore, while the nanoparticle population is predominantly spherical and nanoscale, the optical data indicate minor plasmonic coupling effects, which have been critically acknowledged to ensure consistency between spectroscopic and microscopic observations.

XRD analysis of ss-AgNPs

The XRD pattern obtained for the synthesized ss-AgNPs (Fig. 3b) exhibits characteristic reflections at 2θ values matching the (111), (200), (220), and (311) lattice planes, confirming their crystalline nature, which are in good agreement with the (JCPDS) Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards file No. 04-0783 for face-centered cubic (fcc) crystalline silver. The prominent reflection at the (111) plane indicates that the particles predominantly grow along this crystallographic orientation, which is a typical feature of thermodynamically stable fcc silver nanoparticles35.

The peak clarity and high intensity reveal superior crystallinity, while the absence of unwanted reflections affirms the purity of the sample’s phase, indicating that no significant oxide or impurity phases were formed during synthesis. The observed peak sharpness implies that the synthesized material consists of nanoparticles in the nanometer scale. XRD data analysis was performed using, OriginPro 2025b software. This was confirmed through Scherrer’s equation, which calculated an average crystallite size of roughly 6.02 nm. The observed diffraction pattern aligns with previously reported results for silver nanoparticles synthesized via green and chemical reduction methods. For instance, Nkosi et al. reported comparable peaks at 2θ ≈ 38.1°, 44.3°, 64.4° and 77.5°, corresponding to the same set of Miller indices, confirming the fcc structure36. Similarly, Ibrahim et al. (2024) observed identical diffraction features for biosynthesized AgNPs, emphasizing the (111) plane dominance as a signature of crystalline AgNPs38.

The preferential orientation along (111) plane contributes to enhanced surface reactivity and stability, which is crucial for the nanoparticles’ catalytic and antimicrobial properties37. This crystallographic behavior indicates that the synthesis route effectively promotes nucleation and crystal growth consistent with metallic silver characteristics.

FTIR analysis of ss-AgNPs

The FTIR spectrum of the synthesized ss-AgNPs (Fig. 3c) exhibits characteristic bands corresponding to biomolecules involved in nanoparticle reduction and stabilization. A broad band around ~ 3300 cm−1 is assigned to O–H/N–H stretching vibrations, indicating hydroxyl and amine groups from phenolics and proteins39–41. This observation is consistent with recent reports, such as Kumar et al. who observed an O–H peak at 3339.54 cm−1 in biogenic AgNPs39, and Bhatt and Saraswat, who reported a shift from 3184 to 3299 cm−1 due to polyphenol involvement in reduction and capping40. The weak band near ~ 2900 cm−1 corresponds to aliphatic C–H stretching, confirming organic capping agents. Bands at ~ 1740 cm−1 (C=O stretching) and ~ 1550 cm−1 (amide II) indicate the presence of carbonyl and proteinaceous compounds bound to the Ag surface. Similar amide-associated bands near ~ 1648 cm−1 have been reported in AgNPs synthesized using Cytobacillus firmus, confirming protein-mediated stabilization. The slight shift in band positions toward higher wavenumbers in the present study suggests stronger ligand–metal interactions. The peak at ~ 1100 cm−1 (C–O/C–O–C stretching) further supports the role of polysaccharides and polyphenols in stabilization.

Unusual bands at ~ 2164, ~ 2045, and ~ 1974 cm−1 may correspond to weak unsaturated bond vibrations or silver–ligand interactions, possibly reflecting chemical transformations during nanoparticle formation. Low-wavenumber peaks at ~ 522 and ~ 487 cm−1 are attributed to Ag–O/Ag–N/Ag–S vibrations, consistent with literature reports assigning bands in the 400–600 cm−1 region to metal–ligand bonding (e.g., Ag–O stretching reported near 809 cm−1 in Sidr leaf-mediated AgNPs)43. Overall, the comparative analysis confirms that hydroxyl-, amine-, and carbonyl-containing biomolecules functioned as reducing and capping agents, forming a stable ligand shell around the ss-AgNPs. The observed band shifts relative to previous studies further indicate strong surface binding and enhanced nanoparticle stability42–44.

FTIR analysis was performed on the as-synthesized, non-calcined silver nanoparticles to identify functional groups associated with fungal metabolites involved in reduction and stabilization. Calcination at 500 °C (4 h) was carried out subsequently to remove residual organic components and enhance crystallinity prior to XRD analysis. Therefore, FTIR results reflect the presence of surface-bound biomolecules before thermal treatment. It is important to note that FTIR analysis was conducted on the as-synthesized nanoparticles before calcination. Therefore, the observed amide I and amide II bands correspond to proteinaceous biomolecules from Fusarium solani involved in reduction and stabilization. Calcination at 500 °C removes most organic residues; hence, post-calcination samples would not exhibit these characteristic amide vibrations.

SEM analysis of ss-AgNPs

The synthesized ss-AgNPs were visualized using SEM to evaluate their surface morphology and microstructural characteristics at magnifications of 500 × , 1,500 × and 3,500 × (Fig. 4a–c). The micrographs reveal irregularly shaped, rough-surfaced particles exhibiting significant aggregation and formation of micron-sized clusters. Although individual nanoparticle domains can be observed, their fusion into larger agglomerates suggests incomplete stabilization during synthesis. The particle assemblies display a heterogeneous size distribution, with estimated diameters in the range of a few micrometers, implying that the nanoscale primary particles are aggregated into larger secondary structures. Such aggregation may result from insufficient capping or high ionic strength in the reaction medium. Similar morphological patterns have been noted in recent studies where aggregation of AgNPs was shown to reduce surface area and influence their physicochemical and biological properties45–49. The observed rough and irregular morphology, however, may enhance surface reactivity and facilitate interaction with biological interfaces, which can be advantageous in antimicrobial and catalytic applications. In summary, SEM analysis verifies the formation of ss-AgNPs, displaying significant aggregation and uneven morphology, consistent with previously reported biosynthesized or chemically reduced silver nanoparticle systems.

TEM analysis of ss-AgNPs

The TEM analysis (Fig. 5a–c) provides direct evidence of the nanoscale structure and morphology of the synthesized ss-AgNPs. The micrographs, recorded at magnifications corresponding to 10 nm and 20 nm scales, reveal the ss-AgNPs are predominantly adopt spherical to quasi-spherical forms, showing uniform contrast distribution, indicating well-crystallized metallic silver domains. The mean particle size was determined from TEM images using image analysis software (ImageJ version 1.53j), by measuring multiple particles (n = 100). The measured particle diameters, as marked in the images, range between approximately 4.5 nm and 9.5 nm, exhibiting a mean particle size of about 6.13 nm (Fig. 5d). Nanoparticles are relatively well-dispersed, though minor agglomeration can be observed in localized regions, likely due to van der Waals attraction during solvent evaporation or inadequate surface stabilization. The narrow particle size distribution suggests effective control of the nucleation and growth process during synthesis, consistent with reported morphologies of chemically and green-synthesized AgNPs in recent studies45,47,48. The absence of significant deformation or irregular structures further supports the formation of stable, crystalline nanoparticles. Such nanoscale dimensions and uniform morphology are known to enhance surface area and reactivity, thereby improving potential performance in antimicrobial, catalytic, and plasmonic applications46,49.

EDS analysis of ss-AgNPs

The EDS spectrum of ss-AgNPs confirms the successful formation of silver nanoparticles with high elemental purity (Fig. 4d). A prominent and intense characteristic peak observed at approximately 3.0 keV corresponds to the Ag Lα emission line, which is a signature peak of metallic silver. Quantitative analysis indicates that Ag constitutes 88.2 wt% (54.1 at%), demonstrating that silver is the dominant component in the synthesized nanomaterial. In addition to Ag, minor signals of oxygen (10.21 wt%, 42.2 at%), silicon (1.49 wt%, 3.51 at%), and chlorine (0.1 wt%, 0.19 at%) were detected. The presence of oxygen is commonly attributed to surface oxidation of AgNPs or adsorption of oxygen-containing biomolecules from plant-mediated synthesis routes, leading to the formation of a thin Ag–O layer on the nanoparticle surface. Similar observations of oxygen signals in biosynthesized AgNPs have been reported in recent studies, where surface oxidation and capping phytochemicals contributed to the O peak in EDS spectra50,51.

The trace amount of silicon is likely associated with the glass substrate used during SEM analysis or environmental contamination, as Si peaks are frequently detected due to the underlying glass slide or sample holder52. The minimal chlorine content may originate from precursor salts (e.g., AgCl intermediates) or residual chloride ions from plant extracts and reaction media. Several recent reports have documented similar Cl traces in green-synthesized AgNPs, attributing them to incomplete washing or phytochemical interactions with chloride-containing biomolecules53,54. Importantly, no additional impurity peaks corresponding to other metallic elements were observed, indicating high purity of the synthesized ss-AgNPs. Overall, the EDS results confirm the successful synthesis of predominantly metallic silver nanoparticles with minor surface-associated elements, consistent with previously reported green synthesis studies.

Zeta potential of ss-AgNPs

Electrokinetic characterization by laser Doppler electrophoresis yielded a mean zeta potential of − 6.9 mV (single narrow distribution) and an electrophoretic mobility of − 0.000053 cm2 V−1 s−1 for the formulated ss-AgNPs (Fig. 3d). The negative sign for both parameters indicates a net anionic character of the particle surface; however, the magnitude of the zeta potential is small and lies well within the range conventionally associated with limited electrostatic stability. In practice, suspensions with |ζ|≥ ~ 30 mV are typically considered electrostatically stable, whereas values closer to 0 mV are prone to particle–particle attraction, aggregation and sedimentation55. The observed mobility corresponds to the low zeta potential via the Smoluchowski relation under typical measurement conditions and is consistent with weak electrophoretic response, further supporting that electrostatic repulsion is insufficient to prevent aggregation under the present dispersion conditions. Compared with recent reports of stabilized AgNPs—where zeta potentials in the range of approximately − 30 to − 45 mV are commonly described following citrate, polymeric (e.g., PVP/PEG) or optimized green capping—our particles show substantially lower electrostatic stabilization56,57. Other recent studies of biosynthesized or protein-coated AgNPs have highlighted similar cases in which adsorption of neutral or zwitterionic biomolecules and/or screening by salts reduces the effective surface charge and drives the zeta potential toward zero, with a concomitant increase in aggregation tendency14,58.

The ζ-potential of the synthesized ss-AgNPs was measured as − 6.9 mV, indicating weak electrostatic stabilization according to classical DLVO theory. While absolute ζ-values greater than ± 30 mV are typically associated with strong electrostatic repulsion, biologically synthesized nanoparticles frequently exhibit lower values due to steric or electrosteric stabilization mechanisms arising from surface-bound biomolecules31,32. Proteinaceous and polysaccharide components secreted by Fusarium solani may form a surface corona that provides steric hindrance against aggregation, even when electrostatic repulsion is limited. Similar ζ-potential ranges (− 5 to − 15 mV) have been reported for fungal-mediated AgNPs exhibiting moderate colloidal stability33,59. Nevertheless, the relatively low ζ-value suggests limited long-term stability, which is consistent with the secondary SPR band observed in UV–Vis spectra and minor clustering in TEM images. Future stabilization strategies could include surface functionalization with biopolymers (e.g., chitosan), PEGylation, citrate capping, or pH optimization to enhance electrostatic repulsion and improve dispersion stability60,61.

Nanoparticle agglomeration and interparticle interactions can significantly influence optical properties and colloidal behavior. The presence of a secondary absorption band in the UV–Vis spectrum and the moderate ζ-potential observed in this study suggest partial agglomeration or plasmonic coupling effects among ss-AgNPs. Such phenomena have been discussed in recent studies, where nanoparticle agglomeration alters localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) features and affects dispersion stability under varying physicochemical conditions62,63. These reports emphasize that factors such as ionic strength, capping efficiency, and biomolecular corona thickness contribute to nanoscale aggregation behavior, leading to broadened or red-shifted optical signatures consistent with our observations.

Biological applications

Antimicrobial analysis

Antibacterial analysis of mycosynthesized ss-AgNPs was assessed against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial strains performed via agar well diffusion method and the data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation of three independent experiments (Table 1 and Fig. 6). The ss-AgNPs (100 µg/mL) exhibited moderate antibacterial effect with inhibition zones varying from 12 ± 0.81 mm to 16 ± 0.81 mm, depending on the bacterial strain. Among Gram-positive bacteria, Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus saprophyticus displayed inhibition zones about 13 ± 0.81 mm and 14 ± 0.47 mm, respectively, while among Gram-negative strains, Enterococcus faecalis demonstrated the highest susceptibility (16 ± 0.81 mm). In contrast, the positive control antibiotic ciprofloxacin (30 µg/mL) produced substantially larger zones of inhibition (19–29 mm), whereas the negative control (DMSO) showed no zone of inhibition. These results confirm that ss-AgNPs possess broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, though the magnitude is lower than that of conventional antibiotics under the tested conditions31,59.

Although the agar well diffusion method demonstrated measurable antibacterial activity of ss-AgNPs against the tested bacterial strains, this technique primarily provides qualitative or semi-quantitative assessment of inhibition zones. Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) would offer a more precise evaluation of antimicrobial potency by establishing the lowest concentration required to inhibit visible growth and to achieve bactericidal effects, respectively. The absence of MIC/MBC data represents a limitation of the present study. Therefore, future investigations will employ standardized broth microdilution assays to determine MIC and MBC values, enabling more rigorous quantitative validation and improved comparison with previously reported antimicrobial studies.

Anti-biofilm activity

The formulated ss-AgNPs revealed significant anti-biofilm activity contrary to three clinically relevant bacterial species. As shown in Table 2, at 1000 µg/mL, the highest concentration examined, the biofilm inhibition reached 69.14 ± 0.19% for Bacillus subtilis, 73.53 ± 0.10% for Enterococcus faecalis, and 61.17 ± 0.88% for Staphylococcus aureus. At lower concentrations (e.g., 62.5 µg/mL) inhibition was much lower (19.88 ± 0.76% for B. subtilis; 47.25 ± 0.10% for E. faecalis; 30.78 ± 0.46% for S. aureus), illustrating a clear concentration-dependent effect. The highest inhibition of 73.53% (E. faecalis) suggests that among the tested strains, the ss-AgNPs were most effective in disrupting or preventing biofilm formation by this organism under the assay conditions. E. faecalis biofilms are known to possess a comparatively less dense extracellular polymeric matrix under certain conditions, which may facilitate better penetration and interaction of AgNPs. Additionally, differences in cell surface charge, membrane permeability, and metabolic activity could contribute to increased nanoparticle uptake and disruption of biofilm integrity. The observed trend across concentrations further supports that E. faecalis is more sensitive to ss-AgNP treatment than the other tested strains. These results align with recent literature demonstrating that green-synthesized or surface-modified AgNPs can effectively reduce biofilm biomass by 50–90% depending on species, concentration and exposure time68,69. The somewhat lower inhibition values compared to maximal literature reports may reflect differences in nanoparticle size, capping agents, bacterial species/strain, biofilm maturity or exposure duration. Mechanistically, the nanoparticles may inhibit biofilm initiation via interference with microbial adhesion, extracellular polymeric substance production or quorum-sensing signaling, and may penetrate established biofilms to reduce biomass69,70. Overall, our findings demonstrate that the synthesized ss-AgNPs hold promising anti-biofilm activity which supports potential applications in mitigating biofilm-related infections or surface fouling scenarios.

Anti-cancer activity

The synthesized ss-AgNPs demonstrated a significant dose-responsive cytotoxicity against A549 lung cancer cells as shown in Table 5 and Fig. 7. At 5 µg/mL the cell viability relative to control was 99.50 ± 21.76%, at 12 µg/mL was 94.67 ± 10.48% (both p > 0.05), whereas at 25 µg/mL viability dropped to 83.52 ± 16.26% (p < 0.05). At elevated concentrations of 50 µg/mL and 100 µg/mL, cell viability decreased markedly to 32.35 ± 4.3% and 27.01 ± 1.49%, respectively (p < 0.05). These outcomes imply that the IC50 for the ss-AgNPs is between approximately 25–50 µg/mL under the conditions tested. When compared to literature, similar studies report IC50 values of ~ 49.52 µg/mL for phyto-synthesized AgNPs on A549 cells71 and ~ 51.93 µg/mL for green-synthesized AgNPs from Momordica charantia on A549 cells72—which support the potency observed here. The steep decline in viability at higher doses suggests that the nanoparticles effectively induce cellular stress, possibly via mechanisms including (ROS) reactive oxygen species generation, mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis induction or cell-cycle arrest73. The comparatively higher viability at lower doses and large standard deviations underline the heterogeneity of response at sub-cytotoxic levels and suggest possible variability in uptake or nanoparticle-cell interactions. From a mechanistic standpoint, these results support the hypothesis that the surface capping biomolecules on the AgNPs may enhance cellular internalization or facilitate interaction with intracellular targets, thereby contributing to the observed cytotoxicity. The observed reduction in cell viability (~ 27%) can be reasonably correlated with the physicochemical characteristics of the synthesized AgNPs. In the present study, the nanoparticles exhibited a small size (~ 6 nm) and spherical morphology, which are known to enhance cellular uptake due to their high surface area-to-volume ratio. Smaller nanoparticles can more readily penetrate cellular membranes, leading to increased intracellular accumulation. Additionally, the high surface reactivity of AgNPs facilitates the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can induce oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and subsequent apoptosis in cancer cells. The relatively uniform size distribution and minimal aggregation, as indicated by TEM/XRD analysis, may further contribute to consistent cellular interactions and enhanced cytotoxic effects. Surface functional groups derived from the biological synthesis process may also play a role in stabilizing the nanoparticles and improving their interaction with cellular components.

Similar to our observations with A549 cells, recent reports also highlight the anticancer potential of biologically synthesized AgNPs. Also, the anticancer potential against A549 cells were evaluated with Rhizoctonia solani mediated Co3O4 NPs in earlier studies74. Therefore, therapeutic selectivity and biosafety cannot be conclusively established. Future investigations will include comparative cytotoxicity studies using normal epithelial cell models to calculate the selectivity index (SI), which is essential for assessing translational potential. Recent reports have highlighted the importance of such comparative analyses to validate nanoparticle-mediated anticancer selectivity32,34.

Based on previous reports, biosynthesized AgNPs commonly exert biological activity through ROS overproduction, oxidative stress-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction, membrane disruption, and apoptosis induction32,34,59. However, these mechanisms were not directly evaluated herein. Future studies will incorporate intracellular ROS quantification, membrane permeability assays, mitochondrial membrane potential analysis, and apoptosis marker evaluation (e.g., caspase activation, Annexin V/PI staining) to validate the underlying molecular pathways.

Anti-oxidant activity

The DPPH assay was employed to evaluate the antioxidant activity of the synthesized ss-AgNPs for free radical scavenging assay and the results are demonstrated in Fig. 8a. The ss-AgNPs exhibited a concentration-responsive increase in radical scavenging potential, with percent inhibition values varying from 58.54 ± 0.47% at 25 µg/mL to 90.07 ± 0.10% at 125 µg/mL. The derived IC50 value of the ss-AgNPs was found to be 35.85 µg/mL, indicating a strong antioxidant capacity. Although the activity was lower than that of the standard ascorbic acid (IC50 < 25 µg/mL), nanoparticles demonstrated substantial free radical-quenching efficiency, confirming their potential as effective antioxidants. The increase in inhibition percentage with concentration suggests that the scavenging efficiency of the AgNPs relies on the availability of reactive sites on the nanoparticle surface capable of donating electrons or hydrogen atoms to neutralize DPPH radicals. This behavior is consistent with previously reported studies showing that, the antioxidant activity of AgNPs arises predominantly from the bioactive phytochemicals acting as capping and reducing agents on their surface. Recent studies have also documented similar concentration-dependent DPPH scavenging activity of green-synthesized AgNPs, supporting our findings. For instance, Arya et al.75 reported potent antioxidant activity of Cassia occidentalis-derived AgNPs with over 85% inhibition at higher concentrations, while Khan et al.76 demonstrated that Withania coagulans-mediated AgNPs showed strong radical scavenging efficiency in a dose-dependent manner. Similarly, Barik et al.77 observed comparable antioxidant responses from Azadirachta indica-based AgNPs.

The antioxidant potential of biosynthesized AgNPs has been highlighted in recent literature. For instance, Ali et al. demonstrated significant radical scavenging activity for fungal-derived AgNPs and discussed the contribution of surface-bound biomolecules to enhanced antioxidant effects10. The current DPPH assay results align with these findings, suggesting that capping agents from fungal metabolites may contribute to the capacity of ss-AgNPs to neutralize free radicals. Thus, consistent with previous reports, the structural attributes of AgNPs—including surface chemistry and stabilization—play a crucial role in modulating antioxidant efficacy.

The slightly lower potency of AgNPs compared to pure ascorbic acid may result from the slower diffusion of nanoparticles or the dependence on surface-bound biomolecules for radical neutralization rather than direct molecular interactions. Nevertheless, the observed IC50 and inhibition profile confirm that the synthesized AgNPs possess notable antioxidant potential, suggesting their suitability for applications in biomedicine, cosmetics, and food preservation where oxidative stress mitigation is desirable.

Anti-inflammatory activity

Protein denaturation

In the current investigation, anti-inflammatory potential of synthesized ss-AgNPs was estimated via protein denaturation assay (temperature-induced denaturation of Bovine serum albumin). The ss-AgNPs revealed a clear concentration-responsive increase at a concentration of 50 µg/mL, protein denaturation was suppressed by 51.49 ± 0.43%, at 100 µg/mL reached 57.22 ± 0.44%, at 200 µg/mL reached 69.55 ± 0.42%, at 250 µg/mL reached 79.69 ± 0.18%, and at 500 µg/mL achieved 84.73 ± 0.39%. The calculated IC50 was 22.90 µg/mL. When compared with the standard drug (diclofenac) which showed % inhibition of 76.55 ± 0.16% at 50 µg/mL, with a maximum of 94.89 ± 0.40% at 500 µg/mL, it is clear that the ss-AgNPs, while somewhat less potent than the standard, still demonstrate substantial inhibition of protein denaturation and hence promising anti-inflammatory activity (Fig. 8b). These results suggest that the ss-AgNPs can effectively stabilize protein structure under stress, thereby potentially interrupting inflammatory processes that are triggered by protein denaturation or aggregation. The dose-dependent profile supports the notion of active surface interactions between nanoparticle–surface capping biomolecules and the protein substrate, likely impeding conformational changes and aggregation. These findings align with recent reports; for instance, a 2024 study observed 89.79 ± 0.02% inhibition of BSA denaturation by green-synthesized AgNPs78. Another recent investigation found that AgNPs prepared via herbal formulations showed substantial dose-dependent inhibition in the BSA/egg albumin denaturation assay79. The somewhat lower potency of our AgNPs compared to the standard may reflect slower kinetics of nanoparticle-protein interaction, diffusion limitations, or less access of active surface moieties compared to the low-molecular weight standard drug. Nonetheless, the IC50 of ~ 22.9 µg/mL is favorable and indicates that the engineered AgNPs could serve as anti-inflammatory agents or adjuncts in biomedical applications where protein-denaturation driven inflammation (e.g., heat stress, aggregation-mediated injury) plays a role. Future work should extend these findings by comparing with other inflammation assays (e.g., membrane stabilization, enzyme inhibition of COX/LOX systems) and by exploring how surface capping agents influence the efficacy of these nanoparticles.

Anti-diabetic activity

In the current investigation, the formulated ss-AgNPs exhibited significant α-amylase inhibitory activity, an in vitro indicator of anti-diabetic potential by retarding starch breakdown and thus controlling post-prandial glucose rise. According to the results (Fig. 8c), the % inhibition by the ss-AgNPs increased from 36.06 ± 0.21% at 25 µg/mL to 43.62 ± 0.38% at 50 µg/mL, 46.02 ± 0.77% at 75 µg/mL, 46.99 ± 1.58% at 100 µg/mL, and reached 58.88 ± 0.51% at 125 µg/mL. The measured IC50 for the ss-AgNPs amounted to 93.82 µg/mL. The standard reference, metformin, showed much lower inhibition at comparable concentrations (6.40 ± 0.13% at 25 µg/mL, rising to 77.37 ± 0.46% at 125 µg/mL), indicating that while the ss-AgNPs are clearly active, their potency remains lower than the standard drug. These data demonstrate a dose-dependent effect and suggest that the surface functional moieties of the ss-AgNPs may form interactions within the active site of α-amylase to hinder carbohydrate hydrolysis. Our findings are consonant with recent studies showing that biosynthesized AgNPs exhibit inhibitory effects on α-amylase and α-glucosidase enzymes under in vitro conditions80–82. For instance, one study reported IC50 values in the low tens of µg/mL for AgNPs prepared via plant extracts83. Another investigation found that green-synthesized AgNPs reached ~ 80% α-amylase inhibition at ~ 100 µg/mL84. The relatively higher IC50 (~ 93.82 µg/mL) in the present work suggests room for optimization, possibly via reducing nanoparticle size, improving surface capping bioactives, or increasing active-site accessibility. Overall, the α-amylase inhibition results support the potential of these ss-AgNPs as anti-diabetic agents in a supplementary role, warranting further mechanistic studies, α-glucosidase assays and in vivo validation.

Compared with previously reported fungal-mediated AgNP systems cited in this manuscript, the Fusarium solani-derived ss-AgNPs demonstrate a distinctly advantageous biological profile (Table 7). The ultra-small particle size (~ 6 nm) is notably smaller than most reported fungal AgNPs (typically 8–30 nm), which likely enhances surface reactivity, cellular penetration efficiency, and ROS-mediated bioactivity. Importantly, the anti-biofilm inhibition (73.53%) exceeds or lies at the upper threshold of previously reported fungal systems (commonly 55–70%), indicating enhanced interference with microbial quorum sensing and surface adhesion mechanisms. Furthermore, the antioxidant IC50 value (35.85 µg/mL) is comparatively lower than most cited fungal-derived AgNP systems, reflecting improved radical scavenging capacity. When considered collectively—small particle size, competitive antibacterial zones, superior antibiofilm inhibition, and strong cytotoxic activity—the present ss-AgNPs exhibit a multifunctional bioactivity profile that positions them among the more biologically efficient fungal-derived AgNP systems currently reported. Rather than incremental improvement in a single assay, the superiority of the present nanoparticles lies in their balanced and consistently high performance across multiple in vitro models.

The observed biological activities of ss-AgNPs can be directly correlated with their physicochemical characteristics. UV–Vis and TEM analyses confirmed nanoscale, predominantly spherical particles, which provide a high surface area-to-volume ratio, facilitating enhanced interaction with bacterial cell walls and biofilm matrices. Smaller nanoparticles are known to penetrate microbial membranes more efficiently, leading to increased membrane disruption, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and intracellular damage. The crystalline nature of the nanoparticles, confirmed by XRD, supports efficient Ag⁺ ion release, which contributes to antimicrobial and antibiofilm activity through protein denaturation and interference with enzymatic systems. FTIR analysis indicated the presence of biomolecule-derived functional groups on the nanoparticle surface, suggesting a biological capping layer that may enhance biocompatibility while still allowing surface reactivity. Although the ζ-potential value (− 6.9 mV) indicates moderate colloidal stability, slight aggregation may promote localized plasmonic coupling and sustained silver ion release, potentially influencing biological efficacy. Furthermore, the antioxidant and cytotoxic effects may be attributed to nanoparticle-mediated ROS modulation and surface-driven redox interactions. Therefore, the biological performance of ss-AgNPs is strongly governed by their nanoscale size, surface chemistry, crystallinity, and interfacial interactions, demonstrating a clear structure–activity relationship.

Conclusion

The present study successfully demonstrated the biosynthesis, detailed characterization, and multifaceted biological evaluation of small-sized silver nanoparticles (ss-AgNPs) produced via a fungal-mediated green synthesis approach. Spectroscopic and microscopic analyses, including UV–Vis, XRD, FTIR, SEM, TEM and EDS collectively provided evidence for the formation of crystalline, predominantly spherical ss-AgNPs with nanoscale dimensions (~ 6.13 nm) and face-centered cubic (fcc) structure. The spectrum of UV–Vis spectroscopy attributed a distinct SPR peak centered at 499 nm, indicative of metallic silver formation, while XRD and TEM analyses validated their crystallinity and uniform morphology. FTIR spectroscopy further revealed the presence of surface-bound biomolecules containing hydroxyl, amine, and carbonyl groups, which acted as both reducing and stabilizing agents, conferring colloidal stability and biocompatibility to the nanoparticles.

Electrokinetic measurements revealed a slightly negative zeta potential (− 6.9 mV), suggesting limited electrostatic stabilization and some degree of aggregation, consistent with SEM observations. Nevertheless, the nanoparticles retained strong physicochemical integrity and bioactivity. The biological assays revealed broad-spectrum and concentration-dependent functional properties. The ss-AgNPs exhibited notable antibacterial activity against both Gram-negative bacteria and Gram-positive, demonstrating moderate to strong anti-biofilm inhibition (up to 73.53%) along with significant cytotoxicity toward A549 lung cancer cells with IC50 between 25 and 50 µg/mL. Their potent antioxidant activity (IC50 = 35.85 µg/mL) demonstrated efficient radical scavenging potential, while the anti-inflammatory assays confirmed effective membrane stabilization and inhibition of protein denaturation, with IC50 values of 22.90 µg/mL. Additionally, ss-AgNPs displayed appreciable α-amylase inhibitory activity with an IC50 of 93.82 µg/mL highlighting their promising anti-diabetic potential.

Overall, the synthesized ss-AgNPs exhibited wide range of bioactivities—antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, cytotoxic and enzyme inhibitory—underscoring their potential in biomedical and therapeutic applications. The study further emphasizes that fungal-mediated synthesis offers a sustainable, eco-friendly, and efficient route for producing multifunctional nanomaterials with tunable physicochemical and biological properties. Future studies should aim to optimize synthesis parameters to improve stability and effectiveness of nanoparticles, exploring mechanistic pathways of biological action, and conducting in vivo evaluations to validate their safety and therapeutic potential.
