Mapping the proteome landscape of Indian Zebu (Sahiwal) spermatozoa using high-resolution mass spectrometry and in-silico annotation
Authors: Vanya Bhushan, Syed Azmal Ali, Abhishek Parashar, Sudarshan Kumar, Ashok Kumar Mohanty
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractProteomic analysis of sperm cells offers significant insights into proteins’ structural, functional, and localization aspects within biological systems. Sahiwal, a native Indian cattle breed, is well known for its disease resistance, calving ease, and resilience to drought. This study addressed the gap in Sahiwal’s comprehensive sperm proteome profiling data. The research involved the global in-silico quantitative high-resolution mass spectrometry-based protein profiling of Indian Zebu sperm, identifying 4651 sperm proteins. Beyond mere identification, the study characterized these proteins at a sub-organellar level to facilitate a better understanding of their functional attributes. Gene Ontology analysis of sperm proteins facilitated the segregation of proteins based on their function, localization, and mode of action. The study revealed that despite the limited number of organelles, sperm cells encapsulate a wide array of crucial proteins, compensating for the deficiency of organelles through the presence of multifunctional proteins. Most identified sperm proteins actively participate in spermatogenesis, motility, acrosome reaction, capacitation, and seminal plasma binding, directly or indirectly. Notably, the results not only present the highest number of identified bovine sperm proteins but also hold the potential to pave the way for empirical research on sperm functionality, egg-sperm interaction, sperm-sex sorting biomarkers, sperm quality, and bull fertility.Keywords: Spermatozoa, quantitative proteomics, MaxQuant, KEGG, gene ontology, cell organelle

Introduction

The fertilizing ability of sperm depends on properly elucidating all mechanisms required for sperm to fertilize the egg. Thus, the potential of sperm to fertilize an egg is contingent upon correct development and maturation, which includes motility, structural integrity, viability, and the ability to reach the egg. In vitro tests are performed to diagnose problems with sperm function. Published literature describes our understanding of the molecular function of sperm and implies that minor dysfunctions in sperm biology (or a combination of several suboptimal functions) could be one of the causes of male infertility.1 Despite this, limited knowledge is available describing the in-depth analysis of sperm cell proteome profiling. Proteomic profiling of spermatozoa was conducted using high-throughput LC-MS/MS techniques to understand the molecular aspects of sperm biology in bulls and male fertility.

Proteomics is one of the most effective approaches for elucidating the molecular mechanisms behind male fertility.2 Sperm cells, upon maturation, have a restricted capacity for transcription and translation. Additionally, proteins are critical cell effectors.1 Post-translational modifications of proteins also influence the biology and function of sperm cells. Global proteome analysis sheds light on the molecular pathways necessary for sperm biology, aids in discovering potential male fertility indicators, and may contribute to preserving endangered species by identifying key reproductive biomarkers.3,4

Spermatozoa possess unique genetic, cellular, and chromatin properties crucial in fertility and inheritance.5 Due to the absence of most somatic cell features, the complexity of sperm proteome is distinctly different and critical to understand. Sperm cells have minimal cytoplasm and organelles, which makes them distinctive.6,7 Since transcriptionally silent, most cell-surface modifications are guided by post-translational processes mediated by sperm interaction with their surroundings.8 The specialized nature of mature mammalian sperm cells, which stops performing transcription and translation after spermatogenesis, makes their protein species and proteome complexity fascinating to investigate. Sperm cells are suitable for proteomics research because of low protein expression, decreased number of proteins, and the dynamic range of protein abundances relative to other cell types. Furthermore, the sperm cell has fewer membrane proteins than most cell types, owing to its low membrane content.9–12 To study the sperm cell biome, understanding each protein’s physiological activity is crucial.13–15 MS-based sperm proteomics has revealed the heterogeneity of protein populations in distinct subcompartments of sperm, such as sperm surface proteins,16 membrane rafts,17 the nucleus,18 the head region,19 the acrosomal domain,20 and the tail and flagellum.21

Sahiwal (Bos indicus) is India’s premier indigenous milch breed.22 Over many years of selective breeding, the breed has developed disease resistance, heat tolerance, and good performance on low-quality roughages. The breed produces high-quality semen and milk, making it economically valuable. The demand for elite males has grown significantly due to a scarcity of confirmed bulls with superior sperm qualities for continuing a successful breeding program.23 To the best of our knowledge, the presented article provides a comprehensive profile of the spermatozoa proteome of Sahiwal cattle of Indian origin. It serves as a complete reference database for the genetic resources of Indian cattle.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The National Dairy Research Institute’s Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) examined and approved the animal experiment (NDRI). All experiments followed IAEC-NDRI rules and regulations (Ethics Approval Number: 41-IAEC-18-75; 27th January 2018). No animals were injured or harmed during the study.

Sample collection

Fresh semen ejaculates were collected from the Artificial breeding research facility of ICAR-NDRI, Karnal. A total of ten test animals (10 Sahiwal bulls) were selected (n = 10) for sample collection. These bulls were in good health and compliance with regular management procedures.

All bulls were 30–40 months of age and weighed 390–550 kgs on average. Bulls were fed high-nutrition quality forage (green fodder) supplemented with mineral supplements, concentrated feed (oils and seeds), and water. Bulls were housed in well-ventilated, clean pens undergoing routine vaccination, deworming, and medical checkups. All bull ejaculates were collected on the same day (the first ejaculate of the day for all bulls). The average semen volume collected from each bull was 4–6 ml. To avoid any seasonal variation (heat stress) in semen quality, ejaculates were collected in December. Semen ejaculates were collected utilizing an artificial vagina (AV standard temperature 42 °C) over a dummy or teaser animal. Sperm morphological and physiological parameters were evaluated post-semen collection.

Assessment of membrane integrity

The integrity of the sperm membrane was assessed using the CFDA-PI method.24 Firstly, 0.5 mg/mL CFDA was added to 10 million washed spermatozoa (5 mL) and incubated for 15 minutes at 37 degrees Celsius in the dark. Then, 2 mL of 0.3 mg/ml PI was added for 2 minutes. The spermatozoa with green fluorescence indicated branes, while those with red fluorescence indicated damaged membranes.

Assessment of capacitation

The state of sperm capacitation was evaluated using chlortetracycline (CTC).25 Briefly, an equal amount of CTC solution (3.87 mg per 10 mL in PBS) and 10 million sperm suspensions were incubated at 37° degrees Celsius for 15 minutes in the dark. 3 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) was added to the previously described solution and then incubated for two minutes. Spermatozoa were counted in three patterns: F (uncapacitated), B (capacitated), and AR (acrosome responded).

The process of sperm capacitation was assessed using chlortetracycline (CTC).25 In brief, a mixture of CTC solution (3.87 mg per 10 mL in PBS) and 10 million sperm suspensions were incubated at 37 degrees Celsius for 15 minutes in the dark. Next, 3 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) was added to the solution and incubated for two minutes. Spermatozoa were categorized into three patterns: F (uncapacitated), B (capacitated), and AR (acrosome reacted).

Assessment of acrosome reaction

Fluorescein isothiocyanate peanut agglutinin (FITC-PNA) staining was employed to evaluate acrosome response.24 Ten million washed sperm pellets were incubated with 5 Ml of 0.025 mg/Ml FITC-PNA at 370 degrees Celsius for 10 minutes, followed by 1 Ml of 0.3 mg/Ml PI in the dark for 3 minutes. Three patterns were counted: nonviable with intact acrosome (FITC-PNA negative and PI positive), viable with intact acrosome (FITC-PNA negative and PI negative), and feasible with damaged acrosome (FITC-PNA positive and PI negative).

For the fluorescent tests described above, sperm smears were produced on glass slides, covered with a coverslip, and a drop of DABCO was applied to prevent the fluorescence from fading. Two hundred spermatozoa were counted per smear using a fluorescent microscope (Olympus BX51, USA).

Separation of spermatozoa from seminal plasma and sperm cell protein extraction

The pooled semen samples (n = 10) were centrifuged (4500 RPM/10 min/25 °C) to separate spermatozoa from seminal plasma. The cell pellet was washed three times in 1X PBS. Obtained cell pellets was at −80 °C (for later use). Only live sperm cells were taken for the following experiment. 1 ml of sperm cell was layered upon 45% and 90% (1 ml each) percoll density gradient solution (Sigma, diluted with 1.5 M Tris-cl, pH 7.3) followed by low centrifugation (750 × g/10 minutes/25 °C), to separate the viable cells from dead sperm cells. Using Neubaurs Chamber (Marienfeld, Germany), 100x 106 sperm cells were counted and resuspended in 1 volume of ice-cold PBS (phosphate-buffered saline, pH 7.4, 1X). The obtained cell pellet was washed (wash X 3) with five volumes of ice-cold PBS (1X, pH 7.4). After that, the sperm pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer (2% SDS in 0.1 M Tris buffer, pH-6.8, 18 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), and 1x Protease inhibitor cocktail (Halt-Thermo Scientific) for 30 min at RT with constant shaking. The solution was sonicated on ice for ten bursts (3 sec on, 5 sec off). The lysate was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min, and protein in the soluble fraction was transferred to a fresh tube. Extracted proteins were then precipitated using three volumes of ice-cold acetone for 30 min at −20 °C, centrifuged at 2000 g for 5 min at four °C, washed twice with 70% acetone, and dried out. The dried protein pellet was then subjected directly to a 2D cleanup kit.

Protein quantitation

To eliminate interfering components from sperm protein preparation, a ReadyPrep 2D-Clean Up kit (Bio-Rad) was used, and total protein concentration was determined using a Bradford protein estimation kit (Bio-Rad) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

In-solution digestion and fractionation

Amounts of 300 μg of sperm whole cell lysate (from 10 bulls) were subjected to in-solution digestion. The Ready Prep 2D cleanup kit (BioRad) cleaned the protein sample. All instructions, as mentioned by the manufacturer, were followed. The pellet obtained was resuspended in Ammonium Bicarbonate (ABC, 50 mM in LC-grade water). The sample was reduced using ten mM DTT (60 °C, 45 min) and alkylated using 20 mM IAA (20 min in the dark at room temperature). Both DTT and IAA were prepared fresh in 50 mM ABC solution. The protein solution was subjected to trypsin digestion using modified sequencing grade Trypsin (1:20: trypsin: protein) (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and was incubated overnight at 37 °C. The peptide digest dried using SpeedVac-Vaccum Concentrator (Thermo) can be stored at −20 °C until the fractionation.26 Digested peptides were desalted to remove any MS-interfering contaminants using Pierce™ Peptide Desalting Spin Columns. Digested peptides were re-suspended in 0.1% TFA in water, followed by loading the reconstituted peptides in a conditioned and equilibrated desalting column (as instructed in the user manual). Bound peptides were eluted by 50% ACN and 0.1% TFA. Peptides were dried using SpeedVac and stored at −20 °C. To enable larger coverage of proteome sequencing, peptide samples were fractioned (30 fractions) in an orthogonal dimension according to their hydrophobicity before LCMS/MS analysis using the Pierce High pH Reversed-Phase phase fractionation kit. Peptide samples were again resuspended in 0.1% TFA and were bound to the column (as instructed in the user manual). Protein elution was done using different acetonitrile gradients in 0.1% Triethylamine. The Pierce peptide quantitation assay kit determined peptide concentration and yield before loading in LC-MS. Liquid content was evaporated using SpeedVac, and the dry sample was resuspended in 0.1% formic acid (FA) before LC-MS analysis.

Electrospray ionization Tandem mass spectrometry LC-MS/MS analysis

All proteomic analysis studies used the same biological and technical parameters (for LCMS/MS) as done previously in studies involving mass spectrometry-based proteomic profiling in our laboratory.27 The spermatozoa peptides isolated using the Pierce High pH Reversed-Phase phase fractionation kit (30 fractions) were analyzed by mass spectrometry in duplicate using a qTOF mass spectrometer (Bruker, maXis II HD, Germany) interfaced with an Easy Nano-LC 1200 system (Nano-Advance; Bruker) in copy. Peptides were fed onto a pre-analytical column (2 cm × 75 µm, Acclaim PepMap C18 2 µm) in the mass spectrometer using solvent A (0.1% formic acid) at a 300 nL/min flow rate. Peptides were then separated on an analytical column (50 cm × 75 m ID, Pep Map RSLC C18 2 µ m) using a rising gradient of solvent B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min for 90 minutes. Electrospray created positive ions, and q-TOF was then operated in data-dependent acquisition mode (DDA) to transition between MS and MS/MS collection automatically. A precursor ion TOF-MS survey scan was acquired with a resolution of 75,000 and a range of 300–1,800 m/z. Q1 sequentially chooses the six most intense precursor ions for fragmentation by collision-induced dissociation for MS-MS analysis with a set cycle duration of 3 s and a release time of 2 minutes for the exclusion filter (Data acquisition software, version 24.8; Bruker Daltonics).

Data processing and analysis

For identification of the profiled sperm proteome with high confidence, the vendor file format was evaluated using three distinct search engines: Mascot, Comet, and Tandem. For the Mascot search (Matrix Science, UK, version 2.4.1), the ProteinScape platform was used (version 2.0). The peak lists were created using Hyster (Bruker Daltonics) to convert the spectral data to mgf format (Mascot Generic Files). Resulting mgf files were used to identify proteins utilizing the Bovine Uniprot database and typical contamination proteins for spectroscopic analysis. The MS/MS ion search parameter includes tryptic digested sites with two missed cleavage iron-binding flexible modifications on amino acids—methionine for oxidation, N-terminal acetylation, Glnpyro Glu, and Glu-pyro Glu—while cysteine is modified statically as carboxy amidomethylation. The precursor ion tolerance for peptides was set to 20 ppm, with an MS/MS tolerance of 0.01 Da. The ‘ion score threshold’ was set to 30, removing low-quality matches and establishing a minimum peptide length of six amino acids. A false discovery rate (FDR) of 1% was used to eliminate false-positive identification and boost confidence in equal peptide and protein concentrations. A library of decoy reversed sequences was provided for the computation of FDR. The data was researched in parallel using Comet and Tandem search engines in Trans-Proteomic Pipeline (version 5.1.0) (as we performed a similar analysis in Refs.28–30) Initially, the raw data were converted to open mzML files. The TPP pipeline was used to search the mzML files utilizing the shared database and search parameters. TPP first identifies peptides using the Tandem and Comet search engines. In addition, the PeptideProphet and ProteinProphet algorithms were included in the pipeline to determine the probability values of independently inspected peptides and proteins, respectively. The precise mass model to get a high probability in peptideProphet was used to increase the confidence in peptide association and the likelihood of peptides. Additional protein validation was done using PeptideProphet and ProteinProphet scores, except for proteins with two top-ranked peptides, and peptide probability scores of over 95% were retained. The iProphet algorithm verified and combined all search engine results. This approach takes PeptideProphet spectrum-level findings from numerous LC-MS/MS runs as input and computes a unique probability for a unique peptide sequence (or protein sequence). This architecture permits merging many search engine results into a single result. It considers other promoting determinants, such as sibling trials, the distinction between comparable peptide ions, the number of repeat ion identifications, sibling ions, and sibling modification states. An iProphet probability of more than 0.9999 (>0.999) was accepted as the identification threshold for the protein. Using two search engines, we could confidently identify proteins and their respective peptides. Specifically, we only considered proteins identified in both search engines and removed any proteins identified by only one search engine. This approach allowed us to focus on determining the high confidence of the profiled data. All the graphical analyses were conducted in the R program. All discovered proteins were mapped to UniProt’s bovine chromosomal proteome database and evaluated to provide Circos-compatible input files. The Gene Ontology (GO) categories were examined using the DAVID bioinformatics tools, and for subsequent ontological term charting, only genes with a p-adjusted value (FDR) of less than 0.05 were considered. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD041632.

Bioinformatics and functional analysis

Biological systems operate on a network of interconnected metabolic and signaling pathways. Therefore, to discover proteins implicated in fertility, we used Gene Ontology for systems modeling of our proteomic datasets (GO).

Gene Ontology-based functional classification of identified proteins from spermatozoa was carried out using DAVID, a web resource (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/tools.jsp) and STRING version 11.0 (https://string-db.org/)15.

N-terminal signal peptides for proteins were mapped using SignalP version 5.0 (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/SignalP/), membrane proteins helices were identified by TMHMM Server v. 2.0 (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM/) and transmembrane topology and signal peptide was predicted using Phobius (https://phobius.sbc.su.se/). Three different software was used for protein subcellular localization, namely DeepLoc (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/DeepLoc/data.php), MultiLoc (http://www-bs.informatik.uni-tuebingen.de/Services/MultiLoc) and BUSCA (http://busca.biocomp.unibo.it/).

Chromosomal mapping of sperm peptides

All taxed appropriately detected proteins were mapped to the Uniprot bovine chromosomal proteome database (Bos taurus taxed 9931) and analyzed to create files appropriately designed for input to Circos for circular viewing and annotation.

Results

Sperm morphological assessment assays

To understand the bovine sperm proteome, sperm morphological evaluation tests were done. Two hundred sperms were counted per smear for fresh Sahiwal semen. The CFDA/PI analysis indicated that 67% of living sperm cells (L) had an intact membrane, 13% of dying cells (M) had a partly fragmented membrane, and 22% of dead cells (D) had a broken membrane (Figure 1a–b and Graph 1). The FITC/PNA test was used to determine the acrosome reaction status of sperm cells. It was seen that 65 percent of cells were live acrosome intact (LAI), 10% were live acrosome reacted (LAR), 11.5% were dead acrosome intact (DAI), and 20% were dead acrosome reacted (DAR) (Figure 1c and Graph 2). Further CTC assay was done to determine the capacitation status of the sperm cells. It was seen that 60% of sperm cells were non-capacitated (F), 35% were capacitated (B), and 10% were acrosome reacted (AR) (Figure 1d and Graph 3). Temperature and other environmental and physiological parameters are widely known to impact sperm viability. Therefore, a sperm morphological assessment assay, including a cell viability test, was performed to remove any dead or fragmented cell types. Only live, acrosome non-reacted, and non-capacitated sperm cells were taken for proteomics analysis.

Bull spermatozoa protein identification by LC-MS/MS

We studied the in-depth bull spermatozoa proteome through a bottom-up, high-throughput protein identification approach (Figure 2). SDS-PAGE analysis was performed on pooled protein samples from ten animals. All animal-to-animal variations were mitigated through sample pooling, and additional protein samples were obtained to identify the sperm protein profile. We fractionated the extracted protein lysate from indigenous zebu (Sahiwal) sperm samples using an in-solution digestion LC-MS/MS approach. We subjected the sample generated from in-solution tryptic digestion to high-resolution qTOF, nano-LC-MS/MS analysis (Figure 2A).

A critical aspect of assessing current data is the conversion of open file formats and their compatibility with other modules (such as Comet, tandem, and SpectraST) in the Trans Proteomics Pipeline (TPP). A total of 30 fractions were performed, identifying over 5 million spectra, 50,000 peptides, and 4651 proteins (Figure 2B). The uniqueness and confidence in the discovered proteome were additionally validated by cross-mapping the proteome dataset using three separate proteomics software packages (TPP, Protein Scape) (Supplementary Table S1). Only 4651 common proteins across all three software were chosen for further study. All identified proteins were selected with a 1% FDR and protein prophet and prophet values of 0.9999 and 0.095, respectively. The study excluded proteins with a single unique peptide and a single low-quality PSM spectrum (0.5 percent) discovered by decoy search. The final analysis included only proteins with two or more peptide spectral matches. Supplementary Table S1 has a comprehensive list of detected proteins and peptides. This is the most comprehensive bull sperm proteome dataset available to date.

Functional significance and annotation of the bovine sperm proteome

To understand the functional aspect of the sperm proteome, we characterized and curated the subcellular and sub-organizational localization of identified sperm proteins. The confidence of annotation was increased by using multiple organellar location predictor servers. Three servers were used to identify membrane and secretory proteome (TMHMM, SignalP, Phobius). TMHMM 2.0 was used to estimate the transmembrane helix of proteins. Out of 4651 known sperm proteins, 1074 proteins were seen to have one or more transmembrane helices in their structure (Supplementary Table S1). SignalP identified 510 proteins containing the signal peptides (Supplementary Table S1). Transmembrane sperm proteins are instrumental in sperm-egg interaction. In contrast, N-terminal zone-induced acrosome reaction, capacitation induction, and fertilization, whereas N-terminal signal peptides play an essential role in protein targeting and translocation. Phobius software provided a combined transmembrane topology and signal peptide prediction for the identified sperm proteins. Eight hundred seventy-five proteins with transmembrane helices and signaling proteins were discovered (Supplementary Table S1).

Four individual eukaryotic protein subcellular locators were used to study the sub-organellar protein repository. Identified sperm proteins were divided into subcategories based on their cellular location. The results were obtained through a) DeepLoc, a profound neural network-based protein location predictor, which predicts the localization of proteins to 9 subcellular compartments with high confidence. DeepLoc indicated: cell membrane (740 proteins), cytoplasm (1544 proteins), nucleus (1074 proteins), mitochondrion (497 proteins), endoplasmic reticulum (263 proteins), lysosome (36 proteins), Golgi bodies (75 proteins), peroxisomes (61 proteins), and extracellular (436 proteins) (Supplementary Table S3); b) MULocDeep, a deep learning framework predicted localization of proteins to 9 subcellular compartments with high confidence: cytoplasm (2280), plasma membrane (343), mitochondria (447), peroxisomes (189), nucleus (639), lysosome (67), Golgi body (86), extracellular (319), and endoplasmic reticulum (244) (Supplementary Table S3); c) GOASVM, a gene ontology and support vector machine-based localization predictor predicted localization of proteins to 11 subcellular compartments with high confidence: Golgi body (155), nucleus (1250), cytoskeleton (96), extracellular (431), cytoplasm (960), mitochondria (404), plasma membrane (802), endoplasmic reticulum (115), centriole (33), lysosome (37) and peroxisome (6) (Supplementary Table S3); d) BUSCA, an integrated subcellular protein locator predicted localization of proteins to 5 subcellular compartments with high confidence: plasma membrane (117), extracellular (643), cytoplasm (1521), nucleus (949), and mitochondria (508) were the locations determined (Supplementary Table S3). A comprehensive organelle protein probability map was generated using four protein locators, and common proteins with a high probability of occurrence were documented: cytoplasm (350), mitochondria (175), nucleus (234), plasma membrane (20), extracellular (142), Golgi body (9), endoplasmic reticulum (43), lysosome (1) and peroxisome (1) proteins were located with high probability (Figure 3a–i). Interestingly, it has been reported that sperm cells are devoid of the Golgi body, lysosomes, endoplasmic reticulum, and peroxisomes. However, we got some organelle-specific proteins in our study like Golgi body-specific proteins: Bifunctional heparan sulfate N-deacetylase, Galactose-3-O-sulfotransferase 3, Alpha-N-acetylneuraminide alpha-2,8-sialyltransferase; endoplasmic reticulum specific proteins: Glucose-6-phosphatase 3, Protein disulfide-isomerase, Thioredoxin domain-containing protein 11, Peptidyl-prolyl cis-trans isomerase FKBP10; lysosome specific protein: Putative phospholipase B-like 2; and peroxisome specific protein: Hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase-like protein 2. Identifying organelle-specific proteins in sperm shows how these proteins are essential for certain sperm functions like acrosome reaction, egg-sperm binding, signaling pathways, and ROS activities (Supplementary Table S3).

Functional analysis of spermatozoa proteome

Protein ontologies identified the cellular localization and biological processes associated with the spermatozoa proteome. Proteins were found to be enriched in cell migration, oxidation-reduction process, protein folding, cytoskeleton organization, proteolysis, transport, metabolic function, cell adhesion, spermatogenesis, axoneme assembly, protein stabilization, spermatid development, cell-cell bonding, and transmembrane transport (Figure 4a). Majority of the enriched proteins belonged to the nucleus, mitochondria, centriole, acrosome, axoneme, flagellum and manchette (Figure 4b). Gene ontology resulted in a total of 107 proteins were annotated to acrosome, 193 proteins were annotated to the nucleus, 22 were proteins annotated to centriole, 196 were proteins annotated to mitochondria, 96 proteins were annotated to the cell membrane, 229 proteins were annotated to the cytoplasm, 40 proteins were annotated to axoneme, 7 proteins were annotated to manchette, 34 proteins were annotated to the flagellum, 6 proteins were annotated to principal piece of spermatozoa and 134 proteins were annotated as extracellular proteins. The cellular component GO enriched term identifies 19.7% proteins in the extracellular exosome, 19.30% in the cytoplasm, 7.99% in the mitochondria, 8.4% in the nucleoplasm, and 6.9% in the cytosol. Most proteins functioned in the binding of metal ions (9.88%) and ATP (9.5%). The bulk of proteins (9.16%) are involved in ATP binding, followed by metal ion binding (1.2%), RNA binding (7.05%), protein binding (1.40%), and transcription factor activity (1.90%).

Similarly, most proteins in the KEGG pathway were enriched in metabolic pathways (9.06%), PI3K, Akt, MAPK, and RAS signaling pathways, carbon metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation. Also, several proteins identified were related to Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, disease, focal adhesion, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, and endocytosis. In the SMART term, the majority of proteins are SM00181:EGF (2.3%), SM00179:EGF_CA (1.7%), (SMW382: AAA (1.2%), and SM00360:RRM (1.3%). In Interpro term counts, most of the protein percentage was found in EGF-like, conserved site (2.42%), epidermal growth factor-like domain (2.62%), EGF-like calcium-binding (1.69%), armadillo-type fold (2.43%), P-loop containing nucleoside triphosphate hydrolase (5.9%), and pleckstrin homology-like domain (2.42%). Clustering the proteins regarding organelle specificity, sperm proteins revealed expression in mitochondria, nucleus, acrosome, and plasma membrane. In Reactome term counts, 1.69% of proteins were involved in collagen biosynthesis, followed by metabolism, degradation of beta-catenin by the destruction complex, metabolism of RNA, and ubiquitin-mediated degradation of phosphorylated Cdc25A (Supplementary Table S2).

Chromosomal mapping of bovine proteins

Total spectra and sperm protein identified from the different search engines using the Bos Taurus UniProt database were represented as a circos plot (Figure 5). Identified proteins mapped to all the 29 + X Y chromosomes. An exciting fact uncovered was the uneven distribution of the protein expression from the chromosomes in a physiological stage-specific manner. Chromosomes 20, 24, 27, and 28 showed minimum expression of proteins (43, 46, 40, 39, respectively), while chromosomes 3, 19, 18, 5, and 7 expressed maximum proteins (178, 185, 170, 164, 172 respectively). The average number of proteins per chromosome was 39. To remove any bias in the analysis, the enrichment percentage of all the chromosomes was calculated by dividing the total proteins assigned in the database by the actual number of proteins identified in the mapping analysis. The result obtained was the same. The factors responsible for the uneven distribution of proteins to chromosomes are unknown. We further revealed that no Y spermatozoa proteins were obtained in our analysis. One thousand sixty-seven uncharacterized proteins were annotated to WGS (genome-wide study). These WGS annotated uncharacterized proteins may contribute to unraveling novel sperm proteins involved in sperm maturation, energy metabolism, fertilization, and motility. Fifty-seven proteins were annotated to the X chromosome. Surface sex-specific proteins may play a pivotal role in generating novel sex selection methods. Further sperm-egg interaction-specific proteins like IZUMO, CD9, ADAM2, and ZP2 were identified with high confidence in our analysis. These proteins are crucial for successful fertilization and indirectly affect sperm motility, blocking polyspermy, zona penetration, and sperm orientation during fertilization.

Proteomic crosstalk between sperm organelles reveals sperm functional repertoire

Additionally, we classified genes ontologically and found the majority of proteins involved in ATP production, motility, and energy metabolism. The most frequently used GO keywords were mitochondrial proteins (196), followed by proteins found in the nucleus (193), cytoplasm (189), cell membrane (96), acrosome (107), manchette (7), centriole (22), axoneme (40), flagella (34) and extracellular proteins (133) (Figure 6a). Numerous sperm bioactive proteins have been shown to have several physiological functions. The sperm acrosome contains Golgi body-specific proteins, lysosome-specific proteins such as hyaluronidase, and vacuole-specific proteins, indicating that it evolved from the Golgi apparatus and lysosome. Numerous cell membrane-specific proteins, like IZUMO and SPACA, are involved in spermatogenesis and the acrosome response. The cytoplasm of somatic cells acts as a reservoir for translation factors, glycolysis machinery, and other components. In contrast, most of sperm cytoplasm is lost during nucleus elongation during the last stage of spermatogenesis. Most cytoplasmic proteins participate in cytosol transport. Most sperm nucleus proteins were found to aid in the catabolic process of proteasomal proteins. Sperm mitochondria serve as an energy pump, supplying the sperm with the required energy. The sperm tail’s functional unit is the axoneme, which acts as a motor for motility. IFT (intra-flagellar transport) has a role in axoneme formation by transporting accessory structural proteins. Similarly, a manchette is a transient structure that rings the spermatid nucleus as it elongates. It is involved in nuclear shaping and protein transport into the growing tail, a process called intra-manchette transport (IMT) (Figure 6).

Notably, we also reported the identification of 25 testis-specific proteins (Table 1). Notably, mammalian fertilization is a complicated procedure since the location of semen deposition is far from that of fertilization. During a successful fertilization event, several energy-dependent processes like motility, capacitation, hyperactivation, and acrosome response occur in a coordinated form. Mitochondria are critical in the synthesis of ATP through OXPHOS (oxidative phosphorylation). Mitochondrial proteins such as cytochrome C, hexokinase component VIb, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) 29 have been confidently reported in our work. Glycolysis occurs in the flagellum’s head and fibrous sheath and is a primary source of energy generation. Our study identified glycolytic proteins such as phosphoglucokinase isomerase, phosphofructokinase, lactate dehydrogenase, and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPD). While a spermatozoon may exist solely on glycolytic energy, OXPHOS is essential for spermatid differentiation and maturation. Spermatozoa’s preferred metabolic route for energy generation is very species-specific. Glycolysis is required for fertilization in mouse, rat, and human spermatozoa but not in bovine sperm.13 In spermatozoa, metabolic energy generation may be confined to glycolysis or mitochondrial respiration or may occur in combination, depending on the surrounding environment and substrate availability (Figure 6).

Discussion

We chose the Sahiwal breed among several other species in India for this study due to its exceptional milk production, ease of calving, resistance to eye cancer and ticks, drought and bloat tolerance, mellow temperament, and thriving ability in tropical areas.22 Identifying protein markers of fertility could better evaluate a male’s potential for successful reproduction. This work is the most comprehensive characterization of the bovine sperm proteome complexity to date. There are only a few characterization studies targeting the entire sperm cell proteome of the Indicus breed.38 Thus, we designed this study to gain more insight into the spermatozoa proteome of Indian zebu (Sahiwal) cattle, which has never been investigated before. As previously noted, mature sperm lacks the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, peroxisomes, and ribosomes and has lost the ability to express genes (both transcription and translation).50,51 However, by utilizing comprehensive proteome profiling, we were able to resolve the complex sperm proteome coverage and describe a set of proteins belonging to specific organelles, thus expanding the knowledge of the sperm proteome.

The comprehensive proteome analysis enabled us to identify numerous membrane-bound seminal proteins confidently. These include PDC-109,52 BSP proteins such as BSP-A1/A2, BSP-A3, and BSP-30 kDa,53 lactoferrin,54 which is an iron-binding protein, and Spermadhesin (Z13).55 These proteins play essential roles in controlling sperm capacitation and fertilization processes. We also identified the 14-3-3 protein zeta/delta (YWHAZ), a sperm-coating adaptor protein critical for signaling pathway regulation during spermatogenesis.56 Another protein we identified is the epididymis secretory sperm binding protein (TRAI), an epididymal-derived sperm wrapping protein. This protein is often detected in somatic cell melanosomes and sperm cytoplasm and is usually associated with sperm maturation, nourishment, and storage.57

Male infertility is predominantly caused by sperm motility disorders, as defects in the sperm flagella often lead to failure in fertilization.58 Therefore, it is crucial to understand the proteins that are involved in sperm motility in the Sahiwal breed. Several proteins associated with cilia and flagella (CFAPs: CFAP61, CFAP69, CFAP65),59 dynein family proteins (DNAH17, DNAH10, DNAH11),60 SUN5, TTC39B, and TSGA10 have been demonstrated to be essential for sperm flagellum morphogenesis and biogenesis.61–65 Reduced sperm motility has also been linked to deficiencies in outer dense fiber proteins 1, 2, 3, and 4 (ODF1, ODF2),66 sperm-associated antigens (SPAG8, SPAG17, SPAG6), testis-specific gene-10 (TSGA10),67 Ropporin-associated tail protein-1 (ROPN1), ROPN1-like protein (ROPN1L).68 Additionally, proteins such as Tetkin (TEKT5)69 and members of the metalloprotein family, including MMP11 and MMP14, have been shown to influence sperm motility and acrosomal proteolysis.

A critical process in the development of sperm is capacitation, which is a biological reaction that enables sperm to fertilize the egg. Capacitation-inducing proteins such as glutathione S-transferase Mu 3 (GSTM3),70 ACTL7A protein,71 HSP90 protein,72 and calcium-binding tyrosine phosphorylation-regulated protein (CAB-YR)73 play a significant role in this process. Subsequently, following capacitation, a second metabolic step known as the acrosome reaction occurs in the sperm head. The successful acrosome reaction requires acrosomal proteins such as ACR, ACRBP, ARV1, and SPACA family proteins (SPACA 9, SPACA 3), angiotensin-converting enzyme-3 (ACE3), and GLIPR1-like protein 1. In cases where these proteins are defective, the acrosome fragments can result in male infertility.74

The cell differentiation process that leads to the development of mature and differentiated sperm cells is called spermatogenesis.75 The SPATA gene family (SPATA7, SPATA3, SPATA19, SPATA48) regulates spermatogenesis and sperm production.76 Proteins such as spermatid maturation protein 1,77 Na/K-ATPase,78 and spermatid-specific protein 1 are necessary for the structural integrity of sperm flagella, sperm maturation, and sperm reproduction.79 Through proteomic investigation, it has been discovered with high confidence that the TEX gene family (TEX37, TEX55, TEX43, and TEX50), which is expressed in the testis, plays a role in influencing spermatogenesis.80

Our proteomics analysis revealed the proteins involved in sperm-egg fusion associated with the cell membrane. In mammalian fertilization, the most crucial event is the fusion of the sperm and egg. Izumo is a surface protein of the sperm cell that is necessary for sperm-egg fusion.81 Sperm membrane proteins, such as Sp17,82 CD9,83 SPACA3,84 and GLIPR1-like protein 1,85 are also involved in this process. The disintegrin and metalloproteinase domain-containing protein 2 (ADAM2)86 is responsible for the binding of sperm to eggs and the maturation of sperm. Zona pellucida binding protein 2 (ZP2) is required for oocyte-sperm recognition and zona pellucida penetration. It also acts as an animal’s primary inhibitor of polyspermy.87

Ankyrins (ANKRD), tubulin, actin, and motor proteins are the primary cytoskeletal proteins in sperm. ANKRD31 has been shown to assist in spermatogenesis,88 while tubulin (α, β, and γ-tubulin) and actin proteins aid in sperm motility and nuclear remodeling.89 Two major metabolic routes for ATP synthesis in sperm cells are glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS).90 In vitro, a high glucose environment generates hyperactivated motility, while a low glucose environment causes linear motility. Mitochondrial proteins such as NDUFB7, NDUFA8, COX5A, and ATP synthase subunit gamma are involved in energy synthesis.91 Spermatogenesis-associated protein 1992 and Prohibitin (PHB),93 which are membrane proteins, are known to influence sperm motility by altering the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP).

The proteomic landscape of zebu cattle sperm is being explored lately to understand molecular mechanisms that impact fertility. In a recent study, proteomic profiling of Sahiwal bulls identified proteins involved in sperm motility, capacitation, and acrosome reaction, which are essential for successful fertilization. Notably, this study uncovered potential fertility markers related to oxidative stress regulation, sperm-egg interaction, viability, and membrane integrity, underscoring the need for more targeted research in zebu breeds.94 Similarly, another group investigated the sperm proteome of Indonesian Ongole bulls (Bos indicus), revealing proteins linked to heat tolerance, an essential adaptation for fertility in tropical climates. Their comparative analysis between Ongole and Bos taurus bulls highlighted distinct differences in stress response proteins, illustrating the influence of environmental factors on sperm proteomics.95 However, they emphasized the absence of Bos indicus data and the importance of exploring breed-specific proteomic profiles, particularly in tropical breeds such as Sahiwal and Gir. Together, these studies highlight the need for further investigation into the unique proteomic adaptations of tropical cattle to environmental stressors and reproductive challenges, aiming to bridge the gap in our understanding of fertility across diverse cattle breeds.

While proteomic methods have made significant progress in identifying the expression of thousands of proteins in sperm cells and their subcellular localization, technical challenges still need to be overcome to achieve a comprehensive measure of gene expression. For instance, this study may have missed identifying low-abundance proteins in the presence of high-abundance proteins or quantifying high/low isoelectric point proteins, proteins with low solubility or high/low molecular masses, and hydrophobic proteins. Additionally, quantitative MS has higher sensitivity but does not provide information about the intact proteins, tracing the origin of tryptic peptides to the expressed gene complex. Moreover, protein isoforms pose another challenge in protein inference. Therefore, while this study provides valuable information that can promote a better understanding of disease conditions and aid in therapeutic drug designing and semen sexing, it is essential to validate the identified proteins through biochemical techniques like immunocytochemistry and western blot to generate a more conclusive Indicus proteome map.

The current proteomics study limitations and potential solutions

The sperm proteome is highly complex and contains diverse proteins, including many low-abundance proteins that may be important for fertility or disease. However, identifying and quantifying all the proteins in the sperm proteome can be challenging due to its complexity. Additionally, the presence of proteoforms in sperm, resulting from post-translational modifications or alternative splicing, can further complicate the analysis of the sperm proteome using traditional proteomics techniques. Identifying and characterizing these proteoforms in sperm can be challenging and require specialized techniques. Furthermore, distinguishing between different protein species with similar or identical amino acid sequences is also a limitation of proteomics techniques when analyzing the sperm proteome. This can result in misidentification of proteins or the inability to detect specific proteins altogether. Overall, the rules of current bottom-up proteomics techniques in analyzing the sperm proteome must be considered, and specialized approaches may be required to overcome these limitations and obtain a comprehensive understanding of the sperm proteome.

Several possible solutions exist to overcome these limitations when analyzing the sperm proteome. One solution is to use advanced proteomics techniques such as multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) or selected reaction monitoring (SRM), which allow for the targeted detection and quantification of specific proteins and proteoforms. Another solution is to combine proteomics with other complementary techniques, such as transcriptomics and metabolomics, which can provide additional information on gene expression and metabolic pathways. This can help to validate the identification of specific proteins and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the sperm proteome. Furthermore, advances in bioinformatics tools and databases can help identify and characterize proteoforms and distinguish between protein species with similar amino acid sequences. Combining advanced proteomics techniques, complementary technologies, and bioinformatics tools can help overcome proteomics’ limitations when analyzing the complex sperm proteome.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study provides a comprehensive analysis of the sperm proteome in Indian zebu (Sahiwal) cattle, leading to the confident identification of 4561 proteins. Most of these proteins were involved in immunological regulation and host defense, suggesting that Sahiwal cattle may have a higher disease resistance and adaptability level than other breeds. Multiple server-based protein localization techniques enabled the elucidation of the function and interrelationship of many proteins involved in various cellular processes, including spermatogenesis, motility, egg surface recognition, and energy consumption. Notably, the identified proteins related to male fertility represent a potential resource for future research, particularly in identifying fertility-related protein biomarkers. Importantly, this study represents the first comprehensive report on the Sahiwal sperm proteome in Indian zebu cattle, which may serve as a valuable resource for the conservation and genetic improvement of indigenous Indian cattle breeds through the development of molecular tools.
