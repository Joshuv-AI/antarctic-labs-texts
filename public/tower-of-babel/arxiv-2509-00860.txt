Abstract
. We investigate geometric invariants of cuspidal edges on focal surfaces of regular surface. In particular, we shall clarify the sign of the singular curvature at a cuspidal edge on a focal surface using singularities of parallel surface of a given surface satisfying certain conditions.

1.
Introduction

For a regular surface in Euclidean
3
-space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
its parallels and focal surfaces can be examined.
While the initial surface is non-singular,
its parallels and focal surfaces generally have singularities (
[

From the viewpoint of singularity theory of smooth mappings,
singularities that appear on parallel surfaces are
Legendrian singularities,
while those on focal surfaces
are Lagrangian singularities (
[

10
]
).
In both cases, a cuspidal edge is known to be a generic singularity (
[

]
).
It has been established that there is a relationship between
singularities of parallels and focal surfaces of a regular surface (
[
8
,
26
]
).

Conversely, there are numerous studies on
surface with cuspidal edge singularities
from the perspective of differential geometry (cf.
[

).
At a cuspidal edge, several geometric invariants are introduced (
[
16
,
14
,
22
]
).
In particular, geometric invariants called the limiting normal curvature and
the singular curvature play a crucial role.
It has been established that the limiting normal curvature is associated with
the boundedness of the Gaussian curvature at a cuspidal edge (
[
22
,
16
]
).
While the singular curvature relates to convexity or concavity of cuspidal edge.
More precisely, the following fact is known (
[
22
, Theorem 3.1]
):
The Gaussian curvature is bounded near a cuspidal edge
if and only if the limiting normal curvature vanishes along the edge.
Moreover, if the Gaussian curvature takes non-negative values
near a cuspidal edge, then the singular curvature is non-positive at the cuspidal edge.
For a pseudo-spherical front, that is,
a surface with certain singularities whose Gaussian curvature is
negative constant on the set of regular points,
the Gaussian curvature of its focal surface is bounded (cf. (
A.7
)).
In addition, we see the following assertions (
[
27
]
):
If the initial pseudo-spherical front has a cuspidal lips (resp. cuspidal beaks),
then the singular curvature of the focal surface is positive (resp. negative)
at the corresponding cuspidal edge.
Thus it is natural to ask that
how singularities of fronts (parallel surfaces) relate
to geometric properties of cuspidal edges on focal surfaces.

In this paper, we investigate the limiting normal curvature and
the singular curvature at cuspidal edges of focal surfaces of
regular surfaces.
In particular, we deal with a surface whose Gauss map has singularity at some point.
In that case, the Gaussian curvature of parallel surfaces
satisfies certain bounded condition
(see Propositions
3.3
and
3.4
).
Moreover, the limiting normal curvature defined at a corank one singular point
on the parallel surface vanishes in the case (Proposition
3.5
).
Comparing conditions for singularities of parallel surfaces
and focal surfaces, it holds that
the focal surface is a cuspidal edge
if the parallel surface is a swallowtail, a cuspidal edge or a cuspidal beaks
(Proposition
4.1
).
For a curvature line parametrized surfaces,
we give explicit representations for
the limiting normal curvature and the singular curvature
at a cuspidal edge of the focal surface (Propositions
4.3
and
4.5
).
In particular for the singular curvature, we shall show that
relation between the sign of the singular curvature at a cuspidal edge
of the focal surface and singularities of parallel surface (Theorem
4.6
).

2.1.1.
Surfaces parametrized by curvature line coordinates

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a domain.
Let
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an immersion
and
\nu
its unit normal vector.
Then we call the image of
f
or
f
itself a
surface
.
We assume that
f
is a surface without umbilic point in the following.
Then we can take a
curvature line coordinate system

(u,v)
on
U
.
In this case, it holds that

\left\langle{f_{u}},{f_{v}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\nu_{u}},{f_{v}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\nu_{v}},{f_{u}}\right\rangle=0,

where
\left\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\right\rangle
is the canonical inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

(\cdot)_{u}=\partial(\cdot)/\partial u
and
(\cdot)_{v}=\partial(\cdot)/\partial v
.
Moreover, the first and second fundamental forms of
f

can be written as

\operatorname{I}=Edu^{2}+Gdv^{2},\quad\operatorname{II}=Ldu^{2}+Ndv^{2},

where

E=\left\langle{f_{u}},{f_{u}}\right\rangle,\quad G=\left\langle{f_{v}},{f_{v}}\right\rangle,\quad L=-\left\langle{f_{u}},{\nu_{u}}\right\rangle,\quad N=-\left\langle{f_{v}},{\nu_{v}}\right\rangle.

Then the Gauss and Weingarten formulas are as follows:

(2.1)

\displaystyle f_{uu}

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{1}_{11}f_{u}+\Gamma^{2}_{11}f_{v}+L\nu=\frac{E_{u}}{2E}f_{u}-\frac{E_{v}}{2G}f_{v}+L\nu,

\displaystyle f_{uv}

\displaystyle=f_{vu}=\Gamma^{1}_{12}f_{u}+\Gamma^{2}_{12}f_{v}=\frac{E_{v}}{2E}f_{u}+\frac{G_{u}}{2G}f_{v},

\displaystyle f_{vv}

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{1}_{22}f_{u}+\Gamma^{2}_{22}f_{v}+N\nu=-\frac{G_{u}}{2E}f_{u}+\frac{G_{v}}{2G}f_{v}+N\nu,

\displaystyle\nu_{u}

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{1}f_{u},\quad\nu_{v}=-\kappa_{2}f_{v},

where
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are
principal curvatures

of
f
given by

\kappa_{1}=\frac{L}{E},\quad\kappa_{2}=\frac{N}{G}.

Moreover, Codazzi equations can be written as

(2.2)

L_{v}=\frac{1}{2}(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})E_{v},\quad N_{u}=\frac{1}{2}(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})G_{u}.

Lemma 2.1
.

Under the above setting, we have

\frac{E_{v}}{2E}=\Gamma^{1}_{12}=\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{v}}{\kappa_{2}-\kappa_{1}},\quad\frac{G_{u}}{2G}=\Gamma^{2}_{12}=\frac{(\kappa_{2})_{u}}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}}.

Proof.

We consider
\kappa_{1}
.
By the definition, we have

(\kappa_{1})_{v}=\frac{L_{v}}{E}-\frac{LE_{v}}{E^{2}}=\frac{L_{v}}{E}-\kappa_{1}\frac{E_{v}}{E}.

By Codazzi equations (
2.2
), it holds that

(\kappa_{1})_{v}=\frac{E_{v}}{2E}(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})-\kappa_{1}\frac{E_{v}}{E}=\frac{E_{v}}{2E}(\kappa_{2}-\kappa_{1}).

Thus we obtain the assertion for
\kappa_{1}
.
For
\kappa_{2}
, one can show in a similar way.
∎

By (
2.1
) and Lemma
2.1
,
it holds that

f_{uv}=-\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{v}}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}}f_{u}+\frac{(\kappa_{2})_{u}}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}}f_{v}.

Definition 2.2
.

For a surface
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}

parametrized by curvature line coordinate system
(u,v)
,

•

a point
p\in U
is said to be a
\partial_{u}
-ridge point

(resp.
\partial_{v}
-ridge point
)
if
(\kappa_{1})_{u}(p)=0
(resp.
(\kappa_{2})_{v}(p)=0
);

•

a point
p\in U
is said to be a
\partial_{u}
-sub-parabolic point

(resp.
\partial_{v}
-sub-parabolic point
)
if
(\kappa_{2})_{u}(p)=0
(resp.
(\kappa_{1})_{v}(p)=0
).

Ridge points on a surface are originally introduced by Porteous to investigate
singularities of distance squared functions on the surface (
[
20
]
).
For details of ridge and sub-parabolic points on a surface,
see
[
4
,
3
,

,

,

]
.

2.1.2.
Curvature line frame

Let
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a regular surface
without umbilic point.
Let
\nu
be a unit normal vector to
f
.
We then take a curvature line coordinate system
(u,v)
on
U
.
We define maps
\bm{e}_{i}\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
(
i=1,2
) by

(2.3)

\bm{e}_{1}=\frac{f_{u}}{|f_{u}|},\quad\bm{e}_{2}=\frac{f_{v}}{|f_{v}|}.

Then it holds that

\left\langle{\bm{e}_{i}},{\bm{e}_{j}}\right\rangle=\delta_{ij},\quad\nu=\bm{e}_{1}\times\bm{e}_{2},

where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta.
Thus the triplet
\{\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{2},\nu\}
forms an orthonormal frame along
f
.
We call this frame the
curvature line frame
along
f
.

Lemma 2.3

(cf.
[
5
]
)
.

With the above condition, we have

\begin{pmatrix}\bm{e}_{1}\\
\bm{e}_{2}\\
\nu\end{pmatrix}_{u}=\begin{pmatrix}0&x_{1}&x_{2}\\
-x_{1}&0&0\\
-x_{2}&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{e}_{1}\\
\bm{e}_{2}\\
\nu\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{pmatrix}\bm{e}_{1}\\
\bm{e}_{2}\\
\nu\end{pmatrix}_{v}=\begin{pmatrix}0&y_{1}&0\\
-y_{1}&0&y_{3}\\
0&-y_{3}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{e}_{1}\\
\bm{e}_{2}\\
\nu\end{pmatrix},

where

x_{1}=\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{v}|f_{u}|}{(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})|f_{v}|},\quad x_{2}=\kappa_{1}|f_{u}|,\quad y_{1}=\frac{(\kappa_{2})_{u}|f_{v}|}{(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})|f_{u}|},\quad y_{3}=\kappa_{2}|f_{v}|.

Proof.

For
\nu
, it holds that

\nu_{u}=-\kappa_{1}f_{u}=-\kappa_{1}|f_{u}|\bm{e}_{1},\quad\nu_{v}=-\kappa_{2}f_{v}=-\kappa_{2}|f_{v}|\bm{e}_{2}

by Weingarten formulas (
2.1
).
By (
2.3
) and Lemma
2.1
,
(\bm{e}_{1})_{u}

and
(\bm{e}_{2})_{u}
can be calculated as

\displaystyle(\bm{e}_{1})_{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{uu}}{|f_{u}|}-\frac{f_{u}(|f_{u}|)_{u}}{|f_{u}|^{2}}=\frac{1}{|f_{u}|}\left(\Gamma^{1}_{11}f_{u}+\Gamma^{2}_{11}f_{v}+L\nu\right)-\Gamma^{1}_{11}\bm{e}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{E_{v}}{2G|f_{u}|}f_{v}+\frac{L}{|f_{u}|}\nu=\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{v}|f_{u}|}{(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})|f_{v}|}\bm{e}_{2}+\kappa_{1}|f_{u}|\nu,

\displaystyle(\bm{e}_{2})_{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{uv}}{|f_{v}|}-\frac{f_{v}(|f_{v}|)_{u}}{|f_{v}|^{2}}=\frac{1}{|f_{v}|}\left(\Gamma^{1}_{12}f_{u}+\Gamma^{2}_{12}f_{v}\right)-\Gamma^{2}_{12}\bm{e}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{v}}{2E|f_{v}|}f_{u}=-\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{v}}{(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})|f_{v}|}f_{u}=-\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{v}|f_{u}|}{(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})|f_{v}|}\bm{e}_{1}.

By similar calculations, we get

\displaystyle(\bm{e}_{1})_{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{uv}}{|f_{u}|}-\frac{\left\langle{f_{u}},{f_{uv}}\right\rangle}{|f_{u}|^{2}}=\frac{\Gamma^{2}_{12}}{|f_{u}|}f_{v}=\frac{G_{u}}{2G|f_{u}|}f_{v}=\frac{(\kappa_{2})_{u}|f_{v}|}{(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})|f_{u}|}\bm{e}_{2},

\displaystyle(\bm{e}_{2})_{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{vv}}{|f_{v}|}-\Gamma^{2}_{22}\bm{e}_{2}=\frac{1}{|f_{v}|}\left(\Gamma^{1}_{22}f_{u}+N\nu\right)=-\frac{G_{u}}{2E|f_{v}|}f_{u}+\frac{N}{|f_{v}|}\nu

\displaystyle=-\frac{(\kappa_{2})_{u}G}{(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})E|f_{v}|}f_{u}+\kappa_{2}|f_{v}|\nu=-\frac{(\kappa_{2})_{u}|f_{v}|}{(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})|f_{u}|}\bm{e}_{1}+\kappa_{2}|f_{v}|\nu.

Thus we have the assertions.
∎

By this lemma, it holds that

(\bm{e}_{1})_{u}\times(\bm{e}_{1})_{v}=-\frac{\kappa_{1}(\kappa_{2})_{u}|f_{v}|}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}}\bm{e}_{1},\quad(\bm{e}_{2})_{u}\times(\bm{e}_{2})_{v}=-\frac{\kappa_{2}(\kappa_{1})_{v}|f_{u}|}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}}\bm{e}_{2}.

Therefore sets of singularities of
\bm{e}_{1}
and
\bm{e}_{2}
are

(2.4)

\displaystyle S(\bm{e}_{1})

\displaystyle=\{q\in U\mid\kappa_{1}(q)=0\}\cup\{q\in U\mid(\kappa_{2})_{u}(q)=0\},

\displaystyle S(\bm{e}_{2})

\displaystyle=\{q\in U\mid\kappa_{2}(q)=0\}\cup\{q\in U\mid(\kappa_{1})_{v}(q)=0\},

respectively.
Since
S(\nu)=\kappa_{1}^{-1}(0)\cup\kappa_{2}^{-1}(0)
,
if
p\in S(\nu)
, then
p\in S(\bm{e}_{1})
or
p\in S(\bm{e}_{2})
.
See
[
5
]
for singularities of
frame mappings on regular surfaces.

2.2.
Fronts

To investigate parallel surfaces below, we here recall the class of surface with certain singularities
called fronts (
[
1
,
28
]
).
Let
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a
C^{\infty}
map,
where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is an open set.
Then
f
is said to be a
front

if there exists a
C^{\infty}
map
\nu\colon U\to\mathbb{S}^{2}

such that

•

\left\langle{df_{q}(\bm{v})},{\nu(q)}\right\rangle=0
for any
q\in U
and
\bm{v}\in T_{q}\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and

•

the pair
(f,\nu)\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
gives an immersion.

We call the map
\nu
a
unit normal vector field
of
f
.
By definition, an immersion
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a front.

Let
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a front with a unit normal vector field
\nu
.
Then a point
p\in U
is said to be a
singular point
of
f

if
f
is not an immersion at
p
, that is,
\operatorname{rank}df_{p}<2
.
We denote by
S(f)
the set of singular points of
f
.
We define a function
\lambda\colon U\to\mathbb{R}
by

(2.5)

\lambda(u,v)=\det(f_{u},f_{v},\nu)(u,v).

We then call
\lambda
the
signed area density function
of
f
.
It is clear that
S(f)=\lambda^{-1}(0)
holds.
Moreover, a function
\tilde{\lambda}
which is non-zero proportional to
\lambda

is called an
identifier of singularities
of
f
.
A singular point
p\in S(f)
of
f
is said to be of
non-degenerate

if
(\tilde{\lambda}_{u},\tilde{\lambda}_{v})(p)\neq(0,0)
.
Otherwise, it is said to be of
degenerate
.
For a non-degenerate singular point
p\in S(f)
,
there exist a neighborhood
V(\subset U)
of
p

and a regular curve
\gamma\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to V
with
\gamma(0)=p

such that
\tilde{\lambda}(\gamma(t))=0
for
t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
.
This curve
\gamma
is called a
singular curve
for
f

passing through
p=\gamma(0)
.
On the other hand, for a singular point
p\in S(f)

satisfying
\operatorname{rank}df_{p}=1
, there exists
a non-zero vector field
\eta
on
U

such that
\eta_{p}
generates the kernel of
df_{p}
.
This vector field
\eta
is called a
null vector field
for
f
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
f_{i}\colon(U_{i},p_{i})\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
(
i=1,2
) be
C^{\infty}
map germs.
Then
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are
\cal{A}
-equivalent

if there exist diffeomorphism germs
\varphi\colon(U_{1},p_{1})\to(U_{2},p_{2})

and
\Phi\colon(\mathbb{R}^{3},f_{1}(p_{1}))\to(\mathbb{R}^{3},f_{2}(p_{2}))
such that

\Phi\circ f_{1}=f_{2}\circ\varphi
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a front.
Then

(1)

f
is a
cuspidal edge
at

if

is
\cal{A}
-equivalent to the germ
(u,v)\mapsto(u,v^{2},v^{3})

(2)

is a

at

if

is
\cal{A}
-equivalent to the germ

(u,v)\mapsto(u,4v^{3}+2uv,3v^{4}+uv^{2})

\cal{A}

.

2.2.1.

Let
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a front and
\nu
its unit normal vector.
Assume that
f
is a cuspidal edge at

.

\gamma(t)
passing through

\eta

\hat{\gamma}=f\circ\gamma

2.3.
Rational order of functions

Let
\cal{E}_{2,p}
be the local ring consisting of

C^{\infty}
function germs
(\mathbb{R}^{2},p)\to\mathbb{R}
at
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Then
h\in\cal{E}_{2,p}
is said to be of
order
n
at
p

if there exists an integer
n(\geq 1)

such that
h\in\cal{M}_{2,p}^{n}
and
h\not\in\cal{M}_{2,p}^{n+1}
,
where
\cal{M}_{2,p}=\{h\in\cal{E}_{2,p}\mid h(p)=0\}

is the unique maximal ideal of
\cal{E}_{2,p}
and
\cal{M}_{2,p}^{n}
denotes
the
n
th power of
\cal{M}_{2,p}
.
In this case, we write
\operatorname{ord}_{p}{h}=n
.
If
h\not\in\cal{M}_{2,p}
, then
\operatorname{ord}_{p}{h}=0
.
If there is a non-negative integer
n

such that
\operatorname{ord}_{p}{h}=n
, then
h
is said to be of
finite order
.

Let
h_{1},h_{2}\colon(\mathbb{R}^{2},p)\to\mathbb{R}
be function germs.
Let
Z=h_{2}^{-1}(0)
.
Define a functions germ
g=h_{1}/h_{2}\colon(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus Z,p)\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then the
rational order

\operatorname{ord}_{p}{g}
of
g
at
p
is defined by

\operatorname{ord}_{p}{g}\coloneqq\operatorname{ord}_{p}{h_{1}}-\operatorname{ord}_{p}{h_{2}}.

If
h_{1}\in\cal{M}_{2,p}^{\infty}
, then we set
\operatorname{ord}_{p}{g}=\infty
.
If
\operatorname{ord}_{p}{g}=0
, then
g
is called
rationally bounded
at
p
,
and if
\operatorname{ord}_{p}{g}=1
, then
g
is called
rationally continuous
at
p

(
[
15
,
16
]
).
The notions of rational boundedness and rational continuity
are originally defined in
[
16
]
.

We note that since the property
h\in\cal{M}_{2,p}^{n}
does not depend on the choice
of coordinate system,
the order and the rational order do not depend on the choice of coordinate system.

Example 2.7
.

Let
h_{1},h_{2}\colon(\mathbb{R}^{2},0)\to\mathbb{R}
be function germs given by

h_{1}(u,v)=uv,\quad h_{2}(u,v)=u^{2}-v^{2}.

Then we see that
h_{i}\in\cal{M}_{2,0}^{2}
and
h_{i}\notin\cal{M}_{2,0}^{3}
for
i=1,2
.
Thus for
i=1,2
, it holds that
\operatorname{ord}_{0}{h_{i}}=2
.
The set
Z=h_{2}^{-1}(0)
is
Z=\{(u,v)\mid u=\pm v\}
.
We then define a function germ
g\colon(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus Z,0)\to\mathbb{R}
by

g(u,v)=\frac{h_{1}(u,v)}{h_{2}(u,v)}=\frac{uv}{u^{2}-v^{2}}.

This cannot be extended as a bounded function on
(\mathbb{R}^{2},0)
.
However, the rational order
\operatorname{ord}_{0}{g}
of
g
at
0
is

\operatorname{ord}_{0}{g}=2-2=0.

This implies that
g
is rationally bounded at
0
.

3.
Parallel surfaces

We consider the parallel surfaces of a regular surface
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}

without umbilic point.
For a fixed
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,
a
parallel surface

f^{t}
of
f
at distance
t

is defined as

(3.1)

f^{t}(u,v)\coloneqq f(u,v)+t\nu(u,v),

where
\nu
is a unit normal vector to
f
.
Let us take curvature line coordinates
(u,v)
.
Then we see that

(3.2)

f^{t}_{u}=(1-t\kappa_{1})f_{u},\quad f^{t}_{v}=(1-t\kappa_{2})f_{v}

by Rodrigues’ formula.
Thus
\nu
can be also a unit normal vector to
f^{t}
.
Moreover,
f^{t}
is a front for any
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
(
[
28
]
).
By (
2.1
), the first and second fundamental forms of
f^{t}
are

(3.3)

\operatorname{I}^{t}=(1-t\kappa_{1})^{2}Edu^{2}+(1-t\kappa_{2})^{2}Gdv^{2},\quad\operatorname{II}^{t}=(1-t\kappa_{1})\kappa_{1}Edu^{2}+(1-t\kappa_{2})\kappa_{2}Gdv^{2}.

Therefore the Gaussian and mean curvatures for
f^{t}
are

(3.4)

K^{t}=\frac{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}{(1-t\kappa_{1})(1-t\kappa_{2})}=\frac{K}{1-2tH+t^{2}K},\quad H^{t}=\frac{H-tK}{1-2tH+t^{2}K},

where
K
and
H
are the Gaussian and mean curvatures of
f
, respectively.

For each fixed
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,
the set of singular points
S(f^{t})
of
f^{t}
is

S(f^{t})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{2}\{(u,v)\in U\mid\kappa_{i}(u,v)=1/t\}.

Since
f
has no umbilic points,

\bigcap_{i=1}^{2}\{(u,v)\in U\mid\kappa_{i}(u,v)=1/t\}=\emptyset.

If we take
t=1/\kappa_{1}(p)
,

S(f^{t})
coincides with
\kappa_{1}^{-1}(\kappa_{1}(p))
.
This implies that the
constant principal curvature lines
form
the set of singular points of a parallel surface (see
[
8
]
).

3.1.
Singularities of parallel surfaces

In the following of this section, we fix
t=1/\kappa_{1}(p)
.
Then we see that

f^{t}_{u}(p)=0
and
f^{t}_{v}(p)\neq 0
by (
3.2
).
Thus a point
p
is a corank one singular point of
f^{t}
.
Moreover, we set

(3.5)

\tilde{\lambda}(u,v)=\kappa_{1}(u,v)-\kappa_{1}(p).

Then
\tilde{\lambda}^{-1}(0)=S(f^{t})
.
Thus
\tilde{\lambda}
is an identifier of singularities of
f^{t}
.
In addition,
\partial_{u}
give a null vector field for
f^{t}
.
By Fact
2.6
, we have the following immediately.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
8
, Theorems 3.4 and 3.5]
)
.

Let
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a regular surface without umbilic point
and
f^{t}
the parallel surface of
f
at distance
t=1/\kappa_{1}(p)
.
Take a curvature line coordinate system
(u,v)
on
U
for
f
.

(1)

f^{t}
is a cuspidal edge at
p

if and only if
(\kappa_{1})_{u}\neq 0
at
p
.

(2)

f^{t}
is a swallowtail at
p

if and only if
(\kappa_{1})_{u}=0
,
(\kappa_{1})_{v}\neq 0
and
(\kappa_{1})_{uu}\neq 0
at
p
.

(3)

f^{t}
is a cuspidal butterfly at
p

if and only if

(\kappa_{1})_{u}=(\kappa_{1})_{uu}=0
,
(\kappa_{1})_{v}\neq 0
and
(\kappa_{1})_{uuu}\neq 0
at
p
.

(4)

f^{t}
is a cuspidal lips at
p

if and only if
(\kappa_{1})_{u}=(\kappa_{1})_{v}=0
and

\det\operatorname{Hess}(\kappa_{1})>0
at
p
.

(5)

f^{t}
is a cuspidal beaks at
p

if and only if
(\kappa_{1})_{u}=(\kappa_{1})_{v}=0
,

(\kappa_{1})_{uu}\neq 0
and

\det\operatorname{Hess}(\kappa_{1})<0
at
p
.

Corollary 3.2
.

With the same situations in Proposition
3.1
,
we have the following.

(1)

If the parallel surface
f^{t}
is
one of a cuspidal edge, a swallowtail or a cuspidal butterfly at
p
,
then
\operatorname{ord}_{p}\tilde{\lambda}=1
, where
\tilde{\lambda}
is the function as in (
3.5
).

(2)

If
f^{t}
is either a cuspidal lips or a cuspidal beaks at
p
,
then
\operatorname{ord}_{p}\tilde{\lambda}(p)=2
.

Proof.

We take a curvature line coordinate system
(u,v)
.
We assume that
p
is the origin
0
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Then we remark that

\cal{M}_{2,0}=\left\langle{u,v}\right\rangle_{{\cal{E}_{2,0}}},\quad\cal{M}_{2,0}^{2}=\left\langle{u^{2},uv,v^{2}}\right\rangle_{{\cal{E}_{2,0}}},\quad\cal{M}_{2,0}^{3}=\left\langle{u^{3},u^{2}v,uv^{2},v^{3}}\right\rangle_{{\cal{E}_{2,0}}}

hold (
[
10
]
).

Under the above setting, we consider the first assertion.
Assume that
f^{t}
is one of a cuspidal edge, a swallowtail or a cuspidal butterfly
at
p
.
Then it holds that
\tilde{\lambda}(p)=0
and
(\tilde{\lambda}_{u}(p),\tilde{\lambda}_{v}(p))\neq(0,0)
.
Thus we see that
\tilde{\lambda}\in\cal{M}_{2,0}
.
Hence there exist
h_{1},h_{2}\in\cal{E}_{2,0}
such that

\tilde{\lambda}(u,v)=uh_{1}(u,v)+vh_{2}(u,v).

Since
(\tilde{\lambda}_{u}(p),\tilde{\lambda}_{v}(p))\neq(0,0)
,
it follows that
(h_{1}(p),h_{2}(p))\neq(0,0)
.
Therefore
h_{1}\notin\cal{M}_{2,0}
or
h_{2}\notin\cal{M}_{2,0}
.
This implies that
\tilde{\lambda}\notin\cal{M}_{2,0}^{2}
.
Thus we have
\operatorname{ord}_{p}\tilde{\lambda}=1
.

We next consider the case where
f^{t}
is either a cuspidal lips
or a cuspidal beaks at
p
.
Then
\tilde{\lambda}
satisfies

\tilde{\lambda}(p)=\tilde{\lambda}_{u}(p)=\tilde{\lambda}_{v}(p)=0
.
Thus we see that
\tilde{\lambda}\in\cal{M}_{2,0}^{2}
.
Moreover, since
\det\operatorname{Hess}(\tilde{\lambda})(p)\neq 0
,
it holds that
\tilde{\lambda}\notin\cal{M}_{2,0}^{3}
.
Hence we find
\operatorname{ord}_{p}\tilde{\lambda}=2
.
∎

3.2.
Behavior of the Gaussian curvature

We consider behavior of the Gaussian curvature
K^{t}
of
the parallel surface
f^{t}
of a regular surface
f
at a distance
t=1/\kappa_{1}(p)
.
When
f^{t}
has non-degenerate singularity, the following assertion holds.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a regular surface
and
f^{t}
the parallel surface with
t=1/\kappa_{1}(p)
.
Suppose that
f^{t}
is one of a cuspidal edge,
a swallowtail or a cuspidal butterfly at
p
.
Then the Gaussian curvature
K^{t}
is rationally bounded at
p

if and only if
\kappa_{2}
vanishes at
p
.
Moreover,
K^{t}
is bounded near
p

if and only if
\kappa_{2}
vanishes on
S(f^{t})
.

Proof.

By
[
16
, Corollary C]
,

K^{t}
is rationally bounded at
p

if and only if the Gauss map
\nu
of
f^{t}
is singular at
p
.
This is equivalent to the condition that
\kappa_{2}(p)=0
because

\kappa_{1}(p)\neq 0
.
Thus the first assertion holds.
Moreover,
K^{t}
is bounded near
p

if and only if the second fundamental form of
f^{t}

vanishes on
S(f^{t})
(
[
22
, Theorem 3.1]
).
By (
3.3
), it holds that

\operatorname{II}^{t}=(1-t\kappa_{2})\kappa_{2}dv^{2}

on
S(f^{t})
.
Thus the second assertion holds.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a regular surface
whose parallel surface
f^{t}
is a cuspidal lips
or cuspidal beaks at
p
, where
t=1/\kappa_{1}(p)
.
Then the Gaussian curvature
K^{t}
of
f^{t}
is rationally bounded at
p

if and only if
\operatorname{ord}_{p}\kappa_{2}=2
.
Moreover,
K^{t}
is bounded near
p
if and only if
the set of parabolic points
\kappa_{2}^{-1}(0)
contains
S(f^{t})
.

Proof.

By the assumption and Corollary
3.2
,
we see that the function
\tilde{\lambda}
as in (
3.5
)
satisfies
\operatorname{ord}_{p}\tilde{\lambda}=2
.
By (
3.4
) and
t=1/\kappa_{1}(p)
,
the Gaussian curvature
K^{t}
of
f^{t}
can be written as

K^{t}=\frac{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}{t^{2}(\kappa_{1}(p)-\kappa_{1})(\kappa_{1}(p)-\kappa_{2})}=\kappa_{1}(p)^{2}\frac{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}{\tilde{\lambda}(\kappa_{2}-\kappa_{1}(p))},

it follows that the rational order
\operatorname{ord}_{p}K^{t}
of
K^{t}
satisfies

\operatorname{ord}_{p}K^{t}=0
at
p
if and only if
\operatorname{ord}_{p}\kappa_{2}=2
.
This implies that
K^{t}
is rationally bounded at
p

if and only if
\operatorname{ord}_{p}\kappa_{2}=2
.
The boundedness is also proved.
∎

3.3.
The limiting normal curvature

We investigate the limiting normal curvature at a singular point of
f^{t}
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a regular surface without umbilic point
and
\nu
its unit normal vector.
Suppose that the principal curvature
\kappa_{1}
does not vanish on
U
.
If the parallel surface
f^{t}
with
t=1/\kappa_{1}(p)

satisfies
\operatorname{rank}df^{t}_{p}=1
, then the limiting normal curvature

\kappa_{\nu}^{t}
of
f^{t}
satisfies

\kappa_{\nu}^{t}=\frac{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}}

at
p
.

Proof.

We take a curvature line coordinates
(u,v)
on
U
.
We first consider the case where the parallel surface
f^{t}
is a cuspidal edge at
p
.
An identifier of singularities
\tilde{\lambda}
for
f^{t}

is given by (
3.5
).
By Proposition
3.1
,
there exists a function
u(v)
such that

\tilde{\lambda}(u(v),v)=0
.
We set a curve
c(v)=(u(v),v)
, and
define
\hat{c}(v)=f^{t}(u(v),v)
.
Direct calculations show

(3.6)

\displaystyle\hat{c}^{\prime}(v)

\displaystyle=f^{t}_{u}(c(v))u^{\prime}(v)+f^{t}_{v}(c(v))=\left(1-\frac{\kappa_{2}(c(v))}{\kappa_{1}(c(v))}\right)f_{v}(c(t)),

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}^{\prime}(v)

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{1}(c(v))f_{u}(c(v))u^{\prime}(v)-\kappa_{2}(c(v))f_{v}(c(v)),

where
{}^{\prime}=d/dv
and
\hat{\nu}(v)=\nu(u(v),v)
.
Thus we have

\kappa_{\nu}^{t}(v)=-\frac{\left\langle{\hat{c}^{\prime}(v)},{\hat{\nu}^{\prime}(v)}\right\rangle}{|\hat{c}^{\prime}(v)|^{2}}=\frac{\kappa_{1}(c(v))\kappa_{2}(c(v))}{\kappa_{1}(c(v))-\kappa_{2}(c(v))}.

We next show the case where
p
is a non-degenerate singular point of
f^{t}

other than a cuspidal edge.
In this case, we get
(\kappa_{1})_{u}(p)=(\kappa_{1})_{uu}(p)=0

and
(\kappa_{1})_{v}(p)\neq 0
.
Thus by the implicit function theorem,
there exists a function
v(u)
such that
\kappa_{1}(u,v(u))-\kappa_{1}(p)=0
.
Let us set
c(u)=(u,v(u))
and
\hat{c}(u)=f^{t}(u,v(u))
.
Then it holds that

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{c}}(u)

\displaystyle=f^{t}_{u}(c(u))+f^{t}_{v}(c(u))\dot{v}(u)=\left(1-\frac{\kappa_{2}(c(u))}{\kappa_{1}(c(u))}\right)f_{v}(c(u))\dot{v}(u),

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\nu}}(u)

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{1}(c(u))f_{u}(c(u))-\kappa_{2}(c(u))f_{v}(c(u))\dot{v}(u),

where
\hat{\nu}(u)=\nu(u,v(u))
and
\dot{}=d/du
.
Therefore we get

\displaystyle|\dot{\hat{c}}(u)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\kappa_{1}(c(u))-\kappa_{2}(c(u)))^{2}}{\kappa_{1}(c(u))^{2}}\dot{v}(u)^{2}G(c(u)),

\displaystyle-\left\langle{\dot{\hat{c}}(u)},{\dot{\hat{\nu}}(u)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{2}(c(u))(\kappa_{1}(c(u))-\kappa_{2}(c(u)))}{\kappa_{1}(c(u))}\dot{v}(u)^{2}G(c(u)).

Hence we have

\kappa_{\nu}^{t}(p)=\lim_{u\to u_{0}}-\frac{\left\langle{\dot{\hat{c}}(u)},{\dot{\hat{\nu}}(u)}\right\rangle}{|\dot{\hat{c}}(u)|^{2}}=\frac{\kappa_{1}(p)\kappa_{2}(p)}{\kappa_{1}(p)-\kappa_{2}(p)}.

We finally deal with the case where
p
is a degenerate corank one singular point of
f^{t}
.
Since
f^{t}_{u}(p)=0
and
f^{t}_{v}(p)\neq 0
,
the limiting normal curvature can be calculated as

\kappa_{\nu}^{t}(p)=\frac{\left\langle{f^{t}_{vv}},{\nu(p)}\right\rangle}{|f^{t}_{v}(p)|^{2}}

(see
[
16
, p.253]
).
By (
3.2
), we have

f^{t}_{vv}=-t(\kappa_{2})_{v}f_{v}+(1-t\kappa_{2})f_{vv}.

Thus we get

|f^{t}_{v}(p)|^{2}=\frac{(\kappa_{1}(p)-\kappa_{2}(p))^{2}}{\kappa_{1}(p)^{2}}G(p),\quad\left\langle{f^{t}_{vv}(p)},{\nu(p)}\right\rangle=\frac{\kappa_{2}(p)(\kappa_{1}(p)-\kappa_{2}(p))}{\kappa_{1}(p)}G(p),

where we used
N(p)=\kappa_{2}(p)G(p)
.
Therefore
\kappa_{\nu}^{t}(p)
can be written by

\kappa_{\nu}^{t}(p)=\frac{\kappa_{1}(p)\kappa_{2}(p)}{\kappa_{1}(p)-\kappa_{2}(p)}.

Hence we have the assertions.
∎

By this proposition, we get the following.

Corollary 3.6
.

With the same assumptions in Proposition
3.5
,
the limiting normal curvature

\kappa_{\nu}^{t}
of
f^{t}
vanishes at
p

if and only if
\nu
is singular at
p
.
Moreover,
\bm{e}_{2}
is also singular at
p
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.5
,

\kappa_{\nu}^{t}(p)=0
if and only if
\kappa_{2}(p)=0

under the assumptions.
On the other hand,

\nu
is singular at
p

if and only if
\kappa_{2}(p)=0
in this case.
Moreover, by (
2.4
),

\bm{e}_{2}
is singular at
p
if
\kappa_{2}(p)=0
.
Thus we get the conclusions.
∎

4.
Focal surfaces

Let
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a regular surface without umbilic point
parametrized by a curvature line coordinate system
(u,v)
.
Let
\kappa_{i}

(i=1,2)
be principal curvatures for
f
.
Then the maps

(4.1)

C_{i}\coloneqq f+\frac{1}{\kappa_{i}}\nu\colon U\setminus\kappa_{i}^{-1}(0)\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\quad(i=1,2)

are called
focal surfaces
(or
caustics
) of
f
.
By direct calculations, we see that

(4.2)

\displaystyle(C_{1})_{u}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{u}}{\kappa_{1}^{2}}\nu,\quad(C_{1})_{v}=\left(1-\frac{\kappa_{2}}{\kappa_{1}}\right)f_{v}-\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{v}}{\kappa_{1}^{2}}\nu,

\displaystyle(C_{2})_{u}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\kappa_{2}}\right)f_{u}-\frac{(\kappa_{2})_{u}}{\kappa_{2}^{2}}\nu,\quad(C_{2})_{v}=-\frac{(\kappa_{2})_{v}}{\kappa_{2}^{2}}\nu.

Therefore
\bm{e}_{1}
(resp.
\bm{e}_{2}
) as in (
2.3
)
can be taken as a unit normal vector to
C_{1}
(resp.
C_{2}
).
Thus focal surfaces are frontals in this case.
Moreover, the signed area density functions for
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are

(4.3)

\displaystyle\lambda^{C_{1}}

\displaystyle=\det((C_{1})_{u},(C_{1})_{v},\bm{e}_{1})=\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{u}}{\kappa_{1}^{2}}\left(1-\frac{\kappa_{2}}{\kappa_{1}}\right)|f_{v}|,

\displaystyle\lambda^{C_{2}}

\displaystyle=\det((C_{2})_{u},(C_{2})_{v},\bm{e}_{2})=\frac{(\kappa_{2})_{v}}{\kappa_{2}^{2}}\left(1-\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\kappa_{2}}\right)|f_{u}|,

respectively.
Since
\kappa_{1}\neq\kappa_{2}
on
U
,
the sets of singular points of
C_{i}
(
i=1,2
) are

S(C_{1})=\{q\in U\mid(\kappa_{1})_{u}(q)=0\},\quad S(C_{2})=\{q\in U\mid(\kappa_{2})_{v}(q)=0\}

(cf.
[
10
,
20
]
).
For a point
p\in S(C_{1})
(resp.
p\in S(C_{2})
),
we see that
\operatorname{rank}d(C_{1})_{p}=1
(resp.
\operatorname{rank}d(C_{2})_{p}=1
).
Thus one can take

\eta^{C_{1}}=\partial_{u}
(resp.
\eta^{C_{2}}=\partial_{v}
)
as a null vector field for
C_{1}
(resp.
C_{2}
).
Moreover, by Lemma
2.3
, it holds that

(\bm{e}_{1})_{u}\neq 0
(resp.
(\bm{e}_{2})_{v}\neq 0
)
at
p\in S(C_{1})
(resp.
p\in S(C_{2})
).
Hence
C_{1}
(resp.
C_{2}
) is a front at
p\in S(C_{1})

(resp.
p\in S(C_{2})
) (see
[
28
]
).
For singularities of focal surfaces,
we have the following.

Proposition 4.1

(cf.
[
26
]
)
.

(1)

When the parallel surface
f^{t}
with
t=1/\kappa_{i}(p)

is a cuspidal edge at
p
, then
C_{i}
is regular at
p
.

(2)

When
f^{t}
is either a swallowtail, a cuspidal lips
or a cuspidal beaks at
p
, then
C_{i}
is a cuspidal edge at
p
.

Proof.

We show the case of
C_{1}
.
Let us take a curvature line coordinate system
(u,v)
.
Assume that the parallel surface
f^{t}
with
t=1/\kappa_{1}(p)
of
f
is a cuspidal edge at
p
.
Then by Proposition
3.1
, it holds that
(\kappa_{1})_{u}(p)\neq 0
.
Thus
p\notin S(C_{1})
.
This implies that the first assertion holds.
Next, we assume that
f^{t}
is one of a swallowtail, a cuspidal lips or a cuspidal beaks.
Then by Proposition
3.1
, it holds that

(\kappa_{1})_{u}(p)=0,\quad(\kappa_{1})_{uu}(p)\neq 0,\quad(\kappa_{1})_{v}(p)\neq 0.

On the other hand, since an identifier of singularities for
C_{1}
can be taken as
\tilde{\lambda}^{C_{1}}=(\kappa_{1})_{u}

and
\eta^{C_{1}}=\partial_{u}
is a null vector field for
C_{1}
,
we have
\eta^{C_{1}}\tilde{\lambda}^{C_{1}}=(\kappa_{1})_{uu}
, which does not vanish at
p
by the assumption.
Hence
C_{1}
is a cuspidal edge at
p
by Fact
2.6
.
Therefore we get the second assertion.

For the case of
C_{2}
, one can show in a similar way.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

This proposition implies that
if
f
is a front with a cuspidal lips or cuspidal beaks,
then its (suitable) focal surface is a cuspidal edge
at the corresponding point (cf.
[
26
]
).

4.1.
Geometric invariants at cuspidal edges of focal surfaces

We investigate geometric invariants of focal surfaces at cuspidal edges.
Let
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a regular surface
parametrized by a curvature line coordinate system
(u,v)
.
We then assume that
\kappa_{2}(p)=0

and the focal surface
C_{1}
is a cuspidal edge at
p\in U
.
In this case, we shall consider geometric invariants
called the limiting normal curvature
\kappa_{\nu}^{C_{1}}
and
the singular curvature
\kappa_{s}^{C_{1}}
of
C_{1}
at
p
.

By the assumption,
S(C_{1})=((\kappa_{1})_{u})^{-1}(0)
and
(\kappa_{1})_{uu}(p)\neq 0
hold.
Thus there exists a regular curve
\gamma(v)=(u(v),v)
(
|v-v_{0}|<\varepsilon
)
with
\gamma(v_{0})=p
such that

(\kappa_{1})_{u}(u(v),v)=0
by the implicit function theorem.
The curve
\gamma(v)
is a singular curve for
C_{1}
.

Appendix A
Gaussian curvature of the focal surface

For a surface
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
parametrized
by curvature line coordinates
(u,v)
,
we recall the formula for the Gaussian curvature of the focal surface
C_{1}
.
By (
4.2
), the first fundamental form of
C_{1}
is

(A.1)

\operatorname{I}^{C_{1}}=\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{u}^{2}}{\kappa_{1}^{4}}du^{2}+\frac{2(\kappa_{1})_{u}(\kappa_{1})_{v}}{\kappa_{1}^{4}}dudv+\left(\left(1-\frac{\kappa_{2}}{\kappa_{1}}\right)^{2}G+\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{v}^{2}}{\kappa_{1}^{4}}\right)dv^{2}.

Thus we get

(A.2)

\det\operatorname{I}^{C_{1}}=\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{u}^{2}}{\kappa_{1}^{4}}\left(1-\frac{\kappa_{2}}{\kappa_{1}}\right)^{2}G=\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{u}^{2}(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})^{2}G}{\kappa_{1}^{6}}.

On the other hand, the second fundamental form
\operatorname{II}^{C_{1}}

of
C_{1}
with respect to
\bm{n}
is given by

(A.3)

\operatorname{II}^{C_{1}}=-\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{u}L}{\kappa_{1}^{2}|f_{u}|}du^{2}+\frac{G_{u}}{2|f_{u}|}\left(1-\frac{\kappa_{2}}{\kappa_{1}}\right)dv^{2}.

By Lemma
2.1
, we see that

\frac{G_{u}}{2}=\frac{(\kappa_{2})_{u}G}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}}.

Substituting this for (
A.3
), we obtain

(A.4)

\operatorname{II}^{C_{1}}=-\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{u}L}{\kappa_{1}^{2}|f_{u}|}du^{2}+\frac{(\kappa_{2})_{u}G}{\kappa_{1}|f_{u}|}dv^{2}.

Thus we have

(A.5)

\det\operatorname{II}^{C_{1}}=-\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{u}(\kappa_{2})_{u}GL}{E\kappa_{1}^{3}}=-\frac{(\kappa_{1})_{u}(\kappa_{2})_{u}G}{\kappa_{1}^{2}}.

By (
A.2
) and (
A.5
),
the Gaussian curvature
K^{C_{1}}
of
C_{1}
can be calculated as

(A.6)

K^{C_{1}}=\frac{\det\operatorname{II}^{C_{1}}}{\det\operatorname{I}^{C_{1}}}=-\frac{\kappa_{1}^{4}(\kappa_{2})_{u}}{(\kappa_{1})_{u}(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})^{2}}.

Thus the Gaussian curvature
K^{C_{1}}
of
C_{1}

is bounded near a singular point

((\kappa_{1})_{u})^{-1}(0)\subset((\kappa_{2})_{u})^{-1}(0)

In particular, if

is bounded and takes a non-zero value at

,
then
((\kappa_{1})_{u})^{-1}(0)=((\kappa_{2})_{u})^{-1}(0)
.

Assume that the Gaussian curvature of

Then it holds that
\kappa_{2}=c/\kappa_{1}

Thus we see that
