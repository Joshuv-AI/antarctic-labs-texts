Cognitive Impairment Is Associated with Disrupted Motor Planning in Older Adults
Authors: Gabriel Poirier, Charalambos Papaxanthis, Lucie Lordon, Yannick Béjot, Patrick Manckoundia, France Mourey, Jeremie Gaveau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNeurocognitive disorders (NCD) are frequently accompanied by motor impairments that may precede overt cognitive decline. However, most motor markers remain coarse and do not disentangle global motor deterioration from disruptions of specific motor control processes. Motor planning, a process relying on both motor and cognitive resources, may be particularly vulnerable to cognitive impairment yet has rarely been investigated using process-specific motor control frameworks.MethodsWe assessed motor planning abilities in 57 older adults, including cognitively impaired (CI; n = 27) and healthy adult (HA; n = 30) participants. Using a gravity-related motor planning paradigm, participants performed vertical arm movements requiring different interactions with gravitational torque. Directional asymmetries in movement kinematics were used as a process-specific proxy of motor planning. Movement smoothness was quantified using jerk-based measures as a complementary index of global motor execution. Movement duration was carefully controlled across groups and directions, and age effects were accounted for in the analyses.ResultsMovement duration did not differ between groups. Compared with HA participants, the CI group exhibited reduced directional asymmetries, indicating altered motor planning. Within the CI group, the magnitude of directional asymmetries was positively correlated with cognitive status, as assessed by the Mini-Mental State Examination. In contrast, although movement smoothness was globally reduced in CI participants, jerk-based measures were not associated with cognitive status after controlling for age and movement duration.ConclusionThese findings suggest that cognitive impairment in older adults is specifically associated with a disruption of motor planning processes rather than a uniform degradation of motor execution. By dissociating global motor deterioration from planning-related decompensation, this study highlights the value of process-specific motor control measures for understanding sensorimotor alterations in NCD and their potential relevance for early detection.

Introduction

Neurocognitive disorders (NCD) in the aging population pose a major challenge for healthcare systems. Given the limited effectiveness of treatments at advanced stages of these disorders, early detection and intervention are widely recognized as key strategies for improving disease management and clinical outcomes [1–4]. Recent advances in disease-modifying therapies further suggest that individuals treated at earlier stages may derive greater benefit than those receiving treatment at more advanced stages (Shcherbinin et al. [5]; Mintun et al. [6]; Sims et al. [7]; for a review see Rashad et al. [8]). Improving early detection and intervention strategies requires a deeper understanding of the neurobehavioral mechanisms underlying NCD, in particular their impact on sensorimotor function [9–12].

Although NCD are often characterized by the loss of cognitive functions, numerous studies have reported concomitant motor dysfunctions in cognitively impaired individuals, including slower gait speed, gait abnormalities, extrapyramidal signs, and impairments of postural control and fine motor skills (see [9, 11–17]). Growing evidence suggests that the degree of motor deterioration is related to the severity of cognitive impairment [18–21]. Interestingly, motor function deterioration can precede cognitive decline by decades and has been associated with future cognitive deterioration and adverse outcomes [9, 21–27]. Together, these findings support the idea that specific sensorimotor changes may provide valuable insights into the early stages of NCD. This perspective is further reinforced by evidence that pathophysiological brain alterations can begin one to two decades before the clinical onset of symptoms, including in cognitively intact individuals [28–30].

Recent works on age-related sensorimotor deterioration, compensation, and decompensation suggest that researchers need to adopt refined experimental paradigms to disentangle these frequently invoked yet insufficiently understood concepts [11, 31]. The reliable detection of early sensorimotor deterioration likely necessitates the use of fine-grained experimental paradigms. While gross motor measures such as walking speed or maximal force production are valuable in many respects, they may be too coarse to capture the underlying organization of motor control. Such measures reflect the net behavioral outcome of multiple underlying processes – some of which may be impaired, while others serve a compensatory function. To overcome this limitation, we focused on motor planning, a fundamental yet specific component of motor control. Motor planning is thought to govern the selection of the spatiotemporal features of movement, allowing individuals to adopt advantageous strategies from a wide range of possibilities [32]. Although often regarded as a purely motor process, motor planning is known to engage cortical regions associated with higher-order cognitive functions, such as the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, with this engagement being particularly pronounced in older adults [33, 34]. This age-related additional recruitment is commonly interpreted as a compensatory mechanism, enabling the nervous system to preserve functional motor performance despite underlying sensorimotor declines [35–37]. A deterioration of these cognitive resources may therefore lead to decompensation and measurable alterations in motor planning.

To our knowledge, limited research has specifically examined motor planning abilities in cognitively impaired older adults using a process-specific motor control framework. Previous studies have reported associations between cognitive functioning and planning-related aspects of motor behavior in healthy older adults or during simple sequential tasks (e.g., Stöckel et al. [38]; Zhou et al. [39]). However, these studies predominantly relied on experimental paradigms and behavioral measures that conflate anticipatory (feedforward) and reactive (feedback) control processes, making it difficult to unambiguously attribute observed effects to modifications of motor planning itself, particularly in the context of cognitive decline. More recently, work by de Nobile and colleagues [40] has shown that anticipatory processes during reaching movements are disrupted across the Alzheimer’s disease continuum, underscoring the need for focused investigation on motor planning.

Here, we assessed motor planning abilities in cognitively impaired older adults, with a specific focus on how motor planning adapts movement control to the mechanical constraints imposed by the Earth’s gravity. Previous work in healthy young and older adults has shown that motor planning gives rise to distinct kinematic and electromyographic patterns during vertical movements [41–50]. Multiple studies, combining theoretical simulations and experimental manipulations, have interpreted these patterns as motor strategies that optimally exploit gravity effects to minimize muscle effort [44–47, 51]. Directional differences in the kinematics of upward and downward movements – referred to as directional asymmetries – are considered a hallmark of efficient gravity-related motor planning. Building on this framework, we analyzed directional asymmetries in vertical arm movements as a proxy for motor planning abilities in healthy adults (HA) and cognitively impaired (CI) adults. We hypothesized that (i) directional asymmetries would be reduced in the CI group compared to the HA group, reflecting disrupted motor planning, and (ii) within the CI group, directional asymmetries would positively correlate with cognitive status.

In addition to process-specific motor planning metrics, we also quantified movement smoothness using jerk-based measures, which provide a global index of motor execution quality. Previous studies have consistently reported reduced movement smoothness or kinematic features reflecting reduced smoothness in older adults with mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease during fine motor tasks such as handwriting, pointing, or rapid aiming movements [52–55]. These findings indicate that measures of movement smoothness are sensitive to cognitive impairment and sensorimotor deterioration. However, they do not isolate motor planning processes per se, as they reflect the net outcome of multiple feedforward and feedback control mechanisms. Accordingly, in the present study, jerk-based measures were used as complementary indices of global motor control, whereas directional asymmetries were specifically used to probe motor planning processes.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Participants (n = 57) were divided into two groups: CI (cognitively impaired older adults; n = 27; sixteen males), recruited from two geriatric units of Dijon Hospitals (Dijon, France); and HA (healthy older adults, n = 30; fourteen males), recruited from a local community-dwelling older adults’ association. Inclusion criteria were (1) age ≥60 years, (2) absence of Parkinsonian syndrome, pyramidal deficiency, or history of stroke, (3) absence of musculoskeletal deficiency causing pain, balance deficit, or restricted function, and (4) a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of 30 (maximal score) for the HA group and a score comprised between 15 and 29 for the CI group. Experimental Protocol

The experimental protocol was adapted from previous studies investigating gravity-related arm movement control [43, 45–48, 56, 57]. Specifically, we asked participants to perform unilateral single degree-of-freedom vertical arm-pointing movements (rotation around the shoulder joint) toward a visual target in a parasagittal plane with their dominant arm (Fig. 1a). During these movements, the gravitational torque varies as a function of movement direction, whereas inertial demands remain constant. This dissociation makes vertical arm movements a well-established paradigm for investigating gravity-related motor planning. To maximize the effect of gravity on movement control, participants performed movements in two opposite directions from a common starting target: upward and downward. Overall, each participant performed 24 trials (12 trials per direction) over the course of the experiment. Because movement speed is known to vary with both aging and cognitive decline [52, 53], participants were instructed to perform the movements at different speeds centered around their self-selected, natural movement speed. To control for potential confounding effects of movement duration on kinematic measures, the present analyses focused on movement blocks that were selected to ensure comparable movement durations across groups and movement directions. This approach allowed us to minimize the influence of speed-related differences when comparing motor planning and execution metrics between healthy and cognitively impaired participants (see Results and Discussion sections).

a Experimental setup. Participants performed arm-pointing movements between the central target (white sphere) and the upward or downward targets (red and blue spheres) on separate trials. b Illustration of the parameters computed on the velocity profile.

Participants sat on a chair with their trunk aligned with the gravity vector. Three visual targets were presented in the parasagittal plane intersecting the participant’s right shoulder. Targets were displayed at a distance corresponding to the length of the participant’s fully extended arm plus 2 cm. The starting target (Fig. 1a; white sphere) was placed along the anteroposterior horizontal axis crossing the shoulder joint. Consequently, the starting arm position corresponded to a 90° shoulder elevation. The vertical positions of the two other targets (Fig. 1a; blue and red spheres) were individually adjusted for each participant to require a 30° shoulder flexion or extension, corresponding to 120° (upward target) and 60° (downward target) shoulder elevations.

A trial was carried out as follows: the participant positioned their fully extended arm in front of the starting target. After a brief delay (∼2 s), the experimenter verbally indicated the target to be reached using a color code (upward or downward). At the end of the movement, participants were asked to maintain their final arm position (∼2 s) until the experimenter instructed them that they were free to relax their arm. Participants were allowed to rest between trials (∼10 s) to prevent muscle fatigue. Before each block, participants performed a few practice trials (∼5 trials) to familiarize themselves with the task. Upward and downward movements were presented in a randomized order for all participants.

Five reflective markers were placed on the participant’s shoulder (acromion), upper arm (midpoint of the humerus), elbow (lateral epicondyle), wrist (ulnar styloid process), and index finger (fingernail). Three-dimensional marker positions were recorded using an optoelectronic motion capture system (Vicon, Oxford, UK; six cameras; 100 Hz). After system calibration, the spatial measurement error was less than 0.5 mm.

Data Analysis

Kinematic data were processed using custom programs written in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Data processing was consistent with previous studies [43, 45–49, 57]. Marker positions were filtered using a third-order low-pass Butterworth filter (5 Hz cutoff frequency, zero-phase distortion; “butter” and “filtfilt” functions) before computing velocity and acceleration profiles by numerical differentiation (three-point derivative).

Movement onset and offset were respectively defined as the times at which finger velocity rose above and fell below 10% of peak velocity. Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using JASP. Normality of distributions was assessed for all variables using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. To analyze movement duration, LDLJ, and rD-PV, we used repeated-measures analyses of variance with one within-participant factor, Direction (upward vs. downward), and one between-participant factor, Group (HA vs. CI). To specifically probe the relationship between motor planning and cognitive functioning within the CI group, Pearson correlation analyses were performed. The statistical significance threshold was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Movement Duration

Consistent with the experimental design (see Materials and methods), mean (±SD) movement duration was highly comparable between groups (see Fig. 2), amounting to 667 ± 155 ms in the HA group and 666 ± 125 ms in the CI group. Our statistical analysis revealed no significant main effect of Direction (F = 0.62; p = 4.36e−1; ηp2 = 0.01) or Group (F = 2.88e−4; p = 9.87e−1; ηp2 = 5.30e−6). Likewise, the Group × Direction interaction was not significant (F = 0.10; p = 7.47e−1; ηp2 = 0.002).

Relative Duration to Peak Velocity

Building on previous results [45, 46, 48, 57], we compared directional asymmetries (differences between upward and downward) between groups. Statistical analyses revealed significant main effects of Group (F = 15.86; p = 2.02e−4; ηp2 = 0.22) and Direction (F = 57.44; p = 4.27e−10; ηp2 = 0.51). Overall, both groups exhibited directional asymmetries, with shorter rD-PV values for upward than for downward movements (see Fig. 3b; upward vs. downward post hoc comparisons: p = 1.79e−8; Cohen’s d = 1.40 for the HA group and p = 2.35e−3; Cohen’s d = 0.78 for the CI group). However, the magnitude of these directional asymmetries was smaller in the CI group than in the HA group, as indicated by a significant Group × Direction interaction effect (F = 4.58; p = 3.69e−2; ηp2 = 0.09).

Relationship between Cognitive Status and Motor Measures

To further investigate the role of cognitive functioning in motor planning and control, we examined the relationship between MMSE score and LDLJ and MMSE score and rD-PV within the CI group. To this aim, we computed for each participant (i) mean LDLJ, defined as the mean of LDLJ values in upward and downward movements, and (ii) delta rD-PV, defined as the difference between the mean rD-PV value in downward movements and in upward movements (delta rD-PV = rD-PVDown − rD-PVUp), reflecting the magnitude of the directional asymmetry. Participants’ age was included in the model as a covariate using partial correlation analyses to control for a possible confounding effect. LDLJ was significantly correlated with age (r = 0.65; p = 2.75e−4), whereas delta rD-PV was not (r = −0.13; p = 5.19e−1). The association between LDLJ and MMSE score was not significant (r = 0.08; p = 6.93e−1; see Fig. 3c). In contrast, we observed a significant positive correlation between MMSE score and delta rD-PV (r = 0.43; p = 2.75e−2; see Fig. 3d).

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the relationship between motor planning abilities and cognitive status in older adults. Taking advantage of a gravity-related motor planning framework, we analyzed directional asymmetries in vertical arm movements in healthy (HA) and CI older adults. Our results showed reduced directional asymmetries in the CI group compared to the HA group. Additionally, the magnitude of directional asymmetries (i.e., ΔrD-PV) was positively correlated with MMSE score within the CI group. Altogether, these findings suggest that cognitive impairment is associated with a disruption of gravity-related motor planning processes in older adults.

This study is the first to examine specific motor control processes – namely gravity-related motor planning – in cognitively impaired older adults. Such investigations are crucial for advancing our understanding of age-related mobility decline and may ultimately inform prevention and rehabilitation strategies [2, 59], as they enable the identification of potential compensation and decompensation mechanisms [11]. For instance, previous research on gravity-related motor control has shown that this process remains largely preserved during healthy aging, despite age-related structural sensorimotor alterations [48, 50, 60]. Moreover, subtle changes in motor patterns have suggested a possible increase in effort minimization to compensate for these age-related alterations [48, 61, 62]. In this context, the disruption of gravity-related motor planning observed in the CI group may reflect a loss of compensatory mechanisms that are otherwise recruited during healthy aging, i.e., a decompensation phenomenon. Importantly, this interpretation should be considered within a multisystemic framework of motor control. As previously proposed, behavioral changes observed in aging and NCD may reflect deterioration, compensation, or decompensation, and these mechanisms are not always mutually exclusive [11]. Accordingly, reduced directional asymmetries in the CI group should not be interpreted solely as a passive loss of motor planning ability. An alternative, non-mutually exclusive interpretation is that cognitively impaired participants adopted a simplified or more stereotyped motor strategy to reduce the computational, sensory, or executive demands of the task. From this perspective, the reduced tuning of movement kinematics to gravitational constraints may reflect both impaired predictive optimization and an adaptive response to reduced cognitive and multisensory resources. This interpretation reinforces the need to consider motor behavior as the outcome of interacting perceptual, cognitive, sensory, and motor processes, rather than as the expression of a single isolated deficit. Importantly, we do not argue that all age-related differences in motor behavior reflect deterioration; rather, our results suggest that cognitive impairment alters planning-related motor control mechanisms that appear to remain largely preserved, or compensated, during healthy aging.

An important methodological aspect of the present study concerns the interpretation of jerk-based measures of movement smoothness. Previous studies have reported increased movement jerk in individuals with mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer’s disease and have interpreted this finding as reflecting motor deterioration associated with cognitive decline (e.g., [52, 53]). However, jerk is known to be strongly influenced by both age and movement duration, which can confound its relationship with cognitive status. Previous works observed a concomitant increase in movement duration and jerk [52, 53]. In the present study, we ensured that movement duration did not differ between healthy and cognitively impaired participants, and age effects were explicitly controlled for in the analyses. Under these conditions, we found no significant association between jerk and MMSE score within the cognitively impaired group. This result suggests that, when age- and duration-related effects are accounted for, movement smoothness primarily reflects global sensorimotor degradation rather than cognitive impairment per se. In contrast, directional asymmetries – used here as a process-specific index of motor planning – remained significantly associated with cognitive status in the cognitively impaired group. This dissociation highlights a critical distinction between global motor execution measures and planning-related control processes. While movement smoothness appears sensitive to general aging-related factors, planning-related asymmetries selectively covary with cognitive decline, supporting the notion that cognitive impairment preferentially disrupts the ability to optimally plan movements under physical constraints rather than uniformly degrading motor output. Together, these results emphasize the importance of using process-specific motor measures to disentangle execution-related deterioration from planning-related decompensation in aging and NCD.

Our findings also extend previous work investigating the relationship between motor behavior and cognitive functioning in aging. Studies by [38, 39] reported associations between cognitive performance and planning-related aspects of motor behavior in older adults, notably in sequential or action-selection tasks. While these studies provided important initial evidence that motor behavior is sensitive to cognitive status, their experimental paradigms and outcome measures largely conflated anticipatory (feedforward) and reactive (feedback) control processes, limiting the ability to attribute observed effects specifically to motor planning mechanisms. More recently, de Nobile and colleagues [40] demonstrated that anticipatory processes during reaching movements are disrupted across the Alzheimer’s disease continuum, revealing impairments in movement preparation even at relatively early stages of neurocognitive pathology. Although anticipation and motor planning are not equivalent processes, these findings are consistent with the present results and support the idea that planning-related sensorimotor mechanisms are particularly vulnerable to cognitive impairment.

Central to our interpretation is the specificity with which the present task and measures target motor planning. Motor planning and, more generally, motor preparation are not unitary processes. Classical work on preparation for action has emphasized that it may involve several partially overlapping components, including temporal expectancy, attentional set, response selection, and motor presetting [63, 64]. NCD are known to affect several of these processes [65–68], which may therefore contribute to the group differences observed in the present study. More recent accounts, however, define motor planning more specifically as the set of movement-related processes that transform an action goal into a concrete motor command by specifying how the movement will be executed [32]. Importantly, this definition excludes processes related to deciding what the action goal is, such as identifying the relevant target, selecting the intended object or location, or resolving perceptual uncertainty. Instead, once the goal has been specified, motor planning refers to the processes that determine how this goal will be achieved through a particular movement, including its spatiotemporal organization and control policy. Within this framework, our task minimizes several processes related to deciding what action goal should be achieved because the target was explicitly specified before movement onset and remained continuously visible. Nevertheless, the task should not be interpreted as isolating motor planning in a fully modular sense, because specifying how to reach the target necessarily depends on attentional, executive, visuospatial, and sensorimotor processes that contribute to movement preparation and execution. Several features of the paradigm nevertheless reduced demands related to target discovery, goal selection, and online target updating: target locations were simple, static, and continuously visible, and the specific target to be reached was indicated before movement initiation. More importantly, the main outcome measure used here was not global task success or movement speed, but the direction-dependent temporal organization of the velocity profile. This measure was chosen because previous work has shown that directional asymmetries in vertical arm movements emerge from predictive planning processes that incorporate gravitational constraints, rather than from online feedback corrections alone [43, 46, 47]. Thus, directional asymmetries should be viewed as a planning-related marker of predictive motor control under gravitational constraints. Accordingly, the reduced directional asymmetries observed in the CI group suggest that cognitive impairment is associated with a disruption of planning-related predictive motor control. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this disruption may partly reflect alterations in attentional, executive, visuospatial, or sensorimotor processes that contribute to motor planning, rather than an isolated deficit of motor planning in the strictest sense.

Growing behavioral and neurophysiological evidence suggests that premotor and cognitive networks exhibit a strong functional interdependence [69]. In particular, the prefrontal cortex, which is primarily involved in cognitive and executive functions, including memory [70, 71], may serve as an additional resource for motor control. In older adults, changes in prefrontal cortex activity and connectivity have been interpreted as compensatory mechanisms supporting motor performance [36, 37]. Structural and functional alterations of the prefrontal cortex associated with NCD [70, 72] may therefore hinder this compensation process. As neurocognitive pathology affects these compensatory resources, age-related sensorimotor changes may no longer be effectively compensated, becoming more salient and leading to degradation of motor control.

Further research is needed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between motor control and cognition in aging. A limitation of the present study is the absence of direct assessment of sensory and visuospatial functions. Motor planning does not rely solely on motor or cognitive resources but also on the ability to represent target location, body configuration, and the mechanical consequences of movement. This is particularly relevant in the present paradigm because gravity-related motor optimization requires the nervous system to estimate the gravitational torque acting at the shoulder and to integrate this information into the motor plan. Previous research has shown that altering gravity-related cues, including visual, vestibular, and proprioceptive information, can influence the kinematic organization of vertical arm movements [42, 46, 73–77]. Thus, the reduced directional asymmetries observed in the CI group may partly reflect altered perception or multisensory integration of gravity-related cues, rather than a purely motor planning deficit. This possibility is particularly relevant because NCD are frequently associated with visuospatial deficits [9, 78, 79], and emerging evidence also suggests alterations in vestibular processing and multisensory integration in older adults with cognitive impairment [80–82]. However, the available evidence remains limited and heterogeneous, and some measures of verticality perception, such as subjective visual vertical, may be relatively preserved in certain older or cognitively impaired populations [83]. Future studies should therefore combine gravity-related motor planning paradigms with dedicated assessments of visuospatial, vestibular, proprioceptive, and multisensory functions to better disentangle the respective contributions of cognitive, sensory, and motor processes to age- and pathology-related changes in movement control.

Another limitation is the use of the MMSE as a proxy for cognitive status. Although the MMSE is one of the most widely used tools for cognitive disorder screening, it is not the most sensitive instrument for assessing the level or profile of cognitive impairment [84]. The present findings therefore need to be confirmed using comprehensive neuropsychological test batteries and biomarkers of neurocognitive pathology, in conjunction with motor planning measures, in future studies. Because motor planning measures are minimally influenced by age-related peripheral deteriorations (e.g., neuromuscular or cardiorespiratory factors), they may represent promising candidates for the early detection of NCD.
