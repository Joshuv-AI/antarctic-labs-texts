Learning fermionic linear optics with Heisenberg scaling and physical operations
Aria Christensen, Andrew Zhao

Abstract
We revisit the problem of learning fermionic linear optics (FLO), also known as fermionic Gaussian unitaries. Given black-box query access to an unknown FLO, previous proposals required 𝒪 ~ ​ ( n 5 / ε 2 ) \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{5}/\varepsilon^{2}) queries, where n n is the system size and ε \varepsilon is the error in diamond distance. These algorithms also use unphysical operations (i.e., violating fermionic superselection rules) and/or n n auxiliary modes to prepare Choi states of the FLO. In this work, we establish efficient and experimentally friendly protocols that obey superselection, use minimal ancilla (at most 1 1 extra mode), and exhibit improved dependence on both parameters n n and ε \varepsilon . For arbitrary (active) FLOs this algorithm makes at most 𝒪 ~ ​ ( n 4 / ε ) \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{4}/\varepsilon) queries, while for number-conserving (passive) FLOs we show that 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 3 / ε ) \mathcal{O}(n^{3}/\varepsilon) queries suffice. The complexity of the active case can be further reduced to 𝒪 ~ ​ ( n 3 / ε ) \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{3}/\varepsilon) at the cost of using n n ancilla. This marks the first FLO learning algorithm that attains Heisenberg scaling in precision. As a side result, we also demonstrate an improved copy complexity of 𝒪 ~ ​ ( n ​ η 2 / ε 2 ) \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n\eta^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}) for time-efficient state tomography of η \eta -particle Slater determinants in ε \varepsilon trace distance, which may be of independent interest.

Introduction

Fermions are fundamental particles of matter, having half-integer spins and obeying the Pauli exclusion principle. In this work, we consider many-body systems of noninteracting, or
free
, fermions. Such systems are computationally efficient to solve and they serve as invaluable models, for example in descriptions of tight-binding physics or BCS superconductivity
[
10
]
. Algorithmically, free-fermion techniques are at the backbone of many modern electronic and nuclear structure methods, both on classical and quantum computers
[
57
,
27
,
52
]
. The exact solvability of free fermions also has surprising non-fermionic consequences, for example, in connections to the classical Ising model
[
58
]
, interacting quantum spin systems
[
47
,
19
,
16
]
, and matchgate circuits
[
43
,
40
]
.

While the efficient computation of free fermions has been utilized for decades, the rigorous study of their
learnability
has been initiated only recently
[
1
,
54
,
5
]
. In both cases, efficiency ultimately stems from the existence of a complete, polynomial-sized description of these systems. This theme persists among many other well-known families with efficient descriptions, such as Gaussian bosons
[
7
,
18
,
6
,
17
]
, stabilizer and near-stabilizer states
[
51
,
22
,
45
,
25
,
12
]
, and matrix product states
[
13
]
. Broadly speaking, the investigation of such families is motivated by the fact that learning generic, unstructured quantum many-body systems necessarily requires an exponential amount of resources. On the other hand, the compact description of free-fermion circuits has already enabled, among other results, a scalable benchmarking protocol for noisy quantum devices
[
28
]
and a certifiable scheme to demonstrate quantum advantage
[
53
]
.

The primary contribution of this paper is to give efficient algorithms for learning free-fermion unitaries. Let us establish some terminology first: such objects go by a number of different names in the literature, for instance, fermionic {Gaussian unitaries, linear optics, basis rotations, Bogoliubov transformations}. The nomenclature “matchgates” is also common, due to their equivalence with matchgate circuits in one dimension
[
43
,
40
]
. To minimize confusion, for the rest of this paper we shall adopt the terminology
fermionic linear optics
(FLO). This will be convenient because our results delineate between unitaries that conserve particle number, called
passive
FLO, and those that generically do not (
active
FLO). We treat active FLO as a superset of passive FLO, so when we refer to FLO without qualifier we typically mean active FLO unless otherwise specified.

While it is already known that FLOs can be efficiently learned, even in the strongest accuracy metric of diamond distance, prior algorithms are suboptimal in both system size and error dependence
[
53
,
38
,
3
]
. Moreover, we argue that these approaches are in some sense unnatural. First, they involve learning the compact one-body representation of the FLO in an “entry-by-entry” manner. In contrast, optimal unitary tomography algorithms operate by learning “column-by-column,” i.e., by running state tomography on
U|1\rangle,U|2\rangle
, etc.
[
24
]
. This is not only conceptually cleaner, but in fact leads to smaller error bounds (roughly speaking, the errors from learning the full state can be made isotropic, so that they do not compound egregiously as an entrywise estimate would). Second, none of these prior works achieve the gold standard of Heisenberg scaling for estimating unitary processes
[
21
,
70
]
. Finally, they feature resource requirements that are not strictly necessary for the task. This is either in the form of state preparation and measurements that violate fermionic superselection rules
[
59
]
, which we will refer to as unphysical operations; or in the use of entanglement with a large auxiliary system. The algorithms we present in this paper will address all three of these deficiencies.

1.1
Main results

Throughout,
n
denotes the number of fermion modes in our system of interest. We assume a black-box access model: one may query the unknown unitary, potentially multiple times and interleaved with controllable operations, before measuring the system. We say that one such prepare–apply–measure process is a single experimental run.
Because we only deal with pure states and unitary channels in this paper, we make the following convenient but slightly unconventional definitions of trace and diamond distances:

\displaystyle\trdist(|\psi\rangle,|\phi\rangle)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{1-|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{2}},

(1.1)

\displaystyle\diamdist(U,V)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max_{|\psi\rangle}\trdist(U|\psi\rangle,V|\psi\rangle).

(1.2)

Indeed, these coincide with their usual definitions over general mixed states and quantum channels
[
66
]
. We always assume that kets
|\psi\rangle
represent normalized vectors, and
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)
denotes an asymptotic upper bound suppressing polylogarithmic factors.

Learning active FLOs.

Our main contribution is the following result.

Theorem 1.1

(Active FLO learner,
Theorem

6.18
)
.

Let
\Phi
be an FLO. There exists an algorithm which makes
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{4}/\varepsilon)
queries to
\Phi
and uses
\mathrm{poly}(n,1/\varepsilon)
classical computational effort to output an efficient classical description of an FLO
\widehat{\bm{\Phi}}
such that, with high probability,

\diamdist(\widehat{\bm{\Phi}},\Phi)\leq\varepsilon.

(1.3)

Each experiment only requires Fock (standard basis) initial states, implements
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}/\varepsilon)
elementary FLO gates (equivalently, two-qubit matchgates), and uses at most
1
ancillary mode.

This algorithm improves upon the prior art in a number of ways. First and foremost, the query complexity exhibits a
1/\varepsilon
dependence, which is the optimal Heisenberg scaling of quantum metrology. Second, we enjoy improved dependence on system size; the best prior algorithm uses
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{5}/\varepsilon^{2})
queries
[
53
]
. Third, our algorithm only uses Gaussian inputs and operations, which are furthermore physically admissible. We defer a more thorough comparison to prior work in
Section

1.3
.

We remark that the ancillary mode used in this algorithm serves a singular role: to detect a
\pm 1
relative phase that
\Phi
imparts between the even- and odd-parity sectors of the
n
-mode Fock space,
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{F}_{1}
, while obeying superselection rules on the extended
(n+1)
-mode system. This is a rather subtle detail, and in many physically relevant contexts we can drop the ancilla entirely.

Remark 1.2

(Ancilla-free prerequisites)
.

In the algorithm of
Theorem

1.1
, if either:

1.

We can prepare states of the form
\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}
, or

2.

We only demand the output obey (with high probability)

\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{F}_{0}\cup\mathcal{F}_{1}}\trdist(\widehat{\bm{\Phi}}|\psi\rangle,\Phi|\psi\rangle)\leq\varepsilon,

(1.4)

then no ancilla are required.

Broadly speaking,
Item

1
is readily available in qubit-based experiments, while
Item

2
is an operationally meaningful metric for fermionic channels (superselection forbids preparing superpositions between
\mathcal{F}_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}_{1}
in the first place).

Finally, we point out that it is possible to use our techniques to design an efficient algorithm with only
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{3}/\varepsilon)
query complexity. However this approach requires
n
ancilla modes, as it reduces the problem to the tomography of (fermionic) Choi states. This is essentially an improved version of the FLO steps in the algorithms of
[
38
,
3
]
; see
Appendix

B
for formal statements.

Learning passive FLOs.

The algorithm of
Theorem

1.1
is structured such that it learns active and passive components of the FLO in two separate stages (we describe this in
Section

1.2
). In the case that
\Phi
is promised to be passive we can bypass the first stage entirely, yielding an algorithm in its own right for learning passive FLOs.

Theorem 1.3

(Passive FLO learner,
Theorem

5.8
)
.

Let
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}
be a passive FLO. There exists an algorithm which makes
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}/\varepsilon)
queries to
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}
and uses
\mathrm{poly}(n,1/\varepsilon)
classical computational effort to output an efficient classical description of a passive FLO
\widehat{\bm{\Phi}}_{{\mathrm{pas}}}
such that, with high probability,

\diamdist(\widehat{\bm{\Phi}}_{{\mathrm{pas}}},\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}})\leq\varepsilon.

(1.5)

Each experiment only requires Fock initial states, implements
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}/\varepsilon)
elementary FLO gates, and uses at most
1
ancillary mode.

Just as in
Remark

1.2
, if we only care about parity-conserving inputs then we can drop the ancilla. In fact, we can go further if we wish to comport with the number symmetry of passive FLOs. That is, if we restrict to subspaces of fixed particle number, then we only need to apply passive FLO gates throughout the protocol. For fermions this space is
\wedge^{\eta}\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the antisymmetric subspace of
\eta
particles.

Corollary 1.4

(Passive FLO learner within number sectors)
.

Let
0\leq\eta\leq n
be an integer. The algorithm of
Theorem

1.3
can be simplified to produce an output obeying the weaker guarantee,

\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\wedge^{\eta}\mathbb{C}^{n}}\trdist(\widehat{\bm{\Phi}}_{{\mathrm{pas}}}|\psi\rangle,\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}|\psi\rangle)\leq\varepsilon.

(1.6)

This version of the algorithm makes only
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\eta/\varepsilon)
queries, implements
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\eta/\varepsilon)
passive FLO gates per experiment, and uses no ancilla.

As an aside, this result also applies with almost no modification to learning passive
bosonic
linear optics within fixed number sectors. This is because they carry a
\mathrm{U}(n)
-representation that obeys a stability bound identical to that of passive FLOs
[
2
]
.

Improved tomography of Slater determinants.

Our FLO learning algorithm relies heavily on the efficient tomography of fermionic Gaussian states. For pure Gaussian states without number symmetry, the best-known copy complexity is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{3}/\varepsilon^{2})
to achieve
\varepsilon
trace-distance error
[
5
]
. This is essentially sufficient for our active FLO learner, although for technical reasons we describe a variant of the protocol in
Appendix

A
.

In the case of passive FLOs, we mostly restrict to number-conserving Gaussian states known as Slater determinants. In this case, we desire an improved copy complexity when the particle number
\eta\ll n
. We would also like the state tomography protocol to only use number-conserving operations. The best prior result with these desiderata gave a bound of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\eta^{2}/\varepsilon^{4})
copies
[
1
]
, which is insufficient to achieve the query complexity of
Theorem

1.3
. Thus, we need to improve the copy complexity as follows.

Theorem 1.5

(Slater determinant tomography,
Theorems

3.8
and
3.11
)
.

Let
|\psi\rangle
be an
n
-mode,
\eta
-particle Slater determinant. There exists an algorithm which consumes
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n\eta^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
copies of
|\psi\rangle
and uses
\mathrm{poly}(n,\eta,1/\varepsilon)
classical computational effort to output an efficient classical description of a Slater determinant
|\widehat{\bm{\psi}}\rangle
such that

\trdist(|\widehat{\bm{\psi}}\rangle,|\psi\rangle)\leq\varepsilon

(1.7)

with high probability. Each experiment is a single-copy measurement of
|\psi\rangle
and implements
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
elementary passive FLO gates.

In the case of
\eta=1
, the copy complexity can be sharpened to
\mathcal{O}(n/\varepsilon^{2})
(i.e., without any
\log(n)
factors).

From the generic bound
\eta\leq n
, it is clear that our algorithm performs no worse than that of
[
5
]
when applied to Slater determinants. Even in the setting where
\eta=\Theta(n)
, our algorithm still enjoys the advantage of using simpler number-conserving operations. Note that our passive FLO algorithm will not actually need the full strength of
Theorem

1.5
; we use the special
\eta=1
case, simplifying the analysis and avoiding a logarithmic factor. (The active FLO algorithm uses a “perturbative” version applied to Gaussian states; see
Section

6.3
for details.)

1.2
Technical overview

Primer on fermions.

Let us first provide a brief review of fermions in second quantization, FLOs, and Gaussian states. The algebra of fermionic operators on an
n
-mode system is generated by
creation and annihilation operators

a_{j}^{\dagger},a_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
. They obey the canonical anticommutation relations (CAR)
a_{j}a_{k}+a_{k}a_{j}=0
and
a_{j}a_{k}^{\dagger}+a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{j}=\delta_{jk}\mathbb{I}
. Define the number operator
\mathsf{Num}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}
, which has spectrum
\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
. Its
0
-eigenspace is
1
-dimensional, spanned by the so-called vacuum state
|0^{n}\rangle
. We label the vacuum by the all-zeros string to indicate that each mode is unoccupied; the creation operator
a_{j}^{\dagger}
places a fermion into the
j
th mode, for example
a_{j}^{\dagger}|0^{n}\rangle=|0^{j-1}\,1\,0^{n-j}\rangle
. These single-particle Fock states will be rather important in this paper, so we shall use the shorthand
|1_{j}\rangle
. More generally, arbitrary
k
-products of unique creation operators produce all Fock states
|b\rangle
with
|b|=k
particles (where
b\in\{0,1\}^{n}
). These constitute the standard basis of
\wedge^{k}\mathbb{C}^{n}
. The entire Hilbert space for the fermions is then a direct sum of
k
-particle sectors, called
Fock space
:
\mathcal{F}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\wedge^{k}\mathbb{C}^{n}\cong(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
.

If we view arbitrary fermionic operators as (non-commutative) polynomials in the creation and annihilation operators, then FLOs are the class of unitary transformations which preserve polynomial degree. In physics language these are known as
Bogoliubov transformations
. A passive FLO is a unitary operator
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U)
parametrized by a smaller unitary matrix
U\in\mathrm{U}(n)
such that

\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U)^{\dagger}a_{j}\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_{jk}a_{k}.

(1.8)

The map
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}:\mathrm{U}(n)\to\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{F})
is a (projective) representation, which in particular satisfies the group homomorphism property
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U)\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(V)=\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(UV)
.
1
1

1

Technically, projective representations only obey homomorphism up to a potential
(U,V)
-dependent phase; but this phase is global, hence unphysical, so we abuse notation and drop it from our equations. Note that this paper will not use any particularly sophisticated representation theory.
All passive FLOs commute with the number operator; however, the group
\mathrm{U}(n)
is not the broadest possible class of fermionic Bogoliubov transformations.

It is convenient to define the
Majorana operators

\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{2n}
as

\gamma_{j}=a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger},\quad\gamma_{j+n}=-i(a_{j}-a_{j}^{\dagger}).

(1.9)

In this picture, the CAR reduces to a single relation,
\gamma_{p}\gamma_{q}+\gamma_{q}\gamma_{p}=\delta_{pq}\mathbb{I}
. An active FLO is then a unitary
\Phi(Q)
parametrized by an orthogonal matrix
Q\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
such that

\Phi(Q)^{\dagger}\gamma_{p}\Phi(Q)=\sum_{q=1}^{2n}Q_{pq}\gamma_{q}.

(1.10)

As with the passive case,
\Phi:\mathrm{O}(2n)\to\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{F})
is a (projective) homomorphism:
\Phi(Q)\Phi(R)=\Phi(QR)
. Note that
\mathrm{O}(2n)
has two connected components,
\mathrm{SO}(2n)
and
\mathrm{O}^{-}(2n)=\{Q\in\mathrm{O}(2n):\det(Q)=-1\}
. We say that an operator respects superselection if and only if it commutes with the parity operator
\mathsf{Par}=(-1)^{\mathsf{Num}}
. It can be checked that
[\Phi(Q),\mathsf{Par}]=0
if and only if
Q\in\mathrm{SO}(2n)
; otherwise, they anticommute. We can represent passive FLOs in the Majorana basis as

Q=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}U&-\operatorname{Im}U\\
\operatorname{Im}U&\operatorname{Re}U\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SO}(2n)\implies\Phi(Q)=\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U).

(1.11)

Indeed, the set of all such matrices is precisely the group
\mathrm{O}(2n)\cap\mathrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
, which by the two-out-of-three property is isomorphic to
\mathrm{U}(n)
.

A pure
fermionic Gaussian state
is any state of the form
|\psi\rangle=\Phi(Q)|0^{n}\rangle
with
Q\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
. Gaussian states are eigenstates of the parity operator: if
Q\in\mathrm{SO}(2n)
(resp.
\mathrm{O}^{-}(2n)
), then
|\psi\rangle
is a superposition solely over even- (resp. odd-)number Fock states. Gaussian states are fully characterized by a polynomial-sized object called a
covariance matrix

\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
. This is a skew-symmetric matrix with the entries

\Gamma_{pq}=-\frac{i}{2}\langle\psi|[\gamma_{p},\gamma_{q}]|\psi\rangle.

(1.12)

The covariance matrix is orthogonal (
\Gamma\Gamma^{\mathrm{T}}=-\Gamma^{2}=\mathbb{I}
) if and only if
|\psi\rangle
is pure Gaussian.

If
|\psi\rangle
is also an eigenstate of the number operator, say
\mathsf{Num}|\psi\rangle=\eta|\psi\rangle
, then it further lies within a subclass of Gaussian states called
\eta
-particle
Slater determinants
. Any such state can be expressed as
|\psi\rangle=\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U)|1^{\eta}\,0^{n-\eta}\rangle
for some
U\in\mathrm{U}(n)
. Slater determinants are one-to-one with a lower-dimensional object,
2

D
is simply a block of
\Gamma
; for Slater determinants, the other blocks are either zero or a copy of
D

D
is sufficient information.
the
1
-particle
reduced density matrix
(
1
-RDM)
D\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
:

D_{jk}=\langle\psi|a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{k}|\psi\rangle.

(1.13)

is a rank-

|\psi\rangle
is an
\eta

D
and
\Gamma
as one-body representations of their parent Slater/Gaussian states.

Improved error analysis with fermionic shadows.

The foundation of our FLO unitary learning algorithm is fast state tomography. We deploy fermionic classical shadows, which comes in two variations: one which measures in random passive FLO bases
[
48
]
, and one using random parity-conserving active FLO measurements
[
68
,
29
]
. We refer to these as
\mathrm{U}(n)
-shadows and
\mathrm{SO}(2n)
-shadows, respectively. To learn a Gaussian state, we show in
Appendix

A
that
\mathrm{SO}(2n)
-shadows can estimate its covariance matrix
\Gamma
up to
\delta
error in operator norm, using
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}/\delta^{2})
copies of
|\psi\rangle
. Although this complexity was already known since
[
5
]
, we give a particularly clean proof of the statement via shadows.

To learn a Slater determinant, we make the key observation that Low’s estimator for the fermionic RDM
[
48
]
can be expressed as the random matrix

\widehat{\bm{D}}=\bm{V}^{\dagger}E(\bm{b})\bm{V},\quad\text{where }E(b)=(n+1)\diag(b)-|b|\mathbb{I},

(1.14)

where
\bm{V}\sim\Haar(\mathrm{U}(n))
is the random FLO applied before measuring outcome
\bm{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. We show this in
Proposition

3.2
. Then if we estimate
D
by gathering
N
i.i.d. copies of
\widehat{\bm{D}}
, their mean tightly concentrates in a
\delta
-ball around
D
(in operator norm) once
N\gtrsim\frac{\sigma^{2}\log n}{\delta^{2}}
. The variance parameter
\sigma^{2}
here is essentially
\|{\E[\widehat{\bm{D}}^{2}]}\|
, which we show simplifies remarkably:

\widehat{\bm{D}}^{2}=\bm{V}^{\dagger}E(\bm{b})^{2}\bm{V}=(n+1-2\eta)\widehat{\bm{D}}+\eta(n+1-\eta)\mathbb{I}.

(1.15)

Higher-order contributions from
\bm{V}
do not appear, and so we only need the second moment to evaluate
\E[\widehat{\bm{D}}]
. But by construction this is
\E[\widehat{\bm{D}}]=D

[
48
]
, hence with
\|D\|\leq 1
we have
\sigma^{2}=\Theta(n\eta)
. In contrast,
[
48
]
only provided an average-case variance over the individual RDM entries because a useful closed form for the third moment is currently unknown. This is in contrast to the “traditional wisdom” of classical shadows, that having control of the third moment is crucial to assess the variance
[
35
,
65
]
.

When
|\psi\rangle
is a Slater determinant, we can convert the RDM error
\delta
to trace distance
\varepsilon
using the sharp bound of Bittel, Mele, Eisert, and Leone
[
5
]
. As we show in
Theorem

3.8
, it suffices to choose
\delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{\eta}}
. For the special
\eta=1
case, we further recognize that
Eq.

1.14
coincides with the uniform POVM used by Guţă, Kahn, Kueng, and Tropp
[
23
]
for ordinary state tomography. Their improved concentration bound immediately applies to our setting, implying that
N\gtrsim n/\delta^{2}=4n/\varepsilon^{2}
copies suffice.

Reducing passive FLOs to unitary tomography.

We now extend this
\eta=1
reduction to FLOs. Haah, Kothari, O’Donnell, and Tang
[
24
]
showed that, given access to a unitary channel
U
, one can construct an estimate
\widehat{\bm{U}}
such that
\phdist(\widehat{\bm{U}},U)\leq\delta
using
\Theta(n^{2}/\delta)
queries. The metric

\phdist(U,V)\coloneqq\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\|U-e^{i\theta}V\|

(1.16)

is the projective operator-norm distance. Although our setting does not provide direct access to
U
as a channel, we can emulate it by restricting to single-particle inputs. That is, the state
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U)|1_{j}\rangle\in\mathcal{F}
is merely a lifted representation of
U|j\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Its
1
-RDM is
D_{j}=U|j\rangle\!\langle j|U^{\dagger}
, which we can efficiently learn via
\mathrm{U}(n)
-shadows. Because the algorithm of
[
24
]
is based on repeating pure state tomography
[
23
]
over the columns of
U
, this connection is sufficient for us to utilize their algorithm with almost no modification. Note that a naive reduction to state tomography would cost
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}/\delta^{2})
queries; they boost to Heisenberg scaling
1/\delta
by using a bootstrapping process, which we will elaborate on later.

Phase estimation step.

To establish
Corollary

1.4
, we only need a conversion from
\phdist
error;
2.7
tells us that
\delta=\varepsilon/\eta
suffices when we restrict attention to
\wedge^{\eta}\mathbb{C}^{n}
. However to prove the stronger diamond distance result of
Theorem

1.3
, we need to further estimate the
\mathrm{U}(1)
phase on
U
which the
[
24
]
algorithm cannot detect. For FLOs, this is
not
an unphysical global phase, as
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}:e^{i\theta}\mathbb{I}\mapsto e^{i\theta\,\mathsf{Num}}
maps
\mathrm{U}(1)
to a nontrivial operation on
\mathcal{F}
. We accomplish this by inverting the projective estimate:
U\widehat{\bm{U}}^{\dagger}=e^{i\bm{\theta}}\bm{W}
where
\bm{W}\approx\mathbb{I}
. We can then estimate
\bm{\theta}
with standard interferometry: if we could prepare
\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}
in some mode and apply
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(e^{i\bm{\theta}}\bm{W})\approx e^{i\bm{\theta}\,\mathsf{Num}}
, then the quadratures
X=\gamma_{1}
and
Y=\gamma_{n+1}
have expectations
\langle X\rangle=\cos\bm{\theta}
and
\langle Y\rangle=\sin\bm{\theta}
. Taking
\atantwo(\langle Y\rangle,\langle X\rangle)
recovers an estimate of
\bm{\theta}
mod
2\pi
, up to sampling error and the closeness of
\bm{W}
to the identity (see
Section

5.2
for the full error analysis). This implies
Item

1
from
Remark

1.2
.

If we are constrained by fermionic superselection then states of the form
\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}
are forbidden. A partial fix is to instead prepare
\frac{|00\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}
, say in the first two modes; the corresponding quadratures become
X=a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{2}^{\dagger}+a_{2}a_{1}
and
Y=i(a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{2}^{\dagger}-a_{2}a_{1})
. This imprints a phase of
e^{i2\bm{\theta}}
, allowing us to identify
\bm{\theta}
mod
\pi
(not mod
2\pi
). As a consequence, the final estimate carries an ambiguous
\pm 1
phase relative to the even- and odd-parity sectors, as seen by the fact that
e^{i(\theta+k\pi)\,\mathsf{Num}}=\mathsf{Par}^{k}e^{i\theta\,\mathsf{Num}}
for integer
k
. This is the basis for
Item

2
from
Remark

1.2
.

In order to learn
\bm{\theta}
without ambiguity while still using parity eigenstates, we propose appending an ancillary mode
\mathsf{a}
and preparing the state
\frac{|0_{1}0_{\mathsf{a}}\rangle+|1_{1}1_{\mathsf{a}}\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}
. The same interferometric principle holds, but because
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U\widehat{\bm{U}}^{\dagger})\approx e^{i\bm{\theta}\,\mathsf{Num}}
only acts on the system register,
e^{i\bm{\theta}\,\mathsf{Num}}|1_{1}1_{\mathsf{a}}\rangle=e^{i\bm{\theta}}|1_{1}1_{\mathsf{a}}\rangle
acquires the appropriate phase. The quadratures we measure in this case are
X=a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{\mathsf{a}}^{\dagger}+a_{\mathsf{a}}a_{1}
and
Y=i(a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{\mathsf{a}}^{\dagger}-a_{\mathsf{a}}a_{1})
. This is the only piece of our algorithm (for both passive and active FLOs) that requires an ancilla.

Once we have a method to estimate the phase, we can incorporate it cleanly into the
[
24
]
algorithm. This outputs some
\bm{U}^{\sharp}\in\mathrm{U}(n)
such that
\|\bm{U}^{\sharp}-U\|\leq\delta
, using
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}/\delta)
queries to
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U)
. The stability bound of Oszmaniec, Dangniam, Morales, and Zimborás
[
53
]
(
Proposition

) then guarantees
\varepsilon
error in diamond distance if
\delta=\varepsilon/n
.

Extending to active FLOs.

Our strategy for learning active FLOs proceeds in two stages. First, we learn the covariance matrix
\Gamma
of the Gaussian state
\Phi(Q)|0^{n}\rangle
using
\mathrm{SO}(2n)
-shadows. Denoting the estimate by
\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}
, we can extract an orthogonal matrix
\bm{Q}_{{\mathrm{act}}}\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
by computing the normal form for skew-symmetric matrices:
\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}=\bm{Q}_{{\mathrm{act}}}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{Q}_{{\mathrm{act}}}^{\mathrm{T}}
, where

\bm{\Lambda}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\diag(\bm{\lambda})\\
-\diag(\bm{\lambda})&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}.

(1.17)

The suggestive notation
\bm{Q}_{{\mathrm{act}}}
stems from the fact that if
\Phi(R)
is passive, then
\Phi(R)|0^{n}\rangle\propto|0^{n}\rangle
. Thus any orthogonal matrix extracted in this manner is ambiguous to right multiplication by
\mathrm{O}(2n)\cap\mathrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})\cong\mathrm{U}(n)
, so we only learn a “maximally active” component of
Q
this way. As we show in
Lemma

6.1
, if it holds that
\|\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}-\Gamma\|\leq\delta
, then there exists some
\bm{Q}_{{\mathrm{pas}}}\in\mathrm{O}(2n)\cap\mathrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
such that
\|\bm{Q}_{{\mathrm{act}}}\bm{Q}_{{\mathrm{pas}}}-Q\|\leq\delta
.

The second stage of our algorithm then aims to learn
\bm{Q}_{{\mathrm{pas}}}
. We query
\Phi(\bm{Q}_{{\mathrm{act}}}^{\mathrm{T}})\Phi(Q)\approx\Phi(\bm{Q}_{{\mathrm{pas}}})
, which is approximately passive; thus we can (approximately) learn it using the passive FLO algorithm. Supposing that these approximations are good enough, then combining the estimates from both stages yields a suitable estimate of
Q
.

We require a more sophisticated error analysis to handle the fact that
\Phi(\bm{Q}_{{\mathrm{act}}}^{\mathrm{T}})\Phi(Q)
is only approximately passive. Write
\bm{Q}_{{\mathrm{act}}}^{\mathrm{T}}Q=\bm{Z}\bm{Q}_{{\mathrm{pas}}}
where
\bm{Z}\approx\mathbb{I}
, and let
\bm{U}\in\mathrm{U}(n)
be the unique unitary associated to
\bm{Q}_{{\mathrm{pas}}}
. The states used for the passive learner are now
\Phi(\bm{Z})\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{U})|1_{j}\rangle
, which are no longer Slater determinants—the active FLO
\Phi(\bm{Z})
behaves like a perturbation that leaks the ideal state
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{U})|1_{j}\rangle
into other particle-number sectors. Nonetheless, we show that we can still learn their RDMs via
\mathrm{U}(n)
-shadows (
6.9
), and that they are only off by a systematic error on the order of
\|\bm{Z}-\mathbb{I}\|
(
Corollary

6.5
).

The main challenge we encounter is controlling the variance of
\mathrm{U}(n)
-shadows when the state is no longer a number eigenstate. We facilitate this analysis by expressing the Bogoliubov transformation of
\Phi(\bm{Z})
in the quasi-particle basis (
6.10
), rather than the Majorana basis. This allows us to separate out the part of
\bm{Z}
which conserves particle number from the part that mixes occupations. The former affects the systematic error but not the variance. For the latter, we show in
Theorem

6.11
that the variance increases to roughly
\sigma^{2}=\mathcal{O}(n(1+\sqrt{n}\|\bm{Z}-\mathbb{I}\|))
. Thus if we take the precision of the first stage (learning
\Gamma
) as
\delta=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\mathclose{}
, the downstream effect is that we can recover
\sigma^{2}=\mathcal{O}(n)
, same as in the exactly-passive setting. However this costs us an extra factor of
n
, yielding a “base tomography” algorithm for learning
Q
in operator norm with
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{3}/\delta^{2})
queries.

Bootstrapping to diamond distance.

Methods to attain Heisenberg scaling using temporal (rather than spatial) coherence have been explored at least as early as
[
31
,
30
,
41
]
. More recently, these ideas have been adapted to the multi-parameter/many-body regime
[
44
,
37
,
24
,
15
]
. Naturally, we will follow the methodology of
[
24
]
, which already gave the complexity for our passive FLO algorithm with only minor adjustments.

The basic premise behind their bootstrap process is to repeatedly run a “base algorithm” which learns with constant error, wherein each iteration adaptively updates the unitary being queried. For example, suppose we have an algorithm
\alg:(U,\delta)\mapsto\bm{V}
such that
\dist(\bm{V},U)\leq\delta
, where the metric
\dist
is either the projective or non-projective distance of unitary matrices in operator norm. If we could also query
U^{p}
for some integer
p\geq 1
, then the estimate
\bm{V}=\alg(U^{p},\delta)
would still obey
\dist(\bm{V},U^{p})\leq\delta
. One can show that calculating the (principal)
p
th root of
\bm{V}
yields a matrix such that
\dist(\bm{V}^{1/p},U)\leq\frac{\pi\delta}{p}
, provided that
\bm{V}
and
U^{p}
are within a constant-radius ball (radius
=\frac{1}{3\pi}
suffices) of the identity.

Although we cannot simply set
\delta=\Theta(1)
and
p=\Theta(1/\varepsilon)
, as this would violate
U^{p}
being close to
\mathbb{I}
, we can do the next best thing: for each iteration
t=0,1,\ldots,T
, run
\alg((U\widehat{\bm{U}}_{t}^{\dagger})^{p_{t}},\frac{1}{50})
where
\widehat{\bm{U}}_{t}
is the current best estimate of
U
(i.e., by recursively updating
\widehat{\bm{U}}_{t}=\bm{V}^{1/p_{t-1}}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{t-1}
). The constant error
\frac{1}{50}
assures us that the iterates
U\widehat{\bm{U}}_{t}^{\dagger}
are sufficiently close to
\mathbb{I}
, and picking a logarithmically spaced schedule
p_{t}=2^{t}
guarantees that the final estimate
\widehat{\bm{U}}_{T+1}
is
\varepsilon
-close to
U
by an inductive argument, provided that
T=\lceil\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)\rceil
. If each base call uses
q
queries, then the total number over all
T+1
rounds is
\mathcal{O}(q/\varepsilon)
. This bootstrapping argument is easily applicable to the orthogonal group by viewing it as a subgroup,
\mathrm{O}(2n)\subset\mathrm{U}(2n)
.
3
3

3

The fact that
\mathrm{O}(2n)
has a component disconnected from the identity is not an issue because if
Q,R\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
are sufficiently close to each other then
QR^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathrm{SO}(2n)
.

To apply this to FLO learning, we simply use the fact that
\Phi
and
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}
are homomorphisms between their one-body representations and the Fock space. Hence, taking powers on the Fock space (which is what we have physical access to) is equivalent to taking powers on the smaller representations. This is especially nice because (1) all of our classical computation remains time-efficient, and (2) we only need to run the base tomography with constant error throughout the entire process. This second point is noteworthy because the conversion to
\varepsilon
diamond distance requires us to learn the one-body representation with
\varepsilon/n
error; however, with the bootstrap process we only need the number of iterations
T
to depend on
\varepsilon/n
, not each iteration. As a consequence, we only pay linearly in
n
, not quadratically.

1.3
Related work

We are only aware of two prior results specifically on learning FLOs
[
53
,
14
]
. However, two recent works considered near-FLO unitaries
[
38
,
3
]
, and a quick re-analysis of their results in the exact-FLO limit yields two more points of comparison. We also review the existing literature on learning Gaussian states and Slater determinants.

Comparison to
[
53
]
.

The algorithm of Oszmaniec, Dangniam, Morales, and Zimborás uses
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{5}/\varepsilon^{2})
queries to learn active FLOs. It proceeds by preparing initial states from a family
\{|\psi_{q}\rangle\}_{q\in[2n]}
, applying
\Phi(Q)
to each, and then measuring each Majorana operator
\{\gamma_{p}\}_{p\in[2n]}
. The states are defined such that
Q_{pq}=\langle\psi_{q}|\Phi(Q)^{\dagger}\gamma_{p}\Phi(Q)|\psi_{q}\rangle
, allowing them to build an estimate of
Q
entry-by-entry. Because all the Majorana operators anticommute, each expectation value per state must be measured one at a time, requiring
(2n)^{2}
different experiments. They then prove a stability bound, which converts an
\alpha
error on
Q
to an
\alpha n
error on
\Phi(Q)
. This eventually implies that
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{3}/\varepsilon^{2})
samples per experiment achieves the target error.

This approach also does not obey fermionic superselection, both in the initial states
|\psi_{q}\rangle
and the observables
\gamma_{p}
. It is easy to see that
\gamma_{p}
does not commute with
\mathsf{Par}
because it has odd Majorana degree. Meanwhile the states
|\psi_{q}\rangle
are of the form
\frac{|0^{n}\rangle+e^{i\theta}|1_{j}\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}
.

Comparison to
[
14
]
.

Another FLO learning algorithm is due to Cudby and Strelchuk. Rather than diamond distance, they consider the Frobenius distance

\frodist(U,V)\coloneqq\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{2^{n}}|{\tr(U^{\dagger}V)}|}

(1.18)

as their metric. Their algorithm requires
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{13}/\varepsilon_{F}^{4})
queries
4
4

4

Their Theorem 1 claims that
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n/\eta^{2}+n^{2}/\eta^{2})
queries suffice to achieve
n^{3}\eta
Frobenius error, provided that
\eta\leq C/n^{6}
. However, the first term should actually be
n/\eta^{4}
since it corresponds to learning the
squared
entries of
Q
to
\eta
error (e.g., see
[
36
]
). This dominates the total complexity since we take
\eta=\varepsilon_{F}/n^{3}
.
to get an estimate
\widehat{\bm{Q}}
such that
\frodist(\Phi(\widehat{\bm{Q}}),\Phi(Q))\leq\varepsilon_{F}
, provided that
\varepsilon_{F}\leq C/n^{3}
for some constant
C>0
. Like
[
53
]
, their algorithm also learns
Q
entrywise; however, their approach is to perform Bell-like measurements on the Choi state of
\Phi(Q)
to estimate all the magnitudes
|Q_{jk}|
first. A second family of experiments is then performed to deduce the signs.

It is nontrivial to make a faithful comparison between average-case (Frobenius distance) versus worst-case (diamond distance) performance. Indeed, for any
U,V\in\mathrm{U}(2^{n})
:

\frac{1}{2}\frodist(U,V)\leq\diamdist(U,V)\leq\sqrt{2^{n-1}}\frodist(U,V).

(1.19)

In terms of the resources required, this algorithm uses an ancilla register of
n
modes and prepares EPR states
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|b\rangle\otimes|b\rangle
to produce the Choi states. The operations it implements also do not respect superselection rules. Finally, it queries the inverse
\Phi(Q)^{\dagger}
, which is not guaranteed to be available in black-box scenarios.

Learning near-Gaussian states.

A recent line of study concerns fermionic states and unitaries which are “doped” with a few non-Gaussian operations. For states, Mele and Herasymenko
[
49
]
showed that
t
-doped Gaussian states (states prepared by circuits of arbitrary FLO gates but at most
t
non-FLO gates) are efficiently learnable as long as
t=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
. Central to this result is a compressibility lemma which states that any
t
-doped Gaussian state can be expressed as
|\psi\rangle=\Phi(Q)(|\phi\rangle\otimes|0^{n-\Theta(t)}\rangle)
, for some
Q\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
and some non-Gaussian state
|\phi\rangle
on
\Theta(t)
modes. The first step of their algorithm is to learn
\Phi(Q)
by measuring copies of
|\psi\rangle
. Recall however that this does not uniquely determine
Q
, even in the
t=0
setting. Indeed, this is essentially equivalent to the first stage of our active FLO algorithm, returning only an equivalence class from the quotient space
\mathrm{O}(2n)/\mathrm{U}(n)
. This is sufficient for their state tomography protocol, but not for unitaries.

Learning near-Gaussian unitaries.

Iyer
[
38
]
and Austin, Morales, and Gorshkov
[
3
]
both showed that any
t
-doped FLO can be efficiently learned with respect to diamond distance, provided that
t=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
. Their ideas build on the compressibility lemma of
[
49
]
. Taking
t=0
, we can recover algorithms for learning active FLOs. In this regime, both algorithms use
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{5}/\varepsilon^{2})
queries,
5
5

5

The advertised bound of
[
38
]
naively implies
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{6}/\varepsilon^{6})
queries, but this can be substantially improved by relaxing their error parameter
\alpha
from
\varepsilon^{3}/n^{3/2}
to merely
\varepsilon/n
, which is sufficient when
t=0
.
matching that of
[
53
]
. However they rely on the Choi-state approach to learn
Q
, thereby requiring an auxiliary register of
n
extra modes. It is worth noting that
[
3
]
is the only prior work we are aware of that explicitly considers superselection rules in this learning context.

Prior work on learning Slater determinants.

Aaronson and Grewal first considered the efficiently learnability of Slater determinants in
[
1
]
. O’Gorman
[
54
]
showed that
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{7}\eta^{2}/\varepsilon^{4})
copies of an
\eta
-particle Slater determinant suffice (where
\varepsilon
is the trace distance here). Aaronson and Grewal later improved this in the final version of their paper, proving a copy complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\eta^{2}/\varepsilon^{4})

[
1
]
.

To improve the
1/\varepsilon^{4}
dependence, Bittel, Mele, Eisert, and Leone
[
5
]
established a sharp bound on the trace distance between Gaussian states in terms of their covariance matrices. With this, they could demonstrate an algorithm for learning pure fermionic Gaussian states using only
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{3}/\varepsilon^{2})
single-copy measurements. As Slater determinants are a special class of Gaussian states, this result applies to them as well.

Information-theoretic bounds.

Zhao
et al.

[
69
]
showed that, for the family of states prepared by
G
gates, there exists an algorithm that learns any such state using
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(G/\varepsilon^{2})
copies. Any Gaussian state (resp. Slater determinant) can be prepared by a circuit with
G=\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
(resp.
G=\mathcal{O}(n\eta)
) gates
[
42
,
39
]
, implying a quadratic copy complexity. This algorithm is nearly sample-optimal and uses only single-copy measurements, but is computationally inefficient, taking time exponential in
G
.

An alternative scheme was recently introduced by Walter and Witteveen
[
64
]
. They first introduce a pure Gaussian state learner, analogous to Hayashi’s POVM for ordinary state tomography
[
26
]
. This is an entangling POVM over
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
joint copies of the Gaussian state. Then modifying the random purification channel trick
[
55
]
for FLOs, they reduce the mixed case to the pure case with the same copy complexity.
[
64
]
also prove a lower bound for this task, showing that
\Omega(n^{2}/\delta)
copies are necessary to learn within
\delta
infidelity.

As for unitary learning,
[
69
]
also prove upper and lower bounds for learning quantum circuits of gate complexity
G
. When taking the diamond distance, they show that the query complexity is exponential in
G
. However, under the average-case Frobenius distance (defined in
Eq.

1.18
), the complexity becomes nearly linear in
G
. Similar to their state-learning algorithm, this unitary learner is also time-inefficient.

1.4
Discussion

In this work, we provide a learning algorithm to estimate fermionic linear optics with fewer queries than prior art. We improve in the scaling with both system size and precision; but equally importantly, our algorithm takes a natural approach that only uses physical (i.e., parity-conserving) operations and minimal ancilla. For passive FLOs, our algorithmic design borrows strongly from the framework of
[
24
]
, made query- and time-efficient via the one-body representation of FLOs. We show how to furthermore estimate the overall
\mathrm{U}(1)
phase of this representation, which is physically relevant in this context.

For active FLOs, we have to handle two additional details: (1) an error analysis on the quotient space
\mathrm{O}(2n)/\mathrm{U}(n)
, and (2) control over the effect of non-particle-conserving perturbations. This requires us to develop an analysis of fermionic classical shadows
[
48
,
68
,
29
]
for estimating RDMs and covariance matrices using random matrix theory tools. In particular, we show how to reformulate the estimator of
[
48
]
such that we can analyze its worst-case variance without needing to compute the third moment of the associated Haar integral. As a corollary, this allows us to establish a copy complexity for learning Slater determinants which improves upon previously known time-efficient algorithms
[
1
,
54
,
5
]
.

Some important open questions remain:

•

Can the query complexity for the active FLO algorithm be improved to
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}/\varepsilon)
, matching the passive case? In
Appendix

B
we show that this is essentially possible if we allow
n
auxiliary modes to prepare Choi states. Can we still achieve this using at most
1
ancilla?

•

What is the optimal query complexity for learning FLOs? By a simple parameter counting argument plus the optimality of Heisenberg scaling, we conjecture that
\Theta(n^{2}/\varepsilon)
queries are necessary and sufficient, analogous to the bounds of
[
24
]
for generic unitary tomography.

•

Can we apply the precision bootstrap to other efficient unitary learning problems to achieve Heisenberg scaling? For example, algorithms have been recently developed for near-Gaussian fermionic unitaries
[
38
,
3
]
and bosonic Gaussian unitaries
[
17
]
. Although the bootstrap is broadly applicable in principle, it seems challenging to apply it to the latter because such unitaries are represented by a
noncompact
Lie group,
\mathrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
.

Linear algebra

We regularly employ standard matrix factorizations such as the eigendecomposition, singular value decomposition (SVD), etc. One particularly important decomposition for Majorana covariance matrices is the normal form of a skew-symmetric matrix.

Claim 2.1

(Normal form for skew-symmetric matrices)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
be skew-symmetric, i.e.,
A=-A^{\mathrm{T}}
. There exists an orthogonal matrix
W\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
and nonnegative vector
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
such that

A=W\begin{pmatrix}0&\diag(\lambda)\\
-\diag(\lambda)&0\end{pmatrix}W^{\mathrm{T}}.

(2.1)

This decomposition can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
time.

Proof.

The existence of this form is standard up to permutations, e.g., see
[
33
]
. Specialized algorithms exist to compute it
[
11
]
, although it suffices to recognize that for real skew-symmetric matrices, the real Schur decomposition coincides with this normal form (up to a permutation).
∎

We also frequently round our estimated matrices to nearby ones with the appropriate structure (projector, unitary, etc). In operator norm, this incurs only a constant-factor amplification of the error.

Claim 2.2

(Rounded matrix error)
.

Let
A,B
be two matrices of the conformable dimensions. Let
A=X\Sigma(A)Y^{\dagger}
be the SVD of
A
and
\Sigma(B)
the matrix of singular values of
B
. Define
A^{\star}\coloneqq X\Sigma(B)Y^{\dagger}
. Then

\|A^{\star}-B\|\leq 2\|A-B\|.

(2.2)

Proof.

By triangle inequality,

\|A^{\star}-B\|\leq\|A^{\star}-A\|+\|A-B\|=\|\Sigma(B)-\Sigma(A)\|+\|A-B\|

(2.3)

where
\|\Sigma(B)-\Sigma(A)\|=\max_{j}|\sigma_{j}(A)-\sigma_{j}(B)|
. The claim follows from Weyl’s inequality
[
33
]
:

|\sigma_{j}(A)-\sigma_{j}(B)|\leq\|A-B\|\quad\forall j.\qed

Claim 2.5

\mathrm{O}^{-}(2n)=\{R\in\mathrm{O}(2n):\det(R)=-1\}

\Phi(X)=\gamma_{1}
. If

and

implies that

. But the diamond distance is always at most
1
.

Note that for passive FLOs
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U),\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(V)

because of the unitary similarity

\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}A&-\operatorname{Im}A\\
\operatorname{Im}A&\operatorname{Re}A\end{pmatrix}=\Omega^{\mathrm{T}}\begin{pmatrix}A^{*}&0\\
0&A\end{pmatrix}\Omega^{*},\quad\text{where }\Omega=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}&i\mathbb{I}\\
\mathbb{I}&-i\mathbb{I}\end{pmatrix},

(2.9)

for any

. The factor of

Claim 2.7

(Passive FLO stability bound)
.

Let
U,V\in\mathrm{U}(n)
. It holds that

\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\wedge^{\eta}\mathbb{C}^{n}}\trdist(\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U)|\psi\rangle,\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(V)|\psi\rangle)\leq\eta\phdist(U,V).

(2.10)

Proof.

Because
|\psi\rangle\in\wedge^{\eta}\mathbb{C}^{n}
is already antisymmetrized, we can write
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U)|\psi\rangle=U^{\otimes\eta}|\psi\rangle
. Hence

\trdist(\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U)|\psi\rangle,\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(V)|\psi\rangle)=\sqrt{1-|\langle\psi|(U^{\dagger}V)^{\otimes\eta}|\psi\rangle|^{2}}\leq\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\|(U^{\otimes\eta}-e^{i\theta}V^{\otimes\eta})|\psi\rangle\|.

(2.11)

By the max–min inequality and telescoping through tensor products,

\displaystyle\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\wedge^{\eta}\mathbb{C}^{n}}\trdist(\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U)|\psi\rangle,\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(V)|\psi\rangle)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\wedge^{\eta}\mathbb{C}^{n}}\|(U^{\otimes\eta}-e^{i\theta}V^{\otimes\eta})|\psi\rangle\|

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\|U^{\otimes\eta}-e^{i\theta}V^{\otimes\eta}\|

(2.12)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\eta\|U-e^{i\theta/\eta}V\|=\eta\phdist(U,V).\qed

Claim 2.8

(FLO gate complexity)
.

Any FLO on
n
modes can be decomposed into at most
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
elementary two-mode gates, plus at most
1
“reflection gate”
\gamma_{1}
. The algorithm that computes this decomposition runs in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
.

Proof.

Constructive algorithms were first given by
[

,
39
]
, although the fundamental idea dates back to conventional optics
[
56
]
. For completeness, we provide a sketch of the proof. Consider the active case, where without loss of generality we can assume
W\in\mathrm{SO}(2n)
(because any
W^{\prime}\in\mathrm{O}^{-}(2n)
can be written as
W^{\prime}=XW
with
\Phi(X)=\gamma_{1}
). We take the QR factorization of
W=QR
, where
Q
is orthogonal and
R
is triangular. This can be computed systematically by Givens rotation eliminations in
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
time
[
33
]
; that is, we determine
Q=G_{L}\cdots G_{2}G_{1}
where
G_{\ell}
are Givens rotations between two adjacent rows and
L\leq\binom{2n}{2}
. Additionally, since
W
is orthogonal, so too must
R
; this forces
R
to be diagonal.

Suppose the Givens rotation
G
rotates rows

by angle
\theta
. The desired transformation

\Phi(G)^{\dagger}\gamma_{p}\Phi(G)=\begin{cases}\cos(\theta)\gamma_{q}-\sin(\theta)\gamma_{q+1}&\text{if }p=q,\\
\sin(\theta)\gamma_{q}+\cos(\theta)\gamma_{q+1}&\text{if }p=q+1,\\
\gamma_{p}&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(2.14)

can be achieved with
\Phi(G)=e^{-\frac{\theta}{2}\gamma_{p}\gamma_{p+1}}
. Meanwhile since
R
is diagonal and orthogonal, its diagonal entries can only be

\Phi(R)^{\dagger}\gamma_{p}\Phi(R)=\pm\gamma_{p}
. These signs can be implemented by gates of the form
\gamma_{p}\gamma_{q}=ie^{-\frac{\pi}{2}\gamma_{p}\gamma_{q}}
, of which there are at most
n
(see
[
67
, Supplementary Note 4]
). Use the homomorphism
\Phi(W)=\Phi(G_{L})\cdots\Phi(G_{2})\Phi(G_{1})\Phi(R)
to conclude the gate complexity.

For passive FLOs we can restrict to passive elementary gates because the transformation

\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(G)^{\dagger}a_{p}\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(G)=\begin{cases}\cos(\theta)a_{q}-\sin(\theta)a_{q+1}&\text{if }p=q,\\
\sin(\theta)a_{q}+\cos(\theta)a_{q+1}&\text{if }p=q+1,\\
a_{p}&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(2.15)

can be achieved with
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(G)=e^{-\theta(a_{q}^{\dagger}a_{q+1}-a_{q+1}^{\dagger}a_{q})}
. Also note that in this
\mathrm{U}(n)
-representation, the diagonal transformation
R=\diag(e^{i\alpha_{1}},e^{i\alpha_{2}},\ldots,e^{i\alpha_{n}})
is easily implemented via
e^{-i\alpha_{q}a_{q}^{\dagger}a_{q}}a_{p}e^{i\alpha_{q}a_{q}^{\dagger}a_{q}}=e^{i\alpha_{q}\delta_{pq}}a_{p}
.
∎

3.1
Estimating the 1-RDM

It suffices to estimate the
1
-RDM of a Slater determinant to uniquely identify it. This is standard and has been employed in prior works
[
1
,
54
,
5
]
; our contribution here is to demonstrate a variant with (1) improved dependency on particle number, and (2) isotropically distributed errors in the matrix. These subtle properties will later be crucial to obtain our claimed query complexity for FLO learning. To this end, we employ the classical shadows scheme of Low, designed precisely for this scenario
[
48
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\rho
be a quantum state of
\eta
fermions on
n
modes. We say that the
\mathrm{U}(n)
-shadows protocol
is the following procedure: for each copy of
\rho
,

1.

Draw a random unitary matrix
\bm{V}\sim\Haar(\mathrm{U}(n))
.

2.

Apply the FLO transformation
\rho\mapsto\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})\rho\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})^{\dagger}
.

3.

Measure in the standard basis, obtaining the classical outcome
\bm{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with probability
\langle\bm{b}|\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})\rho\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})^{\dagger}|\bm{b}\rangle
.

Each sample is stored as a tuple
(\bm{V},\bm{b})
, which is an efficient classical description of the postmeasurement state
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})^{\dagger}|\bm{b}\rangle\!\langle\bm{b}|\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})
.

Supposing
\rho
has particle number
\eta
, the outcomes
\bm{b}
always have Hamming weight
\eta
because
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})
conserves particle number. Such a protocol amounts to implementing the quantum channel
[
35
]

\mathcal{M}:\rho\mapsto\E[\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})^{\dagger}|\bm{b}\rangle\!\langle\bm{b}|\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})],

(3.1)

where the expectation is taken over the draw of
\bm{V}
and measurement outcomes
\bm{b}
. The inverse
6
6

6

Technically, the pseudoinverse over its image.
of this map may be determined analytically, yielding the formula
\rho=\E[\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})^{\dagger}|\bm{b}\rangle\!\langle\bm{b}|\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V}))]
. By linearity, this allows for estimating many non-commuting observables simultaneously without bias.
[
48
]
derives such estimators for the
k
-RDM elements. The relevant

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\rho
be an
n
-mode state of
\eta
fermions and
D
its
1
-RDM. Let
(\bm{V},\bm{b})
be a single sample obtained by running the
\mathrm{U}(n)
-shadows protocol on a copy of
\rho
. Then the matrix
\widehat{\bm{D}}\coloneqq\bm{V}^{\dagger}E(\bm{b})\bm{V}
, where

E(b)\coloneqq(n+1)\diag(b)-\eta\mathbb{I},

(3.2)

obeys
\E[\widehat{\bm{D}}]=D
.

Proof.

We unpack the notation of
[
48
, Theorem 5]

\widehat{\bm{D}}_{ij}=\langle 1_{i}|\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\pi_{\bm{b}}\bm{V})^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}_{\eta}\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\pi_{\bm{b}}\bm{V})|1_{j}\rangle,

(3.3)

where
\pi_{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
is any permutation matrix that maps
\{i\in[n]:b_{i}=1\}
to
[\eta]
, and the operator
\mathcal{E}_{\eta}
takes the form

\mathcal{E}_{\eta}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{s_{k}+1}\binom{n-s_{k}}{1-s_{k}}\binom{n-\eta+s_{k}}{s_{k}}|1_{k}\rangle\!\langle 1_{k}|,\quad s_{k}=\begin{cases}1,&k\leq\eta,\\
0,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(3.4)

To get the desired expression for
\widehat{\bm{D}}
, first recognize that the coefficients in
\mathcal{E}_{\eta}
simplify either to
n-\eta+1
if

, or otherwise to
-\eta
for the remaining
n-\eta

\pi_{\bm{b}}
out of
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\cdot)
and apply it to

to get

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{\eta}^{(\bm{b})}&\coloneqq\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\pi_{\bm{b}})^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}_{\eta}\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\pi_{\bm{b}})\\
&=(n-\eta+1)\sum_{k:\bm{b}_{k}=1}|1_{k}\rangle\!\langle 1_{k}|-\eta\sum_{k:\bm{b}_{k}=0}|1_{k}\rangle\!\langle 1_{k}|\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}E(\bm{b})_{kk}|1_{k}\rangle\!\langle 1_{k}|.\end{split}

(3.5)

Finally, use
\langle 1_{k}|\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})|1_{j}\rangle=\bm{V}_{kj}

to arrive at

\begin{split}\widehat{\bm{D}}_{ij}&=\langle 1_{i}|\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}_{\eta}^{(\bm{b})}\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})|1_{j}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle 1_{i}|\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})^{\dagger}|1_{k}\rangle E(\bm{b})_{kk}\langle 1_{k}|\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})|1_{j}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}[\bm{V}^{\dagger}]_{ik}E(\bm{b})_{kk}\bm{V}_{kj}=\bm{V}^{\dagger}E(\bm{b})\bm{V}.\end{split}

(3.6)

The claim

follows from the fact that classical shadows produces unbiased estimators.
∎

Originally,

To bound the copy complexity, we use the matrix Bernstein inequality (

Lemma 3.3

Let

\eta

Since each sample is identically distributed, let us temporarily drop the

\|\bm{X}\|\leq\frac{\|\widehat{\bm{D}}\|+\|D\|}{N}\leq\frac{n+1}{N},

For

\begin{split}E(\bm{b})^{2}&=(n+1)^{2}\diag(\bm{b})^{2}-2\eta(n+1)\diag(\bm{b})+\eta^{2}\mathbb{I}\\

7
7

7

\mathopen{}\left\|(n+1-2\eta)D+\eta(n+1-\eta)\mathbb{I}-D^{2}\right\|\mathclose{}\leq(n+1)+\eta(n+1)+1.

6.1
Learning from the vacuum

Let
\Phi(Q)
be the unknown FLO and
\Gamma
the covariance matrix of
\Phi(Q)|0^{n}\rangle
. In
Appendix

A
, we show that there is an algorithm
\mathsf{GaussianTomo}
that can learn
\Gamma
to within
\delta_{{\mathrm{act}}}
error using
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}/\delta_{{\mathrm{act}}}^{2})
copies. Recall that
\Gamma=QJQ^{\mathrm{T}}
where

J=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbb{I}\\
-\mathbb{I}&0\end{pmatrix}

(6.1)

is the covariance matrix of the vacuum. Then from the normal form of an estimate to
\Gamma
, we can extract an approximation of
Q
, modulo some unknown passive FLO. The following lemma shows that the error on
\Gamma
directly translates to an error on
Q
under the appropriate quotient distance.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
. Set
\Gamma_{1}=Q_{1}JQ_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}
and
\Gamma_{2}=Q_{2}JQ_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}
. Then

\min_{R\in\mathrm{O}(2n)\cap\mathrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})}\|Q_{1}-Q_{2}R\|\leq\|\Gamma_{1}-\Gamma_{2}\|.

(6.2)

Proof.

For convenience, we diagonalize the covariance matrices:

\Gamma_{1}=U_{1}DU_{1}^{\dagger},\quad\text{where }U_{1}=Q_{1}\Omega^{\mathrm{T}},\ \Omega\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}&i\mathbb{I}\\
\mathbb{I}&-i\mathbb{I}\end{pmatrix},\text{ and }D=\begin{pmatrix}i\mathbb{I}&0\\
0&-i\mathbb{I}\end{pmatrix},

(6.3)

and similarly
\Gamma_{2}=U_{2}DU_{2}^{\dagger}
with
U_{2}=Q_{2}\Omega^{\mathrm{T}}
. By unitary invariance of the operator norm, we have

\|\Gamma_{1}-\Gamma_{2}\|=\|U_{1}^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{1}-\Gamma_{2})U_{2}\|=\|DW-WD\|

(6.4)

where
W\coloneqq U_{1}^{\dagger}U_{2}
. Expressing

W=\begin{pmatrix}W_{11}&W_{12}\\
W_{21}&W_{22}\end{pmatrix},

(6.5)

we get

\begin{split}\|\Gamma_{1}-\Gamma_{2}\|&=\|DW-WD\|\\
&=\mathopen{}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}W_{11}&W_{12}\\
-W_{21}&-W_{22}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}W_{11}&-W_{12}\\
W_{21}&-W_{22}\end{pmatrix}\right\|\mathclose{}\\
&=\mathopen{}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}0&2W_{12}\\
-2W_{21}&0\end{pmatrix}\right\|\mathclose{}\\
&=2\max\{\|W_{12}\|,\|W_{21}\|\}.\end{split}

(6.6)

In fact, from the structure of
W=\Omega^{*}(Q_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}Q_{2})\Omega^{\mathrm{T}}
, we have
W_{21}=W_{12}^{*}
, hence
\|\Gamma-\Gamma^{\prime}\|=2\|W_{12}\|
. This can be seen by direct calculation:

\begin{split}W&=\Omega^{*}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}}_{Q_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}Q_{2}}\Omega^{\mathrm{T}}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}(A+D)+i(B-C)&(A-D)-i(B+C)\\
(A-D)+i(B+C)&(A+D)-i(B-C)\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}W_{11}&W_{12}\\
W_{12}^{*}&W_{11}^{*}\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(6.7)

Furthermore, the singular values of the blocks
W_{11},W_{12}
are tightly related. This can be seen from the CSD of
W\in\mathrm{U}(2n)
, which states that there exist unitaries
X_{1},X_{2},Y_{1},Y_{2}\in\mathrm{U}(n)
such that
[
33
]
:

\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}&0\\
0&X_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}W_{11}&W_{12}\\
W_{21}&W_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}Y_{1}^{\dagger}&0\\
0&Y_{2}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}C&S\\
-S&C\end{pmatrix}.

(6.8)

Here,
C
is a diagonal matrix containing the singular values of
W_{11}
in non-increasing order and
S=\sqrt{\mathbb{I}-C^{2}}
contains the singular values of
W_{12}
(in reverse order). Note that the singular values of the blocks all lie within
[0,1]
due to unitarity.

Next we compare this to
\|Q_{1}-Q_{2}R\|
for some
R\in\mathrm{O}(2n)\cap\mathrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
. It is useful to express

\begin{split}\|Q_{1}-Q_{2}R\|&=\|\mathbb{I}-Q_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}Q_{2}R\|\\
&=\|\mathbb{I}-W\Omega^{*}R\Omega^{\mathrm{T}}\|\\
&=\mathopen{}\left\|\mathbb{I}-W\begin{pmatrix}V^{*}&0\\
0&V\end{pmatrix}\right\|\mathclose{},\end{split}

(6.9)

where the final line comes from parametrizing

R=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}V&-\operatorname{Im}V\\
\operatorname{Im}V&\operatorname{Re}V\end{pmatrix}

(6.10)

for some
V\in\mathrm{U}(n)
and conjugating it with
\Omega^{*}(\cdot)\Omega^{\mathrm{T}}
(the calculation is analogous to
Eq.

6.7
). Take the left polar decomposition of
W_{11}
:

W_{11}=HZ,\text{ where }H\coloneqq\sqrt{W_{11}W_{11}^{\dagger}},\ Z\in\mathrm{U}(n).

(6.11)

The minimum over all
V\in\mathrm{U}(n)
is at most the value at any particular point, so we can get an upper bound by evaluating the norm at
V=Z^{\mathrm{T}}
:

\begin{split}\min_{R\in\mathrm{O}(2n)\cap\mathrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})}\|Q-Q^{\prime}R\|&=\min_{V\in\mathrm{U}(n)}\mathopen{}\left\|\mathbb{I}-W\begin{pmatrix}V^{*}&0\\
0&V\end{pmatrix}\right\|\mathclose{}\\
&\leq\mathopen{}\left\|\mathbb{I}-W\begin{pmatrix}Z^{\dagger}&0\\
0&Z^{\mathrm{T}}\end{pmatrix}\right\|\mathclose{}\\
&=\mathopen{}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}-W_{11}Z^{\dagger}&W_{12}Z^{\mathrm{T}}\\
W_{12}^{*}Z^{\dagger}&\mathbb{I}-W_{11}^{*}Z^{\mathrm{T}}\end{pmatrix}\right\|\mathclose{}\\
&=\mathopen{}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}-H&W_{12}Z^{\mathrm{T}}\\
W_{12}^{*}Z^{\dagger}&\mathbb{I}-H^{*}\end{pmatrix}\right\|\mathclose{}.\end{split}

(6.12)

We proceed by triangle inequality, splitting the matrix into its diagonal and off-diagonal blocks. For the diagonal blocks, since
H
is Hermitian,
H^{*}=H^{\mathrm{T}}
has the same spectrum. Thus it suffices to consider the norm of
\mathbb{I}-H
. By construction, the eigenvalues of
H
are the singular values of
W_{11}
, so from the CSD we have that
0\preceq H\preceq\mathbb{I}
and hence

\begin{split}\|\mathbb{I}-H\|&=1-\sigma_{\min}(H)\\
&=1-\langle n|C|n\rangle\\
&=1-\sqrt{1-\langle 1|S|1\rangle^{2}}\\
&\leq\langle 1|S|1\rangle^{2}=\|W_{12}\|^{2}.\end{split}

(6.13)

Meanwhile, the off-diagonal blocks clearly have operator norm
\|W_{12}\|
. Altogether, combining these facts with
Eq.

6.6
we get

\begin{split}\min_{R\in\mathrm{O}(2n)\cap\mathrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})}\|Q_{1}-Q_{2}R\|&\leq\mathopen{}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}-H&W_{12}Z\\
W_{12}^{*}Z^{*}&\mathbb{I}-H^{*}\end{pmatrix}\right\|\mathclose{}\\
&\leq\mathopen{}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}-H&0\\
0&\mathbb{I}-H^{*}\end{pmatrix}\right\|\mathclose{}+\mathopen{}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}0&W_{12}Z\\
W_{12}^{*}Z^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}\right\|\mathclose{}\\
&\leq\|W_{12}\|^{2}+\|W_{12}\|\\
&\leq 2\|W_{12}\|\\
&=\|\Gamma_{1}-\Gamma_{2}\|,\end{split}

(6.14)

which is what we had set out to prove.
∎

Claim 6.3

Let
\rho

D

C&D\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\frac{B-C+i(A+D)}{2}.

(6.19)

Furthermore, the induced spectral norm of

\|T\|_{\infty\to\infty}\coloneqq\sup_{\|X\|\neq 0}\frac{\|T(X)\|}{\|X\|}=1.

(6.20)

Proof.

Recall the relation between annihilation/creation operators and Majorana operators:

a_{j}=\frac{\gamma_{j}-i\gamma_{j+n}}{2},\quad a_{j}^{\dagger}=\frac{\gamma_{j}+i\gamma_{j+n}}{2}.

(6.21)

Also recall that the covariance matrix entries are

\Gamma_{j,k}=-i\tr(\gamma_{j}\gamma_{k}\rho)+i\delta_{jk}.

(6.22)

Thus we can write the

-RDM as

\begin{split}D_{jk}&=\tr(a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{k}\rho)\\
&=\frac{1}{4}\mathopen{}\left(\tr(\gamma_{j}\gamma_{k}\rho)+\tr(\gamma_{j+n}\gamma_{k+n}\rho)+i\tr(\gamma_{j+n}\gamma_{k}\rho)-i\tr(\gamma_{j}\gamma_{k+n}\rho)\right)\mathclose{}\\
&=\frac{1}{4}\mathopen{}\left(2\delta_{jk}+i\Gamma_{j,k}+i\Gamma_{j+n,k+n}-\Gamma_{j+n,k}+\Gamma_{j,k+n}\right)\mathclose{}.\end{split}

(6.23)

Equivalently in block matrix form, if we write

\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_{11}&\Gamma_{12}\\
\Gamma_{21}&\Gamma_{22}\end{pmatrix}

(6.24)

with each block an
n\times n
real matrix, then

D=\frac{\mathbb{I}+\frac{1}{2}(\Gamma_{12}-\Gamma_{21})+\frac{i}{2}(\Gamma_{11}+\Gamma_{22})}{2}=\frac{\mathbb{I}+T(\Gamma)}{2}.

(6.25)

To compute the induced norm of
T
, observe that we can write

T(X)=iPXP^{\dagger},\quad\text{where }P\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}&i\mathbb{I}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 2n}.

(6.26)

Since
PP^{\dagger}=\mathbb{I}
,
\|P\|=1
. Hence
\|T(X)\|\leq\|P\|^{2}\|X\|=\|X\|
. For the reverse direction, consider
X=\diag(\mathbb{I},\mathbb{I})
. Then
T(X)=i\mathbb{I}
attains
\|T(X)\|=\|X\|
.
∎

With this, we can bound the distance between the RDMs of any two states which are FLO-rotated from some common initial state. Again, this result holds for generic initial states.

Lemma 6.4
.

Fix an
n
-mode state
\sigma
. Let
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
and define
\rho_{1}\coloneqq\Phi(Q_{1})\sigma\Phi(Q_{1})^{\dagger}
,
\rho_{2}\coloneqq\Phi(Q_{2})\sigma\Phi(Q_{2})^{\dagger}
. Let
D(\rho_{1}),D(\rho_{2})
be their respective
1
-RDMs. Then

\|D(\rho_{1})-D(\rho_{2})\|\leq\|Q_{1}-Q_{2}\|.

(6.27)

Proof.

Let
\Gamma(\sigma)
be the covariance matrix of
\sigma
. By
6.3
, we have
D(\rho_{1})=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}+T(Q_{1}\Gamma(\sigma)Q_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}))
and
D(\rho_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}+T(Q_{2}\Gamma(\sigma)Q_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}))
. Set
W\coloneqq Q_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}Q_{2}
. Using linearity of
T
and the fact that
\|T\|_{\infty\to\infty}=1
, we get

\displaystyle\|D(\rho)-D(\rho^{\prime})\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|T(Q_{1}\Gamma(\sigma)Q_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}-Q_{2}\Gamma(\sigma)Q_{2}^{\mathrm{T}})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|T\|_{\infty\to\infty}\|Q_{1}\Gamma(\sigma)Q_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}-Q_{2}\Gamma(\sigma)Q_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\Gamma(\sigma)-W\Gamma(\sigma)W^{\mathrm{T}}\|

(6.28)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\Gamma(\sigma)(\mathbb{I}-W^{\mathrm{T}})+(\mathbb{I}-W)\Gamma(\sigma)W^{\mathrm{T}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{I}-W\|=\|Q_{1}-Q_{2}\|.\qed

(6.29)

As a special case, we get the desired bound between
D_{j}
and
|u_{j}\rangle\!\langle u_{j}|
.

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
Z\in\mathrm{SO}(2n)
and
U\in\mathrm{U}(n)
. For the states
|\psi_{j}\rangle=\Phi(Z)\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U)|1_{j}\rangle
it holds that

\|D_{j}-|u_{j}\rangle\!\langle u_{j}|\|\leq\|Z-\mathbb{I}\|,

(6.30)

where
D_{j}
is the
1
-RDM of
|\psi_{j}\rangle
and
|u_{j}\rangle=U|j\rangle
.

Proof.

Apply
Lemma

6.4
with
\sigma=\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U)|1_{j}\rangle\!\langle 1_{j}|\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(U)^{\dagger}
,
Q_{1}=Z
, and
Q_{2}=\mathbb{I}
.
∎

At this point, we can execute the error analysis for learning
U
(aka,
Q_{{\mathrm{pas}}}
). For each
j\in[n]
, let
\overline{\bm{D}}_{j}
be the averaged estimate for
D_{j}
. Suppose we have the uniform guarantee:

\|\overline{\bm{D}}_{j}-D_{j}\|\leq\delta_{{\mathrm{pas}}}\quad\text{except with probability }\eta_{{\mathrm{pas}}},

(6.31)

for some
\delta_{{\mathrm{pas}}},\eta_{{\mathrm{pas}}}\in(0,1)
. It remains to determine the sufficient number
N_{{\mathrm{pas}}}
of copies of
|\psi_{j}\rangle
to achieve this; we will address this aspect later in
Section

6.3
(see
Corollary

6.12
). For now, the intuition is that as long as
\delta_{{\mathrm{act}}}
is small enough, the copy complexity compared to the passive case is nearly unchanged.

Supposing that the guarantee holds, the analogue of
Proposition

5.1
is as follows.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\overline{\bm{D}}_{1},\ldots,\overline{\bm{D}}_{n}
be as in
Eq.

6.31
. Take their top eigenvectors
|\widehat{\bm{u}}_{j}\rangle
and concatenate them into the columns of a matrix
\widehat{\bm{U}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. If we construct the unitary matrix
\bm{U}^{\star}\coloneqq\bm{X}\bm{Y}^{\dagger}
, where
\widehat{\bm{U}}=\bm{X}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{Y}^{\dagger}
is the SVD of
\widehat{\bm{U}}
, then except with probability at most
n\eta_{{\mathrm{pas}}}
,

\min_{\Theta\in\diag(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\bm{U}^{\star}-Ue^{i\Theta}\|\leq 4\sqrt{2n}(\delta_{{\mathrm{pas}}}+\delta_{{\mathrm{act}}}),

(6.32)

where
\delta_{{\mathrm{act}}}
is the error from
Corollary

6.2
.

Proof.

We condition on the event in
Eq.

6.31
, which occurs for all
j\in[n]
except with probability at most
n\eta_{{\mathrm{pas}}}
by a union bound. Use
2.2
to assert that

\begin{split}\||\widehat{\bm{u}}_{j}\rangle\!\langle\widehat{\bm{u}}_{j}|-|u_{j}\rangle\!\langle u_{j}|\|&\leq 2\|\overline{\bm{D}}_{j}-|u_{j}\rangle\!\langle u_{j}|\|\\
&\leq 2(\|\overline{\bm{D}}_{j}-D_{j}\|+\|D_{j}-|u_{j}\rangle\!\langle u_{j}|\|)\\
&\leq 2(\delta_{{\mathrm{pas}}}+\delta_{{\mathrm{act}}}).\end{split}

(6.33)

It is straightforward to convert this to a Euclidean distance between the vectors:

\begin{split}\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\||\widehat{\bm{u}}_{j}\rangle-e^{i\theta}|u_{j}\rangle\|&=\sqrt{2-2|\langle\widehat{\bm{u}}_{j}|u_{j}\rangle|}\\
&\leq\sqrt{2(1-|\langle\widehat{\bm{u}}_{j}|u_{j}\rangle|^{2})}\\
&=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\||\widehat{\bm{u}}_{j}\rangle\!\langle\widehat{\bm{u}}_{j}|-|u_{j}\rangle\!\langle u_{j}|\|_{1}\\
&\leq\sqrt{2}\||\widehat{\bm{u}}_{j}\rangle\!\langle\widehat{\bm{u}}_{j}|-|u_{j}\rangle\!\langle u_{j}|\|\\
&\leq 2\sqrt{2}(\delta_{{\mathrm{pas}}}+\delta_{{\mathrm{act}}}).\end{split}

(6.34)

Hence there exists some diagonal real matrix
\Theta
such that

\|\widehat{\bm{U}}-Ue^{i\Theta}\|\leq\|\widehat{\bm{U}}-Ue^{i\Theta}\|_{F}\leq 2\sqrt{2n}(\delta_{{\mathrm{pas}}}+\delta_{{\mathrm{act}}}),

(6.35)

from which

6.32
follows by another application of
2.2
.
∎

Remark 6.7
.

The astute reader may recognize that we use a lossy conversion from operator norm to Frobenius norm in

. In contrast, the analogous proof of

. We apply the same here, using the states

(6.36)

where

is the discrete Fourier transform. Note that the presence of

causes no fundamental obstruction, as the success of the trick only relies on an error bound of the form

.

.

Let

. Suppose

Then given estimates for the

-RDMs of all

states

and

, each obeying

6.31
, we can output a unitary matrix

such that
\phdist(\bm{U}^{\sharp},U)\leq 25\varepsilon_{{\mathrm{pas}}}
with probability at least
1-2n\eta_{{\mathrm{pas}}}
.

Proof.

The proof is identical to
Proposition

5.2
, using
Lemma

6.6
as the base “up to column phases” algorithm.
∎

6.3
\mathrm{U}(n)
-shadows beyond number symmetry

[
48
]
originally described the
\mathrm{U}(n)
-shadows protocol within a fixed particle number subspace. This restriction is of course meaningful, but it turns out to be not necessary. The purpose of this subsection is to show how the method extends to arbitrary states, regardless of number symmetry. This is crucial for our analysis, because the probe states
|\psi_{j}\rangle
and
|\widetilde{\psi}_{j}\rangle
do not have such symmetry.

The key fact about classical shadows is that the learnability of observables is ultimately state-agnostic. There is a simple sufficient criterion to check: whether or not the observable
O
is orthogonal to the kernel of the measurement channel
\mathcal{M}
. Indeed, recall that classical shadow estimators are of the form
\tr(O\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\bm{\sigma}))
, where
\bm{\sigma}
is the postmeasurement state obeying
\E[\bm{\sigma}]=\mathcal{M}(\rho)
for input state
\rho
. But
\mathcal{M}
is self-adjoint, so this is equal to
\tr(\mathcal{M}^{-1}(O)\bm{\sigma})
. If
O\in\ker(\mathcal{M})
, then the pseudoinverse returns
0
and so the shadows protocol cannot learn
\tr(O\rho)
for any
\rho
. More generally, if
O
has any support in
\ker(\mathcal{M})
, then that component is killed and we cannot recover the associated information. Conversely, however, if
O\in\ker(\mathcal{M})^{\perp}
then
\E[\tr(O\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\bm{\sigma}))]=\tr(O\rho)
for
all
states
\rho
.

With this in mind, we can show that the estimator from
Proposition

3.2
is capable of learning the fermionic
1
-RDM of any state, regardless of any symmetry it obeys.

Claim 6.9
.

The estimator from
Proposition

3.2
continues to obey
\E[\widehat{\bm{D}}]=D
for
any
quantum state
\rho
, provided that we generalize the definition of
E(b)
to

E(b)\coloneqq(n+1)\diag(b)-|b|\mathbb{I},

(6.38)

where
|b|
is the Hamming weight of
b\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Proof.

As alluded to above, this follows from a more general property of classical shadows. Recall that the
\mathrm{U}(n)
-shadow channel
\mathcal{M}
is an average over two basic operations: rotation by the group
\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\mathrm{U}(n))
and projection into the number basis
\{0,1\}^{n}
. Both are block-diagonal in the number basis, which implies that
\mathcal{M}
admits the orthogonal decomposition

\mathcal{M}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\mathcal{M}_{k}.

(6.39)

In the case that
\rho
lies entirely in one particular number sector, say
k
, then all postmeasurement states
\bm{\sigma}=\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})^{\dagger}|\bm{b}\rangle\!\langle\bm{b}|\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(\bm{V})
also lie in that subspace. Hence
\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\bm{\sigma})=\mathcal{M}_{k}^{-1}(\bm{\sigma})
and we recover the familiar estimator from
Proposition

3.2
:

\begin{split}\widehat{\bm{D}}_{ij}&\coloneqq\tr(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\bm{\sigma}))\\
&=\tr(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}\mathcal{M}_{\eta}^{-1}(\bm{\sigma}))\\
&=[\bm{V}^{\dagger}((n+1)\diag(\bm{b})-k\mathbb{I})\bm{V}]_{ij}.\end{split}

(6.40)

Now consider arbitrary
\rho
; each instance of
\bm{\sigma}
still lies in some
|\bm{b}|
-number sector, although
|\bm{b}|
itself is now a random variable. Therefore
\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\bm{\sigma})=\mathcal{M}_{|\bm{b}|}^{-1}(\bm{\sigma})
so replacing
k\to|\bm{b}|
in
Eq.

6.40
shows the claim.
∎

6.3.1
Perturbed RDM estimation

The first modification of the analysis concerns the columnwise estimates of
U
. We re-analyze the application of matrix Bernstein for the states
|\psi_{j}\rangle
and
|\widetilde{\psi}_{j}\rangle
, which are of the form
\Phi(Z)|\phi\rangle
for an arbitrary single-particle Slater determinant
|\phi\rangle
. It will be useful to work with
Z
in the ladder operator (rather than Majorana) representation. This is standard fare for working with Bogoliubov transformations, but for completeness we show how to derive it from the Majorana representation in
Appendix

D
.

Claim 6.10
.

Let
Z\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
. The FLO transformation by
Z
on annihilation operators
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
can be expressed as

\Phi(Z)^{\dagger}a_{j}\Phi(Z)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\left(\alpha_{jk}a_{k}+\beta_{jk}^{*}a_{k}^{\dagger}\right)\mathclose{},

(6.41)

where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
are such that

\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta^{*}\\
\beta&\alpha^{*}\end{pmatrix}=\Omega Z\Omega^{\dagger},\quad\text{where }\Omega=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}&i\mathbb{I}\\
\mathbb{I}&-i\mathbb{I}\end{pmatrix}.

(6.42)

The matrix variances are bounded as follows.

Theorem 6.11
.

Let
|\psi\rangle=\Phi(Z)|\phi\rangle
where
Z\in\mathrm{SO}(2n)
and
|\phi\rangle
is a single-particle Slater determinant. Let
\widehat{\bm{D}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\bm{D}}_{N}
be i.i.d. copies of its
\mathrm{U}(n)
-shadow estimate
\widehat{\bm{D}}=\bm{V}^{\dagger}E(\bm{b})\bm{V}
, and define
\bm{X}_{\ell}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}(\widehat{\bm{D}}_{\ell}-D)
where
D
is the
1
-RDM of
|\psi\rangle
. Suppose that
\|Z-\mathbb{I}\|\leq\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}
for some
0<c<1
. Then the parameters for the matrix Bernstein inequality (
Proposition

2.4
) applied to the sequence
(\bm{X}_{\ell})_{\ell\in[N]}
can be taken as

B=\frac{n+1}{N}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma^{2}\leq\frac{C_{1}n+C_{2}}{N},

(6.43)

where
C_{1}=2(\sqrt{2+7c^{2}+c^{4}}+1)+c^{2}
and
C_{2}=5+8c^{2}+c^{4}
.

Proof.

The norm bound
B
is the same as in
Lemma

3.3
because it still holds that
\|D\|\leq 1
and
\|\widehat{\bm{D}}\|=\|E(\bm{b})\|=\max\{n+1-|\bm{b}|,|\bm{b}|\}\leq n
.

For the variance, recall
Eq.

3.10
except replacing
\eta
with
|\bm{b}|
:

E(\bm{b})^{2}=(n+1-2|\bm{b}|)E(\bm{b})+|\bm{b}|(n+1-|\bm{b}|)\mathbb{I}.

(6.44)

Then since
\widehat{\bm{D}}^{2}=\bm{V}^{\dagger}E(\bm{b})^{2}\bm{V}
, we have

\begin{split}\sigma^{2}&=\mathopen{}\left\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\E[\bm{X}_{\ell}^{2}]\right\|\mathclose{}=\frac{1}{N}\mathopen{}\left\|{\E[\widehat{\bm{D}}^{2}]-D^{2}}\right\|\mathclose{}\\
&=\frac{1}{N}\mathopen{}\left\|{\E[(n+1-2|\bm{b}|)\widehat{\bm{D}}+|\bm{b}|(n+1-|\bm{b}|)\mathbb{I}]-D^{2}}\right\|\mathclose{}\\
&=\frac{1}{N}\mathopen{}\left\|{(n+1)D-2\E[|\bm{b}|\widehat{\bm{D}}]+\mathopen{}\left((n+1)\E|\bm{b}|-\E|\bm{b}|^{2}\right)\mathclose{}\mathbb{I}-D^{2}}\right\|\mathclose{}.\end{split}

(6.45)

In contrast to the number-conserving case,
|\bm{b}|
is now a random variable, so we need to control its first two moments. These are simply the moments of the particle number operator
\mathsf{Num}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}
with respect to the fixed state
|\psi\rangle
, as the random measurement bases
\bm{V}
commute with
\mathsf{Num}
. We defer this calculation to
Lemma

6.15
below, where we show that

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\mathsf{Num}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\leq 1+\|\beta\|_{F}^{2},

(6.46)

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\mathsf{Num}^{2}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\leq 2+7\|\beta\|_{F}^{2}+\|\beta\|_{F}^{4},

(6.47)

where
\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is given by the representation of
Z
described in
6.10
.

To bound the norm of
\beta
in terms of
\delta_{{\mathrm{act}}}\geq\|Z-\mathbb{I}\|
, observe that

\|\beta\|=\mathopen{}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\beta&0\end{pmatrix}\right\|\mathclose{}=\mathopen{}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\mathbb{I}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha-\mathbb{I}&\beta^{*}\\
\beta&\alpha^{*}-\mathbb{I}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right\|\mathclose{}\leq\|\Omega Z\Omega^{\dagger}-\mathbb{I}\|.

(6.48)

Hence the Frobenius norm obeys
\|\beta\|_{F}^{2}\leq n\delta_{{\mathrm{act}}}^{2}
and so

\displaystyle\E|\bm{b}|

\displaystyle=\langle\psi|\mathsf{Num}|\psi\rangle\leq 1+n\delta_{{\mathrm{act}}}^{2},

(6.49)

\displaystyle\E|\bm{b}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi|\mathsf{Num}^{2}|\psi\rangle\leq 2+7n\delta_{{\mathrm{act}}}^{2}+n^{2}\delta_{{\mathrm{act}}}^{4}.

(6.50)

A crude triangle inequality suffices to get an estimate of
Eq.

6.45
:

N\sigma^{2}\leq(n+2)+2\|{\E[|\bm{b}|\widehat{\bm{D}}]}\|+(n+1)\E|\bm{b}|+\E|\bm{b}|^{2}.

(6.51)

To handle the cross term we apply Cauchy–Schwarz for expectations:

\begin{split}\|{\E[|\bm{b}|\widehat{\bm{D}}]}\|&=\max_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\mathopen{}\left|{\E[|\bm{b}|\langle v|\widehat{\bm{D}}|v\rangle]}\right|\mathclose{}\\
&\leq\max_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\mathopen{}\left(\sqrt{\E|\bm{b}|^{2}}\sqrt{\E[\langle v|\widehat{\bm{D}}|v\rangle^{2}]}\right)\mathclose{}\\
&\leq\sqrt{\E|\bm{b}|^{2}}\sqrt{\E\|\widehat{\bm{D}}\|^{2}}\\
&\leq n\sqrt{\E|\bm{b}|^{2}}.\end{split}

(6.52)

The advertised bound for
\sigma^{2}
follows from setting
\delta_{{\mathrm{act}}}=\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}
and some elementary algebra.
∎

Thus, provided that we execute the first-stage learning (
Corollary

6.2
) with error
\delta_{{\mathrm{act}}}=\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
, the number of copies to estimate the RDM of
\Phi(Z)|\phi\rangle
is nearly the same as that for
|\phi\rangle
itself. This establishes the size of
N
sufficient to guarantee the event in
Eq.

6.31
.

Corollary 6.12
.

Let
|\psi\rangle=\Phi(Z)|\phi\rangle
,
D
, and
(\widehat{\bm{D}}_{\ell})_{\ell\in[N]}
be as in
Theorem

6.11
. Fix
\delta_{{\mathrm{pas}}},\eta_{{\mathrm{pas}}}\in(0,1)
. Except with probability
\eta_{{\mathrm{pas}}}
, it holds that

\|\overline{\bm{D}}-D\|\leq\delta_{{\mathrm{pas}}},\quad\text{where }\overline{\bm{D}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\widehat{\bm{D}}_{\ell},

(6.53)

provided that

N\geq\frac{(C_{1}^{\prime}n+C_{2}^{\prime})\log(2n/\eta_{{\mathrm{pas}}})}{\delta_{{\mathrm{pas}}}^{2}}.

(6.54)

The constants above can be taken as
C_{i}^{\prime}=2(C_{i}+\frac{1}{3})
where
C_{1},C_{2}
are also from
Theorem

6.11
.

Proof.

Follows from applying
Theorem

6.11
to
Proposition

2.4
.
∎

The remainder of this subsection is devoted to proving the claimed bounds on
\langle\psi|\mathsf{Num}|\psi\rangle
and
\langle\psi|\mathsf{Num}^{2}|\psi\rangle
from
Eqs.

6.46
and
6.47
. We will need the following formulation of Wick’s theorem due to Lieb
[
46
]
(see also
[
4
,
34
]
for more modern treatments and generalizations).

Proposition 6.13

(Wick’s theorem)
.

Let
|\psi\rangle
be a fermionic Gaussian state and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}
any sequence of single-mode operators, i.e.,

b_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\left(A_{jk}a_{k}+B_{jk}a_{k}^{\dagger}\right)\mathclose{}

(6.55)

for arbitrary complex coefficients
A_{jk},B_{jk}\in\mathbb{C}
. Define the skew-symmetric matrix
S\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
whose entries are the two-point correlators:

S_{pq}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\langle\psi|b_{p}b_{q}|\psi\rangle&\text{if }p<q,\\
-S_{qp}&\text{if }p>q,\\
0&\text{if }p=q.\end{cases}

(6.56)

Then the many-body correlator is the Pfaffian of
S
:

\langle\psi|b_{1}\cdots b_{m}|\psi\rangle=\pf(S).

(6.57)

To form the associated
S
matrix, we need to establish the relevant two-point correlators. It will be convenient to view the FLO
\Phi(Z)
as rotating the modes, while taking the state in Wick’s theorem as
|\phi\rangle
.

Claim 6.14
.

Let
|\phi\rangle
be a Slater determinant. Let
Z\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
as in
6.10
. Define the quasi-particle operators

b_{j}\coloneqq\Phi(Z)^{\dagger}a_{j}\Phi(Z),\quad b_{j}^{\dagger}=\Phi(Z)^{\dagger}a_{j}^{\dagger}\Phi(Z)

(6.58)

for each
j\in[n]
. Then

\displaystyle\langle\phi|b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{k}|\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=[\alpha^{*}C\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\beta^{\dagger}]_{jk},

(6.59a)

\displaystyle\langle\phi|b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{k}^{\dagger}|\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=[\alpha^{*}C\beta^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\alpha^{\dagger}]_{jk},

(6.59b)

\displaystyle\langle\phi|b_{j}b_{k}^{\dagger}|\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=[\mathbb{I}-\alpha C^{\mathrm{T}}\alpha^{\dagger}-\beta^{*}(\mathbb{I}-C)\beta^{\mathrm{T}}]_{jk},

(6.59c)

\displaystyle\langle\phi|b_{j}b_{k}|\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=[\beta^{*}C\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}+\alpha(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\beta^{\dagger}]_{jk},

(6.59d)

where
C_{pq}\coloneqq\langle\phi|a_{p}^{\dagger}a_{q}|\phi\rangle
is the
1
-RDM of
|\phi\rangle
.

Proof.

First, we establish
Eq.

6.59a
:

\begin{split}\langle\phi|b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{k}|\phi\rangle&=\sum_{p,q=1}^{n}\langle\phi|\mathopen{}\left(\alpha_{jp}^{*}a_{p}^{\dagger}+\beta_{jp}a_{p}\right)\mathclose{}\mathopen{}\left(\alpha_{kq}a_{q}+\beta_{kq}^{*}a_{q}^{\dagger}\right)\mathclose{}|\phi\rangle\\
&=\sum_{p,q=1}^{n}\langle\phi|\mathopen{}\left(\alpha_{jp}^{*}\alpha_{kq}a_{p}^{\dagger}a_{q}+\beta_{jp}\beta_{kq}^{*}a_{p}a_{q}^{\dagger}\right)\mathclose{}|\phi\rangle\\
&=\sum_{p,q=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\left(\alpha_{jp}^{*}\alpha_{kq}\langle\phi|a_{p}^{\dagger}a_{q}|\phi\rangle+\beta_{jp}\beta_{kq}^{*}(\delta_{pq}-\langle\phi|a_{q}^{\dagger}a_{p}|\phi\rangle)\right)\mathclose{}\\
&=[\alpha^{*}C\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\beta^{\dagger}]_{jk},\end{split}

(6.60)

where we used the fact that
\langle\phi|a_{p}a_{q}|\phi\rangle=\langle\phi|a_{p}^{\dagger}a_{q}^{\dagger}|\phi\rangle=0
on the second line and the CAR
a_{p}a_{q}^{\dagger}+a_{q}^{\dagger}a_{p}=\delta_{pq}
on the third. Then the derivation for
Eq.

6.59b
is analogous, merely swapping the roles of
\alpha
and
\beta
acting on the right. Finally,
Eq.

6.59c
is a consequence of the identity
\langle\phi|b_{j}b_{k}^{\dagger}|\phi\rangle=\delta_{jk}-\langle\phi|b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{j}|\phi\rangle
(the
b_{j}
’s obey the same CAR because anticommutators are unitarily invariant), and
Eq.

6.59d
follows from
\langle\phi|b_{j}b_{k}|\phi\rangle=\langle\phi|b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{j}^{\dagger}|\phi\rangle^{*}
(also recall that
C
is Hermitian).
∎

We are now ready to prove the claim from
Eqs.

6.46
and
6.47
.

Lemma 6.15
.

Let
|\psi\rangle=\Phi(Z)|\phi\rangle
where
Z\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
and
|\phi\rangle
is a single-particle Slater determinant. It holds that

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\mathsf{Num}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\leq 1+\|\beta\|_{F}^{2},

(6.61)

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\mathsf{Num}^{2}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\leq 2+7\|\beta\|_{F}^{2}+\|\beta\|_{F}^{4},

(6.62)

where
\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is as in
6.10
.

Proof.

We begin with the first moment. Define
b_{j}
as in
6.14
. Using
Eq.

6.59a
, we get

\begin{split}\langle\psi|\mathsf{Num}|\psi\rangle&=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle\psi|a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}|\psi\rangle\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle\phi|b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{j}|\phi\rangle\\
&=\tr(\alpha^{*}C\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\beta^{\dagger}).\end{split}

(6.63)

We simplify this via the following facts. First, unitarity of
\Omega Z\Omega^{\dagger}
implies that
\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}\alpha^{*}+\beta^{\mathrm{T}}\beta^{*}=\mathbb{I}
. Second,
\tr(C)=1
because
|\phi\rangle
is a single-particle state. Finally, because both
\beta^{\mathrm{T}}\beta^{*}
and
C
are PSD,
\tr(\beta^{\mathrm{T}}\beta^{*}C)\geq 0
. Altogether, these imply that

\begin{split}\langle\psi|\mathsf{Num}|\psi\rangle&=\tr((\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}\alpha^{*}+\beta^{\mathrm{T}}\beta^{*})C)-2\tr(\beta^{\mathrm{T}}\beta^{*}C)+\|\beta\|_{F}^{2}\\
&\leq 1+\|\beta\|_{F}^{2},\end{split}

(6.64)

which is
Eq.

6.61
.

Next we consider the second moment. For each term in
\mathsf{Num}^{2}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{k}
, we apply Wick’s theorem (
Proposition

6.13
). Recall that the Pfaffian of a
4\times 4
matrix
S
is
\pf(S)=S_{12}S_{34}-S_{13}S_{24}+S_{14}S_{23}
. Hence with
Eq.

6.59
,

\begin{split}\langle\psi|a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{k}|\psi\rangle&=\langle\phi|b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{j}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}|\phi\rangle\\
&=\langle\phi|b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{j}|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}|\phi\rangle-\langle\phi|b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{k}^{\dagger}|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|b_{j}b_{k}|\phi\rangle+\langle\phi|b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{k}|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|b_{j}b_{k}^{\dagger}|\phi\rangle\\
&=\langle\phi|b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{j}|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}|\phi\rangle\\
&\hphantom{=~}-[\alpha^{*}C\beta^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\alpha^{\dagger}]_{jk}[\beta^{*}C\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}+\alpha(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\beta^{\dagger}]_{jk}\\
&\hphantom{=~}+[\alpha^{*}C\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\beta^{\dagger}]_{jk}[\mathbb{I}-\alpha C^{\mathrm{T}}\alpha^{\dagger}-\beta^{*}(\mathbb{I}-C)\beta^{\mathrm{T}}]_{jk}.\end{split}

(6.65)

Now sum each term from Wick’s theorem over
j
and
k
. The first simply yields
\langle\psi|\mathsf{Num}|\psi\rangle^{2}
. The second is

\begin{split}&\hphantom{=~}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}[\alpha^{*}C\beta^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\alpha^{\dagger}]_{jk}[\beta^{*}C\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}+\alpha(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\beta^{\dagger}]_{jk}\\
&=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}[\alpha^{*}C\beta^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\alpha^{\dagger}]_{jk}[\beta C^{*}\alpha^{\dagger}+\alpha^{*}(\mathbb{I}-C^{\dagger})\beta^{\mathrm{T}}]_{jk}^{*}\\
&=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}[\alpha^{*}C\beta^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\alpha^{\dagger}]_{jk}[\beta C^{\mathrm{T}}\alpha^{\dagger}+\alpha^{*}\beta^{\mathrm{T}}-\alpha^{*}C\beta^{\mathrm{T}}]_{jk}^{*}\\
&=-\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}[\alpha^{*}C\beta^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\alpha^{\dagger}]_{jk}[\alpha^{*}C\beta^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\alpha^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}\beta^{\mathrm{T}}-\beta\alpha^{\dagger}]_{jk}^{*}\\
&=-\|\alpha^{*}C\beta^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\alpha^{\dagger}\|_{F}^{2},\end{split}

(6.66)

where on the last line we used that
\alpha^{*}\beta^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta\alpha^{\dagger}=0
due to unitarity of
\Omega Z\Omega^{\dagger}
. The third term from Wick’s theorem is

\begin{split}&\hphantom{=~}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}[\alpha^{*}C\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\beta^{\dagger}]_{jk}[\mathbb{I}-\alpha C^{\mathrm{T}}\alpha^{\dagger}-\beta^{*}(\mathbb{I}-C)\beta^{\mathrm{T}}]_{jk}\\
&=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}[\alpha^{*}C\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\beta^{\dagger}]_{jk}[\mathbb{I}-\alpha^{*}C^{\dagger}\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}-\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{*})\beta^{\dagger}]_{jk}^{*}\\
&=\tr(\alpha^{*}C\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\beta^{\dagger})-\|\alpha^{*}C\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\beta^{\dagger}\|_{F}^{2}\\
&=\langle\psi|\mathsf{Num}|\psi\rangle-\|\alpha^{*}C\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\beta^{\dagger}\|_{F}^{2}.\end{split}

(6.67)

We collect these results to obtain

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\mathsf{Num}^{2}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi|\mathsf{Num}|\psi\rangle^{2}+\|\alpha^{*}C\beta^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\alpha^{\dagger}\|_{F}^{2}+\langle\psi|\mathsf{Num}|\psi\rangle-\|\alpha^{*}C\alpha^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\beta^{\dagger}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\|\beta\|_{F}^{2})^{2}+(1+\|\beta\|_{F}^{2})+\|\alpha^{*}C\beta^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\alpha^{\dagger}\|_{F}^{2}.

(6.68)

It remains to bound the last term. Note that the Frobenius norm has the property
\|AB\|_{F}\leq\|A\|_{F}\|B\|
, which is stronger than mere submultiplicativity. Use this property to get

\begin{split}\|\alpha^{*}C\beta^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\alpha^{\dagger}\|_{F}&\leq\|\alpha^{*}C\beta^{\mathrm{T}}\|_{F}+\|\beta(\mathbb{I}-C^{\mathrm{T}})\alpha^{\dagger}\|_{F}\\
&\leq\|\alpha\|\|C\|\|\beta\|_{F}+\|\beta\|_{F}\|\mathbb{I}-C\|\|\alpha\|\\
&\leq 2\|\beta\|_{F},\end{split}

(6.69)

where we used the additional fact that
\|\alpha\|\leq 1
since
\alpha
is a block within a unitary matrix. Plug this bound into
Eq.

6.68
to conclude the result of
Eq.

6.61
.
∎

We remark that one could obtain a slightly tighter bound by placing the Frobenius norm onto the
C
and
\mathbb{I}-C
terms rather than
\beta
in
Eq.

6.69
. We opt for the looser bound above due to clarity of presentation, as this ultimately only affects constant factors in our analysis.

Theorem 6.16
.

Let

Proof.

Begin by observing that the unitary we apply is

(6.71)

where

and

5.5
. The analysis is therefore identical to

5.5
, up to conjugating

and
Y
by
\Phi(Z)

. We will show shortly that

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{\Psi}(\theta)|X|\widetilde{\Psi}(\theta)\rangle

\displaystyle=r\cos(\theta+\xi),

(6.72a)

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{\Psi}(\theta)|Y|\widetilde{\Psi}(\theta)\rangle

\displaystyle=r\sin(\theta+\xi).

(6.72b)

The proof then follows

5.5
and

except that now

.

We conclude with the derivation of

6.72
. Embed

\begin{split}\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(W)^{\dagger}a_{m}a_{n+1}^{\dagger}\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(W)&=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n+1}[W\oplus 1]_{mj}[W\oplus 1]_{n+1,k}^{*}a_{j}a_{k}^{\dagger}\end{split}

(6.75)

and

\begin{split}\langle\Psi(\theta)|a_{j}a_{k}^{\dagger}|\Psi(\theta)\rangle&=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\left(\langle 0^{n+1}|a_{j}a_{k}^{\dagger}|0^{n+1}\rangle+e^{i\theta}\langle 0^{n+1}|a_{j}a_{k}^{\dagger}|1_{1}1_{n+1}\rangle\right.\mathclose{}\\

\begin{split}\langle\Psi(\theta)|\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(W)^{\dagger}a_{m}a_{n+1}^{\dagger}\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(W)|\Psi(\theta)\rangle=[(W\oplus 1)B(W^{\dagger}\oplus 1)]_{m,n+1}=0\end{split}

(6.77)

since the last column of

\langle\Psi(\theta)|\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(W)^{\dagger}\Phi(Z)^{\dagger}a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{n+1}^{\dagger}\Phi(Z)\Phi_{\mathrm{pas}}(W)|\Psi(\theta)\rangle=\frac{1}{2}e^{-i\theta}[\alpha W]_{11}^{*}.\qed

\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T}n^{\omega+3}\log(n/\delta_{t})\right)\mathclose{}=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(n^{\omega+3}(\log(n/\delta)T+T^{2})\right)\mathclose{}=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(n^{\omega+3}\log^{2}(n/{\min\{\varepsilon,\delta\}})\right)\mathclose{}.\qed

Tomography of Gaussian states

To learn Gaussian states of indeterminate particle number, we deploy a version of fermionic shadows employing active FLO measurements

Definition A.1
.

Let
\rho
be a quantum state on
n

\mathrm{SO}(2n)
-shadows protocol

1.

Draw a random matrix

\rho\mapsto\Phi(\bm{R})\rho\Phi(\bm{R})^{\dagger}

\bm{b}\in\{0,1\}^{n}

\langle\bm{b}|\Phi(\bm{R})\rho\Phi(\bm{R})^{\dagger}|\bm{b}\rangle

Each sample is stored as a tuple
(\bm{R},\bm{b})

\Phi(\bm{R})^{\dagger}|\bm{b}\rangle\!\langle\bm{b}|\Phi(\bm{R})

As before, this defines a quantum channel
\mathcal{M}

\rho=\E[\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\Phi(\bm{R})^{\dagger}|\bm{b}\rangle\!\langle\bm{b}|\Phi(\bm{R}))]

\mathrm{SO}(2n)
-shadows were designed to efficiently recover few-body fermionic observables; the covariance matrix estimator can be expressed compactly as follows.

Proposition A.2

Let
\rho

n
-mode state and
\Gamma

\mathrm{SO}(2n)

\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}=(2n-1)\bm{R}^{\mathrm{T}}J(\bm{b})\bm{R}

-(-1)^{\diag(b)}&0\end{pmatrix},

The formula can be derived from any of the aforementioned papers on matchgate shadows; we will follow

Appendix D
Quasi-particle representation of FLOs

Proof (of
6.10
).

Define the following “vector-of-operators” notation:

\vec{A}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\vec{A}_{1}\\
\vec{A}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{where }\vec{A}_{1}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{n}\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\vec{A}_{2}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}^{\dagger}\\
\vdots\\
a_{n}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix},

(D.1)

\vec{\gamma}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\vec{\gamma}_{1}\\
\vec{\gamma}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{where }\vec{\gamma}_{1}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma_{n}\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\vec{\gamma}_{2}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{n+1}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma_{2n}\end{pmatrix}.

(D.2)

These two are related via

\vec{A}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Omega\vec{\gamma}\quad\text{where }\Omega=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}&i\mathbb{I}\\
\mathbb{I}&-i\mathbb{I}\end{pmatrix}.

(D.3)

We also use the notation that operators transform elementwise, e.g.,

\Phi(Z)^{\dagger}\vec{\gamma}\Phi(Z)=\begin{pmatrix}\Phi(Z)^{\dagger}\gamma_{1}\Phi(Z)\\
\vdots\\
\Phi(Z)^{\dagger}\gamma_{2n}\Phi(Z)\end{pmatrix}=Z\vec{\gamma}.

(D.4)

Write
Z
in
n\times n
blocks:

Z=\begin{pmatrix}Z_{11}&Z_{12}\\
Z_{21}&Z_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

(D.5)

Then we straightforwardly compute:

\begin{split}\Phi(Z)^{\dagger}\vec{A}\Phi(Z)&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Phi(Z)^{\dagger}(\Omega\vec{\gamma})\Phi(Z)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\Phi(Z)^{\dagger}\begin{pmatrix}\vec{\gamma}_{1}+i\vec{\gamma}_{2}\\
\vec{\gamma}_{1}-i\vec{\gamma}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\Phi(Z)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}Z_{11}\vec{\gamma}_{1}+Z_{12}\vec{\gamma}_{2}+iZ_{21}\vec{\gamma}_{1}+iZ_{22}\vec{\gamma}_{2}\\
Z_{11}\vec{\gamma}_{1}+Z_{12}\vec{\gamma}_{2}-iZ_{21}\vec{\gamma}_{1}-iZ_{22}\vec{\gamma}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}(Z_{11}+iZ_{21})(\vec{A}_{1}+\vec{A}_{2})-i(Z_{12}+iZ_{22})(\vec{A}_{1}-\vec{A}_{2})\\
(Z_{11}-iZ_{21})(\vec{A}_{1}+\vec{A}_{2})-i(Z_{12}-iZ_{22})(\vec{A}_{1}-\vec{A}_{2})\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}[Z_{11}+Z_{22}-i(Z_{12}-Z_{21})]\vec{A}_{1}+[Z_{11}-Z_{22}+i(Z_{12}+Z_{21})]\vec{A}_{2}\\
[Z_{11}-Z_{22}-i(Z_{12}+Z_{21})]\vec{A}_{1}+[Z_{11}+Z_{22}+i(Z_{12}-Z_{21})]\vec{A}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta^{*}\\
\beta&\alpha^{*}\end{pmatrix}\vec{A}.\end{split}

(D.6)

where we have defined
\alpha\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}[Z_{11}+Z_{22}-i(Z_{12}-Z_{21})]
and
\beta\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}[Z_{11}-Z_{22}-i(Z_{12}+Z_{21})]
in the final line. In particular, one checks that

\Omega Z\Omega^{\dagger}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta^{*}\\
\beta&\alpha^{*}\end{pmatrix}.\qed
