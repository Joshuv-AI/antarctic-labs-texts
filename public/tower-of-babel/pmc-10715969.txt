Successful Pregnancy After Cardiac Arrest in a Woman With Severe Coronary Vasospasm
Authors: Thara S Ali, Ashley K Russell, Yuliya Paulenka, Emily R Baker, Leigh C Hickerson, Cynthia C Taub, Kerrilynn C Hennessey
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 37-year-old gravida 5, para 3 woman presented with an unplanned pregnancy 6 weeks after experiencing a cardiac arrest caused by ventricular fibrillation from coronary vasospasm. She opted to continue the pregnancy with medical management despite ongoing chest pain and delivered a healthy female infant via vaginal delivery at 37 weeks.Key Words: cardiac arrest, coronary vasospasm, pregnancy, ventricular tachycardia

Case Presentation

A 37-year-old gravida 5, para 3 female presented to the cardio-obstetrics clinic with an unplanned pregnancy 6 weeks after hospitalization for severe coronary vasospasm complicated by ventricular fibrillation (VF) and cardiac arrest.

Before this event, her medical history was notable for smoking, chronic hypertension, and multiple psychiatric comorbidities. She had 3 prior uncomplicated pregnancies with healthy children born by normal spontaneous vaginal deliveries. There was no family history of premature coronary disease, congenital cardiac conditions, or sudden cardiac death.

Approximately 6 weeks before presentation, she complained of acute burning chest pain and experienced a witnessed cardiac arrest at home requiring cardiopulmonary resuscitation, including intravenous amiodarone and defibrillation for VF. Her initial electrocardiogram (Figure 1) showed sinus tachycardia without diagnostic ST-segment changes, QTc prolongation, pre-excitation, or pathologic Q waves. An echocardiogram demonstrated normal biventricular size and function, with normal wall thickness and no segmental wall motion abnormalities or significant valve disease. Emergent coronary angiography was performed with immediate spasm of the right femoral artery access site. On engaging the left main artery, the patient went into ventricular tachycardia, which rapidly deteriorated into refractory VF requiring 8 defibrillations, amiodarone, and lidocaine. A single shot of the left coronary system was obtained showing sequential stenosis of the left anterior descending and left circumflex arteries (Figure 2). She was started on a nicardipine infusion for severe vasospasm. Angiography was aborted without administering intracoronary nitroglycerin or injecting the right coronary artery. Her mental status recovered, and a subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was inserted. She was transitioned to amlodipine and had no further ventricular arrhythmia. She was discharged on aspirin, a high-intensity statin, lisinopril, amlodipine, and nitroglycerin as needed. Smoking cessation was strongly recommended, but contraception was not discussed before discharge.

Coronary Angiogram Showing Coronary Vasospasm

Before her initial cardio-obstetrics visit, we reviewed her recent coronary angiogram with the interventional cardiologist who had performed her procedure and an outside expert for evidence of spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD). Although coronary vasospasm was considered the most likely explanation for her presentation, our team could not exclude atherosclerosis because her right coronary artery had not been evaluated. Her vital signs were notable for pulse of 95 beats/min, blood pressure of 139/69 mm Hg, and oxygenation of 100% on room air. On physical examination, she was well kept and euvolemic, with normal cardiac examination findings. The patient was counseled that pregnancy was not advisable because of a high risk for a maternal cardiac event, including ventricular arrhythmia and acute coronary syndrome (ACS). The estimated maternal cardiac event rate using the CARPREG II (Cardiac Disease in Pregnancy II) risk prediction score was at least 15% for a prior history of cardiac event or arrhythmia (3 points) and more than 40% if there was undiagnosed atherosclerosis (additional 2 points).1 The patient did not want to consider termination. Her case was discussed in a multidisciplinary team, including cardiology, electrophysiology, anesthesia, and maternal-fetal medicine (MFM). Her lisinopril and statin were discontinued for potential teratogenicity. Her amlodipine was up-titrated, and nitroglycerin was continued. Despite these adjustments, she continued to report intermittent chest burning, fatigue, dyspnea, dizziness, and sweating. A coronary computed tomography angiogram was obtained and excluded obstructive atherosclerosis (Figure 3). There was no ventricular arrhythmia on weekly ICD interrogations through pregnancy.

The patient was planned for induction of labor at 39 weeks but experienced escalating psychological distress regarding her physical fitness for pregnancy toward the end of her third trimester and had multiple emergency visits for chest pain. After an extensive shared decision-making discussion with the patient, her family, and our multidisciplinary team, she was brought in for induction of labor at 37 weeks. She continued her amlodipine and long-acting nitrate through delivery. Her ICD was disabled during labor, and external defibrillator pads were placed. A healthy female infant was delivered via uncomplicated vaginal delivery with induction. She agreed to a progesterone-only contraceptive at discharge with plans for outpatient tubal ligation.

Question 1: What is the differential diagnosis for this patient’s recent ACS?

The differential diagnosis for her ACS includes obstructive epicardial atherosclerosis or embolism of the right coronary artery, SCAD, coronary vasospasm (idiopathic or substance related), takotsubo syndrome, myocarditis, and coronary microvascular dysfunction. Coronary angiography and coronary computed tomography angiogram excluded obstructive atherosclerosis or embolism. Her catheterization films were reviewed with SCAD experts from multiple institutions and thought not to represent SCAD. Takotsubo syndrome and myocarditis were thought to be unlikely in the setting of normal left ventricular systolic function. Smoking is a risk factor for both coronary microvascular dysfunction and vasospasm. Femoral spasm was noted when obtaining arterial access. She experienced transient electrical and hemodynamic instability with contrast injection into the left coronary arteries, which had severe sequential stenosis by angiography. She did not undergo administration of high-dose intracoronary nitroglycerin because of hemodynamic instability at the time of her catheterization; however, she had no further ventricular arrhythmia on a calcium-channel blocker, which is the treatment of choice for vasospasm.

Question 2: How does the classification of her initial ACS influence management?

This is the first report, to our knowledge, of severe coronary vasospasm resulting in cardiac arrest being safely managed through pregnancy with a calcium-channel blocker. There are very few data to guide risk stratification and management of coronary vasospasm during pregnancy. Pregnancy may, however, be a trigger for coronary vasospasm, and therefore the patient was counseled that the pregnancy had increased risk. Proposed mechanisms include primary systemic vasodilation leading to increased sympathetic hormones, release of vasopressin, and stimulation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.2 A previously published case describes increased episodes of coronary vasospasm during pregnancy with refractory ventricular arrhythmias requiring high-dose medications and chemical sympathectomy for management.3 Identification of epicardial coronary disease or microvascular dysfunction would have prompted initiation of antiplatelet therapy, a nitrate, and a beta blocker. A statin would be added after delivery and breastfeeding. Beta blockers would also be important if SCAD had been identified, whereas the role of antiplatelet and statin therapy is less clear.

Question 3: Who should be counseled regarding contraception after an ACS?

Contraception should be discussed with all women of reproductive age with acquired cardiovascular disease and recommendations made regarding the timing and advisability of future pregnancy. According to the U.S. medical eligibility criteria for contraception, levonorgestrel intrauterine devices, combined hormonal contraceptives and depot medroxyprogesterone acetate, should be avoided in patients with cardiovascular disease.4 We therefore recommended a progesterone-only contraceptive as a bridge to outpatient tubal ligation.

Question 4: What specific risk factors for recurrent maternal cardiovascular event were identified in this patient?

Active tobacco use was the patient’s primary risk factor for coronary vasospasm. Early in her pregnancy, obstructive epicardial coronary disease had not been fully excluded, and she had ongoing symptoms of chest pain; therefore, our team considered her risk for ACS more broadly. Pregnancy is a time of high maternal physiologic stress because of conditions such as high circulatory volume, high cardiac output, and increased sympathetic tone.2 The risk of ACS in pregnancy is 3 to 4 times higher than for nonpregnant women of reproductive age.5 Smoking, chronic hypertension, advanced maternal age, diabetes, and pre-eclampsia are independent risk factors for ACS in pregnancy.6 Our patient had 3 of these risk factors. Maternal and obstetric outcomes for pregnant women with ischemic heart disease have been published using prospective, multicenter The Registry of Pregnancy and Cardiac Disease data.6 The median age in this population was 36 years, and 17% were smokers; however, only 1 woman experienced ventricular arrhythmia before pregnancy, and there were no pregnancies among women with a history of cardiac arrest. Of 104 pregnant women with pre-existing ischemic heart disease, 11 experienced ACS during pregnancy, which highlights the complexity of this patient’s clinical presentation. Notably, women who had undergone revascularization before pregnancy did not have fewer events than women who did not.6

Using these data, our cardio-obstetrics team estimated that there was a high risk of maternal adverse cardiac events during pregnancy that could not be completely characterized using the CARPREG II risk score or modified World Health Organization risk score, particularly in light of her recent cardiac arrest, symptom status in early pregnancy, and ongoing diagnostic evaluation for her presenting ACS.

Question 5: What preparations are necessary for delivery planning in a woman with a history of cardiac arrest secondary to ventricular tachycardia with an ICD?

A vaginal mode of delivery is appropriate for most women with cardiovascular disease7 unless Valsalva is contraindicated, that is, when there is a high risk aortopathy, severe pulmonary hypertension, or an acute decompensation of a cardiac condition. Delivery timing is typically recommended by MFM, with adjustments made for fetal and maternal status in the later stages of pregnancy. High-risk cardiovascular patients may be offered induction of labor at 39 weeks to ensure the appropriate staff and equipment are available for a controlled and smooth delivery.7,8 Cesarean deliveries are most often performed for fetal and obstetric indications. Epidural analgesia can reduce the cardiac work of delivery and should be recommended early in labor for women who may not tolerate the elevations in afterload, heart rate, and sympathetic tone that accompany delivery.9 An assisted second stage of labor may be considered for women who are not expected to tolerate prolonged Valsalva in consultation with MFM. In our patient, medications to minimize the risk of ischemia and vasospasm were continued through delivery. There are no professional society guidelines to determine whether an intensive care setting is superior to the maternity ward for pregnant women with high cardiovascular risk. Because our patient had no evidence of ongoing ischemia or ventricular arrhythmia, our multidisciplinary team planned for delivery on the labor and delivery unit. Her subcutaneous ICD was disabled during labor to avoid inappropriate shocks from oversensed uterine contractions or myopotentials,10 and she was placed on cardiac telemetry with external defibrillator pads, where she had a successful induction of labor. Misoprostol and methylergonovine were avoided because of the theoretical risk of vasospasm. Our contingency planning for evidence of chest pain or ongoing vasospasm during delivery included intravenous nicardipine and emergent cesarean section.

Conclusions

The management of pregnant women with a history of ACS is highly individualized and team based. The risk of pregnancy, medical management, and delivery preparations require careful consideration for each patient. Contraception should be routinely recommended in women with a recent ACS to allow for cardio-obstetric risk assessment before family planning.
