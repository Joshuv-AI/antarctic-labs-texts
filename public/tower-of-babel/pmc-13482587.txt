Creatinine-normalized urinary lipocalin-2 in children with obesity: association with insulin resistance in a cross-sectional study
Authors: Aydin Celik, Busra Karadag Celik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildhood obesity is associated with insulin resistance and early cardiometabolic risk. Lipocalin-2 (LCN2), a biomarker linked to inflammation, metabolic regulation, and renal tubular stress, has been proposed as a potential indicator of obesity-related complications. However, data regarding urinary LCN2 in pediatric obesity remain limited. This study aimed to evaluate 24-h urinary LCN2 levels and their associations with metabolic parameters in children with obesity. This cross-sectional study included 43 children with obesity and 32 age-matched healthy controls aged 11–16 years. Anthropometric and biochemical assessments included fasting glucose, insulin, lipid profile, and renal-related urinary markers. Insulin resistance was assessed using HOMA-IR. Twenty-four-hour urinary LCN2 was measured by ELISA and normalized to urinary creatinine. Group comparisons and exploratory multivariable analyses were performed using appropriate statistical tests. The urinary LCN2/creatinine ratio was significantly higher in children with obesity than in controls. Absolute urinary LCN2 concentrations did not significantly differ between groups. Within the obesity group, urinary LCN2 concentrations were higher in participants classified as insulin resistant. Creatinine-normalized urinary LCN2 differed between children with obesity and controls and showed associations with insulin resistance status. These findings suggest that urinary LCN2 may reflect early metabolic or renal-related alterations in pediatric obesity. Introduction

Childhood obesity has become a major global health concern, with a substantial increase in prevalence over recent decades1. Children with obesity are at high risk of developing insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, systemic inflammation, and early cardiometabolic complications2,3. Importantly, obesity during childhood frequently persists into adulthood, significantly increasing the risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease4. Therefore, identifying early biomarkers associated with metabolic and organ-related complications in pediatric obesity is of considerable clinical importance.

Obesity is characterized not only by excess adiposity but also by complex endocrine and inflammatory alterations. Adipose tissue acts as an active endocrine organ, secreting adipokines that regulate glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and inflammatory responses5. Among these molecules, lipocalin-2 (LCN2), also known as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), has attracted increasing attention because of its involvement in metabolic regulation, inflammation, and tissue stress responses6. Recent reviews further highlight the emerging role of LCN2 in obesity-related metabolic dysfunction and insulin resistance7–9.

Experimental studies have suggested that LCN2 may participate in energy homeostasis, modulation of hepatic gluconeogenesis, and regulation of adipose tissue remodeling10. In adults, circulating LCN2 levels have been associated with obesity, hyperglycemia, and insulin resistance; however, reported findings remain inconsistent11,12. These inconsistencies suggest that LCN2 may reflect complex, stage-dependent metabolic and inflammatory pathways rather than a single linear relationship with adiposity.

Beyond its metabolic functions, LCN2 has also been extensively investigated as a biomarker of renal tubular injury and kidney stress13. Since obesity may contribute to early renal hyperfiltration, endothelial dysfunction, and subclinical kidney injury, urinary biomarkers may provide complementary information beyond circulating markers. Nevertheless, data regarding urinary LCN2 levels in pediatric obesity are scarce. Most existing studies have focused on serum measurements, and limited information is available on 24-hour urinary LCN2 excretion in children with obesity, particularly in relation to insulin resistance.

Given the increasing burden of childhood obesity and the need for non-invasive biomarkers reflecting early metabolic or renal-related alterations, further investigation of urinary LCN2 in pediatric populations is warranted. To our knowledge, the relationship between 24-hour urinary LCN2 levels and metabolic parameters in children with obesity has not been systematically evaluated14.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between 24-hour urinary LCN2 levels and metabolic and clinical parameters, particularly insulin resistance, in children with obesity.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted at the Pediatric Endocrinology outpatient clinic of Kecioren Training and Research Hospital, Ankara, Turkiye. Participant recruitment and data collection were performed in 2012 as part of an institutional specialty thesis project, and the current manuscript represents a subsequent secondary analysis of those prospectively collected data. The study aimed to evaluate urinary lipocalin-2 (LCN2) levels and their associations with metabolic parameters in children with obesity.

Participants and eligibility criteria

The study included 43 children with obesity and 32 healthy age-matched controls aged 11–16 years. Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) at or above the 95th percentile for age and sex according to nationally validated reference standards developed by Bundak et al.15.

Healthy controls were recruited from children attending the same institution for routine health evaluations and had BMI values within the normal reference range for age and sex.

Children were excluded if they had chronic systemic diseases (including respiratory, gastrointestinal, or cardiovascular disorders), endocrine diseases (e.g., Cushing syndrome or hypothyroidism), syndromic obesity (e.g., Prader–Willi syndrome), acute infection at assessment, or a history of medication use known to affect body weight or metabolic status (e.g., corticosteroids or antipsychotics).

Anthropometric and pubertal assessment

All participants underwent detailed physical examination. Body weight was measured using a calibrated digital scale with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes. Height was measured using a wall-mounted stadiometer. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2).

Pubertal staging was assessed according to Tanner criteria16. In girls, breast development stage II or higher was considered pubertal onset, whereas in boys, testicular volume ≥ 4 mL was accepted as pubertal onset.

Biochemical measurements

Venous blood samples were obtained from all participants in the morning after at least 12 h of overnight fasting. Serum glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and creatinine concentrations were measured using standard automated enzymatic methods in the hospital biochemistry laboratory. Serum insulin levels were measured using a commercially available immunoassay method. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) was determined by immunoturbidimetric assay. Serum cortisol levels were measured from morning fasting samples using routine chemiluminescent immunoassay methods.

Insulin resistance was evaluated using the Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) index. HOMA-IR was calculated using the standard formula: fasting insulin (µIU/mL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)/405. Puberty-specific cut-off values were applied to define insulin resistance, as previously described16.

Urinary LCN2 assessment

Twenty-four-hour urine samples were collected from all participants using standardized collection instructions. Total urine volume was recorded, and aliquots were centrifuged to remove debris and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Urinary lipocalin-2 (LCN2), also known as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), was measured using a commercially available Human Lipocalin-2/NGAL ELISA kit (BioVendor, Czech Republic) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Urinary LCN2 concentrations were expressed as ng/mL.

To account for urine concentration variability, urinary LCN2 was additionally normalized to urinary creatinine concentration and expressed as LCN2/creatinine ratio (ng/mg).

Urinary microalbumin levels and urinary albumin/creatinine ratio were also assessed using standard laboratory methods.

Imaging assessment

Abdominal ultrasonography was performed in children with obesity by an experienced radiologist using standard institutional protocols. Hepatic steatosis was assessed based on increased hepatic echogenicity compared with the renal cortex and graded according to routine radiological evaluation. Ultrasonographic findings were recorded for descriptive clinical assessment.

Renal function assessment

Renal function was assessed using estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), calculated by the Schwartz formula based on serum creatinine levels. Results were expressed as mL/min/1.73 m2.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate according to data distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as number (percentage).

Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test or Student’s t-test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when required.

Correlations between urinary LCN2 parameters and clinical or biochemical variables were assessed using Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses, according to variable distribution.

To explore independent associations with urinary LCN2/creatinine ratio in children with obesity, multivariable linear regression analysis was performed using clinically relevant covariates selected a priori, including age, BMI-SDS, hs-CRP, and insulin resistance status.

Variance inflation factor (VIF) values were examined to assess multicollinearity. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 75 participants were included, comprising 43 children with obesity and 32 healthy controls. There were no significant differences between groups regarding age or sex distribution (p > 0.05). BMI, BMI-SDS, waist circumference, and hip circumference were significantly higher in children with obesity than in controls (all p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Urinary LCN2 findings

Absolute urinary LCN2 concentrations did not significantly differ between children with obesity and controls (p = 0.830). However, the urinary LCN2/creatinine ratio was significantly higher in the obesity group than in controls (4.12 ± 3.01 vs. 2.14 ± 2.11 ng/mg, p = 0.004) (Table 2).

Exploratory subgroup analysis according to insulin resistance status

Insulin resistance was more frequently identified in children with obesity than in controls (p < 0.001). Within the obesity group, urinary LCN2 concentrations were higher in participants classified as insulin resistant than in those without insulin resistance (3.32 ± 2.94 vs. 1.66 ± 0.20 ng/mL, p = 0.018). In contrast, the difference in urinary LCN2/creatinine ratio between subgroups was not statistically significant (p = 0.368) (Table 3).

Correlation analysis

In the obesity group, neither absolute urinary LCN2 concentrations nor the urinary LCN2/creatinine ratio showed significant correlations with fasting glucose, lipid parameters, hs-CRP, eGFR, urinary microalbumin, or urinary albumin/creatinine ratio (all p > 0.05).

In the control group, absolute urinary LCN2 concentrations demonstrated significant positive correlations with HDL-C (r = 0.808, p < 0.001), fasting insulin (r = 0.619, p < 0.001), hs-CRP (r = 0.796, p < 0.001), urinary microalbumin (r = 0.694, p < 0.001), and urinary albumin/creatinine ratio (r = 0.625, p < 0.001).

No other statistically significant correlations were observed. Full correlation results are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Multivariate regression analysis

A multivariable linear regression model was constructed to explore factors independently associated with the urinary LCN2/creatinine ratio within the obesity group. Age, BMI-SDS, hs-CRP, and insulin resistance status were included as clinically relevant covariates. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) values.

In this exploratory model, insulin resistance status was independently associated with a higher urinary LCN2/creatinine ratio (β = 0.34, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.58, p = 0.006). Age, BMI-SDS, and hs-CRP were not significantly associated with the outcome. VIF values ranged from 1.15 to 1.62, indicating no evidence of relevant multicollinearity. The model explained 31% of the variance in urinary LCN2/creatinine ratio (adjusted R2 = 0.31) (Table 4).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that the urinary LCN2/creatinine ratio was significantly higher in children with obesity than in healthy controls, whereas absolute urinary LCN2 concentrations did not significantly differ between groups. In addition, insulin resistance status was associated with higher urinary LCN2 measures within the obesity group. These findings suggest that creatinine-normalized urinary LCN2 may reflect obesity-related metabolic or renal-related alterations; however, the underlying mechanisms are likely multifactorial.

Obesity is characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation, adipose tissue dysfunction, and progressive insulin resistance, all of which contribute to early cardiometabolic risk17,18. Adipose-derived mediators are increasingly recognized as contributors to obesity-related complications. In this context, LCN2 has been implicated not only in inflammatory pathways but also in glucose homeostasis, lipid metabolism, and tissue stress responses7–9,19.

Experimental studies have shown that LCN2 expression may increase in response to high-fat diet exposure and inflammatory signaling12,20. LCN2 has also been linked to hepatic gluconeogenesis and adipocyte remodeling10,19. These observations provide biological plausibility for an association between urinary LCN2 measures and metabolic dysfunction, although causality cannot be inferred from the present cross-sectional design.

Clinical studies remain inconsistent. While some adult studies reported positive associations between LCN2 and BMI, fasting glucose, or triglyceride levels6,21, others failed to demonstrate clear relationships with metabolic syndrome components11,22. Pediatric findings are similarly heterogeneous, with reports of elevated, reduced, or unchanged LCN2 levels14,23,24. Such discrepancies may reflect differences in biological matrix (serum vs. urine), pubertal stage, assay methodology, and normalization strategies.

A key contribution of our study is the evaluation of 24-h urinary LCN2 normalized to creatinine. Although absolute urinary LCN2 concentrations were similar between groups, creatinine normalization revealed a significant between-group difference. This finding highlights the importance of urinary concentration adjustment in pediatric biomarker studies, where hydration status and urine dilution may substantially influence raw concentrations25. In addition, the use of 24-h urine collection may reduce short-term variability compared with spot urine sampling.

Interestingly, urinary LCN2 measures were not consistently correlated with lipid parameters, inflammatory markers, or renal indices within the obesity group. In contrast, several correlations were observed in controls. This pattern may suggest that regulatory relationships present under physiological conditions become less predictable in the setting of obesity-related metabolic stress. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the modest sample size.

LCN2 has also been extensively studied as a biomarker of renal tubular injury and kidney stress13,28. Childhood obesity has been associated with glomerular hyperfiltration, endothelial dysfunction, and early structural renal changes that may precede overt kidney disease29. Although we did not observe a direct association between urinary LCN2 and eGFR in the obesity group, elevated creatinine-normalized urinary LCN2 may reflect subtle tubular stress or early renal-metabolic interactions rather than established renal impairment. The absence of a significant relationship with urinary albumin parameters may further suggest that these changes, if present, could occur before classical glomerular markers become abnormal.

From a clinical perspective, creatinine-normalized urinary LCN2 may warrant further investigation as a complementary non-invasive marker of early metabolic or renal stress in children with obesity.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It evaluated 24-hour urinary LCN2 in a pediatric population, an area that remains limited in the existing literature. The inclusion of a healthy age-matched control group and the application of puberty-specific HOMA-IR cut-off values improved the clinical interpretability of the findings16. In addition, urinary LCN2 was assessed both as an absolute concentration and after creatinine normalization, allowing a broader evaluation of urinary biomarker patterns.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, the sample size was modest, particularly in subgroup analyses according to insulin resistance status, and findings from these comparisons should be interpreted cautiously. Third, serum LCN2 concentrations were not measured simultaneously, which limited direct comparison between circulating and urinary profiles. Fourth, the study was conducted at a single center, which may limit generalizability.

Future prospective studies with larger multicenter pediatric cohorts are needed to clarify whether urinary LCN2 is associated with longitudinal changes in metabolic or renal risk.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study, children with obesity had a higher urinary LCN2/creatinine ratio than healthy controls, whereas absolute urinary LCN2 concentrations were similar between groups. Urinary LCN2 measures also showed associations with insulin resistance status.

These findings suggest that creatinine-normalized urinary LCN2 may reflect early metabolic or renal-related alterations in pediatric obesity. Larger prospective multicenter studies are required to clarify its clinical relevance and longitudinal utility.
