Influenza Vaccination Responses in Disabled Stroke Patients: A Single‐Center Prospective Observational Study
Authors: Achiraya Pakngao, Sureerat Suwatcharangoon, Pintip Ngamjanyapron, Kobporn Boonnak, Thanitta Suangtamai, Porpon Rotjanapan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the immunological response to influenza vaccination, the incidence and severity of influenza infection, and the side effects of the vaccination in patients with ischemic stroke.MethodsThis prospective observational study was conducted between 2023 and 2024 at Ramathibodi Hospital. Enrolled patients received standard doses of inactivated trivalent influenza vaccine. Seroprotection and seroconversion were assessed using hemagglutination inhibition tests before (T0) and 4–8 weeks (T1) after vaccination. Additional clinical information was gathered, and adverse events were monitored.ResultsIn total, 161 individuals completed the hemagglutination inhibition analysis. The mean ages (±SD) of the control, functionally independent, and functionally dependent groups were 69.40 (±7.40) years, 71.40 (±8.60) years, and 75.90 (±8.30) years, respectively (p < 0.001). The proportion of participants with hemagglutination inhibition titers greater than 1:40 for the A/Sydney (H1N1) strain was significantly greater in the functionally independent group than in the functionally dependent group (p = 0.031 at T0 and p = 0.003 at T1). The seroconversion rates were significantly higher in the control group than in the functionally independent or functionally dependent group (34.20% vs. 25.40% vs. 19.40%, p = 0.033). The overall incidence rate of probable influenza was 6.2 cases per 100 person‐years, and no serious adverse events related to vaccination were reported.InterpretationThe rates of seroprotection were higher for the functionally independent group than for the functionally dependent group. Therefore, the functionally dependent group may require a modified influenza vaccination strategy to achieve more effective immune responses.

Introduction

Influenza, an infectious disease caused by the influenza virus, primarily affects the respiratory system. Common symptoms range from mild upper respiratory issues to more severe lower respiratory tract infections, such as pneumonia. Influenza can also lead to serious complications, including cardiovascular and neurological problems [1]. A systematic review conducted in Thailand found that patients aged 60 and older had the highest healthcare expenses associated with severe cases of influenza [2]. Additionally, a study by Walker et al. in 2019 indicated that patients aged 65–80 with cerebrovascular disease are more likely to develop severe respiratory symptoms when infected with influenza compared to those without cerebrovascular disease [3]. In Thailand, the incidence of influenza virus infections tends to increase during the rainy season (June to August) and the winter season (October to February); however, influenza viruses can circulate throughout the year [4, 5]. Therefore, the current recommendation is that the general public, especially extremely old individuals, receive annual influenza vaccinations [6].

In Thailand, the available influenza vaccine is an inactivated virus, either trivalent or quadrivalent. The vaccine contains hemagglutinin glycoprotein, which stimulates the immune system through CD4 and CD8 T cells and promotes the production of antibodies by B cells in the subsequent phase. The effectiveness of antibody production can be measured using the hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) assay. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), a titer of 1:40 is considered to indicate 50% protection against infection, a level referred to as “seroprotection.” [7] Gross et al. reported that 57% of healthy older adults achieved a hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) titer of 1:40 or higher after receiving the trivalent inactivated influenza vaccine, whereas only 7% of functionally dependent older adults reached the same titer following immunization [8]. Another clinical study by Kelsen et al., who assessed the relationship between cell‐mediated immune response and the lack of mobilization conditions in astronauts working in space, revealed decreased levels of interleukin‐2 (IL‐2) in astronauts who underwent prolonged head‐down bed rest. This condition led to T‐cell anergy and a reduction in CD4 T cells [9]. In 2013, Hoff et al. found that individuals in a 60‐day bed rest group showed decreased co‐expression of CD45RA and CD25 in T helper cells, suggesting a condition of immunodepression [10]. These studies strongly indicate that immobility can suppress immune responses, suggesting that vaccine responses may be poor in populations with mobility issues, including patients with cerebrovascular disease.

Immobility issues in patients with cerebrovascular disease can increase their risk of developing infectious complications, such as urinary tract infections and pneumonia [11]. This heightened risk can also be attributed to the potential for stroke‐induced immunodepression (SIID), which is characterized by lymphopenia, splenic atrophy, and elevated levels of anti‐inflammatory cytokines [12], and which typically develops within 1–3 days following the onset of a stroke [13]. Furthermore, after experiencing a stroke, patients exhibit varying abilities to regain their pre‐stroke functionality, as revealed by evaluations using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), which categorizes patients based on their mobility and ability to perform self‐care [14].

Patients who have suffered an ischemic stroke may show a reduced response to vaccination due to several interconnected factors, including advanced age, immunosenescence, and stroke‐related immunodepression [13]. Advanced age is a significant factor in weakening the immune response. As people age, their immune system function declines—a phenomenon known as immunosenescence [15]. This decline involves a decrease in both the number and function of various immune cells, particularly T and B lymphocytes, which are essential for recognizing pathogens and generating protective antibodies after vaccination. As a result, older individuals often produce lower levels of antibodies and have less durable immunity compared to younger individuals, making them inherently more vulnerable, even prior to experiencing a stroke [16]. Thus, the combination of SIID and immobility in elderly patients with stroke raises the possibility that they may be immunocompromised to a sufficient extent that influenza vaccination may not reach the desired 1:40 hemagglutinin titer in this population.

In the present study, our goal was to investigate whether patients diagnosed with ischemic stroke and who exhibit different degrees of physical impairment would also show different responses to influenza vaccination when compared to vaccinated individuals without a stroke diagnosis.

Methods

Study Cohort and Participants

A prospective observational cohort study was conducted at Ramathibodi Hospital from April 2023 to March 2024. The study population was divided into three groups: the control group (C), which included patients with no history of ischemic stroke; the functional independent group (FI), which consisted of ischemic stroke patients with an mRS score of 0–2; and the functional dependent group (FD), comprising ischemic stroke patients with an mRS score of 3–5.

All ischemic stroke patients over the age of 60, with a stroke onset of 6 months or more, were screened for eligibility. Once enrolled, the patients were categorized into the FI and FD groups to give a 1:1:1 ratio with the C group. The study excluded individuals with the following conditions: active cancer, positive HIV status, a prior history of organ transplantation, hypersensitivity to the influenza vaccine, connective tissue disease, a history of receiving an influenza vaccination within the past 6 months, or a history of treatment with anti‐T‐cell therapy or corticosteroids (equivalent to 20 mg of prednisolone) for more than 2 weeks within the 3 months prior to enrollment.

Definitions

The Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) classifies stroke patients based on their ability to care for themselves [14].

Functional independence (FI) was defined as patients who had mRS scores of 0–2 at 6 months after an ischemic stroke.

Functional dependence (FD) was defined as patients who had mRS scores of 3–5 at 6 months after ischemic stroke.

Antibody Response to Influenza

The hemagglutination inhibition assay was utilized for the quantitative detection of influenza antibodies. According to the WHO, seroprotection is defined as a titer of ≥ 1:40, which corresponds to a 50% reduction in the risk of disease. A titer above 1:160 indicates up to 95% protection against influenza infection. Seroconversion is defined as a more than fourfold increase in titer that achieves seroprotective levels [17].

Baseline Characteristics and Data Collection

After obtaining informed consent, demographic data and relevant information were collected from all participants. Each participant received a standard‐dose trivalent inactivated influenza vaccine, which included the 2023 Southern Hemisphere strains: A/Sydney (H1N1), A/Darwin (H3N2), and B/Austria. Blood samples were taken to assess absolute lymphocyte counts, cytokine levels, and HAI before vaccination (T0). Four to eight weeks after vaccination (T1), a second blood sample was collected to measure absolute lymphocyte counts and HAI again.

The incidence and severity of influenza infections were evaluated by interviewing the participants by telephone and reviewing their medical records using a questionnaire at three and 6 months after vaccination. Adverse events related to the influenza vaccination were monitored using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. This monitoring involved ongoing communication between the researcher and participants through telephone interviews and a mobile communication application within 72 h of immunization and continued afterward if participants reported any new concerns [17].

Blood samples (3 mL) were collected at T0 and T1 to evaluate immune responses. Antibody titers were measured using an HAI assay with the following viral strains: A/Sydney/5/2021 (H1N1) pdm09‐like, A/Darwin/9/2021 (H3N2)‐like, and B/Australia/1359417/2021‐like. The serum was treated with a receptor‐destroying enzyme (Denka Seiken, Tokyo, Japan) at a ratio of 300 μL of enzyme to 25 μL of serum. This mixture was incubated overnight at 37°C, followed by red blood cell adsorption and heat inactivation. The treated sera were then serially diluted, mixed with eight hemagglutination units in 50 μL of virus, and incubated at 25°C for 30 min. Hemagglutination inhibition was assessed using 0.5% goose erythrocytes for H1N1 and influenza B, or 0.75% guinea pig erythrocytes for H3N2. HAI titers were reported as the reciprocal of the highest serum dilution that completely inhibited hemagglutination. We analyzed cytokines using the LEGENDplex Human Inflammation Panel 1 with a V‐bottom plate. For this test, we used 4 mL of blood, which was allowed to clot for at least 30 min. The blood was then centrifuged for 20 min to remove debris, and the supernatant was assayed immediately or stored at ≤ −20°C. We added 25 mixed beads to the specimen plates, then added 150 μL of wash buffer, shook the mixture for 1 min, and scanned the samples using a flow cytometer. The LEGENDplex Data Analysis Software Suite is designed to analyze LEGENDplex flow cytometry data files and was developed in collaboration with Qognit. Cytokine concentrations falling below the limit of detection (LOD) were assigned a fixed value equal to the LOD (e.g., 0.67 pg/mL for IL‐1β) to enable statistical inclusion in the comparative analyses. This conservative imputation method was chosen in consultation with a biostatistician to maintain the integrity of the nonparametric tests while acknowledging the assay's technical sensitivity.

Outcome Assessment

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the immune response to influenza vaccination in patients with ischemic stroke, both those who were functionally dependent and those who were independent. We also aimed to compare this immune response with that of individuals of the same age who did not have a stroke diagnosis.

The secondary objectives included evaluating the incidence and severity of influenza infections and assessing the adverse effects of the influenza vaccine within this patient population. The primary endpoint of the study was to document the diagnosis of influenza, determine the need for antiviral therapy against the influenza virus, and record any occurrences of death, withdrawal from the study, or assessments conducted 6 months after enrollment.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using STATA version 18.0. The general characteristics of the participants were compared between groups using the chi‐square test for categorical data and the ANOVA test for continuous data. The Kruskal–Wallis test was employed to evaluate the distribution of plasma cytokine levels. Linear mixed‐effects models were utilized to assess the linear prediction of HAI at time points T0 and T1. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The sample size was calculated using a formula for comparing the proportions of HAIs among the C, FI, and FD groups following influenza vaccination. The Type I error was set at 0.05, and the Type II error was set at 0.2. Considering an estimated dropout rate of 20%, a total of 219 participants were required, with 73 participants allocated to each group.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 575 patients with a history of ischemic stroke were screened for eligibility. Of these, 450 were excluded, as 255 patients declined to participate and 195 were excluded based on specific criteria (Figure 1). Consequently, 125 patients were enrolled—63 in the FI group and 62 in the FD group—who had a history of ischemic stroke lasting longer than 6 months. An additional 73 patients with no history of ischemic stroke were included as the control (C) group. Thirty‐seven participants failed to return for follow‐up and did not complete the HAI analysis (Figure 1).

A total of 161 patients completed the HAI analysis, with 46 classified as C, 55 as FI, and 60 as FD. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics. The mean ages (±SD) were as follows: 69.40 (±7.40) years for C, 71.40 (±8.60) years for FI, and 75.90 (±8.30) years for FD. The majority of participants were female (56.06%). The FD group had a significantly higher median NIHHS score compared to the FI group (8 [5, 10.8] vs. 5 [4, 9], p = 0.020). The distribution of stroke subtypes according to the TOAST classification was similar across groups (p = 0.563), with small‐vessel occlusion being the most common subtype. Baseline cytokine levels among the groups (Table S1) showed all levels falling within normal ranges. The FI and FD groups showed higher baseline cytokine levels compared to the C group, including both pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory cytokines. This difference was statistically significant for most cytokines except for interferon (IFN)‐α2, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐α, monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)‐1, and interleukin (IL)‐17A.

Within the pro‐inflammatory cytokine panel, the levels of IFN‐α2, IFN‐γ, IL‐6, IL‐12p, and IL‐18 were significantly higher in the FD than in the FI or C groups. The IL‐8 levels were elevated in the FI group, and all the differences were statistically significant. IL‐1β levels were low to undetectable across all groups. The baseline levels of IL‐10 and IL‐33 were higher in the FD than in the FI group. Notably, IL‐23, which exhibits both pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory properties, was significantly higher in the FD than in the FI and C groups.

The seroprotection rates showed no significant difference among the groups for the B/Austria strain at HAI ≥ 1:40 (38.40% for FI, 54.00% for FD, and 53.20% for C, p = 0.117), with a similar trend for the A/Darwin (H3N2) strain, with 26.00% for FI, 20.00% for FD, and 9.70% for C (p = 0.052). However, seroprotection for the A/Sydney (H1N1) strain at HAI ≥ 1:40 was significantly higher for the FI than for the FD and control groups (28.60% for FI, 13.70% for FD, and 12.90% for C; p = 0.035). The seroprotection rate in the FI group was notably higher than that of the other groups. Notably, only a minority of patients achieved HAI ≥ 1:160 (Table 1).

Sex‐stratified analyses revealed distinct cytokine patterns associated with functional status. In male participants, IL‐1β (p = 0.043), IL‐12 (p = 0.033), and IL‐33 (p = 0.007) differed significantly across functional groups. Pairwise comparisons showed that IL‐1β differed between C and FD (p = 0.0096) and between FI and FD (p = 0.022), while IL‐12 differed between control and FI (p = 0.018) and between control and FD (p = 0.008). IL‐33 showed significant differences across all pairwise comparisons (C vs. FI, p = 0.005; C vs. FD, p = 0.002; FI vs. FD, p = 0.002).

In contrast, in female participants, IL‐6 (p < 0.001), IL‐10 (p = 0.003), and IL‐23 (p < 0.001) were significantly associated with functional status, with differences observed between control and FD and between FI and FD.

When cytokine levels were compared between sexes within each functional group, a significant sex‐related difference was observed only for IL‐23 (p = 0.026) in the FD group, while other cytokines showed no significant differences between males and females.

Seroconversion

In the intention‐to‐treat analysis, seroconversion rates were significantly higher in the C than in the FI and FD groups for A/Sydney (H1N1) (46.60% vs. 39.70% vs. 25.80%; p = 0.043). The rates were similar for A/Darwin (H3N2) (34.20% vs. 25.40% vs. 19.40%) but not significantly different. For B/Austria, the seroconversion rates were higher in the C than in the FI group (50.70% vs. 37.10%; p = 0.026).

In the per‐protocol analysis, seroconversion rates (Figures 2 and 3) were higher in the C than in the FI and FD groups for A/Sydney (H1N1) (53.10% vs. 51.00% vs. 34.00%, p = 0.108), A/Darwin (H3N2) (39.10% vs. 32.70% vs. 25.50%, p = 0.325), and B/Austria (46.90% vs. 40.80% vs. 48.90%, p = 0.198). However, none of these comparisons showed statistical significance. The seroconversion rates for A/Sydney (H1N1) between the C and FD groups were statistically significant (53.10% vs. 34.00%, p = 0.046).

Biological Sex vs. Immunogenicity

At T1, no statistically significant sex differences were observed in seroprotection rates for any influenza strain in the per‐protocol analysis. For A/Sydney (H1N1), seroprotection occurred in 62/98 (63.3%) females and 36/62 (58.1%) males (p = 0.51). For A/Darwin (H3N2), rates were 55/98 (56.1%) in females and 35/62 (56.5%) in males (p = 0.96). For B/Austria, seroprotection was observed in 78/98 (79.6%) females and 55/62 (88.7%) males (p = 0.13). In the intention‐to‐treat analysis, findings remained non‐significant for all strains.

Similarly, no statistically significant sex differences were identified in seroconversion at T1. For A/Sydney (H1N1), seroconversion rates were 49/98 (50.0%) in females and 26/62 (41.9%) in males (p = 0.19). For A/Darwin (H3N2), rates were 32/98 (32.7%) in females and 21/62 (33.9%) in males (p = 0.82). For strain B, rates were 44/98 (44.9%) in females and 36/62 (58.1%) in males (p = 0.013) in the per‐protocol analysis; not significant in the intention‐to‐treat analysis. Overall, no statistically significant sex differences were observed in the intention‐to‐treat population.

When stratified by functional status, no statistically significant sex differences were observed in either seroprotection or seroconversion across any subgroup.

Rate of Influenza Infections and Adverse Events

At 6 months following influenza vaccination, the following possible influenza infections were documented: 11 out of 73 (15.07%) in the C group, 11 out of 63 (17.46%) in the FI group, and 9 out of 62 (14.51%) in the FD group. Discussion

Influenza is widely recognized as a significant global health threat, especially for older adults. Annual influenza vaccinations are recommended to enhance herd immunity, with high‐dose formulations suggested to improve antibody responses among the elderly [18]. However, research conducted by Cheung et al. has shown that the level of seroprotection in this population, particularly against the H1N1 and H3N2 strains, is lower than that observed in children and younger adults [19]. However, evidence about the effectiveness of standard‐dose influenza vaccinations is limited in elderly patients whose health and mobility have further deteriorated due to an ischemic stroke.

Aging alone affects the immune system, but a stroke can further increase vulnerability by causing a state known as systemic immunodepression [15]. This condition is often referred to as “stroke‐induced immunodepression syndrome,” and it involves significant changes in immune cell populations and their functions. Acute brain injury from a stroke triggers the release of stress hormones such as glucocorticoids and catecholamines. These hormones suppress immune responses and promote inflammation, which reduces the body's ability to effectively respond to vaccine antigens, leading to weakened seroprotection [20]. Research indicates that during the acute phase after a stroke, patients experience decreased T‐cell function and lymphopenia, making them more susceptible to infections and diminishing their vaccine responses. Together, these challenges hinder the efficacy of vaccines in stroke patients. The combination of age‐related immune decline and the acute stress and immunomodulatory effects of an ischemic stroke significantly limits the immune system's ability to generate a strong and protective response to vaccination [15, 20].

In addition to the previously mentioned factors, cytokine analysis offers an in‐depth understanding of the physiological mechanisms that contribute to the lower vaccination response observed in ischemic stroke patients. After an ischemic stroke, the cytokine profiles undergo complex and often contradictory changes that result in surges in pro‐inflammatory cytokines in the brain and systemic anti‐inflammatory responses that can lead to immunodepression. Key cytokine changes analyzed after a stroke include a significant early increase in pro‐inflammatory mediators, such as IL‐6, TNF‐α, and IL‐1β. While these cytokines are necessary for initiating the inflammatory cascade, their systemic effects can cause a shift toward immune tolerance, rendering the body less reactive to foreign antigens, including those present in vaccines [21, 22].

Conversely, a stroke also triggers the endogenous release of anti‐inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐10 and transforming growth factor (TGF)‐β, that serve as protectants to limit brain damage. However, this surge of anti‐inflammatory cytokines can also suppress cellular immunity, directly hindering the activation and proliferation of T‐cells and impairing the antigen‐presenting cell function required for a robust vaccine‐induced immune response. Beyond T‐cell alterations, B cells are an important source of cytokines, including IL‐6, IL‐10, and TNF‐α. Alteration in B‐cell function may further contribute to cytokine imbalance. This creates an environment that dampens the effective immune response to vaccination, further explaining the reduced protection seen in this vulnerable patient population [22].

The aim of this study was to evaluate baseline immune profiles, vaccine responses, and the rates of influenza infection among patients who have experienced a stroke. All of the participants were elderly, and most did not have other comorbidities that could influence immune function. Our findings highlight a critical link between the initial neurological insult and subsequent long‐term immune dysfunction. The FD group presented with significantly higher baseline NIHSS scores compared to the FI group, reflecting a more extensive initial brain injury. This heightened severity not only dictates the degree of physical dependence but also potentially appears to be a major determinant of the persistent systemic inflammatory state. The correlation between higher NIHSS and functional dependence suggests that the magnitude of the initial stroke may set a physiological trajectory that significantly impairs the host's ability to mount robust immune responses years after the event.

Our cellular data support the presence of a suppressed immune baseline in disabled stroke patients. The FD group had significantly lower absolute lymphocyte counts than the FI and control groups. This relative lymphopenia, coupled with the functional dependence following severe initial stroke (higher NIHSS), suggests a state of chronic stroke‐induced immunodepression. While we were unable to assess the Neutrophil‐to‐Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) due to data limitations, the observed lower lymphocyte count serves as a standalone indicator of an impaired cellular immune reservoir. This baseline deficit likely contributes to the blunted humoral response following influenza vaccination, as adequate lymphocyte populations are essential for effective antigen recognition and subsequent antibody production.

The observation of increased baseline cytokine levels in the FI and FD groups suggested that T‐cell function was preserved rather than exhausted. Both pro‐inflammatory cytokines (including IL‐1β, IFN‐α2, IFN‐γ, MCP‐1, IL‐6, IL‐12p, and IL‐18) and anti‐inflammatory cytokines (such as IL‐10, IL‐33, and IL‐23) were found at higher levels in the stroke groups, particularly in the FD group. The immunological data included in this study provide a mechanistic explanation for why functionally dependent patients exhibit impaired vaccine responses. We observed that the FD group, which had the highest initial severity, maintained significantly elevated levels of pro‐inflammatory and regulatory cytokines, most notably IL‐10 and IL‐18. This phenomenon suggests that severe initial brain injury leads to a persistent pro‐inflammatory milieu. This baseline cytokine pattern likely prevents the immune system from effectively recognizing and responding to new antigens, such as the influenza vaccine, as evidenced by the significantly lower seroconversion rates in the FD group.

The FD group exhibited elevated levels of both IL‐6 and IL‐10. IL‐6 promotes B‐cell differentiation and antibody production, whereas IL‐10, produced by regulatory B cells, modulates immune activation and limits the inflammatory response. In combination with significantly lower absolute lymphocyte counts observed in the FD group. These findings might indicate a chronic, low‐grade inflammatory state resembling immunosenescence in stroke patients [23].

We defined seroprotection as an HAI titer of ≥ 1:40, indicating 50% effectiveness in preventing disease [24]. To evaluate the impact of pre‐existing immunity on vaccine responsiveness, we analyzed baseline seropositivity (HAI titers ≥ 1:40 at T0). As shown in Table 1, there were significant differences in baseline seropositivity among groups for certain strains (e.g., A/H1N1). We addressed this potential confounder by focusing our primary analysis on seroconversion which specifically measures the increase in antibody titers. This approach effectively mitigates the influence of varying baseline immune statuses, whether derived from pre‐stroke or post‐stroke exposures, on our observed outcomes. The FI group exhibited the highest seroprotection rates against the A/Sydney (H1N1) strain, followed by groups FD and C (36.70% vs. 14.90% vs. 14.10%) at baseline, although the exact mechanism remains unclear. The seroprotection rates for the A/Darwin (H3N2) and B/Austria strains were generally lower than those reported in a similar age group in a cohort from Thailand, who showed 50% for H1N1 and 65.91% for H3N2. However, these rates in the current study were higher than those found in another study focusing on the B strain, which reported rates ranging from 14% to 25% [25]. In comparison, the findings of a multicenter control study on human infection conducted in 2019 indicated that both the control and stroke groups exhibited lower seroprotection rates for H1N1 when compared to healthy adults, who were reported to have a seroprotection rate of 48.7% [26]. The seroprotection rates observed in our study were similar to those reported in pediatric data, which indicated rates of 22% for H1N1, 20% for H3N2, and 37% for the B strain [27].

Influenza vaccination stimulates both humoral and cellular immunity through mechanisms that resemble natural infections. Effective T‐cell responses activate B cells, leading to antibody production. The recognition of antigens by pattern recognition receptors triggers T helper 1 (Th1) cells, which secrete Th1 cytokines, such as TNF, IFN‐γ, and IL‐2. At the same time, Th2 cells and T follicular helper cells produce cytokines, including IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13, and CXCL10, that help to balance the immune response. This interaction between pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory cytokines reflects the coordinated immune signaling essential for effective antibody generation [28].

After vaccination, seroprotection rates were higher in the FI than in the C and FD groups. These results align with previous studies reporting a seroprotection rate of 66% in the elderly population regarding H1N1. In the present study, for the H3N2 strain, seroprotection rates were 58.9%, 42.9%, and 33.9% in the C, FI, and FD groups, respectively, and all these rates were lower than previously reported values. In comparison to earlier studies, the seroprotection rates for the B strain were higher, while the rates for both H1N1 and H3N2 were lower than those observed in healthy adults [29].

The hypothesis of our study was that the highest HAI titers would be observed in individuals with the fewest pre‐existing inflammatory processes at baseline (i.e., the C group), followed by the FI group and the FD group. Among the patients in our study, the FI group exhibited higher rates of seroprotection and seroconversion than the FD group; however, these differences were not statistically significant across all strains. The median HAI titer in the FD group increased less than in the other groups for the A strain, suggesting that the FI group may have experienced a lower degree of immunosenescence compared to the FD group. Despite these findings, the lack of statistically significant seroconversion rates among the groups was attributed to the high baseline HAI titers in the population. Additionally, our study revealed that the FD group had a higher rate of infections and a shorter time to diagnosis of influenza compared to the other groups.

Our study has several strengths. It is the first study conducted in a real‐world setting involving a post‐ischemic stroke population to evaluate immunological responses related to both humoral immunity and baseline cytokine levels. The precise classification of dependency status also enhances the reliability of our findings. Additionally, we implemented thorough exclusion criteria designed to minimize the influence of confounding factors on the immune status of the study population.

Our study does have some limitations. First, the sample size was small due to logistical constraints during enrollment, which may have impacted our findings. Second, we did not record certain lifestyle factors, such as smoking status, nor did we compile a comprehensive list of non‐immunomodulatory medications. Although we strictly excluded patients taking high‐dose corticosteroids and anti‐T cell therapies to minimize confounding effects on immunogenicity, we cannot completely rule out the potential influence of other concurrent medications or smoking on the immune response. Third, the lack of complete differential white blood cell counts, such as absolute neutrophil counts, which precluded the calculation of the NLR. Although the NLR is a recognized marker of systemic inflammation, we addressed this by analyzing a comprehensive panel of cytokines and absolute lymphocyte counts instead. Fourth, the influenza incidence rate may have been affected by the widespread use of masks during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Fifth, the lack of specific historical data regarding influenza infections or vaccinations occurring prior to the stroke ictus. While our focus on seroconversion and the exclusion of individuals vaccinated within the 6 months prior to enrollment helped stabilize our baseline assessments, we cannot entirely rule out the long‐term influence of remote pre‐stroke immunological memory on the current baseline seropositivity observed in our cohort. Sixth, the lack of post‐vaccination cytokine measurements precludes analysis of the immediate cytokine response to the influenza vaccine. However, given the resource limitations inherent in this study, our focus on baseline levels has successfully highlighted the long‐term immunological signatures associated with functional dependence and their impact on influenza immunization. Finally, our study relied on telephone interviews and medical record reviews to collect information on infections, which may have affected the accuracy of the data.

Conclusions

In this study, patients with ischemic stroke had pre‐existing inflammatory processes that were not present in the control group. In the stroke groups, functional independence was associated with a higher rate of seroprotection and seroconversion than was observed in the functionally dependent group. The present recommendations are for administration of the standard‐dose influenza vaccine to the general population, including the functionally independent stroke patients in our FI group. However, implementing different influenza vaccination strategies for the functionally dependent patients in our FD group may improve their vaccination responses. Overall, influenza vaccination appears to be a safe preventive measure against infections in the stroke population.
