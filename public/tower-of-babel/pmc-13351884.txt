Optimal standardized ileal digestible lysine levels in low-protein diets: Effects on performance of lactating sows and their offspring
Authors: Mengmeng Han, Yu Gong, Xiao Lian, Mengliao Wan, Zekun Yang, Yonglang Wang, Qiuping Guo, Yehui Duan, Hongyu Chen, Fengna Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The potential changes in limiting amino acid (AA) requirements for lactating sows fed low-protein (LP) diets and the underlying physiological mechanisms remain unclear. This study evaluated the productive and physiological responses of lactating sows to LP diets, focusing on the optimal standardized ileal digestible lysine (SID Lys) supply. A total of 84 Landrace × Large White sows with similar parity (4.06 ± 0.16), expected farrowing dates and body weight (BW; 260.65 ± 1.25 kg) were randomly assigned to 6 groups with 14 replicates in each group and 1 sow per replicate. The control diet (CON) contained 18% crude protein (CP) and 0.78% SID Lys. Five LP diets contained 15% CP and similar net energy (NE) levels, with SID Lys levels of 0.66%, 0.72%, 0.78%, 0.84%, and 0.90%, respectively, achieved by adjusting the corn-to-soybean meal ratio and crystalline amino acids (CAAs) supplementation. The trial lasted for 28 d (from d 107 of gestation to d 21 postpartum). Compared with CON, 0.78% SID Lys-LP diet increased milk immunoglobulin M content (t-test, P = 0.002), improved apparent CP digestibility, and reduced serum urea nitrogen (SUN) content at d 14 and 21 of lactation, and also reduced milk urea nitrogen concentrations (t-test, P < 0.05). However, dietary CP reduction significantly decreased serum insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) levels and the digestibility of dietary crude fiber (t-test, P < 0.05). When the dietary SID Lys level in LP diets was further decreased to 0.66%, both sow and piglet production performance significantly declined (P < 0.05). However, increasing SID Lys to 0.84% in LP diets improved piglet performance, and the overall performance of both sows and piglets was not significantly different from that of CON, with the 0.84% SID Lys-LP group demonstrating better apparent utilization of dietary nitrogen and AA compared to all other groups (P > 0.05). A nonlinear fitting of sensitive indicators with linear and quadratic effects was conducted to estimate optimal SID Lys requirements for lactating sow. Breakpoints for 14 d SUN, maximizing piglet benefits (weaning litter weight and individual weight), and sow BW loss were 0.80%, 0.81%, and 0.85% using the broken-line linear model, and 0.86%, 0.90%, and 0.90% using the broken-line quadratic model. In conclusion, the LP diet containing 0.78% SID Lys did not adversely affect sow performance but affected piglet growth performance. Reducing dietary SID Lys in LP diets impaired the growth and development of 21 d suckling piglets, whereas increasing SID Lys and supplementing CAAs (Met, Thr, Trp, and Val) restored performance and mitigates adverse effects. The broken-line linear and quadratic models suggest the optimal range of SID Lys in LP diets for sows is between 0.80% and 0.90%.

Introduction

Low-protein (LP) diets, formulated by reducing crude protein (CP) levels and supplementing crystalline amino acids (CAAs) to meet swine amino acid (AA) requirements, use the net energy (NE) system and standardized ileal digestible (SID) AA values for precise nutritional formulation, demonstrating dual benefits in terms of economic efficiency and environmental sustainability (Adeola et al., 2016; Fracaroli et al., 2017). When the requirements for AA other than lysine (Lys) in animals are primarily driven by protein synthesis, the ideal amino acid profile (IAAP) based on Lys can significantly simplify diet formulation and facilitate large-scale application (van Milgen and Dourmad, 2015). The NRC (2012) de-emphasized CP recommendations for lactating sows, instead prioritizing the supply of SID AA formulated according to the IAAP as the cornerstone of protein nutritional requirements (Tokach et al., 2019). Studies have confirmed that the LP diets supplemented with CAAs based on the IAAP improve nitrogen utilization efficiency and reduce environmental nitrogen excretion without impairing the reproductive performance of sows, and may even improve lactation performance and piglet growth rates (Hojgaard et al., 2019a,b; Huber et al., 2018).

Lys, as the first-limiting AA in corn-soybean meal based diets (Wang et al., 2018), directly affects sow lactation performance through its bioavailability and metabolic efficiency. Notably, the net mammary uptake of Lys serves as the rate-limiting determinant of milk protein synthesis (Trottier et al., 2002). Lys undergoes first-pass metabolism in the intestinal tract, where a portion is catabolized by splanchnic tissues, while the remaining fraction is prioritized for body maintenance and mammary utilization (Hu et al., 2020; Li et al., 2008). Notably, LP diets may reduce the intestinal oxidative loss of Lys (van Goudoever et al., 2000). It has been reported that branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) and Arg are not only utilized for milk protein synthesis but also contribute to maintaining metabolic homeostasis in mammary tissues (Trottier et al., 1997), which suggests that estimating dietary AA requirements based solely on milk composition may introduce systematic bias. For sows with high litter sizes, the SID Lys requirements during lactation average up to 40 to 60 g/d to maximize litter gain (Tokach et al., 2019). Some studies have suggested that increasing Lys intake reduces sow body weight (BW) loss and protein mobilization, but its effects on piglet growth rate and subsequent reproductive performance remain controversial (Gourley et al., 2017; Shi et al., 2015; Xue et al., 2012). Previous studies have not reached a consensus on the relationship between Lys and fat metabolism in lactating sows, and mechanistic evidence remains limited (Gourley et al., 2017; Shi et al., 2015). Huber et al. (2015) found that when dietary SID Lys was maintained at 0.74%, reducing CP from 17.0% to 14.3% still improved milk protein content, nitrogen utilization, and litter weight gain. Previous studies have shown that LP diets have broad effects on protein metabolism and immune responses in animals (Wang et al., 2018; Yin et al., 2010). Similarly, differences in protein levels and AA contents in milk may broadly affect the types and concentrations of free amino acids (FAA) in the serum and muscles of suckling piglets. Some hormones, such as growth hormone (GH), insulin (INS), and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), are widely recognized as growth-promoting factors. Notably, IGF-1 is considered an important factor in regulating protein metabolism and primarily promotes protein synthesis in muscle tissue (Liu et al., 2006). The differences in the levels of immunoglobulins and growth-related hormones in milk may have a significant impact on the growth and health of suckling piglets. Trp, Thr, sulfur-containing amino acids (SAAs), and BCAAs have all been reported to promote intestinal development and improve gut health in piglets (Mou et al., 2019). However, it is currently unclear whether feeding lactating sows LP diets with different SID Lys levels affects milk composition, and subsequently impacts the gut health of piglets. Consequently, the optimal Lys supplementation level under LP diets conditions requires further exploration.

The objective of this study was to comprehensively evaluate the effects of different SID Lys levels in LP diets supplemented with CAAs on the physiological and productive responses of lactating sows and their offspring. Based on these findings, broken-line linear and quadratic models were used to determine the optimal SID Lys supplementation level in LP diets for lactating sows. These findings may provide critical data to support the development of balanced patterns for limiting AA in lactating sows fed LP diets, thereby advancing the precision formulation and scientific application of LP diets in swine production.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

The animal experiment was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Subtropical Agriculture, Chinese Academy of Sciences (ISA-2023–0002).

Experimental design and diets

A total of 84 multiparous Landrace × Large White sows with similar parity (4.06 ± 0.16), expected farrowing dates (7 d), and BW (260.65 ± 1.25 kg) were selected and randomly assigned to 6 treatment groups based on BW, with 14 replicates per group and 1 sow per replicate. The experimental period lasted 28 d, from d 107 of gestation to d 21 postpartum. Detailed information on the sows is provided in Table S1.

The basal diet was formulated as the control diet (CON) according to the NRC (2012) recommendations for NE and SID AA, and contained 18% CP and 0.78% SID Lys (based on parity ≥ 2; sow BW at farrowing, 210 kg; litter size, 11.5; piglet growth rate, 230 g/d; 90% dry matter basis). Sows in the LP groups received 15% CP diets with SID Lys levels of 0.66%, 0.72%, 0.78%, 0.84%, and 0.90%, respectively, while SID Met, Thr, Trp, Val, Ile, and Arg levels were balanced to be equivalent to those in CON through supplementation through CAAs supplementation (Table S2). Additionally, the concentrations of SID AA other than Lys were maintained at nearly uniform levels, all of which exceeded the NRC (2012) recommendations. Five LP diets were formulated by adjusting the corn-to-soybean meal ratio and CAAs supplementation to maintain consistent CP (15%) and NE levels. The SID AA contents were calculated based on analyzed total AA values and the SID digestibility coefficients (NRC, 2012) of the feed ingredients. Feeding management

The trial was conducted at a commercial pig farm in Zhuzhou, Hunan, China. The farrowing room temperature was maintained at 22 to 28 °C using an air curtain and fan system. All sows were feed-restricted for 1 week before farrowing, with daily feed allowances of 4, 4, 4, 3, 3, 3, and 2 kg/d, and were fed 3, 4, 4, and 5 kg/d from d 1 to 4 after farrowing, respectively. From d 5 until weaning, sows had ad libitum access to feed and water. All sows were fed twice daily at 08:00 and 18:00. Creep feed was not provided to litters during lactation. All piglets were ensured to receive colostrum, and litter size in each group was adjusted within 3 d after farrowing to ensure similar litter sizes among groups (approximately 12 ± 1 piglets); excess piglets were transferred to non-experimental sows. Daily feed intake of sows and piglet growth were recorded, and standard farm health management and immunization procedures were implemented.

Sample collection

Feed samples were collected from each group by multipoint random sampling, homogenized, and subsampled to approximately 500 g using the quartering method, then stored at 4 °C for nutrient composition analysis. During farrowing, the umbilical cord was immediately tied with numbered cords to ensure that the piglets were matched with their respective placentas. After placental weight was recorded, approximately 10 g of fresh placental tissue (3-5 cm from the cord marking point) was immediately collected and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Blood samples (10 mL) was collected from the auricular vein of sows at 17:00, before the evening meal, at farrowing and on d 7, 14, and 21 postpartum using vacuum tubes. After standing, blood samples were centrifuged at 4 °C (3000 × g, 10 min), and serum was stored at −80 °C for analysis. After each sow delivered 3 piglets, the nipples were washed with normal saline, and a total of 25 mL colostrum was collected from the three mammary regions (front, middle, and rear) without oxytocin injection. On the day before weaning, 30 mL milk was collected using the same procedure, and stored at −20 °C. During the final 3 d of the trial, 200 g fresh rectal fecal samples were randomly collected from 10 sows in each group. Then 100 g fresh feces were mixed with 10% HCl at a ratio of 4:1 to fix volatile nitrogen, dried to constant weight at 65 °C, ground through a 40-mesh sieve, and stored at −20 °C for subsequent analysis of nutrients digestibility. At weaning, on d 21 postpartum, 1 piglet per litter with a BW closest to the litter average was randomly selected from each group (n = 10) for slaughter. After 12 h of fasting, 20 mL anterior vena cava blood was collected, centrifuged after standing, and serum was then separated and stored at −80 °C. Three 1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm samples of the longissimus dorsi (LD) muscle were collected, and two 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm tissue aliquots were collected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for FAA quantification. In addition, jejunal tissue was fixed in 10% paraformaldehyde for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to observe intestinal morphology.

Performance recording and chemical analyses

Sow feed intake was recorded daily. Sow BW and backfat thickness (BF) were measured on d 107 of gestation and d 21 of lactation. After weaning, the weaning-to-estrus rate, weaning-to-estrus interval (WEI), pregnancy rate, and returning estrus rate of sows were recorded. Litter weight and litter size were recorded at farrowing and on d 3, 7, 14, and 21 of lactation. Piglet fecal consistency was assessed twice daily at 08:00 and 16:00. Diarrhea rate was calculated according to Tan et al. (2021):Diarrhearate(%)=100×Numberofdiarrheicpiglets/(Numberofpiglets×Numberofexperimentaldays).

The contents of CP, ether extract (EE), CF, and acid-insoluble ash (AIA) in feces and diets were analyzed according to GB/T 6432-2018, GB/T 6433-2006, GB/T 6434-2022, and GB/T 23742-2009, respectively (China National Standard, 2006, 2009, 2018a; 2022). Specifically, CP was analyzed using an automatic analyzer (vario MAX cube, Elementar Analysensysteme GmbH, Langenselbold, Germany), EE using an automatic fat analyzer (SOX416 C, Gerhardt, Königswinter, Germany), and CF using an automatic fiber analyzer (FIBRETHERM, C, Gerhardt Analytische Systeme GmbH, Bonn, Germany). The mufflefurnace combustion system (FO610C, Yamato Scientific Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was used to determine the AIA content. The Ca and P contents were analyzed according to GB/T 6436-2018 and GB/T 6437-2018 (China National Standard, 2018b, c) using an inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES 5100, Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). Milk regular composition was analyzed using a MilkoScan FT+200 Type 76150 milk analyzer (Foss MilkoScan, Hillerød, Denmark).

The concentrations of immunoglobulins A (IgA; CSB-E12063P), IgG (CSB-E06804p), IgM (CSB-E06805P), secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA; CSB-E12063p), INS (CSB-E06828p), and IGF-1 (CSB-E06829p) in milk were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Wuhan Huamei Biotech Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For all ELISA kits used, the intra-assay and inter-assay coefficient of variation were <8% and <10%, respectively. The FAA contents in milk and serum were determined as follows: 0.3 mL milk was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant (0.2 mL) was mixed with an equal volume of 10% sulfosalicylic acid, vortexed, and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C (11,180 × g, 15 min), filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane, and analyzed using an ultra-performance liquid chromatograph instrument equipped with a MassTrak AAA column (2.1 mm × 150 mm, 1.7 μm) (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). Prior to analysis, the amino acids were derivatized using the AccQ-Tag Ultra method (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). As described previously (Han et al., 2024), FAA concentrations in LD muscle were determined by ion-exchange chromatography using an L-8900 high-speed AA analyzer (Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Serum biochemical indices, including total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), glucose (GLU), triglycerides (TG), SUN, total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), were determined using a Roche automatic biochemical analyzer (Cobas c311, F. Hoffmann La Roche Ltd., Basel, Switzerland) and commercial kits (F. Hoffmann-La Roche Ltd., Basel, Switzerland). The concentrations or activities of very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL; 20230615), prolactin (PRL; J07031855), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH; C0319070368), estradiol (E2; C0318080301), GH (C0318090302), leptin (LEP; C0318110905), INS (C0318100303), and IGF-1 (C0318080301) in serum were determined using ELISA kits (Wuhan Huamei Bioengineering Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Intestinal tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, trimmed, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, paraffin-infiltrated, and embedded. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed using a commercial kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Stained sections were examined under a light microscope (CKX-31, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), and 10 random fields per sample were analyzed using ImageJ (v1.53) to measure villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD). The villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (V/C) was calculated to assess jejunal morphology.

Calculations and statistical analyses

Total milk yield = Piglet average daily gain (ADG) × 4 × Litter size × Lactating day (Noblet and Etienne, 1989);Apparentnutrientdigestibility(%)=100–100×(b×c)/(a×d);where a and b represent the nutrient contents in the feed and feces on a DM basis, respectively; and c and d represent the AIA contents in the feed and feces on a DM basis, respectively.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 software (IBM Inc., Armonk, NY, USA) with the general linear model:Yij=μ+Ji+eij,where Yij is the dependent variable; μ is the overall mean; Ji is the fixed effect of treatment; and eij is the random error. Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Levene tests were used to assess normality and homogeneity of variance. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used when the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were met; otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. Duncan's test was used to compare differences among groups. Orthogonal polynomial contrasts evaluated linear and quadratic effects of dietary SID Lys levels in LP diets groups. Results are presented as means and standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance is set at P < 0.05, and highly significant differences are defined as P < 0.01. Values within a row with different superscript letters differ significantly. A t-test was performed between CON and 0.78% SID Lys-LP groups.

The nonlinear curve-fitting module in OriginPro 2022 was used to estimate optimal SID Lys supply in LP diets using broken-line linear and quadratic models, with sensitive response indicators as dependent variables. The models were defined as follows:

Broken line linear model:ifx<R,y=L+U×(R–x);whenx≥R,y=L.

Broken line quadratic model:ifx<R,y=L+U×(R–x)2;whenx≥R,y=L;where y is the response variable; R is the estimated requirement (breakpoint); L is the plateau response; and U is the slope (rate constant).

Notably, sows in the LP diets groups had lower ADFI as dietary SID Lys levels increased (linear, P = 0.032) during week 2 of lactation. The average daily standardized ileal digestible Lys intake (ADSLI) differed among groups (P < 0.001), and increased with increasing dietary SID Lys levels (linear and quadratic, P < 0.001). Discussion

Lys is recognized as the first-limiting AA in corn-soybean meal-based diets for lactating sows (Guan et al., 2004). Previous studies have also demonstrated that when dietary protein levels were reduced by 3% to 15%, Lys remained the first-limiting AA for lactating sows, severe dietary Lys deficiency negatively affected sows and piglets (Han et al., 2025). In animal nutrition requirement research, dose–response modeling is a critical approach for determining AA requirements. When estimating AA requirements in swine, response indicators vary across physiological stages. For lactating sows, commonly used indicators include milk yield, piglet weaning weight, sow BW loss during lactation, and SUN (Greiner et al., 2020; Hojgaard et al., 2019b). Current nutritional guidelines recommend SID Lys requirements for multiparous sows within a range of 0.74% to 0.82%, whereas studies on LP diets have reported variable estimates ranging from 0.63% to 0.90%, likely due to differences in evaluation criteria and statistical models (Gourley et al., 2017; Greiner et al., 2020; Hojgaard et al., 2019b). Among factors influencing requirement estimates in dose–response trials, the choice of statistical models is pivotal. For datasets showing an initial linear response followed by a plateau phase, both broken-line linear and broken-quadratic, or curvilinear plateau, models provide robust fits (Baker, 1986; Gloaguen et al., 2011). The broken-line quadratic model accounts for inter-individual variability within experimental cohorts and dynamic nutrient demands of animals (Robbins et al., 2006). The present results showed that, under a 15% CP diet, the optimal SID Lys levels for maximizing piglet performance, including weaning litter weight and individual weight, were 0.81% using the broken-line linear model and 0.90% using broken-line quadratic model. The corresponding values for minimizing sow body reserve loss during lactation were 0.85% (broken line linear model) and 0.90% (broken line quadratic model), respectively.

When dietary NE levels were kept consistent, reducing dietary CP from 18% to 15% numerically increased sow ADFI, although no significant differences was observed between treatments. ADSLI increased linearly and quadratically with increasing dietary SID Lys levels, suggesting that Lys supplementation provided greater benefits when other EAA were balanced. Notably, sows fed the LP diet containing 0.84% SID Lys achieved a daily SID Lys intake of 52.64 g and showed favorable overall performance, which wasconsistent with the model-prediction range and previous findings (Hojgaard et al., 2019b; Tokach et al., 2019). In this study, severe dietary Lys deficiency exacerbated sow BW loss during lactation without affecting BF loss. Moreover, sow BW loss decreased linearly and quadratically with increasing dietary SID Lys levels, whereas reducing dietary CP in diets balanced with Lys, Met, Thr, Trp, and Val did not significantly affect sow BW loss. Earlier studies have indicate that decreasing CP to 14.3% with CAAs supplementation had no significant effect on sow BW loss (Huber et al., 2015), whereas increasing SID Lys supplementation improved sow BW loss (Gourley et al., 2017). Although BF loss did not differ significantly among groups, the 0.66% SID Lys-LP diet group showed higher LDL-C concentrations at farrowing and on d 14 of lactation, and higher VLDL concentrations on d 21 of lactation, then the 0.84% SID Lys-LP group. These results suggest that dietary Lys deficiency in LP diets may induce lipid metabolism disorders in sows, although the underlying mechanisms require further investigation. Analysis of metabolic hormones (INS, LEP, GH, and IGF-1) showed that reducing CP to 15% with 0.78% SID Lys significantly decreased IGF-1 levels at at farrowing and on d 21 of lactation. However, IGF-1 and GH concentrations increased linearly and quadratically with increasing SID Lys levels, eliminating differences between the 0.84% and 0.90% SID Lys-LP groups and CON. GH has been reported to promotetissue growth and repair partly through IGF-1, a growth factor secreted by the liver and other tissues that is important for cell growth, proliferation, and metabolism (Al-Samerria and Radovick, 2023). Together, GH and IGF-1 may regulate tissue mobilization and repair in sows. Excessive BW loss during lactation can adversely affect subsequent reproductive performance (Tokach et al., 2019). In this study, changing dietary CP level did not significantly affect reproductive indices in sows. However, as dietary SID Lys levels increased, the weaning-to-estrus rate, WEI, pregnancy rate, and returning estrus rate improved. The serum reproductive hormone results showed no significant difference in E2 concentrations, whereas FSH, and IGF-1 concentrations increased linearly and quadratically with increasing dietary SID Lys levels on d 21 of lactation. FSH affects normal follicular development, and the WEI is related to changes in plasma gonadotropins, steroids, leptin, IGF-1, and INS (Madej et al., 2018), indicating that a balanced Lys supply is necessary to support the normal ovulation and estrus in sows.

Lactation performance directly influences piglet growth and development. Specifically, high-quality milk is characterized not only by higher concentrations of milk protein, milk fat, lactose and other conventional nutrients, but also by abundant AA, fatty acids, minerals and immunoglobulins (Ren et al., 2022). The present study showed that reducing dietary CP level or limiting SID Lys in the diet decreased milk yield, as indicated by trends toward reduced litter weight on d 14 and 21 and ADG from d 3 to 21. In LP diets, increasing SID Lys levels linearly and quadratically improved piglet weight and ADG. Notably, compared with the 0.84 SID Lys-LP group, the 0.90% SID Lys-LP group did notshow further improvements despite the higher dietary SID Lys level. This may indicate that, at this level, one or more AAs other than Lys became first-limiting AA, consistent with patterns reported in previous studies (Gourley et al., 2017; Hojgaard et al., 2019b). Analyses of colostrum and mature milk showed that reducing dietary CP did not affect conventional milk nutrients but tended to decrease mature milk protein, MUN, and IGF-1levels, whereas IgM levels increased. In LP diets, increasing SID Lys supplementation linearly and quadratically increased IgM, IGF-1, lactose, protein, fat-free dry matter, total solids, and sIgA levels in colostrum or mature milk. Previous studies have indicated that higher IGF-1 levels in milk are positively correlated with infant weight gain (Kon et al., 2014), and that AA supplementation during gestation improves colostral immunoglobulin levels (Yuan et al., 2022). Therefore, the improvements observed in the present study may be attributable to enhanced absorption and utilization of other AA associated with Lys supplementation, thereby increasing immunoglobulin concentrations in milk. Further analysis revealed that both dietary CP and SID Lys levels influenced FAA profiles in mature milk from sows. Reducing dietary CP level decreased Trp and Phe contents, whereas in LP diets, SID Lys levels significantly affected mature milk concentrations of Lys, Met, Trp, Ile, Phe, Ala, and Tyr. Notably, the 0.84% SID Lys-LP group had higher EAA and TAA contents in mature milk than the 0.66% and 0.72% SID Lys-LP groups. This likely resulted from the 0.84% SID Lys-LP diet aligning more closely with the IAAP required for mammary maintenance and lactation, thereby enhancing the uptake of bioavailable AA for milk protein synthesis (NRC, 2012), which is consistent with the higher milk protein content observed in this group. In summary, decreased dietary CP levels can affect the quality of colostrum and mature milk, and further increasing SID Lys supplementation may improve the nutritional value of colostrum and mature milk.

SUN and AA contents have been validated as biomarkers for predicting and evaluating AA requirements and utilization efficiency in animals (Donsbough et al., 2010). The present results showed that reducing dietary CP levelsignificantly decreased sow SUN levels on d 14 and 21 of lactation. In LP diets, SUN showed linear and quadratic responses to increasing SID Lys levels, with the lowest SUN observed in the 0.84% SID Lys-LP group, indicating maximal nitrogen utilization efficiency. Previous studies have shown that there is a close positive correlation between MUN and SUN concentrations (Broderick and Clayton, 1997). In the present study, MUN levels in colostrum and mature milk decreased linearly as dietary SID Lys levels increased. This indicated that increasing SID Lys level improved the AA balance in low-protein diets and reduced dietary AA wastage. Interestingly, serum FAA analysis revealed significantly higher Tyr and Ile levels in the CON than in all LP diet groups. Reports have indicated that Tyr acts as a signal of changes in dietary protein levels in Drosophila; when dietary protein levels decrease, tissue Tyr content declines, thereby slowing protein synthesis, reducing mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 activity, and increasing feed intake (Kosakamoto et al., 2022). Evidence demonstrates that a low-Ile diet enhances hepatic insulin sensitivity, stimulates ketogenesis, and increases energy expenditure, thereby mitigating obesity development through these coordinated metabolic adaptations (Yu et al., 2021). In this study, increasing SID Lys supplementation in LP diets elevated serum Lys levels, whereas the 0.84% SID Lys-LP group had significantly lower Thr, Val, Phe, Ser, Pro, EAA, and NEAA concentrations than the 0.66% SID Lys-LP group. A reasonable explanation is that the AA ratios in the 0.84% SID Lys-LP group were more balanced, resulting in greater AA utilization. When one EAA is deficient in serum, relative excesses of other AA may occur, manifested as lower concentrations of the deficient AA and higher levels of the others (Gloaguen et al., 2012; Henry et al., 1992; Regmi et al., 2017). Interestingly, the 0.84% and 0.90% SID Lys-LP groups showed higher apparent CP digestibility than CON, indicating that reducing CP while optimizing Lys supplementation enhanced nitrogen utilization and reduced nitrogen excretion in waste. These findings suggest that lowering CP levels while supplementing Lys at appropriate levels may enhance dietary nitrogen utilization and reduce nitrogen pollutants in waste.

Research on the effects of LP diets in lactating sows has primarily focused on piglet weight gain, with limited investigation of tissue AA metabolism and intestinal morphology in their offspring. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) are established biomarkers of hepatic injury, as elevated serum activities typically indicate impaired liver function (Khanyile et al., 2016). Studies have demonstrated that intrauterine growth-restricted piglets exhibit reduced growth performance, hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction, and increased ALT/AST activity (Bai et al., 2022). Increased GLU intake in suckling piglets enhances glycogen reserves, vitality, and weaning weight (Engelsmann et al., 2019). Elevated serum TG and TC may predispose animals to hepatic metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular disorders (Liu et al., 2021), whereas HDL-C transports TG to the liver for catabolism, and reflects systemic lipid metabolism status (Miller, 1990). The present study showed that increasing SID Lys levels in LP diets of sows reduced serum ALT, AST, and TG levels in piglets while increasing GLU levels. In addition, piglets from the 0.66% SID Lys-LP group had significantly higher TG/TC and lower HDL-C levels than those from CON, indicating that reduced CP and SID Lys levels in sow diets disrupted offspring hepatic function and lipid metabolism. This study provides the first evidence that maternal LP diets modulate offspring hepatic function and lipid metabolism beyond weight-related outcomes.

Protein turnover is essentially a dynamic process of AA metabolism in various tissues, in which dietary CP and AA levels broadly influence serum and muscle FAA profiles and metabolic processes (Duan et al., 2016; Morales et al., 2015). Similarly, variations in milk protein and AA profiles may substantially shape FAA composition in the serum and muscle tissues of suckling piglets. FAA in the peripheral circulation can be efficiently transported between tissues, and EAA-rich diets enhance systemic protein anabolism, particularly in skeletal muscle (Church et al., 2020). In this study, suckling piglets from sows fed the 0.66% SID Lys-LP diet had significantly lower serum EAA, NEAA, and TAA levels than those from sows fed the 0.84% SID Lys-LP diet and CON. In LP diets, increasing SID Lys levels in sow diets linearly increased serum Met, Thr, Trp, His, Ser, Gly, and Pro levels in suckling piglets. These trends paralleled the elevated levels of Met, Thr, Trp, His, and Pro in mature milk from sows, suggesting that higher milk protein and FAA contents in the 0.84% and 0.90% SID Lys-LP groups contributed to the observed differences in serum FAA profiles of piglets. Tissue protein synthesis is regulated by intracellular FAA availability, making FAA profiles reliable indicators of animal nutritional status (Xiao et al., 2023). The results showed that reducing dietary CP level in lactating sow diets did not significantly affect FAA contents in the LD muscle of suckling piglets, whereas the 0.84% SID Lys-LP diet resulted in significantly higher levels of EAA, TAA, Lys, Thr, Trp, Leu, Cys, and Tyr than the 0.66% SID Lys-LP diet. This suggests that the enhanced availability of EAA in the 0.84% SID Lys-LP group promoted protein synthesis, ultimately supporting skeletal muscle growth and development in piglets.

Intestinal villus morphology is critical for nutrient absorption, as taller villi expand the epithelial surface area, whereas villus atrophy increases CD (Wang et al., 2020). Thus, the V/C ratio serves as a robust indicator of intestinal health. Generally, intestinal growth and development, digestive absorption, and mucosal barrier function in piglets require nutritional support. Trp, Thr, SAAs, and BCAAs have been shown to enhance intestinal development and gut health in piglets (Mou et al., 2019). The present study showed that increasing SID Lys supplementation in LP diets for sows linearly and quadratically increased jejunal VH and the V/C ratio in piglets. This effect may be attributed to improved milk quality in sows, including elevated immunoglobulin and FAA levels, which may promote intestinal structural development in suckling piglets.

Conclusion

Reducing dietary CP from 18% to 15% decreased serum IGF-1 levels and CF digestibility but had no adverse effects on sow body condition or subsequent estrus performance, while achieving relatively higher serum AA utilization efficiency. Broken-line linear modeling identified optimal SID Lys levels in LP diets as 0.85% for minimizing BW loss, 0.82% for maximizing milk yield, 0.81% to 0.82% for maximizing litter weight and piglet ADG, and 0.81% for maximizing individual weight on d 21. The broken-line quadratic model estimated the corresponding SID Lys levels for these indicators as 0.90%, 0.90%, 0.90%, 0.90% and 0.89%, respectively. Overall, the recommended SID Lys requirement in LP diets for lactating sows ranges from 0.80% to 0.90%.
