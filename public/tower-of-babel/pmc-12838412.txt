Effect of BCG vaccination on the progression of latent tuberculosis infection to active disease in contacts: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shunli Cai, Tianwei Yan, Yutian Yang, Xin Guo, Xiang Li, Hongbo Chen, Jian He, Yuan Xia, Yingchen Zhou, Qingtao Tan, Xinhua Cun, Guozhong Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccination is well established for preventing severe tuberculosis (TB) in children; however, its effectiveness in reducing the progression of latent TB infection (LTBI) to active disease remains uncertain, particularly in real-world contact settings.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis by searching EMBASE, PubMed, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library, with data collection completed on December 21, 2024. Eligible studies included individuals with confirmed TB exposure and a positive interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) result. Pooled risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the Mantel-Haenszel method under a random-effects model, with between-study variance estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) and Wald-type confidence intervals applied for uncertainty estimation. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I2and τ2 statistics.ResultsTwenty studies comprising 3,056 participants were included. Using a random-effects model, prior administration of the Bacillus Calmette‑Guérin (BCG) vaccine was associated with a lower risk of LTBI progressing to active TB (RR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.40–0.82; I2 = 38%). This association was stronger in low-incidence settings (RR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.31–0.74; I2 = 30%) and among children under 15 years (RR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.33–0.58; I2 = 10%). No significant association was observed in high-incidence settings (RR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.27–1.83; I2 = 87%) or among individuals over 15 years (RR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.31–2.64; I2 = 0%).ConclusionsBCG vaccination was associated with a significantly lower risk of LTBI progression to active TB, with the strength of this association varying by geographic region and age. These findings highlight the need to consider regional TB burden and age-specific factors when formulating vaccination policies.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12318-y.

Background

Latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) is characterized by the presence of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB) in the host, where the infection is suppressed by the immune system without progressing to active tuberculosis [1, 2]. While most individuals with LTBI remain asymptomatic and non-infectious, immune suppression due to conditions, such as HIV infection, immunosuppressive therapy, or malnutrition, can trigger the reactivation of latent infection into active TB [2]. This progression not only threatens individual health but also exacerbates the global TB burden through airborne transmission, posing a major public health challenge. As a result, interrupting the transition from LTBI to active TB has become a critical focus in global TB control strategies.

Since its introduction in 1921, Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) has remained the cornerstone of tuberculosis (TB) prevention and the only widely implemented anti-TB vaccine [3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) strongly recommends BCG vaccination for newborns in high TB burden settings to mitigate the risk of severe TB manifestations [4]. Substantial evidence supports its protective efficacy: a study by Mangtani et al. reported that BCG vaccination confers up to 90% protection (95% CI: 23%–99%) against neonatal TB meningitis [5]. Furthermore, an individual participant data meta-analysis of 26 cohort studies demonstrated that BCG vaccination significantly reduces TB risk in children under five years of age (adjusted odds ratio 0.63, 95% CI 0.49–0.81) [6]. Collectively, these findings underscore the critical role of BCG in safeguarding against severe pediatric TB.

Historically, the evaluation of BCG’s efficacy in preventing TB infection was primarily based on the tuberculin skin test (TST), which cannot differentiate between TB infection, BCG vaccination, and non-tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infections [7]. This limitation hindered the accurate assessment of BCGs role in preventing LTBI. Early discoveries in T-lymphocyte-mediated immunity and interferon-γ production enabled the development of in-vitro assays capable of detecting antigen-specific cellular immune responses [8]. Building on this foundation, whole-blood interferon-γ release assays (IGRA’s) were introduced in the late 1990s and were subsequently commercialized in Australia as the QuantiFERON‑TB test, offering substantially improved diagnostic specificity by distinguishing TB infection from prior BCG vaccination. These advances provided a stronger methodological basis for evaluating BCG’s association with LTBI progression [9]. A 2014 meta-analysis using IGRA found that BCG vaccination reduced the risk of infection by 19% (95% CI: 8–29%) in children exposed to TB [10]. This study is the first to employ IGRAs rather than TST, providing more robust evidence for the preventive effect of BCG against infection.

In low TB burden countries such as the United States, over 85% of TB cases result from the reactivation of latent infection [11]. Therefore, preventing the progression of LTBI to active TB is thus a crucial goal for global health. However, the potential association between prior BCG vaccination and reduced progression from IGRA-defined LTBI to active TB has not been systematically examined. Existing reviews have primarily evaluated infection risk or severe pediatric disease. To address this gap, the present study synthesizes observational evidence from both children and adults with IGRA-defined LTBI to examine whether prior BCG vaccination is associated with differences in progression to active TB.

Method

We conducted a meta-analysis following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) and Meta-analyses Of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) guidelines [12, 13]. This study has been registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024588720).

Search strategy

We searched EMBASE, PubMed, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from database inception to December 21, 2024, with no language restrictions. Search terms included “BCG Vaccine,” “tuberculosis,” and “contact”. The search strategy for each database is provided in the Supplementary Table S1. Non-English studies were included in the search, with titles, abstracts, and relevant sections translated using professional tools for screening and data extraction. We manually checked the reference lists of relevant articles to identify additional potentially relevant papers.

Study selection criteria

We included observational studies that: (1) had known exposure to people with active TB; (2) divided participants into BCG and non-BCG groups; (3) diagnosed latent TB infection using an interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA; QuantiFERON or T-SPOT.TB); (4) LTBI progressed to active TB. We excluded studies that: (1) including participants with active or suspected TB at baseline; (2) did not provide information on BCG vaccination; (3) detection of LTBI using TST; (4) were abstracts, letters, case reports, or reviews.

Two researchers (TWY and YTY) independently reviewed the titles and abstracts of the retrieved literature. Selected articles were then read in full to assess eligibility based on the inclusion criteria. They independently evaluated and extracted all eligible literature that met the criteria. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Data extraction

Non-English/foreign-language abstracts and articles were accurately translated for full-text review. In a preconceived and standardized data extraction form, information was collected on the first author’s name, year of publication, title, journal, study country, study design, participants demographics, BCG-related details, IGRA related information, number of people who develop active tuberculosis in BCG-vaccinated and unvaccinated individuals, follow-up time. Data on TB incidence were obtained from the BCG World Atlas (http://bcgatlas.org/index.php; updated 2020). We allowed one year deviations from age categories (e.g., < 16 years grouped with < 15 years). Two authors (TWY and YTY) independently conducted study selection and data extraction, cross-checking the extracted data. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality for observational studies was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies - of Interventions) [14]. The tool evaluates seven domains of bias: (1) bias due to confounding; (2) bias in selection of participants into the study; (3) bias in classification of interventions; (4) bias due to deviations from intended interventions; (5) bias due to missing data; (6) bias in measurement of outcomes; and (7) bias in selection of the reported result. The assessment was conducted independently by two researchers (TWY and YTY), and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third researcher (SLC).

Definitions

Exposure was defined as individuals who had close contact with a microbiologically or radiologically confirmed TB index case, either by living in the same household or through significant interaction outside the household [6].

LTBI was defined as a positive result of IGRA in contacts. Progression to active TB refers to the development of clinical symptoms, imaging abnormalities or microbiological evidence of active TB in patients with LTBI when the immune system fails to effectively control the disease [10].

Statistical analysis

For the primary outcome, we examined the risk of progression to active tuberculosis among individuals with LTBI confirmed by IGRA. For each study, the effect measure was calculated as a crude risk ratio (RR) derived from 2 × 2 tables of LTBI-positive participants, comparing BCG-vaccinated and unvaccinated groups according to whether they progressed to active TB. Subgroup analyses were prespecified based on factors previously reported to influence variation in BCG effectiveness across settings, including TB incidence category, country income level, latitude, age group, contact type, and the method used to ascertain BCG vaccination. Latitude was examined because environmental mycobacterial exposure differs across geographic regions [5], while country income level was considered because socioeconomic conditions may affect both BCG uptake and baseline TB exposure risk [10]. These factors were included to explore contextual differences highlighted in earlier studies. TB incidence settings were defined as low ( < 20 per 100 000), medium (20–99 per 100 000), or high (≥100 per 100 000) per WHO classification [15].

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.0 with the meta package. Pooled effect estimates were expressed as RR with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), calculated using the Mantel–Haenszel method under a random-effects model. Between-study variance (τ2) was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) approach. The 95% CI for the pooled effect was calculated using the Wald-type.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic, the Chi-squared test, and the I2 statistic. A p-value < 0.05 for the Chi-squared test was considered statistically significant, and I2 values were interpreted on a 0–100% scale. Prespecified subgroup differences were evaluated using univariate random-effects meta-regression when the number of included studies permitted.

Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially excluding each study to assess the robustness of the findings. Publication bias and small-study effects were assessed using funnel plot inspection and Egger’s regression test, applied when at least 10 studies were available. Funnel plots were evaluated to distinguish potential publication bias from heterogeneity-related asymmetry All statistical tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence was appraised using the GRADE framework. Two researchers (TWY and YTY) independently assessed the five GRADE domains-risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias-for the outcome included in the meta-analysis (Supplementary Table S3). The certainty of evidence was classified as high, moderate, low, or very low following standard GRADE guidance. GRADEpro GDT software was used to prepare the Summary of Findings tables, and all decisions regarding downgrading or upgrading were documented and justified in the accompanying footnotes.

Results

A total of 1,980 eligible studies were identified, and after duplicate removal, 1,262 articles were screened based on title and abstract. Following full-text review of 673 papers, 20 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Among these, 14 studies were conducted in low TB incidence areas, 4 in medium-incidence areas, and 2 in high-incidence areas. BCG vaccination status was determined through the presence of a BCG scar or medical records in 17 studies. The ROBINS-I assessment found no studies with a low risk of bias. Of the 20 studies, 10 had a serious risk, 8 a moderate risk, and 2 a critical risk (Supplementary Figure S4 and Figure S5). A major issue was deviations from intended interventions: 60% of studies (12/20) had serious/critical risk or missing information. In 7 studies (35%), preventive therapy given to LTBI-positive participants likely reduced the observed difference in TB incidence between groups, potentially leading to an underestimation of BCG’s protection.

A total of 3,056 participants from the 20 studies were included in the analysis. Using a random-effects model, the pooled RR of progression from LTBI to active TB among BCG-vaccinated individuals was 0.57 (95% CI: 0.40–0.82, I2 = 38%; prediction intervals: 0.21–1.58; Fig. 2), corresponding to a 43% lower observed risk compared with unvaccinated individuals.

Although subgroup-specific estimates varied, none of the between-subgroup differences were statistically significant in the univariable meta-regression (all p-values for subgroup differences > 0.05; Table 2), indicating that these apparent variations did not represent significant effect modification. In low TB incidence areas, BCG vaccination was associated with a 52% lower risk of progression (RR = 0.48; 95% CI: 0.31–0.74, I2 = 30%). In contrast, the estimates for medium (RR = 1.28; 95% CI: 0.38–4.24, I2 = 0%) and high TB incidence settings (RR = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.27–1.83, I2 = 87%) were not statistically significant and were based on a limited number of studies, resulting in wide confidence intervals and reduced statistical stability. A similar trend was observed when stratifying by country income: the strength of the association was greatest (RR = 0.53; 95% CI: 0.38–0.76, I2 = 8%) in high income settings, but decreased in Subgroup analyses examining assay differences showed broadly consistent results. Both IGRA platforms demonstrated a protective association, with QFT based studies showing an RR of 0.63 (95% CI: 0.42–0.94, I2 = 35%) and T-SPOT.TB based studies an RR of 0.32 (95% CI: 0.07–1.60, I2 = 50%), although the between-platform difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.2137) and the T-SPOT.TB estimate was based on few studies. Similarly, stratification by TB outcome definition showed that studies using microbiologically confirmed endpoints retained a significant protective association (RR = 0.50; 95% CI: 0.31–0.81, I2 = 57%), whereas clinically diagnosed (RR = 0.77; 95% CI: 0.33–1.80, I2 = 0%) or mixed-definition studies (RR = 0.96; 95% CI: 0.34–2.69, I2 = 0%) produced imprecise, non-significant estimates; the between-group comparison was also not statistically significant (p = 0.2441).

Subgroup analysis based on the overall ROBINS-I judgment revealed a clear pattern. Studies with a critical risk of bias showed no evidence of a protective effect (RR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.22–4.28, I2 = 72%). In contrast, a significant protective association was observed in studies with a serious risk of bias (RR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.33–0.91, I2 = 36%) and studies with a moderate risk of bias (RR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.28–1.00, I2 = 34%).

Age-stratified analysis indicated a significant association with lower progression risk among children under 15 years (RR = 0.44; 95% CI: 0.33–0.58, I2 = 10%). Further stratification revealed that the protective effect was consistent among children under 5 years (RR = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.31–0.61, I2 = 0%). In contrast, no statistically significant association was observed in individuals over 15 years (RR = 0.90; 95% CI: 0.31–2.64, I2 = 0%). Studies relying on BCG scar (RR = 0.51; 95% CI: 0.33–0.78, I2 = 40%) or medical records (RR = 0.48; 95% CI: 0.09–2.51, I2 = 37%) showed significant associations with lower progression risk, whereas studies based on self-reported vaccination history did not yield statistically significant results (RR = 0.95; 95% CI: 0.38–2.38, I2 = 44%), see Table 2.

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings. Excluding individual studies did not lead to significant changes in the pooled effect estimate (Supplementary Figure S1). Additionally, results remained consistent when using both random-effects and fixed-effects models (Supplementary Figure S2). The funnel plot appeared without obvious asymmetry (Supplementary Figure S3). Egger’s test was non-significant (p = 0.76), providing no statistical evidence of publication bias, although the test may have limited power in observational meta-analyses.”

Discussion

This study provides a focused meta-analysis assessing whether prior BCG vaccination is associated with reduced progression from IGRA-confirmed latent TB infection to active disease, addressing an aspect not specifically examined in previous reviews. Our pooled analysis demonstrates a 43% reduction in progression risk among BCG-vaccinated participants (RR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.40–0.82), with a stronger association observed in low TB incidence areas (RR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.31–0.74) and children under 15 years (RR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.33–0.58). These findings support the evidence that BCG-vaccinated individuals experience lower progression risk, highlighting priority populations for targeted vaccination strategies.

Given that nearly one-quarter of the global population is affected by LTBI [36], with 5–15% at risk of developing active TB [37], the observed association between BCG vaccination and lower progression risk represents a meaningful advance in TB control. However, the effectiveness of BCG is not uniform across epidemiological contexts. Our findings indicate that BCG vaccination was associated with a 52% lower risk among TB contacts in low-incidence settings, where vaccination strategies typically target high-risk groups (such as close contacts, HIV-positive individuals, and migrants). This supports current immunization policies in these regions. By contrast, no statistically significant association with lower progression risk was observed in high-burden settings, likely reflecting the intense force of transmission, where ongoing exposure to Mycobacterium tuberculosis may overwhelm BCG-conferred immunity. Nevertheless, in many resource-limited, high-incidence regions, BCG remains the most widely available and affordable preventive tool [38, 39]. Importantly, the findings for medium- and high-incidence settings are based on only six studies, resulting in limited statistical stability; these subgroup estimates should therefore be regarded as exploratory and interpreted with caution.

The duration of BCG-induced protection remains controversial. Abubakar’s team’s research indicates that the protective effect of BCG against active tuberculosis may not exceed 15 years [40]. However, Aronson et al. confirmed through cohort studies that BCG vaccination in childhood can maintain protection for up to 50–60 years [41]. Our age-stratified analysis revealed a 56% lower observed risk among children under 15 years, while no significant association was found in adults. This pattern is consistent with evidence that BCG-induced adaptive T-cell memory wanes during adolescence or early adulthood. Prevent immunological studies further suggest that BCG induces two layers of immunity: an early, T-cell–mediated adaptive response that provides the major protective effect in childhood, and a longer-lasting form of trained innate immunity that persists into adulthood but is less specific and more inflammatory [42]. This age-related shift in immune profile may diminish BCG’s capacity to prevent progression from latent infection to active disease in older individuals [43]. Taken together, these mechanisms offer a biologically plausible explanation for the strong protective association observed in children and the absence of a significant association in adults, and highlight the need to consider age-dependent immune responses when optimizing BCG vaccination strategies.

Additionally, previous studies have indicated that NTM may influence the protective efficacy of BCG. In high-latitude areas, the impact of NTM is less pronounced, resulting in a stronger protective effect of BCG [5]. However, we were unable to assess whether latitude is a significant factor contributing to this heterogeneity, as only two studies were conducted in areas with latitudes below 20°. Furthermore, the positive association between country income level and BCG efficacy may reflect the impact of better healthcare infrastructure in higher-income countries, including enhanced latent TB screening, preventive therapy access, and vaccine cold-chain maintenance. These factors can improve early diagnosis and timely interventions, thus enhancing BCG’s protective effect. In contrast, lower-income settings often face challenges like limited healthcare access and higher co-infection rates (e.g., HIV), which may reduce BCG efficacy. These factors may contribute to the stronger associations observed in these settings.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the use of unadjusted effect estimates from observational studies leaves room for residual confounding, as unmeasured factors such as socioeconomic conditions, nutrition, and healthcare access may influence the observed association. Second, although included 12 studies were rated as having a serious or critical risk of bias, only critical-risk studies produced highly imprecise estimates, underscoring the influence of study quality and the need for better-designed LTBI cohorts. Finally, although the funnel plot did not suggest substantial asymmetry and Egger’s test was non-significant, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Egger’s test has limited statistical power in meta-analyses with a modest number of observational studies, and subtle small-study effects may still be present. Therefore, the possibility of publication bias cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this meta-analysis found that prior BCG vaccination was consistently associated with a reduced risk of progression from latent TB infection to active disease among TB contacts, with more pronounced protection observed in children and in low-incidence settings. Because all included studies were observational, these associations cannot establish causality, and residual confounding-such as differences in healthcare access or socioeconomic status-remains possible. Nonetheless, the findings suggest that BCG may contribute not only to preventing severe childhood TB but also to reducing progression among high-risk exposed populations. These insights are directly relevant to global TB control goals, particularly efforts to interrupt the reservoir of latent infection and prevent progression in vulnerable groups. Strengthening LTBI management, refining age- and region-specific vaccination policies, and conducting high-quality longitudinal studies will be essential for determining how BCG can be optimally integrated into strategies aimed at achieving the WHO End TB targets.
