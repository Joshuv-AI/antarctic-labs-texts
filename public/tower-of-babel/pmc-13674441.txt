Host‐specific and deterministic microbiome assembly in major coleopteran stored‐grain pests
Authors: Abrar Muhammad, Xiaoqiang Shen, Lichao Chen, Enhui Guo, Huarui Zhang, Xiaoyu Lei, Chao Sun, Jianhua Lv, Yongqi Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dual DNA‐ and RNA‐based sequencing profiled the microbiomes of five major coleopteran stored‐grain pests and their wheat flour substrate. The microbiomes differed significantly among hosts and from flour, yet 11 core bacterial and 9 core fungal taxa were shared across hosts. RNA data revealed host‐specific active microbiota. Deterministic processes dominated community assembly, exceeding 90% in Rhyzopertha dominica and Oryzaephilus surinamensis. Homogeneous selection identified eight conserved bacterial clades. Although microbiomes were strongly host‐specific, they showed no significant correlation with host phylogeny, indicating that environmental filtering and deterministic selection, rather than evolutionary relatedness, are the dominant forces shaping these pest‐associated communities.

To the Editor,

Stored grain pests pose a significant threat to global food security. High‐value crops suffer not only direct losses during storage, but also quality declines due to contamination and mycotoxin production [1, 2]. As much as 9% of grain is lost after harvest in industrialized countries, and up to 20% in developing regions. These losses can reach tens of millions of tonnes annually and are primarily caused by Coleopteran and Lepidopteran insects, which together account for over 70% of stored‐grain pests [3, 4]. The urgent need for sustainable pest control has increased interest in manipulating insect‐associated microbiota, as symbionts affect nutrition, immunity, and detoxification [5]. However, stored‐grain pests remain understudied, with limited comparative research across species and insufficient consideration of metabolically active taxa. Therefore, we profiled the bacterial and fungal communities of five economically important beetles, including Tribolium castaneum, Latheticus oryzae, Rhyzopertha dominica, Oryzaephilus surinamensis, and Sitophilus zeamais (Table S1), along with their wheat‐flour substrate. Unlike most DNA‐only surveys, we employed both DNA and RNA amplicon sequencing to capture both total and transcriptionally active microbes. We also assessed community assembly processes and identified conserved taxa and phylogenetically stable clades. Our results indicate that these pests harbor distinct, host‐associated microbiomes primarily shaped by deterministic ecological processes, rather than solely by host phylogeny.

HOST‑SPECIFIC DIVERSITY AND CORE CONSORTIA

Divergent communities despite a common diet

Bacterial 16S sequencing (DNA‐ and RNA‐based) and fungal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) sequencing (DNA‐based) were used to profile and compare microbial diversity across the pests and the flour substrate (Tables S2–S4). Microbial diversity varied among hosts (Figure 1A,B, Tables S5,S6). Chao1 richness and Shannon diversity analyses revealed that S. zeamais harbored the most diverse bacterial community, followed by T. castaneum, whereas R. dominica and O. surinamensis exhibited relatively simple communities (Figure 1A). These patterns were evident in both total and active bacterial datasets, suggesting that host‐specific structuring is not confined to dormant or background taxa but also includes the metabolically active fraction of the microbiome. The common diet of flour supported high microbial diversity but clustered separately from insect‐associated communities (Figure 1C, Table S7). Using t‐distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t‐SNE), we identified distinct clusters for each beetle species, with flour samples forming a separate cluster (Figure 1C). Fungal patterns were more restricted, as DNA‐based ITS data identified fungal communities in L. oryzae, R. dominica, S. zeamais, and flour, but not in T. castaneum or O. surinamensis. Additionally, fungal RNA was not detected in any of the five pest species. Furthermore, host‐specific taxonomic patterns were more pronounced in RNA data (Figures S1–S3), indicating that active microbiota better reflect true diversity and host interactions than total DNA communities [6, 7]. These patterns indicate strong host filtering despite uniform rearing conditions and suggest that diet alone does not homogenize microbiota.

A conserved core microbiota coexists with host‐specific taxa

Although most amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were host‐specific, we identified 11 bacterial ASVs present in all pests (Figure 1D). These included Bacillus, Sphingomonas, Phyllobacterium, Ralstonia, Acidovorax, Delftia, Acinetobacter, Serratia, Escherichia‐Shigella, Lactobacillus, and Pseudomonas. Their ubiquity across hosts suggests adaptation to both insect and substrate niches, making them promising targets for community‐level interventions. Additionally, nine core fungal ASVs, including Gibberella, Penicillium, and Trichomonascus, among others, were shared across L. oryzae, R. dominica, and S. zeamais (Figure 1D). However, the inference of fungal roles was limited. ITS sequences were undetectable in T. castaneum and O. surinamensis, and no fungal RNA was recovered from any species, indicating extremely low fungal biomass or host‐mediated suppression. The absence of fungal amplification likely reflects both biological scarcity and technical biases, such as primer mismatch and extraction inefficiency. Therefore, future work should verify fungal presence using different primers, increased sequencing depth, and qPCR to differentiate between methodological and biological explanations. For stored‐product protection, the core microbiome may facilitate scalable substrate or community‐level interventions, with several taxa demonstrating recognized ecological and functional significance [8, 9, 10].

ECOLOGICAL ASSEMBLY AND THE INFLUENCE OF HOST PHYLOGENY

Deterministic processes dominate assembly

To disentangle the ecological drivers of microbiome assembly, we applied the normalized stochasticity ratio (NST) (Figure 2A). R. dominica and O. surinamensis exhibited strongly deterministic assembly (over 90%), whereas T. castaneum, L. oryzae, and S. zeamais displayed a mixture of deterministic (60%–70%) and stochastic (30%–40%) contributions. A dissimilarity‐overlap analysis further confirmed non‐neutral dynamics (Fraction of negative slope (Fns) = 0.02, p < 0.05). The high determinism observed in R. dominica and O. surinamensis coincided with the dominance of vertically transmitted endosymbionts (Wolbachia) (Figure S3A), suggesting that strong host‐mediated selection (or low within‐species variability) drives their microbial composition. The tightly regulated microbial environment in R. dominica and O. surinamensis indicates that these species host finely adapted microbes capable of successful colonization and persistence. This likely reflects long‐term co‐adaptation between the host and microbe, which is critical for their ecological success [11, 12]. In contrast, the mixed assembly mode in T. castaneum, L. oryzae, and S. zeamais suggests that random events, such as microbial dispersal and environmental fluctuations, play a meaningful role in increasing microbial community variability [11, 12, 13].

Limited phylogenetic signal

Because the five pest species belong to multiple coleopteran lineages, we also examined whether the microbiome structure aligns with the evolutionary relatedness of the hosts. A host phylogeny was constructed using mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase I (COI) sequences and compared it with Bray–Curtis dendrograms of bacterial communities (Figure 2B). The dendrograms appeared visually congruent, with closely related hosts clustering together; however, Mantel tests revealed no significant correlation between host phylogenetic distances and microbiome dissimilarity (Figure 2C). RNA‐based profiles yielded higher, though still non‐significant, correlation coefficients than DNA profiles. These findings suggest that shared environment and diet can obscure phylogenetic signals and that studying only five host species may be insufficient to detect subtle evolutionary effects. Future studies should incorporate additional taxa and ecological variables, such as digestive physiology and behavior, to clarify the relative roles of host evolution and environmental convergence in shaping insect‐associated microbiota.

CONSERVED CLADES AND METABOLICALLY ACTIVE MICROBES

Phylogenetic conservation of core lineages

Using a homogeneous selection (HoS) framework, we identified eight bacterial clades with low phylogenetic turnover across hosts (Figure 2D). These clades were enriched in Actinobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria, and Bacilli, highlighting their evolutionary stability under storage conditions. Their persistence suggests that they are not incidental background taxa but evolutionarily and ecologically stable components of the stored‐grain pest microbiome [12, 13]. Importantly, several of these conserved bacterial species overlapped with the core ASVs, suggesting that core taxa are not random but rather belong to evolutionarily conserved lineages that may confer important functions to their beetle hosts [14, 15]. Although conserved fungal ASVs were detected, over 80% of ITS reads in L. oryzae and R. dominica remained unclassified (Figure S3B), underscoring the need for improved fungal databases and targeted culturing.

Transcriptionally active communities

RNA sequencing enabled us to differentiate between metabolically active and total microbes. Across various hosts, several genera were significantly enriched in RNA profiles compared to DNA, indicating high transcriptional activity (Figure 2E, Table S8). In T. castaneum, the genera Serratia, Ralstonia, Pseudomonas, Enterococcus, and Burkholderia displayed RNA enrichment. L. oryzae showed an enrichment of Serratia; O. surinamensis exhibited increased transcription of Candidatus Brownia; and S. zeamais showed enrichment of Advenella. In flour, active taxa included Pseudomonas, Burkholderia, and Herbaspirillum. Many RNA‐enriched taxa overlapped with HoS clades, reinforcing the notion that conserved lineages are not only metabolically active but also functionally important. Given their versatile metabolic versatility, these genera may play a critical role in nutrient acquisition, detoxification, and the production of antimicrobial compounds [8, 9, 10, 16]. Overall, the convergence of core membership, homogeneous selection, and RNA enrichment strengthens the argument that these microbiomes contain a stable, active bacterial consortium shaped by strong host‐associated ecological filtering.

Our integrated DNA/RNA sequencing of five major coleopteran stored‐grain pests and their flour substrate reveals that host identity significantly shapes microbiota, even with a shared diet. A small core of bacterial taxa is conserved across the pests, and deterministic ecological processes reflecting host filtering and endosymbiont dominance drive community assembly. Phylogenetically stable clades overlap with metabolically active taxa, highlighting their potential functional importance. These findings identify promising targets for microbiome‐based pest management. Future research must verify the functional roles of core microbes, disentangle technical artifacts from biological scarcity (especially for fungi), and validate laboratory observations under realistic storage conditions. Only through such rigorous validation can microbiome manipulation become a practical tool for protecting grain supplies from pest damage.
