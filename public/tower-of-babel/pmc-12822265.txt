A new biomarker for screening Hiesho (cold hypersensitivity) in young females through measurement of the foot temperature-recovery rate after a cooling test
Authors: Tianyi Wang, Keisuke Shima, Yuko Ohno, Masayuki Endo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeHiesho (cold hypersensitivity) is a common condition in Japan and other Asian countries, particularly among females, and has substantial health implications. Despite the high prevalence of Hiesho, the existing screening methods for this condition remain controversial and largely subjective. This study aimed to explore the potential of using the foot skin temperature–recovery rate after a cooling test as a quantitative biomarker for Hiesho.MethodsSixty–two young females underwent a cooling test, which was followed by foot–temperature measurements. The participants also completed the Terasawa Hiesho Questionnaire. Data analysis included receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve evaluation and statistical tests to determine biomarker efficacy.ResultsHiesho was identified in 31 participants. The Hiesho and non–Hiesho groups showed significant differences in temperature–recovery rates, particularly at specific foot regions and time intervals. The Aligned Rank Transform was used for post–hoc pairwise comparisons. At the medial metatarsal 15 min post–cooling, the values for the Hiesho and No–Hiesho groups differed significantly (p < 0.01, Cohen’s d = 0.663, 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.231, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {\infty }$$\end{document}). The ROC curve identified the temperature–recovery rate at the medial metatarsal at 15 min post–cooling as the most effective biomarker, with a suggested screening threshold of < 44.68% (area under the curve = 0.701, 95% CI: [0.572, 0.811]).ConclusionThis study demonstrated significant differences in foot temperature–recovery rates between individuals with and without Hiesho, indicating its potential as a reliable biomarker. Despite these limitations, this approach offers a promising quantitative method for Hiesho assessment, and warrants further investigation.

Introduction

Hiesho, also known as cold hypersensitivity, cold sensitivity, or cold intolerance in English, and referred to as (Jue–leng) or (Shou–zu–ni–leng) in Chinese, () in Japanese, and in Korean,is a frequently occurring condition, particularly among females [1]. Hiesho affects nearly 50% of young Japanese women [2, 3], with similar prevalence rates ranging from 48% to 66% reported across East Asian populations [4–7]. In addition to Japan, the concept of Hiesho is widespread in countries such as China [8, 9], Korea [10], Thailand [11], and Iran [12].

The hallmark symptom of Hiesho is abnormal cold hypersensitivity, especially in the hands and feet, which occurs even in environments where others do not feel discomfort [13]. The sensation of abnormal coldness in Hiesho is not a single disease entity, but a symptom complex that can arise from several distinct pathophysiological mechanisms. Three common underlying causes include (1) peripheral artery disease, which impairs blood circulation to the extremities; (2) autonomic nerve dysfunction, which disrupts the body’s thermoregulatory control; and (3) hypometabolism, which reduces the body’s overall heat production [13]. Hiesho is associated with chronic conditions such as anemia, hypotension, and chronic gastritis [14].

Nakamura classified the factors contributing to Hiesho into two primary categories: internal and external [15]. Internal factors include hormonal imbalances, blood flow issues, and vasomotor nerve dysfunction, whereas external factors include lifestyle influences such as smoking, excessive workload, and psychological stress. Yoshino et al. highlighted the consistent onset of Hiesho symptoms from puberty, indicating a congenital predisposition rather than a purely acquired condition [4]. Recent genetic studies have provided additional insights; for instance, Wu et al. observed reduced expression of the TRPM2 and KCNK2 genes, which are crucial for temperature regulation and sensory perception, in Japanese women [16]. These reductions in gene activity likely exacerbate cold sensitivity and impair thermal recovery, contributing to the hallmark symptoms of Hiesho. The strong cultural and medical recognition of Hiesho in East Asia reflects both traditional medical paradigms that emphasize thermal regulation as a marker of health and potential genetic or environmental factors that may contribute to a higher prevalence in this population. This geographical context underscores the importance of developing validated diagnostic methods to bridge traditional medical concepts by using contemporary biomedical approaches.

However, the existing methods for diagnosing Hiesho primarily rely on self–reported data, such as the Terasawa Hiesho Questionnaire and Cold Intolerance Symptom Severity (CISS). The Terasawa Hiesho Questionnaire consists of 20 questions, including three main questions, five related questions, and 12 minor questions. Participants who answered positively to at least two main questions, one main question and two or more related questions, or four or more related questions were defined as having Hiesho; otherwise, they were considered to not show Hiesho. The CISS is a well–validated instrument primarily used in the Western medical literature to quantify the severity of cold intolerance. The CISS assesses six main items with several sub–questions evaluating three core areas: experienced symptoms, actions taken to prevent or ease symptoms, and consequences for activities of daily living. The total score ranges from 4 to 100, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. Despite their utility, a fundamental limitation of both the Terasawa Hiesho Questionnaire and CISS is their subjective nature. Diagnosis or severity assessment relies on patient self–reporting, which can vary significantly depending on subjective interpretation, cultural background, and environmental context. Moreover, the lack of an objective physiological metric limits the universal application of these instruments as well as their reliability. Consequently, a quantitative and universally applicable screening method based on direct physiological measurements is essential to complement or potentially supersede these subjective tools.

One promising approach involves examining the temperature differences between body regions. Wang et al. found that patients with Hiesho exhibit a significant temperature difference between the forehead and plantar regions owing to heightened cutaneous vasoconstrictor (CVC) activity [3]. CVC activation constricts peripheral blood vessels, reducing blood flow to the extremities without significantly affecting central regions such as the forehead [17]. Excessive sympathetic nerve activity has also been documented in these patients, which leads to increased CVC activity, decreased peripheral blood flow, and lower skin temperatures [18]. Experimental studies involving cold exposure have established that in comparison with healthy individuals, patients with Hiesho exhibit prolonged sympathetic activation and significantly slower recovery of hand and foot skin temperatures [19–24]. However, research on this topic has predominantly focused on the hands, leaving the thermoregulatory dynamics of the plantar foot, a site of critical importance, relatively underexplored. The plantar region is a particularly relevant test site for Hiesho for several physiological reasons [25]. As the most distal part of the body, it is highly susceptible to vasoconstriction, and it exhibits pronounced temperature changes. Meanwhile, evidence suggests that cold sensitivity and thermoregulatory responses differ between the hands and feet [26, 27], with one study indicating a lower cold sensation threshold in the feet of individuals with Hiesho [28], which suggests heightened sensitivity. Therefore, a detailed investigation of foot temperature recovery may provide a potentially more sensitive window into the impaired peripheral circulation characteristics in Hiesho. Despite its relevance, a comprehensive, region–specific analysis of plantar temperature recovery and a validated quantitative threshold for diagnosing Hiesho on the basis of this recovery are currently lacking.

Building on these physiological insights, we hypothesized that the recovery rate of plantar skin temperature following a cooling test could serve as a reliable biomarker for diagnosing Hiesho. Therefore, this study aimed to quantify plantar temperature–recovery rates in females with Hiesho and evaluate the viability of this metric for distinguishing individuals with and without Hiesho.

Cooling test and data analysis

The cooling test is illustrated in Fig. 1. First, participants were asked to sit still on a chair and immerse their feet in a foot bath filled with cold water (15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document}C) up to the ankles for 5 min. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve is a fundamental tool for evaluating the diagnostic performance of biomarkers and visualizing the trade–off between sensitivity (Se) and specificity (Sp) across all possible cutoff values [29]. The curve was plotted within a unit square, where the y–axis represents the true–positive rate (Se) and the x–axis represents 1-Sp (the false–positive rate). The lower–left corner (Se = 0, Sp = 1) corresponds to the most conservative cutoff value, whereas the upper–right corner (Se = 1, Sp = 0) corresponds to the most liberal cutoff value. A perfect diagnostic test has an ROC curve that passes through the upper–left corner (Se = 1, Sp = 1), yielding an area under the curve (AUC) of 1.0. In contrast, an uninformative test equivalent to random chance followed a 45° diagonal line (AUC = 0.5). The AUC serves as a global index of the discriminatory power of a biomarker, representing the probability that a randomly selected diseased individual has a higher or lower biomarker value than a randomly selected non–diseased individual [30]. Statistic analysis

The TRR was calculated using MATLAB (MathWorks, ver. 2022B). Statistical analyses were performed using JASP statistical software (ver. 0.95.2.0, The Netherlands). ROC analysis was conducted using MedCalc statistical software (ver. 22.023). The median, mean, and standard deviation of all the measured data were computed. An independent t–test was used to test for characteristic differences (i.e., age, height, and weight) between the Hiesho and No–Hiesho groups. Before selecting parametric or nonparametric procedures, we used the Shapiro–Wilk test as the primary method for normality checks. The Shapiro–Wilk test is widely recommended for small–to–moderate sample sizes and has greater power to detect departures from normality than the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test [38, 39], which is the best choice for testing the normality of data [40]. The equality of variances was tested using Levene’s test.

The analysis of TRRs presents a challenge common to physiological data; the outcomes were non–normally distributed and derived from a multi–factor repeated measures design. Initial checks using the Shapiro–Wilk test confirmed that the data violated the assumption of normality required for the parametric analysis of variance (ANOVA) (see Appendix Tables 8 and 9). Traditional nonparametric tests (e.g., the Mann–Whitney U test) are limited to single factors and are not suitable for analyzing interactions.

Therefore, we employed the Aligned Rank Transform (ART) procedure, a nonparametric method that allows factorial ANOVA on ranked data while maintaining appropriate type I error rates for the main effects and interactions [41]. The analysis was conducted using the ARTool software [42]. The procedure was applied to a two–way model with factor class (Hiesho, No–Hiesho) and period (5, 10, 15, and 20 min after the cooling test). Briefly, the ART procedure involved the following steps.1. Alignment: For each interaction effect (the main effects and their interactions), the data were aligned to remove all other effects.2. Ranking: The aligned responses were then assigned to mid–ranks.3. ANOVA: A standard factorial ANOVA was performed on the aligned ranks.The success of the alignment was verified by sanity checks, which confirmed that non–target effects were eliminated (F = 0.00, p = 1.00; See Appendix Tables 10 and 11).

Our primary hypothesis concerns the interaction between the Class and Period. A significant interaction indicated that the difference between the Hiesho and No–Hiesho groups depended on time point. Following a significant interaction, we performed post–hoc pairwise comparisons using ART contrast (ART–C) [43]. The ART–C procedure generates new aligned ranks, specifically for contrasts of interest, thus ensuring controlled error rates. We tested our hypothesis that the recovery rate in the Hiesho group would be lower than in the No–Hiesho group at each time point. P–values from these post–hoc tests were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the method inherent to the ART–C procedure. Cohen’s d was calculated as a measure of the effect size. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study successfully identified a quantitative physiological biomarker of Hiesho: the TRR at the medial metatarsal 15 min after a cooling test. Our results provide robust objective evidence that individuals with Hiesho exhibit a significantly impaired capacity to rewarm their feet after the cooling test. This result is consistent with that of a previous study [44], in which Kusumi et al. found that patients with Hiesho demonstrated reduced recovery rates after the cooling test. This finding directly translates into a validated diagnostic cutoff (TRR \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document} 44.68%), offering a concrete tool to address the field’s reliance on subjective questionnaires.

The significantly slower TRR across multiple foot regions in the Hiesho group provided direct physiological evidence of dysfunctional peripheral circulatory control. This impaired rewarming is consistent with the pathophysiological model for Hiesho, which is driven by heightened sympathetic nervous system activity and excessive peripheral vasoconstriction [45]. Therefore, the TRR can be interpreted as a functional assay of the ability of the microvasculature to rebound from sympathetic cold stimuli.

A key methodological advancement was our comprehensive mapping of the plantar surface of the foot, which revealed that not all regions were equally discriminative. The medial metatarsal area (Point 6) consistently showed the greatest and most sustained differences at 15 min after the cooling test. This finding is physiologically plausible, since recent methodological research has identified the medial plantar area as a reliable site for thermal recovery assessment [46]. The fact that our most powerful biomarker emerged from this region rather than from the foot digits underscores the importance of its anatomical specificity. Our proposed method is essential for empirically identifying this optimal site and resolving the methodological question of where and when to measure the most effective screening.

The proposed TRR cutoff (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document} 44.68%) demonstrated fair diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.701) with balanced sensitivity (64.5%) and specificity (71.0%). The fair agreement with the Terasawa Hiesho Questionnaire (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa$$\end{document} = 0.32) must be interpreted considering the fundamental constraint of this field: the absence of an objective gold standard. The Terasawa Hiesho Questionnaire is itself a subjective instrument. Therefore, the observed discrepancy is not merely a shortcoming of this method but may also reflect the inherent noise and cultural dependencies of symptom–based diagnosis. The proposed method represents a crucial step away from this subjectivity. A positive likelihood ratio (+LR) of 2.22 indicates that an individual meeting the TRR cutoff is more than twice as likely to be classified as Hiesho, providing tangible clinical utility for risk stratification.

A review of the existing literature on Hiesho assessment reveals diverse approaches, ranging from subjective questionnaires to objective physiological measurements. To contextualize our work in this field, we summarized the key studies in Table 6 and evaluated them on the basis of three criteria that are crucial for developing a robust, clinically actionable screening tool: (1) the use of a quantitative evaluation method, which reduces subjectivity; (2) the provision of a specific cutoff value, which is essential for consistent diagnosis; and (3) the inclusion of temporal and spatial data, which captures the dynamic nature of thermoregulation. Although the methodologies of these studies varied, including symptom severity scores, laser Doppler imaging, and thermography, this comparison highlights a consistent gap. As listed in Table 6, several existing methods lack one or more key elements. Some rely solely on subjective questionnaires [12, 47], while others provide quantitative data without a validated diagnostic cutoff [24, 48, 49] or capture spatial thermography data but not the critical temporal recovery component [3, 50]. Our study directly addressed this gap. The proposed method, which uses dynamic thermal imaging to calculate the TRR, is the first to integrate all three criteria; it is fully quantitative, provides a validated cutoff value (< 44.68%), and incorporates both temporal and spatial data. Nevertheless, this study had several limitations. First, it exclusively involved young Japanese females, limiting the generalizability of the findings across age groups and populations. The prevalence and characteristics of Hiesho vary with age and cultural context [51, 52]. Therefore, future research should include diverse demographic characteristics and consider the effects of lifestyle and environmental factors. Second, while the regions of interest for temperature measurement were defined on the basis of anatomical landmarks, they were manually delineated. This introduced the potential for interoperator variability. Future studies should aim to implement and validate a fully automated process, potentially leveraging machine learning algorithms for anatomical landmark detection [53], to further standardize the methodology and improve its scalability and reproducibility in diverse clinical settings. Third, this study operated within a recognized field constraint: the absence of a universally accepted objective gold standard for diagnosing Hiesho. Currently, diagnosis is primarily reliant on self–reported symptoms captured through questionnaires, such as the Terasawa Hiesho Questionnaire used as our reference standard. Although this is an established practice, it implies that the proposed quantitative method is validated against a subjective benchmark. However, it also powerfully underscores the context for the present study, since it highlights the urgent need for quantitative biomarkers and contributes to the development of objective physiological diagnostic tools to complement and refine current subjective assessments.

Conclusion

This study highlights the potential of the foot skin temperature–recovery rate after a cooling test as a viable biomarker for Hiesho screening. Despite the challenges in quantitatively diagnosing Hiesho, the observed differences in temperature–recovery rates between females with Hiesho and healthy individuals suggest that this biomarker could play a critical role in differential diagnosis. These findings offer promising implications for the development of a standardized and objective screening method for Hiesho. However, further research is needed to validate these results across diverse populations and refine the diagnostic criteria to enhance their clinical applicability.
