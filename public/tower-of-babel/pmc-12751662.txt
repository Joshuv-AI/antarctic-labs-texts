Birth weight distribution of neonates in Lombardy: development of CedAP charts and comparison with national and international charts
Authors: Sara Tunesi, Elena Spada, Corrado Celata, Antonio Giampiero Russo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBirth weight (BW) is an indicator for the assessment of neonatal health. No recent studies have specifically evaluated the distribution of BW in infants born in Lombardy and no updated regional neonatal charts are available. The aim of this study is to trace region-specific BW charts (CedAP charts) for neonates born in Lombardy and to compare them with the INeS charts, specific for Italian neonates, and with the Intergrowth21st (IG21) international neonatal charts.MethodsData of 361,105 singleton live births were extracted from the certificate of assistance in childbirth (CedAP) registry. The Extended Mechanistic Growth Function method was used to trace CedAP charts. The comparison with the INeS and the IG21 charts was performed by centiles shape, computing z-scores means and standard deviation, and by the proportion of small-for-gestational-age (SGA), appropriate-for-gestational-age (AGA), and large-for-gestational-age (LGA) detected with each chart.ResultsThe CedAP charts are similar to the INeS charts, particularly at lower centiles, while at higher centiles the CedAP charts tend to show slightly higher values. Comparisons with the IG21 charts show more differences. According to INeS charts, the mean BW z-score is close to expected, with minor deviations observed across gestational ages (GA). According to IG21 z-scores, the mean values were negative for preterm classes and positive for term neonates. The SDs are comparable in all the three charts. The proportion of neonates classified as SGA is overestimated in IG21 charts at GA < 33 weeks and underestimated at term. In contrast, the INeS and CedAP classifications are more consistent, with CedAP showing a slight underestimation of SGA at term. The IG21 charts tend to classify fewer preterm neonates as LGA.ConclusionWhile IG21 represent a useful tool for studies of populations comparison, CedAP and INeS charts may be more appropriate for assessing neonates in Lombardy and throughout all Italy. Background

Birth weight (BW) is a critical indicator for neonatal assessment. The current charts specific to Italian neonates are the INeS charts [1–3], which were traced in 2010 from a planned study with prospective data collection involving Italian third-level Centers. Although there are no reasons suggesting a different BW distribution for neonates born in Lombardy compared to the rest of Italy, no specific comparison has been conducted; and specific regional curves are not currently available. Moreover, INeS charts do not account for non-Italian neonates, who represent a significant proportion of Lombardy’s population: in 2020, 30.5% of neonates born in Lombardy had mothers with non-Italian citizenship [4]. Alternative Charts to INeS derive from the Intergrowth21st project, which are defined as references for gestational ages (GA) between 24 and 32 weeks, and as standard thereafter [5–7].

The distinction between a reference and a standard lies in their target populations, which are defined by their inclusion/exclusion criteria. Reference charts are derived from a population without exclusion criteria for risk factors affecting fetal growth (e.g., intrauterine growth restriction, maternal morbidities) and thus describe “how BW actually is” in that population. In contrast, standard charts are based on a population with highly restrictive inclusion criteria, defining the characteristics of infants who fully express their fetal growth potential, and describe “how BW should be” [8]. Due to their strict selection criteria, standard charts typically show lower variability and narrower centile bands compared to reference charts. By construction, when charts are applied to their target populations, 10% of neonates are classified as small-for-gestational-age (SGA), 80% as appropriate-for-gestational-age (AGA), and 10% as large-for-gestational-age (LGA). These proportions deviate from the expected values to the extent that the assessed population differs from the target population. Therefore, when standard charts are applied to a general population (which includes neonates with non-optimal fetal growth) a higher proportion of neonates is expected to fall below the 10th and above the 90th centiles. This aspect should be carefully considered in clinical practice, as it may affect diagnostic thresholds and management choices.

In Lombardy, the Agency for Health Protection of the Metropolitan Area of Milan (ATS-MI) covers a population of over 3.5 million people across the provinces of Milan and Lodi. From 2014 to 2022, ATS-MI recorded an average of 26,000 births per year [9], representing 35% of all births in Lombardy. ATS-MI has 18 maternity units, four of which provide neonatal intensive care unit. For each birth, a Certificate of Assistance in Childbirth (CedAP [4]) is issued, recording data such as delivery date, neonate’s sex, malformations, multiple birth, GA, vital status, and anthropometric traits at birth (weight, length, and head circumference).

Methods

Aim, design, and setting of the study

The aim of this study is to trace region-specific neonatal charts for the Lombardy population and to compare them with the Italian and international charts used in clinical practice: the INeS charts, specific for Italian infants [3], and the Intergrowth21st charts which are international charts [7]. The study was conducted in the Lombardy Region, utilizing records collected from the ATS-MI CedAP (Birth Assistance Certificate).

In this paper, the INeS charts will be referred to as INeS, and the Intergrowth21st neonatal charts as IG21.

Study population

Birth information (date of birth, GA in completed weeks of amenorrhea, multiple birth, length, weight, head circumference, and malformations) was extracted from the ATS-MI CedAP. The study population consists of all single live-born babies delivered up to 42 weeks of GA between 2010 and 2022. Fetal hydrops and congenital anomalies were excluded, as well as for record where GA, sex, or BW were not available.

Statistical analysis

BW was expressed as a z-score according to the INeS, and the IG21. Outliers were identified and excluded using a two-step process: (1) BW outside the range of mean ± 4xSD, computed by GA and sex based on the study population (internal coherence); (2) BW z-score exceeding ± 4 according to either INeS or IG21st (external coherence).

The CedAP-charts were traced using the Extended Mechanistic Growth Function (EMGF) method, specifically using the EGLF-4 function as used in tracing the INeS [10]. The derived centiles were then parameterized in terms of smooth sex and GA specific L, M, and S curves just as in the Cole and Green LMS (GC-LMS) methods [11]. (see Additional file 1).

CedAP-charts were compared with INeS, and IG21 neonatal charts based on centile shape, and their suitability to describe the study population. This aspect was explored by calculating BW z-score mean and standard deviation (SD), and by classifying the study population into Small for GA (SGA: BW < 10th centile), Large for GA (LGA: BW > 90th centile), and Appropriate for GA (AGA: 10th ≤ BW ≤ 90th centile).

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

Results

The CedAP registry collected data on 386,354 records of neonates born between 2010 and 2022, of which 361,914 satisfied the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data from 809 (0.2%) neonates were identified as outliers, resulting in a study population consisting of 361,105 single live-born babies. Among all neonates, 69.1% were born to mothers with Italian citizenship, and assisted reproductive technology was reported in 4.1% of pregnancies. Boys represented 51.3% and approximately 95% of the neonates were born at term (GA ≥ 37 weeks) with a median (interquartile range) GA of 39 (38–40) weeks. The mean (standard deviation) BW was 3,199 (463) g for girls and 3,327 (485) g for boys, while the mean BW z-score was − 0.04 (1.01) according to INeS and + 0.09 (0.93) according to IG21. The differences among the three charts lead to different classifications of babies based on their BW and GA (Fig. 3). Specifically, the CedAP charts slightly underestimate the SGA-infants at term (GA ≥ 37 weeks) and overestimate them between 28 and 33 weeks, generally underestimating LGA-infants across all GA classes, except for 32–33 weeks in girls and 28–33 weeks in boys. The INeS tend to overestimate SGA-infants before 33 weeks in both sexes; thereafter, the observed percentage of SGA-infants aligns more closely with the expected values. In girls, LGA-infants are overestimated up to 32–33 weeks GA, and in boys the overestimation is observed up to 33 weeks. The observed percentage aligns closely with the expected one from 34 weeks onward.

The IG21 exhibits a distinct trend: the SGA-infants are overestimated for both sexes up to 33 weeks (when the IG21 are reference), reaching nearly double the expected percentage in the 28–31 weeks classes. At term (≥ 37 weeks), when IG21 are standard, the SGA-infants are underestimated by about 3%. Conversely, the LGA-infants are underestimated up to 31 weeks and within the 34–36 weeks classes in girls, as well as up to 36 weeks in boys; at term (≥ 37 GA weeks) the proportion is approximately 10%.

The INeS exhibit the highest level of misclassification in neonates with GA < 28 weeks, where 4% of neonates classified as AGA by the CedAP charts are classified as SGA by the INeS. Discussion

This study developed BW CedAP charts specific to neonates born in the Milan Metropolitan Area (Lombardy), and compared them with INeS (national charts) [3] and IG21 (international charts) [7]. The findings provide insights into the consistency and differences among these charts and underscore their applicability for clinical use.

The CedAP charts and INeS are very similar, particularly at lower centiles, where the distributions nearly overlap. Differences become more pronounced in the upper centiles, where CedAP charts tend to show slightly higher values. This difference is likely attributable to the broader sampling of the CedAP charts, which include all births in Lombardy including all care unit level and parental origin, as well as to differences in data sources: the INeS are based on an ad-hoc planned study with measurements taken prospectively by trained operators, while CedAP data are derived from routine administrative records, where precision is less rigorously controlled. Despite these limitations, the quality of BW data appears adequate and results in variability by GA comparable to that observed in the INeS chart, which was derived from a planned study (as reflected in the spacing between centiles).

Another significant methodological distinction is that CedAP registry uses completed weeks of amenorrhea to define GA, whereas INeS dataset provides finer granularity including days, often using first-trimester ultrasound dating. This might partially explain the earlier occurrence of slope peaks observed in CedAP-charts, where peaks precede those in INeS-charts by approximately one week. This pattern aligns with the behaviours of the parameter tau in the EGLF-4 function used to trace both CedAP charts and INeS, facilitating a direct comparison of chart shapes (Additional file 1).

In contrast, the IG21 exhibit broader differences from both the CedAP charts and the INeS. Notably, they tend to underestimate the percentages of SGA-neonates at term, while classifying a higher percentage of neonates as SGA at earlier GAs. Conversely, the proportion of LGA-neonates is very low among preterm neonates (nearly half of the expected at < 28 weeks) and reaches 10% at term. This finding diverges from expectations based on their definition as “reference” and “standard”: by definition, reference charts should display a greater spread of centiles than standard ones, as their inclusion/exclusion criteria are less restrictive [8]. Thus, when standard charts are applied to a “real” population, both the percentage of SGA-and LGA-neonates are expected to exceed the 10%. Similarly, the z-score standard deviation (SD) should be greater than 1, reflecting the broader variability of the “real”-world compared to the more restricted target population of a standard. However, in the IG21, an SGA percentage exceeding 10% is observed within the GA range defined as “reference”, while a percentage lower than 10% is observed within the GA range defined as “standard”. A similar pattern, though less pronounced, is observed for the LGA percentage. The z-score SD also behaves contrary to expectations: it is > 1 in the “reference” GA rage, and < 1 in the standard GA range. Additionally, these charts start from 24 weeks, which limits their utility for extreme preterm neonates.

Consistent with findings from other studies [13–16], the use of local charts, when available, should be preferred for the clinical assessment of the neonates because these charts are more representative of the specific demographics and health characteristics of the population.

Although weight-for-GA classification is based on a statistical distribution, in clinical practice it is often used as a proxy to identify neonates at increased risk of adverse outcomes. Each neonatal chart can thus be considered a distinct screening test. Depending on the chart used, the same neonate may be classified differently, potentially affecting clinical decision-making. A more informative comparison of these tools would require estimating their sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values for relevant short- and long-term outcomes. Unfortunately, this was not possible in the present study because follow-up data were unavailable in the CedAP registry.

The CedAP charts should be interpreted as a descriptive representation of birthweight distribution in a large and heterogeneous regional population. Their aim is not to replace existing national or international charts, but to complement them by reflecting local characteristics.

It should also be considered that the ATS-MI area includes several referral centers for high-risk pregnancies. This may result in a higher proportion of complex cases compared to the general population. However, the potential bias introduced by this is limited: most very preterm neonates are already delivered in tertiary hospitals across regions, term neonates with pathology are few, and the applied exclusion criteria remove the cases most likely to affect centile estimation.

Conclusion

While the IG21 serve as a valuable tool in specific contexts, such as comparative studies across different Countries, the CedAP charts and the INeS may be better suited for the assessment of neonates in Lombardy. These findings suggest that the INeS, despite their age, remain relevant for clinical practice, with CedAP charts offering complementary, region-specific support. Further studies are needed to assess the clinical predictive performance of different neonatal charts in identifying neonates at risk for adverse outcomes.
