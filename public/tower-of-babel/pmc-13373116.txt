Life’s Essential 8-Defined Cardiovascular Health and Mortality Risk in Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic Health Stages 0–4: A Cohort Study
Authors: Ruiyang Niu, Qiqi You, Wan Fu, Lingqi Wei, Jingjing Zeng, Yanyan Jia, Xinyi Zhang, Xiaoying Li, Shaoyong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome represents a primary contributor to global morbidity and mortality. Despite the Life’s Essential 8 framework offering a comprehensive assessment of cardiovascular health (CVH), its prognostic utility in CKM syndrome remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the association between Life’s Essential 8-defined CVH and mortality risk in CKM health stages 0–4.MethodsData were analyzed from 302,171 UK Biobank participants in CKM health stages 0–4. CVH scores were calculated based on eight components: diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep health, body mass index, blood lipids, blood glucose, and blood pressure. All-cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality were ascertained through linked health records. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to examine associations between CVH scores and mortality risks. The predictive factors for mortality risk were explored using the gradient boosting machines method.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 12.7 years, 20,282 all-cause and 3,979 CVD deaths occurred. Compared with low CVH, high CVH was associated with 55% (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.42–0.49) and 67% (95% CI: 0.27–0.41) reductions in all-cause and CVD mortality. The inverse association of CVH scores with CVD mortality was stronger in non-advanced stages (P for interaction = 0.002). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a significant nonlinear association for all-cause mortality (P for nonlinear < 0.001), but not for CVD mortality (P for nonlinear = 0.936). Machine learning analysis indicated that among the eight components, nicotine exposure and blood glucose scores had the highest relative influence on mortality prediction.ConclusionsHigher CVH scores were associated with lower mortality risk in CKM health stages 0–4, with stronger associations observed in non-advanced stages. Among the eight components, nicotine exposure and blood glucose scores showed stable and strong associations with mortality. These findings support the potential value of CVH assessment for risk stratification across CKM health stages.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44197-026-00590-3.

Background

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, an integrated pathobiological network comprising cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease, and metabolic disorders, constitutes a substantial health challenge with high morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Epidemiological evidence reveals its emergence as a significant contributor to American deaths in 2021 [3]. Driven by interconnected molecular pathways, its pathogenesis causes multi-organ dysfunction via low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, and endothelial dysfunction, forming a vicious cycle accelerating renal impairment, atherosclerosis, and cardiovascular risk [4]. Despite advances in treatment, individuals at CKM health stage 4 had 3.7 times greater all-cause mortality than those at stage 0 [5], underscoring the critical urgency for early risk stratification and targeted interventions.

In 2022, the American Heart Association (AHA) launched the Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) framework, updating cardiovascular health (CVH) metrics by integrating four health behaviors and four health factors [6]. While LE8 demonstrates robust prognostic value in general populations [7–10], with high LE8 scores associated with 46% lower all-cause mortality and 63% lower CVD mortality [11], its utility within the CKM health staging framework has yet to be systematically investigated. Given the unique multi-organ pathophysiology in CKM syndrome [4], conventional risk models may fail to fully capture the compounding effects of metabolic dysregulation and subclinical organ damage. For instance, hyperglycemia and chronic inflammation in CKM syndrome may disproportionately amplify vascular damage [12], emphasizing the prognostic significance of glucose and lipid components in the LE8 framework. However, no systematic evaluation has explored how the association of LE8 with mortality varies across CKM health stages, nor which components are the key predictors of mortality.

To bridge these knowledge gaps, this research used data from UK Biobank to: (1) investigate the association of LE8-defined CVH scores with all-cause/CVD mortality in CKM health stages 0–4; (2) identify which CVH metrics have the strongest association with mortality; and (3) assess stage-specific prognostic value of CVH metrics.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

The UK Biobank recruited roughly 500,000 individuals aged 40–69 years at baseline, with subsequent ongoing follow-up [13]. The North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee approved this study (21/NW/0157), and the research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All enrolled individuals provided written informed consent, and comprehensive study details remain publicly available at https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/.

We identified eligible participants from the initial cohort of 502,392 individuals. Firstly, 125,024 individuals lacking data for determining CKM health stages were excluded, leaving 377,368 individuals at stages 0–4 of CKM health. Subsequently, individuals with missing data on death cause (n = 128), CVH scores (n = 27,500), and covariates (n = 47,569) were further excluded to obtain the final analytic cohort of 302,171 participants (sFigure 1).

Definition of CKM Health Stages 0–4

Following the AHA framework for CKM health [4], we defined disease stages for study participants (sMethods). Stage 0 is defined as the absence of overweight/obesity, no documented metabolic risk factors, no chronic kidney disease, and absence of subclinical/clinical CVD manifestations. Individuals progress to Stage 1 if they exhibit overweight/obesity, abdominal obesity, and/or prediabetes, absent other metabolic risk factors or chronic kidney disease. Stage 2 is defined as individuals with metabolic risk factors or chronic kidney disease but without subclinical/clinical CVD. Collectively, stages 0–2 constitute non-advanced CKM health stages.

Individuals qualifying for Stage 3 display subclinical cardiovascular pathology plus at least one of: overweight/obesity, metabolic risk factors, or chronic kidney disease. Subclinical status confirmation requires documented: chronic kidney disease G4/G5 or Framingham-derived high 10-year CVD risk estimation. Stage 4 comprises patients with established CVD who additionally present with overweight/obesity, metabolic risk factors, or chronic kidney disease. Stages 3–4 are classified as advanced CKM health stages [14].

Assessments of CVH

The AHA-recommended LE8 scoring system served as our CVH assessment tool (sMethods) [6]. Diet was assessed using a modified Healthy Diet Index adapted to the UK Biobank data (sTable m4) [15, 16]. The index included six positive and six negative components. Each was scored as 0 or 1 based on whether the participant met a pre-specified criterion, yielding a raw diet score from 0 to 12. The raw score was then converted to the LE8 0–100 scale using population percentile thresholds (sTable m3). Physical activity measure was derived from participant-reported weekly minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activities, with each minute of vigorous activity counted as two minutes of moderate activity. This summed equivalent volume was applied to assign the LE8 physical activity score following AHA guidelines. Nicotine exposure was quantified using a composite score based on smoking status and time since quitting. Current smokers included those who reported smoking on most or all days or only occasionally. Former smokers were identified as those who reported smoking on most or all days in the past but were not current smokers, and years since quitting was calculated as age at recruitment minus age when stopped smoking.

Sleep health was defined using participant-provided estimates of habitual sleep per 24-hour period. Body mass index originated from anthropometric assessments of weight and height (kg/m²). Blood pressure was obtained in two sequential clinical readings, with the mean serving as the final measure. Non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) levels quantified by deducting high-density lipoprotein cholesterol from total cholesterol. Blood pressure and non-HDL-C levels were categorized and scored according to AHA’s LE8 criteria, with a 20-point deduction applied to participants receiving antihypertensive/cholesterol lowering medication. Blood glucose levels were quantified using hexokinase enzymatic assays, and glycated hemoglobin levels were measured via high-performance liquid chromatography. Diabetes history was determined using ICD-10 codes (E10-E14), random blood glucose levels, and measured glycated hemoglobin values. These casual glycated hemoglobin measurements and diabetes status were used to assign blood glucose scores.

All eight components were rescaled to 0–100 ranges. The composite CVH score was derived as their unweighted mean and stratified using AHA criteria: 0–49, 50–79, and 80–100 [6]. A higher score indicated superior CVH status.

Assessment of Other Factors

Demographic characteristics, socioeconomic factors, cancer history, and medication usage were collected through standardized questionnaires. Ethnicity was categorized into: White, Mixed, Asian, Black, or Other. Educational attainment comprised six levels, with “college or university degree” representing the highest level and “other professional qualifications or none” the lowest. Pre-tax household income fell into five categories. Area-level socioeconomic deprivation, measured by the Townsend Deprivation Index, was derived from participants’ residential postcodes. Cancer history was determined based on the question, “Has a doctor ever told you that you have had cancer?”. Data on medication use for cholesterol, blood pressure, or diabetes were obtained via a touchscreen self-report question (“Do you regularly take any of the following medications?”), allowing multiple selections. Responses were classified into five groups: cholesterol-lowering medication, blood pressure medication, insulin, any combination and no use of the listed medications.

Ascertainment of Outcome

All-cause and CVD mortality served as the primary endpoints. Vital status and cause-of-death information (including dates) were sourced through linkage to national death registries. Underlying mortality causes were coded according to the ICD-10 criteria. Person-time was calculated from baseline to the earliest of death or administrative censoring (November 30, 2021).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical processing was implemented in RStudio and Stata 17.0 with α = 0.05 significance level. Baseline characteristics were described as follows: continuous variables using mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables using counts and percentages. Differences among categories were evaluated with one-way analysis of variance, Kruskal-Wallis, or χ² tests.

Cox proportional hazards models were employed to evaluate the association between CVH scores/components and the risk of all-cause/CVD mortality in CKM health stages 0–4. For CVD mortality, we used cause-specific Cox models, in which non-CVD deaths were treated as censored events at the time of death. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals and adequately satisfied for primary analyses. Influential observation diagnostics were also examined, with no material distortion of main results identified. The dose-response association between CVH scores and mortality risk was explored using restricted cubic spline analysis. A gradient boosting machine analysis (five-fold cross-validation) was conducted to rank the predictive importance of the eight components [17]. Variable importance was based on relative influence, and model performance was evaluated using the C-index. We also evaluated the impact of CVH scores/components on mortality risk, stratifying by non-advanced and advanced CKM health stages. Subsequently, we examined the joint effects of CVH scores and CKM health stages (non-advanced/advanced) on mortality.

To detect effect heterogeneity, subgroups were defined by sex and age (cutoff: 65 years). Sensitivity analyses were implemented to assess outcome reliability: (1) excluding participants with outcomes < 5 years after baseline to minimize potential reverse causation; (2) multiple imputation for missing covariates; (3) validating results with alternative CVH categorizations (tertile-based [18] and Sun J et al.’s method [19]; (4) constructing a weighted CVH score using β coefficients of individual components; (5) using Fine-Gray competing risks regression to estimate cause-specific hazards for CVD mortality, accounting for non-CVD death as a competing event; (6) repeating main analyses in participants with CKM syndrome (stages 1–4); (7) using robust standard errors to confirm the robustness of the findings.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Table 1 presented participants’ baseline characteristics (mean age 56.1 years; 48.1% males). The distribution across CVH categories was as follows: low CVH (9.0%), moderate CVH (79.0%), and high CVH (12.0%). Notably, the high CVH group exhibited obvious demographic disparities, with a younger age and lower proportion of male participants, indicating that favorable CVH status was highly demographic-dependent. Compared with participants with high CVH, those with low CVH were more likely to be non-White, with lower education/income levels, higher prevalence of cancer history, and greater use of antihypertensive, antidiabetic, and lipid-lowering drugs (P < 0.001). They also had elevated cardio-metabolic risk factors like body mass index, non-HDL-C, blood glucose, glycated hemoglobin, and blood pressure (P < 0.001). The baseline comparison of included versus excluded participants were shown in sTable 1. Most characteristics showed negligible between-group differences, while excluded individuals were slightly older with modestly higher blood glucose and glycated hemoglobin (|standardized mean difference| > 0.1).

Association between CVH scores/components and mortality in CKM health stages 0–4

Over a median follow-up of 12.7 years, 20,282 all-cause and 3,979 CVD deaths occurred. Compared with low CVH, high CVH was associated with 55% (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.42–0.49) and 67% (95% CI: 0.27–0.41) reductions in all-cause and CVD mortality (Fig. 1). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a significant inverse trend between CVH scores and all-cause/CVD mortality (P for overall < 0.001). A significant nonlinear association was observed for all-cause mortality (P for nonlinear < 0.001), but not for CVD mortality (P for nonlinear = 0.936) (Fig. 2).

All CVH components except blood lipids scores were inversely associated with all-cause mortality. For CVD mortality, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep health, body mass index, blood glucose, and blood pressure scores were related to lower risks (P < 0.05), whereas diet and blood lipids scores exhibited no significant association (P > 0.05) (Table 2). In the gradient boosting machine analysis, the C-index was 0.764 for all-cause mortality and 0.828 for CVD mortality. Variable importance analysis showed age and CKM health stages were the two dominant predictors of both all‑cause and CVD mortality. Among the eight components, nicotine exposure and blood glucose scores had the highest relative influence on mortality prediction (sTable 2).

Association Between CVH Scores/Components and Mortality in Non-advanced/Advanced CKM Health Stages

Higher CVH scores were shown to be inversely associated with all-cause and CVD mortality risks in both non-advanced (stages 0–2) and advanced CKM heath stages (stages 3–4) (Fig. 1). In non-advanced stages, higher CVH scores (80–100) corresponded to hazard ratios (HRs) of 0.45 (95% CI: 0.42–0.49) for all-cause mortality and 0.26 (95% CI: 0.20–0.33) for CVD mortality. Similarly, in advanced stages, CVH scores ranging 80–100 was related to 49% (HR: 0.51, 95% CI: 0.42–0.63) and 46% (HR: 0.54, 95% CI: 0.38–0.77) reduced risks of all-cause and CVD mortality, respectively. Although the association between CVH scores and all-cause mortality risk did not differ significantly between the two groups (P for interaction = 0.376), the inverse association of CVH scores with CVD mortality was stronger in non-advanced stages (P for interaction = 0.002).

Individuals at non-advanced stages and with high CVH scores exhibited the lowest mortality risks compared to those at advanced stages and with low CVH scores. Specifically, the HR for all-cause mortality was 0.27 (95% CI: 0.25–0.29) and for CVD mortality was 0.11 (95% CI: 0.08–0.13) (sTable 3). Both nicotine exposure and blood glucose scores showed significantly inverse associations with mortality in both non-advanced and advanced stages (P < 0.001). In advanced stages, abnormally low body mass index or blood pressure levels were associated with increased mortality risk (sTable 4 and sTable 5).

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

CVH scores were inversely associated with both all-cause and CVD mortality. For all-cause mortality, the association did not differ by sex (P for interaction = 0.718) but was stronger in younger participants (P for interaction < 0.001). For CVD mortality, significant interactions were observed for both sex (P for interaction = 0.004) and age (P for interaction < 0.001), with stronger inverse associations in females and younger participants (sTable 6).

Eight sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of our primary associations (sTable 7). These included excluding early events, addressing missing covariates via multiple imputation, testing alternative CVH score grouping strategies, using weighted CVH scores, applying competing risks regression for CVD mortality, restricting analyses to participants with CKM syndrome (stages 1–4), and employing robust standard errors. Results across these sensitivity analyses were consistent with the primary findings in both direction and statistical significance, confirming the reliability of our observations.

Discussion

This study provided novel evidence that optimal LE8-defined CVH was associated with substantial survival benefits in CKM health stages 0–4. Relative to low CVH, high CVH was linked to 55% and 67% lower risks of all-cause and CVD mortality, respectively. Notably, the inverse association for CVD mortality was stronger in non-advanced CKM health stages. Among the eight CVH components, nicotine exposure and blood glucose scores were both associated with lower mortality risk, while paradoxical associations were observed for low body mass index and blood pressure in advanced CKM health stages.

CVH has been consistently associated with lower mortality risk across diverse populations, including the general population, individuals with chronic kidney disease, and those with obesity [19–21]. Our current investigation extended this evidence by identifying favorable associations between optimal CVH and survival outcomes within the CKM health staging framework. Potential biological pathways that may be related to the observed associations included oxidative stress [22], chronic low-grade inflammation [23, 24], and endothelial dysfunction [25]. As potential mediators were assessed at baseline concurrently with the exposure, the temporal sequence between CVH and these factors could not be established. Consequently, formal mediation analyses were not conducted in the current study. Future investigations with repeated measurements of biomarkers and multi‑omics approaches could help to clarify the relationships and further characterize the roles of these pathways.

The stage-dependent attenuation of the favorable association between CVH scores and CVD mortality revealed critical insights into the pathophysiological heterogeneity across CKM health stages. In non-advanced stages, the presence of more reversible risk factors and better organ function reserves likely strengthened the favorable links with CVH optimization, which underscored the value of early-life lifestyle interventions for sustained health promotion [6]. In populations with impaired glucose tolerance, lifestyle interventions have been associated with significant benefits, including delayed diabetes onset, CVD/microvascular risk reduction, and lower mortality [26]. In advanced stages, however, the favorable associations of intensive risk factor management may be counterbalanced given multisystem comorbidities, age-related frailty, and shortened lifespan [27]. This pattern was corroborated by prior evidence showing that the favorable associations of CVH scores with lower stroke risk weaken with advancing age. Specifically, the burden of stroke eliminated by controlling high blood pressure declined from 53% in middle age to 39% in older age [28].

Through gradient-boosting machine learning analysis, we quantified the strengthen of associations between individual variables and mortality risk in individuals at CKM health stages 0–4. While age and CKM health stages emerged as the most influential predictors in the overall model, nicotine exposure and blood glucose scores were the most impactful modifiable CVH components. These findings aligned with prior evidence indicating nicotine exposure as the second important contributor among the eight components to all-cause mortality in American adults [19]. A research has also identified smoking as the second-largest contributor to the population-attributable fraction of all-cause mortality [29]. Nicotine exposure has been associated with endothelial dysfunction and increased oxidative stress in prior studies, which were correlated with accelerated atherosclerotic progression and higher cardiovascular event rates [30]. Blood glucose scores also showed a robust association with mortality risk in both non-advanced and advanced stages. Hyperglycemia at hospital admission has been reported to be related to increased mortality risk in patients with acute myocardial infarction, regardless of pre-existing diabetes [31]. Zhang Y et al. also demonstrated that among various health factors, blood glucose was the only factor significantly associated with CVD mortality risk [32]. Together, these findings suggested that nicotine cessation and glycemic control may serve as potentially impactful, modifiable priorities across the spectrum of CKM health stages.

Our analysis revealed that, among participants at CKM health stages 0–4, those with more ideal (higher) non-HDL-C scores had increased mortality. This paradoxical relationship, combined with the controversial prognostic value of blood lipids in existing research, underscored the complexity of risk stratification within this population. Major cohort analyses demonstrated divergent mortality linkages across lipid metrics. Han L et al. observed no significant association between non-HDL-C and all-cause/CVD mortality, while noting its potential link to elevated cancer-related mortality [33]. Consistent with these results, Sun J et al. emphasized the need for re-evaluating the ideal lipid levels, given the heterogeneous evidence base [19]. The observed paradox may be partially explained by reverse epidemiology, a phenomenon frequently documented in elderly populations. Low total cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were related to frailty, a condition accompanied by muscle weakness, immune dysfunction, and metabolic disturbance, which in turn correlated with higher mortality burden [34]. This age-related confounding was particularly relevant in individuals with advanced CKM health stages, who commonly carried multiple chronic comorbidities with physiological features overlapping frailty. Additionally, real-world evidence from patients with coronary artery disease has revealed a U-shaped relationship between low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels and all-cause mortality [35]. These collective observations indicated that extreme lipid profiles may reflect underlying subclinical conditions such as malnutrition and chronic inflammation, rather than acting as independent cardiovascular risk markers alone.

In non-advanced stages, lower body mass index and blood pressure were linked to reduced mortality. Excessive obesity has been associated with insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, and increased hemodynamic stress on the cardiovascular system [36, 37], collectively elevating the risk of cardiovascular events and mortality. Chronic hypertension may contribute to hypertension-mediated organ damage. Key manifestations such as left ventricular hypertrophy and microalbuminuria characterized preclinical CVD [38]. However, our analysis revealed a paradoxical shift in advanced stages, where lower body mass index and blood pressure were related to higher mortality risk. This phenomenon likely reflected the unique clinical and physiological characteristics of advanced CKM health stages, which typically involved older age, multimorbidity, and heightened disease severity. Prior evidence has suggested that in older populations, a body mass index below recommended ranges or systolic blood pressure < 110 mmHg was associated with elevated mortality [39, 40], a pattern potentially attributed to unmeasured confounding factors including frailty, sarcopenia, and cardiac cachexia [41, 42]. Accordingly, conventional risk factor benchmarks derived from general populations and generalized targets may not be directly applicable to advanced CKM health stages. Further longitudinal studies with careful adjustment for time-varying confounders (e.g., functional status, nutritional markers) are needed to clarify these relationships.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, health behavior data were self-reported, which may be subject to social desirability bias or recall errors. Second, the application of equal weighting for CVH components assumed a uniform contribution to mortality risk, an assumption that may not hold true. Sensitivity analysis using weighted scores produced similar results, yet this method remains constrained by its study-specific weight assignments and inability to capture complex interactions among components. Third, the cohort’s predominance of White participants from higher socioeconomic strata [43], together with small baseline differences between included and excluded participants (i.e., the included cohort being slightly younger and having lower blood glucose and glycated hemoglobin levels), may introduce selection bias and restrict the generalizability of our findings. Fourth, despite multivariable adjustment being performed, residual confounding factors such as functional status and nutritional markers may still affect observed associations. Fifth, to reduce attrition and selection bias from high rates of missing urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio data, the definition of chronic kidney disease relied solely on estimated glomerular filtration rate and ICD-10 codes, which may miss some cases with chronic kidney disease.

Conclusions

Higher LE8-defined CVH scores were associated with lower risks of all-cause/CVD mortality in CKM health stages 0–4, with stronger inverse associations observed in non-advanced stages. Among the eight components, nicotine exposure and blood glucose scores showed stable and strong associations with mortality outcomes, while the associations of body mass index and blood pressure scores with mortality differed between non-advanced and advanced stages. These findings support the use of CVH assessment for prognostic risk stratification within the CKM staging framework and highlight the value of integrating stage-specific characteristics into future risk stratification strategies.
