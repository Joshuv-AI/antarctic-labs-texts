Overcoming the nuclear barrier in nonviral gene therapy: mechanisms, carrier design, and translational perspectives on NLS peptide and DNA targeting sequence strategies
Authors: Taegeun Lee, Ye-Rin Kim, Euijin Lee, Sae-Ock Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEfficient delivery of plasmid DNA (pDNA) into the nucleus remains a major bottleneck in nonviral gene therapy, particularly in post-mitotic and slowly dividing cells where the nuclear envelope constitutes a permanent barrier to passive entry. This review considers the two main strategies for active nuclear targeting: nuclear localisation signal (NLS) peptides, which recruit importin-α/β receptors directly to DNA cargo, and DNA nuclear targeting sequences (DTS), which exploit NLS-bearing transcription factors to mediate piggyback nuclear import. Rather than re-cataloguing nuclear import biology, we focus on the conditions under which each strategy succeeds or fails, incorporating advances in nuclear pore complex architecture and permeability, karyopherin-FG-nucleoporin electrostatics, and cytoplasmic constraints such as barrier-to-autointegration factor (BAF)-mediated clustering of exogenous DNA. NLS classes are compared with universal (SV40 enhancer), inducible (NF-κB) and tissue-specific (SMGA, SP-C, T1α) DTS elements, and four chemical coupling strategies are evaluated across peptide-, polymer- and lipid-based nanoassemblies. To address the near-absence of NLS/DTS mechanisms in current clinical pDNA programmes, we propose the Nuclear Accessibility Index (NAI): a three-axis framework integrating cell division status, carrier-DNA accessibility and intracellular signalling context. Treating the NAI as an axis vector rather than a summed score defines permissive, conditional and excluded regions of the design space. Two matter in practice: one in which highly proliferative cells render active targeting redundant, and one in which the rate-limiting problem is carrier engineering rather than the addition of a signal. The NAI offers design principles for nuclear-targeted nanocarriers and defines the preconditions for therapeutically relevant active import.Keywords: Nuclear localisation signal, DNA nuclear targeting sequence, nonviral gene delivery, plasmid DNA, nuclear pore complex, importin, nanoassembly, nuclear accessibility index

Introduction

Efficient delivery of plasmid DNA (pDNA) to the nucleus is a major hurdle for clinical gene therapy and DNA vaccines. In post-mitotic or slowly dividing tissues such as muscle, skin and dendritic cells the nuclear envelope remains intact, so pDNA can enter only through nuclear pore complexes (NPCs) or during infrequent mitosis, and only ~1–5% of administered non-viral DNA is estimated to reach the nucleus (Yao et al. 2013). Microinjection studies established the consequence directly: DNA retained in the cytoplasm gives virtually no expression, whereas nuclear injection yields expression in the majority of cells (Dean et al. 2005). Nuclear import is therefore the rate-limiting step for most therapeutically relevant, non-dividing targets (Munkonge et al. 2003). In practice, this leads to high doses, variable responses and transient expression: the ZyCoV-D DNA vaccine required three intradermal doses of 2 mg to achieve ~66% efficacy (Khobragade et al. 2022), responses are dose-dependent and variable (Dean 1997), and because plasmids remain episomal their expression is diluted in proliferating cells and may require re-dosing. Electroporation-enhanced DNA vaccines achieve ~100-1000x higher antigen expression than naked DNA, illustrating how strongly outcome tracks with the efficiency of nuclear access (Sardesai and Weiner 2011). A major determinant is the cell cycle: dividing cells transiently lose the envelope and admit even large plasmids passively, whereas quiescent tissues such as neurons and cardiomyocytes depend entirely on active transport, which is one reason intramuscular DNA vaccines are less immunogenic in large animals than in rodents (Liu 2011; Yao et al. 2013).

Efforts to enhance nuclear entry must also be considered in relation to platform safety. Plasmids are designed to remain extrachromosomal, and regulatory expectations are that integration occurs at a rate far below the spontaneous mutation rate (U.S. Food and Drug Administration. 2007; Martinez-Puente et al. 2022); no integration-related adverse events have been attributed to DNA vaccines or therapies, although electroporation modestly increases integration risk and insertional mutagenesis remains a low-probability hazard for plasmid platforms (Ledwith et al. 2000). Unmethylated CpG motifs and cytosolic double-stranded DNA also engage innate sensors such as endosomal TLR9 and cytosolic cGAS-STING; this is advantageous for vaccines but can produce local or systemic inflammation, and delivery methods that raise the cytosolic DNA load amplify the effect. pDNA elicits little anti-vector immunity compared with viral vectors, which is a regulatory advantage (Kay 2011), but repeated dosing, carrier immunogenicity and the tissue injury caused by physical delivery methods all remain regulatory considerations (Sardesai and Weiner 2011; Martinez-Puente et al. 2022).

Regardless of route, DNA must traverse the central channel of the NPC, whose functional aperture for passive diffusion is on the order of ~5–9 nm (Beck and Hurt 2017). Recent structural studies have refined this view substantially. Integrative modelling that combines artificial-intelligence-based structure prediction with in-cell cryo-electron tomography has produced a near-complete model of the human NPC scaffold in multiple conformational states (Mosalaganti et al. 2022), and in cellulo tomography has shown that NPCs are not fixed apertures but dilate and constrict in response to cellular energy state and to osmotic or mechanical stress (Zimmerli et al. 2021). Synthetic DNA origami-based NPC mimics have demonstrated directly that channel width is itself a primary determinant of permeability, with modest changes in pore diameter producing large changes in the size cut-off for transport (Feng et al. 2024). Coarse-grained simulations further indicate that the electrostatic character of karyopherins and of the FG-nucleoporin meshwork, the phenylalanine-glycine-rich segments that form the permeability barrier inside the NPC, is a key ingredient of selective transport, rather than hydrophobic FG interactions alone (Mishra et al. 2025). The barrier faced by a several-kilobase, highly anionic plasmid-carrier complex is therefore both narrower and more electrostatically discriminating than a fixed-diameter pore model suggests. Size and surface charge of the import-competent complex matter as much as the presence of a targeting signal. For classical cargos, importin-α-β docks at the NPC and passes the pore in a stepwise Ran-GTP-mediated cycle, with importin-β able to open and envelop cargo as it negotiates the FG barrier (Stewart 2007). Tat and related CPPs appear to induce transient openings or to use non-canonical FG interactions. Importantly, in non-dividing cells the nucleus remains sealed, so all of these routes depend on active transport. Permeabilized-cell assays and receptor inhibition support this conclusion: the importin-β inhibitor importazole abolished the enhanced expression obtained from NLS-conjugated DNA, confirming a classical importin-β-dependent mechanism in that system (Soderholm et al. 2011).

A second, more recently recognised constraint operates upstream of the NPC. Plasmid DNA that reaches the cytosol is not a naked, freely diffusing substrate. Barrier-to-autointegration factor (BAF), a small dsDNA-bridging protein, binds exogenous DNA in a sequence-independent manner within minutes of endosomal escape and cross-links it into compact clusters that become surrounded by nuclear-envelope-like membranes. Attempts to relieve this constraint have yielded an unexpected result. Co-delivery of the BAF-inactivating kinase VRK1 with TFAM-based DNA nanoparticles reduced BAF clustering as intended, yet lowered rather than raised reporter expression, indicating that BAF association may in fact facilitate the mitotic route of nuclear entry (Greitens et al. 2025). Cytosolic DNA availability is therefore not a monotonic determinant of nuclear delivery, and an intervention that relieves one barrier can antagonise another. This point is directly relevant to the framework developed below. Any evaluation of NLS- or DTS-based strategies must therefore specify the cytoplasmic state of the DNA and not only the presence of a targeting signal.

Non-viral gene delivery systems have achieved substantial progress in cellular uptake and endosomal escape; however, nuclear entry of pDNA remains rate-limiting, especially in non-dividing cells where the nuclear envelope is intact (Dean 1997; Subramanian et al. 1999). Two major strategies have been developed to address this barrier: (i) incorporation of nuclear localisation signal (NLS) peptides that recruit importins, and (ii) DNA targeting sequence (DTS) elements that leverage transcription factor-mediated nuclear import (Dean 1997; Mesika et al. 2001). Evidence increasingly suggests that the effectiveness of these strategies depends strongly on nanocarrier structure and the availability of DNA in the cytosol (Skowicki et al. 2024).

NLS peptides exploit one of several related nuclear import mechanisms (Zanta et al. 1999). Classical importin-α/β targeting delivers the largest effect sizes for plasmid DNA, with clear structural steps and requirements (ATP, Ran, WGA-sensitivity). Importin-β–only and CPP pathways provide alternative, sometimes size-permissive routes that are qualitatively different (e.g. WGA/ATP independence). Engineered and multivalent NLS designs can boost delivery but must be carefully optimised (e.g. one site-specific NLS per plasmid). Quantitatively, a single site-specifically attached NLS was reported to raise expression by up to ~1000-fold, but this value is the upper end of a 10-1000-fold range that varies with cell type, the therapeutically relevant post-mitotic setting lying at its lower end (Zanta et al. 1999); more recent enzymatic peptide-conjugation chemistry reports order-of-10-fold improvements in growth-arrested cells (Mohamedshah et al. 2026).

DTS elements are DNA motifs that bind transcription factors (or cofactors) in cytosol; transcription factor NLSs recruit importins and enable NPC passage of the plasmid–protein complex, especially in non-dividing cells (Dean et al. 1999). Early mechanistic work showed plasmid nuclear import is sequence-specific and requires cytosolic factors plus defined DNA sequences in digitonin-permeabilized cells, supporting the transcription factors-coating piggyback model (Wilson et al. 1999). DTS ‘flavours’ differ by transcription factor availability and regulation. SV40 enhancer functions broadly across many cell types (Young et al. 2003), whereas NF-κB DTS is stimulus-regulated (import increases upon NF-κB activation) (Mesika et al. 2001). Tissue-specific DTS (e.g. SMGA, SP-C) rely on lineage transcription factors and can enforce cell-type selectivity at the level of nuclear import (Miller and Dean 2008).

Here, these strategies are considered within the structural and functional context of peptide-, polymer- and lipid-based nanoassemblies, emphasising how carrier architecture governs nuclear delivery efficiency. The scope of this review is deliberately distinct from recent reviews of nuclear-targeted therapeutics. Goswami et al. surveyed NLS-tagged systems across the full range of nuclear-acting therapeutics, from small molecules and proteins to radionuclides and inorganic nanoparticles, from the standpoint of general nuclear import principles (Goswami et al. 2024). The present review is restricted to plasmid DNA as the cargo and therefore addresses three areas that fall outside that scope: (i) DTS elements and transcription factor-mediated piggyback import, which have no counterpart in protein or small-molecule delivery and which impose an entirely different set of cell-type constraints; (ii) a systematic appraisal of clinical-stage pDNA programmes and of why nuclear targeting mechanisms are almost never declared in them; and (iii) a decision framework, the Nuclear Accessibility Index, that specifies the conditions under which each strategy is worth deploying. Accordingly, the mechanistic sections below have been kept deliberately compact, with primary data consolidated in Tables 1–3, so that emphasis falls on comparative interpretation and on recent work rather than on re-narrating well-established nuclear import biology.

NLS peptide-mediated nuclear targeting

Classical NLS motifs: monopartite and bipartite signals

Classical NLSs (cNLSs) comprise either a single basic cluster (monopartite) or two basic clusters separated by a spacer of approximately 10−12 residues (bipartite), and are recognised by importin-α, which in turn binds importin-β to form the transport-competent complex that docks at the NPC (Figure 1, Table 1). The prototype is the SV40 large T-antigen motif PKKKRKV, frequently written PKKKRRV in delivery constructs; nucleoplasmin (KRPAATKKAGQAKKKK) is the canonical bipartite example, engaging both the major and the minor binding pocket of importin-α. Because the basic side chains occupy defined pockets within the armadillo-repeat domain, single residue substitutions markedly reduce transport activity. This sensitivity provides the most informative negative control available in this field (Robbins et al. 1991; Lange et al. 2007; Pulliam et al. 2009; Chook and Suel 2011). Recent coarse-grained modelling has clarified that the charge of karyopherins and the electrostatic properties of FG-nucleoporins are co-limiting determinants of translocation kinetics, providing a biophysical rationale for the sensitivity of cNLS-mediated import to single-residue substitutions noted above (Mishra et al. 2025).

Mechanistic pathways of NLS-mediated nuclear delivery of plasmid DNA. Various nuclear localisation signal (NLS) platforms facilitate plasmid DNA transport into the nucleus through distinct receptor usage and nuclear trafficking mechanisms. In the classical importin α/β pathway, monopartite or bipartite basic NLS motifs, such as the SV40 T-antigen NLS and nucleoplasmin-derived NLS, are recognised by importin α, which subsequently recruits importin β to mediate docking and translocation through the nuclear pore complex (NPC). In contrast, non-classical pathways utilise alternative nuclear transport receptors, including direct importin-β binding or transportin-mediated import, as exemplified by IGFBP3-derived motifs and the hnRNP A1 M9 domain. Histone-derived NLS systems employ importin-4-dependent trafficking of histone H3/H4 tail motifs and additionally promote chromatin-associated nuclear retention after NPC translocation. Auxiliary trafficking mechanisms, including CPP-mediated intracellular uptake, endosomal escape, microtubule-dependent transport, and NPC docking factors, may further enhance nuclear accumulation of plasmid DNA. Collectively, these pathways demonstrate that NLS-mediated plasmid delivery involves multiple mechanistically distinct strategies that improve nuclear import efficiency and transgene expression.

For plasmid delivery, the monopartite SV40 motif became the historical benchmark. Collas et al. reported that SV40 NLS peptide electrostatically complexed with plasmid DNA raised the proportion of reporter-positive zebrafish embryos from approximately 6% to 70%, with reverse-sequence control peptides inactive (Collas et al. 1996); in the same model, NLS-directed delivery of plasmid to embryo nuclei was rapid and was shown to be energy dependent and sensitive to wheat germ agglutinin (Collas and Aleström 1997). The result is mechanistically decisive but was obtained at a 100:1 NLS:DNA molar ratio, at which peptide constitutes the dominant mass component of the complex; it should therefore be read as proof of principle rather than as a translatable formulation parameter. Zanta et al. subsequently showed that a single covalently attached NLS on a 3.3-kb plasmid increased luciferase expression from ~10-fold in non-dividing primary cells (human monocyte-derived macrophages, rat dorsal root ganglion neurons) to ~1000-fold in rapidly dividing transformed lines, with no simple correlation to tissue origin or cell-cycle status, and with mutation of key lysine residues abolishing the effect (Zanta et al. 1999). The frequently quoted ‘10-1000-fold’ figure therefore describes a range across cell types rather than a single expected gain, and the therapeutically relevant post-mitotic setting sits at its lower end.

Bipartite motifs are considerably less validated as DNA-delivery elements than their mechanistic standing would suggest. Their longer, conformationally constrained architecture makes correct display after conjugation to DNA or to a carrier more demanding, and the primary literature contains few direct plasmid-conjugation studies that use nucleoplasmin-like signals as the principal transport enhancer. They are retained in Table 1 as a structurally defined cNLS class whose receptor behaviour is well understood, and as an underexploited design opportunity, rather than as an established delivery solution (Lange et al. 2007; Pulliam et al. 2009).

The potential of combining classical import routes with cell-penetrating peptides has been demonstrated in a non-mammalian context: a CPP/DNA/SV40-NLS triple nanocomplex recently achieved efficient nuclear gene delivery in Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, with the NLS component directing plasmid DNA toward the nucleus after CPP-mediated membrane translocation, and yielding stable genetic transformation that was not achieved by CPP/DNA binary complexes alone (Sim et al. 2025). Although the import machinery of microalgae differs from that of mammalian cells, this result extends the principle of CPP–NLS cooperativity beyond the systems in which it was originally established and illustrates the modularity of the design logic.

Non-classical and importin-β-direct signals

Several nuclear import signals bypass importin-α entirely. The M9 domain of hnRNP A1 (~38 residues) lacks basic clusters and is recognised by transportin (importin-β2), defining a mechanistically separate route (Pollard et al. 1996; Nakielny et al. 1996; Siomi et al. 1997). The C-terminal basic region of IGFBP3 (KKGFYKKKQCRPSKGRKR, residues 215–232) binds importin-β directly without an importin-α adaptor (Jaques et al. 1997; Schedlich et al. 2000). The importin-β binding (IBB) domain, an intrinsic autoinhibitory module of importin-α itself, engages importin-β with high affinity and has been repurposed as a delivery element: coupling an IBB peptide to plasmid DNA in lipoplexes produced roughly a 20-fold transfection increase, although the peptide also altered lipoplex physicochemical properties, so that the gain reflects a combination of receptor recruitment and altered complex architecture (Carrière et al. 2003; Lott and Cingolani 2011; Pumroy and Cingolani 2015).

This class is relevant for two reasons. It offers redundancy: where importin-α availability or competition limits classical import, alternative karyopherins may remain accessible. It also complicates DNA association. Unlike cationic cNLS peptides, M9 and IBB are not intrinsically nucleic-acid binding, so DNA attachment must be engineered separately through conjugation chemistry or carrier functionalization. In the best-defined example, an IGFBP3-derived peptide was either co-complexed noncovalently with chitosan/pDNA polyplexes or covalently ligated to chitosan while the plasmid remained electrostatically associated with the polymer, yielding an approximately twofold gain that the homologous IGFBP5-derived peptide did not reproduce (Bitoque et al. 2021). Thus, the main attraction of this class is receptor diversification rather than ease of formulation.

Protein-derived polybasic motifs used for carrier functionalization

A further group of motifs, derived from chromatin- and DNA-binding proteins, functions less as a discrete targeting tag than as a dual-purpose element that simultaneously condenses DNA and directs it toward the nucleus. The histone H3 and H4 N-terminal tails (~25−30 residues, lysine/arginine-rich) are imported in mammalian cells principally by importin-4 rather than by importin-α; systematic mapping against seven importins showed that H3 residues 11–27 form the primary binding segment for importin-β, Kapβ2 and importin-4, with dissociation constants of 77 nM and 57 nM for Kapβ2 and importin-5 respectively, comparable to canonical NLS sequences (Baake et al. 2001; Soniat et al. 2016). In delivery systems these tails are incorporated into electrostatic polyplexes, usually together with PEI, so that the histone sequence contributes both nuclear targeting and DNA condensation; H3/PEI hybrids transfected substantially more cells than either component alone, and importin-4 knockdown reduced nuclear localisation and chromatin deposition approximately fourfold, linking nuclear retention to the deposition machinery rather than to pore transit alone (Reilly et al. 2012; Ross and Sullivan 2015). HMGB1 is used in a similar fashion, as full-length protein or as HMGB1A rather than as an isolated peptide, acting as a DNA-binding and DNA-bending bridge within ternary PEI complexes and yielding gains of roughly 2.6−4.9-fold; its two nuclear localisation regions (residues 28−44 and 179−185) are regulated by acetylation and phosphorylation, making HMGB1 a prototype of a conditional rather than a constitutive targeting signal (Bonaldi et al. 2003; Muller et al. 2004; Youn and Shin 2006; Shen et al. 2009).

Virus- and growth-factor-derived motifs are typically grafted onto the carrier rather than onto the plasmid. The influenza B nucleoprotein core motif KRTR (residues 44−47) forms a complex with importin-α/β and, when conjugated to PAMAM dendrimers that condense DNA electrostatically, gave transfection comparable to PEI in HepG2 cells and markedly higher than PEI in A549 cells (Wanitchang et al. 2013; Sherry et al. 2014; Lee et al. 2014). An FGF3-derived 24-mer (RLRRDAGGRGGVYEHLGGAPRRRK) is imported via karyopherin-α1 and, displayed on PAMAM, matched PEI in HEK293 and HeLa cells and exceeded it in NIH3T3 cells (Kiefer et al. 1994; Antoine et al. 1997; Lee et al. 2014). A common feature of this group is that the NLS is frequently attached to the carrier and not to the DNA, so that the resulting gains reflect the behaviour of the functionalized particle as a whole; the corresponding entries in Table 1 record the attachment mode explicitly for this reason.

Engineered, multivalent and screening-derived designs

Recent work has moved from using natural NLSs toward engineering and screening motifs for delivery performance. Multivalency is the simplest lever: tandem cNLS repeats and hybrid uptake/targeting constructs such as TAT-SV40 (YGRKKRRQRRRP-KKKRKV) increase local basic charge density, improve nucleic-acid condensation and combine distinct trafficking functions. Yi et al. reported an approximately 200-fold increase in luciferase expression when an SV40-type NLS was added to a TAT-based carrier; heterodimeric TAT-NLS systems outperformed the corresponding monofunctional peptides, and a lipopeptide TAT-NLS formulation yielded compact, homogeneous and storage-stable DNA nanoparticles (Yi et al. 2012; Doh 2015; Pambudi et al. 2024). Because the CPP component contributes chiefly to uptake and cytosolic access rather than to receptor-mediated import, these systems follow mixed intracellular itineraries and their gains should not be attributed to nuclear targeting alone.

This caveat applies with particular force to CPP-derived motifs themselves. HIV Tat (YGRKKRRQRRR) accumulates in the nucleus and is often grouped with NLSs, but Tat-mediated nuclear entry is relatively insensitive to wheat germ agglutinin, does not display the ATP dependence of classical import, and can carry cargo larger than 90 nm, well beyond what cNLS-driven import accommodates (Vives et al. 1997; Nitin et al. 2009). Arginine-rich viral motifs can nonetheless be recognised directly by importin-β (Palmeri and Malim 1999; Truant and Cullen 1999), which places this class in a mechanistic grey zone between membrane translocation and receptor-mediated transport. Tat-like peptides may therefore enable nuclear entry of unusually large assemblies while offering less mechanistic interpretability than classical cNLS motifs.

A particularly important recent development is the systematic screening of conjugation chemistry alongside motif identity. Mohamedshah et al. used Escherichia coli tRNA guanine transglycosylase to generate DNA oligonucleotide-peptide conjugates that are subsequently ligated into linear gene cassettes (DNA-PepTAG), allowing NLS identity, linker length and NLS valency to be varied independently. In growth-arrested cells transfected by lipofection, NLS-modified cassettes increased nuclear localisation, mRNA transcription and eGFP expression by up to approximately 10-fold, and screening across growth-arrested human cell lines revealed cell-type-specific preferences, with two motifs, a PLSCR1-derived sequence and an extended SV40 variant (extSV40), performing consistently well across cell types and across cassette sizes from 1.3 to 7 kbp (Mohamedshah et al. 2026). Two aspects of this work should be considered separately. The gains are of the order of 10-fold in the difficult growth-arrested setting, consistent with rather than superior to the lower end of the classical range; the advance lies in reproducibility, modularity and dose reduction rather than in effect size. Separately, the study reframes the historical inconsistency of NLS conjugation as a chemistry problem, in line with earlier work showing that a classical NLS can fail entirely when the conjugation site compromises peptide accessibility or plasmid function (Sebestyen et al. 1998; Ciolina et al. 1999; Neves et al. 1999; van der Aa et al. 2005). Conditional designs point in the same direction: a photocleavable protecting group installed on Lys-128 of the SV40 NLS renders the signal inactive until ultraviolet irradiation restores it, enabling spatiotemporal control of import kinetics (Fanara et al. 2000; Watai et al. 2001). Parallel progress in protein-cargo delivery, in which anthrax protective antigen was used to translocate an anti-CRISPR protein for precision genome editing, illustrates how cytosolic entry and nuclear targeting are increasingly designed as a single continuous problem (Vera et al. 2025).

Transcription factor NLSs: the mechanistic bridge to DTS

A final category is mediated not by a free peptide attached to DNA but by a DNA-bound transcription factor that carries its own NLS, exemplified by the NF-κB motif RKKRRQRR. Mesika et al. showed that binding of NLS-bearing NF-κB p50 to plasmid DNA enhanced both cytoplasmic migration toward the nucleus and nuclear entry itself, indicating that karyophilic DNA-binding proteins support pre-nuclear trafficking as well as NPC transit; reported enhancements for this general strategy reach approximately 31-fold, although the value varies with construct, cell type and whether direct protein association or NF-κB-responsive DNA sequences are used (Mesika et al. 2001, 2005, Breuzard et al. 2008; Yao et al. 2013). This has two implications. Presentation matters more than intrinsic signal strength: a DNA-bound transcription factor displays its NLS in an evolved structural context and can additionally engage dynein-mediated transport (Mikenberg et al. 2007; Midoux et al. 2017), which is why piggyback systems frequently outperform simple peptide-DNA electrostatic complexes. And this category is the conceptual bridge to the DTS strategies considered in Section 3: whereas the systems described above supply the karyophilic protein exogenously and rely on its non-covalent association with DNA, DTS-based approaches encode the transcription factor recognition sequence within the plasmid itself, making nuclear targeting an intrinsic and permanent property of the DNA construct rather than a formulation variable. This distinction has direct practical consequences for manufacturing, stability and dose reproducibility, and motivates the separate mechanistic treatment that follows.

DNA nuclear targeting sequences (DTS) and transcription factor–mediated nuclear import of plasmid DNA

DTS elements are cis-acting DNA motifs that recruit NLS-bearing transcription factors in the cytosol, so that the resulting DNA-protein complex is imported by the classical importin machinery (Figure 2, Table 2). Because the targeting function is encoded in the plasmid sequence rather than added chemically, DTS elements are attractive for non-dividing cells, in which the nuclear envelope remains intact. Three functional categories are distinguished throughout this section: universal elements such as the SV40 enhancer (~72 bp), inducible elements such as NF-κB binding sites, and tissue-specific promoter-derived elements such as SMGA, SP-C and T1α. In every case the efficiency of the mechanism is bounded by two carrier-level conditions that recur throughout this review: the plasmid must survive long enough in the cytosol to be bound, and it must remain sufficiently decondensed for transcription factors to reach their motifs.

General mechanism of DTS-mediated nuclear transport. (1) Transcription factors (TFs) in the cytoplasm recognise and bind to DNA targeting sequences (DTS) on the plasmid DNA. (2) The nuclear localisation signals (NLSs) of the bound TFs become exposed and coat the plasmid DNA. (3) Importin α/β recognises the NLSs and associates with the TF–DNA complex to form an import complex. (4) The complex translocates through the nuclear pore complex (NPC). (5) In the nucleus, the plasmid DNA is released from the import machinery and becomes available for transcription by RNA polymerase II. Abbreviations: DTS, DNA targeting sequence; TF, transcription factor; NLS, nuclear localisation signal; NPC, nuclear pore complex; RNA Pol II, RNA polymerase II.

General mechanism of DNA nuclear targeting sequences

DTS elements function indirectly. Unlike NLS peptides, which are recognised directly by transport receptors, DTS motifs recruit cytoplasmic transcription factors that carry intrinsic NLSs; the bound factors effectively coat the plasmid and mediate import through the importin-α/β machinery in a Ran-GTP-dependent ‘piggyback’ manner (Dean et al. 2005; Degiulio et al. 2010). The sequence dependence of this process was established by Wilson and colleagues, who showed that plasmids containing specific enhancer or promoter sequences accumulate in the nuclei of non-dividing cells, whereas otherwise identical plasmids lacking them remain cytoplasmic until cell division occurs (Wilson et al. 1999). Consistent with the model, import of DTS-containing plasmids is blocked by wheat germ agglutinin and requires cytosolic importin-α, importin-β and Ran (Miller and Dean 2008).

A related point, developed further in Section 9, is that DTS activity is evident only where the nuclear envelope is a genuine barrier. In dividing cells the envelope disassembles during mitosis and permits passive access of plasmid DNA to daughter nuclei, so the advantage conferred by a DTS largely disappears in proliferating populations (Young et al. 2003; Degiulio et al. 2010). This single constraint accounts for much of the discrepancy between the large fold-changes reported in quiescent model systems and the modest benefit observed in rapidly dividing cells.

SV40 enhancer (Universal DTS)

The most widely studied DTS is the Simian Virus 40 (SV40) enhancer, a short enhancer sequence of approximately 72 bp that contains binding sites for multiple ubiquitous transcription factors. These include NF-κB, AP-1, AP-2, Oct-1, TEF-1, and other broadly expressed regulatory proteins (Lam and Dean 2010; van Gaal et al. 2011). Because these transcription factors are present in most cell types, the SV40 enhancer functions as a universal DTS capable of promoting nuclear import in a wide range of mammalian cells. The mechanism underlying SV40-mediated nuclear import involves the binding of these transcription factors to the enhancer sequence in the cytoplasm. Each transcription factor contains one or more NLS motifs, allowing the resulting plasmid–protein complex to interact with importin-α/β and undergo active nuclear transport through the nuclear pore complex (Dean et al. 2005).

Young et al. reported that plasmids containing the SV40 enhancer produced markedly increased gene expression after delivery to rat vascular smooth muscle, with luciferase expression approximately 10-fold higher at two days and more than 40-fold higher at three days relative to identical plasmids lacking the DTS; in situ hybridisation confirmed that the increase reflected greater nuclear accumulation of plasmid DNA rather than increased transcriptional activity alone (Young et al. 2003). Comparable behaviour has been observed in cultured fibroblasts, epithelial cells, endothelial cells and muscle cells, in which SV40-DTS plasmids localise efficiently to the nucleus while plasmids lacking the sequence remain largely cytoplasmic until cell division occurs (Miller and Dean 2008).

NF-κB binding sequence DTS

Another important DTS class is based on NF-κB binding motifs, typically consisting of repeated copies of the consensus sequence GGGACTTTCC. NF-κB is a transcription factor composed primarily of p65 (RelA) and p50 subunits and plays a central role in inflammatory signalling pathways. In resting cells, NF-κB remains inactive in the cytoplasm because its nuclear localisation signal is masked by the inhibitory protein IκBα. Upon cellular stimulation by inflammatory signals such as TNF-α or IL-1β, IκBα becomes phosphorylated and subsequently degraded, exposing the NLS of NF-κB and allowing the transcription factor to translocate to the nucleus (Abbott et al. 2000).

Mesika et al. demonstrated that plasmids containing tandem NF-κB binding motifs can function as signal-responsive DTS elements (Mesika et al. 2001). When NF-κB becomes activated, it binds to these motifs on the plasmid in the cytoplasm and transports the DNA into the nucleus via its NLS. In their experiments, plasmids containing NF-κB sites exhibited approximately 12-fold higher nuclear entry and reporter gene expression compared with plasmids lacking the motif.

Increasing the number of NF-κB motifs further improves performance: constructs carrying multiple tandem binding sites enhanced nuclear import efficiency, and FRET imaging of DNA/polymer complexes reported up to a 60-fold increase in nuclear plasmid accumulation relative to control plasmids (Breuzard et al. 2008). This value derives from fluorescence-based quantification of nuclear polyplexes rather than from functional expression data, and the two readouts should not be equated (Breuzard et al. 2008; Lam and Dean 2010). The practical value of this class lies in its stimulus responsiveness. Because NF-κB activation depends on cellular signalling, plasmid nuclear import can be regulated externally with stimuli such as TNF-α or Toll-like receptor agonists, coupling gene delivery to a defined physiological or pathological state (Le Guen et al. 2021). This is also the property that Axis III of the NAI framework is designed to capture.

Le Guen and colleagues subsequently asked how far NF-κB-interacting DTS modules can be optimised rather than adopted as fixed enhancer fragments. Using a matched set of plasmids differing only in their 3NF elements, previously described motifs comprising three κB sites separated by optimised spacers, they found that 3NF insertion can confer modest to substantial advantages for nonviral gene transfer, both in human cell lines in vitro and in vivo, but that the magnitude of the benefit was conditional on the transfection setting and on NF-κB pathway activity (Le Guen et al. 2021). This work supports the view that DTS motifs are amenable to rational optimisation, while also showing that their benefit is context-dependent rather than intrinsic.

CRE (cAMP response element)–based DTS and microtubule-coupled trafficking

CRE motifs (consensus TGACGTCA) are recognised by CREB, which carries an intrinsic NLS. CREB-mediated DTS activity involves directed cytoplasmic trafficking as well as nuclear targeting: microinjection and live-cell imaging have shown that CRE-containing plasmids associate with microtubules and undergo dynein-driven movement toward the microtubule organising centre near the nucleus, whereas plasmids lacking CRE sites exhibit only random Brownian diffusion and fail to reach the nuclear envelope efficiently (Lam and Dean 2010; Badding et al. 2012). This example shows that a DTS can improve two sequential steps, cytoplasmic transport and nuclear import, which complicates the attribution of any single fold-change to nuclear entry alone.

Tissue-specific DTS elements: SMGA, SP-C and T1α promoters

Tissue-specific DTS elements exploit lineage-restricted transcription factors and therefore confer cell selectivity at the level of nuclear import rather than at the level of transcription. The smooth muscle γ-actin (SMGA) promoter contains CArG boxes (consensus CC(A/T)6GG) recognised by serum response factor together with binding sites for Nkx3.1 and Nkx3.2 (Vacik et al. 1999). Plasmids carrying this promoter accumulate in the nuclei of smooth muscle cells but not in fibroblasts or endothelial cells, mutation of either the CArG box or the Nkx sites abolishes import activity, and in rat vasculature SMGA-DTS plasmids produced reporter expression selectively in the smooth muscle layer with minimal expression elsewhere (Vacik et al. 1999; Miller and Dean 2008; Young et al. 2008). Importantly, this shows that DTS elements can restrict nuclear access itself, not merely transcription after nuclear entry.

A similar principle applies in the lung. The surfactant protein C (SP-C) promoter binds TTF-1 (Nkx2.1) and GATA-6, which are strongly expressed in alveolar type II epithelial cells; in mouse lung, SP-C-containing plasmids produced approximately threefold more reporter-positive alveolar cells than control plasmids (Degiulio et al. 2010). The T1α (podoplanin) promoter, active in alveolar type I cells, contains HNF3/Foxa and TTF-1 sites, and disruption of either abolishes nuclear transport activity, indicating that coordinated occupancy rather than any single factor drives import (Gottfried et al. 2016). Across all three elements the same design constraint recurs: selectivity is purchased at the cost of absolute efficiency, because import can occur only where the required factors are expressed. This trade-off is formalised as Axis III of the NAI framework in Section 9.

Comparative summary of DTS classes

The DTS elements summarised in Table 2 share a common principle: sequence-specific recruitment of NLS-bearing transcription factors to plasmid DNA (Wilson et al. 1999). They differ mainly in transcription-factor distribution and regulation. The SV40 enhancer is broadly active across multiple mammalian cell types (Young et al. 2003). NF-κB motifs are stimulus-responsive, and optimised 3NF sequences show that this class can be rationally engineered (Mesika et al. 2001; Le Guen et al. 2021). CRE elements couple cytoplasmic trafficking with nuclear import (Badding et al. 2012). SMGA, SP-C and T1α promoter-derived DTS elements are lineage-restricted (Miller and Dean 2008; Degiulio et al. 2010; Gottfried et al. 2016).

In non-dividing cells, these elements can increase nuclear plasmid accumulation and gene expression by an order of magnitude or more, depending on cell type, motif design and carrier context (Mesika et al. 2001; Young et al. 2003). Their common limitation is equally systematic: DTS activity requires transcription-factor availability, cytosolic plasmid survival and sufficient carrier unpacking to expose the relevant binding sites (Bai et al. 2017; Skowicki et al. 2024). Recent work on engineered DTS modules and nuclear-targeted nanoassemblies reinforces this interpretation by showing that DTS function must be considered together with carrier architecture and cytosolic DNA accessibility, rather than treated as an isolated sequence effect (Le Guen et al. 2021; Skowicki et al. 2024).

Chemical and structural coupling strategies

Figure 3 summarises the four principal approaches for associating a targeting signal with plasmid DNA. Although many of the studies that established these strategies are relatively old, they remain useful for identifying several practical limitations, including poor control of stoichiometry, linkage stability, steric accessibility of the targeting signal, and preservation of plasmid function. More recent studies have focused on improving these parameters, particularly the position, linker length, and number of NLS peptides attached to DNA (Mohamedshah et al. 2026). The following sections therefore summarise each coupling strategy briefly, emphasising its advantages and limitations for current carrier design.

Chemical strategies for DNA and peptide binding.

Electrostatic mixing/ionic binding

Electrostatic mixing provides a simple method for associating cationic NLS peptides with the negatively charged DNA backbone and does not require additional conjugation chemistry. However, the number and position of bound peptides cannot be precisely controlled, and the complexes may partially dissociate during intracellular trafficking. In addition, highly cationic peptides can alter DNA condensation and stability. Therefore, increased reporter expression may result not only from enhanced nuclear import but also from improved DNA protection or intracellular stability. Electrostatic association is useful for initial screening, but direct measurement of nuclear DNA accumulation is needed to confirm NLS-mediated nuclear import (Collas et al. 1996; Hebert 2003; Dean et al. 2005).

Covalent conjugation

Covalent conjugation provides a more stable association between NLS peptides and DNA and allows better control of conjugate structure than electrostatic complexation. However, earlier studies showed inconsistent results. Covalently attached NLS peptides retained importin-α binding and enhanced nuclear accumulation in some experimental systems, whereas other studies showed little or no improvement after delivery into intact cells (Sebestyen et al. 1998; Ciolina et al. 1999; Neves et al. 1999; van der Aa et al. 2005). These differences suggest that the conjugation site and accessibility of the peptide are important determinants of activity.

More recently, Mohamedshah et al. developed the DNA-PepTAG approach, which allows NLS identity, linker length and peptide number to be varied independently (Mohamedshah et al. 2026). In growth-arrested cells, optimised NLS-modified gene cassettes showed increased nuclear localisation and gene expression, whereas some conjugation configurations were less effective. These results indicate that covalent attachment alone is not sufficient and that the way in which the NLS is presented on DNA is important for efficient nuclear targeting.

Bridging strategy (streptavidin–biotin)

Bridging strategies link DNA to nuclear transport machinery indirectly through a high-affinity pair, most commonly biotin-streptavidin. Nagasaki et al. combined biotinylated plasmid DNA with a streptavidin-importin-β fusion, recruiting a transport factor to DNA without direct peptide-DNA synthesis, and reported increased nuclear localisation and reporter expression (Nagasaki et al. 2005). The approach is highly modular, but the protein adaptors are large, which increases complex size, raises immunogenicity and manufacturability concerns, and complicates movement through the crowded cytoplasm; many proof-of-concept studies also relied on microinjection, which bypasses membrane translocation and endosomal escape. Its elegance therefore exceeds its current translational relevance (Hebert 2003; Dean et al. 2005).

DNA peptide clamp

The DNA peptide clamp combines sequence-selective recognition with stable non-covalent association. NLS peptides are tethered through peptide nucleic acid (PNA) clamps or other triplex-forming modules that bind the major groove without covalent modification of the DNA backbone. Brändén et al. used PNA-NLS clamps to enhance nuclear import of plasmids in digitonin-permeabilized cells, recruiting importin-α/β and increasing nuclear accumulation of reporter DNA (Branden et al. 1999), and the design permits spatially defined peptide positioning while preserving peptide orientation. Its adoption is limited by three constraints: high-affinity triplex formation requires homopurine-rich target sites (Egholm et al. 1995), PNA synthesis and purification are specialised and costly (Nandhini et al. 2023), and bulky clamps may impair endosomal release or interfere with transcription factor binding if positioned near regulatory elements (Vickers et al. 1995). Nevertheless, the clamp remains attractive where covalent modification is unacceptable or sequence-guided modularity is required.

Nanoassembly platforms and structural determinants of nuclear delivery

Modern non-viral delivery systems form complex nanoassemblies, and their structure strongly affects intracellular trafficking and nuclear accessibility (Figure 4, Table 3). In this section, we focus mainly on structural properties that influence NLS- or DTS-mediated nuclear delivery, including DNA condensation and unpacking, accessibility of the targeting motif, and cytosolic release of pDNA. Peptide-, polymer-, and lipid-based carriers are discussed with emphasis on how these properties affect nuclear delivery rather than on their general transfection mechanisms.

Peptide-based nanoassemblies

Peptide carriers are supramolecular assemblies rather than simple NLS conjugates, and fall into two principal architectures, peptiplexes and peptidic micelles, which differ in how they reconcile condensation with signal accessibility (Skowicki et al. 2024). The mechanisms of NLS recognition are not repeated here; this section addresses only the structural determinants that modulate them.

Peptiplexes

Peptiplexes are electrostatically assembled complexes between cationic peptides and DNA that provide condensation, nuclease protection and tunable surface charge and size (Saccardo et al. 2009). The N/P ratio is the dominant design variable: increasing N/P yields smaller, more compact particles with higher surface charge, improving uptake but increasing cytotoxicity and, critically, reducing the accessibility of NLS motifs and DTS sites to their cognate partners. Hydrophobic residues contribute to stabilisation alongside electrostatics, so peptiplex assembly is governed by both forces. That NLS incorporation enhances transfection was established in Section 2. The relevant issue here is different: the same electrostatic parameter that determines particle quality also determines whether the incorporated signal can be read at all. Very high peptide:DNA ratios used in early proof-of-concept work (≥100:1) illustrate this tension in its extreme form and, as noted in Section 2.1, represent a mechanistic benchmark rather than a clinically translatable formulation (Collas et al. 1996; Zanta et al. 1999).

Advanced peptide systems integrate multiple functional domains: Targeting ligands (e.g. REDV), Cell-penetrating peptides (e.g. TAT), Nuclear localisation signals (NLS), Endosomal escape motifs (e.g. histidine-rich domains). These modular systems enable coordinated intracellular trafficking, where each domain contributes to overcoming a specific biological barrier (Li et al. 2018).

Subramanian et al. combined a nonclassical transport signal (hnRNP A1 M9) with a cationic scaffold (ScT) to form DNA-binding constructs via electrostatic interaction, which improves the nuclear step in non-dividing cells; they reported 83% transfection and a 63-fold expression increase in confluent endothelium after lipofection (Subramanian et al. 1999). Mechanistically, these peptide scaffolds simultaneously address DNA binding/condensation and nuclear trafficking, making them powerful but also prone to confounding (e.g. a ‘nuclear targeting’ label may partly reflect improved endosomal escape or cytosolic stability).

Peptidic micelles

Amphiphilic peptides self-assemble into micellar structures that can encapsulate nucleic acids and drugs (Habibi et al. 2016), offering structural versatility, multi-compartment targeting (for example nucleus and mitochondria) and stimuli-responsive behaviour such as pH-dependent charge reversal. Tarvirdipour et al. developed a self-assembling amphiphilic peptide platform forming multicompartment micellar nanoparticles that entrap DNA cargoes up to 100 nucleotides while maintaining structural stability (Tarvirdipour et al. 2020). The same platform was subsequently made nuclear-competent by appending an N-terminal KRKR nuclear localisation signal to the amphiphilic peptide, so that a single self-assembly step yields NLS-functionalized multicompartment micelles with the oligonucleotide held between compartments; binding assays against FG-nucleoporins and karyopherins confirmed that nuclear entry proceeded through karyopherin recognition rather than through non-specific membrane translocation, and anti-BCL2 antisense oligonucleotides delivered in this way produced up to 86% target knockdown in MCF-7 cells (Tarvirdipour et al. 2022). Because the cargo was an oligonucleotide rather than a plasmid, this result establishes the assembly principle rather than plasmid-scale capacity, but it is instructive that adding the signal to the peptide monomer, rather than to the cargo, was sufficient. McCarthy et al. described RALA, a bio-inspired amphipathic peptide that condenses plasmid DNA into sub-100 nm particles and gains α-helicity at acidic pH to drive endosomal disruption, achieving high transfection with minimal cytotoxicity in vitro and after systemic administration (McCarthy et al. 2014). More recent work extends the modular principle: a cell-penetrating-peptide-based triple nanocomplex that combines uptake, condensation and nuclear targeting functions in a single assembly achieved efficient nuclear gene delivery in a system where the nuclear envelope and cell wall are both limiting, underlining that the productive unit is the assembly rather than any single peptide domain (Sim et al. 2025).

Pros/cons and application of peptide carrier

Peptide carriers are highly engineerable and can incorporate endosomal escape motifs and receptor ligands, but face stability and manufacturing-consistency constraints; hybrid peptide-lipid systems are increasingly used to combine efficient uptake with programmable intracellular routing. For DTS-containing plasmids the trade-off is explicit: strong peptide condensation prolongs cytosolic residence and thereby increases the opportunity for transcription factor binding, whereas over-condensation occludes the DTS motifs themselves and disables the piggyback mechanism.

This balance between condensation and NLS/DTS accessibility, and its effect on nuclear delivery efficiency, is represented by Axis II of the NAI framework (Section 9).

Polymer-based nanoassemblies

Polymer carriers are used principally as polyplexes and as polymeric micelles or polymersomes. The two architectures differ in how they reconcile condensation with signal display, and in both cases nuclear targeting is achieved by NLS conjugation or histone-mimicking strategies.

Polyplexes: electrostatic assembly and cell-cycle dependence

Polyplexes are formed through the direct electrostatic condensation of anionic nucleic acids with cationic polymers, such as polyethylenimine (PEI), poly-L-lysine (PLL), or chitosan. The primary advantage of these complexes lies in their high DNA-packing efficiency and, in the case of PEI, the ‘proton sponge effect’ which facilitates endosomal escape. However, the nuclear delivery efficiency of standard polyplexes is heavily constrained by their reliance on passive entry during mitosis. As reported by Brunner et al., the gene transfer efficiency of PEI-based polyplexes is significantly higher in dividing cells, whereas it remains marginal in growth-arrested populations due to the intact nuclear envelope acting as a physical barrier (Brunner et al. 2000; Grosse et al. 2006).

To overcome this cell-cycle dependence, recent strategies have focused on the direct modification of the polyplex scaffold with nuclear-targeting motifs. These include the covalent or electrostatic integration of classical nuclear localisation signals (NLS) (Bitoque et al. 2021) or viral core-derived peptides, such as the adenovirus-derived μ (mu) peptide, which has been shown to enhance nuclear import even in non-dividing cells (Murray et al. 2001). Chitosan systems provide a concrete NLS ‘add-on’ example with quantified gain: integrating an IGFBP-3–derived NLS peptide into chitosan polyplexes (co-complexation or covalent ligation) produced an approximately 2× transfection increase, whereas an IGFBP-5–derived peptide did not, illustrating sequence specificity and carrier-context dependence (Bitoque et al. 2021). Nevertheless, the performance of modified polyplexes is often limited by a trade-off between binding stability and cargo unpacking; excessively strong electrostatic interactions can prevent the release of the DNA in a form that is accessible to the nuclear import machinery (van der Aa et al. 2005).

Two reporting considerations follow. Polyplex studies should specify particle size, zeta potential and N/P ratio, because these parameters often dominate uptake and endosomal escape and can masquerade as nuclear targeting effects (Skowicki et al. 2024). DTS insertion can be particularly valuable in polymeric systems for non-dividing targets, because it supplies an active import programme after cytosolic release, but only if unpacking kinetics permit transcription factor binding (Dean et al. 1999).

Polymeric micelles and polymersomes: structural engineering for programmed transport

Polymeric micelles and polymersomes are self-assembled structures formed from amphiphilic block copolymers. Relative to polyplexes they offer superior colloidal stability and protection against enzymatic degradation, and, more important for nuclear delivery, a well-defined corona that serves as a programmable platform for the spatial display of targeting ligands, enabling multivalent interactions with nuclear transport receptors (Torchilin 2007; Bae and Kataoka 2009).

A key distinction of these structured carriers is their ability to facilitate active nuclear transport and intra-nuclear retention through advanced surface engineering. For instance, micellar systems decorated with histone-mimicking motifs, such as the H3 tail peptide, do not merely traverse the nuclear pore complex but actively exploit importin-4-mediated pathways to promote chromatin deposition and sustained gene expression (Ross and Sullivan 2015). The most direct evidence that an intact synthetic vesicle can be moved by the canonical import machinery comes from polymersomes carrying a bipartite nucleoplasmin NLS coupled by thiol–ene click chemistry to a PMOXA–PDMS–PMOXA membrane: karyopherins were required to recognise, bind and escort the particles through the NPC, Ran-GTP promoted their release into the nucleoplasm, and transmission electron microscopy resolved individual carriers in transit within pores and subsequently inside the nucleus (Zelmer et al. 2020). This leads to two considerations for carrier design. First, receptor-mediated transport can carry an assembled vesicle across the pore, so the operative size limit for an import-competent particle lies well above the passive diffusion limit discussed in Section 1. Second, this is one of the few systems in which a bipartite nucleoplasmin signal has been validated as a delivery element, and it is displayed on the carrier surface rather than conjugated to the nucleic acid, precisely the configuration identified in Section 2.1 as structurally well understood but underexploited. Unpacking kinetics can additionally be tuned through pH-responsive or redox-sensitive linkages in the block copolymer backbone, so that cargo is shielded in transit and released in the perinuclear environment (Murray et al. 2001; Bitoque et al. 2021).

Lipid-based nanoassemblies

Lipid-based carriers, including lipoplexes, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), and solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) are highly efficient for cellular uptake but often limited by endosomal entrapment and inefficient cytosolic release. Consequently, NLS-mediated nuclear targeting becomes effective only when DNA is released in an import-competent form (Subramanian et al. 1999).

Escriou et al. provided a ‘critical assessment’ framework for cationic lipid gene transfer, explicitly separating barriers: plasma membrane entry, lipoplex disassembly, and nuclear import, and demonstrating that cell status (growth arrest vs synchronised cycling) substantially affects outcomes (Escriou et al. 2001). To overcome limitations of lipid-based carriers, several strategies have been developed: NLS-intercalator conjugates (e.g. NLS-PEG-acridine) and Steroid-mediated nuclear targeting (e.g. dexamethasone via glucocorticoid receptor transport). These approaches enhance nuclear delivery by coupling DNA protection, targeting, and transport mechanisms.

Zhang et al. designed PEG–tris(acridine) intercalators bearing short NLS peptides to protect DNA and enhance nuclear delivery; with Lipofectamine 2000 or Exgen 500, they reported 15–16-fold expression improvement for lipoplexes and 10–11-fold for polyplexes in difficult-to-transfect BBB endothelial cells under optimised ratios (Zhang et al. 2009). Mechanistically, the system couples: (i) DNA protection via intercalation and PEG shielding, (ii) carrier condensation compatible with standard transfection agents, and (iii) NLS presentation at a controlled effective density (reported as ~5–6 NLS peptides per DNA molecule).

Wang et al. describe dexamethasone-conjugated lipid SLNs with folate targeting and NLS components to increase nuclear uptake of genetic materials, representing a strategy that leverages nuclear translocation of receptor–ligand complexes (a ‘ligand-directed nuclear routing’ motif) (Wang et al. 2012). In this study, transferrin-PEG-PE + Dexamethasone-lipid SLNs showed higher transfection than nonmodified controls in vitro/in vivo, though studies frequently report ‘higher’ rather than a standardised fold-change, which complicates cross-study effect-size synthesis.

Recent Biomaterials work describes an ‘all-in-one’ lipid–polymer hybrid nanoparticle (FLNP) system specifically engineered for the gene therapy of ischaemic diseases, reflecting a broader shift toward hybrid architectures that combine polymer stability with lipid-mediated uptake and escape tuning (Chen et al. 2025). These nanocarriers integrate multiple intracellular trafficking functions, facilitating efficient gene transfection through a synergistic mechanism that involves membrane fusion-mediated cellular entry and robust lysosome avoidance. By co-delivering the hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) gene to stimulate angiogenesis and the catalase (CAT) gene to mitigate oxidative stress, this platform provides a comprehensive ‘all-in-one’ therapeutic approach to the complex pathological microenvironment of ischaemic tissues.

Lipid systems are tunable and clinically validated for RNA delivery, but DNA nuclear delivery requires either efficient endosomal escape combined with NPC-compatible trafficking, or reliance on mitosis, which is often unavailable in the target tissue. DTS modules can add value in lipid systems precisely because lipoplexes underperform in non-dividing cells; their utility nevertheless remains contingent on DNA release and transcription factor recruitment after endosomal escape.

Another informative carrier class uses DNA-binding proteins themselves as the assembly scaffold. TFAM-based nanoparticles condense plasmid DNA through a natural high-mobility-group protein, and recent work combining them with the BAF-inactivating kinase VRK1 shows how carrier design interacts with the cytoplasmic barriers described in Section 1: reducing BAF-mediated DNA clustering did not improve, and in fact reduced, reporter expression (Greitens et al. 2025). For this review, the result has a broader implication for carrier design. Increasing the amount of free, accessible cytosolic DNA is not by itself sufficient, and may be counterproductive if it removes a protein interaction that contributes to nuclear entry. Carrier optimization and nuclear targeting must therefore be evaluated jointly rather than as independent additive variables.

Experimental methods for evaluating nuclear delivery

The experimental approaches listed in Figure 5 are essential for determining whether an NLS or a DTS genuinely enhances nuclear delivery rather than merely improving cellular uptake, DNA condensation or post-mitotic expression (Figure 5). Nuclear/cytoplasmic fractionation followed by qPCR provides the most direct bulk biochemical readout of nuclear DNA accumulation and has been widely used in plasmid-trafficking studies (Cohen et al. 2009). Fluorescence imaging, including FISH, FRET and confocal microscopy, adds spatial information and can distinguish nuclear-envelope association from true intranuclear localisation (Lechardeur et al. 1999; Breuzard et al. 2008). Reporter gene assays such as luciferase or GFP remain useful functional readouts but are indirect, because higher expression can also arise from altered DNA stability or intracellular processing (Paguio et al. 2010). Finally, modular combinatorial variation of DNA-peptide conjugates allows candidate motifs to be compared under matched conditions: in the DNA-PepTAG workflow, NLS identity, linker length and valency are varied independently, and the resulting conjugates are ranked by paired measurement of nuclear localisation and reporter expression in growth-arrested cells, in which mitotic nuclear-envelope breakdown is minimised so that active import is obligatorily tested. Ranking therefore rests on these paired functional readouts rather than on sequencing of barcoded libraries, and the method compares a defined panel of conjugates rather than a library-scale pool (Mohamedshah et al. 2026).

Experimental evaluation methods for assessing nuclear delivery of DNA via NLS or DTS. (1) Nuclear/cytoplasmic fractionation followed by qPCR quantitatively measures the amount of DNA that accumulates in the nucleus. (2) Fluorescence imaging approaches, including FISH, FRET and confocal microscopy, visualise intracellular localisation of DNA–peptide complexes and distinguish true intranuclear localisation from mere nuclear-envelope association. (3) Reporter gene assays, using luciferase or GFP reporters, evaluate functional nuclear delivery by quantifying reporter activity, although they provide an indirect readout. (4) Modular combinatorial variation of DNA–peptide conjugates, in which NLS identity, linker length and NLS valency are varied independently and a defined panel of candidates is ranked by paired measurement of nuclear localisation and reporter expression in growth-arrested cells. Combining at least one direct measure of nuclear DNA accumulation with one functional readout is recommended to draw robust conclusions about genuine nuclear transport.

For this reason, stronger studies in this field generally combine at least two orthogonal readouts: one that directly measures nuclear DNA abundance and another that measures biological output. This is especially important for highly cationic peptides, because these molecules can alter condensation, endosomal escape, or intracellular retention independently of authentic NPC-mediated import. Accordingly, the evaluation methods listed in Figure 5 should be read not merely as technical options, but as safeguards against overinterpreting expression enhancement as proof of nuclear transport (Ciolina et al. 1999; Breuzard et al. 2008; Mohamedshah et al. 2026).

Mechanistic and engineering differences

NLS strategies recruit importin receptors directly to the DNA cargo, whereas DTS elements exploit the intrinsic NLS motifs of DNA-bound transcription factors through a piggyback mechanism. The functional consequence is that NLS efficiency is governed principally by peptide accessibility within the carrier, whereas DTS efficiency is governed principally by transcription factor availability in the target cell. These are different failure modes, and they are addressed by different interventions: reformulation in the first case, and cell-type selection or pathway stimulation in the second.

These mechanistic differences also lead to distinct engineering requirements. NLS-based systems require peptide synthesis and a method for coupling the peptide to DNA or to the carrier, including electrostatic complexation, covalent conjugation, bridging strategies or carrier-surface functionalization (Sebestyen et al. 1998). These approaches offer flexibility but introduce additional variables such as stoichiometry, linker length, conjugation site, peptide orientation and manufacturing consistency (van der Aa et al. 2005).

DTS-based systems require no additional peptide component. The targeting element is inserted into the plasmid sequence and replicated during plasmid manufacture (Dean et al. 1999). This is a major advantage for simplicity and quality control. However, DTS design is less modular than peptide addition because motif function depends on the biological state of the target cell and may overlap with promoter or enhancer functions (van Gaal et al. 2011).

Recent studies support this mechanistic distinction at the formulation level. Mohamedshah et al. showed that NLS identity, linker length and valency strongly influence expression from DNA–peptide conjugates in growth-arrested cells, demonstrating that NLS design is a presentation problem as much as a motif-selection problem (Mohamedshah et al. 2026). In contrast, Le Guen et al. showed that optimised NF-κB-interacting DTS modules can improve nonviral gene transfer, emphasising transcription-factor context as a central design variable for DTS strategies (Le Guen et al. 2021).

Cell-type specificity and its limits

A major advantage of DTS-based strategies is the potential for cell-type or stimulus-dependent specificity. Because DTS activity depends on transcription-factor binding, nuclear import can be restricted to cells expressing the cognate factors (Miller and Dean 2009). Tissue-specific DTS elements such as SMGA, SP-C and T1α promoter-derived sequences can therefore confer selectivity at the level of nuclear entry, not merely at the level of transcription after nuclear entry (Miller and Dean 2008; Degiulio et al. 2010; Gottfried et al. 2016).

Inducible DTS elements add a second layer of control. NF-κB-binding motifs become active when NF-κB signalling is stimulated, linking nuclear import to inflammatory or immune activation states (Mesika et al. 2001). The 2021 3NF study supports the feasibility of engineering optimised transcription-factor-interacting DTS modules rather than relying only on naturally occurring enhancer fragments (Le Guen et al. 2021).

The same dependency also limits these advantages. Because the mechanism requires transcription factor binding, import efficiency varies with the availability and activation state of the relevant factors, and cells lacking them, or lacking the upstream signal, show minimal DTS-mediated import (Lam and Dean 2010). This introduces variability across cell types and physiological conditions. More fundamentally, the cell-cycle dependence established in Section 3 remains the principal constraint on translational utility: in proliferating populations the nuclear envelope is not a persistent barrier, and the selective advantage of DTS-mediated import largely disappears. Section 9 formalises this constraint as an explicitly excluded region of the design space rather than as a graded penalty.

NLS strategies generally provide less intrinsic cell-type specificity because importins are broadly expressed. Their strength lies instead in general applicability: if the carrier exposes the peptide properly and the target cell maintains a functional importin/NPC system, an NLS can in principle operate across many cell types (Lange et al. 2007). However, this breadth also means that NLS addition alone does not restrict expression to a desired lineage unless it is combined with cell-specific promoters, receptor-targeted carriers or tissue-restricted DTS elements (Senzer et al. 2013; Lee et al. 2014).

Clinical translation and regulatory landscape

Nuclear targeting strategies such as NLS peptides and DTS elements have been investigated extensively in preclinical systems, yet numerous pDNA platforms have entered clinical development without invoking them (Figure 6 for the development timeline; Table 5 for sponsors, indications and trial identifiers). The nuclear delivery strategies actually used in the clinic are rarely described in terms of NLS or DTS mechanisms; they rely instead on physical permeabilization such as electroporation (Daud et al. 2008), receptor-targeted endocytosis such as TfR-targeted immunoliposomes (Senzer et al. 2013), polymer-mediated DNA condensation and intracellular trafficking such as PEI complexation (Sidi et al. 2008), and local intratumoral, intramuscular or intravesical high-dose and repeated administration (Belch et al. 2011; Kessler et al. 2021). FDA approval status and regulatory considerations

As of August 10, 2026, the FDA list of approved cellular and gene therapy products did not include a nonviral therapeutic product based on directly administered plasmid DNA for therapeutic gene expression (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2026). FDA guidance for plasmid DNA vaccines emphasises preclinical testing, product characterisation and manufacturing considerations for plasmid DNA products (U.S. Food and Drug Administration. 2007). Although this guidance focuses on infectious disease vaccines rather than non-infectious therapeutic pDNA products, it remains relevant because it illustrates the regulatory emphasis on quality, consistency, safety and immunogenicity (U.S. Food and Drug Administration. 2007).

These requirements are particularly challenging for pDNA expression systems because transgene expression depends on multiple sequential barriers: tissue distribution, cellular uptake, endosomal escape, cytosolic survival, nuclear entry and transcriptional competence (Bai et al. 2017). Within this landscape, NLS/DTS mechanisms remain mostly preclinical optimization strategies. Clinical pDNA programmes have instead favoured interventions that can be standardised procedurally, such as injection route, electroporation parameters, dose schedule, receptor targeting and local exposure.

This position may change in the near term. Detalimogene voraplasmid (formerly EG-70) is an intravesical, non-viral product in which plasmid DNA is formulated on an oligochitosan-based delivery platform, and it is currently the most advanced therapeutic pDNA candidate in late-stage clinical development for high-risk, BCG-unresponsive non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer with carcinoma in situ (Abou Chakra et al. 2026). It holds expedited-development designations from the FDA, and the sponsor has stated that initiation of a Biologics License Application filing is planned for the second half of 2026; at the time of writing no marketing application had been submitted. It is relevant here for two reasons. First, if approved it would establish the first regulatory precedent for a directly administered therapeutic pDNA product. Second, and consistent with the pattern developed below, its declared mechanism rests on local intravesical exposure, carrier chemistry and transgene design rather than on any explicit NLS- or DTS-mediated nuclear import module, so it does not alter the conclusion that active nuclear targeting remains absent from clinical-stage pDNA development. Because it remained investigational at the time of writing, it is discussed here as a regulatory outlook and is not included among the six programmes with reported clinical outcomes that are analysed in Table 5 and Figure 6.

Tavokinogene telseplasmid (IL-12 pDNA) + electroporation

Tavokinogene telseplasmid, also known as TAVO-EP, is a clinically advanced example of physical pDNA delivery. The platform involves intratumoral injection of IL-12-encoding plasmid DNA followed by electroporation, which transiently increases plasma-membrane permeability and enhances intracellular DNA availability (Daud et al. 2008). Clinical protocols specify physical parameters such as pulse duration, electric field strength and electrode configuration, making electroporation a controllable procedural delivery variable (Daud et al. 2008).

In melanoma studies, intratumoral IL-12 plasmid electroporation has been associated with local and systemic immune effects. Algazi et al. reported that intratumoral delivery of tavokinogene telseplasmid yielded systemic immune responses in metastatic melanoma patients (Algazi et al. 2020). A related phase II study combining IL-12 plasmid transfection with PD-1 blockade showed immune activity in immunologically quiescent melanoma (Algazi et al. 2020).

TAVO-EP is therefore best interpreted as a physical pDNA delivery platform rather than as an active NLS/DTS nuclear-targeting strategy. Its mechanism depends primarily on intratumoral administration and electroporation-mediated DNA uptake, with nuclear entry inferred from IL-12 expression and downstream immune effects. The KEYNOTE-695 study combining TAVO-EP with pembrolizumab, registered as NCT03132675, reported a confirmed objective response rate of 10.2% by blinded independent central review, below its pre-specified threshold (Ma et al. 2026; ClinicalTrials.gov 2026a). This dataset represents a distinct trial population from the 2020 melanoma studies and is therefore considered separately here (Algazi et al. 2020a, 2020b; Ma et al. 2026; ClinicalTrials.gov 2026a).

SGT-53 (TP53 pDNA targeted immunoliposome)

SGT-53 is a systemically administered pDNA nanocomplex in which a wild-type TP53 plasmid is encapsulated in a cationic liposome and decorated with an anti-transferrin receptor single-chain antibody fragment (Senzer et al. 2013). The design aims to exploit transferrin receptor overexpression on tumour cells to enhance receptor-mediated uptake (Senzer et al. 2013).

In the first-in-human Phase I study, exogenous p53 DNA was detected by PCR in metastatic tumour tissues but not in normal skin, supporting tumour-targeted delivery (Senzer et al. 2013). This finding provides important clinical evidence for systemic receptor-targeted pDNA delivery. However, SGT-53 should not be described as a defined active nuclear-import strategy. Its declared design emphasises tumour targeting and cellular uptake, not NLS- or DTS-mediated nuclear entry.

SGT-53 has continued clinical development in combination regimens. For example, NCT02340117 evaluates SGT-53 plus gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel for metastatic pancreatic cancer (ClinicalTrials.gov 2026b). This example underscores that clinical translation has focused on receptor-mediated tumour targeting and combination therapy rather than explicit nuclear import engineering. Thus, SGT-53 provides clinical evidence for systemic receptor-targeted pDNA tumour delivery, but not for an engineered NLS- or DTS-mediated nuclear import mechanism.

Engensis (VM202; HGF pDNA intramuscular administration)

Engensis, also known as VM202, is a nonviral plasmid DNA designed to express two isoforms of hepatocyte growth factor (Kessler et al. 2021). It is administered by intramuscular injection and has been evaluated for painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy (Kessler et al. 2021).

No explicit active nuclear-targeting module, such as an NLS peptide or DTS element, is described as the central mechanism for VM202. Instead, the clinical strategy relies on local plasmid administration, repeated dosing and transient local HGF expression (Kessler et al. 2021). The Phase III study reported that the primary endpoint was not met in the main 9-month analysis, whereas an extension cohort showed statistically significant pain reduction and no major safety concerns (Kessler et al. 2021).

VM202 therefore illustrates a recurring translational challenge for pDNA therapy: favourable tolerability and plausible biological activity may coexist with difficulty in achieving consistent primary clinical endpoints. For nuclear targeting, the lesson is that improving nuclear entry may be necessary but is not sufficient; therapeutic gene choice, disease biology, expression duration and endpoint selection must also align.

NV1FGF (FGF-1 pDNA; CLI)

NV1FGF, also known as riferminogene pecaplasmid, is a nonviral FGF-1-expressing plasmid developed for therapeutic angiogenesis in critical limb ischaemia (Belch et al. 2011). The strategy involved repeated intramuscular injections into ischaemic limb muscles, with no explicit NLS or DTS mechanism (Belch et al. 2011).

The Phase III TAMARIS trial enroled 525 patients across 171 sites in 30 countries and showed no significant reduction in major amputation or death at one year (Belch et al. 2011). Despite the absence of major safety concerns, the trial failed to meet its efficacy objective (Belch et al. 2011). This case therefore provides an important negative translational example. It demonstrates that local delivery of an angiogenic pDNA can be safe and biologically plausible, yet still fail to meet hard clinical endpoints.

NV1FGF reinforces the need to distinguish nuclear expression from therapeutic sufficiency. Even if nuclear access occurs, a single-gene pDNA strategy may be inadequate when disease pathophysiology is multifactorial, chronic and spatially heterogeneous.

Allovectin-7 (HLA-B7/β2m pDNA-lipid complex)

Allovectin-7, or velimogene aliplasmid, consisted of plasmids encoding HLA-B7 and β2-microglobulin complexed with cationic lipids and injected intratumorally (Gonzalez et al. 2006). The therapeutic concept was to increase tumour immunogenicity by inducing local expression of an allogeneic MHC molecule (Gonzalez et al. 2006).

In Phase II (e.g. weekly injection for 6 weeks), objective response rate (ORR) of 9.1% and low toxicity were reported, and in high-dose Phase II, ORR of 11.8% and absence of Grade 3/4 adverse events were reported (Gonzalez et al. 2006). The subsequent Phase III trial in chemotherapy-naive metastatic melanoma (NCT00395070) did not meet its primary (response rate) or secondary (overall survival) endpoints, and the programme was discontinued (ClinicalTrials.gov 2026c). This outcome warrants an explicit caveat: it was reported only in company communications and has not been published in a peer-reviewed journal, nor are outcome data available from the trial registry. We therefore describe it qualitatively and do not reproduce the survival figures circulated in press releases. Thus, Allovectin-7 illustrates the same translational pattern observed in other locally administered pDNA programmes: detectable expression and acceptable tolerability do not necessarily translate into durable clinical benefit.

Allovectin-7 therefore represents lipid-complexed intratumoral pDNA immunogene therapy rather than an active nuclear-targeting platform. Like TAVO-EP, it relied on intratumoral administration and localised gene expression, but did not incorporate an explicit NLS or DTS strategy.

BC-819 (H19-DTA pDNA; intravesical administration)

BC-819, also known as inodiftagene vixteplasmid, is an intravesical pDNA-based therapy in which H19 regulatory sequences drive expression of diphtheria toxin A selectively in H19-expressing malignant bladder cells (Sidi et al. 2008). This design is not an NLS/DTS nuclear import strategy; rather, it uses tumour-associated transcriptional regulation to restrict toxin expression once plasmid DNA reaches the nucleus.

In a Phase I/II marker-lesion study, intravesical BC-819 was evaluated in superficial bladder cancer refractory to bacillus Calmette–Guérin (Sidi et al. 2008). A later Phase IIb marker-lesion trial for intermediate-risk non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer reported prevention of new tumour growth in approximately two-thirds of patients and ablation of roughly one-third of marker lesions (Gofrit et al. 2014). However, a subsequent open-label Phase II study in BCG-unresponsive NMIBC, NCT03719300, was terminated for lack of efficacy (ClinicalTrials.gov 2026). Together, these studies indicate that H19-regulated toxin expression can produce local antitumor activity in selected NMIBC settings, but does not by itself overcome upstream delivery and efficacy barriers.

Why NLS/DTS mechanisms are rarely central in clinical pDNA products

Together, these examples show a consistent translational pattern. Although NLS peptide conjugation and DTS-mediated piggyback import are well supported mechanistically, they are rarely described as core mechanisms in clinical-stage pDNA products (Zanta et al. 1999; Wilson et al. 1999). Instead, development programmes have favoured physical permeabilization, receptor-mediated uptake, local administration, repeated dosing and promoter specificity.

First, NLS/DTS effects are highly context-dependent. Their activity depends on cell-cycle status, carrier unpacking, cytosolic DNA survival, transcription-factor availability and signalling state (Skowicki et al. 2024). Human tissues and tumours are heterogeneous across these variables, making a nuclear-targeting effect that is robust in a controlled model system difficult to reproduce clinically.

Second, clinical development favours controllable procedural variables. TAVO-EP emphasises electroporation as a defined physical delivery procedure (Daud et al. 2008). SGT-53 emphasises transferrin receptor-mediated tumour targeting (Senzer et al. 2013). VM202 emphasises local intramuscular administration and repeated dosing (Kessler et al. 2021). These variables are easier to standardise than intracellular nuclear import mechanisms.

Third, adding NLS peptides or complex conjugation chemistry increases manufacturing and regulatory complexity. Peptide identity, coupling efficiency, stoichiometry, stability, impurity profile and immunogenicity would all require characterisation (Sebestyen et al. 1998). DTS elements are simpler from a manufacturing standpoint because they are encoded within the plasmid sequence, but their performance is tied to biological context and may be difficult to validate as a dominant mechanism of action in humans (van Gaal et al. 2011).

Fourth, clinical failures of otherwise safe pDNA products indicate that expression gain alone is insufficient. NV1FGF, Allovectin-7 and BC-819 each show that favourable tolerability and plausible local expression do not guarantee efficacy (Gonzalez et al. 2006; Belch et al. 2011; Gofrit et al. 2014). Thus, even a successful nuclear-targeting module must be integrated with an appropriate therapeutic gene, disease model, dosing strategy and endpoint.

Overall, NLS and DTS strategies remain scientifically compelling but clinically under-validated. Current clinical pDNA programmes are designed mainly around electroporation, receptor-mediated uptake, local high-dose exposure, repeated dosing and promoter specificity. The translational challenge is not merely to add a nuclear-targeting signal, but to demonstrate that active nuclear import is the dominant, measurable and clinically meaningful limiting step in the intended product.

The nuclear accessibility index (NAI): an integrated design framework

Rationale and conceptual basis

The preceding sections have catalogued NLS peptide classes (Section 2), DTS elements and their transcription factor–mediated mechanisms (Section 3), chemical conjugation strategies (Section 4) and the structural diversity of peptide-, polymer-, and lipid-based nanoassemblies (Section 5). Together, these findings point to a single unresolved problem: no integrative framework currently exists that predicts, for a given experimental or therapeutic context, whether a given nuclear targeting strategy is likely to be effective before empirical testing. This gap has practical consequences. The apparent discrepancy between the strong mechanistic evidence for NLS- and DTS-mediated nuclear import in model systems and their near-absence as explicitly stated mechanisms in clinical products (Section 8) suggests that laboratory-derived insights frequently fail to translate, not because the mechanisms are incorrect, but because the preconditions for their operation are not simultaneously satisfied in the target tissue.

We therefore propose the Nuclear Accessibility Index (NAI, Figure 7, Table 6), a structured three-axis scoring framework that integrates the three principal determinants of nuclear delivery competence: (i) cell division status, which governs whether passive mitotic access is available or whether active import through intact nuclear pore complexes (NPCs) is obligatory; (ii) carrier–DNA accessibility, which determines whether the plasmid is available in the cytosol in a form accessible to importin receptors and transcription factors; and (iii) the intracellular signalling context, which governs whether the relevant import receptors and transcription factors are available and in their nuclear-import-competent state. Each axis is scored on a three-point scale (Munkonge et al. 2003; Dean et al. 2005; Yao et al. 2013). The three scores are retained primarily as an ordered vector; their sum is used only as a secondary descriptive shorthand, for the reasons set out in Section 9.5.

Axis I—cell division status

The cell cycle is the most well-established determinant of non-viral transfection efficiency. In actively dividing cells, nuclear envelope breakdown during mitosis provides transient, passive access for cytoplasmic DNA to daughter nuclei, largely circumventing the need for active NPC-mediated import. Accordingly, numerous studies have shown that lipoplex-, polyplex-, and peptiplex-mediated transfection yields 50- to 3,000-fold higher gene expression when cells are in G2/M relative to G1, independent of the nuclear targeting strategy employed (Brunner et al. 2000). In contrast, post-mitotic or deeply quiescent cells such as neurons, terminally differentiated myocytes and resting dendritic cells retain an intact nuclear envelope throughout their lifespan, so plasmid nuclear entry is exclusively dependent on active NPC-mediated transport (Dean et al. 2005). The incremental advantage of DTS-mediated active import is correspondingly and markedly reduced in proliferating populations, because mitotic nuclear-envelope breakdown provides an alternative entry route that does not depend on transcription factor recruitment (Wilson et al. 1999; Young et al. 2003). This concerns the nuclear-import contribution specifically: many DTS elements are promoter or enhancer fragments and may continue to influence transcription after nuclear entry, so a reduced import advantage should not be read as a loss of all function.

For NAI scoring, Axis I assigns the following values: actively dividing cells = 1 (passive mitotic access available; active import strategies provide minimal incremental benefit); quiescent but reversibly arrestable cells = 2 (NE intact; active import required; DTS and NLS both relevant); terminally differentiated or post-mitotic cells = 3 (NE permanently intact; highest import barrier; maximum need for active nuclear targeting strategies (Brunner et al. 2000; Dean et al. 2005).

Axis II—carrier–DNA accessibility

Nuclear targeting mechanisms, whether NLS-peptide–based or DTS-based, share a common prerequisite: the plasmid must be present in the cytosol in a form that is accessible to the relevant import machinery. This prerequisite is frequently overlooked when NLS or DTS strategies are evaluated in isolation from their carrier context. For NLS-mediated import, the NLS motif must be physically accessible to importin-α/β receptors; steric occlusion of the NLS within a highly condensed polyplex can abolish importin binding even when the peptide is covalently attached to the DNA (Ciolina et al. 1999; Skowicki et al. 2024). For DTS-mediated import, the binding sites must be accessible to cytoplasmic transcription factors, a condition that is similarly compromised when plasmid DNA is over-condensed by a cationic carrier at high N/P ratios (Lam and Dean 2010; Bai et al. 2017). Conversely, under-protected DNA, delivered with insufficient carrier or after premature complex dissociation, faces rapid cytoplasmic degradation by DNase II and other nucleases, reducing the available substrate for nuclear import (Bai et al. 2017).

Axis II therefore captures the balance between protection and accessibility. Over-condensed complexes (e.g. PEI at excessive N/P ratios, over-cationic peptiplexes) = 1 (transcription factor and importin access both impaired; DTS nonfunctional; NLS sterically masked); under-protected or prematurely dissociated complexes = 2 (DNA accessible but cytosolic half-life severely shortened; import window reduced); optimally formulated carriers = 3 (sufficient condensation for nuclease protection, sufficient DNA accessibility for importin and transcription factor recruitment; both NLS and DTS pathways can operate) (Ciolina et al. 1999; Bai et al. 2017; Skowicki et al. 2024).

Axis III—intracellular signalling context

Even when both cell division status and carrier accessibility are favourable, nuclear import efficiency depends on the availability and activation state of the intracellular transport receptors and transcription factors that actually execute nuclear entry. For the classical NLS pathway, effective import requires cytosolic importin-α and importin-β, Ran-GTP, and a functional NPC (Zanta et al. 1999; Dean et al. 2005). These components are broadly expressed, making the classical NLS strategy generally applicable; however, their relative abundance and activity can vary across cell types and under stress conditions. For DTS-mediated import, the critical determinant is transcription factor availability. Universal DTS elements such as the SV40 enhancer recruit broadly expressed factors (NF-κB, AP-1, Oct-1) and are therefore active in most cell types (Young et al. 2003). In contrast, inducible DTS elements, exemplified by the NF-κB binding sequence, depend on signal-dependent unmasking of the NF-κB NLS by IκBα degradation, and are active only when the relevant upstream pathway is engaged. Tissue-specific DTS elements (SMGA, SP-C, T1α promoters) are active only in cells that express the required lineage transcription factors and are essentially non-functional outside that lineage (Vacik et al. 1999; Miller and Dean 2009; Degiulio et al. 2010).

Axis III assigns the following values: classical importin pathway constitutively active, ubiquitous TFs available = 3 (both cNLS and universal DTS can function; highest strategy flexibility); inducible TF pathway available but requiring activation = 2 (inducible DTS functional upon stimulation; cNLS functional; strategy may require co-administration of activating stimulus); tissue-specific TF pathway required = 1 (only tissue-specific DTS functional; cNLS may operate but DTS provides cell selectivity; off-target expression suppressed) (Mesika et al. 2001; Miller and Dean 2009; Degiulio et al. 2010).

From axis scores to an axis vector: composite score, regions and strategy assignment

The three axis scores can be summed to give a composite NAI score in the range 3-9, which is convenient shorthand for reporting. The composite value is, however, deliberately secondary. Summation is a lossy operation: it treats the three axes as interchangeable and additive, whereas they are neither. We therefore express the framework primarily as an ordered axis vector, NAI = (Axis I, Axis II, Axis III), and use the composite score only as a summary statistic that is meaningful within, but not across, the regions defined in Section 9.6.

This problem is illustrated by degeneracy. A composite score of 7 can arise as (1, 3, 3), (3, 3, 1) or (2, 2, 3), among other combinations. Consider (1, 3, 3), a rapidly proliferating cancer cell delivered with an optimally formulated carrier. Here mitotic nuclear-envelope breakdown provides an alternative route for nuclear entry, substantially reducing the incremental benefit of active nuclear targeting; the sequence-specific advantage of a DTS is therefore largely bypassed. In (3, 3, 1), a terminally differentiated cell in which only lineage-restricted transcription factors are available, active import is obligatory and a tissue-specific DTS is the strategy of choice, with absolute efficiency traded for selectivity. In (2, 2, 3) the limiting problem is neither the cell cycle nor the signalling environment but the formulation itself. The same composite score therefore supports three incompatible recommendations, which is precisely the information that summation discards. The axes are also of different kinds: Axis I is a biological precondition that the designer cannot alter, Axis II is an engineering variable that is in principle fully solvable by reformulation, and Axis III is primarily a determinant of specificity rather than of raw efficiency. Reporting the vector preserves this distinction; reporting only the sum does not.

To illustrate the practical utility of the NAI framework, three representative scenarios are considered.

Example 1—Post-mitotic primary neurons (for example rat dorsal root ganglion neurons), optimally condensed polyplex, constitutive classical importin pathway: NAI = (3, 3, 3), composite 9, permissive region. Axis I = 3 (terminally post-mitotic, so active import is obligatory), Axis II = 3 (condensation optimal and the targeting signal still accessible), Axis III = 3 (importin-α/β and the Ran-GTP gradient constitutively available). Recommended strategy: a classical NLS combined with a universal (SV40) DTS in an optimally condensed carrier. This is the setting in which Zanta et al. obtained NLS-dependent expression gains in non-dividing primary neurons (Zanta et al. 1999), and in which sequence-specific DTS-mediated import is demonstrable at all, since it can be resolved only where the nuclear envelope remains intact throughout (Wilson et al. 1999; Young et al. 2003).

Example 2—Growth-arrested epithelial cells, LNP delivery with incomplete endosomal escape, constitutive importin pathway: NAI = (2, 1, 3), composite 6, carrier-limited region. Axis I = 2 (reversibly quiescent), Axis II = 1 (DNA retained in endosomal compartments; cytosolic availability severely limited), Axis III = 3 (importin active). Although the composite score is intermediate, the vector identifies unambiguously that carrier redesign, and specifically improvement of endosomal escape, is the primary bottleneck, and that adding NLS peptides without addressing Axis II will not rescue nuclear delivery. This is consistent with reports that NLS enhancement fails when DNA does not reach the cytosol in an importin-accessible form (Grosse et al. 2006; Lam and Dean 2010), and with the observation that manipulating cytosolic DNA availability can have non-monotonic effects on expression (Greitens et al. 2025).

Example 3—Proliferating cancer cells, optimally condensed lipoplex, constitutive importin pathway: NAI = (1, 3, 3), composite 7, excluded region. The composite score is high, yet Axis I = 1 places the scenario in the excluded region: passive mitotic access already accounts for the bulk of nuclear entry, so the incremental benefit of active nuclear targeting is small, and smallest for DTS elements, whose sequence-specific advantage is largely bypassed. This prediction is consistent with the well-established observation that DTS elements yield large expression gains in non-dividing cells but much smaller gains in proliferating populations (Wilson et al. 1999; Young et al. 2003), and it illustrates why the composite score alone would have been misleading here.

Permissive, conditional and excluded regions of NAI space

Because the three axes are not interchangeable, the NAI is better read as a partition of a 3 × 3 x 3 design space into regions with qualitatively different recommendations, rather than as a continuous score (Figure 8). Five regions are defined, and they are evaluated in a fixed order of precedence: Axis I first, because it determines whether active import is required at all; Axis II second, because it determines whether any import signal can be read; and Axis III last, because it determines which signal to choose and what selectivity it will confer.

Excluded region (Axis I = 1). In actively proliferating populations, nuclear envelope breakdown during mitosis provides periodic passive access, and the incremental benefit of active nuclear targeting is small, and smallest for DTS elements, whose sequence-specific import advantage is largely bypassed once the envelope disassembles (Wilson et al. 1999; Brunner et al. 2000; Young et al. 2003). The claim is an engineering one about where effort is worth spending, not a mechanistic assertion that import signals cease to function. This region contains most cultured tumour cell lines, activated lymphocytes and rapidly regenerating tissue. The framework's recommendation here is negative and deliberately so: investment should be directed to uptake, endosomal escape, cytosolic stability and dose rather than to nuclear targeting chemistry. Recognising this region explicitly also explains part of the reproducibility problem in the literature, since many NLS and DTS comparisons have been performed in exactly the cell types where the mechanism is least able to contribute.

Carrier-limited region (Axis II = 1, Axis I ≥ 2). Here the nuclear envelope is a genuine barrier but the plasmid is not available in an import-competent form, whether because it is over-condensed, trapped in endosomes or prematurely degraded. Axis II differs from the other two axes in that it is an engineering variable: unlike the proliferative state of the target tissue or the transcription factor repertoire of a given lineage, it is fully under the control of the formulator and can in principle be raised to 3 by reformulation (Ciolina et al. 1999; Bai et al. 2017; Skowicki et al. 2024). The recommendation is therefore sequential rather than additive: correct the carrier, then re-score. Adding an NLS or a DTS before doing so will not rescue delivery. The non-monotonic behaviour observed when cytosolic DNA availability is manipulated directly (Greitens et al. 2025) is a reminder that Axis II should be optimised empirically rather than simply maximised.

Specificity-dominated region (Axis III = 1, Axis I ≥ 2, Axis II ≥ 2). When only lineage-restricted transcription factors are available, absolute nuclear import efficiency is limited but selectivity is high. This region is not a penalty but a design opportunity: tissue-specific DTS elements such as SMGA, SP-C and T1α restrict nuclear import, and therefore expression, to the intended cell type (Miller and Dean 2008; Degiulio et al. 2010; Gottfried et al. 2016). The recommended configuration is a tissue-specific DTS as the primary targeting element, optionally combined with a classical NLS to provide an efficiency floor, with the explicit expectation that absolute expression will be lower than in the permissive region and that this is the price of restricted expression.

Intermediate region (Axis II = 2, Axis I ≥ 2, Axis III ≥ 2). Here the plasmid is accessible to importins and to transcription factors, but insufficient condensation shortens its cytosolic half-life, so the window during which import can occur is open but narrow. This region differs from the carrier-limited region in degree rather than in kind: the limitation is again the formulation, but it constrains the duration of the opportunity rather than abolishing it. A single targeting strategy is therefore appropriate, and nuclease protection should be improved before NLS and DTS elements are combined, because the incremental benefit of adding a second mechanism is small while the substrate is still being lost to cytosolic degradation (Lechardeur et al. 1999; Bai et al. 2017).

Permissive region (Axis I ≥ 2, Axis II = 3, Axis III ≥ 2). Only in this region are both mechanisms fully operable and additive, and only here is a combined NLS + DTS design expected to deliver its maximum benefit (Table 6). Post-mitotic or quiescent, well-formulated, signalling-competent systems such as Example 1 fall here, and it is the region toward which carrier engineering in the carrier-limited region should aim. Expressing the framework in this way also makes its falsifiability explicit: the framework predicts that measured NLS/DTS gains should be large in the permissive region, near zero in the excluded region regardless of formulation quality, and recoverable in the carrier-limited region only after Axis II is corrected. Each of these is testable with existing methods (Section 6), and systematic screening across defined cell states offers a direct route to empirical calibration of the region boundaries (Mohamedshah et al. 2026).

Limitations and future directions

The NAI framework is deliberately conceptual rather than quantitative: the three-point ordinal scale offers strategic guidance rather than a numerical prediction of transfection efficiency, and the region boundaries of Section 9.6 are proposed as testable hypotheses rather than calibrated thresholds. Four refinements are foreseeable. The axes could absorb further determinants of nuclear entry: cytoplasmic trafficking along microtubules (Mesika et al. 2005; Midoux et al. 2017), endosomal escape, cytosolic DNA half-life, and protein-mediated sequestration of cytosolic DNA (Greitens et al. 2025). Axis III could be decomposed into importin isoform availability, Ran-GTP gradient integrity and transcription factor activation state. Cell-to-cell heterogeneity, a known source of variance in non-viral transfection, is not yet represented (Breuzard et al. 2008; Skowicki et al. 2024). And the treatment of the pore is coarse: because NPC diameter is dynamic and transport selectivity substantially electrostatic (Zimmerli et al. 2021; Mosalaganti et al. 2022; Feng et al. 2024; Mishra et al. 2025), complex size and surface charge may warrant an explicit fourth axis.

Despite these limitations, the NAI links the mechanistic landscape reviewed above with the translational question posed in Section 8. Electroporation, receptor-mediated endocytosis and local high-dose administration all raise Axis II by flooding the cytosol with accessible DNA without acting on Axes I or III. That is a coherent strategy in the carrier-limited region, but not for the post-mitotic, tissue-restricted targets that constitute the hardest and most therapeutically important class. Screening platforms that read nuclear localisation and expression in parallel across defined cell states (Mohamedshah et al. 2026) offer the most direct route to empirical calibration.

Conclusion

Nuclear delivery of plasmid DNA remains a fundamental bottleneck in nonviral gene therapy, particularly in post-mitotic and quiescent cells where the nuclear envelope is a permanent barrier to passive entry. The two strategies developed to overcome it operate on distinct principles. NLS peptides, which include classical monopartite and bipartite motifs together with their engineered, histone-derived and CPP-hybrid variants, recruit the importin-α/β machinery directly to the DNA cargo. DTS elements instead recruit endogenous NLS-bearing transcription factors to specific plasmid sequences, achieving piggyback import that is most powerful in non-dividing cells and that can confer cell-type specificity when lineage-restricted factors are engaged. Both depend critically on the carrier in which they are deployed: over-condensation occludes the signal, whereas insufficient protection allows cytoplasmic degradation before import can occur.

Despite this mechanistic evidence, neither strategy has been incorporated as a declared core mechanism in any approved nonviral product. Clinical programmes have relied instead on electroporation, receptor-mediated endocytosis and local high-dose administration. All of these raise cytosolic DNA availability without engaging the import machinery directly. Bridging this gap requires a framework that treats cell division status, carrier-DNA accessibility and intracellular signalling context together. The Nuclear Accessibility Index does so, and applied as an axis vector rather than as a single sum it partitions the design space into regions with qualitatively different recommendations, as set out in Section 9.6.

Realising the therapeutic potential of NLS- and DTS-based nuclear targeting will require their co-design with carriers that guarantee cytosolic accessibility, screening platforms that read nuclear localisation and expression in parallel, and validation in the post-mitotic, tissue-restricted contexts that constitute the most clinically urgent targets for nonviral gene medicine.
