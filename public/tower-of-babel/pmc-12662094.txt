Periprocedural Volume Management After Ablation
Authors: Jiaqi Mi, Andrea Natale, Dhanunjaya Lakkireddy
Published: 2025

Postablation volume management remains an underaddressed but increasingly recognized challenge, especially in patients with heart failure (HF) undergoing atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation.1 Approximately 20 to 26% of patients experience HF symptoms within 30 days postprocedure, often due to fluid overload stemming from procedural irrigation, sedation, elevated left atrial pressures, and neurohormonal activation.2 HF is a leading cause of 30-day readmissions after ablation, with rates ranging from 2.8% to 17%, depending on the arrhythmia type and patient cohort.3 For electrophysiologists, these readmissions impact patients’ recovery, increase morbidity, and inflate health care costs.

In recent years, pulsed field ablation (PFA) has emerged as a rapidly adopted technology for AF ablation, offering myocardial selectivity and reduced collateral injury. However, accumulating clinical observations suggest that PFA may be associated with a higher degree of acute atrial stunning compared with traditional radiofrequency ablation. This transient but significant impairment in left atrial contractile function can elevate left atrial pressures, promote pulmonary congestion, and predispose susceptible patients—particularly those with underlying HF—to early postprocedural decompensation. The large, open-architecture basket-style catheters used in current-generation PFA systems may contribute to this hemodynamic impact, and the associated changes in atrial electromechanical properties typically recover over time. Given these dynamics, meticulous postprocedural volume management becomes even more critical. In this context, protective strategies such as rapid-acting intranasal bumetanide, coupled with structured discharge planning and patient education, could provide timely decongestion during the vulnerable recovery period, potentially mitigating symptoms and improving short-term outcomes.4

Despite the predictability of this complication, postablation volume management has changed little over time. Oral loop diuretics remain the default, despite known limitations in their delayed onset of action, variable gastrointestinal absorption, and reduced efficacy in patients with gut edema or diuretic resistance contribute to a high-risk window for volume overload, arrhythmia recurrence, prolonged hospitalization, and readmissions.5 Emerging evidence supports the use of rapid-acting, nonoral diuretic therapies to achieve timely and consistent decongestion during the vulnerable periprocedural period. Incorporating agents such as intranasal bumetanide into postablation care pathways offers a practical, targeted strategy to address fluid overload and improve procedural recovery, particularly in patients with HF.

Catheter ablation is inherently associated with intraprocedural fluid loading, compounded by postablation inflammation, atrial stunning, and neurohormonal perturbations. These changes elevate left atrial pressure and promote pulmonary congestion and atrial stretch. This hemodynamic burden contributes to both early arrhythmia recurrence and HF exacerbation, particularly within the first 72 hours postablation.6 While short-term oral diuretics can help during early recovery, their limitations often necessitate rescue therapy. We suggest the targeted use of fast-acting nonoral diuretics—most notably intranasal bumetanide—for patients at high risk of postprocedural decompensation.

Bumetanide nasal spray (BNS) circumvents gastrointestinal absorption, delivering rapid and consistent systemic drug levels through the richly vascular nasal mucosa. Phase I pharmacokinetic studies demonstrate that BNS achieves peak plasma concentrations within 30 to 60 minutes, with significantly less interpatient variability compared to oral formulations.7 This route is particularly beneficial in patients with diuretic resistance, gastrointestinal hypoperfusion, or postanesthesia nausea and delayed gastric emptying. Notably, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration has approved the New Drug Application for BNS (RSQ-777) to treat edema associated with HF, liver, and kidney disease under the Prescription Drug User Fee Act on September 14, 2025, has been assigned to the application. Moreover, intranasal administration is user-friendly, allowing potential implementation via outpatient or nurse-administered protocols. This makes it a promising tool for mitigating fluid retention during the vulnerable postablation window. From a pharmacologic standpoint, intranasal delivery bypasses first-pass metabolism and achieves predictable systemic exposure through rapid mucosal absorption. Unlike oral formulations, which may be compromised by bowel edema or poor perfusion, intranasal bumetanide ensures more reliable uptake. Early studies suggest bioavailability comparable to intravenous dosing, with favorable tolerability and minimal local irritations. These features underscore its potential to bridge the gap between oral and parenteral therapy in both acute and chronic decompensated HF.

To evaluate the clinical utility of intranasal diuretics, both mechanistic and outcome-based markers are important. Mechanistically, effectiveness may be assessed through natriuresis, urine volume, short-term weight loss, and changes in renal sodium handling. Hemodynamic improvements—such as reductions in filling pressures and pulmonary congestion—provide further objective evidence. Biomarker changes, particularly decreases in natriuretic peptides (B-type natriuretic peptide or N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide), serve as sensitive indicators of decongestive efficacy. Clinically, improvements in dyspnea, peripheral edema, and functional status reflect symptomatic benefit, while reduced emergency visits and readmissions may serve as system-level outcomes. Finally, patient-reported measures, including quality-of-life indices and treatment satisfaction, will be critical for determining long-term adoption.

Subcutaneous furosemide (SCF) represents another viable nonoral strategy with bioavailability comparable to intravenous administration. SCF has demonstrated consistent systemic exposure and improved decongestion in high-risk HF populations. In the AT HOME-HF (Avoiding Treatment in Hospital with Subcutaneous Furosemide for Worsening Heart Failure) pilot study, outpatient SCF use led to greater volume reduction (mean weight loss −2.02 kg at day 3; P = 0.035), along with improved dyspnea and functional status compared to optimized oral therapy.8 While effective, SCF typically requires prefilled infusion devices, making it more suited to transitional care programs than routine outpatient use.

Integrating nonoral diuretics into postablation care provides an opportunity to move beyond pharmacology and reshape routine practice. For patients undergoing AF ablation—particularly those with underlying HF—early volume offloading reduces atrial wall stress, neurohormonal changes, and the likelihood of arrhythmia recurrence.9 This need is heightened by the fact that many AF patients are discharged the same day, creating a narrow window to forecast these complications and a greater propensity to develop outside the hospital. In this context, a proactive approach is indispensable. Patients should be counseled on self-monitoring for early warning signs such as rapid weight gain, progressive dyspnea, orthopnea, or worsening edema, and given clear thresholds for when to contact their care team. Providing a predischarge prescription for intranasal loop diuretics on a pro re nata basis can empower high-risk patients to act quickly if symptoms arise.

Such patient-centered strategies are most effective when supported by system-level processes. Embedding early postdischarge phone calls, nurse-led symptom checks, and scheduled follow-ups into electrophysiology (EP) workflow ensures timely detection of decompensation before hospitalization is required. A multidisciplinary “care continuum” model—integrating electrophysiologists, HF specialists, and nursing staff—further strengthens this safety net, allowing rapid adjustment of therapy and reinforcing education. Training nurses to reinforce fluid monitoring at discharge and during follow-up enhances consistency across the care team.

While AF represents the highest-volume group, the utility of nonoral diuretics extends to other arrhythmia types. In supraventricular tachycardia with tachycardia-induced ventricular dysfunction, prompt decongestion may relieve transient postprocedural palpitations and dyspnea, while in more hemodynamically tenuous settings such as ventricular tachycardia ablation in patients with known ventricular dysfunction, careful fluid balance is essential to avoid circulatory compromise. Across arrhythmias, the ability to provide rapid, reliable decongestion with intranasal bumetanide or SCF expands the toolkit available to EP teams (Figure 1).

Beyond clinical benefits, these strategies carry the potential to improve health care efficiency. Incorporating nonoral diuretics into standard postablation protocols could reduce 30-day readmission rates—which remain near 25% among congestive heart failure populations—shorten hospital stays, support outpatient ablation feasibility, and lessen the overall financial burden of HF care, currently exceeding $15 billion annually in the United States.10

Intranasal bumetanide is currently U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved, with potential market availability projected in Q4 2025. Its approval offers a timely option for rapid, noninvasive volume management in postprocedural HF care. While future randomized trials are warranted to assess its clinical impact—with endpoints such as natriuretic peptide trends, fluid status metrics, arrhythmia recurrence, and health care utilization—real-world protocols and observational data can guide thoughtful early adoption in the meantime.

Nonoral loop diuretics—particularly intranasal bumetanide—should therefore be viewed not only as HF therapies, but as procedural adjuncts for electrophysiology. The combination of mechanistic plausibility, early pharmacokinetic evidence, and practical delivery methods supports their formal evaluation in ablation-specific protocols. Moving forward, multicenter registries and pragmatic trials in the EP population will be essential to define optimal patient selection criteria, quality reduction in readmission rates, and confirm the feasibility of implementation across diverse practice settings.

Ultimately, addressing postablation volume overload requires more than traditional oral strategies. Rapid-acting nonoral options, led by intranasal bumetanide, offer a practical and physiologically targeted solution to bridge the gap between procedural success and durable recovery. Their benefits will be maximized when paired with structured discharge planning, patient self-monitoring, and coordinated follow-up, extending their value beyond the procedure itself. As same-day discharge becomes standard after AF ablation, embedding these strategies into routine practice will be essential for reducing preventable readmissions and advancing patient-centered outcomes.
