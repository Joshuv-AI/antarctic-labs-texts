Paraneoplastic Pemphigus Associated With Renal Cell Carcinoma. Rare Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Halkawt Babarasul Ahmed, Hawnaz Atta Karim, Othman Abdulrahman Mohammed, Jeza Muhamad Abdul Aziz, Mohammed Kaka Bra Salih, Parshal Bhandari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionParaneoplastic pemphigus (PNP) is an uncommon autoimmune blistering disorder frequently associated with underlying malignancies. Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is the most common primary renal cancer. It is associated with a wide range of paraneoplastic syndromes, but occasionally presents as a dermatological finding.Case presentationA 56-year-old woman with severe oral and cutaneous lesions was diagnosed with PNP associated with renal cell carcinoma (RCC). Introduction

Paraneoplastic pemphigus (PNP), also known as paraneoplastic autoimmune multi-organ syndrome (PAMS), is an uncommon, potentially fatal, blistering disorder of autoimmune origin that presents with severe mucocutaneous erosions and polymorphous cutaneous eruptions as a feature of an associated malignancy.1,2 PNP was first reported in 1990 and is characterized by a complex clinical presentation and a strong association with malignancy, most commonly hematological malignancies such as non-Hodgkin lymphoma, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, and Castleman disease.3,4 However, thymomas, sarcomas, and carcinomas of epithelial origin have also been described, although much less frequently. 5

RCC is the most frequent primary tumor of the kidney, representing approximately 80–85% of all renal neoplasms.5,6 RCC is notorious for its polymorphous clinical presentations, ranging from a variety of paraneoplastic syndromes. 6 These syndromes can precede or coincide with the diagnosis of RCC, affect multiple organs, and are considered to be due to ectopic hormone production, cytokine release, or immune-mediated effects associated with the tumor.6,7 Although paraneoplastic syndromes, including hypercalcemia, hypertension, and liver failure, are well described in RCC, dermatological findings are rare. Of these, paraneoplastic pemphigus in association with RCC is extremely rare, and there have been only three properly documented cases described in the literature.4,5,8 We report a case of PNP associated with RCC in a 56-year-old woman. This case report was prepared in accordance with the CARE checklist report. 9

Case Presentation

A 56-year-old woman presented to the clinic with multiple oral ulcerations, particularly in the buccal region and lips, difficulty eating, and impaired nutrition of two years’ duration. After one month, the lesions spread to most of the body. There was no history of smoking, vomiting, gross hematuria, or jaundice. The symptoms persisted on the day of admission, and she developed painful oral lesions and multiple blisters all over her body that started to rupture and erode, similar to burned skin and mucocutaneous distribution (Figure 1A and B). On physical examination, her temperature was 39°C. Lung auscultation revealed normal findings. The heart sounds were normal. Her baseline blood investigations, including renal and liver function tests, were also within normal limits, and her urine analysis showed 6-8 pus cells. Ultrasound of the abdomen revealed a right, well-defined, homogeneous, hyperechoic lesion at the cortex of the mid portion, measuring 14 x 17 mm. The left kidney, urinary bladder, and bilateral ovaries were normal. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen with contrast was performed to search for intraabdominal lesions, revealing a focal mass in the right kidney (upper cortex) measuring 16×21×20 mm. The mass had an inhomogeneous density and was consistent with renal carcinoma, with a low attenuated cystic lesion at the lower pole of the spleen measuring 6–7 mm and at the tail of the pancreas measuring 4-5 mm present, and no retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy was present. The remaining abdominal organs were normal. An oral mucosal biopsy was performed and sent for histopathological examination. Histopathological examination revealed squamous epithelium with suprabasal acantholysis and moderate chronic inflammatory cell infiltration of the dermis with marked eosinophils, as well as a few spongiotic areas with pieces of scaling and inflammation with apoptotic keratin bodies. The features of the oral lesions indicated pemphigus (Figure 2A). Direct immunofluorescence(DIF) of the skin biopsy revealed intense granular deposition along the basement membrane zone (BMZ) and intercellular deposits of IgG (+3) and C3 (+2) within the epidermis in a fish-net pattern, indicating autoimmune activity associated with pemphigus (Figure 2B). IgM and IgA were Negative.

The clinical and histopathological findings required differentiation from several autoimmune blistering disorders. The differential diagnoses considered included pemphigus vulgaris, mucous membrane pemphigoid, erosive lichen planus, erythema multiforme/Stevens–Johnson syndrome spectrum, and other autoimmune blistering disorders. The presence of suprabasal acantholysis on histopathology, combined with intercellular IgG and C3 deposition within the epidermis in the fish-net pattern on direct immunofluorescence (DIF), supported a diagnosis within the pemphigus spectrum and helped exclude mucous membrane pemphigoid (which typically shows linear basement membrane zone deposits) and lichenoid interface dermatitis without acantholysis. However, indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) on rat bladder epithelium was not performed due to a lack of local availability. The polymorphic clinical presentation, presence of internal malignancy (renal cell carcinoma), and the histological and immunofluorescence features collectively fulfilled the Camisa and Helm diagnostic criteria for PNP. The patient was diagnosed with PNP secondary to renal cell carcinoma.

The patient was treated with rituximab (1 g as an infusion) and after two weeks, another dose of 1 g infusion in combination with steroids (prednisolone 0.5 mg/kg) until control, which was later tapered by 25% each month until we reached 5 mg/day. After 6 months, a maintenance dose of rituximab (500 mg) was administered at 6, 9, 12, and 18 months to prevent relapses. After one dose of rituximab, the lesions disappeared by 90%, and after the second dose, complete clinical remission was achieved at 6 months with resolution of all mucocutaneous lesions, and the patient was psychologically comfortable (Figure 3A and B). She was counselled regarding the prognosis of PNP and the associated malignancy, and surgical excision of the tumor was planned following stabilization of her dermatological condition. Unfortunately, the patient was subsequently lost to follow-up before the tumor could be surgically resected. Therefore, long-term oncological and dermatological outcomes, including definitive surgical management of the renal tumor, could not be confirmed (Table 1).

Discussion

PNP, also known as paraneoplastic autoimmune multi-organ syndrome (PAMS), in association with renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is an extremely unusual clinical condition, with only three case reports published in PubMed.4,5,8 Before 2022, only two cases of PNP complicating RCC had been reported.4,8 One of these was the case of a 64-year-old PNP female patient diagnosed with renal cell carcinoma with sarcomatoid differentiation, an uncommon finding comprising approximately 5% of all RCC and advised for a dose of corticosteroid at frequent interval then the lesions responded well except buccal mucosa. 4 Sarcomatoid RCC is generally associated with poor prognosis and frequently metastasises to the lungs, bones, and liver, which renders its correlation with dermatological paraneoplastic phenomena particularly striking. 4 Another case was reported by Aessopos et al. 8 reported a 72 years old female who initially had a painful oral ulcer, which was diagnosed as lichen planus, and was treated with prednisolone for 6 months with poor improvement. 8 The patient was subsequently discovered to harbor a 4 cm renal mass compatible with renal carcinoma. 8 In 2002, Gaurav et al reported a 45-year-old male patient with painful oral erosions for 7 months and cutaneous lesions for 2 months. The patient was a chronic smoker, and based on history, examination, and investigations, the patient was diagnosed as a case of PNP secondary to renal cell carcinoma. 5 Aessopos et al reported a limited response to conventional immunosuppressive therapy with poor overall prognosis. 8 Gupta et al reported partial clinical improvement following combination immunosuppressive therapy, but the disease still proved difficult to control. 4 In our case, mucocutaneous lesions were completely clinically remitted with rituximab therapy, as also noted by Gaurav et al, with better short-term response obtained with rituximab-based therapy. 5

Our patient also had marked oral involvement and extensive mucocutaneous lesions. Similar to the early stage RCC associated PNP case reported by Garuve et al. our patient had a small, localized renal mass without documented retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy or distant metastasis. 5 While other researchers reported sarcomatoid RCC associated PNP which showed aggressive tumor features and metastatic lymph node involvement. 4 The patient’s case in the long term is limited as a definitive renal tumor resection and subsequent oncological outcome could not be confirmed. The diagnosis of PNP is difficult, as it can present as a variety of other blistering autoimmune diseases, including pemphigus vulgaris, mucous membrane pemphigoid, erosive lichen planus, and erythema multiforme/Stevens–Johnson syndrome spectrum.1-3 The diagnostic criteria proposed by Camisa and Helm and newer diagnostic approaches, subsequent refinements remain important for differentiating PNP from other blistering diseases and reducing the risk of misdiagnosis.10-12 Researchers proposed three major and three minor diagnostic criteria. Three major criteria, or two major and two minor criteria, were required for diagnosis. The important criteria are polymorphic mucocutaneous eruptions, simultaneous internal neoplasms, and serum antibodies with a specific immunoprecipitation pattern, with the minor criteria being histological evidence of acantholysis, intercellular and basement membrane fluorescence on DIF, and the presence of autoantibodies in the patient’s serum by indirect IF staining on rat bladder epithelium.11,12 Based on these criteria, the present case was diagnosed as PNP, fulfilling two major criteria (polymorphic mucocutaneous eruptions and simultaneous internal neoplasm) and two minor criteria (histological acantholysis and intercellular/basement membrane fluorescence on DIF). However, immunoprecipitation and indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) staining on rat bladder epithelium, which would have provided additional diagnostic confirmation, were not performed due to limited availability of these specialized tests locally.

The pathophysiology of PNP results from the induction of autoantibodies to desmosomal and hemidesmosomal proteins, such as desmogleins, desmocollins, and plakin family proteins, which are essential for the integrity of the epithelium. 13 They cause acantholysis and blister formation, which are characteristic features of the disease. 14 Several sources of these autoimmune mechanisms exist: antitumor immune responses, dysregulated cytokine production, and autoantibodies produced by tumor-originating B cells. 15 Clinically, PNP has a polymorphic appearance and may involve the mucosa and skin. 16 The most classic clinical finding is recalcitrant, painful stomatitis, which can also present with various dermatologic phenotypes, including lichenoid eruptions and erythema multiforme-like lesions to frank blisters. 5

Identification of renal cortical lesions, histopathology, and immunofluorescence findings. Acantholysis, spongiosis, eosinophil-rich infiltrate, and intercellular IgG/C3 deposition are consistent with the established diagnostic features of PNP.5,8 PNP has a poor prognosis, and the mortality rate has been reported to be as high as 90%, with most patients dying within a year of diagnosis, frequently resulting from systemic complications such as breakdown sepsis, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, and bronchiolitis obliterans. 17 However, the favorable short-term outcome in our case can be attributed to early diagnosis and prompt initiation of treatment with rituximab and corticosteroids, as reported in a previous study, which reported several cases of PNP successfully treated,1,3 which achieved effective control of cutaneous lesions, and measures were adopted to monitor for potential pulmonary complications, including bronchiolitis obliterans, a life-threatening complication of PNP, which is related to the side effect of using rituximab. 3 Rituximab was selected because PNP is an autoantibody-mediated disease and rituximab targets B-cell-mediated antibody production, while systemic corticosteroids were used to rapidly control severe mucocutaneous inflammation.2,3,13 Recurrence may occur, particularly if the underlying tumor persists or immune activity continues. Therefore, long-term collaboration among dermatology, urology, pathology, and oncological surveillance is required.2,18 Nevertheless, the long-term prognosis remains uncertain due to loss to follow-up. The time at which diagnosis is made seems to be of primary importance in outcome, since early detection and treatment of the underlying malignant process may result in the best overall survival and resolution of paraneoplastic features. This highlights the need to look for occult malignancies, including RCC, in patients with unusual autoimmune and inflammatory diseases. Severe refractory stomatitis, polymorphic skin lesions, characteristic histopathological findings, and immunopathological studies are essential to distinguish PNP from these entities. In particular, the diagnostic criteria proposed by Camisa and Helm and subsequent refinements remain important for differentiating PNP from other blistering diseases and reducing the risk of misdiagnosis.10,11

A proposal for the surgical removal of the renal tumor was made after the stabilization of the dermopathy, but the patient was not followed up in our care system; therefore, the tumor resection could not be documented. Therefore, long-term oncological outcomes, durability of mucocutaneous remission, risk of recurrence, pulmonary complications, and delayed treatment-related side effects could not be substantiated.

Rituximab and systemic corticosteroids may be limited by infection risk, frailty, diabetes, hypertension, renal impairment, malignancy-related factors, and planned surgery. These issues are particularly important in patients with RCC or other comorbidities.2,3,13

A single case report cannot establish causality, incidence, comparative treatment efficacy, or generalizable prognosis; however, it is useful for documenting rare clinical associations.

Indirect immunofluorescence on rat bladder epithelium was not available locally. Genetic testing and environmental exposure assessment were not performed. The patient was lost to follow-up, limiting confirmation of renal tumor resection and long-term outcome. Despite these limitations, this case highlights a rare association between PNP and RCC and emphasizes the importance of malignancy screening in patients with severe refractory mucocutaneous disease. Emphasizing that a single case report cannot establish causality, comparative treatment, or generalize treatment effectiveness. Future studies should focus on registry-based reporting of PNP-RCC cases, standardized diagnostic testing, treatment response, relapse risk, and long-term outcomes.

Conclusion

PNP should be considered as a rare but important paraneoplastic phenomenon of RCC. A complete workup is important for diagnosing the underlying tumor associated with this phenomenon. Rituximab combined with systemic corticosteroids achieved complete short-term clinical remission of mucocutaneous lesions in this patient. However, the long-term effectiveness remains uncertain because definitive renal tumor management was not documented and the patient was lost to follow-up. Early diagnosis and multi-organ involvement for the underlying malignancy are crucial for a better prognosis. Close surveillance and long-term follow-up are essential for detecting potential relapse, monitoring for pulmonary complications (particularly bronchiolitis obliterans), and managing the underlying malignancy.
