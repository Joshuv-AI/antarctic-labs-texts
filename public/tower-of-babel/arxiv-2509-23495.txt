Abstract
. We consider an energy functional that arises in micromagnetic and liquid crystal theory on thin films. In particular, our energy comprises a non-convex term that models anti-symmetric exchange as well as an anisotropy term. We devise an algorithm for energy minimization in the continuous case and show weak convergence of a subsequence towards a solution of the corresponding Euler–Lagrange equation. Furthermore, an algorithm for numerical energy minimization is presented. We show empirically that this numerical algorithm converges to the correct solutions for a benchmark problem without the need for user-supplied parameters, and present a rigorous convergence analysis for important special cases.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Model problem

We consider a two-dimensional bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Given
\mu,\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the energy

(1)

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\,|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+\kappa\,\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\curl\boldsymbol{u}+\frac{\mu}{2}\,|\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{for all }u\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega):=[H^{1}(\Omega)]^{3},

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the
i
-th canonical basis vector and the 2D (vector)
\curl
-operator is defined by

(2)

\curl\boldsymbol{u}:=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{i}\!\times\!\partial_{i}\boldsymbol{u}.

We aim to solve the following minimization problem:

(3)

\text{Find}\quad\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\big\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)\mathrel{\big|}|\boldsymbol{u}|=1\text{ a.e.\ in }\Omega\big\}\quad\text{such that}\quad\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})=\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v}).

The task of solving such minimization problems arises in the theories of micromagnetics and nematic liquid crystals on thin (planar) films
[
DDPR22
]
.

The seminal work
[
Alo97
]
introduced a constructive algorithm for computing minimizers to the three-dimensional Dirichlet energy with Dirichlet boundary conditions, which is a simplified variant of (
1
).
The work
[
Bar05
]
provided and analyzed a finite element version of this algorithm, which indeed involves some subtle technicalities to carry over the argument from the continuous case.
In particular, the algorithm poses a certain angle condition to the underlying triangulation; see Definition
4.1
below.

1.2.
Contributions of the present work

First, we present a surrogate energy functional which has the same set of minimizers as (
7
) while consisting only of positive contributions, regardless of the involved parameters
\kappa
and
\mu
.
This makes the surrogate energy much more convenient for analysis and numerical algorithms.
In particular, the surrogate energy is subsequently used to formulate an iterative algorithm in the spirit of
[
Alo97
]
for computing minimizers of (
7
).
Our algorithm provides a constructive way of finding a minimizing sequence (up to a subsequence) with monotonically decreasing energy starting from an arbitrary initial function.
In analogy to
[
Alo97
]
, we show convergence of this algorithm to solutions of the corresponding Euler–Lagrange equations.
We stress that our problem deals with a model that is considerably more general than that of
[
Alo97
]
, as it incorporates terms of lower order than the Dirichlet energy; in particular, the antisymmetric exchange term
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\curl\boldsymbol{u}
.
This poses several challenges, which we overcome by means of the surrogate energy.
We note that our approach can cover an even larger class of models than (
1
) by permitting different kinds of anisotropy terms; see Section
2.2
.
Furthermore, we present a numerical algorithm in the spirit of
[
Bar05
]
, which provides a convenient way of computing approximations to minimizers of
\mathcal{E}
.
We give implementation details overcoming difficulties that arise from considering natural boundary conditions and that substantially enhance the algorithm given in
[
Bar05
]
.

1.3.
Outline

In Section
2
, we introduce a surrogate energy for (
1
) that is easier to work with on the analytical as well as on the numerical level.
We proceed by stating our energy minimization algorithm in Section
3
, as well as a convergence result.
Finally, we present a practical finite element discretization in Section
4
together with some implementation details, numerical experiments, and a convergence analysis for important special cases.

u_{3}

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}

2.
Surrogate energy
\mathcal{J}

2.1.
Setup

To find minimizers of
\mathcal{E}
from (
1
), we seek minimizers of a surrogate energy with nicer properties for both analysis and numerics.
To this end, we introduce the two-dimensional
helical derivatives
(cf.
[
Mel14
,
DIP20
]
)

(4)

\partial_{i}^{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{v}:=\partial_{i}\boldsymbol{v}+\kappa\,\boldsymbol{v}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{i},\qquad\nabla_{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{v}:=(\partial_{1}^{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{v},\partial_{2}^{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{v}),\qquad\Delta_{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{v}:=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\partial_{i}^{\mathfrak{h}}\partial_{i}^{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{v},

and define, with
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, the surrogate energy

(5)

\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{v}):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\big[|\nabla_{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}+g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})\big]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega),

where the bilinear form
g_{\gamma}\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\!\times\!\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
reads

(6)

g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}):=\begin{cases}\gamma\,(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3})(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3})&\gamma\geqslant 0,\\
|\gamma|\,(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3})&\gamma<0.\end{cases}

Note that all terms in
\mathcal{J}
are positive (which is required by our analysis below) as opposed to the terms in
\mathcal{E}
from (
1
).
With
\mathcal{M}
from (
3
), the surrogate minimization problem then reads as follows:

(7)

\text{Find}\quad\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{M}\quad\text{such that}\quad\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u})=\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{v}).

2.2.
Relationship of
\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{E}

It is straightforward to verify, using the direct method of the calculus of variations, that the minimization problems for both
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{J}
over
\mathcal{M}
admit solutions.
Moreover, as we now demonstrate, the two problems are variationally equivalent: for suitable choices of the parameters, minimizers of
\mathcal{J}
coincide with that of
\mathcal{E}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For
\gamma=\mu-\kappa^{2}
, it holds that

\big\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{M}\mathrel{\big|}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})=\inf_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v})\big\}=\big\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{M}\mathrel{\big|}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u})=\inf_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{v})\big\}.

Proof 2.2
.

We begin by examining the relation between the terms
|\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}|^{2}
and
|\boldsymbol{v}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}|^{2}
.
For any
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, it holds that

(8)

|\boldsymbol{v}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{j}|^{2}=\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq j}^{3}|\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{i}|^{2},

which can be seen by explicit computation. From the definition (
6
) of
g_{\gamma}
, we see that

(9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\gamma\geqslant 0:\quad&g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})=\gamma\,|\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}|^{2},\\
\gamma<0:\quad&g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})=-\gamma\,|\boldsymbol{v}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:cross-dot-identity}}}{{=}}\gamma\,|\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}|^{2}-\gamma\,|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}.\end{split}

For all
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, this yields that
g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})=\gamma\,|\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}|^{2}+C\,|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}
with
C=|\min\{0,\gamma\}|\geq 0
.
Finally, we follow
[
DIP20
]
and expand the helical term in
\mathcal{J}
to see that

(10)

\begin{split}|\nabla_{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{eq:helical-derivatives}}}}{{=}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}+2\kappa\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\curl\boldsymbol{v}+\kappa^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}|\boldsymbol{v}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{i}|^{2}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{eq:cross-dot-identity}}}}{{=}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}+2\kappa\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\curl\boldsymbol{v}+\kappa^{2}(|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}|^{2}).\end{split}

Combining the identities above and recalling that
\mu=\kappa^{2}+\gamma
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\big[|\nabla_{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}+g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})\big]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{eq:helical-relationship}}}}{{=}}\int_{\Omega}\Big[\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}+\kappa\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\curl\boldsymbol{v}+\frac{\kappa^{2}+\gamma}{2}|\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}|^{2}+\frac{\kappa^{2}+C}{2}|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}\Big]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v})+\frac{\kappa^{2}+C}{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

For
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{M}
, we have that
|\boldsymbol{v}|=1
a.e. in
\Omega
and hence
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=|\Omega|
.
Thus,
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{v})=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v})+\mathrm{const}
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{M}
so that the sets of minimizers of
\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{E}
coincide.

Remark 2.3
.

It is also of practical interest to consider anisotropy that favors out-of-plane configurations instead of in-plane ones, which is modeled by the modified energy functional

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\int_{\Omega}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\,|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+\kappa\,\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\curl\boldsymbol{u}+\frac{\mu}{2}\,|\boldsymbol{u}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}|^{2}\Big]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

If
\gamma=-(\mu+\kappa^{2})
, in analogy to the above computations, we see that

\big\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{M}\mathrel{\big|}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{u})=\inf_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{M}}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{v})\big\}=\big\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{M}\mathrel{\big|}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u})=\inf_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{v})\big\}.

Note that, thanks to (
8
) and the unit-length constraint, any mixture of out-of-plane and in-plane anisotropies is encompassed by our
g_{\gamma}
term for an appropriate choice of
\gamma
. Such combinations arise, for instance, in the thin-film limit of three-dimensional models with a stray-field contribution; see
[
DMRS20
,
Di 20
,
DDPR22
]
.

2.3.
Euler–Lagrange equations of
\mathcal{J}

For fixed
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, we denote by
\boldsymbol{g}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v})\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)
the Riesz representative of the linear form induced by
g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\cdot)
from (
6
), i.e.,

\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{g}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v})\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{with}\quad\boldsymbol{g}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v}):=\begin{cases}\gamma\,v_{3}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}&\gamma\geqslant 0,\\
|\gamma|\,(v_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}+v_{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2})&\gamma<0.\end{cases}

Lemma 2.4
.

In strong form, the Euler–Lagrange equations associated with the minimization problem for
\mathcal{J}
in (
7
) are

(11)

-\Delta_{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{g}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{u})=\big(|\nabla_{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})\big)\boldsymbol{u}\quad\text{in }\Omega,

subject to the pointwise constraint
|\boldsymbol{u}|=1
in
\Omega
and the natural boundary condition

(12)

\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\boldsymbol{u}=\kappa\boldsymbol{n}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{u},\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

where
\boldsymbol{n}
denotes the unit outward normal on
\partial\Omega
.

Moreover, a function
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{M}
is a weak solution of (
11
) if and only if it satisfies the variational identity

(13)

\int_{\Omega}\big[(\boldsymbol{u}\!\times\!\nabla_{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{u}):\nabla_{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{v}+g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{u})\big]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=0\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)\cap\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Proof 2.5

(Sketch of proof)
.

The argument parallels the harmonic-map case.
First, consider an appropriate variation of the energy to derive the strong Euler–Lagrange equations (
11
).
Next, insert the vector field
\boldsymbol{v}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{u}
as a test function in the weak formulation and employ standard vector identities to show that any weak solution of the Euler–Lagrange equations satisfies (
13
).
The converse implication is obtained by the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations.

3.
Energy minimization

3.1.
Computation of minimizers

Finding (numerical) solutions of (
7
) is challenging for two main reasons.
First, the pointwise constraint
|\boldsymbol{u}(x)|=1
a.e. in
\Omega
is non-convex, so standard minimization algorithms designed for convex constraints do not apply.
Second, minimizers may fail to be unique (for instance, when
\kappa=0
and
\gamma\geq 0
the energy is invariant under orthogonal transformations that fix the
\mathbf{e}_{3}
-axis).
For these reasons, a direct attack on the Euler–Lagrange equations is generally not viable.

To handle the unit-length constraint, we use the nearest-point projection onto the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, i.e.,

(14)

\Pi\colon\mathcal{M}^{+}\to\mathcal{M},\,\Pi\boldsymbol{v}\coloneqq\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{|\boldsymbol{v}|},\quad\text{where }\mathcal{M}^{+}\coloneqq\big\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)\mathrel{\big|}|\boldsymbol{v}|\geqslant 1\text{ a.e.\ in }\Omega\big\}.

For variational and numerical purposes, it is convenient to denote by
\mathcal{K}[\boldsymbol{u}]
the tangent space to a given function
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{M}
:

(15)

\mathcal{K}[\boldsymbol{u}]:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)\mathrel{|}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0\text{ a.e.\ in }\Omega\}.

While
\mathcal{M}
is non-convex (though weakly closed) in
\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)
,
\mathcal{K}[\boldsymbol{u}]
is a closed linear subspace of
\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)
and hence a Hilbert space for each
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{M}
; this makes
\mathcal{K}[\boldsymbol{u}]
a natural space for linearized computations around
\boldsymbol{u}
.

Finally, we introduce the symmetric bilinear form

(16)

a(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v}):=\int_{\Omega}\bigl[\nabla_{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{w}:\nabla_{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{v}+g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v})\bigr]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega).

With these ingredients (the projection
\Pi
, the tangent spaces
\mathcal{K}[\boldsymbol{u}]
, and the bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
) we adapt and generalize the numerical scheme from
[
Alo97
]
to compute minimizers of (
5
).

{algorithm}

[Continuous energy minimization]

Input:
Initial guess
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}\in\mathcal{M}
.

Loop:
For all
n=0,1,2,\ldots
, do

(i)

Find
\boldsymbol{w}^{n}\in\mathcal{K}[\boldsymbol{u}^{n}]
such that

(17)

a(\boldsymbol{w}^{n},\boldsymbol{v})=a(\boldsymbol{u}^{n},\boldsymbol{v})\qquad\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{K}[\boldsymbol{u}^{n}].

(ii)

Set
\boldsymbol{u}^{n+1}:=\Pi(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{w}^{n})
.

Output:
Sequence of functions
(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}
.

Our main result states well-posedness and convergence of Algorithm
3.1
to a critical point of
\mathcal{J}
.
The proof is postponed to Subsection
3.2
below.

Theorem 3.1
.

Algorithm
3.1
is well-defined, i.e., for any
\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\in\mathcal{M}
, the variational formulation (
17
) admits at least one solution
\boldsymbol{w}^{n}\in\mathcal{K}[\boldsymbol{u}^{n}]
and
\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{w}^{n}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}
.
For any sequence
(\boldsymbol{u}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by Algorithm
3.1
, there exists a subsequence (not relabeled) and a function
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{M}
such that, along this subsequence,

(18)

\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\rightharpoonup\boldsymbol{u}\quad\text{weakly in }\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)\text{ as }n\to\infty

and
\boldsymbol{u}
is a solution of the Euler–Lagrange equations (
11
) of
\mathcal{J}
.

While the existence of a solution
\boldsymbol{w}^{n}\in\mathcal{K}[\boldsymbol{u}^{n}]
to (
17
) is ensured by Theorem
3.1
, uniqueness might fail in general. Clearly,
uniqueness of the solution to (
17
) is guaranteed when Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed on
\partial\Omega
(or at least on a nontrivial portion of
\partial\Omega
), as considered in
[
Alo97
,
Bar05
]
.

As our analysis is concerned with natural boundary conditions (cf. (
12
)), the uniqueness of step (i) in Algorithm
3.1
cannot be taken for granted.
In fact, for certain parameter regimes, additional constraints on
\boldsymbol{w}^{n}
are required to secure uniqueness.
The precise circumstances under which non-uniqueness may occur are characterized in point (a) of the proposition below.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose that
\Omega
is connected.
Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{M}
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\in\mathcal{K}[\boldsymbol{u}]
be two solutions of the Euler–Lagrange equations (
17
).
Then, there hold the following assertions:

(a)

For
\kappa=0
, the difference
\boldsymbol{w}_{1}-\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\in\mathcal{K}[\boldsymbol{u}]
is constant and satisfies

•

\boldsymbol{w}_{1}-\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\perp\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}
a.e. in
\Omega
if
\gamma>0
,

•

\boldsymbol{w}_{1}-\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\in\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}\}
a.e. in
\Omega
if
\gamma<0
.

(b)

For
\kappa\neq 0
, i.e., in the presence of the anti-symmetric exchange term, the difference
\boldsymbol{w}_{1}-\boldsymbol{w}_{2}
vanishes.
In particular, the solution of (
17
) is unique.

Proof 3.3
.

Since
\mathcal{K}[\boldsymbol{u}]
is a linear space, there holds
\boldsymbol{w}_{1}-\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\in\mathcal{K}[\boldsymbol{u}]
.
By assumption,

a(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}-\boldsymbol{w}_{2},\boldsymbol{v})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:weakELbilinear}}}{{=}}0\qquad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{K}[\boldsymbol{u}].

For
\boldsymbol{v}:=\boldsymbol{w}_{1}-\boldsymbol{w}_{2}
, we obtain

(19)

0=a(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})=\int_{\Omega}\big[|\nabla_{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}+g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})\big]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Non-negativity of
g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})
implies
\nabla_{\mathfrak{h}}\boldsymbol{v}=0=g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})
a.e. in
\Omega
. In particular, we have

(20)

\partial_{i}\boldsymbol{v}+\kappa\,\boldsymbol{v}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{i}=0\qquad\text{for }i=1,2.

Taking the dot product of (
20
) with
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
, we obtain that

(21)

\begin{split}&\mathllap{\mathrm{(a)}}\penalty\ \penalty\ \partial_{1}v_{1}\phantom{{}-\kappa\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}}=0,\qquad\mathllap{\mathrm{(c)}}\penalty\ \penalty\ \partial_{1}v_{2}+\kappa\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}=0,\qquad\mathllap{\mathrm{(e)}}\penalty\ \penalty\ \partial_{1}v_{3}-\kappa\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}=0,\\
&\mathllap{\mathrm{(b)}}\penalty\ \penalty\ \partial_{2}v_{1}-\kappa\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}=0,\qquad\mathllap{\mathrm{(d)}}\penalty\ \penalty\ \partial_{2}v_{2}\phantom{{}+\kappa\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}}=0,\qquad\mathllap{\mathrm{(f)}}\penalty\ \penalty\ \partial_{2}v_{3}+\kappa\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}=0.\end{split}

Next, we now consider the assertions
(a)–(b)
separately and exploit
g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})=0
.
Recall that
g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})\simeq|\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}|^{2}
for
\gamma>0
and
g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})\simeq|\boldsymbol{v}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}|^{2}
for
\gamma<0
.

(a)
: For
\kappa=0
, (
21
) simplifies to
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}
; hence,
\boldsymbol{v}
is constant.
For
\gamma>0
, we have that
v_{3}=0
a.e. in
\Omega
and hence
\boldsymbol{v}\perp\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}
.
For
\gamma<0
, we have
v_{1}=0=v_{2}
a.e. in
\Omega
and hence
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}\}
.

(b)
: For
\kappa\neq 0
, we examine the sign of
\gamma
.
For
\gamma>0
, there holds
v_{3}=0
.
Column
(
21
e–f
)
implies that also
v_{1}=v_{2}=0
, and hence
\boldsymbol{v}=0
.
For
\gamma<0
, we have
v_{1}=0=v_{2}
.
By
(
21
a–d
)
, there follows
v_{3}=0
and hence
\boldsymbol{v}=0
.
For
\gamma=0
, the relations
\partial_{1}v_{1}=\partial_{2}v_{2}=0
prove that
v_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})=v_{1}(x_{2})
and
v_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=v_{2}(x_{1})
, i.e.,
v_{1}
is independent of
x_{1}
while
v_{2}
is independent of
x_{2}
.
Adding
(
21
b–c
)
, we see
\partial_{1}v_{2}(x_{1})+\partial_{2}v_{1}(x_{2})=0
for every
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\Omega
.
This implies that
\partial_{1}v_{2}(x_{1})
and
\partial_{2}v_{1}(x_{2})
are both constant.
Hence,
(
21
b
)
yields that
\kappa\,v_{3}=\partial_{2}v_{1}(x_{2})
is constant.
From
\kappa\neq 0

Lemma 3.6

\displaystyle|\nabla_{\mathfrak{h}}\Pi(\boldsymbol{u})|^{2}

For all

4.1.
Algorithm

In this section, we extend the approach of
[
Bar05
]
.
To this end, let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a conforming triangulation of
\Omega
into compact non-degenerate triangles.
Let
\mathcal{N}_{h}
be the set of nodes of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
We define the lowest-order finite element space

\mathcal{S}^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{h}):=\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\mathrel{|}v|_{T}\text{ is affine }\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

A convenient basis of
\mathcal{S}^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
is the so-called nodal basis
\{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}}\mathrel{|}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}\in\mathcal{N}_{h}\}
consisting of hat functions, i.e.,
\mathcal{T}_{h}

\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}^{\prime})=\delta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}^{\prime}}
for all
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{h}
.
A vector-valued finite element function
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{h}):=[\mathcal{S}^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{h})]^{3}
and its Jacobian can then be written as

\boldsymbol{v}_{h}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}\in\mathcal{N}_{h}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}})\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}\in\mathcal{N}_{h}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}})\nabla\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}},

respectively, where the column vectors
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are the values at the nodes
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}\in\mathcal{N}_{h}
; note that
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}
is pointwise in
\mathbb{R}^{3\!\times\!2}
with
(\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{jk}=\partial_{k}v_{h,j}

For
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}^{1}}(\mathcal{T}_{h})

|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}|=1
a.e. in
\Omega
can only be satisfied by constant functions.
Therefore, it is natural to prescribe the constraint only at the nodes.
In analogy to Section
3
, we define
\mathcal{M}_{h}:=\{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}^{1}}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\mathrel{|}|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}})|=1\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}\in\mathcal{N}_{h}\}
and
\mathcal{K}_{h}[\boldsymbol{u}_{h}]:=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}^{1}}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\mathrel{|}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}})\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}})=0\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}\in\mathcal{N}_{h}\}
as well as the nodal unit-length projection

(33)

\displaystyle\Pi_{h}

\displaystyle\colon\mathcal{M}_{h}^{+}\to\mathcal{M}_{h}\colon\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\mapsto\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}\in\mathcal{N}_{h}}\frac{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}})}{|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}})|}\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}},

However, there still holds
|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}|\leqslant 1

\Omega

\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\in\mathcal{M}_{h}

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}_{h}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})&:=\frac{1}{2}a_{h}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}^{1}}(\mathcal{T}_{h}),\text{ where }\\

Proof 4.5

The statements (a)–(b) follow analogously to the proof of Proposition

.
To show that the saddle point problem (

is coercive on the kernel of

To show surjectivity, it suffices to show that, for any
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\mu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}\}

To show coercivity of

on the kernel of
\mathcal{B}

\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathcal{K}_{h}[\boldsymbol{u}_{h}]
with
\mathcal{B}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}

Remark 4.11

\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)

For the mass lumping term, we first notice that

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{I}_{h}(v^{h})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}v^{h}-v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{I}_{h}-1)v^{h}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Since

\int_{\Omega}|v^{h}-v|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\lesssim(\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)})\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\to 0.

The third term can be treated by the local approximation property of the nodal interpolation on each element, which yields that

\int_{T}\big|(\mathcal{I}_{h}-1)v^{h}\big|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leqslant|T|^{1/2}\lVert(\mathcal{I}_{h}-1)v^{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(T)}\lesssim|T|^{1/2}h^{2}\lVert D^{2}v^{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(T)}.

in detail, we see that, regardless of the sign of

, this expression is a sum of products of the form

for some

For the Hessian of such a product, we obtain with

,
\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}=1
, and

that

\displaystyle\lVert D^{2}(u_{h,i}u_{h,j}\varphi_{k})\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(T)}

\displaystyle\qquad=\lVert u_{h,i}u_{h,j}D^{2}\varphi_{k}+\varphi_{k}\nabla u_{h,i}\nabla u_{h,j}^{\intercal}+u_{h,j}\nabla u_{h,i}\nabla\varphi_{k}^{\intercal}+u_{h,i}\nabla u_{h,j}\nabla\varphi_{k}^{\intercal}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(T)}

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\big(\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\big)\lVert\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{W}^{2,\infty}(T)}\lesssim 1.

Combining the estimates (
48
)–(
49
) and summing over all elements, we conclude that

(50)

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{I}_{h}\big(g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h\to 0}}{{\longrightarrow}}\quad\int_{\Omega}g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\!\times\!\boldsymbol{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Step 6.2 (Right-hand side of (

We first observe that there holds

An explicit computation in the spirit of (

Thus, summing over all elements, we get that

\lVert\nabla(\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim h\lVert\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{W}^{2,\infty}(\Omega)}\to 0\quad\text{as }h\to 0.

Recall that

\lVert\nabla(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\mathcal{J}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})^{1/2}+\mathcal{J}_{h}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h})^{1/2}\leqslant C_{0}^{1/2}+\mathrm{tol}_{h}^{1/2}

\int_{\Omega}\big[\nabla(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{w}_{h}):\nabla(\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})+\mathcal{I}_{h}(g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{w}_{h},\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}))\big]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\to 0\quad\text{as $h\to 0$.}

With
\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leqslant\mathrm{tol}_{h}\to 0
and the boundedness of

38

Analogously to (

Therefore, it follows that

\displaystyle\liminf_{h\to 0}\mathcal{J}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})

\displaystyle=\liminf_{h\to 0}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}:\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{I}_{h}\big(g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{\Omega}\big[\nabla\boldsymbol{u}:\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+g_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})\big]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u}).

This concludes the proof.

Note that using mass lumping for the discrete energy is necessary in order to prove Lemma

Without the nodal projection, energy decrease for the

4.5.

To test our algorithm in the case of

This work considers the three dimensional micromagnetic model

While

Furthermore, we consider the anti-symmetric exchange term

The material parameters are those of cobalt, i.e.,

, and

.
The strength of the anti-symmetric exchange term is varied in the range

.
The computational domain
\Omega
is a disk of diameter

within the plane spanned by
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}

[

]

With the exchange length

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3},

Remark 4.16

We note that considering the two-dimensional curl

Remark 4.17
.

Numerically, the initial configuration (
55
) is meta-stable for
D=0
in the thin-film case.
In the thin-film 2D model of
[
DDPR22
]
, the stray-field contribution is approximated by
|\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}|^{2}
, which causes the stray field of
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{h}
to be parallel to
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}
.
Together with
\Delta\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{h}
and anisotropy also being parallel to
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}\perp\mathcal{K}_{h}[\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{h}]
, the initial tangential update
\boldsymbol{w}_{h}
vanishes.
In 3D, however, the stray field would not be pointing purely in the
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}
direction because of boundary effects, thus exerting a small force perpendicular to
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}
and perturbing that initial state.
For this reason, we introduced a small divergent vector field for the disc part
r<$15\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}$
to artificially perturb the initial state:

\boldsymbol{u}^{0}(x,y)=\Pi_{h}\big(\varepsilon\cdot(x,y,0)^{\intercal}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{3}\big)
for some small
\varepsilon>0
.
For
D=0
, this proved sufficient to prevent the system from being trapped in the initial meta-stable state.
For
D\neq 0
, this modification is not necessary.
