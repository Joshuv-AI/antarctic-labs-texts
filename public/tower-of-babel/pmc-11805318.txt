Assessment of Marginal Fit and Accuracy of Crowns Fabricated Using CADCAM Milling and 3D Printing Technology
Authors: M.V. Sunil Kumar, Rajesh Kumar, Ravindher S. Saini, Rajesh Vyas, Saraswathi Bai, Sunil K. Vaddamanu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:This study evaluates the accuracy of marginal and internal fits of dental crowns fabricated using CAD/CAM milling and 3D printing technologies. The hypothesis posited significant differences between these techniques, confirmed by data showing notable disparities.Materials and Methods:Using the silicone replica technique, 44 points on each crown were measured, with seven crowns per method to ensure reliability.Results:Results indicated that 3D printing demonstrated superior precision, with the smallest discrepancies in occlusal, axial, and marginal gaps. While milling faced challenges such as material wastage and potential ceramic microfractures, it remained a viable option.Conclusion:The findings suggest that 3D printing could significantly improve crown quality and patient outcomes, despite higher upfront costs. Further research is necessary to validate these results and explore the long-term efficacy and cost-effectiveness of these techniques. This study underscores the clinical implications of choosing appropriate crown fabrication methods, highlighting the potential of advanced CAD/CAM technologies to enhance dental prosthetic manufacturing.

INTRODUCTION

The integration of computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technologies has revolutionized the field of dental prosthetics, offering advanced methods for the fabrication of crowns and fixed partial dentures.[1] Traditionally, the lost-wax technique has been the cornerstone of crown fabrication, but it is labor-intensive and requires a high level of expertise.[2] This method also suffers from limitations such as wax pattern distortions and material detection difficulties due to the lustrous nature of wax. Furthermore, properties such as thermosensitivity and elastic memory of wax can hinder the effectiveness of the final product.[3]

In recent years, CAD/CAM technologies, encompassing subtractive and additive manufacturing, have emerged as promising alternatives. Subtractive manufacturing, which includes milling, utilizes computer numerical control (CNC) milling machines to sculpt crowns from solid blocks of material. This method offers improved restoration quality but faces challenges such as material wastage, tool wear, and potential ceramic microfractures. Conversely, additive manufacturing, particularly 3D printing, constructs objects layer by layer from digital schematics, handling intricate geometries and minimizing waste. However, it can introduce minor deviations due to scanning limitations.[4]

Empirical studies on the precision and durability of digitally produced restorations have yielded mixed results. Some research highlights the superior accuracy of CAD/CAM techniques, while others indicate they perform less favorably compared to traditional methods. This inconsistency underscores the need for comprehensive data analysis to better understand the capabilities and limitations of these advanced fabrication methods.[5]

The success of dental crowns is critically dependent on their marginal fit, as a poor fit can lead to significant clinical issues such as cement solubility, plaque retention, secondary caries, gingival inflammation, and restoration or tooth failure. Ensuring a precise internal fit and optimal seating is essential for the longevity and effectiveness of the crown. Parameters such as internal fit at axial and occlusal points, marginal gap, and absolute marginal discrepancy are crucial indicators of the quality of the restoration.[67]

This study aims to compare the fit accuracy of dental crowns produced by CAD/CAM milling and 3D printing. We hypothesize that there are notable differences in fit accuracy between these techniques. By analyzing these differences, we hope to elucidate the strengths and nuances of each method, thereby contributing valuable insights to the field of dental restoration manufacturing. Our goal is to enhance patient outcomes and by applying improved prosthesis manufacturing techniques.

MATERIALS AND METHOD

A typodont maxillary first molar with shoulder margin was used for the study purpose. Dies was prepared by replicating this molar by using the duplicating silica. The 4 stone was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, manipulated under a vacuum mixer to eliminate the air bubbles, and poured into the duplicate silicone. After setting, dies were retrieved. Seven dies were selected for each method.

For subtractive techniques, dies were scanned using the laboratory CADCAM software, and the digital replica was designed using Exocad software. The copings were designed with an ideal measurements like 25 μm die spacing. The coping were fabricated using Ceramil Motion 2 and Ceramill ZI discs.

For the additive manufacturing technique, the. stl file was exported from the CAD system for 3D printing. BEGO Verseo L system was used for printing the coping with 0.7 mm thickness. The occlusal surface was excluded from the design to focus on marginal fit and internal adaptation.

The conventional cementation technique was used to measure the marginal adaptation. The coping was cemented on the dies by using the luting RelyX luting cement. The cemented copings were sectioned in the middle, mesiodistally by using the precision saw. These sections were examined under a scanning electronic microscope with a setting magnification of 1x.

Statistical analysis was performed by using the two-way ANOVA, Tukey’s HSD, and t-tests in SPSS (P ≤ 0.05).

DISCUSSION

This study compared the marginal and internal fit accuracy of dental crowns produced by CAD/CAM milling and 3D printing technologies. The results confirmed the hypothesis, showing that 3D printing provided superior precision across all measured points, including occlusal, axial, and marginal gaps. The layer-by-layer construction in 3D printing allows for meticulous control, which likely contributes to its enhanced accuracy.[8910]

However, both techniques have limitations. The silicone replica method, while reliable, can tear easily, complicating precise measurements. Conversely, the Rely-X luting cement used in conventional methods can affect viscosity and accuracy due to its unique composition. Minor defects from precision saw sectioning also influence readings.

Milling demonstrated commendable results but faced challenges like material wastage and potential ceramic microfractures inherent to its subtractive nature. Despite these issues, milling remains viable, given its established use in dental labs and technician familiarity.[1112]

The study’s findings underscore the clinical implications of choosing the right crown fabrication method. 3D printing’s superior consistency and reduced need for adjustments suggest it could improve crown quality and patient outcomes. However, the high upfront costs of 3D printers and CAD/CAM milling machines must be considered. While promising, 3D printing requires further research to fully realize its potential.[1314]

Moreover, the variability in results between different studies highlights the need for comprehensive, large-scale investigations to validate these findings. Future research should focus on assessing the long-term efficacy and cost-effectiveness of these techniques in diverse clinical settings. Studies involving larger sample sizes and varied clinical conditions will be crucial in establishing definitive conclusions about the best practices for crown fabrication.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrates that 3D printing provides superior marginal and internal fit accuracy for dental crowns compared to milling. While 3D printing shows promise for enhancing crown quality and patient outcomes, practical implementation requires consideration of costs and further research to validate long-term efficacy. These findings highlight the potential of advanced CAD/CAM technologies to improve dental prosthetic manufacturing.
