Global vulnerability assessment of mobile telecommunications infrastructure to climate hazards using crowdsourced open data
Authors: Edward J. Oughton, Tom Russell, Jeongjin Oh, Sara Ballan, Jim W. Hall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate change is intensifying coastal flooding, riverine flooding and tropical cyclones, yet risks to telecommunications infrastructure remain poorly quantified despite growing societal dependence on digital connectivity. We present a global, event-based geospatial assessment of mobile cellular infrastructure vulnerability, combining open crowdsourced asset data with global climate hazard models under historical conditions and future scenarios, RCP4.5 and RCP8.5. We analyse approximately 19.7 million mobile basestations globally, estimating exposure and direct damage across a range of event return periods. Under RCP8.5 in 2050, a 0.1% annual probability tropical cyclone event affects an estimated 3.7 million basestations, causing USD 44.7 billion in direct damage. Under RCP8.5 in 2080, equivalent coastal flooding affects 268,000 basestations, causing USD 6.44 billion in damage. Riverine flooding affects more assets globally, approximately 2.8 million, but damages remain broadly stable at USD 46–47 billion. Telecommunications infrastructure assets should therefore be systematically incorporated into climate risk assessments.

Introduction

Climate change is one of the major challenges of our time, with society and the economy needing to rapidly adapt to an increase in the frequency and severity of disasters1–4. Adaptation of critical infrastructure is therefore essential, particularly for lifeline utilities, such as energy, transportation, water, waste, and telecommunications networks5–9. However, infrastructure assessment research focusing on climate vulnerability has largely neglected telecommunication infrastructure, such as mobile cellular networks.

Indeed, whereas there have been evaluations for other critical infrastructure sectors such as transportation10–12 or power13, the global vulnerability assessment of telecommunication infrastructure from natural disasters has received little systematic consideration in the literature, often due to a lack of data. Cell site locations derived from OpenStreetMap consist of only approximately 141,478 communication towers and 80,750 masts globally14, only a tiny fraction of global sites. Thus, the focus has been on field-based post-disaster damage evaluation, following flooding or tropical cyclones. Such damage assessments include the Katrina, Gustav, Ike and Harvey hurricanes, Chile’s 2011 earthquake and tsunami, New Zealand’s 2011 earthquake in Christchurch, and the Great Earthquake and Tsunami in the Tohoku Region of Japan15–20. More recent critical infrastructure assessments have excluded telecommunication infrastructure21, despite the vital role played in natural hazard preparedness and resilience, both through early warning and recovery22–24.

Generally, two broad failure mechanisms dominate the impacts of natural hazards on cellular telecommunications infrastructure. First, direct exposure to floodwaters and extreme winds can damage active electronic components, including radio equipment, backup generators, and fuel storage, leading to partial or complete service disruption. Post-event assessments following Hurricane Katrina illustrate that while elevating some cellular equipment reduced recovery times in parts of New Orleans, inconsistent construction practices often left critical components at ground level vulnerable to inundation, particularly energy supply equipment. Second, indirect logistical failures, most notably interruptions to fuel supply chains for backup generators, can lead to extended power outages even if physical damage to communications equipment is limited25. When such failures occur, recovery and reconstruction costs can be substantial. For example, following Hurricane Matthew in The Bahamas, telecommunications infrastructure accounted for up to 63% of total infrastructure replacement costs, driven by widespread flooding and wind damage26.

Cell phone towers have typically been built to withstand certain hazard exposures (e.g., wind speeds) above which they may fail. However, they are still vulnerable and at risk from hazards below these thresholds too, for example, via high wind speeds during hurricanes. Throughout Hurricane Maria in 2018, winds of up to 280 km/h felled more than 90 percent of the cell phone towers in Puerto Rico. Risks are more moderate at lower wind speeds, with only ~25% of towers downed by ~80 mph winds during Hurricane Sandy across affected regions27. Unfortunately, this communication disruption hinders coordination and decision-making, as disaster response professionals and resources are not directed to areas most in need28,29.

The contribution of this paper is to undertake a global assessment of the climate hazard vulnerability of mobile cellular infrastructure assets. This is important because quantitative scenario analysis can produce geospatial hazard information that can be used in multiple different ways. Firstly, such information can help support adaptation decisions by policymakers and/or Mobile Network Operators, with regard to strengthening existing infrastructure assets30. Secondly, such information can inform location planning of future assets, along with building design codes for extra protection in areas of higher vulnerability from multi-hazard threats31,32. Finally, hazard information can enable risk exposure to be shared among many different parties, for example, by businesses and households sharing risk with insurers (who in turn share risk with re-insurers)33,34.

Discussion

The magnitude and spatial distribution of impacts identified here are broadly consistent with earlier post-event assessments and sectoral studies, which have documented the sensitivity of telecommunications infrastructure to flooding and wind hazards. However, previous work has largely been limited to local or national case studies35,36, often focusing on individual disaster events37,38. By contrast, this analysis provides a global, multi-hazard risk assessment of mobile cellular infrastructure, placing telecommunications on a comparable analytical footing with transport and energy infrastructure in global risk assessments.

Importantly, the results reinforce earlier qualitative findings that telecommunications assets can experience widespread disruption even when direct physical damage is limited, particularly for wind hazards. At the same time, the high direct damage costs associated with riverine flooding align with evidence from major flood events, where recovery requires extensive replacement of electrical and electronic components.

A key limitation of this analysis is that it estimates direct physical damage only, and does not account for losses arising from reduced or lost network functionality. As documented in the literature, service outages may result from power loss, fuel shortages, congestion, or backhaul failure even when physical damage to basestations is minimal. The omission of functional losses implies that the reported damage costs are likely to represent a lower bound of total economic impacts. Empirical evidence from past disasters suggests that indirect and functional losses can exceed direct costs by a factor of 2–3, depending on event duration, population exposure, and economic dependence on connectivity39,40. Given that tropical cyclones are estimated to affect over 3.7 million basestations in extreme future scenarios, even short-duration outages could translate into substantial unquantified economic and social costs. Incorporating functional loss modelling therefore remains a critical priority for future research.

Uncertainty in the results arises from multiple interacting sources. First, climate model uncertainty varies across hazards. For example, riverine flooding exhibits a spatially heterogeneous response to future climate scenarios. Across the countries included, mean total riverine flooded area increases under future climate scenarios relative to the historical baseline, depending on event probability. However, affected basestation counts increase by less than 1%. This divergence is consistent with changes in the geographical distribution of inundation, because increases in total flooded area do not necessarily produce proportional increases in the intersection between flooding and mapped infrastructure. Estimated damage is additionally influenced by flood depth at exposed locations. In contrast, coastal flooding and tropical cyclones show more pronounced increases under high-emissions scenarios, driven by sea-level rise and changes in extreme wind characteristics, respectively. Second, spatial resolution differences contribute to uncertainty. Flood hazard layers are typically resolved at ~1 km, while tropical cyclone wind fields are resolved at ~10 km. These resolutions may smooth local extremes, particularly in complex coastal or urban environments, potentially underestimating peak hazard intensity at individual sites. As a result, localized damage hotspots may not be fully captured.

Uncertainty also arises from the crowdsourced nature of the mobile infrastructure dataset. While OpenCelliD provides unprecedented global coverage, basestation locations are not always precisely measured. Indeed, positions are estimated rather than surveyed, meaning for some cells positional uncertainty may lead to misclassification of hazard exposure, particularly near flood boundaries or coastlines. At global scale, such errors are expected to average out, but they will likely affect results at finer spatial resolutions. Additionally, data completeness varies substantially by region. In low-income regions, particularly parts of Africa, the estimated impacts for some hazards are low or negligible. This likely reflects underrepresentation of assets rather than genuinely low exposure, implying that regional and income-group comparisons should be interpreted with caution. Improved asset inventories in data-scarce regions would likely increase estimated exposure and damage in future assessments.

The use of generic exposure–damage curves, adapted from structurally similar infrastructure, represents another important limitation. While this approach is consistent with existing infrastructure risk assessments, it does not explicitly capture telecommunications-specific failure modes or recovery dynamics. The failure mechanisms identified in the literature provide valuable context for interpreting the damage estimates (from loss of backup power to catastrophic backhaul failure), but are not modelled explicitly. Future research could improve reliability by (i) developing telecommunications-specific fragility and functionality loss models, (ii) integrating higher-resolution hazard datasets where available, (iii) incorporating network topology and interdependencies with power and transport systems, and (iv) improving global asset inventories through hybrid crowdsourced and remote sensing approaches. While many uncertainties are unavoidable in global analysis, targeted methodological advances would enable more comprehensive and policy-relevant assessments of climate risks to digital infrastructure.

To conclude, in this paper, a global assessment was undertaken evaluating the potential impacts to mobile cellular infrastructure. Using open crowdsourced data equating to 19.7 million 2G, 3G, and 4G unique base stations, potentially damaged assets and direct damage costs were estimated for two climate scenarios (RCP4.5 and RCP8.5) against the historical baseline. The results were reported for a range of return-period hazard maps with different annual probabilities.

In the baseline historical scenarios for an event with a 0.1% annual probability, the largest number of affected basestations was found to be from tropical cyclones (2.95 m), followed by riverine flooding (2.81 m) and coastal flooding (153.9 thousand). However, the number of affected basestations did not necessarily yield the same magnitude in terms of direct damage impacts. For example, the historical estimated damage cost was $40.8 billion for tropical cyclones, $46.4 billion for riverine flooding, followed by $3.5 billion for coastal flooding. The results suggest that many basestations can be affected by high winds from tropical cyclones but may only receive very minor damage.

When examining the impacts under future climate scenarios, the results suggest that tropical cyclones and coastal flooding show the largest projected increases in the most severe high emissions futures explored. For example, for a 0.1% annual probability event under RCP8.5, the number of affected basestations is estimated to rise to 3.7 million for tropical cyclones (an increase of 25%), equating to $44.7 billion in direct damage (an increase of 10%). Equally, for coastal flooding, the number of potentially affected basestations for an event with a 0.1% annual probability under RCP8.5 is 267.8 thousand (an increase of 74%), equating to direct damage costs of $6.44 billion (an increase of 84%). Riverine infrastructure impacts remain comparatively stable for the countries included despite an increase in total flooded area, with substantial variation among countries and climate models. This pattern is consistent with changes in the geographical distribution of inundation.

The findings demonstrate the need for risk analysts to include mobile communications (and telecommunications more broadly) in future critical infrastructure assessments. Indeed, this paper contributes a proven assessment methodology to the literature for use in future research for assessing this critical infrastructure sector.

Climate hazard data

For coastal and riverine flooding, we use Aqueduct flood hazard maps, which are derived from ensembles of CMIP5 (Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 5) global circulation models, downscaled and used to force global hydrological and hydraulic models41. These layers are available for two climate scenarios (as well as a 1980 historical baseline), from the CMIP5 archive, including (i) Representative Concentration Pathway 4.5 (steady carbon emissions) (intermediate climate outcomes) (RCP 4.5) and (ii) Representative Concentration Pathway 8.5 (rising carbon emissions) (limited climate outcomes) (RCP 8.5).

The former scenario (RCP 4.5) represents a mid-range pathway involving modest carbon mitigation by 2040 onwards and is the most likely Earth’s current global trajectory42,43. Whereas the latter (RCP 8.5) reflects a worst-case of rising carbon emissions with little carbon mitigation taking place. Four main return periods are also selected for assessment, including events equating to 1% annual probability (1-in-100-year), 0.4% annual probability (1-in-250-year), 0.2% annual probability (1-in-500-year), and the most extreme 0.1% annual probability (1-in-1000-year).

Coastal flooding in this analysis represents storm surge–driven coastal inundation superimposed on projected mean sea-level rise, as provided by the Aqueduct coastal flood hazard datasets41. The Aqueduct coastal flood layers are derived from hydrodynamic storm surge modelling, with sea-level rise projections taken from the RISES-AM framework and applied as static offsets to baseline water levels. Multiple sea-level rise percentiles are considered (5th, 50th and 95th) to capture uncertainty (including associated storm-surge inundation). Estimates of land subsidence are also incorporated. These coastal hazard layers do not explicitly account for wave run-up, coastal erosion, or compound flooding processes.

Riverine hazards in this evaluation represent overflow river flooding events, most likely to occur in river basins from excessive water loads. The Aqueduct river hazard modelling used the PCRaster Global Water Balance hydrological model to simulate flood inundation, with forcing data for future climate conditions taken from the Inter-sectoral Impact Model Intercomparison Project (ISI-MIP) (Hempel et al. 2013), which used five global circulation models (GCMs) from different institutions: (i) GFDL-ESM2M from the US Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory (NOAA), (ii) HadGEM2-es from the UK Met Office Hadley Centre, (iii) IPSL-CM5A-LR from the French Pierre-Simon Laplace Institute, (iv) MIROC-ESM-CHEM from the Japanese Atmosphere and Ocean Research Institute (The University of Tokyo), National Institute for Environmental Studies, and Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology, and (v) NorESM1-M from the Bjerknes Centre for Climate Research, at the Norwegian Meteorological Institute. The current analysis takes the ensemble of flood hazard maps provided by Aqueduct to obtain broad statistical estimates given each of these five global circulation model options.

Return period maps for maximum wind speeds experienced during tropical cyclones are obtained from Bloemendaal et al. 2020 and 2022 for present climate conditions and a single future epoch and climate scenario (2050s, SSP5-8.5), for a fixed set of return periods between 10 and 10,000 years, at 10 km resolution, for each cyclone basin46,47. The future climate conditions are derived from a set of general circulation models: CMCC-CM2-VHR4, CNRM-CM6-1-HR, EC-Earth3P-HR and HadGEM3-GC31-HM. These models are evaluated and briefly described in the literature48. The SSP (Shared Socio-economic Pathway) SSP5-8.5 is the scenario developed for CMIP6 (Phase 6 of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project), which corresponds most closely to the CMIP5 RCP8.5 scenario49. Each set of return period maps is calculated from 10,000 years of synthetic tracks modeled using the Synthetic Tropical cyclOne geneRation Model (STORM). The global maximum wind speed return period maps are mosaiced together across all basins to provide global hazard maps50.

Mobile infrastructure asset data

Crowdsourced data for December 2022 are obtained from OpenCelliD, covering approximately 19.7 million 2G, 3G, and 4G basestations globally. The Global System for Mobile Communications (GSM), commonly referred to as 2G, enables voice and Short Messaging System (SMS) services, and accounts for 11.9% of cellular basestations (2.35 million). Although increasingly obsolete in high-income countries, 2G remains widely used in many low- and middle-income countries. Third-generation (3G) technology consists primarily of the Universal Mobile Telecommunications System (UMTS) standard, enabling basic mobile data services (~3 Mbps), and accounting for 72.9% of the dataset (14.4 million). Fourth-generation (4G) cellular technology, known as Long Term Evolution (LTE), enabled mass-market mobile broadband with peak speeds of ~100 Mbps, accounting for 15.1% of the dataset (2.99 million). In this analysis, we additionally process 4G cells which share the same basestation identifier by taking the centroid of the points that share this information. By contrast, Fifth-generation (5G) technology has been deployed primarily since 202051, based on the 5G New Radio (NR) standard, supporting enhanced mobile broadband52,53. Due to limited commercial deployment for the time period assessed ( <3000 cells), this technology group is excluded from this analysis.

Vulnerability analysis

Mobile cellular towers generally consist of the three main design types identified within the literature review, including monopoles, self-supported structures, or guyed structures. These assets usually rely on steel or aluminium for the structural frame, utilizing concrete at the base of the tower either for the foundation plinth or to secure guy lines. Vulnerability to hazards and the consequential damage cost is estimated using an exposure-damage curve approach, which is a standard way in the literature to relate, for example, the depth of flooding inundation or wind speed to a potential direct damage cost54–57. However, there are not readily available damage curves for communication towers. Therefore, existing studies in the literature adopt a similar approach to assessing the vulnerability of electricity infrastructure assets, such as distribution poles and transmission pylons, which share similar structural characteristics58. Indeed, some studies utilize curves from the US HAZUS model54 for electricity infrastructure to assess the vulnerability of communications infrastructure assets59, as will be adopted here. Unfortunately, within the US HAZUS model, the development of fragility relationships specifically for telecommunications infrastructure assets based on inundation has been stated as deferred to a later date (p7-10), with no current update on the status of these improvements54 (thus, identifying an important area of future research).

For flooding, we adopt a depth-damage curve from the Netherlands, developed to represent flooding impacts on electricity and communication infrastructure systems58. Generally, smaller inundation depths (e.g., <0.3 m) expect only minimal damage (25%), but by the time the asset is submerged in 2 meters of water, complete failure is expected. This would include loss of active electronic equipment and the structure. Even if towers do not functionally collapse in a flood, it is expected there will be structural issues with the foundation due to scour erosion and other physical processes. For reference, the depth-damage curve can be found in Table S1 in the Supplementary Information Section S2.

For tropical storms, we adopt a wind-damage curve for the tallest available latticed electricity transmission tower (4YR23 N20C1)60, designed for Extra High Voltage routes >400 kV. This curve was also stated in a report for the World Bank focusing on engineering options for increased infrastructure resilience55. Although not exact for cell towers, there are strong structural similarities in materials and design, justifying usage here. For example, there is a very low probability of damage for wind speeds below 50 m/s (180 km/hr), increasing to failure at speeds exceeding 80 m/s (280 km/hr). We use this relatively conservative probability of failure curve to estimate a damage state based on wind exposure, which can then inform damage costs. For reference, the wind-damage curve can be found in Table S2 in Supplementary Information Section S2.

Subsequently, for each hazard, the investment costs for rebuilding mobile assets are adapted from the existing literature61. Broadly, the cost of building a new three-sector 4G macro cell site is approximately $100,000, equating to approximately $33,333 total capital expenditure per basestation. Thus, a cell with a flooding depth of 0.6 meters and a damage quantity of 0.5 leads to a direct damage cost estimate of $16,666.5. As identified in the literature review, the specifics of the damage depend on the cell tower construction. At the lower end of the fragility curve, damage to electronic equipment could take place. In more severe outcomes, this could include the full destruction of both active electronic radio equipment and passive civil engineering tower structures, requiring a total rebuild of a site62.

The intersection process is carried out using a range of Python packages. For example, managing spatial point data is carried out via geopandas, and then raster-based queries of the hazard layers are undertaken using a combination of rasterio and rasterstats. Flooded surface area was calculated by summing the geodesic area of inundated raster cells using the WGS84 ellipsoid, accounting for latitude variations in cell area. For each lower layer region, this information is exported to a comma-separated value file, and then later aggregated for reference in the following results section.
