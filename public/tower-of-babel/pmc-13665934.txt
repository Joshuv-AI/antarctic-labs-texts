Global conservation additionality hindered by persisting fishing pressure in marine protected areas
Authors: Millenne Ohanna, Juliette Jacquemont, Joachim Claudet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Marine protected areas (MPAs) are the cornerstone of global marine conservation strategies. Yet, their capacity to achieve conservation gains (i.e., conservation additionality) hinges on the decrease in human threats to biodiversity within their boundary, a feature rarely measured or reported. Here, we test a central but underexamined question in global conservation policy: Have MPA implementations actually reduced within-boundary fishing pressure, currently the main direct human threat to marine biodiversity? Using a stratified before-after-control-impact approach, we investigated 861 MPAs of varying levels of protection across all ocean basins, representing half of global MPA coverage, and found that most implementations were not followed by decreases in industrial fishing effort. Protection levels strongly predicted the direction and magnitude of relative changes in fishing effort after implementation. Only fully protected areas were associated with significant decreases in fishing effort within boundaries. Highly protected areas were only associated with lower rates of increase in fishing effort within compared to outside boundaries. Both fully and highly protected areas were implemented in areas experiencing initial low fishing effort, reducing the overall abatement in fishing effort achieved. Conversely, areas with higher initial fishing effort tended to be attributed to MPAs with low levels of protection, also limiting abatement. Larger MPAs were associated with greater decreases in fishing effort, while time since implementation had no significant effect. Complementing targets of protection coverage and protection levels with conservation additionality targets, such as pressure abatement, could shift incentives from quantity to effectiveness of interventions and ensure positive biodiversity conservation outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

The growing impacts of climate change and human activities on the ocean have led to widespread degradation of marine biodiversity and are threatening the capacity of the ocean to deliver critical ecosystem services to humanity (1–5). Among the strategies explored to reverse this trajectory (6, 7), area-based conservation measures have proven among the most effective at curbing impacts from local human activities (8, 9). The most prominent area-based conservation tools are marine protected areas (MPAs) (10), managed primarily for marine biodiversity conservation. The designation of MPAs has accelerated over the past two decades (11), driven by increasingly ambitious international targets. Notably, the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework has set the target of protecting 30% of the coastal and marine waters of the 196 parties within the Convention of Biological Diversity by 2030 (12). However, the outcomes of MPA expansion have routinely fallen far below expectations, largely because the quality of MPAs has been neglected in favor of ease of implementation and of the short-term interests of industrial sectors (13–15).

The predominance of quantity over quality of conservation measures is favored by the indicators used to track progress toward conservation targets, which have been largely limited to quantitative areal metrics, such as MPA coverage (14, 16). This indicator choice has incentivized states to prioritize the quantity of MPA coverage while neglecting qualitative criteria including effective management (17), level of protection (18), ecological representation (19), and equitable governance (20, 21). Beyond mere qualitative features, these criteria represent key enabling conditions for protected areas to deliver positive outcomes for nature and people (18).

The additionality of MPAs, i.e., the degree to which MPAs deliver net positive outcomes compared to a baseline scenario, hinges on the reduction of impactful human activities to marine biodiversity (22–24). Because fishing activities are the most widespread and impactful human activities to marine ecosystems (4, 25, 26) and can be regulated by MPAs, the reduction of fishing pressure within MPAs represents one of the largest levers to achieve conservation additionality (24, 27). Whether MPAs lead to a reduction in fishing pressure depends on multiple factors, including the stringency of regulations adopted (i.e., level of protection) (18, 28), their siting in low versus high fishing areas (27, 29), and enforcement capacity (17, 30). The development of frameworks defining levels of protection on the basis of the human uses allowed in MPAs (18, 28) has been paramount in providing a more comprehensive view of qualitative progress toward conservation targets and has revealed major conservation shortfalls (30–32). However, levels of protection alone do not inform on whether an MPA reduces human pressures, including fishing. MPAs with the highest levels of protection are often implemented far from human centers and in areas of low fishing pressure, a practice known as residual conservation (33, 34). Even under high levels of protection, MPAs sited in low-use areas have minimal to no effect on human pressures, greatly limiting their conservation additionality. Therefore, quantifying changes in fishing pressure following the implementation of MPAs has been suggested as a more direct and ecologically meaningful indicator of conservation progress (23, 35, 36).

Regional-scale studies, mostly conducted in European waters, have shown that most MPAs experience comparable or even higher levels of fishing effort than surrounding waters, particularly in partially protected areas where some extractive activities are permitted (22, 37–41). At the global scale, growing literature has quantified the level of fishing effort occurring in MPAs (38, 42) thanks to expanding databases leveraging tracking technologies, including Automatic Identification System (AIS) and satellite imagery. These studies have evidenced the widespread presence of industrial fishing activities inside MPAs (43) and that highly and fully protected areas experience lower levels of fishing effort (42). However, these assessments did not account for baseline levels of fishing effort existing before MPA implementation and, as such, do not test whether conservation initiatives have curbed human pressures. Assessing temporal changes in fishing effort following MPA implementation rather than fishing effort postimplementation alone is necessary to (i) test whether low levels of fishing effort in some MPAs are due to an actual reduction in fishing effort or to residual conservation, (ii) identify which MPA features are associated with the highest levels of fishing reduction, and (iii) evaluate whether MPA levels of protection can be used as proxies for a temporal decrease in fishing effort and, in turn, conservation additionality.

Here, we test a central yet underexamined premise of conservation science: whether MPAs, as currently designated and reported, effectively reduce fishing pressures, a requisite to achieve conservation additionality and subsequent in situ biodiversity gains. To do so, we evaluate the contribution of 861 MPAs implemented across ocean basins between 2014 and 2022 in reducing industrial fishing effort within their boundaries, which we propose as a new measure of conservation additionality. To control for biases that can affect the assessment of MPA outcomes, we normalize changes in fishing effort within boundaries with changes in fishing effort surrounding boundaries of each MPA. Last, we test whether established drivers of MPA effectiveness, including the level of protection, age, size, and location, also influence the magnitude of fishing effort abatement. We show that over the 2014 to 2024 decade, the global expansion of MPA coverage has resulted in a minimal decrease in industrial fishing effort within MPA boundaries, except in fully protected areas. Our findings highlight the importance of embedding additionality targets and requirements, such as pressure abatement, into conservation policies to shift incentives from quantity to effectiveness of interventions and ensure the delivery of positive biodiversity outcomes.

RESULTS

Industrial fishing effort dynamics following MPA implementation

To test whether MPA implementations lead to decreases in industrial fishing effort within their boundaries, we combined 12 years (2012 to 2024) of spatially explicit AIS fishing data from Global Fishing Watch (GFW) (44) with the distribution of MPAs reported by the World Database on Protected Areas (WDPA; fig. S1) and MPA Guide assessments (fig. S2) (31, 45–49).

The MPAs that satisfied the constraints imposed by the spatial resolution and time span of the GFW dataset (i.e., larger than 0.1 km2 and implemented between 2014 and 2022) represented half (49.3%) of the global MPA coverage (50), covering 14,257,793 km2 or 3.9% of the global ocean area (Fig. 1 and figs. S1 to S3). For each MPA, we calculated mean yearly values (n = 2 to 10 years, depending on MPAs’ implementation year) of industrial fishing effort (fishing hours km−2 year−1) occurring (i) within and (ii) surrounding their boundaries, both before and after MPA implementation (see Materials and Methods).

Across International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) categories, i.e., a classification system categorizing protected areas on the basis of their primary management goals, we found that most MPAs experienced an increase in fishing effort following their implementation (58% of MPAs studied and 66% of studied protection coverage; fig. S4). The remaining MPAs experienced either a decrease (40%) or an absence of change (2%) in fishing effort within their boundaries after implementation. We found no impact of the IUCN categories of MPAs (e.g., category IA “strict reserve” versus VI “sustainable uses”) on temporal changes in fishing effort (χ2 = 26.53, df = 28, P = 0.544).

Given the absence of relation between IUCN categories and changes in fishing effort, we focused on MPAs with known levels of protection from the MPA Guide (n = 517), a classification system based on the human activities allowed inside MPAs (18). We found again that more than half of these MPAs (55%) experienced an increase in fishing effort within their boundaries following implementation (Fig. 2). However, unlike IUCN categories, levels of protection had an impact on temporal changes in fishing effort (χ2 = 53.61, df = 24, P = 0.0005). Higher levels of protection were associated with a higher proportion of MPAs experiencing decreases in fishing effort within their boundaries following implementation [R2 (coefficient of determination) = 0.759; fig. S5]. This was the case for more than three quarters (78%) of fully protected areas (i.e., no fishing permitted) and more than half (54%) of highly protected areas (i.e., few, infrequent, and low-impact fishing gears permitted; Fig. 2A). By contrast, only a third (33%) of MPAs that are minimally protected (i.e., multiple and high-impact gears permitted) or incompatible with conservation (i.e., dredging, dumping, or mining activities permitted) experienced a decrease in fishing effort. Lightly protected (multiple and moderate-impact gears permitted) and unclassified MPAs (i.e., MPAs lacking sufficient documentation to determine the protection level) experienced similar proportions of increase (∼50%) and decrease (∼50%) in fishing effort (Fig. 2B).

Magnitude of change in industrial fishing effort

We quantified the direction and magnitude of change in industrial fishing following MPA implementation using logarithm response ratios (lnRRs) of mean yearly fishing effort (fishing hours km−2 year−1). We first quantified temporal changes inside and outside MPAs by calculating the lnRRs of mean fishing effort before and after MPA implementation in each of these areas (Fig. 3, B and C). We then normalized temporal changes occurring inside MPAs with temporal changes occurring outside MPAs to obtain a before-after-control-impact (BACI) impact assessment design (Fig. 3A). BACI impact assessment designs control for spatial and temporal biases that can otherwise affect the assessment of conservation outcomes (51, 52).

When considering all MPAs implemented between 2014 and 2022 (n = 517), we found that mean fishing effort was significantly higher after than before implementation both inside (lnRR = 0.3 ± 0.2; Fig. 3B) and outside MPA boundaries (lnRR = 0.4 ± 0.1; Fig. 3C). However, we found no significant change in fishing effort when normalizing temporal changes inside MPAs with temporal changes outside MPA boundaries (lnRR = −0.1 ± 1.2; Fig. 3A).

The direction and magnitude of change in fishing effort inside MPAs were correlated to levels of protection. Highest protection levels were associated with the strongest decreases in fishing effort and the lowest protection levels with the strongest increases (Fig. 3B). After controlling for outside trends, only the implementation of fully protected areas was associated with a significant decrease in fishing effort (BACI; lnRR = −1.1 ± 0.5; Fig. 3A and table S1). This outcome was driven by a decrease in fishing effort inside but not outside MPAs following implementation. In highly protected areas, fishing effort significantly increased outside but not within boundaries, resulting in a slight relative decrease in fishing effort within the MPAs (lnRR = −0.4 ± 0.4; Fig. 3A). By contrast, fishing effort significantly increased in minimally protected and unprotected areas, both inside (lnRR = 0.7 ± 0.5 and 0.6 ± 0.4, respectively) and outside (lnRR = 0.5 ± 0.3 and 0.7 ± 0.2) boundaries.

The number of fishing activities detected by GFW have increased over time because of the growing adoption of AIS by fishing vessels, its mandated use by some nations, and the improvement in AIS satellite reception and interference processing (fig. S6) (53). As a result, temporal changes in fishing efforts conflate increases in vessel detection with actual changes in fishing effort. While the BACI counterfactual design controls for this temporal bias, which should affect MPAs and their surroundings similarly, we ran additional analyses restricted to the 2018 to 2024 period, during which vessel detection stabilizes (fig. S7). Mixed-effects meta-regression models indicated no significant difference in effect sizes between the full (2012 to 2024) and restricted datasets (QM = 0.480, P = 0.49), and post hoc linear contrasts confirmed the absence of significant temporal changes within any protection levels (all P > 0.383; table S2). We found again no mean change in fishing effort across the 53 MPAs implemented between 2020 and 2022 and confirmed that fully protected areas experienced the highest decrease in fishing effort (lnRR = −1.0 ± 2.2, n = 4). However, low sample sizes prevented from testing the significance of results by level of protection.

Quantifying levels of fishing effort before MPA implementation

The absolute value of decrease in industrial fishing effort following MPA implementation results from two metrics: (i) the level of fishing effort existing before the MPA’s implementation and (ii) the subsequent relative change in fishing effort following MPA implementation. As such, the higher relative decreases in fishing effort (ratio of change) in fully and highly protected areas could still mask low absolute decreases (hours year−1) if preimplementation fishing efforts are low.

We found that fully and highly protected areas were implemented in areas experiencing lower fishing effort than MPAs with lower levels of protection (P < 0.01; Fig. 4 and table S3). Among all protection levels, highly protected areas were established in the areas with the lowest preimplementation fishing effort (0.3 hours km−2 year−1), whereas lightly protected areas were implemented in locations with the highest fishing effort (4.3 hours km−2 year−1; Fig. 4). Moreover, 14.7% of the studied MPAs (2.1% of studied protection coverage) were established in areas where no industrial fishing activity was found before implementation.

Drivers of changes in industrial fishing effort

In addition to protection levels, we evaluated the influence of size, age, and oceanic zone of MPAs on changes in industrial fishing effort (BACI lnRR; Fig. 5). We found a positive relation between size and decrease in fishing effort, with larger MPAs associated with higher decreases in fishing effort [LOESS (locally estimated scatterplot smoothing) regression, R2 = 0.015, P = 0.002; Fig. 5B]. We found a significant (P = 0.01) but nonlinear relationship between age and change in fishing effort (Fig. 5A). Decreases in fishing effort tended to increase with MPA age from 2 to 5 years but then to decrease with MPA age from 5 to 10 years. No significant relationship was found between MPA age and changes in fishing effort when stratifying analyses by protection level (fig. S8).

Mean changes in fishing effort were comparable between oceanic zones (coastal, offshore, or high seas; P > 0.05; Fig. 5C and table S4). However, in coastal zones (<12 nautical miles from shore), only fully protected areas displayed significant decreases in fishing effort (lnRR = −1.4 ± 0.7, n = 23), while in offshore zones (between 12 and 200 nm from shore), both fully (lnRR = −0.6 ± 0.5, n = 12) and highly (lnRR = −1.1 ± 0.8, n = 11) protected areas displayed significant decreases in fishing effort. No protection level displayed significant changes in fishing effort in the high seas, but the sample size was small (n = 7).

DISCUSSION

Limited decreases in fishing effort within MPAs

Our study does not challenge the demonstrated ecological effectiveness of fully protected and well-enforced MPAs but highlights a mechanism that hinders the capacity of many MPAs to achieve in situ positive conservation outcomes: the lack of pressure abatement. We found that industrial fishing effort remained unchanged or even increased following the implementation of most MPAs worldwide since 2014. This result was found under both temporal and spatiotemporal (BACI) counterfactual designs and was robust to sensitivity analyses (including to temporal increases in AIS coverage; see below). By adding a temporal dimension to previous spatial analyses of fishing activities occurring in MPA (38, 42, 43), we demonstrate not only that most MPAs are fished but also that most MPAs have not contributed to reducing fishing effort. This distinction is fundamental given that temporal changes in human pressures, rather than the final level of human pressure itself, best predict the ecological outcomes of a conservation measure (22, 54). In other fields of sustainability, such as carbon offsetting, demonstrating the net added benefits of interventions, a quality referred to as additionality, constitutes a requirement of reporting schemes (55). The absence of additionality requirements in conservation policies, both on land and in the ocean, has favored strategies that minimize short-term costs to industrial sectors, such as fisheries, over maximizing biodiversity gains. The resulting limited pressure abatement in protected areas can explain why limited ecological recovery is observed despite growing conservation coverage (54).

Residual conservation and its implications for additionality

Our findings reaffirm the prevalence of residual conservation practices, i.e., the biased placement of protected areas toward low-use areas, especially for MPAs with higher levels of protection (13, 27, 33, 34, 56). While previous studies have highlighted that little-to-no industrial fishing occurs in highly protected MPAs (42), our results suggest that this originates from the placement of MPAs in areas already subject to minimal fishing effort rather than from the abatement of preexisting fishing effort. While this strategy, which may reflect the influence of fisheries interest in conservation planning, may facilitate the rapid expansion of MPA coverage, it neglects the original objective of MPA implementation: achieving additional in situ positive biodiversity outcomes.

Drivers of pressure reduction versus drivers of ecological outcomes

We found that the level of protection was a strong predictor of the direction and magnitude of change in fishing effort following implementation. The correlation between higher levels of protection and a higher decrease in fishing effort demonstrates that in addition to protecting areas from potential future threats, highly and fully protected areas also contribute to decreasing pressures within their boundaries. We observed two mechanisms of relative pressure reduction: In fully protected areas, fishing effort decreased within boundaries, while in highly protected areas, fishing effort increased around but not inside boundaries. These mechanisms reflect two pathways through which additionality accrues: decreased versus avoided impacts (57). In either case, the higher reduction of fishing effort in fully and highly protected areas explains why positive biodiversity outcomes are most consistently found in those protected areas (32) and underscores the importance of prioritizing fully protected areas to achieve real additional conservation progress (9, 58–60).

We found that larger but not older MPAs were associated with higher reduction in fishing effort. Larger MPAs have also been associated with enhanced biodiversity outcomes (9, 60, 61), which is typically explained by ecological theories, including the higher proportion of species’ spatial range being protected and the reduction of edge effects (38, 62–64). Here, we show that the ecological effectiveness of larger MPAs also likely derives from higher reduction in fishing effort. This effect could be explained because larger MPAs tend to benefit from higher management capacity, their location tends to be better known by fishers and other users, and their scale might lead to more profound changes in users’ behavior and spatial preferences.

By contrast, while older MPAs have been repeatedly associated with higher ecological benefits (9, 60, 61), we did not find that older MPAs were linked to higher reduction in fishing effort. This finding does not challenge the abundant literature linking MPA age to improved biodiversity outcomes, which is explained by the time span required for marine populations to grow, reproduce, and disperse and for the cascading trophic interactions to materialize (65, 66). Rather, it suggests that the effect of MPA implementation on human uses does not change significantly over time. This result is in part intuitive, because regulations in MPAs are typically permanent and static, and thus, the activities regulated should be expected to decrease at once when the MPA is implemented. This also supports the theory that compliance does not require time to accrue but rather that processes influencing compliance mostly take place before MPA implementation (67). However, we still expected to observe a correlation between MPA age and fishing reduction due to the time required for new regulations to be communicated upon, for enforcement to be implemented, and for perception of MPA benefits to accrue and, in turn, influence behaviors (68). The complex and context-dependent dynamics of these processes might contribute to the absence of a linear trend between MPA age and decreases in fishing effort.

Challenges in assessing changes in fishing effort

Several data limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the adoption of AIS by fishing vessels over time and improvements in AIS detection generate temporal increases in fishing activities detected by GFW, irrespective of trends in actual fishing efforts (69). We addressed this temporal bias, as well as potential temporal biases from broader fleet dynamics, market forces, or fuel prices, by standardizing temporal changes in MPAs with temporal changes surrounding MPAs. Furthermore, we performed sensitivity analyses that control for changes in AIS detection by (i) restricting the study period to 2018 to 2024 (fig. S7), when vessel detection stabilizes, and by (ii) only considering vessels present across the entire study period (fig. S9). The robustness of our results across these analyses comforts our main findings. Yet, values of temporal increases in fishing effort based on before/after response ratios alone (i.e., non-BACI designs) are likely overestimated, and strict causality between MPA implementation and changes in fishing effort observed is not certain. Second, while AIS-based fishing data capture most of industrial fishing activities (44), they only capture a fraction of small-scale and artisanal fisheries (53), which are particularly prevalent in nearshore MPAs (44, 70). The underdetection of fishing activities nearshore is further accentuated because high coastal AIS traffic and strong terrestrial signals reduce the ability of satellites to detect vessel transmissions (53). AIS data do not either capture some illegal operations occurring within MPAs, during which AIS transponders can be disabled (53, 71, 72). As such, our study only tests the abatement of a portion of industrial fishing effort by MPAs and, in particular, does not imply their absence of effects on small-scale fisheries. Last, important disparities in AIS coverage exist between regions, with, for example, fishing vessels in the Caribbean and Southeast Asia being less equipped than European fleets (44, 53). For example, the use of satellite imagery has revealed vessel presence in 163 MPAs with no AIS activities in Southeast Asia (42). As a result, differences in fishing effort trends between regions or oceanic zones should be interpreted with caution. The development of novel monitoring technologies such as satellite imagery (42, 43, 53) now offers an opportunity to better account for unreported, illegal, and small-scale fisheries and to address some of the aforementioned limitations.

Toward qualitative tracking of conservation progress

Indicators used to track progress toward global conservation targets are currently limited to areal-oriented metrics such as protection coverage. While IUCN categories provide a qualitative classification of protected areas based on their main objectives, they often fail to reflect actual protection levels or ecological effectiveness (28). Here, we also found that IUCN categories do not predict levels of fishing reduction in MPAs. The MPA Guide represents a major step forward by classifying MPAs into levels of protection according to the activities permitted within their boundaries. Higher levels of protection have been systematically linked to higher conservation effectiveness (32, 60). Here, we show that they are also associated with higher levels of fishing reduction. However, levels of protection do not account for residual conservation or enforcement variability and thus provide only a partial proxy for conservation additionality. To more accurately assess the performance of MPAs, it is essential to move from input- to output-based metrics by integrating human-use data, such as spatialized fishing effort, alongside metrics capturing biodiversity outcomes. While not a direct measurement of biodiversity recovery, pressure abatement represents an intermediate indicator of conservation effectiveness, which, unlike most ecological metrics, can now be tracked at the global scale, at least for most existing industrial-level uses. Unlike ecological metrics, which may need time to accrue and require resource-intensive in situ monitoring (9, 73), human pressure patterns offer more immediate insights into whether protection is altering behaviors and reducing threats. By explicitly quantifying the reduction in industrial fishing activities across hundreds of MPAs globally (half of the global protected coverage), this study provides one of the most comprehensive assessments of MPA effects on impactful human activities. In doing so, it reaffirms the need for management plans that curb human pressures, enforcement systems that ensure that regulations are respected, and monitoring that quantifies and reports on pressure abatement. Embedding targets of conservation additionality in conservation policies, along metrics such as coverage and protection levels, will be key to rebalance incentives from quantity to effectiveness of interventions and safeguard biodiversity objectives against diverting short-term interests.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data collection

Distribution and level of protection of MPAs

We obtained MPA distributions and IUCN categories from the WDPA open access maps (74). Following best practices developed by Thomas et al. (75), we excluded terrestrial protected areas and clipped terrestrial parts of mixed protected areas using a land vector (Natural Earth version 5.1.1, 10-m resolution). MPAs with invalid geometries, either originating from the datasets itself, produced during the clipping process with the land vector, or produced during the creation of buffers (see the “Buffer areas” section), were excluded from the analysis (n = 248 of 1109). We selected MPAs implemented between 2014 and 2022 (n = 997) because global spatialized fishing effort data from the GFW are available from 2012 to 2024, and our analysis required at least two-yearly values of fishing effort after and before MPA implementation. We selected MPAs whose area and surrounding buffer were larger than 0.1 km2 (n = 861) because the resolution of the GFW dataset is 0.01° (460-m side cells at the equator), meaning that the location of fishing activities cannot be accurately attributed to areas smaller than ∼0.1 km2.

IUCN management categories classify protected areas into six classes according to their primary management objectives. The IUCN management categories represent a spectrum of human use management ranging from exclusive biodiversity protection (Ia) to integrated human use and extraction (VI). While there is not a direct correspondence between IUCN categories and levels of protection as defined by the MPA Guide (28), IUCN categories have the advantage of being defined for most protected areas.

We obtained the level of protection of MPAs as defined by the MPA Guide (18) from multiple datasets, including the Marine Protection Atlas [MPAtlas; Marine Conservation Institute (46); n = 86], Aminian-Biquet et al. (47) for OSPAR MPAs (n = 650), Claudet et al. (45) for MPAs in the Mediterranean (n = 19), Claudet et al. (31) for MPAs in France (n = 1), Castro-Cadenas et al. (48) for MPAs in Spain (n = 26), and Field et al. (49) for MPAs in South Africa (n = 79). MPAs listed by Pike et al. (13) and Sullivan-Stack et al. (76) were already incorporated in the MPAtlas.

We retained only MPAs with a known WDPAID and harmonized the diverse terminology used to describe protection levels across datasets into the following categories:

1) “Full,” “fully,” and “fully protected area” were categorized as “Fully.”

2) “High,” “highly,” and “highly protected area” were categorized as “Highly.”

3) “Moderate,” “moderately,” “light,” and “lightly” were categorized as “Lightly.”

4) “Minimal,” “minimally,” “poorly,” and “poorly protected area” were categorized as “Minimally.”

5) “Unprotected,” “unprotected area,” and “incompatible” were categorized as “Unprotected.”

6) “Unclassified” or missing values (NA) were categorized as “Unclassified.”

When an MPA was assessed in multiple datasets with conflicting levels of protection, we prioritized information from the most recent dataset. Because MPAs can overlap spatially because of different designation processes, we retained only the designation offering the highest IUCN category or level of protection for any given area to avoid redundant counting. This process involved creating individual vector layers for each level of protection, merging these layers sequentially from higher to lower protection categories, and visually inspecting overlaps in QGIS (77). In addition, all unclassified MPAs overlapping with defined protection levels were excluded. After retaining only MPAs implemented between 2014 and 2022 and filtering out those smaller than 0.1 km2, we obtained two final datasets: one consisting of 997 non-overlapping MPA polygons classified by IUCN categories along with 1092 unclassified polygons (total n = 2089) and another comprising 541 non-overlapping MPA polygons characterized according to the MPA Guide levels of protection along with 320 unclassified polygons (total n = 861).

Buffer areas

We designed buffer areas around MPAs to obtain a spatial counterfactual to changes in fishing pressure occurring inside MPAs. We calculated buffer width (B) as a function of the area (x) of the corresponding MPA to ensure that buffer sizes were proportional to the dimensions of the MPAs. The buffer width was set to a minimum of 5 km and a maximum of 30 km to adequately capture effects such as spillover and edge effectsB (x)=0.1×AπB (x)=5 if B (x)<5, B (x)=30 if B (x)>30, B (x)=B (x) otherwise

These buffer sizes align with values commonly used in the literature [e.g., Kerr et al. (78) and Strain et al. (79)]. We clipped buffer zones using the same land vector as for MPAs. When an MPA was entirely surrounded by another, we assigned it the buffer area of the encompassing MPA.

Fishing effort

We obtained spatialized global fishing data using the daily fishing activity dataset (version 3.0) from the GFW (44). This dataset provides the location, time, and fishing gear associated with 2,408,476,936 fishing events from 2012 to 2024 at a 0.01° resolution. This dataset includes fishing effort and vessel presence data obtained from AISs. Briefly, fishing vessels are identified by a vessel registry identification, and fishing activities are detected via a neural network algorithm classifier developed by the GFW (44) and manually reviewed by the GFW and regional experts. The GFW dataset captures ∼50 to 70% of global industrial fishing effort (44); however, it does not cover most of small-scale fisheries because of the limited adoption of AIS by smaller vessels. Furthermore, some regions of the world benefit from better AIS vessel coverage than others (72).

We summed daily fishing hours (hours day−1) across calendar years to obtain yearly fishing hours for each cell (0.1° resolution). We focused on fishing hours instead of total hours of vessel presence because the goal of the study was to assess changes in human pressure, and fishing activity results represent a much larger pressure to marine life than mere vessel presence. We then calculated the yearly fishing effort in each cell (hours km−2 year−1) by dividing the yearly fishing effort (hours year−1) in each cell i by the cell’s area. Data analysis

Counterfactual designs

Testing the effects of MPAs is challenging because of caveats associated with most counterfactual designs (51, 52). To address some of these limitations, we tested the effect of MPA implementation on fishing pressure abatement using three complementary experimental designs: (i) before-after-impact (BAI), (ii) before-after-control (BAC), and (iii) BACI.

In before-after designs (80), we compare fishing pressure before and after MPA implementation within the protected area and the buffer zone. These designs test for temporal changes in fishing activities in response to MPAs’ implementation. These temporal changes can occur either directly within the MPAs’ boundaries as a result of new regulations, as well as around the MPA as a result of fishing displacement and of behavioral changes of fishermen (e.g., fishing the line) (81). In the BACI design (82, 83), we normalize temporal changes occurring inside the MPA with temporal changes observed outside the MPA. BACI designs allow to control for the phenomenon occurring at a larger spatial scale than the MPA, which are likely linked to factors external to the MPA implementation.

Data selection

We excluded MPAs where no fishing vessel was reported across the entire time series evaluated (2012 to 2024) within their boundaries or buffer zone because we could not evaluate variations in fishing activities in such instances. While the absence of fishing effort reported could reflect the absence of fishing activity, it could also reflect locations where fishing activities are present but not captured in the GFW dataset. Industrial fishing activities not captured by GFW include vessels that are not equipped with AIS, AIS data that are not shared with the GFW, and AISs intentionally turned off during illegal operations (71). This resulted in the exclusion of 353 MPAs from our analyses.

For the same reason, we cropped the time series of fishing effort for each MPA by excluding null data points of fishing efforts occurring in initial years (e.g., 2012 and 2013) because this could reflect that vessels in the area were not yet reported to the GFW. Once fishing activities were reported in the time series, all subsequent years were conserved in our dataset, and the absence of fishing vessels were considered as null fishing effort. Once data point series were cropped for each MPA, we only conserved MPAs for which at least two data points were available before the year of MPA implementation. When the within-study variance vi=0 (n = 58), we assigned the second quantile, ensuring a small but nonzero variance.

Meta-analysis

We calculated the value of fishing effort before the implementation of an MPA i as the mean fishing pressure that occurred between 2012 and the year before implementation for areas both inside (X¯before IN,i) and outside (X¯before OUT,i) MPAs. Similarly, the value of fishing effort after the implementation of an MPA i was calculated as the mean fishing pressure that occurred between the year following establishment and 2020 for areas both inside (X¯after IN,i) and outside (X¯after OUT,i) MPAs. The value of fishing effort that occurred on the implementation year of a given MPA was excluded from analyses because enforcement of regulations may or may not apply on that year depending on MPAs’ management.

For each level of protection or IUCN category j, we calculated the effect size of change in fishing effort using three different logarithm response ratios (lnRRi)lnRRi=ln(X¯after IN,i,jX¯before IN,i,j)for BAI designslnRRi=ln(X¯after OUT,i,jX¯before OUT,i,j)for BAC designs, andlnRRi=ln(X¯after IN,i,j/X¯before IN,i,jX¯after OUT,i,j/X¯before OUT,i,j)for BACI designs, with X¯IN,i,j and X¯OUT,i,j being the mean yearly value of fishing effort inside and outside each MPA i and X¯before,i,j and X¯after,i,j being the mean yearly value of fishing effort before and after MPA implementation within the MPA boundaries, respectively.

We replaced null mean fishing values by 0.001 to allow the calculation of ratios while keeping a very low fishing effort value. We replaced null and extremely low variance of effect size vei values by 0.03 to avoid overweighting individual MPAs in the overall effect size calculation.

For BAI and BAC designs, a positive lnRRi indicates an increase in fishing effort following MPA designation, both inside and outside the MPA. For BACI designs, a positive lnRRi indicates that the mean fishing pressure is higher inside the MPA compared to its buffer area.

We assessed differences in initial fishing pressures (pre-MPA implementation) between levels of protection using Welch’s analysis of variance (ANOVA) test for non-normally distributed data (84). Likewise, we tested for differences in fishing pressure abatement following MPA implementation between levels of protection. When significant differences were detected, we proceeded to identify which categories differed using the Games-Howell paired test (85).

Effect of age, size, and location of MPAs

We used generalized additive models with LOESS smoothing (86) to evaluate the influence of age and size of MPAs on the magnitude of fishing abatement, considering all levels of protection simultaneously. These models allowed for flexible, nonlinear relationships between the predictors (age and size) and the response variable across the full dataset.

For location effects, we conducted meta-analyses separately for each level of protection within each oceanic zone. Locations were classified on the basis of distance from shore as: coastal (≤12 nautical miles), offshore (>12 and ≤200 nautical miles), and high seas (>200 nautical miles). This stratified approach accounted for variation in fishing effort responses across different marine zones.

Mitigating temporal biases in apparent fishing effort

Sensitivity analyses

Since the creation of the GFW database in 2012, an increasing number of vessels have adopted AIS technology, resulting in an increase in reported fishing effort (2788% increase between 2012 and 2024) that does not reflect the actual increase rate of fishing activities in the ocean (fig. S6). Furthermore, the development of dynamic AIS technology, which reduces reception interferences in areas of high vessel density, has allowed to increase AIS reception and thus increase fishing effort reported in the GFW in areas that previously had limited AIS coverage, such as Southeast Asia. Because increases in reported fishing effort rather than fishing effort itself represent a bias in our temporal analyses, we ran additional analyses in which we only considered vessels reported from the beginning of the GFW time series in 2012. This approach allows to phase out biases originating from fishing activities that appear in an area because the associated vessel was only reported by the GFW halfway through the time series. To do so, we used the maritime mobile service identifier dataset provided by the GFW at a 0.1 resolution and selected all fishing activities associated with the maritime mobile service identifier reported since 2012. We then performed all analyses described above with this 2012 to 2024 data subset (fig. S9). In addition, to further assess the influence of temporal biases in vessel detection, we conducted a second sensitivity analysis restricting the dataset to the period of 2018 to 2024, during which AIS detection stabilizes (fig. S7 and table S2). Differences in effect sizes between the full (2012 to 2024) and restricted datasets were evaluated using mixed-effects meta-regression models (87, 88), including time period, protection level, and their interaction as moderators. Post hoc linear contrasts (87) were performed to test simple effects of time within each protection level.
