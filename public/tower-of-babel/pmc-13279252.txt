ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is major uncertainty among physicians on how to best use the novel hs‐cTnI (high sensitivity‐cardiac troponin I)‐VITROS assay, as the limit of quantification (2 ng/L), applied by the US Food and Drug Administration and other regulators, is higher than the limit of detection (1 ng/L)‐based rule‐out cutoffs from recent pilot studies for 0/1‐hour and 0/2‐hour algorithms.MethodsThis prospective, multicenter study aimed to derive and validate higher limit of quantification‐based assay‐specific cutoffs for safe and efficient rule‐out of myocardial infarction among adult patients presenting with acute chest discomfort to the emergency department. Final diagnoses of myocardial infarction were centrally adjudicated by 2 independent cardiologists, blinded to hs‐cTnI‐VITROS concentrations.ResultsAmong 2931 eligible patients, 467 (16%) were diagnosed with myocardial infarction. In the derivation cohort (n=1466), a cutoff ≤2 ng/L at presentation in patients with chest pain onset >3 hours (direct rule‐out) and ≤2 ng/L in combination with a 1‐hour absolute change of ≤1 ng/L ruled out 51% of patients, with a sensitivity of 99.1% (96.9%–99.8%). Predefined rule‐in thresholds (0 hour‐value ≥40 ng/L [direct rule‐in] or an absolute change within 1 hour of ≥4 ng/L) identified 16.9% of patients, with a specificity of 95.9% (94.7%–96.9%). Very high sensitivity and specificity were confirmed in the validation cohort (n=1465). Comparable findings were observed for the alternative 0/2‐hour algorithm with ≤2 ng/L as the rule‐out threshold.ConclusionsThe alternative 0/1‐hour and 0/2‐hour algorithms using limit of quantification‐based rule‐out cutoffs of ≤2 ng/L with the hs‐cTnI‐VITROS assay demonstrated very high safety and efficacy in suspected myocardial infarction.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/; Identifier: NCT00470587.

cTn (cardiac troponins), alongside the 12‐lead ECG, form the cornerstone of the diagnostic workup for the rule‐out and rule‐in of myocardial infarction (MI) in the emergency department (ED). 1 , 2 , 3 The advent of hs‐cTn (high‐sensitivity cTn)‐assays enabled precise measurement of cTn concentrations even within the normal range and the derivation of hs‐cTn‐based, assay‐specific, accelerated diagnostic triage algorithms. 2 , 3 These allowed for earlier rule‐in and rule‐out of non–ST‐segment–elevation MI (NSTEMI), translating into shorter ED stays, reduced health care costs, and decreased diagnostic uncertainty for both clinicians and patients. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Accordingly, guidelines from the American College of Cardiology, the American Heart Association, and the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) strongly recommend the use of hs‐cTn assays in rapid triage algorithms. 2 , 3

Some experts and regulators, including the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), emphasize the need for high precision also in the low range and require a slightly modified use from that in most other countries: very low concentrations should be reported only down to the limit of quantification (LOQ), the lowest concentration that can be measured with good precision (<20% coefficient of variation [CV]), compared with the often lower limit of detection (LOD), the highest apparent analyte concentration expected to be found when replicates of a sample containing no analyte are tested. 8 , 9 This also applies to the recently developed hs‐cTnI‐VITROS assay (Ortho Clinical Diagnostics). For both the ESC 0/1‐hour and the ESC 0/2‐hour algorithms, the assay‐specific threshold for direct rule‐out of NSTEMI with the hs‐cTnI‐VITROS assay was 1.0 ng/L. 10 , 11 This discrepancy caused major uncertainty in clinical practice, as the LOQ is 1.23 ng/L and the lower end of its reporting range is set at 1.5 ng/L in most countries. Given this discrepancy, there is a critical need to evaluate whether a hs‐cTnI‐VITROS concentration of 2 ng/L or higher can also ensure safe and efficient rule‐out of NSTEMI.

Therefore, the aim of this large multicenter study was to derive and validate new, LOQ‐based and FDA‐conforming criteria for the ESC 0/1‐hour and 0/2‐hour algorithms for ruling out NSTEMI using the hs‐cTnI‐VITROS assay, with all diagnoses centrally adjudicated by 2 independent cardiologists.

METHODS

Study Design and Population

This study was a secondary analysis of the APACE (Advantageous Predictors of Acute Coronary Syndromes Evaluation, ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT00470587) study, a prospective, multicenter, international diagnostic study conducted in 12 centers across five European countries. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 The APACE study had the prespecified aim to investigate the diagnostic performance of new biomarkers, including hs‐cTn assays, in adult patients presenting with symptoms suggestive of MI to the ED.

For this analysis, exclusion criteria were (1) the presence of STEMI, (2) end‐stage renal disease requiring long‐term dialysis, or (3) or an unclear final diagnosis even after final adjudication and at least 1 elevated hs‐cTnI measurement, thereby possibly indicating MI. Patients were further excluded if they did not have 2 serial hs‐cTnI‐VITROS measurements required for deriving new cutoffs. Specifically, 2 distinct cohorts were defined: 1 with hs‐cTnI‐VITROS measurements at 0 hours and 1 hour, and another with measurements at 0 hours and 2 hours. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethics committees of all participating institutions. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Reporting adhered to the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies statement 15 (Table S1). Because of the sensitive nature of the data collected for this study, data and materials are not publicly available.

Central Adjudication of Final Diagnosis

Central adjudication of the final diagnosis was performed at the core laboratory of the University Hospital Basel, Switzerland, by 2 independent cardiologists applying current guidelines and the Fourth Universal Definition of MI. 2 , 3 , 16 In cases of disagreement, a third senior cardiologist was consulted to reach a final decision. The adjudication process followed established methodologies, integrating both clinical and study‐specific assessments, as previously described. 10 , 12 , 17

Hs‐cTnI Assay

Blood samples were collected into heparin plasma and serum tubes at presentation to the ED (0 hours) and serially thereafter (1, 2, 3, and 6 hours after presentation). Serial sampling was discontinued if the patient was discharged or transferred to the catheterization laboratory for acute intervention. After centrifugation, samples were frozen at −80 °C until assayed in a blinded fashion in a dedicated core laboratory. According to the manufacturer, the 99th percentile upper reference limit for hs‐cTnI‐VITROS in serum was 11 ng/L overall, with sex‐specific values of 9 ng/L for women and 12 ng/L for men, and a corresponding coefficient of variation of <7% at the upper reference limit, a limit of detection of 0.39 ng/L, a LOQ (<20% CV) of 1.23 ng/L, and within‐laboratory precision <10% at 1.84 ng/L. 18

Study End Points

The primary diagnostic end point was NSTEMI (type 1 or type 2) at presentation to the ED. The primary prognostic end point was 5‐year all‐cause death. The secondary prognostic end point was 5‐year cardiovascular death or MI. The best validated hs‐cTnI‐assay (Architect) and hs‐cTnT‐assay (Elecsys Gen 5) was used for the direct comparison of the diagnostic discrimination of the hs‐cTnI‐VITROS assays. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 To assess the robustness of the diagnostic performance of the algorithms, a sensitivity analysis against an outcome including only type 1 NSTEMI was performed.

Follow‐Up

To assess death or major adverse cardiovascular events, follow‐up was conducted at 3, 12, 24, and 60 months after discharge through telephone contact or written correspondence. The cause of death was determined using hospital records, family physician documentation, or national death registries.

Derivation and Validation of the 0/1‐Hour and 0/2‐Hour Algorithm

For the evaluation of early rule‐in and rule‐out strategies, the ESC 0/1‐hour and 0/2‐hour algorithms were applied as recommended by current guidelines. 2 The 0/1‐hour algorithm used hs‐cTnI measurements obtained at ED presentation and 1 hour post presentation (±30 minutes), whereas the 0/2‐hour algorithm relied on measurements at presentation and 2 hours post presentation (±30 minutes). Early rule‐out was based on very low initial hs‐cTnI measurements and allowed for immediate discharge if chest pain onset had occurred >3 hours before ED arrival. Early rule‐in was determined by markedly elevated initial hs‐cTnI measurements or a significant absolute change over the 0/1‐hour or 0/2‐hour interval.

Assay‐specific cutoffs were derived according to the concept of the ESC hs‐cTn 0/1‐hour and 0/2‐hour algorithms in a derivation and validation design. 5 As the hs‐cTnI‐VITROS assay had already been derived and validated in 2 different studies for the 0/1‐hour and 0/2‐hour algorithm, we aimed to maintain methodological consistency by adhering to the same approach for each algorithm. 10 , 11 Both studies performed a randomized split sample technique to divide the cohort into derivation and internal validation cohort, however, the distribution differed as follows: In the 0/1‐hour cohort, rule‐out thresholds were derived from 50% and validated in the remaining 50%, whereas in the 0/2‐hour cohort, rule‐out thresholds were derived from 70% and validated in the remaining 30% of the cohort.

We adhered to the previous studies regarding rule‐in thresholds, which included a hs‐cTnI measurement of ≥40 ng/L at 0h, or a delta increase of 4 ng/L at 1 hour for the 0/1‐hour algorithm, or delta increase of 5 ng/L at 2 hours for the 0/2‐hour algorithm. For a direct comparison, the performance of the previously derived LOD‐based rule‐out cutoff was also assessed in this larger cohort and were defined as ≤1 ng/L in patients presenting with chest pain onset >3 hours (direct rule‐out) and ≤2 ng/L at presentation in combination with a 1‐hour absolute change of ≤1 ng/L for the 0/1‐hour algorithm, and ≤1 ng/L in patients presenting with chest pain onset >3 hours (direct rule‐out) and ≤2 ng/L at presentation in combination with a 2‐hour absolute change of ≤3 ng/L for the 0/2‐hour algorithm.

In accordance with the previous studies, target sensitivity and negative predictive value (NPV) for rule‐out were >99.0% and 99.5%, respectively. Target positive predictive value (PPV) was >70%. 10 , 11

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as median (25th–75th percentiles [Q1–Q3]) and categorical variables as count (percentage). Nonparametric statistical descriptors were used by default to minimize assumptions about the underlying distribution of the data. The 95% CIs were calculated using Wilson’s method if not otherwise specified. For the direct comparison of the diagnostic accuracy between the hs‐cTnI‐VITROS and the hs‐cTnI‐Architect assays, receiver‐operating characteristics curves were constructed and their corresponding area under the curve assessed. CIs of area under the curve and P values for comparison of area under the curve were computed as recommended by DeLong et al. 19

Safety of the newly derived ESC 0/1‐hour and 0/2‐hour algorithms was assessed as the sensitivity and NPV of ruling out index NSTEMI and accuracy as the specificity and PPV of ruling in index NSTEMI. Efficacy was determined by the number of patients triaged toward rule‐out or rule‐in as a proportion of the total study sample. The safety performance of new derived cutoffs was further evaluated in prespecified subgroups: age (<65 versus ≥65 years), sex, early presenters defined as time since chest pain onset to ED presentation ≤3 hours, history of diabetes and the presence or absence of myocardial ischemia on the ECG (ST‐segment depression or T‐wave inversion). For the survival analysis, patients were grouped according to their final allocation of the ESC algorithm (rule‐in, observe, rule‐out). Five‐year all‐cause mortality was plotted in Kaplan–Meier curves, and the log‐rank test was used for comparison between groups. For the 5‐year MI or cardiovascular death outcome, the Cumulative Incidence Function method was used for plotting the incidence of the occurrence of the composite outcome while taking competing risk (noncardiovascular death) into account, and the Gray’s test was used for comparison between groups. Patients were included only once (first admission).

All statistical comparisons were 2 sided, and P values of <0.05 indicated statistically significant differences. All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and packages (with version number) are listed in the Table S2.

Diagnostic Discrimination for NSTEMI

The diagnostic discrimination of hs‐cTnI‐VITROS was 0.949 (95% CI, 0.941–0.958) and comparable to hs‐cTnI‐Architect and hs‐cTnT‐Elecsys‐Gen‐5 at 0.942 (95% CI, 0.934–0.950) and 0.932 (95% CI, 0.923–0.941), respectively (Figure 1).

Derivation and Internal Validation of the 0/1‐Hour Algorithm

In the derivation cohort (n=1466, 15.8% with NSTEMI), a cutoff ≤2 ng/L in patients presenting with chest pain onset >3 hours (direct rule‐out) and ≤2 ng/L at presentation in combination with a 1‐hour absolute change of ≤1 ng/L ruled out 748 (51%) patients with a sensitivity and NPV of 99.1% (95% CI, 96.9–99.8%) and 99.7% (95% CI, 99.0–99.9%), respectively (Figure 2A). The diagnostic performance was consistent in all predefined subgroups (Figure S2). Predefined thresholds for rule‐in of NSTEMI (0‐hour value ≥40 ng/L [direct rule‐in] or an absolute change within 1 hour of ≥4 ng/L), ruled in 248 (16.9%) patients with a specificity and PPV of 95.9% (95% CI, 94.7–96.9%) and 79.8% (95% CI, 74.4–84.4%), respectively. A total of 470 (32.1%) remained in the observation group, of which 32 patients (6.8%) had an adjudicated NSTEMI. In the validation cohort (n=1465, 16.7% with NSTEMI), the high NPV and sensitivity for rule‐out and the high PPV and specificity for rule‐in of NSTEMI were confirmed (Figure 2B) and comparable to the performance of the previously derived slightly lower LOD‐based rule‐out cutoffs (Figure S3).

Derivation and Validation of the 0/2‐Hour Algorithm

In the derivation cohort (n=1574, 18.4% with NSTEMI), a cutoff ≤2 ng/L in patients presenting with chest pain onset >3 hours (direct rule‐out) and ≤2 ng/L at presentation in combination with a 2‐hour absolute change of ≤2 ng/L ruled out 773 (49.1%) patients with a sensitivity and NPV of 99.2% (95% CI, 97.1%–99.8%) and 99.7% (95% CI, 99.1%–99.9%), respectively (Figure 3A). The diagnostic performance was consistent in all predefined subgroups (Figure S4). Predefined thresholds for rule‐in of NSTEMI (0‐hour value ≥40 ng/L [direct rule‐in] or an absolute change within 2 hours of ≥5 ng/L), ruled in 289 (18.4%) patients with a specificity and PPV of 94.6% (95% CI, 93.2%–95.7%) and 75.1% (95% CI, 69.8%–79.7%), respectively. A total of 512 (32.5%) remained in the observation zone, of whom 70 patients (13.7%) had an adjudicated NSTEMI. In the internal validation cohort (n=675, 18.7% with NSTEMI), the high NPV and sensitivity for rule‐out and the high PPV and specificity for rule‐in of NSTEMI were confirmed (Figure 3B) and again comparable to the performance of the previously derived slightly lower LOD‐based rule‐out cutoffs (Figure S5).

Prognosis

In the 0/1‐hour cohort, death from any cause within 5 years occurred in 289 patients, with cumulative mortality rates of 2.7% (39 deaths) in the rule‐out group, 16.4% (143 deaths) in the observation group, and 22% (107 deaths) in the rule‐in group (Figure 4A). The 5‐year cumulative incidence for MI or cardiovascular death was 3.6% (95% CI, 2.7%–4.7%) in the rule‐out group, 16% (95% CI, 13%–18%) in the observation group, and 21% (95% CI, 17%–25%) in the rule‐in group. Within 30 days, the cumulative incidence for MI or cardiovascular death in the rule‐out group was 0.2% (95% CI, 0.1%–0.6%) (Figures S6 and S7).

Cumulative incidence of 5‐year all‐cause death in the 0/1‐hour and 0/2‐hour cohort.

Kaplan–Meier plot depicting 5‐y cumulative incidence of all‐cause death in patients with chest pain stratified by diagnostic algorithm in the (A) 0/1‐hour cohort and (B) 0/2‐hour cohort. Log‐rank test for between‐group comparison for all‐cause death was P<0.0001.

In the 0/2‐hour cohort, a total of 245 deaths were recorded, with cumulative mortality rates of 3.3% (34 deaths) in the rule‐out group, 16.8% (114 deaths) in the observation group, and 23.7% (97 deaths) in the rule‐in group (Figure 4B). The 5‐year cumulative incidence for MI or cardiovascular death was 4.4% (95% CI, 3.2%–5.8%) in the rule‐out group, 15% (95% CI, 13%–18%) in the observation group, and 22% (95% CI, 18%–26%) in the rule‐in group. Within 30 days, the cumulative incidence for MI or cardiovascular death in the rule‐out group was 0.4% (95% CI, 0.1%–1%) (Figures S8 and S9).

DISCUSSION

This large, prospective, international multicenter study addressed major uncertainties on the best possible LOQ‐based and FDA‐conform clinical application of the hs‐cTnI‐VITROS assay using the ESC 0/1‐hour or 0/2‐hour algorithm. We report 5 major findings:

First, the hs‐cTnI‐VITROS assay demonstrated excellent diagnostic discrimination for NSTEMI at ED presentation, performing comparably to the best validated hs‐cTnI (Architect) and hs‐cTnT (Elecsys Gen 5) assay. Second, novel assay‐specific, LOQ‐based, and FDA‐conforming cutoffs as part of the 0/1‐hour and 0/2‐hour algorithms were very effective and provided excellent sensitivity and NPV for rule‐out, and excellent specificity and PPV for rule‐in of NSTEMI. Specifically, a hs‐cTnI‐VITROS cutoff ≤2 ng/L, in patients presenting with chest pain onset >3 hours (direct rule‐out) and ≤2 ng/L at presentation in combination with a 1‐hour absolute change of ≤1 ng/L ruled out nearly half of the patients with a very high sensitivity and NPV. Rule‐in cutoffs were identical to the previously derived ones: 0‐hour value ≥40 ng/L (direct rule‐in) or an absolute change within 1 hour of ≥4 ng/L. Overall, the diagnostic performance was comparable to that of the 0/1‐hour and 0/2‐hour algorithms using the previously derived slightly lower LOD‐based rule‐out cutoffs. 10 , 11 Third, diagnostic performance was consistent in all predefined subgroups. Fourth, when restricting the diagnostic end point to NSTEMI type I, as done in previous studies on hs‐cTn assays, 6 , 20 , 21 , 22 sensitivity and NPV improved further. Fifth, the long‐term prognostic performance of both the 0/1‐hour and 0/2‐hour algorithms was excellent and showed substantial differences in all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular event rates across the triage groups, with very low event rates over 30 days and 5 years in the rule‐out group.

These findings have important clinical implications. Physician and institutions working with the hs‐cTnI‐VITROS assay now have detailed guidance and reassurance on the FDA‐conforming assay‐specific cutoffs for the assessment of patients presenting with acute chest discomfort to the ED. Specifically, it is now clear that hs‐cTnI‐VITROS concentrations ≤2 ng/L can be safely used within the 0/1‐hour and 0/2‐hour algorithms for immediate rule‐out in patients presenting 3 hours or more after chest pain onset to the ED. 23 , 24 Consistent with current ESC and American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association recommendations, these algorithms must be applied in conjunction with clinical assessment and 12‐lead ECG interpretation to ensure optimal safety and diagnostic performance. 2 , 3

These findings also extend and corroborate prior studies deriving assay‐specific cutoffs for another hs‐cTnT/I assay (hs‐cTnT‐Elecsys), for which the LOQ‐based and FDA‐conforming lower limit of reporting (6 ng/L, LOQ [<20% CV]) differed from that used in other countries and used for the derivation of the initial ESC 0/1‐hour and ESC 0/2‐hour algorithms (LOD 3–5 ng/L, depending on the analyzer used). 8 , 9 , 25 , 26 Consistently, small modifications of the rule‐out details of the ESC 0/1‐ hour and ESC 0/2‐hour algorithms allowed to achieve an excellent balance between safety and efficacy. 8 , 9 , 25 This highlights the generalizability of the underlying concepts of the ESC 0/1‐hour and 0/2‐hour algorithms. It also indicates that the assay‐specific rule‐out cutoffs were derived applying very rigorous and conservative rules that aimed to prioritize safety, given the very high sensitivity requested by users. 2 , 3 Accordingly, even slightly higher immediate rule‐out cutoffs than those initially derived and validated (2 versus 1 ng/L for hs‐cTnI‐VITROS, 6 versus 5 ng/L for hs‐cTnT‐Elecsys, and 5 versus 4 ng/L for hs‐cTnI‐Architect) still seem to provide very high sensitivity and can be considered for clinical use. 6 , 8 , 9 , 25 As any increase in the rule‐out cutoffs invariably will increase the efficacy of rule‐out, these alternative rule‐out cutoffs may particularly be attractive for health care settings under high economic pressure that need to prioritize efficacy.

This study has several strengths. First, it is based on a large prospective, international, multicenter cohort, increasing statistical power and the generalizability of results. Second, our findings remained consistent across 5 predefined subgroups, ensuring consistency across key patient groups. Third, by incorporating long‐term follow‐up, this study was able to provide further reassurance regarding the safety of the derived rule‐out‐cutoffs, and the appropriateness of the rule‐in cutoffs.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, APACE was conducted in patients presenting with acute chest discomfort to the ED; applicability to lower‐risk populations, such as those presenting to outpatient clinics, requires further investigation. Second, we cannot generalize these findings to patients with terminal renal disease on chronic dialysis, as they were excluded. Third, while the hs‐cTnI VITROS 0/1‐hour and 0/2‐hour algorithm demonstrated strong performance in the validation cohort, external validation in an independent cohort remains warranted. Last, the performance of the novel derived hs‐cTnI‐VITROS ESC 0/1‐hour and ESC 0/2‐hour algorithms was assessed using central adjudication by 2 independent cardiologists. However, patient care was not guided by these algorithms and rather based on local hs‐cTnT/I‐assays. Therefore, additional prospective studies accompanying the implementation of these novel cutoffs into routine clinical care are still needed.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that newly derived LOQ‐based and FDA‐conforming 0/1‐hour and 0/2‐hour algorithms using a hs‐cTnI VITROS rule‐out threshold of ≤2 ng/L are both safe and diagnostically accurate for the rule‐out of NSTEMI. These findings support the clinical adoption of a new slightly higher threshold, supporting safe and efficient triage of patients.
