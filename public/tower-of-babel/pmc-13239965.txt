Unveiling Knowledge, Attitudes, and Preventive Practices Toward Dengue Among Residents of Rangpur City, Bangladesh: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Abu Reza Md. Towfiqul Islam, Md. Alimul Raji Limon, Md. Mujahidul Islam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDengue (DG) is a growing global public health threat. Bangladesh continues to endure an increasing burden despite persistent vector‐control initiatives. Effective DG prevention relies on adequate community knowledge, favorable attitudes, and sustained protective practices. However, evidence on DG‐related knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) remains limited for residents of Rangpur City Corporation (RpCC), Bangladesh.AimsThis study aims to evaluate DG‐related KAP and identified socio‐demographic determinants of preventive behaviors among RpCC residents.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey was performed among 609 adults using a pre‐tested structured questionnaire. Associations were examined using χ2, ANOVA, or Fisher's exact tests, while univariate and multivariable logistic regression identified predictors of KAP outcomes.ResultsMost respondents perceived their residences as moderately safe from DG. One‐fifth reported prior DG experience, with significantly higher reporting among older respondents and males (p < 0.05). Good knowledge was strongly associated with favorable attitudes (OR = 3.35; 95% CI: 2.31–5.07) and practices (OR = 2.22; 95% CI: 1.72–2.96). Female respondents showed better attitudes and practices than males. Residents of mixed or low‐rise housing, along with those with lower education or unemployment, exhibited weaker KAP profiles, indicating potential dengue risk hotspots.ConclusionThese findings reveal a persistent knowledge–practice gap, underscoring the need for context‐specific, community‐centered dengue control in RpCC. Targeted risk communication and stronger community–municipal coordination may enhance sustained preventive behaviors and dengue control effectiveness.

Introduction

Dengue (DG) is one of the fastest‐spreading mosquito‐borne viral diseases in the world and remains a persistent public health challenge in tropical and subtropical regions [1, 2]. DG transmission has increased and intensified because of rapid urbanization, climatic change, high population density, poor water and waste management, and changing human behaviors, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries [3, 4]. Over the past 10 years, South Asia has experienced a pronounced rise in DG cases. Likewise, Bangladesh has emerged as a significant hotspot due to rapid urban growth, monsoon‐related breeding conditions, and encountering continuous issues with vector management and surveillance systems [5]. In addition, Bangladesh experiences recurrent DG outbreaks with increasing geographic expansion beyond large metropolitan areas [6]. In this case, although as capital city Dhaka has received substantial scientific research attention and programmatic emphasis, other secondary cities such as Rangpur are becoming increasingly vulnerable more susceptible because their urban expansion surpasses infrastructural development and public health capability [2, 7]. As a fast‐growing city in northern Bangladesh, Rangpur exemplifies this evolving risk profile which is characterized by urban densification, inconsistent and unplanned drainage and water storage methods, and comparatively insufficient routine surveillance [8]. To prevent DG effectively and sustainably, it is crucial to comprehend the behavioral variables at the community level in these contexts.

Despite the implementation of vector control programs, it is still hard to stop the spread of DG. This illustrates that mostly top‐down and chemical‐based methods such as routine fogging, do not work very well. A growing body of evidence indicates that community knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) are indispensable for effective prevention of DG [9, 10]. Source reduction at the household level, consistent personal protection, and timely healthcare seeking are essential to halt transmission, particularly where Aedes breeding sites are widespread and frequently replenished [11].

Some previous studies reveal that higher DG knowledge does not necessarily translate into appropriate preventive behavior which is reflecting a persistent knowledge–practice gap [12, 13]. Studies also highlight that although school‐based, university‐based, and community interventions can enhance knowledge and attitudes [14], however, behavioral change frequently lacks consistency without ongoing engagement, supportive environments, and locally adapted implementation [15, 16]. These findings show the necessity for context‐specific KAP research that not only delineate knowledge and attitudes but also examine the conversion of awareness into preventive action.

An increasing volume of KAP research has been undertaken in several regions including South and Southeast Asia, Africa, and Latin America. Cross‐sectional studies among general populations and specific groups such as healthcare workers, students, and rural communities consistently demonstrate heterogeneity in DG‐related knowledge and preventive behaviors, influenced by socio‐demographic factors, environmental exposures, and institutional variables [17, 18, 19]. In South Asia, studies from Pakistan, Nepal, and India report moderate‐to‐high awareness but suboptimal practices, particularly regarding breeding‐site elimination and early healthcare seeking [20, 21]. Evidence from Southeast Asia further highlights the influence of socio‐ecological variables, perceived risk, and faith in authorities on the formulation of preventative behavior and ongoing engagement in vector management [5, 22].

In Bangladesh, DG KAP research has predominantly concentrated in Dhaka and specific subpopulations such as university students, slum residents, and youth which is highlighting significant disparities in KAP across socio‐economic strata [1, 6, 7, 23]. Although these studies provide significant insights, they remain geographically concentrated and often emphasize descriptive comparisons, with insufficient attention given to modeling the behavioral pathway that connects knowledge and attitudes to preventative practices.

Therefore, several gaps still exist. First, secondary cities such as Rangpur city, where urbanization is accelerating, are underrepresented in the Bangladeshi DG literature despite increasing vulnerability. Second, numerous studies regard knowledge, attitude, and practice as distinct domains instead of experimentally examining their interrelationships. Third, limited evidence is available on the behavioral transition from awareness to action in Bangladesh, which is essential for the formulation of interventions. Lastly, there are not enough studies in new emerging cities that use multivariable models to find independent predictors of KAP outcomes. This makes it hard to find the most important subgroups for targeted programming.

To fill these gaps, this study presents a comprehensive DG KAP assessment among residents of Rangpur City Corporation (RpCC). The study combines correlation analysis with univariate and multivariable logistic regression to assess the relationship between knowledge and attitudes with preventative actions and to pinpoint independent factors of each KAP component. This study elucidates dengue prevention as a behavioral continuum from knowledge to action, which is providing policy‐relevant data to strengthen community engagement and risk communication in an understudied urban context.

Below, we outline the objectives of this study. First, we want to assess DG‐related knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices among RpCC residents. Second, we intend to examine associations between socio‐demographic characteristics and KAP domains and quantify interrelationships among knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Finally, we aim to identify independent predictors of adequate knowledge, favorable attitudes, and appropriate preventive practices using multivariable logistic regression.

This study contributes to DG prevention research by providing evidence from a rapidly growing secondary city in Bangladesh, empirically examining the connections between awareness, attitudes, and preventive measures, and producing actionable insights to facilitate integrated, behavior‐focused DG control strategies.

Methodology

Conceptual Framework

This study utilized the KAP framework, a widely used behavioral model for evaluating community preparedness and responses to infectious disease prevention, including DG [24, 25]. The KAP model, which began as research on demographics and family planning in the 1950s [26], is now commonly applied in public health to evaluate (i) what individuals know about a health threat, (ii) how they perceive and appraise that threat, and (iii) how that knowledge and those perceptions lead to preventive behaviors [11, 12].

The framework assumes that enhanced information fortifies risk awareness and attitudes, which subsequently promotes adoption of protective practices. This pathway is particularly relevant for controlling DG, as stopping the disease depends a lot on actions taken by individuals and communities, like getting rid of places where mosquitoes can breed, utilizing personal protection, and getting care quickly, instead of just on centralized vector‐control efforts. KAP assessments offer a clear operational method for identifying domain‐specific deficiencies (for instance, sufficient knowledge coupled with inadequate practices) and guiding customized risk communication and behavior modification efforts [7, 27]. Although KAP studies have been conducted in DG‐endemic settings [21], evidence is still scarce for RpCC, a developing urban area experiencing heightened risk. Thus, this study utilized the KAP framework to quantify DG‐related knowledge, attitudes, and practices among RpCC residents and to investigate the socio‐demographic factors influencing the transition from awareness to action.

Study Area Description

RpCC is a rapidly growing urban center in northern Bangladesh and the administrative headquarters of Rangpur Division. The city has 33 wards and serves as a regional hub for education, healthcare, commerce, and public administration, attracting sustained in‐migration from surrounding areas [28]. Rapid urban growth has contributed to the conversion of vegetation and agricultural land into built‐up areas, which has put more strain on infrastructure, the environment, and public health services. If these developments are not matched by proper planning and effective service provision, they could make the risks of managing water, sanitation and vector‐borne diseases.

RpCC is located between 25°38′–25°52′ N latitude and 89°05′–89°20′ E longitude and it occupies around 205.75 km2. RpCC has an estimated population of about 1 million, with a population density of about 4167 persons/km2. DG transmission in Bangladesh typically peaks during the monsoon and post‐monsoon seasons, when rainfall and waterlogging create favorable breeding conditions for Aedes mosquitoes. It is located next to the Ghaghat River, which influences drainage conditions and seasonal waterlogging that can cause seasonal flooding that makes good places for mosquitoes to flourish. Soils are predominantly alluvial (≈80%), with the remainder mainly Barind soil [29]. These traits, along with monsoonal rainfall and low‐lying land, can help hold water on the surface during the rainy season. Rangpur has a subtropical monsoon climate, with temperatures typically ranging from ~11°C in winter to ~32°C in the pre‐monsoon period [30]. The combination of climate adaptability, rapid urbanization, inconsistent drainage infrastructure, and widely used water storage practices create favorable conditions for Aedes mosquito proliferation and DG transmission which make RpCC an appropriate place for assessing DG‐related KAP (Table A1).

Study Design and Ethical Considerations

A cross‐sectional survey was conducted to evaluate DG‐related KAP, perceived residential safety, and self‐reported DG history among adult residents of RpCC. The study involved minimal risk and adhered to ethical standards, including voluntary participation, informed consent, confidentiality, and anonymity. The Research Approval Committee of the Department of Public Administration at Begum Rokeya University in Rangpur (Approval No. BRUR/PAD/RAC/2025/059) examined and approved this study. We used an online questionnaire through Kobo Toolbox to collect data. Eligibility criteria included residence within RpCC, and age ≥ 18 years. The cover page of the survey listed the study objectives, promised confidentiality, and explained the rights of the participants. Before taking part, participants gave their electronic informed consent. No incentives were provided, and respondents could withdraw at any stage without consequences.

Survey Instrument and Data Collection

A structured questionnaire was developed in Bengali and English based on literature review, expert consultation, pilot testing, and cultural adaptation [2, 24]. The instrument comprised three sections: (i) socio‐demographic characteristics; (ii) perceived residential safety and DG history; and (iii) dengue‐related KAP. We used the question “How safe do you think your current home is from dengue?” graded on a five‐point Likert scale (very safe, safe, moderately safe, unsafe, very unsafe) to measure perceived safety. DG history was recorded as Yes/No/Maybe.

The KAP module had 28 closed‐ended questions: 12 knowledge questions (scored 0–1; “Maybe” scored 0.5), eight attitude items measured on a five‐point Likert scale (strongly agree to strongly disagree), and eight practice items with binary responses (Yes/No). We used Cronbach's alpha to check for internal consistency. This indicates acceptable reliability for knowledge (α ≥ 0.65) and good reliability for attitude and practice (α > 0.75), consistent with previous KAP studies [31, 32].

Between June and July 2025, we collected data by using Kobo Toolbox. This study applied a stratified random sampling strategy and trained enumerators with prior survey experience supported recruitment and dissemination of the survey link. Using Morgan's table, the minimum sample size was 625 at a 95% confidence level [1]. This study approached approximately 700 residents and 609 of them gave us full responses that we could use for further analysis. This is a statistically typical sample size. The 609 samples in the RpCC are anticipated to be enough for analyzing any indicator, as it is extensively utilized by the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) for performing various indicator cluster data surveys [28]. Data completeness and consistency were monitored within Kobo Toolbox before export to Microsoft Excel for cleaning and subsequent analysis.

Data Analysis

The study applied Python (version 2.7), RStudio (version 1.2.5042), and SPSS (version 26) to clean, manage, and analyze the data statistically following approaches used in related DG KAP studies [33]. We used Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests to check if continuous variables were normal, and the results showed that they were not. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). We applied χ 2 tests and Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables and Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests for nonparametric comparisons to evaluate the links between socio‐demographic factors and perceived safety, DG history, and KAP category (good vs. poor). Furthermore, we used ANOVA when the assumptions were met. Dunn's post hoc test with Bonferroni adjustment was employed for multiple comparisons following significant overall tests.

Interrelationships among KAP domains were evaluated by employing Spearman's rank correlation, with confidence intervals computed following Bonett and Wright [34]. Composite KAP scores were calculated and classified as “good” or “poor” using an 80% cut‐off threshold [3]. Predictors of good knowledge, attitude, and practice were examined using univariate logistic regression, and variables with p < 0.25 were entered into multivariable logistic regression models to obtain adjusted estimates [7]. The findings are presented as odds ratios (ORs) and adjusted odds ratios (aORs), accompanied with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). α = 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Results

Socio‐Demographic Profile

A total of 700 individuals participated in the survey. Of these, 91 respondents (13.0%) were excluded due to incomplete questionnaires, resulting in a final analytic sample of 609 respondents who completed all survey sections (Table A2). More than half of the participants were aged 18–25 years (n = 329, 54.0%). The sample was broadly balanced by sex, comprising 331 males (54.35%) and 278 females (45.65%). Most respondents were not living with their families (n = 424, 69.62%). The majority resided in high‐rise housing (> 5 storeys) (n = 415, 68.1%), while 194 respondents (31.9%) lived in low‐rise housing (≤ 5 storeys). Educational attainment was predominantly at the tertiary level (n = 518, 85.6%). Students constituted the largest occupational group (n = 320, 52.55%).

Safety Concerns and DG Experience

Table A2 summarizes respondents perceived safety against DG and DG survey experience. Most participants rated their current residence as moderately safe (n = 433, 42.96%), followed by safe (n = 293, 29.07%) and unsafe (n = 165, 16.37%). Respondents living with their families reported significantly higher perceived safety than those living without family members. Perceived safety also differed significantly by housing type; post hoc analyses indicated that residents of both high‐rise and low‐rise buildings expressed greater confidence than those residing in mixed housing units. Occupation was significantly associated with perceived safety, with employed respondents reporting higher confidence than unemployed respondents.

Overall, 21.33% of respondents reported prior DG experience. DG experience varied significantly across age groups, with older respondents (36–45 years and above) reporting higher experience than younger respondents (18–25 years). Male respondents reported significantly higher DG experience than females. Significant associations were also observed with employment status and education: unemployed respondents and those with education below the tertiary level reported higher DG experience than employed participants, students, and those with tertiary education.

Knowledge Regarding DG Fever

Most respondents demonstrated good knowledge of DG across key indicators (Table 1). High proportions correctly identified the potential fatality of DG (87.68%), common symptoms—including rash, headache, high fever, joint pain, muscle pain, and nausea (79.97%)—and the role of the female Aedes mosquito in DG virus transmission (88.10%). Respondents were also aware of characteristic vector features and behaviors, such as the striped body pattern (82.43%), breeding in both indoor and outdoor environments (80.46%), and peak biting times in the early morning and late evening (76.19%). Additionally, 72.25% correctly reported the possibility of multiple DG infections, and 84.24% identified key outbreak‐control measures, including environmental cleanliness and elimination of breeding sites. About 56.65% of respondents correctly recognized this mode of transmission, indicating a moderate level of awareness, while a substantial proportion (43.35%) lacked this knowledge.

Attitudes Toward DG Fever

Overall, respondents expressed favorable attitudes toward DG prevention and control (Table 2). Participants generally agreed that responsibility for eliminating Aedes breeding sites is shared among individuals, families, communities, and relevant authorities. They also endorsed regular implementation and monitoring of preventive measures, even outside outbreak periods. Respondents indicated willingness to participate in community‐based DG control activities and strongly supported seeking immediate medical care for affected family members. These findings reflect a high level of perceived responsibility and readiness to engage in preventive actions.

Practices Toward DG Fever

Preventive practices were commonly reported at the household level (Table 3). Nearly 90% of respondents (89.98%) reported using mosquito‐control measures such as aerosol or liquid repellents, coils, electric mats, and/or bed nets. Most respondents also reported covering (87.85%) and cleaning (83.91%) water storage containers and keeping plant pots free of stagnant water (85.88%), indicating substantial engagement in source‐reduction practices. Additionally, 82.92% reported seeking hospital‐based testing and treatment for suspected DG.

Many respondents reported infrequent communication with local authorities regarding fogging or coordinated monitoring of mosquito breeding sites. Associations Within the KAP Domains

Correlation analysis revealed positive relationships between knowledge and attitude, knowledge and practice, and attitude and practice (Table 4). Univariate logistic regression further identified knowledge as a significant predictor of both attitude and practice, and attitude as a significant predictor of practice. Respondents with good knowledge were more likely to demonstrate a good attitude (OR = 3.35; 95% CI: 2.31–5.07) and good preventive practices (OR = 2.22; 95% CI: 1.72–2.96) than those with poor knowledge. Similarly, respondents with good attitudes were substantially more likely to report good practices (OR = 4.52; 95% CI: 3.12–6.61).

Determinants of KAP Level

Overall, 389 respondents (63.88%) demonstrated good knowledge, 523 (85.88%) reported a good attitude, and 414 (67.98%) reported good preventive practices (Table 5). Univariate logistic regression identified several socio‐demographic predictors of KAP outcomes. For knowledge, living arrangement, housing type, and occupation were significant determinants. Respondents not living with family members had lower odds of good knowledge than those living with family (OR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.51–0.97). Residents of low‐rise housing had reduced odds of good knowledge compared with those in high‐rise housing (OR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.53–1.02), and unemployed respondents had lower odds than students (OR = 0.63; 95% CI: 0.46–0.98).

Discussion

Socio‐Demographic Factors Associated With DG Status

Residents reported substantial concern about DG risk, and a meaningful proportion indicated prior DG experience. Such perceptions are plausible in settings with recurrent outbreaks, where routine surveillance may underestimate transmission because asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic infections often remain undetected without active case finding. Notably, DG experience was higher among older respondents. Although DG burden is frequently reported among children and young adults in many Asian contexts, increasing seroprevalence and clinically apparent DG among older age groups has been documented in some urban settings. Older adults may also experience greater morbidity due to immunosenescence and comorbidities, which can intensify adverse outcomes once infected.

Male respondents reported higher DG experience than females, consistent with findings from other DG‐endemic regions. This pattern may reflect differential exposure through occupational mobility and time spent outdoors, increasing contact with Aedes mosquitoes. A higher incidence of DG among unemployed respondents may correlate with neighborhood and housing circumstances, duration spent in high‐risk micro‐environments, and disparities in prevention resources and access to timely information and interventions. These results are consistent align with eco‐bio‐social frameworks showing that DG risk is shaped by the interaction of socio‐economic conditions, housing quality, water storage practices, and environmental sanitation [35].

People's sense of safety also changed depending on where they lived and what kind of house they had. People who lived with family members said they were more confident, which could be because of social support and common household preventative activities. Conversely, residents of low‐rise or mixed housing settings may face greater exposure to breeding sites associated with dense living conditions, shared water storage, unregulated waste disposal, and insufficient coordinated vector management. Similar urban studies emphasize that built‐environment characteristics and neighborhood‐level factors affect both perceived risk and preventive activities [5, 15].

Residents' Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices

Respondents demonstrated significant awareness of principal DG symptoms and the function of Aedes mosquitoes, indicating that fundamental awareness messages are effectively penetrating the community. Similar levels of general DG awareness have been documented among educated communities in South Asia and in synthesis studies using KAP evidence [7, 12]. However, significant deficiencies remained in knowledge of vector breeding ecology and pregnancy‐related DG risks. These deficits have practical significance because misconceptions about breeding sites undermine household source reduction, while limited understanding of pregnancy‐related risk may delay preventive behaviours and timely care seeking. However, knowledge regarding pregnancy‐related DG risks was only partially assessed and remains limited.

Overall, people had positive attitudes toward prevention. They agreed that everyone in a home, community, or authority should share responsibility and that preventative action should continue even after an outbreak. Such perceptions are consistent with evidence that collective efficacy and shared responsibility can strengthen engagement in DG control [15, 35]. Nevertheless, reported practices reflected a common pattern: household‐level measures (e.g., repellents and cleaning/covering water containers) were frequently adopted, whereas community–authority linkage behaviours (e.g., reporting breeding sites, requesting fogging, or participating in coordinated monitoring) were less common. Similar gaps have been identified where information fails to result in enduring collective action due to the lack of facilitating structures, transparent reporting mechanisms, and confidence in local services [10, 36]. Studies among teachers and youth further indicate that practice adoption depends on feasibility, cues to action, and convenience of habitual behaviors [23].

Relying on “trusted sources” for DG information presents an opportunity to strengthen targeted risk communication. Evidence supports the implementation of structured communication techniques, encompassing digital and mHealth methodologies, to augment preventive behaviors in urban environments, especially when associated with responsive services and feedback systems [5]. However, message design should be sensitive to literacy, perceived risk, and local context to prevent expanding inequalities [3].

Socio‐Demographic Determinants of KAP Level

KAP levels consistently differed based on housing context, education, and employment position, demonstrating that DG preparedness is influenced by structural and socio‐economic factors. High‐rise residents generally demonstrated stronger KAP profiles than those in low‐rise housing. This suggests that the style of housing may be related to differences in the quality of infrastructure, environmental management, and access to organized services. This pattern aligns with eco‐bio‐social studies showing that water storage, waste management, and housing density condition influence both exposure risk and preventive behaviors [35].

Lower educational attainment and unemployment were associated with poorer knowledge and attitudes, aligning with extensive evidence indicating that education enhances comprehension of transmission and prevention rationales, while workplace settings may reinforce preventive norms through institutional messaging [11, 12]. Male respondents also demonstrated weaker attitude and practice profiles, which may reflect differences in risk perception, prevention motivation, or exposure patterns. A significant and policy‐relevant finding is that respondents with prior DG experience reported poorer practices. This counterintuitive association may reflect risk normalization, fatigue with preventive routines, or misperceptions about immunity following prior infection. Given that secondary infection can increase the likelihood of severe DG, targeted counselling and follow‐up risk communication for previously infected individuals is warranted [36].

Finally, the observed positive correlations among KAP and regression evidence indicates that knowledge predicts both attitudes and practices which support the role of education and risk communication as mechanisms for behaviour change. Similar relationships have been reported across diverse populations and environment [20, 31].

Implications for DG Control Strategies

Although this study reveals the role of KAP in shaping DG preventive behaviors, it is significant to recognize that individual and household‐level actions alone are insufficient to substantially reduce DG incidence, particularly in rapidly urbanizing settings. Structural and environmental conditions, for instances, housing design, drainage systems, piped water supply, and waste management play a crucial role in determining Aedes mosquito proliferation [37]. These findings are consistent with broader evidence emphasizing that sustainable DG control depends heavily on improvements in urban planning and infrastructure [3]. Considering this context, this study does not consider KAP as a standalone solution. Rather, we find KAP as a complementary and enabling component within an integrated DG prevention framework. Community knowledge, attitudes, and practices can enhance the effectiveness of structural interventions by promoting household‐level source reduction, encouraging timely healthcare‐seeking behavior, and fostering community participation in vector surveillance and control initiatives [38]. While individual and household‐level preventive behaviors are important, sustainable DG control depends fundamentally on structural interventions such as improved urban planning, drainage systems, and reliable water supply. In this context, KAP should be viewed as a complementary component that facilitates community engagement and enhances the effectiveness of broader environmental and institutional control strategies [10, 12, 33].

This integrated perspective is particularly relevant in the context of Bangladesh, where many urban areas including Rangpur face persistent challenges related to unplanned urbanization, inadequate drainage systems, limited piped water supply, and insufficient waste management [39]. As large‐scale urban planning and infrastructure improvements are often resource‐intensive and time‐consuming, immediate reductions in DG risk cannot rely solely on structural interventions. In such contexts, strengthening community awareness and promoting practical preventive behaviors can serve as an important interim strategy to reduce exposure and vulnerability [14, 37]. Therefore, to effectively control DG, a multi‐faceted strategy is essential which includes (i) long‐term structural and urban planning initiatives, (ii) responsive and well‐coordinated municipal services and (iii) sustained community involvement facilitated by context‐specific risk communication. KAP enhancements can play a critical supportive role within this broader system, but they should be done along with, not instead of, expenditures in public health and urban areas.

Limitations

The representativeness of the study sample has significant limitation. Although this study used stratified sampling strategy, the use of an online questionnaire administered through KoboToolbox may have disproportionately captured responses from younger, more educated, and digitally connected individuals. This is reflected in the sample composition where a large percentage of respondents had tertiary‐level education (85.6%) and were students (52.55%). This distribution differs from the broader demographic profile of Rangpur and urban Bangladesh. According to the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics [22], tertiary‐level education and student representation are substantially lower in the emerging city like RpCC than other big cities [40], indicating that less‐educated and socio‐economically diverse groups may be underrepresented in this study. While this study assessed awareness of vertical transmission, it did not evaluate knowledge of other adverse pregnancy outcomes associated with DG infection (e.g., miscarriage, preterm birth, and low birth weight), which represent an important area for future research and targeted risk communication.

As a result, the observed levels of DG‐related knowledge and awareness may be overestimated that compel us to interpret the findings of this study with extra caution in terms of generalizability. It is noted that individuals with lower literacy levels or limited access to digital platforms may have been less likely to participate which is potentially excluding vulnerable groups who are often at higher risk of DG exposure. The present study did not assess knowledge or acceptance of DG vaccination, which represents an important gap given the evolving vaccine landscape. The current research highlights the need for future KAP studies to include vaccine awareness, acceptance, and willingness‐to‐pay, especially in anticipation of potential vaccine introduction. Therefore, this study recommend that future studies should adopt probability‐based sampling techniques and incorporate mixed‐mode data collection methods that will include face‐to‐face surveys for ensuring more inclusive representation across different socio‐economic and educational groups.

Conclusion

This study applied a structured KAP framework and multivariable modelling to analyze DG fever–related knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices among residents of RpCC. In general, the people who answered the survey showed a decent understanding of DG symptoms and transmission and reported generally favorable attitudes toward prevention. Nevertheless, significant deficiencies remained in knowledge and practice, especially with Aedes breeding ecology, pregnancy‐related risks, and community‐level engagement, indicating a distant knowledge–practice gap. Furthermore, socio‐demographic disparities were apparent including males, low‐rise/mixed‐housing residents, individuals with lower educational attainment, unemployed respondents, and those with prior DG experience consistently exhibited weaker KAP profiles, suggesting priority subgroups for targeted interventions. The observed positive associations between knowledge, attitude, and practice and the identification of structural determinants such as housing type and employment status underscore that DG prevention is shaped not only by individual awareness but also by the broader eco‐bio‐social environment in which households' function.

The findings of this study have direct public health implications. DG control in RpCC should move beyond temporary fogging and focus on long‐term, community‐based strategies that reduce the number of breeding sites in homes, enhance communication about risks, and make it easier for communities and municipalities to work together to report breeding sites and respond quickly. Although DG vaccination is not yet part of routine public health programs in Bangladesh, emerging vaccines such as TAK‐003 (Qdenga) highlight the need for future studies to assess community awareness, acceptance, and readiness for vaccination. Integrating vaccination with existing vector‐control strategies may offer a more comprehensive and sustainable approach to DG prevention. Interventions should be customized to high‐risk groups and neighborhood contexts, focusing on rectifying misconceptions about reinfection, enhancing practical guidance on breeding‐site elimination, and encouraging prompt healthcare‐seeking behavior.

Future research should adopt longitudinal and intervention designs to examine causal pathways and assess the efficacy of specific behavioral and communication tactics. Mixed‐methods studies that combine survey data with entomological and environmental assessments would enhance validity and facilitate the development of more actionable DG prevention strategies. Extending similar analyses to other secondary cities would enhance the development of scalable, context‐sensitive DG control strategies in Bangladesh.
