Around the center
M. Gorelik, V. Hinich, V. Serganova

Abstract
. The center of a semisimple Lie algebra can be described as the algebra of W W -invariant functions on the dual of the Cartan subalgebra. The centers of many Lie superalgebras have a similar description, but the defining equivalence relation on the dual of the Cartan subalgebra is not given by a finite group action. Lagrangian equivalence relations that we introduce generalize the action of a subgroup of the orthogonal group. Using them, we present a new proof of a result by Ian Musson about the centers of Lie superalgebras. Our proof is not based on a case-by-case analysis.



1.
Introduction

1.1.

Let
V
be a complex vector space and
R\subset V\times V
be an equivalence relation. We denote by
\mathbb{C}[V]=SV^{*}
the ring of polynomial
functions on
V
and by
\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
the subring of invariant functions,

\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}=\{f\in\mathbb{C}[V]|\ f(x)=f(y)\ \forall(x,y)\in R\}
.

This notion generalizes the invariant subring
\mathbb{C}[V]^{G}
where
G

is a group of automorphisms of
V
: the group action defines an equivalence relation and the two notions of invariant subring coincide.
In this case the quotient
V/G
is an affine variety
with the ring of functions
\mathbb{C}[V]^{G}
. In particular, the set of
maximal ideals
\mathtt{spec}\ \mathbb{C}[V]^{G}
identifies with the set of
G
-orbits
in
V
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a semisimple Lie algebra. The center
Z\mathfrak{g}

of the enveloping algebra of
\mathfrak{g}
identifies, via Harish-Chandra map, with the invariant subring
\mathbb{C}[V]^{W}

where
V=\mathfrak{h}^{*}
is dual to the Cartan subalgebra and
W
the Weyl group. Therefore the maximal ideals of
\mathbb{C}[V]^{W}
describe the central characters
and they correspond to
W
-orbits in
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
.

Having in mind an application to the description of the centers of
enveloping algebras of finite dimensional Lie superalgebras, we study
more general quotients
V/R
.

1.2.
Generalities

There is a correspondence between relations
R\subset V\times V
and
subrings of
\mathbb{C}[V]
, similar to the relation between subgroups
of the Galois group and field extensions in the Galois theory.

To any relation
R\subset V\times V
we assign the subring

\mathcal{A}(R):=\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
; in the opposite direction, we assign to any subring

A\subset\mathbb{C}[V]
the relation
\mathcal{R}(A)
consisting of all pairs
(x,y)

such that
f(x)=f(y)
for all
f\in A
.

It is obvious that
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{R}(A))\supset A
for any
A\subset\mathbb{C}[V]
and

\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A}(R))\supset R
for any
R\subset V\times V
. Moreover, it is
standard that
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A}(R))=\mathcal{A}(R)
and
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{R}(A))=\mathcal{R}(A)
.

1.2.1 Definition
.

1.

A subring
A\subset\mathbb{C}[V]
is called
saturated

if
A=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{R}(A))
.

2.

A relation
R\subset V\times V
is called
saturated
if

R=\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{R}(R))
.

Saturated relations are automatically equivalence relations.
There is a one-to-one correspondence between
saturated equivalence relations and
saturated subrings.

1.2.2 Definition
.

An equivalence relation
R
on
V
is called
detectable
if the embedding

\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}\to\mathbb{C}[V]
establishes a bijection
between the set of maximal ideals of
\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
and the quotient
V/R
.
In other words, for any maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}\subset\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
the set
of common zeroes of
f\in\mathfrak{m}
is an equivalence class with respect to
R
(in particular, this set is nonempty).

Note the following

1.2.3 Lemma
.

Any detectable relation is saturated.
A saturated relation
R
is detectable if, in addition,
for every maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}\subset\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}

its extension
\mathbb{C}[V]\mathfrak{m}
is a proper ideal in
\mathbb{C}[V]
.

Proof.

Let
(x,y)\not\in R
and let
\mathfrak{m}_{x}
,
\mathfrak{m}_{y}
be the maximal ideals
of
\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
defined by the equivalence classes of
x
and
y
respectively. Since
\mathfrak{m}_{x}\neq\mathfrak{m}_{y}
, there is
f\in\mathfrak{m}_{x}\setminus\mathfrak{m}_{y}
.
Then
f(x)=0
but
f(y)\neq 0
. The converse is obvious as, if
\mathbb{C}[V]\mathfrak{m}

is a proper ideal, the set common zeroes of
f\in\mathfrak{m}
is nonempty.
∎

1.2.4 Proposition
.

Let
R\subset V\times V
be a saturated equivalence relation on
V
.
Then
R
is Zariski closed in
V\times V
.

Proof.

Let
A=\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}\subset\mathbb{C}[V]
.
The relation
R
is saturated, so
(x,y)\in R
iff
f(x)=f(y)
for any
f\in A
. In other words,
R
is defined by the ideal of
\mathbb{C}[V\times V]=\mathbb{C}[V]\otimes\mathbb{C}[V]
generated by the elements
f\otimes 1-1\otimes f
,
f\in A
.
∎

1.2.5.
An example

Here is an example of a Zariski closed equivalence relation that is not
saturated. Let
V=\mathbb{C}
and let
R
be an equivalence relation on
\mathbb{C}

generated by the condition
z\sim\frac{1}{z}
. The relation
R
is Zariski closed
as it is given by the equation
(x-y)(xy-1)=0
. A polynomial
f
satisfying the condition
f(z)=f(\frac{1}{z})
is obviously constant,
so
\mathcal{A}(R)=\mathbb{C}
and
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A}(R))=V\times V
.

1.3.

The finite group actions are detectable since
\mathbb{C}[V]
is a finite
extension of the ring of invariants, and the claim follows from Going-Up Theorem. The aim of our work is
to prove detectability of certain equivalence relations appearing in Lie superalgebra theory.

1.3.1.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a basic classical (finite-dimensional
Kac-Moody) Lie superalgebra with a Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
and the Weyl group
W
. The Harish-Chandra projection identifies the center
Z\mathfrak{g}
of the universal enveloping algebra of
\mathfrak{g}
with a subalgebra of
S\mathfrak{h}^{W}
that, by the works of A. Segreev and V. Kac, see
[
S99
,
K84
,
G04
]
,
has form
\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
where
V=\mathfrak{h}^{*}
and the equivalence relation
R

is described as follows. Let
\Delta\subset\mathfrak{h}^{*}
be the root system of

\mathfrak{g}

with the bilinear form
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
and the set of isotropic roots
\Delta^{\mathit{iso}}
. Then
R
is generated by the Weyl group action, together with the condition

(x,x+\lambda\alpha)\in R,\textrm{ for any }\alpha\in\Delta^{\mathit{iso}},\langle x|\alpha\rangle=0.

If
\Delta^{\mathit{iso}}
is empty (for example, if
\mathfrak{g}
is a Lie algebra),
the detectability of
R
follows from the above observation
1.3
.
Detectability of
R
for basic classical
\mathfrak{g}
has been recently established by I. Musson,
see
[
M
]
.

1.3.2 Theorem

(I. Musson)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a basic classical Lie superalgebra,
\mathfrak{h}
a Cartan subalgebra,
Z\subset S\mathfrak{h}
the image of the center of
U\mathfrak{g}

under the Harish-Chandra projection. Then

1.

For any maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
of
Z
there exists
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
so that
\mathfrak{m}=Z\cap\mathfrak{m}_{\lambda}
, where
\mathfrak{m}_{\lambda}

is the ideal of polynomials vanishing at
\lambda
.

2.

Z\cap\mathfrak{m}_{\lambda}=Z\cap\mathfrak{m}_{\mu}
if and only if
(\lambda,\mu)\in R
for the equivalence relation
R
defined above.

These two properties together are equivalent to detectability by
1.2.3
.
Musson’s proof is based on a case-by-case analysis. The main
motivation of our work was to find a general argument for this sort
of questions.

1.4.

This paper is based on two essential observations. The first one
is the idea that, for an equivalence relation
R
on
V
, the quotient
space
V/R
, endowed with an appropriate structure of a ringed space
(we use in this paper the Kempf’s language of “spaces with functions”,
see
[
K
]
) is a meaningful geometric object containing more
information than merely the ring of global regular functions
\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
.

The other point is the description of a beautiful family of
equivalence relations on
V
that we call Lagrangian equivalence
relations. Almost all examples of equivalence relations appearing in
Lie superalgebra theory are Lagrangian; it is easy to see that the
restriction of a linear Lagrangian relation to an appropriate subfactor remains Lagrangian; this is convenient for the inductive argument in proving detectability.

1.5.

Here is a baby example of an invariant ring of the type we will be
dealing with. Let
V=\operatorname{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}(v,w)
and the equivalence relation is
generated by the condition
cv\sim 0\ \forall c\in\mathbb{C}
. The invariant subring of

\mathbb{C}[x,y]
(
x
and
y
form the dual basis to
\{v,w\}
) is

A=\mathbb{C}+y(x,y)
. This is a non-Noetherian ring with the fraction field

\mathbb{C}(x,y)
. If a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}\subset A
does not contain
y
, it corresponds to a maximal ideal of
A_{y}=\mathbb{C}[x,y]_{y}
.
The ring
A/(y)
is a
(non-Noetherian) local ring whose maximal ideal has square zero. Therefore, there is a unique maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}\subset A
containing
y
.

We see that the quotient
V/R
has a natural stratification, consisting in this example of two strata, the open stratum isomorphic to

\mathbb{A}^{2}\setminus\mathbb{A}^{1}
and the closed stratum being a point.

1.6.

The argument both in Musson’s and in the present paper goes by
induction on strata
and it is of crucial importance that the restriction of functions
to the next stratum is surjective. The proof of surjectivity,
in the context of finite dimensional Lie superalgebras, was given in
the preprint
[
G20
]
, based on Sergeev-Veselov description of the
centers of Lie superalgebras. In the present paper the surjectivity
follows from elementary sheaf theory.

2.
Lagrangian relations

2.1.
Linear relations

Let
V
be a complex vector space of dimension
n
endowed with a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form
v,w\mapsto\langle v|w\rangle
.

We are going to study a class of equivalence relations on
V
that we call Lagrangian equivalence relations.

We define a bilinear form
B
ob
V\times V
by the formula

B((v,w),(v^{\prime},w^{\prime}))=\langle v|w\rangle-\langle v^{\prime}|w^{\prime}\rangle.

2.1.1 Definition
.

A linear relation on
V
is a vector subspace
L\subset V\times V
. A linear relation
L
is called

isotropic
if
B|_{L}=0
and is called a
linear Lagrangian relation
if it is isotropic of maximal dimension
\dim(V)
.

Since
B
is symmetric,
L
is isotropic iff any
(x,y)\in L
satisfies

\langle x|x\rangle=\langle y|y\rangle
.

2.1.2 Example
.

For an endomorphism
\alpha:V\to V
the graph
\Gamma_{\alpha}=\{(v,\alpha(v))\}
is a linear Lagrangian relation iff
\alpha
is an isometry.

2.2.
Composition

Given two relations
L,L^{\prime}\subset V\times V
, their composition

L^{\prime}\circ L
is defined as the collection of pairs
(x,z)
for which there
exists
y
with
(x,y)\in L
and
(y,z)\in L^{\prime}
. The composition of
linear relations is linear. The definition of composition is chosen so that, for
\alpha,\beta\in\operatorname{End}(V)
,

\Gamma_{\alpha}\circ\Gamma_{\beta}=\Gamma_{\alpha\circ\beta}
.

2.2.1 Lemma
.

If
L,L^{\prime}
are isotropic relations, their composition is
also isotropic.

Proof.

If
(x,y)\in L
then
\langle x|x\rangle=\langle y|y\rangle
; if

(y,z)\in L^{\prime}
then
\langle y|y\rangle=\langle z|z\rangle
. Thus,

\langle x|x\rangle=\langle z|z\rangle
for any
(x,z)\in L^{\prime}\circ L
.
∎

If
L
is a linear relation, the relation
L^{-1}:=\{(x,y)|(y,x)\in L\}
is also
linear. It is isotropic iff
L
is isotropic.
For a linear relation
L
we denote by
p_{1},p_{2}
the projections of

L\subset V\times V
to the two copies of
V
. We further denote

K_{i}=\mathrm{Ker}(p_{i})\subset L
.

2.2.2 Lemma
.

Let
L
be a linear Lagrangian relation. Then
\dim K_{1}=\dim K_{2}
.

Proof.

Denote
k_{i}=\dim K_{i}
.
Let us calculate the dimension of
L\circ L^{-1}
. The fiber product

L\times_{V}L^{-1}
has dimension
2k_{2}+(n-k_{2})
and the kernel of
its projection to
V\times V
is
K_{1}\times_{V}K_{1}
that is isomorphic
to
K_{1}
as the projection
p_{2}:K_{1}\to V
is injective. Therefore,

\dim L\circ L^{-1}=n+k_{2}-k_{1}
. The composition is isotropic, so

k_{2}\leq k_{1}
. By symmetricity we get the equality.
∎

2.2.3 Definition
.

Let
L
be a linear Lagrangian relation. The number
\dim K_{1}=\dim K_{2}
is
called
the atypicality
of
L
.

For example, a linear Lagrangian relation of atypicality
0
is a graph of an isometry on
V
.
We denote by
a(L)
the atypicality of
L
.

2.2.4 Lemma
.

Let
L
be a linear Lagrangian relation. Then
p_{1}(L)
is the orthogonal complement of
p_{1}(K_{2})
. In particular,
p_{1}(L)
and
p_{2}(L)
are coisotropic subspaces of
V
.

Proof.

An element of
K_{2}
is
(x,0)\in L
. If
y\in p_{1}(L)
, there exists

(y,z)\in L
. Since
B((x,0),(y,z))=0
, we have
\langle x|y\rangle=0
.
Finally, since
p_{1}|_{K_{2}}
is injective and
\dim K_{2}+\dim p_{1}(L)=n
,
we deduce that
p_{1}(K_{2})
is the orthogonal complement of
p_{1}(L)
.
∎

Finally, here is the main result of this subsection.

2.2.5 Proposition
.

The set of linear Lagrangian relations on
V
is a monoid with respect to
the composition.

Proof.

Let
L,L^{\prime}
be linear Lagrangian relations,
k=a(L)
and

k^{\prime}=a(L^{\prime})
. The composition

L^{\prime}\circ L
is isotropic. Let us calculate the dimension of
L^{\prime}\circ L
. We have

L^{\prime}\circ L=\mathrm{Im}(L\times_{V}L^{\prime}\to V\times V),

so
\dim(L^{\prime}\circ L)=k+k^{\prime}+\dim(p_{2}(L)\cap p_{1}(L^{\prime}))-\dim(p_{2}(K_{1})\cap p_{1}(K^{\prime}_{2}))
.
Since
p_{2}(K_{1})=p_{2}(L)^{\perp}
and
p_{1}(K_{2}^{\prime})=p_{1}(L^{\prime})^{\perp}
,

p_{2}(K_{1})\cap p_{1}(K_{2}^{\prime})=(p_{2}(L)+p_{1}(L^{\prime}))^{\perp}
and therefore

\dim(L^{\prime}\circ L)=k+k^{\prime}+n-\dim(p_{2}(K_{1})+p_{1}(K^{\prime}_{2}))-\dim(p_{2}(K_{1})\cap p_{1}(K^{\prime}_{2}))=n.

∎

2.2.6 Lemma
.

One has

\max(a(L),a(L^{\prime}))\leq a(L^{\prime}\circ L)\leq a(L)+a(L^{\prime}).

Proof.

Obviously
p_{1}(L\circ L^{\prime})\subset p_{1}(L)
and

p_{2}(L\circ L^{\prime})\subset p_{2}(L^{\prime})
. This immediately implies that

a(L^{\prime}\circ L)\geq\max(a(L),a(L^{\prime}))
.
To get the second inequality, let us estimate the dimension of
p_{1}(L^{\prime}\circ L)
.
Denote
P=p_{2}(L)\cap p_{1}(L^{\prime})
. Obviously,
\dim(P)\geq n-a(L)-a(L^{\prime})
,
so
\dim p_{2}^{-1}(P)\geq n-a(L^{\prime})
. Thus,
\dim p_{1}(L^{\prime}\circ L)=\dim p_{1}(p_{2}^{-1}(P))\geq n-a(L)-a(L^{\prime})
. This implies that
a(L^{\prime}\circ L)\leq a(L)+a(L^{\prime})
.
∎

2.3.
More on linear Lagrangian relations

Linear Lagrangian relations of atypicality
0
are defined by isometries of
V
.
A general linear Lagrangian relation can also be described in terms of
isometries.

2.3.1 Lemma
.

Let
V_{0}
and
V^{\prime}_{0}
be two coisotropic subspaces of
V
of the same dimension. Let
V_{1}=V_{0}^{\perp}
and
V_{1}^{\prime}=(V_{0}^{\prime})^{\perp}
. Then there is a one-to-one correspondence between linear Lagrangian relations
L
with
p_{1}(L)=V_{0}
and
p_{2}(L)=V_{0}^{\prime}
and isometries
\alpha:V_{0}/V_{1}\to V_{0}^{\prime}/V_{1}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We look at
L\subset V_{0}\times V_{0}^{\prime}
as at a relation between
V_{0}
and
V_{0}^{\prime}
. We claim that the same relation is given by an isometry

\alpha
. For
v\in V_{0}
choose
(v,v^{\prime})\in L
and send
v\in V_{0}

to
v^{\prime}
. This map is uniquely defined modulo
V_{1}
and
V_{1}^{\prime}
and it
preserves the norm as
0=B((v,v^{\prime}),(v,v^{\prime}))=\langle v|v\rangle-\langle v^{\prime}|v^{\prime}\rangle
.
∎

2.3.2 Definition
.

A linear Lagrangian relation
E
is called
idempotent
if
E\circ E=E
.

2.3.3 Example
.

Let
V_{0}
be a coisotropic subspace of
V
and let
V_{1}=V_{0}^{\perp}
.
Then

E_{V_{0}}:=\{(v,w)\in V\times V|v\in V_{0},\ w\in V_{1}\}

is an idempotent linear Lagrangian relation.

We will prove soon that any idempotent linear Lagrangian relation is of the above form.

2.3.4 Lemma
.

Any linear Lagrangian relation
L
can be presented as

L=\Gamma_{\gamma}\circ E_{V_{0}},

where
\gamma
is an isometry of
V
and
V_{0}=p_{1}(L)
.

Proof.

Let
L
be Lagrangian and
\gamma\in O(V)
.
Obviously
p_{1}(\Gamma_{\gamma}\circ L)=p_{1}(L)
and

p_{2}(\Gamma_{\gamma}\circ L)=\gamma(p_{2}(L))
.

Let
V_{0}:=p_{1}(L)
and
V_{0}^{\prime}:=p_{2}(L)
.
Choose

\gamma\in O(V)
such that
V_{0}^{\prime}=\gamma(V_{0})
.
Then
V_{0}=p_{1}(\Gamma_{\gamma^{-1}}\circ L)=p_{2}(\Gamma_{\gamma^{-1}}\circ L)
. So by the previous description it is defined by an isometry of
V_{0}/V_{0}^{\perp}
. Any such isometry can be lifted to an isometry of
V

preserving
V_{0}
. This implies the claim.
∎

We are now ready to deduce the characterization of idempotent Lagrangian
relations.

2.3.5 Proposition
.

Let
E
be an idempotent linear Lagrangian relation. Then
E=E_{V_{0}}

for
V_{0}=p_{1}(E)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.3.4
,
E=\Gamma_{\gamma}\circ E_{V_{0}}
where

V_{0}=p_{1}(E)
and
\gamma\in O(V)
.
Since
E
is idempotent,

E_{V_{0}}\circ\Gamma_{\gamma}\circ E_{V_{0}}=E_{V_{0}}.

We will deduce first of all that
\gamma(V_{0})=V_{0}
;
the rest will be very easy.

We have
E_{V_{0}}=\{(v,v+w)|v\in V_{0},\ w\in V_{1}\}
where
V_{1}=V_{0}^{\perp}
.
Then

E_{V_{0}}\circ\Gamma_{\gamma}\circ E_{V_{0}}=\{(v,\gamma(v+w)+w^{\prime})|v\in V_{0},\ w,w^{\prime}\in V_{1},\ \gamma(v+w)\in V_{0}\}.

Since this space coincides with
E_{V_{0}}
, we have

\forall v\in V_{0}\ \quad\exists w\in V_{1}:\gamma(v+w)\in v+V_{1}.

Choose elements
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
in
V_{0}
whose images in
V_{0}/V_{1}

form a basis. Choose
w_{i},w_{i}^{\prime}\in V_{1}
such that

\gamma(v_{i}+w_{i})=v_{i}+w^{\prime}_{i}.

Replacing
v_{i}
with
v_{i}+w_{i}
and
w_{i}^{\prime}
with
w_{i}
we simplify
the formulas and get

\gamma(v_{i})=v_{i}+w_{i}.

Since
\gamma
is an isometry and
V_{1}=V_{0}^{\perp}=\operatorname{Span}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}^{\perp}
, we deduce that
\gamma(V_{1})=V_{1}
. This implies that

\gamma(V_{0})=V_{0}
.

As a result,
E=\Gamma_{\gamma}\circ E_{V_{0}}
satisfies
p_{1}(E)=p_{2}(E)=V_{0}
.
By Lemma
2.3.1

E
is described by an isometry
\alpha
of
V_{0}/V_{1}
. Then

E\circ E
is described by the isometry
\alpha^{2}
, so
\alpha
is idempotent and therefore
\alpha=1
.
∎

Note the following

2.3.6 Lemma
.

For any linear Lagrangian relation
L
the composition
L^{-1}\circ L
is
idempotent.

Proof.

Let
E=L^{-1}\circ L
and let
V_{0}=p_{1}(L)
. Then
V_{0}=p_{1}(E)
and

E
contains the diagonal
\Delta_{V_{0}}=\{(v,v)|v\in V_{0}\}
. This leads
to the direct decompositions

E=\Delta_{V_{0}}\oplus K_{1}=\Delta_{V_{0}}\oplus K_{2}
that implies

E=E_{V_{0}}
.
∎

2.4.
Lagrangian equivalence relations

2.4.1 Definition
.

An equivalence relation
R\subset V\times V
that is a union of linear Lagrangian relations,
R=\cup_{\alpha\in A}L^{\alpha}
, is called a Lagrangian equivalence relation.

Note: a Lagrangian equivalence relation is not a linear Lagrangian relation!

In this paper we are mostly interested in
Lagrangian equivalence relations having a finite number of components.

In other words, a Lagrangian equivalence relation is defined by a
collection
\{L^{\alpha}\}
of linear Lagrangian relations, with unit (the diagonal
V\subset V\times V
), closed under the composition and taking inverse.

2.4.2 Definition
.

Given a Lagrangian equivalence relation
R=\cup_{\alpha\in A}L^{\alpha}
,
a subspace
p_{1}(L^{\alpha})\subset V
is called a

special coisotropic subspace

of
V
.

If
V_{0}
is a special coisotropic subspace with
V_{1}=V_{0}^{\perp}
,

R
contains an idempotent component
E_{V_{0}}:=\{(v,v+w)|v\in V_{0},w\in V_{1}\}
by Lemma
2.3.6
.
Special coisotropic subspaces are in one-to-one correspondence with the idempotent components of
R
.

2.4.3.
The Weyl group

Let
R=\cup_{\alpha\in A}L^{\alpha}
be a Lagrangian equivalence relation. The components of
atypicality
0
form a group that will be called the Weyl group
W
of
R
.
Any
s\in W
is an isometry of
V
such that the graph
\Gamma_{s}
is a
component of
R
. Equivalently,
W
is the group of invertible elements
of the monoid
R
. We think of
W
as a group of isometries of
V

rather than the group of invertible components of
R
.

The group
W
acts on
\{L^{\alpha},\alpha\in A\}
by composing on the right and on the left.
We have some obvious identities

1.

p_{1}(\Gamma_{s}\circ L^{\alpha})=p_{1}(L^{\alpha})
,
p_{2}(\Gamma_{s}\circ L^{\alpha})=s(p_{2}(L^{\alpha}))
.

2.

E_{s(V_{0})}=\Gamma_{s}\circ E_{V_{0}}\circ\Gamma_{s^{-1}}
.

2.5.
Reduction

Let
R
be a Lagrangian equivalence relation on
V
having a finite number of components and let
V_{0}
be a special
coisotropic subspace. Set
V_{1}=V_{0}^{\perp}
. The main result of this subsection, Proposition
2.5.3
, asserts that the restriction
of
R
to
V_{0}
induces
a Lagrangian equivalence relation on
V_{0}/V_{1}
which we will denote by
R|_{V_{0}/V_{1}}
.

2.5.1.

Note that
(v,v+w)\in R
for any
v\in V_{0}
and
w\in V_{1}
, therefore

R\cap(V_{0}\times V_{0})
is invariant with respect to the shifts
by
V_{1}\times V_{1}
.
The intersection
R\cap(V_{0}\times V_{0})=\cup_{\alpha\in A}L^{\alpha}\cap(V_{0}\times V_{0})
is a finite union of vector subspaces of
V_{0}\times V_{0}
; since it is invariant with respect to
V_{1}\times V_{1}
,
it is the union of the
V_{1}\times V_{1}
-invariant components
among
L^{\alpha}\cap(V_{0}\times V_{0})
.

2.5.2 Lemma
.

Let
L
be a linear Lagrangian relation and let
V_{0}
be a coisotropic subspace of
V
with
V_{1}=V_{0}^{\perp}
. Then the intersection

L\cap(V_{0}\times V_{0})
is
V_{1}\times V_{1}
-invariant iff

L\subset V_{0}\times V_{0}
.

Proof.

If
L\cap(V_{0}\times V_{0})
is
V_{1}\times V_{1}
-invariant then

L\supset V_{1}\times V_{1}
, so
L=L^{\perp}\subset(V_{1}\times V_{1})^{\perp}=V_{0}\times V_{0}
.

Vice versa, if
L\subset V_{0}\times V_{0}
then

L\supset V_{1}\times V_{1}
. Therefore,

L\cap(V_{0}\times V_{0})=L
is
V_{1}\times V_{1}
-invariant.

∎

2.5.3 Proposition
.

Let
R=\cup_{\alpha\in A}L^{\alpha}
be a Lagrangian equivalence relation
and let
V_{0}\subset V
be a special coisotropic subspace with
V_{1}=V_{0}^{\perp}
. Then the restriction of
R
to
V_{0}
induces an
equivalence relation on
V_{0}/V_{1}
which is also Lagrangian. Its components are of the form
L^{\alpha}/(V_{1}\times V_{1})
for

\alpha\in A^{\prime}
where

A^{\prime}=\{\alpha\mid L^{\alpha}\subset V_{0}\times V_{0}\}=\{\alpha\mid E_{V_{0}}\circ L^{\alpha}\circ E_{V_{0}}=L^{\alpha}\}.

Proof.

Only the second equality in the description of
A^{\prime}
requires explanation.
For any
\alpha\in A
the intersection
L^{\alpha}\cap(V_{0}\times V_{0})
lies
in (at least one)
L^{\alpha^{\prime}}
satisfying the condition

L^{\alpha^{\prime}}\subset V_{0}\times V_{0}
. It is easy to present one of them:
this is

L^{\alpha^{\prime}}:=E_{V_{0}}\circ L^{\alpha}\circ E_{V_{0}}
. So,
\alpha\in A^{\prime}
iff

L^{\alpha}=E_{V_{0}}\circ L^{\alpha}\circ E_{V_{0}}
.
∎

2.6.
Discriminant

Let
R
be a Lagrangian equivalence relation on
V
. The discriminant
of
R
,
\Delta_{R}
, is defined as the union of all proper
special coisotropic subspaces of
V
. Thus, if
R=\cup L^{\alpha}
,

\Delta_{R}=\bigcup_{a(L^{\alpha})>0}p_{1}(L^{\alpha}).

2.6.1 Lemma
.

Let
W
be the Weyl group of a Largangian equivalence relation
R

with finitely many components.
Then
\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}\subset\mathbb{C}[V]^{W}
and
the rings
\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
and
\mathbb{C}[V]^{W}
have the same field of fractions.

Proof.

The discriminant
\Delta_{R}
is Zariski closed in
V
, so there is a

W
-invariant polynomial
T
vanising on
\Delta_{R}
: such is,
for instance,
T=\prod_{s\in W}s(f)
where
f
vanishes on

\Delta_{R}
. This implies that
T\in\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
and moreover
T\mathbb{C}[V]^{W}\subset\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
.
Therefore, the localizations of
\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
and of
\mathbb{C}[V]^{W}
with respect
to
T
, coincide.
This proves the lemma.
∎

2.7.
Regular Lagrangian equivalence relations

2.7.1 Definition
.

A Lagrangian equivalence relation
R
on
V
with finitely many components is called

1
-
regular
if either
\Delta_{R}=\emptyset
(all components
L^{\alpha}
have atypicality
0
, that is,
R=W
), or
\Delta_{R}=WV_{0}
where
V_{0}
is a special coisotropic subspace of codimension
1
.

If a Lagrangian equivalence relation
R
is
1
-regular with a
codimension
1
special coisotropic subspace
V_{0}\subset V
, one can
easily describe the Weyl group of the reduction of
R
to
V_{0}/V_{1}
.

2.7.2 Proposition
.

Let
R
be
1
-regular. Then the Weyl group
W^{\prime}
of the
equivalence relation
R^{\prime}:=R|_{V_{0}/V_{1}}
is
the quotient of the stabilizer
\{s\in W|s(V_{0})=V_{0}\}

modulo the normal subgroup of
s\in W
inducing the identity on
V_{0}/V_{1}
.

Proof.

We are looking for
L^{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in A^{\prime}
, satisfying the condition
p_{1}(L^{\alpha})+V_{1}=V_{0}
. Since
p_{1}(L^{\alpha})\subset V_{0}
is also coisotropic,

p_{1}(L^{\alpha})\supset p_{1}(L^{\alpha})^{\perp}\supset V_{1}
so in fact

p_{1}(L^{\alpha})=V_{0}
. By 1-regularity,
L^{\alpha}=E_{V_{0}}\circ\Gamma_{s}
for some
s\in W
and the condition
p_{2}(L^{\alpha})=V

implies that
s
stabilizes
V_{0}
.
∎

2.7.3 Definition
.

A Lagrangian equivalence relation
R
is called
regular
if for any special
coisotropic subspace
V_{0}
with
V_{1}=V_{0}^{\perp}
the equivalence
relation
R^{\prime}=R|_{V_{0}/V_{1}}
is
1
-regular.

Obviously, if
R
is regular, the reduction
R^{\prime}=R|_{V_{0}/V_{1}}
is
also regular for any special coisotropic subspace
V_{0}
of
V
.

2.8.
Product of equivalence relations

Let
V,V^{\prime}
be two vector spaces with equivalence relations
R\subset V\times V
and
R^{\prime}\subset V^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}
. The equivalence relation

R\times R^{\prime}
on
V\times V^{\prime}
is defined in an obvious way:

((v,v^{\prime}),(w,w^{\prime}))\in R\times R^{\prime}
iff
(v,w)\in R
and
(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})\in R^{\prime}
.
The isomorphism
\mathbb{C}[V\times V^{\prime}]=\mathbb{C}[V]\otimes\mathbb{C}[V^{\prime}]
defines a homomorphism

\mu:\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}\otimes\mathbb{C}[V^{\prime}]^{R^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{C}[V\times V^{\prime}]^{R\times R^{\prime}}.

2.8.1 Lemma
.

The map
\mu
is bijective.

Proof.

The map
\mu
is obviously injective. Let us verify its surjectivity.
Given an invariant polynomial
f\in\mathbb{C}[V\times V^{\prime}]^{R\times R^{\prime}}
, let

f=\sum p_{i}\otimes q_{i},

with
p_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[V]
and
q_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[V^{\prime}]
, be a shortest presentation of
f
.
This means, in particular, that
p_{i}
are linearly independent in
\mathbb{C}[V]

and
q_{i}
are linearly independent in
\mathbb{C}[V^{\prime}]
.
Note that for any
y\in V^{\prime}
the function
f(-,y)=\sum p_{i}q_{i}(y)
belongs to
\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
. By Lemma
2.8.2
below we deduce that there are

y_{i}\in V^{\prime}
such that the matrix
\left(q_{i}(y_{j})\right)
is invertible. This
implies that all the
p_{i}
belong to
\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
. In a similar way
we deduce that all the
q_{i}
belong to
\mathbb{C}[V^{\prime}]^{R^{\prime}}
, so that
f\in\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}\otimes\mathbb{C}[V^{\prime}]^{R^{\prime}}
.
∎

The following lemma must be well-known.

2.8.2 Lemma
.

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}:X\to\mathbb{C}
be
n
linear independent functions
on an infinite set
X
. Then there exist
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X
such that
the matrix
(f_{i}(x_{j}))
is invertible.

Proof.

Let
k
be the maximal number for which there exist
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}

that yield a rank
k
matrix
(f_{i}(x_{j}))
. We have to verify that
k=n
.
Assume
k<n
. There exists a nonvanishing
k
-minor of the matrix
(f_{i}(x_{j}))
; without loss of generality we can assume that this is the
minor formed by the functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
. We will now prove that
the functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k+1}
are linearly dependent. Look at the

(k+1)\times(k+1)
-matrix formed by the values of the functions

f_{1},\ldots,f_{k+1}
at the points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},x
of
X
. We get a square matrix that is not
invertible for all values of
x\in X
. This means that its determinant
vanishes. Decomposition along the last column yields a linear dependence
of
f_{i}(x)
.
∎

Product of equivalence relations preserves detectability.

2.8.3 Lemma
.

Let
R
and
R^{\prime}
be detectable equivalence relations on
V
and
V^{\prime}
, respectively. Then
R\times R^{\prime}
is a detectable equivalence relation on
V\times V^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Denote
A=\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
,
A^{\prime}=\mathbb{C}[V^{\prime}]^{R^{\prime}}
. Let
\mathfrak{m}\subset A\otimes A^{\prime}
be a maximal ideal.

We have
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(A\otimes A^{\prime})/\mathfrak{m}\leq\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}[V]\otimes\mathbb{C}[V^{\prime}]=\aleph_{0}
, whereas
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}(x)>\aleph_{0}
,
so
(A\otimes A^{\prime})/\mathfrak{m}=\mathbb{C}
. This implies that the set of maximal ideals of
A\otimes A^{\prime}

coincides with
\mathrm{Hom}(A\otimes A^{\prime},\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{Hom}(A,\mathbb{C})\times\mathrm{Hom}(A^{\prime},\mathbb{C})
.
The rest of the claim is obvious.
∎

2.9.
Semiregularity

2.9.1 Definition
.

A Lagrangian equivalence relation
R
on a vector space
V

is called
1
-semiregular if
V
admits an orthogonal decomposition

V=V^{1}\times\ldots\times V^{k}
,
R=R^{1}\times\ldots\times R^{k}
such that
all
R_{i}
are
1
-regular Lagrangian equivalence relations on
V_{i}
.

2.9.2 Definition
.

A Lagrangian equivalence relation
R
on a vector space
V
is called
semiregular if for any special coisotropic subspace
V_{0}
of
V
with
V_{1}=V_{0}^{\perp}
the equivalence relation
R^{\prime}=R|_{V_{0}/V_{1}}
is

1
-semiregular.

The main result of our paper is

2.10 Theorem
.

Any semiregular Lagrangian equivalence relation
R
is
detectable: there is a one-to-one correspondence between the
maximal ideals of
\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
and the set
V/R
of equivalence classes
of
V
modulo
R
.

2.11.
Proof of Theorem
2.10

We will now present the proof of Theorem
2.10
, modulo a surjectivity
result
2.11.1
.
Using Lemma
2.8.3
, we can assume that

R
is
1
-regular (and semiregular).
Let
V_{0}
be a special coisotropic subspace of
V
of codimension

1
. The discriminant
\Delta_{R}
has form
\cup_{s\in W}s(V_{0})
.

We denote by
T\in\mathbb{C}[V]
the
W
-invariant polynomial of a minimal degree
that vanishes at
V_{0}
. Obviously
T\in\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
.
Set
R^{\prime}:=R|_{V_{0}/V_{1}}
.
Our proof of Theorem
2.10

is based on the following result.

2.11.1 Proposition
.

The restriction map

i^{*}:\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}\to\mathbb{C}[V^{\prime}]^{R^{\prime}}

is surjective, with the kernel
\operatorname{rad}(T)
.

The proof of Proposition
2.11.1
is given in Subsection

.

2.11.2.

We complete the proof of Theorem

by induction. A maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}\subset\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
is called

if

. Typical maximal ideals correspond
to the maximal ideals of the localization
\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}_{T}=\mathbb{C}[V]^{W}_{T}
, see
Lemma

. This implies that the maximal ideals of

\mathbb{C}[V]
over a typical

form one

-orbit
which is an equivalence class of

.

Let us now deal with an atypical maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}\subset\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}
.
Then
T\in\mathfrak{m}
, so
\mathfrak{m}
contains
\operatorname{rad}(T)
and thus by
Proposition

it defines a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}
of
\mathbb{C}[V^{\prime}]^{R^{\prime}}

form a
single equivalence class

of

the set of zeros of

in

of

.

3.
Spaces with functions

We will use the formalism of “spaces with functions”
[
K
]
, 1.1, to
describe quotients of
V
by an equivalence
relation.

Recall that a space with functions (over the field
\mathbb{C}

in this note) is a pair
(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})
where
X
is a topological space,

\mathcal{O}_{X}
is a subsheaf of the sheaf of functions
U\mapsto\operatorname{Map}(U,\mathbb{C})

such that

•

if
U
is an open subset of
X
and
f\in\mathcal{O}_{X}(U)
then the set

\{x\in U|f(x)=0\}
is closed in
U
.

•

\mathcal{O}_{X}(U)
is a subring of
\operatorname{Map}(U,\mathbb{C})
. If
f\in\mathcal{O}_{X}(U)
does not vanish at
U
then
\frac{1}{f}\in\mathcal{O}_{X}(U)
.

A morphism of spaces with functions
f:(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})\to(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y})
is a
continuous map
f:X\to Y
such that for any
g\in\mathcal{O}_{Y}(V)
the map

f^{*}(g):f^{-1}(V)\to\mathbb{C}
is in
\mathcal{O}_{X}(f^{-1}(V))
.

The category of spaces with functions is very convenient to work with reduced algebraic varieties of
finite type over an algebraically closed field. It appears that it is also convenient for studying quotients by equivalence relations.

3.1.
Factor space

Given a space with functions
(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})
and an equivalence relation
R
on
X
,
one defines a new space with functions
(X/R,\mathcal{O}_{X/R})
, universal
among morphisms
f:(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})\to(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y})
for which
f(x)=f(y)
for any

(x,y)\in R
. As a topological space,
X/R
is precisely the quotient
of
X
modulo
the equivalence relation (the set
X/R
with the weakest topology
making the canonical map
\pi:X\to X/R
continuous). For an open subset
U\subset X/R
the ring
\mathcal{O}_{X/R}(U)
consists of
the functions
f:U\to\mathbb{C}
for which the composition

\pi^{-1}(U)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi}}{{\to}}U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}\mathbb{C}
belongs to
\mathcal{O}_{X}(\pi^{-1}(U))
.

3.2.
Subspace

Given a space with functions
(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})
and a subset
Z\subset X
,
there is a canonical structure of a space with functions on
Z
that is
universal among the morphisms
f:(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y})\to(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})
for which
f(Y)\subset X
. As a topological space,
Z
acquires the induced topology.
For an open subset
V\subset Z
, a function
f:V\to\mathbb{C}
is regular if
for any
x\in V
there exists open
U\subset X
with
x\in U
and a regular function
\tilde{f}
on
U
such that

f_{|U\cap V}=\tilde{f}_{|U\cap V}
. A map of spaces with functions

f:(Z,\mathcal{O}_{Z})\to(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})
is called an embedding if
f
establishes a
homeomorphism of
Z
with
f(Z)\subset X
and identifies the sheaf

\mathcal{O}_{Z}
with the sheaf on
f(Z)
described above.

3.2.1 Lemma
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})
be a space with functions,
Z\subset X
closed
and let
i:(Z,\mathcal{O}_{Z})\to(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})
be a closed embedding of
spaces with functions.
The canonical map of sheaves
\mathcal{O}_{X}\to i_{*}(\mathcal{O}_{Z})
is surjective whose kernel
I
is the ideal of regular functions vanishing at
Z
.

Proof.

The canonical map in question is defined by the collection of maps

\mathcal{O}_{X}(U)\to i_{*}\mathcal{O}_{Z}(U)=\mathcal{O}_{Z}(Z\cap U)

that assign to any regular function on
U
its restriction to
Z\cap U
.
The kernel is defined by the formula

I(U)=\{f\in\mathcal{O}_{X}(U)|f_{|U\cap Z}=0\}.

It remains to verify that the map of sheaves
\mathcal{O}_{X}\to i_{*}\mathcal{O}_{Z}

is surjective. It suffices to prove that for any
x\in X
the map
of stalks
\mathcal{O}_{x,X}\to(i_{*}\mathcal{O}_{Z})_{x}
is surjective. If
x\not\in Z
then

(i_{*}\mathcal{O}_{Z})_{x}=0
. Assume
x\in Z
. Given a stalk

(U,g\in\mathcal{O}_{Z}(U\cap Z))
, there exists
U^{\prime}
containing

and a function

\tilde{g}\in\mathcal{O}_{X}(U^{\prime})
such that
\tilde{g}_{|U^{\prime}\cap U\cap Z}=g_{|U^{\prime}\cap U\cap Z}

defined by
\tilde{g}
lifts
(U,g)
.
∎

3.3.

We will need the following elementary result.

z=t^{-1}sy
, so
f(z)=f(y)
. This proves that
we can define
\tilde{f}(z)=f(y)
where
z=sy
and this definition will
be independent of the presentation
z=sy
. This defines an
R
-invariant
function
\tilde{f}:WV_{0}\to\mathbb{C}
extending
f:V_{0}\to\mathbb{C}
. It remains to prove that the function
\tilde{f}
is regular. This follows from
Lemma
3.3.1
.
∎

3.4.5.

Here is the last claim of Proposition
2.11.1
.
The kernel of
i^{*}:\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}\to\mathbb{C}[V^{\prime}]^{R^{\prime}}
is
T\mathbb{C}[V]^{W}
. This is a radical ideal as
\mathbb{C}[V^{\prime}]^{R^{\prime}}
is reduced. On the other hand,

(T\mathbb{C}[V]^{W})^{2}=TT\mathbb{C}[V]^{W}\subset T\mathbb{C}[V]^{R}=(T)
. Therefore,

T\mathbb{C}[V]^{W}=\operatorname{rad}(T)
.

4.
Example: weak generalized root systems

In this section we apply Theorem
2.10
to prove detectability of the equivalence
relations defined in
1.3.1
. The rings defined by these relations are isomorphic to the centers of universal enveloping algebras of finite-dimensional Kac-Moody superalgebras.

In fact we consider a larger class of
equivalence relations coming from weak generalized root systems
in the sense of
Serganova, see
[
S
]
, Sect. 7. We check that these relations are Lagrangian
and semiregular, see
4.4
. Therefore they are detectable.
The case of Kac-Moody superalgebras is related to the generalized root systems that are special cases of weak generalized roots systems.

In this section

V
is a finite-dimensional complex vector space with a nondegenerate
symmetric bilinear form.

4.1.
Weak generalized root systems

Recall a generalization of the notion of root system due to V. Serganova,
[
S
]
.

4.1.1 Definition
.

A finite subset
\Delta\subset V\setminus\{0\}
is called a weak generalized root system (WGRS) if

1.

\Delta=-\Delta
.

2.

If, for
\alpha\in\Delta
,
\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle\neq 0
,
then for any
\beta\in\Delta
one has
k_{\alpha,\beta}:=\frac{2\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle}{\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle}\in\mathbb{Z}
and

s_{\alpha}(\beta):=\beta-k_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha\in\Delta
.

3.

If, for
\alpha\in\Delta
,
\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle=0
,
then for every
\beta\in\Delta
such that
\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle\neq 0

at least one of
\beta+\alpha
,
\beta-\alpha
, belongs to
\Delta
.

4.1.2 Definition
.

Let
\Delta\subset V
be a symmetric subset (that is, satisfying the
condition
\Delta=-\Delta
).
We say that
\Delta
is
indecomposable

if
\Delta
can not be decomposed into a union of two non-empty mutually orthogonal symmetric subsets.

Let
\Delta
be a WGRS. We denote by
\Delta^{\mathit{iso}}
the set of
\alpha\in\Delta
such that

\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle=0

(isotropic roots)
and let
\Delta^{\mathit{an}}=\Delta\setminus\Delta^{\mathit{iso}}

(anisotropic roots)
.
We define the Weyl group
W
as the group generated by
the reflections
s_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\Delta^{\mathit{an}}
.

4.2.
Properties of WGRS

In this subsection (except for
4.2.2
(3)) a WGRS

\Delta
is not required to be finite.

We will use the following easy fact: if
\beta
,
\beta^{\prime}
are nonorthogonal isotropic roots and
\gamma:=\beta^{\prime}-\beta\in\Delta
, then

\gamma\in\Delta^{\mathit{an}}
and
s_{\gamma}\beta=\beta^{\prime}
.

4.2.1 Lemma
.

Let
\Delta
be an indecomposable WGRS.
Let
\beta,\beta^{\prime}
be mutually orthogonal isotropic roots
and
\beta^{\prime}\not=\pm\beta
.
Then there exists
\delta\in\Delta^{\mathit{an}}

which is not orthogonal to both

\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Since
\Delta
is irreducible, there is a chain

\beta=\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}=\beta^{\prime}

of roots such that

\langle\gamma_{i}|\gamma_{i+1}\rangle\neq 0
. We can assume that
n

is minimal possible. Then
\langle\gamma_{i}|\gamma_{j}\rangle=0
for

|i-j|>1
. We will show that one can choose a minimal chain as above such that all
\gamma_{i}
are isotropic. Let
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{i-1}
be isotropic and
\gamma_{i}\in\Delta^{\mathit{an}}
. Then

\gamma_{i}^{\prime}:=s_{\gamma_{i}}(\gamma_{i-1})=\gamma_{i-1}-c\gamma_{i}
with

c\neq 0
, so replacing
\gamma_{i}
with
\gamma_{i}^{\prime}
we get another chain
connecting
\beta
with
\beta^{\prime}
with
\gamma_{i}^{\prime}
isotropic.
Note that for a shortest chain of
\gamma_{i}\in\Delta^{\mathit{iso}}
connecting
\gamma_{1}
with
\gamma_{n}
, the sum
\delta_{j}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{j}\gamma_{i}
is a root (we allow ourselves to replace
\gamma_{j}
with
-\gamma_{j}
if needed). Thus
\delta_{n}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}
and

\delta_{n-1}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}\gamma_{i}
are two roots
which are not orthogonal to
\beta,\beta^{\prime}
, and

\langle\delta_{n}\mid\delta_{n}\rangle\not=\langle\delta_{n-1}\mid\delta_{n-1}\rangle
, so at least
one of them is not isotropic.
∎

4.2.2 Lemma
.

Let
\Delta\subset V
be an indecomposable WGRS and
\beta,\beta^{\prime}
be two
isotropic roots. Let
V_{1}=\operatorname{Span}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})
and
V_{0}=V_{1}^{\perp}
.

1.

If
\langle\beta\mid\beta^{\prime}\rangle\not=0
, then

\beta^{\prime}=\pm s_{\alpha}\beta
for some

\alpha\in\Delta^{\mathit{an}}
.

2.

If
\langle\beta\mid\beta^{\prime}\rangle=0
and
\beta^{\prime}\not=\pm\beta
,
then there exist
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\Delta^{\mathit{an}}
such that
for
w:=s_{\alpha_{2}}s_{\alpha_{1}}
one has
w(\beta)=\pm\beta^{\prime}
.

3.

If
\Delta
is finite, then there exist
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\Delta^{\mathit{an}}
such that
for
w:=s_{\alpha_{2}}s_{\alpha_{1}}
one has
w(\beta)=\pm\beta^{\prime}
,

w^{2}=\mathrm{id}
and
w|_{V_{0}/V_{1}}=\mathrm{id}
.

Proof.

If
\langle\beta\mid\beta^{\prime}\rangle\not=0
, then,
changing the signs if necessary we can assume that

\alpha=\beta-\beta^{\prime}
is a root. Since
\langle\beta\mid\beta^{\prime}\rangle\not=0
,

\alpha\in\Delta^{\mathit{an}}
and
s_{\alpha}\beta^{\prime}=\beta
. This gives 1.

Consider the case
\langle\beta\mid\beta^{\prime}\rangle=0
.
By Lemma
4.2.1
there exists
\delta\in\Delta^{\mathit{an}}
which is not orthogonal to both
\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}
.
Then
\beta_{1}:=s_{\delta}\beta
is an isotropic root
which is not orthogonal to both
\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}
.
Changing the signs if necessary we can assume that

\gamma=\beta-\beta_{1}
and
\gamma^{\prime}=\beta^{\prime}-\beta_{1}
are roots.
Since
\beta,\beta_{1},\beta^{\prime}
are isotropic,
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}

are anisotropic and
s_{\gamma}\beta_{1}=\beta
,
s_{\gamma^{\prime}}\beta_{1}=\beta^{\prime}
. Therefore
\beta=s_{\gamma^{\prime}}s_{\gamma}\beta^{\prime}
. This proves 2.

For 3 note that

\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}=\beta-\beta^{\prime}
has square length zero.
Since
\Delta
is finite,
\Delta^{\mathit{an}}
forms a classical root system,
so
\langle\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}\mid\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=0
implies

\langle\gamma\mid\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=0
.
Hence
w^{2}=\mathrm{id}
. Then
w\beta=\beta^{\prime}
and
w\beta^{\prime}=\beta
,
so
wV_{1}=V_{1}
and
wV_{0}=V_{0}
. Let
V_{2}=\operatorname{Span}(\beta,\beta^{\prime},\beta_{1})^{\perp}
. Then

V_{0}=V_{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}\beta^{\prime}
. If
v\in V_{2}
, then
\langle v\mid\gamma\rangle=\langle v\mid\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=0
, so
wv=v
. Since

w\beta^{\prime}-\beta^{\prime}=\beta-\beta^{\prime}\in V_{1}
, one obtains
wv\in v+V_{1}
for all
v\in V_{0}
. This completes the proof.
∎

4.2.3 Definition
.

A subset
S\subset\Delta^{\mathit{iso}}
is called an
iso-set
if

1.

S=-S
.

2.

\langle s|t\rangle=0
for all
s,t\in S
.

Iso-sets are ordered by inclusion.
For
v\in V
we denote by
A_{v}^{\mathit{max}}
the collection of maximal iso-sets
orthogonal to
v
.

4.2.4 Lemma
.

Let
S,S^{\prime}\in A^{\mathit{max}}_{v}
. There exists
w\in\mathtt{Stab}_{W}(v)
such that

S^{\prime}=w(S)
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction in the cardinality of
S^{\prime}\setminus S
. Let
\alpha\in S^{\prime}\setminus S
. Then
S\cup\{\alpha\}
is not an iso-set, so there exists
\beta\in S
such that
\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle\neq 0
. This implies that
\alpha\pm\beta\in\Delta
; without loss
of generality we can assume
\gamma=\alpha-\beta\in\Delta
. Obviously,

\gamma\in\Delta^{\mathit{an}}
so
s_{\gamma}\in W
. The formula

s_{\gamma}(x)=x-2\langle x|\gamma\rangle/\langle\gamma|\gamma\rangle

implies that
s_{\gamma}
preserves
v
and
S\cap S^{\prime}
, and

s_{\gamma}(\alpha)=\beta
.
This implies the claim.
∎

4.2.5 Corollary
.

The group
W
acts transitively on the set of maximal iso-sets.
In particular, all maximal iso-sets have the same cardinality.

∎

4.3.
Equivalence relation defined by a WGRS

Given a WGRS
\Delta
, we define an equivalence relation
R
on
V
as the one generated by the action of
W

and by the condition

\langle v\mid v+c\alpha\rangle\in R\textrm{ for }\ \alpha\in\Delta^{\mathit{iso}},\langle v|\alpha\rangle=0,\ c\in\mathbb{C}.

Since
R
is generated by linear Lagrangian relations
\Gamma_{s_{\alpha}}
,
\alpha\in\Delta^{\mathit{an}}
, and

E_{\alpha^{\perp}}
,
\alpha\in\Delta^{\mathit{iso}}
,
R
is a Lagrangian
equivalence relation by
2.2.5
. The following result explicitly describes
R
.

4.3.1 Proposition
.

Let
v\in V
and let
S\in A_{v}^{\mathit{max}}
. Then

(v,v^{\prime})\in R\Longleftrightarrow v^{\prime}\in W(v+\operatorname{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}(S)).

Proof.

Obviously the elements of
W(v+\operatorname{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}(S))
are equivalent to
v
.
To prove that any
v^{\prime}
equivalent to
v
lies in
W(v+\operatorname{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}(S))
,
we will verify that for any
v^{\prime}\in W(v+\operatorname{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}(S))
and any

\beta\in\Delta^{\mathit{iso}}
such that
\langle v^{\prime}|\beta\rangle=0
, one has

v^{\prime}+c\beta\in W(v+\operatorname{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}(S))
. We have
v^{\prime}=w(v+x)
where
x\in\operatorname{Span}(S)
. Clearly
S\in A_{v+x}^{\mathit{max}}
so
w(S)\in A^{\mathit{max}}_{v^{\prime}}
. By
Lemma
4.2.4
there exists
w^{\prime}\in W
such that

w^{\prime}(v^{\prime})=v^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime-1}(\beta)\in w(S)
that is
\beta=w^{\prime}w(\alpha)

for some
\alpha\in S
. Finally, we have

v^{\prime}+c\beta=w^{\prime}(v^{\prime})+c\beta=w^{\prime}w(v+x+c\alpha)\in w^{\prime}w(v+\operatorname{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}(S)).

∎

4.3.2 Corollary
.

1.

The components of
R
have form

\Gamma_{w}\circ E_{S^{\perp}}
where
S
is an iso-set and
w\in W
.

1.

The special coisotropic subspaces of
R
are

S^{\perp}
where
S
runs through the set of iso-sets in
R
.

4.4.
Semiregularity of
R

Let
\Delta
be a WGRS.
By Lemma
4.2.2
,

W
acts transitively on
\Delta^{\mathit{iso}}/\pm 1
.
Therefore, the equivalence relation
R
is
1
-semiregular. The following result implies that it is in fact semiregular.

4.4.1 Proposition
.

Let
\Delta
be a WGRS in
V
and let
\alpha\in\Delta^{\mathit{iso}}
. Denote

V^{\prime}=V_{0}/V_{1}
where
V_{1}=\mathbb{C}\alpha
and
V_{0}=V_{1}^{\perp}
and let
\Delta^{\prime}

be the image of
\Delta\cap V_{0}
in
V^{\prime}
(we exclude
\pm\alpha
whose image in
V^{\prime}
is zero). Then
\Delta^{\prime}
is a WGRS in
V^{\prime}
and the corresponding
equivalence relation
R^{\prime}
on
V^{\prime}
is the one induced on
V^{\prime}
by
R
.

Proof.

Obviously
\Delta^{\prime}=-\Delta^{\prime}
and the projection
V_{0}\to V_{0}/V_{1}
preserves the
bilinear form (it is degenerate on
V_{0}
). The conditions 1 — 3
of Definition
4.1.1
obviously hold, so
\Delta^{\prime}
is a WGRS.
Let us denote (temporarily) by
R^{\prime\prime}
the equivalence relation on
V^{\prime}

induced by
R
. It remains to verify that the relation
R^{\prime}

defined by
\Delta^{\prime}
coincides with
R^{\prime\prime}
.

By
4.3.2
all components of
R
have form

E_{S^{\perp}}\circ\Gamma_{w}
where
S
is an iso-set and
w\in W
.
By Proposition
2.5.3
, such a component gives rise to an irreducible component of
R^{\prime\prime}
iff

S^{\perp}\subset\alpha^{\perp}
and
w(S^{\perp})\subset\alpha^{\perp}
, that
is, iff
\{\alpha,w^{-1}(\alpha)\}\in S
.

If
\alpha=w^{-1}(\alpha)
, this
means that
w\in\mathtt{Stab}(\alpha^{\perp})
and
\alpha\in S
, so that the
component

E_{S^{\perp}}\circ\Gamma_{w}
determines a component of
R^{\prime}
.
If
\alpha\neq w^{-1}(\alpha)
,
there exists, by Lemma
4.2.2
, an element
y\in W
such that

y(\alpha)=w^{-1}(\alpha)
and
y(w^{-1}(\alpha))=\alpha
, and such that

y
acts trivially on the quotient
(\alpha,w^{-1}(\alpha))^{\perp}/\operatorname{Span}(\alpha,w^{-1}(\alpha))
. This implies that
y
acts also trivially
on the subquotient
S^{\perp}/\operatorname{Span}(S)
, so that

E_{S^{\perp}}\circ\Gamma_{y}=E_{S^{\perp}}
. Finally,

E_{S^{\perp}}\circ\Gamma_{w}=E_{S^{\perp}}\circ\Gamma_{y}\circ\Gamma_{wy^{-1}}=E_{S^{\perp}}\circ\Gamma_{wy^{-1}}
and
wy^{-1}(\alpha)=\alpha
which reduces this case to the case
w(\alpha)=\alpha
.
This proves the claim.
∎
