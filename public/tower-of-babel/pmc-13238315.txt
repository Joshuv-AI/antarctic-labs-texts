A Narrative Inquiry of Meaning Making and Leader Identity Development among College Students
Authors: Hannah M Sunderman, Kate D McCain, Jonathan Orsini, Tori Pedersen, Ethan Carlson, Grant Holst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLeader identity development (LID) and meaning making are essential and interconnected developmental processes. The current study explored LID and meaning making from childhood to emerging adulthood among college students (N = 26). Utilizing two open‐ended interviews and a narrative storytelling approach, we explored participants’ LID high, low, and turning points. A process of LID emerged in which leadership development experiences resulted in personal development outcomes following meaning‐making opportunities and a sense of personal worthiness.

Introduction

Grounded in meaning making, leader identity is how individuals make sense of themselves as leaders (Day and Harrison 2007). Leader identity has been regarded as critical to leader development (Miscenko et al. 2017). Leader identity is developed through meaning making, which are the cognitive processes that allow people to understand experiences (Zaar et al. 2020). While leader identity development (LID) is an outcome of meaning making, LID also fosters enhanced meaning‐making structures (Chung and Personette 2019). Among college students, leader identity is crucial to leadership development, and meaning making is essential to leadership education (Guthrie and Jenkins 2018; Komives et al. 2005, Komives et al. 2006; McCain and Matkin 2024).

While LID is critical for student development, and meaning making is necessary for LID, there has yet to be research explicitly purposed to explore the interaction of LID and meaning making among college students, the focus of the current study, despite previous scholars having proposed the connection (e.g., Hammond et al. 2017; Miscenko et al. 2017; Sunderman and Orsini 2024; Zaar et al. 2020). Therefore, we utilized open‐ended interviews with a narrative storytelling approach to understand LID high points, low points, and turning points (McAdams 2007) from childhood through emerging adulthood. Participants’ stories were inductively coding, and the findings revealed a process of LID where those who engaged in meaning making and felt worthiness (i.e., their view of leadership aligned with their view of themselves; Orsini et al. 2024b) following leadership experiences achieved personal development outcomes.

In tandem with the results from a scoping review on LID and meaning making (Sunderman and Orsini 2024), a portion of the emerging findings from the current research study were shared in Orsini et al. (2024b). Additionally, Sunderman et al. (2025) analyzed the leader identity low points specifically utilizing emotion coding, revealing an experience of coherent positive resolution in which the low points were reframed as periods of growth. The full research study and results, including implications for practice, are presented in the following article.

Literature Review

Leader Identity Development

LID has been a category in leadership development research for two decades (Vogel et al., 2021). While empirical research has been conducted on LID, there has been a wealth of theoretical work spanning contexts and ages (Sunderman and Orsini 2024; Vogel et al., 2021). Two lines of research have been influential in LID theory. First, Lord and Hall's (2005) work on leader identity and skill development proposed that developing leadership skills depended on engaging in leadership behaviors, which were more likely to occur when one saw oneself as a leader. As the leader identity became more prominent, it was increasingly important as a metacognitive structure that influenced the interpretation of a group's relational and collective actions (Lord and Hall, 2005).

Several manuscripts furthered Lord and Hall's (2005) model, including Day and Harrison's (2007) integrative model of LID and DeRue and Ashford's (2010) model of leadership identity construction. Day and Harrison (2007) conceived of leader identity as a “sub‐component of one's identity that relates to being a leader or how one thinks of oneself as a leader” (p. 365). DeRue and Ashford (2010) theorized that because leadership involved groups of people participating in an influence process, LID occurred across multiple domains including individual internalization, relational recognition, and collective endorsement, suggesting a more dynamic, social, and relational interpretation of LID.

The second line of research influencing LID scholarship is the grounded theory by Komives et al. (2005), which led to a six‐stage LID model among college students (Komives et al. 2006). Komives et al. 2005, Komives et al. 2006) suggested that LID was a cyclical process where students began with a simplistic conception of leadership and moved through stages with more complex understandings. The model was unique in that it integrated psychosocial development, cognitive theories of intellectual and moral development, social identity theories of leadership, and integrative theories of cognitive and psychosocial development (Komives et al., 2009). Without meaning‐making opportunities, students are less likely to make sense of their curricular and co‐curricular experiences in a way to enhances their leader identity, thus undermining their development.

Notably, intrapersonal development, which includes identity exploration and development, and interpersonal competence, which includes effective leadership, are key domains and learning outcomes of higher education, making them vital areas for student development (Council for the Advancement of Standards in Higher Education, 2023; Kezar, 2023). Underscoring the essentiality of leadership in higher education, Devies and Guthrie (2022) found 842 higher education mission statements included in the word “leader” or “leadership,” which is 16.6% of all higher education institutions in the United States.

Recent scholarship has also emphasized the temporal nature of leader development across the lifespan, including leadership experiences prior to higher education (Johnson et al. 2023). Cannon et al. (2024) conceptualized a model for early leader development that argued the leader mindset is composed of leader identity, leader knowledge structures, and leadership self‐efficacy, which begin in childhood. Despite identity being developed throughout the lifespan, few studies have addressed LID through a temporal lens (Orsini and Sunderman, 2024; Vogel et al., 2021). Liu et al. (2021) called for research attention to the role of LID in leadership development throughout developmental changes.

Meaning Making

Meaning making can be difficult to operationalize due to the vast amount of literature on the topic and the lack of consensus behind a single overarching theory (Weick 1995). Fundamentally, meaning allows individuals to make sense of experiences. Krauss (2005) suggested that meaning is an epistemological understanding of what is happening plus a teleological account of why something is happening. Meaning making is critically important to human motivation because meaning provides the cognitive structures to interpret lived experiences (Krauss 2005). Scholars and philosophers have understood the meaning‐making process as essential to human development, especially in educational contexts, for almost 100 years. Dewey (1933) wrote, “Only when things about us have meaning for us is any such thing as intentional, deliberative control of them possible” (p. 19). Because meaning making includes both retrospective and prospective thinking to construct interpretations of reality, meaning making and narrative construction are highly interconnected (Weick 1995).

Ibarra and Barbulescu (2010) suggested that narratives are a primary mechanism for making meaning and reconstructing identities during transitions. Kegan (1994) proposed that people are “active organizers of their experience” through meaning‐making processes that develop through stages over time. Kegan's (1994) foundational work influenced Baxter Magolda's (2008) theory of self‐authorship, which explained an individual's capacity to define and describe one's identity and beliefs through narrative. Meaning making, therefore, is deeply connected to identity development through reflective interpretation of experience.

LID and Meaning Making

Scholars have identified meaning making as necessary for LID (Chung and Personette 2019; Miscenko et al. 2017; Owen 2023). In a recent scoping review of 100 articles at the intersection of LID and meaning making, over half were published since 2017, highlighting a growing scholarly emphasis on the connection between these constructs (Sunderman and Orsini 2024). Several recent theoretical articles have explicitly discussed the relationship between meaning making and LID, proposing that changes in meaning‐making structures can both influence and result from LID (Chung and Personette 2019; Zaar et al. 2020).

One seminal example of the importance of meaning making in the LID process was Hammond et al. (2017) model explaining LID across multiple domains, which indicated how identity drives the internal creation of meanings. However, despite recognition of this relationship, there remain notable gaps in the literature. Few studies have explored how individual development, such as meaning‐making capacity, influences LID (Miscenko et al. 2017; Vogel et al., 2021). Additionally, Sunderman and Orsini (2024) found that only two of 100 articles utilized personal narratives, despite narrative being considered integral to meaning making (Cannon et al. 2024).

Furthermore, few studies have addressed LID using a temporal or lifespan perspective, despite identity being developed throughout the lifespan (Erikson 1968; Mitchell et al. 2021). While much of the LID literature focuses on college students in the present moment, retrospective storytelling has been proposed as a method to approximate a lifespan approach to leader identity development. Scholars have argued retrospective sensemaking supports temporal identity integration by connecting past, present, and future views of the self (Syed and McLean 2016).

While the current study is not longitudinal, it nonetheless sought to approximate the need for a temporal lens in LID research by asking participants to reflect on LID beginning in childhood through retrospective storytelling, rather than focusing on their LID in their current stage as college students. Considered holistically, the literature suggests that LID is deeply intertwined with meaning making through reflection, narrative construction, and temporal sensemaking. LID is influenced by how individuals interpret leadership experiences, how they define leadership, and how these definitions align with their evolving self‐concept. As such, centering meaning‐making processes is essential to understanding how individuals come to see themselves as leaders across time and contexts.

Methods

The current qualitative study utilized narrative inquiry to explore LID and meaning making from childhood to emerging adulthood. In line with communicated narrative sensemaking (CNSM), we focused on the content and process of participants’ stories (Koenig Kellas 2017). Notably, CNSM connects stories with individual and relational identity development, making the CNSM framework particularly relevant for LID (McCain and Matkin 2024). Our scope was the content of leader identity stories across college students’ lifespan.

Participants

Participants were college students from 19 to 48 years old (M = 20; N = 26; see Table 1). Participants were recruited through IRB‐approved emails and enrolled in one of four large, predominantly white institutions across the United States: Midwestern, Southeastern, Southwestern, and Mid‐Atlantic. The institutions were chosen based on the affiliations and access of the research team. Purposeful sampling required participants to be college students engaged in leadership. At Midwestern (n = 17) participants were in a leadership minor or a leadership mentoring program. At Southwestern (n = 6) participants were in a national leadership honor society. At Southeastern (n = 2) participants were in an interdisciplinary leadership minor. At Mid‐Atlantic (n = 1) participants were in a leadership minor.

Data Collection

The interviews were conducted via Zoom and transcribed during the 2021–2022 academic year. The data collection process consisted of two interviews conducted by Undergraduate Research Assistants (URAs). The first interview was a narrative inquiry approach exploring high points, low points, and turning points in LID from childhood through adulthood. Adapted from McAdams' (2007) life narrative interview, a high point was an episode or moment that stood out as an especially positive LID experience. A low point was a negative or bad LID experience. A turning point was an episode that marked an important change in LID.

For each scene, interviewers asked participants to share the story in detail (e.g., when, where, who, and what feelings encompassed the experience). At the start of the first interview, participants completed a turning point graph, mapping their LID over time (i.e., the y‐axis was LID; the x‐axis was time: childhood through emerging adulthood; Orsini et al. 2024a). The turning points graph were utilized for reflection and data triangulation (Creswell and Poth 2018). In the second interview, participants elaborated on the LID experiences they mapped on their turning point graph (e.g., “Describe how you knew your leader identity was developing”).

Data Analysis

Interviews were transcribed and inductively coded for thematic results. An inductive analysis allowed codes and themes to emerge organically through participants’ responses, providing insight into how participants framed their LID and meaning‐making experiences (Miles et al. 2014). The initial coding phase consisted of three groups with two researchers per group (i.e., one URA and one faculty) analyzing two interviews. We met to discuss and determine intercoder agreement by comparing significant statements and codes to identify convergence and divergence through a process of reflexivity, dialogue, and multiple refinements of code (Burkholder et al. 2020).

A codebook was established, which was utilized by the URAs to analyze the remaining interviews. Overall, 2,090 significant statements emerged, which were collapsed into 15 categories. The 15 categories were collapsed into four themes. Data were verified using memoing and triangulation of multiple data points. Given the research team members’ experience with the phenomenon and the proximity of the URAs to the sample (i.e., college students engaged in leadership programs), reflexive discussions regarding positionality were included in the weekly meetings (e.g., “How have your social identities shaped your LID?”).

Findings

Results revealed a process of LID where those who engaged in meaning making and felt worthiness (i.e., their view of leadership aligned with their view of themselves; Orsini et al. 2024b) following leadership development experiences achieved personal development outcomes (see Figure 1). In line with narrative research, the results are reported following the chronological description of the LID process that emerged from participants’ stories (Creswell and Poth 2018).

A Process Model of Leader Identity Development through Meaning Making.

Leadership Development Experiences

Throughout the high, low, and turning point stories, participants shared that the process of LID began with a leadership development experience, which included experiences with extracurricular activities, formal education, destructive leadership, and self‐doubt.

Participants frequently told stories of extracurricular involvements and activities as the first step in their chronological process of LID. For example, Kara shared that her leader identity began when she was a babysitter, an experience she described as “baby steps.” Leadership experiences continued to further Kara's LID: “The high point [would be] where I did the camp leader [at] the children's hospital…because I worked with older people and younger people.” Kara expressed that these experiences of informal leadership prepared her to engage in leadership coursework. Leadership coursework then allowed her to make meaning of her previous experiences as LID catalysts.

Building on Kara's narrative, additional participants discussed formal leadership education opportunities as the leadership development experiences that cultivated their leader identity. Josh described his experience in a leadership minor as the first step of a LID process in which he engaged in reflection and experienced personal development. Josh said, “When I was 19, I started learning more about leadership. I added a leadership minor, and I started to take on more leadership roles…I've been able to learn and apply a bunch of those skills and see the impact.”

Other participants discussed leadership positions as central to their LID. For example, Michelle discussed her position as a manager:

I would say one of the high points, a couple of years after high school in college I got in [job position], and I started at an entry‐level for about a year. And then…I was asked to apply and interview for a manager. That's a high point only because it really showed me my capability from just being a student.

Through their low‐point stories, participants provided insight into how negative leadership experiences (e.g., burnout, destructive leaders, isolation, lack of confidence, and self‐doubt) also influenced their LID. Haley described how her leader identity developed after making sense of being burnt out from too many leadership roles:

Being overwhelmed with a lot of things and thinking that I wouldn't be as good of a leader because I was dropping positions or things, so recognizing that as a low point and then looking back at it now, I see that taking something off my plate was the most leadership thing I could have done. When you think you can only have power if you have that title and need to do everything for everyone, then you aren't really being a good leader because you aren't focused, and you don't have time to dedicate your resources to the things that really matter to you. I've grown from that, knowing that it's okay to say no, and it's okay to ask for help because then it makes you more powerful.

Haley's decision to quit some leadership roles became a redemptive sequence as she redefined what it meant to be a leader and experience personal development through “knowing my limits.”

Overarchingly, participants discussed leadership development experiences, ranging from childhood to emerging adulthood, as central to how they viewed themselves as leaders. While some participants described extracurricular and informal activities and negative leadership experiences as early as elementary school, formal leadership education experiences and leadership roles occurred in late high school or college. Interestingly, the context of the leadership development experiences shared by participants were widely varied, including military, band, and FFA, emphasizing that the places and spaces in which LID occurs are broad.

Meaning Making

Following leadership development experiences, participants described two different types of meaning‐making experiences that influenced their LID: (a) meaning making through reflection and (b) meaning making through affirmation and seeing impact.

Meaning Making through Reflection

When discussing meaning‐making experiences that connected to their LID, participants highlighted that reflection occurred in a variety of formats: (a) internally (e.g., individual thought), (b) externally (e.g., with friends, mentors, and classes), (c) retrospectively (i.e., reflection on previous experiences); and (d) concurrently (i.e., reflection on current experiences). Regarding internal reflection, Cooper recognized a shift in his leader identity to “serve others” through religious reflection. Cooper explained:

[Religion] was a big turning point for me because instead of wanting to serve myself and be on top of the podium, I really took on the form of just being a servant leader…I started to work with kids that were about 15, 16 years old, and I was about 20…That is the reason…why I'm able to be here, who I am today, and be the leader I believe I am.

Regarding external reflection, Allison described how reflection in leadership classes contributed to her LID, “Studying leadership throughout my courses, a lot of our assignments are reflective based and that has allowed me to reflect on different aspects of who I am as a leader…even formal, written reflections over time start to make a difference.”

Concerning retrospective reflection, Ashley reflected on early experiences that influenced her LID. Ashley described getting lost and receiving help from another child, who was a new student in their class. Ashley included the new student, demonstrating her care for relationships. Ashley said, “If I would've been asked to reflect in early high school, I probably would've noticed that [experience] and that would have helped me live into my leadership skills sooner,” emphasizing how retrospective reflection allowed her to recognize her younger self as a leader.

Related to concurrent reflection, after being rejected from a leadership position, Michael said “I remember going back to my dorm, I was upset…then I texted someone that I was close with, he's like, oh, let's go get lunch…we'll talk it over at lunch…it was that day where I realized, you know, they're [the selection commitment] wrong. I know what I'm doing [to lead] is right.” In sum, participants explicated meaning‐making moments that occurred within themselves and through conversations with others that allowed leadership development experiences to shift their leader identity.

Meaning Making through Affirmation and Impact

In addition to making meaning of their developmental experiences through reflection, participants found meaning through receiving affirmation and seeing their impact, further highlighting the essential role of interactions with others as meaning‐making experiences that further LID. Nadia shared a story where she saw her impact as a tutor to a high school student receiving a D in science. Through working together, the student received a B+ and, ultimately, attended college. Nadia said:

I went to her graduation party…she gave me a letter that was thanking me… I was so shocked…I didn't think I was doing that much for her. It was just giving her the tools necessary to succeed. But then after reading that letter, I understood that I had done more than that. I think that was a really big high, because not only did it influence my leadership capacity, it really influenced what I wanted to do in the future too.

Participants also shared specific individuals (e.g., supervisor or teacher) who affirmed their leader identity. For example, when Ashley was named band captain, the band director shared “why I was selected and that opened my eyes to [my leader identity]. It was something that I was doing naturally, and then it got brought to my attention.” These stories illustrate participants' experiences with receiving affirmation and seeing their impact as meaning‐making moments that allowed leadership development experiences to shift LID.

Worthiness

The meaning participants made of their leadership developmental experiences was impacted by congruence or incongruence between their conceptualization of themselves as leaders and their conceptualization of leadership (i.e., “worthiness”), which is connected to self‐worth theory (Covington 2009; Orsini et al. 2024b). When participants’ view of leadership (e.g., “Leaders are in charge of a group”) matched their view of themselves (e.g., “I am in charge of a group, so I am a leader”), they were more likely to see themselves as a leader. Conversely, when participants’ view of leadership (e.g., “Leaders are in charge of a group”) did not match their view of themselves (e.g., “I am not in charge of a group, so I am not a leader”), they experienced incongruence and either changed their view of leadership or change their view of themselves to achieve congruence.

While participants often had a position‐oriented view of leadership in elementary, middle, and high school, participants frequently noted a shift to a process‐oriented view of leadership while in college (Komives et al. 2005, Komives et al. 2006). For example, Allison found meaning in her position as a state leader with a career development program; therefore, Allison's LID low point occurred when her state office position was over (Orsini et al. 2024b). At the turning point of entering college and leaving formal leadership roles, Allison realized, “I don't need to be the chairperson…to be recognized as a leader,” demonstrating a changing view of leadership to bring alignment between their view of leadership (i.e., leadership doesn't require a position) and their behavior (i.e., Allison did not hold a formal leadership position but could still be a leader).

Maria, in reflecting on the turning point of her LID, shared that leadership positions were initially extremely important, “Getting leadership, getting the title of ‘I am a leader,’ helps reinforce that I am a leader.” However, after facing several rejections during her first year of college, Maria reflected that her LID turning point was “just getting over myself and realizing I have other opportunities to make a difference on campus.” When Maria experienced incongruence between seeing leadership as “titles” and then being rejected from leadership experiences at the start of college, she redefined leadership to go beyond positions and, in so doing, continued to see herself as a leader.

Chris described his LID high point during senior year of high school, highlighting the intersection of meaning making through affirmation and worthiness:

It was after our last football game… I wasn't like a team captain but some of the upperclassmen came up to me and like, “we really appreciate what you did for the team. At practice we tried to copy you and follow your example” …I didn't really think I was having that big of an impact but made me realize the impact I was having.

Looking back on his high point four years later, Chris shared, “Some of my identity came from that recognition…I relied upon it to find my worth…And I just see that still today with different organizations I'm in where it's like someone doesn't have to say ‘you're a leader’ for you, for me, to be a leader.” Chris’ experience with affirmation shifted his view of himself and his view of leadership, allowing him to see himself as a leader even when he wasn't in a leadership position. In sum, participants’ meaning making of their leadership development experiences was influenced by worthiness, which is alignment between how individuals define leadership and how they define themselves (Orsini et al. 2024b).

Personal Development Outcomes

As participants engaged in meaning making and shifted their conceptualizations of worthiness, they experienced personal development. Specifically, participants described self‐awareness and self‐exploration following LID and meaning‐making experiences. Josh explained, “I'm able to reflect and learn a lot more…and I'm trying to apply it as opposed to just regurgitating it.” In addition to self‐awareness, participants experienced enhanced confidence, collaboration, and skill. Nadia shared, “In high school, I started to have more confidence in not only who I am, but what I had to say… This started to solidify my identity…and what I had to offer in terms of my leadership.”

Participants also perceived a change in skill following the LID process, including enhanced adaptability, communication, and emotional intelligence. Jessie shared, “I think it was more than developing as a leader per se, it was developing my skills and the attributes that made me strong and helped me stand out, which I was then able to develop throughout my life to become a leader.” In sum, through leader identity high, low, and turning points, participants made meaning of leadership development experiences in line with their view of worthiness, which contributed to perceived personal development.

Discussion

The results of the current narrative inquiry on LID and meaning making among college students offer significant contributions to the fields of leadership development and student affairs, arguing for (a) recognizing the diversity of experiences across the lifespan that facilitate LID in college students; (b) centering meaning‐making experiences in both the theory and practice of LID; and (c) recognizing worthiness as a component of LID.

First, the leadership development experiences described by participants were varied, ranging from work to band to mentoring. The breadth of experiences illustrates how the contexts and settings in which LID can be developed and validated are expansive, frequently beginning in childhood. While previous research on LID has largely focused on a particular time or setting (e.g., college), the current results emphasize, that the LID of college students begins at a young age (Cannon et al. 2024).

Second, participants' descriptions of their high, low, and turning points stressed the centrality of meaning making following leadership development experiences in LID. Notably, meaning making occurred retrospectively and concurrently (Weick 1995), as well as internally and externally. Ryan et al. (2024) emphasized the benefits of reflecting on early life experiences to support leadership development, saying, “Leading acts may not be explicitly labeled as ‘leadership’ at the time of enactment but may be retrospectively identified as such upon reflection…This retrospective sense‐making is done in the present but is strongly determined by the past and shapes the future understanding of oneself as a leader” (p. 613). Retrospective sense‐making supports the temporal identity integration (i.e., a cohesive sense of self across time).

Finally, the results indicated worthiness is a central element of college student LID (Orsini et al. 2024b). When participants’ perceptions of themselves were congruent with their perceptions of leadership (which is connected to their implicit view of leadership; Offerman and Coates, 2018), the meaning made of leadership development experiences catalyzed LID and personal growth. When participants experienced incongruence (i.e., their view of themselves was not aligned with their perceptions of leadership), they described feeling frustrated or confused, seeking to reconcile the incongruence. Theoretically, these results demonstrate that LID is intertwined with implicit views of leadership and self‐concept, supporting previous work by Latta (2021) that linked LID and implicit leadership theories to understand “views of the current self with aspirational leader identities” (p. 47).

It is noteworthy that implicit leadership theory and self‐worth theory, despite being separate streams of research, are similar because how individuals interpret events and attribute meaning is central to evaluations of both leadership and worthiness, a conceptualization affirmed in our findings. In the current study, participants demonstrated that the desire to feel worthy could motivate a change in self‐concept to align with perceptions of leadership to be more congruent with self‐concept. For additional discussion of some of the psychosocial constructs connected to our operationalization of worthiness (e.g., mattering, Rosenberg and McCullough 1981; self‐esteem, Mruk 2013; and self‐worth theory, Covington 2009), see Orsini et al. (2024b), which discussed worthiness as a preliminary result of the current research with further implications for teaching college student LID.

While it can be tempting to overlook meaning making due to programmatic and time constraints (Crookall 2010), leadership educators and student affairs professionals are essential in helping individuals make meaning of leadership experiences throughout their lifespan so that students see themselves as leaders (Komives et al. 2005, Komives et al. 2006). In encouraging students to reflect on LID throughout their lifespans and drawing connections between who students have been and who students are, educators may support the continued development of students’ leader identity and identity integration, including temporal identity integration, “the coordination of past, present, and future views of self” (Syed and McLean 2016, p. 112).

The clear articulation by participants of leader identity experiences beginning in elementary school, along with the benefits of retrospective reflection, encourages leadership educators to approach LID with a lifespan perspective (Erikson 1968; Mitchell et al. 2021; Cannon et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2021). Practitioners engaging students in LID might structure reflection on where individuals have been and where they are currently, utilizing methods such as turning point graphing and interviewing to facilitate reflection (Orsini et al. 2024a). Finally, to maximize LID, leadership educators are urged to facilitate reflection on how students see themselves, for example by pausing group discussions to analyze leadership as it is occurring and connecting these moments to students’ views of themselves as leaders (Weng and Hubbard 2022).

Limitations and Future Research Directions

The current study possessed several limitations. Although we had participants from four institutions, some institutions were more represented than others. We also utilized convenience sampling to recruit participants who had expressed an interest in leadership (e.g., leadership minor, mentoring program). Future research is encouraged to explore LID and meaning making among students engaging in leadership through informal channels. Additionally, while narrative methods allow for participant voice, the stories are unique, thus different participants had different LID narratives.

Specifically, when asked about what other identities influenced their LID, some participants expressed the influence of minoritized cultural, racial, and religious identities, yet most participants struggled to identify other social identities that influenced their LID. Therefore, minoritized social identities did not emerge as a theme. The effects of minoritized identities were likely underreported because of the sample. We urge researchers to continue exploring the impact of minoritized social identities on LID (Fuselier and Beatty 2023), particularly within the context of meaning making (Orsini and Sunderman 2024).

We encourage future researchers to extend the current results. Namely, three participants shared how quitting a position or organization shaped their LID and contributed to their worthiness. Future research may explore quitting and other events initially perceived as failure as acts of LID. Additionally, we recommend an in‐depth analysis of worthiness and meaning making in LID and quantitative testing of the proposed framework among a larger and more diverse sample. Finally, conducting longitudinal research on stories of LID and meaning making (e.g., data collection at four timepoints throughout college) would support the development of narrative‐based interventions for a variety of college student populations.
