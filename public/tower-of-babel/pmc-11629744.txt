Daily glycome and transcriptome profiling reveals polysaccharide structures and correlated glycosyltransferases critical for cotton fiber growth
Authors: Sivakumar Swaminathan, Corrinne E. Grover, Alither S. Mugisha, Lauren E. Sichterman, Youngwoo Lee, Pengcheng Yang, Eileen L. Mallery, Josef J. Jareczek, Alexis G. Leach, Jun Xie, Jonathan F. Wendel, Daniel B. Szymanski, Olga A. Zabotina
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYCotton fiber is the most valuable naturally available material for the textile industry and the fiber length and strength are key determinants of its quality. Dynamic changes in the pectin, xyloglucan, xylan, and cellulose polysaccharide epitope content during fiber growth contribute to complex remodeling of fiber cell wall (CW) and quality. Detailed knowledge about polysaccharide compositional and structural alteration in the fiber during fiber elongation and strengthening is important to understand the molecular dynamics of fiber development and improve its quality. Here, large‐scale glycome profiling coupled with fiber phenotype and transcriptome profiling was conducted on fiber collected daily covering the most critical window of fiber development. The profiling studies with high temporal resolution allowed us to identify specific polysaccharide epitopes associated with distinct fiber phenotypes that might contribute to fiber quality. This study revealed the critical role of highly branched RG‐I pectin epitopes such as β‐1,4‐linked‐galactans, β‐1,6‐linked‐galactans, and arabinogalactans, in addition to earlier reported homogalacturonans and xyloglucans in the formation of cotton fiber middle lamella and contributing to fiber plasticity and elongation. We also propose the essential role of heteroxylans (Xyl‐MeGlcA and Xyl‐3Ar), as a guiding factor for secondary CW cellulose microfibril arrangement, thus contributing to fiber strength. Correlation analysis of profiles of polysaccharide epitopes from glycome data and expression profiles of glycosyltransferase‐encoding genes from transcriptome data identified several key putative glycosyltransferases that are potentially involved in synthesizing the critical polysaccharide epitopes. The findings of this study provide a foundation to identify molecular factors that dictate important fiber traits.

INTRODUCTION

Cotton (Gossypium species) is the most widely used natural textile fiber globally, with an estimated value of US $64 billion annually (USDA, 2024). Four cotton species have been domesticated, and among them Gossypium hirsutum is most widely planted because of its high yield and environmental adaptability (Hu et al., 2021; Jareczek et al., 2023; Viot & Wendel, 2023). Cotton fiber trichomes reach about 2.5–4 cm in length during 45–50 days post‐anthesis (DPA). The matured fiber is a dried cell wall (CW) containing about 95% cellulose and traces of non‐cellulosic polysaccharides like callose, pectins, hemicelluloses, dried glycoproteins, sugars, and minerals (Haigler et al., 2012). During development, the CW polysaccharides are synthesized and dynamically undergo remodeling, which results in finer industrial‐quality fibers. The quality of the fiber varies between cotton species, which is determined by fiber length and strength (Kim & Triplett, 2001).

Fiber development is an intricately regulated process and there are morphological landmarks that have been used to define key events. It is often divided into five sequential overlapping events: initiation and tapering, elongation, transition, secondary CW (SCW) deposition, and desiccation/maturation (Haigler et al., 2012; Stiff & Haigler, 2016). Unicellular trichoblasts (fiber initials) bud from the seed surface at −1 DPA, and undergo tapering and it is a precise cytoskeleton‐dependent CW remodeling phase, which is completed by 2 DPA (Graham & Haigler, 2021; Stiff & Haigler, 2016; Yanagisawa et al., 2022). Primary CW (PCW) synthesis occurs over the first 2–3 weeks, during which cotton fiber develops its characteristic length and fineness (Applequist et al., 2001; Meinert & Delmer, 1977). Fiber elongation rates begin to slow early in development, well before the transition to SCW synthesis (Schubert et al., 1973; Wilson et al., 2024). During the transition stage (16–20 DPA), cessation of PCW, beginning of SCW cellulose accumulation, and CW thickening occur. A drastic increase in SCW cellulose deposition, as well as CW thickening, happens during 16–35 DPA. Thereafter, the cytoplasm dries out, and the dried matured fibers twist, which makes them easily spun into threads (Haigler et al., 2012; Kim & Triplett, 2001).

Cotton fiber development is a complex process, involving the coordination of gene expression networks, hormone signaling, physiology, and biosynthesis that determine CW polysaccharide composition, which in turn determines fiber quality (Haigler et al., 2012; Jan et al., 2022; Jareczek et al., 2023). Complex gene regulation and interactions underlie cotton fiber development, as noted by the array of genome and transcriptome studies that have compared cotton fiber development among species, among accession, and/or among conditions (Gallagher et al., 2020; Gilbert et al., 2014; Grover et al., 2024; Islam et al., 2016; Jareczek et al., 2023; Li et al., 2020; MacMillan et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2022; You et al., 2023). This wealth of information has revealed numerous transcription factors and gene networks, whose complex interrelationships result in differences in the final cotton fiber phenotype (e.g., length, strength, etc). Earlier studies on Arabidopsis thaliana genome predicted to encode over 570 glycosyltransferases (GTs) belonging to 42 carbohydrate‐active enzymes (CAZYs) families accounting for approximately 2% of genome. The richness of GTs mainly involved in the synthesis of polysaccharides in plants is attributed to the presence of polysaccharide‐rich CW (Cantarel et al., 2009; Xuan et al., 2021). However, many of the Arabidopsis GTs are not functionally characterized and the number is much higher in cotton (Xuan et al., 2021). Functionally characterized cotton GTs reported so far, are presented in the “Discussion” section.

Several metabolic studies have been conducted on fiber CW polysaccharides which include, monosaccharide composition analysis, glycan microarrays‐polysaccharide linkage analysis, microscopy techniques involving staining and immunolocalization, and glycome profiling with antibodies recognizing plant polysaccharide epitopes (Avci et al., 2013; Guo et al., 2019; Hernandez‐Gomez et al., 2015, 2017; Pettolino et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2009). These and other studies demonstrated that the fiber CW composition changes dynamically throughout development, especially during elongation and the transition to SCW synthesis. Nevertheless, the finer details of some of the non‐cellulosic polysaccharides' epitope structures during fiber development are needed to completely understand the molecular dynamics of fiber growth.

In young fiber between 0 and 2 DPA, a pectin‐rich, xyloglucan‐depleted outer sheath develops. By 4 DPA, the synthesis of a cotton fiber middle‐lamella (CFML) is formed. The CFML serves to join adjacent fibers into tissue‐like, highly organized, and tightly packed bundles, which eventually facilitates fiber elongation smoothly within the confined boll space (Haigler et al., 2012). Recent studies on glycome profiling of fiber with numerous monoclonal antibodies recognizing polysaccharide epitopes reported the presence of both fucosylated‐ and non‐fucosylated xyloglucans, pectic arabinans/galactans, and homogalacturonans (HGs) in CFML (Avci et al., 2013; Guo et al., 2019; Hernandez‐Gomez et al., 2017; Singh et al., 2009; Vaughn & Turley, 1999), and callose, cellulose, and heteromannans at PCW (Hernandez‐Gomez et al., 2015).

During the growth phase, the impact of fiber turgor pressure, expansin (CW loosening protein), expression of a broader class of CW‐synthesizing/remodeling enzymes, and the dynamics of polysaccharides in PCW and CFML, and cellulose microfibril decides the fate of cotton fiber plasticity, expansion, length, and spinnability, in different species (An et al., 2007; Harmer et al., 2002; Keynia et al., 2022; Michailidis et al., 2009; Orford & Timmis, 1998; Shao et al., 2011; Shimizu et al., 1997; Yanagisawa et al., 2022).

During the transition stage, transcriptionally regulated CW degrading enzymes break down the CFML, which leads to the reduction of pectin and xyloglucan molecular mass and the separation of fibers (Guo et al., 2019; Meinert & Delmer, 1977; Singh et al., 2009; Tokumoto et al., 2002). Also during the transition stage, a unique “winding” CW layer is formed with differently oriented cellulose microfibrils than the microfibrils usually seen in PCW (Seagull, 1993). Also, during the transition stage, callose deposition reaches a peak, and it was proposed that both the callose and cellulose might be involved in the formation of the winding layer and contributing to fiber strength (Hinchliffe et al., 2011; Hsieh et al., 1995; Kerr, 1946).

At the onset of SCW thickening, the rate of cellulose synthesis increases drastically, and both microtubules and cellulose fibrils adopt a steep helix relative to the fiber axis (Abidi et al., 2014; Maltby et al., 1979; Martin & Haigler, 2004; Meinert & Delmer, 1977; Seagull, 1993). The SCW mainly contains cellulose, and its content and biophysical properties, such as degree of crystallinity, fibril angle‐twist, and degree of polymerization mainly decide the strength of the fiber (Haigler et al., 2012).

Earlier research clearly points out that the fiber elongation and the transition stage (interplay of elongation and the beginning of SCW thickening), are the most crucial stages that determine the outcome of fiber quality (Haigler et al., 2012; Jan et al., 2022; Jareczek et al., 2023; Tuttle et al., 2015). In the present study, large‐scale glycome and transcriptome profiling (Grover et al., 2024) was carried out in parallel with fiber‐phenotyping (Wilson et al., 2024) on fibers purified daily from 6 to 25 DPA for 20 consecutive days covering the critical fiber elongation and the transition stages. A broad collection of carbohydrate‐specific monoclonal antibodies was utilized for glycome profiling in combination with monosaccharide analyses. Further, correlation analysis was carried out between the polysaccharide epitopes (from glycome data) and fiber phenotypes and GT genes (from transcriptome data) involved in synthesizing the critical polysaccharide epitopes. Correlation analysis identified putative target GT genes that are predicted to influence cotton fiber CW composition. Our study focused exclusively on GTs, which might play a key role in synthesizing polysaccharide‐rich CW of cotton fiber. Detailed global transcriptome analysis of more than 74 000 G. hirsutum genes is reported in a collaborative study (Grover et al., 2024). Overall, our high temporal resolution study has discovered several additional to earlier reported CW polysaccharide epitopes and GTs that could be key factors contributing to fiber length, strength, and quality of the cotton fiber.

RESULTS

Polysaccharide and monosaccharide composition during cotton fiber development (6–25 DPA)

Alcohol insoluble CW, dried‐lyophilized buffer soluble pectin‐enriched‐fraction (50 mm CDTA:50 mm ammonium oxalate buffer extracts), alkali‐soluble hemicellulose‐enriched‐fraction (4 m KOH extracts), and total cellulose (amorphous and crystalline) from 6 to 25 DPA cotton fibers were each weighed and summarized (Figure 1a; Figure S1; Table S1). As expected, the weight of total CW content and content of polysaccharide fractions per boll increased gradually from 6 to 25 DPA (Table S1a). However, percentage‐wise, the pectin, and hemicellulose, in the total CW slowly started decreasing from 32.7 to 22.3% at 6 DPA and reached around 6.6 and 7.3% by 25 DPA, respectively (Figure 1a; Table S1b). In contrast, the total cellulose increased gradually from 26.4% at 6 DPA, reached 40% at 16 DPA, and increased drastically afterward to reach 81.9% at 25 DPA. The crystalline cellulose content was 2.6% at 6 DPA and reached 13.6% at 16 DPA. After 16 DPA, it rapidly increased and reached 52.4% by 25 DPA.

Monosaccharide composition of buffer soluble pectin‐enriched‐fractions showed that the relative content of arabinose slightly increased (17–23%) and glucose significantly increased (13–31%) between 6 and 25 DPA (Figure 1b; Table S2a). The content of the xylose (4.6 to 1.6%), mannose (5.4 to 2.3%), fucose (0.6 to 0.2%), and GalA (20 to 5.1%) significantly decreased between 6 and 25 DPA. The monosaccharide composition of alkali‐soluble hemicellulose‐enriched‐fractions showed that glucose content (38.5–74.2%) significantly increased from 6 to 25 DPA (Figure 1c; Table S2b). The content of arabinose slightly decreased (7.1 to 2.6%) between 6 and 22 DPA. Galactose (19.4 to 8.5%), xylose (13.9 to 4.7%), and mannose (14.4 to 1.5%) showed significant reductions between 6 and 25 DPA.

Glycome profiling and self‐organizing maps of diverse epitope patterns of the polysaccharides

Seventy‐one different antibodies were used to profile cotton fiber polysaccharide epitopes (Figure 2; Table S3). Since the ELISA data for each epitope originate from two different fractions of the same sample, for practicality, the naming of epitopes was suffixed with either “‐P” for buffer soluble pectin‐enriched‐fractions or “‐HC” for alkali‐soluble hemicellulose‐enriched‐fractions. The glycome data provided the temporal variability of CW composition as a function of developmental time. A challenge in this form of data analysis is to objectively define groups of epitopes that share similar temporal profiles. Using the data obtained from the glycome profiling of buffer soluble and alkali‐soluble fractions (Tables S4 and S5), self‐organizing maps (SOM)‐based clusters and heat maps were generated to visualize and classify the polysaccharide epitope distribution patterns (Figure 3; Table S6). SOM for the buffer soluble fractions showed that most of the polysaccharide epitope patterns started with a higher amount at around 6 DPA, and gradually reduced until 25 DPA (Figure 3a). In contrast, the SOM for the alkali‐soluble fractions showed more variability in the epitope patterns (Figure 3c). The heat maps displayed clearly that the epitope profile pattern was reproducible among all developmental stages of all the three biological replications sampled. Both the SOM and heat maps conclusively allowed us to group the epitopes further into four “categories” (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4; Figures 4, 5, 6, 7).

The first category includes 51 different polysaccharide epitopes that are highly abundant at early stages and reduced with varying slopes and timings to low levels by 25 DPA (Table 1; Figure 4). Most of the buffer‐soluble pectin, xyloglucan, and xylan epitopes displayed this pattern. Interestingly, each of these three polysaccharides distinctly formed three different separate subgroups within the first category. For example, most of the pectin epitopes located in a set of nearby SOM groups and formed a distinct subgroup (SOM groups G3‐P, G7‐P, G8‐P, G11‐P, G12‐P, G15‐P, G16‐P, and G19‐P; Figures 3a and 4a; Table S6a), where their content declined slowly starting from 12 DPA. On the contrary, the content of most of the xyloglucan (SOM groups G1‐P, G2‐P, G5‐P, and G6‐P; Figures 3a and 4b) and RG‐I‐β‐1,4‐linked‐galactan epitopes (SOM group G1‐P; Figures 3a and 4b) reduced drastically around 16 DPA, coinciding with the transition stage to SCW synthesis, which forms the second subgroup. The third subgroup mainly has the xylan epitopes (SOM groups G9‐P, G10‐P, G13‐P, G14‐P, and G17‐P; Figures 3a and 4c), and their content reduced slowly and gradually from the beginning of 6 DPA.

Another set of SOM groups containing 14 of the epitopes (all from the alkali‐soluble fraction) were placed in a second category (Table 2; Figure 5). The content of these epitopes was either low at early DPAs and then rapidly increased around 12 DPA (Figure 5a) or gradually increased from the beginning (Figure 5b) to a higher level at later stages. Highly branched RG‐I epitopes (β‐1,6 linked galactans and arabinogalactans; SOM groups G13‐HC, G14‐HC, G17‐HC, and G18‐HC) and three of the xylan epitopes (SOM groups G14‐HC and G17‐HC) constitute this second category (Figures 3c and 5; Table S6b).

Correlation analysis of cotton fiber polysaccharide epitopes and fiber phenotypes

Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) analyses were carried out between the profiles of glycome epitopes and profiles of fiber phenotypes generated simultaneously from a related collaborative study (Wilson et al., 2024) to predict the potential epitopes involved in governing the phenotypes. Phenotypes such as fiber elongation rate, turgor pressure, microfibril orientation, and cellulose content were used, and a PCC cut‐off value of ≥0.7 was considered to be a strong positive correlation (Table S7; Figure S2a).

PCC results revealed that many of the pectin and buffer‐soluble xyloglucan and xylan epitopes categorized in Table 1 significantly correlated and closely mirrored the decreasing fiber growth rates from 6 to 25 DPA (Figure 8a; Table S7). Few of the pectin and xyloglucan epitopes highly correlate with the fiber turgor pressure profile, which peaked at 16 DPA and reduced drastically to a low level at later stages (Figure 8b; Table S7).

The profile of microfibril orientation rapidly changes around 16 DPA, coinciding with the transition to SCW synthesis (Figure 8c; Wilson et al., 2024). Profiles of three of the xylan epitopes (Xyl‐MeGlcA‐1‐HC, Xyl‐MeGlcA‐2‐HC, and Xyl‐3Ar‐HC) and two of the highly branched RG‐I epitopes (RG‐I‐Gal3‐6‐HC and RG‐I‐Gal3‐7‐HC) were highly correlated with the profile of microfibril orientation (Figure 8c; Table S7). Interestingly, the profile of microfibril orientation is similar to the profile of cellulose content (Figure 8d). The cellulose content drastically increased after 16 DPA coinciding with the beginning of SCW synthesis, and here also the profiles of three of the above‐mentioned xylan epitopes highly correlated with the cellulose content profile (Figure 8c,d; Table S7).

Correlation analysis of cotton fiber polysaccharide epitopes and transcripts of glycosyltransferases potentially involved in the biosynthesis of the epitopes

Gossypium hirsutum is an allotetraploid, and its gene families tend to be large. Therefore, it is challenging to predict which particular glycosyltransferase (GT) gene/protein might be responsible for the observed developmental profile of a CW polysaccharide epitope. However, to predict the most likely GT gene(s), correlation analyses were conducted using the glycome data and transcriptome data collected at the same time points. Observing a high correlation between the transcript expression pattern of GTs and polysaccharide epitope abundance would suggest the potential involvement of particular GTs in the synthesis of specific epitopes. Our study is focused on GTs that are responsible for synthesizing the critical polysaccharide epitopes of fiber CW. Other genes involved in the complex process of fiber development, such as gene expression networks, hormone signaling, transcription factors, and so on (Jareczek et al., 2023) are presented in a separate collaborative study (Grover et al., 2024), and are not discussed here.

First, the list of Arabidopsis GTs known or predicted to be involved in the biosynthesis of CW polysaccharides was generated (Table S8a). Then, using Arabidopsis GTs gene sequences, corresponding homologous G. hirsutum GT genes (Chen, Sreedasyam, et al., 2020) were identified from the Phytozome (https://phytozome‐next.jgi.doe.gov/). Identified Arabidopsis and G. hirsutum GTs are listed in Table S8. Using this information, the data on transcript levels of putative G. hirsutum GTs at different DPA were collected from the transcriptome data generated in our collaborative study (Grover et al., 2024). Next, the PCC analyses were carried out between the profiles of polysaccharide epitopes (from glycome data) and profiles of expressed transcripts of GT genes (from transcriptome data) (Figure S2b), and the results are presented below.

Correlation analysis of HG related epitopes and the corresponding transcripts of glycosyltransferase genes

In Arabidopsis and some other plants, 15 galacturonosyltransferases (GAUTs) and 10 galacturonosyltransferase‐like (GATLs) enzymes are involved in HG backbone biosynthesis by adding galacturonic acid (GalA) to the growing chain of HG backbone. There are also HG methyl transferases (CGRs/QUAs) and O‐acetyltransferases (TBLs/TBRs) involved in decorating the HG backbone with methyl and acetyl groups, respectively (Atmodjo et al., 2013; Engle et al., 2022) (Figure 2).

Our search for Arabidopsis homologous GTs revealed that there are 48 GAUTs, 24 GATLs, 24 methyltransferases (CGRs/QUAs), and 14 acetyltransferases (TBLs/TBRs) genes expressed in G. hirsutum fibers, which includes both A and D homoeologs (Table S8b). The profiles of three of the HG epitopes (HG‐BBde‐1‐P, HG‐BBde‐2‐P, and HG‐BBMe‐P) were correlated with subsets of expression profiles from the genes in the above three classes of enzymes. The PCC correlations ranged from −0.9 to +0.9 in the entire gene set; however, the levels of transcripts of 10 GAUTs, 3 GATLs, 6 methyltransferases (CGRs/QUAs), and 3 acetyltransferases (TBLs/TBRs) genes had PCC values exceeded 0.7 cut‐off and were defined as candidate genes involved in the observed HG profiles (Figure 9a; Table S8c). Correlation analysis of rhamnogalacturonan‐I related epitopes and the corresponding transcripts of glycosyltransferase genes

The rhamnogalacturonan‐I (RG‐I) primary backbone is branched with the β‐1,4‐galactans, which constitute the secondary backbone of RG‐I molecules. Further, the β‐1,4‐galactans are decorated with β‐1,6‐linked galactan, and arabinan side chains (Figure 2). Galacturonosyltransferases (RG‐I:GalATs), RG‐I:rhamnosyltransferases (RRTs), and O‐acetyltransferases (TBR/TBL) are required to synthesize the primary backbone of RG‐I (Figure 2) (Amos et al., 2022; Atmodjo et al., 2013). β‐1,4‐galactan synthases (GALSs) synthesizes β‐1,4‐galactan secondary backbone of RG‐I. The AGP‐GALTs synthesize the β‐1,3‐galactan secondary backbone of RG‐I or arabinogalactan protein (AGP). RG‐I and AGPs of CW share many structural epitopes, and it is hard to distinguish the origin of these epitopes from these two sources (Thorne et al., 2023). The β‐1,6‐galactosyltransferases (GALT29A/GALT31A) are involved in adding β‐1,6‐linked galactans to the secondary backbone (Showalter & Basu, 2016). β‐arabinofuranosyl transferases (RAY1) are known to participate in adding arabinose to β‐1,6‐linked galactans (Atmodjo et al., 2013).

The search for the cotton orthologs to Arabidopsis revealed that there are 8 RG‐I:GalATs, 34 RRTs, 14 TBR/TBLs, 6 GALSs, 34 AGP:GALTs, 10 GALT29A/GALT31As, and 2 RAY1s exists in cotton fibers, including both A and D homoeologs (Table S8b). Three of the RG‐I primary backbone (RG‐I‐BB) epitopes profiles from the buffer soluble fraction were PCC analyzed with the transcript profiles of 8 RG‐I:GalATs, 34 RRTs, and 14 TBR/TBLs, which are the enzymes involved in backbone synthesis. PCC analysis results showed that genes of 1 RG‐I:GalAT, 3 RRTs, and 4 TBRs significantly correlated with the RG‐I‐BB epitopes (Figure 9b; Table S8d). In our study, two of the antibodies recognized the β‐1,4‐galactan secondary backbone epitopes (Gal4‐BB3‐HC and Gal4‐BB6‐HC). Both the epitopes and the transcript profile of the 8 RG‐I:GalATs, 34 RRTs, 14 TBR/TBLs, and 6 GALSs genes were PCC analyzed and it showed that 1 RG‐I:GalAT, 4 RRTs, 3 GALSs, and 3 TBRs genes significantly correlated with both the epitopes from β‐1,4‐galactan secondary backbone (Figure 9c; Table S8e).

In the present study, 13 antibodies were used to detect the RG‐I‐β‐1,6‐linked galactans which are linked to the β‐1,3‐galactan or β‐1,4‐galactan secondary backbones of RG‐I. PCC analysis was conducted between 7 RG‐I β‐1,6‐linked galactan epitopes (content goes down during 6–25 DPA) and the transcripts of 8 RG‐I:GalATs, 34 RRTs, 14 TBR/TBLs, 6 GALSs, 34 β‐AGP:GALTs, 10 GALT29A/GALT31A, 2 RAY1s, 4 FUTs, and 16 GlcATs genes. The PCC result showed that there was a high correlation with 1 RG‐I:GalAT, 1 RRT, 2 GALSs, 3 AGP:GALTs, and 2 TBRs genes (Figure 9d; Table S8f). Similar way PCC analysis between 5 RG‐I β‐1,6‐linked galactan epitopes (content goes up during 6–25 DPA) and the same transcripts mentioned above revealed that 1 RG‐I:GalAT, 2 RRT, 2 AGP:GALTs, 2 GlcATs, and 2 TBL3s genes were significantly correlated (Figure 9e; Table S8g). The same transcripts were PCC analyzed with 7 arabinogalactan (AG/AGP) epitopes and it showed that 1 RG‐I:GalAT, 1 RRT, 2 GALSs, 3 AGP:GALTs, and 1 TBR genes were highly correlated (Figure 9f; Table S8h).

Correlation analysis of xylan related epitopes and the corresponding transcripts of glycosyltransferase genes

The xylan β‐1,4‐xylosyltransferases (IRXs; irregular xylem), UDP‐GlcA: xylan α‐glucuronyltransferases (GUXs), glucuronoxylan methyltransferase‐like proteins (GXMT), and xylan arabinosyltransferases (XATs) are responsible for the synthesis of xylan molecules (Figure 2). IRXs are involved in xylan backbone synthesis. GUXs are responsible for adding α‐1,2‐d‐glucuronic acid (GlcA) to the xylan backbone and the GXMTs methylate GlcA molecules. XATs are involved in decorating xylan backbones with arabinose, which results in arabinoxylans (Smith et al., 2017). Several O‐acetyltransferases (ESK/RWA/TBL) are required to acetylate the xylan backbone. It was found that cotton fiber has 30 IRXs, 16 GUXs, 22 GXMTs, and 40 O‐acetyltransferases (ESK/RWA/TBL) homologous to Arabidopsis genes including from both A and D cotton homoeologs (Table S8b).

The profiles of the five of the xylan backbone (Xyl‐BB) epitopes from the buffer soluble fractions were PCC analyzed with the transcript profiles of 30 IRXs and 40 O‐acetyltransferases (ESK/RWA/TBL), which are the enzymes involved in backbone synthesis. The results showed that profiles of 4 IRXs, 2 ESKs, and 1 TBL30 genes correlated significantly with these epitopes (Figure 10a; Table S8i). PCC analysis of the Xyl‐GlcA epitope profile with the transcript profiles of 30 IRXs, 16 GUXs, and 44 ESKs/RWAs/TBLs showed that 4 IRXs, 2 GUXs, 2 ESKs, and 2 TBL30s genes correlated significantly with this epitope (Figure 10b; Table S8j).

Correlation analysis of xyloglucan‐related epitopes and the corresponding transcripts of glycosyltransferase genes

In Arabidopsis, xyloglucan β‐1,4‐glucan synthases (cellulose synthase‐like‐C enzymes; CSLCs; CSLC4/5/6/8/12), UDP‐Xyl: xyloglucan α‐1,6‐xylosyltransferases (XXTs), xyloglucan β‐1,2‐galactosyltransferases (MUR3 and XLT2), and a GDP‐l‐Fuc: α‐1,2‐l‐fucosyltransferases (FUT1) are involved in biosynthesis of xyloglucans (XyG) (Figure 2) (Julian & Zabotina, 2022). The CSCLs are involved in the synthesis of the glucan backbone of XyG. Five different XXTs (XXT1 through XXT5) attach xylose residues to the glucose at different positions in the glucan backbone. Further, MUR3 and XLT2 galactosylate the specific xyloses in XyG. Finally, FUT1 fucosylates the galactose in the third side chain of the XyG subunit. In addition, O‐acetyltransferases (AXY4/9) are known to acetylate the galactose residues in the second side chain of XyG. The search for genes homologous to Arabidopsis genes showed that cotton has 16 CSLCs, 10 XXTs, 8 MUR3s, 4 XLT2s, 4 FUT1s, and 12 O‐acetyltransferases (AXY4/9), including from A and D cotton genomes (Table S8b).

PCC analysis of 4 xylosylated glucan backbone epitopes of XyG (XG‐XXs and XG‐XXXGs) from the buffer soluble fraction against transcripts of 16 CLSCs and 10 XXTs, showed that 7 CLSCs and 5 XXTs genes were highly correlated (Figure 11b; Table S8n). In another set of PCC analyses, the profiles of 5 galactosylated XyG (XG‐XLXs and XG‐Ls) epitopes significantly correlated with the transcripts of genes of six CLSCs, five XXTs, five galactosyltransferases (four MUR3s and one XLT2), and one AXY4 (Figure 11c; Table S8o). PCC analysis of three fucosylated XyG (Fuc and XG‐FF) epitopes and the transcripts of enzymes putatively involved in synthesizing these epitopes showed a high correlation for eight CSLCs, five XXTs, five galactosyltransferases (four MUR3s and one XLT2), one FUT1, and one AXY4 genes (Figure 11d; Table S8p).

DISCUSSION

Cotton fiber CW polysaccharide composition and complex remodeling of polysaccharide epitopes during development ultimately determine the outcome of fiber quality. Significant efforts have been made to study the cotton fiber CW polysaccharide compositional changes during development (Avci et al., 2013; Guo et al., 2019; Hernandez‐Gomez et al., 2017; Meinert & Delmer, 1977; Pettolino et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2009). The uniqueness and power of the present study is that the glycome, transcriptome, and fiber‐phenotypes profiling was carried out using cotton bolls collected daily for 20 consecutive days (6–25 DPA). The 20 days include key phases of fiber development, where extensive multiple phases of CW remodeling occur that determine the fiber quality. This large‐scale correlative study with high temporal resolution quantitatively analyzed the fiber phenotypes, fiber CW polysaccharides, and glycosyltransferase‐encoding genes, which allowed us to discover dynamic changes that occur in cotton fiber CW during the development with better understanding.

Predicted polysaccharide epitopes and CW synthesizing glycosyltransferases potentially contribute to fiber elongation

The glycome profile data revealed that most of the highly branched RG‐I pectin epitopes, like β‐1,4‐linked galactans, β‐1,6‐linked galactans, and arabinogalactans were present in high amounts at earlier stages of fiber development (Figures 4 and 6; Tables 1 and 3) and significantly decreased around 16–18 DPA, coinciding with the CFML degradation (Guo et al., 2019) and cessation of fiber elongation (Wilson et al., 2024). A similar profile was observed for buffer‐soluble callose, xyloglucans, and xylans in our and earlier studies (Avci et al., 2013; Guo et al., 2019; Singh et al., 2009). The polysaccharide content estimation also clearly showed the reduction of pectins and hemicelluloses percentages in CW during the transition stage, which is also corroborated by the decrease in monosaccharides like galacturonic acid, xylose, galactose, and fucose (Figure 1; Tables S1 and S2). Only glucose content was found to increase in both the buffer and alkali‐soluble extracts, which might be due to the soluble amorphous cellulose leached out from the CW during extraction.

It is known that the large side chains in highly branched pectins prevent extensive interactions among adjacent polysaccharides to maintain plasticity to support cell elongation, whereas less branched polysaccharides promote bonding and rigidification of CW, thus preventing cell elongation (Atmodjo et al., 2013). For example, highly branched RG‐I (with arabinan and galactan side chains) has reduced interaction with cellulose or xyloglucans as a result of steric impediments, which resulted in increased CW plasticity (Höfte & Voxeur, 2017; Zykwinska et al., 2005). Also, the highly branched RG‐I was reported to prevent the formation of cross‐linking of HG by Ca2+ bridges (Jones et al., 2003). Increased methyl‐deesterification of HG was reported to increase the cross‐linking of HG domains by Ca2+ bridges and contribute to CW stiffening (Kaczmarska et al., 2022). Our and also earlier study (Pettolino et al., 2022) demonstrated that the content of alkali‐soluble methyl‐esterified‐HG in fiber decreased after 13 DPA, whereas the deesterified‐HG remained high (Tables 1 and 4). These results provide further proof that a decrease in branched RG‐I, combined with an increase in deesterified HG and cellulose content at the beginning of the transition phase, might be contributing to the rigidification of the CW and hindering further cell elongation.

Further using PCC, we analyzed the transcript profiles of glycosyltransferases that are directly involved in synthesizing these polysaccharide epitopes along with the polysaccharide profiles to find out the putative GTs involved in fiber elongation and development. The CW polysaccharide epitope abundance in different stages of fiber development is dynamically changed by the presence or absence of both glycosyltransferases (GTs) and glycosylhydrolases (GHs). However, in this study, we mainly focused on analyzing GTs, while analyses of GHs will be carried out in a separate study in the future.

Using correlation data, we can predict a subset of potential GTs that are likely to synthesize HG. The PCC analysis demonstrated that the expression of 3 GAUT1s, 1 GAUT3, 2 GAUT4s, 2 GAUT6s, 1 GAUT9, 1 GAUT10, 1 GATL3, and 2 GATL9s significantly correlated with the HG epitope profiles (Figure 9a; Table S8c) and generally were expressed prior to 15 DPA, as previously reported (Fan et al., 2021). The significantly correlated cotton homologous genes identified in our study appear to differ from the key GAUTs found in Arabidopsis. In Arabidopsis, GAUT1 is the only essential catalytically active enzyme that forms obligatory complexes with GAUT7 (Atmodjo et al., 2013; Engle et al., 2022). Since we did not observe a similar correlation for cotton homolog to GAUT7, it will be interesting to investigate in the future whether the cotton GAUT1 protein also gets processed and requires any other GAUT protein to be localized in cotton Golgi.

Even though the information on the functional role of GAUT‐like (GATL) GTs is limited, recently three of the G. hirsutum GATLs, by over‐expression/silencing (Gohir.D05G092000/GhGATL3‐1Dt; Gohir.A03G144000/GhGATL9‐1At; and Gohir.D02G167200/GhGATL9‐1Dt) study, were reported to positively correlate to pectin synthesis and fiber elongation (Zheng et al., 2020). The PCC analysis from our study also confirmed that these three GATLs significantly correlated with the HG epitope profiles (Figure 9a; Table S8c). Also, the prime importance of methyl esterification of HG status in maintaining CW integrity was shown recently through mutant Arabidopsis and rice plants, and these studies proved the functional role of a QUA2 (methyltransferse) in methyl esterification of HG (Du et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2017). In our study, we have identified significantly correlated three homologous QUA2s and three other methyltransferase genes (Table S8b). Further, molecular analysis is needed in the future to conclude the extent to which the significantly correlated GATLs, and methyltransferases identified here contribute to pectin synthesis, along with the GAUTs, in cotton fiber CW elongation and development.

Similar to methyl esterified‐HG, the content of buffer‐extractable callose decreased after 13 DPA (Figure 11a). In an earlier study, the presence of two types of callose in cotton fiber was reported, the buffer soluble highly branched callose and less soluble unbranched callose that can be extracted only in strongly basic conditions (Pettolino et al., 2022). It is possible that loosely bound buffer‐soluble branched callose present in PCW also contributes to the plasticity and elongation of the fiber, as proposed in an earlier study (Feng et al., 2021). The same study reported a highly expressed G. hirsutum fiber‐specific callose synthase and speculated its role in fiber elongation. Interestingly, our PCC analysis also identified the same callose synthase gene (Gohir.A04G103650/GhGSL01‐4At) with the highest correlation to the callose profile (Figure 11a; Table S8m). However, biochemical and molecular investigation is needed in the future to confirm its role in fiber development.

A companion study reported that the growth rate of fiber decreases during the entire developmental time course, and the turgor pressure of the fiber peaks at around 16 DPA and decreases rapidly to reach a low level at 22 DPA, coinciding with the complete cessation of fiber elongation (Wilson et al., 2024). As reported previously, the turgor pressure plays a role in the elongation of fiber and reaches its peak coinciding with the attainment of maximum fiber length (Wilson et al., 2024). Profiles of many of the highly branched RG‐I epitopes significantly correlated to the fiber growth rate and the estimated turgor pressure profile (Figures 4 and 8; Table S7). The buffer‐soluble xyloglucans and xylans similar to pectins began to decrease as early as at 12 DPA, and the profile of these epitopes also strongly correlated with the fiber growth rate curve (Figures 4 and 8; Table S7). Most likely, the presence of these epitopes in the CW supports CW plasticity and building turgor pressure to promote fiber elongation. On the other hand, degradation of these highly branched epitopes combined with an increase in SCW cellulose accumulation (Figures 1a and 4) might facilitate the CW rigidification, reduction in turgor pressure, and cessation of fiber elongation.

RG‐I is a branched complex structure, and it varies highly between different species (Figure 2; Kaczmarska et al., 2022). Only a few GTs involved in the synthesis of RG‐I, mainly from Arabidopsis, were functionally characterized. RG‐I backbone is synthesized by RG‐I:Galaturonosyltransferases (RG‐I:GalATs) and RG‐I:rhamnosyltransferases (RRTs). So far, only one Arabidopsis RG‐I:Galaturonosyltransferase (RG‐I:GalAT1; AT1G28240) was functionally characterized (Amos et al., 2022). It is interesting to note that in our study also only one set out of four sets of cotton homologous RG‐I:GalAT1 genes from A and D genome (Gohir.A06G127100/GhRGI:GalAT1‐3At and Gohir.D06G132000/GhRGI:GalAT1‐3Dt), were found to significantly correlate with the RG‐I epitopes (Figure 9; Table S8d–h). Earlier experiments using Arabidopsis confirmed the involvement of two RG‐I:Rhamnosyltransferases (RRT1, AT5G15740 and RRT4, AT1G14020) in RG‐I backbone synthesis (Amos et al., 2022; Takenaka et al., 2018). In our study, a total of seven RRT genes, including RRT1 and RRT4 (two RRT1s, two RRT4s, two RRT7s, and one RRT9), were found to be significantly correlated (Table S8d–h). However, the confirmative role of cotton RG‐I:GalATs and RRTs in RG‐I backbone synthesis needs functional validation in the future.

RG‐I primary backbone is decorated with the secondary β‐1,4‐galactan backbone, arabinans, β‐1,6‐galactans, and arabinogalactans. A sharp decline of β‐1,4‐galactans immediately after 16 DPA, shown in our study (Figure 4b), has also been reported earlier, coinciding with the cessation of fiber elongation (Guo et al., 2019; Singh et al., 2009). These authors proposed that β‐1,4‐galactan molecules may be components of CFML and correlated with fiber elongation. An Arabidopsis GALS protein (GALS1; AT2G33570), which participates in β‐1,4‐galactan secondary backbone synthesis, was functionally characterized by biochemical assays (Liwanag et al., 2012). We found three of the cotton GALS1 (Gohir.A09G123700/GhGALS1‐1At, Gohir.A11G311800/GhGALS1‐2At, and Gohir.D11G328400/GhGALS1‐2Dt), homologous to the Arabidopsis genes, significantly correlated with the β‐1,4‐galactan epitope profiles (Figure 9c; Table S8e). An earlier transcriptomic and glycomic study in three cotton species (G. hirsutum, G. barbadense, and G. arboretum) also predicted the same GALS1 homolog gene to be involved in β‐1,4‐galactan synthesis (Guo et al., 2019).

Besides RG‐I polysaccharides, the Arabinogalactan proteins (AGPs) are highly galactosylated and play pivotal roles in diverse developmental processes in plants. About 17 different GTs belonging to seven distinct classes have been reported to glycosylate AGPs in Arabidopsis, and not many are identified in other crops (Kaur et al., 2021; Showalter & Basu, 2016). Recently, a cotton AGP:GALT (Gohir.A04G041100/GhAGP:GALT1‐2At) homologous to an Arabidopsis AGP:GALT (AT3G14960) was found to play a functional role in galactosylation of AGPs (Qin et al., 2017). Through silencing/overexpression, it was found to be negatively correlated to fiber length/development. Here, the same gene, together with four other cotton AGP:GALT genes, were found to be significantly correlated in PCC analysis (Table S8b). It will be interesting to see in future molecular investigations if any of these AGP:GALTs play a positive role in fiber elongation.

The buffer extractable xyloglucan epitopes present at high content at earlier DPAs were significantly decreased during the transition stage (Figure 4b). In Arabidopsis, the completely branched structure of xyloglucan (XLFG type) is synthesized by CLSCs (CSLC4/5/6/8/12), XXTs (XXT1/2/3/4/5), galactosyltransferases (MUR3/XLT2), and a fucosyltransferase (FUT1) (Julian & Zabotina, 2022; Pauly & Keegstra, 2016; Zhang et al., 2023). From our transcriptome analysis, we did not find cotton homologous genes for CSLC6, CSLC8, XXT1, XXT4, and XXT5 (Table S8b). The PCC analysis for the CSLC transcripts revealed that transcripts of two genes, each from CSLC4, CSLC5, and CSLC12 were highly correlated with all the XyG epitopes (Figure 11; Table S8n–p). Earlier studies in Arabidopsis reported all five CSLCs are involved in the XyG synthesis (Kim et al., 2020), however, to different extent in different tissues. The authors demonstrated that CSLC4 and CSLC5 had the highest levels of expression in vegetative tissues, whereas expression of CSLC6 and CSLC12 was specific to flowers and seeds.

Among cotton XXTs, one homologous to Arabidopsis XXT2 and four to XXT3 were found to significantly correlate with the XyG epitopes (Figure 11; Table S8n–p). In Arabidopsis, XXT1, XXT2, and XXT5 have the highest levels of expression in most tissues (Chou et al., 2012; Zabotina et al., 2012) and were demonstrated to be the major players in XyG synthesis (Zabotina et al., 2008, 2012). In Arabidopsis, XXT5 plays a dominant role, and XXT3 and XXT4 play a minor complementary role in adding xylosyl residue specifically to the third glucose in the glucan backbone to synthesize XXXG‐type XyGs (Culbertson et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2023). On the contrary, our correlation data suggest that in cotton fiber, XXT2 and XXT3 are the key enzymes involved in XyG synthesis. It is plausible that in cotton fiber, four of the XXT3 proteins might be substituting the function carried by XXT5 in Arabidopsis. However, in the vegetative tissues, the expression of the XXT genes might vary, and it will require separate investigation.

Correlation analysis revealed that the transcripts of four MUR3s, one XLT2, one fucosyltransferase, and an O‐acetyltransferase significantly correlated with the XyG epitopes (Figure 11c,d; Table S8o,p). A previous study using transcriptomic and glycomic analyses in cotton fiber of two species reported the possible involvement of CSLC4 (Gohir.A09G024900/GhCSLC04‐1At), XXT2 (Gohir.D02G054500/GhXXT2‐1Dt), MUR3 (Gohir.A11G133800/GhMUR3‐1At), and FUT1 (Gohir.A07G064300/GhFUT1‐1At) in cotton fiber xyloglucan synthesis, which corroborates our findings (Guo et al., 2019) (Table S8n–p). Another study involving a short fiber mutant of G. hirsutum demonstrated that the transcripts of GTs involved in xyloglucan synthesis were differentially expressed in mutants and suggested that the following GTs might be important for fiber elongation: Gohir.A09G024900 (GhCSLC04‐1At), Gohir.D06G023800 (GhCSLC12‐2Dt), Gohir.A05G063100 (GhCSLC05‐2At), Gohir.D05G065700 (GhCSLC05‐2Dt), Gohir.D02G054500 (GhXXT2‐1Dt), Gohir.A03G067800 (GhXXT3‐1At), Gohir.D12G181700 (GhXXT3‐2Dt), Gohir.D10G136900 (GhMUR3‐2Dt), Gohir.A11G133800 (GhMUR3‐1At), and Gohir.D13G077500 (GhFUT1‐2Dt) (Naoumkina et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2024). Applying our large‐scale study with higher temporal resolution allowed us to identify the same genes, but also several additional GTs that are significantly correlated with the xyloglucan epitope profiles (Table S8n–p). For example, transcripts of eight CLSCs, four XXT3s, and four MUR3s homologous genes were found significantly correlated with fucosylated‐XyGs detected in the glycome profiles (Figure 11; Table S8). However, the actual contribution of all the reported and additional enzymes identified in our study will need further experimental validation.

In previous studies of cotton fiber, a lot of attention was paid to CFML. It is an adhesive layer of fiber CW that supports fiber elongation and its degradation leads to the cessation of fiber elongation (Hernandez‐Gomez et al., 2017; Singh et al., 2009). Pectins are known to support cell–cell adhesion (Kaczmarska et al., 2022). Previous immunolabeling‐TEM studies reported the presence of HGs, xyloglucans, and possibly RG‐I‐galactan epitopes in CFML and proposed that these particular polysaccharides contribute to fiber elongation (Avci et al., 2013; Guo et al., 2019; Singh et al., 2009). Our study clearly showed that HG, xyloglucans, and also many specific branched RGI epitopes (galactans and arabinogalactans) significantly reduced during the transition stage coinciding with CFML degradation (Tables 1 and 3). Taken together, our results and earlier studies indicate that the most likely combination of the HGs, xyloglucans, and branched RG‐Is present in the CFML and other polysaccharides and CW loosening proteins, such as expansins present at PCW might collectively control the fiber plasticity and elongation.

Predicted polysaccharide epitopes and CW synthesizing glycosyltransferases potentially contribute to fiber strength

Events such as CFML degradation, and accumulation of callose, and SCW cellulose reportedly leading to fiber CW rigidification and increased strength (Fang et al., 2023; Jaquet et al., 1982; Maltby et al., 1979; Martel & Giovannoni, 2007; Pettolino et al., 2022; Rowland et al., 1984; Vicente et al., 2007). Interestingly, few specific polysaccharide epitopes (Xyl‐MeGlcA‐1‐HC, Xyl‐MeGlcA‐2‐HC, Xyl‐3Ar‐HC, RG‐I‐Gal1/2/3‐β‐1,6‐galactans, AG‐3‐2‐HC, AG‐4‐HC, and AG‐4/Gal3‐BB‐HC) were absent or low at earlier DPAs but increased significantly between 12 and 14 DPAs, which coincides with the beginning of transition stage and SCW cellulose synthesis (Figure 5; Table 2). Our correlation analysis showed a large number of xylan β‐1,4‐xylosyltransferases (IRX9, 10, 14, 15), O‐acetyltransferases (EKS1/RWAs/TBLs), xylan α‐glucuronyltransferases (GUXs), and glucuronoxylan methyltransferases (GXMTs) were highly correlated with the Xyl‐MeGlcA and Xyl‐3Ar epitopes profile (Figure 10c,d).

An earlier study using four domesticated species of cotton also reported a heteroxylan epitope and few correlated transcripts (Hernandez‐Gomez et al., 2015) increased during transition stages, which matches the findings of our study. The authors proposed that the heteroxylan epitopes might be involved in the regulation of cellulose deposition in SCW. Some of the most recent transgenic (overexpression/silencing) and biochemical studies in G. hirsutum, functionally characterized many GTs (involved in the biosynthesis of Xyl‐MeGlcA and Xyl‐Ar epitopes) and confirmed their involvement in SCW formation and fiber strength (Chen, Guo, et al., 2020; Li et al., 2014, 2024). Coincidentally, all the GTs characterized from these recent studies were also found correlated in our present investigation. These genes are Gohir.D09G147400 (GhIRX9‐3Dt), Gohir.D02G175900 (GhIRX10‐1Dt), Gohir.A11G196300 (GhIRX14‐2At), Gohir.A12G073300 (GhIRX15‐1At), Gohir.D12G071000 (GhIRX15‐1Dt), Gohir.A11G196000 (GhIRX15‐2At), Gohir.D11G050700 (GhIRX15‐3Dt), Gohir.D04G131600 (GhTBL3‐2Dt), Gohir.A11G056000 (GhGUX1‐1At), Gohir.A10G082400 (GhGUX1‐2At), Gohir.D13G122300 (GhXMT2‐1Dt), Gohir.A01G140600 (GhXMT3‐1At), and Gohir.D12G267900 (GhXAT‐2Dt). In addition, we found many other genes from each category that also significantly correlated with the Xyl‐MeGlcA and Xyl‐3Ar epitopes profile (Table S8k,l), which warrants future functional validation. Further, these results indicate the prime importance of the potential involvement of these xylan epitopes and these large sets of enzymes in SCW assembly and fiber‐strengthening process.

Interestingly, it was shown earlier that glucuronoxylans (Xyl‐GlcA) are the predominant type of xylan found within the SCW of hardwoods and herbaceous dicot species like poplar and Arabidopsis (Jacobs et al., 2001; Mazumder et al., 2012; Smith et al., 2017; Zhong et al., 2005). Previous experiments showed that an unbranched xylan backbone is structurally similar to cellulose and can spontaneously bind to cellulose microfibrils affecting their orientation and aggregation during CW synthesis (Atalla et al., 1993; Smith et al., 2017; Vian et al., 1986). Studies in woody tissues showed that glucuronoxylans deposited in the transition zones between the “S” layers participate in organizing the cellulose microfibrils and change their orientation (Atalla et al., 1993; Vian et al., 1986). It is worth noting that in our study, the profile of the three xylan epitopes and the correlated transcripts highly matches the profiles of cellulose content, the expression of SCW synthesizing CESAs, and the microfibril orientation (Figures 1a, 8c,d and 10c,d; Figure S3) and the CW thickness phenotype profile reported earlier in a collaborative study (Wilson et al., 2024). The cellulose content and highly correlated SCW‐CESAs (CESAs 4/7/8) were found to increase dramatically around 16 DPA, which coincides with the transition stage and the beginning of SCW accumulation (Figure S3; Table S9). The highly correlated SCW‐CESAs (CESAs 4/7/8) identified in our study match the known homologous SCW‐CESAs present in Arabidopsis (CESAs 4/7/8) (Kim et al., 2019) and previously reported cotton SCW‐CESAs (Guo et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2022).

Interestingly, few of the RG‐I‐β‐1,6‐galactan epitopes and significantly correlated transcripts that go up during SCW accumulation (Table 2; Figure 5) were highly similar to the profiles of three xylan epitopes and microfibril orientation (Figure 8c; Table S7). The involvement of callose and cellulose in “winding CW layer” formation and its contribution to the strengthening of the fiber has been proposed as well (Hinchliffe et al., 2011; Hsieh et al., 1995; Kerr, 1946). Recently in an Arabidopsis QUA2 mutant, it was shown that the methyl esterification of HG was reduced, and as a result, it severely affected the crystallinity of cellulose and the integrity of CW (Du et al., 2020). In the future, it might be worth investigating whether any of the three xylan epitopes (Xyl‐MeGlcA‐1‐HC, Xyl‐MeGlcA‐2‐HC, and Xyl‐3Ar‐HC), RG‐I‐β‐1,6‐galactans, callose, and HG epitopes identified here participate in cellulose microfibril re‐arrangement, thus promoting SCW synthesis and increased strength of the mature fiber.

In conclusion, this paper integrated glycome profiling, transcriptomics, and multiple phenotypes with high temporal resolution. The fiber growth process is not simple, and there is a complicated temporal sequence of CW polysaccharide remodeling that defines morphological and material property traits that underlie fiber quality. Our study uncovered some of the highly branched RG‐I pectin epitopes, in addition to the previously reported buffer‐soluble HGs and xyloglucans, could play an important role in the formation of the CFML and PCW and contribute to fiber plasticity and elongation. On the other hand, the identified heteroxylans (Xyl‐MeGlcA‐1‐HC, Xyl‐MeGlcA‐2‐HC, and Xyl‐3Ar‐HC) might function as a regulating factor for SCW cellulose microfibrils arrangement and further strengthening of the fibers. Our study also identified several highly promising correlated genes of cotton polysaccharide‐synthesizing GT enzymes. Additional molecular and biochemical experiments will be required in future to validate the functional role of the identified polysaccharide epitopes and putative enzymes in cotton fiber elongation and strength.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Cotton plant growing conditions and boll collection

Gossypium hirsutum accession TM1 plants were grown in a growth chamber (Conviron E‐15, Controlled Environments, Inc., Pembina, ND, USA) under controlled conditions. Plants were grown individually in a two‐gallon pot containing a potting mix of 4:2:2:1 ratio of soil:perlite:bark:chicken grit. Growth chambers were programmed for 16‐h days with 500 μmol of light and a temperature of 28°C. At anthesis, flowers were self‐pollinated and tagged. At each daily developmental timepoint from 6 to 25 DPA, three replicate bolls were collected at mid‐day, labeled, and stored at −80°C until used for CW or RNA extraction.

CW and polysaccharide extraction

Alcohol‐insoluble CW material and, further, the buffer‐soluble and alkali‐soluble polysaccharide fractions from the cotton fiber were extracted as per an established protocol (Avci et al., 2013). In brief, using a sharp razor blade and tweezers, the developing fibers were removed from the developing seeds carefully in a Petri dish kept under cold conditions in an ice bucket. Enough care was taken to avoid the seed coat while separating the fibers. The harvested cotton fibers from all the seeds in a single boll were pooled and ground in liquid nitrogen, and from the powdered fiber the CW was extracted using different solvents. From the CW material, polysaccharides were fractionated by sequential extraction using two different solutions, first using a 50 mm CDTA:50 mm ammonium oxalate (1:1) buffer, followed by 4 m KOH to extract buffer soluble and alkali‐soluble polysaccharides fractions, respectively (Figure S1) (Zabotina et al., 2012). Since, the epitopes originate from two different extraction solvents, either buffer‐soluble (50 mm CDTA–50 mm ammonium oxalate extract) or alkali‐soluble (4 m KOH extract) fractions, for practicality, the naming of epitopes are denoted by the suffix “‐P” and “‐HC”, respectively. The final pellet (containing a mixture of amorphous and crystalline celluloses) that remained after the 4 m KOH extraction was also weighed. The crystalline cellulose content present in this final pellet was measured by the Updegraff reagent method (Updegraff, 1969).

Glycome profiling of epitopes of buffer‐soluble and alkali‐soluble polysaccharide fractions

The amount of sugar in the buffer‐ soluble and alkali‐soluble polysaccharide fractions from each sample was determined by the phenol‐sulfuric acid method (Pattathil et al., 2012), and each well of ELISA plate (Costar; 3595) was loaded with an equal amount of polysaccharide sample dissolved in water (50 μl/well from a 60 μg μl−1 solution). Glycome profiling is an ELISA‐based method and it was carried out as described previously (Pattathil et al., 2010, 2012; Zabotina et al., 2012) with 71 different polysaccharide epitope‐specific antibodies (Table S3) that were selected based on the literature (Avci et al., 2013; Thorne et al., 2023).

Monosaccharide composition was determined according to previously described methods (Brenner et al., 2012). The CW fractions (1 mg) were hydrolyzed with 2 m trifluoroacetic acid and analyzed by high‐performance liquid chromatography using an anion‐exchange chromatography column (Dionex, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). The column was calibrated using nine monosaccharide standards (Brenner et al., 2012).

SOM clustering

SOM is a machine‐learning method for clustering analysis (Kohonen, 1990; Wehrens & Kruisselbrink, 2018). We used the SOM algorithm to cluster the glycome epitopes from two separate interpolated datasets: buffer soluble and alkali‐soluble polysaccharide fractions. The SOM grid structures were adopted as a grid structure with five rows and four columns for each data set.

Cotton fiber phenotype measurements

The cotton fiber phenotype data such as fiber growth rate, cellulose microfibril orientation, and turgor pressure were obtained from our published collaborative study (Wilson et al., 2024) that was conducted on the same cotton variety grown under the same environmental conditions. Briefly, the maximum fiber length was determined between the chalaza and the tip of combed fiber and then fitted with a logistic regression curve. This logistic regression curve was converted to growth rate as a function of DPA. To get the percentage fiber growth rate, the growth rate was divided by the mean fiber length at each sampling point. The microfibril orientation was estimated using calcofluor‐stained fibers imaged under an inverted spinning disc confocal microscope (Zeiss Observer Z1) using a water immersion lens (63×, 1.2 NA; Zeiss) at 405 nm UV laser for excitation. The microfibril angle deviation from the transverse plane of the fiber growth direction was calculated using the OrientationJ plugin from the microfibril orientation data (Wilson et al., 2024). The turgor pressure measurement was generated by interpolating five measurements in Ruan et al. (2001) with an assumption that the transitions between measured time points were smooth (Wilson et al., 2024).

RNA extraction and RNA sequencing

In parallel to glycome profiling, RNAseq and transcriptomic profiling were carried out for the cotton fibers collected simultaneously for the same number of days (6–25 DPA) in a collaborative study (Grover et al., 2024). Briefly, a modified version of the Sigma Plant Spectrum Total RNA kit (Sigma–Aldrich) was used to extract RNA from the fibers. Library construction (NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit) and sequencing (PE150; Illumina NovaSeq 6000) were conducted by the Iowa State University DNA facility. Data were processed using Trimmomatic version 0.39 (trimmomatic/0.39‐da5npsr) (Bolger et al., 2014) from Spack (Gamblin et al., 2015) for read and quality trimming. The genes from the transcriptome were annotated based on the published reference G. hirsutum genome (Chen, Sreedasyam, et al., 2020). The transcripts per million (TPM) output by Kallisto for each sample were imported into R/4.2.2 (R Core Team, 2022) and RNA‐seq quality was assessed by the consistency of the number of genes expressed over time and among replicates. The number of expressed genes per sample (TPM >0) was plotted across developmental time using ggplot2, and visual outliers were discarded. RNAseq reads were deposited into the NCBI‐SRA under PRJNA1099209.

Statistical analysis

All experimental mean data, including PCC analysis, were subjected to statistical analysis using R software. The data of each experiment were initially tested for significance by analysis of variance, and subsequently, Fisher's protected least significant difference test value was used to compare treatment means at P < 0.05.
