Impact of Oncology Patients’ Knowledge and Awareness on Emergency Visits and Hospital Admissions: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Nada Alsuhebany, Shuroug A Alowais, Waad Saleh Alanazi, Taif Z Alanazi, Sarah Ibrahim Aldaghreer, Bashayer Alshehri, Nadin Almosnid, Salwa Y Hafez, Lama Alfehaid, Mashael Almuqrin, Khalid Al Sulaiman, Khalid A Alamer, Ibrahim M Asiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChemotherapy remains a cornerstone of cancer treatment, but is frequently accompanied by adverse effects that can affects patients’ quality of life. In Saudi Arabia, patients’ understanding of chemotherapy regimens and related toxicities is not well characterized. This study aimed to evaluate chemotherapy awareness among cancer patients and examine its association with hospital admissions and emergency department (ED) visits.MethodsThis cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted at a tertiary hospital in Saudi Arabia, between year of 2022 to 2023. Adult cancer patients receiving at least one chemotherapy cycle were interviewed using a validated, investigator-developed questionnaire assessing beliefs and knowledge about cancer diagnosis, treatment regimens, and side effects. Awareness levels were classified as highly aware or not/low aware based on self-reports verified against electronic medical records (EMR). Descriptive statistics summarized demographic characteristics, while independent t-tests compared mean awareness scores between groups. Negative binomial regression examined associations between awareness levels, hospital admissions, and ED visits, adjusting for demographic covariates, with a sensitivity analysis additionally adjusting for advanced (stage IV) disease.ResultsA total of 270 participants were enrolled (mean age 52 years; 61.1% female). Breast (33.7%) was the most common diagnosis, with 48.1% at stage IV disease. Overall, 69.3% of patients were classified as highly aware. Highly aware patients demonstrated significantly greater knowledge of treatment goals, chemotherapy regimens, and side effects. Greater awareness was associated with higher rates of ED visits and hospital admissions in adjusted analyses. Female gender and limited English fluency were also significant predictors of higher hospital admissions.ConclusionMost oncology patients demonstrated high awareness of their chemotherapy. Awareness was not independently associated with emergency visits or hospital admissions; instead, female gender and limited English proficiency were the principal correlates of higher utilization. Structured patient education is needed to translate awareness into effective self-management and reduce healthcare utilization.Keywords: awareness, knowledge, chemotherapy, adverse events, Saudi Arabia

Background

Cancer ranks as the second leading cause of global mortality, with nearly 10 million deaths attributed to it in 2020.1,2 In Saudi Arabia, the Global Cancer Observatory (GCO) reported 27,885 new cancer cases and 13,069 deaths in 2022.3 With the increasing incidence of cancer, there has been a corresponding rise in the demand for chemotherapy. However, while chemotherapy is effective in treating cancer, it is often accompanied by adverse effects that can impact patients’ overall well-being, including mental health, sexual health, and work-life balance.4 These side effects can significantly contribute to morbidity, reduced quality of life, and potential treatment non-adherence.4,5 Patient education regarding chemotherapy regimens and their potential side effects is, therefore, crucial. Importantly, some adverse effects are manageable through patient education and the use of readily available supportive care medications at home.6 Despite the recognized importance of patient education, limited evidence is available regarding oncology patients’ understanding of their chemotherapy regimens and treatment-related toxicities in Saudi Arabia, particularly in relation to healthcare utilization outcomes such as emergency department visits and hospital admissions.

While existing research in Saudi Arabia explores cancer patients’ awareness of chemotherapy side effects and their management,7–10 there is a lack of studies specifically focused on patients’ knowledge regarding their chemotherapy regimens. One study revealed that a significant portion (84.7%) of patients could not recall their chemotherapy drug names.7 Similarly, another study reported that only 13.7% of patients demonstrated full understanding of their treatment plans when compared to their physicians.8 Regarding side effects, a study in Jeddah found that a mere 21.2% of patients accurately identified the three most significant side effects associated with their chemotherapy, while 22.4% were entirely unaware of any potential adverse effects.8 Another study demonstrated that cancer patients scored an average of 45.8% on a knowledge test regarding chemotherapy side effects, with 11.1% demonstrating even lower understanding.7 Furthermore, a separate study explored the impact of oral chemotherapy side effects, such as inflammation, ulcers, and dehydration, on patients’ psychological well-being, revealing that at least 60% of patients experienced at least one psychological symptom.9 This aligns with other research highlighting the potential for patient education to mitigate these preventable adverse effects.10

The relationship between patient awareness and healthcare utilization is complex and may operate in different directions. Greater awareness of chemotherapy regimens and adverse effects may reduce emergency department visits and hospital admissions by improving symptom recognition, encouraging appropriate self-management, and promoting timely use of supportive care measures at home.11 Conversely, patients with greater awareness may be more vigilant regarding treatment-related symptoms and therefore seek medical attention more frequently.12,13 Given these potentially competing mechanisms, the association between patient awareness and healthcare utilization remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.

Although several studies from Saudi Arabia have evaluated patients’ awareness of chemotherapy side effects, important gaps remain regarding patients’ knowledge of their specific chemotherapy regimens and the potential relationship between awareness and healthcare utilization. Therefore, further investigation is warranted. This cross-sectional descriptive study aimed to assess cancer patients’ awareness and knowledge regarding their chemotherapy regimens and potential adverse effects as a comprehensives cancer center in Saudi Arabia. In addition, the study explored the association between patients’ awareness levels and prior emergency department visits or hospital admissions related to chemotherapy within the preceding year.

Methods

Study Design

This was a single-center, cross-sectional descriptive study evaluating oncology patients’ awareness during chemotherapy. Data collection was conducted using an interview-based questionnaire. The questionnaire assessed patients’ demographics, level of awareness regarding their chemotherapy regimen, knowledge of the specific drugs used, and potential adverse events associated with the treatment. The primary objective of this study was to investigate the level of awareness and knowledge among oncology patients regarding their chemotherapy regimen and its potential adverse effects. Our secondary objective was to explore the association between the level of patient awareness and prior hospital admissions or emergency department (ED) visits reported during the study period.

Study Participants and Sample Size

This study included adult oncology patients undergoing chemotherapy at the outpatient infusion unit at a tertiary care center with a comprehensive cancer center in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The sample consisted of adults newly diagnosed with cancer who had at least received one cycle of treatment to ensure education was provided to them. Patients diagnosed with cancer who have not received chemotherapy or are under the age of 18 were excluded from participation. We aimed to recruit 261 participants to ensure sufficient statistical power for our analyses. The sample size calculation was based on the total number of newly diagnosed cancer patients who initiated a chemotherapy regimen for the first time at our cancer center in the previous year (N = 617). While the required number of participants was determined to be 261, we targeted to recruit about 300 subjects, since there is a possibility of non-compliance or missing responses. We applied simple random sampling technique from the list of all eligible subjects to prevent selection bias. All other eligible subjects were not recruited because the required number had already been attained. Assuming a 95% confidence level, a margin of error of ±5%, and an expected awareness proportion of 50%, the initial sample size was determined using the Cochran Formula. When adjusted for a finite population, the final sample size was 261 patients.

Data Collection

Data for this study were collected over a one-year period, from November 2022 to November 2023, from patients receiving chemotherapy infusions at the comprehensive cancer center’s outpatient infusion unit. We used an investigator-developed questionnaire, administered as an interview-based survey in the infusion unit. Item generation aligned with the study objectives, focusing on beliefs about the appropriateness of education and knowledge of chemotherapy regimens and adverse effects. Four subject-matter experts reviewed the draft for face and content validity, confirming clarity, relevance, and coverage. The final instrument consisted of two sections: a Beliefs section with four items and a Knowledge section with seven items. All items employed a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree, with no reverse-coded items. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia (IRB No: NRC22R/430/08). The study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient Awareness Assessment

Patient awareness regarding cancer diagnosis and treatment was determined based on 6 awareness related questions from investigator-developed instrument, which was assessed through patient self-report and later validated against patient EMR records. The 6 items examined patient awareness related to: (1) cancer diagnosis, (2) year of diagnosis, (3) cancer stage, (4) current treatment, (5) treatment cycle number and (6) treatment goal (ie, cure versus palliation).

Each item was answered by the patient on a Likert scale or Yes, and No, reflecting patient’s level of knowledge and confidence in the recollection. Each patient answer was compared with corresponding EMR documentation and scored 1 if patient’s answer was within the pre-specified threshold range of concordance with EMR-recorded information; otherwise the answer was scored 0. A composite awareness score was obtained by summing the scores of 6 dichotomized items and could vary from 0 to 6. This score was then categorized into 3 categories of awareness (not aware – 0–1, low awareness – 2–3, high awareness – 4–6). This categorization was later combined not aware patients with low awareness patients as one group of patients and high group patients as second group of patients. The content of the items was reviewed by oncology pharmacists for clinical relevance, content and face validity before the beginning of data collection. Then, a pilot study with 28 participants evaluated feasibility, validity, comprehension, and timing of the developed questionnaire. The median completion time ranged from 7 to 12 minutes, and internal consistency was acceptable with Cronbach’s alpha above 0.71 for the overall scale. No items were added, removed, or reworded after the pilot. Psychometric evaluation was limited to an assessment of content validity and internal consistency reliability with Cronbach’s α of 0.71), which indicate an acceptable coherence.

Prior to commencing the interview process, informed consent was obtained from all participants, emphasizing the voluntary nature of their participation and ensuring the anonymity of their responses. The first section of the questionnaire assessed participants’ cancer treatment awareness. This section gathered information on their level of understanding of their diagnosis, chemotherapy regimen, potential treatment-related adverse events, available management strategies for these adverse events, and any prior emergency room visits or hospitalizations within the past year due to chemotherapy-related complications. The second section focused on collecting demographic data, including participants’ age, gender, nationality, marital status, educational level, smoking history, and healthcare professional status (Appendix 1 and 2). After interviewing the patients, data collectors checked the electronic medical records (EMR), and verified the patients’ information in terms of cancer diagnosis and stage, chemotherapy regimens, emergency department (ED), and hospital admissions. Emergency department visits and hospital admissions were assessed as prior events occurring within the 12 months preceding questionnaire administration, based on patient report and EMR verification when available.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize the demographics and other relevant characteristics of participating cancer patients. Means and standard deviations (SD) were reported for continuous variables, while frequencies and percentages were used for categorical variables. To compare self-reported beliefs and knowledge among cancer patients with the measured level of awareness in EMR, we conducted an independent t-test. Awareness classification was based on concordance between selected patient self-reported knowledge items and corresponding information verified in the electronic medical record (EMR). Participants were categorized into two groups for analysis: highly aware/aware and not aware/low awareness. Patients whose self-reported knowledge was concordant with the relevant EMR-documented information were categorized as highly aware/aware, whereas those with discordant responses were categorized as not aware/low awareness. For descriptive interpretation, overall awareness was operationalized using predefined cutoffs: scores of 0–1 indicated not aware, 2–3 indicated low awareness, and 4–6 indicated highly aware. These categories were used as an operational classification for comparison purposes within this study.

We tested the assumption of equal variances, which was met based on the p-value obtained from Levene’s test. Therefore, the data were homogeneous and had equal variance, and we reported the pooled t-test results. To assess patients’ self-reported understanding and knowledge, a structured questionnaire was administered using a 5-point Likert scale: 5 = Strongly Agree 4 = Agree 3 = Neutral 2 = Disagree 1 = Strongly Disagree. The mean responses for each item were compared between the Not Aware/Low Awareness group and the Highly Aware group. A t-test analysis was conducted to determine statistical differences, with significance levels set at p < 0.05 (*) and p < 0.01 (**). Higher scores indicate a greater self-reported understanding of cancer-related information. Statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) between groups were interpreted as indicating differences between self-reported understanding and awareness classification as operationalized using EMR-verified information.

We further conducted exploratory negative binomial regression models to examine associations between awareness level, selected demographic characteristics, and the counts of emergency department visits and hospital admissions in the prior year. Negative binomial regression was used because the count outcomes were overdispersed (Pearson chi-square/degrees-of-freedom ratio = 2.34 for ED visits and 2.21 for hospital admissions). Crude and adjusted incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals were estimated, and a sensitivity analysis additionally adjusted for advanced (stage IV) disease. The regression analyses were intended as exploratory association analyses within a cross-sectional design and should not be interpreted as establishing temporal or causal relationships. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05, and all tests were 2-tailed. Descriptive statistics and group comparisons were performed using SAS statistical software (version 9.4, SAS Institute, Cary, NC), and the negative binomial regression models were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

In our study, we screened 617 eligible patients, approached 306 patients, and interviewed 270 participants (Figure 1). The average age of participants were 52 years. The gender distribution showed that 105 (38.9%) were male, while 165 (61.1%) were female. Regarding marital status, 178 (65.9%) were married, and 83 (30.7%) were categorized as single, widowed, or divorced. Regarding education, 94 (34.8%) had a high school diploma, and 77 (28.5%) had a bachelor’s degree or higher. The smoking status of the participants indicated that 220 (81.5%) were non-smokers, 30 (11.1%) were ex-smokers, and 20 (7.4%) were current smokers. The majority of participants were Saudi nationals (95.6%), while only 12 (4.4%) were residents of other nationalities. Among the participants, only 11 (4.1%) were healthcare professional employees.

The distribution of cancer diagnoses included 38 (14.1%) participants with colon and rectal cancer, 91 (33.7%) with breast cancer, 9 (3.3%) with lung cancer, 38 (14.1%) with lymphoma, multiple myeloma, and leukemia, and 94 (34.8%) with other types of cancer. The distribution of cancer stages showed that 19 (7.0%) participants were at stage I, 15 (5.6%) at stage II, 53 (19.6%) at stage III, 130 (48.1%) at stage IV, and 20 (7.4%) had hematologic cancer (Table 1). Demographic information was comparable between the high-aware and low/no-aware groups, including age, gender distribution, marital status, education, smoking status, nationality, employment in healthcare, English language fluency, cancer type, and stage of disease.

The Relationship Between Self-Reported Awareness and the Rate of Emergency Department (ED) Visits

In the negative binomial models (Table 3), awareness level was not associated with the rate of ED visits in either crude (IRR 1.03; 95% CI 0.68–1.57) or adjusted (IRR 1.08; 95% CI 0.79–1.47; p = 0.63) analyses. The only statistically significant predictor was English fluency: patients who did not speak English had a higher rate of ED visits than those with advanced fluency (adjusted IRR 2.50; 95% CI 1.20–5.21; p = 0.015), with a borderline elevation for intermediate fluency (adjusted IRR 1.97; 95% CI 0.99–3.95; p = 0.055). Female gender showed a non-significant trend (adjusted IRR 1.37; 95% CI 0.97–1.93; p = 0.07). Age, marital status, education, smoking status, nationality, and healthcare employment were not significantly associated with ED visits.

The Relationship Between Self-Reported Awareness and Hospital Admission Rates

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we assessed oncology patients’ awareness and knowledge of chemotherapy regimens and adverse events and explored their association with prior emergency visits and hospital admissions. In our cohort, the majority of patients (~69%) were classified as having moderate to high awareness based on self-report and concordance with EMR-verified information. This awareness classification was associated with greater knowledge of treatment goals, regimens, and side effects. Awareness was not independently associated with ED visits or hospital admissions. Instead, higher utilization was associated with limited English fluency for ED visits and with female gender for hospital admissions.

Comparable studies in different settings have reported variable levels of patient knowledge. In a Saudi study, only 8% of patients achieved high knowledge scores regarding chemotherapy and its side effects.7 Similarly, a descriptive study from Pakistan reported that although patients expressed positive attitudes toward chemotherapy, their actual knowledge and practices were limited.14 In Japan, educational level significantly influenced comprehension of chemotherapy among outpatients.15 In contrast, Patients in our study report a higher self-perceived knowledge; however, discrepancies with EMR data suggest overestimation of awareness. These findings reinforce the notion that, while patients may appear to self-report high awareness, objective measurement in the EMR often reveals discrepancies.

Language proficiency and health communication are critical determinants of the quality of oncology care. In our study, limited English proficiency was independently associated with higher ED visits, with a similar but non-significant trend for hospital admissions, indicating that communication barriers may contribute to reactive rather than preventive healthcare use. This finding aligns with multiple lines of evidence. In a statewide telephone survey of 1,200 Californians, limited-English-proficient respondents were significantly more likely to report difficulties understanding medical information and adverse medication reactions, even when treated by language-concordant physicians (adjusted OR 3.2 and 2.3, respectively), underscoring persistent comprehension gaps despite interpreter or bilingual support.16 Likewise, a study by Diamond et al systematically reviewed 33 studies and found that language-concordant care improved at least one clinical or communication outcome in 76% of studies, highlighting the protective effect of shared language on comprehension and adherence.17 At the systems level, Chen et al demonstrated that non-English-speaking callers were far less likely to be provided access to cancer care than English-speaking callers, revealing structural communication barriers from the very first point of contact.18 Collectively, these studies illustrate how language discordance operates throughout the cancer-care continuum, from access and comprehension to adherence and unplanned utilization, emphasizing the need for multilingual educational materials, interpreter integration, and culturally adapted counseling to reduce disparities and improve patient outcomes.16–19

In our cohort, awareness was not independently associated with ED visits once demographic factors were accounted for, indicating that knowledge alone did not drive unplanned care. The frequency of ED visits and admissions may also be influenced by the quality of outpatient and discharge education provided to patients. Hence, education without clear symptom-triage guidance may increase cautious healthcare-seeking behavior.20–23 These findings suggest that awareness alone may not translate into lower unplanned care without concurrent improvements in access to outpatient triage and same-day oncology review pathways.

Female gender was the principal correlate of hospital admission in our cohort, a finding aligned with reports that women often report symptoms earlier and utilize emergency services more frequently than men.22 Together with language-related disparities, these findings highlight the complex social, behavioral, and system-level factors shaping oncology care and unplanned hospital use. Organizations should provide clear, well-structured, comprehensible, and language-appropriate education on chemotherapy regimens and their potential adverse effects. One study found that almost half of oncology patients’ ED visits and admissions are preventable.23 Hence, integrating oncology pharmacists and patient educators to deliver reinforcement using visual aids, simplified language, and teach-back techniques can improve retention and decrease the risk of ED visits.24,25 Oncology educational programs should also emphasize appropriate healthcare-seeking behaviors, clarifying which symptoms require urgent attention versus those that can be managed routinely, to avoid unnecessary ED visits. Interventions such as 24/7 nurse or pharmacist telephone triage, multilingual discharge materials, and culturally tailored counseling may help shift utilization from unplanned emergency care toward proactive outpatient management. Hence, the value of integration of organized patient education, especially those faced with language barriers could help manage their symptoms along with the assistance of outpatient triaging.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing temporal or causal relationships between awareness and healthcare utilization. Because awareness showed no independent association with either outcome, reverse causation does not affect the study’s central findings, which instead identify female gender and limited English proficiency as the main correlates of higher utilization. Second, the study was conducted at a single center, which may limit generalizability to other settings within Saudi Arabia. Third, the questionnaire was investigator-developed and, although reviewed for face and content validity and pilot tested for clarity and feasibility, additional psychometric validation was not performed. Therefore, the awareness classification should be interpreted as an operational study-specific measure rather than a fully validated latent construct. Fourth, residual confounding by unmeasured clinical variables remains possible, including performance status, line of therapy, comorbidity burden, and regimen-specific toxicity risk. Fifth, self-reported responses are subject to recall and social desirability bias, and some participants may have overestimated their understanding of their diagnosis or treatment. Although EMR verification reduced some of this variability, discrepancies between self-report and documented information remained. Sixth, some ED visits or hospitalizations occurring outside the study institution may not have been fully captured. Finally, the heterogeneity of cancer types, treatment regimens, and disease stages in the sample may have influenced both awareness and healthcare utilization patterns.

Conclusion

Patient awareness of chemotherapy was generally high but uneven across domains, with gaps in identifying regimens and in defining treatment goals. Awareness was not independently associated with ED visits or hospital admissions; female gender and limited English proficiency were the principal correlates of higher utilization. Future studies should test whether integrating structured education with proactive symptom-management pathways can reduce unplanned care, particularly for patients with language barriers and those receiving high-toxicity regimens.
