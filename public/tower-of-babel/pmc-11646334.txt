Persistent Inequality in Access to Rheumatology Care for Females After the COVID‐19 Pandemic
Authors: Steven J. Katz, Carrie Ye
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo examine the effect of biological sex on wait‐times to first rheumatology appointment in a central triage system before, during and after the COVID‐19 pandemic.MethodsDe‐identified data of patients referred to one centralised Rheumatology referral centre between November 2019 and December 2023 were extracted from the electronic medical record. Variables collected and analysed included time from referral to first appointment, biological sex, referral period, triage urgency, age, and geographic location.Results19,681 referrals were identified. In the pre‐COVID period, there was no significant difference in wait‐times by biological sex or age. After adjusting for triage level, age and geographic location, females waited significantly longer in the peri‐COVID period versus males (10.2 days, 95% CI 7.1, 13.3), which persisted in the post‐COVID period (7.5 days, 95% CI 4.0, 11.1). Similarly, younger patients waited longer than older patients in the peri‐COVID period (4.7 fewer days per decade increase in age (95% 3.9, 5.6)). This age discrepancy persisted through the post‐COVID period (2.3 days, 95% CI 1.6, 3.5). Geographic location was a significant predictor of wait‐times in the post‐COVID period, with those outside of Edmonton waiting longer than in Edmonton. Once the change in referral pattern from Northwest Territories was accounted for, this discrepancy ceased.ConclusionsFemale and younger patients have been disproportionately impacted by wait‐time increases during the COVID‐19 pandemic, with minimal improvements observed during the post‐COVID period. These findings should prompt further investigation into the underlying causes of these observed inequities in access to rheumatology care to identify solutions.

Introduction

Rheumatic diseases are common; over 6 million Canadians and 60 million Americans suffer from arthritis or one of a hundred other rheumatic conditions (Badley et al. 2019; Barbour et al. 2017). Despite this, there are fewer than 400 full time clinical equivalent rheumatologists in Canada, or less than 1 rheumatologist per 100,000 population (Barber et al. 2017), resulting in challenges to timely access to rheumatology care (Widdifield et al. 2016; Anon), which is further compounded by delays in patient presentation and referral. Timely referral results in the best treatment and outcomes for patients, as evidence demonstrates that early initiation of DMARDs for inflammatory arthritis leads to better disease control and prevents disease progression (Ramiro et al. 2020). Central triage systems are designed to prioritise incoming rheumatology referrals that would most benefit from early assessment. The University of Alberta’s central triage system in Edmonton, Canada, has been previously reported as a robust system that correctly assigns referral urgency in a simple cost‐effective manner (Carpenter et al. 2014).

The COVID‐19 pandemic had a significant impact on the healthcare system. In rheumatology, much of the focus was on the effects of immunosuppression on COVID‐19 treatment and outcomes in those with autoimmune inflammatory rheumatic diseases (Hazlewood et al. 2021; Mikuls et al. 2021). Less clear was the impact the pandemic had on initial access to care for rheumatology services and whether this impact affected men and women equally. We previously reported that wait times for an initial appointment with a rheumatologist during the COVID‐19 pandemic were significantly longer for female and younger patients, differences that did not exist prior to the COVID‐19 pandemic (Katz et al. 2023). It is unknown if these discrepancies in access to rheumatology care persisted in the post‐COVID period or returned to their pre‐COVID baseline. Here, we conduct an updated retrospective cohort study, including the pre‐, peri‐ and post‐COVID periods, examining the effect of biological sex, age and geographical location on wait time to first rheumatology appointment in a group practice that employs a central triage system.

Methods

Central Triage System

The triage system within the Division of Rheumatology at the University of Alberta has been described previously (Carpenter et al. 2014). During the study period, 12 rheumatologists participated. Briefly, each week, one rheumatologist reviewed and triaged incoming patient referrals. Triage was based on the referral letter and information provided therein; a rheumatologist could choose to look up additional investigation results, but this was not required. There was no standardized evaluation or scoring system used. The triage rheumatologist then assigned a pre‐assessment diagnosis and triage level.

Triage level was assigned based on the pre‐assessment diagnosis and the need for early access to care. Triage levels were categorised as urgent, semi‐urgent, or routine, with ideal target wait times of less than 7 days, 6 weeks and no target, respectively. Urgent consults were those with organ threatening disease and were typically facilitated through direct physician‐to‐physician communication. Semi‐urgent consults were those with a suspicion of an inflammatory process such as inflammatory arthritis or inflammatory connective tissue diseases. Routine consults were conducted for cases suspected of having non‐inflammatory conditions such as osteoarthritis or soft tissue ailments. Once the triage level was assigned, the central triage administrator contacted patients consecutively for an appointment based on their triage level.

Study Design

In November 2019, the central triage system moved from eClinician, an EPIC‐ based outpatient electronic medical record, to ConnectCare, an updated EPIC‐ based comprehensive universal electronic medical record. ConnectCare allows for specific reporting of patient referral wait‐times and patient characteristics. We extracted de‐identified data of all referred patients between November 2019 and December 2023. Patient‐level variables collected included: time from referral to first appointment, biological sex, referral period, triage level, age and geographic location. The referral period was defined as pre‐COVID prior to March 1 2020, peri‐COVID from March 1, 2020 to June 30, 2022 (when all restrictions were lifted in the geographic catchment area), or post‐COVID from July 1, 2022 to December 31, 2023. Geographic location was identified using postal code data as in the city (within the Greater Edmonton Metropolitan area) and outside the city (outside the Greater Edmonton Metropolitan area).

During the post COVID period, the Northwest Territories (NWT), a federal territory in Canada located directly north of the province of Alberta, shuttered their rheumatology programme, resulting in their rheumatology patients being transferred simultaneously to Edmonton through the central triage system. While patients from NWT had been referred to our centre in the past, this was a significant and sudden increase. This unexpected influx of over 300 patients was triaged, but bookings were delayed for this group due to administrative and logistical barriers. No other changes were made to the triage system during this study period. The University of Alberta Medical Ethics Board approved this project (Pro00124125).

Statistical Analysis

Summary statistics were used to describe the differences between men and women. Continuous variables were compared using Student's t‐test, dichotomous variables were compared with the chi‐square test and categorical exposures (triage level, referral period) were compared using one‐way analysis of variance.

Linear regression was used to determine invariable associations of wait time. Multivariable linear regression models were used to adjust for biological sex, age, referral period, region and triage level. Given that our original study found that there were discrepancies in wait times based on age and gender, we examined the effect modification of referral period on the impact of gender and age on wait times by including the two‐way interaction terms sex*referral period and age*referral period in the multivariable model. Because these interaction terms were significant, multivariable linear regression models were run, stratified by Covid period (pre‐/peri‐/post‐COVID), using a Bonferroni‐adjusted alpha error cutoff for significance of p < 0.016. Sensitivity analysis was performed by running the multivariable linear regression model excluding NWT referrals. Statistical analysis was completed with STATA/BE 17.0 (StataCorp. 2021. Stata Statistical Software: Release 17. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).

Results

The referral cohort included 19,681 individuals with an average age of 53.5 years and 13,233 women (67.2%) and 6448 men (32.8%, Table 1). The average age of men referred over the study period was older than that of women (55.1 vs. 52.7 years). Their triage level was less likely to be routine (47.9% vs. 54.6%) and their average wait times were shorter than that of females (78.8 vs. 90.1 days). Wait times increased for all triage categories of referrals from the pre‐to the peri‐COVID periods, and further increased for those triaged as semi‐urgent and urgent in the post‐COVID period (Table 2). However, the wait times for those triaged as routine dropped from 102 to 96 days from the peri‐COVID to the post‐COVID periods.

In crude, unadjusted analysis, females waited an average of 11.3 (95% CI 9.0, 13.6, p < 0.001) days longer for their first appointment than males (Table 3). Compared to the pre‐COVID period, wait times during the peri‐COVID and the post‐COVID periods were significantly longer by 25 and 23 days, respectively (p < 0.001). Wait times were 4 days shorter for every decade increase in age and 3.6 days longer for those living outside Edmonton. Compared to those triaged as routine, those triaged as semi‐urgent had their first appointment 20 days earlier and those triaged as urgent had their first appointment on average 81 days sooner (p < 0.001).

In the pre‐COVID period, wait times did not differ by age in the fully adjusted model (p = 0.926). However, in the peri‐COVID period, for every decade of increasing age, the wait times were 4.7 days shorter (95% 3.9, 5.6, p < 0.001) and in the post‐COVID period, the wait times were 2.6 days shorter (95% CI 1.6, 3.5, p < 0.001). The effect of age on wait times was significantly less in the post‐COVID period as compared to its effect during the peri‐COVID period (p = 0.002).

In all referral periods, those with higher urgency in triage level waited significantly fewer days than those triaged as routine. Those who lived outside of Edmonton did not wait significantly longer for their first appointment compared to those living in Edmonton pre‐COVID and peri‐COVID (both p > 0.016). Post‐COVID, those living outside of Edmonton waited 6.6 days longer than those living in Edmonton (95% CI 3.3, 10.0, p < 0.001). However, once the 303 NWT referrals were excluded from the main cohort (Table S1), those living outside of Edmonton no longer had a longer wait time than those living in Edmonton, in any of the COVID periods (Table S2).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that compared to the pre‐COVID period, biological sex discrepancies in wait times for new rheumatology referrals persist from the peri‐ COVID to the post‐COVID period. While longer wait times during the COVID pandemic have been previously described (Abdulaziz et al. 2022; Stickels et al. 2022; Dolan et al. 2022), persistence of longer wait times has not been widely reported. Even more alarmingly, in follow‐up to the original study (Katz et al. 2023), women continue to wait significantly longer than men post‐COVID compared to pre‐COVID, without a significant improvement noted from the peri‐COVID period to the post‐COVID period. This differs from the wait time differences we initially observed for age during the peri‐COVID period (Katz et al. 2023). While younger patients are still waiting longer for their first appointments post‐COVID period compared to the pre‐COVID period, there has been a significant reduction in the age effect on wait times compared to the peri‐COVID period. Geographic location of patients did not impact first appointment wait times between the pre‐ and peri‐ COVID periods. Reassuringly, there is still no difference observed in the post‐COVID period once the sudden influx of NWT referrals was accounted for.

As previously described, appointments in the central triage system were assigned consecutively based on triage level alone, meaning that personal characteristics such as age, biological sex and geographic location should not impact wait times. We previously speculated that the longer wait times for women during the peri‐COVID period may be due to the different impact that the COVID‐19 pandemic and the associated public health restrictions had on women compared to men, with multiple studies suggesting greater impact on employment and a higher burden of childcare during the pandemic compared to men (Yildirim et al. 2021; Zamarro et al. 2021). Our study re‐examined wait times in the post‐COVID period, defined as a period when all public health restrictions were lifted in the geographic catchment area. Despite this, women continue to wait longer for appointments. This follow‐up study did not investigate the underlying cause for the ongoing discrepancy. It is possible that while public health restrictions were lifted, the patterns of individuals or employers have not yet substantially changed during the post‐COVID study period, resulting in an ongoing wait time gap. For instance, even without public health restrictions, children may still be staying home from school more often due to illness, which could be more of a burden on women than men. With a longer follow‐up period post‐COVID‐19, perhaps we would see a return to the pre‐COVID observed equity in wait times for men and women. However, the continued discrepancy with no significant sign of improvement in the year and half since restrictions were lifted is disappointing. It is reassuring to determine improvement in wait times based on age, although this effect has not returned to that of the pre‐COVID period. It is possible here too that childcare issues are still impacting younger adults more than older adults, but this is a conjecture with no literature specifically addressing this to date.

We acknowledge that this study has limitations. The time periods studied are limited, with the pre‐COVID period covering only 6 months prior to the COVID19 pandemic due to when ConnectCare launched in the region. This may impact our ability to detect differences between groups in the pre‐COVID time period, although there were no other changes in our triage process. Similarly, while longer, the post‐COVID period in this study is limited to approximately 18 months. A longer follow‐up period would allow us to examine trends over time in the post‐COVID period. In addition, the post‐COVID period was significantly impacted by the influx of NWT patients to the central triage process. Although we could exclude this group from our analysis, the impact of this influx of referrals on our remaining cohort is unknown, although there is no reason to think that the impact would have been different for men versus women in the non‐NWT cohort. Finally, while this study divides the time periods into 3 discrete periods, we recognise that the peri‐ and post‐COVID‐19 periods are likely more complex, with waves of increased COVID‐19 infections, varying degrees of public health restrictions, and individual choices. A detailed review of the infection rates, health restrictions and personal choices over the triage period could help answer more detailed questions, but was outside the scope of this study.

Wait times in general continue to be higher in post‐COVID‐19 as compared to pre‐COVID‐19 patients, but more alarmingly, female and younger patients continue to be impacted by longer wait times than men and older patients. These findings should be investigated at other rheumatology centres as well as across different specialities to determine if this finding is unique to our centre or speciality. Continued research is necessary to monitor these observed discrepancies, and more importantly, to understand the root causes of these discrepancies so as to develop interventions to ensure equity in access to healthcare post‐COVID‐19 pandemic.
