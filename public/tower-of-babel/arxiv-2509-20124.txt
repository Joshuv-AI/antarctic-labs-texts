Probability Signature: Bridging Data Semantics and Embedding Structure in Language Models
Junjie Yao, Zhi-Qin John Xu

Abstract
The embedding space of language models is widely believed to capture the semantic relationships; for instance, embeddings of digits often exhibit an ordered structure that corresponds to their natural sequence. However, the mechanisms driving the formation of such structures remain poorly understood. In this work, we interpret the embedding structures via the data distribution. We propose a set of probability signatures that reflect the semantic relationships among tokens. Through experiments on the composite addition tasks using the linear model and feedforward network, combined with theoretical analysis of gradient flow dynamics, we reveal that these probability signatures significantly influence the embedding structures. We further generalize our analysis to large language models (LLMs) by training the Qwen2.5 architecture on the subsets of the Pile corpus. Our results show that the probability signatures are faithfully aligned with the embedding structures, particularly in capturing strong pairwise similarities among embeddings. Our work uncovers the mechanism of how data distribution guides the formation of embedding structures, establishing a novel understanding of the relationship between embedding organization and semantic patterns.



1
Introduction

In recent years, deep neural network-based large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable performance
(
Comanici et al. 2025
;
OpenAI et al. 2024
;
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
)
. The development of these models has largely followed what Richard Sutton termed “the bitter lesson”–that the most effective approach to improving AI performance has historically been to leverage greater computational resources, larger models, and more data, rather than incorporating human knowledge or specialized architectures
(
Sutton 2019
)
. This trend has been formalized through scaling laws, which quantify the relationship between model performance and factors such as model size, dataset size, and computational budget through power law relationships
(
Kaplan et al. 2020
)
. While these scaling laws provide valuable quantitative predictions for model performance, they also reveal a concerning limitation: the power law relationship suggests that achieving further significant improvements may require prohibitively large increases in model and data size, making continued scaling increasingly impractical and resource-intensive.

One promising approach to address these limitations is to develop a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms that drive transformer models’ success in natural language processing (NLP). The No Free Lunch theorem establishes that no single algorithm can perform optimally across all problem domains, highlighting the fundamental importance of understanding both the characteristics of the data and the properties of the algorithms that process it
(
Wolpert & Macready 1997
)
.
Recent research has made significant progress in uncovering key properties of deep learning models, including the edge of stability phenomenon
(
Wu et al. 2018
;
Cohen et al. 2021
)
, frequency principle
(
Xu et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2025a
)
, attention patterns
(
Elhage et al. 2021
;
Olsson et al. 2022
;
Bhojanapalli et al. 2020
)
, parameter distribution properties
(
Kovaleva et al. 2021
;
Dar et al. 2023
)
, condensation phenomenon
(
Luo et al. 2021
;
Xu et al. 2025b
)
, and embedding structure
(
Cai et al. 2021
)
.
There has also been some investigation into data characteristics—such as the power-law decay of correlations between elements (like pixels) as a function of their distance
(
Ruderman 1994
)
. However, there is a significant gap in understanding how these two fundamental aspects—algorithmic properties and data characteristics—interact to produce the remarkable performance.

The embedding space, which acts as the encoder of the language tokens, therefore provides an ideal entry point for investigating how algorithmic properties and data characteristics interact. Ideally, the structure of embeddings should reflect the semantic relationships among tokens. A concrete example involves digits such as
0,1,2,\dots
, which possess a natural ordering. Their embedding vectors accordingly display an ordered structure consistent with this numerical sequence, reflecting basic reasoning capabilities in mathematical tasks
(
Mikolov et al. 2013b
;
Ethayarajh et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Yao et al. 2025
)
.
However, the cause of the consistency between embedding structures and semantic structures is still an open question, and the driving factors of the embedding structure are still not well characterized.

In this work, we identify a set of probability signatures that encode the semantic information in the data into the structure of the embedding space of language models. Such probability signatures are constructed based on label distribution, input distribution, input/output co-occurrence distribution, etc, that systematically capture inherent token-level relationships and reflect semantic structures. This result is achieved via utilizing an embedding-based model with gradient flow analysis of embedding vectors and unembedding vectors for well-designed variable-controlled experiments. We further extend our findings to LLMs with realistic corpora, such as the Qwen2.5 architecture
(
Team 2024
)
and subsets of the Pile dataset
(
Gao et al. 2020
;
Biderman et al. 2022
)
.
The analysis approach with the controlled experiments offers a promising methodology to uncover more and more probability signatures that can bridge the data semantics and embedding structure in language models.

Related Work

Parameter analysis in LLMs

Investigating the underlying parameter properties in LLMs is crucial for understanding the foundation of models. Some works focus on the specific modules in models.
Elhage et al. 2021
;
Olsson et al. 2022
uncover mechanisms such as induction heads from the attention module.
Bhojanapalli et al. 2020
reveals the rank-collapse phenomenon of the attention matrix.
Geva et al. 2021
;
Geva et al. 2022
;
Dai et al. 2022
investigates the characteristics and functions of the FFN in LLMs. Additionally, analysis of a single neuron has been widely employed in mechanism interpretation, particularly in circuits analysis
Hanna et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Hanna et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
, sparse autoencoders (SAE)
Huben et al. 2024
;
Bricken et al. 2023
, transcoders
Dunefsky et al. 2024
, and cross-layer transcoders (CLT)
Ameisen et al. 2025
. There are also some studies investigating the global properties of all parameters.
Dar et al. 2023
;
Katz et al. 2024
introduce a framework for interpreting all parameters of Transformer models by projecting them into the embedding space.
Kovaleva et al. 2021
;
Yu et al. 2025
provide an analysis of the parameter distribution, demonstrating the significance of these outliers. In this work, we will focus on the embedding space, explaining the formation of its structure from both experimental and theoretical perspectives.

Embedding structure and representation learning

Since the introduction of static word embeddings by
Mikolov et al. 2013a
;
Pennington et al. 2014
and the adoption of contextualized embeddings
(
Devlin et al. 2019
;
Peters et al. 2018
)
, significant attention has been devoted to analyzing embedding properties.
Gao et al. 2019
;
Ethayarajh 2019
;
Timkey & van Schijndel 2021
explore the anisotropy of embedding space, while
Cai et al. 2021
show that embeddings exhibit isotropy within clusters.
Liu et al. 2022
offers insights into grokking by emphasizing the role of well-organized embedding structures.
Zhang et al. 2024
establishes a connection between embedding structure and model generalization, and
Yao et al. 2025
provides an analysis of this relationship. In contrast to prior work, our study focuses on the connection between embedding structure and data properties, offering a novel insight for understanding how embeddings are organized.

3
Embedding-based Model

We first explain the basic notation of embedding space. Given a vocabulary
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{N}^{+}
with size
d_{\rm vob}
. We denote a trainable matrix
\bm{W}^{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\rm vob}}
as the embedding matrix for
\mathcal{V}
, where
d
is the hidden dimension. For any
x\in\mathcal{V}
, we denote
\bm{e}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm vob}}
as its one-hot vector and
\bm{W}^{E}_{x}:=\bm{W}^{E}\bm{e}_{x}
as the embedding vector of
x
, which is intuitively the
x
-th column of
\bm{W}^{E}
. For a sequence
\bm{X}=\left[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{L}\right]\subset\mathcal{V}
, we define its one-hot representation as
\bm{e}_{\bm{X}}:=\left[\bm{e}_{x_{1}},\bm{e}_{x_{2}},\cdots,\bm{e}_{x_{L}}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm vob}\times L}
and
\bm{W}^{E}_{\bm{X}}:=\left[\bm{W}^{E}_{x_{1}},\bm{W}^{E}_{x_{2}},\cdots,\bm{W}^{E}_{x_{L}}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times L}
as the embedding sequence of
\bm{X}
. Similarly,
\bm{W}^{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm vob}\times d}
represents the unembedding matrix and
\bm{W}^{U}_{\nu}:=\bm{W}^{U,T}\bm{e}_{\nu}
(the
\nu
-th row of
\bm{W}^{U}
) means the unembedding vector for any
\nu\in\mathcal{V}
.

We denote the models functioning on the embedding of the input sequence as embedding-based models. We provide the following formulation:

Definition 1
.

Given a vocabulary
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{N}^{+}
with size
d_{\rm vob}
and a sequence
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}
with length
L
. An embedding-based model
F
taking
\bm{X}
as input could be formulated as

F\left(\bm{X}\right)=\bm{W}^{U}G\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\bm{X}}\right),

where
G
means the mapping in the hidden space.

Embedding-based models have been widely applied in various domains, particularly in NLP. Investigating the characteristics of the embedding space via the gradient flow dynamics is essential for understanding the embedding structures. Given a dataset
\left\{\left(\bm{X}^{i},y^{i}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
, we utilize cross-entropy as the loss function:

\ell^{i}=-\log\text{Softmax}\left(F\left(\bm{X}^{i}\right)\right)_{y^{i}}=-\log\frac{\exp F\left(\bm{X}^{i}\right)_{y^{i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\rm vob}}\exp F\left(\bm{X}^{i}\right)_{j}}.

Denote that
\bm{p}^{i}={\rm Softmax}\left(F\left(\bm{X}^{i}\right)\right)
. Let
\odot
represent the Hadamard (element-wise) product and
T
mean the transpose, we obtain the following results.

Proposition 1
.

Given an embedding-based model
F
with an embedding matrix
\bm{W}^{E}
. For any token
x\in\mathcal{V}
, the gradient flow of
\bm{W}^{E}_{x}
(the
x
-th column of
\bm{W}^{E}
) can be formulated as:

\frac{d\bm{W}^{E}_{x}}{dt}=\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}\frac{r_{x,\nu}}{N_{x,\nu}}\left(\bm{W}^{{U},T}\bm{e}_{\nu}\right)\odot\sum_{i=1}^{N_{x,\nu}}G^{(1)}\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\bm{X}_{\left(x,\nu\right)}^{i}}\right)-\frac{r^{\rm in}_{x}}{N^{\rm in}_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\rm in}_{x}}G^{(1)}\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\bm{X}^{i}_{x}}\right)\odot\left(\bm{W}^{{U},T}\bm{p}_{x}^{i}\right),

where
N^{\rm in}_{x},N_{x,\nu}
denotes the count of sequences containing
x
and the count of sequences containing
x
with label
\nu
,
r^{\rm in}_{x}=\frac{N^{\rm in}_{x}}{N},r_{x,\nu}=\frac{N_{s,\nu}}{N}
,
G^{(1)}
represents the derivative of
G
with respect to
\bm{W}^{E}_{x}
.
\bm{X}^{i}_{x}
is the
i
-th sequence containing
x
and
\bm{X}^{i}_{\left(x,\nu\right)}
denotes the
i
-th sequence containing
x
with label
\nu
.
\bm{p}_{x}^{i}
means the output probability of the model over
\bm{X}^{i}_{x}
.

Proposition 2
.

Given an embedding-based model
F
with an unembedding matrix
\bm{W}^{U}
. For any token
\nu\in\mathcal{V}
, the gradient flow of
\bm{W}^{U}_{\nu}
(the
\nu
-th row of
\bm{W}^{U}
) can be written as

\frac{d\bm{W}^{U}_{\nu}}{dt}=\frac{r^{\rm out}_{\nu}}{N^{\rm out}_{\nu}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\rm out}_{\nu}}\left[G\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\bm{X}_{\left(\cdot,\nu\right)}^{i}}\right)\right]^{T}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{p}^{i,\nu}\left[G\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\bm{X}^{i}}\right)\right]^{T},

where
N^{\rm out}_{\nu}
denotes the count of sequences with label
\nu
and
r^{\rm out}_{\nu_{j}}=\frac{N^{\rm out}_{\nu}}{N}
.
\bm{X}_{\left(\cdot,\nu\right)}^{i}
means the
i
-th sample which takes
\nu
as the label.
\bm{p}^{i,\nu}
means the
\nu
-th element of
\bm{p}^{i}
.

In this work, we employ three embedding-based architectures:

•

Linear model.
F_{\rm lin}\left(\bm{X}\right)=\bm{W}^{U}\sum_{x\in\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{E}_{x}
.

•

Feedforward network.
F_{\rm ffn}\left(\bm{X}\right)=\bm{W}^{U}\sigma\left(\sum_{x\in\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{E}_{x}\right)
, where
\sigma
denotes the element-wise nonlinear activation.

•

Transformer-based architecture. We employ the Qwen2.5 architecture in Section
6
and the Llama 2 architecture
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
in Appendix
C.4
.

4
Probability Signature

In the field of deep learning, the data characteristics play a critically important role in both the training dynamics and the final performance of the model. Given a training dataset
\left\{\left(\bm{X}^{i},y^{i}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
sampled from distribution
\pi
, we define the following
probability signatures
:

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}_{x}^{y}=\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(y=\nu\mid x\in\bm{X}\right)\bm{e}_{\nu},\quad\bm{\phi}_{x}^{\bm{X}}=\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(x^{\prime}\in\bm{X}\mid x\in\bm{X}\right)\bm{e}_{x^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}_{x}^{\bm{X}\mid y}=\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}\bm{e}_{\nu}\left(\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{P}\left(x^{\prime}\in\bm{X}\mid x\in\bm{X},y=\nu\right)\bm{e}_{x^{\prime}}\right)^{T},\quad\bm{\varphi}_{\nu}^{\bm{X}}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(x\in\bm{X}\mid y=\nu\right)\bm{e}_{x}.

\bm{\phi}_{x}^{y}
denotes the distribution of the label given that the input sequence
\bm{X}
contains element
x
. It captures the association between a specific input element
x
and its label.

\bm{\phi}_{x}^{\bm{X}}
represents the probability that, given
\bm{X}
contains
x
, it also contains another input element
x^{\prime}
. It characterizes the co-occurrence relationship between different elements.

\bm{\phi}_{x}^{\bm{X}\mid y}
indicates the probability that, when
\bm{X}
contains
x
and the label is fixed, the sequence additionally includes
x^{\prime}
. It further delineates the relationship among different elements in the sequence under a specified label condition.

\bm{\varphi}_{\nu}^{\bm{X}}
denotes the probability distribution of the element
x
contained in
\bm{X}
conditional with the label being
\nu
. It reflects the dependency between input elements and the label from a reverse perspective.

5
Addition Task

The addition task has become an important benchmark for studying the characteristics of language models. Many studies have found that the digits’ embeddings exhibit an ordered structure consistent with the natural sequence of numbers
(
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Yao et al. 2025
)
. To demonstrate the significant influence of probability signatures on the model’s embedding space, we design three types of composite addition tasks to perform
variable-controlled experiments
. Assuming all tokens belong to positive integers, and we denote an anchor set by
\mathcal{A}
, whose elements represent different addition operations, i.e., anchor
\alpha_{1}
means addition with
\alpha_{1}
. Given a input sequence
\bm{X}=\left[z,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\right]
, which means the composite function
\left(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\right)
on the key
z
, we define the following tasks:

•

Addition task.
f_{\rm add}\left(\bm{X}\right)=z+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\quad\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
. For each anchor pair
\left(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\right)
,
z
is sampled from the same set
\mathcal{Z}
with
\mathcal{Z}\cap\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
. In
f_{\rm add}
,
\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{y}
are distinct with varying anchor
\alpha
while
\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{\bm{X}}
are identical across anchors.

•

Addition task with the same value domain.
\tilde{f}_{\rm add}\left(\bm{X}\right)=z+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\quad\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
. For anchor pair
\left(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\right)
,
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{\left(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\right)}=\mathcal{Y}-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}
where
\mathcal{Y}
denotes the label domain, which is identical for all anchor pairs. In
\tilde{f}_{\rm add}
,
\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{\bm{X}}
are distinct with varying anchor
\alpha
while
\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{y}
are identical for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
.

•

Module addition.
f_{\rm mod}\left(\bm{X}\right)=\min\mathcal{Z}+\left(z+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\mod\mid\mathcal{Z}\mid\right),\quad\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
and
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. Both
\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{\bm{X}}
and
\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{y}
are identical with different anchors, while
\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{\bm{X}\mid y}
are distinct.

In this work, we set
\mathcal{A}=\left\{11,12,\cdots,20\right\}
and
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{Z}=\left\{101,102,\cdots,140\right\}
. Figure

\alpha
in each task, revealing that the difference among
\alpha
lies in the global horizontal shift. The detailed formulations are provided in Appendix

5.2
Embedding Matrix

In the addition tasks, the anchors exhibit a strict ordering due to the numerical sequence. This provides an ideal setting for the embedding space to develop a corresponding ordered relationship. To formally quantify the formation of the ordered structure, we define the following metric:

R_{\rm order}\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}\right)={\rm Corr}\left(\cos\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}\right),\left\{\mid\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}\mid\right\}_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\right).

R_{\rm order}\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}\right)
reflects the relationship between embedding similarity and anchor difference. A strong negative
R_{\rm order}\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}\right)
(approximately
-1
) indicates that the similarity decreases systematically with increasing anchor difference, confirming the presence of a hierarchical organization in the anchor embeddings. Figure
2
A represents the distribution of
\cos\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}\right)
for the three tasks with
F_{\rm lin}
and
F_{\rm ffn}

2
B depicts the corresponding evolution of
R_{\rm order}\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}\right)

f_{\rm add}

For the task
\tilde{f}_{\rm add}

(Embedding of Linear Model)

Let

7
Conclusion

In this work, we investigate the formation of embedding structures in language models. By interpreting the relationship between embedding organization and semantic structure through the lens of data distribution, we propose the probability signatures and design the addition tasks to conduct variable-controlled experiments. Our findings demonstrate that probability signatures play a crucial role in shaping the embedding structure and reflecting underlying semantic relationships. An extended analysis of LLMs further confirms our analysis. This study establishes a bridge between data semantics and embedding space, offering new insights into the understanding of the joint impact of model, data and optimization method. For future work, we plan to extend our theoretical analysis into a comprehensive framework. Besides, we aim to incorporate the self-attention mechanism into our analysis of the LLMs, which is essential for capturing more subtle and complex relationships among embeddings that remain beyond the reach of our current methods.

B.1
Probability Signatures in Addition Tasks

We provide a formulation of the following probability in the three addition tasks. We denote
U\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
and
U\left(\mathcal{Z}\right)
as the discrete uniform distribution over
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{Z}
, respectively.
A
and
Z
are the random variables following
U\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
and
U\left(\mathcal{Z}\right)
. For the task
f_{\rm add}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(y=\nu\mid\alpha\in\bm{X}\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(A+Z=\nu-\alpha\right),\quad\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(z\in\mathcal{X}\mid\alpha\in\bm{X}\right)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Z}|},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(z\in\bm{X}\mid\alpha\in\bm{X},y=\nu\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(A=\nu-\alpha-z\right)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\delta_{\nu-\alpha-z\in\mathcal{A}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(\alpha^{\prime}\in\bm{X}\mid\alpha\in\bm{X},y=\nu\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(Z=\nu-\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}\right)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Z}|}\delta_{\nu-\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(z\in\bm{X}\mid y=\nu\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(A+A=\nu-z\right),\quad\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(\alpha\in\bm{X}\mid y=\nu\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(A+Z=\nu-\alpha\right),

where
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A},z\in\mathcal{Z}
.It’s noted that besides the co-occurrence probability
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(z\in\mathcal{X}\mid\alpha\in\bm{X}\right)

\alpha
or
\nu
. Figure
7

\tilde{f}_{\rm add}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(y=\nu\mid\alpha\in\bm{X}\right)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|},\quad\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(z\in\mathcal{X}\mid\alpha\in\bm{X}\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(Y-A=z+\alpha\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(z\in\bm{X}\mid\alpha\in\bm{X},y=\nu\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(A=\nu-\alpha-z\right)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\delta_{\nu-\alpha-z\in\mathcal{A}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(\alpha^{\prime}\in\bm{X}\mid\alpha\in\bm{X},y=\nu\right)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Z}|},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(z\in\bm{X}\mid y=\nu\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(A+A=\nu-z\right),\quad\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(\alpha\in\bm{X}\mid y=\nu\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(A+Z=\nu-\alpha\right).

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(y=\nu\mid\alpha\in\bm{X}\right)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Z}|},\quad\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(z\in\mathcal{X}\mid\alpha\in\bm{X}\right)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Z}|},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(\alpha^{\prime}\in\bm{X}\mid\alpha\in\bm{X},y=\nu\right)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Z}|},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(z\in\bm{X}\mid y=\nu\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(\left(A+A\text{ mod}|\mathcal{Z}|\right)=\nu-\min\mathcal{Z}-\left(z\text{ mod}|\mathcal{Z}|\right)\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(\alpha\in\bm{X}\mid y=\nu\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(\left(A+Z\text{ mod}|\mathcal{Z}|\right)=\nu-\min\mathcal{Z}-\left(\alpha\text{ mod}|\mathcal{Z}|\right)\right).

In the Qwen2.5-3B-base model, the embedding matrix and unembedding matrix are the same one, which aims for computational source saving. Under this condition, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{W}^{E}_{s}}{dt}

\displaystyle=r^{\rm in}_{s}\bm{W}^{{U},T}\bm{\phi}_{s}^{\rm next}+r_{s}^{\rm out}\bm{W}^{E}\bm{\varphi}_{s}^{\rm pre}+\bm{\eta}

Theoretical Details

D.1
Proof of Proposition
1

Lemma 1
.

Given a model
F
and data pair
\left(\bm{X},y\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{+,L}\times\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
\ell=-\log\text{Softmax}\left(F\left(\bm{X}\right)\right)_{y}
, we have that

\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial F\left(\bm{X}\right)}=\bm{p}-\bm{e}_{y},

(5)

where
\bm{p}=\text{softmax}\left(\bm{X}\right).

Proof.

It’s noted
\ell=-F\left(\bm{X}\right)_{y}+\log\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\rm vob}}\exp F\left(\bm{X}\right)_{j}
, then we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial F\left(X\right)_{i}}=-\delta_{i=y}+\frac{\exp F\left(\bm{X}\right)_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\rm vob}}\exp F\left(\bm{X}\right)_{j}}=\bm{p}_{i}-\delta_{i=y},

where
\delta_{i=y}=1
if
i=y
else
0
. This indicates that
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial F\left(\bm{X}\right)}=\bm{p}-\bm{e}_{y}
.
∎

With Lemma
1
, we could obtain the derivative of
\ell
with respect to
\bm{W}^{E}_{x}
for any
x\in\mathcal{V}
as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell^{i}}{\partial\bm{W}^{E}_{x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial F\left(\bm{X}^{i}\right)}{\partial\bm{W}^{E}_{x}}\frac{\partial\ell^{i}}{\partial F\left(\bm{X}^{i}\right)}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{W}^{U,T}\left(\bm{p}^{i}-\bm{e}_{y^{i}}\right)\right)\odot G^{\left(1\right)}\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\bm{X}^{i}}\right).

Then the gradient flow of
vW^{E}_{x}
could be obtained by

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{W}^{E}_{x}}{dt}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\ell^{i}}{\partial\bm{W}^{E}_{x}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\bm{W}^{U,T}\left(\bm{p}^{i}-\bm{e}_{y^{i}}\right)\right)\odot G^{\left(1\right)}\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\bm{X}^{i}}\right),

Since
{\rm diag}\left(G^{\left(1\right)}\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\bm{X}^{i}}\right)\right)=0
if
x\notin\bm{X}^{i}
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{W}^{E}_{x}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{x}^{\rm in}}\left(\bm{W}^{U,T}\left(\bm{e}_{y^{i}_{x}}-\bm{p}^{i}_{x}\right)\right)\odot G^{\left(1\right)}\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\bm{X}^{i}_{x}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{x}^{\rm in}}{N_{x}^{\rm in}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{x}^{\rm in}}\left(\bm{W}^{U,T}\left(\bm{e}_{y^{i}_{x}}-\bm{p}^{i}_{x}\right)\right)\odot G^{\left(1\right)}\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\bm{X}^{i}_{x}}\right).

Since that
y^{i}_{x}
takes value
\nu\in\mathcal{V}
, we can rewrite this formation as

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{W}^{E}_{x}}{dt}=\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}\frac{r_{x,\nu}}{N_{x,\nu}}\left(\bm{W}^{{U},T}\bm{e}_{\nu}\right)\odot\sum_{i=1}^{N_{x,\nu}}G^{(1)}\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\bm{X}_{\left(x,\nu\right)}^{i}}\right)-\frac{r^{\rm in}_{x}}{N^{\rm in}_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\rm in}_{x}}G^{(1)}\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\bm{X}^{i}_{x}}\right)\odot\left(\bm{W}^{{U},T}\bm{p}_{x}^{i}\right).

D.3
Proof of Corollary
1

With proposition
1
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}^{{U},T}\left(\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}r_{\alpha,\nu}\bm{e}_{\nu}-\frac{r^{\rm in}_{\alpha}}{N^{\rm in}_{\alpha}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\rm in}_{x}}\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{W}^{U,T}r^{\rm in}_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}\frac{r_{\alpha,\nu}}{r_{\alpha}^{\rm in}}\bm{e}_{\nu}-\frac{1}{N^{\rm in}_{\alpha}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\rm in}_{x}}\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{i}\right).

Utilizing that
{\rm softmax}\left(\bm{f}\right)=\frac{1}{d_{\rm vob}}\mathbf{1}+\frac{1}{d_{\rm vob}}\bm{f}+\mathcal{O}\left(d_{\rm vob}^{-2}\bm{f}\right)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}^{U,T}r^{\rm in}_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}\frac{r_{\alpha,\nu}}{r_{\alpha}^{\rm in}}\bm{e}_{\nu}-\frac{1}{N^{\rm in}_{\alpha}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\rm in}_{\alpha}}\left(\frac{1}{d_{\rm vob}}\mathbf{1}-\frac{1}{d_{\rm vob}}\bm{W}^{U}\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{z_{i}}+\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha_{i}}+\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(d_{\rm vob}^{-2}\bm{W}^{U}\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{W}^{U,T}r^{\rm in}_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}\frac{r_{\alpha,\nu}}{r_{\alpha}^{\rm in}}\bm{e}_{\nu}-\frac{1}{d_{\rm vob}}\mathbf{1}+\frac{1}{d_{\rm vob}}\bm{W}^{U}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\rm in}_{\alpha}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\rm in}_{\alpha}}\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{z_{i}}+\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha_{i}}\right)+\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(d_{\rm vob}^{-2}\bm{W}^{U}\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{W}^{U,T}r^{\rm in}_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}\frac{r_{\alpha,\nu}}{r_{\alpha}^{\rm in}}\bm{e}_{\nu}-\frac{1}{d_{\rm vob}}\mathbf{1}+\frac{1}{d_{\rm vob}}\bm{W}^{U}\left(\sum_{x\in\left(\mathcal{Z}\cap\mathcal{A}\right)}\frac{N_{\alpha,x}^{\rm in}}{N_{\alpha}^{\rm in}}\bm{W}^{E}_{x}+\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(d_{\rm vob}^{-2}\bm{W}^{U}\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}\right)\right).

Let
N\rightarrow\infty
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}}{dt}=

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{U,T}r^{\rm in}_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(y=\nu\mid\alpha\in\bm{X}\right)\bm{e}_{\nu}-\frac{1}{d_{\rm vob}}\mathbf{1}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{1}{d_{\rm vob}}\bm{W}^{U}\left(\sum_{x\in\left(\mathcal{Z}\cap\mathcal{A}\right)}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\left(x\in\bm{X}\mid\alpha\in\bm{X}\right)\bm{W}^{E}_{x}+\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(d_{\rm vob}^{-2}\bm{W}^{U}\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{U,T}r_{\alpha}^{\rm in}\left(\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{y}+\frac{1}{d_{\rm vob}}\bm{W}^{U}\bm{W}^{E}\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{\bm{X}}\right)+\bm{\eta},

where
\bm{\eta}=\bm{W}^{U,T}r_{\alpha}^{\rm in}\left(\frac{1}{d_{\rm vob}}\left(\bm{W}^{U}\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{1}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(d_{\rm vob}^{-2}\bm{W}^{U}\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}\right)\right)
contains the higher-order term and the data independent term.

D.4
Proof of Corollary
2

Proof.

Since the small initialization, we assume that the activation function can be approximated by the following form with the Weierstrass approximation theorem.

\displaystyle\sigma\left(\sum_{x\in\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{E}_{x}\right)=C_{0}+C_{1}\left(\sum_{x\in\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{x}^{E}\right)+C_{2}\left(\sum_{x\in\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{x}^{E}\right)^{\odot 2}+\bm{\epsilon}.

With the loss of the generalization, we assume that
C_{0}=0,C_{1}=1,C_{2}=\frac{1}{2}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}}{dt}=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}\frac{r_{\alpha,\nu}}{N_{\alpha,\nu}}\left(\bm{W}^{{U},T}\bm{e}_{\nu}\right)\odot\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\alpha,\nu}}\left(\mathbf{1}+\sum_{x\in\bm{X}_{(\alpha,\nu)}^{i}}\bm{W}^{E}_{\bm{X}_{(\alpha,\nu)}^{i}}\right)}_{\bm{J}^{y}}

\displaystyle-\underbrace{\frac{r^{\rm in}_{\alpha}}{N^{\rm in}_{\alpha}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\rm in}_{\alpha}}\left(\mathbf{1}+\sum_{x\in\bm{X}_{\alpha}^{i}}\bm{W}^{E}_{\bm{X}_{\alpha}^{i}}\right)\odot\left(\bm{W}^{{U},T}\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{i}\right)}_{\bm{J}^{p}}.

For the term
\bm{J}^{y}
we have

\displaystyle\bm{J}^{y}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}^{{U},T}\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}r_{\alpha,\nu}\bm{e}_{\nu}+\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}r_{\alpha,\nu}\left(\bm{W}^{{U},T}\bm{e}_{\nu}\right)\odot\left(\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2N_{\alpha,\nu}}\sum_{i=1}^{2N_{\alpha,\nu}}\bm{W}_{x^{i}_{(\alpha,\nu)}}^{E}\right).

Let
N\rightarrow\infty
, we have that

\displaystyle\bm{J}^{y}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}^{U,T}r^{\rm in}_{\alpha}\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{y}\odot\left(\mathbf{1}+\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}\right)+\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}{\rm diag}\left(\bm{W}^{U}_{\nu}\right)r_{\alpha,\nu}\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{P}\left(x^{\prime}\in\bm{X}\mid\alpha\in\bm{X},y=\nu\right)\bm{W}^{E}_{x^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}^{U,T}r^{\rm in}_{\alpha}\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{y}\odot\left(\mathbf{1}+\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}\right)+\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}{\rm diag}\left(r_{\alpha,\nu}\bm{W}^{U}_{\nu}\right)\bm{W}^{E}\left(\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{\bm{X}\mid y,T}\right)_{\nu}^{T}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}^{U,T}r^{\rm in}_{\alpha}\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{y}\odot\left(\mathbf{1}+\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}\right)+\mathbb{T}\cdot\left(\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{\bm{X}\mid y}\right)^{T},

where
\mathbb{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\rm vob}\times d_{\rm vob}}
,
\mathbb{T}_{:,:,\nu}=r_{\alpha,\nu}{\rm diag}\left(\bm{W}^{U}_{\nu}\right)\bm{W}^{E}
for
\nu\in\mathcal{V}
and 0 otherwise.

Similarly, for the term
\bm{J}^{p}
, we have that

\displaystyle\bm{J}^{p}=

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{U,T}r^{\rm in}_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{d_{\rm vob}}\mathbf{1}-\frac{1}{d_{\rm vob}}\bm{W}^{U}\left(\left(\bm{W}^{E}-{\rm diag}\left(\bm{W}^{U,T}\mathbf{1}\right)\right)\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{\bm{X}}+\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}\right)+\bm{\epsilon}\right),

where
\bm{\epsilon}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{d^{2}_{\rm vob}}\bm{W}^{U}\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}\right)
. Then we have that

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}}{dt}=\mathbb{T}\cdot\left(\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{\bm{X}\mid y}\right)^{T}+\bm{\eta}_{\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{y}}+\frac{1}{d_{\rm vob}}\bm{\eta}_{\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{\bm{X}}},

where
\bm{\eta}_{\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{y}}=\bm{W}^{U,T}r^{\rm in}_{\alpha}\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{y}\odot\left(\mathbf{1}+\bm{W}^{E}_{\alpha}\right)
,
\bm{\eta}_{\bm{\phi}_{\alpha}^{\bm{X}}}=d_{\rm vob}\bm{J}^{p}
.
∎

D.6
Proof of Corollary
4

Proof.

The next-token-prediction training loss could be formulated as

\ell^{i}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=1}^{L-1}{\rm CrossEntropy}\left(F_{\rm lan}\left(\bm{X}_{:t}\right);\bm{e}_{\bm{X}_{t+1}}\right).

So we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell^{i}}{\partial\bm{W}^{E}_{s}}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=1}^{L-1}\bm{W}^{U,T}\left(\bm{p}^{i}_{t}-\bm{e}_{\bm{X}^{i}_{t+1}}\right)\odot\left(\delta_{\bm{X}^{i}_{t}=s}\mathbf{1}+\tilde{F}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\bm{X}^{i}_{:t}\right)\right).

Furthermore, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{W}^{E}_{s}}{dt}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{NL}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{L-1}\bm{W}^{U,T}\left(\bm{e}_{\bm{X}^{i}_{t+1}}-\bm{p}_{t}^{i}\right)\odot\left(\delta_{\bm{X}^{i}_{t}=s}\mathbf{1}+\tilde{F}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\bm{X}^{i}_{:t}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{NL}\bm{W}^{U,T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{L-1}\delta_{\bm{X}^{i}_{t}=s}\bm{e}_{\bm{X}^{i}_{t+1}}+\frac{1}{NL}\bm{W}^{U,T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{L-1}\bm{e}_{\bm{X}^{i}_{t+1}}\odot\tilde{F}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\bm{X}^{i}_{:t}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{NL}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{L-1}\bm{W}^{U,T}\bm{p}_{t}^{i}\odot\left(\delta_{\bm{X}^{i}_{t}=s}\mathbf{1}+\tilde{F}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\bm{X}^{i}_{:t}\right)\right).

Since the small initialization, assuming that
||\bm{W}||_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}\left(d^{-\gamma}\right)
for any trainable parameter matrix
\bm{W}
, we have that
||\tilde{F}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\bm{X}^{i}_{:t}\right)||_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}\left(d^{1-2\gamma}\right)
in the initial stage. Let
N\rightarrow\infty
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{W}^{E}_{s}}{dt}=r^{\rm in}_{s}\bm{W}^{U,T}\left(\bm{\phi}^{\rm next}_{s}-\bm{\eta}^{E}\right),

where
\bm{\eta}^{E}=\sum_{t=1}^{L-1}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bm{p}\mid\bm{X}_{t}=s\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(d^{1-2\gamma}\bm{\phi}^{\rm next}_{s}\right)
.
Similarly, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{W}^{U}_{s}}{dt}=\frac{1}{NL}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{L-1}\left(\delta_{\bm{X}^{i}_{t+1}=s}-\bm{p}^{i,s}_{\bm{X}^{i}_{:t}}\right)\left(\bm{W}^{E,T}_{\bm{X}^{i}_{t}}+\tilde{F}\left(\bm{X}^{i}_{:t}\right)^{T}\right),

where
\bm{p}^{i,s}_{\bm{X}^{i}_{:t}}
means the
s
-th element of the output probability with input sequence
\bm{X}^{i}_{:t}
. Let
N\rightarrow\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{W}^{U}_{s}}{dt}=r^{\rm out}_{s}\left(\bm{W}^{E}\bm{\varphi}^{\rm pre}_{s}\right)^{T}+\bm{\eta}^{U},

where
\bm{\eta}^{U}=\sum_{t=1}^{L-1}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bm{p}^{s}_{\bm{X}_{:t}}\bm{W}^{E,T}_{\bm{X}_{t}}\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(r^{\rm out}_{s}d^{1-2\gamma}\left(\bm{W}^{E}\bm{\varphi}^{\rm pre}_{s}\right)^{T}\right)
.
∎
