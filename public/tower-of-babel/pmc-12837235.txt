Interpretable machine learning for differentiating SCA3 and MSA-C using gait and postural features from wearable sensors
Authors: Yuanyuan Xiao, Kailiang Luo, Yue Zhang, Wanli Zhang, QiKui Sun, Bingwei He, ShiRui Gan, Xinyuan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpinocerebellar ataxia type 3 (SCA3) and the cerebellar subtype of multiple system atrophy (MSA-C) both manifest with cerebellar ataxia and postural instability in the early stages, leading to considerable clinical overlap and frequent misdiagnosis or delayed intervention. Current diagnostic tools offer limited support when genetic confirmation is unavailable or neuroimaging findings are inconclusive. Clinical rating scales, while commonly used, rely heavily on subjective judgment and lack sensitivity to dynamic monitoring. Therefore, this study aims to develop an interpretable machine learning model based on gait and postural features acquired through wearable sensors, in order to assist in distinguishing SCA3 from MSA-C and to explore their potential as digital biomarkers for clinical application.MethodsThis study included 74 individuals diagnosed with SCA3, 43 individuals with multiple system atrophy of MSA-C, and 45 age-matched healthy controls (HC). All participants were in the mild to moderate stages of disease. Gait and postural control data were collected using a wearable smart insole system, and 98 digital features were extracted. Classification models were developed using XGBoost, LightGBM, random forest, and logistic regression. To enhance model interpretability, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were applied to quantify the contribution of each feature and to identify the most informative digital biomarkers.ResultAmong all models, LightGBM achieved the highest accuracy (95.91%) and ROC-AUC (0.9962). SHAP analysis identified Cadence CV and center of pressure (COP) features under eyes-closed (EC) conditions as top contributors across all models, supporting their potential as digital biomarkers. Gait and postural features provided complementary information. Even at mild to moderate disease stages, individuals with SCA3 and those with MSA-C showed distinguishable motor patterns. Specifically, individuals with SCA3 exhibited greater medio-lateral (ML) postural instability under visual deprivation, while those with MSA-C showed increased variability in gait rhythm, indicating distinct movement phenotypes.ConclusionThe integration of gait and postural stability features with interpretable machine learning models enables effective differentiation between SCA3 and MSA-C. It also facilitates the identification of clinically relevant digital biomarkers, supporting early diagnosis and personalized rehabilitation planning in cerebellar ataxia.Clinical trial number NTC04010214.Keywords: Spinocerebellar ataxia, Multiple system atrophy, Center of pressure, Gait analysis, Machine learning, SHAP

Introduction

Spinocerebellar Ataxia Type 3 (SCA3) is an autosomal dominant polyglutamine neurodegenerative disorder caused by expanded CAG trinucleotide repeats in the ATXN3 gene [1, 2]. Multiple System Atrophy of the cerebellar type (MSA-C), by contrast, is a sporadic neurodegenerative condition. Despite their distinct etiological origins, both disorders present with highly overlapping clinical phenotypes. Both diseases are characterized by progressive cerebellar ataxia, postural instability, and coordination deficits [3, 4]. The considerable symptom overlap—particularly in early disease stages—accurate clinical differentiation between SCA3 and MSA-C remains challenging. This diagnostic ambiguity is further compounded by the fact that cerebellar ataxia often constitutes the predominant or sole presenting feature in both diseases, potentially leading to misdiagnosis and delays in timely intervention [3].

Traditional diagnostic tools, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), provide limited adjunctive value in differential diagnosis. For example, the "hot cross bun sign" (HCBS), commonly observed in patients with MSA, typically appears in the middle to late stages of the disease [5]. However, HCBS can also be present in patients with SCA [6] ,thereby diminishing its diagnostic specificity. Similarly, cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers such as -synuclein remain under active investigation and have not yet been integrated into routine clinical workflows due to their uncertain diagnostic performance [7]. Current research has primarily focused on brain alterations in SCA3 and MSA-C, with most studies limited to comparisons between patient and healthy control groups. Consequently, clinical symptoms and MRI findings alone are insufficient for a definitive differential diagnosis. In practice, diagnosis still heavily relies on genetic testing and clinical expertise [3, 8, 9], both of which are resource-intensive and difficult to scale across larger populations.

Postural instability and gait disturbance are hallmark features of cerebellar dysfunction, often resulting in increased fall risk and reduced mobility. Among available assessment techniques, quantitative analysis of balance during quiet standing—particularly through center of pressure (COP) sway metrics—has proven to be a reliable indicator of cerebellar impairment [10, 11]. COP-derived metrics such as sway amplitude and velocity have shown strong associations with the extent of structural cerebellar degeneration [12]. For instance, Galvão et al. [13] utilized a force plate system to demonstrate that individuals with SCA3 exhibit significantly greater and faster postural sway than healthy controls (HC), particularly in the absence of visual input. Similarly, Panyakaew et al. [14] found that MSA patients showed broader COP trajectories under eyes-closed (EC) conditions compared to those with Parkinson’s disease. While these findings underscore the diagnostic value of postural metrics, the reliance on laboratory-based force platforms hinders their applicability in routine or remote clinical settings, where portability and accessibility are essential.

To enhance diagnostic precision and accessibility, recent studies have incorporated artificial intelligence and wearable data to support clinical decision-making. Ghayth et al. [15] used multiple wearable-sensor datasets combined with resampling and neural-network-based learning to classify disease severity in ataxic disorders, achieving high accuracy. Peng et al. [16] investigated the application of a machine learning model for predicting the age of onset of disease in the SCA3 population. The findings of the study indicated that the XGBoost model exhibited the optimal performance in predicting age. Ngo et al. [17] collated data from multiple wearable sensor devices and subjected it to machine learning analysis, thereby identifying the presence of ataxia in terms of severity and differentiating between various types of ataxia. Gupta et al. [18] collected exercise data from patients with SCAs and MSA-C using wearable devices and employed machine learning to construct a differentiation model and a disease severity assessment model. However, they did not differentiate or compare SCAs and MSA-C. Collectively, these studies suggest the potential of wearable-based metrics for severity assessment and disease differentiation, but direct comparisons between SCA3 and MSA-C remain scarce. Nevertheless, to date, no studies have systematically compared SCA3 and MSA-C using wearable gait and postural biomarkers, despite the urgent clinical need for reliable differential diagnosis.

To address this issue, the current study proposes a sensor-based intelligent diagnostic framework designed to differentiate between SCA3, MSA-C, and HC by utilizing gait and postural biomarkers, which were collected and preprocessed using a smart insole system equipped with pressure sensors and inertial measurement units (IMUs). A suite of machine learning algorithms, including LightGBM, XGBoost, and Random Forest (RF), was utilized to execute a three-class classification task, with performance evaluated through fivefold cross-validation. The performance of the models was assessed using metrics such as accuracy, F1-score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC). Among the tested models, LightGBM achieved the best performance, attaining the highest average accuracy (95.91%) and ROC-AUC (0.9962) across the folds. Furthermore, SHAP-based interpretability analysis revealed that the most influential features in the model were highly consistent with statistically significant parameters, reinforcing their interpretability and clinical relevance as potential biomarkers. This study not only provides a technological pathway for early assisted diagnosis of ataxia spectrum disorders, but also offers a viable framework for investigating individualized motor phenotype assessment based on wearable sensors, which holds significant potential for clinical dissemination.

Methods

Study design and participants

This research was conducted in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol, consent form, and associated documents received approval from the local ethics committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, Fuzhou, China (MRCTA, ECFAH of FMU [2022] 399), prior to the initiation of the study. Furthermore, the study conformed to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. An independent data safety monitoring board evaluated the safety and quality of the study. SCA3 patients were recruited from the Organization in South-East China for Cerebellar Ataxia Research (OSCCAR)(NTC04010214) in the Department of Neurology of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04010214).

Informed written consent was obtained from all participants at the time of enrollment. The primary cohort for this study was recruited through hospital appointments or as relatives of patients. A total of 74 patients with a molecular diagnosis of SCA3, 43 patients with MSA-C, and 45 HC were enrolled in the study. Enrollment of SCA3 patients was contingent upon the following inclusion criteria: (1) a confirmed genetic diagnosis of SCA3, (2) manifestation of ataxia symptoms, (3) voluntary consent to participate in the cohort study, (4) age of 18 years or older, and (5) a shoe size ranging from 36.5 to 44, which was required to ensure proper fitting of the insole-type wearable sensor system. Patients with MSA-C were enrolled using the following criteria: (1) <4 years MSA-C medical history, (2) independent walk (or with assistance), (3) >3 years life expectancy, (4) contraceptive measures for women of childbearing age. The exclusion criteria employed for the selection of SCA3 and MSA-C were as follows: (1) patients with medical history of stroke, encephalitis, and epilepsy, (2) patients with serious cognitive and behavioral disorders, or mental illness, (3) patients with severe medical illness (such as kidney failure, convulsions, stomach ulcers, liver disease) and uncontrolled high blood pressure or diabetes, (4) patients with head injury, neurosurgery, or mental issues within the head, (5) patients who took investigational products within 4 weeks prior to this enrollment, or who are pregnant or breastfeeding, (6) Patients with metallic particles in the eye, medical pumps like implanted cardiac pacemaker or neurostimulators, and surgical clips (above the shoulder line), etc. [19].

Objective data acquisition system

This study is based on an insole system for data acquisition and analysis of balance, in which the pressure sensor unit is wirelessly connected to a microcontroller STM32F103C8T6 via Bluetooth for data processing, and the inertial measurement unit includes a 6-axis inertial sensor (Shenzhen, China, Witte Intelligence, JY61) with dimensions of . A real-time display system based on the Visual Studio .NET software, specifically developed using C#.NET, was used to collect, display, and store the gait data. The smart insole exhibits exceptional sensitivity and accuracy (), robust stability (no signal alteration following a 1500-cycle test), rapid response time (), and a broad detection range ( to ). The detailed preparation process, operational principles, and sensing performance of the smart insole have been thoroughly documented in a previous study [20]. Furthermore, the insole’s thin, flexible, and lightweight design, coupled with its user-friendly and customizable features, makes it particularly suitable as a wearable device for research on gait abnormalities and balance dysfunction, without being constrained by specific scenarios. Before each recording session, all pressure sensors were calibrated to each participant’s body weight to ensure a consistent baseline and minimize drift. The IMU module was also zeroed prior to data collection. Plantar pressure and IMU data streams were synchronized in real time via a shared timestamp protocol, with temporal alignment verified by comparing event markers at the start and end of each trial. Differences in sampling frequency were corrected by linear interpolation to achieve precise temporal matching across modalities.

Feature parameter extraction

The procedure for extracting COP variables is illustrated in Fig. S1a. Initially, during the assessment, plantar force data were transmitted in real-time to the corresponding computational program via the smart insoles system. This system performed trimming and preprocessing to ensure uniformity in data shape and consistency in temporal spans. Following this, the preprocessed data, along with sensor positions, were synchronously input into the COP calculation model that predicted the participant’s real-time COP position and calculated associated variables (Fig. S1b). Detailed COP calculation flow is presented in Supplementally Note 1. Then COP sway area and other dynamic characteristics, including the total sway area of the COP, overall velocity, overall acceleration, and the velocity and acceleration of each foot in the medio-lateral (ML) and antero-posterior (AP) directions during the performance of balance tasks with eyes-closed(EC) and eyes-opened(EO), were analyzed. Detailed definitions of these variables and their corresponding calculation equations are presented in Table S1.

For gait analysis, plantar pressure and 3-axis IMU data were jointly processed to extract spatiotemporal features such as walking speed, stride length, cadence, stance phase, and double-support phase. Participants performed a 15-meter straight walking task at a self-selected pace on a fixed walkway, and walking distance was measured based on the predefined path length rather than sensor integration. The detailed gait-phase determination algorithm and corresponding flowchart are presented in Table S1 and Fig. S2 of the Supplementary Materials. This streamlined analytical framework enabled consistent and reproducible computation of postural and gait parameters across all participants.

Statistical analysis for the main cohort

Normality of each quantitative variable was assessed using both the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests, with the latter providing higher sensitivity for moderate sample sizes. For group comparisons among three independent diagnostic groups (SCA3, MSA-C, and HC), the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis H test was employed due to violations of normality assumptions. Post-hoc analysis was conducted using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction to determine specific group-wise differences.

For each parameter, boxplots with overlaid individual data points were generated to visualize distribution and significant differences. Parameters with statistically significant Kruskal-Wallis results (p < 0.05) were further evaluated for their discriminative performance using ROC analysis. Area under the curve (AUC) values were computed to quantify diagnostic accuracy between disease and control groups [21].

All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.11 (SciPy, scikit-posthocs, seaborn) and SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp, NY, USA). A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant, and a p-value of <0.01 was considered highly statistically significant [22].

Machine learning classification and interpretability

All classification experiments were conducted using a fivefold stratified cross-validation strategy, where the dataset was partitioned into five mutually exclusive folds with preserved class distributions. In each fold, feature standardization was performed using Z-score normalization based solely on the training fold statistics (mean and standard deviation) to prevent information leakage. Feature selection steps, including statistical filtering and AUC-based ranking, were also performed within each training fold to ensure independence from the validation data. Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was then applied exclusively to the training folds to address class imbalance by generating synthetic minority samples via nearest-neighbor interpolation. Model performance was evaluated in each fold using classification accuracy, macro-averaged F1 score, and ROC-AUC, where ROC curves were constructed through pairwise group comparisons (MSA-C vs. SCA3, MSA-C vs. HC, and SCA3 vs. HC). Feature contributions were further analyzed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values, calculated on the entire dataset using the best-performing model. SHAP-based interpretability techniques, similar to the approach used by Trabassi et al. [23], were applied to identify the most important features driving classification decisions. By using SHAP, we were able to gain insights into the relationship between gait and postural features and disease classifications, particularly in the context of cerebellar ataxia.

Statistical analysis and feature selection outcomes in the main cohort

Given the large number of extracted gait and postural features, parameters were categorized based on task conditions (gait vs. balance). From each category, representative parameters were selected for visualization and ROC-based analysis by integrating statistical significance, with diagnostic performance, measured by the AUC. To examine the distribution and intergroup differences of parameters among the three cohorts SCA3, MSA-C, and HC, boxplots with overlaid individual data points were constructed (Fig. S3). Out of a total 98 parameters, eight were selected for visualization based on the outcomes of the Kruskal–Wallis H test followed by Dunn’s test (Fig. 1). All displayed parameters showed statistically significant differences between diagnostic groups (p < 0.05), and the post hoc Dunn’s test revealed clear group separations. A comprehensive summary of the statistical analyses is provided in Table S2.

To evaluate the discriminative ability of gait features across different diagnostic categories, ROC curve analysis was performed on the top-ranked parameters. Figure 2 presents the ROC curves for three selected pairwise comparisons. The results demonstrated that SCA3 could be more readily distinguished from the other groups, with several features yielding AUC values exceeding 0.9. For instance, Toe-off Angle CV achieved an AUC of 0.944 in the SCA3 vs. HC comparison, and Cadence CV consistently ranked among the top-performing features across both group comparisons. In contrast, the differentiation between MSA-C and HC (Fig. 2b) proved to be more challenging, as evidenced by generally lower AUC values—for example, EC-R-ML Velocity yielded an AUC of 0.881.

Based on the results of the aforementioned statistical analyzes, this section provided an initial evaluation of the feature variables for subsequent training of the machine learning model. Parameters with nonsignificant group differences in the Kruskal-Wallis H test (p > 0.05) were excluded from consideration. To further refine the feature set, Dunn’s tests were examined. Features were prioritized if they demonstrated significant differences (p 0.05) in at least two out of three pairwise group comparisons, with particular in the clinically challenging comparison between SCA3 and MSA-C. The parameters that show weak differentiation, limited to the HC vs. MSA-C or HC vs. SCA3 contrasts were deprioritized. For example, features such as EC-ML Acceleration and EO-AP Acceleration were excluded due to their lack of statistical significance or inadequate pairwise group separation. Ultimately, the retained features were cross-validated against ROC-AUC rankings, with greater weight assigned to those achieving an AUC > 0.85 in at least one pairwise comparison. These thresholds were set to ensure robustness and discriminative reliability, where significance in of 3 Dunn’s post hoc tests reflects consistency across groups, and an ROC-AUC above 0.85 indicates excellent classification performance commonly accepted in biomedical studies.

Classification performance results

Following the completion of feature screening, a multiclassification model was developed to evaluate the diagnostic discriminative ability of the retained gait and postural control variables. The analysis focused on three distinct diagnostic categories: SCA3, MSA-C, and HC. These categories were modeled through the implementation of three integrated learning algorithms: XGBoost, LightGBM, and RF, respectively. Additionally, logistic regression was incorporated as a control baseline model. To address the issure of category imbalance and enhance the model’s robustness, a fivefold hierarchical cross-validation strategy was employed during training, incorporating SMOTE oversampling with Z-score normalization in each fold. Model hyperparameters were determined through empirical tuning based on cross-validation performance rather than default settings. Specifically, the learning rate, number of trees, and maximum tree depth were iteratively adjusted to achieve an optimal balance between convergence stability and generalization performance. Typical configurations included a small learning rate (0.005–0.1) with 1000–2000 trees for the boosting models (LightGBM and XGBoost), and approximately 2000 trees with a minimum leaf sample size of 2 for the Random Forest model. Final parameters were selected according to the average F1 score obtained from fivefold cross-validation, ensuring stability and fairness across all models.

The performance of each classifier, evaluated in terms of accuracy, precision, F1 score, and other metrics is shown in Table 2. All machine learning models outperform the baseline model across all metrics, achieving an average accuracy of 94.99%, a ROC-AUC of exceeding 0.99, and minimal cross-fold fluctuations. Considering the class imbalance among the three diagnostic groups, balanced accuracy was additionally computed for each model. Specifically, the recall for each class was calculated within every cross-validation fold, and the mean of these recall values was used to represent the balanced accuracy. The final results were reported as the average values across folds.

Weight assessment of features

To assess the relative contribution of each feature across various classifiers, we computed global SHAP feature importance rankings for the two ensemble-based models (Fig. 5). LightGBM and Random Forest were selected for interpretability analysis due to their high and stable performance, representing the boosting and bagging paradigms, respectively. Although XGBoost also performed well (ROC-AUC = 0.9906), its results were slightly less stable and conceptually similar to LightGBM; therefore, it was not included to avoid redundancy. The results indicated that Cadence CV and postural control features under EC conditions—such as EC-ML Velocity and EC-L ML Acceleration SD—consistently emerged as top contributors across the models. Notably, some variability in feature importance was observed among classifiers, and several of the highly weighted features aligned with the statistically significant parameters identified in "Classification performance results" section, reinforcing their discriminative relevance.

As shown in Fig. 6a, Cadence CV and several postural control features under EC conditions retain top-ranking positions, mirroring their prominence in the three-class model. However, comparative analysis reveal shifts in feature importance relative to Fig. 5: for instance, EC-L ML Velocity and EC-R-ML Velocity exhibit increased relevance in the binary context, whereas the importance of features such as Toe-out Angle and Pace diminished. These changes suggest that specific features may exhibit enhanced discriminative power when the classification scope is narrowed to SCA3 vs. MSA-C.

To further elucidate how individual feature values influence model output, we generated SHAP dependence plots for representative variables (Fig. 6b, c). In Fig. 6b, EC-ML Velocity exhibits an overall inverse relationship with SHAP values, with a scattered distribution in the mid-range, suggesting non-linear effects and local variability. Conversely, Cadence CV (Fig. 6c) demonstrates a more linear and monotonic pattern, with SHAP values increasing progressively alongside higher feature values. While formal regression-based quantification or effect-size estimation was beyond the current scope, these trends visually reflect monotonic and non-linear relationships consistent with physiological expectations. Future studies will aim to incorporate quantitative regression or non-linear modeling to further validate these observations. These plots illustrate the continuous influence of feature magnitude on model predictions and provide nuanced insight into the directional and magnitude-dependent behavior of key predictors.

Discussion

Key findings

This study systematically evaluated the motor performance of patients with SCA3 and MSA-C, as well as and HC, using wearable pressure sensors and IMUs during specific walking and standing tasks. Multiple machine learning models were employed to classify the three groups, with SHAP analysis introduced to enhance model interpretability. The LightGBM model demonstrated superior classification performance compared to other models. This superiority was partially attributed to metrics such as Cadence CV and postural balance metrics (e.g., Acceleration, Velocity) in the EC standing tasks, which were crucial for disease recognition. To further validate the robustness of the selected features, we examined their importance within the LightGBM framework, which showed high consistency with the statistically selected features and confirmed the stability of the final feature set.

Compared to the HC group, both SCA3 and MSA-C cohorts exhibited markedly reduced stride length, decreased cadence, and a prolonged stance phase, indicating impaired locomotor control. These observations were consistent with the findings of Schniepp et al. [24], who also reported gait parameter variability as a significant marker of cerebellar dysfunction. However, our study extends this by showing that these gait changes are not only associated with motor deficits but also serve as reliable diagnostic markers when combined with posture balance features, which was not the focus of Schniepp’s work. During static balance assessments, particularly under EC conditions, patients exhibited significantly elevated COP acceleration and sway area compared to HC—with MSA-C individuals exhibiting the most pronounced deviations. This pattern implied an enhanced reliance on visual input for postural regulation, especially within the MSA-C group. These findings were in agreement with those of Panyakaew et al. [14], who highlighted visual deprivation-induced postural destabilization as a hallmark kinematic feature of MSA, distinguishing it from other parkinsonian syndromes.

Furthermore, our SHAP-based analysis consistently identified Cadence CV as one of the most impactful features across all classification models. Its pronounced variability suggests considerable instability in gait rhythmicity, reinforcing the importance of temporal gait regulation in the pathophysiology of cerebellar ataxia. SHAP-based interpretability analyses revealed that Cadence CV and postural balance metrics under EC conditions—such as EC-R-ML Velocity and EC-L-ML Acceleration standard deviation—consistently emerged as top-ranking features across all three classifiers. Given the relatively small dataset and the high ROC-AUC values observed in this study, the potential risk of overfitting remains a consideration, even with the use of cross-validation. However, the fivefold stratified cross-validation procedure helped mitigate this risk by evaluating the model’s performance across multiple data splits. Furthermore, SHAP-based interpretability analyses demonstrated that feature importance rankings were consistently stable across all cross-validation folds, suggesting that the model’s predictions are robust and not overly reliant on any particular fold. This consistency across folds further supports the model’s reliability and its potential for generalizability to other datasets.

Comparison with other interpretable machine learning techniques, while SHAP is a powerful tool for feature attribution due to its ability to provide consistent and locally accurate explanations, other techniques such as LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) and permutation importance offer alternative perspectives on model interpretability. LIME generates local surrogate models to explain individual predictions, but it can suffer from instability when the model is highly non-linear. In contrast, permutation importance evaluates feature importance by measuring the change in model performance after randomly permuting feature values. However, it does not account for interactions between features as effectively as SHAP. The key advantage of SHAP over these methods is its ability to provide a unified framework that guarantees consistency in feature attributions, ensuring that features with higher contributions are ranked higher across all instances.

This study advanced the existing research by incorporating static balance tasks into the feature set and employing SHAP-based interpretability to improve model transparency. Unlike the work of Ghayth et al. [15], which utilized an ANN-MLP for binary classification between SCA and HC without considering static postural features, and achieved an accuracy of 93.81%, our framework integrates postural balance parameters under a more challenging three-class classification setting. The proposed LightGBM model attained an accuracy of 95.91% and a ROC-AUC of 0.9962, demonstrating that the inclusion of postural metrics substantially enhances the discriminative power of the classification system. While Gupta et al. [18] utilized deep neural networks to process continuous gait signals with automated feature extraction and modeling, our study adopts a theory-driven approach grounded in established principles of kinematics and postural control. The features selected within our framework possess clear biomechanical interpretations and clinical relevance, thereby facilitating a more profound understanding of disease phenotypes and enhancing the translational potential of the classification outcomes.

Importantly, the top-ranking features identified by SHAP encompassed both gait-derived and postural balance metrics, highlighting that the classifiers did not rely on a singular motor domain but instead leveraged complementary information from both dynamic and static modalities. Gait-related parameters primarily reflect temporal-spatial control during locomotion, whereas balance-related features capture the neuromechanical responses to sensory perturbations—particularly under visual deprivation. This multimodal integration enhances the robustness and sensitivity of the models in discerning distinct phenotypic manifestations of cerebellar ataxia.

Compared to the multiclass classification setting, SHAP results from the binary classification task revealed notable differences in feature importance rankings. Postural balance features in the ML direction remained dominant, while gait-related features such as Stride Time exhibited marked declines in ranking. Although Cadence CV retained significant importance, it no longer ranked as the top contributing variable. This discrepancy is not contradictory but rather reflects the .inherent differences in SHAP’s explanation mechanisms between multiclass and binary classification tasks. Differences in SHAP feature rankings between the multiclass and binary classification tasks reflect the redistribution of feature contributions across decision boundaries. This shift arises from the distinct optimization objectives and attribution schemes of each task. The mathematical details of SHAP decomposition and the generalized SHAP framework are provided in Supplementary Note 2.

Furthermore, the statistical analyses presented in "Classification performance results" and Weight assessment of features sections validate the observed redistribution of SHAP rankings. While certain gait features demonstrated overall significance in the Kruskal-Wallis H test, their between-group differences—particularly between SCA3 and MSA-C—were relatively limited, explaining their diminished importance in the binary task. In contrast, postural balance features, especially under EC conditions, exhibited stronger discriminative capacity between these two groups, thus maintaining high model attribution in the binary classification context.

This is further illustrated in Fig. 6b, c, where SCA3 patients exhibited pronounced instability in ML sway under visual deprivation, whereas MSA-C patients displayed more irregular gait rhythmicity and elevated cadence variability. These observations are consistent with findings by Liu et al. [25] , who reported that postural control deficits in SCA3 are especially pronounced along the ML axis in the absence of visual input. The maintenance of postural equilibrium relies on the integrated functioning of proprioceptive, vestibular, and visual sensory inputs. Damage to the cerebellum frequently impairs proprioceptive pathways, thereby compromising sensorimotor integration [26, 27]. Although both SCA3 and MSA-C fall under the spectrum of cerebellar ataxias, they exhibit distinct neuropathological profiles that may account for the observed phenotypic divergence. SCA3 predominantly involves the cerebellar vermis and dorsal spinal cord columns, regions critically implicated in mediolateral (ML) postural adjustments. In this context, the vermis is involved in regulating the body’s lateral stability, and its degeneration leads to a loss of control over ML sway, resulting in increased instability when standing or walking. In contrast, MSA-C more frequently affects the cerebellar hemispheres and dentate nuclei, which are essential for regulating gait rhythm and temporal coordination. The dentate nuclei play a critical role in fine-tuning gait cadence and stride length; thus, degeneration in this area causes rhythmic disturbances, reflected as a decreased cadence and increased variability. These anatomical differences may underlie the contrasting motor control deficits observed in each subtype.

Clinical implications

Clinically, high values of Cadence CV indicate greater instability in gait rhythm, suggesting early-stage cerebellar dysfunction, particularly in MSA-C patients. High values of EC-L ML Acceleration SD reflect increased variability in postural control, indicative of impaired multisensory integration, which is particularly evident in SCA3 patients who may show greater reliance on visual input for balance. These indicators are valuable for clinicians to assess the severity of motor deficits and tailor rehabilitation strategies accordingly. The strong coherence between the model outputs, statistical significance, and clinical pathophysiology highlights the diagnostic relevance of these features. Notably, Cadence CV and EC-induced variations in COP dynamics reflect key aspects of gait instability and postural control deficits, which are common in cerebellar ataxia, with differences observed between SCA3 and MSA-C.

Furthermore, Shah et al. [28] identified gait variability as one of the most discriminative features in SCA patients, with particular emphasis on double support duration as a key parameter. In our study, Cadence CV consistently emerged as one of the top predictors across all classification models, highlighting the diagnostic value of rhythm-related gait variability in pathological states. Although Double Support did not attain the same level of model-based importance as Cadence CV, it still demonstrated statistical significance (p < 0.05), thereby corroborating Shah’s findings regarding the utility of gait variability features in the classification of spinocerebellar ataxia.

The compensatory role of visual input in postural control, particularly under sensory deprivation conditions, has been well-documented. Vision provides the central nervous system with crucial spatial orientation cues relative to the external environment, thereby compensating for impaired somatosensory feedback [29]. This mechanism explains the marked postural instability observed in patients under eyes-closed conditions. Several studies have explored the potential of visual feedback interventions to improve balance control [30]. Notably, the top-ranking postural features identified in our models—such as EC-ML Velocity and EC-L ML Acceleration SD—are predominantly concentrated along the ML axis. This suggests that ML stability may serve as a more sensitive discriminator of ataxia subtypes than AP metrics. In support of this mechanism, Bao et al. [31] demonstrated that ML postural parameters significantly differentiated MSA from Parkinson’s disease, whereas AP measures did not exhibit such discriminative power. subtype.

Future directions and limitations

Notably, the discriminative power of certain gait-related features is relatively limited in the comparison between MSA-C patients and HC (Fig. 2b), which may be partially attributed to sampling characteristics. In clinical practice, the rapid disease progression of MSA-C often results in a scarcity of patients at early to moderate stages who retain sufficient ambulatory function for gait assessment. Consequently, the included MSA-C cohort may represent a less typical presentation, thereby diminishing the apparent gait-related differences relative to HC. This potential sampling bias could partly account for the reduced statistical significance and lower classification performance observed in ROC analysis for this group comparison.

Despite its contributions, the present study is subject to several limitations. Firstly, the relatively modest sample size, particularly the under representation of MSA-C patients—may constrain the generalizability of the classification models. Secondly, although SHAP analysis enhances model interpretability, tree-based algorithms inherently rely on internal metrics such as information gain and split frequency, which may not readily translate into clinician-friendly interpretations at the individual-patient level. Consequently, a certain degree of opacity remains in model decision-making. Additionally, the postural assessment did not systematically account for potential confounders, including participants’ emotional state, lifestyle factors, or fatigue, all of which could affect balance performance. Future research should aim to establish larger, multicenter prospective cohorts, with particular emphasis on expanding the representation of the MSA-C population, to improve the diversity and robustness of model training. Longitudinal tracking of gait and postural parameters may provide insights into their sensitivity to disease progression and support early detection and treatment evaluation. To enhance clinical applicability, future efforts should aim to streamline current multi-parameter models into more practical decision-support tools, and to develop intuitive human–machine interfaces that facilitate real-time assessment and monitoring by neurologists and rehabilitation professionals.

Conclusion

This study represents the first integration of SHAP-based interpretability with wearable gait and postural balance features to differentiate between SCA3 and MSA-C. Our findings highlight that spatiotemporal gait and postural metrics provide consistent and complementary discriminative power across multiple machine learning models. Among them, Cadence CV and COP velocity emerged as robust digital biomarkers, revealing subtype-specific motor control deficits. By combining model performance with transparent interpretability, this work bridges the gap between algorithmic accuracy and clinical insight.

The proposed framework has clear translational potential for developing bedside diagnostic tools and remote monitoring platforms based on wearable data, enabling early screening and individualized rehabilitation assessment in ataxia patients. Beyond this application, the approach establishes a scalable paradigm for multimodal modeling and feature optimization in broader neurodegenerative disease research.
