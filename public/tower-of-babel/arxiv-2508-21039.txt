Far from equilibrium attractors in phase space
Michał Spaliński

Abstract
We clarify the connection between attractor solutions known from studies of Bjorken flow in conformal models of relativistic fluid dynamics and the more general description of attractors as submanifolds in phase space. We show how to determine sections of the attractor on slices of constant proper time, and point out that in conformal models one may choose a parametrisation of the extended phase space such that the intersection of the attractor with a slice of constant proper time is the same on all slices. In consequence, it is possible to project these intersections and one finds that the result reproduces the attractor solution. This projection allows for an expressive picture of hydrodynamisation, but the attractor can be equally well described without taking advantage of this convenience. An immediate implication of these observations is that conformal symmetry does not play a significant role in the emergence of early-time attraction. We illustrate this by describing the hydrodynamic attractor in a simple model where conformal symmetry is broken by the equation of state. Our findings can be applied to studies of hydrodynamic attractors in more general situations, also beyond the context of quark-gluon plasma dynamics.



I
Introduction

Hydrodynamic attractors act as a template for near-equilibrium behaviour,
capturing universal features which can be parametrised using
the gradient expansion. It has however been noted that in some model systems
these attractors extend into the far from equilibrium regime, providing a
fascinating opportunity to study emergent universality in nonequilibrium
phenomena. In this paper we provide an approach to exploring attractors
across diverse fields, such as high energy physics, cold atom systems and
cosmology.

The most prominent example of a far from equilibrium attractor appears in the
context of quark-gluon plasma (QGP) dynamics, where Mueller-Israel-Stewart
theory
Muller:1967zza
;
Israel:1979wp
;
Israel:1976tn
(MIS) is a key element
of the phenomenological description of heavy ion
collisions
Busza:2018rrf
. These simulations involve flows well outside
the domain of applicability of hydrodynamics at early stages, when the
energy-momentum tensor is very anisotropic. Their success in describing
experimental data suggests a degree of universality emerging while the system is
still very far from equilibrium
Heller:2015dha
, so that this universality
cannot be attributed to hydrodynamics. A key issue is the origin of this
attractor and the
question why does MIS theory seem to be able to mimic it, given the hugely more
complex dynamics of QCD. Within kinetic theory
one can convincingly argue that the early-time regime is characterised by
free-streaming, while the emergence of hydrodynamic behaviour at late times
signals the dominance of interactions
Blaizot:2017ucy
;
Blaizot:2019scw
.
More generally, it is clear that the answer lies in the assumptions which define
Bjorken flow
Bjorken:1982qr
— longitudinal boost invariance and
homogeneity in the transverse plane. Of these, the crucial dynamical assumption
is boost invariance, which — as emphasised by Bjorken — is a characteristic
property of particle production in QCD at high energies. When boost invariance
is imposed on MIS theory, it receives this key dynamical information which is
instrumental in the emergence of early-time attraction. The importance of boost
invariance in connection with early-time attraction was highlighted in the
recent paper
Chen:2024pez
where it was found that breaking
boost-invariance in the initial conditions tends to wash out the early-time
attraction — this is in contrast to relaxing the condition of transverse
homogeneity
An:2023yfq
(see also
Refs.
Kurkela:2019set
;
Ambrus:2021sjg
;
Ambrus:2021fej
;
Ambrus:2022koq
).

Very different examples of hydrodynamic attractors were recently described in
the context of
nonrelativistic physics, where they were identified in a Fermi gas
close to unitarity with a time-dependent scattering
length
Fujii:2024yce
;
Mazeliauskas:2025jyi
;
Heller:2025yxm
. In these
cases, early-time attraction may appear because the system under
consideration is externally driven in a specific manner. At a mathematical
level, this drive provides a mechanism similar to the boost invariance of
particle production in QCD.
It thus becomes timely and important to develop methods of identifying
attractors which can be effective in diverse settings.
Experimental programs in the field of cold atomic
gases may provide direct access to hydrodynamic attractors and in this
way they might also indirectly contribute to the understanding of QGP dynamics.

The existence of a hydrodynamic attractor far from equilibrium was originally
put forward in Ref.
Heller:2015dha
in the context of conformal MIS theory
and has been developed in various ways and generalised to other settings (for
reviews see
Refs.
Florkowski:2017olj
;
Soloviev:2021lhs
;
Jankowski:2023fdz
;
Spalinski:2022cgj
).
These studies took advantage of technical simplifications due to conformal
symmetry in an essential way. To explore attractors in the wide range of
circumstances where they may occur, one cannot
rely on such simplifications.
A more general approach is to recognise that attractors should be viewed as
submanifolds of phase space, as was put forth in Ref.
Heller:2020anv
,
along with an attempt to define the attractor purely locally, without appealing
to late or early time limits. This approach is very general, but it invites
questions about its relation to the idea of an attractor solution. Moreover,
the known examples where far from equilibrium attraction occurs are
characterised by potentially singular behaviour at early times, and the
attractor can be identified mathematically by imposing regularity conditions on
the nonequilibrium degrees of freedom. In view of this, here we extend and
elaborate on the approach used in Ref.
An:2023yfq
, where the attractor
surface in phase space is defined using regularity conditions at asymptotically
small times. The basic question addressed in this paper is the relationship
between the phase space point of view and the concept of the attractor solution
in the sense of Ref.
Heller:2015dha
. Clarifying this relationship leads
to a cohesive and effective framework allowing the direct study of attractors in
general settings.

Our point of departure is the observation that a natural description of the
attractor can be obtained by means of its sections on constant proper time
slices. In Section
II
below it will be shown that they can easily be
calculated; in particular, in the hydrodynamic regime these curves can be
obtained analytically in parametric form — a simple fact which has so far
eluded attention. The key point is that in general, sections of the attractor
evolve from slice to slice; however in the special case of conformal models it
is possible to choose a parametrisation of phase space such that these sections
are the same on all slices. This allows one to project the sections, and the
result coincides with the original concept of an attractor solution put forward
in Ref.
Heller:2015dha
. At the same time, this simplification turns out
to be just an economy of description which does not play a fundamental role in
the emergence of early-time attraction. The choice of variables used to
parametrise phase space is purely a matter of convenience, and the attractor can
be studied equally well in any parameterisation. An obvious but important
corollary is that it is completely straightforward to look for hydrodynamic
attractors in nonconformal models using this approach, even though such models
do not admit a parameterisation which leads to sections of the attractor which
would be the same on each time slice. As an illustration, in
Section
III
we will consider MIS theory with an equation of state
which breaks conformal symmetry. This model cannot be treated effectively using
the original approach of Ref.
Heller:2015dha
, but using the regularity
conditions at early time the attractor is easily seen to emerge in a
qualitatively similar way to the conformal case.

II
Conformal MIS theory

To introduce our approach, in this Section we reconsider the example of
conformal MIS theory; we describe how to identify the attractor locus in the
phase space of solutions and explain how its sections at constant proper time
are related to the concept of an attractor solution introduced in
Ref.
Heller:2015dha
.

II.1
The attractor locus on phase space slices

MIS theory is expressed in terms of the classical fields
{\cal E}
(the energy
density),
u^{\mu}
(the flow velocity) and
\pi^{\mu\nu}
(the shear-stress
tensor). They satisfy the following set of partial
differential equations

\displaystyle u\cdot\nabla{\cal E}

\displaystyle=-({\cal E}+p)\nabla\cdot u+u^{\nu}\nabla^{\mu}\pi_{\mu\nu},

(1a)

\displaystyle({\cal E}+p)u\cdot\nabla u_{\mu}

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{\mu\nu}\nabla^{\nu}p-\Delta_{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\lambda}\pi^{\nu\lambda},

(1b)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mu\alpha}\Delta_{\nu\beta}u\cdot\nabla\pi^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=-\left(1+\frac{4}{3}\tau_{\pi}\nabla\cdot u\right)\pi_{\mu\nu}-2\eta\sigma_{\mu\nu},

(1c)

where
\nabla_{\mu}
is the covariant derivative,
\Delta_{\mu\nu}\equiv g_{\mu\nu}+u_{\mu}u_{\nu}
is the transverse projector,

\sigma_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\mu\alpha}\Delta_{\nu\beta}(\nabla^{\alpha}u^{\beta}+\nabla^{\beta}u^{\alpha}-\frac{2}{3}\Delta^{\alpha\beta}\nabla\cdot u)
is
the shear tensor,
\eta
is the shear viscosity and
\tau_{\pi}
is the
relaxation time for
\pi_{\mu\nu}
. Throughout this paper we will set the bulk
viscosity as well as the bulk pressure to zero. In this Section we will also
assume an equation of state and transport coefficients dictated by conformal
invariance:

{\cal E}=3p=a\,T^{4},\qquad\eta=\frac{4}{3}a\,C_{\eta}T^{3},\qquad\tau_{\pi}=C_{\tau}T^{-1},

(2)

where
C_{\eta}
,
C_{\tau}
and
a
are dimensionless constants (see
e.g.
Florkowski:2017olj
). For the case of Bjorken flow, the
energy-momentum tensor of the conformal MIS theory can be parametrised in terms
of only two functions of proper time
\tau\equiv\sqrt{t^{2}-z^{2}}
: the effective
temperature
T(\tau)
, and the pressure anisotropy
{\cal A}(\tau)
(for details
see, e.g.
Spalinski:2022cgj
;
Jankowski:2023fdz
),
t
is the lab frame time and
z
is the
coordinate along the collision axis. Eqs. (
1
) then reduce
to
An:2023yfq

\displaystyle\tau\partial_{\tau}\ln T(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{18}{\cal A}(\tau),

(3a)

\displaystyle\tau\partial_{\tau}{\cal A}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{8C_{\eta}}{C_{\tau}}-\frac{\tau T(\tau)}{C_{\tau}}{\cal A}(\tau)-\frac{2}{9}{\cal A}^{2}(\tau).

(3b)

Eqs. (
3
) are not autonomous: in addition to
T,{\cal A}
one also needs the
value of
\tau
to fully describe the state at a given instant. Formally, such
nonautonomous systems can be equivalently presented in an autonomous form at the
price of introducing an evolution parameter, say
s
, and an additional
independent variable (see e.g.
Guckenheimer2002
). In the case of
Eq. (
3
),
\tau
is replaced by
\tau(s)
with the additional equation of
motion
\dot{\tau}=1
. The resulting phase space is effectively

3
-dimensional, parametrised by
(T,{\cal A},\tau)
, and solutions can be viewed
as given parametrically as functions of
s
. As noted in
Ref.
Heller:2020anv
, the existence of an attractor is expressed by
generic solutions congregating along a specific submanifold of this extended
phase space.

In what follows, it will be most convenient to think of this extended phase
space as a set slices at constant values of the proper time. On each such slice
at some specified value
\tau
, a solution of Eq. (
3
) is the point

(T(\tau),{\cal A}(\tau))
. The attractor hypersurface can therefore be described by
its sections on the constant proper time slices, which in this case are curves
in the
(T,{\cal A})
planes at constant values of
\tau
.

Sections of the attractor are simplest to characterise in the late-time,
hydrodynamic regime where approximate asymptotic solutions are provided by the
gradient expansion. Supposing that initial conditions are set at
\tau=\tau_{0}
, Eqs. (
3
) imply the
well-known asymptotic behaviour of generic solutions at proper times
\tau\gg\tau_{0}

T(\tau)\sim\Lambda(\Lambda\tau)^{-\frac{1}{3}}\,,\qquad\quad{\cal A}(\tau)\sim 8C_{\eta}(\Lambda\tau)^{-\frac{2}{3}}

(4)

where
\Lambda
is an integration constant which depends on the initial
conditions.
We propose to view these equations at a fixed value of
\tau
as a parametric
representation of a
curve parametrised by the value of

\Lambda
. This
implies that on a proper time slice at a sufficiently large value of
\tau\gg\tau_{0}
, generic solutions lie asymptotically close to this curve, which is a
section of the hydrodynamic attractor on that particular time slice. Obviously,
the shape of this curve changes from slice to slice.

The value of
\Lambda
determines which point on the attractor section
corresponds to a specific initial condition. At such large times only a part of
the initial data is still accessible through the value of
\Lambda
. The
remaining initial data enters only through exponentially-suppressed transseries
contributions
Heller:2015dha
. While Eq. (
4
) displays only the
leading asymptotic behaviour, subleading terms are easily calculated and could
be included. The resulting curves provide a parametric representation of the
attractor sections in the late time regime. The accuracy of this representation
improves with the number of terms included up to some optimal number, as
expected for an asymptotic series with vanishing radius of
convergence
Heller:2015dha
;
Heller:2021oxl
.

The appearance of a hydrodynamic attractor at asymptotically late times is
hardly surprising for a system which eventually approaches equilibrium. It is
however not clear in general how early this attractor begins to govern the
dynamics. In boost-invariant systems such as MIS theory the attractor appears
due to the presence of special solutions for which the pressure anisotropy is
regular at early times. This is however not always clear, as is the case with
supersymmetric Yang-Mills
theory
Romatschke:2017vte
;
Spalinski:2017mel
;
Kurkela:2019set
. In the model
under consideration it is however straightforward to examine the asymptotic
behaviour of solutions of Eq. (
3
) at early time. One then finds that
there are two solutions for
{\cal A}(\tau)
regular at
\tau=0
,
with
An:2023yfq

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}{\cal A}(\epsilon)\equiv{\cal A}_{\pm}=\pm 6\sqrt{\frac{C_{\eta}}{C_{\tau}}}

(5)

Imposing the initial condition for the pressure anisotropy corresponding to the
upper sign and allowing arbitrary initial conditions for
T(\epsilon)
one
obtains a family of solutions which span a hypersurface in phase space — this
is the attractor.

Figure 1:

The evolution of the attractor (the red curve) on a set of constant proper
time slices in the extended phase space parametrised by
(T,{\cal A},\tau)
. At
the initial time (taken as
0.1
fm) the initial conditions for the solutions
plotted as blue dots are set uniformly in a range of initial temperatures and
pressure anisotropies. At
\tau=0.5
fm all the solutions are in the vicinity
of the attractor, with pressure anisotropies ranging from around
1
to almost

3
. Subsequent evolution follows the (evolving) attractor. The range of

In practice, one can determine this locus numerically by imposing the initial
condition
{\cal A}_{+}
along with some set of initial conditions for
T(\epsilon)

spanning a range of initial temperatures. This way a set of solution is
obtained, which thread the attractor surface in phase space. One can then
determine the attractor section on any slice by evaluating these solutions on
that slice and interpolating – this is how the red curves in Fig.
1

were determined. It is plain to see from these plots that the attractor is
evolving from slice to slice. Furthermore, this figure clearly demonstrates the
distinct three stages of evolution noted in
Heller:2020anv
(see also
Ref.
An:2023yfq
). The first stage is a rapid approach to the
nonequilibrium part of the attractor which can be attributed to the longitudinal
expansion; the second stage describes descent onto the attractor and is
characterised by the decay of exponentially damped nonhydrodynamic degrees of
freedom; and then the third, hydrodynamic stage, encompassing evolution along
the attractor which is captured by the gradient expansion. An important point,
emphasised in Ref.
An:2023yfq
, is that in the context of heavy ion
collisions freezeout occurs while the exponential corrections are still not
negligible. In physical problems where this happens, detection of such
transient, nonhydrodynamic contributions opens the window to physics beyond the
hydrodynamic regime and would allow the detection of scales characterising the
universal constraints set by causality
Heller:2022ejw
;
Heller:2023jtd
.

II.2
Relation to earlier work and attractor solutions

The parametrisation of phase space used above is not distinguished in any way:
one could, for instance, use
(T,T^{\prime},\tau)
as in Ref.
Heller:2020anv

and set the appropriate initial condition for
T^{\prime}\equiv\partial_{\tau}T
to determine
the attractor surface. However, in order to make contact with earlier work of
attractors in conformal models, in this Section we will use the dimensionless
variable
w\equiv\tau T
instead of
T
. In the hydrodynamic regime the
parametric equations of the attractor slice
at some specific value of
\tau
given in Eq. (
4
) become

w(\tau)\sim(\Lambda\tau)^{-\frac{1}{3}}\,,\qquad\quad{\cal A}(\tau)\sim 8C_{\eta}(\Lambda\tau)^{-\frac{2}{3}}

(6)

This curve is just the well known Navier-Stokes result
{\cal A}\sim 8C_{\eta}/w
(see
e.g. Ref.
Florkowski:2017olj
), expressed in a parametric form with

\Lambda
playing the role of a parameter along the curve. In conformal models

\Lambda
always enters in the combination
\Lambda\tau
, so when the phase
space in parametrised using
(w,{\cal A},\tau)
, the attractor curve is
the
same across all slices
, because a change in time can be undone by change in

\Lambda
. This is illustrated in Fig.
2
, which plots exactly the same
data as Fig.
1
, but using the
(w,{\cal A},\tau)
parameterisation. The
attractor sections are identical on each slice, so they may be projected to the

(w,{\cal A})
plane — in this parametrisation one can thus treat
w
as the
parameter along the curve instead of
\Lambda
.

, but with the

(w,{\cal A},\tau)
. Note that in this

The fact that the above situation comes about is related to the observation that
upon introducing the dimensionless variable
w
, Eqs. (
3
) can be cast
into the form
Jankowski:2020itt

\displaystyle C_{\tau\Pi}\left(1+\frac{{\cal A}}{12}\right){\cal A}^{\prime}+\frac{C_{\tau\Pi}}{3w}{\cal A}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{8C_{\eta}}{w}-{\cal A}\right),

(7a)

\displaystyle\frac{d\log T}{d\log w}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\cal A}-\ 6}{{\cal A}+12}

(7b)

In various incarnations, Eq. (
7a
) (or its analogues) has been the basis
for most studies of attractors (references can be found in the
reviews
Florkowski:2017olj
;
Soloviev:2021lhs
;
Spalinski:2022cgj
;
Jankowski:2023fdz
).
The point is that this equation can be analysed independently of the conservation equation,
Eq. (
7b
), which can be solved once a solution to Eq. (
7a
) is obtained.
Eq. (
7a
) has two regular solutions, one of which acts as an attractor. This
attractor solution is precisely the section of the phase space attractor in the

(w,{\cal A},\tau)
parameterisation of phase space. Note also that Eq. (
7a
)
invites the interpretation of
w
as an evolution parameter, a point of view
which has been adopted in most attractor literature. This approach has been very
fruitful, but its benefits do not generalise beyond conformal dynamics. In
general the pressure anisotropy is not a function of
w
alone, and so equations
such as Eqs. (
7
), where Eq. (
7a
) is decoupled, do not generalise
beyond conformal models.

For future application the crucial point is that the attractor section does not
need to be the same on each time slice, and in general is not. Even in
conformal cases, in a generic parametrisation such as the one used in the
previous subsection, the attractor section will depend on the slice — as
clearly seen in Fig.
1
. Thus,
(w,{\cal A},\tau)
is just one possible
parametrisation of the extended phase space of solutions of Eq. (
3
).
This choice can be convenient from the point of view of making contact with
earlier work, but we can just as well use other variables.

III
A nonconformal model

The results presented in the previous Section imply that simplifications
following from conformal symmetry are not essential for the study of
hydrodynamic attractors, and that in the phase space setting, nonconformal
models can be addressed in exactly the same manner as conformal models. To make
this clear, this Section introduces a simple nonconformal model of MIS theory
which can be analysed in full analogy with the conformal case. The main goal
is to provide an illustrative example, so this model does not aim to
capture essential features of QGP even at temperatures above the confinement
transition. One effect which will be neglected is the appearance of bulk
viscosity, which is known to be very small at high temperature and it rises
sharply only close to the transition temperature.
This simplification will be taken further by neglecting bulk pressure entirely,
which leads to the technical convenience that the extended phase space remains

3
-dimensional so it is very easy to compare with conformal MIS theory. With
this assumption, a straightforward way to break conformal symmetry is
through the equation of state.
While there exist excellent parametrisations of the QCD equation of state
obtained through lattice computations (see e.g. Ref.
HotQCD:2014kol
),
the goal here is to find a form which would capture the physics for
T>T_{c}
at a
qualitative level but be simple enough to allow some analytic calculations. We
will choose an equation of state which is conformal at high temperature but
introduces a mass scale, denoted by
m
, which parametrises the departure from
conformality. The restoration of conformal symmetry at high temperature (which
also implies the vanishing of bulk pressure) suggests that the early-time
attraction should survive the symmetry breaking.

III.1
The equation of state

The model equation of state we adopt reads:

p=\frac{a}{3}\frac{T^{4}}{1+\frac{m^{2}}{T^{2}}}

(8)

where
m
is a constant parameter. At
T\gg m
one recovers the conformal result
p\sim a/3T^{4}
.
Standard thermodynamic relations imply that
the entropy density
s
and energy density
{\cal E}
are given by

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\frac{4a}{3}T^{3}\frac{\left(1+\frac{3m^{2}}{2T^{2}}\right)}{\left(1+\frac{m^{2}}{T^{2}}\right)^{2}}

(9a)

\displaystyle{\cal E}

\displaystyle=aT^{4}\frac{\left(1+\frac{5m^{2}}{3T^{2}}\right)}{\left(1+\frac{m^{2}}{T^{2}}\right)^{2}}

(9b)

The mass scale
m
happens to coincide with the position of the maximum of the normalised trace anomaly

\Delta(T)\equiv\frac{{\cal E}-3p}{T^{4}}=\frac{2a}{3}\frac{m^{2}T^{2}}{\left(m^{2}+T^{2}\right)^{2}}

(10)

i.e.
\Delta^{\prime}(m)=0
. In the following, whenever numerical value are needed we
set
m=200
MeV and take
a=8\pi^{2}/15
, which is the value appropriate for
pure
SU(3)
Yang-Mills theory in the limit of high temperature.

The speed of sound in this model is found to be

(11)

Its asymptotic behaviour at
T\gg m
and
T\ll m
is given by

\displaystyle c_{s}^{2}(T)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{9}\frac{m^{2}}{T^{2}}+O\left(\frac{m^{4}}{T^{4}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad c_{s}^{2}(T)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{5}+\frac{8}{75}\frac{T^{2}}{m^{2}}+O\left(\frac{T^{4}}{m^{4}}\right)

(12a)

Thus, as the temperature falls, the speed of
sound drops from the conformal value

to
c_{s}^{2}=1/5
.

III.2
Ideal fluid

The simplicity of the equation of state greatly facilitates the study of the
effects of conformal symmetry breaking also away from equilibrium.
For the ideal fluid, conservation of the energy momentum tensor implies

\tau\partial_{\tau}\ln T(\tau)+c_{s}(T(\tau))=0

(13)

Unlike the ideal fluid evolution equation for Bjorken flow (obtained from
Eq. (
13
) by setting
m=0
), this equation does not have an exact power law
solution, but it can be directly integrated. One can also easily check that at late time

T(\tau)\sim\frac{\Lambda}{(\Lambda\tau)^{1/5}}+\frac{4\Lambda^{2}}{15m^{2}}\frac{\Lambda}{(\Lambda\tau)^{3/5}}+\dots

(14)

where
\Lambda
is an integration constant. Compared to the conformal result
for Bjorken flow, Eq. (
4
), the rate of temperature drop is slower.
All powers of the proper time appear in the above series, but one can check
that the entropy density falls off as
1/\tau
exactly, which expresses the
conservation of entropy in ideal fluid flow.

Nonconformal MIS

We will now use the nonconformal equation of state given in Eq. (
8
) in the
evolution equations of MIS theory. For simplicity we will keep the transport

Upon inserting the equation of

) into the MIS evolution equations Eq. (

\displaystyle\tau\partial_{\tau}\log T(\tau)

\displaystyle\tau\partial_{\tau}{\cal A}(\tau)

\displaystyle R_{2}(T)

\displaystyle S_{1}(T)

\displaystyle S_{2}(T)

III.4
The late proper time expansion

As in the conformal case, Eqs. (

At late times one finds the asymptotic behaviour

\displaystyle T(\tau)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\Lambda}{(\Lambda\tau)^{1/5}}\left(1+\frac{4\Lambda^{2}}{15m^{2}}\frac{1}{(\Lambda\tau)^{2/5}}+\left(\frac{\Lambda^{4}}{45m^{4}}-\frac{C_{\eta}}{3}\right)\frac{1}{(\Lambda\tau)^{4/5}}+\dots\right)

The attractor

The description of the attractor does not differ from the conformal
model discussed in Section
II
. In particular, at late times,
Eqs. (
17

section of the attractor on a slice of fixed
\tau

\Lambda
as the
parameter along this curve.
To describe the attractor at early times one needs to study the solutions to
Eqs. (
15
) for
\tau\to 0
. This analysis leads to the conclusion that
solutions for which the pressure anisotropy remains finite satisfy the same
initial conditions as in the conformal case, i.e.
{\cal A}(\epsilon)={\cal A}_{+}+O(\epsilon)
. This determines the attractor section on any proper time slice.
Proceeding in this way on obtains the set of plots shown in Fig.
3
.
In this case, the shape of the attractor and the evolution of its sections are
somewhat different than in conformal MIS theory, but the role it plays in the
dynamics of the system is the same: generic solutions coalesce on the attractor
and then evolve along it.
Both in this Section and in the previous one we have used the
(T,{\cal A},\tau)
parametrisation of phase space, so one can easily compare the evolution
of the attractor curve from slice to slice in the two models.
However, in the absence of conformal symmetry there is no parametrisation in which the attractor
sections would be the same on each proper time slice.
Despite this, there is no
doubt when comparing Fig.
3
and Fig.

that generic solutions
follow the attractor in a very similar way.

IV
Summary and outlook

We have emphasised that the possible emergence of a far from equilibrium
hydrodynamic attractor in ultrarelativistic heavy ion collisions is due to
approximate boost invariance, which captures a characteristic feature of
particle production in QCD. The notion of a hydrodynamic attractor extending
into the prehydrodynamic regime was introduced in
Heller:2015dha
in the
case of conformal MIS theory, where a number of fortuitous circumstances hold
and lead to significant technical simplifications. As a result, the attractor
was defined as a specific solution to a first order ordinary differential
equation satisfied by the pressure anisotropy
{\cal A}(w)
. Such
decoupled equations arise only in the case of conformal models, so to generalise the
attractor concept one needs to adopt a different
perspective
Heller:2020anv
;
Jankowski:2023fdz
;
An:2023yfq
, namely that the
attractor is a submanifold in an extended phase space. Two different
approaches to determining it have been proposed: Ref.
Heller:2020anv

introduced a local notion of attractor, which was not connected to any
asymptotic limits, while in Ref.
An:2023yfq
the attractor hypersurface
was defined by imposing regularity of the pressure anisotropy at asymptotically
small proper time (in the spirit of Ref.
Heller:2015dha
). Here we have
further developed this second approach which directly addresses the origin of
early-time attraction. We have shown how the attractor hypersurface can be
described explicitly by its sections on constant proper time slices. In the
hydrodynamic regime, the attractor section on a given time slice can be obtained
analytically in parametric form where the parameter along the curve is provided
by the asymptotic data.

We do not aim to make general statements about the occurrence of far from
equilibrium hydrodynamic attractors, but we emphasise that our approach allows
for the investigation of hydrodynamisation regardless of the presence or absence
of conformal symmetry. To illustrate this, we have formulated a simple MIS
theory where conformal symmetry is broken through the equation of state. In
consequence, the evolution equations do not decouple and there is little benefit
from introducing the
w,{\cal A}
variables which greatly simplify the conformal
case. Both with and without conformal symmetry, we have chosen to parametrise
phase space using the pressure anisotropy
{\cal A}
and the effective temperature

T
, since these variables have a clear and direct physical interpretation.
Regardless of parametrisation, the phase space approach leads to the same
qualitative picture of the attractor as was established in the conformal case in
Section
II
: there is a well-defined section of the attractor surface on
each proper time slice of the extended phase space and generic solutions follow
it already at high pressure
anisotropy. The basic picture of hydrodynamisation also remains the same: it
begins with a rapid approach to the attractor, followed by exponential
nonhydrodynamic mode decay (which is captured by a transseries
containing contributions carrying nonhydrodynamic data present in the initial
conditions) and then finally a hydrodynamic approach to
equilibrium
Heller:2020anv
;
An:2023yfq
.

The phase space approach is directly applicable to many other problems. One
immediate generalisation of the examples discussed here is the inclusion of bulk
pressure. A little further afield is the treatment of nonconformal kinetic
theory models
Jaiswal:2022udf
;
Chattopadhyay:2021ive
;
Jaiswal:2021uvv
.
Another promising direction are applications to nonrelativistic systems. The
problem of equilibration and emergence of hydrodynamic behaviour arises in many
physical settings, and the approach described
should facilitate studies of attractors also beyond the
context of heavy ion collisions.
