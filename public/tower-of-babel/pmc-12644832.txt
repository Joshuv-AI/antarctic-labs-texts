Graphene oxide-iron oxide based thin film nanocomposite membrane for congo red dye removal and simultaneous salt recovery from aqueous stream
Authors: Animikh Goswami, Banasri Roy, Somak Chatterjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent work focusses on the synthesis of iron oxide impregnated-graphene oxide incorporated polyvinylidene fluoride based thin-film nanocomposite (TFN) membrane and its application in Congo Red dye removal. Interfacial polymerization with 0.02 wt% graphene oxide significantly improved performance of prepared TFN membranes, achieving over 94% rejection of dye with permeability of 3.6 l/m2·h·bar. Testing performance of the membrane was further validated in the presence of real-life feed wastewater, showing 72% dye rejection over 12 h, while an enhanced 94% rejection was achieved with synthetic dye solution. Superior performance was observed in removal of dissolved solids, conductivity, salinity, nitrate, sulfate and dyes compared to commercial nanofiltration membranes. Effect of different operating conditions on membrane performance were examined, confirming its robust nature. Comprehensive characterization of prepared membrane revealed its superiority in texture and chemical nature. Optimized TFN membrane demonstrated recovery efficiency of 70% for sodium chloride and magnesium sulfate solutions, indicating its potential for desalination approach. Thermal regeneration and long-term filtration studies were conducted to evaluate membrane’s lifespan, indicating long-term durability and effectiveness in industrial applications. Studies also indicated weight loss of 62% over two months, which corroborated biodegradable nature of the membrane samples after exhaustion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25455-w.Keywords: Thin film nanocomposite membrane, Graphene-oxide, Polyvinylidene fluoride, Congo red dye, Salt recovery, BiodegradabilitySubject terms: Environmental sciences, Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Wastewater pollution, a critical environmental issue, poses significant threats to ecosystems and human health. Amongst numerous contaminants found in industrial wastewater, synthetic dyes have high toxicity, persistent nature and possess potential to execute severe environmental damages. These dyes, commonly used in industries, such as, textiles, plastics and paper, are often resistant to conventional wastewater treatment processes, thereby leading to their accumulation in water bodies. Hence, efficient removal of these dyes from wastewater is essential to mitigate their adverse effects on aquatic life and human population1–3. In response to the limitations of traditional dye removal techniques, such as adsorption, chemical coagulation and biological treatment, other advanced technologies are also being explored4–7. In this context, thin film nanocomposite (TFN) membranes have emerged as a promising solution due to their superior removal efficiency and multifunctional capabilities. These membranes, which integrate nanomaterials into a thin polymeric matrix, offer enhanced adsorption, degradation and filtration properties, making them particularly effective for treating dye-laden and salt-containing wastewater streams8–10.

Usually, TFN membranes are defined by their unique structure, where nanoparticles, carbon nanotubes and graphene oxide (GO) are incorporated into a thin film layer by interfacial polymerization. Their integration not only improves the membrane’s mechanical strength and chemical stability but also enhances its dye removal capacity. Compared to conventional filtration, TFN membranes have higher surface area and are fouling resistant. These reasons lead to its usage for efficient and sustainable wastewater treatment11. Several research has been focused on enhancing the efficiency of TFN membranes by the integration of versatile nanomaterials. For example, hydrophilic titanium dioxide (TiO2) and polyethyleneimine (PEI) were adhered to the surface of polydopamine (PDA)-coated membrane using vacuum filtration12. Silver nanoparticles were added to this matrix using the in-situ method, importing antibacterial properties and significant potential in the remediation of wastewater containing colorants12. Multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) and α-iron oxide (Fe2O3) were added to modify GO-incorporated polysulfone nanofiltration membranes, which provided high rejection against dye and salt solution without reducing the flux13. Comparatively, higher surface area of nanomaterials, such as, GO, allows for increased interaction with dye molecules. Therefore, GO incorporated membranes exhibit superior adsorption capabilities due to their large surface area and presence of functional groups that facilitates dye binding. Additionally, GO-based membranes exhibit excellent ion selectivity due to electrostatic interactions between its functional groups and the ions in solution. This selective permeation is essential for effective salt recovery. For instance, a GO-based membrane can adsorb and degrade dye molecules, followed by selective permeation of salts. GO incorporation in PVDF (polyvinylidene fluoride) boosts permeability, antifouling and mechanical stability14. This multi-faceted approach maximizes efficiency of wastewater treatment process. For example, polyethyleneimine-modified graphene oxide-polyacrylic acid-polyvinyl alcohol-glutaraldehyde membrane shows 99.5% Congo Red dye rejection15. Integration of multiple functionalities within GO-based membranes leads to their superior performance in complex wastewater environments11. Several studies have shown that GO also has excellent antifouling and chlorine resistance properties, which enhances the membrane’s lifespan16–19. In this context, Fe2O3 also worth mentioning for their adsorptive removal of dye molecules from wastewater8. Addition of iron oxide (Fe2O3) nanoparticles to the polymer solution usually improve hydrophilicity of composite membrane, in comparison to its pure polymeric form20. This improvement in hydrophilicity can be explained by the presence of nanoparticles, which lowers the surface tension of the PVDF-PVP matrix. As a result, water spreads more easily and adheres better to the membrane surface. Another contributing factor could be the migration of nanoparticles to the membrane surface during non- solvent induced phase inversion process. Furthermore, the hydrophilic characteristics of the embedded nanoparticles enhance the membrane’s hydrophilicity21,22. This is primarily due to the polar nature of hydroxyl groups and oxygen atoms on the surface of iron oxide, which interact with water molecules through Van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonding23.

The primary mechanism for dye removal in Fe2O3-based membranes involves surface adsorption. For example, iron-based metal oxide framework has been embedded in polysulfone matrix for removal of Congo Red, Eriochrome black, methylene blue and rhodamine B8. In another work the same nanoparticles have been used for methylene blue and methyl orange removal24. There have been numerous studies which have used graphene oxide and iron oxide nanoparticles separately, but there are no reports where their combined effects have been studied.

Hence, this study aims to use both these functional materials, i.e., GO and Fe2O3 nanoparticles in TFN for dye removal and salt recovery. This study is novel in two aspects. Firstly, synthesized TFN membranes explore the synergistic effect of GO and iron oxide nanoparticles, as stated above. Additionally, preparation of GO has been performed using an eco-friendly and cost-effective method from agricultural precursors, like, corn cobs. In detail, TFN membranes have been prepared by using a mixed matrix substrate, i.e., Fe2O3 nanoparticles embedded in polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) polymer, using n-n-dimethyl formamide (DMF) as solvent. Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) was added to the composite polymer solution for increasing mechanical strength. PVP serves as an effective hydrophilic modifier for PVDF based membranes, enhancing their surface wettability. Incorporation of PVP facilitates the fabrication of membranes exhibiting well-developed structural integrity, enhanced mechanical strength and elevated porosity, along with improved surface hydrophilicity25. Numerous studies have demonstrated that incorporation of PVP not only improves the membrane’s porosity but also significantly increases pure water flux26. Moreover, due to its non-toxic nature and excellent solubility in both water and various organic solvents, PVP is considered a highly suitable additive in membrane fabrication processes. Following preparation of this mixed matrix layer, it was subjected to interfacial polymerization for deposition of GO-incorporated polyamide layer. Prepared sets of membranes were characterized in terms of inherent and surface-based material properties, followed by selection of best-performing membrane for a dynamic batch filtration of Congo Red dye. Both synthetic and industrial wastewater was used as feed during the filtration study. Thermal regeneration of the membrane was also performed to highlight its performance recyclability. Biodegradability test was conducted to evaluate the synthesized membrane’s environmental impact.

All the materials and chemicals used in this study are listed in appendix A(Table-S1) of supplementary information. All the chemicals were of analytical grade and used without further purification. Pvt. Ltd., Chennai, India.

Methods

GO preparation from corn cobs

GO is usually prepared from different lignocellulosic biomasses, such as, corn cobs, sugar cane bagasse, rice husk, etc27. However, in our study, corn cobs biomass was chosen to prepare GO. Firstly, it was washed thoroughly to remove underlying dirt particles. Following this, it was dried at room temperature (25 °C) for overnight duration and it was powdered in the next step, using a food mixer. Powdered mass was then sieved using a standard set of screens (Tyler) and particles having 250-micron average size were used for further catalytic graphitization process, which was carried out by dispersing powdered biomass in a homogeneous solution of 2(M) iron nitrate and 0.5(M) isopropyl alcohol28–30. Subsequently, filtered powder from the dispersion was dried in an oven at 70 °C for overnight duration. This dried sample was then pyrolyzed in a tubular furnace at 900 °C in an inert (nitrogen) atmosphere for two hours. Pyrolyzed mass was then recovered and purified from residual metal ions, using 1(M) hydrochloric acid. This acid-purified mass was washed repeatedly to ensure a neutral pH. Obtained graphite powder was air-dried and used for obtaining GO powder using improved Hummer’s method31,32. Briefly, one gram of graphite powder was dispersed in 23 ml of 0.4(M) sulphuric acid under slow stirring (20–50 rpm) in an ice bath (temperature maintained at 20 °C). Simultaneously, potassium permanganate and hydrogen peroxide, having concentration of 0.02 (M), were added dropwise in the dispersion to ensure oxidation of graphite to GO. Once the oxidation process was completed, residual mass of GO was centrifuged, followed by intermittent washing with hydrochloric acid and distilled water, bringing the final pH to the neutral range (6.5–7.5). Exfoliation of obtained GO was then performed by sonication of its water-based dispersion. Unreacted graphite settled at the bottom of the dispersion during sonication. Finally, exfoliated GO powder was obtained by drying powdered material from the supernatant and stored in an airtight container. A schematic representation of the stepwise preparation of Graphene oxide from corn cobs(biomass) is given in Appendix E (Fig. S8(a)) of the supplementary information.

Membrane preparation

Polymeric membranes were prepared using conventional non-solvent induced phase separation (NIPS) technique33. Briefly, PVDF polymer, having 24 wt% was dissolved DMF at 70 °C. Following this, 0.5 wt% of PVP was added to the viscous solution through high stirring (350–400 rpm) and sonication in order to ensure complete dissolution. Composite polymer solution was prepared by addition of (0.1 wt%) Fe2O3 through simultaneous stirring and sonication, as stated previously. Prepared viscous polymer solution was then poured onto a glass plate, attached over membrane casting instrument. The polymer solution was spread over the glass plate with the help of a doctor’s blade (maintained at a gap of 330 μm) at a speed of 420 mm/min and temperature range of (25–30)0C. Evenly spread solution over the plate was immersed in a gelation bath (distilled water added with few drops of formalin, in order to arrest bacterial growth). Following this, phase separation took place, resulting in solidification of a polymer34. Phase inversed membrane was peeled off from the surface of the glass plate and immersed in a solution, containing 2 wt% aqueous piperazine (PIP) for two minutes. Following this, the membrane was taken out and excess water from its surface was drained off using a roller. It was again immersed in a combined solution of 0.2 wt% of TMC and n-hexane for one minute to ensure interfacial polymerization for synthesis of thin film composite (TFC) membranes. Prepared TFC membranes was dried in an oven at 60 °C for a duration of ten minutes, followed by ringing in deionized water, for further usage. In order to synthesize TFN membrane, the above steps were followed similarly, except for the aqueous PIP solution which contained dispersed GO nanoparticles at various weight percentages. Schematic representation of the membrane synthesis procedure was given in Appendix E (Fig. S8(b)-S8(d)) of the supplementary information. Composition and nomenclature of prepared membranes are outline in appendix F(Table-S2) of supplementary information.

Membrane performance evaluation

Pure water permeability, porosity, molecular weight cut-off, pH at point of zero charge(pHZPC) and contact angle of prepared membranes was performed using established methods, available in literature35,36. Salt recovery and dye rejection by the prepared membranes was determined using a conductivity and spectrophotometric measurement, respectively37. Detailed methods have been discussed in the supplementary section (refer to appendix B).

Measurement of various parametric conditions, concentration of feed and permeate

A digital water and soil analysis kit was calibrated beforehand (Please see the “Instruments” section under Materials and Methods) and was used to measure the following items of feed and filtrate: (i) pH, (ii) dissolved solids, (iii) conductivity, (iv) salinity and (v) dissolved oxygen, as per conventional method38. Spectroscopy based analytical equipment (Please see the “Instruments” section under Materials and Methods) was utilized to measure ionic (nitrate, sulfate and chloride) concentration of feed and permeate samples.

Regeneration and antifouling studies

Feed having 1000 mg/l concentration of Congo Red dye was used to analyse the recyclability of the prepared membranes for different cycles. In each cycle, TFN membrane was directed towards filtration in a batch cell at a transmembrane pressure drop of 4.14 bar for a duration of four hours. Permeate flux and corresponding dye rejection was noted during this experimentation. Following completion of each cycle, used membranes were washed thrice with DI35 and kept in an oven at 70 °C for one hour. After the thermal regeneration of the used membrane was performed, it was again used for the next cycle, similar to the first one. The methods employed for Anti-fouling studies are described in appendix B of supplementary information.

Biodegradability test

Biodegradability test was conducted following previously established protocols with slight modifications to suit the experimental requirements39. TFN membrane sample (having an area of 38 cm²) were buried at a depth of 20 cm in soil that was prepared and maintained within a controlled research laboratory setting. In order to ensure a consistent level of moisture (conducive to microbial activity), water (approximately 150 ml) was sprayed on the soil at regular intervals. Degradation rate of the material was assessed through a soil burial test by calculating the percentage weight loss of the membrane sample over a period of 60 days (at a regular interval of 10 days). Results and discussion

Morphology and elemental analysis

Surface morphology

Morphological analysis of the synthesized membrane was performed using FE-SEM, as mentioned (see the “Instruments” section under Materials and Methods). Corresponding results are shown in Fig. 1. It has been observed from this figure that pure polymeric membrane, M0 (comprising of PVDF and PVP) almost shows a planar surface without any substantial pores/openings. However, due to addition of iron oxide nanoparticles in the polymer melt, probable restriction of polymer segmental mobility takes place and few pores have been formed on the surface of M1 membrane (see Fig. 1b). But, once the substrate, i.e., M1 membrane is subjected to interfacial polymerization by PIP/ultrapure water and TMC/n-hexane, few irregular particles start appearing on the surface (M2 membrane). These irregular particles might be polyamide layer, which seems to have non uniform distribution over M2 membrane. Contrary to this, when GO (0.02 wt%) is used in aqueous PIP solution for interfacial polymerization, interconnected polyamide dense structure starts appearing on the surface of M3 membrane, as observed from Fig. 1d. With further increase in weight% of GO (0.04 wt%), the polyamide network shows dense and uniform distribution, unlike, M2 membrane. M5 (comprising pure polymeric membrane with GO (0.02 wt%) impregnated thin film layer) membrane shows almost a similar structure to M3 membrane. Still, better analysis can be provided from cross-sectional images, which are presented in Fig. 2.

It is observed from Fig. 2u that there is three-layer structure in the cross section of M3 membrane. Filtration performance

Membrane selection

As mentioned in membrane performance evaluation under the section materials and methods, all the prepared membranes (cut as circular discs, having a diameter of 6.5 cm) were subjected to compaction using an unstirred batch filtration cell at a transmembrane pressure drop of 5.52 bar, using distilled water. This was especially performed for ensuring uniformity of the pores. Following this, permeability of each synthesized membranes was assessed. Additionally, parameters, like, porosity, contact angle (measure of hydrophilicity) and dye rejection (using 1000 mg/l of Congo Red) were determined and the corresponding data have been presented in appendix F (Table-S2) of supplementary information. It has been observed from this table that M0 (polymeric membrane) has a high hydraulic permeability (72 l/m2·h·bar). However, upon addition of Fe2O3 nanoparticles, membrane (M1) exhibits a drastic reduction in permeability (14.4 l/m2·h·bar). Due to interfacial polymerization of the same membrane with PIP/TMC/n-hexane, permeability reduced again (0.108 l/m2·h·bar for M2), possibly due to formation of additional layers across the cross section (refer to Fig. 2). The results exactly matched with that of the consequent porosity of the membranes. For example, porosity of M0 membrane is 63%, which reduces to 55% (M1 membrane) and 22% (M2 membrane). Simultaneous improvement in hydrophilicity has also been observed due to impregnation of Fe2O3 nanoparticles and subsequent interfacial polymerization. For example, contact angle of M0 membrane is 70.70, which reduces to 46.90 (M1) and 51.80 (M2). Further, when GO nanoparticles (0.02 wt%) have been dispersed in aqueous PIP for interfacial polymerization, resulting permeability was improved for M3 and M5 membranes (3.6 l/m2·h·bar). However, on increasing amount of GO in aqueous PIP (0.04 wt%), permeability is reduced (0.72 l/m2·h·bar). This might be due to agglomeration and non-uniform dispersion of GO in aqueous PIP49. Surprisingly, the porosity and hydrophilicity of the prepared TFN membranes (M3 and M4) show an exactly opposite trend. For example, M3 membrane has a porosity of 28%, which remains almost same for M4 membranes (27%). However, contact angle for M3 membrane is 57.90 which reduces marginally to 55.30 for M4 membrane, thereby indicating an improvement in hydrophilicity. The reason for the same can be attributed to membrane surface proliferation due to GO nanoparticles, which does not block the macrovoid or the pores formed. M5 membrane shows almost similar trend in case of porosity and hydrophilicity as M3, with porosity 27% and contact angle 58.65°. However, its dye removal capacity(79%) is significantly lower than the M3 membrane (94%). Comparing the dye removal efficiency of all prepared membranes, M3 membranes shows highest dye removal capacity (94%), due to simultaneous presence of GO in the active layer and Fe2O3 nanoparticles in the substrate8,13. Therefore, by striking a balance between permeability and dye removal efficiency, M3 membrane have been used for further studies. In each experiment, a feed volume of 250 ml was maintained (no stirring was used in any experiment) and all experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Effect of pH

In this section, the effect of pH on the flux (using Congo Red dye at a concentration of 1000 mg/l) and percentage rejection using the M3 membrane was studied. Transmembrane pressure drop was kept at 2.76 bar in an unstirred batch filtration cell. Corresponding observations are recorded in Fig. 5a and b, respectively. pH ranging from 3 to 11 was used as the determining parameter to observe change in dye flux and rejection percentage. It can be observed from Fig. 5a, that at lower pH, i.e., pH 3 initial onset flux was higher, compared to neutral and alkaline pH. For example, when the solution pH was 3, permeate dye flux for M3 membrane was 20 l/m2·h at an onset time of 30 min. However, when the solution pH was in the alkaline range, i.e., at pH 11, the onset flux reduced to 11.3 l/m2·h. Congo Red, being an anionic diazo dye, is attracted towards the membrane in acidic conditions, which induces higher flux. But, in alkaline range, the attraction is rather feeble and therefore facilitation towards the membrane is not established50. Surprisingly, at a neutral pH, i.e., at pH 7, the onset permeate flux (2.8 l/m2·h) was lower compared to alkaline range. At this pH, the azo dye being effectively neutrally charged does not become attracted towards the membrane surface, thereby resulting in reduced flux. Another observation can be drawn from this figure. As the filtration time progresses, flux is reduced. For example, at pH 3, onset flux reduces from 20 l/m2·h to 14 l/m2·h over a time of six hours. Similarly, for pH 11, this decrement is observed from 11.3 l/m2·h to 7.5 l/m2·h. This happens due to clogging of the pores by the dye molecules, which results in fouling. However, decrement is not extensive for the experiment performed at neutral pH, where the flux remained constant at 2.8 l/m2·h over six hours of filtration time. This might be due to floating conditions of dye molecules in the residual feed, which is unable to clog the pores51.

Effect of pH on (a) permeate flux and (b) rejection percentage over filtration time for the M3 membrane. Error bars correspond to the standard deviation of the measurements with n = 3 replicates.

Variation of rejection percentage can be observed from Fig. 5b. In this case, electrostatic attraction mediated removal is well depicted from the experimental results. For example, at pH 3, rejection percentage of Congo Red dye was 88% at 30 min. Gradually, with experimental duration, the same increased to 95% at six hours. In case of pH 11, it is observed that the percentage of rejection was 60%, which increased to 65% over six hours’ duration. At neutral pH, rejection percentage increased from 55% to 94%, comprehensively. This happens due to reduced fouling nature of the dye at neutral pH, which ensure its increased interaction with the membrane surface. Another reason can be related to the surface charge of the membrane. Incidentally the pH at zero-point charge (pHZPC) of M3 membrane is 6.9 (refer to appendix D of supplementary information). When the operating pH goes below than the isoelectric point of M3 membrane, electrostatic interaction between the positively charged membrane surface and the anionic Congo Red dye molecules increases, thereby enhancing removal at lower pH. However, an opposite the scenario can be observed at a higher pH, since repulsive force become dominant between the negatively charged membrane surface and anionic dye molecules.

Effect of concentration

Three different concentrations i.e., 1000, 500 and 250 mg/l of Congo-Red dye was prepared at neutral pH for these experiments and the results are shown in Figs. 6a and b. Other experimental conditions, i.e., transmembrane pressure drop and unstirred nature of batch cell was kept same, as mentioned in previous section. It can be observed from these two figures that M3 membrane resulted in enhanced permeate flux at lower dye concentration. This happens due to lesser distribution of dye molecules at lower concentration. For example, it can be observed from Fig. 6a that the onset flux was 8 l/m2·h, 7 l/m2·h and 2.8 l/m2·h at a dye concentration of 250 mg/l, 500 mg/l and 1000 mg/l, respectively. Extent of flux reduction was also higher at lower dye concentration, due to increased effect of sedimentation52.

Effect of concentration on (a) permeate flux and (b) rejection percentage over filtration time for the M3 membrane. Error bars correspond to the standard deviation of the measurements with n = 3 replicates.

For example, dye flux reduced from 8 l/m2·h to 5.8 l/m2·h for 250 mg/l, dye concentration over a duration of six hours. The same trend has been observed for 500 mg/l, where the decrement was from 7 l/m2·h to 4.5 l/m2·h. However, at higher dye concentration, i.e., 1000 mg/l, sedimentation occurs during the beginning of filtration process. It is probably, due to this reason, the flux remains steady at 2.8 l/m2·h. Simultaneously, a higher dye rejection percentage was observed for 1000 mg/l and 500 mg/l. For example, onset rejection percentage was 88% for both 1000 mg/l and 500 mg/l (refer to Fig. 6b). This increased marginally to 94% and 90% for 1000 mg/l and 500 mg/l, respectively after six hours of filtration. But, at a lower concentration of 250 mg/l, the rejection percentage increased from 62% to 80%, due to lower driving force and sedimentation rate52,53.

Effect of pressure

Three transmembrane pressures, i.e., 1.38 bar, 2.76 bar and 4.14 bar was applied in different experiments to study its effect on the permeate flux and dye rejection percentages for M3 membrane over six hours of filtration time. pH of the medium was kept constant at 7 ± 0.5, while Congo Red dye concentration was 1000 mg/l. Corresponding observations are recorded at Figs. 7(a) and (b). It can be observed from the first figure., i.e., Fig. 7a that onset permeate flux increases with transmembrane drop, i.e., driving force51. For example, at 1.38 bar, onset permeate flux was 1.2 l/m2·h, which increases to 2.8 l/m2·h and 3.9 l/m2·h, at 2.76 bar and 4.14 bar, respectively.

Effect of pressure on (a) permeate flux and (b) rejection percentage over filtration time for the M3 membrane. Error bars correspond to the standard deviation of the measurements with n = 3 replicates.

It is quite apparent that flux reduction is not triggered heavily with time at the three pressures drop. For example, over six hours of duration, flux reduction occurs from 1.2 l/m2·h to 1 l/m2·h for 1.38 bar; while for 2.76 bar, reduction occurs from 2.8 l/m2·h to 2.6 l/m2·h. Lastly, for 4.14 bar, flux was reduced from 3.9 l/m2·h to 3.5 l/m2·h for six hours duration. With respect to Fig. 7b, As indicated in existing literature, the rejection percentage tends to increase with the transmembrane pressure drop51,54. For example, onset rejection is 92% for 4.14 bar, while it is lower, i.e., 88% for 2.76 bar and 81% for 1.38 bar. Additionally, rejection percentages increase at lower pressure drops. For example, after six hours of filtration, rejection percentage for 1.38 bar was observed to be 85%, while for 2.76 bar, the same was 94%. However, at comparatively higher pressure drop, i.e., 4.14 bar, rejection remained same at 94–95%.

Regeneration study

Regeneration studies were conducted for analysing reusability of prepared membrane in addition to its feasibility for scaling up in industrial operation55,56. A novel method was adopted to portray the regeneration of prepared TFN membrane, i.e., M3 (Please see the " membrane performance evaluation " section under Materials and Methods). Corresponding observations are recorded in Fig. 8. It can be observed from this figure that permeate flux reduces from 5.4 l/m2·h to 3.9 l/m2·h at 4.14 bar, following the end of first cycle.

Effect of thermal regeneration on permeate flux and dye rejection of M3 membrane. Error bars correspond to the standard deviation of the measurements with n = 3 replicates.

After the membrane was thermally dried, 77% of initial permeate flux (5.4 l/m2·h) was recovered. For example, the permeate flux at the onset of second regeneration cycle was 4.2 l/m2·h. This probably occurred due to the delamination effect of TFN membranes. In this case, the membrane loses its effectiveness in holding the dye molecules tightly and thereby it is released, while imparting fresh efficiency to the membrane57. However, this effect is not observed at the end of the second regeneration cycle. For example, 47% of initial flux, i.e., 4.2 l/m2·h is lost while the membrane is thermally regenerated. For example, at the end of second regeneration cycle, permeate flux is 2 l/m2·h. Surprisingly, at the end of third regeneration cycle, permeate flux of M3 membrane remains the same, i.e., 2 l/m2·h, although there has been flux reduction during filtration. This happens probably due to saturation effect, incurred during the last cycle. Interestingly in the case of dye rejection thermal regeneration has considerable impact. For example, initial rejection was 92%, which decreases to 88% at the end of first regeneration cycle. While the same is 91% and 89% at the end of second and third regeneration cycle. Finally, following the operation after fourth regeneration cycle, rejection percentage was estimated as 91%. This hints that thermally regenerated membranes can be used as an alternative for continuous operation, instead of virgin membranes.

It can be observed from this figure (see Fig. 9) that dye rejection was improved for both synthetic and industrial grade dye feed with time. Steady performance was noted for both cases after seven hours of filtration. For example, onset rejection was 85% which increased to 94% after 12 h of filtration time, for synthetic dye solution. Salt recovery

Different salts, i.e., sodium chloride and magnesium sulfate have been utilized to study the effect of sieving by the prepared TFN membrane (M3). In addition, composite salt mixture, i.e., sodium chloride and magnesium sulfate along with the dye solution have been used as feed to understand the performance of M3 membrane under various composition at 2.76 bar. Details about the same have been mentioned in Fig. S7(a-b) (appendix D of the supplementary information). It can be observed from these figures (Figs. S7(a-b)) that in case of a single salt containing feed, recovery for the same is high. For example, recovery is 93.8% and 96.7% for sodium chloride and magnesium sulfate, respectively, at an onset time of 30 min. This is due sieving effect between the hydrated salt molecules and the pore diameter. For example, hydrated radius of sodium chloride and magnesium sulfate is 55 nm and 0.55 nm, respectively, while the pore radius of M3 membrane is 10 nm (as calculated from the correlation between molecular weight cut off and pore radius)35. The molecular weight cut off (MWCO) of the M3 membrane is 90 kDa (detailed discussion of about MWCO was provided in appendix D of the supplementary information). The experimental pH at zero-point charge (pHZPC) of the M3 membrane is 6.9 (detailed discussion of about pHZPC was provided in appendix D of the supplementary information).

However, as filtration progresses, recovery for the salt, i.e., presence of salt in filtrate becomes lower. For example, recovery is 80.8% and 75.4% for sodium chloride and magnesium sulfate, respectively, at a time of six hours. This might be due to defective pore structure, which occurs over the filtration cycle. In case of dye loaded salt feed, various scenarios have been observed. Firstly, onset recovery of salt becomes slightly lower in comparison to single component salt. For example, at 30 minutes’ duration, 91.3% recovery have been observed for dye loaded sodium chloride feed, which was 93% in case of sole sodium chloride feed. As, sodium is present in Congo Red dye, comparatively easy solubilisation of the salt in dye solution is observed with the salt present in feed, thereby increasing its overall concentration. This is one of the main reason for reduction in recovery of sodium salts, especially when loaded with Congo Red dye. It is also quite obvious that the recovery of salts becomes higher with increased feed concentration. Therefore, after six hours of filtration, recovery of sodium salt becomes 78%, which is lower than 80.8% in case of single salt (sodium chloride feed). This result is aligned to the existing literature37. However, in case of dye loaded magnesium sulfate feed, recovery of salt remains unaffected, as magnesium does not solubilize with sodium present in Congo Red dye. Therefore, its onset recovery is 94% (almost same with single magnesium salt feed). Additionally, presence of dye also facilitates magnesium salt recovery with filtration time. For example, magnesium salt recovery after six hours of filtration is 69% (which is 75% for single magnesium sulfate feed). In this regard, it is worthy to mention that salt recovery in the permeate stream is highly beneficial in the aspect of wastewater treatment, due to its reuse in dyeing process, which in turn reduces reliance on fresh salt and minimizes environmental impact.

Rejection of dye in the presence of sodium chloride and magnesium sulfate was performed to understand how different salts influence dye separation (refer to Fig. S7(c) in appendix D of supplementary information). As sodium chloride (2000 mg/l) was added to Congo Red dye solution (1000 mg/l), rejection percentage gradually increased from 51% (at 30 minutes’ duration) to 74% after six hours. Dye molecules, such as, Congo red have an inherent tendency to self-associate or aggregate in aqueous solution. Therefore, presence of NaCl significantly promotes this dye aggregation process over time. Due to increase of salt concentration in feed solution, dye aggregation is inherently triggered to a higher extent. This occurs because salts effectively screen the electrical double layer repulsion that exists between the same dye molecules. Once this electrostatic repulsion is reduced, attractive Van der Waals forces become dominant, leading to a more rapid and extensive aggregation of dye molecules58,59. This phenomenon is often termed as “salting-out” due to the reduced solubility and increased aggregation of the dye in the presence of higher salt concentrations60. Experimental studies have explicitly demonstrated a positive correlation between the aggregation degree, salting-out rate of reactive dyes and salt concentration61. Aggregation of organic dyes are particularly prevalent in solutions with high ionic strength and is directly influenced by the presence of electrolytes. However, it has also been observed that while adding sodium chloride, rejection of dye by the membrane gets reduced at a specific time instant. For example, at three hours, dye rejection by M3 membrane was 88%. However, the same gets reduced to 67% in the presence of sodium chloride salt in the solution. Therefore, it is also apparent that salts, especially, sodium chloride poses an inhibitory effect on rejection of dyes by TFN membranes at a certain time instant, as per earlier studies58–60.

In contrast, addition of magnesium sulfate (2000 mg/l) to the dye solution resulted in higher dye rejection rate, from 91% at an onset time of 30 min. The rejection percentage stabilized at 95% following the end of six hours filtration time. Significant difference of dye rejection in presence of magnesium sulfate, in comparison to sodium chloride indicates that bivalent nature of magnesium ions plays a crucial role in improving dye rejection. Higher ionic strength and stronger electrostatic interactions caused by magnesium sulfate likely create more substantial barrier, enhancing dye rejection capability of the membrane.

Comparison of filtration performance for prepared TFN membrane and industrial grade NF membrane

A study was conducted to compare the performance of prepared TFN membrane (i.e., M3 membrane) against industrial grade NF membrane in presence of industrial dye wastewater. Experimental conditions were kept at 2.76 bar over a filtration time of 12 h. Permeability of commercial flat-sheet nanofiltration membrane is reported to be 1.08 l/m2.h.bar and the synthetic Congo Red dye (concentration: 1000 mg/l) rejection is 94% (at 2.76 bar). Performance of M3 and industrial nanofiltration (NF) membranes in treating industrial dye wastewater were shown in Table 1. Key parameters, such as, pH, TDS, conductivity, salinity, DO, nitrate, chloride, sulfate and Congo Red dye, permeate flux were measured at one and 12 h. M3 membrane demonstrated superior performance across all parameters. Significant reduction in TDS was observed from 17,800 ppm (parts per million) to 16,100 ppm and 5200 ppm over one hour and 12 h, respectively. Conductivity was reduced from 28.3 mS/cm (millisiemens per centimetre) to 24.2 mS/cm (one hour) and 7.5 mS/cm (12 h). Salinity was also reduced from 21,000 ppm to 17,000 ppm and 8,000 ppm over one hour and 12 h, respectively. Prepared membrane (M3) effectively removed nitrate (118 mg/L to 2 mg/L), sulfate (11,550 mg/L to 350 mg/L). It could also achieve 72% reduction in Congo Red dye. But chloride removal through the membrane was moderate (2,800 mg/L to 2,400 mg/L). M3 membrane also showed minimal improvement in DO levels, increasing from 0.61 mg/L to 0.8 mg/L over 12 h. In M3 membrane the permeate flux remained almost constant over the 12 h period (only 21% reduction, from 2.8 l/m2·h to 2.19 l/m2·h.) In contrast, industrial NF membrane exhibited moderate performance, with only marginal reductions in TDS (17800 ppm to 17,200 ppm), conductivity (28.3 mS/cm to 28 mS/cm) and salinity (21,000 ppm to 20,000 ppm). Removal of nitrate (118 mg/L to 71 mg/L), sulfate (11,550 mg/L to 4,760 mg/L), chloride (2,800 mg/L to 2,500 mg/L) and Congo Red dye (34% rejection), was lesser effective than M3 membrane. Additionally, DO levels declined significantly, i.e., from 0.61 mg/L to 0.1 mg/L over the entire filtration period. Permeate flux was also reduced significantly (40%) from 2.63 l/m2·h at one hour to 1.58 l/m2·h. at 12 h. Biodegradability test

Biodegradability test (Please see the " Biodegradability Test " section under Materials and Methods) was performed and the result was shown in Fig. 10a. It was observed from this figure that at initial day (day 0) of the biodegradability test, M3 membrane showed a flower-like structure (see Fig. 10b). However, the structure was destroyed at the end of the experiment (day 60) (see Fig. 10c). At the end the test, surface of M3 membrane revealed cracks due to the consumption of its polymer layer by micro-organism thriving in soil.

Comparison of various types of membrane in reported literature and this study on Congo Red dye rejection versus water flux.

It also provided evidence that after 30 days of the test, 50% of the initial weight of the membrane was lost. Following that, degradation process slowed down in comparison to the initial time. At the end of the experimental time, i.e., at day 60), weight of M3 membrane was reduced to 62% of its original weight. The observed results may be attributed to the inherent alkalinity of Rajasthan’s soil, which generally exhibits a pH range of 7.5 to 9.562. Under such.

alkaline conditions, polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) is susceptible to degradation63, potentially influencing the experimental outcomes. Observed findings corroborates with similar type of TFN membrane in existing literature39. As per the general trend of standardization rule, biodegradable polymers must have 90% of their mass reduction in atmospheres, like, water, carbon dioxide and biomass, especially after six months. Considering substantial loss in weight after two months, it can be inferred that the membrane can be degraded fully after the standard period of six months. Thereby, it can be considered as a biodegradable material.

Comparative analysis

GO-Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles-based dual polyamide layer M3 membrane, despite having lower dye rejection of 94%, offers significant advantages in terms of pure water flux, making it an effective solution for wastewater treatment. With a flux of 9.9 l/m²·h at 2.76 bar, it provides a balanced performance, ensuring efficient water permeability while maintaining a relatively high dye rejection rate. Performance of GO-Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles-based dual polyamide layer TFN membrane was compared with various membranes in terms of flux and Congo Red rejection (refer to Fig. 11), membranes like the polyamide multilayer64 and PA/ZIF-8(B)65 offer higher rejection rates (97% and 99.9%, respectively), but their flux rates (7.45 and 6.23 l/m²·h) are significantly lower, reducing their overall efficiency. PES-ZIF-8/BMA membrane66 offer rejection rate of 96% for Congo Red with flux rate 3.09 l/m2.h respectively. PVA-PSF67 and cellulose acetate NF68 achieves comparative similar flux (10.21 l/m²·h) and 10.76 l/m²·h with 99.45% and 99.1% rejection.

(a) Biodegradability performance of M3 membrane. Error bars correspond to the standard deviation of the measurements of weight with n = 3 replicates. (b) Scanning electron micrographs of M3 membrane (b) at day-0 and (c) at day-60 with the magnification of 25 kX.

HDA − TMC/PSF69 and PEI-modified GO/PAA/PVA/GA15 membrane shows significantly lower pure water flux 6.62 l/m²·h and 2.31 l/m²·h, respectively. Prepared TFN membrane in this study offers higher flux while maintaining moderate rejection efficiency, providing a balance between filtration performance and operational efficiency. In most of the cited references46–52, salt retention study, especially with respect to monovalent and divalent salts were not performed in detail. Current study showcases the advantage of using GO-Fe₂O₃ based TFN membrane for salt recovery, while simultaneously performing dye rejection. This makes it a promising candidate for practical applications where enhanced permeability is prioritized without significantly compromising rejection capabilities.

Conclusion

Iron oxide nanoparticle-embedded polyvinylidene fluoride films were prepared using phase inversion method. This was followed by interfacial polymerization of composite films with graphene oxide to develop novel dual polyamide layer-based thin-film nanocomposite membranes. In addition, a novel synthesis method for preparation of graphene oxide from rice husks and corn cobs was employed, which can be environmentally sustainable and economically advantageous. Following conclusions can be drawn from the study:

(i)Morphology revealed dense polyamide layer on the membrane surface and an increased average pore size (1.1 μm) in the best performing membrane (having 0.02 wt% graphene oxide, 0.1 wt% iron oxide, 24 wt% polyvinylidene fluoride and 0.5 wt% of polyvinyl pyrrolidone). Cross-sectional images of the same membrane revealed a three-layer structure, consisting of polyamide layer on both ends with substrate in the middle. Transmission electron microscopic images suggested that iron oxide nanoparticles have an average size of 25 nm.
