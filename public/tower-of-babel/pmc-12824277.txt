Miniaturised wideband metasurface polarisation converter with high angular stability
Authors: Cho Hilary Scott Nkimbeng, Sangjoon Do, Heesu Wang, Daeyeong Yoon, Hakjune Lee, Yong Bae Park, Ikmo Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents a single-layer miniaturised wideband transmissive linear-to-circular (LTC) polarisation converter. The unit cell employs symmetrically arranged meandered metallic strips with engineered gaps that govern its resonance behavior. The meandered geometry increases inductance, reduces electrical size, and enhances polarisation conversion efficiency. The metasurface converter achieves wideband LTC conversion for both x- and y-polarised incidences from 5.23 to 12.85 GHz, corresponding to an 84.3% fractional bandwidth, with an axial ratio (AR) below 3 dB. It exhibits high angular stability, sustaining performance up to 85° for the transverse electric mode and 60° for the transverse magnetic mode. Within the AR bandwidth, the insertion loss remains low at 1.6–2.69 dB. The metasurface features an ultra-thin profile of 0.0038 λo with compact lateral dimensions of 0.0904 λo × 0.0904 λo, where λo denotes the wavelength at the center frequency (9.04 GHz) of the AR bandwidth. Both numerical simulations and experimental measurements validate its effectiveness in delivering wideband, wide-angle stable polarisation conversion with high transmission efficiency.Keywords: Circular polarisation, Linear polarisation, Linear-to-circular polarisation converter, Metasurface, Miniaturisation, WidebandSubject terms: Engineering, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Polarisation is a fundamental property of electromagnetic wave propagation and plays a critical role in many applications. Among various polarisation states, circularly polarised (CP) waves (see, e.g., Refs.1–7 are particularly advantageous in wireless communication systems compared with linearly polarised (LP) waves. Their benefits include reduced sensitivity to multipath effects and lower polarisation mismatch. Efficient control and manipulation of electromagnetic wave polarisation, especially CP wave generation and conversion, are therefore essential, as numerous physical phenomena and practical systems depend on polarisation8,9.

Conventional polarisation control techniques typically rely on birefringent crystals or Faraday-effect-based devices. While effective, these methods are often bulky and difficult to miniaturise10,11. To overcome these limitations, metasurfaces have emerged as a promising solution for efficient polarisation manipulation12–18. Metasurface-based polarisation converters enable precise control of both the amplitude and phase of reflected or transmitted waves, making them particularly attractive for linear-to-circular (LTC) polarisation conversion under LP incidence19–37. The design of such converters requires a careful balance of several key performance factors, including compact electrical size, low profile, wide axial ratio (AR) bandwidth, high transmission efficiency, and robust angular stability. Achieving these goals typically involves optimising material properties, unit cell geometry, and resonant elements while ensuring structural symmetry.

To meet these demands, various multilayer and single-layer metasurface designs have been explored. Multilayer approaches often incorporate air gaps30,31 or stacked substrates32,33 to enhance performance. For instance, in Ref.30, a wideband LTC polarisation converter was introduced using two metallic frequency-selective surface (FSS) layers separated by an air gap, while Ref.31 proposed a three-layer meander-line design optimised for oblique incidence. Although both achieved low insertion loss, they suffered from limited AR bandwidth, restricted angular stability, and large electrical thickness due to the air gap. Similarly, multilayer configurations in Refs.32,33 employed miniaturised FSS elements and complex geometries to widen the AR bandwidth and improve angular stability, yet they remained bulky and suffered from constrained angular performance. To address these shortcomings, single-layer metasurface designs34–37 have been developed. In Ref.34, a crossed-dipole-shaped metasurface demonstrated wide AR bandwidth, low profile, and good angular stability. The work in Ref.35 combined meander lines with loops to enhance bandwidth and oblique-angle stability while reducing lateral size. A double-sided metallic structure on a single dielectric substrate was employed in Ref.36, providing wide bandwidth and angular stability, whereas Ref.37 used a concentric square aperture with truncated corners to improve angular robustness. Despite these advances, single-layer designs often required larger lateral dimensions, exhibited higher insertion losses in some cases or delivered only limited AR bandwidth. None of them simultaneously achieved wide AR bandwidth, low insertion loss, compact profile, and broad angular stability.

Building on these studies, the present work introduces a miniaturised wideband transmissive LTC polarisation converter that addresses the above challenges. The proposed unit cell employs diagonally oriented, symmetrically arranged meandered metallic strips separated by engineered narrow gaps, which tailor the resonance characteristics. This configuration increases inductance, reduces electrical size, and enhances polarisation conversion efficiency. The resulting design achieves wideband LTC conversion for both x- and y-polarised incidences, covering 5.23–12.85 GHz (84.3% fractional bandwidth) with an AR below 3 dB. It features an exceptionally compact size of 0.0904 λo × 0.0904 λo × 0.0038 λo, where λo is the free-space wavelength at the centre frequency of the AR bandwidth (9.04 GHz). Moreover, the converter maintains stable performance up to 60° incidence with a low insertion loss of 1.6–2.69 dB. With its wide frequency coverage, compact profile, low loss, and strong angular stability, the proposed metasurface is highly suited for C- and X-band satellite communications, radar systems, and remote-sensing applications, where reliable polarisation conversion across a wide range of incident angles is essential.

Metasurface design

Figure 1 shows the unit cell of the proposed miniaturised wideband polarisation converter metasurface, implemented on a single-layer RT/Duroid 5880 substrate. The unit cell has compact dimensions of 3 mm × 3 mm × 0.127 mm, with a relative permittivity (εr) of 2.2 and a loss tangent (tan δ) of 0.0009. It consists of diagonally oriented, symmetrically arranged meandered metallic strips separated by a central gap (g2). Each strip is further segmented by narrow gaps (g1 and g3), which strongly influence the resonance behaviour and enhance polarisation conversion efficiency. The meandered configuration increases both inductance and capacitance, resulting in a compact unit cell with optimised electromagnetic performance. Its diagonal orientation enhances anisotropic responses, enabling efficient polarisation conversion across a broad frequency range. A related uniplanar meander structure with good angular stability was reported in Ref38. ; however, that design produced only LP waves and required a larger profile and lateral size.

Unit cell of the proposed structure: (a) top view and (b) side view.

The proposed meandered design achieves wideband polarisation conversion and high angular stability by precisely controlling the amplitude and phase of transmitted waves. LTC conversion occurs when the orthogonal polarisation components attain nearly equal amplitudes with a 90° phase difference. The meandered strips increase inductance, while the narrow gaps introduce capacitance, forming an LC resonance that governs bandwidth and phase response. The interaction between inductance and capacitance ensures a stable 90° phase shift, enabling efficient polarisation conversion. Additionally, the multiple current paths defined by strip lengths l1, l2, and l3 redistribute surface currents, fine-tune anisotropic impedance, and strengthen phase control. This occurs because the different meander lengths create multiple current loops that change how currents flow and interact along the x- and y-directions. The longer paths add more inductance, while the shorter ones increase capacitive coupling. Together, they balance the impedance in both directions, equalised the transmitted amplitudes, and keep a stable 90° phase difference needed for circular polarisation conversion. The resonant behaviour of the strips also minimises impedance mismatch, improves transmission efficiency, and ensures near-equal transmitted amplitudes. Together, these mechanisms enable highly efficient LTC polarisation conversion.

The unit cell design also ensures excellent AR performance at oblique incidence, achieving high angular stability through careful structural and electromagnetic optimisation. The diagonal symmetry plays a vital role in maintaining a balanced phase response for varying incidence angles, which further suppresses polarisation distortions. This is because the metallic strips are mirror-symmetric along the 45° diagonal axis, ensuring that the two orthogonal field components (Ex and Ey) experience nearly identical boundary conditions and electromagnetic environments, even under oblique incidence. As a result, both components accumulate comparable phase delays and maintain balanced amplitudes, minimizing phase deviation and preserving the polarisation state with minimal degradation in axial ratio as reported in Ref.38. The increased effective electrical length of the meandered structure stabilizes the phase difference even at large oblique angles, while strong capacitive coupling from the narrow gaps fine-tunes the resonance and maintains a phase shift of approximately 90°. The anisotropic response further enhances angular stability by enabling controlled phase modulation along orthogonal axes, suppressing undesired polarisation mixing38–41. Distributed broadband resonances improve impedance matching, reduce reflection losses, and sustain high transmission efficiency. As a result, the polarisation conversion efficiency, transmission magnitude, and phase response remain stable over wide angular ranges, making the design highly suitable for satellite communications, radar, and sensing applications. The optimised geometrical parameters of the miniaturised LTC converter are: P = 3 mm, l1 = 2.2 mm, l2 = 2.4 mm, l3 = 2.6 mm, w1 = 0.1 mm, g1 = 0.1 mm, g2 = 0.1 mm, and g3 = 0.1 mm.

Unit cell design evolution

The design evolution of the proposed polarisation converter is illustrated in Fig. 2a–d, corresponding to unit cells A, B, C, and the final design D, respectively. Their performance in terms of phase difference, transmission coefficient, AR, and total transmittance is compared in Fig. 3a–d.

Evolution of the unit cell design: (a) unit cell A, (b) unit cell B, (c) unit cell C, and (d) the proposed configuration.

X-to-CP transmission characteristics of the proposed converter under normal X-polarised incidence (total transmittance).

The key principle guiding this evolution is the progressive enhancement of inductance (L) and capacitance (C) from unit cell A to unit cell D.

Unit cell A consists of a basic cross-shaped metallic structure with four symmetrically arranged strips (Fig. 2a). Due to its simplicity, both L and C are low, which limits polarisation conversion efficiency and angular stability.

Unit cell B evolves from A by incorporating diagonal metallic strips, transforming the cross into an asymmetric interconnected structure (Fig. 2b). The additional strips increase both L and C, improving the overall electromagnetic response. As shown in Fig. 3a, the phase difference of unit cell B remains close to 90° from 5 to 22 GHz. The difference between co- and cross-polarised transmission coefficients (txx and txy) stays within 3 dB across 5–23 GHz (Fig. 3b). The AR remains below 3 dB from 7.48 to 22.77 GHz (101.1% fractional bandwidth), as shown in Fig. 3c. The total transmittance varies between 2.89 and 1.80 dB across 7.43–22.77 GHz (Fig. 3d).

Unit cell C modifies unit cell B by introducing multiple closely spaced meandered strips (Fig. 2c), which extend current paths and strengthen electromagnetic coupling. This increases L and C, producing a more efficient phase response, enhanced polarisation conversion, improved angular stability, and better transmission performance. As shown in Fig. 3a, the phase difference is approximately 90° between 5.4 and 14.4 GHz. Figure 3b indicates that (txx – txy) remains within 3.5 dB over 4.5–16 GHz. The AR stays below 3 dB from 5.92 to 14.42 GHz (83.6%), as shown in Fig. 3c. The total transmittance ranges from 1.6 to 2.72 dB within this band (Fig. 3d). The structural modifications also reduce the electrical size.

Unit cell D (proposed design) further evolves from unit cell C by introducing non-uniform meandered strips of varying lengths (Fig. 2d). This asymmetry optimizes L and C, lowers the resonant frequency, and enhances phase control, resulting in wider AR bandwidth and improved angular stability. Enhanced capacitive coupling and impedance matching further refine wave interactions. As shown in Fig. 3a, the phase difference approaches 90° from 5 to 13 GHz. The transmission difference (txx – txy) remains within 3.4 dB from 5 to 14 GHz (Fig. 3b). An AR < 3 dB is achieved over 5.23–12.85 GHz (84.3%), as shown in Fig. 3c. The total transmittance ranges from 1.6 to 2.69 dB within this frequency band (Fig. 3d). At the centre frequency of the AR bandwidth (9.04 GHz), the electrical size is only 0.0904 λo × 0.0904 λo × 0.0038 λo.

Equivalent circuit

To elucidate the electromagnetic mechanism governing broadband polarisation conversion, the metasurface is modeled using an equivalent LC circuit network that illustrates the coupling between the orthogonal electric-field components E1 and E2. Each metallic strip contributes inductance (L) through current flow along its length, while each gap introduces capacitance (C). The impedance discontinuities between the substrate (Zsub) and free space (Zair) define the transmission boundaries of the circuit. The equivalent circuit model of the unit cells (A to D) is analysed to explain their respective resonance behaviors and phase-control mechanisms.

For unit cell A, when an incident linearly polarised wave Ei impinges on the structure, it excites two orthogonal electric-field components, E1 and E2 oriented along the x- and y-directions, respectively, as shown in Fig. 4. Each component passes through its corresponding LC branch, where the inductor (L) and capacitor (C) together determine the transmission amplitude and phase. The inductor produces a lagging phase response, while the capacitor contributes a leading phase response. Because the inductive and capacitive reactances of the two branches (L1C1 and L2C2) differ slightly, the transmitted fields E1 and E2 acquire unequal phase delays and magnitudes. Circular polarisation is achieved when | E1 | = | E2| and the phase difference arg (E1) – arg (E2) is equal to 90°.

Equivalent circuit of unit cell A.

Similarly, as shown in Fig. 5, for unit cell B, diagonal metallic strips are added to connect the orthogonal arms, creating angled current paths that enhance both inductive and capacitive coupling. This configuration improves the interaction between the orthogonal branches, resulting in a broader and more stable phase difference across frequency compared to unit cell A, leading to improved polarisation conversion efficiency and stronger angular stability.

Equivalent circuit of unit cell B.

For unit cell C, each diagonal arm is replaced by multiple closely spaced meandered metallic strips, forming a longer current path and introducing stronger capacitive coupling between adjacent segments, as shown in Fig. 6. The meandering increases the total inductance by elongating the current path, while the narrow gaps between successive turns introduce enhanced capacitive coupling between adjacent segments. In the equivalent circuit, the orthogonal x- and y-polarised components (E1 and E2) are modelled using distinct LC networks. The E1 component is associated with a longer effective current path and is therefore characterised by inductive elements L1 and L2 in combination with capacitance C1, while the E2 component experiences a different current distribution and is represented by an LC configuration incorporating L1, C1, and the additional capacitance C2. Compared to unit cells A and B, the increased effective inductance and enhanced capacitive interaction in unit cell C introduce multiple resonant poles that overlap across the frequency band. This behavior leads to a smoother and more stable phase response, maintaining a phase difference between E1 and E2 close to 90° across a wide frequency range, while the amplitude difference between the two components is minimised. As a result, the transmitted CP wave exhibits improved stability and a broader operational bandwidth in unit cell C compared to unit cells A and B.

Equivalent circuit of unit cell C.

Finally, the equivalent circuit of unit cell D for the orthogonal x- and y-polarised components (E1 and E2) is shown in Fig. 7. Unit cell D has a similar equivalent circuit to unit cell C; however, the introduction of non-uniform meandered strips with varying segment lengths and gap spacings modifies the effective current paths and the strength of capacitive coupling across the structure. This non-uniform geometry introduces a broader range of local inductive and capacitive effects, thereby influencing the resonance distribution and providing enhanced control over the phase response between (E1 and E2) to maintain a stable 90° phase difference. Additionally, the amplitude imbalance between the two components is further reduced, leading to improved stability of the transmitted CP wave and broader operational bandwidth compared to unit cells A, B, and C.

Equivalent circuit of unit cell D.

Surface current distribution

To confirm this mechanism, the current distribution of the proposed design unit cell D at the center frequency 9.04 GHz has been plotted. As shown in Fig. 8, the meandered line segments form multiple current paths corresponding to the different lengths (l1, l2, and l3) confirming the expected current redistribution within the unit cell. To further understand the circular polarisation mechanism of the unit cell, the surface current distributions are analysed at four phase states (ωt = 0°, 90°, 180°, 270°). As shown in Fig. 8a, at ωt = 0°, the surface current vectors primarily point downward along the lower meandered arms. When the phase advances to 90°, the surface current reverses direction and points upward along the opposite meander paths shown in Fig. 8b.

Numerical analysis

The polarisation conversion performance of the proposed miniaturised wideband LTC design was evaluated through numerical simulations performed in ANSYS High-Frequency Structure Simulator (HFSS), using the optimised parameters presented in Section II. Unit-cell simulations employed Floquet ports and master–slave boundary conditions. In these simulations, the incident plane wave’s electric field vector was oriented at 45° to the x-axis, ensuring equal magnitudes and phases for the x- and y-components.

For a y-polarised wave propagating in the + z direction, the structure induces both a co-polarisation component (x-to-x, denoted txx) and a cross-polarisation component (y-to-x, denoted txy with phase φxy). Successful LTC polarisation conversion requires two conditions: (i) the phase difference Δφyx = arg (txx)–arg(txy) must be ± 90°, and (ii) the amplitudes must satisfy |txx| = |txy|. Angular stability refers to the ability of a polarisation converter to maintain performance across varying incident angles26–34. For practical applications, this property is essential, as it minimises performance degradation under non-normal incidence. The proposed converter’s angular stability was assessed in terms of AR bandwidth and total transmittance under both transverse electric (TE) and transverse magnetic (TM) modes. Results for TE mode are shown in Fig. 10a and b, while TM mode is illustrated in Fig. 11a and b. The proposed unit cell achieves high angular stability due to its diagonally symmetric configuration and meander-line geometry. The mirror symmetry along the 45° diagonal axis, ensures that both orthogonal electric-field components (Ex and Ey) encounter nearly identical boundary conditions under oblique incidence, preserving equal phase delay and amplitude balance. Meanwhile, the meandered metallic strips extend the current paths and enhance inductive coupling, allowing gradual phase variation rather than abrupt discontinuities. This balanced inductive–capacitive behavior maintains a stable 90° phase difference and minimizes axial ratio degradation across a wide range of incident angles.

Simulated performance under varying incident angles for TE polarisation: (a) axial ratio and (b) total transmittance.

Simulated performance under varying incident angles for TM polarisation: (a) axial ratio and (b) total transmittance.

As shown in Fig. 10a, the AR bandwidth in TE mode remains stable up to 87°, maintaining AR < 3 dB over a wide frequency range. Figure 10b further demonstrates that total transmittance remains high up to 60°, with attenuation observed beyond 80°, especially near resonance frequencies. This superior angular stability arises from the consistent interaction of the electric field with the metasurface. In TE mode, the electric field remains perpendicular to the incidence plane, ensuring stable phase response and impedance matching across different angles. In TE incidence, the electric field is tangential to the surface and aligned with the meandered strip geometry, allowing it to excite the horizontal current paths effectively even at large angles. Because this tangential field component does not diminish significantly with angle, the metasurface preserves balanced orthogonal currents and maintains a near-quadrature phase difference, resulting in a stable axial-ratio response across wide angles. This behavior is consistent with previously reported angle-dependent TE/TM responses in anisotropic metasurface structures, where TE modes retain stronger in-plane coupling than TM modes42,43.

In TM mode (Fig. 11a), the AR bandwidth is stable up to 60° but deteriorates significantly at higher angles, leading to reduced polarisation conversion efficiency. This degradation results from the strong angle dependence of the TM mode, which disrupts phase matching and resonance conditions. Nevertheless, Fig. 11b shows that total transmittance remains favourable at lower angles, allowing efficient wave transmission even at high oblique incidence. Thus, although the converter demonstrates limited angular stability in TM mode, it maintains high transmission efficiency, making it suitable for applications where efficient transmission is prioritised over wide-angle polarisation conversion. The difference in transmittance at oblique angles as shown in Figs. 10b and 11b, respectively, for TE- and TM-polarised incidence can be explained by their distinct field interactions with the metasurface. Under TE-polarised incidence, the tangential electric-field component remains strong and continues to couple effectively into the meandered metallic paths, resulting in higher transmission at moderate angles. Under TM-polarised incidence, this tangential component decreases rapidly with angle, while the normal component does not efficiently excite surface currents, leading to a more pronounced reduction in TM transmittance. Additionally, the inherent difference in the Fresnel transmission coefficients of TE and TM waves at oblique incidence further contributes to this disparity44,45.

Parametric analysis

This study examined how key parameters influence the performance of the unit cell. Numerical simulations were carried out to optimize the miniaturised wideband unit cell. First, the unit cell’s behavior was analysed with all parameters fixed at their optimal values. Then, a parametric analysis was performed by independently varying each design parameter to evaluate its effect.

The effect of key parameters such as the narrow gaps g1, g2, and g3 on the phase difference, transmittance and the AR were analysed.

The effect of the gap between the inner parallel strips (g1) on the phase difference, transmittance and AR was analysed as shown in Fig. 12a–c, respectively. An increase in gap (g1) leads to an upward shift in the phase as illustrated in Fig. 12a. Typically, an increase in the gap leads to reduce capacitive coupling and should shift the resonance frequency upwards but in this design with the meander-line structure, increasing (g1) also extends the current path and strengthens the inductive effect between adjacent strips. This enhanced inductance dominates over the reduced capacitance, resulting in a slower phase transition and a slight downward frequency shift. Also, as (g1) increases, the transmittance is improved as shown in Fig. 12b. Finally, an increase (g1) in leads to a downward frequency shift in the AR due to the combined LC effect as shown in Fig. 12c.

Effect of the gap between the inner parallel strips strip (g1): (a) phase, (b) transmittance, and (c) axial ratio.

The effect of the diagonal symmetry gap (g2) on the phase difference, transmittance, and AR is shown in Fig. 13a–c, respectively. The gap (g2) represents the separation between the intersecting diagonal branches of the unit cell and plays a vital role in maintaining the structural symmetry along the 45° diagonal axis. An increase in g2 weakens the capacitive coupling across the diagonal arms, thereby reducing the overall capacitance of the structure. As a result, the phase response experiences a downward shift, as shown in Fig. 13a. Also, as (g2) increases, the transmittance improves, as seen in Fig. 13b. Finally, the decrease in capacitance associated with the wider (g2) leads to a slight upward shift in the axial ratio resonance frequency, as illustrated in Fig. 13c.

As (g3) increases, the capacitive coupling between the outer strips weakens, which reduces the effective capacitance of the structure. Experimental results

A prototype of the proposed miniaturised wideband polarisation converter was fabricated using standard printed circuit board manufacturing techniques to experimentally validate its performance. The prototype consisted of 30 × 72 unit cells, covering a total area of 150 mm × 360 mm, as shown in Fig. 15a. The measurement setup is illustrated in Fig. 15b. A horn antenna mounted on a rail system provided precise control over distance and positioning relative to the sample. An XYZ positioning stage ensured accurate movement and alignment, maintaining a fixed separation equal to the horn antenna’s focal length. The sample holder was equipped with a rotating stage, enabling incident- angle variation while keeping the antenna direction fixed. To minimise reflections and diffraction caused by mounting hardware, a focusing lens horn antenna was used. The antenna was connected to a vector network analyser (VNA) via a waveguide-to-coaxial adapter, flange, and cables, ensuring stable and reliable data acquisition. Two horn antennas connected to an Agilent VNA (N5230C) were employed, with the prototype positioned centrally between them. In this configuration, one antenna transmitted y-polarised incident waves while the other received the transmitted signal, enabling measurement of the transmission coefficient from txx. The cross-polarisation coefficient and txy were obtained by rotating the sample 90° while keeping the antennas fixed. Both the magnitude and phase of the co-polarised (txx) and cross-polarised (txy) transmission coefficients were recorded directly from the VNA.

(a) Fabricated prototype and (b) measurement setup.

For TE-polarised incidence, the electric field was oriented perpendicular to the plane of incidence, whereas for TM-polarised incidence, the transmitting horn was rotated so that the electric field lay within the incidence plane. Using the measured amplitudes and phases of (txx) and (txy), the axial ratio for both TE- and TM-polarised incidence was calculated from the orthogonal transmitted field components using Eq. 1. Figures 16 and 17 compare the measured and simulated results for TE and TM modes. In Fig. 16a, the simulated 3-dB AR bandwidth for the TE mode extends from 5.23 to 12.85 GHz (84.3%) at 0° and 3–13 GHz (125%) at 60°. Experimentally, the AR bandwidth spans 6–12.85 GHz (72.6%) at 0° and 5.23–13 GHz (85.3%) at 60°. Figure 16(b) shows that the measured transmittance within the AR bandwidth ranges from − 2.6 dB to − 1.43 dB at 0° and − 4.3 dB to − 2.1 dB at 60°, closely matching the simulations (–2.3 to − 1.43 dB at 0°, and − 4.3 to − 2.1 dB at 60°).

For the TM mode, Fig. 17a shows that simulations predict a 3-dB AR bandwidth of 5.23–12.85 GHz at 0° and 8–11 GHz at 60°. The corresponding measurements yield 5.5–12.9 GHz at 0° and 8–11.9 GHz at 60°. As shown in Fig. 17b, the simulated transmittance at 0° ranges from –2.6 dB to − 1.43 dB, closely aligning with the measured − 2.2 dB to − 1.4 dB. At 60°, the simulated range (–2.1 dB to − 1.0 dB) also agrees well with the measured range (–2.1 dB to − 1.3 dB).

The slight discrepancies between simulated and measured results are primarily attributed to fabrication tolerances, which are critical given the fine dimensions of the structure (line widths and gaps as small as 0.1 mm). Small deviations during fabrication can shift resonant frequencies and affect conversion efficiency. Additional factors include reduced polarisation purity, horn antenna pattern variations at large incidence angles, minor alignment errors, and the absence of finite aperture effects in periodic simulation models.

However, its thickness (0.035 λo) and lateral size (0.24 λo × 0.24 λo) are larger, and its angular stability (40°) is significantly worse. Its insertion loss (2.4 dB) is marginally lower. In Ref.35, the converter achieves a relatively thin profile (0.02 λo) but remains thicker than the proposed design. Its AR bandwidth is only 46.8%, and angular stability is limited to 30°, with a higher insertion loss of 3 dB. The design in Ref.36 has a thickness of 0.13 λo, larger lateral dimensions (0.11 λo × 0.21 λo), and achieves a 70% AR bandwidth—still below the proposed value. It suffers from higher insertion loss (3 dB) and reduced angular stability (55°). Finally, the converter in Ref.37 is 0.075 λo thick with a lateral size of 0.25 λo × 0.25 λo. Its AR bandwidth (37.3%) is less than half of the proposed design, and angular stability (45°) is again lower, though its insertion loss (2.19 dB) is slightly better.

Overall, the proposed miniaturised wideband polarisation converter demonstrates a markedly reduced profile, broader AR bandwidth (except for Ref.34), and superior angular stability compared to prior works. While its insertion loss is marginally higher than in some designs30,31,33,37, the proposed converter achieves an excellent trade-off between profile size, AR bandwidth, and angular stability, establishing its advantage over state-of-the-art alternatives30–37.

Despite the advantages of the proposed design over existing metasurface configurations, the present work also has some practical limitations. The angular measurements are restricted by the physical constraints of the experimental setup, and the behavior at very high incident angles (80°) is therefore supported primarily through simulations. The narrow-gap features introduce sensitivity to fabrication tolerances, which may cause slight shifts in the resonance. Furthermore, the design is realised on a specific substrate, and variations in material properties could influence the AR bandwidth, transmittance, and angular response. These considerations define the practical scope of the present work and highlight opportunities for future optimisation.

Conclusion

This study introduced a miniaturised, wideband, transmissive LTC polarisation converter with high angular stability. The unit cell was designed with symmetrically arranged, diagonally oriented meandered metallic strips separated by engineered gaps to control resonance characteristics. The meandered structure increases inductance, thereby reducing the electrical size while preserving high polarisation conversion efficiency.

The proposed design achieves an ultra-thin profile of 0.0038 λo at the centre frequency of 9.04 GHz, with compact lateral dimensions of 0.0904 λo × 0.0904 λo. It supports wideband LTC polarisation conversion for both x- and y-polarised incidences, covering 5.23–12.85 GHz with a fractional bandwidth of 84.3% and an axial ratio (AR) below 3 dB. The metasurface converter maintains wide angular stability up to 60°, ensuring reliable polarisation conversion under oblique incidence. Within the AR bandwidth, the insertion loss remains low (1.6–2.69 dB), highlighting the design’s efficiency.

With its broad frequency coverage, miniaturised form factor, and stable wide-angle performance, the proposed polarisation converter is well suited for C- and X-band applications such as satellite communications, radar systems, and remote sensing. The results confirm its effectiveness as a compact and efficient solution for wideband polarisation conversion with high transmission efficiency.
