Feasibility of the OdonAssist device for assisted vaginal birth in a low-resource setting: ASSIST Ethiopia study protocol
Authors: Michele Orsi, Fatimata Seydou Sall, Stefania Fieni, Thomas Lihoreau, Virginie Nerich, Fabio Manenti, Flavio Antonio Bobbio, Admasu Taye, Dereje Merga, Sophie Cot, Frederic Mauny, Nicolas Mottet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn sub-Saharan African countries, the population-based assisted vaginal birth (AVB) rate is approximately 1% as compared with 16% in Western Europe. Consequently, women experiencing prolonged labour often face limited access to prompt intervention, leading to maternal and perinatal complications or unnecessary caesarean sections (CS). The OdonAssist device has been developed to be safe, user-friendly and more acceptable than currently used AVB devices. We propose to conduct a study in Ethiopia to evaluate if the implementation of this innovation is feasible and may contribute to improving the access to AVB while reducing unnecessary CSs.Methods and analysisWe designed a single-centre feasibility study at Saint Luke Catholic Hospital (Wolisso, Ethiopia), a secondary facility where AVB is routinely performed by midwives and health officers under gynaecologist supervision, reflecting the local health system. Following a quasi-experimental design, we will include three groups of 20 women: an intervention group (OdonAssist), a vacuum extraction cohort and a control group of second-stage CS (performed without a prior trial of instrumental birth). The primary objective is to assess the clinical and methodological feasibility of the OdonAssist by collecting preliminary data on safety, acceptability and quantifying potential efficacy relative to the current standard of care. An exploratory economic evaluation of direct healthcare costs will be performed.Ethics and disseminationApproved by the Oromia Regional Health Bureau. Introduction

Prolonged or complicated second stage of labour occurs in approximately 10–15% of births, while contributing to 4–13% of all maternal deaths and 25% of neonatal deaths worldwide.1,4 Assisted vaginal birth (AVB) can be safer than an emergency caesarean section (CS) in the short and long term for mothers and babies, by reducing the actual risk of maternal haemorrhage and the future incidence of abnormally invasive placenta, stillbirth and preterm birth5,9; AVB can also be more cost-effective than a CS.10 The current options for AVB, forceps and vacuum, require complex skills and can be associated with significant trauma to both mothers and babies.8 11 Insufficient training, limited availability of functioning devices and concerns about complications are most relevant barriers to access to AVB, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where the highest potential benefit is expected. Although the WHO lists AVB as a vital function for all basic emergency obstetric care facilities,12 African countries commonly report AVB as the least available of these functions.3

Annually, approximately 10–15% of the 134 000 000 births worldwide require AVB or second-stage CS to avoid serious maternal and neonatal complications.13 14 In Ethiopia, it is estimated that 12% of pregnant women have a complicated labour, which causes between 17% and 36% of maternal deaths, 24% of neonatal deaths, 36% of stillbirths and high rates of obstetric fistulas.15,20 The Ethiopian government has made improving maternal and neonatal health a national priority and has identified limited access to AVB as an obstacle to be overcome.18 21

The OdonAssist (Maternal Newborn Health Innovations, Public Benefit Corporation, USA), an innovative simple and potentially less traumatic mechanism of action, is certified as a class I medical device under European Union medical device regulation 2017/745 (UDI-DI: 18881300626010), designed to improve women’s access to AVB worldwide by overcoming the entrenched obstacles and barriers that limit its use with the presently available options of forceps and vacuum.3 The device (figure 1) has been developed to expedite delivery according to internationally recognised clinical indications—that is prolonged second stage of labour, foetal distress and maternal exhaustion—and studies in Europe demonstrated it to be safe, user-friendly and more acceptable than currently used AVB devices (forceps and vacuum) because of its innovative mechanism of action.1322,25

Components of the OdonAssist device: (1) fastening band; (2) deflation button; (3) bulb pump with pressure limiter; (4) plastic sleeve with handle; (5) inflatable cuff; (6) applicator; (7) cup. We propose to conduct a feasibility study of the OdonAssist device at Saint Luke Catholic Hospital & College of Nursing and Midwifery (SLCH) in Wolisso, Ethiopia. Unlike high-resource settings where AVB is primarily a specialist procedure, this study evaluates the device’s implementation within the Ethiopian task-shifting framework, where AVB is typically performed by midwives and health officers under senior supervision. This study aims to determine the feasibility of this new technology in a representative low-resource setting and assess its acceptability by parturients and healthcare providers to inform future impact evaluation studies.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

The ASSIST Ethiopia study is a feasibility study of women who require an AVB for a recognised clinical indication at SLCH in Wolisso, Ethiopia. We will follow a quasi-experimental design (pre-test–post-test approach). Three groups of women will be constituted. One control group with medically indicated AVBs using vacuum, another control group with second-stage CS and the intervention group with medically indicated AVBs using the OdonAssist device. For each eligible patient, therapeutic indications, technical and organisational conditions will be considered, in particular the availability/presence of a practitioner involved in the study (especially trained in using the OdonAssist). Treatment will then be assigned on a competitive basis, starting with the control groups.

SLCH is a second-level general hospital with a 163-bed capacity and availability of blood transfusion centre. On average, the institution annually assists 3500–4000 institutional deliveries, with a rate of referred parturients (mainly from health centres) around 10% and CS rate of 17%. Episiotomy rate is about 20% overall, while in case of AVB, it is approximately 80%. Epidural analgesia is not available, but mothers are allowed to adopt their preferred position during labour; spinal analgesia is preferred in case of CS, while for episiotomy or suture of perineal laceration, pudendal block or perineal infiltration with lidocaine is provided. For the Ob-Gyn Department, one obstetrician-gynaecologist and two integrated emergency surgical officers (IESO, health officer who received specific national surgical training programme) are the senior staff alternately on duty and available on call for the delivery ward; they also cover daily service for Ob-Gyn outpatient department, supervision of antenatal clinic, elective surgery and maternity ward. Consequently, midwives are allowed, under senior supervision, to perform AVB as per clinical indication and according to the national protocol.26 For the Paediatrics Department, one paediatrician supervises the paediatric ward, neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), paediatric outpatient department and malnutrition service. Therefore, newborn resuscitation in the delivery ward, as well as eventual admission to NICU, is activated by midwives and managed with the support of the general practitioner on duty and the NICU staff. All staff involved in the clinical delivery of the protocol are of Ethiopian nationality, ensuring the study reflects local clinical practice and human resource realities.

Eligibility

The population of this study will be pregnant women aiming for a vaginal birth who plan to give birth at SLCH. Women likely to be included will receive information on the study in early pregnancy (12–28 weeks) via paper information leaflets at any hospital admission/visit. Members of the study team will then approach women after 28 completed weeks of pregnancy during antenatal appointments or antenatal admissions to discuss the study and offer women the opportunity to watch a video and read an information leaflet explaining the study, with the aim of proposing recruitment for the investigation.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Women will be eligible for participation in this study only if all of the following criteria are met at the time of consent: ≥18 years; singleton pregnancy >36+0 weeks’ gestation; negative antenatal screen for HIV and Hepatitis B; in active labour and requiring an AVB for a clinical indication as per local guidelines26 (ie, prolonged second stage, non-reassuring fetal status, maternal exhaustion or combined indications); the vertex is 1 cm or more below the ischial spines22 24 25 and there is no obstetric indication for an alternative method of AVB; signed informed consent.

Women will not be eligible to participate in this study if they meet any of the following criteria: the fetal vertex is at or above the ischial spines, diagnosis of a fetal skull abnormality (ie, macrocephaly) or osteogenesis imperfecta, suspicion of a fetal bleeding disorder (von Willebrand’s disease, immune thrombocytopenia, haemophilia), intrauterine fetal death in the current pregnancy, sensitive to latex, currently serving a prison sentence, and there is a fetal bradycardia which is ongoing and has not recovered.

Study procedure and patient management

Control groups (nested cohorts)

A pragmatic, availability-based allocation method will be adopted to assign women who confirmed participation to the study groups (figure 2). In accordance with the inclusion and eligibility criteria, patients will be included in the control groups. Once the expected number of subjects has been reached in the control groups, patients will be included in the intervention group if they meet the criteria. Otherwise, they will not be included in the study. When AVB will be indicated, vacuum-assisted delivery will be provided according to National Guidelines26 (nested cohort group 1). If delivery will be directly assisted by second-stage CS, without attempt of AVB, the fetal-maternal dyad will be assigned to the nested cohort group 2.

Intervention group

Once the control groups are completed, if the woman agrees to an AVB, an operator who has received specific training in using the OdonAssist will assist the birth with the device. The obstetrician, IESOs and midwives will be trained for the use of the OdonAssist device in multiple theoretical and practical sessions by international experts with experience derived from previous feasibility studies in Europe.22 25 27 Additionally, training for Good Clinical Practice (GCP) principles will be provided to the staff involved in the conduction of the present feasibility study according to the WHO standard and recent amendments.28 Should the birth not be achieved with the OdonAssist (failure), the Senior on duty will use his/her clinical judgement and experience on an individual case basis to complete the birth using vacuum or CS as appropriate. According to the indication and foetal-maternal status, and similarly to the vacuum technique, a maximum of three applications will be allowed for the OdonAssist.25,27 Criteria to define failure will be as follows: the head does not advance with each pull; the fetus is not delivered within 30 min; the device is removed without delivery of the baby’s head. In case of failure, the Senior on duty will decide if vacuum-assisted delivery can be attempted or to proceed with CS.25,27 To ensure perinatal safety, a cumulative interval of 30 min will be considered to declare AVB failure, either considering OdonAssist and vacuum.

Economic evaluation

Due to the feasibility design, and for exploratory purposes, a partial economic evaluation based on a cost analysis will be conducted to compare the three interventions from the payer’s perspective. The analysis will focus on direct medical healthcare resource use and the associated costs, including costs related to intervention (device, hospitalisation, inpatient and outpatient treatments), monitoring (clinical and laboratory), consultations and management of complications over the duration of delivery admission. Indirect medical and intangible costs will not be analysed.

Safety of intervention

A comprehensive assessment of the safety of the OdonAssist device will be undertaken following every attempted birth, including patient-related and device-related outcomes. Systematic data collection will ensure capture of any adverse events associated with the use of the device. Follow-up will be performed by the research team. The Trial Management Group composed of all investigators at the study site and the Sponsor will review safety data regularly. The trial will be supervised by the Trial Steering Committee (TSC) including epidemiologist, statistician, medical-device and regulation experts, and all investigators and Sponsor representative. The TSC will oversee the study progress and ensure adherence to the GCP principles.28 Audit will be performed when requested by the Sponsor or the SLCH Management Team. Adverse events will be classified and reported in accordance with the Ethiopian Food and Drug Authority and ethics committee requirements.29 30

Patient and public involvement

Previous feasibility studies in England involved women and their partners in the development of the ASSIST and ASSIST II studies.24 27 While several patient associations dedicated to pregnancy and childbirth exist in England, evidence of similar specific, well-established institutions in Ethiopia is poorly documented. Given this scenario, a patient and public involvement plan will be explored by the Sponsor through its engagement with local institutions based on the results of this feasibility study, with the aim of adequately designing future impact evaluation studies.

Sample size calculation

This feasibility study is not expected to demonstrate a statistically significant difference between the arms on the selected endpoints, but rather to quantify the feasibility elements and differences observed between the groups, along with their 95% CI. The number of subjects required was determined to be consistent with approaches used in pilot and/or feasibility studies, and in line with the Food and Drug Administration guidance on early feasibility studies of medical devices.31 We retained the number of 20 AVBs using the OdonAssist and fixed the same sample size for each of the two control groups. This choice is the result of a balance between a sufficiently high number of subjects to ensure accurate estimates and the smallest possible number of patients included, thereby limiting the time required to complete the study and, consequently, the time before the results are available.

Data collection and management

The delivery ward staff will be specifically trained for data collection according to the study protocol. All study documents, including participant information leaflets, consent forms and questionnaires, have been professionally translated into local language (Oromiffa). Data collection is conducted through face-to-face interviews by trained Ethiopian staff to ensure participant understanding and data quality.32 All study data will first be collected using paper-based forms; subsequently, anonymised data will be uploaded into a secure, password-protected electronic online database specific to the study (https://chrub.tentelemed.com). Access will be allowed to trained local staff and the University of Besançon for formal analysis. All paper-based and electronic data and files will be transferred to a secure archive within 2 years of the end of the study, at SLCH and the University of Besançon, respectively. Data will be retained for 25 years after the end of the study. Data procedures will comply with the provisions of the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679.

Statistical analysis

Clinical outcomes will be reported as frequency and proportion, mean (SD) or median (IQR) depending on their nature and distribution. The overall rate of successful AVB birth will be reported and the frequency of unsuccessful births will be reported by the criteria defined for success. The overall number of safety events will be reported, as well as the number of events by main reasons for adverse events. Events related to the device failure and/or misuse of the device will also be described. The difference between the OdonAssist group and the control groups (vacuum and emergency CS in the second stage of labour) will be calculated. No formal statistical test will be performed on these differences, but our analysis will allow for assessing the expected effect on the use of OdonAssist. Calculated proportion, means and differences will be presented with a 95% CI.

Discussion

AVB has the potential to reduce unnecessary CSs and improve maternal and neonatal outcomes, particularly in settings where access to comprehensive obstetric care is limited.1,38 In Sub-Saharan Africa and other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), prolonged second stage of labour often leads to intrapartum stillbirth, uterine rupture or maternal morbidity and mortality.2 33 Despite WHO’s recognition of AVB as a basic emergency obstetric function, it is rarely practised in many LMICs.2 The barriers include lack of training, equipment and confidence in safe AVB performance among clinicians.2 3

The OdonAssist device offers a simplified and potentially safer alternative to traditional AVB tools.34 35 Prior studies in high-income countries suggest good safety and acceptability by patients’ and healthcare providers.23,2527 However, context-specific studies are needed to confirm its safety profile while addressing the challenges and opportunities for its use in LMICs.36 Unlike previous feasibility studies in Europe,24 25 access to and utilisation of epidural analgesia for labour pain management are often limited in Sub-Saharan Africa,37,39 including the context of this study. This aspect will provide crucial knowledge about parturients’ acceptability of this innovative technology, informing the design of future impact evaluation studies. By contrast, the institutional rate of episiotomy is relatively high, especially in case of AVB. Although these are frequent findings both in high- and LMICs,40,44 WHO recommends avoiding routine episiotomy and advocates for a rate around 10%, encouraging selective use to be considered in case of fetal distress or instrumental delivery.45 Considering previous experience in Europe,25 46 the introduction of the OdonAssist in the present study may provide the opportunity to reconsider institutional practice and improve the alignment of obstetric assistance with WHO recommendations.

Considering the wider perspective, in most Sub-Saharan African countries—including Ethiopia—health systems are structured around primary health centres and district hospitals, often without specialist obstetricians. In these contexts, midwives, health officers and general practitioners play a central role in intrapartum care.3 Empowering these cadres to safely perform AVB could significantly contribute to the reduction of morbidity and mortality associated with prolonged second stage labour.3 15 16 18 Increasing the access to AVB in low-resourced settings is crucial to reduce the burden of complications deriving from parturients’ referral during labour and from CS performed in the second stage of labour.2 9 Several successful AVB re-introduction initiatives have been reported. In Uganda, vacuum use rose from 0.6% to 3.7%, reducing intrapartum stillbirths by 23.6% and uterine rupture by 25.5%.47 Similar results were achieved in Tanzania, Mozambique and Papua New Guinea through training, supervision and guideline development.48 49

Given this scenario, the ASSIST Ethiopia study will provide important feasibility and safety data to assess whether this model can be extended using the OdonAssist device. If successful, it will provide the data necessary to conduct an impact-evaluation study to formally demonstrate the efficacy and safety of the device in the context of healthcare systems in developing countries. The results of this study could also support scale-up and training of non-specialist providers in safe AVB techniques, aligned with WHO recommendations36 to improve maternal and neonatal outcomes.
