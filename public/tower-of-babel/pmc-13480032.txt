Time-dependent adaptation and clinical efficacy of complete dentures: evidence from a Bayesian mixed-effects model
Authors: Junhong Liu, Yi Lin, Xinlu Bing, Hui Qi, Yueming Wang, Cheng Li, Feng Qiao, Ying Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the clinical efficacy of Biofunctional Prosthetic System (BPS) complete dentures during the early adaptation period, with a focus on functional outcomes and patient-reported quality of life as assessed by the Oral Health Impact Profile for Edentulous Patients (OHIP-EDENT).MethodsA single-arm prospective cohort study evaluated masticatory efficiency (ME), occlusal function (occlusal contact area [OCA], asymmetry index of occlusal force [AOF]), and OHIP-EDENT at baseline, 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months post-insertion. A Bayesian mixed-effects model was employed to analyze repeated measures, providing robust probabilistic inference on temporal trajectories and influencing factors.ResultsData from 18 patients were analyzed. Denture-wearing time was the principal determinant of outcomes. OHIP-EDENT scores improved within the first week, while ME and OCA showed progressive consolidation by 1 and 3 months, revealing a staged adaptation process. No consistent associations were observed for age, sex, or prior denture experience within the early observation period.ConclusionsTime emerged as the main determinant of early BPS denture adaptation. The findings delineate a sequential adaptation pattern in which early psychosocial benefits, as measured by OHIP-EDENT, precede functional recovery. These results underscore the importance of structured clinical follow-ups at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months, and demonstrate the methodological value of Bayesian mixed-effects models in enhancing inference in prosthodontic research and generating clinically relevant guidance for patient management. Background

Edentulism is highly prevalent among older adults worldwide and imposes substantial functional, nutritional, and psychosocial burdens [1, 2]. Diminished masticatory performance, dietary restrictions, and reduced quality of life are common consequences [3, 4], and the demand for prosthodontic rehabilitation continues to grow with an aging population [2].

Conventional complete dentures (CCDs) remain the primary non-surgical treatment option for patients who are ineligible for or decline implant-supported prostheses [5, 6]. However, their performance is compromised by residual ridge resorption, mucosal resiliency, saliva characteristics, and neuromuscular control, which collectively affect support, retention, and stability. These factors contribute to marked interindividual variability in adaptation and function [7–9], underscoring the need for optimized protocols. The Biofunctional Prosthetic System (BPS) was developed to standardize CCD fabrication, emphasizing patient-specific border molding, functional impressions, precise jaw relation records, and coordinated laboratory procedures [10–12]. Although prior studies have reported favorable outcomes with BPS in terms of retention, stability, and patient satisfaction, the evidence remains limited by methodological constraints [13–15].

Most previous investigations relied on conventional frequentist approaches such as univariate, multivariate, or logistic regression analyses [9, 16–20]. These methods are vulnerable to inflated Type I error rates under multiple testing, hierarchical data structures, or model misspecification, and they often fail to adequately account for data correlation and confounding. Such limitations can increase false-positive findings and weaken the reliability of conclusions. In contrast, Bayesian mixed-effects models incorporate prior knowledge, flexibly handle complex data, and generate probabilistic interpretations of estimates, thereby improving inference validity and robustness, particularly in studies with small to moderate samples.

Clinically, early post-insertion adaptation involves dynamic changes in neuromuscular control and occlusal function [21]. Capturing these trajectories requires concurrent evaluation of both objective functional outcomes and patient-reported measures, alongside patient- and prosthesis-related factors that may shape adaptation [22]. The first three months after denture delivery represent a critical period for evaluating these patterns. From a methodological perspective, Bayesian mixed-effects models not only address intra-individual correlations inherent in repeated measures but also enhance inference stability with weakly informative priors, while predictive assessments mitigate overfitting.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to characterize the 3-month adaptation trajectories of clinical outcomes in edentulous patients rehabilitated with BPS complete dentures and to identify associated influencing factors. Specifically, we evaluated masticatory efficiency (ME), occlusal contact area (OCA), asymmetry index of occlusal force (AOF), and Oral Health Impact Profile for Edentulous Patients (OHIP-EDENT). Potential predictors included time, residual ridge condition, prior complete denture (CD) experience, prosthetic expectations, age, and sex. We hypothesized that (1) clinical outcomes would improve progressively during the adaptation period; (2) patient- and prosthesis-related factors, including residual ridge condition, prior CD experience, prosthetic expectations, age, and sex, would be significantly associated with the magnitude of change.

Methods

Study design and participants

This single-arm prospective cohort study was conducted in a hospital-based setting between 2022 and 2024. The study population consisted of edentulous patients scheduled for BPS denture restoration.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board (TMUhMEC20220815). It was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR; registration no. ChiCTR2400082742). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data collection and variables

Demographic and clinical data were recorded for all participants. In addition to denture-wearing time, five key variables were evaluated: age, sex, prior CD experience, prosthetic expectations, and residual ridge condition.

Prior CD experience was defined as previous use of complete dentures before the current treatment and was recorded as a binary variable (yes/no). Prosthetic expectations were assessed pre-treatment through one-on-one interviews with participants (and family members when appropriate), addressing three domains: functional needs, aesthetic concerns, and comfort requirements. Based on the interview responses, participants were categorized into low or high expectation groups.

Residual ridge condition was evaluated during the pre-prosthetic examination and classified as mild alveolar ridge resorption (Classes I and II) or severe alveolar ridge resorption (Classes III and IV), according to the classification systems proposed by Atwood [25] and Cawood [26].

Outcome measures

BPS denture performance was assessed using both objective and subjective measures within the first 3 months post-insertion.

Masticatory performance

Masticatory efficiency (ME) was evaluated at baseline (immediately post-insertion) and at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months post-insertion using a standardized absorbance-based method. Briefly, each participant chewed 5 g of peeled dried peanuts freely for 30 s without swallowing. The residual bolus was collected from the oral cavity and denture surfaces, suspended in distilled water to a final volume of 1000 mL, and gently mixed. The absorbance of the suspension was measured at 600 nm using a BioPhotometer (Eppendorf, Germany), with distilled water serving as the blank control. Each sample was measured in triplicate, and the mean absorbance value was recorded; higher absorbance values indicated greater masticatory efficiency.

Occlusal function

Occlusal function was assessed using a computerized occlusal analysis system (T-Scan III, Tekscan Inc., USA). Participants were seated in an upright position, and an appropriately sized sensor was positioned with the occlusal frame aligned to the midline of the maxillary central incisors. Subjects were instructed to bite the sensor with maximum force. Each measurement was repeated three times, and the mean values were calculated.

The system automatically recorded occlusal contact area (OCA) and the asymmetry index of occlusal force (AOF). OCA was defined as the total contact area between the maxillary and mandibular arches in maximum intercuspation and was quantified by converting occlusal contact contour lines into pixel-based areas. The contour lines refer to the closed curves generated by the T-Scan system that delineate the boundaries of occlusal contact areas during occlusion [27]. AOF was defined as the imbalance in occlusal force distribution between the left and right sides of the dental arch. Using high-resolution pressure sensors, the T-Scan system recorded the percentage distribution of occlusal force on each side and generated dynamic occlusal maps, enabling quantitative assessment of occlusal force asymmetry [28]. All measurements were performed by the same operator to minimize operational bias.

Patient-reported outcome

Oral health-related quality of life was measured using the OHIP-EDENT [29] at baseline (pre-restoration) and all follow-up visits. Each of the 19 items was rated on a five-point Likert scale (0–4), yielding a total score from 0 to 76, with lower scores indicating better quality of life.

Clinical fabrication of BPS dentures

Maxillary and mandibular complete dentures were fabricated using a standardized Biofunctional Prosthetic System (BPS) protocol by the same senior dentist and dental technician. The BPS protocol is a biofunctionally oriented complete denture concept that integrates functional impression techniques, accurate maxillomandibular relationship recording, and balanced occlusal design to optimize denture stability, retention, and patient adaptation [10, 30].

In contrast to conventional complete denture fabrication, the standardized BPS protocol emphasizes three core elements:

functional border molding under active neuromuscular conditions,

functional border molding under active neuromuscular conditions,

accurate jaw relation recording using a Gnathometer system,

accurate jaw relation recording using a Gnathometer system,

a coordinated clinical-laboratory workflow designed to reduce operator-dependent variability.

a coordinated clinical-laboratory workflow designed to reduce operator-dependent variability.

Clinically, the BPS workflow comprises four visits. At the first visit, primary impressions were obtained using a dual-viscosity alginate double-impression technique with Accu-dent trays or frame cut-back trays, allowing simultaneous preliminary jaw relation registration using a centric tray. At the second visit, final impressions were recorded using a custom tray equipped with a Gothic arch tracer (Gnathometer) in a closed-mouth, patient-guided functional impression, during which definitive maxillomandibular relations are established. At the third visit, a functional try-in was conducted, and both clinician and patient satisfaction were assessed. At the fourth visit, the final dentures were delivered. This approach contrasts with the conventional complete denture protocol, which typically employs a single-consistency alginate primary impression without preliminary jaw relation recording, an open-mouth, clinician-guided passive functional molding technique, and determination of definitive jaw relations only after fabrication of the final cast.

A schematic overview of the standardized BPS workflow is provided in Supplementary Fig. S1, and representative images of the Accu-dent trays, frame cut-back tray, centric tray, and custom tray with a Gnathometer are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2.

Sample size estimation

Sample size was determined using a Bayesian simulation-based iterative approach [31]. The prior distribution of the effect parameter θ (representing the mean treatment effect on the primary outcome) was specified as N (0.5, 0.42), and the target criterion was set as a prior predictive success probability of ≥ 80%.

A series of 10,000 simulations indicated that n = 8 was the minimum required to achieve this probability. Sensitivity analysis of the prior effective sample size (ESS), following the recommendations of van de Schoot et al. [32], suggested a range of 7–9. Considering feasibility, n = 9 was chosen as the minimum required sample size, which still met the success probability criterion under conservative priors.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.1). Bayesian mixed-effects models were fitted using the brms (Bayesian Regression Models using Stan) package to evaluate the effects of time, age, sex, residual ridge condition, prior CD experience, and prosthetic expectations on ME, OCA, AOF, and OHIP-EDENT.

The continuous variable “age” was standardized (mean = 0, SD = 1), reflecting the outcome change per one-standard-deviation increment in age and mitigating estimation bias from its inherent numerical range. To improve model fitness and meet the normality assumption of the linear models, AOF was natural log-transformed. All subsequent statistical analyses involving AOF were performed using the transformed data. For each outcome, 15 candidate models (Supplementary Table S1) were compared using the leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV) with the leave-one-out information criterion (LOOIC) to determine the optimal model structure.

Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling involved 4 chains (2000 warm-up, 1000 sampling iterations), generating an effective sample size of 4000. To ensure convergence, the adapt-delta parameter was set to 0.99 and the maximum tree depth to 15. Model diagnostics included the potential scale reduction factor (Rhat < 1.01), effective sample size (ESS; both Bulk-ESS (bulk effective sample size) and Tail-ESS (tail effective sample size) > 1000), and inspection of stable trace plots. Fewer than five divergent transitions were resolved by increasing adapt-delta to 0.999 and extending the warm-up phase.

Model performance was evaluated using LOOIC. Models with ΔLOOIC ≤ 2 were considered comparable, and the final model was selected by balancing predictive accuracy and theoretical interpretability. Statistical inferences were based on posterior mean estimates with 95% Bayesian credible intervals (CrIs); effects were considered supported when CrIs excluded zero. Following Bayesian interpretation guidelines, we report the probability that effects are in the expected direction rather than traditional significance testing.

Discussion

Consistent with our hypotheses, clinical outcomes improved significantly during the 3-month adaptation period, supporting Hypothesis (1). ME increased at 1 month and further at 3 months; OCA rose significantly at 3 months; and OHIP-EDENT scores decreased by 1 week, with further reductions thereafter. Hypothesis (2) was not supported, as residual ridge condition, prior CD experience, prosthetic expectations, age, and sex were not significantly associated with clinical outcomes. This likely reflects the conservative nature of Bayesian analysis and the small sample size, which widened credible intervals and encompassed zero. The current evidence is therefore insufficient to establish clinically meaningful associations, and further validation in larger cohorts is required.

The earlier increase in ME and delayed augmentation of OCA align with staged neuromuscular adaptation and progressive occlusal balance development. Notably, ME at 1 week after denture insertion was lower than immediately post-insertion, corroborating previous reports of short-term declines in masticatory performance due to palatal coverage and prosthesis adaptation [33]. This transient reduction, also observed in our study, represents a common physiological response to new prostheses. Significant improvements at 1 and 3 months confirm successful adaptation, consistent with literature demonstrating that masticatory function gradually stabilizes over approximately 3 months [34–36].

Unlike ME, improvements in occlusal function were delayed, becoming significant only at 3 months. This lag likely reflects the dependence of occlusal processes on neuromuscular control, as muscle memory adaptation and masticatory pattern reconstruction typically require approximately 3–6 months [16]. Early improvements in ME may represent short-term compensatory responses that help restore basic function during the initial adaptation phase, even before occlusal balance is fully achieved. However, such improvements are generally modest in magnitude and may not translate into statistically significant differences at the group level when ME is compared before and after CCD replacement [37, 38] or across different occlusal concepts [39, 40], supporting the view that occlusal refinement is a time-dependent process involving time, neuromuscular adaptation, and precise adjustments.

The selection of different baseline time points reflected the evaluation characteristics of each outcome: OHIP-EDENT was assessed pre-restoration as a subjective quality-of-life measure, whereas ME and OCA required denture wear to enable physiologically meaningful and safe measurement. The baseline time point for OHIP-EDENT (pre-restoration) was intentionally set earlier than that of ME and OCA (immediately post-insertion) to separately capture subjective oral health and early functional adaptation. Significant score reductions reflect rapid relief of edentulism-related impacts on aesthetics, social participation, and comfort [41]. These psychosocial benefits appeared earlier than objective occlusal improvements, suggesting that subjective adaptation precedes functional stabilization. The absence of detectable associations with log-transformed AOF may reflect limited statistical power or the model specification that excluded time, which may have masked time-varying changes. These findings should not be interpreted as occlusal symmetry being irrelevant, but rather as requiring further exploration with larger samples and refined measures.

Sex- and age-related differences were also explored. Although no statistically significant differences were observed, males exhibited consistently better outcomes across ME, OCA, AOF, and OHIP-EDENT, consistent with prior studies showing higher masticatory performance [42, 43] and lower Oral Health Impact Profile scores in males [44]. Age has been identified as a potential predictor of denture success, with older patients demonstrating reduced muscular and mucosal adaptability [7, 45], though other studies found minimal or no association between age and bite force [46, 47]. Our findings align with the latter. Clinically, less severe alveolar resorption is generally associated with better denture retention, stability, and function [48]. However, this relationship was not observed in the present study, which may reflect the use of an optimized complete denture fabrication protocol that can partially compensate for unfavorable alveolar ridge conditions in the short term [10, 45].

Patients’ expectations and prior denture experiences may influence treatment outcomes. High expectations can lead to disappointment if not met, while prior experience may facilitate adaptation by providing a reference point. Cohort studies have shown that prior denture use supports faster adaptation, particularly in masticatory recovery and mandibular denture comfort [49, 50]. However, our results did not reveal such effects, possibly due to the limited sample size or the conservative Bayesian approach yielding wide credible intervals.

Although long-term stabilization of oral health-related quality of life may require up to six months, previous studies suggest that the early adaptation process after denture insertion can be reliably captured within the first three months [21, 51]. Moreover, our previous studies suggest that optimized complete denture protocols, such as the BPS protocol, may facilitate faster early adaptation compared with conventional approaches. Accordingly, the 3-month time point in the present study was intentionally selected to evaluate early post-insertion adaptation rather than long-term quality-of-life stabilization.

This study highlights the methodological advantages of Bayesian analysis. Despite the small sample, Bayesian estimation stabilized inference and consistently supported time as the dominant predictor, with time-inclusive models showing the strongest predictive accuracy. Importantly, non-significant covariates with wide credible intervals (CrIs) should not be interpreted as having no effect. Bayesian methods offer key advantages over frequentist approaches: (1) they integrate prior evidence with observed data through prior distributions, which is especially valuable for small samples [32]; (2) they provide posterior distributions to directly quantify uncertainty and yield probabilistic interpretations [52]; and (3) they naturally accommodate hierarchical correlations, avoiding strict independence assumptions and enhancing inference robustness [53, 54].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the small sample size restricted power to detect weak effects. Second, the single-center design without a control group limited causal inference and generalizability. Third, the 3-month follow-up captured only early adaptation and not medium- to long-term stability. Finally, reliance on patient-reported measures without blinding may have introduced response bias. These factors should be addressed in future multi-center studies with longer follow-up and larger sample sizes.

Conclusion

Time emerged as the primary determinant of the clinical efficacy of BPS complete dentures, acting independently of other clinical variables. During the adaptation period, psychosocial improvements occurred early, followed by the progressive consolidation of masticatory efficiency and occlusal contacts. These findings highlight the importance of structured follow-ups and targeted occlusal management during the early phase of adaptation, while underscoring the need for controlled, long-term studies to evaluate the durability and comparative effectiveness of outcomes.
