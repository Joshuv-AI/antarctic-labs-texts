Mechanics Post-TAVR for Trileaflet vs Bicuspid Aortic Valves
Authors: Breandan B Yeats, Aniket Venkatesh, Sri Krishna Sivakumar, Hoda Hatoum, Milad Samaee, Venkateshwar Polsani, Pradeep K Yadav, Vinod H Thourani, David Meier, Stephanie L Sellers, Ajit Yoganathan, Lakshmi P Dasi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBicuspid aortic valve (BAV) disease is the most common congenital heart defect and is associated with elliptical openings and excess calcification. Limited data are available on long-term efficacy of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) for BAV patients.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to compare simulated mechanical metrics post-TAVR between BAV and trileaflet patients with calcific aortic stenosis.MethodsPre-TAVR computed tomography scans of trileaflet (n = 22) and BAV (n = 25) were used to segment the aortic valve. Simulated balloon-expandable stent deployment was performed from a previously developed protocol. The bioprosthetic leaflets were then pressurized under a physiological pressure. Stent waist expansion ratio, aspect ratio (AR), leaflet systolic normalized geometric orifice area (GOA), and maximum leaflet diastolic stress were measured.ResultsBAVs had lower waist expansion ratio (87.8% ± 2.6% vs 89.4% ± 1.4%; P < 0.001), lower waist AR (94.6% ± 2.8% vs 98.2% ± 1.0%; P < 0.001), lower normalized GOA (88.9% ± 0.8% vs 89.5% ± 0.7%; P < 0.01), and higher maximum stress (2.81 ± 0.44 vs 2.29 ± 0.25 MPa; P < 0.001) on average compared to trileaflet patients, respectively. Within the BAV group, calcium volume was correlated with waist AR (r = −0.75, P < 0.001), normalized GOA (r = −0.68, P < 0.001), and maximum stress (r = 0.66, P < 0.001).ConclusionsBalloon-expandable TAVR for BAV has worse simulated mechanical metrics compared to TAVR for trileaflet aortic valve patients and is associated with high calcium volume.Key words: bicuspid aortic valve, calcific aortic valve disease, computational modeling, transcatheter aortic valve replacement

Bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) disease is the most common congenital heart valve defect and has a strong association with aortic stenosis (AS) typically caused by calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD).1,2 Although BAV patients usually present at a younger age and are often treated with surgical aortic valve replacement, transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) is a promising alternative in selected patients with BAV. However, due to anatomical differences with trileaflet AS, not all BAV patients are suitable for TAVR and this population has generally been excluded from low-risk trials.3, 4, 5

BAV patient registries and meta-analysis have shown similar outcomes in TAVR for trileaflet vs BAV patients.6, 7, 8, 9 However, it has also been shown BAV patients with excess raphe calcification have higher mortality after 2 years compared to patients with minimal calcification.10 This is likely attributed to higher rates of moderate-severe paravalvular leak and aortic root injury.11 In addition, many BAV anatomies are prone to causing asymmetrical device configurations due to heavy and asymmetrical calcification and elliptical openings of the native valve. Asymmetrical deployments are defined by ellipticity, underexpansion, tilt with respect to the native annulus, and off-centered with respect to the native annulus.12, 13, 14 Asymmetrical deployments have been associated with hypoattenuated leaflet thickening , which is associated with thrombosis, and adverse hemodynamics.12,15, 16, 17 However, it is unknown how these asymmetrical deployments impact the transcatheter heart valve (THV) durability.

Thus, there remains limited clinical knowledge on the durability of the THV bioprosthetic leaflets post-TAVR in BAV patients compared to trileaflet aortic valve patients. Detailed study of the mechanics of the bioprosthetic leaflets in patient-specific configurations may provide more insight into the risk of valve failure.18 In the absence of clinical data, simulation techniques with high-resolution computed tomography (CT) scans can produce patient-specific models19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26 which can output a range of various mechanical metrics such as THV underexpansion, ellipticity, systolic opening area, and maximum bioprosthetic leaflet stress which are thought to be associated with THV failure.27, 28, 29 The purpose of this study was to compare simulated post-TAVR bioprosthetic leaflet mechanical metrics in trileaflet vs BAV patients.

Methods

Study population

Pre-TAVR CT scans of the following 2 cohorts were collected from Piedmont Hospital: patients with BAV and those with trileaflet aortic valves. All study material was collected under Institutional Review Board–approved protocols. All CT scans were taken in 2021 using standard pre-TAVR protocol of contrast enhanced multiphase chest CT with 0.6 mm slice thickness (Siemens SOMATOM Force). Initial radiologist pre-TAVR evaluation was performed including assessment of CAVD, aortic valve morphology determination, and annulus area measurement. Only patients with CAVD were used and were separated by morphology, consecutive scans were extracted for simulation analysis consisting of 31 patients with BAV morphology and 25 with trileaflet aortic valve morphology. Scans with insufficient contrast or significant motion artifacts were excluded due to inability to perform 3D reconstruction of the anatomy resulting in 25 BAV patient CTs and 22 trileaflet aortic valve CTs included in the study.

Simulation workflow

CTs were processed through a defined workflow. This involved 1) anatomical segmentation with 3D reconstruction, 2) simulated deployment of a balloon-expandable (BE) THV stent deployment, and 3) simulated bioprosthetic leaflet pressurization followed by measurement extraction (Figure 1).

Case Processing Workflow

Anatomical reconstruction was performed using threshold based techniques to capture the aortic root, left ventricle, ascending aorta, aortic valve leaflets, and calcium nodules (left), TAVR was then simulated deploying a BE THV stent inside the native aortic valve anatomy (middle), and the mechanics of the leaflets were simulated through cardiac pressurization to analyze the systolic GOA and diastolic stress (right). BE = balloon-expandable; GOA = geometric orifice area; TAVR = transcatheter aortic valve replacement; THV = transcatheter heart valve.

Anatomical reconstruction

Each case was segmented in Materialise Mimics Innovation Suite 23.0 ensuring accurate reconstruction of the aortic root, left ventricle, and ascending aorta including detailed anatomical features such as native leaflet shapes, leaflet thicknesses, raphe structures, and calcium distributions using the same segmentation procedure used previously.21 The thresholds used for segmenting the root and calcium were patient specific and based on the Hounsfield Unit (HU) CT histogram by isolating the range of HU values for different regions. The histogram peak representing the blood volume was used as the primary boundary with the lower HU side base being the minimum root threshold and the higher side base being the minimum calcium threshold with both parts having a maximum threshold equal to the highest intensity pixel (Figure 1). Patient-specific thresholding must be used due to patient-specific variability in the pixel intensities likely caused by variability in the concentration of contrast dye in the blood. Following thresholding, the parts were manually cleaned to isolate the region of interest. Thresholding alone is not accurate in isolating the leaflets thus the boundary was drawn manually using the software segmentation tools. Previously, this threshold based technique has been shown to result in acceptable operator variability.30 For bicuspid cases, the Sievers type was assessed and the commissural angle and calcium volume were measured. The Sievers type was determined by a radiologist during the pre-TAVR workup with all further anatomical segmentation and analysis performed by 1 trained graduate student operator.

Stent deployment simulation

All simulations were performed in Abaqus 2021. A model of the SAPIEN 3 (Edwards Lifesciences) BE THV for the 23, 26, and 29 mm, that was previously developed, was used for this study which was shown to have 2.5% average error in waist expansion ratio (ER) in a series of post-CTs following TAVR in BAV.19 The model assumes the same calcium and aortic root material properties across all patients due to the inability to measure patient-specific material properties. In this study, the THV stent was deployed following the manufacturer’s indications for the use consisting of sizing based on annulus area, nominal balloon volume, and 20% of the valve height positioned below the annulus and 80% of the valve height positioned above the annulus. The ER and aspect ratio (AR) at the stent waist were measured post deployment. ER is the ratio of the diameter based area with the nominal area (Equation 1) and AR is the ratio of the minimum diameter with the maximum diameter (Equation 2) and have both been used previously to quantify the THV expansion.21

Bioprosthetic leaflet model

A detailed description of the bioprosthetic leaflet model development and validation can be found in the supplementary material. In summary, computer-aided design models of the bioprosthetic leaflet were created based on micro-CT scans of the actual device (Supplemental Figure 1). Following stent deployment, a mapping process was used to deform the attached edge of each bioprosthetic leaflet onto the corresponding attached edge on the deformed stent (Supplemental Figure 1). After displacement, the leaflets were pressurized to simulate both systolic and diastolic configurations by applying a physiological pressure curve starting with ramping to systolic pressure to open the leaflets followed by ramping to diastolic pressure to close the leaflets (Supplemental Figure 3). Since the leaflets quickly stabilize under diastolic pressure, the entire diastolic phase was not simulated with an assumption that 0.2 s was sufficient. The material properties used were a second order Ogden isotropic hyperelastic model used previously for modeling bioprosthetic bovine leaflets derived from mechanical stress testing.31

The maximum principal stress in diastole was recorded ignoring any elements within 3 elements from any boundary conditions. The systolic geometric orifice area (GOA) was measured by recording the node locations at the free edges of the leaflets then in Matlab R2021a creating a surface from the closed contour of all 3 leaflets and finally using a surface integral to solve for the area of the opening. For comparison between cases, the GOA was normalized using the nominal area calculated from the device size. Qualitative agreement was found in the leaflets systolic and diastolic shapes when compared to the device placed in a custom left heart flow loop for the SAPIEN 23 mm (Supplemental Figure 4).

Statistical analysis

The simulation outputs, consisting of the stent ER and AR at the stent waist and the bioprosthetic leaflet maximum diastolic stress and the systolic GOA, were compared between the bicuspid group and the trileaflet control. Continuous variables are reported as the mean ± SD and categorical variables as count (percentage). Normal distribution of continuous variables was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared using a 2-sided t-test for equal variances or a 2-sided Welch’s t-test for unequal variances, whereas categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher exact test (expected cell count <5) as applicable. In addition, a repeated univariable analysis was performed within the bicuspid group to test for associations between the anatomical parameters, consisting of the calcium volume, presence of a raphe, and the commissural angle, and the simulation outputs consisting of stent waist ER, stent waist AR, bioprosthetic leaflet systolic GOA, and bioprosthetic leaflet diastolic maximum stress for a total of 12 comparisons. Association of continuous variables was evaluated using linear regression to obtain the correlation coefficient (r). A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, and a Bonferroni correction factor was applied to the multiple correlation analysis to reduce the risk of type I error. All statistical analysis was performed using JMP Pro (version 16, JMP Statistical Discovery LLC).

Results

Patient characteristics

The patient population characteristics are shown in Table 1. The trileaflet group had a higher age compared to the BAV group with averages of 79 ± 9 years and 67 ± 11 years, respectively (P < 0.001). Diabetes rate was found to be higher in the trileaflet group compared to the BAV group with rates of 40.9% and 8.0%, respectively (P = 0.006). Calcium volume was found to be lower in the trileaflet group compared to the BAV group with averages of 265.3 ± 230.7 mm3 and 556.7 ± 533.9 mm3, respectively (P = 0.019). The average annulus area in the trileaflet group was lower than the bicuspid group with averages of 450 ± 95.7 mm2 and 513.9 ± 131.9 mm2, respectively; however, this was not statistically significant (P = 0.067). No differences were found in sex (54.5% male vs 64.0% male; P = 0.51), body mass index (29.5 ± 6.1 kg/m2 vs 29.1 ± 4.3 kg/m2; P = 0.79), hypertension (90.9% vs 72.0%; P = 0.10), atrial fibrillation (22.7% vs 8.0%; P = 0.16), or coronary artery disease (31.8% vs 32.0%; P = 0.99). The BAV group had 28.0%, 68.0%, and 4.0% Sievers type 0, type I, and type II, respectively, with a population commissure angle of 155.5 ± 16.4 degrees.

The bicuspid group resulted in lower mean waist ER (87.8% ± 2.6% vs 89.4% ± 1.4%) compared to the trileaflet group, respectively, with an absolute mean difference of 1.6% (95% CI: 0.4%-2.8%; P = 0.013), and lower mean waist AR (94.6% ± 2.8% vs 98.2% ± 1.0%) with an absolute mean difference of 3.6% (95% CI: 2.4%-4.8%; P < 0.001). The advantages of the trileaflet valve were predominantly in having lower average calcium volume and importantly having more uniform distributions of calcium normally in circular patterns with nodules commonly extending from the native commissures and around the free edge of the leaflet. In the trileaflet morphology, the THV stent was able to push the calcium upward and radially to conform to the overall circular distribution. However, in the bicuspid morphology, the valve shape was often elliptical and the resistance of calcium was higher due to the increased volume and radially facing nodules when a raphe was present, resulting in deformed stent configurations. The bicuspid group resulted in lower mean normalized GOA (88.9% ± 0.8% vs 89.5% ± 0.7%) compared to the trileaflet group, respectively, with a mean absolute difference of 0.6% (95% CI: 0.2%-1.0%; P = 0.005). There was a larger range in the bicuspid distribution, similar to what was seen in the stent expansion outputs, indicating a significant impact of the precise bicuspid anatomical characteristics. This is further explored in the following sections.

Comparison within the bicuspid group

Example cases of native valves with high and low calcium volumes are shown in Figure 6. The low calcium volume case resulted in a cylindrical deployment with optimal bioprosthetic leaflet closure. Overall, the high calcium volume case was more deformed and had elevated bioprosthetic leaflet stress levels along with leaflet overlap and folding. Repeated univariable analysis comparing the simulation outputs to anatomical features revealed calcium volume was significantly associated with the waist AR (r = −0.75, P < 0.001), normalized GOA (r = −0.68, P < 0.001), and maximum stress (r = 0.66, P < 0.001) following Bonferroni correction for type I error (P < 0.004) (Supplemental Figure 5). No statistically significant correlations were found based on Sievers type; however, type 0 had greater AR and GOA and lower leaflet maximum stress on average. No statistically significant correlations were found with commissural angle. One outlier was removed from the repeated univariable analysis due to unicuspid morphology which was defined as having an additional fusion region between the 2 leaflets making it fundamentally different from the other type 0 or type I BAVs.

Simulation results of a bicuspid case with low calcium volume resulting in a circular stent profile (left) and with high calcium volume resulting in a deformed stent profile and elevated stress, overlap, and folding in the bioprosthetic leaflets (right).

Although overall calcium volume gave correlation to simulated outputs within the bicuspid group, the precise distribution of calcium was also important. Figure 7 demonstrates a case with significant underexpansion but with only slightly above average total calcium volume. The large radially pointing calcium nodule prevented the stent from expanding fully at the waist even though the native valve did not have extreme total calcium volume. Abnormal anatomical features also impacted the stent deformation such as additional fused regions making the native valve opening very small (Figure 8).

A native valve with a large radially facing calcium nodule causing adverse stent waist underexpansion due to the resistance of the calcium nodule. This demonstrates the importance of not only calcium volume but shape and distribution on its impact on stent expansion in the model.

Native Valve With Additional Cusp Fusion of the Fused Leaflets

The additional fusion makes the native valve opening relatively small causing significant underexpansion of the stent in the simulation.

With respect to the bioprosthetic leaflet pressurization, the elevated deformity in the bicuspid THV deployment caused adverse bioprosthetic leaflet closure. The elliptical shape of the valve caused 1 leaflet to be stretched along the long-axis of the ellipse whereas the adjacent leaflets shared the opposite side giving them a decreased circumferential length. This caused the stretched leaflet to overlap with the other leaflets at the coaptation region under diastolic pressure which in turn raised the stress levels at the adjacent leaflets commissures due to the increased downward pressure. In addition, because the stent was underexpanded at the waist, the region of the leaflet between the commissure and the base had a decreased radius causing folding of the leaflet.

Discussion

This study provides insight into the impact of BAV on THV stent expansion with a potential detrimental downstream effect on leaflet function and stress which have the potential to alter long-term valve function (Central Illustration). Worsened stent deformation, elevated bioprosthetic leaflet stress, and lower GOA may reduce the device lifespan compared to trileaflet aortic valve patients. Close attention to calcification severity and patterns will allow for more informed planning of TAVR for BAV patients to avoid significant waist underexpansion. Large radially pointing calcium nodules and eccentric nonuniform features of the anatomy represent the biggest obstacle to complete cylindrical expansion and optimized bioprosthetic leaflet mechanics.

Patient characteristics

The studied patient population of trileaflet aortic valve patients were representative of the general population of trileaflet patients treated with TAVR with respect to age, sex, and device sizing.1 Comparatively, the BAV group was younger, had more males, and had larger annulus areas on average which is consistent with known BAV characteristics.32,33 The Sievers type distribution was also consistent with the general population of predominantly type I.34

Stent and leaflet mechanics

The BETTER TAVR registry of BAV patients with post-TAVR CT scans reported an average relative expansion percent (MinimumAreaOutflowArea+InflowArea2) of 88.1% which is comparable to the average ER found in this study (87.8%), although the metrics have slightly different definitions.35 Direct quantitative comparison of waist expansion/deformation in bicuspids compared to trileaflet morphologies has not been reported previously; however, there have been qualitative reports showing visible differences in stent ellipticity and waist underexpansion which align with the results presented in this study.13,14,36

Leaflet mechanics in bicuspid patients has not been reported previously in a patient-specific study. Leaflet mechanics in elliptical configurations was consistent with previous findings by Sun et al.25 in a nonpatient-specific simulation of elliptical bioprosthetic leaflet pressurization demonstrating elevated stress levels at the commissures and leaflet overlap compared to a circular configuration consistent with results presented in this study. Simulated bioprosthetic leaflet stress has been used historically to approximate the risk of leaflet mechanical failure where geometric and material property changes to the stent and leaflets have been performed to minimize stress.36, 37, 38, 39 Results from this study indicate that due to the geometric differences in the final THV deployment in trileaflet vs bicuspid patients, we see a difference in the average maximum stress. However, precise values of maximum stress in the SAPIEN leaflets which lead to mechanical failure has yet to be determined. With respect to the simulated leaflet systolic GOA, although the findings were statistically significant, they may not be clinically meaningful due to the very small difference in magnitude.

Based on the presented data, a simple calcium volume cutoff of >750 mm3 in the device landing zone may be used in its association with worsened simulated durability metrics. However, close attention to calcification severity and patterns will allow for more informed planning of TAVR for BAV patients to avoid significant waist underexpansion rather than looking at the calcium volume alone. Large radially pointing calcium nodules, unicuspid morphology, and eccentric nonuniform features of the anatomy contain the biggest challenges in having a fully expanded cylindrical expansion. Further correlation with clinical outcomes will provide more concrete relationships between anatomical features, TAVR deformation, and structural durability.

Due to the complexity of identifying these features and correlating them to simulated metrics, an alternative could be in the use of the simulated metrics directly as part of pre-TAVR planning rather than attempting to analyze each calcium nodule in detail and correlating it to stent expansion. The model currently has manual anatomical analysis with a semiautomated simulation workflow. If used prospectively, the return time would likely be a few days and require engineer labor time and computational resources. Commercially, the use of similar models has already been implemented and made available for clinical use such as DASI Simulations Precision TAVI and FEops HEARTguide which offers simulated visual predictions of stent expansions but lacks bioprosthetic leaflet mechanics predictions.40,41 These models could also be useful in performing device design iterations in optimizing the simulated mechanical metrics by performing the same model processing with different stent and leaflet geometries.

Study limitations

Limitations of the study population include the relatively low sample size which gives the potential that the trileaflet population was skewed and/or missing some representations such as heavy calcium or other problematic calcium distributions (maximum calcium volume of 776.4 mm3 in the trileaflet group). This is potentially more significant in the bicuspid group which has more known variations in patient characteristics, meaning there are likely patients with abnormal or abstract anatomical features not included in this study which may be relevant to broader clinical treatment of bicuspid patients with TAVR. The unicuspid case was excluded from the repeated univariable analysis due to having fundamental differences in the anatomy making it not be a representative sample of a type 0 or type I BAV. The small native opening of the leaflets caused the stent to have poor expansion in the model even though the calcium volume was relatively low. Further investigation into this patient group should be performed independently with more samples similar to it to fully understand how the stent expansion is impacted from the small native opening.

Some limitations of the model exist. The stent deployment validation was based on a study of 8 post-CT scans which had an average error of 2.5% in waist expansion where this residual error is expected in the presented study along with additional error accumulated from potentially unvalidated anatomical characteristics.21 This error in the stent simulation may contribute to the error in the leaflet simulation as the leaflet attached edge is determined by the stent expansion where if the stent is more or less expanded, at the waist in particular, it may alter the stress during diastolic pressurization and the opening achievable during systolic pressurization. Another limitation in the bioprosthetic leaflet pressurization model is that it is assessing for the function of the leaflets without using a fluid-based solver. Relying on an entirely structural simulation may result in simplifications of the mechanics of the valve opening and closing compared to using fluid pressure gradients to drive structural deformation which is a much more complex mechanism. This may have more impact during the heavily dynamic portions of the simulation such as opening and closing and may not have as much impact while the leaflet is under constant pressure and relatively static during the late diastolic portion of the simulation. Clinical alterations to sizing, positioning, or balloon filling volumes are also not considered where it is common in BAV patients to consider strategies outside of the manufacturer instructions for use or conventional trileaflet treatment strategies.

Future studies

Future clinical studies can include a randomized trial comparing long-term TAVR lifespan in trileaflet vs BAVs. Future engineering studies can be conducted further investigating the structural durability of asymmetric deployments. Benchtop accelerated fatigue testers are commonly used when long-term clinical data are unavailable.8 Studies can investigate variable transcatheter aortic valve (TAV) eccentricities and underexpansions on how they relate to the fatigue tester number of cycles until failure. The bioprosthetic leaflet mechanics can also be further investigated with the suggested configurations in a benchtop setting where leaflet diastolic pinwheeling and systolic GOA can be measured as a function of eccentricity and underexpansion. Molds or 3D prints of patient-specific segmentations have also been used previously and could prove useful for further studies of benchtop TAV deployment.42, 43, 44

This study only considered the use of BE THVs where self-expanding THVs are also used to treat patients with BAVs with AS. There is some existing clinical experience comparing the 2 device designs in BAVs, Deutsch et al. showed, in a single-center study, similar performance between the device designs with hemodynamics being more favorable with the self-expanding THVs but higher average rates of paravalvular leak and pacemaker (n = 106).45 A future simulation study could analyze the BE and self-expanding THV-simulated mechanical metrics using similar methods presented in this study to directly compare the 2 devices in the same patient anatomies.

Conclusions

In this study using computational modeling, patients with BAVs undergoing TAVR had worse simulated mechanical metrics compared to patients with trileaflet aortic valves. This appeared to be driven by the presence of excessive calcification or abnormal anatomical features where not all BAVs have these characteristics. This provides preliminary simulation-based insights as the first investigative step and not a definitive evidence of reduced durability. Further studies are required to understand the implications of the present findings on in vivo THV durability and, in the future, may help identify which BAV patients may be susceptible of reduced TAVR durability.
