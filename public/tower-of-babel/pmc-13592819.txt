Initiating Apomorphine Therapy Without Antiemetic Pretreatment: Real-World Data From a Nurse-led Parkinson Disease Support Program
Authors: Cindy Happel, Mindy Grall, Andrea Formella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Trimethobenzamide has been the antiemetic of choice in the United States for people with Parkinson disease initiating subcutaneous apomorphine injection (SC-APO) therapy to rapidly manage OFF episodes. Following a cessation of trimethobenzamide production in 2021, SC‑APO was increasingly initiated without antiemetic pretreatment.The US labeling was subsequently updated in 2022 to recommend a lower SC-APO starting dose when initiating treatment without trimethobenzamide.METHODS:Data from the SC-APO Clinical Nurse Navigator support program were analyzed for new patient starts between January 2019 and March 2024, spanning periods before and after trimethobenzamide absence and SC-APO labeling changes. SC-APO starting dose patterns and 90-day treatment continuation were compared between patients who started with and without antiemetic pretreatment.RESULTS:Among 2634 patients, SC-APO initiations without trimethobenzamide increased from 32.8% before cessation of production (2019-2020) to nearly 100% by the end of 2021. During 2021, most prescribers (56.7%) continued to use the 0.2 mL (2.0 mg) starting dose; following the 2022 labeling update, use of the 0.1 mL (1.0 mg) starting dose increased, reaching 86% by early 2024. The 90-day SC-APO continuation rates increased from 75.4% when used with trimethobenzamide pretreatment to 83.1% without it.CONCLUSION:SC-APO is now routinely started without antiemetic pretreatment, with higher overall rates of early treatment continuation. Flexible starting doses and titration, together with nurse‑led patient education and support, may facilitate successful treatment initiation and continuation in contemporary clinical practice.

Managing motor fluctuations remains a major challenge in the long-term management of Parkinson disease (PD). Oral PD therapies, including levodopa, can have an unpredictable onset due to dietary interactions and delayed gastric emptying, contributing to OFF episodes (periods of reduced mobility due to waning medication effect) and reduced quality of life.1 Subcutaneous apomorphine injections (SC-APO, Apokyn®) bypass the gastrointestinal tract and provide rapid relief of OFF symptoms, with clinical benefit typically observed within 10 minutes.2–4 Once an effective dose is established, substantial dose adjustments during maintenance therapy are uncommon in clinical practice.3,5

Apomorphine is a potent, nonselective dopamine agonist with affinity across all dopamine receptors.6 As with other dopaminergic therapies, apomorphine may cause nausea and vomiting during treatment initiation, and antiemetic pretreatment with trimethobenzamide has been standard practice in the United States (US).7 However, cessation of oral trimethobenzamide production in 2021 created uncertainty regarding how best to initiate SC‑APO. In response, a “low and slow” titration strategy (similar to that used for other dopamine agonists) was proposed.8 Informed by prior studies, including early data from the present analysis,7,9 the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved an update to the SC-APO label in 2022 to accommodate starting without trimethobenzamide. Prescribers are now advised to consider initiating SC-APO injections using a 0.1 mL (1.0 mg) instead of a 0.2 mL (2.0 mg) starting dose and then titrating based on individual patient tolerability and effect.10,11 To examine outcomes of SC‑APO starts with and without trimethobenzamide, we conducted a retrospective drug utilization review of real‑world data from the SC-APO Clinical Nurse Navigator (CNN) program across the years before and after the oral trimethobenzamide shortage and subsequent SC-APO labeling update.

Background

PD is the second most common neurodegenerative condition worldwide, with ~1.2 million people affected in the United States.12,13 Although most patients initially respond well to oral dopaminergic replacement therapy, the majority of levodopa‑treated patients eventually develop motor fluctuations associated with functional impairment and reduced quality of life.1 SC-APO has been used in the United States for over 2 decades as an on-demand therapy for OFF episodes that emerge between scheduled doses of PD medications. The use of antiemetic pretreatment with SC‑APO originated from empirical experience in the United Kingdom, where the peripherally acting dopamine antagonist domperidone was used to prevent apomorphine‑induced nausea and vomiting.14 This practice carried into US studies where trimethobenzamide was used in place of domperidone.15–17

Successful initiation of therapies for advancing PD depends not only on medication‑related factors but also on the availability of structured clinical support, especially during treatment initiation. Nursing involvement plays a central role in patient education, expectation setting, and early treatment success.18,19 In the United States, patients prescribed SC-APO are offered access to a specialized clinical nurse navigator (CNN) program. This manufacturer-sponsored program is delivered through a third-party contractor and staffed by registered nurses with experience in PD. Operating within regulatory compliance constraints, CNNs do not make independent clinical decisions, but rather provide structured education intended to support and facilitate in-home SC-APO initiation and ongoing use. In this context, education includes training in self-administration, discussion of potential adverse events and mitigation strategies, guidance on medication maintenance use, proper storage of medication, and assistance with ordering supplies and refills. CNNs also reinforce the importance of communication between patients and their health care providers (HCPs) and support the implementation of prescriber-directed dosing and titration plans. When trimethobenzamide is prescribed as part of the treatment initiation regimen, CNNs review its appropriate use with the patient and reinforce prescriber instructions before SC‑APO initiation.

Methods

Patients prescribed SC-APO sign an authorization form consenting to participation in the CNN program. This allows for the release of health information necessary to provide CNN support services, and the conduct of data analytics. Ethics committee approval was not required for this analysis, as it involved a summary of an existing database without additional patient contact or identifiable personal data.

Data were extracted from the CNN database for new patient SC-APO starts between January 1, 2019 and March 31, 2024. This includes ~2 years before the cessation of trimethobenzamide production and 21 months following the FDA-labeling change for SC-APO injection. For each new patient, data were collected on completion or attempted completion of a trimethobenzamide reminder phone call (indicating trimethobenzamide was dispensed), the prescribed starting dose of SC‑APO, and whether SC‑APO was discontinued within 90 days of treatment initiation. Discontinuation was determined based on review of CNN data entries captured through September 30, 2024.

As specific reasons for discontinuation are not captured in the CNN database, all‑cause discontinuation within 90 days was considered to be a reasonable surrogate measure of SC‑APO initiation tolerability because—despite routine antiemetic use—nausea and vomiting were among the more common adverse event-related reasons for discontinuation in clinical trials (~5.5%).5,14,20 No a priori statistical analyses were planned for this analysis. To explore confounding influences, post hoc odds ratios were calculated to evaluate the association between trimethobenzamide use and 90-day treatment continuation. Analyses were conducted in 2 settings: limited to the years in which trimethobenzamide was available, and across all calendar years. Logistic regression models were used with 90‑day continuation as the outcome, SC‑APO starting dose and calendar year as covariates, and trimethobenzamide use and, in the overall analysis, trimethobenzamide availability as fixed effects.

Results

Data were available for 2634 patients. Of these, 1532 (58.2%) initiated SC‑APO before 2021; 397 (15.1%) initiated treatment in 2021 (the year trimethobenzamide production ceased); and 705 (26.8%) initiated treatment after 2021. Before 2021, only 32.8% of patients initiated SC‑APO without trimethobenzamide pretreatment. This proportion doubled by mid‑2021 and reached 100% by 2022 (Fig 1a). Initiation dose selection remained similar across 2019-2021, [0.2 mL (2.0 mg)] for 59.3% of patients in 2019, 61.4% in 2020, and 56.7% in 2021 (Fig 1b). After the dosing update to the US Prescribing Information (implemented ~September 2022), use of the 0.1 mL (1.0 mg) starting dose rose to 64.8% of patient initiations in the last quarter of 2022, 70.2% of initiations in 2023, and 86.0% in the first quarter of 2024 (Fig 1b).

(b) The proportion (%) of patients prescribed each starting dose of SC-APO (0.1 mL [1 mg], 0.2 mL [2 mg], or other) before and after the cessation of trimethobenzamide production and subsequent FDA label update that was approved in June of 2022 and fully implemented by September 2022. Abbreviations: mg, milligram; mL, milliliter; n, total patients initiated on SC-APO by quarter in the database; Q, quarter (3-month interval); SC-APO, subcutaneous apomorphine injection.

Between 2019 and 2021, the 90‑day continuation rates were similar for SC‑APO initiations conducted with versus without trimethobenzamide [75.4% vs. 76.6% of new patient starts; post hoc adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of 0.970 (95% CI, 0.777-1.212; P=0.79)]. The 90‑day continuation increased to 90.1% of new patient starts between January 1, 2022 and March 31, 2024 (Fig 2). Across the full study period, the 90‑day continuation was 75.4% for SC‑APO starts conducted with trimethobenzamide versus 83.2% without trimethobenzamide; post hoc analysis yielded an aOR of 0.95 (95% CI, 0.76-1.19; P=0.67).

Discussion

The findings of this analysis suggest that, when supported by a structured nurse program including targeted patient education and an SC‑APO initiation strategy designed to mitigate nausea—antiemetic pretreatment is not essential for successful SC‑APO initiation. In addition to informing the implemented label change, these findings have direct implications for contemporary PD nursing practice, highlighting the role of nurses in assessing educational needs, delivering tailored education, and supporting safe and tolerable initiation of therapy.

Nurses have long played a central role in initiating, titrating, and monitoring dopaminergic therapies, particularly in the context of adjunctive, advanced, and device‑aided therapies.21 The present study exemplifies how the use of real-world nursing data can inform practice change, quality improvement, and policy decisions. Analyses of the CNN dataset provided an important source of clinical evidence supporting changes to US prescribing information and demonstrated that the new labeling recommendations appeared effective in supporting successful SC-APO treatment initiation without antiemetic pretreatment in a setting that included CNN education, close monitoring with flexible dose titration driven by patient reports of tolerability and therapy effect, and ongoing communication between the patient and their HCP.

Apomorphine initiations are now routinely conducted without antiemetic pretreatment. Delphi consensus guidelines have also been produced to give guidance on how to design titration schedules based on individualized dose response.22 Although prompted by the prior lack of suitable alternatives to trimethobenzamide, our analysis supports continued use of a flexible SC-APO titration approach without antiemetic pretreatment, even now that trimethobenzamide has returned to limited availability as a generic formulation. Reviews of apomorphine use outside the US suggest that prophylactic antiemetic treatment is not always necessary or helpful when initiating SC-APO therapy.22,23,24 In addition to increasing medication burden and the potential for adverse effects, routine use of an oral antiemetic is impractical in the context of an on‑demand therapy such as SC‑APO, which is not necessarily used daily.

Trimethobenzamide has uncertain efficacy as an antiemetic, both in general and as a pretreatment for apomorphine.8,25 It was originally marketed in the late 1950s before the FDA required new drugs to show evidence of efficacy.8,25 Trimethobenzamide had an additional regulatory review in the 1970’s.25 This review found insufficient evidence for a broad efficacy claim, and restricted its approved uses to postoperative nausea and vomiting and nausea associated with gastroenteritis. Trimethobenzamide is associated with adverse events in patients with PD, including increased reports of somnolence, dizziness, and falls.7,8 The American Geriatrics Society has previously cautioned against its use in older individuals, suggesting that it is “one of the least effective antiemetic drugs, yet it can cause extrapyramidal adverse effects”.26 In addition, its lower oral bioavailability27 may pose problems for patients as PD is already associated with delays in medication absorption.

Recognizing the importance of patient education and psychological and social support in PD management, the CNN program was established to provide an additional layer of nurse‑led educational support in the U.S. where in‑home nursing services are less common.28,29 Instead of pharmacological prophylaxis, nursing education, delivered in coordination with prescribing HCPs, shifted toward the potential benefits of a “low and slow” approach, early recognition of intolerance, and reassurance about transient adverse effects. Studies with continuously infused apomorphine show that extra support in the first months of treatment for patients experiencing initial difficulties can make the difference between long-term success and treatment discontinuation, and this is also the case with intermittent apomorphine injections.30 Apomorphine infusion therapy recently gained approval in the United States. Based on requests for services, the CNN program is proving pivotal in helping patients initiate and titrate this therapy at home, largely without antiemetic pretreatment.

Strengths of our analysis include comprehensive data capture for new patient starts, a large sample size, and a real‑world setting. The CNN database represents a unique and valuable real‑world data resource. Patients are given the opportunity to opt in to CNN services, and the results may not be generalizable to those who do not have access or who decline CNN program services. Limitations include the indirect assessment of antiemetic use and tolerability, and the absence of specific adverse event data in the CNN database. We cannot exclude other reasons for treatment attrition, such as device‑use challenges, cost considerations, or nongastrointestinal adverse effects. The lack of demographic data and prescriber information in the available database means that we cannot guarantee that the type of patients selected to receive SC-APO did not change based on trimethobenzamide availability. Nevertheless, post hoc aOR did not demonstrate an advantage associated with its use. Future studies should evaluate strategies for patient management in case clinically significant nausea or vomiting is observed, including slowing the titration schedule, lowering the SC-APO dose, or studying the utility of alternate antiemetic options.

Conclusion

These real‑world data demonstrate that, with structured nursing education and support, SC‑APO can be successfully initiated without antiemetic pretreatment. More than 80% of patients who initiated SC‑APO without trimethobenzamide prophylaxis remained on treatment at 90 days. These findings are particularly pertinent to nurses involved in PD care, as they provide evidence to support contemporary initiation strategies and inform clinical decision‑making.
