Semiconductor Wannier equations: a real- t ime, real-space a pproach to the nonlinear optical response in crys ta ls (ATATA)
Eduardo B. Molinero, Bruno Amorim, Misha Ivanov, Graham G. Brown, Giovanni Cistaro, João M. Viana Parente Lopes, Álvaro Jiménez-Galán, Pablo San-Jose, Rui E. F. Silva

Abstract
We develop the semiconductor Wannier equations (SWEs), a real-time, real-space formulation of ultrafast light-matter dynamics in crystals, by deriving the equations of motion for the electronic reduced density matrix in a localized Wannier basis. Working in real space removes the structure-gauge ambiguities that hinder reciprocal-space semiconductor Bloch equations. Electron–electron interactions are included at the time-dependent Hartree plus static screened-exchange (TD-HSEX) level. Decoherence is modeled with three complementary channels: pure dephasing, population relaxation, and distance-dependent real-space dephasing; providing physically grounded damping for strong-field phenomena such as high-harmonic generation. Conceptually, the SWEs bridge real-space semiclassical intuition with many-body solid-state optics, offering a numerically robust and gauge-clean alternative to reciprocal-space approaches for nonlinear optical response and attosecond spectroscopy in solids.



I
Introduction

In 2011, the discovery of high-harmonic generation (HHG) in solids

(
Ghimire et al. 2011
)
opened a new frontier in attosecond
science, extending concepts originally developed in atomic and molecular
systems
(
Krausz and Ivanov 2009
)
to the condensed matter domain

(
Kruchinin et al. 2018
;
Ghimire and Reis 2019
;
Heide et al. 2024
;
Cavaletto et al. 2025
)
.
This breakthrough demonstrated that high-order harmonics could be
generated directly from crystalline solids subjected to strong mid-infrared
laser fields, unveiling a new regime of ultrafast light-matter interaction
governed by the interplay of band structure
(
Vampa et al. 2015
)
,
Bloch electron dynamics
(
Ghimire et al. 2011
)
, and quantum
coherence
(
Floss et al. 2018
;
Brown et al. 2024a
)
.

Since then, high-harmonic spectroscopy has emerged as a powerful tool
to probe electronic and structural properties of solids on femtosecond
and attosecond timescales
(
Heide et al. 2024
)
. Applications
range from observation of Bloch oscillations
(
Ghimire et al. 2011
)
,
optical reconstruction of the bandstructure
(
Vampa et al. 2015
)
,
electron-hole dynamics
(
Hohenleutner et al. 2015
)
, probing of collective
excitations
(
Molinero et al. 2024
)
, imaging of van-Hove singularities

(
Uzan et al. 2020
)
, topological phase transitions
(
Silva et al. 2019a
)
,
and dynamics in strongly correlated materials
(
Silva et al. 2018
;
Murakami et al. 2018
)
,
to name a few.

The understanding of high-harmonic generation (HHG) in atoms and molecules
is deeply rooted in the semiclassical three-step model
(
Corkum 1993
;
Krause et al. 1992
)

and its quantum counterpart, the Lewenstein model
(
Lewenstein et al. 1994
)
.
These foundational works underpin the field of attosecond physics,
offering essential physical insight and establishing a clear connection
between HHG and classical electron trajectories
(
Smirnova and Ivanov 2014
;
Amini et al. 2019
)
.

Ab-initio
simulations, especially those solving the time-dependent
Schrödinger equation in real space where electron trajectories can
be directly visualized
(
Lara-Astiaso et al. 2016
)
, were instrumental
in identifying electron recollision as the primary mechanism behind
high-harmonic emission in the early days of attoscience
(
Krause et al. 1992
)
.

Several theoretical frameworks have been used to model HHG in solids,
including semiclassical approaches
(
Osika et al. 2017
;
Parks et al. 2020
)
,
time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT)
(
Tancogne-Dejean et al. 2017
;
Tancogne-Dejean and Rubio 2018
)
,
and the semiconductor Bloch equations (SBEs)
(
Lindberg and Koch 1988
;
Kira and Koch 2008
;
Kira and Koch 2011
;
Yue and Gaarde 2022
;
Gu and Kolesik 2022
;
Silva et al. 2019b
)
.
Among these approaches, the SBEs in the length gauge, where the coupling
to a homogeneous electric field is described via the scalar potential

\phi(\boldsymbol{r},t)=-\boldsymbol{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}\left(t\right)
,
have proven particularly successful in capturing the underlying physics
and providing valuable insight into the HHG process
(
Yue and Gaarde 2022
;
Parks et al. 2023
)
.

The advantages of the length gauge formulation of the SBEs when compared
to the velocity gauge are numerous
(
Yue and Gaarde 2022
;
Parks et al. 2023
)
.
First, less bands are required for the convergence of numerical simulations
when compared to velocity gauge
(
Yakovlev and Wismer 2017
;
Yue and Gaarde 2020
)
.
This is also the case in atomic and molecular targets, i.e., when
only bound-bound transitions are involved, the length gauge is preferable
over the velocity gauge
(
Kobe 1979
;
Bertolino et al. 2022
)
. Second,
the inclusion of dephasing is most transparently done in the length
gauge
(
Yue and Gaarde 2022
)
. Last, the decomposition of currents
into inter and intraband components is only natural when working in
the length gauge
(
Yue and Gaarde 2022
)
.

Despite its advantages, working in the length gauge presents a significant
challenge due to the fact that the position operator, which appears
in the light–matter interaction term, is ill-defined in a crystal
with periodic boundary conditions
(
Ventura 2021
)
. The
standard solution is to define the position operator in the thermodynamic
limit, which requires computing derivatives of the Bloch functions
with respect to Bloch’s crystal momentum
(
Blount 1962
)
.
The gauge freedom in defining the Bloch functions up to a random phase,
which we will refer to as
structure gauge
, poses severe difficulties
to the numerical solution of the SBEs
(
Jiang et al. 2018
;
Yue and Gaarde 2020
)
.
Solutions to this technical problem have been proposed in the literature,
such as the twisted parallel transport gauge
(
Yue and Gaarde 2020
)
,
the gauge invariant SBEs
(
Parks et al. 2023
;
Parks et al. 2024
)
, and the use
of maximally localized Wannier functions to construct a smooth gauge
in the reciprocal space
(
Silva et al. 2019b
)
.

In solid state systems, the seminal work of Vampa
et al

(
Vampa et al. 2014
)

introduced a semiclassical framework for HHG, emphasizing the role
of electron-hole pair trajectories in the process. However, most standard
theoretical tools available to describe electron dynamics in solids,
such as the SBE, are usually formulated in reciprocal space. A theoretical
toolbox formulated in real-space is essential to bridge the gap between
the real-space semiclassical picture and current theoretical methods.

In this work, we present an alternative formulation of the SBEs, the
semiconductor Wannier equations (SWEs), where the dynamical object
propagated in time is the one-electron reduced density matrix, represented
in a real-space basis of localized Wannier orbitals. We will show
that with this approach, the problems that stem from the
structure
gauge
freedom are absent. Furthermore, real-space dephasing to model
solid-state HHG makes the use of unphysically short dephasing times
unnecessary
(
Brown et al. 2024a
;
Brown et al. 2024b
)
. Also, the inclusion
of excitonic effects
(
Cistaro et al. 2022
)
is substantially
easier than in the SBEs formulation. Finally, in the context of solid-state
attoscience, we demonstrate that SWEs are significantly more efficient
than SBEs opening the way to simulate larger and complex systems.

From a conceptual standpoint, this work opens new avenues for exploring
theoretical quantities in real space to advance the interpretation
of solid-state HHG. It also contributes to bridging the gap between
the concepts developed in atomic and molecular attoscience and their
counterparts in the solid-state regime.

II
Theoretical Formalism

In this section, we will derive the equations of motion that form
the backbone of the semiconductor Wannier equations (SWEs). In the
following, we will use atomic units unless otherwise stated.

II.1
General formulation

II.1.1
Hamiltonian

We start by writing a general electronic Hamiltonian,
\hat{\mathcal{H}}\left(t\right)
,
that is the sum of a single-particle time-dependent Hamiltonian,
\hat{h}\left(t\right)=\hat{h}^{0}+\hat{U}\left(t\right)
,
and a time-independent electron-electron interaction term,
\hat{V}
.
In a second quantization formalism, we have that

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{ij}h_{ij}\left(t\right)c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}}_{\hat{h}\left(t\right)}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ijkl}V_{kl}^{ij}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{l}c_{k}}_{\hat{V}}

(1)

\displaystyle h_{ij}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=\int d\boldsymbol{r}\phi_{i}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{r}\right)\hat{h}\left(t\right)\phi_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{r}\right)

(2)

\displaystyle V_{kl}^{ij}

\displaystyle=\int d\boldsymbol{r}d\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\phi_{i}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{r}\right)\phi_{j}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right)v_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right)\phi_{l}\left(\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right)\phi_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{r}\right)

(3)

where
c_{i}^{\dagger}
(
c_{i}
) is the creation (annihilation)
operator for an electron in state
i
with wavefunction
\phi_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{r}\right)

and
v_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right)
is the electron-electron
interaction potential. Since the interaction potential is of the form,

v_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right)=v_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right)=v_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{r}\right)
,
the tensor
V_{kl}^{ij}
has the following symmetries:
V_{kl}^{ij}=V_{lk}^{ji}=\left(V_{ij}^{kl}\right)^{*}
.

In this work, the time-dependent perturbation,
\hat{U}\left(t\right)
,
will be a light-matter interaction term. Under the dipole approximation
and using the electromagnetic length gauge,
\hat{U}\left(t\right)

can be written as

\hat{U}\left(t\right)=-e\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}\left(t\right),

(4)

where
e=-1
is the electron charge,
\boldsymbol{E}\left(t\right)

is the applied homogeneous electric field and
\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}

is the position operator.

II.1.2
Observables and the one-electron reduced density matrix

In most of the cases, we are interested in calculating observables
that are single-particle operators, which have the general form
\hat{O}=\sum_{ij}O_{ij}c_{i}^{\dagger}(t)c_{j}(t)

(from hereinafter we will be using the Heisenberg picture). The expectation
value of a single-particle operator can be written as
\left\langle\hat{O}(t)\right\rangle=\sum_{ij}O_{ij}\rho_{ji}(t)=\mathrm{tr}\left(O\rho(t)\right)
,
where

\rho_{ij}(t)\equiv\left\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}(t)c_{i}(t)\right\rangle

(5)

is the one-electron reduced density matrix (1RDM). Note that the indexes
are switched.

Regarding optical response, two of the most crucial and important
observables are the position and velocity operators. The position
operator is just expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\boldsymbol{r}_{ij}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}

(6)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\int d\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\phi_{i}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right)\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\phi_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right).

(7)

The velocity operator,
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}
, can be calculated
using the Heisenberg equations of motion

\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}=-i\left[\hat{\boldsymbol{r}},\hat{\mathcal{H}}\left(t\right)\right].

(8)

The electron-electron interaction term,
\hat{V}
, is an operator
that only depend on the position of the electrons, therefore, it commutes
with
\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}
. Furthermore, if the time-dependent perturbation,

\hat{U}\left(t\right)
, also commutes with the position, as it is
the case when working under the dipole approximation in the length
gauge, the velocity operator can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\boldsymbol{v}_{ij}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}=-i\left[\hat{\boldsymbol{r}},\hat{h}^{0}\right]

(9)

where
\boldsymbol{v}_{ij}=-i\sum_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{ik}h_{kj}^{0}-h_{ik}^{0}\boldsymbol{r}_{kj}\right)

are the velocity matrix elements and
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\rangle=\mathrm{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{v}\rho\right)
.
It is important to emphasize that the calculation of the velocity
operator is done without assuming any approximations. In the following,
we will approximate the equations of motion by performing a mean-field
decoupling, effectively adding a self-energy term,
\Sigma
, to
h^{0}
.
One might be tempted to include in the definition of the velocity
operator this self-energy term,
\Sigma
. This would be incorrect,
however Eq (
9
) was derived without any approximation,
and it is the correct expression for
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}
even
in the presence of
\Sigma
.

II.1.3
Equations of motion for the one-electron reduced density matrix

Using the Heisenberg equation, we will determine the equation of motion
for the 1RDM,
\rho_{ij}(t)=\left\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}(t)c_{i}(t)\right\rangle
.
We have used
QuantumAlgebra.jl

(
Sánchez-Barquilla et al. 2020
;
Feist and contributors 2021
)

to deal with the quantum operator algebra. We have that (omitting
the time-argument for sinplicity)

\displaystyle-i\frac{d\left(c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{i}\right)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\left[\hat{\mathcal{H}}\left(t\right),c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{i}\right]

(10)

\displaystyle-i\frac{d\rho_{ij}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left[\hat{\mathcal{H}}\left(t\right),c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{i}\right]\right\rangle

(11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}h_{\alpha j}\left(t\right)\rho_{i\alpha}-\sum_{\alpha}h_{i\alpha}\left(t\right)\rho_{\alpha j}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma}V_{\gamma j}^{\alpha\beta}\left\langle c_{\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{\beta}^{\dagger}c_{\gamma}c_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma}V_{j\gamma}^{\alpha\beta}\left\langle c_{\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{\beta}^{\dagger}c_{\gamma}c_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma}V_{\beta\gamma}^{\alpha i}\left\langle c_{\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{\beta}c_{\gamma}\right\rangle

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma}V_{\beta\gamma}^{i\alpha}\left\langle c_{\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{\beta}c_{\gamma}\right\rangle.

We arrive to an equation of motion for the 1RDM that involves the
computation and evolution of the expectation value of four-operators,

\left\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{l}c_{k}\right\rangle
.
If we proceed to compute the equation of motion for the mean value
of four-operators, we will get equations involving mean values of
six-operators. If we proceed further, we end up with an infinite hierarchy
of operators, also know as

Bogoliubov–Born–Green–Kirkwood–Yvon
(BBGKY) hierarchy
(
Kira and Koch 2008
;
Kira and Koch 2011
;
Sánchez-Barquilla et al. 2020
)
.
In the following, we will use a mean-field decoupling to obtain a
close set of equations for the 1RDM.

II.1.4
Mean-field decoupling

To obtain a closed set of equations, we can apply a mean-field decoupling
to the
\left\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{l}c_{k}\right\rangle

terms, equivalent to a Hartree-Fock approximation. Specifically, we
will approximate
(
Bruus and Flensberg 2004
;
Ribeiro 2023
)

\displaystyle\left\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{l}c_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\left\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{k}\right\rangle\left\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{l}\right\rangle-\left\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{l}\right\rangle\left\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\rho_{ki}\rho_{lj}-\rho_{li}\rho_{kj}.

(12)

Note that in this decoupling, we neglect anomalous terms,
\left\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}\right\rangle

and
\left\langle c_{i}c_{j}\right\rangle
, that may be relevant
to superconductivity
(
Lobo et al. 2025
)
. Replacing this
decoupling in the equation of motion for the 1RDM, Eq. (
11
),
we obtain the equation of motion for the 1RDM within the time-dependent
Hartree-Fock approximation

i\frac{d\rho_{ij}}{dt}=\left[h\left(t\right),\rho\right]_{ij}+\left[\Sigma^{F}\left[\rho\right],\rho\right]_{ij}+\left[\Sigma^{H}\left[\rho\right],\rho\right]_{ij}

(13)

where
\Sigma^{F}
and
\Sigma^{H}
are the Fock and Hartree self-energies
(mean-field potentials) and are defined as

\displaystyle\Sigma^{F}\left[\rho\right]_{ij}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{kl}V_{kj}^{il}\rho_{kl}

(14)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{H}\left[\rho\right]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{kl}V_{jk}^{il}\rho_{kl}.

(15)

Up to this point,
\hat{h}^{0}
has been treated as a purely non-interacting
Hamiltonian. However, in practical calculations,
\hat{h}^{0}
, and
therefore the equilibrium RDM
1
1

1

The equilibrium RDM is just
\rho^{0}=F_{\mu,T}\left(h^{0}\right)
,
being
F_{\mu,T}
the Fermi function for chemical potential
\mu

and temperature
T
. In the eigenbasis of
\hat{h}^{0}\left|\varepsilon_{i}\right\rangle=\varepsilon_{i}\left|\varepsilon_{i}\right\rangle
,

\rho_{ij}^{0}=\delta_{ij}F_{\mu,T}\left(\varepsilon_{i}\right)
.
,
\rho^{0}
, are obtained from either tight-binding parametrizations
of the experimental equilibrium dispersion or from different levels
of
ab-initio
calculations, such as
GW

(
Martin et al. 2016
)

or hybrid DFT calculations
(
Martin 2020
)
. In both
cases, the resulting
\hat{h}^{0}
already includes some level of
electron-electron interactions, in the form of a single-particle self-energy

\Sigma_{0}
at equilibrium. Therefore, in the equation of motion
Eq. (
13
) we must subtract the equilibrium ground-state
self-energy,
\Sigma_{0}=\Sigma^{F}\left[\rho_{0}\right]+\Sigma^{H}\left[\rho_{0}\right]
,
to avoid double counting.

Secondly, the Fock self-energy neglects important screening effects,
that are usually taken into account within a random-phase approximation
(RPA)
(
Bruus and Flensberg 2004
;
Martin et al. 2016
)
. As a result, the
interaction potential
V
in Eq. (
14
) is often replaced
with
W
, the RPA-screened interaction potential. Note that, this
argument does not apply to the Hartree self-energy since RPA screening
would result in diagrams that are not single-particle irreducible,
leading to double-counting of screening. The screened interaction

W
is, in general, a dynamical quantity,
W(\omega)
, as it depends
on the state of the system. However, in order to keep the equations
of motion local in time we will employ a common approximation, known
as static exchange (SEX), by taking the static limit, i.e.
W\approx W(\omega\rightarrow 0)

(
Martin et al. 2016
)
. Therefore, our final equation of motion
will read

\displaystyle i\frac{d\rho_{ij}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\left[h\left(t\right)+\Sigma^{HSEX}\left[\rho\right]-\Sigma_{0},\rho\right]_{ij},

(16)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{SEX}\left[\rho\right]_{ij}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{kl}W_{kj}^{il}\rho_{kl},

(17)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{HSEX}\left[\rho\right]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{SEX}\left[\rho\right]_{ij}+\Sigma^{H}\left[\rho\right]_{ij},

(18)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{HSEX}\left[\rho^{0}\right].

(19)

The
\Sigma_{0}
term is chosen to be the full self-energy at equilibrium,
regardless of the level of approximation used to compute
h^{0}

and
\rho^{0}
. This will ensure that
\rho^{0}
, the equilibrium
1RDM, will remain a stationary solution of the equations of motion,
in the absence of driving. This approach is sometimes known in the
literature as TD-BSE
(
Attaccalite et al. 2011
)
or TD-HSEX
(
Sangalli et al. 2018
;
Sangalli 2021
;
Sangalli et al. 2023
)
.

The specific form of
V_{kl}^{ij}
and
W_{kl}^{ij}
can be simplified
when working with a localized basis, as occur in a tight binding formulation.
Specifically, assuming that the orbitals
\phi_{i}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{r}\right)

are strongly localized around the center of the orbital,
\bm{\tau}_{i}=\left\langle i\left|\boldsymbol{r}\right|i\right\rangle
,
we can approximate

\displaystyle V_{kl}^{ij}

\displaystyle=\int d\boldsymbol{r}d\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\phi_{i}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{r}\right)\phi_{j}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right)V\left(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right)\phi_{l}\left(\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right)\phi_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{r}\right)

\displaystyle\approx V\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{j}\right)\int d\boldsymbol{r}d\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\phi_{i}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{r}\right)\phi_{j}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right)\phi_{l}\left(\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right)\phi_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{r}\right),

(20)

which is a valid approximation, provided
V\left(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right)

does not vary strongly within typical size of an orbital. If we further
recall the ortogonality of Wannier orbitals,
\int d\boldsymbol{r}\phi_{i}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{r}\right)\phi_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{r}\right)=\delta_{ik}
,
we obtain

V_{kl}^{ij}\approx\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}V\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{j}\right)

(21)

where
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{i},\,\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{j}

are the centers of the orbitals
i
and
j
. In the same way,

W_{kl}^{ij}\approx\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}W\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{j}\right).

(22)

We refer to this as the ultra-localized orbital approximation
(
Ribeiro 2023
)
.
In the same way,

W_{kl}^{ij}\approx\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}W\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{j}\right).

(23)

Within this approximation, we can then calculate
\Sigma^{SEX}\left[\rho\right]

and
\Sigma^{H}\left[\rho\right]
as

\displaystyle\Sigma^{SEX}\left[\rho\right]_{ij}

\displaystyle\approx-W\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{j}\right)\rho_{ij},

(24)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{H}\left[\rho\right]_{ij}

\displaystyle\approx\delta_{ij}\sum_{k}\rho_{kk}V\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{k}\right).

(25)

When working with spin-degenerate models, we must multiply the Hartree
term,
\Sigma^{H}\left[\rho\right]
, by 2 since it depends on the
total charge density. Therefore,

\Sigma^{H}\left[\rho\right]_{ij}\approx\delta_{ij}\sum_{k}s_{k}\rho_{kk}V\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{k}\right)

(26)

where
s_{k}
takes into account the spin-degeneracy of the
k

orbital.

II.1.5
Decoherence

The equation of motion derived in the previous subsection, Eq. (
16
),
is purely coherent, with no dissipation effects. However, decoherence
plays a major role in the modelization of electronic dynamics in solids
under intense lasers, particularly in high harmonic generation
(
Vampa et al. 2014
;
Floss et al. 2018
;
Brown et al. 2024a
;
Brown et al. 2024b
)
.
To that purpose, we must include decoherence in our framework. We
will include three forms of decoherence: pure dephasing, relaxation
and real-space dephasing.

In the seminal work by Vampa
et al
(
Vampa et al. 2014
)
, it
was found that HHG spectrum obtained by solving the SBEs in ZnO was
very noisy and agreement with experiments was only achieved if ultrashort
dephasing times of few femtoseconds,
T_{2}\equiv\gamma_{D}^{-1}
,
were introduced in the simulations. Pure dephasing is introduced in
the basis that diagonalizes the unperturbed Hamiltonian,
\hat{h}^{0}\left|\varepsilon_{i}\right\rangle=\varepsilon_{i}\left|\varepsilon_{i}\right\rangle
.
The pure dephasing term in the equation of motion, in the eigenbasis
of
\hat{h}^{0}
, reads

\mathcal{L}_{D}\left[\rho\right]_{ij}=\gamma_{D}\left(\delta_{ij}-1\right)\rho_{ij}.

(27)

Despite pure dephasing being commonly used in numerical simulations
of HHG in solids, it does not account for population relaxation. To
that purpose, within the relaxation time approximation and assuming
that the relaxation rate is much smaller than the driving frequency,
we include relaxation to thermal equilibrium,
\rho^{0}

(
Terada et al. 2024
;
Cox and Javier García de
Abajo 2014
;
Sato and Rubio 2021
)
.
The relaxation term reads as

\mathcal{L}_{r}\left[\rho\right]=-\gamma_{r}\left(\rho-\rho_{0}\right),

(28)

where
\gamma_{r}\equiv\left(2T_{r}\right)^{-1}
is the relaxation
rate.

Recently, Graham
et al

(
Brown et al. 2024a
;
Brown et al. 2024b
)
introduced
an additional term that implements real-space dephasing where the
rate of dephasing depends on the distance between orbitals. In this
specific case, we must work in a basis where the single-particle orbitals
are well localized. Having
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}
as the center
of state
i
, we have that the real-space dephasing term reads as

\mathcal{L}_{rs}\left[\rho\right]_{ij}=-\gamma_{rs}\left(\left|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{j}\right|\right)\cdot\left(\rho-\rho^{0}\right)_{ij},

(29)

where
\gamma_{rs}\left(\left|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{j}\right|\right)

is a dephasing rate that depends on the distance between orbitals

i
and
j
and is zero at the origin,
\gamma_{rs}\left(0\right)=0
.
The functional form that will be used for
\gamma_{rs}
is a polynomial
function that only acts on coherences between states whose distance
is greater than
R_{rs,cut}
, i.e.

\gamma_{rs}\left(r\right)=\begin{cases}0&r<R_{rs,cut}\\
\beta_{rs}\left(r-R_{rs,cut}\right)^{\alpha_{rs}}&r\geq R_{rs,cut}\end{cases}

(30)

where
\alpha_{rs}
is the degree of the polynomial. This form of
real-space dephasing is similar to the one used in
(
Brown et al. 2024a
)

and resembles the complex absorbing potentials used in atomic and
molecular strong field calculations
(
Scrinzi 2010
;
Richter et al. 2013
;
Pedatzur et al. 2015
;
Silva et al. 2016
)
.

II.2
Crystalline systems

We are now in position to lay down the equations of motion for the
electronic RDM in a crystalline solid in real-space. Let us consider
an infinite crystalline solid with the Bravais lattice

\boldsymbol{R}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}n_{i}\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\ n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},

(31)

where
D
is the dimension of the system,
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
are
the primitive lattice vectors and
\boldsymbol{b}_{i}
denotes the
primitive reciprocal lattice vectors such that
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}_{j}=2\pi\delta_{ij}
.
Let us consider a set of
M
localized Wannier spin-orbitals in each
cell
w_{\alpha}\left(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{R}\right)=\left\langle\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{R}\alpha\right\rangle
.
These Wannier orbitals are characterized by two indexes
\left(\boldsymbol{R},\alpha\right)
,
with
\mathbf{R}
the center of the orbital and
\alpha
the orbital
type, and form an orthonormal basis, i.e.,
\left\langle\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\beta|\boldsymbol{R}\alpha\right\rangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R}}
.
We will denote
\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R}\right)\equiv\left\langle c_{\boldsymbol{R}\beta}^{\dagger}c_{\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\alpha}\right\rangle

and
\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\equiv\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{R}\right)
.
As a consequence of the periodicity of our system, the equilibrium
state,
\rho^{0}
, obeys

\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{0}\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)=\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{0}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R}\right).

(32)

This is a consequence of Bloch’s theorem, that in reciprocal space
only allows for non-vanishing coherences between states with the same

\boldsymbol{k}
.

Two basic quantities in the problem of light-matter interaction are
the unperturbed Hamiltonian,
\hat{h}^{0}
, and the position operator,

\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}
. Their matrix elements can be either calculated
from electronic structure calculations followed by a wannierization
procedure
(
Marzari et al. 2012
)
or be defined in the context
of a phenomenological tight-binding model. For the description of
light-matter interaction under the dipole approximation it is necessary
to have the knowledge of their matrix elements

\displaystyle h_{\alpha\beta}^{0}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\equiv

\displaystyle\left\langle\boldsymbol{0}\alpha\left|\hat{h}^{0}\right|\boldsymbol{R}\beta\right\rangle,

(33)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\equiv

\displaystyle\left\langle\boldsymbol{0}\alpha\left|\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}\right|\boldsymbol{R}\beta\right\rangle.

(34)

The unperturbed Hamiltonian is invariant under lattice translations,
i.e.,
\left\langle\boldsymbol{0}\alpha\left|\hat{h}^{0}\right|\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)\beta\right\rangle=\left\langle\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\alpha\left|\hat{h}^{0}\right|\boldsymbol{R}\beta\right\rangle
.

On the other hand, the position operator is not invariant under lattice
translations and transforms as

\left\langle\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\alpha\left|\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}\right|\boldsymbol{R}\beta\right\rangle=\left\langle\boldsymbol{0}\alpha\left|\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}\right|\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)\beta\right\rangle+\delta_{\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R}}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\boldsymbol{R}.

(35)

At first glance, the second term in Eq. (
35
)
might seem problematic as it breaks the periodicity of our problem.
However, under the dipole approximation, the reduced density matrix
in real-space is an object that remains invariant under lattice translations,
i.e.
\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)=\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R}\right)
.
Lets take a closer look at the equation of motion for the RDM by only
taking into account the problematic
\delta_{\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R}}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\boldsymbol{R}

term

i\frac{d\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R}\right)}{dt}=-\boldsymbol{E}\left(t\right)\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R}\right).

(36)

Taking into account that in our initial state,
\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{0}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R}\right)=\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{0}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)
,
we can see that the previous term in the equation of motion will maintain
the lattice invariance of the 1RDM. In the following, we will always
assume implicitly this property of the 1RDM, i.e.

\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)=\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right),

(37)

and it is this quantity that will be our central dynamical object.

Both self-energy terms,
\Sigma^{H}
and
\Sigma^{SEX}
, will also
be invariant under lattice translations. The SEX self-energy term
has the form

\displaystyle\Sigma^{SEX}\left[\rho\right]_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)

\displaystyle=-W\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\alpha}-\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\beta}+\boldsymbol{R}\right)\right)\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right),

(38)

where
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\alpha}=\left\langle\boldsymbol{0}\alpha\left|\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}\right|\boldsymbol{0}\alpha\right\rangle

is the center of the
\alpha
Wannier function at the
\boldsymbol{0}

unit cell. The Hartree self-energy can be written as

\displaystyle\Sigma^{H}\left[\rho\right]_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{R}}\sum_{\gamma}\rho_{\gamma\gamma}\left(\boldsymbol{0}\right)X_{\gamma\alpha},

(39)

\displaystyle X_{\gamma\alpha}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}}s_{\gamma}V\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\alpha}-\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}\right)\right)

(40)

where
s_{\gamma}
takes into account the spin-degeneracy of the
orbital
\gamma
.

II.2.1
Equations of motion in Wannier basis

We are now in position to derive the equations of motion for
\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)
,
where the upperscript
L
stands for length gauge, as defined by
Eq. (
4
). In the following, we will omit
the indexes for the Wannier orbitals implying that we are referring
to a
M\times M
matrix. The resulting equation of motion is

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}}\left[h^{0}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right),\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{E}\left(t\right)\cdot\sum_{\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}}\left[\boldsymbol{r}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right),\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{E}\left(t\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{R}\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}}\left[\Sigma^{HSEX}\left[\rho^{L}\right]\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right),\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\sum_{\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}}\left[\Sigma_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right),\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+i\mathcal{L}_{incoh}\left[\rho^{L}\right]\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right),

(41)

where we group all incoherent terms, discussed in
II.1.5
,
into

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{incoh}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{r}+\mathcal{L}_{rs}+\mathcal{L}_{D}.

(42)

The explicit formulation of the incoherent terms in the case of a
periodic crystal will be shown later.

II.2.2
Length gauge and Peierls gauge

The term
\boldsymbol{E}\left(t\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{R}\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)

in Eq. (
41
) clearly diverges in a crystal
and this poses severe numerical difficulties. These difficulties might
be solved by working with sufficiently bigger lattices, since it should
be expected that
\rho\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\rightarrow 0
when

\left|\boldsymbol{R}\right|\rightarrow\infty
. At the same time,
dealing with the
\boldsymbol{E}\left(t\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{R}

term for big
\boldsymbol{R}
will require a very fine temporal resolution.
However, one might find a more elegant solution to this problem. In
a reciprocal space formulation, the position operator is expressed
as a gradient in
k
-space
(
Blount 1962
;
Ventura et al. 2017
;
Ventura 2021
)
.
One might numerically discretize the gradient, as done in previous
works
(
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
;
Cistaro et al. 2022
)
. An alternative
way to deal with the gradient of the density matrix in the reciprocal
space is to move to the accelerated basis
(
Yue and Gaarde 2022
)
.
In our real-space approach, a time-dependent translation in reciprocal-space
is just a time-dependent phase shift in real-space. Therefore, one
might define
\rho^{P}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)
, the real-space
1RDM in the Peierls gauge, as

\rho^{P}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)=\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\phi_{P}\left(t,-\boldsymbol{R}\right)

(43)

where
\phi_{P}\left(t,\boldsymbol{R}\right)=\exp\left(-i\boldsymbol{A}_{L}\left(t\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{R}\right)

is the Peierls phase between unit cells and
\boldsymbol{A}_{L}\left(t\right)=-\int^{t}\boldsymbol{E}\left(t^{\prime}\right)dt^{\prime}

is the laser vector potential. We might define
\mathcal{G}^{P\rightarrow L}

as the transformation from the length gauge to the Peierls gauge as

\mathcal{G}^{L\rightarrow P}\left[\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\right]\equiv\rho^{P}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)=\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\phi_{P}\left(t,-\boldsymbol{R}\right)

(44)

and the corresponding inverse transformation,
\mathcal{G}^{P\rightarrow L}
.
The equation of motion for
\rho^{P}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)

is

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\rho^{P}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}}\phi_{P}\left(t,-\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}\right)\left[h^{0}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)+\boldsymbol{E}\left(t\right).\boldsymbol{r}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right),\rho^{P}\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}}\phi_{P}\left(t,-\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}\right)\left[\Sigma^{HSEX}\left[\rho^{L}\right]\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)-\Sigma_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right),\rho^{P}\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R^{\prime}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+i\phi_{P}\left(t,-\boldsymbol{R}\right)\mathcal{L}_{incoh}\left[\rho^{L}\right]\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right),

(45)

where we can see that the term
\boldsymbol{E}\left(t\right).\boldsymbol{R}\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)

is no longer involved in the propagation, as it is encoded in the
time-dependent Peierls phase,
\phi_{P}
. Eq. (
45
)
is what we call the semiconductor Wannier equations, a real-space
equivalent of the semiconductor Bloch equations. This is our preferred
formulation of the equations of motion. Up to this point, the problematic
transition dipole moments in the reciprocal space states did not appear
in any of our equations and therefore we avoid the problem of the

structure gauge
freedom. This is one of key features that makes
the SWEs more suitable than the SBEs

II.2.3
Periodic boundary conditions

The equations of motion for
\rho^{P}
, Eq. (
45
),
are defined for an infinite crystal and for a practical implementation
one must truncate our sums over
\boldsymbol{R}
to a finite set
of lattice vectors. The most common choice is to work in a supercell
approach. For that, we can define the supercell Bravais lattice as

\boldsymbol{R}_{S}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}d_{i}\boldsymbol{a}_{S,i},\ d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},

(46)

where
\boldsymbol{a}_{S,i}=N_{i}\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
are the primitive
lattice vectors of the supercell,
N=\prod_{i=1}^{D}N_{i}
is the
number of unit cells in the supercell. Furthermore, we impose Born–von
Karman periodic boundary conditions, i.e.
c_{\boldsymbol{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}\equiv c_{\left(\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{R}_{S}\right)\alpha}^{\dagger}
.
The Bravais lattice vectors enclosed into the origin supercell are
given by

\boldsymbol{R}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}n_{i}\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\ n_{i}\in\left[\lfloor-N_{i}/2\rfloor,\lfloor N_{i}/2-1\rfloor\right].

(47)

From now on, the infinite sums in
\boldsymbol{R}
will be replaced
by a sum of Bravais vectors in a single supercell, according to Eq.
(
47
). The choice of periodic boundary conditions
lead to a discretization of our Brillouin zone, i.e.

\boldsymbol{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{m_{i}}{N_{i}}\boldsymbol{b}_{i},\ m_{i}\in\left[0,N_{i}-1\right],

(48)

which defines a Monkhorst-Pack reciprocal space grid
(
Monkhorst and Pack 1976
)
.
In the following, all the sums over
\boldsymbol{k}
will be assumed
to be done on this reciprocal space grid.

We can define the Bloch states associated with the localized Wannier
orbitals as

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{\boldsymbol{k}\alpha}^{W}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}}\left|\boldsymbol{R}\alpha\right\rangle

(49)

\displaystyle c_{\boldsymbol{k}\alpha}^{\dagger,W}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}}c_{\boldsymbol{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}

(50)

\displaystyle c_{\boldsymbol{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}}c_{\boldsymbol{k}\alpha}^{\dagger,W}

(51)

that are constructed from the localized Wannier orbitals. The upperscript

W
refers to the Wannier structure gauge
(
Wang et al. 2006
;
Marzari et al. 2012
;
Silva et al. 2019b
)
.
It is important to distinguish between two distinct contexts in which
the term gauge is used: the
laser gauge
, referring to the choice
between velocity and length gauges in the light-matter interaction,
and the
structure gauge
, associated with the inherent freedom
in selecting a Bloch basis for the crystal Hamiltonian. In the
W

gauge, we can also define the reduced density matrix in the reciprocal
space,
\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{W}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)=\left\langle c_{\boldsymbol{k}\beta}^{\dagger,W}c_{\boldsymbol{k}\alpha}^{W}\right\rangle
.
This RDM is related with
\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)
by
a simple discrete Fourier transform

\displaystyle\rho^{W}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}}\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right),

(52)

\displaystyle\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}}\rho^{W}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right).

(53)

It is useful to define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{L\rightarrow W}\left[\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\equiv\rho^{W}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)

(54)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}}\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)

(55)

and the corresponding inverse transformation,
\mathcal{F}^{W\rightarrow L}
.

II.2.4
Initial state

In order to obtain the initial state, we must diagonalize
\hat{h}^{0}
.
In the basis,
\left|\psi_{\boldsymbol{k}\alpha}^{W}\right\rangle
,

\hat{h}^{0}
is expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{h}^{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\alpha\beta}h_{\alpha\beta}^{0,W}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)c_{\alpha\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger,W}c_{\beta\boldsymbol{k}}^{W},

(56)

\displaystyle h^{0,W}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}.\boldsymbol{R}}h^{0}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)

(57)

and by diagonalizing
h^{0,W}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)
we have

\displaystyle\hat{h}^{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}n}h_{nn}^{0,H}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)c_{n\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger,H}c_{n\boldsymbol{k}}^{H},

(58)

\displaystyle h^{0,H}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)=

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)h^{0,W}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)U\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right),

(59)

where
U\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)
is the unitary matrix that diagonalizes

h^{0,W}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)
and the upperscript
H
refers
to the Hamiltonian structure gauge. The equilibrium RDM in the Hamiltonian
gauge is just

\rho_{nm}^{0,H}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)=\delta_{nm}F_{\mu,T}\left(h_{nm}^{0,H}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)\right)

(60)

where
F_{\mu,T}(\epsilon)=\left(e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}+1\right)^{-1}

is the Fermi-Dirac function for a chemical potential
\mu
and inverse
temperature
\beta
. It is useful to define

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{W\rightarrow H}\left[\rho^{W}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\equiv\rho^{H}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)

(61)

\displaystyle=U^{\dagger}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)\rho^{W}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)U\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)

(62)

and the corresponding inverse transformation,
\mathcal{U}^{H\rightarrow W}
.
Note that the choice of
U\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)
is not unique.
This fact stems from the structure gauge freedom to choose the eigenstates,
i.e.
\left|\psi_{\boldsymbol{k}n}^{W}\right\rangle\rightarrow e^{i\theta_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)}\left|\psi_{\boldsymbol{k}n}^{W}\right\rangle
.
However, one must notice that
\rho^{0,W}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)

remains unaltered by this phase transformation. The
\mathcal{U}^{W\rightarrow H}

transformation will allow to have access to populations of bands and
to compute terms that are inherent to the
H
gauge, such as, interband
and intraband currents, and the inclusion of the pure dephasing term,

\mathcal{L}_{D}
.

II.2.5
Observables

The velocity operator, in contrast to the position operator, is invariant
under lattice translations. For a general invariant operator under
lattice translations,
\hat{O}
, its mean value normalized to the
unit cell can be expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle O\right\rangle}{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta\boldsymbol{R}}O_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\rho_{\beta\alpha}\left(-\boldsymbol{R}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta\boldsymbol{R}}O_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\left(\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\right)^{*}.

(63)

The explicit expression for the velocity operator matrix elements
in real space is

\boldsymbol{v}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)=i\left(\boldsymbol{R}h^{0}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)-\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}}\left[\boldsymbol{r}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right),h^{0}\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)\right]\right).

(64)

We can recognize in the first (second) term of the velocity operator
the intraband (interband) term expressed in a real space basis
(
Silva et al. 2019b
)
.
The expression for the total current is, according to eq. (
63
),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{j}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=e\frac{\left\langle\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle}{N}=e\sum_{\alpha\beta\boldsymbol{R}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\left(\rho_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\right)^{*}.

(65)

II.2.6
Decoherence

In Eq. (
45
), we did not show the explicit
form of the incoherent terms that were grouped in
\mathcal{L}_{incoh}\left[\rho^{L}\right]
.
The first thing to notice is that we should move to the
L
gauge.
In our work, as discussed in Sec.
II.1.5
, we include
three forms of decoherence. The pure dephasing term,
\mathcal{L}_{D}
,
is naturally expressed in the
H
gauge. By applying
\mathcal{F}^{L\rightarrow W}

and
\mathcal{U}^{W\rightarrow H}
, one move
\rho
to the
H

gauge where
\mathcal{L}_{D}
is simply

\mathcal{L}_{D}\left[\rho^{H}\right]_{ij}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)=\gamma_{D}\left(\delta_{ij}-1\right)\rho_{ij}^{H}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)

(66)

and after this apply the corresponding inverse transformations,
\mathcal{U}^{H\rightarrow W}

and
\mathcal{F}^{W\rightarrow L}
.

The relaxation term,
\mathcal{L}_{r}
, is simply expressed as

\mathcal{L}_{r}\left[\rho^{L}\right]\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)=\gamma_{r}\left(\rho^{0,L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)-\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\right).

(67)

Regarding the real space dephasing term,

\mathcal{L}_{rs}\left[\rho^{L}\right]\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)=\gamma_{rs}\left(d\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\right)\odot\left(\rho^{0,L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)-\rho^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)\right)

(68)

where
d_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\right)=\left|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\beta}-\boldsymbol{R}\right|
,

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\alpha}
is the center of the
\alpha
Wannier
function in the center unit cell and
\odot
denotes the Hadamard
product. The functional form of
\gamma_{rs}
is given by Eq. (
30
).
Effectively, with this term we are suppressing coherences between
orbitals that are separated by distances greater than
R_{rs,cut}

and and can therefore suppress trajectories that acquire a phase larger
than
2\pi
, and destructively interfere in the far-field
(
Brown et al. 2024a
;
Gaarde et al. 2008
)
.
Having this analogy in mind, it will be useful to express
R_{rs,cut}

in units of the excursion of an electron that acquires roughly a
2\pi

phase, i.e.
R_{rs,0}=4\pi\omega_{L}/\left(\left|e\right|E_{0}\right)
,
where
E_{0}
and
\omega_{L}
are the amplitude and frequency of
the laser field.

III
Results

In this section, we show numerical results that were obtained with
the SWEs formalism. We first show the behavior of the equilibrium
reduced density matrix and its decay. We also calculate the linear
optical conductivity for monolayer hBN and monolayer MoS
2
, the
convergence of the SWEs with respect to the formulation of the SBEs
in
(
Silva et al. 2019b
)
, the impact of pure and real-space dephasing
in the HHG spectrum and finally the HHG spectrum for MoS
2
.

III.1
Decay of equilibrium density matrix

Figure 1:
Scatter plot of the coherences between localized orbitals
for monolayer hBN (blue circles) and graphene, at zero (black circles)
and finite temperature (orange circles).

The basic object in the SWEs is the electronic reduced density matrix
expressed in a basis of Wannier localized orbitals. Despite working
with extended periodic systems, where Bloch states are delocalized,
the coherences of the electronic density matrix decay rapidly with
the distance between orbitals.
Nearsightedness
of electronic
matter
(
Kohn 1996
;
Prodan and Kohn 2005
)
is actually the
core assumption for linear scaling electronic structure methods
(
Goedecker 1999
;
Prentice et al. 2020
)
.

In Fig.
1
, we show the decay of coherences in the density
matrix for graphene, at zero and finite temperature (
T=1000\,\mathrm{K}
),
and monolayer hBN. For both cases, we model graphene and monolayer
hBN with a tight-binding model
(
Castro Neto et al. 2009
)
, with
a nearest neighbor hopping
t_{0}=2.8\,\mathrm{eV}
and a distance
between neighboring atoms of
1.4457\>\text{\AA}
. The gap energy
in the monolayer hBN is set to be
4.52\,\mathrm{eV}
. As expected,
hBN (a gapped insulator) and graphene at finite temperature (a finite
temperature semi-metal) exhibit an exponential decay of the coherences,
whereas graphene at zero temperature displays an algebraic decay
(
Goedecker 1998
;
Goedecker 1999
)
.
It is the
nearsightedness
of electrons, depicted in Fig.
1
,
that make a real-space approach a compelling alternative to the reciprocal
space methods.

III.2
Linear optical conductivity

Figure 2:
Band-structure and total density of states (DOS) of
monolayer hBN (a) and of monolayer 1H-MoS
2
(b). In panels (c,d),
we show the linear optical conductivity of monolayer hBN and 1H-MoS
2
,
respectively, in the IPA (orange lines) and TD-HSEX (black lines)
approximations.

To validate the SWEs approach, we have calculated the linear optical
conductivity of hBN and MoS
2
. In both cases we have a point
group symmetry
D_{3h}
and for symmetry reasons,
\sigma_{xy}=0

and
\sigma_{xx}=\sigma_{yy}

(
Ventura 2021

Real-space dephasing

The requirement for ultrashort dephasing times to match experimental
high-harmonic spectra in solids remains an open question. A recently
proposed method seeks to address this issue by introducing real-space
dephasing
(
Brown et al. 2024a

, which arises naturally within the SWEs
framework.

Fig.

The laser field is the same as in Fig.

we used a supercell with

Both real-space and pure dephasing reduce interferences in the high-harmonic

(
Boroumand et al. 2025

dephasing approach, making it a more reliable and physically consistent
alternative to simple pure-dephasing models.

IV
Conclusions

We have introduced the semiconductor Wannier equations (SWEs), a real-time,
real-space framework for describing ultrafast light–matter interaction
and nonlinear optical response in crystalline solids. By expressing
the electronic reduced density matrix in a localized Wannier basis,
the SWEs provide a gauge-clean alternative to reciprocal-space semiconductor
Bloch equations (SBEs), avoiding the numerical instabilities caused
by the structure-gauge freedom of Bloch states. The method naturally
incorporates electron–electron interactions at the time-dependent
Hartree plus screened-exchange (TD-HSEX) level and includes physically
motivated decoherence mechanisms, pure dephasing, population relaxation,
and distance-dependent real-space dephasing, offering a robust modeling
of strong-field and high-harmonic generation dynamics.

We have shown that the SWEs reproduce key optical properties, such
as the linear optical conductivity and excitonic features of monolayer
hBN and MoS
2
, in agreement with established approaches. Benchmarking
against the SBEs formalism demonstrated that the SWEs achieve significantly
faster numerical convergence, enabling efficient simulations with
moderate supercell sizes. Importantly, the real-space dephasing model
introduced here provides a physically grounded alternative to the
ultrashort pure-dephasing times often used to fit experimental HHG
spectra, avoiding artificial enhancement of low-order harmonics while
preserving the coherent features of the signal.

Conceptually, the SWEs bridge semiclassical real-space intuition,
central to attosecond physics, with many-body solid-state optics,
opening new avenues for interpreting HHG and other strong-field phenomena
in complex materials. Their computational efficiency, robustness to
gauge ambiguities, and compatibility with
ab-initio
Hamiltonians
make the SWEs a promising platform for exploring nonlinear optical
response, excitonic dynamics, and attosecond spectroscopy in emerging
quantum materials.

Future developments may include the incorporation of fully dynamical
screening, coupling to phonons and other bosonic modes, and extensions
to strongly correlated systems beyond mean-field approximations. These
advances will further strengthen the SWEs as a versatile and predictive
tool for next-generation ultrafast solid-state optics.

Appendix A
Rytova-Keldysh potential

As we are dealing with 2D materials, the Rytova-Keldysh potential

(
Rytova 2018
;
Keldysh 1979
;
Van Tuan et al. 2018
)
is our
choice to model screening effects in the self-energy term,
\Sigma^{SEX}
.
The Rytova-Keldysh potential reads

W_{RK}\left(r\right)=\frac{e^{2}}{4\epsilon_{0}\left(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}\right)r_{0}}\left[H_{0}\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)-Y_{0}\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)\right],

(70)

where
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
are the dielectric constants
of the top and bottom medium,
r_{0}
is the screening length and

H_{0}
,
Y_{0}
are the zero-order Struve and Neumann special functions.
In order to avoid the divergence of the potential at
r=0

(
Wu et al. 2015
;
Uría-Álvarez et al. 2024
)
,
we renormalize
r\rightarrow\sqrt{r^{2}+r_{min}^{2}}
. Furthermore,
for large distances, i.e.
r>r_{RK,cut}
, we employ a radial cutoff
and take
W_{RK}
to be zero. In pratice, to avoid artifacts from
periodic images in the calculation, we will set
r_{RK,cut}
to be
of the radius of the largest sphere that fits inside the supercell.
The renormalization and radial cutoff are also applied to the bare
Coulomb potential in the Hartree term,
\Sigma^{H}
.
