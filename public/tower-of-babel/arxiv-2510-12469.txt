Proof of Cloud: Data Center Execution Assurance for Confidential VMs
Filip Rezabek, Moe Mahhouk, Andrew Miller, Stefan Genchev, Quintus Kilbourn, Georg Carle, Jonathan Passerat-Palmbach

Abstract
Confidential Virtual Machines (CVMs) protect data in use by running workloads inside hardware-isolated environments. In doing so, they also inherit the limitations of the underlying hardware. Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs), which enforce this isolation, explicitly exclude adversaries with physical access from their threat model. Commercial TEEs, e.g., Intel TDX, thus assume infrastructure providers do not physically exploit hardware and serve as safeguards instead. This creates a tension: tenants must trust provider integrity at the hardware layer, yet existing remote attestation offers no way to verify that CVMs actually run on physically trusted platforms, leaving today’s CVM deployments unable to demonstrate that their guarantees align with the TEE vendor’s threat model. We bridge this confidence gap with Data Center Execution Assurance (DCEA), a design generating “Proofs of Cloud.” DCEA binds a CVM to its underlying platform using vTPM-anchored measurements, ensuring CVM launch evidence and TPM quotes refer to the same physical chassis. This takes advantage of the fact that data centers are often identifiable via TPMs. Our approach applies to CVMs accessing vTPMs and running on top of software stacks fully controlled by the cloud provider, as well as single-tenant bare-metal deployments with discrete TPMs. We trust providers for integrity (certificate issuance), but not for the confidentiality of CVM-visible state. DCEA enables remote verification of a CVM’s platform origin and integrity, mitigating attacks like replay and attestation proxying. We include a candidate implementation on Google Cloud and Intel TDX that leverages Intel TXT for trusted launch. Our design refines CVMs’ threat model and provides a practical path for deploying high-assurance, confidential workloads in minimally trusted environments.

Introduction

Many applications rely on commodity
Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs)
to protect data
in use
.
Technologies such as Intel
Trust Domain Extensions (TDX)
and AMD
Secure Encrypted Virtualization (SEV)
-
Secure Nested Paging (SNP)
adopt a cloud-centric threat model: they assume an attacker can control every layer of host software, yet can never physically access the server in the rack (chassis)
[
22
]
.
Under that assumption, the
TEE
’s remote attestation certifies the CPU model, microcode, and measured launch state, but it carries no evidence of
where
the processor is installed.
A determined operator who
does
have physical custody can therefore migrate or, by default, operate an otherwise certified workload onto hardware in an uncontrolled environment, leaving the verifier with no cryptographic way to detect this change.

This gap in
TEE
s’ threat model is a blind spot that represents more than an academic question. The most recent attacks against TEEs leveraged physical access to the host machine to extract Intel
Software Guard Extensions (SGX)
attestation keys
[
47
,
21
]
. While they remain out of scope according to the threat model assumed by commercial TEE vendors, third-party users interacting with a TEE-protected service do not have the ability to verify the TEE’s location via a standard attestation mechanism.
It becomes especially relevant in sensitive domains such as financial services, confidential data processing, and decentralized systems.
Decentralized Finance (DeFi)
increasingly relies on
TEEs
s to guard valuable assets
[
18
,
42
]
, but participants are mutually untrusted and not clearly identified. This is at odds with the gap in the threat model. The sole attestation fails to show whether a machine resides in a data center whose operator has little economic incentive to tamper with it.
The rest of this paper treats the missing environment provenance as a first-class goal and proposes
Data Center Execution Assurance (DCEA)
as a way to address it.

Our work closes the gap between existing
TEE
threat models and the additional trust that must be placed in the underlying infrastructure operator to provide physical protection, but the rest remains within the
TEE
threat model.
We argue that
TEE
attestations lack explicit infrastructure binding and thus propose extending them to offer
DCEA
, a novel approach that cryptographically associates a confidential workload not only with a vetted software and hardware state, but also with a known infrastructure environment.
This is achieved by combining two roots of trust - one provided by the
TEE
manufacturer and the second by the infrastructure owner, e.g., in the form of
Trusted Platform Module (TPM)
.

We target
Confidential VMs (CVMs)
deployed on a bare-metal host or as a virtualized instance on the cloud infrastructure.
Because the current default
TEE
attestation says nothing about the machine’s physical location, they cannot provide
DCEA
.
Essentially,
DCEA
enshrines the data center as a disinterested, mutually trusted third party, on the economically rational assumption that mounting a chassis-level attack would damage the provider’s reputation and future revenue far more than any benefit it might gain.
We therefore develop a solution for two settings: the common managed-
CVM
and the more demanding bare-metal deployment.
Both scenarios, along with their attack surfaces, are detailed in
Section

4
.

At a high level, we set up two parallel roots of trust - one from the
Trust Domain (TD)
and the second from the cloud provider.
For the
TD
flow, we prototype on Intel
TDX
as the only platform that currently exposes both
CVM
and bare-metal modes in
Google Cloud Platform (GCP)
.
For the second root of trust, we establish a trust chain from the physical
TPM
(or cloud
Virtual TPM (vTPM)
) to the
CVM
, utilizing the provider’s root of trust and its respective certificate chain. Here, we assume that the TPM’s
EK Certificate (EKC)
is issued by the cloud provider.

DCEA
generalizes to any
TEE
offering comparable attestation primitives.

2.1
Trusted Platform Module & TPM Quote

The
TPM
enhances platform security by providing a secure boot mechanism.
During the boot process, the
Core Root of Trust for Measurement (CRTM)
, typically the BIOS, initiates a sequence of integrity measurements.
These measurements are stored in
TPM
’
PCRs
, creating a trust chain
[
49
]
.
A critical component of
TPM
’s attestation capability is the
Endorsement Key (EK)
, a unique asymmetric key pair embedded in the
TPM
by the manufacturer
[
14
]
.
The
EK
’s public portion, often accompanied by an
EKC
, is a root of trust.
For our setting, we assume that
EKC
originates from an issuer, which can certify and ensure the physical security of the platform.
To preserve user privacy and ensure that
EK
is less likely to be compromised,
TPMs
utilize
AKs
, derived from
EK
.

AKs
can sign the attestation data, such as the values of
PCR
, without revealing the
EK
, thus enabling secure and private attestation processes.

EKC
also accompanies the signed information from
TPM
, allowing the root of trust verification back to the certificate issuer.

.

In virtualized environments, the
vTPM
extends the capabilities of the physical
TPM
to
Virtual Machines (VMs)
, providing each
VM
with its own isolated instance of
vTPM

[

.
This helps to maintain the integrity of each
VM
, especially in multi-tenant cloud infrastructures.

vTPM
emulates
TPM
functionalities within the hypervisor layer.

vTPMs
support the
VM
lifecycle operations such as suspend, resume, and migration.
When a
VM
is migrated from one host to another, its
vTPM
state can be securely transferred, preserving the
VM
’s trust state across different physical machines.
However, the security of
vTPMs
is inherently tied to the trustworthiness of the hypervisor.
The
vTPM
maintains for each
VM
its
PCR
values, containing the information about the guest OS boot information, as shown in
Figure

2
.
Therefore, we will sometimes refer to
CVM
’s
PCR
stack as (v)
PCR
.
If the hypervisor is compromised, the isolation and security guarantees provided by
vTPMs
can be undermined
[
14
]
.

2.2
Trusted Execution Technology

As shown in
Figure

2
, Intel
TXT
is extending the
PCR
stack.
Intel
TXT

[
28
]
is a hardware-based security feature that protects computing platforms against software-based attacks, including those targeting firmware, BIOS, and rootkits.
Intel
TXT
establishes a chain of trust starting from the platform’s
TPM
, extending through the BIOS and into the operating system or hypervisor.
During the boot process, each component—such as the BIOS, option ROMs, and the
Master Boot Record (MBR)
are measured using cryptographic hashes, and these measurements are stored in the
TPM
’s
PCRs
.
This process ensures that any unauthorized modifications to the system’s launch configuration can be detected, as they would result in different hash values compared to known good measurements.
Intel
TXT
works in combination with Intel
Virtualization Technology (VT)

[
9
]
to provide a trusted launch environment for
VMs
, offering hardware-based isolation and attestation capabilities.
Unlike
TPM
, Intel
TXT
has a larger
Trusted Computing Base (TCB)
due to the reliance on
TPM
, the CPU chipset, the motherboard, and the system busses.
Importanly, this technology is widely spread on Intel CPUs, especially all those including Intel
TDX
.

Figure

2
shows the
PCRs
stack of
TPM
and Intel
TXT
.

TPM
reaches up to PCR 0-7 by default and
TXT
appends additional
PCRs
to the stack.
The initial steps of BIOS are measured and stored in PCR 0-3 and are part of the static chain of trust.
The
MBR
is the first step of the dynamic chain of trust.
The SINIT
Authenticated Code Module (ACM)
is responsible for the security checks until it reaches the
Measured Launch Environment (MLE)
.

2.3.1
Intel TDX Attestation

In the context of Intel
TDX

CVMs
are referred to as
TDs
.
A
TD
’s remote attestation enables external parties to verify the integrity and authenticity of a
TD
and provide information about the code running inside of it.
The attestation report, or "quote," generated during this process contains several fields that provide insights into the
TD
’s configuration and state.

For
DCEA
we need to understand the trust assumptions of the attestation process and have an overview of host-controlled components.
We are especially interested in the
TD
quote fields and the information they provide.
The
TDX
attestation process relies on a root of trust established through interaction with Intel, which provides two Intel
SGX
enclaves and a certificate chain verifying the authenticity of the platform.
The flow begins with the
TD Quoting Enclave (TDQE)
, a special-purpose
SGX
enclave responsible for generating a
TD
-specific asymmetric key pair.
The
TDQE
creates an
SGX
report containing its identity and a hash of the
AK
public key.
This report is verified locally by the
Provisioning Certificate Enclave (PCE)
, another
SGX
enclave also signed by Intel, using symmetric keys established via
SGX
’s local attestation mechanism on the host OS.
The
PCE
then derives the
Provisioning Certification Key (PCK)
key from platform-specific
EGETKEY
instructions, which reflect the host’s
TCB
level and package identity.
The
PCE
signs the report from the
TDQE
, thus generating a certificate that vouches for the authenticity of the
TD
’s attestation key.
This process is rooted in Intel’s
Intel Provisioning Certification Service (PCS)
, which issues X.509
Provisioning Certification Key Certificates (PCKCs)
binding the public part of the
PCK
key to the host platform.

The resulting quote, signed by the
TDQE
using its
AK
, includes measurements of the
TD
and can be verified against the
PCKC
chain provided by Intel.
This mechanism ensures that the quote was produced by a genuine
TD
running on a legitimate Intel
TDX
platform.
However, running a
TD
and legitimate platform does not translate to understanding where the host operates.
Therefore, we introduce
DCEA
, which combines Intel’s checks with extension to the environment where the platform operates for stronger trust assumptions.
The
TD
’s memory and execution context are isolated from the hypervisor and host OS via the
Secure Arbitration Mode (SEAM)
, which mediates sensitive operations and enforces
TDX
-specific protections.
Memory encryption and key management are also handled by
Multi-key Total Memory Encryption (MKTME)
, which derives symmetric keys per-
TD
from hardware-resident secrets and the configuration specific to
TDX
.
Overall, the attestation root of trust in Intel
TDX
builds on
SGX
enclaves,
PCK
, and
PCS
, forming a hardware-rooted, verifiable trust chain for remote attestation.

The attestation report includes fields such as
TEE_TCB_SVN
, indicating the security version number of the
TEE
’s
TCB
and
MRSEAM
, providing the
SEAM
module measurements.
For more details, there are also
SEAMATTRIBUTES
, detailing the configuration of the
SEAM
module, and
TDATTRIBUTES
field representing the attributes of the
TD
.
These attributes mainly aim to verify the expected security settings.
Measurements like
MRTD
(
Measurement of Trust Domain (MRTD)
),
MROWNER
, and
MROWNERCONFIG
provide cryptographic hashes representing the
TD
’s initial state, configuration, owner identity, and owner-specific configuration.
Runtime measurements are captured in
RTMR[0]
through
RTMR[3]
(
Runtime Extendable Measurement Register (RTMR)
), reflecting dynamic components loaded after
TD
initialization.
Last,
report_data
is a 64-byte field supplied by the
TD
, often used to include nonce or other verifier-specific data
[
30
,
29
]
.

3
Confidential VMs Security in Practice

This section highlights the discrepancy between the threat model of current
TEEs
implementations and the information provided in the attestations.
We then dive into a detailed threat model that highlights the role of the verifier and provides them with information about the execution location of the
TEE
.

3.1
Physical-Access Gap in TEE Attestations

Existing attestation reports produced by Intel
TDX
and AMD
SEV
-
SNP
certify the state of an enclave running on a genuine chip recognized by the vendor, but reveal nothing about the location of the hosting machine.
A malicious operator can therefore serve an enclave and produce a valid attestation while secretly hosting the workload on hardware under their physical control.
This becomes especially prominent with attacks allowing to extract the attestation keys
[
47
,
21
]
.
Our goal is to close this "location-oblivious" gap by binding the
TEE
’s attestation to an additional
TPM
quote that authenticates the host as part of a public cloud-provider platform.
With this discrepancy between the
TEEs
threat model and the attestation flow, we can see that the security gap stems from the trust in the operator to provide, but not to leverage their physical access to the machine to exploit side channel attacks.
Today’s commercial available
CVM
solutions, e.g., Intel
TDX
and AMD
SEV
-
SNP
, already raise the bar against a malicious hypervisor by encrypting memory, measuring launch, and enabling remote attestation, thereby minimizing host-software attacks on the
VM

[
27
,
39
,
10
,
34
]
.
Nevertheless, recent work shows that even a malicious hypervisor can be used to extract secrets from the
VM
memory
[
46
]
.

The
TEEs
’s threat model excludes sophisticated side channels, where the attacker controls most of the infrastructure stack (e.g., the hypervisor or management software), but cannot extract plaintext data from the encrypted
VM
memory or tamper with its execution without detection.
The side channels are cache-based, branch prediction, page fault, chip tampering, and others
[
54
,
55
,
47
,
21
]
.

Although we primarily focus on Intel
TDX
, it is also important to monitor Intel
SGX
, which is used in the attestation flow of Intel
TDX
.
The attestation flow of Intel
TDX
and AMD
SEV
-
SNP
does not include any information about the underlying system provider.
Therefore,
DCEA
combines the attestation flow enabled by
CVM
with
TPM
quote from two separate roots of trust.

Cloud providers are incentivized by their reputation not to misbehave and attempt physical attacks on their own data center. Our threat model, therefore, embraces the data center side of the cloud provider as an element of the
TCB
, providing physical protection to the TEE host’s chassis and issuing valid certificates to its
TPM
s.
From the perspective of the
TD
and verifier, the threat model is only strengthened, as in an ideal scenario, we gain additional confidence about the provider.
If the
vTPM
binding to
TD
is compromised, we fall back to the default
TD
threat model. We further expand on the threat model in
Section

3.2
.

We note that while
TDX
and
SEV
-
SNP
significantly raise the bar for host-based attacks, they inherently rely on the CPU vendor (Intel or AMD) for implementation correctness, firmware integrity, remote attestation, and provisioning of encryption keys.
Even though recent work has shown that the
TEE
threat model is not as strong as it is assumed to be for
CVM
and missing some crucial
TCB
information and their verifiability
[
22
]
.

As commonly assumed in commodity
TEE
literature, we treat the CPU vendor’s key infrastructure as a single root of trust, and do not attempt to mitigate microcode backdoors or similar hardware vendor threats.
Such supply-chain risks, while real, are orthogonal to the location problem addressed here.
Similarly, we do not consider sophisticated networking attacks, such as DNS spoofing, among others.
In summary, commodity
TEEs
’ attestations prove "what code is running" but not "where it is running".
By matching a provider-signed
TPM
quote with the standard
TEE
attestation report, our protocol enables the verifier to confirm that the confidential
VM
resides on cloud hardware outside the attacker’s physical reach.
This eliminates the last practical avenue for side-channel attacks conducted by adversaries with physical access in this setting.

3.2
Threat Model

The primary objective of this threat model is to facilitate the secure deployment and interaction with confidential workloads by third parties in cloud environments.
In these settings, tenants possess limited operational visibility. They rely exclusively on the hardware root of trust and the
TD
isolation provided by the
TEE
. The system must demonstrate attestation correctness, even if the platform operator behaves adversarially.

3.2.1
Overview of parties and scenarios

This threat model assumes that the cloud provider’s physical infrastructure is trustworthy. It also assumes the provider does not engage in attacks that require physical access to the trusted execution environment
TEE
host, as described in Section
3.1
.

The cloud provider, comprising the Host Manager
\raisebox{0.35pt}{\kern0.8mm
\scalebox{0.70}{M}
}⃝
and Hardware Provider
\raisebox{0.35pt}{\kern0.8mm
\scalebox{0.70}{P}
}⃝
, is modeled as an entity with a potentially adversarial software stack. Its measured launch process and certificate issuance infrastructure are assumed to be verifiable. The following behaviors are considered: (i) the provider is trusted to issue valid
EKC
for its
TPMs
and to provision
AK
that are correctly measured and sealed under expected
PCR
values; (ii) the provider is
not
trusted to maintain confidentiality for data processed or stored in software-visible components, such as the
vTPM
, which are fully accessible to the host operating system and hypervisor; and (iii) integrity guarantees are considered meaningful only when they are cryptographically verifiable, for example, through measured boot flows, sealed keys, and hardware-generated attestations.

A critical requirement for the
EKC
is issuance by an entity capable of certifying the infrastructure of a trusted provider, such as
GCP
certifying an
EK
within its own data center.

3.2.2
Trust Assumptions

Adversary Capabilities.

We assume a strong software adversary that (i) controls the host OS, hypervisor, and virtualization stack (including any
vTPM
instances); (ii) can intercept, delay, reorder, drop, replay, or inject any messages exchanged between the tenant, verifier, and (v)
TPM
; (iii) can provision arbitrary software on the host to masquerade as trusted infrastructure; and (iv) may collude with malicious insiders who possess host-level administrative access.

Adversary Limitations.

As per commercial
TEE
s’ threat model, we assume that CPU vendors (e.g., Intel, AMD) issue authentic microcode and root keys; the
TPM
is genuine and has a valid
EKC
signed by a public root; and the CPU enforces the correct isolation of
TDs
and implements
RTMRs
as specified. Therefore, our design does not consider the hardware vendor as an active participant.

Assuming trust in the cloud provider’s infrastructure, we rule out: physical tampering with CPUs,
TPMs
, or server hardware, including chip extraction, side-channel sensors, or cold-boot attacks; compromise of the CPU or
TPM
manufacturer’s supply chain (e.g., issuance of fraudulent
EKCs
or malicious microcode updates); attacks on hardware vulnerabilities unknown at deployment time (e.g., speculative execution exploits); and denial-of-service attacks, which may prevent availability but do not affect integrity.

With these assumptions, the adversary cannot forge hardware signatures, produce valid attestations inconsistent with the measured launch, or extract sealed keys from the
TPM
. The following Sections
4
and
5
describe our proposed architecture and evaluate how it achieves the desired guarantees.

5.1
Security Goals

Our design aims to provide the verifier with clear and enforceable guarantees against a powerful host-level adversary. At a high level, the verifier must be convinced that attestation evidence (

These goals apply in both deployment scenarios. In

are properly provisioned and tightly linked to the

5.2
Possible Attack Strategies

5.2.1
Relay/Proxy Attacks

This attack stems from the premise that the tenant cannot communicate with the physical
TPM
out-of-band. A malicious Host Manager
\raisebox{0.35pt}{\kern0.8mm
\scalebox{0.70}{M}
}⃝
can initiate an attack by relaying each quote request to a second, honest machine it controls (attack
A2
). The verifier still receives a valid certificate chain, yet the returned PCR_0-7 describes the remote decoy server. Meanwhile, the
RTMRs
inside the local
TD
refer to the attacker
-
controlled host, producing the
PCR
/
RTMR
mismatch captured in
A3
. Figure
7(b)
depicts this class of attacks that we refer to as a "mix-and-match" view, where
TD
and
TPM
evidence come from distinct platforms.

A more advanced variant, which we label as the "Frankenstein attack," has the attacker launch a
TD
locally while relaying
TPM
interactions to a remote cloud host. In this configuration, the tenant’s
TD
runs on the attacker’s hardware, but each
vTPM
interaction is directed to the decoy server employed in
A2
. The naive relay attack still discloses the
PCR
/
RTMR
mismatch described in
A3
, whereas the sophisticated Frankenstein declination only succeeds if the adversary synchronizes nonces and reproduces matching
PCR
digests and hashes. This attack introduces additional complexity, changing a previously detectable inconsistency (
A3
) into a covert attack.

Even without moving the
TD
,
\raisebox{0.35pt}{\kern0.8mm
\scalebox{0.70}{M}
}⃝
can abuse its network position to tamper with attestation channels. It can intercept or amend messages on the
TD
-
vTPM
path (
A4
), on the host-
TPM
path (
A4
), or simply replay previously captured quotes (
A1
).
A4
mimics trustworthiness without performing the full Frankenstein relay.

5.2.2
Platform identity

Another attack vector involves forging the identity of the platform itself. A malicious host may reuse or fake a valid
EK
certificate from a trusted
TPM
(e.g., issued by Intel or Google Cloud), misleading the verifier into believing the attestation originates from a legitimate platform (
A5
). This problem is worse if the verifier only checks the certificate and does not independently compare measurement-based evidence.

5.2.3
vTPM manipulation

The
vTPM
itself can be a target for manipulation.
The host may instantiate a modified
vTPM
binary that does not follow expected specifications, leaks cryptographic material, or signs attacker-controlled measurement values (

The adversary may also substitute the expected

The adversary can also inject an incorrect or misleading

vTPM

A3

Step 2: Provision and Seal the Attestation Key

The

using

Step 3: In-Guest Evidence Binding

The (v)
TPM

via a paravirtual transport (e.g.,

,

TD

TD

vTPM
is provider-operated in

escrow secrets in the

signature validate hardware provenance. Where appropriate, simultaneous challenges and tight
Round Trip Time (RTT)
bounds can expose relay attacks. These challenges convert hardware measurements and bindings into verifier-visible signals that detect forgery, replay, proxying, and inconsistency, even when the transport is fully under the host’s control.

6.2
Attack Mitigations

The section summarizes how the
DCEA
protocol, as described in Steps 1 to 4, mitigates the attacks covered in
Table

2
.

A1: Quote / measurement forgery.

TD
reports are unforgeable under the
TD
’s
QE
’s signature, and
vTPM
quotes are unforgeable under the
AK
’s signature (
Step 4
). Sealing the
AK
to
PCR
17-18 (
Step 2
) blocks key use after any host-stack change, so no forged quotes can be produced off the measured state. The
TD
binds the expected
hash(AK_{pub})
(
Step 3
), which further ensures that only the attested key can produce an acceptable signature. Together, signatures prevent forgery, and
PCR
policy prevents post-change misuse.

A3: Measurement inconsistency.

By extension, to the previous mitigation, the verifier cross-checks
TD

RTMRs
against
PCR
17–18 (Step 4). Any mismatch is detected immediately. The
TD

\leftrightarrow
vTPM
binding (
Step 3
) guarantees the two artifacts refer to the same machine, making "silent drift" observable. This applies to both
S1
&
S2
.

A5: Identity & key substitution.

AK
provenance is tied to the platform’s
EK
using an
EK
-anchored registrar or provider-issued
AK
certificate. This is checked during verification in
Step 2
and
Step 4
. The
TD
embeds the exact
AK_{pub}
hash it expects (
Step 3
) so that a substituted key will not validate against the
TD
report. Similarly, a spoofed
EK
chain is rejected during platform-origin checks. It applies to
S1
&
S2
.

A6: Privilege-level component compromise.

Host-level changes, such as a tampered
vTPM
binary, are measured into
PCR
18 at launch (
Step 1
). The sealed
AK
becomes unusable under the new measurements (
Step 2
). Unable to use the real
AK
, the attacker is left with the only option to spoof a new
AK
, but quotes from the altered stack will not verify against the TD-embedded
hash(AK_{pub})
(
Step 3
), which the verifier confirms during composite-evidence checks (
Step 4
). Altered binaries will modify
PCR
18, which fails
AK
policy, hence, the corresponding quote will be rejected. This mainly affects
S2
, but also constrains provider stack changes in
S1
.

A4: Channel interception / tampering.

Although the paravirtual transport is host-controlled, it does not undermine endpoint integrity because signatures are verified under the bound AK (
Steps 3–4
), and freshness is enforced by nonces (
Step 4
). Reordering, replay, or injection cannot produce an acceptable composite attestation without the attested key and state. (Applies to
S1
&
S2
.)

Targeted Defense for Relay/Proxy (A2)

Mitigating a "Frankenstein" proxy that launches a
TD
locally while relaying all (v)
TPM
interactions to a remote host requires all the steps of the protocol: (i) the
TD
-bundled
AK
hash exposes cross-machine quotes; (ii)
AK
sealing to
PCR
17–18 prevents key use off-platform; (iii) nonce freshness defeats replays; (iv) and concurrent challenges with timing bounds make long proxy paths observable. Together, these measures render A2 detectable or impractical under our threat model.

7
Discussion

7.1
Comparing Scenarios: Challenges and Limitations

The protocol, threat model and analysis of potential attacks and mitigations identify significant trade-offs and considerations when deploying
DCEA
systems in multitenant cloud environments.
Both evaluated scenarios:
S1
and
S2
leverage similar foundational attestation flows, but differ significantly in flexibility, trust assumptions, and control granularity.

Functionally, both scenarios employ a similar attestation flow, leveraging
TDX
quotes, cryptographic binding to
AK
, and remote verifiability of
PCR
values.
S2
, when available on the underlying cloud, is the preferred deployment model due to its greater control, flexibility, and auditability.
S2
requires that additional components of the stack, such as the hypervisor and BIOS, should be made open-source to enable third parties to verify the stack details.
With a fully managed bare-metal setup, tenants can enforce per-
TD
unique identifiers, customize
PCR
measurement policies, and benefit from boot-time integrity guarantees via
TXT
all the way to the hypervisor.
Although this approach increases complexity, such as requiring extra logic to be deployed in the
TD
to evaluate quote freshness or binding, it offers stronger assurance and is more resilient to replay or proxy-based attacks.

A practical limitation lies in the dependency on
PCR
measurement flows.
These may differ between platforms or virtualization stacks, especially in
S1
where the tenant has no control over the
vTPM
instantiation and the way (v)
PCR
are appended.
For
S2
, such configurations are at least inspectable by the Host Manager
\raisebox{0.35pt}{\kern0.8mm
\scalebox{0.70}{M}
}⃝
using a specific
vTPM
implementation.
The
PCR
value interpretation, especially for higher-indexed registers, may not always reflect a uniform boot flow unless explicitly standardized across providers
[
19
]
.
This discrepancy may create challenges when comparing the
PCR
values with
RTMRs
, making reproducibility harder in such cases.
If the
PCR
and
RTMR
values do not match, a mechanism must be implemented to reconstruct the sequence of events that led to a given
PCR
population.
This could be resolved if
vTPM
is provided along with the
TD
as a part of the
TCB
from the chip manufacturer.
Such a solution would unify the deployments across different providers and provide better security guarantees instead of
vTPM
based completely on software, as is the case for
S1
.
Similarly, to harden the host OS’s capabilities, using a minimal kernel/image with basic functionality and reproducible builds would be beneficial.

7.2
Offering Unique Identities in Multitenancy

Next, in multitenant environments, it is possible to reuse
vTPM
components and their respective
AK
.
In practice, a single
vTPM
certificate chain may be shared across multiple
CVMs
on the same physical host.
While technically feasible, this weakens the uniqueness guarantees of attestation and may complicate the detection of identity cloning or proxy attacks.
Ideally, each
TD
or
CVM
should be bound to a uniquely provisioned
AK
.
This requirement is especially critical in environments where tenants lack the ability to control or verify host configurations.

To enhance accountability and enforce the uniqueness of
AKs
, a global or domain-scoped registry of
AK
public keys could be established.
This registry could be implemented using a
Certificate Transparency (CT)

[
25
]
or registration on public blockchains.
Each newly provisioned
AK
would be registered along with metadata such as the issuing entity, timestamp, and optionally a
TD
identifier or platform attestation.
Verifiers could then consult this registry to detect duplicate
AKs
, ensuring that
CVM
or
TD
is not impersonating nor cloning already provisioned identities.
Additionally, the
TD
itself could verify during boot or session setup whether its
AK
is unique and consistent with expected infrastructure bindings.
Such a registry adds transparency and allows audits to detect violations of identity guarantees across tenants or providers.
In single-tenancy deployments, where a single
CVM
operates per hardware node, verifying the public registry ensures that only one
AK
and its corresponding
EK
are present, confirming expected behavior.

7.3
Privacy Considerations

The
vTPM
’s certificate chain often encodes deployment details such as
cloud region and availability zone
[
43
]
.
Forwarding the entire
vTPM
quote to the verifier would therefore expose information the tenant may wish to keep private.
In the flow of
Figure

6
, the
TD
already holds both artifacts, so the obvious alternative is to perform the consistency check inside the
TD
and release only a single-bit result.
A possible solution to retain public verifiability without leaking the chains is to generate a
Zero-Knowledge Proof (ZKP)
to convince the verifier that first, the
vTPM
quote and
TD
attestation report are consistent, and second, the certificate chain of
vTPM
is included in a pre-defined set of approved cloud providers.

Concretely, cloud vendors would maintain a list of root certificates from which the
vTPM
certificate chain is derived. The prover could then show set inclusion via a Merkle tree membership proof
[
12
]
.
The same approach applies to
PPIDs
-based solution, where the cloud provider lists the
PPIDs
running in their infrastructure, and the verifier can query it.
We do not impose any specific requirements on the proof system.
The resulting circuit should fit comfortably within a modern
TD
memory.
The main challenge this method would likely face is convincing cloud providers to publish the input material required as part of the
ZKP
extension.

7.4
Related Work

A particular research avenue focuses on location-binding attestations for
DCEA
, aligning with our threat model that aims to ensure computations occur within specific, trusted environments
[
35
,
8
,
44
]
.
Existing solutions typically rely on latency-based verification or external geolocation signals such as GPS or cellular networks.
However, these approaches either suffer from noise-induced inaccuracies on the network path or require additional hardware that may be exposed to the
CVM
and lack the required precision
[
35
]
.
In contrast, our approach builds on already available infrastructure, enabling extensibility within existing deployment strategies.

Our threat model introduces the "Frankenstein proxy" attack, where a malicious host attempts to convince a verifier that a
TD
is running in a trusted environment when it is not.
The proxy attack is similar to the concatenation attack introduced in
[
48
]
, but expands to attackers owning multiple hardware devices rather than a single platform.
Malicious hypervisor proxying requests away from
vTPM
motivates also the
vtpm-td
solution, which runs a dedicated
vTPM
in a
TD

[
20
]
.
Other similar classes of proxy and replay attacks have been explored in prior work, such as
[
40
]
, which introduces the Time-Of-Check to Time-Of-Use (TOCTOU) problem in remote attestation.
This vulnerability arises when attestation results become stale due to the time gap between measurement and execution, enabling adversaries to evade detection by quickly unloading malicious software.
While our solution does not implement continuous attestation, it lays the foundation for stronger assurance by tightly coupling the
TD
’s launch to the host’s measured state using Intel
TXT
and
vTPM
mechanisms. As shown in
Appendix

B
,
TPM
overheads are low, making continuous attestation feasible.
Any post-launch changes in the firmware or hypervisor would alter these measurements and be detectable on re-attestation.
As part of our protocol implementation, solutions such as TGX
[
16
]
could be used, as they combine
TPM
with Intel
SGX
for generation of MRSIGNER key
[
16
]
.

From a privacy perspective, prior work has highlighted concerns regarding sensitive information disclosure in
CT
logs
[
33
,
23
]
. In our setting, we envision the use of
ZKPs
to allow a
TD
to prove membership in a set of trusted hardware identities without revealing the exact one. Privacy-preserving mechanisms such as private set membership proofs or anonymous credential schemes could be integrated to mitigate traceability risks.

Finally, the
DCEA
concept is not limited to cloud deployments: a trusted certifying authority could vouch for on-premise hardware by combining tamper-evident enclosures and intrusion-detection records, much like Apple’s hardware-integrity program
[
11
]
.

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we addressed a long-standing gap in
CVMs
’ threat model: standard
CVM
attestations prove
what
code is running but not
where
, leaving external clients exposed to hosts that run confidential workloads on hardware they control.
We therefore adopted a threat model in which the entire host software stack may be adversarial.
The external client, or verifier, is an independent party that needs hardware-rooted evidence of both code identity and physical location.

DCEA
contributes three building blocks towards this problem.
First, it layers
TPM
quotes, Intel
TXT
measurements, and sealed
vTPM
identities to create a verifiable chain from chassis to guest, even when the host OS and hypervisor are malicious.
Second, it introduces
PCR
-
RTMR
cross-checks that defeat advanced relay attacks, including the newly described Frankenstein Proxy attack.
This attack consists of the host booting the
TD
on hardware it fully controls while proxying
TPM
requests to another server.
The Frankenstein Proxy is the most challenging of the six software-only vectors we identified, along with forgery, mismatched digests, relay, channel tampering, identity spoofing, and
vTPM
manipulation.
Third, we propose a reference implementation and reproducible measurement policies that run unchanged on today’s
TDX
hardware and discrete
TPMs
. These pieces collectively negate all six attacks identified in our security analysis.

The
CVM
threat model is not one-size-fits-all: while many enterprise workloads tolerate the assumption that providers will not mount physical attacks, permissionless and adversarial settings are the norm in decentralized systems. These new settings demand cryptographic evidence of platform provenance.
DCEA
supplies this "Proof of Cloud" by binding guest attestation to provider-anchored measured boot, allowing remote verifiers to confirm execution on trusted data-center hardware rather than on-premises or untrusted hosts. By closing the location-awareness gap,
DCEA
unlocks the potential of confidential computing to support emerging industries, such as
DeFi
, which are actively building on
CVM
. Fully trustworthy multi-tenant cloud infrastructure lowers the barriers of entry for new service operators, which, by definition, fosters decentralization.

per
vTPM
instance ensures that any attestation token or quote can be traced to a single
CVM
, irrespective of how many
TDs
or classical
VMs
run on the same host.
Without such uniqueness, cryptographic evidence loses its provenance: two
CVMs
could present the same public key and therefore be indistinguishable to an external relying party, in case the workload is the same.

In the provisioning flow, the operator generates a fresh
AK
for every
vTPM
instance during
CVM
instantiation.
The
AK
can then be embedded in the
TD
’s
report_data
(or encoded in a provider-signed
vTPM
certificate), any remote verifier can bind the
TD
’s quote to that exact
vTPM
.
A theoretical hazard arises when the host software is allowed to launch
TDs
from arbitrary images without inspecting their embedded
vTPM
state and respective
PCR
values.
If the host manager
\raisebox{0.35pt}{\kern0.8mm
\scalebox{0.70}{M}
}⃝
accidentally clones a snapshot that already contains an
AK
, the new
CVM
inherits the same key material.
Both tenants would then share a single attestation identity even though their runtime stacks may diverge, undermining auditability and potentially enabling cross-
VM
replay of signed quotes.
Hence, the platform’s control plane must provide a post-launch sanity check that rejects any instance whose
AK
collides with an existing entry in the provider’s key registry.
Overall, if the provider creates two identical
CVMs
with the same
AK
, it can be recognized.
This, in general, means that the security of higher-level protocols rests on the assumption that no two distinct
TDs
ever share the same
AK
.
