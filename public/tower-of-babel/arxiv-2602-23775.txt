Novel Stein-type Characterizations of Bivariate Count Distributions with Applications
Shaochen Wang, Christian H. Weiß

Abstract
The derivation and application of Stein identities have received considerable research interest in recent years, especially for continuous or discrete-univariate distributions. In this paper, we complement the existing literature by deriving and investigating Stein-type characterizations for the three most common types of bivariate count distributions, namely the bivariate Poisson, binomial, and negative-binomial distribution. Then, we demonstrate the practical relevance of these novel Stein identities by a couple of applications, namely the deduction of sophisticated moment expressions, of flexible goodness-of-fit tests, and of novel tests for the symmetry of bivariate count distributions. The paper concludes with an analysis of real-world data examples.



Keywords
: bivariate binomial distribution; bivariate negative-binomial distribution; bivariate Poisson distribution; goodness-of-fit tests; Stein characterization; symmetry tests.

Introduction

The idea to uniquely characterize a distribution family by certain moment identities dates back to
stein72
;
stein86
, and one thus speaks of “Stein’s method” and “Stein identities” in this regard. Stein identities have been developed for various distribution families, see
sudheesh09
;
sudheesh12
;
landsman16
;
wang2023
for examples and references. In particular, such Stein identities have been derived for several
univariate
count distributions, i.e., referring to some discrete-quantitative random variable
X
with range contained in
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
.
Probably the most relevant cases for practice are, see
sudheesh12
:

Poisson (Poi) distribution:

X\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\mu)
with
\mu>0
if and only if

\mathbb{E}\big[X\,f(X)\big]\ =\ \mu\,\mathbb{E}\big[f(X+1)\big]

(1.1)

holds for all bounded functions
f:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
;

binomial (Bin) distribution:

X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(N,\pi)
with
N\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
and
\pi\in(0,1)
if and only if

(1-\pi)\,\mathbb{E}\big[X\,f(X)\big]=\pi\,\mathbb{E}\big[(N-X)\,f(X+1)\big]

(1.2)

holds for all bounded functions
f:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
;

negative-binomial (NB) distribution:

X\sim\mathrm{NB}(\nu,\pi)
with
\nu>0
and
\pi\in(0,1)
if and only if

\mathbb{E}\big[X\,f(X)\big]\ =\ (1-\pi)\,\mathbb{E}\big[(\nu+X)\,f(X+1)\big]

(1.3)

holds for all bounded functions
f:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
.

The first identity, (
1.1
), is usually referred to as the Stein–Chen identity, in view of the contribution by
chen75
.

Univariate Stein identities, such as those in (
1.1
)–(
1.3
), have found various applications in the literature. They have been used for moment computations
(
weiss2022
)
, for developing goodness-of-fit tests
(
betsch22
;
weiss2023
)
as well as control charts
(
weiss2025
)
, for generalized types of moment estimation
(
wang2023
;
nik2024
;
ebner25
)
, and they have also been applied in the time-series case
(
aleksandrov22
;
aleksandrov24
)
. In what follows, however, we are not concerned with univariate count random variables, but we consider
bivariate
count distributions instead. More precisely, we extend the aforementioned univariate Stein identities (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) to the respective bivariate case, namely by considering the bivariate Poisson distribution in Section
2
, the bivariate binomial distribution (of type I) in Section
3
, and the bivariate negative-binomial distribution in Section
4
. Afterwards, Section
5
presents various directions for applying the bivariate Stein characterizations. We demonstrate that our novel Stein identities allow to derive demanding moment expressions, to develop bivariate goodness-of-fit tests as well as tests for the symmetry of a bivariate distribution, and we illustrate their application in practice by a couple of real-world data examples. Finally, we conclude in Section
6
and outline opportunities for future research.

2
Stein Characterization of Bivariate Poisson Distribution

The bivariate Poisson distribution is a probability distribution that models the joint occurrence of two non-negative integer-valued random variables,
(X_{1},X_{2})
with range
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}
, and is often used to describe paired count data with possible cross-correlation. It extends the univariate Poisson distribution by incorporating a covariance structure, making it suitable for applications where two events are related, such as the frequency of defects in two production lines, or the number of goals scored by two teams in a sports match
(
karlis03
)
.

Given three independent random variables
Z_{0},Z_{1},Z_{2}
with
Z_{i}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{i})
for
i=0,1,2
, the bivariate count random vector

\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},X_{2})^{\top}=(Z_{0}+Z_{1},\ Z_{0}+Z_{2})^{\top}

(2.4)

is said to be bivariately Poisson (BPoi) distributed according to
\mathrm{BPoi}(\lambda_{0};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
.
The marginals satisfy
X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{i})
for
i=1,2
, and the cross-covariance equals
\operatorname{Cov}(X_{1},X_{2})=\lambda_{0}
. The BPoi-distribution is suitable for equidispersed count, i.e., where
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\mathbb{V}[X_{i}]
is satisfied for
i=1,2
.
Characterized by parameters for each variable’s mean and their covariance, this distribution provides a flexible framework for analyzing dependent count data in fields like statistics, epidemiology, and engineering, see
kocherlakota14
for further details and references.

Theorem 1

(Stein characterization of bivariate Poisson distribution)
.

Suppose
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\mathrm{BPoi}(\lambda_{0};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
, then for any function

f:\mathbb{N}_{0}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
such that the following expectation exist, the following two identities hold:

\mathbb{E}[X_{1}\,f(X_{1},X_{2})]=\lambda_{1}\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]+\lambda_{0}\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)],

(2.5)

\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\,f(X_{1},X_{2})]=\lambda_{2}\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)]+\lambda_{0}\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)].

(2.6)

Conversely, if the identities (
2.5
)–(
2.6
) hold for all
f:\mathbb{N}_{0}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
, and assuming that
(X_{1},X_{2})
is a non-negative integer-valued random vector, then
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\mathrm{BPoi}(\lambda_{0};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
.

Note that the identities (
2.5
)–(
2.6
) include identities (15)–(16) in
weiss2022
as a special case.

Proof.

We first suppose
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\mathrm{BPoi}(\lambda_{0};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
and write
p(x,y):=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=x,X_{2}=y)
as the probability mass function (pmf) of
(X_{1},X_{2})
. Recall that (see Section 4.1.1 in
kocherlakota14
)

\begin{array}[]{l}x\,p(x,y)=\lambda_{1}\,p(x-1,y)+\lambda_{0}\,p(x-1,y-1),\\[4.30554pt]
y\,p(x,y)=\lambda_{2}\,p(x,y-1)+\lambda_{0}\,p(x-1,y-1),\end{array}

(2.7)

with the convention
p(x,y)=0
if
x<0
or
y<0
. Now, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}\,f(X_{1},X_{2})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}x\,f(x,y)\,p(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}f(x,y)\,[\lambda_{1}\,p(x-1,y)+\lambda_{0}\,p(x-1,y-1)]

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\sum_{x,y}f(x,y)\,p(x-1,y)+\lambda_{0}\sum_{x,y}f(x,y)\,p(x-1,y-1)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]+\lambda_{0}\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)],

which proves (
2.5
). (
2.6
) can be proved similarly or by a symmetric argument, so we omit the details here.

Conversely, let (
2.5
)–(
2.6
) hold.
For an arbitrary function
g:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
, define
f(x,y)=g(y)
for all
x\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then, (
2.6
) yields

\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\,g(X_{2})]=(\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{2})\,\mathbb{E}[g(X_{2}+1)].

Thus, from the Stein–Chen characterization (
1.1
) of the Poisson distribution, we know
X_{2}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{2})
; analogously, one proves
X_{1}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1})
.

Next, we take
f(x,y)=s^{x}t^{y}
,
|s|,|t|<1
, and denote the joint probability generating function (pgf) of
(X_{1},X_{2})
as
G(s,t)=\mathbb{E}[s^{X_{1}}t^{X_{2}}],|s|,|t|<1
. Then, from (
2.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle s\,\frac{\partial}{\partial s}G(s,t)=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}\,s^{X_{1}}t^{X_{2}}]=\lambda_{1}\,s\,G(s,t)+\lambda_{0}\,st\,G(s,t),

that is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}G(s,t)=\lambda_{1}\,G(s,t)+\lambda_{0}\,t\,G(s,t)=(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{0}\,t)\,G(s,t)

holds for all
|s|,|t|<1
. The above partial differential equation can be solved by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\ln G(s,t)=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{0}t,

which further implies
G(s,t)=\exp\{\lambda_{1}s+\lambda_{0}st+\theta(t)\}
, where
\theta(t)
is a function only depends on
t
. Observe that on the one hand,
G(1,t)=\mathbb{E}[t^{X_{2}}]=\exp\big((\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{2})(t-1)\big)
because of
X_{2}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{2})
. On the other hand,
G(1,t)=\exp\{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{0}t+\theta(t)\}
. Thus, we get
\theta(t)=(t-1)\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{1}
. So finally,

G(s,t)=\exp\Big\{\lambda_{1}(s-1)+\lambda_{2}(t-1)+\lambda_{0}(st-1)\Big\},\quad|s|,|t|<1,

which uniquely determines the joint pmf of
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\mathrm{BPoi}(\lambda_{0};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.
∎

Remark 1
.

If
\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{2}=0
, i.e.,
X_{2}\equiv 0
, then (
2.5
) reduces to the well-known Stein characterization (
1.1
) of the univariate Poisson distribution.

3
Stein Characterization of Bivariate Binomial Distribution

Let
\mathbf{Z}_{k}:=(Z_{k1},Z_{k2})^{\top}
,
k\geq 1
, be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) bivariate Bernoulli random vectors with parameters
a_{1},a_{2}\in(0,1)
and
0<a<\min\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
. The common joint pmf of
\mathbf{Z}_{k}
is given by

p_{ij}:=\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{Z}_{k}=(i,j)^{\top}),\quad i,j\in\{0,1\},

with

p_{11}=a,\quad p_{10}+p_{11}=a_{1},\quad p_{01}+p_{11}=a_{2}.

(3.8)

Equivalently, one may use the correlation parameter

\phi_{a}:=\operatorname{Corr}(Z_{k1},Z_{k2})=\frac{a-a_{1}a_{2}}{\sqrt{a_{1}a_{2}(1-a_{1})(1-a_{2})}}.

Define the random vector

\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},X_{2})^{\top}:=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{Z}_{k}\quad\text{with }N\in\mathbb{N}.

Then,
\mathbf{X}
is said to follow the
bivariate binomial (BVB) distribution of type I
with parameters
N,a_{1},a_{2},\phi_{a}
, denoted by

\mathbf{X}\sim\mathrm{BVB}(N;a_{1},a_{2},\phi_{a}).

Its range is
\{0,1,\dots,N\}^{2}
, the marginal distributions are
X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(N,a_{i})
for
i=1,2
, and
\operatorname{Corr}(X_{1},X_{2})=\phi_{a}
.
According to
kocherlakota14
, the joint pgf of
\mathbf{X}
is

G(z_{1},z_{2}):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[z_{1}^{X_{1}}z_{2}^{X_{2}}\bigr]=\bigl(p_{00}+p_{10}z_{1}+p_{01}z_{2}+p_{11}z_{1}z_{2}\bigr)^{N},

(3.9)

where
p_{00},p_{10},p_{01},p_{11}
are determined by (
3.8
).

Theorem 2

(Stein characterization of bivariate binomial distribution)
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed and
(X_{1},X_{2})
be a non-negative random vector with range
\{0,1,\ldots,N\}^{2}
. Then,
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\mathrm{BVB}(N;a_{1},a_{2},\phi_{a})
if and only if for all functions
f:\{0,1,\ldots,N\}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, the following two identities hold:

\displaystyle p_{00}\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1},X_{2})]+p_{01}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)]

(3.10)

\displaystyle=p_{10}\,\mathbb{E}[(N-X_{1})\,f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]+p_{11}\,\mathbb{E}[(N-X_{1})\,f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)],

and

\displaystyle p_{00}\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{2}f(X_{1},X_{2})]+p_{10}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{2}f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]

(3.11)

\displaystyle=p_{01}\,\mathbb{E}[(N-X_{2})\,f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)]+p_{11}\,\mathbb{E}[(N-X_{2})\,f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)],

where the parameters
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
, and
\phi_{a}
are uniquely determined by
p_{ij}
with
i,j\in\{0,1\}
via (
3.8
).

Proof.

We first prove the necessity part. Let
p(x_{1},x_{2})
denote the joint pmf of
\mathrm{BVB}(N;a_{1},a_{2},\phi_{a})
, and let
G(z_{1},z_{2})
be its joint pgf defined in (
3.9
). Differentiating
G
with respect to
z_{1}
and then multiplying both sides by
P(z_{1},z_{2})
yields

P(z_{1},z_{2})\frac{\partial G}{\partial z_{1}}=N(p_{10}+p_{11}z_{2})\,G(z_{1},z_{2}),

(3.12)

where
P(z_{1},z_{2})=p_{00}+p_{10}z_{1}+p_{01}z_{2}+p_{11}z_{1}z_{2}
is the pgf of the bivariate Bernoulli distribution (
3.8
). We rewrite the left-hand side of (
3.12
) as

\displaystyle P(z_{1},z_{2})\frac{\partial G}{\partial z_{1}}

\displaystyle=(p_{00}+p_{10}z_{1}+p_{01}z_{2}+p_{11}z_{1}z_{2})\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2})\,z_{1}^{x_{1}-1}z_{2}^{x_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2})\bigl[p_{00}\,z_{1}^{x_{1}-1}z_{2}^{x_{2}}+p_{10}\,z_{1}^{x_{1}}z_{2}^{x_{2}}+p_{01}\,z_{1}^{x_{1}-1}z_{2}^{x_{2}+1}+p_{11}\,z_{1}^{x_{1}}z_{2}^{x_{2}+1}\bigr].

(3.13)

Then, we reindex each term as follows:

•

p_{00}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2})z_{1}^{x_{1}-1}z_{2}^{x_{2}}=p_{00}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}(x_{1}+1)p(x_{1}+1,x_{2})z_{1}^{x_{1}}z_{2}^{x_{2}}
,

•

p_{10}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2})z_{1}^{x_{1}}z_{2}^{x_{2}}=p_{10}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2})z_{1}^{x_{1}}z_{2}^{x_{2}}
,

•

p_{01}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2})z_{1}^{x_{1}-1}z_{2}^{x_{2}+1}=p_{01}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}(x_{1}+1)p(x_{1}+1,x_{2}-1)z_{1}^{x_{1}}z_{2}^{x_{2}}
,

•

p_{11}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2})z_{1}^{x_{1}}z_{2}^{x_{2}+1}=p_{11}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2}-1)z_{1}^{x_{1}}z_{2}^{x_{2}}
.

The right-hand side of (
3.12
) is given by

\displaystyle N(p_{10}+p_{11}z_{2})G(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=Np_{10}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}p(x_{1},x_{2})z_{1}^{x_{1}}z_{2}^{x_{2}}+Np_{11}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}p(x_{1},x_{2})z_{1}^{x_{1}}z_{2}^{x_{2}+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}\bigl[Np_{10}p(x_{1},x_{2})+Np_{11}p(x_{1},x_{2}-1)\bigr]z_{1}^{x_{1}}z_{2}^{x_{2}}.

(3.14)

Equating coefficients of
z_{1}^{x_{1}}z_{2}^{x_{2}}
in (
3
) and (
3
), we obtain the recurrence relation

\displaystyle p_{00}(x_{1}+1)p(x_{1}+1,x_{2})+p_{10}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle+p_{01}(x_{1}+1)p(x_{1}+1,x_{2}-1)+p_{11}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2}-1)

\displaystyle=Np_{10}p(x_{1},x_{2})+Np_{11}p(x_{1},x_{2}-1).

Multiplying both sides of the above relation by an arbitrary test function
f(x_{1}+1,x_{2})
, summing over all
x_{1},x_{2}
and re-indexing each term, we get that the left-hand side is equal to the sum of the following four terms:

•

Term 1: Let
y_{1}=x_{1}+1
, then

p_{00}\sum_{y_{1},x_{2}}f(y_{1},x_{2})y_{1}p(y_{1},x_{2})=p_{00}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1},X_{2})].

•

Term 2: We have

p_{10}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}f(x_{1}+1,x_{2})x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2})=p_{10}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})].

•

Term 3: Let
y_{1}=x_{1}+1
,
y_{2}=x_{2}-1
, then

p_{01}\sum_{y_{1},y_{2}}f(y_{1},y_{2}+1)y_{1}p(y_{1},y_{2})=p_{01}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)].

•

Term 4: Let
y_{2}=x_{2}-1
, then

p_{11}\sum_{x_{1},y_{2}}f(x_{1}+1,y_{2}+1)x_{1}p(x_{1},y_{2})=p_{11}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)].

The corresponding right-hand side equals

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}f(x_{1}+1,x_{2})

\displaystyle\bigl[Np_{10}p(x_{1},x_{2})+Np_{11}p(x_{1},x_{2}-1)\bigr]

\displaystyle=Np_{10}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}f(x_{1}+1,x_{2})p(x_{1},x_{2})+Np_{11}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}f(x_{1}+1,x_{2})p(x_{1},x_{2}-1)

\displaystyle=Np_{10}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]+Np_{11}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)].

Thus, we finally get

\displaystyle p_{00}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1},X_{2})]

\displaystyle+p_{10}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]

\displaystyle+p_{01}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)]+p_{11}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)]

\displaystyle=Np_{10}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]+Np_{11}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)],

so (
3.10
) follows. (
3.11
) is proved similarly or by a symmetric argument.

Next, we prove the sufficiency part. To determine the joint distribution, consider the joint pgf

G(s,t)=\mathbb{E}[s^{X_{1}}t^{X_{2}}]
with
|s|,|t|\leq 1
.
First, we choose
f(x,y)=s^{x}t^{y}
in (
3.10
) and compute each term:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1},X_{2})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}s^{X_{1}}t^{X_{2}}]=s\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}(s,t),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}s^{X_{1}+1}t^{X_{2}}]=s^{2}\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}(s,t),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}s^{X_{1}}t^{X_{2}+1}]=st\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}(s,t),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}s^{X_{1}+1}t^{X_{2}+1}]=s^{2}t\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}(s,t),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[s^{X_{1}+1}t^{X_{2}}]=s\,G(s,t),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[s^{X_{1}+1}t^{X_{2}+1}]=st\,G(s,t).

Substituting into (
3.10
):

p_{00}s\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}+p_{10}s^{2}\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}+p_{01}st\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}+p_{11}s^{2}t\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}=Np_{10}s\,G+Np_{11}st\,G.

For
s\neq 0
, we cancel out
s
:

\left[p_{00}+p_{10}s+p_{01}t+p_{11}st\right]\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}=N(p_{10}+p_{11}t)G.

(3.15)

Similarly, from (
3.11
), we obtain

\left[p_{00}+p_{10}s+p_{01}t+p_{11}st\right]\frac{\partial G}{\partial t}=N(p_{01}+p_{11}s)G.

(3.16)

Denoting the Bernoulli pgf by
P(s,t)=p_{00}+p_{10}s+p_{01}t+p_{11}st
again, the equations (
3.15
) and (
3.16
) imply

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log G}{\partial s}=\frac{N(p_{10}+p_{11}t)}{P(s,t)},\quad\frac{\partial\log G}{\partial t}=\frac{N(p_{01}+p_{11}s)}{P(s,t)}.

(3.17)

Integrating the first equation with respect to
s
(treating
t
as a constant), we obtain

\log G(s,t)=N\log P(s,t)+H(t),

where
H(t)
is a function of
t
only. So differentiating this expression with respect to
t
, and
comparing it to the second expression in (
3.17
), we get
H^{\prime}(t)=0
for all
t
. Thus,
H(t)
is a constant, say
H(t)=C
. Since bivariate pgfs satisfy
G(1,1)=1
and
P(1,1)=1
, so we must have
H(1)=0
. Therefore,
H(t)\equiv 0
and, as a consequence,

G(s,t)=[p_{00}+p_{10}s+p_{01}t+p_{11}st]^{N}

holds for
|s|\leq 1,|t|\leq 1
. This is exactly the pgf of
\mathrm{BVB}(N;a_{1},a_{2},\phi_{a})
, where
a_{1}=p_{10}+p_{11}
,
a_{2}=p_{01}+p_{11}
, and

\phi_{a}=\frac{p_{11}-a_{1}a_{2}}{\sqrt{a_{1}a_{2}(1-a_{1})(1-a_{2})}}.

Since the pgf uniquely determines the distribution, we conclude
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\mathrm{BVB}(N;a_{1},a_{2},\phi_{a})
, and the proof of Theorem
2
is complete.
∎

Remark 2
.

When
a_{2}=p_{11}+p_{01}=0
, i.e.,
X_{2}\equiv 0
, the Stein characterization in (
3.10
) reduces to

(1-a_{1})\mathbb{E}[Xf_{0}(X)]=a_{1}\mathbb{E}[(N-X)f_{0}(X+1)]

for any bounded function
f_{0}
defined on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. This is exactly the Stein characterization of
X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(N,a_{1})
, recall (
1.2
).

Remark 3
.

Take
f(x,y)=g(y)
independent of
x
in (
3.11
). Then,

\displaystyle(p_{00}+p_{10})\,\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\,g(X_{2})]

\displaystyle=(p_{01}+p_{11})\,\mathbb{E}[(N-X_{2})\,g(X_{2}+1)],

that is,

(1-a_{2})\,\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\,g(X_{2})]=a_{2}\,\mathbb{E}[(N-X_{2})\,g(X_{2}+1)]

where we used
a_{2}=p_{01}+p_{11}
. This is exactly the classical Stein identity for
X_{2}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(N,a_{2})
, recall (
1.2
), so
X_{2}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(N,a_{2})
. Similarly, taking
f(x,y)=h(x)
in (
3.10
) gives
X_{1}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(N,a_{1})
.

Remark 4
.

Suppose
Np_{11}\to\lambda_{0}
,
Np_{10}\to\lambda_{1}
and
Np_{01}\to\lambda_{2}
for
N\to\infty
, which also implies that
p_{10},p_{01},p_{11}\to 0
and
p_{00}\to 1
. Then, by the Poisson approximation, we have
(X_{1},X_{2})\to_{d}\mathrm{BPoi}(\lambda_{0};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
, and (
3.10
)–(
3.11
) reduce
to (
2.5
)–(
2.6
). So the result in Theorem
2
is compatible with Theorem
1
.

4
Stein Characterization of Bivariate Negative-binomial Distribution

With
\nu\in(0,\infty)
,
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in(0,1)
, and
\pi_{0}\in(-\pi_{1}\pi_{2},1)
such that
\pi_{\bullet}:=\sum_{i=0}^{2}\pi_{i}<1
holds, the bivariate negative-binomial (BNB) distribution
\mathrm{BNB}(\nu;\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{0})
is defined by the bivariate pgf

G(z_{1},z_{2})=\left(\frac{1}{1-\pi_{\bullet}}-\frac{\pi_{1}}{1-\pi_{\bullet}}z_{1}-\frac{\pi_{2}}{1-\pi_{\bullet}}z_{2}-\frac{\pi_{0}}{1-\pi_{\bullet}}z_{1}z_{2}\right)^{-\nu},

(4.18)

see Section 5 in
kocherlakota14
or Section 2.2 in
aleksandrov24
for details. The special case
\pi_{0}=0
is also known as the negative-multinomial distribution.
We have a Poisson limit for
\nu\rightarrow\infty
, namely
\mathrm{BPoi}(\lambda_{0};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
if
\nu\pi_{i}/(1-\pi_{\bullet})\rightarrow\lambda_{i}
for
\nu\rightarrow\infty
. The marginals are univariate NB-distributions, namely
X_{1}\sim\mathrm{NB}(\nu,\frac{1-\pi_{\bullet}}{1-\pi_{2}})
and
X_{2}\sim\mathrm{NB}(\nu,\frac{1-\pi_{\bullet}}{1-\pi_{1}})
, respectively. The corresponding marginal means are

\mu_{1}=\nu\,\frac{\pi_{0}+\pi_{1}}{1-\pi_{\bullet}}

and

\mu_{2}=\nu\,\frac{\pi_{0}+\pi_{2}}{1-\pi_{\bullet}}
, respectively, and the cross-covariance equals
\operatorname{Cov}(X_{1},X_{2})=\nu\,\frac{\pi_{0}+\pi_{1}\pi_{2}}{(1-\pi_{\bullet})^{2}}
. For simulating the
\mathrm{BNB}(\nu;\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{0})
-distribution, it is useful to know that the distribution of
X_{1}|x_{2}
is a convolution of the distributions
\mathrm{Bin}(x_{2},\frac{\pi_{0}}{\pi_{2}+\pi_{0}})
and
\mathrm{NB}(\nu+x_{2},1-\pi_{1})
if
\pi_{0}>0
, and that
X_{1}|x_{2}\sim\mathrm{NB}(\nu+x_{2},1-\pi_{1})
if
\pi_{0}=0
.

Theorem 3

(Stein characterization of bivariate NB distribution)
.

Let
\nu\in(0,\infty)
be fixed and
(X_{1},X_{2})
be a non-negative random vector valued on
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}
. Then
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\mathrm{BNB}(\nu;\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{0})
if and only if for all functions
f:\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, provided that the below moments exist, the following two identities hold:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}\,f(X_{1},X_{2})]

\displaystyle-\pi_{2}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{1}\,f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)]

(4.19)

\displaystyle=\pi_{1}\,\mathbb{E}[(\nu+X_{1})\,f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]+\pi_{0}\,\mathbb{E}[(\nu+X_{1})\,f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)]

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X\,_{2}f(X_{1},X_{2})]

\displaystyle-\pi_{1}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\,f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]

(4.20)

\displaystyle=\pi_{2}\,\mathbb{E}[(\nu+X_{2})\,f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)]+\pi_{0}\,\mathbb{E}[(\nu+X_{2})\,f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)].

Proof.

We first prove the necessity part. Suppose
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\mathrm{BNB}(\nu;\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{0})
. Let
p(x_{1},x_{2})=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=x_{1},X_{2}=x_{2})
denote the joint pmf. From (
4.18
), we have

G(s,t)=\left(\frac{1-\pi_{\bullet}}{1-\pi_{1}s-\pi_{2}t-\pi_{0}st}\right)^{\nu}=\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}p(x_{1},x_{2})s^{x_{1}}t^{x_{2}}.

(4.21)

Differentiating with respect to
s
gives:

(1-\pi_{1}s-\pi_{2}t-\pi_{0}st)\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}=\nu(\pi_{1}+\pi_{0}t)\,G(s,t).

(4.22)

Now, substituting the series expansion from (
4.21
) into (
4.22
) yields

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}

\displaystyle x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2})s^{x_{1}-1}t^{x_{2}}-\pi_{1}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2})s^{x_{1}}t^{x_{2}}-\pi_{2}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2})s^{x_{1}-1}t^{x_{2}+1}

\displaystyle\qquad-\pi_{0}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2})s^{x_{1}}t^{x_{2}+1}

\displaystyle=\nu\pi_{1}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}p(x_{1},x_{2})s^{x_{1}}t^{x_{2}}+\nu\pi_{0}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}p(x_{1},x_{2})s^{x_{1}}t^{x_{2}+1}.

Multiplying both sides by
s
and comparing the coefficients of
s^{x_{1}}t^{x_{2}}
, we get following relation:

\displaystyle x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle-\pi_{1}(x_{1}-1)p(x_{1}-1,x_{2})-\pi_{2}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2}-1)-\pi_{0}(x_{1}-1)p(x_{1}-1,x_{2}-1)

\displaystyle=\nu\pi_{1}p(x_{1}-1,x_{2})+\nu\pi_{0}p(x_{1}-1,x_{2}-1).

(4.23)

Next, we use (
4
) for expressing expectations. Multiplying (
4
) by
f(x_{1},x_{2})
, summing over all possible
x_{1},x_{2}
, and re-indexing each sum, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2})f(x_{1},x_{2})=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1},X_{2})],

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}(x_{1}-1)p(x_{1}-1,x_{2})f(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{y_{1},x_{2}}y_{1}p(y_{1},x_{2})f(y_{1}+1,x_{2})=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})],

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}x_{1}p(x_{1},x_{2}-1)f(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{x_{1},y_{2}}x_{1}p(x_{1},y_{2})f(x_{1},y_{2}+1)=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)],

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}(x_{1}-1)p(x_{1}-1,x_{2}-1)f(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{y_{1},y_{2}}y_{1}p(y_{1},y_{2})f(y_{1}+1,y_{2}+1)=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)],

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}p(x_{1}-1,x_{2})f(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{y_{1},x_{2}}p(y_{1},x_{2})f(y_{1}+1,x_{2})=\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})],

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}p(x_{1}-1,x_{2}-1)f(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{y_{1},y_{2}}p(y_{1},y_{2})f(y_{1}+1,y_{2}+1)=\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)].

As a consequence, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1},X_{2})]

\displaystyle-\pi_{1}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]

\displaystyle-\pi_{2}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)]-\pi_{0}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)]

\displaystyle=\nu\pi_{1}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]+\nu\pi_{0}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)],

which proves (
4.19
). By a symmetric argument, (
4.19
) is obtained similarly.

For the sufficiency part, assume (
4.19
) and (
4.20
) hold for all bounded
f
. Take
f(x,y)=s^{x}t^{y}
with
|s|,|t|<1
, and let
G(s,t)=\mathbb{E}[s^{X_{1}}t^{X_{2}}]
be the corresponding joint pgf of
(X_{1},X_{2})
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}s^{X_{1}}t^{X_{2}}]

\displaystyle=s\frac{\partial G}{\partial s},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}s^{X_{1}+1}t^{X_{2}}]

\displaystyle=s^{2}\frac{\partial G}{\partial s},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}s^{X_{1}}t^{X_{2}+1}]

\displaystyle=st\frac{\partial G}{\partial s},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}s^{X_{1}+1}t^{X_{2}+1}]

\displaystyle=s^{2}t\frac{\partial G}{\partial s},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[s^{X_{1}+1}t^{X_{2}}]

\displaystyle=s\,G(s,t),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[s^{X_{1}+1}t^{X_{2}+1}]

\displaystyle=st\,G(s,t).

Substituting these into (
4.19
), we get following partial differential equation:

s\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}-\pi_{1}s^{2}\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}-\pi_{2}st\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}-\pi_{0}s^{2}t\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}=\nu\pi_{1}s\,G+\nu\pi_{0}st\,G.

(4.24)

Division by
s
, for
s\neq 0
, yields

(1-\pi_{1}s-\pi_{2}t-\pi_{0}st)\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}=\nu(\pi_{1}+\pi_{0}t)\,G.

(4.25)

Similarly, from (
4.20
), we obtain

(1-\pi_{1}s-\pi_{2}t-\pi_{0}st)\frac{\partial G}{\partial t}=\nu(\pi_{2}+\pi_{0}s)\,G.

(4.26)

Dividing (
4.25
) and (
4.26
) by
G
yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log G}{\partial s}=\frac{\nu(\pi_{1}+\pi_{0}t)}{1-\pi_{1}s-\pi_{2}t-\pi_{0}st},\quad\frac{\partial\log G}{\partial t}=\frac{\nu(\pi_{2}+\pi_{0}s)}{1-\pi_{1}s-\pi_{2}t-\pi_{0}st}.

(4.27)

Integrating the first equation in (
4.27
) with respect to
s
(treating
t
as constant), we get

\log G(s,t)=-\nu\log(1-\pi_{1}s-\pi_{2}t-\pi_{0}st)+H(t),

(4.28)

where
H(t)
depends only on
t
. Differentiating (
4.28
) with respect to
t
and comparing with the second equation of (
4.27
) gives

\frac{\nu(\pi_{2}+\pi_{0}s)}{1-\pi_{1}s-\pi_{2}t-\pi_{0}st}+H^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\nu(\pi_{2}+\pi_{0}s)}{1-\pi_{1}s-\pi_{2}t-\pi_{0}st}.

(4.29)

Thus,
H^{\prime}(t)=0
, so
H(t)
is constant. Since
G(1,1)=1
, (
4.28
) implies the equality

0=-\nu\log(1-\pi_{1}-\pi_{2}-\pi_{0})+H(1).

(4.30)

Therefore
H(t)\equiv\nu\log(1-\pi_{\bullet})
, and thus

G(s,t)=\left(\frac{1-\pi_{\bullet}}{1-\pi_{1}s-\pi_{2}t-\pi_{0}st}\right)^{\nu},

(4.31)

which is exactly (
4.18
). This completes the proof of Theorem
3
.
∎

Remark 5
.

When
\nu\to\infty
and
\nu\pi_{i}\to\lambda_{i}
for
i=0,1,2
(hence,
\pi_{i}\to 0
for
i=0,1,2
), then (
4.19
) and (
4.20
) reduce to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}f(X_{1},X_{2})]

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]+\lambda_{0}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{2}f(X_{1},X_{2})]

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)]+\lambda_{0}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)],

which are exactly the Stein characterizations for the bivariate Poisson distribution in Theorem
1
.

Remark 6
.

When
\pi_{2}=\pi_{0}=0
, the
\mathrm{BNB}(\nu;\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{0})
distribution reduces to a univariate NB-distribution for
X_{1}
and
X_{2}\equiv 0
. In this case,
\pi_{\bullet}=\pi_{1}
, and the Stein characterization (
4.19
) becomes

\mathbb{E}[X_{1}g(X_{1})]=\pi_{1}\mathbb{E}[(\nu+X_{1})g(X_{1}+1)]

(4.32)

for some univariate bounded function
g
. This is the classical Stein characterization for
\mathrm{NB}(\nu,1-\pi_{1})
, recall (
1.3
). We can obtain similar result if we take functions
f(x,y)
that depend on only one of the variables
x,y
, in analogy to Remark
3
.

5
Applications

As briefly surveyed in Section
1
, Stein characterizations of distributions generally offer a broad potential for applications. In what follows, we briefly sketch possible application areas of the Stein characterizations for the bivariate Poisson, binomial, and negative-binomial distribution as provided by Theorems
1
–
3
, whereas a more comprehensive investigation will be conducted in separate articles as part of future research.

5.1
Moment Calculations

In
weiss2022
, Stein identities of the bivariate Poisson distribution were used for moment computations, namely by deriving recursive schemes for certain families of joint moments. Since their investigations have been quite comprehensive, we just add one illustrative example to the
\mathrm{BPoi}(\lambda_{0};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
-derivations of
weiss2022
, namely concerning the quadratic deviation between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
. Taking the difference of equations (
2.5
) and (
2.6
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}[(X_{1}-X_{2})\,f(X_{1},X_{2})]=\lambda_{1}\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]-\lambda_{2}\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)].

(5.33)

Hence, using
f(x,y)=x-y
, it immediately follows from (
5.33
) that

\mathbb{E}[(X_{1}-X_{2})^{2}]=\lambda_{1}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{1}+1-X_{2}]-\lambda_{2}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{1}-X_{2}-1]=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})^{2}.

(5.34)

The expected absolute difference, in turn, follows by using the sign function
f(x,y)=\textrm{sgn}(x-y)
instead.

Considerably less moment formulae have been derived for the bivariate binomial and NB distribution so far.
The novel Stein identities for the bivariate binomial distribution in Section
3
and for the bivariate NB distribution in Section
4
can be applied in a similar way as before, which is illustrated in the sequel for some examples.

First, defining the function
f(x_{1},x_{2})=\mathds{1}(x_{1}=x)\cdot\mathds{1}(x_{2}=y)
for fixed values
x,y\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathds{1}(\cdot)
denotes the indicator function, the Stein characterization of
\mathrm{BVB}(N;a_{1},a_{2},\phi_{a})
according to Theorem
2
implies the following recursive schemes for probability calculations:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\,}l}p_{00}\,x\,p(x,y)=&-p_{01}\,x\,p(x,y-1)\\
&+p_{10}\,(N-x+1)p(x-1,y)+p_{11}\,(N-x+1)\,p(x-1,y-1),\\[4.30554pt]
p_{00}\,y\,p(x,y)=&-p_{10}\,y\,p(x-1,y)\\
&+p_{01}\,(N-y+1)\,p(x,y-1)+p_{11}\,(N-y+1)\,p(x-1,y-1),\end{array}

(5.35)

which is analogous to the recursive scheme (
2.7
) for the bivariate Poisson distribution.
Similarly, Theorem
3
for
\mathrm{BNB}(\nu;\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{0})
implies that

\begin{array}[]{r@{\,}l}x\,p(x,y)=&\pi_{2}\,x\,p(x,y-1)\\
&+\pi_{1}\,(\nu+x-1)\,p(x-1,y)+\pi_{0}\,(\nu+x-1)\,p(x-1,y-1),\\[4.30554pt]
y\,p(x,y)=&\pi_{1}\,y\,p(x-1,y)\\
&+\pi_{2}\,(\nu+y-1)\,p(x,y-1)]+\pi_{0}\,(\nu+y-1)\,f(x-1,y-1)].\end{array}

(5.36)

Both schemes (
5.35
) and (
5.36
) are useful for probability calculations in practice, because the closed formulae for the respective pmfs of
\mathrm{BVB}(N;a_{1},a_{2},\phi_{a})
and
\mathrm{BNB}(\nu;\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{0})
as given in
kocherlakota14
are rather sophisticated and computationally demanding.

Next, let us consider joint factorial moments of
\mathrm{BVB}(N;a_{1},a_{2},\phi_{a})
and
\mathrm{BNB}(\nu;\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{0})
. Denote the falling factorials by
x_{(k)}=x\cdots(x-k+1)
with
x_{(0)}=x
and the corresponding bivariate factorial moments by
\mu_{(r,s)}:=\mathbb{E}[(X_{1})_{(r)}\,(X_{2})_{(s)}]
. Obviously, the marginal factorial moments arise by setting either
s=0
or
r=0
.
Now, inserting
f(x,y)=(x-1)_{(r-1)}\,y_{(s)}
into (
3.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle p_{00}\,\mathbb{E}[(X_{1})_{(r)}\,

\displaystyle\,(X_{2})_{(s)}]+p_{01}\,\mathbb{E}[(X_{1})_{(r)}\,(X_{2}+1)_{(s)}]

\displaystyle=p_{10}\,\mathbb{E}[(N-X_{1})\,(X_{1})_{(r-1)}\,(X_{2})_{(s)}]+p_{11}\,\mathbb{E}[(N-X_{1})\,(X_{1})_{(r-1)}\,(X_{2}+1)_{(s)}].

Here, we can split

(N-X_{1})\,(X_{1})_{(r-1)}=(N-r+1)\,(X_{1})_{(r-1)}-(X_{1})_{(r)}
and

(X_{2}+1)_{(s)}=(X_{2}-s+1\ +s)\,(X_{2})_{(s-1)}=(X_{2})_{(s)}+s\,(X_{2})_{(s-1)}
. Hence, the joint factorial moments
\mu_{(r,s)}
satisfy the recursive scheme

\displaystyle p_{00}\,\mu_{(r,s)}

\displaystyle+p_{01}\,\mu_{(r,s)}+p_{01}\,s\,\mu_{(r,s-1)}=p_{10}\,(N-r+1)\,\mu_{(r-1,s)}-p_{10}\,\mu_{(r,s)}

\displaystyle+p_{11}\,\big((N-r+1)\,\mu_{(r-1,s)}+(N-r+1)\,s\,\mu_{(r-1,s-1)}-\mu_{(r,s)}-s\,\mu_{(r,s-1)}\big).

This can be rewritten as

\mu_{(r,s)}=(N-r+1)\,a_{1}\,\mu_{(r-1,s)}-s\,a_{2}\,\mu_{(r,s-1)}+(N-r+1)\,s\,a\,\mu_{(r-1,s-1)}.

(5.37)

Analogously, we could use (
3.11
) for deriving a mirror-inverted recursive scheme for
\mathrm{BVB}(N;a_{1},a_{2},\phi_{a})
.

Next, inserting
f(x,y)=(x-1)_{(r-1)}\,y_{(s)}
into (
4.19
) (or analogously into (
4.20
)), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X_{1})_{(r)}\,

\displaystyle\,(X_{2})_{(s)}]-\pi_{2}\,\mathbb{E}[(X_{1})_{(r)}\,(X_{2}+1)_{(s)}]

\displaystyle=\pi_{1}\,\mathbb{E}[(\nu+X_{1})\,(X_{1})_{(r-1)}\,(X_{2})_{(s)}]+\pi_{0}\,\mathbb{E}[(\nu+X_{1})\,(X_{1})_{(r-1)}\,(X_{2}+1)_{(s)}].

Here, we can split

(\nu+X_{1})\,(X_{1})_{(r-1)}=(\nu+r-1)\,(X_{1})_{(r-1)}+(X_{1})_{(r)}
, and

(X_{2}+1)_{(s)}=(X_{2})_{(s)}+s\,(X_{2})_{(s-1)}
as before. Hence, the joint factorial moments
\mu_{(r,s)}
satisfy the recursive scheme

\displaystyle\mu_{(r,s)}

\displaystyle-\pi_{2}\,\mu_{(r,s)}-\pi_{2}\,s\,\mu_{(r,s-1)}=\pi_{1}\,(\nu+r-1)\,\mu_{(r-1,s)}+\pi_{1}\,\mu_{(r,s)}

\displaystyle+\pi_{0}\,\big((\nu+r-1)\,\mu_{(r-1,s)}+(\nu+r-1)\,s\,\mu_{(r-1,s-1)}+\mu_{(r,s)}+s\,\mu_{(r,s-1)}\big).

This can be rewritten as

\displaystyle(1-\pi_{\bullet})\,\mu_{(r,s)}=

\displaystyle(\nu+r-1)\,(\pi_{1}+\pi_{0})\,\mu_{(r-1,s)}

(5.38)

\displaystyle+s\,(\pi_{2}+\pi_{0})\,\mu_{(r,s-1)}+(\nu+r-1)\,s\,\pi_{0}\,\mu_{(r-1,s-1)}.

Both schemes (
5.37
) and (
5.38
) are very useful for practice, as the closed-form expressions for the factorial moments of
\mathrm{BVB}(N;a_{1},a_{2},\phi_{a})
and
\mathrm{BNB}(\nu;\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{0})
, as given in
kocherlakota14
, are quite cumbersome. Generally, it appears to be a promising task for future research to derive further moment formulae for
\mathrm{BVB}(N;a_{1},a_{2},\phi_{a})
and
\mathrm{BNB}(\nu;\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{0})
, e.g., expressions for inverse or partial moments, like it was done by
weiss2022
in case of the bivariate Poisson distribution.

5.2
Goodness-of-fit Testing

As already surveyed in Section
1
, univariate Stein identities have been widely used for constructing goodness-of-fit (GoF) tests, see
betsch22
;
weiss2023
;
aleksandrov22
;
aleksandrov24
for approaches and references. GoF-testing of bivariate distributions is more demanding due to the increased complexity of the probability models, but Stein characterizations again offer the potential for constructing appropriate test statistics. This shall be briefly illustrated in the sequel for the case of testing a bivariate-Poisson null hypothesis, while more comprehensive analyses as well as different null hypotheses are recommended for future research. Inspired by existing works on univariate Stein-GoF tests for Poissonity, we propose to derive a test statistic from (
5.33
), namely

T_{1;f}:=\frac{(\mu_{1}-\sqrt{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}\,\rho)\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]-(\mu_{2}-\sqrt{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}\,\rho)\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)]}{\mathbb{E}[(X_{1}-X_{2})\,f(X_{1},X_{2})]},

(5.39)

where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
in (
5.33
) were substituted by the moment expressions
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{i}-\sqrt{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}\,\rho
for
i=1,2
with
\mu_{i}=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]
and
\rho=\operatorname{Corr}(X_{1},X_{2})
. Here, we followed the recommendation of
best97
to express
\lambda_{0}
as
\sqrt{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}\,\rho
instead of
\operatorname{Cov}(X_{1},X_{2})
. The sample version
\widehat{T}_{1;f}
of statistic (
5.39
) follows by substituting all population moments by corresponding sample moments. If the null hypothesis
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},X_{2})^{\top}\sim\mathrm{BPoi}(\lambda_{0};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
is satisfied, then
T_{1;f}=1
for any choice of
f
, so deviations of
\widehat{T}_{1;f}
from 1 may indicate a violation of the BPoi-null.

Before looking at some simulation results, it is interesting to study the special case
f(x,y)=x-y
. Then, denoting
\sigma_{i}^{2}=\mathbb{V}[X_{i}]
for
i=1,2
,
T_{1;f}
becomes

\displaystyle T_{1;x-y}=

\displaystyle\frac{(\mu_{1}-\sqrt{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}\,\rho)\,(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}+1)-(\mu_{2}-\sqrt{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}\,\rho)\,(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}-1)}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\mu_{1}^{2}-2(\sqrt{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}\,\rho+\mu_{1}\mu_{2})+\sigma_{2}^{2}+\mu_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}-2\sqrt{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}\,\rho}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}+(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}-2\sqrt{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}\,\rho}.

This shows that the special case
f(x,y)=x-y
leads to a kind of bivariate dispersion index, similar to those in
crockett79
,
loukas86
,
best97
, and
weiss2019
, for example. Conversely, this implies that the “bivariate Stein index” (
5.39
) constitutes a flexible extension of existing bivariate dispersion indexes.

While more comprehensive analyses are planned for future research, we did an initial simulation study in order to investigate the potential of BPoi-GoF tests based on a bivariate Stein index according to (
5.39
). We considered two instances of the “weight function”
f
involved in (
5.39
), namely the alternating functions
f_{a}(x,y)=x^{a}-y^{a}
with
a\in\{1/2,1\}
. As competitors, we initially considered the “classical” dispersion indexes investigated by
best97
. However, in agreement to their analyses, we recognized severe size distortions for the tests related to the index of
loukas86
(denoted by
I_{B}
and
I_{B}^{*}
in
best97
). Thus, we finally decided to focus on the recommended dispersion-index test of
best97
, namely

T^{*}\ =\ \frac{\mu_{2}^{2}(\sigma_{1}^{2}-\mu_{1})^{2}+\mu_{1}^{2}(\sigma_{2}^{2}-\mu_{2})^{2}-2\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\,(\sigma_{1}^{2}-\mu_{1})\,(\sigma_{2}^{2}-\mu_{2})\,\rho^{2}}{2\mu_{1}^{2}\mu_{2}^{2}(1-\rho^{4})},

(5.40)

which constitutes a simplified type of Crockett’s index
crockett79
, and where we corrected a misprint in the denominator on p. 427 of
best97
. The dispersion-index test uses the sample counterpart
\widehat{T}^{*}
as the test statistic, computed from a bivariate count data set
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}
. Its asymptotic distribution satisfies
n\,\widehat{T}^{*}\sim\chi_{2}^{2}
under the null hypothesis of BPoi-counts.

In our simulation study, we considered all null and alternative scenarios like in
best97
with sample sizes
n\in\{50,100,200,500\}
, complemented by further alternatives from the BNB- and BVB-family. More precisely, the considered distributions are

\begin{array}[]{@{}l}\text{``BPoi-1''}=\mathrm{BPoi}(0.1;1.25,0.8),\quad\text{``BPoi-2''}=\mathrm{BPoi}(1;5,5),\\

\text{``BHerm-1''}=\mathrm{BHerm}(0.75,0.25,0.5,0.15,0.1),\\
\text{``BHerm-2''}=\mathrm{BHerm}(1,0.75,1.25,0.5,1),\\

5.3
Testing for Symmetry

In analogy to the general GoF-testing discussed in Section
5.2
, bivariate Stein characterizations may also be utilized for the more specific task of testing for the symmetry of the considered bivariate distribution, i.e., if
(X_{1},X_{2})
has the same distribution as
(X_{2},X_{1})
.

Let us start again with the case
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\mathrm{BPoi}(\lambda_{0};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
, then the additional requirement of symmetry is equivalent to requiring
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=:\lambda
. Consider the moment
\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)]
, which appears in both equations (
2.5
) and (
2.6
).
If
f
is an alternating function, i.e., if
f(x,y)=-f(y,x)
(like the examples considered in Section
5.2
), then

\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)]=-\mathbb{E}[f(X_{2}+1,X_{1}+1)]
. Under the
additional
symmetry of
(X_{1},X_{2})
, the last moment satisfies
\mathbb{E}[f(X_{2}+1,X_{1}+1)]=\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)]
, i.e., altogether, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)]=-\mathbb{E}[f(X_{2}+1,X_{1}+1)]=-\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)],

so
\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2}+1)]=0
follows. Hence, if
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\mathrm{BPoi}(\lambda_{0};\lambda,\lambda)
, then the original identities (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) reduce to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}\,f(X_{1},X_{2})]=

\displaystyle\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\,f(X_{1},X_{2})]=

\displaystyle\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)].

This implies to define the test statistic

T_{2;f}:=\Big|\mathbb{E}[X_{1}\,f(X_{1},X_{2})]-\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]\Big|+\Big|\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\,f(X_{1},X_{2})]-\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)]\Big|

(5.42)

with alternating function
f
.
\lambda
may be substituted by the moment expression
\mu(1-\rho)
with
\mu=\frac{1}{2}(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})
,
and the sample version
\widehat{T}_{2;f}
of statistic (
5.42
) follows again by substituting all population moments by corresponding sample moments.
If
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\mathrm{BPoi}(\lambda_{0};\lambda,\lambda)
, then
T_{2;f}=0
, so
\widehat{T}_{2;f}>0
indicates a possible violation from the
\mathrm{BPoi}(\lambda_{0};\lambda,\lambda)
-null.

Instead of this specific symmetry hypothesis (symmetry within the BPoi-distribution), one may also more generally consider the null of a symmetric bivariate distribution (this would also cover, e.g., a symmetric BVB- or BNB-distribution). Then, we can just look at the moments
\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]
and
\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)]
. If
f
is alternating, and if
(X_{1},X_{2})
is symmetric, then

\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]=-\mathbb{E}[f(X_{2},X_{1}+1)]=-\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)],

i.e., the test statistic

T_{3;f}:=\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1}+1,X_{2})]+\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1},X_{2}+1)]

(5.43)

with alternating function
f
becomes zero for
(X_{1},X_{2})
being symmetrically distributed. For the sample version
\widehat{T}_{3;f}
of statistic (
5.43
), we again substitute all population moments by corresponding sample moments, and deviations of
\widehat{T}_{3;f}
from 0 indicate a possible violation of the (general) symmetry-null. Note, however, that although the test statistic
T_{3;f}
itself is not restricted to a certain distribution family, the implementation of the test (either based on asymptotic derivations or a bootstrap approach) typically requires parametric assumptions.

Remark 7
.

The case of a symmetric bivariate count distribution is particularly relevant in time series analysis.
Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}=\{\ldots,-1,0,1,\ldots\}}
be a stationary count process with mean
\mu>0
and autocorrelation function
\rho(h)=\operatorname{Corr}(X_{t},X_{t-h})
with time lag
h\in\mathbb{N}
. There is a large family of so-called Poisson integer-valued autoregressive moving-average (INARMA) models, which satisfies that pairs
(X_{t},X_{t-h})
are distributed according to
\mathrm{BPoi}\big(\rho(h)\,\mu;\ (1-\rho(h))\,\mu,\ (1-\rho(h))\,\mu\big)
, see Section 3.1 in
aleksandrov24
. Thus, together with (
5.33
), it follows that the equality

\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[(X_{t}-X_{t-h})\,f(X_{t},X_{t-h})\big]}{\mathbb{E}\big[f(X_{t}+1,X_{t-h})-f(X_{t},X_{t-h}+1)\big]}=\big(1-\rho(h)\big)\,\mu

(5.44)

has to hold, irrespective of the choice of
f
, as long as the expectation in the denominator is non-zero. Note that the latter excludes the use of any symmetric function
f
, only such
f
satisfying
f(x,y)\not=f(y,x)
are allowed within (
5.44
). It should be noted that analogous model families with symmetric BVB- or BNB-distributions for the pairs
(X_{t},X_{t-h})
exist, see
aleksandrov22
and
aleksandrov24
for details.
Future research should investigate if relations such as (
5.44
) could be useful for model diagnostics in count time series analysis, in analogy to
weiss2019
.

, and the considered distributions are those of equation (

, the

2
show that again, all tests have empirical sizes (the rejection rates for scenarios “BPoi-2”, “BPoi-5”, and “BPoi-7”) being reasonably close to the nominal 5 %-level. Regarding the power concerning asymmetry
within
the BPoi-family (remaining BPoi-scenarios), we have increasing power for increasing sample size
n
, where the best power is usually achieved by one of the
T_{3;f}
-tests, closely followed by the
T_{2;f_{1/2}}
-test. So the less specific
T_{3;f}
-statistic appears to be the preferable solution here. Things change, however, if the alternative is
outside

T_{3;f}
-test is hardly powerful at all, which is plausible as its statistic solely focuses on symmetry. In fact, we conjecture that it might be possible in future research to develop a non-parametric implementation of the
T_{3;f}
-test, which allows to test a symmetry-null irrespective of the apparent distribution family. For the remaining alternatives, where we are concerned with both non-BPoi and asymmetric distributions, the
T_{3;f}
-tests show improved power but still, they are clearly outperformed by any of the
T_{2;f}
-tests. Again, this is plausible as the
T_{2;f}
-statistic aggregates both the distributional deviations and the asymmetry. Here, it is interesting to note that in most cases, both instances
T_{2;f_{1}}
and
T_{2;f_{1/2}}
perform virtually the same. But for the BVB-alternatives being characterized by underdispersion, the weight function
f_{1/2}
appears to be beneficial. Altogether, the proposed symmetry tests constitute a promising approach for uncovering asymmetry, where the weight function
f_{1/2}
either leads to similar or even better results than
f_{1}
. The latter is opposite to the GoF-tests in Section
5.2
, where
f_{1}
is the preferable choice. In both cases, an in-depth analyses on the optimal choice of
f
appears to be a challenging task for future research.

6
Conclusions

In this research, we derived novel Stein characterizations for the three most important types of bivariate count distribution, namely the bivariate Poisson, binomial, and negative-binomial distribution. We also demonstrated that these Stein characterizations offer a broad potential for applications in theory and practice. More precisely, we showed how the Stein identities can be utilized for moment calculations, where the novel recursive schemes for bivariate pmf and factorial moments of BNB- and BVB-distribution appear to be particularly useful from a computational point of view. We also deduced test statistics for the goodness-of-fit or the symmetry of bivariate count distributions, which showed an appealing performance in simulations and real-world data examples.

Given the limited length of a research article, it was of course not possible for us to elaborate on the above application areas in full detail. Instead, it became clear that our novel Stein characterizations offer the opportunity for various follow-up research projects. Among others, the Stein tests for goodness-of-fit or symmetry deserve a more comprehensive analysis, e.g., with respect to optimal weight functions
f
or a non-parametric implementation of the
T_{3;f}
-test. Future research might also try to utilize our bivariate Stein characterizations for the remaining application areas reviewed in Section
1
, such as model diagnostics for count time series analysis.
