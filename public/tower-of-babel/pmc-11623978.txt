Muscle weakness, myositis and scleroderma induced by durvalumab
Authors: Josu Aguado Suquia, Pilar Carballosa de Miguel, Alba Naya Prieto, Maria Jesus Lobo Rebollo, Teresa Blázquez Sánchez, Sarah Béatrice Heili Frades
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) have shown a change in the prognosis of multiple malignancies, but a wide range of immune‐related adverse events (irAE) can occur. We present a case of a 72‐year‐old Caucasian male treated with Durvalumab for epidermic lung cancer, who developed a sclerosis‐like syndrome and myositis. The patient was admitted to our hospital with respiratory failure due to muscular weakness and pulmonary hypertension. Differential diagnoses pointed to scleroderma secondary to ICI. We report a rare case of ICI‐induced axonal polyneuropathy, myopathy and RNA polymerase III positive scleroderma. As this therapy is widely recommended, adverse effects may become more frequent. Early identification and treatment are warranted.Keywords: axonal neuropathy, immune checkpoint inhibitor, immune‐related adverse event, myositis, scleroderma

INTRODUCTION

Preoperative immunotherapy plus neoadjuvant chemotherapy has demonstrated better results for non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) compared with neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone, with higher pathological response rates and potential increases in overall survival. However, this combination can activate the immune system more intensely, potentially leading to autoimmune reactions. As immune checkpoint inhibitors become more widely used, an increase in adverse events associated with the immune system should be considered.

CASE REPORT

A 72‐year‐old male with hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and smoking 30 pack‐years. A mass was first observed in the upper right lobe in May 2021. Samples, obtained through endobronchial ultrasound with transbronchial needle aspiration, showed metastatic epidermic cancer on 4R adenopathy, confirming T3N2M0 stage IIIB epidermic lung cancer. The patient was treated with neoadjuvant Durvalumab (1500 mg) plus chemotherapy (4 cycles of Carboplatin AUC 6 + Paclitaxel 200 mg/m2 every 3 weeks starting August 2021) followed by surgery and adjuvant Durvalumab (1500 mg every 4 weeks). The patient participated in the AEGEAN trial, as this therapy was not standard care at the time. Preoperative respiratory function tests were normal. Therefore, we performed video‐assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) right superior lobectomy in November 2021. Subsequently, the patient was treated with adjuvant therapy, starting January 2022 achieving radiologic and pathologic complete response on successive controls.

When presenting for the 8th cycle (July 2022) the patient reported general articular and muscular pain as well as loss of sensitivity and strength of hands and feet that limited his daily life activities. The treatment was suspended and grade II axonal polyneuropathy 1 was investigated. Electromyography and magnetic resonance showed posterior paraspinal muscular edema. One month after the last cycle, dysphagia was first observed. It's important to mention that the patient did not have any symptoms or findings on the clinical examination conducted before treatment, and a blood test with autoimmune markers was not performed prior to the diagnosis.

During the following months, leg edema and echocardiography revealed dilation of the right ventricle with preserved ejection fraction, managed with diuretics. In September 2023, the patient was admitted to our hospital with severe muscular weakness, ineffective cough, respiratory failure with hypercapnia and respiratory acidosis in the absence of increased work of breathing. The patient was treated with non‐invasive ventilation (NIV). A complementary echocardiography showed severe Pulmonary Hypertension with right ventricle dysfunction, flattened interventricular septum, with estimated pulmonary artery pressure of 80 mmHg and a peak systolic tricuspid valve gradient of 65 mmHg. This was subsequently confirmed by right ventricle catheterization and after Angio‐CT excluded pulmonary embolism and interstitial lung disease.

The patient presented cutaneous induration on legs and arms, weakness of extremities (with impossibility of ambulation) and of cervical and scapular muscles (with dropped head). The results of a forearm epidermis biopsy were compatible with scleroderma. Articular affection or Raynaud's disease symptoms were not observed. An MRI showed edema on different muscular groups and generalized myopathy. Autoimmune disease blood test was negative and muscle enzymes (CK) were not elevated. The patient declined muscular biopsy. Repeat autoimmune blood test showed positive RNA polymerase III, with negative results for the remainder panel, including biomarkers of myasthenia gravis (anti‐AChR antibody) and myositis. Repeat electromyography supported the chronic nature of the neurologic disorder.

The principal diagnosis was an overlap of inflammatory myopathy and scleroderma potentially induced by immunotherapy (Durvalumab). After discussion by a multidisciplinary team, the patient was treated with 3 daily administrations of methylprednisolone 500 mg and immunoglobulin therapy (IVIG) (2 days) followed by methylprednisolone with gradual dose reduction and Rituximab. In the next few days after treatment initiation, slight improvement of skin sclerosis and notable progressive improvement of muscular tone were observed, as the patient was able to walk short distances and hold his head up. The patient continued the respiratory rehabilitation program initiated during hospital admission. Pulmonary hypertension treatment was initiated, with Bosentan 62.5 mg every 12 h and Tadalafil 10 mg once a day with good response.

DISCUSSION

We present a rare case of immunotherapy‐induced axonal polyneuropathy, myopathy, and RNA polymerase III‐positive scleroderma. Most cases that reported immunotherapy‐related polyneuropathy and muscular disorders were related to myasthenia gravis‐like, 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 especially with Nivolumab and Ipilimumab. In contrast, the characteristics of the present case did not show that type of association, since it was excluded by an electromyography and negative anti‐AChR antibody on autoimmune blood test. Myositis was commonly found on the reports. 6 Similarly to our case, it usually affects proximal and distal muscular groups and is associated with dysphagia or dysarthria. 5 Skeletal muscle cells express PD‐L1, which prevents T‐cells autoimmune reaction. Hence, anti PD‐L1 antibody could lead to damage of the skeletal muscle caused by inflammatory response, 7 with a potential role for CD4+ and CD8+ lymphocytes as reported for a similar case involving Durvalumab on neoadjuvant scheme. 8 Unfortunately, the exact mechanism leading to the myopathy is unknown as muscular biopsy was not performed.

As the scleroderma was diagnosed after the start of ICI therapy, we consider it associated with the treatment itself (Checkpoint Inhibitor‐Associated Scleroderma or ICI‐SSc). As opposed to primary scleroderma, ICI‐SSc often shows negative antinuclear antibodies and specific antibodies and lower frequency of Raynaud's sign. 9 Also, an association between cancer and RNA polymerase III positive scleroderma was found in a retrospective study, 10 which supports the paraneoplastic cause and makes it difficult to differentiate from the ICI, as happened in the reported case.

We do not consider that the pulmonary hypertension of our patient is secondary to an ICI induced myocarditis, as observed in other cases, 2 , 3 , 11 because it was not supported by the magnetic resonance and the echocardiography.

To our knowledge, this is a very rare case of Durvalumab‐induced scleromyositis, a similar case was reported by a Spanish group 12 but with less severe muscular involvement and positiveness of anti‐PM‐Scl antibody. The severity of the symptoms described here warrants caution when administering this treatment, with an early identification of cutaneous sclerosis or muscular involvement. We also want to emphasize the importance of an early and aggressive treatment. Further reports will help us understand the mechanisms of ICI reactions, in order to improve outcomes and the quality of life for our patients.
