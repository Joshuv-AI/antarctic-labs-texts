Nicholas J. Christoffersen, Kyle Luh, Sean O’Rourke, Calum Shearer

Abstract
. We study the gaps between consecutive singular values of random rectangular matrices. Specifically, if M M is an n × p n\times p random matrix with independent and identically distributed entries and Σ \Sigma is a n × n n\times n deterministic positive definite matrix, then under some technical assumptions we give lower bounds for the gaps between consecutive singular values of Σ 1 / 2 ​ M \Sigma^{1/2}M . As a consequence, we show that sample covariance matrices have simple spectrum with high probability. Our results resolve a conjecture of Vu [ Probab. Surv. , 18:179–200, 2021]. We also discuss some applications, including a bound on the spacings of eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix of random bipartite graphs.



1.
Introduction

The gaps between eigenvalues have been objects of significant study in random matrix theory. A famous example is the Wigner surmise for the density of level spacings of normalized eigenvalues
[
33
,
50
]
. Gaps between eigenvalues for various models of random matrices have been studied for a variety of matrix models in the context of gap distributions, extreme gap statistics, and overcrowding (Wegner) estimates; we refer the reader to
[
33
,
42
,
13
,
41
,
12
,
8
,
7
,
37
,
6
,
32
,
11
,
43
,
1
,
10
,
14
,
9
]
and references therein.

A square matrix is said to have
simple spectrum
if its eigenvalues all have (algebraic) multiplicity one. It was conjectured by Vu in
[
49
]
that many matrix models have simple spectrum with high probability. This was established for a class of Wigner matrices by Tao and Vu in
[
44
]
. In particular, the results hold for the adjacency matrix of Erdős–Rényi random graphs, which addressed a conjecture of Babai and is related to the graph isomorphism problem
[
3
]
.
The gaps between complex eigenvalues of non-Hermitian random matrices with independent entries were studied in
[
15
]
; these results were improved upon in
[
29
]
.

Related to this is the study of the minimum gap between any two eigenvalues. In
[
35
]
, Nguyen, Tao and Vu showed that the minimum gap between eigenvalues of a random Wigner matrix with subgaussian entries is greater than
n^{-3/2-o(1)}
with high probability, where
o(1)
denotes a quantity tending to zero as
n
tends to infinity.
These results have also been extended to a class of sparse symmetric matrices by the second author and Vu in
[
30
]
. Recent progress has also been made by Han in
[
17
]
on bounding the probability the eigenvalues of Wigner matrices satisfy a fixed linear equation.

In this paper, we take a similar approach to
[
35
]
in studying the spacing of singular values of a random matrix
\Sigma^{1/2}M
, where
M
is an
n\times p
random rectangular matrix with independent entries and
\Sigma
is an
n\times n
deterministic positive definite matrix. The squares of the singular values are equal to the eigenvalues of the sample covariance matrix
M^{T}\Sigma M
, and the block matrix
\begin{pmatrix}0&\Sigma^{1/2}M\\
M^{T}\Sigma^{1/2}&0\end{pmatrix}
has eigenvalues equal to the singular values of
M
and their negatives. For this reason, one expects some of the tools developed for Wigner matrices to carry over to our setting. We demonstrate the analogous results to those in
[
35
]
for singular values of
\Sigma^{1/2}M
, under various assumptions on the matrix
\Sigma
and the entries of
M
. In particular, our results address a conjecture of Vu from
[
49
]
for sample covariance matrices.
Our proof builds on the arguments from
[
35
]
and utilizes a number of techniques from non-asymptotic random matrix theory
[
38
]
, while also overcoming a number of technical obstacles that do not arise in
[
35
]
.

1.2.
The model and assumptions

We will consider the random matrix
\Sigma^{1/2}M
, where
M
is an
n\times p
random rectangular matrix with independent entries and
\Sigma
is an
n\times n
deterministic positive definite matrix. We make the following assumptions concerning
M
and
\Sigma
.

Assumption 1.1
.

M
is an
n\times p
random matrix, where
p=\lambda n
and the aspect ratio
\lambda
satisfies

(1.1)

c_{\ref{matrix_model}}\leq\lambda\leq 1

for some constant
c_{\ref{matrix_model}}>0
. In particular, this implies that
p\leq n
. We also make the assumption that the entries of
M
are independent and identically distributed copies of the real-valued random variable
\xi
, where
\xi
has mean zero, unit variance, and satisfies

(1.2)

\mathbb{E}[\xi^{4}]=m_{4}

for some absolute constant
m_{4}<\infty
. The random variable
\xi
is called the
atom variable
of
M
.
Further,
\xi_{ij}
will be used to denote the
(i,j)
-th entry of
M
. We define for
k=1,2,3,4

m_{k}=\mathbb{E}[\absolutevalue{\xi}^{k}],

and note that by Jensen’s inequality,
m_{k}\leq m_{4}^{4/k}<\infty
for
k=1,2,3
.

Assumption 1.2
.

\Sigma
is an
n\times n
deterministic positive definite matrix which satisfies

(1.3)

\norm{\Sigma},\norm{\Sigma^{-1}}\leq L^{2}

for some absolute constant
L\geq 1
. It follows that
\Sigma^{1/2},\Sigma^{-1/2}
are well-defined and have norms bounded by
L
.

Regarding the bounds on
\lambda
in the above assumptions, we note that the non-trivial singular values of
M
and
M^{\mathrm{T}}
are the same, so the assumption that
c_{\ref{matrix_model}}\leq\lambda\leq 1
can be replaced with the bound
c_{\ref{matrix_model}}\leq\lambda\leq c_{\ref{matrix_model}}^{-1}
without further explanation in the case where
\Sigma=I
. For simplicity, we restrict to the case when
\lambda
is bounded above by one.

In the case where
\Sigma\neq I
, simplicity of the eigenvalues of
\Sigma^{1/2}M
does still follow from the simplicity of the eigenvalues of
M^{T}\Sigma^{1/2}
, however, in what follows we consider the eigenvalues of
M^{T}\Sigma M
, not
\Sigma^{1/2}M^{T}M\Sigma^{1/2}
. Regardless, the result follows analogously, with the minor modifications to proofs described below. Through the course of the paper, for a right singular vector
u
and left-singular vector
v
of
\Sigma^{1/2}M
, we often work with
u
and
w=\Sigma^{1/2}v/\left\|\Sigma^{1/2}v\right\|
rather than
v
directly, as it allows us to leverage the independence of entries of
M
and
M^{T}
. In the case where one considers
M\Sigma^{1/2}
in place of
\Sigma^{1/2}M
, one simply must interchange the roles of
u
and
v
. That is, for a singular vector pair
(u,v)
, one will do similar manipulations in the proofs that follow, but replacing
u
with
\Sigma^{1/2}u/\left\|\Sigma^{1/2}u\right\|
and replacing
w
with
v
. In doing so, one can replace the assumption
c_{\ref{matrix_model}}\leq\lambda\leq 1
with
c_{\ref{matrix_model}}\leq\lambda\leq c_{\ref{matrix_model}}
, just as in the
\Sigma=I
case discussed above.

We define the event
\mathcal{E}_{K}
to be

(1.4)

\mathcal{E}_{K}:=\left\{\norm{M}\leq K\sqrt{n}\right\}\ \wedge\ \left\{\sigma_{p}(M)\neq 0\right\},

for
K\geq 1
a constant.

We will state some results in the case when the atom variable
\xi
is subgaussian. Recall that a random variable
\xi
is called
subgaussian
if there exists a constant
Q>0
(called the
subgaussian moment
of
\xi
) such that

\mathbb{P}\left\{\absolutevalue{\xi}>t\right\}\leq\frac{1}{Q}e^{-Qt^{2}}

for all
t>0
.

1.3.
Main results

We now state our main result.

Theorem 1.3

(Main result)
.

Let
M
and
\Sigma
be as in Assumptions
1.1
and
1.2
, and assume
K\geq 1
. Then there exist constants
c,C_{\ref{main1}}>0
(depending on
c_{\ref{matrix_model}},K,L
and
m_{4}
) such that, for any
n^{-c}\leq\alpha\leq c
and
\delta\geq n^{-c/\alpha}
,

(1.5)

\sup_{1\leq i\leq p-1}\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma_{i}^{2}(\Sigma^{1/2}M)-\sigma_{i+1}^{2}(\Sigma^{1/2}M)\leq\sigma_{i+1}(\Sigma^{1/2}M)\delta n^{-1/2}\wedge\mathcal{E}_{K}\right)\leq C_{\ref{main1}}\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{\alpha}}

where
\mathcal{E}_{K}
is defined in (
1.4
).

A few comments concerning Theorem
1.3
are in order. First, Theorem
1.3
is the analogue of Theorem 2.1 from
[
35
]
. Unlike the results from
[
35
]
, we see that the

-th gap between the squared singular values depends on the location of the
(i+1)
-st singular value. Figures

1.4.
Proof Strategy

In order to effectively demonstrate the main ideas of our proof, we describe the proof strategy for the case when
\Sigma
is the identity matrix. We present the proof of the general case in the bulk of the paper.

Our overall strategy is similar to that used in
[
35
]
. We begin by decomposing the matrix
M
as

M=\begin{pmatrix}M^{\prime}\mid X\end{pmatrix},

where
X
is the final column of
M
. Note that by our assumptions, the entries of
X
are independent from
M^{\prime}
. We now manipulate the eigenvalue-eigenvector equation
M^{T}Mu=\sigma_{i}^{2}(M)u
in order to relate the gap size
\sigma_{i}^{2}(M)-\sigma_{i+1}^{2}(M)
to singular vectors of the minor
M^{\prime}
and to the random vector
X
. Indeed, if one decomposes
u
as

u=\begin{pmatrix}u^{\prime}\\
b\end{pmatrix}

where
u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}

then the eigenvalue-eigenvector equation becomes

M^{T}Mu=\begin{pmatrix}M^{\prime T}M^{\prime}&M^{\prime T}X\\
X^{T}M^{\prime}&X^{T}X\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u^{\prime}\\
b\end{pmatrix}=\sigma_{i}^{2}(M)\begin{pmatrix}u^{\prime}\\
b\end{pmatrix},

the first
(n-1)
rows of which read as

M^{\prime T}M^{\prime}u^{\prime}+M^{\prime T}Xb=\sigma_{i}^{2}(M)u^{\prime}.

By multiplying this equation on the left by
\hat{v}^{T}
, where
\hat{v}
is a right singular vector of
M^{\prime}
corresponding to the
i
-th largest singular value, and then rearranging terms, we find that if
v
is a left singular vector of
M^{\prime}
, the following holds:

\absolutevalue{v^T X}\leq\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}(M)-\sigma_{i}^{2}(M^{\prime})}{\sigma_{i}(M^{\prime})|b|}\leq\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}(M)-\sigma_{i+1}^{2}(M)}{\sigma_{i}(M^{\prime})|b|},

where the Cauchy interlacing law has been applied. Therefore, the problem of bounding the gaps between squared singular values from below can be reduced to bounding the probability that
\absolutevalue{v^T X}
is small (along with showing that, on average, a coordinate of a singular vector
u
is sufficiently large). This probability is known as a
small ball probability
. Importantly, our argument ensures that the random vector
X
is independent of the entries of the singular vector
v
. One is then interested in the question
‘for which vectors
v
is the small ball probability low?’
, and then showing that, typically, all singular vectors are in such a class of vectors. In the context of small ball probabilities, such a vector
v
is known as a
coefficient vector
.

One class of vectors for which the small ball probability is potentially quite high is that of
compressible vectors
, defined as those vectors within a small Euclidean distance of the
sparse vectors
, which are the vectors supported on only a few of their coordinates (see Section
3
for the relevant definitions). This is due to the fact that when calculating
v^{T}X
for such vectors, only relatively few coordinates of
v
are significant, so that one is not getting significant contributions from all
n
sources of randomness in the vector
X
. In Section
3
we show that the probability that there exist compressible singular vectors is exponentially small.

Vectors which are not compressible are known as
incompressible vectors
. In general, these have better anti-concentration bounds than compressible vectors. However, for vectors where the coordinates have an additive structure (for example the all-ones vector), the corresponding anti-concentration bounds are too weak for our purposes. Therefore, in Section
4
we show that generically the coordinates of a singular vector do not have additive structure. The study of the relation between small ball probabilities and the additive structure of the corresponding coefficient vector is know as inverse Littlewood-Offord theory. Our treatment of small ball probabilities via compressible and incompressible vectors is similar to that presented in
[
39
]
. A concept known as the
least common denominator
(LCD) detects such additive structure, with low LCD vectors having high additive structure. We use a refinement of LCD, know as
regularized LCD
(originally introduced by Vershynin in
[
47
]
), which is better suited to the sorts of decomposition arguments we use, due to detecting additive structure present in relatively small chunks of a given vector. While incompressible vectors with low LCD have better anti-concentration bounds than compressible vectors, the trade-off is that there are more of them then there are compressible vectors. Therefore, we use a delicate
\varepsilon
-net argument reminiscent of those in
[
35
,
47
]
to show that the probability that any singular vectors lie in a level set of small regularized LCD is low.

The main technical hurdle is proving anti-concentration bounds for singular vectors with a given regularized LCD. One could attempt to show that the probability
\mathbb{P}\left\{\norm{M^TMu - a}\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\right\}
is small for any
a\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
in order to demonstrate that for fixed
u
and
\sigma^{2}
, it is unlikely that
M^{T}Mu=\sigma^{2}u
. However it appears difficult to prove anti-concentration bounds for the sample covariance matrix
M^{T}M
, due to the nature of this matrix product being such that it does not have independent entries or even independent rows or columns, which is an assumption typically used in arguments bounding small ball probabilities (in order to apply, for example, Lemma
2.2
). Instead, we establish in Lemma
4.4
a bound for
Mx
and
M^{T}y

simultaneously
anti-concentrating around a pair of points, for
x
and
y
with sufficiently large regularized LCD. With this established, we can apply anti-concentration bounds for vectors with large regularized LCD in order to control the gap size
\sigma_{i}^{2}(M)-\sigma_{i+1}^{2}(M)
.

The inclusion of the covariance matrix
\Sigma
does not change any of the main mathematical ideas of our proofs, but leads to slightly more involved arguments. Our covariance matrix
\Sigma
is assumed to have bounded operator norm, independent of
n
and
p
in order to successfully use our
\varepsilon
-net arguments.

1.5.
Applications

The gap between eigenvalues of random matrices is important to applications, in particular to numerical linear algebra (e.g.,
[
35
,
28
]
). In practice, when one works with rectangular and non-symmetric matrices, one is concerned with
singular value gaps
, rather than eigenvalue gaps
[
23
]
. Even in the case of symmetric matrices, singular value gaps can play an important role. For instance, the power method (also known as power iteration) is an algorithm to find the largest eigenvalue (by absolute value) and its corresponding eigenvector. The convergence rate of the error given by the power method is exponential, with base given by the ratio
\sigma_{2}/\sigma_{1}=1-\frac{\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}
. Power iteration and its related ideas (e.g., Krylov subspaces, Lanczos Algorithm) are still an area of active research
[
51
]
. In particular, variants of the Lanczos Algorithm (whose convergence also depends on gaps of subsequent singular values
\sigma_{k+1}-\sigma_{k}
) are found in standard numerical linear algebra programs in MATLAB, Mathematica, Scipy (python), and others through their dependency on Fortran’s ARPACK library
[
24
]
. In particular, these methods and other variants are found in various implementations of principal component analysis (PCA) and singular value decomposition (SVD) algorithms
[
34
,
52
]
, as well as neural network architectures and computations
[
26
,
36
]
. Power iteration also appears in recommendation engines from Twitter (X) and Google Search
[
16
,
20
]
. Even modern, machine learning-based recommendation engines use SVD algorithms that assume simplicity of singular values
[
36
]
.

As a particular example, we examine the graph isomorphism problem (GI), which asks if two given graphs
G,G^{\prime}
are isomorphic. Famously, it is not known if GI is solvable in polynomial time, nor if it is NP-complete
[
2
]
. However, there are several polynomial-time algorithms to determine if
G
and
G^{\prime}
are isomorphic assuming that the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrices are simple or of bounded multiplicity
[
25
,
3
,
21
]
. To convey the intuition for the algorithm in
[
25
]
, suppose that the entries of the eigenvectors of
A
and
B
corresponding to
\lambda_{1}
have distinct magnitudes; then this specifies the only possible mapping between the nodes of
G
and
G^{\prime}
. The actual algorithm needs to proceed more carefully, accounting for entries with identical magnitudes in the eigenvectors and iteratively partitioning the nodes into possible isomorphism classes.

The tractability of GI for graphs with simple spectrum suggests a natural question, posed by Babai
[
3
]
, of whether most
n
vertex graphs fall into this class. This was answered in the affirmative in
[
44
,
35
]
. Quantitatively, in
[
35
]
, the authors prove that of the
2^{\binom{n}{2}}
simple graphs on
n
vertices, all but a
\delta_{n}
proportion of the graphs have simple spectrum, where
\delta_{n}
is smaller than
n^{-C}
for any constant
C
. However, it is often of interest to restrict GI to subsets of all possible graphs with certain combinatorial properties.
For example, GI is known to be solvable in polynomial time in the case of planar graphs
[
19
]
, graphs with bounded degree
[
31
]
and others
[
2
]
. GI, and more generally the subgraph isomorphism problem, restricted to bipartite graphs is of recent interest
[
46
,
22
]
. Since the number of bipartite graphs that span two sets of size
n
is exactly
2^{n^{2}}
, which is an exponentially small fraction of all possible graphs on these vertices, the result in
[
35
]
does not provide any information on the proportion of bipartite graphs with simple spectrum.

Note that the adjacency matrix of a bipartite graph is of the form
\begin{pmatrix}0&M\\
M^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}
, so that the non-zero eigenvalues of this matrix are precisely
\pm\sigma_{i}(M)
(the non-zero singular values of
M
and their negations). Hence, if one is able to conclude that the singular values of
M
and
M^{\prime}
are distinct, then
GI
is solvable for the associated pair of graphs in polynomial time.

Theorem 1.8
.

Of the
2^{n^{2}}
bipartite graphs on
n+n
vertices, the proportion of graphs whose adjacency matrices have non-simple spectrum is at most
(C\exp(-n^{c} ))
for constants
C,c>0
.

This theorem is not an immediate application from the main result, as the adjacency matrix of such a graph is of the form
\begin{pmatrix}0&M\\
M^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}
, where
M
has non-zero mean. In particular, the spectral norm of
M
is of order
n
, rather than order
\sqrt{n}
, as discussed in Remark
3.4
. However, we generalize our main result to include this class of random matrices in Remark
5.8
.

As a consequence, this shows that GI is solvable in polynomial time on all but a
Ce^{-cn^{c}}
proportion of bipartite graphs on
n+n
vertices.

Corollary 1.9
.

There exist constants
C,c>0
, such that there exists a polynomial-time algorithm for bipartite graphs on
n+n
vertices after excluding at most

(C\exp(-cn^{c} ))2^{n^{2}}

graphs. Phrased probabilistically, there exists a polynomial-time algorithm that resolves GI on two uniformly sampled bipartite graphs on
n+n
vertices that succeeds with probability at least
1-(C\exp(-cn^{c} ))
.

Proof.

Theorem
1.8
allows us to conclude that non-zero eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix are simple with the requisite probability. The well-established results on the singularity of square Bernoulli matrices assure us that the adjacency matrix is non-singular (see, for example,
[
45
]
). Thus, any of the algorithms from
[
25
,
3
,
21
]
apply.
∎

We remark that it is possible to use our methods to prove that the non-zero eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix for
unbalanced
bipartite graphs are simple as well. However, in this case, the null space of the adjacency matrix is deterministically large. The algorithm in
[
25
]
can be modified by restricting attention to the eigenvectors corresponding to non-zero eigenvalues, but we do not pursue this algorithmic direction here.

1.6.
Outline

The rest of the paper is devoted to the proofs of our main results. We present some anti-concentration bounds in Section
2
. Technical bounds concerning compressible and incompressible singular vectors are presented in Sections
3
and
4
, respectively. Finally, these tools are combined in Section
5
to complete the proofs.

2.
Anti-concentration tools

We begin by stating some lemmas regarding the anti-concentration of random vectors, which will be used during our proofs.

The Lévy concentration function is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(X,\varepsilon):=\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mathbb{P}\left\{\norm{X-a}\leq\varepsilon\right\},

where
X
is a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
\mathcal{L}(X,\varepsilon)
bounds the
small-ball probabilities
of
X
, i.e., the probabilities that
X
falls in a ball of radius
\varepsilon
.

We begin by showing that our assumptions on the boundedness of the
4
th moment of
\xi
implies that
\xi
obeys the following (relatively weak) anti-concentration bound (which, while being a standard result, we include a proof of for completeness).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\xi
be a random variable with mean
0
and variance
1
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\xi^{4}]=m_{4}

for some constant
m_{4}<\infty
. Then for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists
p=p(\varepsilon,m_{4})<1
depending only on
\varepsilon
and
m_{4}
such that

(2.1)

\mathcal{L}(\xi,\varepsilon)\leq p.

Proof.

We note that

\displaystyle\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\left\{|\xi-a|>\varepsilon\right\}\leq\max\left\{\sup_{a\in[-3,3]}\mathbb{P}\left\{|\xi-a|>\varepsilon\right\},\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-3,3]}\mathbb{P}\left\{|\xi-a|>\varepsilon)\right\}\right\}.

For
\absolutevalue{a}>3
, the probability can be bounded as follows: for
\varepsilon<1
, we have by Chebyshev’s inequality and the variance
1
assumption that

\displaystyle\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-3,3]}\mathbb{P}\left\{|\xi-a|>\varepsilon\right\}\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{|\xi|\geq 2\right\}\leq\frac{1}{4}.

On the other hand, for
a\in[-3,3]
, we apply the Paley-Zygmund inequality, noting that the random variable
\absolutevalue{\xi-a}^{2}=(\xi-a)^{2}
has expectation

\mathbb{E}[(\xi-a)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}]-2a\mathbb{E}[\xi]+a^{2}=1+a^{2},

so that for any
\varepsilon<1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{|\xi-a|>\varepsilon\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\left(\mathbb{E}(\xi-a)^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[(\xi-a)^{4}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+a^{2}-\varepsilon^{2})^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[(\xi-a)^{4}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+a^{2}-\varepsilon^{2})^{2}}{m_{4}-4a\mathbb{E}\xi^{3}+6a^{2}+a^{4}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1-\varepsilon^{2})^{2}}{m_{4}+12m_{3}+54+81}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1-\varepsilon^{2})^{2}}{148m_{4}}

\displaystyle=1-p(\varepsilon,m_{4}).

In the penultimate line, we used that
m_{4}\geq 1
and
m_{4}\geq m_{3}
by Hölder’s inequality.
∎

Now, let us state the Tensorization Lemma (
[
47
, Lemma 3.4]
), which we will use throughout various proofs. This lemma allows one to use bounds on small ball probabilities of each individual coordinate of a random vector in order to bound on the small ball probability of the random vector in its totality.

Lemma 2.2

(Tensorization Lemma)
.

Let
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with independent coordinates
X_{i}
. Suppose there exist
\varepsilon>0
and
0<p<1
independent of
i
such that

\mathcal{L}(X_{i},\varepsilon)\leq p

for all
i
. Then there exist
\varepsilon_{1}>0
and
0<p_{1}<1
depending only on
\varepsilon
and
p
such that

\mathcal{L}(X,\varepsilon_{1}\sqrt{m})\leq p_{1}^{m}.

We shall typically use the above lemma in the situation that
X
is a product of the form
Mu
, where
M
is a random matrix, and
u
is a fixed unit vector. Also note that we will often write
e^{-cm}
for some
c>0
in place of
p_{1}^{m}
when applying this lemma.

Note that an entry of
Mu
looks like
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{ij}u_{j}
. Therefore, we also often use the following lemma
[
47
, Lemma 3.3]
on the anti-concentration of sums.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\xi_{1},\dots\xi_{n}
be independent random variables with unit variances and centered
4
th moments uniformly bounded by
m_{4}<\infty
. Then for every
0<\varepsilon<1
, there exists
0<p<1
depending only on
\varepsilon
and
m_{4}
such that for every
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in S^{m-1}
, the sum
S=\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}\xi_{i}
satisfies

\mathcal{L}(S,\varepsilon)\leq p.

3.
Compressible singular vectors

We begin by proving a lemma (Lemma
3.3
) which will enable us to prove that with probability
1-C_{\ref{nocompressible}}e^{-c_{\ref{nocompressible}}n}
, all singular vectors of our matrix
model are incompressible, where we introduce the relevant definitions and constants below.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
c_{0}>0
. A vector
v=(v_{1},\dots v_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is called
c_{0}
-sparse
if it is supported on at most
\lfloor c_{0}m\rfloor
coordinates (where
i\in\left\{1,2,...,m\right\}

Definition 3.2
.

Let

. A unit vector

is known as
(c_{0},c_{1})
-
compressible
(or just

if one is suppressing parameters) if it is within a Euclidean distance
c_{1}
of the set of
c_{0}
-sparse vectors. Such a set is denoted by
\textup{Comp}_{m}(c_{0},c_{1})
. In other words,

\textup{Comp}_{m}(c_{0},c_{1})=\left\{v\in S^{m-1}\colon\exists c_{0}\text{-sparse }x\text{ such that }\norm{v-x}\leq c_{1}\right\}.

A unit vector which is not compressible is called
incompressible
. The set of incompressible vectors is denoted as
\textup{Incomp}_{m}(c_{0},c_{1}):=S^{m-1}\setminus\textup{Comp}_{m}(c_{0},c_{1})
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

and

1.1
and
1.2

and

depending on

(3.1)

\mathbb{P}\left\{\inf_{u\in\textup{Comp}_{p}(c_{0},c_{1})}\norm{M^T \Sigma M u - \sigma^2 u}\leq c_{\ref{compcomp}}n\wedge\mathcal{E}_{K}\right\}\leq C_{\ref{compcomp}}e^{-\alpha_{\ref{compcomp}}n}

and

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\inf_{\Sigma^{1/2}v^{\prime}/\norm{\Sigma^{1 / 2}v'}\in\textup{Comp}_{n}(c_{0},c_{1})}\norm{\Sigma^{1 / 2}M M^T \Sigma^{1 / 2} v' - \sigma^2 v'}\leq c_{\ref{compcomp}}n\wedge\mathcal{E}_{K}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\ref{compcomp}}e^{-\alpha_{\ref{compcomp}}n}.

Proof.

Note that we will introduce

By

4.
Incompressible singular vectors and regularized LCD

Having shown that there is only a small probability that any singular vector is compressible, in this section we turn our attention to the incompressible vectors. Our ultimate goal is to show that singular vectors are sufficiently disordered, so that when their dot product is taken with a column of our random matrix
M
the resulting quantity has strong anti-concentration properties. We then use this to give a lower bound on the gap size between squared singular values.

Compressible vectors give relatively weak anti-concentration bounds. However, this may also be the case for an incompressible vector such that its coordinates are close to having additive structure. For example, the vector
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(1,\dots,1)
has relatively weak anti-concentration, due to the potential of many cancellations occurring if we, for example, were to take its dot product with a Rademacher random vector. Therefore, we wish to show that the case that a singular vector has such structure only rarely occurs. The key tool for detecting the extent to which a particular vector has strong anti-concentration bounds is the
least common denominator
, or
LCD
. In this case, we use the so-called
regularized LCD
, which is more robust under the types of decomposition arguments used during our proofs. At the end of this section, we use a somewhat technical
\beta
-net argument to demonstrate that in general, singular vectors have sufficiently large regularized LCD with high probability.

4.1.
Small ball probabilities via LCD

We use the definition of
least common denominator
(LCD) as in
[
35
]
, a concept originally introduced by Rudelson and Vershynin (see
[
39
]
,
[
40
]
).
We begin by including the relevant concepts and notation from
[
35
]
needed for our argument. Given parameters
\kappa,\gamma
with
\gamma\in(0,1)
, for a unit vector
x\in S^{m-1}
:

\operatorname{LCD}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x):=\inf\left\{\theta>0\colon\text{dist}(\theta x,\mathbb{Z}^{m})<\min\left(\gamma\norm{\theta x},\kappa\right)\right\},

where
\kappa
and
\gamma
can potentially depend on
m
. Note that for the purpose of proving our main results we will only need the case where
\gamma
is a constant , which we choose to be
1/2
. However, we still include
\gamma
in our intermediate results as it does not increase the complexity of our proofs. We primarily utilize the related notion of
regularized LCD
(originally introduced by Vershynin in
[
47
]
), which is defined in
[
35
]
as

\widehat{\operatorname{LCD}}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x,\alpha)=\max\left\{\operatorname{LCD}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x_{I}/\norm{x_{I}})\colon I\subset\text{spread}(x),\absolutevalue{I}=\lceil\alpha n\rceil\right\},

where
x\in\textup{Incomp}_{m}(c_{0},c_{1})
, and
\text{spread}(x)
is a subset of the indices of
\{1,\ldots,m\}
such that

\frac{c_{1}}{\sqrt{2m}}\leq\absolutevalue{x_k}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{0}m}}

for every
k\in\text{spread}(x)
, and
\absolutevalue{\text{spread}(x)}=\lceil c^{\prime}m\rceil
where
c^{\prime}=c_{0}c_{1}^{2}/4
. Note that such a subset
\text{spread}(x)
of the specified cardinality exists by the definition of incompressibility (for a proof, see
[
39
]
Lemma 3.4).
The parameter
\alpha
satisfies
0<\alpha<c^{\prime}/4
, where
\alpha
may be chosen to depend on
m
.

The importance of regularized LCD is its relation to small ball probabilities:

Lemma 4.1

(
[
35
]
Lemma 5.8)
.

Assume that
\xi_{1},\dots\xi_{m}
are i.i.d. (real-valued) random variables and let
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{m})
. Assume also that there exist
\varepsilon_{0},p_{0}>0
such that
\mathcal{L}(\xi_{i},\varepsilon_{0})\leq 1-p_{0}
for all
i
. If
x\in\textup{Incomp}_{m}(c_{0},c_{1})
, then for
\kappa>0
and any
\varepsilon
which satisfies

\varepsilon\geq\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{c_{0}\widehat{\operatorname{LCD}}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x,\alpha)}

there exists a constant
C_{\ref{incompanticonc}}
such that

\mathcal{L}(\xi\cdot x,\varepsilon)\leq C_{\ref{incompanticonc}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\gamma c_{1}\sqrt{\alpha}}+e^{-\Theta(\kappa^{2})}\right),

where
C_{\ref{incompanticonc}}
depends only on
\varepsilon_{0}
and
p_{0}
(and therefore
m_{4}
).

Remark 4.2
.

While
[
35
, Lemma 5.8]
requires that the

be subgaussian, by replacing
[

, Lemma 5.5]
in its proof with
[

\mathcal{L}(\xi_{i},\varepsilon_{0})\leq 1-p_{0}
.

Note that Lemma
4.1
continues to hold in the case where
\xi
has non-zero mean, since the Lévy concentration function is shift-invariant in the first argument. Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}((\xi+\mu)\cdot x,\varepsilon)=\mathcal{L}(\xi\cdot x+\mu\cdot x,\varepsilon)=\mathcal{L}(\xi\cdot x,\varepsilon)

as
\mu\cdot x
is a deterministic number so does not affect the supremum in the definition of

. As stated in Lemma
2.1
,
\varepsilon_{0},p_{0}
exist by the assumption that
\operatorname{Var}\xi=1,\mathbb{E}\xi^{4}\leq m_{4}
. Furthermore, they depend only on
m_{4}
.

Remark 4.3
.

In fact, the proof of the above lemma shows that the result holds for

replaced by
x_{J}
, where
J
is any subset of coordinates that contains
I
, the set of coordinates where regularized
\operatorname{LCD}
is achieved.

We use the above lemma to give a bound on the simultaneous anti-concentration of
\Sigma^{1/2}Mu
and
M^{T}\Sigma^{1/2}v
. In the case of incompressible singular vectors, it is more convenient to work with the intersection of events

\left\{\Sigma^{1/2}Mu=\sigma v\right\}\wedge\left\{M^{T}\Sigma^{1/2}v=\sigma u\right\}

(i.e. the singular vector equations for
\Sigma^{1/2}M
) instead of the individual events

\left\{M^{T}\Sigma Mu=\sigma^{2}u\right\}

or

\left\{\Sigma^{1/2}MM^{T}\Sigma^{1/2}v=\sigma^{2}v\right\},

(i.e. the eigenvalue equations for
M^{T}\Sigma M
and for
\Sigma^{1/2}MM^{T}\Sigma^{1/2}
), as the required anti-concentration results are difficult to apply to matrices of the form

due to the lack of independent rows and columns.

The next proposition is stated without reference to
\Sigma
. It bounds the probability that any fixed pair
(x,y)
of incompressible vectors are
approximate
singular vectors, with the level of approximation dependent on the regularized LCD of
x
and
y
. After proving this proposition, we employ a net argument in the next section, at which point we manipulate the inequalities appearing in the event in
(

)
into a form that accounts for the covariance matrix

Lemma 4.4
.

Let the matrix
M
satisfy Assumption
1.1
.
Then there exists a constant
C_{\ref{anti-concentration argument}}
(depending only on

(4.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sup_{(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}}&\mathbb{P}(\left\{\norm{M x - a}\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\right\}\wedge\left\{\norm{M^Ty - b}\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}\sqrt{p}\right\})\\

Proof.

First, let
I(x)\subset\left\{1,\dots,p\right\}
be the coordinates of

is achieved, and

Fix

,

where

\displaystyle x=\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
x^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix},b=\begin{pmatrix}b^{\prime}\\
b^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{ and }\quad y=\begin{pmatrix}y^{\prime}\\
y^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix},b=\begin{pmatrix}a^{\prime}\\

where

Writing things out in terms of our decomposition, we see that

\displaystyle Mx-a

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
x^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}a^{\prime}\\
a^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle M^{T}y-b

B^{T}&D^{T}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y^{\prime}\\

It therefore follows that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\norm{Mx-a}^{2}&=\norm{Ax' + Bx'' - a'}^{2}+\norm{Cx'+Dx''-a''}^{2}\\

Now let

by independence of

and

\displaystyle(Bx^{\prime\prime})_{i}

4.2.
Epsilon nets for regularized LCD level sets and the union bound argument

We first recall a lemma (
[
35
, Lemma 11.1]
) that bounds the size of regularized LCD level sets.

Lemma 4.5
.

For
m^{-c}\leq\alpha\leq c^{\prime}/4
and any
D\geq 1
, the level set

S_{D}=\left\{x\in\textup{Incomp}_{m}(c_{0},c_{1})\colon\widehat{\operatorname{LCD}}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x,\alpha)\leq D\right\}

has a
\beta
-net
\mathcal{N}
with
\beta=\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{\alpha}D}
such that

(4.9)

\absolutevalue{\mathcal{N}}\leq\frac{(CD)^{m}}{\sqrt{\alpha m}^{c^{\prime}m/2}}D^{2/\alpha}

for
C
a constant depending on
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
.

We shall use the above result, combined with our anti-concentration results for singular vectors, to prove that, with high probability, there are no singular vectors in
S_{D}
. However, singular vectors with smaller LCD exhibit weaker anti-concentration. Fortunately such vectors admit a net of smaller cardinality, which motivates us to define the following:

Definition 4.6
.

We define the set

S_{D,k,\mathbb{R}^{m}}=\left\{x\in\textup{Incomp}_{m}(c_{0},c_{1})\colon 2^{-(k+1)}D\leq\widehat{\operatorname{LCD}}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x,\alpha)\leq 2^{-k}D\right\},

where
0\leq k\leq\log_{2}D
(since for every
x\in\textup{Incomp}_{m}(c_{0},c_{1})
,
\widehat{\operatorname{LCD}}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x)\geq 1
).

By replacing
D
with
D/2^{k}
in (
4.9
), we see that
S_{D,k,\mathbb{R}^{m}}
has a
2^{k}\kappa/\sqrt{\alpha}D
net
\mathcal{N}_{k}
of cardinality

\absolutevalue{\mathcal{N}_k}\leq\frac{(CD)^{m}}{(2^{k})^{m}(\sqrt{\alpha m})^{c^{\prime}m/2}}(2^{-k}D)^{2/\alpha}.

Instead of attempting a
\beta
-net argument for all of
S_{D}
at once, we instead break the problem down into examining each set

separately, before union bounding over the possible values of

.

Before proving our next proposition, we provide an equivalent formulation of the singular vector equations

(4.10)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{1/2}Mu

\displaystyle=\sigma v,

(4.11)

\displaystyle M^{T}\Sigma^{1/2}v

\displaystyle=\sigma u,

where
u
and
v
are unit vectors. Let us call

so that
w
is a unit vector. Note that

\norm{\Sigma^{-1/2}w}=\frac{\norm{v}}{\norm{\Sigma^{1/2}v}}=\frac{1}{\norm{\Sigma^{1/2}v}}

and

v=\Sigma^{-1/2}w\norm{\Sigma^{1/2}v}=\frac{\Sigma^{-1/2}w}{\norm{\Sigma^{-1/2}w}}.

Then (
4.10
) reads as

\Sigma^{1/2}Mu=\sigma\frac{\Sigma^{-1/2}w}{\norm{\Sigma^{-1/2}w}}.

By dividing both sides of (
4.11
) by
\norm{\Sigma^{1/2}v}
, we obtain

M^{T}w=\frac{1}{\norm{\Sigma^{1/2}v}}\sigma u=\sigma\norm{\Sigma^{-1/2}w}u.

This motivates the formulation of the following proposition, which shows that for a fixed value of
\sigma_{0}
, with high probability there are no singular vector pairs
(u,v)
with singular value
\sigma_{0}
such that
u
or
\Sigma^{1/2}v/\norm{\Sigma^{1/2}v}
have low regularized
\operatorname{LCD}
.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
M
and
\Sigma
satisfy Assumptions
1.1
and
1.2
. There exist positive constants
c
and
C_{\ref{approx sv}}
depending on
c_{0}
and
c_{1}

Remark 5.8
.

We remark that Theorem
5.1
can be extended to rank-1 perturbations of
M
. More precisely, one can replace
M
by
M+\eta wz^{T}
, where
w,z
are deterministic unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, respectively, and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
deterministic with
|\eta|=\exp(o(n))
. Note that
\|\eta wz^{T}\|=|\eta|
. As a consequence, Corollaries
5.2
and
5.4
both hold in the case of replacing
M
with
M+\eta wz^{T}
. As the proof only requires minor modifications, we just give an outline of the argument below.

We consider two cases, depending on the size of
\eta
.

Case 1:
First, suppose that
|\eta|\leq 3LK\sqrt{n}
. In this case, the norm of
M+\eta wz^{T}
is of the same order as
\|M\|
and the entire argument is identical up to some slight changes to the constants. For example, in each place where one considers a net of possible singular values of
M
, an equally fine net of possible singular values of
M+\eta wz^{T}
is now 4 times as large (or 16 times as large in the case of the squared singular values). Such a difference in net size is immaterial. In this case, we do not need to make use of the rank-1 condition, so in fact, the argument applies to any perturbation with sufficiently small operator norm. For more details, see Remark
3.4
.

Case 2:
We now consider the case that
3LR\sqrt{n}\leq\left\|R\right\|\leq\exp(o(n))
when
\Sigma=I
. This presents several difficulties. In this setting, simply replacing
M
with
M+\eta wz^{T}
would require significantly finer nets to account for the larger norm, yet the probability bounds remain the same, which quickly renders most of the arguments infeasible. There is good reason to expect this breakdown as for large
\eta
, the dominant left and right singular vectors are near
w
and
z
, which may not have the same properties as the singular vectors of a centered random matrix.

We begin by establishing a lower bound on the gap between
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
.
On the event
\mathcal{E}_{K}
, by Weyl’s inequality,

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(M+\eta wz^{T})\geq|\eta|-\left\|M\right\|\geq 2KL\sqrt{n},

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}(M+\eta wz^{T})\leq\sigma_{2}(\eta uv^{T})+\left\|M\right\|\leq KL\sqrt{n},

(note that the second inequality holds independent of the norm of
\eta
, as
\eta wz^{T}
is rank-1)
so
|\sigma_{1}(M)-\sigma_{2}(M)|\geq KL\sqrt{n}
.
In several steps of the proof of Theorem
1.3
, for example in Proposition
3.5
, Lemma
4.9
, Proposition
4.7
and Theorem
4.10
, we assume that our singular values are of order
KL\sqrt{n}
. Due to the substantial gap between
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
, for the remainder of the argument, we can focus our attention on the singular values which have size at most
KL\sqrt{n}
, which resolves one aspect of this problem. For example, it suffices to prove Theorem
4.10
restricted to the case where
(u,v)
is a singular vector pair associated to some singular value
\sigma_{i}
where
2\leq i\leq p
.

However, even when restricting our attention to smaller singular values, the norm of
M+\eta wz^{T}
still appears in our arguments. For example, in Section
4
, the covering arguments for a singular vector pair
(u,v)
do not make use of any knowledge of other singular pairs, so we apply the argument to the entire unit sphere for each singular vector pair, in which case, naively, we must invoke the norm of
M+\eta wz^{T}
when deciding on the size of the net. To address this issue, we rely on the rank-1 structure of
\eta wz^{T}
.

Let
T\subset S^{p-1}
. The key observation is that the image of the unit sphere under
\eta wz^{T}
is a bounded, one-dimensional set, which therefore admits an
\varepsilon
-net of small cardinality. For example, to naively control

\mathbb{P}(\sup_{x\in T}\left\|(M+\eta wz^{T})x\right\|<\varepsilon)

we consider an
\varepsilon/2\|M+\eta wz^{T}\|
-net of
T
, that we denote by
\mathcal{N}
, and use the union bound to conclude that

\mathbb{P}(\sup_{x\in T}\left\|(M+\eta wz^{T})x\right\|<\varepsilon)\leq|\mathcal{N}|\sup_{x\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbb{P}(\left\|(M+\eta wz^{T})x\right\|<2\varepsilon).

However, we can instead consider an
\varepsilon/2\|M\|
-net of
T
, which we call
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
, and a
\varepsilon/2
-net of
[-2\eta,2\eta]\subset\mathbb{R}
, which we denote by
\mathcal{N}^{\prime\prime}
. Now, we observe that if
x\in T
is such that

\left\|(M+\eta wz^{T})x\right\|<\varepsilon

then there exists a

and

such that

\displaystyle\|(M+\eta wz^{T})x-My-tw\|\leq\|M\|\|x-y\|+|\eta z^{T}x-t|\|w\|\leq\varepsilon.

Thus,

\mathbb{P}(\sup_{x\in T}\left\|(M+\eta wz^{T})x\right\|<\varepsilon)\leq|\mathcal{N}^{\prime}||\mathcal{N}^{\prime\prime}|\sup_{x\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime},t\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime\prime}}\mathbb{P}(\left\|Mx+tw\right\|<2\varepsilon).

Crucially,

and as

is a net over a one-dimensional space, its cardinality is insignificant (so long as the bound on the size of

We now sketch the use of this idea in the different sections of our argument.
Suppose that there exists a compressible right singular vector
