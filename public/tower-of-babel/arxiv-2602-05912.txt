Thermal-Drift Sampling: Generating Random Thermal Ensembles for Quantum Chaos Diagnostics
Jiyu Jiang, Mingrui Jing, Jizhe Lai, Xin Wang, Lei Zhang

Abstract
Random thermal states of many-body Hamiltonians underpin studies of thermalization, chaos, and quantum phase transitions, yet their generation remains costly when each Hamiltonian must be prepared individually. We introduce the thermal-drift channel, a measurement-based operation that implements a tunable nonunitary drift along a chosen Pauli term. Based on this channel, we present a measurement-controlled sampling algorithm that generates thermal states together with their Hamiltonian “labels” for general physical models. We prove that the total gate count of our algorithm scales cubically with system size, quadratically with inverse temperature, and as the inverse error tolerance to the two-thirds power, with logarithmic dependence on the allowed failure probability. We also show that the induced label distribution approaches a normal distribution reweighted by the thermal partition function, which makes an explicit trade-off between accuracy and effective range. Numerical simulations for a 2D Heisenberg model validate the predicted scaling and distribution. As an application, we compute unfolding-free level-spacing ratio statistics from sampled thermal states of a 2D transverse-field Ising model and observe a crossover toward the Wigner–Dyson prediction, demonstrating a practical and scalable route to chaos diagnostics and random matrix universality studies on near-term quantum hardware.



Introduction.—

Considerable attention has been directed toward the generation of random quantum states, which offers a resource analogous to random numbers in classical stochastic theories
maziero2015random

While pure states are idealized, random mixed states effectively describe realistic quantum many-body systems, elucidating thermalization and phase structures
khemani2017critical

and random matrix theory predictions

;

Importantly, thermal states of generic many-body Hamiltonians exhibit highly nontrivial correlations and often volume-law entanglement, making them representative of the “typical” mixed states encountered in quantum chaos and finite-temperature phases

. This positions random thermal states as a minimal yet physically grounded ensemble for probing universal many-body phenomena, motivating the development of quantum algorithms that can efficiently generate them without relying on detailed spectral knowledge or classical simulation of the underlying dynamics.

Setting.—

We now formalize the sampling task.
Given a set of

Quantum thermal-drift channel.—

Ground state preparation is known to be QMA-hard in the worst case
watrous2008quantum
;
chowdhury2020variationala
, and low-temperature thermal state preparation faces comparable complexity barriers
aharonov2013quantum
.
A standard ‘sample-then-prepare’ pipeline, which first selects a Hamiltonian
H
and subsequently attempts to prepare its thermal state, is therefore often prohibitively costly. To circumvent this, we implement a randomized thermalization protocol directly on the quantum hardware.

We define the thermal-drift channel
\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
for any Pauli operator
\sigma
, which is the core component of our algorithm. For any input state
\rho
, the channel is written in,

~\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}(\rho)=\frac{\mu}{2}\big(\lvert\uparrow\rangle\!\langle\uparrow\rvert\otimes{\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}^{\uparrow}}(\rho)+\lvert\downarrow\rangle\!\langle\downarrow\rvert\otimes{\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}^{\downarrow}}(\rho)\big),

(2)

where
\mu<1
is a scaling factor. Here, for any state
\rho
, the maps
{\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}^{\uparrow}}
and
{\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}^{\downarrow}}
are two completely positive and non-trace-preserving operations defined as
e^{+\tau\sigma/2}\rho e^{+\tau\sigma/2}
and
e^{-\tau\sigma/2}\rho e^{-\tau\sigma/2}
, respectively,
with a thermal strength
\tau>0
.
The first register of
\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}(\rho)
naturally generates a random branch.
Measuring this flag register indicates which direction the state is thermalized toward, with probabilities proportional to the corresponding unnormalized traces.

The thermal-drift channel can be implemented using system-ancilla coupling in a dilated Hilbert space, followed by measuring and tracing out the flag register. In particular, we consider the following map

~\mathcal{N}_{\approx}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1+\mu}\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}(\rho)+\frac{\mu}{1+\mu}\lvert\circlearrowleft\rangle\!\langle\circlearrowleft\rvert\otimes\rho,

(3)

where
|\mathord{\uparrow}\rangle
,
|\mathord{\downarrow}\rangle
and
|\mathord{\circlearrowleft}\rangle
are mutually orthogonal states. This map can be implemented within
{\mathcal{O}\!\left(n\right)}
quantum elementary gates and
2n
ancilla qubits. Then, with failure probability at most
\delta
, the thermal-drift channel is simulated by repeatedly applying
\mathcal{N}_{\approx}
and the associated measure-and-trace operations until the measurement outcome is not ‘
\mathord{\circlearrowleft}
’, using at most
\log(1/\delta)
repetitions. In particular, the same state and ancillas can be reused without postselection. We refer to the Supplementary Material for a detailed implementation.

Algorithm overview.—

We now present the thermal state sampling algorithm, implemented as a circuit of
N
blocks applied to an initial state
\rho_{0}
.
In block
k
, a thermal-drift channel
\mathcal{N}_{\sigma_{j_{k}}}
is applied, where
\sigma_{j_{k}}
is selected independently from the set
\Sigma
. The probability of choosing
\mathcal{N}_{\sigma_{j}}
is weighted by the bound
{h}_{j}
, so that the distribution is
{h}_{j}/\lambda
. The thermal strength is fixed across all blocks to
\tau=\beta\lambda/N
.
After the thermal-drift channel, a measure-and-trace operation
\mathcal{M}_{m_{k}}
is applied to the flag register, generating a measurement outcome
m_{k}
. This outcome is recorded as
m_{k}\in\left\{{\kern 0.20828pt}+1,-1{\kern 0.20828pt}\right\}
corresponding to
\uparrow
and
\downarrow
.

The full circuit implementation is labeled by an ordered list of values
{\bm{j}}=\left\{{\kern 0.20828pt}j_{1},\cdots,j_{N}{\kern 0.20828pt}\right\}
and an ordered list of directions
{\bm{m}}=\left\{{\kern 0.20828pt}m_{1},\cdots,m_{N}{\kern 0.20828pt}\right\}
, that corresponds to the channel

{\mathcal{E}_{{\bm{j}},{\bm{m}}}}=\mathcal{M}_{m_{N}}\circ\mathcal{N}_{\sigma_{j_{N}}}\circ\ldots\circ\mathcal{M}_{m_{1}}\circ\mathcal{N}_{\sigma_{j_{1}}}.

(4)

In particular, one can collect the accumulated drift along each Pauli axis
\sigma_{j}
, which defines the overall Hamiltonian label

H_{{\bm{j}},{\bm{m}}}=\frac{\lambda}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}j_{k}=j}m_{k}\sigma_{j}.

(5)

This Hamiltonian can be understood as the label associated with the operation that maps the initial state
\rho_{0}
to the output state
{\mathcal{E}_{{\bm{j}},{\bm{m}}}}(\rho_{0})
. When
\rho_{0}
is the maximally mixed state, the output state is then a copy of its thermal state

{\mathcal{E}_{{\bm{j}},{\bm{m}}}}(\rho_{0})\approx G_{\beta,H_{{\bm{j}},{\bm{m}}}}.

(6)

Finally, sampling indices with weights
{h}_{j}/\lambda
controls the effective coefficient scales, so that the induced labels conform to the envelope specified by
{h}
, thereby completing the task of thermal state sampling.

Sampling error and distribution.—

Unlike randomized algorithms such as qDRIFT
campbell2019random
, where the randomness is Markovian and can be analyzed directly in channel form, each measurement outcome in our algorithm depends on the evolving state, and hence the process is generally non-Markovian. We therefore use probabilistic tools to analyze the sampling error and the induced distribution over labels.

We start with the sampling error
\epsilon
, quantified by the trace distance between the output state of our algorithm and the ideal thermal state of the sampled Hamiltonian.
Given the sampled outcomes
{\bm{j}}
and
{\bm{m}}
, we consider the effective Hamiltonian
H_{\approx}
such that
{\mathcal{E}_{{\bm{j}},{\bm{m}}}}(\rho_{0})=e^{-\beta H_{\approx}}/\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{-\beta H_{\approx}}\right]
.
Then the sampling error is bounded by
\epsilon\leq\beta\lVert H_{{\bm{j}},{\bm{m}}}-H_{\approx}\rVert_{\infty}
.
When the number of steps
N
is not large enough so that most sampled Pauli operators are distinct, the sampling error behaves like the discretization error of a second-order Trotter product formula, scaling as
{\mathcal{O}\!\left(\beta^{3}\right)}

childs2018toward
. However, when
N
is sufficiently large so that Pauli operators are likely repetitive, we treat
{\bm{m}}
as random variables conditional on a fixed
{\bm{j}}
. In this regime, the Freedman inequality for matrix martingales
tropp2011freedman
implies an
{\mathcal{O}\!\left(\beta^{2}\right)}
scaling almost surely. This leads to the following.

Theorem 1

For any system size
n
, inverse temperature
\beta
, and sufficiently large
N
, our algorithm uses
{\mathcal{O}\!\left(nN\log(1/\delta)\right)}
gates to sample a thermal state and its label, with precision
{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(n^{3/2}\lambda^{3}\beta^{3}N^{-3/2}\right)}
and failure probability at most
\delta
.

Here
{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(\cdot\right)}
omits the logarithm terms.
Theorem
1
is numerically verified in Figure
2
(a). For increasing inverse temperature starting at
\beta=1
, we test our algorithm with number of steps
N={\mathcal{O}\!\left(\beta^{k}\right)}
for
k=1.5
,
2
and
2.5
, and observe that the average trace-distance error scales as
{\mathcal{O}\!\left(\beta^{2-k}\right)}
, consistent with Theorem
1
. Unless extra clarified, we take
N=n^{2}\beta^{2}\epsilon^{-2/3}
in the rest of experiments.

To obtain a theoretical analysis of the sample distribution,
we consider an asymptotic regime where the number of sampling steps
N
tends to infinity, so that every possible label
H\in{\mathfrak{H}}
can be sampled.
Then our algorithm can be modeled as a random walk
(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})_{k}
on an
L
-dimensional integer lattice
{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{L}
, such that
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}
and
\mathbf{x}^{(N)}
correspond to the identity matrix and the sampled label
H
, respectively.
Such a walk sequence satisfies for all
k\geq 1
,

\textup{Pr}\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}^{(k-1)}=\pm e_{j}\right)=\textup{Pr}\!\left(m_{k}=\pm 1\right)\cdot{h}_{j}/\lambda,

(7)

where
e_{j}
denotes the
j
-th unit vector. Applying lattice-based random walk theory
spitzer2001principles
yields a Gaussian approximation to the endpoint distribution under appropriate scaling. Combined with the thermal reweighting from
\textup{Pr}\!\left(m_{k}=\pm 1\right)
, the sampling rule takes a product form involving the partition function and a normal density.

Theorem 2

For any inverse temperature
\beta
, a label
H\in{\mathfrak{H}}
can be asymptotically sampled by our algorithm with probability proportional to
\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{-\beta H}\right]D(\mathbf{x})
, where
D
is an
L
-dimensional multivariate normal distribution and
\mathbf{x}
satisfies
\mathbf{x}_{j}=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[H\sigma_{j}\right]/\lambda\cdot N
.

We numerically show that a similar result holds in non-asymptotic cases. Taking
\beta=2
, we sample from our algorithm and visualize one-dimensional slices of the marginal distribution of a chosen Pauli coefficient. As shown in Figure
2
(b), the empirical distribution closely matches the ideal one. Note that the difference mainly occurs in both ends of the distribution, which will get smaller as the number of sampling step further increases.

Theorems
1
and
2
together imply a trade-off between sample accuracy and effective sample range. Due to the nature of random walks, higher accuracy comes with a more concentrated label distribution, and vice versa. As shown in Figure
2
(c), for
\beta=2
, as the number of steps
N
increases from
{\mathcal{O}\!\left(\beta\right)}
to
{\mathcal{O}\!\left(\beta^{3}\right)}
, the inverse precision
1/\epsilon
increases while the effective sample range
\lVert H\rVert_{\infty}
decreases at a similar rate. Therefore, despite the efficiency of a single execution, it is not recommended to use our algorithm for preparing thermal states of one fixed Hamiltonian. However, this characteristic makes our algorithm particularly well-suited for ensemble-level applications.

Application to the level statistics.—

A natural application of our thermal state sampler is level statistics, which characterizes thermodynamic phases in quantum many-body systems
potter2015universal
. Given any random density operator with an ordered many-body eigenvalue spectrum
\{\lambda_{j}\}
, one defines the adjacent logarithmic level spacings
\delta_{j}=\log(\lambda_{j}/\lambda_{j+1})
and the dimensionless spacing (gap) ratio
oganesyan2007localization
;
sierant2020model
,

r_{j}=\frac{\min\{\delta_{j},\delta_{j+1}\}}{\max\{\delta_{j},\delta_{j+1}\}}\in[0,1],

(8)

whose distribution is insensitive to spectral unfolding and thus widely used in finite-size studies
serbyn2016spectral
;
buijsman2019random
;
odavic2023random
. In ergodic systems satisfying the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis, strong level repulsion leads to Wigner–Dyson statistics
wigner1967random
;
buijsman2019random
, whereas in the many-body localization phase, emergent local integrals of motion suppress correlations and produce Poisson level statistics
pal2010manybody
;
khemani2017critical
. The gap ratio and disorder-averaged
\langle r\rangle
therefore provide a clear diagnostic of the crossover or transition between these regimes
rabson2004crossover
;
serbyn2016spectral
.

We further apply our framework as a diagnostic tool to assess the chaotic nature of the generated states. In particular, we apply our algorithm to investigate the level statistics of the generated thermal state ensemble. We compare an initial reference state whose spectrum exhibits near-Poisson statistics with the output states produced by our sampling algorithm.
As shown in Figure
3
, aggregating
\left\{{\kern 0.20828pt}r_{j}{\kern 0.20828pt}\right\}
over samples yields a robust, unfolding-free diagnostic. The analysis uncovers a clear signature of thermalization that the level statistics shift from a Poissonian distribution in the reference state to a Wigner–Dyson distribution in the output ensemble. This observation indicates that the sampler accesses a substantially more chaotic sector of Hilbert space than the structured input, consistent with the interpretation that the generated states are not efficiently captured by simple classical models.

Conclusion and outlook.—

We have developed a method to efficiently generate random thermal states for Hamiltonians drawn from broad Pauli ensembles, which opens several directions.
On the physics side, our method provides direct access to level statistics, entanglement measures, and finite-temperature signatures without reliance on exact diagonalization or classical Monte Carlo methods
temme2011quantum
.
On the computation side, our method generates thermal randomness in comparison to Haar randomness for benchmarking quantum devices. More broadly, our results suggest a shift from Hamiltonian-specific thermalization protocols toward measurement-controlled ensemble sampling, with potential implications for quantum simulation in sign-problem-limited regimes and for quantum machine learning, where thermal states serve as training data and their Hamiltonian labels enable supervised learning of many-body properties
huang2021power
;
lloyd2018quantum
;
poulin2009sampling
.
Our framework integrates quantum algorithms, statistical mechanics, and machine learning, providing tools for both near-term quantum applications and fundamental many-body physics. We expect these directions to further clarify the role of randomness, temperature, and complexity in interacting quantum systems.

A.3
Level statistics of quantum density operators

This section outlines the framework for analyzing the spectral properties of the generated quantum thermal states. Level statistics, commonly applied to Hamiltonian energy spectra to diagnose quantum chaos and ergodicity
wigner1967random
;
oganesyan2007localization
, extend naturally to density operators
\rho
via the modular Hamiltonian.

For a strictly positive density operator
\rho
, its spectral properties are closely related to those of the fictitious Hamiltonian governing the thermal distribution. We define the modular Hamiltonian
H
by
\rho=\frac{e^{-H}}{\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-H})}
. The eigenvalues of
\rho
, denoted by
\{p_{i}\}
, are directly related to the spectrum of
H
, denoted by
\{\xi_{i}\}
, via the transformation
\xi_{i}=-\ln(p_{i})-\ln Z
. The level statistics of
\rho
are thus analyzed through the unfolding of the "entanglement energies" or modular levels
\{\xi_{i}\}
.

To avoid the ambiguities associated with spectral unfolding in finite-size systems, we utilize the adjacent gap ratio statistics introduced by Oganesyan and Huse
oganesyan2007localization
. Let the sorted spectrum of the modular Hamiltonian be ordered such that
\xi_{1}\leq\xi_{2}\leq\dots\leq\xi_{D}
, where
D
is the dimension of the Hilbert space. We define the nearest-neighbor level spacing as:

\delta_{n}=\xi_{n+1}-\xi_{n}\geq 0.

(S14)

The dimensionless level spacing ratio
r_{n}
is defined as:

r_{n}=\frac{\min(\delta_{n},\delta_{n+1})}{\max(\delta_{n},\delta_{n+1})}=\min\left(\tilde{r}_{n},\frac{1}{\tilde{r}_{n}}\right)\in[0,1]

(S15)

where
\tilde{r}_{n}=\delta_{n+1}/\delta_{n}
. The distribution of
r_{n}
, denoted as
P(r)
, and its mean value
\langle r\rangle
, serve as robust indicators of the nature of the correlations in the quantum state. The following summarizes the known results of the average spacing ratios with respect to the specific RMT ensembles
atas2013distribution
:

•

Integrable (Poissonian) limit
: Systems with uncorrelated levels, typical of integrable models or localized phases, exhibit level spacings following a Poisson distribution with
P(r)=\frac{2}{(1+r)^{2}}
and
\langle r\rangle_{\text{Poisson}}\approx 0.386
.

•

Chaotic (Wigner–Dyson) limit
: Ergodic quantum systems exhibit level repulsion characteristic of Random Matrix Theory (RMT), with statistics depending on the symmetry class:
\langle r\rangle_{\text{GOE}}\approx 0.536
for systems with time-reversal symmetry, and
\langle r\rangle_{\text{GUE}}\approx 0.603
for systems without.

In our algorithm,
\langle r\rangle
values approaching the GUE/GOE limits in the sampled thermal states indicate that the generated state captures the complexity and correlations of a thermalizing many-body system. Conversely,
\langle r\rangle\approx 0.39
would suggest a failure to capture thermal correlations or an underlying integrability in the generator set.

Pseudo code for our thermal state sampler

This section develops two technical components used to analyze Algorithm
1
. First, we prove the trace-norm error bound between the algorithmic output state and the target thermal state (Theorem
S12
). Second, we set up the random-walk viewpoint for the induced Hamiltonian coefficients, which underlies the sampling-probability analysis in Theorem

.

C.1
Error analysis

Fix the step number
N
and a sequence of indices
(j_{k})_{k=1}^{N}\subset[L]
selecting Pauli operators from
\Sigma=\left\{{\kern 0.20828pt}\sigma_{j}{\kern 0.20828pt}\right\}_{j=1}^{L}
, as in Algorithm
1
. Let
\rho_{k}
denote the (normalized) system state after
k
steps, and define the step size
\tau=\lambda\beta/N
. Then
(\rho_{k})_{k=0}^{N}
evolves according to the Markovian update rule

~\rho_{0}=I/2^{n},\quad\rho_{k}=\mathcal{M}_{j_{k}}\circ\mathcal{N}_{j_{k}}(\rho_{k-1})=\frac{e^{B_{k}}\,\rho_{k-1}\,e^{B_{k}}}{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{B_{k}}\,\rho_{k-1}\,e^{B_{k}}\right]},

(S85)

where
\mathcal{M}_{j_{k}},\mathcal{N}_{j_{k}}
are specified by Theorem
S7
for
\sigma_{j_{k}}
. The corresponding random variable
B_{k}
takes the form

B_{k}=\begin{cases}\tau\sigma_{j_{k}}/2,&\textrm{ with probability }{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{\tau\sigma_{j_{k}}}\rho_{k-1}\right]}/{2\cosh\tau},\\
-\tau\sigma_{j_{k}}/2,&\textrm{ with probability }{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{-\tau\sigma_{j_{k}}}\rho_{k-1}\right]}/{2\cosh\tau}.\end{cases}

(S86)

Iterating Equation (
S85
) shows that the final output state can be written as

\rho_{N}=\frac{\big(\prod_{k=N}^{1}e^{B_{k}}\big)\rho_{0}\big(\prod_{k=1}^{N}e^{B_{k}}\big)}{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\text{ same above}\right]}=\frac{\big(\prod_{k=N}^{2}e^{B_{k}}\big)e^{2B_{1}}\big(\prod_{k=2}^{N}e^{B_{k}}\big)}{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\text{ same above}\right]},

(S87)

where
\prod_{k=N}^{2}
denotes the ordered product from
N
down to
2
. The corresponding target thermal state is

~G_{\beta,H}=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{-\beta H}\right]}\textrm{\quad for \quad}H=-\frac{2}{\beta}\sum_{k=1}^{N}B_{k}.

(S88)

In this subsection, we prove Theorem
S12
, which shows that, with probability at least
1-\delta
and for sufficiently large
N
,

\lVert\rho_{N}-G_{\beta,H}\rVert_{1}\leq{\mathcal{O}\!\left(\big(n+\log(N/\delta)\big)^{3/2}\lambda^{3}\beta^{3}N^{-3/2}\right)}.

(S89)

Two lemmas simplify the proof. The first lemma compares the palindromic product in
\rho_{N}
with the exponential of the accumulated generator.

Lemma S10

Let
(B_{k})_{k=1}^{N}
be a sequence of matrices. Denote
S_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}B_{j}
. Suppose
\max_{1\leq j\leq N}\lVert B_{j}\rVert_{\infty}=\tau\ll 1
. Then

\log\left(\big(\prod_{j=N}^{2}e^{B_{j}}\big)e^{2B_{1}}\big(\prod_{j=2}^{N}e^{B_{j}}\big)\right)=2S_{N}+\Delta_{N}+{\cal R}_{N},

(S90)

where
\Delta_{k}=-\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=2}^{k}\left[B_{j}+2S_{j-1},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[B_{j},{\kern 1.66656pt}2S_{j-1}\right]\right]
denotes the terms of order
3
and
{\cal R}_{N}
denotes the remainder terms.
We have

\lVert{\cal R}_{N}\rVert_{\infty}={\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sum_{r=5,r\textrm{ is odd}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{k=2}^{N}\lVert\operatorname{ad}^{r-1}_{2S_{k-1}}(B_{k})\rVert_{\infty}\right)}.

(S91)

Proof.

For Hermitian
X
and
Y
, since
(e^{X}e^{Y}e^{X})^{-1}=e^{-X}e^{-Y}e^{-X}
, we have

\displaystyle\log\left(e^{X}e^{Y}e^{X}\right)+\log\left(e^{-X}e^{-Y}e^{-X}\right)=0,

(S92)

which implies
f(X,Y)=\log\left(e^{X}e^{Y}e^{X}\right)=-f(-X,-Y)
.
Hence, the BCH expansion of
\log\left(e^{X}e^{Y}e^{X}\right)
contains no even-order terms.

We proceed by induction using the palindromic products

M_{k}\coloneqq\Big(\prod_{j=k}^{2}e^{B_{j}}\Big)e^{2B_{1}}\Big(\prod_{j=2}^{k}e^{B_{j}}\Big),\quad Z_{k}\coloneqq\log(M_{k}).

(S93)

We have
M_{1}=e^{2B_{1}}
, and for
k\geq 2
,
M_{k}=e^{B_{k}}M_{k-1}e^{B_{k}}=e^{B_{k}}e^{Z_{k-1}}e^{B_{k}}
.

For
k=1
, we have
Z_{1}=\log(e^{2B_{1}})=2B_{1}=2S_{1}
, so the claim holds.

Now fix
k\geq 2
and assume inductively that
Z_{k-1}=2S_{k-1}+\Delta_{k-1}+{\cal R}_{k-1}
, where
\Delta_{k-1}
collects the cubic terms and
{\cal R}_{k-1}
collects the remaining odd-order terms of degree at least
5
. Applying Lemma
S3
to
e^{B_{k}}e^{Z_{k-1}}e^{B_{k}}
gives

\displaystyle Z_{k}

\displaystyle=\log\left(e^{B_{k}}e^{Z_{k-1}}e^{B_{k}}\right)

(S94)

\displaystyle=\log\left(e^{B_{k}}e^{2S_{k-1}+\Delta_{k-1}+{\cal R}_{k-1}}e^{B_{k}}\right)

(S95)

\displaystyle=2S_{k}+\Delta_{k-1}+{\cal R}_{k-1}-\frac{1}{6}\left[B_{k}+2S_{k-1}+\Delta_{k-1}+{\cal R}_{k-1},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[B_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}2S_{k-1}+\Delta_{k-1}+{\cal R}_{k-1}\right]\right].

(S96)

Separating the cubic contribution from higher-order terms yields

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{k-1}-\frac{1}{6}\left[B_{k}+2S_{k-1},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[B_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}2S_{k-1}\right]\right],

(S97)

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{k}

\displaystyle={\cal R}_{k-1}-\frac{1}{6}\left[B_{k}+2S_{k-1},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[B_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}\Delta_{k-1}+{\cal R}_{k-1}\right]\right]-\frac{1}{6}\left[\Delta_{k-1}+{\cal R}_{k-1},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[B_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}2S_{k-1}+\Delta_{k-1}+{\cal R}_{k-1}\right]\right].

(S98)

Iterating the recursion for
\Delta_{k}
gives

\displaystyle\Delta_{N}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{N-1}-\frac{1}{6}\left[B_{N}+2S_{N-1},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[B_{N},{\kern 1.66656pt}2S_{N-1}\right]\right]

(S99)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{6}\sum_{k=2}^{N}\left[B_{k}+2S_{k-1},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[B_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}2S_{k-1}\right]\right].

(S100)

It remains to bound the remainder terms in
{\cal R}_{N}
. Write
{\cal R}_{k}=\sum_{r\geq 5,\,r\text{ odd}}{\cal R}_{k,r}
, where
{\cal R}_{k,r}
denotes the homogeneous terms of total order
r
, and assume
\lVert B_{k}\rVert_{\infty}={\mathcal{O}\!\left(\lVert S_{k-1}\rVert_{\infty}\right)}
for
k\geq 2
.
From the recursive definition of
{\cal R}_{k}
, the order-
r
component obeys

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{k,r}={\cal R}_{k-1,r}+\frac{1}{6}\left[B_{k}+2S_{k-1},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[{\cal R}_{k-1,r-2},{\kern 1.66656pt}B_{k}\right]\right]+\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=3}^{r-4}\left[{\cal R}_{k-1,j},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[{\cal R}_{k-1,r-1-j},{\kern 1.66656pt}B_{k}\right]\right].

(S101)

We claim that, for each odd
r\geq 5
, the increment
{\cal R}_{k,r}-{\cal R}_{k-1,r}
is dominated (up to constants depending only on
r
) by terms of the form
\operatorname{ad}^{r-1}_{2S_{k-1}}(B_{k})
. The claim is immediate for
r=5
, and the recursion preserves the dominance for higher odd orders.

Finally, since the coefficient of
\operatorname{ad}^{r-1}_{X}(Y)
in the BCH expansion of
\log(e^{X}e^{Y}e^{X})
has magnitude
2/r!
, there exists a constant
C_{r}
(depending only on
r
) such that, for all odd
r\geq 5
,

\displaystyle\lVert{\cal R}_{N,r}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{r}}{r!}\sum_{k=2}^{N}\lVert\operatorname{ad}^{r-1}_{2S_{k-1}}(B_{k})\rVert_{\infty}.

(S102)

Summing over odd
r\geq 5
gives the stated bound on
\lVert{\cal R}_{N}\rVert_{\infty}
.

The second lemma controls the effect of normalizing matrix exponentials.

Lemma S11

Let
A
,
E
be two Hermitian matrices. Then

\lVert\frac{e^{A+E}}{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{A+E}\right]}-\frac{e^{A}}{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{A}\right]}\rVert_{1}\leq 2\lVert E\rVert_{\infty}.

(S103)

Proof.

Consider
t\in[0,1]
,
A(t)=A+tE
and
\rho(t)=e^{A(t)}/{\cal Z}(t)
with
{\cal Z}(t)=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{A(t)}\right]
.
By Lemma
S1
, we have

~\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}{\kern 0.83328pt}t}e^{A(t)}=\int_{0}^{1}e^{sA(t)}Ee^{(1-s)A(t)}{\kern 1.66656pt}\textup{d}{s}.

(S104)

Differentiating
\rho(t)=e^{A(t)}/{\cal Z}(t)
gives

\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}{\kern 0.83328pt}t}\rho(t)=\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}{\kern 0.83328pt}t}\left(e^{A(t)}\cdot\frac{1}{{\cal Z}(t)}\right)=F(t)-\rho(t)\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[F(t)\right],

(S105)

where

F(t)=\frac{1}{{\cal Z}(t)}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}{\kern 0.83328pt}t}e^{A(t)}=\int_{0}^{1}\rho(t)^{s}E\rho(t)^{1-s}{\kern 1.66656pt}\textup{d}{s}.

(S106)

By Lemma
S2
,
\lVert F(t)\rVert_{1}\leq\lVert E\rVert_{\infty}
.
In addition,

\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[F(t)\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\rho(t)^{s}E\rho(t)^{1-s}\right]{\kern 1.66656pt}\textup{d}{s}=\int_{0}^{1}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\rho(t)E\right]{\kern 1.66656pt}\textup{d}{s}=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\rho(t)E\right]\leq\lVert E\rVert_{\infty}.

(S107)

Therefore
\lVert\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}{\kern 0.68283pt}t}\rho(t)\rVert_{1}\leq 2\lVert E\rVert_{\infty}
for all
t\in[0,1]
, and integrating over
t
yields

\lVert\rho(1)-\rho(0)\rVert_{1}\leq 2\lVert E\rVert_{\infty},

(S108)

as claimed.

The main error bound is stated and proved below. The argument is most informative when indices repeat frequently (i.e.,
N\gg\max(L,n)
), where the palindromic product can be compared sharply with an exponential via higher-order BCH control, yielding a tighter bound than a second-order Trotter decomposition
childs2018toward
.

Theorem S12

(Complete version of Theorem
1
)

Let
N={\Omega\!\left(n\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}\right)}
and
\delta>0
. Then
\rho_{N}
in Equation (
S85
) and
G_{\beta,H}
in Equation (
S88
) satisfy

\textup{Pr}\!\left(\lVert\rho_{N}-G_{\beta,H}\rVert_{1}>{\mathcal{O}\!\left(K_{0}^{3/2}N^{3/2}\tau^{3}\right)}\right)\leq\delta

(S109)

for
K_{0}=(n+2)\log 2+\log(N/\delta)
.

Proof.

Denote
S_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}B_{i}
. By Lemma
S10
,

E\coloneqq 2S_{N}-\log\left(\big(\prod_{k=N}^{2}e^{B_{k}}\big)e^{2B_{1}}\big(\prod_{k=2}^{N}e^{B_{k}}\big)\right)=\frac{1}{3}\left(E_{1}-2E_{2}\right)-R_{N}

(S110)

for
E_{1}=\sum_{k=2}^{N}\left[B_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[B_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}S_{k-1}\right]\right]
,
E_{2}=\sum_{k=2}^{N}\left[S_{k-1},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[B_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}S_{k-1}\right]\right]
and some remainder term
R_{N}
. Lemma
S11
reduces the trace-norm comparison to a spectral-norm bound on
E
:

\displaystyle\lVert\rho_{N}-G_{\beta,H}\rVert_{1}=\lVert\frac{e^{\log\big(\prod_{k=N}^{2}e^{B_{k}}\big)e^{2B_{1}}\big(\prod_{k=2}^{N}e^{B_{k}}\big)}}{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\textrm{same above}\right]}-\frac{e^{E+\log\big(\prod_{k=N}^{2}e^{B_{k}}\big)e^{2B_{1}}\big(\prod_{k=2}^{N}e^{B_{k}}\big)}}{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\textrm{same above}\right]}\rVert_{1}\leq 2\lVert E\rVert_{\infty}.

(S111)

It therefore suffices to upper bound
\lVert E_{1}\rVert_{\infty}
,
\lVert E_{2}\rVert_{\infty}
, and
\lVert R_{N}\rVert_{\infty}
with high probability, and then combine these bounds.

We begin by controlling
\lVert S_{k}\rVert_{\infty}
. Let
{\cal F}_{k}=(B_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
be the natural filtration, define the drift
D_{k}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[B_{k}{\kern 1.66656pt}|{\kern 1.66656pt}{\cal F}_{k-1}\right]
, and let
M_{k}=B_{k}-D_{k}
. Then
S_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}D_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{i}
. For the drift term, we have

\displaystyle\lVert D_{k}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\lVert\mathbb{E}\!\left[B_{k}{\kern 1.66656pt}|{\kern 1.66656pt}{\cal F}_{k-1}\right]\rVert_{\infty}=\lVert\left[\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{\tau\sigma_{j_{k}}}\rho_{k-1}\right]}{2\cosh\tau}-\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{-\tau\sigma_{j_{k}}}\rho_{k-1}\right]}{2\cosh\tau}\right]\cdot\frac{\tau\sigma_{j_{k}}}{2}\rVert_{\infty}

(S112)

\displaystyle\leq\lvert\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{\tau\sigma_{j_{k}}}\rho_{k-1}\right]-\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{-\tau\sigma_{j_{k}}}\rho_{k-1}\right]\rvert\cdot\frac{\tau}{4\cosh\tau}

(S113)

\displaystyle=\lvert(e^{\tau}-e^{-\tau})\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\sigma_{j_{k}}\rho_{k-1}\right]\rvert\cdot\frac{\tau}{2(e^{\tau}+e^{-\tau})}

(S114)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\tau\tanh\tau=\tau^{2}+{\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tau^{4}\right)}<0.1\tau,

(S115)

for
\tau\ll 1
. The sequence
(M_{k})_{k}
is a martingale difference sequence of Hermitian matrices with respect to
({\cal F}_{k})_{k}
, since
\mathbb{E}\!\left[M_{k}{\kern 1.66656pt}|{\kern 1.66656pt}{\cal F}_{k-1}\right]=0
.
In particular,
\lVert M_{k}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lVert B_{k}\rVert_{\infty}+\lVert D_{k}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\tau+0.1\tau=1.1\tau
.
Applying Lemma
S4
, for any
k
and
t_{k}>0
,

\textup{Pr}\!\left(\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{i}\rVert_{\infty}>t_{k}\textup{ and }\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\!\left[M_{i}^{2}{\kern 1.66656pt}|{\kern 1.66656pt}{\cal F}_{i-1}\right]\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{k\tau^{2}}{4}\right)\leq 2^{n+1}\exp\!\big(-\frac{t_{k}^{2}/2}{k\tau^{2}/4+1.1\tau t_{k}/3}\big).

(S116)

We also have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\!\left[M_{i}^{2}{\kern 1.66656pt}|{\kern 1.66656pt}{\cal F}_{i-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\!\left[B_{i}^{2}{\kern 1.66656pt}|{\kern 1.66656pt}{\cal F}_{i-1}\right]-D_{i}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\tau^{2}}{4}I-D_{i}^{2}

(S117)

\displaystyle\implies\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\!\left[M_{i}^{2}{\kern 1.66656pt}|{\kern 1.66656pt}{\cal F}_{i-1}\right]\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lVert\frac{\tau^{2}}{4}I-D_{i}^{2}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lVert\frac{\tau^{2}}{4}I\rVert_{\infty}=\frac{k}{4}\tau^{2},

(S118)

so choosing
K=(n+1)\log 2+\log\delta^{-1}
and

t_{k}=\tau\sqrt{kK/2}+2.2\tau K/3
implies
(t_{k}/\tau)^{2}/(k/2+\frac{2.2}{3}t_{k}/\tau)\geq K
, and therefore

\displaystyle~\textup{Pr}\!\left(\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{i}\rVert_{\infty}>t_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n+1}\exp\!\big(-\frac{t_{k}^{2}}{k\tau^{2}/2+2.2\tau t_{k}/3}\big)=2^{n+1}\exp\!\big(-\frac{(t_{k}/\tau)^{2}}{k/2+2.2/3\cdot(t_{k}/\tau)}\big)

(S119)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n+1}e^{-K}=\delta.

(S120)

Combining the drift and martingale parts yields

\lVert S_{k}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{i}\rVert_{\infty}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lVert D_{i}\rVert_{\infty}\implies\textup{Pr}\!\left(\lVert S_{k}\rVert_{\infty}>t_{k}+k\tau^{2}\right)\leq\delta.

(S121)

We next bound
\lVert E_{1}\rVert_{\infty}
. Since
B_{k}\sim\left\{{\kern 0.20828pt}\tau\sigma_{j_{k}}/2,-\tau\sigma_{j_{k}}/2{\kern 0.20828pt}\right\}
,

\displaystyle\lVert E_{1}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\lVert\sum_{k=2}^{N}\left[B_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[B_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}S_{k-1}\right]\right]\rVert_{\infty}

(S122)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=2}^{N}\lVert\left[B_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[B_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}S_{k-1}\right]\right]\rVert_{\infty}=\sum_{k=2}^{N}\lVert B_{k}^{2}S_{k-1}+S_{k-1}B_{k}^{2}-2B_{k}S_{k-1}B_{k}\rVert_{\infty}

(S123)

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=2}^{N}\lVert S_{k-1}-\sigma_{j_{k}}S_{k-1}\sigma_{j_{k}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\tau^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{N}\lVert S_{k-1}\rVert_{\infty}

(S124)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\textup{Pr}\!\left(\lVert E_{1}\rVert_{\infty}>\tau^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{N}(t_{k}+k\tau^{2})\right)\leq\delta.

(S125)

The term
E_{2}
is handled in a similar way. Assume
\lVert S_{k}\rVert_{\infty}\leq t_{k}+k\tau^{2}
(we account for this event in the final probability bound). Define
Q_{k}=\left[S_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[M_{k+1},{\kern 1.66656pt}S_{k}\right]\right]
, so that

\left[S_{k-1},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[B_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}S_{k-1}\right]\right]=Q_{k-1}+\left[S_{k-1},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[D_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}S_{k-1}\right]\right]

(S126)

with
\lVert\left[S_{k-1},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[D_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}S_{k-1}\right]\right]\rVert_{\infty}\leq 4\lVert S_{k}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\lVert D_{k+1}\rVert_{\infty}=4(t_{k}+k\tau^{2})^{2}\tau^{2}
.
One can verify that
\mathbb{E}\!\left[Q_{k}{\kern 1.66656pt}|{\kern 1.66656pt}{\cal F}_{k}\right]=0
and

\displaystyle\lVert Q_{k}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 4\lVert S_{k}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\lVert M_{k+1}\rVert_{\infty}=4.4(t_{k}+k\tau^{2})^{2}\tau,

(S127)

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{E}\!\left[Q_{k}^{2}{\kern 1.66656pt}|{\kern 1.66656pt}{\cal F}_{k-1}\right]\rVert_{\infty}\leq 4\lVert S_{k}\rVert_{\infty}^{4}\lVert M_{k+1}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\leq 20(t_{k}+k\tau^{2})^{4}\tau^{2},

(S128)

\displaystyle\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\!\left[Q_{k}^{2}{\kern 1.66656pt}|{\kern 1.66656pt}{\cal F}_{k-1}\right]\rVert_{\infty}\leq 20N(t_{N}+N\tau^{2})^{4}\tau^{2}.

(S129)

Lemma
S4
therefore implies that, for any
k
and
s_{k}>0
,

\textup{Pr}\!\left(\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{k}Q_{i}\rVert_{\infty}>s_{k}\right)\leq 2^{n+1}\exp\!\big(-\frac{s_{k}^{2}/2}{20N(t_{N}+N\tau^{2})^{4}\tau^{2}+4.4(t_{k}+k\tau^{2})^{2}\tau s_{k}/3}\big).

(S130)

Taking

s_{k}=\sqrt{40KN}(t_{N}+N\tau^{2})^{2}\tau+8.8(t_{k}+k\tau^{2})^{2}K\tau/3

simplifies the bound to

\textup{Pr}\!\left(\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{k}Q_{i}\rVert_{\infty}>s_{k}\right)\leq 2^{n+1}e^{-K}\leq\delta.

(S131)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\lVert E_{2}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\lVert\sum_{k=2}^{N}\left[S_{k-1},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[B_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}S_{k-1}\right]\right]\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lVert\sum_{k=2}^{N}Q_{k-1}\rVert_{\infty}+\sum_{k=2}^{N}\lVert\left[S_{k-1},{\kern 1.66656pt}\left[D_{k},{\kern 1.66656pt}S_{k-1}\right]\right]\rVert_{\infty}

(S132)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\textup{Pr}\!\left(\lVert E_{2}\rVert_{\infty}>s_{N}+4\sum_{k=2}^{N}(t_{k}+k\tau^{2})^{2}\tau^{2}\right)\leq\delta.

(S133)

We now combine the bounds. Since
N\gg n
, for all
k\leq N
we have

t_{k}+k\tau^{2}\leq t_{N}+N\tau^{2}={\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tau\sqrt{NK}\right)}+N\tau^{2}={\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tau\sqrt{NK}\right)}
.
Therefore,
\lVert E_{1}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\tau^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{N}(t_{k}+k\tau^{2})={\mathcal{O}\!\left(N\tau^{3}\sqrt{NK}\right)}
and

\lVert E_{2}\rVert_{\infty}\leq s_{N}+4\sum_{k=2}^{N}(t_{k}+k\tau^{2})^{2}\tau^{2}={\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tau^{3}K^{3/2}N^{3/2}\right)}
,
which implies
\lVert E_{1}-2E_{2}\rVert_{\infty}={\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tau^{3}K^{3/2}N^{3/2}\right)}
.

It remains to bound the remainder term. By Lemma
S10
,

\lVert{\cal R}_{N}\rVert_{\infty}={\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sum_{r=5,r\textrm{ is odd}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{k=2}^{N}\lVert\operatorname{ad}^{r-1}_{2S_{k-1}}(B_{k})\rVert_{\infty}\right)}.

(S134)

For an odd order
r\geq 5
, applying Lemma
S4
yields

\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{k=2}^{N}\lVert\operatorname{ad}^{r-1}_{2S_{k-1}}(B_{k})\rVert_{\infty}=\frac{2^{r}}{r!}{\mathcal{O}\!\left((\sqrt{NK}\tau)^{r}\right)}=o(\tau^{3}K^{3/2}N^{3/2}).

(S135)

Alternatively, the triangle inequality gives

\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{k=2}^{N}\lVert\operatorname{ad}^{r-1}_{2S_{k-1}}(B_{k})\rVert_{\infty}=\frac{2^{2r}}{r!}{\mathcal{O}\!\left(N\tau(\sqrt{NK}\tau)^{r-1}\right)}.

(S136)

For
r^{\star}=\left\lceil\frac{\log(K\tau^{2}\sqrt{NK})}{\log(\tau\sqrt{NK})}\right\rceil
, this implies
\lVert R_{N}\rVert_{\infty}={\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tau^{3}K^{3/2}N^{3/2}\right)}
.
We therefore apply Lemma
S4
for
r<r^{\star}
and use the triangle inequality for
r\geq r^{\star}
; the remaining tail (
r>r^{\star}
) decays exponentially and can be neglected at the stated precision.

Substituting these bounds into
\lVert\rho_{N}-G_{\beta,H}\rVert_{1}\leq 2\lVert E\rVert_{\infty}
yields

\textup{Pr}\!\left(\lVert\rho_{N}-G_{\beta,H}\rVert_{1}\leq{\mathcal{O}\!\left(K^{3/2}N^{3/2}\tau^{3}\right)}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

(S137)

It remains to account for the overall failure probability. The proof applies Lemma
S4

(r^{\star}-1)/2+N
times. Setting
\delta^{\prime}=\delta/((r^{\star}-1)/2+N)
and taking a union bound ensures that the total failure probability is at most
\delta
.
Under this rescaling,

K=(n+1)\log 2+\log\bigl(((r^{\star}-1)/2+N)/\delta\bigr)
.
Given
N\gg n
and
\tau\sqrt{NK}\ll 1
, we may assume
N\gg K
and obtain

\displaystyle r^{\star}\leq 2+\frac{\log(K\tau)}{\log(\tau\sqrt{NK})}\ll N,

(S138)

which implies

K\leq(n+2)\log 2+\log(N/\delta)\ll N
.
Taking
K_{0}=(n+2)\log 2+\log(N/\delta)
therefore yields the stated bound.

C.2
Sample distribution

Recall that Algorithm
1
generates a path
{\cal P}=\big((j_{1},m_{1}),\ldots,(j_{N},m_{N})\big)\in[L]\times\left\{{\kern 0.20828pt}1,-1{\kern 0.20828pt}\right\}
.
In Section
C.1
, we assumed
{\cal P}
is fixed so that the evolution sequence can be analyzed without randomness. This section uses a random walk to incorporate the randomness of
j_{k}
and
m_{k}
.

Consider how the sampled Hamiltonian evolves as the path develops. Stopping the algorithm at step
k\in[N]
yields the intermediate Hamiltonian
H^{(k)}=\sum_{t=1}^{k}m_{t}\sigma_{j_{t}}
. The sampling process can thus be described as a sequence
(H^{(k)})_{k}
, with
H^{(N)}
being the final output. Equivalently, this sequence is a random walk on a lattice:

~\mathbf{x}^{(k)}=\sum_{t=1}^{k}m_{t}e_{j_{t}}\in\left\{{\kern 0.20828pt}-N,\ldots,N{\kern 0.20828pt}\right\}^{\times L},\quad\mathbf{x}^{(0)}=0,

(S139)

where
e_{j_{t}}
is the standard vector in the lattice corresponding to
\sigma_{j_{t}}
,
and every forward step satisfies

\textup{Pr}\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}^{(k-1)}=\pm e_{j}\right)=\textup{Pr}\!\left(m_{k}=\pm 1\right)\cdot{h}_{j}/\lambda.

(S140)

The sign of
m_{k}
is determined by the quantum measurement in Theorem
S7
, which is difficult to analyze directly.
We therefore introduce a reference probability measure
Q
with
\textup{Pr}_{Q}\!\left(m_{k}=\pm 1\right)=1/2
, giving

~\textup{Pr}_{Q}\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}^{(k-1)}=\pm e_{j}\right)={h}_{j}/2\lambda

(S141)

corresponding to
N\to\infty
. The relation between the reference probability measure and the true probability measure is stated by the likelihood ratio
L_{N}({\cal P})=\textup{Pr}\!\left({\cal P}\right)/\textup{Pr}_{Q}\!\left({\cal P}\right)
in the following statement.

Lemma S13

If
N={\Omega\!\left(n\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}\right)}
, then for
H=H^{(N)}
,

\log L_{N}({\cal P})=\log\!\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{-\beta H}\right]\right)+{\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}}{N}\right)}.

(S142)

Proof.

For convenience, denote
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}
as the thermal state of Hamiltonian with respect to
\mathbf{x}
and
M_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\sigma_{j}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}\right]

By Theorem
S7
, the outcome distribution at step
t
given the Pauli operator
\sigma_{j_{t}}
can be written as

\textup{Pr}\!\left(m_{t}=\pm 1\mid j_{t}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1\mp\tanh\tau\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\sigma_{j_{t}}M_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-1)})\right]\Bigr)

(S143)

Since
\textup{Pr}_{Q}\!\left(m_{t}=\pm 1\mid j_{t}\right)=1/2
and the probabilities for sampling
j_{t}
for both measures are the same, the likelihood ratio takes the product form

L_{N}({\cal P})=2^{N}\prod_{t=1}^{N}\textup{Pr}\!\left(m_{t}=\pm 1\mid j_{t}\right)=\prod_{t=1}^{N}\Bigl(1-m_{t}\tanh(\tau)M_{j_{t}}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-1)})\Bigr).

(S144)

Using
\log(1+y)=y+{\mathcal{O}\!\left(y^{2}\right)}
and
\tanh\tau=\tau+{\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tau^{3}\right)}
gives

\log L_{N}(j,m)=-\tau\sum_{t=1}^{N}m_{t}M_{j_{t}}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-1)})+{\mathcal{O}\!\left(N\tau^{2}\right)}.

(S145)

Denote the parition function of
H^{(t)}
at the
t
-th step as

{\cal Z}(\mathbf{x}^{(t)})=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\exp\!\left(-\beta H^{(t)}\right)\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\exp\!\left(-\tau\sum_{j=1}^{L}\mathbf{x}^{(t)}_{j}\sigma_{j}\right)\right].

(S146)

Observe that differentiating
\log{\cal Z}(\mathbf{x})
with respect to
\mathbf{x}_{j}
gives

\partial_{\mathbf{x}_{j}}\log{\cal Z}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{{\cal Z}(\mathbf{x})}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\left(-\tau\sigma_{j}\right)\exp\!\left(-\tau\sum_{j=1}^{L}\mathbf{x}_{j}\sigma_{j}\right)\right]=-\tau M_{j}(\mathbf{x}).

(S147)

Therefore, since
\mathbf{x}^{(t)}-\mathbf{x}^{(t-1)}=m_{t}e_{j_{t}}
,

-\tau m_{t}M_{j_{t}}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-1)})=m_{t}\partial_{\mathbf{x}^{(t-1)}}\log{\cal Z}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-1)})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log{\cal Z}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-1)})\cdot\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(t)}-\mathbf{x}^{(t-1)}\bigr).

(S148)

Because
{\cal Z}(\mathbf{x})
depends on
\mathbf{x}
through
\tau\mathbf{x}
and
\tau\ll 1
, a Taylor
expansion of
\log{\cal Z}
along one lattice step gives

\log{\cal Z}(\mathbf{x}^{(t)})-\log{\cal Z}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-1)})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log{\cal Z}(\mathbf{x}^{(t-1)})\cdot\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(t)}-\mathbf{x}^{(t-1)}\bigr)+{\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tau^{2}\right)}.

(S149)

Summing over
t=1,\dots,N
and substituting into Equation (
S145
) yields

\log L_{N}(j,m)=\log{\cal Z}(\mathbf{x}^{(N)})-\log{\cal Z}(0)+{\mathcal{O}\!\left(N\tau^{2}\right)}.

(S150)

Finally,
{\cal Z}(0)=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[I\right]=2^{n}
and

{\cal Z}(\mathbf{x}^{(N)})=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\exp(-\tau\sum_{j}\mathbf{x}_{j}\sigma_{j})\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{-\beta H^{(N)}}\right]
, so
the statement follows from
N\tau^{2}=\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}/N
.

We now state the theoretical result for the sample distribution.

Theorem S14

(Complete version of Theorem
2
)

Suppose
n\gg 1
and
N={\Omega\!\left(n\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}\right)}
. Let
H=\sum_{j=1}^{L}c_{j}\sigma_{j}
and
p_{j}=c_{j}/\lambda
.
When
\sum_{j}c_{j}^{2}\leq N\lambda^{2}
, the datum
(H,G_{\beta,H})
can be sampled by Algorithm
1
with probability proportional to
\frac{1}{2^{n}}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{-\beta H}\right]\cdot D(\mathbf{x})
,
where
D
is given as

D(\mathbf{x})=\frac{a_{N}(\mathbf{x})}{(2\pi N)^{L/2}\sqrt{\prod_{j=1}^{L}p_{j}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{2}\right)+o\bigl(N^{-L/2}\bigr).

(S151)

Proof.

Fix
H=\sum_{j=1}^{L}c_{j}\sigma_{j}
on the output grid, and let

\mathbf{x}\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{L}
be the corresponding endpoint vector
\mathbf{x}_{j}=c_{j}N/\lambda
. Denote
{\cal P}_{\mathbf{x}}
the set of paths whose endpoint equals
\mathbf{x}
.
Then the event that Algorithm
1
returns
H
is exactly the
endpoint event
\mathbf{x}^{(N)}=\mathbf{x}
.

By definition of the likelihood ratio and Lemma
S13
,

\textup{Pr}\!\left({\cal P}\right)=\textup{Pr}_{Q}\!\left({\cal P}\right)\cdot\exp\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{-\beta H}\right]\right)+{\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}}{N}\right)}\right).

(S152)

When
\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert={\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sqrt{N}\right)}
. Therefore, absorbing the
\mathbf{x}
-independent normalization into the proportionality constant, summing over all
{\cal P}
with same endpoint, we obtain

\displaystyle\textup{Pr}\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{(N)}=\mathbf{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[e^{-\beta H}\right]\right)+{\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}}{N}\right)}\right)\cdot\sum_{{\cal P}\in{\cal P}_{\mathbf{x}}}\textup{Pr}_{Q}\!\left({\cal P}\right)

\displaystyle\propto\frac{1}{2^{n}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[e^{-\beta H}\right]\cdot\textup{Pr}_{Q}\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{(N)}=\mathbf{x}\right).

(S153)

Denote
D(\mathbf{x})=\textup{Pr}_{Q}\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{(N)}=\mathbf{x}\right)
. Substituting Lemma
S5
yields
the Gaussian approximation claimed in the theorem.

Appendix D
Experimental setting

This section provides the numerical settings used in each experiment. All experiments are performed on a
3\times 3
two-dimensional grid of
n=9
qubits, with sites labeled
1,\ldots,9
in row-major order. The nearest-neighbor pairs
\langle i,j\rangle
consist of
12
edges connecting horizontally and vertically adjacent sites. For the Heisenberg ensemble, the sampled Hamiltonians take the form

H_{\mathrm{Heis}}=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\left(c_{ij}^{(X)}X_{i}X_{j}+c_{ij}^{(Y)}Y_{i}Y_{j}+c_{ij}^{(Z)}Z_{i}Z_{j}\right),

(S154)

where the coefficients
c_{ij}^{(\alpha)}
are generated by the sampling algorithm and satisfy
|c_{ij}^{(\alpha)}|\leq h
for a uniform bound
h>0
. For the transverse-field Ising ensemble, the sampled Hamiltonians take the form

H_{\mathrm{Ising}}=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}c_{ij}Z_{i}Z_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}X_{i},

(S155)

where the coefficients
c_{ij}
and
g_{i}
are similarly bounded. In both cases,
\lambda=\sum_{j}h_{j}
denotes the sum of all coefficient bounds. Throughout, we set the step-number constant
C=\lambda^{2}/\epsilon_{0}^{2/3}
with target precision
\epsilon_{0}=10^{-6}
.

For Figure 2, all three panels use the Heisenberg ensemble with the maximally mixed state
I/d
as the initial state. In panel (a), we scan the inverse temperature from
\beta=1
to
\beta=10
and test three scaling exponents
k\in\left\{{\kern 0.20828pt}1.5,2.0,2.5{\kern 0.20828pt}\right\}
, which define the step number as
N=C\beta^{k}
. For each parameter pair
(\beta,N)
, we perform
100
independent sampling runs and record the maximal trace-distance error
\epsilon
among the samples. Panels (b) and (c) both fix
\beta=2
. In panel (b), we set
N=C\beta^{2}
and generate
10\,000
independent samples to obtain the empirical coefficient distribution; the theoretical curve is computed from the normal distribution predicted by Theorem 2, reweighted by the thermal partition function and estimated via Monte Carlo with
10\,000
samples. In panel (c), we scan the scaling exponent
k
from
1
to
3
and perform
100
independent runs for each
N=C\beta^{k}
. The inverse precision is defined as
1/\epsilon
, where
\epsilon
is the maximal trace-distance error among the samples. The effective sample range is quantified by the operator norm
\lVert H\rVert_{\infty}
of each sampled Hamiltonian; we report the sample mean and standard error over the ensemble.

For Figure 3, we use the transverse-field Ising ensemble to generate the output states. The initial state is a thermal state of a Heisenberg Hamiltonian, whose modular spectrum exhibits near-Poisson statistics in this finite-size setting. We fix
\beta=2
and
N=C\beta^{2}

20
