There are many excellent manuscripts in this issue. Catton et al [1] present sobering data regarding estimated per-hour income for arthroplasty surgeons early in practice. It highlights the importance of advocacy for our specialty in Washington for Medicare reimbursement and with commercial payers. There is a need for specialty-trained surgeons in arthroplasty, who are willing and able to provide complex and revision care, and the financial implications to the surgeon need to be considered.

Roth et al [2], using Michigan Arthroplasty Registry Quality Initiative data from 6 hospitals, report cementless total knee arthroplasty rose from 3.3% in 2017 to 17.1% in 2024, an over 5-fold increase. The cementless cohort was younger, with more comorbidities, but had a shorter length of stay and fewer readmissions than the cemented cohort. The value of registries in tracking the results of such a massive change in practice is critical, and Michigan Arthroplasty Registry Quality Initiative is to be congratulated on the public service its data collection and sharing provides.

Spinopelvic interaction and its influence on dislocation after total hip arthroplasty remains a hot topic of investigation. Oetojo et al [3] present data showing that while pelvic tilt changes following total hip arthroplasty, the degree to which it occurs is dependent upon the status of the contralateral hip, with the biggest change occurring in patients with a normal contralateral hip. Additional longitudinal studies on this topic are needed to further validate these findings and to help guide surgical planning and execution, to minimize dislocation risk.

Chung et al [4] report the failure of a proximal body of a modular fluted tapered stem, which has become the workhorse implant for periprosthetic fracture and revision total hip arthroplasty. It is an important lesson of the importance of proximal support to mitigate cantilever stress on the implant.

Finally, Hodo et al [5] report on successful salvage of a knee that had significant deformity and periarticular erosion and instability in a patient with neurofibromatosis 1, using a hinged implant. The article provides a detailed review of the condition and highlights the role of constraint in patients with neuromuscular conditions.

As always, I am incredibly grateful for the opportunity to serve as Editor-in-Chief and could not possibly fulfill the responsibilities of managing the journal without the help of Taylor Bowen, our Associate Editors, Editorial Board, and the staff at American Association of Hip and Knee Surgeons and Elsevier.

See you in Dallas!
