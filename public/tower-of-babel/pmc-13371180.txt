Belonging to self: Flint women meeting needs, securing safety and finding voice
Authors: Maji Hailemariam, Tatiana E Bustos, Bethel Ataro, Cindy Nguyen, Bernadel L Jefferson, Sharon Saddler, Bryan Spencer, Raven Miller, Fallon Richie, Garrett Brown, Jonne McCoy White, Jennifer E Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBelonging is a fundamental human need that influences health and well-being. Women’s ability to belong to themselves is affected by structural disinvestment, environmental injustice, poverty and chronic precarity. Guided by the belongingness framework, this study explores solutions of women in the Flint area to increase “belonging to self,” which encompasses basic needs, services, safety and autonomy.MethodsUsing a community-based participatory research approach, we interviewed 100 women and community representatives. In-depth interviews (Fall 2017–Spring 2018) explored strengths, needs, aspirations, and solutions. A team of community-academic pairs analyzed the data.ResultsFour interrelated themes emerged: Basic Needs, Services, Safety, and Autonomy. (1) Basic Needs included gaps in food, water, and housing, disproportionately affecting women with mental health or substance use challenges, domestic violence survivors, and pregnant women. (2) Services revealed deficits in childcare, maternal support, education, employment, healthcare, and transportation, often compounded by distrust and bureaucratic barriers. (3) Safety encompassed physical and emotional vulnerabilities limiting daily life. (4) Autonomy reflected the interplay of personal agency, relational dynamics, and structural constraints. Across themes, participants proposed community-rooted, trauma-informed solutions—including 24-hour childcare, mentorship, community gardens, skill-building, and peer support—to enhance well-being, equity, and women’s ability to belong to themselves.ConclusionComplex and interconnected factors shape women’s experiences of belonging to oneself. Themes of basic needs, access to services, safety, and autonomy can frame pathways to health and wellbeing for women in challenging contexts. Despite limited access to opportunity, women shared collective strategies for survival, support, and reimagining their futures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04543-7.Keywords : Belonging, Women, Flint, Autonomy, Safety, Women's health, Wellbeing

Background

The desire to belong—to oneself, to others, and to a cause greater than oneself, is a fundamental human need. Understanding this human desire can serve as a powerful tool to explaining various health, social and environmental justice concerns [1, 2]. Experiences of belonging are often shaped by one’s ability to access resources, having a sense of safety, involvement in meaningful relationships, and the ability to exercise personal autonomy [3–5]. Women living in pockets of poverty face multifaceted challenges across individual, interpersonal and structural domains [6]. These challenges impede their access to resources, compromise health and well-being, and constrain opportunities to pursue a meaningful life. These barriers are particularly pronounced in post-industrial cities such as Flint, Michigan, where decades of structural disinvestment, environmental neglect, and racialized poverty have produced a landscape of chronic precarity. Women, especially mothers and caregivers, are disproportionately affected by these adversities [7, 8].

Flint, Michigan presents a case example of cumulative disadvantage as well as unique assets and community resourcefulness. The city has experienced prolonged economic decline following the collapse of the automotive industry, leading to unemployment, housing insecurity, depopulation, and deteriorating infrastructure. Nearly 40% of women and single mothers live below the federal poverty line, a figure substantially higher than both state and national averages. A majority of Flint’s residents live in areas characterized as “food deserts” [9]. Women in Flint also experience limited educational attainment and unstable, often precarious employment opportunities characterized by low wages, few benefits, and instability [10]. These and other intersectional experiences of structural violence pose challenges to women’s physical, emotional, and economic well-being.

Flint also has unique and longstanding assets, including a legacy of grassroots activism and collective strength, exemplified by the 1936–1937 sit-down strikes that gave rise to the United Auto Workers union [11, 12]. This tradition of organizing has since extended to public health, environmental justice, and education, underpinning the city’s robust infrastructure for community-engaged research and action [13, 14]. For example, Flint is widely recognized for its rich history of Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR) and is home to the nation’s only academic department co-developed and co-led with community members—an institutional model rooted in shared governance and mutual accountability. Several women in Flint are also the first community leaders of national organizations. For example, the first non-academic and non-physician president of the American Public Health Association is from Flint. The city’s existing assets are grounded in the strength and resourcefulness of its residents, providing a sustainable foundation for building community engagement efforts for impact.

Alongside experiences of poverty, violence, and resource constraints, Flint women and girls engage in everyday acts of innovation, survival, caregiving, mutual support, and community-building. Our previous study on assets of Flint area women identified strength, resourcefulness, creativity, determination, tenacity, wisdom and the ability to nurture others as assets that can be leveraged to respond to needs [3].

The Flint Women’s Study, conducted by Michigan State University researchers and Flint-area residents [14], utilized CBPR to identify the lived experiences, needs, hopes, dreams, and proposed solutions of women across Genesee County. Drawing on principles of intersectionality [15, 16], the study engaged women from diverse backgrounds, including young women, mothers, older adults, LGBTQIA+ individuals, and women with disabilities. The intersecting marginalized identities of women in Flint — including race, ethnicity, class, disability, and sexual and gender identity — and their compounding effects on structural violence and health have been described in detail elsewhere [15]. The parent study aimed to explore the individual, interpersonal and structural determinants that shape women’s health and well-being and women’s ideas for improving them.

Nested within the Flint Women’s Study, the current study adopts the belongingness framework encompassing belonging to self, belonging to others, and belonging to something greater than self [17]. Prior work using this dataset examined “belonging to something greater than self” dimension [18]. The current study focuses on the “belonging to self” dimension of the belongingness framework to understand how women in Flint conceptualize individual level factors affecting their health and wellbeing.

In this study, belonging to self refers to the pursuit of personal agency and self-worth, shaped by the structural conditions in which they live. Poverty, unmet basic needs, neighborhood safety concerns and inaccessible services, undermine women’s capacity to claim a sense of self-worth and agency. In this way, social and environmental determinants of health also function as determinants of belonging to self. Conversely, when women access resources, exercise agency, and contribute to their communities, belonging to self is cultivated, even within contexts of chronic precarity [1, 3, 18]. Using qualitative data from in-depth interviews with women in Flint, this paper examined how the pursuit of “belonging to self” unfolds. By centering women’s voices, we contribute to ongoing scholarship on the social and structural determinants of health and offer insights into how a sense of belonging to self is both constrained and cultivated within a context marked by both disinvestment and resourcefulness.

Methods

Study design

The current study is a sub-study of the broader Flint Women’s Study that identified the strengths, needs, hopes, dreams and solutions of 100 women and/or people serving women in the area of Flint, Michigan [14]. The study followed a community-partnered design whereby community members were actively involved in all phases of the research process, including the design, data collection, analysis, and dissemination of findings, with the aim of producing actionable insights. Grounded on theories of belongingness [17], the study applied qualitative research methodology with CBPR principles. More details on the study design rationale have been published in prior work [14, 18]. Power dynamics between academic and community researchers were managed through strategies consistent with CBPR principles, described in detail in our published methods paper [14]. Briefly, community partners co-created the interview guide, served as co-interviewers in the initial 5–7 interviews, led participant recruitment, co-analyzed data, and contributed to manuscript writing. Limited funding precluded compensating community partners for co-interviewing across all 100 interviews.

Sampling

The sample included communities with direct lived experiences and understanding, including women living in Flint (93%) and/or community representatives serving women in Flint, Michigan (including pastors, volunteers, health and social service providers; about 87% were both residents and served other women in Flint. Eligibility criteria included: (1) be 18 or older, and (2) identified as a woman living in the Flint area who wanted to voice their perspectives, and/or (3) identified as a community member or someone serving women in Flint. Purposive and snowball sampling procedures were used for recruitment and to leverage community assets through personal networks for broader reach of women. The research team also conducted outreach through local community-based organizations by distributing flyers and other recruitment materials in public places that were trusted sources (e.g., faith-based agencies, social service agencies, libraries, or other residential areas).

Data collection

Data were collected using semi-structured interviews from 27 June 2017 to 28 March 2018. A semi-structured interview protocol was co-developed with community partners. Open-ended questions explored the strengths, needs, and aspirations of women as well as any solutions to improve their overall well-being (see supplemental file 1). Interviews were conducted face-face by experienced qualitative researchers (JJ; female, PhD in clinical psychology and MH; female, PhD in mental health epidemiology) at a time and location that was convenient for all participants. Interviews were audio recorded. Community partners were also involved as co-interviewers, when possible. Each interview lasted from 60 to 90 min.

Data analysis

Audio files were transcribed verbatim and then reviewed for quality assurance. All transcripts were de-identified and assigned unique IDs for confidentiality. Once quality checks of transcripts were completed, all files were uploaded to NVivo to prepare for data analysis [19]. The coding team included five community and academic partner pairs. Following CBPR principles, we intentionally included community members who lived and worked in Flint or Genesee County to leverage cultural context and expertise that would make the findings more meaningful to Flint women. A qualitative coding training was developed to train community partners in qualitative coding. The training included an introduction to NVivo 12 qualitative analysis software [19] and qualitative coding techniques with practice exercises. After completing the training, each pair was assigned a random set of transcripts to code independently and then discussed any discrepancies in team meetings to work toward consensus. All transcripts were double-coded. To ensure rigor and trustworthiness, we addressed four key criteria. Credibility was established through member checking. Results were presented back to all participants who were invited to review the study results. Dependability and confirmability were ensured through double coding by community and academic pairs, with discrepancies resolved through consensus in team meetings. Finally, transferability was supported by detailed descriptions of our diverse sample and study context [14].

Data saturation

Theoretical saturation [20] was achieved through an iterative process with a large sample. After approximately 50 interviews, a mid-sample audit identified gaps in emerging themes, prompting targeted recruitment of underrepresented groups [14]. Saturation was considered reached when key themes were conceptually stable and no new insights emerged from subsequent interviews.

Positionality statement

The research team reflects diverse positionalities that inform this work. MH, a Black immigrant woman with a PhD in mental health epidemiology and MSW, and JJ, a white woman with a PhD in clinical psychology, conducted interviews. As women living and working in Flint, they bring community-informed perspectives while attending to their power and privilege as researchers through collaborative and reflexive approaches. Community co-analysts—including Black women, a Black man, and long-standing Flint residents—brought lived expertise to data analysis, each paired with an academic partner. Their perspectives strengthened interpretation and enhanced the authenticity of findings. Additional team members reflected a range of positionalities, including a Latina daughter of immigrants, a Black woman and physician, an Asian American medical student, and researchers with deep roots in Flint and Genesee County. Across the team, there was a shared commitment to power-sharing and reflexivity, recognizing how lived experience and professional training shape the research process.

Framework analysis

De-identified transcripts were qualitatively coded using framework analysis and the belongingness framework [21]. Framework analysis applies thematic analysis techniques and is particularly effective for managing large datasets with interdisciplinary teams [22]. The belonginess framework includes multi-level dimensions that encompass (1) belonging to self; (2) belonging to others, and (3) belonging to something greater than self. The Belongingness Framework was selected by academic partners following data collection as a structuring tool to manage a large qualitative dataset. Community partners reviewed and approved the framework prior to coding. While the framework shaped how data were organized and meaning was made, its post-hoc application to existing data preserved the integrity of participants’ narratives during collection. The coding team charted data into an NVivo file that pre-populated the dimensions of the belongingness framework for coding; each dimension included smaller codes that related to the broader dimension. Focusing on the first dimension—belonging to self—the coding team identified major themes related to basic needs, access to and quality of services, safety and autonomy. Themes were identified if found salient within and across responses. A detailed description of the coding framework is published elsewhere [14]. In the current study, we present results only on the dimension related to belonging to self.

Results

A total of 100 interviews were conducted for the study. Over half of participants identified as African-American (53%), 41% identified as white and 7% identified as other race and ethnicities. Participant age also ranged from 18 to 84 years old (M = 47.95, SD = 15.25). We report themes under the “belonging to self” dimension of the belongingness framework. Four themes emerged, including (1) basic needs; (2) services; (3) safety and (4) autonomy.

Basic needs

Access to basic necessities emerged as foundational to belonging to self. Participants described how survival demands consumed daily life, leaving little room for the pursuit of personal agency and self-worth.

Nutrition Equity

Access to food, potable water, housing and other needs were among some of the most recurrent issues raised by our participants. Concerns related to access to food were linked to depopulation. Participants described how Flint experienced food deserts due to the out migration of businesses. Individuals with health vulnerabilities including pregnant women, the elderly, moms with young children, and disabled women were reported to experience greater challenges due to lack of access to food and clean water.

Nutritious food and water is a definite huge need right now. We actually have a client right now that is pregnant and she’s drinking bottled water because we can’t guarantee that the water is safe for her and her unborn child to drink. Social Worker, age 35.

Speaking on the vulnerability of pregnant women, another participant also described the importance of accessing healthier food options:

I know a lot of people—like, statistically, our maternal death rate is really high in America. I know that it’s higher in poorer areas. I think that access to more than it just prenatal vitamins is important, access to healthier foods, access to cooking classes, access to readily made food that is healthy and affordable… Pet groomer, age 22.

According to some of the participants, even when food is available, it is not affordable or mostly unhealthy.

. ...access to healthy food is a challenge. I think because healthy food is expensive, and it’s hard to make ends meet, so women have to choose. Also, the healthiest stuff also takes a longer time to prepare often, and doesn’t have the same shelf-life. Neighborhood group president, age 38.

Participants also reported a high prevalence of chronic illnesses in the community due to poor nutritional practices and lifestyle factors as indicated below:

I see a lot of women who are coming in my courtroom who may be my age—or, like, 20 years younger than me—and look 30 years older than me because of poor, untreated, let’s say, health issues that are related to diet and lifestyle: diabetes, high blood pressure, those kinds of things, that really are limiting. The health of people here is terrible compared to other places you go. University professor, age 63.

Proposed solutions

In response to these challenges, participants proposed community-driven, family-oriented strategies that involve them in the planning to develop initiatives around nutrition, food and water access that work. Encouraging local food growing initiatives, such as neighborhood gardens, was seen as an effective way to shift attitudes toward healthy eating. One participant described how involving children in gardening made them more curious and invested in the food they ate:

I think that is making them more likely to eat it. They’re proud of what they grow. They’re more interested in the process. There’s a lot of good that can be done in changing a child’s mindset because children have a lot of sway with their parents. I think a lot of the cycle breaking can happen when children are pretty young, but it needs to be family oriented and not just aimed at the child. Community health volunteer, 28.

These early experiences were said to have a ripple effect, influencing not just children but also their families, and potentially breaking intergenerational cycles of poor nutrition. Women also learned ways to address some of their needs and taught other women to do so. For example, a lack of access to grocery stores often meant a lack of access to feminine hygiene products. One participant sewed cloth pads for her neighbors and taught them how to make their own pads. Maker spaces in Flint enabled some interviewees to teach other women to make basic necessities like boots and mittens.

Housing

Respondents reported that women who are vulnerable were especially hard hit by lack of housing. Women with children and those fleeing a domestic violence situation were said to experience particular challenges accessing homeless shelters, compounded by the high levels of gun violence in parts of the city.

As far as affordable housing, I see a lot of it. We have women shelters, however, there’s a struggle. There’s always a struggle for homeless women, especially with children, getting into the shelters. Social worker, age 43.

Participants described other women trapped in unsafe conditions, waiting for help while facing fear and insecurity. However, resources for such women were said to be few with existing shelters often full, making lack of available resources one of the biggest barriers to housing. One participant noted how there aren’t enough housing options to support the women that need them:

There are more women that need subsidized housing than there is subsidized housing. Minister at church, age 55.

Participants shared that many women, particularly those who are pregnant or have children, find themselves living in their cars while they await housing assistance. Even when available, houses are not accessible to those who need mobility support:

The homes aren’t equipped for medical needs. Some of the people that I have—I’ve got a couple right now that are awaiting placement. They need specific—like maybe, they need one with a wheelchair ramp, or they need one story. A lot of them are two-story houses, and they can’t climb up the stairs, or they need somebody to give them their medicine, or they need help, maybe, with bathing. There’s just some more medical needs there, and these houses aren’t equipped for it. Social worker, age 32.

Mental health was also another barrier to access housing in the area due to the severity of mental health symptoms and stigma. Participants noted how lack of accommodations in housing people with mental illness can leave them without stability:

Mentally ill people are the hardest people to find housing for. They are not stable, so they don’t maintain, necessarily, a household. Some of them are violent, or they just wander off. They need to have stopgaps in place, like that wellness coach that goes out to check on them to make sure. … In the meantime, they put themselves in really precarious situations. Then, we have no place for them to be. CEO of a shelter, age 40.

Some of the participants working in clinical settings said that families experience exceptional challenges when they seek housing assistance. Unhoused families and couples were often forced to separate when seeking shelter, as most facilities house individuals by gender. As a result, many choose to stay on the streets or in abandoned buildings to remain together.

If you were homeless and you are a family, you cannot go anywhere together. The husband has to go to one shelter and the wife has to go to another. These families choose to live on the street. Recovery worker, age 43.

Proposed solutions

To address these needs, participants suggested increasing awareness about how to access existing housing resources and services in the area and developing more safe, affordable housing options for women and children. Another group of participants suggested the provision of wrap around services through shelters to promote independence. These include job-training, childcare and education. To help individuals with mental illness with housing, participants recommended having a navigator or a caseworker assisting them with housing related challenges including tracking and paying for utilities and ensuring that the person is not unhoused. Participants also praised the self-initiated help seeking behavior of women to utilize public programs to address housing insecurities:

A lot of people around here,—they know who to call. They know that there’s options. They’re not just sitting there worrying about what they’re gonna do. They’re walking up to the agencies they know that can service them. They’re calling 211, which is a great resource for people in this county. I tell everybody, if you ever need anything, call 211….Community volunteer, age 35.

Services

Participants consistently described service gaps across childcare, transportation, education, employment, and healthcare access. Their narratives revealed that inaccessible, dehumanizing, or bureaucratically burdensome systems did more than leave needs unmet — they communicated to women that their needs were unworthy of investment, directly undermining their sense of belonging to self. These interconnected issues were described as structural and persistent, often reinforcing cycles of poverty and instability.

Childcare and support for mothers

Access to affordable, quality childcare was repeatedly named as one of the most urgent and unmet needs. Participants highlighted the stringent eligibility requirements for public childcare subsidies. One woman said that to qualify for childcare assistance through the Department of Health Services, one has to be nearly homeless, have a serious mental health condition, or live in extreme poverty. Another participant added that even when assistance is provided, the financial support is insufficient:

Childcare is just extremely too high… We need to go back to it bein’ a village because that really helped mothers… They need that village. Moms of young children – they need quality daycares at affordable prices, and the state of Michigan is almost a joke. If daycare is $640, and you give a mom makin’ minimum wage $220 a month, what’s that gonna do? They need affordable daycare. They actually need funds that are there to really help them.” Social worker, age 30.

Lack of childcare impacted nearly every aspect of women’s lives—from the ability to attend medical appointments alone to engaging in work or education. One participant noted the inaccessibility of supposedly public spaces and lack of accommodations for children:

What are you supposed to do with your kid if you don’t have a place for that kid to go? Not everybody qualifies for child care assistance, or not everybody has someone, so what are you supposed to do if you’re supposed to be carrying on your life, making these appointments, or even going back to work. So many work places are not child friendly… X pediatrician doesn’t have books, like kid stuff there, which is weird…There are places that are supposed to be for kids, and I see they don’t have the plug covers on the outlets.” Post Partum Support Group Member, age 39.

Access to childcare resources is also influenced by safety concerns in intimate partnerships and family dynamics. Some women who had experienced sexual abuse as children were afraid to leave their children with others. In addition, participants explained that women carry the burden of caring. This lopsided childcare responsibility is apparent especially when the partner is violent or is not available.

Proposed Solutions

Participants proposed a range of potential solutions, including 24-hour childcare, on-site childcare services at workplaces, and better paying childcare jobs for local women, aligning with the need for expanded support network to address service gaps. Other participants also called for higher quality childcare and support services that equip women for success, such as peer support groups and counseling, reminders about good parenting skills, among other basic parenting programs (see Table 1).

Education to obtain employment

Education

Education emerged as both an individual and a collective lever for upward mobility. Participants framed access to education as crucial for preventing cycles of poverty, early pregnancy, and systemic injustice. In response to a question about women’s greatest need, one woman reflected,

My personal opinion is education because without education, the same cycle of poverty and injustice continues. I say that because I was in the system as a child. Now I’m on the other side of that. I wished growing up that education would have been more instilled, more talked about, more readily available so that I don’t make the same mistakes my parents did. Community volunteer, age 33.

Participants also noted the psychological impacts of lacking educational access on both confidence and opportunity, obscuring the power of women to believe that their situations can change.

When they look at people who are wealthy, or they look at people who can speak better than them, whatever the case may be, it’s always in view of my own self-worth. I don’t have a job because I didn’t go to school, because I’m dumb and I couldn’t go to school. They don’t realize that because of the neighborhood that they lived in, they had poor quality teachers and higher dropout rates and that’s the reason why they didn’t make it. I’ve yet to meet a woman in Flint who wasn’t incredibly sharp and incredibly dangerous If you tried to talk her into a corner, she can argue her way out of everything because she’s had to. Child Protective Services (CPS) worker, age 33.

Older women broadened this discussion by connecting it to future generations, where some older women expressed the need to help young mothers better understand the value of education for supporting their children’s futures.

Proposed solutions

Free or subsidized college, mentoring programs, and access to safe creative outlets were named as mechanisms for engagement in education and prevention programs for healthy relationships and community wellness. One participant underlined the role of education in mobilizing women’s purpose and abilities:

“Once you have a purpose that helps you with your intent and your choices… you’re gonna make those better choices—further your education… to be self-sustaining.” CEO, Age 36.

Other participants emphasized how education can help with self-care, proper diet and nutrition, exercise, and mindfulness. They highlighted education as an important driver of access to mental health care and reducing stigma that may hinder women from reaching their optimal well-being. They also suggested financial literacy, such as teaching women how to budget, as a solution to address education needs that may help them navigate challenges.

Employment

Job insecurity and low wages were cited as significant barriers to meeting basic needs. Inadequate compensation further limited access to childcare, healthy food, and transportation. Participants reported holding multiple part-time jobs without benefits and facing unequal pay due to gender.

“You don’t even know that there’s unfair practices until you just happen to run across it… You see that in the media now, where some women were paid less—some women actresses were paid less than some men actors, and they would’ve never known. They just happened to find out about it. Women and men are still looked at as unequal counterparts, and we’re treated differently.” Social Worker, Age 47.

Proposed solutions

Several participants emphasized the need for quality jobs with livable wages as foundational to family stability. Employment, education, and childcare were understood as deeply linked. Mentorship was raised as a strategy to broaden horizons, particularly for teens. Exposure to possibilities outside Flint was seen as key to breaking cycles of poverty and violence.

Health and mental health care

Barriers to physical and mental healthcare for women and their children were prominent, with transportation as a complicating factor interfering with access to care. Several participants described receiving referrals to specialists in a city 60 miles away —a challenge without reliable transportation or a car. Mental health, particularly trauma, disabilities, and postpartum depression, was raised repeatedly as a prevalent experience across Flint.

I would definitely say PTSD specifically childhood trauma is huge, sexual assault is huge especially in the lower income neighborhoods. It’s not uncommon when I had CPS cases that they were second and third generations who were getting a CPS case. Their mom had CPS and their grandparents had CPS, and a long, long history of being in and out of foster care, being abused by their partners, abused by the people that are supposed to care for them. CPS worker, age 33.

Another participant also emphasized how current behavior is shaped by traumatic childhood experiences:

I still have ways that I react to things that are wholly inappropriate and definitely informed by a traumatic childhood, but we really try to minimize the impact of that on our child. I also now have the benefit of working among early childhood educators. Postpartum doula and volunteer, age 28.

Despite the great need, participants described a lack of affordable, culturally competent providers in Flint. When services were available, quality often varied by insurance status with even more barriers to navigating complicated health systems to receive support for care. One participant further stated,

There’s so much that Medicare, and Medicaid require of these people that they can’t do, and there’s so much red tape, and policy that you have to follow that is unrealistic. Somebody needs help. Somebody needs healthcare. If somebody has a stroke, and they can’t walk, you would think that their insurance would pay for assistance, and they don’t. Social worker, age 32.

Proposed solutions

Many participants emphasized the need for removing obstacles and providing more access to resources and facilities that help women with holistic services (e.g., a one-stop shop) specialized in women’s health that extends into housing and other resources. Other solutions focused on the need to reevaluate mental health unique to women by creating more outlets for women to share their stories and broaden understanding on what kind of services would work.

Transportation

Transportation was a cross-cutting barrier affecting access to other community services and resources. Inadequate public transit, safety concerns, and stigma were cited as reasons why many avoid the bus system. “There are a lot of fears, and I think many legitimate, of theft and aggression while on the bus,” one participant explained. Limited access to phones further complicated transportation logistics for medical care.

Proposed solutions

While some services such as Ride to Wellness—a comprehensive non-emergency medical transportation program—were noted as helpful, participants expressed the need for safer, more accessible transit and walkable infrastructure. Biking and walking were suggested not only for mobility but as means of exercise and community connection.

Safety

Participants identified safety as foundational to belonging to self and among the community’s most urgent and complex needs. Their accounts revealed that environments marked by physical and emotional vulnerability directly eroded women’s capacity to exercise agency and develop a sense of self-worth. Safety, as participants described it, encompassed physical, psychological, and structural dimensions.

Physical safety

Across the interviews, physical safety emerged as a major concern for women in Flint. Physical safety concerns were pervasive, with many participants describing environments marked by community violence, gunfire, drug activity, and the lingering threat of human trafficking. Participants expressed anxiety about the safety of their children, especially sons and male partners, citing racial profiling, police brutality, and street violence as daily realities. Women reported feeling unsafe using public transportation due to the high likelihood of harassment and kidnapping. Participants also noted that women sometimes make dangerous trade-offs, such as relying on transactional relationships for rides or protection from violence.

Interviewer: You think the women are getting used?

Interviewee: Yes, I do. I do. I think there’s a lot of men—that’s a good way to put it. I think there’s a lot of men who aren’t working. I think there’s a lot of men who are relying on women and children, mothers, for housing, for things like that, shelter.

Interviewer: Why do the women put up with it, or accept it?

Interviewee: Protection from violence. Access screener, age 43.

Threat of violence was apparent throughout the community, regardless of age groups.

All of a sudden you have alarms going off on top of gunshots and police. All of these kids who’ve been—they’re in residential because they’ve been severely traumatized and sexually abused. Now you have one maybe if you’re lucky, two staff members trying to calm down seven to eight kids because of this and that’s insanity to me. Social worker, age 43.

Feelings of vulnerability were also by a lack of trust in law enforcement and emergency systems, leaving many women to feel that they could not rely on formal institutions for protection.

I think a lot of it is the police don’t come. I had a client a couple years ago who—her home was being broken into. They were able to scare away the intruder and the police didn’t come for four days to do fingerprints or do even an investigation of any kind. She felt let down that nobody was gonna come and protect her from whoever was trying to get in. Community member, age 35.

Stability

Stability was frequently discussed as a foundational component of safety. Participants expressed that most women lack stable housing, income, and educational opportunities.

… I think because of the chaos of poverty and then the transient residents cuz a lot of people are functionally homeless even though they’re living with a roof over their head, but they’re living with this friend or that friend. There’s, I think what I mean by the chaos of poverty is really the lack of constancy. Then the high schools have a low graduation rate. Medical doctor, age 55.

They hoped to create a secure environment for their children and themselves, emphasizing the importance of support systems, whether from partners, family, or the broader community.

… most women just want to live comfortably, without fear for their life, without fear for their children, or for their health. They wanna be able to make a living and provide for their families. I think if you asked a lot of them, they wouldn’t want anything extravagant, they just want to not, every day, be such a struggle. Social worker, age 32.

Proposed solutions

To address the above safety concerns, the recommendations our participants provided include the development of age-appropriate and structured summer programs that engage youth in positive ways, such as paid summer jobs. Participants also talked about the need for recentering the world to value young women and girls and to build their self-confidence and sense of autonomy to protect them from trafficking. Participants suggested that this could be achieved through mentoring, school and afterschool programs that focus on girls to provide them with creative and artistic avenues for growth and self-expression. Almost all participants suggested the need for clean and secure public places. They said that these will reduce the burden of chronic illness by promoting healthy physical activity.

One of the main proposed solutions for safety was to value and support girls and provide them with ample opportunities to learn, grow, and realize their potential.

These pimps promise the girls the world. They say, ‘I’m the only one that can keep you safe, I’m the only one that will provide for you, I’m the only one that cares about you, I’m the only one that loves you,’ and they’re actually right. We wanna create a community where the pimps aren’t right. What we have to do is we have to value our girls, and we have to give them opportunity, and we have to build them up, and we have to make sure that they’re not going without, so that they don’t need what the pimp is initially selling them. That’s what I would, first and foremost. Program coordinator, age 43.

Participants also recommended fostering trusting relationships with law enforcement and emergency systems. They also suggested creation of clean and secure public places and recreational areas for children and families.

Autonomy

Autonomy emerged as another central dimension of belonging to self. Participants described autonomy not simply as freedom of choice, but as the capacity to make purposeful, informed decisions and exercise personal agency. Through their accounts, it became clear that belonging to self requires not only safety and access to resources, but an internalized sense of competence, worth, and self-determination that structural conditions had systematically constrained. For women in Flint, however, this dimension of belonging was undermined by the absence of foundational conditions needed to claim it, including life skills, role models, and economic opportunities. Participants described growing up without guidance on budgeting, grocery shopping, cooking, or household management, leaving them without the frameworks for empowered decision-making that belonging to self requires. They emphasized that autonomy is not simply freedom but competence—the ability to manage time, resources, and responsibilities in ways that affirm dignity and enable social mobility.

Foundational life skills

Participants described needs in foundational life skills to build self-confidence to navigate relationships and promote their sense of autonomy. Women shared stories about navigating intimate relationships and parenting in contexts where guidance and positive modeling were often absent.

I think parenting teens can be hard on your self-esteem. Just every bit of bad you feel about yourself your teenagers amplify and can make you feel even worse. I think moms can struggle a lot more with feeling competent and like they’re meaningful. Social worker, age 49.

Participants endorsed that most women lacked foundational life skills like budgeting or household management, often because such skills were never modeled for them. Other participants also discussed self-love and self-worth as variables that could enhance women’s sense of autonomy:

It’s like when you know your self-worth, you quit givin’ people discounts, right? A lot of them really don’t. They don’t see the value that they have. They just see they need to do this or they need to do that. They don’t give themselves credit for how far they’ve come, the things that they’ve gone through and overcame. They only see the road that still has to be walked or ran or whatever the case may be. Community health advocate, age 42.

The absence of maternal, sibling, or communal support left many without frameworks for making empowered choices in their relationships. For some, this meant struggling to break cycles of unhealthy partnerships, while for others it meant shouldering the emotional labor of raising children without tools for modeling respectful or safe dynamics.

Structural barriers

Structural barriers also played a significant role in shaping the conditions for women’s autonomy. Participants pointed to the absence of clear pathways to social and economic mobility. Specifically, one participant described the “benefits cliff” where a small increase in income renders them ineligible for different social services.

There are those people in those gaps. They can’t get help. They’re just out there. I’m working, and that knocks me off the box for maybe food stamps and childcare. But I’m not makin’ enough. I’m not makin’ enough to pay my own childcare. Then a lot of times, I see those ladies just give up. They’ll just go back to not even workin’, and just to meet the guidelines of the different DHS programs. or they go underground and do the things that are not lawful to do to be able to do or get what they need. Community member, age 57.

Proposed solutions

Participants also provided ways to address concerns related to women’s autonomy. Women described creating grassroots spaces to share knowledge, support one another, and model new ways of living.

Self-resilience? Self-confidence? A lot of our women come from an abusive situation that really tears down your self-confidence, so having, maybe, a mentor in their life or someone to give them that spark of, “You can do it. You have value.” Community member, age 44.

To assist women gain their autonomy, participants recommended activities including organizing social events, initiating peer education projects, and reclaiming parenting through classes and collective reflection about good parenting. Promoting access to vocational training, financial literacy, or alternative education programs that can also help broaden their opportunities. Others described helping youth imagine alternate futures, challenging narratives of inevitability around poverty, early pregnancy, or failure by exposing them to worlds beyond their immediate neighborhoods as well as career opportunities, including STEM fields or study abroad programs for inspiration. Discussion

This study explored needs and proposed solutions of women in Flint using the belongingness framework. As part of the belonging to self dimension, the study presented qualitative results from 100 participants describing how Flint women describe their basic needs, access to services, sense of safety and autonomy in an environment marked by systemic disinvestment, poverty, systemic mistrust and other structural barriers. In this study, the concept of belonging to self is not a simple psychological achievement, rather a collective experience shaped by shared realities. Structural disinvestment does not only deprive women of material resources. Instead, it communicates that women’s lives are expendable, their needs unworthy of investment, and their voices inconsequential [23, 24]. Although the findings reflect place-specific realities of women in Flint, results are consistent with patterns documented in other minority-majority and post-industrial U.S. communities, particularly across the Rust Belt [25, 26]. Similar to cities in the US Rust Belt, economic decline, racial segregation, and subsequent poverty have produced neighborhood conditions that undermine health, safety, and well-being. However, Flint women’s narratives also offer a rich account of resourcefulness, collective care, and grounded recommendations to inform policies and programs [3].

Inadequate access to healthy food and affordable and safe housing were recurring issues for women in Flint. In the areas of basic needs, women proposed expanding access to fresh food through community markets, developing initiatives engaged with women to find effective ways to shift attitudes about nutrition. Moreover, introducing wrap around programs that could offer women comprehensive services for their unique needs in housing, food, job development, childcare, and education may support women’s dignity and sense of belonging to self. Introducing small-scale, neighborhood-based healthy food interventions in areas where large retailers have disappeared has been an effective strategy to address this need and may offer a solution for nutrition equity [9]. At policy level, expanding WIC and SNAP eligibility and improving nutritional quality of these programs is apparent [27, 28]. To increase access and engagement, healthcare and social service systems and policymakers should routinely audit service utilization to identify marginalized groups and develop targeted programs, such as outreach, navigation support, or culturally tailored care. Addressing these gaps is therefore not only critical for meeting material needs but also for creating the conditions under which belonging to self can be sustained.

Gaps in services echo findings from a previous study that highlighted how intersecting identities (e.g., young age, race, disability, sexual and gender identities) compound poor access to essential supports in childcare, education, employment, health and mental health care, and transportation [15]. To address gaps in service systems, many participants proposed solutions to build and apply trauma-informed systems that center women’s lived experiences and span across sectors for holistic services. At the systems level, policymakers and healthcare administrators should streamline eligibility requirements for public insurance programs, expand reimbursement for mental health providers, and invest in non-emergency medical transportation infrastructure to reduce structural barriers to care access.

Concerns about neighborhood safety—related to crime, drug use, and physical deterioration—were tied to women’s access to safe walking places. Prior research in Flint has linked neighborhood vacancy to poor mental health outcomes [29] and identified community greening as a promising strategy to reduce crime [30]. Land Bank’s Clean & Green program and other related policy responses can help reduce blight and restore women’s sense of belongingness and pride in their community [31]. Consistent with these findings, women’s recommendations were focused on providing more lighting, better property maintenance, and youth programming to promote positive behaviors. Other solutions suggested the development of initiatives that may foster trusting relationships with law enforcement and emergency systems to navigate history of distrust. These findings underscore the importance of investing in the physical and social fabric of neighborhoods. Findings also affirm that safety is a prerequisite for belonging to self, women cannot exercise agency or cultivate self-worth in environments where physical and emotional vulnerability constrain their daily movement and choices. Women’s recommendations for neighborhood investment are thus simultaneously recommendations for restoring the conditions under which belonging to self becomes possible.

Findings on women’s sense of autonomy were framed not as an individual trait or freedom of choice, but as a collective practice nurtured through care, competence, and connection. Autonomy was specifically tied to competence, self-confidence, and relational support. Similar to prior studies, perceptions of autonomy and self-worth were often mediated by the presence of positive social support [32, 33]. On the other hand, negative experiences with one’s neighborhood undermine women’s autonomy by limiting their access to social support [32]. Participants also highlighted the value of role models to promote autonomy, especially in parenting and partnerships, where the absence of positive role models left them feeling unprepared and undervalued. In areas of autonomy, women proposed solutions with intergenerational support systems, mentoring, peer-led education, parenting groups, and exposure to new possibilities as means to support women’s economic mobility. These findings are supported by prior studies on intergenerational caregiving relationships where older women found purpose and meaning by caring for and supporting young children as well as studies on the assets of Flint Women as key to reclaiming agency [3, 8, 15]. Autonomy is the dimension of belonging to self, most directly expressed in women’s own voices, their desire to feel competent, valued, and self-determining. That women articulated autonomy not as individual freedom, but as collectively nurtured practice reflects a distinctly community-rooted understanding of what belonging to self requires. At the policy level, investing in mentorship infrastructure, vocational training, and financial literacy programs, particularly in disinvested communities, will create the structural conditions under which women can develop the competence and agency that belonging to self requires.

Overall, this study reinforces the importance of listening to women and centering their solutions. Taken together, the four themes, basic needs, services, safety, and autonomy, are not parallel or independent domains. They are interconnected dimensions of belonging to self, each shaping and reinforcing the others. When any one is absent, the capacity for self-worth and personal agency is undermined. When all four are addressed, the conditions for belonging to self are restored. Their insights move beyond documentation of harm and provide a vision of what it would take to belong, to feel safe, valued, connected, and in control.

Community-informed solutions pointed to the need for funding trauma-informed and peer-led service programs, investing in neighborhood safety through greening and infrastructure improvements, and shifting institutional cultures to center respect, empathy, and autonomy. These recommendations build on prior work by Flint-area women that identifies community assets and resilience as critical to health equity efforts [3]. We recommend that policymakers, service providers, and researchers should engage with the community with the understanding of and willingness to leverage the assets and lived experiences within the community.

Conclusion

Complex and interconnected factors shape women’s experiences of belonging to oneself. Themes of basic needs, access to services, safety, and autonomy can frame pathways to health and wellbeing for women in challenging contexts. Despite limited access to opportunity, women shared collective strategies for survival, support, and reimagining their futures.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. The sample of 100 participants is large for a qualitative study of this depth, providing a robust dataset that lends credibility to the identified themes. The community-partnered design is a particular strength, women contributed directly to the narratives about their own community through co-creation of the interview guide, data analysis, and manuscript development, ensuring that findings reflect community priorities and ways of knowing. The longstanding research partnership underlying this work provided a strong foundation of trust and mutual accountability that enhances the quality and authenticity of the data. Moreover, a significant proportion of participants occupied dual roles as both community residents and human service providers. This dual perspective strengthened the dataset, capturing both firsthand experiences of navigating structural barriers to belonging and the insights of those supporting others through similar challenges.

Several limitations should be noted. Data were collected in a single post-industrial city, which may limit transferability to other contexts, though findings are consistent with patterns documented in similar communities. Additionally, we did not collect data on participants’ income or employment status, nor were these variables included in the analysis. This limits our ability to examine how socioeconomic variation within the sample may have shaped women’s experiences of belonging to self. Furthermore, given the context-specific and relatively small sample in Flint, we did not disaggregate findings by multiple demographic characteristics to minimize the risk of participant identifiability. The analysis therefore focuses on shared themes across participants rather than subgroup comparisons. We acknowledge that this aggregate approach limits the application of an intersectional critical lens, and that multiple identities may have shaped experiences of belonging to self in ways not fully captured here [15]. Community partners did not participate as data collectors which we acknowledge as a constraint on full power-sharing in data collection. Finally, while the interview guide was designed to assess general community needs, the qualitative nature of the sessions frequently led to deep, unsolicited disclosures of trauma, including domestic violence, childhood sexual abuse, violence, and human trafficking. Because the research did not anticipate these narratives a priori, formal independent safeguards such as a standardized list of clinical resources or national hotlines were not integrated into the informed consent process. Future researchers working in similar community contexts should anticipate the presence of trauma narratives even when they are not the primary object of inquiry.
