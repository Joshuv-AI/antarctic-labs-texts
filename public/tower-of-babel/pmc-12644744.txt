Hierarchical Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 electrocatalyst enabling phenolic contaminant-to-chemical conversion
Authors: Renyu Wang, Gong Zhang, Lizhe Liu, Qinghua Ji, Huachun Lan, Jiuhui Qu, Huijuan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Valorizing phenol in wastewater to cyclohexanol via electrochemical hydrogenation (ECH) offers a promising strategy for advancing a circular economy that shifts from pollutant control to resource recovery, but remains challenged by the absence of efficient and selective catalysts in complex wastewater matrices. Herein, we report a hierarchical Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst with coupling multi–level electronic state to improve the phenol ECH performance. The metal-semimetal-insulator hierarchical interface effectively induces the integration of H* evolution and phenol activation, and the unsaturated Ru–O coordination formed by precise anchoring of Ru on Al2O3 lowers the reaction energy barrier, achieving a 4.3-fold increase in the reaction rate over that on the pristine Ru catalyst. Consequently, the ECH process applied to actual phenol-containing wastewater demonstrates a stable cyclohexanol selectivity of approximately 80% for over 120 h, making it more economically feasible and environmentally beneficial than the conventional adsorption-Fenton oxidation treatment. Our work provides valuable guidance for wastewater treatment from environmental burden to sustainable recovery.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, organic wastewater treatment has primarily targeted pollutant removal by oxidizing organic compounds into carbon dioxide (CO2)1. As a typical scenario, phenol is one of the most prevalent and hazardous species, widely used as a raw material in the production of industrial chemicals such as phenolic resins and adipic acid2,3. Its extensive industrial use, along with unreacted residues and water-intensive purification processes4,5, results in the annual discharge of over 10 million tons of high-concentration phenol wastewater (several hundred to several thousand milligrams per liter), with an estimated treatment cost exceeding $500 million6. Currently, a conventional treatment widely used for high-concentration phenol wastewater involves removing the majority of phenol through adsorption, followed by Fenton oxidation to mineralize the residual phenol7. However, this method generates substantial amounts of spent adsorbents and iron sludge that require disposal as hazardous waste, and the mineralization of phenol releases significant amounts of greenhouse gases (Fig. 1a)8,9. As the shift toward a circular and low-carbon economy accelerates, wastewater is increasingly viewed not as waste but as a potential stream encompassing valuable resources10. Renewable energy-driven electrocatalytic hydrogenation (ECH) for converting phenol to cyclohexanol offers a promising green and sustainable alternative11,12, and the produced cyclohexanol that serves as a high-value precursor for nylon-6 and nylon-66 production effectively offsets the treatment costs13. The phenol ECH reaction adheres to the Langmuir–Hinshelwood (L–H) mechanism, in which H* is generated on the catalyst surface (Volmer step) through proton reduction (acidic electrolytes) or water dissociation (neutral or alkaline electrolytes) and subsequently reacts with the aromatic ring of adsorbed phenol to produce cyclohexanol14,15. However, H* also serves as an intermediate in the competing hydrogen evolution reaction (HER)16, which depletes the surface H* available for the phenol ECH reaction to reduce the efficiency of cyclohexanol production and poses safety risks of potential H2/O2 mixture explosions. Specifically, an imbalance favoring excessively strong H* adsorption relative to phenol increases the ECH reaction barrier because tight binding of H* inhibits its transfer to phenol, redirecting it toward competing HER pathways. Conversely, excessively strong phenol adsorption coupled with weak H* adsorption not only limits the availability of surface H* necessary but also leads to overcrowding of adsorbed phenol, physically hindering the proximity and alignment required for efficient H* transfer and interaction17. Therefore, a catalyst design that strategically balances H* and phenol adsorption from the perspective of the interfacial reaction kinetics to optimize the directional transfer of H* is crucial for improving the phenol ECH efficiency.

Herein, we demonstrate that the metal‒semimetal‒insulator hierarchical interface constructed by precise anchoring of Ru on Al2O3 forms a multi-level electronic state coupling structure, which effectively enhances the reaction kinetics of phenol ECH (Fig. 1b). Notably, unlike catalysts with randomly distributed active sites, the directionally assembled Ru clusters on the Al2O3 support enable integration of localizable coherence and the ECH reaction owing to the following factors: (1) Through diffusion and aggregation during high-temperature calcination, Ru atoms on Al2O3 form nanoscale Ru clusters that reduce the surface energy, and the nanoscale quantum size effect promotes the Volmer step, forming a H*-enriched region that serves as a reservoir providing a sufficient H* source for the phenol ECH reaction. (2) Through precise modulation of the spatial distribution of Ru clusters at the atomic level by controlling the thermal decomposition-driven aggregation dynamics, the permeation of Ru at the Ru–Al2O3 interface is affected, resulting in appropriate H* desorption and spatial migration capabilities. (3) The interfacial stress induced by Ru reconstruction disrupts the Al2O3 lattice symmetry, forming unsaturated Ru–O coordination, which effectively lowers the hydrogenation energy barrier of phenol, facilitating close energy level alignment with H* desorption. (4) The Lewis Acid sites of Al2O3 support interact with phenolic hydroxyls to polarize the substrate, thereby coupling surface adsorption chemistry with interfacial electronic modulation to uniquely accelerate ECH reaction. As a result, compared with the pristine Ru catalyst, the phenol ECH reaction rate increased 4.3-fold on the directionally assembled Ru clusters on the Al2O3 support, which pioneers a promising strategy for wastewater resource recovery through precise interfacial engineering of the catalyst.

Results

Synthesis and structural characterization

The catalyst was synthesized in the following steps to design a multi-level electronic state coupling structure: Ti fibers with well electrical conductivity first was used as the substrate to ensure rapid charge carrier transport, and Al2O3 was subsequently anchored onto the Ti fibers via a coating and calcination process to provide robust support for the Ru load. Moreover, the Lewis acid sites on Al2O3 could interact with the hydroxyl oxygen of phenol, inducing charge polarization within the aromatic ring via electron density redistribution18. Finally, the RuCl3 precursor solution was dispersed on the Al2O3 support and then annealed in a reducing atmosphere (Fig. 2a). During thermal reduction, RuCl3 decomposition is driven by surface energy minimization, leading to the formation of uniformly dispersed Ru nanoclusters anchored on Al2O3 through strong metal‒support interactions. The controlled aggregation achieves an optimal catalyst size that balances two critical factors: avoiding the limited active site availability of larger particles and mitigating the insufficient H* adsorption associated with excessively small clusters. Therefore, the nanoscale quantum size effect could contribute to the formation of a H*-enriched region as a reservoir that ensures a sufficient supply of H* for the phenol ECH reaction. Furthermore, the crystalline defects of Al2O3 induced by high-temperature calcination facilitate permeation of RuCl3 into the Al2O3 lattice at the contact interface. In this process, the interface is reconstructed, and an unsaturated Ru–O coordination environment is generated, providing a suitable site for lowering the energy barrier of the phenol ECH reaction. This architecture enables effective electron transport either through interfacial Ru–O coordination or via the continuous Ru nanoclusters network, despite the insulating nature of Al2O3. To confirm the successful preparation of the catalyst, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was initially used, as shown in Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 1. The notably rough intertwined fibrous rod-like structures of the titanium fiber substrate after the above preparation process preliminarily confirmed loading of Al2O3 and Ru. The pristine Ru catalyst without Al2O3 also was loaded onto titanium fibers as a control, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. The spatial distributions of Ru and Al2O3 on the catalyst surface were further investigated via transmission electron microscopy (TEM), in which darker contrast regions were identified as Ru clusters, whereas white lamellar structures corresponded to Al2O3 sheets (Fig. 2c). Aberration-corrected TEM (AC-TEM), complemented by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), provided further atomic-scale structural features, confirming that the well-crystallized regions were Ru clusters and that the amorphous regions were Al2O3 (Supplementary Fig. 3). Notably, lattice fringes with a spacing of 2.05 Å, corresponding to the (101) plane of hexagonal Ru, verified the successful anchoring of Ru atoms onto the Al2O3 support. Furthermore, partially isolated Ru embedded within the Al2O3 matrix was observed at the periphery of the Ru clusters (marked with dashed lines), suggesting structural reconstruction of Ru at the Ru–Al2O3 interface (Fig. 2d).

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to probe the electronic structure of the catalysts. The high-resolution 3d5/2 spectrum of the pristine Ru catalyst clearly shows Ru0 characteristic peaks at 280.2 eV without the signature of oxidized Ru species, confirming the fully reduced state of the pristine Ru catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 4). In contrast, the XPS spectrum of the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst exhibits an additional characteristic peak at 281.3 eV (Supplementary Fig. 5), assigned to partially oxidized Ruδ+, indicating its formation arises from the reconstruction of Ru within the Al2O3 lattice19. The structural evolution was driven by Ru–O orbital hybridization, which disrupted the long-range lattice order of Al2O3 through the generation of localized defects and interfacial strain. As a result, a unique semimetallic electronic environment developed in the catalyst20, which played a pivotal role in modulating the electronic structure to reduce the ECH reaction energy barrier. Complementary insights from Ru K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge spectra (XANES) further validate the XPS findings. For the pristine Ru catalyst, the absorption edge position is almost identical to that of Ru foil, indicating a fully reduced metallic state. In contrast, the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst exhibits a slight positive shift in the edge position (Fig. 2e), confirming the coexistence of metallic Ru and semimetallic Ru oxides. This integration induces a hybrid electronic structure that regulates the balance between metallic and covalent interactions, which might influence its catalytic properties.

The variability of the electronic structure strongly depends on the coordination environment of the metal. Therefore, Fourier transform extended X-ray absorption fine-structure (FT-EXAFS) spectroscopy was used to further investigate the local coordination environment. The pristine Ru catalyst shows no discernible Ru–O scattering path (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 1) while the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst displays two distinct coordination environments corresponded to the Ru–O bond (~1.50 Å) and Ru–Ru bond (~2.30 Å), revealing that orbital hybridized Ru–O coordination formed during the Ru reconstruction at the Ru–Al2O3 interface. This hybridization redistributed the electron density by enabling electron donation from Ru to O sites on the Al2O3 surface, enhancing the nucleophilicity of these O sites and thereby promoting phenol adsorption and activation. Furthermore, compared with the value of 1.95 Å in commercial RuO2, the slight elongation of the Ru–O bond to 2.02 Å likely arose from surface strain induced by Ru incorporation into the Al2O3 lattice. This elongation weakened the covalency of the Ru–O bond, endowing it with semimetallic properties (Supplementary Tables 2, 3)21. Notably, the reduced Ru–O coordination number in Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3, relative to commercial RuO2, further indicated that geometric deformation during interface reconstruction resulted in fewer O ligands being bound to Ru, forming an unsaturated coordination environment. This conclusion was corroborated by the lower intensity of the k³-weighted Ru K-edge peak in k-space (Supplementary Fig. 6). The reduction in structural order increased the electron density in the Ru d orbital and enhanced the catalytic activity22. Wavelet transform (WT) analysis further confirms the presence of Ru–O bond in the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst, whereas no such bonds are detected in the pristine Ru catalyst (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 7), and this observation verifies the formation of Ru–O coordination originates from the reconstruction of Ru species at the Ru–Al2O3 interface.

Reaction kinetics studies

To elucidate the contribution of the multilevel electronic state coupling structure, the phenol ECH performance on the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst was analyzed, with product fragment ion patterns characterized via gas chromatography‒mass spectrometry (Supplementary Fig. 8). Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the phenol concentration on the pristine Ru catalyst with randomly distributed active sites decreased from 10.6 mM to 6.7 mM after 120 min, yielding 2.1 mM cyclohexanol. In comparison, the phenol ECH reaction activity on the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst remarkable increased, reducing the phenol concentration to 2.2 mM while generating 7.8 mM cyclohexanol, with a selectivity of 92.6% (Fig. 3a). Despite Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 possessing a 2.53-fold higher surface area than pristine Ru due to the porous nature of the Al2O3 support, the enlarged-geometric-area Ru electrode prepared to match the same surface area of Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 exhibited only partially improved activity but remained far inferior to Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3, indicating that surface area effect makes only a limited contribution to the overall catalytic activity (Supplementary Fig. 9). These results illustrate the enhancement of the ECH reaction kinetics mainly originates from the synergistic interaction between Ru and Al2O3 induced by orbital hybridization and coordination unsaturation. The increase in the reaction kinetics led to a significant improvement in the Faradaic efficiency of the phenol ECH reaction from 12.9% to 50.2% (Supplementary Fig. 10) while effectively suppressing the undesired HER. The fact that the carbon balance was greater than 95% further confirmed the reliability of the results, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 11. Additionally, the generation of cyclohexanone as a minor intermediate in the ECH process arose from the initial hydrogenation of phenol with four adsorbed H*, followed by subsequent two-electron transfer reduction of the carbonyl (C=O) bond to yield cyclohexanol (Fig. 3b)23,24. This trend could be attributed to the fact that the phenol ECH process fundamentally relies on the delicate and intricate synergy between Ru and Al2O3. The evidence from TEM images clearly revealed that as the Ru/Al2O3 ratio increased, both the size of the Ru clusters and the Ru coverage on Al2O3 increased (Supplementary Fig. 14). Specifically, at low Ru loadings, the limited number of Ru sites directly led to insufficient generation of H*, and the sparse distribution of Ru on the surface also hindered the formation of an optimized Ru–O coordination interface, which impeded efficient coupling of metallic and covalent bonding interactions and ultimately reduced the ECH reaction rate. Conversely, at high Ru loadings, overcrowded Ru particle aggregation disrupted the localized Ru–O coordination, resulting in ineffective phenol activation. Meanwhile, the deconvolution of the Ru 3d XPS spectra revealed that the optimized electrode contained a moderate fraction of Ruδ⁺ species (~6.7%, Supplementary Fig. 15), which displays a critical impact on catalytic activity. Therefore, Ru–O coordination at the optimized Ru/Al2O3 ratio emerges as a key factor in enabling the formation of an ideal multilevel electronic state coupling structure that bridges H* generation with phenol adsorption.

To further verify the necessity of Ru–O coordination at Ru-Al2O3 interface, we also conducted control experiments employing Nafion as the binder to immobilize Ru powder and RuO2 powder on Al2O3, thereby eliminating the Ru–Al2O3 interaction. The resulting Ru(NP) and RuO2(NP) electrodes exhibited significantly lower phenol hydrogenation performance, highlighting the crucial role of the Ru–O interfacial coordination in enabling efficient electron–proton coupling and selective product formation (Supplementary Fig. 16). Furthermore, to examine the generality of this interfacial engineering strategy, we also evaluated La2O3, CeO2, and TiO2 as support under identical conditions. In all cases, thermal reduction induced local Ru structural reconstruction and defect formation in the oxides, which promoted the emergence of unsaturated Ru–O coordination at the metal–oxide interface. These supports also expose surface Lewis acidic sites that interact with phenolic hydroxyls, thereby contributing to substrate polarization17,23. Consistently, Ru/Ruδ+/La2O3, Ru/Ruδ+/CeO2, and Ru/Ruδ+/TiO2 catalysts all exhibited higher activity than the pristine Ru catalyst, confirming that the enhancement arises from the universal role of Ru–O interfacial coordination (Supplementary Fig. 17).

The synergy of Ru and Al2O3 enhanced the electronic environment for phenol activation to accelerate the four-electron hydrogenation of phenol to cyclohexanone. Additionally, the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst outperformed the Ru/C catalyst and previously reported catalysts25–28 in phenol ECH, further highlighting the importance of designing tailored interfacial properties to improve the catalytic activity (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 19).

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves offer crucial mechanistic insights into the relationship between the electronic structure and interfacial dynamics during the phenol ECH reaction. The reduction peak at ~−160 mV, corresponding to phenol reduction, marks the maximum surface concentration gradient of phenol arising from interface mass transfer limitations29. On the Ru catalyst, this peak appeared at a lower phenol concentration (5 mM), reflecting the lack of a structured coordination environment, as randomly distributed active sites hinder effective reactant distribution (Supplementary Fig. 20). In contrast, the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst exhibited this reduction peak at a higher phenol concentration (20 mM), indicating that the unsaturated coordination environment with unoccupied d orbitals facilitated stronger bonding interactions with phenol and mitigated mass transfer limitations by enhancing the effective reactant distribution (Fig. 3f). In addition, it is noteworthy that the current decreases progressively with increasing phenol concentration. In the absence of phenol, the H* generated via the Volmer (H+ + e- + *→ H*) step readily undergoes the Heyrovsky or Tafel step to form H230. When phenol concentration increases, more phenol molecules occupy the catalyst surface active sites used for H–H recombination and redirect the utilization of partial H* toward phenol ECH reaction31,32. Owing to the multistep proton–electron transfer with a relatively higher reaction barrier, the reaction rate of ECH is lower than that of HER; moreover, the mass transport of phenol as the reactant is more easily restricted than that of the abundant protons in HER, resulting in the progressive current decrease as phenol concentration increases. This trend also provides direct evidence for the interaction between H* and phenol, which has been reported in many studies on the hydrogenation of organic compounds33–35. Compared with the pristine Ru catalyst with randomly distributed active sites, the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst had a significantly lower electron transfer resistance (Rct)36 and a larger electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) (Supplementary Figs. 21, 22). This advantage is attributed to the well-aligned orbital hybridization and localized coordination, which accelerate carrier migration and reduce defect-related scattering37.

Stability and universality

The durability of the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst was assessed via chronopotentiometry at a current density of 50 mA cm−2 over 300 h (Fig. 3g). The electrode potential remained stable at ~−910 mV throughout the test, suggesting negligible structural degradation or active site deactivation. This electrochemical stability was corroborated by the SEM analysis of the post-reaction electrode, which revealed that the morphological integrity was preserved (Supplementary Fig. 23). Recognizing that potential stability does not unambiguously confirm the persistence of hydrogenation activity, the ECH performance of the catalyst after 300 h stability test was performed to directly probe the catalyst’s functional durability. The Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst after 300 h stability test displayed a cyclohexanol selectivity of 87.6% (Supplementary Fig. 24), nearly identical to that of the fresh electrode (92.6%). The functional durability is attributed to the strong interfacial anchoring of Ru clusters on the Al2O3 support, which effectively prevents active site detachment under prolonged operation.

To establish the practical viability of the phenol ECH reaction, we systematically investigated the interplay between critical water quality parameters and reaction mechanisms. First, pH-dependent studies revealed a decrease in cyclohexanol selectivity under alkaline conditions (Supplementary Fig. 25), which was attributed to fewer protons being available for H* intermediate formation. This aligns with the established proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) theory, in which elevated pH impedes hydrogen species protonation, thereby decoupling essential electron‒proton synergies during hydrogenation38,39. Notably, the phenol ECH reaction demonstrated exceptional resilience to common aqueous anions (Cl⁻, CO32-, PO43-), with a variation in performance of less than 7%, underscoring kinetic dominance by substrate‒electrode interactions rather than electrolyte ion effects (Supplementary Fig. 26). Additionally, to evaluate the catalyst’s resistance against trace metal contaminants that are commonly present in real wastewater, we performed eight consecutive ECH cycles in electrolyte intentionally doped with Fe3+, Cu2+, and Ni2+ (each 1 mg L−1). Although post-reaction EDS mapping revealed minor deposition of these metal ions on the electrode surface (Supplementary Fig. 27), the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 electrode showed no discernible attenuation in phenol conversion and cyclohexanol selectivity across the cycles (Fig. 3h), indicating that the Ru-O interfacial coordination endows the catalyst with strong resistance to impurity-induced deactivation and ensures robust operation under complex water matrices.

The versatility of the system was further demonstrated through efficient treatment of structurally diverse phenolic pollutants, including chlorophenol and cresol, via tailored reaction pathways: chlorophenol (CP) underwent sequential dechlorination–hydrogenation to cyclohexanol, whereas cresol was directly hydrogenated to methylcyclohexanol. Remarkably, >85% product selectivity was maintained across all the substrates (Fig. 3i), indicating the insensitivity of the catalytic interface to electronic and steric perturbations introduced by different substituents. In addition, the multi-level electronic state coupling ensured uniform activation of phenolic substrates by facilitating efficient coordination with the aromatic ring. This minimized the influence of substituents on the reaction pathway, allowing consistent hydrogenation activity regardless of the substituent type or position (e.g., 2-, 3-, or 4-substituted). Additionally, the significant reduction in the toxicity of compounds treated through the ECH reaction elucidated its alignment with green chemistry principles, providing a sustainable and versatile solution for industrial wastewater remediation (Supplementary Fig. 28).

Mechanism for phenol ECH reaction on Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst

The phenol ECH reaction on the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst was further elucidated using in situ electrochemical ATR surface-enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR-SEIRAS) with a homemade electrochemical cell (Supplementary Fig. 29). The potential-dependent spectral evolution revealed that as the applied potential was stepped increasingly negative, a broad band centered at ~3255 cm−1 (assigned to O–H vibrations40) gradually intensified, together with bands at 1213 and 1297 cm−1 corresponding to C–O(H) stretching, confirmed the effective formation of cyclohexanol product (Fig. 4a). In parallel, a peak at 3743 cm−1 associated with the O–H stretching mode generated alongside production of H* from water splitting41, which demonstrated the coupled activation of phenolic substrates and H* intermediates at the catalyst‒electrolyte interface. Moreover, the emergence of signals at 1650 and 1697 cm−1, characteristic of C=O stretching42, together with the vibrations of the aromatic ring (C=Cring) at 1515 and 1558 cm−1 43, indicated the presence of cyclohexanone intermediates and partially hydrogenated ring structures. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was employed to track H* species during the phenol ECH reaction. While negligible signals were observed on Al2O3 and weak signals were observed on metallic Ru, the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst exhibited a prominent nine-peak DMPO-H adduct signature44 (Fig. 4b). This stable H* trapping profile indicated that the unsaturated Ru–O coordination structure stabilized H* intermediates through localized electronic modulation, which was corroborated by theoretical calculations (Supplementary Fig. 31). To further examine the interaction between H* and phenol, EPR measurements were also conducted with varying phenol concentrations, which revealed a progressive suppression of the DMPO-H signal at elevated phenol levels (Supplementary Fig. 32). This trend confirms that phenol effectively consumes interfacial H* through hydrogenation, supporting the role of H* as a key intermediate and the effective activation of phenol at the Ru–O interface. Complementary quenching experiments with multiple H* scavengers further confirmed the critical involvement of H* in phenol hydrogenation, as evidenced by the consistent suppression of phenol removal upon quencher addition (Supplementary Fig. 33). The vacancy migration energy barrier for H* was investigated to further understand the mechanism of efficient H* generation on the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 34). The substantially lower energy threshold for H* diffusion across Ru sites compared to those across the Ru–O and Al2O3 sites indicated that the Ru domains were kinetically favorable reservoirs for H* transfer, whereas adjacent Ru–O sites facilitated subsequent H* transfer to adsorbed phenol. In such spatially resolved H* management, rapid Ru-mediated diffusion coupled with Ru–O-assisted utilization synergistically enhanced the overall reaction efficiency.

Crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) analysis was employed to investigate the bonding and antibonding interactions between phenol and the active sites on the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst (Fig. 4c). The integrated COHP (ICOHP) quantification indicated that the Ru–O coordination at the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 interface (−3.37 eV) resulted in an appropriate interaction with phenol, strategically positioned between strong adsorption on Al2O3 (−3.50 eV) and weak adsorption on Ru (−2.62 eV). This intermediate binding originated from the Ru–O orbital hybridization-induced electronic redistribution, creating a suitable hydrogenation site45, which is essential to avoid either excessive stabilization or insufficient adsorption in the phenol ECH process. Additionally, the Ru–O sites could accept H* that rapidly migrated from the H* reservoir at the Ru sites, ensuring a rapid H* supply for the phenol ECH reaction. Consequently, an integrated micro-interface that enables localized coherence of the phenol ECH reaction via a multilevel electronic state coupling structure significantly improves the reaction activity.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were systematically performed to elucidate the reaction mechanism, with the reaction pathway explicitly mapped in Fig. 4d. Geometric optimization of the intermediates (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Figs. 35, 36 and Supplementary Data 1) conclusively established that the first hydrogenation step was the rate-determining step (RDS). Comparative analysis of the Gibbs free energy barriers revealed that Ru–O sites exhibited the most favorable thermosdynamics (ΔG = 1.10 eV), with a barrier that was significantly lower than those at Ru sites (ΔG = 1.23 eV) and Al2O3 sites (ΔG = 1.46 eV). This superior performance originated from the intermediate adsorption strength and the finely tuned electronic structure at the Ru–O coordination site. To further explore the electronic interactions during the RDS, differential charge density analysis was performed (Fig. 4f), which revealed stark contrasts among the active sites. While the intermediate was engaged at the Ru site and Al2O3 site through a single electron-deficient region, a dual interaction site characterized by both electron-rich and electron-deficient regions was established at the Ru–O site. This dual-site configuration fostered a more balanced electronic environment, effectively mitigating localized charge accumulation46. As a result, the electron transfer at the Ru–O site (0.0298 e⁻) was significantly lower than that at the Ru site (0.128 e⁻) and the Al2O3 site (0.0383 e⁻), indicating the critical role of Ru–O coordination in lowering the energy barrier of the phenol ECH reaction.

Techno-economic analysis and environmental impact assessment

The conventional treatment of phenol-rich industrial wastewater remains energy intensive and environmentally burdensome, but the proposed phenol ECH system addresses these challenges through synergistic integration of wastewater remediation and value-added chemical synthesis (Fig. 5a). In the future, the ECH system could also employ renewable energy sources for driving the electrochemical conversion of phenol into cyclohexanol under mild conditions to reduce the reliance on fossil fuels and further reduce the environmental burden. After cyclohexanol recovery via phase separation, the treated water can be safely discharged into natural water bodies to enable sustainable water reuse. This ECH system establishes a closed-loop resource cycle in which wastewater constituents are systematically converted into commercial products, effectively bridging environmental remediation with circular economic objectives. To evaluate the feasibility of the proposed concept, a two-electrode system was used to convert phenol in real wastewater from salicylic acid production into cyclohexanol via the ECH process. Since phenol is used as a raw material in salicylic acid production, a significant amount of unreacted phenol remains in wastewater (48.3 mM), and its water quality parameters are detailed in Supplementary Table 4. Throughout six consecutive batch experiments, despite the complexity of the real wastewater matrix, the cyclohexanol selectivity remained stable at ~80% (Fig. 5b). A preliminary techno-economic analysis (TEA) using a model incorporating seven critical parameters related to both operating and capital costs provided a comprehensive evaluation of the economic performance of the ECH system (Supplementary Fig. 37)47,48. The key factors include the electricity price, cyclohexanol selectivity, current density, and operating voltage, with detailed calculations available in Supplementary Text 1. The conventional treatment (adsorption–Fenton oxidation) was also analyzed for comparative purposes, as it represents a method widely adopted for treating organic industrial wastewater. While conventional treatment incurs a cost of $28.6 per ton of phenol wastewater, the ECH process reduces this expenditure to $10.9 per ton. Additionally, the recovery of cyclohexanol as a valuable byproduct generates revenue of $5.9 per ton (Supplementary Tables 5, 6), resulting in a further reduction in net treatment cost to $5.0 per ton. Notably, electricity accounts for 48.4% of the total expenditure in the ECH process according to a detailed cost breakdown (Fig. 5d). This underscores the transformative potential of renewable energy integration: as renewable energy becomes more prevalent and electricity costs decline, the economic advantages of the ECH system are expected to significantly grow, further confirming its role as a cost-effective and sustainable solution for phenol wastewater treatment.

The environmental superiority of the phenol ECH process over conventional treatment was assessed via a cradle-to-gate life cycle assessment (LCA, Supplementary Fig. 38)49,50, which has details in Supplementary Text 2 and specific results summarized in Supplementary Tables 7, 8. The results revealed that the difference in carbon emissions between the ECH process and conventional treatment is not significant when coal is used to generate electricity. Notably, a critical distinction exists between the conventional treatment and the phenol ECH process in terms of carbon emission sources: whereas the carbon emissions in the conventional treatment are derived principally from reagent consumption and material utilization, the carbon footprint of the ECH system is predominantly attributable to electricity usage. This unique dependency makes the carbon footprint of the ECH process highly responsive to the integration of renewable energy sources (Fig. 5e). Comparative analysis suggests that substituting coal-derived electricity with photovoltaic energy would result in a remarkable 91.4% carbon emission reduction in the ECH process, compared with a modest 10.0% reduction in conventional treatment (Supplementary Tables 9, 10), indicating that when photovoltaic energy is used as the electricity source, the ECH process reduces carbon emissions by 128.3 kg CO2-eq per ton of phenol wastewater treated relative to the conventional treatment. These results underscore the transformative potential of coupling the ECH process with renewable energy not only as a cleaner alternative for wastewater treatment but also as a significant contributor to global carbon reduction initiatives.

To comprehensively evaluate the sustainability of the ECH process, we further conduct an analysis encompassing 16 environmental impact categories including resource depletion, human health, ecotoxicity, and atmospheric effects with nuclear energy as the electricity source (Supplementary Table 11)51. Traditional treatment imposes considerable environmental burdens due to the reliance on high levels of unsustainable inputs and the generation of hazardous waste. These burdens manifest as significant resource depletion and terrestrial ecotoxicity, with land use (LU) and terrestrial ecotoxicity (TE) values reaching 2.0 m2·yr−1 crop equiv. and 301.1 kg 1,4-DCB, respectively. In contrast, the ECH process demonstrates a marked ability to alleviate these impacts, reducing LU by 93.9% and TE by 82.7%. Moreover, the green electrochemical nature of ECH notably decreases the use of chemical reagents, resulting in pronounced improvements across categories related to human health and water ecology. Specifically, Human non-carcinogenic toxicity (HNT) is reduced by 28.6%, while freshwater ecotoxicity (FEc) and marine ecotoxicity (MEc) decline by 62.5% and 64.5%, respectively. Beyond its significant contribution to reducing carbon emissions, the widespread application of ECH process also exerts distinctly positive effects in terms of other atmospheric domains (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Table 12). Furthermore, adopting the ECH process to valorize phenol wastewater into cyclohexanol, as compared with the industrial production of cyclohexanol from benzene thermocatalytic hydrogenation (Supplementary Text 3 and Supplementary Table 13), yields pronounced reductions across all analyzed environmental impact categories (Supplementary Table 14), thereby underscoring its potential to partially displace conventional petrochemical routes with a more sustainable alternative.

Discussion

In summary, we successfully designed an integrated microinterface that leverages multi-level electronic state coupling to achieve localizable coherence of the phenol ECH reaction, thereby significantly enhancing the reaction activity. The Ru–O coordination formed through precise anchoring of Ru onto Al2O3 induces orbital hybridization and coordination unsaturation to lower the phenol ECH reaction energy barrier, which enables a 4.3-fold increase in the reaction rate compared with that on the Ru catalyst with randomly distributed active sites. Furthermore, semimetallic Ru–O coordination, which balances metallic and covalent bonding interactions to increase H* generation and substrate activation, enables the ECH versatility for substituted phenols, with selectivity exceeding 85%. Compared with the conventional treatment for phenol wastewater, the ECH process has dual economic and environmental benefits, pioneering a promising approach for wastewater resource recovery by engineering catalyst interfaces to reduce reaction energy barriers and achieve targeted selectivity for value-added products.

Methods

Materials

All reagents used in the experiment without any further purification are described in detail in Supplementary Text 4. Titanium fibers (≥99% purity) with a thickness of 0.4 mm were purchased from Suzhou Sinero Technology Co., Ltd. (China). All the solutions were prepared using Milli-Q ultrapure water.

Preparation of Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst

Ti fibers (2 × 2.5 cm2) were ultrasonically cleaned in acetone, ethanol, and deionized water for 20 min each. A 200 mM AlCl₃ solution (150 µL) was drop-cast onto the fibers, followed by calcination at 600 °C for 10 min, which was repeated 10 times, with a final 2 h calcination, forming an Al2O3 support layer. To prepare Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst, RuCl3 solutions of various concentrations (10–150 mM) were applied (100 µL per cycle) to the Al2O3-coated fibers, which were subsequently dried at 60 °C, and this process was repeated 10 times. The fibers were then annealed at 300 °C for 2 h in 90% N2/10% H2, yielding the Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst. The Ru loading amount of the obtained electrodes range from 0.2 to 3 mg·cm−2. For comparison, a pristine Ru catalyst was prepared by directly depositing Ru on Ti fibers under identical conditions without the Al2O3 support layer. Details of the other catalysts are provided in Supplementary Text 5, and the catalyst characterization is described in Supplementary Text 6.

Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical measurements were performed on an electrochemical workstation (AUTOLAB PGSTAT302N) using a conventional three-electrode system in which the prepared catalyst, Pt foil, and an Ag/AgCl electrode were used as the working, counter, and reference electrodes, respectively. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed from −0.41 to 0.39 V vs. RHE at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was carried out at open-circuit potential with a 10 mV AC perturbation over 10−2-105 Hz. The electrochemical double-layer capacitance (Cdl) was estimated from the CV curves in the non-Faradaic region and calculated as half the slope of the linear fitting curve of the capacitive currents (ΔJ, Janodic - Jcathodic) versus the scan rate. The effective electrochemical surface area (ECSA) is positively correlated with Cdl to estimate the relative ECSA values. The measured potentials were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale using the equation E(RHE) = E(Ag/AgCl) + 0.059 pH + 0.197. All electrochemical tests were carried out under ambient atmospheric conditions at 30 °C and conducted without iR compensation.

Electrocatalytic hydrogenation setup

Phenol ECH was conducted in a dual-chamber H-cell separated by a bipolar membrane (3 cm diameter, 130 μm thickness, Fumasep® FBM). The anode compartment contained 0.1 M Na2SO4 electrolyte (pH 2.0 ± 0.2 adjusted with H2SO4) with a platinum foil counter electrode. Unless otherwise stated, 0.1 M Na2SO4 electrolyte (pH 2.0 ± 0.2 adjusted with H2SO4) with 10.6 mM phenol (1,000 ppm) was added to the cathode compartment, and the synthesized Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 catalyst was employed as the working electrode. A three-electrode configuration was maintained at a constant current density (−50 mA·cm−2) using an electrochemical workstation, with the reaction temperature stabilized at 30 °C via a thermostatic water bath. All batch experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data are presented as the means ± standard deviations. A two-electrode system separated by a bipolar membrane was assembled to conduct batch experiments to evaluate the ECH performance for actual salicylic acid production wastewater. The Ru0/Ruδ+/Al2O3 cathode was integrated with a commercial RuO2 anode in the system, which was powered by a DH1765-1 DC source with real-time voltage monitoring.
