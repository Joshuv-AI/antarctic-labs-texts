From Survival to Recovery: 25 Years of Paediatric Critical Care Transformation in Israel
Authors: Yael Applbaum, Shulamit Gordon, Isaac Manaster, Ziona Haklai, Joseph Mendlovic, Adi Avniel Aran, Uri Pollak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo evaluate 25‐year national trends in paediatric intensive care utilisation, patient outcomes, rehospitalisations and regional resource distribution in Israel.MethodsRetrospective, population‐based cohort study of all paediatric (0–17 years) ICU hospitalisations in Israel between 1999 and 2023. We linked the National Hospital Discharge Register with paediatric ICU bed capacity data and Central Bureau of Statistics population estimates. Outcomes included age‐adjusted ICU admission rates, in‐hospital and 1‐year post‐discharge mortality, rehospitalisation at 7, 30 and 365 days, and paediatric ICU beds per 100 000 children.ResultsThe proportion of hospitalisations involving ICU care increased from 3.53% to 6.1%. In‐hospital ICU mortality declined from 4.3% to 2.3%, and 1‐year post‐discharge mortality decreased from 3.2% to 2.6%. One‐year rehospitalisation occurred in 46% of ICU survivors versus 29% of non‐ICU patients. Between 2018 and 2023, national paediatric ICU bed capacity rose by 23%, yet regional bed‐to‐population ratios varied up to 2.6‐fold.ConclusionsOver 25 years, paediatric ICU utilisation and capacity nearly doubled, accompanied by reductions in mortality. However, persistently high rehospitalisation rates and regional disparities underscore the need for structured post‐ICU follow‐up, standardised admission criteria, equitable resource allocation and further research to clarify drivers of ICU utilisation and long‐term outcomes.Keywords: hospital mortality, outcome assessment (health care), paediatric intensive care units, patient readmission, registries

Introduction

Paediatric intensive care emerged as a separate discipline during the 1950s polio epidemic, with the first ICUs established in the late 1950s–1960s [1, 2, 3]. Since then, the field has evolved beyond a primary focus on survival, emphasising recovery trajectories and the promotion of long‐term functioning for both children and their families [4, 5, 6, 7]. Technological advancements have transformed critical care through sophisticated physiologic monitoring, enhanced infusion technologies, computerised medication administration systems, point‐of‐care diagnostics and pharmaceutical innovations [8]. Concurrently, ICU design has incorporated evidence‐based practices to better meet the needs of patients, families and healthcare providers [9].

Israel's healthcare system provides universal coverage through four competing health maintenance organisations serving approximately 10 million people [10]. With children comprising 33% of the population, paediatric healthcare faces unique demands [11]. The epidemiology of paediatric critical illness has shifted significantly, with preventive care reducing traditional ICU admissions while improved management of congenital and chronic conditions has created a growing population of medically complex children requiring specialised care [12, 13, 14]. In the United States, the proportion of hospitalised children requiring ICU care increased from 10.6% to 15.5% between 2001 and 2019, with more children having underlying comorbidities (46.2% to 57.0%) and technology dependence (16.4% to 23.5%) [15]. With these changes, ICU mortality decreased from 2.5% to 1.8%, though hospital costs nearly doubled [15].

Despite clinical importance, comprehensive longitudinal studies examining utilisation trends, outcomes and resource allocation in paediatric intensive care remain scarce. Key knowledge gaps persist regarding long‐term utilisation patterns across populations, evolving mortality and post‐discharge outcomes, geographic disparities in resource distribution, and the impact of systemic disruptions such as the COVID‐19 pandemic [16, 17, 18, 19].

To address these gaps, we conducted a 25‐year (1999–2023) national analysis of paediatric critical care in Israel. Our objectives were to: (1) describe temporal trends in PICU utilisation; (2) assess changes in inpatient and post‐discharge mortality; (3) characterise rehospitalisation patterns following critical illness; (4) evaluate regional variation in critical care resource distribution; (5) examine the relationship between patient characteristics and care outcomes; and (6) assess the impact of major external events on PICU services.

This study aims to inform clinical decision‐making, strategic resource planning and health policy development in Israel and in other nations with comparable healthcare systems, as the field increasingly prioritises recovery and long‐term outcomes for critically ill children and their families.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

This retrospective, population‐based study analysed all paediatric hospitalisations in Israel between 1 January 1999 and 31 December 2023. Data were obtained from the Israeli National Hospital Discharge Register, supplemented with paediatric ICU bed capacity from the National Registry of Health Care Facilities and population statistics from the Israeli Central Bureau of Statistics.

Study Population

All urgent and elective hospitalisations in acute care hospitals of patients aged 0–17 years were included. Maternity and neonatal ICU admissions were excluded. Premature neonates as well as term neonates who require ICU‐level care immediately after birth are routinely admitted to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs). Therefore, neonatal critical care delivered during the birth hospitalisation, particularly prematurity‐related illness and immediate postnatal critical illness in term infants, is not captured in this PICU‐based analysis. Exceptions include neonatal extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), which may be provided in PICU/PCICU depending on local organisation and postoperative care after neonatal cardiac surgery, which is typically delivered in a paediatric cardiac ICU (PCICU). Importantly, neonates who re‐present to hospital more than 24 h after discharge from the birth hospitalisation are generally not readmitted to NICU; if ICU‐level care is required, they are admitted to PICU. These NICU/PICU triage practices remained stable throughout the study period. Nearly 180 000 patients were identified and categorised into five age groups: < 1, 1–4, 5–9, 10–14 and 15–17 years.

Health System and Organisation of Paediatric Critical Care in Israel

Israel has a universal health‐care system with paediatric inpatient services provided across multiple regions, and critically ill children may be managed locally or transferred based on clinical needs and availability of subspecialty services. Selected high‐complexity services are centralised; notably, paediatric cardiac surgery is performed only in three centres (two in the Central district and one in Jerusalem), and postoperative care is typically delivered in dedicated paediatric cardiac intensive care units (PCICU/CVICU). Consequently, regional PICU utilisation and capacity should be interpreted in the context of referral patterns to these centralised services. Israel does not operate a dedicated national PICU transport system. When inter‐hospital transfer is required, transport is arranged through the national emergency medical services (EMS) system with hospital‐based physician/nurse support as locally indicated.

PICU Workforce Training and Credentialing in Israel

Paediatric intensivists complete paediatric residency training (4.5 years), followed by subspecialty fellowship training in paediatric intensive care (2.5 years). Specialty and subspecialty training programs are supervised by the corresponding board within the National Medical Scientific Council. The minimum national requirement is two board‐certified paediatric intensive care physicians; there is no rigid national ratio of beds to physicians across PICUs. Nursing qualification requirements comprise a 4‐year baccalaureate program leading to a Bachelor of Arts degree in Nursing from a university‐affiliated nursing school, together with licensure as a registered nurse under the auspices of the Israeli Ministry of Health. After approximately 1 year of structured local ICU training, nurses typically attend an advanced practice program in intensive care for children and neonates. These core training requirements were established in the early 1990s and did not materially change during the study period. In 2025, the first cohort of Advanced Nurse Practitioner in Paediatric Intensive Care was graduated; thus, not influencing staffing during the study period.

Definitions and Outcomes

ICU hospitalisation was defined as any stay in a paediatric intensive care unit, excluding neonatal ICUs. Surgical status was defined as undergoing at least one major surgical operation prior to PICU admission during the index hospitalisation. Outcomes included the proportion of hospitalisations with ICU care, age‐adjusted ICU admission rates, in‐hospital and 1‐year post‐discharge mortality, rehospitalisation rates at 7, 30 and 365 days, and regional ICU bed capacity per 100 000 children.

ICU bed counts were derived from the Israeli MOH annual publication ‘Licensed inpatient hospital beds’, which reports licensed beds by ward category as specified in each institution's MOH‐issued registration certificate. In these reports, the MOH ‘Intensive Care Division ‐ Pediatrics’ category includes Paediatric ICU and Paediatric Cardiac ICU. Because this is a licensing‐based administrative classification, analyses should be interpreted as trends in licensed ICU‐designated capacity rather than staffed operational ICU beds.

Statistical Analysis

Trends were examined by 5‐year intervals and stratified by age, gender and surgical status. Age‐adjusted mortality rates were standardised to the annual paediatric population. Statistical significance was determined using principles of binary distribution. All analyses were performed with SAS version 9.4.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Israeli Ministry of Health Institutional Review Board with a waiver of informed consent for de‐identified data. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and reported following the STROBE guidelines.

Infants (< 1 year) consistently exhibited the highest ICU admission rates, while all age groups demonstrated rising utilisation over time. The most pronounced relative increases were among children aged 1–4 and 5–9 years (Table 1). Mortality Outcomes

In‐hospital mortality for ICU patients declined markedly over the study period, while 1‐year post‐discharge mortality also improved (Table 1). In contrast, both in‐hospital and post‐discharge mortality among non‐ICU patients remained consistently low.

ICU Bed Capacity and Regional Disparities

Across the study period (1999–2023), the national paediatric ICU bed rate increased from 4.56 to 6.13 beds per 100 000 children (≈34% increase). Considerable regional disparities were present throughout and persisted in 2023, with bed rates ranging from 4.33 (South) to 11.43 (Tel Aviv) beds per 100 000 children, approximately a 2.6‐fold difference (Figure 3). Discussion

This comprehensive 25‐year national analysis of paediatric critical care in Israel highlights significant shifts in ICU utilisation, patient outcomes and resource distribution between 1999 and 2023. The findings underscore a transformation in the epidemiology and delivery of paediatric intensive care, with notable implications for clinical decision‐making, health system planning and research prioritisation.

Increasing Utilisation of Paediatric ICU Services

The steady rise in the proportion of paediatric hospitalisations involving ICU care, from 3.53% in 1999 to 6.1% in 2023, may reflects a considerable shift in paediatric healthcare, possibly indicating changes in paediatric disease burden, healthcare delivery practices, availability of technologies and beds or all the above. The steepest increase in age‐adjusted ICU admission rates occurred mainly after approximately 2018. These findings raise the possibility that part of the late‐period increase in ICU admissions reflects increased PICU availability (i.e., ‘supply‐sensitive’ intensive care), potentially lowering admission thresholds and reducing access constraints. While similar trends have been reported in other high‐income countries, the magnitude of increase observed in Israel is particularly pronounced [7, 20]. Nevertheless, our data do not include measures of unit occupancy, transfer delays, staffing ratios, or physiologic illness severity; therefore, we cannot quantify the relative contribution of capacity expansion versus changing case‐mix or practice patterns, nor infer causality.

Several factors likely underlie the increasing rate of paediatric ICU utilisation above and beyond the increase in availability of beds. Advances in medical technology and pharmacotherapy have improved survival among children with complex congenital and chronic conditions, contributing to a growing population with ongoing intensive care needs [12]. Evolving clinical guidelines have also broadened the criteria for ICU admission, as accumulating evidence supports the benefits of early critical care intervention [21]. Additionally, the implementation of paediatric early warning systems and rapid response teams has likely enhanced the early identification of clinical deterioration, facilitating more timely ICU transfers [22].

The particularly sharp increase in ICU utilisation between 2019 and 2023 (from 4.8% to 6.1%) coincided with the COVID‐19 pandemic, which profoundly disrupted healthcare systems worldwide. Although paediatric ICU admissions for acute COVID‐19 were relatively uncommon compared to adults [16], the pandemic likely influenced care‐seeking behaviours, delayed presentations of routine paediatric illnesses, and contributed to greater illness severity at the time of admission. In addition, the emergence of novel conditions such as Multisystem Inflammatory Syndrome in Children (MIS‐C), a syndrome frequently requiring intensive care, may have further increased ICU demand during this period [23].

Improving Survival in Paediatric Critical Care

One of the most notable findings in our study is the marked decline in in‐hospital mortality among children admitted to the PICU, from 3.2% in 1999–2003 to 2.6% in 2019–2023 (an 18.8% relative reduction). While this trend is consistent with improvements in paediatric critical care and broader health‐system developments over time, our registry‐based data do not include physiologic severity‐of‐illness measures or other granular clinical variables, and we therefore cannot attribute the decline to care quality alone. Changes in case‐mix, admission thresholds, and coding practices may also have contributed. Similar downward trends have been reported in other high‐income settings [4, 24], and our findings suggest that Israel has experienced a comparable population‐level improvement in outcomes for critically ill children over the past two decades.

Several potential factors may have contributed to the observed improvements in paediatric ICU survival. Advances in critical care management, such as lung‐protective ventilation strategies, goal‐directed therapies and enhanced sepsis management, were widely adopted during the study period [25]. In parallel, the development of paediatric‐specific clinical protocols, specialised training programs and quality improvement initiatives likely promoted standardisation of care and reduced the incidence of preventable complications [26].

The decline in paediatric ICU mortality may also be partly attributable to expanded access to specialised critical care services, as reflected by the 34% increase in ICU bed capacity the study period. This expansion may have reduced delays in critical care delivery, delays that have been previously associated with increased mortality in both paediatric and adult populations [27]. However, it is important to note that changes in admission criteria and case mix over time may partially explain the observed mortality trends. With expanded ICU capacity and potentially lower thresholds for ICU admission, the average severity of illness among admitted patients may have decreased over time, contributing to improved survival rates. Further research incorporating illness severity scores would be valuable to adjust for this potential confounding factor.

In addition, changes in paediatric palliative care availability and end‐of‐life decision‐making may influence measured PICU mortality trends by altering admission thresholds and the location of death (PICU vs. ward). In Israel, paediatric practice patterns differ from many adult contexts: children receiving palliative‐focused care and those approaching end of life, if hospitalisation is required, are commonly admitted to the PICU rather than being managed and dying on general paediatric wards. Haematology‐oncology patients are a recognised exception in which end‐of‐life care may occur in specialised hemato‐oncology settings; this practice pattern did not materially change during the study period. However, because palliative care involvement and limitation‐of‐therapy decisions are not captured in the administrative dataset, the contribution of evolving palliative care practices to temporal trends in unadjusted PICU mortality cannot be quantified.

Age‐Specific Vulnerabilities and Implications

Our findings reinforce that hospitalised infants under 1 year of age constitute a particularly vulnerable subgroup, exhibiting consistently higher ICU admission rates across all time periods (6.1% overall, compared to 3.9%–4.2% in older paediatric age groups). This pattern aligns with previous studies demonstrating infants' increased susceptibility to critical illness, attributed to their immature immune systems, limited physiologic reserves and distinct pathophysiologic responses to illness and injury [13].

The markedly greater relative increase in ICU utilisation among children aged 1–4 years (90.3% from 1999–2003 to 2019–2023), compared to infants (66.7%), is noteworthy and may reflect evolving patterns of childhood illness or shifting ICU admission criteria for this age group. This trend warrants further investigation, particularly in light of potential preventive opportunities through vaccination programs, injury prevention efforts and early management of chronic conditions [28]. The consistently higher ICU utilisation rates across all paediatric age groups in recent years compared to earlier periods of the study emphasise the need for continued investment in paediatric critical care resources and specialised training across the developmental spectrum. Age‐specific clinical guidelines and quality metrics may be warranted to address the unique needs of different paediatric populations [5].

Long‐Term Outcomes and Rehospitalisation Patterns

Our analysis of rehospitalisation patterns reveals concerning disparities between children with and without ICU admission. One year after discharge, rehospitalisation rates were substantially higher among children who had an ICU stay (46% vs. 29%), suggesting that critical illness in childhood may have enduring effects that extend beyond the index hospitalisation. Particularly striking is that 20% of these children required subsequent ICU admission within 1 year, compared to just 3% of those initially hospitalised outside the ICU.

These findings align with emerging literature on post‐intensive care syndrome in paediatrics (PICS‐p), which encompasses a new or worsening physical, cognitive, emotional and social morbidities that persist after critical illness [29]. The high rate of subsequent ICU admissions suggests that many critically ill children either have underlying vulnerabilities that predispose them to recurrent critical illness or develop new vulnerabilities because of their ICU stay [30].

The transient trend, although not reaching statistical significance, in 1‐year post‐discharge mortality among ICU patients, from 3.2% in 1999–2003 to 3.7% in 2004–2008 before subsequently declining, warrants further investigation. This temporary rise may reflect changes in discharge practices during that period, with sicker children potentially being discharged earlier or it could be related to evolving availability and quality of post‐discharge care services [31]. These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive discharge planning and robust post‐discharge follow‐up programs for children who have experienced critical illness. Dedicated post‐ICU follow‐up clinics, integration with community paediatric services and long‐term developmental monitoring may be necessary to address the ongoing needs of this vulnerable population [18].

Demographic and Clinical Risk Factors

Our analysis identified several key demographic and clinical factors associated with ICU utilisation. Our findings show that a substantial proportion of PICU admissions occur in the postoperative setting following major surgery (9.6% vs. 3.4% for non‐surgical patients), underscoring the continued importance of postoperative monitoring capacity and perioperative planning for children undergoing complex operations [32]. However, because our analysis does not further stratify procedures by operative category, urgency, or complexity within the ‘major surgery’ group, these data should be interpreted as describing utilisation patterns associated with major surgical pathways rather than quantifying the physiologic impact of surgical trauma per se. Nevertheless, this finding has important implications for surgical planning, resource allocation and the development of enhanced recovery pathways specifically designed for paediatric surgical patients [33].

Although minimal, the observed gender difference in ICU utilisation—4.6% for males versus 4.4% for females—is consistent with prior studies suggesting increased vulnerability to certain critical illnesses among male children [34]. This disparity may be influenced by a combination of biological factors, including sex‐based differences in immune response and hormonal regulation, as well as behavioural patterns that increase injury risk among males [35]. The persistently higher age‐adjusted mortality rates for males throughout the study period further supports this gender‐based vulnerability.

The strong association between hospital lengths of stay and underscores the need for heightened clinical vigilance in children with prolonged hospitalisations. The sharp rise in ICU admission rates, from 1.5%–1.9% for stays of 0–2 days to 43.9% for stays ≥ 15 days, likely reflects both the complexity of underlying illness and the accumulating risk of clinical deterioration over time [36].

This finding emphasises the importance of proactive monitoring systems and escalation protocols for children with extended hospital stays [37].

Regional Variations and Health System Implications

The documented regional disparities in paediatric ICU bed capacity raise important questions about equity of access to critical care services across Israel. Across the study period (1999–2023), the national paediatric ICU bed‐to‐population ratio increased from 4.56 to 6.13 beds per 100 000 children, yet meaningful regional variation persisted throughout. In 2023, there was an approximately 2.6‐fold difference between the highest (Tel Aviv: 11.43 beds per 100 000 children) and lowest (South: 4.33 beds per 100 000 children) regional ratios (Figure 3). Such disparities may translate into different local thresholds for ICU admission, affect the timing and feasibility of inter‐hospital transfers, and contribute to delayed access to critical care for children in regions with relatively lower capacity [38, 39].

Interpretation of ‘regional need’ should also consider that Israel's paediatric referral landscape is not uniformly distributed. Several regions host referral centres for a variety of specialised medical and surgical services, which can concentrate higher‐acuity case‐mix and postoperative or procedure‐related demand within specific hospitals and catchment areas. Accordingly, bed‐to‐population ratios may reflect not only residential population distribution but also patterns of regional and inter‐regional referral, and the true demand for PICU resources may vary depending on the location of these referral services and evolving clinical pathways over time.

At the same time, Israel's geography is relatively compact and travel distances between regions are often short; in practice, transferring a child across district boundaries may at times be easier than travelling across a large district from one edge to another. This has two implications: first, ‘within‐region’ bed counts may underestimate functional access when cross‐region care is routine; and second, capacity planning should be informed by actual referral flows and transport logistics rather than administrative borders alone. Even in this setting, persistent bed‐capacity differences remain relevant because they can influence local admission thresholds, the availability of immediate beds during surges and the resilience of the system under stress.

These findings highlight the need for national strategic planning for paediatric critical care resources that balances centralisation of highly specialised services with equitable geographic access [40]. Alternative models such as hub‐and‐spoke systems, telemedicine support for community hospitals and enhanced transportation networks may help bridge regional gaps while maintaining quality of care [41].

Impact of the COVID‐19 Pandemic

The marked rise in ICU utilisation and age‐adjusted mortality rates between 2020 and 2022 aligns with the COVID‐19 pandemic and represents one of the most significant disruptions to paediatric healthcare delivery in recent history. Although the direct burden of COVID‐19 infection was generally milder in children compared to adults [42], our findings indicate substantial strain on paediatric critical care services during this period. Several factors may account for this trend. Direct effects of COVID‐19, including complications such as MIS‐C, created new demands for critical care [43]. Indirect effects, such as delayed care‐seeking for common paediatric conditions, may have led to more severe illness at presentation [44]. Additionally, resource constraints, including staff shortages and the repurposing of paediatric facilities for adult patients, likely further compromised care delivery during pandemic peaks [17]. Notably, ICU utilisation remained elevated in 2023 (6.5%), even after the acute phase of the pandemic, suggesting possible enduring shifts in disease patterns or healthcare delivery. These findings underscore the need for continued surveillance and research into the long‐term consequences of the pandemic on child health and healthcare systems [45, 46].

Strengths and Limitations

This study offers several notable strengths, including its comprehensive national scope, a 25‐year longitudinal design and the integration of diverse healthcare utilisation and outcome metrics. These features provide a robust foundation for examining trends in paediatric critical care at a population level.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the reliance on administrative data limits our ability to assess illness severity, an important contextual factor for interpreting trends in ICU utilisation and outcomes. Second, changes in diagnostic coding, admission criteria and healthcare delivery models over the study period may affect the consistency and comparability of data across time. Third, we lacked longitudinal national data on PICU physician and nursing workforce size and therefore could not evaluate whether workforce growth paralleled bed‐capacity expansion. Future analyses incorporating unit‐level staffing metrics alongside case mix and outcomes are needed to contextualise national trends. Follow‐up Year rehospitalisations and mortality was limited to 1999–2022 since data for 2024 was not yet complete.

In addition, while we characterised patterns in resource use and mortality, the dataset lacks information on survivors' quality of life, functional status and developmental outcomes, critical dimensions of paediatric critical care that remain underexplored. Finally, we did not evaluate the role of socioeconomic factors, which are likely to influence both access to and outcomes of critical care services.

Conclusions

This comprehensive national study documents substantial changes in paediatric critical care in Israel over the past 25 years, marked by increased ICU utilisation and significant reductions in mortality. While these improvements reflect advancements in clinical care, technology and system capacity, ongoing challenges remain, among them high rates of post‐discharge rehospitalisation and pronounced regional disparities in ICU resources.

To address these challenges, future research should explore the underlying drivers of increased ICU admissions, distinguishing between changing disease epidemiology and evolving admission practices. There is also a pressing need for strategies to support children and families following critical illness, including enhanced transitional care, multidisciplinary follow‐up and targeted interventions for those at highest risk of recurrent ICU admission. Reducing regional inequities will require coordinated national planning to ensure equitable access to high‐quality paediatric critical care across all districts.

From a policy perspective, investment in paediatric ICU capacity must be matched by efforts to standardise care quality and strengthen the interface between acute and community‐based services. Development and implementation of national quality metrics specific to paediatric critical care could facilitate benchmarking and continuous improvement. Finally, lessons from the COVID‐19 pandemic underscore the importance of resilience and adaptability within healthcare systems to ensure continuity of paediatric critical care during future public health emergencies.
