Knowledge, attitudes, and practices of cervical cancer preventive services among mothers living with HIV in Lagos, Nigeria: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mengmeng Jia, Agatha Wapmuk, Titilola Gbaja-Biamila, Oba A Abudulrasheed, Akeem Blessing Lateef, Joseph D Tucker, Oliver Chukwujekwu Ezechi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen living with HIV are at higher risk of cervical cancer, and their cervical cancer preventive behaviors may influence those of their daughters. Women’s preventive behaviors may cluster within families. This study was conducted to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of cervical cancer prevention services among mothers living with HIV (MLWH) in Lagos, Nigeria.MethodsIn this cross-sectional descriptive study conducted between June 8 and July 14, 2023, mothers aged 25–65 years who had at least one daughter younger than 26 years and were attending the Nigerian Institute of Medical Research-AIDS Prevention Initiative Nigeria (NIMR-APIN) clinic for HIV services were included by convenience sampling method. Data were collected through interviews conducted by trained research assistants. Participants were categorized into no, poor, fair, and good knowledge level groups.ResultsOf the 507 participants surveyed, 133/507 (26.2%) reported an average monthly income lower than 18,000 Naira (23.7 USD). A total of 133/507 (26.2%) MLWH had ever undergone cervical cancer screening, including three who had also received HPV vaccination. Three mothers had their daughters vaccinated. 350 (69.0%) of MLWH were aware of cervical cancer, 290 participants (57.2%) had knowledge of cervical cancer screening, and only 29/507 (5.7%) participants were informed about the HPV vaccine. Mothers who were reluctant (adjusted OR = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.06–0.55) or hesitant (adjusted OR = 0.06, 95% CI: 0.01–0.28) to undergo screening themselves showed significantly lower odds of expressing willingness to vaccinate their daughters.ConclusionsThis study revealed that only one-quarter of MLWH had ever been screened, underscoring the urgent need for enhanced cervical cancer screening in this group. Given the observed association between mothers’ screening willingness and their intent to vaccinate their daughters, targeted dyadic interventions that combine education, counseling, and facilitated access to screening and vaccination services may help enhance both maternal screening uptake and HPV vaccination coverage among their daughters.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27320-5.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Preventive services, Mother, HIV

Background

Cervical cancer is caused by a persistent infection of high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV). It ranks as the second most prevalent cancer among women in Nigeria, with an estimated 13,676 new cases and 7,093 deaths in 2022 [1]. Unfortunately, most cervical cancer cases in Nigeria are detected at later stages [2].

Women living with HIV (WLWH) experience a higher prevalence of HPV infection and are more likely to develop persistent infections that progress to cervical cancer, resulting in a substantially increased cancer risk compared to women without HIV [3–6]. Notably, approximately 25% of cervical cancer cases occur in WLWH, and 20.4% of the cases were attributable to HIV, with most of them occurring in women under age 45 in Africa [7]. Nigeria bears a substantial HIV burden, with approximately two million adults living with HIV and an estimated HIV prevalence of 1.5% among women aged 15–49 years in 2024 [8–10]. Given their substantially elevated cervical cancer risk, concentration within reproductive-age women, and regular engagement with HIV care services, WLWH represent a strategically important subgroup for national cervical cancer elimination efforts.

However, cervical cancer is preventable and curable if detected early. Vaccination against HPV, screening, and treatment of pre-cancer lesions are cost-effective ways to prevent cervical cancer, even in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) with limited health resources [10, 11]. Cervical cancer incidence and mortality rates have declined precipitously over the past half-century in countries with routine, effective cervical cancer screening and early detection programs [12]. Despite the WHO and the Nigerian Society of Obstetrics and Gynaecology calls for routine HPV screening among WLWH [13, 14], Nigeria lacks a national screening program, and screening coverage among women is estimated at only 8.7% [15–17]. It is reported that WLWH represented around 2% of the population of women being screened for cervical cancer, and WLWH had increased odds of follow-up screening after the first findings of a routine pap smear [18]. Barriers to cervical screening in Nigerian women include lack of knowledge, health services factors, belief that screening is unnecessary, fear of outcome and procedure, and financial constraints [19]. WLWH often experience additional barriers such as stigma and competing health priorities [20].

Cervical cancer preventive behaviors cluster within families. Mothers’ screening experiences may influence their daughters’ HPV vaccination uptake by shaping risk perception, preventive attitudes, and engagement with healthcare services, while daughters’ vaccination may in turn promote screening behaviors among mothers [21–24]. In Nigeria, mothers and other female caregivers play a central role in communication and decision-making regarding sexual and reproductive health [25]. However, mothers of in-school adolescents in Nigeria showed poor knowledge of HPV infection and vaccination, but 65.8% were willing to vaccinate their adolescents against HPV [26].

Given that lack of awareness/knowledge is a commonly cited reason for the non-uptake of preventive measures against cervical cancer, the knowledge and attitudes towards cervical cancer prevention among mothers living with HIV (MLWH) will not only affect the uptake of screening in mothers themselves, but also the uptake of vaccination in their daughters [19, 27, 28]. However, there is a paucity of data on this particular population. Thus, this study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and uptake of cervical cancer preventive services among MLWH in Lagos, Nigeria.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted at the HIV clinic of the Nigerian Institute of Medical Research (NIMR) between June 8 and July 14, 2023. This HIV clinic is one of the largest HIV prevention, treatment, and care centers in Nigeria. It provides comprehensive HIV care and treatment services to adults, pregnant women, children, and adolescents living with HIV.

Study population

Women living with HIV and visiting the HIV clinic at NIMR regularly, aged 25–65 years, who have a daughter aged 26 years or below, and who were willing to participate, were eligible for this study. Women who were severely ill, could not finish the survey, or underwent a hysterectomy were not eligible for this study.

Procedure

The questionnaire was designed and pretested in 25 women to assess comprehensibility, appropriateness of language, sensitivity of questions, and the duration of administration. The questionnaire was modified and finalized based on feedback from the pretest. An additional file shows the details of the questionnaire. (Additional file 1: Questionnaire).

Female research assistants who completed tertiary education and spoke English as well as one of the local dialects were trained before the survey. In the one-day training, the inclusion and exclusion criteria, informed consent process, and interpretation of each item and option were explained in detail.

A convenience sampling method was used to recruit participants, which may limit the representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of the findings. All eligible women were invited, and those who agreed to participate were interviewed in a private space near the clinic. For those who did not speak English, the interview was conducted in their local dialect by native-speaking research assistants. Information on socio-demographic characteristics, reproductive characteristics, date of diagnosis of HIV infection, knowledge of cervical cancer prevention, uptake of cervical cancer preventive services, and their willingness to undergo cervical cancer screening was collected.

Completed questionnaires were reviewed on-site for completeness and logical consistency, and any issues were addressed immediately with participants. Consequently, the final dataset contained no missing values, and all enrolled participants were included in the analyses.

Scoring

The level of knowledge was assessed using a series of 12 items, some of which had multiple correct answers, yielding a total of 32 correct responses. Each correct response was assigned a score of 1. In contrast, an incorrect answer or a “don’t know” response received a score of 0. For example, if a participant identified vaccination as a preventive measure for cervical cancer, this resulted in a score of “1” for that specific question. Conversely, a participant who recognizes vaccination, screening, and the avoidance of multiple sexual partners as preventive measures attained a score of “3” for the same question. These individual scores were then aggregated to establish a comprehensive threshold for the level of knowledge regarding cervical cancer. In the absence of a universally accepted standard for cervical cancer knowledge categorization, percentage-based cut-offs commonly used in KAP studies and informed by modified Bloom’s cut-off points were applied: < 50% (poor, scores < 5), 50–74% (fair, scores 5—7), and ≥ 75% (good, scores ≥ 8). The practice of cervical cancer prevention among MLWH was assessed by their history of cervical cancer vaccination and screening for themselves, as well as vaccination for their daughters. The willingness towards undergoing preventive services was assessed by answering “Yes” to the corresponding questions.

Data analysis

Normally distributed and skewed continuous variables were expressed as mean with standard deviation (SD) and median with interquartile range (IQR), respectively. Frequency and percentage (%) were used to describe category variables. Bivariate analysis was done using Student’s t-test (or Mann–Whitney U test) and Pearson’s Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Demographic variables (age, tribe, religion, marital status, fertility history, education, employment status, income level) and purposefully selected variables (duration of HIV infection and treatment, uptake of screening) that were significant with p < 0.10 in univariate logistic regressions, were included in the multivariable logistic regression analyses. Data were double-entered and compared using SPSS (version 26, IBM). All analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc.).

Results

Socio-demographic information of participants

A total of 507 MLWH were included in our study. The age distribution was primarily within the 35–44-year (38.4%) and 45–54-year (42.1%) brackets, with a median age of 45 (IQR: 40, 50). Most women were Christian (N = 436, 86.0%). Three hundred forty-one (341) women (67.3%) were married, and more than 52% (N = 267) were taking care of one daughter or female dependent. Three hundred forty-four (344) participants (67.9%) were self-employed, and more than one-fourth had an average monthly income of less than 18,000 Naira (approximately 23.7 USD). Almost 90% of them were diagnosed with HIV infection at least five years ago.

Among all participants, 350 (69%) women had some awareness of cervical cancer. The main sources of information were doctors (n = 107), counselors (n = 107), and nurses (n = 88). A higher proportion of women who have primary education or less (28.0% vs. 10.9%), those who are self-employed (79.0% vs. 62.9%), and those earning an average monthly income lower than 25,000 Naira (34.4% vs. 22.6%) were found in those who never heard about cervical cancer compared to their counterparts who did (Table 1).

Knowledge about cervical cancer and its prevention

Among those 350 women who were aware of cervical cancer, 152 (43.4%) knew that the virus is the cause of cervical cancer, 217 women (62.0%) were knowledgeable about at least one of the risk factors for cervical cancer, with having multiple sexual partners being the most commonly recognized. Furthermore, 275 (61.7%) participants knew at least one way to prevent cervical cancer, with screening being the most frequently cited method (Table 2, Additional file 2. sTable 1). Fifty-five participants had heard about HPV, and only 29 participants had heard about the HPV vaccine.

In general, 162 (32.0%) have no knowledge about cervical cancer and its prevention at all. Based on our evaluation criteria, 139 (27.4%) MLWH had poor knowledge, 109 (21.5%) had fair knowledge, and 97 (19.1%) had relatively good knowledge (Table 2).

Uptake and attitude towards cervical cancer preventive services for MLWH themselves

Three participants had been vaccinated against HPV, and 133 (26.2%) had ever been screened, including the three vaccinated individuals. Of the 133, 95 had only been screened once, with only 16 having been screened within the past year. Pap smear was the most widely used HPV screening method (n = 89) (Table 3). Among those who never had screening before, the most cited reasons were no awareness of cervical cancer screening, followed by the high cost of the test, not knowing where to get screening done, and fear of the test results (Fig. 1). Older women who had a longer duration of HIV infection, and had higher levels of knowledge were associated with the history of cervical cancer screening in the multivariable regression (Table 4).

A vast majority of participants, 483 (95.3%), expressed a desire to undergo screening in the future, while 8 (1.6%) were unsure whether they wanted to be screened. When it came to payment for the screening, 80% of the women were unwilling to pay any fee, and only 17 were willing to pay more than 5,000 Naira (approximately 6.6 USD). Most of them (N = 352, 71.7%) preferred their sample to be collected by clinicians. 168 (34.2%) of them preferred a Pap smear, while 155 (31.6%) of them were undecided about their preferred test (Table 3). The primary motivation for wanting to undergo screening was the desire to avoid cervical cancer (n = 380, Fig. 2), whereas the leading reason for avoidance was that they were not engaging in risky sexual behaviors (n = 7).

Uptake and attitude of HPV vaccination for the daughters of MLWH

Only three of them vaccinated their daughters against HPV. Three women were unsure whether their daughters had been vaccinated.

Four hundred fifty-nine (90.5%) wanted to vaccinate their daughters in the future, with 111 of them wanting to get it for free. However, 48 women hesitated or declined to vaccinate their daughters. The main reason for hesitation or unwillingness was a lack of awareness of the HPV vaccine (n = 13) (Table 3).

Mothers who were reluctant (OR = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.06–0.55) or hesitant (OR = 0.06, 95% CI: 0.01–0.28) to get themselves screened showed lower odds of expressing willingness to vaccinate their daughters (Table 5). Women aged 35–44 years had a higher willingness to vaccinate their daughters than those aged 25–34 years (OR = 3.80, 95% CI: 1.31–10.98). Compared to those with an income level of 18,000–35,000 Naira, those with an income of more than 50,000 Naira had a higher willingness to vaccinate their daughters (OR = 3.98, 95% CI: 1.27–12.47).

Discussion

This study revealed that only 26% of MLWH had undergone cervical cancer screening, and HPV vaccination rates (3/507) were remarkably low in Lagos, Nigeria. The uptake history was associated with their age, duration of HIV infection, and their knowledge level. Despite the low uptake rate of cervical cancer preventive services, there was a high level of expressed interest in future screening for themselves and vaccination for their daughters. Their willingness to be screened in the future was associated with their desire to vaccinate their daughters. Our study expands the literature by focusing on a Nigerian context with a high burden of cervical cancer, obtaining data about MLWH’s practice, knowledge, and attitude towards cervical cancer preventive services.

Our study reveals low uptake of cervical cancer preventive services among WLWH in Lagos. Similar low uptake of cervical cancer screening among WLWH has been reported in other African and LMIC settings, ranging from 20% to 35% among WLWH, despite higher perceived risk in this population [29, 30]. Our findings are consistent with these reports and further highlight persistent gaps in access and utilization of preventive services among WLWH in West Africa. The limited HPV knowledge, low willingness to pay, stigma and discrimination related to HIV, and healthcare system barriers [15] may contribute to the low uptake rates. Strategies to bridge this gap include integrating cervical cancer screening into existing HIV services, which have been proven to be feasible and effective [31–33]. In resource-limited settings, this can be operationalized through task-shifting to nurses or community health workers, self-sampling for HPV testing, and leveraging HIV clinic infrastructure for counseling and follow-up. These approaches can expand coverage, reduce clinic workload, and increase uptake among high-risk populations while remaining practical and cost-effective.

Although HPV testing is recommended as the primary screening strategy [13, 14], the Pap smear was the most widely used (66.9%) and preferred (34.2%) screening strategy in our study. It is worth noting that there are still 20–30% of MLWH who indicated that they did not have a preferred screening test or methods for specimen collection. That may be due to a lack of awareness or confidence in collecting samples themselves. Some studies have already demonstrated the feasibility of using self-sampled HPV testing with colposcopy immediate management of WLWH in Nigeria [34]. However, currently, few African countries offer HPV screening because of the relatively high cost, shortage of trained healthcare workers, and limited laboratory infrastructure. With the widespread adoption of HPV screening as the primary modality for cervical cancer screening, it is imperative for the government to identify innovative financing and procurement mechanisms to improve the affordability and availability of HPV testing, especially the awareness and acceptability of self-collection among WLWH, which could significantly reduce barriers such as geographic inaccessibility, lack of healthcare workforce, and sociocultural issues [12].

Our data suggest low HPV vaccination rates among WLWH in Nigeria. However, since our study, Nigeria launched a nationwide HPV vaccine program [35], decreasing financial barriers that could prevent young girls from getting vaccinated. This initiative mirrors the successful model observed in Rwanda, the first African country to implement a national HPV vaccination program. Rwanda’s program has been remarkably effective, achieving about 98% coverage among the eligible population between 2011 and 2018, and has witnessed a dramatic decrease in the prevalence of vaccine-targeted HPV [36, 37]. However, the low level of HPV vaccine knowledge observed among mothers is particularly concerning. Limited knowledge may undermine the effectiveness of national vaccination programs by reducing demand, delaying uptake, and increasing susceptibility to misinformation. Targeted educational interventions addressing HPV-related disease burden, vaccine safety, and benefits are therefore essential to maximize the impact of HPV vaccination programs.

Mothers play a pivotal role in their daughters’ health decisions, especially regarding preventive measures against reproductive diseases [25]. Our findings indicate that mothers’ willingness to get screened is associated with their willingness to vaccinate their daughters against HPV. In addition, mothers’ screening histories are also associated with daughters’ likelihood of HPV vaccination across ethnic and socioeconomic strata [21, 38]. This connection underscores the importance of educating and engaging mothers to promote HPV vaccination.

This is the first study to evaluate the uptake and attitudes towards cervical cancer preventive services, not only for MLWH themselves but also for their daughters. Through interviews conducted by our investigators, we successfully recruited illiterate participants, thereby reducing potential selection biases. However, there are several limitations. First, the study utilized a convenience sample from a single clinic in Lagos. Thus, the findings may not be representative of all the mothers living with HIV in Nigeria or other similar settings. Women who attend the HIV clinic of NIMR may differ from those receiving care elsewhere and those who were not on treatment in terms of socioeconomic status, health-seeking behaviors, and access to preventive services. This limited the generalizability of the findings and introduced potential selection bias. Second, we did not sample women who were not on ART or lost to care. These women may have lower rates of cervical cancer screening. Their exclusion may therefore bias the sample toward women with better engagement in healthcare, potentially leading to higher observed screening uptake and willingness than in the broader population of WLWH. Third, all data were based on self-reports, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly regarding sensitive questions such as screening and vaccination behaviors. Social desirability bias may lead to an overestimation of reported willingness toward future screening and HPV vaccination, as participants may have provided responses perceived as socially or medically acceptable. In addition, expressed willingness to undergo future screening or vaccinate their daughters may not accurately reflect actual behavior.

Conclusion

This study revealed that only approximately one-quarter of mothers living with HIV had ever undergone cervical cancer screening, underscoring the urgent need to strengthen cervical cancer prevention services for this high-risk population. Integrating cervical cancer education, HPV screening, and vaccination counseling into routine HIV care may offer a pragmatic and sustainable approach to improving both maternal screening uptake and HPV vaccination among daughters. Given the strong association between mothers’ screening willingness and their willingness to vaccinate their daughters, HIV clinic visits represent a critical opportunity for dyadic, family-centered interventions to reduce cervical cancer burden in Nigeria. These findings support efforts to achieve the WHO 90–70–90 cervical cancer elimination targets by 2030.
