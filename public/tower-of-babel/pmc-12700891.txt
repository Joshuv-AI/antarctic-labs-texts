Pre and postharvest application of regulators enhances bell pepper quality and antioxidant levels during cold storage
Authors: Mozhgan RaeesiNejad, Hamed Kaveh, Hassan Feizi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-harvest quality maintenance of bell peppers (Capsicum annuum L.) presents significant challenges in commercial agriculture due to their high perishability and sensitivity to cold storage conditions. While ethylene regulators show promise in extending storage life, the optimal timing and method of application remain unclear. To evaluate the effectiveness of two ethylene regulators (1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP) and sodium nitroprusside (SNP) as a nitric oxide donor) applied at different developmental stages on quality retention and antioxidant preservation in bell peppers during cold storage. Green Bianca and Red California Wonder bell pepper cultivars were treated with either 1 µmol L-1 1-MCP or 5 µmol L-1 SNP at three timing intervals: pre-harvest, post-harvest, or both. Following a factorial design with three replications, fruits were stored at 7 ± 0.5 °C with 80–85% relative humidity for 28 days. Comprehensive analysis included physical attributes (firmness, weight loss, shape index), chemical properties (pH, titratable acidity, soluble solids), bioactive compounds (vitamin C, phenolics, flavonoids), pigments (chlorophylls, carotenoids, lycopene), and physiological responses (chilling injury, ion leakage, antioxidant capacity). Pre-harvest application of SNP proved most effective for both cultivars, with Green Bianca showing superior retention of vitamin C (73.19 mg100g-1), antioxidant capacity (61.06%), and minimal weight loss (0.19%). Red California Wonder demonstrated optimal response to pre-harvest NO treatment, maintaining high antioxidant activity (45.16%) and vitamin C content (99.92 mg100g-1). Treatment timing significantly influenced effectiveness, with pre-harvest applications showing particular efficacy in maintaining membrane integrity and reducing chilling injury while preserving bioactive compounds. This study establishes the timing-dependent efficacy of ethylene regulators in bell pepper preservation and provides valuable insights for developing cultivar-specific post-harvest protocols. Introduction

Post-harvest quality maintenance of horticultural produce has become increasingly critical due to growing global health consciousness and consumer demand for nutritious foods. Epidemiological studies consistently demonstrate that higher consumption of fruits and vegetables correlates with reduced mortality from cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and other chronic conditions, while also slowing aging processes. This protective effect stems from their abundant essential compounds, including vitamins, minerals, phenolic compounds, natural antioxidants, fiber, and other bioactive constituents1.

Bell pepper (Capsicum annuum L.), a member of the Solanaceae family, represents one of humanity’s oldest domesticated crops, originating in the Western Hemisphere and now cultivated worldwide2. While technically an annual warm-season crop, it can exhibit perennial characteristics in temperate climates. Bell peppers have gained particular prominence in modern diets due to their exceptional combination of nutritional value, color diversity, and flavor profiles. Their rich content of bioactive compounds and potent antioxidant properties makes them one of the most widely consumed fresh vegetables globally3.

The nutritional profile of bell peppers is particularly noteworthy for its high ascorbic acid content and diverse antioxidant compounds, which play crucial roles in human nutrition and physiological functioning4. They contain significant quantities of neutral phenolic compounds and flavonoids, including quercetin, luteolin, and capsaicin. These bioactive compounds exhibit various medicinal properties, including anti-diarrheal, antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-diabetic, and anti-mutagenic activities5. Additionally, carotenoids, responsible for the yellow, orange, and red hues in different varieties, serve not only as pigments but also as crucial antioxidants6–8.

Consumer attention increasingly focuses on both external and internal quality attributes of vegetables. While appearance quality encompassing shape, color, size, freshness, and texture drives initial marketability, internal quality factors such as nutritional content and health benefits have gained equal importance. Modern quality assessment considers both visual attributes and nutritional value, including vitamins, minerals, carbohydrates, fiber, and bioactive compounds9.

Bell peppers face significant post-harvest challenges like other horticultural produces with high perishability results in substantial losses due to physiological deterioration, dehydration, nutrient depletion, nutritional degradation, microbial infections, and physiological disorders such as chilling injury (CI) during storage10. CI is a temperature-induced stress response that occurs when bell peppers are exposed to sub-optimal cold storage conditions (typically below 7–10 °C), leading to symptoms such as surface pitting, calyx browning, increased electrolyte leakage, and heightened susceptibility to fungal pathogens11. These injuries compromise both visual appeal and nutritional quality, especially in sensitive cultivars. Recent studies indicate that post-harvest losses may exceed 10% in developed countries and reach up to 50% in tropical regions with inadequate cold chain infrastructure12. The extent and nature of such losses vary depending on storage parameters, including temperature regimes, atmospheric composition, ethylene levels, and hygiene practices13. Therefore, evaluating chilling injury alongside other quality-related parameters such as pigment degradation, antioxidant retention, and bioactive compound preservation is essential for developing robust post-harvest management strategies. Ethylene, a key plant hormone regulating numerous developmental processes from seed germination to senescence, plays a crucial role in post-harvest deterioration. Bell peppers exhibit non-climacteric ethylene production during maturation and color development, producing very low levels (0.1–1.2 µl/kg hr at 10–20 °C)14. This presents both challenges and opportunities for post-harvest management through ethylene regulation.

Two promising compounds for post-harvest quality maintenance are 1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP) and nitric oxide (NO). 1-MCP functions as a competitive inhibitor of ethylene perception15,16, binding irreversibly to ethylene receptor sites and preventing subsequent signal transduction cascades. This inhibition of ethylene biosynthesis enzymes like ACO and ACS delays ripening and preserves fruit quality during storage17. Similarly, NO serves as a multifunctional signaling molecule involved in various plant processes, including senescence regulation, chlorophyll production, and fruit ripening18. Recent systematic analysis has demonstrated NO’s significant role in maintaining post-harvest quality through complex molecular mechanisms involving membrane transport, genetic translation, and metabolism pathway regulation19.

While ethylene regulators show promise in extending storage life, several critical questions remain unanswered. First, the optimal timing of application whether pre-harvest, post-harvest, or combined remains unclear and may vary by cultivar. Second, the interaction between ethylene regulation and pigment metabolism, particularly in cultivars with different color profiles, is poorly understood. Finally, the relationship between treatment timing and the preservation of bioactive compounds has not been systematically investigated. Understanding these relationships is crucial for developing effective, cultivar-specific post-harvest protocols.

Despite extensive research on these compounds in various fruits, their timing-dependent effects on bell pepper quality maintenance remain inadequately explored. Previous research has shown the potential benefits of preharvest applications of plant growth regulators in delaying fruit maturity20, suggesting the importance of application timing in post-harvest quality management. Given bell peppers’ increasing popularity and nutritional significance, developing effective post-harvest treatments is crucial. This study investigates the timing-dependent effects of 1-MCP and NO applications on quality retention and antioxidant preservation in two commercially important bell pepper cultivars with contrasting pigment profiles: Red California Wonder and Green Bianca. The research specifically aims to: (1) determine the optimal timing of ethylene regulator application for each cultivar; (2) evaluate the relationship between treatment timing and preservation of bioactive compounds; (3) investigate the cultivar-specific responses in pigment metabolism and quality retention; and (4) develop practical recommendations for commercial post-harvest management. By comprehensively analyzing physical attributes, chemical properties, bioactive compounds, and physiological responses during cold storage, this study seeks to establish evidence-based protocols for extending market life while maintaining nutritional quality.

Materials and methods

Plant material and experimental design

Bell pepper (Capsicum annuum L.) cultivars ‘Red California Wonder’ and ‘Green Bianca’ were sourced from a commercial greenhouse in Jiroft County, Kerman Province. A total of 720 fruits were used in the experiment, with 360 fruits per cultivar. Each treatment group consisted of 40 fruits (3 replications × 10 fruits per replication × 4 sampling times). Fruits were selected at commercial maturity stage with uniform size (80 ± 5 g weight, 8 ± 0.5 cm length) and color development (fully green for Green Bianca, fully red for Red California Wonder). The experiment followed a factorial arrangement in a completely randomized design with three replications, evaluating two ethylene regulators applied at different developmental stages.

Treatment applications and storage conditions

Ethylene regulator treatments consisted of 1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP, analytical grade, Sisco Research Laboratories, Pvt. Ltd.) at 1 µL L⁻¹ and sodium nitroprusside (SNP, analytical grade, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 5 µL L⁻¹, with untreated fruits serving as controls. Treatments were applied at three timing intervals: pre-harvest (one week before harvest), post-harvest, or combined pre/post-harvest application. For pre-harvest treatment, solutions were sprayed using a calibrated backpack sprayer (Model XF-16 A, 16 L capacity, working pressure 0.2–0.4 MPa) onto 40 plants per cultivar until run-off. Post-harvest applications involved immersing fruits in 1-MCP solution for 2 min or SNP solution for 10 min. Combined treatment received both applications as described.

Treatment concentrations and timings were determined through preliminary experiments (data not shown). In brief, concentrations of 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 µL L⁻¹ 1-MCP and 2.5, 5.0, and 7.5 µL L⁻¹ SNP were tested on both cultivars (n = 15 fruits per treatment) for 14 days storage. The 1.0 µL L⁻¹ 1-MCP and 5.0 µL L⁻¹ SNP concentrations showed optimal results in maintaining fruit quality while being cost-effective. Similarly, immersion times of 1, 2, and 3 min for 1-MCP and 5, 10, and 15 min for SNP were evaluated, with 2 min and 10 min respectively showing best results based on fruit firmness and weight loss measurements.

Following treatment, fruits were packed in conventional bell pepper containers and transferred to the Horticultural Sciences Laboratory at University of Torbat Heydarieh. Storage conditions were maintained at 7 ± 0.5 °C with 80–85% relative humidity using calibrated environmental chambers (Model KBF720, Binder GmbH, Germany) equipped with integrated temperature and humidity control systems. Quality assessments were performed at 7-day intervals over a 28-day storage period.

Although SNP (sodium nitroprusside) was effective in modulating postharvest physiology in this study, it must be emphasized that SNP is not approved for use in food products due to its potential toxicity and cyanide release. Therefore, its use is strictly for experimental purposes. Future research should explore food-safe NO or naturally derived NO-releasing compounds or direct NO fumigation methods to achieve similar benefits without safety concerns.

Trait measurements

While our study primarily focused on the application of ethylene inhibitors such as 1-MCP and nitric oxide (SNP) as regulatory treatments, direct measurement of endogenous ethylene production was not performed. Nonetheless, previous research has demonstrated that such treatments can effectively modulate ethylene biosynthesis and sensitivity pathways, leading to delayed ripening and reduced senescence in bell pepper and similar crops21,22. Future studies integrating ethylene gas quantification (e.g., via gas chromatography) would be valuable in further elucidating the mechanistic basis of the observed postharvest responses and in validating the practical efficacy of these treatments under commercial conditions.

Physiological responses

Weight loss percentage was monitored throughout storage using an analytical balance (Model GX-4000, A&D Co. Ltd., Japan, precision ± 0.01 g). Measurements were calculated using the equation: Weight loss% = [(Initial fruit weight - Fruit weight at sampling time)/Initial fruit weight] × 100.

Fruit firmness was assessed using a JY-3 model hand-held firmness tester (Shangqiu Sansi Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., China) equipped with an 8 mm probe. Measurements were taken from three points on each fruit and expressed in kg/cm². Fruit shape index was determined by measuring fruit length and diameter using an INSIZE digital caliper (Model 1108 − 150, accuracy ± 0.02 mm), calculating the ratio of fruit length to fruit diameter (see Supplementary Figure S1).

Chilling injury was evaluated by examining fruits for four typical symptoms: surface pitting, water soaking, calyx browning, and decay. Three trained evaluators independently assessed each fruit using a 5-point scale: 0 = no injury; 1 = slight (≤ 25% surface affected); 2 = moderate (26–50%); 3 = severe (51–75%); and 4 = extremely severe (> 75%). Chilling injury index was calculated using the formula: CI Index = Σ[(injury level) × (number of fruit at that level)]/(total number of fruit × maximum injury level) × 100.

Additionally, decay incidence was recorded separately by counting fruits showing any signs of fungal growth or deterioration, expressing results as percentage of total fruits per experimental unit.

Ion leakage, an indicator of membrane integrity, was measured using 2.5 cm diameter fruit peel discs. The discs were placed in containers with 20 mL distilled water, and initial electrical conductivity (EC1) was recorded after 24 h using a calibrated conductivity meter (Model DDS-307 A, Shanghai INESA Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., China, accuracy ± 1%). Samples were then incubated in a thermostatically controlled water bath (Model WNB22, Memmert GmbH, Germany) at 100 °C for 1 h. After cooling to room temperature, secondary electrical conductivity (EC2) was measured. Ion leakage percentage was calculated using the equation: Ion leakage % = (EC1 / EC2) × 100.

Fruit volume was determined using the water displacement method. Individual fruits were submerged in a graduated cylinder (2000 mL, accuracy ± 10 mL) containing a known volume of water, and the volume increase was recorded in milliliters (mL). Measurements were taken at each sampling interval to track changes during storage.

All physiological measurements were performed on three replicate samples per treatment combination at each sampling interval throughout the 28-day storage period. Visual assessments (chilling injury and decay) were conducted by trained evaluators to ensure consistency in scoring.

Chemical quality parameters

Chemical quality parameters included soluble solids content (SSC), titratable acidity (TA), and pH. SSC was measured by placing a few drops of filtered fruit juice on a hand-held refractometer (Model PAL-1, Atago Co., Ltd., Japan, range 0–53° Brix, resolution 0.1°) and recording the reading in degrees Brix. TA was determined through titration with 0.1 N sodium hydroxide until reaching pH 8.1–8.3, expressing results as percentage of citric acid according to the equation: TA = (0.064 × mL NaOH used) / mL sample × 100. Juice pH was measured directly using a calibrated digital pH meter (Model PH500C, accuracy ± 0.01). The maturity index was calculated as the SSC/TA ratio.

Bioactive compound analysis

Total phenolic content was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method. Fresh fruit tissue (5 g) was homogenized in 20 mL of ethanol: acetone (3:7 v/v) solution under liquid nitrogen. After 1-hour homogenization at room temperature, samples were filtered through Whatman No. 4 filter paper. The extract (200 µL) was diluted to 5 mL with distilled water, followed by addition of 500 µL Folin reagent (1:1 with water). After 1 min, 1500 µL sodium carbonate solution (20 g/100 mL) was added. Samples were incubated for 2 h in darkness at room temperature before measuring absorbance at 765 nm using a JENWAY 6300 spectrophotometer. Total phenolic content was calculated using a gallic acid standard curve (see supplementary Figure S2) and expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per 100 g fresh weight.

Vitamin C content was measured using the iodometric method. Fruit extract (20 mL) was combined with 150 mL distilled water and 1 mL starch indicator in a beaker. The solution was titrated with 0.005 M potassium iodide/potassium iodate solution until the first signs of color change (dark blue) appeared. Vitamin C content was calculated based on the volume of iodine solution consumed and expressed as mg/100 g fresh weight.

Antioxidant capacity and flavonoids

Antioxidant capacity was determined through DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging ability following Du et al. (2009) with modifications23. Extract (200 µL) was combined with 1800 µL DPPH solution (6.25 × 10⁻⁵ M) in Falcon tubes, shaken using a vortex mixer (Model VX-200, Labnet International, USA), and incubated for 30 min in darkness at room temperature. Absorbance was measured at 515 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (Model 6300, JENWAY, UK), and antioxidant capacity was calculated as percent DPPH inhibition using the equation: %DPPHsc = (ACONT-ASAMP)/ACONT×100, where ACONT represents DPPH absorbance and ASAMP represents sample plus DPPH absorbance.

All spectrophotometric measurements were performed in triplicate using the same spectrophotometer described above. Standard curves were prepared fresh for each analysis day, with R² values > 0.998 considered acceptable for quantification.

Total flavonoid content was measured by combining 150 µL of extract with 1700 µL of 30% ethanol, 75 µL of 0.5 M sodium nitrite (NaNO₂), and 75 µL of 0.3 M aluminum chloride (AlCl₃). After 5 min, 500 µL of 1 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was added and vortexed. Following 10–15 min incubation, absorbance was measured at 506 nm using the same spectrophotometer. Flavonoid concentration was calculated using a catechin standard curve (see supplementary Figure S3) and expressed as mg catechin equivalents per liter.

Pigment analysis

Chlorophyll and carotenoid contents were determined by grinding 0.5 g of fresh plant material to a fine powder using liquid nitrogen in a pre-chilled mortar and pestle. The powder was extracted with 20 mL of 80% acetone and centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 10 min using a refrigerated centrifuge (Model 5810 R, Eppendorf, Germany). The supernatant’s absorbance was measured at 663 nm for chlorophyll a, 645 nm for chlorophyll b, and 470 nm for carotenoids using the UV-visible spectrophotometer. Contents were calculated using the following equations:

Chlorophyll a = (19.3 × A663–0.86 × A645) V/100 W.

Chlorophyll b = (19.3 × A645–3.6 × A663) V/100 W.

Carotenoids = 100(A470) − 3.27(mg chl a) − 104(mg chl b)/227.

where V represents extract volume (mL) and W represents sample fresh weight (g).

Lycopene content was determined by extracting 2.5 g sample with 4 mL distilled water, stirring for 1 min, followed by addition of 50 mL hexane/acetone/absolute ethanol (2:1:1). After 10 min stirring and addition of 7.5 mL distilled water with 5 min further stirring, the absorbance of the organic phase was measured at 502 nm using the same spectrophotometer.

Results and discussion

Physical quality parameters and membrane integrity

The analysis of variance revealed that storage time, ethylene regulator treatments (NO and 1-MCP), and their interactions significantly affected the physical quality parameters and membrane integrity indicators in both Green Bianca and Red California Wonder bell pepper cultivars (see Supplementary Tables S1–S6 for detailed ANOVA results).

In both cultivars, weight loss, firmness, shape index, ion leakage, and chilling injury were highly responsive to treatment effects over time. Significant main effects of storage time (p ≤ 0.01) were observed for all traits, while the treatment effect was also highly significant (p ≤ 0.01) in nearly all cases. Moreover, the interaction between storage time and treatment (S × T) was significant for all parameters in both cultivars, highlighting the dynamic and time-dependent nature of treatment responses. In contrast, application timing alone and its higher-order interactions were generally non-significant, with a few exceptions at the p ≤ 0.05 level.

In Green Bianca, the main effects of storage time and treatment were highly significant (p ≤ 0.01) for all traits. The S × T interaction was also significant (p ≤ 0.01), indicating that treatment effects varied over time. The application timing (A) was significant only for weight loss (p ≤ 0.05), and non-significant for all other traits. Higher-order interactions were not significant.

In Red California Wonder, storage time, treatment, and their interaction (S × T) were again significant (p ≤ 0.01) for all traits. The application timing (A) was significant only for weight loss (p ≤ 0.05). No other interaction terms reached significance for the evaluated traits.

The consistent reduction in weight loss and chilling injury, coupled with the preservation of firmness and shape index, especially under NO and 1-MCP treatments, suggests that both compounds helped maintain membrane integrity and mitigate postharvest deterioration. Ion leakage patterns further supported these findings, showing lower electrolyte leakage in treated fruits compared to controls, particularly during the later stages of storage.

The strong correlations between physical parameters (see Supplementary Table S7) reflect interconnected cellular processes during storage. Weight loss triggers cell wall restructuring, leading to reduced firmness through altered pectin metabolism and cell wall hydration states. This relationship explains why both NO and 1-MCP treatments, which maintain membrane integrity, simultaneously preserve multiple quality attributes. The cultivar-specific responses suggest genetic differences in cell wall composition and metabolism affect treatment efficacy.

Overall, these results demonstrate the potential of ethylene regulators to improve the physical shelf-life of bell pepper fruits under cold storage, with cultivar-specific differences in sensitivity likely due to inherent differences in membrane composition, water loss regulation, and stress tolerance mechanisms.

Physical quality changes during storage

Both cultivars showed distinct responses to treatments throughout storage (Fig. 1). Weight loss exhibited highly significant treatment effects (p ≤ 0.01) and storage time interactions, with initial values below 1% progressively increasing during storage. Red California Wonder showed better response to 1-MCP treatment (T2 = 1.0 µL/L), maintaining minimal weight loss (0.22%) through day 7, while Green Bianca responded optimally to NO treatment with 0.59% loss under post-harvest application. Control fruits of both cultivars exhibited complete quality deterioration by day 28, demonstrating the critical role of ethylene regulation in moisture retention.

The strong negative correlation between weight loss and firmness (r = -0.89, p ≤ 0.01) reflects the interconnected nature of these quality parameters (see Supplementary Table S7). Initial firmness values (Red California Wonder: 8.33 kg/cm²; Green Bianca: 8.10 kg/cm²) were maintained better with NO treatment, showing cultivar-specific timing responses. These findings align with Li et al. (2024)24, who demonstrated that firmness reduction results from degradation of cell wall structures and middle lamella components, particularly involving decreases in hemicelluloses, cellulose, and pectin.

The effectiveness of NO treatment in maintaining membrane integrity can be attributed to three primary mechanisms: (1) direct scavenging of reactive oxygen species, (2) upregulation of antioxidant enzyme systems, and (3) stabilization of membrane lipids through interaction with membrane proteins. The cultivar-specific differences in treatment response suggest varying baseline levels of antioxidant systems and membrane composition between Green Bianca and Red California Wonder, with Green Bianca showing superior membrane stability under cold stress.

The strong correlation between ion leakage and chilling injury (r = 0.89) suggests membrane damage is a primary driver of cold-induced deterioration. NO treatment’s superior performance in preventing membrane damage likely stems from its dual role in both physical membrane stabilization and cellular stress response pathways. This finding has practical implications for cold storage protocols, suggesting that pre-storage NO treatment could allow for lower storage temperatures without increasing chilling injury risk.

The superior performance of NO treatment in maintaining firmness corresponds with Lai et al. (2011)’s findings that NO delays pectin degradation by suppressing cell wall-degrading enzyme expression25. Similarly, 1-MCP’s effectiveness supports Wei et al. (2021)’s observations that ethylene inhibition prevents respiratory increases and subsequent quality loss26. The cultivar-specific responses align with Blankenship and Dole (2003)’s findings regarding varietal differences in cell-to-cell adhesion and cellular compactness affecting treatment efficacy21.

Shape index showed significant treatment effects but less pronounced application timing influence. Red California Wonder maintained better morphological stability under pre/post-harvest combined application of 1-MCP (T2), while Green Bianca responded optimally to post-harvest treatment. The moderate correlation between shape index and firmness (r = 0.72, p ≤ 0.01) suggests that tissue integrity maintenance contributes to morphological stability.

The integrated analysis of physical quality parameters demonstrates that ethylene regulator treatments effectively maintain fruit structural integrity through multiple mechanisms. The strong correlations between weight loss, firmness, and shape index suggest that these parameters work in concert to determine overall fruit quality. The superior performance of NO treatment, particularly at pre-harvest stage for Green Bianca and post-harvest for Red California Wonder, indicates the importance of optimizing application timing based on cultivar characteristics. These findings have important implications for commercial postharvest management, suggesting that cultivar-specific treatment protocols could significantly extend marketable shelf life.

Membrane integrity and chilling response

Membrane integrity serves as a fundamental indicator of cellular health and stress tolerance in fresh produce. The maintenance of membrane stability directly influences various quality attributes including texture, nutrient retention, and resistance to pathogen invasion. In our study, ion leakage patterns showed strong correlation with chilling injury development (r = 0.89, p ≤ 0.01), reflecting their mechanistic relationship in stress response.

Ion leakage showed significant treatment (T) and storage time (S) effects (p ≤ 0.01), with a strong S × T interaction (Fig. 2). Starting from initial values of 15.92% and 20.64% for Red California Wonder and Green Bianca respectively, ion leakage increased significantly during storage. In Red California Wonder, maximum leakage (63.30%) occurred with pre-harvest treatment at day 28, while lowest leakage (17.21%) was maintained with NO treatment at day 21. Similarly, Green Bianca showed peak leakage (67.84%) under post-harvest application but maintained better membrane integrity (17.15%) with NO treatment at day 14.

The strong positive correlation between ion leakage and weight loss (r = 0.85, p ≤ 0.01) and negative correlation with firmness (r = -0.82, p ≤ 0.01) demonstrates the interconnected nature of membrane integrity and physical quality parameters (see Supplementary Table S7). These relationships support Yang et al. (2011)’s findings that membrane damage leads to increased cellular leakage and subsequent quality deterioration27.

NO treatment’s effectiveness in maintaining membrane integrity aligns with Wu et al. (2012)’s observations that NO reduces oxidative damage and maintains membrane stability28. The cultivar-specific responses and timing effects suggest that membrane protection mechanisms vary with genetic background and developmental stage, supporting Wang et al. (2022)’s findings regarding genotype-dependent stress responses29.

Chilling injury development showed significant treatment and timing effects. Starting from 0.00% at harvest, Red California Wonder reached maximum injury (40.48%) under pre-harvest treatment by day 28, while Green Bianca demonstrated better tolerance with peak injury of 36.77%. These patterns align with Zhang et al. (2020)’s research explaining how cold conditions trigger membrane lipid phase transitions affecting permeability30 while in the control we can see in the Fig. 2 that fruits totally being destroyed after 28 days under storage condition. This severe chilling injury in control fruits demonstrates that 7 °C storage is below the optimal temperature threshold for pepper varieties, confirming the necessity of protective treatments. The progressive deterioration observed validates our treatment approach as an effective strategy for maintaining fruit quality under cold storage conditions.

The differential effectiveness of treatments between cultivars supports Wang et al. (2022)’s findings regarding genetic influences on treatment efficacy29. The significant interaction between storage time and treatment indicates that timing of application is crucial for maximizing protective effects. These results suggest that optimizing treatment protocols requires consideration of both cultivar characteristics and application timing.

Chemical quality attributes and ripening indicators

Chemical quality parameters of bell pepper fruits including pH, titratable acidity (TA), soluble solids content (SSC), and the sugar: acid ratio were significantly influenced by storage time and ethylene regulator treatments in both Green Bianca and Red California Wonder cultivars (see Supplementary Tables S1–S6 for full ANOVA results).

In both cultivars, storage time (p ≤ 0.01) had a significant effect on all chemical attributes, reflecting ongoing ripening and senescence processes under cold storage. Ethylene regulator treatments (NO and 1-MCP) also showed highly significant effects (p ≤ 0.01) on pH, SSC, and TA. Furthermore, the interaction between storage time and treatment (S × T) was significant for all traits, indicating that treatment effectiveness varied over the storage duration.

In contrast, application timing (A) and its interactions were largely non-significant, except for a mild effect on pH in Green Bianca. These findings suggest that the timing of treatment application had limited influence on chemical quality retention under the experimental conditions.

Overall, the chemical quality attributes followed distinct trends in the two cultivars. In Red California Wonder, 1-MCP-treated fruits showed higher SSC and maintained more stable sugar: acid ratios, suggesting effective suppression of ethylene-driven ripening and metabolic activity. Conversely, Green Bianca responded more favorably to NO treatment, showing better retention of acidity and a slower rise in pH. These cultivar-specific responses highlight differences in ethylene sensitivity and nitric oxide signaling, likely stemming from underlying genetic variation in postharvest metabolism.

The elevated SSC levels observed in treated fruits likely resulted from reduced sugar catabolism rather than ongoing accumulation. This interpretation is supported by the sugar: acid ratio, which remained relatively stable in treated samples while increasing in untreated fruits. The combined effect of ethylene inhibition (by 1-MCP) and membrane protection (by NO) appears to preserve chemical quality by slowing down respiration, reducing acid loss, and maintaining solute compartmentalization during storage.

NO treatment was particularly effective for Red California Wonder, maintaining highest pH (5.06) during week 3, while 1-MCP proved optimal for Green Bianca, sustaining pH 6.46 through week 4.

Soluble solids content and sugar: acid ratio

SSC showed significant treatment effects with strong storage time interactions. Red California Wonder exhibited highest SSC (9.94 °Brix) at pre-harvest stage in week 1, declining to 6.17 °Brix with pre- and post-harvest treatment by week 4. Green Bianca showed peak SSC (7.11 °Brix) at pre- and post-harvest stages in week 2. 1-MCP treatment proved most effective for Red California Wonder, maintaining highest SSC (10.22 °Brix) in week 1, while control treatments showed complete quality loss (0.00 °Brix) by week 4 in both cultivars (Fig. 4).

Effect of ethylene regulator treatments and application timing on Soluble Solids Content (Right) and Sugar to acid ratio (%) (Left) of bell pepper cultivars during cold storage (28 days at 7 ± 0.5 °C). Vertical bars indicate least significant differences at p ≤ 0.05.

The sugar: acid ratio demonstrated significant correlations with both SSC (r = 0.65, p ≤ 0.01) and TA (r = -0.89, p ≤ 0.01), reflecting its role as an integrated ripening indicator (see Supplementary Table S8). Red California Wonder showed highest ratios (4.15) during pre-harvest stage in week 1, decreasing to 2.38 by week 4. Green Bianca exhibited maximum ratio (8.04) at pre-harvest stage in week 3. NO treatment maintained highest ratios in Red California Wonder (4.38) through week 4, while 1-MCP proved optimal for Green Bianca (10.31) at week 4.

These results align with Watkins (2006)’s findings regarding 1-MCP’s variable effects on soluble solids content across different fruits31. While our results showed positive effects in bell peppers, similar to findings in pineapple, papaya, and apple, this differs from observations in strawberries where SSC decreased with 1-MCP treatment. The maintenance of higher SSC in treated samples supports Wills et al. (2003)’s research showing varied responses across different commodities32.

The effectiveness of NO treatment corresponds with findings by Singh et al. (2013) and Zaharah and Singh (2011) and Zhu et al. (2009), who demonstrated that NO treatment delays increase in soluble solids content in various fruits33–35. Similar to our observations, Zhu et al. (2008) reported that NO treatment reduced SSC in kiwifruit by inhibiting ethylene production36.

Regarding sugar: acid ratio, our findings support Carlos and Kader (1999)’s conclusion that maintaining optimal taste and flavor relates to effective control of water loss and reduced respiration rates37. The preservation of favorable SSC/TA ratios in treated samples aligns with Modares et al. (2014)’s observations in strawberry fruit, where 1-MCP treatment effectively preserved the flavor index38.

Antioxidant systems and Post-harvest stress response

Storage time and ethylene regulator treatments (NO and 1-MCP) significantly influenced antioxidant-related parameters in both Green Bianca and Red California Wonder bell pepper fruits (see Supplementary Tables S1–S6 for full ANOVA results). Notably, vitamin C, total phenolics, and flavonoids were strongly affected by both factors (p ≤ 0.01), and the storage time × treatment interaction (S × T) was also highly significant for these traits, indicating that treatment effectiveness varied during storage progression.

In contrast, application timing (A) had a limited role, showing a significant effect only on total phenolics, and not on vitamin C, antioxidant capacity, or flavonoids. This suggests that while the timing of treatment application may influence the biosynthesis of phenolic compounds, its overall effect on the antioxidant system is minor compared to the direct action of the treatments themselves.

Among treatments, 1-MCP was particularly effective in preserving vitamin C and antioxidant capacity in Red California Wonder, whereas NO showed superior effects in Green Bianca, especially in maintaining higher phenolic and flavonoid contents. These findings align with cultivar-specific postharvest stress responses, suggesting that NO and 1-MCP may modulate different defense pathways related to antioxidant preservation.

Green Bianca showed peak vitamin C levels (73.19 mg100g− 1) with post-harvest treatment in week 1. The 1-MCP treatment proved most effective for Red California Wonder, maintaining elevated vitamin C levels (101.21 mg100g− 1), while NO treatment showed superior results for Green Bianca (79.58 mg100g− 1). Notably, control treatments resulted in complete vitamin C loss (0.00 mg100g− 1) in both cultivars by week 4 (Fig. 5).

Pigment metabolism and color development

Pigment metabolism traits were measured separately in the two cultivars. Chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoids were assessed in Green Bianca, while lycopene was measured in Red California Wonder (see Supplementary Tables S1–S6 for ANOVA results). In Green Bianca, storage time, treatment, and S × T interaction were highly significant (p ≤ 0.01) for chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoids. Application timing (A) was not significant for any trait, except chlorophyll b (p ≤ 0.05). Higher-order interactions were not significant. In Red California Wonder, lycopene was significantly affected by storage time, treatment, and their interaction (S × T) (all p ≤ 0.01). No significant effects were observed for application timing or higher-order interactions. These findings indicate that pigment-related responses to ethylene regulators are cultivar-specific and vary depending on the type of pigment and storage duration.

Chlorophyll content and carotenoids in green Bianca

In Green Bianca cultivar, chlorophyll degradation showed significant treatment effects (p ≤ 0.01) and storage time interactions. Initial chlorophyll a (0.14 mg g− 1) and chlorophyll b (0.07 mg g− 1) contents progressively decreased during storage, with the rate of degradation significantly influenced by treatments. NO treatment, particularly at pre-harvest stage, proved most effective in maintaining chlorophyll stability, preserving chlorophyll a level at 0.12 mg/g through day 14 (Fig. 7).

The effectiveness of NO in delaying chlorophyll degradation aligns with Zhang et al. (2019)’s findings that NO inhibits chlorophyllase activity and protects chloroplast structure50. The significant S × T interaction (p ≤ 0.01) suggests that treatment timing is crucial for chlorophyll retention, with pre-harvest application showing superior results.

Carotenoid accumulation in Green Bianca showed strong treatment responses. Initial carotenoid content (18.56 mg g− 1) increased during storage, but NO treatment effectively moderated this increase compared to control treatments. These findings support Hornero-Mendez and Minguez-Mosquera’s (2002) report regarding the coordinated nature of chlorophyll degradation and carotenoid synthesis during pepper ripening51.

Lycopene development in red California Wonder

Lycopene content, analyzed exclusively in Red California Wonder, demonstrated significant treatment effects (p ≤ 0.01) and strong storage time interactions. From initial content (0.10 mg 100 g− 1), control fruits showed rapid lycopene accumulation during storage, while both NO and 1-MCP treatments effectively moderated this increase. The significant S × T interaction (F = 377.85, p ≤ 0.01) indicates that treatment effectiveness varied throughout storage duration.

Pre-harvest NO application proved most effective in regulating lycopene development (Fig. 8), supporting findings by Fraser and Bramley (2004) regarding the timing-dependent regulation of carotenoid biosynthesis52. The moderation of lycopene accumulation by ethylene regulators aligns with Kato et al. (2004)’s research demonstrating the role of ethylene in coordinating pigment metabolism during ripening53.

The differential responses between cultivars and treatments suggest that pigment metabolism regulation varies with genetic background and developmental stage. These findings parallel Chang et al. The superior performance of NO treatment in maintaining initial pigment profiles supports research by Corpas et al. (2018) demonstrating NO’s role in chloroplast function and pigment stability55.

Conclusion

This comprehensive study demonstrated the significant potential of 1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP) and nitric oxide (NO) treatments in maintaining bell pepper quality during postharvest storage, with clear differences observed between the Green Bianca and Red California Wonder cultivars. The effectiveness of these treatments varied significantly depending on application timing and cultivar-specific characteristics.

Green Bianca exhibited optimal storage quality following preharvest NO treatment, maintaining superior vitamin C content, antioxidant capacity, minimal weight loss, and enhanced chlorophyll retention. Similarly, Red California Wonder responded optimally to preharvest NO treatment, demonstrating high antioxidant activity and vitamin C content while effectively moderating lycopene development.

Treatment effectiveness varied between cultivars: 1-MCP proved more effective for Red California Wonder, whereas NO treatment was superior for Green Bianca. Application timing emerged as a crucial factor, with preharvest applications being particularly effective in maintaining membrane integrity and reducing chilling injury while preserving bioactive compounds.

The biochemical basis for quality retention involves multiple mechanisms, including cultivar-specific responses in pigment metabolism and antioxidant systems. Both treatments effectively inhibited ethylene-mediated ripening processes while maintaining membrane integrity and preserving antioxidant enzyme activities.

These findings establish the timing-dependent efficacy of ethylene regulators in bell pepper preservation, with preharvest NO treatment emerging as particularly effective for both cultivars. This study provides valuable insights for developing cultivar-specific postharvest protocols, offering promising strategies for extending market life and maintaining nutritional quality during cold storage.

These findings have several important implications for commercial post-harvest management:

Treatment timing should be customized based on cultivar, with pre-harvest applications showing particular promise for Green Bianca and combined pre/post-harvest treatment for Red California Wonder.

Additionally, efforts should be directed toward developing practical and scalable application methods suitable for commercial use. Finally, conducting an economic analysis of various treatment timing options will be essential for optimizing cost-effectiveness and guiding real-world adoption.
