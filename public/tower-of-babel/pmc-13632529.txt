Within-patient carbapenem heteroresistance in klebsiella pneumoniae associated with a repeat-associated blaNDM-1 resistance-module deletion
Authors: Meiling Jiao, Sibo Wang, Qingyu Meng, Hao Liu, Juntian Jiang, Xuesong Xu, Chao Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Longitudinal clinical isolates and independent-colony sequencing supported recent phenotypic diversification within a closely related Klebsiella pneumoniae lineage.•A repeat-associated plasmid deletion removed a blaNDM-1-containing resistance module and was supported by reassemble data, read-level validation, and molecular assays.•The module-lacking isolate showed improved growth under antibiotic-free conditions, whereas the present design does not establish module loss as the sole cause of the fitness difference.•The repeat architecture was detected in diverse blaNDM-1-positive plasmids, supporting its potential relevance to resistance-module instability.Keywords: Klebsiella pneumoniae, Carbapenem heteroresistance, blaNDM-1, Homologous recombination, Within-host evolution

Introduction

Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) has emerged as one of the most clinically important multidrug-resistant pathogens, driven by the global dissemination of high-risk clonal lineages and mobile resistance determinants (Ernst et al., 2020; Lei et al., 2024). Among these determinants, blaNDM genes are of particular concern because they are frequently carried on transferable plasmids and may occur on transposon-associated segments inserted within complex multidrug-resistance regions, thereby limiting therapeutic options and complicating infection control (Wyres et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021). Despite extensive investigations of CRKP at the population level, the behavior of resistance determinants within individual hosts remains incompletely understood.

Antimicrobial resistance can change under altered selective pressures through plasmid loss, rearrangement, or gene deletion. blaNDM genes are frequently carried on transposon-associated mobile segments that can occur near class 1 integron remnants, insertion sequences, and repeated DNA (Zhang et al., 2017; Macesic et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2019). The qacEΔ1–sul1 segment is part of the 3′ conserved region of class 1 integrons; its presence marks a common structural context but does not by itself demonstrate integron activity. Repeated DNA within these regions can provide substrates for rearrangement and may alter resistance phenotypes.

Most evidence for resistance instability comes from in vitro systems or indirect genomic observations (McGann et al., 2012; Tavares et al., 2022; Lopez-Diaz et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2025; Hudson et al., 2014). Clinical studies have focused mainly on resistant subpopulations emerging from susceptible populations, whereas loss of resistance within a closely related lineage is less well characterized (Pereira et al., 2021; Pal and Andersson, 2024; Cho et al., 2025; Nicoloff et al., 2019; Andersson et al., 2019). Direct clinical observations that connect a defined plasmid structural change with coisolated phenotypic variants are therefore uncommon.

We investigated whether a plasmid-borne blaNDM-1 region could be lost from a closely related clinical lineage and contribute to coexisting carbapenem-resistant and carbapenem-susceptible isolates. Such heterogeneity may complicate susceptibility assignment when routine testing captures only one colony type from an evolving population.

We analyzed longitudinal Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates from one patient to define their clinical provenance, genomic relationship, plasmid structures, and resistance phenotypes. Short- and long-read sequencing, read-level structural validation, molecular assays, and growth experiments were used to evaluate a repeat-associated deletion model. We also assessed the distribution of related repeat architectures among publicly available complete blaNDM-1-positive plasmids.

The study provides evidence that a repeat-associated deletion of a blaNDM-1-containing region coincided with carbapenem phenotypic diversification in one closely related clinical lineage. The findings are hypothesis-generating and require testing in additional clinical collections.

Methods

Bacterial isolates

The patient was hospitalized from October 11 through November 19, 2024, for a complicated lower-limb wound infection at the China–Japan Union Hospital of Jilin University, Changchun, China. Wound secretions from the same anatomical site were cultured on three occasions. The October 14 culture yielded blaKPC-positive Klebsiella pneumoniae KP01 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa PA01; the November 1 culture yielded KP02; and the November 5 culture yielded KP03 and KP04. Subsequent mechanistic analyses focused on the clonally related KP02, KP03, and KP04 isolates. Species identification used matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (Bruker Daltonics, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined using the broth microdilution method following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. The antimicrobial agents tested were imipenem, meropenem, meropenem–vaborbactam, aztreonam–avibactam, ceftazidime–avibactam, piperacillin–tazobactam, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, cefepime, amikacin, ciprofloxacin, sitafloxacin, colistin, tigecycline, and eravacycline.

Interpretation of the MIC values followed the CLSI 2024 criteria, unless otherwise specified. Breakpoints for colistin and aztreonam–avibactam were interpreted according to the EUCAST 2024 guidelines, whereas tigecycline and eravacycline were interpreted according to the FDA 2023 criteria. Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 were used as quality control strains.

Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted with the Omega Bio-tek Bacterial DNA Kit (D3350) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Short-read whole-genome sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ platform. KP02 and KP04 additionally underwent long-read sequencing, and the corresponding short- and long-read datasets were used for the original hybrid assemblies and for the revised structural analyses. Library preparation and run metadata are reported with the sequencing data submission.

Independent-colony libraries were prepared with the Rapid Plus DNA Lib Prep Kit for Illumina (RK20208) and sequenced in PE150 mode on a NovaSeq X Plus Series platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Original short-read data were quality filtered with Sickle, and the original hybrid assemblies were generated with SPAdes 3.12.0. Reassemble data sequences were generated independently by assembling long reads with Flye 2.9.6 and polishing circular contigs twice with the corresponding Illumina reads using Polypolish 0.6.1 after rotation of each replicon between rounds. The KP02 chromosome in the reassemble data assembly was selected after assembly-quality and read-mapping checks as the fixed reference. For the independent-colony datasets, adapters and low-quality bases were removed with Trimmomatic 0.38 using the TruSeq3-PE adapter library, a 4-bp sliding window at mean quality 20, and a minimum read length of 50 bp. Three independently picked colonies from each of KP02, KP03, and KP04 underwent paired-end sequencing. Cleaned reads were analyzed against the fixed reassemble data KP02 chromosome with Snippy 4.6.0; pairwise differences were counted only at positions called as A, C, G, or T in both samples. Within-colony alternative alleles were screened from independently mapped reads using depth, allele-count, strand-support, and quality filters. MLST was assigned with the PubMLST workflow. Plasmid replicons were screened with ABRicate 1.0.1 against the PlasmidFinder database dated 14 January 2025 using minimum identity of 80% and coverage of 60%. Resistance genes and pMLST profiles were determined with ResFinder and the corresponding pMLST workflow, respectively. Detailed parameters and quality-control results are provided in the Supplementary Methods and Tables S3–S6.

Serial passage

Serial passaging experiments were performed to evaluate the evolutionary dynamics of strain KP02 under reduced antibiotic pressure. KP02 cells were cultured in 5 mL of antibiotic-free LB broth and in LB broth supplemented with sub-inhibitory concentrations of cefoperazone–sulbactam. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking and passaged every 24 h by transferring 1:1000 into fresh medium. Serial passaging was continued for ten consecutive days. At each passage, bacterial cultures were plated on LB agar plates without antibiotics. Antibiotic susceptibility was monitored by placing imipenem (10 µg), meropenem (10 µg), and cefoperazone–sulbactam (105 µg) disks onto the plates to track phenotypic changes during serial passage.

PCR validation and quantitative reverse-transcription PCR

Genomic DNA for structural PCR and Sanger validation was extracted with the Omega Bio-tek Bacterial DNA Kit (D3350). Segment PCR used 2 × EasyTaq PCR SuperMix with dye (TransGen Biotech), and outward-facing PCR used 2 × MegaPfu Premix with dye (TOLOBIO, AS231). Amplicons were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis, purified with the Omega Bio-tek Gel Extraction Kit (D2500), and subjected to Sanger sequencing. Primer sequences, expected products, cycling conditions, and lane assignments are reported in Supplementary Table S15. Total RNA was isolated with the Omega Bio-tek E.Z.N.A. Bacterial RNA Kit (R6950–01). cDNA was prepared using the FastKing RT Kit with gDNase (Tiangen, KR116), and RT-qPCR used ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711) with rpoB as the reference gene. The original RT-qPCR experiments displayed in Fig. 5E used three independent RNA extractions per isolate, each measured in technical triplicate. A later five-isolate RT-qPCR run was analyzed separately as confirmatory supplementary evidence. Samples without an amplicon in the expected melting-temperature range were classified as target not detected. For graphical display only, target-not-detected wells were represented by a baseline 2^-ΔΔCt value of 1; these display values were not interpreted as relative blaNDM-1 expression. Well-level results and assay calls for both datasets are provided in Supplementary Table S16.

Growth kinetics and in vitro competition assay

Growth kinetics were evaluated by monitoring optical density over time. Overnight cultures were diluted 1:100 in fresh LB broth and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm; OD600 was measured hourly. For the in vitro competition experiment, KP02 and KP04 cultures were adjusted to 0.5 McFarland, mixed 1:1 in LB broth, and sampled every 2 h. Serial dilutions were plated on antibiotic-free LB agar for total counts and on LB agar containing meropenem (4 mg/L) for the carbapenem-resistant fraction. Because differential plating distinguishes resistance phenotype rather than an immutable lineage marker, this assay was interpreted as supportive evidence rather than definitive lineage-specific competition.

Distribution of direct-repeat architectures among complete blaNDM-1-positive plasmids

To assess the distribution of related repeat architectures, a nonredundant collection of complete blaNDM-1-positive plasmid sequences was retrieved from the NCBI nucleotide database. Sequences were annotated with Bakta v1.6.1, and related directly repeated sul1–qacEΔ1–ISVsa3 architectures were visualized. Whole-plasmid relationships were estimated with Mash v2.3 and visualized with iTOL v6; representative structures were compared with Easyfig v2.2.5. This contextual analysis did not estimate the frequency or mechanism of deletion in public plasmids.

Additional experiments

Conjugation, electroporation, and Galleria mellonella infection assays were conducted as described in the Supplementary Methods. Plasmid DNA used for electroporation was purified with the QIAGEN Plasmid Midi Kit. Detailed procedures, controls, and source images are provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

Results

Clinical course and bacterial isolation

The period from October 11 through November 19, 2024, represents the complete hospitalization, rather than an inferred duration of infection or an isolate-recovery interval. Wound secretions from the same anatomical site were cultured on October 14, November 1, and November 5. The October 14 culture yielded PA01 and blaKPC-positive KP01; the November 1 culture yielded blaNDM-1-positive, carbapenem-resistant KP02. On November 5, KP03 and KP04 were recovered as morphologically distinct colonies from the same wound specimen and the same primary culture plate. KP03 was carbapenem resistant and KP04 was carbapenem susceptible. The original wound material and quantitative colony counts are unavailable, so the relative abundance and exact time of emergence of these phenotypes cannot be reconstructed. KP02, KP03, and KP04 belonged to ST37/KL104 and were investigated as a closely related clinical lineage (Fig. 1).

Antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of KP02 and KP04

KP02 exhibited extensive resistance to multiple antibiotic classes. High-level resistance was observed for several β-lactams (cefepime, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone; MICs >32 μg/mL) and aztreonam (MIC = 64 μg/mL). β-lactam–β-lactamase inhibitor combinations remained ineffective (cefoperazone–sulbactam >128 μg/mL; piperacillin–tazobactam >256 μg/mL). Carbapenem resistance was confirmed (imipenem 8 μg/mL; meropenem 16 μg/mL) and was not reversed by novel inhibitors meropenem–vaborbactam or ceftazidime–avibactam; however, aztreonam-avibactam susceptibility was retained (MIC = 0.125 μg/mL). KP02 also displayed resistance to non-β-lactam agents, including tigecycline, amikacin, fluoroquinolones, colistin, and sulfamethoxazole. In contrast, KP04 exhibited a substantially different profile: restored susceptibility to carbapenems (imipenem <0.06 μg/mL; meropenem <0.03 μg/mL) and to inhibitor combinations (meropenem–vaborbactam, ceftazidime–avibactam, aztreonam–avibactam all <0.03 μg/mL). Non-β-lactam resistance persisted for several agents (Table 1).

Repeat-associated blaNDM-1 module loss in reassembled plasmids

In the reassemble data assemblies, Pkp02_1 and Pkp04_1 were 253,885 bp and 243,402 bp, respectively. Both were IncHI1B/IncFIB(K) multireplicon plasmids; neither was typeable by the current IncHI1 pMLST scheme and no IncF RST profile was assigned. Pkp04_1 lacked a 10,483-bp interval present in Pkp02_1. The interval was delimited by two directly oriented, identical 3116-bp repeats containing qacEΔ1 and sul1, and Pkp04_1 retained one corresponding repeat at the junction. Long-read alignments, flanking Illumina depth, an independently generated Flye assembly, and three independently sequenced KP04 colonies supported the deleted structure. These data support a repeat-associated plasmid deletion and reduce the likelihood of an assembly artifact; they do not identify the molecular pathway responsible for the rearrangement (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S9).

Phenotypic diversification during serial passage

Under antibiotic-free serial passage, two colony morphotypes became distinguishable at passage 10. KP02-G10-R formed larger colonies and retained carbapenem resistance, whereas KP02-G10-S formed smaller colonies and was carbapenem susceptible (Fig. 4A). Inhibition zones for carbapenems increased during serial passage, and KP02-G10-S showed restored carbapenem susceptibility. The representative MIC and disk-diffusion results and the complete susceptibility-panel profile are shown in Fig. 4.

Molecular validation of blaNDM-1 module loss and candidate circular intermediate

Structural PCR and Sanger sequencing supported the presence of the blaNDM-1-containing region in KP02 and its absence in KP04. An outward-facing PCR product was detected in KP02 at the expected size for the 5804-bp amplicon predicted from a 10,483-bp candidate circular model; no corresponding product was detected in KP04. The purified product was assembled as a 5804-bp sequence matching the predicted circular path, and bidirectional Sanger reads crossed the modeled junction. These data support a sequence-verified candidate circular intermediate, while the abundance and complete 10,483-bp circular molecule remain unresolved. RT-qPCR shown in Fig. 5E detected target-specific blaNDM-1 amplification in KP02, KP03, KP02-G10, and KP02-G10-R. KP04 and KP02-G10-S were classified as target not detected because their signals were undetermined or late and nonspecific. For graphical display, target-not-detected wells were represented by a baseline 2^-ΔΔCt value of 1; the corresponding bars do not indicate detected blaNDM-1 transcripts. The raw Ct values and the final assay calls are reported in Supplementary Table S16. An additional five-isolate RT-qPCR run provided concordant supplementary confirmation (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Antibiotic-free fitness advantage associated with the module-lacking plasmid state

Under antibiotic-free conditions, KP04 showed higher growth and final biomass than KP02 (Fig. 6). During the 4-h competition assay, the meropenem-resistant fraction declined rapidly; because differential plating does not provide a lineage-invariant marker, this observation is supportive rather than definitive lineage-specific evidence. Conjugation yielded blaNDM-1-positive E. coli J53 transconjugants, supporting transferability of the KP02 plasmid. KP02 and KP04 produced comparable dose-dependent mortality profiles in Galleria mellonella across the tested inocula (Supplementary Figs. S1 and S3). Together, these findings show an association between absence of the resistance module and improved fitness under antibiotic-free conditions (Supplementary Table S11).

Discussion

This case links carbapenem phenotypic heterogeneity to a defined plasmid structural difference within a closely related ST37/KL104 Klebsiella pneumoniae lineage. KP03 and KP04 were recovered from the same primary culture, and independent-colony sequencing showed that the resistant and susceptible phenotypes represented stable major genomic states. The loss of the blaNDM-1-containing region in Pkp04_1 therefore provides a genomic explanation for the susceptible phenotype, rather than a susceptibility result alone. The observation extends reports of within-patient plasmid dynamics and reversion to carbapenem susceptibility in Klebsiella pneumoniae (Villa et al., 2013; Stohr et al., 2020). It also highlights a practical diagnostic issue: selection of a single colony can under-sample phenotypic diversity, particularly when resistance is heterogeneous or transiently silent (Pereira et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2024; Göttig et al., 2016; Qin et al., 2024; Wagner et al., 2023).

The 10,483-bp difference in this study was bounded by directly repeated sul1-qacEΔ1-containing regions. blaNDM genes commonly occur in complex resistance regions that combine transposon-associated segments, class 1 integron remnants, and repeated sequences (Tavares et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2025; Pereira et al., 2021). Such architecture creates opportunities for local rearrangement and changes in resistance-gene content. Here, reassembled plasmids, independent assembly, long-read mapping, depth analysis, segmented PCR, and Sanger sequencing all supported two plasmid configurations. The 5804-bp outward-facing PCR product further supports a candidate circular configuration and provides a testable model for excision of the resistance module during plasmid remodeling.

The phenotypic experiments place the structural finding in a selection context. Under antibiotic-free serial passage, KP02 generated resistant and susceptible derivatives after ten passages, and KP04 reached a higher final biomass in antibiotic-free medium. Fitness trade-offs associated with plasmid-borne resistance and resistance-gene dosage have been described in Klebsiella pneumoniae and other pathogens (Pal and Andersson, 2024; Cho et al., 2025; Göttig et al., 2016). Our findings support the hypothesis that reduced antibiotic pressure can enrich module-lacking variants when their growth advantage outweighs the benefit of maintaining carbapenem resistance. This balance may help explain why closely related isolates with contrasting phenotypes can emerge during prolonged infection or laboratory passage.

The contextual plasmid comparison places this case within a broader structural setting and identifies related directly repeated sul1–qacEΔ1–ISVsa3-associated configurations across diverse plasmid backgrounds. Together with the conjugative transfer of the KP02 plasmid, this observation suggests that repeat-associated resistance regions merit surveillance in diverse plasmid backgrounds (Yang et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2019). Long-read plasmid-resolved sequencing and multi-colony sampling are particularly valuable when discordant colony morphologies or antimicrobial susceptibility results are observed. These approaches can identify structural variants that are difficult to infer from a single consensus genome or a single colony.

Two limitations define the causal scope of this study. The unavailable original wound material precludes reconstruction of the initial subpopulation frequencies and temporal order of diversification. Genetic perturbation and isogenic reconstruction will be required to identify the molecular pathway responsible for the deletion and to quantify the module-specific component of the fitness difference. The documented coexistence of stable plasmid states, however, provides a clinically grounded model for investigating resistance-module instability in larger longitudinal collections.

Conclusion

In this patient-derived ST37/KL104 lineage, a repeat-associated blaNDM-1 module deletion coincided with carbapenem phenotypic diversification. The data provide a clinically grounded model for testing in broader collections.
