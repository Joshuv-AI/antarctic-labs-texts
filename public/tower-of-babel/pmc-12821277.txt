The effect of exercise intervention on the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index in adolescents with overweight or obesity: a systematic review and Bayesian dose–response meta-analysis
Authors: Meichun Yang, Shu Pan, Qiqi Wang, Yidan Jiang, Xi Li, Xi Fan, Dian Zhan, Deping Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent obesity and associated insulin resistance (IR) are major public health issues. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a simple IR surrogate, but exercise’s quantitative effect on it and optimal dose–response remain unclear. This study systematically evaluated exercise’s impact on the TyG index, fasting blood glucose (FBG), and triglycerides (TG) in adolescents with Overweight or Obesity.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis following PRISMA guidelines. We searched five electronic databases up to August 2025 for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of exercise interventions (≥ 4 weeks) in adolescents with Overweight or Obesity (≤ 18 years) with a non-exercise control group that reported FBG and TG data, from which the TyG index was calculated. Independent Bayesian random-effects meta-analyses and meta-regressions were performed for the three outcomes, with the Mean Difference (MD) as the effect measure.ResultsTwelve RCTs (733 participants) showed exercise significantly improved the TyG index (MD = -0.16, 95% CrI -0.23 to -0.09; moderate certainty) and reduced FBG (MD = -3.50 mg/dL, 95% CrI -5.26 to -1.65; low certainty), while a non-significant reduction was observed for TG (MD = -4.08 mg/dL, 95% CrI -9.91 to 1.66; very low certainty). A non-linear dose–response was observed, with optimal effects at ~ 50–60 min/session and 1000–1500 MET·min/week. Age/sex had no moderating effects.ConclusionExercise effectively improves the TyG index and insulin sensitivity in adolescents with Overweight or Obesity, with optimal benefits in a specific dose range, highlighting the need for precise, moderate-dose exercise prescriptions. Because the TyG index can be easily calculated from routine fasting TG and glucose measurements, it represents a low-cost, scalable marker of metabolic risk and a particularly useful tool for clinical and public health monitoring in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06433-7.Keywords: Exercise intervention, Adolescents with overweight and obesity, TYG index, Bayesian dose-response analysis, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Adolescent Overweight or Obesity represent a serious global public health challenge of the twenty-first century [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the total number of children and adolescents aged 5–19 with Overweight or Obesity worldwide has surpassed 390 million [2]. The prevalence rate has more than doubled in the past three decades, reaching an alarming 20% [2]. This trend is equally severe in many countries, including China, placing immense pressure on future national health and healthcare systems [3]. Adolescent obesity not only impairs current physical and mental health but also poses a profound long-term health risk, significantly increasing the likelihood of developing type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and various chronic non-communicable diseases in adulthood [4]. The core pathophysiological link is Insulin Resistance (IR) [5]. Therefore, effectively identifying and intervening in insulin resistance during adolescence is crucial for breaking the chain of disease and safeguarding future health.

In clinical practice and large-scale screening, the application of the gold standard method for directly assessing insulin resistance is limited by its complexity and high cost [6]. For this reason, the Triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), an indicator calculated using only fasting triglycerides and blood glucose, has become a widely recognized, simple, economical, and reliable surrogate marker for insulin resistance [7, 8]. Numerous studies have confirmed that the TyG index is not only highly correlated with the results of the gold standard test but can also effectively predict metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular risk in adolescents [9–11]. Given this, systematically evaluating the effects of various interventions, especially cornerstone lifestyle interventions such as exercise, on improving the TyG index in adolescents with obesity has clear and significant public health guidance implications. In the face of adolescent obesity, interventions such as medication or surgery are often limited due to side effects, high risks, or poor adherence, making them difficult to implement widely [12]. Lifestyle interventions, including exercise, dietary modifications, and behavioral counseling, constitute the first-line approach for managing adolescent obesity [13]. Therefore, identifying an intervention that is safe, sustainable, and easily implemented is crucial. Against this backdrop, exercise intervention, as a fundamental non-pharmacological therapy, offers distinct advantages. Exercise is not only safe and low-cost but, more importantly, can produce broad metabolic benefits independent of weight loss [14]. Physiological studies have confirmed that regular exercise directly improves the body's glucose utilization and lipid metabolism, partly through mechanisms such as AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation and glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) translocation in skeletal muscle, which enhance glucose uptake [15, 16]. Additionally, exercise increases lipoprotein lipase (LPL) activity, facilitating triglyceride (TG) clearance [17, 18]. These mechanisms directly target the two core components of the TyG index [15, 19]. Consequently, exercise is regarded as a cornerstone strategy for improving insulin resistance and overall metabolic health in adolescents, offering a highly promising pathway to address this public health challenge.

Although multiple meta-analyses have confirmed that exercise can improve indicators such as fasting blood glucose (FBG) and homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) in adolescents with Overweight or Obesity [20–22], a key evidence gap persists: no systematic review or meta-analysis to date has directly synthesized the effect of exercise on the TyG index [23, 24]. While existing studies provide indirect evidence for this topic [20–22, 25], inconsistencies in data reporting from primary trials (e.g., lack of complete data required to calculate the TyG index) [8], significant methodological heterogeneity (e.g., modality, intensity, and dose) [20, 21], and insufficient control for key confounding factors like physiological insulin resistance during puberty [26, 27] mean that the academic community still lacks a clear and quantified, evidence-based answer to the question, "To what extent can exercise intervention actually improve the TyG index?" Therefore, conducting a meta-analysis specifically targeting this question to fill this gap is particularly necessary.

The core objective of this study is to determine the true effect of exercise intervention on the TyG index in children and adolescents with overweight or obesity through a meta-analysis. Based on the above physiological mechanisms, we hypothesized that exercise interventions that activate AMPK, promote GLUT4 translocation, and enhance LPL-mediated triglyceride clearance would improve the TyG index by enhancing glucose uptake and promoting triglyceride clearance in this population (16, 46). To this end, we will systematically include clinical trials that have reported data on fasting triglycerides (TG) and fasting blood glucose (FBG). We will utilize this primary data to recalculate a unified TyG index and its effect size for each study. Finally, a pooled analysis will be conducted, aiming to provide the first direct, quantified, evidence-based answer to the question of whether exercise can effectively improve the TyG index.

Method

This systematic review and meta-analysis was designed and conducted in strict accordance with the standards of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [28]. The entire process and reporting adhere to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [29]. Furthermore, the protocol for this study has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (registration number: CRD420251120755) to ensure the transparency and rigor of the research.

Search strategy

We conducted a systematic literature search of five electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library (primarily the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, CENTRAL), CNKI, and Web of Science. The search period will extend from the inception of each database to August 2025. The search strategy will combine subject headings and free-text words. For the population, search terms will include "overweight," "obesity," "pediatric," and "adolescent," among others. For the intervention, terms will include "exercise," "physical activity," and "training." For outcome measures, terms will cover "triglyceride-glucose index," "TyG," "triglycerides," "glucose," and "insulin resistance." The search will primarily focus on literature in English, but no language restrictions will be applied to the studies that are finally included. To ensure a comprehensive search, we will also manually screen the reference lists of all included studies and relevant systematic reviews. The complete search strategy for each database is detailed in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Study selection

After deduplicating all citations retrieved from database searches, two investigators will screen the literature independently in two stages. The initial screening will involve a review of titles and abstracts to identify and remove irrelevant articles. The second stage will consist of a full-text assessment of the remaining potentially relevant articles to determine final eligibility based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements at any screening stage will be settled through consensus-based discussion or arbitration by a third reviewer. The process will be conducted and reported in line with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, and a PRISMA flow diagram will be used to illustrate the study selection process.

Data extraction and management

Two investigators independently will perform data extraction using a pre-designed and pilot-tested standardized form to ensure consistency and accuracy. The extracted data will include: (1) basic study information, such as the first author and year of publication; (2) baseline characteristics of the study population, including sample size, age, and degree of obesity; (3) details of the intervention, such as the type, frequency, intensity, and duration of exercise; and (4) the primary outcome measures for this study, specifically the mean, standard deviation (SD), and corresponding sample size for fasting triglycerides and fasting blood glucose in each group, both pre- and post-intervention. Any discrepancies in the extracted data will be resolved through consensus-based discussion or, if necessary, arbitration by a third reviewer. In cases where data are missing or reported unclearly in the original studies, we will attempt to contact the corresponding authors to obtain the necessary supplementary information to ensure data completeness.

Risk of bias and evidence quality

The methodological quality of the included studies will be independently assessed by two researchers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool 2 (RoB 2) [30]. This tool evaluates the risk of bias across five domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Based on these domains, the overall risk of bias for each study will be judged as "Low risk," "Some concerns," or "High risk." Following this, we will use the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) system is used to appraise the overall quality of evidence for the primary outcomes [31]. The certainty of the evidence will be rated as High, Moderate, Low, or Very Low, based on factors including the studies' risk of bias, inconsistency of results, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Any disagreements during the assessment process will be resolved through discussion or by a third-party arbitrator.

Measures of treatment effect

This study will assess the effect of exercise intervention on three primary outcomes: fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglycerides (TG), and the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index. For these three continuous variables, the Mean Difference (MD) and its 95% Credible Interval (CrI) will be used as the uniform effect size measure. The MD represents the absolute difference in the mean change from baseline to post-intervention between the intervention and control groups for each outcome. For FBG and TG, the means and standard deviations will be directly extracted from the original studies. Statistical analysis

Independent Bayesian meta-analyses will be conducted for each of the three outcomes: FBG, TG and TyG index. All statistical analyses for this study will be conducted using R software (version 4.5.1). We will perform a Bayesian meta-analysis within a Bayesian multilevel modeling framework using the brms package [35]. A random-effects model will be specified to estimate the effect of the exercise intervention, with the individual studies included as a random effect to fully account for between-study heterogeneity [36]. The prior distributions for the model will be set as follows: a normal (0,1) prior will be used for the model's intercept (representing the overall average effect), and a Cauchy (0,1) prior will be applied to the standard deviation of the random effects (τ), which represents between-study heterogeneity [37]. The model will be fitted using multiple Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains with a sufficient number of iterations to ensure convergence. Model convergence will be diagnosed by assessing the Potential Scale Reduction Factor and visually inspecting trace plots. The final effect size will be reported as the mean of its posterior distribution along with the 95% Credible Interval (CrI). To visually represent the results from individual studies and the pooled effect, a forest plot will be generated using the ggplot2 package. Furthermore, to assess potential publication bias, we will generate a funnel plot with the aid of the meta package, supplemented by quantitative evaluation using Egger's and Begg's tests [34].

To further investigate potential sources of heterogeneity and the influence of study-level characteristics on the intervention effect, we conducted a multivariable Bayesian meta-regression analysis. This model will include the following study-level covariates: participant characteristics (baseline age, sex, Body Mass Index (BMI), and TyG index), intervention characteristics (intervention duration in weeks, intensity, the duration of a single exercise session (in minutes), and the total weekly exercise dose). The weekly exercise dose will be estimated as session duration × weekly frequency. For each exercise modality (including continuous aerobic training, high-intensity interval training, resistance training, and combined programs), a Metabolic Equivalent (MET) value was assigned according to the reported activity type and target intensity in the original trial, using the standard values from the latest 2024 Compendium of Physical Activities for Adults [38], with the acknowledgment that this is a recognized limitation as a pediatric-specific compendium is not yet available. For mixed or interval-based interventions (e.g., HIIT or combined aerobic and resistance training), we calculated a time-weighted average MET across the different components and intensity phases within each session, and this compendium-based procedure was applied consistently to all included studies to derive the weekly exercise dose in MET·min/week.Non-linear dose–response relationships between weekly exercise dose and changes in the TyG index, FBG, and TG were modeled using a Bayesian generalized additive meta-regression implemented in brms, with a penalized spline smooth for weekly dose (s(weekly_dose, k = 4)) and a random intercept for study. In this framework, the spline basis dimension was set to k = 4 to allow a flexible but smooth curve, and knot locations were determined automatically by the underlying mgcv algorithm across the observed range of weekly exercise doses rather than being fixed a priori at specific dose values.

Results

Included studies

A total of 390 records were identified through database (n = 388) and registry (n = 2) searches. After removing 22 duplicate records, 2 records marked as ineligible by automation tools, and 67 records for other reasons, 299 unique records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 179 were excluded as clearly irrelevant, leaving 120 articles for full-text retrieval. We were unable to obtain the full text for 17 of these, resulting in 103 articles being assessed for eligibility. During the full-text evaluation, 91 articles were excluded for the following primary reasons: incomplete outcome data (n = 43), non-conforming study design or population (n = 38), and improper control group (n = 10). Finally, 12 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis. A detailed flow diagram of the study selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of the literature screening process. The diagram illustrates the identification, screening, and inclusion of studies, starting from 390 initial records and resulting in 12 studies included in the review

Study characteristics

A total of 12 RCTs were included in this analysis. The publication years of these studies ranged from 2007 to 2024. Geographically, the studies were predominantly from Brazil and China, with four studies from each country. The remaining studies were conducted in the United States (n = 2), Estonia (n = 1), and Spain (n = 1). Collectively, the studies comprised 733 participants, with ages primarily concentrated between 12 and 18 years. Intervention types included high-intensity interval training, moderate-intensity continuous training, aerobic training, resistance training, combined training, sports games, and comprehensive physical activities. The total duration of the interventions varied widely, from as short as 4 weeks to as long as 8 months. The frequency of sessions ranged from two to seven times per week, while the duration of a single session varied from 30 to 90 min. All studies included a non-exercise control group that was instructed to maintain their usual lifestyle or not participate in structured exercise during the study period. The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Appendix 2.

Risk of bias and quality of evidence

The evaluation results for ROB2 demonstrated a high degree of consistency across the three metrics: for each outcome measure, 10 of the 12 studies were rated as having "some concerns," and two were rated as "low risk." The "some concerns" rating was primarily due to issues in the randomization process and deviations arising from the nature of the interventions. In contrast, the risk of bias related to missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result was low in the majority of the studies. Detailed results of each assessment are shown in Appendix 3. According to the GRADE evidence quality assessment, the quality of evidence was rated as Low for FBG, Very Low for TG, and Moderate for the TyG index. The downgrading of evidence levels was primarily due to the risk of bias prevalent in the included studies. Furthermore, the evidence quality for FBG and TG was downgraded further due to significant heterogeneity across studies. The evidence for TG was downgraded again due to the imprecision of its pooled effect estimate, as the credible interval crossed the null value. Meta-analysis

FBG

Based on 12 studies (28 comparisons), the Bayesian hierarchical random-effects model showed that exercise intervention lowered FBG compared to control, with a pooled effect of MD = −3.5 mg/dL (95% CrI −5.3, −1.7) (Fig. 2). The heterogeneity, characterized by the random-effects standard deviation (τ = 7.02, 95%CrI 4.61 to 10.88), was moderate, and the sampling and convergence were good (R̂ ≈ 1, sufficient ESS) (Table 2, Appendix 5). The dose–response analysis revealed a significant non-linear relationship. The posterior predictive curve based on single-session duration indicated that the effect reached its maximum reduction (approx. −3 mg/dL) at around 50–70 min per session, with diminishing returns and a gradual return towards null as the duration exceeded ~ 90 min per session. By 120 min per session, the posterior median was close to or slightly above zero. Similarly, the curve based on total weekly dose (MET·min/week) also showed an initial decrease followed by an increase, with an optimal range around 1000–1500 MET·min/week (maximum reduction, posterior median approx. −3 mg/dL). As the dose further increased (> ~ 1800–2000 MET·min/week), the effect tended to regress towards null with increased uncertainty (Fig. 3). In addition, no significant moderating effect of intervention duration was found. Meta-regression on age and the proportion of females did not show a stable moderating effect (the CrI crossed zero). The assessment for publication bias indicated slight asymmetry, but this did not alter the quantitative conclusions (Fig. 4).

TG

For the TG results, the Bayesian hierarchical random-effects model showed a pooled effect of exercise versus control of MD = −4.08 mg/dL (95% CrI −9.91 to 1.66), with moderate between-study heterogeneity (τ = 9.45, 95% CrI 4.44 to 16.60) (Fig. 2). The model achieved good convergence (R̂ = 1.00 for all parameters; high Bulk/Tail ESS) (Table 2, Appendix 5). The dose–response analysis revealed that the greatest TG reduction occurred with single-session durations of ≈50–60 min, while the effect rebounded and trended towards unfavorable after > ~ 90–120 min. The number of intervention weeks was approximately in a negative linear relationship with the effect, meaning longer durations led to lower TG. In terms of total weekly volume (MET·min·wk⁻1), a benefit was observed in the ≤ ~ 800–1000 range, but the effect approached null after > ~ 1000–1500 and turned unfavorable with increased uncertainty at ~ 2000 (Fig. 3). The meta-regression for age and proportion of females showed no stable moderating effect, as their credible intervals crossed zero. The funnel plot indicated slight asymmetry but did not alter the main conclusion (Figure S4).

TyG

The Bayesian hierarchical random-effects model of the included studies showed that exercise, compared to control, lowered the TyG index, with a main effect of MD = −0.16 (95% CrI −0.23, −0.09) (Fig. 2). The between-study heterogeneity was low to moderate (τ = 0.16, 95% CrI 0.09, 0.28), and sampling and convergence were sufficient (R̂ = 1.00 for all key parameters, adequate Bulk/Tail ESS) (Table 2, Appendix 5). The dose–response relationship was non-linear: the greatest reduction was observed with single-session durations of about 50–60 min, after which the effect rebounded and approached or crossed zero at > ~ 90–120 min per session. The number of intervention weeks showed an approximately linear negative correlation with the effect, with longer durations leading to greater reductions. In terms of total weekly volume (MET·min·wk⁻1), there was a continuous slight decrease in the ~ 0–1000 range, followed by a gradual rebound towards zero after > ~ 1500, reaching or slightly exceeding zero by ~ 2000 with increased uncertainty (Fig. 3). The meta-regression slopes for age and proportion of females were in the same direction as the overall effect, but their credible intervals both crossed zero, indicating no stable moderating effect. The funnel plot showed slight asymmetry, which had a limited impact on the main conclusion (Figure S5).

Discussion

Principal findings

This is the first meta-analysis to systematically quantify the effect of exercise on the TyG index in adolescents with overweight or obesity and to conduct an in-depth exploration of its dose–response relationship. Our core finding is that regular exercise intervention can effectively improve the TyG index, a key surrogate marker for insulin resistance. This effect is also reflected in its significant lowering effect on fasting blood glucose. Although exercise also showed a favorable trend in reducing triglycerides, the evidence on this point is not yet conclusive. Crucially, this study reveals that the effect of exercise is not a simple case of "more is better." We identified a non-linear "optimal effect range" relationship between exercise dose and metabolic improvement: for both single-session duration and total weekly volume, moderate doses yielded the most significant benefits, whereas excessively high or low doses could lead to diminished effects. From a behavioral perspective, this pattern of diminishing returns at high doses can be interpreted through the lens of the Health Belief Model. As exercise prescriptions escalate to very high weekly volumes, the increased time commitment, physical fatigue, and potential conflict with academic or social responsibilities are likely to raise adolescents' "perceived barriers" to adherence. Empirical studies applying the Health Belief Model in adolescent populations, such as those at high risk for obesity, have consistently identified perceived barriers as a key factor hindering healthy behavior adoption [39]. Since perceived barriers are among the strongest negative predictors of sustained exercise behavior [40], our findings generate the hypothesis that the attenuated benefits observed at the highest doses may partly reflect reduced long-term adherence, rather than a purely physiological ceiling effect. This hypothesis-generating insight warrants further investigation in future trials specifically designed to measure and address behavioral mediators. Interestingly, unlike single-session dosage, a longer total intervention period (more weeks) appeared to be linearly correlated with more sustained improvements. Furthermore, in this analysis, age and sex did not show a significant moderating effect on the intervention's effectiveness.

Comparison with previous studies

The core conclusions of this study are highly consistent with existing high-level evidence. For instance, previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses [20, 21] have already confirmed that regular exercise can significantly reduce traditional insulin resistance markers such as FBG and HOMA-IR in adolescents with overweight or obesity. Our analysis reaffirms the positive effect of exercise on FBG. However, regarding TG, although we observed a favorable downward trend, the pooled effect size was not statistically significant, and its evidence quality was rated as very low. This may be related to TG's high physiological variability, its sensitivity to short-term dietary changes, and the significant heterogeneity in intervention protocols and durations among the included studies [41, 42]. The metabolic adaptation of blood lipids may require a longer intervention period than blood glucose to manifest stably, and some short-term studies may have failed to capture its definitive changes [43]. Despite the inconclusive effect on TG alone, the definitive improvement in FBG, combined with the favorable trend for TG, collectively contributes to our most central finding: exercise significantly and stably reduces the TyG index. Nevertheless, the overall trend toward lower TG concentrations was consistent with the significant improvements observed for the TyG index and FBG, suggesting that longer intervention durations, higher exercise doses, or tighter control of dietary factors may be required to achieve statistically robust reductions in TG. Mechanistically, this integrated improvement has a solid physiological basis. In terms of glucose metabolism, exercise can activate signaling pathways like AMPK, promoting the translocation of the GLUT4 glucose transporter to the cell membrane, thereby increasing glucose uptake and utilization by muscles [16, 44]. Regarding lipid metabolism, regular exercise enhances the activity of lipoprotein lipase (LPL), accelerating the breakdown and clearance of triglycerides from the blood [18, 45]. Therefore, exercise effectively improves the TyG index by simultaneously acting on these two key links: glycemic control and lipid regulation. This highlights the advantage of the TyG index as an integrated marker, capable of capturing an overall metabolic improvement signal that a single, highly variable marker like TG might miss [46, 47]. The unique contribution of this study is that it moves beyond the focus of previous research on single or traditional IR markers [20, 21] and provides, for the first time, quantitative evidence for the clinical hypothesis that exercise intervention can improve the TyG index. Given the convenience and effectiveness of the TyG index [46, 48], this conclusion provides strong support for clinicians to use this simple marker to evaluate and monitor the effects of exercise interventions.

The non-linear dose–response relationship revealed in this study offers a more precise perspective for optimizing exercise prescriptions. We found that the metabolic benefits of exercise exist within an "optimal effect range," where moderate doses, both in terms of single-session duration and total weekly volume, yield the greatest benefits, while excessively high doses may lead to diminished effects. This finding echoes the conclusions of some previous studies. For instance, a network meta-analysis [20] indicated that moderate-to-vigorous training sessions of about 60 min were most effective for improving insulin resistance in adolescents. Similarly, another meta-analysis [49] found that combined training was superior to aerobic exercise alone, which also suggests that the balance between the "quality" and "quantity" of exercise is more critical than simply pursuing high volume. Furthermore, our meta-regression analysis did not find that the participants' mean age or sex composition had a significant moderating effect on the improvement of the TyG index from exercise. This result is consistent with findings from some observational studies; for example, one study [19] noted that the TyG index is itself a robust predictor across different ages and sexes in children and adolescents. Meanwhile, other systematic reviews [50] have suggested that adolescents may respond more consistently to interventions compared to adults, who exhibit greater heterogeneity. Together, these points indicate that within the recommended dosage range, the benefits of exercise intervention for improving the TyG index in adolescents may have good generalizability.

Clinical implications

The results of this study provide clear and actionable guidance for clinicians and public health practitioners. First, our analysis confirms that exercise intervention is an effective first-line non-pharmacological therapy for improving insulin resistance in adolescents with overweight or obesity. Clinicians can confidently recommend regular exercise as a core prescription. More importantly, this study highlights the immense value of the TyG index as a clinical monitoring tool. Since it only requires routine fasting blood glucose and triglyceride tests, doctors can conveniently and cost-effectively quantify the improvement in a patient's insulin sensitivity following an exercise intervention, without relying on expensive and complex tests, and adjust treatment plans accordingly. Second, the most critical clinical contribution of this study is the revelation of a non-linear dose–response relationship for exercise, meaning it is not a case of "more is better." Our data provide a basis for developing more precise exercise prescriptions: we recommend controlling single-session duration to around 50–70 min and aiming for a total weekly volume within the "optimal effect range" of 1000–1500 MET-minutes. Critically, our findings based on the Health Belief Model suggest that the diminished effects at high doses may be linked to increased "perceived barriers" (e.g., time burden, fatigue), which reduce adherence [39, 40]. Therefore, moving beyond a one-size-fits-all approach, clinicians should adopt a patient-centered strategy. This involves collaboratively setting realistic exercise goals that fall within the identified optimal range, proactively discussing and problem-solving potential barriers (such as time management and fatigue), and emphasizing the tangible benefits of moderate-dose exercise to enhance patients' self-efficacy and perceived benefits. This behavioral approach is essential to translate the prescription into long-term adherence and sustained metabolic health improvements. These findings align broadly with the WHO 2020 physical activity guidelines for children and adolescents, which recommend at least 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity per day [51]. Our identified optimal range of ~ 50–70 min per session and 1000–1500 MET·min/week provides a more fine-grained, evidence-based specification within this framework. This finding suggests that when guiding patients, clinicians should emphasize training "smarter" rather than just "harder," to avoid diminished effects or decreased adherence resulting from the pursuit of excessively high exercise volumes. At the same time, our results also underscore the importance of long-term adherence, as a greater total number of intervention weeks is associated with more significant health benefits, which can help encourage patients to establish sustainable, long-term exercise habits. Finally, our meta-regression analysis indicates that within the recommended dosage range, the benefits of exercise intervention have good generalizability across different ages and sexes among adolescents. This facilitates the provision of initial, universal exercise recommendations, making health advice more feasible to disseminate. In summary, this study provides comprehensive evidence-based support for clinical practice regarding "what to recommend (exercise)," "how to monitor (using the TyG index)," and "how to optimize (with precise dosage and long-term adherence)."

Limitations

The conclusions of this study should be interpreted in the context of the following limitations. First, the included studies exhibited significant clinical heterogeneity in their exercise intervention protocols. These programmes differed substantially in type (e.g., aerobic, high-intensity interval training, resistance training), intensity, single-session duration, and progression schedules. This diversity made it difficult to isolate the optimal effect of any single modality. Moreover, it is plausible that these qualitative differences contributed to the unexplained between-study variance (residual τ) observed in the meta-analyses of FBG and TG, even after accounting for quantitative factors like weekly exercise dose in our models. Second, according to the risk of bias assessment, most of the primary studies had "some concerns" regarding their methodology, particularly in the randomization process and the implementation of blinding, which could affect the stability of our pooled effect size. Furthermore, important potential confounding factors, such as pubertal staging, were rarely reported in the original studies, preventing us from adjusting for them in the meta-regression; consequently, some of the between-study heterogeneity could not be fully explained. Finally, although our assessment for publication bias did not find a significant impact, the slight asymmetry of the funnel plot suggests that publication bias from small-sample studies may exist(Supplementary Appendix 4).In addition, when calculating the standard deviation of the TyG index using the delta method, we assumed a fixed correlation coefficient (ρ = 0.5) between fasting TG and FBG because this parameter was not reported in the original trials; this simplifying assumption may have introduced some imprecision in the estimated variability of the TyG index. Therefore, future research requires higher-quality, larger-sample, and more uniformly reported randomized controlled trials to validate and refine the findings of this study.

Conclusion

Through the first Bayesian meta-analysis on this topic, this study confirms that exercise intervention significantly improves the TyG index in children and adolescents with overweight or obesity, serving as an effective non-pharmacological strategy to enhance their insulin sensitivity. Importantly, the benefits of exercise are not a case of "more is better," but rather exist within a non-linear "optimal effect range": moderate single-session durations and total weekly volumes yield the greatest benefits. Meanwhile, longer-term, sustained intervention is associated with more significant improvements, highlighting the importance of long-term adherence. These findings demonstrated good consistency across different ages and sexes among adolescents. In summary, this study provides key evidence-based support for clinical practice, advocating for the use of the TyG index as a monitoring tool and the formulation of precise, moderate exercise prescriptions to improve the metabolic health of adolescents.
