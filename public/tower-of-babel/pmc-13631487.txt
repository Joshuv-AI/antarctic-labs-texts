Prostatic Artery Embolization with Cyanoacrylate Glue for Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia: A Systematic Review and Single-arm Meta-analysis of Short-term Outcomes
Authors: Iago Vinicius Maia Hezel, Filipe Ramos Barra, Eduardo Germano Teixeira, Maria Clara Merighi, Elisa Carvalho Ferreira, Gustavo Regus Schuster, Raphael Braz Levigard, Bernard Campos Araruna Giancristoforo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis synthesized evidence on the safety and efficacy of prostatic artery embolization with cyanoacrylate glue for benign prostatic hyperplasia. PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials were systematically searched, and eight studies comprising 723 patients were included. Clinically meaningful improvements were observed in International Prostate Symptom Score (−10.44), prostate volume (−25.56 mL), and International Prostate Symptom Score–Quality of Life (−2.52). Subgroup analyses of follow-up up to 6 months showed no significant differences compared with longer follow-up. Adverse events occurred in 13% of patients. Mean fluoroscopy time was 26.64 minutes, and mean radiation exposure was 105.82 Gy·cm2. Overall, glue may be safe and effective in the short term, with apparently similar clinical outcomes and potential technical advantages compared to particles, as suggested by indirect comparison; however, long-term and high-quality evidence remains limited.

Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) represents a major global public health burden. In 2019, the global prevalence was estimated at 94 million cases, representing a 70.5% increase since the year 2000, with the highest prevalence observed among men aged 75 to 79 years (24,300 per 100,000) [1]. This condition may lead to prostate enlargement and is one of the main causes of obstructive lower urinary tract symptoms in men, significantly impairing patients’ quality of life. Management strategies range from conservative approaches and pharmacological therapy to surgical intervention. Surgical procedures, such as transurethral resection of the prostate, are still considered the gold standard therapy but are associated with potential complications, including bleeding, retrograde ejaculation, urinary incontinence, infection, and impotence. These complications have driven growing interest in minimally invasive alternatives, such as prostatic artery embolization (PAE).

PAE is currently recognized as an alternative for invasive interventions and is recommended for patients with BPH who refuse or cannot undergo surgery [2]. A recent meta-analysis comparing it to surgical procedures reported no statistically significant difference in efficacy, while PAE was associated with fewer adverse events [3]. Notably, only studies involving particulate embolic agents were included, and evidence regarding alternative materials, such as liquid glues, remains limited. Emerging reports on glue suggest comparable efficacy to particles in terms of both volumetric reduction and symptomatic improvement, along with similar adverse event profiles and shorter fluoroscopy times. Nonetheless, data on radiation exposure remain inconsistent across the studies. Therefore, the present study aims to provide pooled estimates to complement the existing evidence regarding the safety and efficacy of PAE using cyanoacrylate glue for the treatment of BPH.

Methods

This systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis was performed and reported in accordance with the Cochrane Collaboration Handbook for Systematic Review of Interventions [4] and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis statement guidelines [5].

Search strategy and data extraction

The search was systematically conducted across PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials from inception to December 10, 2025. The full search strategy employed is available in the supplementary material. The reference lists of all included studies, as well as from previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses, were manually searched for additional studies. The results were extracted and exported to Rayyan, where screening was performed independently by two authors without the use of automatic tools. In case of disagreement, a third author was consulted. After the identification of all eligible studies, data were extracted into a predesigned Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. The protocol for this meta-analysis was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) on September 5, 2025, under registration number CRD420251076705.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion in this meta-analysis was restricted to studies that met the following eligibility criteria: (1) randomized trials and observational cohorts; (2) enrolling patients with BPH; (3) an intervention arm involving PAE performed with cyanoacrylate glue. In addition, no time restriction was applied, and studies were included if they reported any of the outcomes of interest. When overlapping populations were identified, only the study with the longest follow-up was included. Population overlap was assessed directly with the authors when feasible or inferred based on coincident study periods, institutional settings, procedural characteristics, and eligibility criteria. Case reports, studies for which only the abstract was available, and registered trials without published results to this point were excluded.

Outcomes and subgroup analyses

Primary outcomes were improvement in prostate volume (PV) and symptom frequency and severity at the end of follow-up. Radiation exposure, fluoroscopy time (FT), and adverse events were also assessed as secondary outcomes. A subgroup analysis was conducted for studies reporting clinical outcomes up to 6 months versus those with longer follow-ups, as PAE is known to reach its peak efficacy at approximately this time point [6].

Outcomes definitions and measurement units

PV was assessed by magnetic resonance imaging and/or ultrasound before and after the procedure and was reported in milliliters (mL). Symptom frequency and severity were evaluated using the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) and the IPSS–Quality of Life (IPSS-QoL) scales [7, 8]. Radiation exposure was reported as the dose–area product (DAP), expressed in Gy·cm2, and FT was reported in minutes (min). DAP values reported in other units were converted to Gy·cm2 using standard SI conversion factors, as detailed in the supplementary material.

Quality assessment

Quality assessment was performed independently by two authors using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Interventions tool [9]. Disagreements were resolved by a third author, and the results were graphically presented using the Robvis tool [10].

Statistical analysis

A meta-analysis of each outcome was performed according to data availability. Studies that did not report the outcome or reported it in a way that could not be incorporated into data synthesis were excluded from the pooled analyses. For studies with multiple endpoints, data from the end of follow-up was used.

Bamshad et al. [11] reported each outcome based on different numbers of patients (N) due to the loss of data. Therefore, meta-analysis of each outcome was conducted based on its respective N, which is provided in each figure and table when applicable. In addition, Sawalha et al. [12] performed embolization with glue for numerous conditions, but only data concerning the BPH subgroup were incorporated into statistical analyses.

All the meta-analyses were performed with 95% CIs estimated through a REML random-effects model due to variability across studies. Heterogeneity was assessed with I2 statistics. p < 0.05 and I2 > 50% were considered substantial. On such occasions, leave-one-out sensitivity tests were performed. If heterogeneity persisted, exploratory meta-regression was performed for further investigation. A detailed description of the statistical methodology is available in supplementary material.

Results

Literature search

The initial search yielded 100 studies, and all of them were first screened by title and abstract. After removal of ineligible studies, 18 remained and were fully reviewed. Of these, eight studies were included according to eligibility criteria. A detailed flow diagram of the study selection process is presented in Figure 1. The included studies, seven retrospective cohorts and one non-randomized trial, comprise a total of 723 patients. Only one study used α-hexyl cyanoacrylate glue (AHCA) [12], while the remaining used NBCA. In both cases, it was mixed with iodized oil to achieve radiopacity and modulate polymerization, with dilution ratios ranging from 1:5 to 1:12 (mostly 1:8) depending on flow dynamics and embolization strategy. Three studies included a control group involving PAE performed with particles [13-15], while the remaining were single-arm designs. Follow-up periods ranged from 7.1 weeks to 3 years. Inclusion criteria included moderate-severe lower urinary tract symptoms (IPSS > 7, IPSS-QoL > 2) persisting for at least 6 months, inadequate response to medical therapy, impossibility or refusal to undergo surgery, and PV >30 to 45 mL. Exclusion criteria included prostate malignancy confirmed by biopsy, urinary tract infection, and advanced renal impairment. One study did not report the primary outcomes of interest [15]. Secondary outcomes

With respect to radiation exposure and FT, two studies did not report these outcomes [12, 13], and radiation exposure data from another study [16] could not be incorporated into the pooled analysis because it was measured using a different physical quantity from that reported by the other included studies. Mean radiation exposure during the procedure was 105.82 Gy·cm2 (95% CI: 47.33-236.59; I2: 99.6%), and mean FT was 26.64 min (95% CI: 23.73-29.56; I2: 88.1%). Six studies reported adverse events [11, 14-18], which occurred at a pooled proportion of 0.13 (95% CI: 0.08-0.22; I2 = 79.3%), and no major complications were documented (Figure 3). Adverse events included penile pain, hematoma, transient erectile dysfunction, urinary tract infection, glans necrosis, post-embolization syndrome, acute urinary retention, hematospermia, retropubic pain, prostatitis, catheter malfunction, epididymal-orchitis, and rectal edema with tenesmus. All events resolved spontaneously or with proper conservative management. Table 3 provides an overview of the technical features and complications reported in each study.

Sensitivity analyses

High heterogeneity was found in the analyses of PV, adverse events, FT, and radiation exposure.

For PV, heterogeneity was eliminated after the exclusion of Salet et al. [14], which was the study with the lowest IPSS at baseline and, thus, had a much smaller decrease in PV compared with the others. Therefore, heterogeneity may be explained by the intrinsic variability across study populations.

Sensitivity test for adverse events eliminated heterogeneity after exclusion of Bamshad et al. [11]. The low adverse event rate observed in this study, the largest cohort in this analysis, may reflect differences in reporting practices, follow-up duration, procedural expertise, or patient selection, and should be interpreted within this broader clinical context.

In sensitivity tests for FT and radiation exposure, heterogeneity remained elevated regardless of which study was excluded, indicating that no single study contributed exclusively to the heterogeneity.

Meta-regression

Exploratory univariate meta-regression analyses were conducted for RE and FT using age and PV as covariates, as well as to examine the association between both outcomes. One study previously included in the meta-analysis of both outcomes [15] had to be excluded from the meta-regression involving PV due to lack of data.

Meta-regression indicated that mean age was significantly correlated with radiation exposure (β = 24.02, p < 0.001), explaining 82.3% of the between-study heterogeneity; however, substantial residual heterogeneity remained (I2 = 91.6%). No significant associations were observed between radiation exposure and PV or FT, nor between FT and age or PV. The limited number of studies warrants cautious interpretation of the results. Detailed results and graphical analyses are provided in the supplementary material.

Quality assessment

Among the eight studies, four were judged to have a serious overall risk of bias [15-18], while the remaining four were rated as moderate. Domains 3, 6 and 7 were judged to have low risk of bias across most of the studies. Domain 1 (bias due to confounding) was rated as moderate to serious across studies. This primarily reflects the observational and non-randomized designs of the studies, which introduce substantial variability in pre-procedural characteristics across studies, notably IPSS and PV, and may lead to biased results. Domain 4 (bias due to deviations from intended interventions) was assessed only in the three studies that included a clearly defined control group [13-15] whereas the remaining single-arm studies did not permit the evaluation of this domain. Domains 2 (bias in selection of participants) and 5 (bias due to missing data) were both assessed as having a moderate risk of bias, primarily owing to the exclusion of some participants prior to study initiation and the presence of significant missing outcome data, respectively (Figure 4).

Discussion

Clinical efficacy

Our meta-analysis, which included 723 patients with symptomatic BPH, demonstrated consistent and clinically meaningful improvements in IPSS, IPSS-QoL, and PV following glue-based PAE. No statistically significant differences between the two subgroups (follow-up ≤ 6 months vs > 6 months) were observed. These findings suggest that PAE with glue may achieve clinically relevant improvements within the first 6 months after the procedure, with no statistically significant additional gains detected at longer follow-up intervals, a temporal pattern comparable to that reported in particle-based series [6].

A meta-analysis of 1,254 patients who underwent PAE with particles reported greater improvement in IPSS than our findings 12 months after the procedure (-16.2 vs -10.44) and a less pronounced mean reduction in PV (-20.3 vs -25.56 mL) [19]. This may be partially explained by variations in baseline PV between the two studies (77.3 vs 99.05 mL), because larger prostates tend to have a greater decrease in size and smaller decreases in IPSS, as suggested by multivariable analysis [14]. On the other hand, two of the included studies performed a direct comparison between glue and particles for efficacy and reported no statistically significant difference between the two techniques [13, 14]. Therefore, the difference between meta-analyses results may be attributed to the intrinsic variability of the populations, and glue-based PAE may provide outcomes within the range reported for particulate embolization, although no formal pooled comparison was performed.

Technical features

Due to its practicality and faster embolization, the use of glue permits shorter FT and, consequently, shorter procedure duration than particles, as reported by the only two studies that performed a direct comparison for this outcome [14, 15]. However, in only one of them did the lower FT result in less radiation exposure to patients [14]. After performing an indirect comparison with a meta-analysis of 22 studies using particles, which used the same methodology for data synthesis [20], the mean FT in our study was numerically lower (26.64 vs 49 min). Although mean radiation exposure was also lower (105.82 vs 181.6 Gy·cm2), the confidence intervals largely overlapped (47.33-236.59 vs 125.7-262.4). In the present context, and in contrast to conclusions drawn by some previous studies, it cannot be definitively stated that PAE performed with glue results in lower radiation exposure to patients. A reduction in FT does not necessarily correspond to a proportional decrease in overall radiation dose. Notably, meta-regression showed no statistically significant correlation between these outcomes. Dose–area product is strongly influenced by the number of angiographic acquisitions, cone-beam CT runs, and imaging parameters, rather than fluoroscopy duration alone. Although we observed a tendency toward lower radiation exposure with glue, the studies showed high variability in the results, which are comparable with particle series in some cases [21]. Our findings are in accordance with the observations of Sanghvi et al. [15] and Vizzuso et al. [18].

The high variability in the pooled analyses of FT and radiation exposure, which was not explained by sensitivity analysis or meta-regression, reflects the strong correlation between these parameters and the protocols of each center, the intrinsic difficulty of each patient procedure, and the experience of the interventionist. In addition, as also observed by Sanghvi et al. [15], unlike microspheres, FT and radiation exposure with glue did not have a clear correlation with PV, as also indicated by univariable meta-regression in our study. Therefore, the radiation profile of glue-based PAE might be protocol-dependent and cannot be inferred from fluoroscopy duration alone. Our study complements a recent narrative review suggesting a radiation advantage with glue by providing proper pooled statistical analysis [22]. Although the findings are in accordance with the literature, the results should be interpreted with caution because, given the limited number of included studies, associations may be unstable and the statistical power of meta-regression is limited.

Long-term outcomes

NBCA is also expected to reduce reintervention rates due to its combined effect of permanent distal and proximal arterial occlusion. In contrast, many patients who receive PAE with particles require reintervention, approximately 76% of them due to recanalization of the prostatic artery [23], which affects the long-term efficacy of the procedure. Similarly, particle series reported 23% of symptom recurrence after a mean of 67.4 months after PAE [6], and re-embolization rates of 3.3% of patients within 24 months [24]. No study included in our meta-analysis reported symptom recurrence or recanalization rates, possibly due to the short follow-up compared with studies using particles. Therefore, this outcome could not be assessed in our study, and any assumption regarding long-term superiority remains speculative.

Complications

Although some physicians may fear the risk of non-target embolization due to glue’s flow dynamics, its shorter polymerization time reduces the risk of reopening anastomoses and non-target embolization. Furthermore, lipiodol increases visualization of the embolic agent, and if there is any risk of glue migration to a dangerous collateral or reflux during embolization, it can be immediately stopped, unlike with microparticles, where inadvertent embolization of non-target structures can occur without clear visualization. Glue also carries risks, such as polymerization occurring too distally or too proximally, causing reflux and catheter adherence [25]. These factors likely explain why glue requires a steeper learning curve and may not be preferred by less experienced operators. Notably, despite ongoing concerns regarding glue safety, no major complications were reported in the included studies within the available follow-up. Minor complications occurred at a proportion of 0.13 and all resolved spontaneously or with conservative management. This is in line with adverse event rates in particle series (11%-36%) [6, 24], with serious complications reported in a minority of cases.

Limitations of the study

Due to the single-arm approach, this study precludes a direct comparison between glue and particles. Therefore, cross-study comparisons should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive.

In addition, the existing evidence regarding PAE with glue is still incipient; thus, the present meta-analysis is limited by the exclusive inclusion of non-randomized and observational studies. This design profile increases susceptibility to confounding and selection bias. Furthermore, the follow-up periods in the studies included were restricted to a maximum of 3 years and were predominantly 3 months, restricting the analysis to the short-term and limiting considerations regarding the long-term effectiveness of PAE with glue. Additionally, substantial heterogeneity in radiation reporting metrics may limit the external validity of pooled radiation analyses, while highlighting the need to standardize imaging protocols.

Despite the differences between the physical properties and dynamics of the cyanoacrylate glues used (AHCA and NBCA) [26], we could not perform a comparison between them given the limited number of studies that used AHCA.

Conclusions

PAE with cyanoacrylate glue may be a safe and effective minimally invasive option for alleviating BPH-related symptoms, providing short-term clinical and volumetric improvements possibly comparable to those reported with particulate embolization. It also likely offers the technical advantage of shorter FT, while its overall radiation profile appears to be protocol-dependent rather than intrinsically reduced. Glue also results in low complication rates, although it has a steeper learning curve. However, given the limitations of the existing evidence, and although the findings are consistent with the current body of literature, definitive conclusions regarding durability and comparative effectiveness and safety remain premature. Well-designed randomized controlled trials with larger cohorts and long-term follow-up are warranted to confirm these findings and clarify whether glue-based PAE may ultimately represent an equivalent or potentially advantageous alternative to microparticles. Ultimately, the choice of embolic agent should be individualized according to patient characteristics and operator experience.
