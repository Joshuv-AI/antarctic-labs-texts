Mapping the distribution of tuberculosis cases and associated factors identified through routine program implementation and community-based active screening in Central Ethiopia
Authors: Habtamu Milkias Wolde, Melak Getu, Getachew Seid, Delenasaw Yewhalaw, Gemeda Abebe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmear-positive TB patients greatly contribute to community-level transmission of this disease. Locating hotspots would make it easier to prioritize and target control interventions. This study aimed to assess the spatial distribution of smear-positive index TB cases and their secondary cases and the predictors of clustering of smear-positive TB cases.MethodsThis study was conducted in the Silti Zone of Central Ethiopia from 2020 to 2022. The data of smear-positive index TB patients were collected from the unit TB registries of healthcare facilities. Contacts of all index TB patients were screened in the community and tested to identify secondary TB patients. We performed spatial analysis, including Moran’s I statistic, the Getis-Ord Gi* statistic and geographically weighted regression (GWR), to assess the global distribution, local clustering and predictors of clustering of smear-positive TB patients, respectively. Additionally, we used inverse distance weighting (IDW) interpolation to predict the distribution of smear-positive TB cases and develop a continuous raster map for places with no data.ResultsSpatial autocorrelation analysis revealed that the distribution of smear-positive TB patients exhibited significant clustering (Moran’s I = 0.70029; p value < 0.000). The Getis-Ord Gi* output indicated the presence of statistically significant hotspots as well as cold spots in the study area. Significant hotspots were found in 11 Kebeles of the Silti, Dalocha and Misrak Silti districts. Significant coldspots were also found in five kebeles of the Silti and Misrak districts. GWR analysis revealed that no education, primary education, family size and thatched roof houses were significant predictors of the spatial clustering of smear-positive TB cases. We also found that the majority of the secondary TB cases were found in hotspots identified through spatial analysis.ConclusionThe study revealed a heterogenous distribution of smear positive TB in the study area and it could act as a model that can be replicated in other regions. The identified hotspots of TB could be targeted through location-based interventions such as systematic active screening in the form of outreach programs to improve the performance of TB prevention and control, including reducing the transmission of TB. Educational status, family size and housing type were some of the factors that significantly influenced the spatial distribution of smear-positive TB in the study area. The distribution of secondary TB cases found through household contact screening coincided with the identified hotspots, indicating greater transmission of the disease in these places.Keywords: Spatial analysis, Hotspots, Coldspots, Clustering, Tuberculosis, Ethiopia

Background

The global epidemiology of tuberculosis is uneven, and it mostly affects 30 countries with high burdens, which are located in sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia [1]. Even in these countries, the distribution of TB still tends to vary considerably. Both the incidence and prevalence appear to be concentrated in geographic areas with low socioeconomic status and dense populations [2]. Usually, areas within neighborhoods with higher concentrations of TB cases indicate the occurrence of local transmission, which is usually associated with unfavorable social determinants of health, such as poverty, overcrowding or marginalization [3]. Studies indicate that TB interventions targeting local hotspots could be effective in identifying and treating more patients which could interrupt continued transmission [4, 5]. However, for such interventions to be effective, localized hotspots or clusters should be identified based on routinely reported TB data and other assessments. Furthermore, such hotspots/clusters must be stable enough for some time until interventions are carried out. Identifying localized areas meeting such criteria could help TB programs prioritize interventions and make informed decisions to reduce TB incidence [6].

In most high-burden countries, TB programs rely on patients passively presenting themselves to healthcare facilities for TB-related symptoms to identify cases. With a slightly modified approach in some settings, all patients receiving both outpatient and inpatient services are screened for TB. However, all of these methods have several drawbacks. First, only patients with apparent symptoms are diagnosed, and those with milder symptoms could be missed. Patients with mild symptoms could also hesitate to visit a healthcare facility. Second, patients who have limited access to care due to various factors are also not reached and hence are not diagnosed [7, 8]. Studies show that in Ethiopia, approximately one-third of all TB cases are missed every year [9]. Active case finding interventions are occasionally implemented but are usually challenging in high burden settings due to the enormous resources they require. In addition, community-wide implementation of active case finding may not be effective [5]. Narrowing interventions to prioritized high-risk groups such as household contacts of TB patients is therefore necessary. Targeting geographic areas with a relatively greater burden of clustering of TB patients could also be another approach for focused intervention. Such spatially targeted interventions, however, are usually not implemented due to factors including lack of evidence on their effectiveness and uncertainty on whether routine data can accurately pinpoint geographic hotspots or clusters of TB cases [5].

The existence of heterogeneity in the distribution of TB cases means that geographic areas with greater clustering could be prioritized and targeted with interventions to identify more cases, while places with a dispersed distribution of cases could be addressed when resources are available [5, 10, 11]. This approach could be cost effective, especially in high burden settings where attempts to address all the various high-risk groups at the same time are usually not feasible and/or very challenging. It is well established that smear-positive TB cases are more infectious and contribute to the faster transmission of the disease in the community than smear-negative and extrapulmonary TB cases [12–14]. Hence, the advantages of identifying and targeting these particular groups of patients through active screening immediately after their diagnosis could be two-fold. First, the approach would narrow the interventions toward a few but effective transmitters of the disease, saving time and other resources. Second, it could contribute to identifying and treating more cases there by interrupting the transmission chain in the community [10, 11]. Hence, in this study, we aimed to map the distribution of smear-positive TB cases and their secondary cases and the factors associated with their distribution in the Silti Zone of Central Ethiopia. The specific objectives were to model how TB cases were distributed in the study area, to predict the pattern of clustering in areas without data points through interpolation and to identify the factors associated with the clustering of the smear positive TB cases.

Methods

Study setting and period

The current study was conducted in four districts and two town administrations in the Silti zone of Central Ethiopia. The four districts were Hulbareg, Silti, Dalocha and Mistrak Silti, whereas the two town administrations were Worabe and Kibet (Fig. 1). A district (Woreda) is the third administrative level in Ethiopia and has a well-defined population, administrative structures and clearly demarcated geographic boundaries. The districts and town administrations are further divided into 102 kebeles (the fourth and lowest administrative units in Ethiopia). The combined projected population of the study area for 2022 was 486,246 [15].

Map of the study area (this is the map of the four woredas/districts where the study was conducted)

Healthcare facilities in the zone provide TB diagnosis and treatment services. Health posts refer presumptive TB cases (starting in 2010) to hospitals and health centers where they are further assessed and tested for TB using either the GeneXpert test or smear microscopy. Patients identified to have TB are referred to health posts or referring health centers to continue their treatment. DOTS are provided by Health Extension Workers (HEWs), where patient adherence to treatment is supervised until the end of treatment.

Patients receiving TB treatment are referred to health centers or hospitals at the second, fifth and sixth months of treatment to assess progression and smear conversion using AFB microscopy [16]. Patients with severe weight loss are also provided with supplementary foods such as plumpy nuts, and their weight is regularly monitored during scheduled visits to health centers. According to the zonal annual performance report, in 2022, the case notification of all forms of TB in the study districts ranged from 60 per 100,000 people in Kibet town to 212 per 100,000 people in Silti district [17]. The current study was conducted from September 2020 to December 2022.

Study design

Initially, the routine programmatic implementation of TB services in the participating facilities was reviewed and the required data was extracted. The data of smear-positive index TB patients diagnosed up to one year before the commencement of the study was collected retrospectively. Then, the data of newly diagnosed smear positive TB patients was also collected prospectively from the unit TB registries of the selected healthcare facilities in the study districts. Furthermore, a systematic active screening of the household contacts of the smer positive index TB cases was conducted at the community level, which involved a house-to-house screening and testing. The data of the secondary TB cases found through the community based active screening was also used in this study.

Definitions

An index TB case is a smear-positive TB case that has been diagnosed through routine service and registered in the unit TB registers of the healthcare facilities in the study area and is the first TB case in the households during the study period. A smear-positive TB patient was defined as a patient for whom at least one sputum specimen was positive according to AFB smear microscopy. A secondary case is a TB case identified after screening and testing the household contacts of the smear-positive index TB cases.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Bacteriologically confirmed smear positive index TB cases aged 16 years or older registered in the unit TB registers of the study facilities were included into the study. Those having smear negative or extrapulmnary TB and aged less than 16 years were excluded as their risk of transmitting TB to their contacts is lower compared to smear positive TB cases. Index TB cases had to have at least one household member living with them, otherwise they were excluded. Index TB cases whose household contacts have been previously screened for TB were also excluded from the study.

Sample size estimation and sampling procedure

In this study, apart from the geographical mapping of the TB cases in the study area, the yield of secondary TB cases among household contacts of the index cases was estimated for two different (i.e., one time vs. two time) screening methods. Hence, a two-population proportion formula was used to estimate the sample size with 5% level of significance and 1.7% margin of error and using estimates from previous studies [1, 2]. The minimum sample size for was estimated to be 360 smear positive TB cases but a total of 387 were included into the study after data collection. Later after a community based systematic screening of household contacts, 23 secondary TB cases were identified and included in to this study.

The study participants (samples) were recruited and included both retrospectively and prospectively. Those index cases diagnosed one year before the commencement of the study were identified and included since the risk of developing TB among contacts is high until at least two years post exposure. But, the number of existing smear positive index TB cases were not adequate for the study. Hence, additional newly diagnosed smear positive index TB cases in the study facilities were included prospectively into the study until the required sample size was reached.

Data collection

Smear-positive index TB cases were identified from the unit TB registries of healthcare facilities registered from Jan 2019 to September 2021 with the aim of screening the household contacts of TB patients. After identifying the smear-positive index cases, the contact addresses of either the index case or treatment supporter were taken from the registries. Community-level house-to-house visits were then conducted to screen for household contacts. Individual data of index TB patients and their household contacts, including sociodemographic, environmental and clinical variables, were collected during the screening process. Additionally, household-level variables such as housing type and area, family size, number of windows and type of cooking methods used were collected. The geo-locations of the index TB cases were taken based on their household location during the data collection. For the purpose of protecting their privacy, coordinates were taken approximately 20 m away from the households. After community-level active screening and testing of sputum samples, additional secondary TB cases were identified. GeneXpert MTB/RIF assays and TB cultures were performed to identify secondary cases.

Data management and statistical analysis

Both spatial and nonspatial data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2019, where data coding, arranging and cleaning were also performed. It was also used to convert the data into a CSV (comma-delimited) file for use in ArcGIS. Various epidemiologic methods were used to analyze the geographical distribution of TB cases and the associated factors. Stata version 17 (Stata Corporation, College Station, Texas 77845, USA) was used to calculate descriptive statistics such as medians and interquartile ranges, frequencies and percentages regarding the characteristics of smear-positive TB patients and their households.

Spatial analysis

We used ArcGIS version 10.5 to analyze the existence of geographical variations in the distribution of smear-positive TB cases and to identify the factors associated with the variation in the distribution. Spatial autocorrelation was measured using the global Moran’s I statistic to assess and understand the overall distributional pattern of smear-positive TB cases in the study area. This test statistic is used to analyze the global distribution of the data across the study area, which produces values ranging between − 1 and + 1. A Moran’s I closer to -1 indicates significant dispersion of cases, whereas an index closer to + 1 indicates clustering of cases [18]. We used incremental autocorrelation to calculate the maximum peak distance where the spatial dependence/correlation of the cases became prominent. The maximum peak distance was then used to conduct the hotspot analysis. The Getis-Ord Gi* statistic was used to explore and identify the local hot spots and cold spots of smear-positive TB patients. After the hotspot analysis, we also used the inverse distance weighting (IDW) interpolation method using GIZ scores to predict the distribution of smear-positive TB cases for locations with no data closer to the study area. The interpolation method uses a linear combination of data at nearby measured places to predict the distribution of cases in locations that have not been measured [19].

Geographically weighted regression (GWR)

A set of various demographic, environmental and other variables were included in the spatial regression models to analyze the factors associated with the geographical distribution of the TB patients. We used ordinary least squares regression followed by a geographically weighted regression model for this purpose. Through bivariate regression models, variables with a p value < 0.2 were selected for inclusion in the multivariate regression model. Model diagnostics such as multicollinearity were checked on the final model to remove variables having a variance inflation factor (VIF) of six or more. We also used the residuals of the multivariate model to examine for the presence of spatial autocorrelation.

Results

Study population and TB incidence

The study area covered 102 kebeles in four districts and two town administrations in the Silti Zone. The population in the study area ranged from 3,000 to 12,000 per kebele. Of the 102 kebeles, 37 had at least one smear-positive TB case registered in healthcare facilities during the study period, and the number of cases ranged from 2 to 58 per kebee. The lowest notification rate was 100 per 100,000 people, while the highest notification rate was 1,390 per 100,000 people. After apply the inclusion criteria, a total of 12 cases aged < 16 years, seven cases living alone in the household and two cases with incomplete data were removed from the initial 409 smear postitive TB cases identified from the registers in the health care facilities. Hence, the data of 387 smear positive TB cases was considered for analysis (Fig. 2). Screening of the household contacts of the smear-positive index TB patients yielded 23 TB cases of all forms.

Characteristics of secondary TB patients

Through active screening of 1,276 household contacts of the 387 smear positive index TB cases, a total of 23 TB cases were identified. Among them, 22 were smear positive, while one was smear negative but culture positive. The majority (64%, n = 14) of the TB patients were male, and their median age was 28 years (IQR = 18–45 years). Approximately 60% of the patients had no education, and 34% had only primary education. Close to 92% of the TB patients had no history of previous treatment, and the majority (96%) were nonsmokers. Approximately 83% reported chronic cough, and 74% reported a productive cough. Only 13% of the patients reported blood-stained sputum, while approximately 30% had fever. While 35% of the patients reported night sweats, approximately 30% also reported weight loss. Nearly 17% reported chest pain, and approximately 65% reported chronic fatigue. The median duration between the observation of the first symptom and treatment initiation was 30 days (IQR = 23–30 days). The average daily duration of contact with the index TB patients was 12 h (IQR = 8–18 h).

Spatial distribution of TB cases according to the smear positive index in the study area

The results of spatial analysis indicated that the distribution of smear-positive TB patients was clustered in the study area. The global Moran’s I value was 0.70029 (p value < 0.000) (Fig. 3 left). The maximum distance at which the clustering of the TB cases peaked was 8 km based on the incremental autocorrelation result (Fig. 3 right).

Spatial autocorrelation (left) and incremental autocorrelation (right) of the Smear Postive Index of TB cases in the Silti zone, 2020–2021

The highest concentration of cases was observed in the towns of Warabet Shama, Werabe and Kibet in the study area, where the number of smear-positive TB cases ranged from 32 to 58 per Kebele. Birhan Kitkita, Ashute Burako and Dalocha towns also had higher distribution of smear-positive TB cases during the study period. Furthermore, the distribution map shows that greater clustering of smear-positive TB cases was observed in Kebeles which are located in the northern and northwestern parts of the study area. On the other hand, most of the Eastern, Southern and Southwestern parts of the study area had a lower distribution of smear-positive TB cases (Fig. 4).

Hotspots and coldsptos of smear-positive TB

Hotspot analysis was performed using the Gettis Ord GI* statistic to identify loal areas of hotspots and coldspots for smear-positive TB patients in the study area. GIZ scores and p values were compared to determine the statistical significance of the differences among the local clusters. The greater the GIZ score (both positive and negative), the greater the significance level. The results with high GI* outputs indicate hotspots, while low GI* outputs indicate coldspots.

As shown in Fig. 5 below, the red circles indicate statistically significant hotspots at the 99% confidence level. The orange circles indicate places with statistically significant hotspots at the 95% confidence level. On the other hand, the deep green circles indicate coldspots with 99% confidence, whereas the light green circles indicate coldspots with 95% confidence. Yellow circles indicate areas where there were no significant clusters.

Hotspot and cold spot distributions of smear-positive TB patients in the Silti Zone of Central Ethiopia, 2020–2021

Thus, statistically significant hotspots were found in Yeteker, Woliya 6, Boze Sabola, Koto Baloso, Asano, Aredol Anshebele, Dildate Mezer, Albazerzemu Shidger, Ashute Burako, Sada Gora and Balo Keriso Kebeles of the Silti, Dalocha and Misrak Silti districts. On the other hand, statistically significant coldspots were found in Dalocha town, Dalocha Telqasa, Burqa Delpa, Dube Godibamo and Dobo Bedono kebeles of the Silti and Misrak districts. The Hulbareg district had a significant hotspot at the Albazerzemu kebele but had no significant coldspot. Birhan Kitkita, Warabet Shama, Kibet Twon, Sinana Gerara, Anshabeso, Arat Ber, Dacha Geisila and Agode Lubrera all had no statistically significant clusters (Fig. 5).

Inverse distance weighting (IDW) interpolation based on the GIZ values predicted that most of the kebeles in the eastern, western and southern parts of the study area (shaded in green and yellow) had significantly lower clustering of smear-positive TB cases. However, the northern and central regions (shared in brown and white) had greater predicted clustering of smear-positive TB patients (Fig. 6).

Predictors of the spatial distribution of Smear postive TB patients

The 387 smear-positive TB cases were spatially aggregated into their respective 37 kebeles for the purpose of identifying hotspots and associated factors. Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression was first performed to assess the factors associated with the spatial distribution of smear-positive TB cases in the study sites in the Silti Zone. As shown in Table 3 below, the OLS model explained approximately 97% (adjusted R2 = 0.973) of the spatial variation in smear-positive TB cases in the Silti zone of Central Ethiopia. A robust p value was used to determine the statistical significance of the variables included in the final model since the Koenker (BP) statistic was significant. The OLS results also show that the joint Wald statistic is statistically significant (p < 0.001), which indicates that the overall regression model is significant. There was no multicollinearity, as evidenced by the small variance inflation factors (VIF < 7.5). Furthermore, the spatial autocorrelation results of the standardized regression residuals showed that they were not spatially correlated, i.e., they were randomly distributed (Moran’s I = -0.041951, p < 0.850) (Fig. 7).

According to the geographically weighted regression analysis, having no education, having a primary education, family size and number of thatched huts with grass cover were significantly associated with the distribution of smear-positive TB in the study area. Both no education and primary education were positively associated with an increase in the number of smear-positive TB patients. As the number of participants with no formal education increased, the number of smear-positive TB cases increased in Misrak Silti kebeles such as Tuto Zagere, Agode Lubrera, Dobo Bedeno and Kuno Kurtefa but had a relatively weaker association in the kebeles of the Silti district. Similarly, an increase in the number of participants with primary eduation had a strong positive association with increased smear-positive TB cases in kebeles of the Hulbareg district, such as Birhan Kitkita, Obisho Wacho and Kerate town. Family size also had a positive association with the number of smear-positive TB cases per kebele. An increase in family size had a strong positive association with an increased number of smear-positive TB cases in the kebeles of the Silti district. In contrast, this association was weaker in the Kebeles of Misrak Silti district. On the other hand, living in huts with grass cover was negatively associated with increased smear positivity. An increase in the number of houses with grass cover was associated with a decrease in smear positivity. This association was stronger in parts of the Misrak Silti district and weaker in the kebeles of the Hulbareg and Silit districts.

By testing 231 sputum samples from household contacts with symptoms of TB, an additional 23 TB cases of all forms were identified. Discussion

Despite the various efforts being made to control TB, TB remains one of the greatest challenges of the health system in Ethiopia. Evidence on its spatial epidemiology could be helpful in its prevention and control. In this study, we found that the distribution of smear-positive TB cases varied in different parts of the study area, and the notification rates ranged from 0.01 to 1.36%. The findings of the global Moran’s analysis confirm that the distribution of smear-positive TB cases in the study area is clustered. Numerous studies have reported the heterogeneity of the TB distribution and clustering of TB cases in certain geographic areas [3–5, 10, 20]. Most of these studies describe the distribution of all forms of TB, but we focused on smear-positive TB, as these are fewer but more efficient transmitters of the disease, mostly in high burden and resource-poor settings. It is known that smear-positive TB cases constitute the greatest share of the transmission of TB in the community [12], although smear-negative cases can also transmit the disease to a lesser extent [21]. Early identification and prompt treatment of such cases would prove to be effective in interrupting transmission and substantially reducing the incidence of new cases [14].

Hotspot analysis further revealed areas of statistically significant hotspots and coldspots of smear-positive TB patients. Kebeles in the Misrak Silti and Silti Districts had the most common significant static hotspots. Most of the coldspots were found in Dalocha district. A similar study conducted in the Sidama zone in Ethiopia is in agreement with our finding that significant clustering of smear-positive TB cases was found in 192 kebeles in eight districts [22]. A study in the Dabat district of Northern Ethiopia also described the clustering of smear-positive TB cases [23]. Another study in China on smear-positive TB also revealed global and local spatial autocorrelation of such cases, indicating the presence of significant hotspots as well as coldspots [24]. This finding can have two practical advantages in terms of narrowing the scale as well as the areas of intervention. Programs could be cost- and time-effective by prioritizing smear-positive TB patients by focusing on hotspot areas. Previous studies have consistently confirmed the advantage of spatially targeted screening of contacts to improve the yield of TB [10, 11].

In this study, we used the inverse distance weighting (IDW) interpolation method to predict the distribution of smear-positive TB cases in places other than the measured points, thereby developing a continuous rasterized map. According to the results, significant hotspots lie in the central and northern parts of the study area, with the highest concentration of smear-positive TB cases occurring in Yeteker, Woliya 6 and Boze Sabola kebeles. These places would benefit from a targeted screening of either the entire population or contacts of index cases. On the other hand, significant dispersion of smear-positive TB cases was observed in the southeastern parts of the study area. Such predictions for parts of an area with no measured data are helpful for making informed decisions, including prioritization and resource allocation [25, 26]. Studies indicate the beneficial application of IDW in identifying the geographical heterogeneity of TB and the direction of tailored strategies in specific geographic areas [25–27]. For instance, such data could be used for systematic surveillance of TB, which could improve the coverage and performance of TB control [23].

The GWR analysis revealed that no formal education, primary education, family size and the presence of tachtched houses with grass cover were significantly associated with the number of smear-positive TB patients in the study area. A possible explanation for the association between education and smear-positive TB patients could be that less educated individuals are more likely to have inadequate awareness of TB [28, 29], and lower education could also be a barrier to prompt diagnosis and treatment [28]. Furthermore, the more individuals are uneducated/less educated in a given area, the more likely they are to face or live in poverty, which is one of the known determinants of TB [30]. A larger family size is associated with a greater risk of developing TB [31] and is also directly linked to crowding. Crowded living conditions are also known to increase the prevalence of TB [32]. In this study, hut houses with grass cover were negatively linked to smear-positive TB, unlike the other factors. These houses, which are usually built in rural areas, have a single door and room, but the walls usually do not reach the ceiling thereby allowing adequate ventilation for the household.

We identified 23 secondary TB cases of all forms through screening and testing household contacts of smear-positive index TB cases. The screening of household contacts was not geographically targeted. However, the distribution of the identified secondary cases closely followed the distribution of the smear-positive index TB cases. More secondary cases were identified in areas with clustering of smear-positive TB cases, and fewer or no cases were identified in areas with nonsignificant clustering as well as in areas with significant coldspots. The secondary cases identified in kebeles, such as Ashute Burako and Woliya 6, corresponded with some of the statistically significant hotspots of smear-positive TB cases we found. Similarly, the absence of secondary TB cases in Dalocha district corresponds with the finding that the district was mostly a cold spot with a smaller/dispersed distribution of smear-positive TB cases. This finding again confirms the importance of targeted intervention to improve case detection as well as reduce the transmission of the disease in the community [10, 11].

The use of GIS has pinpointed the areas with statistically significant hotspots/clustering of smear-positive TB cases, which would otherwise be difficult through other means of statistical analysis. The other strength is the use of geographically weighted regression to identify predictors that directly link the coefficients of the predictors with specific places in the study area. The study also used both secondary data collected through routine services and primary data collected through community-level houses for house screening to assess relationships. On the other hand, the study had several limitations. Some important kebele-level aggregate predictor variables were not included during the data collection. The temporal dimension of the clustering of TB was not assessed, as only two years of data were used. This would have informed about the stability of the spatial clusters over a period of time. Additionally, smear negative TB cases could play some role in the transmission of TB which were not considered in this study. The other limitation was the unexpected occurrence of the Covid 19 pandemic in the midst of the study which might have affected the the number of smear positive index TB cases that could have been identified in the registers of the healthcare facilties. However, the magnitude of all the mentioned shortcomings had no significant impact on the findings and validity of the study.

Conclusion

Our study provides an important contribution to the understanding of the distribution of TB in the study area and is a model that can be replicated in other similar settings with higher prevalence of TB. Significant hotspots of smear-positive TB cases were found in the study area. They can be targeted by location-based interventions such as active screening to identify and treat more TB cases which in turn reduce the transmission of TB in the community. Socioeconomic factors such as educational status, family size and housing type were found to significantly influence the spatial distribution of smear-positive TB in the study area. Locations of the secondary TB cases identified through household contact screening coincided with the hotspots identified through spatial analysis, which indicated a greater transmission of the disease in these places.
