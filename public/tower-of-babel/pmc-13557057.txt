Shedding light on the formation mechanisms of Islamic polychrome lusters: A Cu, Fe, Ag micro XANES study
Authors: Ruth Sadurní, Judit Molera, Clement Hole, Hugo Alexander Suarez Orduz, Judith Juanhuix, Marine Cotte, Trinitat Pradell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Islamic luster is a ceramic glaze decoration exploiting the quantum-confined optical response of noble metallic nanoparticles to produce a colorful metallic and iridescent appearance. Upon firing, silver and copper initially present in an overpaint diffuse into the glaze and are reduced to Ag0 and Cu0 nanoparticles. This study examines the relationship between luster colors and paint composition. The six analyzed shards are 9th century Abbasid polychrome lusters exhibiting green, red, ochre, and brown colors. Key chemical factors are revealed by imaging 16 luster cross sections with different micro- and nanoanalytical methods, including UV-Vis spectroscopy, Ag L3-, Cu-, and Fe K-edge micro x-ray absorption spectroscopy (μ-XAS), micro x-ray diffraction (μ-XRD), and field emission scanning electron microscopy (FIB-FE-SEM). These factors connect together (i) the Cu/Ag ratio; (ii) the Cu, Ag, and Fe speciation; (iii) the nature, size, and concentration of metallic nanoparticles; and (iv) the luster color. Notably, the Cu/Ag ratio balances the competitive reactions between Ag+ reduction and Cu+ disproportionation.

INTRODUCTION

Luster is a ceramic glaze decoration with a colorful metallic and iridescent appearance that exploits the quantum confined optical response of metallic nanoparticles [surface plasmon resonance (SPR)] (1–3). It consists of a submicrometric layered structure beneath the glaze surface, with a total thickness ranging from a few hundred nanometers to several micrometers, containing metallic silver and/or copper nanoparticles, which exhibits a wide range of colors (green, yellow, ochre, red, and brown) with a metallic (golden, coppery, and silvery) and iridescent (bluish and purplish) appearance (4–12). Although this nanostructure may bring to mind a product of modern nanoscience and technology, the fact is that the first luster was produced at least 1300 years ago in the Abbasid caliphate (750–1250, probably Bashra, present Iraq) (13, 14). Consequently, the study of historical luster layers has attracted much interest from the scientific community, notably regarding the materials and processes used in their production, their optical properties, and their relationship with the luster nanostructure (1, 3–11, 15–21).

To obtain traditional lusters, a paint rich in metal ion (Ag+, Cu+, and Cu2+) is applied at the surface of a previously fired glazed ceramics. Through a second firing in a wood-fueled kiln, the paint metal ions diffuse into the glaze surface through an exchange with glaze alkali ions (Na+ and/or K+). During the final heating stage, gases produced by the wood combustion reduce the metal ions to their metallic state leading to nucleation and growth of metallic copper (Cu0) and/or silver (Ag0) nanoparticles and, in some cases, cuprite (Cu2O) (17). To enable this process, the luster must be fired at a temperature above the glass transition temperature of the glaze to increase the ionic diffusivity of the alkali ions and below its softening temperature to prevent the luster paint from sticking to the glaze. After the firing, the paint residue is rubbed off to reveal the luster layer underneath. Analyses of remaining paint found on archeological objects (22, 23), as well as historical references (24–26), show that it is essentially composed of copper and silver salts, a sulfur-containing compound (Hg-, Cu-, Fe- or As- sulfides, or Fe/Cu mixed sulfates), tin-lead calx, and gum Arabic, the latter replaced in later recipes by a clay rich in iron oxides. Besides, the presence of reducing agents in the glaze—such as Fe(II), Sb(III), or Sn(II)—can also favor the precipitation of the metallic copper and silver particles (27, 28). Other metallic nanoparticles, such as Au and Pt, have been reported in lusters in more modern productions. In these cases, the particles are generally obtained by incorporating a solution of the metallic ions in regal acid into the glaze precursor before firing instead of ionic exchange process (29, 30).

The composition of the paint and the glaze, combined with the firing procedures, result in different luster nanostructures and, consequently, in different colors and metallic or iridescent appearances. The yellows, greens, browns, and the reds are due to the dominant dipolar contribution to the SPR absorption cross section of silver nanoparticles (≤30 nm) and copper nanoparticles (≤50 nm), respectively (2, 3). Larger nanoparticles have higher SPR multipolar contributions, responsible for the splitting and redshift of the silver absorption peak, resulting in a change of color, and the increase in the scattering contribution that produces a blue-purple iridescence (31, 32). However, the most distinctive feature of luster is the metallic-like shine under reflected light: golden (2) or coppery (18) for silver and copper nanoparticles respectively. Similar to photonic materials (3), the optical response of an ensemble of particles is no longer due to the scattering by individual particles but rather to the collective effect (33), producing intense colors and a strong reflectivity.

Noteworthy, the glaze composition impacts the ion diffusivity and, consequently the volume fraction of metallic nanoparticles at the surface of the glaze. For instance, large divalent cations such as lead are known to reduce the ion diffusivity (20, 34). This leads to both more concentrated and larger nanoparticles, increasing the multiscattering and favoring the metallic-like shine (31–33). However, a too low diffusivity may result in the coalescence of the nanoparticles, resulting in a silvery or dark dull appearance of the decoration (35). Besides, a stronger reducing atmosphere could have a similar impact.

Furthermore, the role of the different ions present in the luster paint (i.e., Cu2+, Cu+, and Ag+) and those present in the glaze (Fe2+ and Fe3+) on the luster optical properties, particularly its color, has not been investigated thoroughly (2, 5–7, 35). Although several attempts have been made to determine the speciation of copper and silver in the luster layer, the submicrometric thickness has prevented the analysis across the luster layer, and the measurements have always been made directly on the surface (5–7, 36). This procedure does not give any information about the speciation across the luster layer, i.e., where the nanoparticles are present and underneath. This is addressed in the present study, which focuses on a series of luster samples belonging to the oldest production, the 9th century Abbasid luster (Abbasid Caliphate, 750–1258 CE). The production is characterized by the use of polychrome luster, with green, yellow, ochre, brown, and red decorations on a single object (13, 37). The sample corpus is particularly exceptional as four polychrome samples display together green, ochre, and brown or red-brown decorations, ensuring that these colors were obtained with the same firing conditions. The corpus additionally includes two polychrome red fragments, already precharacterized (34), and which complement very well the palette. While it is unlikely that these ceramics were obtained during the exact same firing, they all belong to the same production, making their firing conditions comparable.

Considering that the color and shine of the luster layers depend on the nature of nanoparticles (Cu0, Ag0, and Cu2O), thickness, and volume fraction of the particles in the luster layer, as well as on the type of ions involved in the production, a series of microanalytical techniques were combined to image cross sections across the luster layer and the glaze underneath. These included focused ion beam and field emission scanning electron microscopy (FIB-FE-SEM), micro x-ray diffraction (μ-XRD) to determine the nature of the nanocrystalline particles and micro x-ray absorption spectroscopy (μ-XAS) to determine the depth-resolved speciation of copper, silver, and iron ions. These results were correlated to ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy and exploited for theoretical calculations of the extinction and scattering cross sections.

RESULTS

Color characterization

Figure 1 shows the six sherds selected for this study. All of them were obtained from the Ashmolean Museum and were produced in the same workshops. ALX242, ALX245, and ALX244 were sampled on both the external (E) and internal (I) surfaces, and each exhibit polychrome lusters with green, red, brown, and ochre decorations on a white background. By contrast, P624 and P717 (37) exhibit an overall red color accompanied by silvery and black decorations. P51, (36) is decorated in a style similar to that of ALX245, featuring green, ochre, and brown luster decorations. This object was sampled only on the external side, where two luster layers (green and yellow) were found to overlap.

The color of the lusters was measured using UV-Vis spectroscopy (Fig. 2A and table S1). The absorption and scattering of the light by the metal nanoparticles is dominated by the collective resonance of the free electrons resulting from surface polarization, known as SPR. For small particle sizes (below 30 nm in diameter for Ag0 and 50 nm for Cu0), absorption dominates the optical response (2, 12, 18, 38). Conversely, for larger particles, scattering dominates the optical response and is responsible for the iridescence, which was not observed at the macroscale in our samples. When there is a high density of particles in the layers, an increased reflectance gives rise to the effect of golden-like and coppery-like shine for silver and copper lusters, respectively. In our samples, golden reflectance is observed in the green lusters and coppery reflectance in the red luster of P717-E (2, 12, 18). The wavelength and shape of these resonances depend on the optical functions of the metals involved and of the dielectric constant of the matrix (glaze). Therefore, the color displayed by the luster layers is mainly related to the metal species present and the size of the particles. As can be seen for the different luster colors in Fig. 2A, ochres and greens contain principally silver nanoparticles (SPR band around 410 nm) whereas red-browns and browns also contain copper nanoparticles (additional band at 560 nm). The Cu to Ag ratio is higher in red-brown and lower in other brown lusters. In the case of red lusters, only the band of copper nanoparticles is detected. Figure 2B shows the calculated SPR position and width as a function of the silver particle size using two refractive indices for the glass matrix (2). When the particle diameter decreases below 10 nm (referred to as clusters), the peak position increases slightly but, more importantly, becomes very broad, resulting in a shift in color from green to ochre. The width of the silver SPR indeed suggests the presence of very small particles in the ochre lusters compared to the green ones. In the case of Cu nanoparticles, these marked shifts in function of particle size are not observed (12).

Elemental composition, nature of the crystalline phases, and distribution across the glaze

The composition of the glaze itself is a crucial factor as it determines the viscosity, diffusivity, and solubility of the various species involved in the luster formation, notably its composition, micro- and nanostructure, and optical properties. The chemical composition of the glazes was measured using FE-SEM-EDS on the glaze cross section, averaging signal from the crystals and the glassy matrix. The nanoparticles were too small to measure their exact composition with this method. However, the particles nature (pure metal or alloy) could be determined by μ-XRD on thin cross sections (∼100 μm) cut from small fragments of the samples embedded in epoxy resin. In some cases (Table 1), the shift of peak positions of the silver nanoparticles revealed a substitution of Ag by Cu up to about 3% (39). Data were acquired in transmission mode, at the XALOC beamline (40) of the ALBA synchrotron light source (Cerdanyola, Spain).

As detailed in table S2, the elemental compositions of the glazes are comparable, the main differences lying in the concentrations of the heavy elements such as tin and lead, which vary between samples and between the inner and outer sides of the same object. As these are bowls and dishes, it is the interior that is visible, and consequently, the internal glaze is thicker and more elaborately decorated. Elemental analyses revealed that the inner glaze contains higher concentrations of tin and lead. The presence of cassiterite (SnO2) was confirmed by μ-XRD (see Fig. 3). This phase is a common opacifier as it scatters light very efficiently due to its high refractive index, which explains its predominant presence in the more refined internal decorations. In these productions, lead-tin calx (a mixture of PbO, SnO2, and Pb2SnO4) was deliberately added to increase the opacity of the inner glazes to which the luster decorations were applied. Quartz, cristobalite, and pyroxenes were also identified in the glazes and are characteristic of this production (36).

The copper and silver content measured by FE-SEM-EDS directly from the surface of the luster decorations reveals a strong correlation with the luster color (Table 1). Green and green-brown have a Cu/(Cu + Ag) ratio of between 10 and 30%, ochre-brown round 60%, ochre around 80%, red-brown between 86 and 90%, and red above 90%. Regarding the nature of the phases, as measured by μ-XRD (Fig. 3), only metallic silver nanoparticles are detected in the green luster, only metallic copper nanoparticles are in the red luster, and a mixture of silver and copper nanoparticles is in the red-brown and green-brown lusters. Copper nanoparticles predominate in the red-brown luster, while silver nanoparticles predominate in the green-brown luster. This is fully consistent with the above interpretation of the UV-Vis spectra. Regarding the ochre and ochre-brown decorations, only a very broad peak (around 24°) could be detected in the μ-XRD pattern of sample ALX245I-O, suggesting the presence of very small silver nanoparticles, as hypothesized from the UV-Vis spectroscopy data.

Complementarily to the FE-SEM-EDS measurements performed on the sherd surfaces, it is also relevant to investigate the distribution of copper and silver through the glaze structure, bearing in mind that these metals were introduced into the glaze via an ionic exchange from the paint. Therefore, cross sections of the different luster colors were prepared by FIB and analyzed using FE-SEM-EDS. Figure 4 shows a selection of images of the corresponding copper and silver depth profiles for all the colors. The images highlight notable differences in both particle size and distribution. As illustrated in Fig. 4B, all colors except ochre and ochre-brown contain nanoparticles with a diameter of ∼10 to 15 nm, forming a thin layer of ∼1-μm thickness near the surface (at a depth of around 200 nm below). This layer is usually thinner in the greens than in the reds and browns. In contrast, no nanoparticles are visible in the ochre and ochre-brown samples, which is consistent with the broad μ-XRD peak and the broad UV-Vis band. These results suggest that the particles in these colors are much smaller (i.e., below the detection limit for FE-SEM images). In addition, the copper-rich layer is much thicker, ⁓10 μm, compared to the other colors (Fig. 4C). On the basis of these observations, in the following discussion, the term “luster layer” will refer to the nanoparticle-rich layer, and “underlying glaze” will refer to the layer below where the silver and copper are still present, albeit at a lower concentration. This latter layer differs from the bulk glaze, in which copper and silver are absent.

In terms of elemental concentrations, the black dots in Fig. 5 correspond to the edge jump intensity of the XAS data for each element, and they are proportional to the concentration of each element in a first approximation. Therefore, they can be qualitatively compared with the depth profiles in Fig. 4. However, the larger size and greater penetration of the x-ray beam, [∼0.8 μm by 0.5 μm (hor × ver) and tens of microns, respectively] compared to the FE-SEM beam (5 nm by 5 nm and 1 μm), limit the spatial resolution of the x-ray data. Conversely, the limit of detection is better for the x-ray beam than for the FE-SEM-EDS. The results obtained using both techniques are consistent and complementary. Consequently, thicker layers are estimated using x-rays than electrons.

Regarding species concentrations and distributions, results vary across depth and across the different luster colors. The multilayer profiles result from the competition between two phenomena: (i) precipitation of the metallic nanoparticles and (ii) diffusion of the ionic species. The latter depends on the charge, size of the ions, and the presence of metallic nanoparticles. The first observation is that the luster layer (i.e., containing metallic nanoparticles) can be distinguished, as qualitatively highlighted by the vertical dotted lines. The presence and distribution of the metallic species are consistent with μ-XRD, UV-Vis, and FE-SEM analyses (see Fig. 4).

Deep in the underlying layer (about 20 μm), the speciation of Cu and Fe is comparable, regardless of color, with ∼75% of Cu(I) and 25% of Cu(II) and 75% of Fe(II) and 25% of Fe(III), respectively. When detected, silver is only present as Ag(I) in this layer (see Fig. 5A).

Regarding the green and green-brown lusters (see Fig. 5, A and B), which have a high silver content [⁓20% Cu/(Cu + Ag) and ⁓30% Cu/(Cu + Ag), respectively], silver is mainly present as Ag0 in the luster layer. In the case of the green-brown specifically, metallic copper is also present, while Cu(II) is very low. Conversely, there is no Cu0 in the green luster, but the Cu(II)/Cu(I) ratio increases significantly (up to 50%). Last, there is also a slight increase in Fe(III) in both lusters.

In the ochre-brown and ochre lusters (Fig. 5, C and D), which have a higher copper content [⁓60% and ⁓80% Cu/(Cu + Ag), respectively], the silver is also mainly present as Ag0, and Cu0 is not detected. This is consistent with μ-XRD and UV-Vis data. Regarding the copper and silver species in the ochre-brown luster (Fig. 5C), the cross sections reveals three layers: from 0- to 8-μm deep, metallic silver and relatively high Cu(II); from 8 to 17 μm, ionic silver only but still high Cu(II); and below 17 μm, a decrease of Cu(II) to reach the deep underlying layer composition discussed above. In this case, Ag forms very small clusters of metallic silver (indeed undetectable in the FE-SEM images; Fig. 4), which let Cu ions diffuse further inside the glaze. Conversely, the high density of nanoparticles in green lusters limited this diffusion, resulting the absence of this intermediate layer. In the ochre, copper speciation remains constant throughout the stratigraphy. On average, iron is slightly more oxidized at the surface than below, but the differences are minimal.

The red-brown and red lusters (Fig. 5, E and F) are copper-rich [⁓90 to 100% Cu/(Cu + Ag)]. In these cases, the silver content was insufficient to determine the silver speciation. In the thin (∼5 μm) surface layer, copper is present as a mixture of Cu(I) and Cu0 nanoparticles. At the interface between the two layers, a slight increase in Cu(II) can be observed before reaching the proportion of the underlying layer. No significant change in the iron speciation is observed across the entire stratigraphy.

Overlapping luster colors: The case of sample P51-G-Y

Sample P51E-G-Y is unique because it contains two luster paints, one green and one yellow, which are applied side by side and partially overlap (Fig. 6A). In the analyzed region, the yellow luster is found beneath the green layer. Speciation analysis reveals the presence of four layers (Fig. 6D). Silver is present as Ag0 in the surface of the green luster [Fig. 6C, layer (1)]. In layer (2), the proportion of Ag(I) increases rapidly, together with a decrease in the total silver content. At a depth of ∼15 μm, the total silver content and Ag0 increase again, forming a thick (∼20 μm) layer (3), which is attributed to the second yellow luster layer. Although the amount of metallic silver increases, it is less that in the green layer. In this case, the particles are too small to be observed with FE-SEM (Fig. 6B). In layer (4), metallic silver disappears with only a small amount of silver present as Ag(I). In layers (1) and (3), copper appears to be relatively more oxidized [higher proportion of Cu(II)] than in layers (2) and (4). This is consistent with the above data, which showed positive correlation between the Cu(II)/Cu(I) ratio and the Ag0/Ag(I) ratio in the green luster. This again indicates that copper plays a role in reducing silver to the metallic state. Regarding the iron speciation, iron also appears to be slightly more oxidized in layers (1) to (3) than in layer (4) (Fig. 6E). These results suggest that the iron from the glaze may also be involved in the precipitation of the metallic silver nanoparticles, as observed in the ochre cases.

DISCUSSION

Chemical reactions

In this study, 16 cross sections sampled from six polychrome shards from the oldest luster production (9th century Abbasid) were analyzed using a combination of UV-Vis, FE-SEM images, μ-XRD, and μ-XAS analyses. These analyses revealed that the noble metal-enriched areas within the luster layers can generally be divided into two distinct layers. The most superficial layer (∼1- to 10-μm thick) contains metallic silver and/or copper nanoparticles, as well as Cu(I) and Cu(II). In the case of Ag-containing lusters, Fe is found slightly more oxidized in this layer. In good agreement with this result, in a recent experiment designed to evaluate the role of iron oxide (Fe2O3) added to the luster paint in developing luster layers (46), we found that the presence of Fe(II) in the glass played an active role in reducing silver and copper ions to their metallic states once the ions entered the glass via ionic exchange. In this experiment, a soda-lime blown glass substrate was used to avoid the presence of tin in modern float glass. However, the substrate contained 0.06% FeO as an impurity. This was sufficient to facilitate the precipitation and growth of metallic nanoparticles. The underlying glaze layer contains only ionic copper [both Cu(I) and Cu(II), in a ratio of about 3:1], and silver in the form of Ag(I) only, but no crystalline compounds. Consequently, the layer containing the metallic nanoparticles only forms part of the luster decoration itself. The presence of ionic copper and silver beneath this layer indicates that both species diffuse deeper than the layer containing the nanocrystalline particles.

As detailed above, the color of lusters depends on the nature, size and concentration of metallic nanoparticles and is related to the Cu/Ag ratio. Broadly speaking, three main colors can be distinguished: silver rich green lusters [below 30 atomic % (at %) Cu/(Cu + Ag)], silver and copper ochre lusters [between 60 and 80% at % Cu/(Cu + Ag)], and copper-rich red lusters [above 85% at % Cu/(Cu + Ag)]. In all cases, browner equivalents of each color exist, containing a higher total concentration of the two metals. Schematic representation of the distribution of Ag, Cu, and Fe species present in the green (A), ochre (B), and red (C) lusters. Cu(I) is present everywhere and it is not shown. The blue and orange arrows indicate the diffusion of silver and copper ions inside the glaze respectively.

Regarding element distributions and chemical reactions, silver nanoparticles predominate in the green, green-brown, ochre and ochre-brown lusters. In addition, the luster layer contains higher proportions of Cu(II) and Fe(III) than the underlying glaze (see Figs. 5, A to C, and 6D). This suggests that the reaction Cu(I) + Ag(I) → Cu(II) + Ag0 and, to a lesser extent, Fe(II) + Ag(I) → Fe(III) + Ag0 contribute to the formation of the luster layer. In the ochre lusters, the silver content is low compared to copper, and only small silver clusters form. Cu(I) ions diffuse quickly and deeply, producing a thick, copper-rich layer. Silver ions diffuse more slowly than copper ions and Cu(I) ions diffuse faster than Cu(II) ions (47, 48).

Conversely, silver is either absent or present in insignificant quantities in the red-brown and red lusters (see Fig. 7C). These lusters have a thin, concentrated layer of metallic copper nanoparticles (⁓1- to 2-μm thick). An accumulation of Cu(II) can be observed just below the luster layer (see Fig. 5F), while the oxidation state of the iron remains unchanged throughout. Hence, iron does not seem to be involved in the precipitation of the copper nanoparticles. Regarding Sn, the concentrations measured are relatively low (below 3 weight %). Furthermore, it was added in these glazes as a calx (oxidized mixture of lead and tin oxides, SnO2, PbO, and Pb2SnO4). It has been found that when Sn was added as a Sn(IV) compound in a soda-lime glass, Sn was present in the glass as Sn(IV) (49). In our case, we have found that tin is present as cassiterite crystals in the glaze (SnO2) (37), as detected by XRD (Fig. 3). Tentative measurements were made at the Sn L3 edge to assess the speciation of Sn diluted in the glassy matrix; however, overlaps with the potassium K emission lines affected spectra quality too much to get any reliable conclusion. Hence, the presence of Sn(II) acting as a reducing agent cannot be fully ruled out. This leads us to hypothesize that the solubility of copper under reducing conditions may result in metallic copper being formed through the disproportionation reaction 2 Cu(I) → Cu0 + Cu(II) (50), with the diffusion of Cu(II) into deeper regions. This mechanism has also been proposed for the development of copper red glasses by annealing (51).

Interpretation in terms of manufacturing process

As mentioned in the introduction, luster is obtained through an ion-exchange process whereby copper and silver ions diffuse from the paint into the glaze, replacing the alkali ions. To enable this process, the luster must be fired at a temperature between the glass transition temperature and glaze softening to favor ionic diffusivity and prevent the luster paint from sticking to the glaze. The glass transition (Tg) and deformation (Td) temperatures correspond to the viscosity values of log(η, Pa·s) = 12 and log(η, Pa·s) = 10.5, respectively. The viscosity of the glass can be estimated within the softening range from the glass composition and the Vogel-Fulcher-Tammann viscosity-temperature relationship (52). Considering the composition of the glazes (table S2), we determined Tg ≈ 550°C and Td ≈ 590°C, which provides an estimate of the luster firing temperature range.

This process requires an oxidizing atmosphere in the earlier steps of the firing (as much as can be expected in a wood-fueled kiln) to ensure the oxidation of the sulfide into a sulfate melt, followed by reducing conditions (usually achieved by burning wet wood). These conditions must be maintained for several minutes while the kiln cools down to avoid the reoxidation of the layers (17).

During the ion exchange process, the silver and copper ions present in the paint enter the glaze and diffuse inside it. Silver is present as Ag+ in the paint and diffuses in the glaze as such. Conversely, copper is most probably present as Cu2+ species in the paint. However, we have observed that it is mainly present as Cu+ in the luster layer and underlying glaze. Therefore, given that the paint most probably contains sulfide and that the firing atmosphere is partially reducing, it is likely that the Cu2+ in the paint is at least partially reduced to Cu+ before entering the glaze. The speciation of copper in the glass will also depend on the composition, particularly the basicity, of the glaze. As mentioned above, Cu+ diffuses faster than Cu2+, which explains also why it is detected deeper.

Our data shows that if both Cu(I) and Ag(I) ions are present during this process, they interact with each other. In most cases, copper is oxidized to Cu(II) and silver is reduced to Ag0. As it can be seen in Figs. 5 (A to C) and 6, the fraction of Cu(II) is higher in the Ag0-rich areas. Cu+ and, to a lesser extent, Cu2+ can then diffuse deeper inside the glaze. The mobility of Cu+ in alkali silicate glasses is substantially greater than that of Cu2+ (48), which explains why the proportion of Cu(I) is consistently higher (75%) in the underlying glaze, regardless of color.

For silver-rich lusters, a small amount of copper aids the reduction and growth of the metallic silver nanoparticles, resulting in a green color. Conversely, the data suggests that when only copper is present and silver is negligible, Cu(I) can disproportionate into Cu2+ and Cu0 nanoparticles, leading to red lusters. For lusters with intermediate Cu/Ag ratios, only metallic silver clusters form, and some of the copper oxidizes to Cu2+. In this case, the lower relative amount of silver compared to green lusters prevents the formation of large silver nanoparticles, resulting in ochre lusters (see Fig. 5, C and D). The higher diffusivity of copper ions compared to silver ions results in a thicker copper-rich layer. However, the concentration of Cu(I) is insufficient for the disproportionation reaction to occur, meaning metallic copper nanoparticles are rarely found. This result reinforces the hypothesis that a disproportionation reaction occurs mainly in the absence of silver. It is noteworthy that the important role of reducing agents in silver-based lusters suggests that the cooling atmosphere can be relatively oxidizing. Conversely, for copper-based lusters, the reducing conditions should be maintained during the cooling to prevent any reoxidizing of the copper.

For the first time, the combination of UV-Vis spectroscopy, Ag L3-edge, Cu and Fe K-edge μ-XAS analysis, μ-XRD, and FE-SEM has revealed the correlation between the speciation of copper, iron, and silver, as well as the nature of the nanocrystalline precipitates and the different colors of lusters. Microimaging techniques revealed how these parameters evolve across the glaze depth. This information is essential for understanding the complex physical and chemical processes involved in producing multicolor lusters, a skill that was mastered by craftspeople.

Our measurements show that the luster can be divided into two layers. The first layer is close to the glaze surface and contains silver and/or copper nanoparticles, as well as dissolved Cu(I) and Cu(II). The second layer is nanoparticle-free and contains copper in the form of Cu(I) and Cu(II) (with a higher proportion of Cu(I)), as well as silver in the form of Ag(I), if present. The presence of these ionic species confirms the hypothesis that luster was obtained through an ionic exchange process involving the diffusion of ionic silver and copper into the glaze.

Our analyses suggest that the differences in composition and microstructure between different luster colors are primarily due to the relative amounts of copper and silver present in the luster. The concentration of silver and/or copper species and nanoparticles near the glaze surface is controlled by the relative amounts of copper and silver in the paint, as well as by the redox reactions between them. Together, they determine the type (silver only, copper only, or mixed silver and copper), amount, distribution, and size of the nanoparticles.

Silver is reduced to its metallic state through the oxidation of Cu(I) and Fe(II) in the glaze. If only silver particles crystallize, their size influences the color of the luster: green when they are above 10 nm and ochre when they are below 5 nm. When both metallic copper and silver particles are present, the color is dictated by their relative proportions: green-brown or ochre-brown, depending on whether silver or copper predominates. In these cases, the darker the color, the higher the concentration of the particles or species present.

In the absence of silver, or when it is present in small quantities, copper is reduced to its metallic state through the disproportionate reaction of Cu(I) to Cu0 and Cu(II). This reaction requires a high concentration of copper. In this case, iron does not appear to play a significant role in the reduction of copper. The resulting lusters are red-brown and red.

Such a combination of physics and chemistry could only be understood through the correlation of micro- and nanoanalytical techniques. The complexity of these chemical systems highlights the level of expertise developed by the Abbasid craftspeople to achieve such a wide range of colors. Preparing and analyzing mock-up samples would be the next step in better assessing the individual impact of the different manufacturing parameters. It will also be interesting to extend the research to the role of other metals in these productions, notably iron and tin.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample selection

Samples ALX242, ALX244, ALX245 (part of the Kaczmarczyk collection, Ashmolean Museum), and P51 (part of the collection of luster samples of the Ashmolean Museum) display the different colors studied: green, red, brown, and ochre. Samples P624 and P717 (Ashmolean Museum) show an overall red color accompanied by silvery and black decorations. These samples have been studied (37) but are included because they were also produced in the same workshops and show a pure red color, slightly different from the other polychrome samples.

UV-Vis analysis

UV-Vis transmission measurements were obtained using a Shimadzu 2700 double-beam spectrophotometer. Measurements were recorded between 200 and 800 nm. Color coordinates were calculated using the International Commission on Illumination (CIE Lab 1976; https://cie.co.at/) protocol with standard illuminant D65; the color data are shown in table S1.

Sample preparation, microscopy, and chemical analysis

Fragments of the samples were embedded in epoxy resin, and cross sections were cut and polished with a diamond paste down to 1-μm grade. The polished sections were examined under reflected light using a Nikon Eclipse LV100D optical microscope (OM) equipped with an Infinity 1.3C camera. The sections were coated with a carbon layer (10-nm thick) and were further examined in a cross-beam workstation (Zeiss Neon 40), equipped with a field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) GEMINI column. The chemical composition of the glazes was determined by EDS (Ultima EDS Detector, Oxford Instruments, Aztec Oxford Instruments). Analyses were implemented using a 20-kV acceleration voltage with 1.1-nm lateral resolution and 20 nA current. Mineral and glass standards were used for calibration and the oxygen content was calculated by stoichiometry. Secondary standards were used to verify the analyses (Corning A and B and Oxford DLH1) (table S3) (53). The chemical analysis of the glazes of the samples are shown in table S2. An FIB (Ga-ions, 30-kV acceleration voltage) was used to obtain polished cross-sections and high-resolution FE-SEM secondary electron images (5 kV) of the luster layers (54).

μ-XRD analysis

The nature of the particles present in the luster layers was determined by μ-XRD analyses on thin cross sections (∼100 μm) cut from small fragments of the samples embedded in epoxy resin using a low speed diamond saw. The synchrotron μ-XRD patterns were collected at the XALOC beamline (40) of the ALBA synchrotron light (Cerdanyola, Spain) in transmission geometry using a large area photon counting detector (Pilatus3 6M). The beam was focused down to 20 μm by 5 μm [width × height full width at half maximum (FWHM)], and its energy was tuned to 12.66 keV (λ = 0.97926 Å). The samples were mounted vertically to take advantage of the small vertical beam size. The optimal regions to obtain the μ-XRD patterns were localized by scanning the sample vertically through the beam with a step size of 2.5 μm. Triplicates of the scan were performed for each sample. The two-dimensional (2D) XRD patterns were integrated using the FIT2D program and the d-spacing was calibrated with a LaB6 standard. The XRD data were identified using the Powder Diffraction File Database of the International Centre for Diffraction Data (ICS) (55).

Microanalyses at the Fe and Cu K-edge and Ag L3-edge

Fe and Cu K-edge and Ag L3-edge μ-XAS data were obtained at the ID21 beamline using the scanning x-ray microscope (SXM-II) (41). X-rays were generated with an undulator, and their energy was adjusted with a Si (111) double-crystal monochromator. The monochromator was calibrated using the first inflection point of copper and iron metal foils and silver metallic powder deposited on S-free tape (maximum of the first derivative at 8978.9 eV for Cu foil, 7112.0 eV for Fe foil, and 3351.1 eV for Ag powder). The spectra corresponding to the metal foils and to the main oxides are shown in fig. S3.

For the analyses of all samples, the SXM-II end-station was used, in which the beam was focused down to 0.8 μm by 0.5 μm (width × height FWHM) using a Ni-coated Kirkpatrick-Baez mirror system. Samples were mounted vertically, at an angle of 62° with respect to the incident beam. X-rays were detected using a single energy dispersive silicon drift detector (SGX, 80 mm2) at 28° from the sample surface. Sample live visualization and selection of the regions of interest were implemented using the Daiquiri graphical user interface (56). μ-XRF maps were acquired at 9.3 keV, over 2D regions to locate elements and identify regions of interest. From these maps, lines of interest were selected, perpendicular to the luster surface. Point XANES were acquired along these lines, over regular steps, at the different edges of interest.

Data analysis

Data analysis in Cu K-edge XANES spectra was performed using MCR-ALS, implemented through the SpectroChemPy library version 0.6.9 (57) in a customized Python environment paired with range Data Mining (version 3.36.2) (58). The MCR-ALS algorithm used 1484 experimental normalized spectra as input. Initial guesses were based on three previously normalized experimental reference spectra of Cu0, Cu(I) glass, and Cu(II) glass. The number of components was determined to be three, which is in line with previous knowledge of the sample composition.

The MCR-ALS optimization algorithm was executed with a convergence tolerance (tol) of 1.0, while all other parameters were kept at default values according to the SpectroChemPy library. The ALS optimization log converged with a change percentage of 0.67%. After convergence, the spectral profile (St), concentration profile (C), and residual matrices (experimental – reconstructed) were extracted. Figure S1A illustrates the spectral profile matrix (St), which includes the three reconstructions of the initial guesses represented by continuous lines, fig. S1B illustrates the reconstruction of the experimental data, and Fig. 1C illustrates the residuals matrix. Model validity was assessed by comparing the reconstructed and experimental datasets and through residual inspection. The SD of the residuals was 0.019, the lack of fit 2.16%, and the coefficient of determination (R2) = 0.998, indicating a reliable quantitative determination of the Cu species fractions (59). Initial guesses, shown as dotted lines in Fig. 1A, and spectral profile (St), shown as continuous lines in the same subplot, were also compared graphically using the R2. The value of R2 close to 1 supported the reliability of the initial guesses.

The data analysis in Ag L3-edge XANES spectra was carried out using a combined analysis of EFA and MCR-ALS, both implemented via the SpectroChemPy library. Before analysis, all spectra were normalized. Initially, EFA was performed on the complete set of experimental spectra to estimate preliminary concentration profiles matrix (C). The number of components was set at two (n = 2), with a cutoff value of 0 and other parameters at their default values per SpectroChemPy. Subsequently, the concentration profile matrix (C) from EFA was used as the initial guess for the MCR-ALS algorithm (60). In this stage, optimization was performed using a convergence tolerance (tol) of 0.1, with other parameters maintained at default settings. The ALS optimization log converged with a change percentage of 0.046%. After convergence, spectral profiles (St), concentration profiles (C), and residuals (Experimental - Reconstructed) matrices were obtained. Model validity was assessed by comparing the reconstructed and experimental datasets, along with the inspection of the residuals that tended toward zero.

The data analysis of the Fe K-edge spectra was implemented by focusing on the pre-edge feature, which is particularly sensitive to the valence state and local geometry. The weighted peak position (centroid) is known to shift to higher energies as the ferric to ferrous iron ratio increases (41), and the area provides an estimate of the coordination state. The centroid can be used to calculate the oxidation state of iron in glasses (42, 43) following the procedure described in the literature. The onset of the absorption edge was modeled by fitting the energy regions below and above the pre-edge feature with an inclined arctangent function (61), and then the peaks were fitted using the exact Voigt profile. Profile fitting was performed by using the Trust Region Reflective algorithm, as implemented in the standard Python library scipy.optimize (62). The FWHM and the Gaussian fraction were constrained to be shared by all peaks. Furthermore, the Gaussian contribution dominates the peak profile of the pre-edge spectrum (XG > 0.9) measured at standard resolution (61). For this reason, we decided to set the Gaussian contribution to 1. Nevertheless, the results obtained did not change significantly when a lower Gaussian contribution was used or even leaving it as a free parameter of the fit. Figure S4 shows Fe K pre-edge region corresponding to two of the spectra, one from the luster, and the other from the glaze areas of a green luster (P51E-G-Y). The shift in the edge and different pre-edge peak ratios are related to the oxidation state of iron in the glaze. The fits are accurate (see fig. S4), the error in the centroid position (of about 7112.2 eV) varies between 0.02 and 0.09 eV, which translates into an error in the iron speciation fraction varying between 0.01 and 0.04.
