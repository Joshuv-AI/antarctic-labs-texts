Elements of Saito theory via Batalin–Vilkovisky algebras
Alexey Basalaev

Abstract
. Saito theory associates to a quasihomogeneous isolated singularity the structure of a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold. This structure is not unique, depending on the special choice of a primitive form or, equivalently, a good basis. We study primitive forms and respective Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds via BV-algebras. In particular, we give recursive formulae for the primitive form of K. Saito and the R-matrix of Givental using BV-algebra computations.



1.
Introduction

Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds were introduced by B. Dubrovin and have appeared as an important tool in such areas of mathematics as integrable systems, mathematical physics, and mirror symmetry. The sources of Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds include formal deformation theory, cohomological field theory, and Saito theory of isolated singularities (cf.
[
M
]
). In this text we focus on the last one, building up the connection to the previous two.

1.1.
Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold of a singularity

A Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold is the set of data
(M,\circ,\eta,e)
, where
M
is a complex manifold,
\circ
is a fiberwise
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
–bilinear commutative and associative product on its holomorphic tangent sheaf
{\mathcal{T}}_{M}
,
\eta
is a non–degenerate
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
–bilinear form on
{\mathcal{T}}_{M}
, such that the Frobenius algebra property
\eta(X\circ Y,Z)=\eta(X,Y\circ Z)
holds for any
X,Y,Z\in{\mathcal{T}}_{M}
. It is required that the Levi-Civita connection
{\nabla}\hskip-3.41432pt{\text{\footnotesize{\bf/}}}
of
\eta
is flat, and the unit vector field
e
of the product is
{\nabla}\hskip-3.41432pt{\text{\footnotesize{\bf/}}}
–flat. Let
t^{\alpha}
be flat coordinates and
{\partial}/{\partial}t^{\alpha}
the dual vector fields. Denote by
C:=\sum_{\alpha}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t^{\alpha}}\circ\right)dt^{\alpha}
the
{\mathcal{T}}_{M}
–endomorphism-valued
1
–form. It is required that
{\nabla}\hskip-3.41432pt{\text{\footnotesize{\bf/}}}C=0
. Moreover both
\eta
and
\circ
are required to be quasihomogeneous with respect to some Euler vector field
E
.

To any Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold one can associate the connection on
M\times{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}_{z}
given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{X}:=\nabla_{X}+\frac{1}{z}C_{X},\quad\widetilde{\nabla}_{\frac{d}{dz}}:=\frac{d}{dz}+\frac{1}{z}\left(B_{0}+\frac{B_{\infty}}{z}\right)

for
C_{X}(Y):=X\circ Y
,
B_{0}(Y):=E\circ Y
and the diagonal grading operator
B_{\infty}
.

This new connection is flat. It provides an important piece of data of a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold. Conversely, given a manifold
M
with a flat pairing
\eta
and a fiberwise product
\circ
on
{\mathcal{T}}_{M}
satisfying the Frobenius algebra property, if
\widetilde{\nabla}
is flat, then
M
is a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold (cf. Proposition 2.1 in
[
D2
]
and
[
Sa
]
).

The structure of every Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold can be encoded by just one function
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{F}}(t_{1},\dots,t_{\mu})
called its potential.
The associativity of the product
\circ
then implies that
{\mathcal{F}}
is subject to a big system of PDEs called the
WDVV equation
.

1.1.1.
Saito–Frobenius manifold

Consider a quasihomogeneous polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]
with
0\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
— the only critical point. It defines a Jacobian algebra

\mathrm{Jac}(f):={\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]/(\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{1}},\dots\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{N}})

that is a finite–dimensional algebra with a pairing called the
residue pairing
. Let
\phi_{1}({\bf x}),\dots,\phi_{\mu}({\bf x})\in{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]
be polynomials whose classes generate
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
. Introduce the new function

F({\bf x},{\bf s}):=f({\bf x})+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\mu}s_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}({\bf x})

with parameters
s_{1},\dots,s_{\mu}
varying in a small open neighbourhood of the origin
{\mathcal{S}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{\mu}
. We call
F
the unfolding of
f
and
{\mathcal{S}}
the unfolding space.

It is well–known that the unfolding space
{\mathcal{S}}
carries the structure of a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold (cf.
[
ST
,
D1
,
D2
,
Sa
,
H
]
). To construct it, one considers the
{\mathbb{C}}[z]
–module

{\mathcal{H}}_{f}^{(0)}:=\Omega^{N}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}[z]/(zd+df\wedge)\Omega^{N-1}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}[z],

called the
Brieskorn lattice
, and its completion
{\mathcal{H}}_{f}:={\mathcal{H}}_{f}^{(0)}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}[z]}{\mathbb{C}}((z^{-1}))
. Assuming
f
varies in a family given by the unfolding, we get the
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}[z]
–module
{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}
and its completion
{\mathcal{H}}_{F}
.
Both
{\mathcal{H}}_{f}^{(0)}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}
are free rank
\mu
modules over
{\mathbb{C}}[z]
and
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}[z]
respectively. However, they also have a
{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}]
–module structure given by
p({\bf x})\cdot[\phi]d^{N}{\bf x}=[p\phi]d^{N}{\bf x}
.

The space
{\mathcal{H}}_{F}
is endowed with the connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{GM}}:{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathcal{S}}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{F}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{F}
proposed by K. Saito:

\displaystyle\nabla^{\mathrm{GM}}_{X}[\phi]d^{N}{\bf x}:=[X\cdot\phi({\bf x})+\frac{1}{z}(X\cdot F)\phi({\bf x})]d^{N}{\bf x},

\displaystyle\nabla^{\mathrm{GM}}_{\frac{d}{dz}}[\phi]d^{N}{\bf x}:=[\frac{d\phi}{dz}+\frac{1}{z^{2}}F\phi-\frac{1}{z}N\phi]d^{N}{\bf x},

where
X\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathcal{S}}
and
X\cdot
stands for the directional derivative.
This connection looks similar to that of Eq. (
1.1
); however, there are two major differences: 1. it is not a connection on
{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathcal{S}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}[z,z^{-1}]]
; 2. its
z
–dependence is much more complicated. These two issues are resolved with the help of K. Saito’s
primitive form
.

1.1.2.
Primitive form and good basis

A primitive form is a special element
\zeta\in{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}
such that the map
\sigma:{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}/z{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}
,
X\mapsto z\nabla^{\mathrm{GM}}_{X}
is an isomorphism and the connection
\sigma^{-1}\cdot\nabla^{\mathrm{GM}}\cdot\sigma
has the form of Eq. (
1.1
) (cf.
[
SK2
,
ST
]
), giving a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold structure on
{\mathcal{S}}
.

Another way to construct the Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold structure on
{\mathcal{S}}
is by taking a special choice of an
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}[z]
–basis
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{\mu}
of
{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}
, called a
good basis
. Computed in this basis, the connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{GM}}
takes the form of Eq. (
1.1
).

Existence of a primitive form was proved by M. Saito (cf.
[
SM1
,
SM2
]
). He also showed that primitive forms are in one-to-one correspondence with good bases.

We will denote the Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold of
f
fixed by the good basis
\omega_{\bullet}
by
M_{f}^{\omega}
and its potential by
{\mathcal{F}}^{\omega}
.

1.2.
BV–algebra for a hypersurface singularity

Let
{\mathrm{PV}}^{i}:=\wedge^{i}{\mathcal{T}}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}
and
{\mathrm{PV}}:=\sum_{i\geq 0}{\mathrm{PV}}^{i}
. It has an algebra structure given by the wedge product. Construction with a holomorphic volume form on
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
gives an isomorphism
{\mathrm{PV}}^{i}\cong\Omega_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}^{N-i,0}
to the space of holomorphic
(N-i)
–forms. Denote by
{\partial}:{\mathrm{PV}}^{i}\to{\mathrm{PV}}^{i-1}
the operator lifted from the holomorphic differential on
\Omega_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}^{\ast,0}
.
Then
{\partial}^{2}=0
, but
{\partial}
does not satisfy the Leibniz rule, being a 2nd order operator. It defines a Gerstenhaber bracket on
{\mathrm{PV}}
by

\{\alpha,\beta\}:={\partial}(\alpha\cdot\beta)-({\partial}\alpha)\cdot\beta-(-1)^{|\alpha|}\alpha\cdot{\partial}(\beta).

For
f
as in the preceding section, consider another differential
\overline{{\partial}}_{f}
on the same space
{\mathrm{PV}}
. Set
\overline{{\partial}}_{f}:=\{f,\cdot\}
. Then

H^{\ast}({\mathrm{PV}},\overline{{\partial}}_{f})\cong\mathrm{Jac}(f)\quad\text{ and }\quad H^{\ast}({\mathrm{PV}}[z],\overline{{\partial}}_{f}+z{\partial})\cong{\mathcal{H}}_{f}^{(0)}.

The operators
\overline{{\partial}}_{f}
and
{\partial}
serve as a differential and a BV–operator on
{\mathrm{PV}}
respectively, giving the BV–algebra
({\mathrm{PV}},\overline{{\partial}}_{f},{\partial})
. A similar BV–algebra was used in
[
LLS
]
, however with antiholomorphic directions as well. We do not use them here, but keep the notation.

With the help of the unfolding function
F
, the BV–algebra above can be extended to a new BV–algebra
({\mathrm{PV}}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}},\overline{{\partial}}_{F},{\partial})
.
This BV–algebra can be endowed with a connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{PV}}
similar to that of
\nabla^{\mathrm{GM}}
.

Consider the
{\mathrm{PV}}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}((z^{-1}))
–operators

\displaystyle\nabla_{v}^{\mathrm{PV}}(a):=v(a)+z^{-1}av(F),\quad\forall v\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathcal{S}},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}z}}^{\mathrm{PV}}(a):=\frac{{\partial}a}{{\partial}z}-z^{-2}aF.

Direct computations show that
[\nabla_{v}^{\mathrm{PV}},\overline{{\partial}}_{F}+z{\partial}]=0
and
[\nabla_{\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}z}}^{\mathrm{PV}},\overline{{\partial}}_{F}+z{\partial}]=z^{-1}(\overline{{\partial}}_{F}+z{\partial})
, showing that
\nabla^{\mathrm{PV}}
is well–defined on the cohomology
H^{\ast}({\mathrm{PV}}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}((z^{-1})),\bar{\partial}_{F}+z{\partial})
.

1.2.1.
BV operator trivialization

An operator
\Phi\in{\mathrm{End}}({\mathrm{PV}}[z])
is called a
BV–operator trivialization
of
({\mathrm{PV}},\overline{{\partial}}_{f},{\partial})
if

\Phi(\overline{{\partial}}_{f}+z{\partial})=\overline{{\partial}}_{f}\Phi.

In
[
KMS
]
Khoroshkin, Markarian and Shadrin constructed a quasi-isomorphism between the hypercommutative operad and the operad of BV–algebras with resolved BV–operator.

Having fixed the trivialization above, their construction allows us to associate to any
k
–tuple of cohomology classes
[\phi_{\bullet}]\in H^{\ast}({\mathrm{PV}},\overline{{\partial}}_{f})
the number
\langle[\phi_{1}],\dots,[\phi_{k}]\rangle^{\Phi}
, such that the generating function
{\mathcal{F}}^{\Phi}
of all such numbers is a solution to the WDVV equation.

The starting question of the research performed in this paper was to find the trivialization such that
{\mathcal{F}}^{\Phi}
coincides with
{\mathcal{F}}^{\omega}
.

1.3.
In this paper

We prove that the BV–algebra of a quasihomogeneous singularity trivializes. We construct a specific trivialization
\Phi^{\omega}
of the BV–operator associated to every good basis
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{\mu}
and show that the Dubrovin connection
\nabla^{\omega}
of
M_{f}^{\omega}
is an essential lift of the connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{PV}}
(Proposition
6.3
in the text).

In Theorem
7.2
we give a recursive formula computing the primitive form associated to a given good basis.

Finally, in Theorem
8.5
we give a recursive formula for the R–matrix of the Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold
M^{\omega}_{f}
using the trivialization
\Phi^{\omega}
and the computations in
{\mathrm{PV}}
. This shows that the BV–algebra generating function computed via the BV–operator trivialization
R\cdot\Phi^{\omega}
coincides with the Saito theory potential
{\mathcal{F}}^{\omega}
.

2.
Brieskorn lattice of a singularity

In this section we give a fast track through the construction of a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold on the singularity unfolding space. This incorporates work of many people, especially K. Saito and M. Saito, to collect together the data fitting B. Dubrovin’s theory. We do not aim to give a full account on the Saito–Frobenius theory, mostly highlighting only those definitions and conventions that will be needed later on. For a full presentation see
[
H
]
.

2.1.
Jacobian algebra

In what follows we will work with quasihomogeneous isolated singularities defined by a polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]
.
Namely, we will assume that there is a set of numbers
q_{1},\dots,q_{N}
,
q_{k}\in{\mathbb{Q}}\cap(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, such that
f
is a linear combination of monomials
x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{N}^{a_{N}}
subject to
a_{1}q_{1}+\dots+a_{N}q_{N}=1
, and
{\bf x}=0
is the only point in
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
such that
\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{1}}=\dots=\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{N}}=0
.

Associated to
f
are the algebra
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
and the
\dim(\mathrm{Jac}(f))
–dimensional vector space
\Omega_{f}
:

\mathrm{Jac}(f):={\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]/(\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{1}},\dots\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{N}}),\quad\Omega_{f}:=\Omega^{N}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}/df\wedge\Omega^{N-1}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}.

The property of
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
to be finite–dimensional is equivalent to the property of
f
defining an isolated singularity and is equivalent to the fact that the partial derivatives
(\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{1}},\dots,\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{N}})
form a regular sequence. This last algebraic property will be of great importance in this text.

It is obvious that there is a vector space isomorphism
\mathrm{Jac}(f)\cong\Omega_{f}
by
[\phi({\bf x})]\mapsto[\phi({\bf x})d^{N}{\bf x}]
where
d^{N}{\bf x}:=dx_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{N}
. An important advantage of
\Omega_{f}
is that it is endowed with the bilinear form

\eta([\phi_{1}d^{N}{\bf x}],[\phi_{2}d^{N}{\bf x}]):=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sqrt{-1})^{N}}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\phi_{1}\phi_{2}d^{N}{\bf x}}{\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{1}}\cdots\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{N}}}

where the integration is taken over the cycle
\Gamma:=\{|\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{1}}|=\varepsilon,\dots,|\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{N}}|=\varepsilon\}
for some small
\varepsilon
.

The bilinear form is non–degenerate and well known under the name of the Poincaré
residue pairing
. The isomorphism above allows one to lift it to
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
. We will denote by
\eta_{f}:\mathrm{Jac}(f)\otimes\mathrm{Jac}(f)\to{\mathbb{C}}
this lifted residue pairing. It follows immediately from the construction that

\eta_{f}([\phi_{1}],[\phi_{2}]\circ[\phi_{3}])=\eta_{f}([\phi_{1}]\circ[\phi_{2}],[\phi_{3}])

for any
[\phi_{\bullet}]\in\mathrm{Jac}(f)
and
\circ
being the product of
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
. The equality above is called the
Frobenius algebra
property.

To the end of this text, fix
\mu:=\dim\mathrm{Jac}(f)
, known under the name of
Milnor number
of
f
. The algebra
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
is also called the
Jacobian algebra
.

2.2.
Weight and Hessian

Introduce the weight function
\mathrm{wt}:{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{Q}}
. Set
\mathrm{wt}(x_{k}):=q_{k}
. Then
\mathrm{wt}(f)=1
and the weight function descends to both
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
and
\Omega_{f}
.

Consider the polynomial
\mathrm{hess}(f):=\det\left(\frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{{\partial}x_{i}{\partial}x_{j}}\right)_{i,j=1}^{N}
, called the
Hessian polynomial
of
f
. Then

\mathrm{wt}(\mathrm{hess}(f))=\sum_{k=1}^{N}(1-2q_{k}).

This polynomial is very important for
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
due to the following two facts: 1.
\mathrm{wt}[\mathrm{hess}(f)]\geq\mathrm{wt}[\phi]
for any homogeneous
[\phi]\in\mathrm{Jac}(f)
, the equality being reached if and only if
[\phi]=c[\mathrm{hess}(f)]
; 2.
\eta_{f}([1],[\mathrm{hess}(f)])=\mu
and
\eta_{f}([1],[a])=0
for any
[a]
belonging to the complement of
{\mathbb{C}}\langle[\mathrm{hess}(f)]\rangle
in
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
.

2.3.
Brieskorn lattice

Next to
\Omega_{f}
, another essential object associated to the polynomial
f
is the
{\mathbb{C}}[z]
–module

{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{f}:=\Omega^{N}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}[z]/(zd+df\wedge)\Omega^{N-1}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}

called the
Brieskorn lattice of
f
. It is a
{\mathbb{C}}[z]
–module of rank
\mu
. A basis can be taken to be
\{[\phi_{1}({\bf x})d^{N}{\bf x}],\dots,[\phi_{\mu}({\bf x})d^{N}{\bf x}]\}
where the polynomials
\phi_{\bullet}\in{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}]
are taken to be such that their classes generate
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
as a
{\mathbb{C}}
–vector space.

Let
{\mathbb{C}}((z^{-1}))
stand for the ring of Laurent series in
z^{-1}
. Define the completion of the Brieskorn lattice by

{\mathcal{H}}_{f}:={\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{f}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}[z]}{\mathbb{C}}((z^{-1})).

Extend the weight function
\mathrm{wt}
to
\Omega^{N}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}[z]
and
{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}][z]
by setting
\mathrm{wt}(z):=1
. Obviously, it descends to both
{\mathcal{H}}_{f}^{(0)}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{f}
.

3.
BV algebras and homotopic resolution

Let
(V,d)
be a graded dg algebra. It is called a Batalin–Vilkovisky or just BV algebra if it is equipped with an additional operator
\Delta
, such that
\Delta^{2}=0
,
\Delta d+d\Delta=0
, and
\Delta
is of order two with respect to the product of
V
.

Assuming
z
as a formal variable, let
\Phi\in\mathrm{End}(V)[[z]]=\mathrm{Id}+\sum_{k\geq 1}\Phi_{k}z^{k}
be a homotopy of the complexes

(V^{\bullet}[[z]],d+z\Delta)\to(V^{\bullet}[[z]],d).

Namely,
\Phi
should satisfy

(3.1)

\Phi(d+z\Delta)=d\Phi.

Such power series
\Phi
were considered in
[
KMS
]
and called
\Delta
–resolutions
of the BV algebra. Given a
\Delta
–resolution, Khoroshkin, Markarian and Shadrin constructed in
[
KMS
]
the quasi-isomorphism of the Hycomm and BV operads. We use it here in a slightly modified manner.

3.1.
BV–algebra of the polyvector fields

Denote by
{\mathrm{PV}}
the space of smooth polyvector fields on
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
. Namely,
{\mathrm{PV}}:=\bigoplus_{i\geq 0}{\mathrm{PV}}^{i}
with
{\mathrm{PV}}^{i}:=\wedge^{i}{\mathcal{T}}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}
. For
\theta_{k}:=\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x_{k}}
being the basis vectors of
{\mathcal{T}}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}
, an element of
{\mathrm{PV}}^{i}
is a linear combination with complex coefficients of

\alpha(x)\theta_{r_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge\theta_{r_{i}}

with
1\leq r_{\bullet}\leq N
and
\alpha
being a holomorphic function on
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
. The index value
i=0
is allowed, in which case we will write the unit
1
instead of the
0
–length wedge product.

Assign to
A\in{\mathrm{PV}}^{i}
the grading
i
and denote
|A|:=i\mod 2
. Then the wedge product gives a
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
–graded product structure on
{\mathrm{PV}}
with the unit
1\in{\mathrm{PV}}^{0}
.

The space
{\mathrm{PV}}
has natural operators
\theta_{p}\wedge:{\mathrm{PV}}^{i}\to{\mathrm{PV}}^{i+1}
for any
p
. In what follows we are going to use the operator
\dfrac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\theta_{p}}:{\mathrm{PV}}^{i}\to{\mathrm{PV}}^{i-1}
acting trivially on the
\alpha(x)
multiple above and satisfying

\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\theta_{a}}(\theta_{b}\wedge)+(\theta_{b}\wedge)\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\theta_{a}}=\delta_{a,b}.

Once and for all, fix a holomorphic nowhere vanishing function
\lambda
and a holomorphic volume form

\Omega_{\lambda}:=\frac{1}{\lambda}dx_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{N}.

The contraction operator
\vdash\Omega_{\lambda}
provides an isomorphism
{\mathrm{PV}}^{i}\cong{\mathcal{A}}^{N-i}
to the space of
{(N-i,0)}
–forms on
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
. Denote by
{\partial}
the operator on
{\mathrm{PV}}
lifted by this isomorphism from the Hodge theory holomorphic differential. We have

\displaystyle{\partial}\left(\alpha(x)\bigwedge_{j\in J}\theta_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x_{k}}\left(\frac{\alpha(x)}{\lambda}\right)\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\theta_{k}}\left(\bigwedge_{j\in J}\theta_{j}\right).

One notes immediately that
{\partial}^{2}=0
.

The operator
{\partial}
does not satisfy the Leibniz rule. Instead,
{\partial}
is of second order with respect to the product.
The BV operator
{\partial}
defines a non–trivial bracket

\{A,B\}:=(-1)^{|A|}{\partial}(AB)-(-1)^{|A|}{\partial}(A)B-A{\partial}(B)

satisfying the Jacobi identity. In particular, for any polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]
consider the operator
\{f,-\}:{\mathrm{PV}}\to{\mathrm{PV}}
taking the bracket with
f(x)\cdot 1\in{\mathrm{PV}}^{0}
. It acts by

(3.2)

\left\{f,\alpha(x)\bigwedge_{j\in J}\theta_{j}\right\}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{k}}\alpha(x)\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\theta_{k}}(\bigwedge_{j\in J}\theta_{j}).

3.2.
BV–algebra of a hypersurface singularity

Let
f\in{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]
define a quasihomogeneous isolated singularity at the origin
{\bf x}=0\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
.

Following
[
LLS
]
, associate to it the operator
\bar{\partial}_{f}:{\mathrm{PV}}\to{\mathrm{PV}}
by

\bar{\partial}_{f}:=\{f,-\}.

We have
(\bar{\partial}_{f})^{2}=\{f,\{f,-\}\}=0
.
Similarly
\bar{\partial}_{f}{\partial}+{\partial}\bar{\partial}_{f}=\{f,-\}{\partial}+{\partial}\{f,-\}=0
because
{\partial}/{\partial}\theta_{\bullet}
anticommute. In particular, the new differential
\bar{\partial}_{f}
provides a Batalin–Vilkovisky algebra structure
({\mathrm{PV}},\bar{\partial}_{f},{\partial})
.

It is not hard to see (cf. Lemma 2.2 of
[
LLS
]
) that
H^{k}({\mathrm{PV}},\bar{\partial}_{f})=0
unless
k=0
, and there is an algebra isomorphism

(3.3)

\displaystyle H^{\ast}({\mathrm{PV}},\bar{\partial}_{f})

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{Jac}(f),

\displaystyle[\phi\cdot 1]

\displaystyle\mapsto[\phi].

Similarly we have the isomorphism

(3.4)

\displaystyle H^{\ast}({\mathrm{PV}}[z],\bar{\partial}_{f}+z{\partial})

\displaystyle\cong{\mathcal{H}}_{f}^{(0)},

\displaystyle[\phi(x,z)\cdot 1]

\displaystyle\mapsto[\phi(x,z)d^{N}{\bf x}].

It shows that the Brieskorn lattice can be studied with the help of the BV algebra
({\mathrm{PV}}[z],\overline{{\partial}}_{f},{\partial})
. However, note that the latter has a big advantage — its product descends to a non–trivial product in cohomology.

3.3.
Topological trivialization of
({\mathrm{PV}},\overline{{\partial}}_{f},{\partial})

Denote by
\Phi^{top}
the special trivialization of the BV–operator
{\partial}
, inducing a trivial action on the cohomology w.r.t.
\overline{{\partial}}_{f}
. It should satisfy

\Phi^{top}(\overline{{\partial}}_{f})=(\overline{{\partial}}_{f}+z{\partial})\Phi^{top}

as in Eq. (
3.1
). The name
topological
will be justified later.

Set

(3.5)

\Phi^{top}\left(g({\bf x})\cdot\bigwedge_{j\in J}\theta_{j}\right):=\widetilde{\Phi}^{top}\left(g({\bf x})\right)\cdot\bigwedge_{j\in J}\theta_{j},

for a
{\mathbb{C}}
–linear map
\widetilde{\Phi}^{top}:{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}]\to{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}]\otimes{\mathbb{C}}[z]
that we define below.

The trivialization property is then equivalent to the following equality:

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Phi^{top}\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{k}}\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\theta_{k}}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{k}}+z\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x_{k}}\right)\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\theta_{k}}\Phi^{top}.

Consider
{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}]
as an infinite–dimensional
{\mathbb{C}}
–vector space. Let
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{\mu}
be polynomials whose classes generate
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
.

The polynomials
\prod_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{k}}\right)^{p_{k}}\phi_{\alpha}
with all possible
\alpha
and natural
p_{1},\dots,p_{N}
generate
{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}]
as a vector space. The set of all such polynomials is independent of relations because
(\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{1}},\dots,\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{N}})
is a regular sequence. Hence we have constructed a special basis of
{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}]
.

Because
f
is quasihomogeneous with
\mathrm{wt}f=1
, the partial derivatives
\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{k}}
are quasihomogeneous with
\mathrm{wt}(\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{k}})=1-q_{k}>0
; we may assume
\phi_{\bullet}
to be quasihomogeneous too. The weight of any basis element
\prod_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{k}}\right)^{p_{k}}\phi_{\alpha}
, computed by the function
\mathrm{wt}
, is positive unless
r_{1}=\dots=r_{N}=0
and
\phi_{\alpha}=1
.
Then every quasihomogeneous polynomial
a(x)\in{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}]
is written as a finite
{\mathbb{C}}
–linear combination of the special basis elements.

Set
\widetilde{\Phi}^{top}(\phi_{\alpha})=\phi_{\alpha}
and more generally

(3.6)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}^{top}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{k}}\right)^{p_{k}}\phi_{\alpha}\right):=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{k}}+z\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x_{k}}\right)^{p_{k}}\ \phi_{\alpha}.

The expression on the RHS is well-defined because the operators
\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{k}}+z\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x_{k}}
commute with each other for different values of
k
, and no order in the product is important.

Note that the map
\widetilde{\Phi}^{top}
is quasihomogeneous because the weight of
f
and
z
are both equal to
1
. In particular,
\mathrm{wt}(\widetilde{\Phi}^{top})=0
.

Let
\Phi^{top}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\Phi^{top}_{k}z^{k}
with
\Phi^{top}_{k}\in\mathrm{End}({\mathrm{PV}})
. Then
\Phi^{top}_{0}=\mathrm{Id}
and
\Phi^{top}
is invertible.

3.4.
Computational aspects

The trivialization
\Phi^{top}
is easy to compute. Indeed it is defined explicitly in the
{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}]
—basis
\prod_{k=1}^{N}(\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{k}})^{r_{k}}\phi_{\alpha}
. In order to find the trivialization in the monomial basis
\prod_{k=1}^{N}x_{k}^{m_{k}}
, one needs to find the transition matrix between the two bases.
This is done via the inverse map
(\Phi^{top})^{-1}
. To find it one can use the formal power series expansion
(\Phi^{top})^{-1}=\sum_{m\geq 0}(-1)^{m}\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\Phi_{k}^{top}z^{k}\right)^{m}
that is again not hard to compute.

For
f=x_{1}^{3}+x_{2}^{3}x_{1}
, take
\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{7}\}=\left\{1,x_{1},x_{1}^{2},x_{2},x_{1}x_{2},x_{1}^{2}x_{2},x_{2}^{2}\right\}
.

\displaystyle x_{2}^{3}=\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{1}}\phi_{1}-3\phi_{3},\quad x_{1}x_{2}^{2}=\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{2}}\frac{\phi_{1}}{3},\quad x_{1}^{3}=\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{1}}\frac{\phi_{2}}{3}-\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{2}}\frac{\phi_{4}}{9},

\displaystyle x_{2}^{4}=\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{1}}\phi_{4}-3\phi_{6},\quad x_{1}x_{2}^{3}=\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{2}}\frac{\phi_{4}}{3},\quad x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}=\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{2}}\frac{\phi_{2}}{3},\quad x_{1}^{3}x_{2}=\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{1}}\frac{\phi_{5}}{3}-\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{2}}\frac{\phi_{7}}{9},

\displaystyle\qquad x_{1}^{4}=\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{1}}\frac{\phi_{3}}{3}-\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}x_{2}}\frac{\phi_{5}}{9}.

Then
\Phi^{top}
acts by

\displaystyle x_{2}^{3}\mapsto x_{2}^{3},\quad x_{1}x_{2}^{2}\mapsto x_{1}x_{2}^{2},\quad x_{1}^{3}\mapsto x_{1}^{3}+\frac{2z}{9},

\displaystyle x_{2}^{4}\mapsto x_{2}^{4},\quad x_{1}x_{2}^{3}\mapsto x_{1}x_{2}^{3}+\frac{z}{3},\quad x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}\mapsto x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2},\quad x_{1}^{3}x_{2}\mapsto x_{1}^{3}x_{2}+\frac{zx_{2}}{9},\quad x_{1}^{4}\mapsto x_{1}^{4}+\frac{5zx_{1}}{9}.

Denote by
[\phi]_{f}
the class of
\phi
in
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
. Composed with the isomorphism of Eq. (
3.4
), the inverse
(\Phi^{top})^{-1}
gives the following map
{\mathcal{H}}_{f}^{(0)}\to\mathrm{Jac}(f)[z]
:

\displaystyle 0=[x_{2}^{3}d^{2}{\bf x}]\mapsto[x_{2}^{3}]_{f}=0,\quad 0=[x_{1}x_{2}^{2}d^{2}{\bf x}]\mapsto[x_{1}x_{2}^{2}]_{f}=0,\quad[x_{1}^{3}d^{2}{\bf x}]\mapsto[x_{1}^{3}]_{f}-\frac{2z}{9}[1]_{f}=-\frac{2z}{9}[1]_{f},

\displaystyle 0=[x_{2}^{4}d^{2}{\bf x}]\mapsto[x_{2}^{4}]_{f}=0,\quad[x_{1}x_{2}^{3}d^{2}{\bf x}]\mapsto[x_{1}x_{2}^{3}]_{f}-\frac{z}{3}[1]_{f}=-\frac{z}{3}[1]_{f},\quad[x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}d^{2}{\bf x}]\mapsto[x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}]_{f}=0,

\displaystyle[x_{1}^{3}x_{2}d^{2}{\bf x}]\mapsto[x_{1}^{3}x_{2}]_{f}-\frac{z}{9}[x_{2}]_{f}=-\frac{z}{9}[x_{2}]_{f},\quad[x_{1}^{4}d^{2}{\bf x}]\mapsto[x_{1}^{4}]_{f}-\frac{5z}{9}[x_{1}]_{f}=-\frac{5z}{9}[x_{1}]_{f}.

4.
Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds

Investigation of the Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds was sparked by the work of B. Dubrovin. In this text we only consider them in the application to Saito theory.

Let
M=(M,{\mathcal{O}}_{M})
be a connected complex manifold of dimension
\mu
. Denote by
{\mathcal{T}}_{M}
its holomorphic tangent sheaf.

A
Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold structure on M
(cf.
[
D1
,
D2
]
) is a tuple
(\eta,\circ,e,E)
, where
\eta
is a non–degenerate
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
–symmetric bilinear form on
{\mathcal{T}}_{M}
,
\circ
is an
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
–bilinear product on
{\mathcal{T}}_{M}
, defining an associative and commutative
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
–algebra structure with a unit
e
, and
E
is a holomorphic vector field on
M
, called the Euler vector field, which are subject to the following axioms:

(1)

\eta(X\circ Y,Z)=\eta(X,Y\circ Z)
for all
X,Y,Z\in{\mathcal{T}}_{M}
.

(2)

The Levi–Civita connection
{\nabla}\hskip-3.41432pt{\text{\footnotesize{\bf/}}}
of
\eta
is flat.

(3)

The tensor
C:{\mathcal{T}}_{M}\otimes_{{\mathcal{O}}_{M}}{\mathcal{T}}_{M}\to{\mathcal{T}}_{M}
defined by

C_{X}Y:=X\circ Y
, for all
X,Y\in{\mathcal{T}}_{M}
is flat with respect to
{\nabla}\hskip-3.41432pt{\text{\footnotesize{\bf/}}}
.

(4)

The unit element
e
of the
\circ
-algebra is a

{\nabla}\hskip-3.41432pt{\text{\footnotesize{\bf/}}}
-flat holomorphic vector field.

(5)

There is
d\in{\mathbb{C}}
, such that the metric
\eta
and the product
\circ
are homogeneous of degree
2-d
and
1
respectively with respect to the Lie derivative

Lie_{E}
of the Euler vector field
E
: that is,

Lie_{E}(\eta)=(2-d)\eta,\quad Lie_{E}(\circ)=\circ.

The structure of a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold can be locally described by an analytic function
{\mathcal{F}}
, called its
potential
. Namely, let
\mu=\dim M
and
t_{1},\dots,t_{\mu}
be flat coordinates of the Levi-Civita connection above. At a point
p\in M
, consider
T_{p}M={\mathbb{C}}\langle\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t_{1}},\dots,\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t_{\mu}}\rangle
. Assume in addition that
t_{1}
is such that
e=\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t_{1}}
,
\eta_{ij}=\eta(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t_{i}},\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t_{j}})
– the components of
\eta
in the basis fixed, and
\eta^{ij}
being components of
\eta^{-1}
. Then there is a function
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{F}}(t_{1},\dots,t_{\mu})
such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t_{i}}\circ\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t_{j}}=\sum_{k,l=1}^{\mu}\frac{{\partial}^{3}{\mathcal{F}}}{{\partial}t_{i}{\partial}t_{j}{\partial}t_{l}}\eta^{lk}\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t_{k}},

(4.2)

\displaystyle\eta_{ij}=\frac{{\partial}^{3}{\mathcal{F}}}{{\partial}t_{1}{\partial}t_{i}{\partial}t_{j}},\qquad E\cdot{\mathcal{F}}=(3-d){\mathcal{F}}+\text{terms quadratic in $t_{\bullet}$}.

Locally the potential
{\mathcal{F}}
fully encodes the data of a Frobenius manifold
M
.

4.1.
Formal Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold

Consider a formal power series
{\mathcal{F}}\in{\mathbb{C}}[[t_{1},\dots,t_{\mu}]]
that satisfies Eq. (
4.2
) for some
E
,
\eta
and
d
. Introduce the product
\circ
on the formal vectors
\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t_{1}},\dots,\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t_{\mu}}
by the formula (
4.1
). Let this product be commutative and associative.

Then
{\mathcal{F}}
is said to define a
formal
Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold. It would become a true Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold if one associates a complex manifold
M
to
{\mathcal{F}}
, such that
{\mathcal{F}}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
.

Note that the associativity condition on the product becomes a system of PDEs on the function
{\mathcal{F}}
called the
WDVV equation
.

In particular, the genus zero generating functions of Gromov–Witten theory or any cohomological field theory define some formal Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds. This follows from the topology of the moduli space of genus zero curves, implying that the corresponding generating functions satisfy the WDVV equation.

Examples of formal Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds are given by topological cohomological field theories. Let
(A,\circ,\eta,e)
be a
\mu
–dimensional Frobenius algebra; then its topological cohomological field theory has the genus zero potential

{\mathcal{F}}^{top}(t_{1},\dots,t_{\mu}):=\frac{1}{3!}\sum_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}=1}^{\mu}\eta(\phi_{\alpha_{1}}\circ\phi_{\alpha_{2}},\phi_{\alpha_{3}})t_{\alpha_{1}}t_{\alpha_{2}}t_{\alpha_{3}}.

Even though formal Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds lack such an important property as analyticity, they enjoy the following additional advantage. A. Givental introduced in
[
G01a
,
G04
]
a group action on the space of formal Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds. This group action will play an important role later on.

4.2.
Dubrovin connection

Associate to any Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold the connection on
M\times{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}_{z}
given by

(4.3)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{X}:={\nabla}\hskip-3.41432pt{\text{\footnotesize{\bf/}}}_{X}+\frac{1}{z}C_{X},\quad\widetilde{\nabla}_{\frac{d}{dz}}:=\frac{d}{dz}+\frac{1}{z}\left(B_{0}+\frac{B_{\infty}}{z}\right)

for
C_{X}(Y):=X\circ Y
,
B_{0}(Y):=E\circ Y
, and the diagonal grading operator
B_{\infty}(Y):=\dfrac{2-d}{2}Y-\nabla_{Y}E
.

This new connection is flat. It provides an important piece of data of a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold. In particular, its flat sections reconstruct the product uniquely.

Given a manifold
M
with a flat pairing
\eta
and a fiberwise product
\circ
on
{\mathcal{T}}_{M}
satisfying the Frobenius algebra property, if
\widetilde{\nabla}
is flat, then
M
is a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold (cf. Proposition 2.1 in
[
D2
]
and
[
Sa
]
). This gives one of the ways to construct Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds.

4.3.
Givental’s group action

Let
\eta
be a
\mu\times\mu
matrix of a constant non–degenerate bilinear form. The space of all matrix–valued formal power series
R=\mathrm{Id}+\sum_{k\geq 1}R_{k}z^{k}
with
R_{k}\in\mathrm{Mat}({\mathbb{C}},\mu)
, satisfying the condition
R(z)\eta^{-1}R^{T}(-z)=\eta^{-1}
, is called
Givental’s group
.

By A. Givental, this group acts on the space of formal Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds of dimension
\mu
with metric
\eta
(cf.
[
G01a
]
). This group action is now known under the name of Givental’s group action
1
1

1

also called the upper-triangular group action.
.

Givental’s action has appeared to be very important in different applications. It can be introduced via the graph summation formula
[
DbSS
]
or by the action of a differential operator
[
G01a
,
L08
]
. However, the particular formulae will not play any role in our text and are therefore skipped.

4.4.
From BV/
\Delta
to a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold

In
[
KMS
]
Khoroshkin, Markarian and Shadrin constructed the quasi-isomorphism of operads
\theta:\mathrm{Hycomm}\to\mathrm{BV}/\Delta
. To every BV–algebra
(V,d,\Delta)
with trivialized BV–operator they associate the map

\theta_{n}:H^{\ast}(V,d)^{\otimes n}\to H^{\ast}(V,d).

In case
H^{\ast}(V,d)
is a Frobenius algebra with pairing
\eta
and unit
1
, the correlators

\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle^{\Phi}:=\eta\left(1,\theta_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\right)

are the correlators of some genus zero cohomological field theory. This construction strongly depends on the choice of the BV–operator trivialization, and this is the reason why we put superscript
\Phi
on the correlators.

In particular, if
H^{\ast}(V,d)\cong{\mathbb{C}}\langle\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{\mu}\rangle
, then the generating function

{\mathcal{F}}^{\Phi}(t_{1},\dots,t_{\mu}):=\sum_{n\geq 3}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}}\langle\phi_{\alpha_{1}},\dots,\phi_{\alpha_{n}}\rangle^{\Phi}t_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots t_{\alpha_{n}}\in{\mathbb{C}}[[t_{1},\dots,t_{\mu}]]

is a solution to the WDVV equation and defines a formal Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold.

4.4.1.
Topological trivialization
\Phi^{top}

For the BV–algebra
({\mathrm{PV}},\overline{{\partial}}_{f},{\partial})
, the trivialization
\Phi^{top}
was called topological due to the following reason:

\Phi_{k}^{top}([a])\equiv 0\in H^{\ast}({\mathrm{PV}},\overline{{\partial}}_{f}+z{\partial}),\quad\forall k\geq 1\ \text{and}\ [a]\in H^{\ast}({\mathrm{PV}},\overline{{\partial}}_{f}).

Then the construction of Khoroshkin, Markarian and Shadrin gives

{\mathcal{F}}^{\Phi^{top}}(t_{1},\dots,t_{\mu})=\frac{1}{3!}\sum_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}}\langle\phi_{\alpha_{1}},\phi_{\alpha_{2}},\phi_{\alpha_{3}}\rangle^{\Phi^{top}}t_{\alpha_{1}}t_{\alpha_{2}}t_{\alpha_{3}}=\frac{1}{3!}\sum_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}}\eta(\phi_{\alpha_{1}}\circ\phi_{\alpha_{2}},\phi_{\alpha_{3}})t_{\alpha_{1}}t_{\alpha_{2}}t_{\alpha_{3}}.

This is exactly the genus zero potential of the topological CohFT associated with
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
.

We are going to use special trivializations of
{\partial}
in order to get the Saito theory Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold later on.

4.5.
Different trivializations

Another major result of
[
KMS
]
is the following. Fix the BV–algebra
(A,d,\Delta)
, and let
\Phi
be a trivialization of the BV–operator. Assume
R=\mathrm{Id}+\sum_{p\geq 1}z^{p}R_{p}\in{\mathrm{End}}(V)[[z]]
commutes with
d
. Then
R\Phi
is another trivialization of the BV–operator of the same BV–algebra.

Let
H^{\ast}(V,d)
be endowed with the pairing and unit. Then we have two sets of correlators

\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle^{\Phi}\quad\text{and}\quad\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle^{R\Phi}

giving, a priori, two different generating functions
{\mathcal{F}}^{\Phi}
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{R\Phi}
. Khoroshkin, Markarian and Shadrin prove:

Theorem 4.1

(Theorem 6.3 in
[
KMS
]
)
.

If
R
, assumed as an operator on
H^{\ast}(V,d)
, satisfies the condition
R(z)\eta^{-1}R^{T}(-z)=\eta^{-1}
, then the generating functions
{\mathcal{F}}^{\Phi}
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{R\Phi}
are connected by the Givental action of
R
(see Section
4.3
).

5.
Brieskorn lattice and BV algebra of the unfolding

Given a quasihomogeneous singularity, Saito’s theory allows one to construct a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold structure on the unfolding space of the singularity. This construction is not unique, depending on the additional choice — primitive form or opposite subspace. Main references are
[
ST
,
H
]
and the citations therein.

5.1.
Unfolding of a singularity

Consider an
unfolding
of
f
. Namely, the function
F:Z\to{\mathbb{C}}
, where
Z={\mathbb{C}}^{N}\times{\mathcal{S}}
, for some open neighbourhood of the origin
{\mathcal{S}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{\mu}
with coordinates
s_{\bullet}
, defined by

F({\bf x},{\bf s})=f(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{\mu}\phi_{k}(x)s_{k}.

Here the polynomials
\phi_{\bullet}\in{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}]
are taken to be such that their classes generate
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
. We also assume these polynomials to be homogeneous with respect to the function
\mathrm{wt}
introduced in previous sections.

Let
p:Z\to{\mathcal{S}}
be the projection on the second factor. Consider the critical sheaf

{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{C}}:={\mathcal{O}}_{Z}/\left({\partial}_{x_{1}}F,\dots,{\partial}_{x_{N}}F\right).

Then
p_{\ast}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{C}}
is an
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}
–module of rank
\mu
with the essential product structure.

Let
(\Omega^{\bullet}_{Z/{\mathcal{S}}},d_{Z/{\mathcal{S}}})
stand for the de Rham complex relative to
p
. Consider the direct image sheaf

{\mathbb{R}}^{k}p_{\ast}\left(\Omega^{\bullet}_{Z/{\mathcal{S}}}[z],zd_{Z/{\mathcal{S}}}+dF\wedge\right).

It vanishes for
k\neq N
. And when
k=N
, it is isomorphic to the sheaf

{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}:=\Omega^{N}_{Z/{\mathcal{S}}}[z]/(zd_{Z/{\mathcal{S}}}+dF\wedge)\Omega^{N-1}_{Z/{\mathcal{S}}}[z].

The fiber of it over a point
{\bf s}\in{\mathcal{S}}
is given by
{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{g}
with
g:{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{C}}
given by
g(x)=F(x,s)
. This sheaf is a locally free
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}[z]
–module of rank
\mu
(cf. Proposition 3.5 of
[
IMRS
]
).

5.1.1.
Gauss–Manin connection

{\mathcal{H}}_{F}
is endowed with the Gauss–Manin connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{GM}}
defined as follows. Let
v\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathcal{S}}
,
\phi\in{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}[z]
, and
d^{N}{\bf x}=dx_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{N}
. Denote by
v(\phi)
the directional derivative along
v
and by
[\phi d^{N}{\bf x}]
the class of
\phi d^{N}{\bf x}
in
{\mathcal{H}}_{F}
.

(5.1)

\displaystyle\nabla_{v}^{\mathrm{GM}}[\phi d^{N}{\bf x}]:=\left[(v(\phi)+z^{-1}\phi\cdot v(F))d^{N}{\bf x}\right],

(5.2)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}z}}^{\mathrm{GM}}[\phi d^{N}{\bf x}]:=\left[\left(\frac{{\partial}\phi}{{\partial}z}-z^{-2}\phi\cdot F-\frac{N}{2}z^{-1}\phi\right)d^{N}{\bf x}\right].

The Gauss-Manin connection satisfies the Leibniz rule and is flat.

This connection is not of the form of Dubrovin connection (
4.3
). Some additional work has to be done in order to put it in the right form. There are two ways to do that. The first one uses the period mapping defined by the primitive form, and the second one is essentially in the form of S. Barannikov’s semi-infinite variations of Hodge structures.

Both approaches make use of the higher residue pairing and good bases.

5.1.2.
Higher residue pairing

K. Saito introduced the pairing (cf.
[
SK2
]
)

K_{F}:{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}\otimes_{{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}[z]

called the
higher residue pairing
, uniquely defined by the following properties.
Let

K_{F}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\sum_{p\geq 0}z^{p}K_{F}^{(p)}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

then we have

(1)

K_{F}^{(p)}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=(-1)^{p}K_{F}^{(p)}(\omega_{2},\omega_{1})
.

(2)

K_{F}(z\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=-K_{F}(\omega_{1},z\omega_{2})=zK_{F}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
.

(3)

K_{F}^{(0)}
defines the pairing

{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}/z{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}/z{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}\to{\mathbb{C}},\quad\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2}\mapsto K_{F}^{(0)}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

which coincides with the residue pairing
\eta
.

(4)

K_{F}
is flat with respect to the Gauss–Manin connection:

\xi\cdot K_{F}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=K_{F}(\nabla_{\xi}^{\mathrm{GM}}\omega_{1},\omega_{2})-K_{F}(\omega_{1},\nabla_{\xi}^{\mathrm{GM}}\omega_{2})

for
\xi=z^{2}\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}z}
or
\xi\in z{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathcal{S}}
.

This is a theorem of K. Saito that these properties fix the higher residue pairing completely.

The higher residue pairing extends to a pairing
\omega_{F}:{\mathcal{H}}_{F}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{F}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}
by

\omega_{F}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}):=\mathrm{res}_{z=0}K_{F}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})dz.

The Brieskorn lattice
{\mathcal{H}}_{f}^{(0)}
is endowed with the pairing
K_{f}:{\mathcal{H}}_{f}^{(0)}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{f}^{(0)}\to{\mathbb{C}}[z]
given by
K_{f}:=K_{F}\mid_{{\bf s}=0}
. Similarly,
{\mathcal{H}}_{f}
has the pairing
\omega_{f}:=\omega_{F}\mid_{{\bf s}=0}
.

5.1.3.
Weight function

Extend the weight function
\mathrm{wt}
to
{\mathcal{H}}_{F}
. To do this we need to extend
\mathrm{wt}
to
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}
. This is done by requiring
\mathrm{wt}(F)=1
. Then
\mathrm{wt}(s_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha})=\mathrm{wt}(s_{\alpha})+\mathrm{wt}(\phi_{\alpha})=1
for any
\alpha=1,\dots,\mu
, giving
\mathrm{wt}(s_{\alpha})=1-\mathrm{wt}(\phi_{\alpha})
.

For quasihomogeneous singularities, the flatness of
K_{F}
with respect to the Gauss–Manin connection in the
{\partial}/{\partial}z
direction is equivalent to the condition

K_{F}^{(p)}([\alpha d^{N}{\bf x}],[\beta d^{N}{\bf x}])=0\ \text{unless}\ \deg\alpha+\deg\beta=p+\deg\mathrm{hess}(f).

for any homogeneous elements
[\alpha d^{N}{\bf x}],[\beta d^{N}{\bf x}]\in{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}
. Note that this condition holds for
K_{F}^{(0)}
and the residue pairing.

5.2.
Good bases

A subspace
{\mathcal{L}}\subset{\mathcal{H}}_{f}
is called a
good opposite subspace
if the following four conditions hold:

(1)

{\mathcal{H}}_{f}={\mathcal{H}}_{f}^{(0)}\oplus{\mathcal{L}}
,

(2)

the direct sum decomposition above is quasihomogeneous with respect to
\mathrm{wt}
,

(3)

{\mathcal{L}}

7.
Primitive form calculus

Another way to construct a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold in Saito theory is via the period mapping of K. Saito (
[
SK3
]
).

Fix an element
\zeta\in{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}
and consider the map
\Psi:{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}/z{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}
given by
\Psi(X):=z\nabla_{X}\zeta
. If one makes a special choice of
\zeta
, called nowadays the
primitive form
of K. Saito, then
\Psi
is an isomorphism of
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}
–modules transforming the connection
\nabla^{{\mathrm{GM}}}
to a Dubrovin connection.

The choice of primitive form is usually not unique. Its existence was proved by M. Saito
[
SM1
,
SM2
]
. He also proved that there is a one-to-one correspondence between primitive forms and good bases of the previous sections.

7.1.
“Perturbative” primitive form calculus

Fix a point
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
. It was observed in
[
LLS
]
that the formal operator
e^{(F-f)/z}
commutes two differentials:

(\overline{{\partial}}_{f}+z{\partial})e^{(F-f)/z}=e^{(F-f)/z}(\overline{{\partial}}_{F}+z{\partial}).

This is a starting point to the following recipe to compute the primitive form.

Fix an opposite subspace
{\mathcal{L}}
of
{\mathcal{H}}_{f}^{(0)}
. Let
B:=z{\mathcal{L}}\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{f}^{(0)}
. Introduce the exponential operator
e^{(F-f)/z}:B\to B((z^{-1}))[[{\bf s}]]
by

e^{(F-f)/z}\left([\phi_{\alpha}d^{\bf x}]\right):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\beta=1}^{\mu}\sum_{m\geq-k}h^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta,m}\frac{z^{m}}{k!}[\phi_{\beta}d^{N}{\bf x}]

for
h_{\alpha\beta,m}^{(k)}
obtained by the following equality in
{\mathcal{H}}_{f}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}
:

[z^{-k}(F-f)^{k}\phi_{\alpha}d^{N}{\bf x}]=\sum_{\beta=1}^{\mu}\sum_{m\geq-k}h^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta,m}\frac{z^{m}}{k!}[\phi_{\beta}d^{N}{\bf x}].

Note that the exponential operator depends on the choice of the opposite subspace. Its components
h^{(k)}_{\alpha\beta,m}
assume decomposing an
{\mathcal{H}}_{f}
element in the basis fixed by the opposite subspace.

C. Li, S. Li and K. Saito prove:

Theorem 7.1

(Theorem 4.15 of
[
LLS
]
and Theorem 3.7 of
[
LLSS
]
)
.

There exists a unique pair
(\zeta,J)
with
\zeta\in B[z][[{\bf s}]]
and
J\in[d^{N}{\bf x}]+z^{-1}B[[z^{-1}]][[{\bf s}]]
such that

(7.1)

J=e^{(F-f)/z}(\zeta).

The element
\zeta
is the primitive form fixed by the opposite subspace
{\mathcal{L}}
.

The theorem above works in the ring of formal power series in
{\bf s}
; however, it is known from the general theory that there is a unique analytic primitive form associated to the fixed opposite filtration. Then the formal primitive form
\zeta
above is a series expansion of the analytic primitive form (cf. Section 4.3 of
[
LLS
]
).

7.2.
BV algebra primitive form calculus

For any
p\geq 0
, let
{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf s}]_{p}
stand for the finite–dimensional subspace of
{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf s}]
spanned by monomials of total degree
p
in
s_{1},\dots,s_{\mu}
. For any
\phi\in{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf s}]

\phi_{(p)}
the image of
\phi
under the projection
{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf s}]\to{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf s}]_{p}
.

Fix a basis
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{\mu}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}
.
For
\zeta\in{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}
, let
\zeta_{(p)}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}
be its
p
–homogeneous component in
{\bf s}
. Namely,

\zeta=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\mu}d_{\alpha}({\bf s},{\bf z})\omega_{\alpha}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\zeta_{(p)}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\mu}\left(d_{\alpha}({\bf s},{\bf z})\right)_{(p)}\omega_{\alpha}.

Obviously we have
\zeta=\sum_{p\geq 0}\zeta_{(p)}
.

Denote by
\pi_{>0}
and
\pi_{\leq 0}
the projections
{\mathbb{C}}((z^{-1}))\to z{\mathbb{C}}[z]
and
{\mathbb{C}}((z^{-1}))\to{\mathbb{C}}[[z^{-1}]]
given by

\displaystyle\pi_{>0}(\sum_{k=-\infty}^{m}a_{k}z^{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}z^{k},\quad\pi_{\leq 0}(\sum_{k=-\infty}^{m}a_{k}z^{k})=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{0}a_{k}z^{k}.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\Phi_{f}^{\omega}
be the
{\partial}
–trivialization associated with the opposite subspace
{\mathcal{L}}
.
Then the primitive form
\zeta
associated to
{\mathcal{L}}
is found via the recursive formula for its components in the associated good basis:

(7.2)

\displaystyle\zeta_{(p)}=-\left[\pi_{>0}\cdot(\Phi_{f}^{\omega})^{-1}\Big(\sum_{a=1}^{p}\frac{(F-f)^{a}}{z^{a}a!}\zeta_{(p-a)}\Big)\right]_{f},\quad p\geq 1,

where
[-]_{f}
stands for taking the classes in
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
.

Proof.

Using the isomorphism
\Upsilon_{0}
of Eq.
3.4
, we can equivalently solve Eq. (
7.1
) in
H^{\ast}({\mathrm{PV}}((z^{-1}))\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}},\overline{{\partial}}_{f}+z{\partial})
.
Slightly abusing the notation, we use the same letters
J
and
\zeta
for the respective elements in
H^{\ast}({\mathrm{PV}}((z^{-1}))\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}},\overline{{\partial}}_{f}+z{\partial})
and
H^{\ast}({\mathrm{PV}}[z]\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}},\overline{{\partial}}_{F}+z{\partial})
.

Note that under
\Upsilon_{0}
and
\Upsilon_{1}
, the exponential operator
e^{(F-f)/z}
is mapped to the operator of multiplication by the
{\mathrm{PV}}^{0}[[z^{-1}]]
–element

.

We have by construction
\Phi_{f}^{\omega}(J)=J
because
\Phi_{f}^{\omega}
acts identically on the good basis elements.
Then Eq. (
7.1
) is equivalent to

J=\left[\pi_{\leq 0}\cdot(\Phi_{f}^{\omega})^{-1}\Big(\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(F-f)^{k}}{z^{k}k!}\zeta\Big)\right]_{f}

where both

and

Then we have

because
\Phi_{f}^{\omega}
acts identically on the good basis elements. This concludes the proof because
\Phi_{f}^{\omega}
does not depend on the variables
{\bf s}
, and
F-f\in{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}]\otimes{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf s}]_{1}
.

[
LLS

8.
R–matrix of a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold

It was first observed by B. Dubrovin (cf. Lemma 4.2
[
D2
]
) that under the action of a formal power series
R=\mathrm{Id}+\sum_{k\geq 1}z^{k}R_{k}
with
R_{k}\in\mathrm{Mat}({\mathbb{C}},\mu)
(called gauge transform in loc.cit.), Dubrovin’s connection
4.3
assumes a very simple form:

R^{-1}\cdot\widetilde{\nabla}_{\frac{d}{dz}}(R\cdot X)=\frac{dX}{dz}-z^{-2}B_{0}(X).

This formal power series satisfies

(8.1)

[B_{0},R_{m+1}]=(m+B_{\infty})R_{m},\quad m\geq 0.

It was introduced by B. Dubrovin for classification purposes and later used by A. Givental in
[
G04
]
in order to define the higher genus potential of the Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold.

Proposition 8.1

(Proposition of Section 1.3
[
G04
]
)
.

For any Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold in a neighborhood of a semisimple point, the following holds:

•

The solution
R
to Eq. (
8.1
) exists. It can be chosen to satisfy the “symplectic” condition

R(z)\eta^{-1}R^{T}(-z)=\eta^{-1}.

•

If
R
is a homogeneous solution to Eq. (
8.1
):

R_{k}=-\frac{1}{k}\iota_{E}dR_{k},\quad\forall k\geq 1,

then it satisfies the symplectic condition above and is unique.

The unique solution
R
of the proposition above will be called the
R–matrix of a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold
(also called Givental’s R–matrix). Its importance is justified by the following theorem.

Theorem 8.2

(
[
G01b
,
G04
]
)
.

Let
(M,\circ,\eta,e)
be a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold.
At a semisimple point
p\in M
, its potential is reconstructed from the topological theory potential of
(T_{p}M,\circ,\eta)
via the Givental action of the R–matrix of the Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold.

It is important to note that the R–matrix of a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold depends on the choice of the semisimple point
p
.

The coordinate
z
in the R–matrix above is completely formal for a generic Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold. However, it coincides with the
z
coordinate of
{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}
and
\nabla^{\mathrm{GM}}
for Saito theory Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds. Note that we have there
\mathrm{wt}(z)=1
. Then the homogeneity condition of the R–matrix above is just
\mathrm{wt}R=0
.

8.1.
R–matrix in PV

We apply Proposition
6.3
in order to rewrite the condition on the R–matrix of a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold.

Denote
\Phi:=\Upsilon_{1}\Phi^{\omega}
. We have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}z}}^{\omega}(R(a))=\Phi^{-1}\cdot\nabla^{\mathrm{PV}}_{\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}z}}\left(\Phi(R(a))\right).

Denote
\widetilde{R}:=\Phi\cdot R
. The equality above rewrites as

(8.2)

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}\left(\frac{{\partial}a}{{\partial}z}-z^{-2}Fa\right)=\frac{{\partial}\widetilde{R}}{{\partial}z}(a)+\widetilde{R}(\frac{{\partial}a}{{\partial}z})-z^{2}F\widetilde{R}(a).

(8.3)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle[F,\widetilde{R}](a)=z^{2}\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}z}\widetilde{R}(a)

that should hold in
H^{\ast}({\mathrm{PV}}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}[z],\overline{{\partial}}_{F}+z{\partial})
for all
a
.

In what follows we construct the operator
\widetilde{R}
solving the equation above explicitly. To do this we need some ingredients.

Lemma 8.3
.

In a neighborhood of a semisimple point
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
, there is an operator
{\mathcal{A}}=\sum_{k\geq 0}A_{k}z^{k}\in{\mathrm{End}}(H^{\ast}({\mathrm{PV}}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}}[z],\bar{\partial}_{F}+z{\partial}))
such that
F\cdot{\mathcal{A}}=\mathrm{Id}
.

Proof.

The action of the operator
F\cdot
in
H^{\ast}({\mathrm{PV}}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}},\bar{\partial}_{F})
is given by multiplication by the Euler vector field. This operator is known to be invertible at a semisimple point. Denote this inverse by
A_{0}
. The higher
A_{k}
are found recursively by the following procedure. It’s enough to solve

(\Phi^{\omega})^{-1}\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}A_{k}z^{k}F\right)-1\equiv 0\quad\text{in}\ H^{\ast}({\mathrm{PV}}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{S}},\bar{\partial}_{F})\otimes{\mathbb{C}}[[z]].

Let
(\Phi^{\omega})^{-1}=\sum_{p\geq 0}\overline{\Phi}_{p}z^{p}
. Then the
k
–th order in
z
of the equation above reads

0\equiv\sum_{a+b=k}\overline{\Phi}_{a}(A_{b}F)=A_{k}F+\sum_{a=1}^{k}\overline{\Phi}_{a}(A_{k-a}F).

It allows to express
A_{k}
via
A_{0},\dots,A_{k-1}
becasue
F\cdot
is invertible.

∎

One notes immediately that every
A_{k}
is homogeneous of degree
-(k+1)
for all
k\geq 0
.

For all
