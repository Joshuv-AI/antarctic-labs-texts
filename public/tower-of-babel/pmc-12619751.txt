Feed quantitative restriction effect on performance, carcass quality, blood parameters and immunity of broiler chickens
Authors: Magdalena Solka, Joanna Marchewka, Aneta Jaszczyk, Abbas Sefaty, Alireza Seidavi, Mehrdad Bouyeh, Silvia Ondrašovičová, Hanna Michnowska, Patryk Sztandarski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of early-age, short-term quantitative feed restriction followed by compensatory refeeding with energy and protein-dense diets on the performance, carcass traits, blood biochemistry, and immune response of broiler chickens. A total of 270 Ross 308 chicks were allocated to a 2 × 4 factorial design plus a control group. Treatments included two restriction durations (days 8–12 and 8–17) and four feed restriction intensities (22.5%, 25.0%, 27.5%, and 30.0%) based on standard nutritional recommendations, along with a control group fed ad libitum. Growth performance was recorded weekly, and carcass characteristics, organ weights, blood metabolites, and antibody titers (HI and SRBC) were assessed at day 42. Data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA under a completely randomized design, and treatment means were compared using Duncan’s multiple range test at a significance level of P < 0.05. Feed restriction temporarily reduced feed intake, weight gain, and efficiency parameters during the restriction period especially in the 10-day and higher intensity groups (P < 0.05). However, birds exhibited compensatory growth during refeeding, with final body weights and carcass yields comparable to controls. Abdominal fat percentage was significantly lower in restricted birds, particularly in the 10-day, 30% group (P < 0.05). Triglyceride and VLDL levels were reduced in the 25% restriction group, while other blood lipids and glucose remained unaffected. HI antibody titers against Newcastle disease were significantly higher in some restricted groups, notably the 5-day, 30% group (P < 0.05), with no adverse effects on SRBC responses. These findings suggest that early short-term feed restriction, when followed by nutrient-dense refeeding, can improve feed efficiency and reduce fat deposition without compromising carcass yield or humoral immunity.

Introduction

Poultry production has undergone significant advancements over the past decades, with broiler chickens exhibiting remarkable genetic improvements in growth rate and feed efficiency (Zuidhof et al. 2014; Tallentire et al. 2016). Currently, modern poultry production necessitates strategies that enhance efficiency, while maintaining animal health, welfare and product quality. Specific selection of broilers for high growth rate by the poultry industry has resulted in commercial genotypes that can reach high market weight within a short rearing period (Boyle 2005). The selection for high growth rate is marked by increased carcass fat, increased carcass water content, and increased breast muscle (Havenstein et al. 2003). This accelerated growth predisposes birds to metabolic disorders, including ascites, skeletal deformities, and sudden death syndrome (Julian 2005; Davoodi-Omam et al. 2019). To mitigate these issues and to improve feed efficiency, while reducing fat deposition, various feeding strategies have been employed, among which quantitative feed restriction is a widely adopted approach (Mirshamsollahi 2013; De Jong et al. 2014). That feed restriction has been extensively investigated for its ability to improve feed conversion efficiency, regulate body composition, and reduce excessive fat deposition (Saleh et al. 2005; Jahanpour et al. 2013; 2014a, b; Rahimi et al. 2015). Previous studies have demonstrated that moderate feed restriction can lead to improved feed efficiency and reduced incidence of metabolic diseases (Tolkamp et al. 2005; Rahimi et al. 2015). Furthermore, it has been shown that early-age feed restricted broilers could compensate for initial retarded growth with reduced carcass fat (Molapo and Webb 2014). In turn, some reports have shown that feed restriction can reduce carcass and body weights at slaughter (Leeson and Summers 2001), whereas others have reported that feed restriction has no effect on carcass characteristics (Mirshamsollahi 2013). However, the application of severe short-term feed restriction raises concerns regarding its potential negative impact on immune competence and physiological homeostasis.

The potential health impacts of feed restriction require deeper investigation, as severe restriction may induce stress, compromise immune function, and alter hematological parameters, potentially affecting the overall health and physiological stability of broiler chickens (Gous 2010; Davoodi-Omam et al. 2019). Additionally, while compensatory growth following the restriction period may offset body weight deficits, the physiological costs associated with such an approach require further exploration (Zubair and Leeson 1996). Understanding these impacts is crucial for determining the trade-offs between economic benefits and potential health risks in commercial broiler production.

The immune system plays a fundamental role in protecting birds from pathogens, and any nutritional stressor, including feed restriction, may influence immune competence (Kidd 2004). Studies suggest that feed restriction can suppress immune responses, potentially increasing susceptibility to infections (Puvadolpirod and Thaxton 2000; Davoodi-Omam et al. 2019). Changes in hematological parameters, such as hematocrit (HCT), hemoglobin (Hb), and leukocyte profiles, can serve as indicators of physiological stress and overall health status (Maxwell et al. 1990). Evaluating these factors in the context of severe short-term restriction will provide insights into how broilers cope with feed deprivation and subsequent refeeding (Luger et al. 2001).

In light of these challenges, the current study investigates how early short-term quantitative feed restriction, followed by refeeding with a nutrient-dense diet, influences growth performance, physiological responses, and immune function in broilers. This approach aims to uncover whether such a feeding strategy can mitigate the negative effects of rapid growth while maintaining production efficiency and provide valuable insights for optimizing feeding strategies in intensive poultry systems.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

All experimental procedures were approved by Committee of Rasht Branch, Islamic Azad University, Rasht, Iran (Approval No. 11750103921014). The number of animals was kept to the minimum required, and all efforts were made to reduce discomfort.

Experimental design, animals, and housing

The study was conducted in Fuman, Guilan Province, Iran, using 270 Ross 308 male broiler chicks. Birds were randomly assigned to eight treatments in a 2 × 4 factorial design evaluating two restriction durations (5 or 10 days, from day 8 onward) and four levels of quantitative feed restriction (22.5, 25.0, 27.5, or 30.0% below the Ross 308 standard feeding guide) plus a control group. The ninth group served as control and was fed according to the standard guide from day 1 to day 42 without restriction.

Birds were housed in floor pens (1.5 × 1 m) within an environmentally controlled facility (temperature 24–32 °C, relative humidity 55–65%). From day 2, lighting was set at 23 h light and 1 h darkness. Tunnel ventilation was used to minimize heat stress. Feed was offered in trays for the first two weeks and in standard feeders thereafter. Water was provided ad libitum via drinkers cleaned twice daily. Vaccinations were administered according to veterinary recommendations. Each treatment included three replicates, with 10 birds per pen.

Growth performance

Feed intake (FI) and body weight gain (BWG) were recorded weekly per pen. FI was calculated as feed offered minus feed residues; BWG as the difference between final and initial body weight. At day 42, birds and residual feed were weighed to calculate cumulative FI, BWG, and feed conversion ratio (FCR):\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FCR=Total\;body\;weight\;gain\;(g)/Total\;feed\;intake\;(g)$$\end{document}

Mortality was recorded daily. Body weights of dead birds were included in corrected FCR calculations.

Carcass and organ measurements

At day 42, one representative male per replicate (closest to mean body weight) was slaughtered after a 6-h fast (water available). Birds were euthanized by cervical dislocation, head and shanks were removed, and carcasses were eviscerated. Hot carcass weight and carcass yield (% of live body weight) were determined.

Viscera (liver, heart, spleen, pancreas, bursa of Fabricius) were excised, blotted, and weighed; organ weights were expressed relative to live body weight. The gastrointestinal tract was separated into duodenum, jejunum, ileum, cecum, rectum, and colon using standard anatomical landmarks. Segments were emptied, rinsed, blotted, weighed, and their lengths measured with a ruler. Abdominal fat was dissected, weighed, and expressed relative to body weight.

Blood collection and biochemical analysis

At day 42, blood samples (1 mL) were taken from the wing vein of representative males into EDTA tubes. Samples were processed within 2 h; plasma was separated (3000 × g, 10 min), stored at −20°C, and analyzed using commercial biochemical kits.

Immune response

Birds were vaccinated against avian influenza (day 1), infectious bronchitis (day 1), Gumboro disease (day 19), and Newcastle disease (days 1, 5, 8, 21). Antibody titers against Newcastle disease were assessed at days 11 and 27, and against avian influenza at days 30 and 40, using the hemagglutination inhibition (HI) test.

For additional assessment, birds were injected subcutaneously with sheep red blood cells (SRBC; 0.5 mL of 10% suspension in PBS) at days 11 and 29. Blood samples were collected at days 25 and 42 to evaluate primary and secondary antibody responses.

Statistical analysis

The study was analyzed as a completely randomized design with nine treatments. Data were subjected to the GLM procedure of SPSS (1997) (version 7.5). Data were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA under a completely randomized design, and treatment means were compared using Duncan’s multiple range test at a significance level of P < 0.05. Although the factorial ANOVA approach was suitable for detecting the main and interaction effects between restriction duration and intensity, the restriction level represents a quantitative variable.

During the second week, both main factors significantly influenced performance. Birds subjected to 10-day restriction exhibited significantly lower daily feed intake (40.033 ± 0.1 16 g/day) and weight gain (24.332 ± 0.301 g/day) compared to those restricted for 5 days (45.476 ± 0.116 g/day and 30.439 ± 0.301 g/day, respectively; P < 0.001; Table 2). Feed efficiency was also reduced (1.648 ± 0.017 vs. 1.496 ± 0.0,17 P < 0.001). Energy (133.017 ± 0.340 vs. 117.095 ± 0.340 kcal/day) and protein intake (9.323 ± 0.024 vs. 8.207 ± 0.024 g/day) were lower, while energy (4.376 ± 0.049 vs. 4.819 ± 0.049 kcal/g) and protein efficiency (0.307 ± 0.003 vs. 0.338 ± 0.003) were higher in the 10-day group (p < 0.001). A significant duration × intensity interaction was observed (P < 0.05), indicating that the negative impact of intensity was more pronounced in the 10-day groups (Table 2).

In weeks 3 and 4, compensatory growth occurred. Duration showed a significant main effect. The 5-day group maintained significantly higher feed intake (92.833 ± 1.285 and 136.886 ± 2.429 g/day) compared to the 10-day group (84.842 ± 1.285 and 126.798 ± 2.429 g/day, P = 0.010; Table 3). Weight gain was also significantly higher in the 5-day group during weeks 4 and 5 (P < 0.05). Other traits did not differ significantly (P >0.05). Restriction intensity alone did not affect growth (P >0.05). Discussion

This study demonstrated that early short-term feed restriction temporarily reduced growth performance, particularly in birds restricted for 10 days. However, compensatory growth during refeeding enabled most birds to reach final body weights and carcass yields comparable to controls by day 42. These results confirm the capacity of broilers to recover from early nutritional challenges and highlight the potential of feed restriction to reduce abdominal fat deposition, particularly at higher restriction intensities (Zubair and Leeson 1996; Molapo and Webb 2014). Nevertheless, it should be acknowledged that the sample size per treatment was limited, which may reduce the statistical power to detect subtle differences, particularly in immune or biochemical parameters.

The novelty of this study lies in its multifactorial design, combining two restriction durations (5 and 10 days) with four intensities (22.5%, 25.0%, 27.5%, and 30.0%), followed by refeeding with nutrient-dense diets. Few prior studies have investigated both duration and intensity simultaneously within such a factorial design (Zulkifli et al. 2000; Leeson and Summers 2005). Conducted research has primarily focused on moderate restriction or longer durations (de Oliveira Grieser et al. 2017). Moreover, this study assessed multiple biological outcomes growth performance, carcass composition, organ development, blood biochemistry, and humoral immunity providing a comprehensive evaluation of physiological responses to feed restriction. However, only one commercial broiler strain (Ross 308) was studied, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other genotypes with different growth potentials or metabolic responses.

Feed efficiency improved during and after the restriction period, particularly at higher restriction intensities, supporting the hypothesis that nutrient utilization becomes more efficient following short-term deprivation (Saleh et al. 2005; Teimouri et al. 2005). Although feed intake and weight gain were suppressed during restriction, final body weights and carcass yields were not significantly different from controls. These findings are consistent with previous reports that moderate early restriction does not impair carcass characteristics when followed by adequate refeeding (Mirshamsollahi 2013; Jahanpour et al. 2015). Still, the trial was limited to market age (42 days), so long-term physiological consequences, including skeletal health, oxidative stress, or post-slaughter meat quality, were not assessed.

The focus on cumulative 42-day performance provides a more practical evaluation of the feeding strategy from a production perspective. Although short-term feed restriction temporarily reduced growth rate and feed intake, these effects were transient and did not influence final body weight, carcass yield, or feed efficiency at market age. This emphasizes that the compensatory growth observed during refeeding is sufficient to restore overall performance, confirming the resilience of broilers subjected to short-term quantitative restriction.

Abdominal fat content was significantly reduced in restricted groups, particularly in the 10-day, 30% group. This aligns with earlier evidence that feed restriction helps control fat deposition and supports the production of leaner carcasses, which are increasingly valued in consumer markets (Molapo and Webb 2014; Rahimi et al.2015). Importantly, muscle yields (breast, drumstick, wings) were maintained, indicating preservation of muscle accretion during compensatory growth. This is beneficial from a commercial standpoint, as leaner carcasses are often preferred in poultry markets focused on health-conscious consumers.

Although breast and leg muscle yields were numerically lower in some restricted groups, the differences were not statistically significant. This suggests that muscle development was maintained despite the nutritional challenge, as also reported by Urdaneta-Rincon and Leeson (2002). This preservation of muscle mass indicates a prioritization of protein accretion during the refeeding phase.

The reductions in triglycerides and VLDL observed in restricted groups, particularly at 25% restriction, suggest modulation of lipid metabolism. These findings are consistent with previous studies linking feed restriction to improved lipid profiles (Shabani et al. 2015). Other blood metabolites (total cholesterol and HDL or LDL) remained largely unaffected, indicating that the restriction protocols did not compromise overall metabolic stability.

The immune response outcomes provide valuable insight into the physiological adaptability of broiler chickens subjected to early quantitative feed restriction. Antibody titers against NDV were reduced in birds exposed to a 10-day restriction compared with those restricted for 5 days, suggesting transient suppression of vaccine responsiveness under prolonged deprivation. Conversely, a short, high-intensity (30%) restriction elicited higher NDV titers, indicating that brief nutritional stress may act as a controlled stimulus enhancing immune activation. The absence of treatment effects on SRBC antibody responses and the slightly elevated IgG levels in the most restricted groups further suggest that overall humoral immunity was preserved or even transiently stimulated.

These findings demonstrate that the immunomodulatory effects of feed restriction depend on both duration and intensity. While short, intensive restriction may prime immune mechanisms without adverse effects, extended deprivation appears to exceed the birds’ adaptive capacity. This aligns with recent evidence that moderate or short-term restriction enhances immune responsiveness (Urdaneta-Rincon and Leeson, 2002; Shabani et al., 2015), highlighting the inherent immunological plasticity of broilers.

From a practical standpoint, the results of this study have direct implications for commercial broiler production, where feed accounts for the largest share of input costs. Implementing a short-term feed restriction strategy during early growth phases could contribute to reduced feed consumption without compromising final body weight, carcass yield, or immune competence. The observed improvements in feed efficiency and reductions in fat deposition support economic goals of producing leaner birds more efficiently. Moreover, the findings suggest that restriction protocols do not necessarily impair immune responsiveness, alleviating concerns about disease susceptibility. These results are especially relevant in systems where feed efficiency and carcass quality must be balanced with welfare considerations and product uniformity. At the same time, the absence of behavioral and welfare indicators in the present study represents a limitation, as these aspects are critical for fully evaluating the implications of feed restriction under commercial conditions.

The strategy of refeeding high-energy, high-protein diets following restriction also mirrors practical interventions used in industry to support growth recovery. As such, the current findings may inform the design of cost-effective, phased feeding programs aimed at improving sustainability in poultry production. Future research should build on these results by incorporating broader welfare, health, and immunological endpoints to provide a more comprehensive assessment of early feed restriction strategies.

While the current results offer practical guidance for the development of staged feeding programs in commercial broiler production, several methodological and biological limitations should be considered. The study used a factorial ANOVA model, which was appropriate for detecting main and interactive effects of duration and intensity of restrictions. However, since the intensity of restrictions constituted a continuous gradient, regression-based methods could have provided a more detailed analysis of quantitative dose-response relationships. The experiment was conducted on a single commercial genotype (Ross 308) and lasted 42 days, which may limit the ability to extrapolate the results to other genetic lines or longer production cycles. Therefore, the potential long-term effects of early restrictions, such as changes in oxidation status, skeletal integrity, or post-slaughter meat quality, remain unclear. Although the study assessed antibody titers and immune organ weights, it did not include more advanced immunological tests (e.g., cytokine profiling, lymphocyte subtyping) or behavioral and welfare indicators, which are essential for a comprehensive evaluation of management interventions. Furthermore, the composition of the gut microbiota, which is a key modulator of both metabolism and immunity (Jahanpour et al. 2014b) was not examined. Including these aspects in future studies would allow for a better understanding of the mechanisms of action and practical significance of early feed restriction strategies.

In summary, this study provides strong evidence that short-term quantitative feed restriction, particularly at high intensity but limited duration, can improve feed efficiency and carcass leanness while maintaining immune function. Longer restriction periods, however, may transiently compromise vaccine responsiveness. These findings support the potential of controlled feed restriction as a management tool in broiler production but also highlight the importance of optimizing both intensity and duration to balance economic benefits with bird health and welfare.

Conclusions

Early short-term feed restriction induced transient adjustments in growth and physiology without compromising overall performance or immune competence. Broilers subjected to short, high-intensity restriction exhibited improved feed efficiency and reduced abdominal fat, followed by complete compensatory growth after refeeding. The maintenance or slight enhancement of antibody responses in these groups suggests that mild nutritional stress can stimulate adaptive physiological mechanisms rather than impair immune function. Collectively, the results indicate that early feed restriction can serve as a controlled management tool to improve efficiency and carcass leanness in broiler production while maintaining homeostatic balance.
