TAVR for Women
Authors: Dritan Useini
Published: 2026

I read with great interest the study1 reporting 7-year outcomes of balloon-expandable vs self-expanding valves in women undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). The authors should be congratulated for addressing 2 topics of growing importance in contemporary cardiovascular medicine: long-term outcomes after TAVR and sex-specific analyses.

The study is particularly noteworthy because women remain underrepresented in many cardiovascular trials, including coronary artery bypass grafting and percutaneous coronary intervention studies, where female participation often remains below 20%.2 In contrast, women constitute approximately half of patients enrolled in major TAVR trials,3 creating a unique opportunity to generate robust evidence in a population historically underrepresented in cardiovascular research. As interest in gender medicine continues to grow, studies specifically addressing long-term outcomes in women are particularly welcome.

The present report is also timely in light of emerging evidence regarding long-term outcomes after aortic valve intervention. Recent meta-analyses of randomized trials have suggested that the early procedural advantages of TAVR may not necessarily translate into superior long-term outcomes, with signals toward improved survival after surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) beyond 5 years.4 Simultaneously, contemporary network meta-analyses have challenged the concept of a uniform class effect among transcatheter valves, demonstrating important differences in long-term outcomes between individual TAVR platforms and highlighting the continuing relevance of SAVR as a benchmark therapy.5

Against this background, the authors provide valuable long-term data suggesting that valve selection in women, particularly those with small aortic annuli, remains complex and cannot be reduced to a simple comparison between TAVR and surgery or between transcatheter platforms. Their findings represent an important contribution to the evolving discussion on personalized valve therapy and sex-specific cardiovascular care.

Ultimately, individualized prediction of long-term outcomes, incorporating gender, patient anatomy, device selection, and life expectancy, should guide the heart team's decision-making—moving beyond age or surgical risk scores as a primary criterion.
