The RNA-binding protein RBFOX2 suppresses colorectal cancer proliferation and metastasis by reducing FUBP1 mRNA stability to induce mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis
Authors: WenDong Liu, GuangMing Dai, GuoLong Liao, Peng Ouyang, JinTuan Huang, Meng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveColorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide. Aberrant expression of RNA binding fox-1 homolog 2 (RBFOX2) has been implicated in tumorigenesis and progression; however, its biological functions and clinical significance in CRC remain poorly understood. This study aimed to elucidate the role of RBFOX2 in CRC.MethodsAnalysis of RBFOX2 expression levels in clinical CRC specimens was initially conducted utilizing the Human Protein Atlas database, with immunohistochemistry (IHC) data providing validation. The transcriptional and protein levels of RBFOX2 and far upstream element binding protein 1 (FUBP1) were quantitatively examined in 42 matched pairs of CRC and adjacent non-cancerous tissues using RT-qPCR and Western blot. The molecular interaction between RBFOX2 and FUBP1 was assessed via RNA immunoprecipitation and dual-luciferase reporter assays. FUBP1 transcript stability was determined following actinomycin D treatment. Functional assays, including MTT and colony formation, were performed in SW480 cells to assess proliferative capacity, whereas Transwell and wound-healing assays were used to evaluate migratory and invasive abilities. Mitochondrial ultrastructure was visualized by electron microscopy. Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) were measured by flow cytometry, and concentrations of malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH), and Fe²⁺ were determined using specific commercial assay kits. For in vivo studies, stable RBFOX2-overexpressing SW480 cells were inoculated into mouse models to establish subcutaneous xenografts and metastatic tumors. Tumor tissues were subsequently analyzed by IHC for Ki-67 expression and by hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining to evaluate metastatic nodules.ResultsRBFOX2 was downregulated in CRC. Overexpression of RBFOX2 suppressed CRC cell proliferation and migration in vitro and inhibited tumor growth and metastasis in vivo. RBFOX2 interacted with FUBP1 and regulated its mRNA stability, contributing to ferroptosis induction. FUBP1 overexpression reversed RBFOX2-mediated suppression of CRC cell proliferation and migration.ConclusionRBFOX2 interacts with FUBP1, destabilizes its mRNA, and promotes ferroptosis, thereby inhibiting CRC proliferation and metastasis, whereas FUBP1 exerts oncogenic effects. These findings provide novel insights into CRC molecular mechanisms and establish a theoretical foundation for targeted therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05203-w.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most prevalent cancer globally, known for its high aggressiveness, and accounts for 5.8% of all cancer cases and 8.8% of cancer-related fatalities worldwide [1]. Even with improvements in surgical methods and targeted treatments, the outlook and 5-year survival rates are still disappointing [2, 3], with metastasis being the primary cause of death, frequently occurring in the liver and peritoneum. Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) in tumorigenesis, progression, and metastasis. RBPs regulate RNA metabolism, stability, and translation, modulating key biological processes such as proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and metabolism [4]. Investigating RBP-mediated molecular mechanisms in CRC may reveal novel therapeutic targets to improve patient outcomes.

The RBFOX family, particularly RBFOX2 (RNA binding fox-1 homolog 2), is implicated in cancer pathogenesis [5]. This family consists of highly conserved RNA-binding proteins that regulate tissue-specific alternative splicing [6], including RBFOX2 (RNA Binding Protein Fox-1 Homolog 2). RBFOX2 contains a single high-affinity RNA recognition motif domain that binds to mRNA 3’ untranslated regions, enabling precise regulation of target gene expression and influencing critical biological processes such as epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), differentiation, survival, invasion and tumor suppression [7–9]. Altered RBFOX2 expression correlates strongly with cancer progression in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [10], myeloid leukemia [11] and nasopharyngeal carcinoma [12]. However, RBFOX2 function appears context-dependent, exhibiting varying effects across cancer types. Recent studies indicate that RBFOX2-mediated microexon splicing plays a key role in CRC metastasis [13], though its precise mechanisms in CRC are still not fully understood.

Far Upstream Element Binding Protein 1 (FUBP1), a single‑stranded DNA‑binding protein, plays a pivotal role in transcriptional regulation, protein synthesis, and RNA processing [14]. Its expression is frequently dysregulated in various cancers, suggesting its involvement in tumorigenesis. Functionally, FUBP1 exerts potent pro‑proliferative and anti‑apoptotic effects. For example, in oral squamous cell carcinoma, FUBP1 acts as an oncogene that promotes cell proliferation and is associated with poor prognosis [15]. In clear cell renal cell carcinoma, it facilitates tumor proliferation and progression [16], whereas in glioma, it accelerates glycolysis and promotes neuroblastoma cell growth [17]. Clinical studies demonstrate that FUBP1 is overexpressed in CRC tissues, and high expression levels are significantly correlated with shorter survival and poorer prognosis [18, 19]. Although FUBP1 functions as a potential oncogene in CRC development, its regulatory mechanisms and relationship with RBFOX2 require further investigation.

Ferroptosis is a type of cell death that does not involve apoptosis and depends on iron, caused by the buildup of iron inside cells and the production of lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS) [20]. The distinct metabolic profiles of cancer cells, particularly regarding ROS production and lipid/iron metabolism, may render them more susceptible to this cell death pathway [21]. Mitochondria, as central organelles for energy metabolism and redox balance, play crucial roles in ferroptosis. Mitochondrial dysfunction leads to ROS accumulation, triggering lipid peroxidation and cell death. Emerging evidence links ferroptosis to CRC biology. For example, RSL3 induces ROS accumulation and ferroptosis in CRC cells [22]. Xu et al. reported that ferroptosis specifically targets colorectal cancer stem cells (CCSCs) through SLC7A11 downregulation, potentially overcoming chemoresistance [23]. While RBFOX2 has been shown to promote endometrial cancer cell proliferation and ferroptosis resistance through splicing regulation [24], its role in inducing ferroptosis requires further exploration.

This study aims to investigate the function of RBFOX2 and its molecular mechanisms in CRC. The hypothesis proposes that RBFOX2 suppresses CRC proliferation and metastasis by regulating FUBP1 mRNA stability, thereby influencing mitochondrial function and ferroptosis. To test this hypothesis, a series of in vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted to examine RBFOX2-mediated regulation of FUBP1 mRNA stability, its effects on mitochondrial function and ferroptosis in CRC cells, and its impact on tumor proliferation and metastasis.

Materials and methods

Analysis of the human protein atlas (HPA) database

Protein expression patterns of RBFOX2 and FUBP1 in normal and CRC tissues were investigated using the HPA database. The HPA database provides immunohistochemistry (IHC) data from cancer patients, along with detailed antibody information for each IHC analysis. IHC staining results were categorized as strong, moderate, weak, or negative based on staining intensity and the percentage of stained cells. Additionally, the prognostic significance of RBFOX2 and FUBP1 expression in CRC patients was evaluated through the database.

Clinical sample collection

From January 2023 to January 2024, 42 paired CRC tissue samples and adjacent normal tissues were obtained from patients undergoing surgical resection without prior radiotherapy or chemotherapy at The First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) confirmed pathological diagnosis following colectomy, (2) complete clinicopathological records including gender, age, tumor location, diameter, differentiation status, and hepatic metastasis (Table 1). Immediately after resection, specimens were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University.

Cell culture

This investigation employed two distinct cell lines: normal human colonic epithelium (HCo) and SW480. The SW480 cell line was acquired from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank (China), whereas HCo cells were procured from BLUEFBIO Life Sciences (China). Both cell populations were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Clark Bioscience, USA) and maintained under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂, humidified atmosphere). For experimental procedures, cells at the third passage were selected. Routine quality control, including monthly mycoplasma screening using the MycoAlert detection system (Lonza, USA), was performed, with all cell lines consistently testing negative for contamination.

For experiments, SW480 cells were seeded in culture dishes containing complete medium and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, cells were trypsinized, centrifuged, and resuspended in fresh medium. The cells were then plated in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/well and treated with 1 µM Ferrostatin-1 (Ferr-1), a ferroptosis inhibitor, for 48 h.

Cell transfection

The overexpression plasmids (pcDNA3.1-RBFOX2, containing full-length human RBFOX2 cDNA cloned into pcDNA3.1 vector and pcDNA3.1-FUBP1) and negative control plasmid (pcDNA3.1) were obtained from GenePharma (China). Transient transfection was performed in SW480 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfection efficiency was evaluated by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) after 24 h, followed by subsequent experiments.

Cell proliferation assay

The metabolic activity of SW480 cells was evaluated as an indicator of proliferative capacity using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) colorimetric method. Cells were plated in 96-well culture plates at an initial density of 1 × 10⁴ viable cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h before experimental manipulations. Following transfection, cellular metabolic activity was measured at 24-hour intervals (24, 48, and 72 h) by adding 10 µL of MTT reagent (5 mg/mL; Vazyme, China) to each well, followed by a 4-hour incubation under standard culture conditions. After careful removal of the culture supernatants, the insoluble formazan product was solubilized with 100 µL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) per well with gentle mixing for 10 min. Optical density was measured at 570 nm using a full-spectrum microplate spectrophotometer, with values normalized to untreated control wells.

Colony formation assay

To evaluate the cellular proliferative potential, transfected SW480 cells were plated in 6-well culture plates at a low density of 500 cells per well. The cells were maintained in complete growth medium, consisting of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Following seeding, the plates were incubated for 14 days under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂, and a humidified environment) to allow for the development of discrete colonies. After the incubation period, the culture medium was gently aspirated, and the adherent cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The resulting colonies were fixed using a 75% ethanol solution for 10 min at room temperature and then stained with a 0.1% crystal violet solution for an additional 10 min. Colonies defined as containing more than 50 cells were considered viable clones and were manually counted. Documentation was performed using brightfield microscopy. The clonogenic efficiency was subsequently determined as the percentage of initially seeded cells that gave rise to a visible colony.

Transwell assay

The migratory and invasive capabilities of SW480 cells were assessed utilizing a Transwell chamber system. Polycarbonate membranes with 8-µm pores were employed to separate the upper and lower compartments. For the invasion assay, the membrane surface was pre-coated with a layer of Matrigel to simulate the extracellular matrix; conversely, membranes for the migration assay remained uncoated. Cell suspensions were prepared in serum-free medium, and SW480 cells were seeded into the upper chamber at densities of 5 × 10⁴ cells/well for migration analysis and 8 × 10⁴ cells/well for invasion analysis. The lower chamber was supplemented with 600 µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS, which served as a chemoattractant. Following a 24-hour incubation period under standard culture conditions, the non-migratory and non-invasive cells remaining on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed using a cotton swab. The cells that had traversed the membrane were then subjected to fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. After three washes with PBS, the immobilized cells were stained with a 0.1% crystal violet solution for 15 min at ambient temperature. Excess dye was removed by subsequent PBS washes, and the membranes were air-dried prior to imaging. To quantify cellular movement, images from five randomly selected fields per membrane were acquired using an optical microscope. The number of cells in these images was counted employing ImageJ software, providing a quantitative measure of migratory and invasive potential.

Wound healing assay

Genetically modified cells were plated in 24-well culture plates at a concentration of 5 × 10⁴ cells per well and allowed to proliferate until reaching 90% monolayer confluency. Mechanical wounding was performed by creating uniform linear scratches across the cell monolayer using sterile pipette tips, followed by extensive washing with phosphate-buffered saline (Beyotime) to eliminate dislodged cells. Migration progression was documented at baseline (0 h) and 48 h post-wounding using a brightfield microscopy (Olympus). Quantitative analysis of wound closure kinetics was performed using Image-Pro Plus image analysis software (v6.0; Media Cybernetics, USA), with migration rates calculated as percentage wound area reduction over time.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cell apoptosis was quantitatively analyzed through Annexin V-Fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC)/propidium iodide (PI) dual staining (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After 48 h of transfection, cells (1 × 10⁵) were harvested, washed twice with chilled PBS, and reconstituted in 500 µL of 1 × binding buffer. For fluorescence labeling, cellular suspensions were incubated with 5 µL each of FITC-Annexin V and PI solutions respectively for 30 min at room temperature in darkness. Cellular viability status was determined using a FACScan flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), which differentiated four distinct populations: viable, early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and necrotic cells based on differential staining patterns.

Electron microscopy

For ultrastructural examination, genetically modified cells underwent primary fixation in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution at 4 °C for a minimum of 4 h. Following thorough rinsing with 0.1 M PBS, secondary fixation was performed using 1% osmium tetroxide. Cellular specimens were then contrasted with 2% aqueous uranyl acetate for 30 min, followed by three 10-minute washed with distilled water. Complete dehydration was achieved using a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 90%, and absolute), followed by pure acetone treatment. Infiltration and embedding were accomplished using EPON 812 epoxy resin (Sigma-Aldrich), with polymerization conducted at 60 °C for 16 h. Ultrathin Sect. (70 nm thickness) were prepared and imaged at 120 kV acceleration voltage using a Talos L120C transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Oxidative stress analysis

Cellular ROS content was determined utilizing a commercial ROS detection kit (CA1410-100T; Solarbio, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were suspended in serum-deprived medium containing 10 µM 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (1:1000 dilution) were maintained at 37 °C for 45 min to allow probe uptake and oxidation. Following PBS washing procedures, fluorescent signal intensity corresponding to ROS levels was measured by flow cytometric analysis.

To evaluate oxidative damage markers, cellular homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The collected supernatants were analyzed for malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and reduced glutathione (GSH) content using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (eBioscience, USA). Following a 30-minute incubation with detection reagents at 37 °C, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer. Sample concentrations were determined by comparing optical density values against standard curves generated in parallel experiments.

Iron level detection

Intracellular ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) levels were measured using an Iron Assay Kit (ab83366; Abcam, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Treated cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, lysed in iron assay buffer, and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min. The iron reducer was added to both samples and standards, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. An iron probe was subsequently added and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 60 min. Absorbance was measured at 593 nm using a microplate reader.

Actinomycin D assay

For mRNA stability analysis, SW480 cells with RBFOX2 knockdown were cultured in 12-well plates and treated with 5 µg/mL actinomycin D or DMSO for the indicated time points. Cells were lysed in RNAiso Plus for RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Total cellular proteins were isolated using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride protease inhibitor (Beyotime), with clarified lysates subsequently incubated at 4 °C for 16 h with specific primary antibodies including rabbit anti-RBFOX2 (ab133621; Abcam), mouse anti-FUBP1 (67927-1-Ig; Proteintech, USA), or species-matched control IgG (30000-0-AP; Proteintech). Antigen-antibody complexes were then captured through incubation with 50 µL protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 h at 4 °C, followed by five washes with ice-cold lysis buffer. Bound proteins were ultimately eluted and analyzed via Western blot using horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG; ab6721; Abcam) or HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (ab9165; Abcam) for target protein detection.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Luciferase activity was quantified using the commercial Dual-Luciferase® detection system (E1910; Promega, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Following 48-hour co-transfection with gene-specific siRNA and reporter constructs, cellular extracts were prepared using 1 × passive lysis buffer. The lysates were washed twice with ice-cold PBS prior to centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove particulate matter. For luminescence measurement, 40 µL of Luciferase Assay Reagent II was combined with 10 µL of clarified supernatant and vortexed for 10 s. Immediately after, 40 µL of Stop & Glo® Reagent was added to sequentially quantify firefly and Renilla luciferase activities using a GloMax® 20/20 detection system (Promega).

RT-qPCR

RNA isolation from both tissue specimens and cultured cells was carried out using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with subsequent quantification performed through bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA; Invitrogen). cDNA was generated employing the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Japan). RT-qPCR analysis was executed in triplicate reactions utilizing SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Invitrogen) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). Gene expression levels were determined using the comparative threshold cycle (2−ΔΔCt) method, with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) serving as the endogenous reference. The specific primer sequences utilized in this study are provided in Table 1.

Western blot

Following PBS washing (twice, ice-cold), cellular and tissue specimens were lysed in RIPA buffer (FD008; Vazyme, China) for 20 min on ice. Protein concentrations were determined via BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein lysates were resolved by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, USA). Membrane blocking was performed for 2 h at room temperature with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST). Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C, including those targeting RBFOX2 (H00023543-M01; Thermo Fisher Scientific), FUBP1 (ab181111; Abcam), E-cadherin (20874-1-AP; Proteintech), N-cadherin (22018-1-AP; Proteintech), and GAPDH (60004-1-IG; Proteintech). After primary antibody incubation, membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37 °C: goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab172730; Abcam) or goat anti-mouse IgG (7076P2; Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Signal detection was conducted using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (E411-04; Vazyme), and images were captured with a FluorChem™ M system (ProteinSimple, USA).

Xenograft mouse model

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University. Twenty BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, 18–22 g; Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animals Co., Ltd.) were randomly divided into overexpression negative control (oe-NC) (n = 5) and an RBFOX2-overexpressing (oe-RBFOX2) (n = 5) groups. Following two washes with phosphate-buffered saline, stably transfected SW480 cells were resuspended in sterile physiological saline (1 × 10⁶ cells/mL) and 200 µL of cell suspension was administered via subcutaneous injection in the right flank region. Animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at 22 ± 3 °C with 50 ± 15% humidity and 12-hour light/dark cycles. Tumor dimensions were measured weekly using digital calipers, with volume calculated as: (short diameter)² × (long diameter) × 0.5. After 6 weeks, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation for tumor collection.

For metastasis studies, 3 × 10⁶ SW480 cells in 100 µL PBS were introduced through tail vein injection (n = 5/group). After 6 weeks, lungs and other potential metastatic organs were harvested. Tissues were fixed in 4% PFA for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, with remaining samples stored at -80 °C for molecular analysis.

IHC

Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% PFA, dehydrated through ethanol series, and paraffin-embedded. Section (4 μm) were deparaffinized in xylene (Beyotime) and rehydrated. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 0.3% H₂O₂ for 10 min at room temperature. After 1-hour blocking with 5% FBS/0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against RBFOX2 (ThermoFisher, H00023543-M01), FUBP1 (Abcam, ab181111), or Ki-67 proliferation marker (Abcam, ab15580). Following 1-hour incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam, ab6720) at room temperature, signals were developed using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine substrate (Vector Labs, USA) and counterstained with hematoxylin for 2 min. Five random high‑power fields per section were observed and imaged under a Leica microscope. Using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA), the percentage of positive cells and staining intensity in each field were quantified. The percentage of positive cells was scored as 4 (76–100%), 3 (51–75%), 2 (26–50%), or 1 (0–25%). Staining intensity was scored as 3 (strong), 2 (moderate), 1 (weak), or 0 (no signal). The final IHC score (range 0–12) was calculated as the product of these two scores.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences 21.0 (IBM, USA) and GraphPad Prism 5.0 (La Jolla, USA), with all experiments repeated at least three times. Data were presented as median with interquartile range for non-normally distributed variables or mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables, as appropriate. Comparisons between paired continuous variables were conducted using two-tailed Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test, while comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance or Kruskal-Wallis test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

RBFOX2 is downregulated in CRC and its low expression significantly correlates with tumor size and distant metastasis

The expression profile of the RBFOX2 protein in human tumors was assessed using the HPA database. Analysis of 11 CRC tissue samples from the HPA database revealed weak staining in six cases, with the remaining five showing no detectable expression (Fig. 1A). Representative IHC images showed intense RBFOX2 staining in normal colonic glandular epithelium, whereas expression was markedly reduced in CRC tissues and primarily localized to the cytoplasm and cell membrane (Fig. 1B). Further interrogation of the HPA database evaluated the relationship between RBFOX2 levels and patient survival. The analysis indicated a trend toward lower cancer‑specific survival in patients with low RBFOX2 expression, although this did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.12, Fig. 1C). In contrast, Kaplan‑Meier survival analysis of the 42 clinical specimens from this study demonstrated a significant association between low RBFOX2 expression and worse overall survival in CRC patients (P < 0.05, Fig. 1D). Clinicopathological correlation analysis further indicated that reduced RBFOX2 expression was significantly linked to larger tumor size (P = 0.03) and the presence of liver metastasis (P = 0.03), as detailed in Table 2, but not with other clinical parameters. To definitively characterize RBFOX2 expression, RT‑qPCR and Western blot analyses were conducted. These experiments confirmed that both mRNA and protein levels of RBFOX2 were significantly lower in the 42 CRC tissue samples compared with matched adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1E). This downregulation was consistently observed in mouse tumor tissues and cell lines (Figs. 1F, G), a finding corroborated by IHC staining (Fig. 1H). Collectively, these results indicate that RBFOX2 expression is substantially decreased in CRC.

RBFOX2 overexpression suppresses CRC cell proliferation and migration in vitro

Transfection with the pcDNA3.1‑RBFOX2 plasmid significantly increased RBFOX2 levels in SW480 cells, confirming effective transfection (Fig. 2A). The MTT assay showed that RBFOX2 upregulation markedly suppressed SW480 cell proliferation (Fig. 2B). This anti‑proliferative effect was further supported by a colony formation assay, in which RBFOX2 overexpression substantially reduced the clonogenic ability of the cells (Fig. 2C). Besides inhibiting growth, RBFOX2 overexpression significantly induced apoptosis in SW480 cells, as determined by flow cytometry (Fig. 2D). RBFOX2 also influenced cellular motility. Both migration and invasion capacities were significantly impaired upon RBFOX2 upregulation, as shown by Transwell assays (Fig. 2E). Consistent with this finding, wound healing assays confirmed a pronounced reduction in the migratory ability of RBFOX2‑overexpressing cells (Fig. 2F). Western blot analysis of EMT markers indicated that elevated RBFOX2 expression increased E‑cadherin and decreased N‑cadherin protein levels (Fig. 2G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that RBFOX2 is involved in regulating CRC cell proliferation, apoptosis, and migration.

RBFOX2 overexpression suppresses CRC proliferation and metastasis in vivo

To assess the in vivo effects of RBFOX2, a xenograft model was generated by implanting stably transfected SW480 cells into nude mice. Successful overexpression of RBFOX2 in the resulting tumors was confirmed at both mRNA and protein levels using RT‑qPCR and Western blot analyses (Fig. 3A). Compared with the control group, mice bearing RBFOX2‑overexpressing tumors showed a significant reduction in tumor volume progression and a significantly reduced final tumor weight (Fig. 3B, C). IHC evaluation also revealed a substantial decrease in Ki‑67‑positive cells within these tumors, indicative of suppressed proliferative activity (Fig. 3D). To investigate the effect of RBFOX2 on metastasis, an experimental metastasis model was established via tail vein injection of tumor cells. Histological examination using H&E staining showed that liver tissues from RBFOX2‑overexpressing mice contained fewer and smaller metastatic nodules, whereas control mice displayed numerous large metastatic lesions (Fig. 3E). These in vivo results demonstrate that RBFOX2 not only inhibits tumor growth but also attenuates metastatic potential in CRC.

RBFOX2 overexpression induces mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis

Electron microscopic examination revealed that RBFOX2 overexpression induced abnormal mitochondrial morphology, as evidenced by significantly reduced mitochondrial volume and fragmented cristae in SW480 cells compared to controls (Fig. 4A). Analysis of key ferroptosis markers further demonstrated that RBFOX2 upregulation led to a significant increase in cellular ROS levels, as measured by flow cytometry (Fig. 4B). This upregulation also resulted in elevated MDA content, decreased GSH levels (Fig. 4C), and markedly enhanced intracellular Fe²⁺ accumulation (Fig. 4D). These results collectively demonstrate that RBFOX2 overexpression triggers mitochondrial damage and activates the ferroptosis pathway in CRC cells.

FUBP1 is upregulated in CRC and correlates with tumor progression and metastasis

Analysis of the HPA database showed strong FUBP1 protein expression in all 10 CRC samples examined (Fig. 5A). Representative IHC images revealed weak FUBP1 staining in normal colonic glandular epithelium, whereas CRC tissues exhibited markedly increased expression, primarily localized to the nucleus (Fig. 5B). High FUBP1 expression was associated with significantly worse overall survival in patients (Fig. 5C). Clinicopathological correlation analysis demonstrated significant associations between elevated FUBP1 levels and increased tumor size, hepatic metastasis, and advanced clinical stage (Table 3). Consistent with this, Kaplan‑Meier survival analysis confirmed that high FUBP1 expression predicted poorer overall survival (Fig. 5D). Moreover, analysis of 42 matched CRC/normal tissues by RT‑qPCR and Western blot revealed elevated FUBP1 mRNA and protein levels in tumor specimens (Fig. 5E). Subsequent validation in murine models consistently showed upregulation of FUBP1 in tumor tissues and cell lines by RT‑qPCR (Fig. 5F), Western blot (Fig. 5G), and IHC (Fig. 5H), thereby confirming FUBP1 overexpression in CRC.

RBFOX2 interacts with FUBP1 and regulates its mRNA stability to modulate ferroptosis

Bioinformatic analysis using the ENCORI database predicted FUBP1 mRNA as a potential binding target of RBFOX2 (Fig. 6A). The physical interaction between RBFOX2 and FUBP1 was confirmed in both SW480 cells and CRC mouse models by Co‑IP followed by Western blot analysis, with reciprocal pulldown assays further validating the binding (Fig. 6B, C). In dual‑luciferase reporter assays, knockdown of RBFOX2 significantly enhanced luciferase activity driven by the wild‑type FUBP1 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR), an effect that was abolished upon mutation of the predicted RBFOX2‑binding sites within the FUBP1 3′UTR (Fig. 6D). mRNA stability assays using actinomycin D demonstrated that RBFOX2 overexpression reduced the half‑life of FUBP1 mRNA (Fig. 6E), consistent with RT‑qPCR results showing decreased FUBP1 expression following RBFOX2 upregulation (Fig. 6F). Notably, overexpression of FUBP1 in RBFOX2‑high cells counteracted the RBFOX2‑mediated elevation in ROS levels, an effect similarly reversed by the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferr‑1 (Fig. 6G). Both FUBP1 overexpression and Ferr‑1 treatment abrogated the changes in ferroptosis markers induced by RBFOX2, reversing the increases in MDA and Fe²⁺ and restoring GSH levels (Fig. 6H, I). Additionally, upregulation of RBFOX2 significantly suppressed the mRNA expression of the key ferroptosis‑related genes GPX4 and SLC7A11, effects that were markedly reversed by either FUBP1 overexpression or Ferr‑1 treatment (Fig. 6J).

FUBP1 overexpression reverses RBFOX2-mediated suppression of CRC cell proliferation and migration in vitro

Co-transfection with pcDNA3.1-RBFOX2 and pcDNA3.1-FUBP1 led to a significant upregulation of FUBP1 in SW480 cells (Fig. 7A). MTT and colony formation assays demonstrated that elevated FUBP1 levels antagonized the suppressive effects of RBFOX2 on cell proliferation (Fig. 7B) and colony-forming ability (Fig. 7C). Flow cytometry analysis showed that upregulation of FUBP1 mitigated the pro-apoptotic effect induced by RBFOX2 (Fig. 7D). Additionally, Transwell and wound healing assays revealed that FUBP1 overexpression restored the migratory and invasive capacities inhibited by RBFOX2 (Fig. 7E, F). Moreover, increased FUBP1 expression resulted in reduced E-cadherin and increased N-cadherin levels, thereby counteracting the modulation of EMT markers by RBFOX2 (Fig. 7G). Collectively, these results indicate that FUBP1 overexpression rescues the inhibitory effects of RBFOX2 on the proliferation, apoptosis, and motility of CRC cells in vitro.

Discussion

Metastasis and recurrent disease remain major obstacles to favorable outcomes in CRC patients. Elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms is essential for identifying novel therapeutic strategies. This investigation focused on the functional interaction between the RNA-binding proteins RBFOX2 and FUBP1. This study provides the first evidence that RBFOX2 binds to FUBP1, destabilizes its mRNA, and thereby induces mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis, ultimately suppressing CRC proliferation and metastasis. Furthermore, elevating FUBP1 expression effectively counteracts the tumor-suppressive effects of RBFOX2 overexpression on CRC progression, offering new insights into the pathogenesis of CRC.

Consistent with data from the HPA and findings from the present cohort of 42 clinical specimens, RBFOX2 expression was significantly lower in CRC tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues. Reduced RBFOX2 levels were significantly correlated with larger tumor size, liver metastasis, and shorter overall survival in patients, supporting its tumor-suppressive role in CRC. Notably, survival analysis based on the HPA database indicated a non-significant trend toward poorer cancer-specific survival with low RBFOX2 expression, whereas analysis of the clinical samples in this study revealed a statistically significant association with worse overall survival. This discrepancy may stem from methodological differences: the HPA analysis used cancer-specific survival (considering only deaths directly attributed to CRC), while the current study employed overall survival. Given that CRC patient mortality is frequently associated with metastatic complications (e.g., liver failure), overall survival may more accurately reflect the impact of RBFOX2 on disease progression. Moreover, the HPA database aggregates samples from diverse geographical regions and pathological subtypes, potentially introducing confounding factors. In contrast, the samples analyzed here were obtained from a single institution, providing more homogeneous clinical baseline characteristics, which may enhance the detection of RBFOX2’s prognostic significance. Although previous studies, in agreement with our results, have documented downregulation of RBFOX2 mRNA in CRC tissues [25], Danan-Gotthold et al. reported upregulation of RBFOX2 protein expression in CRC [26]. Recent research by Jbara et al. described metastasis-suppressive functions for RBFOX2 in pancreatic cancer and delineated associated alternative splicing programs [10]. Other reports have shown that RBFOX2 is significantly upregulated and exerts oncogenic effects in gastric cancer and oral squamous cell carcinoma [27, 28]. Collectively, these observations indicate that RBFOX2 is not a molecule with a single function but exhibits context-dependent expression and activity across cancer types. In CRC, RBFOX2 acts as a lowly expressed tumor suppressor, whereas it is highly expressed and promotes tumorigenesis in cancers such as gastric cancer and oral squamous cell carcinoma. These divergent roles likely arise from the distinct molecular landscapes, signaling networks, and tumor microenvironments characteristic of different malignancies. This concept is supported by the work of Liao et al. which suggested that RBFOX2 can function as either a tumor suppressor or an oncogene depending on the cellular context and its binding partners [29].

RBFOX2 has emerged as a particularly significant factor in oncogenesis, demonstrating multifaceted involvement in malignant progression through regulation of diverse pathological features such as neoplastic proliferation, metastatic dissemination, epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity, iron-dependent cell death, stemness preservation, and drug resistance mechanisms. IHC analyses derived from the Human Protein Atlas reveal a predominant cytoplasmic and membranous distribution pattern of RBFOX2. Ferroptosis represents a novel iron-dependent programmed cell death pathway driven by the accumulation of lipid peroxides and redox imbalance [30]. Mitochondria are now recognized as central regulators of this process. Cells undergoing ferroptosis display characteristic mitochondrial alterations, such as shrinkage, loss of cristae, and the accumulation of lipid peroxides and ROS within the organelle [31, 32]. The functional role of RBFOX2 was further validated through cellular and animal experiments. Overexpression of RBFOX2 in SW480 cells suppressed their proliferative, migratory, and invasive capacities while promoting apoptosis. In mouse models, RBFOX2 overexpression inhibited primary tumor growth and liver metastasis formation. At the mechanistic level, increased RBFOX2 led to mitochondrial abnormalities—including decreased volume and reduced cristae—along with elevated ROS, MDA, and Fe²⁺ levels, accompanied by diminished GSH. These changes collectively indicate the induction of mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis. Bioinformatic analysis using the ENCORI database, co‑immunoprecipitation, and dual‑luciferase reporter assays confirmed that RBFOX2 binds directly to the 3′UTR of FUBP1 mRNA via its RNA recognition motif (RRM), thereby destabilizing the transcript and reducing FUBP1 expression. Additional qRT‑PCR analysis revealed that FUBP1, in turn, directly upregulates the expression of the key ferroptosis‑related genes GPX4 and SLC7A11. Thus, the RBFOX2/FUBP1 axis represents a central mechanism through which RBFOX2 promotes ferroptosis, providing a new perspective on its tumor‑suppressive mechanism.

RBFOX2 Regulates EMT, an Essential Biological Process for Cancer Cell Invasion and Metastasis [33]. RBFOX2 modulates EMT, a critical process that enhances cancer cell invasion and metastatic dissemination. EMT confers a more migratory and invasive phenotype upon stationary epithelial cells by weakening intercellular adhesion. The transmembrane protein E‑cadherin is essential for maintaining epithelial polarity and stable cell‑cell contacts, thereby preserving the epithelial state [31, 34]. Its downregulation is strongly correlated with increased migratory and invasive capacities in tumor cells. Consistent with established literature, experimental evidence indicates that RBFOX2 suppresses EMT in CRC cells by upregulating E‑cadherin. Transwell and wound‑healing assays confirmed that RBFOX2 overexpression significantly inhibits the migration and invasion of CRC cells, suggesting that RBFOX2 may influence tumor cell behavior by modulating EMT‑related transcription factors or extracellular matrix proteins. EMT is initiated by extracellular signals within the tumor microenvironment, which activate key intrinsic signaling pathways in cancer cells. For instance, upregulation of the canonical WNT pathway is widely recognized to promote EMT and drive CRC progression [35].

The role of FUBP1 in various cancers has attracted increasing interest. As noted earlier, FUBP1 is highly expressed in CRC tissues and is associated with aggressive tumor phenotypes and poor prognosis. In this study, elevated FUBP1 expression was also observed in CRC cells. FUBP1 overexpression reversed the inhibitory effects of RBFOX2 on CRC cell proliferation and migration, as well as its modulation of ferroptosis‑related markers, further confirming FUBP1 as a key downstream effector of RBFOX2. Moreover, while RBFOX2 overexpression increased E‑cadherin and decreased N‑cadherin levels—thereby inhibiting EMT—FUBP1 overexpression counteracted these effects. This suggests that the RBFOX2‑FUBP1 axis can also influence CRC metastasis by regulating EMT. Additionally, studies by Jia et al. and Wang et al. propose that FUBP1 may exert its oncogenic functions through the c‑Myc pathway [18, 36]. This mechanism has not been further validated in the present study; future research should explore the downstream targets and signaling pathways of FUBP1 in CRC.

This study provides the first elucidation of the molecular mechanism by which RBFOX2 suppresses tumor growth through regulating FUBP1 mRNA stability. These findings not only establish a novel molecular basis for understanding the anticancer activity of RBFOX2 but also highlight its substantial translational potential. The distinct expression patterns of RBFOX2 in CRC and other cancers support its promise as a biomarker for diagnostic and prognostic applications. Specifically, reduced RBFOX2 levels in CRC tissues correlate significantly with larger tumor size, liver metastasis, and poorer overall survival, indicating its utility for objective risk stratification of patients. Such stratification could guide the development of personalized clinical management and therapeutic strategies. Given the association between aberrant RBFOX2 expression and unfavorable clinical outcomes in CRC, therapeutic approaches aimed at activating RBFOX2 or inhibiting FUBP1 represent promising novel strategies. This may offer a targeted treatment option, especially for patients resistant to conventional chemotherapy. Beyond the mechanism described here, RBFOX2 likely participates in other post‑transcriptional regulatory processes, such as modulating translation and RNA stability, which were not extensively investigated in the current study. Further exploration of these areas may reveal additional dimensions of RBFOX2’s role in cancer biology and could potentially open new therapeutic avenues.

This study is the first to demonstrate that RBFOX2 suppresses CRC proliferation and metastasis by regulating FUBP1 mRNA stability, thereby influencing mitochondrial function and ferroptosis. The study both elucidates fundamental mechanisms of RBP-driven tumorigenesis and uncovers a translational opportunity for CRC therapy development. Targeting the RBFOX2-FUBP1 axis may offer a new treatment strategy for CRC patients, particularly those resistant to conventional therapies. However, this study has certain limitations. First, the clinical sample size is relatively small (42 cases), which may affect the generalizability of the correlation analysis between RBFOX2/FUBP1 expression levels and clinicopathological characteristics (e.g., tumor size, liver metastasis) of CRC patients. Future studies should expand to multi-center, large-scale clinical cohorts to enhance the reliability and broader applicability of the conclusions. Second, although the interaction mechanism between RBFOX2 and FUBP1 has been preliminarily elucidated, their precise regulatory network and potential signaling pathways require further investigation. Additionally, future studies should focus on clinical trials exploring targeted therapies for CRC based on the functions of RBFOX2 and FUBP1.

In summary, this study provides an in-depth exploration of RBFOX2’s role in CRC and its underlying molecular mechanisms, offering new insights into CRC progression and laying a theoretical foundation for developing innovative treatment strategies. These findings may contribute to advancing precision and personalized therapies for CRC.
