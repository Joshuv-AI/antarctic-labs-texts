Horizontal transfer of chromosomal DNA mediated by an integrative and conjugative element generates frequent localized recombination in Novosphingobium aromaticivorans
Authors: Marco N. Allemann, Leah H. Hochanadel, Delyana P. Vasileva, Joshua K. Michener
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHorizontal gene transfer is an important evolutionary process by which DNA is exchanged between cells that are physically co-located but not direct evolutionary descendants. Horizontal transfer of highly divergent DNA is relatively easy to detect and can produce major phenotypic changes, exemplified by the acquisition of antibiotic resistance determinants. However, transfer of high-identity DNA, for example, between strains of the same species, is likely to be more frequent, harder to detect, and highly impactful in aggregate. In this work, we demonstrate that soil isolates of the alphaproteobacterium Novosphingobium aromaticivorans can exchange chromosomal DNA, leading to multiple unselected recombination events spanning approximately 10% of the chromosome. Chromosomal recombination was directional and more efficient near an integrative and conjugative element (ICE), and required a relaxase found in the ICE. Recombination could not be observed in strains from closely related Novosphingobium species. In combination, these results suggest that ICE-mediated recombination can efficiently recombine DNA within N. aromaticivorans, increasing the adaptive potential of the species while also enforcing species boundaries through preferential intraspecific recombination.IMPORTANCEHorizontal gene transfer is a key process in bacterial evolution. Mechanisms for transfer of mobile genetic elements are well-characterized, but less is known about how chromosomal DNA is recombined. In this work, we demonstrate that integrative and conjugative elements can efficiently recombine chromosomal DNA between strains of Novosphingobium aromaticivorans but not between different Novosphingobium species. We conclude that integrative and conjugative element-mediated chromosomal recombination can be an important adaptive mechanism within a species, due to its ability to recombine nearby chromosomal alleles, but also serves to delineate species-specific gene pools as a result of its limited phylogenetic range.

INTRODUCTION

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) is a major evolutionary mechanism in microbes (1). Many examples of HGT are generated by transfer of distinct mobile genetic elements (MGEs) such as plasmids, and these MGEs frequently carry genes encoding highly impactful genetic factors such as antibiotic resistance markers or pathogenicity determinants (2). Transfer of genomic DNA, particularly between closely-related strains, is also thought to occur frequently but is harder to detect through comparative genomics (3, 4). Intraspecific recombination is likely an important mechanism in bacterial speciation and the maintenance of species-specific gene pools (5–7).

Horizontal transfer of genetic material between bacteria has been demonstrated to occur via diverse mechanisms including conjugation, transduction, and transformation, and these mechanisms can transfer both chromosomal and extrachromosomal DNA (8, 9). Mobilization of chromosomal DNA by conjugation is exemplified by the F plasmid of Escherichia coli, which integrates into the chromosome and mobilizes adjacent DNA through a process known as “high-frequency recombination” or Hfr (10). Other examples of conjugative mobilization of genomic DNA include distributive conjugative transfer in Mycobacteria, wherein numerous, unlinked fragments of chromosomal DNA can be transferred in a single conjugative event (11). Additional bacterial genera such as Mycoplasma (12), Yersinia (13), Helicobacter (14), Haemophilus (15), and Vibrio (16) have also been shown to transfer genomic DNA between relatively closely related strains via species-specific processes. However, more general processes are likely responsible for the majority of intraspecific conjugative DNA exchange.

Integrative and conjugative elements (ICEs) are a group of MGEs that passively replicate while site-specifically integrated into the host chromosome but then excise, circularize, and transfer into neighboring cells by conjugation (17, 18). While conjugative plasmids have been well-studied, ICEs are thought to be at least as abundant in bacterial genomes (19). During conjugative transfer, ICEs primarily transfer themselves using site-specific recombinases. However, in a process similar to plasmid-mediated Hfr, ICEs can initiate conjugation prior to excision, leading to transfer of adjacent chromosomal DNA outside of the ICE (20). Given the broad abundance of ICEs in prokaryotes, their potential ability to transfer chromosomal DNA, and strain-level variation in acquisition (21), ICE-mediated Hfr has the potential to serve as a general mechanism for chromosomal exchange.

Relatively little is known about the pattern of DNA recombination within the recipient cell, whether mediated by an ICE or a chromosomally integrated plasmid. Hfr mating experiments were used to map the E. coli chromosome and demonstrated transfer of multiple loci and inferred genetic distances based on the likelihood of simultaneous transfer (22). However, chromosome-wide nucleotide-level analysis of the resulting transconjugants has only recently been described. Reported examples of chromosomal recombination through conjugative transfer between strains of E. coli (23) or Bacteroides fragilis (24) have described recombination primarily of a single contiguous segment of DNA. A more recent approach in E. coli using engineered Hfr measured variable rates of recombination, with progeny inheriting between 1 and 16 DNA fragments from the donor (25). In addition to its evolutionary role in the environment, the ability to recombine large segments of chromosomal DNA makes Hfr-mediated genetic exchange a useful tool for strain construction and analysis (26–28). Therefore, understanding the likely genomic consequences of Hfr-mediated recombination can guide the design and application of these approaches.

Many studies on bacterial HGT have been conducted with model strains such as E. coli or pathogens and other host-associated microbes. However, the rhizosphere can support dense microbial populations and therefore is an important site for HGT (29). Sphingomonads are gram-negative alphaproteobacteria found abundantly in the rhizosphere that have been extensively studied for their abilities to degrade a wide range of anthropogenic and naturally occurring aromatic compounds (30). Among these sphingomonads, Novosphingobium aromaticivorans F199 (hereafter “F199”) has recently emerged as a promising model organism for discovery of novel lignin degradation pathways and lignin valorization to bioproducts (31–38). The genome of F199 has been sequenced and consists of the main chromosome (3.3 Mb) and two large plasmids designated pNL1 (184 kb) and pNL2 (500 kb). The plasmid pNL1 was previously sequenced and was shown to contain regions related to plasmid replication, self-mediated conjugal transfer, and catabolism of aromatic compounds (39). F199 was isolated from a subsurface borehole at the U.S. Department of Energy Savannah River Site (40). Two other N. aromaticivorans strains were isolated contemporaneously from a separate borehole at the same site (41) but have not been characterized to the same degree as F199.

In this work, we characterized genomic recombination between strains of N. aromaticivorans. We demonstrated that recombination is directional, with one strain consistently contributing a minority of the progeny genome, and is mediated by an ICE present in the minor parent. Recombination was efficient between strains of N. aromaticivorans but was not detected in closely related Novosphingobium strains. Recombination was localized to approximately 10% of the genome proximal to the ICE integration site, but chimeric progeny often contained multiple independent recombination events within this recombined region. We also observed ICE-independent recombination between mutants of N. aromaticivorans F199 and demonstrated that these recombination events were not mediated by plasmid-dependent Hfr. In combination, these results demonstrate that ICE-mediated Hfr in N. aromaticivorans leads to efficient intraspecific recombination, which could contribute to intraspecific genetic diversification and interspecific genetic segregation.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Characterization of intraspecific recombination in N. aromaticivorans

We previously generated recombinant strains of N. aromaticivorans using a modified protoplast fusion protocol (42). In the protoplast fusion process, the outer membrane and peptidoglycan are removed through chemical and enzymatic treatment to generate cells surrounded by only a single membrane (43). Protoplasts can then be chemically fused, providing an opportunity for genome-wide recombination (42). Crossing F199 ΔhisB::kan with F199 ΔleuB::cat, followed by selection for kanR cmR progeny, readily yielded a F199 ΔhisB::kan ΔleuB::cat strain, as confirmed by genome resequencing. Crossing F199 ΔhisB::kan ΔleuB::cat with B0695, followed by selection for kanR leu+ progeny, yielded chimeric progeny strains. Genome resequencing demonstrated that these chimeric progeny inherited the majority of their genomes from F199, including the ΔhisB::kan marker, but recombined different segments of DNA from B0695, including the wild-type leuB allele (27). Unselected DNA that was introduced from B0695 was primarily observed near the leuB locus, in a region spanning approximately 400 kbp.

While optimizing the recombination process, we occasionally observed recombinant progeny in our control crosses, which used parental strains that had not been treated with lysozyme. While these progeny were generated at lower rates than those from lysozyme-treated parents, resequencing identified similar patterns of localized recombination near the selection marker. Since progeny from untreated parents could not be the result of protoplast fusion, we investigated other mechanisms that could yield large-scale genome recombination.

To further characterize the lysozyme-independent recombination mechanism, we repeated the same B0695 × F199 ΔhisB::kan ΔleuB::cat cross, but without lysozyme treatment of the parents (Table S1). We split the resulting cell mixture and selected for progeny with either kanR leu+ or cmR his+ phenotypes, since these would indicate recombination between the parental strains (Fig. S1). This approach typically generated at least 10-fold more kanR leu+ progeny than cmR his+ progeny, consistent with different rates of recombination at the hisB and leuB loci. Similarly, recombinant kanR leu+ progeny typically appeared within 3 days, while cmR his+ colonies took more than a week to appear. Inclusion of DNAse did not affect the formation of recombinant progeny, suggesting that recombination was not due to uptake of free extracellular DNA (i.e., by natural competence).

We next sequenced 12 progeny for each of the phenotypes, kanR leu+ or cmR his+. Eleven of the 12 kanR leu+ progeny were unique recombinants, with patterns of recombination similar to those observed previously. Only 5 of the 12 cmR his+ progeny were unique; the genomes of the remaining 7 were duplicates of 1 of these 5. Regardless of the selection, F199 contributed the majority of the genomic DNA, with smaller fragments recombined from B0695 (Fig. 1). The recombined fragments were located near the selection marker, either leuB or hisB.

To test whether these results were strain-specific, we generated additional parental strains, B0695 ΔleuB::cat and B0522 ΔhisB::kan. Crossing B0695 leuB::cat with B0522 hisB::kan yielded kanR cmR progeny but not his+ leu+ progeny. Resequencing the genomes of ten kanR cmR progeny demonstrated that B0522 contributed the majority of the genomic DNA, including ΔhisB::kan, with the DNA from B0695 located near the ΔleuB::cat marker (Fig. S2). Overall, results with B0522 were similar to those using F199, though quantitatively less efficient: B0695 contributed a minority of the progeny genomes, and the leuB locus was transferred more efficiently than hisB. We note that the ICE att site is located farther from the origin of replication in B0522 compared to F199, though it is unclear whether this difference affects conjugation.

To evaluate the potential phylogenetic breadth of this mechanism for chromosomal recombination, we constructed a B0695 ΔhisB::kan ΔleuB::cat mutant and crossed it with three closely related Novosphingobium species: Novosphingobium stygium DSM12445, Novosphingobium capsulatum DSM30196, and Novosphingobium subterraneum DSM12447 (79%–83% average nucleotide identity, Fig. S3). None of these crosses yielded kanR leu+ or cmR his+ progeny, with an estimated detection limit for the recombination frequency of 10−10.

Analysis of chromosomal recombination patterns

Individual homologous recombination events can be estimated based on genome resequencing. However, the precision of this approach depends on the frequency of variable nucleotide positions that differentiate the donor and recipient DNA. Consistent with our previous approach (42), we measured the minimum length for each recombined segment of DNA, from the first contiguous B0695-derived variant to the last; the full length of recombined DNA could be substantially longer (Fig. S4B). Additionally, we analyzed each genome as the result solely of recombination from B0695 into F199. Some of the patterns of recombination could be explained equally parsimoniously by sequential recombination of B0695 and F199 DNA, perhaps in a merodiploid intermediate (Fig. S4C). B0695 differs from F199 at 24,747 nucleotide positions, producing a marker roughly every 144 bp.

Progeny resulting from crossing B0695 × F199 ΔhisB::kan ΔleuB::cat contained 1–14 B0695-derived DNA fragments per strain (mean 5.07, median 5; Fig. 2A). The distribution of fragment lengths was bimodal, with one population of small (<10 bp) fragments representing ~18% of the total recombination events and a second population with a median length of 13.3 kbp representing the remaining 82% (Fig. 2B). We note that the population of short recombination events could be an artifact of the low frequency of nucleotide markers. However, the maximum possible length of these fragments, typically 300 bp, is still substantially lower than the average length of the longer population. The total fraction of the F199 genome replaced by B0695-derived DNA varied from 0.8% to 13.7% (mean 4.0%, median 2.2%; Fig. 2C). No significant differences were observed between his+ catR and leu+ kanR progeny. All observed recombination events were broadly homologous, replacing F199 alleles with B0695 alleles that varied at specific, rare nucleotides. However, some large recombined fragments contained internal examples of non-homologous recombination, where B0695-specific genes were introduced into the F199 chromosome. These examples of non-homologous recombination were likely mediated through RecA-dependent processes, since the flanking sequences were homologous.

Compared to previous reports using engineered Hfr in E. coli (25) or protoplast fusion in Bacillus subtilis (27, 42), the numbers of recombined fragments in N. aromaticivorans were similar to engineered Hfr, with a mean of approximately 5 fragments per strain, but both were lower than protoplast fusion, which generated a mean of approximately 20 fragments per strain. All three methods recombined a broad range of fragment sizes. Additionally, the percentage genome recombined from a single cross was similar, approximately 2%–3%. Compared to protoplast fusion, the other two methods generated higher local recombination rates across a smaller portion of the genome.

Elucidation of recombination mechanisms

We first sought to explain recombination between F199 ΔhisB::kan and F199 ΔleuB::cat, which we used to generate the F199 ΔhisB::kan ΔleuB::cat double mutant. All three N. aromaticivorans strains contain homologs of pNL1 (39), a self-mobilizable plasmid that can transfer to related sphingomonads (44). We hypothesized that transient chromosomal integration of this plasmid could also mediate Hfr conjugation (45). To test this hypothesis, we used a strain of F199, known as “F199C,” that we generated previously during serial propagation with coumaric acid as a sole growth substrate. Resequencing F199C demonstrated that it spontaneously lost pNL1, similar to our previous reports of large deletions in pNL1 during serial propagation with related lignin-derived aromatic compounds (33, 46). Other than the loss of pNL1, no other significant mutations were observed in F199C relative to F199. Therefore, we constructed F199C ΔhisB::kan and F199C ΔleuB::cat strains and compared the efficiency of recombination between otherwise equivalent strains, F199C ΔhisB::kan × F199C ΔleuB::cat versus F199 ΔhisB::kan × F199 ΔleuB::cat. Both crosses yielded progeny at equivalent rates (Fig. 3), and analysis of the F199C-derived progeny confirmed both the presence of both resistance markers as well as the lack of pNL1. Therefore, we concluded that the mechanism for recombination between F199 mutants is not pNL1-mediated Hfr. However, we were unable to identify a plausible alternative mechanism for generation of these recombinant progeny. Based on computational analyses, no additional genes in the genome of F199 or in pNL2 have been identified as encoding proteins for conjugal transfer (47).

Chromosomal recombination is not mediated by the conjugative plasmid pNL1. Wild-type F199 ΔhisB::kan was crossed with F199 ΔleuB::cat, both of which contain pNL1, and compared to F199C ΔhisB::kan crossed with F199C ΔleuB::cat, both of which lack pNL1. Both crosses generated recombinant kanR cmR progeny at similar rates. Since donor and recipient cannot be distinguished, recombination efficiency is calculated as the ratio of transconjugants to initial cells.

Therefore, we next examined potential mechanisms for directional recombination from B0695 into F199 or B0522. Comparing the genomes of B0695, B0522, and F199 identified a putative integrative and conjugative element (ICE) present in B0695 but absent from the other two strains (47). This ICE, hereafter “ICENa0695A,” is integrated into a leucine transfer RNA gene located approximately 400 kbp away from the leuB locus (Fig. 1; Fig. S5). Similar to previous reports in V. cholerae (20) and B. fragilis (24), we hypothesized that ICENa0695A could be responsible for efficiently mobilizing adjacent chromosomal DNA from B0695 into B0522 or F199. This hypothesis was consistent with the observed patterns of genomic recombination, since recombination of B0695 DNA was primarily observed in the genomic region between ICENa0695A and the leuB locus. Notably, the ICE was not transferred to the recombinant progeny. It is unclear whether the distal hisB locus could be transferred through a similar ICE-dependent mechanism, but the physical separation on the chromosome could account for lower recombination efficiency. Increased rates of recombination after lysozyme treatment, as initially observed, could result from physiological stress triggering ICE-mediated conjugation.

To test this hypothesis, we constructed a strain of B0695 in which the putative relaxase in ICENa0695A was replaced with the cat marker. We then crossed F199 ΔhisB::kan ΔleuB::cat with either wild-type B0695 or B0695 ΔtraI::cat and selected for kanR leu+ progeny. The cross with B0695 ΔtraI::cat yielded no kanR leu+ progeny, while the cross using wild-type B0695 efficiently produced kanR leu+ progeny (Fig. 4). Based on these results, we concluded that recombination of DNA adjacent to the leuB locus from B0695 into F199 was mediated by ICENa0695A through a mechanism similar to high-frequency recombination (Hfr). Comparing the recombination efficiency of F199 crossed either with F199 (Fig. 3) or B0695 (Fig. 4), the ICE-dependent mechanism was approximately 40-fold more efficient.

Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrated that an ICE present in N. aromaticivorans B0695 could efficiently transfer and recombine chromosomal DNA into two other N. aromaticivorans strains but not into more distantly related Novosphingobium species. Further studies comparing bona fide ICE transfer with ICE-mediated Hfr would illuminate factors that limit recombination breadth. Multiple fragments of DNA could be integrated into a single recipient, spanning a broad range of lengths. Enhanced intraspecific recombination is a key process in the adaptation and maintenance of bacterial species, and our results highlight ICE-mediated HGT as a plausible mechanism for intraspecific recombination of diverse DNA sequences. This process could also be engineered to construct recombinant bacterial strains for purposes such as genetic mapping or biotechnology.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

Novosphingobium strains were routinely grown at 30°C in R2A medium. Minimal defined media for Novosphingobium strains was medium 457 (DSMZ) with 0.2% glucose as a sole carbon source. E. coli strains were grown in Luria-Bertani medium. For solid media, agar was included at 15 g/L. The antibiotics kanamycin (50 µg/mL), streptomycin (100 µg/mL), and chloramphenicol (15 µg/mL) were used as required. The amino acid supplements histidine (100 µM) and leucine (300 µM) were added to defined media as needed. When necessary, diaminopimelic acid (DAP) was used at 60 µg/mL.

Mutagenesis in Novosphingobium aromaticivorans

Mutants of N. aromaticivorans strains (Table 1) were generated by allele exchange mutagenesis using the plasmid pAK405 as described previously (31, 48). Resistance cassettes for kanamycin or chloramphenicol were cloned between homologous regions (500–700 bp) upstream and downstream of the gene to be replaced. The sequence for the kanamycin cassette was derived from the pAK405 backbone (48). The chloramphenicol resistance cassette was designed by placing the chloramphenicol resistance gene from pRL271 (49) under the control of the artificial sphingomonad psyn2 promoter described previously (48). All pAK405 knockout constructs were synthesized by Genscript or assembled using HiFi Assembly (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) following manufacturer recommendations.

Strains and plasmids

Derivatives of pAK405 were mobilized into N. aromaticivorans via biparental conjugation using E. coli WM6026, a DAP auxotroph, as described previously (31). Selection for transconjugants was performed on R2A + kanamycin or R2A + chloramphenicol agar at 30°C. Selections were performed in the absence of DAP to counterselect against the WM6026 donor strain. Exconjugants were clonally purified by restreaking to the same selection media. Exconjugants were grown overnight in R2A broth without antibiotics and plated to R2A with streptomycin supplemented with 2.5 g/L NaCl to counterselect against the integrated plasmid. Selections in R2A without additional NaCl often produced colonies that were both streptomycin- and kanamycin-resistant, suggesting that pAK405 had not recombined out of the chromosome. Streptomycin-resistant colonies were screened by patching for appropriate amino acid auxotrophies on 457 media with 2 g/L glucose. Gene replacements were further verified with colony PCR and whole genome resequencing.

Novosphingobium conjugations

Novosphingobium strains were grown overnight in R2A broth at 30°C with the appropriate antibiotic selection(s). The next day, cells were pelleted and washed with R2A broth to remove residual antibiotics, and equivalent cell numbers, as calculated by OD600, of washed cells from each strain were mixed together, pelleted, and concentrated into a minimal volume which was then spotted onto the surface of a dry R2A agar plate. Once the conjugation mixture dried, it was incubated at 30°C for 16–24 h. After incubation, cells were scraped off the surface of the agar plate and resuspended in R2A media lacking carbon. For conjugations that required double antibiotic selection, resuspended cells were plated to R2A containing the appropriate combination of antibiotics. For conjugations that required auxotrophic counterselection against one of the strains, the resuspended cells were washed a minimum of three times, by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 1 min at room temperature and resuspension in carbon source-free 457 media, before plating to selective media to remove nutrient carryover.

DNA extraction and genomic sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated from Novosphingobium strains using a Promega Wizard Genomic DNA kit following the manufacturer’s guidelines for gram-negative bacteria with minor modifications; a second centrifugation step was included after the protein precipitation to improve DNA quality.

Genomes of N. aromaticivorans B0522 and B0695 were sequenced de novo at the University of Maryland Institute for Genome Sciences. Genomic DNA was sequenced using a PacBio Sequel at 500× to 800× coverage and assembled de novo using HGAP. The genome of F199 had been published previously.

Genomic DNA from recombinant progeny was prepared for Illumina library construction, and libraries were sequenced on the MiSeq Illumina platform to generate paired-end 300-bp reads using V3 chemistry. Reads were mapped to the recipient reference genome (F199 or B0522) using Geneious. Variant nucleotides were identified based on a frequency threshold of 0.8 and compared to the corresponding donor sequence to confirm that the observed nucleotide variant was also present in the donor. The fragment length was calculated based on the minimum distance necessary to recombine a single contiguous stretch of variant nucleotides (Fig. S4) (27).
