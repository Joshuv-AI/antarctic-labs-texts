The regulatory mechanisms of CAM/CML gene family in governing mycorrhizal fungi in Cremastra appendiculata (Orchidaceae)
Authors: Qiong Wang, Xuewei Zhao, Xi Yuan, Zhong-Jian Liu, Yu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCalmodulin (CAMs) and calmodulin-like proteins (CMLs) play pivotal roles in sensing and decoding Ca2+ signals, thereby regulating of various physiological processes. Although CAM/CML genes in numerous plants have been extensively studied, their specific functions and mechanisms in orchids remain unexplored. In order to reveal the characteristics of the CAM/CML family in Cremastra appendiculata, a comprehensive analysis was performed at the genome-wide level, focusing on its physicochemical attributes, phylogenetic associations, gene structure, Cis-acting elements, and qRT-PCR.ResultsA total of 78 CAM/CML genes were identified in C. appendiculata, including five CAPPCAM and 73 CAPPCML, divided into ten subgroups (Subgroup I-X). Subgroups I and II contain one to four coding DNA sequences (CDS), while subgroup IX includes 9 genes with only one CDS and the remaining genes possess 4–12 CDS. The collinearity analysis revealed seven collinear CAM/CML gene pairs between C. appendiculata and Arabidopsis thaliana, indicating the homology of CAM/CML genes between these two species. The cis-elements in the CAPPCAMs/CMLs promoter mainly enrich methyl jasmonate (MeJA) elements (202/1120, 18%). The qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the CAPPCAM/CAPPCML gene family plays important roles in responding to mycorrhizal symbiosis. Notably, root tissues demonstrated a marked induction effect upon symbiosis, with gene expression regulation exhibiting distinct tissue specificity.ConclusionsThis study not only lays the foundation for clarifying the functions of such genes in mycorrhizal symbiosis of C. appendiculata, but also highlights its potential for biotechnology development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07889-6.Keywords: CAM/CML gene family, Cremastra appendiculata, Mycorrhizal fungi

Introduction

In plant cells, calcium is not only an important component of the cell wall, but also plays a central role in cell signal transduction [1]. When plants feel changes in the external environment, such as light, temperature, water, salt concentration, etc., these stimuli will trigger ion channels or transporters on the cell membrane, resulting in a rapid increase in the concentration of free calcium ions in the cell, thus activating the “calcium signal” [2]. Hence, Calciumions (Ca²+) as second messenger is particularly important in enabling plants to respond adeptly to a myriad of environmental stresses and challenges [3]. Calcium signals require calcium-binding proteins (sensors) with EF-hand motifs to detect and transmit the signal. These proteins undergo structural changes upon calcium binding, converting the signal into biochemical responses [4]. Calmodulin (CAMs) and calmodulin-like proteins (CMLs) represent one of the earliest identified and most extensively characterized calcium sensor families. These proteins bind Ca²+ via EF-hand motifs to modulate target protein activity, thereby mediating diverse physiological processes in plants, including growth, development, and stress adaptation [5]. Structurally, CAM is a highly conserved 149-amino acid protein containing four EF-hand motifs (two pairs), each capable of binding a single Ca²+ [6]. In contrast, CMLs are plant-specific calcium sensors that typically possess 1–6 EF-hand motifs [7]. As pivotal calcium sensors in plants, CAM and CML are frequently co-investigated due to their structural and functional homology. Both proteins regulate target activity through calcium-induced conformational shifts, governing developmental processes and stress adaptation [8]. Evolutionary conservation of their EF-hand domains suggests divergence from a common ancestor, followed by functional specialization [7]. Integrated investigation of their mechanisms elucidates molecular strategies underlying plants’ coordination of environmental adaptation with developmental homeostasis [9].

With the completion of whole genome sequencing of plants, CAM/CML has been found in many plant cell types. Analysis of a large number of plants showed that the number of CML genes was much greater than that of CAM, for example, there are 7 CAMs and 50 CMLs in the A. thaliana [7], 6 CAMs and 52 CMLs in the Solanum lycopersicum [8, 10], 45 CAMs and 32 CMLs in rice [11], and 7 CAMs and 19 CMLs in Nelumbo nucifera [12]. In A. thaliana, studies have demonstrated that AtCAM7 directly binds to promoters of light-responsive genes to promote photomorphogenesis [13], AtCAM2 and AtCML25 regulate pollen germination and pollen tube elongation [14, 15], AtCAM3 contributes to heat stress adaptation [16], AtCML4 and AtCML5 are membrane proteins that play a crucial role in vesicle trafficking within the plant endomembrane system [17], ATCML9 is involved in regulating plant tolerance to salt stress [18], and AtCAM4 negatively regulates cold tolerance in plants by interacting with a novel CAM-binding protein PATL1 [19]. CML24 regulates root mechanoresponses, pollen tube growth, and flowering [20–23]. CML12, CML38, CML39, and CML43 modulate developmental processes [24–26]. CML8, CML41, CML42, CML46, and CML47 enhance resistance to bacterial pathogens [27–30]. CML20 acts as a negative regulator, while CML24 and CML37 positively regulate ABA signaling during drought stress [23, 31]. In Glycine soja, GsCML27 enhances bicarbonate stress tolerance but suppresses salt/osmotic stress responses during early growth [32]. In Oryza sativa, OsCAM1-1 mediates thermotolerance by activating downstream heat-responsive genes [33]. OsCML16 governs root architecture and drought adaptation [34]. In Triticum aestivum, TaCML20 overexpression improves water-soluble carbohydrate accumulation and grain yield [35]. In Medicago truncatula, MtCML40 negatively regulates salt tolerance via MtHKT-dependent Na+ accumulation [36]. MtCML42 enhances cold tolerance through MtCBF1/4 activation and coordinates flowering time via MtABI5 repression and MtFTa1 induction [37].To sum up, CAM and CML genes show rich and diverse functional roles in plant growth and development.

Orchidaceae is one of the world’s largest extant angiosperms [38], consisting of more than 750 genera and 31,000 species [39], with diverse flower morphology, complex mycorrhizal relationships, and unique pollination mechanisms [40–42]. As a typical representative of mycorrhizal plants, orchids have a symbiotic relationship with fungi throughout their entire life cycle, from seed germination to flowering and fruit. This close symbiotic relationship not only reflects the interdependence and co-evolution of organisms, but also serves as an exemplary system for dissecting molecular mechanisms underlying plant-fungus interactions. The colonization process of orchid mycorrhizal fungi in orchid plants is a mutual process in which fungi continuously infect plants and plants and fungi respond to each other [43]. In this process, many kinds of genes and gene families may participate in the biological regulation of the symbiosis between orchids and mycorrhizal fungi. Although the functional role of CAM/CML in most plants has been elucidated, there are relatively few studies on CAM/CML in orchid mycorrhizal fungi. In order to further investigate the characteristics of CAM/CML genes in the evolution of orchid mycorrhizal fungi, In this study, genome-wide identification, comparison and expression of CAM/CML genes in C. appendiculata were studied.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

This study divided a same batch of C. appendiculata seeds into two cohorts. The mycorrhizal symbiosis treatment group was established by sowing seeds under natural conditions within Taikuanhe National Nature Reserve in Shanxi Province, with seedlings harvested following one year of cultivation. The axenic treatment group was established by sowing seeds in sterile culture chamber at the Beijing National Botanical Garden. The growth conditions were 12 h photoperiod (25 ± 1℃) and 85% relative humidity, with seedlings collected after one year of cultivation. Root, stem, and leaf samples were collected from both groups with three biological replicates per group per tissue type, frozen with liquid nitrogen and stored in a refrigerator at − 80℃.

Identification and physicochemical properties of the CAM/CML

Genomic data of C. appendiculata can be downloaded from the China National GeneBank DataBase (CNGBdb) (https://db.cngb.org/, accessed on 5 July 2024). From the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org, accessed on 9 July 2024) downloaded A. thaliana CAM/CML gene family protein sequences and as a query sequence. The Blast tool of TBtools-II v2.119 [44] and a specific Hidden Markov Model (HMM),— i.e. EF-hand 7.hmm (PF 13499)— were used to search and identify potential CAM/CML genes in the genome of the C. appendiculata. Setting the e-value to 1 × 10− 5. The genes obtained were analyzed with the NCBI Batch CDD (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, accessed on 10 August 2024) to screen and retain the genes with complete CAM/CML domains. The Protein Paramter Calc tool of TBtools-II v2.119 [44] was used for physicochemical property analysis. By calculating the protein sequence of candidate genes, Results for amino acid composition (AA), isoelectric point (pI), molecular weight (MW), grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), instability index (II) and aliphatic index (AI) can be obtained. Using online tool WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hjc.jp, accessed on 30 August 2024) to predict the subcellular localization.

Phylogenetic analysis

The protein sequences of CAM/CML of A. thaliana and CAM/CML of C. appendiculata were input into MEGA 11.0 for ClustalW sequence alignment. To construct a neighbor-joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree with a bootstrap value of 1000 and other parameters default. The online tool iTOL (https://itol.embl.de, accessed on 16 August 2024) was used for visualization.

Protein conservative domain and gene structure analysis

Using the online software MEME (https://meme-suite.org, accessed on 12 August 2024) to analyze the conserved motifs of CAM/CML genes, and the prediction number was set to ten. The MAST file was downloaded for gene structure visualization. TBtools-II v2.119 [44] was used to integrate phylogenetic trees, conserved protein motifs, and general comparative maps of gene structures.

Collinearity analysis and location analysis on chromosome

Using One Step MCScanX-SuperFast in TBtools-II v2.119 [44] to determine the collinearity relationships among C. appendiculata and A. thaliana and to identify the collinear blocks of CAM/CML genes in C. appendiculata genome files. The GFF file of the C. appendiculata genome was imported into TBtools-II v2.119 [44] to generate chromosomal location maps for each CAM/CML gene.

Cis-acting regulatory elements analysis

Upstream sequences (2000 bp) of CAM/CML genes were extracted from TBtools-II v2.119 [44] based on the GFF file of the C. appendiculata genome and analyzed on the PlantCARE website (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/, accessed on 18 August 2024). Manually exclude the number of promoter elements less than ten and retain the remaining elements. TBtools-II v2.119 [44] was used for visualization.

Expression analysis

The expression of C. appendiculata in three different tissues—root, stem, and leaf—was analyzed during sterile and mycorrhizal developmental stages. The expression level of each gene was transformed into Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM). Finally, a heatmap was constructed using TBtools-II v2.119 [44] to visualize the expression of CAM/CML genes in different tissues.

qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the roots, stems, and leaves of C. appendiculata using the RaPure Total RNA Plus Kit. Subsequently, the first strand DNA was synthesized using HiScript II 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit(+ gDNA wiper) for quantitative PCR (qPCR; Vazyme, Nanjing, China). To ensure the removal of genomic DNA, qRT-PCR was performed using the ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Primers were designed using Primer Premier 5 software for candidate genes and reference genes for qRT-PCR using Primer Premier 5 and their specificity was confirmed by primer blast on the NCBI website. In this study, the translation elongation factor 1 beta (ef-1β) gene of C. appendiculata was used as a reference gene. The relative expression of the target gene was determined using the 2−∆∆CT method. All qRT-PCR analysis were performed in three technical replicates.

Results

Identification and physicochemical properties

By employing the CAM/CML protein sequences of A. thaliana as references, 78 CAM/CML genes were systematically identified in C. appendiculata through integrated BLAST and Hidden Markov Model (HMM) screening. Among these, five were classified as CAM genes (CAPP17183, CAPP04810, CAPP16294, CAPP08729, CAPP08730), while the remaining 73 were annotated as CML genes. By predicting and analyzing their physical and chemical properties. The results reveal that the amino acid (aa) ranges from 95 residues (CAPP15798) to 602 residues (CAPP15334) (Table 1). Isoelectric points (pI) spanned from 3.88 (CAPP06431) to 9.45 (CAPP15798), with 72 proteins exhibiting acidic properties (pI < 7) and six displaying basic traits (pI > 7). Molecular weight (MW) ranges from 10935.23 kDa (CAPP09937) to 66566.8 kDa (CAPP15334). More than half of the 78 CAM/CML proteins had member instability index (II) greater than 40. In addition, the grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) of 78 CAM/CML proteins is predicted to be negative, indicating that they are all hydrophilic proteins.

According to the constructed phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1), a total of 135 CAM/CML genes of A. thaliana and C. appendiculata were divided into ten subgroups, represented by I-X. The had the highest number were 48 in subgroup IX, followed by 14 in subgroups II, VII and X (Fig. 1), while subgroup II had the least members (3). As shown in Fig. 1, all 7 AtCAM (i.e., AT1G6644.1, AT2G27030.3, AT2G41110.1, AT3G43810.1, AT3G56800.1, AT5G21274.1, and AT5G37780.1) and five CAPPCAM (i.e., CAPP17183, C APP04810, CAPP16294, CAPP08729, CAPP08730) were clustered into subgroup I.

Protein Conservative domain and gene structure analysis

To comprehensively characterize the genetic structural characteristics of the CAPPCAM/CML gene family, conserved motifs and exon-intron structures were analyzed using the online software MEME (Fig. 2). The results show that the distribution of the conserved domains of proteins with the same branches is similar. Most of the subgroups have different degree of motif deletion, and subgroup VI contains only motif 1 and motif 2, but no other motifs. At the same time, most members contain motif 1, motif 2, motif 4, and motif 5, which are the most conserved regions of CAM/CML proteins (Fig. 2). Motifs 9-motifs 6-motifs 7-motifs 3-motifs 8-motifs 10 are unique to subgroup IX. In addition, subgroups I and II were contain 1–4 CDS, while subgroup IX has 9 genes containing only one CDS and the remaining genes each contain 4–12 CDS. Most of the remaining subgroups contain only one CDS.

Collinearity and location analysis on chromosome

CAM/CML genes are unevenly distributed across chromosomes (Fig. 3). Among these Chr2, Chr11, and Chr12 had the highest numbers. The remaining chromosomes are distributed from one to five. Collinearity analysis was performed using the One Step MCScanX-Super Fast tool in TBtools-II v2.119 [44]. As illustrated in Fig. 4, seven CAM/CML gene pairs with collinearity relationships were found in the interspecific relationship between C. appendiculata and A. thaliana, indicating homology between the CAM/CML genes of C. appendiculata and A. thaliana (Fig. 4B). In addition, seven pairs of segmental duplication events were found in the interspecific relationship of C. appendiculata (Fig. 4A). Combined with the location of genes on chromosomes, three pairs of tandemly duplicated genes were found (Fig. 3), one pair resides chromosome 3 (CAPP08729 and CAPP08730) and two pairs clustered on chromosome 20 (CAPP01292 and CAPP01293, CAPP15798 and CAPP15799).

Tissue expression patterns of CAPPCAMs/CMLs

To detect the expression patterns of CAPPCAM/CAPPCML genes, we calculated the FPKM values of 78 CAPPCAM/CAPPCML transcripts in three different tissues (root, stem, and leaf) under both sterile and mycorrhizal conditions. A heatmap was generated using the FPKM values to visualize the expression patterns of CAPPCAM/CAPPCML transcripts (Fig. 6). The results showed that with the exception of CAPP01293 and CAPP01292, the vast majority of genes were detected in at least one tissue. Further analysis revealed that multiple CAM/CML genes were up-regulated under mycorrhizal symbiosis conditions, particularly significantly in root tissues, such as CAPP13006, CAPP05926, and CAPP10731, suggesting that mycorrhizal symbiosis may promote the establishment and maintenance of symbiotic relationships by activating calcium signaling pathways. Additionally, some genes like CAPP07173 also showed up-regulation in stem and leaf tissues, although generally to a lesser extent than in roots, indicating that the symbiotic process may systematically regulate calcium signaling-related genes. In contrast, a small number of genes (e.g., CAPP02909) were significantly down-regulated or maintained low expression levels under symbiotic conditions, suggesting they may be non-essential or specifically suppressed during the symbiotic process.

qRT-PCR analysis

To investigate the expression patterns of CAM/CML genes during mycorrhizal symbiosis in C. appendiculata, we selected 9 genes for qRT-PCR analysis. As shown in Fig. 7, these genes exhibited strong and tissue-specific responses to mycorrhizal symbiosis. In root tissues, mycorrhizal symbiosis showed a significant induction effect, with most genes demonstrating relatively low expression levels in non-mycorrhizal roots but significant upregulation in mycorrhizal roots (such as CAPP13006, CAPP05926, and CAPP10731). This suggests they may play crucial roles in early signal recognition during root-fungus interaction or in the formation of symbiotic structures at later stages. In contrast, in leaf tissues, genes such as CAPP15125 and CAPP13934 showed significantly higher expression in non-mycorrhizal leaves than in mycorrhizal leaves, indicating that their functions in leaves might be unrelated to symbiosis or even negatively regulated during the systemic establishment of symbiosis. The expression patterns in stem tissues appeared complex, intermediate between roots and leaves. Some genes, like CAPP13006, were induced in mycorrhizal stems, reflecting the systemic transmission of symbiotic signals, while others, such as CAPP05926 and CAPP10731, showed higher expression in non-mycorrhizal stems, similar to the pattern observed in leaf tissues.

Discussion

As a pivotal second messenger, Ca²+orchestrates plant stress adaptation through transient cytoplasmic concentration shifts [45, 46], which are decoded by conserved calcium sensors like calmodulin (CAM) and calmodulin-like proteins (CMLs). These sensors, characterized by Ca²⁺-binding EF-hand motifs, amplify signaling cascades to activate stress-responsive physiology [47, 48]. The CAM/CML gene families have been extensively characterized across diverse plant species. For instance, eight PeCAM genes and 51 PeCML genes were present in Populus euphratica [49]. Four MdCAM genes and 58 MdCML genes were revealed in Malus × domestica [50]. In the genomes of Lycopersicon esculentum Miller, Nicotiana tabacum L. and Solanum tuberosum L. were identified six, seven and four CAM genes [8]. However, research on these genes remains largely unexplored in Orchidaceae, one of the largest angiosperm families [51]. In this study, we report the first identification of five CAM and 73 CML genes in C. appendiculata. The CML repertoire significantly exceeds those in model plants (A. thaliana, Oryza sativa) [52], surpasses Lycoris radiata (52) and Vitis vinifera (62) [10, 53], but remains smaller than Brassica oleracea var. capitata (79) [54], indicating a lineage-specific expansion of CMLs potentially driven by gene duplication events or genome size variations [9]. Physicochemical property analysis (Table 1) revealed that the molecular weights (MW) of 78 CAM/CML proteins ranged from 10,935.23 to 66,566.8 kDa. Over half of these members exhibited instability index (II) greater than 40, yet al.l are hydrophilic proteins (GRAVY < 0). The isoelectric points (pI) spanned from 3.88 to 9.45, with acidic proteins constituting a significant majority (72/78, 92%). CAPPCMLs displayed longer amino acid sequences (95–602 aa) compared to Hordeum vulgare (80–551 aa) [55], while Cucumis sativus (81–251 aa) and Medicago sativa (65–266 aa) CMLs were comparatively shorter [56, 57]. The marked variation in CAM/CML gene family protein lengths across species suggests strong evolutionary adaptation to functional demands, potentially regulating biological activity through impacts on protein structural stability. Investigating these length differences provides critical insights into the evolutionary pressures and functional diversification of CAM/CML proteins among plant species.

Phylogenetic analysis provides an evolutionary perspective for interpreting the biological significance of gene families by revealing the evolutionary trajectories, functional differentiation patterns and adaptive variations of genes. Therefore, in this article the 78 CAM/CML members were distributed across 10 subgroups in the constructed phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1). The clustering patterns of 57 AtCAM/AtCML proteins showed congruence with previous studies using independent phylogenetic constructions based on A. thaliana [6]. It is worth noting that five CAPPCAMs and seven ATCAMs cluster together into a subgroup I (Fig. 1), indicating that they have a high degree of sequence identity. This finding aligns with Pang et al., bioinformatic study on the CAM gene family in Bruguiera gymnorrhiza, which demonstrated that proteins within the same phylogenetic subgroup exhibit conserved exon-intron architectures across multiple species [58]. This study further validated this pattern through MEME motif analysis. The results demonstrated that proteins within the same phylogenetic branch exhibited similar distributions of conserved domains (Fig. 2). Most members contained motifs 1, 2, 4, and 5, which constitute the most conserved structural regions in CAM/CML proteins. The unique 9-6-7-3-8–10 tandem motifs in subgroup IX suggest the emergence of novel functional modules. Regarding gene structure, subgroups I and II typically contained one to four coding sequences (CDS), while genes in other subgroups predominantly possessed a single CDS.In addition to CDS quantity, we found that the vast majority of CML genes outside subgroup IX lack introns, consistent with findings from studies on AtCML, OsCML, and BrCML [6, 11, 53]. The significant elongation of introns in subgroup IX may originate from positive selection favoring long introns during evolution—longer introns enhance natural selection efficiency by increasing recombination frequency between exons [59], indicating that introns may influence the evolution and functional diversification of the CAM/CML gene family. Integrated analysis of the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1) and gene structure diagrams (Fig. 2) revealed that these genes exhibit conservation across species while undergoing repeated duplication and divergence events, forming diversified functional modules. This diversity may enable C. appendiculata to adapt to variable environmental conditions, enhancing its survival and reproductive capacity.

Analysis of evolutionary drivers for gene family expansion indicates that tandem and segmental duplications serve as pivotal molecular mechanisms in plant adaptive evolution, critically promoting the emergence of novel traits and the development of genetic diversity [60–63]. Through synteny analysis and chromosomal localization of the C. appendiculata CAM/CML gene family, this study identified seven cross-species syntenic gene pairs between C. appendiculata and A. thaliana (Fig. 4B), demonstrating significant evolutionary homology. Chromosomal mapping further revealed three tandem duplication gene clusters (Fig. 3) and seven segmental duplication pairs (Fig. 4A). Cis-acting elements are located within the gene promoter region and play a key role in managing transcription and gene expression and responding to abiotic stresses [64, 65]. The concurrent analysis of Cis-acting elements revealed that the promoter regions of 78 CAPPCAM/CML genes were rich in stress response elements. Methyl jasmonate (MeJA)-responsive elements (18%) and light-responsive elements (11%) predominated, followed by abscisic acid (ABA)-associated elements (9%), aligning with the mechanism by which plants enhance their environmental resistance through hormone signals [66, 67]. Furthermore, developmental regulatory elements such as circadian control and endosperm expression were identified, confirming the gene family’s coordinated involvement in both growth regulation and abiotic stress responses through multidimensional regulatory networks.

Orchid plants under natural conditions must establish a stable symbiotic relationship with a specific fungus and obtain nutrients from it in order to grow successfully. These specific fungi are defined as orchid mycorrhizal fungi [40]. Therefore, investigating the expression patterns of CAM/CML gene families during symbiosis establishment is critical for elucidating the functional significance of these calcium signaling components in orchid-fungal mutualism. Previous studies have shown that calmodulin (CAM) and calmodulin-like (CML) genes are widely involved in the regulation of various plant tissues during growth and development. For example, in Mangifera indica, the concentration of Ca2+ in the floral organ at the stage of petal, stamen and pistille differentiation is significantly higher than in other tissues, accompanied by a simultaneous upregulation of CAM/CML content [70]. By comparing the dwarfing mutant of A. thaliana with wild-type plants, Galon et al., found differences in the expression levels of AtCAM and AtCML, suggesting that these genes play an important role in the regulation of stem development [68]. In addition, Yang et al.. further demonstrated that CAM/CML expression showed a dynamic down-regulation trend during the development of seed roots [69]. This study, through analyzing transcriptome data from different tissues under sterile and mycorrhizal conditions, revealed that CAPPCAM/CML genes exhibit significant tissue-specific expression (Fig. 6). Genes significantly upregulated during mycorrhizal symbiosis (such as CAPP13006, CAPP05926, and CAPP10731) may serve as key regulators in this process. Further qRT-PCR validation of 9 differentially expressed genes yielded results consistent with the transcriptome data (Fig. 7), collectively confirming the important role of the CAM/CML gene family in responding to mycorrhizal symbiosis in C. appendiculata. A core set of CAPPCAM/CAPPCML genes (e.g., CAPP13006 and CAPP10731) were strongly and specifically induced in mycorrhizal roots, strongly suggesting their involvement in calcium signal transduction during key steps such as root-fungus recognition and symbiotic interface establishment. C. appendiculata appears to regulate a specific set of calmodulin signaling components to establish a molecular environment conducive to symbiosis in the roots, while potentially using systemic signaling to adjust the physiological state of aerial parts, thereby successfully establishing and maintaining a mutualistic symbiotic relationship with mycorrhizal fungi.

Conclusion

The Ca2+ signaling system mediates plant growth and development through calmodulin (CAM) and calmodulin-like (CML) proteins, with evolutionary expansion and functional diversification of the CAM/CML gene family playing central roles in species adaptation. In this study, through methods such as phylogenetic analysis, gene structure, promoter prediction, chromosome localization and gene expression analysis, five CAM and 73 CML genes were identified for the first time in C. appendiculata. The number of CML genes was significantly higher than that of most species, suggesting that this family may have undergone specific expansion during evolution. Physicochemical analysis revealed that all 78 CAM/CML proteins are hydrophilic, with molecular weights spanning 10.9–66.6 kDa, and over half exhibiting high instability, indicative of dynamic regulatory roles. Phylogenetic analysis divided these genes into ten subgroups. CAPPCAMs in subgroup I showed high homology with AtCAMs, while subgroup IX likely evolved novel functional modules via unique motif combinations (9-6-7-3-8–10) and elongated introns. The gene expression profiles revealed that 9 candidate genes were highly expressed during mycorrhizal symbiosis, with root tissues serving as the primary site for calcium signaling pathway responses. These findings suggest that CAM/CML-mediated calcium signaling may regulate the interaction between C. appendiculata and orchid mycorrhizal fungi. This discovery provides crucial molecular evidence for elucidating the regulatory mechanism of calcium signaling in orchid mycorrhizal symbiosis.
