Tuberculous discitis following traumatic intravesical Bacillus Calmette-Guérin treatment: illustrative case
Authors: Ishan R. Perera, Sydney E. Moriarty, Jaret S. Ly, Heather Heitkotter, Daniel Carr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND Tuberculous spondylitis/discitis is a rare presentation of mycobacterial infection, typically secondary to Mycobacterium tuberculosis; however, patients receiving intravesical Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) are at risk of developing tuberculous spondylitis secondary to Mycobacterium bovis.OBSERVATIONSThis article reports a rare case of tuberculous spondylitis/discitis with a recent history of traumatic intravesical BCG therapy for superficial bladder cancer, which required surgical decompression of an abscess in the setting of intractable back pain.LESSONSThis case highlights the importance of a focused, yet relevant understanding of a patient’s history in the development of one’s differential diagnosis and overall surgical management. This patient, with a recent history of traumatic BCG instillation, presented at a short interval for intractable back pain and underwent surgery for discitis and epidural abscess without immediate speciation. Importantly, patients with likely infectious spondylitis/discitis, without obvious bacterial speciation, who have any history of intravesical BCG treatment should be considered for tuberculous etiology. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26705

Tuberculous spondylitis was described in 1779 by Sir Percivall Pott,1,2 with an incidence of 1 case per 2 million persons in the United States in 2011.3 The bacterial etiology of tuberculous spondylitis is the genus Mycobacterium, including nontubercular M. avium, M. bovis, M. microti, and M. africanum.1 These bacteria are characterized by prolonged periods of dormancy with reemergence in favorable conditions.1 Spinal involvement is typically secondary to hematogenous spread via paradiscal vessels causing paradiscal disease.1

Patients receiving Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG), attenuated M. bovis for intravesical treatment of superficial bladder cancer, rarely develop tuberculous spondylitis let alone discitis, a known complication with less than 5% incidence, with delayed onset from 14 days to 12 years after therapy.4,5 Importantly, superficial or non–muscle-­invasive bladder cancer accounts for 70% of new bladder cancer diagnoses, fueling the increasing utilization of intravesical BCG therapy.6 Risk factors include epithelial injury within the bladder or urethra, bladder tumor resection, pelvic radiation therapy, cystitis, biopsy, or prostate resection.5 Epithelial damage has been noted as a potential risk factor for hematogenous spread via Batson’s plexus, contributing to the systemic complications reported in the literature.7 Interestingly, the delay in complication presentation is likely linked to the ability of M. bovis bacilli to remain dormant. Some studies demonstrate positive urine cultures for years following initial BCG treatment.5

Illustrative Case

We report the case of a 77-year-old male patient with a known history of both high-grade and low-grade noninvasive urothelial carcinoma of the bladder who underwent multiple intravesical BCG treatments after his diagnosis in January of 2023, with treatment complicated by recurrence of low-grade cancer and a severe urethral tear during BCG instillation in 2024. Importantly, between 2023 and 2025 he was instilled 12 times with BCG; his final and traumatic dose was in August of 2024. Notably, other than cancer he was not immunocompromised, had not traveled to an endemic country, and had participated in at-risk behaviors. He initially presented to an outside clinic in February of 2025 for back pain since December of 2024 and underwent MRI of the lumbar spine, which appeared to demonstrate a disc herniation with severe stenosis (Fig. 1). Conservative therapies, including oral medication, injections, and physical therapy, were attempted but failed. He presented to our clinic in August of 2025 with only severe progressive back pain for 8 months without an obvious precipitating event. On examination, he did not appear toxic, appeared to be in pain, and maintained 5/5 strength throughout, normal sensation and 2+ reflexes, but had tenderness to palpation of the low back. Considering his symptoms were out of proportion to his imaging study and the failure of conservative therapies, he was scheduled for laminectomy and discectomy, with a repeat MRI study to be performed. MRI demonstrated L3–4 discitis and epidural abscess, which had progressed from his previous MRI examination (Fig. 2). Given the severity of his pain and debility, L3–4 minimally invasive laminectomy and discectomy was scheduled. On presentation, his laboratories were notable for a white blood cell count 9.21 K/μL, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) level of 30 mm/hr, and C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 36.6 mg/L. Intraoperatively, a left-sided tubular approach allowed for decompression, phlegmon washout, and removal of disc material. Epidural purulence was encountered during a right-sided approach. Successful decompression was appreciated with a nerve hook, and all samples were sent for pathological analysis. Immediately postoperatively, he was started on empiric antibiotics: vancomycin, cefepime, and metronidazole—notably lacking antimycobacterial coverage. Four days later, intraoperative pathology demonstrated disc inflammation consistent with an epidural abscess; however, periodic acid-schiff and acid-fast bacilli stains were negative. Additionally, bacterial, fungal, nontuberculous, and tuberculous polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tests were negative, and interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs) were not completed. He was discharged on oral linezolid with sulfamethoxazole and trimethoprim for empiric coverage of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, until he could be scheduled for outpatient intravenous antibiotic therapy, which he had initially refused due to insurance difficulties and high out-of-pocket cost. On postoperative day 23, we were notified that an acid-fast bacilli culture was positive for M. bovis. The patient is currently receiving a 9-month outpatient regimen of isoniazid with pyridoxine and rifampin. On follow-up, he has reported severe gastric reflux, which is worse when supine; otherwise, he reports resolution of pain. Radiographic imaging of the spine was negative for obvious bony destruction or significant malalignment (Fig. 3). Discussion

Observations

This case demonstrates the rare development of tuberculous discitis potentially related to a recent history of traumatic intravesical instillation during BCG treatment for superficial epithelial bladder cancer. The infectious complications of intravesical BCG range from local tuberculosis of the bladder with strictures secondary to the inflammatory process8 to disseminated miliary tuberculosis.9,10 These infectious complications are rare, with a 0.4%–1.5% prevalence reported;10,11 however, tuberculous spondylitis comprises an even smaller cohort of these patients, with fewer than 50 cases reported in the literature.12,13

Patients typically present with back pain and radiculopathy, without red flag complaints.5,7,12 Preoperative imaging may demonstrate findings concerning for discitis, epidural fluid collections, and occasionally kyphotic deformities.1,5,7,12 Typically, tuberculous spondylitis/discitis is not high on the differential when a majority of findings predominantly involve the disc; thus, diagnosis is delayed by final pathology results, as was experienced in our case. Early suspicion may prompt PCR testing, which typically demonstrates undifferentiated Mycobacteriaceae,7 while QuantiFERON-TB Gold/IGRA results should be negative, as BCG strains lack the RD1-encoded ESAT-6 and CFP-10 antigens detected by these assays.14,15 This combination of positive PCR with negative IGRA supports M. bovis as the causative organism.15

Medical treatment centers on isoniazid and rifampin with or without ethambutol, but should avoid pyrazinamide due to the inherent resistance of M. bovis.5,11 Importantly, no randomized controlled trials have been published on the recommendations for treatment of complications secondary to intravesical BCG;11 additionally, studies have failed to demonstrate benefit of prophylactic isoniazid prior to intravesical BCG for the prevention of spondylitis.5 Similarly, treatment duration has been debated, ranging from 9 to 12 months.7,12 Surgical treatment requires, at minimum, decompression and evacuation of the epidural involvement, as was performed in our case; however, cases with instability, especially with notable kyphosis, should include stabilization.5,12 Recent literature has favored posterior stabilization over anterior-alone or combined approaches.16,17 In this case, minimally invasive decompression was selected considering the focal area of infection and the overall smaller surgical corridor. Generally, it has been recommended to withhold BCG instillation for 2 weeks in the setting of recent urinary trauma.10,11

The literature agrees that Batson’s plexus is the leading facilitator of hematogenous spread. This is further supported by the 20% concomitant finding of infectious aneurysms.15,16,18 However, there is a paucity of discussion regarding the method by which these bacilli cross the epithelial barrier into the bloodstream. A single-institution review reported that 80% (4/5) of patients who developed BCG-related infections had urogenital epithelial trauma associated with intravesical BCG instillation.11 The presence of this risk factor was not specifically reported in any of the other case reports reviewed.9,10

Lessons

Infectious complications of intravesical BCG, although rare, can result in significant morbidity, with spondylitis/discitis typically requiring surgical intervention for diagnosis and months of antibiotics with significant medication-related side effects, including hepatotoxicity, renal toxicity, vision changes, and neuropathy.19 It is important to gather an in-depth infectious history on patients presenting with imaging findings consistent with osteomyelitis, osteodiscitis, or epidural abscess. This history should include any etiologies for immunocompromise, including cancer and its treatment. Importantly, patients with any history of intravesical BCG treatment, especially with a history of traumatic instillation and without obvious speciation in their perioperative phase, should receive PCR testing for suspected tuberculous spondylitis.
