A case of Bundibugyo virus disease treated with monoclonal antibodies and remdesivir
Authors: Alexander Uhrig, Frieder Pfäfflin, Pinkus Tober-Lau, Victor M Corman, Miriam S Stegemann, Paulina Tarnow, Monika Rottstegge, Katrin Moira Heim, Felix Bremer, Thomas Cronen, Tilman Lingscheid, Han Ngoc Le, Dominik Geus, Mary J Choi, William A Fischer, Wesley R Campbell, Aneesh K Mehta, Marylyn M Addo, Placide Mbala-Kingebeni, David Hillus, Christian Gaebler, Stephan Becker, Janine Michel, Andreas Nitsche, Andreas Kurth, Martin Witzenrath, Till D Best, Stephan Günther, Lisa Oestereich, Manuel Koch, Matthias Zehner, Henning Gruell, Christian Drosten, Florian Klein, Charité Ebola Task Force, Florian Kurth, Leif Erik Sander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinical data on Bundibugyo virus (BDBV) infection are limited, and no approved virus-specific treatment exists. Here we describe the case of a previously healthy 39-year-old healthcare worker who acquired BDBV infection while working in Ituri Province, Democratic Republic of the Congo, and was medically evacuated to Germany. He received MBP134, an investigational combination of two monoclonal antibodies, under a US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Emergency Investigational New Drug (eIND) authorization, together with remdesivir and supportive care. Viral RNA concentrations were highest in oropharyngeal swabs and plasma and became undetectable in blood, throat swabs, urine and stool by day 13 after symptom onset and in semen by day 25. The patient’s serum demonstrated neutralizing activity against the current BDBV outbreak strain and other orthoebolaviruses. We detected BDBV-induced antibody responses not attributable to MBP134 or prior vaccination, providing evidence of endogenous humoral immunity after combined antiviral treatment. The patient recovered and was discharged on day 22 after symptom onset. This case report provides detailed insights into the clinical course, virologic dynamics and immune responses in BDBV infection during combined antiviral treatment; larger clinical studies are needed to assess the efficacy and clinical utility of these therapeutic approaches.Subject terms: Viral infection, Outcomes research, Biological therapy

Main

On 15 May 2026, the Ministry of Public Health, Hygiene and Social Welfare of the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) declared an outbreak of Ebola virus disease (EVD) in Ituri Province1. The outbreak was declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern by the World Health Organization (WHO) on 17 May 2026 (refs. 2,3). As of 12 August 2026, 4,449 cases and 2,069 deaths have been confirmed4. Unlike previous large EVD outbreaks, the current outbreak is caused by BDBV, an Orthoebolavirus species associated with two prior outbreaks, one in Uganda in 2007 and one in the DRC in 2012, totaling 206 confirmed cases5. BDBV differs genetically and antigenically from Zaire ebolavirus (EBOV), and no vaccine or therapy is approved for BDBV infection. MBP134, an investigational combination of two broadly neutralizing monoclonal antibodies, has demonstrated activity against multiple Orthoebolavirus species, including BDBV, in preclinical studies6 and has completed a phase 1 clinical trial7. Clinical treatment data for Bundibugyo virus disease (BVD) are scarce. Here we report the clinical course, virologic findings and management of a US healthcare worker who acquired BDBV infection during the ongoing outbreak and was medically evacuated to the high-level isolation unit (HLIU) in Berlin, Germany.

An otherwise healthy 39-year-old male healthcare worker based in Ituri Province, DRC, developed severe myalgia and general malaise. In the setting of rapidly progressive symptoms and emerging reports of an EVD outbreak in the region, he entered voluntary self-isolation that day, after close contact with his family for approximately 6 hours. A detailed exposure history is provided in the Methods. The next day, he developed fever (38.9 °C) despite self-medication with acetaminophen (Fig. 1a,b). Despite antimalarial chemoprophylaxis and a negative malaria rapid diagnostic test, empirical antimalarial treatment was initiated. A quantitative polymerse chain reaction (qPCR) targeting multiple pathogenic Orthoebolavirus species, including BDBV (RadiOne Pan-Ebola Kit) on EDTA plasma, returned positive on day 2 of illness (Fig. 1c). The patient had received one dose of rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine 41 months prior (Ervebo; approved single-dose regimen for immunization against EBOV).

By day 3 of illness, the patient had developed severe abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, confusion and orthostatic symptoms requiring intravenous fluid resuscitation. On day 4, medical evacuation to Germany was arranged via Bunia (DRC) and Entebbe (Uganda). By arrival in Bunia, his condition had deteriorated markedly, with fever to 39.8 °C, persistent vomiting, hypotension and signs of encephalopathy. Given the absence of an approved treatment for BDBV infection and its high case−fatality rate, emergency investigational use of MBP134 was pursued based on evidence of efficacy against BDBV in non-human primates and available human safety data. After FDA eIND authorization, MBP134 (50 mg kg−1) was administered on day 4 during a layover and crew change, followed by off-label administration of a 200-mg loading dose of remdesivir. Treatment was well tolerated despite persistent vomiting. During transport, he received intravenous fluids, ondansetron, promethazine and acetaminophen (Extended Data Fig. 1).

On arrival in Berlin on day 5, the patient reported profound asthenia but was able to walk a short distance with assistance. Myalgia and abdominal pain had improved, and he was fully oriented (Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) 15). Despite repeated antipyretic treatment, he was febrile (38.4 °C) and tachycardic (110 beats per minute) but normotensive (110/70 mmHg). He was tachypneic (28−30 breaths per minute), with an oxygen saturation (SpO2) of 93% on ambient air. Lung ultrasound showed increased bilateral B lines, and chest radiography showed early bilateral basal pulmonary venous congestion. Echocardiography and abdominal ultrasound were unremarkable. There were no signs of bleeding. Laboratory testing revealed thrombocytopenia (73 × 109 per liter), lymphopenia (0.6 × 109 per liter) and elevated aminotransferase levels (alanine aminotransferase (ALT): 243 U l−1, aspartate aminotransferase (AST): 575 U l−1), amylase (102 U l−1), creatine kinase (511 U l−1) and C-reactive protein (CRP) (13.6 mg dl−1). Coagulation studies were within normal range. Tests for malaria and dengue were negative (full laboratory results in Fig. 1c,d and Supplementary Table 1).

During the first 24 hours after admission (day 5 of illness), the patient remained febrile and asthenic, with orthostatic symptoms, increasing confusion, recurrent vomiting and intermittent diarrhea. He developed a fine macular pruritic rash predominantly involving the trunk. Tachypnea improved over the first 2 days. Remdesivir was continued at 100 mg daily, alongside antipyretics, ondansetron and low-volume intravenous fluids (1 l per day) until day 6, when he tolerated adequate oral fluid intake. From day 7, the patient improved markedly and remained afebrile without antipyretic treatment.

Although platelet and lymphocyte counts rapidly normalized and CRP decreased steadily, transaminase levels continued to increase until day 8, peaking at 635 U l−1 and 773 U l−1, respectively; bilirubin remained normal. To mitigate potential drug-induced liver injury, remdesivir was withheld for 1 day, after which transaminases declined (Fig. 1a,c and Supplementary Table 1). No serious adverse events attributable to MBP134 or remdesivir were observed (Supplementary Table 2).

Viral RNA concentrations were highest in oropharyngeal swabs obtained on day 5, reaching approximately 9 × 107 copies per milliliter and exceeding plasma viral RNA concentrations on the same day (7 × 106 copies per milliliter), before declining steadily (Fig. 1b). Consistent with increasing viral replication early in the disease course, the initial plasma sample obtained in the DRC on day 2 after symptom onset contained approximately 106 copies per milliliter. Viral RNA was transiently detected in urine on days 5−7, and stool showed only low-level positivity on day 9. By day 13, viral RNA was below the detection limit in plasma, oropharyngeal swabs, stool and urine. Semen contained approximately 2.5 × 103 copies per milliliter measured on day 20 but was negative for BDBV RNA on day 25. Attempts to culture virus from oropharyngeal swabs and plasma samples on days 5 and 6, as well as semen on day 20, were unsuccessful in three independent specialized laboratories (Fig. 1b).

Identical BDBV genome sequences were obtained from oropharyngeal swab and plasma samples obtained on day 5. Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic analysis showed that the patientʼs virus clustered within the species Orthoebolavirus bundibugyoense and was clearly distinct from other Orthoebolavirus species (Extended Data Fig. 3). Within the BDBV clade, the sequence clustered with genomes from the 2026 outbreak8 but separately from sequences from the 2007 and 2012 outbreaks, consistent with a new putative zoonotic introduction of the 2026 lineage into humans.

Nodes with bootstrap support below 79% (1,000 replicates) were collapsed into polytomies. Genome coverage was 100% for the oropharyngeal swab and 94.3% for the plasma sample. Mean sequencing depths were 41.1 and 7.6, respectively. The sequence showed 99.96% nucleotide identity to the 2026 outbreak sequence PP_006XCJJ.1, compared with 98.75–98.77% and 98.82–98.85% identity to sequences from the 2012 and 2007 outbreaks, respectively. At the amino acid level, all determined viral proteins were identical to those of PP_006XCJJ.1 and showed >98% identity to the corresponding proteins of representative sequences from the 2007 and 2012 outbreaks (PP_006X70S.1 and PP_006X62P.1, respectively).

After sequential BDBV RNA-negative samples, the patient was transferred from the HLIU to the infectious diseases ward under barrier nursing precautions on day 17 and was discharged on day 22 with persistent asthenia but otherwise in good clinical condition. At follow-up on day 25, mild asthenia persisted, laboratory values had further improved and PCR was negative for BDBV RNA in blood, oropharyngeal swabs, urine and semen.

High serum anti-BDBV glycoprotein (GP) IgG levels were detected from day 5 (1 day after MBP134 infusion) (Fig. 2a,b), with a transient increase until day 8 (Fig. 2b). Baseline GP-specific IgG could not be assessed because no serum was available from before MBP134 infusion. Anti-BDBV nucleoprotein IgM was detected by immunofluorescence (Extended Data Fig. 4). In addition, BDBV-GP-specific IgM and IgA were detected in a dilution-dependent manner, whereas controls 24 months after rVSV-ZEBOV vaccination spiked with MBP134, and MBP134 in buffer showed no binding above background (Fig. 2c). Because MBP134 binds membrane-bound trimeric GP but not secreted glycoprotein (sGP), anti-EBOV sGP IgG was measured to distinguish endogenous Orthoebolavirus-reactive IgG from administered monoclonal antibodies. Anti-EBOV sGP IgG increased between days 5 and 6 and remained elevated, whereas rVSV-ZEBOV-vaccinated controls showed only low levels 24 months after vaccination, and MBP134 showed no sGP binding (Fig. 2d).

Neutralizing activity was detected in all purified serum IgG from day 5 onward against lentiviral pseudoviruses expressing GP from EBOV, a 2007 BDBV isolate or a BDBV 2026 sequence amplified from a patient sample (Fig. 2e), whereas unexposed and unvaccinated controls showed no detectable neutralizing activity (Fig. 2e). Neutralization of authentic EBOV, Sudan virus and BDBV isolates was confirmed by focus reduction neutralization testing (Fig. 2f).

This case provides detailed clinical and virological observations from a patient with BVD. Viral RNA concentrations across body compartments differed from previous reports in patients with EVD, most notably the comparatively high concentrations in oropharyngeal swabs.

As no approved virus-specific treatment for BVD exists, MBP134 was administered after eIND authorization during air transport to Berlin6,9. Its clinical benefit remains unknown. However, marked clinical improvement occurred within 3 days. Despite gastrointestinal symptoms and early signs of capillary leak, aggressive intravenous fluid replacement was not required, and no further evidence of third spacing occurred.

MBP134 has demonstrated neutralizing activity against historic BDBV isolates. We found that this activity was retained against the GP of the current outbreak strain in a pseudovirus neutralization test. The inability to recover replication-competent virus despite high viral RNA concentrations may have reflected neutralization of infectious virions by MBP134, although this cannot be established without pretreatment culture data.

The patient had received a dose of rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine, which contains the antigenically distinct EBOV GP, 41 months before infection. Antibody responses detected after MBP134 administration could, therefore, derive from prior vaccination, BDBV infection or MBP134. Endogenous antibody production may be reduced in patients with EVD receiving monoclonal antibody therapy10. We, therefore, measured antibodies against sGP, which is not recognized by either antibody in MBP134, to distinguish host from therapeutic antibodies. The increase in anti-sGP IgG, together with anti-nucleoprotein IgM and anti-BDBV-GP IgA and IgM, supports preserved endogenous humoral immunity to BDBV after MBP134 treatment. The relative contributions of endogenous immune responses and antiviral therapy to the clinical course cannot be determined.

As recently demonstrated for SARS-CoV-2, selection of escape variants under monoclonal antibody pressure may lead to loss of efficacy11. Although orthoebolaviruses show substantially less antigenic diversification, treatment-emergent escape remains possible. Here, we could not assess potential treatment-emergent variants because viral RNA concentrations rapidly declined to levels precluding reliable viral genome sequencing.

In addition to experimental antibody therapy, our patient received remdesivir, a nucleotide prodrug whose active metabolite inhibits the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase12. However, remdesivir is not a preferred treatment for EVD, as outcomes were inferior to the monoclonal antibodies mAb114 and REGN-EB3 in the PALM trial13, and the WHO issued a conditional recommendation against its use for EVD in 2022. For BDBV, in vitro activity has been demonstrated and may exceed that against EBOV14, but evidence for clinical benefit is lacking. In rhesus macaques, combining remdesivir and MBP413, a variant of MBP134 with extended serum half-life, improved survival when initiated 6 days after Sudan virus exposure compared to MBP431 alone (80% versus 20%)15. Together, these findings support further evaluation of remdesivir−monoclonal antibody combination therapy, particularly in patients with advanced disease and high viremia13,16.

Rollin et al.17 reported that liver failure with increased AST/ALT ratio and renal failure are associated with increased mortality in Sudan virus disease, findings also recently reported in a fatal BVD case in Uganda8. Our patient had substantial liver injury on admission but preserved hepatic synthetic function and renal function. The AST/ALT ratio peaked on day 5 and declined thereafter, despite a transient increase in absolute transaminase levels until day 9. Whether this course reflected the natural history of disease, antiviral therapy or both cannot be determined.

A semen sample obtained on day 20 was PCR positive at low levels, whereas follow-up testing on day 25 was negative. Monoclonal antibody therapy may promote Ebola virus persistence in immune-privileged sites by altering viral clearance mechanisms during acute infection10. Conversely, a small study suggested that a 5-day course of remdesivir reduced EBOV RNA levels in semen18. Whether combined MBP134−remdesivir treatment impacted viral persistence in our patient remains unknown.

The main limitation is the single-patient nature of this report, precluding attribution of the clinical course or virologic findings to individual interventions, their combined effects or the natural course of disease. Strengths of this report include detailed longitudinal clinical, virologic and immunologic characterization and evaluation of neutralizing activity against the current outbreak strain.

Methods

This research complied with all relevant ethical regulations and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Charité – Universitätsmedizin Berlin (EA4/255/23). Details are provided below.

Ethics and regulatory considerations

The patient was enrolled in the Markers for the Early Detection of Infections (MEDICS) study, a prospective, longitudinal cohort study conducted at Charité – Universitätsmedizin Berlin (German Clinical Trials Register identifier: DRKS00034622). The MEDICS study aims to improve the diagnostics, risk stratification and prognosis of infectious diseases through the longitudinal analysis of clinical, molecular and immunological parameters. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Charité – Universitätsmedizin Berlin (approval number: EA4/255/23). The patient provided written informed consent for study participation and biomaterial collection and specifically for the publication of his clinical details (including potentially identifying information) in this case report upon admission to the HLIU (day 5 after symptom onset), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and documented in the medical records. The MEDICS study protocol did not influence the clinical care or procedures described in this case.

Control samples were collected in an observational protocol investigating antibody responses approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty of the University of Cologne (approval number: 16-054; German Clinical Trials Register identifier: DRKS00010169).

After notification of a symptomatic US citizen and his high-risk household contacts (see Gaebler et al., in press), the infectious diseases consultant for the US Department of State worked with National Emerging Special Pathogens Training and Education Center (NETEC) consultants to identify medical countermeasures available or under development for BDBV. Emergency access to MBP134 was initiated on day 3 after symptom onset through consultation with Mapp Biopharmaceutical, the FDA and the Administration for Strategic Preparedness and Response (ASPR). FDA eIND authorization was granted at 00:45 Central Africa Time on day 4. MBP134 was administered in the evening of day 4 under an individual FDA eIND authorization, after a favorable risk−benefit assessment based on the high case−fatality rate and absence of approved interventions for BVD, preclinical evidence of efficacy of MBP134 against BDBV6,9 and limited but favorable human safety data. This activity was reviewed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), was deemed not to constitute research and was conducted consistent with applicable federal law and CDC policy (see, for example, 45 CFR part 46; 21 CFR part 56; 42 USC §241(d); 5 USC §552a; 44 USC §3501 et seq.).

A dose of 50 mg kg−1 was selected based on the regimen specified in the WHO SOLIDARITY Ebola Disease trial protocol and the MBP134 Expanded Access Protocol (CLINPRT-7). The selection of this dose was informed by available phase 1 safety and pharmacokinetic data, preclinical efficacy data and prior clinical experience with MBP134. Mapp Biopharmaceutical had no role in treatment decisions.

Remdesivir was administered off-label at a loading dose of 200 mg, followed by 100 mg once daily and continued until BDBV RNA was undetectable in plasma by RT−qPCR.

Adverse events were monitored throughout hospitalization through daily clinical evaluation, vital sign monitoring and routine laboratory testing (Supplementary Table 2), and severe adverse events were determined according to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. Particular attention was given to known potential toxicities of remdesivir (for example, hepatotoxicity and infusion reactions) and MBP134 (for example, infusion and hypersensitivity reactions).

Exposure, history, treatments, treatment setting and safety measures

A detailed history identified one probable and one possible exposure event. On day 9 before symptom onset, the patient performed an ultrasound examination, and, on day 8 before symptom onset, he performed surgery on a 14-week pregnant woman who presented with threatened abortion as well as suspected cholecystitis and who subsequently developed severe hemorrhagic manifestations and died. On day 8 before symptom onset, he also performed colonoscopy on a male patient presenting with hematochezia who died shortly thereafter. Standard personal protective equipment was used during both procedures, and no specific high-risk exposure was recalled. However, several healthcare workers and family caregivers subsequently developed a similar hemorrhagic illness and died.

The patient initially reported myalgia, malaise and asthenia on day 1. From day 2, he developed fever, vomiting and diarrhea, which progressed through day 3. On day 3, he also developed mild cognitive impairment. During the initial phase of illness (days 1−3), the patient self-administered artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) for suspected malaria despite a negative malaria test as well as acetaminophen for fever and ondansetron for nausea and vomiting. He attempted to maintain oral fluid intake. With worsening gastrointestinal symptoms and clinical deterioration, intravenous fluid replacement was subsequently initiated. Detailed information on treatment during the outpatient phase before medical evacuation is limited. The evolution of clinical signs and symptoms and administered treatments is detailed in Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 1.

Medical evacuation was performed by a Federal Aviation Administration (FAA)-certificated specialty aviation and charter company using a modified Gulfstream aircraft equipped with an Aeromedical Biological Containment System—a negative-pressure isolation system designed for the transport of patients with high-consequence infectious diseases. After arrival in Berlin, the patient was transported by a specialized ambulance team experienced in the management of high-consequence infectious diseases and wearing protective suits with powered air-purifying respirators (PAPRs) to the HLIU at Charité – Universitätsmedizin Berlin.

The patient was admitted to the Berlin HLIU, a 20-bed facility with negative-pressure rooms that is physically separated from the remainder of the university hospital. For this patient, a dedicated isolation section comprising two intensive care patient rooms, an operating room, a point-of-care laboratory and staff entry and exit areas with airlocks and decontamination facilities was activated. Patient care was provided by teams wearing fully enclosed protective suits with PAPRs and working in 3-hour bedside shifts; at least one physician and one nurse were present at all times. In a separate section of the unit, five family members classified as high-risk contacts were quarantined under public health supervision and cared for by infectious diseases and pediatric teams using standard personal protective equipment (ref. 19).

Clinical samples were collected in the HLIU by trained staff wearing protective suits with PAPRs under high-containment precautions. Samples for point-of-care diagnostic testing were handled in a biosafety cabinet with laminar airflow. Samples for further virologic or immunologic analyses were transferred in sealed biocontainment transport containers (Bio-Bottle) to the biosafety level 4 (BSL-4) laboratory at the Robert Koch Institute (RKI) according to established procedures. Sample inactivation was performed within the BSL-4 laboratory by experienced personnel using established and validated protocols before downstream analyses.

The patientʼs sex was self-reported as ‘male’.

MBP134

MBP134 is an investigational drug comprising two broadly neutralizing human monoclonal antibodies, ADI-15878 and ADI-23774, under development for EVD by Mapp Biopharmaceutical6,9. Detailed sequence information for both component antibodies is publicly available in the patent cited20. Additional information on the structural and functional characterization and preclinical efficacy of MBP134 is provided in the patent and previous publications6,9,20.

RNA extraction, BDBV RNA detection and viral load estimation

Nucleic acids were extracted from EDTA plasma, urine and swab samples taken daily using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen), broadly following the manufacturerʼs instructions. For biosafety reasons, the initial extraction steps were performed within the HLIU, where native samples were first mixed with the kit lysis buffer at a ratio of 1:4, or, in case of swab, with 4 ml of lysis buffer, incubated at 70 °C for 15 minutes and subsequently supplemented with ethanol as part of the standard extraction procedure. An input volume of 140 µl was used, and nucleic acids were eluted in 100 µl.

Stool samples, if available, were processed in a similar manner, using approximately half a pea-sized amount of material suspended in 4 ml of lysis buffer. Successful extraction of RNA was confirmed in all samples by real-time RT−PCR detection of β-2-microglobulin (B2M) mRNA21,22 or by detection of BDBV RNA (Supplementary Table 2).

For detection and quantification of BDBV RNA, two commercially available real-time RT−PCR assays were used: the BDBV component in the Altona RealStar Filovirus Type RT−PCR Kit 2.0, which is equivalent to the Altona RealStar Bundibugyo Virus RT−PCR Kit 1.0, and the TIB MOLBIOL LightMix Modular Bundibugyo Virus Kit. Both assays include an internal amplification control to assess potential PCR inhibition. Quantification was performed using serial dilutions of in vitro-transcribed and photometrically quantified RNA based on the Altona RealStar BDBV assay. Additionally, urine and semen samples were quantified by RKI in-house digital PCR using the Qiagen QIAcuity platform.

For the in-house PCR at RKI, samples for nucleic acid extraction were inactivated using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturerʼs instructions. In brief, 140 µl of sample was added to 560 µl of AVL containing carrier RNA and internal controls, mixed and incubated for 10 minutes at room temperature. Inactivation in the BSL-4 laboratory at RKI was completed by addition of 560 µl of ethanol and subsequent mixing. Extraction was completed using automated extraction with the QIACube Connect. Input volume per sample was 140 µl for swab, plasma and urine samples and 50 µl for semen samples; elution volume was 60 µl.

The following real-time RT−PCRs were performed at RKI: Altona RealStar Filovirus Screen RT−PCR Kit 1.0, Altona RealStar Filovirus Type RT-PCR Kit 2.0 and a BDBV-specific in-house PCR with a template volume of 5 µl (see results in Supplementary Table 1) (protocol in-house PCR: https://www.rki.de/DE/Themen/Infektionskrankheiten/Infektionskrankheiten-A-Z/E/Ebola/Downloads/Protokoll-PCR-Bundibugyo-Virus.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=2). Potential PCR inhibition was assessed using the internal control of the Altona Filovirus Screen assay or an in-house control.

For the first day of sampling, we relied on the cycle threshold (Ct) value (34.67) obtained with the RADIONE Ebola Detection Kit (model RP017) in the DRC. In the absence of a BDBV-specific quantification standard for this kit, viral RNA concentration could only be estimated approximately by extrapolation based on the reported limit of detection of this assay (800 copies per milliliter) and available comparative performance data23. Viral concentration for the single positive semen sample was determined by digital PCR, although positivity was observed only with the RKI in-house RT−PCR assay and not with the Altona screening RT−PCR kit.

Samples obtained on the first day were additionally tested using two further commercially available RT−PCR assays (Altona RealStar Filovirus Screen RT−PCR Kit 1.0 and TIB MOLBIOL LightMix Modular Ebola Virus), two in-house RT−PCR assays (manuscript in preparation) and three generic conventional PCR assays, all of which yielded results consistent with those of the BDBV-specific RT−PCR assays described above.

High-throughput sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

For library preparation, 10 µl of the RNA eluate of first available swab and plasma samples was used with the KAPA RNA HyperPrep Kit (Roche) according to the manufacturerʼs instructions.

RNA was fragmented for 6 minutes at 85 °C, and libraries were amplified for 14 PCR cycles. The resulting libraries were pooled in equimolar amounts and subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina MiniSeq platform (300-cycle run).

Bioinformatic processing of raw sequencing data and virus discovery were performed as previously described21. Raw reads were mapped to a reference sequence in Geneious Prime version 2022.0.1, and the resulting alignments were manually reviewed. Consensus sequences were called using a 75% majority threshold.

The complete coding region of the BDBV genome obtained from the oropharyngeal swab was used for phylogenetic analysis.

No sequence differences were detected between the oropharyngeal swab and plasma samples (both day 5) at positions for which sequence information was available from both specimens. The oropharyngeal swab yielded 100% genome coverage, with a mean sequencing depth of 41.1 (range, 4−92). The plasma sample yielded 94.3% genome coverage, with a mean sequencing depth of 7.6 (range, 0−25). The consensus sequences were identical across the overlapping covered genomic regions.

The sequence (PP_006XXY5.1) was aligned together with National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) reference sequences for the other Ebola virus species and additional BDBV sequences retrieved from the Pathoplexus, including a sequence from the 2026 outbreak (PP_006XCJJ.1) and previous BDBV outbreaks, using MAFFT version 7.526.

A phylogenetic tree was then constructed using IQ-TREE version 3.1.2 and visualized using TreeViewer version 2.2.0. The accession IDs for the reference sequences are as follows: Bombali: NC_039345.1, Bundibugyo: NC_014373.1, Reston: NC_004161.1, Sudan: NC_006432.1, Tai Forest: NC_014372.1 and Zaire: NC_002549.1.

The BDBV sequence was made publicly available immediately through the Pathoplexus database (https://pathoplexus.org/; accession number PP_006XXY5.1).

Virus isolation

At Bernhard Nocht Institute for Tropical Medicine (BNITM) Vero E6 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells in a T25 flask 1 day before infection. Cells were inoculated with 250 µl of patient serum (sampled on 21 May 2026), 100 µl of heparin full blood and 100 µl of heparin plasma. Fifteen milliliters of throat swap medium (DMEM) was concentrated to 200 µl using an Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter, 100-kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO). Then, 100 µl of concentrated supernatant was used for inoculation. Cells were transferred on day 2 after inoculation to a T75 flask and to a T175 flask on day 5. Supernatant for RT−PCR was taken on days 2, 5, 11 and 15 after inoculation and stored at −80 °C for RT−PCR and virus titration. All tested supernatants were RT−PCR negative (Altona Diagnostics, RealStar Filovirus Screen RT−PCR Kit 1.0 RUO).

Virus neutralization assay

The orthoebola viruses used for the neutralization experiments were EBOV Mayinga (1976; AF08633.2), SUDV Gulu (2000; AY729654.1) and BDBV (2007; KU182911.1). All viruses were amplified in Vero E6 cells (American Type Culture Collection (ATCC)) and quantified by immunofocus assay. Sequences were verified by next-generation sequencing. Virus stocks were aliquoted in single-use aliquots and stored at −80 °C in the BSL-4 laboratory of BNITM. The BDBV strain was kindly provided by CDC, Atlanta, under Material Transfer Agreement D-565-15.

Immunofocus reduction neutralization assay was used to determine the virus neutralization capacity against authentic viruses. Patient sera were diluted three-fold in DMEM (without additives) over 10 steps starting from 1:10, resulting in dilution from 1:10 to 1:196,830. The diluted samples were mixed at equal volume with 100 infectious virus particles of BDBV, EBOV or SUDV and incubated for 1 hour at 37 °C. The serum/virus mixtures were used to inoculate Vero E6 cells that were seeded in 96-well plates at a cell density of 3 × 104 cells per well the day before. After 1.5 hours, the inoculum was removed and replaced by overlay medium (DMEM, 10% FCS, 1% methyl cellulose) for 3 days (EBOV) or 4 days (BDBV, SUDV). The overlay medium was removed, and cells were fixed in 4.5% formaldehyde in PBS for 60 minutes. After permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 minutes and blocking with 2% FCS in PBS for 60 minutes, the infected cells were visualized through staining with a polyclonal mouse anti-EBOV serum, followed by incubation with an anti-mouse horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibody and TMB. Spots were counted using an ELISpot reader (CTL EliSpot Reader ImmunoSpot S6 Ultra-V Analyzer). Fifty-percent inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were calculated with a sigmoidal 4PL regression model using GraphPad Prism 11 software. Pooled serum of healthy donors was included as negative controls. All healthy donors were recruited in Germany without reported exposure to Ebola or previous vaccination against Ebola viruses.

Immunofluorescence detection of IgM antibodies

Vero E6 cells were infected with BDBV and harvested at 50% infection rate through trypsinization. Cells were resuspended in a low volume of PBS and spotted onto 12-well immunofluorescence slides. Cells were dyed and fixed in 100% acetone. Fixed slides were stored frozen. For detection of IgM antibodies, the sera were diluted as follows: patient sera two-fold over 10 steps starting from 1:20. Slides were incubated with diluted sera for 30 minutes at 37 °C in a humid chamber, washed with PBS and stained with a secondary anti-human IgM secondary antibody labeled with FITC. Evans blue visualization of infected cells was done with a fluorescence microscope.

GP production and purification

For ELISA, the ectodomain of Bundibugyo virus GP (YP_003815435; amino acids: 33−650) was amplified from synthetic gene plasmids using specific PCR primers. PCR products were digested with the appropriate restriction enzymes (NheI and BglII), and the resulting fragments were cloned into a modified Sleeping Beauty transposon expression vector containing an N-terminal BM40 signal peptide sequence and a C-terminal T4 fibritin foldon followed by a Twin-Strep tag. HEK293 cells were co-transfected with the transposase (0.2 g) and the ectodomain-containing plasmid (1.8 g). After transfection, cells were selected with puromycin. After 1 week, surviving cells were expanded and cultured in triple flask, and protein expression was induced with doxycyclin. BDBV GP ectodomains were filtered and purified using a Strep-Tactin XT 4Flow column (IBA Lifesciences). After a high salt wash with 1 M NaCl, 40 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, protein was eluted with 100 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM biotin, pH 8.0, and subsequently dialyzed against PBS. Protein concentration was determined based on the calculated extinction coefficients of the different proteins. Purified viral GP was stored at −80 °C until use.

For sGP production, the EBOV sGP sequence (GenBank accession: KJ660347) was cloned into the pCAGGS backbone as described previously24. The construct was further modified to carry an Avi-tag and a His-tag for purification. HEK293E cells were maintained in FreeStyle Medium (Life Technologies) at 37 °C with 6% CO2 and shaking at 110 r.p.m. At a concentration of 0.8 × 106 cells per milliliter, transfection was performed using 25-kDa polyethylenimine (PEI; Polysciences) with 1 µg of DNA per milliliter cell suspension. After 7 days of incubation, the supernatant was harvested, filtered (Nalgene, 0.45-µm filter; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated with Protino Ni-NTA agarose beads (Macherey-Nagel) overnight. Beads were pelleted, transferred into a column and washed once with NPI-10 buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, pH 8.0) and once with NPI-20 buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, pH 8.0). His-tagged sGP was eluted with NPI-100 buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, 100 mM imidazole, pH 8.0), and the buffer was exchanged to PBS using centrifugal filter units (Amicon MWCO 10 kDa; Merck). Purified sGP was filtered (0.22 µm; Merck) and stored at −80 °C until use.

Determination of antibody concentrations and binding reactivity by ELISA

Anti-BDBV GP IgG concentrations in serum samples (inactivated using 0.5% Triton X-100 and 0.5% Tween 20 at 60 °C for 30 minutes) were quantified by ELISA, performed as previously described (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41596-021-00554-w) with minor modifications. ELISA plates (Corning) were coated with BDBV-GP (3 µg ml−1 in PBS) overnight at 4 °C, followed by blocking with 5% BSA and 0.1% Tween 20 in PBS for 90 minutes at room temperature. For standard curve generation and as assay controls, MBP134 at 1,000 µg ml−1, pooled plasma from healthy individuals (treated with Triton X-100/Tween 20 and spiked with 1,000 µg ml−1 MBP134) and pooled plasma from individuals vaccinated with rVSV-ZEBOV 2 years earlier (treated with Triton X-100/Tween 20 and spiked with 1,000 µg ml−1 MBP134) were used. Serial dilutions (1:3) of patient samples and controls were added, starting at a dilution of 1:20. Bound IgG antibodies were detected using HRP-conjugated anti-human IgG antibody (SouthernBiotech, 1:2,000 in 5% BSA/PBS). The reaction was developed with ABTS substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and absorbance was measured at 415 nm/695 nm using a Tecan plate reader. Anti-BDBV IgG concentrations were determined based on the standard curve generated from rVSV-ZEBOV vaccinee plasma spiked with MBP134, defining 1 a.u. ml−1 as the signal determined for a standard sample containing MBP134 at a concentration of 1 µg ml−1.

Anti-EBOV sGP IgG reactivity was performed as for anti-BDBV IgG except for differences in coating (sGP coated at a concentration at 5 µg ml−1 for 90 minutes at 37 °C), the use of control samples that were not spiked with MBP134 and not treated with Triton X-100/Tween 20 and the use of a 1:50 starting dilution, followed by a single 1:3 dilution and a subsequent 1:2 dilution series.

Anti-BDBV GP IgM and IgA reactivity was performed as described for IgG except for the use of HRP-conjugated anti-human IgM and IgA detection antibodies (SouthernBiotech, each diluted 1:2,000 in 5% BSA/PBS) and the use of an MBP134 starting concentration of 100 µg ml−1 in controls. Binding curves were fitted in GraphPad Prism (four parameters with variable slope).

Purification of serum IgGs

To isolate serum IgGs, serum was diluted 1:10 in Dulbecco’s PBS (DPBS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and IgG was purified by three sequential passes over a 1.5-ml protein G agarose column (Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific). After extensive washing with DPBS, bound IgG was eluted using glycine-HCl (pH 3) and immediately buffered with 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). Buffer exchange to PBS and concentration of the purified IgG were carried out using 30-kDa MWCO filters (Merck Millipore).

Cloning and production of pseudotyped lentiviral particles

For the production of pseudotyped lentiviral particles, full filovirus GP coding regions (GenBank accession numbers: EBOV AF086833.2 and BDBV FJ217161) with their original leader peptides were codon optimized (VectorBuilder Codon Optimization Tool) and ordered as gene fragments cloned into expression plasmids (pTwist CMV BG WPRE Neo; Twist Bioscience). Lyophilized plasmids were solved in nuclease-free water (Ambion) at a final concentration of 100 ng µl−1 and pre-amplified by bacterial transformation. For the 2026 BDBV strain, BDBV RNA isolated from patient material was first reverse transcribed by RT−PCR, followed by specific amplification of the GP region by PCR. The GP sequence was cloned into an expression vector (CMV BG WPRE Neo) using sequence- and ligation-independent cloning (SLIC) with T4 DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs (NEB)) and transformed into NEB Turbo Competent E. coli (NEB). Forty-eight colonies were screened by PCR and gel electrophoresis, and positive clones were subjected to Sanger sequencing. Amplification of the plasmid containing the correct GP sequence was performed by Midi preparation (Macherey-Nagel) according to the manufacturerʼs protocol. To introduce an additional adenine into the GP editing site, which is normally added by the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase during GP-mRNA transcription, site-directed mutagenesis (NEB) was performed using suitable primers.

The pseudotyped lentivirus was produced as described previously with minor modifications25,26. In brief, individual plasmids encoding HIV-1 Tat, HIV-1 Gag/Pol, HIV-1 Rev, firefly luciferase followed by an IRES-ZsGreen and the corresponding filovirus protein construct were co-transfected in HEK293T cells 4−6 hours after a change to FreeStyle 293 Expression Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using FuGENE 6 Transfection Reagent (Promega). Cells were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Culture supernatants were harvested at 40 hours and 72 hours after transfection and subsequently filtered through a 0.45-µm PVDF syringe filter and stored at −80 °C.

To determine virus titers, HEK293T cells were infected with serial dilutions of viral supernatants. After 48 hours of incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2, cells were lysed for 2 minutes with luciferin/lysis buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 0.3 mM ATP, 0.5 mM Coenzyme A, 17 mM IGEPAL; all Sigma-Aldrich) containing 1 mM D-luciferin (GoldBio) in Tris-HCl. Luciferase activity was quantified using a microplate reader (Berthold Technologies, Tristar 5; 1 second counting time). For neutralization assays, viruses were diluted in FreeStyle medium containing 1% BSA to yield relative light units (RLUs) approximately 1,000-fold higher than those of uninfected controls, corresponding to approximately 50,000−100,000 RLUs.

Pseudovirus neutralization tests

Neutralization activity of purified serum IgGs and MBP134 was tested in a microneutralization assay in a BSL-2 facility. Serial dilutions of serum IgG or monoclonal antibodies were prepared in DMEM (Gibco) containing 1 mM L-glutamine in a 96-well format and incubated with pseudovirus for 1 hour at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Subsequently, 1.25 × 104 HEK293T cells were added per well and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for another 48 hours. Luciferase activity was measured as described above. Negative controls (cells only and virus only) and virus-positive controls (infected cells without samples) were measured on each assay plate. The mean RLU of negative controls was subtracted as background signal, and IC50 values were calculated as the sample concentration leading to a 50% reduction of RLUs in comparison to the virus-positive control. Neutralization curves were fitted in GraphPad Prism (four parameters with variable slope). IgG purified from pooled serum of healthy donors was included as negative controls. All healthy donors were recruited in Germany without reported exposure to Ebola or previous vaccination against Ebola viruses.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical analyses were performed because this study describes a single patient. Data are presented descriptively. Sample size was not predetermined by statistical methods. The number of timepoints analyzed was determined by the availability of plasma and serum samples collected during clinical care, and no data were excluded. Where appropriate, technical replicates were performed to assess measurement reproducibility, as specified in the respective Methods sections and in the figure legends. No data were excluded from the analysis. For pseudovirus and authentic neutralization assays, IC50 values were derived by nonlinear regression using a four-parameter logistic model in GraphPad Prism 11 software from three or two independent experiments, respectively.
