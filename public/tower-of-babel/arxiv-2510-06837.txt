A Quantum Linear Systems Pathway for Solving Differential Equations
Abhishek Setty

Abstract
We present a systematic pathway for solving differential equations within the quantum linear systems framework by combining block encoding with Quantum Singular Value Transformation (QSVT). The approach is demonstrated on a complex tridiagonal linear system and extended to problems in computational fluid dynamics: the heat equation with mixed boundary conditions and the nonlinear Burgers’ equation. Our scaling analysis of the heat equation shows how discretization influences the minimum singular value and the polynomial degree required for QSVT, identifying circuit-depth overhead as a key bottleneck. For Burgers’ equation, we illustrate how Carleman-linearized nonlinear dynamics can be efficiently block encoded and solved within the QSVT framework. These results highlight both the potential and limitations of current methods, underscoring the need for efficient estimation of minimum singular value, depth-reduction techniques, and benchmarks against classical reachability. This pathway lays a foundation for advancing quantum linear system methods toward large-scale applications.

5
Discussion

In this work, we have consolidated major advances in the literature and outlined a systematic pathway for solving differential equations within the quantum linear systems framework. By bridging algorithmic concepts with circuit-level implementation, we identify key bottlenecks that must be addressed for practical deployment.

A key component of our approach is the use of QSVT in combination with block encoding of sparse matrices for matrix inversion. A notable advantage of this method is its reusability: once a phase sequence is optimized for a chosen
\kappa_{2}
, it remains valid for any matrix
A
with
\kappa_{1}=1/\sigma_{\text{min}}
, provided
\kappa_{2}>\kappa_{1}
. This property can significantly reduce preprocessing overhead in practical implementations.

We demonstrate the utility of this framework by implementing matrix inversion on a complex linear system and extending it to differential equations relevant to CFD. In particular, we consider the linear heat equation and the Carleman-linearized Burgers’ equation. The latter illustrates how nonlinear dynamics can be embedded into a linear framework, with Carleman linearization itself representing an active area of research.

Our scaling analysis of the heat equation underscores a critical challenge: the circuit depth overhead caused by polynomial approximations of the inverse function. This motivates further work on depth-reduction strategies, such as singular value amplification
low2017hamiltonian
,
gilyen2019quantum
, or alternative approximation methods.

Another important open problem is the efficient estimation of
\sigma_{\text{min}}
for sparse matrices at scale, which is essential for computing the QSVT phase factors
\phi
. Likewise, progress in quantum state sampling and observable estimation will be required to ensure that post-processing costs do not erode the theoretical speedups. By addressing these challenges, future research can help close the gap between theory and practice, ultimately advancing quantum linear solvers toward large-scale applications.

Quantum Signal Processing (QSP) is a framework for implementing polynomial transformations on quantum computers. Conceptually, QSP combines two single-qubit rotations: a fixed
signal rotation

W

A.1
W_{X}
Convention for QSP

In the

W_{X}(a)=\begin{bmatrix}a&i\sqrt{1-a^{2}}\\

(8)

which corresponds to an

rotation with angle
\theta=-2\cos^{-1}{a}
. The signal processing operator is

(9)

Appendix D
Finite Difference Methods for Heat Equation

Consider the heat equation
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\nu\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}
, where
\nu>0
is the thermal diffusivity. The discrete variables are
t_{n}=n\Delta t,\,x_{i}=i\Delta x,\,u_{i}^{n}=u(x_{i},t_{n})

\frac{u_{i}^{n+1}-u_{i}^{n}}{\Delta t}=\nu\frac{u_{i+1}^{n}-2u_{i}^{n}+u_{i-1}^{n}}{\Delta x^{2}}+O(\Delta t,\Delta x^{2}),

(24)

where
O(\Delta t,\Delta x^{2})
denotes the truncation error. Rearranging gives

u^{n+1}_{i}=\lambda u_{i+1}^{n}+(1-2\lambda)u_{i}^{n}+\lambda u_{i-1}^{n},

(25)

with
\lambda=\nu\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x^{2}}
. In vector form, this is
\vec{u}^{n+1}=A\vec{u}^{n}+\vec{f}^{\text{BC}}
, where
\vec{f}^{\text{BC}}
accounts for boundary conditions. This scheme avoids matrix inversion but is conditionally stable, with stability governed by von Neumann analysis

,
ames2014numerical

\Delta t_{\text{exp}}^{\text{max}}\leq\frac{\Delta x^{2}}{2\nu}.

(26)

For
\Delta t>\Delta t_{\text{exp}}^{\text{max}}
, the explicit scheme diverges, while the implicit method remains stable for any
\Delta t
, making it preferable for larger time steps.

The implicit discretization is

\frac{u_{i}^{n+1}-u_{i}^{n}}{\Delta t}=\nu\frac{u_{i+1}^{n+1}-2u_{i}^{n+1}+u_{i-1}^{n+1}}{\Delta x^{2}}+O(\Delta t,\Delta x^{2}),

(27)

which rearranges to

-\lambda u_{i-1}^{n+1}+(1+2\lambda)u_{i}^{n+1}-\lambda u_{i+1}^{n+1}=u_{i}^{n}.

(28)

This leads to the linear system

A\vec{u}^{n+1}=\vec{b}^{n},

(29)

where
\vec{b}^{n}=\vec{u}^{n}+\vec{f}^{\text{BC}}
. Such systems can be efficiently solved using QSVT
Section

3
.

For boundary conditions, let the solution domain be

with grid points

. We impose a Dirichlet condition on the left boundary,

(30)
