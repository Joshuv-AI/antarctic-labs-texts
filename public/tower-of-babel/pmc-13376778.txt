A blockchain-secured 6G smartgrid framework for resilient renewable energy integration and intelligent anomaly detection
Authors: Ismail Hossain, Abu Shufian, Morium Akter Munny, Nowshad Amin, Rayan Hamza Alsisi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe integration of intermittent renewable energy into smart grids introduces critical vulnerabilities in security, transparency, and real-time resilience. This paper presents a novel blockchain-secured 6G Smart grid framework that synergistically integrates sixth generation (6G) ultra-reliable low-latency communication (URLLC), distributed ledger technology, and ensemble machine learning to establish a secure, scalable, and intelligent energy ecosystem. The proposed architecture leverages adaptive 6G network slicing to support differentiated services-including peer-to-peer energy trading, grid control, and cybersecurity monitoring-while ensuring sub-30 ms latency and robust connectivity. A permission blockchain layer provides decentralized trust, immutability, and automated transaction validation via formally verified smart contracts. An ensemble learning model combining XGBoost, Random Forest, and LightGBM enables real-time multi-dimensional anomaly detection across energy, network, and transaction layers. The framework is evaluated using a synthetically generated dataset of 5000 hourly records encompassing energy generation, consumption, 6G network performance, and blockchain transactions. Experimental results demonstrate a blockchain transaction success rate of 95.16% and sustained network latency below 30 ms across all slices, even under cyberattack conditions. Reported using class-based anomaly-detection metrics, the model achieves a Recall of 0.93 and F1-score of 0.97 for the anomaly class, and a Recall of 1.00 and F1-score of 0.99 for the normal class, with an overall accuracy of 0.98. The proposed system provides a foundational architecture for resilient, autonomous, and secure renewable energy management in next generation decentralized smart grids.Keywords: 6G networks, Machine learning and blockchain technology, Smartgrid security, Network slicing and cybersecurity, Energy tradingSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The global energy landscape is undergoing a profound transformation driven by the urgent need for sustainable development and the rapid advancement of digital technologies. Decentralized renewable energy systems, particularly microgrids, have emerged as critical components in this transition, enabling efficient integration of distributed energy resources while enhancing grid resilience and energy accessibility1. However, this paradigm shift introduces complex challenges in security management, operational reliability, and transaction transparency that traditional energy systems were not designed to address. The proliferation of intermittent renewable sources, such as solar and wind generation, creates dynamic supply-demand imbalances that require sophisticated monitoring and control mechanisms. Photovoltaic forecasting accuracy is essential for reliable smart micro-grid operation and market-oriented scheduling in renewable-rich systems. Blockchain-enabled open electricity markets can support decentralized settlement, while standardization and regulatory constraints remain key barriers to data-driven micro-grid adoption2. Simultaneously, the increasing connectivity of energy infrastructure through Internet of Things devices expands the attack surface for potential cyber threats, risking grid stability and public safety. Furthermore, the growing adoption of peer-to-peer energy trading models demands secure, transparent, and efficient transaction mechanisms that conventional centralized systems cannot adequately provide.

Recent technological advancements in communication networks and distributed systems offer promising solutions to these challenges. Sixth-generation wireless technology introduces unprecedented capabilities in ultra-reliable low-latency communication, network slicing, and massive machine-type communications, providing the necessary infrastructure for real-time microgrid operations3. Concurrently, blockchain technology brings inherent advantages in security, transparency, and decentralization through its immutable distributed ledger and innovative contract capabilities4. One study introduces a decentralized electricity trading framework for connected EVs using blockchain and machine learning to remove trusted intermediaries and improve transaction trustworthiness5. The work motivates blockchain-assisted P2P trading as a pathway to improved settlement efficiency and profitability optimization in decentralized energy markets. The integration of these technologies creates a synergistic ecosystem where 6G networks enable seamless connectivity and real-time data exchange, while blockchain ensures transaction integrity and system security. This convergence addresses fundamental requirements for modern microgrid operations, including real-time monitoring, secure energy trading, cyber threat detection, and automated control systems (Fig. 1). However, the practical implementation of this integrated approach requires careful consideration of technical constraints, performance optimization, and security implications across multiple operational dimensions.

Blockchain technology for energy system

Blockchain technology represents a decentralized digital ledger system that operates as a distributed database, maintaining an immutable, chronologically ordered sequence of data blocks, with each new transaction being systematically appended to this ever-expanding chain32. The inherent security of blockchain systems derives from advanced cryptographic protocols, where every transaction initiates the generation of an asymmetric cryptographic digital signature that serves to validate and authenticate the transaction’s legitimacy. This cryptographic framework establishes a paired key infrastructure comprising public and private keys, which are exclusively controlled by the transaction participants within the network ecosystem. The public key component, employed in the data decryption process, remains openly accessible to all network participants, while the corresponding private key remains confidential to its owner, ensuring secure transaction authorization and identity verification33.

The diagram typically represents these characteristics as interconnected or mutually reinforcing elements, highlighting their collective role in creating a secure, transparent, and trustworthy decentralized system.

The increasing penetration of distributed renewable energy resources and the evolution toward 6G-enabled smart microgrids necessitate a secure, transparent, and decentralized energy management infrastructure. Blockchain technology has emerged as a promising solution to address critical challenges related to trust, data integrity, cybersecurity, and real-time coordination in modern energy systems34. Blockchain is a distributed ledger technology (DLT) that enables secure and immutable recording of transactions across a decentralized network. In energy systems, blockchain facilitates trusted data exchange among microgrid participants, including distributed energy resources (DERs), prosumers, utilities, and grid operators, without reliance on a centralized authority. Each transaction-such as energy generation, consumption, storage, or trading-is cryptographically signed and grouped into blocks. These blocks are sequentially linked using cryptographic hash functions, ensuring data immutability and resistance to tampering. Consensus mechanisms, such as Proof of Stake (PoS) or Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT), are commonly preferred in energy applications due to their low latency and energy efficiency compared to P roof of Work (PoW)35. Blockchain enhances the integration of renewable energy sources by ensuring secure coordination and verification of distributed generation units. Through smart contracts, energy generation and consumption are automatically validated based on predefined rules, enabling transparent peer-to-peer (P2P) energy trading within microgrids. PBFT is selected for its deterministic finality and bounded latency, essential for real-time energy trading in 6G microgrids. Unlike PoS or PoA, PBFT provides immediate transaction finality and tolerates Byzantine faults in permissioned networks without PoW’s energy cost or PoS’s economic vulnerabilities, ensuring secure, low-latency consensus aligned with URLLC requirements. Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT) algorithms are critical for distributed systems, as they solve the Byzantine generals’ problem. This classic dilemma involves a group of general-representing network nodes in a blockchain context-attempting to agree on a joint action despite unreliable communication and the presence of potential traitors37. In the original analogy, Byzantine generals must coordinate their armies to attack a citadel at the same time, even though messages between them may be intercepted, lost, or tampered with. Similarly, in blockchains, nodes must reach consensus on whether to validate a block or set of transactions across a decentralized and potentially unstable network. The challenge is further heightened by the possibility that some participants may be malicious sending false information, altering messages, or refusing to communicate-with the goal of sabotaging the consensus process38. The core objective is to ensure that honest nodes can still agree on a consistent decision, even if a subset behaves dishonestly. Translated to blockchain terms, this means preventing a minority of untrustworthy or malicious nodes from corrupting the validation of illegitimate transactions. Because PBFT relies on known, trusted validators to individually verify transactions, it is particularly well-suited for permission or consortium blockchain environments where participant identity is controlled and trust is higher. Decentralized systems integrating blockchain’s P2P features.

Peer-to-peer energy transaction processing initiates with direct energy exchange between prosumers and consumers, followed by smart contracts autonomously executing payment settlements calculated from real-time tariff and consumption data (Fig. 7). These transactions are encrypted using advanced cryptographic methods such as homomorphic encryption and aggregated into blocks, which are validated by blockchain miners and broadcast network-wide for decentralized consensus verification, ensuring fund availability and transaction integrity41. The blockchain maintains an immutable, chronologically ordered ledger through cryptographic hashing and digital signatures, linking blocks into a secure chain that preserves a complete transaction history, while secondary protocol layers are concurrently explored to enhance scalability and transaction throughput without compromising the foundational security architecture of the decentralized system.

Peer-to-peer (P2P) energy trading framework based on blockchain technology. Smart meters are installed at each household, enabling participants to act as prosumers or consumers. The trading parties, denoted as Node A (prosumer) and Node B (consumer), exchange energy through a smart contract. Transaction authorization is performed using digital signatures generated with the participants’ private keys. Validated transactions are encrypted, grouped into blocks, and recorded on the blockchain through sidechain and root-chain mechanisms.

Despite recent advances in blockchain technologies, practical deployments continue to face cybersecurity challenges including forks, selfish behavior, consensus-layer attacks, privacy/anonymity trade-offs, and smart-contract vulnerabilities; therefore, the proposed framework adopts a permissioned ledger with explicit mitigations aligned to smart grid requirements. Fork-related uncertainty is reduced using PBFT-style consensus with deterministic finality once quorum agreement is reached, thereby limiting competing-chain scenarios that are more typical in public PoW environments. Selfish mining is primarily associated with competitive PoW mining and is not directly applicable in the proposed setting because block validation is performed by authenticated consortium validators rather than open miners. To address consensus-layer risks (compromised or Byzantine validators), the framework assumes controlled validator admission, quorum thresholds, and governance actions (revoking validator membership) in the event of misbehavior. Privacy and access governance are supported through permissioned participation (controlled membership) and cryptographic authentication using public–private keys and digital signatures, with sensitive transaction payloads protected via encryption while retaining verifiable metadata on-chain for auditability. Finally, smart-contract security is strengthened through strict authorization checks, input validation, and systematic testing/verification practices to reduce implementation flaws and ensure that only legitimate energy-trading and settlement operations are executed.

6G communication infrastructure

As 5G networks continue their global rollout, researchers and industry pioneers are already envisioning its successor-6G. Expected to debut around 2030, 6G promises to revolutionize connectivity by merging the physical, digital, and biological worlds into a seamless, intelligent ecosystem42. 6G (sixth-generation wireless) is the next iteration of cellular technology, building upon 5G’s foundation but with transformative advances in speed, latency, intelligence, and applications. It aims to support data rates up to 1 terabit per second (Tbps)−100 times faster than peak 5G-with near-zero latency and ubiquitous coverage extending to oceans, space, and remote areas43. The deployment of sixth generation (6G) wireless communication technology represents a paradigm shift in microgrid infrastructure, enabling unprecedented levels of connectivity, intelligence, and operational efficiency. Unlike previous wireless generations, 6G systems are specifically engineered to simultaneously support three critical communication paradigms: Enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB) for high-data-rate applications, Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC) for mission-critical control functions, and Massive Machine-Type Communication (mMTC) for extensive IoT device integration44. This trifecta of capabilities positions 6G as the ideal communication backbone for modern microgrids, which demand heterogeneous service requirements across distributed energy resources, real-time monitoring systems, and secure transaction processing.

Datasets and feature analysis

The analysis utilizes a high-fidelity synthetic dataset of 5000 hourly records, generated to reflect real-world operation and integrated with simulated 6G network and blockchain transaction logs. Energy generation metrics capture solar and wind generation patterns, battery storage dynamics, and state-of-charge variations. Consumption patterns monitor residential, commercial, and industrial load profiles with temporal resolution enabling detailed pattern analysis. Network performance indicators track latency, response time, and packet loss metrics across all communication channels. Blockchain transaction records document energy trading volumes, associated costs, and verification status with cryptographic integrity assurance. Cybersecurity events log attack detection incidents and system-generated anomaly flags with precise timestamps for correlation analysis. Network attacks and anomalies were injected into the synthetic dataset using a transparent, rule-based procedure that overlays controlled perturbations on normal operating trajectories of the microgrid and 6G network. First, baseline normal time-series were generated for demand, renewable generation, storage/SOC dynamics, key electrical measurements (voltage, current, power factor), network-slicing KPIs (latency, response time, packet loss), and blockchain transaction variables (transaction cost, validation status). Next, anomaly windows were created by sampling an event start time and duration, after which attack-specific perturbations with tunable intensity were applied: (i) False Data Injection (FDI) was modelled as bounded, temporally correlated bias (step shift or gradual drift) added to selected meter/SCADA features (demand, voltage/current) while enforcing physical plausibility limits; (ii) DoS-like network degradation was modelled as bursty increases in latency/response time accompanied by elevated packet loss; (iii) transaction-layer anomalies were modelled by increased verification delay and failure probability together with transaction-cost spikes; and (iv) replay/out-of-order behavior was modelled by duplicating or reordering a small fraction of transaction records to emulate tampering at the logging/communication interface. A rigorous data preprocessing pipeline ensured data quality and consistency throughout the analysis. Multi-source data streams were synchronized using precise timestamp matching, with interpolation applied for minor timing discrepancies below 5-second thresholds. Employed forward-fill method for short gaps (< 3 consecutive missing values) and linear interpolation for longer gaps, with maximum gap tolerance of 6 h to maintain data integrity. Statistical outliers were identified using interquartile range method with 1.5×IQR threshold, followed by Winsorization at 5th and 95th percentiles to minimize information loss while reducing outlier impact. StandardScaler implementation ensured all features maintained zero mean and unit variance, preventing feature magnitude disparities from influencing model training.

Across all variables, the observations are broadly spread over their respective operating ranges, indicating good coverage of the feature space rather than concentration in a narrow regime. Specifically, energy demand and battery storage span approximately 0–500 kWh, solar generation covers about 20–300 kWh, and wind generation extends from roughly 0–250 kWh. The market and blockchain-related variables also exhibit wide variability, with energy traded ranging from about 10–100 kWh and transaction cost from approximately 0–50 USD. For the communication-performance indicators, latency varies near 5–50 ms, response time spans about 10–100 ms, and packet loss remains within 0–5%. Overall, these distributions suggest that the dataset captures diverse operational conditions for both energy and network/blockchain dimensions, supporting robust model training and evaluation.

Limitations and future work

While the proposed framework demonstrates significant advancements in secure renewable energy integration, several limitations must be acknowledged, and corresponding future research directions are identified. The validation relies on synthesized data, limiting insight into real-world deployment challenges such as hardware variability, physical-layer interference, and advanced persistent threats. Scalability under city-wide microgrids or during extreme demand fluctuations remains unverified, and the economic and regulatory alignment of the assumed transaction models requires further study. Additionally, the framework depends on 6G capabilities still in development and does not address quantum-era cryptographic threats or sophisticated insider attacks.

Future work will be directed toward real-world validation through hardware-in-the-loop experimentation and pilot deployments in partner microgrid facilities. Adaptive and hierarchical consensus protocols will be developed to maintain efficiency in expansive, heterogeneous networks. Integration with existing energy market mechanisms and regulatory standards will be formalized through compliant smart contract templates and dynamic tariff models. To ensure long-term security, post-quantum cryptographic schemes will be integrated into the blockchain layer. Further intelligence will be embedded into the 6G slicing controller through AI-driven predictive resource management and self-healing network functions. Lastly, federated learning frameworks will be extended to enable privacy-preserving, collaborative threat detection across multiple administrative domains, while explainable AI interfaces will be designed to enhance operator situational awareness and decision-making during security incidents.

Conclusion

This study introduced a comprehensive blockchain-enabled 6G microgrid framework that integrates secure energy trading, intelligent anomaly detection, and resilient network communication for future renewable energy ecosystems. Through extensive simulations and analytical evaluation, the framework achieved high transaction reliability, ultra-low latency, and strong cyberattack detection accuracy, validating the synergy between blockchain and 6G network intelligence. The ensemble-based machine learning models, particularly Gradient Boosting and Random Forest, provided superior classification performance with AUC values above 0.97. The normal class achieved Recall 1.00 and F1-score 0.99, while the anomaly class achieved Recall 0.93 and F1-score 0.97, enabling precise and rapid threat identification. Meanwhile, blockchain verification mechanisms-maintained data immutability and transaction transparency, ensuring trustworthy peer-to-peer energy exchanges even under adversarial conditions. The consistent latency and packet loss performance across network slices further demonstrated the system’s robustness and compliance with URLLC standards. The proposed design not only secures the microgrid infrastructure but also establishes a scalable and intelligent operational paradigm for next-generation 6G-powered smart energy networks. Future work will focus on integrating quantum-resistant blockchain protocols, federated learning-based privacy preservation, and energy-efficient consensus mechanisms to further strengthen scalability and sustainability in large-scale decentralized power systems.
