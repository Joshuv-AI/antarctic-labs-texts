Introduction

The aim of this paper is to prove regularity results and Schauder estimates for the second order differential operator

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}a_{ij}(x)\,X_{i}X_{j}=a^{ij}(x)\,X_{i}X_{j},

(1.1)

where
X_{1},\,...,\,X_{m}
are Hörmander vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n>m
), homogeneous w.r.t. a family of non-isotropic dilations (but not necessarily left-invariant w.r.t. any Lie group structure), and
A(x)=[a_{ij}(x)]_{1\leq i,j\leq m}
is a symmetric and uniformly positive-definite matrix whose coefficients are Hölder-continuous functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
w.r.t. the control distance induced by
X_{1},\,...,\,X_{m}
.

According to Einstein’s convention, in (
1.1
) and whenever not specified otherwise, any repeated index appearing once as apex and once as subscript will be implicitly summed from
1
up to
m
.

Let us give a general overview of the existing literature on this topic. After the famous paper by Hörmander
[
14
]
on hypoellipticity, a lot of work has been devoted to the analysis of Hörmander operators, and later also to more general nonvariational operators modeled on Hörmander vector fields, of the form (
1.1
).

In particular, a local regularity theory in both Hölder spaces and Sobolev spaces induced by the vector fields, as well as the associated estimates,
have been first proved by Folland
[
13
]
for a wide class of hypoelliptic operators, invariant under the action of Carnot groups; without such assumptions on the underlying algebraic structure, analogous results were proved in local form by Rothschild, Stein
[
16
]
for general Hörmander sum of squares. Later, this local theory has been extended by Bramanti, Brandolini
[
10
]
,
[
9
]
first to evolution operators
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{L}-\partial_{t}
with
\mathcal{L}
as in (
1.1
), and then to the wider class of operators
a^{ij}(x)X_{i}X_{j}-X_{0}
, with
\{X_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m}
Hörmander vector fields and coefficients
a_{ij}
either Hölder-continuous or satisfying a VMO condition.

In Sobolev spaces, global versions of these results have been then established:

•

in the stationary case, by Biagi, Bonfiglioli, Bramanti
[
3
]
for homogeneous Hörmander sum of squares; later by Biagi, Bramanti
[
4
]
for operators modeled on homogeneous Hörmander vector fields with
VMO
coefficients;

•

in the evolution case, by Biagi, Bramanti
[
6
]
for a class of Kolmogorov-Fokker-Planck operators with coefficients VMO in space and just measurable in time.

In Hölder spaces, instead, the only global result is the one developed by Biagi, Bramanti
[
7
]
for Kolmogorov-Fokker-Planck operators with coefficients Hölder-continuous in space and measurable in time, whereas a correspondent global regularity theory for stationary operators is so far lacking. The main motivation of this paper is precisely to fill this void, developing a global regularity theory parallel to
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
in the context of Hölder spaces.

We give a brief anticipation on the procedure that will lead us to that result.

As usually done in this context, we will first lift
\mathcal{L}
to a similar operator
\mathcal{\widetilde{L}}
on a higher dimensional space, having the algebraic structure of Carnot group (see Theorem
1.3
); then prove global Schauder estimates in this richer framework, which is easier in view of the underlying group structure.
The idea is to use integral representation formulas that allow to express any smooth function and its derivatives in terms of the fundamental solution of the “model” operator obtained by “freezing” the coefficients of
\mathcal{\widetilde{L}}
at a fixed point of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The existence of the latter, as well as its powerful properties, are the result of a deep theory about sublaplacians on Carnot groups, started by Folland
[
13
]
and then enriched by Bramanti, Brandolini
[
9
]
.

In the end of the paper we will finally remove the group structure and extend our result to the original class of operators (
1.1
), by means of the global lifting method by Biagi, Bonfiglioli
[
1
]
, which is an adaptation of the classical by Folland
[
12
]
; we will also exploit some relations between Hölder spaces in the original setting and in the lifted framework, which we owe to
[
10
]
.

Let us clarify the theoretical setting. We assume that
X_{1},\,...,\,X_{m}
are smooth vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(n>m)
with the following properties:

(H1)

X_{1},\,...,\,X_{m}
are
1
-homogeneous w.r.t. a family of non-isotropic dilations
\{\delta_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>0}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which act as

\delta_{\lambda}(x)=(\lambda^{\sigma_{1}}x_{1},\,...,\,\lambda^{\sigma_{n}}x_{n})\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for suitable integers}\>1=\sigma_{1}\leq...\leq\sigma_{n}.

Explicitly, this means that for every smooth function
f
it holds

X_{i}(f\circ\delta_{\lambda})=\lambda(X_{i}f)\circ\delta_{\lambda}\quad\forall 1\leq i\leq m,\,\lambda>0.

(H2)

X_{1},\,...,\,X_{m}
are linearly independent (as vector fields) and satisfy Hörmander’s rank condition in the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Remark 1.1
.

(H1) and (H2) imply that
Hörmander’s rank condition for
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
actually holds in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see
[
2
, Remark 3.2]
for more details).

Keeping the notation above, we denote by

q:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}

the
homogeneous dimension
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
w.r.t.
\delta_{\lambda}
, which we assume to be strictly greater than
2
. We point out that this is not an actual restriction, as the only case ruled out is when
\mathcal{L}
is a uniformly elliptic operator in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, for which global regularity results are already well known.

Let
d_{X}
denote the
control distance
induced by
X_{1},\,...,\,X_{m}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see Section
2
for the precise definition).

For
\Lambda\in(0,1)
, let
\mathcal{M}_{\Lambda}
be the set of symmetric constant matrices defined as

\mathcal{M}_{\Lambda}=\{B=[b_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}:B=B^{T},\,\Lambda|\xi|^{2}\leq b^{ij}\,\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\leq\Lambda^{-1}|\xi|^{2}\>\,\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\}.

Let now
\Lambda\in(0,1),\,\alpha\in(0,1)
be fixed parameters. Consider a matrix

(H3)

[a_{ij}(x)]\in\mathcal{M}_{\Lambda}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with entries
a_{ij}
satisfying

|a_{ij}(x)-a_{ij}(x^{\prime})|\leq c\,d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})^{\alpha}\quad\forall x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,1\leq i,j\leq m

for some constant
c>0
.

To keep track of the dependence of the constants, for any
k\geq 0
we denote

||a||_{k,X}:=\max_{1\leq i,\,j\leq m}||a_{ij}||_{C^{k,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

whenever the latter is well defined, i.e. provided that
\{a_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{m}\subset C^{k,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see Section
2
for the definition of the spaces
C^{k,\alpha}_{X}(\cdot)
); when there is no risk of confusion, we will drop the “
X
” and write just
||a||_{k}
.

Under these assumptions, the operator (
1.1
) is degenerate elliptic, but the missing directions are “recovered” by commutators, in the sense of
[
14
]
. Note, however, that in view of the fact that its coefficients
a_{ij}
are not smooth, the operator
\mathcal{L}
is not hypoelliptic. For this kind of operators, we want to prove the following:

Theorem 1.2

(Global Schauder estimates)
.

Under assumptions (H1)-(H2)-(H3) on
\mathcal{L}
, there exists a positive constant
c_{0}=c_{0}(\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m},\Lambda,\alpha,||a||_{0,X})
such that for every
u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the following estimate holds:

||u||_{C^{2,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq c_{0}\,\{||\mathcal{L}u||_{C^{\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+||u||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\}.

(1.2)

If moreover we assume that, for some
k\geq 1
,
a_{ij}\in C^{k,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
1\leq i,j\leq m
, then there exists a constant
c_{k}=c(k,\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m},\Lambda,\alpha,||a||_{k,X})>0
such that, for every
u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfying
\mathcal{L}u\in C^{k,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, there hold
u\in C^{k+2,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

||u||_{C^{k+2,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq c_{k}\left\{||\mathcal{L}u||_{C^{k,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+||u||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right\}.

We stress that in the above theorem (which is the main result of the present paper) we are just requiring a homogeneity property for the Hörmander vector fields
X_{1},\,...,\,X_{m}
to hold, without however assuming the presence of any underlying group structure.

Let
\mathfrak{a}=Lie\{X_{1},\,...,\,X_{m}\}
denote the smallest Lie algebra containing
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
. Due to the homogeneous structure of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\delta_{\lambda})
we have that
\mathfrak{a}
is finite dimensional (as a real vector space), so we can set
N:=dim(\mathfrak{a})
.
Note that (H2) implies
N\geq n
, but in this paper we always assume
N>n
, ruling out just the trivial case where there already exists a group of translations that make
X_{1},\,...,\,X_{m}
left-invariant (as noted in
[
5
, Section 2]
).
Called
p:=N-n\geq 1
, we denote the points of the lifted space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
by
(x,\xi)
, with
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

We summarize the main results of the global lifting by Biagi, Bonfiglioli
[
1
]
:

Theorem 1.3

(Global lifting)
.

Under the above assumptions (of which we inherit the notation), there exists a Carnot group
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R}^{N},*,D_{\lambda})
of step
\sigma_{n}
with
m
generators
\widetilde{X}_{1},\,...,\,\widetilde{X}_{m}
, which are related to
X_{1},\,...,\,X_{m}
as follows:

\widetilde{X}_{i}=X_{i}+\sum_{j=n+1}^{N}R_{j}(x,\xi)\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{j}},

(1.3)

for suitable smooth functions
\{R_{j}\}_{j=n+1}^{N}
. Moreover, there exist integers
1\leq\tau_{1}\leq...\leq\tau_{p}
such that the “lifted” dilations
D_{\lambda}
act as

D_{\lambda}(x,\xi)=(\delta_{\lambda}(x),\delta_{\lambda}^{*}(\xi)),\quad\text{with}\>\delta_{\lambda}^{*}(\xi)=(\lambda^{\tau_{1}}\xi_{1},\,...,\,\lambda^{\tau_{p}}\xi_{p}).

For the proof see
[
1
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Remark 1.4

(Comparison with Rothschild-Stein’s lifting)
.

Let us point out some crucial differences between the lifting in Theorem
1.3
and the first famous lifting result, proved by Rothschild and Stein in
[
16
]
.

The latter states that
any
system of Hörmander vector fields can be lifted on a higher dimensional space to a system of free Hörmander vector fields, which, in turn, are locally approximated (in the sense of Taylor series) by the generators of a Carnot group; i.e., it is a very general lifting result, but holding only in local form and consisting in a two-step approximation.

Conversely, Folland’s lifting (of which Theorem
1.3
is a useful corollary) directly lifts a system of homogeneous Hörmander vector fields to a set of generators of a Carnot group, and that lifting is performed globally; this is made possible by the homogeneous structure, which makes the above lifting less general than Rothschild-Steins’s one, but also much more powerful.

Defined
\widetilde{a}_{ij}(x,\xi):=a_{ij}(x)
for every
(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the idea of the paper is to prove a result analogous to Theorem
1.2
for the “lifted” operator

\mathcal{\widetilde{L}}=\widetilde{a}^{ij}(x,\xi)\,\widetilde{X}_{i}\widetilde{X}_{j},

(1.4)

which is much nicer thanks to the presence of an underlying group structure; once we will have proved this, in Section
5
we will remove the group structure and come back to our original setting by means of (
1.3
) and some relations between original and lifted Hölder spaces.

Let us make a basic example of how the lifting procedure actually works:

Example 1.5
.

Let us consider
\mathbb{R}^{2}\ni(x,y)
, endowed with the dilations
\delta_{\lambda}(x,y)=(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}y)
. The vector fields

X_{1}=\partial_{x},\quad X_{2}=x\partial_{y}

are
1
-homogeneous w.r.t.
\delta_{\lambda}
and satisfy Hörmander’s rank condition in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, since
X_{1}=\partial_{x}
and
[X_{1},X_{2}]=\partial_{y}
obviously span
\mathbb{R}^{2}
at any point.

We lift
X_{1},\,X_{2}
to new vector fields
\widetilde{X}_{1},\,\widetilde{X}_{2}
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\ni(x,y,\xi)
as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}_{1}=X_{1}=\partial_{x},\quad\widetilde{X}_{2}=X_{2}+\partial_{\xi}=x\partial_{y}+\partial_{\xi}

and we introduce the following translations and dilations on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

\displaystyle(x,y,\xi)*(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},\xi^{\prime}):=(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime}+x\xi^{\prime}-x^{\prime}\xi,\xi+\xi^{\prime});

\displaystyle D_{\lambda}(x,y,\xi)=(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}y,\lambda\xi).

It is well known that
(\mathbb{R}^{3},*,D_{\lambda})
is a Carnot group (the so called
Heisenberg group

\mathbb{H}^{1}
) and
\widetilde{X}_{1},\,\widetilde{X}_{2}
are its generators, so
X_{1},\,X_{2}
have been lifted to generators of a Carnot group on a strictly higher dimensional space; here “lifted” means that the actions of
X_{i}
and
\widetilde{X}_{i}
on any function
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
that do not depend on the added variable
\xi
coincide.

As already mentioned above, the main step in the proof of Theorem
1.2
consists in proving an analogous result in the context of Carnot groups, namely for operators (
1.4
). Since this result may have an independent interest, we state it explicitly:

Theorem 1.6

(Global Schauder estimates in Carnot groups)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a Carnot group in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with generators
Y_{1},\,...,\,Y_{m}
and homogeneous dimension
Q>2
. Fixed
\alpha\in(0,1),\,\Lambda\in(0,1)
, consider a matrix
[a_{ij}(z)]\in\mathcal{M}_{\Lambda}
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with coefficients
a_{ij}\in C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for all
1\leq i,j\leq m
, then let
L=a^{ij}(z)\,Y_{i}Y_{j}
.

There exists a constant
c_{0}=c_{0}(\mathbb{G},\Lambda,\alpha,||a||_{0,Y})>0
such that the inequality

||u||_{C^{2,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq c_{0}\left\{||Lu||_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}+||u||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\right\}

(1.5)

holds for any function
u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

We will also prove the following version for higher order derivatives:

Theorem 1.7

(Higher order estimates in Carnot groups)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.6
and further requiring that, for some
k\geq 1
,
a_{ij}\in C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for all
1\leq i,\,j\leq m
, there exists a constant
c_{k}=c(k,\mathbb{G},\Lambda,\alpha,||a||_{k,Y})>0
s.t., for every
u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
with
Lu\in C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, there hold
u\in C^{k+2,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and

||u||_{C^{k+2,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq c_{k}\left\{||Lu||_{C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}+||u||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\right\}.

(1.6)

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Sections
2
-
4
we always work under the further assumption that a Carnot group structure is available, namely we build the tools for the proof of Theorems
1.6
and
1.7
. In particular, in section
2
we introduce basic notions and known results about Carnot groups, as well as the associated notation; we also collect some lemmas that will come at hand repeatedly during the procedure. In Section
3
we prove a local version of the desired Schauder estimate in Carnot groups, which is then turned into (
1.5
) through interpolation inequalities and cutoff functions. In section
4
, suitable representation formulas for derivatives of smooth functions are deduced, and then exploited to obtain Hölder estimates for higher order derivatives. Finally, in section
5
we extend all the above results to our original setting (that is, when no group of translations is available) by means of the abovementioned lifting method, culminating with the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

2
Preliminaries on Carnot groups

From now on until the end of Section
4
, we assume that
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is endowed with a Carnot group structure
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R}^{N},*,D_{\lambda})
, namely

(i)

*
is a Lie group law on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(to which we refer as “translation”),

(ii)

\{D_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>0}
is a family of automorphisms of
(\mathbb{R}^{N},*)
(called “dilations”), acting as

D_{\lambda}(x)=(\lambda^{\sigma_{1}}x_{1},...,\lambda^{\sigma_{N}}x_{N})\quad\text{for suitable integers}\>1=\sigma_{1}\leq...\leq\sigma_{N}.

(iii)

the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{a}
of left-invariant vector fields on
(\mathbb{R}^{N},*)
admits a stratification
\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{a}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{2}\oplus\,...\,\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{s}
, where:
\mathfrak{a}_{1}
is the span of
m
linearly independent vector fields
Y_{1},\,...,\,Y_{m}
, which are
1
-homogeneous w.r.t.
\{D_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
;
\mathfrak{a}_{k}=[\mathfrak{a}_{1},\mathfrak{a}_{k-1}]
for every
2\leq k\leq s
and
[\mathfrak{a}_{1},\mathfrak{a}_{s}]=0
.

We recall that condition (iii) can be equivalently stated by asking that there exist smooth vector fields
Y_{1},...,Y_{m}
which are left-invariant w.r.t.
*
,
1
-homogeneous w.r.t.
\{D_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
and satisfy Hörmander condition in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(that is, together with their commutators of arbitrary order, they span
\mathbb{R}^{N}
at any point of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
itself).
The vector fields
Y_{1},\,...,\,Y_{m}
are called
(Lie) generators
of
\mathbb{G}
, whereas
s\geq 1
is called
step
of
\mathbb{G}
and stands for the length of commutators of
Y_{1},\,...,\,Y_{m}
that are required to fulfill Hörmander condition at any point.
We also denote by

Q=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}

the
homogeneous dimension
of
\mathbb{G}
, which we assume to be strictly larger than
2
.

Let also
Y_{m+1},...,Y_{N}
be smooth vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
\mathcal{A}=\{Y_{1},...,Y_{N}\}
is a basis of
\mathfrak{a}
with the following structure:

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\,...\,\cup\mathcal{A}_{s}:\>\mathcal{A}_{1}=\{Y_{1},\,...,\,Y_{m}\},\>\mathcal{A}_{i}\>\text{basis of}\>\mathfrak{a}_{i}\>\forall 1\leq i\leq s.

For all
1\leq i\leq N
, we denote by
Y_{i}^{R}
the (unique) right-invariant vector field agreeing with
Y_{i}
at the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, which is clearly homogeneous of the same degree as
Y_{i}
, namely
\sigma_{i}
. Given any multindex
I=(i_{1},\,i_{2},\,...,\,i_{h})
with
i_{1},\,i_{2},\,...,\,i_{h}\in\{1,\,2,\,...,\,N\}
, we denote by
Y_{I}
the differential operator
Y_{i_{1}}\,Y_{i_{2}}\,...\,Y_{i_{h}}
; analogously,
Y_{I}^{R}
stands for
Y_{i_{1}}^{R}\,...\,Y_{i_{h}}^{R}
. According to the usual notation, we also denote by
l(I)=h
the length of
I
and by
|I|=\sigma_{i_{1}}+...+\sigma_{i_{h}}
its “weight” (that is, the degree of homogeneity of the operator
Y_{I}
); we point out that, whenever a multindex
I
has entries only in
\{1,...,m\}
(which will almost always be our case) the notions of length and weight coincide, thus we will just employ the symbol
|I|
. For homogeneity of notation, when convenient we will use the symbol
Y_{I}
also in the case
|I|=0
, implicitly meaning that
Y_{I}
is the identity operator, i.e.
Y_{I}u\equiv u
for any function
u
.

We recall some useful results based on invariance and homogeneity:

Proposition 2.1
.

For all
1\leq i\leq N
and functions
f,\,g:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
for which the following integrals converge absolutely, there holds:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(Y_{i}^{R}f)(y^{-1}*x)\,g(y)\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(y^{-1}*x)\,(Y_{i}g)(y)\,dy.

(2.1)

Moreover, if
f
is a smooth function, homogeneous of degree
\beta\geq 0
, and
j\in\{1,\,...,\,N\}
is such that
\sigma_{j}>\beta
, then
Y_{j}
and
f
commute, namely

Y_{j}(fg)=f\cdot Y_{j}g\hskip 14.22636pt\forall g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

(2.2)

Proof.

For the proof of (
2.1
) see
[
9
, Proposition 3.47]
. As for (
2.2
), note that for all
k\in\{1,...,N\}
with
\sigma_{k}\geq\sigma_{j}
the function
\partial_{x_{k}}f
is smooth and homogeneous of degree
\beta-\sigma_{k}<0
, thus it identically vanishes (otherwise it would be unbounded at the origin, contradicting the smoothness assumption on
f
). On the other side, denoting
Y_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{ij}(x)\,\partial_{x_{i}}
, the
\sigma_{j}
-homogeneity of
Y_{j}
implies the
(\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j})
-homogeneity of
b_{ij}
(for any
i
), which in turn yields
b_{ij}\equiv 0
for all
i\in\{1,...,N\}
s.t.
\sigma_{i}<\sigma_{j}
. Therefore
b_{ij}\not\equiv 0
only if
\sigma_{i}\geq\sigma_{j}>\beta
, so

(Y_{j}f)(x)=\sum_{i:\,\sigma_{i}\geq\sigma_{j}}b_{ij}(x)\,\partial_{x_{i}}f(x)=0\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

and the thesis follows by Leibniz rule.
∎

For the sake of developing a Schauder theory in Hölder spaces, we must define suitable Hölder spaces that are coherent with our group structure. To this aim, we recall that a Carnot group can be “naturally” endowed with a distance, that is the so called
control distance
induced by the generators
Y_{1},...,Y_{m}
, of which we now briefly give the definition.

Definition 2.2
.

Denoted by
C_{x,y}(\delta)
the set of absolutely continuous curves
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
\gamma(0)=x,\,\gamma(1)=y
and

\gamma^{\prime}(t)=\alpha^{i}(t)\,(Y_{i})_{\gamma(t)}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for a.e. }t\in(0,1),\,\text{with}\,||\alpha^{i}||_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}<\delta\>\,\,\forall 1\leq i\leq m

(for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\delta>0
), the
control distance
(also called
Carnot-Caratheodory distance
, or in short
CC distance
) induced by
Y_{1},...,Y_{m}
is defined as the map
d_{Y}(x,y):=\inf\{\delta>0:C_{x,y}(\delta)\neq\emptyset\}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Whenever
Y_{1},...,Y_{m}
satisfy Hörmander condition in the whole space, it is well known (see
[
9
, Theorems 1.45-3.54]
) that
d_{Y}
is indeed a distance on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, as well as that it is left-invariant w.r.t. translations induced by
*
and
1
-homogeneous w.r.t. dilations
D_{\lambda}
(i.e., coherent with the group structure), namely:

\displaystyle d_{Y}(z*x,z*y)=d_{Y}(x,y)\hskip 14.22636pt\forall x,\,y,\,z\in\mathbb{R}^{N};

(2.3)

\displaystyle d_{Y}(D_{\lambda}(x),D_{\lambda}(y))=\lambda\,d_{Y}(x,y)\hskip 14.22636pt\forall x,\,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\lambda>0.

(2.4)

It is straightforward that the induced map
||x||_{Y}:=d_{Y}(x,0)
is a
homogeneous norm
on
\mathbb{G}
, according to the following definition:

Definition 2.3
.

A
homogeneous norm
on the Carnot group
\mathbb{G}
is a continuous map
||\cdot||:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to[0,+\infty)
satisfying the following properties:

(i)

||x||=0\iff x=0
;

(ii)

||D_{\lambda}(x)||=\lambda||x||
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\lambda>0
;

(iii)

there exists a constant
c\geq 1
such that
||x||\leq c||x^{-1}||
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
;

(iv)

there exists a constant
\kappa\geq 1
for which
||x*y||\leq\kappa(||x||+||y||)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
(
quasi-triangle inequality
).

We point out, however, that the CC distance
d_{Y}
(and hence also
||\cdot||_{Y}
) need not be smooth, which could lead to regularity issues. For this reason, we introduce a smooth homogeneous norm equivalent to the latter:

||\cdot||:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to[0,+\infty),\quad||x||=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}|x_{i}|^{2\sigma_{N}!/\sigma_{i}}\right)^{1/(2\sigma_{N}!)},

(2.5)

where the
\sigma_{i}
’s are the exponents of the dilations
\{D_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>0}
.

It is immediate to check that the above is indeed a homogeneous norm, smooth outside the origin. The equivalence of
||\cdot||_{Y}
and
||\cdot||
follows by the equivalence of any couple of homogeneous norms on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(see
[
9
, Theorem 3.12]
).

We will perform most of our computations w.r.t. the norm
||\cdot||
, and then “translate” the results in terms of the CC norm and distance just by equivalence. The
quasisymmetric quasidistance
induced by
||\cdot||
and the associated balls will be respectively denoted by
d(\cdot,\cdot)
, which acts as

d(x,y):=||y^{-1}*x||,\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

and
B_{r}(x_{0})
(for any
r>0
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
).
We recall that
d(\cdot,\cdot)
has the following properties:

(i’)

d(x,y)\geq 0
for every
x,\,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
d(x,y)=0\iff x=y
;

(ii’)

d(D_{\lambda}(x),D_{\lambda}(y))=\lambda d(x,y)
for all
x,\,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\lambda>0
;

(iii’)

there exists
c\geq 1
such that
d(x,y)\leq cd(y,x)
for all
x,\,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
;

(iv’)

there exists a constant
\kappa\geq 1
such that
d(x,y)\leq\kappa(d(x,z)+d(z,y))
for all
x,\,y,\,z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
(
quasi-triangle inequality
).

((i’)-(iii’)-(iv’) being the usual definition of quasisymmetric quasidistance, (ii’) following directly by homogeneity of
||\cdot||
).

We recall a sort of “Lagrange theorem” holding true in Carnot groups, w.r.t. any homogeneous quasisymmetric quasidistance:

Lemma 2.4
.

There exists a constant
\overline{c}=\overline{c}(\mathbb{G})>0
such that for every
u\in C^{1}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and every
x,\,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
there holds

|u(x)-u(x^{\prime})|\leq\overline{c}\,d(x,x^{\prime})\,\sup_{d(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\leq\overline{c}d(x,x^{\prime})}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|Y_{i}u(x^{\prime\prime})|.

For the proof see
[
8
, Lemma 7.6]
.

We now introduce Hölder spaces induced by our generators
Y_{1},...,Y_{m}
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
k\geq 1
be an integer. We denote by
C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)\equiv C^{0,\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)
the set of bounded functions
u
over
\Omega
that satisfy

|u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)}:=\sup_{x,\,x^{\prime}\in\Omega,\,x\neq x^{\prime}}\frac{|u(x)-u(x^{\prime})|}{d_{Y}(x,x^{\prime})^{\alpha}}<+\infty,

and by
C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)
the set of functions
u\in C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)
such that, for every
|I|\leq k
, the directional derivative
Y_{I}u
exists (pointwise) and belongs to
C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)
.
For such functions, we also denote:

\displaystyle||u||_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)}:=||u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+|u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)};

\displaystyle||u||_{C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)}:=||u||_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)}+\sum_{1\leq|I|\leq k}||Y_{I}u||_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)}.

Finally, the notation
C^{k,\alpha}_{0,Y}(\Omega)
will stand for the space of functions
u\in C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)
compactly supported in
\Omega
.

Notation 2.6
.

When convenient, we will use the following compact notation:

\displaystyle||D^{k}u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}:=\sum_{|I|=k}||Y_{I}u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)};\quad|D^{k}u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)}:=\sum_{|I|=k}|Y_{I}u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)};

\displaystyle||D^{k}u||_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)}:=\sum_{|I|=k}||Y_{I}u||_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)}

(for any
k\geq 1
, provided all the above quantities are well-defined).

Fixed
\Lambda\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let
A(x)=[a_{ij}(x)]\in\mathcal{M}_{\Lambda}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with entries
a_{ij}\in C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for any
1\leq i,j\leq m
; we denote

||a||_{0,Y}:=\max_{1\leq i,\,j\leq m}||a_{ij}||_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})};\quad||a||_{k,Y}:=\max_{1\leq i,\,j\leq m}||a_{ij}||_{C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})},\>k\geq 1

(whenever the above quantities are well defined).
We focus on the operator

L=a^{ij}(x)\,Y_{i}Y_{j}.

(2.6)

For any
k\geq 0
, it is well known
C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)
is a vector space and moreover, given
f,\,g\in C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)
, there holds
fg\in C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)
and

||fg||_{C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)}\leq||f||_{C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)}||g||_{C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)}.

(2.7)

An immediate consequence is that
Lu\in C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)
whenever
u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)
. Furthermore, for any
u\in C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\eta))
vanishing at a point
z\in B_{R}(\eta)
, it holds

|u(x)|=|u(x)-u(z)|\leq c\,d(x,z)^{\alpha}|u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\eta))}\leq c\,R^{\alpha}|u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\eta))},

and so, for whatever fixed
R_{0}
and any
0<R\leq R_{0}
:

\displaystyle||u||_{L^{\infty}(B_{R}(\eta))}\leq cR^{\alpha}|u|_{{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\eta))}};\quad||u||_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\eta))}\leq c^{\prime}(R_{0})|u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\eta))}.

(2.8)

Given
\overline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, let us consider the following operator with constant coefficients
a_{ij}
(obtained by “freezing” the coefficients
a_{ij}(\cdot)
of
L
in
\overline{x}
):

\displaystyle L_{\overline{x}}=a^{ij}(\overline{x})\,Y_{i}Y_{j}.

(2.9)

It is immediate that also
L_{\overline{x}}u\in C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)
for every
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N},\,u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{Y}(\Omega)
.

We stress that
L_{\overline{x}}
belongs to a class of operators for which many results are well known in literature, namely
L_{\overline{x}}
is a
sublaplacian
on the Carnot group
\mathbb{G}
(modulo a change of variables): indeed, denoting by
B=[b_{ij}(\overline{x})]_{i,j}
the unique symmetric positive-definite matrix such that
B^{2}=A(\overline{x})
and setting

W_{i}:=b^{ij}(\overline{x})\,Y_{j}\hskip 14.22636pt1\leq i\leq m,

we get
L_{\overline{x}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}W_{i}^{2}
, being
W_{1},\,...,\,W_{m}
Lie-generators of
\mathbb{G}
by construction. Then the following deep result holds true for
L_{\overline{x}}
:

Theorem 2.7

(Fundamental solution of
L_{\overline{x}}
)
.

L_{\overline{x}}
admits a
fundamental solution

\Gamma_{\overline{x}}
, namely a function
\Gamma_{\overline{x}}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}
that admits continuous derivatives up to order
2
along
\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
(in its domain of definition), solves the equation
L_{\overline{x}}u=0
outside the origin and satisfy

u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Gamma_{\overline{x}}(y^{-1}*x)\,L_{\overline{x}}u(y)\,dy\quad\forall u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

(2.10)

Moreover,
\Gamma_{\overline{x}}
is smooth outside the origin,
(2-Q)
-homogeneous and satisfies the following uniform estimates:

\Theta_{\Gamma_{\overline{x}},p}:=\sup_{|I|\leq p}\,\sup_{||w||=1}|Y_{I}\Gamma_{\overline{x}}(w)|\leq c(\mathbb{G},\Lambda,p)\hskip 14.22636pt\forall p\geq 0.

For the proof see
[
9
, Theorem 6.18]
. Actually, we remark that the representation formula (
2.10
) holds true more in general for any
u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{0,Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, as can be shown via an approximation argument in Carnot groups (see
[
9
, Proposition 8.49]
).

We point out that the reason why
L_{\overline{x}}
plays an important role in our procedure is that, in view of the Hölder-continuity of the coefficients
a_{ij}
, the operator
L_{\overline{x}}
is a “good approximation” (at least locally, near
\overline{x}
) of our original operator
L
, in the sense that the approximation error
L_{\overline{x}}-L
is “small”, in a suitable sense. What we mean by that is made precise by the statement of the following lemma, which gives a quantitative control on the action of
L_{\overline{x}}-L
:

Lemma 2.8
.

For every
R>0
,
\overline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{0,Y}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))
it holds

\displaystyle|(L_{\overline{x}}-L)u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))}\leq c(\alpha,||a||_{0})\,R^{\alpha}|D^{2}u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))}.

Further requiring
a_{ij}\in C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))
,
k\geq 1
for any
1\leq i,\,j\leq m
, then for every
u\in C^{k+2,\alpha}_{0,Y}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))
and
|I|=k
we have

\displaystyle|Y_{I}(L_{\overline{x}}-L)u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))}\leq c(\alpha,||a||_{k})\left\{R^{\alpha}|D^{k+2}u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))}+||u||_{C^{k+1}_{Y}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))}\right\}.

Proof.

Recalling the definitions (
2.6
),(
2.9
), for any
x,y\in B_{R}(\overline{x})
one finds

\displaystyle(L_{\overline{x}}-L)u(x)-(L_{\overline{x}}-L)u(y)=[a^{hl}(\overline{x})-a^{hl}(x)]\times

\displaystyle\times[Y_{h}Y_{l}u(x)-Y_{h}Y_{l}u(y)]+[a^{hl}(y)-a^{hl}(x)]\,Y_{h}Y_{l}u(x^{\prime})\quad\forall x,\,y\in B_{R}(\overline{x}).

By Hölder continuity of
\{a_{hl}\}_{h,l}
and equivalence between
d_{Y}
and
d

|a^{hl}(x_{1})-a^{hl}(x_{2})|\leq c(||a||_{0})\,d(x_{1},x_{2})^{\alpha}\hskip 14.22636pt\forall x_{1},\,x_{2}\in B_{R}(\overline{x}),\,1\leq h,l\leq m

Moreover, since
Y_{h}Y_{l}u\in C^{\alpha}_{0,Y}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))
, by (
2.8
) we have

\displaystyle|Y_{h}Y_{l}u(y)|\leq||Y_{h}Y_{l}u||_{L^{\infty}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))}\leq c(\alpha)R^{\alpha}|Y_{h}Y_{l}u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))}

(2.11)

hence

\displaystyle|(L_{\overline{x}}-L)u(x)-(L_{\overline{x}}-L)u(y)|\leq c(\alpha,||a||_{0})d(x,y)^{\alpha}R^{\alpha}|D^{2}u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))}

and the first claim follows just by definition of the Hölder seminorm
|\cdot|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}}
.

Let us turn to the second part.
Fixed an integer
k\geq 1
and a multindex
I
with
|I|=k
, by iterated Leibniz rule we get

\displaystyle Y_{I}(L_{\overline{x}}-L)u(x)=\sum_{J\subseteq I}\,Y_{I\setminus J}\left(a^{hl}(\overline{x})-a^{hl}(x)\right)\,Y_{J}Y_{h}Y_{l}u(x),

where both
J
and
I\setminus J
are meant as ordered submultindex of
I
, and also the case
J=\emptyset
is considered. Then

\displaystyle Y_{I}(L_{\overline{x}}-L)u(x)-Y_{I}(L_{\overline{x}}-L)u(y)=\sum_{J\subseteq I}\{Y_{I\setminus J}(a^{hl}(\overline{x})-a^{hl}(x))\times

\displaystyle\times\left[Y_{J}Y_{h}Y_{l}u(x)-Y_{J}Y_{h}Y_{l}u(y)\right]+(Y_{I\setminus J}a^{hl}(y)-Y_{I\setminus J}a^{hl}(x))\,Y_{J}Y_{h}Y_{l}u(y)\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{J\subseteq I}\{A_{J}^{(1)}(x,y)+A_{J}^{(2)}(x,y)\}.

(2.12)

To shorten the notation, let us denote
B_{R}(\overline{x})
just by
B_{R}
. As for
A_{J}^{(1)}
:

\displaystyle|A_{I}^{(1)}(x,y)|\leq d(x,y)^{\alpha}|a^{hl}|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R})}d(x,\overline{x})^{\alpha}|Y_{I}Y_{h}Y_{l}u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R})}

\displaystyle\quad\leq c(||a||_{0})\,R^{\alpha}\,d(x,y)^{\alpha}|D^{k+2}u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R})};

\displaystyle|A_{J}^{(1)}(x,y)|\leq||Y_{I\setminus J}a^{hl}||_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}\,d(x,y)^{\alpha}|Y_{J}Y_{h}Y_{l}u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R})}

\displaystyle\quad\leq c(||a||_{k})\,d(x,y)^{\alpha}||u||_{C^{k+1,\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R})}\quad\forall J\subsetneq I.

Similarly, with also the aid of (
2.8
), for any
J\subseteq I
we obtain

\displaystyle|A_{J}^{(2)}(x,y)|\leq|Y_{I\setminus J}a^{hl}|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R})}\,d(x,y)^{\alpha}||Y_{J}Y_{h}Y_{l}u||_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}\leq|Y_{I\setminus J}a^{hl}|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R})}\times

\displaystyle\times d(x,y)^{\alpha}|Y_{J}Y_{h}Y_{l}u|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{R})}\leq c(\alpha,||a||_{k})\,R^{\alpha}\,d(x,y)^{\alpha}|D^{k+2}u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R})}.

Plugging the above estimates in (
2.12
) one gets

\displaystyle|Y_{I}(L_{\overline{x}}-L)u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R})}\leq c(\alpha,||a||_{k})\left\{R^{\alpha}|D^{k+2}u|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R})}+||u||_{C^{k+1,\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R})}\right\}.

∎

We will also exploit the following abstract result, that holds for any
(2-Q)
-homogeneous function that is smooth outside the origin. Note that, by Theorem
2.7
, the fundamental solution
\Gamma_{\overline{x}}
of the operator

has these properties, hence everything in the following theorem holds true in particular for
\Gamma_{\overline{x}}
.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\})
be homogeneous of degree
2-Q
, and

\Theta_{f,k}:=\sup_{|I|\leq k}\,\sup_{||w||=1}|Y_{I}f(w)|.

(2.13)

for any
k\geq 0
. Then, for any
1\leq h,\,l\leq m
, there holds

\int_{r<||y||<R}Y_{h}Y_{l}f(y)\,dy=0\quad\forall R>r>0,

(2.14)

and for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\phi\in C^{2,\alpha}_{0,Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
we have

Y_{l}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(y^{-1}*x)\,\phi(y)\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}Y_{l}f(y^{-1}*x)\,\phi(y)\,dy;

(2.15)

\displaystyle Y_{h}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}Y_{l}f(y^{-1}*x)\,\phi(y)\,dy=P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}Y_{h}Y_{l}f(y^{-1}*x)\,\phi(y)\,dy+c_{hl}\phi(x)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{d(x,y)>\epsilon}Y_{h}Y_{l}f(y^{-1}*x)\,\phi(y)\,dy+c_{hl}\phi(x)

(2.16)

with

(2.17)

being

the outer normal vector to the surface
\{||w||=1\}
. Moreover, for any

, there exist constants

and

s.t.

|Y_{h}Y_{l}f(x)|\leq c\,||x||^{-Q}\quad\forall||x||\leq R_{0},

(2.18)

and for every

satisfying

and

we have

|Y_{h}Y_{l}f(y*x)-Y_{h}Y_{l}f(x)|\leq c\,||y||\cdot||x||^{-Q-1}.

(2.19)

For the proof of (

) see
[
9
, Theorem 6.31]
; (

) is straightforward from (

) by dominated convergence, since

. With (

) at hand, see
[

, Theorem 6.33]
for (

)-(
2.17
) under the stronger assumption that

), and

We conclude this section with some useful comments on the above theorem. First, the vanishing property (

) actually makes the integral of
Y_{i}Y_{j}f
(with

as in Theorem

) vanish not only on spherical shells, but on several type of radially symmetric domains, indeed:

P.V.\int_{||x||<r}Y_{i}Y_{j}f(x)\,dx=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\epsilon<||x||<r}Y_{i}Y_{j}f(x)\,dx=0\hskip 14.22636pt\forall r>0.

(2.20)

Moreover, from dominated convergence (based on (

)), there holds

\displaystyle\int_{||\eta||=1}(Y_{i}Y_{j}f)(\eta)\,dS(\eta)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{1-\epsilon<||x||<1+\epsilon}(Y_{i}Y_{j}f)(x)\,dx=0

and consequently, for any radial smooth function

with compact support, by

-homogeneity of

we have

\displaystyle\int_{||w||>\epsilon}(\psi Y_{i}Y_{j}f)(w)\,dw=\int_{||w||>\epsilon}\Psi(||w||)\,Y_{i}Y_{j}f(D_{||w||^{-1}}(w))\,||w||^{-Q}\,dw

\displaystyle=\int_{\epsilon}^{+\infty}\Psi(\rho)\,\rho^{-Q}\int_{||\eta||=1}Y_{i}Y_{j}f(\eta)\,dS(\eta)=0\quad\forall\epsilon>0,

hence by taking the limit as

\displaystyle P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(\psi Y_{i}Y_{j}f)(w)\,dw=0.

(2.22)

Furthermore, in force of (

) and (

), we have the following control on

(here below we set

\displaystyle|c_{hl}|\leq\Theta_{f,1}\int_{||w||=1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|b_{i}(w)|\,dw\leq\Theta_{f,1}\,\omega_{Q}\sum_{k=1}^{N}||b_{i}||_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1}(0))}=c(\mathbb{G})\,\Theta_{f,1},

where

denotes the measure of the unit ball

3
Global Schauder estimates in Carnot groups

3.1
Local estimates for second order derivatives

The first step towards the proof of Theorem
1.6
consists in showing a similar estimate in local form, namely just for functions
u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
with “small” compact support. Specifically, we begin by proving the following:

Theorem 3.1

(Local Schauder estimates in Carnot groups)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.6
, there exist positive constants
c_{0}
and
R_{0}
, depending only on
\mathbb{G},\,\Lambda,\,\alpha,\,||a||_{0}
, such that for every
0<R\leq R_{0}
and for every
u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
with
supp(u)\subseteq B_{R}(\overline{x})
the following inequality holds:

||D^{2}u||_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))}\leq c_{0}\,|Lu|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))}.

(3.1)

For a reason that will become clear later, we prove the following general form of the above theorem:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\})
be a
(2-Q)
-homogeneous function, let
1\leq h,\,l\leq m
and
T_{f}
be the operator defined as

(T_{f}\phi)(x):=Y_{h}Y_{l}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(y^{-1}*x)\,\phi(y)\,dy\hskip 14.22636pt\forall\phi\in C^{\alpha}_{0,Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Then there exists a constant
c_{0}=c_{0}(\mathbb{G},\alpha,\Theta_{f,3})>0
such that for every
0<R\leq 1
,
\overline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\phi\in C^{\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
with
supp(\phi)\subset B_{R}(\overline{x})
it holds

||T_{f}\phi||_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))}\leq c_{0}\,|\phi|_{C^{\alpha}_{Y}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))}.

For the proof, we will exploit multiple times the following result:

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
\beta>0
, there exists a constant
c=c(\beta)>0
such that for all
r_{2}>r_{1}>0
there hold

\displaystyle\int_{d(x,y)>r_{2}}d(x,y)^{-\beta-Q}\,dy\leq cr_{2}^{-\beta};

\displaystyle\int_{r_{1}<d(x,y)<r_{2}}d(x,y)^{-Q}\,dy\leq c\,\log\left(\frac{r_{2}}{r_{1}}\right);

\displaystyle\int_{d(x,y)<r_{1}}d(x,y)^{\beta-Q}\,dy\leq cr_{1}^{\beta}.

For the proof see

Now we prove the properties of

by induction on

, the case

. Fixed

, assume the thesis holds true for

. The smoothness of

As for the uniform estimates (

\displaystyle Y_{H}\Gamma_{\overline{x}}^{I;J}=r_{i_{1}j_{1}}Y_{H}\Gamma_{\overline{x}}^{I\setminus\{i_{1}\};J\setminus\{j_{1}\}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{h=m+1}^{N}\,\theta^{j_{1},l_{2}...,l_{\sigma_{h}}}Y_{H}Y_{l_{2}}^{R}...Y_{l_{\sigma_{h}}}^{R}(r_{i_{1}h}\Gamma_{\overline{x}}^{I\setminus\{i_{1}\};J\setminus\{j_{1}\}}).

∎

It follows that, by analogy with the proof of Theorem

Theorem 4.3

(4.5)

(Global regularity result)

The proof is strongly based on the following deep theorem (see

Theorem 4.5
.

Let

be a system of Hörmander vector fields in a bounded domain

, let

, nonnegative integer

,

, for any

For the sake of completeness, we point out that the cited theorem is much more general than what we actually need, since no underlying algebraic structure is required for the system
\{X_{i}\}_{i}
; moreover, the original theorem in
[

]
also gives a local estimate of

and

makes it unsuitable for our purposes. Instead, we will rely on the global estimate (

To prove Theorem
4.4
, we need also the following result about radial cutoff functions:

Lemma 4.6
.

For any
R\geq 1
and any positive integer

, there exists a radial function
\phi_{R}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N};[0,1])
such that
\phi_{R}\equiv 1
in

outside

and all derivatives up to order

We show that the following function does the job:

\phi_{R}(x)=\begin{cases}1\hskip 56.9055pt\text{if}\>||x||\leq R\\
e^{\frac{1}{||x||-2R}+\frac{1}{R}}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{if}\>R<||x||<2R\\

Everything except the

Let us prove that for any (non-empty) multindex

there holds

Y_{I}\phi_{R}(x)=\phi_{R}(x)\sum_{2\leq h\leq 2k}\,\sum_{1-k\leq p\leq 0}\frac{f_{h,p,I}(x)}{(||x||-2R)^{h}}\quad\>R<||x||<2R,

for suitable functions
\{f_{h,p,I}\}_{h,p}
, each smooth in
\{R<||x||<2R\}
and homogeneous of some nonpositive degree
p
. We proceed by induction on

; if
k=1
, i.e.
Y_{I}=Y_{i}
for some

, we have (for

)

\displaystyle Y_{i}\phi_{R}(x)=\phi_{R}(x)Y_{i}\left(\frac{1}{||x||-2R}\right)=-\frac{\phi_{R}(x)}{(||x||-2R)^{2}}Y_{i}||x||.

By definition (
2.3
) and

, it is clear that

is homogeneous of degree

, assume the thesis holds for every

\displaystyle Y_{I^{\prime}}\phi_{R}(x)=\sum_{2\leq h\leq 2k,\,1-k\leq p\leq 0}Y_{i}\left(\phi_{R}(x)\frac{f_{h,p,I}(x)}{(||x||-2R)^{h}}\right)=

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{h,p}\left\{\frac{Y_{i}\phi_{R}(x)f_{h,p,I}(x)}{(||x||-2R)^{h}}+\frac{\phi_{R}(x)Y_{i}f_{h,p,I}(x)}{(||x||-2R)^{h}}-h\frac{\phi_{R}(x)f_{h,p,I}(x)}{(||x||-2R)^{h+1}}Y_{i}||x||\right\}

; moreover
u\in C^{k+1,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
by inductive assumption, so the whole right-hand side is finite (and independent of
R
). Taking the limit as
R\to+\infty
:

||u||_{C^{k+2,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq c_{k}\,\left\{||Lu||_{C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}+||u||_{C^{k+1,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\right\}<+\infty,

thus
u\in C^{k+2,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
∎

Combining Theorems
4.3
and
4.4
we readily get Theorem
1.7
.

5
Removal of the group structure

Let us return to our original framework, with
X_{1},\,...,\,X_{m}
homogeneous (but not left-invariant) Hörmander vector fields on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\delta_{\lambda})
and
A=A(x)
satisfying (H3) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some constants
\Lambda\in(0,1),\,\alpha\in(0,1),\,||a||_{0,X}>0
; let also
\widetilde{X}_{1},\,...,\,\widetilde{X}_{m}
and
\widetilde{A}=\widetilde{A}(x,\xi)
be their lifted versions, in the sense of Theorem
1.3
.

Since
\widetilde{X}_{1},\,...,\,\widetilde{X}_{m}
are Lie generators of a Carnot group
\mathbb{G}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the theory developed in Sections
2
-
4
applies to the operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}=\widetilde{a}^{ij}(\cdot)\,\widetilde{X}_{i}\widetilde{X}_{j}
, namely there is a constant
c_{0}=c_{0}(\mathbb{G},\Lambda,\alpha,||a||_{0})>0
s.t. for any
v\in C^{2,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
there holds

||v||_{C^{2,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq c_{0}\left\{||\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}v||_{C^{\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}+||v||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\right\}

(5.1)

and, if moreover
\widetilde{a}_{ij}\in C^{k,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
k\geq 1
for all
1\leq i,j\leq m
, then there exists a constant
c_{k}=c(k,\,\mathbb{G},\,\Lambda,\,\alpha,\,||\widetilde{a}||_{k})>0
such that for any
v\in C^{2,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
with
\mathcal{\widetilde{L}}v\in C^{k,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
there holds
v\in C^{k+2,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and

||v||_{C^{k+2,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq c_{k}\left\{||\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}v||_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}+||v||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\right\}.

Denoted by
d_{X}
and
d_{\widetilde{X}}
the CC distances induced by
X_{1},\,...,\,X_{m}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and by
\widetilde{X}_{1},\,...,\,\widetilde{X}_{m}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(respectively), in order to deduce Theorem
1.2
from (
5.1
) we need a couple of results (Lemma
5.1
and Lemma
5.2
below) relating Hölder seminorms in the original space and in the lifted space.

In the following, we denote by
E:C(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C(\mathbb{R}^{N})
the “extension” operator
(Ef)(x,\xi):=f(x)
. We point out that, although in principle
X_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq m
) is a vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can equivalently think of it as a vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
which does not act on the variables
\xi_{1},\,...,\,\xi_{p}
; with this notation, it is immediate that
X_{i}
and
E
commute, namely
\widetilde{X}_{i}(Ef)=E(X_{i}f)
for all
1\leq i\leq m
, and recursively
\widetilde{X}_{I}(Ef)=E(X_{I}f)
for any multindex
I
(for all
f
smooth enough).

Lemma 5.1
.

If
f\in C^{\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
\widetilde{f}:=Ef\in C^{\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and

\displaystyle||\widetilde{f}||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}=||f||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\quad|\widetilde{f}|_{C^{\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq|f|_{C^{\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

For the proof see
[
9
, Proposition 10.39]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

If a continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is such that
\widetilde{f}\in C^{\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, then
f\in C^{\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and there exists a constant
c>0
(independent of
f
) such that

||f||_{C^{\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq c\,||\widetilde{f}||_{C^{\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}.

Proof.

For any fixed
R>0
and
(\overline{x},\overline{\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we have (cf
[
10
, Proposition 8.3]
)

|f|_{C^{\alpha}_{X}(B_{R}(\overline{x}))}\leq c\,|\widetilde{f}|_{C^{\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(B_{R}(\overline{x},\overline{\xi}))}\leq c\,|\widetilde{f}|_{C^{\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}

for some constant
c>0
that does not depend on
f
nor
R
nor
(\overline{x},\overline{\xi})
). Then, given a covering
\{B_{R}(\overline{x_{i}})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there holds

|f|_{C^{\alpha}_{X}(B_{R}(\overline{x_{i}}))}\leq c\,|\widetilde{f}|_{C^{\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\hskip 14.22636pt\forall i\in\mathbb{N}

with
c
independent of
i
by construction, which clearly yields

||f||_{C^{\alpha}_{X}(B_{R}(\overline{x_{i}}))}\leq c\,||\widetilde{f}||_{C^{\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\hskip 14.22636pt\forall i\in\mathbb{N}

(with
c
independent on
i
). Taking the supremum on
i
, we get

||f||_{C^{\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq c\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}||f||_{C^{\alpha}_{X}(B_{R}(\overline{x_{i}}))}\leq c^{\prime}\,||\widetilde{f}||_{C^{\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}.

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Given
u\in C^{k+2,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
\widetilde{u}:=E(u)\in C^{k+2,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and

\displaystyle||u||_{C^{k+2,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq c\,||\widetilde{u}||_{C^{k+2,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})},

(5.2)

\displaystyle||\mathcal{\widetilde{L}}\widetilde{u}||_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R^{N})}}\leq||\mathcal{L}u||_{C^{k,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

(5.3)

where
c>0
is the constant of Lemma
5.2
.

Proof.

Since
\widetilde{X}_{I}\widetilde{u}=E(X_{I}u)
for all
|I|\leq k+2
, by Lemma
5.1
we have
\widetilde{u}\in C^{k+2,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
; hence Lemma
5.2
applies for any
Y_{I}u,\,|I|\leq k+2
, yielding

\displaystyle||u||_{C^{k+2,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\sum_{|I|\leq k+2}||X_{I}u||_{C^{\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\quad\leq c\sum_{|I|\leq k+2}||\widetilde{X}_{I}\widetilde{u}||_{C^{\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}=c\,||\widetilde{u}||_{C^{k+2,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}.

Since
\widetilde{a}_{ij}:=E(a_{ij})
then the commutation of
\{Y_{i}\}_{i}
with the extension
E
gives also
\mathcal{\widetilde{L}}\widetilde{u}=E(\mathcal{L}u)
, so by Lemma
5.1
we immediately get (
5.3
).
∎

Finally:

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Given
k\geq 0
, let
u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, let
\widetilde{u}=E(u)
and let us assume that
a_{ij}\in C^{k,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
1\leq i,j\leq m
and
\mathcal{L}u\in C^{k,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(which automatically holds for
k=0
, in view of (H3)). For
k\geq 1
, by Lemma
5.1
we have
\widetilde{a}_{ij}\in C^{k,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
\widetilde{u}\in C^{2,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
\mathcal{\widetilde{L}}\widetilde{u}\in C^{k,\alpha}_{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
; so
\widetilde{u}\in C^{k+2,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and (by either Theorem
1.6
if
k=0
or
1.7
if
k\geq 1
) we have

||\widetilde{u}||_{C^{k+2,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq c(k,\mathbb{G},\Lambda,\alpha,||a||_{k})\left\{||\mathcal{\widetilde{L}}\widetilde{u}||_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}+||\widetilde{u}||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\right\}.

(5.4)

Then, in view of Lemma
5.3
, we get
u\in C^{k+2,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

\displaystyle||u||_{C^{k+2,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq||\widetilde{u}||_{C^{k+2,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq c(k,\mathbb{G},\Lambda,\alpha,||a||_{k})\{||\mathcal{\widetilde{L}}\widetilde{u}||_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\widetilde{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}+

\displaystyle+||\widetilde{u}||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\}\leq c^{\prime}(k,\mathbb{G},\Lambda,\alpha,||a||_{k})\left\{||\mathcal{L}u||_{C^{k,\alpha}_{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+||u||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right\}.

∎
