RE D shift : quasinormal modes of black holes embedded in halos of matter
Laura Pezzella 1,2,3,4 , Kyriakos Destounis 1,2,5 , Andrea Maselli 3,4 , Vitor Cardoso 5,6

Abstract
We investigate the (axial) quasinormal modes of black holes embedded in generic matter profiles. Our results reveal that the axial QNMs experience a redshift when the black hole is surrounded by various matter environments, proportional to the compactness of the matter halo. Our calculations demonstrate that for static black holes embedded in galactic matter distributions, there exists a universal relation between the matter environment and the redshifted vacuum quasinormal modes. In particular, for dilute environments the leading order effect is a redshift 1 + U 1+U of frequencies and damping times, with U ∼ − 𝒞 U\sim-{\cal C} the Newtonian potential of the environment at its center, which scales with its compactness 𝒞 {\cal C} .

II.1
Hernquist-type matter around
static BHs

An exact, fully-relativistic solution that describes a BH immersed in a halo of matter was obtained recently
[
67
]
. The environmental contribution in this case is accounted for through the Einstein cluster approach, which describes the matter content in terms of an anisotropic stress-energy tensor, with only tangential (
P_{t}
) and vanishing radial pressure (
P_{r}=0
), i.e.
T^{\mu}{{}_{\nu}}=\text{diag}(-\rho,0,P_{t},P_{t})
. Choosing the energy density of the fluid to follow the Hernquist distribution
[
62
]
, i.e.,

\rho=\frac{M(a_{0}+2M_{\rm BH})(1-2M_{\rm BH}/r)}{2\pi r(r+a_{0})^{3}},

(1)

where
M
and
a_{0}
are the halo mass and length scale, allows one to obtain a BH solution in a closed
analytical
form. Observations and galaxy formation simulations indicate that Eq. (
1
) could be a good proxy for elliptical galaxies and galactic bulges. The geometry of spacetime is given by the metric

ds^{2}=-f(r)dt^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{1-\frac{2m(r)}{r}}+r^{2}(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta d\varphi^{2}),

(2)

where the mass function
m(r)
is of the form

m(r)=M_{\textrm{BH}}+\frac{Mr^{2}}{(a_{0}+r)^{2}}\Bigg(1-\frac{2M_{\textrm{BH}}}{r}\Bigg)^{2},

(3)

where
M_{\textrm{BH}}
is the BH mass. The factor
1-2M_{\rm BH}/r
in the equations above is chosen to truncate the matter at the BH horizon. It can be chosen freely, in Eq. (
1
) at the expense of not having a
closed-form
solution for the spacetime geometry. Other truncation factors, such as
1-4M_{\rm BH}/r
will mimic matter distributions which conform better with relativistic evolutions of dark matter profiles
[
79
,
80
]
.

At small distances, Eq. (
3
)
describes a source of mass
M_{\textrm{BH}}
, while at large distances it is a spacetime of ADM mass
M_{\textrm{ADM}}=M+M_{\rm BH}
. The lapse function
f(r)
can be obtained from
m(r)
by imposing that the
(r,r)
component of Einstein’s equations is zero:

f(r)=\Bigg(1-\frac{2M_{\textrm{BH}}}{r}\Bigg)e^{\Upsilon},

(4)

with

\begin{split}\Upsilon&=-\pi\sqrt{\frac{M}{\xi}}+2\sqrt{\frac{M}{\xi}}\arctan\frac{r+a_{0}-M}{\sqrt{M\xi}},\\
\xi&=2a_{0}-M+4M_{\textrm{BH}}.\end{split}

(5)

The BH spacetime has an event horizon at
r=r_{h}=2M_{\textrm{BH}}
and a curvature singularity at
r=0
. Assuming
\xi>0
and a hierarchy of scales, i.e.
M_{\textrm{BH}}\ll M\ll a_{0}
, ensures that there are no curvature singularities outside the horizon. In the context of dark matter physics, we note that the configuration has a peak tangential pressure
P\lesssim M\rho/a_{0}
away from the BH, thus it is Newtonian and may mimic cold dark matter well.

Equation (
4
) highlights a generic feature of the geometry of BHs surrounded by
any
spherically symmetric matter distribution. In the weak-field limit, this geometry satisfies

f(r)=\left(1-\frac{2M_{\rm BH}}{r}\right)e^{2U},

(6)

with
U
being the Newtonian potential of the matter distribution
[
81
]
. For the Hernquist distribution discussed here,
\Upsilon=2U
. We will use Eq. (
6
) to calculate the corrections to the light ring and its frequency, and determine the QNM frequencies in the eikonal regime.

II.2
Static BHs surrounded by generic matter profiles: a numerical study

The Hernquist distribution (
1
) belongs to a more general class of two-parameter profiles, described by the following semi-analytic
relation:

\rho(r)=\rho_{0}(r/a_{0})^{-\gamma}\left[1+\left(r/a_{0}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{(\gamma-\beta)/\alpha}\ .

(7)

where
\rho_{0}
is the energy density at
r=a_{0}
.
The exponents
\beta
and
\gamma
control the slope of the distribution at large and small distances, respectively, while
\alpha
determines the sharpness of the slope transition
[
82
]
. Equation (
7
) motivated Refs.
[
72
,
75
]

to develop a numerical pipeline to constructs static, and spherically symmetric BH solutions embedded in spherically symmetric, but otherwise generic, astrophysical environments.

In this work, we employ the numerical approach developed in
[
72
,
75
]
and analyze two specific combinations of the
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
parameters from Eq. (
7
) in order to build numerical BH solutions: (i)
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(1,4,1)
, which represents the Hernquist distribution, i.e. the numerical “Hernquist-type BHs”, and (ii)
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(1,3,1)
, corresponding to the numerical “NFW-type BHs” where the distribution of matter follows the NFW profile. Since the total mass of the NFW profile is logarithmically divergent, we define a radial cutoff
r_{c}
, such that
M(r>r_{c})=0

[
72
]
. Hereafter we fix
r_{c}=5a_{0}
. Finally, to mimic the overdense cusp expected to form due to adiabatic accretion, and truncate the matter distribution at the horizon, we scale the profiles such that
\rho(r)\rightarrow\left(1-2M_{\mathrm{BH}}/r\right)\rho(r)

[
67
,
72
,
75
]
.

The BH solutions embedded within the profiles
(

) are constructed as follows:

1.

Take the spherically-symmetric, static spacetime described by line element in Eq. (
2
), with
m(r)
a generic function.

2.

Prescribe the density profile
\rho(r)
. The mass profile is recovered by solving the continuity equation
m^{\prime}(r)=4\pi r^{2}\rho(r)
.

3.

The metric function
f(r)
and the tangential pressure
P_{t}(r)
are determined from the
G_{rr}
component from the Bianchi identities,
respectively, as

\frac{f^{\prime}(r)}{f(r)}=\frac{2m(r)/r}{r-2m(r)},\quad 2P_{t}(r)=\frac{m(r)\rho(r)}{r-2m(r)}.

(8)

For a given
\rho(r)
the solutions to the continuity equation and of Eqs. (
8
) fully specify the background spacetime.

The ADM mass of the spacetime is given by

M_{\textrm{ADM}}=M+M_{\textup{BH}}=m(\infty)
, where
M
is the total mass contained within the halo. At the horizon the metric approaches the Schwarzschild geometry, with vanishing matter density.

II.3
Axial perturbations

With a spacetime in hand, we can assess the changes in the QNM spectrum of BHs due to environmental effects. For this, we need to treat linearized fluctuations of the spacetimes we just discussed. Consider perturbations of the metric and stress-energy tensors:

g_{\mu\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}^{(0)}+g_{\mu\nu}^{(1)}\quad,\quad T_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{env}}=T_{\mu\nu}^{(0)\mathrm{env}}+T_{\mu\nu}^{(1)\mathrm{env}}.

(9)

The first-order components are decomposed in terms of standard axial and polar tensor spherical harmonics
[
83
,
84
,
85
]
that satisfy the perturbed field equations

G_{\mu\nu}^{(1)}=8\pi T_{\mu\nu}^{(1)\mathrm{env}}.

(10)

In this work we focus on axial type perturbations, for which metric and fluid fluctuations decouple (under the assumption that there are no dissipation mechanisms in the fluid
[
86
]
). Then, axial-type gravitational fluctuations are completely governed by a single, one-dimensional, Schrodinger-like equation for a master function
\psi_{\ell m}(r)

[
87
,
72
]

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi_{\ell m}(r)}{\mathrm{d}r_{*}^{2}}+\left(\omega^{2}-V_{\ell}(r)\right)\psi_{\ell m}(r)=0,

(11)

where
\ell=2,\dots,\infty
,
m=-\ell,\dots,\ell
and
r_{*}
is the tortoise coordinate, defined as
\mathrm{d}r_{*}/\mathrm{d}r=\left[f(r)(1-2m(r)/r)\right]^{-1/2}
. The scattering potential
V_{\ell}(r)
reads
[
87
,
72
]
:

V_{\ell}(r)=f(r)\left(\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}-\frac{6m(r)}{r^{3}}+\frac{m^{\prime}(r)}{r^{2}}\right).

(12)

When
m(r)=M_{\mathrm{BH}}
we recover the usual vacuum Schwarzschild background and Eq. (
11
) reduces to the well-known Regge-Wheeler equation
[
83
]
. Figure
2
shows the scattering potential as a function of the radial coordinate for the Hernquist and the NFW profile, assuming
\ell=2
and different values of the halo compactness. The inset in each plot shows the relative difference Eq. (
12
) and the vacuum limit.

Although not demonstrated in this paper, we have found that Eq. (
12
) represents a specific class of a universal scattering potential that depends on the spin
s
of the perturbing field:

V_{s\ell}=f(r)\left[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}+\frac{2m(r)(1-s^{2})}{r^{3}}-\frac{m^{\prime}(r)(1-|s|)}{r^{2}}\right]\ .

(13)

Equation (
13
) generalizes the well known results for Schwarzschild BHs
[
19
]
with a constant mass term, i.e.
m(r)=M_{\rm BH}
. Scalar and electromagnetic perturbations are described by
s=0
and
s=\pm 1
, respectively, while for gravitational (axial) modes,
s=\pm 2
, we recover Eq. (
12
).

III
Setup for QNM calculations

We compute the QNM frequencies for the numerical profiles described in Sec.
II.2
, using the exact Hernquist model of
II.1
as a benchmark to validate our procedure (see Appendix
B
for a convergence
study).

To integrate Eq. (
11
) we first solve the background equations, according to the following two-step procedure:

1.

We choose a density profile
\rho(r)
according to the Hernquist or NFW model. We integrate the equations for the mass function
m(r)
from the horizon
r_{\textup{h}}=2M_{\mathrm{BH}}
where
m(r_{\textup{h}})=M_{\mathrm{BH}}
to a coordinate radius
r_{\textup{out}}\gtrsim 10^{7}a_{0}
that corresponds to our spatial infinity and guarantees asymptotic flatness. We then solve backwards the equation for the metric function
f(r)
, imposing that, in the far-field limit,
m(r\rightarrow r_{\textup{out}})=M_{\textrm{ADM}}

and the radial function can be written as

f(r)\simeq 1-\frac{2M_{\mathrm{ADM}}}{r}+\mathcal{O}(r^{-3}).

(14)

2.

The numerical solution for
m(r)
and
a(r)
allows to compute the tangential pressure
P_{t}(r)
.

QNM calculations require to impose boundary conditions at the horizon and at the infinity. To improve the numerical integration along our domain, we compactify the radial interval by performing the coordinate transformation
x=1-r_{\textup{h}}/r
with
x\in[0,1]
, where
x=0
corresponds to the BH horizon and
x=1
to spatial infinity.

At
x=0
we assume the solution describes a purely ingoing wave:

\psi_{\ell m,\textup{h}}(x)=x^{-\frac{i\omega r_{\textup{h}}}{\sqrt{r_{\textup{h}}f^{\prime}(r_{\textup{h}})}}},

(15)

while purely outgoing boundary condition at spatial infinity is satisfied by the relation

\psi_{\ell m,\rm out}(x)=e^{\frac{ir_{\textup{h}}\omega}{1-x}}(1-x)^{-2iM_{\mathrm{ADM}}}.

(16)

The ansatz for the wave function reduces to

\psi_{\ell m}(x)\sim e^{\frac{ir_{\textup{h}}\omega}{1-x}}(1-x)^{-2iM_{\mathrm{ADM}}}x^{-\frac{i\omega r_{\textup{h}}}{\sqrt{r_{\textup{h}}f^{\prime}(r_{\textup{h}})}}}\ .

(17)

This expression generalizes the boundary condition obtained both for the Schwarzschild BH and the exact Hernquist-type BH
[
72
]
.

We integrate the master equation (
11
) for
\psi_{\ell m}
supplied by the boundary conditions (
15
)-(
16
) using the generalized matrix method described in the following section.

III.1
Generalized matrix method for QNMs

Matrix methods are powerful computational schemes that transform one-dimensional master equations like Eq. (
11
) into a matrix equation. The basis of matrix methods is the manipulation of the ordinary differential equation that governs perturbation propagation through proper decomposition of the spatial derivatives. The equation is discretized with
N
equispaced (or otherwise) grid points and transformed into a matrix equation which can be recast in terms of eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

The matrix method
[
88
,
89
,
90
,
91
,
92
,
93
,
94
,
95
]
that is utilized and generalized here makes use of grid points in a small region of a query point to estimate its derivatives by employing a Taylor expansion. A key step of the method is to discretize the unknown eigenfunction in order to transform the differential equation and its boundary conditions into a homogeneous matrix equation. Based on the information about
N
scattered grid points, Taylor series are carried out for the unknown eigenfunction up to
N
-th order for each grid point. The resulting homogeneous system of linear algebraic equations is solved for the eigenvalues.

Here we generalize, for the first time, the matrix method
[
88
,
89
]
for generic, numerically-constructed and spherically-symmetric BH metrics, as explained in Sec.
II.2
. In a nutshell, when the numerical BH solutions are built, the result is a vector for each of the metric tensor components. Each vector is
interpolated
with high-accuracy through the standard interpolation formula, which finds a value between two points on the curve of a function. The resulting interpolated metric tensor components can then be accessed at any point of the interpolation function, thus giving us the ability to be re-discretized in order for the matrix method to work on the grid points chosen. A more analytic description for the the generalization of the matrix method is given in Appendix
A
.

\tau_{0}(x)\phi^{\prime\prime}(x)+\lambda_{0}(x)\phi^{\prime}(x)+\sigma_{0}(x)\phi(x)=0,

(18)

where
\tau_{0}(x),\,\lambda_{0}(x),\,\sigma_{0}(x)
are
\omega
-dependent functions and
\phi(x)
is a general radial function. By discretizing the interval

, it is possible to introduce
N
random or evenly
distributed points with
x_{1}=0
and
x_{N}=1
. Cramer’s rule allows to discretize all derivatives
[
88
,
89
]
and rewrite Eq. (

) in matrix form, as
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{F}=0
, where
\mathcal{M}
is a square matrix that depends on the eigenvalues
\omega
of the system and
\mathcal{F}=\left(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},\dots,f_{N}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}
with
f_{i}=\phi(x_{i})
. The homogeneous matrix equation should satisfy
\det(\mathcal{M})=0
. The latter provides an algebraic equation to calculate the eigenvalues of any particular eigenvalue system, which can be solved numerically. The generalized boundary conditions and the generic functions

for

IV.1
A result of sufficient generality concerning light ring & QNM frequencies in the eikonal regime

Before discussing QNMs, we will show that for
any

[
96

, this result then provides a robust basis to expect that to leading order QNM frequencies will be redshifted by environments.

V
Conclusions

“Listening” to the Universe with QNMs is the foundation of BH spectroscopy. Decades of developments in the field of ringdown physics has provided significant information regarding the “death” of stars and the formation of BHs through binary coalescence. In the near future, we expect that the BH spectroscopy program will be the most undisputed scheme for analyzing the relaxation of perturbed compact objects and understanding the nature of binary remnants. Nevertheless, only recently BH spectroscopy started taking into account the effects of surrounding astrophysical environments and galactic matter when calculating QNMs.

In fact, since almost all BHs reside in some sort of environment (accretion disks, galactic and extra-galactic) the effort towards a complete BH spectroscopy program that includes environmental effects is undergoing rapid advancements. Very recently, a fully-relativistic, static and spherically symmetric BH was found that is embedded in a spherical matter halo
[
67
]
. This solution does not contain
impromptu
post-Newtonian additions to the metric that mimic environmental effects. To the contrary, it is a fully-relativistic BH solution of the Einstein field equations surrounded by an anisotropic generic fluid.

Understanding the environmental effect on BH QNMs is now very timely and of utmost importance for current and future detections of GWs. In fact, a spurt of analyses have taken place very recently for a unique, exact, BH solution surrounded by a Hernquist-type cold matter
[
67
,
68
,
87
,
55
]
. Since this solution is the only one that currently exist in closed form (besides some thin-shell matching solutions
[
69
]
and analytic approximations
[
70
]
), using different matter galactic halos leads to numerical BH solutions surrounded by these matter halos
[
72
,
75
]
.

Here, we have initiated the use of numerically-constructed BH configurations with different matter halos to find their
axial QNMs
and observe the overall effect of the environment to the modes. We show that BHs with environments possess scalar, electromagnetic and axial gravitational QNMs that are governed by a single-parameter effective potential. The analyses in
[
67
,
25
]
have shown that the exact solution from Ref.
[
67
]
possesses scalar and axial QNMs that are
redshifted
, both in frequency and (absolute value of) decay timescale. In this work, we solidify that this effect on axial QNMs is universal, no matter the energy density (in our study, numerical Hernquist and numerical NFW-type BHs). This is shown by the use of matrix method from Ref.
[
88
,
89
]
which we generalized so that it works for numerically-constructed backgrounds. The code is publicly available in Ref.
[
109
]
and can be used not only for environmental effects, but also for other kinds of solutions such as hairy BHs that do not possess an exact, closed form, and BHs with accretion disks.

Overall, our code behaves very well, in accord to all tests we have performed, and produces convergent QNMs (see Appendix
B
). Since axial perturbations do not couple to the surrounding matter, a redshift of modes, as compactness increases, is adequate. Therefore, the difference between axial QNMs due to environmental effects can be equivalent to redshifted vacuum Schwarzschild QNMs. However, we expect redshift scaling to break for polar perturbations, which couple to matter variables
[
75
]
. The polar sector of gravitational perturbations couples to matter and this will enable us to test GR in its strongest regime. Calculations of polar QNMs for different families of matter profiles are underway and will be addressed in a followup publication, along with an assessment
of their detectability by future GW observations.

[
110
]
appeared, that contains results for axial QNMs, for an exact metric, that are partially comparable to ours.

Appendix A
Generalized matrix method

One of the numerical computation techniques to solve eigenvalue problems of differential equations is the matrix method
[
88
,
89
]
. With this method, it is possible to transform the Schrödinger-like equation into a matrix equation via the proper manipulation of the ordinary differential equation that governs perturbation propagation through proper decomposition of the spatial derivatives.

The proposed matrix method in
[
88
,
89
]
is a non-grid-based interpolation approach, where the interpolation is based on Taylor expansions. This method discretizes the spatial coordinate so that the differential equation, as well as its boundary conditions, are transformed into a homogeneous matrix equation. With this procedure the eigenfunction is expanded in the vicinity of all grid points, and therefore the precision of the algorithm is potentially improved. The data points do not need to sit on a specified grid for the method to work (equispaced in
[
89
]
, delimited expansion
[
92
]
and Chebyshev points
[
93
]
). Using the information of a set of
N
randomly scattered data points, Taylor series are carried out for the unknown eigenfunction up to
N
-th order for each data point. Then, the resulting homogeneous system of linear algebraic equations is solved for the eigenvalues.

The main advantage of this method is that, choosing the right coordinate transformations, the discretization of the wave function and its derivatives are made to be independent of any specific metric, as long as we are aware of the spacetime’s asymptotic nature. Since our goal is to carry out an interpolation based on the information of a set of
N
scattered points distributed in a small neighborhood, the Taylor series has to be applied
N
times to each one of the data points around
x_{0}
. The resulting equation can be written as a matrix product

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{M}D,

(24)

where
\mathcal{F}
and
D
are
N\times 1
column vectors and
\mathcal{M}
is a
N\times N
matrix.
\mathcal{F}
contains the value of the function
f(x)
at each data point
N
,
D
contains the value of
f(x)
and its derivatives at
x_{0}
and the matrix
\mathcal{M}
consists of
N
rows such that the
i
-th row contains increasing power functions of the coordinate relative difference between the
i
-th data point and the query point
x_{0}
.

If one considers a univariate function
f(x)
and the data points as function values at coordinates
x_{i}
, with
i=1,2,\dots,N
, then the vectors
\mathcal{F}
,
D
and the matrix
\mathcal{M}
can be written as

\mathcal{F}=\left(f(x_{1}),f(x_{2}),f(x_{3}),\dots,f(x_{N})\right)^{\mathrm{T}},

(25)

\mathcal{M}=\begin{pmatrix}1&x_{1}-x_{0}&\frac{(x_{1}-x_{0})^{2}}{2}&\frac{(x_{1}-x_{0})^{3}}{3!}&\dots&\frac{(x_{1}-x_{0})^{N}}{N!}\\
1&x_{2}-x_{0}&\frac{(x_{2}-x_{0})^{2}}{2}&\frac{(x_{2}-x_{0})^{3}}{3!}&\dots&\frac{(x_{2}-x_{0})^{N}}{N!}\\
1&x_{3}-x_{0}&\frac{(x_{3}-x_{0})^{2}}{2}&\frac{(x_{3}-x_{0})^{3}}{3!}&\dots&\frac{(x_{3}-x_{0})^{N}}{N!}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&x_{N}-x_{0}&\frac{(x_{N}-x_{0})^{2}}{2}&\frac{(x_{N}-x_{0})^{3}}{3!}&\dots&\frac{(x_{N}-x_{0})^{N}}{N!}\end{pmatrix},

(26)

D=\left(f(x_{0}),f^{\prime}(x_{0}),f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0}),\dots,f^{(N)}(x_{0})\right)^{\mathrm{T}}.

(27)

If the determinant of
\mathcal{M}
is non-zero, the column vector
D
can be expressed in terms of
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{M}
as
D=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{F}
. In particular, if one is interested in only a few particular elements (derivatives) from
D
, it is possible to use Cramer’s rule to evaluate them

D_{i}=\frac{\det(\mathcal{M}_i)}{\det(\mathcal{M})},

(28)

where
\mathcal{M}_{i}
is the matrix formed by replacing the
i
-th column of
\mathcal{M}
by the column vector
\mathcal{F}
. For instance,
f^{\prime}(x_{0})=\det(\mathcal{M}_2)/\det(\mathcal{M})
.
This way, it is possible to rewrite the
n
-th order derivatives
f^{(n)}(x)
using (
28
) and all the derivatives of the
N
data points are written as linear combinations of the function values
f(x_{i})
. Substituting the derivatives into the eigenequation in study, one obtains
N
equations with
f(x_{1}),f(x_{2}),\dots,f(x_{N})
as its variables.

After properly applying the boundary conditions by multiplying with the correct asymptotic behavior of the solutions of the master equation (
11
), one ends up with a homogeneous differential equation

\tau_{0}(x)\phi^{\prime\prime}(x)+\lambda_{0}(x)\phi^{\prime}(x)+\sigma_{0}(x)\phi(x)=0.

(29)

When dealing with numerically-constructed backgrounds of static and spherically-symmetric BHs embedded in generic matter halos, one first obtains the data points of all metric tensor components, as discussed in Sec.
II.2
, interpolate them so that they can be used as functions in the rest of the calculations. and tweak the internal precision of every calculation to
70
digits for increased accuracy. These interpolation functions can then be used as normal functions at any grid point we desire. Then, by taking into account incoming waves at the event horizon and outgoing waves at infinity, we find the form that solutions must satisfy at the boundaries in the wave function

\phi(x)=e^{-\frac{ir_{\textup{h}}\omega}{x-1}}(1-x)^{-i\omega(2M+r_{\textup{h}})}x^{-\frac{ir_{\textup{h}}\omega}{\sqrt{r_{\textup{h}}f^{\prime}(r_{\textup{h}})}}}R(x).

(30)

In this case, the boundary conditions become

and
R(1)=R_{1}
, where
R_{0}
and
R_{1}

\chi(x)=x(1-x)R(x).

(31)

\chi(0)=\chi(1)=0
