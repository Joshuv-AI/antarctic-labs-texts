Dual Nd isotopic archives in fossil teeth reveal bottom-current control of rare-earth element enrichment in deep-sea sediments
Authors: Zongqi Zou, Shengping Qian, Qiong Wu, Xuran Liang, Fan Yang, Le Zhang, Pengli He, Yi-Gang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global emergence of deep-sea sediments enriched in rare-earth elements and yttrium (REY) after the Late Eocene cooling coincides spatiotemporally with Antarctic bottom water (AABW) expansion, yet the underlying mechanism remains unresolved. Here, we present in situ elemental and neodymium isotopic analyses of apatites extracted from Oligocene-Miocene sediments along the North Pacific AABW pathway. Results show ∼90% of the REY budget resides in inner dentin of fossil teeth. The dentin and coexisting authigenic fluorapatites display similar εNd values (∼−5) matching North Pacific seawater, whereas outer dense layer carries a distinct, less-radiogenic εNd (∼−10) resembling South Pacific seawater. This isotopic decoupling establishes fossil teeth as dual archives: The outer layer preserves the εNd signature of the fish’s living environment, while the inner dentin records its depositional environment. We propose that AABW collects, transports, and sorts fossil teeth along its flow path, ultimately leading to the global occurrence of REY-rich sediments in downstream basins.

INTRODUCTION

Deep-sea sediments in ocean basins are emerging as a promising new source of rare-earth elements and yttrium (REY) (1–4), with the seminal work of Kato et al. (1) revealing their widespread distribution in the oligotrophic Pacific seafloor. Current framework suggests that bioapatites (fossil teeth), which are extremely rich in REY (up to several weight %), serve as the principal REY reservoir (3–9). However, this notion presents a paradox when considering that REY-rich sediments typically develop in low-productivity regions (1, 5). The provenance of these fossil teeth in deep-sea sediments and their transformation from REY-poor living teeth (e.g., <100 ng/g) (10, 11) to REY-rich postmortem remain unclear.

To date, the established deposition ages for REY-rich sediments predominantly postdate the Late Eocene cooling event (12–14). The opening of the Tasman Gateway and the Drake Passage, coupled with the Late Eocene climatic transition, gave rise to the permanent Antarctic Ice Sheet, which subsequently promoted the development of northward-flowing Antarctic bottom water (AABW) (15, 16). Notably, voyage expeditions have revealed that shallow REY-rich sediments developed in the Pacific Ocean are consistently located along the pathway of AABW (Fig. 1) (3, 12, 17). This spatiotemporal coupling has led to a prevailing hypothesis that links REY enrichment in these sediments to fish proliferation (3, 12, 17). In this view, it is hypothesized that the nutrient-rich AABW has elevated the productivity in the oligotrophic ocean where fish debris accumulates. However, direct evidence for this mechanism is currently lacking, and the validity of this hypothesis needs to be tested.

Neodymium isotopes are powerful tracers in this context because their short seawater residence time (a few hundred years) relative to global ocean mixing (∼1500 years) (18). Such a short residence time allows water masses to retain distinct Nd isotopic fingerprints (16, 19–21). The dense enamel of fish teeth theoretically inhibits adsorption of REYs, while the dentin is much more prone to modification by seawater/pore fluids (7, 9, 20). Different domains within a fossil tooth may thus encode paleoseawater chemistry from the organism’s living environment to its final burial setting. Therefore, systematic in situ analysis of Nd isotopes and REY within individual teeth can reconstruct the full lifecycle of a tooth from biogenic incorporation, through postmortem transport, to final burial. This approach establishes fossil teeth as dual archives for deciphering their provenance.

In this study, we carried out in situ REY and Nd isotopic analyses on fossil teeth and authigenic carbonate fluorapatite (CFA) from a gravity core along the AABW pathway in the North Pacific Basin, which has multiple REY enrichment layers (8). Our results reveal dual Nd isotopic archives in fossil teeth. We show that the outer dense layer (enamel and dense dentin) resists diagenesis and thus retains the initial Nd isotopic signature of the living environment. The inner porous dentin, by contrast, takes up REY from pore fluids during diagenesis, resulting in extreme REY enrichment and altered Nd isotopes. These findings, combined with the bottom water and pore fluid compositions recorded by authigenic CFA in the pelagic realm, allow us to develop a model that describes how paleocirculation controls the enrichment of bioapatites in the oligotrophic open ocean.

RESULTS

The western and central Pacific are two major deep-sea REY-rich sediment areas that may be influenced by the AABW (Fig. 1) (3, 12). A sediment core ∼720 cm in length below the seafloor (cmbsf) was collected at site P57 (155.06°E, 19.91°N), located off Minamitorishima Island in the western Pacific Ocean (Figs. 1 and 2). This core has been well characterized and has a Sr isotopic age of <25 Ma (Fig. 2 and fig. S1) (8, 22), consistent well with the Miocene or younger ages of REY-rich sediments around Minamitorishima Island (3, 12, 13). This core shows at least two REY-rich layers (2000 to 6000 μg/g) at intervals of 285 to 435 and 615 to 720 cmbsf (Fig. 2A). These REY peaks are coupled with peaks of P2O5, CaO, and number of hand-picked fossil fish teeth (8). Eight sediment layers of the P57 core, below, at, and above the REY enrichment layer, were selected for mineral separation. The separated fossil teeth and authigenic CFA that preserve the chemical signals of pore fluids and/or bottom water were analyzed for morphology, in situ chemical compositions, and in situ Nd isotopic compositions (Figs. 3 to 5). A core (∼720 cm) from site P10 (176.54°E, 12.14°N) in the central Pacific (Fig. 1) is also studied for the purpose of comparison, as its bulk sediment major and trace element, Sr isotopic composition, and in situ Nd isotopic compositions of the tip of fossil teeth are available (fig. S1) (23).

Mineralogical characteristics

The sedimentary phosphate phases fall predominantly into two categories: fossil teeth and pelletal apatite grains (Figs. 3 and 4 and fig. S2). The pelletal apatite grains are composed mainly of authigenic CFA, with few rounded fossil tooth debris (Fig. 3A). A key characteristic of the authigenic CFAs is their intraclastic and oolitic textures (Fig. 3, B to E). Most of these grains have much higher REY concentrations than fossil teeth (Fig. 4). Nevertheless, Liao et al. (24) estimated that authigenic CFA accounts for only ∼9% of the total sedimentary REY inventory in the western Pacific Ocean.

Fossil teeth, the other major phosphate phase, exhibit a distinct layered structure (Fig. 4). Petrographic microscopy reveals that fossil teeth comprise an innermost pulp cavity, an intermediate porous dentin layer, and an outermost composite layer of dense dentin and enamel (Fig. 4 and figs. S2 and S3). The analyzed fossil teeth are predominantly composed of dentin and enamel. Notably, porous dentin constitutes the major part of the tooth, contrasting sharply with the thin outer dense layer (Fig. 4). The volume of porous dentin decreases from the root to the tip (Fig. 4 and figs. S3 to S5). Where the pulp has decomposed (25), the resulting cavity is often filled with secondary silicate materials (fig. S3).

Analytical strategy controls observed signals

Chemically, in situ laser ablation (multiple collector) inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry [LA-(MC)-ICP-MS] reveals contrasting compositional patterns depending on sample preparation and analytical strategy. Intact fossil teeth (not polished to expose the interior) exhibit marked compositional zoning (Fig. 5), consistent with previous studies (9, 26, 27). Specifically, REY concentrations decrease from the root to the tip, and the root shows more radiogenic Nd isotopic signatures than the tip.

By contrast, polished fossil teeth (sectioned and polished to expose the interior) reveal a fundamentally different pattern (Fig. 4). In their longitudinal cross sections, both the inner porous dentin and the outer dense layer are exposed (Fig. 4 and fig. S3). The dense layer is rich in CaO and P2O5 relative to the porous dentin (Fig. 4, A and C, and fig. S3, A to D). REY mapping of these cross sections further indicates that REY concentrations in the porous dentin are two to three orders of magnitude higher than those in the dense layer (Fig. 4, B and D, and fig. S3, H to J). Quantitatively, the dentin accounts for about 90% of the total REY inventory of a fossil tooth (see the Supplementary Materials and fig. S4). REYs are distributed relatively uniformly within each domain (porous dentin and dense layer), with no discernible chemical zoning inside either domain (Fig. 4D and fig. S3). This uniformity is consistent with previous observations of nearly homogeneous high REY concentrations across inner dentin (28). In some teeth, however, a thin compositional transitional layer is present between the porous dentin and the dense layer (Fig. 4, C and D and fig. S3).

Artifactual mixing of dense layer and dentin during LA-ICP-MS analysis

Tip-root chemical zoning has been recognized as a notable feature of fossil teeth (unpolished) in previous studies (9, 26). Notably, intact fossil teeth exhibit distinct εNd values between the tip and the root (Fig. 5) (9, 29). The 143Nd/144Nd ratio changes only through the radioactive decay of 147Sm over time. Because the Sm/Nd ratios of seawater (∼0.20) (30) and living fish teeth (∼0.19) (10) are nearly identical, biological Sm-Nd fractionation is negligible, and the εNd signal is a robust, quasi-conservative tracer of water masses (31, 32). Thus, an alternative mechanism is required to explain the observed covariations of REY concentration and εNd.

In situ analyses of intact fossil teeth reveal a two-component mixing trend between a high–REY-εNd, low–P-Ca end-member and a low–REY-εNd, high–P-Ca end-member (Figs. 4 and 5). These end-member compositions are directly observed from in situ electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA) and REY mapping on longitudinal cross sections of fossil teeth, where the outer dense layer and inner porous dentin are separately exposed. On these cross sections, EPMA data show that the dense layer falls on the high–CaO-P2O5 end-member, whereas porous dentin falls on the low–CaO-P2O5 end-member (Fig. 4, A and C, and fig. S3). Elemental mapping further confirms that the dense layer has extremely low REY concentrations compared to porous dentin (Fig. 4D and fig. S3). In contrast, LA-(MC)-ICP-MS spot analyses on intact fossil teeth could sample both the outer dense layer and the underlying porous dentin simultaneously (fig. S5). In an intact, cone-shaped fossil tooth, the inner dentin is surrounded by an outer thin dense layer (enamel and dense dentin) except at the root base (Fig. 4 and fig. S3). The proportion of the dense layer increases from root to tip, with the tip consisting almost entirely of dense layer (Fig. 3 and figs. S3 to S5). This dense layer is typically only ∼10 to 30 μm thick and would be further thinned during sample preparation. The laser beam of LA-(MC)-ICP-MS has a penetration depth of ∼10 to 20 μm and a beam diameter (33 to 112 μm) that often exceeds the diameter of the inner dentin (fig. S5). Consequently, each spot analysis simultaneously samples both the REY-poor dense layer and the underlying REY-rich porous dentin, generating a mixed geochemical signal. Hence, the compositions obtained from intact teeth in this, and previous studies most likely reflect variable mixing of the two end-members (fig. S5). The absence of zoning within each domain on cross sections does not contradict the presence of tip-root zoning in intact fossil teeth; the two observations arise from different sampling geometries and scales of analysis.

DISCUSSION

Notably, REY concentrations vary little across stratigraphic layers for fossil teeth (Fig. 2 and fig. S1), implying that the bulk REY concentration in sediments is primarily determined by the abundance of apatite phases. Accordingly, understanding sedimentary REY enrichment requires addressing two interconnected questions: (i) the mechanism of REY enrichment within individual teeth and (ii) the processes controlling the physical accumulation of fossil teeth.

REY enrichment through fluid-pervasive porous dentin

The root portion of fossil tooth exhibits Nd isotopic compositions similar to authigenic CFA, sediment, and North Pacific seawater (Fig. 5). Together with the seawater-like post-Archean Australian Shale (33)–normalized pattern of fossil fish teeth (fig. S6), this observation indicates that the teeth incorporated REY from seawater and/or pore fluids (5, 26). The prevailing model holds that REY enter through the root base and then diffuse toward the tip, a hypothesis largely based on chemical zoning observed in intact fossil teeth (26, 27). However, our REY mapping of longitudinal tooth sections reveals no notable internal chemical zoning within either the inner dentin or the dense outer layer; REY are distributed relatively uniformly throughout each phase (Fig. 4 and fig. S3). As discussed above, the discrepancy between previous studies and the present study is attributable to the different sampling geometries and scales of analysis.

Although the outer dense layer limits direct exchange across the external surface, fluid access is maintained through the root base and internal pore networks. Consequently, REY uptake in fossil teeth likely occurs predominantly within the inner dentin. Morphologically, the inner dentin exhibits a sponge-like, highly porous microstructure (Fig. 4 and fig. S4). Notably, in many specimens, the pulp cavity forms a central channel that facilitates fluid percolation toward the tooth tip (fig. S3). This pervasive porosity allows fluids to infiltrate the entire inner porous dentin volume (fig. S4), greatly increasing the reactive surface area for REY uptake. This mechanism aligns with Xu et al. (34) who reported rapid REY enrichment in fossil teeth from surficial sediments within thousands of years. The high efficiency of this porosity-controlled process also explains the consistently high REY concentration of fossil teeth across diverse sedimentary layers (Fig. 2) (34–36). The presence of only a thin chemical transition layer in dense layer indicates that this process is negligible there (Fig. 4), thereby preserving its original geochemical signature. Collectively, these observations refine the prevailing diffusion-based model of REY uptake, which assumes inward diffusion from the basal surface, and instead support a revised model in which fossil teeth rapidly acquire REYs through fluid-pervasive porous dentin (fig. S4A). This model posits that fluids percolate from root to tip (“fluid diffusion”), with dentin simultaneously sequestering REY from the permeating fluids.

Dual paleoenvironment archives of fossil teeth

The Nd isotopic compositions obtained from intact fossil teeth in this study define a mixing trend between a high–REY-εNd end-member and a low–REY-εNd end-member (Fig. 5). As discussed above, this variability reflects the proportion of high-REY inner porous dentin versus low-REY outer dense layer sampled in each spot analysis. Furthermore, within individual teeth, the root exhibits more radiogenic εNd values than the tip (Fig. 5, A and B). Given that the volume of the dense outer layer increases from the root to the tip in intact fossil teeth (Fig. 4 and fig. S5), we conclude that the outer dense layer and the inner dentin are characterized by low and high εNd values, respectively.

Following death, initially REY-poor teeth become enriched in REY through uptake into the porous dentin, while the dense layer remains intact in REY (Fig. 4 and fig. S4). Notably, several fossil teeth exhibit exceptionally higher εNd values in their roots compared to the Pacific seawater (Fig. 5, C and D). This elevated εNd may be indicative of the inputs from Circum-Pacific arc volcanics (37, 38), which are characterized by radiogenic Nd isotopes. These elevated εNd values imply that the overlying water column (∼−5) alone cannot account for the Nd isotopes observed in the root portions (εNd = −7.7 to −0.49; Fig. 5C); instead, pore fluids and/or seawater near the surface sediment that have reacted with the sediments must be taken into consideration (21, 29, 39). Overall, the Nd isotopic composition of inner dentin appears to record those of bottom water or early diagenetic pore fluids. This observation clarifies why Nd isotopes in fossil teeth root generally match those in bulk sediments and authigenic minerals (e.g., CFA and micronodules) that precipitated from seawater or pore fluids (Fig. 5) (39, 40). Since inner dentin constitutes ∼90% of the total REY inventory in fossil teeth (fig. S4), previously reported Nd isotope data for bulk fossil teeth grains most likely reflect that of the dentin fraction (15, 16).

In contrast, the outer dense layer is characterized by low REY concentrations and less-radiogenic Nd isotopes that are distinct from those of ambient fluids and surrounding sediments (Fig. 5). This distinctiveness supports the hypothesis that the dense layer has experienced limited postdepositional modification by ambient fluids. If notable alteration had occurred, then the dense layer is expected to have more radiogenic Nd isotopic composition of pore fluids. Given that living fish teeth have low REY concentrations and that diagenetic pore fluids typically record more radiogenic Nd isotopes than the dense layer (as seen in authigenic CFAs; Fig. 5), the dense layer likely preserves a Nd isotopic signature close to that of the fish’s living environment. It should be noted, however, that limited by spatial resolution, our measured tip εNd values may not fully represent the true end-member composition of the outer dense layer if some porous dentin was included in the laser spot, in which case the true value would be even lower (less radiogenic). Nevertheless, fossil teeth can be robustly interpreted as dual paleoenvironmental archives: The outer dense layer records the Nd isotopic composition of the organism’s living environment, whereas the inner porous dentin reflects postmortem alteration in the depositional environment.

Allochthonous fossil teeth in REY-rich sediments

The formation of large-scale fossil tooth accumulations in the oligotrophic Pacific Ocean remains an enigmatic phenomenon (Fig. 1). This aggregation could arise from either localized fish proliferation or an increased influx of allochthonous teeth. Prevailing interpretations have favored the former, invoking intensified upwelling and biological productivity driven by AABW-seamount interactions in the western Pacific (3, 12, 22). However, our Nd isotope data challenge this in situ proliferation model, especially for the western-central Pacific where bottom currents prevail.

Our Nd isotopes of fossil teeth provide key clues to understand this phenomenon, with the Nd isotopes of inner porous dentin and outer dense layer respectively recording the environments of the fish living and final depositional site (Fig. 5). The strongly unradiogenic εNd values found in the dense layer thus cannot be attributed to simple biological inheritance. To interpret these signatures, we first evaluate two previously invoked mechanisms, both of which, as we show below, are ultimately inconsistent with the observations.

The first possibility is that the dense layer inherited dissolved Nd from the modified AABW, which initially possesses highly unradiogenic εNd values (<−9). However, this hypothesis conflicts with the well-documented evolution of AABW during its northward transit. Because of progressive boundary exchange, AABW becomes increasingly radiogenic along its pathway, reaching εNd values of approximately −5 before entering the western-central equatorial Pacific (Fig. 6), comparable to modern North Pacific deep-water values (41, 42). Therefore, modified AABW cannot account for the extremely unradiogenic εNd values (down to −10) preserved in the dense layer of fossil teeth.

The second hypothesis posits an enhanced contribution from Asian eolian dust. However, the nonphosphate insoluble residue from the REY-rich interval at site P57 yields εNd values higher than −6.5, showing no detectable eolian dust contribution. This inference is reinforced by data from nearby Core GC02 (3), in which the REY-rich interval (from ∼11.5 to ∼9.5 Ma) coincides with the most negative εNd values. Since ∼9.5 Ma, REY contents have been lower, and εNd values have remained stable and more radiogenic, although the flux of Asian dust increased markedly during this later period. This decoupling between dust input and Nd isotopes demonstrates that dust has limited effect on the seawater Nd budget in this region and thus cannot explain the negative εNd values in the REY-rich layer. Moreover, although Asian dust itself has reported εNd values of approximately −8.6 to −10.5 (43), fish incorporate dissolved Nd from seawater that is mixed with local and volcanic inputs, resulting in a dissolved Nd isotope signature substantially more radiogenic than the dust end-member. Therefore, neither modified AABW nor eolian dust responsible for the strongly unradiogenic εNd values (as low as −10) recorded in the dense layer of our fossil teeth.

We therefore propose an alternative model in which some of the fossil teeth in REY-rich sediments were allochthonous. Although the precise habitat depth of these fossil fish cannot be determined, this uncertainty does not affect our interpretation because seawater Nd isotopic compositions in the North Pacific exhibit a clear contrast with those of the fossil teeth. Across both surface and deep waters, North and equatorial Pacific seawater consistently shows more radiogenic εNd values than its southern counterpart (Figs. 5 and 6) (31, 44, 45). Therefore, if these fossil teeth were purely local, their dense layer (enamel and dense dentin) must match the North Pacific seawater Nd isotopic signature. However, fossil teeth dense layer samples from the western and central Pacific (23) and even whole North Pacific Ocean record significantly more unradiogenic εNd values (as low as −10) than coexisting sediments and seawater (including both deep and surface data) (Figs. 2 and 5 and fig. S1). Similar observations have recently been made for fossil teeth from the Tiki Basin in the eastern South Pacific, where εNd values from bioapatite profiles reveal a two–end-member mixing: One end-member is represented by primitive less-radiogenic εNd values from the tooth tip, and the other is represented by pore water (29). This suggests that the observations made in this study may be only confined to a single site but may reflect a global phenomenon. Given that the dense layer resists postdepositional alteration and retains the Nd isotopic signal of the fish’s living environment, its isotopic contrast with ambient environments (Figs. 2 and 5) strongly suggests that a substantial proportion of the fossil teeth in the REY-rich sediments are allochthonous, most likely derived from distant, isotopically distinct regions.

The intraclastic and oolitic textures of the authigenic CFA, together with the rounded to subrounded porous dentin pellets (Fig. 3A), which are characteristic of phosphorites formed by physical winnowing and reworking (46, 47), support the hypothesis of a hydrodynamic transport process. This explains why the age of some fossil teeth in the upper sediment layers is older than that of teeth in deeper layers (28). Furthermore, the low-productivity pelagic region where these deposits occur would have provided insufficient food to support a substantial population of living fish. Therefore, the combination of abraded textures, reversed age-depth relationship, and the oligotrophic nature of the depositional environment collectively argue against in situ mortality of living fish and instead support a postmortem hydrodynamic transport.

Bottom current serves as a fossil teeth acquirer

The provenance of fossil teeth and their physical aggregation within deep-sea sediments, particularly in oligotrophic settings, serve as pivotal determinants in the formation of REY-rich sediments. From the perspective of the entire modern Pacific Ocean, there are two regions that may have contributed fossil teeth with less-radiogenic Nd isotopes to the western Pacific basin (Fig. 6). One possible region is the continental shelf of the western Pacific Ocean. However, it should be pointed out that the emerging of volcanic arc at the west Pacific margin (since 50 Ma) and subsequent opening of west Pacific marginal basins (48) had isolated the Pacific basin from the continental shelf (Fig. 6 and fig. S7). This long-term isolation is consistent with the persistently relatively radiogenic seawater Nd isotopes (εNd > −5) recorded in the adjacent Philippine Sea since the Eocene (49). Moreover, there is no evidence for persistent bottom currents in west Pacific continental shelf, which are capable of transporting fossil teeth from these shelves to the western Pacific basin, let alone into Central Pacific basin.

The surficial REY-rich sediments (<5 m in depth) in North Pacific Ocean are located at the downstream of modern AABW pathway (Fig. 1) (1). Note that in the modern Pacific Ocean, there is a north-south Nd isotopic gradient with more less-radiogenic values in the South Pacific (Figs. 5 and 6). This gradient is caused by the northward flow of AABW, which mixes with local seawater and carries its isotopic signature along the deep Pacific basin. In detail, a branch of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current evolves into the AABW (16, 50, 51), which travels northward through the North Pacific (52), particularly across areas where REY-rich sediments are usually developed (Fig. 1). This northward-flowing bottom current and a north-south Nd isotopic gradient in the Pacific Ocean have persisted from the early Oligocene (∼34 Ma) to the present (Fig. 5E), following the opening of the Tasman Gate and Drake Passage and the establishment of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (15, 51). The paleogeographic reconstruction shows that our studied sites were located at the west-middle Pacific over the past 30 Ma (53) (fig. S7). Accordingly, the fact that fossil tooth tips exhibit less radiogenic Nd isotopes than their host sediments and authigenic CFAs, together with the similarity of these nonradiogenic values to those of upstream source areas, suggests that the teeth were likely transported from upstream regions by the northward current system (Fig. 6). In this proposed model, living fish acquires the Nd isotopic signature of the upstream seawater in their living environment. After death, the fish remains are deposited locally. These sediments were subsequently eroded, transported, and hydrodynamically sorted by AABW (Fig. 5 and fig. S10). Following long-distance transportation and sorting by bottom current, the postmortem fish teeth accumulated at the North Pacific basins. Given that porous dentin is highly susceptible to early diagenetic resetting (Figs. 4 and 5) (9, 29), its final Nd isotopic signature predominantly reflects the depositional environment. Any REY absorbed during transport may have had its original isotopic signature reset, so the porous dentin is best understood as having continuously incorporated REY from fluids throughout transport and subsequent burial diagenesis (Fig. 6). This proposal is supported by the remarkedly higher proportion (>30%) of allochthonous fossil fish teeth in REY-rich layers compared to REY-poor layers (Fig. 2, D and E, and fig. S1). This highlights that the physical aggregation of fossil fish teeth, mediated by bottom currents, is key to the formation of REY-rich sediments.

There are two key points to note regarding this model. First, the model does not require that all fossil teeth deposited in the North Pacific originate from the Southern Ocean. Instead, only teeth carrying distinctly less-radiogenic εNd signatures can be confidently identified as having formed in water masses different from those of the North Pacific burial site. Fish teeth originating from other regions, whose Nd isotopes are similar to the local North Pacific composition, cannot be unambiguously traced. Under this framework, persistent bottom-water circulation acted as a transport and concentrating mechanism for fish teeth derived from multiple source regions along its pathway. This explains why the εNd values of the teeth range from nonradiogenic εNd (∼−10) resembling South Pacific seawater to εNd values (∼−5) matching North Pacific seawater. Second, the proposed mechanism does not imply a single, rapid depositional episode. Instead, fish teeth were progressively redistributed and accumulated through sustained northward flow of bottom currents over extended periods. Despite circulation pathways likely varied through time, the overall flow direction remained essentially from the Southern Ocean toward the North Pacific.

Summary and outlook

In summary, we argue that the initial REY and Nd isotopic compositions in porous dentin of the fossil teeth can be easily modified by fluids. The enrichment of REY in deep-sea sediments can be achieved via REY uptake into the porous dentin. Since the Eocene, the development of permanent polar ice sheets has triggered the formation of northward bottom currents, notably AABW, establishing a stable deep circulation system (16, 54). Since the formation of current tectonic pattern of ocean, the global major bottom currents dissipate at the Pacific and Indian basins. They act as natural repositories, collecting fossil teeth via ocean currents worldwide. Without relying solely on local fish proliferation, this mechanism best explains the formation of thick, REY-rich sediment layers in oligotrophic Pacific basins. Instead, stable, long-lived deep currents are the primary drivers of tooth accumulation in distal depositional environments. The downstream basins, functioning as “treasure basins,” are the key mining areas.

The proposed mechanism likely operates on a global scale. Bottom currents of Global Overturning Circulation [e.g., AABW and North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW)] travel through most of the global oceans and eventually flows into the Pacific and the Indian basins (Fig. 6) (50). The REY-rich sediments are usually deposited in the oceanic basins along the downstream of these bottom currents (1, 55–58). These couplings demonstrate the global relevance of our model. We thus posit a conceptual framework in which bottom currents act as agents of acquisition, filtering, and long-distance transport of postmortem fish debris, while downstream oceanic basins (e.g., the North Pacific) serve as the terminal receptacles where transported materials accumulate (Fig. 6D and fig. S8). This framework provides a predictive model for deep-sea REY exploration. If bottom-current transport and hydrodynamic sorting govern the accumulation of REY-rich apatites, then the most prospective REY deposits should preferentially occur in downstream sedimentary basins where persistent bottom-current deceleration promotes long-term particle trapping. These hydrodynamic depocenters can therefore be identified using reconstructions of deep-ocean circulation together with basin-scale topography, providing a strategy for predicting undiscovered REY-rich sediments.

Following the approach established in this study, future in situ Nd isotope analysis targeting the diagenetically resistant domains within biological fossils (e.g., the dense layer of conodonts and fossil fish teeth) could yield more pristine seawater εNd records that are largely free from postdepositional contamination. By minimizing diagenetic overprinting and provenance ambiguity, these records would provide more reliable constraints on past ocean circulation and their links to major climatic and biotic events. More broadly, this study highlights the allochthonous nature of fossil fish teeth, which has important implications for the interpretation of biogenic apatite archives in general. Although fossil fish teeth are widely used as paleoenvironmental archives, particularly in studies of deep-sea environments, their oxygen isotope compositions are commonly interpreted as records of local seawater conditions (59, 60). If transported after death, however, fossil teeth may instead preserve environmental signals from distant source regions rather than the depositional site. This ambiguity can be resolved by coupling oxygen isotope analyses with in situ Nd isotopes, the latter serving as a robust provenance tracer. By identifying whether the teeth are locally derived or transported from distant regions, this dual proxy approach can help disentangle local versus remote signals and greatly enhance the reliability of paleoceanographic reconstructions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The samples analyzed in this study were obtained from the sediment coring cruise of the R/V Haiyangdizhi-6 in the Pigafetta Basin, western Pacific Ocean, during August to September 2014 (8, 22). In this study, we conducted detailed microstructural observations coupled with in situ Nd isotopic and chemical analyses on fossil fish teeth and authigenic CFA at the State Key Laboratory of Deep Earth Processes and Resources, Guangzhou Institute of Geochemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Analyses were performed on authigenic CFA and different parts of fossil fish teeth. In addition, REY mapping was applied to fossil fish teeth to visualize their internal distribution patterns.

The hand-picked fossil fish teeth and authigenic CFAs were observed and recorded using a Carl Zeiss SUPRA 55 SAPPHIRE field emission scanning electron microscope. Fossil fish teeth are inherently composite structures, consisting of multiple mineralogically and chemically distinct layers. Therefore, some intact fish teeth were polished to reveal their inner layers, facilitating microscopic observation and REY mapping analysis.

In situ concentrations analysis

Major element analyses were performed on a CAMECA SXFive-FE EPMA. EPMA analyses were conducted with a beam size of 2 μm, a beam current of 10 nA, an accelerating voltage of 15 kV, and a peak counting time of 20 s. In situ trace element concentration analyses were conducted by laser ablation inductively coupled plasma sector field mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-SF-MS) using an Element XR (Thermo Fisher Scientific) ICP-SF-MS coupled with a 193-nm (ArF) Resonetics RESOlution M-50 laser ablation system. Analyses used a laser beam size of 33 μm, an energy density of 4 J/cm2, and a repetition rate of 6 Hz. Calcium concentration, previously measured by EPMA, was selected as the internal standard element. The NIST SRM 610 was used for calibration, and the NIST SRM 612 with known chemical compositions was analyzed to monitor data quality. The detailed analytical procedures and data reduction strategy followed (61). Most measured elements agreed with reference values within 8%, and the analytical precision (2RSD) was better than 10%.

Element mapping

REY mapping was conducted on the longitudinal cross sections of fossil fish teeth using a NWR FemtoUC femtosecond laser system (New Wave Research, Fremont, CA, USA) coupled with a commercially available dual concentric injector plasma torch in the ICP-time-of-flight mass spectrometry system (icpTOF R, Tofwerk AG, Switzerland) (62). The analysis used a 6-μm beam scanned at 3 μm/s, with a fluence of 6 J/cm2 at 6 Hz. NIST SRM 610 served as the external standard for semiquantitative calibration, successfully generating the REY distribution maps.

In situ Nd isotope analysis

In situ Nd isotope analyses were performed with a Neptune Plus MC-ICP-MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with the same laser ablation system for trace element analysis. The laser was operated at a beam diameter of 82 to 112 μm, a repetition rate of 5 to 7 Hz, and an energy density of ∼4 J/cm2. Interference correction for 144Sm on 144Nd was based on measuring 147Sm intensity with a natural 144Sm/147Sm = 0.20504. The mass bias factor of Sm was calculated from the measured isotopic ratio of 147Sm/149Sm and its accepted value of 1.08507 (63). The mass bias of 143Nd/144Nd was corrected by normalization to 146Nd/144Nd = 0.7219 with an exponential law. The more detailed analytical procedure, interference correction, and data reduction strategy can be found in (64, 65). The analytical results for reference apatites of Durango (143Nd/144Nd = 0.512492 ± 0.000027, 2 SD, n = 35) and MAD (0.511364 ± 0.000053, 2 SD, n = 20) agree well with their reference values (0.512493 ± 0.000021 and 0.511352 ± 0.000012 for Durango and MAD, respectively) within analytical uncertainty (data S1) (66).
