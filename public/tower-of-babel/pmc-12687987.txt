The present and future of trigeminal nerve block in Japan: anatomical foundations as a key to patient safety
Authors: Toru Yamamoto, Takutoshi Inoue, Joe Iwanaga, Akiko Okada-Ogawa, Naotaka Kishimoto
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

This Letter highlights recent advancements in trigeminal nerve block (TNB) with an emphasis on its expanding clinical applications and the critical role of anatomical knowledge in ensuring procedural safety. It also underscores the need for improved educational and training frameworks for future dental anesthesiologists.

Traditionally, TNB has been used to treat drug-resistant trigeminal neuralgia and other forms of intractable chronic orofacial pain such as migraines and cluster headaches [1]. The procedure was frequently performed using blind techniques, advancing the needle according to external anatomical landmarks and patient feedback and, in some instances, by evaluating responses to minimal electronic stimulation intensity (approximately 0.5 mA). However, this method was associated with a risk of complications, such as accidental puncture of blood vessels. Subsequently, fluoroscopic guidance (real-time imaging using X-rays) has been introduced to improve safety; however, the risk of vascular puncture remained, as the visualization of blood vessels under fluoroscopy is limited, and radiation exposure is unavoidable.

Ultrasound guidance was introduced to overcome these limitations, allowing the real-time visualization of soft tissues and vascular structures without radiation exposure. This technique allows real-time visualization of nerves and adjacent vascular structures, reducing the risk of procedural errors and complications, such as vascular puncture, local anesthetic systemic toxicity, and hematoma formation [1]. Specific applications of TNB include its use at the bedside or in clinical settings without fluoroscopic equipment, as well as in patients for whom X-ray exposure should be avoided such as pregnant women.

More recently, ultrasound-guided TNB has been explored for chronic pain management and for intraoperative applications. Several clinical studies have investigated its potential to stabilize hemodynamic fluctuations during surgery and reduce postoperative analgesic requirements [2].

Safety and vascular risk in ultrasound-guided TNB

An ultrasound-guided trigeminal nerve block (TNB) is typically performed using an infrazygomatic approach. The ultrasound probe is placed below the zygomatic arch and longitudinally oriented along the lateral face. After identifying the lateral pterygoid muscle and plate, a needle (Stimuplex® D, 22G, 100 mm, B-Braun, Melsungen, Germany) is advanced in-plane toward the pterygoid lateral plate, where local anesthetic (0.375% ropivacaine, 10 ml) is injected. The anesthetic then diffuses through the foramen rotundum to the middle cranial fossa, blocking the three branches of the trigeminal nerve (V1–V3) [3].

A previous review [3] demonstrated the benefits of ultrasound guidance in nerve blocks in the oral and maxillofacial regions. The authors emphasized that in extraoral approaches, direct visualization of vascular structures contributes substantially to safer procedures. The extraoral approach allows direct access to the maxillary (V2) and mandibular (V3) nerves, while avoiding intraoral contamination. However, the head and neck contain complex blood vessel networks. To ensure patient safety, clinicians must possess precise anatomical knowledge and proficiency in ultrasound techniques. In the TNB, the targeted nerves (V2 and V3) lie close to several important blood vessels, including the maxillary artery and its branches (Fig. 1). These structures are close to the needle path and pose the risk of serious complications if punctured. Fortunately, several measures, such as real-time color Doppler scanning, confirming contact with bone, aspiration before injection, and slow, incremental injection (0.2–0.5 ml), greatly reduce the chances of hematoma or intravascular injection [3].

Clinical significance and future perspectives

A clinical study by Molins et al. [4] found that ultrasound-guided maxillary nerve block effectively reduced opioid use and postoperative pain in patients undergoing bimaxillary osteotomy. This suggests that TNB is not only safe, but also useful for managing postoperative pain after maxillofacial surgery.

Additionally, new studies are beginning to explore how TNB may help control intraoperative vital signs and improve patient postoperative quality of life. As interest in these new applications grows, further evidence is expected to support the broader clinical value of TNB.

Simultaneously, safely performing ultrasound-guided TNB requires detailed anatomical knowledge of the nerve paths and adjacent vascular structures. In particular, the area around the medial pterygoid muscle includes critical vascular structures such as branches of the maxillary artery. Surgical mistakes involving these areas can lead to serious complications. Therefore, practitioner skills and anatomical knowledge are directly linked to procedural safety. Consequently, Japan urgently needs to build practical training systems for Cadaver Surgical Training (CST). CST helps clinicians learn the three-dimensional anatomy required for nerve blocks and improves their ability to identify nerves and vessels under ultrasound guidance. However, the “Joint Statement on the Use of Donated Bodies for Medical and Dental Education and Research” issued in September 2025 does not include the Japanese Dental Society of Anesthesiology or Japanese Society of Orofacial Pain among its signatories. CST is primarily conducted in medical and dental schools and requires dedicated facilities, adequate funding, and trained personnel. These findings highlight the need for broader institutional involvement and support. In the United States, clinical trainee access to cadavers after graduation remains relatively easier than in Japan; however, hands-on training is not routinely implemented, and re-evaluation of educational curricula to meet contemporary needs may be warranted.

In addition to CST, hands-on training using 3D anatomical models can also be useful. Technologies such as augmented reality (AR) could also be used to overlay 3D anatomical maps onto ultrasound images and guide needle paths more precisely [5].

Large-scale prospective studies across multiple institutions are required to better understand the clinical value of TNB. These studies should investigate not only the effects during surgery, but also the postoperative and long-term outcomes. With robust research and strong training systems, more dental anesthesiologists will be able to safely and effectively perform TNB. Ultimately, this will lead to improved patient safety and increased satisfaction.

Conclusion

As ultrasound-guided TNB becomes more common, it is expected to reduce the risk of complications and improve patient pain control. To support this progress, it is essential to deepen and share anatomical knowledge, particularly regarding the surrounding nerves and blood vessels. Clinical education systems should incorporate training methods such as CST and AR-based 3D model simulations. These tools will help the next generation of dental anesthesiologists to acquire the skills necessary to safely perform TNB in clinical practice.
