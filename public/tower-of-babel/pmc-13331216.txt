Regional transfer of children with appendix-related concerns to outside cross-sectional imaging
Authors: Sindhu Vasini Mannava, R. Cartland Burns
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Diagnostic discordance between general and pediatric radiologists with respect to abdominal computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is established, particularly with respect to acute appendicitis. In this study, we aimed to identify the negative pediatric radiologist interpretation rate among patients transferred to our institution for appendix-related concerns.Material and Methods:We retrospectively reviewed all children transferred from statewide outside facilities to our standalone tertiary care children’s hospital from June 2018 to July 2023 for appendix-related concerns.Results:Out of 1041 appendix-related transfers during the 5-year study period, 889 had outside abdominal CT or MRI. Of this cohort, 103 (11.6%) patients had a negative interpretation by pediatric radiologists. Thirty-nine patients (37.9%) were given pre-transfer antibiotics, primarily due to concern for appendicitis (92.3%). Other acute findings noted on pediatric radiologist review included a thickened bladder and increased stool burden. Additional imaging was performed post-transfer in 30 (29.1%) patients as follows: Appendix ultrasound (US) (60%), abdomen US (3.3%), right upper quadrant US (13.3%), testicular US (3.3%), and pelvic US (43.3%). Sixty-seven (65%) patients were discharged home from the emergency department with the remainder admitted. The median (Q1, Q3) length of stay at our institution was 6.9 (4.2, 16.2) h. Only one patient underwent post-discharge appendectomy.Conclusion:During the study period, 11.6% of transferred patients with outside CT/MRI had negative interpretations by our pediatric radiologists. Despite barriers, teleradiology has the potential to transform appendix-related transfers by enabling diagnostic clarity. The financial and social burdens incurred by potentially avoidable transfers could be significant, warranting further investigation.

INTRODUCTION

Across the country on a yearly basis, hundreds of patients are transferred from regional facilities to high-volume children’s hospitals due to appendix-related concerns. One study cited confirmed or possible appendicitis as one of the top ten diagnoses prompting interfacility emergency department (ED) transfer of children.[1] Whether a child presents to an urgent care, pediatrician’s office, community hospital ED, or major children’s hospital, the workup of appendicitis includes a combination of physical exam, laboratory tests, and imaging.[2] Although ultrasound (US) is a safe and cost-effective tool in the diagnosis of pediatric appendicitis, all outside facilities may not be able to employ this modality due to limited operator availability or experience. Children who present to regional hospital EDs are 3 times more likely to undergo a computed tomography (CT) scan for appendicitis workup than those who present to a standalone children’s hospital, children’s hospital within a larger hospital, children’s specialty hospital, or hospital with a pediatric wing.[3] With a higher sensitivity and specificity than US, CT imaging serves as a rapid and reliable diagnostic modality for appendicitis workup in regional hospitals with the added risk of ionizing radiation. A third option, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the appendix, is rarely used, tedious, and costly although it achieves the same task as CT without ionizing radiation exposure. Despite the reliability and ease of obtaining cross-sectional abdominal imaging in the workup of pediatric appendicitis, interpretation of these studies remains a challenge at outside facilities without the trained pediatric radiologist’s interpretation.

At our institution, we observed frequent transfers from outside facilities for appendix-related concerns. We noted that many transferred patients had already undergone cross-sectional abdominal imaging before arrival, often interpreted by a general radiologist as positive for appendicitis. It is routine practice at our standalone children’s hospital that pediatric radiologists reinterpret these outside images to corroborate or challenge existing outside imaging reports. Given the trend of negative interpretations at our hospital, we sought to identify the rate of negative interpretations of outside cross-sectional imaging among patients transferred to our institution with appendix-related concerns. Furthermore, we aimed to identify clinical features of patients with negative interpretations and costs associated with transfer. Our ultimate goal in performing this study was to support the need for teleradiology and remote interpretations of pediatric abdominal cross-sectional imaging in an effort to better inform pediatric transfers from regional facilities.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

After obtaining Institutional Review Board approval (#21259), we performed a retrospective cohort study of patients who were transferred from outlying institutions to our standalone children’s hospital for appendix-related concerns from June 2018 to July 2023. We identified patients using our institution’s transfer registry and manually screened patients, excluding those transferred for other indications based on clinical documentation. We included patients 18 years and younger who underwent cross-sectional abdominal imaging at an outside facility and had a negative interpretation at our institution. We defined negative interpretations as those that did not identify any evidence of acute appendicitis or interpretations that did not confirm acute appendicitis nor any other definitive diagnosis. Any patients who underwent appendectomy upon arrival at our institution, received nonoperative treatment for appendicitis, or had an equivocal pediatric radiologist interpretation of cross-sectional imaging were excluded from secondary outcome analysis but included in the negative interpretation ratio calculation (primary outcome) as described below. All interpretations of imaging at our institution were completed by board-certified pediatric radiologists who were not blinded to outside hospital radiologist interpretations before performing their own interpretation. All data were collected and maintained through the REDCap electronic data capture tool hosted by our institution.[4]

Our primary outcome measure was the rate of negative interpretations for appendicitis among patients transferred to our institution for appendix-related concerns during the study period. To calculate this, we defined the numerator as the number of patients with negative abdominal cross-sectional interpretations and the denominator as all patients who underwent outside abdominal cross-sectional imaging. Given that cross-sectional imaging was performed at various institutions across the state, there was no standardized CT/ MRI or appendicitis diagnosis protocol used. Furthermore, we did not collect data explicitly on CT/MRI technique, contrast use, or slice thickness. Our secondary outcome measures included additional workup performed after transfer to our institution, rate of outside hospital antibiotic administration before transfer, transportation method for transfer, length of hospital stay at our institution, post-discharge return to ED or readmission, estimated cost of ambulance transportation, and total cost of care at our institution. We obtained all clinical data points through electronic medical record review. We defined post-discharge return to ED or readmission as events that occurred at our institution and for which data were available in our electronic medical record. We estimated the cost of ambulance transportation based on the 2024 Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services medical transport fee schedule due to a lack of access to true cost data. Finally, data on the cost of admission at our institution were limited to patients admitted after January 2020 and included facility costs only. Physician charges were not included in cost estimates.

We reported descriptive data as frequencies for categorical variables and median with first (Q1) and third (Q3) quartiles for continuous variables. We did not perform any comparative analysis between groups or multivariable analysis. Given this, we were unable to account for covariates such as concomitant abdominal imaging findings, additional imaging studies (USs, etc.), and laboratory findings which may have impacted secondary outcomes of interest. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA).

RESULTS

Patient selection and baseline characteristics

We analyzed 1041 appendix-related transfers from outside hospitals to our children’s hospital from June 2018 to July 2023 [Figure 1]. Among this cohort, 889 patients (85.4%) had an outside CT or MRI of the abdomen/pelvis, and within this group, 103 patients had a negative interpretation by our pediatric radiologists after arrival at our institution. Therefore, the rate of negative interpretations among transfers to our children’s hospital was 11.6%. The median (Q1, Q3) age of patients with negative interpretations was 9.9 (7.3, 13.3) years, and the majority of patients had government-based insurance (57.3%) [Table 1]. Only 11 patients among this cohort had baseline gastrointestinal disease including gastroesophageal reflux disease, chronic abdominal pain, chronic constipation, irritable bowel syndrome, biliary pathology, and pancreatic insufficiency.

Clinical outcomes

Most patients in our cohort were transferred to our institution from regional community hospitals (85.4%) while a minority of patients were transferred from regional hospitals within our university health system (10.7%) or primary care office/ urgent care (3.9%) [Table 2]. Most patients had outside laboratories performed (95.1%) and underwent outside CT abdomen/pelvis only (86.4%). Only one patient in our cohort underwent an outside MRI abdomen/pelvis rather than a CT. Outside cross-sectional abdominal imaging reports were as follows: “Consistent with appendicitis” (19.4%), “possible appendicitis” (36.9%), “appendicitis cannot be excluded” (24.3%), “no definitive diagnosis” (10.7%), and “not consistent with appendicitis” (13.1%). In addition to cross-sectional imaging, outside US was performed in 14 patients (13.6%). Among available outside US reports, the results were as follows: “Non-visualized appendix” (78.6%), “inconclusive” (35.7%), “prominent mesenteric lymph nodes” (7.1%), and “normal appendix” (7.1%). Across all 103 patients, 37.9% received antibiotics at the outside facility before transfer. The indication for antibiotics in the majority of cases was documented as related to appendicitis (92.3%) while some patients received antibiotics for other reasons such as urinary tract infection or ear infection.

Transfer request data were available for 80 out of 103 patients (77.7%), and among this cohort, the median (Q1, Q3) time from transfer request initiation to arrival at our children’s hospital was 2.4 (1.5, 4.2) h [Table 3]. Across all patients with negative interpretations for appendicitis, 53.4% were transported through ambulance and 39.8% came to our institution in a private vehicle. On interpretations performed at our institution, 98.1% were “not consistent with appendicitis” and 1.9% provided “no definitive diagnosis.” Other acute findings were noted on pediatric radiologist interpretation in 39 patients (37.9%) and included “Increased stool burden” (25.6%), “thickened bladder” (23.1%), “enteritis or colitis” (17.9%), or “ovarian pathology” (17.9%). At our institution, 30% of patients underwent additional imaging on top of pediatric radiologist interpretation of outside cross-sectional imaging, most commonly appendix US (60%) and pelvic US (43.3%). Most transferred patients with negative interpretations were discharged home from our ED (65%) while the rest were admitted either to the pediatric surgery service (18.4%) or another service (16.5%). The pediatric surgery team was consulted for 81.6% of all transferred patients with negative interpretations and the median (Q1, Q3) length of stay at our hospital was 6.9 (4.2, 16.2) h.

DISCUSSION

In the era of high patient volumes and specialized medicine, we increasingly rely on the expertise of those with a clinical focus in a particular subject matter. While this often leads to superior patient outcomes, it also confers a quick trigger to escalate, refer, and transfer patients from points of general care to super-specialized centers. This is the impetus behind frequent pediatric transfers from community EDs to high-volume children’s hospitals. Inevitably, along with necessary transfers, the potentially avoidable transfer of patients across tens and hundreds of miles is an accepted risk. Mohr et al. noted that potentially avoidable pediatric transfers were common and comprised a significant portion of healthcare costs with over a thousand dollars of additional associated costs per patient.[5] Peebles et al. found that over half of pediatric patient transfers from community EDs to children’s hospitals resulted in discharge home and noted the potential role of second opinion telemedicine in reducing unnecessary transfers.[6] Transfer decision-making in pediatrics is fraught with challenges including fear of missing a critical diagnosis, diagnostic uncertainty, and lack of resources at the initial point of care. The decision is typically made using the sending physician’s history and physical examination and any objective measures such as imaging and laboratory studies. Importantly, at regional facilities where much of this workup is initiated, it is often a general radiologist who is tasked with interpreting a pediatric patient’s imaging. This can be particularly challenging in the setting of infrequent pediatric imaging interpretations at these facilities.

The interpretation discrepancy between general radiologists at referring centers and pediatric specialty radiologists is significant, as is the correlation between the final diagnosis and the latter’s interpretation.[5] Eakins et al. found a 48.4% negative interpretation rate among pediatric radiologists who evaluated CT scans thought to be positive for appendicitis by community radiologists. [7] Among other pediatric diagnoses with high rates of disagreement by pediatric radiologists are gastroenteritis (80%), hydronephrosis (66.7%), intussusception (42.9%), and small bowel obstruction (40%).[7] In our cohort, most patients who were transferred to our hospital had outside CT or MRI and 11.6% of these patients had negative interpretation by our pediatric radiologists. In comparison to the limited studies on this topic, our rate is slightly lower than the 14.7% false positive rate described by Grant et al.[8] Keating et al. found that, among 110 outside CT scans interpreted by community radiologists as “equivocal for appendicitis,” pediatric radiologists interpreted these as positive (19.1%), negative (69.1%), and equivocal (11.8%).[9] It is evident that interpretation discordance exists and may be attributed to increased experience in pediatric imaging interpretation and specialized fellowship training among pediatric radiologists. Importantly, this discordance influences major patient care decisions including interfacility transfer.

In addition to cross-sectional imaging, assessment for acute appendicitis involves numerous clinical factors that play a role in management decision-making. O’Guinn et al. compared appropriate appendicitis transfers (defined as those who underwent appendectomy) with avoidable transfers (those who were not treated for appendicitis) and found that 59.3% of avoidable transfers underwent outside CT imaging.[10] The authors noted that white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, migratory abdominal pain, and anorexia all had a positive predictive value for appendicitis that was >80%.[10] Although this study did not consider pediatric radiologists’ interpretation of outside CT imaging, the authors highlight the complexity and interplay among the many clinical parameters that determine the diagnosis of appendicitis. This can be seen in our data, where 28% of patients with negative interpretations for appendicitis underwent additional laboratory studies, and 30% underwent additional US imaging workup after transfer to our institution. Furthermore, 35% of patients were admitted to the hospital for observation despite having a negative interpretation. Patients with negative CT or MRI who were admitted often had persistent abdominal pain and were followed with serial exams. Evidently, transfer decision-making in pediatric patients with concern for appendicitis boils down to more than a single imaging datapoint. In our cohort, 11 patients (13.1%) were transferred to our institution despite outside CT/MRI reports that were “not consistent with appendicitis.” While the reason for transfer is unclear in this context, it is likely that the patient’s examination and symptoms prompted transfer. It is possible that a teleradiology consultation with a pediatric radiologist would have provided some clarity or reassurance for the community ED team so that further workup could have been pursued locally in lieu of transfer. This is a clear example of how, even in the setting of diagnostic uncertainty, regional ED teams can benefit greatly from a remote interpretation of cross-sectional imaging by a specialized radiologist.

Our data, in conjunction with other limited studies on this topic, support the need for improved teleradiology and remote interpretation infrastructure to help pediatric surgeons, community ED physicians, and children’s hospital ED physicians triage and facilitate appropriate transfers for appendicitis. This endeavor would have the potential to not only avoid transfers in patients with negative CT or MRI for appendicitis but, more importantly, it would more robustly inform transfer decision-making and patient/family expectations related to transfer of care. It is not uncommon for patients and families to receive counseling in community EDs regarding “positive imaging” for appendicitis. Based entirely on the outside facility’s radiology read, patients are often started on antibiotics before transfer and are informed that the surgery team at the accepting hospital will perform an appendectomy and fix the problem. It is understandable that families are confused and frustrated when they arrive at their destination only to learn that the interpretation is negative and the patient does not in fact have appendicitis. After several hours of waiting and traveling from hospital to hospital, weary families often find themselves tens and hundreds of miles from home and susceptible to distrust in the medical system as a result of this diagnostic discordance. These indirect costs of travel, parental time off work, missed school days for the patient and/or siblings, family burden, and rearranging childcare contribute to poor clinical experiences and patient satisfaction.

The benefit of having a remote interpretation of imaging by a pediatric radiologist is at minimum threefold and includes: 1. Confirmation of appendicitis and accurate patient counseling regarding potential for surgery before transfer, 2. Negation of appendicitis diagnosis on CT/MRI, prompting further workup at community ED or transfer to children’s hospital in resource-limited settings, and 3. Support of an alternate diagnosis on CT/MRI which can either be treated at the community ED or children’s hospital after transfer (if indicated). With the addition of a pediatric radiologist’s evaluation of cross-sectional imaging, appropriate clinical decisions regarding antibiotic administration and expanded workup of abdominal pain can be facilitated in the community ED. Perhaps even more importantly, patients and families can feel confident in the provided radiologic diagnosis and confusion can be minimized. Given user variability and limited availability at regional facilities, US interpretation may not be as feasible or reliable when compared to cross-sectional imaging. Potential barriers to interfacility teleradiology consultation include billing, credentialing, liability issues, access to images, and pediatric radiologist availability.

One argument against teleradiology is the potential burden placed on pediatric radiologists with an increase in imaging interpretation requests from outside hospitals. While there is no data to support this increase in workload in systems where pediatric teleradiology is already implemented, it is important to note that patients are often transferred to children’s hospitals specifically to receive pediatric radiologist reinterpretations. This is due to licensing and credentialing barriers which prevent our pediatric radiologists from accessing and reviewing pediatric imaging statewide before transfer. Therefore, it is very possible that there will be little to no change in workload, simply a change in the timing of the reinterpretation request (pre- versus post-transfer). Our study was not designed to address this question, and therefore, further research is necessary to determine whether this will truly increase pediatric radiologists’ workload. The incentive for healthcare systems to develop strategies to overcome these barriers is the potential for decreased unnecessary interfacility transfers and subsequent system-wide costs.[11] In our cohort, we were limited to facility costs for patients admitted after 2020 and cost estimation for transportation. While this is the best available data at our institution, it is possible that the true cost of transfer and admission is far greater than our estimates. In addition to financial cost, we also must consider the time and resources (e.g., Emergency Medical Services (EMS) transportation) diverted from other patients in need and placed toward transferring patients with ultimately negative CT/MRI interpretations.

Our study was limited by retrospective chart review data and any inaccuracies and deficiencies inherent to the electronic medical record. We presented characteristics of a singular group, patients with negative CT/MRI interpretations, without a comparison group to demonstrate superiority in outcomes. While this was the extent of our project aim, further retrospective and prospective studies are necessary to investigate improvement in patient and healthcare system outcomes with the implementation of teleradiology mechanisms. We did not include CT/MRI technique variability, contrast use, slice thickness, or consensus reinterpretation in our data collection, and these factors may have impacted our outcomes. Our cost analysis was limited by a lack of transportation cost data and physician charge data at our institution. Post-discharge data on return to ED and readmission were limited by electronic medical record capture of encounters within our institution. We did not include misinterpretation of positive cross-sectional imaging (false negative imaging) by community radiologists in our data collection or analysis. While this is an important component of the overall diagnostic discordance between pediatric and community radiologists, our study was not designed to capture this cohort appropriately. Finally, it is possible that our data were impacted by practice changes during the COVID-19 pandemic including diagnostic testing, admission patterns, and regional transfer criteria.

CONCLUSION

The diagnosis of pediatric appendicitis is made using a combination of patient history, physical exam, laboratory findings, and imaging. Children evaluated for appendicitis at regional facilities are more likely to undergo cross-sectional imaging compared to US due to limited resource availability and user dependence. Diagnostic discordance between general radiologists at community hospitals and pediatric radiologists at high-volume children’s hospitals has previously been established, particularly with respect to acute appendicitis. At our institution, we identified a 11.6% negative interpretation rate among patients transferred to our children’s hospital with outside cross-sectional imaging. Implementation of a pediatric teleradiology protocol for outside CT/MRI scans performed to evaluate for acute appendicitis may reduce unnecessary interfacility transfers, inform decision-making at community EDs in the setting of negative imaging, and promote diagnostic accuracy when counseling patients and families. Future studies should aim to design, implement, and analyze such a protocol to determine differences in clinical outcomes, cost outcomes, and patient satisfaction.
