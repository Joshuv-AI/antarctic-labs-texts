Effect of periodontal parameters on maxillary sinus floor augmentation outcomes in a group of periodontitis patients: a retrospective study
Authors: Mehmet Utku Ozcan, Ezgi Gurbuz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesStudies investigating the impact of periodontal disease on sinus floor augmentation (SFA) have focused on periodontal disease history; however, detailed periodontal parameters remain insufficiently addressed. The aim was to evaluate the effects of clinical and radiographic periodontal parameters on SFA outcomes.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study included data from SFA procedures performed using the lateral window approach in patients with periodontitis. Data comprised clinical attachment level (CAL), probing pocket depth (PPD), bleeding on probing, plaque index, gingival index (GI), and radiographic periodontal bone loss data obtained before periodontal treatment. Residual bone height, sinus width, sinus volume, sinus membrane thickness (SMT0), bone gain (BG), graft height (GH), sinus volume difference (SVD), and sinus membrane thickness difference (SMTD) were evaluated on cone beam computed tomography images taken before SFA and at the fourth month. Graft resorption (GR) was assessed by comparing panoramic radiographs taken immediately and four months after SFA.ResultsThis study included 84 sinuses from 60 patients (31 males, 29 females; mean age: 47.38 ± 10.02 years). No significant differences were observed in BG, GH, and SVD regarding clinical and radiographic periodontal parameters. SMTD demonstrated significantly greater values in severe CAL ≥ 3 mm, CAL ≥ 5 mm, PPD ≥ 6 mm, and moderate GI groups (p = 0.007, p = 0.006, p = 0.023, and p = 0.012, respectively). GR was significantly higher in the SMT0 0–2 mm group and the severe CAL ≥ 5 mm group (p = 0.033 and p = 0.034, respectively).ConclusionsClinical periodontal parameters may influence graft resorption and membrane thickness in SFA procedures performed in patients with periodontitis, although comparisons were not adjusted.Clinical relevanceEven under stable periodontal conditions, baseline attachment levels may still influence membrane thickness and graft resorption in the early period following sinus augmentation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07466-1.

Introduction

Decreased bone height in the posterior maxilla due to maxillary sinus pneumatization and alveolar bone resorption often necessitates sinus floor augmentation (SFA) to ensure sufficient bone volume for dental implant placement. Several approaches have been proposed for SFA, with the transalveolar approach and the lateral window technique being the most commonly utilized [1]. The lateral window approach typically involves creating an access window in the lateral wall, elevating the membrane using specialized instruments, and placing bone grafts or substitutes beneath the elevated membrane.

SFA is expected to provide sufficient bone quality and quantity to ensure successful implant placement [2]. In this context, the techniques and biomaterials used for sinus augmentation do not exhibit superiority over one another and are applied predictably [1, 3]. However, several factors may influence the outcome of the procedure. Among local factors, complications such as membrane perforation, bleeding, and sinusitis are particularly significant [4]. Local anatomical factors affecting bone formation or contributing to complications include antral septa, septal deviation, accessory ostium, residual bone height (RBH), sinus width (SW), and sinus membrane thickness (SMT) [5–7]. In addition to local factors, patient-related factors including age, gender, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, medications, smoking, and sinus pathologies may affect the augmentation results [8].

Periodontal disease is regarded as a pathological factor that may influence augmentation outcomes [9–14]. It has been suggested that periodontal pathogen contamination influences clinical and radiological outcomes in patients with a history of periodontitis [9]. Additionally, angiogenesis and tissue remodeling after sinus augmentation are influenced by periodontal disease, leading to altered histological outcomes and negatively impacting bone metabolism [10, 11]. However, despite these claims, some researchers have shown that there is no significant association between periodontitis and SFA outcomes [15, 16]. Studies investigating the impact of periodontal disease on SFA outcomes have primarily focused on the history of periodontal disease; however, detailed clinical and radiographic periodontal parameters remain insufficiently addressed [9–16]. The aim was to evaluate the effect of detailed clinical and radiographic periodontal parameters on SFA outcomes.

Materials and methods

This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Kutahya Health Sciences University (28.11.2023/No. 2023/13–29). Patients were invited to the clinic, informed that their data would be used in the study, and written informed consent was obtained.

Selection criteria

In this study, data from SFA procedures performed using the lateral window approach by a single clinician (E.G.) at the Department of Periodontology, Faculty of Dentistry, Kutahya Health Sciences University between November 2018 and August 2023 were analyzed. Inclusion criteria were defined as follows: patients aged 18 years or older; American Society of Anesthesiologists class I or II physical status; a diagnosis of periodontitis according to the 2017 World Workshop classification; stable periodontal status immediately before SFA (defined as < 10% of sites with bleeding on probing [BOP] and no sites ≥ 4 mm with bleeding); availability of baseline periodontal data including attachment level, pocket depth, gingival, bleeding, and plaque scores obtained before initial periodontal treatment; availability of cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans before SFA and at implant placement; availability of digital panoramic radiographs taken before periodontal treatment, immediately after augmentation, and four months later; and two-stage sinus augmentation procedure.

Exclusion criteria included patients with uncontrolled systemic diseases; conditions affecting bone metabolism; patients using bisphosphonates or similar medications; patients who had undergone radiotherapy in the head and neck region; patients with a baseline periodontal diagnosis other than periodontitis; untreated periodontal disease; smokers or alcohol consumers; pregnant or postpartum women; cases where simultaneous implant placement or horizontal augmentation was performed with sinus augmentation; patients with acute maxillary sinus pathologies, such as sinus opacification or air-fluid level; cases with membrane perforation; and patients with low-quality CBCT or digital panoramic images.

Surgical procedure

A three-dimensional evaluation was made using CBCT obtained before the SFA procedure for patients whose periodontal treatments were completed. Following clinical and radiographic findings, patients were referred to the Department of Otorhinolaryngology in cases recommended by Shanbhag et al. [17]. Patients deemed suitable for augmentation were scheduled, and preoperative antibiotics (1 g amoxicillin tablet; Largopen, Bilim Ilac, Istanbul, Turkey) were started the day before the procedure.

Local infiltration and posterior superior alveolar nerve block anesthesia were administered to the patients using 2 ml of an injectable solution containing 80 mg articaine hydrochloride and 0.02 mg epinephrine (Maxicaine Fort, Vem Ilac, Ankara, Turkey). A midcrestal incision was made in the keratinized gingiva, and vertical releasing incisions were placed anteriorly and posteriorly, extending beyond the mucogingival junction. A mucoperiosteal flap was elevated to access the lateral wall. The window was delineated using a piezoelectric surgical instrument (Piezotome Cube, Acteon, Mérignac, France) (Fig. 1), meticulously removed, and the sharp edges were smoothed to prevent perforation. After accessing the sinus, the sinus membrane was carefully elevated using sinus elevators. The elevation level and perforation status were assessed by allowing the patient to breathe through the nose. Upon confirming the absence of perforation, a large-particle xenograft (1–2 mm) (Cerabone, Botiss Biomaterials, Zossen, Germany) was placed beneath the elevated membrane. The bone window was repositioned over the grafted site, and advanced platelet-rich fibrin (A-PRF), prepared by centrifuging the patient’s blood at 1300 rpm for 8 min, was applied over the window. After hemostasis was achieved, the incisions were closed using absorbable polyglycolic acid sutures (Vicryl, Ethicon, New Jersey, USA).Fig. 1Intraoperative images of sinus augmentation procedure. a Preparation of lateral window. b Removal of lateral window. c Application of xenograft beneath the elevated membrane. d Placement of advanced platelet-rich fibrin over the window

Postoperative instructions were provided to the patients, including a prescription for a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug and an antimicrobial mouth rinse. Ice application was recommended during the first 48 h after surgery to reduce swelling. Patients were advised to follow a lukewarm or cold and soft diet and to avoid using straws or blowing their noses. Sutures were removed two weeks after the surgical procedure. Four months after augmentation, a full-thickness flap was elevated, and implant surgery was performed.

Clinical and radiographic parameters

Clinical and radiographic examination data were obtained before periodontal treatment. Clinical measurements, including clinical attachment level (CAL) (measured from the level of cemento-enamel junction [CEJ] to the base of the gingival sulcus), probing pocket depth (PPD) (measured from gingival margin level to the base of the gingival sulcus), BOP (percentage of sites demonstrating bleeding upon probing) [18], plaque index (PI) (a score between 0 and 3 depending on the degree of plaque) [19], and gingival index (GI) (a score between 0 and 3 depending on the inflammatory status of the gingiva) [20], were recorded using a Williams periodontal probe (ASA Dental, Bozzano, Italy). Periodontal bone loss (PBL) was assessed on the digital panoramic radiographs taken before the periodontal treatment [21]. Radiographic root length was measured from the CEJ to the root apex. The percentage of bone loss was calculated using the following formula: (distance between CEJ and alveolar crest − 1 mm)/root length × 100. Optimal alveolar bone height was defined as 1 mm apical to the CEJ [21, 22].

Digital panoramic radiographs and CBCT scans were acquired using an OP300 device (Instrumentarium, Tuusula, Finland). The images were captured at 90 kV and 3.2 mA, with an exposure time of 8.14 s and a field of view of 80 × 150 mm. The data were saved in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format. Measurements on the CBCT images were conducted using OnDemand3D software (CyberMed, Seoul, Korea).

Cross-sectional images derived from the preoperative CBCT were analyzed for residual bone height (RBH0) (alveolar crest-to-sinus floor distance), sinus width (SW0) (horizontal distance between the lateral and medial sinus walls, measured 10 mm from the alveolar crest) [6], sinus volume (SV0) (a formula of 5/3 x π x mesiodistal width x buccopalatal width was used to calculate sinus cavity volume) [23], lateral wall thickness (LWT) (thickness measured coronally at 5 mm from the deepest point of the sinus floor) [24], and sinus membrane thickness (SMT0) (vertical measurement between the sinus floor and the membrane apex) [25].

The clinical and radiographic periodontal parameters analyzed for the impact on primary and secondary outcomes included CAL, PPD, BOP, PI, GI, and PBL, while the radiographic sinus parameters were RBH0, SW0, LWT, and SMT0. To assess the effect of clinical and radiographic parameters on the outcomes, subgroups were created within the sample, and an analysis was performed on a sinus-specific basis. Sinuses were grouped into mild, moderate, and severe categories: CAL groups were defined by the percentage of sites with attachment loss of ≥ 3 mm and ≥ 5 mm, and PPD groups were created based on the percentage of sites exhibiting probing depths of ≥ 4 mm and ≥ 6 mm (0%: absent; 0–32%: mild; 33–66%: moderate; 67–100%: severe). PI and GI were categorized according to the score distributions (0.5–1.0 for mild, 1.1–2.0 for moderate, and > 2.0 for severe), and PBL was divided into categories based on the percentage of bone loss (< 25%: mild; 25–50%: moderate; >50%: severe). The following groupings were defined for each parameter: BOP as < 30% and ≥ 30%; RBH0 as < 3 mm and ≥ 3 mm; SW0 as narrow (< 8 mm), average (8–10 mm), and wide (> 10 mm); LWT as ≤ 1 mm and > 1 mm; and SMT0 as 0–2 mm, 2–10 mm, and > 10 mm.

Outcome measures

On the CBCT images obtained at the time of implant surgery, RBH1, SV1, SMT1, and graft height (GH) (vertical distance between the deepest point of the sinus floor and the highest point of the graft material) were measured on the reference section determined based on anatomical structures (nasal septum, spina nasalis anterior) in the preoperative CBCT and at the angle identified in this reference section [15]. The primary outcome of this study was bone gain (BG), measured as the difference between RBH1 and RBH0. Secondary outcomes included GH, sinus volume difference (SVD), and sinus membrane thickness difference (SMTD).

Another secondary outcome of this study was graft resorption (GR). GR was assessed by measuring the distance between the top of the crest and the top of the graft using ImageJ software (LOCI, University of Wisconsin, Madison, USA) on panoramic radiographs obtained immediately after the procedure and four months after augmentation [11]. The ramus breadth value measured on the CBCT images was used for calibration in the panoramic images.

Intra-observer calibration

Clinical and radiographic measurements were conducted by a single blinded investigator (M.U.O.). To evaluate intra-observer reliability, clinical measurements from ten randomly selected patients were repeated after a two-day interval, while radiographic images were remeasured following a one-month interval.

Statistical analysis

The sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) at a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05). As the study was novel in nature, a medium standardized effect size of 0.30 [26] and a power value of 0.80 were used to estimate the minimum sample size, which was calculated to be 84.

The data were analyzed using SPSS software (Windows version 25, IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Descriptive statistics for the data (frequency, percentage, mean, standard deviation [SD], median, minimum [min], and maximum [max]) were first obtained. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality, while Levene’s test was applied to examine the homogeneity of variances. When the assumption of normality was met, two groups were compared using an independent samples t-test; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed instead. When comparing three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted if normality was present; otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. Post hoc Bonferroni tests identified specific group differences. To examine associations between continuous variables, Pearson correlation was employed when the data were normally distributed, and Spearman correlation was used otherwise. The Pearson chi-square test was used to analyze the associations between categorical variables. Fisher’s exact test was preferred when the expected values were 5 or fewer. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value < 0.05.

A test–retest analysis was conducted to assess the consistency of clinical and radiographic variables measured at different times by the same investigator. Variability between the initial and subsequent measurements was evaluated using Pearson product-moment and Spearman rank-order correlation tests. Measurements were considered consistent when the correlation coefficients between the repeated measurements exceeded the minimum threshold of 0.70.

Results

A total of 102 two-stage sinus augmentation procedures performed on 76 patients were reviewed for eligibility. Two sinuses were excluded due to simultaneous horizontal augmentation, four due to membrane perforation, and 12 due to poor image quality in CBCT or digital panoramic radiographs. As a result, 84 SFAs from 60 patients (29 women and 31 men) with a mean age of 47.38 ± 10.02 years were included in the study. Patients were classified as having stage 1 (n = 16, 26.7%), stage 2 (n = 23, 38.3%), stage 3 (n = 16, 26.7%), and stage 4 (n = 5, 8.3%) periodontitis. Regarding the grade, the majority of patients (n = 44, 73.3%) had grade B periodontitis, while 10 patients (16.7%) had grade C, and the remaining 6 patients (10%) had grade A periodontitis.

All patients exhibited uneventful healing after the procedure. A total of 138 implants, including Anyridge (Megagen, Gyeongsan, South Korea), ST (Megagen, Gyeongsan, South Korea), and Bilimplant (Bilimplant System, Proimtech, Istanbul, Turkey) systems, were placed in the augmented sinus regions. Following augmentation, the patients were monitored for an average of 2.12 ± 1.43 years.

A difference in GR was also observed among the SMT0 groups (p = 0.032) (Fig. 3). Discussion

Periodontal disease may influence sinus augmentation through multiple direct or indirect factors. The direct effect of periodontal disease on SFA outcomes is believed to occur through periodontal pathogens [9, 27–29]. Periodontal pathogens, toxins, and inflammatory mediators may reach the maxillary sinus either via blood and lymphatic vessels or directly through the trabecular structure of the maxillary bone. This can result in sinus infection and potentially adverse effects on sinus augmentation [9]. Verdugo et al. [9] and Manor et al. [29] performed sinus augmentation surgery on periodontally maintained patients, similar to this study, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analyses of samples taken from the sinus regions revealed the presence of periodontal pathogens even after the periodontal disease was treated.

Galindo-Moreno et al. [10, 11] observed a higher amount of non-mineralized tissue in biopsies obtained from augmented sinuses, as well as greater GR following SFA, in patients with periodontitis under maintenance therapy. In the present study, consistent with the studies mentioned above, a significantly higher GR value was observed in the severe CAL ≥ 5 mm group, and a negative correlation was found between SVD, which represents graft volume, and BOP. However, correlation analysis revealed a weak negative relationship between CAL and GR. While correlation analysis considered outliers, the nonparametric test used to compare groups minimized the effect of outliers on the data.

In a retrospective study by Anduze-Aucher et al. [15], a regression analysis was conducted on 37 SFAs performed with a lateral window approach, and it was concluded that the postoperative surgical space—that is, the sum of crest and graft height—did not demonstrate a significant difference according to the periodontal disease history. However, no information was available regarding periodontal maintenance. In a prospective study, Alayan and Ivanovski [16] performed linear regression analysis and reported that peri-implant marginal bone levels in 53 implants placed in augmented sinuses of periodontally maintained patients did not differ according to disease history. However, a similar prospective study evaluated 267 implants in periodontally maintained patients over a five-year follow-up and concluded that periodontal disease history affected marginal bone levels [12]. In a recent retrospective study with a large sample size and long follow-up, it was demonstrated that the implant failure rate in sinuses augmented with the lateral window approach was higher in patients with periodontal disease history and who did not comply with maintenance treatment [13]. To the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first to comprehensively evaluate the effects of periodontal parameters on augmentation outcomes. Although the methodology differed substantially from that of previous studies, GR was found to be influenced by CAL in a cohort of periodontitis patients who underwent SFA under stable periodontal conditions. However, it should be clear that CAL group comparisons were not adjusted for confounding variables.

Inflammatory infiltration of the sinus due to periodontal disease may also lead to an increase in membrane thickness [27, 28]. The results of this study, consistent with the literature, demonstrated higher SMTD values in the severe CAL ≥ 3 mm, CAL ≥ 5 mm, PPD ≥ 6 mm, and moderate GI groups compared to the mild groups. Increased membrane thickness in periodontitis patients may be one of the indirect effects of periodontal disease on SFA outcomes. This issue can be clarified using regression models that include increases in membrane thickness, periodontal disease parameters, and sinus augmentation outcomes. Indeed, because the regression assumptions were not met in this study, a model could not be created.

When the effect of SMT0 on SFA outcomes was examined, no significant differences were observed in the primary outcome or in GH, SVD, and SMTD. However, GR was significantly greater in the thin membrane group than in the thick membrane group. Considering that Schneiderian membrane thickness increases in inflammatory conditions and that the osteogenic potential of the membrane may be activated in this case [30], this result is not coincidental. Although a recent retrospective study reported no significant association between membrane thickness and bone formation [31], further investigations are warranted to elucidate this relationship.

SFA procedures are often required for the rehabilitation of periodontitis patients due to alveolar bone loss resulting from periodontal disease [32]. The reduction in RBH caused by periodontal disease may indirectly affect the outcome of the procedure. It has been suggested that RBH influences bone quantity after sinus augmentation and that greater RBH is associated with reduced changes in graft dimensions [33]. However, several studies examining the effect of RBH on SFA outcomes have reported contradictory findings [32, 34]. Buchmann et al. [32] argued that when periodontal treatment is completed and optimal oral hygiene conditions are achieved in periodontitis patients, a reduction in RBH does not affect the success of sinus augmentation therapy. Similarly, in this retrospective study, no significant differences were observed among the RBH groups regarding primary and secondary outcomes. The correlation analysis revealed a moderate negative relationship between RBH and BG. This result may be related to the decrease in the expected height increase for ideal-length implant placement as RBH increases.

Apart from the impact of periodontal parameters, another noteworthy finding in this study was the moderate positive correlation between SW and SVD. The buccopalatal dimension of the sinus influences graft volume, and with increasing SW, graft volume decreases [6]. Soardi et al. [23] argued that the best bone formation outcomes could be achieved in narrow and short sinuses, while other sinus sizes exhibited similar amounts of bone. In this study, the included cases did not have narrow sinuses, and no differences were observed between the average and wide sinus groups in terms of primary and secondary outcomes. Since there are methodological differences among studies in terms of surgical technique, sample size, bone evaluation methods, and SW threshold values [6, 23], the effect of sinus dimensions on augmentation is another issue that needs to be investigated.

In this study, the relationship between periodontal disease and sinus augmentation was investigated, and some limitations were acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and the lack of random sampling mean that the internal and external validity of the findings should be interpreted with caution, making causal inferences challenging. However, unlike previous studies, this research provided a detailed analysis of the impact of clinical and radiographic periodontal parameters on augmentation outcomes, potentially offering a significant contribution to the field. Another limitation is the unequal distribution of samples across groups. This issue can be addressed through randomized, multi-center, prospective studies with larger sample sizes. Furthermore, although the groups used to determine the degree of periodontal disease have been used in similar studies [35, 36], the two thresholds in the CAL and PPD groups, in particular, may be confusing. Therefore, it would be more beneficial to obtain statistically significant cut-off values using advanced statistical methods.

Various approaches have been utilized to assess the results of SFA, including three-dimensional tomography, digital panoramic radiography, histomorphometric analysis, micro-computed tomography, peri-implant marginal bone level, and implant survival rates. Of these, micro-computed tomography stands out as a valuable method, as it provides high-resolution, three-dimensional insights into newly formed bone. The absence of such an analysis in the present study limits the interpretation of the findings.

Considering the relationship between gingival phenotype, RBH, and the sinus membrane [37], the lack of phenotype among the clinical periodontal data represents another limitation.

In addition, to draw clinically meaningful conclusions, endodontic infections, another source of infection that can lead to pathological changes in the sinus, should also be taken into consideration.

Based on unadjusted comparisons and limitations of the current study, clinical periodontal parameters appear to influence graft resorption and membrane thickness in the early period following SFA, even under stable periodontal conditions. However, further randomized, multi-center, prospective trials with expanded sample sizes, employing advanced diagnostic methods such as histomorphometry and micro-computed tomography, and including patients with periodontal conditions beyond periodontitis, are warranted to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the influence of periodontal disease on sinus augmentation outcomes.
