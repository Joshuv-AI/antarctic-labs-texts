ν ​ p \nu p -process in Core-Collapse Supernovae: Imprints of General Relativistic Effects
and Amol V. Patwardhan

Abstract
The origin of certain proton-rich isotopes in the solar system, particularly Mo 92 , 94 {}^{92,94}{\rm Mo} and Ru 96 , 98 {}^{96,98}{\rm Ru} , has been a long-standing puzzle. A promising explanation is the ν ​ p \nu p -process, which is posited to operate in the neutrino-driven outflows that form inside core-collapse supernovae after shock revival. Recent studies have identified several relevant physical effects that influence the yields of this process. The impact of General Relativity (GR) on the ν ​ p \nu p -process yields, however, remains unexplored. In this work, we perform a comparative analysis of the time-integrated yields of the p p nuclei up to A ≲ 105 A\lesssim 105 in Newtonian and fully GR neutrino-driven outflows, using a detailed model of a time-evolving outflow profile. The two main GR effects are the gravitational shift of neutrino energies and post-Newtonian corrections to the gravitational potential. These effects together suppress the production of seed nuclei, significantly boosting the ν ​ p \nu p -process yields in our 18 M ⊙ M_{\odot} progenitor model. Most of the production of the crucial Mo 92 , 94 {}^{92,94}{\rm Mo} and Ru 96 , 98 {}^{96,98}{\rm Ru} p p isotopes in this model occurs in an optimal time window, 1–3 seconds after shock revival. Interestingly, the same does not apply to the shielded isotope Nb 92 {}^{92}{\rm Nb} , a large fraction of which is produced in the subsequent ejecta. The impact of GR on this isotope is especially large, with its final abundance boosted by a factor of 25 compared to a Newtonian calculation. In our 12.75 M ⊙ M_{\odot} model, an additional GR effect is observed: the outflow transitions to the supersonic regime several seconds into the explosion, causing the yields to drop. This study quantifies the important role GR effects play in the ν ​ p \nu p -process and provides guidance for identifying optimal conditions in future self-consistent supernova simulations.

Introduction

A vast majority of the stable nuclides in the Universe are produced via “slow” neutron captures (

Such proton-rich isotopes are observed in nature, for example, in meteoritic samples from our solar system. Roughly 35

-rich isotopic abundances in the solar system.

An alternative solution, extensively investigated in the past, consists of a primary process that functions like the isospin mirror of the

Here, we will focus on another mechanism, which we regard to be the most promising solution to this long-standing problem: the

15
,
16

The

20

[
23
]
. To compute the nucleosynthetic yields, one has to model the time evolution of this system, run the reaction network calculation on the time snapshots and integrate the results over time. This can be done either by taking a full simulation of the explosion and postprocessing it, or by creating a realistic, semi-analytic time-evolving model of the outflows. The latter approach has the advantage that it allows one to explore the sensitivity of the yields on various physics ingredients.

Over time, several such physical ingredients have been identified. The first and most obvious is the electron fraction in the NDO,
Y_{e}
. One must have at least
Y_{e}>0.5
to assure proton-rich conditions, with values

\nu_{e}
and
\bar{\nu}_{e}
. Other relevant quantities are the lengths of time spent in the seed nuclei formation window (3–6 GK) and in the window following freeze-out from nuclear quasi-statistical equilibrium (QSE) between the iron group seeds and heavier nuclei (

What other relevant physical phenomena should one consider? The next natural target for investigation are the effects of general relativity (GR), which are the focus of the present paper. While the GR effects are expected to be small at the radii of
\sim 200\mbox{--}2000

Surveying the available literature confirms this basic estimate while also raising further questions.
Studies report increases of entropy per baryon by as much at 30–40 units

In light of these questions, a more systematic analysis of both the formalism and results of GR hydrodynamics in connection to their Newtonian counterparts appears motivated.

Our goal in the present paper is to undertake such a systematic study of the GR effects, on the outflows themselves, and on the ensuing
\nu p
-process. We intend to not only carefully incorporate the fully relativistic physics, as previous works have done, but also analyze the relative import of individual GR corrections, e.g., strengthening of gravity, enthalpic mass, neutrino blueshift, etc. For this, we present a derivation of the NDO equations in a form where the identification and physical content of the relativistic hydrodynamic corrections are transparent. We then take the relativistic outflows as input to our nucleosynthesis calculations and quantify the impacts of the relativistic corrections on the production of different
p
-isotopes. We also present results of GR outflows with termination shocks and assess the effect of the supersonic transition on nucleosynthesis. Following this, we explore the dependence of our conclusions on the relevant physics inputs, particularly the progenitor mass.

To model the yields, we compute the trajectories of tracer particles for a number of time snapshots and then integrate the yields over time. We use our own model of the NDO, which incorporates all GR effects, but can also perform calculations in the fully Newtonian limit or with some of the GR effects turned on and off at will. In each snapshot, the calculation gives the thermodynamic conditions as a function of time and distance to the PNS. Using these results, we post-process the trajectories with the nuclear reaction network

2
General-Relativistic Steady-State Outflows

Below, we derive the relativistic steady-state hydrodynamic equations for spherically symmetric outflows. From the form of these equations, the reduction to Newtonian hydrodynamics is seen to consist of two simple substitutions with clear physical interpretation: (i) relativistic correction to velocity and gravitation and (ii) incorporation of the plasma’s enthalpic (radiation) mass. As in Refs.
[
21
,
22
]
, we do not assume a simple radiation equation of state with a fixed number of relativistic degrees of freedom (RDF)
g_{*}=5.5
. Instead, for the radiation component, we model the evolution of RDF with temperature and present explicit forms of the relevant expressions as they enter into the fluid equations. We also discuss the modification of the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions across termination shocks for transonic outflows. Finally, the physical boundary value problem for solving these differential equations is defined, which combines in a continuous fashion the steady-state outflow with the homologous expansion behind the front shock.

For simplicity, we shall write our equations in units where
\hbar=c=k_{B}=1
.

2.2
Reduction to Newtonian hydrodynamics

Our relativistic steady-state equations reduce to the familiar Newtonian ones under four simple prescriptions, classified as either pertaining to the form of the hydrodynamic equations or to the heating terms. For hydrodynamics, the two reductions are

1.

\mu\rightarrow 1
, which identifies the coordinate and physical velocities, i.e.
u\to v
, and ignores corrections for gravitation and relativistic velocities.

2.

\rho+P\rightarrow\rho_{b}
, i.e. neglecting thermal energy (and pressure) in relation to mass energy, which, for instance,
modifies the form of adiabatic sound speed and overestimates it.

For convenience, we refer to the second Newtonian approximation as the omission of ‘radiation mass’ (
m_{\gamma}\to 0
), since
\rho+P
appears in place of
\rho_{b}
in the relativistic Euler equation (
2.10
). Strictly, we should have called this ‘enthalpic density’ as the ensemble also includes non-relativistic baryon gas. It is worth accentuating that in Newtonian dynamics, there is no enthalpic inertia, and inertia is identified with mass. The corresponding sound speed under this approximation is readily acquired by taking
\rho+P\to\rho_{b}
only in the denominator of Eq. (
2.17
).
2
2

2

This is justified by our decomposition (
2.14
) and that we chose to write the thermodynamic identity in terms of entropy in Eq. (
2.4

We observe that these substitutions are consistent between the fundamental fluid equations (
2.4
) and (
2.10

2.6

2.15

2.9

3

Of course, we could not have instantiated the substitution

2.2

An exception to these simple prescriptions is for the conserved quantity

If we adopt both of these reductions, which encompass (i) weak gravity, (ii) non-relativistic bulk flow, and (iii) domination of baryon mass over thermal energy
\bar{\rho}=\rho_{\gamma}+\bar{\rho}_{b}

2.3
Discontinuity across a termination shock

A famous solution to the stellar outflow equations describes expansion in vacuum
[
47
]
, where
P_{\infty}
, the far pressure at infinity, vanishes. This vacuum (or wind) solution coincides with a critical subsonic solution for all radii up to the sonic point, after which the wind solution becomes supersonic while the critical subsonic. As shown in Ref.
[
22
]
, steady-state solutions of outflows with termination shocks can be obtained by placing the shock after the sonic point and imposing the Rankine-Hugoniot (RH) conditions of discontinuity at that position.

For relativistic outflows, the physical conditions are still the conservation of baryon, momentum, and energy flows. Baryon mass continuity (c.f. Eq. (
2.1
)) requires

n_{1}u_{1}=n_{2}u_{2}\,,

(2.27)

using subscripts 1, 2 to denote quantities before and after the shock respectively. Density of momentum is
T^{0i}
and in spherical symmetry,
T^{0r}
. The flow of momentum
T^{rr}
should be conserved across the shock, as not to be affected by heating:

(\rho_{1}+P_{1})u_{1}^{2}+\left(1-\frac{2GM}{r}\right)P_{1}=(\rho_{2}+P_{2})u_{2}^{2}+\left(1-\frac{2GM}{r}\right)P_{2}\,,

(2.28)

where
r
is the position of the shock. Third, Eq. (
2.20
) indicates that
I
should be conserved since any particle moves across the shock radius in infinitesimal time:

\left(\frac{\rho_{1}+P_{1}}{n_{1}}\right)^{2}\left(1+u_{1}^{2}-\frac{2GM}{r}\right)=\left(\frac{\rho_{2}+P_{2}}{n_{2}}\right)^{2}\left(1+u_{2}^{2}-\frac{2GM}{r}\right).

(2.29)

Given
(u_{1},n_{1},T_{1})
, we would like to calculate
(u_{2},n_{2},T_{2})
from these RH conditions. If we assume radiation domination over the non-relativistic baryon gas (which usually holds by an order of magnitude in pressure), an appropriate approximation at such radii, Eqs. (
2.27
) and (
2.28
) yield a quadratic equation of
n_{2}(T_{2})
. Adopting the positive root, the energy equation (
2.29
) can then be applied to numerically solve for
T_{2}
.

One may consider these relativistic revisions of the Newtonian Rankine-Hugoniot conditions a rather fastidious exercise, as the outflow is expected to become non-relativistic at such large radii. It is true that the gravitational correction
2GM/r
at the sonic point is small, usually a few percents. The domination of mass energy density over radiation is also usually by about two orders of magnitude. Yet, for the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions, the difference between Newtonian and relativistic sound speeds can be significant close to the sonic point. For simplicity, assuming
g_{*}
is constant across the shock, the velocity discontinuity from the Newtonian conditions is
[
22
]

\frac{v_{2}}{v_{1}}=\frac{\rho_{1}}{\rho_{2}}=\frac{{2T_{1}S_{1}}/{m_{N}}+v_{1}^{2}}{7v_{1}^{2}}=\frac{6v_{s}^{2}+v_{1}^{2}}{7v_{1}^{2}}\,,

(2.30)

assuming Newtonian sound speed (with constant
g_{*}
) in radiation domination. Since before the shock,
v_{1}>v_{s}
, velocity is always diminished (density always increased) across the shock. Yet, the relativistic sound speed (c.f. Eq. (
2.14
), with
P_{b}\to 0
in radiation domination) is smaller than the Newtonian due to the incorporation of radiation mass. In this case,
2T_{1}S_{1}/m_{N}>6v_{s}^{2}
, and one may thus obtain with Eq. (
2.30
) an unphysical positive jump in velocity and negative jump in baryon density, for termination shocks sufficiently close to the sonic point. This occurs in the near-critical regime where
v_{1}
is only slightly larger than
v_{s}
. In fact, in computing outflows with termination shocks discussed in Sec.
4.2
, this issue of a positive velocity discontinuity was not infrequently encountered close to the sonic point, persisting outward for about 1% of the sonic radius, which is still more than 20% of the PNS radius. As Ref.
[
22
]
argued, SN outflows can often be near-critical, so the regime close to the sonic point is not entirely an idle concern. This issue is resolved when using the relativistic conditions above.

At this juncture, we also observe that the implementation of three species of effective RDF, as described in App.
A
, is necessary to address the same issue. Had one implemented only one species of
g_{*}(T)
, the condition in Eq. (
2.30
) still follows, and a positive velocity jump can also occur close to the sonic point and persist over an even larger radius than the GR effect. The impact of using
g_{*}(T)
, contra
g_{*}={\rm const}
, for transonic solutions with termination shocks is itself considerable (c.f. Sec.
10.1
), as the vacuum solution already differs significantly.

2.4
Boundary value problems

Prior to comparing general relativistic and Newtonian NDOs, one must specify the conditions under which the outflows are solved and compared. As we shall see concretely in the results of Sec.
4.1
, this is a source of significant difference between results reported in the literature.

The most straightforward method of comparison is as an “initial” value problem, where the state is specified at some radius, e.g., the PNS radius. As such, the GR and Newtonian outflows can differ drastically, not infrequently with a typical subsonic Newtonian outflow compared with a supersonic GR outflow. However, the physical character of the outflow is better known by the thermal conditions (temperature and entropy) at the neutrinosphere and the far pressure of the material expanding behind the front shock
[
18
]
. Thus, it is standard to compare outflows as a boundary value problem (BVP). Admittedly, this renders it more difficult to intuitively anticipate the effects of hydrodynamic corrections to the outflow’s evolution, than for an initial value problem. Because the comparison implies not that “everything being equal” at some radius between the two outflows but instead “everything being so” that the near and far boundary conditions are specified.

The relativistic hydrodynamic corrections do not alter the structure of the Newtonian equations, which contain a vacuum solution and two regimes of subsonic and transonic outflows demarcated by a critical (subsonic) solution. As such, the unified method of solving the BVP presented by Ref.
[
22
]
applies.

Now, the NDO merges at some far radius with the expanding material behind the front shock (FS). This cold material is far from the PNS and surrounds the hot bubble where the NDO forms. It can be approximately modeled as a homologous, i.e. Hubble-like, expansion
[
37
]
. To account for the merging of these two regimes of the outflow,
we adopt a heuristic prescription of “gluing” the steady-state portion with the homologous expansion behind the front shock
[
37
]
, whose Hubble-like velocity profile is given by

v_{h}(r)=\frac{v_{\rm FS}}{r_{\rm FS}}\,r\,,

(2.31)

once the radius and velocity of the FS are specified.
5
5

5

With the motion of the front shock, the physical system is truly time-dependent, and the problem arises of how to model time dependence under a steady-state description. We address this in Sec.
6.1
.
The intersection between
v_{h}(r)
and the velocity profile of the NDO
v(r)
gives an estimate of where merging occurs. Thus, we define a BVP by imposing the far pressure
P_{f}
, which is continuous across the merging point, at the radius
r_{g}
, where

v(r_{g})=v_{h}(r_{g})\,.

(2.32)

For
r>r_{g}
, we consider the outflow to be determined by the homologous expansion and will compute it in a different way when synthesizing tracer trajectories (see Sec.
6.1
). Note that
r_{g}
, in contrast with a fixed far radius, is not a priori known before solving the outflow equations. In this sense, the far boundary condition is dynamic. Nevertheless, the existence of solutions for this BVP follows from that of the traditional BVP (
P_{f}
imposed at a fixed radius). This is apparent from Fig.
1
, which shows the velocity and pressure profiles of solutions of the GR steady-state outflow equations in Sec.
2.1
. All these outflows have the same temperature and baryon density at the gain (starting) radius.

We assume that the intersection of

r_{g}
and lower

r_{g}

P_{f}

v_{0}
to determine the critical value that just avoids giving rise to unphysical supersonic transitions.

We observe that for relatively far gluing positions, pressure would have approached its asymptotic value, for which the results of this BVP will not differ significantly from the traditional one.

Having defined the BVP and described the method of its solution, let us return to the discussion of comparing GR and Newtonian outflows. Neither of the two hydrodynamic corrections from relativity discussed in Sec.
2.2
requires modification of the (thermal) boundary conditions of
T
and
S_{\gamma}
at the gain radius and
P_{f}
at
r_{g}
. So we may impose the same boundary values across outflows including these corrections. What remains to be specified are the parameters of neutrino heating, in particular the luminosities and energy moments. For relativistic outflows, which experience gravitational redshift, neutrino energies must be specified together with the radius. In the studies of
Cardall
&
Fuller
,
Otsuki+
,
Thompson+
(as well as in the post-Newtonian example discussed in Ref.
[
19
]
), Newtonian and GR outflows are prescribed the same neutrino luminosities and energy moments at the neutrinosphere radius. As a result, large enhancements of entropy (
\Delta S\sim 30
) were obtained. In contrast, in our analysis using neutrino luminosities and spectra predicted by SN simulations, which are reported far from the PNS, e.g. at 500 km, as external input
[
48
]
,
following Ref.
[
37
]
. Therefore, we assume the same neutrino luminosity for both GR and Newtonian outflows at this large radius and
not
at the PNS. This means, for a Newtonian outflow, the luminosity at the neutrinosphere is the same as at large radii; while for a relativistic outflow, neutrino energies must be
blueshifted
from the far radius to the neutrinosphere. As a result, while heating rates are similar at large distances, they differ significantly near the PNS, where the outflows originate. This difference in setup from the previous GR studies of

3.2
Processes of neutrino heating and cooling

Processes of neutrino heating and cooling have a formative role in determining the outflows, and they enter the equations of Sec.
2.1
via the heating rate
\dot{q}
. Here, we follow Refs.
[
19
,
20
]
and implement the dominant processes of free nucleon heating and cooling
\nu_{e}+n\leftrightarrow p+e^{-}
and
\bar{\nu}_{e}+p\leftrightarrow n+e^{+}
, heating from elastic neutrino-electron scattering
e+\nu\rightarrow e+\nu
, and cooling through
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation
e^{-}+e^{+}\rightarrow\nu+\bar{\nu}
. In computing these rates, which have been reported in the literature, the following approximations are made: (i) ignoring initial state chemical potentials as well as final state Pauli blocking, (ii) assuming that momentum transfer in these processes is small compared to the nucleon mass (so that the thermal motion of nucleons can be ignored in computing the rates), and (iii) assuming relativistic electrons and positrons (e.g., neglect electron mass as well as the neutron-proton mass difference). These approximations are reasonable in the Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling phase, in the regions close to the PNS where most of the heating occurs
[
19
]
.

In writing the equations in Sec.
2.1
and Sec.
2.2

\dot{q}
as per baryon. It is more standard to express the heating terms as per baryon mass. Thus, we denote the specific heating rate per baryon mass as

(3.4)

The largest contribution to heating is given by the (anti)neutrino absorption by free nucleons
\nu_{e}+n\to p+e^{-}

where the first and second terms in parentheses refer to processes involving
\nu_{e}
and

10^{6}

L_{i,51}

\rho_{8}

in Newtonian geometry.
Moreover, we define the blueshift factor in the Schwarzschild geometry as

\Phi(r)=\sqrt{\frac{1-2GM/R_{\rm ref}}{1-2GM/r}}\,,

(3.8)

which becomes unity in the Newtonian geometry. The second contribution to heating comes from neutrino and antineutrino scattering by electrons and positrons
e+\nu\rightarrow e+\nu
. Its rate is given by

\dot{Q}_{\nu e}\approx 2.17N_{A}\frac{T_{\rm MeV}}{\rho_{8}}\left(L_{\nu_{e},51}\,\epsilon_{\nu_{e}}+L_{\bar{\nu}_{e},51}\,\epsilon_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}+\frac{6}{7}\,L_{\nu_{x},51}\,\epsilon_{\nu_{x}}\right)\frac{1-g_{1}(r)}{R_{\nu 6}^{2}}\Phi(r)^{5}~{\rm MeV}~{\rm s}^{-1}~{\rm g}^{-1}\,,

(3.9)

where
\epsilon_{i}=\langle E_{i}^{2}\rangle/\langle E_{i}\rangle
, (
i=\nu_{e},\,\bar{\nu}_{e},\,\nu_{x}
) is in MeV and we assume the same contribution from non-electron (anti)neutrinos
\nu_{x}=\nu_{\mu},\,\bar{\nu}_{\mu},\,\nu_{\tau},\,\bar{\nu}_{\tau}
. Note that the redshift/blueshift correction to the heating terms
\dot{Q}_{\nu N}
and
\dot{Q}_{\nu e}
can significantly affect the heating rates, as they scale with the sixth and fifth power of the factor
\Phi
, respectively. For instance, assuming
M=1.8~M_{\odot}
and an
R_{\rm ref}
of hundreds of kilometers, the value of
\Phi
at
r=30

1.1
, leading to an enhancement of
78\%
for
\dot{Q}_{\nu N}
and
62\%
for
\dot{Q}_{\nu e}
due to blueshift.

The dominant cooling process is due to electron and positron captures by free nucleons, i.e. the inverse of the reactions in Eq. (
3.5
). Its rate is given by

\dot{Q}_{eN}\approx 2.27\,X_{N}\,N_{A}T_{\rm MeV}^{6}~{\rm MeV}~{\rm s}^{-1}~{\rm g}^{-1}\,,

(3.10)

where
X_{N}
is given by Eq. (
3.6
). Finally, the second contribution to cooling is represented by electron-positron pair annihilation into neutrino-antineutrino pairs of all flavors, approximately described by

\dot{Q}_{e^{+}e^{-}}\approx 0.144~N_{A}\,\frac{T_{\rm MeV}^{9}}{\rho_{8}}~{\rm MeV}~{\rm s}^{-1}~{\rm g}^{-1}\,.

(3.11)

Taking the aforementioned heating and cooling processes the net heating rate
\dot{Q}
is given by

\dot{Q}=\dot{Q}_{\nu N}+\dot{Q}_{\nu e}-\dot{Q}_{eN}-\dot{Q}_{e^{+}e^{-}}\,.

(3.12)

In the equation for the total
\dot{Q}
, we neglect the heating contribution stemming from neutrino-antineutrino pair annihilation into electron-positron pairs given its subdominant role
[
20
]
.

Close to the PNS, the total
\dot{Q}
is dominated by the cooling processes because of the strong dependence on temperature, i.e.
\sim T^{6}
for electron capture on nucleons and
\sim T^{9}
for electron-positron pair annihilation. As the outflow expands and cools down, neutrino heating processes dominate heating the inner layers in the atmosphere of the PNS, resulting in the formation of the NDO.

Results: Analysis of GR Effects on Outflows

In this section, we present results of relativistic outflows defined in Sec.
2
, modeling stellar and neutrino properties as specified in Sec.
3

M_{\odot}
and 12.75
M_{\odot}
—both with an assumed PNS mass of 1.8
M_{\odot}
. More massive progenitors tend to produce more subsonic outflows with a higher far pressure, while a lighter progenitor, coupled with a relatively fast out-moving front shock, allows the appearance of transonic outflows with termination shocks. We examine both cases in this section. For continuity with earlier works
[
19
,
40
,
22
,
37
]
, we
assume radiation domination
in our thermal ensemble,
while the analysis of the non-relativistic baryon-gas component is reserved for Sec.

As our results will concretely demonstrate, the considerable enhancement by GR in entropy of
\sim 30
units reported in the literature
[

,
40
,
20
,
21
]
is due to the convention of prescribing the same neutrino spectra to relativistic and Newtonian outflows at the neutrinosphere rather than at some far radius, where simulations and observations report luminosities. Relying on such inputs from simulations prompts us to adopt the latter convention where luminosities are prescribed the same values at a far radius for both relativistic and Newtonian outflows. In this case, Newtonian and GR outflows have a much more subdued entropy difference, such as reported in
[
37
]
. A fully consistent approach would involve modeling the PNS dynamics together with the outflow in both GR and Newtonian frameworks.

5
\nu p
-process: Stages and Conditions

In the previous sections we have analyzed how general relativistic corrections affect the NDO. From now on, we will focus on their effects on the production of
p
nuclides via the
\nu p
-process. This nucleosynthesis process proceeds in a sequence of thermodynamic stages as the outflow from the PNS expands and cools. Each stage is characterized by different nuclear reactions and equilibrium conditions that determine the evolution of the composition. Below, we summarize the key stages of the
\nu p
-process
[
14
,
17
,
37
]
.

Stage I: NSE and seed formation
.–
The nucleosynthesis path starts in the proximity of the PNS, where the temperature is a few MeV (
T\gtrsim 10
GK) and the density is above
10^{9}\ {\rm g/cm^{3}}
. In these conditions, the plasma is at nuclear statistical equilibrium (NSE), which means that
n
and
p
form nuclei which are immediately broken apart, so that the abundances do not change. Additionally, the charged current weak processes
\nu_{e}\,n\rightleftharpoons e^{-}\,p
and
\bar{\nu}_{e}\,p\rightleftharpoons e^{+}\,n
are also in equilibrium, so that, locally, the electron fraction
Y_{e}
depends on the relative fluxes and energy spectra of
\nu_{e}
and
\bar{\nu}_{e}
. As the temperature drops to around
T\sim 9\,\mathrm{GK}
,
\alpha
-particles (
4
He) begin to form in significant amounts. As long as
T>6\,\mathrm{GK}
, the triple-
\alpha
reaction (
3\alpha\rightleftharpoons{{}^{12}\mathrm{C}}
) remains in equilibrium, but little net production of heavier nuclei occurs. As the temperature decreases below
T<6\,\mathrm{GK}
, the triple-
\alpha
reaction falls out of equilibrium, and seed nuclei with mass number
A\gtrsim 12
begin to form in quasi-statistical equilibrium (QSE). This phase (for
T>4~{\rm GK}
) leads to the production of the seed nucleus
{}^{56}{\rm Ni}
, representing the starting point of the
\nu p
-process. Seeds remain in QSE with heavier nuclei until the temperature drops further (to 3 GK). It is therefore instructive to define the timescale
\tau_{1}
, i.e. the time spent between
T=6
GK and
T=3
GK, representing the duration of the seed production window
[
17
]
. Another useful indicator is the entropy per baryon of the radiation component during this stage—a higher entropy implies a lower baryon density for a given temperature, and consequently, a slower triple-
\alpha
reaction, stymieing seed nuclei formation.

Stage II: Neutron production and bypass of waiting points
.– Outflow continues to cool down. When
T<3\,\mathrm{GK}
, the system freezes out of QSE, and the
\nu p
-process starts, with the nucleosynthesis proceeding beyond
{}^{56}{\rm Ni}
. In the temperature window
3\,\mathrm{GK}>T>1.5\,\mathrm{GK}
(the so-called yellow band, as originally presented in Ref.
[
17
]
), a subdominant population of free neutrons is produced via antineutrino captures on free protons,
p(\bar{\nu}_{e},e^{+})n
.
This process is facilitated if the outflow has a large number of free protons per
{}^{56}{\rm Ni}
seed nucleus at
T=3
GK. The subdominant population of neutrons that results from neutrino capture triggers
(n,p)
and
(n,\gamma)
exchange reactions that allow the nucleosynthetic flow to bypass slow
\beta^{+}
-decay waiting points. Combined with proton captures
(p,\gamma)
, this enables the synthesis of nuclei along the
rp
-process chain. The longer its duration, the higher the efficacy of this process. Therefore, it is useful to define the timescale
\tau_{2}
, i.e. the time spent between
T=3
GK and
T=1.5
GK, representing the duration of the
\nu p
-process. The ratio
\tau_{2}/\tau_{1}
is a good indicator of the efficacy of the
\nu p
-process. In this context, another useful parameter is the ratio of neutrons produced (by antineutrino capture on protons) to seed nuclei, given by

\Delta_{n}=\frac{Y_{p}\,n_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}}{Y_{A\geq 12}}\,,

(5.1)

where
Y_{p}
and
Y_{A\geq 12}
are the proton and the seed fractions at
T=3
GK, while
n_{\bar{\nu_{e}}}
represents the number of
\bar{\nu}_{e}
captured per free proton in the yellow band, leading to neutron formation. More explicitly
[
17
]

n_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}=\int_{T=3~{\rm GK}}^{T=1.5~{\rm GK}}\lambda_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}dt\,,

(5.2)

where
\lambda_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}
is the rate for electron antineutrino capture on free protons
p(\bar{\nu}_{e},\,e^{+})n
. This can be approximately written as
[
16
]

\lambda_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}\approx 0.06\,\frac{L_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}}{10^{52}~\erg/\sec}\,\frac{T_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}}{4~\MeV}\left(\frac{10^{8}~\cm}{r}\right)^{2}\,,

(5.3)

which is a function of the neutrino emission properties and the distance to the neutrinosphere. The dependence of the rate on
r
in Eq. (
5.3
) encodes the scaling of the antineutrino flux
\mathcal{F}_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}\propto r^{-2}
. Therefore,
\lambda_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}
will decrease with distance, while
\Delta_{n}
decreases as well making the
\nu p
-process less efficient if the yellow band is located farther from the PNS. It is worth defining two critical radii,
R_{3}
and
R_{1.5}
, which represent the distances at which the outflow enters the yellow band at
T=3
GK and leaves it at
T=1.5
GK, respectively.

Stage III: Proton capture freeze-out and late-time neutron captures
.– As the temperature falls below
T<1.5\,\mathrm{GK}
, Coulomb barriers prevent further proton captures via
(p,\gamma)
reactions. However, free neutron production via neutrino interactions on free protons
p(\bar{\nu}_{e},e^{+})n
continues at these late times. The number of neutrons produced from neutrino captures on protons is given by
[
17
]

\Delta_{n}^{\prime}=\frac{Y_{p}\,n_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}^{\prime}}{Y_{A\geq 12}}\,,

(5.4)

where
n_{\bar{\nu_{e}}}^{\prime}
is the number of
\bar{\nu}_{e}
captured per free proton in the temperature range
T<1.5
GK

n_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}^{\prime}=\int_{T\leq 1.5~{\rm GK}}\lambda_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}dt\ .

(5.5)

These late-time neutrons enable additional
(n,\gamma)
and
(n,p)
reactions that help push the composition toward the valley of stability. Notably, the metastable isotope
{}^{92}\mathrm{Nb}
, which is shielded from
\beta^{+}
decays of
p
-rich nuclei by the stable
92
Mo, can be synthesized in the
\nu p
-process in this way. The late-time availability of a few neutrons per seed nucleus (
\sim
3-6) proves to be optimal for the efficient production of these isotopes
[
37
]
.

Stage IV: Late-time decays to stability
.– At late times, by
t\sim 10^{9}\,\mathrm{s}
, the
\beta^{+}
decays of unstable nuclei complete the formation of stable isotopes.

1.

We construct tracer trajectories based on the computed steady-state NDOs.

2.

We compute the nucleosynthesis yields using the reaction network
SkyNet

[

,
60
]

Details of our methodology are presented below, with results discussed in Sec.
7
and Sec.
8
.

6.1
Synthesis of tracer trajectories

Computation of nucleosynthetic yields requires tracer trajectories covering the stages described in Sec.
5
. Following Ref.
[
37
]
, we divide such trajectories into two segments: the first one is the outward acceleration of the tracer from the PNS surface, followed by deceleration through interaction with the surrounding slowly expanding material. The initial stages of the
\nu p
-process occur during this first leg. In the second segment, the tracer, upon joining the homologous expansion of the material behind the front shock, moves at an approximately constant velocity
[
17
,
61
]
. In this phase, which needs to be modeled over several seconds, the latest stages of the
\nu p
-process occur, including the synthesis of
{}^{92}{\rm Nb}
.

Since the duration of the first segment is
\Delta t\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
s, we generate this part of the trajectory from the steady-state NDO discussed in Sec.
2
. Specifically, for each trajectory, the underlying outflow is determined with the neutrino heating properties and boundary conditions at the time the tracer merges into the homologous expansion, i.e., at the end of the segment. This choice of adopting conditions at the later merger time (rather than the launch time) allows us to obtain a trajectory continuous with the second segment. Nevertheless, here the inherent limitation of our steady-state approximation becomes apparent, since the duration from launch to merger is non-negligible. Indeed, this is an important and ineluctable source of error for our analysis. For instance, one may prefer to use instead the conditions at launch time, which is likely more appropriate for the earlier portion of the trajectory. Yet, under this convention, there will be a discontinuity with the second segment of the trajectory, since the far boundary has altered (
P_{f}
has lowered) by the time the tracer merges since the front shock has proceeded further outward.

The second segment of the trajectory starts at the gluing radius
r_{g}
defined in Eq. (
2.32
), when the NDO’s velocity falls to the value estimated from homologous expansion (see, e.g. the thin solid lines in the velocity panels of Fig.
3
and Fig.
4
). After the tracer reaches this gluing position, the state of the tracer is dictated by the properties of the homologous material and its entropy before merging. Specifically, the tracer velocity is subsequently held constant at its value at
r_{g}
, where the velocities of the steady-state NDO and the homologous material agree. Its entropy
S
is also kept constant ever after. Additionally, its temperature
T
and baryon number density
n
are determined from the entropy
S(n,T)
the tracer had immediately before gluing and the far pressure
P_{f}(n,T)
, which is continuous across the boundary between the NDO and the homologously expanding material. This procedure allows us to generate
T(t)
and
n(t)
for the second segment of the trajectory based on the time evolution of the far pressure
P_{f}
, which is computed from the progenitor as described in Sec.
3.1
.

This gluing prescription enables us to follow the tracers through the different stages of the
\nu p
-process, enabling the exploration of various physical conditions without requiring full SN simulations. As mentioned, an intrinsic limitation of this framework is the steady-state assumption for the first segment of duration
\Delta t
, which prevents us from capturing the time evolution of neutrino properties and boundary conditions during this phase. In our analysis, we assume that these properties remain constant over the duration
\Delta t\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
s, taking the values they have at the gluing point.

However, as discussed in more detail in Sec.
6.2
, when computing the yields with
SkyNet
, we do evolve the neutrino luminosities and the PNS radius also during the initial segment of the trajectory. Specifically, we assume that the outflow solution at time
t
corresponds to a trajectory launched at
t_{\rm launch}\equiv t
, and we internally evolve
L_{\nu_{i}}
and
R_{\rm PNS}
in
SkyNet
, starting from their values at that time. The time-evolving
R_{\rm PNS}
represents a significant advancement over our previous work in Ref.
[
37
]
.
However, a fully consistent treatment of time-dependent NDO and a systematic assessment of the steady-state approximation are left to future work.

6.2
Computation of yields and time evolution

We compute nucleosynthesis yields by post-processing the tracer trajectories described in the previous section with the open-source reaction network
SkyNet

[
41
,
60
]
. In particular, for each mass element launched at a given
t_{\rm launch}
, we use as
SkyNet
inputs the time evolution of its temperature
T
and density
\rho
, its location
r
, as well as the PNS radius
R_{\rm PNS}
and (fixed) mass
M_{\rm PNS}
. We stress that the time evolution of the PNS radius represents an improvement over the constant
R_{\rm PNS}
assumed in Ref.
[
37
]
. Moreover, we provide
SkyNet
with the time-dependent electron (anti)neutrino luminosities
L_{\nu_{i}}
(
\nu_{i}=\nu_{e},\,\bar{\nu}_{e}
) and the properties of the neutrino emission spectra
T_{\nu_{i}}
and
\eta_{\nu_{i}}
, defined in Sec.
3.2
. To be consistent with the outflow solutions, we also take into account GR effects on the neutrino temperatures and luminosities when needed.
7
7

7

In
SkyNet
, for each of the eight cases encompassing the different relativistic corrections outlined in Sec.
4
, an appropriate binary choice of blueshift vs no-blueshift (consistent with the respective outflow solutions) is made for the neutrino luminosities and temperatures.
For further details about the system setup with
SkyNet
we refer the reader to App.
C
.

Each iteration of
SkyNet
allows us to compute instantaneous yields produced by a single mass element launched at
t_{\rm launch}
. However, to compare our results with the solar system abundances, the computation of the time integrated yields is required. To this end, we sequentially solve outflow equations
starting at
t_{\rm launch}=1
s, with increments of
\Delta t_{\rm launch}=0.1
s between each launched tracer.
For each outflow solution, we compute the tracer trajectories and provide
Skynet
with the quantities previously described. In this way, we are able to simulate the SN evolution and the resulting yields during all the cooling phase.

8.3
Stage III nucleosynthesis and
{}^{92}{\rm Nb}
production

As discussed in the previous sections, relativistic effects have a quantifiable impact on the production of metastable isotopes, such as
{}^{92}{\rm Nb}
and
{}^{98}{\rm Tc}
, which are produced in stage III of the
\nu p
-process, many seconds after the trajectories are launched, when the temperature is lower than 1.5 GK. Notably, the time-averaged yields of these nuclides are enhanced by a factor of
\sim 25
and
\sim 350
, as shown in Tab.
4
. This strong enhancement is related to the optimal production time window of
{}^{92}{\rm Nb}
, which occurs around
t_{\rm launch}\sim 5
s in our benchmark model; about
2.5
s after the peak production of the other
p
nuclides such as
{}^{92,94}{\rm Mo}
and
{}^{96,98}{\rm Ru}
(see Fig.
9
). These metastable nuclides are produced at later times due to an increase in the number of neutrons per seed in stage III,
\Delta_{n}^{\prime}
(see Eq. (
5.4
)). Specifically, for
t_{\rm launch}\gtrsim 2.5
s, the NDOs enter the homologous expansion phase at temperatures below 1.5 GK. This implies that the yellow band is exited earlier and at smaller radii, closer to the PNS (as indicated by the kink in
\tau_{2}
and
R_{1.5}
for
t_{\rm launch}\gtrsim 2.5
s; see Fig.
8
). Consequently, more antineutrinos are captured at later times, leading to an increased neutron production (
\Delta_{n}^{\prime}
) and enhancing the efficiency of nuclide production during stage III. At these late launch times, relativistic effects are more pronounced due to a smaller PNS radius (see Fig.
2
). As discussed in Sec.
8.1
, relativistic effects can have a larger impact on the number of available neutrons per seed
\Delta_{n}^{\prime}
in stage III.
This is due to the reduction in
n_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}^{\prime}
for the GR case relative to NW staying roughly constant with time, in combination with the GR enhancement of the ratio
Y_{p}/Y_{A\geq 12}
, which gets stronger with time. Therefore,
\Delta_{n}^{\prime}
strongly increases (by a factor
\sim 3
) in the optimal production time window for
{}^{92}{\rm Nb}
, thus explaining the important role of GR effects in the synthesis of this nuclide.

As shown by the green star in Fig.
11
,
{}^{92}{\rm Nb}
is sufficiently produced in the GR case, while its production is extremely suppressed for NW, being two orders of magnitude lower than what suggested by observations. Here, we normalize the
{}^{92}{\rm Nb}
production factor to
3\times 10^{-3}
times the solar system meteoritic
{}^{92}{{\rm Mo}}
abundance. We choose this normalization since
{}^{92}{\rm Nb}
itself is unstable and cannot be observed in the present-day solar system. However, studies of meteoritic compositions, combined with galactic chemical evolution models, have shown that the ratio of
{}^{92}{\rm Nb}/^{92}{\rm Mo}
at production has to be in the range
10^{-3}
–
10^{-2}

[
7
,
63
,
64
,
65
]
. This amount is sufficient to explain the solar system observations of
{}^{92}{\rm Zr}
, into which
{}^{92}{\rm Nb}
eventually decays
[
7
,
63
,
64
,
65
]
. Similar predictions can also be made for the other shielded isotopes produced by late-time neutrons. For
{}^{98}{\rm Tc}
, we predict an abundance of approximately
5\times 10^{-5}
relative to
{}^{98}\mathrm{Ru}
, which is consistent with the current upper limit observed in the solar system
[
66
]
, owing to its half-life of
4.2
Myr
[
67
]
.

This discussion highlights that the most pronounced GR effects appear in the production of metastable isotopes synthesized through late-time neutron captures, partly owing to the fact the
92
Nb production peaks at later launch times, when the GR effects on seed production are stronger.
It is also noteworthy that
{}^{92}{\rm Nb}
, along with a fraction of
{}^{92}{\rm Mo}
, may alternatively be produced in mildly neutron-rich NDOs
[
68
]
.

Other Corrections

So far in our analysis, our numerical results concerned the effects of GR corrections. In this final section prior to the conclusion, we assess the impact of other relevant physical components on the
\nu p
-process: variable RDF
g_{*}(T)
and the non-relativistic baryonic gas in the thermal ensemble of the plasma.

10.1
Variable effective relativistic degrees of freedom

In this paper, we have implemented a consistent set of
g_{*}(T)
’s (see App.
A
) in our steady-state equations; however, their effect on determining the outflows remains to be examined. As they enter into the thermal state functions, their influence is accentuated and most conspicuous when the non-relativistic baryon gas is ignored. Thus, we again assume radiation domination in this section. Their impact is also more transparently attributed if we assume the far pressure
P_{f}
is imposed at a fixed radius, so that
g_{*}(T)
’s modification of velocity is not fed-back to the determination of the far boundary location
r_{g}
.

When solving the BVP, the zeroth-order correction one could make without implementing
g_{*}(T)
in the evolution equations is to use
g_{*}^{P}(T)
when matching with the far pressure
P_{f}
. Otherwise, assuming
g_{*}=5.5
, the terminal temperature is bound to be an underestimate of the correct value. Thus, for the comparison in this section, which assumes
P=P_{\gamma}(T)
, we stipulate that the outflows, with and without
g_{*}(T)
, share the far temperature
T_{f}
at a fixed far radius of
10^{4}
km. We also impose the same boundary conditions at the gain radius, i.e., temperature and radiation entropy, where
g_{*}\simeq 5.5
.

For subsonic solutions, the influence of
g_{*}(T)
is small in temperature and entropy (usually sub-percent level for the latter) while more noticeable in velocity and baryon density. The negligible change in entropy is reasonable, since the entropy development in the heating region is effectively concluded before temperature decreases to below the electron mass. In turn, the terminal baryon density is smaller by the fraction
g_{*}^{S}(T_{f})/5.5
, since the far temperature
T_{f}
is the same. An interesting effect of
g_{*}(T)
is the acceleration of the outflow velocity. As the velocity at the gain radius approaches the critical value for the wind solution, the outflow with variable
g_{*}
can have a peak subsonic velocity that is a few times higher than that in the constant-
g_{*}
scenario. This is due to the correction of sound speed as given in Eq. (
2.14

v_{s}^{2}=\frac{P_{\gamma}}{\rho+P_{\gamma}}\Bigg(\frac{4+\tilde{g}_{*}^{P}}{3+\tilde{g}_{*}^{S}}\Bigg)\,.

(10.1)

For constant
g_{*}

{4}/{3}

19

10.2
Baryonic gas component

In the numerical results concerning GR effects on outflows and nucleosynthesis presented so far, we assumed radiation dominates in the thermal ensemble. Though a pertinent approximation for the body of the NDO, the baryon gas constitutes a coequal thermal component in the plasma around the gain radius. With the radiation entropy
S_{\gamma}=6
at the PNS as we have employed, radiation pressure and internal energy somewhat exceed those of the baryon gas. Yet, since here is also the region of intense heating that determines entropy development, it is plausible that the incorporation of the baryonic gas has a formative effect on the character of the outflow.
The other place in our computations we assumed radiation domination is in computing the far pressure from the progenitor profile assuming homologous expansion. In this cold material, radiation pressure is comparable to the baryonic component, so
P_{f}
will be increased. This latter correction of
P_{f}
, which renders outflows more subsonic, has a subdued effect for the outflows and nucleosynthesis of the 18
M_{\odot}
model, though for outflows with near-critical far pressures, such as for the 12.75
M_{\odot}
progenitor, this inclusion can proscribe or delay a supersonic transition. More influential and interesting is the incorporation of the baryon gas in the evolution equations themselves.

Here, we study the impact of incorporating the baryon gas on subsonic outflows, based on our benchmark 18
M_{\odot}
progenitor model, which has comparatively optimal yields. As noted earlier in Sec.
2.1
, we assume that the baryon gas is
monatomic
, without tracking the composition of nuclides. This exaggerates its contribution at later stages of the outflow and thus tends to slightly overstate the impact of the baryon gas as presented here. The GR outflows at
t=2.5

11
Summary and Conclusions

In this paper, we have investigated and quantified the effects of General Relativity on the yields of the
\nu p
-process in a core-collapse supernova. Our findings can be summarized as follows.

•

Although the main stage of the process—the one involving repeated captures of protons and neutrons on seed nuclei—occurs hundreds of kilometers from the surface of the PNS where GR effects are small, the GR corrections to the
\nu p
-process yields are nonetheless significant. The reason for this is that the GR effects impact the crucial region tens of kilometers above the PNS, which hosts the engine of the neutrino-driven outflow. Changes there affect the entire test particle trajectory, even qualitatively altering the nature of the outflow, under some circumstances. The inclusion of GR effects generally leads to improved
\nu p
yields, but with several important caveats.

•

The two main impacts of GR are due to the gravitational shift of the neutrino energies with radius and the deepening of the effective gravitational potential. This was shown by investigating “partial GR” implementations, which were designed to deconstruct the relevant physics. Since neutrino cross sections are energy-dependent, taking the gravitational shifts into account changes the total energy deposited into the outflow
[
20
]
, thereby affecting the expansion speed, the mass loss rate, and the entropy per baryon in the outflow. The deepening of the effective gravitational potential also impacts the value of the entropy, but with an opposite sign.

•

In turn, these two effects can influence the
\nu p
yields in several different ways. The increase in the expansion speed close to the PNS surface decreases the time the material spends in the seed-forming region, which suppresses seed production, thus leading to a more favorable neutron-to-seed ratio during the main
\nu p
stage. The would-be negative impact on the entropy caused by the neutrino energy shift is more than compensated by the modification of the effective gravitational potential and the resulting modest entropy boost further assists the yields.

•

On the other hand, the enhanced heating can, under certain circumstances, accelerate the material to supersonic speeds. As we had shown before
[
37
]
, supersonic outflows are generally characterized by poor
\nu p
yields and our studies here further confirm this. Whether the GR effects cause the outflow to become supersonic depends on the interplay of the neutrino luminosities, the progenitor mass and the front shock expansion rate. In the cases considered here, the 18
M_{\odot}
model remained safely subsonic, while the 12.75
M_{\odot}
model exhibited a supersonic transition, with the accompanying
\nu p
yield drop. The 12.75
M_{\odot}
model perfectly illustrates the near-critical nature
[
22
]
of the outflow in supernova conditions.

•

The calculations here confirm that the most of the crucial
p
nuclides, especially
92,94
Mo and
96,98
Ru, are produced in a well-defined time window, lasting 1-2 seconds. This optimal window is defined by the interplay of two factors: (i) the duration of time the material spends in the temperature region between 3 and 1.5 GK and (ii) the PNS radius, which sets the entropy of the outflow material. Thus, in modeling the
\nu p
-process yields, it is important to take the time evolution of the PNS radius into account.

•

The analysis of the
92
Nb yields deserves a special mention. This important nuclide serves as an astrochronometer and its origins have been the focus of much research over the decades. It was shown in
[
37
]
that, despite being shielded from beta decay paths by the stable
92
Mo, it is nonetheless produced in the
\nu p
-process. This production is driven by late-time neutrons, produced after the mass element enters the temperature region
\lesssim 1.5
GK. Here we show that the optimal time window for the production of
92
Nb occurs later than that of the standard
\nu p
nuclides. At these times, the radius of the PNS is considerably decreased
[
61
,
69
,
56
,
70
]
, therefore, the GR effects on the outflow and hence on the yields are more pronounced. We find that GR boosts the
92
Nb yields by a factor of 25, the largest effect of all nuclides considered.

In comparing our outflow model with those in the earlier literature, one must specify which quantities are being held fixed as one switches between the Newtonian and GR calculations. This specifically applies to the neutrino energy spectra, which we take as external inputs from published explosion simulations. Since most modern numerical simulations report their spectra at 500 km
[
61
,
71
,
54
,
55
,
72
,
70
,
56
,
73
]
, rather than at the neutrinosphere, this is the convention we adopt. It should also be noted that this is the relevant convention from the experimental point of view. We confirmed that this is the largest source of differences between us and earlier studies which assumed that the GR and Newtonian setups have the same neutrino spectra at the neutrinosphere. Other differences include accounting for the varying number of radiation degrees of freedom, including radiation inertia terms in the sound speed and, very importantly, the self-consistent treatment of the subsonic or supersonic nature of the outflow.

Future studies should explore the impact of flavor oscillations, quantify the limitations of the steady-state approximation for the first stage of the outflow and assess the role of multi-dimensional hydrodynamics. The latter two can be pursued by incorporating into the nucleosynthesis study the outputs of detailed numerical simulations. Our results in this paper should provide guidance for identifying optimal conditions for these studies and for ensuring the relevant GR effects are included.

\displaystyle=4\pi r^{2}\rho_{b}u=\text{constant}\implies\frac{2}{r}+\frac{1}{\rho_{b}}\frac{d\rho_{b}}{dr}+\frac{1}{u}\frac{du}{dr}=0,

(B.1)

\displaystyle u\frac{du}{dr}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\rho+P}\frac{dP}{dr}\left(1+u^{2}-\frac{2GM}{r}\right)-\frac{GM}{r^{2}},

(B.2)

\displaystyle\dot{Q}

\displaystyle=u\left(\frac{d\epsilon}{dr}-\frac{P}{\rho_{b}^{2}}\frac{d\rho_{b}}{dr}\right).

(B.3)

where
u=dr/d\tau=vy
is the radial component of the four-velocity, with
y=[(1-2GM/r)/(1-v^{2})]^{1/2}
. Here, the specific heating rate
\dot{Q}
per unit mass should be interpreted as the proper time-derivative
dQ/d\tau
. In the relativistic case, an aspect that requires careful consideration is the distinction between the baryon density
\rho_{b}=m_{N}n
and the total density
\rho=\rho_{b}+\rho_{b}\epsilon
, where
\epsilon
is the specific internal energy. As a result,
the sound speed
v_{s}
acquires a relativistic correction when compared to the Newtonian expression.

Here, for simplicity, we adopt the same approximations of
Cardall
&
Fuller
: a constant number of RDF (
g_{\star}=11/2
) and omission of the baryonic gas in the thermal ensemble. The pressure, specific internal energy, and entropy per baryon then assume the forms:

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\frac{11\pi^{2}}{180}T^{4},

(B.4)

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle=\frac{11\pi^{2}}{60}\frac{T^{4}}{\rho_{b}}=\frac{3P}{\rho_{b}},

(B.5)

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\frac{11\pi^{2}}{45}\frac{T^{3}}{\rho_{b}/m_{N}},

(B.6)

and the sound speed can be calculated as:

v_{s}^{2}=\frac{\partial P}{\partial\rho}\,\bigg|_{S}=\frac{1}{\partial(\rho_{b}+\rho_{b}\epsilon)/\partial P\,\big|_{S}}=\frac{1}{3+\partial\rho_{b}/\partial P\,\big|_{S}}=\frac{TS}{3m_{N}}\left(1+\frac{TS}{m_{N}}\right)^{-1},

(B.7)

where the last equality above follows from

\frac{\partial P}{\partial\rho_{b}}\bigg|_{S}=\frac{11\pi^{2}}{180}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{b}}\left(\frac{45}{11\pi^{2}}\frac{\rho_{b}S}{m_{N}}\right)^{4/3}\bigg|_{S}=\frac{4P}{3\rho_{b}}=\frac{TS}{3m_{N}},

(B.8)

which incidentally is the expression for
v_{s}^{2}
in the Newtonian limit.
Next, from Eqs. (
B.4
) and (
B.6
), we have

\rho+P=\rho_{b}+4P=\rho_{b}\left(1+\frac{TS}{m_{N}}\right).

(B.9)

Moreover, from the definition
u=v\,y
,

1+u^{2}-\frac{2GM}{r}=1-\frac{2GM}{r}+\frac{v^{2}}{1-v^{2}}\left(1-\frac{2GM}{r}\right)=\frac{1-2GM/r}{1-v^{2}}=y^{2},

(B.10)

which shows that
y^{2}
corresponds to the GR factor
\mu
in Eq. (
2.11
), appearing in our outflow equations. For later use, we also calculate the derivative of
y
:

\begin{split}\frac{dy}{dr}&=\frac{1}{1-v^{2}}\left[-\frac{(1-v^{2})^{1/2}}{(1-2GM/r)^{1/2}}\,\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{GM}{r}\right)+\frac{(1-2GM/r)^{1/2}}{(1-v^{2})^{1/2}}\,v\,\frac{dv}{dr}\right]\\[5.0pt]
&=\frac{1}{1-v^{2}}\left[-\frac{1}{y}\,\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{GM}{r}\right)+yv\,\frac{dv}{dr}\right].\end{split}

(B.11)

Let’s begin our exercise with the
heat equation
: Eq. (
B.3
). Since,
dq=TdS
, and with
dQ=dq/m_{N}
(per unit mass vs per baryon), one can write

\displaystyle\dot{Q}=\frac{dQ}{d\tau}=\frac{1}{m_{N}}\frac{dq}{dr}\frac{dr}{d\tau}=\frac{uT}{m_{N}}\frac{dS}{dr}

\displaystyle\implies\boxed{vy\frac{dS}{dr}=\frac{\dot{Q}m_{N}}{T}}

(B.12)

which is precisely Eq. (3) from
Cardall
&
Fuller
and our Eq. (
2.4
), given that
y=\sqrt{\mu}
.

From Eq. (
B.6
), one can then write

\frac{\dot{Q}}{vy}=\frac{3S}{m_{N}}\frac{dT}{dr}-\frac{TS}{m_{N}}\frac{1}{\rho_{b}}\frac{d\rho_{b}}{dr},

(B.13)

where we have used Eqs. (
B.6
) and (
B.4
) to obtain the last equality.

Next, we can deal with the
Euler equation
: Eq. (
B.2
). This has the same form of our Eq. (
2.10
). Using Eqs. (
B.5
), (
B.9
), and (
B.10
), we can write

u\frac{du}{dr}=-\frac{1}{\rho_{b}(1+TS/m_{N})}\frac{11\pi^{2}}{45}T^{3}\,\frac{dT}{dr}\,y^{2}-\frac{GM}{r^{2}}=-\frac{S/m_{N}}{1+TS/m_{N}}\,y^{2}\,\frac{dT}{dr}-\frac{GM}{r^{2}},

(B.14)

which, using the product rule for derivatives, can be further rewritten as

u\frac{du}{dr}=-\frac{1}{1+TS/m_{N}}\,y^{2}\left[\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{TS}{m_{N}}\right)-\frac{T}{m_{N}}\frac{dS}{dr}\right]+\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{GM}{r}\right),

(B.15)

where we have assumed that the mass
M
supplying the gravitational potential is overwhelmingly concentrated within the PNS, allowing us to neglect the
dM/dr
term. The
dS/dr
term on the right-hand side (RHS) can be replaced using Eq. (
B.12
). Dividing through by
y^{2}
and using
u=vy
on the left-hand side (LHS), this becomes

v\frac{dv}{dr}+\frac{v^{2}}{y}\frac{dy}{dr}=-\frac{d}{dr}\log\left(1+\frac{TS}{m_{N}}\right)+\frac{1}{1+TS/m_{N}}\frac{\dot{Q}}{vy}+\frac{1}{y^{2}}\,\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{GM}{r}\right)

(B.16)

Plugging in Eq. (
B.11
) on the LHS and using Eq. (
B.10
) we obtain

\begin{split}&\frac{d}{dr}\left[\frac{v^{2}}{2}\right]+\frac{v^{2}}{1-v^{2}}\frac{d}{dr}\left[\frac{v^{2}}{2}\right]-\frac{v^{2}}{1-2GM/r}\,\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{GM}{r}\right)\\
&=-\frac{d}{dr}\log\left(1+\frac{TS}{m_{N}}\right)+\frac{1}{1+TS/m_{N}}\frac{\dot{Q}}{vy}+\frac{1}{y^{2}}\,\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{GM}{r}\right).\end{split}

(B.17)

Gathering similar terms together, simplifying, and rearranging yields the result

\boxed{vy\,\frac{d}{dr}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-v^{2})+\log\left(1+\frac{TS}{m_{N}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1-\frac{2GM}{r}\right)\right]=\frac{\dot{Q}}{1+TS/m_{N}}\,,}

(B.18)

which is Eq. (2) from
Cardall
&
Fuller
.

Finally, let us invoke the
continuity equation
: Eq. (
B.1
), corresponding to our Eq. (
2.9
) since
\rho_{b}=m_{N}\,n
. Starting with Eq. (
B.14
), we can replace
S/m_{N}\,dT/dr
using Eq. (
B.13
), and
d\rho_{b}/dr
using Eq. (
B.1
), yielding:

\begin{split}u\frac{du}{dr}&=-\frac{y^{2}}{1+TS/m_{N}}\,\left[\frac{\dot{Q}}{3vy}+\frac{TS}{3m_{N}}\frac{1}{\rho_{b}}\frac{d\rho_{b}}{dr}\right]-\frac{GM}{r^{2}}\\
&=-\frac{y^{2}}{1+TS/m_{N}}\,\left[\frac{\dot{Q}}{3vy}-\frac{TS}{3m_{N}}\left(\frac{2}{r}+\frac{1}{u}\frac{du}{dr}\right)\right]-\frac{GM}{r^{2}}\,.\end{split}

(B.19)

Using
u=vy
, dividing through by
y^{2}
, rearranging the terms, and using the expression for sound speed from Eq. (
B.7
) gives

\left(v-\frac{v_{s}^{2}}{v}\right)\frac{1}{y}\,\frac{d(vy)}{dr}=\frac{1}{r}\left[2v_{s}^{2}-\frac{GM}{ry^{2}}\right]-\frac{1}{1+TS/m_{N}}\,\frac{\dot{Q}}{3vy}\,.

(B.20)

Expanding the LHS and using Eq. (
B.11
) for
dy/dr
, one obtains:

\left(v-\frac{v_{s}^{2}}{v}\right)\left[\frac{dv}{dr}+\frac{v^{2}}{1-v^{2}}\frac{dv}{dr}-\frac{v}{1-v^{2}}\,\frac{1}{y^{2}}\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{GM}{r}\right)\right]=\frac{2v_{s}^{2}}{r}-\frac{GM}{r^{2}y^{2}}-\frac{\dot{Q}}{3vy\,(1+TS/m_{N})}\,.

(B.21)

Rearranging and grouping similar terms results in

\boxed{\frac{v}{1-v^{2}}\left(1-\frac{v_{s}^{2}}{v^{2}}\right)\frac{dv}{dr}=\frac{1}{r}\left[2v_{s}^{2}-\frac{(1-v_{s}^{2})}{(1-2GM/r)}\frac{GM}{r}\right]-\frac{\dot{Q}}{3vy\,(1+TS/m_{N})}\,.}

(B.22)

where we have used Eq. (
B.10
) and again neglected the
dM/dr
terms. This result is similar to Eq. (1) of
Cardall
&
Fuller
,
but not exactly the same
.
Cardall
&
Fuller
have
TS/(3m_{N})
in place of
v_{s}^{2}
inside the square brackets on the RHS, but as we know from Eq. (
B.7
), that is not entirely correct in the relativistic treatment. Since the continuity equation was explicitly imposed in the derivation of this equation, the omission of relativistic corrections to the sound speed in its numerical implementation evidently results in non-conservation of
\dot{M}
, as we discovered. Thus, we are able to recover Eqs. (2) and (3) in
Cardall
&
Fuller
and obtained a corrected form of their Eq. (1), including the relativistic sound speed correction. We have verified that
\dot{M}
is conserved when Eq. (
B.22
) is employed.

Appendix C
Methods: System Setup with
SkyNet

In this Appendix, we provide a more detailed description of the set up of our nucleosynthesis calculation with the open source reaction network
SkyNet

[
41
,
60
]
. We adopt the majority of nuclear reaction rates from the
Reaclib
library, version 2.2
[
76
,
77
]
, with two exceptions: (1) modern, medium-enhanced triple-
\alpha
reaction rates from Ref.
[
39
]
are incorporated, as per Refs.
[
78
,
36
,
37
]
, and (2) the correct rate coefficient for
92
Nb
\to

92
Mo beta decay, consistent with the measured upper limit on this branch, is used, as discussed in Ref.
[
37
]
.

Enabling the ‘self-heating’ and ‘neutrino heating’ options in
SkyNet
allows it to recompute the temperature (or equivalently, the entropy) at each step, given the density profile and an initial temperature as inputs. The ‘self-heating’ option takes into account the nuclear binding energy released as the reaction network is evolved. However, since we are already providing
T
and
\rho
profiles as inputs from the outflow calculations,
we have chosen to turn off the self-heating and neutrino heating options in
SkyNet
for the results shown here.
We have checked that the input entropy
S
from the outflow solutions are in good agreement with the entropy per baryon computed by
SkyNet
, and consequently, the nucleosynthetic yields are also not qualitatively different.

To be consistent with the steady-state outflow solutions found in Sec.
2
, we have modified
SkyNet
to account for the blueshift/redshift of neutrino temperatures and luminosities, relative to those at a reference radius
R_{\text{ref}}
. For the
SkyNet
runs requiring a blueshift (see Sec.
7
for details), we implement the blueshift factor

\Phi(r)=\sqrt{\frac{1-2GM/R_{\rm ref}}{1-2GM/r}}\approx\sqrt{\frac{1}{1-2GM/r}}\,,

(C.1)

where we have taken
R_{\rm ref}=500
km (where the neutrino luminosities are extracted) in Eq. (
3.8
). Since the luminosities go as four powers of the neutrino energy, the input luminosities near the PNS are enhanced by
\Phi^{4}
in
SkyNet
whenever there is blueshift implemented in the models. Analogously, the neutrino temperatures
T_{\nu_{i}}
are enhanced by one power of
\Phi
.

We start each nucleosynthesis run at
T\approx 2.5
MeV (
\sim
27 GK), in conditions of NSE. The abundance
Y_{A,Z}
of each species is computed using an NSE calculation for
T>9
GK, while at lower temperature
SkyNet
switches to a full network calculation. At times beyond those provided in the input trajectory files,
SkyNet
extrapolates the time evolution of the outgoing ejecta by assuming
T(t)\propto t^{-2/3}
and
\rho(t)\propto t^{-2}
which follow from mass flow conservation (
r^{2}\rho u={\rm constant}
) and entropy conservation (
T^{3}/\rho={\rm constant}
) in the homologous expansion phase (
r\propto t
,
u={\rm constant}
) after the outflow solution
[
79
,
17
]
. Finally, the nucleosynthesis calculation ends
10^{9}
s after
t_{\rm launch}
when most beta decays have occurred and (meta)stable isotopes have been produced.
