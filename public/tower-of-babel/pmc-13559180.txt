Ethnic Differences in Unmet Care Needs Among Cancer Survivors in Utah Using a Mixed Methods Approach
Authors: Sara G. Jackson, Anne C. Kirchhoff, Sandra L. Edwards, Kimberly A. Herget, Marjorie E. Carter, Rachel R. Codden, Blessing S. Ofori‐Atta, Marie Nagata, Carol Sweeney, Morgan M. Millar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCancer survivors' unmet care needs can negatively affect well‐being and quality of life, yet few studies focus on how Hispanic/Latino (Hispanic) survivors' needs may differ from those of non‐Hispanic White (NHW) survivors.AimsTo explore unmet needs of cancer survivors by ethnicity.MethodsA population‐based sample of adult survivors in Utah was surveyed. Demographic and cancer characteristics of survivors reporting unmet needs and those without needs were compared using chi‐square, Wilcoxon rank sum, and Fisher's exact tests. Crude and adjusted logistic regression models predicting likelihood of reporting unmet needs were conducted, stratified by ethnicity. Open‐ended questions assessed unmet care needs and were analyzed using directed content analysis.ResultsAmong 2015 survey respondents, 516 described unmet needs in open‐ended responses; 31.3% of Hispanic survivors reported unmet care needs compared to 25.2% of NHW survivors (p = 0.019). Among both Hispanic and NHW survivors, age was inversely associated with odds of reporting unmet needs (Hispanic: adjusted OR 0.97; CI 0.95, 0.99; NHW: adjusted OR 0.96; CI 0.95, 0.97). Unmet needs of the sample aligned with the Social Ecological Model (SEM) categories: (1) Individual; (2) Interpersonal; (3) Organizational; and (4) Structural. Financial burden, a structural unmet care need, was reported by 24 (22.2%) Hispanic survivors compared to 45 (11.0%) NHW survivors (p = 0.004).ConclusionsIn this state‐wide sample, Hispanic cancer survivors were more likely to report unmet care needs than NHW survivors, especially financial burden.

Background

Cancer survivors—defined as an individual from the time of cancer diagnosis through the rest of life—may face adverse effects from cancer and/or cancer treatment [1, 2, 3]. Assessing survivors' needs is important as unmet care needs can adversely affect their well‐being and quality of life [4, 5, 6, 7]. Some studies have found that Hispanic or Latino (referred to as Hispanic hereafter) cancer survivors report more unmet needs than non‐Hispanic White (NHW) survivors [8, 9]. With projections that the U.S. Hispanic population will reach 111 million by the year 2060 [10], including significant growth specific to the older Hispanic population (ages in which cancer diagnosis becomes more frequent) [11], there will likely be an increase in Hispanic survivors in the years to come.

Assessing unmet cancer care needs at the state level affords benefits that cannot be achieved with healthcare system‐specific samples, including allowing state cancer control programs to identify priorities and design interventions that have the greatest possible reach. Many studies of survivors' unmet needs rely on patient‐reported outcome measures which may capture unmet needs in predetermined areas [12, 13], but do not elicit nor examine open‐ended data which could help identify new areas of concern [2] and foster a greater understanding of survivors' unmet needs.

Thus, given the projected increase in the prevalence of Hispanic cancer survivors and the potential negative effects of not adequately addressing their needs, the purpose of this study was to determine the most prevalent unmet care needs of Hispanic and NHW survivors and explore any demographic or cancer characteristics associated with reporting unmet needs for Hispanic and NHW survivors. Ultimately, our goal was to gain a more in‐depth understanding of survivors' unmet care needs, which can inform cancer control efforts to address survivors' needs and improve their well‐being and quality of life.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

Given the purpose of the study, a mixed methods approach was deemed appropriate as it would provide the greatest depth of understanding about a relatively understudied topic [14]. In this convergent design, quantitative and qualitative data were simultaneously collected, analyzed, and then the findings were merged [14]. We identified and sampled eligible survivors from the Utah Cancer Registry, a population‐based registry that collects and maintains information on all reportable cancer diagnoses in the state of Utah. The Utah Cancer Registry meets high standards of complete case ascertainment and follow‐up set by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's National Program of Cancer Registries (NPCR) and the National Cancer Institute's Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program. This study was reviewed by the University of Utah Institutional Review Board, which deemed no human subjects research approval was required. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The survey was conducted annually from 2018 through 2022. Each year, a new sample of 500–800 survivors was selected for the study using stratified random sampling. To obtain a diverse sample of survivors, we oversampled Hispanic individuals (2019–2022) and survivors residing in areas of Utah where the percentage of uninsured residents exceeded the state median proportion of uninsured (2018–2019). Eligible participants were Utah residents diagnosed at age 18 or older between 2012 and 2020. All reportable, invasive cancer diagnoses were included, with certain exceptions [15, 16]. Web‐based and paper versions of the questionnaire were available in English and Spanish. Potential participants received a pre‐notification letter containing a brochure with information about the Utah Cancer Registry, followed by a formal letter inviting them to participate accompanied by either a survey web address or a paper questionnaire and a stamped return envelope. Up to three reminders were used for non‐respondents, consisting of a brief reminder letter, a replacement questionnaire (or first paper questionnaire for those who had initially received a web address) and stamped return envelope, and finally a reminder phone call [15]. To encourage participation, we included a $2.00 cash pre‐incentive. Further details on the study design have previously been reported [15, 16, 17].

Data Collection

The questionnaire included structured questions on topics such as general health, pain, survivorship care plans, health care access, health behaviors, medical expenses, and social connectedness. The questionnaire contained two open‐ended questions: (1) “Please tell us some things that you believe are missing from the care you are receiving as a cancer survivor, that is to say, your unmet needs,” and (2) “If you have any other thoughts or comments that you would like us to know, please share them here.”

Data Analysis

Statistical Methods

We calculated counts and percentages to summarize participants' demographic and cancer characteristics. We used chi‐square tests to analyze demographic differences between respondents and non‐respondents. To assess whether demographic and cancer characteristics varied among those reporting unmet cancer care needs versus those without needs, we used chi‐square, Wilcoxon rank sum, and Fisher's exact tests. We then ran unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression models to assess the relationship between unmet cancer care needs and other variables, stratified by ethnicity. Multivariable models included the following covariates: age, sex, cancer site, and health insurance status.

Qualitative Methods

Initial Coding and Framework Selection

Responses to open‐ended questions were imported into Dedoose qualitative analysis software [18] and read by two authors (SGB, SLE) to gain familiarity with the content and generate inductive codes. Preliminary ideas were annotated in Dedoose and discussed collectively. Open‐ended responses with no unmet needs (e.g., responses stating “no needs”, “not applicable”, “none”, etc.) and those that solely reported positive aspects of survivors' care were coded as such, tallied to report their frequency, and removed from additional qualitative analyses.

Initial review revealed the intersection between individual, interpersonal, organizational, and structural needs, making the Social Ecological Model (SEM) an appropriate theoretical framework to guide analysis [19]. The SEM has been widely adopted as a public health promotion model [20] as it describes how health, human behavior, and experiences are influenced by the “interrelations among environmental conditions and human behavior and well‐being.” The SEM has previously informed studies of cancer screening [21, 22] and survivorship [3, 23].

Coding Process

Two authors (SGJ, MMM) conducted primary coding using directed qualitative content analysis within a modified SEM framework [24, 25, 26]. All participant responses reporting unmet needs were coded as individual, interpersonal, organizational, and/or structural according to the four levels of the SEM.

The coders developed a codebook containing code labels, definitions, and example quotations. To assess reliability, 10% of randomly selected responses were independently coded by both coders [27]. Intercoder reliability, calculated via Dedoose software [18], yielded a Cohen's Kappa of 0.65, indicating good agreement [28]. The coders met to discuss discrepancies using the codebook definitions. When disagreements arose from unclear coding rules or definitions, they were revised collaboratively. After revising the codebook and reaching consensus on all discrepancies, an additional 10% of randomly selected responses were coded, producing a Cohen's Kappa of 0.73, demonstrating improved agreement.

Final Coding and Subcode Development

The primary coder coded the remaining responses using the four levels of the SEM and noted patterns that could constitute subcodes within each SEM level. The research team provided input on identified subcodes, and the codebook was iteratively updated until all authors agreed (see Table 5 for SEM level, subcode labels and definitions, and example quotations). The primary coder then applied subcodes to all responses previously coded at the SEM level (individual, interpersonal, organizational, or structural). The research team validated the subcodes. Finally, code frequencies for each SEM level and subcode were extracted from Dedoose to compare subcode counts across SEM levels and between Hispanic and NHW survivors. Fisher's exact tests were calculated to determine if the unmet needs reported by Hispanic and NHW's were significantly different at p < 0.05.

Results

Over 5 years of data collection, 2015 cancer survivors completed the survey (57.0% weighted response rate). Response likelihood varied significantly by several variables, including race and ethnicity, with non‐Hispanic White survivors more likely to participate than Hispanic or Latino survivors (p < 0.001); age, with survivors under 45 being less likely to respond (p < 0.001), and insurance type at diagnosis, with uninsured individuals less likely to participate (p < 0.001).

Open‐ended questions were completed by 1151 (57.1%) participants (n = 208 Hispanic and n = 943 NHW), 813 did not complete the open‐ended questions, and 51 were excluded from analyses (see Figure 1). We considered participants who did not complete the open‐ended questions to have no unmet care needs. Of the 1151 participants responding to at least one open‐ended question, 458 (39.8%) had no unmet needs and 177 (15.4%) wrote only positive comments about their cancer care. The remaining 516 (44.8%) respondents reported at least one unmet need, including 108 Hispanic and 408 NHW participants.

Quantitative Results

Table 1 displays demographics, cancer characteristics, and responses to select structured survey questions for those reporting unmet care needs (n = 516, 26.3%) and those without unmet care needs (n = 1448, 73.7%) in open‐ended survey questions. Hispanic survivors were more likely to report unmet needs (108, 31.3%) than NHW survivors (408, 25.2%, p = 0.019). Females were also more likely to report unmet needs than males (30.8% compared to 20.8%, p < 0.001). Survivors with unmet needs were younger (median = 62, range = 52–69) than those without needs (median = 68, range = 60–76). Employment status was significantly associated with reporting unmet needs, with those who were unable to work due to illness or disability most likely to report unmet needs (50.0%) and those who were retired least likely (16.8%, p < 0.001). Individuals with no health insurance were more likely to report unmet needs than those with any type of insurance (39.1% vs. 25.9%, p = 0.43). Those who were satisfied with their health care were less likely to report unmet needs (20.1%) than those who were unsatisfied (47.7%, p < 0.001). Qualitative Results

Qualitative data from open‐ended text responses were categorized utilizing the four levels of the Social Ecological Model (SEM): Individual, interpersonal, organizational, and structural.

Individual

Unmet needs at the individual level included a survivor's internal knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, behavior, prior experiences, self‐concept, personality traits, symptoms/side effects of cancer treatments, and other personal factors affecting unmet needs following cancer. Subcodes within this SEM level included: physical, psychological, and knowledge. See Table 5 for definitions and example quotations of each subcode.

Interpersonal

Interpersonal needs involved social interactions with other people (e.g., family members, friends, colleagues, or healthcare providers) and feelings and emotions resulting from interactions. Subcodes within interpersonal unmet needs included humanism, peer support, information (from a specific provider), and family support.

Organizational

Organizational needs were those about organizations, such as healthcare organizations, whose operational policies or procedures (formal or informal) resulted in survivors' unmet needs. This category included subcodes of financial assistance (from the organization at which one receives cancer care), alternative options, provider coordination, follow‐up (by providers), explanation, medical errors, and availability (of services/doctors).

Structural

Structural unmet care needs resulted from local, state, national, or global laws and policies. These included needs related to health insurance, financial burden, government benefits, medical marijuana, the opioid crisis, transportation and care accessibility, and support for cancer research.

The descriptive statistics calculated on the qualitative results illustrated that Hispanic survivors' top unmet care needs included the following subcodes: physical (22.2% of Hispanic survivors reported this unmet need), financial burden (22.2%), psychological (14.8%), health insurance (14.8%), follow‐up (13.9%), and explanation (13.9%) (see Table 4). For NHW survivors, the unmet needs reported with the greatest frequency were physical (31.6% of NHW survivors reported this need), humanism (17.6%), knowledge (15.4%), health insurance (15.0%), follow‐up (13.0%), and psychological (13.0%). Overall, the SEM level with the highest number of reported unmet needs was individual (34.8% of total reported unmet needs), followed by organizational (27.8%), structural (23.5%), and interpersonal (13.8%). The subcodes with the highest frequency counts overall were physical (18.0%), humanism (10.1%), knowledge (8.6%), and health insurance (8.6%) (see Table 5 for example quotations under each code label).

In comparing the frequency of unmet needs by ethnic group, we found that physical unmet needs were mentioned by 31.6% of NHW, but only by 22.2% of Hispanic survivors, although this difference was not statistically significant (p‐value = 0.059). Financial burden was reported by 22.2% of Hispanic survivors compared with 11.0% of NWH survivors (p‐value = 0.004). There were no other statistically significant differences between NHW and Hispanic survivors in the frequency of reporting any other unmet needs categories.

Discussion

This analysis of a large, multiyear probability sample of Utah cancer survivors illuminates a variety of unmet needs and explores differences in needs reported by Hispanic and NHW survivors. Results of our multivariable analyses indicate that age was inversely associated with odds of reporting unmet needs for both Hispanic and NHW survivors, a finding that resonates with prior results from the American Cancer Society (Burg, 2015 #1285). Our finding that NHW females were more likely to report unmet cancer care needs than NHW males in unadjusted models aligns with previous research demonstrating that women with cancer were more likely to report unmet care needs related to psychological health, patient care, support, and sexuality [29]. However, there is little research that specifically delineates the differences in unmet care needs by both sex and ethnicity, limiting the ability to draw concrete conclusions. As Luo and Colditz state, “Sex disparities should be better examined by the stratification of race…The roles of SDOHs [social determinants of health] in sex disparities in cancer and how they interplay with sex remain to be elucidated” [30].

In alignment with the SEM, we found survivors experienced unmet needs at individual, interpersonal, organizational, and structural levels. Physical needs were the most reported for both Hispanic and NHW survivors. Additionally, examining open‐ended data identified humanism as a new area of concern that has not typically been included as a closed‐ended response option to questions on survivors' unmet care needs. Humanism was the most frequently reported interpersonal need of cancer survivors in Utah, with survivors commenting that bedside manner and patient‐provider communication are integral to their well‐being. Survivors who didn't feel listened to or respected carried these negative experiences with them and continued to feel the need to make their experiences heard. Some research shows that empathy from physicians may lower cancer patients' distress and depression and improve their satisfaction and quality of life [31, 32]. Therefore, the education and training of medical, nursing, and other health professions should at a minimum integrate patient‐provider communication and the use of empathy.

This mixed methods integration demonstrated that Hispanic survivors were more likely than NHW survivors to report unmet care needs, specifically related to financial burden. These findings align with research by Harris and colleagues which discovered that having an “underrepresented minority background” or a preference for a language other than English was associated with greater financial toxicity [33]. Our findings also mirror studies demonstrating the association of Hispanic ethnicity with cancer‐related financial toxicity, higher prevalence of unmet healthcare needs, healthcare unaffordability, and financial stress compared to other racial/ethnic groups [34, 35]. Financial toxicity often extends beyond medical bills and interferes with basic needs: adults with a history of cancer are more likely than those without to report food insecurity [36]. In addition, among survivors, a lower income is associated with a greater likelihood of food insecurity [36].

On the other hand, financial burden was less frequently reported by NHWs as an unmet need in open‐ended questions. Estimates from the Federal Reserve indicate that NHW individuals have the highest level of median and mean family wealth ($188,200 and $983,400 respectively) than any racial or ethnic group, with Hispanic families' median and mean family wealth at $36,100 and $165,500 respectively [37]. Thus, NHW survivors in this study may have had greater wealth from which they could draw in this time of need.

Implications

Clinical Implications

This study highlights that regardless of ethnicity, survivors were most likely to identify unmet physical needs related to their cancer care or treatment. Clinicians should routinely ask about unmet physical needs throughout the survivorship period and address concerns as they arise. As working‐age adults with a history of cancer are more likely than those without cancer to report an inability to work due to health issues and being limited in the amount or type of work they can perform, clinicians may also want to discuss employment‐related disruptions early in the cancer trajectory [36]. Prior research highlights the fact that roughly 40% of working cancer survivors younger than 40 years do not have paid sick leave, which can result in absences, job loss, and the loss of employer‐based medical insurance, further affecting financial strain [38].

Our findings also illustrate that regardless of ethnicity, unmet needs related to humanism are as likely as unmet needs related to health insurance. This demonstrates that in addition to referring survivors to financial counselors, similar attention must be paid to developing a trusting and warm patient‐provider relationship [31, 32]. Despite similarities in the unmet needs of Hispanic and NHW survivors, our findings also suggest that survivors from different cultural backgrounds may prioritize different unmet needs. Clinicians should follow the survivors' lead and ask them about their priorities, rather than assume a one‐size‐fits‐all approach. Similarly, prior literature demonstrates that the presentation of financial hardship can vary greatly. For example, there are three domains of financial hardship—behavioral changes (such as foregoing medical care), material costs (such as problems paying bills), and psychological consequences (such as worry about finances). Thus, clinicians must learn to identify any of the former as signs of financial hardship and act accordingly [39].

Research Implications

Utilizing a mixed methods approach and the SEM provided a novel and more in‐depth understanding of the level of influence in which unmet care needs occur. Future population‐based studies may find merit in analyzing open‐ended responses, despite the time and effort needed to analyze these data. Our findings support the design of interventions that are appropriate to meet specific survivors' needs [3]. Per the US Department of Health and Human Services, “Because significant and dynamic interrelationships exist among these different levels of health determinants, interventions are most likely to be effective when they address determinants at all levels.” [40] The results from this mixed method study add to our knowledge regarding the types of unmet needs faced by Hispanic and NHW cancer survivors, which can be used to design and test appropriate interventions. In addition to physical needs, Hispanic survivors were concerned about financial burden, and thus interventions designed to reduce financial strain should be made a priority for this population. For example, for those purchasing health insurance through the Affordable Care Act marketplace, which is the case for many Hispanic individuals [41], enhanced financial assistance could be provided, a strategy that is overwhelmingly supported by cancer survivors [42]. Furthermore, financial toxicity measures, such as the Comprehensive Score for Financial Toxicity‐Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy (COST‐FACIT), should be standardly and consistently utilized to assess survivors' financial hardship, and Spanish versions should be obtained when needed [43, 44].

As health insurance needs were often intertwined with needs related to financial burden, health insurance navigators could be employed by cancer treatment centers to improve survivors' access to and understanding of medical insurance policies. This need is especially critical given that survivors experience financial hardship regardless of whether they have public or private health insurance, and those with high‐deductible plans and no health savings accounts are most likely to experience financial hardship [36]. Furthermore, it is imperative that organizations hire bilingual health insurance navigators and financial aid experts so Spanish‐speaking Hispanic survivors have equitable access to culturally and linguistically appropriate services. Future research should focus on designing appropriate multi‐level interventions and determining the efficacy and sustainability of these interventions for specific priority populations. In addition, future research should aim to better understand the differences in unmet care needs by sex and stratified by race and/or ethnicity.

Limitations

As this study relied on self‐reporting of unmet needs, response bias may cause an underestimation of the unmet needs of cancer survivors, particularly among Hispanic survivors who were less likely to respond to the survey, and may be more likely to acquiesce than non‐Hispanic individuals [45]. Furthermore, participants who responded to the survey may represent those who have had more positive experiences with cancer care than those who chose not to respond to this survey. We considered a lack of response to open‐ended questions as an indication of the absence of unmet needs, which may also underestimate survivors' cancer care needs. While this study oversampled Hispanic populations in later years and made modifications to increase participation among this population (such as introducing bilingual mailers in 2019), most participants were White. Participants were mainly located in urban areas, and all resided in Utah, so our findings, although from a population‐based sample, may not be generalizable outside of Utah. The open‐ended question asking survivors about any unmet needs was placed directly after the section of the survey focused on health care access so it may have captured more information related to health care access than other needs. Non‐response was comparable to what can be expected in well‐conducted health surveys, but survivors who completed the survey may have had different experiences than non‐respondents. Finally, the qualitative analysis was conducted by investigators with prior experience and knowledge regarding cancer survivorship, and therefore their frame of reference may have influenced coding decisions. The quantitative analysis of close‐ended survey questions used in combination with the qualitative analysis may address this potential limitation.

Conclusion

This study illustrates the benefits of integrating quantitative and qualitative data to produce a more in‐depth understanding of a relatively unstudied area. Overall, those who reported unmet needs on open‐ended questions were statistically different from those without unmet care needs in multiple characteristics including ethnicity, sex, employment status, health insurance, and satisfaction with healthcare. However, our qualitative findings illustrated that the only significant difference in unmet care needs reported between Hispanic and NHW survivors was financial burden. To address cancer survivors' unmet needs, tailored interventions need to reflect each group's intersectional social position, meet each group's specific needs, and be delivered at multiple socioecological levels. With the projected growth of the Hispanic population, and projections for an increased prevalence of cancer survivors in the years to come, it is vital that clinicians, researchers, organizational leaders, and policymakers work together to address the unmet needs of this historically underserved and minoritized group.
