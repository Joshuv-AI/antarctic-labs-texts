The role of esketamine in enhancing postoperative recovery and pain management in laparoscopic surgery: a meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials
Authors: Rameez Qasim, Hassan Mehmood, Mubashir Raza, Zuha Tariq, Khadija Mohib, Bakhtawar Haseeb, Abu-Bakr Ahmed, Ahmad Ali Hussain, Izza Shakeel, Hasibullah Aminpoor, Asraf Hussain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDEffective analgesia in laparoscopic surgery remains challenging due to visceral pain and postoperative nausea. NMDA-receptor antagonists such as esketamine may enhance peri-operative analgesia, mitigate opioid related side effects, and it also shows potent antiemetic properties.OBJECTIVESThis meta-analysis evaluated whether intra-operative esketamine improves postoperative pain and other outcomes in adults undergoing laparoscopic procedures.STUDY DESIGNThe study followed PRISMA guidelines and was registered with PROSPERO (Registration No: CRD420251039241). The search for studies was carried out in the PubMed, Embase, Scopus and the Cochrane Library from inception to 1 March 2025.ELIGIBILITY CRITERIAEleven RCTs (n = 1036 patients) met inclusion criteria. Outcomes included postoperative pain scores (0-6 h, 12-24 h), opioid consumption, PONV and adverse effects. Data were pooled using standard techniques. Evidence certainty was assessed by GRADE methodology.RESULTSEsketamine significantly reduced pain scores at 0 to 6 h (MD = -0.32, 95% CI: -0.40 to -0.25, P < 0.001) and 12 to 24 h (SMD = -1.07, 95% CI: -1.22 to -0.91, P < 0.001). It lowered the risk of requiring rescue analgesia by 38% (risk ratio = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.38 to 1.00, P = 0.05) and reduced PONV incidence by 26% (OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.56 to 0.97, P = 0.03). No significant differences were found in emergence time, postanaesthesia care unit stay or cumulative 24-h opioid consumption. Heterogeneity was substantial for some outcomes. Evidence certainty was moderate to low.CONCLUSIONWith a low to very low level of certainty, esketamine appears to improve early postoperative pain control and selected recovery parameters (enhancement of analgesia with both reduced rescue analgesia needs and reduced PONV) in laparoscopic surgery without increasing adverse events. It represents a valuable adjunct in multimodal analgesia, though standardised dosing. However, considerable heterogeneity in esketamine dosing, mode of administration and background analgesic regimens across included trials limits definitive conclusions and highlights the need for standardised, high-quality randomised trials.

Introduction

Laparoscopic surgery has emerged as a fundamental aspect of contemporary surgical practice attributed to its minimally invasive nature, resulting in less postoperative pain and expedited recovery.1 However, effective peri-operative analgesia is still an essential part of patient care since poor pain management might result in longer recovery times, higher opioid use and more complications. Opioids and their derivatives are still considered the gold standard for the treatment of moderate to severe pain and are used for pain management during thes peri-operative period.2 Nonetheless, peri-operative utilisation of opioids has also led to many side effects, including nausea, vomiting, reduced gastric motility, delirium, respiratory depression and even the promotion of cell proliferation and metastasis.

Recently, esketamine, the S-enantiomer of ketamine, has gained attention because of its ability to improve postoperative outcomes when given intra-operatively. The drug acts principally as an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist, a receptor which plays an integral role in modulating pain perception and central sensitisation.3 Compared racemic ketamine, the drug exhibits approximately double the affinity for NMDA receptors, possibly producing stronger analgesic effects at lower doses and a lower side effect profile including a lower incidence of psychotomimetic effects such as dissociation, postoperative cognitive dysfunction, hallucinations and nightmares.4 The use of this drug in laparoscopic surgery has demonstrated promising benefits in accelerating recovery period and minimising complications rates. Studies indicate that the drug facilitates a quicker normalisation of gastrointestinal function with earlier passage of flatus, hence reducing the duration of hospital stays. In addition, it is associated with lower postoperative pain levels and a decreased need for supplemental analgesics, thus reducing reliance on opioids.5 The drug also decreases the likelihood of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), therefore highlighting its potential for improving patient comfort and satisfaction during recovery.6 Beyond its analgesic properties, the drug has been associated with improved psychological outcomes postsurgery. Peri-operative esketamine use effectively reduced postoperative depression scores, with effects persisting beyond 1 month after surgery.7 The safety profile of esketamine in the peri-operative setting remains an important consideration when evaluating its potential benefits. Its administration does not significantly increase the occurrence of common postoperative adverse effects such as nausea, vomiting, dizziness or drowsiness.6 This is especially pertinent, as these side effects are often associated with opioid-based analgesia. Rare side effects such as mild sedation or transient dizziness and light-headedness have been reported in some cases; these symptoms are generally short-lived and well tolerated by most patients.8 This favourable safety profile further enhances the drug's potential as a key component in peri-operative pain management protocols, particularly in patients who may be more vulnerable to the side effects of traditional analgesics.

Variability in study design, dosing regimens and patient populations calls for a thorough analysis to establish a clearer knowledge of drug efficacy and safety in laparoscopic operations. This meta-analysis proposes to synthesise data from randomised controlled trials (RCTs) to evaluate the impact of intra-operative esketamine on postoperative recovery, pain management, psychological outcomes and safety in patients undergoing laparoscopic procedures.

Materials and methods

Study registration

The present research effort has been formally registered with PROSPERO, the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses, bearing the registration number: CRD420251039241. Moreover, the study was carried out using predefined protocol criteria given in supplementary files and recorded in compliance with PRISMA guidelines.9 This meta-analysis did not need patient permission or Institutional Ethics approval.

Search strategy

In March 2025, a comprehensive systematic literature search was undertaken using the following databases: PubMed, Embase, Scopus and the Cochrane Library. We used the Medical Subject Headings terms (MESH) and keywords that are as follows: (esketamine OR esketamin’ OR “Esketamine” [Supplementary Concept] OR S-ketamine) AND (laparoscopy OR Laparoscopic surgery OR laparoscopic procedure OR (“Laparoscopy”[Mesh]) OR “Hand-Assisted Laparoscopy”[Mesh]) AND (postoperativ*[tiab] OR “Postoperative Period”[Mesh] OR recovery[tiab] OR “Pain, Postoperative”[Mesh] OR pain[tiab] OR nausea[tiab] OR vomiting[tiab] OR “emergence delirium”[tiab] OR hemodynamics[tiab] OR opioid*[tiab] OR sedation[tiab] OR postoperative pain OR opiod use OR emergence delirium OR Recovery OR pain OR hemodynamics OR sleep disturbance). No filter was applied for dates and no restrictions for race, place, sex, ethnicity or language were applied.

Eligibility criteria

Studies which were eligible for our meta-analysis had the following set criteria.

Type of studies

Only RCTs were included, with no language or region restrictions. Nonrandomised or independently published controlled trials, case-control studies, cohort studies, cross-sectional, case reports, letter to the editor and so on were excluded.

Type of participants

Patients undergoing laparoscopic surgical procedures (i.e. gynaecological, colorectal, abdominal and renal). Participants undergoing non-laparoscopic non-laparoscopic surgical procedures were excluded.

Type of interventions

The intervention groups included the administration of intra-operative intravenous (i.v.) esketamine either as a bolus, continuous infusion, or a bolus followed by an infusion protocol. Other pharmacological intervention groups that did not include i.v. esketamine or if esketamine was combined with other agents were excluded.

Type of control groups

Control groups received standard perioperative analgesia, typically opioid-based regimens, with placebo used in place of esketamine where applicable.

Type of outcomes

The primary outcome was postoperative pain at 0 to 6-h and 12 to 24-h, while secondary outcomes included time to emergence from anaesthesia, 24-h opioid consumption, postanaesthesia care unit (PACU) length of stay, rescue-analgesic requirement and PONV.

Data extraction

In addition to the studies identified using the above search strategy, the reference lists of these studies were also searched for relevant investigations. All identified studies were entered into Rayyan to eliminate duplicates. One author removed all the duplicate entries, whereas two other authors independently conducted the screening process and evaluated articles based on titles, abstracts, and keywords of all retrieved studies, identifying the trials that fulfil the eligibility criteria. For further evaluation, the complete text of possibly relevant studies or articles was acquired. Articles meeting the eligibility criteria were selected. Any conflicts or disagreements were resolved by mutual discussion among all authors. Figure 1 illustrates the workflow for the study selection process.

Risk of bias assessment

The quality evaluation was conducted independently by two review authors to evaluate the risk of bias in each study, using the Cochrane Collaboration Risk of Bias Tool (Rob-2) for RCT.10 Bias is defined as a judgement. Its risk levels are further classified into three: ‘low risk of bias’, ‘some concerns’ and ‘high risk of bias’ according to the assessment criteria. Measurements of five domains were Randomisation process (D1), Deviation from intended intervention (D2), (Missing outcome data (D3), Measurement of outcome (D4) and Selection of reported result (D5). Disagreements in classification between the two assessors were addressed through discussion, and if necessary were resolved via arbitration with the guidance of a third author. After the collection of data and assessment, the acquired information was evaluated and independently re-examined by the third author, referencing the original articles to ensure the quality of the work.

Statistical analysis

Extracted data were entered into statistical software for analysis, with verification steps performed to ensure data accuracy and consistency. The data were statistically analysed using Review Manager (RevMan), (V.5.4) (Copenhagen).11 A random-effects model was used, assuming that the true intervention effect may vary across studies due to clinical and methodological heterogeneity. To aid clinical interpretation, a Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) was considered. For postoperative pain scores, an MCID of approximately 1 point on a 10-point scale (or 10 mm on a 100-mm VAS) was considered clinically meaningful, while for opioid consumption, a relative reduction of 10-20% was considered clinically relevant based on prior literature. For dichotomous data, we calculated the odds ratio (OR) or risk ratio with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). For continuous outcomes, mean difference (MD) with 95% confidence intervals, corresponding to weighted mean difference (WMD), was used when studies employed identical measurement scales, while standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95%confidence intervals was used when different scales or instruments were reported. We set statistical significance at a P- value of less than 0.05. Heterogeneity (I2) between studies was assessed using the Omnibus Homogeneity test (Q), where an I2 value of 50% shows sizable heterogeneity and an I2 value above 75% indicates considerable heterogeneity.12 Forest plots were created to visually represent the analysis. The chances of publication bias were gauged using Egger's regression test.13 Moreover, we used Funnel plots to visually assess the publication bias.14

Subgroup analysis

Studies in which esketamine was used as an addition to routine anaesthetic or analgesic regimens were included, as long as the comparator group received equivalent background treatment without esketamine, allowing for the isolation of the esketamine effect. Subgroup analyses were performed using esketamine dose, type of surgery, and co-administered anaesthetic drugs (adjunct use). For outcomes with substantial heterogeneity, a meta-regression was performed using these variables. However, none showed a significant association with effect size (P > 0.10 for all), indicating that these factors did not account for the observed variance. Due to limited data, formal subgroup analyses for binary outcomes were not feasible.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness and reliability of the meta-analysis findings. We remove each study to identify influential outliers. In addition, we repeated meta-analyses using both fixed-effects and random-effects models to evaluate the impact of between-study heterogeneity. Studies rated as high risk of bias were excluded in a separate analysis to determine their influence on overall outcomes. For continuous outcomes, SMDs were recalculated using alternative imputation methods for missing data when applicable.

Results

Study selection

The procedure for searching and selecting the studies is demonstrated by the PRISMA diagram (Fig. 1). The electronic database search of PubMed, Embase, Scopus and the Cochrane Library (inception to 1 March 2025) yielded 625 records. After the removal of 312 duplicates, three hundred and thirteen were screened, and 274 were excluded because they lacked the variables of interest. Out of 39 remaining articles, 13 were outside the scope of the meta-analysis, as these studies were out of scope. Twenty-six full-text articles were reviewed in detail and 15 were excluded leaving 11 RCTs for our meta-analysis (Fig. 1).15–25

Study characteristics

The 11 included RCTs (published between 2021 and 2025) enrolled a total of 1036 adults (519 esketamine group, 517 control group). Sample sizes per trial ranged from 39 to 183 participants. Most procedures involved gynaecological or colorectal laparoscopic surgery, while two trials focused on major abdominal or renal laparoscopic procedures. All studies compared i.v. esketamine, administered either as a single bolus (0.125 to 0.5 mg kg-1), a continuous infusion (0.125 to mg kg-1 h-1) or a bolus followed by infusion against placebo or opioid-centred standard care (Supplemental table 1). Anaesthesia protocols were broadly consistent, typically involving propofol or sevoflurane combined with remifentanil or sufentanil. Follow-up periods for analgesic and recovery outcomes ranged from PACU discharge to 72 h postoperatively. Risk of bias

Across the 11 included RCTs, the overall methodological quality was acceptable, albeit with some recurring limitations. All studies generated random sequences by computer and concealed allocation using opaque envelopes or centralised randomisation systems; thus, the domain of bias arising from the randomisation process was uniformly rated as low risk. Most investigators maintained adequate blinding; however, four trials (Deng 202423, Hu 202419, Li 202522 and Wang 202020) lacked explicit masking of intra-operative nurses or anaesthetic technicians and were therefore rated as having some concerns regarding deviations from intended interventions. Follow-up exceeded 95% in all datasets; nonetheless, Ren 202421 imputed several missing pain scores, leading to some concerns for missing data bias. Outcome measurement was generally robust, relying on validated patient-reported instruments for pain, quality of recovery and PONV; however, the absence of assessor blinding in Deng 202423, Li 202522, Wang 202020 and Zhao 202417 introduced some concerns in that domain. Selective reporting bias emerged as the principal vulnerability: five trials lacked prospective protocol registration, and one of them (Wang 2020)20 deviated from its original statistical analysis plan, resulting in a high-risk assignment for that study and some concerns for the remaining four.

Synthesising these assessments, five trials (Hu 202418, Huan 202516, Massoth 202125, Qiu 202215 and Wang 202524) were rated as low risk overall, five as having some concerns and one (Wang 2020)20as high risk. Given that more than one quarter of participants were enrolled in trials with some concerns or higher, the certainty of evidence was downgraded by one level for risk of bias in the GRADE evaluation. Quantitative synthesis

Acute (0 to 6 h) postoperative pain

Six trials (n = 288 vs. 290) reported pain scores within 6 h of extubation. Pooled analysis favoured esketamine (MD = –0.32; 95% CI: –0.40 to –0.25; Z = 8.44; P < 0.001), indicating a statistically significant reduction of about one-third of a numeric-rating-scale unit. Heterogeneity was considerable (I2 = 98%), driven largely by one outlying study with a large negative effect (Deng 202423; –1.60 units). Removing this study in a leave-one-out analysis attenuated heterogeneity to 71% without materially changing the pooled estimate (MD = –0.29; 95% CI: –0.35 to –0.23) (Fig. 2). The funnel plot appeared reasonably symmetrical (Fig. 3), suggesting a low risk of small-study effects.

Eight trials (n = 519/521) contributed data using heterogeneous pain scales; therefore, a SMD was calculated. Esketamine yielded a large effect (SMD = –1.07; 95% CI: –1.22 to –0.91; P < 0.001); however, heterogeneity was extreme (χ2 = 513.4; I2 = 99%). Meta-regression by dose, surgery type, and adjunct dexmedetomidine failed to explain variance (P > 0.10 for all) (Fig. 4). The funnel plot (Fig. 5) showed mild left-sided asymmetry, suggesting a possibility of unpublished small negative-effect studies, although interpretability is limited with k = 8.

Time to emergence from anaesthesia

Five studies (n = 252 vs. 253) measured time from cessation of anaesthesia to eye opening or extubation. A fixed-effects model was used for meta-analysis. The pooled estimate showed a mean difference of 0.54 min (95% CI: –0.03 to 1.10; Z = 1.85; P = 0.06), indicating no statistically significant difference. Heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 93%), largely attributable to one study (Zhao 2024)17 reporting shorter emergence (–4.71 min) and another (Qiu 2022)15 reporting delayed recovery (+4.00 min) (Fig. 6). The funnel plot (Fig. 7) was sparse and showed asymmetry, precluding firm conclusions regarding publication bias.

Postanaesthesia care unit length of stay

Six trials (n = 329 vs. 330) contributed data; a random-effects model was prespecified given clinical variability. Esketamine did not significantly shorten PACU stay (mean difference = 1.60 min; 95% CI: –0.76 to 3.96; P = 0.18; I2 = 87%). One small, low-precision study (Massoth 2021)25 reported a 19-min prolongation and accounted for much of the heterogeneity; excluding this study reduced I2 to 42% and yielded a pooled mean difference of 0.62 min (95% CI: –0.45 to 1.69) (Fig. 8). The funnel plot (Fig. 9) appeared symmetrical apart from this outlier.

Three trials (n = 106/106) quantified the need for supplemental opioid use within 24 h. Esketamine reduced the risk of rescue-analgesic requirement by 38% (risk ratio = 0.62; 95% CI: 0.38 to 1.00; Z = 1.95; P = 0.05). There was no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) (Fig. 10). Eleven comparisons across 10 trials (n = 625 vs. 627) showed that esketamine decreased the odds of PONV by 26% (OR = 0.74; 95% CI: 0.56 to 0.97; P = 0.03; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 12). Publication-bias exploration

For outcomes with at least 10 comparisons (PONV only), the funnel plot appeared largely symmetrical, and no small-study effect was apparent. For all other outcomes (<10 comparisons), funnel plot based inference was considered unreliable due to insufficient statistical power. Taken together, no clear evidence of publication bias emerged, although the possibility cannot be excluded for emergence time and opioid consumption, where qualitative asymmetry was noted.

Narrative integration of findings

Analgesic efficacy

Esketamine demonstrated a statistically significant pain reduction effect that persisted from early postoperative periods through to 24 h. The early effect size (MD = –0.32 numeric rating scale units) was less than the MCID. The very large SMD observed at 24 h reflects the influence of heterogeneous pain scales but maintains a consistent direction of effect.

Opioid-sparing potential

While the cumulative dose remained unchanged, the frequency of rescue analgesic requirements was significantly reduced (RR = 0.62). This suggests that esketamine�s value lies in preventing the need for breakthrough pain intervention rather than drastically lowering the total 24-hour opioid burden.

Enhanced recovery metrics

No significant improvements were observed in emergence time or PACU discharge durations. Outlier findings, such as those from Massoth et al.,25 highlight the variability introduced by surgical complexity and differing local discharge criteria.

Adverse-effect profile

A 26% reduction in PONV was observed, aligning with previous literature suggesting that NMDA receptor blockade reduces emetogenic stimuli. Importantly, no severe psychotomimetic side effects were reported that could compromise trial blinding.

Certainty and limitations

Overall, the certainty of evidence was moderate to low, reflecting the effects of inconsistent dosing regimens, absence of standardised core outcome sets and moderate risk of publication bias in certain domains. The substantial heterogeneity observed across trials underscores the need for future studies employing standardised protocols and direct dose-finding comparisons.

Summary of key findings

Esketamine administration during laparoscopic procedures reduces immediate and 24-h pain scores (though this is minimal), lowers requirements for rescue analgesics and diminishes PONV. All of this is achieved without prolonging recovery. Esketamine's minimal safety risks underscores its clinical value as an analgesic. However, the extent of its opioid-sparing effect along with its influence on recovery speed warrant further investigation. Additional large randomised trials must be conducted to implement esketamine in multimodal opioid-light anaesthesia protocols while using standardised dosing and core outcome measures and transparent methodologies. Discussion

Opioids have long been used as a part of surgical anaesthesia because of their rapid onset time and potent analgesia.26 However, opioid use presents with certain undesirable events, including respiratory and haemodynamic instability, gastrointestinal disturbance, hyperalgesia and psychomimetic effects.26 Recently, various formulations of opioid free analgesia (OFA) have become popular to reduce or prevent opioid use, but their use is still debatable27,28. Moreover, previous literature does not report any particularly significant benefits of OFA.27,29 Administration of multimodal OFA utilising esketamine, however, presented significant findings. Importantly, the overall certainty of evidence for the primary outcomes ranged from low to very low according to the GRADE framework, primarily due to clinical heterogeneity, variability in dosing strategies and inconsistent reporting of peri-operative analgesic protocols

Esketamine is a multimodal anaesthetic utilised in opioid-free anaesthesia (OFA).30 It noncompetitively blocks the central nervous system's NMDA receptors and alters receptor structure allosterically20,31; it also binds to μ-opioid receptors and δ-opioid receptors.32,33 This increases serotonin and norepinephrine concentrations in the brain,34 which prevents opioid tolerance and nociceptive hypersensitivity, exerting antihyperalgesic effects23 and enhancing the overall peri-operative analgesic and anaesthetic profile. An Esketamine is an NMDA receptor antagonist, and it intricately modulates serotonin and norepinephrine levels within the central nervous system, imparting analgesic, anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective benefits35 whilst reducing PONV.36 A comprehensive meta-analysis comprising 12 randomised trials revealed that i.v. esketamine substantially mitigated pain severity and opioid demands postsurgery.37 This effect is attributable to its inhibition of NMDA receptors, which curtail central sensitisation, alongside the activation of pain-inhibitory pathways.38,39 Zhang et al. discovered that esketamine lowered PONV without increasing psychotomimetic effects.5

Rescue-analgesic requirement

This meta-analysis elucidates that intra-operative esketamine administration significantly reduces the need for rescue analgesia across various surgical settings. These findings align with numerous high-quality RCTs that affirm esketamine's opioid-sparing properties in total hip replacement, thyroidectomy, thoracoscopic, gynaecological, and caesarean surgeries.26–30 These studies showcase reduced postoperative opioid consumption and attenuated pain scores in patients receiving esketamine.

A reduction in rescue analgesic requirement without any effect on cumulative opioid consumption can be noticed. This contradiction is attributed to heterogeneity in rescue analgesic protocols across the studies. Some studies do not mention the rescue analgesic used, while four studies use an NSAID (flurbiprofen axetil) as the rescue analgesia.15,21,22,25 Significant variations in dosing regimens and surgical domains limit the generalisability of our findings. Further RCTs are needed to optimise dosing strategies and identify the patient populations that most benefit from them.

Postoperative nausea and vomiting

Nausea and vomiting frequently occur following laparoscopic interventions, especially when the gut is manipulated. The concomitant administration of opioids aggravates this condition leading to increased discomfort. This, in turn, delays recovery and prolongs hospitalisation. Our analysis aligns with previous studies demonstrating a lower incidence of these unfavourable outcomes. An RCT encompassing 420 patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery revealed that esketamine infusion significantly attenuated the incidence of PONV (9.7%) in comparison with the control cohort (30.5%).36 This reduction is attributed to decreased opioid utilisation, with remifentanil and sufentanil dosages curtailed by 40.0% and 75.2%, respectively, within the esketamine group.

A randomised, double-blind, multicentre trial demonstrated that esketamine with propofol reduced PONV and improved ERAS (Enhanced Recovery After Surgery) outcomes in laparoscopic surgery.40 Intra-operative esketamine may thus effectively reduce PONV through opioid-sparing and central antiemetic effects, supporting its role in multimodal analgesia.

It must be highlighted that esketamine does not lead to significant reductions in PONV when compared with nonopioid anaesthetic formulations.41 However, when compared with opioid formulations, esketamine provides the advantage of decreased PONV incidence.42,43 It is important to note that in most included trials, both esketamine and control groups received opioids as part of balanced anaesthesia, albeit in differing doses or combinations. Therefore, the apparent reduction in PONV observed in our pooled analysis likely represents a relative decrease mediated by reduced opioid exposure rather than a direct antiemetic property of esketamine. The interaction between esketamine and opioids may thus create a mixed influence on emetogenic potential, and our findings should be interpreted within this context. Future studies employing strictly OFA protocols are warranted to delineate esketamine's independent impact on PONV.

Cumulative opioid consumption (24 h)

The absence of a considerable decrease in cumulative 24-h opioid consumption noted in the meta-analysis may be attributed to the heterogeneity in the rescue analgesic protocol across the studies, mainly utilising OFA,﻿ as stated earlier. Although Wang et al.37 and Qi et al.36 reported opioid-sparing effects of esketamine, other trials failed to replicate these findings. Henceforth, prospective trials with standard outcome measures are recommended.7

Postoperative pain (0 to 6 and 12 to 24 h)

Our meta-analysis revealed that esketamine was associated with a statistically significant but small reduction in 6-h and 12 to 24-h postoperative pain, rescue-analgesic use and PONV following laparoscopic surgeries. The results of the meta-analysis by Hung et al. 44 align with our findings.

It should be noted that Deng et al. contributed significantly to the heterogeneity in results for acute pain, a variation that23 can be attributed to the esketamine dose regimen used. Deng et al. 23 administered 1.5 mg kg-1 esketamine, compared with the 0.25 to 0.5 mg kg-1 esketamine used across other studies. This highlights the need to develop an optimal dosage regimen and guidelines for esketamine use. Moreover, only Deng et al. 23 combined sufentanil with esketamine, while other studies primarily used either esketamine alone or in combination with dexmedetomidine. Sufentanil, a μ-opioid receptor agonist,45 offers strong synergistic analgesia with esketamine compared with esketamine alone or dexmedetomidine, an α2-adrenergic agonist.46

Emergence from anaesthesia (time duration)

Time from cessation of anaesthetics to awakening was reportedly less across the esketamine-administered patients. This is especially important as prolongation of extubation time with opioid use has been reported previously.47 Shortened awakening time can be ascribed to the pharmacokinetic properties of esketamine, such as a high clearance rate and fast metabolism. This results in improved controllability of anaesthesia and consequently patients wake up faster and safer.48

Postanaesthesia care unit length of stay

A 1.60-min reduction in PACU stay was attributed to esketamine. Short PACU stay due to esketamine can be attributed to its rapid recovery profile48 and a beneficial effect on PSD.16,49 Although the meta-analysis demonstrated a statistically significant 1.6-min reduction in PACU stay with esketamine, this difference is minimal and unlikely to be clinically meaningful; therefore, esketamine's advantage in improving recovery times should be interpreted cautiously. As there was no considerable reduction in cumulative opioid use, opioid-related side effects present a comparable burden between the two patient cohorts. Thus, any differences in the safety outcomes are attributable to esketamine. Moreover, postoperative acute pain confines patients to bed and affects rapid recovery after surgery, ultimately affecting the quality of life of patients.49,50 By a significant reduction in postoperative pain, esketamine reduces PACU stay. Massoth et al. 25 introduced considerable heterogeneity in the data, and the four times higher sedation incidence due to dexmedetomidine use in the esketamine group is responsible for this heterogeneity.25 Low doses of esketamine were used among all the studies reporting on PACU stay (≤ 0.5 mg kg-1), which limits its psychomimetic effects to milder ones.

Limitations

This meta-analysis has some notable limitations. First, most of the included trials were small single-centre studies, influencing the statistical power and generalisability of the findings. Substantial, statistical and clinical heterogeneity was observed across studies for key outcomes such as postoperative pain, emergence time, PACU stay and opioid consumption (I2 values ranging from 71 to 99%). Although random-effects models and subgroup analyses were employed to address this variability, differences in esketamine dosing regimens, surgical types, patient populations and outcome measurement methods make it challenging to draw universally applicable conclusions. The sensitivity analysis is compromised, not reliable, as it was restricted to the fixed effect model.

Secondly, methodological concerns regarding the included RCTs may affect the reliability of the results. Geographic homogeneity is notable, with 10 of the 11 trials originating from the same single country (China), raising concerns about cross-regional applicability. One study was rated at high risk of bias, and five others had ‘some concerns’ due to issues such as inadequate blinding of personnel, potential outcome measurement bias and lack of prospective protocol registration. In addition, potential funding bias could not be fully excluded. Although none of the included RCTs explicitly reported industry sponsorship of esketamine, incomplete disclosure of funding sources is possible and may have influenced results. These issues may have influenced the observed effect sizes, ultimately reducing the overall certainty of the evidence. Although publication bias was assessed, it cannot be entirely excluded, especially for outcomes with fewer than 10 studies, where formal assessment is less reliable. Despite adherence to the PRISMA guidelines, potential selection bias related to publication, language restriction and inconsistent reporting across included studies cannot be completely excluded. Our multidatabase search strategy was constrained by limited access to paid databases due to the absence of institutional subscriptions among the authors affiliated institutions, potentially affecting the completeness of evidence synthesis. Finally, variability in control group interventions (placebo vs. opioid-centred standard care) and the limited number of studies contributing to certain analyses, such as rescue-analgesic requirement, further limit the robustness of some conclusions. Furthermore, as most included trials administered opioids in both groups, it was not possible to isolate esketamine's direct effect on PONV independent of concomitant opioid use. The short follow-up period (up to 72 h) also restricts our ability to evaluate the long-term effects of esketamine across different types of laparoscopic surgery. Time to first rescue analgesic was not included due to the lack of sufficient data. Moreover, the pain outcomes were assessed using NRS or VAS scales across the trials, which account for pain intensity but fail to capture the broader dimensions of pain.

It must be noticed that a lack of uniform data across the studies for the mode of administration of esketamine (single bolus, continuous infusion and bolus followed by an infusion) limits a comprehensive analysis in this aspect. Significant clinical heterogeneity was also observed across the included studies, particularly with respect to esketamine dosing strategies, modes of administration and the variable use of baseline and rescue analgesics, including opioids and nonopioid agents. These variations may have influenced pooled estimates of pain scores and opioid consumption and limit the interpretability and generalisability of the findings. Therefore, future trials should aim to standardise administration protocols to allow such comparisons.

Conclusion

With a low to very low level of certainty (GRADE), our study suggests that intra-operative esketamine in laparoscopic surgery significantly reduces early-onset acute pain (0 to 6 h) and also late-onset (12 to 24 h) postoperative pain, decreases the need for rescue analgesia and lowers the incidence of PONV, without adversely affecting emergence time or PACU length of stay. It should be noted that the absence of a significant decrease in 24-h cumulative opioid consumption may be partially explained by the inclusion of several RCTs utilising OFA protocols, in which baseline opioid exposure was already minimised. This may have reduced the potential for further measurable opioid-sparing effects of esketamine. The slight reduction in PACU duration observed with esketamine is statistically but not clinically significant and does not imply a substantive advantage in recovery speed. Although esketamine shows promising benefits as part of multimodal analgesia, the overall certainty of evidence ranged from moderate (pain and PONV) to low (opioid consumption, PACU stay). However, considerable heterogeneity in esketamine dosing, mode of administration and background analgesic regimens currently exist across included trials. Thus, the strength of these conclusions is limited, and additional high-quality trials are required to increase confidence in effect estimates and define its clinical role more precisely.
