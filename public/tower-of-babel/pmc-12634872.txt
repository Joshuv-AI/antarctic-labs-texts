Anesthetic Management for Pituitary Tumor Resection in a Patient With Acromegalic Cardiomyopathy: A Case Report
Authors: Shuting Yang, Mi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcromegalic cardiomyopathy is one of the common comorbidities of cardiovascular diseases in patients with acromegaly, and it is also a major risk factor affecting the survival rate of patients with acromegaly. The perioperative management of such patients is a challenge for anesthesiologists. In this case report, we describe a patient with acromegalic cardiomyopathy who successfully underwent transsphenoidal endoscopic resection of a pituitary lesion under general anesthesia. For the perioperative anesthesia management of patients with acromegalic cardiomyopathy, it is crucial to closely monitor their high airway risk and compensatory cardiac function challenges. By comprehending the pathophysiological mechanisms of the disease and conducting thorough preoperative diagnostic evaluations, implementing individualized strategies and fostering multidisciplinary collaboration can effectively enhance the perioperative safety of these patients.

Introduction

A debilitating endocrine‐metabolic pathology, acromegaly is defined by sustained hypersecretion of growth hormone (GH) and consequent marked increases in circulating insulin‐like growth factor‐1 (IGF‐1) biomarkers [1]. More than 95% of cases are caused by pituitary somatotroph adenomas. The pathological overproduction of GH and IGF‐1 fundamentally drives comprehensive somatic transformations, manifesting as pronounced enlargement of connective tissues, bone structures, and cartilaginous elements, thereby engendering the prototypical clinical presentations of acromegaly. Additionally, it results in multiple organ and system complications, including cardiovascular, respiratory, and gastrointestinal disorders, as well as dysregulation of glucose and lipid metabolism [2]. These complications contribute to elevated mortality rates, particularly due to cardiovascular disease and stroke [3, 4, 5]. Acromegalic cardiomyopathy is one of the common comorbidities of cardiovascular diseases in patients with acromegaly, and it is also a major risk factor affecting the survival rate of patients with acromegaly. The perioperative management of such patients is a challenge for anesthesiologists.

Case History

An adult male, aged 55, arrived at the clinic describing prolonged macrodactyly and craniofacial modifications persisting for half a decade. Approximately 5 months prior, he sought emergency care at our hospital due to dyspnea on exertion. Comprehensive cardiac ultrasonographic examination disclosed generalized cardiac chamber expansion, compromised left ventricular performance with an ejection fraction (EF) measuring 21% (reference range:50%–70%), significant aortic and pulmonary vessel dilation, minimal valvular insufficiency at mitral and tricuspid regions, marginal aortic valve leakage, and pronounced pulmonary vascular resistance elevated to 56 mmHg. He was diagnosed with dilated cardiomyopathy, heart failure, ventricular premature beats, and hypertension and was admitted to the Department of Cardiology for further management. During hospitalization, acromegalic features were noted. Laboratory tests showed an elevated GH level of 31.4 ng/mL (reference range: 0.00–3.00 ng/mL) and an IGF‐1 level of 418.0 ng/mL (reference range: 45–210 ng/mL). Pituitary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) unveiled an oval‐shaped abnormal signal within the sellar region, distinctly demarcated, measuring approximately 2.0 × 1.5 cm. (Figure 1). The pathological focus demonstrated isointensity on T1‐weighted imaging (T1WI) and subtle hyperintense signals on T2‐weighted imaging (T2WI). Post‐contrast imaging showed enhancement with relatively low signal intensity, while the hyperintensity of the posterior pituitary was preserved, suggestive of a sellar mass, with pituitary neuroendocrine tumor being the primary consideration. After an endocrinology consultation, a diagnosis of acromegalic cardiomyopathy was made. The endocrinology team recommended optimizing cardiac function before considering neurosurgical intervention for tumor resection. During hospitalization, the patient's condition improved after receiving standardized heart failure management, antiarrhythmic therapy, and blood pressure regulation. He was subsequently discharged. The patient subsequently underwent comprehensive diagnostic procedures and treatment planning at our hospital's dedicated Neurosurgery Department.

At the time of hospital entry, the patient demonstrated clear consciousness, optimal cognitive alertness, and maintained a standard postural alignment. Patient anthropometric measurements documented a stature of 175 cm, body mass of 92.5 kg, with a calculated body mass index (BMI) of 30.20 kg/m2. Vital signs were as follows: blood pressure (BP) 129/84 mmHg, heart rate (HR) 66 bpm, respiratory rate (RR) 18 breaths/min, and body temperature 36.2°C. Ophthalmological examination revealed cataractous changes in both eyes, but visual acuity was normal. The patient exhibited facial features characteristic of acromegaly, including prominent zygomatic bones, frontal bossing, and mandibular enlargement, along with thickened nasal and labial tissues and macroglossia. Percussion indicated bilateral cardiac enlargement, with the left cardiac border extending 0.5 cm beyond the left midclavicular line at the fifth intercostal space. Cardiac auscultation revealed a regular rhythm at 66 bpm, with a systolic blowing murmur over the mitral valve area; no additional murmurs or extra heart sounds were detected in other valvular auscultation regions. The patient's limb movements were normal, but both hands and feet were noticeably enlarged, with thickened fingers and toes.

The patient's laboratory results revealed the following: GH level of 12.30 ng/mL (reference range: 0.00–3.00 ng/mL), IGF‐1 at 379.0 ng/mL (reference range: 45–210 ng/mL), Prolactin at 3.23 ng/mL (reference range: 3.46–19.40 ng/mL), Insulin‐like Growth Factor Binding Protein‐3 (IGFBP‐3) at −38.1 μg/mL (reference range: 3.4–6.8 μg/mL), and high‐sensitivity cardiac troponin at 0.033 ng/mL (reference range: 0.000–0.034 ng/mL). Other biochemical test results were unremarkable. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed global cardiac enlargement, with a predominance in the left heart, a left ventricular end‐diastolic diameter of 70 mm (reference range: 45–55 mm), thickened left ventricular walls, and diffuse hypokinesis. Left ventricular systolic dysfunction was noted, with an EF of 41% (reference range:50%–70%). Additionally, dilation of the ascending aorta was observed, along with mild aortic and tricuspid regurgitation and mild‐to‐moderate mitral regurgitation. A standard electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus rhythm, abnormal Q waves in leads III and aVF, frequent premature ventricular contractions occurring in bigeminy, intraventricular conduction delay, left axis deviation, and ST‐segment and T‐wave abnormalities. Considering the patient's symptoms, physical findings, laboratory results, and imaging examinations, a diagnosis of sellar mass (pituitary neuroendocrine tumor) and acromegalic cardiomyopathy was established. The patient was scheduled for transsphenoidal endoscopic pituitary lesion resection under general anesthesia.

Methods

The patient underwent a preoperative anesthetic assessment and was classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status Class III and New York Heart Association (NYHA) Functional Class II. The patient exhibited normal head and neck mobility, with a thyromental distance of 5 cm, Mallampati classification of III, and an interincisor distance of three fingerbreadths. A history of snoring was also noted. Given the patient's condition and the surgical requirements, general anesthesia with tracheal intubation using a video laryngoscope was selected. A difficult airway management plan was prepared as a precaution.

The patient adhered to routine preoperative fasting and fluid restriction protocols. Prior to surgical intervention, peripheral venous access was established in the upper extremity, complemented by face mask oxygen administration and continuous cardiac electrical activity monitoring. The baseline vital signs recorded were: HR of 69 beats per minute, BP of 149/77 mmHg, and oxygen saturation (SpO2) of 99% on 2 L/min of pure oxygen via a face mask. Under ultrasound guidance, a left radial artery puncture and catheterization were performed for invasive blood pressure (IBP) monitoring and arterial blood gas analysis. Additionally, a right internal jugular vein catheter was placed under ultrasound guidance for central venous pressure (CVP) monitoring. For anesthetic induction, preoxygenation with 6 L/min oxygen flow was administered via a face mask for 5 minutes. Subsequently, intravenous induction agents were slowly administered in the following sequence: midazolam 3 mg, etomidate 20 mg, and sufentanil 35 μg. After the administered medications had taken full effect, 50 mg of rocuronium was injected intravenously. Shortly thereafter, a 7.5# reinforced airway tube was successfully intubated under video laryngoscopic guidance, achieving an optimal placement depth of 23 cm. The intubation procedure was uneventful. The anesthesiologist adjusted the ventilator parameters based on the patient's partial pressure of end‐tidal carbon dioxide (PetCO2) and SpO2 to ensure a tidal volume of 8–10 mL/kg, SpO2 maintained at 99%–100%, and PetCO2 within the range of 35–45 mmHg. Intraoperative anesthesia was maintained using a combined intravenous‐inhalational technique. Anesthetic management involved continuous intravenous administration of propofol (2–4 mg/(kg·h)), remifentanil (0.1–0.15 μg/(kg·min)), and cisatracurium (2 μg/(kg·min)), concurrently with sevoflurane inhalation, targeting a minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) of 0.6–1.0. Anesthetic drug dosages and fluid administration rates were adjusted based on bispectral index (BIS), IBP, HR, urine output, and fluid balance, ensuring a BIS range of 45–60 and a body temperature of 36°C–37°C. Intermittent blood gas analysis was conducted intraoperatively to maintain hemoglobin levels above 80 g/L (reference range: ≥ 70 g/L), hematocrit (Hct) above 25%, potential of hydrogen (pH) within 7.35–7.45, base excess (BE) between −2 and + 2, and blood glucose and electrolyte levels within normal physiological ranges. The patient's vital signs remained stable throughout the surgery. An additional intraoperative dose of the 5‐HT3 receptor antagonist ondansetron and nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) flurbiprofen axetil were administered. The procedure lasted 180 min, with an estimated blood loss of approximately 30 mL. A total of 1500 mL of intravenous fluids was administered, and urine output was recorded at 450 mL.

Conclusion and Results

Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for further management. On the second day following surgical intervention, the patient was relocated to the general ward, subsequently achieving discharge with a notably improved clinical status on the sixth postoperative day. The pathological diagnosis of the resected surgical specimen was as follows: pituitary neuroendocrine tumor/growth hormone adenoma in the sellar region (overall dimensions: 0.8 × 0.5 × 0.3 cm).

Discussion

Acromegalic cardiomyopathy is a chronic progressive syndrome of cardiac dysfunction characterized by concentric hypertrophy of both ventricles and impaired diastolic function. Despite initial cardiac involvement being observed in merely 66% of individuals during acromegaly's diagnostic phase, the longitudinal progression suggests that virtually all patients (approximately 90%) will ultimately manifest cardiomyopathic [6]. The pathological mechanism of acromegalic cardiomyopathy is primarily associated with metabolic remodeling induced by prolonged exposure of myocardial tissue to excessive GH and IGF‐1 [7]. The comprehensive cardiac alterations accompanying acromegaly are methodically segmented into three sequential pathophysiological stages. In the early stage, which is reversible, myocardial concentric hypertrophy, increased heart rate, and elevated cardiac output are observed, often manifesting as a high‐output cardiac syndrome; such clinical manifestations are especially discernible in patients experiencing a relatively shortened disease trajectory. Within the intermediate developmental stage, myocardial tissue demonstrates escalated hypertrophic changes, concurrent with deteriorating diastolic cardiac performance and reduced left ventricular functional capacity during exertional challenges. In circumstances of inadequate clinical management, the disease trajectory reaches its final manifestation, characterized by comprehensive myocardial dysfunction involving simultaneous disruptions in diastolic and systolic cardiac performance, ultimately resulting in the emergence of congestive heart failure [8, 9, 10].

The choice of anesthetic technique for patients with acromegalic cardiomyopathy depends on the patient's condition, the surgical approach, available resources, and the anesthesiologist's preference. General anesthesia is the primary anesthetic method for transsphenoidal endoscopic pituitary tumor resection in patients with acromegalic cardiomyopathy. Anesthesiologists must carefully evaluate the patient's medical history, with particular attention to high‐risk airway features and cardiac compensatory function [11]. Notably, if the patient exhibits symptoms of heart failure, these should be managed before proceeding with surgical intervention.

Acromegaly‐associated pathological processes induce comprehensive anatomical respiratory tract remodeling, manifesting through substantial soft tissue hyperplasia that systematically transforms oral, nasal, lingual, palatal, epiglottic, and aryepiglottic structural configurations, as well as mandibular overgrowth leading to prognathism, which predisposes them to a predictably difficult airway. Studies indicate that up to 70% of patients with acromegaly may suffer from obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) [12, 13]. In a prospective study by Schmitt et al. [14] involving 128 patients with acromegaly, 20% of patients with a preoperative Mallampati score of grade I or II were still found to have a difficult airway. This finding suggests that even in the absence of significant abnormalities on preoperative airway assessment, the incidence of unexpected difficult airways in acromegalic patients is substantially higher than in the general population. Nemergut et al. [15] reported that 9.1% of patients with acromegaly experienced failed initial attempts at direct laryngoscopy for intubation, necessitating a second intubation attempt. Therefore, for perioperative airway management in acromegalic patients, anesthesiologists must proactively prepare for difficult airway scenarios. Preoperative airway risk stratification can be performed using a combination of modified Mallampati classification and three‐dimensional airway reconstruction. Additionally, appropriate airway management equipment should be readily available, including oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airways, video laryngoscopes, laryngeal masks, fiberoptic bronchoscopes, and percutaneous tracheostomy kits. Adhering strictly to the Practice Guidelines for the Management of the Difficult Airway, a stepwise airway intervention strategy should be implemented [16].

Critical considerations for anesthetic protocols in acromegalic cardiomyopathy encompass: 1. Minimizing excessive intravascular volume loading; 2. Mitigating potential sudden elevations in systemic vascular resistance to diminish arrhythmia and thromboembolism risks; 3. Preserving optimal myocardial performance and cardiac output to guarantee appropriate circulatory perfusion, with particular emphasis on coronary blood flow [17, 18].

Preoperative ECG and echocardiography are crucial examinations for patients with acromegalic cardiomyopathy, as they directly assess arrhythmias and the systolic and diastolic functions of both ventricles associated with the condition [19, 20]. During surgery, in addition to routine ASA standard monitoring, it is advisable to incorporate IBP monitoring, CVP monitoring, neuromuscular monitoring, and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) to maintain optimal hemodynamic stability and anesthetic depth. Mechanical ventilation potentially reduces venous circulation and cardiac stroke volume; therefore, initial respiratory tidal volume should be set within the 6–8 mL/kg range and carefully titrated to attenuate its impact on cardiac chamber filling and hemodynamic equilibrium.

Patients with acromegalic cardiomyopathy are particularly sensitive to the potential cardiodepressive effects of anesthetic agents [17]. Most commonly used general anesthetics have been reported to induce myocardial depression. Consequently, anesthetic induction should involve agents that exert minimal inhibitory effects on the circulatory system. In this case, etomidate and midazolam, which have minimal cardiovascular impact, were selected as intravenous anesthetics. The maintenance of anesthesia through a combination of intravenous and inhalational anesthesia allows for more effective control of both anesthetic depth and duration while reducing the dosage and adverse effects of various drugs, thereby enhancing the precision of anesthetic management. In cases where myocardial suppression is suspected, positive inotropic agents such as dobutamine, dopamine, norepinephrine, phosphodiesterase inhibitors, and levosimendan may be administered.

Intraoperative fluid strategy for acromegalic cardiomyopathy patients demands a precise, personalized protocol, carefully modulated based on diverse clinical parameters and individual physiological feedback to mitigate the risk of fluid surplus, which could trigger pulmonary vascular complications and potentially escalate to myocardial insufficiency [18], as well as insufficient fluid administration, which can result in inadequate ventricular filling and a significant reduction in cardiac output, compromising perfusion to vital organs. Common indicators for intraoperative fluid assessment include urine output, IBP, CVP, HR, hemoglobin concentration, and lactate levels. Additionally, TEE can be utilized for continuous and direct monitoring of cardiac function and volume status [21].

Since endoscopic transsphenoidal pituitary tumor resection involves surgical manipulation in the craniofacial region, special attention should be given to protecting the patient's eyes and preventing limb compression during the procedure. Postoperatively, patients typically experience mild to moderate pain, and effective postoperative analgesia is beneficial in mitigating hemodynamic fluctuations following surgery. Anesthesiologists can employ a multimodal analgesic approach, incorporating NSAIDs, acetaminophen, gabapentin, and local anesthetics [22]. Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) occurs in approximately 7.5% of patients undergoing pituitary tumor resection [23]. The administration of 5‐HT3 receptor antagonists, such as ondansetron, can effectively reduce the incidence of PONV. In parallel, perioperative anesthetic considerations for acromegalic cardiomyopathy patients require intricate monitoring and systematic assessment of pituitary hormonal landscapes to optimize surgical and physiological outcomes, with close monitoring of urine output to detect potential diabetes insipidus. Systematic intraoperative examination of arterial blood gas profiles enables clinicians to comprehensively assess metabolic status and maintain cellular homeostatic mechanisms.

Conclusions

Ultimately, effectively managing anesthesia for acromegalic cardiomyopathy patients during transsphenoidal pituitary tumor excision demands intricate comprehension of underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and meticulous preoperative diagnostic assessment. The primary objectives of anesthetic management include addressing the challenges of a difficult airway, ensuring precise hemodynamic control, and maintaining endocrine homeostasis. By implementing individualized strategies and fostering multidisciplinary collaboration, perioperative safety for these patients can be effectively ensured.
