Learning Non-Local Molecular Interactions via Equivariant Local Representations and Charge Equilibration
Paul Fuchs, Michał Sanocki, Julija Zavadlav

Abstract
Graph Neural Network (GNN) potentials relying on chemical locality offer near-quantum mechanical accuracy at significantly reduced computational costs. By propagating local information to distance particles, Message-passing neural networks (MPNNs) extend the locality concept to model interactions beyond their local neighborhood. Still, this locality precludes modeling long-range effects, such as charge transfer, electrostatic interactions, and dispersion effects, which are critical to adequately describe many real-world systems. In this work, we propose the Charge Equilibration Layer for Long-range Interactions (CELLI) to address the challenging modeling of non-local interactions and the high computational cost of MPNNs. This novel architecture generalizes the fourth-generation high-dimensional neural network (4GHDNN) concept, integrating the charge equilibration (Qeq) method into a model-agnostic building block for modern equivariant GNN potentials. A series of benchmarks show that CELLI can extend the strictly local Allegro architecture to model highly non-local interactions and charge transfer. Our architecture generalizes to diverse datasets and large structures, achieving an accuracy comparable to MPNNs at about twice the computational efficiency.



1
Introduction

Machine learning potentials (MLPs) are powerful tools for modelling interatomic interactions, as they can potentially achieve near-quantum mechanical accuracy at a fraction of the computational cost
(
Anstine & Isayev 2023
)
. This high accuracy combined with better scaling can enable precise simulations of larger systems than density functional theory (DFT), allowing the study of complex phenomena that would otherwise be computationally inaccessible
(
Frank et al. 2024
;
Ko et al. 2021
)
. However, their accuracy in large systems is often limited by the fact that most MLPs rely on local descriptors and assume that atomic interactions are dominated by their immediate environment
(
Anstine & Isayev 2023
)
. While effective for short-range interactions, this assumption limits their ability to capture long-range electrostatic interactions, charge transfer, and dispersion effects
(
Ko et al. 2021
;
Shaidu et al. 2024
)
.

The inability of MLPs to accurately model long-range interactions can greatly limit their applicability in real-world scenarios unless the computational framework incorporates an additional mechanism to address these interactions
(
Anstine & Isayev 2023
)
. Long-range interactions are crucial in several key physical phenomena, including molecular aggregation, protein folding, or the behaviour of ionic liquids
(
Zhou & Pang 2018
;
Baskin et al. 2022
)
.
For instance, in proteins, long-range interactions have been shown to play a significant role in their structure and function, with most residues participating in such interactions
(
Gromiha & Selvaraj 1999
;
Zhou & Pang 2018
)
, some of which can theoretically span up to 15 Å
(
Galante & Tkatchenko 2023
)
. As a result, even MLPs with near quantum-level accuracy for short-range interactions would be unable to fully capture the behaviour of such proteins without additional schemes considering these long-range effects.

This limitation has been widely acknowledged in the scientific community
(
Plé et al. 2023
;
Frank et al. 2024
;
Kosmala et al. 2023
;
Song et al. 2024
;
Ko et al. 2023
;
Shaidu et al. 2024
)
, prompting the development of various approaches aimed at addressing different types of long-range interactions
(
Anstine & Isayev 2023
)
. A common approach to account for some of the long-range effects involves modelling long-range electrostatic interactions, which often involves dynamically distributing charges across a system
(
Plé et al. 2023
)
. To this end charge equilibration (Qeq) scheme first introduced by
Rappe & Goddard III 1991
can be used. This mechanism enables precise modelling of long-range electrostatics and charge transfer by predicting environment-dependent electronegativities, which are then used to redistribute charge by minimizing the total system energy using Qeq
(
Ghasemi et al. 2015
)
. By incorporating non-local charge transfer, Qeq can expand the applicability of MLPs to more complex systems while maintaining a balance between computational efficiency and accuracy
(
Gubler et al. 2024
)
. This approach has been successfully applied in the development of both classical force fields and MLPs
(
van Duin et al. 2001
;
Ko et al. 2021
;
Ghasemi et al. 2015
)
, most notably in the fourth-generation high-dimensional neural network (4GHDNN). Alternatively, one could increase the cutoff radius to capture long-range interactions; however, this would cause a cubic growth in the number of atoms within the receptive field, leading to severe scalability challenges
(
Frank et al. 2024
)
and making large-scale system simulations unfeasible. In addition, it would further complicate the learning task, due to the expanding chemical configuration space, thus intensifying the challenge of sufficient sampling
(
Anstine & Isayev 2023
)
.

On the other hand, recent advancements in Graph Neural Network (GNN) architectures, particularly equivariant methods like Allegro introduced by
Musaelian et al. 2023
, which leverage E(3)-equivariance to enforce physical symmetries, are able to better generalize across diverse chemical spaces. Unlike 4GHDNN, which require hand-crafted symmetry functions that scale exponentially with the number of atom types and become impractical for large and diverse datasets
(
Batzner et al. 2022
)
like OE62
(
Stuke et al. 2020
)
, GNNs overcome this limitation by learning atomic representations directly from their environments
(
Unke et al. 2021b
)
. These advancements could also further enhance the viability of MLPs with Qeq by improving charge prediction accuracy, thereby enabling more precise modelling of electrostatic long-range interactions.

In this work, we introduce the Charge Equilibration Layer for Long-range Interactions (CELLI), a novel architecture that integrates Qeq with equivariant approaches to overcome the inherent locality of MLPs. Although our framework is model-agnostic, our implementation employs Allegro
(
Musaelian et al. 2023
)
, which we also use as a benchmark due to its locality, to demonstrate the necessity of the Qeq scheme for CELLI to account for long-range effects. This combination enhances the ability of MLPs to predict energy and forces accurately, particularly in systems dominated by long-range effects. To validate our method, we tested it on benchmark systems for long-range interactions, including cases where accurately modelling charge transfer is crucial
(
Ko et al. 2021
)
, as well as the OE62 dataset
(
Stuke et al. 2020
)
to demonstrate its generalization across more diverse chemical space. CELLI bridges the gap between local GNNs and the global charge redistribution capabilities of 4GHDNN, representing a significant advancement in MLP development.

2
Background

2.1
Graph Neural Networks

Molecular systems can be represented as graphs by describing atoms as nodes and defining edges between neighboring atoms within a fixed cutoff radius, allowing GNNs to learn atom-centered representations.
In the first step, GNNs embed this graph, assigning initial features
\bm{h}_{i}^{0}
to the nodes and features
\bm{x}_{ij}
to the edges from atom species
Z_{i}
and atom displacements.
Subsequently, GNNs encode the graph by iteratively updating edge and node features that are finally read out to obtain node, edge, and graph property predictions.

The popular class of Message-passing neural networks (MPNNs) class, first formalized by
Gilmer et al. 2017
, encodes the graph by iteratively performing message-passing

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{i}^{l+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}\mathcal{M}^{l}(\bm{h}_{i}^{l},\bm{h}_{j}^{l},\bm{x}_{ij}),

(1)

\displaystyle\bm{h}_{i}^{l+1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}^{l}(\bm{m}_{i}^{l+1},\bm{h}_{i}^{l}),

(2)

where
\mathcal{M}^{l}
and
\mathcal{U}^{l}
are learnable functions of the layer
l
.
As messages
\bm{m}_{i}^{l}
contain information from all graph neighbors
j\in\mathcal{N}(i)
of a particle
i
, MPNNs pass information of each atom’s neighborhood along the graph.
Therefore, message-passing gradually expands the atom’s receptive field and enables the capture of many-body correlations
(
Musaelian et al. 2023
)
.

Unfortunately, this information propagation complicates parallelized implementations of GNNs, e.g., in large-scale atomistic MD frameworks such as LAMMPS
(
Thompson et al. 2022
)
.
Therefore, strictly local architectures such as Allegro
(
Musaelian et al. 2023
)
have been proposed.
Conceptually, Allegro updates the directed edge features through the steps

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{ij}^{l+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}(i)}\mathcal{W}^{l}\left(\bm{x}_{ij}^{l},\bm{x}_{ik}^{l}\right),

(3)

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{ij}^{l+1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}^{l}\left(\bm{w}_{ij}^{l+1},\bm{x}_{ij}^{l+1}\right),

(4)

where
\bm{w}_{ij}^{l+1}
contains information from all edges that originate from the same node.
Corresponding to the message-passing framework, the function
\mathcal{W}^{l}
encodes information about the environment of an edge into the update function
\mathcal{U}^{l}
.
However, as two directed edges between nodes can contain different information (
\bm{x}^{l}_{ij}\neq\bm{x}^{l}_{ji}
), no information is passed along the graph.

2.2
Efficient Computation of Electrostatic Interactions

Electrostatic effects are commonly approximated by coulombic interactions.
For a system of
N
charges
Q
with Gaussian density, located at the centers of the particles
\bm{R}
, the coulombic interaction potential is

\displaystyle U_{\text{Coul}}(\bm{R},\bm{Q})=\sum_{i}^{N}\sum_{j>i}^{N}\frac{\operatorname{erf}(\alpha_{ij}r_{ij})}{r_{ij}}Q_{i}Q_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{2\alpha_{ii}}{\sqrt{\pi}}Q_{i}^{2},

(5)

where
\alpha_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\gamma_{i}^{2}+\gamma_{j}^{2})^{-1/2}
depends on the radii
\gamma_{i}
of the charges separated by a distance
r_{ij}=\lVert|\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j}\rVert

(
Ghasemi et al. 2015
)
.
These interactions can extend over larger distances as the interaction decays approximately with the factor
1/r
.
Moreover, the contributions from distant charges must be accurately captured without truncation or oversimplification
(
Anstine & Isayev 2023
)
.
Therefore, coulombic interactions are more challenging to model efficiently than, e.g., short-ranged van-der-Waals interactions.

Nevertheless, classical approaches have been proposed to model long-range interactions efficiently without computing direct pairwise interactions beyond a small cutoff.
Essentially, these methods decompose the interaction potential into a rapidly decaying short-range part and a smooth but slowly decaying long-range part.
The methods then treat the short-range part directly like other short-range interactions.
However, as the long-range part still accounts for contributions from distant charges, a more efficient computation requires a different treatment.
For example, the Fast Multipole Method
(
Greengard & Rokhlin 1987
)
hierarchically groups particles and computes distant interactions between these clusters collectively to achieve a
O(N)
scaling with respect to the number of particles.
Especially for periodic systems, the Smooth Particle Mesh Ewald (SPME) method
(
Essmann et al. 1995
)
computes long-ranged interactions more efficiently in the reciprocal space. Similar to the short-ranged part in real space, the long-ranged part decays quickly in the reciprocal space and can be truncated without losing accuracy.
Additionally, by mapping charges to a grid leveraging B-spline interpolation for smooth gradients and employing fast Fourier transforms, it achieves a computational complexity of
O(N\log N)
. Notably, SPME is not limited to periodic systems but can be generalized to systems with partial or fully non-periodic boundary conditions, e.g., to treat isolated clusters
(
Martyna & Tuckerman 1999
)
.

2.3
Charge Equilibration Method (Qeq)

Several approaches can compute long-ranged electrostatic interactions accurately and efficiently in many systems.
Nevertheless, these interactions must be adequately parametrized for the respective systems by assigning partial charges to the atoms.
Assigning fixed partial charges can introduce significant errors due to charge transfer induced by changes in the chemical environment
(
Ghasemi et al. 2015
)
.
Therefore, methods with dynamic partial charge assignment are necessary to accurately model molecular systems with significant electrostatic interactions.

To model environment-dependent partial charges, the Charge Equilibration (Qeq) method
(
Rappe & Goddard III 1991
)
proposes to redistribute charges in the system to minimize the total energy while maintaining charge conservation
\sum_{i=1}^{N}Q_{i}=Q_{\text{tot}}
.
In the Qeq method, the contribution of charges to the total energy

\displaystyle U_{\text{Qeq}}(\bm{R},\bm{Q})=U_{\text{Coul}}(\bm{R},\bm{Q})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\chi_{i}Q_{i}+\frac{J_{ii}}{2}Q_{i}^{2}\right]

(6)

consists of the coulombic interaction between charges
U_{\text{Coul}}
given in equation (
5
) and a second-order approximation of the charge-core interaction determined by the electronegativities
\chi_{i}
and chemical hardnesses
J_{i}
.
Due to the form of the coulombic interaction, the charge energy is quadratic in
\bm{Q}
.
Consequently, the minimum of
U_{\text{Qeq}}
is the solution of the linear system

\displaystyle\left[\left.\frac{\partial^{2}U_{\text{Coul}}}{\partial Q_{i}\partial Q_{j}}\right|_{\bm{R}}+J_{ii}\right]Q_{j}=-\chi_{i},

(7)

subject to the charge conserving equality constraint
\bm{1}^{T}\bm{Q}=Q_{\text{tot}}
.
For smaller systems, direct linear solvers can determine the optimal charges within a short runtime.
However, due to the cubic scaling with the number of particles
O(N^{3})
, several other approaches have been proposed to solve the system in quadratic
(
Nakano 1997
)
or quasi-linear time
(
Gubler et al. 2024
)
,
leveraging efficient treatments of long-range interactions outlined in section
2.2
.

3
CELLI Architecture

The Charge Equilibration Layer for Long-range Interactions (CELLI) generalizes the approach from
Ko et al. 2021
to recent equivariant GNN architectures.
Similar to
Ko et al. 2021
, we split the total potential energy

U=U_{\text{Coul}}+\Delta U

into an electrostatic component
U_{\text{Coul}}
and a correction
\Delta U
.
Following the CENT method
(
Ghasemi et al. 2015
)
, CELLI uses the Qeq method to compute partial charges
\bm{Q}
(subsection
2.3
) accounting for non-local charge transfer.
Subsequently, CELLI embeds the equilibrated partial charges as non-local features back into the GNN which learns the correction
\Delta U
.
Thus, instead of learning charges and the potential energy through separate NNs, CELLI enables flexible integration of Qeq-based charge prediction and non-local interaction modeling within a single model.

We describe CELLI using the example of the Allegro architecture
(
Musaelian et al. 2023
)
, visualized in figure
1
.
Allegro is a strictly local equivariant GNN that learns scalar features
\bm{x}_{ij}^{l}
and tensorial features
V_{ij}
for the directed edges
ij
of a graph through a sequence of
L
tensor-product layers (outlined in subsection
2.1
), which we call
Interaction Layers
in the following.

Inclusion of CELLI into the strictly local Allegro architecture. Allegro predicts the total energy

and partial charges
Q
using scalar
\bm{x}_{ij}^{l}
and tensorial
V_{ij}^{l}
edge features of an input graph with node positions
\bm{R}
, node species
\bm{Z}
, and connectivity
\mathcal{N}
.
\oplus
denotes a weighted residual update. Radii
\gamma_{i}^{\text{exp}}
are only used by CELLI.

To extend Allegro, we insert CELLI at one location between the Interaction Layers.
CELLI embeds and applies the Qeq method to learn long-ranged electrostatic interactions and partial charges using the latent scalar features.
As global charge transfer might affect the local electronic structure and thus the local many-body interactions
(
Ko et al. 2021
)
, CELLI embeds the equilibrated charges into the latent features
\bm{x}_{ij}^{l+1}
passed to the following tensor product layers. Finally, the readout layer uses the latent features to predict per-edge energies summing up to the correction potential
\Delta U
.

In the following, we provide a more detailed description of CELLI (Figure
2
).

Electronegativity Embedding

The latent features from the previous
l
Interaction Layers encode scalar descriptions of the edge’s local environment.
A multi-layer perceptron
\operatorname{MLP}_{\chi}
predicts a per-edge contribution to the electronegativity.
Summing up the contributions of all directed edges from
i
to
j
, multiplied by a species-invariant learnable factor
f
, yields the electronegativity
\chi_{i}=f\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}(i)}\operatorname{MLP}_{\chi}\left(\bm{x}_{ij}^{l}\right)
of particle
i
.

Species Embedding

The hardnesses
J_{i}
and charge radii
\gamma_{i}
of the particle
i
are fixed species-dependent parameters.
The soft plus activation function denoted as
\sigma_{+}(x)=\log(1+\exp(x))
ensures the positiveness of the raw hardness values
J_{i}=\sigma_{+}(\tilde{J}_{i})
.
We found that appropriate radii can crucially determine whether the optimization converges.
Therefore, we base the charge radii on single-bond covalent radii
\gamma_{i}
taken from
Pyykkö & Atsumi 2009
and learn a positive scaling factor to obtain
\gamma_{i}=\frac{\sigma_{+}(s_{i})}{\log(2)}\gamma_{i}^{\text{exp}}
. Additionally, we embed species as features
\bm{c}_{i}(Z_{i})
for later use in the charge embedding.

Charge Equilibration Method (Qeq)

The charge equilibration method takes the environment-dependent electronegativities, species-dependent hardnesses, and species-dependent charge radii to predict partial charges and the coulombic potential (subsection
2.3
).
As different systems require different treatments of the long-range electrostatic interactions, the method optionally employs, e.g., the Smooth Particle Mesh method
(
Essmann et al. 1995
)
for periodic systems.

Charges Embedding and Latent Feature Update

We embed the learned charges into the scalar features to provide non-local information to the network.
Therefore, a first multi-layer perceptron
\operatorname{MLP}_{Q}
generates charge-dependent features
\bm{y}_{ij}=\operatorname{MLP}_{Q}(Q_{i},\bm{c}_{i})
using the equilibrated charge of the particle and the species embedding
\bm{c}_{i}
. These features are then used in a second multi-layer-perceptron
\operatorname{MLP}_{\bm{x}}
together with the scalar features from the previous layer to learn the residual update
\bm{x}_{ij}^{l}=\operatorname{MLP}_{\bm{x}}\left(\bm{y}_{ij},\bm{x}_{ij}^{l}\right)p_{\text{env}}\left(\lVert\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j}\rVert\right)
, where
p_{\text{env}}
is a polynomial envelope function.

4
Related Work

Physics Based Approaches

The challenge of modelling long-range effects has long been recognized as a major obstacle in the development of MLPs
(
Anstine & Isayev 2023
)
, leading to a wide range of proposed solutions. One approach involves training neural networks on corrected energies, where long-range contributions, such as van der Waals
(
Rowe et al. 2020
)
or electrostatic interactions
(
Artrith et al. 2011
)
, are subtracted. However, capturing long-range electrostatic effects requires dynamically redistributing atomic charges across the system to account for changes in the chemical environment. This is particularly challenging, as it demands consideration of both local bonding environments and the global electrostatic landscape
(
Ghasemi et al. 2015
)
. Thus, traditional methods relying on fixed or locally assigned charges often fail to account for these dynamic, non-local effects
(
Ko et al. 2021
)
.

The Charge Equilibration Neural Network (CENT) was introduced to address these challenges by obtaining point charges through the Qeq scheme, based on predicted electronegativities
(
Ghasemi et al. 2015
)
. Notably, this approach was later adapted in 4G-HDNNPs, which combine Qeq with Behler-Parrinello Neural Networks by employing two separate networks for electronegativity and atomic energy predictions
(
Ko et al. 2021
)
. While this method accurately captures global charge distributions in simple systems with non-local effects, it requires two separate neural networks and is constrained by the use of hand-crafted descriptors, limiting its generalizability to chemically diverse datasets.

Alternative methods have since emerged, including those that predict charges directly
(
Song et al. 2024
;
Plé et al. 2023
)
, those inferring partial charges and dipole moments directly from energy and forces
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
, and others that estimate energy and forces based on atomic properties inspired by the Qeq scheme without the need for charge estimation
(
Shaidu et al. 2024
)
. While direct charge prediction may seem advantageous, it often requires additional correction schemes to ensure charge conservation and prevent unphysical behaviour
(
Song et al. 2024
;
Plé et al. 2023
)
, which is inherently addressed within the Qeq framework
(
Rappe & Goddard III 1991
)
. Furthermore, point charge-based methods generally assume chemical locality, which becomes problematic in systems where non-local effects dominate
(
Unke et al. 2021a
)
. Additionally, many such approaches depend on the availability of point charges in training datasets. This dependency not only limits their applicability—since not all datasets include point charges—but also introduces inconsistencies, as different charge partitioning methods (e.g., Hirshfeld, Bader) can yield varying results
(
Shaidu et al. 2024
)
.

Charge Independent Approaches

Some methods aim to bypass point charges altogether. For instance, the Self-Consistent Field Neural Network (SCFNN) employs two networks connected via a self-consistent loop to separately model short- and long-range interactions
(
Gao & Remsing 2022
)
. Another popular direction involves reciprocal space methods
(
Yu et al. 2022
;
Kosmala et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
, which transform real-space information into reciprocal space to model long-range interactions. Those approaches attempt to improve the accuracy of energy and force predictions, by encoding long-range effects through global descriptors or Fourier space representations. While reciprocal space techniques often emphasize modelling long-range effects, some approaches like Neural P
3
M
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
incorporate the exchange of information between short-range and long-range terms to provide a more comprehensive framework. Additionally, the Euclidean Fast Attention (EFA) scheme has been proposed
(
Frank et al. 2024
)
, which learns molecular representations embedded in Euclidean space using a linear-scaling attention-like mechanism. This method integrates over the unit sphere and respects relevant physical symmetries, it also requires additional methods specialized in local interactions and is not inherently applicable to modelling short-range effects
(
Frank et al. 2024
)
. Unfortunately, methods based on lattice vectors have limited generalizability, as they struggle with differently oriented structures or other supercells, and cannot easily be applied to simulations in realistic conditions
(
Frank et al. 2024
)
. Despite these advances, explicitly predicting charge distributions can be advantageous, as it enables direct observation of charge transfer and allows for straightforward simulation of systems in external electric fields.

Experiments & Results

We performed all experiments in the deep-learning framework JAX using chemtrain
(
Fuchs et al. 2025
)
to train the models.
Therefore, we adapted JAX-MD
(
Schoenholz & Cubuk 2021
)
, and JAX compatible implementations of Allegro
(
Mario & Daigavane
)
and DimeNet++
(
Thaler & Zavadlav 2021
;
Gasteiger et al. 2022
)
.

First, we benchmark our approach on the four systems introduced in
Ko et al. 2021
, using the provided DFT data. These systems require accurate representation of long-range effects and offer a means for visual inspection of the results, ensuring that the model does not produce unphysical behaviour. However, because these benchmarks consist of relatively small and simple systems, they cannot fully demonstrate the generalizability across wide chemical space and the advantageous scalability of our scheme with the size of the system. To address this, we included the OE62 dataset
(
Stuke et al. 2020
)
, which allows us to evaluate the performance of our model on larger and more diverse systems of varying sizes, providing a complementary assessment to the simpler benchmarks focused on single structures with different charge states. It was also utilized by
Kosmala et al. 2023
to model long-range interactions, facilitating easier evaluation. To assess CELLI’s performance, we compared it to DimNet++, which previously demonstrated the best accuracy among baseline models at a comparable computational cost
(
Kosmala et al. 2023
)
.

Gold Dimers on MgO(001) Surface

The final benchmark is a periodic system consisting of a gold dimer (Au
2

Two configurations were considered: “wetting,” where both Au atoms lie near Mg atoms, and “non-wetting,” where one Au atom binds to an O atom while the other remains farther away. These configurations assess the model’s ability to capture adsorption energies and forces in charge-sensitive periodic settings.

In all four benchmark cases, we observed significant improvements over the models presented in
Ko et al. 2021
(Table

However, we also demonstrated substantial improvement in accuracy over the baseline Allegro model, indicating that our performance cannot be solely attributed to advancements in architecture. This highlights the effectiveness of the Qeq scheme combined with Allegro in accounting for long-range effects. In addition to demonstrating improvements in prediction error, we conducted experiments on these systems to verify whether the model could accurately represent long-range effects and the resulting changes in the PES. Importantly, in all cases (Figures

Interestingly, the reduction in error for sodium chloride clusters and AuMgO was significantly higher for forces and energies than for charges, whereas for silver clusters, the error reduction was much more pronounced for charges than for energies and forces. In fact, a slight increase in the energy RMSE was observed for silver clusters. For example, in the AuMgO system, the RMSE for forces was reduced by approximately 42 %, and for energies by 39 %, while the error for charges remained nearly unchanged. This discrepancy is possibly caused by the existence of different charge partitioning schemes that would minimize the prediction error for forces and energies

. These results suggest that prioritizing charge prediction may not always lead to an improved model.

The OE62 dataset provides a diverse benchmark for the evaluation of our model, as it consists of 62,000 organic molecules extracted from the Cambridge Structural Database, with DFT-optimized geometries at the PBE level, including van der Waals corrections
(
Stuke et al. 2020
)
. OE62 spans a broad chemical space, with up to 174 atoms and 16 elements, offering a comprehensive test case for assessing the scalability and generalizability of models on chemically complex and diverse systems.

To conduct a more detailed analysis of CELLI’s performance and scalability, we evaluated two CELLI versions and four Allegro versions, which differed in model size and the presence of an additional tensor product layer, as well as DimeNet++ with hyperparameters as in
Kosmala et al. 2023
. Our results (Table
2
) show that CELLI outperformed the baseline Allegro models with only a marginal increase in computational cost. Notably, even the small CELLI model outperformed the largest Allegro model, demonstrating that applying the Qeq scheme is more effective than merely increasing model size, as certain effects cannot be captured without an appropriate long-range correction method.

While CELLI showed a slight increase in MAE compared to DimeNet++, it outperformed it in RMSE and achieved significant computational efficiency, reducing runtime by a factor of 2.4 for the small version and 1.8 for the large version. The discrepancy between the RMSE and MAE results could be due to significant outliers in DimeNet++, which highlights the generalizability of CELLI. Additionally, CELLI enables efficient parallelization across multiple GPUs, further enhancing its scalability. Based on data from
Kosmala et al. 2023
, CELLI is also likely to outperform PAINN and SchNet. This highlights CELLI’s ability to deliver comparable or superior accuracy to state-of-the-art MPNNs while operating at a fraction of the computational cost, making it highly suitable for practical applications such as large-scale MD simulations.

To further illustrate CELLI’s advantages, we assessed its scaling behaviour by measuring forward-pass times for systems with varying atom counts. CELLI and Allegro demonstrated significantly better scaling than DimeNet++, particularly for larger molecules, which are more representative of many systems present in MD studies. This underscores CELLI’s potential for efficient and accurate MD simulations of large, complex structures. In principle, adding the CELLI layer to DimeNet++ could further improve its accuracy with only a minor increase in computational cost. However, this lies outside the scope of our study, as our focus is on methods suited for practical applications.

6
Discussion

Our proposed approach, which integrates advanced neural network architectures with the Qeq scheme, offers a solution to the long-standing challenge of accurately modelling long-range interactions in MLPs. Strictly local MLPs struggle with long-range electrostatic effects, whereas our method, through dynamic charge redistribution, can effectively capture complex electrostatic environments, enhancing predictive accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency. The substantial improvements observed across multiple benchmark cases underscore the potential of our method to advance the modelling of systems with significant long-range effects. The utilized architecture offers several advantages over other existing methods. Unlike the 4GHDNNP utilized by
(
Ko et al. 2021
)
, it does not rely on Behler-Parrinello Neural Networks, which limits its applicability to simple systems. Additionally, it is not constrained by the use of fixed lattice vectors during training
(
Frank et al. 2024
)
, as seen in lattice-based methods
Kosmala et al. 2023
. Furthermore, the incorporation of physics-based priors likely enhances its ability to generalize better than other approaches
(
Thürlemann et al. 2023
)
, which may explain the discrepancy between the RMSE and MAE results in the OE62 dataset observed for CELLI and DimeNet++. It is also significantly more cost-effective than MPNNs like DimeNet++, increasing its viability for modelling large-scale and bulk systems due to its improved scaling. This combination of high accuracy, generalizability, and efficiency makes CELLI ideal for systems with strict computational cost constraints.

Appendix B
Methods

B.1
Preparation of Energies

The reference energies in the datasets contain large negative shifts.
Therefore, we shift the reference energies
U
by species-dependent constant shifts
U_{s}
to obtain the target energies

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{i}=U_{i}-\sum_{s=1}^{S}U_{s}N_{s,i}

(8)

where
N_{s,i}
counts the occurrences of species
s
in sample
i
.
We determined the shifts
U_{s}
through a ridge-regression fit to the dataset.

B.2
Model Optimization

We train the models via the Force Matching method
(
Ercolessi & Adams 1994
;
Fuchs et al. 2025
)
.
Therefore, we optimize the parameters
\theta
to minimize the loss function

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\biggl[

\displaystyle\gamma_{U}\lVert U(\theta,\bm{R}_{i})-\hat{U}_{i}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{F}}{3N_{i}}\lVert\bm{F}(\theta,\bm{R}_{i})-\hat{\bm{F}}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{Q}}{N_{i}}\lVert\bm{Q}(\theta,\bm{R}_{i})-\hat{\bm{Q}}\rVert^{2}\biggr]

(9)

between the reference values
\hat{U},\hat{\bm{F}},\hat{\bm{Q}}
and the model predictions
U,\bm{F},\bm{Q}
for
D
samples
\bm{R}
of the training dataset via stochastic optimization using the ADAM optimizer
(
Kingma & Ba 2017
)
and a polynomial step-size schedule with weight decay.
The parameters
\gamma_{U},\gamma_{F},\gamma_{Q}
balance the contributions of the targets to the loss and are set problem-specific.
We monitor the convergence by empirically estimating the loss on a disjoint validation split and select the parametrization
\theta
that yielded the lowest error on the validation split.

B.3
Timing OE62 Forward Passes

We evaluate the forward pass run times for all models on a single
Nvidia A100
.
For evaluating the computational performance, we partition the training split at
[25,50,75,115,174]
atoms per molecule and chose the 5000 largest structures.
For each subset, we choose the maximum number of edges and triplets to account for the maximum required by any sample in the subset.
We then time the forward pass for batch sizes of
[1,10,25,50,100]
for the ahead-of-time compiled model.
